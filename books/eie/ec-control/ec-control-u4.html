4.1 Frequency response and frequency-domain specifications

A stable linear system has a simple habit with sine waves: feed it A sin(ωt), and after the start-up transient dies, out comes a sine of the same frequency. Only two things change: amplitude and phase. That is frequency response. It is used to read a system's speed, smoothness and stability from sine tests, and to design controllers in Unit 5.

You need the transfer function idea from Unit 1, the second-order response and damping ratio from Unit 2, and complex-number magnitude and angle.

Sine in, sine out

A stable linear system does not create new frequencies. Apply A sin(ωt) and wait for the start-up transient to decay. The steady-state output is a sine of the same ω, with amplitude multiplied by |G(jω)| and phase shifted by ∠G(jω). This is the frequency response. To get G(jω), replace s by jω in the transfer function G(s). The complex number G(jω) carries both numbers: its modulus is the gain, its argument is the phase.

G(jω) = G(s) with s = jω

G(s) is the transfer function; j = √(−1); ω is angular frequency in rad/s.

yss(t) = A |G(jω)| sin(ωt + ∠G(jω))

A is input amplitude in input units; t is time in s; |G(jω)| is the gain, output amplitude divided by input amplitude (dimensionless when input and output share the same units). ∠G(jω) is phase in degrees or radians.

  1. Write G(s) as a ratio of polynomials in s.
  2. Replace every s by jω.
  3. Find |G(jω)| = √(real² + imaginary²).
  4. Find ∠G(jω) = tan⁻¹(imaginary/real), with the correct quadrant.
  5. Multiply the input amplitude by |G(jω)| and add ∠G(jω) to the phase.

Reading the frequency-domain numbers

The magnitude curve of a low-pass system usually starts at its DC value, may rise to a peak, then falls. The peak height is the resonant peak Mr; the frequency where it occurs is the resonant frequency ωr. The bandwidth ωb is the frequency at which the magnitude has fallen to 1/√2 of its zero-frequency value, a drop of 3 dB. The cut-off frequency is that same boundary. For an open-loop transfer function, gain crossover ωgc is where |G(jω)| = 1 (0 dB), and phase crossover ωpc is where ∠G(jω) = −180°. These two crossover frequencies are the raw data for gain and phase margins in 4.6.

  • Mr: maximum magnitude, dimensionless.
  • ωr: frequency of maximum magnitude, rad/s.
  • ωb: bandwidth where magnitude is 1/√2 of DC magnitude, rad/s.
  • ωgc: open-loop gain crossover, |G(jωgc)| = 1.
  • ωpc: open-loop phase crossover, ∠G(jωpc) = −180°.

Standard second-order system

Take the standard second-order low-pass model G(s) = ωn² / (s² + 2ζωns + ωn²). Put s = jω and divide top and bottom by ωn². With r = ω/ωn, the denominator becomes (1 − r²) + j2ζr. The magnitude and phase follow directly.

M = |G(jω)| = 1/√((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²)

r = ω/ωn is normalized frequency; ζ is damping ratio; M is dimensionless.

φ = ∠G(jω) = −tan⁻¹(2ζr/(1 − r²)) for r < 1

φ is phase in degrees or radians. For r > 1 use the quadrant of 1 − r², or atan2(2ζr, 1 − r²).

Differentiate M² with respect to r and set the derivative to zero. A peak exists only when ζ < 1/√2 ≈ 0.707. Then r = √(1 − 2ζ²), which gives the resonant frequency and peak. Bandwidth comes from setting M = 1/√2 and solving for r².

ω<sub>r</sub> = ω<sub>n</sub>√(1 − 2ζ²),  M<sub>r</sub> = 1/(2ζ√(1 − ζ²))

valid for 0 ≤ ζ < 1/√2 (about 0.707); r = ω/ωn is dimensionless; ωr is in rad/s; Mr is dimensionless. For ζ ≥ 1/√2 there is no resonant peak.

ω<sub>b</sub> = ω<sub>n</sub>√((1 − 2ζ²) + √((1 − 2ζ²)² + 1))

ωb is the bandwidth in rad/s for a system with unity DC gain.

A larger Mr means a more oscillatory closed-loop response; a wider bandwidth means a faster response. 4.2 makes this link exact for the second-order case.

Worked example

Question. A second-order system has transfer function G(s) = 100/(s² + 6s + 100). Find ζ, ωn, Mr, ωr, ωb, and the steady-state output for input r(t) = 2 sin(9t) applied at the plant input.

  1. Compare the denominator with s² + 2ζωn s + ωn². ωn² = 100, so ωn = 10 rad/s. Also 2ζωn = 6, so ζ = 6/(2 × 10) = 0.3.
  2. Use Mr = 1/(2ζ√(1 − ζ²)). 1 − ζ² = 1 − 0.09 = 0.91. √0.91 = 0.9539. 2ζ√(1 − ζ²) = 2 × 0.3 × 0.9539 = 0.5724. Mr = 1/0.5724 = 1.747 ≈ 1.75.
  3. Use ωr = ωn√(1 − 2ζ²). 1 − 2ζ² = 1 − 0.18 = 0.82. √0.82 = 0.9055. ωr = 10 × 0.9055 = 9.055 rad/s ≈ 9.06 rad/s.
  4. Use the bandwidth formula. 1 − 2ζ² = 0.82. (1 − 2ζ²)² + 1 = 0.82² + 1 = 1.6724. √1.6724 = 1.2932. Sum = 0.82 + 1.2932 = 2.1132. √2.1132 = 1.4537. ωb = 10 × 1.4537 = 14.537 rad/s ≈ 14.54 rad/s.
  5. At ω = 9 rad/s, r = ω/ωn = 9/10 = 0.9. 1 − r² = 1 − 0.81 = 0.19. 2ζr = 2 × 0.3 × 0.9 = 0.54. M = 1/√(0.19² + 0.54²) = 1/√(0.0361 + 0.2916) = 1/√0.3277 = 1/0.5725 = 1.747.
  6. M at the test frequency happens to be close to Mr because 9 rad/s is near ωr = 9.06 rad/s; at any other frequency M would be smaller.
  7. Phase = −tan⁻¹(0.54/0.19) = −tan⁻¹(2.842) = −70.6°. Output amplitude = A M = 2 × 1.747 = 3.494 ≈ 3.49.

Answer. ωn = 10 rad/s, ζ = 0.3, Mr = 1.75, ωr = 9.06 rad/s, ωb = 14.54 rad/s; the steady-state output is 3.49 sin(9t − 70.6°).

Exam traps

  • Frequency response is the steady-state response, not the transient response. The sine output is valid only after the start-up transient has decayed.
  • Mr and ωr formulas exist only when ζ < 1/√2. For ζ = 0.8 there is no resonant peak, so do not substitute into the formula.
  • Do not confuse ωr with ωn. In the worked example ωn = 10 rad/s but ωr = 9.06 rad/s.
  • Bandwidth is measured from the zero-frequency magnitude, not from the resonant peak. For unity DC gain, ωb is where the magnitude is 0.707 of DC gain.
  • Gain crossover is where |G(jω)| = 1; phase crossover is where ∠G(jω) = −180°. Swapping these two is a common exam slip.

4.2 Correlation between time and frequency response

A second-order system has two sets of numbers that describe the same behaviour. In the frequency domain we measure the resonant peak Mr and the bandwidth ωb. In the time domain we measure overshoot Mp, peak time tp and settling time ts. For an exact second-order model, formulas connect them: damping ratio ζ sets the peak and overshoot; natural frequency ωn sets the width and speed. This link lets you read a Bode or polar plot and predict the step response, or set a frequency specification from a time specification. It is used in servo tuning, drive design and filter specification.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for Mr, ωr and bandwidth, and Unit 2 for the time-domain specifications Mp, tp and ts.

The exact second-order bridge

For the standard second-order closed-loop transfer function T(s)=ωn2/(s2+2ζωns+ωn2), both the frequency and time responses are fixed by ζ and ωn. The resonant peak is the largest value of |T(jω)|. It exists only when ζ<0.707; at larger ζ the magnitude falls steadily. The resonant frequency is where that peak occurs.

Mr = 1 / (2ζ√(1 − ζ2))

Mr is the peak resonance magnitude (no unit); ζ is the damping ratio (no unit), with 0 < ζ < 0.707 for a real peak.

ω<sub>r</sub> = ω<sub>n</sub>√(1 − 2ζ<sup>2</sup>)

ωr is the resonant frequency in rad/s; ωn is the undamped natural frequency in rad/s; ζ is the damping ratio.

Bandwidth ωb is the frequency where |T(jω)| falls to 1/√2 of its zero-frequency value, about 0.707. For the same T(s) it is:

ω<sub>b</sub> = ω<sub>n</sub> √( (1 − 2ζ<sup>2</sup>) + √( (1 − 2ζ<sup>2</sup>)<sup>2</sup> + 1 ) )

ωb is the bandwidth in rad/s; ωn is the natural frequency in rad/s; ζ is the damping ratio.

The time-domain formulas come from the underdamped step response, 0 < ζ < 1.

M<sub>p</sub> = 100 exp(−πζ / √(1 − ζ<sup>2</sup>))

Mp is the percentage overshoot in percent; ζ is the damping ratio; exp is the exponential function.

t<sub>p</sub> = π / (ω<sub>n</sub>√(1 − ζ<sup>2</sup>))

tp is the peak time in seconds; ωn is the natural frequency in rad/s; ζ is the damping ratio.

ts ≈ 4 / (ζωn)

ts is the 2% settling time in seconds; ζ is the damping ratio; ωn is the natural frequency in rad/s.

At ζ=0.4 and ωn=5 rad/s these give Mr=1.36, ωr=4.12 rad/s, ωb=6.87 rad/s, Mp=25.4%, tp=0.686 s and ts=2 s. The figure maps the same numbers.

How the numbers move together

The trend is monotonic. As ζ falls, the resonant peak grows and the step response overshoots more. As ωn rises, the bandwidth grows and the response becomes faster; Mr and percentage overshoot stay the same because they depend only on ζ. A small table for ωn=5 rad/s shows the pattern.

For small ζ, √(1−ζ²)≈1, so Mr≈1/(2ζ). This is a quick estimate, not the exact value. The same graph shape gives a rough phase-margin rule: PM≈100ζ degrees. At ζ=0.4 this predicts PM≈40°. Use it as a first check only; read the exact PM from the Bode plot in Chapter 4.6.

Where the bridge holds and where it breaks

The formulas above are exact only for the standard second-order model with no zeros. A higher-order system has extra poles and zeros. Its response can be estimated from the dominant pole pair: the complex pair closest to the imaginary axis with the smallest ζ. That estimate is only a first look. A slow real pole adds delay and increases settling time; a zero near the dominant pair can increase overshoot even when ζ looks healthy. Do not apply ts=4/(ζωn) to a third-order system without checking.

The peak formula also has a boundary. For ζ>0.707 there is no resonant peak and ωr is not defined; at ζ=0.707 the response is maximally flat, with ωr=0 and Mr=1. Treat ζ≥0.707 as no useful resonance. Reporting a larger Mr from the formula in that range is wrong. For a higher-order system, compute the exact closed-loop frequency response and compare it with the step response; Chapter 4.7 shows the M-circle and Nichols-chart method. If the dominant-pole estimate and the exact response disagree, trust the exact response.

Worked example

Question. A unity-feedback second-order system has the closed-loop transfer function T(s)=25/(s²+4s+25). Find ζ, ωn, Mr, ωr, bandwidth, percentage overshoot, peak time and 2% settling time.

  1. Compare the denominator with s²+2ζωn s+ωn². ωn²=25, so ωn=5 rad/s. 2ζωn=4, so ζ=4/(2×5)=0.4.
  2. Mr=1/(2ζ√(1−ζ²)). ζ²=0.16; 1−ζ²=0.84; √0.84=0.9165. Denominator=2×0.4×0.9165=0.7332. Mr=1/0.7332=1.3639≈1.36. The peak exists because ζ<0.707.
  3. ωr=ωn√(1−2ζ²)=5√(1−0.32)=5√0.68=5×0.8246=4.123≈4.12 rad/s.
  4. For bandwidth, 1−2ζ²=0.68. (1−2ζ²)²+1=0.4624+1=1.4624. √1.4624=1.2093. Sum=0.68+1.2093=1.8893. √1.8893=1.3745. ωb=5×1.3745=6.872≈6.87 rad/s.
  5. Mp=100 exp(−πζ/√(1−ζ²))=100 exp(−π×0.4/0.9165)=100 exp(−1.3711)=100×0.2539=25.39≈25.4%.
  6. tp=π/(ωn√(1−ζ²))=3.1416/(5×0.9165)=3.1416/4.5826=0.6856≈0.686 s.
  7. ts≈4/(ζωn)=4/(0.4×5)=4/2=2 s.

Answer. Mr ≈ 1.36, ωr ≈ 4.12 rad/s, ωb ≈ 6.87 rad/s, percentage overshoot ≈ 25.4%, tp ≈ 0.686 s, ts ≈ 2 s.

Exam traps

  • Using Mr=1/(2ζ√(1−ζ²)) when ζ≥0.707. For ζ>0.707 there is no resonant peak and ωr is not defined; at ζ=0.707 the response is maximally flat, ωr=0 and Mr=1. Treat ζ≥0.707 as no useful resonance.
  • Confusing ωr with ωb. For an underdamped second-order system, ωr<ωb; at ζ=0.4, ωr=4.12 rad/s and ωb=6.87 rad/s.
  • Applying ts≈4/(ζωn) to a higher-order system with a slow pole or a nearby zero. Check the dominant-pole assumption first, then use the exact response.
  • Forgetting that Mp is a percentage. The formula already multiplies by 100; writing Mp=0.254 means a fraction, not 25.4%.

4.3 Bode plots

A Bode plot draws the open-loop frequency response G(jω) as two graphs: gain in decibels and phase in degrees, both against a logarithmic frequency axis. It exists to turn multiplication of complex factors into addition of straight-line segments. Engineers use it to sketch a transfer function by hand, to see which corner frequencies dominate, and to read closed-loop stability from the 0 dB and −180° crossings. The same curves guide loop shaping in motor drives, power supplies and process loops.

You need: 4.1 Frequency response, the transfer function from Unit 1, and basic logarithms and decibels.

Gain in decibels and the log frequency axis

A Bode plot shows open-loop G(jω) as two curves: magnitude and phase, both against frequency on a log axis. Magnitude is in decibels: |G|dB = 20 log10 |G(jω)|. Phase is ∠G(jω) in degrees. The frequency axis uses log10(ω). Equal distances mean equal ratios; one decade is ×10 in frequency. This matters because a simple pole (1+jωT)−1 changes gain by −20 dB/decade after ω=1/T. Multiplication of magnitudes becomes addition of dB values: 20 log10|G1G2| = 20 log10|G1| + 20 log10|G2|. On a log frequency axis the straight-line asymptotes of each factor add. One pole at the origin gives −20 dB/decade and −90°; one zero at the origin gives +20 dB/decade and +90°. Use this for hand sketches and for reading loop gain on a servo or power-supply loop.

Standard factors and their asymptotes

Each factor in G(jω) has a low-frequency asymptote and a corner. Here T is a time constant in seconds, ωn is a natural frequency in rad/s, ζ is dimensionless damping ratio, and K is the constant gain.

Sketching rules and reading stability

  1. Write G(s) in time-constant form, with factors (1+sT) and quadratic factors.
  2. Find the low-frequency gain: put s→0. If there are n poles at the origin, start with slope −20n dB/decade; m zeros at the origin give +20m.
  3. Mark every corner: 1/T for a simple factor, ωn for a quadratic factor.
  4. Draw the asymptote from left to right. At each corner add slope: +20 for a simple zero, −20 for a simple pole, +40 for a quadratic zero, −40 for a quadratic pole.
  5. Draw phase by adding each factor’s phase; the phase curve is smooth, not a step.
  6. Read stability for an open-loop stable system: find gain crossover ωgc where |G|dB=0 and phase crossover ωpc where ∠G=−180°. Closed-loop stability needs the 0 dB crossing to occur before phase reaches −180°, or gain below 0 dB at ωpc. Margins are quantified in 4.6.

From asymptotes to transfer function: the low-frequency line gives K/sn; each slope change gives a factor. A −20 dB/decade change at ωc is a simple pole with T=1/ωc; +20 is a simple zero; ±40 is a quadratic pair. Some syllabi call this the Bode plot approach. Polar plots in 4.4 and Nyquist plots in 4.5 show the same data in one complex-plane curve.

Worked example

Question. The open-loop transfer function of a unity-feedback system is G(s)=100/((s+1)(s+10)). (a) Sketch its asymptotic Bode magnitude plot, marking low-frequency gain, corner frequencies and slopes. (b) Find the exact gain crossover frequency and the phase there.

  1. Write G(s) in time-constant form: G(s)=100/((s+1)(s+10))=10/((1+s)(1+s/10)).
  2. Low-frequency gain: put s=0. G(0)=10. In dB: 20 log10(10)=20 dB.
  3. Corners: first factor (1+s) has corner ω=1 rad/s. Second factor (1+s/10) has T2=0.1 s, so corner ω=1/T2=10 rad/s.
  4. Slopes: below 1 rad/s, 0 dB/decade. Between 1 and 10 rad/s, one simple pole adds −20 dB/decade, so slope is −20 dB/decade. Above 10 rad/s, the second simple pole adds another −20 dB/decade, so slope is −40 dB/decade. The asymptote passes 20 dB at ω=1, 0 dB at ω=10, and −40 dB at ω=100 rad/s.
  5. Exact gain crossover: set |G(jω)|=1. |G(jω)|=100/(√(1+ω²)√(100+ω²)). Square: (1+ω²)(100+ω²)=10000. Let x=ω². Then x²+101x+100=10000, so x²+101x−9900=0. x=(−101+√(101²+4×9900))/2=(−101+√49801)/2=61.08. ωgc=√61.08=7.82 rad/s.
  6. Phase at ωgc: ∠G(jωgc)=−tan−1(ωgc)−tan−1(ωgc/10)=−82.7°−38.0°=−120.7°.

Answer. Low-frequency gain 20 dB; corners at 1 and 10 rad/s (T2=0.1 s); slopes 0, −20 and −40 dB/decade; exact gain crossover ωgc=7.82 rad/s; phase there −120.7°.

Exam traps

  • Magnitude in dB uses 20 log10, not 10 log10. The 10 log10 form is for power.
  • The corner of (1+jωT) is at ω=1/T, not at T. Slopes are per decade, not per octave; −20 dB/decade is about −6 dB/octave.
  • An asymptotic 0 dB crossing is only an estimate. A nearby corner lowers or raises the exact curve, so the exact gain crossover can move; solve |G(jω)|=1 when asked.
  • The initial slope comes from poles or zeros at the origin: n poles give −20n dB/decade, m zeros give +20m dB/decade. Constant K shifts the curve vertically and does not change slope.
  • At a simple-pole corner the phase is −45°, not −90°. The phase reaches −90° only as ω becomes much larger than the corner.

4.4 Polar plots

A polar plot draws the complex number G(jω) as a vector whose tip moves while ω runs from 0 to ∞. The distance from the origin is the gain |G(jω)|; the angle is the phase ∠G(jω). One curve therefore shows both quantities that a Bode plot separates. Engineers use it to see quickly how an open-loop system starts, how it ends, where it crosses the real axis, and how close it comes to the critical point −1+j0. Some syllabi call it the frequency locus.

You need 4.1 Frequency response and frequency-domain specifications and complex numbers.

The frequency locus

Put s=jω in the open-loop transfer function G(s). The result G(jω) is a complex number for each frequency ω. Write it as G(jω)=X(ω)+jY(ω). X(ω) is the real part and Y(ω) is the imaginary part, both in the same units as G. The polar plot is the curve traced by the tip of the vector from the origin to X(ω)+jY(ω) while ω runs from 0 to ∞. Draw an arrow on the curve to show increasing ω.

G(jω) = X(ω) + jY(ω),   |G(jω)| = √(X² + Y²),   ∠G(jω) = tan⁻¹(Y/X)

X(ω) is the real part, Y(ω) is the imaginary part, |G(jω)| is the gain at frequency ω in rad/s, and ∠G(jω) is the phase angle in degrees or radians.

Each point carries two numbers. The distance from the origin is the magnitude |G(jω)|. The angle from the positive real axis, measured anticlockwise, is the phase ∠G(jω)=tan⁻¹(Y/X), with the quadrant chosen from the signs of X and Y. So one polar plot shows gain and phase together. A Bode plot separates them; a polar plot does not.

To sketch by hand, make a small table of ω values. For each ω compute X and Y, plot the point, and join the points smoothly. Always mark the start (ω→0⁺) and end (ω→∞); these two points identify the system type quickly.

Start and end points for Type 0, 1 and 2

Type means the number of poles of G(s) at the origin s=0. A pole at the origin is an integrator. In G(jω), a factor 1/(jω) has magnitude 1/ω and phase −90°. At ω→0⁺, 1/ω→∞. Each such pole adds −90° and makes the starting magnitude infinite.

For a proper system, the magnitude at ω→∞ is 0. The end phase is (m−n)×90°, where n is the total number of poles and m is the number of zeros. Angles that differ by 360° are the same direction. For no zeros, the end phase is −n×90°. For G(s)=1/(s(s+1)(s+2)), Type 1 starts at infinite magnitude with −90° phase and ends at the origin with +90° phase (same as −270°).

Crossings and what the plot tells you

Two crossings are easy to compute and often asked. A real-axis crossing has Im G(jω)=0. Solve that for positive ω. An imaginary-axis crossing has Re G(jω)=0. Keep only ω>0; the point ω=0 is the start.

G(jω) = 1/(a+jb) = (a − jb)/(a² + b²),   Im G(jω) = −b/(a² + b²)

a and b are the real and imaginary parts of the denominator at frequency ω; setting Im G = 0 means b = 0.

The real-axis crossing is important because the critical point is −1+j0. The distance from the curve to −1 measures how close the closed loop is to instability; gain margin and phase margin make this precise (see 4.6). For the example, Im G=0 gives ω=√2 rad/s and G=−1/6, so the curve crosses the negative real axis between 0 and −1.

Read the plot as a map of gain and phase. At low ω, the direction shows the number of integrators. At high ω, the approach to the origin shows relative degree. Near the negative real axis, the plot is close to the dangerous direction. Full encirclement and the Nyquist criterion are treated in 4.5.

Worked example

Question. Sketch the polar plot of G(s)=1/(s(s+1)(s+2)). Find its start as ω→0⁺, its end as ω→∞, and its real-axis crossing. Give the crossing frequency and the value of G there.

Exam traps

  • Use ω from 0 to ∞ only. Negative frequencies appear in the Nyquist plot, not in the standard polar plot.

  • When solving Im G=0, use G=1/(a+jb)=(a−jb)/(a²+b²); the imaginary part is −b/(a²+b²), not +b.
  • Type is not the same as order. Type counts only poles at the origin; order counts all poles of G(s).
  • Phase can be written as +90° or −270°. The direction is the same; do not call it a different curve.

4.5 Nyquist plot and Nyquist stability criterion

The Nyquist plot is the polar plot of G(jω)H(jω) closed over a contour that wraps the right half of the s-plane. Its purpose is to count closed-loop right-half-plane poles without solving the characteristic equation. The count is an encirclement count around −1. Engineers use it to test stability when a parameter, often gain K, varies, and to see how close a design is to instability. It handles open-loop poles on the imaginary axis with small detours. See polar plots for the ω≥0 curve this chapter closes.

You need: 4.4 polar plots, the characteristic equation and Routh-Hurwitz from Unit 3, and complex mapping of a contour.

The contour and the count

The Nyquist contour is a closed path in the s-plane. Start at s=−j∞ on the imaginary axis, go up the jω axis to s=+j∞, then follow a large semicircle of radius R→∞ through the right half-plane back to −j∞. It encloses the entire right half-plane. If G(s)H(s) has a pole on the jω axis, push a small semicircle of radius r→0 into the right half-plane around that pole, so the contour does not pass through the pole.

Mapping every point of this contour through G(s)H(s) gives the Nyquist plot. The open-loop transfer function is G(s)H(s); s=σ+jω is the complex frequency; the critical point is −1+j0. The plot is not just the usual ω≥0 curve: it includes the negative-frequency mirror and the large semicircle image.

Z = N + P

N is the number of clockwise encirclements of −1 by the Nyquist plot; P is the number of open-loop poles of G(s)H(s) in the right half-plane; Z is the number of closed-loop poles in the right half-plane. Closed-loop stability needs Z=0, so N=−P. If N is counted counterclockwise, the formula becomes Z=P−N; choose one convention and keep it.

  1. Write G(s)H(s) and find all open-loop poles. Count P.
  2. Mark any pole on the jω axis and add the small detour.
  3. Sketch or compute G(jω)H(jω) for ω from 0 to ∞, then mirror for negative ω. Add the image of the large semicircle.
  4. Count clockwise encirclements of −1. Call it N.
  5. Compute Z=N+P. Stable if Z=0.

Encirclement means the plot makes a full loop around −1, not merely passes near it. A plot that crosses the negative real axis left of −1 and returns on the other side can enclose the point.

Type 0, Type 1, Type 2 plots and gain

The system type is the number of open-loop poles at s=0. A Type 0 system has none. Its Nyquist plot starts at a finite point on the real axis for ω=0 and ends at the origin. A Type 1 system has one pole at s=0. The small detour around the origin sends the plot to a large semicircle at infinity; the ω=0+ branch comes in from infinity along an asymptote. A Type 2 system has two poles at s=0; its small detour produces an even larger sweep at infinity. These infinities are the images of the detour, not values of G(jω) at ω=0.

Increasing gain K multiplies every point of the plot by K. The shape does not change; the plot expands from the origin. For a stable open-loop system (P=0), the closed-loop system is stable while the plot does not enclose −1. The critical gain is the K that makes the plot pass exactly through −1. Below it, N=0 and Z=0. Above it, the plot encloses −1 and Z>0: closed-loop poles move into the right half-plane. For the example below, K=6 is critical; above it two closed-loop poles enter the right half-plane.

Relative stability is the distance from the plot to −1. A plot that passes far to the right of −1 has room for extra gain; a plot that nearly touches −1 is close to instability. Gain margin and phase margin measure this distance, and are covered in 4.6.

Some syllabi call the result the Nyquist stability criterion. The count is exact; the sketch only helps you count.

Worked example

Question. For G(s)H(s)=K/[s(s+1)(s+2)] with K>0, use the Nyquist criterion to find the range of K for closed-loop stability.

  1. Open-loop poles: s=0, s=−1, s=−2. None lie in the right half-plane, so P=0. The pole at s=0 needs a small detour.
  2. Put s=jω: G(jω)H(jω)=K/[jω(1+jω)(2+jω)].
  3. Multiply the denominator: jω(1+jω)(2+jω)=−3ω²+j(2ω−ω³).
  4. The plot crosses the negative real axis when the imaginary part of the denominator is zero: 2ω−ω³=ω(2−ω²)=0. For ω>0, ω=√2≈1.414 rad/s.
  5. At ω=√2 rad/s, the real part of the denominator is −3(√2)²=−6. So G(j√2)H(j√2)=K/(−6)=−K/6.
  6. Set the crossing equal to the critical point: −K/6=−1 gives K=6. For K<6 the crossing is to the right of −1, so N=0 and Z=N+P=0. For K>6 the crossing is left of −1, N=2 clockwise, and Z=2. K=6 puts the plot exactly through −1, which is marginal.
  7. Stability requires Z=0, so 0<K<6.

Answer. Closed-loop stability holds for 0<K<6; K=6 is marginal, and K>6 is unstable.

Exam traps

  • Sign convention: if N is clockwise, use Z=N+P. If N is counterclockwise, use Z=P−N. Mixing the two flips the answer.
  • P counts open-loop right-half-plane poles of G(s)H(s), not closed-loop poles. Z is the closed-loop count you want.
  • A pole on the jω axis needs a small detour. Without it, the plot is wrong and the encirclement count can change.
  • A point exactly at −1 is marginal, not stable. Treat it as the boundary of stability, not as an encirclement.
  • If you use only the ω≥0 polar plot, add its mirror image for negative frequencies before counting encirclements.

4.6 Gain margin and phase margin

Gain margin and phase margin answer one practical question: how much can the loop gain rise, or how much extra phase lag can appear, before the closed loop becomes unstable? They are read from the open-loop frequency response, not from the closed-loop response. The idea is used in motor drives, power supplies, process loops and anywhere a controller must survive gain changes and delay. Both margins measure the distance of the open-loop plot from the critical point −1 + j0.

You need 4.3 Bode plots for logarithmic axes and 4.5 Nyquist plot and Nyquist stability criterion for encirclements, plus the stability ideas of Unit 3.

Crossover frequencies and the two margins

The open-loop frequency response G(jω)H(jω) is a complex number at each angular frequency ω in rad/s. The critical point is −1 + j0. If this point lies on the open-loop locus, the closed-loop characteristic equation 1 + G(jω)H(jω) = 0 has a root on the jω-axis, so the closed loop has a pole on the jω-axis. The closed loop is marginally stable only when all its other closed-loop poles are in the left half-plane. The two margins measure how far the locus stays from that point.

The gain crossover frequency ωgc is the frequency where the open-loop magnitude is one, that is |G(jωgc)H(jωgc)| = 1, or 0 dB. The phase crossover frequency ωpc is the frequency where the open-loop phase is −180°, that is ∠G(jωpc)H(jωpc) = −180°. A system may have more than one crossover; use the one that gives the smallest margin for design.

GM = −20 log₁₀ |G(jω<sub>pc</sub>)H(jω<sub>pc</sub>)| dB

ωpc is the phase crossover frequency in rad/s, where ∠G(jωpc)H(jωpc) = −180°; |·| is magnitude; GM is in dB.

PM = 180° + ∠G(jω<sub>gc</sub>)H(jω<sub>gc</sub>)

ωgc is the gain crossover frequency in rad/s, where |G(jωgc)H(jωgc)| = 1 (0 dB); PM is in degrees.

GM is the extra gain in dB that can be added at the phase crossover before the loop magnitude becomes one. If |G(jωpc)H(jωpc)| = 0.5, then GM = −20 log₁₀ 0.5 = 6.02 dB, so the gain can rise by a factor 106.02/20 = 2 before marginal stability. PM is the extra phase lag in degrees that can be added at the gain crossover before the phase reaches −180°. If the phase at ωgc is −150°, PM = 30°.

Reading margins from plots

On a Bode plot, read GM at the phase crossover and PM at the gain crossover. The order matters. The steps below use the magnitude curve in dB and the phase curve in degrees.

  1. Draw the 0 dB line on the magnitude plot. Find every frequency where the magnitude curve crosses 0 dB; take the relevant one as ωgc.
  2. Draw the −180° line on the phase plot. Find every frequency where the phase curve crosses −180°; take the relevant one as ωpc.
  3. At ωpc, read the magnitude in dB. GM is the negative of this value: GM = −Mpc, where Mpc = 20 log₁₀|G(jωpc)H(jωpc)| dB. A magnitude below 0 dB gives positive GM.
  4. At ωgc, read the phase in degrees. PM is the phase value plus 180°. A phase above −180° gives positive PM.
  5. On a Nyquist plot, let the negative real-axis crossing be at −a, where a>0. For a stable open loop, GM = 20 log₁₀(1/a) dB and the allowed gain factor is 1/a. PM is the angle from the negative real axis to the point where the locus crosses the unit circle: with phase φ at that crossing, PM = 180° + φ. The same sign rules apply.

For a stable minimum-phase open-loop system, positive GM and positive PM mean the closed loop is stable. Zero means marginal. Negative means unstable. If the open-loop system is itself unstable, these sign rules do not apply; count encirclements of −1 as in 4.5.

GM can be infinite. This happens when the phase never reaches −180° at any finite frequency, so there is no phase crossover. If a 0 dB crossing exists, PM is read there; otherwise PM is undefined even though GM may be infinite. The Nichols chart in 4.7 shows the same two distances on one grid.

What the numbers predict

PM is the better quick indicator of damping. A small PM means the closed-loop poles are close to the jω-axis, so a step input gives a large overshoot and a ringing response. The link between PM and overshoot is approximate; for a second-order system, the damping ratio and PM are related, and 4.2 gives the time-domain translation. GM protects against gain changes: ageing, temperature, a replaced sensor, or a higher plant gain can push the loop magnitude up. A common design target is PM of about 45° to 60° and GM of 6 dB or more, but the right values depend on the plant and the allowed overshoot.

The margins are properties of the open loop. They do not require solving for the closed-loop transfer function T(s). This is why they are used in tuning: measure or simulate G(jω)H(jω), read the two numbers, and adjust the controller. A loop with PM = 5° may still be stable in a simulation, but it will be sensitive to delay and gain error. A loop with large GM but small PM is not automatically safe: phase lag from a transport delay grows with frequency and can break the loop while the gain margin looks healthy.

4.7 Closed-loop frequency response: M and N circles, Nichols chart

Closed-loop frequency response tells how the finished feedback system responds to sine waves, but solving T(jω)=G(jω)/(1+G(jω)) at every frequency is slow. M and N circles draw constant closed-loop magnitude and phase directly in the open-loop plane. The Nichols chart moves the same contours to a gain-in-dB versus phase-in-degrees grid, where an open-loop frequency curve can be plotted point by point. Engineers use these charts to read resonant peak, resonant frequency and bandwidth without factoring the characteristic equation. Unity feedback is assumed here.

You need: 4.1 Frequency response, 4.3 Bode plots, 4.5 Nyquist plot, 4.6 Gain and phase margins, and complex arithmetic.

From open-loop G to closed-loop T

With unity feedback, the closed-loop transfer function is T(s)=G(s)/(1+G(s)). Put s=jω. Write G(jω)=X+jY, where X is the real part and Y is the imaginary part. The closed-loop magnitude is M=|T(jω)|. The closed-loop phase is φ=∠T(jω). M is a pure number; φ is in degrees or radians.

T(jω) = G(jω) / (1 + G(jω))

G(jω) is the open-loop frequency response; T(jω) is the closed-loop frequency response for unity feedback.

The characteristic equation is 1+G(s)=0. Near a frequency where G(jω) approaches −1, the denominator is small and M becomes large. That is why the point −1 controls both stability and resonance. Solving T at every ω means complex division at every frequency. The M and N circles avoid this: they are fixed contours drawn once in the G-plane. Any computed G(jω) point falls on one M contour and one N contour, so closed-loop magnitude and phase are read directly. The Nichols chart applies the same idea with different axes.

M circles and N circles

Set M=|T|. From M²=|G|²/|1+G|² one obtains the M-circle equation. For M≠1 it is a circle in the X-Y plane. Every point on it gives closed-loop magnitude M. For M=2 the centre is (−4/3,0) and the radius is 2/3. For M=4 the centre is (−16/15,0) and the radius is 4/15. Larger M gives a smaller circle closer to −1+j0.

(X + M²/(M² − 1))² + Y² = (M/(M² − 1))²

X=Re G(jω), Y=Im G(jω), M=|T(jω)|. Centre is (−M²/(M²−1), 0); radius is M/|M²−1|. Valid for M≠1.

For M=1 the denominator M²−1 is zero. The equation reduces to 2X+1=0, that is X=−1/2. This is a vertical straight line, not a circle.

The phase contours use N=tan φ, where φ=∠T. For G=X+jY, completing squares gives the N-circle equation. Each N circle is the locus of constant N=tan φ, that is constant closed-loop phase modulo 180°. One contour serves φ and φ+180°; the chart labels distinguish the actual phase branch.

(X + 1/2)² + (Y − 1/(2N))² = 1/4 + 1/(4N²)

N=tan φ; φ=∠T(jω). Centre is (−1/2, 1/(2N)); radius is √(1/4 + 1/(4N²)).

Nichols chart and reading closed-loop values

The Nichols chart plots open-loop gain 20log10|G(jω)| in dB on the vertical axis and open-loop phase ∠G(jω) in degrees on the horizontal axis. The M and N circles of the G-plane are redrawn as M and N contours on this grid. A point on an M contour has the same closed-loop magnitude M.

  1. Choose frequencies ω1, ω2, ... covering the range of interest.
  2. Compute G(jω)=X+jY at each frequency.
  3. Convert to gain 20log10√(X²+Y²) dB and phase atan2(Y,X) degrees.
  4. Plot each pair (phase, gain) and join the points in increasing ω.
  5. At each point, read the surrounding M and N contours.
  6. The largest M on the curve is the resonant peak Mr; the frequency there is ωr. The bandwidth ωb is the frequency where M falls to |T(0)|/√2, that is 3 dB below the low-frequency closed-loop gain.

Changing gain K shifts the curve vertically on a Nichols chart. The shape stays the same and the M readings change. This is how gain adjustment is judged on the chart.

Worked example

Question. For a unity-feedback system G(s)=50/[s(s+1)(s+10)], evaluate G(j2). Then find the closed-loop T(j2), its magnitude M in dB and closed-loop phase φ in degrees; also compute N=tan φ. State whether the point lies inside the M=2 circle or the M=4 circle.

  1. At ω=2 rad/s, s=j2. (1+j2)(10+j2)=6+j22. Multiplying by j2 gives j2(6+j22)=−44+j12. Hence G(j2)=50/(−44+j12).
  2. Multiply numerator and denominator by −44−j12: G(j2)=50(−44−j12)/(44²+12²)=(−2200−j600)/2080=−1.0577−j0.2885.
  3. 1+G(j2)=−0.0577−j0.2885. As exact fractions G=(−55−j15)/52, so 1+G=(−3−j15)/52.
  4. T=G/(1+G)=(−55−j15)/(−3−j15)=(55+j15)/(3+j15). Multiply by (3−j15): T=(390−j780)/234=1.6667−j3.3333.
  5. M=√(1.6667²+3.3333²)=3.7268. In dB, 20log10(3.7268)=11.43 dB.
  6. φ=atan2(−3.3333,1.6667)=−63.435°. Then N=tan φ=tan(−63.435°)=−2.000 (dimensionless).
  7. M=2 circle: centre (−1.333,0), radius 0.667. Distance from G(j2) to centre = √[(−1.058+1.333)²+(−0.289)²]=0.399<0.667, so inside. M=4 circle: centre (−1.067,0), radius 0.267. Distance = √[(−1.058+1.067)²+(−0.289)²]=0.289>0.267, so outside. Thus 2<M<4.

Answer. T(j2)=1.667−j3.333, M=3.727 (11.43 dB), φ=−63.435°, N=tan φ=−2.000; the point is inside the M=2 circle and outside the M=4 circle.

Exam traps

  • M circles live in the open-loop G-plane, not the closed-loop plane.
  • The critical point is −1. A point on the M=1 line has closed-loop magnitude 1, not open-loop magnitude 1.
  • Nichols chart axes are open-loop gain in dB and open-loop phase in degrees; do not plot closed-loop phase on the horizontal axis.
  • On an N circle, N is tan φ, not φ itself.
  • Bandwidth is read from closed-loop magnitude, not from the −3 dB point of the open-loop Bode gain.

Remember What is the M=1 locus in the G-plane? 2 marks

Understand Why are M circles drawn in the open-loop plane rather than the closed-loop plane? 3 marks

The open-loop G(jω) is known or measured. T is then obtained from T=G/(1+G). Drawing M circles in the G-plane lets us read |T| directly from the known G point without computing T at every frequency.

Apply A point has G=−1+j0.6. Is it inside the M=2 circle? 4 marks

M=2 circle: centre (−1.333,0), radius 0.667. Distance from (−1,0.6) to centre is √(0.333²+0.6²)=0.686. This is greater than 0.667, so the point is outside the M=2 circle.

Apply On a Nichols chart, an open-loop point has gain 6 dB and phase −120°. The M contour through it reads 1.4. If this is the highest M on the curve, find Mr and state whether a resonant peak exists. 4 marks

Mr=1.4. A resonant peak exists because Mr>1.

Analyze Explain why increasing open-loop gain shifts a Nichols curve upward and can increase Mr. 5 marks

Adding gain adds a constant dB to every point, so the whole curve moves up. In the G-plane this moves the curve closer to −1. The curve can enter higher-M contours, so the largest M on the curve, Mr, rises. Too much gain makes the curve pass near or around −1 and the system becomes unstable.

Analyze A G(jω) curve lies entirely outside the M=2 circle. Can the closed-loop system have Mr=2.5? Explain. 6 marks

No. Outside the M=2 circle in that region, closed-loop magnitude is less than 2. If the whole curve stays outside, every point has M<2, so Mr<2. Mr=2.5 would require some part of the curve to lie inside the M=2 circle.

Takeaways

  1. A stable linear system fed a sine wave returns a sine wave of the same frequency, changed only in amplitude by |G(jω)| and in phase by ∠G(jω).
  2. Frequency-domain numbers are not decoration: Mr and ωb of a second-order system fix overshoot and settling time, so a specification written in one domain can be met in the other.
  3. The Bode plot is popular because logarithms turn the multiplication of factors in G(jω) into addition of straight-line segments that can be sketched by hand.
  4. A polar plot shows magnitude and phase together as one curve, which is compact but loses the clear frequency scale that a Bode plot keeps.
  5. The Nyquist criterion counts encirclements of −1 and settles closed-loop stability even when the open-loop transfer function itself is unstable.
  6. Gain margin and phase margin are distances from the critical point −1, so they measure relative stability, not just yes-or-no stability.
  7. M circles, N circles and the Nichols chart give the closed-loop frequency response straight from the open-loop curve without computing T(s).
  8. Only a few universities go deep into Nichols chart reading; almost all syllabi insist on Bode sketching, polar sketching and the Nyquist criterion.

Glossary

Absolute stability
Whether the system is stable or unstable at all, without reference to how stable.
Asymptote
The straight line that a Bode segment approaches at very low or very high frequency.
Bandwidth
The range of frequencies over which the magnitude stays within 1/√2 of its low-frequency value.
Conditionally stable system
A system that is stable for a middle range of gain but unstable for both very small and very large gain.
Corner frequency
The frequency 1/T at which a first-order factor begins to change the Bode slope.
Critical point
The point −1 + j0 in the open-loop plane, whose encirclement decides closed-loop stability.
Cut-off frequency
The upper frequency of the bandwidth, where the magnitude is −3 dB.
Decade
A factor of ten in frequency.
Decibel
Twenty times the base-10 logarithm of a magnitude ratio.
Encirclement
A full turn of a closed locus about a point, counted with sign by direction.
Frequency response
The complex ratio of output sine to input sine at each frequency, written G(jω).
Gain crossover frequency
The frequency at which the open-loop magnitude equals one.
Gain margin
The factor by which loop gain may rise before the closed loop becomes unstable, expressed in dB.
M circle
A locus of constant closed-loop magnitude drawn in the open-loop plane.
Minimum phase system
A system with no poles or zeros in the right half of the s-plane, so its gain and phase are uniquely linked.
Nichols chart
A gain-versus-phase grid with M and N contours for reading closed-loop response from open-loop data.
Nyquist path
The contour made of the jω axis and a large right-half-plane semicircle used in the Nyquist test.
Octave
A factor of two in frequency.
Phase crossover frequency
The frequency at which the open-loop phase equals minus 180 degrees.
Phase margin
The phase lag that may be added at gain crossover before the closed loop becomes unstable.
Polar plot
The path of G(jω) in the complex plane as frequency runs from zero to infinity.
Relative stability
How far a stable system is from the edge of instability, measured by gain and phase margins.
Resonant frequency
The frequency at which the magnitude curve reaches its peak.
Resonant peak
The maximum value of the closed-loop magnitude curve.
Sinusoidal steady state
The condition reached after transients die out, when every signal in a stable linear system is a sine wave of the input frequency.
