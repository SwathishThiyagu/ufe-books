5.1 On-off (two-position) control

An on-off controller has only two outputs: full on and full off. It compares output y with reference r, takes error e = r − y, and switches when e crosses a threshold. The output cannot settle at the set point; it overshoots, switches and cycles. This limit cycle is normal. Thermostats, fridge compressors, water-level pumps and irons use it because their actuator is a relay, contactor or valve. Only a few universities teach this in detail.

You need: Unit 1 closed-loop block diagrams and Unit 2 first-order response.

Ideal relay and hysteresis

Write e = r − y. r is set point, y is output, e is error. An ideal two-position controller gives u = Umax when e > 0 and u = Umin when e < 0. u is controller output; Umax is the upper level (heater on); Umin is the lower level (heater off). At e = 0 the output is held or undefined.

A real relay has hysteresis ±h. It switches on only when e > +h and off only when e < −h. h is in the units of the error, i.e. of the measured variable, e.g. °C. Full dead band is 2h. Hysteresis stops chatter from noise; a wider band gives fewer switches but a larger swing. The controller remembers its last state inside the band, so it is not a pure function of e.

u = U<sub>max</sub> if e > +h; u = U<sub>min</sub> if e < −h

e = r − y is error; h is half the hysteresis band; Umax and Umin are the two output levels.

Cycle time and duty cycle

For a first-order plant, let a be rise rate and b fall rate near the set point. a and b are rise and fall rates in units of the measured variable per minute, e.g. °C/min. The output travels 2h. On time ton = 2h/a. Off time toff = 2h/b. Cycle T = ton + toff. Duty D = ton/T.

D = t<sub>on</sub> / (t<sub>on</sub> + t<sub>off</sub>) × 100%

ton is on time; toff is off time; D is duty cycle in percent.

A tank heater with a = 0.5 °C/min, b = 0.2 °C/min and h = 1 °C gives ton = 4 min, toff = 10 min, T = 14 min, D = 28.6 %. Average power D×Umax + (1−D)×Umin is only an energy balance, not a linear gain.

A second-order plant can ring. Its limit cycle is built from linear response pieces between switches; amplitude and period depend on damping and h.

Relay model and where it is used

Model the ideal relay as u = Uavg + (Umax−Umin)/2 × sign(e), where Uavg = (Umax+Umin)/2. With Umin = 0, Uavg = Umax/2. The output jumps between two fixed levels at e = 0, so there is no finite slope (no gain) there; the output is bounded between Umin and Umax. The relay is nonlinear, so one transfer function cannot describe the whole loop.

Use on-off control with a contactor, solenoid valve, relay or thyristor switch. It is cheap and robust, but poor when cycling wears valves or tight control is needed. PWM makes the average continuous; PID is the next step (see 5.2). Relay auto-tuning uses the same limit cycle to estimate ultimate gain and period. Some syllabi call this two-position control.

Worked example

Question. A tank heater switches on at 60 °C and off at 62 °C. With the heater on the temperature rises at 0.5 °C/min; with the heater off it falls at 0.2 °C/min. Find the on time, off time, cycle time and duty cycle.

  1. Band = 62−60 = 2 °C.
  2. t_on = 2/0.5 = 4 min.
  3. t_off = 2/0.2 = 10 min.
  4. T = 4+10 = 14 min.
  5. D = 4/14×100 = 28.571…% ≈ 28.6%.

Answer. On time = 4 min, off time = 10 min, cycle time = 14 min, duty cycle = 28.6 %.

Exam traps

  • Ignoring hysteresis gives infinite switching; a real loop has a limit cycle, and average output is not a linear gain.
  • Sign: e = r − y; e > 0 means y is below set point, so a heater switches on. Swapping the sign reverses the action.
  • Hysteresis width is 2h, not h. Using h halves the calculated on and off times.
  • Duty cycle is t_on/T, not the ratio Umax/Umin.
  • On-off has no proportional gain; do not use a fixed K for stability checks.

5.2 P, PI, PD and PID controllers

A PID controller turns the error between set point and output into a control signal using three parallel actions: proportional, integral and derivative. Proportional action reacts to the present error, integral action removes offset, and derivative action responds to the error's slope. The same three-term structure runs motor drives, furnace temperature loops, drone attitude control and flow control in process plants. This chapter writes the parallel PID transfer function, defines each gain with its unit, and shows how P, PI, PD and PID change first-order and second-order responses. Tuning rules follow in 5.3.

You need Unit 2 steady-state error and second-order damping, plus the Routh test from Unit 3; tuning rules are in 5.3.

Three parallel branches

Let e(t)=r(t)−y(t) be the error, with r(t) the set point and y(t) the measured output. The parallel PID controller is defined by its transfer function:

C(s) = K<sub>p</sub> + K<sub>i</sub>/s + K<sub>d</sub>s

C(s) is the controller transfer function; Kp is the proportional gain (dimensionless in normalized units); Ki is the integral gain (s−1); Kd is the derivative gain (s); s is the Laplace variable.

In the time domain the control signal is u(t)=Kpe(t)+Ki∫0te(τ)dτ+Kdde/dt. The first term looks only at the present error. The second term adds area under the error, so a small remaining offset keeps pushing the control until the offset disappears. The third term uses the slope, so it pushes early when the error is changing quickly.

  • P: gain now. Larger Kp speeds the loop and reduces offset, but too large Kp causes overshoot.
  • I: memory of accumulated error. Removes step offset; adds phase lag and can slow or oscillate.
  • D: slope. Adds damping and speed, but amplifies sensor noise.

Effect on first- and second-order plants

For a first-order plant G(s)=K/(τs+1) with unity feedback and P control only, the closed-loop time constant becomes τ/(1+KKp). Define K as the plant gain (dimensionless here), τ as the open-loop time constant in seconds, and Kp as the proportional gain. A larger Kp gives a faster rise and smaller step error. The step error is 1/(1+KKp); it falls but never reaches zero. Adding I makes the loop type 1, so the step error becomes zero. The cost is a slower approach and possible overshoot.

For the second-order plant G(s)=ωn²/[s(s+2ζωn)] with unity feedback, proportional gain Kp gives the characteristic equation s²+2ζωns+Kpωn²=0. Here ζ is the damping ratio (dimensionless) and ωn is the natural frequency in rad/s. Compare with s²+2ζeffωn,effs+ωn,eff²=0. Then ωn,eff=ωn√Kp and ζeff=ζ/√Kp. So P raises speed but lowers damping. PD control C(s)=Kp+Kds adds a zero and changes the s coefficient to 2ζωn+Kdωn², so ζeff increases; PD adds damping. PID keeps the integral for zero offset and uses derivative for damping.

PI control raises the system type by one. A type 1 plant such as the one in the worked example becomes type 2, so a unit ramp gives zero steady-state error. A type 0 plant becomes type 1: step error becomes zero, but ramp error is finite. Some syllabi call PID the three-term controller.

Standard and series forms

The parallel form above is the easiest to add branch by branch. The standard form is C(s)=Kp(1+1/(Tis)+Tds). Here Ti=Kp/Ki is the integral time in seconds, and Td=Kd/Kp is the derivative time in seconds. Kp still sets the overall gain.

The series form, also called the interacting form, is C(s)=Kc(1+1/(Tis))(1+Tds). Kc is the series gain. The two forms give the same input-output behaviour only for particular gain combinations. Before replacing one controller with another, compare coefficients; do not set Kd=Td unless Kp=1.

Reading a loop: P sets speed, I removes offset, D adds damping. In frequency terms, I adds phase lag at low frequency, and D adds phase lead near the loop band. Real derivative action is noisy, so industrial PID adds a filter; real integral action needs anti-windup. Those practical limits are separate from the linear transfer function.

Worked example

Question. For G(s)=1/[s(s+2)] with unity feedback, (a) find Kp so the closed-loop damping ratio ζ=0.5; (b) with Kp=4 and PI integral gain Ki=1 s−1, find the steady-state error to a unit ramp.

  1. (a) With P control, the closed-loop characteristic equation is 1+KpG(s)=0. Put G(s)=1/[s(s+2)]: 1+Kp/[s(s+2)]=0, so s²+2s+Kp=0.
  2. Compare s²+2s+Kp=0 with s²+2ζωns+ωn²=0. The s coefficient gives 2ζωn=2, so ζωn=1. With ζ=0.5, ωn=1/0.5=2 rad/s.
  3. Then Kp=ωn²=2²=4. The proportional gain is dimensionless.
  4. (b) The PI controller is C(s)=Kp+Ki/s=4+1/s=(4s+1)/s. The open loop is L(s)=C(s)G(s)=(4s+1)/[s²(s+2)].
  5. L(s) has two poles at s=0, so the open loop is type 2. For a unit ramp R(s)=1/s², the velocity error constant is Kv=lims→0sL(s)=∞.
  6. Steady-state ramp error is ess=1/Kv=1/∞=0.

Answer. Kp=4, ωn=2 rad/s, and the unit-ramp steady-state error with Kp=4 and Ki=1 s−1 is 0.

Exam traps

  • Mixing parallel and series gains: in C(s)=Kp+Ki/s+Kds, Kd has unit seconds; in standard form Td=Kd/Kp also has unit seconds, but Kd=Td only when Kp=1.
  • Derivative action amplifies high-frequency sensor noise. A large Kd can saturate the actuator even when the true error is smooth.
  • Integral action winds up during actuator saturation: the error stays nonzero, the integral keeps growing, and the response overshoots when the actuator recovers.
  • PI raises system type by one. For a type 0 plant it removes step error but leaves a finite ramp error; it does not force ramp error to zero.
  • P alone leaves offset in a first-order loop. Increasing Kp reduces offset but never removes it, and too large Kp lowers damping and causes oscillation.

5.3 PID tuning methods

PID tuning is the step that turns three gains into a working controller. Trial and error is slow and unsafe on a real plant, so tuning rules measure one simple plant behaviour and compute Kp, Ti and Td. The best-known closed-loop rule is Ziegler-Nichols: raise proportional gain until the loop oscillates continuously, record that gain Ku and the period Pu, then read the settings from a table. It is used in process control, motor drives, temperature loops and any place where a plant can safely be pushed to its stability edge.

You need: Unit 2 transient response (settling time, damping) and chapter 5.2 PID structure.

The tuning problem and the ZN closed-loop test

Tuning means choosing Kp, Ti and Td for the plant in front of you. A gain that works on a small lab motor can saturate a furnace. Rules replace guesswork with a measured plant number.

Ziegler-Nichols closed-loop tuning uses the loop itself. Keep integral and derivative action off: set Ki=0 and Kd=0, so the controller is proportional only. Apply a small set-point step. Start with low Kp and increase it in small steps. After each step, watch the plant output.

  1. Set the controller to P only: Ki=0, Kd=0.
  2. Raise Kp slowly and apply a small step.
  3. If the output decays, raise Kp. If it grows, lower Kp.
  4. At constant-amplitude oscillation, record Ku and Pu.
  5. Use Ku and Pu in the ZN table, then retest.

At one gain the output oscillates with constant amplitude. That gain is Ku, the ultimate gain. The time for one full cycle of that oscillation is Pu, the ultimate period, in seconds. Ku is not the normal operating gain. It is the stability-edge gain found by test. Use a small step and be ready to stop the test; many real loops cannot be pushed this far safely.

Ziegler-Nichols settings

Once Ku and Pu are known, the ZN table gives settings. For PID:

Kp = 0.6 Ku

Kp is proportional gain; Ku is the ultimate gain from sustained oscillation.

Ti = 0.5 Pu

Ti is integral time in seconds; Pu is the ultimate period in seconds.

Td = 0.125 Pu

Td is derivative time in seconds; Pu is the ultimate period in seconds.

Ki = Kp / Ti

Ki is integral gain in s−1; Kp and Ti are as above.

Kd = Kp Td

Kd is derivative gain in s; Kp and Td are as above.

These five numbers define the PID controller. Kp sets immediate push, Ti sets how fast integral action repeats the error, Td sets the derivative preview. Ti and Td are times in seconds. Ki is not Ti; Ki is Kp divided by Ti. Kd is not Td; Kd is Kp times Td.

For PI control, remove derivative action:

Kp = 0.45 Ku

Kp is proportional gain; Ku is the ultimate gain.

Ti = Pu / 1.2

Ti is integral time in seconds; Pu is the ultimate period in seconds.

Ki = Kp / Ti

Ki is integral gain in s−1; Kp and Ti are as above.

PI is common on noisy flow and pressure loops because derivative amplifies noise. The ZN PID rule is more aggressive and gives faster response with more overshoot.

Using the settings, limits and other rules

Set the computed gains in the controller, then test with real set-point changes and disturbances. Check overshoot, settling time and control effort. If the valve or drive saturates, reduce Kp or increase Ti. ZN PID is deliberately aggressive; many plants need detuning, often to about half the proportional gain or to a PI setting. A loop that cycles at the process frequency is not necessarily at Ku.

The ZN closed-loop method needs a loop that can be oscillated safely. For a plant that cannot, open-loop tuning uses a recorded step response. Reaction-curve tuning fits a first-order-plus-dead-time model. Cohen-Coon uses the same step data and a different table. Those are names to recognise; this chapter computes only the ZN settings.

After tuning, record Ku, Pu and the final gains. A later mechanical change alters them. Digital PID uses the same Kp, Ti and Td but samples every T seconds; see chapter 5.8.

Worked example

Question. A plant under proportional control oscillates continuously when Kp=Ku=8 and the oscillation period is Pu=4 s. Find the Ziegler-Nichols PID settings Kp, Ti, Td, Ki and Kd.

  1. For PID, Kp = 0.6 Ku = 0.6 × 8 = 4.8.
  2. Ti = 0.5 Pu = 0.5 × 4 s = 2 s.
  3. Td = 0.125 Pu = 0.125 × 4 s = 0.5 s.
  4. Ki = Kp / Ti = 4.8 / 2 s = 2.4 s−1.
  5. Kd = Kp Td = 4.8 × 0.5 s = 2.4 s.

Answer. The ZN PID settings are Kp=4.8, Ti=2 s, Td=0.5 s, Ki=2.4 s−1 and Kd=2.4 s.

Exam traps

  • Ku is the gain for sustained constant-amplitude oscillation, not the normal operating gain. A gain that only wobbles a little gives a wrong Pu.
  • Ti and Td are times in seconds. Ki and Kd are not times; Ki = Kp/Ti and Kd = KpTd.
  • Measure Pu after the oscillation settles, between successive peaks of the same cycle. The first transient peak gives a wrong period.
  • Do not mix parallel-form gains (Kp, Ki, Kd) with series-form settings (Kp, Ti, Td) without converting.
  • ZN PID is aggressive. Expect overshoot; reduce Kp or increase Ti if the actuator saturates.

5.4 Lead compensator design

A lead compensator is a first-order controller that adds positive phase around the gain crossover of a feedback loop. It has a zero at −z rad/s, a pole at −p rad/s with p>z, and gain Kc, so C(s)=Kc(s+z)/(s+p). The extra phase raises phase margin, allowing a faster closed-loop response. It is used in servo drives, power-supply loops, robot joints and process loops where speed is limited by phase lag, not by steady-state accuracy. A few syllabi call it phase-lead compensation.

You need the root-locus angle criterion from Unit 3 and Bode phase margin from Unit 4; 5.7 compares the two design routes.

What Lead Compensation Does

Write the lead compensator as C(s)=Kc(s+z)/(s+p). Here z is the zero location in rad/s, p is the pole location in rad/s, and Kc is the gain. The lead condition is p>z. Then α=z/p is less than 1. The zero corner frequency z is lower than the pole corner frequency p. The zero adds positive phase first; the pole subtracts phase later. The net phase is positive over the useful band.

∠C(jω)=tan⁻¹(ω/z)−tan⁻¹(ω/p),  φm=sin⁻¹((1−α)/(1+α)),  ωm=√(zp)

∠C(jω) is the compensator phase in degrees at frequency ω rad/s; z and p are in rad/s; α=z/p is dimensionless; φm is the maximum phase in degrees; ωm is the frequency in rad/s where φm occurs.

The phase peak lies at the geometric mean ωm=√(zp), not at the arithmetic mean. For z=2.33 rad/s and p=10.73 rad/s, α=0.217, ωm=5 rad/s, and φm=40°. The phase curve in the figure rises to that peak and falls on both sides. This shape is why lead design places ωm near the new gain crossover.

Bode Design Steps

Bode design treats phase margin as the target. Read the uncompensated phase margin PMold and gain crossover from the Bode plot. Choose a target PMreq. Add a safety margin δ of 5° to 10°, because the crossover moves after the lead is added.

  1. Compute the required phase boost φm=PMreq−PMold+δ. If φm≤0, lead is not needed.
  2. Find α from sinφm=(1−α)/(1+α), so α=(1−sinφm)/(1+sinφm). Keep 0<α<1.
  3. Place ωm at the desired new gain crossover ωc′. Check the choice after plotting the compensated loop.
  4. Set z=ωm√α and p=ωm/√α. This gives z/p=α and √(zp)=ωm.
  5. Choose Kc so the compensated magnitude is 0 dB at ωm: Kc=1/(√α |G(jωm)|). If a low-frequency gain is specified, fix Kc first and recheck the crossover.
  6. Plot the compensated Bode plot. Verify PMreq is met; if not, increase δ or move ωm and repeat.

The gain formula uses |G(jωm)|, the uncompensated plant magnitude at ωm. At ωm the lead magnitude is Kc√α. Setting the product |C(jωm)G(jωm)| to 1 gives the Kc formula above. If the final phase margin is short, the usual cause is that the actual crossover has moved away from the chosen ωm.

Root-Locus Lead Design

Root-locus design places the closed-loop poles directly. Pick a desired dominant pole sd from the time-domain specifications. For a lead compensator, sd must satisfy the angle criterion and the magnitude criterion.

  1. Choose the zero z, often near the plant pole that limits speed.
  2. At sd, compute ∠G(sd). Solve ∠(sd+z)−∠(sd+p)+∠G(sd)=−180° for the angle of sd+p.
  3. Use geometry to find p from that angle. Check p>z; if not, change z.
  4. Use the magnitude criterion Kc=|sd+p|/(|G(sd)| |sd+z|).

The worked example below uses G(s)=1/[s(s+2)] and sd=−2+j2√3. The chosen zero is z=2 rad/s. Solving the angle criterion gives p=4 rad/s; the magnitude criterion gives Kc=16. The zero at −2 rad/s cancels the plant pole at −2 rad/s, so the compensated open loop is 16/[s(s+4)]. The closed-loop characteristic equation is s²+4s+16=0, whose roots are the desired pair.

Worked example

Question. Design a lead compensator for G(s)=1/(s(s+2)) to place dominant closed-loop poles at s=−2±j2√3. Choose the zero at z=2 rad/s. Find the pole p and gain Kc.

  1. The desired pole is sd=−2+j2√3≈−2+j3.464 rad/s, and z=2 rad/s.
  2. Plant angle: ∠sd=120°, ∠(sd+2)=90°, so ∠G(sd)=−120°−90°=−210°.
  3. Angle criterion: ∠C(sd)+∠G(sd)=−180°, so ∠C(sd)=−180°−(−210°)=30°.
  4. Compensator angle: ∠(sd+z)−∠(sd+p)=30°. Since sd+z=sd+2=j3.464, ∠(sd+z)=90°. Thus ∠(sd+p)=90°−30°=60°.
  5. Geometry: sd+p=(p−2)+j3.464. tan60°=3.464/(p−2)=√3, so p−2=2 and p=4 rad/s.
  6. Magnitudes: |sd|=4, |sd+2|=3.464, |sd+4|=4, |G(sd)|=1/(4×3.464)=0.07217.
  7. Magnitude criterion: Kc=|sd+p|/(|G(sd)| |sd+z|)=4/(0.07217×3.464)=16.
  8. Result: C(s)=16(s+2)/(s+4).

Answer. The lead compensator is C(s)=16(s+2)/(s+4), with p=4 rad/s and Kc=16.

Exam traps

5.5 Lag compensator design

A lag compensator is a first-order transfer function with a zero farther from the origin than its pole. It keeps low-frequency gain high and reduces gain at higher frequency. That relative boost lowers steady-state error or lets a designer restore phase margin after raising gain. You meet it in temperature loops, DC motor speed control and plants where accuracy matters more than speed. Design is done on the Bode plot: set the gain for error, then place a zero and pole to keep phase margin.

You need: Unit 2 steady-state error and static error constants; Unit 4 Bode plots, gain margin and phase margin; and the lead contrast in 5.4 Lead compensator design.

The lag transfer function

Define the lag compensator as C(s) = Kc(s + z)/(s + p). Kc is a real gain; z is the zero in rad/s; p is the pole in rad/s; and z > p > 0. Let β = z/p. β is greater than 1. The DC gain is C(0) = Kcβ. The high-frequency gain is C(∞) = Kc. So low-frequency gain is β times high-frequency gain.

φ(ω) = tan<sup>−1</sup>(ω/z) − tan<sup>−1</sup>(ω/p)

φ(ω) is the compensator phase in degrees or radians; z is the zero in rad/s; p is the pole in rad/s; ω is frequency in rad/s.

The phase is negative at every ω because z > p. The largest lag magnitude is φm = sin−1((β − 1)/(β + 1)). It occurs at ωm = √(zp). The phase there is −φm. A lag compensator never adds positive phase. It trades phase for gain. For z = 0.5 rad/s and p = 0.05 rad/s, β = 10, ωm = 0.158 rad/s and φm = 54.9°. The figure uses these values.

Bode design procedure

  1. Set the open-loop gain K for the error specification using Unit 2. For a type-1 plant and a unit ramp, ess = 1/Kv, where Kv is the velocity error constant.
  2. Plot K G(jω). Record the gain crossover ωc and the phase margin.
  3. Choose a new crossover ωc′ where the plant phase leaves enough margin. Place the lag zero about one decade below it: z = ωc′/10.
  4. Choose β from the required low-frequency boost or the allowed high-frequency attenuation. Compute the pole as p = z/β.
  5. Set Kc. C(s) = Kc(s + z)/(s + p) has DC gain Kcβ and high-frequency gain Kc. Use Kc = 1 to keep high-frequency gain unchanged and DC gain β. Use Kc = 1/β to keep DC gain unchanged and high-frequency gain 1/β.
  6. Check PM = 180° + ∠G(jωc′) + ∠C(jωc′). If PM is too small, lower z, increase β, or move ωc′ to a lower value.

Why it works and what it costs

On the Bode plot, a lag lowers high-frequency gain and moves crossover left. The plant usually has less negative phase there, so phase margin rises. If the lag raises low-frequency gain by β, then Kv rises by β and ramp error falls by β. The cost is lower bandwidth, slower rise and longer settling. A lag pole very near the origin creates a slow tail in the response.

In root-locus design, a lag adds a pole-zero pair near the origin. The pole is closer to the origin than the zero. That pair raises low-frequency gain. Root-locus details are in 5.7 Compensator design using root locus and Bode plot. When one lag cannot meet both error and phase margin, use the combined section in 5.6 Lag-lead compensator design.

5.6 Lag-lead compensator design

A lag-lead compensator is two first-order sections in cascade. The lag section raises low-frequency gain, so steady-state error falls. The lead section adds positive phase near gain crossover, so phase margin and speed rise. One section alone often cannot do both jobs without hurting the other. You meet the idea in motor drives, process loops and power converters, where a plant is slow and also has large steady-state error. The design rule is simple: set the lag first, then the lead, and keep their frequency ranges apart.

You need: Chapter 5.4 for lead phase and Chapter 5.5 for lag gain, plus Bode plots from Unit 4.

Form and frequency behaviour

A lag-lead compensator puts a lag section and a lead section in cascade. The lag section lifts low-frequency gain, so steady-state error falls. The lead section adds positive phase near crossover, so phase margin rises. One section alone often cannot meet both the error and the margin specification.

C(s) = K<sub>c</sub> (s+z<sub>1</sub>)/(s+p<sub>1</sub>) × (s+z<sub>2</sub>)/(s+p<sub>2</sub>)

C(s) is the compensator transfer function; s is the Laplace variable, and on the Bode plot s = jω with ω the frequency in rad/s; Kc is the gain constant; z1 and p1 are the lead zero and pole in rad/s with p1 > z1; z2 and p2 are the lag zero and pole in rad/s with z2 > p2.

  • Lead bracket: DC gain z1/p1 < 1, because p1 > z1; the gain rises towards 1 as frequency goes high. Maximum phase boost is near √(z1p1).
  • Lag bracket: DC gain z2/p2 > 1, falling to 1 at high frequency.
  • Product DC gain: (z1/p1)(z2/p2); Kc sets the absolute level.
  • High-frequency limit: each bracket → 1, so the product → 1.

The order of the two brackets does not matter; the product is the same. The block diagram shows the lag section first only by convention.

Bode design: lag first, lead second

Bode design uses gain margin, phase margin and steady-state error. The lag section is set at low frequency; the lead section is set near crossover. Keep the two frequency bands about one decade apart.

  1. Find the low-frequency gain factor β > 1 needed from the steady-state error specification. The lag bracket supplies this boost.
  2. Place the lag zero at ωz2 ≈ 0.1ωc, one decade below the intended gain crossover. Place the lag pole at ωp2 = ωz2/β.
  3. The lag bracket supplies the low-frequency boost β; Kc only scales the whole compensator. Set Kc = (required error-constant gain)/(plant DC gain × β). Raising Kc a second time to meet the error constant makes the low-frequency gain β times too large.
  4. Add the lead zero below the new crossover and the lead pole above it. Choose the pole-zero spacing so the lead phase lifts the phase margin to the target.
  5. Check the Bode plot. If phase margin or crossover moves too far, shift the lead pair and repeat.

The lead maximum phase is φmax = sin−1((α−1)/(α+1)), where α = p1/z1 > 1.

Root-locus form

Root-locus design starts from a desired dominant closed-loop pole. Place the lag pair near the origin. The lag pole p2 sits closer to the origin than the lag zero z2. This pair adds only a small angle at the desired poles but raises low-frequency gain. Place the lead zero z1 near the desired pole direction; place the lead pole p1 farther left so the angle condition is met. The magnitude condition then gives Kc. Keep lag and lead corner frequencies separated by about a decade; otherwise the lag phase cancels the lead phase at the desired pole.

Worked example

Question. A lag-lead compensator has lead zero 2 rad/s, lead pole 8 rad/s, lag zero 0.2 rad/s and lag pole 0.02 rad/s. Find the DC gain of the bracket product, its high-frequency limit, the phase at 4 rad/s, and Kc for an overall DC gain of 1.

  1. Write C(s) = Kc (s+2)/(s+8) × (s+0.2)/(s+0.02). The corner frequencies are 2, 8, 0.2 and 0.02 rad/s.
  2. DC gain of the brackets = (2/8)(0.2/0.02) = 0.25 × 10 = 2.5.
  3. High-frequency limit: each bracket tends to 1, so the product tends to 1 × 1 = 1.
  4. Lead phase at 4 rad/s = tan−1(4/2) − tan−1(4/8) = 63.43° − 26.57° = 36.87°.
  5. Lag phase at 4 rad/s = tan−1(4/0.2) − tan−1(4/0.02) = 87.14° − 89.71° = −2.57°.
  6. Total phase = 36.87° − 2.57° = 34.30° ≈ 34.3°. For overall DC gain 1, Kc × 2.5 = 1, so Kc = 0.4. With this Kc, the high-frequency gain is 0.4.

Exam traps

  • Designing lead and lag at the same corner frequency: their phases add with opposite signs and cancel. Separate the bands by about a decade.
  • Forgetting Kc in the DC gain: the bracket product has its own DC gain (z1/p1)(z2/p2); Kc must divide it to meet the specified overall DC gain.
  • Placing the lag zero below its pole: for a lag section z2 > p2. If you reverse them you get a lead section and the steady-state gain falls.
  • Using the high-frequency limit as the DC gain: each bracket tends to 1 at high frequency, so the product tends to 1; the DC value is z1z2/(p1p2).
  • Adding lead phase without rechecking crossover: lead raises crossover, which changes the phase of both sections; always re-evaluate phase margin.
  • Counting the lag boost β twice: the lag bracket already gives the low-frequency boost, so Kc must not be raised again to meet the same error constant.

5.7 Compensator design using root locus and Bode plot

A compensator is an extra block placed with the plant so the closed-loop system meets its specification. Root-locus design moves the dominant closed-loop poles to a chosen point; Bode design shapes gain crossover, phase margin and gain margin. Both routes can produce a lead block, which adds phase, or a lag block, which adds low-frequency gain. They are used in servo drives, power converters and process loops where a plain gain cannot meet both speed and steady-state error. Some syllabi call this analytical design of controllers.

You need: Unit 3 root locus and Unit 4 Bode plots and margins; chapter <a href='#lead-compensator-design'>5.4 to <a href='#lag-lead-compensator-design'>5.6 for the compensator forms.

Two routes, one target

Specifications come first. A specification may give damping ratio ζ, natural frequency ωn, settling time, steady-state error, or margins. Convert time-domain targets to a desired dominant pair s0 = −ζωn ± jωn√(1−ζ²). The uncompensated locus usually misses s0; the compensator bends it through s0.

Root locus uses the angle criterion. For s0 on the compensated locus, ∠C(s0)G(s0) = ±(2k+1)180°. The magnitude criterion then sets gain: |C(s0)G(s0)| = 1. If the angle is short by φ, a lead zero and pole supply that φ. A lag pair placed near the origin supplies gain without much angle change at s0.

Bode uses the open-loop frequency response L(jω)=C(jω)G(jω). Gain crossover ωgc is where |L|=1, that is 0 dB. Phase margin PM = 180° + ∠L(jωgc). Gain margin GM = −20 log10|L(jωpc)|, where ωpc is the phase crossover. Lead raises PM near crossover; lag raises low-frequency gain for smaller steady-state error.

The two routes share the same C(s), so a design from one route must be checked by the other.

∠C(s<sub>0</sub>)G(s<sub>0</sub>) = ±(2k+1)180°, |C(s<sub>0</sub>)G(s<sub>0</sub>)| = 1

s0 is the desired closed-loop pole; C is the compensator; G is the plant; k is an integer; the angle is in degrees.

Root-locus route

Draw the uncompensated root locus and mark s0. Compute the angle deficiency φ = ±(2k+1)180° − ∠G(s0). This is the phase the compensator must add at s0.

For a lead, use C(s)=Kc(s+z)/(s+p) with |p|>|z|. Its angle at s0 is ∠(s0+z) − ∠(s0+p). Choose z and p so this equals φ. Place the zero near a slow plant pole and the pole farther left. Then Kc = 1/[|(s0+z)/(s0+p)| |G(s0)|].

For a lag, use |z|>|p|. Put the pole close to the origin and the zero to its left. The angle at s0 is small, while the gain ratio z/p raises the error constant. Keep the lag pair well below the expected crossover.

After choosing C(s), find all closed-loop poles. Check that no extra pole is closer to the imaginary axis than s0. If one is, the response is dominated by that pole and s0 is not useful.

Bode route and cross-check

Plot the uncompensated G(jω). Read its PM, GM and low-frequency gain. Decide the new gain crossover from the required PM plus a safety margin of about 5°.

For lead, place the zero below the new crossover and the pole above it. The maximum phase occurs at ωm = √(zp), and the peak phase is sin φm = (p−z)/(p+z). Set ωm near the new crossover. The lead raises PM and bandwidth, so the response is faster but more noise-sensitive.

For lag, place the zero and pole below crossover. The pole is closer to the origin than the zero, so the low-frequency gain rises by z/p. Keep the lag phase at crossover small, usually a few degrees. The cost is lower bandwidth and a slower response.

Verify both routes. A root-locus point check does not give PM or GM. A Bode margin check does not place the dominant poles. Use a step response, percent overshoot, settling time, steady-state error, PM and GM. If one check fails, adjust the compensator and repeat.

  • Cancelling a plant pole in C(s)G(s) can hide an unstable or lightly damped mode; check the cancellation and internal stability.

5.8 Digital controllers and digital PID

A digital controller reads the error only at fixed instants, T seconds apart, and computes the control from a difference equation. A microprocessor or PLC cannot integrate continuously, so the PID law is rewritten as a sum and a difference. The sampler takes e(kT); the zero-order hold keeps u[k] constant until the next sample. This is the normal form of PID in motor drives, temperature loops, power converters and PLCs. The gains keep their usual meanings, but T now enters every calculation, so a wrong T changes the effective controller.

You need: Chapter 5.2 for P, I and D action, and the rise-time definition from Unit 2 for choosing T.

The sampled loop and the positional form

A sampled-data loop has a continuous plant and a discrete controller. The error is e(t) = r(t) − y(t), where r(t) is the reference and y(t) is the measured output. Every T seconds the sampler closes and the ADC produces e[k] = e(kT), for k = 0, 1, 2, ... T is the sampling period in seconds. The digital PID computes u[k], the control at sample k, and the zero-order hold (ZOH) keeps u(t) = u[k] for kT ≤ t < (k+1)T.

u[k] = K<sub>p</sub>e[k] + K<sub>i</sub>TΣ<sub>j=0</sub><sup>k</sup>e[j] + (K<sub>d</sub>/T)(e[k] − e[k−1])

u[k] is the control at sample k; e[k] = e(kT) is the error at that sample; Kp is dimensionless; Ki is in s−1; Kd is in s; T is the sampling period in s; and e[−1] = 0.

The first term is present error, the second is accumulated past error, and the third is the recent trend. The sum uses rectangular integration; the difference is a backward derivative. Set e[−1] = 0 at start-up. Kp is dimensionless, Ki is in s−1, and Kd is in s, so every term has the same output unit.

The figure shows the order: sum, sampler, PID, ZOH, plant, sensor. The sampler and PID are the new parts; the ZOH is a register and DAC. The sensor and plant remain continuous.

Velocity form, windup and derivative kick

Positional form gives the absolute control u[k]. Velocity form gives the change in control. The old sum disappears; only three errors are stored. Velocity form gives bumpless transfer when switching from manual to automatic, because the output moves from its present value.

Δu[k] = K<sub>p</sub>(e[k] − e[k−1]) + K<sub>i</sub>T e[k] + (K<sub>d</sub>/T)(e[k] − 2e[k−1] + e[k−2])

Δu[k] = u[k] − u[k−1]; set e[−1] = e[−2] = 0 at start-up; other symbols are as in the positional form.

Integral windup is the growth of the sum while the actuator is saturated. The controller keeps adding error that the plant cannot answer, so the output overshoots when the error reverses. Anti-windup clamps the sum, stops integration, or back-calculates from the actual actuator output. Derivative kick is a large derivative term caused by a step in the reference. Use derivative on the measurement y[k], or filter the derivative.

The positional form is easier to read; the velocity form is safer in code with saturation. Both use the same Kp, Ki, Kd and T.

Discretization and choosing T

Two standard substitutions replace s with an expression in z−1, where z−1 means a delay of one sample. Backward difference uses s ≈ (1 − z−1)/T. Tustin uses s ≈ (2/T)(1 − z−1)/(1 + z−1). Backward difference is simple. Tustin maps the frequency axis more accurately and is preferred when phase error matters.

Choose T small compared with the closed-loop rise time, often T ≤ rise time/10 to rise time/20. Small T improves resolution but costs computation and amplifies sensor noise in the derivative term. An anti-alias filter before the ADC is standard. The same rule applies when a PLC scans a slow temperature loop or a drive controls a motor.

If T is too large, the discrete loop behaves like a low-resolution system: extra phase lag, limit cycles, and poor disturbance rejection.

Worked example

Question. A digital PID has Kp=2, Ki=3 s−1, Kd=0.5 s and T=0.1 s. The errors are e[0]=1, e[1]=0.5, e[2]=0.2. Find u[2] using the positional form.

  1. Write the data with units: Kp=2, Ki=3 s−1, Kd=0.5 s, T=0.1 s, e[0]=1, e[1]=0.5, e[2]=0.2.
  2. Proportional term: Kpe[2] = 2×0.2 = 0.4.
  3. Integral term: e[0]+e[1]+e[2] = 1+0.5+0.2 = 1.7. Then KiTΣe[j] = 3×0.1×1.7 = 0.51.
  4. Derivative term: (Kd/T)(e[2]−e[1]) = (0.5/0.1)(0.2−0.5) = 5×(−0.3) = −1.5.
  5. Add the three terms: u[2] = 0.4+0.51−1.5 = −0.59.

Answer. u[2] = −0.59 in the controller output unit, for example volts.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting T in the integral term: use KiTΣe[j], not KiΣe[j]. With T=0.1 s the error is a factor of 10.
  • Forgetting to divide by T in the derivative term: use (Kd/T)(e[k]−e[k−1]), not Kd(e[k]−e[k−1]). With T = 0.1 s the derivative term comes out ten times too small, so the damping is lost.
  • Using continuous gains after changing T: the effective integral gain is KiT and the effective derivative gain is Kd/T, so retune when T changes.
  • Ignoring derivative kick and integral windup: a reference step can saturate the actuator, and the sum can keep growing while saturated.
  • Confusing positional and velocity forms: positional gives u[k]; velocity gives Δu[k]=u[k]−u[k−1]. The examiner may ask which one needs the old sum.

Takeaways

  1. On-off control is the cheapest controller but the output always cycles; the differential gap sets the size and speed of that cycle.
  2. P action cuts rise time but leaves offset, I action removes offset but slows the loop, D action damps overshoot but amplifies noise.
  3. Tuning rules such as Ziegler-Nichols convert two or three measured plant numbers into PID gains, giving a safe starting point that is then trimmed by hand.
  4. A lead compensator adds phase near the gain crossover frequency, so phase margin and speed both go up, but high-frequency noise gets amplified.
  5. A lag compensator keeps the gain high at low frequency and low at high frequency, which reduces steady-state error without giving up phase margin.
  6. A lag-lead compensator is used when the plant needs both a large error constant and extra phase margin, which neither section can give alone.
  7. Root-locus design places closed-loop poles where you want them; Bode design shapes gain and phase margins. Both realize lead and lag networks.
  8. A digital PID is a difference equation run every T seconds, so the sampling period itself becomes a design parameter.
  9. Always check the compensated design by simulation: margins and error constants are necessary targets, not proof of a good transient.

Glossary

Aliasing
A high-frequency signal appearing as a false low frequency because the sampling rate is too low.
Bumpless transfer
Switching a controller between manual and automatic without a jump in the actuator signal.
Cascade compensation
A compensator placed in series with the plant, ahead of the summing junction's forward path.
Compensator
A network or algorithm added to a loop to improve its steady-state accuracy or its transient response.
Dead band
Range of error over which a controller makes no change in output.
Derivative kick
Sudden jump in controller output when the set point is stepped, caused by differentiation of the error.
Difference equation
Recurrence relation that computes the present control output from present and past samples.
Dominant poles
The pair of closed-loop poles closest to the imaginary axis, which sets the visible transient.
Error signal
Set point minus measured output, e(t) = r(t) − y(t), the input to the controller.
Gain crossover frequency
Frequency at which the open-loop magnitude equals 1, or 0 dB.
Gain margin
Factor, or its decibel value, by which the gain can rise before the loop becomes unstable.
Hysteresis
Deliberate difference between the error level that turns the output ON and the level that turns it OFF.
Integral windup
Growth of the integral term during actuator saturation, which delays recovery and causes overshoot.
Offset
Steady-state error left by proportional-only control.
On-off control
Control in which the actuator has only two positions, fully ON or fully OFF.
Phase crossover frequency
Frequency at which the open-loop phase equals −180°.
Phase margin
180° plus the open-loop phase at the gain crossover frequency.
Process reaction curve
Open-loop response of a plant to a step input, used to extract gain, delay and time constant.
Proportional band
Percentage of the input span over which a proportional controller output travels its full range.
Sampling period
Time interval T between two successive samples in a digital controller.
Set point
The value the controlled variable should have.
Ultimate gain
Proportional gain at which a closed loop under P control oscillates with constant amplitude.
Ultimate period
Period of the sustained oscillation at the ultimate gain.
Zero-order hold
Device that holds a sampled value constant until the next sample arrives.
