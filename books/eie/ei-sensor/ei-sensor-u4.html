4.1 Piezoelectric transducers and their signal conditioning

A piezoelectric crystal produces charge when squeezed and changes shape when a voltage is applied. This reversible pair is the direct and the inverse piezoelectric effect. The effect turns force, pressure and vibration into an electric signal without any external power, so the sensor is active. It also works backwards: the same crystal can push sound into air, water or metal. Quartz, PZT ceramics and PVDF film are the common materials. You meet these transducers in engine pressure measurement, vibration monitoring, medical scanners and ultrasonic distance sensors.

You need Unit 1 for dynamic characteristics and Unit 3 for capacitance and the parallel-plate formula C = ε0 εr A / t.

Direct and inverse piezoelectric effect

Press a quartz plate between two metal electrodes and charge appears on the electrodes. Remove the force and the charge falls back to zero. The charge reverses sign only if the force is reversed in direction, that is, if the plate is stretched instead of compressed. This is the direct piezoelectric effect: mechanical stress produces electric polarisation. Apply a voltage across the same plate and it becomes slightly thicker or thinner. This is the inverse effect: electric field produces strain. Both effects are linear and reversible for small signals, so one crystal can sense and also generate sound.

Q = d F

Q is the charge in coulombs (C); d is the charge coefficient in C/N, often quoted in pC/N; F is the applied force in newtons (N).

V = g t P

V is the open-circuit voltage in volts; g is the voltage coefficient in V·m/N, with g = d/ε; ε is the permittivity of the crystal, ε = ε0 εr, with ε0 = 8.85×10⁻¹² F/m and εr the relative permittivity (4.5 for quartz); t is the plate thickness in m; P is the pressure in N/m² (Pa). Some syllabi call g the voltage sensitivity.

  • Quartz: d = 2.3 pC/N and εr ≈ 4.5. Very stable, low output, good for calibration.
  • PZT (lead zirconate titanate): d = 300–600 pC/N. High output, most common in sensors and ultrasound.
  • PVDF polymer film: d ≈ 20–30 pC/N. Thin, flexible, acoustic impedance close to water and tissue.

The coefficient depends on the crystal cut and on the direction of force. For poled ceramics and polymer films, a datasheet gives d33 for force along the poling axis and d31 for force at right angles to it; for quartz the quoted coefficient is d11.

Equivalent circuit and the steady-force problem

Electrically the crystal is a charge source q in parallel with its own capacitance Cc and a leakage resistance R. For a plate of area A and thickness t, Cc = ε0 εr A / t, with ε0 = 8.85×10⁻¹² F/m. R represents the crystal resistance plus insulator and cable leakage; for quartz it can exceed 10¹² Ω. The source is a charged capacitor, not a battery.

fL = 1 / (2π R Cc)

fL is the low-frequency cut-off in hertz; R is the leakage resistance in ohms; Cc is the crystal capacitance in farads.

The charge leaks through R with time constant τ = R Cc. A steady force therefore gives a voltage that decays to zero. Below fL the reading is wrong; above it the output follows the dynamic force. This is why piezoelectric sensors measure vibration, shock, pulses and cylinder pressure, not a weight sitting on a scale.

Signal conditioning: why a charge amplifier

A plain voltage amplifier measures V = q/(Cc + Ck + Cin), where Ck is cable capacitance and Cin is amplifier input capacitance. Any change in cable length or position changes the gain, and a moving cable adds triboelectric noise.

A charge amplifier avoids this. The crystal connects to the virtual ground of an inverting op-amp, and a feedback capacitor Cf carries the charge. The output is Vout = −q/Cf, independent of Cc, Ck and Cin. A feedback resistor Rf discharges Cf and sets the low cut-off fL = 1/(2π Rf Cf).

Vout = −q / Cf

Vout is the amplifier output voltage in volts; q is the charge from the crystal in coulombs; Cf is the feedback capacitance in farads.

After the charge amplifier a low-pass filter removes high-frequency noise, and the output goes to a recorder or ADC. For pressure, a diaphragm or a stack of crystal plates converts pressure to force. For a stack of n plates the charges add, so q = n d A P and Vout = −n d A P/Cf, with n = 1 for a single diaphragm. For ultrasound, a PZT element is driven by a short high-frequency burst and then listens for the echo. Echo timing and ranging are covered in Ultrasonic and acoustic sensors.

Worked example

Question. A quartz disc has area 2 cm², thickness 1 mm, d = 2.3 pC/N and εr = 4.5. A dynamic force of 10 N is applied along the sensitive axis. (a) Find the charge produced. (b) Find the crystal capacitance. (c) Find the open-circuit voltage. Take ε0 = 8.85×10⁻¹² F/m.

  1. Convert area and thickness: A = 2 cm² = 2×10⁻⁴ m²; t = 1 mm = 1×10⁻³ m.
  2. Charge: Q = d F = 2.3 pC/N × 10 N = 23 pC = 23×10⁻¹² C.
  3. Capacitance: Cc = ε0 εr A / t = 8.85×10⁻¹² F/m × 4.5 × 2×10⁻⁴ m² ÷ 1×10⁻³ m = 7.965×10⁻¹² F ≈ 7.97 pF.
  4. Open-circuit voltage: V = Q / Cc = 23×10⁻¹² C ÷ 7.965×10⁻¹² F = 2.89 V ≈ 2.9 V.

Answer. The charge is 23 pC, the crystal capacitance is 7.97 pF and the open-circuit voltage is 2.9 V.

Exam traps

  • A piezoelectric sensor cannot measure a steady weight. The charge leaks away, so only a changing force gives a lasting output.
  • d is charge per newton (C/N or pC/N); g is voltage per unit stress (V·m/N). Do not mix the two coefficients.
  • Cable capacitance spoils a voltage amplifier but not a charge amplifier. Read the question: the answer changes with the circuit.
  • The unit pC/N means 10⁻¹² C/N. A d of 2.3 pC/N is not 2.3 C/N.
  • The crystal's sharp mechanical resonance is used for ultrasound, not for force measurement. A force sensor works well below resonance.

4.2 Ultrasonic and acoustic sensors

An ultrasonic sensor sends a burst of sound above human hearing and listens for the echo. The delay gives distance, level or flow. An acoustic sensor measures audible sound pressure. Both work without touching the target, so they suit parking aids, tank level, blood-flow probes and crack checks. This chapter shows how time of flight becomes distance and what limits the reading.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for the piezoelectric transmitter and receiver, and Unit 1 for first-order and second-order dynamic response.

Sound, wavelength and the pulse-echo idea

Sound is a pressure wave. A vibrating transducer pushes the air next to it, and the push travels outward. Humans hear about 20 Hz to 20 kHz. Sound above 20 kHz is ultrasound.

The medium sets the speed v. In air at 20 °C, v ≈ 343 m/s. In water, v ≈ 1480 m/s. In steel, v ≈ 5900 m/s.

λ = v / f

λ is wavelength in metres, v is speed of sound in m/s, f is frequency in Hz. A 40 kHz pulse in air has λ = 343 / 40 000 = 8.6 mm.

Short wavelength sees fine detail. In air a 40 kHz wave has λ near 8.6 mm, so it cannot resolve much below that.

Pulse-echo sends a short burst, stops transmitting and listens. The echo returns after time t, the time of flight. The wave went out and back, so the path is twice the distance.

d = v t / 2

d is distance to target in metres, v is speed in m/s, t is round-trip time in seconds. The 2 accounts for the out-and-back path.

  • One crystal may transmit then receive; two crystals may face the same way.
  • A timer starts when the pulse leaves and stops when the echo crosses a threshold.
  • Soft, angled or tiny targets return little sound, so the echo is weak and the reading fails.

Beam angle, resolution and the transducer

The crystal is wider than a wavelength, so the beam leaves as a cone. The half-angle θ shrinks as crystal diameter D grows and widens as λ grows.

sin θ ≈ 1.22 λ / D

θ is half-angle in radians, λ is wavelength in metres, D is crystal diameter in metres. This is a rough guide for a round piston face.

Range resolution is set by pulse length. If the pulse lasts τ seconds, targets closer than v τ / 2 merge into one echo. A short pulse resolves better but carries less energy, so it reaches less far.

Most ultrasonic transducers are piezoelectric (Chapter 4.1). A ceramic disc such as lead zirconate titanate is cut to half a wavelength thick so it rings at the wanted frequency. A matching layer helps sound enter air. A backing block damps the ring so the pulse ends quickly.

Acoustic sensors and Doppler flow

An acoustic sensor measures audible sound pressure. A microphone is the common example. Pressure swing in air is tiny, so sound pressure level uses a logarithmic scale against 20 µPa.

L = 20 log₁₀ (p / p₀)

L is sound pressure level in dB, p is rms sound pressure in Pa, p₀ = 20 µPa = 2 × 10⁻⁵ Pa. A quiet room is near 30 dB; a loud market near 80 dB.

Through-beam mode has transmitter facing receiver; anything blocking the path triggers output. Reflective mode has transmitter and receiver side by side and the target returns the sound. A car parking aid is reflective; a bin-full detector is often through-beam.

Level measurement times the echo from a liquid surface. The sensor measures d, the distance from the sensor down to the surface. The liquid level is tank height minus d, so as t and d grow, the level falls. Correct v for temperature, roughly 0.6 m/s per °C near room temperature, or the reading drifts.

Flow can be measured from moving particles. A moving reflector shifts the returned frequency: the Doppler effect.

Δf = 2 f v cos α / c

Δf is frequency shift in Hz, f is transmitted frequency in Hz, v is target speed in m/s, α is angle between beam and flow, c is speed of sound in the medium in m/s. The 2 is for the outward and return shifts.

A Doppler flowmeter on a pipe needs dirt, bubbles or cells to reflect, because it measures particles, not the pipe wall. Non-destructive testing uses the same pulse-echo hardware at megahertz frequencies to find cracks in welds.

Worked example

Question. A car parking sensor sends a 40 kHz ultrasonic pulse in air at 20 °C, where the speed of sound is 343 m/s. The echo from an obstacle returns after 12 ms. (a) Find the distance to the obstacle. (b) Find the wavelength in air. (c) If the pulse lasts 1.0 ms, what is the range resolution?

  1. List the data: v = 343 m/s, t = 12 ms = 0.012 s, f = 40 kHz = 40 000 Hz, pulse length τ = 1.0 ms = 1.0 × 10⁻³ s.
  2. The path is 2d = v t = 343 m/s × 0.012 s = 4.116 m.
  3. Halve it: d = 4.116 / 2 = 2.058 m ≈ 2.06 m.
  4. Wavelength: λ = v / f = 343 / 40 000 = 8.575 × 10⁻³ m = 8.58 mm.
  5. Range resolution: v τ / 2 = 343 × 1.0 × 10⁻³ / 2 = 0.1715 m ≈ 0.17 m.

Output

d = 2.058 m
λ = 8.575 mm (8.575 × 10⁻³ m)
resolution = 0.1715 m

Answer. The obstacle is about 2.06 m away, the wavelength in air is about 8.58 mm, and the range resolution is about 0.17 m.

Worked example 2

Question. A 1 MHz ultrasonic Doppler probe is clamped on a pipe at an angle of 45° to the flow. The speed of sound in the liquid is 1500 m/s and the measured frequency shift is 400 Hz. Find the flow speed.

  2. Start from Δf = 2 f v cos α / c.
  3. Rearrange: v = Δf c / (2 f cos α).
  4. Substitute: v = 400 × 1500 / (2 × 1 × 10⁶ × 0.7071) = 6.0 × 10⁵ / 1.4142 × 10⁶ = 0.4243 m/s.
  5. Convert: 0.424 m/s ≈ 0.42 m/s.

Output

0.4243

Answer. The flow speed is about 0.42 m/s.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the factor 2 in d = v t / 2, which doubles the distance. The wave went out and came back, so the path is twice the distance.
  • Using 343 m/s in water or steel. The speed belongs to the medium, not to the sensor; in steel it is about 5900 m/s.
  • Mixing up frequency and period. A 40 kHz pulse has a period of 25 µs, not 40 ms.
  • Quoting the sound pressure level formula with 10 log₁₀ instead of 20 log₁₀. Pressure and voltage use 20; power and intensity use 10.
  • Treating the Doppler shift as a single trip. The wave shifts once on the way to the moving particle and once on the way back, so the factor 2 is needed again.

4.3 Seismic transducers and accelerometers

A seismic transducer measures vibration with a proof mass hung on a spring and damper inside a case. When the case moves, the mass lags behind. The relative motion between mass and case is the output. Below the natural frequency this motion follows acceleration; above it, it follows displacement. The same second-order model therefore explains two instruments: the accelerometer and the seismometer. Accelerometers fire airbags, watch machine bearings and level phones. Seismometers record earthquakes. This chapter derives the mass-spring-damper response, shows how to choose the working range, and connects it to piezoelectric and capacitive sensing.

You need Unit 1 second-order dynamic characteristics (natural frequency, damping ratio, amplitude ratio) and piezoelectric transducers for the sensing element.

A mass on a spring and damper

Fix a case to a vibrating surface. Inside, a seismic mass m sits on a spring of stiffness k and a damper of coefficient c. Let x(t) be the mass displacement and y(t) the case displacement, both measured in metres from rest. The relative displacement is z = x − y. This z is what most seismic sensors actually measure.

m (d²z/dt²) + c (dz/dt) + k z = −m (d²y/dt²)

m is the seismic mass in kg; c is the damping coefficient in N·s/m; k is the spring constant in N/m; z is the relative displacement in m; y is the case displacement in m; t is time in s.

Divide by m and write the standard second-order form. The natural angular frequency is ωn = √(k/m) rad/s, and the natural frequency is fn = (1/2π)√(k/m) Hz. The damping ratio is ζ = c/(2√(km)). For a sinusoidal case motion y = Y sin(2πft), define the frequency ratio r = f/fn, where f is the vibration frequency in Hz.

H = 1 / √((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²)

H is the amplitude ratio of relative displacement per unit case acceleration, scaled by 1/ωn²; D = √((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²) is the denominator, so H = 1/D; r = f/fn is dimensionless; ζ is the damping ratio.

The relative displacement amplitude is Z = (A/ωn²) H, where A is the case acceleration amplitude in m/s². At low r, H ≈ 1, so Z ∝ A: the output reads acceleration. At high r, the displacement ratio Z/Y = r²H tends to 1, so the output reads displacement. The phase lag is φ = arctan(2ζr/(1 − r²)), with a 90° lag at r = 1.

Accelerometer: work far below resonance

An accelerometer is a seismic transducer used with f ≪ fn, usually r below about 0.2. Then H is close to 1 and the relative displacement is almost exactly A/ωn². The sensor output is proportional to acceleration. A high fn gives a wide usable band. A small mass and a stiff spring raise fn.

  • Piezoelectric accelerometer: a crystal or ceramic stack is compressed by the seismic mass. It produces charge q = d·F, where d is the piezoelectric charge constant in C/N and F is the dynamic force in N. A charge amplifier converts q to a voltage. It cannot measure true DC acceleration.
  • Capacitive MEMS accelerometer: a silicon proof mass moves between capacitor plates, and the capacitance change gives acceleration. It can measure DC and is small enough for phones and airbags.

Damping is chosen near ζ = 0.6 to 0.7. Damping near ζ = 0.7 removes the peak near r = 1 and extends the flat band to higher r, and it keeps the phase shift close to linear, so the waveform stays faithful. Too little damping gives a sharp peak at r = 1; too much damping slows the response.

Mounting matters. The case, adhesive or magnet has its own resonance. If that mounting resonance falls near the measurement band, the output shows a false peak. Keep the mounting resonance well above the highest frequency you need. The same rule applies to the sensor's own resonance: never use an accelerometer at or near fn.

Seismometer and practical uses

A seismometer uses the other end of the curve. With f ≫ fn, the mass hardly follows the case, so the relative displacement z approaches the case displacement y. The instrument then reads ground displacement. A low fn needs a soft spring and a large mass. A short-period seismometer has a higher fn; a long-period one has a lower fn. Damping near critical reduces the free ringing after a shock.

In a car airbag, a MEMS accelerometer detects the sudden deceleration of a crash and the electronic control unit fires the bag within milliseconds. Worked example

Question. An accelerometer has natural frequency fn = 10 kHz and damping ratio ζ = 0.7. Find the amplitude ratio H and the percentage error at a vibration frequency f = 1 kHz.

  1. Frequency ratio: r = f/fn = 1000/10000 = 0.1.
  2. Compute terms: r² = 0.01, 1 − r² = 0.99. 2ζr = 2 × 0.7 × 0.1 = 0.14.
  3. Denominator: √((0.99)² + (0.14)²) = √(0.9801 + 0.0196) = √0.9997 = 0.99985.
  4. Amplitude ratio: H = 1/0.99985 = 1.00015.
  5. Error: (H − 1) × 100% = 0.015%. Positive means the reading is high.

Answer. At 1 kHz the amplitude ratio is H = 1.00015, so the accelerometer reads 0.015 % high.

Exam traps

  • Using an accelerometer at or near its natural frequency: H becomes large and the phase shifts, so the waveform is wrong even if the sensor does not break.
  • Forgetting the mounting resonance: a stud, glue or magnet can add a peak inside the measurement band.
  • Mixing the two ratios: H = 1/D is the acceleration response; Z/Y = r²/D is the displacement response used by a seismometer.
  • Dropping units in fn = (1/2π)√(k/m): use k in N/m and m in kg to get Hz; grams or N/mm give a wrong frequency.
  • Thinking zero damping is best: low ζ gives a sharp resonance peak and a large phase change; about ζ = 0.7 is often chosen.

4.4 Hall effect sensors

A thin semiconductor strip carrying current in a magnetic field develops a small voltage across its width. The field pushes the moving charge carriers to one side, so one edge becomes slightly positive and the other slightly negative. This is the Hall effect, and the voltage it makes is proportional to the field. Hall sensors use it to measure magnetic flux density, to detect the position of a magnet, to count gear teeth, to clamp around a wire and read its current without breaking the circuit, and to find north in a phone compass. They are small, cheap, wear-free and work from DC to high frequency.

You need: sensitivity from Unit 1 (see static characteristics) and the idea of drift current in a semiconductor.

The Hall effect and the Hall voltage

Take a thin rectangular plate of n-type semiconductor. Send a current I along its length. Apply a magnetic field B perpendicular to the plate face. Each electron drifting with velocity v feels the Lorentz force F = qv × B, which pushes it sideways towards one edge.

Charge piles up on that edge until the electric field it creates pushes back with equal force. That sideways field, measured as a voltage between the two side contacts, is the Hall voltage VH. The build-up takes picoseconds, so the sensor responds almost instantly.

VH = RH I B / t

VH is the Hall voltage in volts; RH is the Hall coefficient in m³/C; I is the control current in amperes; B is the flux density in tesla; t is the plate thickness in metres.

RH = 1 / (n e)

n is the carrier density in carriers per m³; e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C is the electron charge. For p-type material the sign of RH flips, so the Hall voltage reverses.

Two design facts follow. Thin plates give large VH because t is in the denominator. Low carrier density gives large RH. InSb with n around 2 × 10²² m⁻³ is about four to five times more sensitive than silicon with n around 10²³ m⁻³ at the same current, because its carrier density is lower. Its high electron mobility also keeps the plate resistance low, so a useful current needs only a small drive voltage and makes less noise. GaAs sits in between and drifts less with temperature.

The Hall coefficient is a material property of the plate. It is not the sensitivity of the sensor. Sensitivity is VH per tesla, S = RH I / t, and it also depends on the current you choose to push and on the thickness. Two plates of the same InSb, one 0.1 mm thick and one 0.3 mm thick, give outputs in a 3:1 ratio at the same current.

Driving the plate and reading the output

The plate can be driven by a constant current source or by a constant voltage. Current drive is standard: I is fixed, so VH = RH(T) I B / t changes only through the smaller drift of RH. The much larger drift caused by plate resistance changing the current is removed. Voltage drive makes the current fall as the plate heats, so the output drifts.

A practical sensor includes more than the plate. The Hall voltage is only a few millivolts, so a differential amplifier with high common-mode rejection sits beside it on the same chip. Many parts add a Schmitt comparator so the output is a clean digital switch.

  • Linear sensor: output is an analog voltage proportional to B, used for position and current measurement.
  • Threshold switch: output snaps high or low when B crosses a set level, used for proximity, gear-tooth counting and shaft speed.
  • Ratiometric output: the analog output scales with supply voltage, so the analog-to-digital converter should share that supply.

Signal-conditioning details belong to Unit 6 (see differential amplifiers). Two non-idealities always remain. Offset is the output with B = 0, caused by misalignment of the side contacts; it can be trimmed at manufacture or measured and subtracted. Temperature drift comes from RH and n changing with heat. A ratiometric part makes the output track the supply so a shared-supply ADC cancels supply error; a chopper-stabilised amplifier removes amplifier offset drift. The drift of RH itself needs temperature compensation or a matched pair.

A compass is a special case. The horizontal field of the earth is about 25 µT to 65 µT, a few hundred to a thousand times weaker than the field of a small magnet at a gear tooth (tens of millitesla). The compass part needs a high-sensitivity material and a low-noise amplifier, often with a flux concentrator and set/reset coils to cancel offset.

4.5 Magnetostrictive and magneto-elastic sensors

Magnetostrictive sensors use a magnetic material that changes length in a magnetic field, and changes its magnetization when stressed. The same coupling runs both ways. A coil can drive the material to make sound or strain; a force can change the material to make a coil voltage. These sensors measure force, torque and position and generate ultrasound. They are rugged and can take high force, so they appear in machine shafts, sonar, cleaning baths and delay lines.

You need Unit 2's magneto-resistive sensors for the magnetic-field background.

Joule, Villari and Wiedemann effects

A magnetostrictive material changes shape when a magnetic field passes through it. This is the Joule effect. The fractional change in length is λ = ΔL / L, where ΔL is the change in length in metres and L is the original length in metres. λ is dimensionless. Engineers often quote it in ppm (parts per million): 1 ppm = 10⁻⁶. A positive λ means the rod expands; a negative λ means it contracts.

λ = ΔL / L

λ is the magnetostriction as a dimensionless fraction; multiply by 10⁶ to express it in ppm. ΔL and L are in the same unit.

The inverse is the Villari effect: stress applied to the material changes its magnetization. A pickup coil around the rod sees this as a voltage. The Wiedemann effect is a twist: a longitudinal field and a circumferential field together make the rod twist. These three effects give force, torque and delay-line sensors.

The curve of λ against field is not a straight line. Without a bias field it is nonlinear with hysteresis: it starts with near-zero slope at H = 0, rises steeply at intermediate field and then saturates. The same field can give different strains on the rising and falling path. A permanent magnet or a DC current in a coil sets a bias field. The sensor then works on a steeper, straighter part of the curve. Bias also decides the sign of the output for small changes.

Nickel gives small negative magnetostriction and is cheap and stable. Metglas, an amorphous alloy, has low loss and fast response with modest strain. Terfenol-D, a rare-earth iron alloy, gives large strain up to about 1000 to 2000 ppm and high force, but it is costly and brittle.

Torque, delay lines and ultrasound

A magneto-elastic torque sensor uses a shaft with a magnetostrictive band or ring. Two coils sit near the shaft. Torque puts the band under shear stress. The Villari effect changes the magnetic permeability along one diagonal and reduces it along the other. The coils see a change in inductance or induced voltage. The output is proportional to torque over a limited range. Such sensors are non-contact and need no slip rings on a rotating shaft.

A magnetostrictive delay line sends a current pulse through a wire or ribbon. The pulse makes a local strain and a magnetic wave. A pickup coil at a known distance detects the wave. The delay is set by length and by the speed of the magneto-elastic wave. A force on the line changes the speed or the loss, so the delay or amplitude carries the measurement. These lines also make delay elements in older memories and filters.

A magnetostrictive ultrasonic transducer uses a coil around a nickel or Terfenol-D rod. An AC current at the resonant frequency makes the rod expand and contract and launch sound. At the receiving end, sound strains the rod and the coil gives a voltage. This is the magnetic cousin of the piezoelectric transducer in Section 4.1. Sonar, welding, cleaning and level sensing use it where ruggedness matters.

The strengths are high force capacity, a solid metal body, tolerance of high temperature, and simple coils with no sliding contacts. The weaknesses are hysteresis, nonlinearity, eddy-current loss at high frequency, and a need for stable bias.

Bias, limits and signal conditioning

Bias is the first design choice. A small permanent magnet in the rod or a DC current in a bias coil moves the operating point away from the flat, quadratic zero-field region, where the slope dλ/dH is nearly zero, to the steepest, most linear part of the λ-H curve. The AC drive or the stress then rides on a steady field. The output becomes larger and more linear, but the bias itself must be stable; a drift in bias looks like a drift in force.

The second choice is mechanical prestress. A compressed rod can push and pull around a mean stress. Prestress also reduces the effect of temperature and makes the strain more repeatable. The rod and the coils are mounted in a rigid frame. The frame must take the reaction force without bending; otherwise the sensor reads its own mounting.

Signal conditioning is simple: an AC bridge or a tuned coil for the drive, a small induced voltage from the pickup, then a filter and a demodulator. The output is a DC voltage or a digital delay. Hysteresis, eddy currents and temperature are the main errors. Nickel is cheap and stable; Metglas gives fast response; Terfenol-D gives large strain and high force but is costly and brittle. Use the sensor when you need high force and a tough body, not when you need micrometre accuracy.

Worked example

Question. A Terfenol-D rod is 50 mm long. At a bias field its magnetostriction is 1200 ppm. Find the change in length in mm and in μm.

  1. Convert the strain to a dimensionless fraction: λ = 1200 ppm = 1200 × 10⁻⁶ = 1.200 × 10⁻³.
  2. Write the original length in mm: L = 50 mm.
  3. Use ΔL = λ L = (1.200 × 10⁻³) × 50 mm = 0.060 mm.
  4. Convert to micrometres: 0.060 mm × 1000 μm/mm = 60 μm.

Answer. The rod changes length by 0.060 mm, which is 60 μm.

Exam traps

  • Do not use λ = ΔL/L with L in mm and ΔL in μm; convert both to the same unit first.
  • Magnetostriction is not linear without bias. A single sensitivity number is valid only near the chosen bias field.
  • The Villari effect is the inverse of Joule magnetostriction; the Wiedemann effect is twist, not simple expansion.
  • A magnetostrictive transducer is not a Hall sensor. Hall sensors read field with a semiconductor; magnetostrictive sensors change length or magnetization.
  • Hysteresis means the same stress can give different output on loading and unloading; do not calibrate one direction only.

4.6 Photoelectric transducers and fibre optic sensors

Photoelectric sensors convert light into an electrical signal. A source sends a beam, and a receiver measures how much light arrives. If an object blocks, reflects or bends the beam, the receiver current changes and the sensor gives a switching or measurement output. Fibre optic sensors use the same idea but guide light inside a thin glass or plastic fibre, so the electronics can stay far from a harsh or live measurement point. You meet them in conveyor counting, safety guards, banknote scanners and strain or temperature monitoring on bridges.

You need: basic optics of reflection and refraction, and the idea of a transducer from 4.1.

Photoelectric effect and three beam arrangements

Photoelectric transducers use the photoelectric effect: light of frequency f delivers photons of energy E = hf. Here h is Planck's constant and f is frequency in hertz. If E is larger than the material's work function Φ, electrons escape and create current. Below the threshold frequency, brighter light gives no electrons. Above it, more light gives more current. A photoelectric sensor therefore matches its light source to a receiver that responds to that wavelength.

Fibre structure, total internal reflection and numerical aperture

A fibre has a core of refractive index n1 surrounded by cladding of refractive index n2, with n1 > n2. Light entering the core hits the core-cladding boundary. If the angle inside the core is greater than the critical angle θc, total internal reflection occurs and light stays in the core. The critical angle satisfies sin θc = n2/n1.

NA = √(n1² − n2²)

NA is the numerical aperture, a dimensionless number; n1 is the core refractive index; n2 is the cladding refractive index.

θa = sin⁻¹(NA)

θa is the acceptance angle, the largest half-angle at the input for which light is guided; NA is the numerical aperture.

  1. An LED launches light into the core.
  2. The core guides light by total internal reflection.
  3. A sensor head changes the guided light by bending, pressure or a shutter.
  4. A photodiode converts the received light into current.

Fibre optic sensing, loss and applications

An intensity-modulated fibre sensor makes the sensor head change how much light reaches the photodiode. Bending, pressure or displacement moves a shutter or changes coupling, so received power changes. The reading is a change in light, not the absolute light level. Source drift can look like a measurement change, so a reference channel or ratiometric reading is used.

Loss(dB) = 10 log₁₀(Pin / Pout)

Pin is input optical power in mW or W; Pout is output optical power in the same unit; Loss(dB) is positive when Pout < Pin.

A fibre Bragg grating is a periodic change in the core refractive index. It reflects one wavelength. Strain or temperature shifts that wavelength, so the reading is encoded in wavelength rather than brightness.

  • Fibre sensors are immune to electrical noise because the signal is light.
  • They have no sparks at the measurement point and suit flammable areas.
  • They are small and can work over long distances.
  • Uses include counting, safety curtains, temperature and strain monitoring.

Question. A fibre optic link launches 1 mW and receives 0.2 mW after 2 km. Find the loss in dB and in dB/km.

Exam traps

  • A fibre sensor reads a change in light. If the LED output drifts, the receiver sees a change that is not from the measurand. Use a reference channel or ratiometric measurement.
  • Through-beam, retroreflective and diffuse are not the same. Through-beam needs an emitter and a separate receiver; diffuse uses the object as the reflector.
  • Loss in dB is positive when Pout < Pin. Use 10 log₁₀ for optical power, not 20 log₁₀.
  • Numerical aperture is not the acceptance angle. NA is a number; the acceptance angle is sin⁻¹(NA) in degrees or radians.
  • The photoelectric effect has a threshold frequency. Increasing intensity below threshold does not start emission.

4.7 Digital displacement sensors and position encoders

A position encoder turns displacement into a digital number. An incremental encoder sends pulses; counting them gives how far a shaft or slide moved. An absolute encoder gives a full code at every position, correct as soon as power comes on. Output is already digital, so no ADC is needed, the value does not drift along the cable, and a microcontroller reads it directly. CNC machines, printer paper feeds, robot joints and servo motors use encoders.

You need resolution from Unit 1 and the idea of a light beam interrupted from photoelectric transducers.

Incremental encoders: channels A, B and Z

A rotary incremental encoder is a disc with N slots at equal angles, spinning between a light-emitting diode (LED) and a photodetector. Light through a slot makes the detector conduct; the solid part blocks light. The detector current becomes a square wave with N pulses per revolution.

A second track B has windows a quarter of a slot pitch ahead of track A, so B leads A by 90° of the pulse cycle. A third track, the index or Z channel, has one window and gives one pulse per revolution.

A linear scale works the same way. A glass or steel rule carries a grating with pitch p, the distance between lines, often 20 µm. The read head slides along it and produces the same A and B pulses. To use an incremental encoder: count pulses from A and B; bring the shaft to the Z pulse once at start-up to fix mechanical zero; multiply count by resolution to get displacement.

resolution = p / 4

p is grating pitch in µm on a linear scale (or slot pitch in degrees on a rotary disc); the answer carries the same unit as p, per count: µm per count for a linear scale, degrees per count for a rotary one. For p = 20 µm, resolution = 5 µm.

Quadrature decoding: four counts per slot

Channels A and B are two square waves 90° apart. As a two-bit code the pair walks through 00 → 01 → 11 → 10 → 00. Four edges occur per slot, so a decoder counting every edge gets four counts per slot: ×4 or full quadrature decoding.

resolution is the smallest angle one count represents, in degrees; N is slots per revolution; 360° is one full turn.

Absolute encoders: binary, Gray code, magnetic and optical

An absolute encoder has N concentric tracks on one disc, each read by its own LED and photodetector. The N-bit word read in parallel is different for every position, giving 2^N positions. No counting and no homing: position is on the wires immediately.

Plain binary code has a flaw. Going from 0111 to 1000, all four bits must change together. If the most-significant-bit track's window is a little wider, or the head is tilted, the read head can see 1111 for a moment — a large false jump. Gray code fixes this: only one bit changes between neighbouring positions, so the worst boundary error is one count.

The figure shows three Gray-code tracks. Read column by column: each column differs from the one beside it in exactly one track. Software converts the Gray word to binary, then to an angle, by repeated exclusive-OR from the most significant bit down.

Construction splits into two types. Optical encoders give high resolution on servo motors and CNC axes. Magnetic encoders magnetise a drum in a coded pattern and read it with Hall sensors (see Hall effect sensors); they cost less and survive dust, oil and vibration, so they suit machine tools and vehicle steering.

θ_res = 360° / 2^N

θ_res is smallest angle one code word represents, in degrees; N is number of tracks; 2^N is number of positions per revolution.

Choosing between the two families

  • Incremental: few wires, cheap, very high resolution; count is lost at power-off and needs homing.
  • Absolute: position survives power-off, no homing; more wires, higher cost.
  • Optical: fine pitch, high resolution; needs clean dry environment.
  • Magnetic: coarser but sealed and rugged; works in coolant spray and dust.

4.8 Tachogenerators, stroboscope and variable frequency sensors

Speed is everywhere on a plant floor: motor shafts, turbine rotors, paper rolls. Three cheap ways turn speed into an electrical reading. A tachogenerator is a small generator whose voltage rises with speed. A stroboscope flashes light at a known rate and freezes the shaft for the eye. Variable-frequency sensors carry the measurement in a signal's frequency, read by a counter or a frequency-to-voltage circuit.

You need: Faraday's law of induction from basic physics, and the first- and second-order response from Unit 1, Foundations: Sensor Characteristics and Errors.

DC and AC tachogenerators

A tachogenerator is a small machine coupled to the shaft. A permanent magnet gives a constant field, so the induced emf depends only on speed.

E = K ω

E is the generated emf in volts (V); ω is the angular speed in rad/s; K is the machine constant in V·s/rad.

The DC output ripples at fripple = S n / 60, with S commutator segments and n the speed in rpm. An RC filter with cutoff 1/(2πRC) well below fripple smooths it.

Loading is the main error: the meter draws current I and the armature resistance Ra drops V = E − I Ra, so a low-impedance meter reads low. Use a high-impedance meter.

An AC tachogenerator has no commutator or brushes. Its amplitude rises with speed and its frequency is f = p n / 60, with p the number of pole pairs and n the speed in rpm. Rectify and filter it, or count the frequency.

Stroboscope and aliasing

A stroboscope is a xenon tube flashing at a rate f set by an oscillator. Paint z marks on the shaft; the eye sees a mark only when a flash lights it.

f = k z n / 60

f is the flash rate in flashes per second (Hz); z is the number of marks painted on the shaft; n is the true speed in rpm; k = 1, 2, 3, … The image looks still whenever this holds.

At k = 1 the dial reads the true speed. At k = 2 or 3 the image is still but you see two or three copies of the mark, and the dial reads two or three times the true speed. At a whole-number fraction of the fundamental you see one still mark again and read half, or a third, of the true speed. This is aliasing.

Set the flash rate high, well above the expected speed, and lower it. The first single still image you reach is the true speed, f = z n / 60. A slow drift means the rate is slightly off, which lets you tune exactly.

Uses: calibrating tachometers, checking fan blades, watching a loudspeaker cone, inspecting printing registration.

Variable-frequency sensors and frequency-to-voltage conversion

A vibrating wire of length L, tension T and mass per length μ vibrates at f = (1/2L)√(T/μ), so pressure on a capsule becomes a frequency. A reluctance pick-up facing a toothed wheel gives one pulse per tooth.

Frequency signals travel well: cable resistance and amplifier gain do not change the count. A counter gates pulses for a time Tg and shows N = f × Tg counts, so the resolution is 1/N.

Vout = Vref × T × fin

Vout is the average DC output in volts; Vref is the pulse height in volts; T is the fixed pulse width in seconds set by a monostable; fin is the input frequency in Hz.

The formula holds for fin ≤ 1/T; above 1/T the pulses run together, the monostable stays high, and Vout flattens at Vref. Set T for the highest frequency you expect to measure.

One monostable plus an RC averaging filter turns any frequency sensor into a DC voltage read as speed, force or pressure. Counting pulses in position encoders is the digital alternative.

Worked example

Question. A DC tachogenerator with K = 0.05 V/(rad/s) runs at 1500 rpm; the commutator has 12 segments. Find (a) the emf, (b) the ripple frequency, (c) where to put the filter cutoff.

  1. ω = 1500 × 2π / 60 = 157.08 rad/s.
  2. E = K ω = 0.05 × 157.08 = 7.85 V.
  3. fripple = S n / 60 = 12 × 1500 / 60 = 300 Hz.
  4. Cutoff near 30 Hz, a tenth of the ripple, damps it about ten times while the speed signal passes.

Answer. 7.85 V at 1500 rpm, ripple at 300 Hz, cutoff near 30 Hz.

Worked example 2

Question. A pick-up on a 60-tooth gear feeds 200 µs pulses of height 5 V to an RC averaging filter. The output is 0.25 V. Find the pulse frequency and the shaft speed in rpm.

  1. fin = Vout / (Vref T) = 0.25 / (5 × 200 × 10⁻⁶) = 250 Hz.
  2. One pulse per tooth: n = 60 fin / 60 = 250 rpm.

Answer. The pulse frequency is 250 Hz and the shaft turns at 250 rpm.

Exam traps

  • rad/s against rpm: dropping the 2π/60 factor makes the answer wrong by 9.55 times. Convert first, always.
  • The stroboscope also freezes the image at k = 2 or 3, so the dial shows two or three times the true speed. Lower the rate from a high value and stop at the first single still image.
  • A low-impedance voltmeter loads a DC tachogenerator and reads low because of the I Ra drop; examiners ask why the open-circuit emf is quoted.
  • In a frequency-to-voltage converter the scale is Vref × T, not Vref × Tg; mixing the pulse width with the counter gate time gives a wrong volts-per-hertz figure.

4.9 Force-balance transducers

A force-balance transducer does not read how far a spring bends. It detects a tiny beam motion and drives a coil current until the coil's magnetic force cancels the input force. The coil current then measures force or pressure. This closed loop gives high accuracy and a current output that survives long cables. It appears in pressure transmitters, precision weighing and servo accelerometers.

You need: feedback and amplifiers (see Unit 6, Signal Conditioning, Data Acquisition and Actuators) and linearity and hysteresis (see Unit 1, Foundations: Sensor Characteristics and Errors).

How the loop balances force

An input force pushes a balance beam. A displacement sensor sees the small motion. An error amplifier drives current through a coil in a magnetic gap. The coil force opposes the input. The beam settles where the two forces are equal.

F = B I L

F is force in newtons (N); B is gap flux density in tesla (T); I is coil current in amperes (A); L is total conductor length in the coil in metres (m).

  1. Apply the input force to the beam.
  2. Sense the small displacement and amplify it as an error signal.
  3. Drive the coil; its force pushes the beam back.
  4. Read the coil current at balance; it equals the input force divided by B L.

The beam moves only micrometres, so spring creep and hysteresis hardly affect the reading. Only a few universities teach this loop in detail.

Linearity, speed and stability

Closed-loop sensitivity is 1/(B L) amperes per newton. B and L are stable, so linearity comes from the coil and magnet, not the spring.

High loop gain keeps the error small. Too much gain with beam mass and sensor lag causes oscillation. Response is set by beam dynamics; a pressure transmitter settles in tens of milliseconds.

A stable magnet and a stiff coil former matter; temperature changes in the magnet shift the span.

  • Current output is immune to cable resistance.
  • Zero and span are set electrically.
  • An overload stop protects the beam.

Applications

A pressure transmitter uses a diaphragm to turn pressure into force on the beam. A weighing platform does the same with weight. A force-balance accelerometer uses a seismic mass; acceleration becomes force. The output is a current, often 4–20 mA, easy to send to a control room.

Worked example

Question. A force-balance pressure transmitter has a coil of total conductor length 50 m in a magnetic gap of flux density 0.25 T. The input force from the diaphragm is 2 N. Find the coil current at balance.

  1. At balance the coil force equals the input force: F = B I L.
  2. Rearrange: I = F / (B L).
  3. Product B L = 0.25 T × 50 m = 12.5 T·m.
  4. I = 2 N / 12.5 T·m = 0.16 A.

Answer. The coil current is 0.16 A, which the transmitter sends as its output signal.

Exam traps

  • Do not compute the current from the spring deflection; the spring is not the measuring element.
  • Open-loop testing gives the wrong answer: with no feedback the beam moves and the coil current is not proportional to force.
  • L is the total conductor length in metres, not the number of turns; B is in tesla, not gauss (1 T = 10⁴ G).
  • A stiffer spring does not improve accuracy; loop gain and coil constants do.
  • Sign: the coil force must oppose the input force, so the amplifier polarity matters.

4.10 Phototube, photomultiplier tube and scintillation counter

A phototube turns light into electrons in a vacuum. A photomultiplier tube (PMT) adds a chain of dynodes, so one photon can release a measurable pulse of about a million electrons. A scintillation counter puts a scintillator in front: radiation makes a flash of light, and the PMT turns that flash into an electrical pulse. These devices count single photons and nuclear particles. They appear in radiation survey meters, gamma cameras for medical imaging, X-ray and gamma spectroscopy, and low-light research.

You need: the photoelectric effect and light-to-current ideas from 4.6, and the static characteristics sensitivity and resolution from Unit 1.

Phototube: light to electrons

A phototube is a sealed glass tube with a vacuum inside. Its inside surface carries a photocathode: a thin layer of a material such as caesium antimonide that emits electrons when light falls on it. A metal anode sits a few centimetres away. A voltage supply, usually 100 V to 300 V, makes the anode positive with respect to the photocathode. Light enters through a clear window and hits the photocathode. If a photon has enough energy, it ejects an electron by the photoelectric effect. The electric field pulls the electron to the anode and a current flows in the external circuit.

Ip = η e Φp

Ip is the photocurrent in amperes; η is the quantum efficiency, the number of electrons per incident photon (0 to 1); e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C is the electron charge; Φp is the number of photons arriving per second.

The current is small, often nanoamperes or less. The tube has no internal gain. Its response is fast because electrons cross a short gap in vacuum. The dark current is the small current that flows with no light, caused by thermionic emission from the photocathode and leakage. Cooling the tube reduces thermionic dark current. A phototube is used where a simple, fast light detector is enough, for example in old colourimeters and flame detectors.

Photomultiplier tube: dynode gain

A PMT is a phototube with extra electrodes called dynodes between the photocathode and the anode. Each dynode is held about 80 V to 150 V more positive than the one before. The electron emitted from the photocathode is called a photoelectron. It is accelerated into dynode 1. It hits with enough energy to knock out several secondary electrons. These electrons are accelerated to dynode 2, and each knocks out more. The chain gives an avalanche.

G = δⁿ

G is the total gain (dimensionless); δ is the secondary emission ratio, the number of secondary electrons released per incident electron at one dynode; n is the number of dynodes. The anode current is Ia = G Ik, where Ik is the photocathode current.

With δ = 4 and n = 10, G = 1,048,576. The anode charge for N photoelectrons is Q = N e G. Two limits matter: timing and dark current. Transit time is the average time an electron takes to travel from the photocathode to the anode. The spread in these arrival times, called transit-time spread, limits timing resolution; the finite pulse width and the dead time limit the count rate. Dark current is multiplied by G, so a PMT can produce dark pulses with no light. Cooling and a stable high-voltage supply reduce these problems.

Scintillation counter: radiation to light to pulse

A scintillation counter detects radiation that produces too little light on its own to be sensed directly, so a scintillator first converts the radiation into a light flash. A scintillator is a material that absorbs radiation and emits visible light. Sodium iodide activated with thallium, written NaI(Tl), is common for gamma rays. It is a transparent crystal, and it gives a light flash when a gamma photon deposits energy. The flash enters the PMT photocathode. The PMT multiplies the photoelectrons and gives one voltage pulse per radiation event.

The pulse height is proportional to the light flash, and for full energy absorption the flash is proportional to the radiation energy. A multichannel analyser sorts pulses by height into an energy spectrum. The count rate gives intensity; the peak position gives the isotope or the energy. This is gamma spectroscopy. A discriminator rejects small dark pulses. The dead time is the short interval after a pulse when the system cannot count another; it limits high rates.

Uses include radiation survey meters, gamma cameras in nuclear medicine, PET scanners, X-ray astronomy, and checking gamma sources in industry. NaI(Tl) is hygroscopic, so it must be sealed. The PMT must be optically coupled to the crystal so that light reaches the photocathode.

Worked example

Question. A photomultiplier tube has 10 dynodes. Each dynode releases 4 secondary electrons for every electron that hits it. The photocathode emits 100 photoelectrons in one light pulse. Find the total gain, the number of electrons at the anode, and the anode charge. Use e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C.

  1. Gain per stage δ = 4; number of stages n = 10.
  2. Total gain G = δⁿ = 4¹⁰ = 1,048,576.
  3. Anode electrons Na = N G = 100 × 1,048,576 = 104,857,600 = 1.048576×10⁸.
  4. Charge Q = Na e = 1.048576×10⁸ × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C = 1.6777216×10⁻¹¹ C ≈ 1.68×10⁻¹¹ C.

Answer. Gain = 1.048576×10⁶; anode electrons = 1.048576×10⁸; charge ≈ 1.68×10⁻¹¹ C.

Exam traps

  • Gain is δⁿ, not nδ: 10 dynodes with δ = 4 give 1,048,576, not 40.
  • Dark current is multiplied by the same gain, so a PMT shows dark counts; cooling the photocathode reduces them.
  • A phototube has no gain; only the PMT has dynodes. Do not use the two names interchangeably.
  • In a scintillation counter the scintillator makes light; the PMT only multiplies photoelectrons. A gamma ray is not counted directly by the PMT.
  • Pulse height gives energy, pulse count rate gives intensity. Do not use count rate to identify the isotope energy.

4.11 Photodiodes, phototransistors, photovoltaic and photoconductive cells; spectrophotometric uses

Semiconductor photodevices turn light into an electrical signal. A photodiode gives a current, a phototransistor gives a larger current, a photovoltaic cell gives a voltage, and a photoconductive cell changes resistance. These devices are cheap, small and fast, so they appear in light meters, remote controls, solar panels, safety curtains and laboratory instruments. A spectrophotometer uses them to measure how much light a solution absorbs; from that absorption it finds concentration. This chapter explains each device, compares them, and then shows the source, monochromator, sample and detector path of a spectrophotometer with the Beer-Lambert law.

You need: photoelectric transducers and fibre sensors and a basic picture of a semiconductor pn junction.

Photodiode, phototransistor, photovoltaic and photoconductive cells

A photodiode is a reverse-biased pn junction. Light absorbed in the depletion region creates electron-hole pairs. The electric field sweeps electrons to the n side and holes to the p side, so an external circuit sees a photocurrent. Reverse bias widens the depletion layer, lowers junction capacitance and makes the response faster. The photocurrent is Ip = R P, where Ip is photocurrent in A, P is incident optical power in W, and R is responsivity in A/W. Responsivity depends on wavelength and on the semiconductor.

Ip = R P

Ip is photocurrent in A; R is responsivity in A/W; P is incident optical power in W.

Dark current Id is the small reverse current with no light. It adds an offset and shot noise. A PIN photodiode puts an intrinsic layer between p and n. The thicker absorption layer raises responsivity and keeps capacitance low. An avalanche photodiode uses a high field to create impact ionization and internal gain, but it needs a higher bias and adds excess noise.

  • Phototransistor: a photodiode drives the base of a transistor, so the current is multiplied by current gain β. It is more sensitive but slower than a photodiode.
  • Photovoltaic cell: an unbiased pn junction. Light produces a forward voltage and can deliver power to a load; it is a solar cell, not a linear light sensor.
  • Photoconductive cell or LDR: light lowers the resistance of a semiconductor film, often CdS. It is cheap and simple but slow, nonlinear and has memory effects.

Spectral response and comparison

Spectral response is the device output plotted against wavelength λ. The band gap sets the long-wavelength limit: a photon with energy hν below the band gap is not absorbed, where h is Planck constant and ν is optical frequency. Silicon peaks in the near infrared near 850–900 nm; indium gallium arsenide works near 1300–1550 nm for fibre links; cadmium sulphide responds in the visible. Always state the wavelength when you quote responsivity.

Spectrophotometer and the Beer-Lambert law

A spectrophotometer measures absorption at one chosen wavelength. A source gives white light: tungsten lamp for visible, deuterium lamp for ultraviolet, or an LED for a fixed wavelength. A monochromator uses a prism or diffraction grating to pass a narrow band. The beam passes through a cuvette of path length l and reaches a detector such as a photodiode, phototransistor or photomultiplier tube. The readout compares the sample with a blank.

  1. Set the wavelength and zero the instrument with a blank cuvette.
  2. Measure the blank intensity I₀.
  3. Measure the sample intensity I.
  4. Compute transmittance T = I / I₀ and absorbance A = −log₁₀ T.
  5. Use A = ε c l to find concentration c when ε and l are known.

T = I / I₀

T is transmittance, dimensionless; I is transmitted light intensity; I₀ is incident or blank light intensity.

A = −log₁₀ T = log₁₀(I₀ / I)

A is absorbance, dimensionless; T is transmittance; I and I₀ are transmitted and incident intensities.

A = ε c l

A is absorbance; ε is molar absorptivity in L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹; c is concentration in mol/L; l is path length in cm.

Absorbance is logarithmic, so it adds when samples add. A = 0.30 means I/I₀ = 10⁻⁰·³⁰ = 0.50, so half the light is transmitted. The linear range is limited: too high c gives A above about 2, and stray light and detector nonlinearity bend the curve. Use a dilution or a shorter cell. Applications include dyes and inks, protein assays, nitrate and phosphate in water, haemoglobin in clinical chemistry, drug dissolution and HPLC detection.

Worked example

Question. A spectrophotometer reads absorbance A = 0.30 in a 1.00 cm cell. The molar absorptivity at that wavelength is 1500 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Find the concentration in mol/L.

  1. Write the Beer-Lambert law: A = ε c l.
  2. List the known values: A = 0.30, ε = 1500 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹, l = 1.00 cm.
  3. Rearrange for concentration: c = A / (ε l).
  4. Compute the denominator: ε l = 1500 × 1.00 = 1500 L mol⁻¹.
  5. Divide: c = 0.30 / 1500 = 2.0×10⁻⁴ mol/L.

Answer. The concentration is 2.0×10⁻⁴ mol/L.

Exam traps

  • Absorbance is logarithmic. Doubling concentration doubles A only inside the linear range; it does not double transmittance.
  • A = ε c l needs c in mol/L and l in cm. Convert a path length given in mm or a concentration given in mmol/L before dividing.
  • Dark current is not a light signal. It adds an offset, so zero or subtract it correctly and do not treat it as absorbance.
  • Responsivity in A/W is wavelength dependent. A value quoted at 850 nm cannot be used at 1550 nm without a new calibration.
  • An LDR is slow and nonlinear; a photovoltaic cell is a power source. Neither is a good choice for fast, precise spectrophotometry.

Takeaways

  1. Piezoelectric sensors suit dynamic force, pressure, vibration and ultrasound; a charge amplifier conditions their tiny charge.
  2. Ultrasonic sensors use echo time or Doppler shift for distance, level and flow without touching the target.
  3. A seismic mass on a spring and damper reads acceleration below resonance and displacement above resonance.
  4. Hall effect sensors measure magnetic field, current or position without contact.
  5. Magnetostrictive and magneto-elastic sensors link stress and magnetization, so they sense force, torque and position and make sound waves.
  6. Photoelectric and fibre optic sensors convert light changes into electrical signals and allow remote or distributed measurement.
  7. Incremental encoders count pulses, while absolute encoders give a unique code for every position.
  8. Speed can come from a tachogenerator voltage, a stroboscope flash or a signal frequency; force-balance feedback gives high accuracy.

Glossary

Absolute encoder
Position sensor that gives a unique digital code for each shaft position.
Absorbance
Measure of light absorbed by a sample, A = log₁₀(I₀ / I).
Accelerometer
Sensor that gives an output proportional to acceleration, often using a seismic mass.
Acoustic impedance
Product of density and sound speed, Z = ρ c, that controls reflection at a boundary.
Charge amplifier
Circuit that converts a piezoelectric charge into a voltage while keeping input impedance high.
Dynode
Secondary-emission electrode inside a photomultiplier tube.
Force-balance transducer
Sensor that uses feedback force to cancel an input force, so coil current measures the input.
Hall coefficient
Material constant R_H = 1/(n q) that relates Hall voltage to current density and magnetic field.
Hall effect
Creation of a transverse voltage when a current-carrying conductor is placed in a magnetic field.
Incremental encoder
Position sensor that gives pulses as the shaft moves, so position is counted from a datum.
Magneto-elastic effect
Change of magnetization or permeability of a material when it is stressed.
Magnetostriction
Change of length of a magnetic material when it is magnetized.
Optical fibre
Thin glass or plastic strand that guides light by total internal reflection.
Photoconductive cell
Light-dependent resistor whose resistance falls as illumination increases.
Photodiode
Semiconductor junction that gives a current proportional to incident light.
Photoelectric transducer
Sensor that converts light intensity into an electrical current or voltage.
Photomultiplier tube
Phototube with dynodes that multiply emitted electrons to give high sensitivity.
Phototransistor
Bipolar transistor whose collector current is controlled by light falling on the base region.
Phototube
Vacuum tube with a photocathode that emits electrons when light falls on it.
Photovoltaic cell
Junction device that produces a voltage when light falls on it.
Piezoelectricity
Property by which a crystal produces charge under mechanical stress and changes shape under voltage.
Scintillator
Material that emits light when ionizing radiation passes through it.
Seismic mass
Proof mass mounted on a spring and damper whose motion indicates vibration.
Stroboscope
Instrument with a flashing lamp used to freeze or measure periodic motion.
Tachogenerator
Small generator used to produce a voltage proportional to rotational speed.
Ultrasonic transducer
Device that sends and receives sound waves above 20 kHz.
