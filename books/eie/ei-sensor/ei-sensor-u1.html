1.1 Classification of sensors; active and passive sensors

A sensor must hand over what it measures as an electrical signal, and there are only two ways to do it. Either the sensor makes its own emf (electromotive force, a generated voltage) from the measurand's energy, or it changes its impedance and lets an external supply deliver the current. The first kind is called active or self-generating, the second passive or externally energised. This one label decides the wiring: a high-impedance load for the first, an excitation source plus a bridge or divider for the second. Choosing sensors for a plant, a car or a phone starts here.

You need: basic circuits — Ohm's law, ideal voltage and current sources, and power P = VI = I²R.

Sensor, transducer, actuator

A sensor responds to a physical quantity and gives an output that carries information about it. The quantity being measured is the measurand: the temperature of a motor bearing, the pressure in a pipeline, the light falling on a solar panel.

A transducer converts one form of energy into another. Every sensor is a transducer, but we say sensor when we care about the measurement and transducer when we care about the conversion. An actuator works the other way: electrical input, physical output, such as the solenoid that closes a valve. An inverse transducer converts an electrical input into a non-electrical output — for example a piezoelectric crystal driven by a voltage to launch ultrasound, or the pen motor that moves the pen of a chart recorder.

Most measurement devices follow the same chain, from the physical world to a number on a display:

  1. Measurand — the quantity to be measured, for example the temperature of a bearing.
  2. Sensing element — the part that first responds, such as a diaphragm or a thermistor bead.
  3. Variable-conversion element — turns that response into an electrical variable: resistance, charge, inductance or emf.
  4. Variable-manipulation element — amplifies, filters and converts it, ending as a voltage or a digital count.
  5. Output stage — a display, a recorder or a controller input.

Some syllabi call this the general input-output configuration of a measurement system. Not every device has all five stages. A thermocouple's conversion element produces an emf directly, so a separate conversion stage does not exist.

Active and passive sensors

Classify by energy source and the answer is one of two words. An active sensor (also called self-generating) converts the measurand's energy directly into electrical energy. It needs no external supply. A passive sensor (also called externally energised) generates no signal of its own. It converts the measurand into a change of resistance, inductance or capacitance, and an external supply must be present for that change to become a signal.

  • Active: thermocouple (Seebeck emf), piezoelectric crystal (charge on stress), photovoltaic cell (light gives emf), tachogenerator (emf proportional to speed).
  • Passive: RTD (resistance temperature detector), thermistor, strain gauge, potentiometer, LVDT (linear variable differential transformer), photoconductive cell, capacitive sensor.

The wiring consequence in one line: an active sensor is a voltage or charge source and needs a high-impedance load; a passive sensor is a variable impedance and needs excitation plus a bridge or divider.

ΔV = I × ΔR

ΔV is the signal voltage in volts, I is the excitation current in amperes, and ΔR is the change in sensor resistance in ohms. The current comes from the external supply, not from the sensor.

Two practical details follow. For an active sensor the output is often small — millivolts — so the amplifier's input impedance must be far larger than the sensor's own source resistance, otherwise the emf is loaded down. For a passive sensor the excitation is a design choice: too small and the signal is buried in noise, too large and the current heats the sensor and shifts its own reading.

Other classification axes

Energy source is the most useful split, but exams also ask for the others. By output form, a sensor is analog (a continuous voltage, like a thermocouple) or digital (pulses or a code word, like a shaft encoder). Digital output is far more immune to noise over long cable than an analog voltage, though the cable and grounding still have to be done properly. By physical principle, sensors are resistive, inductive, capacitive, piezoelectric, thermoelectric, photoelectric or Hall-effect; each is a small physics experiment, as 1.2 explains. By contact, a sensor touches the process (an RTD inside a tank) or does not (an infrared thermometer, an ultrasonic level sensor). Non-contact sensing suits moving, hot or sterile objects, because it does not load them. By application, we speak of industrial, biomedical, automotive, aerospace or domestic sensors.

Worked example

Question. Classify each device as active or passive, and justify with one number where the data allows. (a) A Pt100 RTD reads 100.0 Ω at 0 °C and is run at 1 mA excitation. (b) A piezoelectric accelerometer gives 12 mV per g (g = 9.81 m/s², the acceleration due to gravity) with no supply connected. (c) A photodiode in photovoltaic mode gives 0.45 V in sunlight. (d) The same photodiode in photoconductive mode with 5 V reverse bias.

  1. (a) The excitation current is I = 1 mA = 1×10⁻³ A and the resistance is R = 100.0 Ω. The power dissipated in the RTD is P = I²R = (1×10⁻³)² × 100.0 = 1×10⁻⁶ × 100.0 = 1×10⁻⁴ W, that is 0.10 mW.
  2. That 0.10 mW comes from the external supply. The RTD only sets how much current flows, so it is passive, and the excitation value fixes the self-heating error.
  3. (b) 12 mV per g appears with no supply at all, so the crystal generates the emf from the applied force. Active.
  4. (c) 0.45 V in sunlight with no bias supply: light energy is converted into electrical energy. Active.
  5. (d) With 5 V reverse bias the diode needs an external supply, and the light only changes the size of the bias current. Passive.
  6. Rule used throughout: ask where the energy in the output comes from. Measurand → active. External supply → passive.

Answer. (a) Passive, self-heating 0.10 mW from 1 mA through 100.0 Ω; (b) active; (c) active; (d) passive.

Worked example 2

Question. An active sensor has an open-circuit emf of 25 mV and a source resistance of 0.5 kΩ. It feeds an amplifier of input impedance 1 MΩ. What voltage reaches the amplifier?

  1. This is a divider between source resistance R_s = 500 Ω and input resistance R_in = 1×10⁶ Ω.
  2. Fraction reaching the input = R_in / (R_in + R_s) = 1 000 000 / 1 000 500 = 0.99950.
  3. Voltage at the input = 25 mV × 0.99950 = 24.99 mV.
  4. The loss is only 0.01 mV, which is why active sensors are specified against a high-impedance load.

Answer. 24.99 mV reaches the amplifier, a loss of 0.01 mV from loading.

Exam traps

  • Marking a photodiode passive by habit. It is active in photovoltaic mode (no bias, light makes the emf) and passive in photoconductive mode (reverse bias, light changes the current).
  • Confusing the sensing element with the transducer. The diaphragm is the sensing element; the capacitive pressure transducer is the whole device with its terminals.
  • Calling a passive sensor 'no power needed'. It generates no signal of its own and cannot work without excitation — without the supply the output is zero. Note that it does draw current from that supply, which is why excitation level matters.
  • Forgetting that excitation changes the reading. A passive sensor's own heating depends on I² × R, so excitation is part of the measurement design, not an afterthought.
  • Writing 'active sensor' for any sensor that needs a power supply to run its amplifier. The label describes the sensing element only.

1.2 Physical principles of sensing

A sensor turns a physical stimulus into a measurable electrical change. The trick is not electrical: it is a known physical law. A force bends a beam and changes strain gauge resistance; two dissimilar metals joined at two junctions, with the junctions at different temperatures, make a small emf; light creates charge carriers. The principles group by energy domain — mechanical, thermal, electrical, magnetic, radiant, chemical — and name the measurand, the intermediate variable and the electrical output. The same law often serves two measurands; the packaging, not the equation, decides what the sensor measures. That is how a phone knows orientation and a car knows tyre pressure.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for active and passive vocabulary, and basic electricity and magnetism — charge, field, potential, Ohm's law and Faraday's law.

The sensing chain

Every sensing path has five stages: stimulus → sensing element → physical law → parameter change → electrical output. The figure shows this chain. The law is the middle: it converts a non-electrical quantity into ΔR, ΔC, ΔL or Δemf. A resistance change needs a bridge or divider; a capacitance change needs an AC bridge or charge amplifier; an emf can go straight to a high-impedance amplifier. A passive sensor changes R, C or L and needs a supply; an active sensor makes its own emf (see Chapter 1.1).

Electrical quantities are the language. Charge Q is in coulombs (C). Note that C is also the usual symbol for capacitance, in farads; here the coulomb and the farad are different quantities. Electric field E = V/d is in volts per metre (V/m), with V the potential difference in volts and d the plate separation in metres. Potential energy per charge is V. For a capacitor, Q = CV, so C = Q/V, in farads (F). A parallel-plate capacitor has C = εA/d, where A is plate area in m², ε = ε₀εᵣ is permittivity in F/m, ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m, and εᵣ is relative permittivity. These equations are why a capacitive sensor needs a changing gap, area or dielectric.

Mechanical and thermal laws

Resistance is R = ρL/A. R is in Ω; ρ is resistivity in Ω·m; L is length in m; A is cross-section in m². Stretching a wire increases L and decreases A, so R rises. The piezoresistive effect writes this as ΔR/R = GF·ε, where GF is the gauge factor (dimensionless), ε is strain (m/m), and ΔR is the change in resistance. The effect works under steady load. A metal strain gauge has GF near 2; a semiconductor gauge is much larger but more temperature-sensitive. Typical use: a load cell weighs a truck. Construction and bridge circuits belong to Unit 2.

Piezoelectricity is different. A stress on quartz or PZT displaces charge: q = d·F, where q is charge in coulombs, d is the charge coefficient in C/N, and F is force in newtons. It gives a voltage across the crystal capacitance. It cannot hold a steady reading because the charge leaks away, so it suits vibration, impact and ultrasonic pulses. See Unit 4 for devices.

Thermal law: the Seebeck effect. Two different metals joined at two points with a temperature difference ΔT produce an emf e = S·ΔT, where S is the Seebeck coefficient in V/K. The measurand is temperature difference; the output is a small dc voltage, often µV to mV. Cold-junction compensation is needed before you can read absolute temperature; thermocouple laws are in Unit 2.

Magnetic, radiant and chemical laws

A coil has L ≈ N²µA/l, where L is inductance in henries (H), N is turns, µ is core permeability in H/m, A is core area in m², and l is magnetic path length in m. Faraday's law gives e = −N·dΦ/dt, where e is induced emf in volts, N is the number of turns, Φ is the magnetic flux through one turn in webers (Wb), and t is time in seconds; the minus sign is Lenz's law. A moving core changes reluctance and therefore L. A magnetostrictive or magneto-elastic material changes shape or permeability under stress, so a coil around it reads force. Hall effect: V_H = K_H·I·B/t, where V_H is Hall voltage in volts, K_H is the Hall coefficient (m³/C), I is current in amperes, B is flux density in tesla, and t is sheet thickness in metres. It senses magnetic field or, with a fixed magnet, position. See Unit 4.

Radiant laws: the photovoltaic effect gives an emf when light creates electron–hole pairs across a junction; the photoconductive effect lowers resistance when photons add carriers. The measurand is irradiance or wavelength; the intermediate is carrier generation; the output is emf or ΔR. A photodiode in a light meter is one use. Photodiodes and phototransistors are in Unit 4.

Chemical: a hygroscopic layer absorbs water, changing its resistance or capacitance. The measurand is relative humidity; the intermediate is water uptake; the output is ΔR or ΔC. A capacitor can sense displacement or humidity: the equation C = εA/d is the same, but one package lets pressure change d, and another lets humidity change εᵣ. Packaging decides. See Unit 5 for humidity sensors.

Worked example

Question. A capacitive pressure sensor has two plates of area A = 25 mm² separated by d = 0.10 mm of air (εᵣ ≈ 1, ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m). Pressure pushes the gap down by 0.01 mm. Find C before and after, the change in pF, the percentage change, and the small-signal sensitivity |dC/dd| at the start in pF/µm.

  1. Convert: A = 25 mm² = 25×10⁻⁶ m². Initial gap d₁ = 0.10 mm = 1.0×10⁻⁴ m. New gap d₂ = 0.10 − 0.01 = 0.09 mm = 9.0×10⁻⁵ m. Air gives ε = ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m.
  2. C₁ = ε₀A/d₁ = (8.854×10⁻¹² × 25×10⁻⁶) / (1.0×10⁻⁴) = 2.2135×10⁻¹² F = 2.21 pF.
  3. C₂ = ε₀A/d₂ = (8.854×10⁻¹² × 25×10⁻⁶) / (9.0×10⁻⁵) = 2.4594×10⁻¹² F = 2.46 pF.
  4. ΔC = C₂ − C₁ = 0.2459×10⁻¹² F = 0.246 pF ≈ 0.25 pF. Percentage change = (0.2459 / 2.2135) × 100 = 11.11 % ≈ 11.1 %.
  5. Small-signal sensitivity at the start: |dC/dd| = ε₀A/d₁² = C₁/d₁ = 2.2135×10⁻¹² / 1.0×10⁻⁴ = 2.2135×10⁻⁸ F/m. Since 1 pF/µm = 1×10⁻⁶ F/m, this is 0.0221 pF/µm ≈ 0.022 pF/µm. The sign is negative physically: C rises as d falls.

Answer. C₁ = 2.21 pF, C₂ = 2.46 pF, ΔC = 0.25 pF (+11.1 %), and |dC/dd| = 0.022 pF/µm.

Exam traps

  • Do not confuse piezoresistive with piezoelectric. Piezoresistive changes resistance under strain and works for steady loads; piezoelectric makes charge under stress and cannot hold a steady reading without charge leakage.
  • The capacitance formula is non-linear in d. A 10 % reduction in gap (d falls to 0.9 d) raises C by about 11 % (1/0.9 = 1.111), not 10 %; a 10 % increase in gap lowers C by about 9.1 %. The relation is non-linear and not symmetric.
  • A Seebeck emf depends on the temperature difference, not the absolute temperature. A thermocouple still needs a cold junction before it reports absolute temperature.
  • In V_H = K_H·I·B/t, the thickness t is in the denominator. Doubling the sheet thickness halves the Hall voltage for the same current and field.
  • A photovoltaic cell gives an emf; a photoconductive cell changes resistance and needs a bias supply. They are not interchangeable circuits.

Analyze The same parallel-plate capacitor can be sold as a displacement sensor or a humidity sensor. What changes in each package, and why is the equation not enough to tell them apart? 3 marks

Both use C = εA/d. In the displacement package, the measurand moves the plates and changes d. In the humidity package, a hygroscopic dielectric absorbs water and changes εᵣ. The equation is identical; the packaging and the exposed material decide which variable the outside world can change.

e = S·ΔT = 40 µV/°C × 25 °C = 1000 µV = 1.0 mV.

Analyze A Hall sensor follows V_H = K_H·I·B/t. What happens to V_H if the active sheet is made thinner, with the same current and field? 3 marks

V_H rises inversely with t. Halving t doubles V_H for the same I and B. Practical limits are resistance, self-heating and mechanical strength of the thin sheet.

1.3 Units, standards and calibration of sensors

A sensor output is a number, and a number means nothing until two people agree what it counts. Units give that agreement; standards give a physical reference; calibration connects the two. Without this chain a pressure reading of 1.98 is just a voltage. This chapter builds the chain from the seven SI base units to the instrument on a plant floor, defines traceability, separates calibration from verification and adjustment, and ends with the static calibration procedure and its certificate.

You need: Chapter 1.2 on what the sensor is converting, so that the input unit and the output unit are both clear before you compare them.

Units come first

A sensor turns a physical quantity into a number. The number stays useless until everyone agrees on the size of the unit. The SI system fixes seven base units: the metre (m) for length, the kilogram (kg) for mass, the second (s) for time, the ampere (A) for current, the kelvin (K) for thermodynamic temperature, the mole (mol) for amount of substance, and the candela (cd) for luminous intensity.

Every other unit on a datasheet is built from these. 1 N = 1 kg·m/s²; 1 Pa = 1 N/m²; 1 V = 1 kg·m²/(s³·A); 1 Ω = 1 V/A; 1 F = 1 C/V; 1 Hz = 1/s. The degree Celsius is a shifted kelvin: t(°C) = T(K) − 273.15, so a temperature difference of 1 °C equals 1 K, but 20 °C is 293.15 K, not 20 K.

Two habits catch most errors. Write the unit beside every number. Run a dimensional check: sensitivity = output unit ÷ input unit. A pressure sensor giving 19.9 mV/kPa has the dimensions of voltage per pressure (V/Pa); the numerical value changes with the unit, the dimension does not. To convert 0.137 V/psi to V/kPa, divide by 6.89476 kPa/psi: S = 0.137 V/psi ÷ 6.89476 kPa/psi = 0.0199 V/kPa = 19.9 mV/kPa. To go the other way (mV/kPa → mV/psi), multiply by 6.89476. Rule: multiply by the ratio that cancels the old unit, and check direction: 1 psi is bigger than 1 kPa, so a sensitivity per psi is a bigger number than per kPa.

The ladder of standards and traceability

You cannot check a volt against nature on a shop floor. So the world uses a ladder, and every rung is calibrated against the rung above it.

  • International: the BIPM, near Paris, coordinates the SI and key comparisons between national institutes.
  • National primary: in India, CSIR–National Physical Laboratory, New Delhi, maintains the national standards and realises the SI units.
  • Secondary: held by calibration laboratories and calibrated against the national standard. Some syllabi call this the reference standard; both names mean the same rung.
  • Working: the dead-weight tester, standard resistor or calibrator in the plant instrument room, checked against a secondary standard.

Each rung is more accurate than the one below, typically by a factor of four to ten. A standard only as good as the device under test proves nothing.

Traceability is what makes the ladder usable: an unbroken chain of calibrations, each link with a stated uncertainty, each documented, ending at a primary standard. A sensor is traceable when you can show that chain with certificates, uncertainties and dates. Traceability does not make an instrument accurate; it makes its error known and comparable.

Calibration, verification, adjustment, and the static procedure

Calibration compares the instrument output with a known input produced by a standard, and records the result. It is a measurement and a record; it need not change anything. Verification is a pass/fail check: is the reading inside the allowed limits? Adjustment is the physical turning of the zero and span trims so the output matches the ideal line. You adjust after you calibrate, then calibrate again to prove the adjustment worked.

  1. Hold other inputs constant — temperature, supply voltage, mounting torque, vibration — and record them; they belong on the certificate.
  2. Pick five to ten input values over the range, including both ends.
  3. Apply them going up (up-scale); record the output at each point.
  4. Apply them going down (down-scale); record again. The gap at the same input is the hysteresis.
  5. Fit a straight line: the end-point line joining the extremes, or a least-squares line.
  6. Read sensitivity (slope), zero offset (intercept), non-linearity (largest deviation from the line) and hysteresis.
  7. Write the certificate: range, sensitivity, zero offset, hysteresis, uncertainty, conditions held, standard used and its traceability, date.

Full scale in this chapter means the full-scale output, the output at the upper range limit (4.00 V in the example). It is the %FS denominator. Some texts use the span instead — here 4.00 − 0.02 = 3.98 V — which gives 0.754 % instead of 0.75 %. These static numbers are defined in Chapter 1.4; repeated-reading uncertainty is in Chapter 1.6.

Worked example

Question. A 0–200 kPa pressure sensor is calibrated against a dead-weight tester with temperature held at 25 °C and supply at 10 V. Up-scale readings: pressure 0, 50, 100, 150, 200 kPa give output 0.02, 1.01, 1.98, 3.02, 4.00 V. Find the end-point sensitivity in V/kPa and mV/kPa, the zero offset, and the largest deviation from the end-point straight line as a percentage of full scale.

  1. End-point sensitivity S = (4.00 − 0.02) V ÷ 200 kPa = 3.98 V ÷ 200 kPa = 0.0199 V/kPa = 19.9 mV/kPa.

  3. End-point line: V(ideal) = 0.02 + 0.0199 × P, with V in volts and P in kPa.
  4. At 0 kPa: ideal 0.020 V, measured 0.02 V, deviation 0.000 V.
  5. At 50 kPa: ideal 1.015 V, measured 1.01 V, deviation −0.005 V.
  6. At 100 kPa: ideal 2.010 V, measured 1.98 V, deviation −0.030 V.
  7. At 150 kPa: ideal 3.005 V, measured 3.02 V, deviation +0.015 V.
  8. At 200 kPa: ideal 4.000 V, measured 4.00 V, deviation 0.000 V.

Exam traps

  • Never fix a span error with the zero knob, or a zero error with the span knob. Adjust zero first, then span, then recheck zero — the two interact until both settle.
  • An instrument that passed a verification check has not been calibrated. Verification gives pass/fail; calibration records the deviation and its uncertainty.
  • The denominator of %FS is the full-scale output, not the reading. A 0.03 V deviation on a 4.00 V full-scale output is 0.75 % FS but about 1.5 % of the 1.98 V reading where it occurs. Using the 3.98 V span instead gives 0.754 % FS.
  • Hysteresis and non-linearity are separate numbers. Hysteresis compares up-scale with down-scale at the same input; non-linearity compares one sweep with the fitted straight line.
  • A Celsius difference equals a kelvin difference, but absolute temperatures differ by 273.15. Mixing the two wrecks gas-law and cold-junction calculations.

1.4 Static characteristics: accuracy, precision, resolution, sensitivity, linearity

For an input that is not changing, a sensor is fully described by a small set of numbers. Each number answers a different question: how close to truth (accuracy), how repeatable (precision), how small a change it can see (resolution), how much output per input (sensitivity), how straight its graph is (linearity), and how it behaves going up versus down (hysteresis) or over time (drift). These are the numbers on a datasheet, a calibration certificate and a purchase order. They let you compare a cheap pressure module with an expensive transmitter honestly.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for sensor types and Chapter 1.3 for the calibration line and units.

Range, span, sensitivity, accuracy and precision

Range is the two limits the sensor is made for, such as 0–250 kPa or −40 to 125 °C. Span is the difference between those limits: 250 kPa in the first example. A sensor can have a non-zero lower limit, for example 50–250 kPa, so range and span are not the same. Sensitivity is the slope of the output-versus-input graph:

S = Δy / Δx

S is sensitivity in output units per input unit (mV/kPa, mV/V per kPa, or counts/°C); Δy is a small output change; Δx is the input change that caused it.

For a straight sensor, S = output span / input span. A 0–50 mV output over 0–250 kPa gives S = 50 mV / 250 kPa = 0.2 mV/kPa.

Accuracy is how close the mean indicated value is to the true value. Quote it as ± value, ± % of full scale (%FS) or ± % of reading, and say which. Error is e = indicated − true. The fixed part of error is bias; the scatter of repeated readings is precision or repeatability, usually a standard deviation or a band.

Resolution, linearity, hysteresis and drift

Resolution is the smallest input change that gives a detectable output change. A display showing 100.1 kPa has resolution 0.1 kPa. Threshold is the smallest input that causes any output from rest. Dead band is an input range over which the output does not change. Resolution belongs to the whole chain: sensor, amplifier and display.

Linearity is the maximum deviation of the measured curve from a chosen straight line, as ± %FS. Three lines are named. Terminal-based (end-point) joins the two ends of the calibration curve. Best-fit (least-squares) minimises the sum of squared deviations. Independent picks the line with the smallest maximum deviation. Always name the line when quoting linearity.

L = max |y_meas − y_line| / y_span × 100%

L is linearity in %FS; y_meas is the measured output; y_line is the chosen straight-line output at the same input; y_span is the full-scale output span.

Hysteresis is the maximum difference between up-scale and down-scale readings at the same input, expressed as %FS. Friction, backlash or magnetic memory cause it. Drift is a slow change with time (zero drift, span drift) or temperature (thermal drift), quoted per month or per °C. Tolerance is the allowed manufacturing deviation from nominal. Also, ±1 %FS on a 0–250 kPa device is ±2.5 kPa at every reading; ±1 % of reading at 10 kPa is only ±0.1 kPa. At low readings, %FS is the larger number. Time-varying behaviour is Chapter 1.5.

A practical order for reading a datasheet

  1. Fix range and span. Check that the working input sits inside the range with margin.
  2. Find sensitivity and output span. Check the amplifier or ADC can accept that output.
  3. Read accuracy with its reference: ± value, ± %FS or ± %reading.
  4. Separate bias from precision. Calibration shows bias; repeated readings show scatter.
  5. Compare resolution with the smallest change you must detect. A fine display does not fix a coarse sensor.
  6. Check linearity and name the straight line used. Then check hysteresis and drift.
  7. If low readings matter, convert %FS to the actual input at that reading.

The measured curve has a small zero offset and a bow, so near mid-span it can sit about 0.6 mV above the line. At 0.2 mV/kPa that is 3 kPa. Exam traps

  • Accuracy and precision are different. A sensor can be precise and still inaccurate by a fixed bias.
  • ± %FS and ± %reading differ hugely at low readings. 1 %FS on 0–250 kPa is ±2.5 kPa anywhere; 1 % of a 10 kPa reading is only ±0.1 kPa.

  • Linearity without naming the straight line is incomplete. Terminal-based, best-fit and independent linearity give different numbers.
  • Hysteresis is up/down at the same input; drift is change with time or temperature. A zero shift after warming is drift, not hysteresis.

1.5 Dynamic characteristics: zero, first and second order

A sensor does not jump to a new reading the instant the input changes. Heat must flow, a mass must move, a diaphragm must flex. The delay is captured by a mathematical model: zero order (instant), first order (one energy store, one time constant τ), or second order (two energy stores, natural frequency ωn and damping ζ). These constants fix the lag, overshoot and usable frequency range. They decide whether a thermocouple can follow a furnace cycle or a pressure sensor can catch a pump pulse.

You need: Chapter 1.4 for sensitivity K and full scale (static characteristics), and first-order differential equations and complex numbers from mathematics.

Transfer function and zero order

Write the input as x(t) and output as y(t). In the Laplace domain, the transfer function is H(s) = Y(s)/X(s). Here s is the complex frequency variable, Y(s) is the Laplace transform of the output, and X(s) is that of the input. H(s) is the sensor's mathematical model: it tells how the sensor modifies any input. Some syllabi call it the mathematical model of the transducer. The order is the number of independent energy stores.

A zero-order sensor has H(s) = K, so y(t) = K x(t) at every instant. K is the sensitivity, in output units per input unit (from Chapter 1.4). The output has no lag. A pure resistor divider measuring a slowly varying voltage is close to zero order. A potentiometer is close to zero order when its load resistance is much larger than its own resistance. A heavy load causes a loading error: the output becomes a nonlinear function of wiper position, but the response stays instant. The real lag in a potentiometer comes from the mass of the wiper, which makes it second order at high speeds. Zero order is the ideal we compare against.

First order: one time constant

A first-order sensor obeys τ(dy/dt) + y = Kx. τ is the time constant in seconds, dy/dt is the rate of change of output, and K is the static sensitivity. Its transfer function is H(s) = K/(1 + τs).

For a step of size A at t = 0, starting from zero, y(t) = K A (1 − e−t/τ). At t = τ the output is 63.2 % of final; at 3τ it is 95.0 %; at 5τ it is 99.3 %. So τ is not the settling time.

For a ramp input of slope R, the output becomes a ramp of the same slope after a start-up, but lags it by τR. A thermocouple dipped into a bath is the standard picture. Its bead has mass m, specific heat c, surface area As and convection coefficient h, so τ = mc/(hAs). Here m is in kg, c in J/(kg·K), As in m² and h in W/(m²·K), so τ comes out in seconds. A larger bead or weaker convection gives a larger τ.

For a sinusoidal input x = A sin(ωt), the amplitude ratio is 1/√(1 + (ωτ)²) and the phase lag is tan⁻¹(ωτ). At ω = 1/τ the ratio is 0.707, the −3 dB bandwidth. The impulse response is (K/τ)e−t/τ.

Second order: natural frequency and damping

A second-order sensor obeys (1/ωn²)(d²y/dt²) + (2ζ/ωn)(dy/dt) + y = Kx. ωn is the undamped natural frequency in rad/s; ζ is the damping ratio, dimensionless. The transfer function is H(s) = K / (s²/ωn² + 2ζs/ωn + 1). A mass m on a spring k with damper c gives ωn = √(k/m) and ζ = c/(2√(km)).

For a step of size A: when ζ < 1 the sensor is underdamped, the output rises past the final value and rings at the damped natural frequency ωd = ωn√(1 − ζ²) in rad/s. The first peak is at the peak time tp = π/ωd, and the peak overshoot is Mp = exp(−πζ/√(1 − ζ²)), a fraction of the final value. As a fraction, Mp = 0.527 means 52.7 %. At ζ = 1 the system is critically damped: the fastest response with no overshoot. At ζ > 1 it is overdamped: slow, with no overshoot. Most sensors aim for ζ around 0.6 to 0.8.

For a sinusoid, put r = ω/ωn. The amplitude ratio is 1/√((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²). Resonance peaks near r = √(1 − 2ζ²), and only for small ζ is the peak sharp; it exists only when ζ < 0.707. A ramp input leaves a steady lag of 2ζ/ωn times the slope. The impulse response is a sine at the damped natural frequency ωd = ωn√(1 − ζ²) rad/s, whose amplitude decays as e−ζωn t.

Worked example

Question. A thermocouple bead has τ = 2 s. It is taken from 25 °C and dipped into a bath at 100 °C. (a) Find the reading at t = 2 s and t = 6 s. (b) If the bath is ramped at 2 °C/s, find the steady lag. (c) A pressure diaphragm has fn = 1 Hz and ζ = 0.2. Find overshoot, peak time and 2 % settling time.

  1. Step size A = 100 − 25 = 75 °C.
  2. At t = 2 s = τ: T = 25 + 75(1 − e−1) = 25 + 75 × 0.6321 = 72.4 °C.
  3. At t = 6 s = 3τ: T = 25 + 75(1 − e−3) = 25 + 75 × 0.9502 = 96.3 °C.
  4. Ramp lag = τ × slope = 2 s × 2 °C/s = 4 °C. The reading stays 4 °C below the bath.
  5. ωn = 2πfn = 2π × 1 = 6.283 rad/s.
  6. ωd = ωn√(1 − ζ²) = 6.283√0.96 = 6.156 rad/s.
  7. Overshoot Mp = exp(−π × 0.2/√0.96) = exp(−0.641) = 0.527, i.e. 52.7 %.
  8. Peak time tp = π/ωd = π/6.156 = 0.51 s.
  9. 2 % settling time ≈ 4/(ζωn) = 4/(0.2 × 6.283) = 4/1.257 = 3.2 s.

Answer. The thermocouple reads 72.4 °C at 2 s and 96.3 °C at 6 s, lags a 2 °C/s ramp by 4 °C; the diaphragm overshoots by 52.7 %, peaks at 0.51 s and settles in about 3.2 s.

Exam traps

  • Reading τ as the time to reach the final value — it is the time to reach 63.2 %.
  • Using the overshoot formula for ζ = 1 or ζ > 1; it only holds for ζ < 1.
  • Putting fn in Hz directly into formulas that need ωn in rad/s; convert with ωn = 2πfn.
  • Confusing first-order bandwidth ω = 1/τ with second-order natural frequency ωn; they are different quantities.

1.6 Error analysis and statistical analysis of measurement data

A reading from a sensor is an estimate, not a verdict. The true value is hidden; what we have is a number plus the effects of blunders, bias and noise. Error analysis separates those effects, and statistics turns repeated readings into a mean and a believable uncertainty. This chapter builds the sentence that every calibration certificate and lab report must contain: best estimate ± uncertainty, with the method stated. It is used in watt-hour meter testing, ECG front ends, IRCTC coach temperature loggers and any place where a decision depends on a measured number.

You need: Chapter 1.4 for bias, precision and resolution, and Chapter 1.3 for the calibration deviations that feed the systematic uncertainty.

Three kinds of error, and what averaging does

A measurement error is the difference between the reading and the true value: absolute error = xm − xtrue, where xm is the measured value and xtrue is the accepted true value. Relative error = (xm − xtrue)/xtrue. Percentage error = relative error × 100 %. The correction is the negative of the error; add it to the reading to make the reading better.

  • Gross error or blunder: misread display, wrong range, wrong unit. Statistics does not cure it; repeat the measurement.
  • Systematic error or bias: calibration offset, scale-factor error, loading, drift. It has the same sign and roughly the same size on every repeat.
  • Random error: noise, vibration, supply ripple, thermal agitation. It changes sign and size from reading to reading.

The two error types respond differently to averaging. A bias of +0.2 V keeps the mean +0.2 V high no matter how many readings you take. Random scatter with standard deviation s falls to s/√n in the mean of n independent readings. That is why a calibration offset must be found from a standard, not from many repeats.

Mean, spread and the uncertainty of the mean

Take n repeated readings x1, x2, …, xn at the same input. The best estimate of the true value is the sample mean x̄ = Σxi/n. The median is the middle value when the readings are sorted. The range is the largest reading minus the smallest. Median and range are quick checks; the mean and standard deviation use all the data.

s² = Σ(xi − x̄)²/(n − 1)

s² is the sample variance, in squared units of the reading; xi is one reading; x̄ is the sample mean; n is the number of readings. Use n − 1 because the deviations are measured from x̄, not from the unknown true value. Dividing by n understates the scatter.

sx̄ = s/√n    and    x̄ ± k·sx̄

s is the sample standard deviation, in the reading's units; sx̄ is the standard uncertainty of the mean, also called the standard error; k = 1, 2, 3 gives roughly 68 %, 95 %, 99 % confidence if the scatter is normal.

Draw a histogram of the deviations. The normal distribution is a fair model when many small independent random effects act, the histogram is roughly symmetric, and there is no drift or outlier. It is a poor model for a warm-up drift or a single loose connection.

Combining uncertainties, resolution and a straight-line fit

y = a + b  →  uy = √(ua² + ub²)

ua and ub are standard uncertainties of a and b, in their units; uy is the standard uncertainty of the result y, in the same units as y.

y = ab or y = a/b  →  uy/|y| = √((ua/a)² + (ub/b)²)

uy/|y| is the fractional uncertainty of y; ua/a and ub/b are fractional uncertainties of the inputs. For a power law y = an (a raised to the power n) with exact n, multiply the fractional uncertainty of a by |n|: uy/|y| = |n| × ua/|a|.

The display resolution adds a rectangular uncertainty of ± half the last digit. For a 0.01 V display, half the last digit is 0.005 V. Convert it to a standard uncertainty by dividing by √3, giving 0.0029 V, then combine it in quadrature with the scatter. A straight-line fit y = mx + c gives slope m = [nΣxy − ΣxΣy]/[nΣx² − (Σx)²] and intercept c = (Σy − mΣx)/n. The correlation coefficient r near +1 or −1 says the line is a good model; r near 0 says it is not. Reject an outlier only with a stated rule, such as more than 3s from the mean plus a recorded physical cause.

Exam traps

  • Dividing by n instead of n − 1 in the sample variance understates s and makes the result look better than it is.
  • Adding uncertainties arithmetically instead of in quadrature. For independent random errors, squares add.
  • Quoting s of one reading when the question asks for the uncertainty of the mean. The mean's uncertainty is s/√n.

  • Stopping at the scatter and forgetting the resolution term. Combine both in quadrature before quoting the interval.
  • Deleting an outlier just because it looks bad. State a rejection rule and record the physical cause.

When many small independent random effects act, the histogram is roughly symmetric, and there is no drift or outlier. A skewed histogram or a warm-up trend calls for a different model.

Not for the exam. Industry writes uncertainty statements using the GUM framework, separating Type A uncertainty from repeated readings and Type B uncertainty from certificates and specifications. Calibration laboratories often check these intervals with Monte Carlo simulation. Sensor drift over months is studied with Allan variance, which separates white noise from bias instability. Search for GUM measurement uncertainty, Type A and Type B uncertainty, least-squares calibration and Allan variance.

Takeaways

  1. Active sensors generate their own electrical signal; passive sensors need an external excitation supply and modify it.
  2. Every sensor rests on a physical law that converts the measurand into a change of resistance, capacitance, inductance or emf.
  3. Calibration is a documented comparison against a traceable standard, not just an adjustment of the zero knob.
  4. Static characteristics answer different questions: accuracy is closeness to truth, precision is repeatability, and resolution is the smallest detectable change.
  5. Linearity, hysteresis and drift describe how far a real sensor departs from an ideal straight-line response.
  6. First-order sensors lag with a time constant τ; second-order sensors add overshoot and ringing set by damping ratio ζ and natural frequency ωₙ.
  7. Error analysis separates systematic bias from random scatter and reports a best estimate with an uncertainty.
  8. A measurement chain from sensor to display must be analysed as a system, because each block adds its own error and lag.

Apply

Apply A passive sensor has resistance 500 Ω at 0 kPa and 520 Ω at 100 kPa. It is connected in a voltage divider with a 500 Ω series resistor and a 5 V supply. Calculate the output at 0 kPa and at 100 kPa, and the sensitivity in mV/kPa. 12 marks

Use Vout = 5 × R / (R + 500); At 0 kPa: Vout = 2.500 V; At 100 kPa: Vout = 5 × 520 / 1020 = 2.549 V; Change = 0.049 V = 49 mV; Sensitivity = 49 mV / 100 kPa = 0.49 mV/kPa; State that the response is nonlinear and sensitivity is an average over the range.

Apply A calibration table gives increasing and decreasing readings for a pressure sensor. Explain how to compute span, average sensitivity, hysteresis and maximum linearity error from the table. 13 marks

Span = full-scale output − zero output; Average sensitivity = span / input span; Hysteresis = maximum difference between increasing and decreasing outputs at the same input; Linearity error: compare output with a best-fit or end-point straight line; Express each error in output units and as a percentage of span; Draw a labelled calibration curve.

Apply A first-order temperature sensor has a time constant of 2 s. It is suddenly placed in a bath whose temperature steps by 40 °C. Find the output at 2 s, the time to reach 95% of the step, and the reading error at 10 s. 12 marks

First-order step response: y(t) = A(1 − e^(−t/τ)); At t = τ = 2 s, output reaches 63.2% of 40 °C = 25.3 °C; For 95%, t = 3τ = 6 s; At 10 s, fraction = 1 − e^(−5) = 0.9933; Reading error = 40 × e^(−5) = 0.27 °C; State assumptions: single time constant, no self-heating.

Apply A second-order accelerometer has natural frequency 100 rad/s and damping ratio 0.2. Calculate the percentage overshoot and peak time for a step input, and comment on its usable frequency range. 13 marks

Percentage overshoot = 100 × e^(−πζ / √(1 − ζ²)); For ζ = 0.2, overshoot ≈ 52.7%; Peak time = π / (ωₙ√(1 − ζ²)) ≈ 0.032 s; Low damping causes severe ringing near resonance; For flat response, use ω well below ωₙ and prefer ζ near 0.6 to 0.8; Sketch the step response.

Apply Ten repeated readings of a voltage are 5.01, 4.99, 5.00, 5.02, 4.98, 5.00, 5.01, 4.99, 5.00 and 5.00 V. Calculate the mean, sample standard deviation, standard error and a 95% confidence interval. 12 marks

Mean = 5.000 V; Compute deviations and sample standard deviation s; Standard error = s / √n with n = 10; 95% confidence interval uses t ≈ 2.262 for 9 degrees of freedom; Report result as mean ± expanded uncertainty with units; Separate random uncertainty from any systematic calibration bias.

Analyze and evaluate

Evaluate A parcel-weighing scale for a UPI payment counter must measure loads from 0.1 kg to 20 kg with 5 g resolution. Choose a sensing principle, justify active or passive operation, and evaluate the static characteristics needed. 15 marks

Load cell with strain gauges as a passive Wheatstone bridge sensor; Justify: linear, repeatable, rugged and available in low-profile platforms; Resolution depends on bridge excitation, amplifier gain and ADC bits; Accuracy must include hysteresis, creep and temperature drift; Calibration with standard masses and traceability; Discuss wiring, shielding and signal conditioning.

Evaluate A calibration report shows zero drift of 0.5% span, hysteresis of 1% span and a linearity error of 0.8% span over a 0–200 kPa range. Evaluate whether the sensor suits a process needing ±1.5 kPa accuracy. 15 marks

Convert each error to kPa: span = 200 kPa; Zero drift = 1.0 kPa; hysteresis = 2.0 kPa; linearity = 1.6 kPa; Hysteresis alone exceeds the ±1.5 kPa limit; Combine errors in the worst case and by root-sum-square; Recommend recalibration, a better sensor or reduced range; State that accuracy is not the same as resolution.

Analyze A first-order sensor with time constant τ measures a ramp input of slope k. Derive the steady-state lag error and evaluate how τ affects the reading during a fast temperature rise. 13 marks

Model τ dy/dt + y = x(t), with x = kt; Steady-state solution gives y = k(t − τ); Lag error = kτ, proportional to slope and time constant; Smaller τ gives smaller dynamic error; For a step change, the error decays as e^(−t/τ); Recommend sensor selection based on the fastest expected ramp.

Evaluate Compare piezoresistive and capacitive MEMS pressure sensors for a tyre-pressure monitor. Consider sensitivity, temperature drift, power, size and signal conditioning. 14 marks

Piezoresistive: Wheatstone bridge, simple readout, moderate sensitivity, temperature drift; Capacitive: high sensitivity, low power, needs capacitance-to-voltage circuitry; Both can be micromachined and made small; Tyre monitor needs wide temperature range and long battery life; Evaluate drift, packaging and calibration cost; Give a reasoned choice with trade-offs.

Glossary

Accuracy
The closeness of a measured value to the true value.
Active sensor
A self-generating sensor that produces an electrical output without an external excitation supply.
Actuator
A device that converts an electrical signal into a physical action or quantity.
Adjustment
A physical or software change made to bring an instrument's output closer to the true value.
Calibration
The process of comparing a sensor's output with a reference standard and recording the result.
Damping ratio
The ratio of actual damping to critical damping in a second-order system.
Dead band
The range of input change that produces no detectable change in output.
Drift
A slow change in output with time or ambient conditions when the input is constant.
Hysteresis
The difference between output readings for the same input when approached from opposite directions.
Inverse transducer
A transducer that converts an electrical signal back into a non-electrical physical quantity.
Linearity
The closeness of a sensor's calibration curve to a straight line.
Measurand
The physical quantity that a sensor is intended to measure.
Passive sensor
A sensor whose electrical property changes with the measurand and which needs an external supply.
Precision
The closeness of repeated measurements to one another.
Random error
An unpredictable scatter in repeated readings that can be reduced by averaging.
Range
The lower and upper limits of input over which a sensor is specified to operate.
Resolution
The smallest input change that produces a detectable output change.
Sensitivity
The ratio of output change to input change, S = Δy/Δx.
Sensor
A device that responds to a physical quantity and produces a measurable output.
Span
The difference between the maximum and minimum output over the specified range.
Systematic error
A repeatable error that shifts all readings by the same amount or in the same direction.
Time constant
The time for a first-order sensor to reach 63.2% of its final response to a step input.
Traceability
A documented chain of comparisons linking a measurement to a recognised standard.
Transducer
A device that converts one form of energy into another, often a physical quantity into an electrical signal.
Uncertainty
A quantified range around a measured value within which the true value is expected to lie.
Verification
Checking that an instrument meets a stated specification or requirement.
