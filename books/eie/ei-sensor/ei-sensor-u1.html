1.1 Classification of sensors; active and passive sensors

A sensor must hand over what it measures as an electrical signal, and there are only two ways to do it. Either it makes its own emf (electromotive force, a generated voltage) from the measurand’s energy, or it changes its impedance and lets an external supply deliver the current. The first kind is active (self-generating), the second passive (externally energised), and this one label decides the wiring.

You need: basic circuits — Ohm's law, ideal voltage and current sources, and power P = VI = I²R.

Sensor, transducer, actuator

A sensor responds to a physical quantity and gives an output that carries information about it. The quantity being measured is the measurand: the temperature of a bearing, the pressure in a pipeline, a patient’s pulse.

A transducer converts one form of energy into another. Every sensor is a transducer, but we say sensor when we care about the measurement and transducer when we care about the conversion. An actuator works the other way: electrical input, physical output, such as the solenoid that closes a valve. An inverse transducer converts an electrical input into a non-electrical output — for example a piezoelectric crystal driven by a voltage to launch ultrasound, or the pen motor that moves the pen of a chart recorder.

Most measurement devices follow the same chain, from the physical world to a number on a display:

  1. Measurand — the quantity to be measured, for example the temperature of a bearing.
  2. Sensing element — the part that first responds, such as a diaphragm or a thermistor bead.
  3. Variable-conversion element — turns that response into an electrical variable: resistance, charge, inductance or emf.
  4. Variable-manipulation element — amplifies, filters and converts it, ending as a voltage or a digital count.
  5. Output stage — a display, a recorder or a controller input.

Some syllabi call this the general input-output configuration of a measurement system, or the functional elements of an instrument. A bedside blood-pressure channel shows all five: the measurand is arterial pressure; the diaphragm of the disposable transducer is the sensing element; a strain-gauge bridge on that diaphragm converts its bending into a voltage; an amplifier and filter manipulate it; the monitor shows 120/80 mmHg. Not every device has all five stages. A thermocouple produces an emf directly, so it needs no separate conversion element.

Active and passive sensors

Classify by energy source and the answer is one of two words. An active sensor (also called self-generating) converts the measurand's energy directly into electrical energy. It needs no external supply. A passive sensor (also called externally energised) generates no signal of its own. It converts the measurand into a change of resistance, inductance or capacitance, and an external supply must be present for that change to become a signal.

  • Active: thermocouple (Seebeck emf), piezoelectric crystal (charge on stress), photovoltaic cell (light gives emf), tachogenerator (emf proportional to speed).
  • Passive: RTD (resistance temperature detector), thermistor, strain gauge, potentiometer, LVDT (linear variable differential transformer), photoconductive cell, capacitive sensor.
  • At the bedside: the piezoelectric crystal of an ultrasound probe receiving echoes is active; the thermistor of a temperature probe and the photodiode of a pulse oximeter, run with a bias supply, are passive.

The wiring consequence in one line: an active sensor is a voltage or charge source and needs a high-impedance load; a passive sensor is a variable impedance and needs excitation plus a bridge or divider.

ΔV = I × ΔR

ΔV is the signal in volts, I the excitation current in amperes and ΔR the change of sensor resistance in ohms; the current comes from the supply, not the sensor.

Two practical details follow. An active sensor’s output is often only millivolts, so the amplifier’s input impedance must be far larger than the sensor’s source resistance, or the emf is loaded down (worked example 2). For a passive sensor the excitation is a design choice: too small and the signal is buried in noise, too large and the current heats the sensor and shifts its own reading.

Other classification axes

Energy source is the most useful split, but exams also ask for the others. By output form, a sensor is analog (a continuous voltage, like a thermocouple) or digital (pulses or a code word, like a shaft encoder); a digital signal resists noise on a long cable far better, though grounding still matters. By physical principle, sensors are resistive, inductive, capacitive, piezoelectric, thermoelectric, photoelectric or Hall-effect (see physical principles of sensing). By contact, a sensor touches the process (an RTD inside a tank) or does not (an infrared forehead thermometer); non-contact sensing suits moving, hot or sterile objects. By stage, a primary transducer turns the measurand into an intermediate non-electrical quantity and a secondary transducer turns that into an electrical signal: a Bourdon tube bends with pressure and moves the core of an LVDT, which gives the voltage. By application, we speak of industrial, biomedical, automotive or domestic sensors.

Worked example

Question. Classify each device as active or passive, and justify with one number where the data allows. (a) A Pt100 RTD reads 100.0 Ω at 0 °C and is run at 1 mA excitation. (b) A piezoelectric accelerometer gives 12 mV per g (g = 9.81 m/s², the acceleration due to gravity) with no supply connected. (c) A photodiode in photovoltaic mode gives 0.45 V in sunlight. (d) The same photodiode in photoconductive mode with 5 V reverse bias.

  1. (a) P = I²R = (1 × 10⁻³ A)² × 100.0 Ω = 1 × 10⁻⁴ W = 0.10 mW. That power comes from the external supply; the RTD only sets how much current flows. Passive, and the excitation fixes its self-heating.
  2. (b) 12 mV per g appears with no supply connected, so the crystal turns the mechanical energy of the force into the signal. Active.
  3. (c) 0.45 V in sunlight with no bias: light energy becomes electrical energy. Active.
  4. (d) With 5 V reverse bias the supply drives the current and light only changes its size. Passive.
  5. Rule: ask where the energy in the output comes from. Measurand: active. External supply: passive.

Answer. (a) Passive, self-heating 0.10 mW from 1 mA through 100.0 Ω; (b) active; (c) active; (d) passive.

Worked example 2

Question. An active sensor has an open-circuit emf of 25 mV and a source resistance of 0.5 kΩ. What voltage reaches (a) an amplifier of input resistance 1 MΩ and (b) one of 10 kΩ? Give the loading loss of each in mV and %.

  1. The sensor and the amplifier form a divider between the source resistance Rs = 500 Ω and the input resistance Rin: Vin = e × Rin/(Rin + Rs).
  2. (a) Rin = 1 MΩ: fraction = 1 000 000/1 000 500 = 0.999500. Vin = 25 mV × 0.999500 = 24.9875 mV ≈ 24.99 mV.
  3. Loss = 25 − 24.9875 = 0.0125 mV, which is 0.0125/25 × 100 = 0.05 %.
  4. (b) Rin = 10 kΩ: fraction = 10 000/10 500 = 0.95238. Vin = 25 × 0.95238 = 23.81 mV.
  5. Loss = 25 − 23.81 = 1.19 mV, which is 4.76 %: a calibration error far larger than most sensors’ accuracy class.
  6. Rule: keep Rin at least 1000 times Rs to hold the loading loss near 0.1 % or less.

Answer. (a) 24.99 mV, loss 0.0125 mV (0.05 %); (b) 23.81 mV, loss 1.19 mV (4.76 %). An active sensor needs a high-impedance load.

Exam traps

  • Marking a photodiode passive by habit. It is active in photovoltaic mode (no bias, light makes the emf) and passive in photoconductive mode (reverse bias, light changes the current).
  • Confusing the sensing element with the transducer. The diaphragm is the sensing element; the capacitive pressure transducer is the whole device with its terminals.
  • Calling a passive sensor “no power needed”. It makes no signal of its own and reads zero without excitation; it draws current from that supply, which is why the excitation level matters.
  • Writing 'active sensor' for any sensor that needs a power supply to run its amplifier. The label describes the sensing element only.

1.2 Physical principles of sensing

A sensor turns a physical stimulus into a measurable electrical change. The trick is not electrical: it is a known physical law. A force bends a beam and changes strain gauge resistance; two dissimilar metals joined at two junctions, with the junctions at different temperatures, make a small emf; light creates charge carriers. The principles group by energy domain — mechanical, thermal, electrical, magnetic, radiant, chemical — and name the measurand, the intermediate variable and the electrical output. The same law often serves two measurands; the packaging, not the equation, decides what the sensor measures. That is how a phone knows orientation and a car knows tyre pressure.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for active and passive vocabulary, and basic electricity and magnetism — charge, field, potential, Ohm's law and Faraday's law.

The sensing chain

Every sensing path has five stages: stimulus → sensing element → physical law → parameter change → electrical output. The law is the middle: it converts a non-electrical quantity into ΔR, ΔC, ΔL or Δemf. A resistance change needs a bridge or divider; a capacitance change needs an AC bridge or charge amplifier; an emf can go straight to a high-impedance amplifier. A passive sensor changes R, C or L and needs a supply; an active sensor makes its own emf (see Chapter 1.1).

Electrical quantities are the language. Charge Q is in coulombs (C). Note that C is also the usual symbol for capacitance, in farads; here the coulomb and the farad are different quantities. Electric field E = V/d is in volts per metre (V/m), with V the potential difference in volts and d the plate separation in metres. Potential energy per charge is V. For a capacitor, Q = CV, so C = Q/V, in farads (F). A parallel-plate capacitor has C = εA/d, where A is plate area in m², ε = ε₀εᵣ is permittivity in F/m, ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m, and εᵣ is relative permittivity. These equations are why a capacitive sensor needs a changing gap, area or dielectric.

Mechanical and thermal laws

Resistance is R = ρL/A. R is in Ω; ρ is resistivity in Ω·m; L is length in m; A is cross-section in m². Stretching a wire increases L and decreases A, so R rises; in a semiconductor the strain also changes ρ itself, which is the piezoresistive effect. Both are summed in the strain-gauge equation ΔR/R = GF·ε, where GF is the gauge factor (dimensionless), ε is strain (m/m) and ΔR is the change in resistance. It works under a steady load. A metal gauge has GF near 2, mostly from the change of shape; a semiconductor gauge reaches 50 to 200 but is far more temperature-sensitive. A load cell weighs a truck this way; construction and bridges come with the resistive transducers and strain gauges.

Piezoelectricity is different. A stress on quartz or PZT displaces charge: q = d·F, where q is charge in coulombs, d is the charge coefficient in C/N, and F is force in newtons. It gives a voltage across the crystal capacitance. It cannot hold a steady reading because the charge leaks away, so it suits vibration, impact and ultrasonic pulses. Piezoelectric transducers are treated with the piezoelectric and Hall-effect sensors.

Thermal law: the Seebeck effect. Two different metals joined at two points with a temperature difference ΔT produce an emf e = S·ΔT, where S is the Seebeck coefficient in V/K. The measurand is temperature difference; the output is a small dc voltage, often µV to mV. Cold-junction compensation is needed before you can read absolute temperature; the thermocouple laws come with the thermocouples, among the resistive transducers.

Magnetic, radiant and chemical laws

A coil has L ≈ N²µA/l, where L is inductance in henries (H), N is turns, µ is core permeability in H/m, A is core area in m², and l is magnetic path length in m. Faraday’s law gives e = −N·dΦ/dt, where e is induced emf in volts, Φ is the magnetic flux through one turn in webers (Wb), and t is time in seconds; the minus sign is Lenz’s law. A moving core changes reluctance and therefore L. A magnetostrictive material changes permeability under stress, so a coil around it reads force. Hall effect: VH = RH·I·B/t, where VH is the Hall voltage in volts, RH is the Hall coefficient in m³/C, I is current in amperes, B is flux density in tesla, and t is sheet thickness in metres. It senses a magnetic field or, with a fixed magnet, position.

Radiant laws: the photovoltaic effect gives an emf when light creates electron–hole pairs across a junction; the photoconductive effect lowers resistance when photons add carriers. The measurand is irradiance or wavelength; the intermediate is carrier generation; the output is emf or ΔR. A photodiode in a light meter is one use. Photodiodes and phototransistors have their own chapter with the optical sensors.

Chemical: a hygroscopic layer absorbs water, changing its resistance or capacitance. The measurand is relative humidity; the intermediate is water uptake; the output is ΔR or ΔC. A capacitor can sense displacement or humidity: the equation C = εA/d is the same, but one package lets pressure change d, and another lets humidity change εᵣ. Packaging decides. Humidity sensors come with the chemical sensors.

Worked example

Question. A capacitive pressure sensor has two plates of area A = 25 mm² separated by d = 0.10 mm of air (εᵣ ≈ 1, ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m). Pressure pushes the gap down by 0.01 mm. Find C before and after, the change in pF, the percentage change, and the small-signal sensitivity |dC/dd| at the start in pF/µm.

  1. Convert: A = 25 mm² = 25×10⁻⁶ m². Initial gap d₁ = 0.10 mm = 1.0×10⁻⁴ m. New gap d₂ = 0.10 − 0.01 = 0.09 mm = 9.0×10⁻⁵ m. Air gives ε = ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m.
  2. C₁ = ε₀A/d₁ = (8.854×10⁻¹² × 25×10⁻⁶) / (1.0×10⁻⁴) = 2.2135×10⁻¹² F = 2.21 pF.
  3. C₂ = ε₀A/d₂ = (8.854×10⁻¹² × 25×10⁻⁶) / (9.0×10⁻⁵) = 2.4594×10⁻¹² F = 2.46 pF.
  4. ΔC = C₂ − C₁ = 0.2459×10⁻¹² F = 0.246 pF ≈ 0.25 pF. Percentage change = (0.2459 / 2.2135) × 100 = 11.11 % ≈ 11.1 %.
  5. Small-signal sensitivity at the start: |dC/dd| = ε₀A/d₁² = C₁/d₁ = 2.2135×10⁻¹² / 1.0×10⁻⁴ = 2.2135×10⁻⁸ F/m. Since 1 pF/µm = 1×10⁻⁶ F/m, this is 0.0221 pF/µm ≈ 0.022 pF/µm. The sign is negative physically: C rises as d falls.

Answer. C₁ = 2.21 pF, C₂ = 2.46 pF, ΔC = 0.25 pF (+11.1 %), and |dC/dd| = 0.022 pF/µm.

Exam traps

  • Do not confuse piezoresistive with piezoelectric. Piezoresistive changes resistance under strain and works for steady loads; piezoelectric makes charge under stress and cannot hold a steady reading without charge leakage.
  • The capacitance formula is non-linear in d. A 10 % reduction in gap (d falls to 0.9 d) raises C by about 11 % (1/0.9 = 1.111), not 10 %; a 10 % increase in gap lowers C by about 9.1 %. The relation is non-linear and not symmetric.
  • A Seebeck emf depends on the temperature difference, not the absolute temperature. A thermocouple still needs a cold junction before it reports absolute temperature.
  • In VH = RH·I·B/t, the thickness t is in the denominator. Doubling the sheet thickness halves the Hall voltage for the same current and field.
  • A photovoltaic cell gives an emf; a photoconductive cell changes resistance and needs a bias supply. They are not interchangeable circuits.

Analyze The same parallel-plate capacitor can be sold as a displacement sensor or a humidity sensor. What changes in each package, and why is the equation not enough to tell them apart? 3 marks

Both use C = εA/d. In the displacement package, the measurand moves the plates and changes d. In the humidity package, a hygroscopic dielectric absorbs water and changes εᵣ. The equation is identical; the packaging and the exposed material decide which variable the outside world can change.

Apply A thermocouple has Seebeck coefficient S = 40 µV/°C and sees a temperature difference ΔT = 25 °C. Find the emf. 2 marks

e = S·ΔT = 40 µV/°C × 25 °C = 1000 µV = 1.0 mV.

Analyze A Hall sensor follows VH = RH·I·B/t. What happens to VH if the active sheet is made thinner, with the same current and field? 3 marks

VH rises in inverse proportion to t: halving t doubles VH for the same I and B. The limits are the higher resistance, self-heating and the strength of the thin sheet.

1.3 Units, standards and calibration of sensors

A sensor output is a number, and a number means nothing until two people agree what it counts. Units give that agreement; standards give a physical reference; calibration connects the two. This chapter runs the chain from the SI units down through primary, secondary and working standards to the instrument on a plant floor or a hospital ward, and ends with the static calibration procedure.

You need: Chapter 1.2 on what the sensor is converting, so that the input unit and the output unit are both clear before you compare them.

Units come first

A number stays useless until everyone agrees on the size of the unit. The SI has seven base units: the metre (m), kilogram (kg), second (s), ampere (A), kelvin (K), mole (mol) and candela (cd).

Since 20 May 2019 each base unit is fixed by giving an exact value to a constant of nature, so any well-equipped laboratory can realise it:

Every other unit on a datasheet is built from these. 1 N = 1 kg·m/s²; 1 Pa = 1 N/m²; 1 V = 1 kg·m²/(s³·A); 1 Ω = 1 V/A; 1 F = 1 C/V; 1 Hz = 1/s. The degree Celsius is a shifted kelvin: t(°C) = T(K) − 273.15, so a temperature difference of 1 °C equals 1 K, but 20 °C is 293.15 K, not 20 K.

Two habits catch most errors. Write the unit beside every number. Run a dimensional check: sensitivity = output unit ÷ input unit. A pressure sensor giving 19.9 mV/kPa has the dimensions of voltage per pressure (V/Pa); the numerical value changes with the unit, the dimension does not. To convert 0.137 V/psi to V/kPa, divide by 6.89476 kPa/psi: S = 0.137 V/psi ÷ 6.89476 kPa/psi = 0.0199 V/kPa = 19.9 mV/kPa. To go the other way (mV/kPa → mV/psi), multiply by 6.89476. Rule: multiply by the ratio that cancels the old unit, and check direction: 1 psi is bigger than 1 kPa, so a sensitivity per psi is a bigger number than per kPa.

The ladder of standards and traceability

You cannot check a volt against nature on a shop floor. So the world uses a ladder, and every rung is calibrated against the rung above it.

  • International: the BIPM, near Paris, coordinates the SI and the key comparisons between national institutes.
  • Primary: the national standards, kept by the national metrology institute (in India, CSIR–National Physical Laboratory, New Delhi). They realise the SI units directly, for example a caesium clock or a Josephson voltage array, and are used only to calibrate secondary standards, never for routine work.
  • Secondary: standard resistors, Zener voltage references, gauge blocks and standard weights held by accredited calibration laboratories and checked against the primary standards. Some syllabi call this rung the reference standard.
  • Working: the dead-weight tester, decade box or process calibrator in a plant or hospital workshop, checked against a secondary standard and used every day on field instruments.

Each rung is more accurate than the one below, typically by a factor of four to ten; a standard only as good as the device under test proves nothing. A hospital’s biomedical engineering team works on the bottom rungs: it checks a patient monitor’s pressure channel against a calibrated digital manometer and its temperature channel against a reference thermometer in a stirred bath.

Traceability is what makes the ladder usable: an unbroken chain of calibrations, each link with a stated uncertainty, each documented, ending at a primary standard. A sensor is traceable when you can show that chain with certificates, uncertainties and dates. Traceability does not make an instrument accurate; it makes its error known and comparable.

Calibration, verification, adjustment, and the static procedure

Calibration compares the instrument output with a known input produced by a standard, and records the result. It is a measurement and a record; it need not change anything. Verification is a pass/fail check: is the reading inside the allowed limits? Adjustment is the physical turning of the zero and span trims so the output matches the ideal line. You adjust after you calibrate, then calibrate again to prove the adjustment worked.

  1. Hold other inputs constant — temperature, supply voltage, mounting torque, vibration — and record them; they belong on the certificate.
  2. Pick five to ten input values over the range, including both ends.
  3. Apply them going up (up-scale); record the output at each point.
  4. Apply them going down (down-scale); record again. The largest gap at the same input is the hysteresis.
  5. Fit a straight line: the end-point line joining the extremes, or a least-squares line.
  6. Read sensitivity (slope), zero offset (intercept), non-linearity (largest deviation from the line) and hysteresis.
  7. Write the certificate: range, sensitivity, zero offset, hysteresis, uncertainty, conditions held, standard used and its traceability, date.

In this book %FS means a percentage of the full-scale output span: output at the upper limit minus output at the lower limit (3.98 V in the example). Some datasheets divide by the upper-limit output (4.00 V) instead; for a small zero offset only the third figure changes. The static numbers are defined in static characteristics; repeated-reading uncertainty in error analysis.

Worked example

Question. A 0–200 kPa pressure sensor is calibrated against a dead-weight tester with temperature held at 25 °C and supply at 10 V. Up-scale, pressures 0, 50, 100, 150, 200 kPa give 0.02, 1.01, 1.98, 3.02, 4.00 V; down-scale, the same pressures give 0.03, 1.03, 2.02, 3.04, 4.00 V. Find the end-point sensitivity, the zero offset, the largest deviation of the up-scale curve from the end-point line, and the hysteresis, each as %FS.

  1. End-point sensitivity S = (4.00 − 0.02) V ÷ 200 kPa = 3.98 V ÷ 200 kPa = 0.0199 V/kPa = 19.9 mV/kPa.
  2. Zero offset = output at zero input = 0.02 V. Output span = 4.00 − 0.02 = 3.98 V.
  3. End-point line: Videal = 0.02 + 0.0199 × P, with V in volts and P in kPa. The table gives Videal, the up-scale deviation and the up/down gap at each point.
  4. Largest |deviation| of the up-scale curve = 0.030 V, at 100 kPa (the curve sags below the line there).
  5. Non-linearity = 0.030 V ÷ 3.98 V × 100 = 0.75 %FS. In pressure terms, 0.030 ÷ 0.0199 = 1.5 kPa.

1.4 Static characteristics: accuracy, precision, resolution, sensitivity, linearity

For an input that is not changing, a sensor is fully described by a small set of numbers. Each number answers a different question: how close to truth (accuracy), how repeatable (precision), how small a change it can see (resolution), how much output per input (sensitivity), how straight its graph is (linearity), and how it behaves going up versus down (hysteresis) or over time (drift). These are the numbers on a datasheet, a calibration certificate and a purchase order. They let you compare a cheap pressure module with an expensive transmitter honestly.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for sensor types and Chapter 1.3 for the calibration line and units.

Range, span, sensitivity, accuracy and precision

Range is the two limits the sensor is made for, such as 0–250 kPa or −40 to 125 °C. Span is the difference between those limits: 250 kPa in the first example. A sensor can have a non-zero lower limit, for example 50–250 kPa, so range and span are not the same. Sensitivity is the slope of the output-versus-input graph:

S = Δy / Δx

S is sensitivity in output units per input unit (mV/kPa, mV/V per kPa, or counts/°C); Δy is a small output change; Δx is the input change that caused it.

For a straight sensor, S = output span / input span. A 0–50 mV output over 0–250 kPa gives S = 50 mV / 250 kPa = 0.2 mV/kPa.

Accuracy is how close the mean indicated value is to the true value. Quote it as ± value, ± % of full scale (%FS) or ± % of reading, and say which. Error is e = indicated − true. The fixed part of error is bias; the scatter of repeated readings is precision or repeatability, usually a standard deviation or a band.

Resolution, linearity, hysteresis and drift

Resolution is the smallest input change that gives a detectable output change. A display showing 100.1 kPa has resolution 0.1 kPa. Threshold is the smallest input that causes any output from rest. Dead band is an input range over which the output does not change. Resolution belongs to the whole chain: sensor, amplifier and display.

Linearity is the maximum deviation of the measured curve from a chosen straight line, as ± %FS. Three lines are named. Terminal-based (end-point) joins the two ends of the calibration curve. Best-fit (least-squares) minimises the sum of squared deviations. Independent picks the line with the smallest maximum deviation. Always name the line when quoting linearity.

L = max |ymeas − yline| / yspan × 100 %

L is linearity in %FS; ymeas is the measured output; yline is the chosen straight-line output at the same input; yspan is the full-scale output span.

Hysteresis is the maximum difference between up-scale and down-scale readings at the same input, expressed as %FS. Friction, backlash or magnetic memory cause it. Drift is a slow change with time (zero drift, span drift) or temperature (thermal drift), quoted per month or per °C. Tolerance is the allowed manufacturing deviation from nominal. Also, ±1 %FS on a 0–250 kPa device is ±2.5 kPa at every reading; ±1 % of reading at 10 kPa is only ±0.1 kPa. At low readings, %FS is the larger number. Time-varying behaviour is in dynamic characteristics.

A practical order for reading a datasheet

  1. Fix range and span. Check that the working input sits inside the range with margin.
  2. Find sensitivity and output span. Check the amplifier or ADC can accept that output.
  3. Read accuracy with its reference: ± value, ± %FS or ± %reading.
  4. Separate bias from precision. Calibration shows bias; repeated readings show scatter.
  5. Compare resolution with the smallest change you must detect. A fine display does not fix a coarse sensor.
  6. Check linearity and name the straight line used. Then check hysteresis and drift.
  7. If low readings matter, convert %FS to the actual input at that reading.

1.5 Dynamic characteristics: zero, first and second order

A sensor does not jump to a new reading the instant the input changes: heat must flow, a mass must move, a diaphragm must flex. The delay is captured by a mathematical model: zero order (instant), first order (one energy store, one time constant τ) or second order (two energy stores, natural frequency ωn and damping ζ). These constants decide whether a thermocouple can follow a furnace or a catheter can follow a heartbeat.

You need: Chapter 1.4 for sensitivity K and full scale (static characteristics), and first-order differential equations and complex numbers from mathematics.

Transfer function and zero order

Write the input as x(t) and output as y(t). In the Laplace domain, the transfer function is H(s) = Y(s)/X(s). Here s is the complex frequency variable, Y(s) is the Laplace transform of the output, and X(s) is that of the input. H(s) is the sensor's mathematical model: it tells how the sensor modifies any input. Some syllabi call it the mathematical model of the transducer. The order is the number of independent energy stores.

A zero-order sensor has H(s) = K, so y(t) = K x(t) at every instant. K is the sensitivity, in output units per input unit (from static characteristics). The output has no lag. A pure resistor divider measuring a slowly varying voltage is close to zero order. A potentiometer is close to zero order when its load resistance is much larger than its own resistance. A heavy load causes a loading error: the output becomes a nonlinear function of wiper position, but the response stays instant. The real lag in a potentiometer comes from the mass of the wiper, which makes it second order at high speeds. Zero order is the ideal we compare against.

First order: one time constant

A first-order sensor obeys τ(dy/dt) + y = Kx. τ is the time constant in seconds, dy/dt is the rate of change of output, and K is the static sensitivity. Its transfer function is H(s) = K/(1 + τs).

For a step of size A at t = 0, starting from zero, y(t) = K A (1 − e−t/τ). At t = τ the output is 63.2 % of final; at 3τ it is 95.0 %; at 5τ it is 99.3 %. So τ is not the settling time.

For a ramp input of slope R, the output becomes a ramp of the same slope after a start-up, but lags it by τR. A thermocouple dipped into a bath is the standard picture. Its bead has mass m, specific heat c, surface area As and convection coefficient h, so τ = mc/(hAs). Here m is in kg, c in J/(kg·K), As in m² and h in W/(m²·K), so τ comes out in seconds. A larger bead or weaker convection gives a larger τ.

For a sinusoidal input x = A sin(ωt), the amplitude ratio is 1/√(1 + (ωτ)²) and the phase lag is tan⁻¹(ωτ). At ω = 1/τ the ratio is 0.707, the −3 dB bandwidth. The impulse response is (K/τ)e−t/τ.

Second order: natural frequency and damping

A second-order sensor obeys (1/ωn²)(d²y/dt²) + (2ζ/ωn)(dy/dt) + y = Kx. ωn is the undamped natural frequency in rad/s; ζ is the damping ratio, dimensionless. The transfer function is H(s) = K / (s²/ωn² + 2ζs/ωn + 1). A mass m on a spring k with damper c gives ωn = √(k/m) and ζ = c/(2√(km)).

For a step of size A: when ζ < 1 the sensor is underdamped, the output rises past the final value and rings at the damped natural frequency ωd = ωn√(1 − ζ²) in rad/s. The first peak is at the peak time tp = π/ωd, and the peak overshoot is Mp = exp(−πζ/√(1 − ζ²)), a fraction of the final value. As a fraction, Mp = 0.527 means 52.7 %. At ζ = 1 the system is critically damped: the fastest response with no overshoot. At ζ > 1 it is overdamped: slow, with no overshoot. Most sensors aim for ζ around 0.6 to 0.8.

For a sinusoid, put r = ω/ωn. The amplitude ratio is 1/√((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²). Resonance peaks near r = √(1 − 2ζ²), and only for small ζ is the peak sharp; it exists only when ζ < 0.707. A ramp input leaves a steady lag of 2ζ/ωn times the slope. The impulse response is a sine at the damped natural frequency ωd = ωn√(1 − ζ²) rad/s, whose amplitude decays as e−ζωn t.

1.6 Error analysis and statistical analysis of measurement data

A reading from a sensor is an estimate, not a verdict. The true value is hidden; what we have is a number plus the effects of blunders, bias and noise. Error analysis separates those effects, and statistics turns repeated readings into a mean and a believable uncertainty: best estimate ± uncertainty, with the method stated, as every calibration certificate, ECG lab report and vaccine cold-chain log needs.

You need: Chapter 1.4 for bias, precision and resolution, and Chapter 1.3 for the calibration deviations that feed the systematic uncertainty.

Three kinds of error, and what averaging does

A measurement error is the difference between the reading and the true value: absolute error = xm − xtrue, where xm is the measured value and xtrue is the accepted true value. Relative error = (xm − xtrue)/xtrue. Percentage error = relative error × 100 %. The correction is the negative of the error; add it to the reading to make the reading better.

  • Gross error or blunder: misread display, wrong range, wrong unit. Statistics does not cure it; repeat the measurement.
  • Systematic error or bias: calibration offset, scale-factor error, loading, drift, or a blood-pressure cuff too small for the arm, which reads high every time. It has the same sign and roughly the same size on every repeat.
  • Random error: noise, vibration, supply ripple, thermal agitation. It changes sign and size from reading to reading.

The two error types respond differently to averaging. A bias of +0.2 V keeps the mean +0.2 V high no matter how many readings you take. Random scatter with standard deviation s falls to s/√n in the mean of n independent readings. That is why a calibration offset must be found from a standard, not from many repeats.

Mean, spread and the uncertainty of the mean

Take n repeated readings x1, x2, …, xn at the same input. The best estimate of the true value is the sample mean x̄ = Σxi/n. The median is the middle value when the readings are sorted. The range is the largest reading minus the smallest. Median and range are quick checks; the mean and standard deviation use all the data.

s² = Σ(xi − x̄)²/(n − 1)

s² is the sample variance, in squared units of the reading; xi is one reading; x̄ is the sample mean; n is the number of readings. Use n − 1 because the deviations are measured from x̄, not from the unknown true value. Dividing by n understates the scatter.

sx̄ = s/√n    and    x̄ ± k·sx̄

s is the sample standard deviation, in the reading's units; sx̄ is the standard uncertainty of the mean, also called the standard error; k = 1, 2, 3 gives roughly 68 %, 95 %, 99 % confidence if the scatter is normal.

Draw a histogram of the deviations. The normal distribution is a fair model when many small independent random effects act, the histogram is roughly symmetric, and there is no drift or outlier. It is a poor model for a warm-up drift or a single loose connection.

Combining uncertainties, resolution and a straight-line fit

y = a + b  →  uy = √(ua² + ub²)

ua and ub are standard uncertainties of a and b, in their units; uy is the standard uncertainty of the result y, in the same units as y.

y = ab or y = a/b  →  uy/|y| = √((ua/a)² + (ub/b)²)

uy/|y| is the fractional uncertainty of y; ua/a and ub/b are the fractional uncertainties of the inputs. For a power y = an with exact n, multiply the fractional uncertainty of a by |n|.

Limiting (guaranteed) errors are the worst case instead. When a datasheet gives ± limits, absolute limits add for a sum or difference and fractional limits add for a product or quotient. A 100 Ω ± 0.5 % resistor carrying 2 A ± 1 % dissipates P = I²R = 400 W within ±(2 × 1 + 0.5) = ±2.5 %, that is ±10 W. Quadrature gives the likely error; the limiting error is the most it can be.

The display resolution adds a rectangular uncertainty of ± half the last digit. For a 0.01 V display, half the last digit is 0.005 V. Convert it to a standard uncertainty by dividing by √3, giving 0.0029 V, then combine it in quadrature with the scatter. A straight-line fit y = mx + c gives slope m = [nΣxy − ΣxΣy]/[nΣx² − (Σx)²] and intercept c = (Σy − mΣx)/n. The correlation coefficient r near +1 or −1 says the line is a good model; r near 0 says it is not. Reject an outlier only with a stated rule, such as more than 3s from the mean plus a recorded physical cause.

Exam traps

  • Dividing by n instead of n − 1 in the sample variance understates s and makes the result look better than it is.
  • Adding uncertainties arithmetically instead of in quadrature. For independent random errors, squares add.
  • Quoting s of one reading when the question asks for the uncertainty of the mean. The mean's uncertainty is s/√n.
  • Treating half the last digit as a standard uncertainty. It is a rectangular half-width; divide by √3 before combining.
  • Deleting an outlier just because it looks bad. State a rejection rule and record the physical cause.
  • Adding fractional limits for a sum. For y = a + b add the absolute limits; fractions add only for products and quotients.

Takeaways

  1. Active sensors generate their own electrical signal; passive sensors need an external excitation supply and modify it.
  2. Every sensor rests on a physical law that converts the measurand into a change of resistance, capacitance, inductance or emf.
  3. Calibration is a documented comparison against a traceable standard, not just an adjustment of the zero knob.
  4. Static characteristics answer different questions: accuracy is closeness to truth, precision is repeatability, and resolution is the smallest detectable change.
  5. Linearity, hysteresis and drift describe how far a real sensor departs from an ideal straight-line response.
  6. First-order sensors lag with a time constant τ; second-order sensors add overshoot and ringing set by damping ratio ζ and natural frequency ωₙ.
  7. Error analysis separates systematic bias from random scatter and reports a best estimate with an uncertainty.
  8. A measurement chain from sensor to display must be analysed as a system, because each block adds its own error and lag.

Understand Compare active and passive sensors. Explain how the classification decides wiring and signal conditioning, and give two examples of each. 12 marks

Points to cover. Define active and passive sensors with self-generating and externally energised behaviour; Examples: thermocouple and piezoelectric sensor as active; strain gauge and thermistor as passive; Active sensors need no excitation but may need high-impedance amplifiers; Passive sensors need a stable supply and a bridge or divider circuit; Mention output form and typical signal-conditioning blocks.

Model answer.

  1. Definitions. An active (self-generating) sensor converts energy taken from the measurand directly into an electrical output; it needs no supply to sense. A passive (externally energised) sensor changes one of its electrical parameters, resistance, inductance or capacitance, when the measurand changes, and an external supply turns that change into a voltage or current.
  2. The deciding question. Ask where the energy in the output signal comes from. From the measurand: active. From a battery or excitation source: passive. A sensor whose amplifier needs a supply is not active for that reason; the label describes the sensing element only.
  3. Examples. Active: a thermocouple (Seebeck emf, about 41 µV/°C for type K), a piezoelectric crystal (charge q = d·F under force), a photovoltaic cell and a tachogenerator. Passive: an RTD such as Pt100, a thermistor, a strain gauge, a potentiometer, an LVDT and a capacitive sensor. In a hospital, the crystal of an ultrasound probe receiving echoes is active; the thermistor of a temperature probe is passive.
  4. Wiring of an active sensor. The sensor is a source with an emf e and a source resistance Rs. The amplifier input Rin forms a divider with it, so the voltage read is e·Rin/(Rin + Rs). With e = 25 mV and Rs = 500 Ω:
    RinVinloading loss

So the signal conditioning for an active sensor is a high-input-impedance amplifier (an instrumentation amplifier or a charge amplifier for a piezoelectric crystal), plus what the principle needs: cold-junction compensation for a thermocouple, a charge amplifier that sets the low-frequency limit for a piezo element.
  6. Wiring of a passive sensor. The sensor needs excitation: a constant current, or a voltage feeding a divider or a Wheatstone bridge. The signal is ΔV = I × ΔR for a current-excited resistor. A Pt100 at 100.0 Ω run at 1 mA gives 100 mV and dissipates P = I²R = (10⁻³)² × 100 = 0.10 mW, which warms the element: the excitation level is part of the measurement design.
  7. Signal conditioning for a passive sensor: a stable excitation source (its drift appears directly in the output, unless the circuit is ratiometric), a bridge to null the large standing output, a differential amplifier, and for inductive or capacitive sensors an AC carrier with a demodulator.

Remember State four physical principles used in sensors. Explain how each converts a measurand into an electrical quantity. 10 marks

Points to cover. Variable resistance: strain gauge, thermistor; Variable capacitance: parallel-plate capacitive pressure sensor; Variable inductance: LVDT or reluctance sensor; Thermoelectric emf: thermocouple; Piezoelectric charge: piezoelectric accelerometer; For each, name the physical law and the electrical output.

Model answer.

  1. Idea. Every sensor uses a physical law that converts the non-electrical measurand into a change of an electrical quantity: resistance, capacitance, inductance, emf or charge. Name the law, the measurand, the intermediate change and the output for each principle.
  2. 1. Variable resistance (strain and temperature). R = ρL/A. Stretching a wire raises L and lowers A; the strain-gauge equation is ΔR/R = GF·ε with GF ≈ 2 for metal foil. A 120 Ω gauge with GF = 2 at a strain of 500 × 10⁻⁶ changes by 120 × 2 × 500 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.12 Ω. Thermistors and RTDs use the change of ρ with temperature. Output: ΔR, read in a bridge. Use: load cells, the disposable blood-pressure transducer.
  3. 2. Variable capacitance. C = ε₀εrA/d. Pressure moves a diaphragm and changes d; humidity changes εr; a moving plate changes A. With A = 25 mm² and d = 0.10 mm of air, C = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² × 25 × 10⁻⁶/10⁻⁴ = 2.21 pF; closing the gap to 0.09 mm gives 2.46 pF, an 11.1 % rise for a 10 % gap change, so the response is non-linear in d. Output: ΔC, read with an AC bridge or charge amplifier.
  4. 3. Variable inductance and induction. L ≈ N²µA/l. A moving iron core changes the reluctance of the magnetic path and so the inductance; in an LVDT it changes the coupling between a primary and two secondaries. Faraday’s law e = −N·dΦ/dt gives an emf when flux changes, the basis of tachogenerators and the electromagnetic blood-flow meter. Output: ΔL (with an AC carrier) or emf.
  5. 4. Thermoelectric emf. Two dissimilar metals joined at two junctions at different temperatures give an emf e = S·ΔT (Seebeck effect). With S = 40 µV/°C and ΔT = 25 °C, e = 1.0 mV. The output depends on the temperature difference, so the reference junction must be known or compensated. Output: a small DC emf, read by a high-impedance amplifier.
  6. 5. Piezoelectric charge. A force on quartz or PZT displaces charge: q = d·F. With d = 2.3 pC/N (quartz) and F = 10 N, q = 23 pC. The charge leaks away, so the sensor reads changing forces only: vibration, impact, ultrasound echoes. Output: charge, converted by a charge amplifier.

Understand Explain calibration, verification, adjustment and traceability. Why is traceability necessary in a calibration record? 10 marks

Points to cover. Calibration: comparison with a standard and recording results; Verification: checking that the instrument meets a specification; Adjustment: physical change to bring output closer to the true value; Traceability: unbroken chain of comparisons with stated uncertainties to a national standard; Traceability makes readings legally and technically defensible.

Model answer.

  1. Calibration. The documented comparison of an instrument’s output with known inputs produced by a standard of smaller uncertainty, under stated conditions. It is a measurement and a record; it need not change the instrument. Its result is a table or line of deviations with their uncertainty, written on a certificate.
  2. Verification. A pass/fail check that the instrument’s error lies within stated limits, for example ±0.5 %FS. A verified instrument has not necessarily been calibrated: verification gives a verdict, calibration gives the numbers.
  3. Adjustment. The physical or software change of zero and span so that the output matches the ideal line. The order is: calibrate (“as found”), adjust zero then span, recheck zero because the two interact, then calibrate again (“as left”).
  4. Worked illustration. A 0–200 kPa sensor checked against a dead-weight tester at 25 °C and 10 V supply gave:
    P (kPa)up Vdown Vend-point line V
    00.020.030.020
    1001.982.022.010

  5. Sensitivity = 3.98 V/200 kPa = 19.9 mV/kPa; zero 0.02 V; largest deviation from the end-point line 0.030 V at 100 kPa = 0.75 % of the 3.98 V span; hysteresis 0.04 V = 1.0 %FS. That is the calibration. If the tolerance is ±1 %FS, verification passes on linearity and is borderline on hysteresis. Adjustment would remove the 0.02 V zero offset but cannot remove hysteresis.
  6. Traceability. An unbroken chain of calibrations, each with a stated uncertainty and documented, linking the result to the SI units through national primary standards. A typical chain: SI definition (fixed constants since 2019) → national primary standard at the national metrology institute → secondary standard of an accredited calibration laboratory → working standard (the dead-weight tester) → the field sensor. Each rung is four to ten times more accurate than the one below.

(iii) It is demanded by quality systems and regulators: an auditor asks for the certificate of the standard used, and of the standard that calibrated it. (iv) It dates the claim: when a certificate expires the chain is broken, and accuracy can no longer be claimed even if the readings repeat well.
  8. Check. Traceability does not make an instrument accurate; it makes its error known. A sensor with a 2 % error and a valid traceable certificate is usable with a correction; a sensor that repeats to 0.1 % with no certificate has unknown accuracy.
Remember Define accuracy, precision, resolution, sensitivity, linearity, span, range and dead band. Give one practical example of each. 12 marks

Points to cover. Accuracy versus precision with a target analogy; Resolution as smallest detectable input change; Sensitivity as Δy/Δx with units; Linearity as maximum deviation from a best-fit or end-point straight line; Range = the input limits; span = their difference; Dead band as input change that produces no output change.

Model answer.

  1. Take one example sensor throughout: a pressure sensor, range 0–250 kPa, output 0–50 mV.
  2. Range. The input limits the sensor is made for: 0 to 250 kPa. Span. The difference of the limits: 250 − 0 = 250 kPa. A 50–250 kPa sensor has the same upper limit but a span of 200 kPa, so range and span are different ideas.
  3. Sensitivity. The slope of output against input, S = Δy/Δx. Here S = 50 mV/250 kPa = 0.2 mV/kPa. A disposable blood-pressure transducer is rated 5 µV/V/mmHg: at 5 V excitation it gives 25 µV/mmHg, so 120 mmHg gives 3.0 mV.
  4. Accuracy. How close the mean reading is to the true value, quoted as ± value, ± %FS or ± % of reading. Ten readings at a true 100 kPa with mean 99.6 kPa have a bias of −0.4 kPa = −0.16 %FS. ±1 %FS allows ±2.5 kPa at any reading; ±1 % of reading allows only ±0.1 kPa at 10 kPa.
  5. Precision. How closely repeated readings agree, as a standard deviation or a band. With s = 0.5 kPa, the 3s band is ±1.5 kPa = ±0.6 %FS. A precise instrument can be inaccurate: a fixed bias moves the mean while the readings stay close together (a tight cluster of shots away from the bull’s-eye).
  6. Resolution. The smallest input change that gives a detectable output change. A display reading 100.1 kPa resolves 0.1 kPa = 0.04 %FS. A clinical thermometer resolving 0.1 °C is an everyday example.
  7. Linearity. The largest deviation of the calibration curve from a stated straight line (end-point, best-fit or independent), as %FS. A curve that bows 0.6 mV above the end-point line has non-linearity 0.6/0.2 = 3 kPa = 1.2 %FS.
  8. Dead band. The input range over which the output does not change at all, typically when the input reverses direction, caused by backlash or friction. A dial gauge whose pointer does not move until the input has reversed by 0.5 kPa has a 0.5 kPa dead band. (Threshold is the smallest input that moves the output from rest at zero.)

Understand Distinguish systematic, random and gross errors. Define absolute error, relative error and percentage error. 10 marks

Points to cover. Systematic error or bias: repeatable shift, correctable by calibration; Random error: scatter, reduced by averaging and statistics; Gross error or blunder: human mistake, rejected, not averaged; Absolute error = measured − true, with units; Relative error = absolute error / true value; Percentage error = relative error × 100%.

Model answer.

  1. Gross errors (blunders). Human or procedural mistakes: reading the wrong scale, writing 1.98 as 1.89, using the wrong range or unit, a loose lead. They are large and irregular. Statistics cannot correct them; they are prevented by care and checks, and found by repeating the measurement. A reading many standard deviations from the rest is a candidate, rejected only with a stated rule and a recorded cause.
  2. Systematic errors (bias). Errors with the same sign and nearly the same size on every repeat. Sources: instrumental (calibration offset, scale-factor error, zero drift), environmental (temperature, supply voltage, magnetic fields), loading (a meter drawing current from the circuit it measures), and method (a blood-pressure cuff too small for the arm reads high every time). Averaging does not reduce them; they are found by calibration against a standard and removed by a correction.
  3. Random errors. Small unpredictable variations from noise, vibration, supply ripple and thermal agitation. They change sign and size from reading to reading. They are described by the standard deviation s of repeated readings, and the mean of n independent readings has standard uncertainty s/√n, so averaging reduces them.
  4. Absolute error. e = xm − xtrue, in the unit of the reading, where xm is the measured value and xtrue the true (accepted) value. The correction is −e.
  5. Relative error. er = (xm − xtrue)/xtrue, dimensionless. Percentage error = er × 100 %.
  6. Worked example. A voltmeter reads 5.10 V across a 5.00 V reference.

    Absolute error5.10 − 5.00+0.10 V

  7. How averaging separates them. Ten readings of a 5.000 V reference with mean 5.000 V and s = 18.3 mV have zero bias and a standard uncertainty of the mean 18.3/√10 = 5.8 mV. If the meter also had a +0.2 V calibration offset, the mean of ten, or of a thousand, readings would still be 0.2 V high: only the 5.8 mV term shrinks with n.
  8. Reducing each kind. Gross errors: two people read critical values, ranges are checked before reading, and an automatic data logger replaces hand copying. Systematic errors: calibrate against a traceable standard, apply the correction, hold or compensate the influencing inputs (temperature, supply), and choose a meter whose input impedance makes loading negligible. Random errors: shield and ground the leads, filter the signal, and average n readings so that the scatter of the mean falls as 1/√n.
  9. Check. Classify each error by asking: is it a mistake (gross), does it repeat (systematic), or does it scatter (random)? Then the cure follows: repeat, calibrate, or average.

Evaluate A neonatal weighing scale must measure from 0.1 kg to 20 kg with a resolution of 5 g. Choose a sensing principle, justify active or passive operation, and evaluate the static characteristics needed. 15 marks

Points to cover. Load cell with four strain gauges in a full Wheatstone bridge: passive; Justify against piezoelectric (no static reading); Divisions needed and the signal per division; Non-linearity, hysteresis, creep and temperature drift within one division; Calibration with standard masses and traceability; Excitation, amplifier, ADC, filtering and hygiene.

Model answer.

  1. Principle. A strain-gauge load cell: four foil gauges on a machined aluminium or steel element, wired as a full Wheatstone bridge, carry the weighing platform. The gauges change resistance by ΔR/R = GF·ε (GF ≈ 2) with the elastic strain of the element.
  2. Active or passive. The bridge is passive: it needs an excitation voltage, and the output is proportional to it (rated output in mV/V). A piezoelectric (active) sensor is ruled out because a weight is a static force and the piezoelectric charge leaks away; it cannot hold a reading for the seconds a weighing takes. Passive operation also allows ratiometric measurement: the ADC reference is taken from the same excitation, so supply drift cancels.
  3. Number of divisions. 20 kg/5 g = 4000 divisions. A 12-bit converter has 4096 codes, too few once noise and tare are allowed for; a 24-bit sigma-delta converter is used in practice.
  4. Signal per division. Choose a 30 kg cell (50 % overload margin for a baby pushed down hard) with rated output 2 mV/V and 5 V excitation: 10 mV at 30 kg. At 20 kg the output is 10 × 20/30 = 6.67 mV, and one 5 g division is 6.67 mV × 5/20 000 = 1.67 µV. The amplifier’s input noise and drift must be well below this.
  5. Static characteristics required. One division is 5/30 000 = 0.017 % of the rated capacity. So the combined non-linearity and hysteresis of the cell must stay near or below 0.017 % of rated output; creep (slow output change under constant load) must be smaller over the 10 to 30 s of a weighing; zero and span temperature drift must stay within one division over the room’s 15 to 35 °C, using the cell’s built-in compensation resistors.
  6. Error budget (illustrative, in grams at 20 kg).
    SourceRequirement
    Resolution5 g (one division)
    Non-linearity plus hysteresis≤ 5 g (0.017 % of 30 kg)
    Creep in 30 s≤ 2.5 g
    Zero drift, 15 to 35 °C≤ 5 g, or auto-zero at each tare
    Electronic noise≤ 1.67 µV peak at the bridge
  7. Dynamic point. A baby moves, so the force fluctuates. A low-pass filter or averaging over 2 to 3 s steadies the reading, and the display waits until successive averages agree within one division.
  8. Calibration. With traceable standard masses (for example 1, 5, 10 and 20 kg, each calibrated against secondary standards), up-scale and down-scale, at the clinic’s temperature; the certificate records non-linearity and hysteresis, and the scale is checked again after any shock.
  9. Practical design. Shielded four- or six-wire cable (sense lines remove the cable’s voltage drop), a sealed, cleanable platform, overload stops, battery operation for isolation from mains leakage, and a tare key for the blanket.
  10. Evaluation. The strain-gauge load cell meets the 5 g resolution with margin in electronics but only just in mechanics: the cell’s combined error class and creep decide the result, so they, not the ADC bits, set the specification.

Points to cover. Model answer.

  1. Model. A first-order sensor with unit sensitivity obeys τ dy/dt + y = x(t). The input is a ramp starting at t = 0: x = kt, with k in °C/s (or any unit per second), and y(0) = 0.

Evaluate Compare piezoresistive and capacitive MEMS pressure sensors for a tyre-pressure monitor. Consider sensitivity, temperature drift, power, size and signal conditioning. 14 marks

Points to cover. Piezoresistive: Wheatstone bridge, simple readout, moderate sensitivity, temperature drift; Capacitive: high sensitivity, low power, needs capacitance-to-voltage circuitry; Both can be micromachined and made small; Tyre monitor needs wide temperature range and long battery life; Evaluate drift, packaging and calibration cost; Give a reasoned choice with trade-offs.

Model answer.

  1. Need. A tyre-pressure monitor sits inside the wheel: pressure around 200 to 250 kPa, temperature from below freezing to well above 100 °C after hard braking, strong shocks, and a sealed battery that must last for years. Power and drift decide the choice more than raw sensitivity.
  2. Piezoresistive MEMS sensor. A thin silicon diaphragm with four diffused resistors in a Wheatstone bridge. Pressure bends the diaphragm; the resistors change by the piezoresistive effect, gauge factor 50 to 200, so the output is tens of millivolts at full scale and easy to read with a DC amplifier. Weak points: the bridge draws current whenever it is on, and silicon piezoresistance falls with temperature, so offset and span need temperature compensation.
  3. Capacitive MEMS sensor. The diaphragm is one plate of a capacitor over a fixed electrode; pressure changes the gap: C = εA/d. A switched-capacitor readout charges a few picofarads, so the energy per reading is tiny and there is no standing current. Temperature effects are smaller because C depends on geometry, not on a temperature-sensitive resistivity. Weak points: C is non-linear in d (a 10 % gap reduction raises C by 11.1 %), the signal is a fraction of a picofarad, and stray capacitance must be guarded, so the readout chip is more complex.

Glossary

Accuracy
The closeness of a measured value to the true value.
Active sensor
A self-generating sensor that produces an electrical output without an external excitation supply.
Actuator
A device that converts an electrical signal into a physical action or quantity.
Adjustment
A physical or software change made to bring an instrument's output closer to the true value.
Calibration
The process of comparing a sensor's output with a reference standard and recording the result.
Damping ratio
The ratio of actual damping to critical damping in a second-order system.
Dead band
The range of input change that produces no detectable change in output.
Drift
A slow change in output with time or ambient conditions when the input is constant.
Hysteresis
The difference between output readings for the same input when approached from opposite directions.
Inverse transducer
A transducer that converts an electrical signal back into a non-electrical physical quantity.
Limiting error
The guaranteed worst-case error of a reading or of a result computed from several readings.
Linearity
The closeness of a sensor's calibration curve to a straight line.
Measurand
The physical quantity that a sensor is intended to measure.
Passive sensor
A sensor whose electrical property changes with the measurand and which needs an external supply.
Precision
The closeness of repeated measurements to one another.
Primary standard
A national standard that realises an SI unit directly; used only to calibrate secondary standards.
Random error
An unpredictable scatter in repeated readings that can be reduced by averaging.
Range
The lower and upper limits of input over which a sensor is specified to operate.
Resolution
The smallest input change that produces a detectable output change.
Secondary standard
A standard calibrated against the primary standard and held by a calibration laboratory.
Sensitivity
The ratio of output change to input change, S = Δy/Δx.
Sensor
A device that responds to a physical quantity and produces a measurable output.
Span
The difference between the upper and lower range limits; the output span is the matching difference of outputs.
Systematic error
A repeatable error that shifts all readings by the same amount or in the same direction.
Time constant
The time for a first-order sensor to reach 63.2% of its final response to a step input.
Traceability
A documented chain of comparisons linking a measurement to a recognised standard.
Transducer
A device that converts one form of energy into another, often a physical quantity into an electrical signal.
Uncertainty
A quantified range around a measured value within which the true value is expected to lie.
Verification
Checking that an instrument meets a stated specification or requirement.
Working standard
A standard used every day to calibrate field instruments, checked against a secondary standard.
