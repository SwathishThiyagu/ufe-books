3.1 Self and mutual inductance transducers; variable reluctance

A coil wound on an iron core stores magnetic energy. Its inductance depends on how easily flux crosses the magnetic path. Put a movable iron armature across a small air gap, and the gap reluctance controls the inductance. A tiny movement changes the gap, so the coil inductance changes. This is the variable reluctance transducer. It measures displacement, thickness and pressure, and needs AC excitation because a steady current gives no inductive change. Two coils can also be coupled so that movement changes mutual inductance.

You need: Unit 1 sensitivity, linearity and calibration; basic magnetic circuits (reluctance and Faraday's law) from physics.

Reluctance sets self inductance

Self inductance L is the flux linkage per ampere in one coil. For a coil of N turns on a magnetic circuit of reluctance R, L = N²/R. Reluctance R is the magnetic resistance: R = l/(μ A), where l is path length in metres, A is cross-sectional area in square metres, and μ is permeability in henries per metre (H/m). Iron has large μ; air has μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m.

L = N² / R

L is self inductance in henries (H); N is the number of turns (no unit); R is the reluctance of the magnetic path in ampere-turns per weber (A·t/Wb) or H⁻¹.

R = g / (μ₀ A)

g is air-gap length in metres; μ₀ is free-space permeability = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m; A is the gap area in square metres.

An iron core with a coil and a movable armature has a narrow air gap g. The total reluctance is the iron reluctance plus the gap reluctance. Iron μ is hundreds or thousands of times μ₀, so the gap usually dominates. Then R ≈ g/(μ₀ A), and L ≈ μ₀ A N² / g. Movement of the armature changes g, so L changes. This is the single-coil, or self-inductance, variable reluctance transducer.

  1. Construction: a laminated iron core, one coil, a movable iron armature, and a small air gap.
  2. Operation: energise the coil with AC; the current sets flux; the gap sets reluctance; L follows.
  3. Read-out: put the coil in an AC bridge; armature movement unbalances the bridge.

Two-coil mutual inductance type

A second coil can be placed on the same magnetic path. Current in coil 1 sets flux; part of it links coil 2. Mutual inductance M is the flux linkage in coil 2 per ampere in coil 1. M = k√(L₁L₂), where L₁ and L₂ are the self inductances and k is the coupling coefficient, 0 ≤ k ≤ 1. k = 1 means all flux links both coils; k = 0 means no shared flux.

M = k √(L₁ L₂)

M is mutual inductance in henries (H); L₁ and L₂ are the self inductances of the two coils in henries; k is the coupling coefficient, 0 ≤ k ≤ 1.

In the mutual-inductance transducer, coil 1 is the primary and coil 2 is the secondary. The armature or core moves and changes the air gap under one coil. That changes L₁, L₂ and k. The AC voltage induced in the secondary, e₂ = M di₁/dt, changes with position. A larger gap gives lower M and lower output. Construction uses two coils on a common iron core, with a movable armature or a movable core.

Characteristics and applications

For a simple air gap, L ∝ 1/g. The curve is nonlinear. Sensitivity is dL/dg = -μ₀ A N² / g². The minus sign means L falls as g rises. The magnitude falls as g rises, so the sensor is most sensitive at small gaps. Keep the working gap small and calibrate over the range.

S = dL/dg = - μ₀ A N² / g²

S is sensitivity in henries per metre (H/m); the negative sign shows L falls when g increases; other symbols are as above.

Inductance does not respond to a steady current. The coil needs AC excitation, typically a few kilohertz. The bridge output is an AC voltage whose amplitude follows displacement. A phase reversal can indicate direction in some circuits; bridge details are in Unit 6.

  • Displacement: armature movement changes gap; range typically from fractions of a millimetre to a few millimetres.
  • Thickness: a metal sheet under the core changes the effective gap; thickness or profile can be checked.
  • Pressure: a diaphragm moves the armature; pressure bends the diaphragm and changes the gap.
  • Some syllabi call these variable reluctance transducers or inductive transducers. For a more linear displacement output, see LVDT.

Worked example

Question. A variable reluctance displacement sensor has N = 500 turns, core area A = 2 × 10⁻⁴ m², nominal air gap g = 1.0 mm, and μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m. Find the inductance at 1.0 mm and at 1.2 mm, and the percentage change.

  1. Convert gap: g₁ = 1.0 mm = 1.0 × 10⁻³ m; g₂ = 1.2 mm = 1.2 × 10⁻³ m.
  2. Use L ≈ μ₀ A N² / g, because the iron reluctance is small compared with the air gap.
  3. Compute numerator: μ₀ A N² = (4π × 10⁻⁷)(2 × 10⁻⁴)(500²) = 6.283 × 10⁻⁵ H·m.
  4. At g₁: L₁ = 6.283 × 10⁻⁵ / 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 6.283 × 10⁻² H = 0.06283 H = 62.83 mH.
  5. At g₂: L₂ = 6.283 × 10⁻⁵ / 1.2 × 10⁻³ = 5.236 × 10⁻² H = 0.05236 H = 52.36 mH.
  6. Percentage change = ((L₂ - L₁)/L₁) × 100 = ((0.05236 - 0.06283)/0.06283) × 100 = -16.67%.

Answer. The inductance is 0.06283 H at 1.0 mm and 0.05236 H at 1.2 mm; the gap rise of 20% gives a fall of 16.67%.

Exam traps

  • Treating L as linear in gap. L ∝ 1/g, so a 20% gap rise gives about 16.7% fall, not 20%.
  • Using a DC ohmmeter to measure inductance. A coil's resistance is not its inductance; AC excitation and a bridge or meter are needed.
  • Forgetting the air gap dominates. Iron reluctance is small, but at very small gaps it can matter; do not use L = μ₀AN²/g blindly for a closed core.
  • Mixing self and mutual inductance. L uses one coil; M uses two coils and k. M = k√(L₁L₂), not L₁ + L₂.
  • Ignoring sign and direction. L falls as gap rises; a larger gap gives lower output. Phase can indicate direction only in a suitable circuit.

3.2 LVDT: construction, characteristics and applications

An LVDT (linear variable differential transformer) turns a small straight-line movement of a magnetic core into an AC voltage. At the centre the two secondary windings give equal voltages and the output is zero. Slide the core either way and one secondary gains while the other loses, so the difference is proportional to the distance moved, and its phase tells the direction. The core never touches the windings, so there is no wear and almost no friction. You will find LVDTs in machine tools, hydraulic valves, thickness gauges and pressure transmitters.

You need: 3.1 for mutual inductance and reluctance, and Unit 1 for linearity and sensitivity.

Construction

An LVDT is a transformer built on a hollow insulating tube called the former. One primary winding and two secondary windings sit on this tube. A rod of magnetic material, the core, slides inside the tube without touching it.

  • Former: an insulating cylinder, often fibreglass or epoxy, that holds the windings and lets the core move freely.
  • Primary winding: wound at the centre of the former and driven by an AC source.
  • Secondary windings S₁ and S₂: identical, wound symmetrically on either side of the primary, so a centred core links both equally.
  • Core: soft iron or nickel–iron with high permeability, fixed to the part whose movement is measured.
  • Shield: a magnetic shield of high-permeability alloy around the assembly, plus an earthed case, to reject stray fields and electrostatic pickup.

The two secondaries are connected in series opposition, so the terminals give the difference of their voltages. A drift in supply voltage or temperature hits both windings almost equally, and the difference cancels it. That is why the device is called differential, and why it is steadier than the single-coil variable-reluctance sensor of 3.1.

V_out = V₁ − V₂ = K · x

V₁ and V₂ are the rms voltages induced in S₁ and S₂, in volts; x is the core displacement from the centre position, in millimetres, positive toward S₁; K is the sensitivity in volts per millimetre at the excitation actually used. Datasheets often quote sensitivity per volt of excitation, S in mV/(mm·V); at an excitation of V_exc volts rms the constant is K = S × V_exc, in volts per millimetre.

How the output voltage is formed

The primary is excited from an AC source, usually a few volts rms at a frequency between about 1 kHz and 10 kHz. The primary current sets up a magnetic flux along the tube. The core, a good path for flux, carries that flux to the secondaries, and each secondary gets a voltage by mutual induction.

  1. Hold the core exactly at the centre. Both secondaries link the same flux, so V₁ = V₂ and V_out = 0. This is the null position.
  2. Move the core toward S₁. More flux passes through S₁ and less through S₂, so V₁ rises, V₂ falls, and V_out = V₁ − V₂ grows in phase with the excitation.
  3. Move the core the other way. Now V₁ < V₂ and the difference swings 180° in phase; the magnitude is the same for the same distance, but the sign has flipped.
  4. Feed the output to a phase-sensitive demodulator, which compares it with the excitation and produces a signed DC voltage.

Magnitude alone cannot separate the two sides of null, because a plain voltmeter reads the same on both. The phase carries the direction, and a demodulator recovers it (see Unit 6, Signal Conditioning, Data Acquisition and Actuators).

Displacement is then read from x = V_out / K. The relation holds only inside the linear range, roughly the middle portion of the core travel.

Applications

  • Displacement: direct measurement of valve lift, beam deflection or specimen extension, static or slowly varying.
  • Thickness: a roller rides on the sheet or foil and the core moves with the roller, so a change in thickness becomes a voltage. Used on paper, plastic film and metal strip.
  • Pressure: a Bourdon tube or diaphragm moves the core; pressure becomes displacement and then voltage (see 3.7 for how the sensing element itself is built).
  • Position feedback: servo systems, machine-tool slides, hydraulic spool position and aircraft control surfaces.
  • Why it is chosen: it measures static displacement, needs no contact, and gives a bipolar output that tells direction as well as size.

Worked example

Question. An LVDT is excited at 5 V rms. At a core position the secondary voltages are V₁ = 2.00 V rms and V₂ = 1.60 V rms. The sensitivity is 0.50 V/mm at that 5 V rms excitation. Find (a) the differential output, (b) the displacement, (c) the direction, (d) the output at null.

  1. Differential output: V_out = V₁ − V₂ = 2.00 − 1.60 = 0.40 V rms.
  2. The constant K = 0.50 V/mm applies at the 5 V rms drive used here; the per-volt figure is S = 100 mV/(mm·V), and 100 mV/(mm·V) × 5 V = 0.50 V/mm.
  3. x = V_out / K = 0.40 / 0.50 = 0.80 mm.
  4. V₁ is larger than V₂, so the core has moved toward S₁, the side whose secondary gained voltage. Direction: 0.80 mm toward S₁.
  5. At null the core is centred, so V₁ = V₂ and V_out = 0 V rms ideally; a real unit leaves a few millivolts of residual voltage.
  6. The same 0.40 V rms reading would appear at 0.80 mm on the S₂ side, but with opposite phase. Only the phase separates the two.

Answer. V_out = 0.40 V rms, displacement = 0.80 mm toward S₁, and the ideal null output is 0 V.

Exam traps

  • Reading the AC magnitude alone as displacement. A plain voltmeter gives the same reading on both sides of null; the phase must be demodulated to get the sign.
  • Writing V_out = V₁ + V₂. The secondaries are in series opposition, so the output is the difference; the sum is what a wrongly wired unit gives, and it stays large even at null.
  • Assuming the output is zero at null in a real sensor. Residual voltage from winding mismatch and harmonics always leaves a small offset.
  • Using V_out = K·x beyond the linear range and expecting a straight line near the ends of core travel.
  • Forgetting the excitation level. A sensitivity quoted per volt of excitation must be multiplied by the actual drive voltage before it is used as K.

3.3 Induction potentiometer, synchros and microsyn

A rotary inductive device turns shaft angle into an AC voltage. One winding sits on the stator, one on the rotor, and the mutual inductance between them changes as the shaft turns, so the induced voltage carries the angle in its amplitude and phase. Three devices use this idea: the induction potentiometer, the synchro pair and the microsyn. Only the microsyn is truly brushless, because its rotor carries no winding. The other two reach their rotor winding through slip rings. Their gain over a resistive potentiometer is no sliding contact and no wiper noise. Uses: radar antenna pointing, valve position feedback, AC servo error detection.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for mutual inductance and Chapter 3.2 for amplitude-plus-phase output.

The common idea: angle sets the coupling

Most of these devices put one winding on the stator and one on the rotor that follows the shaft. The excitation drives one winding; its flux crosses the air gap and links the other. The strength of that link is the mutual inductance M, in henry (H). Turning the shaft changes M smoothly: M = M_max cos θ, where M_max is the peak mutual inductance and θ is the shaft angle measured from maximum coupling.

By Faraday's law the output is an AC voltage at the excitation frequency f, with amplitude proportional to M and therefore to cos θ. When θ passes the null, M changes sign and the output flips 180° in phase. Amplitude gives the size of the angle, phase gives the side of the null.

The output is a carrier: a fixed-frequency AC wave whose envelope follows the shaft. A phase-sensitive demodulator turns it into a signed DC voltage. Keep f well above the fastest shaft motion.

Induction potentiometer

An induction potentiometer is a rotary transformer with a distributed winding. The stator winding is spread over many slots, so its mutual inductance to the rotor winding varies smoothly with angle instead of in steps. The rotor carries a single winding, fed through slip rings, and the output is taken from the stator. Nothing slides on a resistance track.

e_o = k · E_x · sin θ

e_o is the output rms voltage (V); k is the coupling constant, less than 1; E_x is the rms excitation voltage (V); θ is the shaft angle from electrical zero.

Here θ is measured from electrical zero, the position of minimum coupling, so the cos becomes a sin. The sine is linear only near the null, where sin θ ≈ θ with θ in radians. A few tens of degrees is the usual working range; beyond that the scale factor falls away. Full 360° measurement needs a resolver or a two-speed arrangement.

At the null the output is not exactly zero. Winding imperfections and space harmonics leave a residual called the null voltage, typically a few millivolts, and it sets the smallest angle the device can resolve. Against a resistive potentiometer: no sliding contact, no wear, no contact noise, but you need AC excitation and demodulation, and the range is limited. Typical uses are valve stem position and slow remote angle indication.

Synchro transmitter and control transformer

A synchro transmitter has a single-phase rotor on the shaft and a three-phase stator: three identical windings spaced 120° electrical. The rotor is excited. Each stator phase is coupled to the rotor axis at its own angle, so the three phase voltages are K·E_r·cos θ, K·E_r·cos(θ − 120°) and K·E_r·cos(θ + 120°), where E_r is the rotor excitation (V rms), θ is the rotor angle and K is a constant. The three voltages together fix θ over the full 360°.

The control transformer looks the same but works in reverse. Its three-phase stator is wired to the transmitter's stator, so it receives the same flux pattern; its single-phase rotor is the output. With zero set at θ_R = θ_T, the rotor voltage is e = E_max·sin Δθ, where E_max is the output at 90° error (V rms), θ_R is the control transformer rotor angle and θ_T is the transmitter rotor angle.

Near the null sin Δθ ≈ Δθ in radians, so the output measures the error directly, and its phase against the excitation says which way the error lies. Feed it to a servo amplifier and the motor turns until Δθ = 0. This is the standard error detector of an AC position servo, used in radar antenna pointing and valve positioners.

The two units are not interchangeable. The transmitter rotor is a low-impedance primary that must hold flux constant; the control transformer rotor is a high-impedance secondary built to give a small, clean null.

Microsyn and practical checks

A microsyn is a compact, sensitive version. The stator and a passive rotor both carry fine teeth separated by a very small air gap. Four stator windings are connected as a bridge: two excited, two giving the output. At the null the teeth align and the bridge balances; a small rotation spoils the alignment and unbalances the bridge, so a tiny angle gives a large output. The rotor has no winding, so no slip rings are needed. Microsyns appear in gyro pick-offs and precision instruments.

Exam traps

  • Using degrees in E_max·Δθ. The sine form accepts degrees, but the linear form needs radians.
  • Assuming the control transformer output is proportional to Δθ itself. At 10° the sine is 0.174, not 0.10.
  • Treating a synchro transmitter and a control transformer as interchangeable. Their rotor windings are designed for opposite roles.
  • Reading only the magnitude. Without phase you cannot tell which side of the null the error lies on.
  • Confusing the induction potentiometer (one output winding, cannot separate θ from 180° − θ) with the synchro transmitter (three stator voltages fix the angle over 360°).

3.4 Eddy current transducers

An eddy current transducer is a contactless inductive sensor. An AC-excited coil in a probe sets up an alternating magnetic field. When a conducting target sits near the probe, the field induces circulating eddy currents in the target. These currents create their own field, which opposes the coil field and changes the coil impedance. The change depends on the gap, called lift-off, and on the target material. Eddy current probes measure small displacement, vibration, thickness and cracks, and sort metals by conductivity, often in dirty workshops where optical sensors fail.

You need: 3.1 for inductance and magnetic reluctance, and Unit 1 for calibration and sensitivity.

Principle: eddy currents load the coil

Drive the probe coil with an alternating current. The coil sets up an alternating magnetic field around its face. Bring a conducting target near the face. The changing field induces an electromotive force (EMF) in the target. Because the target is a closed conducting path, this EMF drives circulating currents. These are eddy currents. By Lenz's law their magnetic field opposes the coil field. The opposition reduces the effective inductance and adds resistive loss in the coil. The target is not touched and no wire is connected to it.

The coil impedance is Z = R + jωL. Here Z is the coil impedance in ohms (Ω), R is its resistance in Ω, L is its inductance in henry (H), f is the excitation frequency in hertz (Hz), ω = 2πf is the angular frequency in rad/s, and j is the imaginary unit. Eddy currents raise R and lower L for a non-magnetic target. For a magnetic target such as steel, permeability also raises L, so the net change is a combined effect. The gap between probe and target is called lift-off.

High excitation frequency helps. It makes the skin depth small: δ = √(2ρ/(ωμ)). Here δ is skin depth in metre (m), ρ is target resistivity in Ω·m, ω is angular frequency in rad/s, and μ is target permeability in H/m. Eddy currents then concentrate near the surface, so the probe is sensitive to surface cracks and thin layers.

Construction and signal chain

A typical probe has a small coil wound on a ferrite or ceramic former. The coil sits behind a protective face, often epoxy, so the target does not touch it. A shield around the coil limits stray fields. The oscillator drives the coil at a high frequency, often hundreds of kilohertz to a few megahertz. At that frequency the coil impedance is large enough to show a small gap change as a usable voltage change.

The bridge compares the probe-coil impedance with a reference arm. A change in gap, conductivity or permeability unbalances the bridge. The demodulator rectifies and filters the bridge output, giving a DC voltage proportional to the change. The output stage displays or sends the value. The oscillator, bridge and demodulator are signal-conditioning circuits; Unit 6 covers them. Figure 1 shows this chain.

Some instruments use a phase-sensitive demodulator. Lift-off and conductivity change the impedance in different directions, so the phase of the bridge signal helps separate gap from material.

Characteristics and applications

The sensing range is short, of the order of the coil diameter; a 5 mm coil gives a few millimetres of useful range. The output is analog and fast, so it can track vibration. It needs no contact, so there is no wear on the target. Dust, oil and dirt usually do not block the magnetic field. The reading depends on target conductivity and permeability, so the same gap gives different outputs on aluminium, brass and steel. Calibration is therefore material-specific. Some syllabi call these eddy current sensors or eddy current proximity probes.

  • Displacement and vibration: measure shaft runout or small gap changes in a machine.
  • Thickness: measure metal sheet thickness or a nonconductive coating on a metal base.
  • Crack detection: a surface crack interrupts eddy currents and changes coil impedance.
  • Conductivity sorting: separate metals or check heat treatment.
  • Proximity: detect that a metal part has arrived; for a clean ON/OFF output see 3.5.

The linear range is small, so do not extrapolate the calibration from zero to large gaps. Keep the probe perpendicular to the target and keep the lift-off within the rated range.

Worked example

Question. An eddy current displacement sensor has a linear calibration: at gap 1.0 mm its output is 6.0 V, and sensitivity is −2.5 V/mm (output falls as gap increases). Find the output at 2.2 mm and the gap when the output is 4.5 V.

  1. Write the linear calibration: V(g) = V₀ + S(g − g₀). Here V₀ = 6.0 V at g₀ = 1.0 mm, and S = −2.5 V/mm.
  2. For g = 2.2 mm, the gap change is g − g₀ = 2.2 − 1.0 = 1.2 mm.
  3. The sensitivity term is S(g − g₀) = −2.5 V/mm × 1.2 mm = −3.0 V.
  4. The output is V = 6.0 V − 3.0 V = 3.0 V.
  5. For V = 4.5 V, substitute: 4.5 = 6.0 − 2.5(g − 1.0).
  6. Rearrange: 4.5 − 6.0 = −1.5 V, and −1.5 V ÷ (−2.5 V/mm) = 0.6 mm = g − 1.0.
  7. The gap is g = 1.0 mm + 0.6 mm = 1.6 mm.

Answer. At 2.2 mm the output is 3.0 V, and an output of 4.5 V corresponds to a gap of 1.6 mm.

Exam traps

  • Using one calibration for all metals: aluminium, brass and steel have different conductivity and permeability, so the same gap gives different volts.
  • Dropping the sign of sensitivity: −2.5 V/mm means output falls as gap rises; a rising output means the gap is smaller.
  • Treating the range as zero to infinity: the linear range is only a small band around the set gap, roughly set by coil diameter.
  • Confusing lift-off with target thickness: lift-off is the probe-to-target gap; a coating changes the effective gap but is not the same quantity.
  • Calling it an ON/OFF proximity switch: this transducer gives an analog measurement; the switching version is a different use, see 3.5.

3.5 Proximity sensors

A proximity sensor answers: is the target here yet? It turns a short-range physical change — metal near a coil, or any object near an electrode — into a clean ON/OFF signal. Nothing touches the target, so nothing wears. Factories use them to count parts, find the end of a press stroke, guard a gate, and sense liquid level through a tank wall. Some syllabi call them proximity switches, because the output is a switch, not a measurement.

You need: 3.4 Eddy current transducers for why a conductor near a coil changes its impedance, and the threshold and calibration ideas from Unit 1, Foundations: Sensor Characteristics and Errors.

What the datasheet numbers mean

One sensor cannot switch at the same distance for every target, so the datasheet fixes the target, temperature and supply voltage first.

Rated sensing distance Sn is the switching distance with a standard square mild-steel target at 20–25 °C and rated supply voltage. It compares sensors; it is not a promise for your part. With that target the real operate point can fall between 0.9 Sn and 1.1 Sn.

S = K × Sn

S is usable switching distance in mm for the actual target; K is the material correction factor from the datasheet, 1.00 for mild steel; Sn is rated sensing distance in mm. K values differ by maker.

Hysteresis is the gap between ON distance as the target approaches and OFF distance as it leaves; release is farther. It stops chattering when a part rests at the field edge. Repeatability is the spread of the operate point over repeated cycles, usually a few percent of Sn.

Flush (shielded) sensors have a metal ring round the coil, so the field leaves only from the front face; they can sit in a steel bracket. Non-flush (unshielded) types reach farther but need clear space round the face.

Inductive: the field loses energy

The sensing element is a coil on a ferrite core behind the face, driven by an oscillator at roughly 100 kHz to 1 MHz.

  1. The oscillator holds the coil at fixed frequency and steady amplitude in air.
  2. A metal target enters the field. Alternating flux induces eddy currents in it (see 3.4).
  3. Eddy currents dissipate energy as heat. The energy comes from the coil, so field amplitude falls.
  4. A peak detector rectifies coil voltage and feeds a Schmitt trigger, a comparator with two thresholds. These are the datasheet hysteresis.
  5. Below the lower threshold the output turns ON; above the upper threshold, as the target leaves, it turns OFF.

Iron and steel are magnetic and conductive, so they change inductance too and give the longest range. Aluminium, copper and brass only carry eddy currents, lose less energy, and need K below 1.

The output is a transistor switch, not a relay contact. A PNP (sourcing) output connects the load to +24 V when ON; an NPN (sinking) output connects it to 0 V. Normally open (NO) conducts only with a target present; normally closed (NC) conducts until a target appears, the safe choice for a guard.

Capacitive: the target changes a capacitor

A capacitive sensor uses a flat metal electrode behind the face as one capacitor plate. The target is the other plate, and the gap is the dielectric.

C = ε₀ εr A / d

C is capacitance in farads; ε₀ = 8.85 × 10−12 F/m; εr is relative permittivity of the gap material (about 1 for air, 80 for water); A is effective plate area in m²; d is gap in m.

A hand, plastic bottle or rising liquid raises εr in the gap, so C rises. The circuit turns C into a voltage — often an RC oscillator whose frequency falls as C rises — and a threshold stage switches the output.

It detects paper, plastic, wood, grain and water, and senses level through a plastic tank wall without touching the liquid. Drift is the weakness: humidity, dust and condensation on the face change C too, so most units carry a sensitivity trimmer. Range depends on εr, so water switches from far away while thin plastic must come closer.

Measurement uses

  • Counting parts. A sensor over a conveyor gives one pulse per part; a counter or PLC adds them up.
  • End of stroke. On a hydraulic press, a sensor on the frame confirms the ram has reached the bottom before the next step.
  • Safety guard. An NC sensor on a gate holds a safety relay closed; when the gate opens, the machine stops.
  • Level. A capacitive sensor clamped outside a plastic tank switches when the liquid inside reaches its height.
  • Speed. Counting pulses from a rotating gear's teeth in one second and dividing by the number of teeth gives revolutions per second: pulses per second ÷ number of teeth.

3.6 Capacitive transducers: types and signal conditioning

Two metal plates with an insulator between them store charge when a voltage is applied. The capacitance depends on plate area, plate gap and the dielectric between them. A capacitive transducer makes one of these three quantities follow the measurand, so capacitance becomes a measure of displacement, pressure, level or humidity. The change is small, often less than a picofarad, so a signal-conditioning circuit must convert it into a usable voltage. These transducers load the object very little, resolve nanometre motion, and are cheap to make, which is why they sit inside phones, touch panels, pressure gauges and microphones.

You need: electrostatics, namely C = Q/V and the parallel-plate capacitor, plus resolution and linearity from Unit 1.

The parallel-plate capacitor as a sensor

Capacitance is the charge stored per volt, C = Q/V, measured in farads (F). For two parallel plates the value is fixed by geometry and material.

C = ε₀ εr A / d

C is capacitance in farads (F); ε₀ is the permittivity of free space = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m; εr is the relative permittivity of the material between the plates (1.0006 for air, about 2.1 for PTFE, about 80 for water); A is the overlapping plate area in square metres (m²); d is the plate gap in metres (m).

A transducer moves exactly one of d, A or εr with the measurand and holds the other two fixed. The capacitance then carries the reading.

The change is small: a plate of 100 mm² at a 0.5 mm gap gives about 1.8 pF. The circuit therefore works in picofarads. The plates can be light and stiff, so the sensor barely loads the object and can resolve nanometre motion. The same smallness is the weakness — a few picofarads of cable capacitance is as large as the signal.

  • High resolution and low force on the object.
  • C ∝ 1/d when the gap moves, so the response is nonlinear.
  • Stray capacitance, cable length and temperature set the accuracy limit.

The three types and their construction

  • Variable gap: a diaphragm or moving plate changes d. Used for pressure and sound (see 3.7) and for small displacements. Because C ∝ 1/d, the movement is kept to a small fraction of d so the curve stays near linear.
  • Variable area: one plate slides over the other, so A changes at fixed d. C ∝ A, so the output is linear in displacement, and shaped plates give a chosen law for angular sensing.
  • Variable dielectric: a material of different εr moves into the gap. Used for liquid level (a vertical rod dipped in the liquid), sheet thickness, humidity (a polymer film whose εr follows moisture) and touch (a finger changes the field).

Construction is two plates inside a metal case. One plate is often a thin diaphragm clamped over a back plate, with a gap of 0.05 mm to 0.5 mm filled with air, oil or a solid dielectric. A guard ring surrounds the measuring plate: a metal ring held at the same potential by a guard drive. Field lines that would leave the plate edge and reach the case end on the guard instead, so they are not counted in the measurement. The case is grounded and the cable is shielded, with the shield driven at guard potential.

Signal conditioning: from picofarads to volts

  1. Excite with an AC carrier, typically 1 kHz to 100 kHz at a few volts. AC avoids charge build-up and lets a bridge work.
  2. Convert C to voltage: an AC bridge with the sensor as one arm, or a capacitance-to-voltage converter (charge amplifier, switched-capacitor or sigma-delta type) giving V ∝ C.
  3. Amplify with a high input impedance amplifier; at balance the bridge output is close to zero.
  4. Demodulate with a phase-sensitive detector, so the output is DC and keeps the sign of the capacitance change.
  5. Filter out carrier ripple, then linearise, because a gap sensor follows 1/d and needs correction.
  6. Drive the guard and the cable shield from a buffer at signal potential, so stray capacitance to the shield draws no current from the measuring node.

Keep the input cable short and the node guarded. Every extra picofarad at the input adds to C and reads as a gap error. Keep the electronics at stable temperature, because εr and plate size drift with heat.

Worked example

Question. A parallel-plate capacitive displacement sensor has plate area A = 100 mm², air gap d = 0.50 mm, and air with εr = 1. Take ε₀ = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m. (a) Find C. (b) The gap falls to 0.40 mm; find the new C and the percentage change in C. (c) Why is the percentage change in C larger than the percentage change in d?

  1. Convert units: A = 100 mm² = 100 × 10⁻⁶ m² = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m². d = 0.50 mm = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ m.
  2. C = ε₀εrA/d = (8.854 × 10⁻¹²)(1)(1.0 × 10⁻⁴)/(5.0 × 10⁻⁴).
  3. Numerator = 8.854 × 10⁻¹⁶ F·m. Divide by 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ m: C = 1.771 × 10⁻¹² F ≈ 1.77 pF.
  4. New gap d' = 0.40 mm = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ m. C' = 8.854 × 10⁻¹⁶/4.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.214 × 10⁻¹² F ≈ 2.21 pF.
  5. Percentage change = (C' − C)/C × 100 = (2.214 − 1.771)/1.771 × 100 = 0.443/1.771 × 100 = 25.0 %.
  6. The gap fell by 0.10/0.50 = 20 %, but C follows 1/d and the ratio 0.50/0.40 = 1.25, so C rises by 25 %. Only for very small movements does ΔC/C ≈ −Δd/d hold; at 20 % the nonlinearity shows.

Exam traps

  • Treating C as linear in gap. C ∝ 1/d, so a 20 % fall in gap gives a 25 % rise in C, and the error grows as the movement becomes a larger share of d.
  • Ignoring stray capacitance. Cable and amplifier input add picofarads in parallel with a sensor that may itself be only 2 pF, so the offset and its drift can exceed the signal. Guard and shield the input node.
  • Using the wrong type for the job: gap for pressure and sound, area for displacement, dielectric for level, thickness and humidity. A gap sensor used for level gives the wrong law.
  • Unit slips: 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m² and 1 mm = 10⁻³ m, so a 0.5 mm gap is 5 × 10⁻⁴ m, not 5 × 10⁻³ m. A wrong power of ten here moves the answer by ten.

3.7 Capacitor microphone and capacitive pressure sensor

A capacitor microphone and a capacitive pressure sensor use the same trick. A thin diaphragm is one plate of a capacitor. Sound or fluid pressure pushes the diaphragm, the gap changes, the capacitance changes, and a circuit turns that into a voltage. The microphone serves speech, music and noise measurement. The pressure sensor serves low-pressure, differential and flow measurements. The change is tiny, so the amplifier and wiring matter as much as the plates.

You need: capacitive transducers: types and signal conditioning for C = ε₀A/d and charge-to-voltage conversion.

Capacitor microphone: how sound moves a plate

A capacitor microphone, also called a condenser microphone in some syllabi, has a thin metallised plastic or metal diaphragm. The diaphragm is one plate. A fixed back plate is the other. The air gap between them is d. Sound pressure pushes the diaphragm, the gap changes, and the capacitance follows the gap.

  1. Sound pressure reaches the diaphragm.
  2. The diaphragm moves towards the back plate.
  3. The gap d decreases.
  4. The capacitance C increases.
  5. The charge or voltage on the plates changes, and the preamplifier gives an output.
  6. On the opposite half-cycle the diaphragm moves away, the gap increases and C decreases; the output follows the sound waveform.

C = ε₀ A / d

C is capacitance in farads (F); ε₀ = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m is the permittivity of free space; A is the overlapping plate area in square metres (m²); d is the gap in metres (m).

ΔC / C ≈ x / d

ΔC is the change in capacitance in F; C is the initial capacitance in F; x is the diaphragm deflection towards the fixed plate in m; d is the initial gap in m. This small-change approximation holds when x is much smaller than d.

The back plate has holes or slots so air can move. Without holes, trapped air would resist the diaphragm and reduce sensitivity. The diaphragm must be thin and tight, like a drum skin, so small sound pressures produce motion.

A simple capacitor microphone needs a steady polarising voltage Vp across the plates. The charge is Q = C Vp. When C changes, charge must move, and a charge amplifier measures that charge. An electret microphone replaces the external supply with a permanent charge in a polymer layer. It still needs a high-impedance preamplifier.

Signal conditioning, response and practical limits

The capacitance change is tiny, so the preamplifier decides performance. Two modes are used, and they behave differently.

Charge amplifier mode: the input is a virtual ground, and the feedback capacitor Cf collects the charge. With the input near zero volts, the cable capacitance carries almost no signal voltage and does not change the output. It still affects noise and stability, so keep the cable short and quiet.

Voltage preamplifier mode: the input impedance must be very high. The cable capacitance now adds in parallel with the microphone capacitance and forms a divider, so a long cable reduces the signal at the amplifier. Put a buffer or the preamplifier itself at the microphone.

|Vout| = Vp ΔC / Cf

Vout is the output voltage magnitude in V; Vp is the polarising voltage in V; ΔC is the capacitance change in F; Cf is the feedback capacitor in F.

  • Flat frequency response over a wide audio band.
  • Small diaphragms give good high-frequency and transient response; sensitivity rises with diaphragm area, so large diaphragms are more sensitive but roll off sooner.
  • Low noise with a good preamplifier.
  • Very high input impedance in voltage mode.
  • Cable capacitance matters in voltage mode; a charge amplifier is largely immune.

Capacitive pressure sensor

A capacitive pressure sensor uses a diaphragm as one plate and a fixed plate or deposited electrode as the other. Pressure on one side deflects the diaphragm, the gap changes and C changes. Signal conditioning converts ΔC to a voltage or a digital reading.

C = ε₀ A / (d − x)

x is the pressure-driven deflection in m; d is the undeflected gap in m; A is the plate area in m²; ε₀ is the permittivity of free space in F/m.

For small deflection ΔC/C ≈ x/d, as in the microphone. For large deflection the denominator d − x changes a lot, the response becomes nonlinear, and calibration or linearisation is needed.

Three forms exist. An absolute sensor has a sealed vacuum reference on one side. A gauge sensor vents to atmosphere on one side. A differential sensor has two pressure ports and measures their difference. Capacitive sensors suit low-pressure and differential work because a small gap change gives a usable capacitance change.

Thin diaphragms and small gaps give sensitivity but make the sensor delicate. Overpressure can touch the plates or bend the diaphragm permanently. Mechanical stops and a careful housing protect it.

Worked example

Question. A capacitor microphone has diaphragm area A = 25 mm², gap d = 20 μm, and relative permittivity εr = 1. Find C. A sound pressure moves the diaphragm by 0.020 μm towards the back plate. Find the new gap, the new C, the change in C, and the percentage change in C.

  1. Convert the area: A = 25 mm² = 25 × 10⁻⁶ m².
  2. Convert the gap: d = 20 μm = 20 × 10⁻⁶ m.
  3. Motion and permittivity: x = 0.020 μm = 2.0 × 10⁻⁸ m; εr = 1, so ε = ε₀ = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m.
  4. Initial capacitance: C = ε₀A/d = (8.854 × 10⁻¹²)(25 × 10⁻⁶)/(20 × 10⁻⁶) = 11.0675 pF.
  5. New gap: d′ = d − x = 19.98 μm.
  6. New capacitance: C′ = ε₀A/d′ = (8.854 × 10⁻¹²)(25 × 10⁻⁶)/(19.98 × 10⁻⁶) = 11.0786 pF.
  7. Change in capacitance: ΔC = C′ − C = 0.01108 pF.
  8. Percentage change: (ΔC/C) × 100 = 0.1001%.

Answer. C = 11.07 pF; new gap = 19.98 μm; new C = 11.08 pF; change = 0.011 pF; percentage change = 0.1001%.

Exam traps

  • Connecting a capacitor microphone straight to a low-impedance amplifier. It needs a charge amplifier or a very high input impedance, or the signal collapses.
  • Ignoring cable capacitance in a voltage-preamplifier design. The cable adds capacitance in parallel, divides the signal, and can make two identical microphones read differently. A charge amplifier at the microphone avoids this.
  • Mixing electret and externally polarised microphones. An electret has a permanent charge; a plain capacitor microphone needs a polarising supply.
  • Confusing absolute, gauge and differential pressure ports. A sealed vacuum reference, a vent to atmosphere, and two pressure ports are not interchangeable.
  • Using the small-change formula for large diaphragm deflection. C = ε₀A/(d − x) becomes nonlinear when x is a large fraction of d.

Check yourself

Remember What changes inside a capacitor microphone when sound arrives? 2 marks

The diaphragm moves, so the gap d changes. The capacitance C changes according to C = ε₀A/d.

Understand Why does a plain capacitor microphone need a polarising voltage or an electret? 3 marks

It needs steady charge on the plates. With polarising voltage Vp, charge Q = C Vp changes when C changes. An electret supplies a permanent charge instead.

Apply A capacitive pressure sensor has C = 50 pF at d = 10 μm. Pressure reduces the gap by 0.10 μm. Use the small-change formula to find the percentage change in C and the new C. 4 marks

x/d = 0.10/10 = 0.010, so ΔC/C ≈ 0.010 = 1.0%. New C ≈ 50 pF × 1.010 = 50.5 pF.

Analyze In a voltage-preamplifier design, why can a long cable lower the sensitivity of a capacitor microphone? 5 marks

The cable capacitance adds in parallel with the microphone capacitance and forms a divider with it, so less signal voltage reaches the amplifier. A buffer or the preamplifier at the microphone avoids this. A charge amplifier is not affected the same way, because its input is a virtual ground and carries almost no signal voltage.

Understand Distinguish absolute, gauge and differential capacitive pressure sensors. 3 marks

Absolute: one side is a sealed vacuum reference. Gauge: one side vents to atmosphere. Differential: two pressure ports, and the output depends on the pressure difference.

Analyze Why does a capacitive pressure sensor become nonlinear for large diaphragm deflection? 5 marks

C = ε₀A/(d − x). For small x, ΔC/C ≈ x/d is nearly linear. For large x, the denominator changes strongly, so C rises faster than a straight line.

Apply A capacitor microphone has C = 10 pF and a polarising voltage of 100 V. A sound changes C by 0.01 pF. Find the charge change. 4 marks

ΔQ ≈ Vp ΔC = 100 × 0.01 × 10⁻¹² = 1 × 10⁻¹² C = 1 pC.

Takeaways

  1. A coil's inductance is set by the reluctance of its magnetic path, so any mechanical movement that changes an air gap becomes a measurable inductance change.
  2. The LVDT gives a differential AC output that is linear, frictionless, and whose phase reverses as the core crosses the null, so magnitude and direction both come out of one device.
  3. Rotary inductive devices such as the induction potentiometer, synchro and microsyn convert shaft angle into a voltage through stator–rotor mutual inductance.
  4. An eddy current transducer reads the impedance change of a coil near a conductor, so it measures gap, conductivity or defects with no contact and no wear.
  5. A proximity sensor is judged by its sensing range, hysteresis, repeatability and switching frequency, and its wiring is chosen as PNP or NPN to match the PLC input.
  6. A capacitive transducer can be built to respond to gap, area or dielectric, and a differential plate arrangement linearises the output and cancels common errors.
  7. Capacitive sensors need charge amplifiers or AC bridges because the capacitance change is tiny, often picofarads.
  8. Microphones and pressure sensors share the same physics: a diaphragm moves one plate of a capacitor, and the gap change becomes a voltage.

Remember and understand

Understand Define magnetic reluctance and derive the expression for the inductance of a coil in terms of turns and reluctance. Explain, with a sketch, how a variable reluctance transducer uses an air gap to measure displacement. 10 marks

𝓡 = l/(μ₀μᵣA), units A·t/Wb; Flux Φ = NI/𝓡, linkage NΦ, so L = N²/𝓡; Air gap dominates reluctance because μᵣ of air is 1 against thousands for iron; Gap change gives a large L change; sketch of E-core or plunger type; Output is non-linear with gap; single coil needs a bridge to convert L change to voltage.

Understand Draw the construction of an LVDT and explain its working. Why are the two secondaries connected in series opposition, and what happens to the output magnitude and phase as the core moves from below null to above null? 13 marks

Former, primary at centre, two identical secondaries S₁ and S₂, movable soft-iron core, shield; Primary excited with AC, usually 1–10 kHz; Series opposition so output = e₁ − e₂, zero at null; Core off-centre: nearer secondary has larger EMF, output rises with displacement; Output magnitude proportional to displacement, phase flips by 180° across null; Graph of output vs displacement: linear between about ±0.25 to ±0.4 of full range.

Remember Explain the construction and principle of operation of an induction potentiometer and a synchro transmitter. State two applications of each. 10 marks

Induction potentiometer: rotor winding and distributed stator winding, mutual inductance varies with rotor angle; Output amplitude follows a sine or cosine of shaft angle; single-phase AC excitation; Synchro: three-phase stator with 120° spaced windings, single-phase rotor; Rotor angle sets stator flux pattern, so the three stator voltages identify the shaft angle; Applications: angular position, remote indication, control transformer in servo loops.

Understand Explain the principle of an eddy current transducer. How do coil impedance, gap and target conductivity affect the reading, and what are its practical applications? 10 marks

Coil driven by AC, alternating field induces eddy currents in nearby conductor; Eddy currents create an opposing field, changing coil impedance (L and R); Impedance change depends on gap, conductivity, permeability and frequency; Higher frequency gives a shallower penetration (skin depth); Applications: displacement, vibration, thickness of foil, crack detection, conductivity sorting; Non-contact, no wear, but only works on conductors.

Understand Explain the principle of a capacitive transducer. Describe the three ways its capacitance can change and the signal-conditioning circuits used to convert the change into a voltage. 12 marks

C = ε₀εᵣA/d with terms defined; Gap-changing type: C varies as 1/d, non-linear; Area-changing type: C varies linearly with overlap area; Dielectric-changing type: used for level and moisture; Differential three-plate capacitor linearises output and cancels common effects; Signal conditioning: AC bridge, charge amplifier, op-amp oscillator/demodulator circuits; High impedance, needs guarding and shielding against stray capacitance.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare inductive, LVDT, eddy current and capacitive transducers for displacement measurement under the headings: range, linearity, contact, sensitivity to environment, cost and typical application. 14 marks

LVDT: mm to hundreds of mm, very linear, no contact, robust, costlier; Variable reluctance: small range, non-linear, cheap, needs bridge; Eddy current: small gap, non-contact, needs conducting target, sensitive to conductivity; Capacitive: small range, very high resolution, affected by humidity and stray capacitance; Selection depends on target material, environment and resolution needed; Give one Indian industrial example such as measuring roll gap or dam gate position.

Evaluate A plant reports an LVDT reading that does not return to zero when the core returns to the mechanical null. Evaluate the possible causes and the corrections. 13 marks

Residual voltage from winding mismatch, stray capacitance, harmonics; Core not at electrical null; mechanical zero differs from electrical zero; Excitation frequency drift and phase shift in demodulator; Temperature change in windings and former; Corrections: null adjustment, phase-sensitive demodulation, stable oscillator, shielding, calibration at zero and full scale.

Evaluate Compare an LVDT-based diaphragm pressure gauge with a capacitive pressure sensor for measuring 0 to 1 bar in a process line. Which would you choose and why? 14 marks

LVDT type: robust, large output, but has moving core, more parts, slower; Capacitive: small, high resolution, fast, but low capacitance needs charge amplifier and guarding; Capacitive needs sealed reference chamber and good temperature compensation; Both give non-contact sensing of the diaphragm; Choice depends on accuracy, size, cost and environment; justify a clear decision.

Analyze A proximity sensor in a press safety guard trips intermittently. Analyse the likely causes using the ideas of sensing range, hysteresis, repeatability, flush mounting and output type. 12 marks

Target distance near the edge of nominal range, so hysteresis and repeatability matter; Non-flush mounting causes side sensing of nearby metal; Wrong output type (PNP vs NPN) or NO vs NC wiring against the load; Supply ripple, cable length and electrical noise from the press; Speed of approach against switching frequency; Fixes: correct mounting, margin of 60–80% of range, screening, correct polarity.

Evaluate Explain why the capacitor microphone needs a high-impedance preamplifier and a polarising voltage. Compare its signal-conditioning needs with those of a capacitive pressure sensor. 12 marks

Fixed charge q = CV, so V = q/C; a small gap change gives a voltage change; Very small capacitance (tens of pF) means very high source impedance; Preamplifier must have high input impedance and low input capacitance, placed close to the capsule; Polarising voltage (or electret charge) is needed to set q; Pressure sensor may use a charge amplifier or AC bridge with synchronous demodulation; Both need guarding and shielding against stray capacitance.

Glossary

Air gap
The non-magnetic part of a magnetic path, whose reluctance is large and changes with mechanical movement.
Capacitance
The charge stored per unit volt, C = ε₀εᵣA/d for a parallel-plate capacitor.
Charge amplifier
A circuit that converts charge from a capacitive sensor into a voltage independent of cable capacitance.
Control transformer
A synchro used as a receiver that outputs a voltage proportional to the angular error between two shafts.
Demodulator
A circuit that converts the AC output of an inductive transducer into a DC voltage whose sign shows direction.
Differential capacitor
A three-plate capacitor whose two halves change in opposite directions, giving a linear output.
Eddy current
Current induced in a conductor by a changing magnetic field, flowing in closed loops that oppose the change.
Electret
A permanently charged dielectric used in capacitor microphones to avoid an external polarising supply.
Flush (shielded) sensor
A proximity sensor with a metal shield that can be mounted level with a metal surface without false operation.
Guard ring
A conductor held at the same potential as a sensor plate to remove stray field and stray capacitance effects.
Hysteresis
The difference between the switch-on and switch-off distances of a proximity sensor.
Induction potentiometer
A rotary transformer whose output amplitude varies with the shaft angle of its rotor.
LVDT
A transformer with one primary and two opposed secondaries whose differential output is proportional to core displacement.
Microsyn
A small, sensitive rotary variable-reluctance pick-off used for fine angle and rate measurement.
Mutual inductance
The flux linkage produced in one coil by unit current in another coil.
Null position
The core position at the centre of an LVDT where the two secondary EMFs cancel and the output is zero.
PNP output
A sensor output that switches the positive supply to the load, used with sinking PLC inputs.
Proximity sensor
A non-contact switch that changes state when a target comes within its sensing range.
Reluctance
The opposition a magnetic circuit offers to flux, equal to length divided by permeability times area.
Repeatability
How closely a sensor switches at the same distance on repeated approaches.
Residual voltage
The small output left at null because the two secondaries are not perfectly identical.
Sensing range
The distance at which a proximity sensor reliably switches for a stated standard target.
Skin depth
The depth to which an alternating field penetrates a conductor, decreasing as frequency and conductivity rise.
Synchro
A rotary electromagnetic device that transmits or compares shaft angles between two places over wires.
Variable reluctance transducer
A transducer whose inductance changes because a mechanical input changes the reluctance of its magnetic path.
