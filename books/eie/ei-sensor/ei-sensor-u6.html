6.1 Signal conditioning: amplification, filtering, linearization, isolation

Signal conditioning turns a weak, noisy, non-linear sensor output into a clean standard voltage or current that the next stage can digitise safely. A strain gauge may give only 2 mV at full load; an ADC needs volts. Conditioning amplifies, filters, linearises and isolates. It also attenuates large signals and matches impedance so a high-resistance sensor is not loaded. You meet it in PLC input cards, data loggers, medical front ends and every industrial transmitter that sends 4-20 mA to a control room.

You need sensitivity, linearity and accuracy from the static characteristics of sensors, and the output of a strain-gauge bridge.

Amplification, attenuation and loading

A sensor output is small and often sits on a larger offset. An amplifier multiplies the difference by a fixed gain. For the difference amplifier in the worked example, gain is set by two resistors: G = Rf / Rin. Here Rf is feedback resistance in ohms and Rin is input resistance in ohms. With Rf = 100 kΩ and Rin = 200 Ω, G = 500. The same gain in decibels is GdB = 20 log₁₀(G) = 20 log₁₀(500) = 54.0 dB. dB is a ratio, not a voltage.

G = Rf / Rin,   GdB = 20 log₁₀(G),   Vout = Vin × R₂/(R₁+R₂)

G is voltage gain, no unit; Rf is feedback resistance in Ω; Rin is input resistance in Ω; GdB is gain in decibels; Vout and Vin are voltages in V; R₁ and R₂ are divider resistors in Ω, with the output taken across R₂ (the divider attenuates; it is not part of the amplifier gain).

An instrumentation amplifier is three op-amps in one package. It has high input impedance, so it does not load a bridge, and it rejects the voltage common to both inputs. That common voltage may be 2.5 V of offset while the signal is 2 mV. A plain op-amp difference circuit with low input resistance would draw current from the bridge and reduce the signal. In a medical front end the same chain reads an ECG: about 1 mV of heart signal on up to ±300 mV of electrode offset, so the first stage is a differential amplifier of modest gain, the hum is filtered, and an isolation barrier keeps the patient's leakage current down to microamperes.

Large signals need attenuation, not gain. A resistive divider gives Vout = Vin × R₂/(R₁+R₂). Impedance matching means the next stage input resistance is much larger than the source resistance, often at least 10 times. A pH electrode may have 100 MΩ source resistance, so a 1 MΩ ADC input would load it badly.

Filtering the noise

Mains hum at 50 Hz, switching supplies at 20 kHz and radio pickup add to the signal. A first-order RC low-pass filter passes low frequencies and attenuates high ones. It uses a resistor R in ohms and capacitor C in farads. The cutoff frequency is fc = 1/(2πRC). At fc the output falls to 1/√2 = 0.707 of the input, a drop of 3 dB. Above fc the gain falls by about 20 dB per decade.

Magnitude: |Vout/Vin| = 1/√(1+(f/fc)²). Here f is the signal frequency in hertz. With R = 10 kΩ and C = 1 µF, RC = 10⁴ × 10⁻⁶ = 0.01 s, so fc = 1/(2π × 0.01) = 15.9 Hz. That cuts 50 Hz hum to about a third of its size, but it also slows a fast strain pulse. Pick fc above the highest real signal frequency and below the noise.

A filter is not a magic cleaner. It adds delay and phase shift. For a step input it gives a slow exponential rise with time constant τ = RC = 0.01 s. For 50 Hz hum at a 15.9 Hz cutoff, the attenuation is 1/√(1+(50/15.9)²) = 0.303, about -10.4 dB, so a 1 V hum becomes 0.303 V. Filters with sharper cutoffs are in filters and amplifiers.

Linearisation, isolation and protection

Many sensors are not straight lines. A thermocouple output is a small polynomial in temperature; a thermistor resistance falls exponentially. Linearisation makes the conditioned output proportional to the measured quantity. A breakpoint circuit compares the input with several reference voltages. Between two breakpoints it adds a slope correction. The straight line between points (x₁,y₁) and (x₂,y₂) is y = y₁ + (y₂-y₁)(x-x₁)/(x₂-x₁). Here x is the raw sensor signal and y is the corrected output.

Software linearisation stores a lookup table of raw ADC counts against known temperatures. Between table entries it interpolates with the same formula. It is cheap and accurate if the table is calibrated. A thermistor is often linearised with one fixed parallel resistor over a narrow range, or with a table over a wide range. A thermocouple needs cold-junction compensation first.

Electrical isolation breaks the direct current path between the sensor side and the computer side. A transformer isolates AC signals; an opto-coupler uses an LED and phototransistor for digital or slow analogue signals. Isolation stops ground loops, where two earth points at slightly different voltages drive a current through the signal cable. It also protects the ADC from surges on a long cable. An isolation amplifier carries two ratings: a test voltage, say 2.5 kV for one minute, and a much lower working voltage it may carry continuously between its two sides.

Two practical cases close the chain. A 0-10 V transmitter feeding a 3.3 V ADC needs a divider. A 4-20 mA current loop uses a 250 Ω resistor to make 1-5 V; the current is the same along the loop, so cable resistance does not spoil it. Transmission is covered in signal transmission.

Worked example

Question. A strain-gauge bridge gives 2 mV at full load. A difference amplifier uses Rf = 100 kΩ and Rin = 200 Ω. Find the voltage gain, the amplifier output, and the cutoff frequency of a first-order RC low-pass filter with R = 10 kΩ and C = 1 µF that follows it. Also give the gain in dB.

  1. Voltage gain: G = Rf/Rin = 100 kΩ / 200 Ω = 100000/200 = 500.
  2. Amplifier output: Vout = Vin × G = 2 mV × 500 = 1000 mV = 1.0 V.
  3. Gain in dB: GdB = 20 log₁₀(500) = 20 × 2.699 = 53.98 dB ≈ 54.0 dB.
  4. Filter time constant: τ = RC = 10 kΩ × 1 µF = 10⁴ Ω × 10⁻⁶ F = 0.01 s.
  5. Cutoff: fc = 1/(2πτ) = 1/(2π × 0.01 s) = 15.915 Hz ≈ 15.9 Hz.

Worked example 2

Question. A thermistor temperature probe for patients feeds a 12-bit ADC. The calibration table holds 2010 counts at 36.0 °C and 1890 counts at 38.0 °C. (a) The ADC reads 1950 counts; find the temperature by linear interpolation. (b) The same logger also takes a 0-10 V transmitter into a 0-3.3 V ADC input through a divider with R₁ = 22 kΩ and R₂ = 10 kΩ. Find the input at full scale and check that it fits.

  1. (a) Interpolate between the two table points: T = T₁ + (x − x₁)(T₂ − T₁)/(x₂ − x₁).
  2. Substitute: T = 36.0 + (1950 − 2010)(38.0 − 36.0)/(1890 − 2010) = 36.0 + (−60)(2.0)/(−120) = 36.0 + 1.0 = 37.0 °C.
  3. The counts fall as temperature rises because an NTC thermistor's resistance falls; the formula handles the sign by itself.
  4. (b) Divider: Vout = 10 V × 10 kΩ / (22 kΩ + 10 kΩ) = 100/32 = 3.125 V.
  5. Check: 3.125 V is below 3.3 V, so full scale fits with a 5 % margin; the divider's 32 kΩ is a light load on a transmitter that can drive 2 kΩ or more.

Answer. The patient reads 37.0 °C, and the divider gives 3.125 V at full scale, inside the 3.3 V input.

Exam traps

  • Gain multiplies offset drift and noise with the signal. A gain of 500 on 2 mV also makes 0.1 mV of input offset into 50 mV at the output; accuracy does not improve by gain alone.
  • dB is a ratio: 20 log₁₀(Vout/Vin). Do not write 500 dB for a voltage ratio of 500; it is about 54 dB.
  • In fc = 1/(2πRC), R must be in ohms and C in farads. Using 10 kΩ as 10 and 1 µF as 1 gives a wrong 0.0159 Hz.
  • Isolation stops ground loops and protects against surges; it does not fix a non-linear sensor or remove all noise. A dirty signal is still dirty after the isolator.

6.2 Data acquisition systems: elements and fundamentals

A data acquisition system (DAS) reads many sensors and turns their voltages into numbers a computer can store, compare and plot. One analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) is shared among many channels, so the designer must fix three things: the smallest voltage step the system can see, how many samples per second each channel gets, and the order in which channels are read. DAS hardware sits inside power-plant monitoring racks, patient monitors, machine-tool controllers, water-treatment panels and student lab benches.

You need signal conditioning, and the dynamic characteristics of sensors for response and settling time.

The chain of elements

A DAS is a chain. Each block hands a signal to the next, and the weakest block sets the accuracy of the whole reading. The order is fixed, and the figure shows it left to right.

  1. Transducer converts the physical variable (temperature, pressure, strain) into an electrical quantity. Its output may be a few millivolts, a resistance change or a current.
  2. Signal conditioner amplifies, filters, linearises and isolates that output, delivering a standard range such as 0-5 V or 4-20 mA. See signal conditioning.
  3. Multiplexer is a switch bank that connects one input channel at a time to the shared converter. Eight or sixteen channels to one ADC is common.
  4. Sample-and-hold freezes the selected voltage on a capacitor while the ADC works, so the input does not move during conversion.
  5. ADC converts the held voltage into a binary number. Its bit count sets the resolution and its conversion time sets the speed.
  6. Processor or controller selects channels, starts conversions, scales raw counts into engineering units (degrees Celsius, kPa, newton) and applies limits.
  7. Store and display or bus keeps the numbers locally, shows them on a screen, or sends them over RS-485, Ethernet, USB or a fieldbus to a supervisory system.

The multiplexer is what makes a DAS cheap: one accurate converter serves many sensors. The price is time. Every extra channel scanned is time the converter cannot spend on another channel.

Resolution, sampling rate and channel scan

Resolution is the smallest voltage change the converter can distinguish. It depends only on the full-scale span and the number of bits.

ΔV = VFS / 2ⁿ

ΔV is the resolution in volts (one least significant bit, 1 LSB); VFS is the full-scale span in volts, the difference between the highest and lowest input, so a ±5 V range has VFS = 10 V; n is the ADC bit count and 2ⁿ the number of output codes.

Speed is shared. The converter produces numbers at a fixed total rate, and the multiplexer divides that rate among the scanned channels.

fch = ftotal / N ,    Tconv ≤ 1 / ftotal

fch is the samples per second each channel gets, in samples/s (S/s); ftotal is the total converter rate in S/s; N is the number of channels actually scanned; Tconv is the time allowed for one conversion, in seconds, including multiplexer settling and sample-and-hold acquisition.

The sampling rule follows from fch. To reconstruct a waveform, the highest frequency present in that channel must stay below half its own sample rate: fmax < fch / 2 (the Nyquist limit). A 1 kS/s channel therefore handles signals up to 500 Hz. When the input carries faster components they fold down as false slow waves, which is why the conditioner's low-pass filter is placed before the multiplexer. Aliasing on a sampled display is shown in display instruments.

Sample-and-hold, converter types and architectures

A sample-and-hold circuit closes a switch, charges a capacitor to the input voltage, then opens. The ADC converts the frozen value. Two times matter. The acquisition time is how long the capacitor takes to settle to the new input. The aperture time is the short, uncertain window when the switch opens; a changing input gives an error of (rate of change) × (aperture time). A 1 V/µs ramp with a 50 ns aperture smears the sample by 50 mV, which is 20 LSB for a 12-bit converter on a 10 V span. A sample-and-hold is compulsory whenever a slow converter must read a signal that changes quickly during the conversion time.

  • Successive approximation (SAR): compares the input against a trial word bit by bit; medium speed, medium cost, the usual general-purpose choice.
  • Flash: 2ⁿ − 1 comparators compare the input against every decision level at once, converting in a single step; fastest, expensive, used for video and radar.
  • Sigma-delta: oversamples and filters; slow per sample but very high resolution and excellent noise rejection, used for thermocouples and load cells.

Three system architectures carry these blocks into the field. A PC plug-in card puts the multiplexer, ADC and bus interface on one board inside a desktop, cheap for a lab. A stand-alone unit is a boxed instrument with its own display and memory, used where no computer sits. A distributed or SCADA system spreads small remote units near the plant and links them to a central master over a fieldbus or Ethernet, saving long sensor cables. See signal transmission for how those links stay clean.

Worked example

Question. A data acquisition system has 8 input channels and an input range of ±5 V. Its 12-bit ADC is shared by a multiplexer that scans 4 channels, each at 1 kS/s. Find the resolution in volts, the time available for one conversion, and the highest signal frequency each channel can carry.

  1. Full-scale span: VFS = (+5 V) − (−5 V) = 10 V. The bipolar range spans 10 V, not 5 V.
  2. Code count: 2ⁿ = 2¹² = 4096 codes.
  3. Resolution: ΔV = VFS / 2ⁿ = 10 V / 4096 = 2.4414 × 10⁻³ V = 2.44 mV per LSB.
  4. Total converter rate: ftotal = 4 channels × 1000 samples/s = 4000 S/s.
  5. Conversion time budget: Tconv = 1 / ftotal = 1 / 4000 S/s = 250 × 10⁻⁶ s = 250 µs. Multiplexer settling and sample-and-hold acquisition must fit inside this 250 µs.
  6. Highest signal frequency per channel: fch = 1000 S/s, so fmax < fch / 2 = 500 Hz.

Answer. Resolution is 2.44 mV, each conversion must finish within 250 µs, and each channel carries signals up to 500 Hz.

Exam traps

  • Applying the half-sampling-rate rule to the total converter rate instead of the per-channel rate. One shared ADC divides its speed among all scanned channels, so fmax < fch / 2, not ftotal / 2.
  • Using 5 V as the span for a ±5 V range. The span is the difference between the limits, 10 V. Using 5 V by mistake gives ΔV half the true value, so the reported resolution is twice as fine as the hardware really is.
  • Confusing resolution with accuracy. A 12-bit converter has 2.44 mV steps but may be accurate only to 0.1 % of span because of offset, gain and reference errors.

6.3 Signal transmission

A measurement is made at the sensor and needed at the readout; the cable between them is where accuracy is lost. Signal transmission moves the value without noise, lost volts or ground errors. A flow transmitter on a process pipe reports to a control room 200 m away over two wires. This chapter covers the two-wire 4–20 mA loop, its resistance limit, resistance-sensor wiring, and digital links.

You need signal conditioning for what conditioning does before transmission.

Voltage or current

A voltage link is simplest. Cable resistance and receiver input form a divider, so the reading depends on cable length and copper temperature. The ends also sit at different earth potentials; a volt of ground difference adds to a voltage signal and looks like measurement.

A current link avoids both. Every element in a series loop carries the same current. The transmitter sets it; wire resistance changes only the voltage needed to push it, never the value. Ground difference does not change a current.

The 4–20 mA loop

Two wires carry supply and signal. The transmitter is a current sink: it draws current proportional to the measurement, 4 mA at range bottom to 20 mA at top.

The 4 mA start is a live zero. Zero of measurement is 4 mA, not 0 mA. A loop current of 0 mA then means the loop is broken or the transmitter is dead, so the loop reports its own failure.

The receiver puts a precision sense resistor in the loop and measures its voltage.

Vsense = I × Rsense

Vsense is the sense-resistor voltage in volts; I is loop current in amperes; Rsense is the sense resistance in ohms. A 250 Ω resistor gives 1 V at 4 mA and 5 V at 20 mA.

Rloop,max = (Vsupply − Vmin) / 0.020 A

Rloop,max is total cable plus resistor resistance in ohms; Vsupply is supply voltage in volts; Vmin is minimum voltage the transmitter needs, about 10 V to 12 V. Beyond this, the transmitter cannot reach 20 mA.

Wiring, lead resistance and digital links

Use screened twisted pair. Twisting makes both wires pick up the same interference, which cancels; the screen, earthed at one end, shunts electrostatic noise to ground.

Lead resistance matters for resistance sensors, as the second worked example shows. A 2-wire RTD adds its leads to the element and reads high. A 3-wire connection cancels the lead drop if the leads match. A 4-wire (Kelvin) connection uses current-carrying and voltage-sensing leads separately, so lead resistance disappears.

Digital links carry the value as a number. RS-485 is a cheap differential bus for many instruments on one pair. HART puts a small digital signal on top of 4–20 mA. Fieldbus replaces the analogue value. Fibre suits very long runs and high-voltage isolation; wireless suits moving or remote plant. Hospitals use both: telemetry carries the ECG of a walking patient to the nursing station, and fibre-optic links keep metal cable out of an MRI scanner's field.

Worked example

Question. A 2-wire transmitter runs from 24 V and needs 10 V minimum. The receiver uses a 250 Ω sense resistor. Find maximum loop resistance and receiver voltage range.

  1. At full scale I = 20 mA = 0.020 A.
  2. Available volts = 24 V − 10 V = 14 V.
  3. Rloop,max = 14 V / 0.020 A = 700 Ω.
  4. At 4 mA: Vsense = 0.004 A × 250 Ω = 1 V.
  5. At 20 mA: Vsense = 0.020 A × 250 Ω = 5 V.

Answer. Total loop resistance must not exceed 700 Ω; the receiver sees 1 V to 5 V.

Worked example 2

Question. A Pt100 RTD (0.385 Ω per °C near 0 °C) is 20 m from its transmitter; each lead has 0.75 Ω. (a) Find the error of a 2-wire connection. (b) A 3-wire connection leaves a lead mismatch of 0.05 Ω; find its error. (c) The transmitter is ranged 0 to 150 °C on 4–20 mA and sends 12.4 mA. Find the temperature.

  1. (a) In 2-wire both leads add to the element: 2 × 0.75 = 1.5 Ω. Error = 1.5 / 0.385 = 3.9 °C, read high.
  2. (b) In 3-wire the two lead resistances sit in opposite bridge arms and cancel, leaving only their difference: 0.05 / 0.385 = 0.13 °C.
  3. (c) Span: 20 − 4 = 16 mA for 150 °C. Fraction: (12.4 − 4) / 16 = 8.4 / 16 = 0.525. T = 0.525 × 150 = 78.75 °C.
  4. Check: 4 mA would give 0 °C and 20 mA 150 °C; 12.4 mA is just above mid-current (12 mA, 75 °C), so 78.75 °C is consistent. A 4-wire connection would remove even the 0.13 °C.

Answer. The 2-wire connection reads 3.9 °C high, the 3-wire 0.13 °C; 12.4 mA means 78.75 °C.

Exam traps

  • 0 mA is a fault, not a zero reading. Live zero means bottom of range is 4 mA.
  • The current is the same everywhere in the loop; wire resistance changes voltage, not current.
  • A 2-wire RTD reads high by its lead resistance; only a 4-wire connection removes it fully.
  • Rloop,max counts cable both ways plus the sense resistor, not cable one way.
  • 4 mA is a real offset (the live zero). It is removed at the receiver when voltage is scaled to engineering units, not by changing anything in the loop.

6.4 Pneumatic and hydraulic actuators

Pneumatic and hydraulic actuators use fluid pressure to produce force and motion. Air at 6–8 bar or oil at 100–200 bar pushes a piston. The hardware is simple, robust and spark-free, so it drives clamps, presses, valves and robot grippers. Here you learn the actuation chain, the force formula F = pA, and how to choose between air and oil.

You need school physics of force and pressure, F = pA.

Pneumatic actuators: air at 6–8 bar

A compressor fills a receiver at about 6–8 bar. The air passes through an FRL unit: a filter removes water and dirt, a regulator sets the working pressure, and a lubricator adds oil mist for moving parts. A direction control valve then sends air to the cylinder. The common 5/2 solenoid valve has five ports and two positions; an electrical signal shifts the spool, so the valve is the electro-pneumatic interface and the cylinder is the actuator. A single-acting cylinder uses air on one side and a spring to return. A double-acting cylinder uses air on both sides and gives force in both directions.

Force is F = pA, where p is gauge pressure in Pa and A is piston area in m². Speed is set by a flow control valve, not by pressure. Air is compressible, so position stiffness is low and cushions are needed at the stroke ends. Typical uses are pick-and-place, packaging, clamps and bus doors.

Hydraulic actuators: oil at 100–200 bar

A pump sends oil at 100–200 bar. A relief valve opens to limit maximum pressure and protect the circuit. A direction valve sets motion; a servo valve moves its spool continuously to control flow and direction from an electrical command. The cylinder gives force F = pA. Oil is almost incompressible, so the actuator is stiff and holds position under load. A 50 mm bore at 150 bar gives F = 150 × 10⁵ Pa × 1.963 × 10⁻³ m² ≈ 29.4 kN, 150/6 = 25 times the theoretical pneumatic force at the same bore. Hydraulic motors give rotary motion. These actuators suit excavators, presses, injection moulding clamps and aircraft surface actuation. The cost is leakage, heat, filtration and a power pack. Fluid cleanliness matters: a grain of dirt can jam a servo valve. Cylinders may be single-acting or double-acting, as in pneumatics.

Valves and selection

Direction control valves are named by ports and positions: 5/2, 4/3 and so on. Flow control valves set speed. Pressure control valves include relief and reducing valves. Valve characteristics include flow against spool travel, dead band and hysteresis. Electro-pneumatic means a solenoid pilot shifts the air valve; the solenoid coil itself is covered in solenoids, relays and motors. For closed-loop position, a servo valve with a position sensor gives a servo actuator.

Choose pneumatics for clean, fast, low-force tasks: pick-and-place, packaging, bus doors. Choose hydraulics for high force, high stiffness and smooth slow motion. Compare force per unit size, stroke, speed, stiffness, cleanliness, noise and cost per unit force.

Medicine uses both. Ventilators and anaesthesia machines meter gas with proportional solenoid valves, blood-pressure monitors inflate the cuff with a small pump and release it through a valve, and compression devices squeeze the legs with air to prevent clots. Hydraulic or electro-hydraulic rams raise operating tables, patient lifts and dental chairs, where a smooth, stiff, slow motion under a heavy load is what matters.

Question. A double-acting pneumatic cylinder has bore 50 mm and runs on air at 6 bar gauge with a mechanical efficiency of 0.9. Find the usable extension force.

  1. Gauge pressure p = 6 bar = 6 × 10⁵ Pa.
  2. Bore d = 50 mm = 0.050 m, so piston area A = πd²/4 = π × (0.050)² / 4 = 1.963 × 10⁻³ m².
  3. Theoretical force Fth = pA = 6 × 10⁵ Pa × 1.963 × 10⁻³ m² = 1178 N.
  4. Usable force F = ηFth = 0.9 × 1178 N = 1060 N.
  5. The answer is the force on the load during extension.

Answer. The usable extension force is 1060 N.

Worked example 2

Question. The cylinder of the first example (50 mm bore, 6 bar, efficiency 0.9) has a 20 mm rod. (a) Find the retraction force. (b) Find the theoretical force of a 50 mm hydraulic cylinder at 150 bar and compare it with the pneumatic one.

  1. (a) On the rod side the air acts on the annulus: A = π(D² − d²)/4 = π(0.050² − 0.020²)/4 = π × 0.0021 / 4 = 1.649 × 10⁻³ m².

  3. (b) Hydraulic: F = p A = 150 × 10⁵ Pa × 1.963 × 10⁻³ m² = 29 450 N = 29.4 kN.

6.5 Electrical actuators: solenoids, relays and electric motors

An electrical actuator turns current into motion. A solenoid pushes a plunger a short straight distance. A relay uses that push to open or close contacts, so a small coil signal can switch a large load. A motor turns current into continuous rotation. These three cover most on-off and drive jobs in machines: a washing-machine inlet valve, a relay board switching a pump, a 12 V motor driving a robot wheel. This chapter gives the ratings, the force and speed relations, and the numbers an exam question asks for.

You need magnetic circuits and inductance for how a coil builds flux and how a moving plunger changes inductance, and pneumatic and hydraulic actuators to compare electrical actuators with fluid power.

Solenoid: a short linear push

A solenoid is a coil wound on a tube with a soft-iron plunger inside. Current in the coil makes a magnetic field, and the field pulls the plunger into the coil against a spring. The stroke is short, a few millimetres up to about 10 cm. Force is largest when the plunger is nearly seated and the air gap is small; it falls as the gap opens and the plunger is drawn out. This is the force-stroke curve. Pull-in must be strong enough at the open gap, and once seated a smaller hold current gives the same holding force. Design from the working point on the curve, not from the peak value.

The coil is a resistor with inductance. Pull-in current is the current needed to move the plunger and its load. Hold current is smaller: it only keeps the plunger seated. Many drivers apply full voltage for a moment, then drop to a lower hold voltage, so the coil runs cooler. Duty cycle is on time divided by total cycle time, in per cent. A solenoid rated 10% duty cycle must rest between pulses, or the coil overheats and the insulation fails. Uses: door locks, fuel injectors, washing-machine inlet valves, and in hospitals the pinch valves of dialysis machines and the gas valves of ventilators.

P = I²R     duty cycle = (ton ÷ T) × 100%

P is coil heating in watts; I is coil current in amperes; R is coil resistance in ohms; ton is the on time in seconds; T is the total cycle time in seconds.

Relay: coil on one side, contacts on the other

A relay is a solenoid plus a set of contacts. The coil pulls an armature; the armature carries a moving contact that touches a fixed contact. A spring returns it when the coil is off.

A relay has two independent ratings. The coil rating is the voltage and power needed to work it: a 24 V coil of 480 Ω draws 0.05 A and dissipates 1.2 W. The contact rating is the current and voltage the contacts may switch, say 10 A at 250 V AC. A 24 V coil says nothing about whether those contacts can break a 230 V mains pump. Check both.

Contacts come as normally open (NO, closed only when the coil is energised) and normally closed (NC, closed when the coil is off). The common terminal is the moving contact. A solid-state relay (SSR) replaces the coil-armature-contact assembly with an LED that shines on a light-sensitive triac. The LED is the input, the triac is the output switch, and the light couples them with no moving parts. A triac is two thyristors back to back, so it conducts on both half-cycles of AC. No contacts wear, switching is fast and silent, but the triac drops about 1 V, needs a heat sink, and leaks a small current when off.

DC motor: back emf sets the speed

A DC motor has a magnetic field (from a winding or from permanent magnets) and an armature carrying current. Armature current flowing in the magnetic field (flux) produces torque. As the armature turns it cuts flux and generates a back emf that opposes the supply. At steady state:

Eb = V − IaRa T = kφIa

Eb is back emf in volts; V is supply voltage in volts; Ia is armature current in amperes; Ra is armature resistance in ohms; T is torque in newton metres; φ is flux per pole in webers; k is a motor constant.

Back emf is also proportional to speed, Eb = kφω with ω in rad/s, so speed ≈ (V − IaRa) ÷ (kφ). Speed rises almost in proportion to applied voltage. Torque rises in proportion to armature current. At standstill Eb = 0, so Ia = V ÷ Ra, several times the full-load current; large DC motors need a starter. Types: shunt (field in parallel, nearly constant speed), series (field in series, high starting torque, never run unloaded), permanent-magnet (small, cheap, used in car wipers, robot wheels and the infusion and syringe pumps of a hospital ward).

AC motors: induction and synchronous

A three-phase induction motor has three stator windings fed 120° apart. Their currents make a field rotating at synchronous speed Ns = 120f ÷ P, where Ns is in revolutions per minute, f is supply frequency in hertz and P is the number of poles. The rotating field induces currents in the rotor cage, and the rotor is dragged along a little slower than the field.

s = (Ns − Nr) ÷ Ns

s is slip, a pure number; Ns is synchronous speed in rpm; Nr is rotor speed in rpm.

Slip is 1 at standstill and a few per cent at full load, typically 2% to 5%. Torque rises with slip up to a breakdown value, then falls. At start the rotor is stationary, slip is 1, and the rotor behaves like a short-circuited transformer: starting current is 5 to 8 times full-load current while starting torque is only about 1.5 to 2 times full load. Star-delta starters, soft starters and variable-frequency drives limit this current. A synchronous motor turns exactly at Ns: its rotor, permanent-magnet or DC-fed, locks to the rotating field. It has no slip, so it suits clocks and precise drives, but it has no starting torque of its own.

Worked example

Question. A permanent-magnet DC motor runs from a 12 V supply and draws 2 A at full load. Its armature resistance is 1 Ω. The shaft delivers 18 W. Find (a) the electrical input power, (b) the back emf, (c) the efficiency.

  1. Input power: Pin = V × Ia = 12 V × 2 A = 24 W.
  2. Armature resistance drop: IaRa = 2 A × 1 Ω = 2 V.
  3. Back emf: Eb = V − IaRa = 12 V − 2 V = 10 V.
  4. Copper loss in the armature: Ia²Ra = 2² × 1 = 4 W. So 24 W − 4 W = 20 W becomes mechanical power.
  5. Of that 20 W, 18 W leaves the shaft; the remaining 2 W is friction, windage and iron loss.
  6. Efficiency: η = Pout ÷ Pin = 18 W ÷ 24 W = 0.75, that is 75%.

Answer. Input power 24 W, back emf 10 V, efficiency 75%.

Worked example 2

Question. A 24 V solenoid valve has a coil resistance of 12 Ω. Its driver applies 24 V to pull in, then drops to 6 V to hold. The valve is energised for 2 s in every 20 s. Find (a) the pull-in current and power, (b) the hold current and power, and (c) the duty cycle and the average power if it were held at 24 V.

  1. (a) Pull-in: I = V/R = 24 / 12 = 2.0 A; P = V I = 24 × 2.0 = 48 W.
  2. (b) Hold: I = 6 / 12 = 0.5 A; P = I²R = 0.5² × 12 = 3.0 W, one sixteenth of the pull-in power, because power goes as V².
  3. (c) Duty cycle = ton / T × 100 % = 2 / 20 × 100 % = 10 %. Held at 24 V the average power would be 0.10 × 48 = 4.8 W.
  4. Check: with the peak-and-hold driver the coil runs at 3.0 W while on, far below 48 W, so the same coil could be held continuously without overheating.

Exam traps

  • Coil rating is not contact rating. A 24 V coil may switch 230 V mains, so give both when asked for a relay's ratings.
  • At standstill a DC motor has Eb = 0, so Ia = V ÷ Ra. A 12 V motor with 1 Ω that draws 2 A at load draws 12 A at start; the supply and driver must survive that.
  • Hold current is not pull-in current. Holding needs less current, and running a 10% duty-cycle coil at pull-in current continuously burns it out.

6.6 Stepper motors and servo motors

A stepper motor turns in fixed angular steps. Each pulse from the driver advances the shaft by one step, and the controller counts pulses to know position without feedback. A servo motor closes a loop: an encoder or potentiometer reports the shaft angle, the drive compares it with the command, and the motor corrects the error. Steppers suit cheap positioning in printers, 3D printers and small CNC tables. Servos suit robot joints, conveyor tracking and machine tools where load changes and accuracy must hold. In hospitals, steppers drive syringe and infusion pumps, and servos move surgical robots, X-ray tables and CT gantries.

You need solenoids, relays and motors for motor torque, back emf and the idea of a rotating magnetic field.

Step angle, types and phase sequence

A stepper motor turns in discrete angular steps. Its rotor aligns with the magnetic field set by stator phases. Energise the phases in a fixed order and the rotor jumps from one stable position to the next. The step angle is the angle of one jump.

θs = 360° / N

θs is the step angle in degrees; N is the number of full steps per revolution.

Three common constructions exist. A variable-reluctance motor uses a soft-iron rotor and gives small steps but low torque. A permanent-magnet motor uses a magnet rotor and gives more torque. A hybrid motor combines both and is common at 1.8° per step, that is 200 steps per revolution.

Full-step drive energises one or two phases at a time. The figure shows a one-phase-on sequence: A, then B, then A′, then B′. A′ means A-bar, the opposite phase. Half-step drive alternates one-phase and two-phase states, so the shaft moves half a step each time. Microstepping drives two phases with sine and cosine currents. The field moves smoothly between full-step positions. The commanded increment becomes smaller, but the incremental torque for a small move is small too.

Pull-in torque is the largest load torque at which the motor can start and run without losing steps at a given pulse rate. Pull-out torque is the largest torque it can hold while running after it has started. Pull-in is lower than pull-out. Torque falls as the stepping rate rises because winding inductance limits current build-up. Near the natural frequency the rotor can resonate and lose steps. Half-stepping or mechanical damping reduces this.

Servo motor: error, feedback and modes

Servo motor means a motor used inside a closed loop. A command r(t) sets the wanted position, speed or torque. A sensor measures the actual value y(t). The drive forms the error e(t) = r(t) − y(t) and applies current to reduce it. When e is zero, the shaft holds the command.

Feedback is a potentiometer for a cheap angle measurement, or an encoder for digital position counts. A resolver gives sine and cosine signals as the shaft turns.

DC servo uses a permanent-magnet DC motor with an encoder and a PWM drive. AC servo uses a synchronous motor with an encoder or resolver; the drive controls stator currents by field orientation. Three command modes are common. Position mode holds an angle. Speed mode holds revolutions per minute. Torque mode holds motor current, hence torque.

Speed, position and a lead screw

A lead screw converts rotation into straight motion. The lead p is the distance the nut travels in one revolution, in mm/rev. The pitch is the spacing of neighbouring threads; for a single-start screw the two are equal, and for a two-start screw the lead is twice the pitch. For a stepper with N steps per revolution, one step moves the table by p/N. If pulses arrive at f pulses per second, the shaft speed is n = f/N rev/s, where N must be the steps per revolution of the drive mode in use: 200 in full step, 400 in half step and 3200 at 1/16 microstepping for a 1.8° motor. The table speed is v = n p = f p/N.

v = f p / N

v is table speed in mm/s; f is pulse frequency in pulses per second; p is the lead in mm/rev; N is steps per revolution.

dstep = p / N

dstep is linear movement per step in mm; p is the lead in mm/rev; N is steps per revolution.

Use the same idea for a servo. An encoder with C counts per revolution gives table movement per count p/C. A command of M counts moves the table M p/C mm. This is why a servo can stop at a position even if the load changes: the encoder count is measured, not assumed. With microstepping, use commanded microsteps per revolution for resolution, but remember the mechanical step is still 360°/N.

Worked example

Question. A stepper motor has a step angle of 1.8°. It is driven by 1000 pulses per second. The shaft turns a lead screw of pitch 5 mm. Find (a) steps per revolution, (b) shaft speed in rev/s and rpm, (c) table speed in mm/s, and (d) linear distance per step.

  1. Steps per revolution: N = 360° / θs = 360° / 1.8° = 200 steps per revolution.
  2. One pulse gives one step, so the step rate is 1000 steps per second.
  3. Shaft speed: n = f / N = 1000 / 200 = 5 rev/s.
  4. Shaft speed in rpm: 5 × 60 = 300 rpm.
  5. Table speed: v = n p = 5 rev/s × 5 mm/rev = 25 mm/s.
  6. Linear distance per step: dstep = p / N = 5 mm / 200 = 0.025 mm per step.

Answer. The motor has 200 steps per revolution, runs at 5 rev/s or 300 rpm, moves the table at 25 mm/s, and advances 0.025 mm per step.

Worked example 2

Question. A syringe pump moves the plunger of a 50 mL syringe (inner diameter 26.7 mm) with a 1.8° stepper at 1/16 microstepping on a lead screw of 1 mm lead. Find (a) microsteps per revolution and plunger travel per microstep, (b) volume per microstep, and (c) the pulse rate for an infusion of 10 mL/h.

Exam traps

  • Step angle is not the final resolution after microstepping. A 1.8° motor with 1/16 microstepping gives 0.1125° per commanded microstep, but the mechanical step is still 1.8° and small moves have low incremental torque.
  • Pull-in torque and pull-out torque are not the same. Pull-in is the limit for starting and running; pull-out is the limit while already running. Pull-in is lower.
  • Pulse frequency does not multiply speed by steps per revolution. Shaft speed is f/N rev/s, so more steps per revolution means slower speed for the same pulse rate.
  • Pitch and lead are equal only for a single-start screw; use the lead (travel per revolution), and use the steps per revolution of the drive mode actually set.
  • A servo drive with the feedback sign reversed drives the error larger, not smaller. The shaft runs away instead of holding position.

6.7 Micro actuators: piezoelectric, shape memory and electrostatic

Micro actuators make controlled motion on the scale of micrometres: a valve opens, a mirror tilts, a droplet leaves a nozzle. They exist because coils and magnets do not shrink well; a tiny coil has few turns and high resistance, so magnetic force falls away. Three common families use piezoelectric strain, shape-memory phase change, or electrostatic attraction. You meet them in inkjet print heads, micropumps, RF MEMS switches, the self-test actuator inside some accelerometers, and micro mirrors.

You need piezoelectric transducers and MEMS micromachining; see pneumatic and hydraulic actuators for the macro fluid-power contrast.

Piezoelectric actuation: strain from charge

The inverse piezoelectric effect is the useful half of the story. Apply an electric field across a poled piezoelectric ceramic and it changes shape. The strain is small but the force is large and the response is fast. A PZT plate can reach about 0.1% strain; a 100 µm length therefore moves about 0.1 µm. That is enough for fine positioning but not for a long stroke.

S = d33 E

S is strain (ΔL/L, no unit); d33 is the piezoelectric charge coefficient in m/V, often quoted in pm/V; E is the electric field in V/m. For PZT, d33 is a few hundred pm/V, and the data sheet value depends on the ceramic and its poling.

A stack actuator puts many thin ceramic layers mechanically in series and electrically in parallel. The same low voltage drives every layer, and the small strains add, so the stack gives a larger stroke and a high stiffness. A bimorph is two piezo layers on a thin beam; one expands while the other contracts, and the beam bends. A bimorph gives a larger tip deflection at lower force. Both types can run at kHz rates.

  • Inkjet print head: a piezo element squeezes an ink chamber and shoots a drop.
  • Micropump: a piezo disc flexes a diaphragm.
  • Precision positioning: stacks hold optical parts without gears.

Shape memory alloy: strain from phase change

A shape memory alloy (SMA) changes crystal phase with temperature. Below a transition temperature it is in martensite, which is soft and can be deformed. Heat it and it becomes austenite, and it returns to its remembered shape. The recoverable strain is large, typically 4% to 8%, far more than piezo strain. The force per volume is also high.

The catch is heat. A small SMA wire is heated by passing current, but cooling is by conduction and convection into the surroundings. Cooling is slow, so a simple SMA actuator may cycle at only a few hertz or less. The phase change also has hysteresis: the heating and cooling curves do not coincide. For repeated motion, a bias spring or a second wire pulls the actuator back.

  • Wire or film: NiTi is common; a 50 µm wire can be Joule-heated.
  • Microgripper: two SMA arms close when heated.
  • Microvalve: an SMA plug opens a flow channel.
  • Limit: slow cooling, hysteresis and fatigue set the cycle life.

Nitinol (NiTi) is the medical shape memory alloy. A self-expanding stent is crimped small and cold, slid through a catheter, and opens to its remembered diameter at body temperature; the same alloy makes kink-resistant guidewires and orthodontic arch wires that pull teeth with a steady force. Piezo micropumps deliver insulin and drugs, and piezo elements are the heart of every ultrasound probe.

Electrostatic actuation: force from voltage

Two conducting plates with a gap form a capacitor. Apply a voltage and the plates attract. The capacitance is C = ε0 A / g, where ε0 is the permittivity of free space (8.854 × 10−12 F/m), A is the overlap area in m², and g is the gap in m. The stored energy is U = CV²/2. At constant applied voltage the force is +∂U/∂x with x measured along the closing direction, which gives the formula below; at constant charge the sign flips but the magnitude is the same. Here x is displacement in m and U is stored energy in J.

F = ε0 A V² / (2 g²)

F is force in N; V is applied voltage in V; ε0 is 8.854 × 10−12 F/m; A is area in m²; g is gap in m. The force goes as V², so reversing the voltage does not reverse the force.

A parallel-plate actuator is simple but has pull-in. As the plate moves, g falls and F rises as 1/g². With g0 the initial gap, the spring force rises linearly with displacement x = g0 − g, that is, as the gap g falls. Beyond x = g0/3 (remaining gap 2g0/3) the electrostatic force grows faster than the spring can balance and the plates snap together; a simple voltage control cannot hold a stable position past that point.

A comb drive avoids that in its travel direction. Interlocking fingers change overlap area, not gap, so the force is nearly constant with position. Comb drives still need tens of volts because the gaps are small and the capacitance is only picofarads. They are common in micro mirrors and resonant sensors.

Magnetic, fluidic and where micro actuators are used

Magnetic micro actuation uses a coil or a thin magnetic film. At small scale a coil has few turns and high resistance, so the force is modest and the drive current is high. Magnetic actuation still appears where a large throw or a bistable latch is needed; for coils at macro scale see solenoids, relays and motors. Fluidic micro actuation uses pressure in a channel to push a membrane or a plug. It gives a large force, but it needs valves, seals and a pressure source.

  • RF MEMS switch: an electrostatic or piezo beam closes a radio-frequency contact; low loss and low power when held.
  • Micro mirror: a torsion plate tilts under electrostatic force; arrays steer light in displays and scanners.
  • Micropump and inkjet: piezo or electrostatic drive moves a diaphragm.

6.8 DC and AC bridges: Wheatstone, Kelvin, Maxwell, Hay and Schering

A bridge compares an unknown component against known ones and adjusts until a detector reads zero. At that null the balance equation gives the unknown directly, so the answer depends on ratios, not on source voltage or meter calibration. Wheatstone bridges do this for resistance, Kelvin bridges for very small resistance, and Maxwell, Hay and Schering bridges for inductance and capacitance at AC. Bridges sit inside sensor signal conditioning — strain-gauge load cells, RTDs, inductive and capacitive transducers — wherever a tiny impedance change must be read accurately.

You need resistive transducers and strain gauges, and inductive and capacitive transducers, for impedance ideas; see signal conditioning for where a bridge output goes next.

Wheatstone bridge: four arms and a null

Four impedances form a diamond. A–B is Z1, A–C is Z2, B–D is Z3, C–D is Z4. The source drives A and D. A null detector connects B and C; it may be a galvanometer, a vibration galvanometer or a differential amplifier. Adjust one known arm until the detector reads zero. At that instant the potentials at B and C are equal.

Z1/Z3 = Z2/Z4

Z1 to Z4 are the four arm impedances; at balance the detector between B and C carries no current. For pure resistances this becomes R1/R3 = R2/Rx, so Rx = R2R3/R1, where R1 is arm A–B, R2 is A–C, R3 is B–D and Rx is C–D.

  1. Set the supply to a safe value and connect the detector between B and C.
  2. Adjust the known arm for the smallest detector reading.
  3. Check that the null is sharp, not a broad minimum.
  4. Compute the unknown from the final arm values.

Near balance the detector output is proportional to the supply voltage and to the fractional change in the unknown. With one active arm, a 1% change in Rx gives about 0.25% of the supply voltage (V/400 for small changes), so a low-noise detector and the largest allowed supply improve sensitivity. Below about 1 Ω, lead and contact resistance add to the arms; the null then measures the leads as much as the unknown. Wheatstone bridges serve strain-gauge load cells, RTDs and resistor calibration. In medicine the disposable blood-pressure transducer is a strain-gauge Wheatstone bridge, and thermistor temperature probes and the hot-wire flow sensors of ventilators are read in bridges too.

Kelvin double bridge: four terminals for milliohms

A Kelvin double bridge is for resistances below about 1 Ω. It uses four terminals on the unknown: two current terminals carry the test current, and two potential terminals sense the voltage. The voltage drop in the current leads and contacts is outside the measured path. Two ratio pairs, outer R1–R2 and inner r1–r2, link through a low resistance r. Keep the potential leads separate from the current leads right up to the unknown.

Rx = (R1/R2) Rs

Rx is the low unknown resistance, Rs is a known standard resistor, and R1 and R2 are the outer ratio arms. The equality holds when the inner ratio arms are set to the same ratio, r1/r2 = R1/R2; then the link and lead terms cancel.

The double bridge measures motor and transformer windings, busbar joints, switch contacts, shunts and cable resistance. Precision ohmmeters and LCR meters use the same four-terminal connection so probe leads do not enter the answer.

Maxwell, Hay and Schering: AC bridges

AC bridges balance complex impedances. The source is a sine wave, often 50 Hz, 1 kHz or 10 kHz. The detector is a tuned amplifier or a phase-sensitive detector. Balance needs equal magnitude and equal phase, so one control balances magnitude and another balances phase.

Maxwell bridge: the unknown Lx–Rx arm is balanced by R1 in parallel with C1. At balance Lx = R2R3C1 and Rx = R2R3/R1. Q = ωLx/Rx = ωR1C1. It suits Q from about 1 to 10. R2 and R3 are resistive arms, C1 is in farads, and ω = 2πf.

Hay bridge: the RC arm is series instead of parallel. At balance Lx = R2R3C1/(1 + ω²R1²C1²), with R1 in series with C1. In the Hay bridge Q = ωLx/Rx = 1/(ωR1C1), so ω²R1²C1² = 1/Q². For Q = 10 the correction term is 1/(1 + 1/Q²) = 0.99; for Q = 2 it is 0.80, which is why the bridge is used only above Q ≈ 10.

Schering bridge: the unknown Cx–Rx arm is balanced by standard capacitor C2, resistor R3 and a parallel R4–C4 arm. Balance gives Cx = C2R4/R3 and tan δ = ωR4C4. δ is the loss angle and tan δ the dissipation factor. High voltage stresses insulation, so the bridge tests cables, bushings and transformer insulation.

6.9 Filters, pre-amplifiers, differential and isolation amplifiers

A sensor often gives a small, noisy voltage riding on a larger unwanted component, such as a DC offset, drift, or 50 Hz mains pickup. Filters decide which frequencies reach the next stage. A pre-amplifier raises the weak signal while adding little noise and presenting a high input impedance. A differential amplifier amplifies the difference between two inputs and rejects what both share. An isolation amplifier passes the signal across a barrier, so grounds do not join. Together they turn a raw sensor output into a clean signal for the conditioning chain and for transmission.

You need signal conditioning for the conditioning chain, and basic op-amp circuits (inverting, non-inverting, virtual short).

Filters: choosing the frequency band

A filter is a frequency-dependent voltage divider. It passes a band of frequencies and attenuates the rest. A low-pass filter passes frequencies below its cutoff fc and blocks higher ones. A high-pass filter does the reverse. A band-pass filter passes a band between two cutoffs. A band-stop filter rejects a band. A notch filter is a very narrow band-stop, used to remove 50 Hz mains pickup coupled into long sensor leads.

The cutoff is the frequency where gain falls to 1/√2 = 0.707 of the pass-band value. In decibels that is −3 dB. The order n sets the steepness beyond cutoff: each order adds about 20 dB per decade, or 6 dB per octave, of attenuation. A first-order low-pass has the response below.

|Hlow| = 1/√(1 + (f/fc)²)

Hlow is voltage gain; f is signal frequency in Hz; fc is cutoff frequency in Hz. At f = fc, |Hlow| = 0.707.

|Hhigh| = (f/fc)/√(1 + (f/fc)²)

Hhigh is voltage gain of a first-order high-pass; f and fc are in Hz.

The simplest low-pass is a resistor R in series and capacitor C to ground. Its cutoff is:

fc = 1/(2πRC)

R is series resistance in ohms; C is capacitance in farads; fc is in Hz.

An active filter uses an op-amp to give gain and a low output impedance. The Sallen-Key circuit is a common two-resistor, two-capacitor second-order section. It gives a 40 dB per decade roll-off. Choose the filter before the amplifier when a large out-of-band signal would overload the amplifier. The figure plots the first-order low-pass and high-pass responses against f/fc.

Pre-amplifiers and differential amplifiers

A pre-amplifier is the first amplifier after the sensor. It must add as little noise as possible, because later stages amplify its noise too. At low frequency the pre-amplifier works as a bridging amplifier: input impedance much higher than the source, so a high-impedance sensor such as a piezoelectric element or a thermistor is not loaded. Only when a matched coaxial cable carries a high-frequency signal is the cable terminated in its characteristic impedance (for example 50 Ω) to stop reflections; the amplifier input is then matched to the cable, not to the sensor. Its gain is chosen so the largest expected sensor signal fills the input range of the next stage without clipping.

A differential amplifier has two inputs, V1 and V2, and responds to their difference Vd = V1 − V2. It also responds a little to their average Vcm = (V1 + V2)/2. The differential gain is Ad = Vo/Vd when Vcm = 0. The common-mode gain is Acm = Vo/Vcm when Vd = 0.

CMRR = 20 log₁₀(Ad/Acm)

Ad is differential gain in V/V; Acm is common-mode gain in V/V; CMRR is in dB. Higher CMRR is better.

A single op-amp difference circuit needs four resistors to set Ad. With 1 % resistors the worst-case ratio for a gain of 100 is about (1 + 100)/(4 × 0.01) ≈ 2500, only 68 dB, whatever the design aimed for. The three op-amp instrumentation amplifier avoids this. Two input buffers give high input impedance and set the gain with one resistor; the third op-amp subtracts the two buffered signals. Laser-trimmed resistor networks give CMRR of 80 dB to 120 dB.

The ECG amplifier puts this section together. The heart gives about 1 mV, the electrodes add up to ±300 mV of DC offset, and the body picks up a 50 Hz common-mode voltage from the mains wiring. The front end is an instrumentation amplifier of modest gain (so the offset cannot saturate it), a high-pass filter near 0.05 Hz to remove the offset, a low-pass at 150 Hz for diagnosis or 40 Hz for monitoring, an optional 50 Hz notch, a driven right-leg electrode that feeds back the inverted common-mode voltage, and an isolation barrier.

Isolation amplifiers and input protection

An isolation amplifier passes the signal across an insulating barrier. The input side and output side have no direct electrical path. A transformer-coupled isolation amplifier modulates the signal onto a carrier, passes it through a small transformer, and demodulates it. An optocoupler version uses an LED and photodiode or phototransistor; it is compact but slower and less linear. Two ratings matter. The isolation (test) voltage is the short-duration voltage the barrier survives in a factory proof test, usually applied for about one minute. The working voltage is the much lower continuous voltage the barrier can take in service. Choose a part whose working voltage, not just its test voltage, exceeds the highest continuous voltage between the two grounds.

Isolation breaks ground loops. A ground loop forms when two grounded instruments are joined by a signal cable; the small voltage between their ground points drives a current through the cable screen and adds noise. The loop is broken when one instrument floats on the isolated side. In medical equipment, isolation protects the patient from mains fault current through a catheter or electrode: the patient side floats behind the barrier, and the leakage current that can reach a heart-connected electrode is held to about 10 µA in normal use.

Surge protection at the input uses a series resistor or inductor and a clamp, such as a transient voltage suppressor, metal-oxide varistor or gas discharge tube. The clamp limits a lightning surge or switching spike before it reaches the amplifier. Place the clamp after the series resistance so the resistor limits the clamp current. The isolation barrier follows the clamp; it does not replace the clamp.

6.10 Display instruments: DVM, multimeter, CRO, CRT deflection, DSO and spectrum analyser

A display instrument turns an electrical quantity into a number or a trace. Numbers suit slow, accurate measurements; traces suit waveforms and one-time events. Every display trades accuracy, bandwidth and capture ability. This chapter covers the DVM, CRO, DSO and spectrum analyser, and the numbers that describe them.

You need filters and amplifiers for the amplifiers and filters ahead of a display, and accuracy and resolution from the static characteristics of sensors.

DVM: counts, resolution and accuracy

A digital voltmeter (DVM) converts an input voltage into a display number. The display has N counts, which fixes resolution: it splits the full-scale range into N steps.

resolution = range / counts

range is the full-scale voltage of the selected range, in V; counts is the number of steps the display can show; resolution is the smallest voltage change the display can show, in V.

A 3½ digit display has three full digits and one half digit that is only 0 or 1. Largest reading 1999, so 2000 counts. A 4½ digit meter has 20 000 counts. The sign is separate.

Accuracy is quoted against reading, not range. The counts term is fixed last-digit uncertainty; the percentage term is gain error that grows with reading. Near full scale the percentage term dominates; near zero the counts term dominates. Use the lowest range that does not overload.

  • Ramp DVM: a ramp starts and a counter runs; the input stops the counter. Voltage becomes time, counted.
  • Integrating DVM: integrates input over a whole number of mains cycles (100 ms at 50 Hz). Mains hum integrates to zero.
  • Autozero: meter measures its own offset with input shorted and subtracts it.

A multimeter adds current, resistance and continuity. On voltage ranges the input looks like about 10 MΩ, so a high-resistance source reads low. On current ranges a shunt inserts burden voltage, lost from the circuit.

CRO: CRT deflection and block diagram

The cathode ray tube (CRT) is a vacuum glass envelope. An electron gun fires a thin beam at a phosphor screen; the phosphor glows where the beam lands. Two plate pairs deflect it: vertical plates stacked one above the other push up/down; horizontal plates side by side push left/right. This is electrostatic deflection.

D = L·l·Vd / (2·d·Va), S = D / Vd

D is spot deflection on the screen, in m; L is distance from plate centre to screen; l is plate length; d is plate separation; Vd is voltage between the plates; Va is accelerating voltage; S is deflection sensitivity, in m/V. S falls as Va rises, so a brighter, faster beam needs more amplifier gain.

The panel quotes volts per division. The vertical channel is an attenuator (V/div switch) then a vertical amplifier driving vertical plates. The horizontal channel is a time base generating a linear ramp, then a horizontal amplifier driving horizontal plates. The ramp sweeps the spot once per ramp, so time becomes horizontal distance.

A trigger starts the ramp only when the input crosses a chosen level in a chosen direction. A repeating waveform then stands still. A ×10 probe divides the input by ten before the attenuator, so multiply the panel reading by ten to get actual input.

T = (time/div) × divisions, f = 1 / T

T is one sweep time in s; time/div is the panel setting in s/div; divisions is the number of horizontal divisions the sweep covers (usually 10); f is the frequency of the displayed waveform in Hz.

DSO and spectrum analyser

A digital storage oscilloscope (DSO) replaces phosphor with an ADC, memory and screen. The input passes the same attenuator and amplifier, is sampled at sampling rate fs, stored in N samples, and drawn from memory.

record time = (time/div) × divisions,   N = fs × record time,   fmax = fs / 2

record time is the captured time in s; fs is sampling rate in samples per second; N is record length, the number of stored samples; fmax is the highest frequency the record can show without aliasing, in Hz.

Sampling below 2f, where f is signal frequency, folds the signal to a false low frequency: aliasing. The trace looks stable and is wrong. Raise fs above 2f (Nyquist limit) or use the bandwidth limit filter.

Pre-trigger stores samples from before the trigger, so you see what caused an event. With single-shot capture, a DSO catches a one-time glitch a CRT would miss.

The spectrum analyser displays amplitude against frequency. A swept superheterodyne type mixes input with a swept local oscillator, passes the difference through a band-pass filter of width RBW (resolution bandwidth), detects and plots it. Span is the frequency range shown. Narrow RBW separates close tones and lowers noise floor, but the sweep must be slower. An FFT analyser digitises a block and computes a fast Fourier transform, giving the whole spectrum at once. In medicine the same displays appear at the bedside: a patient monitor is a DSO that sweeps the ECG at the standard 25 mm/s, freezes an arrhythmia for review, and an EEG system uses the FFT to show power in the delta, theta, alpha and beta bands.

Worked example

Question. A 3½ digit DVM on the 2 V range has 2000 counts and accuracy ±(0.5% of reading + 2 counts). It reads 1.000 V. Find resolution and error. A DSO is set to 1 µs/div over 10 divisions and samples at 100 MS/s; find record time and record length.

  1. Resolution = range / counts = 2 V / 2000 = 0.001 V = 1 mV. One count is 1 mV.
  2. Two counts = 2 × 1 mV = 2 mV.
  3. Percentage term = 0.5% of 1.000 V = 0.005 × 1.000 V = 5 mV.
  4. Total error = 5 mV + 2 mV = 7 mV, so true value lies in 1.000 V ± 7 mV.
  5. Record time = (time/div) × divisions = 1 µs/div × 10 div = 10 µs.
  6. Record length N = fs × record time = 100 × 10⁶ samples/s × 10 × 10⁻⁶ s = 1000 samples.
  7. Highest frequency shown without aliasing = fs/2 = 50 MHz.

Answer. Resolution 1 mV; error ±7 mV on a 1.000 V reading; DSO record time 10 µs holding a record length of 1000 samples.

Worked example 2

Question. A CRT has deflection plates 2 cm long and 5 mm apart, with their centre 20 cm from the screen, and an accelerating voltage of 2000 V. (a) Find the deflection sensitivity. (b) Find the plate voltage for a 1 cm deflection. (c) On the same CRO one cycle of a waveform spans 4 divisions at 0.2 ms/div; find its frequency.

  1. (a) S = L l / (2 d Va) = (0.20 × 0.02) / (2 × 0.005 × 2000) = 0.004 / 20 = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m/V = 0.2 mm/V.
  2. (b) Vd = D / S = 0.01 m / 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m/V = 50 V. The vertical amplifier must supply this from a few millivolts at the input.
  3. (c) T = 4 div × 0.2 ms/div = 0.8 ms; f = 1/T = 1 / 0.8 × 10⁻³ = 1250 Hz.
  4. Check: doubling Va to 4000 V halves S to 0.1 mm/V, so a brighter, faster beam needs 100 V for the same 1 cm, as the formula says.

Answer. S = 0.2 mm/V, 50 V gives 1 cm, and the waveform is at 1250 Hz.

Exam traps

  • A 3½ digit display has 2000 counts (largest reading 1999), not 3000. On the 2 V range one count is 1 mV.
  • If a DSO samples below twice the signal frequency, the trace folds to a false low frequency; it looks stable and is wrong. Check fs against 2f.
  • The V/div setting ignores the probe. With a ×10 probe, 3.2 div peak-to-peak at 2 V/div is 3.2 × 2 × 10 = 64 V, not 6.4 V.
  • On a swept spectrum analyser a wide RBW hides two close tones as one peak, and noise floor depends on RBW, so compare spectra at the same RBW.

Takeaways

  1. Signal conditioning must match the sensor output range and impedance to the ADC input range and must reject noise before conversion.
  2. A data acquisition system is only as good as its weakest link: resolution, sampling rate, settling time and channel order all matter.
  3. Current loops and differential transmission protect small signals over long distances.
  4. Pneumatic actuators suit clean, spark-free, moderate-force work; hydraulic actuators suit high force.
  5. Solenoids and relays give on/off motion; motors give continuous motion.

Understand Draw the block diagram of a data acquisition system and explain the job of each block. 10 marks

Points to cover. Transducer and signal conditioner; Multiplexer and sample-and-hold; ADC and processor or controller; Store, display or bus interface.

Model answer.

  1. Block diagram. The blocks sit in signal order:
    transducers → signal conditioners → multiplexer → sample-and-hold → ADC → processor / controller → store, display or bus
                                   ↑ channel select and convert commands from the processor
  2. Transducer. Converts the physical variable (temperature, pressure, strain, a heartbeat) into an electrical quantity: millivolts from a thermocouple or bridge, a resistance change from an RTD, a current from a photodiode.
  3. Signal conditioner. Amplifies, filters, linearises and isolates each sensor output to a standard range such as 0 to 5 V or ±5 V. Its low-pass filter (the anti-aliasing filter) removes components above half the channel's sample rate before sampling, because nothing after the multiplexer can remove them.
  4. Multiplexer. An analogue switch bank that connects one channel at a time to the shared converter, in the order the processor chooses. It lets one accurate ADC serve 8, 16 or more channels; the price is that the converter's speed is shared, fch = ftotal/N.
  5. Sample-and-hold. Closes a switch to charge a capacitor to the input (acquisition), then opens it and holds the value while the ADC converts. It removes the error from the input moving during the conversion; what remains is the aperture error, (rate of change) × (aperture time).
  6. ADC. Converts the held voltage into an n-bit number. The bit count fixes the resolution, ΔV = VFS/2ⁿ, and the converter type (SAR, flash, sigma-delta) fixes the speed.
  7. Processor or controller. Selects channels, starts conversions, scales the counts into engineering units (°C, kPa, mmHg), applies calibration and alarm limits, and time-stamps the readings.
  8. Store, display or bus. Keeps the data in memory, shows it on a screen, or sends it over RS-485, USB, Ethernet or a fieldbus to a supervisory computer.
  9. Worked numbers. 8 channels, ±5 V input, 12-bit ADC, 4 channels scanned at 1 kS/s each:

Remember Define signal conditioning. List and explain amplification, filtering, linearisation and isolation. 8 marks

Points to cover. Definition and need near the sensor; Amplification for small signals; Filtering to remove noise and aliasing; Linearisation and electrical isolation.

Model answer.

  1. Definition. Signal conditioning is the processing between a sensor and the next stage (ADC, transmitter, display) that turns a weak, noisy, non-linear, possibly unsafe sensor output into a clean signal of standard range, for example 0 to 5 V or 4 to 20 mA. It is done close to the sensor so noise and cable losses act on a large, robust signal.
  2. Amplification. Raises a millivolt signal to the ADC range. An instrumentation amplifier is used because it has a very high input impedance (it does not load a bridge) and a high CMRR (it rejects the volts of common-mode offset on both bridge outputs). Gain and its decibel value:
    G = Rf / Rin = 100 kΩ / 200 Ω = 500;  GdB = 20 log₁₀ 500 = 54.0 dB
2 mV × 500 = 1.0 V
  3. Gain multiplies the offset and noise too: 0.1 mV of input offset becomes 50 mV. Large signals need the opposite, attenuation by a divider, and the next stage must have an input resistance well above the source resistance (impedance matching in the measurement sense).
  4. Filtering. Removes interference outside the signal band: 50 Hz mains hum, switching-supply noise, radio pickup, and anything above half the sample rate that would alias. A first-order RC low-pass:
    fc = 1/(2πRC) = 1/(2π × 10 kΩ × 1 µF) = 15.9 Hz
at 50 Hz: |H| = 1/√(1 + (50/15.9)²) = 0.303, about −10.4 dB
  5. A filter also delays the signal and slows a step (time constant RC), so fc is placed above the highest real signal frequency and below the noise.
  6. Linearisation. Makes the output proportional to the measured quantity when the sensor is not linear (thermocouple polynomial, thermistor exponential). Hardware breakpoint circuits add slope corrections between reference levels; software uses a calibration table and interpolates. Worked value: table points 2010 counts at 36.0 °C and 1890 counts at 38.0 °C give, for 1950 counts, T = 36.0 + (1950 − 2010)(2.0)/(1890 − 2010) = 37.0 °C.
  7. Isolation. Breaks the direct conducting path between the sensor side and the computer side, by a transformer, an optocoupler or a capacitive barrier in an isolation amplifier. It breaks ground loops (two earths at different potentials driving current through the signal cable), protects the electronics from surges on long cables, and protects people: an ECG front end floats the patient side so that leakage current stays at microamperes.
  8. Other jobs. Excitation (a bridge or RTD needs a stable supply), cold-junction compensation for thermocouples, surge clamping, and conversion between voltage and current for transmission.
  9. Check. With the numbers above, the 2 mV bridge signal leaves the amplifier at 1.0 V while 1 V of 50 Hz hum at the filter input would leave it at 0.303 V; a second filter order or a notch is needed if the hum must be below 1 % of the signal.
Understand Compare a stepper motor and a servo motor on motion, feedback, cost and load behaviour. 10 marks

Points to cover. Stepper moves in fixed steps, often open-loop; Servo uses encoder or resolver feedback; Servo corrects error under load; Cost and complexity comparison.

Model answer.

  1. Stepper motor. A motor whose rotor moves one fixed angle for each input pulse. Step angle θs = 360°/N; a hybrid stepper has 1.8°, N = 200. Position is the count of pulses sent, so no sensor is needed.
  2. Servo motor. A DC or AC (synchronous) motor inside a closed loop: an encoder, resolver or potentiometer measures the shaft, the drive computes the error e = r − y and drives current to reduce it. It can run in position, speed or torque mode.

Apply A signal cable runs 80 m beside a motor drive. Choose voltage or current transmission, explain shielding and grounding, and calculate loop voltage at 20 mA through 100 Ω. 12 marks

Points to cover. Prefer 4–20 mA current loop for long runs; Use twisted pair with shield grounded at one end; Avoid ground loops and use differential input; V = 20 mA × 100 Ω = 2 V.

Model answer.

  1. The threat. A motor drive switches hundreds of volts in nanoseconds, so the 80 m cable picks up electric (capacitive) and magnetic (inductive) interference, and the drive's earth may sit several volts away from the control-room earth.
  2. Choice: a 4 to 20 mA current loop. In a series loop the current is the same everywhere, set by the transmitter; cable resistance and induced voltages change only the voltage across the loop, not the current. A ground difference that would add directly to a voltage signal does not change a current. The live zero (4 mA) also reveals a broken cable as 0 mA.
  3. Voltage transmission for comparison. A 0 to 10 V signal with 1 V of ground difference between the ends reads 10 % of span wrong; the same 1 V in series with a current loop needs only 1 V more from the supply.
    Effect0–10 V signal4–20 mA loop
    1 V ground difference1 V error, 10 % of spanno error
    100 Ω of cabledivider error with the input resistanceneeds 2 V more at 20 mA
    Broken wirereads 0, looks like a valuereads 0 mA, flagged as fault
  4. Shielding. Use twisted pair, which makes both wires pick up the same magnetic interference so it cancels in the receiver. Put an overall foil or braid screen around the pair to stop electric fields coupling to the conductors.
  5. Grounding. Connect the screen to earth at one end only, normally the receiver (control-room) end; earthing both ends would let the ground difference drive current along the screen, a ground loop. Keep the signal cable in a separate tray, at least 0.3 m from the motor cables, and cross them at right angles. Use an isolated input or isolator at the receiver if the loop must float.
  6. Loop voltage.

  8. Filtering at the receiver. A small RC filter (for example 1 kΩ and 1 µF, fc = 159 Hz) after the sense resistor removes the residual switching noise without slowing a process signal.

Analyze Compare flash, SAR and sigma-delta ADCs on speed, resolution, cost and noise; select one for audio and one for a slow temperature logger. 14 marks

Points to cover. Flash: fastest, low resolution, many comparators; SAR: medium speed, good resolution, common choice; Sigma-delta: slow to medium, high resolution, filters noise; Audio suits sigma-delta; temperature logger suits sigma-delta or SAR.

Model answer.

  1. Flash ADC. A resistor ladder sets 2ⁿ − 1 reference levels and one comparator sits at each level. All comparators decide at once, and a thermometer-to-binary encoder gives the output in a single clock. An 8-bit flash needs 2⁸ − 1 = 255 comparators; a 10-bit one needs 1023. Speed reaches hundreds of megasamples to gigasamples per second, but the comparator count doubles with every bit, so power, chip area, input capacitance and cost rise steeply and practical resolution stops near 8 bits.
  2. Successive approximation (SAR) ADC. One comparator, a DAC and a register perform a binary search, one bit per clock, most significant bit first. Trace for a 4-bit SAR, Vref = 16 V, input 11.3 V:
    ClockTrial codeDAC outputInput above?Bit kept
    110008 Vyesb₃ = 1
    2110012 Vnob₂ = 0
    3101010 Vyesb₁ = 1
    4101111 Vyesb₀ = 1
  3. The result 1011 = 11 is found in n = 4 clocks. Conversion time is n clock periods, so a 16-bit SAR at 16 MHz with a few clocks overhead runs at about 1 MS/s. It needs a sample-and-hold, because the input must not change during the search. Resolution of 12 to 18 bits, moderate power and low cost make it the general-purpose converter of microcontrollers and data loggers.
  4. Sigma-delta ADC. A 1-bit (or few-bit) modulator samples far faster than the Nyquist rate and feeds back its own output through an integrator, so the average of the bit stream follows the input. The loop pushes the quantisation noise up to high frequencies (noise shaping), and a digital decimation filter removes it and reduces the rate. Audio converters sample at 64 × 48 kHz = 3.072 MHz and deliver 24-bit words at 48 kS/s. The digital filter also gives very sharp anti-aliasing, so the analogue filter in front can be a simple RC. The price is latency (the filter delay) and that it is poor for multiplexing many channels.
  5. Ideal noise floor. SNR = 6.02 n + 1.76 dB for a full-scale sine:
    12 bits: 6.02 × 12 + 1.76 = 74.0 dB
16 bits: 6.02 × 16 + 1.76 = 98.08 dB
24 bits: 6.02 × 24 + 1.76 = 146.2 dB (real parts reach about 120 dB, set by analogue noise)
  6. Comparison.
    CriterionFlashSARSigma-delta
    Speedfastest: 100 MS/s to GS/smedium: 100 kS/s to a few MS/sslow to medium: up to about 1 MS/s, with latency
    Resolution6 to 8 bits8 to 18 bits16 to 24 bits
    Hardware2ⁿ − 1 comparators1 comparator, DAC, registerintegrator, 1-bit quantiser, digital filter
    Cost and powerhighlowlow to moderate

  7. Choice for audio. Sigma-delta. Audio needs 20 Hz to 20 kHz with about 100 dB of dynamic range, which a 24-bit sigma-delta at 48 kS/s gives, with its own sharp digital anti-aliasing filter. Flash cannot reach the resolution, and a SAR would need a steep analogue filter at 20 kHz.
  8. Choice for a slow temperature logger. A sampling rate of one reading per second is tiny for any converter, so the choice is set by resolution and mains rejection. A sigma-delta converter is the best fit for a thermocouple or RTD: 24 bits resolve microvolts, and a decimation filter with notches at 50 Hz rejects the hum. A 12- to 16-bit SAR is the cheaper choice when many channels are multiplexed or the sensor already gives volts; flash would waste power and give too few bits.
  9. Check. A type-K thermocouple gives about 41 µV/°C. A 24-bit sigma-delta on ±78 mV has an LSB of 0.156 V / 2²⁴ = 9.3 nV, far below 41 µV, so 0.1 °C is set by noise and the cold junction, not the converter. An 8-bit flash on the same span would give 0.61 mV per step, about 15 °C, useless for the logger.
Analyze A low-level sensor shows 50 Hz hum and a slowly drifting offset. Analyse likely causes and propose differential amplifier, filtering and isolation. 14 marks

Points to cover. Hum from mains pickup or ground loop; Drift from temperature, bias current or electrode offset; Differential amplifier rejects common-mode voltage; Low-pass or notch filter and isolation amplifier reduce hum.

Model answer.

  1. Causes of 50 Hz hum. (a) Capacitive (electric field) coupling from mains wiring onto high-impedance leads and the body, which appears as a common-mode voltage on both inputs. (b) Inductive coupling, where a 50 Hz magnetic field threads the loop formed by the two signal wires and induces a differential voltage. (c) A ground loop: the sensor and the amplifier are earthed at two points with a few hundred millivolts of 50 Hz between them, and the difference drives current along the signal return. (d) A single-ended input, which cannot tell the hum from the signal.
  2. Causes of slow offset drift. (a) Amplifier offset voltage and bias current changing with temperature. (b) Thermocouple EMFs at junctions of different metals in the wiring and connectors. (c) Sensor effects: a bridge zero that moves with temperature, or an electrode half-cell potential that wanders. (d) Supply and reference drift. Worked value: an amplifier with 2 µV/°C offset drift, in a box that warms by 20 °C, at a gain of 1000, moves its output by 2 µV × 20 × 1000 = 40 mV.
  3. Differential amplifier. Bring both sensor wires to an instrumentation amplifier, which amplifies Vd = V₁ − V₂ and rejects the common-mode voltage both wires share. Its high input impedance prevents a source-impedance imbalance from turning common-mode hum into differential hum.
  4. CMRR calculation. Gain 1000, signal 1 mV, common-mode hum 0.5 V:
    CMRRAd/AcmAcmOutput humOutput signal
    60 dB10³10.5 V1.0 V
    100 dB10⁵0.015 mV1.0 V
  5. At 60 dB the hum is half the signal; at 100 dB it is 0.5 % of it. A four-resistor difference amplifier with 1 % resistors at gain 100 reaches only about 68 dB, so a monolithic instrumentation amplifier with trimmed resistors is used.
  6. Filtering. (a) A low-pass just above the signal band, for example 10 Hz for a temperature or strain signal, gives −14 dB at 50 Hz in first order and −28 dB in second order. (b) When the signal band includes 50 Hz, as in an ECG, a notch filter at 50 Hz removes the hum with a narrow stop band. (c) A digital filter after the ADC can average over exactly one mains period (20 ms) to null the hum. Filtering does not remove drift, because drift is slow; a high-pass (for example 0.05 Hz in an ECG) removes it only when the signal has no DC content.
  7. Drift remedies. Use a zero-drift (chopper-stabilised) amplifier of about 0.05 µV/°C, which brings the 40 mV above down to 1 mV. Take the gain early so later stages matter less, keep the input junctions at one temperature, use ratiometric excitation so a supply drift cancels in a bridge, and auto-zero by reading a shorted input every few seconds and subtracting.
  8. Isolation and grounding. If a ground loop remains, an isolation amplifier or an isolated ADC breaks the conducting path, so the ground difference appears across the barrier and not in the signal. Use twisted pair against magnetic pickup and a screen earthed at one end only. In a medical front end, isolation also protects the patient, and a driven right-leg circuit feeds back the inverted common-mode voltage to cut it further.
  9. Check. With 100 dB CMRR the hum is 5 mV against a 1.0 V signal, and a second-order 10 Hz low-pass would cut that to about 0.2 mV; the zero-drift amplifier keeps drift at 1 mV for 20 °C. Both errors are now near 0.1 % of the signal.
Evaluate Evaluate electrostatic, piezoelectric and shape-memory micro actuators for a MEMS RF switch; include pull-in, hysteresis, force and speed. 14 marks

Points to cover. Electrostatic: fast, low force, pull-in instability; Piezoelectric: fast, higher force, small strain; SMA: large motion, slow, hysteresis and heat; RF switch needs low loss, repeatability and reasonable voltage.

Model answer.

  2. Electrostatic actuation. The plate force is F = ε₀AV²/(2g²). For a 200 µm × 200 µm plate, a 2 µm gap and 30 V:
    A = 4 × 10⁻⁸ m²;  F = (8.854 × 10⁻¹²)(4 × 10⁻⁸)(900) / (2 × 4 × 10⁻¹²) = 39.8 µN
  3. Pull-in. The spring force kx rises linearly while the electric force rises as 1/g², so past a travel of g₀/3 the plate snaps down. For a spring constant k = 10 N/m:
    VPI = √(8 k g₀³ / (27 ε₀ A)) = √(8 × 10 × (2 × 10⁻⁶)³ / (27 × 8.854 × 10⁻¹² × 4 × 10⁻⁸)) = 8.18 V
snap-down after 0.667 µm of travel; remaining gap 1.33 µm
  4. For a switch, pull-in is useful: the beam snaps fully closed for a firm contact. Holding needs only a voltage, so the power is almost zero (only leakage current), and release occurs at a lower voltage than pull-in, a small hysteresis that gives clean two-state action. Weaknesses: tens of volts are often needed (a charge pump on the chip), and charge trapped in the dielectric can make the beam stick.

A stack of 100 layers, each moving 50 nm, gives 5.0 µm; a thin-film cantilever bends to a few micrometres at 3 to 10 V. Force is high and response is in microseconds, with no pull-in instability, so position is continuous. Weaknesses: PZT is hard to deposit in a standard process and contaminates CMOS, and ferroelectric hysteresis of 10 to 15 % and creep make the stroke depend on history.
  6. Shape-memory actuation. A nitinol film or wire contracts 4 to 8 % when heated above its transformation temperature. It gives the largest force and stroke per volume, at low voltage. Weaknesses: it must be heated, so it uses milliwatts to watts; it cools slowly, so a cycle takes milliseconds to seconds; its thermal hysteresis is 10 to 30 °C; and it fatigues after about 10⁶ to 10⁷ cycles.

Analyze Explain how a DSO captures a single fast pulse; compare with a CRO using bandwidth, sampling rate, memory and triggering. 12 marks

Points to cover. DSO samples and stores before display; Sampling rate must exceed signal bandwidth; Trigger and memory capture one-time events; CRO shows live trace but cannot store easily.

Model answer.

  1. DSO block chain.
    probe → attenuator and amplifier (sets bandwidth) → ADC (sample rate) → acquisition memory (record length)
          ↑ trigger circuit decides which samples are kept → processor → LCD display, measurements, USB
  2. Capturing a single pulse. (a) Set single-shot (single sequence) mode. (b) Set the trigger to edge, on the expected slope and at a level halfway up the pulse. (c) The ADC runs continuously and writes into a circular memory. (d) When the trigger condition occurs, the scope keeps a set number of samples after it, then stops, so the record holds both pre-trigger samples (what happened before) and the pulse itself. (e) The stored trace stays on screen to be measured, zoomed and saved. An analogue CRO cannot do this: its spot draws the pulse once and the phosphor fades in milliseconds.
  3. Bandwidth and rise time. A scope with bandwidth BW has its own rise time tr ≈ 0.35/BW. The measured rise time is the root sum of squares:
    100 MHz scope: tr,scope = 0.35 / (100 × 10⁶) = 3.5 ns
pulse edge 5 ns: tmeasured = √(5² + 3.5²) = 6.10 ns, 22 % slow
  4. The rule is to use a bandwidth about five times the highest signal frequency, or a scope rise time a third to a fifth of the edge to be measured. The same 0.35/BW rule holds for a CRO; its vertical amplifier sets the bandwidth.
  5. Sampling rate. A DSO needs several samples on the fastest edge: about 2.5 to 5 samples per period of the bandwidth for a repetitive signal, and more for a single shot because there is no second pass to fill the gaps. At 1 GS/s the samples are 1 ns apart, so a 5 ns edge gets five points. A CRO has no sampling: the trace is continuous.
  6. Memory. Record length = sample rate × time window. Too little memory forces the scope to slow its sampling when the time base is long.
    1 GS/s × 10 µs = 10 000 samples
with a 10 000-point memory, a 1 ms window drops the rate to 10 MS/s (100 ns per sample)
  7. Triggering. A CRO trigger only starts the sweep, and a non-repeating event shows once and is gone. A DSO trigger selects which part of the stored record to keep and can look before the event (pre-trigger). Advanced triggers (pulse width, runt, glitch, pattern) catch faults that an edge trigger misses.

Evaluate A Kelvin bridge and a Wheatstone bridge are offered for 0.001 Ω measurement. Evaluate lead resistance, sensitivity and practical use. 12 marks

Points to cover. Wheatstone lead resistance adds error at low R; Kelvin bridge uses four-terminal connection; Kelvin separates current and voltage leads; Choose Kelvin for low resistance, Wheatstone for ordinary R.

Model answer.

  1. Wheatstone bridge on 0.001 Ω. The bridge balances at Rx = R₃(R₂/R₁), but whatever lies in the Rx arm is measured. Two leads of 0.01 Ω each add 0.02 Ω:
    measured = 0.001 + 0.02 = 0.021 Ω;  error = 0.02 / 0.001 × 100 = 2000 %
  2. Contact resistance at the clamps (also milliohms) and thermal EMFs at the joints add more. The Wheatstone bridge is therefore used from about 1 Ω upward, where 0.02 Ω is 2 % or less.
  3. Sensitivity of the Wheatstone bridge here. To keep the unknown from overheating, the current is limited, so the detector sees a tiny unbalance voltage. A 1 mΩ resistor at 10 A gives only 10 mV across itself, and a 0.1 % unbalance gives 10 µV at the detector, near the noise of an ordinary galvanometer.
  4. Kelvin double bridge. The unknown Rx and a standard Rs of similar size are joined by a heavy link of resistance r and carry the same large current. Each has separate current and potential terminals (four-terminal connection). A second set of ratio arms a and b is connected to the potential terminals, so the voltage is sensed inside the current contacts.

with a/b = R₁/R₂ the link term vanishes:  Rx = Rs R₁/R₂

Glossary

4–20 mA loop
A current transmission standard with 4 mA at zero and 20 mA at full scale.
ADC
Analogue-to-digital converter; it turns a continuous voltage into a digital code.
Anti-aliasing filter
A low-pass filter placed before an ADC to remove frequencies above half the sampling rate.
Back EMF
Voltage induced by a changing magnetic field when a coil current is switched off.
CMRR
Common-mode rejection ratio: how strongly a differential amplifier rejects a voltage common to both inputs, 20 log₁₀(Ad/Acm) dB.
Deflection sensitivity
The spot movement on a CRT screen per volt on the deflecting plates, in mm/V.
DSO
Digital storage oscilloscope; it samples and stores waveforms for display and analysis.
Electrical isolation
Separation that lets signal pass but blocks direct current paths between circuits.
Hay bridge
An AC bridge for coils of high Q (above 10), with a series resistor and capacitor in the arm opposite the coil.
Hydraulic control valve
A valve that sets the direction, pressure or flow of oil in a hydraulic circuit.
Isolation amplifier
An amplifier with no conducting path between input and output; rated by a test voltage and a lower continuous working voltage.
Kelvin bridge
A four-terminal bridge that measures very low resistance without lead resistance error.
Linearisation
Correction that makes a non-linear sensor output proportional to the measured quantity.
Maxwell bridge
An AC bridge that compares an inductance with a capacitance in parallel with a resistor; best for Q from 1 to 10.
Notch filter
A band-stop filter with a narrow stop band, used to remove 50 Hz mains hum from an ECG.
Pneumatic cylinder
A tube and piston that produce linear force from compressed air.
Pull-in voltage
The voltage at which electrostatic force overcomes spring force and a micro plate snaps down.
Record length
The number of samples a DSO stores per capture: sample rate times time window.
Relay contact
The moving and fixed metal pieces that open or close a relay circuit.
Resolution
The smallest input change an ADC or display can show, often one least significant bit.
Sampling rate
The number of samples taken per second, measured in samples per second or hertz.
SCADA
Supervisory control and data acquisition; a distributed system for monitoring and controlling plant.
Schering bridge
An AC bridge used to measure capacitance and loss angle of a capacitor.
Servo feedback
A sensor signal that reports actual position or speed so the controller can correct error.
Signal conditioning
The processing of a sensor signal to make it suitable for transmission or digitisation.
Solenoid valve
A valve opened or closed by a solenoid coil.
Step angle
The fixed angle a stepper motor turns for one pulse, equal to 360° divided by steps per revolution.
Surge protection
Components that divert or clamp high-voltage transients before they damage a circuit.
Transducer
A device that changes a physical quantity into an electrical output.
Twisted pair
Two insulated wires twisted together so picked-up noise tends to cancel.
Wheatstone bridge
A four-arm resistance bridge used to find an unknown resistance by null balance.
