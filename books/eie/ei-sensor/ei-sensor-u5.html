5.1 Chemical sensors and biosensors

A chemical sensor turns the concentration of one substance into an electrical signal. A biosensor is a chemical sensor with a biological layer in front of the transducer: the layer recognises one molecule and the transducer measures the change. The idea exists because a bare transducer reacts to many substances, while biology is selective. You meet these devices in a glucose strip, a home test kit ordered on UPI, a water-quality probe and a hospital blood-gas analyser.

You need sensitivity and selectivity from the static characteristics of sensors, and resistance or current change as a signal from resistive transducers. Device details follow in biosensor classes, ISFET, IMFET and glucose sensors.

Analyte, recognition element, transducer

The analyte is the substance being measured: glucose in blood, carbon monoxide in air, nitrate in water. Its amount is reported as a concentration, for example mmol/L or parts per million (ppm).

The recognition element is the part that binds or reacts with one analyte and largely ignores the rest. It can be a chemical layer such as a metal oxide or a solid electrolyte, or a biological molecule such as an enzyme. Some syllabi call it a bioreceptor or a receptor; those names mean the same thing.

The transducer converts the recognition event into a voltage, a current, a resistance or a light level that a circuit can read. The recognition element gives selectivity; the transducer gives the electrical output.

Classification uses two independent axes. By transducer: electrochemical, optical, acoustic, calorimetric. By biological element: enzyme, antibody, whole cell, DNA. A glucose strip, for example, is an enzyme biosensor with an amperometric transducer. Answer both axes when the question is open.

Classifying by the transducer

Electrochemical sensors are the largest family. In an amperometric sensor the electrode is held at a fixed voltage and the current is measured; current rises in proportion to concentration. In a potentiometric sensor ideally no current is drawn (a high-impedance meter reads the voltage) and the voltage follows the logarithm of concentration, as in a pH electrode. A conductometric sensor measures how the conductance of the solution changes as ions are produced.

I = I₀ + S·C

I is the output current in nA; I₀ is the blank offset current in nA (the reading at zero analyte); S is the sensitivity in nA per mmol/L; C is the analyte concentration in mmol/L.

Optical sensors use absorbance (more analyte absorbs more light) or fluorescence (the analyte or a label emits light). Acoustic sensors use a quartz crystal microbalance or a surface acoustic wave device: mass landing on the surface shifts the resonant frequency. Details are in solid electrolyte and SAW sensors. Calorimetric sensors read the heat released by the reaction with a thermistor; the temperature rise depends on how much analyte reacted.

Classifying by the biological element

An enzyme sensor uses one enzyme that catalyses one reaction. Glucose oxidase oxidises glucose and produces hydrogen peroxide; the peroxide is oxidised at an electrode and gives a current proportional to glucose. The enzyme supplies the selectivity, so other sugars in blood barely respond.

An immunosensor uses an antibody that binds one antigen, the molecule it was raised against. Binding changes mass, light or charge at the surface, and that change is transduced. Pregnancy strips and pathogen tests work this way. The binding is strong and specific but slow compared with an enzyme reaction.

A microbial or whole-cell sensor uses living cells as the recognition element. The cells are cheap and tolerate harsh samples but respond slowly. A DNA sensor uses a short probe strand that hybridises with the target strand; the hybrid is read optically or electrochemically.

Advantages: high selectivity, fast response for enzyme devices, and a low sample volume — one drop of blood is enough. Limitations: short shelf life because enzymes denature, drift with time, sensitivity to temperature, fouling of the electrode surface, and the need for regular calibration. A smart sensor package can hold the calibration curve and correct drift, as in smart sensors.

In medicine each class has a bedside use. Enzyme electrodes measure glucose and lactate in blood; potentiometric ion-selective electrodes measure Na⁺, K⁺ and pH in a blood-gas analyser; antibody (immuno) sensors in lateral-flow strips detect pregnancy hormone, cardiac troponin after a heart attack and viral antigens; and DNA sensors detect a pathogen's genes. The choice of element sets what is detected; the choice of transducer sets how the signal is read.

Worked example

Question. An amperometric glucose biosensor has a blank offset current of 5 nA and a sensitivity of 20 nA per mmol/L. A blood sample reads 4.5 mmol/L glucose. Find the output current.

  1. Write the linear model: I = I₀ + S·C.
  2. Identify the blank offset: I₀ = 5 nA. This is the reading with zero glucose, not an error.
  3. Identify the sensitivity: S = 20 nA/(mmol/L).
  4. Identify the concentration: C = 4.5 mmol/L.
  5. Multiply: S·C = 20 × 4.5 = 90 nA. The units cancel as (nA/(mmol/L)) × mmol/L = nA.
  6. Add the offset: I = 5 nA + 90 nA = 95 nA.

Output

95.0

Answer. The output current is 95 nA.

Worked example 2

Question. A glucose biosensor is calibrated with two standards. A 2.0 mmol/L standard gives 45 nA and a 10.0 mmol/L standard gives 165 nA. (a) Find the sensitivity S and the blank current I₀. (b) A fasting patient's sample then gives 120 nA. Find the glucose concentration in mmol/L and in mg/dL (1 mmol/L of glucose = 18.0 mg/dL), and say whether it lies in the normal fasting range of 3.9 to 5.5 mmol/L.

  1. The sensor is linear: I = I₀ + S·C. Two standards give two equations, enough for the two unknowns S and I₀.
  2. Slope: S = (165 − 45) nA / (10.0 − 2.0) mmol/L = 120 / 8.0 = 15 nA per mmol/L.
  3. Blank: I₀ = 45 − 15 × 2.0 = 45 − 30 = 15 nA. Check with the other standard: 15 + 15 × 10.0 = 165 nA, as measured.
  4. Invert the model for the sample: C = (I − I₀)/S = (120 − 15)/15 = 105/15 = 7.0 mmol/L.
  5. Convert: 7.0 × 18.0 = 126 mg/dL.
  6. Compare: 7.0 mmol/L is above the normal fasting range of 3.9 to 5.5 mmol/L; a fasting value of 7.0 mmol/L or more on repeat tests is the usual threshold for diabetes. The meter only reports; the diagnosis is the doctor's.

Answer. S = 15 nA per mmol/L and I₀ = 15 nA. The sample is 7.0 mmol/L, or 126 mg/dL, which is above the normal fasting range.

Exam traps

  • Calling every chemical sensor a biosensor. A biosensor must have a biological recognition element; a metal-oxide gas sensor does not.
  • Answering with the transducer when the question asks for the biological element, or the reverse. Give both axes when the wording is open.
  • Swapping amperometric and potentiometric. Amperometric: fixed voltage, measure current. Potentiometric: zero current, measure voltage.
  • Dropping the blank offset I₀. For 4.5 mmol/L at 20 nA/(mmol/L) the current is 95 nA, not 90 nA.
  • Saying an acoustic sensor measures mass directly. It measures a frequency shift, which is converted to mass using the calibration.
  • Calibrating with one standard only. One point fixes neither the slope nor the blank; a meter needs two standards, or a known zero and one standard, before a sample reading means anything.

5.2 Gas sensors: oxygen, hydrogen and gas-sensitive resistors

Gas sensors turn the amount of a gas into a resistance, a current or a voltage. Three families cover most industrial needs. Oxygen is measured by the Nernst voltage of a hot zirconia cell, by the magnetic pull on oxygen molecules, or by a limiting current. Hydrogen is measured by palladium films and gates, by catalytic beads, or by thermal conductivity. Gas-sensitive resistors use a heated tin dioxide film whose resistance falls when a reducing gas arrives. They sit in kitchen LPG alarms, car exhausts, boilers and steel plants.

You need sensitivity and transfer functions from the static characteristics of sensors, and the Wheatstone bridge from resistive transducers. The full zirconia theory is in solid electrolyte and SAW sensors.

Oxygen sensors

A zirconia cell is a tube or disc of ZrO₂ stabilised with yttria. It conducts O²⁻ ions usefully only above about 350 °C, so practical cells are heated to about 600 to 800 °C. Two porous platinum electrodes coat it: one sees reference air at 20.9 % oxygen, the other sees the sample gas. The cell gives a Nernst EMF:

E = (RT/4F) · ln(p₁/p₂)

E is the cell EMF in volts; R = 8.314 J/(mol·K); T is temperature in kelvin; F = 96 485 C/mol; p₁ is the oxygen partial pressure at the reference electrode and p₂ at the sample electrode; the 4 is the number of electrons in O₂ + 4e⁻ → 2O²⁻.

The output is logarithmic, so a small voltage covers a wide range. At 973 K (700 °C), a pressure ratio of 10 gives only about 48 mV. The reference side is positive when the sample is oxygen-poor. Car engine management, boiler flue gas and steel making use this cell. Full solid-electrolyte theory is in solid electrolyte and SAW sensors.

Paramagnetic oxygen sensors use the fact that O₂ has two unpaired electrons and is strongly paramagnetic; most other gases are weakly diamagnetic. A small glass dumb-bell or a heated wire sits in a magnetic field. Oxygen pulled into the field deflects the dumb-bell, or changes the pressure, and the deflection is read optically or capacitively. This gives a linear reading of percent-level oxygen and is common in flue-gas analysers.

An electrochemical limiting-current cell uses a zirconia disc with a tiny diffusion hole. A voltage is applied; all oxygen reaching the cathode is pumped, and the current saturates at a value set by diffusion. That saturation current is proportional to oxygen concentration, not logarithmic, which suits wide-range and automotive use.

In medicine, oxygen is measured on both sides of the patient. A fast paramagnetic analyser in an anaesthesia machine or ventilator reads the inspired and expired oxygen breath by breath. In blood, the Clark electrode measures the oxygen partial pressure pO₂: a platinum cathode held at about −0.6 to −0.7 V against an Ag/AgCl anode sits behind a thin oxygen-permeable membrane, every oxygen molecule that diffuses through is reduced (O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻), and the current is proportional to pO₂. It is the oxygen channel of every blood-gas analyser and the same electrode that sits under the first glucose biosensors.

Hydrogen sensors

Palladium absorbs hydrogen readily. A thin Pd film changes resistance when H₂ is absorbed; a Pd gate on a MOSFET changes the work function at the Pd/SiO₂ interface and shifts the flat-band voltage; a Pd/n-Si Schottky diode shows a drop in barrier height. These devices work near 100 °C or lower and can detect ppm levels, but they are slow to recover and are poisoned by sulphur compounds.

A catalytic pellistor has two alumina beads, each with a platinum wire coil. One bead carries a catalyst (platinum or palladium), the other is inert. Both are heated to about 500 °C. A combustible gas burns on the active bead and releases heat; the bead temperature rises, the Pt coil resistance rises, and a Wheatstone bridge unbalances. The bridge output is proportional to the heat of combustion, so it reads in % LEL. Oxygen must be present; silicones and lead poison the catalyst.

A thermal conductivity cell uses a heated wire in a small chamber. Hydrogen conducts heat about seven times better than air, so the wire loses heat faster and its resistance drops. This is cheap and robust for high hydrogen concentrations, but it is cross-sensitive to helium and carbon dioxide.

Gas-sensitive resistors and junction devices

A gas-sensitive resistor, also called a chemiresistor, is a sintered bead or thick film of tin dioxide (SnO₂), often doped with a little palladium or platinum, on a ceramic tube. A heater coil inside holds it at 300–400 °C. In clean air, oxygen adsorbs on the surface as O⁻ and O²⁻, trapping conduction electrons, so the film has a high resistance. A reducing gas such as CO, H₂, LPG, methane or ethanol reacts with the adsorbed oxygen and returns electrons to the film, so the resistance falls.

S = Rair / Rgas

S is the sensitivity (a ratio, no units); Rair is the film resistance in clean air in ohms; Rgas is the resistance in the test gas in ohms.

The heater is essential: at room temperature the surface reaction is too slow, and each gas has a temperature at which the response peaks. Selectivity is improved by dopants, by a filter layer, and by choosing the operating temperature. These sensors are cheap and sensitive but not selective; a kitchen alarm responds to cooking alcohol as well as LPG. Trade names include Taguchi. Sulphur gases such as H₂S are also sensed by SnO₂ films, using the same resistance-fall mechanism.

Semiconductor junction devices use a Pd/n-Si Schottky diode or a Pd-gate MOS capacitor. Hydrogen at the interface changes the barrier height or the flat-band voltage, which is read as a voltage shift. They are compatible with silicon processing and are used in low-power hydrogen leak detectors.

Worked example

Question. A heated zirconia oxygen sensor sits in a boiler flue at 700 °C. Reference air is 20.9 % O₂ and the flue gas is 2.0 % O₂. Find the cell EMF and state which electrode is positive.

  1. Convert the temperature: T = 700 + 273 = 973 K. The Nernst equation always needs kelvin.
  2. Write the Nernst equation: E = (RT/4F) ln(p₁/p₂), with p₁/p₂ = 20.9/2.0 = 10.45.
  3. Compute RT/4F = (8.314 J/(mol·K) × 973 K) / (4 × 96 485 C/mol) = 8089.5 / 385 940 = 0.02096 V = 20.96 mV.
  4. Compute ln(10.45) = 2.347.
  5. Multiply: E = 20.96 mV × 2.347 = 49.2 mV.
  6. The reference air side has more oxygen, so the reference electrode is positive.

Answer. E = 49.2 mV, with the reference air electrode positive.

Exam traps

  • Using log₁₀ instead of ln in the Nernst equation. For a ratio of 10.45 at 973 K, log₁₀ gives 1.019 and an EMF of 21.4 mV instead of 49.2 mV.
  • Putting the temperature in degrees Celsius. At 700 °C, T = 973 K; using 700 in the formula gives 35.4 mV instead of 49.2 mV.
  • Dropping the 4 in 4F for the four-electron oxygen reaction. That makes the EMF four times too large.
  • Reversing the sign: the electrode with more oxygen is positive, so the reference air electrode is positive when the exhaust is oxygen-poor.
  • Thinking a chemiresistor's resistance rises for a reducing gas. It falls, because the gas returns trapped electrons to the SnO₂ film.
  • Confusing paramagnetic oxygen sensing with thermal-conductivity hydrogen sensing. Both are physical methods, but they exploit different properties and different gases.

The Nernst cell gives an open-circuit EMF proportional to ln(p₁/p₂), which is logarithmic and needs a reference gas. The limiting-current cell applies a voltage and measures a saturation current set by diffusion through a small hole; that current is linear in oxygen concentration.

5.3 Humidity sensors

Humidity sensors measure how much water vapour is in the air. The useful quantity is usually relative humidity at a stated temperature, because the same air shows a different RH after a small temperature change. A sensor turns water uptake or dew formation into a capacitance, a resistance or an optical signal. You meet these devices in air conditioners, grain stores, incubators, ventilators and data centres.

You need the parallel-plate capacitance from capacitive transducers (C = εA/d, where C is capacitance, ε is permittivity, A is electrode area and d is spacing), thermistors from resistive transducers, and the general sensor chain in chemical sensors and biosensors.

Three ways to describe water vapour

Absolute humidity is the mass of water vapour in one cubic metre of air, in grams per cubic metre (g/m³). It stays the same on heating or cooling only if the volume is fixed, as in a sealed box. Open air expands when heated, so its g/m³ falls a little; the mass of water per kilogram of dry air (the mixing ratio) and the dew point stay fixed.

Relative humidity (RH) is the actual water vapour pressure divided by the saturation vapour pressure at the same temperature, times 100:

RH = 100 × pv / psat(T)

pv is the actual partial pressure of water vapour in pascals (Pa); psat(T) is the saturation vapour pressure at temperature T in kelvin (K); RH is in percent. Warm air holds more vapour, so psat(T) rises with T; the same pv gives a lower RH after heating.

Dew point is the temperature at which psat equals pv. At dew point, RH is 100 %. A surface colder than the dew point collects condensation. Dew point is an absolute measure: warming the air does not change it unless water is added or removed.

These three numbers answer different questions. Absolute humidity gives the amount of water; RH gives how close the air is to condensation at the current temperature; dew point gives the temperature at which condensation starts. A humidity sensor usually reports RH, so it must also state the temperature of measurement.

In hospitals humidity is a treatment variable, not only a comfort figure. A neonatal incubator holds a set RH so that a premature baby does not lose water through its thin skin. A ventilator's heated humidifier delivers gas near 100 % RH at about 37 °C, which is about 44 mg of water per litre, because dry gas damages the airway. Operating theatres keep RH within a set band, typically about 40 to 60 %, to limit static sparks and microbial growth. Each of these loops uses a capacitive sensor read together with a temperature sensor.

Capacitive and resistive sensors

A capacitive humidity sensor uses a thin hygroscopic polymer film, such as polyimide, between two electrodes. Dry polymer has a dielectric constant near 3. Water has a dielectric constant near 80. As the polymer absorbs water vapour, its effective dielectric constant rises, so capacitance rises:

C = εr ε0 A / d

C is capacitance in farads (F); εr is the relative permittivity (dielectric constant); ε0 = 8.854 × 10−12 F/m; A is electrode area in square metres; d is film thickness in metres. The film is thin, so C is small; cable and stray capacitance can matter. A guard ring or a reference capacitor helps.

A resistive humidity sensor uses a porous ceramic such as Al2O3 or TiO2. Water adsorbed on the pore walls forms ions, so surface conduction increases and resistance falls, often by orders of magnitude from dry to wet. The curve is less linear than the capacitive type. The ceramic may need periodic heating to clean the surface. The same surface-adsorption idea drives gas-sensitive resistors.

Both types use AC excitation, typically about 1 kHz. DC would polarise the polymer and electrolyse the water film, causing drift and damage. The AC signal must be small. Datasheets quote response time to a step, hysteresis between rising and falling RH, and temperature coefficient in %RH per °C. A thermal conductivity sensor uses two thermistors: one sealed dry and one exposed. Water vapour changes the thermal conductivity and heat capacity of air, so the exposed thermistor cools differently; the resistance difference gives humidity. It is less selective but works in hot, dirty air.

Calibration and the reference

Saturated salt solutions give a known RH in a sealed jar at a fixed temperature. Common points are LiCl at about 11 % RH, MgCl2 at about 33 % RH and NaCl at about 75 % RH. Place the sensor in the headspace above the slurry, wait for equilibrium, and record the reading. Two or three points give offset and slope. A capacitive sensor is close to straight between 20 % and 80 % RH; do not assume it stays straight near 95 % RH, where saturation bends the curve.

A chilled mirror hygrometer is an accurate reference. It cools a small mirror until dew forms. A light source and photodetector watch the reflected light; dew scatters it. A control loop holds the mirror at the dew-point temperature, read by a thermometer. The method is direct and accurate but slow, costly and sensitive to dirt on the mirror.

A practical reading is complete only with its temperature, calibration history and uncertainty. Quote response time, hysteresis and temperature coefficient in the specification. In a grain store or a server room, a slow sensor can miss a fast change, and a sensor near a cold wall can read a different RH from the room air. Mount it where air moves and where condensation cannot form on the active surface.

Worked example

Question. A capacitive humidity sensor is linear. It reads 100 pF at 20 % RH and 160 pF at 80 % RH. A room reads 145 pF. Find the RH. Also state the sensitivity and the zero-RH intercept.

  1. Write the straight-line relation C = m·RH + b, where C is capacitance in pF, RH is in percent, m is the slope in pF per %RH, and b is the intercept in pF.
  2. Slope m = (C2 − C1)/(RH2 − RH1) = (160 − 100) pF / (80 − 20) % = 60/60 = 1 pF per %RH.
  3. Intercept b = C1 − m·RH1 = 100 − 1 × 20 = 80 pF. This is the extrapolated value at 0 % RH, not a measured point.
  4. For C = 145 pF: RH = (C − b)/m = (145 − 80)/1 = 65 %.
  5. The sensitivity is 1 pF per %RH, the intercept is 80 pF, and the room RH is 65 %.

Answer. The room is at 65 % RH, with sensitivity 1 pF/%RH and zero-RH intercept 80 pF.

Worked example 2

Question. Room air at 25 °C has a water vapour pressure of 1.90 kPa. The saturation vapour pressure is 3.169 kPa at 25 °C and 5.627 kPa at 35 °C; the Magnus formula psat = 0.6109 exp(17.625 T / (T + 243.04)) kPa, with T in °C, gives it at other temperatures. (a) Find the RH. (b) The air is warmed to 35 °C in an incubator with no water added. Find the new RH. (c) Find the dew point.

  1. (a) RH = 100 × pv / psat(25 °C) = 100 × 1.90 / 3.169 = 60.0 %.
  2. (b) Warming adds no water, so pv stays 1.90 kPa while psat rises: RH = 100 × 1.90 / 5.627 = 33.8 %.
  3. (c) At the dew point psat(T) = pv. Invert the Magnus formula: ln(1.90 / 0.6109) = 1.1346, so T = 243.04 × 1.1346 / (17.625 − 1.1346) = 275.75 / 16.490 = 16.7 °C.
  4. Check: the dew point is the same 16.7 °C in (a) and (b), because the amount of vapour did not change. A window colder than 16.7 °C would collect dew.

Answer. The RH is 60.0 % at 25 °C and 33.8 % after warming to 35 °C; the dew point stays at about 16.7 °C.

Exam traps

  • Using a DC ohmmeter on a resistive humidity sensor. DC electrolyses the water film, polarises the ceramic and makes the resistance drift; use the specified AC excitation.
  • Reporting RH without the temperature. Heating the same air lowers RH even though the amount of water and the dew point are unchanged.
  • Extrapolating the straight 20–80 % calibration line to 95 % RH. Polymer sensors bend near saturation, and the error can be several %RH.
  • Ignoring cable capacitance on a capacitive sensor. A few tens of pF of cable can look like a large RH error; keep leads short and use the guard or reference.

5.4 Semiconductor sensors and sensor materials

A semiconductor sensor is a device where the material itself senses. Light, heat, gas, strain or a magnetic field changes the free-carrier density or the width of an internal barrier, and the change appears as a current or voltage. The material choice fixes sensitivity, temperature range and selectivity: silicon for piezoresistors, Hall plates and photodiodes; metal oxides for gases; ceramics and wide-bandgap crystals where silicon would fail.

You need the piezoresistive effect and semiconductor strain gauges from resistive transducers, the Hall effect and photodiodes from the magnetic and photoelectric transducers, and the p-n junction from basic electronics.

Why a semiconductor is a good transducer

A metal has about 10²⁹ free electrons per m³, fixed. Undoped silicon has about 10¹⁶ per m³ at room temperature; doping raises this to about 10²¹ to 10²³ m⁻³, still far below a metal. Light, heat or an adsorbed molecule can then change the number by a large fraction. That large relative change is why semiconductor sensors exist: the readout circuit can be simple and cheap.

Conductivity is σ = q(nμn + pμp), where σ is in S/m, q = 1.6 × 10−19 C, n and p are electron and hole densities per m³, and μn, μp are mobilities in m²/(V·s). A sensor disturbs one of these four numbers. Light raises n and p, as in a photoconductor. Strain changes μ by deforming the band structure (it shifts the energy valleys the carriers sit in), which is the piezoresistive effect. A gas changes surface charge and so the depletion width.

The second readout is a barrier: a p-n diode, Schottky diode or MOS capacitor. Its depletion width and built-in voltage Vbi depend on doping, temperature and surface charge, so junction current or capacitance carries the measurement. Solid-electrolyte and surface-acoustic-wave devices are in solid electrolyte and SAW sensors.

In doped silicon at room temperature the carrier density is fixed by the doping, so resistance drifts only about +0.1 to +0.3 %/K, mainly because mobility falls as T rises. The intrinsic carrier density doubles roughly every 10 K, but it overtakes the doping only at high temperature. A piezoresistor bridge therefore needs a constant-current supply or a thermistor correction in the same package.

The material menu

Silicon is the default: cheap, single-crystal, and micromachined into diaphragms and beams (MEMS micro sensors). Its piezoresistive gauge factor is about 100–200, against about 2 for a metal gauge, because strain changes mobility as well as geometry. Photodiodes respond from 400 nm to 1100 nm, peaking near 850 nm. Plain silicon fails above about 150 °C because junction leakage grows exponentially and the piezoresistor resistance drifts too much for a stable reading; at much higher temperatures the intrinsic carrier density also becomes comparable to the doping.

Silicon carbide (SiC, gap 3.2 eV) and gallium nitride (GaN, gap 3.4 eV) keep both effects small to several hundred degrees Celsius. SiC pressure and gas sensors are quoted for 300–500 °C: engine exhaust, turbine inlets, downhole tools. They cost many times more per wafer, so they appear only where silicon cannot survive.

Metal oxides are the gas and humidity family: SnO₂, ZnO, TiO₂, ZrO₂, Ga₂O₃. Adsorbed oxygen ions take electrons from the surface. An oxidising gas such as oxygen or NO₂ pulls more electrons out and widens the depletion layer, so resistance rises. A reducing gas such as CO or ethanol reacts with the ions, gives the electrons back, and resistance falls. The reaction needs heat, so these sensors run at 200–500 °C from a small heater and draw tens to hundreds of milliwatts. ZrO₂ is the exception: a solid electrolyte oxygen sensor working by Nernst EMF (solid electrolyte and SAW sensors).

GaAs and InP give fast, low-noise and infrared devices: GaAs Hall plates, InGaAs photodiodes for 1300–1550 nm. Conducting polymers such as polypyrrole and polyaniline (PANI) work at room temperature — ammonia and humidity change their doping — but they age and drift. Piezoelectric materials: the ceramic lead zirconate titanate (PZT) and single-crystal quartz convert force to charge. PZT is limited by its Curie point, typically 200–350 °C; quartz is limited by its α–β transition near 573 °C and, in practice, to a few hundred degrees Celsius. Both feed ultrasonic and acoustic sensors and surface acoustic wave devices (solid electrolyte and SAW sensors).

Scaling: why the sensor got small

A surface reaction happens at the surface, but the signal comes from the volume. The figure of merit is surface-to-volume ratio, 6/a for a cube of side a. Going from 1 mm to 1 µm raises it a thousandfold, so a gas sensor becomes faster and more sensitive because most atoms can meet the gas.

One 150 mm wafer holds thousands of dies, so cost per die falls with die area. A mature MEMS pressure sensor die costs a few tens of rupees; packaging is most of the price.

Small is not free. Signal power falls with sensing volume, so 1/f noise and amplifier offset grow relative to the signal. Drift over months — doping change, contamination, package stress — becomes larger than the effect measured. Below a few micrometres, package, drift and noise set accuracy, not the material. Shrink until drift or packaging dominates, then stop. Biosensor versions are in biosensor classes, ISFET, IMFET and glucose sensors.

5.5 MEMS and micro sensors for force, pressure and flow

A micro sensor is built on a silicon wafer by integrated-circuit steps. Micromachining etches a thin diaphragm, beam or channel a few micrometres thick. Pressure, force or flow bends it, and piezoresistors, a capacitor or a heater turns the bending into a voltage. The sensor fits in a few square millimetres. Phone barometers, tyre pressure monitors, blood-pressure cuffs and ventilator flow tubes use these.

You need strain gauges, the gauge factor and load cells from resistive transducers; capacitive transducers and bridge circuits; and the seismic accelerometer.

Piezoresistive pressure sensor

Start with a silicon wafer and etch a cavity into the back until a thin square or circular diaphragm is left, typically 5 to 25 µm thick. The thick rim clamps the diaphragm, so the largest stress sits at the clamped edge, not the centre. A sealed vacuum underneath gives an absolute sensor; an open hole gives a gauge sensor reading the difference of two pressures.

Four resistors are ion-implanted into the surface at the edge. Two run across the edge, along the main stress, and two run parallel to it. In p-type silicon the piezoresistive coefficient along the stress and the one across it have opposite signs, so the first pair rises in resistance and the second pair falls by about the same amount. Strain ε is the fractional change in length (m/m). Resistance change follows gauge factor G: a silicon piezoresistor has G between about 100 and 200, while a metal foil gauge has G near 2. The same strain therefore gives 50 to 100 times more signal.

ΔR/R = G · ε

ΔR is the change in resistance (Ω), R is the unstrained resistance (Ω), G is the gauge factor (dimensionless) and ε is the strain (m/m).

The four resistors form a full Wheatstone bridge fed from supply Vs. Opposite arms shift oppositely, so the four-active bridge gives four times the output of a single active gauge and cancels common temperature drift. A typical part is quoted in millivolts per volt supply per bar, for example 30 mV at 5 V and 1 bar, which is 6 mV/V/bar; commercial parts run from about 5 to 20 mV/V/bar at full-scale pressure. The disposable blood-pressure transducer on a patient's arterial line is this sensor, built to a standard sensitivity of 5 µV per volt per mmHg: at 5 V excitation a pressure of 120 mmHg gives 5 × 10⁻⁶ × 5 × 120 = 3.0 mV. Catheter-tip and implanted versions read pressure inside the heart or skull. An amplifier and ADC follow the bridge, as 30 mV is too small to digitise directly.

Keep deflection below about one fifth of the diaphragm thickness. Beyond that the diaphragm stiffens, the output bends away from a straight line, and the part is sold with a linearity error stated as a percentage of full scale.

Capacitive pressure sensor

Here the diaphragm is one plate of a capacitor, and a fixed plate sits a small gap behind it, sealed in vacuum. The gap is 1 to 2 µm and the plates overlap over an area A of about a square millimetre.

C = εA/d

C is the capacitance (F), ε is the permittivity of the gas in the gap (F/m, about 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m for air or vacuum), A is the overlap area (m²) and d is the gap (m).

Differentiating gives ΔC/C ≈ −Δd/d for small movement: a 1 % squeeze of the gap gives a 1 % rise in capacitance. The 1/d law is nonlinear, so the readout must linearise it, either by forcing the diaphragm back with electrostatic feedback or by letting it touch the backplate over part of the range, flattening the curve.

Reading uses an AC bridge, a charge amplifier, or most often a switched-capacitor circuit. Switches charge the sensor capacitor and a fixed reference capacitor, then dump both charges into an integrator. The output is proportional to Csensor/Cref, so it does not drift with supply or temperature. The sensing node is held at a virtual ground, so stray capacitance to ground carries no signal current, and a guard ring at the same potential blocks surface leakage.

Compared with the piezoresistive type, the capacitive sensor draws almost no DC current, is more linear after feedback, and resolves a few pascal, suiting barometers, altimeters and microphones. Its weaknesses are stray capacitance, electromagnetic interference and package stress, needing shielding and careful mounting.

Force, tactile and flow micro sensors

Put a polymer bump on a thin membrane or cantilever, and the structure measures contact force. An array of such cells under a rubber pad gives a map of force over the contact area, a tactile image. Normal force is read from capacitance between the plates; a pair of cells, one on each side of a bump, gives the shear direction as the bump shifts sideways. Robot grippers, touch panels and Braille displays use this.

For flow, the common micro sensor is a thermal anemometer. A heater sits on a thin membrane, with a thermopile upstream and another downstream. A thermopile is many thermocouples in series, giving a few millivolts without excitation. With no flow, heat spreads equally and both thermopiles see the same temperature. With flow, heat is swept downstream, the downstream thermopile gets hotter, and the difference voltage rises with mass flow but not linearly: near linear at very low flow, flattening at higher flow, so the part uses a calibration look-up table; the sign gives direction. Driving the heater at constant temperature above ambient widens the range.

A second type is the differential-pressure micro flow sensor. A micromachined orifice or narrow channel is etched in the wafer, and the pressure drop across it is read by a diaphragm sensor like the one above. For smooth laminar flow the drop is linear, ΔP = Rh·Q, so Q = ΔP/Rh, where Q is volume flow (m³/s) and Rh is hydraulic resistance (Pa·s/m³). Rh depends on viscosity, so the part is calibrated for one gas or liquid. Gas meters and drug-delivery pumps use it because it has no moving parts.

Coriolis and ultrasonic micro flow sensors exist; their working is only named here. Thermal micro flow sensors sit in ventilators and spirometers to measure breath flow, and in infusion pumps to watch drug flow. Position and speed micro sensors use Hall elements and optical encoder gratings on the same wafer. Acceleration and temperature micro sensors come from the same diaphragm and beam process.

5.6 Micromachining, deposition and micro-fabrication methods

Micro sensors are built on a silicon wafer by adding thin layers and removing them in patterns. Lithography draws the pattern, etching cuts it, and deposition fills or coats it. Bulk micromachining uses a wet etch that stops on crystal planes; surface micromachining uses a sacrificial layer that is dissolved to free a structure. The process choice fixes the shape, the material and the cost. Pressure sensors, accelerometers and gas sensors in phones and cars are made this way, and every added mask adds rupees to the die.

You need silicon and oxide materials from semiconductor sensors and sensor materials, and the diaphragms and beams from MEMS micro sensors.

The pattern loop: lithography, etch and deposition

A micro-fabrication recipe is a loop. A wafer is cleaned and coated with a resist, a polymer that changes solubility when exposed to light. A mask carries the pattern. Exposure through the mask, then development, leaves resist only where the pattern needs it. The resist protects the layer below during etch, or lifts off after deposition.

Etch removes material; deposition adds it. After the loop, the resist is stripped. Each loop needs one mask, and each mask must be aligned to the previous pattern. Alignment error is a real limit, often a fraction of a micrometre. A simple sensor may use two or three masks; a complex one may use ten. Cost rises with mask count because every loop adds exposure, etch, cleaning and yield loss.

  1. Clean the wafer and grow or deposit the first layer.
  2. Spin-coat resist and bake it.
  3. Align the mask, expose, and develop the resist.
  4. Etch the open areas or deposit through them.
  5. Strip the resist and inspect the pattern.
  6. Repeat for the next layer.

d = r t

d is etch depth in micrometres (µm); r is etch rate in µm/min; t is etch time in min. For a wet etch, r depends on crystal plane, temperature and concentration.

Bulk and surface micromachining

Bulk micromachining removes silicon from the wafer body. KOH (potassium hydroxide) or TMAH (tetramethylammonium hydroxide) in water etches (100) silicon much faster than (111) silicon. The (111) planes therefore stop the etch and leave sidewalls at 54.7° to the surface. A pit is wider at the top than at the bottom, so the mask opening must be larger than the final diaphragm. An etch-stop layer can stop the depth: heavily boron-doped silicon etches very slowly, and a silicon-on-insulator (SOI) wafer has a buried oxide that stops the etch cleanly.

Deep reactive ion etching (DRIE) is a dry process that alternates etching and polymer passivation. It gives near-vertical walls and high aspect ratio, meaning depth divided by width can be 10 or more. DRIE does not rely on crystal planes, so it can cut arbitrary shapes, but it is slower and costlier per wafer than a KOH bath.

Surface micromachining, also called the sacrificial-layer process, builds on top of the wafer. A structural layer such as polysilicon is deposited over a sacrificial oxide, patterned, and then the oxide is dissolved in hydrofluoric acid (HF). The structure is released and may be free to move. Stiction is the main failure: capillary forces during drying pull the freed beam down to the substrate and it sticks. Critical-point drying or vapour HF release reduces stiction.

LIGA (X-ray lithography and electroplating) makes tall metal parts with vertical walls. Electroplating alone can make thick nickel or copper structures for coils and moulds. As parts shrink, surface forces and thin-film stress grow in importance compared with weight, so release and drying need more care.

Deposition, bonding and cost

Thermal oxidation grows SiO2 by consuming silicon at 900-1100 °C. It gives a dense, stable oxide for masks, insulation and sacrificial layers. LPCVD (low-pressure chemical vapour deposition) deposits polysilicon, silicon nitride and oxide at around 600-800 °C with good uniformity. PECVD (plasma-enhanced CVD) runs cooler, often 200-400 °C, but the film may contain hydrogen. Sputtering throws atoms from a target by plasma ions and is used for metals and piezoresistive films. Evaporation heats a source in vacuum; it is simple but throws atoms in straight lines, so step coverage is poor. Spin coating spreads a liquid resist or polymer into a thin film. Electroplating uses current to grow metal from a solution onto a seed layer.

5.7 Smart sensors

A smart sensor is a complete measuring chain on one chip or module. The sensing element, an amplifier and filter, an analog-to-digital converter, a small processor and a digital bus sit together. Memory inside holds the calibration coefficients. The output is already in engineering units, addressed, and often checked against an internal reference. You meet these in a phone's pressure and humidity sensors, in a car's manifold pressure sensor, and in a factory transmitter that reports over a bus. The point is to move calibration and correction close to the signal, before noise and cable loss spoil it.

You need the signal-conditioning chain (amplifiers, filters, data acquisition) and the micro sensor being made smart in MEMS micro sensors.

The chain inside the package

The sensing element converts the measurand into a small voltage, current, resistance or capacitance. The amplifier raises this signal above the noise of the next stage, and the filter removes interference outside the band of interest. The ADC turns the conditioned analog voltage into a number. A 12-bit ADC with a 3.3 V reference has 2¹² = 4096 codes, so one code is 3.3 V / 4096 = 0.806 mV. That is resolution at the ADC pin, not the accuracy of the sensor.

A microcontroller (µC) or digital signal processor (DSP) reads the ADC count. Memory beside it holds the calibration coefficients: zero offset, span gain, linearity curve and temperature coefficients. The processor applies these, attaches the unit and sends the result on a digital bus. The memory is what makes the chain smart. Without it the processor has no record of what the element's 10 mV/kPa means, or how that sensitivity falls when the silicon warms.

The order matters. Correction after the ADC cannot recover a signal that clipped the amplifier or sat in the ADC's noise. The analog front end must keep the signal inside the converter's range over the whole operating temperature and supply range. The processor then works on clean numbers.

What the processor does

The processor runs a set of functions. Linearisation and correction run on every sample; self-calibration and self-diagnosis run at power-up or at set intervals. Some syllabi call this the smart-sensor function list.

  • Linearisation maps the raw count to the measurand with a polynomial or lookup table. The table is written at factory calibration.
  • Offset and gain correction subtract the zero count and scale the span. A pressure sensor that reads 12 counts at 0 kPa has that 12 subtracted.
  • Temperature compensation uses an on-chip temperature sensor. Piezoresistive sensitivity falls with temperature, so the processor multiplies by a correction factor.
  • Autoranging changes the amplifier gain when the signal is small or near full scale, keeping the ADC on its useful bits.
  • Self-calibration switches an internal reference voltage or precision resistor to the input and checks the reading. It corrects gain drift, not a cracked diaphragm.
  • Self-diagnosis watches for open circuit, short circuit, overrange and a stuck output, and sets a fault flag in the status byte.
  • The output is a number with a unit, for example 101.3 kPa, not a bare ADC count.

Each function uses memory and code, so the same silicon element can be sold as a raw micro sensor or as a calibrated smart sensor. The calibrated version costs more because of test time, not because the diaphragm is different.

Buses, standards and the accuracy budget

IEEE 1451 defines a Transducer Electronic Data Sheet (TEDS). TEDS is a small memory block that gives the sensor's identity, range, calibration date and coefficients, so a network can read a new sensor and configure itself. The physical link can be I²C or SPI inside a board, UART for a short cable, CAN in a vehicle, or the 4–20 mA current loop with HART digital data superimposed on the same two wires. The 4–20 mA loop carries the measurement as a current; HART adds a low-level digital signal on top without disturbing the current.

A smart sensor carries its own processing and digital interface at chip or module level. An intelligent instrument is a complete panel meter or analyser that may use a smart sensor inside but adds display, keypad and enclosure. The distinction is packaging and scope, not intelligence in the everyday sense. Medicine is full of smart sensors: a digital thermometer probe, a finger pulse-oximeter module that sends SpO₂ in percent, a continuous glucose monitor patch that sends mmol/L to a phone, and the pressure module of a ventilator or infusion pump that flags an occlusion.

Do not quote the 12 bits as accuracy. Bits fix resolution; offset, gain error, nonlinearity, hysteresis and drift fix accuracy until calibration is applied. A 12-bit output can still be 2 % wrong if the ADC reference is wrong or the amplifier offset drifts. The resolution and accuracy budget must list each error source separately: sensor, amplifier, reference, ADC and calibration uncertainty.

Worked example

Question. A piezoresistive pressure sensor has a sensitivity of 10 mV/kPa. Its amplifier has a voltage gain of 100 and drives a 12-bit ADC with a 3.3 V reference. Find (a) the ADC step size in mV, (b) the input-referred step in µV, and (c) the pressure change represented by one least significant bit in Pa.

  1. The ADC full-scale span is 3.3 V = 3300 mV.
  2. A 12-bit ADC has 2¹² = 4096 codes. One step is Q = 3300 mV / 4096 = 0.805664 mV ≈ 0.806 mV.
  3. Referred to the amplifier input, divide by the gain 100: 0.805664 mV / 100 = 0.00805664 mV = 8.05664 µV ≈ 8.06 µV.
  4. The sensor sensitivity is 10 mV/kPa. Since 1 kPa = 1000 Pa, this is 10 mV / 1000 Pa = 0.01 mV/Pa = 10 µV/Pa.
  5. Pressure per LSB = 8.05664 µV / (10 µV/Pa) = 0.805664 Pa ≈ 0.806 Pa.

Answer. One LSB represents 0.806 Pa, which is 8.06 µV at the sensor input and 0.806 mV at the ADC.

Worked example 2

Question. A smart blood-pressure module covers 0 to 40 kPa. At factory calibration at 25 °C it read 12 counts at 0 kPa and 3712 counts at 40 kPa. Its sensitivity falls by 0.2 % per °C above 25 °C. In use at 37 °C it reads 1492 counts. Find the pressure the processor should report, in kPa and mmHg (1 kPa = 7.50 mmHg), and the error if temperature compensation were left out.

  1. Gain: (3712 − 12) counts / 40 kPa = 3700 / 40 = 92.5 counts per kPa.
  2. Offset and gain correction: P = (1492 − 12) / 92.5 = 1480 / 92.5 = 16.0 kPa.
  3. Temperature factor: the sensitivity at 37 °C is 1 − 0.002 × (37 − 25) = 1 − 0.024 = 0.976 of its calibrated value, so the counts are 2.4 % low.
  4. Compensated pressure: P = 16.0 / 0.976 = 16.39 kPa = 16.39 × 7.50 = 123.0 mmHg.
  5. Without compensation the module would report 16.0 kPa = 120.0 mmHg, 3 mmHg low. That is about the whole error a clinical pressure monitor is allowed, so the temperature step is not optional.

Answer. The module should report 16.39 kPa, about 123 mmHg; skipping temperature compensation would give 120 mmHg, 3 mmHg low.

Exam traps

  • Quoting the ADC's 12 bits as the sensor accuracy. Bits give resolution only; offset, gain error and drift set accuracy until calibration.
  • Forgetting the amplifier gain when referring the LSB to the sensor input. A 0.806 mV ADC step is only 8.06 µV at the element.
  • Mixing kPa and Pa. A sensitivity of 10 mV/kPa is 10 µV/Pa, not 10 mV/Pa.
  • Assuming a digital bus removes analog error. The ADC reference, amplifier offset and sensor drift are still analog errors.
  • Calling every digital transmitter an intelligent instrument. A smart sensor is the chip or module with its own processing; an instrument adds display and enclosure.

5.8 Flexible sensors and multi-sensor data fusion

A flexible sensor is built on a plastic, rubber or paper backing, so the whole device bends and wraps around the surface it measures. That opens up skin patches, e-skin and smart packaging that rigid silicon cannot serve. But a bendable sensor drifts, and one reading is never the whole truth. Fusing several sensors — by weighted average, a complementary filter, a Kalman filter or voting — gives one estimate better than any single part.

You need smart sensors for digital output and calibration, and the error analysis and statistical treatment of measurement data from the static characteristics of sensors.

Flexible substrates and printed conductors

A flexible sensor sits on a backing that bends: the sensing film, electrodes and wiring are all deposited on it, so the device wraps around a wrist, a pipe or a food packet. Rigid silicon cannot; it cracks at a fraction of a percent of strain.

Four substrates cover most work. Polyimide (PI) is an amber plastic usable to about 300 °C, so silver ink can be cured and parts soldered. PET is clear and cheap, but its glass transition is near 80 °C, so it shrinks and warps above that; inks on PET are cured at low temperature, and even heat-stabilised film takes only short cures up to about 120 °C. PDMS is a silicone rubber that stretches past 100 % and follows skin. Paper is cheapest and suits single-use test strips.

Conductors are printed, not etched. Screen printing pushes paste through a mesh and lays tracks a few micrometres to about 25 µm thick; it is fast and runs roll-to-roll. Inkjet printing drops ink only where needed, with no mask, but tracks are thinner and less conductive. Silver ink gives the lowest resistance. Carbon ink is cheaper and inert, the usual choice for electrodes and printed resistors. PEDOT:PSS is a transparent, stretchable conducting polymer with much lower conductivity.

Encapsulation is not optional. Oxygen and water vapour oxidise the silver and swell the polymer, so resistance drifts and sensitivity falls. A parylene layer or laminated barrier a few micrometres thick holds the response steady for months. See micro-fabrication methods for the rigid-wafer version.

Printed devices and their ratings

A printed strain gauge is a track of silver or carbon on the flexible film. Its gauge factor is GF = (ΔR/R)/ε, where R is the unstrained resistance in ohms, ΔR the change in ohms, and ε the strain in m/m. A printed silver track has GF of 2 to 5, close to a metal foil gauge.

A crack-based gauge is a thin metal or carbon film on PDMS, deliberately precracked. Stretching opens the cracks and lengthens the conducting path; releasing closes them. GF can exceed 1000, and some designs reach 10 000, but the response is nonlinear and hysteretic and works only to about 1 % strain.

A capacitive pressure pad is two printed electrodes on either side of a foam or PDMS dielectric. C = ε₀εr A/d, where ε₀ = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m, εr is the relative permittivity, A the overlap area in m² and d the thickness in m. Pressing squeezes air out: d falls and εr rises, so C rises. Such pads map seat occupancy and bed pressure over a few kPa.

Printed silver or platinum tracks serve as RTDs, though a printed silver RTD drifts more than platinum wire and needs its own calibration; printed NTC paste with β of 3000 K to 4000 K serves as a thermistor. Dry ECG and EMG electrodes are printed Ag/AgCl on breathable film with no wet gel, so skin-electrode impedance is tens of kΩ at 10 Hz against a few kΩ for gel — the amplifier needs a high input impedance. Sweat patches read pH from a printed iridium oxide electrode and glucose from a printed enzyme electrode (biosensor classes, ISFET, IMFET and glucose sensors).

Ratings to quote: minimum bend radius and fatigue. A printed silver track on 25 µm polyimide survives a bend radius near 1 mm. After 1000 bend cycles to that radius, ΔR/R is usually a few percent, and crack-based gauges drift the most.

Multi-sensor data fusion

One sensor has blind spots, drifts, and a single failure ruins the reading. Fusion combines N readings into one estimate with lower uncertainty, wider coverage and redundancy against failure.

  • Data-level fusion combines raw samples before any processing, for example averaging two thermocouple outputs before linearising them.
  • Feature-level fusion extracts a feature from each sensor, then combines, for example heart rate from ECG and from a pulse oximeter.
  • Decision-level fusion lets each sensor decide first, then combines the decisions by voting.

The inverse-variance weighted average is the workhorse. It holds when the sensors' errors are independent and unbiased. Give each reading weight w = 1/σ², where σ is that sensor's standard deviation in its own units. Then

x̂ = Σ wᵢ xᵢ / Σ wᵢ     σfused = 1 / √(Σ wᵢ)

x̂ is the fused estimate; xᵢ is reading i; wᵢ = 1/σᵢ² in (units of x)⁻²; σfused is the standard deviation of the fused estimate, in the same units as x.

A complementary filter splits the job by frequency: x̂ₖ = α(x̂ₖ₋₁ + ω Δt) + (1 − α)aₖ, where ω is a rate in rad/s, aₖ an angle from the second sensor, Δt the sample interval in s, and α ≈ 0.98. It is standard for tilt from an accelerometer plus a gyroscope.

The scalar Kalman filter adds a changing state. Predict: x̂⁻ = x̂ₖ₋₁ and P⁻ = Pₖ₋₁ + Q. Update: K = P⁻/(P⁻ + R), x̂ₖ = x̂⁻ + K(zₖ − x̂⁻), Pₖ = (1 − K)P⁻. Here zₖ is the new measurement, Q the process-noise variance, R the measurement-noise variance, P the estimate variance and K the gain, between 0 and 1. A large R makes K small, so the filter trusts the model over the sensor.

Majority voting needs an odd count and a tolerance: with three sensors, accept the value that two agree on within the set tolerance (say ±0.5 °C) and flag the third. Dempster–Shafer theory combines belief masses when full probabilities cannot be assigned.

Worked example

Question. Two temperature sensors measure the same point. Sensor 1 reads 30.0 °C with σ₁ = 0.5 °C; sensor 2 reads 30.8 °C with σ₂ = 0.2 °C. The errors are independent and unbiased. (a) Find the inverse-variance weighted fused estimate. (b) Find the standard deviation of the fused estimate and compare it with the better sensor alone.

  1. Weight of a sensor is the inverse of its variance: w = 1/σ².
  2. Sensor 1: w₁ = 1/0.5² = 4 °C⁻².
  3. Sensor 2: w₂ = 1/0.2² = 25 °C⁻².
  4. Sum of weights: Σw = 29 °C⁻².
  5. Weighted sum: 4 × 30.0 + 25 × 30.8 = 120 + 770 = 890.
  6. Fused estimate: T = 890/29 = 30.69 °C. The noisier sensor barely moves the result.
  7. Fused standard deviation: σfused = 1/√29 = 0.186 °C ≈ 0.19 °C.
  8. Compare: the better sensor alone gave 0.20 °C, so the gain is small because σ₂ already dominates. With two sensors at σ = 0.5 °C, fusion would give 0.5/√2 = 0.35 °C, a clear gain.

Answer. The fused estimate is 30.69 °C with σfused ≈ 0.19 °C; the weights are 4 °C⁻² and 25 °C⁻².

Exam traps

  • Averaging two readings 50/50 when their uncertainties differ. The weight is 1/σ², not 1/σ.
  • Forgetting that correlated errors survive averaging. Two sensors on the same board share self-heating and the same reference voltage, so their common offset is not reduced at all.
  • Mixing up standard deviation and variance in the weight formula, or dropping the unit of weight, which is the inverse square of the measured quantity.
  • Confusing bend radius with bend angle. A smaller radius is the harsher test; a device rated at 5 mm may fail at 1 mm after the same number of cycles.
  • Assuming a crack-based gauge is linear over its whole range. Its gauge factor is huge but the response saturates beyond about 1 % strain.

5.9 Solid electrolyte and surface acoustic wave sensors

A solid electrolyte is a solid phase (crystalline ceramic or ion-exchange polymer) that conducts one kind of ion and almost no electrons, so a cell built on it gives a Nernst EMF for that species; the same material in amperometric mode gives a current that is linear in concentration. A surface acoustic wave sensor launches a ripple along a piezoelectric surface and reads how a film on the path slows it down. You meet the first in car lambda sensors and the second in wireless torque and temperature tags.

You need the Nernst cell from gas sensors, and piezoelectric transducers and ultrasonic waves.

Solid electrolytes: one ion, one EMF

A solid electrolyte is a solid phase (crystalline ceramic or ion-exchange polymer) that conducts one kind of ion and almost no electrons. The mobile ion carries the current, so the material is selective by construction. Common pairs: yttria-stabilised zirconia (YSZ) conducts O²⁻, sodium beta-alumina conducts Na⁺, Nafion conducts H⁺, and lanthanum fluoride conducts F⁻. The fuel-cell breath-alcohol tester is a proton-conducting cell of the Nafion type: ethanol is oxidised at one electrode and the current is proportional to the breath alcohol.

σ = σ₀ · exp(−Ea / kT)

σ is the ionic conductivity in S/m; σ₀ is a prefactor in S/m; Ea is the activation energy in J; k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann's constant; T is the temperature in kelvin.

Ea is large, so useful conductivity arrives only at a few hundred degrees Celsius. YSZ conducts usefully only above about 350 °C and is normally run at about 600 to 800 °C. A cold zirconia sensor gives almost no signal, which is why every lambda sensor carries a heater.

In potentiometric mode two electrodes on the electrolyte see two oxygen partial pressures, p₁ and p₂. The cell gives a Nernst EMF: E = (RT / 4F) · ln(p₁ / p₂). Here R = 8.314 J/(mol·K), T is in kelvin, F = 96485 C/mol, and the 4 is the number of electrons per O₂ molecule. This is the zirconia oxygen cell of gas sensors. The output is logarithmic, and a reference gas is mandatory.

Amperometric mode puts a diffusion barrier over the cathode and holds the cell at a voltage that pumps away every oxygen molecule that arrives. The current then saturates at Ilim = (4F·A·D / δ) · C. A is the barrier area in m², D the diffusion coefficient in m²/s, δ the barrier thickness in m, and C the oxygen concentration in mol/m³. The reading is linear in C and needs no reference gas.

Surface acoustic wave sensors

A surface acoustic wave (SAW) travels along the surface of a piezoelectric crystal, with its energy confined to about one wavelength of depth. Quartz, lithium niobate (LiNbO₃) and zinc oxide (ZnO) on silicon are the usual substrates.

An interdigital transducer (IDT) is two interleaved comb-shaped metal electrodes; the centre-to-centre spacing of neighbouring fingers is half an acoustic wavelength, so the finger pattern repeats every wavelength. An RF voltage across the combs makes the surface ripple. The wave runs to a second IDT, which converts it back into a voltage. That pair is a delay line; adding reflectors or closing the loop makes a resonator.

Δf / f = Δv / v

f is the operating frequency in Hz; Δf is its shift; v is the surface wave velocity in m/s; Δv is its change, signed; λ is the acoustic wavelength in m, set by the IDT finger spacing. The IDT fixes λ, and f = v/λ, so the frequency falls when the wave slows. The shift scales with mass per unit area, not total mass.

Mass loading or a soft, viscoelastic film on the delay path slows the wave and adds attenuation. A 0.10 % velocity drop becomes a 0.10 % frequency drop. Wireless passive tags use an antenna, an IDT and a reflector with no battery; a torque twisting the substrate changes the delay, and so does temperature.

In medicine the SAW path becomes an acoustic biosensor. Antibodies are fixed on the delay path; when the matching antigen from a blood or saliva sample binds, the added mass per area slows the wave and the frequency falls, with no label needed. A shear-horizontal SAW, whose particles move in the surface plane, keeps working under liquid, which a plain Rayleigh wave does not. The bulk-wave quartz crystal microbalance is used the same way.

Three neighbouring chemical transducers

  • Catalytic gas detector (pellistor): a heated platinum coil burns the gas, and the temperature rise changes the coil resistance.
  • Surface plasmon resonance: light reflected from a thin metal film shows a sharp dip at one angle because the energy couples into a surface plasmon; binding on the film shifts that angle.
  • Optical chemical sensor: a reagent changes colour or fluorescence when the analyte binds, and the change is read as absorbance or intensity.

Pick the solid electrolyte when you need selectivity to one ion at high temperature, and the SAW device when you need a passive, wireless, small mass or force sensor.

Worked example

Question. A SAW delay line runs at 100 MHz on quartz. An adsorbed film slows the surface wave by 0.10 %. Find the frequency shift, and check it against the film: the maker quotes a mass sensitivity of 13.5 Hz per ng/cm² at 100 MHz, and the film carries 7.4 µg/cm².

  1. Fractional velocity change: Δv/v = −0.10 % = −0.0010. The minus sign means the wave is slower.
  2. For a delay line, f = v/λ and λ is fixed by the IDT, so Δf/f = Δv/v = −0.0010. The fractional frequency shift has the same size and sign as the velocity shift.
  3. Δf = −0.0010 × 100 MHz = −0.100 MHz = −100 kHz. The frequency falls by 100 kHz.
  4. Mass estimate: 7.4 µg/cm² = 7400 ng/cm², so Δf = 13.5 Hz per ng/cm² × 7400 ng/cm² = 99 900 Hz ≈ 100 kHz.
  5. The two numbers agree, so the 0.10 % slowing is consistent with a 7.4 µg/cm² film under the linear mass-loading assumption. The sensitivity is per unit area, so the same total mass spread over twice the area would give half the shift.

Answer. Δf = −100 kHz, a 100 kHz downward shift, matching the 99.9 kHz expected from a 7.4 µg/cm² film.

Exam traps

  • Applying the Sauerbrey equation, a bulk quartz-crystal-microbalance result, to a SAW delay line, and forgetting that the shift follows mass per unit area, not total mass.
  • Reversing the Nernst ratio: the EMF rises when the sample oxygen falls, so preference sits on top of the fraction.
  • Believing a solid electrolyte conducts electrons like a wire. It conducts one ion, and a potentiometric cell still needs a reference gas or electrode.
  • Mixing the two modes: potentiometric is logarithmic and needs a reference; amperometric with a diffusion barrier is linear and needs none.
  • Ignoring the temperature dependence of σ. A zirconia cell below about 350 °C gives no usable reading whatever the oxygen level.

The limiting current is linear in oxygen concentration, needs no reference gas, and is set by diffusion through the barrier, so it is stable against electrode drift. A potentiometric cell is logarithmic and needs a known reference gas.

5.10 Biosensor classes by transducer and biological element; ISFET, IMFET and glucose sensors

A biosensor is named twice: by the biological element that recognises the target and by the transducer that turns recognition into a signal. Enzyme with an amperometric electrode gives the glucose strip; antibody with an optical surface gives the surface plasmon resonance immunosensor. The cases exams ask for are the ISFET, the IMFET and the glucose sensor.

You need chemical sensors and biosensors for the biosensor block diagram, semiconductor sensors and sensor materials for the FET as a transducer, and sensitivity, selectivity and drift from the static characteristics of sensors.

Two axes give every biosensor its name

A biosensor has two parts in series: a biological element that recognises one molecule, and a transducer that converts that recognition into a voltage, a current or a light level. Enzyme + amperometric = glucose sensor. Antibody + optical = surface plasmon resonance immunosensor. DNA + FET = GenFET.

The element sets the selectivity. Four families cover most work.

  • Enzyme: catalyses one reaction and is reusable and fast, for example glucose oxidase, urease, penicillinase.
  • Antibody: grips one antigen tightly; very selective but usually single-use.
  • DNA or aptamer: a strand pairs with its complement; an aptamer is a short folded strand that holds a small molecule.
  • Whole cell: a bacterium or tissue slice, for mixtures, as in BOD and toxicity tests.

The transducer sets the signal: electrochemical (amperometric, potentiometric or conductometric), optical, acoustic (see solid electrolyte and SAW sensors), calorimetric and FET types.

ISFET, IMFET and EnFET

Start with an n-channel MOSFET. Remove the metal gate and leave the gate oxide bare, touching the electrolyte. That is an ISFET, an ion-sensitive field-effect transistor. A Ag/AgCl reference electrode dips into the solution and sets the gate bias: the electrolyte forms the gate, but it has no defined potential of its own.

The bare oxide carries silanol groups. Si–OH ⇌ Si–O⁻ + H⁺, and Si–OH + H⁺ ⇌ Si–OH₂⁺. At high pH the surface loses protons, becomes negative, and pushes electrons out of the channel below. The threshold voltage rises, and at a fixed gate bias the drain current falls because the overdrive VGS − VT shrinks.

ΔVT = 2.303 (kT/q) ΔpH ≈ 59 mV × ΔpH at 25 °C

ΔVT is the threshold shift in volts; k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K; T is the absolute temperature in kelvin; q = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. 59 mV per pH is the ideal slope at 25 °C and 61.5 mV at 37 °C. Bare SiO₂ gives only 30 to 40 mV per pH, so practical ISFETs add a silicon nitride, alumina or tantalum oxide layer that comes close to the ideal.

Two errors dominate: drift, as the oxide hydrates over hours, which needs frequent recalibration; and hysteresis, a reading that depends on whether the previous sample was acid or alkali.

IMFET (immunologically modified FET). An antibody or an antigen is immobilised on the gate. Binding adds charge to the surface and shifts the threshold, so the device is label-free and needs no fluorescent tag. The weakness is ionic strength: counter-ions in a salty buffer screen the bound charge within about a nanometre, so only binding right at the surface is seen.

EnFET (enzyme FET). An enzyme layer sits over the ISFET gate and the local pH change reports the substrate. Urease on urea releases ammonia and the local pH rises; penicillinase on penicillin releases acid and the local pH falls. The initial rate of pH change gives the concentration.

The glucose sensor

Glucose oxidase (GOx) is the enzyme. It carries an FAD (flavin adenine dinucleotide) cofactor and catalyses the reaction below. Glucose is oxidised, oxygen is the electron acceptor, and hydrogen peroxide is the product the electrode sees.

glucose + O₂ → gluconolactone + H₂O₂ (catalysed by GOx)

O₂ is dissolved oxygen; gluconolactone is the oxidised product; H₂O₂ is the species the electrode oxidises.

First generation. The H₂O₂ is oxidised at a platinum electrode held at about +0.6 to +0.7 V against Ag/AgCl. Each glucose molecule hands over two electrons, so the current is proportional to the glucose reaching the enzyme. The flaw is the high potential: ascorbic acid, uric acid and paracetamol in blood also oxidise near +0.7 V and add a false current.

Q = ne F, I = Q / t

Q is charge in coulombs; ne is the moles of electrons, twice the moles of glucose; F = 96 485 C/mol; I is the average current in amperes over t seconds.

Second generation. A mediator such as ferrocene or ferricyanide carries electrons from the enzyme's FAD to the electrode, so the electrode can run at about +0.2 to +0.4 V. Fewer interferents oxidise there, and dissolved oxygen no longer limits the reaction. Third-generation sensors aim at direct electron transfer from the enzyme to the electrode with no mediator at all.

  • Disposable strip: a finger-prick drop (under 1 µL now, 5 to 10 µL in older strips) fills a capillary, a fixed potential is applied for about 5 s, and the current gives the glucose.
  • Continuous monitor: a fine needle in interstitial fluid reports every few minutes for days.

Normal fasting blood glucose is about 3.9 to 5.5 mmol/L, that is 70 to 100 mg/dL using 1 mmol/L = 18 mg/dL. A fasting value of 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL) or more on repeat tests points to diabetes, so a meter must read well across 2 to 30 mmol/L; diagnosis rules belong to a medical text.

Worked example

Question. An older disposable glucose strip is filled with 10 µL of blood whose glucose concentration is 5.5 mmol/L. Each glucose molecule releases 2 electrons at the platinum electrode. The enzyme reaction goes to completion in 10 s. Find (a) the moles of glucose, (b) the moles of electrons, (c) the total charge and (d) the average current. Take F = 96 485 C/mol.

  1. Convert the volume to litres: 10 µL = 10 × 10⁻⁶ L = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ L.
  2. Moles of glucose: ng = c × V = 5.5 × 10⁻³ mol/L × 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ L = 5.5 × 10⁻⁸ mol.
  3. Moles of electrons: ne = 2 × ng = 2 × 5.5 × 10⁻⁸ = 1.1 × 10⁻⁷ mol.
  4. Charge: Q = ne × F = 1.1 × 10⁻⁷ mol × 96 485 C/mol = 1.061 × 10⁻² C = 10.6 mC.
  5. Average current: I = Q / t = 1.061 × 10⁻² C / 10 s = 1.061 × 10⁻³ A = 1.06 mA.
  6. This is an upper bound: a real strip reacts only the glucose near the electrode in a few seconds, so its current is a few microamperes.

Answer. The strip would pass 10.6 mC, an average of about 1.06 mA over the 10 s, if all the glucose reacted; real strips react only a fraction and pass microamperes.

Worked example 2

Question. A blood-gas analyser uses an ISFET with a silicon nitride gate at 37 °C. Its slope is 90 % of the ideal Nernstian value. (a) Find the ideal slope at 37 °C. (b) Find the device slope. (c) A patient's blood pH falls from 7.40 to 7.30. Find the threshold shift and its direction. (d) Find the voltage resolution the readout needs to show 0.01 pH.

  1. (a) Ideal slope = 2.303 kT/q with T = 37 + 273.15 = 310.15 K: 2.303 × 1.381 × 10⁻²³ × 310.15 / 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 0.06154 V = 61.5 mV per pH.
  2. (b) Device slope = 0.90 × 61.5 = 55.4 mV per pH.
  3. (c) ΔpH = 7.30 − 7.40 = −0.10, so ΔVT = 55.4 × (−0.10) = −5.54 mV. More H⁺ makes the surface more positive, so the threshold falls by 5.5 mV and, at fixed gate bias, the drain current rises.
  4. (d) 0.01 pH × 55.4 mV per pH = 0.554 mV, so the readout must resolve about 0.5 mV and the device drift over a run must stay well below that.
  5. Check: a 0.10 pH fall is a large change in blood, yet it is only 5.5 mV, so the reference electrode and the temperature must be held steady.

Answer. The ideal slope at 37 °C is 61.5 mV/pH and the device gives 55.4 mV/pH; a pH fall from 7.40 to 7.30 lowers the threshold by 5.54 mV; the readout needs about 0.55 mV resolution for 0.01 pH.

Exam traps

  • Reversing the ISFET sign. For an n-channel device at a fixed gate bias the output falls as pH rises, because the threshold voltage rises and the overdrive shrinks.
  • Forgetting the 2 electrons per glucose molecule, or using the moles of glucose directly in Q = nF. The factor of two comes from H₂O₂ oxidation at platinum.
  • Calling a bare ISFET a biosensor. It is a pH sensor until an enzyme or antibody layer makes it an EnFET or an IMFET.

Analyze An IMFET gives a strong signal in dilute buffer but almost none in a high-salt buffer at the same antigen concentration. Explain. 5 marks

Ionic screening. Counter-ions in the electrolyte shield the charge of the bound antigen within about a nanometre, the Debye length, so the gate never feels the binding charge. Lowering the ionic strength widens the screening length and the signal returns.

Analyze Why does a ferrocene mediator reduce interference in a glucose sensor? 5 marks

Ferrocene carries electrons from the enzyme's FAD to the electrode at about +0.2 to +0.4 V. Hydrogen peroxide needs +0.6 to +0.7 V. Ascorbate, urate and paracetamol oxidise at the higher potential, so running lower removes most of their false current. The mediator also removes the dependence on dissolved oxygen.

Takeaways

  1. A chemical sensor has recognition plus transduction; a biosensor adds a biological recognition element in front of the same transducers.
  2. Electrochemical sensors split into potentiometric EMF, amperometric current and conductometric conductance, chosen by the analyte reaction.
  3. Gas sensors use resistance, current or EMF; selectivity depends on material, temperature and a filter or reference electrode.
  4. Humidity is reported as relative humidity at a stated temperature because saturation vapour pressure changes strongly with temperature.
  5. In a semiconductor sensor the material is the transducer, so doping, grain size and operating temperature set sensitivity and drift.
  6. MEMS micromachining makes diaphragms, beams and channels on silicon, read by piezoresistors, capacitors or heaters.
  7. A smart sensor outputs a calibrated digital value because signal conditioning and a processor sit inside the same package.
  8. Flexible sensors conform to curved surfaces, and fusion of several readings improves estimates when each sensor has different errors.
  9. A solid electrolyte gives a Nernst EMF for the ion it conducts; a SAW device senses mass or elasticity change on a piezoelectric surface.
  10. Biosensors are named on two axes, biological element and transducer, with ISFET, IMFET and the glucose strip as standard exam cases.
  11. In medicine the same sensors become the glucose meter, the blood-gas analyser (Clark pO₂, ISFET pH), the anaesthesia oxygen monitor, the arterial-line pressure transducer and the ventilator humidifier and flow sensor.

Understand Explain potentiometric and amperometric transduction in electrochemical sensors. State the measured quantity, the driving circuit and one interference for each. 10 marks

Points to cover. potentiometric measures EMF at near-zero current; amperometric measures current at fixed potential; reference electrode and three-electrode cell roles; interferences and selectivity limits.

Model answer.

  1. Potentiometric transduction. The sensing electrode and a reference electrode stand in the solution, and the EMF between them is measured with (ideally) no current flowing. The EMF follows the Nernst relation: E = E₀ + (2.303 RT / zF) log₁₀ a, where a is the ion activity and z its charge. For a singly charged ion at 25 °C the slope is 2.303 × 8.314 × 298.15 / 96 485 = 59.2 mV per decade.
  2. Measured quantity and circuit. The quantity is a voltage that grows with the logarithm of activity. A glass pH electrode has a resistance of 10⁸ Ω or more, so the meter must have an input resistance far above that, 10¹² Ω or more (an electrometer or FET-input op-amp buffer). Drawing current would polarise the electrode and change the reading. A stable reference electrode, Ag/AgCl or calomel, fixes the other half of the cell.
  3. Interference, potentiometric. Other ions also reach the membrane. A glass electrode responds to Na⁺ at high pH (the alkaline error), and an ion-selective electrode is described by the Nikolsky equation with a selectivity coefficient for each interfering ion. Temperature changes the slope (57.2 mV/pH at 15 °C, 59.2 at 25 °C), so the meter needs temperature compensation.
  4. Amperometric transduction. The working electrode is held at a fixed potential at which the analyte (or a product of its reaction) is oxidised or reduced, and the current is measured. When the reaction is limited by diffusion to the electrode, the current is proportional to concentration: I = I₀ + S C.
  5. Circuit: the three-electrode potentiostat. The working electrode carries the reaction; the reference electrode senses the solution potential and carries no current; the counter electrode supplies the current. An op-amp drives the counter electrode so that working-to-reference voltage equals the set value, and a transimpedance amplifier turns the working current into a voltage:
    Vout = − I Rf
120 nA through Rf = 10 MΩ gives Vout = −1.2 V
  6. Interference, amperometric. Any species that reacts at the chosen potential adds current. On a first-generation glucose electrode at +0.6 to +0.7 V, ascorbic acid, uric acid and paracetamol all oxidise and read as glucose. Remedies are a lower potential (a mediator at +0.2 to +0.4 V), a permselective membrane, or a blank electrode without enzyme whose current is subtracted. Dissolved oxygen also varies, which changes the first-generation response.

Model answer.

  1. Definitions first. RH = 100 × pv / psat(T), where pv is the water vapour pressure and psat(T) the saturation vapour pressure at the air temperature. Dew point is the temperature at which psat equals pv.

Points to cover. surface adsorption changes depletion layer and conductance; material and dopant set selectivity; grain size controls sensitivity; heater temperature controls response and recovery.

Model answer.

  1. Definition. A semiconductor gas sensor (chemiresistor) is a heated film or sintered bead of an n-type metal oxide, usually SnO₂, between two electrodes on a ceramic substrate with a heater on the back. A gas that reacts with its surface changes its resistance; the sensitivity is S = Rair / Rgas.
  2. Mechanism. In clean air, oxygen adsorbs on the hot surface and takes electrons from the conduction band, becoming O⁻ or O²⁻. This leaves a depleted layer at every grain surface and a potential barrier at every grain-to-grain contact, so the film resistance is high. A reducing gas such as CO, H₂, LPG or ethanol reacts with the adsorbed oxygen (CO + O⁻ → CO₂ + e⁻) and returns the electrons: the depleted layer thins, the barriers fall and the resistance drops. An oxidising gas such as NO₂ does the opposite and raises the resistance.
  3. Material. The oxide must be a wide-gap semiconductor that is stable in hot air and whose surface exchanges oxygen readily. SnO₂ (gap 3.6 eV) is the standard; ZnO, WO₃ (good for NO₂), TiO₂ and Ga₂O₃ are alternatives, each with a different best gas and temperature.
  4. Doping (catalysts). A small amount of Pd or Pt on the grains speeds the surface reaction and lowers the best working temperature; it also shifts the selectivity, for example Pd towards CO and methane. Bulk dopants such as Sb change the base resistance. A filter layer of charcoal or zeolite in front of the film removes some interfering gases.
  5. Grain size. Conduction crosses every grain, so the smaller the grains, the larger the share of the material that is depleted surface. When the grain diameter falls to about twice the depletion depth (a few nanometres for SnO₂), the whole grain is depleted in air and the response rises sharply. The surface-to-volume ratio shows the trend:
    Grain or cube size aA/V = 6/a
    1 mm6 × 10³ m⁻¹
    1 µm6 × 10⁶ m⁻¹
    20 nm3 × 10⁸ m⁻¹
  6. Small grains must be kept small: at high temperature they sinter and grow, which is one cause of long-term drift.
  7. Heater temperature. Below about 200 °C the surface reaction is too slow and the sensor barely responds; above about 500 °C the adsorbed oxygen leaves and the response falls again. Each gas has a temperature of peak response, typically 300 to 400 °C for SnO₂, so the heater sets both the speed and the selectivity. Some sensors cycle the heater between two temperatures and read the resistance at each to separate two gases. The heater also costs power, tens to hundreds of milliwatts, which limits battery use.
  8. Reading circuit. The film Rs sits in a divider with a load resistor RL on a supply Vc, and an amplifier or comparator watches the load voltage:
    Vout = Vc RL / (Rs + RL)
Vc = 5 V, RL = 10 kΩ:  air Rs = 60 kΩ → 0.714 V;  gas Rs = 4 kΩ → 3.57 V

  4. Calorimetric biosensors. An enzyme column with a thermistor measures the heat of reaction. The rise is small: a thermistor with −4 %/K on 10 kΩ changes by only 10 000 × 0.04 × 0.001 = 0.4 Ω for 1 mK, so a bridge and careful thermal isolation are needed. Almost any enzyme reaction gives heat, so the method is general, but it is slow and bulky.

  6. Sensitivity and interference. Optical methods reach the lowest concentrations, which is why SPR and fluorescence are laboratory research tools. Electrochemical sensors are sensitive enough for metabolites such as glucose, lactate and cholesterol, and the mediator keeps interference down. Acoustic sensors respond to anything that sticks to the surface, so blocking layers are needed against non-specific binding. Calorimetric sensors pick up every source of heat in the room.
  7. Cost, reagents and maintenance. A glucose meter costs a few hundred rupees and its strips a few rupees to tens of rupees each; the strip carries dry reagents with a shelf life of months and needs no cold chain once sealed. A lateral-flow strip needs no instrument at all. SPR, SAW and calorimetric systems need mains power, temperature control, flowing buffers, coated chips and trained staff.
  8. Recommendation for a rural clinic. Electrochemical sensors for quantitative metabolites (glucose, lactate, cholesterol), backed by optical lateral-flow strips for yes/no infection and pregnancy tests.
  9. Defence of the choice. (1) The clinic needs quantitative glucose to manage diabetes, and only the electrochemical strip gives a number from one drop of blood on a battery meter. (2) Results come in seconds, so the patient is treated in one visit. (3) Strips and meters are cheap, robust, and need no cold chain once sealed, no water and no trained technician. (4) The weaknesses, strip shelf life, temperature and haematocrit errors, are handled by meters with built-in temperature and haematocrit correction, sealed strip vials and a control solution checked each week. (5) For infections, where a yes/no answer is enough, lateral-flow strips cost even less and need no power. Acoustic and calorimetric systems are rejected for cost, power and maintenance; SPR is a research instrument.
  10. When the recommendation would change. If the clinic had to measure a protein marker at very low concentration, such as cardiac troponin, a hand-held fluorescence reader of immunoassay strips would be the better choice, still an optical method, and still battery-powered.
  11. Check. Against the normal fasting range of 3.9 to 5.5 mmol/L, the electrochemical strip above gives 73.5 to 97.5 nA, so a 7.0 mmol/L sample at 120 nA stands 22.5 nA above the top of that band, a margin easily read by a pocket meter; the calorimetric sensor would have to read the same glucose as a fraction of a millikelvin.
Evaluate Evaluate a wearable patch that fuses a flexible temperature sensor with a flexible heart-rate sensor. Discuss drift, motion artefact, calibration and the benefit of fusion over one sensor. 14 marks

Points to cover. flexible sensor mounting and skin contact; drift and motion artefact in each channel; fusion rules or estimation; when fusion helps and when it can mislead.

Model answer.

  1. The patch. A polyimide or PDMS patch on the chest carries a printed thermistor (or a printed silver or platinum RTD) for skin temperature, and a heart-rate channel: either printed dry Ag/AgCl ECG electrodes or an optical PPG (LED and photodiode). A small processor fuses the readings and sends them by Bluetooth.
  2. Mounting and skin contact. The patch must follow the skin so that the sensors touch it. A thermistor reads skin temperature, which lies a few degrees below core temperature and changes with room air and sweating, so the patch needs a model (or a heat-flux sensor) to estimate core temperature. Dry ECG electrodes have a skin impedance of tens of kΩ at 10 Hz, so the amplifier needs a very high input impedance. Sweat under the film changes both channels.
  3. Drift in each channel. Printed silver tracks oxidise and crack with repeated bending, so the thermistor or RTD resistance drifts and the temperature reading creeps; encapsulation (parylene or a laminated barrier) and periodic recalibration against a reference thermometer limit it. The ECG baseline wanders with electrode-skin potential and sweat; PPG amplitude drifts with skin perfusion and LED ageing, though heart rate (a timing measurement) is less affected than amplitude.
  4. Motion artefact. Walking moves the electrodes and the skin, adding noise in the same 1 to 3 Hz band as the heartbeat. PPG is worst affected because pressure on the skin changes the blood volume under the sensor. An on-board accelerometer measures the motion so the processor can reject or subtract the corrupted segments.
  5. Calibration. The temperature channel is calibrated at two points (for example 30 °C and 40 °C) against a reference and stored as offset and slope in the patch memory, as in a smart sensor. Heart rate needs no gain calibration but its error is estimated against a reference ECG at rest and while walking.
  6. Fusion rule. Two independent heart-rate estimates are combined by inverse-variance weighting, w = 1/σ². At rest the ECG gives 75 bpm with σ = 1.5 bpm and the PPG gives 72 bpm with σ = 3 bpm.
    ChannelReadingσWeight w = 1/σ²
    ECG75 bpm1.5 bpm0.444 bpm⁻²
    PPG72 bpm3.0 bpm0.111 bpm⁻²
    Fused(0.444 × 75 + 0.111 × 72) / 0.556 = 74.4 bpm1/√0.556 = 1.34 bpm
  7. The fused value lies close to the ECG and its spread, 1.34 bpm, is smaller than the ECG's 1.5 bpm. While walking, the accelerometer can raise the PPG's σ (say to 10 bpm) so its weight falls to 0.01 and the ECG dominates; a Kalman filter does this smoothly by tracking heart rate as a slowly changing state.
  8. Fusion across temperature and heart rate. The two quantities are different, so they are fused at feature or decision level: a fever alarm is more credible when temperature rises and heart rate rises with it (about 10 bpm per °C in adults is a common rule), and a temperature jump with no heart-rate change during exercise is more likely a contact fault or sweat.
  9. When fusion helps. When the errors are independent, the fused estimate is better than either sensor, gaps in one channel are covered by the other, and one failed sensor is detected because it disagrees.
  10. When fusion can mislead. When the errors are correlated: a patch that lifts off the skin spoils both channels at once, and sweat affects both, so averaging gives a confident wrong answer and the 1/√N gain does not apply. Weights set at rest are wrong during exercise. Fusion also hides a slowly drifting channel unless each channel is checked against its own limits.
  11. Check. With equal σ of 1.5 bpm on both channels, fusion would give 1.5 / √2 = 1.06 bpm; with the PPG at 3 bpm the gain is smaller (1.34 bpm), because the better channel already carries 0.444 / 0.556 = 80 % of the weight.

Glossary

Amperometric sensor
An electrochemical sensor that applies a fixed potential and measures the current produced by oxidation or reduction of the analyte.
Analyte
The target substance whose concentration or activity is being measured.
Bioreceptor
The biological part of a biosensor, such as an enzyme, antibody, DNA strand or whole cell, that binds or reacts with the analyte.
Biosensor
A sensor that uses a biological recognition element to detect an analyte and a transducer to produce the electrical output.
Bulk micromachining
A fabrication method that etches deep into a silicon wafer to create cavities, diaphragms, beams or channels.
Catalytic gas detector
A gas sensor that measures heat released when a combustible gas burns on a catalytic bead.
Chemical sensor
A device that turns the concentration of a chemical species into an electrical signal through a recognition layer and a transducer.
Clark electrode
An amperometric oxygen electrode: a platinum cathode behind an oxygen-permeable membrane, used for blood pO₂.
Conductometric sensor
A sensor that measures the change in electrical conductance of a solution or film caused by the analyte.
Dew point
The temperature at which air at a given moisture content becomes saturated and condensation begins.
EnFET
Enzyme FET: an ISFET under an enzyme layer; the enzyme reaction changes the local pH, which the ISFET reads.
Enzyme sensor
A biosensor in which an enzyme converts the target into a product that the transducer can measure.
FAD
Flavin adenine dinucleotide, the cofactor of glucose oxidase that takes the electrons from glucose.
Flexible sensor
A sensor made on a bendable substrate so that it can conform to skin, fabric or a curved surface.
Gas-sensitive resistor
A resistor, usually a metal-oxide film, whose resistance changes when a gas is adsorbed on its surface.
Glucose strip
A disposable amperometric test strip with glucose oxidase and a mediator, used in blood glucose meters.
Haematocrit
The fraction of blood volume taken by red cells; it changes the reading of glucose strips.
IMFET
An immunologically modified field-effect transistor that places an antibody or antigen layer on the gate of a FET biosensor.
IMFET
Immunologically modified FET: an ISFET whose gate carries an antibody or antigen layer, so binding shifts the threshold voltage.
Immunosensor
A biosensor that uses an antibody-antigen binding reaction and a transducer to detect the bound antigen or antibody.
ISFET
An ion-sensitive field-effect transistor whose drain current depends on the ion activity at its exposed gate insulator.
Mediator
A redox molecule such as ferricyanide or ferrocene that carries electrons from an enzyme to the electrode at a low potential.
MEMS
Microelectromechanical systems, small devices made by micromachining that combine mechanical parts with electrical circuits.
Microbial sensor
A biosensor that uses whole microbial cells as the recognition element, often through their respiration or metabolite production.
Piezoresistive effect
The change in electrical resistance of a material when it is mechanically strained.
Potentiometric sensor
An electrochemical sensor that measures an equilibrium EMF at almost zero current and relates it to ion activity.
Relative humidity
The percentage ratio of actual water-vapour pressure to saturation water-vapour pressure at the same temperature.
Sensor fusion
The combination of readings from several sensors to produce an estimate that is better than any single reading.
Smart sensor
A sensor package that includes signal conditioning, processing and a digital interface so it gives calibrated and addressed output.
Solid electrolyte
An ionic conductor that carries one type of ion through its crystal lattice and is used in electrochemical cells.
Surface acoustic wave device
A piezoelectric device in which a surface acoustic wave changes velocity or amplitude when mass or elasticity changes on the surface.
Surface micromachining
A fabrication method that builds thin movable structures by depositing and then removing sacrificial layers on a wafer surface.
