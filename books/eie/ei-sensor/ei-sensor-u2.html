2.1 Potentiometers as displacement sensors

A potentiometer is a resistor with a sliding contact. Move the contact and the resistance between one end and the contact changes in proportion to displacement. Feed the track from a steady supply and the wiper voltage follows position. It is the oldest and cheapest displacement sensor: machine-tool slides, servo feedback, volume controls, and the inside of a Bourdon-tube pressure gauge. Its weakness is the load. The wiper must drive something without drawing much current, or the output bends away from a straight line.

You need: sensitivity, resolution and linearity from the static characteristics of sensors.

Construction and the ideal law

A potentiometer has a resistive track, two end terminals and a wiper that slides on the track. The track is wire wound on an insulating former, carbon film, or conductive plastic. Wire-wound tracks are robust but stepped. Film and plastic tracks are smoother and quieter. Linear types have a straight track of length L (mm). Rotary types wrap the track into a circle. Multi-turn and helical types give many turns for fine setting.

Connect the track ends to a supply Vs (V). Let x (mm) be the wiper distance from the zero end. For a uniform track of total resistance R (Ω), the resistance from the zero end to the wiper is R1 = R·x/L. The rest is R2 = R·(L − x)/L. With no load, the output between wiper and zero end is a divider: V0 = Vs·R1/(R1 + R2) = Vs·x/L = Vs·α. The output is a straight line from 0 V at x = 0 to Vs at x = L.

On a wire-wound track the wiper jumps from turn to turn. With N turns over the stroke, resolution is Δx = L/N, and the output step is Vs/N. On a film or plastic track resolution is effectively continuous, limited by wiper size, contact noise and the readout electronics.

Loading error and the loaded divider

The straight law assumes no current leaves the wiper. A real load RL (Ω) connects across the output. Then R1 is in parallel with RL, and R2 is in series with that combination. The loaded divider gives V0 = Vs·α / [1 + α(1 − α)·(R/RL)]. Write r = R/RL, the load ratio.

The extra term k = α(1 − α)·r is zero at α = 0 and α = 1. Both ends therefore stay exact: at x = 0 the output is 0 V, and at x = L the wiper is tied to the supply through zero series resistance. The term is largest at mid-stroke, α = 0.5, where α(1 − α) = 0.25. Loading error is ΔV = Vs·α·k/(1 + k). For r = 1 at mid-stroke, k = 0.25 and ΔV = Vs·0.5·0.25/1.25 = 0.1·Vs. With Vs = 10 V that is 1.00 V low.

The error is a fraction k/(1 + k) of the ideal reading. It is 20 % of reading at mid-stroke when RL = R. It is only 2.44 % of reading when RL = 10R. Keep the load at least ten times the track resistance, or add a high-impedance buffer. Buffer amplifiers come with signal conditioning.

Practical limits and applications

Linearity is good on a uniform film track, but wire-wound tracks are only linear on average; each turn is a step. Wiper friction adds a mechanical load. The wiper needs a small force or torque to move, and that force can disturb a light mechanism. Wear and dirt raise contact resistance and create contact noise, a random fluctuation in output. Vibration makes the wiper bounce. Life depends on track material, wiper pressure and speed. Wire-wound tracks last long but wear the wiper; conductive plastic tracks wear smoothly and are common in test and servo use.

Use a potentiometer where a cheap, absolute position signal is enough. Machine-tool travel uses a linear track along the slide. Older servo systems use a rotary or multi-turn pot for position feedback. A volume control is a rotary audio potentiometer. A Bourdon-tube pressure gauge can carry a pot: pressure bends the tube, the tube tip moves the wiper, and the voltage reads pressure. Some syllabi call this the variable-resistance transducer or the slider potentiometer. In a hospital, an electrogoniometer straps a rotary potentiometer across a knee or elbow so that the wiper voltage follows the joint angle during gait studies and physiotherapy. Bridges come with signal conditioning, and non-contact position sensing with the inductive and optical sensors.

Worked example

Question. A wire-wound potentiometer has a total resistance of 10 kΩ, is fed from a 10 V d.c. supply and has a 100 mm stroke. (a) Find the ideal output at 50 mm. (b) Find the actual output when a 10 kΩ load is connected across the output. (c) Find the loading error.

  1. Given: R = 10 kΩ, Vs = 10 V, L = 100 mm, x = 50 mm, RL = 10 kΩ.
  2. Fraction of stroke: α = x/L = 50 mm / 100 mm = 0.500.
  3. Ideal output: V0 = Vs·α = 10 V × 0.500 = 5.00 V.
  4. Load ratio: r = R/RL = 10 kΩ / 10 kΩ = 1.00.
  5. Loaded output: V0 = 10 V × 0.500 / [1 + 0.500 × 0.500 × 1.00] = 5.00 V / 1.250 = 4.00 V.
  6. Loading error: ΔV = 5.00 V − 4.00 V = 1.00 V low. As a percentage of the ideal reading: (1.00 V / 5.00 V) × 100 = 20.0 %. As a percentage of full scale: (1.00 V / 10 V) × 100 = 10.0 %.

Answer. The ideal output is 5.00 V; with the 10 kΩ load it is 4.00 V, a loading error of 1.00 V low, which is 20 % of reading and 10 % of full scale.

Worked example 2

Question. A rotary potentiometer electrogoniometer has a 340° track of 5 kΩ, fed from 5 V, and its wiper drives an amplifier of 50 kΩ input resistance. The knee bends from 0° to 90° of the track. Find the ideal and loaded outputs at 90° and the error in degrees; the track is rated 0.5 W. Is the supply within the rating?

  1. Fraction of the track: α = 90/340 = 0.2647. Ideal output = 5 × 0.2647 = 1.324 V; sensitivity 5/340 = 14.7 mV/°.
  2. Load ratio r = 5 kΩ/50 kΩ = 0.1. k = α(1 − α)r = 0.2647 × 0.7353 × 0.1 = 0.01946.
  3. Loaded output = 1.324/(1 + 0.01946) = 1.298 V. Error = 1.324 − 1.298 = 0.0253 V.
  4. In degrees: 0.0253 V ÷ 14.7 mV/° = 1.72°, a reading of about 88.3° for a true 90°.
  5. Power in the track: V²/R = 5²/5000 = 5 mW, far below 0.5 W; the largest safe supply would be √(0.5 × 5000) = 50 V, so heating is not the limit.
  6. Remedy: a buffer amplifier (input resistance above 10 MΩ) makes r ≤ 0.0005 and the error under 0.01°.

Answer. Ideal 1.324 V, loaded 1.298 V, an error of 0.025 V or 1.7° low at 90°. The 5 V supply dissipates only 5 mW against a 0.5 W rating.

Exam traps

  • Quoting Vs·x/L as the real output while a load is connected. The divider is linear only when RL is much larger than R; use the loaded formula whenever the load is within about ten times the track.
  • Mixing x and α. The formula Vs·x/L needs x and L in the same unit; α = x/L is dimensionless. Putting 50 instead of 0.5 into an α formula gives an answer 100 times too large.
  • Saying loading error is largest at the ends. It is zero at x = 0 and x = L. As a fraction of the reading it is largest at mid-stroke, where α(1 − α) peaks; in volts it peaks a little later, near two-thirds of the stroke, at about 0.15·r·Vs for a light load.
  • Confusing resolution with accuracy. A wire-wound pot has resolution L/N from turn spacing; a film pot can resolve finer but still drifts with temperature, wear and contact noise.
  • Reporting loading error on the wrong base. State whether the percentage is of the ideal reading or of full scale; at mid-stroke with RL = R they are 20 % and 10 %.

2.2 Strain gauges: principle, metal and semiconductor types

A strain gauge is a resistor glued to a part. When the part stretches or compresses, the resistor changes resistance in proportion to the strain. The constant of proportionality is the gauge factor. This simple idea turns a mechanical quantity into an electrical one. Engineers use it for load cells, pressure diaphragms, torque shafts and displacement beams. It also measures bone strain, implant load, muscle force and breathing. The next sections separate the mechanics from the resistor, compare metal and semiconductor gauges, and show the bridge output and the errors that matter.

You need: the resistance-to-voltage idea from potentiometers and sensitivity and linearity from the static characteristics of sensors.

Strain, stress and the gauge factor

Stress σ is force per area, σ = F/A, with F in newtons and A in square metres; σ is in pascals (Pa). Strain ε is the fractional change in length, ε = ΔL/L, where ΔL is the change and L is the original length. Strain has no unit. It is often quoted in microstrain: 1 µε = 10⁻⁶. Young's modulus E = σ/ε connects the two for a linear elastic material; E is in Pa. Poisson's ratio ν is the magnitude of transverse strain divided by axial strain. A bar stretched along its axis also contracts sideways by ν times the axial strain.

GF = (ΔR/R)/ε = 1 + 2ν + (Δρ/ρ)/ε

GF is the gauge factor (dimensionless); R is the unstrained resistance in ohms (Ω); ΔR is the change in resistance in Ω; ε is the axial strain; ν is Poisson's ratio; ρ is resistivity in Ω·m and Δρ is its change. The first two terms, 1 + 2ν, are the geometric part; the last term is the piezoresistive part.

For a metal foil gauge the piezoresistive term is small, the geometric term dominates and GF is about 2. For a semiconductor gauge the piezoresistive term is large and GF can be 50 to 200. Some syllabi call GF the k-factor. A gauge factor of 2 means ΔR/R = 2ε. At ε = 500 µε, ΔR/R = 2 × 500 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.001, or 0.1 %.

Metal and semiconductor gauges

By construction there are two families. An unbonded gauge stretches fine wires between a fixed frame and a moving armature; the wires carry the strain directly and form a bridge, as in early blood-pressure transducers. A bonded gauge is glued to the surface of the part and strains with it: a wire grid, an etched metal foil, or a semiconductor element.

A metal foil gauge uses a thin constantan foil, a copper-nickel alloy, etched into a grid and bonded to a polyimide backing. Constantan gives GF about 2, low temperature coefficient and good linearity. The grid is long and narrow so the gauge measures strain along one axis. Gauge lengths run from 0.2 mm to 100 mm. Short gauges fit small stress concentrations; long gauges average strain over a larger area. The backing is glued with cyanoacrylate after surface cleaning. Common resistances are 120 Ω and 350 Ω. Self-temperature-compensated alloys are matched to the test material's expansion so apparent strain is smaller.

A semiconductor gauge is a diffused silicon resistor formed on a silicon diaphragm or beam. Its GF is 50 to 200, so a small strain gives a large ΔR. The price is nonlinearity, a strong temperature coefficient and a smaller strain range. The sign of the piezoresistive term depends on doping type and crystal direction, so read the datasheet; do not assume the resistance always rises. Semiconductor gauges are common in MEMS pressure sensors (see the micro sensors).

Wiring, errors and applications

Wire the gauge as one arm of a Wheatstone bridge. With one active gauge (quarter bridge) the output is Vs·GF·ε/4 for small strain (exactly Vs·ΔR/(4R + 2ΔR)); with two active gauges at opposite strain (half bridge) it is Vs·GF·ε/2; with four (full bridge) Vs·GF·ε. Vs is the bridge excitation voltage. Bridge balance and amplifiers come with signal conditioning.

Lead resistance adds to R and reduces sensitivity. Keep leads short and equal; in a quarter bridge, a long two-wire lead can also make the bridge unbalance. The datasheet's transverse sensitivity coefficient Kt says how much strain perpendicular to the grid changes the reading; it matters in rosettes. Apparent strain is output with no load when temperature changes: the gauge expands differently from the part and its own resistance drifts. A dummy gauge, bonded to an unstrained piece of the same material and wired in the adjacent bridge arm, cancels much of this.

Three gauges at fixed angles form a rosette: rectangular at 0°, 45° and 90°, delta at 0°, 60° and 120°; the three readings give the principal strains and their directions. A gauge on a cantilever makes a displacement transducer: tip deflection δ bends the beam, and the surface strain at the root is ε = 3tδ/(2L²) for thickness t and length L (worked example 2). Gauges on a thin diaphragm make a pressure transducer: near the centre the surface is stretched, near the edge compressed, so two tangential and two radial gauges form a full bridge. The disposable blood-pressure transducer on every intensive-care monitor is this design, rated 5 µV/V/mmHg. Other biomedical uses: bone and implant strain, muscle-force and respiration belts. Force and torque from strain are in load cells and torque measurement.

Worked example

Question. A constantan foil gauge has R = 120 Ω and gauge factor 2.0. It is bonded to a steel member carrying a strain of 500 µε. (a) Find ΔR. (b) Find the percentage change. (c) Repeat for a silicon gauge with gauge factor 100 at the same strain.

  1. Convert strain: ε = 500 µε = 500 × 10⁻⁶ = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴.
  2. For the metal gauge: ΔR = GF·ε·R = 2.0 × 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 120 Ω = 0.12 Ω.
  3. Percentage change: (ΔR/R) × 100 = (0.12/120) × 100 = 0.1 %.
  4. For the silicon gauge: ΔR = 100 × 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 120 Ω = 6.0 Ω.
  5. Percentage change: (6.0/120) × 100 = 5.0 %.

Answer. The metal gauge changes by 0.12 Ω (0.1 %); the silicon gauge changes by 6.0 Ω (5.0 %) at the same 500 µε strain.

Worked example 2

Question. A steel cantilever 50 mm long and 1.0 mm thick carries one gauge on the top and one on the bottom surface at the root (GF = 2.0), wired as a half bridge at 5 V. Find the root strain and the bridge output for a tip deflection of 1.0 mm, and the sensitivity in mV/mm.

  1. Root surface strain: ε = 3tδ/(2L²) = 3 × 1.0 × 1.0/(2 × 50²) = 3/5000 = 6.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 600 µε.
  2. The top gauge sees +600 µε (tension), the bottom −600 µε (compression); in adjacent arms their changes add.
  3. Half-bridge output = Vs·GF·ε/2 = 5 × 2.0 × 6.0 × 10⁻⁴/2 = 3.0 × 10⁻³ V = 3.0 mV.
  4. Sensitivity = 3.0 mV per 1.0 mm = 3.0 mV/mm, linear while the beam stays elastic.

Answer. 600 µε at the root; 3.0 mV from the half bridge; 3.0 mV/mm.

Exam traps

  • Dropping the 10⁻⁶: 500 µε is 500 × 10⁻⁶, not 500. A strain of 500 would be a 50,000 % length change, impossible for elastic metal.
  • Mixing ε and ΔL/L: ε is ΔL/L, so a 0.5 mm stretch on a 1 m bar is 500 µε, not 0.5 µε.
  • Treating GF as a resistance: GF is dimensionless. The same GF gives ΔR = GF·ε·R, so a 350 Ω gauge gives nearly three times the ΔR of a 120 Ω gauge at the same strain.
  • Assuming all semiconductor gauges have positive ΔR: doping type and crystal direction set the sign. Check the datasheet before wiring a bridge.
  • Forgetting transverse sensitivity and lead resistance in a rosette or quarter bridge: both shift the reading even when the axial strain is correct.

2.3 Load cells and torque measurement

A load cell is a spring with a measuring circuit. A shaped metal or alloy element takes the force, and bonded strain gauges turn its small stretch or squeeze into a resistance change. A full bridge makes that change a millivolt signal, calibrated directly in newtons or kilograms. The same idea measures torque when the gauges sit at 45° on a shaft. Load cells weigh trucks and patients, stop cranes from overloading and check motor power on test beds.

You need Chapter 2.2 for gauge factor, bridge configurations and temperature compensation.

Shapes of the elastic element

The elastic element is the metal part that carries the load. Its shape sets where strain appears, how uniform it is, and how much output the bridge gives. The element stays below its yield point, so it returns to zero when the load is removed.

  • Column: a solid or hollow post in compression or tension. Simple and stiff; used in weighbridges and silo scales.
  • Cantilever and double-bending beam: a beam bends under load. Common in small retail scales and platform scales.
  • Shear web: a thin web carries shear; gauges read shear strain. Gives good rejection of off-centre load.
  • Proving ring: a ring deflects under load; used as a reference standard.
  • S-type: shaped like an S, works in tension or compression; used for crane hooks and hoppers.

Four active gauges form a full bridge, placed so that two arms rise and two fall. On a bending beam or shear web two gauges see +ε and two −ε, and the bridge gives Vs·GF·ε. On a column the two axial gauges see −ε but the two transverse gauges see only +νε (the Poisson bulge), so the output is smaller. All four gauges share the same temperature, so a uniform temperature change cancels.

Vo = Vs·GF·ε(1 + ν)/2   (column: two axial, two transverse gauges)

Vo is bridge output in V; Vs is excitation in V; GF is the gauge factor; ε is the axial strain; ν is Poisson’s ratio (about 0.3 for steel). For four gauges of equal strain magnitude, as on a bending beam, the output is Vs·GF·ε.

Reading a load cell datasheet

A load cell is sold by its ratings. The rated capacity is the maximum load for which the output is specified. Safe overload is a higher load the cell can take occasionally without damage. Ultimate overload is the load that may cause permanent change; never use it as a working limit.

Mechanical stops limit travel under overload, and hermetic sealing keeps moisture out of the gauge area. Hydraulic load cells use a piston and pressure gauge, capacitive types plate spacing, piezoelectric types quartz charge; the bonded strain-gauge cell is the common choice for static weight. In hospitals load cells sit under bed legs to weigh patients who cannot stand, under infusion and urine bags, and in the force plates of gait laboratories.

Torque on a shaft

Torque is a twisting moment. A force F at radius r gives torque T = F · r. On a shaft, the surface is in shear, and the principal strains lie at 45° to the axis. Gauges are bonded along those 45° directions, not along the axis, to get the largest signal.

T = F · r

T is torque in N·m; F is force in N; r is radius in m.

P = 2π N T / 60

P is shaft power in W; N is speed in rev/min; T is torque in N·m. If N is in rev/s, use P = 2π N T.

A full bridge on the shaft uses four gauges at ±45°. It adds the tension and compression changes and cancels bending and axial strain. The bridge output is still a few millivolts, so an amplifier sits close to the shaft or on the stator.

  • Slip rings: metal brushes touch rotating rings. Simple, but wear and contact noise.
  • Rotary transformer: a rotating winding is coupled magnetically to a fixed winding. No contact, good for continuous running.
  • FM telemetry: a small transmitter on the shaft sends a frequency-modulated signal to a fixed receiver. No contact, robust, higher cost.

A stationary frame can measure reaction torque: mount the motor or pump on bearings and measure the restraining force at a known radius. This avoids rotating electronics. Applications include motor and pump test beds, torque wrench calibration, and drive-shaft monitoring.

Worked example

Question. A compression load cell is a solid steel column of 20 mm diameter with E = 200 GPa and ν = 0.3. Two 120 Ω gauges (GF = 2.0) are axial and two transverse, in a full bridge excited at 5 V. For a 10 kN load find the stress, the strains, ΔR of each gauge and the bridge output.

  1. Area: A = π(d/2)² = π(0.010 m)² = 3.142 × 10⁻⁴ m².
  2. Stress: σ = F/A = 10 000 N/3.142 × 10⁻⁴ m² = 31.83 × 10⁶ Pa = 31.8 MPa (compressive).
  3. Axial strain: ε = σ/E = 31.83 × 10⁶/200 × 10⁹ = 1.59 × 10⁻⁴ = 159 µε (shortening). Transverse strain: νε = 0.3 × 159 = 47.7 µε (bulging).
  4. ΔR of an axial gauge = GF·ε·R = 2.0 × 1.59 × 10⁻⁴ × 120 = 0.0382 Ω (down); of a transverse gauge 2.0 × 0.477 × 10⁻⁴ × 120 = 0.0115 Ω (up).
  5. Bridge output: Vo = Vs·GF·ε(1 + ν)/2 = 5 × 2.0 × 1.59 × 10⁻⁴ × 1.3/2 = 1.03 × 10⁻³ V = 1.03 mV.
  6. Check: had all four gauges seen 159 µε, the output would be 1.59 mV; the transverse pair supplies only 30 % of that, so the column gives 65 % of it. At 10 kN this is 1.03 mV/(5 V) = 0.21 mV/V, so a working cell uses a slimmer column or a bending element to reach about 2 mV/V.

Answer. σ = 31.8 MPa; ε = 159 µε axial and 47.7 µε transverse; ΔR = 0.038 Ω and 0.011 Ω; bridge output 1.03 mV at 5 V.

Worked example 2

Question. A steel shaft of 25 mm diameter (G = 80 GPa) carries a torque of 100 N·m at 1500 rev/min. Four gauges (GF = 2.0) at ±45° form a full bridge at 10 V. Find the surface shear stress, the strain along the 45° lines, the bridge output and the shaft power.

  1. Shear stress at the surface: τ = 16T/(πd³) = 16 × 100/(π × 0.025³) = 1600/4.909 × 10⁻⁵ = 32.6 × 10⁶ Pa = 32.6 MPa.
  2. Principal strains at ±45°: ε = ±τ/(2G) = 32.6 × 10⁶/(2 × 80 × 10⁹) = 2.04 × 10⁻⁴ = ±204 µε.
  3. Two gauges see +204 µε and two −204 µε, so the full bridge gives Vo = Vs·GF·ε = 10 × 2.0 × 2.04 × 10⁻⁴ = 4.07 mV.
  4. Power: P = 2πNT/60 = 2π × 1500 × 100/60 = 15 708 W = 15.7 kW.

Answer. τ = 32.6 MPa; ε = ±204 µε at 45°; output 4.07 mV at 10 V; shaft power 15.7 kW.

Exam traps

  • Using the quarter-bridge formula for a four-active-gauge load cell. That under-reads by a factor of four.
  • Confusing rated capacity with safe overload and ultimate overload. Rated capacity is the specified working load; safe overload is an occasional higher load; ultimate overload may damage the cell.
  • Bonding torque gauges along the shaft axis. Torsion gives principal strains at 45° to the axis, so axial gauges see little useful strain.
  • Reading sensitivity as output volts instead of millivolts per volt of excitation. A 2 mV/V cell at 5 V gives only 10 mV at full load.
  • Using Vs·GF·ε for a column cell. Its transverse gauges see only νε, so the output is Vs·GF·ε(1 + ν)/2, about 65 % as much.

2.4 Resistance thermometers (RTD) and thermistors

A resistance thermometer measures temperature through resistance. The RTD (resistance temperature detector) is a coil or thin film of pure metal, usually platinum; its resistance rises steadily with temperature. The thermistor is a small bead or disc of sintered ceramic oxide; its resistance falls steeply and nonlinearly. Both are passive, so a small current is passed through them and the voltage drop is read. They serve laboratory work, process plants, air conditioners, car engines and biomedical probes.

You need: calibration, accuracy and first-order response from the sensor characteristics, and potentiometers for turning resistance into voltage.

The RTD: a metal resistor used as a thermometer

An RTD is fine platinum wire, or a thin platinum film on ceramic, with a defined resistance at 0 °C called R₀. Pt100 means R₀ = 100 Ω; Pt1000 means 1000 Ω. The larger value gives a larger voltage for the same current, and, more importantly, makes fixed lead resistance a ten times smaller fraction of the element resistance, so lead error is ten times smaller in °C.

Platinum is standard because it does not oxidise, its curve repeats from batch to batch and over years, and it is nearly linear. Nickel is more sensitive but drifts; copper is linear but oxidises above about 150 °C. Both cover narrower spans.

RT = R₀(1 + αT)

RT is resistance at temperature T (Ω); R₀ is resistance at 0 °C (Ω); α is the temperature coefficient of resistance, 0.00385 /°C for platinum; T is temperature in °C.

Thus a Pt100 reads 138.5 Ω at 100 °C. Sensitivity is 0.385 Ω/°C, or 0.385 %/°C. Platinum works from −200 °C to 600 °C, but over that span the line bends, so the quadratic term is needed.

RT = R₀[1 + AT + BT² + C(T − 100)T³]

Callendar–Van Dusen form. A = 3.9083 × 10⁻³ /°C, B = −5.775 × 10⁻⁷ /°C² and C = −4.183 × 10⁻¹² /°C⁴ for platinum; C is 0 for T ≥ 0 °C.

Leads and self-heating

Connecting wires add resistance. A two-wire circuit measures RT + 2RL, where RL is one lead. For a Pt100, 0.385 Ω is 1 °C, so a 0.5 Ω lead pair reads about 1.3 °C high. Lead resistance also drifts with cable temperature.

A three-wire circuit uses three equal leads and cancels one lead drop against another. It works only while the leads are equal, which is true when they share a cable. A four-wire (Kelvin) circuit sends current through the outer pair and senses voltage across the inner pair. No current flows in the sense leads, so lead and contact resistance drop out. Precision Pt100 work uses four wires.

ΔT = I²RT / PD

ΔT is temperature rise above surroundings (°C); I is measuring current (A); RT is element resistance (Ω); PD is the dissipation constant in the surroundings (W/°C).

That rise is self-heating. At 1 mA through 100 Ω the power is 0.1 mW; with PD = 5 mW/°C the error is 0.02 °C. In still air PD is smaller and the error grows; in stirred water it is larger. Keep current near 1 mA or less, or switch it on only for the reading.

The thermistor: a ceramic with a steep curve

A thermistor is sintered oxides of manganese, nickel, cobalt and iron, pressed into beads, discs or chips and sealed in glass or epoxy. An NTC thermistor falls in resistance as temperature rises; it is the sensing type. A PTC thermistor rises sharply beyond a transition temperature and serves as a self-regulating heater or overcurrent guard, not a thermometer.

RT = R₀ · exp[β(1/T − 1/T₀)]

T and T₀ are absolute temperatures in kelvin; R₀ is resistance at T₀ (usually 25 °C); β is a material constant in kelvin, typically 3000 K to 4000 K.

(1/R)(dR/dT) = −β/T²

Fractional sensitivity per kelvin. At 300 K with β = 3500 K it is −3.9 %/°C, about ten times the 0.385 %/°C of a Pt100. The minus sign means resistance falls as temperature rises.

The gain costs range and straightness: an NTC covers about −50 °C to 150 °C and is far from linear, so even a 50 °C stretch needs a table or linearising network. The dissipation constant PD is the milliwatts that raise it 1 °C; the thermal time constant is the seconds to reach 63.2 % of a step, the first-order response of dynamic characteristics. Beads are small and fast. β and R₀ carry tolerances, so two units from one bin can differ by a degree or more.

Choosing between them, and biomedical probes

For wide span, accuracy and stability, choose Pt100 or Pt1000 with three or four leads. For a narrow span where cost, size and resolution matter more than linearity, choose an NTC and store its curve in the instrument.

Biomedical sensing uses both to about 0.1 °C. A skin or catheter probe must be small and fast, which favours an NTC bead. A reusable clinical thermometer must let tips be swapped without recalibration, which favours interchangeable thermistors or platinum. Current is held to tens of microamperes so I²RT heating stays under a hundredth of a degree.

Worked example

Question. (a) Find the resistance of a Pt100 at 50 °C with α = 0.00385 /°C, and state sensitivity in Ω/°C and %/°C. (b) An NTC is 10 kΩ at 25 °C with β = 3500 K. Find its resistance at 37 °C and average sensitivity in %/°C over that span.

  1. Part (a). RT = R₀(1 + αT) with R₀ = 100 Ω, α = 0.00385 /°C, T = 50 °C.
  2. RT = 100 × (1 + 0.00385 × 50) = 119.25 Ω.
  3. Change = 19.25 Ω over 50 °C, so sensitivity = 0.385 Ω/°C = 0.385 %/°C.
  4. Part (b). T = 310.15 K, T₀ = 298.15 K.
  5. 1/310.15 − 1/298.15 = −1.2978 × 10⁻⁴ K⁻¹.
  6. β times bracket = 3500 × (−1.2978 × 10⁻⁴) = −0.4542.
  7. RT = 10 000 × exp(−0.4542) = 10 000 × 0.63496 = 6349.6 Ω, about 6.35 kΩ.

  9. Ratio = 3.04/0.385 = 7.9, about eight times. The 3.9 %/°C quoted in the text is the local sensitivity at 300 K; averaged over this 12 °C span the figure is lower, about 3.0 %/°C.

2.5 Hot-wire anemometer and resistance hygrometer

A hot-wire anemometer measures flow speed by heating a fine wire and noting how much current is needed to keep it hot. A resistance hygrometer measures humidity by letting a moist film change its electrical resistance. Both are resistance devices: one reads velocity, the other reads water vapour. You meet them in air ducts, wind tunnels, engine intakes, textile mills, cold stores and weather stations.

You need RTDs and thermistors for resistance against temperature, and the first-order thermal lag from dynamic characteristics.

Heat balance and King's law

A hot-wire probe is a platinum or tungsten wire a few micrometres across and a few millimetres long, mounted on two prongs. A current I heats it above the flow temperature Ta. The flow removes heat by convection. At steady state the electrical power equals the heat carried away:

I²Rw = hA(Tw − Ta)

I is heating current in A; Rw is wire resistance in Ω; h is the convective heat-transfer coefficient in W/(m²·K); A is wire surface area in m²; Tw is wire temperature in K; Ta is flow temperature in K.

King's law relates h to flow speed u:

h = a + b√u

h is the convective heat-transfer coefficient; u is flow speed in m/s; a and b are constants found by calibration.

The square-root form comes from boundary-layer heat transfer over a cylinder. Combine it with the resistance–temperature relation of the RTD, Rw = R0[1 + α(Tw − T0)], and the bridge output E follows:

E² = A + B·un

E is output voltage in V; A and B are calibration constants; u is flow speed in m/s; n is near 0.5.

The instrument is linear only in E² against √u. Plotting E against u gives a curve, not a straight line.

Constant current and constant temperature

In constant-current mode the heating current is fixed. When flow rises the wire cools, Rw falls, and the voltage across it drops. The bridge unbalance is the output. This mode is simple but slow: the wire must heat or cool, and its thermal lag is first order.

In constant-temperature mode a feedback amplifier holds Rw, and therefore Tw, fixed. Flow tries to cool the wire; the bridge error makes the amplifier drive more current. That drive current, read as a voltage E, is the output. This mode is faster and less sensitive to ambient temperature, but needs a stable feedback loop.

  • A second unheated cold wire measures Ta for temperature compensation.
  • Calibration uses known velocities in a wind tunnel or a calibrated nozzle.
  • A single wire senses speed normal to it; a split-film or three-wire probe gives direction.
  • Hot-film probes with a thin metallic film on a glass or quartz substrate work in liquids.
  • Uses include duct and wind-tunnel velocity, engine intake flow and turbulence; in medicine, heated-film flow sensors measure breathing flow in ventilators and spirometers.

Resistance hygrometer

Relative humidity (RH) is the ratio of water vapour present to the saturation amount at the same temperature, usually expressed as a percentage. It is not absolute humidity, which is the mass of water vapour per unit volume, and not dew point, the temperature at which air becomes saturated.

A resistance hygrometer has a hygroscopic film between two electrodes: a conductive polymer, a treated ceramic, or a lithium-chloride layer. Water uptake raises ionic conduction, so resistance falls as RH rises. Over a limited range the fall is roughly log-linear, about one decade per 30 % RH.

Typical range is 10 % to 90 % RH, accuracy a few percent RH and response from seconds to minutes; contamination and temperature changes cause drift, so the film must be kept clean and calibrated. Uses: textile mills, cold stores, weather stations and grain storage, and in hospitals the humidity of neonatal incubators and of the warmed gas a ventilator delivers. Capacitive humidity sensors come with the chemical sensors.

  • Mixing up relative humidity and absolute humidity. RH changes with temperature even when the water vapour content stays the same.
  • Forgetting temperature compensation on a hot wire. The wire also responds to Ta, so a cold wire or a compensated bridge is needed.
  • Using inconsistent units in King's law. B carries units of V²/(m s⁻¹)0.5 when u is in m/s.
  • Assuming a resistive hygrometer is linear in RH. It is roughly log-linear, about one decade per 30 % RH.

2.6 Photo-resistive and magneto-resistive sensors

Some materials change resistance when light falls on them, and others when a magnetic field passes through them. Both are passive: they generate nothing, and a small excitation current turns the resistance change into a voltage. The photo-resistive sensor, usually a cadmium sulphide light-dependent resistor, switches street lights and detects flames. The magneto-resistive sensor reads gear teeth, wheel speed, current and compass heading.

You need: strain gauges for reading a small resistance change with a bridge, and sensitivity and linearity from the static characteristics of sensors.

The light-dependent resistor

A photo-resistive sensor, also called a photoconductive cell or light-dependent resistor (LDR), is a resistor made from a photoconductive semiconductor. A thin serpentine track of cadmium sulphide (CdS) or cadmium selenide (CdSe) sits on a ceramic substrate under a clear window. The serpentine shape gives a long, narrow path, so a small current sees a useful resistance. In darkness, few free carriers exist and the resistance is in the megohm range.

When light of enough photon energy falls on the film, it creates electron-hole pairs. Both carriers add to conduction, so carrier density rises and resistance falls. When the light goes off, carriers recombine; this recombination takes tens of milliseconds, which sets the speed. The sensor is slow compared with a photodiode but fine for a street light or a flame alarm.

R = A·E−γ

R is resistance in Ω, E is illuminance in lux, A is a constant in Ω·luxγ, and γ is a dimensionless index between 0.5 and 1.

  • Dark resistance: megohms.
  • At 1000 lux: about 100 Ω to a few kΩ.
  • CdS peak response is near 540 nm, in green-yellow light.
  • Temperature and past light exposure shift the reading; this is light-history memory.

Uses include street-light switching, camera exposure, flame and burglar alarms, and card readers.

Using an LDR in a divider

The LDR is read with a voltage divider or a bridge. A divider is enough when accuracy is loose; a bridge, as in Chapter 2.2, is better when the resistance change is small or temperature drift matters. In the divider, put the LDR in series with a fixed resistor Rf across supply Vs. The output is taken across Rf.

Vout = Vs × Rf / (RLDR + Rf)

Vout is the output voltage in V, Vs is the supply voltage in V, Rf is the fixed resistance in Ω, and RLDR is the LDR resistance in Ω.

The output rises as light rises. The divider is nonlinear: equal lux steps do not give equal volt steps, and sensitivity is highest when RLDR is near Rf. Choose Rf near the geometric mean of the dark and light resistances for a wide range. For on/off control, add a comparator with hysteresis so the lamp does not chatter at dusk.

Magneto-resistive sensors

A magneto-resistive sensor uses a ferromagnetic thin film whose resistance depends on the angle between current and magnetization. In permalloy (nickel-iron), anisotropic magnetoresistance (AMR) gives a few percent change. Giant magnetoresistance (GMR) uses stacked alternating ferromagnetic and nonmagnetic layers and gives tens of percent. The cause is spin-dependent scattering: electrons whose spin is parallel and antiparallel to the layer magnetization scatter differently.

A single film is not linear and has hysteresis. On chip, four films form a Wheatstone bridge; two are shielded and two see the field. The bridge subtracts common temperature drift and gives an output nearly linear with field over a set range. A set/reset strap carries a pulse that flips the magnetization to a known direction before each measurement. A bias magnet or an internal coil pushes the film to the steep part of its curve. The output is a small voltage per volt of supply, proportional to field in the linear range.

Uses include gear-tooth and cam position, wheel speed for ABS, current sensing, electronic compass, and disk-drive heads.

Choosing between them

Both sensors are passive and need signal conditioning. The LDR is cheap and handles a large resistance change, but it is slow, nonlinear, temperature sensitive, and many types contain cadmium. The magneto-resistor is fast and robust and measures field or speed without contact, but it needs a bridge, a set/reset pulse and magnetic design. It senses field, not light. A Hall sensor also senses field but gives a voltage from the Hall effect; a magneto-resistor gives a resistance change. Use the LDR for slow light decisions and the magneto-resistor for motion, current and compass tasks.

Worked example

Question. An LDR follows R = 10⁶/E with E in lux. (a) Find R at 100 lux. (b) The LDR is in series with a 10 kΩ fixed resistor across a 5 V supply, and the output is taken across the fixed resistor. Find the output at 100 lux. (c) Find R and the output at 500 lux.

  1. Use R = 10⁶/E with E in lux. At E = 100 lux, R = 10⁶/100 = 10,000 Ω = 10 kΩ.
  2. The divider gives Vout = 5 × Rf/(RLDR + Rf) with Rf = 10 kΩ. At 100 lux, Vout = 5 × 10,000/(10,000 + 10,000) = 5 × 0.5 = 2.5 V.
  3. At E = 500 lux, R = 10⁶/500 = 2,000 Ω = 2 kΩ.
  4. At 500 lux, Vout = 5 × 10,000/(2,000 + 10,000) = 5 × 10/12 = 4.1667 V ≈ 4.17 V.
  5. Sensitivity check: between 100 and 500 lux the output rises 1.67 V; near 100 lux it changes 12.5 mV per lux, near 500 lux only 1.4 mV per lux, so the fixed resistor sets where the divider is most sensitive.

Exam traps

  • Calling R = A·E−γ linear in lux. It is a straight line only on log-log paper; on linear axes it is a steep curve.
  • Calibrating an LDR with one lux point. γ, temperature and light history change the curve, so use at least two points or a table.
  • Mixing a magneto-resistor with a Hall sensor. The magneto-resistor changes resistance; the Hall sensor generates a voltage. They answer the same field question with different circuits.
  • Forgetting the set/reset strap in an AMR or GMR bridge. Without it the output carries hysteresis and offset from the last field.
  • Reading dark resistance as the light resistance, or dropping the lux unit from E.

2.7 Thermocouples: laws, characteristics and cold-junction compensation

Join two different metals at two points and heat one. A small voltage appears, tens of microvolts per degree, depending only on the difference of the two junction temperatures. That is a thermocouple: the workhorse of industrial temperature measurement. It needs no supply, is small, cheap and rugged, covers −200 °C to 1600 °C, but its output is small, curved, and meaningless unless the cold junction is known.

You need: RTDs and thermistors for the comparison, and the calibration chain and error analysis from the sensor characteristics.

Seebeck, Peltier and Thomson: where the volts come from

A thermocouple is a closed loop of two dissimilar metals with two junctions. Heat one junction and current flows; break the loop and you measure an emf. Seebeck reported this in 1821. Two effects build that emf.

The modern picture: in each wire, carriers at the hot end diffuse toward the cold end, so every wire across a temperature gradient develops an emf along it. Two different metals develop different emfs, and the loop’s net emf is their difference: the Seebeck effect. The emf is generated in the gradient along the wires, not in the junctions themselves, which is why the wires must be homogeneous.

Two heat effects go with it. The Peltier effect: a current crossing a junction of two metals absorbs or releases heat there, the basis of the Peltier cooler. The Thomson effect: a current along one wire with a temperature gradient absorbs or releases heat along its length. Older texts describe the Seebeck emf as the sum of Peltier emfs at the junctions and Thomson emfs along the wires.

Only the difference of the two wires’ emfs survives, so both junctions at the same temperature give exactly 0 mV, whatever that temperature is. Hence the cold junction must be known.

E = S (Th − Tc)

E is the Seebeck emf in mV; S is the Seebeck coefficient of the pair in µV/°C; Th and Tc are the hot and cold junction temperatures in °C. The line is straight only over a small span; over a wide span S changes, so standard tables are used.

The three laws that make thermocouples practical

Three laws turn a pair of junctions into an instrument.

  • Homogeneous circuit: a loop of one homogeneous metal, with its ends at different temperatures, gives no emf. A single wire cannot measure temperature. Useful half: the reading depends only on the two junction temperatures, not on the temperature profile along the wires, provided each wire is homogeneous.
  • Intermediate metals: insert a third metal anywhere, with both ends at the same temperature, and the emf is unchanged. Copper terminals, solder, plugs and the amplifier input are allowed if each added pair is isothermal.
  • Intermediate temperatures: E(T₁,T₃) = E(T₁,T₂) + E(T₂,T₃). This refers all tables to 0 °C and lets the instrument add the cold-junction emf.
E(Th, 0 °C) = E(Th, Tc) + E(Tc, 0 °C)

E(Th, Tc) is the meter reading in mV; E(Tc, 0 °C) is the table value at the cold-junction temperature; E(Th, 0 °C) is the table value you look up to get Th.

Read backwards: the meter's millivolts are a temperature difference, never an absolute temperature.

Cold-junction compensation

The instrument must supply E(Tc, 0 °C) itself. Three methods do it.

  1. Ice bath: hold the cold junction in stirred ice–water at 0 °C, so E(Tc, 0 °C) = 0 and the table gives Th directly. Accurate, laboratory only.
  2. Hardware compensation: a bridge in the terminal block, with a thermistor or copper resistor in one arm, generates a voltage that follows E(Tc, 0 °C) and adds it. The element must sit in the same isothermal block as the terminals.
  3. Software compensation: a second sensor measures block temperature Tc, the microcontroller looks up E(Tc, 0 °C), adds it, and converts the total to Th. Most modern meters work this way.

Extension and compensating leads: the cable from probe to instrument must be the same alloy pair (extension grade), or a cheaper pair with nearly the same emf near room temperature (compensating lead). Copper is not allowed: it moves the reference junction to the probe head or junction box, where the instrument's cold-junction sensor cannot see it.

Keep both terminals on one isothermal block, clamped together and shielded from radiated heat. A gradient across the block appears directly as an error.

Worked example

Question. A K-type thermocouple produces 11.00 mV while its cold junction sits in a terminal block at 30 °C. K-type tables give E(30 °C, 0 °C) = 1.20 mV. (a) Find the emf referred to 0 °C. (b) Find the hot-junction temperature. (c) Find the average Seebeck coefficient.

  1. The meter measures E(Th, Tc) = 11.00 mV, the emf between the hot junction and the cold junction at 30 °C.
  2. Law of intermediate temperatures: E(Th, 0 °C) = 11.00 + 1.20 = 12.20 mV.
  3. K-type table at 12.20 mV, cold junction 0 °C: Th ≈ 300 °C.
  4. Savg = 12.20 mV / 300 °C = 40.7 µV/°C, close to the nominal 41 µV/°C of type K.

Answer. E referred to 0 °C = 12.20 mV; hot junction ≈ 300 °C; average sensitivity ≈ 40.7 µV/°C.

Exam traps

  • Reading the meter's millivolts straight from the table as if the cold junction were at 0 °C. That under-reads by roughly the cold-junction temperature.
  • Subtracting E(Tc, 0 °C) instead of adding it. The correction always makes the emf larger than the reading.
  • Using copper wire to extend the thermocouple. It moves the reference junction to the probe head, where the instrument's cold-junction sensor does not measure.
  • Unit slip: S is in µV/°C while tables are in mV, so 41 µV/°C × 300 °C is 12.3 mV, not 12.3 V.

Takeaways

  1. A potentiometer is the simplest displacement sensor: displacement sets the wiper position, the divider sets the voltage, and the only serious error is loading by the measuring circuit.
  2. A strain gauge turns mechanical strain into a fractional resistance change, and the gauge factor is the single constant that links the two.
  3. Metal foil gauges are linear and stable; semiconductor gauges are far more sensitive but need temperature compensation.
  4. The Wheatstone bridge is the standard signal-conditioning circuit for every resistive sensor in this unit, because it reads small ΔR/R without being swamped by the large standing resistance.
  5. Shape the elastic element properly and a strain gauge measures force, weight or torque with a calibration that is fixed by geometry, not by the electronics.
  6. RTDs are accurate and near-linear but slow; thermistors are very sensitive but nonlinear and limited in range; choosing between them is a trade of linearity against sensitivity.
  7. The same resistance–temperature relation that makes a hot wire cool also makes it a flow sensor, which is why hot-wire anemometry is a temperature measurement in disguise.
  8. Photon and magnetic-field sensors are passive resistors with no moving parts, which makes them cheap, rugged and easy to interface to a bridge.
  9. A thermocouple needs no excitation and works to very high temperatures, but its accuracy depends entirely on knowing or compensating the cold junction.

Understand Define gauge factor. Derive its expression for a single metal wire strain gauge using the resistance formula R = ρL/A, and explain why the value for a metal gauge is close to 2. 13 marks

Points to cover. R = ρL/A written with each symbol defined; Logarithmic differentiation giving ΔR/R = Δρ/ρ + ΔL/L − ΔA/A; Area change from Poisson's ratio: ΔA/A = −2ν·ε; Result GF = 1 + 2ν + (Δρ/ρ)/ε; For metals the resistivity term is small and ν ≈ 0.3, so GF ≈ 2; Comment: semiconductor gauges get their large GF from the resistivity term.

Model answer.

  1. Definition. The gauge factor is the fractional change of resistance per unit strain: GF = (ΔR/R)/ε, where R is the unstrained resistance (Ω), ΔR its change (Ω) and ε = ΔL/L the axial strain (dimensionless, often written in µε). A gauge with GF = 2 changes resistance by 2 parts per million for every microstrain.
  2. Starting point. A wire of length L (m), cross-section A (m²) and resistivity ρ (Ω·m) has R = ρL/A. Stretching it changes all three: L grows, A shrinks by the Poisson effect, and ρ itself changes with the stress in the crystal lattice (the piezoresistive effect).
  3. Logarithmic differentiation. Take logarithms and differentiate:
    ln R = ln ρ + ln L − ln A
dR/R = dρ/ρ + dL/L − dA/A
  4. The area term. For a round wire of diameter D, A = πD²/4, so dA/A = 2·dD/D. Poisson’s ratio ν links lateral and axial strain: dD/D = −ν·dL/L = −ν·ε. Hence dA/A = −2ν·ε: the area falls as the wire is stretched. The same result holds for a rectangular foil strip, since both width and thickness shrink by ν·ε.
  5. Substitute and divide by ε.
    dR/R = dρ/ρ + ε + 2ν·ε
GF = (dR/R)/ε = 1 + 2ν + (dρ/ρ)/ε
  6. Reading the result. The term 1 is the length change, 2ν is the area change, and together 1 + 2ν is the geometric part. The last term (dρ/ρ)/ε is the piezoresistive part, a property of the material.
  7. Why a metal gauge has GF close to 2. For metals ν ≈ 0.3, so the geometric part is 1 + 2 × 0.3 = 1.6. In constantan (the usual foil alloy) the resistivity term adds about 0.4, giving GF ≈ 2.0 to 2.1. Even with no resistivity change at all, a material that kept its volume (ν = 0.5) would give exactly 1 + 1 = 2, so GF near 2 is what geometry alone predicts.

Model answer.

  1. Wire-wound construction. An insulating former (ceramic, glass-filled plastic or an enamelled metal mandrel) carries a single layer of fine resistance wire, usually nickel-chromium or a precious-metal alloy, wound with constant pitch. One edge of the winding is bared and a wiper of phosphor bronze or precious metal presses on it. The two ends of the winding and the wiper are the three terminals. Understand State and explain the three laws of thermoelectric circuits. Show how the law of intermediate metals allows copper leads to be used in a thermocouple circuit. 12 marks

Points to cover. Model answer.

  1. Set-up. A thermocouple is two dissimilar metals A and B joined at a hot (measuring) junction at Th and a cold (reference) junction at Tc. The net emf E depends on the two junction temperatures through the Seebeck coefficient S of the pair, E ≈ S(Th − Tc) over a small span. Three empirical laws let us use it in real circuits.
  2. Law 1: homogeneous circuit. A current cannot be sustained in a circuit of a single homogeneous metal by heat alone, however the temperature varies along it. Consequence: the emf of a thermocouple depends only on the junction temperatures, not on the temperature profile along the wires. A type K wire can pass through a 600 °C furnace wall without error, provided the wire is homogeneous; cold work, contamination or oxidation make it inhomogeneous and create spurious emfs.
  3. Law 2: intermediate metals. A third metal C inserted anywhere in the circuit adds no net emf if both of its junctions are at the same temperature. Consequence: a voltmeter, solder, copper terminals and copper leads can be put in the loop.
  4. Law 3: intermediate temperatures. If a pair gives E(T1, T2) between T1 and T2, and E(T2, T3) between T2 and T3, then E(T1, T3) = E(T1, T2) + E(T2, T3). Consequence: tables referred to 0 °C can be used with a cold junction at room temperature.

    E(300, 30) = E(300, 0) − E(30, 0) = 12.209 − 1.203 = 11.006 mV

E(Th, 0) = 11.00 + 1.20 = 12.20 mV  →  table  →  Th ≈ 300 °C

The terminal block is therefore the cold junction, and the copper leads, the switch contacts and the meter itself add nothing.

  4. RTD (Pt100). R(37 °C) = 100(1 + 0.00385 × 37) = 114.2 Ω; sensitivity 0.385 Ω/°C, 0.0385 Ω per 0.1 °C. At 1 mA that is only 38.5 µV per 0.1 °C. Excellent linearity, stability and repeatability; the element and sheath are larger and slower; cost is higher. The standard for a reference or calibration thermometer.
  5. Thermocouple (type T, copper–constantan). About 41 µV/°C near 37 °C, so 0.1 °C is 4.1 µV, close to amplifier offset and noise. It needs cold-junction compensation accurate to better than 0.1 °C, since a cold-junction error passes straight to the reading. Very small and robust; used in needle probes and catheters for tissue or hyperthermia studies, not routine bedside temperature.
  6. Self-heating and safety. Excitation must not warm the probe or tissue:
    Thermistor at 100 µA: P = (10⁻⁴)² × 6350 = 63.5 µW; in tissue at δ ≈ 2 mW/°C → 0.03 °C
Pt100 at 1 mA: P = (10⁻³)² × 114.2 = 0.11 mW → about 0.06 °C
Thermocouple: no excitation, no self-heating
  7. Electrical safety: the probe touches the patient, so the measuring circuit must be isolated and the leakage current kept in the microampere range; a low-voltage, low-current sensor helps. Probes in contact with mucous membranes are covered by a disposable sheath or are themselves disposable.

Evaluate Evaluate four methods of cold-junction compensation for a thermocouple measuring a furnace at about 900 °C: ice bath, isothermal block with a semiconductor sensor, bridge circuit with a temperature-sensitive resistor, and software compensation with a stored table. 14 marks

Points to cover. Ice bath: most accurate reference but impractical for plant use; Isothermal block with a thermistor or IC sensor: standard practice, good to about 0.5 °C; Compensating bridge in the instrument: simple but drifts and is hard to make accurate; Software with an ITS-90 table and a measured cold-junction value: flexible and now the norm; Also consider extension leads and their own thermoelectric voltages, and the law of intermediate metals; Conclusion: cold-junction error of 1 °C is a real error of about 1 °C at the hot junction because the emf is linear in the difference.

Model answer.

  1. Why compensation is needed. A thermocouple measures E(Th, Tc), not Th. With type K at 900 °C and the cold junction at 25 °C, table values give E(900, 0) = 37.326 mV and E(25, 0) = 1.000 mV, so the meter sees 36.326 mV. Read directly from the table this is 875 °C, 25 °C low. Every method below supplies the missing 1.000 mV (law of intermediate temperatures).
  2. 1. Ice bath. The reference junctions sit in a flask of crushed ice and pure water at 0 °C, so the reading is E(Th, 0) directly and the table applies with no correction. Merits: the most accurate reference, within about 0.1 °C if the ice is made properly and kept stirred; no electronics. Demerits: the ice melts, needs attention and cannot be used on a plant or in a portable instrument. Use: calibration laboratories.
  3. 2. Isothermal block with a semiconductor sensor. The thermocouple wires end on terminals mounted on a copper or aluminium block that keeps both terminals at one temperature (law of intermediate metals). A thermistor, RTD or IC temperature sensor on the block measures Tc, and the circuit adds E(Tc, 0). Merits: standard practice in transmitters and data loggers, typically good to about 0.5 °C. Demerits: the block must really be isothermal; heat from nearby electronics or a draught causes gradients.
  4. 3. Compensating bridge. A Wheatstone bridge in series with the thermocouple has one arm that is a temperature-sensitive resistor (copper or nickel) at the cold junction. As Tc rises the bridge output rises by about S·ΔTc, cancelling the loss in thermocouple emf. Merits: simple and analogue, needs no processor. Demerits: the resistor is near linear while the thermocouple emf is not, so it is exact only near its design temperature; it must be matched to one thermocouple type; the bridge supply and resistors drift. Errors of about 1 °C or more are common away from the design ambient.
  5. 4. Software compensation. The processor measures Tc on the block, converts it to E(Tc, 0) with the ITS-90 reference polynomial for the type in use, adds the measured emf, and inverts the polynomial to get Th. Merits: handles any thermocouple type by changing the table, is exact for the curve, and allows linearisation and diagnostics; it is now the norm. Demerits: still depends on the accuracy of the block sensor.
  6. Working for software compensation.
    Measured emf = 36.326 mV; block sensor reads Tc = 25.0 °C
E(25, 0) from the polynomial = 1.000 mV
E(Th, 0) = 36.326 + 1.000 = 37.326 mV  →  inverse polynomial  →  Th = 900.0 °C
  7. Cold-junction error passes straight through. If the block sensor reads 26 °C instead of 25 °C, the added emf is 1.041 mV, and the result is 901.0 °C. A 1 °C cold-junction error gives about a 1 °C error at 900 °C (1.01 °C, since the type K slope is 40.5 µV/°C at 25 °C and 40.0 µV/°C at 900 °C). The cold-junction sensor’s accuracy is a floor under the whole measurement.
  8. Extension leads. The junction head on a furnace may be at 60 °C while the instrument is at 25 °C. If plain copper wire ran from the head, the cold junction would be at 60 °C but compensated as 25 °C: the reading would be E(900) − E(60) + E(25) = 37.326 − 2.436 + 1.000 = 35.890 mV, about 864 °C, 36 °C low. Type K extension cable (same alloys) carries the cold junction to the instrument block. Reversing its polarity at one end gives a large error, about twice the emf between head and instrument.

At 900 °C the type K tolerance itself (±0.75 % of reading, about ±6.8 °C for standard grade) is larger than the 0.5 °C compensation error, so the cold junction is no longer the weakest link.

Glossary

Anemometer
Instrument for measuring fluid flow speed; the hot-wire form uses the cooling of a heated resistor.
Bridge excitation
The DC or AC supply voltage or current fed to a Wheatstone bridge, which sets the size of the output signal.
Creep
Slow change of sensor output under a steady load, caused by the adhesive or the elastic element settling.
Dummy gauge
An unstrained gauge mounted on the same material and placed in the adjacent bridge arm to cancel temperature effects.
Electrogoniometer
A joint-angle sensor, often a rotary potentiometer strapped across the joint.
Gauge length
Active length of the resistance grid along the measuring axis; strain is averaged over this length.
Hysteresis
Difference between the readings taken while loading and while unloading, for the same applied quantity.
LDR
Light-dependent resistor, another name for a photoresistor used in light-operated switches and exposure meters.
Linearisation
Any circuit or software step that converts a curved sensor characteristic into a straight line over the working range.
Nonlinearity
Maximum deviation of the sensor output from the best straight line, expressed as a percentage of full-scale output.
NTC thermistor
Negative temperature coefficient thermistor, whose resistance falls as temperature rises.
Piezoresistive effect
Change of a material's resistivity caused by mechanical stress, the effect that gives semiconductor gauges their large gauge factor.
Poisson's ratio (ν)
Ratio of sideways contraction to lengthwise extension of a stretched material, about 0.3 for steel and aluminium.
Quarter, half and full bridge
Bridge arrangements with one, two or four active gauge arms, giving output in the ratio 1 : 2 : 4.
Reference (cold) junction
The thermocouple junction held at a known temperature, whose value must be added to the measured emf.
Relative humidity
Water vapour actually present in air as a percentage of the maximum the air can hold at that temperature.
Seebeck coefficient (S)
Emf produced per degree of junction temperature difference, quoted in µV/°C and specific to each metal pair.
Self-heating
Rise in a sensor's own temperature caused by the measuring current, an error source in RTDs and thermistors.
Sensitivity
Change in output divided by the change in input that caused it, with units such as V/mm or Ω/°C.
Span
Difference between the upper and lower limits of the measurable range of a sensor.
Strain (ε)
Change in length divided by original length of a body, a dimensionless number usually quoted in microstrain (10⁻⁶).
Temperature coefficient of resistance (α)
Fractional change of resistance per degree Celsius, 0.00385 /°C for platinum and negative and large for an NTC thermistor.
Thermopile
Several thermocouples in series to multiply the emf, used in radiation sensors and gas detectors.
Three-wire connection
RTD wiring in which the lead resistances cancel in the bridge, leaving only the sensor resistance.
Unbonded strain gauge
Fine wires stretched between a frame and a moving armature, straining directly without being glued to a surface.
Wiper
The sliding contact of a potentiometer that taps a fraction of the track and carries the output voltage.
