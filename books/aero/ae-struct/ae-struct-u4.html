4.1 Symmetrical bending and anticlastic bending

A spar cap, a stringer or a fuselage frame is a beam, and the first questions are how much direct stress a bending moment causes and how much the beam curves. When the moment acts about a principal axis, one property, the second moment of area I, answers both: σ = M y/I and 1/R = M/(E I). The same bending also makes the cross-section curl the other way by the Poisson effect: anticlastic bending.

You need: the second moment of area and the parallel-axis theorem, and the bending moment diagram of a wing or fuselage (from the chapter on loads on an aircraft).

Assumptions and the bending formula

Bending is symmetrical when the moment acts about a principal axis of the section, usually an axis of symmetry. Simple bending theory rests on four assumptions:

  • Plane cross-sections stay plane and normal to the bent axis of the beam.
  • Deflections are small, so the curvature 1/R is close to d²v/dz².
  • The material is linear elastic, with the same E in tension and compression.
  • The moment acts about a principal axis, and no axial force acts.

The derivation follows from these in four steps:

  1. A fibre at distance y from the neutral axis stretches by y/R per unit length, so the strain is ε = y/R.
  2. Hooke's law gives σ = E ε = E y/R: stress grows linearly with y.
  3. No axial force acts, so ∫σ dA = (E/R) ∫y dA = 0. The first moment ∫y dA is zero only about the centroid, so the neutral axis passes through the centroid.
  4. The stresses must add up to the applied moment: M = ∫σ y dA = (E/R) ∫y² dA = E I/R, where I = ∫y² dA about the neutral axis. Put E/R = M/I into σ = E y/R.
σ/y = M/I = E/R,   σmax = M/Z,   Z = I/ymax

σ is the direct stress (Pa) at distance y (m) from the neutral axis; M is the bending moment (N·m); I is the second moment of area about the neutral axis (m⁴); E is Young's modulus (Pa); R is the radius of curvature of the beam axis (m); Z is the elastic section modulus (m³) and ymax the distance to the farthest fibre (m).

Doubling the depth of a rectangular section multiplies I by eight and ymax by two, so the peak stress falls to a quarter. That is why spars are deep and skins thin.

Sign convention and sections that are not symmetric top to bottom

This unit uses one convention throughout: z along the beam, x horizontal and y upwards from the centroid, tension positive. Mx is positive when it puts the fibres at positive y in tension, and My when it puts positive x in tension. Then σ = Mx y/Ixx and the sign comes out of the formula. Upward lift compresses a wing's upper skin, so its root Mx is negative; so is the Mx of a beam sagging under a downward load.

A section not symmetric about its neutral axis, such as a T-section, has two section moduli, Ztop = I/ytop and Zbot = I/ybot. Yielding starts at the fibre farther from the neutral axis (the smaller Z), on whichever face it lies. The compression face matters separately, because thin compressed skin buckles before it yields (see the chapters on plate buckling and crippling).

Anticlastic bending: the saddle

A stretched fibre contracts sideways by ν ε, where ν is Poisson's ratio (about 0.33 for aluminium alloy, 0.3 for steel); a compressed fibre widens. In a bent beam the strain is y/R, so the sideways strain is −ν y/R: it changes sign across the depth. The tension side narrows and the compression side widens, and the cross-section curls in its own plane with the opposite sense to the bending.

1/R′ = ν/R, so R′ = R/ν

R′ is the transverse (anticlastic) radius of curvature of the cross-section (m); R is the radius of the beam axis (m); ν is Poisson's ratio.

For aluminium R′ is about three times R: bend a rubber eraser and its end face stops being rectangular. The bar becomes a saddle.

The curl is free only on a narrow strip. In a wide plate or a stiffened wing box the skin, stringers and ribs hold the section flat, the sideways strain is prevented, and a transverse stress σt = ν σ appears instead, with the same sign as σ: the tension skin is held wider than it wants to be. The skin then works biaxially, which matters for fatigue and buckling. σ = M y/I is unchanged; only the shape and the transverse stress differ. A wide plate held flat is also stiffer by 1/(1 − ν²) (see pure bending of thin plates).

Worked example

Question. An aluminium alloy beam of symmetric I-section has E = 70 GPa, ν = 0.33, I = 8.5 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴ and depth 200 mm. A moment of 20 kN·m acts about its horizontal centroidal axis. Find (a) the largest direct stress, (b) the radius of curvature of the beam axis and (c) the free anticlastic radius of the cross-section.

  1. The section is symmetric, so the neutral axis is at mid-depth: ymax = 200/2 = 100 mm = 0.100 m.
  2. SI units: M = 2.0 × 10⁴ N·m, E = 7.0 × 10¹⁰ Pa.
  3. (a) σmax = M ymax/I = (2.0 × 10⁴ × 0.100)/(8.5 × 10⁻⁶) = 2.353 × 10⁸ Pa = 235.3 MPa, tension on one face and compression on the other.
  4. Check with the section modulus: Z = I/ymax = 8.5 × 10⁻⁵ m³, and M/Z = 2.0 × 10⁴/(8.5 × 10⁻⁵) = 235.3 MPa.
  5. (b) R = E I/M = (7.0 × 10¹⁰ × 8.5 × 10⁻⁶)/(2.0 × 10⁴) = 29.75 m.
  6. Strain check: ε = ymax/R = 0.100/29.75 = 3.36 × 10⁻³, and σ/E = 235.3 × 10⁶/(7.0 × 10¹⁰) = 3.36 × 10⁻³. They agree.
  7. (c) R′ = R/ν = 29.75/0.33 = 90.2 m, about three times R.

Answer. σmax = 235.3 MPa, R = 29.75 m and R′ = 90.2 m.

Worked example 2

Question. A T-section spar cap has a flange 100 mm × 10 mm on top and a web 10 mm thick and 90 mm deep below it (total depth 100 mm). It carries a sagging moment of 3 kN·m (bottom fibres in tension), so Mx = −3 kN·m in this book's convention. Find the neutral axis, I, the two section moduli and the top and bottom stresses. Which face yields first?

  1. Areas: flange A₁ = 1000 mm² with centroid 5 mm below the top; web A₂ = 900 mm² with centroid 10 + 45 = 55 mm below the top.
  2. Centroid from the top: ȳ = (1000 × 5 + 900 × 55)/1900 = 28.68 mm. So ytop = 28.68 mm and ybot = 100 − 28.68 = 71.32 mm.
  3. I = [100 × 10³/12 + 1000 × (28.68 − 5)²] + [10 × 90³/12 + 900 × (55 − 28.68)²] = 569,275 + 1,230,769 = 1,800,044 mm⁴.
  4. Ztop = I/ytop = 62,754 mm³; Zbot = I/ybot = 25,240 mm³.
  5. Top fibre, y = +28.68 mm: σ = Mx y/I = (−3 × 10⁶ × 28.68)/1,800,044 = -47.8 N/mm² (compression).
  6. Bottom fibre, y = −71.32 mm: σ = (−3 × 10⁶)(−71.32)/1,800,044 = +118.9 N/mm² (tension).

Exam traps

  • Measuring y from an outer face or the bottom of the web instead of from the centroidal neutral axis.
  • Using σ = M y/I when the moment is not about a principal axis; for a section with Ixy ≠ 0 use the methods of unsymmetrical bending.
  • Mixing units: M in kN·m with I in mm⁴. Work in N and mm (stress in N/mm² = MPa) or in N and m throughout.
  • Writing R′ = νR instead of R/ν. The anticlastic radius is larger than R, not smaller.
  • Giving the restrained transverse stress as −νσ. When the curl is prevented the transverse stress is +νσ, the same sign as σ.

4.2 Unsymmetrical bending: stresses and neutral axis

A Z-section stringer, an angle or a wing box with unequal spar caps has no axis of symmetry. A moment about the horizontal axis then also produces stress that varies across the width, the neutral axis tilts, and σ = M y/I is wrong. The missing quantity is the product second moment Ixy. This chapter gives the general formula and the three ways examiners ask for it, the k, neutral axis and principal axis methods, which all give the same stress.

You need: the sign convention and σ = M y/I from symmetrical bending, and the section properties Ixx, Iyy and Ixy (worked out for this chapter's Z-section in thin-walled section properties).

The product second moment

Ixy = ∫ x y dA

x and y are centroidal coordinates (mm or m); dA is an element of area; Ixy is in mm⁴ or m⁴. It can be positive, negative or zero.

Ixy is positive when more area lies in the first and third quadrants. With even one axis of symmetry every element at (x, y) has a partner at (−x, y) or (x, −y), the products cancel and Ixy = 0: the centroidal axes are principal. A Z-section's flanges sit in the first and third quadrants, so its Ixy is large and positive. The parallel-axis rule holds for Ixy too: Ixy = Ix′y′ + A x̄ ȳ.

The general formula and the k method

Plane sections stay plane, so σ = k₁ x + k₂ y. The moment conditions Mx = ∫σ y dA and My = ∫σ x dA give Mx = k₁ Ixy + k₂ Ixx and My = k₁ Iyy + k₂ Ixy. Solving:

σ = [(My Ixx − Mx Ixy)/D] x + [(Mx Iyy − My Ixy)/D] y,   D = Ixx Iyy − Ixy²

Mx and My are the bending moments (N·mm) with the sign convention of symmetrical bending; x and y locate the point from the centroid (mm); D is in mm⁸. With Ixy = 0 it reduces to σ = Mx y/Ixx + My x/Iyy.

The k method writes the same formula with three section constants found once per section, ka = Iyy/D, kb = Ixx/D and kc = Ixy/D (books number them differently): σ = (ka Mx − kc My) y + (kb My − kc Mx) x.

tan α = −k₁/k₂ = (Mx Ixy − My Ixx)/(Mx Iyy − My Ixy)

α is the angle of the neutral axis (the line σ = 0 through the centroid), measured from the x axis towards y. With My = 0 it is tan α = Ixy/Iyy.

Under Mx alone a symmetric section has its neutral axis along x, the moment axis. With Ixy ≠ 0 it rotates towards the direction in which the section is most flexible.

The neutral axis method

Once α is known, the stress is proportional to the distance n from the neutral axis:

  1. Find α from the formula above.
  2. Find the second moment about the neutral axis: INA = Ixx cos²α + Iyy sin²α − 2 Ixy sin α cos α.
  3. Find the component of the applied moment about the neutral axis: MNA = Mx cos α − My sin α.
  4. At a point (x, y), n = y cos α − x sin α, and σ = MNA n/INA. The farthest point from the neutral axis carries the largest stress.

The principal axis method

Rotate to the axes u and v where the product second moment vanishes; each moment component then bends the section symmetrically.

tan 2θ = −2 Ixy/(Ixx − Iyy),   Iu, Iv = (Ixx + Iyy)/2 ± √[((Ixx − Iyy)/2)² + Ixy²]

θ is the angle from x to the u axis; Iu = ∫v² dA and Iv = ∫u² dA are the principal second moments (about u and about v).

u = x cos θ + y sin θ,  v = y cos θ − x sin θ;  Mu = Mx cos θ − My sin θ,  Mv = Mx sin θ + My cos θ;  σ = Mu v/Iu + Mv u/Iv

u and v are the coordinates of the point on the principal axes; Mu and Mv are the moment components about u and v.

Check the angle by putting θ back into Iuv = ((Ixx − Iyy)/2) sin 2θ + Ixy cos 2θ: it must come out zero.

Worked example

Question. A Z-section stringer has a 100 mm web and two 50 mm flanges, all 2 mm thick; the top flange points in +x and the bottom flange in −x. Its centroidal properties are Ixx = 666,667 mm⁴, Iyy = 166,667 mm⁴ and Ixy = +250,000 mm⁴. It carries Mx = +1.0 kN·m and My = 0. Find the neutral axis and the stress at the top web corner A (0, 50) mm and at the top flange tip B (50, 50) mm by the k method and by the neutral axis method.

  1. D = Ixx Iyy − Ixy² = 666,667 × 166,667 − 250,000² = 4.861 × 10¹⁰ mm⁸.
  2. k₁ = (My Ixx − Mx Ixy)/D = (0 − 10⁶ × 250,000)/4.861 × 10¹⁰ = -5.143 N/mm³.
  3. k₂ = (Mx Iyy − My Ixy)/D = (10⁶ × 166,667)/4.861 × 10¹⁰ = 3.429 N/mm³.
  4. Point A: σ = -5.143 × 0 + 3.429 × 50 = 171.4 MPa. Point B: σ = -5.143 × 50 + 3.429 × 50 = -85.7 MPa.
  5. Neutral axis: tan α = −k₁/k₂ = 1.500, so α = 56.3° from the x axis. Ignoring Ixy would have put it at 0°.
  6. Neutral axis method: INA = 89,744 mm⁴ and MNA = Mx cos α = 554,700 N·mm. At A, n = 50 cos α = 27.74 mm and σ = MNA n/INA = 171.4 MPa; at B, n = -13.87 mm and σ = -85.7 MPa.

Answer. Neutral axis at 56.3°; +171.4 MPa at the web corner A and -85.7 MPa at the flange tip B (by antisymmetry −171.4 MPa at the bottom corner). Mx y/Ixx would give 75.0 MPa at both.

Exam traps

  • Using σ = Mx y/Ixx on a Z or angle section: 75.0 MPa at the web corner instead of 171.4 MPa.
  • Taking x and y from a corner instead of the centroid.
  • Dropping the sign of Ixy. A Z-section with flanges in the second and fourth quadrants has Ixy < 0, and the neutral axis tilts the other way.
  • Assuming the largest stress is at the flange tip. In the worked example it is at the web–flange corner, the point farthest from the tilted neutral axis.

4.3 Deflection due to unsymmetrical bending

A beam whose section has Ixy ≠ 0 does not deflect in the direction of the load. Push a Z-section cantilever straight down and its tip moves down and sideways, often more sideways than down. The rule is simple: the beam deflects at right angles to its neutral axis. This chapter finds the two deflection components from the moments, then by the principal axes, and ends with a short program for the deflected shape.

You need: the general stress formula and the neutral axis from unsymmetrical bending, and the cantilever and simply supported deflection formulas of symmetrical beams (from the chapter on energy methods for beams).

Curvatures from the moments

Let u(z) and v(z) be the deflections of the centroid in the +x and +y directions. A fibre at (x, y) then has strain ε = −x u″ − y v″ (a prime means d/dz). Putting σ = E ε into Mx = ∫σ y dA and My = ∫σ x dA and solving for the two curvatures gives:

u″ = (Mx Ixy − My Ixx)/(E D),   v″ = (My Ixy − Mx Iyy)/(E D),   D = Ixx Iyy − Ixy²

u″ and v″ are the curvatures (1/mm) in the xz and yz planes; Mx, My are in N·mm with this unit's sign convention; E is in N/mm²; I in mm⁴.

Comparing with the stress formula σ = k₁ x + k₂ y shows u″ = −k₁/E and v″ = −k₂/E. The curvature vector (u″, v″) is therefore along (−k₁, −k₂), which is perpendicular to the neutral axis k₁ x + k₂ y = 0. Every station moves at right angles to the neutral axis. With Ixy = 0 the formulas fall back to v″ = −Mx/(E Ixx) and u″ = −My/(E Iyy).

Integrate each curvature twice along the span, as for an ordinary beam. The constants come from the supports: u = v = 0 and u′ = v′ = 0 at a built-in end; u = v = 0 at a simple support. For a cantilever of length L with a downward tip load W, Mx = W(L − z) and My = 0, so the tip deflections are u = W L³ Ixy/(3 E D) and v = −W L³ Iyy/(3 E D).

The principal axes route

The second route resolves the load instead of the curvature. On the principal axes u and v (found as in unsymmetrical bending) the product second moment is zero, so the beam behaves as two independent symmetric beams:

  1. Resolve the load into components Wu along the u axis and Wv along the v axis.
  2. A load along u bends the beam about the v axis, so its deflection uses Iv: δu = Wu L³/(3 E Iv) for a tip load. Likewise δv = Wv L³/(3 E Iu).
  3. Add δu and δv as vectors and, if needed, rotate back to x and y.

Other supports change only the coefficient (L³/48 for a central load on a simple span, L⁴/8 per unit load for a uniform load on a cantilever). Both planes share the same shape, so the elastic curve lies in one plane.

Deflection shape by computer

When the load or section varies along the span, compute Mx(z) and My(z) at many stations, turn them into u″ and v″, and integrate twice numerically. The second worked example does this in Python; the same lines work in MATLAB. Check that the root values are zero and that the tip matches a closed-form case.

Worked example

Question. The Z-section of the unsymmetrical bending chapter (Ixx = 666,667 mm⁴, Iyy = 166,667 mm⁴, Ixy = 250,000 mm⁴) is an aluminium cantilever, E = 70 GPa, L = 1.2 m, with a downward tip load W = 500 N through the centroid. Find the tip deflection, its direction, and the error made by ignoring Ixy. Check by the principal axes.

  1. In N and mm: L³ = 1.728 × 10⁹ mm³, D = 4.861 × 10¹⁰ mm⁸; Mx = W(L − z), positive (tension at the top), My = 0.
  2. Vertical: v = −W L³ Iyy/(3 E D) = −500 × 1.728 × 10⁹ × 166,667/(3 × 70,000 × 4.861 × 10¹⁰) = -14.11 mm.
  3. Sideways: u = W L³ Ixy/(3 E D) = 500 × 1.728 × 10⁹ × 250,000/(3 × 70,000 × 4.861 × 10¹⁰) = +21.16 mm.
  4. Total: √(21.16² + -14.11²) = 25.43 mm at -33.7° from the x axis, that is 33.7° below horizontal. The neutral axis is at +56.3°, and 56.3° − 90° = −33.7°: the movement is perpendicular to it.
  5. Ignoring Ixy: v = −W L³/(3 E Ixx) = -6.17 mm and no sideways movement.
  6. Principal check: θ = −22.5°, Iu = 770,220 mm⁴, Iv = 63,113 mm⁴. The load (0, −500) resolves into Wu = −W sin θ = 191.3 N and Wv = −W cos θ = -461.9 N.
  7. δu = Wu L³/(3 E Iv) = 24.95 mm and δv = Wv L³/(3 E Iu) = -4.94 mm. Rotating back: x = δu cos θ − δv sin θ = 21.16 mm, y = δu sin θ + δv cos θ = -14.11 mm. Same answer.

Answer. The tip moves 21.16 mm sideways and 14.11 mm down, 25.43 mm in all, at 33.7° below horizontal, perpendicular to the neutral axis. Ignoring Ixy gives only 6.17 mm down.

Worked example 2

Question. The same cantilever carries a uniform downward load w = 0.8 N/mm (800 N/m) instead of the tip load. Compute the deflected shape at five stations with a short program, and check the tip against the closed form u = w L⁴ Ixy/(8 E D), v = −w L⁴ Iyy/(8 E D).

Python program.

# Z-section cantilever, uniform load (N, mm)
E, L, w = 70000.0, 1200.0, 0.8            # N/mm², mm, N/mm (800 N/m)
Ixx, Iyy, Ixy = 666667.0, 166667.0, 250000.0
D = Ixx * Iyy - Ixy ** 2
n = 1200                                  # 1 mm steps
dz = L / n
ku = []; kv = []
for i in range(n + 1):
    z = i * dz
    Mx = w * (L - z) ** 2 / 2
    ku.append(Mx * Ixy / (E * D))         # u'' (sideways)
    kv.append(-Mx * Iyy / (E * D))        # v'' (vertical)
def integrate(k):                          # trapezium rule from the root
    s = [0.0]; d = [0.0]
    for i in range(1, len(k)):
        s.append(s[-1] + (k[i - 1] + k[i]) / 2 * dz)
        d.append(d[-1] + (s[i - 1] + s[i]) / 2 * dz)
    return d
u, v = integrate(ku), integrate(kv)
for z in (300, 600, 1200):
    i = int(z / dz)
    print(f"z = {z:4d} mm   u = {u[i]:6.2f} mm   v = {v[i]:7.2f} mm")

4.4 Thin-walled section approximations and section properties

An aircraft section is mostly thin sheet: skins about 1 to 3 mm thick, webs and stringer flanges a few millimetres. Thin walls allow big simplifications. The stress hardly changes across the thickness, so each wall can be drawn as its mid-line with a thickness t, and terms in t³ can be dropped. This chapter sets out those approximations, gives the sums that turn walls and stringers into Ixx, Iyy and Ixy, works out the section properties of an angle and of the Z-section used in the previous chapters, and says where simple bending theory stops being accurate.

You need: the parallel-axis theorem and σ = M y/I from symmetrical bending, and the product second moment from unsymmetrical bending.

The thin-wall approximations

A wall is thin when its thickness t is small compared with its length s; t/s below about 0.1 covers most skins, webs and stringer flanges. Then:

  • The direct stress is taken as uniform across t, and the wall is drawn as its mid-line.
  • Lengths are measured along the mid-line, and the small overlap of material at corners is ignored.
  • Terms in t² and t³ are dropped against terms in t. A wall therefore has no second moment about its own mid-line.
  • Stringers are replaced by booms: concentrated areas at their centroids, with no second moment of their own.

For a straight wall of length s and thickness t at angle θ to the x axis, the second moments about axes through its own centroid are:

Ixx,0 = t s³ sin²θ/12,   Iyy,0 = t s³ cos²θ/12,   Ixy,0 = t s³ sin θ cos θ/12

t and s in mm give mm⁴. A horizontal wall (θ = 0) has Ixx,0 = 0 and Iyy,0 = t s³/12; a vertical wall (θ = 90°) the reverse. For a curved wall the same idea becomes an integral along the mid-line, for example t r³ ∫sin²φ dφ for a circular arc of radius r.

Section properties step by step

  1. Draw the mid-line, number the walls and booms, and choose any reference axes.
  2. Find the centroid: x̄ = Σ a x/Σ a and ȳ = Σ a y/Σ a, where a is each wall area t s or boom area A.
  3. For each wall, add its own term to the parallel-axis term about the centroid: Ixx = Σ(Ixx,0 + a yc²), Iyy = Σ(Iyy,0 + a xc²), Ixy = Σ(Ixy,0 + a xc yc), where (xc, yc) is the wall centroid measured from the section centroid.
  4. Add the booms: A y², A x² and A x y about the centroid.
  5. Keep the signs of xc and yc: they decide the sign of Ixy.

A table with one row per wall keeps the work clear and is what examiners expect. The same properties serve open and closed sections: the direct stress depends only on A, Ixx, Iyy and Ixy. Shear flow and torsion differ, and the chapter on structural idealisation in shear flow shows how skin areas are lumped into booms for shear analysis.

Where bending theory stops being accurate

Some syllabi ask for the applicability of bending theory. The thin-walled answer assumes plane sections, uniform stress across each wall and elastic material. Three effects break those assumptions in aircraft panels:

  • Shear lag: in a wide skin the direct stress is higher near the webs and lower midway between them, so the peak stress exceeds the beam value. Designers use an effective width.
  • Local buckling and crippling: a thin compressed wall buckles below yield and then carries less load; see the chapters on plate buckling and crippling stress.
  • Plasticity near ultimate load redistributes stress.

Use the thin-walled answer for sizing and first stress estimates, then check it with tests or finite elements.

4.5 Bending of sections with stringers and skew loads

A wing box or fuselage shell is idealised as booms that carry the direct stress, joined by thin skin. With the booms in place, bending a stringer-stiffened section is the unsymmetrical bending problem solved one boom at a time. Two things make it an exam question: the load is skew (lift and drag together give moments about both axes) and the boom areas are usually unequal, which moves the centroid and makes Ixy non-zero. Only a few universities teach this as a separate topic.

You need: the general formula from unsymmetrical bending, the sums of thin-walled section properties, and the shear force and bending moment distribution along a wing (from the chapter on loads on an aircraft).

Boom model and its section properties

Each stringer with its strip of effective skin becomes a boom of area Ai at (xi, yi); the skin between booms is taken to carry only shear. Then:

  1. Find the boom centroid: x̄ = ΣAi xi/ΣAi, ȳ = ΣAi yi/ΣAi.
  2. Measure every boom from it and sum Ixx = ΣAi yi², Iyy = ΣAi xi², Ixy = ΣAi xi yi.
  3. If the layout and the areas are symmetric about at least one centroidal axis, Ixy = 0. Unequal areas on a rectangular layout break the symmetry and give Ixy ≠ 0.

A symmetric section with a skew load needs only σ = Mx y/Ixx + My x/Iyy. An unsymmetric section needs the full formula with Ixy.

Skew loads on a wing and a fuselage

Lift acts upwards and drag acts aft, so a wing section carries moments about both axes. Take z along the span, x aft and y up. Upward lift outboard of a section puts the lower booms in tension, so Mx is negative in this book's convention; aft drag puts the front booms (negative x) in tension, so My is also negative. For a uniform load w per metre over the outboard length l, the moment at the section is w l²/2. A fuselage under a tail load and inertia is treated the same way, with the booms placed round the shell.

The procedure at one station

  1. Find Mx and My at the station from the load distribution, with their signs.
  2. Find the boom centroid and Ixx, Iyy, Ixy.
  3. Compute k₁ = (My Ixx − Mx Ixy)/D and k₂ = (Mx Iyy − My Ixy)/D.
  4. Evaluate σi = k₁ xi + k₂ yi at every boom and check that ΣAi σi = 0.

4.6 Temperature effects in bending

A wing skin in the sun, a fuselage frame beside a hot bleed-air duct and a supersonic skin heated by the airflow all carry temperatures that change across the section. Material that is hotter wants to be longer. If the free thermal strain fits a plane section, the beam simply bends or stretches and no stress arises; whatever does not fit, or whatever the supports prevent, becomes stress. This chapter turns a temperature field into a free curvature and into thermal stresses, for a solid spar and for a boom section. Only a few universities teach it.

You need: plane sections and curvature from symmetrical bending, and the general formula of unsymmetrical bending.

Free thermal strain and free curvature

A temperature rise T (K) gives a free strain α T, where α is the coefficient of thermal expansion (about 23 × 10⁻⁶ per K for aluminium, 12 × 10⁻⁶ per K for steel). If T varies linearly through the depth h, the free strain is linear too, which is exactly what a plane section can follow. The beam bends with no stress at all:

1/RT = α (Ttop − Tbot)/h

RT is the free thermal radius of curvature (m); h is the depth (m); Ttop and Tbot are the face temperatures (K or °C, only the difference matters). The hot face becomes convex.

Restrained beams and non-linear temperature

In general the actual strain ε is plane, ε = ε₀ + y/R (plus an x term), and the stress is σ = E(ε − α T). The beam settles where the stresses give the external force and moments. Two cases are common:

  • Curvature prevented, axial extension free (a spar held straight by its supports): ε₀ is the mean of α T, and σ = E α (Tmean − T). For a linear field the faces carry ±E α ΔT/2, the hot face in compression. These stresses have zero net force but a net moment, which the supports must supply.
  • Free beam, non-linear field: the beam takes up the best-fitting plane strain, and only the part of α T that is not linear in x and y becomes stress. This stress has zero net force and zero net moments: it is self-equilibrating.

For a boom section the free case has a neat recipe. Work out the thermal force and moments, then subtract the free strain the boom cannot have:

NT = Σ E α Ti Ai,  MxT = Σ E α Ti Ai yi,  MyT = Σ E α Ti Ai xi;  σi = −E α Ti + NT/A + (general formula with MxT, MyT)

Ti is the temperature rise of boom i, Ai its area and (xi, yi) its position from the centroid; A is the total boom area. With Ixy = 0 the last term is MxT yi/Ixx + MyT xi/Iyy.

Worked example

Question. An aluminium spar is 100 mm deep, E = 70 GPa, α = 23 × 10⁻⁶ per K. The top flange is at 35 °C and the bottom flange at 5 °C, with a linear variation between. Find (a) the free radius and (b) the face stresses if the supports hold the spar straight but leave it free to extend.

  1. ΔT = 35 − 5 = 30 K; h = 0.100 m.
  2. (a) 1/RT = α ΔT/h = 23 × 10⁻⁶ × 30/0.100 = 6.90 × 10⁻³ per m, so RT = 145 m. The hot top becomes convex: the spar bows upwards.
  3. (b) The mean temperature is 20 °C, so the faces are 15 K above and below the mean.
  4. σ = E α (Tmean − T) = 70 × 10⁹ × 23 × 10⁻⁶ × (∓15) = ∓24.15 MPa.
  5. Top (hot) face: −24.15 MPa, compression. Bottom (cool) face: +24.15 MPa, tension. The supports supply the moment E α (ΔT/h) I that holds the spar straight.

Answer. RT = 145 m when free; held straight, the faces carry −24.15 MPa (hot top) and +24.15 MPa (cool bottom).

Exam traps

  • Using σ = E α ΔT on a free beam with a linear gradient. A free beam bows stress-free.
  • Forgetting the factor 1/2: a spar held straight carries ±E α ΔT/2 at its faces, not E α ΔT.
  • Calling the held-straight stress self-equilibrating. Its net force is zero but its net moment is not; the supports supply it.
  • Putting h in mm when the curvature is wanted per metre.
  • Leaving out the −E α Ti term in a boom section; the formula then gives the thermal "moment stress" only.

Takeaways

  1. About a principal axis, σ = M y/I and 1/R = M/(E I); the neutral axis passes through the centroid.
  2. Poisson's ratio curls a free cross-section to R′ = R/ν; held flat, the skin carries a transverse stress νσ.
  3. With Ixy ≠ 0 the stress is σ = k₁ x + k₂ y and the neutral axis tilts towards the flexible direction.
  4. The k, neutral axis and principal axis methods are one formula; each gives the same stress at a point.
  5. The beam deflects perpendicular to its neutral axis, so a Z-section loaded downwards moves sideways too.
  6. Thin walls are mid-lines with t³ terms dropped; offset walls need their parallel-axis terms, and the signs of xc and yc set the sign of Ixy.
  7. Unequal booms on a rectangle give Ixy ≠ 0; lift and drag give negative Mx and My at a wing station in this convention.
  8. A linear temperature field bends a free beam without stress; restraint or a non-linear field produces stress.

Understand Derive the bending formula σ/y = M/I = E/R for symmetrical bending, stating every assumption, and define the section modulus. 10 marks

Points to cover. Assumptions; Strain ε = y/R from geometry; σ = E y/R by Hooke's law; Zero axial force puts the neutral axis at the centroid; Moment equilibrium gives M = E I/R; Z = I/ymax and two moduli for unsymmetric depth; Limits.

Model answer.

  1. Assumptions: plane sections remain plane and normal to the bent axis; deflections are small; the material is linear elastic with the same E in tension and compression; the moment acts about a principal axis; no axial force acts.
  2. Geometry. Two sections a distance dz apart rotate through dφ about the neutral axis, whose radius is R. A fibre at distance y from the neutral axis has length (R + y) dφ after bending, against R dφ before, so its strain is:
    ε = [(R + y) dφ − R dφ]/(R dφ) = y/R
  3. Stress. Hooke's law gives σ = E ε = E y/R: the stress varies linearly with y and is zero on the neutral axis.
  4. Force equilibrium. With no axial force:
    ∫σ dA = (E/R) ∫y dA = 0  →  ∫y dA = 0
  5. The first moment of area is zero only about the centroid, so the neutral axis passes through the centroid of the section.
  6. Moment equilibrium. The stresses must add up to the applied moment:
    M = ∫σ y dA = (E/R) ∫y² dA = E I/R
  7. Combine the two results (E/R = σ/y and E/R = M/I):
    σ/y = M/I = E/R,   σ = M y/I,   1/R = M/(E I)
  8. Each term: σ is the direct stress at distance y from the neutral axis (N/mm²), M the bending moment (N·mm), I the second moment of area about the neutral axis (mm⁴), E Young's modulus (N/mm²) and R the radius of curvature (mm).
  9. Section modulus. The largest stress is at the farthest fibre: σmax = M ymax/I = M/Z with Z = I/ymax. A section that is not symmetric about its neutral axis has two moduli, Ztop and Zbot, and the smaller one governs yield.
  10. Limits in an aircraft structure: the moment must act about a principal axis (otherwise use the unsymmetrical formula); shear lag in wide skins, local buckling of thin compressed walls and plasticity near ultimate load all make the real peak stress differ from M y/I.
  11. Sign convention: with y measured upwards and M positive when it puts positive y in tension, σ = M y/I gives tension above and compression below the neutral axis for M > 0, and the opposite for M < 0; a wing with upward lift has negative M at the root.
  12. Check with numbers: for I = 8.5 × 10⁶ mm⁴, ymax = 100 mm and M = 20 kN·m, σmax = 2 × 10⁷ × 100/8.5 × 10⁶ = 235.3 N/mm², and for E = 70,000 N/mm² the radius is R = E I/M = 29,750 mm, with ymax/R = 3.36 × 10⁻³ = σ/E, as the formula requires.
Understand What is anticlastic bending? Derive the relation between the anticlastic and the longitudinal curvature, and explain what happens when the curl is restrained. 8 marks

Points to cover. Poisson strain −ν y/R; 1/R′ = ν/R, opposite sense, saddle; Free on narrow strips only; Restrained: σt = νσ; Effect on fatigue and buckling.

Model answer.

  1. Definition. Anticlastic bending is the curvature of the cross-section, in its own plane, in the opposite sense to the longitudinal curvature of the beam.
  2. Origin. A bent beam has longitudinal strain ε = y/R. By Poisson's ratio ν, every fibre also strains sideways by −ν ε = −ν y/R. The tension side (y > 0 for a positive moment) contracts sideways and the compression side expands.
  3. Curvature of the section. The sideways strain varies linearly with y, exactly as the longitudinal strain does, so the section bends sideways with a curvature equal to the gradient of the sideways strain:
    d(sideways strain)/dy = −ν/R   →   1/R′ = ν/R,   R′ = R/ν
  4. The minus sign is the opposite sense: a beam bending into a valley along its length bends into a hill across its width. The surface is a saddle.
  5. Size. For aluminium (ν = 0.33) R′ ≈ 3R. In the worked example R = 29.75 m gives R′ = 29.75/0.33 = 90.2 m. On a 2 mm strip bent to R = 20 m with ν = 0.30, R′ = 66.7 m and the face strain is ±1.5 × 10⁻⁵ sideways.
  6. When the curl is free. Only on a narrow strip or near a free edge, cut-out or hole.
  7. When it is restrained. In a wide plate or a stiffened box the skin, stringers and ribs keep the section flat. The sideways strain must then be zero:
    εt = (σt − ν σ)/E = 0   →   σt = ν σ
  8. The transverse stress has the same sign as the bending stress: the tension skin also carries transverse tension, and the compression skin transverse compression. The skin works biaxially, which affects fatigue at fasteners and the buckling of compression panels. A wide plate held flat is also stiffer than a beam by the factor 1/(1 − ν²).
  9. Why it matters in an exam answer: the relation 1/R′ = ν/R is used to explain why a bent ruler or a thin aircraft panel looks saddle-shaped, why strain gauges on a bent plate read a sideways strain, and why the plate formula uses D = E t³/[12(1 − ν²)] instead of E I: a wide plate cannot curl, so it is stiffer than a narrow beam of the same depth.
  10. Check: σ = M y/I is unchanged in both cases; only the shape of the section and the transverse stress differ.
Understand Explain why the neutral axis of a section with Ixy ≠ 0 does not lie along the axis of the applied moment. Derive tan α for the general case. 10 marks

Points to cover. Symmetrical case for comparison; σ = k₁x + k₂y and the two moment conditions; k₁, k₂ and D; tan α = −k₁/k₂; Physical reason and the Z-section example; Consequences for stress and deflection.

Model answer.

  1. In symmetrical bending under Mx alone, the stress is Mx y/Ixx and the neutral axis is the x axis, the axis the moment acts about. This holds only when x is a principal axis, Ixy = 0.
  2. General case. Plane sections give a stress linear in x and y, σ = k₁ x + k₂ y (zero at the centroid since there is no axial force). The moment conditions are:
    Mx = ∫σ y dA = k₁ Ixy + k₂ Ixx
My = ∫σ x dA = k₁ Iyy + k₂ Ixy
  3. Solving the two equations:
    k₁ = (My Ixx − Mx Ixy)/D,   k₂ = (Mx Iyy − My Ixy)/D,   D = Ixx Iyy − Ixy²
  4. Neutral axis. Setting σ = 0 gives a straight line through the centroid:
    k₁ x + k₂ y = 0   →   tan α = y/x = −k₁/k₂ = (Mx Ixy − My Ixx)/(Mx Iyy − My Ixy)
  5. With My = 0, tan α = Ixy/Iyy. The neutral axis therefore lies along the moment axis (α = 0) only when Ixy = 0. Any product second moment tilts it.
  6. Physical picture. The section is coupled: a moment about x alone must produce a stress varying with x, because otherwise the stresses would give a moment about y. The stress field rotates towards the direction in which the section is most flexible.
  7. Example. For the Z-section with Ixx = 666,667 mm⁴, Iyy = 166,667 mm⁴ and Ixy = 250,000 mm⁴ under Mx alone, tan α = 250,000/166,667 = 1.5 and α = 56.3°. The neutral axis is far from the x axis.
  8. Consequence for stress. The largest stress is at the point farthest from the tilted neutral axis. For the Z-section that is the web–flange corner (171.4 MPa for Mx = 1 kN·m), not the flange tip (−85.7 MPa), and the simple formula would have given 75.0 MPa at both.
  9. Special case of a moment about a principal axis: if the moment acts about a principal axis (u or v), the stress is simply M y/I about that axis and the neutral axis lies along it, even when Ixy ≠ 0 about the original x and y axes.
  10. Consequence for deflection. The beam deflects at right angles to the neutral axis, so it does not move in the plane of the load.
Understand State the approximations used for thin-walled sections and discuss the applicability of simple bending theory to them. 8 marks

Points to cover. Uniform stress across t, mid-line; t³ terms dropped; wall formulas; Booms; Property sums with parallel-axis terms; Shear lag, buckling, plasticity.

Model answer.

  1. Thin wall: thickness t small compared with length s (t/s below about 0.1).
  2. Approximation 1: the direct stress is uniform across t, so each wall is drawn as its mid-line.
  3. Approximation 2: lengths are measured along the mid-line and corner overlaps are ignored.
  4. Approximation 3: terms in t² and t³ are dropped. A straight wall at angle θ then has, about its own centroid:
    Ixx,0 = t s³ sin²θ/12,  Iyy,0 = t s³ cos²θ/12,  Ixy,0 = t s³ sin θ cos θ/12
  5. Approximation 4: stringers become booms, concentrated areas with no second moment of their own.
  6. Section properties then follow from sums over walls and booms, with each wall's centroid (xc, yc) measured from the section centroid:
    Ixx = Σ(Ixx,0 + a yc²),  Iyy = Σ(Iyy,0 + a xc²),  Ixy = Σ(Ixy,0 + a xc yc)
  7. Example. For a 100 × 60 mm box with 2 mm walls, Ixx = 2 × 200 × 30² + 2 × 2 × 60³/12 = 432,000 mm⁴; the dropped t³ term of the horizontal walls is 2 × 100 × 2³/12 = 133 mm⁴, 0.03 % of the total.
  8. Applicability of bending theory. The simple theory is reliable for sizing, but three effects limit it: shear lag in wide skins raises the stress near the webs; local buckling and crippling of thin compressed walls lower their effective area; and plasticity near ultimate load redistributes stress.
  9. Inclined wall example: a wall 60 mm long and 1 mm thick at 30° to x has t s³/12 = 18,000 mm⁴, so about its own centroid Ixx,0 = 18,000 sin²30° = 4,500 mm⁴, Iyy,0 = 13,500 mm⁴ and Ixy,0 = 7,794 mm⁴. Parallel-axis terms are added if its centroid is not at the section centroid.
  10. Effective width in practice: for a compression skin between stringers only a strip near each stringer is counted with the stringer as the boom; the rest buckles and carries little extra load. This reduces I and raises the calculated stress, which is the safe direction.
  11. Conclusion: the thin-walled theory is used for first sizing and stress estimates, then checked by effective-width rules, buckling checks, finite elements and tests.

bottom (20 °C): σ = +32.2 MPa;  mid-depth: 0
  6. The restraining moment that holds the beam straight is the moment that would cause the free curvature:
    I = 0.05 × 0.12³/12 = 7.20 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴
M = E I κ = 70 × 10⁹ × 7.20 × 10⁻⁶ × 7.67 × 10⁻³ = 3,864 N·m = 3.86 kN·m
  7. Check: M y/I = 3,864 × 0.060/7.20 × 10⁻⁶ = 32.2 MPa, equal to the face stress E α ΔT/2. The held-straight stress has zero net force but carries this moment, so it is not self-equilibrating.
  8. Stress profile in the held-straight case: σ varies linearly with depth, from −32.2 MPa at the top through zero at mid-depth to +32.2 MPa at the bottom. Its net force is zero (equal and opposite triangles), so no axial restraint is needed, but its moment is not zero.
  9. Physical check: the hot top wants to be longer than the cool bottom. Held straight, the top is shortened relative to its free length (compression) and the bottom lengthened (tension), as the signs show.
  10. (e) If the ends were also prevented from extending, σ = −E α T, from −96.6 MPa at the top to −32.2 MPa at the bottom (temperatures measured as rises from a 0 °C stress-free state).

Analyze Compare the neutral axis method, the k method and the principal axis method for unsymmetrical bending. Show on one numerical case that they agree, and say when each is most convenient. 14 marks

Points to cover. One stress field; k method formulas and use; Neutral axis method formulas and use; Principal axis method formulas and use; Numerical comparison; Common errors.

Model answer.

  1. All three methods describe the same linear stress field σ = k₁ x + k₂ y, which is fixed by plane sections and the two moment conditions. They differ only in the order of the work.
  2. k method: compute D = Ixx Iyy − Ixy² and the constants, then evaluate at each point.
    σ = (ka Mx − kc My) y + (kb My − kc Mx) x,  ka = Iyy/D, kb = Ixx/D, kc = Ixy/D
  3. Best when one section carries many load cases: the three constants are found once.
  4. Neutral axis method: find α, then INA and MNA, and take σ proportional to the distance from the neutral axis.
    tan α = (Mx Ixy − My Ixx)/(Mx Iyy − My Ixy)
INA = Ixx cos²α + Iyy sin²α − 2Ixy sin α cos α,  MNA = Mx cos α − My sin α
σ = MNA n/INA,  n = y cos α − x sin α
  5. Best when the question asks for the neutral axis and the largest stress: the farthest point from the neutral axis is found by eye.
  6. Principal axis method: find θ and Iu, Iv, resolve the moments and add two simple stresses.
    tan 2θ = −2Ixy/(Ixx − Iyy);  Mu = Mx cos θ − My sin θ,  Mv = Mx sin θ + My cos θ
σ = Mu v/Iu + Mv u/Iv
  7. Best when the principal properties are tabulated (rolled angles) or the deflection is also wanted, since the deflection splits into two independent beams.
  8. Numerical equivalence on one case: Ixx = 12 × 10⁶, Iyy = 4 × 10⁶, Ixy = 2.5 × 10⁶ mm⁴, Mx = 8 kN·m, My = 0, point (40, 60) mm.
    MethodKey numbersσ (MPa)
    k methodk₁ = -0.4790, k₂ = 0.7665 N/mm³26.83
    Neutral axisα = 32.0°, INA = 7,505,618 mm⁴, n = 29.68 mm26.83
    Principal axesθ = -16.00°, Iu = 12,716,991, Iv = 3,283,009 mm⁴26.83
  9. Errors to avoid: a wrong sign of Ixy (tilts the axis the wrong way), the wrong pairing of Mv with u, and coordinates measured from a corner instead of the centroid.
  10. Where each fails in practice: the k method gives no picture of the neutral axis; the neutral axis method needs α to good accuracy because n is sensitive to it; the principal axis method needs care with the rotation signs. Using two methods catches all three kinds of slip.
  11. Conclusion: choose the method that matches what is asked; the answer must not depend on it, so a second method is a good check.
Analyze Show that a beam in unsymmetrical bending deflects perpendicular to its neutral axis, and discuss the consequences for Z-section stringers and wings. 12 marks

Points to cover. Curvatures u″ = −k₁/E, v″ = −k₂/E; Perpendicularity proof; Symmetrical special case; Z-section numbers; Stringer and wing consequences.

Model answer.

  1. Curvatures. With u, v the deflections in x and y, a fibre at (x, y) has strain ε = −x u″ − y v″. Substituting σ = E ε into the moment conditions and solving gives:
    u″ = (Mx Ixy − My Ixx)/(E D) = −k₁/E
v″ = (My Ixy − Mx Iyy)/(E D) = −k₂/E
  2. Direction. The curvature vector (u″, v″) is along (−k₁, −k₂). The neutral axis k₁ x + k₂ y = 0 has direction (k₂, −k₁). Their dot product is −k₁k₂ + k₂k₁ = 0, so the curvature, and hence the deflection, is perpendicular to the neutral axis at every station.
  3. Symmetrical case. With Ixy = 0 and My = 0, k₁ = 0, the neutral axis is the x axis and the beam deflects along y, in the plane of the load. Only then does the beam move in the direction of the load.
  4. Z-section demonstration (Ixx = 666,667, Iyy = 166,667, Ixy = 250,000 mm⁴, cantilever 1.2 m, E = 70 GPa, W = 500 N down):
    u = W L³ Ixy/(3 E D) = +21.16 mm,  v = −W L³ Iyy/(3 E D) = −14.11 mm
resultant 25.43 mm at −33.7°;  neutral axis at +56.3°;  56.3° − (−33.7°) = 90.0°
  5. The tip moves more sideways than down. Ignoring Ixy gives 6.17 mm straight down: wrong in size and direction.
  6. Consequences for stringers: a Z or angle stringer on its own would push sideways on the skin and twist the panel; it is riveted to the skin and held by clips so that it bends about the skin plane.
  7. Consequences for wings: the bending direction of a box with unequal caps or composite skins is not the lift direction; with sweep this changes the local angle of attack and feeds aeroelastic calculations.
  8. Second demonstration, the 80 × 40 × 2 angle as an 800 mm cantilever with a 50 N downward tip load:
    u = -1.429 mm, v = -1.071 mm, direction -143.1°
neutral axis at the root: -53.1°;  difference 90.0°
  9. Principal-axes view: on u and v the two bending directions are independent. The load splits into components, each deflects in proportion to its own flexibility 1/I, and the resultant leans towards the axis with the smaller I. Only when the load lies along a principal axis, or Iu = Iv, does the beam move along the load.
  10. Conclusion: deflection follows the neutral axis, not the load; the two agree only for bending about a principal axis.
Evaluate Discuss the validity of simple bending theory for thin-walled aircraft sections and state the corrections used in practice. 14 marks

Points to cover. Assumptions; What holds; Shear lag; Local buckling and crippling; Twist of open sections; Plasticity, temperature, large deflection; Numerical evidence; Conclusion.

Model answer.

  1. Assumptions of the simple theory: plane sections; uniform direct stress across each thin wall; linear elastic material; small deflections; the shape of the section is kept (no distortion).
  2. What holds well for aircraft sections: the thin-wall approximation itself (t/s small) and the boom idealisation; the general formula with Ixy handles any shape, open or closed.
  3. Limitation 1, shear lag: in a wide skin between webs the direct stress is not uniform; it peaks near the webs and falls between them. Correction: an effective width of skin, or a finite element model, and design for the peak stress.
  4. Limitation 2, local buckling and crippling: thin compressed skin buckles below yield and then carries less load, shedding it to stringers. Correction: effective sheet width and crippling stress checks (see the chapters on stiffened panels and crippling).
  5. Limitation 3, open sections under transverse load: unless the load passes through the shear centre, the section twists as well as bends. Correction: load through the shear centre or add the torsion analysis of the shear flow chapters.
  6. Limitation 4, plasticity: near ultimate load the outer fibres yield and the stress redistributes. Correction: plastic or tangent-modulus methods for ultimate strength.
  7. Limitation 5, temperature: gradients add stresses that the bending formula does not show. Correction: the thermal force and moment terms of the temperature chapter.
  8. Limitation 6, large deflections and cut-outs: very flexible wings and panels with holes break the small-deflection and plane-section assumptions locally.
  9. Evaluation with numbers: in the stringer box example, ignoring Ixy changed the worst boom stress from −151.3 to −137.5 MPa, a 9 % unsafe error; in the Z-section, using M y/I gave 75.0 MPa against 171.4 MPa. Getting the general formula right matters more than these corrections in many exam problems, but in design each correction can be 10 % or more.
  10. Shear lag in more detail: the skin receives its direct stress through shear from the spar webs. Far from the webs the skin strains less, so near a wide cut-out or at the root of a wide box the stress near the webs can be well above M y/I.
  11. Crippling in more detail: a stringer's thin flanges buckle locally and the corners carry on until they yield; the average stress at failure, the crippling stress, can be well below the material's compressive yield for thin sections.
  12. Conclusion: thin-walled bending theory is the right tool for sizing and first stress estimates; final design uses effective widths, buckling and crippling checks, shear-centre loading, thermal terms and finite element or test confirmation.
Analyze A free beam has four 400 mm² booms at (±250, ±100) mm and carries Mx = −20 kN·m. Boom 1 at (250, 100), next to a hot duct, is 60 K warmer than the others (E = 70 GPa, α = 23 × 10⁻⁶ per K). Find the total boom stresses and say when the thermal part can be ignored. 13 marks

Points to cover. Bending stresses; Thermal force and moments; Thermal boom stresses; Superposition table; Equilibrium checks; When to ignore.

Model answer.

  1. Data: four booms of 400 mm² at (±250, ±100) mm, E = 70 GPa, α = 23 × 10⁻⁶ per K; Mx = −20 kN·m (lift); boom 1 at (250, 100) is 60 K warmer than the rest. The beam is free.
  2. Section: symmetric, Ixy = 0.
    Ixx = 4 × 400 × 100² = 1.6 × 10⁷ mm⁴,  Iyy = 4 × 400 × 250² = 1.0 × 10⁸ mm⁴
  3. Bending stresses: σb = Mx y/Ixx:
    top booms (y = +100): −2.0 × 10⁷ × 100/1.6 × 10⁷ = −125.0 MPa
bottom booms: +125.0 MPa
  4. Thermal stresses. With s = E α T₁ = 70,000 × 23 × 10⁻⁶ × 60 = 96.6 N/mm², the thermal force and moments are NT = sA, MxT = sA × 100, MyT = sA × 250. Then:
    σT,i = −E α Ti + NT/(4A) + MxT yi/Ixx + MyT xi/Iyy
boom 1 (250, 100): s(−1 + 1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4) = −s/4
boom 2 (−250, 100): s(1/4 + 1/4 − 1/4) = +s/4
boom 3 (−250, −100): s(1/4 − 1/4 − 1/4) = −s/4
boom 4 (250, −100): s(1/4 − 1/4 + 1/4) = +s/4
  5. Superpose (the material is elastic):
    BoomBending (MPa)Thermal (MPa)Total (MPa)
    1 (hot, top)−125.0−24.15−149.15
    2 (top)−125.024.15−100.85
    3 (bottom)125.0−24.15100.85
    4 (bottom)125.024.15149.15
  6. Checks: the thermal column sums to zero and has zero moments about both axes (self-equilibrating); the totals still give ΣAσ = 0 and ΣAσy = 400 × 100 × (−149.15 − 100.85) + 400 × (−100) × (100.85 + 149.15) = −2.0 × 10⁷ N·mm = Mx.
  7. Result: heating boom 1 raises its compression from 125.0 to 149.15 MPa (+19 %), and also loads boom 4 to +149.15 MPa. A design that ignored the duct would be unsafe at boom 1.
  8. Physical reading: the hot boom pushes the beam into an extra bend and stretch. Booms 2 and 4 are pulled along although they are cold, so they gain tension; boom 3 is shortened and gains compression; boom 1 cannot expand as much as it wants and gains compression.
  9. When the thermal part can be ignored: if the temperature field is linear in x and y and the beam is free, the thermal stress is zero. It cannot be ignored when the field is local (one hot boom) or the supports restrain the thermal bending.

Glossary

Anticlastic curvature
Curl of a cross-section opposite to the bending, caused by Poisson's ratio; radius R/ν.
Bending moment
The internal moment at a section, in N·mm or N·m, equal to the moment of all loads on one side.
Boom
A concentrated area standing for a stringer and its effective skin; it carries direct stress only.
Centroid
The point about which the first moment of area is zero; the neutral axis passes through it in pure bending.
Coefficient of thermal expansion
α, the free strain per kelvin; about 23 × 10⁻⁶ per K for aluminium.
Direct stress
Normal stress along the beam, in N/mm²; positive in tension.
Flexural rigidity
E I, the resistance of a beam to curvature.
k method
The general unsymmetrical formula written with section constants Iyy/D, Ixx/D and Ixy/D.
Mid-line
The centre line of a thin wall, along which lengths and positions are measured.
Neutral axis
The line of zero direct stress through the centroid.
Neutral axis method
Finding the neutral axis first and taking the stress as MNA n/INA.
Poisson's ratio
ν, the ratio of lateral contraction to axial extension; about 0.33 for aluminium alloys.
Principal axes
The pair of centroidal axes about which Iuv = 0 and the second moments are largest and smallest.
Principal axis method
Resolving the moment onto the principal axes and adding two symmetrical-bending stresses.
Product second moment
Ixy = ∫x y dA, in mm⁴; positive, negative or zero.
Second moment of area
I = ∫y² dA about an axis, in mm⁴.
Section modulus
Z = I/ymax, in mm³; σmax = M/Z.
Self-equilibrating stress
A stress field with zero net force and zero net moments, such as the thermal stress of a free beam with a non-linear temperature.
Shear lag
Uneven direct stress across a wide skin, higher near the webs than between them.
Skew load
A load not along a principal axis, giving moments about both axes.
Stringer
A longitudinal stiffener riveted or bonded to the skin.
Thin-walled section
A section whose walls are thin compared with their length, so stress is uniform across the thickness.
Unsymmetrical bending
Bending in which the moment is not about a principal axis, so the neutral axis is not along the moment axis.
Z-section
A stringer shape with two flanges pointing opposite ways; it has no axis of symmetry and a large Ixy.
