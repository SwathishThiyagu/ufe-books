4.1 Symmetrical bending and anticlastic bending

A wing spar cap bends under moment. One section property, the second moment of area I, gives both the stress at every point and the curved shape. For a moment about a principal axis: σ = M y / I and 1/R = M/EI. Poisson contraction makes the cross-section curl the other way: anticlastic bending. Spar, stringer and frame sizing starts here.

You need the second moment of area, the parallel-axis theorem, and the wing bending moment diagram from Unit 1.

Symmetrical bending: the stress formula

Bending is symmetrical when the moment acts about a principal axis and the section is symmetric about the plane of bending. Five assumptions:

  • Plane sections stay plane and normal to the bent axis.
  • Deflections are small, so curvature 1/R ≈ d²v/dx².
  • The material is linear elastic, with equal E in tension and compression.
  • The moment acts about a principal axis; no axial force acts.
  • The wall is thin enough for uniform stress across its thickness (see 4.4).

Derivation:

  1. A plane section rotates as a plane. The fibre at distance y from the neutral axis changes length by y/R, so strain is ε = y/R.
  2. Hooke's law gives σ = E ε = E y / R.
  3. No axial force acts: ∫σ dA = (E/R) ∫y dA = 0. The first moment ∫y dA vanishes only about the centroid, so the neutral axis passes through the centroid.
  4. Internal moment equals stress times lever arm: M = ∫σ y dA = (E/R) ∫y² dA = E I / R, where I = ∫y² dA about the neutral axis.
  5. Eliminate R between steps 2 and 4 of this list: σ = M y / I.

σ = M y / I, 1/R = M / EI, Z = I / y_max

σ is direct stress (Pa or N/mm²) at distance y from the neutral axis; M is bending moment about that axis (N·m); y is distance from the centroidal neutral axis (m), positive towards tension; I is second moment of area about that axis (m⁴); E is Young's modulus (Pa); R is radius of curvature of the beam axis (m); Z is elastic section modulus (m³); y_max is distance to the outermost fibre (m).

Stress grows linearly with y; curvature is fixed by M/EI. The section modulus gives σ_max = M/Z. A rectangular section twice as deep has eight times I but only twice y_max, so σ_max falls four times. This is why spars are deep and skins thin.

Sign convention, stress diagram and section modulus

Take sagging as positive. A simply supported beam under downward load sags: top fibres shorten, bottom fibres stretch. Measure y positive downwards, towards tension. Then σ = +M y / I gives tension below the axis and compression above it. A hogging moment is negative and every sign flips.

  • Stress is zero at the centroidal axis.
  • Stress varies linearly through the depth, with slope M/I.
  • Stress is largest at the outer fibre, y = y_max.
  • For a section symmetric about the axis, top and bottom stresses are equal and opposite.

A section not symmetric about its neutral axis, like a T-section or a wing box with a wide top skin, has different distances to top and bottom fibres and so two section moduli, Z_top = I/y_top and Z_bottom = I/y_bottom. Yielding is governed by the larger distance (the smaller Z), whichever face it is on; the compression face governs skin buckling (see Unit 6). In the example below, I = 8.5 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴ and y_max = 0.10 m, so Z = 8.5 × 10⁻⁵ m³.

Anticlastic bending: the saddle

A stretched fibre contracts sideways by ν ε, where ν is Poisson's ratio: about 0.33 for aluminium alloy, 0.3 for steel. A compressed fibre expands sideways. The transverse strain itself cannot be avoided; only the curl it produces can be restrained.

In a sagging beam the bottom fibre at y = +d/2 has longitudinal strain +y/R, so transverse strain is −ν y/R and it narrows. The top fibre at y = −d/2 widens by +ν y/R. Width change is proportional to y, so the cross-section curls in its own plane.

1/R′ = ν / R, so R′ = R / ν

R′ is transverse (anticlastic) radius of curvature of the cross-section (m); R is longitudinal radius of curvature of the beam axis (m); ν is Poisson's ratio, dimensionless.

R′ is about three times R for aluminium, so the curl is visible: bend a rubber eraser and its end face stops being rectangular. The bar becomes a saddle, curving one way along its length and the opposite way across its width.

The curl is free only on a narrow strip. In a wing box, flanges, stringers, ribs and continuous skin hold the section almost flat. Sideways movement is restrained, and a transverse stress σ_t = −ν σ appears in the skin instead. The panel then works biaxially; this matters for fatigue and compression-skin buckling. Free curl survives near edges: cut-outs, free flanges, holes. Thus σ = M y / I serves both cases; deformation and transverse stress differ: the stiffened box works biaxially, the plain strip curls freely.

Exam traps

  • Measuring y from an outer face, flange or skin instead of the centroidal neutral axis.
  • Using σ = M y / I when the moment is not about a principal axis; see 4.2.
  • Mixing units: M in kN·m with I in m⁴ and E in GPa. Convert first.
  • Writing R′ = νR instead of R/ν. R′ is larger than R.
  • Using one y_max for an asymmetric section; it has two section moduli.

Analyze A wing box and a plain strip of the same material bend to the same radius. Why is anticlastic curl visible in the strip but not the box? 5 marks

On the free strip, side faces move freely, so Poisson strain varies through the depth and the section curls at R′ = R/ν. In the box, flanges, stringers, ribs and skin restrain sideways movement. The 300 mm beam, because σ_max = M y_max/I and its y_max is 150 mm against 50 mm. With the same I, outer-fibre stress is three times larger; deep sections win only when I also grows.

4.2 Unsymmetrical bending: stresses and neutral axis

An aircraft wing spar often has a section that is not symmetric about either centroidal axis. Then a bending moment about one axis also causes stress at points that lie off that axis: the neutral axis tilts away from the moment vector. This chapter gives the general stress formula for such sections. It defines the product second moment I_xy, solves the two moment equations for the constants k₁ and k₂, locates the neutral axis, and shows the equivalent principal-axis route. You need it whenever a thin-walled section carries a skew load, for example a wing box with a fuel mass offset or a fuselage frame with an asymmetric cut-out.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for the sign convention and the meaning of I, and Mohr's circle for second moments.

Why the simple formula fails

For a section bent about a centroidal axis, the simple formula σ = M y / I works only when that axis is principal. The missing quantity is the product second moment. It measures how the area is spread across the four quadrants: positive when more area lies in the first and third quadrants.

I_xy = ∫ x y dA

x and y are centroidal coordinates in m; dA is an area element in m²; I_xy is the product second moment in m⁴.

If the section has an axis of symmetry, each element at (x, y) has a partner at (x, −y) or (−x, y). The integrand xy cancels in pairs, so I_xy = 0. Many aircraft sections—a tapered spar, a wing box with an offset cut-out, a fuselage frame with an asymmetric stringer layout—have no such symmetry. For those, a moment about x also produces stress that varies with x, and σ = M_x y / I_xx is wrong.

The two-constant formula and the neutral axis

The stress is linear in both coordinates: σ = k₁ x + k₂ y, where σ is direct stress in Pa, x and y are centroidal coordinates in m, and k₁, k₂ are constants in Pa/m. Find the constants from the two moment equations.

M_x = ∫ σ y dA, M_y = ∫ σ x dA

M_x and M_y are bending moments about the x and y axes in N·m; σ is direct stress in Pa; y and x are lever arms in m.

Substituting σ = k₁ x + k₂ y gives M_x = k₁ I_xy + k₂ I_xx and M_y = k₁ I_yy + k₂ I_xy. Solve these two equations.

k₁ = (M_y I_xx − M_x I_xy) / D,  k₂ = (M_x I_yy − M_y I_xy) / D,  D = I_xx I_yy − I_xy²

D is the determinant in m⁸; I_xx = ∫ y² dA, I_yy = ∫ x² dA and I_xy = ∫ x y dA are centroidal second moments in m⁴.

σ = [(M_y I_xx − M_x I_xy) x + (M_x I_yy − M_y I_xy) y] / D

σ is direct stress in Pa at the point (x, y); all other symbols are as above.

This is the general centroidal (product-of-inertia) formula; with I_xy = 0 it reduces to σ = M_x y/I_xx + M_y x/I_yy about principal axes. The neutral axis is where σ = 0.

k₁ x + k₂ y = 0 → tan α = y / x = −k₁ / k₂

α is the angle of the neutral axis from the x-axis, measured through the centroid.

When I_xy ≠ 0 the neutral axis is no longer perpendicular to the plane of the applied moment: it tilts away from the direction of the moment vector, which is the line it follows in symmetric bending. With only M_x, tan α = I_xy / I_yy: the tilt comes directly from the product second moment.

Principal axes route

Rotate the axes to u, v so that I_uv = 0. On Mohr's circle for second moments, the centre is C and the radius is R. The principal second moments are I_max and I_min. The principal angle θ_p is measured from the x-axis to the u-axis. Always put θ_p back into the product-second-moment expression and check it is zero.

C = (I_xx + I_yy)/2,  R = √[((I_xx − I_yy)/2)² + I_xy²],  I_max,min = C ± R

C and R are in m⁴; I_max and I_min are the largest and smallest second moments of the section in m⁴.

tan 2θ_p = −2 I_xy / (I_xx − I_yy)

θ_p is the principal angle in radians or degrees, measured from the x-axis to the u-axis.

Use the rotation u = x cos θ_p + y sin θ_p, v = −x sin θ_p + y cos θ_p, so the u-axis is at θ_p from the x-axis. Resolve the moments onto the principal axes, then use the symmetrical-bending formula about each principal axis.

M_u = M_x cos θ_p − M_y sin θ_p,  M_v = M_x sin θ_p + M_y cos θ_p

M_u and M_v are bending moments about the u and v axes in N·m.

σ = (M_u / I_u) v + (M_v / I_v) u

u and v are coordinates along the principal axes in m; I_u and I_v are the principal second moments in m⁴.

This route gives the same stress as the k method. It is shorter when the same section carries many load cases, because the principal properties are fixed and only the moment components change.

Worked example

Question. A thin-walled aircraft section has centroidal second moments I_xx = 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴, I_yy = 1.2 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴ and I_xy = 0.8 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴. A bending moment M_x = 5 kN·m acts about the x-axis and M_y = 0. Find (a) the direct stress at the point x = 50 mm, y = 80 mm, and (b) the angle of the neutral axis from the x-axis. Use the general centroidal formula.

  1. Convert units: M_x = 5000 N·m, M_y = 0, x = 0.050 m, y = 0.080 m.
  2. Denominator: D = I_xx I_yy − I_xy² = (5.0 × 10⁻⁶)(1.2 × 10⁻⁶) − (0.8 × 10⁻⁶)² = 6.0 × 10⁻¹² − 0.64 × 10⁻¹² = 5.36 × 10⁻¹² m⁸.
  3. Constant k₁ = (M_y I_xx − M_x I_xy) / D = (0 − 5000 × 0.8 × 10⁻⁶) / 5.36 × 10⁻¹² = −4.0 × 10⁻³ / 5.36 × 10⁻¹² = −7.46 × 10⁸ Pa/m.
  4. Constant k₂ = (M_x I_yy − M_y I_xy) / D = (5000 × 1.2 × 10⁻⁶ − 0) / 5.36 × 10⁻¹² = 6.0 × 10⁻³ / 5.36 × 10⁻¹² = 1.12 × 10⁹ Pa/m.
  5. Stress: σ = k₁ x + k₂ y = (−7.46 × 10⁸)(0.050) + (1.12 × 10⁹)(0.080) = −3.73 × 10⁷ + 8.96 × 10⁷ = 5.22 × 10⁷ Pa = 52.2 MPa.
  6. Neutral axis: σ = 0 gives k₁ x + k₂ y = 0, so tan α = −k₁ / k₂ = 7.46 × 10⁸ / 1.12 × 10⁹ = 0.667. Thus α = arctan(0.667) = 33.7° from the x-axis toward +y.

Exam traps

  • Using σ = M_x y / I_xx when I_xy is not zero. The product term changes both the stress magnitude and the neutral axis.
  • Taking x and y from a corner, a web centre line or a boom rather than from the centroid of the section. The formula is centroidal; a wrong origin gives wrong k₁ and k₂.
  • Dropping the M_y term when the load is skew. Resolve the applied moment into components about both centroidal axes first.
  • Mixing sign conventions. In this chapter M_x = ∫ σ y dA and M_y = ∫ σ x dA; if you use the opposite sign for M_y, the neutral axis tilts the other way.
  • Forgetting to check I_uv = 0 after finding a principal angle. A small arithmetic slip in θ_p gives a large stress error.

4.3 Deflection due to unsymmetrical bending

A skew load on a section with unequal principal second moments bends the beam in two planes at once. Each plane obeys the ordinary beam formula, so you integrate twice about each principal axis and get two deflection components. The real movement is their vector sum. The tip does not follow the load line: it swings towards the more flexible plane. Designers use this to check that a spar tip, a strut or a control-surface hinge does not move where the drawing assumed. It is the deflection half of the unsymmetrical bending problem whose stresses are in Chapter 4.2.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for the neutral axis and the two-plane split of stress, and the beam deflection formulas of Unit 2.

Curvature in each principal plane

Take u and v as the principal centroidal axes of the cross-section. The product second moment I_uv is zero, so bending in the u–z plane and bending in the v–z plane do not interact: a moment in one plane produces no curvature in the other. That is what turns the deflection problem into two easy beam problems.

1/R_u = M_u / (E I_u)

1/R_u is the curvature of the beam axis in the u–z plane (1/m); M_u is the moment bending the beam in that plane (N·m); E is Young's modulus (Pa); I_u is the second moment of area of the section for bending in the u–z plane (m⁴).

1/R_v = M_v / (E I_v)

1/R_v is the curvature in the v–z plane (1/m); M_v is the moment in that plane (N·m); I_v is the second moment of area for bending in the v–z plane (m⁴).

Label convention: I_u is the second moment about the v axis, I_u = ∫u² dA, the value used in σ = M u / I_u for bending in the u–z plane; I_v = ∫v² dA is about the u axis. Some books swap these labels; check which one the formula is written with.

Curvature is the rate at which the slope changes along the span. The deflection components u(z) and v(z) therefore come from two ordinary integrations, d²u/dz² = M_u/(E I_u) and d²v/dz² = M_v/(E I_v), with the sign convention of Unit 2. The constants are fixed by the supports: slope and deflection are zero at a fixed end, deflection is zero at a simple support.

For common loads, skip the integration. Resolve the load into the two planes, take the closed-form deflection for each plane from Unit 2, and combine at the end. Integrate only for loads that are not in the table.

Cantilever with a skew tip load

  1. Resolve the tip load P acting at angle θ from the u-axis: P_u = P cos θ along u and P_v = P sin θ along v.
  2. Take the cantilever formula in each plane: δ_u = P_u L³/(3 E I_u) and δ_v = P_v L³/(3 E I_v).
  3. Combine the two as vectors: δ = √(δ_u² + δ_v²), at angle β = tan⁻¹(δ_v/δ_u) from the u-axis.
  4. Compare β with θ. The two angles are equal only when I_u = I_v.

δ_u = P_u L³ / (3 E I_u)

δ_u is the tip deflection in the u direction (m); P_u is the load component along u (N); L is the span (m); E is Young's modulus (Pa); I_u is the second moment for bending in the u–z plane (m⁴).

The direction comes from dividing one component by the other: tan β = δ_v/δ_u = tan θ × (I_u/I_v). When the v-plane is the more flexible one, I_v < I_u, so I_u/I_v > 1 and β > θ; the tip swings away from the load line towards v. The tip also lies at right angles to the neutral axis of Chapter 4.2, so the movement and the zero-stress line are perpendicular.

Both components share the same shape along the span, δ_u(z) = δ_u z²(3L − z)/(2L³), and the v component has the same form. The whole elastic curve therefore lies in one plane at angle β, and every station moves in that direction. Small-deflection theory is assumed: β stays fixed as the load grows.

Other supports and design checks

Other supports change the coefficient and the shape in each plane, but both planes still share the same shape, so you resolve, take the tabulated deflection in each plane, and combine as vectors. A simply supported span L with a central load P at angle θ gives δ_u = P_u L³/(48 E I_u) and δ_v = P_v L³/(48 E I_v). A uniform load w N/m gives 5wL⁴/(384 E I) in each plane.

Two checks catch most errors. The resultant must lie between the larger component and the plain sum of the components. The direction must lie between θ and the flexible axis, and on the load line only for a section with I_u = I_v, such as a square or circular tube.

On an aircraft the direction matters as much as the size. A spar tip that moves at 72° while the lift acts at 37° opens or closes the gap at a control-surface hinge and misaligns bolted fittings. Unit 6 carries that direction into aeroelastic calculations.

Worked example

Question. A thin-walled aluminium cantilever has length L = 2 m, E = 70 GPa, and principal second moments I_u = 4 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴ and I_v = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴ for bending in the u–z and v–z planes. A tip load P = 1 kN acts at 36.87° from the u-axis (the 3-4-5 angle). Find the tip deflection in each plane, the resultant deflection and its direction, and compare with the load direction.

  1. Convert to base units: E = 70 GPa = 70 × 10⁹ N/m², P = 1000 N, L³ = 2³ = 8 m³.
  2. Split the load: P_u = 1000 × cos 36.87° = 800 N, P_v = 1000 × sin 36.87° = 600 N.
  3. Plane stiffness: 3E I_u = 3 × 70 × 10⁹ × 4 × 10⁻⁶ = 8.40 × 10⁵ N·m²; 3E I_v = 3 × 70 × 10⁹ × 1 × 10⁻⁶ = 2.10 × 10⁵ N·m².
  4. u-plane: δ_u = 800 × 8 / (8.40 × 10⁵) = 6400 / (8.40 × 10⁵) = 7.62 × 10⁻³ m = 7.6 mm.
  5. v-plane: δ_v = 600 × 8 / (2.10 × 10⁵) = 4800 / (2.10 × 10⁵) = 22.86 × 10⁻³ m = 22.9 mm.
  6. Resultant: δ = √(7.62² + 22.86²) = √(58.1 + 522.5) = √580.6 = 24.1 mm.
  7. Direction: β = tan⁻¹(22.86 / 7.62) = tan⁻¹(3.00) = 71.6° from the u-axis.
  8. Compare: the load is at 36.9° but the tip moves at 71.6°, a swing of 34.7° towards the flexible v-plane, so the tip is not on the load line.

Answer. δ_u = 7.6 mm, δ_v = 22.9 mm, resultant 24.1 mm at 71.6° from the u-axis, while the load acts at 36.9°.

Exam traps

  • Adding the components as plain numbers: 7.6 + 22.9 = 30.5 mm is wrong; the vector sum is 24.1 mm.
  • Drawing the tip deflection along the load line. A skew load on unequal second moments always lands nearer the flexible plane.
  • Pairing a deflection component with the wrong second moment. Each component uses the second moment for its own bending plane, which is the value about the axis perpendicular to that plane.
  • Unit slips: I in mm⁴ with E in GPa. Convert first, since 1 mm⁴ = 10⁻¹² m⁴, and keep L in metres.
  • Using cos θ on the wrong component: the cosine multiplies the component along the axis from which θ is measured.

4.4 Thin-walled section approximations and section properties

A thin wall carries direct stress almost evenly across its thickness. That lets you replace a real wing or fuselage section with a few concentrated boom areas at corners and thin walls between them. Area and second moments then become simple bookkeeping. This chapter shows the thin-wall assumption, the boom-plus-wall idealisation, the half-wall rule, and the section properties I_xx, I_yy and I_xy. These properties feed the bending formulae from Chapter 4.1 and Chapter 4.2.

You need: the bending stress formula and second moments from Chapter 4.1 and the unsymmetrical stress formula from Chapter 4.2.

The thin-wall assumption and boom idealisation

A wall is thin when its thickness t is much smaller than its length s. Direct stress then varies very little across t, so take it uniform across the thickness. The wall also carries shear mainly along its mid-line. This is the thin-walled assumption. It turns a two-dimensional section into a one-dimensional line of walls. The error is small when t/s is below about 0.1, which covers most aircraft skin and web panels.

Real sections have stringers and skin. Replace the stringers by booms: concentrated areas A_i at their centroid. Keep the skin as effective walls. A wall between two booms can be split into the booms. The usual half-wall rule gives each end boom half the wall area, t s / 2. If several walls meet at a boom, add half of each. A stringer's own area is added once. Two versions are used: fully lumped walls, where walls then carry only shear, or boom-plus-wall, where walls keep their direct-stress area and own second moment. The second version is used below.

  • Boom: concentrated area A_i; carries direct stress.
  • Wall: thickness t, length s; carries uniform direct stress and shear.
  • Shared wall: count once only.

Section properties by summation

Choose centroidal axes x and y. Let boom i have area A_i at (x_i, y_i). Let a wall have thickness t, length s, and angle θ measured from the x-axis. Sum over every boom and every wall once. The area is:

A = Σ A_i + Σ t s

A_i is boom area (m²); t is wall thickness (m); s is wall length (m); the first sum is over booms, the second over walls.

For second moments, each wall's own term depends on its direction. A wall along x (θ = 0) has its large t s³/12 term about y, not about x. The general thin-wall sums are:

I_xx = Σ A_i y_i² + Σ [ A_w y_c² + (t s³/12) sin²θ + (s t³/12) cos²θ ]

y_i is boom height; A_w = t s is wall area; y_c is the wall centroid's y-coordinate from the section centroid; θ is wall angle from x; the bracket adds the parallel-axis term A_w y_c² and the wall's own I about x. For thin walls the s t³/12 term is often tiny.

I_yy = Σ A_i x_i² + Σ [ A_w x_c² + (t s³/12) cos²θ + (s t³/12) sin²θ ]

x_i is boom horizontal distance; x_c is the wall centroid's x-coordinate; the same wall appears once; bracket adds A_w x_c² and own I about y.

I_xy = Σ A_i x_i y_i + Σ [ A_w x_c y_c + (t s³/12) sinθ cosθ ]

Product second moment; x_c, y_c are wall centroid coordinates; the wall term is zero for walls along x or y; the small s t³/12 product term is neglected.

These wall sums apply to the boom-plus-wall version; in the fully lumped version the wall area is already inside the boom areas. For a rectangular box, four walls suffice: two horizontal and two vertical. Open and closed sections use the same A, I_xx, I_yy and I_xy, so the direct stress from Chapter 4.1 and Chapter 4.2 is the same. Shear flow, shear centre and torsion differ, and are in Unit 5.

Limits of bending theory

The simple answer assumes plane sections stay plane, stress is uniform across each wall, and the material stays linear elastic. Real aircraft panels break these assumptions. Shear lag makes the direct stress uneven across a wide skin panel; booms near a web carry more than booms far away, so an effective width is used. Local buckling makes a thin compressed wall buckle below the yield stress; after buckling it can carry less, and crippling may follow. Plasticity redistributes stress near ultimate load. These effects make the elastic thin-walled answer optimistic for strength and sometimes unsafe for stiffness. Some syllabi call this the applicability of bending theory: use it for quick sizing and preliminary stress, then check with tests, finite elements, or the methods in Unit 6.

Worked example

Question. A thin-walled rectangular box has mid-line width b = 100 mm, mid-line depth d = 60 mm, and uniform thickness t = 2 mm. The centroidal x-axis is horizontal and the y-axis is vertical. (a) Find I_xx and I_yy. (b) Find the maximum direct stress for M_x = 2 kN·m.

  1. Convert: b = 0.100 m, d = 0.060 m, t = 0.002 m, M_x = 2000 N·m. Top and bottom walls are at y = ±0.030 m. Left and right walls are at x = ±0.050 m.
  2. Two horizontal walls (length b, θ = 0). Each area = b t = 2.00×10⁻⁴ m². Their A y² terms: 2 × 2.00×10⁻⁴ × 0.030² = 3.600×10⁻⁷ m⁴. Their own I_xx: 2 × b t³/12 = 2 × 0.100 × 0.002³/12 = 1.333×10⁻¹⁰ m⁴.
  3. Two vertical walls (length d, θ = 90°). Each area = d t = 1.20×10⁻⁴ m². Their centroids have y = 0, so A y² = 0. Their own I_xx: 2 × t d³/12 = 2 × 0.002 × 0.060³/12 = 7.200×10⁻⁸ m⁴.
  4. I_xx = 3.600×10⁻⁷ + 1.333×10⁻¹⁰ + 7.200×10⁻⁸ = 4.3213×10⁻⁷ m⁴ (= 432,133 mm⁴).
  5. For I_yy, horizontal walls have x = 0, so no A x² term. Their own I_yy: 2 × t b³/12 = 2 × 0.002 × 0.100³/12 = 3.3333×10⁻⁷ m⁴. Vertical walls: 2 × (d t)(0.050²) = 6.000×10⁻⁷ m⁴; own I_yy: 2 × d t³/12 = 8.000×10⁻¹¹ m⁴. Total I_yy = 9.3341×10⁻⁷ m⁴.
  6. Maximum stress is at y = 0.030 m: σ = M_x y / I_xx = 2000 × 0.030 / 4.3213×10⁻⁷ = 1.388×10⁸ Pa = 139 MPa.

Answer. I_xx = 4.3213×10⁻⁷ m⁴, I_yy = 9.3341×10⁻⁷ m⁴, and the maximum direct stress is 139 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Using t s³/12 for I_xx when the wall length s runs along x. For a horizontal wall that term belongs to I_yy; its I_xx own term is s t³/12.
  • Counting a wall shared by two booms twice. Each wall appears once in Σ t s and once in the wall second-moment sum.
  • Mixing millimetres and metres inside I. Convert b, d, t, x and y to one unit before squaring or cubing.
  • Dropping the wall's own second moment because it is small. It is small, but the examiner may ask for it and the sum is incomplete without it.
  • Assuming open and closed sections need different bending formulae. Direct stress is the same; only shear and torsion differ, which is Unit 5.

4.5 Bending of sections with stringers and skew loads

Wing boxes and fuselage frames are stiffened by stringers. For bending stress, the skin between stringers mainly carries shear, so direct stress is taken by stringer areas called booms. This chapter reduces the section to booms at known x and y, finds the boom centroid and second moments from boom areas alone, then applies the unsymmetrical bending formula boom by boom. A skew load needs both M_x and M_y; an unsymmetric section adds I_xy. Only a few universities teach this, but it is how a real wing box is checked.

You need Chapter 4.2, Chapter 4.4, and Unit 1 for the bending moment distribution along a wing or fuselage.

Boom model and section properties

Replace each stringer and its attached skin by a boom of area A_i at coordinates x_i, y_i. Leave out the skin between booms for direct stress; it carries mainly shear, as Chapter 4.4 explains. Find the boom centroid: x̄ = ΣA_i x_i / ΣA_i and ȳ = ΣA_i y_i / ΣA_i. Move the origin there. Then compute I_xx = ΣA_i y_i², I_yy = ΣA_i x_i² and I_xy = ΣA_i x_i y_i. Use m² for A and m for x and y; I is then in m⁴. These are second moments of the boom areas only. If the booms are symmetric about both centroidal axes, I_xy = 0. So is the case with symmetry about one axis only, since the boom pairs then cancel ΣA x y. I_xy is non-zero only when the layout has no such symmetry; then the centroidal axes are not principal and must be rotated by the standard principal-axis formula. The figure's four booms are symmetric, so I_xy cancels.

Symmetric and unsymmetric skew bending

Take M_x positive when it produces tension at positive y, and M_y positive when it produces tension at positive x. Then σ = M_x y/I_xx + M_y x/I_yy. Here M_x and M_y are in N·m, x and y in m, I in m⁴, and σ in Pa. The neutral axis is σ=0: M_x y / I_xx + M_y x / I_yy = 0, a straight line through the centroid tilted by the skew moment. For an unsymmetric boom layout use σ = [(M_x I_yy − M_y I_xy)y + (M_y I_xx − M_x I_xy)x] / (I_xx I_yy − I_xy²). The equations are identical; only I_xy is non-zero. For a stringer-stiffened wing, M_x(z) and M_y(z) come from Unit 1's shear force and bending moment distribution. Quote the moment at the section; do not re-derive it. Apply the formula at every boom. The same steps serve a fuselage frame or a tail plane.

Worked example

Question. A wing box is idealised into four booms of area 200 mm² at (150, 100), (−150, 100), (−150, −100) and (150, −100) mm. All coordinates are in millimetres. It carries M_x = 10 kN·m and M_y = 4 kN·m. Find the stress in each boom.

  1. Boom centroid: x̄=ΣA x/ΣA=0 and ȳ=ΣA y/ΣA=0, so the origin is the centroid.
  2. I_xx = 4(200×10⁻⁶)(0.1²) = 8×10⁻⁶ m⁴. I_yy = 4(200×10⁻⁶)(0.15²) = 1.8×10⁻⁵ m⁴. I_xy = 0 by symmetry.
  3. Boom 1 (0.15, 0.1): M_x y/I_xx = 10×10³×0.1/8×10⁻⁶ = 125×10⁶ Pa = 125 MPa. M_y x/I_yy = 4×10³×0.15/1.8×10⁻⁵ = 33.3×10⁶ Pa = 33 MPa. σ₁ = 125+33 = 158 MPa.
  4. Boom 2 (−0.15, 0.1): σ₂ = 125−33 = 92 MPa.
  5. Boom 3 (−0.15, −0.1): σ₃ = −125−33 = −158 MPa.
  6. Boom 4 (0.15, −0.1): σ₄ = −125+33 = −92 MPa.

Answer. The boom stresses are 158 MPa, 92 MPa, −158 MPa and −92 MPa at booms 1 to 4, to the nearest MPa.

Exam traps

  • Using the centroid of the skin outline instead of the centroid of the boom areas puts the reference axes in the wrong place; this changes I_xx, I_yy and I_xy and therefore changes every boom stress.
  • Leaving I_xy out for an unsymmetric boom layout and using σ=M_x y/I_xx + M_y x/I_yy; this is valid only when I_xy=0.
  • Mixing millimetres with N·m; convert A to m² and x,y to m before computing I and σ.
  • Applying full skin area as direct stress instead of reducing it to boom areas; this overestimates I and underestimates stress.

4.6 Temperature effects in bending

A temperature difference between top and bottom is built-in curvature. A free beam bows with no stress. Stress appears only when curvature or axial extension is restrained. Sun heating an upper wing skin while the lower skin stays cool is one aircraft case. Only a few universities teach this. A bimetallic strip uses the same idea with two expansion coefficients.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for curvature and plane sections.

Free thermal curvature

For a linear rise, ΔT(y) = ΔTmean + k y. y is depth from mid-depth in m; k is the gradient in K/m; ΔTmean is defined in Section 2. Free thermal strain is εT = αΔT, with α in per K. Aluminium has α ≈ 23 × 10−6 per K.

Plane sections stay plane. The free strain difference from bottom to top is α(ΔTtop − ΔTbottom). Over depth h this is the free thermal curvature:

1/RT = α(ΔTtop − ΔTbottom)/h

RT is the free radius in m; h is the depth in m; temperatures are in K. The hot face becomes convex: mid-span moves toward the hot side, and the ends curl toward the cooler side. No stress is needed, so σ = 0.

Restraint, stress and aircraft cases

If curvature is prevented, sections stay straight. Let the total axial strain be ε0, uniform through the depth. The stress is σ(y) = E[ε0 − αΔT(y)], with E in Pa. If the ends are free axially, ∫σ dA = 0, so ε0 = αΔTmean. Here ΔTmean is the area-average rise, ∫ΔT dA/A; for a section symmetric about mid-depth with a linear gradient it equals the mid-depth value. Then

σ(y) = Eα[ΔTmean − ΔT(y)]

σ(y) is axial stress in Pa; E is Young's modulus in Pa; α is in per K; ΔTmean is the area-average rise in K; ΔT(y) is the local rise in K. The resultant force is zero, so no net axial load is needed. But the stress has a net moment of magnitude EαkI, which the restraint must supply. It is not self-equilibrating. Only when both force and moment resultants are zero (for example a non-linear gradient in a free beam) is the stress self-equilibrating. The stress is zero at mid-depth. At the surfaces σ = ∓EαΔT/2, where ΔT is the top-to-bottom difference. The hot face is in compression.

If the ends are also held against axial extension while curvature is prevented, ε0 = 0 and σ(y) = −EαΔT(y).

  • Sun on the upper wing skin with a cool lower skin gives a spar gradient.
  • A hot bleed-air duct heats one side of a fuselage frame; clips and skin restrain the bow.
  • A bimetallic strip: free, it bends; held straight, it develops stress.

Worked example

Question. An aluminium spar is 100 mm deep. E = 70 GPa and α = 23 × 10−6 per K. The top flange is 35 °C and the bottom flange is 5 °C. Find (a) the free radius and (b) the surface stresses if curvature is prevented but axial extension is free.

  1. ΔT = 35 − 5 = 30 K. h = 100 mm = 0.100 m.
  2. 1/RT = αΔT/h = 23 × 10−6 × 30 / 0.100 = 6.9 × 10−3 per m.
  3. RT = 1/(6.9 × 10−3) = 145 m.
  4. Surface stress magnitude is EαΔT/2. Half the top-to-bottom difference is 15 K.
  5. EαΔT/2 = 70 × 109 × 23 × 10−6 × 15 = 24.15 × 106 Pa = 24.15 MPa.
  6. Sign: hot top face −24.15 MPa (compression); cool bottom face +24.15 MPa (tension).

Answer. RT = 145 m; surface stresses are −24.15 MPa on the hot top face and +24.15 MPa on the cool bottom face.

Exam traps

  • Using σ = EαΔT on a free beam. A free beam bows stress-free; only restraint gives stress.
  • Forgetting the factor 1/2. Surface stress is EαΔT/2 for a linear gradient.
  • Wrong sign: with curvature held, the hot face is in compression.
  • Using a uniform rise in the gradient formula. A free uniform rise gives no stress unless axial extension is restrained.
  • Putting h in mm. Convert to m before curvature per metre.

Thin-walled idealisationReplacing a wall by a line of concentrated boom area A_i plus a wall term, valid because direct stress is nearly uniform across the small thickness.4.4
Boom areaA_i = (σ_actual / σ_boom) × (actual area), the equivalent lumped area used in the boom idealisation; for direct stress only it is the actual area.4.4
StringerA longitudinal stiffener in a wing or fuselage that carries direct bending stress and is treated as a boom.4.5
Skew loadA load whose line of action does not pass through the centroid or is not parallel to a principal axis, producing M_x and M_y together.4.5
Temperature gradientA through-depth change of temperature ΔT(y) that creates a free thermal curvature α ΔT / h, with α the coefficient of thermal expansion (per °C).4.6
Restrained thermal stressThe stress left when expansion or curvature is prevented; only the part of the thermal strain that is restrained produces stress.4.6

Takeaways

  1. When the moment acts about a principal axis, σ = M y / I and 1/R = M / (E I) are exact for the ideal beam, and the section also curves sideways by Poisson's effect, giving anticlastic bending.
  2. A non-zero I_xy tilts the neutral axis away from the moment vector, and the stress becomes a linear function of both x and y.
  3. The safest route for an unsymmetrical section is to find the principal axes, apply σ = M y / I about each of them, and add the results.
  4. Deflection is built from two components along the principal axes, and the beam moves perpendicular to the neutral axis, so it does not travel along a skew load.
  5. In a thin wall the direct stress is almost constant across the thickness, so the section reduces to booms carrying area plus thin shear-carrying walls.
  6. A wing box with stringers is just an unsymmetrical-bending problem solved boom by boom.
  7. A temperature gradient is a built-in curvature; a free beam bends with no stress, and only restraint converts that curvature into stress.

Exam question bank

Long answers are expected to derive the general unsymmetrical bending stress expression from the principal-axis form, locate the neutral axis, and then apply it to a thin-walled or stringer-stiffened section with a skew load, including the deflection.

Remember and understand

Understand Derive σ = M y / I for symmetrical bending, stating every assumption behind it. 10 marks

Plane sections stay plane and rotate about the neutral axis; Linear strain variation with y, so linear stress by Hooke's law; Equilibrium of the normal force gives the neutral axis through the centroid; Moment equilibrium gives M = E I / R, hence σ = M y / I; Limits: elastic, small deflection, no shear lag, no local buckling.

Understand What is anticlastic bending? Explain its origin and state how its curvature relates to the longitudinal curvature. 8 marks

Longitudinal extension causes lateral contraction by Poisson's ratio ν; Top surface contracts sideways, bottom surface expands; Cross-section therefore curves in the opposite sense; Transverse curvature ≈ ν / R for an isotropic material; Anti-elastic shape is why a flat plate bends into a saddle.

Understand Explain why the neutral axis of a section with I_xy ≠ 0 is not perpendicular to the applied moment vector. 10 marks

Stress is a linear function of x and y when I_xy is present; Setting σ = 0 gives a straight line through the centroid; Its slope depends on I_xx, I_yy and I_xy, not on symmetry alone; Contrast with the principal-axis case, where the neutral axis is perpendicular to M; Physical picture: the stiffer direction pulls the axis round.

Apply

Apply A section has I_xx = 12 × 10⁶ mm⁴, I_yy = 4 × 10⁶ mm⁴ and I_xy = 2.5 × 10⁶ mm⁴. It carries M_x = 8 kN·m and M_y = 0. Find the stress at the point (x, y) = (40 mm, 60 mm) and the angle of the neutral axis. 13 marks

Evaluate the denominator I_xx I_yy − I_xy²; Substitute into the general stress formula with M_y = 0; Keep units consistent: N and mm; Set σ = 0 and solve for y/x to get tan α; Check the sign convention for M_x and y.

Apply For a cantilever of unsymmetrical section under a tip load, derive the deflection components along the principal axes and hence the magnitude and direction of the total tip deflection. 13 marks

Resolve the load into components on the principal axes u and v; Each component gives its own bending moment distribution; Integrate curvature M/(E I) twice with the correct boundary conditions; Combine u and v as vectors; Show the resultant is normal to the neutral axis.

Apply A thin-walled channel section has flanges of area A_f and a web of thickness t. Idealise it into booms and calculate I_xx and I_yy. 12 marks

Direct stress is nearly uniform across the thin wall; Place boom area at each flange centroid; Add the wall's own second moment t h³/12 where needed; Use Σ A_i y_i² about the centroidal axis; Note when the wall term can be neglected.

Apply A wing box idealised into four booms of areas A₁ to A₄ carries a vertical shear force through a point offset from the centroid. Determine the direct stress in each boom. 13 marks

Replace the offset load by a shear force at the centroid plus a torque; Bending moments M_x and M_y follow from the shear distributions; I_xx, I_yy, I_xy from the boom areas; Apply the general formula boom by boom; State that the torque is carried as shear flow, not direct stress.

Apply A beam of depth h has a linear temperature difference of ΔT between its top and bottom faces and is free to bend. Find the free curvature and the stress if both ends are then fully restrained. 10 marks

Free thermal strain ε_T = α ΔT at each fibre; Compatible curvature gives 1/R = α ΔT / h; No stress when the beam is free; Full restraint applies an equal and opposite moment; Resulting stress varies linearly through the depth.

Apply Describe how the deflection shape of an unsymmetrical beam can be computed numerically, and what checks confirm the result. 12 marks

Discretise the span into stations; Compute M_x, M_y at each station; Divide by E I about each principal axis to get curvature; Integrate numerically with the end conditions built in; Check equilibrium, zero deflection at supports, expected order of magnitude; Plot the deflected shape in both planes.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare the neutral-axis method, the k method and the principal-axis method for unsymmetrical bending, and say when each is most convenient. 14 marks

All three express the same linear stress field; Principal-axis method: rotate to axes where I_xy = 0, then use σ = M y / I twice; Neutral-axis method: work directly with the tilted neutral axis and the distance from it; k method: express stress through section constants k₁, k₂; Choice depends on whether principal properties or direct geometry are known; Numerical equivalence shown by one worked case.

Analyze Explain why an unsymmetrically loaded beam deflects perpendicular to its neutral axis rather than along the load direction. 12 marks

Curvature about each principal axis is driven by the moment about that axis; A skew load produces moments on both principal axes; The two curvatures add as perpendicular vectors; The resultant curvature vector is normal to the neutral axis; Consequence: a coupling between bending directions, absent in symmetrical bending; Practical effect on tip deflection of a swept wing.

Evaluate Discuss the validity of the simple bending theory when applied to thin-walled aircraft sections, and state the corrections needed in practice. 16 marks

Assumptions: plane sections, elastic material, small deflections, no shear lag; Thin walls: direct stress nearly uniform across thickness justifies booms; Shear lag in wide skins makes stress non-linear across the width; Local buckling and crippling reduce effective boom area; Open sections twist under transverse load unless restrained; Closed boxes carry torsion by shear flow, needing shear centre treatment; Conclusion: use the theory for preliminary sizing, refine for certification.

Analyze A beam is subjected to both a bending moment and a through-depth temperature gradient. Show how the total stress is obtained and when the thermal part can be ignored. 13 marks

Thermal strain α ΔT is stress-free if free to expand; Restraint strain is the difference between actual and free strain; Superpose mechanical stress M y / I and restrained thermal stress; Fully free bending: thermal stress zero; Fully restrained: stress E α ΔT pattern; Partial restraint needs compatibility with the end conditions.

Evaluate For a wing idealised as a tapered box with stringers, outline how the bending moment distribution is found and how the boom areas are chosen to keep stress within limits. 15 marks

Loads from lift and inertia give shear force along the span; Integrate shear to get bending moment, apply boundary conditions at tip and root; Boom areas from geometry; check I_xx and I_yy at several stations; Stress at each station from the general formula; Compare with allowable stress including buckling; Iterate boom areas until the design is balanced.

Glossary

Anticlastic curvature
Opposite-sense curvature of the cross-section caused by Poisson contraction during longitudinal bending.
Bending moment
The internal moment at a section, in N·mm, equal to the sum of moments of all loads on one side of the cut.
Boom
A concentrated area at a point of the idealised section, standing for the direct-stress-carrying material of the wall or stringer.
Centroid
The point where the area of the section balances; the neutral axis always passes through it in pure bending.
Coefficient of thermal expansion
The fractional length change per degree Celsius, symbol α, about 23 × 10⁻⁶ per °C for aluminium.
Curvature
The reciprocal of the radius of curvature, in mm⁻¹, equal to M/(E I) for bending about a principal axis.
Deflection
The displacement of a point on the beam axis from its unloaded position, in mm.
Direct stress
Normal stress acting along the length of the beam, in N/mm², produced by bending or axial load.
Flexural rigidity
The product E I of a beam, in N·mm², governing its resistance to bending curvature.
Neutral axis
The line of zero bending stress in a cross-section.
Plane sections assumption
The hypothesis that a flat cross-section remains flat and rotates rigidly during bending, giving linear strain with depth.
Poisson's ratio
The ratio ν of lateral contraction to longitudinal extension in an elastic material, about 0.3 for aluminium.
Principal axes
The pair of perpendicular centroidal axes for which I_xy = 0 and I takes its extreme values.
Product second moment of area
I_xy = ∫ x y dA, in mm⁴, zero when the axes are principal axes of the section.
Restraint
Any support or connection that stops a beam from expanding, contracting or curving freely.
Second moment of area
I = ∫ y² dA about a chosen axis, in mm⁴, measuring how far the area lies from that axis.
Shear flow
Shear force per unit length along a thin wall, in N/mm, used for the torsion and shear carried by the skin.
Shear lag
The reduction of direct stress in a wide skin away from the stiffeners, so plane sections do not stay exactly plane.
Skew load
A transverse load not aligned with a principal axis, so it produces moments about both principal axes.
Skin
The outer thin sheet of a wing or fuselage that carries shear and some direct stress.
Stringer
A longitudinal stiffener that carries direct bending stress and stiffens the skin against buckling.
Thermal stress
Stress produced when free thermal expansion or curvature is prevented by restraint.
Thin-walled section
A section whose wall thickness is small compared with its overall width or depth, so stress is nearly uniform across the thickness.
Wing box
The closed torsion-box part of a wing between the front and rear spars, idealised as skin plus stringers for bending analysis.
