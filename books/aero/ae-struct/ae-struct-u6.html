6.1 Pure bending of thin plates

A thin plate is a flat sheet whose thickness t is small compared with its width. Moments applied along its edges bend it, and the bending moment at any point depends only on how sharply the sheet is curved there. One number, the plate stiffness D = Et³/[12(1 − ν²)], links curvature to moment. This chapter builds that link from the Kirchhoff assumptions, adds the twisting moment, and shows when a bent plate becomes a saddle and when it becomes a dome.

You need: beam bending, σ = M y/I and M = EI/R, and Poisson's ratio.

Kirchhoff assumptions and curvature

A plate is thin when t is under about a tenth of its smaller span. Take x and y in the mid-surface and z upward from it, and let w(x, y) be the deflection.

The Kirchhoff assumptions: straight lines normal to the mid-surface stay straight and normal after bending; the mid-surface does not stretch; the deflection w is small compared with t; and the stress normal to the plate, σz, is negligible. A point at height z then moves in the plane by u = −z ∂w/∂x and v = −z ∂w/∂y. Differentiating gives the strains:

εx = −z ∂²w/∂x²,   εy = −z ∂²w/∂y²,   γxy = −2z ∂²w/∂x∂y

εx, εy are direct strains and γxy the engineering shear strain (dimensionless); z is the height above the mid-surface (m); w is the deflection (m).

Strain is zero at the mid-surface and largest at the faces. Define the curvatures so that a sagging plate has positive curvature:

κx = −∂²w/∂x²,   κy = −∂²w/∂y²,   κxy = −∂²w/∂x∂y

κx and κy are the bending curvatures in the x and y directions and κxy is the twist of the surface, all in m⁻¹. The shear strain is γxy = 2zκxy; the factor 2 belongs to the strain, not to the twist.

Moments and the plate stiffness D

With σz = 0 each layer is in plane stress, so σx = E(εx + νεy)/(1 − ν²), σy = E(εy + νεx)/(1 − ν²) and τxy = Gγxy with G = E/2(1 + ν). A moment per unit width is stress times lever arm summed through the thickness, Mx = ∫σx z dz from −t/2 to t/2. Since ∫z² dz = t³/12, this gives:

Mx = D(κx + νκy),   My = D(κy + νκx),   Mxy = D(1 − ν)κxy

Mx and My are bending moments and Mxy the twisting moment, each per unit length of edge, in N·m/m (that is, N); κ in m⁻¹; ν is Poisson's ratio.

D = E t³/[12(1 − ν²)]

D is the plate stiffness, or flexural rigidity (N·m); E is Young's modulus (Pa); t is the thickness (m).

The twisting moment is ∫τxy z dz = 2G(t³/12)κxy = D(1 − ν)κxy. Some books give Mxy the opposite sign; its size is the same.

Where does 1/(1 − ν²) come from? In cylindrical bending the plate cannot curve in y, so a lateral stress σy = νσx builds up, and each layer behaves as if its modulus were E/(1 − ν²) instead of E. A beam strip of unit width has EI = Et³/12, so a plate held flat across is stiffer by 1/(1 − ν²), about 1.10 for ν = 0.30. If the plate is free to curve in y the two effects cancel and Mx = Et³κx/12, the beam value.

The stress at the faces follows from the moment just as for a rectangular beam of unit width: σx,max = 6Mx/t² and σy,max = 6My/t², tension on the convex face.

Anticlastic, synclastic and cylindrical bending

Apply Mx alone with the edges in y free, so My = 0. Then κy = −νκx: the sheet sags in x and hogs in y, a saddle. This is anticlastic bending. When the two principal curvatures have the same sign the surface is synclastic, like a dome or the centre of a plate under uniform pressure. Cylindrical bending is the case w = w(x) only: κy = 0, and then My = νMx, not zero.

Like a stress element, a plate under Mx, My and Mxy has two perpendicular directions with no twisting moment, on which the bending moments are largest and smallest:

M1,2 = (Mx + My)/2 ± √[((Mx − My)/2)² + Mxy²],   tan 2α = 2Mxy/(Mx − My)

M1 and M2 are the principal moments (N·m/m); α is the angle from x to the direction of M1.

Pure twist, Mxy = T alone, gives principal moments ±T at 45°: a saddle. Whether a surface is anticlastic or synclastic is decided by the signs of the principal curvatures, not of the moments. With both moments positive, the curvature κ2 = (M2 − νM1)/[D(1 − ν²)] is still negative when M2 < νM1, and the plate is a saddle.

Worked example

Question. A flat aluminium alloy plate 3 mm thick has E = 70 GPa and ν = 0.30. It is bent by edge moments Mx with the plate free to curve in y, and its curvature in x is κx = 0.05 m⁻¹. Find D, Mx, My and the largest bending stress. Then repeat for cylindrical bending, κy = 0, at the same κx.

  1. With t³ = 2.7 × 10⁻⁸ m³ and 1 − ν² = 0.91: D = 70 × 10⁹ × 2.7 × 10⁻⁸/10.92 = 173.1 N·m.
  2. Free in y: My = 0 needs κy = −νκx = −0.30 × 0.05 = −0.015 m⁻¹.
  3. Mx = D(κx + νκy) = 173.1 × (0.05 − 0.0045) = 173.1 × 0.0455 = 7.88 N·m/m. Check: Et³κx/12 = 1890 × 0.05/12 = 7.88 N·m/m, the beam-strip value.
  4. My = D(κy + νκx) = 0, as the free edges require. Face stress: σmax = 6Mx/t² = 6 × 7.88/(9 × 10⁻⁶) = 5.25 MPa.
  5. Cylindrical bending: κy = 0, so Mx = Dκx = 173.1 × 0.05 = 8.65 N·m/m and My = νMx = 2.60 N·m/m. Face stress σx = 5.77 MPa, σy = νσx = 1.73 MPa.

Answer. D = 173.1 N·m. Free in y: Mx = 7.88 N·m/m, My = 0, σmax = 5.25 MPa. Cylindrical: Mx = 8.65 N·m/m, My = 2.60 N·m/m, σmax = 5.77 MPa.

Question. The same 3 mm plate (D = 173.1 N·m) carries Mx = 10 N·m/m, My = 4 N·m/m and Mxy = 4 N·m/m at a point. Find the principal moments and their direction, the largest bending stress, and the principal curvatures. Is the surface there synclastic or anticlastic?

Exam traps

  • Using the beam value Et³/12 for D. D carries 1/(1 − ν²); dropping it makes a plate held flat across about 10% too flexible.
  • Mixing the two edge cases: free anticlastic bending has κy = −νκx and My = 0; cylindrical bending has κy = 0 and My = νMx.
  • Putting a factor 2 into the twisting moment. The shear strain is γxy = −2z ∂²w/∂x∂y, but Mxy = D(1 − ν)κxy with κxy = −∂²w/∂x∂y; the 2 is absorbed in D(1 − ν) = 2G t³/12.
  • Judging saddle or dome from the signs of the moments. The shape follows the principal curvatures; two positive moments still give a saddle when M2 < νM1.

6.2 Plates under transverse and in-plane loads

A thin plate can carry load in two ways at once. Lateral pressure q bends it, and in-plane forces Nx, Ny and Nxy stretch or squeeze its mid-surface. Both enter one equilibrium equation, so bending and membrane action combine. Fuselage skin feels cabin pressure as q and fuselage bending as in-plane compression; a wing skin feels fuel pressure and wing bending. Compression makes the same pressure bend the plate further, and the ratio of the applied compression to the buckling load tells you by how much.

You need: the moment–curvature relations and the plate stiffness D from pure bending of thin plates.

The plate equation with lateral load and membrane forces

Cut a small element dx × dy from a plate loaded by a pressure q (N/m²) on its face. Besides the moments Mx, My and Mxy it carries transverse shear forces Qx and Qy per unit length. Moment equilibrium of the element gives Qx = ∂Mx/∂x + ∂Mxy/∂y and Qy = ∂My/∂y + ∂Mxy/∂x; force equilibrium normal to the plate gives ∂Qx/∂x + ∂Qy/∂y + q = 0. Putting in the moment–curvature relations collapses the three equations into one, D∇⁴w = q.

Now let in-plane forces act as well. While the plate is flat they have no component normal to it. Once it bends, Nx on the two faces of the element points along slightly different slopes, ∂w/∂x and ∂w/∂x + (∂²w/∂x²)dx, and leaves a net normal force Nx(∂²w/∂x²)dx dy. Ny and the shear Nxy do the same. These act like extra pressure:

D∇⁴w = q + Nx ∂²w/∂x² + 2Nxy ∂²w/∂x∂y + Ny ∂²w/∂y²

w is the deflection (m); D the plate stiffness (N·m); q the lateral pressure (N/m²); Nx, Ny the in-plane direct forces per unit length (N/m), tension positive; Nxy the in-plane shear force per unit length (N/m); ∇⁴w = ∂⁴w/∂x⁴ + 2∂⁴w/∂x²∂y² + ∂⁴w/∂y⁴.

With no in-plane force the right side is just q. Nx, Ny and Nxy are stress resultants, not stresses: σx = Nx/t, σy = Ny/t and τxy = Nxy/t. A compressive Nx is negative. In a sagging plate ∂²w/∂x² is negative, so a negative Nx adds to q: compression pushes the plate further the way it is already bent, and tension pulls it back towards flat.

Navier and Levy solutions and the edge conditions

Navier's method solves a rectangular plate a × b simply supported on all four edges. Write both the load and the deflection as double sine series, w = ΣΣ Amn sin(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b). Every term has w = 0 and zero bending moment on all four edges, so each edge condition is met term by term. For uniform pressure, the load coefficients are 16q/(π²mn) for odd m and n and zero for even ones, and:

Amn = 16q/[π⁶ D m n (m²/a² + n²/b²)²],   m, n = 1, 3, 5, …

Amn is the amplitude of the (m, n) term (m); a and b are the plate sides (m); m and n count half-waves along x and y.

The first term alone gives 4.15 mm for the plate of the worked example, 2.4% above the full sum: the series converges fast for deflection, more slowly for moments. Summing it for a square plate gives the table values wmax = 0.00406 q a⁴/D and Mx = My = 0.0479 q a² at the centre for ν = 0.3. The deflection coefficient does not depend on ν, because ν is already inside D; the moment coefficient does.

Levy's method needs only two opposite edges simply supported. It expands w in a single sine series along those edges and solves an ordinary differential equation across the plate for each term, so the other two edges can be clamped or free. The edge conditions are:

EdgeDeflectionSecond condition
Simply supportedw = 0bending moment normal to the edge = 0
Clampedw = 0slope normal to the edge ∂w/∂n = 0
Freeno condition on wbending moment normal to the edge = 0 and effective shear Vx = Qx + ∂Mxy/∂y = 0; the twisting moment itself is not zero

Combined bending and in-plane load

For a simply supported plate under a uniform compression Nx = −N, substituting the sine series into the full equation gives each term exactly:

Amn(N) = Amn(0)/(1 − N/Nmn),   Nmn = π²D(m²/a² + n²/b²)²/(m/a)²

Amn(0) is the amplitude without compression; N is the compressive force per unit length (N/m); Nmn is the compression at which the (m, n) shape would stand without any pressure: its buckling load.

The lowest Nmn is the buckling load Ncr of the next chapter. Near it the first term dominates, so the deflection is magnified by about 1/(1 − N/Ncr). Tension does the reverse: N enters with the other sign and the factor becomes 1/(1 + N/Nmn). Small deflections add linearly; once w is comparable with t the mid-surface stretches and membrane forces grow with w, which needs large-deflection theory.

In an aircraft, cabin pressure is q and fuselage or wing bending supplies Nx and Ny; skins also carry Nxy from shear. A compression of 0.4Ncr may be safe for strength, yet it already raises the pressure deflection and bending stress by two thirds. Check both stress and stability.

  2. D = 70 × 10⁹ × (0.004)³/[12 × 0.91] = 4480/10.92 = 410.3 N·m.

Exam traps

  • Entering a compressive Nx as a positive number in D∇⁴w = q + Nx ∂²w/∂x² + …; tension is positive there, and a wrong sign makes compression appear to stiffen the plate.
  • Treating Nx as a stress. Nx is a force per unit length (N/m); σx = Nx/t.
  • Using the coefficient 0.00406 for clamped edges or for a non-square plate. It is for a square plate simply supported on all edges under uniform pressure.
  • Setting the twisting moment to zero on a free edge. The two free-edge conditions are zero bending moment and zero effective shear Qx + ∂Mxy/∂y.
  • Checking only stress and ignoring Nx/Ncr. A compression well below yield can still magnify deflection and bending stress, and buckles the skin at Ncr.

6.3 Buckling of thin plates: governing equation

A thin plate loaded in its own plane can suddenly bend out of plane when the compressive force reaches a critical value. Its bending stiffness D resists the bending, and the in-plane force, tilted by the bending, feeds it. Equating the two gives the plate buckling equation, an eigenvalue problem whose lowest root is the buckling load. Skin between stringers and ribs, spar webs and the flanges of stringers all buckle this way, and the result sets stringer pitch and skin thickness.

You need: the plate equation with in-plane forces from plates under transverse and in-plane loads, and Euler column buckling.

The governing equation for plate buckling

Start from the plate equation with in-plane forces and set the pressure to zero. A flat plate under in-plane load is in equilibrium for any load. Buckling asks a different question: at what load can a slightly bent shape also be in equilibrium? Then the plate can move sideways with no lateral load at all. For compression it is convenient to work with positive numbers, so here Nx, Ny and Nxy are taken positive in compression, which reverses the signs of the previous chapter:

D∇⁴w + Nx ∂²w/∂x² + 2Nxy ∂²w/∂x∂y + Ny ∂²w/∂y² = 0

w is the buckling deflection (m), small compared with t; D = Et³/[12(1 − ν²)] the plate stiffness (N·m); Nx, Ny the in-plane direct forces per unit length (N/m), positive in compression; Nxy the in-plane shear force per unit length (N/m).

The plate is taken as flat, isotropic, of uniform thickness and elastic. The equation is linear and has no load term, so w = 0 is always a solution. A non-zero shape exists only at particular loads, the eigenvalues; the smallest is the critical load. This sudden change from a flat equilibrium to a bent one at a fixed load is bifurcation, the plate form of structural instability. Tension enters with the opposite sign and raises the buckling load.

Rectangular plate under uniaxial compression

Take a plate a × b, length a along the load, simply supported on all four edges, with Nx only. The edges cannot deflect and carry no moment, so w and its second derivative normal to each edge are zero. The shape w = A sin(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b) meets all of these, with m half-waves along the load and n across. Substituting, each fourth derivative brings out a factor (mπ/a)⁴ and so on, and the equation becomes A × [Dπ⁴(m²/a² + n²/b²)² − Nx π²m²/a²] = 0. For A ≠ 0:

Nx = π²D (m²/a² + n²/b²)²/(m/a)²

Nx is the compressive force per unit length (N/m) at which the (m, n) shape is in equilibrium; a and b are the plate sides (m).

Any n > 1 only raises Nx, so the plate buckles with one half-wave across, n = 1. Writing r = a/b, the result takes the standard form:

Ncr = kπ²D/b²,   σcr = kπ²E/[12(1 − ν²)] (t/b)²,   k = (m/r + r/m)²

Ncr is the critical force per unit length (N/m); σcr = Ncr/t the critical stress (Pa); k the buckling coefficient (dimensionless); b the loaded edge width, across the load (m); r = a/b the aspect ratio; m the number of half-waves along the load, chosen to make k smallest.

Each m gives a curve of k against r with minimum 4 at r = m. The plate takes whichever m gives the lowest k. Curves m and m + 1 cross at r = √(m(m + 1)): 1.41, 2.45, 3.46. So the plate changes from one half-wave to two at a/b = 1.41. For a long plate k stays between 4 and 4.5 and tends to 4: the plate buckles into nearly square half-waves about b long, and Ncr ≈ 4π²D/b², independent of length.

Edge restraint, width and thickness

Other edge supports change k. For long plates in uniform compression, with the loaded ends simply supported, the usual values are:

Unloaded long edgesk (long plate)Where it applies
Both simply supported4.0skin between stringers, a web between flanges
One simply supported, one clamped5.42skin next to a stiff spar cap
Both clamped6.97upper bound for skin riveted to stiff stringers
One simply supported, one free0.425a stringer flange (outstanding leg)

Real riveted skins lie between the simply supported and clamped values; designers usually take k = 4 because it is safe. A free edge drops k almost tenfold, which is why outstanding flanges have small b/t limits. Because σcr depends on (t/b)², halving the stringer pitch raises the buckling stress four times, and doubling the skin thickness does the same.

Buckling is not collapse. At Ncr a small deflection grows without extra load, but a plate supported on all its edges then develops membrane tension across the buckles and carries more load, until the stringers fail or the material yields. If σcr exceeds the proportional limit, E in the formula is replaced by a reduced (tangent) modulus, and the elastic answer is too high.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular plate with a = 1.2 m along the load and b = 0.6 m across it is simply supported on all edges. It is 3 mm thick, with E = 70 GPa and ν = 0.30, and is compressed along x. Find D, the buckling coefficient and mode, the critical load per unit width and the critical stress.

  1. D = 70 × 10⁹ × (0.003)³/[12 × 0.91] = 173.1 N·m.
  2. Aspect ratio: r = a/b = 1.2/0.6 = 2.0.
  3. k = (m/r + r/m)²: m = 1 gives (0.5 + 2)² = 6.25; m = 2 gives (1 + 1)² = 4.00; m = 3 gives (1.5 + 0.667)² = 4.69. The lowest is k = 4.00 with m = 2: two half-waves, each 0.6 m long.

6.4 Stiffened panels and effective sheet width

A stiffened panel is a skin with stringers running along the load. In compression the thin skin buckles between the stringers long before the stringers fail. The buckled skin still works, but mostly in narrow strips next to each stringer. The effective width method replaces the real, uneven stress in the skin by a strip of width be working at the stringer stress. Wing upper surfaces, fuselage crowns and tail compression skins are sized this way, and the stringer with its effective skin becomes the boom of the idealised section.

You need: the plate buckling stress σcr = kπ²E(t/b)²/[12(1 − ν²)] from buckling of thin plates.

Sheets and stiffened panels under compression

A flat sheet alone is a poor compression member: its buckling stress varies as (t/b)², so a wide thin sheet buckles at a few MPa. Stringers riveted or bonded to it at a pitch p divide it into narrow bays, each a plate supported along the stringer lines, and the sheet buckling stress rises to σcr = kπ²E(t/p)²/[12(1 − ν²)], with k = 4 if the stringer lines are taken as simple supports.

Above σcr the middle of each bay bows out of plane and stops taking extra load. The sheet next to each stringer is held straight by it and keeps straining with it, so its stress keeps rising with the stringer stress σs. Across a bay the stress is therefore high at the stringers and low at mid-bay. The panel fails only when the stringers fail, by crippling or as columns between ribs. This is why a stiffened panel is much lighter than an unstiffened sheet that carries the same compressive load.

Effective sheet width

Von Kármán replaced the uneven stress by two strips of sheet, one each side of the stringer, working at the full stringer stress σs, with the rest of the bay carrying nothing. He sized the strips by asking how wide a simply supported plate could be and still be on the point of buckling at σs. Setting 4π²E(t/be)²/[12(1 − ν²)] = σs and solving for be:

be = π t/√[3(1 − ν²)] × √(E/σs) = 1.90 t √(E/σs)

be is the total effective width of sheet working with one stringer, half on each side (mm); t the sheet thickness (mm); E Young's modulus and σs the stringer stress, in the same unit; the 1.90 is for ν = 0.3.

As σs rises, be shrinks: the more the panel is loaded, the more the sheet sheds load to the stringers. The formula only applies once the sheet has buckled. When be from it is larger than the pitch p, the stress is below σcr and the whole bay is effective. The stringer and its effective sheet form one effective area, and the panel load per stringer follows:

Ae = Ast + be t,   P = σs Ae

Ae is the effective area per stringer (mm²); Ast the stringer area (mm²); P the load carried by one stringer and its sheet (N).

  • Find the stringer stress σs for the load case.
  • Check whether the sheet has buckled: compare σs with σcr of a bay of width p.
  • If it has, compute be = 1.90 t √(E/σs), and take be = p if the formula gives more.
  • Add the effective sheet to the stringer: Ae = Ast + be t.
  • Use Ae as the boom area in the idealised section, and check the stringer for crippling and for column buckling between ribs.

Limits of the method and shear lag

The 1.90 assumes the stringers hold the bay edges straight and give simple support. Riveted stringers with some rotational restraint give a little more; test-based coefficients are used in design offices. The method also assumes the stringer does not cripple first, so the local strength of the stringer must be checked separately.

Shear lag is a second reason why a wide skin is not fully effective, and it happens even without buckling. In a box beam the bending load enters the skins from the spar webs as shear. The skin strains in shear as it passes the load inwards, so the direct stress is highest next to the webs and falls towards the middle of the skin, instead of being uniform as simple bending theory assumes. Stringer loads then build up over a length along the span instead of at once. Shear lag matters most for wide, short boxes, near wing roots, at cut-outs and where concentrated loads are fed in, and it is handled with an effective width of skin in the same spirit.

6.5 Local buckling and crippling stress: Needham, Gerard

A stringer or frame made of thin flat walls can fail in compression without bending as a whole. Its widest flat element buckles first, at a stress set by its width-to-thickness ratio b/t, and the section then keeps carrying load through its corners until the corners yield and the whole section folds. That final load is the crippling load, and the average stress at it is the crippling stress σcs. Needham's and Gerard's methods estimate σcs from b/t and the material, and the rivets that join stringer to skin bring two more local checks: inter-rivet buckling and wrinkling.

You need: the plate buckling stress and the buckling coefficients for different edge supports from buckling of thin plates.

Local buckling of composite shapes

A thin-walled section such as an angle, channel, Z or hat is a composite shape: a set of flat plate elements joined along their edges. Each element in compression is a long plate. A web between two flanges has both long edges supported; an outstanding flange has one supported and one free. Its elastic local buckling stress is:

σel = kπ²E/[12(1 − ν²)] × (t/b)²

σel is the local buckling stress (N/mm²); k = 4.0 for an element with both long edges simply supported and k = 0.425 for one with a free edge; b the flat width of the element (mm); t its thickness (mm).

Choose k for the edge condition first. The elements of a section buckle together, because they share their edges: the weakest element tries to buckle first and is partly held by its stiffer neighbours, so the section local buckling stress lies between the values for its elements taken alone.

Local buckling does not end the strength of the section. The corners, where two elements meet, cannot buckle and keep taking load after the flat parts have buckled. Load rises until the material at the corners yields; then the section folds locally. This is crippling, and the crippling stress σcs = Pcs/A is the average stress over the whole section at that load. It is above σel for thin sections and cannot exceed the compressive yield stress σcy.

Crippling stress by Needham's and Gerard's methods

Both methods fit tests on short lengths of formed and extruded sections. Needham cut each section into angles and found that an angle's crippling stress depends on its legs through b′/t:

σcs/√(σcy E) = Ce/(b′/t)0.75,   b′ = (a + b)/2

σcs is the crippling stress of the angle (N/mm²); σcy the compressive yield stress (N/mm²); E Young's modulus (N/mm²); a and b the leg lengths (mm); t the thickness (mm); Ce = 0.316 when both leg edges are free, 0.342 when one is free and 0.366 when neither is free.

A channel, Z or hat is cut into angles at the middle of its webs. A cut edge is not free, because the rest of the section holds it, so a channel becomes two angles each with one free edge. Each angle is given its crippling load σcs A, and the section crippling stress is the total load over the total area, σcs = ΣP/ΣA.

Gerard correlated the same kind of tests with a single parameter that counts how many flat elements the section has for its area. For angles, tubes, multi-corner sections and stiffened plates:

σcs/σcy = 0.56 [(g t²/A) √(E/σcy)]0.85

A is the section area (mm²); g is the number of flanges plus the number of cuts needed to divide the section into angles; the other symbols as above.

For two-corner sections (Z, J and channel) Gerard gives a separate formula of the same kind with its own constants, taken from the data sheet used in the course. Both methods must be capped: σcs is never taken above σcy, and data sheets set lower caps for some shapes.

Inter-rivet buckling, wrinkling and the design checks

A skin riveted to a stringer is pinned to it only at the rivets. Between two rivets at pitch s the skin is a short, wide strip in compression that can bow away from the stringer like a column with partly fixed ends. Treating it as a plate strip of length s:

σir = c π²E/[12(1 − ν²)] × (t/s)²

σir is the inter-rivet buckling stress (N/mm²); s the rivet pitch along the load (mm); t the skin thickness (mm); c the end fixity, 4 for fully fixed ends and 1 for pinned ends; flush rivets give less fixity than protruding heads.

If σir is below the stringer stress, the skin bows away between rivets and the panel loses its effective skin; close the pitch or thicken the skin. Wrinkling is a different local failure: in a skin on a stringer flange, the skin and the attached flange buckle together in short waves at the attachment line, and the flange is pulled or pushed out of plane. It depends on skin thickness, flange thickness, rivet size and spacing, and is checked from test-based charts; it can set a lower stress than the crippling of the stringer.

6.6 Flexural-torsional buckling of thin-walled columns

Open thin-walled columns such as channels, angles and Z-sections are stiff in bending but very weak in torsion, and for most of them the centroid C and the shear centre S are at different points. An axial load through C then makes bending and twisting grow together once the column starts to deflect, and the column buckles in a combined bending and twisting mode at a load below its Euler load. Stringers and frames are open sections, so this check decides their length between supports.

You need: Euler column buckling, the shear centre of a channel and the torsion constant J = Σbt³/3 of open sections, and the crippling stress from local buckling and crippling.

Why an open column twists as it buckles

An open section resists torque mainly through its tiny St Venant constant J = Σbt³/3, plus resistance to warping when its ends or neighbours stop the section warping freely. That second effect is measured by the warping constant Cw (mm⁶). The shear centre S is the point a transverse load must pass through to cause no twist.

Let x₀ and y₀ locate S from the centroid C. While the column is straight, an axial load P through C has no moment about the column axis. Once the column deflects by u and v and twists by θ, the section rotates about S, which moves C sideways by an amount proportional to θ and the offset. The axial load, now acting along a curved line through the displaced centroids, produces a twisting moment about S of order P(x₀ v″ − y₀ u″) per unit length, and the twist in turn adds to the bending. The two grow together: flexural–torsional buckling.

When C and S coincide, as in an I-section or a cruciform, the coupling vanishes: the column buckles either by bending about one axis at the Euler load, or by pure twisting about its axis, the torsional instability that short cruciforms show. A singly symmetric section has S on its axis of symmetry; bending about that axis couples with twist, while bending in the plane of symmetry stays a separate Euler mode.

Torsional and flexural–torsional buckling loads

For a pin-ended column free to warp at its ends, three uncoupled loads are defined:

Px = π²E Ix/L²,   Py = π²E Iy/L²,   Pt = (GJ + π²E Cw/L²)/r₀²,   r₀² = (Ix + Iy)/A + x₀² + y₀²

Px, Py are the Euler loads for bending about the centroidal x and y axes (N); Pt the torsional buckling load (N); Ix, Iy the centroidal second moments (mm⁴); A the area (mm²); G the shear modulus (N/mm²); J the torsion constant (mm⁴); Cw the warping constant (mm⁶); L the length (mm); r₀ the polar radius of gyration about the shear centre (mm).

GJ is St Venant's resistance and π²ECw/L² the warping resistance; the second fades as L grows. In general the buckling load is the smallest root of the cubic:

r₀²(P − Px)(P − Py)(P − Pt) − P²x₀²(P − Py) − P²y₀²(P − Px) = 0

x₀, y₀ are the coordinates of the shear centre from the centroid (mm). The x₀ offset couples twist with bending about x, and y₀ with bending about y.

For a section symmetric about x (y₀ = 0) the cubic splits: P = Py is a pure Euler mode, and bending about x couples with twist through (Px − P)(Pt − P) − P²x₀²/r₀² = 0. The smaller root of this quadratic is always below both Px and Pt, so the column is weaker than either mode alone suggests. A closed tube has J hundreds of times larger, so Pt is far above the Euler loads and flexural–torsional buckling hardly ever governs.

Thin-walled column strength

Euler's formula σ = π²E/(L/ρ)² holds for slender columns, with ρ = √(I/A) the radius of gyration. A short thin-walled column cannot reach Euler's stress: it cripples first, at σcs. Between the two, local buckling and bending interact, and a parabola due to Johnson joins them:

σc = σcs [1 − σcs (L′/ρ)²/(4π²E)]

σc is the column failing stress (N/mm²); σcs the crippling stress (N/mm²); L′ the effective length (mm), equal to L for pinned ends; ρ the least radius of gyration (mm).

The parabola starts at σcs for a stub and touches the Euler curve at σ = σcs/2, where L′/ρ = √(2π²E/σcs). Above that slenderness use Euler; below it use the parabola. For open sections, also check the flexural–torsional load, which can be lower than both.

Worked example

6.7 Composites: FRP and orthotropic plies

A fibre-reinforced plastic (FRP) carries load along stiff, strong fibres held in a soft polymer matrix. One thin layer, a ply, is very stiff along its fibres and soft across them: it is orthotropic, and four elastic constants describe it. Wing skins, control surfaces and blades are stacks of such plies at chosen angles. This chapter estimates the ply constants from the fibre and matrix, builds the ply stiffness matrix along its fibres, and rotates it to the axes of the structure.

You need: Hooke's law with Poisson's ratio in plane stress, and the transformation of stress and strain to rotated axes.

Fibres, matrix and the orthotropic ply

An FRP is a matrix, usually epoxy, holding long thin fibres of carbon, glass or aramid. The fibres give stiffness and strength along their length; the matrix keeps them in line, passes load between them by shear and protects them. A ply is one layer, typically about 0.125 mm thick for carbon/epoxy. Axis 1 runs along the fibres, axis 2 across them in the plane, axis 3 through the thickness. A skin ply is in plane stress, σ3 = 0, so only axes 1 and 2 matter.

With all fibres in one direction, the ply has three perpendicular planes of symmetry: it is orthotropic. In the plane four constants describe it: E1 along the fibres, E2 across, the major Poisson's ratio ν12 (the contraction in 2 per unit strain in 1) and the in-plane shear modulus G12. Simple micromechanics estimates the first two from the fibre volume fraction Vf:

E1 = Ef Vf + Em Vm,   1/E2 = Vf/Ef + Vm/Em

Ef and Em are the fibre and matrix moduli (GPa); Vf and Vm = 1 − Vf the volume fractions. Along the fibres both phases stretch equally (rule of mixtures); across them they carry the same stress in series (inverse rule).

The inverse rule is rough, and fails for carbon, whose fibres are soft across their length; measured values are used. A typical carbon/epoxy ply has E1 = 140 GPa, E2 = 10 GPa, ν12 = 0.30 and G12 = 5 GPa. G12 and E2 are low because the matrix carries them.

The four constants and the Q matrix

In its own axes the ply's stress–strain law has no coupling between normal and shear terms:

σ1 = Q11 ε1 + Q12 ε2,   σ2 = Q12 ε1 + Q22 ε2,   τ12 = Q66 γ12

σ1, σ2 are the normal stresses along and across the fibres and τ12 the in-plane shear stress (GPa or MPa); ε1, ε2 the normal strains and γ12 the engineering shear strain.

Q11 = E1/(1 − ν12ν21),   Q22 = E2/(1 − ν12ν21),   Q12 = ν12E2/(1 − ν12ν21),   Q66 = G12

The Q terms are the reduced stiffnesses, in the unit of E; ν21 = ν12E2/E1 is the minor Poisson's ratio.

The relation ν12/E1 = ν21/E2 keeps the matrix symmetric, so ν21 is always the smaller ratio. Q11 is not a modulus. E1 and E2 come from a uniaxial test in which the ply contracts freely sideways. Q11, Q12 and Q22 describe the state in which sideways contraction is fully prevented (ε2 = 0), so the ply is stiffer, and Q11 is slightly larger than E1. The word reduced refers to having set the through-thickness stress σ3 to zero.

Rotating the ply to the structure axes

In a panel the plies lie at angles. Let x, y be the structure axes and θ the angle from x to the fibre direction, anticlockwise positive, with m = cos θ and n = sin θ. Transforming stress and strain gives the stiffness in x, y, written Q̄:

Q̄11 = Q11 m⁴ + 2(Q12 + 2Q66) m²n² + Q22 n⁴,   Q̄16 = (Q11 − Q12 − 2Q66) m³n + (Q12 − Q22 + 2Q66) m n³

Q̄11 is the stiffness along x; Q̄16 couples direct stress along x to shear strain. Q̄22 follows from Q̄11 by swapping m and n, and Q̄26 from Q̄16 likewise.

Q̄12 = (Q11 + Q22 − 4Q66) m²n² + Q12(m⁴ + n⁴),   Q̄66 = (Q11 + Q22 − 2Q12 − 2Q66) m²n² + Q66(m⁴ + n⁴)

Q̄12 is the Poisson coupling term and Q̄66 the shear stiffness in x, y.

Q̄16 and Q̄26 are zero at 0° and 90° and largest in between, near 30° and 60° respectively for this carbon/epoxy. They are extension–shear coupling: pull an off-axis ply along x and it shears as well as stretches. Q̄11 falls very fast as the fibres turn away from the load. This is how a laminate is tailored: 0° plies for axial stiffness, ±45° plies for shear stiffness and to resist buckling, 90° plies for transverse loads.

6.8 Laminate theory, stiffness matrix and composite beams

A laminate is a stack of plies bonded together, each at its own fibre angle. Every ply is rotated to the laminate axes and then added up through the thickness, which gives three stiffness matrices: A for stretching, D for bending and B for the coupling between them. These turn the mid-plane strains and curvatures of a panel into forces and moments per unit width. They are how wing skins, spars and blades are given the stiffness they need, and how a composite strip is given its beam stiffness EI.

You need: the rotated ply stiffness Q̄ from composites: FRP and orthotropic plies, and the curvatures of pure bending of thin plates.

From plies to A, B and D

Describe a laminate by its stacking sequence, the ply angles from one face to the other, such as [0/90/90/0], written [0/90]s because it is symmetric about the mid-plane. Measure z from the mid-plane; ply k lies between zk−1 and zk.

Classical laminate theory makes the Kirchhoff assumption for the whole stack: the strain at height z is the mid-plane strain plus z times the curvature, ε = ε⁰ + zκ. Each ply turns its strain into stress through its own Q̄. Integrating stress through the thickness gives the force per unit width N; integrating stress times z gives the moment per unit width M. Because Q̄ is constant within a ply, the integrals become sums:

Aij = Σ Q̄ij,k (zk − zk−1),   Bij = ½ Σ Q̄ij,k (zk² − zk−1²),   Dij = ⅓ Σ Q̄ij,k (zk³ − zk−1³)

Aij is the extensional stiffness (N/mm); Bij the coupling stiffness (N); Dij the bending stiffness (N·mm); Q̄ in N/mm² (1 GPa = 1000 N/mm²); z in mm; i, j = 1, 2, 6.

A weights every ply by its thickness, so it does not depend on the order of the plies. D weights each ply by roughly z² times its thickness, so the outer plies dominate bending. B weights plies by z, with opposite signs above and below the mid-plane.

What the matrices mean

[N; M] = [A B; B D] [ε⁰; κ]

N = (Nx, Ny, Nxy) are the in-plane forces per unit width (N/mm); M = (Mx, My, Mxy) the moments per unit width (N·mm/mm); ε⁰ the mid-plane strains; κ the curvatures (mm⁻¹).

In a symmetric laminate every ply at +z has a twin at −z, their contributions to B cancel, and B = 0: N = Aε⁰ and M = Dκ, so stretching and bending are independent. In an unsymmetric laminate B ≠ 0: an in-plane load bends the plate and a moment stretches it. The same coupling makes an unsymmetric laminate warp as it cools after cure, because the thermal contraction of the plies acts as an in-plane load.

A balanced laminate has a −θ ply for every +θ ply, which makes A16 = A26 = 0 so that in-plane loads cause no shear. D16 and D26 still survive unless the twins are mirrored about the mid-plane; they couple bending and twisting, which designers use on purpose in aeroelastic tailoring.

Composite beam stiffness

A laminate strip of width b bent about an axis across it behaves as a beam only if we say what happens across the width. If the strip is held so that κy = 0, as in cylindrical bending, Mx = D11κx and EI = bD11.

A narrow beam is free to curl sideways, so My = 0 instead. For a symmetric laminate with no bend–twist coupling, My = D12κx + D22κy = 0 gives κy = −(D12/D22)κx, and then:

EI = b (D11 − D12²/D22)

EI is the bending stiffness of a narrow symmetric laminate beam (N·mm²); b its width (mm); D11, D12, D22 the bending stiffnesses per unit width (N·mm).

This is the laminate form of the anticlastic case for an isotropic plate, where the same algebra gives D(1 − ν²) = Et³/12. A wide panel is closer to κy = 0; real beams lie between the two limits.

6.9 Structural vibrations and aeroelasticity basics

Every structure has natural frequencies at which it prefers to vibrate. A wing, a tailplane or a skin panel that is struck rings at these frequencies, and its damping decides how quickly the motion dies out. In flight the airflow over a deforming wing changes the air loads, and the air loads change the deformation: that interaction is aeroelasticity. At one speed a wing can twist without limit, which is divergence; at another it can oscillate with growing amplitude, which is flutter. This chapter builds the one-degree-of-freedom vibration model, reduces a wing to it, and uses it to explain divergence and flutter.

You need: Newton's second law, beam bending stiffness EI, and lift and pitching moment on an aerofoil.

One degree of freedom: mass, spring and damper

Take a mass m (kg) on a spring of stiffness k (N/m) with a viscous damper c (N·s/m), displaced by x(t) (m) and driven by a force F(t). Newton's law gives m ẍ + c ẋ + k x = F(t). With no force and no damping the mass oscillates for ever at its natural frequency:

ωn = √(k/m),   fn = ωn/2π,   ζ = c/(2√(km))

ωn is the undamped natural frequency (rad/s); fn the same in Hz; ζ the damping ratio (dimensionless); cc = 2√(km) the critical damping (N·s/m).

Damping sets the character of free motion. ζ < 1 is underdamped: the mass oscillates at ωd = ωn√(1 − ζ²) while the motion dies away. ζ = 1 is critically damped: the fastest return with no overshoot. ζ > 1 is overdamped: a slow return with no oscillation. Aircraft structures are lightly damped, with ζ of a few per cent.

Under a harmonic force F₀ sin Ωt the steady response has amplitude X, which depends on the frequency ratio r = Ω/ωn:

X = (F₀/k)/√[(1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²]

X is the amplitude (m); F₀/k the static deflection under F₀ (m); r = Ω/ωn. At r = 1, X = F₀/(2ζk).

Near r = 1 the response peaks: resonance. The peak is 1/(2ζ) times the static deflection, so a structure with ζ = 0.02 responds 25 times more at resonance than to a steady force of the same size. That is why engine, propeller and rotor frequencies are kept away from the structure's natural frequencies.

Reducing a wing to a lumped model

A real wing has distributed mass and stiffness, so it has many natural frequencies, each with a mode shape in which all points move in step: first bending, first torsion, second bending and so on. For a hand estimate, assume a shape φ(x) for one mode and let the wing move as w(x, t) = φ(x) q(t). Kinetic and strain energy then reduce the wing to one mass and one spring for the coordinate q:

M = ∫ μ φ² dx, K = ∫ EI (φ″)² dx, ω² = K/M

M is the generalised mass (kg); K the generalised stiffness (N/m); μ the mass per unit length (kg/m); EI the bending stiffness (N·m²); φ″ the second derivative of the assumed shape; the integrals run over the span.

This is Rayleigh's method. The assumed shape must meet the support conditions (for a cantilever, zero deflection and slope at the root). Any such shape gives a frequency at or above the true one, because a wrong shape is effectively a stiffer structure, so the lowest estimate is the best. A softer laminate, a heavier fuel load or a longer span all lower ω.

Divergence, flutter and reduced frequency

Divergence is static. Take a wing section whose aerodynamic centre lies a distance e ahead of its flexural axis, held by a torsional stiffness K_θ (N·m/rad). Lift L = q S a₁(α₀ + θ) acts at the aerodynamic centre, so it twists the section nose up by θ, which raises the lift further. Moment balance about the flexural axis gives K_θθ = q S e a₁(α₀ + θ), so θ = q S e a₁α₀/(K_θ − q S e a₁). The twist grows without limit as the denominator reaches zero:

qD = K_θ/(e S a₁),   VD = √(2qD/ρ)

qD is the divergence dynamic pressure (N/m²); e the distance of the aerodynamic centre ahead of the flexural axis (m); S the wing area of the section (m²); a₁ = dC_L/dα the lift-curve slope (per rad); VD the divergence speed (m/s); ρ the air density (kg/m³).

Below divergence the twist is magnified by 1/(1 − q/qD). Moving the flexural axis forward (smaller e) or stiffening the wing in torsion raises VD. A swept-back wing bends in a way that reduces its angle of attack and rarely diverges; forward sweep does the opposite.

Flutter is dynamic. The bending and torsion modes of a wing couple through the unsteady air loads, which lag behind the motion. Above the flutter speed the air feeds more energy into the oscillation each cycle than the structural damping removes: the effective damping becomes negative and the amplitude grows. Flutter can occur below the divergence speed, so both must be shown to lie beyond the flight envelope. Mass balance ahead of the flexural axis and high torsional stiffness raise the flutter speed. How strongly the air loads lag is set by the reduced frequency kr = ωb/V, with b the semi-chord (m), ω the frequency (rad/s) and V the airspeed (m/s): small kr means nearly steady air loads, large kr strong lag.

6.10 Plate buckling under shear and combined loading

A flat panel can buckle under in-plane shear alone. Shear is tension along one diagonal and compression along the other, and the compression diagonal wrinkles the plate into inclined waves. Spar webs, fuselage side panels and wing skins between stringers carry shear and compression together, and each load then lowers the load the other can carry. The safe combinations lie inside an interaction curve, and for compression with shear that curve is not a circle.

You need: Ncr = kπ²D/b² and the critical stress formula from buckling of thin plates.

Shear buckling and the coefficient ks

A shear force per unit length Nxy (N/m) on the edges of a plate gives a shear stress τ = Nxy/t. On axes at 45° this is equal tension and compression. The compressed diagonal behaves like a strut and can buckle, so the plate wrinkles in waves inclined to the edges. The buckling equation is the same as for compression, with the Nxy term, and its lowest root has the same form:

Nxy,cr = ks π²D/b²,   τcr = ks π²E/[12(1 − ν²)] × (t/b)²

Nxy,cr is the critical shear force per unit length (N/m); τcr the critical shear stress (Pa); ks the shear buckling coefficient; D the plate stiffness (N·m); b the shorter side of the panel (m).

ks ≈ 5.35 + 4(b/a)²   (all edges simply supported, a ≥ b)

a is the longer side (m). A square plate has ks ≈ 9.35; a long strip tends to 5.35.

Clamped edges raise ks; it is read from charts for that case and must not be mixed with the simply supported fit. Because ks for shear is larger than kc = 4 for compression, a panel takes a larger shear stress than compressive stress before buckling, but webs are thin and their shear buckling stress is still only a few MPa.

Compression combined with shear

When several loads act, express each as a ratio to the stress that would buckle the panel under that load alone: Rc = σ/σcr for compression and Rs = τ/τcr for shear. Buckling occurs when the combination reaches an interaction curve. Tests and analysis give curves of the general form R₁ˣ + R₂ʸ = 1, with exponents that depend on the loads and the panel. For a long flat panel under longitudinal compression and shear:

Rc + Rs² = 1

Rc is the compressive stress ratio σ/σcr and Rs the shear stress ratio τ/τcr, each with its single-load critical stress.

The shear ratio is squared because shear of either sign is equally bad: reversing the shear only mirrors the inclined buckle, so the curve must be symmetric in Rs. Compression enters linearly. Inside the curve the panel is unbuckled. The curve lies outside the straight line Rc + Rs = 1 but inside the circle Rc² + Rs² = 1, so using the circle for this case is unsafe.

If all loads grow in proportion by a factor λ, buckling happens when λRc + λ²Rs² = 1. Solving the quadratic gives the reserve factor and the margin of safety:

λ = 2/[Rc + √(Rc² + 4Rs²)],   MS = λ − 1

λ is the factor on the applied loads at buckling; MS the margin of safety, positive when the panel does not buckle.

Other combinations, such as bending with shear in a spar web, or biaxial compression, have their own interaction curves with different exponents, taken from design charts.

What happens after shear buckling

τcr is the onset of buckling, not collapse. A thin web that has buckled in shear keeps carrying load as a diagonal tension field, anchored by its flanges and stiffeners; the analysis is the tension field theory of thin-webbed beams. Many aircraft webs are designed to buckle well below their ultimate load. A skin that must stay smooth for aerodynamics or to support stringers, however, is sized so that the combined ratio stays inside the curve at limit load, and repeated buckling in service needs a fatigue check at the wrinkle crests and the rivets.

Takeaways

  1. A thin plate bends through D = Et³/[12(1 − ν²)], and the shape of the bent surface is set by its principal curvatures.
  2. Pressure and in-plane forces enter one plate equation; compression magnifies bending by about 1/(1 − N/Ncr).

  5. Thin-walled sections cripple above their local buckling stress and below yield; Needham and Gerard estimate the crippling stress from b/t.
  6. Open columns with an offset shear centre buckle by bending and twisting together, below their Euler and torsional loads.
  7. A ply is orthotropic and is rotated to structure axes as Q̄; a laminate sums its plies into A, B and D, and B = 0 only if it is symmetric.
  8. Structures resonate at their natural frequencies; aeroelastic coupling gives divergence (static) and flutter (dynamic) speeds.
  9. Compression and shear together follow Rc + Rs² = 1 for a long panel; the circle overestimates the allowable shear.

  3. Numbers (aluminium, t = 1.5 mm, 200 mm² stringers): at p = 150 mm, σcr = 25.3 MPa; at p = 100 mm it would be 56.9 MPa, but 50% more stringers are needed.
  4. Effective width: with p = 150 mm and stringers failing at 250 MPa, be = 47.7 mm and the panel of eight stringers carries about 543 kN, an average of 160 MPa on its gross area.
  5. Crippling: the stringer's own crippling stress sets σs at failure. Thinner stringer walls cut weight but lower σcs as (b′/t)−0.75 (Needham); a 30 × 30 × 1.5 angle cripples at about 155 MPa against 300 MPa yield. Z, J and hat sections, with more corners, cripple at higher stress.
  6. Column and flexural–torsional buckling: rib spacing sets the column length of stringer plus effective skin; open Z or channel stringers can twist, and a hat (closed with the skin) resists this.
  7. Local checks: inter-rivet buckling sets the rivet pitch (about 25 to 50 mm for 1.2 to 1.5 mm skin at 150 to 250 MPa); wrinkling sets attachment flange sizes.
  8. Material choice: carbon/epoxy has E1 = 140 GPa at about 1600 kg/m³ against 70 GPa at 2800 kg/m³ for aluminium, so a mainly 0° laminate is several times stiffer per weight along the span. But a single 45° ply has Ex of only 13.2 GPa: the laminate needs ±45° plies for shear and buckling, which lowers its axial stiffness, and B = 0 (symmetric) to avoid warping.
  9. Composite panels are usually not allowed to buckle at limit load, because post-buckled skins can separate from stringers; they are made thicker or of sandwich construction, which raises D greatly. Damage tolerance (impact) and repair also favour thicker skins.
  10. Summary of trade-offs:
    ChoiceGainCost
    closer stringershigher σcr, smaller skinmore stringers, rivets
    skin buckles before ultimatelighter skinstringer load, fatigue, waviness
    thin-walled stringerslighterlower σcs, inter-rivet limits

  11. Conclusion: for a metal wing the lightest design lets the skin buckle above limit load, with stringer pitch and section chosen so that crippling, column and flexural–torsional failure occur at about the same load; for a composite wing the skin is kept unbuckled and laminate stacking is chosen for axial stiffness, shear and buckling together.
  12. Check of the metal numbers: 8 × 250 × (200 + 47.7 × 1.5) = 543 kN.

Glossary

Aspect ratio
a/b, the panel length along the load over its width across it.
Bifurcation
The load at which a second, bent equilibrium appears beside the flat one; the onset of buckling.
Clamped edge
An edge held against deflection and rotation: w = 0 and ∂w/∂n = 0.
Crippling
Local collapse of a thin-walled section after its flat elements have buckled and its corners have yielded.
Curvature
The rate of change of slope, κ = −∂²w/∂x², in m⁻¹.
Divergence
Static aeroelastic instability in which the twist of a wing grows without limit at a critical dynamic pressure.
Effective width
The width of buckled skin which, at the stringer stress, carries the load of the real skin.
Flexural rigidity
Another name for the plate stiffness D.
Flutter
Dynamic aeroelastic instability: a self-excited oscillation that grows when the net damping becomes negative.
Inter-rivet buckling
Buckling of skin away from a stringer between two rivets.
Lamina
One ply of a composite, with all its fibres in one direction.
Laminate
A stack of bonded plies at chosen angles.
Membrane force
An in-plane force per unit length of a plate, Nx, Ny or Nxy, in N/m.
Mid-surface
The surface halfway through the thickness of a plate, from which z is measured.
Orthotropic
Having three perpendicular planes of material symmetry, with different stiffness along each axis.
Reduced frequency
kr = ωb/V, the frequency of motion compared with the time the flow takes to cross the semi-chord.
Shear centre
The point through which a transverse load bends a section without twisting it.
Shear lag
The non-uniform direct stress in a wide flange or skin that receives its load by shear from webs.
Simply supported edge
An edge with no deflection and no bending moment.
Stringer
A longitudinal stiffener attached to the skin.
Symmetric laminate
A laminate whose plies mirror each other about the mid-plane, so that B = 0.
Warping
Out-of-plane displacement of the points of a cross-section under torsion.
Wrinkling
Short-wave buckling of skin and attached stringer flange together at the attachment line.
