6.1 Pure bending of thin plates

A thin plate is a flat sheet whose thickness t is small compared with its width. Loads across its face bend it, and the bending moments at any point depend only on how sharply the sheet is curved there. One number, the plate stiffness D = Et³/[12(1−ν²)], links curvature to moment. This chapter builds that link from the Kirchhoff assumptions and finds Mx, My and the bending stress. Wing skins, fuselage panels and sandwich faces are all designed this way. Only a few universities teach the full derivation; every syllabus uses the results.

You need: bending stress σ = My/I and second moment of area from Unit 4, Bending of Thin-Walled Beams, plus Poisson's ratio and plane stress from strength of materials.

Kirchhoff assumptions and curvature

A plate is thin when t is under about one tenth of its smaller span b. In pure bending only edge moments act: no in-plane force and no pressure on the face. Measure z upward from the mid-surface.

Kirchhoff assumptions: straight normals to the mid-surface stay straight and normal after bending; the mid-surface does not stretch; the deflection w is small compared with t. A point at height z then moves in-plane by u = −z ∂w/∂x and v = −z ∂w/∂y. Differentiating gives εx = −z ∂²w/∂x², εy = −z ∂²w/∂y², γxy = −2z ∂²w/∂x∂y. Strain is zero at the mid-surface and largest at the faces z = ±t/2.

κx = −∂²w/∂x²,  κy = −∂²w/∂y²,  κxy = −2 ∂²w/∂x∂y

w is the deflection in m; x, y are in-plane distances in m; κx, κy are bending curvatures and κxy the twisting curvature, all in m⁻¹.

Moments and the plate stiffness D

Through the thickness the plate is in plane stress, σz = 0, so σx = E(εx + νεy)/(1−ν²) and σy = E(εy + νεx)/(1−ν²). A moment per unit width is stress times z, summed through the thickness, which gives the moment–curvature relations below.

Mx = D(κx + νκy),  My = D(κy + νκx),  Mxy = D(1−ν)κxy

Mx, My are bending moments and Mxy the twisting moment, each per unit width, in N·m/m (which is N).

D = E t³ / [12(1 − ν²)]

E is Young's modulus in Pa, t is thickness in m, ν is Poisson's ratio (no units), and D is the plate stiffness in N·m.

For a beam, EI per unit width is Et³/12. The plate is stiffer by the factor 1/(1−ν²), about 1.10 for aluminium with ν = 0.30, because bending in x also stretches the plate in y and Poisson's effect resists that stretch.

Anticlastic, synclastic and cylindrical bending

Apply Mx alone with the edges free in y, so My = 0. Then κy = −νκx: the sheet curves down in x and up in y, forming a saddle. This is anticlastic curvature; a wide sheet bent over a drum lifts at its free edges. When both curvatures have the same sign the surface is synclastic, like a spherical shell or the centre of a plate under uniform pressure.

Cylindrical bending is the case w = w(x) only. Then κy = 0 and My = νMx, not zero. Mixing these two cases is the commonest error in the topic. Loading in both directions, buckling and stiffened panels come in 6.2 and 6.3.

Worked example

Question. A flat aluminium plate of thickness t = 3 mm has E = 70 GPa and ν = 0.30. Edge moments Mx only are applied, and the plate is free to curve in y, so κy = −νκx. The curvature in x is κx = 0.05 m⁻¹. Find D, Mx, My and the maximum bending stress.

  1. Convert thickness: t = 3 mm = 0.003 m. Then 1 − ν² = 1 − 0.09 = 0.91.
  2. Cube the thickness: t³ = 2.7 × 10⁻⁸ m³.
  3. Plate stiffness: D = Et³/[12(1−ν²)] = (70 × 10⁹ × 2.7 × 10⁻⁸)/(12 × 0.91) = 1890/10.92 = 173.1 N·m.
  4. Free curvature in y: κy = −νκx = −0.30 × 0.05 = −0.015 m⁻¹.
  5. Mx = D(κx + νκy) = 173.1 × (0.05 − 0.30 × 0.015) = 173.1 × 0.0455 = 7.88 N·m/m.
  6. My = D(κy + νκx) = 173.1 × (−0.015 + 0.015) = 0 N·m/m, as expected for a free edge.
  7. Maximum stress at the faces: σmax = 6Mx/t² = (6 × 7.88)/(9 × 10⁻⁶) = 5.25 × 10⁶ Pa = 5.25 MPa.

Answer. D = 173.1 N·m, Mx = 7.88 N·m/m, My = 0, and σmax = 5.25 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Using EI per unit width, Et³/12, instead of D. The answer is then about 10% low because (1−ν²) is dropped.
  • Writing Mx = Dκx and ignoring the νκy term. Both curvatures feed both moments.
  • Mixing the two edge conditions: free anticlastic bending gives κy = −νκx and My = 0, cylindrical bending gives κy = 0 and My = νMx. Examiners ask for the difference.
  • Losing the factor 2 in the twisting curvature κxy = −2∂²w/∂x∂y, so Mxy comes out half the correct value.
  • Treating Mx as a total moment. It is per unit width (N·m/m); multiply by the plate width to get N·m.

6.2 Plates under transverse and in-plane loads

A thin plate can carry load in two ways at once. Lateral pressure q bends it, and in-plane forces Nx, Ny, Nxy stretch or compress its middle surface. Both enter the same equilibrium equation, so bending and membrane action combine. Aircraft skin feels cabin pressure as q and wing bending as in-plane compression. The ratio of applied compression to the plate buckling load tells you how much the plate has softened. The same equation solves rectangular plates by Navier and Levy series.

You need: 6.1 for D and moment–curvature; Unit 2, energy methods for deflection.

The plate equation with membrane forces

Take a thin rectangular plate of thickness t, Young's modulus E and Poisson's ratio ν. Let w(x,y) be the small deflection normal to the plate, in metres. The flexural rigidity is D = Et³/[12(1−ν²)], in N·m. The term (1−ν²) is not optional; it comes from the lateral contraction that a plate is not free to make.

D ∇⁴w = q + Nx ∂²w/∂x² + Ny ∂²w/∂y² + 2Nxy ∂²w/∂x∂y

q is lateral pressure in N/m²; Nx, Ny are in-plane normal forces per unit length in N/m; Nxy is in-plane shear force per unit length in N/m; ∇⁴w = ∂⁴w/∂x⁴ + 2∂⁴w/∂x²∂y² + ∂⁴w/∂y⁴; tension is positive.

With no in-plane forces the right side is just q, and you recover ∇⁴w = q/D from 6.1. A compressive Nx is negative. It then subtracts from the bending stiffness, so the same q gives more w. At a critical compression the plate can deflect with q = 0; that is the buckling problem of 6.3.

Nx, Ny, Nxy are stress resultants, not stresses. Divide by t to get average membrane stresses σx = Nx/t, σy = Ny/t and τxy = Nxy/t. Nxy acts on the twisting curvature ∂²w/∂x∂y, so it enters the equation through the mixed derivative. Plates carrying both lateral pressure and in-plane forces are sometimes called plates under combined bending and membrane action.

Navier and Levy solutions for rectangular plates

Navier's solution works when all four edges are simply supported. Write the load as a double sine series and the deflection as w = ΣΣ A_mn sin(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b). Each term satisfies w = 0 and zero bending moment on the edges. For uniform q on an a × b plate, A_mn = 16q/[π⁶ D m n (m²/a² + n²/b²)²] for odd m,n; even terms vanish.

Levy's solution works when two opposite edges are simply supported and the other two have any condition. Expand only in y, solve an ordinary differential equation in x, and fit the two remaining edges. Clamped edges need w = 0 and ∂w/∂n = 0; a free edge needs zero bending moment normal to the edge Mx = 0 and zero effective shear Vx = Qx + ∂Mxy/∂y = 0. The twisting moment is carried by the effective shear, so it is not set to zero.

EdgeDeflectionSlope / moment
Simply supportedw = 0bending moment normal to edge = 0
Clampedw = 0∂w/∂n = 0
Freeno condition on wbending moment normal to edge Mx = 0 and effective shear Vx = Qx + ∂Mxy/∂y = 0 (the twisting moment is carried by the effective shear, so it is not set to zero)

Moments follow from curvature: Mx = −D(∂²w/∂x² + ν ∂²w/∂y²), My = −D(∂²w/∂y² + ν ∂²w/∂x²), Mxy = −D(1−ν) ∂²w/∂x∂y. These give the bending and twisting stress used in design.

Combined bending and in-plane load

For small deflections the lateral and in-plane terms add linearly. Compression softens the plate; tension stiffens it. A useful measure is Nx/Ncr, where Ncr is the uniaxial buckling load of the same plate from 6.3. For a single buckling mode, the lateral deflection is amplified by about 1/(1 − Nx/Ncr) when Nx is compression. At Nx/Ncr = 0.395 the factor is 1.65, so the 4.05 mm of the worked example becomes about 6.7 mm.

In an aircraft, cabin pressure is q, and fuselage bending or wing bending gives Nx, Ny. Skin panels also carry Nxy from shear. Check both stress and stability: a compression of 0.4 Ncr may be safe for strength but is already reducing stiffness.

The worked example uses a square simply supported plate. The coefficient 0.00406 is for uniform q on a square plate simply supported on all edges, giving wmax at the centre; textbook tables quote it with ν = 0.30 because the moments in the same table depend on ν, but the deflection coefficient itself does not.

Worked example

Question. A square simply supported plate is 800 mm × 800 mm × 4 mm. E = 70 GPa, ν = 0.30, and the lateral pressure is q = 1 kPa. (a) Find D. (b) Find the maximum deflection wmax at the centre. (c) If an in-plane compressive Nx = 10 kN/m acts along one edge, find Nx/Ncr using Ncr for uniaxial buckling of a simply supported square plate.

  1. Convert: a = b = 0.8 m, t = 0.004 m, q = 1 kPa = 1000 N/m², E = 70×10⁹ N/m².
  2. D = Et³/[12(1−ν²)] = 70×10⁹ × (0.004)³/[12(1−0.30²)] = 4480/10.92 = 410.3 N·m.
  3. For a square simply supported plate under uniform q, wmax = 0.00406 q a⁴/D. Here q a⁴ = 1000 × 0.8⁴ = 409.6 N·m², and q a⁴/D = 409.6/410.3 = 0.9984 m. Then wmax = 0.00406 × 0.9984 = 0.004053 m = 4.05 mm.
  4. Ncr = 4π²D/b² = 4 × 9.8696 × 410.3/0.8² = 25,300 N/m = 25.3 kN/m.
  5. Nx/Ncr = 10/25.3 = 0.395.

Answer. D = 410.3 N·m; wmax = 4.05 mm; Ncr = 25.3 kN/m; Nx/Ncr = 0.395.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting (1−ν²) in D. With ν = 0.30 that inflates D by about 10% and makes every deflection 10% too small.
  • Entering a compressive Nx as a positive number. Tension is positive in D∇⁴w = q + Nx w,xx + ...; compression must be negative, or the plate appears to stiffen.
  • Treating Nx as a stress. Nx is N/m; σx = Nx/t is N/m².
  • Using the 0.00406 coefficient for clamped edges or other aspect ratios. It is only for a square plate simply supported on all edges under uniform q; the deflection coefficient does not depend on ν.
  • Checking only stress and ignoring Nx/Ncr. A compression below yield can still buckle a thin skin; the ratio warns you.

6.3 Buckling of thin plates: governing equation

A thin plate loaded in its own plane can suddenly bend out of plane when the compressive membrane force reaches a critical value. The bending stiffness D resists this, but the in-plane force feeds the deflection. Equating the two gives the plate buckling equation. Aircraft skin between stringers and frames works this way: the panel carries compression up to N_cr, then buckles locally. The same equation sets stringer spacing and skin thickness in wings and fuselage.

You need: 6.1 for D, 6.2 for the plate equation, and Euler columns in Unit 3 for the instability idea.

Why the plate equation appears

An in-plane force on a flat plate has no transverse component while the plate stays flat. A small deflection w(x,y) tilts the force, so a compressive Nx produces a transverse component Nxwxx per unit area. The shear Nxy produces 2Nxywxy, and Ny produces Nywyy. The bending stiffness resists with D∇⁴w, where ∇⁴w = wxxxx + 2wxxyy + wyyyy. Equilibrium of the buckled shape gives the governing equation. The theory assumes t is small compared with b and w is small compared with t. Some syllabi call this the governing equation for plate buckling.

D∇⁴w + Nxwxx + 2Nxywxy + Nywyy = 0

w is the small out-of-plane deflection; D is the plate bending stiffness; Nx and Ny are in-plane membrane forces per unit length, positive in compression; Nxy is the in-plane shear force per unit length; subscripts xx, xy, yy mean second partial derivatives.

D = E t³ / [12(1 − ν²)]

E is Young's modulus in Pa; t is plate thickness in m; ν is Poisson's ratio; D comes out in N·m.

The equation is linear and homogeneous, so a non-zero buckled shape exists only at eigenvalues. The smallest eigenvalue is the critical load. Tension reverses the sign of Nx and raises the buckling load.

Simply supported edges and the buckling coefficient

Take a rectangular plate a×b with all four edges simply supported. The edges cannot deflect, w=0, and cannot take moment, so the second derivative normal to each edge is zero. The mode shape that satisfies these conditions is w = A sin(mπx/a) sin(nπy/b). Here m is the number of half-waves along x, n is the number along y, and A is a small amplitude. Substitute into the governing equation with Nx only. Each derivative brings down a factor: wxx=−(mπ/a)²w, wyyyy=(nπ/b)⁴w, and so on.

Nx = π²D[(m/a)² + (n/b)²]² / (m/a)²

Nx is the compressive membrane force per unit length at buckling for mode numbers m and n; D is the plate bending stiffness; a and b are plate lengths along x and y.

Rearrange into the standard form Ncr = kπ²D/b². The coefficient k is dimensionless. For uniaxial compression, n=1 is the lowest mode, so k = (m b/a + a/(m b))².

Ncr = kπ²D/b²

Ncr is the critical compressive membrane force per unit length in N/m; k is the buckling coefficient; D is the plate bending stiffness in N·m; b is the plate width transverse to compression in m.

Aspect ratio, mode number and instability

Plot k against a/b for m=1,2,3. Each branch has a minimum 4 at a/b=m. The controlling branch is the one with the lowest k; m is the integer nearest a/b. At a/b=2, m=1 gives 6.25, m=2 gives 4, and m=3 gives 4.69, so m=2 controls. At a/b=1.5, no integer matches, so k is above 4. For a long plate, m rises with a/b and k approaches 4. Then Ncr≈4π²D/b², independent of length. A long plate buckles in short waves about one width long, not in one long wave.

At N=Ncr the flat shape becomes unstable: a small bending deflection grows without extra load. The plate can still carry more load after buckling by membrane action, covered in 6.4. Local skin buckling between stringers uses this section; crippling is in 6.5. Shear and combined loading are in 6.10.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported rectangular plate has a = 1.2 m, b = 0.6 m, t = 3 mm, E = 70 GPa and ν = 0.30. It is compressed along x. Find D, the buckling coefficient k, the critical load per unit width Ncr, and the critical stress σcr.

  1. D = E t³ / [12(1 − ν²)] = 70×10⁹ × (0.003)³ / [12(1 − 0.30²)] = 173.1 N·m.
  2. a/b = 1.2/0.6 = 2.0.
  3. For a simply supported plate, k = (m b/a + a/(m b))². Try m=1: k = (0.5 + 2)² = 6.25. m=2: k = (1 + 1)² = 4.00. m=3: k = (1.5 + 0.667)² = 4.69. The lowest is k = 4.00 at m = 2.
  4. Ncr = kπ²D/b² = 4π²(173.1)/(0.6)² = 18.98×10³ N/m.
  5. σcr = Ncr/t = 18.98×10³/0.003 = 6.33×10⁶ Pa.

Answer. D = 173.1 N·m, k = 4 with m = 2, Ncr = 18.98 kN/m, and σcr = 6.33 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Using Euler's column formula Pcr=π²EI/L² for a plate. A plate buckles in two directions; k depends on b, edge restraint and mode number, not only on length a.
  • Putting a instead of b in Ncr=kπ²D/b². The width b is transverse to the compression; the aspect ratio a/b is already inside k.
  • Forgetting t³ in D = Et³/[12(1−ν²)], or using t instead of t³. A small thickness error becomes a large buckling-load error.
  • Assuming k=4 for every aspect ratio. k=4 only when a/b is an integer m, or in the long-plate limit; otherwise k is higher.
  • A sign slip. In the given equation Nx is positive in compression; tension reverses the sign and raises the buckling load.

6.4 Stiffened panels and effective sheet width

A stiffened panel is skin plus stringers. In compression the thin skin buckles between stringers before the stringers fail. The buckled skin is not useless, but it carries load mainly near the stringers. Engineers replace the real stress distribution by an effective width of skin working at the stringer stress. This keeps hand calculations simple. The idea is used in wing and fuselage compression panels, where weight must be low but the skin must still contribute. It is also the first step in boom-panel idealisation for bending and shear flow.

You need: 6.3 for plate buckling, and Unit 5 for shear flow, booms and panels.

The compression panel problem

A stiffened panel has a thin skin riveted or bonded to stringers. The stringers run along the load direction. The distance between two stringers is the pitch p. Under compression, the skin is a plate supported along the stringers. It buckles locally at a stress below the stress that would fail the stringer. After buckling, the skin cannot carry the same stress everywhere. The middle of each bay deflects out of plane and sheds load. The skin near a stringer is held straight by the stringer and carries more load. The stress is high at the stringer and low at mid-bay. This non-uniform stress is hard to use in a simple beam calculation. The effective width method replaces it with a smaller width of skin at the uniform stringer stress. That artificial width carries the same load as the real buckled skin. This is why a stiffened panel can be lighter than an unstiffened sheet that carries the same compressive load. The method is used for wing upper surface panels, fuselage crown panels and tail compression panels.

Effective width formula and boom area

The effective width on each side of a stringer is beff. A common design form is the formula below. It applies after the skin has buckled locally between stringers.

beff = C t √(E/σ)

beff is the effective sheet width on each side of the stringer in mm or m; C is a dimensionless effective-width constant; t is skin thickness in the same length unit; E is Young's modulus in MPa or Pa; σ is the compressive stress at the stringer in the same unit as E.

The example uses C = 1.9, a common value for a simply supported sheet. Design charts give C for other edge supports and stress levels. Use E and σ in the same units. The effective width is per side. It must not exceed half the pitch p/2; if beff exceeds p/2, the effective zones from adjacent stringers meet; then count the whole bay width p as effective, because the applied stress is no higher than the local buckling stress of the skin. The stiffener plus its effective skin is treated as a boom in Unit 5.

Aeff = Ast + 2 beff t

Aeff is the effective area per stringer in mm² or m²; Ast is the stringer cross-sectional area; beff is the effective width on one side; t is skin thickness; the factor 2 counts the skin on both sides of the stringer.

  1. Find the skin compressive stress σ at the stringer for the load case.
  2. Choose C for the edge support and stress level.
  3. Put t, E and σ into consistent units.
  4. Compute beff and limit it to p/2 on each side.
  5. Compute Aeff for the stringer plus effective skin.
  6. Use Aeff in the boom idealisation, then check the stringer itself for local buckling and crippling in 6.5.

Limits and shear lag

The effective width formula is for uniform compression and local skin buckling. It does not cover shear lag. Shear lag is the delay of shear stress transfer between skin and stringers. Stringer stress therefore changes over a length instead of jumping at once. It matters near cut-outs, wing roots and concentrated loads. For a closed box, shear lag analysis is a separate problem. The method also assumes the stringer does not cripple first; check local buckling and crippling in 6.5. For composite stiffened panels the same idea exists, but stiffness is directional; see 6.7 and 6.8. In a real panel, skin buckling may be stable and the panel can carry more load after buckling. The effective width is a strength estimate, not a full post-buckling analysis.

Worked example

Question. A stiffened panel has stringer area 200 mm², skin thickness 1.5 mm and E = 70 GPa. At skin stress 100 MPa the effective-width constant is 1.9. Find beff on each side and the effective area per stringer.

  1. List the data: Ast = 200 mm², t = 1.5 mm, E = 70 GPa = 70 000 MPa, σ = 100 MPa, C = 1.9.
  2. Check the modulus-to-stress ratio: E/σ = 70 000/100 = 700. It is dimensionless.
  3. Take the square root: √700 = 26.46.
  4. Compute the effective width: beff = C t √(E/σ) = 1.9 × 1.5 mm × 26.46 = 75.4 mm on each side.
  5. No pitch is given here, so the p/2 limit cannot be checked; with p = 60 mm the limit would be 30 mm per side and beff would need cutting to 30 mm.
  6. Compute the effective area: Aeff = Ast + 2 beff t = 200 + 2 × 75.4 × 1.5 = 200 + 226.2 = 426.2 mm².

Answer. The effective width is 75.4 mm on each side of the stringer, and the effective area per stringer is 426.2 mm².

Exam traps

  • Using the full pitch p as effective skin width after buckling. Only beff on each side is added.
  • Adding beff once when the stringer has skin on both sides, or twice at a free edge. Count the sheet surfaces correctly.
  • Putting σ in MPa and E in GPa in beff = C t √(E/σ). Make E and σ the same unit.
  • Using yield stress in the formula no matter what load the panel carries. Use the actual stiffener stress at that load case.
  • Calling beff the stiffener pitch. beff is a reduced width; pitch is the spacing between stringers.

6.5 Local buckling and crippling stress: Needham, Gerard

A thin flange or web in compression does not wait for the whole column to fail. Its flat plate element buckles locally at a stress set by b/t, then keeps carrying load in a new pattern until crippling. Crippling stress σcrip is the useful compression strength of a thin-walled section. Needham's empirical curves and Gerard's semi-empirical method estimate σcrip from b/t and material data. Designers use these charts for wing stringers, fuselage frames and stiffened panels, where weight matters and local failure often decides the section.

You need: 6.3 for plate buckling, 6.4 for effective width, and Unit 3 inelastic buckling for the yield cut-off.

Local buckling of compression elements

Compression elements behave as plates. A flange attached to a web has one long edge supported and one long edge free. A web between flanges has both long edges supported. The flat width b is the clear width between supports or between a support and a free edge. Thickness is t. The ratio b/t is the main local buckling parameter.

σel = k π² E / [12(1 − ν²)(b/t)²]

σel is the elastic local buckling stress (Pa); k is the plate buckling coefficient: use k = 4.0 for a long plate with both long edges simply supported in compression (a web between flanges); use k ≈ 0.43 for a long plate with one long edge supported and one long edge free (a flange attached to a web). Choose k for the edge condition before computing σel; E is Young's modulus (Pa); ν is Poisson's ratio; b is flat width (m); t is thickness (m).

Thin aircraft sheet has high b/t, so σel can be well below σcy, the compressive yield stress. The element buckles locally, but the panel can still carry more load. The buckle shifts load to stiffer edges and membrane action develops. Final failure is crippling: local yielding and instability combine, and the element cannot carry extra compression. The crippling stress σcrip is the average compressive stress at crippling. When σel is below σcy, σcrip lies between σel and σcy. When σel is above σcy, the element yields before it buckles and σcrip is taken as σcy.

Crippling stress and Needham’s curves

Needham's method is empirical. Needham tested thin-walled sections and plotted σcrip/σcy against b/t for each family: angle, channel, Z, hat, I. Each curve includes local buckling, post-buckling, corner restraint and yielding. Use the curve for the actual family. An angle curve does not apply to a channel.

The curve falls as b/t rises. At low b/t the ratio approaches 1. At high b/t it can be 0.3 or less. The physical cut-off is σcrip ≤ σcy. A chart reading above 1 means the element yields before it cripples; take σcy.

Gerard's method is semi-empirical. It correlates test data using the parameter (b/t)√(σcy/E), with a coefficient that depends on section shape and end conditions. The chart gives σcrip/σcy. The coefficient is not universal. Do not assume one universal formula; use the chart or coefficients supplied with the problem.

The section crippling load is Pcrip = σcrip A, where A is the total cross-sectional area of the thin-walled section (m²). Use the same units for stress and area. For a section with several flat elements, the largest b/t element is usually critical, but the chart for the whole section family is preferred.

Design procedure, inter-rivet buckling and wrinkling

Procedure for one compression element:

  1. Draw the section; mark supported and free edges.
  2. Measure clear flat width b and thickness t for each element.
  3. Compute b/t and the elastic local buckling stress σel.
  4. Select the Needham curve or Gerard parameter for the section family.
  5. Read σcrip/σcy; cap the ratio at 1.0.
  6. Compute Pcrip = σcrip A; check inter-rivet p/t and sheet wrinkling.

Inter-rivet buckling is local buckling of sheet between rivets. The relevant width is rivet pitch p, not panel width b. Put p/t into the elastic plate formula. If p/t is large, sheet buckles between rivets and the rivets no longer support the skin. Reduce pitch or increase thickness.

Sheet wrinkling is short-wave buckling of thin skin restrained by stringers. It depends on skin thickness, stringer bending stiffness and attachment stiffness. It is not the same as general panel buckling. Check wrinkling allowables; it can set a lower stress than the section crippling chart.

For composite shapes, local buckling uses orthotropic plate stiffnesses; the empirical crippling charts are material-specific. See 6.7.

Worked example

Question. A 2024-T3 aluminium alloy angle has compressive yield σcy = 320 MPa, flat width-to-thickness ratio b/t = 15 and cross-sectional area A = 180 mm². The Gerard chart for this angle gives σcrip/σcy = 0.60. Find the crippling stress and the crippling load.

  1. Write the data with units: σcy = 320 MPa, b/t = 15, A = 180 mm², σcrip/σcy = 0.60.
  2. Crippling stress: σcrip = 0.60 × 320 MPa = 192 MPa.
  3. Convert area: A = 180 mm² = 180 × 10⁻⁶ m².
  4. Crippling load: Pcrip = σcrip A = 192 × 10⁶ Pa × 180 × 10⁻⁶ m².
  5. Pcrip = 34,560 N = 34.56 kN.
  6. Round to three significant figures: Pcrip = 34.6 kN.

Answer. The crippling stress is 192 MPa and the crippling load is 34.6 kN.

Exam traps

  • Using the elastic local buckling stress σel as the crippling stress. Crippling includes post-local-buckling and yielding effects.
  • Forgetting the yield cut-off. A chart ratio above 1 is not allowed; σcrip cannot exceed σcy.
  • Using the wrong Needham curve or Gerard coefficient for the section family. An angle, channel and hat section have different curves.
  • Measuring b as the outer width instead of the clear flat width, or mixing free-edge and supported-edge cases.
  • Using panel width b where rivet pitch p is required for inter-rivet buckling, or mixing millimetres and metres in Pcrip = σcrip A.

6.6 Flexural-torsional buckling of thin-walled columns

Open thin-walled columns—channels, angles and Z-sections—often have their centroid C and shear centre S at different points. An axial load through C is eccentric to S. When the column bends, the tilted load adds a twisting component, and bending and twisting grow together. The column then buckles below the Euler load. Aircraft stringers and fuselage frames use such sections, so the check affects sizing. Only a few universities teach this at derivation level; some syllabi call it torsional instability of thin-walled columns.

You need Unit 5 torsion of thin-walled open sections and 6.3.

Centroid, shear centre and coupled mode

Open sections resist torsion mainly by warping. The torsion constant J is small, so they twist easily. The shear centre S is the point where a transverse load causes no twist. In a channel, angle or Z-section, S is usually away from the centroid C.

Let x0 and y0 locate S from C (mm). A compressive load P (N) through C is not applied at S. While the column is straight, P acts along the column axis and creates no torque about that axis. Twist starts only after bending. As the section deflects by u and v (mm), the load line tilts. Its transverse components P u′ and P v′ act at the centroid, offset x0 and y0 from S; u′ and v′ are their slopes. They add a twisting moment of about P(x0 v′ − y0 u′) (N·mm), which grows with deflection. Twist increases bending, bending increases twist. The two modes grow together: flexural–torsional buckling. The coupling terms in the determinant are proportional to x0 and y0 but vanish when the deflections vanish.

When C and S coincide, the coupling terms vanish. A doubly symmetric section then has separate Euler bending modes and a torsional mode. A channel or angle does not. The offset, not the load magnitude alone, sets how strongly the two modes mix. Aircraft stringers and fuselage frames often use these open shapes.

Torsional buckling load

For a pin-ended column of length L, the pure torsional buckling load is:

Pt = (GJ + π²ECw/L²) / r0²

Pt is torsional buckling load (N); G is shear modulus (N/mm²); J is torsion constant (mm⁴); E is Young's modulus (N/mm²); Cw is warping constant (mm⁶); L is effective length (mm); r0² = (Ix + Iy)/A + x0² + y0² (mm²); Ix, Iy are centroid second moments; A is area.

The term GJ is Saint-Venant torsion. The term π²ECw/L² is warping resistance. Long columns make the warping term small; short columns may be warping-dominated.

The full flexural–torsional result is a 3×3 determinant in Px, Py and Pt. Here Px = π²EIx/L² and Py = π²EIy/L² are the Euler buckling loads for bending about the x and y axes (N), with Ix, Iy in mm⁴ and L in mm. The off-diagonal terms contain P x0 and P y0. Set the determinant to zero and take the smallest root. If x0 = y0 = 0, it separates into three independent modes. For a singly symmetric section, bending in the plane of symmetry (about the axis normal to the axis of symmetry) uncouples and is a pure Euler mode. Bending about the axis of symmetry still interacts with torsion, so two modes, not three, combine. Take a channel with a vertical web: torsion couples with bending about the horizontal major axis, while weak-axis bending stays a separate Euler mode.

Worked example

Question. A channel column has A = 400 mm², Ix + Iy = 640000 mm⁴, y0 = 20 mm, x0 = 0, G = 27 GPa, J = 200 mm⁴, E = 70 GPa, Cw = 1.2 × 10⁸ mm⁶ and L = 800 mm. Find the torsional buckling load Pt.

  1. 1. Use N and mm: G = 27000 N/mm², E = 70000 N/mm².
  2. 2. r0² = 640000/400 + 0² + 20² = 1600 + 400 = 2000 mm².
  3. 3. GJ = 27000 × 200 = 5.4 × 10⁶ N·mm².
  4. 4. π²ECw/L² = π² × 70000 × 1.2 × 10⁸ / 800² = 1.295 × 10⁸ N·mm².
  5. 5. Sum = 5.4 × 10⁶ + 1.295 × 10⁸ = 1.349 × 10⁸ N·mm².
  6. 6. Pt = 1.349 × 10⁸ / 2000 = 6.745 × 10⁴ N = 67.5 kN.

Answer. Pt ≈ 67.5 kN.

Exam traps

  • Taking moments about C instead of S. The torsion arm is measured from S.
  • Dropping Cw. Open sections have low J, so warping often controls short columns.
  • Using J for a closed section in the same way. Closed cells resist torsion by shear flow, not warping.
  • Forgetting units: G and E in N/mm², lengths in mm, Cw in mm⁶.
  • Assuming C and S coincide for a channel or angle; they do not.

6.7 Composites: FRP and orthotropic plies

A fibre-reinforced plastic (FRP) carries load along stiff fibres held in a weak polymer matrix. One thin layer, a ply, is stiff along the fibres and soft across them. Such a material is orthotropic and needs four constants. Aircraft wing skins and spacecraft panels use FRP because a tailored ply sequence puts stiffness where the load needs it. This chapter builds the plane-stress stiffness matrix of one ply, along its fibres and rotated to structure axes.

You need stress, strain, E and ν from Unit 1, and plane stress from 6.1.

Fibre, matrix and the orthotropic ply

An FRP is a polymer matrix, usually epoxy, holding long thin fibres of carbon, glass or aramid. The fibres are stiff and strong along their length. The matrix is weak but tough: it separates the fibres, transfers load by shear and seals out moisture.

One layer is a ply, or lamina, often about 0.125 mm thick for carbon/epoxy. Axis 1 runs along the fibres, axis 2 across them in the plane, axis 3 through the thickness. A wing-skin ply is in plane stress (σ3 = 0), so only axes 1 and 2 matter.

The fibres lie in one direction, so stiffness along 1 is far higher than along 2. The material has three symmetry planes and is orthotropic. Four constants describe it: E1 along the fibres, E2 across, ν12 (contraction in 2 per unit strain in 1) and G12 (in-plane shear modulus).

Carbon/epoxy typically has E1 = 140 GPa, E2 = 10 GPa, ν12 = 0.30 and G12 = 5 GPa; glass/epoxy near E1 = 40 GPa, E2 = 8 GPa. G12 is low in both because the matrix carries shear.

The four constants and the Q matrix

Hooke's law for one ply, in its own 1–2 axes, uses a 3 × 3 stiffness matrix Q:

σ1 = Q11 ε1 + Q12 ε2 ;  σ2 = Q12 ε1 + Q22 ε2 ;  τ12 = Q66 γ12

σ1, σ2 are normal stresses (Pa) along and across the fibres; τ12 is the in-plane shear stress (Pa); ε1, ε2 are normal strains and γ12 is the engineering shear strain (all dimensionless).

Q11 = E1/(1 − ν12ν21),  Q22 = E2/(1 − ν12ν21),  Q12 = ν12E2/(1 − ν12ν21),  Q66 = G12

the Q's are reduced stiffnesses (Pa); ν21 = ν12E2/E1 is the second Poisson ratio, dimensionless. All moduli in the same unit, normally GPa.

The denominator appears because the two Poisson effects act together. In a free uniaxial test the ply contracts sideways, which lowers the stress for a given ε1, so the measured E1 is slightly smaller than Q11. Q11 is the stiffness when that sideways contraction is blocked.

Symmetry of Q requires ν12/E1 = ν21/E2, so ν21 = ν12E2/E1, always much smaller than ν12.

Do not call Q11 a modulus. E1 and E2 come from a uniaxial test where the ply contracts freely sideways. Q11, Q12 and Q22 come from the state where sideways contraction is fully prevented (ε2 = 0). That is why Q11 is slightly larger than E1. The word reduced refers to setting the through-thickness stress to zero (σ3 = 0).

Rotating the ply to laminate axes

In a real panel the plies are laid at angles. Take structure axes x–y and let θ be the angle from x to the fibre axis 1, counter-clockwise positive. Write m = cos θ and n = sin θ. The stiffness in x–y is Qbar:

Qbar11 = Q11 m⁴ + 2(Q12 + 2Q66) m²n² + Q22 n⁴

Qbar11 is the stiffness along x (Pa); θ is the ply angle in radians; m = cos θ, n = sin θ. Qbar22 follows by swapping m and n; Qbar12 and Qbar66 have similar standard forms.

Two new terms appear, Qbar16 and Qbar26. They are zero at 0° and 90° and reach their largest values at intermediate angles, near 30° for Qbar16 and near 60° for Qbar26 in a typical carbon/epoxy ply; at 45° both are appreciable. They are extension–shear coupling: pull a 45° ply along x and it also shears. At θ = 0°, Qbar equals Q. At θ = 90°, Qbar11 becomes Q22.

This is how composites are tailored: ±45° plies add shear stiffness, 0° plies axial stiffness, 90° plies transverse and hoop stiffness. Unbalanced coupling gives bend–twist coupling for aeroelastic tailoring. Stacking plies through the thickness comes next in 6.8.

Worked example

Question. A carbon/epoxy ply has E1 = 140 GPa, E2 = 10 GPa, ν12 = 0.30 and G12 = 5 GPa. Find ν21, the factor (1 − ν12ν21), and Q11, Q22, Q12 and Q66.

  1. ν21 = ν12E2/E1 = 0.30 × 10/140 = 0.0214.
  2. ν12ν21 = 0.00643, so 1 − ν12ν21 = 0.9936.
  3. Q11 = 140/0.9936 = 140.91 GPa; Q22 = 10/0.9936 = 10.06 GPa.
  4. Q12 = 0.30 × 10/0.9936 = 3.02 GPa; Q66 = G12 = 5.00 GPa.
  5. Check: Q11 − Q12²/Q22 = 140.91 − 0.91 = 140.00 GPa = E1.

Answer. ν21 = 0.0214, factor = 0.9936, Q11 = 140.91 GPa, Q22 = 10.06 GPa, Q12 = 3.02 GPa, Q66 = 5.00 GPa.

  • Mixing up ν12 and ν21. Only ν12 is measured; ν21 = ν12E2/E1 is always the smaller number.
  • Feeding degrees into sin and cos in a program. The transformation needs radians; convert with θ × π/180 first.
  • Writing Q12 = ν12E1/den. The correct numerator is ν12E2, so Q12 stays close to 3 GPa, not 42 GPa.
  • Believing Qbar16 and Qbar26 are always zero. They vanish only at 0° and 90°; at other angles they couple extension and shear.

6.8 Laminate theory, stiffness matrix and composite beams

A laminate is several fibre-reinforced plies bonded with chosen fibre angles. Each ply is orthotropic, so its stiffness in laminate axes must be rotated first. Summing the rotated plies through the thickness gives three matrices: A for extension, B for extension–bending coupling, and D for bending. These matrices turn mid-plane strains and curvatures into loads and moments per unit width. Engineers use them for wing skins, helicopter blades, pressure vessels and composite beams, where the stacking sequence is chosen to place stiffness where it is needed.

You need ply stiffness and rotated Qbar from 6.7, and plate bending from 6.1.

From plies to A, B, D

A laminate is a stack of fibre-reinforced plies bonded together. Each ply has its own fibre angle. The stacking sequence lists those angles through the thickness, for example [0/90]s. The subscript s means the stack is symmetric about the mid-plane.

Measure the through-thickness coordinate z from the mid-plane. The mid-plane is z=0. Ply k occupies zk−1 to zk. Its thickness is tk=zk−zk−1.

In laminate axes x and y, ply k has reduced stiffness Qbarij, where i,j=1,2,6. Qbar comes from rotating the ply stiffness by its fibre angle; the rotation is in 6.7. Qbar means stiffness in laminate axes.

Aij = Σk Qbarij,k (zk − zk−1)

Aij is extensional stiffness per unit width (N/mm); Qbar is in N/mm²; z is in mm; the sum is over all plies k.

Bij = ½ Σk Qbarij,k (zk² − zk−1²)

Bij is extension–bending coupling stiffness (N); the factor ½ comes from integrating z through the thickness.

Dij = ⅓ Σk Qbarij,k (zk³ − zk−1³)

Dij is bending stiffness per unit width (N·mm); the factor ⅓ comes from integrating z² through the thickness.

The in-plane strain in a ply varies linearly with z. Integrating that strain through the thickness produces A, B and D. A weights each ply by its thickness. D uses the difference (zk³ − zk−1³); for a thin ply this difference is roughly z2 times the ply thickness, so plies far from the mid-plane dominate bending stiffness.

What the matrices mean

The three matrices form the laminate constitutive law. N and M are in-plane force and moment per unit width. ε⁰ is mid-plane strain. κ is curvature.

[N; M] = [[A, B], [B, D]] [ε⁰; κ]

N is in-plane force per unit width (N/mm); M is moment per unit width (N); ε⁰ is mid-plane strain; κ is curvature (1/mm).

For a symmetric laminate, B=0. Extension and bending uncouple: N=Aε⁰ and M=Dκ. For an unsymmetric laminate, B is not zero. An axial load then causes curvature, and a moment causes extension.

A11 is the extensional stiffness along x. D11 is the bending stiffness for curvature κx. Stacking sequence changes D much more than A. A balanced laminate has equal +θ and −θ plies, which removes some shear–extension coupling. A symmetric stacking sequence removes B about the mid-plane.

Composite beam stiffness

For a laminate strip bending along the 1 direction, let M1 be moment per unit width and κ1 curvature. For a symmetric laminate, M1=D11κ1+D12κ2.

EI = b D11 (only when κ2 = 0)

EI is strip bending stiffness for κ2=0 (N·mm²); b is width (mm); D11 is laminate bending stiffness per unit width (N·mm).

The formula EI=bD11 applies when κ2=0, as in cylindrical plate bending, or when D12=0. It is not the general narrow-beam result.

EI = b (D11 − D12² / D22)

EI is narrow-beam bending stiffness for a symmetric laminate (N·mm²); D12 and D22 are the other bending stiffness terms per unit width (N·mm).

For a narrow beam free to anticlastic curvature, M2=0. Then κ2=−(D12/D22)κ1, and the effective stiffness is the second formula. A wide beam behaves more like a plate, and one beam stiffness may not describe it.

The D terms have units of moment per unit width per curvature. Multiplying by width b gives N·mm², the same unit as EI in ordinary beam theory.

Worked example

Question. A [0/90]s laminate has four plies, each 0.125 mm thick. For a 0° ply Q11=140.9 GPa and Q22=10.1 GPa. For a 90° ply the roles swap, so Qbar11=10.1 GPa.   • Using Q instead of Qbar for a 90° ply. At 90°, Qbar11 equals Q22, not Q11.

6.9 Structural vibrations and aeroelasticity basics

Every structure has preferred frequencies, called natural frequencies. A wing, a tailplane or a panel pushed and released will oscillate at these frequencies, and damping decides how quickly the motion dies out. In flight, aerodynamic forces can feed energy back into the motion. That coupling is aeroelasticity. It matters because a wing can twist too far at one speed, called divergence, or oscillate with growing amplitude at another, called flutter. Both are certification issues. This chapter builds the one-degree-of-freedom vibration model, then uses it to explain divergence, flutter and reduced frequency in plain terms.

You need: Unit 2 energy methods for generalized mass and stiffness, and 6.7 for how fibre direction sets stiffness.

One degree of freedom: mass, spring and dashpot

Take a single mass m (kg) on a spring of stiffness k (N/m), with a dashpot of damping c (N·s/m). Let x(t) be displacement in metres. The equation of motion is m (d²x/dt²) + c (dx/dt) + k x = F(t). With no force and no damping, the mass oscillates freely.

ωn = √(k/m),   fn = ωn/(2π)

ωn is the undamped natural frequency in rad/s; k is stiffness in N/m; m is mass in kg; fn is the same frequency in Hz. The 2π converts rad/s to Hz.

Damping is measured by the damping ratio ζ, a pure number. Critical damping is cc = 2√(km). Aircraft structures are lightly damped: ζ is often a few percent. ζ < 1 is underdamped (the mass oscillates as it dies away), ζ = 1 is critically damped (fastest return with no overshoot), and ζ > 1 is overdamped (slow return, no oscillation).

ζ = c/(2√(km))

ζ is the damping ratio; c is the dashpot constant in N·s/m; k is stiffness in N/m; m is mass in kg.

For a harmonic force F0 sin(Ωt), let r = Ω/ωn be the frequency ratio. The steady vibration amplitude is largest near r = 1. This is resonance. Damping limits the peak.

X = (F0/k) / √((1 − r²)² + (2ζr)²)

X is amplitude in metres; F0 is force amplitude in N; k is stiffness in N/m; r = Ω/ωn is frequency ratio; ζ is damping ratio. At r = 1, X = F0/(2ζk).

Modes and lumped models of real structures

A real wing has many masses and stiffnesses, so it has many natural frequencies. Each natural frequency has a mode shape: a shape in which all points move sinusoidally at the same frequency. The first bending mode, the first torsion mode and higher modes each have their own frequency and damping.

For hand calculations, replace the continuous structure by one generalized coordinate q(t). Choose a trial mode shape φ(x), then compute a generalized mass M and generalized stiffness K. The same one-degree-of-freedom formula applies.

  1. Choose a mode shape φ(x) that satisfies the end conditions and is normalized, for example φ = 1 at the tip.
  2. Compute generalized mass M = ∫ m(x) φ² dx over the length.
  3. Compute generalized stiffness K = ∫ EI(x) (d²φ/dx²)² dx for bending, adding torsion stiffness terms if the mode twists.
  4. Estimate the natural frequency from ωn = √(K/M).

This is the Rayleigh quotient from energy methods. It gives an upper estimate of the true fundamental frequency when φ is a reasonable guess. Composite stiffness from 6.8 enters K, so a softer laminate lowers the frequency.

Divergence, flutter and reduced frequency

Aeroelasticity is the feedback between structural motion and aerodynamic force. Static divergence is a torsional instability. Airloads twist a wing; the twist changes the airloads. At a high enough speed, the aerodynamic pitching moment slope equals the elastic torsional stiffness, so any small twist grows without oscillation.

qD = Kθ / (S c Cmα),   VD = √(2qD/ρ)

qD is divergence dynamic pressure in N/m²; Kθ is torsional stiffness in N·m/rad; S is reference area in m²; c is chord in m; Cmα is pitching moment slope per radian; VD is divergence speed in m/s; ρ is air density in kg/m³.

Cmα is negative for a stable section. The formula uses magnitudes, so write qD = Kθ/(S c |Cmα|) and VD = √(2qD/ρ).

Flutter is dynamic. Bending and torsion couple through unsteady airloads. At the flutter speed, the net aerodynamic damping becomes negative and an oscillation grows. Flutter can occur below divergence, so both must be checked.

Unsteady aerodynamics depends on how fast the wing oscillates compared with the airflow. That ratio is the reduced frequency kr = ω b/V. Here b is a reference semi-chord in m, ω is oscillation frequency in rad/s, and V is true airspeed in m/s. Small kr means quasi-steady flow; large kr means aerodynamic lag matters.

Worked example

Question. A wing model has mass m = 500 kg and stiffness k = 200 kN/m. Find its natural frequency in rad/s and in Hz. With damping c = 4000 N·s/m, find the damping ratio ζ.

  1. Convert stiffness: k = 200 kN/m = 200 000 N/m.
  2. Compute ωn = √(k/m) = √(200000/500) = √400 = 20 rad/s.
  3. Convert to Hz: fn = ωn/(2π) = 20/(2π) = 3.18 Hz.
  4. Compute √(km) = √(200000 × 500) = √(100 000 000) = 10 000. Then ζ = c/(2√(km)) = 4000/(2 × 10 000) = 0.20.

Answer. ωn = 20 rad/s, fn = 3.18 Hz, and ζ = 0.20.

Exam traps

  • Reporting ωn in rad/s as fn in Hz without dividing by 2π: 20 rad/s is 3.18 Hz, not 20 Hz.
  • Using ζ = c/√(km) or forgetting the 2: critical damping is cc = 2√(km), so ζ = c/(2√(km)).
  • Calling static divergence flutter: divergence is a non-oscillatory loss of static torsional stiffness; flutter is a growing oscillation.
  • Mixing the two k symbols: spring stiffness k is in N/m; reduced frequency kr = ωb/V is dimensionless.

6.10 Plate buckling under shear and combined loading

A flat plate can buckle under in-plane shear even when no compression acts on it. The shear forces tilt the plate into a diagonal wave. Once compression and shear act together, the two loads help each other, and the safe combination follows an interaction curve, not a simple sum. Aircraft webs, wing skins between stringers and fuselage panels meet this condition. The calculation gives the first buckling load; after that, a web may still carry load by diagonal tension.

You need: the plate stiffness D and the compression buckling load from 6.3, and the in-plane force notation from 6.2.

Shear buckling and the coefficient ks

An in-plane shear force per unit length Nxy (N/m) acts parallel to the plate edges. On a small square element it is equivalent to diagonal compression and diagonal tension at about 45° to the edges. The compression direction can buckle, so a plate may buckle under shear alone.

Nxy,cr = ks π² D / b²

Nxy,cr is the critical shear force per unit length (N/m); ks is the shear buckling coefficient (dimensionless); D is the plate stiffness (N·m); b is the shorter side (m); π² ≈ 9.870.

D = E t³ / [12(1 − ν²)]

E is Young's modulus (Pa), t is thickness (m), ν is Poisson's ratio.

For a rectangular plate simply supported on all four edges, with a the longer side and a/b ≥ 1, ks = 5.34 + 4/(a/b)². A square plate gives ks = 9.34. A long plate gives ks → 5.34. Clamped edges raise ks and therefore the buckling load; do not mix coefficients for different edge conditions.

Procedure: compute D from t and the material; find a/b; choose ks; substitute into Nxy,cr. The result is force per unit length. The total shear on an edge of length L is Nxy × L, in newtons per metre times metre = newtons.

Compression plus shear

Wing and fuselage panels usually carry Nx compression and Nxy shear at the same time. Each load alone has a critical value: Nx,cr = kc π² D / b² and Nxy,cr = ks π² D / b². For a simply supported square plate in compression, kc = 4.0.

(Nx / Nx,cr)² + (Nxy / Nxy,cr)² = 1

Nx is the compressive membrane force per unit length (N/m); Nx,cr and Nxy,cr are the buckling loads for each load acting alone; Nxy is the shear force per unit length (N/m).

At buckling, the point lies on this curve. Inside the curve the plate is stable; outside it has buckled. Setting Nxy = 0 gives Nx = Nx,cr; setting Nx = 0 gives Nxy = Nxy,cr. The curve is a quarter ellipse. Do not write Nx + Nxy = something: the two forces act in different directions. Do not use a straight-line sum unless a syllabus or code tells you to; the quadratic form is the usual energy-based estimate.

Real curves shift with aspect ratio, edge restraint and the sign of shear, but the quadratic is a good exam and preliminary design tool. The figure plots this interaction. The marked point is the worked example: Nx/Nx,cr = 0.395 and Nxy/Nxy,cr = 0.919.

Post-buckling diagonal tension

The value Nxy,cr is the start of buckling, not the collapse load. After a thin web buckles in shear, it can carry more load by forming a diagonal tension field. The plate wrinkles along the tension diagonal, and the flanges or stiffeners anchor the pull. This is why aircraft webs are often allowed to buckle below ultimate load.

The flat-plate formula gives the onset. Design above onset needs the tension-field method, covered with thin-webbed beams in Unit 5. Repeated buckling can cause fatigue, so a panel that buckles in service needs care. Stiffened panels redistribute load after skin buckling; see 6.4. Composite plates add orthotropic stiffness and coupling; see 6.7.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported square plate is 800 mm × 800 mm × 4 mm, with E = 70 GPa and ν = 0.30. Find the shear buckling load per unit length. If Nx = 10 kN/m compression also acts, find the allowable shear per unit length using the quadratic interaction.

  1. Convert: a = b = 0.800 m, t = 0.004 m, E = 70 × 10⁹ Pa, ν = 0.30.
  2. D = E t³ / [12(1 − ν²)] = 70 × 10⁹ × (0.004)³ / [12(1 − 0.30²)] = 410.3 N·m.
  3. Square simply supported plate: a/b = 1, so ks = 5.34 + 4/1² = 9.34.
  4. Nxy,cr = ks π² D / b² = 9.34 × π² × 410.3 / (0.800)² = 59.1 × 10³ N/m = 59.1 kN/m.
  5. Compression: kc = 4.0 for a simply supported square plate. Nx,cr = 4 × π² × 410.3 / (0.800)² = 25.3 × 10³ N/m = 25.3 kN/m.
  6. Interaction with Nx = 10 kN/m: (10/25.3)² + (Nxy/59.1)² = 1.
  7. First term = (0.395)² = 0.156. Then (Nxy/59.1)² = 1 − 0.156 = 0.844.
  8. Nxy/59.1 = √0.844 = 0.919, so Nxy = 0.919 × 59.1 = 54.3 kN/m.

Answer. The shear buckling load alone is 59.1 kN/m; with 10 kN/m compression, the allowable shear is 54.3 kN/m.

Exam traps

  • Adding Nx and Nxy directly, or using (Nx/Nx,cr)+(Nxy/Nxy,cr)=1 when the quadratic interaction is asked. Forces in different directions combine through the interaction curve, not by simple addition.
  • Using ks = 4.0 for shear. 4.0 is the compression coefficient kc for a simply supported square plate; the shear coefficient for the same plate is 9.34.
  • Mixing edge conditions. The coefficient 9.34 is for simply supported edges; clamped edges give a higher ks and a higher load.
  • Forgetting the ν term in D or using t in mm. D needs t in metres, and 1 − ν² is about 0.91, not 1.
  • Treating Nxy,cr as the total shear force. It is force per unit length; multiply by edge length for total force. Also, it is buckling onset, not final failure; diagonal tension follows.

Takeaways

  1. A thin plate carries load mainly by bending curvature, and its stiffness scales with t³ through the plate stiffness D.
  2. Transverse pressure and in-plane membrane forces enter the same plate equation, so bending and stretching act together and cannot be treated separately.
  3. A plate buckles at a stress proportional to (t/b)², so making the sheet thinner or the bay wider drops the buckling stress sharply.
  4. After the skin buckles between stringers, the panel still carries load, but only an effective width of skin close to each stringer is counted.
  5. Short thin-walled compression elements fail by crippling at a stress below yield, and Needham's and Gerard's correlations estimate that stress from the b/t ratio.
  6. An open thin-walled column can fail by twisting and bending together because the load path misses the shear centre; a closed tube resists this far better.
  7. A composite ply is orthotropic, and a laminate is analysed by summing rotated ply stiffnesses into the A, B and D matrices.
  8. Every structure has natural frequencies and damping, and aeroelastic coupling can turn those frequencies into divergence or flutter at a critical speed.
  9. Shear and combined compression–shear loads buckle a plate along an interaction curve, so a shear stress counts even when the direct stress is small.

Write D∇⁴w = q + Nₓ w_xx + N_y w_yy + 2N_xy w_xy; Explain q as transverse pressure per unit area (N/m²) causing bending; Explain Nₓ, N_y, N_xy as in-plane membrane forces per unit length (N/m); Show that compressive membrane forces add a negative stiffness term and can drive the plate to buckling; Note tensile membrane forces stiffen the plate (the basis of tension field action); Give one aircraft example: fuselage skin under pressurisation plus fuselage bending.

Understand Describe the buckling of a thin rectangular plate under uniform uniaxial compression. Write the critical stress and explain the plate buckling coefficient k. 12 marks

Plate is flat and stable up to σ_cr, then deflects into a half-wave pattern in the loaded direction; σ_cr = kπ²E/[12(1−ν²)(b/t)²]; define each symbol and unit; k depends on edge support and aspect ratio a/b; k ≈ 4 for simply supported edges in compression; Number of half-waves along the plate grows with aspect ratio but k stays near its minimum; Post-buckled plate still carries load from the edges; only the middle sheds load; Compare with column buckling: plates have much higher buckling stress for the same b/t because of two-way support.

Understand Explain the concept of effective sheet width in a stiffened panel after the skin buckles between stringers. 10 marks

Skin buckles at a low stress because b/t is large, but the panel does not fail; Buckled skin sheds compression to the edges near the stringers; Define effective width w_e: the width assumed to work at the full stress with each stringer; Give a common form such as w_e ∝ t√(E/σ) and state that w_e falls as stress rises; Total panel load = stringer load + σ × effective skin area; Consequence for design: stringer spacing controls how much skin is useful.

Understand Explain why an open thin-walled column can buckle by bending and twisting together, while a closed tube rarely does. Give the role of the shear centre. 12 marks

Define shear centre and note it is offset from the centroid in open sections; The compressive load acts through the centroid; the section twists about the shear centre; Twist produces warping and longitudinal displacement, which couples with bending; Flexural-torsional buckling load is lower than the pure Euler or pure torsion load; Closed sections have high torsional stiffness and shear centre near the centroid, so coupling is weak; Aircraft examples: open Z-stringers need restraint; closed spars behave better.

Apply

Apply A simply supported aluminium plate has E = 70 GPa, ν = 0.33, thickness 2 mm and loaded edge width 250 mm. Take k = 4.0. Calculate D and the critical buckling stress in compression. 12 marks

Write D = Et³/[12(1−ν²)] and substitute values with consistent units; Show intermediate value of (1−ν²) = 0.891; D ≈ 70×10⁹ × 8×10⁻⁹ /(12 × 0.891) ≈ 52.4 N·m; Write σ_cr = kπ²D/(b²t) and substitute b = 0.25 m, t = 0.002 m; σ_cr ≈ 4 × 9.87 × 52.4/(0.0625 × 0.002) ≈ 16.5 MPa; Comment: this is low compared to yield, so skin buckles early and effective width matters.

Apply Estimate the crippling stress of a thin-walled angle section in 2024 aluminium using Needham's and Gerard's methods, and compare with yield. 12 marks

Define flat width b, thickness t and compute b/t for each element; State the material constants needed: yield stress and ultimate stress; Apply Needham's empirical expression relating crippling stress to b/t and material properties; Apply Gerard's expression σ_cr = g E (t/b)^m with the stated coefficient for the section type; Compare the two answers and note the spread of empirical methods; Explain why crippling stress is below yield: local buckling lets part of the section fail early.

Apply A thin-walled channel column of given cross-section has a stated torsional stiffness and a known offset between centroid and shear centre. Set up the calculation of its flexural-torsional buckling load. 13 marks

Compute section properties: area, second moments Iₓ, I_y, torsion constant J, warping constant Γ; Locate centroid and shear centre; state the eccentricity e; Write the Euler loads Pₓ = π²EI_y/L² and P_y = π²EIₓ/L²; Write the torsional buckling load involving GJ and EΓ; Show that the coupled flexural-torsional load is lower than the smaller of these; State the assumption of pinned ends and mention how end fixity changes the answer.

Apply For a two-ply laminate with given ply properties and fibre angles, show how the A, B and D matrices are built and state what each matrix does. 14 marks

State ply engineering constants E₁, E₂, ν₁₂, G₁₂ and how reduced stiffnesses Q are formed; Rotate Q to the ply angle θ to get the transformed stiffness, with the 1/8 and 3/8 terms from cos⁴ and sin⁴; A = Σ transformed stiffness × ply thickness; units N/m; gives in-plane load–strain relation; B = Σ transformed stiffness × (z_k² − z_{k−1}²)/2; units N; couples extension and bending; D = Σ transformed stiffness × (z_k³ − z_{k−1}³)/3; units N·m; gives bending stiffness; State that a symmetric laminate has B = 0; an unsymmetric one warps when loaded.

Apply Using the effective width idea, estimate the load carried by a stiffened panel in compression once the skin has buckled, and compare with the unbuckled case. 10 marks

Give panel geometry: stringer spacing, skin thickness, number of stringers; Compute skin buckling stress from the plate formula; At a higher applied stress, compute effective width w_e from a stated expression; Panel load = number of stringers × stringer area × stress + effective skin area × stress; Compare with the load the panel would carry if the whole skin worked; Comment on the loss of stiffness and the resulting need for closely spaced stringers.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Explain how combined compression and shear buckle a plate. Why is the interaction a curve and not a simple sum of stresses? 13 marks

Shear alone buckles a plate in a diagonal wave pattern; shear buckling coefficient depends on aspect ratio; Write the interaction relation, e.g. (σ/σ_cr) + (τ/τ_cr)² = 1, and define each term; Explain that shear produces principal tensile and compressive stresses at 45°, so shear feeds the compressive buckling mode; Small shear stress therefore reduces the compressive buckling load even when present alone it is safe; Sketch the interaction curve; note it lies inside the straight-line sum; Aircraft example: wing skin in bending plus torsion of the wing box.

Analyze Distinguish divergence from flutter, and explain the role of natural frequency and damping in each. 12 marks

Define divergence: static instability where aerodynamic moment grows faster than structural restoring moment; Divergence occurs at a speed independent of damping; derive from stiffness balance; Define flutter: dynamic instability where two modes exchange energy with the airflow each cycle; Flutter depends on natural frequencies of the coupled modes and on damping; Damping raises the flutter speed only a little; matching frequencies is the real danger; Aircraft example: control surface flutter and wing torsional divergence; mention design remedies such as mass balance and stiffness.

Analyze Analyse why an unsymmetric composite laminate warps on curing while a symmetric one does not, and what this means for aircraft panels. 13 marks

Each ply shrinks differently along and across the fibres during cool-down; In an unsymmetric layup the plies pull against each other and the B matrix is nonzero; Coupling between extension and bending then produces out-of-plane curvature; Symmetric and balanced layups give B = 0 and no cure warping; Warping also couples compressive loading into bending, lowering buckling stress; Design rules: keep laminates symmetric and balanced, avoid excessive ply angles difference.

Evaluate Evaluate the design trade-offs in sizing a stiffened compression panel for a wing upper surface, covering skin buckling, crippling, effective width and material choice. 15 marks

Skin buckling sets a limit proportional to (t/b)²; thinner skin means closer stringers; Stringers must not cripple before the panel reaches design load; check b/t of each element; Effective width decides how much skin is counted; it falls as stress rises; Shear lag means wide panels do not carry full bending stress across the width; note the effect on the outer stringers; Compare aluminium with carbon fibre reinforced plastic: higher specific stiffness but brittle failure and local buckling of thin walls; Conclude with a balanced choice and mention testing to validate crippling allowables.

Glossary

Aspect ratio
The ratio a/b of plate length to loaded width; it changes the buckling coefficient k but not the basic formula.
Bending moment per unit length
The moment carried by a unit width strip of plate, in N·m per metre of width.
Clamped edge
An edge where deflection and slope are both zero, giving a higher buckling stress than a simply supported edge.
Curvature
The second derivative of deflection, ∂²w/∂x², giving how sharply the plate bends; unit 1/m.
Flat width
The width of an element measured between its corners or supported edges, used in crippling formulas.
Flexural rigidity
Another name for the plate stiffness D, the plate's resistance to bending per unit curvature.
Flutter
A dynamic aeroelastic instability where the structure extracts energy from the airflow and vibration grows without limit.
Frame
A transverse member that holds the stringers in position and keeps the cross-section shape.
Lamina
A single layer of composite material with one fibre direction; also called a ply.
Laminate
A stack of plies bonded together with chosen fibre angles, treated as one structural plate.
Local buckling
Buckling of a single thin element of a section, such as one leg of an angle, while the whole column stays straight.
Mid-surface
The plane halfway through the plate thickness that is taken as the reference surface for deflection and strain.
Poisson's ratio
The ratio of lateral contraction to longitudinal extension under uniaxial stress; the term (1−ν²) appears in plate stiffness.
Post-buckling strength
The extra load a buckled plate carries after the critical stress, because of membrane tension in the buckled shape.
Shear lag
The delay in building up bending stress in skin far from a stiffener, so the stress is not uniform across the panel width.
Simply supported edge
An edge where deflection and bending moment are zero but rotation is free; idealised support in most buckling formulas.
Stringer
A longitudinal stiffening member running along the wing or fuselage that carries axial load together with the skin.
Symmetric laminate
A laminate whose plies are mirrored about the mid-surface, giving zero extension–bending coupling (B = 0).
Thin plate
A flat sheet whose thickness is small compared with its in-plane dimensions, so through-thickness shear is neglected.
Torsion constant
The property J of a section that measures its resistance to St Venant twisting; closed sections have far larger J than open ones.
Twisting moment
The moment per unit length that twists the plate, proportional to (1−ν) times the mixed curvature.
Wagner tension field
A diagonal tensile stress pattern in a thin web after shear buckling, which lets the web carry shear well beyond the buckling load.
Warping
Out-of-plane longitudinal deformation of a thin-walled section under torsion, resisted by the warping constant.
