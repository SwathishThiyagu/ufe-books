3.1 Euler buckling of columns and end conditions

A straight column under axial compression is in equilibrium when it stays straight. That equilibrium is stable only up to a load of π²EI/L_e². At that load a tiny sideways bow grows instead of shrinking: the column buckles. The idea matters because a thin tube can carry far less than its material's squash load, and aircraft struts, engine-mount tubes and truss-rib members are thin. Euler's formula gives the critical load from stiffness and length alone, so it is the first check on any slender compression member.

You need: beam curvature EI d²v/dx² = M from Unit 2, and where a strut sits in a truss rib from Unit 1.

Why a straight strut buckles

Take a straight column of length L, area A and second moment of area I. Push it with an axial load P. If a small sideways deflection v(x) appears, the load line is no longer over the centroid of the section. The load then makes a bending moment M = P v at that section. The column's bending stiffness resists it, and for small slopes the curvature is v'' = d²v/dx². Equilibrium of the slice gives EI v'' = −P v.

EI d²v/dx² = −P v

E is Young's modulus in Pa; I is the second moment of area about the bending axis in m⁴; v is the lateral deflection in m; x is distance along the column in m; P is the axial compression in N.

  1. Pinned ends give v = 0 at x = 0 and x = L.
  2. Set k² = P/(EI). Then v'' + k²v = 0.
  3. Solution v = A sin kx + B cos kx; v(0) = 0 gives B = 0.
  4. v(L) = 0 gives sin kL = 0. The straight column is A = 0; the first buckle is kL = π.
  5. So P_cr = π²EI/L².

The formula is Euler's critical load. Some syllabi call it Euler's theory of struts. It contains no strength term: a stronger alloy with the same E and I buckles at the same load.

  • Perfectly straight: an initial bow adds bending at once; see 3.3.
  • Elastic: P/A must stay below the proportional limit; otherwise use 3.4.
  • Centroidal load: an offset e adds a moment P e.
  • Small deflection: curvature is close to v''.
  • Ends hold position but allow the assumed rotation.

End conditions and effective length

End fixity decides how much of the column can bend. A pinned end allows rotation but holds position. A fixed end allows neither. The effective length L_e is the length of an equivalent pinned-pinned column with the same P_cr. The factor K multiplies the real length: L_e = K L, where K has no unit and L is in m.

P_cr = π²EI/L_e² = π²EI/(K L)²

P_cr is the Euler buckling load in N; K is the effective-length factor; L is the real column length in m.

End conditionKL_eP_cr
Pinned-pinned1.0Lπ²EI/L²
Fixed-fixed0.50.5L4π²EI/L²
Fixed-pinned0.70.7L2.04π²EI/L²
Fixed-free2.02Lπ²EI/(4L²)

Fixed-free is the weakest because the free tip can move sideways with no support. Fixed-fixed is the strongest because the ends force a full sine wave into half the length. Real joints sit between these ideals. A riveted tube end is closer to fixed; a single pin is closer to pinned. When the same member also carries bending, see 3.5.

Slenderness and the weak axis

Slenderness measures how thin a column is. The radius of gyration is r = √(I/A), in m, where A is the cross-section area in m². Slenderness is λ = L_e/r, dimensionless. A stocky column has small λ; a slender one has large λ.

λ = L_e/r = L_e/√(I/A)

λ is the slenderness ratio; L_e is the effective length in m; r is the radius of gyration in m; I is the second moment of area in m⁴; A is the area in m².

Euler's formula is valid while the buckling stress P_cr/A is below the proportional limit. For a mild-steel strut this needs λ roughly above 100; for aluminium alloy the limit is lower. Below that, the column fails by yielding or by inelastic buckling, covered in 3.2 and 3.4.

A column buckles about the axis with the smallest I. For a rectangle, I = b h³/12 about the centroidal axis parallel to b. If h > b, the smaller I is about the other axis, so the column bows in the direction of the smaller dimension. Always compare I about both centroidal axes and use the smaller value.

In aircraft, engine-mount tubes and truss-rib struts are slender compression members. Their ends are often flattened and bolted, which gives partial fixity. A dent or a loose fitting can reduce the load below Euler's value. Local ovalisation or wall buckling can also happen first; that belongs to Unit 6.

Worked example

Question. An aluminium strut has E = 70 GPa, length L = 2.0 m, I = 2.0×10⁻⁷ m⁴ and A = 500 mm². It is pinned at both ends. (a) Find the Euler buckling load and the slenderness ratio. (b) Find the mean stress at buckling and state whether the elastic formula is valid if the alloy's proportional limit is about 200 MPa. (c) Repeat the buckling load for fixed-fixed, fixed-pinned and fixed-free ends.

  1. Convert: E = 70×10⁹ Pa, I = 2.0×10⁻⁷ m⁴, A = 500 mm² = 5.0×10⁻⁴ m², L = 2.0 m.
  2. Pinned ends: K = 1.0, so L_e = 2.0 m.
  3. P_cr = π²EI/L_e² = 9.8696 × 70×10⁹ × 2.0×10⁻⁷ / (2.0)².
  4. E × I = 70×10⁹ × 2.0×10⁻⁷ = 1.4×10⁴ N·m². Then P_cr = 9.8696 × 1.4×10⁴ / 4.0 = 34 543.6 N ≈ 34.5 kN.
  5. r = √(I/A) = √(2.0×10⁻⁷ / 5.0×10⁻⁴) = √(4.0×10⁻⁴) = 0.020 m = 20.0 mm.
  6. λ = L_e/r = 2.0 / 0.020 = 100.0.
  7. σ = P_cr/A = 34 543.6 / 5.0×10⁻⁴ = 6.909×10⁷ Pa = 69.1 MPa. This is below 200 MPa, so the elastic formula is valid.
  8. Fixed-fixed: K = 0.5, so P_cr = 34.5436 / 0.5² = 138.2 kN.
  9. Fixed-pinned: K = 0.7, so P_cr = 34.5436 / 0.7² = 70.5 kN.
  10. Fixed-free: K = 2.0, so P_cr = 34.5436 / 2.0² = 8.6 kN.

Answer. Pinned: P_cr = 34.5 kN, r = 20.0 mm, λ = 100.0, σ = 69.1 MPa; fixed-fixed 138.2 kN; fixed-pinned 70.5 kN; fixed-free 8.6 kN.

Exam traps

  • Using the full length L in π²EI/L² for a fixed or fixed-free strut. Replace L by L_e = K L before squaring.
  • Taking I about the stronger axis. The column bows about the axis with the smaller I, so use I_min.
  • Mixing units: E in GPa, I in mm⁴ and L in m. Convert everything to N and m before substituting.
  • Thinking P_cr depends on yield strength. Euler's load depends on E and I, not on the yield stress; yield only decides whether the elastic formula is allowed.
  • Using Euler for a stocky column where P_cr/A is above the proportional limit. Use the Rankine-Gordon formula or the tangent-modulus method there.

3.2 Rankine-Gordon formula for intermediate columns

Euler's formula suits a long, slender strut and is hopelessly optimistic for a short, stubby one, which simply squashes. Real columns lie between. The Rankine-Gordon formula blends the crushing load and the Euler load into one curve that is safe at both ends and sensible in between. Medium-length struts — fuselage frame members, landing-gear braces, control linkages — fall in that range.

You need the Euler load P_E and the slenderness ratio λ from Chapter 3.1.

Two failure loads, one curve

A short column fails by crushing at the squash load P_c = σ_c A. Here σ_c is the crushing stress in N/mm² and A the area in mm². A long column buckles at the Euler load P_E = π²EI/L_e², with E in N/mm², I in mm⁴ and L_e the effective length in mm. A middle column does neither cleanly: it yields a little, bows a little, and fails below both.

Rankine added the reciprocals: 1/P_R = 1/P_c + 1/P_E. A reciprocal is a flexibility, so the column's two ways of failing add like compliances in series. Rearranged, P_R = P_c P_E/(P_c + P_E).

P_R = σ_c A / (1 + a λ²), a = σ_c / (π²E)

P_R is the Rankine-Gordon failure load in N; σ_c the crushing stress in N/mm²; A the area in mm²; λ = L_e/r the slenderness ratio; r = √(I/A) the radius of gyration in mm; a the Rankine constant, dimensionless when σ_c and E share one unit.

The derivation is two lines. Since I = A r², the Euler load is P_E = π²EA/λ². Put that into P_R = P_cP_E/(P_c + P_E) and divide top and bottom by P_c: P_R = σ_cA/(1 + σ_cλ²/(π²E)). So a = σ_c/(π²E).

Short, long, and everything between

At λ = 0 the term aλ² vanishes and P_R = σ_cA = P_c. At large λ, aλ² ≫ 1, so P_R ≈ σ_cA/(aλ²) = σ_cA π²E/(σ_cλ²) = π²EA/λ² = P_E. The formula finds both limits on its own, with no switch to throw.

P_R never exceeds either load. The reciprocal 1/P_R is larger than 1/P_c and larger than 1/P_E, so P_R is smaller than both. An answer above the Euler load means an arithmetic slip, not a new kind of column.

σ_R = σ_c / (1 + a λ²)

σ_R is the average compressive stress at failure in N/mm²; compare it with the allowable stress, not with σ_c alone.

The constant a normally comes from tests or a handbook, because real struts carry initial bow, residual stress and end restraint that the ideal derivation ignores; a value from σ_c/(π²E) is only a first estimate. λ must always use L_e for the actual end conditions, and σ_c and E must be in the same unit. Some syllabi call the result the Rankine formula or the Gordon-Rankine formula. Only a few universities teach it in the structures paper.

Worked example

Question. A pinned-ended steel strut is 600 mm long, with A = 400 mm², I = 1.0×10⁴ mm⁴, E = 200 GPa and a crushing stress σ_c = 320 MPa. Find the radius of gyration, the slenderness ratio, the squash load, the Euler load and the Rankine-Gordon load.

  1. Radius of gyration: r = √(I/A) = √(1.0×10⁴/400) = √25 = 5 mm.
  2. Pinned ends give L_e = L = 600 mm, so λ = L_e/r = 600/5 = 120, dimensionless.
  3. Squash load: P_c = σ_cA = 320 N/mm² × 400 mm² = 128 000 N = 128 kN.
  4. Euler load, with E = 200 GPa = 200 000 N/mm²: P_E = π²EI/L_e² = 9.8696 × 200 000 × 1.0×10⁴ / 600² = 54 831 N = 54.8 kN.
  5. Constant: a = σ_c/(π²E) = 320/(9.8696 × 200 000) = 1.62×10⁻⁴, dimensionless.
  6. Rankine-Gordon load: P_R = 128/(1 + 1.62×10⁻⁴ × 120²) = 128/(1 + 2.33) = 128/3.33 = 38.4 kN.

Answer. P_R = 38.4 kN, below the Euler load of 54.8 kN and far below the squash load of 128 kN.

Exam traps

  • Mixing units inside a: σ_c in MPa and E in GPa gives a that is a thousand times wrong. Convert both to N/mm² first; a itself has no unit.
  • Using the true length L in λ instead of the effective length L_e. Fixed-free, fixed-pinned and fixed-fixed ends all change L_e and therefore λ (Chapter 3.1).
  • Feeding I into λ instead of r. λ = L_e/r with r = √(I/A); the two differ by a square root of the area.
  • Getting P_R larger than P_E and accepting it. The formula is a reciprocal sum, so P_R is always the smaller of the two loads — check it before writing the answer.

3.3 Imperfections and eccentric loading

A real strut is slightly bowed, or the load sits a little off the centroid. That small offset creates a bending moment, the bending deflects the strut further, and the extra deflection creates more moment. The growth is governed by a secant function that blows up at the Euler load. The secant formula gives the peak stress, not the average, and that is the number a designer checks. It explains why stringer straightness is inspected and why a fuselage frame is jacked with care.

You need Chapter 3.1 for P_E and effective length, and simple beam bending from Unit 2.

Imperfection is an eccentricity

No column is perfectly straight. A machined strut has a small bow; a rolled stringer has a set; a riveted frame carries the load line a millimetre or two from the centroid. The bow can be measured as a mid-span offset a. A load applied at distance e from the centroid produces the first-order moment P e. At mid-span this equals P a when e = a, so a measured mid-span bow a is analysed as an equivalent eccentricity e = a. Strictly the bow obeys EI v″ + P v = −P v₀(x), where v₀(x) is the initial bow shape, and the amplification depends on the bow shape; the secant result with e = a is the standard design estimate and is slightly conservative for a smooth bow. That is why initial curvature and eccentric loading are treated together.

The initial bow is not a defect to ignore. It sets the size of the bending from the first Newton of load. A strut with a = 2 mm and P = 8 kN already carries a primary moment P a = 16 N·m before any sideways deflection is counted. The Euler load from Chapter 3.1 is still the load at which a perfectly straight column would become unstable, but a real column bends from the start and reaches high stress earlier.

The secant formula

Take a pinned strut of length L, bending stiffness EI, and load P applied with eccentricity e at both ends. Let v(x) be the sideways deflection from the straight chord. The bending moment at any section is M = P(e + v). Sagging moment gives EI v″ = −P(e + v), so EI v″ + P v = −P e. Put k² = P/EI. The solution that satisfies v = 0 at both ends is v(x) = e[cos kx + tan(kL/2) sin kx − 1]. At mid-span this becomes v = e[sec(kL/2) − 1], so the mid-span moment is M = P e sec(kL/2).

EI d²v/dx² + P v = −P e

E is Young's modulus (Pa), I is the second moment of area (m⁴), v is sideways deflection (m), P is axial compression (N), e is load eccentricity or equivalent initial bow (m), and x is distance along the strut (m).

σ_max = (P/A)[1 + (ec/r²) sec((L_e/2)√(P/EI))] = (P/A)[1 + (ec/r²) sec((π/2)√(P/P_E))]

σ_max is the peak compressive stress (Pa), P is axial load (N), A is cross-section area (m²), e is load eccentricity or equivalent bow (m), c is distance from centroid to extreme fibre (m), r = √(I/A) is radius of gyration (m), L_e is effective length (m), E is Young's modulus (Pa), I is second moment of area (m⁴), P_E = π²EI/L_e² is Euler load (N), and the sec argument is in radians.

The factor sec((π/2)√(P/P_E)) multiplies the primary moment P e, so it is the moment magnification factor. At P → 0, sec → 1, so the primary moment is unchanged; the moment magnification is 1. At P → P_E, the argument tends to π/2 and sec tends to infinity. The stress factor over P/A is 1 + (ec/r²)sec. It includes the direct stress P/A and the bending stress. It is never 1 for e > 0, because the bending term is present from the start.

Design to σ_max, not σ_avg

Design checks use σ_max. The average stress P/A can sit at 40 MPa while the peak is 61.8 MPa, a factor of 1.55 in the worked example. A stringer specification therefore puts a limit on straightness: the measured bow over a gauge length must stay below a stated fraction, because the bow enters the secant term directly. In assembly, a fuselage frame is jacked and shimmed so the load path stays near the centroid. A jack that pushes off-centre introduces an e that the frame was not analysed for.

Perry-Robertson is the same idea written as an empirical column curve. It starts with an initial bow or eccentricity, allows the material to yield, and produces a smooth curve between the Euler load and the squash load. It is used where the secant formula overestimates the load a column can carry, because past the proportional limit the secant formula still assumes the full elastic stiffness and so understates the stress. Below that limit the secant formula and the Perry-Robertson curve are close.

The secant formula is elastic. If σ_max exceeds the proportional limit, the stiffness falls and the true deflection is larger. That is the subject of Chapter 3.4. The axial-plus-bending interaction for a beam with transverse load is in Chapter 3.5.

Worked example

Question. A pinned aluminium strut is 1.0 m long. E = 70 GPa, A = 200 mm², I = 2.0 × 10⁴ mm⁴, and the extreme fibre is c = 10 mm from the centroid. The axial load P = 8 kN acts 2 mm off the centroidal axis. (a) Find the Euler load. (b) Find the maximum compressive stress by the secant formula. (c) State the amplification over the mean stress P/A.

  1. Convert to SI: A = 200 mm² = 200 × 10⁻⁶ m²; I = 2.0 × 10⁴ mm⁴ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁸ m⁴; c = 10 mm = 0.010 m; e = 2 mm = 0.002 m; L = 1.0 m.
  2. Radius of gyration: r = √(I/A) = √(2.0 × 10⁻⁸ / 200 × 10⁻⁶) = √(1.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 0.010 m = 10 mm.
  3. Euler load: P_E = π²EI/L² = π² × 70 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁸ / 1.0² = 13 817 N = 13.8 kN.
  4. Mean stress: P/A = 8000 / 200 × 10⁻⁶ = 40 × 10⁶ Pa = 40 MPa.
  5. Eccentricity ratio: ec/r² = (0.002 × 0.010) / (0.010)² = 0.20.
  6. Secant argument: (L/2)√(P/EI) = 0.5 × √(8000 / (70 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁸)) = 0.5 × √(8000 / 1400) = 1.195 rad.
  7. Secant: sec(1.195) = 1 / cos(1.195) = 1 / 0.366 = 2.73.
  8. Peak stress: σ_max = 40 × (1 + 0.20 × 2.73) = 40 × 1.546 = 61.8 MPa.
  9. Amplification: σ_max / (P/A) = 61.8 / 40 = 1.55.

Answer. σ_max = 61.8 MPa, about 1.55 times the mean stress P/A = 40 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Using degrees instead of radians: cos(1.195°) ≈ 0.9998 gives sec ≈ 1.00 and σ_max ≈ 48 MPa, not 61.8 MPa. The sec argument is in radians.
  • Dropping the leading 1 in the bracket: σ_max is not (P/A)(ec/r²)sec alone. Without the direct-stress term the example gives only about 21.8 MPa of bending stress.
  • Mixing e and c: The product ec is symmetric, so this example gives the same number if you swap them — but the two are not interchangeable. c is a section property (centroid to extreme fibre, 10 mm here), e is the load offset (2 mm here). Confusing them gives wrong values as soon as e and c differ in role, for example in the bending stress M c / I, where only the section c belongs.
  • Using L instead of L_e: for fixed ends L_e = 0.5L, so the sec argument is halved and the secant is much smaller. Check the end condition before substituting.
  • Applying the elastic secant formula when σ_max exceeds the proportional limit. Then E_t < E and the real stress is larger; see Chapter 3.4.

3.4 Inelastic buckling

Euler's formula assumes the material is linear elastic all the way to buckling. That holds only while the stress is below the proportional limit. On a short or intermediate aircraft strut the stress can pass this limit first, and the material is then softer: its tangent stiffness E_t is less than Young's modulus E. The column buckles below the Euler load. This chapter gives the tangent-modulus and reduced-modulus loads, Shanley's correction, and Johnson's parabola for design checks of intermediate columns.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for P_E = π²EI/L_e² and end conditions, and Chapter 3.2 for the slenderness ratio λ = L_e/r.

Where Euler stops

The proportional limit σ_prop is the highest stress at which the stress-strain curve is still a straight line. Below σ_prop the slope is Young's modulus E. Above it, the slope falls and the material is partly plastic.

At a working stress σ, the tangent modulus E_t is the slope dσ/dε of the stress-strain curve at that point. It is not a constant: E_t = E below σ_prop and E_t < E above it. Engesser's idea is simple: a column buckles by bending, and bending stiffness depends on the current slope, not on the original E.

P_cr = π² E_t I / L_e²

P_cr is the inelastic buckling load (N); E_t is the tangent modulus at the buckling stress (N/mm²); I is the least second moment of area (mm⁴); L_e is the effective length (mm).

The switch from Euler to tangent modulus occurs at the transition slenderness λ_t. Set the Euler stress π²E/λ² equal to σ_prop and solve for λ.

λ_t = √(π² E / σ_prop)

λ_t is the transition slenderness (dimensionless); E is Young's modulus (N/mm²); σ_prop is the proportional limit (N/mm²).

For λ ≥ λ_t, Euler is valid: σ_cr = π²E/λ². For λ < λ_t, the column is inelastic and Euler overpredicts the strength. In design, find λ first, compare with λ_t, and only then choose the formula.

Tangent and reduced modulus

Kármán refined Engesser's model. When a column bows, the concave side is compressed further and loads along the tangent slope E_t, while the convex side unloads elastically with slope E. The bending stiffness is therefore a weighted value called the reduced modulus E_r, with E_t < E_r < E for a real metal. Kármán's load is P_cr = π² E_r I / L_e², where E_r replaces E_t in the same formula.

Shanley settled which modulus starts the buckle. On a rising load path, a perfect column first becomes unstable at the tangent-modulus load P_t = π²E_t I/L_e². It can then carry more load while bending grows, approaching the reduced-modulus load P_r. The true bifurcation is at E_t; E_r is an upper bound for a perfect column and is not the load at which real columns first buckle.

For design, the conservative and simple choice is E_t. Kármán's E_r explains why early tests on perfect specimens gave strengths above Engesser's prediction, but Shanley showed that imperfections and load path make the tangent value the one that matters.

If the stress-strain curve is known, read E_t at the stress you expect, then solve P_cr = π²E_t I/L_e² by trial, because E_t depends on the stress. Handbooks give E_t columns for aluminium and steel.

Johnson's parabola

Johnson's parabolic formula is a closed-form fit for intermediate columns. It uses only σ_y and E, so it is easy for hand calculations.

σ_cr = σ_y − (σ_y² / (4π²E)) λ²

σ_cr is the buckling stress (N/mm²); σ_y is the yield strength (N/mm²); E is Young's modulus (N/mm²); λ = L_e/r is the slenderness ratio (dimensionless).

It is valid up to λ_t = √(2π²E/σ_y). At that point σ_cr = σ_y/2. The parabola and Euler's hyperbola meet there with the same value, so the two curves join smoothly. Above λ_t, use Euler.

For very short members, another failure comes first. A thin-walled angle or a flange buckles locally, or the cross-section cripples, at a stress below the column formula. Those are covered in Unit 6, Plates, Buckling and Composite Structures; do not use Johnson blindly for a short thin-walled strut.

Worked example

Question. An aluminium alloy strut has E = 70 GPa, σ_y = 320 MPa, A = 300 mm² and r = 5 mm. It is pinned at both ends with L = 0.20 m. Find the buckling stress and load, and compare with Euler.

  1. Convert the length: L = 0.20 m = 200 mm. For pinned ends, L_e = L = 200 mm.

  4. Since λ = 40.0 < 65.7, the column is inelastic. Euler is invalid.

Exam traps

  • Quoting Kármán's E_r as the modulus that starts the buckle. Shanley's result is that E_t starts it; E_r is an upper bound.
  • Mixing L in metres with r in millimetres. Convert both to the same unit before computing λ.
  • Using σ_y in place of σ_prop to find λ_t for Euler validity. The proportional limit is the correct cut-off.
  • Applying Johnson above λ_t. The parabola is only valid up to λ_t; above it use Euler.

3.5 Beam-columns

A beam-column is a member that carries axial compression and bending at the same time. The axial load acts on the already deflected shape, so it adds a moment of its own. The bending moment is therefore larger than the moment from the transverse load alone. Design uses an amplified moment, not the first-order one. Fuselage stringers under pressurisation bending and lower wing spar panels in compression are everyday aircraft examples. The same idea explains why a slender strut with a small side load can fail well below its Euler load.

You need beam deflection from Unit 2, the Euler load from chapter 3.1, and the amplification idea from chapter 3.3.

Axial load on a deflected shape

A beam-column carries axial compression P and bending at the same time. The bending may come from a transverse load, from end moments, or from an eccentric line of action. If P were zero, the member would be an ordinary beam and the bending moment would be M(x), the first-order moment. With P present, the member deflects by v(x), and the axial force acts along a line that has moved sideways by v. The axial force therefore adds a moment P v at every section. That extra moment is not small near the Euler load.

EI d²v/dx² + P v = −M(x)

E is Young's modulus, I is the second moment of area about the bending axis, v is the sideways deflection, x is distance along the member, P is axial compression, and M(x) is the first-order bending moment from transverse loads or end moments. The term P v is the extra moment caused by the axial load acting on the deflected shape.

The term P v couples axial load to deflection. You cannot find v from the transverse load alone and then add stresses, because v itself depends on P. The bending moment used for stress must include P v. A fuselage stringer under pressurisation bending also carries axial load from fuselage bending; a lower wing spar panel can carry compression on the ground and bending from lift. Both are beam-columns. A strut with an eccentric end load is the same problem, which is why chapter 3.3 uses amplification.

Amplification for a simply supported member

Take a simply supported member of length L with axial compression P and a central transverse load W. The first-order mid-span moment is M0 = W L/4. The Euler load of the same member is PE = π²EI/L². For a sine-shaped deflection, v = vm sin(πx/L). Substitution into the beam-column equation gives vm = M0/(PE − P). The total mid-span moment is M0 plus P vm.

Mmax = M0 + P M0/(PE − P) = M0/(1 − P/PE)

Mmax is the amplified mid-span moment, M0 is the first-order moment, P is axial compression, and PE is the Euler load of the same member. The factor A = 1/(1 − P/PE) is the moment amplification.

At P = 0, A = 1. At P = 0.5PE, A = 2. As P tends to PE, A grows without limit. The figure plots A against P/PE; the example point at P/PE = 0.5066 has A = 2.027. The formula is exact for a sinusoidal first-order moment. A central point load is close, because its moment diagram is close to a sine; an off-centre load or unequal end moments are only approximate.

For equal end moments M0 at both ends, the exact elastic result is the secant form Mmax = M0 sec((π/2)√(P/PE)), where sec θ = 1/cos θ. This is the same secant that appears in eccentric struts. Use it when the first-order moment is constant along the member. For unequal end moments, a more detailed calculation or second-order computer analysis is needed.

Interaction check in design

The amplified moment is used inside a combined axial and bending check. A simple straight-line interaction is P/Pcr + M/Mu ≤ 1. P is axial compression, Pcr is the compression capacity of the member (squash load or buckling load with the chosen factor), M is the amplified moment, and Mu is the moment capacity about the bending axis. If the sum is below 1, the section is accepted by this check. Codes may use different exponents, but the principle is the same: the moment in the second term is the amplified one, not M0.

  1. Compute PE with the correct effective length.
  2. Compute M0 from the transverse load or end moments.
  3. Find the amplification A = 1/(1 − P/PE).
  4. Set M = A M0 and use M in the interaction check.

For small P/PE, A is close to 1, and the axial load changes little. For P/PE above about 0.5, A is above 2, so a first-order check can be badly unconservative. On a fuselage, a stringer takes axial stress from overall fuselage bending and local bending from pressurisation; the interaction catches the combined effect. On a wing, a lower spar panel in compression on the ground also bends with the wing; the same check applies. Local buckling of the panel is a separate check, covered in Unit 6.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported stringer of length 1.0 m carries 15 kN of axial compression. A transverse load alone gives a mid-span first-order moment M0 = 0.05 kN m. Take EI = 3000 N m², A = 250 mm², I = 3.6×10⁴ mm⁴ and c = 12 mm. Find the peak compressive stress at mid-span.

  5. Amplified moment: Mmax = A M0 = 2.027 × 50 = 101.34 N m = 0.1013 kN m.

Exam traps

  • Adding the axial stress and the first-order bending stress and stopping there. The axial load also increases the moment, and that amplified moment often controls the design.
  • Using P_E for pinned ends when the real member has different end restraint. The effective length changes P_E, and the amplification 1/(1 − P/P_E) changes with it.

  • Applying 1/(1 − P/P_E) to every transverse load or end moment. It is exact only for a sine-shaped first-order moment; it is close for a central load and approximate for other cases. Equal end moments need the secant form.

3.6 Failure theories: maximum principal stress and strain

A loaded point carries six stress numbers. A failure theory turns them into one number for comparison with a tension test. The maximum principal stress theory compares the largest normal stress with strength. The maximum principal strain theory compares the largest normal strain with yield strain. Both are simple, poor for ductile metals, reasonable for brittle castings; only a few universities teach them in the structures paper.

You need principal stresses and Mohr's circle, and Hooke's law (Unit 1).

Principal stresses and principal strains

A point carries σx, σy, σz, τxy, τyz, τzx in MPa. Rotate axes until shear vanishes. The remaining normal stresses are principal stresses, σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ σ3 algebraically. Principal strains ε1, ε2, ε3 lie along them, ε1 along σ1.

ε1 = (σ1 − νσ2 − νσ3)/E

ε1 is principal strain along σ1, dimensionless; stresses in MPa; E is Young's modulus in MPa; ν is Poisson's ratio, about 0.30 for aluminium alloy and 0.33 for steel.

Cycling subscripts gives ε2 and ε3. For plane stress σ3 = 0: ε1 = (σ1 − νσ2)/E, ε2 = (σ2 − νσ1)/E, ε3 = −ν(σ1 + σ2)/E. The third strain is not zero when the third stress is.

Rankine and St Venant

The maximum principal stress theory (Rankine) fails when the largest tensile principal stress reaches the tensile strength: yield strength σ_yt for a ductile metal, ultimate strength σ_ut for a brittle casting. It also fails when the most compressive stress falls to −σ_yc, the compressive strength. With one strength σ_y in both directions, |σ1| ≤ σ_y and |σ3| ≤ σ_y.

The maximum principal strain theory (St Venant) fails when max(|ε1|, |ε2|, |ε3|) reaches σ_y/E, the yield strain in tension. Multiply by E: σ_eq = E·max|ε| = max|σ1 − νσ2 − νσ3|, where σ_eq is equivalent stress in MPa. Safe while σ_eq ≤ σ_y. In plane stress σ_eq = max(|σ1 − νσ2|, |σ2 − νσ1|, ν|σ1 + σ2|).

In the σ1-σ2 plane Rankine's safe region is a square of side 2σ_y. St Venant's is a rhombus: edges σ1 − νσ2 = ±σ_y and σ2 − νσ1 = ±σ_y, long along σ1 = σ2, short along σ1 = −σ2. The third strain adds edges at σ1 + σ2 = ±σ_y/ν, far away for ν = 0.3.

Both ignore shear as a cause. Under pure shear τ, principal stresses are τ and −τ. Rankine predicts failure at τ = σ_y, St Venant at σ_y/(1 + ν) ≈ 0.77σ_y, but a ductile metal yields near 0.58σ_y. Both are unsafe for ductile metals; see 3.7. Rankine suits brittle castings; the strain rule survives for cast parts and some composites, where allowable strain is design data.

3.7 Maximum shear stress, distortion energy and octahedral theories

A ductile metal in a wing fitting sees two or three normal stresses plus shear. It yields when the shear, or the distortion energy, reaches the value measured in a plain tension test. Two criteria bracket the answer: maximum shear stress (Tresca), the conservative one, and distortion energy (von Mises), the usual design choice. Octahedral shear is von Mises in another dress. Stress engineers in design offices use these to turn a multiaxial state into one equivalent stress that is compared with the yield strength σY.

You need Chapter 3.6 for principal stresses and strains.

Tresca and von Mises

Tresca says yield starts when the largest shear anywhere reaches σY/2. In principal stresses σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ σ3:

σeq,T = max(|σ1 − σ2|, |σ2 − σ3|, |σ3 − σ1|) ≤ σY

σ1, σ2, σ3 are principal stresses in MPa; σY is the tensile yield strength in MPa.

In plane stress one principal stress is zero. If the other two have opposite signs, σeq,T is their difference. If they have the same sign, it is the larger magnitude alone. Taking the difference in both cases is the commonest error.

von Mises says yield starts when distortion energy — the strain energy that changes shape, not volume — reaches its tension-test value. Hydrostatic pressure changes volume only and cannot yield a ductile metal. In principal stresses:

σeq,M = √[(σ1 − σ2)² + (σ2 − σ3)² + (σ3 − σ1)²] / √2

σ1, σ2, σ3 are principal stresses in MPa; the result is in MPa.

For plane stress with σ3 = 0 this becomes σeq,M = √(σ1² − σ1σ2 + σ2²). In stress components:

σeq,M = √(σx² − σxσy + σy² + 3τxy²)

σx, σy are normal stresses and τxy is shear stress, all in MPa.

The 3 on τxy² is not optional.

Octahedral shear and comparison

The octahedral plane has a normal making equal angles with the three principal axes. Its shear stress is

τoct = (1/3)√[(σ1 − σ2)² + (σ2 − σ3)² + (σ3 − σ1)²]

τoct is the octahedral shear stress in MPa; σ1, σ2, σ3 are principal stresses in MPa.

Compare the square roots: σeq,M = (3/√2)τoct. So yielding when τoct = √2 σY/3 is exactly von Mises, not a third independent theory. The octahedral shear carries the whole distortion; the mean stress (σ1+σ2+σ3)/3 is the normal stress on the octahedral plane; it changes volume only and leaves τoct unchanged.

Tresca gives the larger equivalent stress, so it is conservative. The von Mises value is √(3/2) times the root-mean-square of the three principal differences, so it still cannot exceed the largest difference. Use Tresca for a quick hand check, von Mises for design and finite element work. Only a few universities teach the octahedral form here.

3.8 Fatigue: S-N curve and endurance limit

Fatigue is failure under a stress that repeats. One application of 500 MPa may be safe; the same stress ten thousand times can start a crack and grow it until the part breaks. The S-N curve records this: stress amplitude S against cycles to failure N. Designers use it for wings, landing gear and engine mounts, where loads reverse in every flight. Only a few universities teach this in the structures paper.

You need: the safe steady stress idea from Chapter 3.7. Mean stress is handled in Chapter 3.9.

The S-N curve

Fatigue has two stages: crack initiation at a notch or surface defect, then crack growth under repeated stress. High-cycle fatigue means many cycles, usually above 10⁴, with stress mostly elastic. Low-cycle fatigue means few cycles, below about 10⁴, with local plastic strain.

A rotating-bending test bends a polished specimen while it spins. Each turn gives one complete stress cycle from tension to compression and back. The test repeats at a fixed stress amplitude S and records the cycles N until the specimen breaks. Plotting S against N gives the S-N curve, also called the Wöhler curve.

S = A N^b

S is stress amplitude in MPa; N is cycles to failure; A is the stress at N = 1 in MPa; b is the fatigue exponent, negative for a falling line.

N is plotted on a log scale because life runs from thousands to millions of cycles. On log₁₀ N against log S the high-cycle part is straight. The figure uses 600 MPa at 10³ cycles and 400 MPa at 10⁶ cycles.

Endurance limit and Basquin's law

The endurance limit, or fatigue limit, Se, is the stress amplitude below which the material survives indefinitely. For many steels it appears as a horizontal line after about 10⁶ to 10⁷ cycles. Below Se no failure occurs; just above it, failure still occurs after a long life.

Aluminium and most non-ferrous alloys have no true endurance limit. Their S-N curve keeps falling. Design quotes them at a fixed life, often 5×10⁸ cycles, called the fatigue strength at that life. Do not assume a flat line for an aluminium wing skin.

Basquin's law fits the sloping part. Taking logarithms gives log S = log A + b log N. Two test points (N₁, S₁) and (N₂, S₂) give b = (log S₂ − log S₁)/(log N₂ − log N₁). Then log A = log S₁ − b log N₁. Use the same logarithm base throughout.

Real parts lose strength through surface finish, size, notches, corrosion and mean stress: a rough surface starts cracks earlier, a large section has more weak spots, a notch concentrates stress, corrosion pits act as cracks, and tensile mean stress raises the effective amplitude. See Chapter 3.9.

Worked example

Question. A steel has ultimate strength 800 MPa. Rotating-bending tests give 600 MPa at 10³ cycles and 400 MPa at 10⁶ cycles; beyond 10⁶ cycles the strength stays 400 MPa. Fit Basquin's law S = A N^b and find the life at S = 500 MPa.

  1. Use points (N₁, S₁) = (10³, 600 MPa) and (N₂, S₂) = (10⁶, 400 MPa).
  2. log 600 = log A + 3b and log 400 = log A + 6b.
  3. Subtract: b = (2.602 − 2.778)/3 = −0.0587.
  4. log A = 2.778 + 3(0.0587) = 2.954, so A = 900 MPa.
  5. At S = 500 MPa: log(500/900) = −0.0587 log N.
  6. log N = 0.2553/0.0587 = 4.35.
  7. N = 10^4.35 = 2.23×10⁴ cycles ≈ 22,300 cycles.
  8. The endurance limit is 400 MPa, half of 800 MPa.

Answer. Basquin's law is S = 900 N^(−0.0587) with S in MPa; at 500 MPa the life is about 2.23×10⁴ cycles (22,300 cycles).

Exam traps

  • Reading N on a linear axis hides the long-life part; use log₁₀ N.
  • Assuming aluminium has an endurance limit like steel; it is quoted at a fixed life instead.
  • Using ultimate strength as the endurance limit; use test data, not a fixed fraction.
  • Calling the fatigue limit the yield stress; fatigue failure can occur far below yield.
  • Using stress range instead of stress amplitude; S in the S-N curve is amplitude, half the range.

3.9 Mean and alternating stresses; stress ratio

A real aircraft part rarely sees a steady stress: a gust, a landing or a pressurisation cycle pushes it up and down. Every fluctuating stress splits into a steady mean part and a to-and-fro alternating part. Two ratios fix the shape of the cycle. A constant-life diagram then folds both parts into a single factor of safety against fatigue. Only a few universities teach this topic at this depth.

You need: Chapter 3.8 for the endurance limit and the S-N curve, and Chapter 3.6 for combining stresses at a point.

Splitting a cycle in two

One fatigue cycle runs between a maximum stress σ_max and a minimum stress σ_min, both in MPa, tension positive. Split it about its midpoint.

σ_m = (σ_max + σ_min)/2    σ_a = (σ_max − σ_min)/2

σ_m is the mean stress, the steady part (MPa); σ_a is the alternating stress, the half-swing about the mean (MPa); the range is σ_r = σ_max − σ_min = 2σ_a.

Two ratios fix the shape of the cycle. The stress ratio is R = σ_min/σ_max. The amplitude ratio is A = σ_a/σ_m. The value of R tells you which cycle you have.

  • R = −1: fully reversed, σ_m = 0 and σ_max = −σ_min — the rotating-bending test of Chapter 3.8.
  • R = 0: zero to maximum (pulsating tension), σ_min = 0, so σ_m = σ_a = σ_max/2.
  • R = 1: steady load, σ_a = 0; fatigue does not arise.

The constant-life (Haigh) diagram

Plot σ_m across and σ_a up. Each point is one cycle. For a chosen life a curve separates safe cycles from failing ones. It meets the vertical axis at the endurance limit S_e, where σ_m = 0, and the horizontal axis at a static strength, where σ_a = 0.

Goodman: σ_a/S_e + σ_m/S_u = 1;  Gerber: σ_a/S_e + (σ_m/S_u)² = 1;  Soderberg: σ_a/S_e + σ_m/S_y = 1

S_e is the endurance limit (MPa), S_u the ultimate tensile strength (MPa), S_y the yield strength (MPa).

Goodman is a straight line from S_e to S_u and the usual conservative choice. Gerber is a parabola through the same two points and follows ductile test data better. Soderberg runs from S_e to S_y and is the most conservative. The Langer line σ_a + σ_m = S_y is plain first-cycle yielding: when the peak stress σ_max = σ_a + σ_m reaches S_y, the part yields at once, whatever its fatigue life.

The factor of safety n scales both stresses until the load point touches the curve. Put nσ_m and nσ_a into the line: Goodman gives 1/n = σ_a/S_e + σ_m/S_u, Soderberg gives 1/n = σ_a/S_e + σ_m/S_y. Gerber gives a quadratic in n; take the positive root.

Worked example

Question. A steel aircraft fitting sees σ_max = 300 MPa and σ_min = −100 MPa. Take S_e = 400 MPa, S_u = 800 MPa and S_y = 600 MPa. Find σ_m, σ_a, R and A, the factor of safety by Goodman, Soderberg and Gerber, and check first-cycle yielding.

  1. σ_m = (σ_max + σ_min)/2 = (300 + (−100))/2 = 100 MPa.
  2. σ_a = (σ_max − σ_min)/2 = (300 − (−100))/2 = 200 MPa.
  3. R = σ_min/σ_max = −100/300 = −0.33. A = σ_a/σ_m = 200/100 = 2.0.
  4. Goodman: 1/n = σ_a/S_e + σ_m/S_u = 200/400 + 100/800 = 0.5 + 0.125 = 0.625, so n = 1.60.
  5. Soderberg: 1/n = σ_a/S_e + σ_m/S_y = 200/400 + 100/600 = 0.5 + 0.167 = 0.667, so n = 1.50.
  6. Gerber: 0.5n + (0.125n)² = 1, that is 0.0156n² + 0.5n − 1 = 0. The positive root is n = (−0.5 + √(0.25 + 0.0625))/0.03125 = 1.89.
  7. Yield check: σ_a + σ_m = 200 + 100 = 300 MPa, below S_y = 600 MPa, so the first cycle does not yield.

Answer. σ_m = 100 MPa, σ_a = 200 MPa, R = −0.33, A = 2.0; n = 1.60 by Goodman, 1.50 by Soderberg and 1.89 by Gerber, and the fitting does not yield on the first cycle.

Exam traps

  • Dividing S_e by σ_a while a tensile mean stress acts. The mean part also spends life; use σ_a/S_e + σ_m/S_u. The shortcut n = S_e/σ_a holds only at R = −1.
  • Writing R = σ_max/σ_min. It is R = σ_min/σ_max, and compression keeps its sign: −100/300 = −0.33, not +0.33.
  • Putting S_u where S_y belongs. The Goodman line ends at S_u, Soderberg at S_y, and the Langer line sits at S_y.
  • Checking the fatigue line only. A large mean stress yields the part on cycle one, so always test σ_a + σ_m against S_y.

Takeaways

  1. Euler load is a bifurcation load: a straight elastic column stays straight below it and bows above it.
  2. Effective length turns fixed, pinned and free end conditions into an equivalent pin-ended column length.
  3. Rankine-Gordon gives a smooth estimate for intermediate columns, between crushing load and Euler load.
  4. Real columns have initial bow or load eccentricity, and the secant factor makes bending grow without limit at the Euler load.
  5. Above the proportional limit the tangent modulus E_t replaces E, so inelastic buckling occurs below the Euler load.
  6. A beam-column must be designed with amplified moments because axial compression increases bending.
  7. Maximum principal stress and maximum principal strain theories compare a complex stress state with a simple tension test.
  8. For ductile metals, Tresca and von Mises criteria predict yield from shear or distortion, with Tresca usually conservative.
  9. Repeated stress below yield can cause fatigue failure; the S-N curve gives cycles to failure at a given stress amplitude.
  10. Mean and alternating stresses combine on a constant-life diagram to give a fatigue factor of safety.

Yield criterion
A rule that predicts the stress state at which a material begins to yield.
