About this unit

The unit is about members in compression and about how a stress state becomes a failure. It starts with the load at which a straight column bows out (Euler) and the effect of its end conditions, then the Rankine-Gordon fit that joins buckling to crushing for columns of middle length. The next two chapters make the column real: it is never quite straight, and it may yield before it buckles. Beam-columns add bending to the axial load. The question then changes from stability to strength: the classical failure theories compare a computed stress state with the yield or fracture strength. The last two chapters cover repeated loads: the S-N curve, the endurance limit, and the mean-alternating diagram that turns a fluctuating load into a factor of safety.

3.1 Euler buckling of columns and end conditions

A straight column under axial compression is in equilibrium when it stays straight. That equilibrium is stable only up to a load of π²EI/Le². At that load a tiny sideways bow grows instead of shrinking: the column buckles. The idea matters because a thin tube can carry far less than its material's squash load, and aircraft struts, engine-mount tubes and truss-rib members are thin. Euler's formula gives the critical load from stiffness and length alone, so it is the first check on any slender compression member.

You need: the beam curvature relation EI d²v/dx² = M, and the idea of a strut as a two-force member in a truss.

Why a straight strut buckles

Take a straight column of length L, area A and second moment of area I. Push it with an axial load P. If a small sideways deflection v(x) appears, the load line is no longer over the centroid of the section. The load then makes a bending moment M = P v at that section. The column's bending stiffness resists it, and for small slopes the curvature is v'' = d²v/dx². Equilibrium of the slice gives EI v'' = −P v.

EI d²v/dx² = −P v

E is Young's modulus in Pa; I is the second moment of area about the bending axis in m⁴; v is the lateral deflection in m; x is distance along the column in m; P is the axial compression in N.

  1. Pinned ends give v = 0 at x = 0 and x = L.
  2. Set k² = P/(EI). Then v'' + k²v = 0.
  3. Solution v = C₁ sin kx + C₂ cos kx, where C₁ and C₂ are constants; v(0) = 0 gives C₂ = 0.
  4. v(L) = 0 gives C₁ sin kL = 0. C₁ = 0 is the straight column; a bent shape needs sin kL = 0, and the first buckle is kL = π.
  5. So Pcr = π²EI/L².

The formula is Euler's critical load. It is also called Euler’s theory of struts. It contains no strength term: a stronger alloy with the same E and I buckles at the same load.

  • Perfectly straight: an initial bow adds bending at once; see imperfections and eccentric loading.
  • Elastic: P/A must stay below the proportional limit; otherwise use inelastic buckling.
  • Centroidal load: an offset e adds a moment P e.
  • Small deflection: curvature is close to v''.
  • Ends hold position but allow the assumed rotation.

End conditions and effective length

End fixity decides how much of the column can bend. A pinned end allows rotation but holds position. A fixed end allows neither. The effective length Le is the length of an equivalent pinned-pinned column with the same Pcr. The factor K multiplies the real length: Le = K L, where K has no unit and L is in m.

Pcr = π²EI/Le² = π²EI/(K L)²

Pcr is the Euler buckling load in N; K is the effective-length factor; L is the real column length in m.

End conditionKLePcr
Pinned-pinned1.0Lπ²EI/L²
Fixed-fixed0.50.5L4π²EI/L²
Fixed-pinned0.70.7L2.04π²EI/L²
Fixed-free2.02Lπ²EI/(4L²)

Fixed-free is the weakest because the free tip can move sideways with no support. Fixed-fixed is the strongest: the buckled shape is a full cosine wave over L, and its middle half, between the points of contraflexure at L/4 and 3L/4, is a pinned half-wave of length L/2, so Le = 0.5L. Real joints sit between these ideals. A riveted tube end is closer to fixed; a single pin is closer to pinned. When the same member also carries bending, see beam-columns.

Slenderness and the weak axis

Slenderness measures how thin a column is. The radius of gyration is r = √(I/A), in m, where A is the cross-section area in m². Slenderness is λ = Le/r, dimensionless. A stocky column has small λ; a slender one has large λ.

λ = Le/r = Le/√(I/A)

λ is the slenderness ratio; Le is the effective length in m; r is the radius of gyration in m; I is the second moment of area in m⁴; A is the area in m².

Euler's formula is valid while the buckling stress Pcr/A is below the proportional limit. Setting π²E/λ² equal to the proportional limit σprop gives the limiting slenderness √(π²E/σprop): about 99 for mild steel (E = 200 GPa, σprop ≈ 200 MPa) and about 59 for an aluminium alloy (E = 70 GPa, σprop ≈ 200 MPa). Euler needs λ above that value. Below that, the column fails by yielding or by inelastic buckling, covered in Rankine-Gordon formula for intermediate columns and inelastic buckling.

A column buckles about the axis with the smallest I. For a rectangle, I = b h³/12 about the centroidal axis parallel to b. If h > b, the smaller I is about the other axis, so the column bows in the direction of the smaller dimension. Always compare I about both centroidal axes and use the smaller value.

In aircraft, engine-mount tubes and truss-rib struts are slender compression members. Their ends are often flattened and bolted, which gives partial fixity. A dent or a loose fitting can reduce the load below Euler's value. Local ovalisation or wall buckling can also happen first; that is a plate-buckling check, made separately.

Worked example

Question. An aluminium strut has E = 70 GPa, length L = 2.0 m, I = 2.0×10⁻⁷ m⁴ and A = 500 mm². It is pinned at both ends. (a) Find the Euler buckling load and the slenderness ratio. (b) Find the mean stress at buckling and state whether the elastic formula is valid if the alloy's proportional limit is about 200 MPa. (c) Repeat the buckling load for fixed-fixed, fixed-pinned and fixed-free ends.

  1. Convert: E = 70×10⁹ Pa, I = 2.0×10⁻⁷ m⁴, A = 500 mm² = 5.0×10⁻⁴ m², L = 2.0 m.
  2. Pinned ends: K = 1.0, so Le = 2.0 m.
  3. Pcr = π²EI/Le² = 9.8696 × 70×10⁹ × 2.0×10⁻⁷ / (2.0)².
  4. E × I = 70×10⁹ × 2.0×10⁻⁷ = 1.4×10⁴ N·m². Then Pcr = 9.8696 × 1.4×10⁴ / 4.0 = 34 543.6 N ≈ 34.5 kN.
  5. r = √(I/A) = √(2.0×10⁻⁷ / 5.0×10⁻⁴) = √(4.0×10⁻⁴) = 0.020 m = 20.0 mm.
  6. λ = Le/r = 2.0 / 0.020 = 100.0.
  7. σ = Pcr/A = 34 543.6 / 5.0×10⁻⁴ = 6.909×10⁷ Pa = 69.1 MPa. This is below 200 MPa, so the elastic formula is valid.
  8. Fixed-fixed: K = 0.5, so Pcr = 34.5436/0.5² = 138.2 kN. Its mean stress would be 138 200/5.0×10⁻⁴ = 276 MPa, above the 200 MPa proportional limit, so this Euler value is not valid: the fixed-fixed strut fails inelastically at a lower load.
  9. Fixed-pinned: K = 0.7, so Pcr = 34.5436/0.7² = 70.5 kN, a mean stress of 141 MPa, below 200 MPa, so Euler is valid.
  10. Fixed-free: K = 2.0, so Pcr = 34.5436 / 2.0² = 8.6 kN.

Answer. Pinned: Pcr = 34.5 kN, r = 20.0 mm, λ = 100.0, σ = 69.1 MPa (Euler valid); fixed-pinned 70.5 kN (valid, 141 MPa); fixed-free 8.6 kN; fixed-fixed 138.2 kN by Euler, but at 276 MPa that is above the proportional limit, so the real fixed-fixed strut fails below it.

Exam traps

  • Using the full length L in π²EI/L² for a fixed or fixed-free strut. Replace L by Le = K L before squaring.
  • Taking I about the stronger axis. The column bows about the axis with the smaller I, so use Imin.
  • Mixing units: E in GPa, I in mm⁴ and L in m. Convert everything to N and m before substituting.
  • Thinking Pcr depends on yield strength. Euler's load depends on E and I, not on the yield stress; yield only decides whether the elastic formula is allowed.
  • Using Euler for a stocky column where Pcr/A is above the proportional limit. Use the Rankine-Gordon formula or the tangent-modulus method there.

3.2 Rankine-Gordon formula for intermediate columns

Euler's formula suits a long, slender strut and is hopelessly optimistic for a short, stubby one, which simply squashes. Real columns lie between. The Rankine-Gordon formula blends the crushing load and the Euler load into one curve that is safe at both ends and sensible in between. Medium-length struts — fuselage frame members, landing-gear braces, control linkages — fall in that range.

You need the Euler load PE and the slenderness ratio λ from Euler buckling of columns and end conditions.

Two failure loads, one curve

A short column fails by crushing at the squash load Pc = σc A. Here σc is the crushing stress in N/mm² and A the area in mm². A long column buckles at the Euler load PE = π²EI/Le², with E in N/mm², I in mm⁴ and Le the effective length in mm. A middle column does neither cleanly: it yields a little, bows a little, and fails below both.

Rankine added the reciprocals: 1/PR = 1/Pc + 1/PE. This is an empirical rule, not a physical model: it is chosen so that the curve passes through the squash load for a very short column and approaches the Euler load for a long one. Rearranged, PR = Pc PE/(Pc + PE).

PR = σc A / (1 + a λ²),   a = σc / (π²E)

PR is the Rankine-Gordon failure load in N; σc the crushing stress in N/mm²; A the area in mm²; λ = Le/r the slenderness ratio; r = √(I/A) the radius of gyration in mm; a the Rankine constant, dimensionless when σc and E share one unit.

The derivation is two lines. Since I = A r², the Euler load is PE = π²EA/λ². Put that into PR = PcP_E/(Pc + PE) and divide top and bottom by Pc: PR = σcA/(1 + σcλ²/(π²E)). So a = σc/(π²E).

Short, long, and everything between

At λ = 0 the term aλ² vanishes and PR = σcA = Pc. At large λ, aλ² ≫ 1, so PR ≈ σcA/(aλ²) = σcA π²E/(σcλ²) = π²EA/λ² = PE. The formula finds both limits on its own, with no switch to throw.

PR never exceeds either load. The reciprocal 1/PR is larger than 1/Pc and larger than 1/PE, so PR is smaller than both. An answer above the Euler load means an arithmetic slip, not a new kind of column.

σR = σc / (1 + a λ²)

σR is the average compressive stress at failure in N/mm²; compare it with the allowable stress, not with σc alone.

The constant a normally comes from tests or a handbook, because real struts carry initial bow, residual stress and end restraint that the ideal derivation ignores; a value from σc/(π²E) is only a first estimate. λ must always use Le for the actual end conditions, and σc and E must be in the same unit. The result is also called the Rankine formula or the Gordon-Rankine formula.

Worked example

Question. A pinned-ended steel strut is 600 mm long, with A = 400 mm², I = 1.0×10⁴ mm⁴, E = 200 GPa and a crushing stress σc = 320 MPa. Find the radius of gyration, the slenderness ratio, the squash load, the Euler load and the Rankine-Gordon load.

  1. Radius of gyration: r = √(I/A) = √(1.0×10⁴/400) = √25 = 5 mm.
  2. Pinned ends give Le = L = 600 mm, so λ = Le/r = 600/5 = 120, dimensionless.
  3. Squash load: Pc = σcA = 320 N/mm² × 400 mm² = 128 000 N = 128 kN.
  4. Euler load, with E = 200 GPa = 200 000 N/mm²: PE = π²EI/Le² = 9.8696 × 200 000 × 1.0×10⁴ / 600² = 54 831 N = 54.8 kN.
  5. Constant: a = σc/(π²E) = 320/(9.8696 × 200 000) = 1.62×10⁻⁴, dimensionless.
  6. Rankine-Gordon load: PR = 128/(1 + 1.62×10⁻⁴ × 120²) = 128/(1 + 2.33) = 128/3.33 = 38.4 kN.

Answer. PR = 38.4 kN, below the Euler load of 54.8 kN and far below the squash load of 128 kN.

Question. A landing-gear brace is a mild-steel tube, 40 mm outside and 32 mm inside diameter, 2.0 m long and pinned at both ends. Take σc = 320 MPa, the test-based Rankine constant a = 1/7500 for pinned mild steel, and E = 200 GPa. Find the Rankine-Gordon load, the safe load with a factor of safety of 3, and compare with Euler.

  1. Area: A = π(40² − 32²)/4 = π × 576/4 = 452.4 mm².
  2. Second moment: I = π(40⁴ − 32⁴)/64 = π × 1 511 424/64 = 74 192 mm⁴.
  3. r = √(I/A) = √(74 192/452.4) = √164 = 12.81 mm (for a tube r² = (D² + d²)/16 = 2624/16 = 164).
  4. λ = Le/r = 2000/12.81 = 156.2; aλ² = 156.2²/7500 = 3.252.
  5. PR = σcA/(1 + aλ²) = 320 × 452.4/4.252 = 144 765/4.252 = 34 046 N = 34.0 kN. Safe load = 34.0/3 = 11.3 kN.

Answer. Rankine-Gordon load 34.0 kN, safe load 11.3 kN; Euler gives 36.6 kN. With the theoretical a = σc/(π²E) = 1.62 × 10⁻⁴ the load would be 29.2 kN, so the test constant matters.

Exam traps

  • Mixing units inside a: σc in MPa and E in GPa gives a that is a thousand times wrong. Convert both to N/mm² first; a itself has no unit.
  • Using the true length L in λ instead of the effective length Le. Fixed-free, fixed-pinned and fixed-fixed ends all change Le and therefore λ (Euler buckling of columns and end conditions).
  • Feeding I into λ instead of r. λ = Le/r with r = √(I/A); I and r are linked by I = Ar², so one cannot stand in for r.
  • Getting PR larger than PE and accepting it. The formula is a reciprocal sum, so PR is always the smaller of the two loads — check it before writing the answer.

3.3 Imperfections and eccentric loading

A real strut is slightly bowed, or the load sits a little off the centroid. That small offset creates a bending moment, the bending deflects the strut further, and the extra deflection creates more moment. The growth is governed by a secant function that blows up at the Euler load. The secant formula gives the peak stress, not the average, and that is the number a designer checks. It explains why stringer straightness is inspected and why a fuselage frame is jacked with care.

You need Euler buckling of columns and end conditions for PE and effective length, and simple beam bending from Unit 2.

Imperfection is an eccentricity

No column is perfectly straight. A machined strut has a small bow; a rolled stringer has a set; a riveted frame carries the load line a millimetre or two from the centroid. The bow can be measured as a mid-span offset a. A load applied at distance e from the centroid produces the first-order moment P e. At mid-span this equals P a when e = a, so a measured mid-span bow a is analysed as an equivalent eccentricity e = a. Strictly the bow obeys EI v″ + P v = −P v₀(x), where v₀(x) is the initial bow shape, and the amplification depends on the bow shape; the secant result with e = a is the standard design estimate and is slightly conservative for a smooth bow. That is why initial curvature and eccentric loading are treated together.

The initial bow is not a defect to ignore. It sets the size of the bending from the first Newton of load. A strut with a = 2 mm and P = 8 kN already carries a primary moment P a = 16 N·m before any sideways deflection is counted. The Euler load from Euler buckling of columns and end conditions is still the load at which a perfectly straight column would become unstable, but a real column bends from the start and reaches high stress earlier.

For a sine-shaped bow of mid-span size a, the exact mid-span moment is Pa/(1 − P/PE). In the worked example (P/PE = 0.579) that is 2.38Pa, against 2.73Pa from the secant with e = a, so the secant estimate is on the safe side.

The secant formula

Take a pinned strut of length L, bending stiffness EI, and load P applied with eccentricity e at both ends. Let v(x) be the sideways deflection from the straight chord. The bending moment at any section is M = P(e + v). Sagging moment gives EI v″ = −P(e + v), so EI v″ + P v = −P e. Put k² = P/EI. The solution that satisfies v = 0 at both ends is v(x) = e[cos kx + tan(kL/2) sin kx − 1]. At mid-span this becomes v = e[sec(kL/2) − 1], so the mid-span moment is M = P e sec(kL/2).

EI d²v/dx² + P v = −P e

E is Young's modulus (Pa), I is the second moment of area (m⁴), v is sideways deflection (m), P is axial compression (N), e is load eccentricity or equivalent initial bow (m), and x is distance along the strut (m).

σmax = (P/A)[1 + (ec/r²) sec((Le/2)√(P/EI))] = (P/A)[1 + (ec/r²) sec((π/2)√(P/PE))]

σmax is the peak compressive stress (Pa), P is axial load (N), A is cross-section area (m²), e is load eccentricity or equivalent bow (m), c is distance from centroid to extreme fibre (m), r = √(I/A) is radius of gyration (m), Le is effective length (m), E is Young's modulus (Pa), I is second moment of area (m⁴), PE = π²EI/Le² is Euler load (N), and the sec argument is in radians.

The factor sec((π/2)√(P/PE)) multiplies the primary moment P e, so it is the moment magnification factor. At P → 0, sec → 1, so the primary moment is unchanged; the moment magnification is 1. At P → PE, the argument tends to π/2 and sec tends to infinity. The stress factor over P/A is 1 + (ec/r²)sec. It includes the direct stress P/A and the bending stress. It is never 1 for e > 0, because the bending term is present from the start.

Design to σmax, not σavg

Design checks use σmax. The average stress P/A can sit at 40 MPa while the peak is 61.8 MPa, a factor of 1.55 in the worked example. A stringer specification therefore puts a limit on straightness: the measured bow over a gauge length must stay below a stated fraction, because the bow enters the secant term directly. In assembly, a fuselage frame is jacked and shimmed so the load path stays near the centroid. A jack that pushes off-centre introduces an e that the frame was not analysed for.

Perry-Robertson is the same idea written as an empirical column curve. It starts with an initial bow or eccentricity, allows the material to yield, and produces a smooth curve between the Euler load and the squash load. It is used where the secant formula overestimates the load a column can carry, because past the proportional limit the secant formula still assumes the full elastic stiffness and so understates the stress. Below that limit the secant formula and the Perry-Robertson curve are close.

The secant formula is elastic. If σmax exceeds the proportional limit, the stiffness falls and the true deflection is larger. That is the subject of inelastic buckling. The axial-plus-bending interaction for a beam with transverse load is in beam-columns.

Worked example

Question. A pinned aluminium strut is 1.0 m long. E = 70 GPa, A = 200 mm², I = 2.0 × 10⁴ mm⁴, and the extreme fibre is c = 10 mm from the centroid. The axial load P = 8 kN acts 2 mm off the centroidal axis. (a) Find the Euler load. (b) Find the maximum compressive stress by the secant formula. (c) State the amplification over the mean stress P/A.

  1. Convert to SI: A = 200 mm² = 200 × 10⁻⁶ m²; I = 2.0 × 10⁴ mm⁴ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁸ m⁴; c = 10 mm = 0.010 m; e = 2 mm = 0.002 m; L = 1.0 m.
  2. Radius of gyration: r = √(I/A) = √(2.0 × 10⁻⁸ / 200 × 10⁻⁶) = √(1.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 0.010 m = 10 mm.
  3. Euler load: PE = π²EI/L² = π² × 70 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁸ / 1.0² = 13 817 N = 13.8 kN.
  4. Mean stress: P/A = 8000 / 200 × 10⁻⁶ = 40 × 10⁶ Pa = 40 MPa.
  5. Eccentricity ratio: ec/r² = (0.002 × 0.010) / (0.010)² = 0.20.
  6. Secant argument: (L/2)√(P/EI) = 0.5 × √(8000 / (70 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁸)) = 0.5 × √(8000 / 1400) = 1.195 rad.
  7. Secant: sec(1.195) = 1 / cos(1.195) = 1 / 0.366 = 2.73.
  8. Peak stress: σmax = 40 × (1 + 0.20 × 2.73) = 40 × 1.546 = 61.8 MPa.
  9. Amplification: σmax / (P/A) = 61.8 / 40 = 1.55.

Answer. σmax = 61.8 MPa, about 1.55 times the mean stress P/A = 40 MPa.

3.4 Inelastic buckling

Euler's formula assumes the material is linear elastic all the way to buckling. That holds only while the stress is below the proportional limit. On a short or intermediate aircraft strut the stress can pass this limit first, and the material is then softer: its tangent stiffness Et is less than Young's modulus E. The column buckles below the Euler load. This chapter gives the tangent-modulus and reduced-modulus loads, Shanley's correction, and Johnson's parabola for design checks of intermediate columns.

You need: Euler buckling of columns and end conditions for PE = π²EI/Le² and end conditions, and Rankine-Gordon formula for intermediate columns for the slenderness ratio λ = Le/r.

Where Euler stops

Slenderness is λ = Le/r, the effective length over the radius of gyration, as in Euler buckling. Two limiting values of λ appear in this chapter: λp, where the Euler stress reaches the proportional limit, and λJ, where Johnson’s parabola meets Euler’s curve.

The proportional limit σprop is the highest stress at which the stress-strain curve is still a straight line. Below σprop the slope is Young's modulus E. Above it, the slope falls and the material is partly plastic.

At a working stress σ, the tangent modulus Et is the slope dσ/dε of the stress-strain curve at that point. It is not a constant: Et = E below σprop and Et < E above it. Engesser's idea is simple: a column buckles by bending, and bending stiffness depends on the current slope, not on the original E.

Pcr = π² Et I / Le²

Pcr is the inelastic buckling load (N); Et is the tangent modulus at the buckling stress (N/mm²); I is the least second moment of area (mm⁴); Le is the effective length (mm).

The switch from Euler to tangent modulus occurs at the proportional-limit slenderness λp. Set the Euler stress π²E/λ² equal to σprop and solve for λ.

λp = √(π² E / σprop)

λp is the proportional-limit slenderness (dimensionless); E is Young's modulus (N/mm²); σprop is the proportional limit (N/mm²).

For λ ≥ λp, Euler is valid: σcr = π²E/λ². For λ < λp, the column is inelastic and Euler overpredicts the strength. In design, find λ first, compare with λp, and only then choose the formula.

Tangent and reduced modulus

Kármán refined Engesser's model. When a column bows, the concave side is compressed further and loads along the tangent slope Et, while the convex side unloads elastically with slope E. The bending stiffness is therefore a weighted value called the reduced modulus Er, with Et < Er < E for a real metal. Kármán's load is Pcr = π² Er I / Le², where Er replaces Et in the same formula.

Shanley settled which modulus starts the buckle. On a rising load path, a perfect column first becomes unstable at the tangent-modulus load Pt = π²Et I/Le². It can then carry more load while bending grows, approaching the reduced-modulus load Pr. The true bifurcation is at Et; Er is an upper bound for a perfect column and is not the load at which real columns first buckle.

For design, the conservative and simple choice is Et. Careful column tests gave strengths between the tangent-modulus and reduced-modulus values, usually close to the tangent value. Shanley’s load-path argument explained why: buckling starts at the tangent-modulus load and the extra strength appears only as bending grows.

If the stress-strain curve is known, read Et at the stress you expect, then solve Pcr = π²Et I/Le² by trial, because Et depends on the stress. Handbooks give Et columns for aluminium and steel.

Johnson's parabola

Johnson's parabolic formula is a closed-form fit for intermediate columns. It uses only σy and E, so it is easy for hand calculations.

σcr = σy − (σy² / (4π²E)) λ²

σcr is the buckling stress (N/mm²); σy is the yield strength (N/mm²); E is Young's modulus (N/mm²); λ = Le/r is the slenderness ratio (dimensionless).

It is valid up to λJ = √(2π²E/σy). At that point σcr = σy/2. The parabola and Euler's hyperbola meet there with the same value, so the two curves join smoothly. Above λJ, use Euler.

For very short members, another failure comes first. A thin-walled angle or a flange buckles locally, or the cross-section cripples, at a stress below the column formula. Those are local-buckling and crippling checks made separately; do not use Johnson blindly for a short thin-walled strut.

Worked example

Question. An aluminium alloy strut has E = 70 GPa, σy = 320 MPa, A = 300 mm² and r = 5 mm. It is pinned at both ends with L = 0.20 m. Find the buckling stress and load, and compare with Euler.

  4. Since λ = 40.0 < 65.7, the column is inelastic. Euler is invalid.

  • Quoting Kármán's Er as the modulus that starts the buckle. Shanley's result is that Et starts it; Er is an upper bound.

3.5 Beam-columns

A beam-column is a member that carries axial compression and bending at the same time. The axial load acts on the already deflected shape, so it adds a moment of its own. The bending moment is therefore larger than the moment from the transverse load alone. Design uses an amplified moment, not the first-order one. Fuselage stringers under pressurisation bending and upper wing stringers, compressed by wing bending in flight and bent by the air load on their skin, are everyday aircraft examples. The same idea explains why a slender strut with a small side load can fail well below its Euler load.

You need beam deflection from Unit 2, the Euler load from Euler buckling of columns and end conditions, and the amplification idea from imperfections and eccentric loading.

Axial load on a deflected shape

A beam-column carries axial compression P and bending at the same time. The bending may come from a transverse load, from end moments, or from an eccentric line of action. If P were zero, the member would be an ordinary beam and the bending moment would be M(x), the first-order moment. With P present, the member deflects by v(x), and the axial force acts along a line that has moved sideways by v. The axial force therefore adds a moment P v at every section. That extra moment is not small near the Euler load.

EI d²v/dx² + P v = −M(x)

E is Young's modulus, I is the second moment of area about the bending axis, v is the sideways deflection, x is distance along the member, P is axial compression, and M(x) is the first-order bending moment from transverse loads or end moments. The term P v is the extra moment caused by the axial load acting on the deflected shape.

The term P v couples axial load to deflection. You cannot find v from the transverse load alone and then add stresses, because v itself depends on P. The bending moment used for stress must include P v. A fuselage stringer under pressurisation bending also carries axial load from fuselage bending; an upper wing stringer is compressed by the upward bending of the wing in flight and bent by the air pressure on its skin panel. Both are beam-columns. A strut with an eccentric end load is the same problem, which is why imperfections and eccentric loading uses amplification.

Amplification for a simply supported member

Take a simply supported member of length L with axial compression P and a central transverse load W. The first-order mid-span moment is M0 = W L/4. The Euler load of the same member is PE = π²EI/L². Suppose first that the first-order moment is itself sine-shaped, M(x) = M0 sin(πx/L), as under a sine-distributed side load. Try v = vm sin(πx/L): substitution into the beam-column equation gives vm = M0/(PE − P) exactly. The total mid-span moment is M0 plus P vm.

Mmax = M0 + P M0/(PE − P) = M0/(1 − P/PE)

Mmax is the amplified mid-span moment, M0 is the first-order moment, P is axial compression, and PE is the Euler load of the same member. The factor A = 1/(1 − P/PE) is the moment amplification.

At P = 0, A = 1. At P = 0.5PE, A = 2. As P tends to PE, A grows without limit. The figure plots A against P/PE; the worked example sits at P/PE = 0.603, where A = 2.52. The formula is exact for a sinusoidal first-order moment. For a central point load the exact factor is tan u/u with u = (π/2)√(P/PE): 1.82 at P = 0.5PE against 2.0 from 1/(1 − P/PE). The simple factor is about 10 % high there, so it is a safe estimate; an off-centre load or unequal end moments need a fuller analysis.

For equal end moments M0 at both ends, the exact elastic result is the secant form Mmax = M0 sec((π/2)√(P/PE)), where sec θ = 1/cos θ. This is the same secant that appears in eccentric struts. Use it when the first-order moment is constant along the member. For unequal end moments, a more detailed calculation or second-order computer analysis is needed.

Interaction check in design

The amplified moment is used inside a combined axial and bending check. A simple straight-line interaction is P/Pcr + M/Mu ≤ 1. P is axial compression, Pcr is the compression capacity of the member (squash load or buckling load with the chosen factor), M is the amplified moment, and Mu is the moment capacity about the bending axis. If the sum is below 1, the section is accepted by this check. Codes may use different exponents, but the principle is the same: the moment in the second term is the amplified one, not M0.

  1. Compute PE with the correct effective length.
  2. Compute M0 from the transverse load or end moments.
  3. Find the amplification A = 1/(1 − P/PE).
  4. Set M = A M0 and use M in the interaction check.

For small P/PE, A is close to 1, and the axial load changes little. For P/PE above about 0.5, A is above 2, so a first-order check can be badly unconservative. On a fuselage, a stringer takes axial stress from overall fuselage bending and local bending from pressurisation; the interaction catches the combined effect. On a wing, an upper-surface stringer compressed by wing bending also bends under the local air load; the same check applies. Local buckling of the panel is a separate plate-buckling check.

3.6 Failure theories: maximum principal stress and strain

A loaded point carries six stress numbers. A failure theory turns them into one number for comparison with a tension test. The maximum principal stress theory compares the largest normal stress with strength. The maximum principal strain theory compares the largest normal strain with yield strain. Both are simple, poor for ductile metals, reasonable for brittle castings.

You need principal stresses and Mohr's circle, and Hooke's law (Unit 1).

Principal stresses and principal strains

A point carries σx, σy, σz, τxy, τyz, τzx in MPa. Rotate axes until shear vanishes. The remaining normal stresses are principal stresses, σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ σ3 algebraically. Principal strains ε1, ε2, ε3 lie along them, ε1 along σ1.

ε1 = (σ1 − νσ2 − νσ3)/E

ε1 is principal strain along σ1, dimensionless; stresses in MPa; E is Young's modulus in MPa; ν is Poisson's ratio, about 0.30 for aluminium alloy and 0.33 for steel.

Cycling subscripts gives ε2 and ε3. For plane stress σ3 = 0: ε1 = (σ1 − νσ2)/E, ε2 = (σ2 − νσ1)/E, ε3 = −ν(σ1 + σ2)/E. The third strain is not zero when the third stress is.

Rankine and St Venant

The maximum principal stress theory (Rankine) fails when the largest tensile principal stress reaches the tensile strength: yield strength σYt for a ductile metal, ultimate strength σut for a brittle casting. It also fails when the most compressive stress falls to −σYc, the compressive strength. With one strength σY in both directions, |σ1| ≤ σY and |σ3| ≤ σY.

The maximum principal strain theory (St Venant) fails when max(|ε1|, |ε2|, |ε3|) reaches σY/E, the yield strain in tension. Multiply by E: σeq = E·max|ε| = max|σ1 − νσ2 − νσ3|, where σeq is equivalent stress in MPa. Safe while σeq ≤ σY. In plane stress σeq = max(|σ1 − νσ2|, |σ2 − νσ1|, ν|σ1 + σ2|).

In the σ1-σ2 plane Rankine's safe region is a square of side 2σY. St Venant's is a rhombus: edges σ1 − νσ2 = ±σY and σ2 − νσ1 = ±σY, long along σ1 = σ2, short along σ1 = −σ2. The third strain adds edges at σ1 + σ2 = ±σY/ν, far away for ν = 0.3.

Both ignore shear as a cause. Under pure shear τ, principal stresses are τ and −τ. Rankine predicts failure at τ = σY, St Venant at σY/(1 + ν) ≈ 0.77σY, but a ductile metal yields near 0.58σY. Both are unsafe for ductile metals; see maximum shear stress and distortion energy theories. Rankine suits brittle castings; the strain rule survives for cast parts and some composites, where allowable strain is design data.

  • Comparing a signed stress with σY. σ3 = −150 MPa passes a 250 MPa tensile check but fails a 120 MPa compressive allowable.
  • Taking σ1 as the stress of largest magnitude. σ1 is algebraically largest; σ3 is most compressive, and its strain may be largest in magnitude.
  • Reading ε1E as a stress in the part. It is a number on the yield-strength scale, not a stress for Mohr's circle.

3.7 Maximum shear stress, distortion energy and octahedral theories

A ductile metal in a wing fitting sees two or three normal stresses plus shear. It yields when the shear, or the distortion energy, reaches the value measured in a plain tension test. Two criteria bracket the answer: maximum shear stress (Tresca), the conservative one, and distortion energy (von Mises), the usual design choice. Octahedral shear is von Mises in another dress. Stress engineers in design offices use these to turn a multiaxial state into one equivalent stress that is compared with the yield strength σY.

You need failure theories: maximum principal stress and strain for principal stresses and strains.

Tresca and von Mises

Tresca says yield starts when the largest shear anywhere reaches σY/2. In principal stresses σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ σ3:

σeq,T = max(|σ1 − σ2|, |σ2 − σ3|, |σ3 − σ1|) ≤ σY

σ1, σ2, σ3 are principal stresses in MPa; σY is the tensile yield strength in MPa.

In plane stress one principal stress is zero. If the other two have opposite signs, σeq,T is their difference. If they have the same sign, it is the larger magnitude alone. Taking the difference in both cases is the commonest error.

von Mises says yield starts when distortion energy — the strain energy that changes shape, not volume — reaches its tension-test value. Hydrostatic pressure changes volume only and cannot yield a ductile metal. In principal stresses:

σeq,M = √[(σ1 − σ2)² + (σ2 − σ3)² + (σ3 − σ1)²] / √2

σ1, σ2, σ3 are principal stresses in MPa; the result is in MPa.

For plane stress with σ3 = 0 this becomes σeq,M = √(σ1² − σ1σ2 + σ2²). In stress components:

σeq,M = √(σx² − σxσy + σy² + 3τxy²)

σx, σy are normal stresses and τxy is shear stress, all in MPa.

The 3 on τxy² is not optional.

Octahedral shear and comparison

The octahedral plane has a normal making equal angles with the three principal axes. Its shear stress is

τoct = (1/3)√[(σ1 − σ2)² + (σ2 − σ3)² + (σ3 − σ1)²]

τoct is the octahedral shear stress in MPa; σ1, σ2, σ3 are principal stresses in MPa.

Compare the square roots: σeq,M = (3/√2)τoct. So yielding when τoct = √2 σY/3 is exactly von Mises, not a third independent theory. The octahedral shear carries the whole distortion; the mean stress (σ1+σ2+σ3)/3 is the normal stress on the octahedral plane; it changes volume only and leaves τoct unchanged.

Tresca gives the larger equivalent stress, so it is conservative. The von Mises value is √(3/2) times the root-mean-square of the three principal differences, so it still cannot exceed the largest difference. Use Tresca for a quick hand check, von Mises for design and finite element work.

Worked example

Question. A ductile panel has σx = 100 MPa, σy = −40 MPa, τxy = 30 MPa and yield strength σY = 250 MPa. Compare Tresca, von Mises, octahedral shear and maximum principal stress; give each factor of safety.

3.8 Fatigue: S-N curve and endurance limit

Fatigue is failure under a stress that repeats. One application of 500 MPa may be safe; repeated about twenty thousand times, the same stress can start a crack and grow it until the part breaks. The S-N curve records this: stress amplitude S against cycles to failure N. Designers use it for wings, landing gear and engine mounts, where loads reverse in every flight.

You need: the safe steady stress idea from maximum shear stress, distortion energy and octahedral theories. Mean stress is handled in mean and alternating stresses; stress ratio.

The S-N curve

Fatigue has two stages: crack initiation at a notch or surface defect, then crack growth under repeated stress. High-cycle fatigue means many cycles, usually above 10⁴, with stress mostly elastic. Low-cycle fatigue means few cycles, below about 10⁴, with local plastic strain.

A rotating-bending test bends a polished specimen while it spins. Each turn gives one complete stress cycle from tension to compression and back. The test repeats at a fixed stress amplitude S and records the cycles N until the specimen breaks. Plotting S against N gives the S-N curve, also called the Wöhler curve. This basic curve is for a fully reversed cycle, mean stress zero (stress ratio R = −1), which is what rotating bending gives; a mean stress shifts it.

S = A Nb

S is stress amplitude in MPa; N is cycles to failure; A is the stress at N = 1 in MPa; b is the fatigue exponent, negative for a falling line.

N is plotted on a log scale because life runs from thousands to millions of cycles. On log₁₀ N against log S the high-cycle part is straight. The figure uses 600 MPa at 10³ cycles and 400 MPa at 10⁶ cycles.

Endurance limit and Basquin's law

The endurance limit, or fatigue limit, Se, is the stress amplitude below which the material survives indefinitely. For many steels it appears as a horizontal line after about 10⁶ to 10⁷ cycles. Below Se no failure occurs; just above it, failure still occurs after a long life.

Aluminium and most non-ferrous alloys have no true endurance limit. Their S-N curve keeps falling. Design quotes them at a fixed life, often 5×10⁸ cycles, called the fatigue strength at that life. Do not assume a flat line for an aluminium wing skin.

Basquin's law fits the sloping part. Taking logarithms gives log S = log A + b log N. Two test points (N₁, S₁) and (N₂, S₂) give b = (log S₂ − log S₁)/(log N₂ − log N₁). Then log A = log S₁ − b log N₁. Use the same logarithm base throughout.

Real parts lose strength through surface finish, size, notches, corrosion and mean stress: a rough surface starts cracks earlier, a large section has more weak spots, a notch concentrates stress, corrosion pits act as cracks, and tensile mean stress raises the effective amplitude. See mean and alternating stresses; stress ratio.

Worked example

Question. A steel has ultimate strength 800 MPa. Rotating-bending tests give 600 MPa at 10³ cycles and 400 MPa at 10⁶ cycles; beyond 10⁶ cycles the strength stays 400 MPa. Fit Basquin's law S = A Nb and find the life at S = 500 MPa.

  1. Use points (N₁, S₁) = (10³, 600 MPa) and (N₂, S₂) = (10⁶, 400 MPa).
  2. log 600 = log A + 3b and log 400 = log A + 6b.
  3. Subtract: b = (2.602 − 2.778)/3 = −0.0587.
  4. log A = 2.778 + 3(0.0587) = 2.954, so A = 900 MPa.
  5. At S = 500 MPa: log(500/900) = −0.0587 log N.
  6. log N = 0.2553/0.0587 = 4.35.
  7. N = 104.35 = 2.23×10⁴ cycles ≈ 22,300 cycles.
  8. The tests give the endurance limit directly: 400 MPa, the flat part beyond 10⁶ cycles. It happens to be half the ultimate strength here, but it is a measured value, not a rule.

Answer. Basquin's law is S = 900 N−0.0587 with S in MPa; at 500 MPa the life is about 2.23×10⁴ cycles (22,300 cycles).

3.9 Mean and alternating stresses; stress ratio

A real aircraft part rarely sees a steady stress: a gust, a landing or a pressurisation cycle pushes it up and down. Every fluctuating stress splits into a steady mean part and a to-and-fro alternating part. The stress ratio fixes the shape of the cycle. A constant-life diagram then folds both parts into a single factor of safety against fatigue.

You need: fatigue: S-N curve and endurance limit for the endurance limit and the S-N curve, and failure theories: maximum principal stress and strain for combining stresses at a point.

Splitting a cycle in two

One fatigue cycle runs between a maximum stress σmax and a minimum stress σmin, both in MPa, tension positive. Split it about its midpoint.

σm = (σmax + σmin)/2    σa = (σmax − σmin)/2

σm is the mean stress, the steady part (MPa); σa is the alternating stress, the half-swing about the mean (MPa); the range is σr = σmax − σmin = 2σa.

The stress ratio R = σmin/σmax fixes the shape of the cycle. The amplitude ratio A = σa/σm carries the same information, A = (1 − R)/(1 + R); it is infinite at R = −1, where σm = 0. The value of R tells you which cycle you have.

  • R = −1: fully reversed, σm = 0 and σmax = −σmin — the rotating-bending test of fatigue: S-N curve and endurance limit.
  • R = 0: zero to maximum (pulsating tension), σmin = 0, so σm = σa = σmax/2.
  • R = 1: steady load, σa = 0; fatigue does not arise.

The constant-life (Haigh) diagram

Plot σm across and σa up. Each point is one cycle. For a chosen life a curve separates safe cycles from failing ones. For infinite life it meets the vertical axis at the endurance limit Se, where σm = 0; for a finite life it meets it at the fatigue strength for that life. It meets the horizontal axis at a static strength, where σa = 0. Only the part with σa ≥ 0 has meaning.

Goodman: σa/Se + σm/Su = 1;  Gerber: σa/Se + (σm/Su)² = 1;  Soderberg: σa/Se + σm/Sy = 1

Se is the endurance limit (MPa), Su the ultimate tensile strength (MPa), Sy the yield strength (MPa).

Goodman is a straight line from Se to Su and the usual conservative choice. Gerber is a parabola through the same two points and follows ductile test data better. Soderberg runs from Se to Sy and is the most conservative. The Langer line σa + σm = Sy is plain first-cycle yielding: when the peak stress σmax = σa + σm reaches Sy, the part yields at once, whatever its fatigue life.

The factor of safety n scales both stresses until the load point touches the curve. Put nσm and nσa into the line: Goodman gives 1/n = σa/Se + σm/Su, Soderberg gives 1/n = σa/Se + σm/Sy. Gerber gives a quadratic in n; take the positive root.

Worked example

Question. A steel aircraft fitting sees σmax = 300 MPa and σmin = −100 MPa. Take Se = 400 MPa, Su = 800 MPa and Sy = 600 MPa. Find σm, σa, R and A, the factor of safety by Goodman, Soderberg and Gerber, and check first-cycle yielding.

  1. σm = (σmax + σmin)/2 = (300 + (−100))/2 = 100 MPa.
  2. σa = (σmax − σmin)/2 = (300 − (−100))/2 = 200 MPa.
  3. R = σmin/σmax = −100/300 = −0.33. A = σa/σm = 200/100 = 2.0.
  4. Goodman: 1/n = σa/Se + σm/Su = 200/400 + 100/800 = 0.5 + 0.125 = 0.625, so n = 1.60.
  5. Soderberg: 1/n = σa/Se + σm/Sy = 200/400 + 100/600 = 0.5 + 0.167 = 0.667, so n = 1.50.
  6. Gerber: 0.5n + (0.125n)² = 1, that is 0.0156n² + 0.5n − 1 = 0. The positive root is n = (−0.5 + √(0.25 + 0.0625))/0.03125 = 1.89.
  7. Yield check: σa + σm = 200 + 100 = 300 MPa, below Sy = 600 MPa, so the first cycle does not yield.

Answer. σm = 100 MPa, σa = 200 MPa, R = −0.33, A = 2.0; n = 1.60 by Goodman, 1.50 by Soderberg and 1.89 by Gerber, and the fitting does not yield on the first cycle.

Exam traps

  • Dividing Se by σa while a tensile mean stress acts. The mean part also spends life; use σa/Se + σm/Su. The shortcut n = Se/σa holds only at R = −1.
  • Writing R = σmax/σmin. It is R = σmin/σmax, and compression keeps its sign: −100/300 = −0.33, not +0.33.
  • Putting Su where Sy belongs. The Goodman line ends at Su, Soderberg at Sy, and the Langer line sits at Sy.
  • Checking the fatigue line only. A large mean stress yields the part on cycle one, so always test σa + σm against Sy.
  • Forgetting compressive yield. With a compressive mean stress the most compressive stress, σmin = σm − σa, can yield the part on the first cycle, so check |σmin| against the compressive yield strength too.

Points to cover. Model answer.

    b = (log 400 − log 600)/(6 − 3) = (2.602 − 2.778)/3 = −0.0587

A member carrying axial compression together with bending moment.
Buckling
Sudden lateral deflection of a slender member under axial compression when the load reaches a critical value.
Column
A structural member loaded mainly along its axis in compression.
Critical load
The load at which a perfect column first becomes unstable in a chosen buckling mode.
Eccentric load
An axial load whose line of action does not pass through the centroid of the section.
Effective length
The length of an equivalent pin-ended column that buckles at the same load as the real column.
Endurance limit
The fully reversed stress amplitude below which a steel survives indefinitely; aluminium alloys have none and are quoted at a fixed life.
Fatigue
Failure by repeated or fluctuating stress at levels below the static yield strength.
Goodman diagram
A constant-life diagram plotting alternating stress against mean stress with a straight failure line.
Mean stress
The average of maximum and minimum stress in a fatigue cycle.
Moment amplification
The increase of bending moment in a beam-column caused by axial compression acting on lateral deflection.
Octahedral shear stress
Shear stress on the octahedral plane; it is proportional to the von Mises equivalent stress.
Principal strain
A normal strain along a principal direction, where shear strain is zero.
Principal stress
A normal stress on a plane where shear stress is zero.
Proportional limit
The highest stress for which stress remains proportional to strain.
Radius of gyration
A section property r = √(I/A) that measures how far the area is spread from the centroid.
Rankine-Gordon formula
An empirical formula that combines crushing load and Euler load for intermediate columns.
S-N curve
A graph of stress amplitude against number of cycles to failure.
Secant formula
A formula for maximum stress in an eccentrically loaded column, containing sec((π/2)√(P/PE)).
Slenderness ratio
The ratio Le/r, used to classify a column as short, intermediate or long.
Stress ratio
R = σmin/σmax, a measure of how a fatigue cycle is biased.
Tangent modulus
The local slope of the stress-strain curve after the proportional limit.
Tresca criterion
A yield criterion based on maximum shear stress reaching the value at yield in tension.
von Mises criterion
A yield criterion based on distortion energy reaching the value at yield in tension.
Yield criterion
A rule that predicts the stress state at which a material begins to yield.
