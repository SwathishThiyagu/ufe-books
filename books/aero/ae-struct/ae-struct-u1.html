1.1 Structural components of aircraft and their functions

Every part of an aircraft exists to carry a load from where it is applied to where it can be reacted. Wing skin collects air pressure, hands it to stringers, stringers hand it to ribs, ribs hand it to spars, spars hand it to the wing root fittings, the fittings hand it to the fuselage, and the fuselage balances it at its frames and bulkheads. Read the airframe this way — as a chain of load paths — and the long list of member names becomes a short list of jobs. Stress engineers, designers and maintenance crews all work from this map.

You need: force, pressure and equilibrium from school physics, nothing more.

The five groups and what each is for

The airframe is everything in the aircraft that carries load: skin, skeleton and joints. It does not include engine internals, avionics or fuel plumbing. It is easier to learn as five groups.

The wing makes lift and passes it inboard to the fuselage. The fuselage carries the payload — passengers and cargo — takes the reactions from wing and tail, and holds the cabin pressure. The empennage, or tail unit, has the fin, which makes side force, and the tailplane, which makes pitch balance. The landing gear takes ground reaction, braking and side loads. Engine mounts and nacelles take thrust and engine weight.

A load path is the route a force takes through the structure. Every member collects a load from a thinner, more flexible neighbour and hands it to a stiffer, stronger one, until the force is balanced at a joint, a bulkhead or the wing root. Nothing in an airframe carries load for its own sake.

Members and their one-line jobs

Inside the groups sit the named members. Some syllabi call the skin the covering, or the stressed skin.

  • Skin: the outer surface. Keeps the aerodynamic shape and, in semi-monocoque construction, carries pressure, shear and part of the bending.
  • Stringers: spanwise stiffeners under the wing skin. They support the skin between ribs and take axial load with it.
  • Longerons: heavy longitudinal fuselage members. With the skin they carry fuselage bending.
  • Ribs: chordwise wing members. They hold the aerofoil shape, support the stringers and pass load to the spars.
  • Frames: rings inside the fuselage. They keep the cross-section shape, support the skin and take floor and cabin loads.
  • Bulkheads: solid partitions. A pressure bulkhead closes the cabin at the nose and the aft end and takes the whole cabin pressure.
  • Spars: the main spanwise wing beams. The caps (top and bottom flanges) take bending; the thin web takes shear.
  • Doublers: extra plates around a cut-out, door or joint. They spread load around the opening so the thin skin is not overstressed.
  • Fittings: compact machined or forged parts that join one member to another — wing root to fuselage, spar to rib, gear to wing.
  • Floor beams: transverse beams that carry the cabin floor and its loads to the frames.

Skin to stringer to rib to spar to fuselage

  1. Air pressure acts normally on a skin panel of area A. The skin bends between its supports and carries the pressure as a distributed load.
  2. The skin passes the load to the stringers, which run spanwise beneath it.
  3. The stringers pass it to the ribs, which run chordwise across the wing.
  4. The ribs pass it to the front and rear spars.
  5. The spar caps take it as bending — compression in the top cap, tension in the bottom cap — and the spar web takes it as shear, all the way to the root.
  6. Root fittings pass it into fuselage frames and bulkheads.
  7. The fuselage carries it as a beam: skin and longerons in bending, frames holding the cross-section and stopping the skin from buckling, floor beams feeding cabin loads into the frames.

P = p × A Ps = P / n

p is the normal pressure on the panel in N/m² (Pa); A is the panel area in m²; P is the total load on the panel in N; n is the number of stringers sharing it; Ps is the load per stringer in N.

Doublers and fittings belong to the chain too. A doubler around a door or an antenna cut-out spreads the same force over more skin, so the thin panel is not overstressed. Every rivet row, bonded lap and bolted fitting must pass that force with a margin, which is why fabrication of structural components decides real strength. The wing as a complete torsion box is worked out in typical wing and fuselage structures.

Worked example

Question. A wing skin panel is 0.6 m long spanwise and 0.4 m wide chordwise. The normal air pressure on it is 15 kPa. Four spanwise stringers share the panel load equally. Find the total load on the panel and the load carried by each stringer.

  1. Pressure p = 15 kPa = 15 000 N/m², because 1 Pa = 1 N/m².
  2. Panel area A = 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.24 m².
  3. Panel load P = p × A = 15 000 × 0.24 = 3 600 N.
  4. Load per stringer Ps = P / n = 3 600 / 4 = 900 N.
  5. Check: 4 × 900 = 3 600 N, which matches the panel load.

Answer. The panel carries 3 600 N in total, and each of the four stringers carries 900 N.

Worked example 2

Question. A tailplane skin panel is 0.5 m long spanwise and 0.25 m wide chordwise. The normal air pressure on it is 12 kPa. Five spanwise stringers share the panel load equally. Find the total load on the panel and the load carried by each stringer.

  1. Pressure p = 12 kPa = 12 000 N/m², because 1 Pa = 1 N/m².
  2. Panel area A = 0.5 × 0.25 = 0.125 m².
  3. Panel load P = p × A = 12 000 × 0.125 = 1 500 N.
  4. Load per stringer Ps = P / n = 1 500 / 5 = 300 N.
  5. Check: 5 × 300 = 1 500 N, which matches the panel load.

Answer. The panel carries 1 500 N in total, and each of the five stringers carries 300 N.

Exam traps

  • Ribs and spars swapped. Ribs run chordwise and keep the aerofoil shape, passing load to the spars; spars run spanwise and take the bending.
  • Treating the skin as shape only. In semi-monocoque construction the skin is a primary load path and carries pressure, shear and part of the bending.
  • Frames and longerons swapped. Frames are rings that hold the cross-section; longerons are longitudinal members that take bending.
  • Calling every ring a bulkhead. A bulkhead is a solid partition, and a pressure bulkhead carries the whole cabin pressure.
  • Pressure unit slip. 15 kPa is 15 000 Pa, not 15 Pa; a wrong conversion makes the answer 1000 times too small.

1.2 Monocoque and semi-monocoque construction

A fuselage or wing must carry loads and keep its shape. Three classic arrangements do this: a truss of bars covered with fabric, a monocoque where the skin alone carries everything, and a semi-monocoque where the skin works with a skeleton. The semi-monocoque wins for modern aircraft because it lets the skin stay thin while stringers and longerons take bending. Only a few universities examine this as a separate topic, but the names appear in every structural description.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for the names of the members, and the idea of a load path.

Three construction types

A truss fuselage is a frame of metal tubes with diagonal wires and fabric covering. The tubes carry the flight loads; the fabric mainly keeps the air out. The truss is light for small aircraft but poor for pressurisation, because a pressure cabin needs a sealed shell, not a fabric bag.

A monocoque shell is different. The skin alone carries bending, shear, torsion and pressure, with no stringer-and-frame skeleton. A pure monocoque is simple but heavy. A thin unstiffened shell buckles at low compression, so the skin must be thick enough to resist buckling. Thick skin adds weight.

A semi-monocoque shell puts a skeleton back under a thin skin. Skin, stringers, longerons and frames share the load. This is the usual construction for modern transport fuselages and many wings. Typical wing and fuselage arrangements are in Chapter 1.3.

Load sharing and pressure

In a semi-monocoque fuselage the skin is a thin sheet of aluminium alloy or composite. It carries shear, torsion and all the cabin pressure. Stringers are small longitudinal members riveted to the skin. They stiffen the skin and share the bending. Longerons are the few large longitudinal members that carry most of the bending, especially near doors and cut-outs. Frames are rings around the fuselage. They hold the cross-section in shape and shorten the skin panels so each panel can resist buckling.

Pressure is the clearest load. Treat a pressurised fuselage as a thin cylinder. Let p be the cabin differential pressure in Pa, r the radius in m and t the skin thickness in m. Buckling of the stiffened panels is left to Unit 6, Plates, Buckling and Composite Structures.

σh = pr/t

σh is hoop stress in Pa; p is differential pressure in Pa; r is radius in m; t is skin thickness in m.

σl = pr/(2t)

σl is longitudinal stress in Pa; the symbols are the same.

The hoop stress is twice the longitudinal stress. Hoop stress comes from pressure pushing sideways on a split cylinder. Longitudinal stress comes from pressure on the end caps.

Worked example

Question. A semi-monocoque fuselage has a circular cross-section of diameter 2.0 m and skin thickness 1.5 mm. The cabin differential pressure is 60 kPa. Find the hoop and longitudinal stresses in the skin.

  1. Diameter D = 2.0 m, so radius r = D/2 = 1.0 m. Thickness t = 1.5 mm = 0.0015 m. Pressure p = 60 kPa = 60 000 Pa.
  2. Hoop stress: σh = pr/t = (60 000 × 1.0) / 0.0015 = 40 000 000 Pa = 40 MPa.
  3. Longitudinal stress: σl = pr/(2t) = σh/2 = 20 000 000 Pa = 20 MPa.

Answer. The hoop stress is 40 MPa and the longitudinal stress is 20 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Do not say the stringers carry the cabin pressure. The skin is the pressure membrane; stringers only stiffen it.
  • Do not swap hoop and longitudinal stress. Hoop stress pr/t is twice longitudinal pr/2t.
  • Do not use diameter in place of radius. For a 2.0 m fuselage, r = 1.0 m.
  • Do not leave kPa or mm in the formula. Convert 60 kPa to 60 000 Pa and 1.5 mm to 0.0015 m.
  • Do not call any stiffened shell a monocoque. A pure monocoque has no stringer-and-frame skeleton carrying bending.

1.3 Typical wing and fuselage structures

A wing carries lift with a thin box: skin panels, stringers, ribs and two spars. The box takes bending as compression in the top skin and tension in the bottom skin, and takes torsion as shear in the closed cell between the spars. A fuselage is a stiffened tube: frames and longerons hold a thin skin in shape and carry bending, shear and cabin pressure. Learn the members, their spacing, and how a load travels from skin to root joint. Every stress calculation later (Unit 4, Unit 5) assumes this layout.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for the load-path idea, and Chapter 1.2 for why the skin needs a skeleton behind it.

Two spars make a torsion box

A spar is a spanwise beam running from root to tip. Most wings have a front spar near the leading edge and a rear spar further back. Each spar has a thin vertical web and two spar caps: spanwise flanges at the top and bottom of the web. Bending bends the wing up, so the top skin and top caps shorten and the bottom skin and bottom caps stretch. The caps take this axial load. The webs take shear.

The skin between the front and rear spar, together with the two webs, closes a cell called the torsion box. A closed cell resists twisting far better than an open sheet, because the shear has a continuous path all round the loop. The box is placed at the deepest part of the aerofoil, where the material is farthest from the twist centre and so works best.

w_box = (x_rear − x_front) × c

w_box is the torsion box width in m; x_front and x_rear are the front and rear spar positions as a fraction of chord (0 to 1); c is the chord in m.

Wing bending, shear and torsion distributions are worked out in Chapter 1.9. Shear flow inside this closed box is Unit 5, Shear Flow, Shear Centre and Torsion.

Ribs and stringers

A rib is a chordwise member: it runs from the leading edge to the trailing edge, crossing the box. Ribs hold the aerofoil section, support the skin and stringers, and pass panel loads into the spars. They also carry local loads in: engine mounts, landing gear, flap and aileron hinges all feed the wing through a few ribs. Ribs sit at a regular pitch, for example 0.45 m on a small wing.

A stringer is a small spanwise stiffener on the inside face of the skin, closely spaced. Stringers stop the thin skin from buckling, and together with the skin they act as the upper and lower flanges in bending.

  1. Aerodynamic pressure on the top skin loads that panel.
  2. The panel passes the load to the stringers running spanwise.
  3. Stringers hand the load to the ribs at each crossing.
  4. Ribs carry it chordwise to the front and rear spar web.
  5. Spars carry it spanwise to the root joint, which hands it to the fuselage frames.

The leading edge and trailing edge are lighter structures behind and ahead of the spars. They keep the profile shape and carry little of the wing bending.

Fuselage: a stiffened tube

The fuselage is a semi-monocoque shell: skin, frames, longerons and bulkheads. Each has one job.

  • Frames are transverse rings, set at a regular pitch along the cabin. They keep the cross-section round, support the skin against buckling, and carry the hoop load from cabin pressure together with the skin.
  • Longerons are lengthwise members, fewer and heavier than stringers. They run along the fuselage and take the bending, with the skin acting as the flange.
  • The skin carries shear from the fuselage bending and carries cabin pressure as hoop tension.
  • Floor beams run across the cabin between frames and carry the floor and the passengers and cargo on it.
  • Pressure bulkheads close the pressurised cabin at the front and the rear. A pressurised fuselage is a pressure vessel, so both ends of the cabin must be sealed by a dome-shaped bulkhead.

A fuselage is also a beam: the tail unit loads the rear fuselage in bending and torsion, and the wing loads the centre section. Fuselage stresses are treated in Chapter 1.10.

Cut-outs and keeping the shape

Doors, windows, access panels and wheel wells cut skin out of the shell. A cut-out removes the material that was carrying the load, so the load must divert around the opening. The usual fix is a doubler: an extra plate riveted or bonded round the hole, thick enough to take the load the missing skin used to take. Corners of cut-outs are given a radius, because a sharp corner raises stress.

A wheel well is worse than a door, because it opens into the lower skin of the wing box itself, the part carrying tension in flight. Designers keep such holes as small as the mechanism allows, put them where the bending is low, and add heavy local reinforcement.

Shape is not cosmetic. The aerofoil section produces the lift and keeps drag low, so ribs and frames must hold it under load. Dents, wrinkles and bulges spoil the airflow and also lower the buckling strength of the panels, which is why handling damage near frames and stringers matters.

Worked example

Question. A wing has a chord of 3.0 m along a spanwise length of 4.5 m from the root. The front spar lies at 15% chord and the rear spar at 60% chord. Ribs are placed at a pitch of 0.45 m. Find the width of the torsion box and the number of ribs.

  1. Front spar position: x_front × c = 0.15 × 3.0 = 0.45 m from the leading edge.
  2. Rear spar position: x_rear × c = 0.60 × 3.0 = 1.80 m from the leading edge.
  3. Box width: w_box = 1.80 − 0.45 = 1.35 m, which is the same as (0.60 − 0.15) × 3.0 = 1.35 m.
  4. Number of bays between ribs: 4.5 m ÷ 0.45 m = 10 bays.
  5. Ribs: one rib closes each bay, plus one at the root, so 10 + 1 = 11 ribs.
  6. The torsion box is 1.35 m wide at every section where the chord is 3.0 m, and 11 ribs close 10 bays of 0.45 m each.

Answer. Torsion box width = 1.35 m; number of ribs = 11.

Exam traps

  • Counting bays as ribs: 10 bays need 11 ribs, because each end of a bay needs a rib.
  • Forgetting that a door, window or wheel well removes load-carrying skin and must be reinforced with a doubler.
  • Mixing up chord fractions with metres: 0.15 is a fraction of chord, 0.45 m is a length.
  • Swapping longeron and frame: longerons run lengthwise and take bending, frames are transverse rings that hold shape and pressure.
  • Thinking the skin alone bends the wing: the stringers and spar caps share the axial load.

1.4 Honeycomb and sandwich structures

A sandwich panel is two thin stiff sheets, the faces, glued to a thick light core. The faces carry tension and compression like the flanges of an I-beam; the core carries shear and holds the faces apart. Spacing the faces out multiplies the bending stiffness while adding little weight. Aircraft use sandwich panels for floor panels, control surfaces such as ailerons and rudders, radomes and helicopter rotor blades. This chapter shows why the arrangement is stiff, what the core looks like, how the panel is glued, and how it fails.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for the idea of a load path, Chapter 1.3 for the skin-stringer panels that sandwich panels partly replace, and simple bending stress and second moment of area from Unit 4, Bending of Thin-Walled Beams.

Two faces far apart

Bend a solid sheet of thickness t and its bending stiffness per unit width is D = E t³/12. Doubling t makes it eight times stiffer and twice as heavy. Most material in a solid sheet sits near the neutral axis and does little work.

A sandwich panel moves material away from the neutral axis. Two faces of thickness t_f are bonded to a core, and their mid-planes are a distance d apart. Per unit width each face has area t_f and sits at d/2 from the neutral axis, so I = 2 × t_f × (d/2)² = t_f d²/2.

D = E_f t_f d² / 2

D is the bending stiffness per unit width, in N·m; E_f is Young's modulus of the face material, in Pa; t_f is the face thickness, in m; d is the distance between the face mid-planes, in m, roughly the core thickness plus one face thickness.

Each face also bends about its own mid-plane, adding E_f t_f³/12, but t_f is a fraction of a millimetre while d is tens of millimetres, so that term is dropped.

The faces act like I-beam flanges, one in compression and one in tension, a couple separated by d. The core acts like the web, carrying the transverse shear that comes with bending, and keeps the faces flat so the compressed face cannot buckle early.

Cores and the glue line

The core is the thick, light middle. Three families cover most aircraft work.

  • Hexagonal honeycomb. Thin foil or paper is formed into half-hexagons, stacked and glued at the node lines, then pulled open into cells. Aluminium honeycomb is stiff and cheap. Nomex honeycomb, aramid paper coated with phenolic resin, resists fire and moisture. Panels are much stiffer along the ribbon direction, the direction the foil runs, so drawings must state the orientation.
  • Foam. Closed-cell PVC and polyurethane foams are cheap; polymethacrylimide foam costs much more but is stiffer and tolerates higher cure temperatures. All work about equally in both in-plane directions and machine easily. For the same weight they carry less shear than honeycomb.
  • Balsa. End-grain wood, still used in some rotor blades and boat hulls.

The adhesive matters as much as the core. A film adhesive is laid between face and core and cured under heat and pressure, in an autoclave, a press or a vacuum bag. The glue line must be continuous. A void, a greasy patch or trapped moisture is a crack waiting to run.

How a sandwich panel fails

A sandwich panel is stiff and strong but not tough. The designer checks each failure mode; the weakest sets the allowable load.

  • Face yielding. The face stress reaches the yield stress of the face material. Rarely the critical mode.
  • Face wrinkling. The compressed face buckles into the core over a short wavelength set by the face bending stiffness and the core stiffness, often only a few times the face thickness. Soft cores allow this well below yield.
  • Core shear. The core carries the shear force, mainly through cell walls aligned with the shear. Its shear strength is low, so shear often governs short, deep panels.
  • Core crushing. A local load, a jack, a bolt or a foot, crushes the cells. Honeycomb is weak in this through-thickness direction.
  • Debonding. The face lifts off the core. The faces then work alone, stiffness collapses and failure can be sudden. Bad surface preparation, moisture or a poor cure causes it.

Bolts are a special problem: a bolt through a sandwich crushes the core, so holes are potted with a solid insert before drilling. Chapter 1.2 showed a solid skin carrying bending; a sandwich does the same job with the material pushed to the outside.

Worked example

Question. An aircraft floor panel has two aluminium faces, each 0.5 mm thick with E = 70 GPa, bonded to a 20 mm honeycomb core. (a) Find the bending stiffness D per metre width. (b) A solid aluminium sheet of the same mass per unit area, with the core weight neglected, is 1.0 mm thick. Find its D and the ratio of the two stiffnesses.

  1. Convert to metres: t_f = 0.5 mm = 0.5 × 10⁻³ m; core thickness = 20 mm = 0.020 m; E_f = 70 GPa = 70 × 10⁹ Pa.
  2. Distance between face mid-planes: d = 0.020 + 0.0005 = 0.0205 m.
  3. Square it: d² = 0.0205² = 4.2025 × 10⁻⁴ m².
  4. Compute E_f t_f = 70 × 10⁹ × 0.5 × 10⁻³ = 3.5 × 10⁷ N/m.
  5. Multiply: 3.5 × 10⁷ × 4.2025 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.4709 × 10⁴ N·m.
  6. Halve it: D = 7.354 × 10³ N·m = 7.35 kN·m per metre width.

Answer. The sandwich panel has D = 7.35 kN·m per metre width against 5.83 N·m for the equal-mass solid sheet, so the sandwich is about 1261 times stiffer in bending.

Exam traps

  • Quoting only the faces' own bending stiffness, 2 × E_f t_f³/12, and forgetting the d² term. That d² term is what the whole idea is about.
  • Using the core thickness as d and forgetting to add one face thickness. With 20 mm core and 0.5 mm faces, d is 20.5 mm, not 20 mm.
  • Checking face stress only. Core shear and debonding are common critical modes, and a panel that passes the face check can still fail in service.
  • Treating honeycomb as isotropic. Stiffness and shear strength differ strongly between the ribbon direction and across it, so orientation must be specified.
  • Mixing units: putting t_f in millimetres into a formula where E is in Pa and d is in metres. The answer comes out a thousand times wrong.

1.5 Aerospace materials and their behaviour

An aircraft material is never chosen on strength alone. The choice trades strength, stiffness, weight, fatigue life, temperature limit, corrosion and cost. A wing needs stiffness so it does not flex too much; a landing gear needs strength and toughness; an engine fitting needs heat resistance. Engineers compare materials using specific strength σult/ρ and specific stiffness E/ρ, where σult is ultimate tensile stress in Pa (N/m²), E is Young's modulus in Pa and ρ is density in kg/m³. These ratios let a light material compete with a strong heavy one. The same trade decides whether a part is aluminium, titanium, steel or carbon fibre.

You need: Chapter 1.4 for why light stiff faces matter.

Specific strength and specific stiffness

Specific strength is ultimate tensile stress divided by density: σult/ρ. Specific stiffness is E/ρ. Units: (N/m²)/(kg/m³) = N·m/kg, also written J/kg or kN·m/kg. A high value means more load carried per kilogram of material. Use ultimate stress for strength comparisons. Use yield stress only when the design limits permanent deformation; mixing the two is a common error. Table 1 gives typical room-temperature values. Composites are quoted along the fibre, because across the fibre they are much weaker.

Aluminium, magnesium, titanium and steel

Aluminium is the default for wings and fuselage because it is light, stiff enough, cheap and easy to form. 2024 has copper and good fatigue resistance. It is used for tension skins but needs cladding or paint because it corrodes. 7075 uses zinc and has high strength. It is used in compression skins and fittings but has lower fatigue resistance and is sensitive to stress corrosion in the T6 temper. 6061 is weaker but welds well and resists corrosion, so it suits frames and secondary structure. Magnesium alloys are lighter than aluminium (ρ ≈ 1740 kg/m³) but have lower modulus (E ≈ 45 GPa), poor corrosion resistance and fire risk. Use is limited to small castings and older airframes. Titanium Ti-6Al-4V has density 4430 kg/m³, E ≈ 114 GPa and ultimate about 950 MPa. It keeps useful strength to roughly 300–400°C; special near-alpha titanium alloys reach about 500–550°C. It appears in engine parts, exhaust areas and highly loaded fittings. High-strength steels such as 300M have density 7850 kg/m³ and ultimate near 1950 MPa. They are used for landing gear and other compact, highly loaded parts where volume is tight and the weight penalty is accepted.

Composites, fatigue, corrosion and repair

CFRP is carbon fibre in a polymer resin. Fibres carry load; resin holds shape and transfers shear. Along the fibre, CFRP has density about 1600 kg/m³, E 70–150 GPa and tensile strength 1200–1800 MPa. It is very stiff and strong per kilogram, but compressive strength is lower, impact damage can be hidden, and the resin limits temperature to about 120–180°C. GFRP uses glass fibre. It is cheaper, less stiff (E 20–40 GPa), and transparent to radar, so it is used for radomes and fairings. Fatigue: aluminium alloys have no true endurance limit. Their S-N curve keeps falling as cycles increase, so designers use a fatigue strength at a chosen life (for example 10⁶ cycles) and design for damage tolerance. Steels do show a genuine endurance limit. 2024 is chosen for fatigue-critical tension skins because of its slow crack growth; 7075 is stronger but less forgiving. Composites do not have a single endurance limit; they accumulate matrix cracks and delamination, so design uses strain limits and damage tolerance. See Unit 3 for fatigue detail. Corrosion: carbon fibre is cathodic to aluminium. A CFRP part bolted to aluminium makes the aluminium the anode and accelerates galvanic attack. Isolate with glass fibre plies, sealant or coatings. 7075-T6 is prone to stress corrosion in the short transverse direction; 2024 needs surface protection. Temperature: aluminium loses strength above about 120–150°C; titanium and steel go higher. Repair: aluminium panels are riveted or bolted; composites need bonded patches, controlled cure and inspection. See Chapter 1.6 for fabrication and joining. Layup and laminate stiffness are in Unit 6; here only the material choice matters.

Exam traps

  • Quoting yield strength where ultimate strength is meant. Specific strength uses σult unless the design is explicitly limited by yield.
  • Comparing materials on strength alone. A strong heavy steel can lose to aluminium or CFRP once density, fatigue and temperature are included.

  • Treating CFRP as isotropic. Its fibre-direction strength is high, but transverse and compressive strengths are much lower.
  • Bolting carbon fibre directly to aluminium without isolation. Carbon is cathodic; the aluminium becomes the anode and corrodes fast.

1.6 Fabrication of structural components

Aircraft parts are made by forming sheet, machining or extruding metal, or laying up composite. They are then joined by rivets, bolts, adhesive or welding. The joint, not the plate, usually decides the strength, because holes cut the net section and load must pass from one part to another. Only a few universities teach fabrication as a full topic. The ideas matter for every spar, rib, frame and skin panel.

You need Chapter 1.5 for material allowables and Chapter 1.3 for which members are joined.

Forming the part

Sheet metal is formed by pressing or stretching it over a die. Stretch forming pulls a clamped sheet over a curved die; the skin stretches plastically and keeps the die shape. Machining cuts a spar or fitting from a solid billet when the shape is thick and few parts are needed. Extrusion pushes hot metal through a die to make long constant-section stringers. Composite parts are laid up ply by ply on a mould and cured in an autoclave under heat and pressure.

The route is: raw sheet or billet, form or machine, surface treatment, jig assembly, joining, inspection. Surface treatment means anodising aluminium or priming it before assembly; the bare metal corrodes and the adhesive needs a clean, prepared surface. A jig holds the parts in their true shape while holes are drilled and fasteners are placed. Inspection checks hole size, fastener grip and the finished shape, and repairs are made before the part leaves the shop.

Making the joint

Rivets are the standard aircraft fastener. A rivet fills its hole, and the driven head clamps the sheets. Bolts carry larger loads and can be removed. Adhesive bonding spreads load over a wide area and avoids holes, but needs careful surface preparation. Seam welding joins thin sheets along a line.

Joints are lap or butt. In a lap joint the load path is eccentric: the sheets are offset, so the joint also feels bending. A butt joint with splice plates is more symmetric. A group of fasteners shares load; the outer rows usually take more, not equal share. Check three things: rivet shear, bearing on the sheet, and net-section tension with holes removed. Holes also start fatigue cracks; an interference-fit bolt squeezes the hole and slows crack growth. Sealant keeps moisture out.

1.7 Loads on aircraft and design considerations

An aircraft in service meets lift, weight, thrust, drag, gusts, landings, ground handling and cabin pressure. To size a member, engineers turn the worst service load into a limit load, then multiply by 1.5 to get the ultimate load. The load factor n = L/W tells how many times the weight the lift reaches. Every wing, fuselage frame and landing gear part is checked against its own critical load case.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for load paths and Chapter 1.3 for which member carries bending, shear and pressure.

Sources of load and the load factor

An aircraft carries four main forces: lift L, weight W, thrust T and drag D. In steady level flight L equals W and T equals D. In a pull-up, a gust or a landing, one force can be much larger than its steady value.

The load factor n = L / W is dimensionless. L is lift in N and W is weight in N. At n = 1 the lift equals weight; at n = 2.5 the lift is 2.5 times weight. A push-over can give a negative n.

Limit load is the largest load expected in normal service. It is not the failure load. For a wing in symmetric flight, limit lift = n W. For a fuselage, limit load may be cabin pressure plus bending from the tail. For landing gear, it is the vertical impact and spin-up load.

  • Flight loads: lift, weight and inertia, thrust and drag, plus aileron, elevator and rudder loads.
  • Gust loads: a vertical gust changes the angle of attack and raises lift sharply.
  • Landing loads: vertical impact, wheel spin-up, braking and side load.
  • Ground handling: taxi bumps, turning, jacking and towing.
  • Cabin pressure: internal pressure times fuselage area, repeated every flight.
  • Tail loads: the horizontal tail often carries a down-load to balance the wing.

Inertia relief and the net section load

Lift is not the only load on a wing section. The mass of the aircraft also pushes down with inertia. For a section of the wing, the net load is the aerodynamic load on the outboard part minus the inertia of the masses outboard of that section. The root sees the sum of these net loads. Using lift alone overestimates the bending moment. This is inertia relief.

The same idea applies to the fuselage. The tail load bends the rear fuselage, but the mass of the tail and rear fuselage opposes the motion. A heavy engine on a wing relieves root bending in flight because its inertia acts downward; on landing, the same engine adds to the load. So the critical case for a member depends on mass distribution.

Chapter 1.9 integrates this net load to get shear force and bending moment. For now, remember: the internal load on a section is the net load, not the aerodynamic load alone.

Ultimate load, factor of safety and design criteria

Design uses a factor of safety of 1.5 on the limit load. Ultimate load = 1.5 × limit load. The structure must carry the ultimate load without failure. At limit load it may deflect but must not suffer harmful permanent deformation. The factor covers small variations in load, material and analysis, not gross errors.

P_ult = 1.5 × P_lim

P_ult is ultimate load in N; P_lim is limit load in N; 1.5 is the aircraft factor of safety, dimensionless.

A critical load case is the combination of loads that gives the highest stress in a particular member. A wing spar may be critical in a gust at high speed; a fuselage frame may be critical under pressure plus bending; landing gear is critical at touchdown. Each member is sized for its own critical case.

  • Safe-life: the part is given a fixed life and retired before cracks are expected. Used for landing gear and some helicopter parts.
  • Fail-safe: the structure has multiple load paths or crack stoppers, so one failed member does not cause collapse.
  • Damage tolerance: the structure is designed so cracks grow slowly and are found by inspection before they become dangerous. Used for modern transport wings and fuselages.

Worked example

Question. An aircraft of mass 60 000 kg is designed for a manoeuvre load factor of 2.5. Take g = 9.81 m/s². Find the limit lift and the ultimate load for the wing. State the factor of safety used.

  1. Weight W = m g = 60 000 × 9.81 = 588 600 N = 588.6 kN.
  2. Limit lift L_lim = n W = 2.5 × 588.6 = 1 471.5 kN = 1.4715 MN ≈ 1.47 MN.
  3. The aircraft factor of safety is 1.5.
  4. Ultimate load P_ult = 1.5 × 1.4715 = 2.20725 MN ≈ 2.21 MN.

Answer. Limit lift = 1.47 MN; ultimate load = 2.21 MN.

Exam traps

  • Applying the 1.5 factor of safety twice, for example to an ultimate load that is already factored.
  • Using the full aerodynamic lift as the root bending load and forgetting inertia relief.
  • Writing kN and MN in the same line without converting: 588.6 kN = 0.5886 MN.
  • Calling the limit load the failure load; the structure must not fail at ultimate, which is 1.5 times limit.
  • Confusing load factor n with factor of safety 1.5; n is a service load ratio, 1.5 is a design multiplier.

1.8 V-n diagram and its use in wing sizing

A V-n diagram is a graph of load factor n against airspeed V for one aircraft. It shows the largest n the wing can produce before it stalls at each speed, and the largest n the structure is allowed to carry. The corners where these limits meet define the critical cases used to size the wing, fuselage and tail. Designers use it to choose manoeuvre speed, dive speed and gust limits, and to check that no combination of speed and load escapes the envelope.

You need limit load and load factor from Chapter 1.7.

The stall boundary

Load factor n is lift divided by weight, n = L/W. At a given speed V the wing can make lift only up to its stall limit. Let C_Lmax be the maximum lift coefficient in the configuration used for the diagram. The one-g stall speed V_S1 is the speed at which level flight needs the whole C_Lmax. From L = ½ ρ V² S C_L, with L = W at n = 1:

V_S1 = √(2(W/S) / (ρ C_Lmax))

W/S is wing loading in N/m²; ρ is air density in kg/m³; C_Lmax is dimensionless.

At a higher speed the same wing can pull more load factor before it stalls. Put L = nW in the lift equation and divide by the one-g case. The ρ, S and C_Lmax cancel:

n = (V / V_S1)²

n is load factor (dimensionless); V is true airspeed in m/s; V_S1 is the one-g stall speed in m/s.

This is the stall line. It is a parabola, not a straight line. At V = V_S1 it gives n = 1; at 2V_S1 it gives n = 4. The wing cannot go above this line at that speed, because it stalls.

Limit load factor, V_A, V_C, V_D and gusts

The structure has a positive limit load factor n_max, often 2.5 for transport aircraft, and a negative limit n_min, often −1.0. These are limit loads; the factor of safety is applied later. The positive stall line meets n_max at the manoeuvre speed V_A:

V_A = V_S1 √(n_max)

V_A is manoeuvre speed in m/s; V_S1 is one-g stall speed in m/s; n_max is positive limit load factor.

Between V_A and the design cruising speed V_C the top of the envelope is flat at n_max. V_C is the normal cruise design speed. V_D is the design dive speed, the highest speed the aircraft may reach. From V_C to V_D the limit falls from n_max to a lower value, 2.0 for transport aircraft, and then the envelope closes steeply at V_D. The lower half is mirrored with n_min.

Gusts add a second boundary. A sharp vertical gust of speed U changes the angle of attack by U/V. The lift curve slope a (per radian) converts this to extra lift. The gust load factor is:

n = 1 ± (ρ V a U) / (2 W/S)

ρ is air density in kg/m³; V is airspeed in m/s; a is lift curve slope per radian; U is gust velocity in m/s; W/S is wing loading in N/m². Use + for an upward gust and − for a downward gust.

This is the sharp-edged gust value. Certification work multiplies the increment by a gust alleviation factor K_g (below 1, from the aircraft mass ratio and wing loading), so the design gust line is shallower than this formula gives.

At low speed the gust line lies above the stall line, so those gust load factors cannot be reached — the wing stalls first and the stall line governs. The gust line crosses below the stall line at a speed V_B, the speed where the two lines meet. Above V_B the gust line lies inside the stall boundary. At high speed the gust increment grows with V, so the gust line can rise above n_max at a speed below V_C. That crossing matters: the structure may feel a gust load larger than the manoeuvre limit even though the pilot is not pulling hard.

Reading the critical corner and sizing

Superimpose the manoeuvre envelope and the gust lines. The design must cover the highest n at every speed inside the flight envelope. The critical corner is usually one of two points: the stall-line corner (V_A, n_max), or the point where a gust line crosses n_max at high speed.

The corner fixes two numbers for sizing: the load factor and the speed at which it acts. Wing bending at the root is driven by the spanwise lift, so a larger n means a larger bending moment. The chordwise and torsion loads also grow with speed. The fuselage feels the same n through the wing attachments and the tail; the tail load depends on how the pilot trims the aircraft at that corner.

Wing sizing uses the corner to set spar cap area, skin thickness and fastener loads. If the gust line is critical, the wing is sized for a load the pilot never commands. If the stall corner is critical, V_A and n_max size it. Take the bending moment from that load case to Chapter 1.9 for the spanwise distribution.

Worked example

Question. A wing has wing loading W/S = 3000 N/m² and C_Lmax = 1.5. Air density is 1.225 kg/m³. The positive limit load factor is n_max = 2.5. Find the one-g stall speed V_S1 and the manoeuvre speed V_A. State the corner that sizes wing bending.

  1. Given: W/S = 3000 N/m², C_Lmax = 1.5, ρ = 1.225 kg/m³, n_max = 2.5.
  2. At n = 1: W/S = ½ ρ V_S1² C_Lmax. Rearrange: V_S1 = √(2(W/S)/(ρ C_Lmax)).
  3. 2(W/S) = 2 × 3000 = 6000 N/m². ρ C_Lmax = 1.225 × 1.5 = 1.8375.
  4. V_S1 = √(6000 / 1.8375) = √3265.3 = 57.14 m/s.
  5. V_A = V_S1 √n_max = 57.14 × √2.5 = 57.14 × 1.581 = 90.35 m/s ≈ 90.4 m/s.
  6. Above 90.4 m/s the stall line gives n below 2.5, so the wing stalls before reaching the limit. The critical corner is (V_A, n_max) = (90.4 m/s, 2.5).

Answer. V_S1 = 57.1 m/s and V_A = 90.4 m/s; the corner (90.4 m/s, 2.5) sizes wing bending.

Exam traps

  • Drawing the stall line as a straight line from the origin to V_A. It is n = (V/V_S1)², a parabola; a straight line under-predicts n at mid speeds.
  • Forgetting the negative-g half. Downward gusts and pushovers load the wing and tail in the opposite sense; the negative limit n_min must be checked.
  • Ignoring gust lines at high speed. A gust line can sit above the manoeuvre top, so the critical load may be a gust the pilot never commands.
  • Mixing true and equivalent airspeed. The stall line uses one definition of V throughout; ρ must match that definition.
  • Rounding V_S1 too early. 57.14 × √2.5 = 90.35 m/s; using 57.1 gives 90.3 m/s. Keep full precision until the final answer.

1.9 Shear force and bending moment distribution on wings

A wing behaves like a cantilever built into the fuselage. Lift pushes it up; wing structure and fuel push it down. The difference is the net load per unit span. Integrating that net load from the tip inward gives the shear force; integrating again gives the bending moment. The bending moment is zero at the free tip and always largest at the root. The shear is also zero at the tip and usually largest at the root, but a heavy concentrated mass on the wing makes it jump at that station. These two diagrams are the first numbers a stress engineer needs, because they show where the wing is worked hardest and what the fuselage carry-through and tail loads must react.

You need: loads on aircraft for the load factor and the V-n diagram for the design cases.

Wing as a cantilever

Take the root at x = 0 and the tip at x = L. The semi-span L is in metres. The net load w(x) is in N/m and is positive upward. It is lift minus wing structure weight minus fuel weight per metre. A positive w means the net load bends the wing upward.

V(x) = ∫xL w(ξ) dξ

V is the shear force in N at station x; w is the net load per unit span in N/m, positive upward; x is distance from the root in m; L is the semi-span in m; ξ is a dummy span coordinate in m.

M(x) = ∫xL w(ξ)(ξ − x) dξ

M is the bending moment in N·m at station x; V, w, x, L and ξ are as above. Use one sign convention throughout: upward net load positive, root at x = 0.

The part of the wing outboard of x carries all the net load beyond x. V(x) is the total of that outboard load. M(x) is that same load multiplied by its lever arm (ξ − x) about the station x. At the free tip there is no outboard load, so V(L) = 0 and M(L) = 0.

For a uniform net load w, V = w(L − x) and M = w(L − x)²/2. At the root V = wL and M = wL²/2. The moment rises with the square of the remaining span, so near the root it grows much faster than shear. That is why the root fitting and the carry-through structure are sized mainly by moment.

A concentrated mass changes the shear picture. An engine, a store or a landing gear on the wing adds a large downward load at one station. The shear diagram jumps downward there, so the root shear need not be the largest shear value on the span. The bending moment is not affected in the same way: it is still zero at the tip and still peaks at the root.

Lift distribution and Schrenk

Real lift is not uniform along the span. It depends on planform, twist and the trailing vortices. A first estimate is still useful before detailed aerodynamic analysis. It gives the spanwise load shape needed for the shear and moment integrals.

An elliptic lift distribution has minimum induced drag for a given span and a given total lift. A rectangular wing tends toward an elliptic shape; a tapered wing carries more lift near the root because the chord is larger there. Schrenk's approximation takes the mean of the elliptic distribution and the planform distribution at each station.

wlift(x) ≈ ½ [ well(x) + wplan(x) ]

wlift is the estimated lift per unit span in N/m; well is the elliptic distribution in N/m; wplan is the planform distribution in N/m, proportional to chord c(x); the constant of proportionality is chosen so the total lift equals the required lift.

Taper moves load inboard. A tapered wing of the same area and total lift has a higher root bending moment than a rectangular wing, because more lift acts near the root. Use Schrenk's shape for preliminary loads, then replace it with wind-tunnel or CFD data when available. The integration method does not change.

Mass relief, carry-through and tail loads

Wing structure and fuel are not lift. Subtract them before integrating. This is mass relief: it lowers the root bending moment. Fuel in the wing gives strong relief when tanks are full, but the critical case may be low fuel, so check every fuel load in the design envelope, from empty tanks to full tanks. A full tank is not automatically the worst case.

The wing root moment is reacted by the carry-through structure and the fuselage frames at the attachment points. The carry-through is the structure that carries the wing root moment across the fuselage. The fuselage behaves as a beam: it receives a concentrated moment and shear at the wing junction, so its bending moment diagram has a jump there.

The tail produces a lift or downforce at a long arm from the centre of gravity. That tail load bends the rear fuselage. The bending moment is zero at the point where the load is applied and grows linearly forward along the fuselage, so it is largest at the wing attachment, where it equals T × l. The carry-through then reacts it. Here T is the tail load in N and l is the distance in m from the wing attachment to the line of action of the tail load. See stress analysis of fuselage and tail unit for how the cross-section carries it.

Worked example

Question. A wing of semi-span 8 m carries a uniform lift of 12 kN/m and has wing structure and fuel of 3 kN/m along the same span. Find the shear force and bending moment at the root and at 2 m from the root.

  1. Net load per unit span: w = 12 − 3 = 9 kN/m.
  2. Semi-span: L = 8 m. At the root x = 0, the outboard length is L − x = 8 m.
  3. Root shear: V = w(L − x) = 9 × 8 = 72 kN.
  4. Root moment: M = w(L − x)²/2 = 9 × 8²/2 = 9 × 64/2 = 288 kN·m.
  5. At x = 2 m from the root, the outboard length is L − x = 6 m.
  6. Shear at 2 m: V = 9 × 6 = 54 kN.
  7. Moment at 2 m: M = 9 × 6²/2 = 9 × 36/2 = 162 kN·m.

Answer. At the root: shear 72 kN and bending moment 288 kN·m. At 2 m from the root: shear 54 kN and bending moment 162 kN·m.

Exam traps

  • Using lift alone and forgetting to subtract wing structure and fuel. The root moment comes out too high because mass relief is missing.
  • Mixing sign conventions. With upward load positive and x from the root, the moment gradient is negative; choose one convention and keep the diagram consistent.
  • Applying M = wL²/2 to a tapered lift distribution. That formula is only for uniform w; integrate the actual w(x).
  • Forgetting the fuselage carry-through. The wing root moment does not disappear at the fuselage; it is reacted by the carry-through and frames.
  • Assuming full fuel is always critical. Fuel relief changes with fuel load; check the fuel cases that your design cases require.

1.10 Stress analysis of fuselage and tail unit

A fuselage in flight is a thin-walled tube doing two jobs at once: it holds cabin pressure and it bends like a beam. The tail unit hangs off the rear end and feeds bending and twist into that beam. Stress analysis asks one question at every point: what stresses meet there, and do they pass the material's failure check? The same arithmetic sizes a window surround, a frame at a wing joint, and a fin root fitting. It is used in every structural repair and every mass-saving change on an aircraft.

You need: bending moment and shear ideas from Chapter 1.9, and the roles of skin, frames and longerons from Chapter 1.3.

The fuselage as a pressure tube and a beam

Cabin pressure p is a differential pressure: inside absolute pressure minus outside ambient pressure. It pushes the skin outward in all directions. On a thin circular cylinder of mean radius r and thickness t, the skin carries two membrane stresses at right angles. Hoop stress acts around the circumference: σ_h = p r / t. Longitudinal stress acts along the axis: σ_l = p r / (2t). p is in Pa, r and t in m, stresses in Pa. Hoop stress is twice longitudinal stress, so a fatigue crack usually runs along the axis, where hoop stress tries to open it.

σ_h = p r / t σ_l = p r / (2t)

σ_h is hoop stress in Pa; σ_l is longitudinal stress in Pa; p is cabin differential pressure in Pa; r is mean skin radius in m; t is skin thickness in m.

The same tube is a beam. Bending moment M at a section produces axial stress σ_b = M y / I. M is in N·m, y is the distance from the neutral axis to the point in m, and I is second moment of area in m⁴. For a thin circular tube of mean radius r and thickness t, I ≈ π r³ t. For the thin skin the outer fibre is at y = r, so y is taken as the mean radius in the section check. Longerons and stringers add area far from the neutral axis, so the real I comes from the section idealisation, not the skin alone. The skin also carries shear between frames and longerons; shear flow is Unit 5. See Chapter 1.1 for load paths and Chapter 1.9 for how M varies.

Combining stresses and local load paths

At a point on the skin, hoop stress and longitudinal stress act on perpendicular faces. They do not add like two pulls on the same rope. The correct check combines them. For a ductile aluminium alloy, use von Mises: σ_vm = √(σ_l,total² − σ_l,total σ_h + σ_h²). The total longitudinal stress is the pressure part plus the bending part at that point: σ_l,total = p r/(2t) ± M y/I. The sign depends on whether bending puts that side in tension or compression. Compare σ_vm with the design allowable; for compression, check buckling separately in Unit 6.

σ_vm = √(σ_l,total² − σ_l,total σ_h + σ_h²)

σ_vm is von Mises equivalent stress in Pa; σ_l,total is the longitudinal stress including pressure and bending in Pa; σ_h is hoop stress in Pa.

Frames are rings that hold the shape and take concentrated loads: floor beams, wing attachment, landing gear, door stops. A frame spreads a local push into ring shear and bending, so the skin is not asked to carry a point load alone. A pressure bulkhead closes the pressurised cabin and turns pressure into edge shear on the shell; a domed bulkhead carries pressure mainly in membrane action, while a flat bulkhead bends. A cut-out such as a window or door removes skin area and breaks the hoop load path. It needs a doubler or thickened frame around it to carry the lost load; the corner needs a smooth radius because a sharp corner raises stress.

Tail unit loads into the rear fuselage

The tailplane carries a balancing load, often downward in steady level flight, and a manoeuvre load that can reverse direction. Treat the tailplane as a cantilever: its load times the distance to the fuselage gives a bending moment on the rear fuselage. That moment is largest at the tailplane root and falls toward the wing. The fin carries a side load from rudder deflection or sideslip. The fin bends as a cantilever and also twists about its span axis; the root joint must take both bending and torque. The fin spar and the fuselage frames that pick it up are sized by these root reactions.

The rear fuselage is therefore a beam under combined bending from tailplane and fin, plus cabin pressure if the pressure cabin extends that far. Near the fin root, skin and frames also see concentrated fitting loads. A common exam question gives a tailplane load and an arm; the answer is a bending moment at a station, then a stress using the fuselage section properties. Torsion of the closed section is covered in Unit 5; here keep to bending stress and joint reactions.

Worked example

Question. A fuselage has mean radius 1.2 m and skin thickness 1.6 mm. The cabin differential pressure is 55 kPa. At a section, the ultimate bending moment is 200 kN·m. The material is 2024 aluminium with yield about 345 MPa. Find the hoop stress, longitudinal pressure stress, second moment of area of the thin skin tube, bending stress at the outer fibre, total longitudinal stress on the tension side, and the von Mises stress there. Comment on the margin.

  1. Convert units: p = 55 kPa = 55 000 Pa; r = 1.2 m; t = 1.6 mm = 0.0016 m; M = 200 kN·m = 200 000 N·m.
  2. Hoop stress: σ_h = p r / t = 55 000 × 1.2 / 0.0016 = 41 250 000 Pa = 41.25 MPa.
  3. Longitudinal pressure stress: σ_l,pressure = p r / (2t) = 55 000 × 1.2 / (2 × 0.0016) = 20 625 000 Pa = 20.625 MPa.
  4. Second moment of area: I ≈ π r³ t = π × 1.2³ × 0.0016 = 8.686 × 10⁻³ m⁴.
  5. Bending stress: σ_b = M y / I = (200 000 × 1.2) / (8.686 × 10⁻³) = 27.63 × 10⁶ Pa = 27.63 MPa. Here y = r = 1.2 m at the outer fibre.
  6. Total longitudinal stress on the tension side: σ_l,total = 20.625 + 27.63 = 48.26 MPa.
  7. Von Mises check: σ_vm = √(48.26² − 48.26 × 41.25 + 41.25²) = 45.16 MPa.
  8. The ultimate-load von Mises stress of 45.2 MPa is well below the ultimate strength of about 470 MPa. The limit-load stress is about two-thirds of this, well below the 345 MPa yield. Fatigue and buckling still need separate checks.

Answer. The tension-side longitudinal stress is 48.3 MPa and the von Mises stress is 45.2 MPa at ultimate load; both pass the ultimate strength check, and the limit-load stresses pass yield.

Exam traps

  • Adding hoop stress straight into the longitudinal total. The two act at right angles and must be combined through von Mises or a principal stress check.
  • Using diameter instead of radius in p r / t. Hoop stress doubles if you slip in d where r belongs.
  • Using absolute cabin pressure instead of differential pressure. At 11 km altitude the outside pressure is only about 23 kPa, so a cabin held near 75 kPa absolute gives a differential of about 50–55 kPa — close to the full cabin pressure. At sea level the same cabin would give almost no differential at all.
  • Treating a thin fuselage tube as a solid circle. The thin-tube value is I ≈ π r³ t, not π r⁴ / 4.
  • Forgetting that bending changes sign around the section. One side is in tension, the other in compression, so the total longitudinal stress is different on each side.

Takeaways

  1. Every aircraft member is a load path: it collects load from a wider area and hands it to a stronger member until the load reaches a support.
  2. Monocoque skins must be thick to avoid buckling, so real aircraft are semi-monocoque, with stringers taking bending and thin skin taking shear.

  4. Sandwich panels give very high bending stiffness for low weight because the faces carry bending and the core carries shear.
  5. Material choice is a trade of strength, stiffness, density, fatigue, temperature, corrosion and cost, never a single property.
  6. Fabrication and joints decide real strength; a riveted or bonded joint, not the plate, is usually the weak point.
  7. Limit load is the worst service load, ultimate load is 1.5 times it, and load factor n = L/W converts flight loads to design loads.

Define spanwise load w(y) in N/m, span b, semi-span s; Shear force V(y) = integral of w from the tip to y; Bending moment M(y) = integral of V from the tip to y; Root values: total lift W = area under w(y); For elliptic loading, root moment is finite and computed by integration; Sketch of shear force and bending moment diagrams, zero at tips; State units: V in N, M in N·m.

Apply For a given set of design speeds and load factor limits, draw the manoeuvre V-n diagram and identify the corner points that must be used for wing sizing. 13 marks

Axes: speed V on x, load factor n on y; Stall boundary n = V²/(stall-related) curving from VA to the stall speed; Positive limit n at points A to D, negative limit below the axis; Design cruising and dive speeds VC and VD marked; Gust lines of increasing slope drawn from VC; Corner points: positive manoeuvre at VA and VD, negative manoeuvre, gust intersections; Each corner is a load case for wing bending and torsion.

Apply A sandwich panel with face thickness t and core thickness c is compared with a solid sheet of the same material and the same weight. Show why the sandwich is far stiffer in bending. 10 marks

Bending stiffness per unit width proportional to E·t·c²/2 for the sandwich (faces far from the neutral axis); Solid sheet stiffness proportional to E·t_solid³/12; Same weight means solid thickness equals the total face material; Faces carry direct tension and compression, core carries shear; Core must resist crushing and shear, faces must not wrinkle; Numerical ratio improves as c increases; Practical limit: core shear failure and face wrinkling before bending failure.

Apply A circular fuselage of radius r and skin thickness t is pressurised to a cabin differential pressure p and also carries a bending moment M. Compute the skin stresses and state which one drives the fatigue life. 13 marks

Hoop stress σ_h = p·r/t; Longitudinal pressure stress σ_l = p·r/(2t); Bending stress from σ = M·y/I, with I from the stiffened section; Superpose pressure and bending stresses at top and bottom of the section; Maximum compressive stress on the upper skin must be below buckling stress; Pressure stress cycles once per flight, so it drives fatigue and crack growth; Crack stoppers and doublers limit damage.

Apply Explain how a riveted lap joint in a fuselage skin carries load, and list the failure modes an engineer checks in its design. 10 marks

Load travels from one sheet into rivets by bearing and then into the second sheet; Rivet shear, sheet bearing, sheet net-section tension; Row-by-row load transfer; first row takes most load; Hole reduces net area and creates stress concentration; Fatigue cracking from hole edges under repeated pressurisation; Multiple rows, sealant and bonding (rivet-bond) reduce load per rivet; Check against ultimate load with factor 1.5.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare aluminium alloy, titanium alloy and carbon fibre composite for a wing upper skin. Justify a choice and state the trade-offs involved. 13 marks

Properties table: density, modulus, strength, fatigue, temperature, corrosion, cost; Specific strength and specific stiffness used for fair comparison; Aluminium: cheap, easy to form and rivet, good fatigue if designed, corrodes; Titanium: high temperature and corrosion resistance, high strength, costly and hard to machine; Composites: high specific stiffness, tailorable, no corrosion, poor at bearing and impact, need bonded or bolted joints, higher inspection cost; Choice depends on load case, temperature, environment and production volume; Sensible conclusion, not a single-property ranking.

Evaluate A semi-monocoque fuselage is cut for a cargo door. Analyse the effect on the structure, and evaluate the role of doublers, frames and crack stoppers in restoring strength. 13 marks

Cut-out removes skin area, so the load path is diverted; Stress concentration at corners; corner radii reduce it; Doublers replace lost skin area and spread load into the surrounding skin; Additional frames at the cut-out edges carry the diverted shear; Pressure loads need a pressure-tight door and a reinforced frame; Crack stoppers and tear straps limit damage growth from the opening; Comparison of strength with the uncut structure and fatigue check.

Evaluate Using the corners of the V-n diagram and a spanwise load distribution, size the wing root spar caps and evaluate how a change in limit load factor or aspect ratio alters the design. 16 marks

Obtain design load factor from the V-n diagram corners including gust cases; Lift = n·W, apply factor of safety 1.5 for ultimate load; Convert lift to spanwise load and integrate for root shear and bending moment; Root moment is shared between front and rear spar caps; Cap area from σ = M/(A·d) with d the distance between caps; Check compression cap for buckling and fatigue life at limit load; Raising n increases moment and cap area roughly in proportion; Higher aspect ratio at the same wing area raises root moment.

Analyze Describe how loads from the tail unit are transferred into the rear fuselage, and analyse why the rear fuselage is often critical for bending and fatigue. 10 marks

Tail plane lift and elevator load produce bending of the rear fuselage; Fin side load and rudder load produce torsion and lateral bending; These enter through fittings and a reinforced frame or bulkhead; Tail load acts at a long lever arm from the centre of gravity; Large bending moments occur in the rear fuselage with less material than the wing box; Fatigue from gust and manoeuvre cycles concentrates at joints and cut-outs; Stiffening by stringers and frames raises buckling strength.

Glossary

Buckling
Sudden sideways deflection of a thin member under compression at a load below its crushing strength.
Bulkhead
A transverse wall-like member that closes a section, carries concentrated load, or holds cabin pressure.
Doubler
An extra plate added around a cut-out or joint to restore the strength taken away.
Fail-safe design
Design in which a crack or failed member is stopped by other members until it is found in inspection.
Fatigue
Failure by repeated load cycles that start and grow a crack at a stress concentration.
Fitting
A local machined or forged component that connects two members and spreads a concentrated load.
Frame
A transverse ring in the fuselage that maintains the cross-section shape and supports stringers and skin.
Gust load
The extra lift created when the aircraft meets an upward or downward air current.
Honeycomb core
A core of hexagonal cells, made from foil or paper, whose walls carry shear between the faces.
Hoop stress
The circumferential tensile stress in a pressurised cylinder, equal to p·r/t.
Limit load
The maximum load expected in service, which the structure must carry without permanent damage.
Load factor
The ratio n = L/W of lift to weight, which measures how many times the aircraft feels heavier than its weight.
Load path
The continuous route by which a force travels from where it is applied to the support that reacts it.
Longeron
A heavy longitudinal fuselage member that acts as a main bending member.
Monocoque
Construction where the skin alone carries all loads, with no internal skeleton.
Neutral axis
The line in a beam section where bending stress is zero and about which the bending stresses balance.
Rib
A chordwise wing member that gives the aerofoil shape and transfers skin and stringer loads to the spars.
Sandwich structure
Two thin stiff faces bonded to a thick light core to obtain high bending stiffness at low weight.
Semi-monocoque
Construction with thin skin supported by frames, stringers and longerons so that each carries part of the load.
Spar cap
The upper or lower flange of a spar, farthest from the neutral axis, carrying the largest bending stress.
Stringer
A small longitudinal stiffener on the inside of the skin that takes axial stress and delays skin buckling.
Torsion box
A closed cell of spars and skin that resists twisting of a wing or tail surface.
Ultimate load
Limit load multiplied by the factor of safety, usually 1.5; the structure must not fail at this value.
V-n diagram
A graph of load factor against equivalent airspeed showing the flight envelope and the critical design cases.
