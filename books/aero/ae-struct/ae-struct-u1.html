1.1 Structural components of aircraft and their functions

Every part of an aircraft exists to carry a load from where it is applied to where it can be reacted. Wing skin collects air pressure, hands it to stringers, stringers hand it to ribs, ribs hand it to spars, spars hand it to the wing root fittings, the fittings hand it to the fuselage, and the fuselage balances it at its frames and bulkheads. Read the airframe this way — as a chain of load paths — and the long list of member names becomes a short list of jobs. Stress engineers, designers and maintenance crews all work from this map.

You need: force, pressure and equilibrium from school physics, nothing more.

The five groups and what each is for

The airframe is everything in the aircraft that carries load: skin, skeleton and joints. It does not include engine internals, avionics or fuel plumbing. It is easier to learn as five groups.

The wing makes lift and passes it inboard to the fuselage. The fuselage carries the payload — passengers and cargo — takes the reactions from wing and tail, and holds the cabin pressure. The empennage, or tail unit, has the fin, which makes side force, and the tailplane, which makes pitch balance. The landing gear takes ground reaction, braking and side loads. Engine mounts and nacelles take thrust and engine weight.

A load path is the route a force takes through the structure. Every member collects a load from a thinner, more flexible neighbour and hands it to a stiffer, stronger one, until the force is balanced at a joint, a bulkhead or the wing root. Nothing in an airframe carries load for its own sake.

Members and their one-line jobs

Inside the groups sit the named members. Some syllabi call the skin the covering, or the stressed skin.

  • Skin: the outer surface. Keeps the aerodynamic shape and, in semi-monocoque construction, carries pressure, shear and part of the bending.
  • Stringers: light stiffeners riveted to the inside of the skin, running spanwise in a wing and lengthwise in a fuselage. They support the skin between ribs or frames and take axial load with it.
  • Longerons: heavy longitudinal fuselage members. With the skin they carry fuselage bending.
  • Ribs: chordwise wing members. They hold the aerofoil shape, support the stringers and pass load to the spars.
  • Frames: rings inside the fuselage. They keep the cross-section shape, support the skin and take floor and cabin loads.
  • Bulkheads: solid partitions. A pressure bulkhead closes the cabin at the nose and the aft end and takes the whole cabin pressure.
  • Spars: the main spanwise wing beams. The caps (top and bottom flanges) take bending; the thin web takes shear.
  • Doublers: extra plates around a cut-out, door or joint. They spread load around the opening so the thin skin is not overstressed.
  • Fittings: compact machined or forged parts that join one member to another — wing root to fuselage, spar to rib, gear to wing.
  • Floor beams: transverse beams that carry the cabin floor and its loads to the frames.

Skin to stringer to rib to spar to fuselage

  1. Air pressure acts normally on a skin panel of area A. The skin bends between its supports and carries the pressure as a distributed load.
  2. The skin passes the load to the stringers, which run spanwise beneath it.
  3. The stringers pass it to the ribs, which run chordwise across the wing.
  4. The ribs pass it to the front and rear spars.
  5. The spar caps take it as bending — compression in the top cap, tension in the bottom cap — and the spar web takes it as shear, all the way to the root.
  6. Root fittings pass it into fuselage frames and bulkheads.
  7. The fuselage carries it as a beam: skin and longerons in bending, frames holding the cross-section and stopping the skin from buckling, floor beams feeding cabin loads into the frames.

P = p × A Ps = P / n

p is the normal pressure on the panel in N/m² (Pa); A is the panel area in m²; P is the total load on the panel in N; n is the number of stringers sharing it; Ps is the load per stringer in N.

Doublers and fittings belong to the chain too. A doubler around a door or an antenna cut-out spreads the same force over more skin, so the thin panel is not overstressed. Every rivet row, bonded lap and bolted fitting must pass that force with a margin, which is why fabrication of structural components decides real strength. The wing as a complete torsion box is worked out in typical wing and fuselage structures.

Worked example

Question. A wing skin panel is 0.6 m long spanwise and 0.4 m wide chordwise. The normal air pressure on it is 15 kPa. Four spanwise stringers share the panel load equally. Find the total load on the panel and the load carried by each stringer.

  1. Pressure p = 15 kPa = 15 000 N/m², because 1 Pa = 1 N/m².
  2. Panel area A = 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.24 m².
  3. Panel load P = p × A = 15 000 × 0.24 = 3 600 N.
  4. Load per stringer Ps = P / n = 3 600 / 4 = 900 N.
  5. Check: 4 × 900 = 3 600 N, which matches the panel load.

Answer. The panel carries 3 600 N in total, and each of the four stringers carries 900 N.

Worked example 2

Question. The front spar of a wing carries, at its root, a bending moment M = 288 kN·m and a shear force V = 72 kN (the root values found in Shear force and bending moment distribution on wings). The centroids of its top and bottom caps are h = 0.40 m apart and the web is 3 mm thick. Assume the caps carry all the bending and the web carries all the shear. Find the axial force in each cap, the cap area needed at an allowable stress of 400 MPa, and the average shear stress in the web.

  1. The caps act as a couple: a compressive force F in the top cap and an equal tensile force F in the bottom cap, h apart. Their moment F × h must equal M.
  2. Cap force F = M / h = 288 000 N·m ÷ 0.40 m = 720 000 N = 720 kN (compression in the top cap, tension in the bottom cap).
  3. Cap area A = F / σ = 720 000 N ÷ (400 × 10⁶ N/m²) = 1.8 × 10⁻³ m² = 1800 mm².
  4. Web shear stress τ = V / (h t) = 72 000 N ÷ (0.40 m × 0.003 m) = 72 000 ÷ 0.0012 = 60 × 10⁶ Pa = 60 MPa.
  5. Check: F × h = 720 kN × 0.40 m = 288 kN·m, the moment we started with.

Answer. Each cap carries 720 kN (top in compression, bottom in tension) and needs about 1800 mm² at 400 MPa; the web carries an average shear stress of 60 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Ribs and spars swapped. Ribs run chordwise and keep the aerofoil shape, passing load to the spars; spars run spanwise and take the bending.
  • Treating the skin as shape only. In semi-monocoque construction the skin is a primary load path and carries pressure, shear and part of the bending.
  • Frames and longerons swapped. Frames are rings that hold the cross-section; longerons are longitudinal members that take bending.
  • Calling every ring a bulkhead. A bulkhead is a solid partition, and a pressure bulkhead carries the whole cabin pressure.
  • Using the full spar depth or the skin-to-skin depth in F = M/h. Use the distance between the cap centroids; a larger h gives a cap force that is too small.
  • Pressure unit slip. 15 kPa is 15 000 Pa, not 15 Pa; a wrong conversion makes the answer 1000 times too small.

1.2 Monocoque and semi-monocoque construction

A fuselage or wing must carry loads and keep its shape. Three classic arrangements do this: a truss of bars covered with fabric, a monocoque where the skin alone carries everything, and a semi-monocoque where the skin works with a skeleton. The semi-monocoque wins for modern aircraft because it lets the skin stay thin while stringers and longerons take bending. Only a few universities examine this as a separate topic, but the names appear in every structural description.

You need: Structural components of aircraft and their functions for the names of the members, and the idea of a load path.

Three construction types

A truss fuselage is a frame of metal tubes with diagonal wires and fabric covering. The tubes carry the flight loads; the fabric mainly keeps the air out. The truss is light for small aircraft but poor for pressurisation, because a pressure cabin needs a sealed shell, not a fabric bag.

A monocoque shell is different. The skin alone carries bending, shear, torsion and pressure, with no stringer-and-frame skeleton. A pure monocoque is simple but heavy. A thin unstiffened shell buckles at low compression, so the skin must be thick enough to resist buckling. Thick skin adds weight.

A semi-monocoque shell puts a skeleton back under a thin skin. Skin, stringers, longerons and frames share the load. This is the usual construction for modern transport fuselages and many wings. Typical wing and fuselage arrangements are in Typical wing and fuselage structures.

Load sharing and pressure

In a semi-monocoque fuselage the skin is a thin sheet of aluminium alloy or composite. It carries shear, torsion and all the cabin pressure. Stringers are small longitudinal members riveted to the skin. They stiffen the skin and share the bending. Longerons are fewer, heavier longitudinal members that run the length of the fuselage and share the bending with the stringers and skin. Beside doors and other cut-outs, where stringers are cut, heavier longerons and edge members take the load round the opening. Frames are rings around the fuselage. They hold the cross-section in shape and shorten the skin panels so each panel can resist buckling.

Pressure is the clearest load. Treat a pressurised fuselage as a thin cylinder. Let p be the cabin differential pressure in Pa, r the radius in m and t the skin thickness in m. Buckling of the stiffened panels is treated later, with plate buckling and stiffened panels.

σh = pr/t

σh is hoop stress in Pa; p is differential pressure in Pa; r is radius in m; t is skin thickness in m.

σl = pr/(2t)

σl is longitudinal stress in Pa; the symbols are the same.

The hoop stress is twice the longitudinal stress. Hoop stress comes from pressure pushing sideways on a split cylinder. Longitudinal stress comes from pressure on the end caps.

Worked example

Question. A semi-monocoque fuselage has a circular cross-section of diameter 2.0 m and skin thickness 1.5 mm. The cabin differential pressure is 60 kPa. Find the hoop and longitudinal stresses in the skin.

  1. Diameter D = 2.0 m, so radius r = D/2 = 1.0 m. Thickness t = 1.5 mm = 0.0015 m. Pressure p = 60 kPa = 60 000 Pa.
  2. Hoop stress: σh = pr/t = (60 000 × 1.0) / 0.0015 = 40 000 000 Pa = 40 MPa.
  3. Longitudinal stress: σl = pr/(2t) = σh/2 = 20 000 000 Pa = 20 MPa.

Answer. The hoop stress is 40 MPa and the longitudinal stress is 20 MPa.

Exam traps

  • Do not say the stringers carry the cabin pressure. The skin is the pressure membrane; stringers only stiffen it.
  • Do not swap hoop and longitudinal stress. Hoop stress pr/t is twice longitudinal pr/2t.
  • Do not use diameter in place of radius. A fuselage 2.0 m in diameter has r = 1.0 m.
  • Do not leave kPa or mm in the formula. Convert 60 kPa to 60 000 Pa and 1.5 mm to 0.0015 m.
  • Do not call any stiffened shell a monocoque. A pure monocoque has no stringer-and-frame skeleton carrying bending.

1.3 Typical wing and fuselage structures

A wing carries lift with a thin box: skin panels, stringers, ribs and two spars. The box takes bending as compression in the top skin and tension in the bottom skin, and takes torsion as shear in the closed cell between the spars. A fuselage is a stiffened tube: frames and longerons hold a thin skin in shape and carry bending, shear and cabin pressure. Learn the members, their spacing, and how a load travels from skin to root joint. The later work on bending of thin-walled beams and on shear flow assumes this layout.

You need: Structural components of aircraft and their functions for the load-path idea, and Monocoque and semi-monocoque construction for why the skin needs a skeleton behind it.

Two spars make a torsion box

A spar is a spanwise beam running from root to tip. Most wings have a front spar near the leading edge and a rear spar further back. Each spar has a thin vertical web and two spar caps: spanwise flanges at the top and bottom of the web. Bending bends the wing up, so the top skin and top caps shorten and the bottom skin and bottom caps stretch. The caps take this axial load. The webs take shear.

The skin between the front and rear spar, together with the two webs, closes a cell called the torsion box. A closed cell resists twisting far better than an open sheet, because the shear has a continuous path all round the loop. The box sits in the deepest part of the aerofoil for two reasons: the torsional stiffness of a closed thin-walled cell grows with the square of the area it encloses, and the bending stiffness grows with the depth between the top and bottom skins.

wbox = (xrear − xfront) × c

wbox is the torsion box width in m; xfront and xrear are the front and rear spar positions as a fraction of chord (0 to 1); c is the chord in m.

Shear force and bending moment along the span are worked out in Shear force and bending moment distribution on wings; the shear flow inside this closed box comes later, with torsion of closed sections (the Bredt-Batho theory).

Ribs and stringers

A rib is a chordwise member: it runs from the leading edge to the trailing edge, crossing the box. Ribs hold the aerofoil section, support the skin and stringers, and pass panel loads into the spars. They also carry local loads in: engine mounts, landing gear, flap and aileron hinges all feed the wing through a few ribs. Ribs sit at a regular pitch, for example 0.45 m on a small wing.

A stringer is a small spanwise stiffener on the inside face of the skin, closely spaced. Stringers stop the thin skin from buckling, and together with the skin they act as the upper and lower flanges in bending.

  1. Aerodynamic pressure on the top skin loads that panel.
  2. The panel passes the load to the stringers running spanwise.
  3. Stringers hand the load to the ribs at each crossing.
  4. Ribs carry it chordwise to the front and rear spar web.
  5. Spars carry it spanwise to the root joint, which hands it to the fuselage frames.

The leading edge, ahead of the front spar, and the trailing edge, behind the rear spar, are lighter structures. They keep the profile shape, carry the slats, flaps and ailerons, and take little of the wing bending.

Fuselage: a stiffened tube

The fuselage is a semi-monocoque shell: skin, frames, longerons and bulkheads. Each has one job.

  • Frames are transverse rings, set at a regular pitch along the cabin. They keep the cross-section round, support the skin against buckling, and carry the hoop load from cabin pressure together with the skin.
  • Stringers are many light lengthwise stiffeners riveted to the skin, typically 150 to 250 mm apart round the section. They stop the skin buckling between frames and carry the axial bending stress with it; on most airliners they do the whole job.
  • Longerons, where fitted, are fewer and heavier lengthwise members; they collect bending load at cut-outs and joints. The skin carries the shear from fuselage bending and torsion, part of the axial bending stress, and the cabin pressure as hoop tension.
  • Floor beams run across the cabin between frames and carry the floor and the passengers and cargo on it.
  • Pressure bulkheads close the pressurised cabin at the front and the rear. A pressurised fuselage is a pressure vessel, so both ends of the cabin must be sealed by a dome-shaped bulkhead.

A fuselage is also a beam: the tail unit loads the rear fuselage in bending and torsion, and the wing loads the centre section. Fuselage stresses are treated in Stress analysis of fuselage and tail unit.

Cut-outs and keeping the shape

Doors, windows, access panels and wheel wells cut skin out of the shell. A cut-out removes the material that was carrying the load, so the load must divert around the opening. The usual fix is a doubler: an extra plate riveted or bonded round the hole, thick enough to take the load the missing skin used to take. Corners of cut-outs are given a radius, because a sharp corner raises stress.

A wheel well is worse than a door, because it opens into the lower skin of the wing box itself, the part carrying tension in flight. Designers keep such holes as small as the mechanism allows, put them where the bending is low, and add heavy local reinforcement.

Shape is not cosmetic. The aerofoil section produces the lift and keeps drag low, so ribs and frames must hold it under load. Dents, wrinkles and bulges spoil the airflow and also lower the buckling strength of the panels, which is why handling damage near frames and stringers matters.

Worked example

Question. A wing has a chord of 3.0 m along a spanwise length of 4.5 m from the root. The front spar lies at 15% chord and the rear spar at 60% chord. Ribs are placed at a pitch of 0.45 m. Find the width of the torsion box and the number of ribs.

  1. Front spar position: xfront × c = 0.15 × 3.0 = 0.45 m from the leading edge.
  2. Rear spar position: xrear × c = 0.60 × 3.0 = 1.80 m from the leading edge.
  3. Box width: wbox = 1.80 − 0.45 = 1.35 m, which is the same as (0.60 − 0.15) × 3.0 = 1.35 m.
  4. Number of bays between ribs: 4.5 m ÷ 0.45 m = 10 bays.
  5. Ribs: a bay has a rib at each end, and neighbouring bays share one. So there is a root rib, a tip rib at 4.5 m and 9 ribs between them: 10 bays need 10 + 1 = 11 ribs.
  6. The torsion box is 1.35 m wide at every section where the chord is 3.0 m, and 11 ribs close 10 bays of 0.45 m each.

Answer. Torsion box width = 1.35 m; number of ribs = 11.

Exam traps

  • Counting bays as ribs: 10 bays need 11 ribs, because each end of a bay needs a rib.
  • Forgetting that a door, window or wheel well removes load-carrying skin and must be reinforced with a doubler.
  • Mixing up chord fractions with metres: 0.15 is a fraction of chord, 0.45 m is a length.
  • Swapping longeron and frame: longerons run lengthwise and take bending, frames are transverse rings that hold shape and pressure.
  • Thinking the skin alone bends the wing: the stringers and spar caps share the axial load.

1.4 Honeycomb and sandwich structures

A sandwich panel is two thin stiff sheets, the faces, glued to a thick light core. The faces carry tension and compression like the flanges of an I-beam; the core carries shear and holds the faces apart. Spacing the faces out multiplies the bending stiffness while adding little weight. Aircraft use sandwich panels for floor panels, control surfaces such as ailerons and rudders, radomes and helicopter rotor blades. This chapter shows why the arrangement is stiff, what the core looks like, how the panel is glued, and how it fails.

You need: Structural components of aircraft and their functions for the idea of a load path, Typical wing and fuselage structures for the skin-stringer panels that sandwich panels partly replace, and simple bending stress and second moment of area, which return in full with the bending of thin-walled beams.

Two faces far apart

Bend a solid sheet of thickness t and its bending stiffness per unit width is D = E t³/12. Doubling t makes it eight times stiffer and twice as heavy. Most material in a solid sheet sits near the neutral axis and does little work.

A sandwich panel moves material away from the neutral axis. Two faces of thickness tf are bonded to a core, and their mid-planes are a distance d apart. Per unit width each face has area tf and sits at d/2 from the neutral axis, so I = 2 × tf × (d/2)² = tf d²/2.

D = Ef tf d² / 2

D is the bending stiffness per unit width, in N·m; Ef is Young's modulus of the face material, in Pa; tf is the face thickness, in m; d is the distance between the face mid-planes, in m, roughly the core thickness plus one face thickness.

Each face also bends about its own mid-plane, adding Ef tf³/12, but tf is a fraction of a millimetre while d is tens of millimetres, so that term is dropped.

The faces act like I-beam flanges, one in compression and one in tension, a couple separated by d. The core acts like the web, carrying the transverse shear that comes with bending, and keeps the faces flat so the compressed face cannot buckle early.

Cores and the glue line

The core is the thick, light middle. Three families cover most aircraft work.

  • Hexagonal honeycomb. Thin foil or paper is formed into half-hexagons, stacked and glued at the node lines, then pulled open into cells. Aluminium honeycomb is stiff and cheap. Nomex honeycomb, aramid paper coated with phenolic resin, resists fire and moisture. Panels are much stiffer along the ribbon direction, the direction the foil runs, so drawings must state the orientation.
  • Foam. Closed-cell PVC and polyurethane foams are cheap; polymethacrylimide foam costs much more but is stiffer and tolerates higher cure temperatures. All work about equally in both in-plane directions and machine easily. For the same weight they carry less shear than honeycomb.
  • Balsa. End-grain wood, still used in some rotor blades and boat hulls.

The adhesive matters as much as the core. A film adhesive is laid between face and core and cured under heat and pressure, in an autoclave, a press or a vacuum bag. The glue line must be continuous. A void, a greasy patch or trapped moisture is a crack waiting to run.

How a sandwich panel fails

A sandwich panel is stiff and strong but not tough. The designer checks each failure mode; the weakest sets the allowable load.

  • Face yielding. The face stress reaches the yield stress of the face material. Rarely the critical mode.
  • Face wrinkling. The compressed face buckles into the core over a short wavelength set by the face bending stiffness and the core stiffness, often only a few times the face thickness. Soft cores allow this well below yield.
  • Core shear. The core carries the shear force, mainly through cell walls aligned with the shear. Its shear strength is low, so shear often governs short, deep panels.
  • Core crushing. A local load, a jack, a bolt or a foot, crushes the cells. Honeycomb is weak in this through-thickness direction.
  • Debonding. The face lifts off the core. The faces then work alone, stiffness collapses and failure can be sudden. Bad surface preparation, moisture or a poor cure causes it.

Bolts are a special problem: a bolt through a sandwich crushes the core, so holes are potted with a solid insert before drilling. A sandwich does the job of a skin-and-stringer panel with the material pushed to the outside.

Worked example

Question. An aircraft floor panel has two aluminium faces, each 0.5 mm thick with E = 70 GPa, bonded to a 20 mm honeycomb core. (a) Find the bending stiffness D per metre width. Exam traps

  • Quoting only the faces' own bending stiffness, 2 × Ef tf³/12, and forgetting the d² term. That d² term is what the whole idea is about.
  • Using the core thickness as d and forgetting to add one face thickness. With 20 mm core and 0.5 mm faces, d is 20.5 mm, not 20 mm.
  • Checking face stress only. Core shear and debonding are common critical modes, and a panel that passes the face check can still fail in service.
  • Treating honeycomb as isotropic. Stiffness and shear strength differ strongly between the ribbon direction and across it, so orientation must be specified.
  • Mixing units: putting tf in millimetres into a formula where E is in Pa and d is in metres. The answer comes out a thousand times wrong.

1.5 Aerospace materials and their behaviour

An aircraft material is never chosen on strength alone. The choice trades strength, stiffness, weight, fatigue life, temperature limit, corrosion and cost. A wing needs stiffness so it does not flex too much; a landing gear needs strength and toughness; an engine fitting needs heat resistance. Engineers compare materials using specific strength σult/ρ and specific stiffness E/ρ, where σult is ultimate tensile stress in Pa (N/m²), E is Young's modulus in Pa and ρ is density in kg/m³. These ratios let a light material compete with a strong heavy one. The same trade decides whether a part is aluminium, titanium, steel or carbon fibre.

You need: Honeycomb and sandwich structures for why light stiff faces matter.

Specific strength and specific stiffness

Specific strength is ultimate tensile stress divided by density: σult/ρ. Specific stiffness is E/ρ. Units: (N/m²)/(kg/m³) = N·m/kg, also written J/kg or kN·m/kg. A high value means more load carried per kilogram of material. Use ultimate stress for strength comparisons. Use yield stress only when the design limits permanent deformation; mixing the two is a common error. The table gives typical room-temperature values; exact figures depend on the product form and temper. Composites are quoted along the fibre, because across the fibre they are much weaker.

Aluminium, magnesium, titanium and steel

Aluminium is the default for wings and fuselage because it is light, stiff enough, cheap and easy to form. 2024 has copper and good fatigue resistance. It is used for tension skins but needs cladding or paint because it corrodes. 7075 uses zinc and has high strength. It is used in compression skins and fittings but has lower fatigue resistance and is sensitive to stress corrosion in the T6 temper. 6061 is weaker but welds well and resists corrosion, so it suits frames and secondary structure. Magnesium alloys are lighter than aluminium (ρ ≈ 1740 kg/m³) but have lower modulus (E ≈ 45 GPa), poor corrosion resistance and fire risk. Use is limited to small castings and older airframes. Titanium Ti-6Al-4V has density 4430 kg/m³, E ≈ 114 GPa and ultimate about 950 MPa. It keeps useful strength to roughly 300–400°C; special near-alpha titanium alloys reach about 500–550°C. It appears in engine parts, exhaust areas and highly loaded fittings. High-strength steels such as 300M have density 7850 kg/m³ and ultimate near 1950 MPa. They are used for landing gear and other compact, highly loaded parts where volume is tight and the weight penalty is accepted.

Composites, fatigue, corrosion and repair

CFRP is carbon fibre in a polymer resin. The fibres carry the load; the resin holds the shape and passes shear between fibres. Along the fibre, CFRP has density about 1600 kg/m³, E of 70 to 150 GPa (70 for woven fabric, the higher values for one-direction tape) and tensile strength 1200 to 1800 MPa. It is very stiff and strong per kilogram, but its compressive strength is lower, impact damage can hide inside it, and the resin limits the temperature to about 120 to 180°C. GFRP uses glass fibre. It is cheaper and less stiff (E 20 to 40 GPa) and lets radar through, so it is used for radomes and fairings.

Fatigue. Aluminium alloys have no true endurance limit: their S-N curve keeps falling as cycles increase, so designers quote a fatigue strength at a chosen life (for example 10⁶ cycles) and design for damage tolerance. Steels do show a genuine endurance limit. 2024 is chosen for fatigue-critical tension skins because cracks grow slowly in it; 7075 is stronger but less forgiving. Composites have no single endurance limit either; they collect matrix cracks and delaminations, so design uses strain limits. The S-N curve itself is treated later, with fatigue and the endurance limit.

  • Corrosion: carbon fibre is cathodic to aluminium. A CFRP part bolted to aluminium makes the aluminium the anode and speeds up galvanic attack, so the joint is isolated with glass-fibre plies, sealant or coatings. 7075-T6 is prone to stress corrosion in the short-transverse direction; 2024 needs cladding or paint.
  • Temperature: aluminium alloys lose strength above about 120 to 150°C; titanium and steel go much higher.
  • Repair: aluminium panels are riveted or bolted; composites need bonded patches, a controlled cure and inspection. Joining is covered in Fabrication of structural components. Ply lay-up and laminate stiffness come later, with laminate theory; here only the choice of material matters.

  • Treating CFRP as isotropic. Its fibre-direction strength is high, but transverse and compressive strengths are much lower.
  • Bolting carbon fibre directly to aluminium without isolation. Carbon is cathodic; the aluminium becomes the anode and corrodes fast.

1.6 Fabrication of structural components

Aircraft parts are made by forming sheet, machining or extruding metal, or laying up composite. They are then joined by rivets, bolts, adhesive or welding. The joint, not the plate, usually decides the strength, because holes cut the net section and load must pass from one part to another. Only a few universities teach fabrication as a full topic. The ideas matter for every spar, rib, frame and skin panel.

You need Aerospace materials and their behaviour for material allowables and Typical wing and fuselage structures for which members are joined.

Forming the part

Sheet metal is formed by pressing or stretching it over a die. Stretch forming pulls a clamped sheet over a curved die; the skin stretches plastically and keeps the die shape. Machining cuts a spar or fitting from a solid billet when the shape is thick and few parts are needed. Extrusion pushes hot metal through a die to make long constant-section stringers. Composite parts are laid up ply by ply on a mould and cured in an autoclave under heat and pressure.

The route is: raw sheet or billet, form or machine, surface treatment, jig assembly, joining, inspection. Surface treatment means anodising aluminium or priming it before assembly; the bare metal corrodes and the adhesive needs a clean, prepared surface. A jig holds the parts in their true shape while holes are drilled and fasteners are placed. Inspection checks hole size, fastener grip (the total thickness a fastener clamps, which sets the fastener length) and the finished shape, and repairs are made before the part leaves the shop.

Making the joint

Rivets are the standard aircraft fastener. A rivet fills its hole, and the driven head clamps the sheets. Bolts carry larger loads and can be removed. Adhesive bonding spreads load over a wide area and avoids holes, but needs careful surface preparation. Seam welding joins thin sheets along a line.

Joints are lap or butt. In a lap joint the load path is eccentric: the sheets are offset, so the joint also feels bending. A butt joint with splice plates is more symmetric. A group of fasteners shares load; the outer rows usually take more, not equal share. Check three things: rivet shear, bearing of the rivet on the sheet, and net-section tension with the holes removed. Rivets are set at least about two diameters from a sheet edge, so the sheet does not tear out in front of the hole. Holes also start fatigue cracks; an interference-fit bolt squeezes the hole and slows crack growth. Sealant keeps moisture out.

1.7 Loads on aircraft and design considerations

An aircraft in service meets lift, weight, thrust, drag, gusts, landings, ground handling and cabin pressure. To size a member, engineers turn the worst service load into a limit load, then multiply by 1.5 to get the ultimate load. The load factor n = L/W says how many times the aircraft’s weight the lift has become: at n = 2.5 the wing lifts 2.5 times the weight. Every wing, fuselage frame and landing gear part is checked against its own critical load case.

You need: Structural components of aircraft and their functions for load paths and Typical wing and fuselage structures for which member carries bending, shear and pressure.

Sources of load and the load factor

An aircraft carries four main forces: lift L, weight W, thrust T and drag D. In steady level flight L equals W and T equals D. In a pull-up or a gust the lift can become several times the weight, and in a landing the ground reaction on the wheels can.

The load factor n = L / W is dimensionless. L is lift in N and W is weight in N. At n = 1 the lift equals weight; at n = 2.5 the lift is 2.5 times weight. A push-over can give a negative n.

Limit load is the largest load expected in normal service. It is not the failure load. For a wing in symmetric flight, limit lift = n W. For a fuselage, limit load may be cabin pressure plus bending from the tail. For landing gear, it is the vertical impact and spin-up load.

  • Flight loads: lift, weight and inertia, thrust and drag, plus aileron, elevator and rudder loads.
  • Gust loads: a vertical gust changes the angle of attack and raises lift sharply.
  • Landing loads: vertical impact, wheel spin-up, braking and side load.
  • Ground handling: taxi bumps, turning, jacking and towing.
  • Cabin pressure: the differential pressure p acts on every square metre of skin, giving hoop and longitudinal tension in the shell, and a load of p times the enclosed area on each pressure bulkhead. It is repeated every flight.
  • Tail loads: the horizontal tail often carries a down-load to balance the wing.

Inertia relief and the net section load

Lift is not the only load on a wing section. In a manoeuvre at load factor n, every mass m on the aircraft pushes down with an inertia load n m g. For a section of the wing, the net load is the aerodynamic load on the outboard part minus the inertia loads of the structure, fuel and engines outboard of that section. The root sees the sum of these net loads. Using lift alone overestimates the bending moment. This is inertia relief.

The same inertia loads act on every seat, galley and fitting in the fuselage, and together with the tail load they bend the fuselage, so each frame station is checked with the masses on either side of it. A heavy engine on a wing relieves root bending in flight because its inertia acts downward against the lift; on landing, when the wing is held up at the gear, the same engine adds to the load. So the critical case for a member depends on mass distribution.

Shear force and bending moment distribution on wings integrates this net load to get shear force and bending moment. For now, remember: the internal load on a section is the net load, not the aerodynamic load alone.

Ultimate load, factor of safety and design criteria

Design uses a factor of safety of 1.5 on the limit load. Ultimate load = 1.5 × limit load. The structure must carry the ultimate load without failure. At limit load it may deflect but must not suffer harmful permanent deformation. The factor covers small variations in load, material and analysis, not gross errors.

Pult = 1.5 × Plim

Pult is ultimate load in N; Plim is limit load in N; 1.5 is the aircraft factor of safety, dimensionless.

A critical load case is the combination of loads that gives the highest stress in a particular member. A wing spar may be critical in a gust at high speed; a fuselage frame may be critical under pressure plus bending; landing gear is critical at touchdown. Each member is sized for its own critical case.

  • Safe-life: the part is given a fixed life and retired before cracks are expected. Used for landing gear and some helicopter parts.
  • Fail-safe: the structure has multiple load paths or crack stoppers, so one failed member does not cause collapse.
  • Damage tolerance: the structure is designed so cracks grow slowly and are found by inspection before they become dangerous. Used for modern transport wings and fuselages.

1.8 V-n diagram and its use in wing sizing

A V-n diagram is a graph of load factor n against airspeed V for one aircraft. It shows the largest n the wing can produce before it stalls at each speed, and the largest n the structure is allowed to carry. Its corners are the critical cases used to size the wing, fuselage and tail.

You need limit load and load factor from Loads on aircraft and design considerations.

The stall boundary

Load factor n is lift divided by weight, n = L/W. At a given speed V the wing can make lift only up to its stall limit. Let CLmax be the maximum lift coefficient in the configuration used for the diagram. The one-g stall speed VS1 is the speed at which level flight needs the whole CLmax. From L = ½ ρ V² S CL, with L = W at n = 1:

VS1 = √(2(W/S) / (ρ CLmax))

W/S is wing loading in N/m²; ρ is air density in kg/m³; CLmax is dimensionless.

At a higher speed the same wing can pull more load factor before it stalls. Put L = nW in the lift equation and divide by the one-g case. The ρ, S and CLmax cancel:

n = (V / VS1)²

n is load factor (dimensionless); V is true airspeed in m/s; VS1 is the one-g stall speed in m/s.

This is the stall line. It is a parabola, not a straight line. At V = VS1 it gives n = 1; at 2VS1 it gives n = 4. The wing cannot go above this line at that speed, because it stalls.

Limit load factors, VA, VC, VD and gusts

The structure has a positive limit load factor nmax and a negative limit nmin: for transport aircraft at least 2.5 and −1.0, for light aircraft about 3.8 and −1.5. These are limit values. The positive stall line meets nmax at the manoeuvre speed VA:

VA = VS1 √(nmax)

VA is the manoeuvre speed in m/s; VS1 is the one-g stall speed in m/s; nmax is the positive limit load factor.

From VA the top stays flat at nmax all the way to VD, the design dive speed; VC, the design cruising speed, lies between them. The bottom is not a mirror image: the negative stall line uses the smaller negative lift coefficient, n = −(V/VS,neg)², and for transport aircraft nmin stays at −1.0 up to VC, then rises in a straight line to 0 at VD. A vertical line at VD closes the envelope.

A sharp vertical gust of speed U changes the angle of attack by U/V, and the lift-curve slope a (per radian) turns this into extra lift:

n = 1 ± ρ V a U / (2 W/S)

ρ is air density in kg/m³; V is airspeed in m/s; a is the lift-curve slope per radian; U is the gust velocity in m/s; W/S is the wing loading in N/m². Use + for an upward gust and − for a downward one.

Each gust line is straight and starts at n = 1 when V = 0. Textbook design gusts (equivalent airspeed) are about 20 m/s at VB, 15 m/s at VC and 7.5 m/s at VD. This is the sharp-edged value; certification multiplies the increment by a gust alleviation factor below 1. At low speed the gust line lies above the stall line, where the wing stalls first; at VC it can end above nmax, a gust load larger than any manoeuvre the pilot may command.

Reading the critical corners: wing, fuselage and tail

The critical points are the corners A (VA, nmax) and D (VD, nmax), the negative corners at VC and VD, and any gust point outside the manoeuvre boundary. Each gives a load factor and the speed at which it acts.

  • Wing: root bending grows with n, so the highest corner sizes the spar caps and skins. Torsion depends on speed as well, because the wing’s nose-down pitching moment grows with dynamic pressure, so the VD corners usually size the torsion box.
  • Fuselage: the corner n is the inertia factor on every mass in the fuselage; each seat, galley, cargo item and rear-mounted engine pushes down with n m g. With the tail load at that corner, it sets the fuselage bending moment.
  • Tail unit: the tailplane load at each corner comes from pitching-moment balance, worked in Stress analysis of fuselage and tail unit. The largest balancing loads usually appear at VA, with the largest n, and at VD, with the largest wing pitching moment. Checked pitching manoeuvres and full-elevator cases at VA are added. The fin is sized by rudder and sideslip cases from VA to VD, which the V-n diagram does not show.

Worked example

Question. A wing has wing loading W/S = 3000 N/m² and CLmax = 1.5. Air density is 1.225 kg/m³. The positive limit load factor is nmax = 2.5. Find the one-g stall speed VS1 and the manoeuvre speed VA. State where the first manoeuvre corner lies.

  1. At n = 1: W/S = ½ ρ VS1² CLmax. Rearrange: VS1 = √(2(W/S)/(ρ CLmax)).
  2. 2(W/S) = 2 × 3000 = 6000 N/m². ρ CLmax = 1.225 × 1.5 = 1.8375.
  3. VS1 = √(6000 / 1.8375) = √3265.3 = 57.14 m/s.
  4. VA = VS1 √nmax = 57.14 × √2.5 = 57.14 × 1.581 = 90.35 m/s ≈ 90.4 m/s.
  5. Below 90.4 m/s the wing stalls before it reaches n = 2.5; above it the wing can reach 2.5. So the first manoeuvre corner is (VA, nmax) = (90.4 m/s, 2.5), and the flat top runs from there to VD.

Answer. VS1 = 57.1 m/s and VA = 90.4 m/s; the manoeuvre corner is at (90.4 m/s, 2.5).

Worked example 2

Question. The same aircraft (W/S = 3000 N/m², ρ = 1.225 kg/m³, nmax = 2.5, nmin = −1.0) has VC = 120 m/s and VD = 160 m/s. The lift-curve slope is a = 5.0 per radian. Exam traps

  • Drawing the stall line as a straight line from the origin to VA. It is n = (V/VS1)², a parabola; a straight line under-predicts n at mid speeds.
  • Mirroring the top of the envelope to make the bottom. The negative stall line uses the smaller negative lift coefficient, and nmin rises from −1.0 at VC to 0 at VD for transport aircraft.
  • Ending the flat top at VC. For transport aircraft the positive limit nmax holds all the way to VD.
  • Ignoring gust lines at high speed. A gust point at VC can sit above the manoeuvre top, so the critical load may be a gust the pilot never commands.
  • Rounding VS1 too early. 57.14 × √2.5 = 90.35 m/s; using 57.1 gives 90.3 m/s. Keep full precision until the final answer.

1.9 Shear force and bending moment distribution on wings

A wing behaves like a cantilever built into the fuselage. Lift pushes it up; wing structure, fuel and engines push it down. Integrating the net load from the tip inward gives the shear force; integrating again gives the bending moment. Both are zero at the free tip and usually largest at the root. These two diagrams are the first numbers a stress engineer needs: they show where the wing is worked hardest and what the carry-through structure must react.

You need: loads on aircraft for the load factor and the V-n diagram for the design cases.

Wing as a cantilever

Take the root at x = 0 and the tip at x = L. The semi-span L is in metres. The net load w(x) is in N/m and is positive upward. It is lift minus wing structure weight minus fuel weight per metre. A positive w means the net load bends the wing upward.

V(x) = ∫xL w(ξ) dξ

V is the shear force in N at station x; w is the net load per unit span in N/m, positive upward; x is distance from the root in m; L is the semi-span in m; ξ is a dummy span coordinate in m.

M(x) = ∫xL w(ξ)(ξ − x) dξ

M is the bending moment in N·m at station x; V, w, x, L and ξ are as above. Use one sign convention throughout: upward net load positive, root at x = 0.

The part of the wing outboard of x carries all the net load beyond x. V(x) is the total of that outboard load. M(x) is that same load multiplied by its lever arm (ξ − x) about the station x. At the free tip there is no outboard load, so V(L) = 0 and M(L) = 0.

For a uniform net load w, V = w(L − x) and M = w(L − x)²/2. At the root V = wL and M = wL²/2. The moment rises with the square of the remaining span, so near the root it grows much faster than shear. That is why the root fitting and the carry-through structure are sized mainly by moment.

An engine, a store or a landing gear adds a downward point load: the shear jumps there and the moment changes slope, as worked example 2 shows.

Lift distribution and Schrenk

Real lift is not uniform along the span: it depends on planform, twist and the trailing vortices. A first estimate gives the load shape the shear and moment integrals need.

An elliptic lift distribution has minimum induced drag for a given span and a given total lift. A rectangular wing carries less lift near its tips than its constant chord suggests, because the tip vortices unload them; a tapered wing carries more lift near the root because the chord is larger there. Schrenk's approximation takes the mean of the elliptic distribution and the planform distribution at each station.

wlift(x) ≈ ½ [ well(x) + wplan(x) ]

wlift is the estimated lift per unit span in N/m; well is the elliptic distribution in N/m; wplan is the planform distribution in N/m, proportional to chord c(x); the constant of proportionality is chosen so the total lift equals the required lift.

Taper moves load inboard. A tapered wing of the same span and total lift has a lower root bending moment than a rectangular wing, because the centre of its lift sits closer to the root; the root shear, equal to the total net load, is the same. Use Schrenk's shape for preliminary loads, then replace it with wind-tunnel or CFD data when available. The integration method does not change.

Mass relief, carry-through and tail loads

Wing structure and fuel are not lift. Subtract them before integrating. This is mass relief: it lowers the root bending moment. Fuel in the wing gives strong relief when tanks are full, but the critical case may be low fuel, so check every fuel state from empty to full tanks. A full tank is not automatically the worst case.

The wing root moment is reacted by the carry-through structure and the fuselage frames at the attachment points. The carry-through is the structure that carries the wing root moment across the fuselage. The fuselage behaves as a beam: it receives a concentrated moment and shear at the wing junction, so its bending moment diagram has a jump there.

The tail produces a lift or downforce at a long arm from the centre of gravity. That tail load bends the rear fuselage. The bending moment is zero at the point where the load is applied and grows linearly forward along the fuselage, so it is largest at the wing attachment, where it equals T × l. The carry-through then reacts it. Here T is the tail load in N and l is the distance in m from the wing attachment to the line of action of the tail load. See stress analysis of fuselage and tail unit for how the cross-section carries it.

Worked example

Question. A wing of semi-span 8 m carries a uniform lift of 12 kN/m and has wing structure and fuel of 3 kN/m along the same span. Find the shear force and bending moment at the root and at 2 m from the root.

  1. Net load per unit span: w = 12 − 3 = 9 kN/m.
  2. Semi-span: L = 8 m. At the root x = 0, the outboard length is L − x = 8 m.
  3. Root shear: V = w(L − x) = 9 × 8 = 72 kN.
  4. Root moment: M = w(L − x)²/2 = 9 × 8²/2 = 9 × 64/2 = 288 kN·m.
  5. At x = 2 m from the root, the outboard length is L − x = 6 m.
  6. Shear at 2 m: V = 9 × 6 = 54 kN.
  7. Moment at 2 m: M = 9 × 6²/2 = 9 × 36/2 = 162 kN·m.

Answer. At the root: shear 72 kN and bending moment 288 kN·m. At 2 m from the root: shear 54 kN and bending moment 162 kN·m.

Worked example 2

Question. The wing of the first example (semi-span 8 m, net distributed load 9 kN/m upward) also carries an engine 3 m from the root. At the design load factor the engine’s inertia load is 40 kN downward (an engine of about 1630 kg at n = 2.5). Find the shear force and bending moment at the root and on each side of the engine, and the largest shear on the span.

  3. Crossing the engine, the outboard load drops by 40 kN: just inboard of it, V = 45 − 40 = 5 kN. M does not jump: it is still 112.5 kN·m at x = 3 m.
  4. Root shear: V = 9 × 8 − 40 = 72 − 40 = 32 kN.

Exam traps

  • Using lift alone and forgetting to subtract wing structure and fuel. The root moment comes out too high because mass relief is missing.
  • Applying M = wL²/2 to a tapered lift distribution or a wing with an engine. That formula is only for uniform w; integrate the actual w(x) and add each point load times its arm.
  • Forgetting the fuselage carry-through. The wing root moment does not disappear at the fuselage; it is reacted by the carry-through and frames.
  • Assuming full fuel is always critical. Fuel relief changes with the fuel load, so check every fuel state from empty to full.

1.10 Stress analysis of fuselage and tail unit

A fuselage in flight is a thin-walled tube doing two jobs at once: it holds cabin pressure and it bends like a beam. The tail unit hangs off its rear end, and the tailplane and fin loads feed bending and twist into that beam. Stress analysis asks what stresses meet at each point and whether they pass the material check. It is the part of this subject that exams most often ask with numbers.

You need: the load factor from Loads on aircraft and design considerations, the corner points of the V-n diagram, and shear and moment diagrams from Shear force and bending moment distribution on wings.

The fuselage as a pressure tube and a beam

Cabin pressure p is a differential pressure: inside absolute pressure minus outside ambient pressure. On a thin circular cylinder of mean radius r and skin thickness t, it gives two membrane stresses at right angles:

σh = p r / t    σl = p r / (2t)

σh is the hoop stress round the circumference and σl the longitudinal stress along the axis, both in Pa; p is in Pa; r and t are in m.

Hoop stress is twice the longitudinal stress, so a fatigue crack in plain skin tends to run along the fuselage. The tube is also a beam: a bending moment M gives an axial stress σb = M y / I. For a thin circular tube I ≈ π r³ t, and the skin sits at y = r. Stringers add area far from the neutral axis, so a stiffened section is often treated as a tube with an equivalent thickness: skin plus the stringer area spread round the circumference.

Fuselage bending and the tail balancing load

Treat the fuselage as a beam held at the wing. At load factor n, every mass m aft of a frame pushes down with n m g, and the tailplane load P acts at the tail. The bending moment at a frame is each load times its distance from the frame, so the rear-fuselage moment is zero at the tail and grows forward to its largest value at the wing attachment, where the carry-through reacts it.

The tail load comes from pitch balance. The wing lift Lw acts a distance a behind the centre of gravity (CG), the wing has a nose-down pitching moment M₀, and the tail load P (upward positive) acts lt behind the CG. Moments about the CG and vertical equilibrium give:

P = −(n W a + M₀) / (lt − a)    Lw = n W − P

W is the weight in N; a and lt are in m; M₀ is in N·m. A negative P is a download.

This is how the V-n diagram sizes the tail and fuselage: each corner gives n and a speed; the speed sets M₀, which grows with dynamic pressure; the formula gives P. The largest balancing loads usually come at VA and VD. The tailplane is itself a cantilever, so its own bending moment peaks at the tailplane root.

Fin loads and local load paths

The fin carries a side load F from rudder or sideslip at a height h above the fuselage axis. The fuselage receives a sideways bending moment and a torque T = F h, which the closed skin carries as a constant shear flow q = T / (2A), A being the area the skin encloses; torsion of closed sections derives this later.

  • Frames take concentrated loads (floor beams, wing, gear and tail fittings) and spread them into the skin as shear.
  • A domed pressure bulkhead carries pressure as membrane tension; a flat one bends and is much heavier.
  • A window or door cut-out breaks the hoop load path, so a doubler or surround frame carries the lost load, with rounded corners.

Combining the stresses

Hoop and longitudinal stresses act on perpendicular faces, so they are not simply added. For ductile aluminium, use von Mises with the total longitudinal stress σx, pressure plus or minus bending:

σvm = √(σx² − σx σh + σh²),   σx = p r/(2t) ± M y/I

σvm is the equivalent stress in Pa; + applies on the tension side of the bend, − on the compression side.

Compare limit-load stresses with yield and ultimate-load stresses with ultimate strength. A compressive σx must also pass a skin and stringer buckling check.

Worked example

Question. A fuselage has mean radius 1.2 m and skin 1.6 mm thick. Cabin differential pressure is 55 kPa and the ultimate bending moment at a section is 200 kN·m.   2. I ≈ π r³ t = π × 1.728 × 0.0016 = 8.686 × 10⁻³ m⁴.

  4. Tension side: σx = 20.63 + 27.63 = 48.26 MPa. Compression side: σx = 20.63 − 27.63 = −7.01 MPa.
  5. σvm = √(48.26² − 48.26 × 41.25 + 41.25²) = √2039.6 = 45.16 MPa. The compression side gives the same 45.16 MPa, because the two values of σx sit equally either side of σh/2.
  6. Margin: 45.2 MPa at ultimate load is far below 485 MPa; at limit load (÷ 1.5) it is about 30 MPa, far below the 345 MPa yield. The −7.0 MPa side still needs a buckling check, and pressure fatigue is checked separately.

Takeaways

  1. Every aircraft member is a load path: it collects load from a wider area and hands it to a stronger member until the load reaches a support.
  2. Monocoque skins must be thick to avoid buckling, so real aircraft are semi-monocoque, with stringers taking bending and thin skin taking shear.
  3. A wing behaves as a torsion box and a cantilever beam; ribs keep the box shape and transfer skin loads to the spars.
  4. Sandwich panels give very high bending stiffness for low weight because the faces carry bending and the core carries shear.
  5. Material choice is a trade of strength, stiffness, density, fatigue, temperature, corrosion and cost, never a single property.
  6. Fabrication and joints decide real strength; a riveted or bonded joint, not the plate, is usually the weak point.
  7. Limit load is the worst service load, ultimate load is 1.5 times it, and load factor n = L/W converts flight loads to design loads.
  8. The V-n diagram is bounded by the stall parabola, a flat top at nmax out to VD, and nmin; any gust point outside it, often at VC, becomes the sizing case.
  9. Shear and bending moment come from integrating the net spanwise load from the tip; point masses make the shear jump, and the root moment is usually the largest.
  10. A pressurised fuselage is a beam and a pressure vessel at once: hoop stress is twice the longitudinal stress, and each flight adds a fatigue cycle.
  11. The tailplane load comes from pitch balance at each V-n corner; it and the tail inertia bend the rear fuselage with a moment that grows from the tail to the wing.

Remember Define monocoque and semi-monocoque construction. Explain why the semi-monocoque is used for almost all modern aircraft. 8 marks

Points to cover. Monocoque: the skin carries all load; it must be thick to resist buckling, so it is heavy; Semi-monocoque: thin skin on frames, stringers and longerons; Skin takes shear, torsion and pressure; stringers and longerons take axial bending stress; Frames keep the cross-section and shorten the buckling length of the skin; Result: high strength for the weight, many load paths, easy repair.

Model answer.

  1. Monocoque. In a monocoque shell the skin alone carries every load: bending, shear, torsion and cabin pressure. There is no skeleton of stringers and frames. Early wooden racing aircraft and some rocket casings and drop tanks are built this way.
  2. Why a pure monocoque is heavy. A thin unstiffened shell buckles at a low compressive stress, and the compressed side of a bending fuselage is exactly where that happens. The only cure without stiffeners is a thicker skin. Weight grows in proportion to thickness, so the shell becomes heavy long before its material is fully stressed in tension.
  3. Semi-monocoque. The skin is kept thin and a skeleton is added under it: longitudinal stringers (and, on some aircraft, a few heavier longerons) and transverse frames or rings. Each part has a job:
  4. MemberMain job
    SkinShear and torsion, cabin pressure (hoop tension), part of the axial bending stress
    StringersAxial bending stress; stiffen the skin against buckling
    LongeronsHeavier axial members; collect load at doors, cut-outs and joints
    FramesHold the round shape; split the skin into short panels; spread concentrated loads
  5. Pressure in a semi-monocoque skin, with numbers. A fuselage 2.0 m in diameter (r = 1.0 m) with 1.5 mm skin at 60 kPa differential pressure:
    σh = p r / t = 60 000 × 1.0 / 0.0015 = 40 MPa
σl = p r / (2t) = 20 MPa
  6. The thin skin carries this tension easily; tension does not buckle it. It is the compression from bending that needs the stringers and frames, so the skeleton puts material exactly where buckling would start.
  7. Why the semi-monocoque wins. (1) Strength for weight: stringers and frames raise the buckling stress of thin skin, so the skin can work near its material strength. (2) Fail-safety: many members share the load, so one cracked stringer or skin bay does not collapse the structure. (3) Cut-outs: doors and windows are framed by local members without thickening the whole shell. (4) Production and repair: standard sheet, extrusions and rivets; a damaged bay is patched locally.
  8. Closing check. The skin of both types carries the pressure; the difference is buckling under bending compression. Monocoque fixes it with thickness (heavy), semi-monocoque with a skeleton (light). Modern composite fuselages still follow the semi-monocoque idea, with stringers co-cured into the skin.

Understand Define limit load, ultimate load, factor of safety and load factor. State the usual values used in aircraft design and explain what each means physically. 8 marks

Points to cover. Limit load: largest load expected in service; no harmful permanent deformation; Ultimate load = 1.5 × limit load; no failure; Factor of safety covers scatter in load, material and analysis; Load factor n = L/W; 1 in level flight, 2.5 typical transport limit; Worked numbers.

Model answer.

  1. Load factor n. n = L/W, lift divided by weight; dimensionless. In steady level flight n = 1. In a pull-up the wing must lift more than the weight, and every mass on the aircraft feels n times its weight as an inertia load. A push-over gives n below 1 and can make it negative.
  2. Limit load. The largest load expected in service, from the worst manoeuvre, gust or landing the rules require. At limit load the structure may deflect but must not yield or deform permanently in a harmful way. For transport aircraft the limit manoeuvre load factor is at least 2.5 (and −1.0); light aircraft use about 3.8.
  3. Factor of safety. A multiplier of 1.5 applied to the limit load. It covers small variations in loads, material strength, manufacture and analysis; it is not a cover for gross errors.
  4. Ultimate load. Ultimate load = 1.5 × limit load. The structure must carry it, at least briefly, without failing.
  5. Numbers. An aircraft of mass 60 000 kg at n = 2.5 (g = 9.81 m/s²):
    weight         W = 60 000 × 9.81      = 588.6 kN
limit lift     L = 2.5 × 588.6        = 1471.5 kN  (1.47 MN)
ultimate load    = 1.5 × 1471.5       = 2207.3 kN  (2.21 MN)
  6. What each means physically. n is how heavy the aircraft feels: at 2.5 a 70 kg passenger presses on the seat with 2.5 × 70 × 9.81 = 1717 N. Limit is the worst the aircraft should ever meet; the gap to ultimate is the margin against uncertainty. Stresses at limit load are compared with yield; stresses at ultimate load with ultimate strength.
  7. Closing check. 2207.3 ÷ 1471.5 = 1.5 and 1471.5 ÷ 588.6 = 2.5. Do not apply 1.5 twice, and do not confuse n (a flight condition) with 1.5 (a design multiplier).

Model answer.

  1. The layout. A transport wing is a two-spar box. The front spar sits near 15 % of the chord and the rear spar near 60 %; with the top and bottom skins they enclose a closed cell, the torsion box. Ribs cross the box chordwise at a regular pitch. Stringers run spanwise on the inside of both skins. The leading edge (ahead of the front spar) and trailing edge (behind the rear spar) are light structures carrying slats, flaps and ailerons.

  3. Step 1, skin. Air pressure acts normally on the skin. A panel 0.6 m × 0.4 m at 15 kPa carries P = 15 000 × 0.24 = 3600 N. The skin spans between stringers like a small plate.

  1. How the load is carried. Tension in the upper sheet presses against the rivet shanks (bearing). Each rivet is cut across the joint plane (single shear in a lap joint). The lower sheet takes the load back by bearing on the other side of the rivets. Friction from the rivet clamp carries a little, but design ignores it.
  2. Failure modes checked. (1) Rivet shear across the faying plane. (2) Bearing: the hole elongates in the thinner sheet. (3) Net-section tension through the line of holes. (4) Edge tear-out, prevented by keeping an edge distance of about 2d. (5) Fatigue cracking from hole edges under repeated pressurisation.
  3. Numbers. 8 rivets, d = 4 mm (A = 12.57 mm²), t = 1.6 mm, w = 100 mm:
    ModeWorkingLoad (N)
    Rivet shear8 × 12.57 × 10⁻⁶ × 200 × 10⁶20 106 (governs)
    Bearing8 × 0.004 × 0.0016 × 480 × 10⁶24 576
    Net section at the first row(0.100 − 4 × 0.004) × 0.0016 × 290 × 10⁶38 976
    Uncut sheet0.100 × 0.0016 × 290 × 10⁶46 400
  4. Strength = 20.1 kN, in rivet shear. Efficiency = 20 106 ÷ 46 400 = 43 %. The net section is checked at the first row, because that row carries the whole sheet load past its holes; the second row sees only what the first row has not already transferred.
  5. Load sharing and eccentricity. Rows do not share equally: the outer rows take more because the sheets stretch between rows. In a single lap the two sheets are offset by one thickness, so the joint bends and the rivet heads pry; that is why fuselage longitudinal laps use three rows, or butt joints with a doubler.
  6. Fatigue. The hole is a stress raiser and the bending of the lap adds stress at the faying surface. Countersunk holes in thin outer skin are especially sensitive, and multi-site damage along a lap row is a known airliner hazard. Sealant and bonding (rivet-bonding) reduce the load per rivet; cold-worked holes and interference fits slow crack growth.
  7. Closing check. Ultimate design load must stay below 20.1 kN; a larger rivet or a third row would move the critical mode toward bearing and raise the efficiency.

Analyze Compare aluminium alloy, titanium alloy and carbon fibre composite for a wing upper skin. Justify a choice and state the trade-offs involved. 13 marks

Points to cover. Upper skin is compression- and buckling-critical, with fatigue, damage and repair concerns; Specific strength and stiffness; buckling index E1/3/ρ; Aluminium: cheap, proven, repairable, ~25 MN·m/kg; Titanium: strong, hot, costly, heavy for buckling; CFRP: high stiffness for weight, impact damage, temperature, lightning, galvanic; Reasoned choice.

Model answer.

  1. What the upper skin needs. In upward bending the upper skin is in compression, so it is designed by panel buckling and compressive strength, not tensile strength. It also needs damage tolerance (tool drops, hail), fuel-tank sealing, lightning protection and repairability.
  2. Data (typical values; CFRP as a quasi-isotropic skin laminate, E about 50 GPa, strength about 600 MPa):
    Materialρ (kg/m³)E (GPa)σ/ρ (kN·m/kg)E/ρ (MN·m/kg)E1/3/ρ
    7075-T6 Al28107120325.31.47
    Ti-6Al-4V443011421425.71.10
    CFRP quasi-isotropic16005037531.32.30
  3. Why E1/3/ρ. A skin panel of given width and length carrying a given compressive load buckles at a stress proportional to E t². For a fixed load, the lightest panel minimises ρ t, which leads to a mass proportional to ρ/E1/3. So a higher E1/3/ρ (units: GPa1/3 per tonne/m³) means a lighter buckling-critical skin.
  4. Aluminium. E1/3/ρ = 1.47. Cheap, easy to form, machine and rivet, inspectable, repairable at any airfield. 7075 or 7150-type alloys suit the compression skin. Drawbacks: corrosion, fatigue at holes, and no gain on stiffness over other metals.
  5. Titanium. High strength, good fatigue and corrosion resistance, keeps strength hot. But E1/3/ρ = 1.10, worst of the three, because its density is 1.6 times aluminium’s while its modulus is only 1.6 times higher, and the cube root weakens the modulus benefit. It is also costly and slow to machine. Titanium suits hot zones and highly loaded fittings, not a large skin.
  6. CFRP. E1/3/ρ = 2.30, about 55 % better than aluminium, so the skin can be much lighter; plies can be laid to suit the loads, and it does not corrode or fatigue like metal. But impact damage can hide inside, compression after impact sets the allowable (lowering the usable strength), lightning needs a metal mesh, joints to aluminium need galvanic isolation, repairs need bonding and controlled cure, and the material and certification are costly.
  7. Choice. For a high-production airliner today, CFRP gives the lightest upper skin and is used on the newest wide-bodies; for a smaller aircraft, or where cost, repair and proven practice matter most, a 7000-series aluminium skin is the sensible choice. Titanium is not chosen for the skin.
  8. Closing check. The ranking reverses depending on the property: on σ/ρ titanium beats aluminium, on E1/3/ρ it loses. A sound answer names the governing failure mode first and then picks the material.
Evaluate A semi-monocoque fuselage of radius 1.9 m at 55 kPa differential pressure is cut for a cargo door 1.8 m long and 2.0 m high. Analyse the effect on the structure, and evaluate the role of doublers, frames, sills and crack stoppers in restoring strength. 13 marks

Points to cover. Cut-out removes skin and stringers; loads must go round; Hoop load N = p r per metre of length; longitudinal p r/2 per metre of height; Stress concentration at corners; radii; Edge frames, sill and header beams, doublers; Door plug loads; crack stoppers; fatigue.

Model answer.

  1. What the cut removes. Skin, several stringers and parts of frames. The shell had carried hoop tension, longitudinal tension, bending stress and shear through that area. All of it must now go round the opening, and the door itself, unless it is a plug type, carries little.
  2. Size of the diverted loads. Hoop tension per metre of fuselage length is Nh = p r; longitudinal tension per metre of height is Nl = p r / 2:
    Nh = 55 000 × 1.9 = 104.5 kN/m  → over 1.8 m of door: 104.5 × 1.8 = 188.1 kN
Nl = 104.5 / 2    = 52.25 kN/m  → over 2.0 m of door: 52.25 × 2.0 = 104.5 kN
  3. Where the loads go. The 188 kN of hoop tension goes into the two heavy edge frames at the forward and aft sides of the door. The 104.5 kN of longitudinal tension, plus the fuselage bending load of the cut stringers, goes into the sill (below) and header (above) beams, which act as heavy longerons. The shear that the cut skin carried passes round the frame of members as a stiff picture frame.
  4. Corners. Where these members meet, the stress concentrates. A sharp corner can raise the local stress several times; generous radii and corner doublers keep it low. Corners are the classic fatigue sites.
  5. Doublers. Extra skin bonded or riveted round the opening restores area and spreads the load from the edge members into the surrounding skin over a longer length, so the rivet rows are not overloaded.
  6. Crack stoppers. Tear straps and frames act as barriers: a crack growing from a corner meets extra material and slows or turns. This makes the design fail-safe, and inspection intervals are set from crack-growth rates.
  7. The door. A plug door, larger than its opening, is pressed into the frame by pressure and carries its pressure load in bearing on stops. A cargo door usually opens outward, so its latches carry the full pressure load: 55 000 × 1.8 × 2.0 = 198 kN. Latch failure is catastrophic, so latches have indicators and redundancy.
  8. Evaluation. A well-framed opening returns the section close to its uncut strength, but always at a weight cost (heavy frames, sills, doublers) and with new fatigue sites. The design is acceptable only when the static check of the edge members and a fatigue and damage-tolerance analysis of the corners both pass.
  9. Closing check. Nl = Nh/2 as for a closed cylinder; the edge frames carry about 94 kN each (188 ÷ 2).
Evaluate An aircraft of weight 600 kN has a wing weighing 60 kN and a semi-span of 15 m. Its V-n diagram gives a manoeuvre limit of 2.5 and a gust point of 2.84 at VC. Using a uniform spanwise net load, size the bending material at the wing root for an effective box depth of 0.6 m and an allowable stress of 450 MPa at ultimate load. Evaluate how raising the limit load factor to 3.0, or raising the aspect ratio at the same wing area, alters the design. 16 marks

Points to cover. Design n is the highest corner: gust 2.84; Net lift per half-wing n(W − Wwing)/2, uniform over the semi-span; Root M = w L²/2; ultimate = 1.5 × limit; Bending material area A = M/(h σ) on each side of the box; Effect of n = 3.0; Effect of aspect ratio: span up, chord and depth down.

Model answer.

  1. Step 1, design load factor. The wing must cover every point of the envelope. The upward gust at VC (2.84) is above the manoeuvre limit (2.5), so n = 2.84 is used for upward bending.
  2. Step 2, net load on one half-wing. Fuselage, payload and fuel outside the wing are carried by the lift; the wing’s own weight relieves itself (mass relief):
    net lift per half-wing = n (W − Wwing) / 2 = 2.84 × (600 − 60) / 2 = 766.8 kN
uniform net load      w = 766.8 / 15 = 51.12 kN/m
  3. Step 3, root bending moment.
    limit    M = w L² / 2 = 51.12 × 15² / 2 = 5751 kN·m
ultimate M = 1.5 × 5751              = 8626.5 kN·m
  4. A real lift distribution is closer to elliptic, whose centre of lift on each half-wing sits at 4L/(3π) = 0.424L instead of 0.5L, so the uniform assumption overestimates the root moment by about 18 %: it is a conservative first estimate.
  5. Step 4, bending material. Top and bottom of the box act as a couple h = 0.6 m apart:
    force in each side   F = M / h = 8 626 500 / 0.6 = 14 377.5 kN

Over a box width of, say, 2.0 m it is an equivalent thickness of 31 950 ÷ 2000 = 16 mm, typical of a mid-size airliner’s root. The compression side must also pass panel buckling, and the tension side must pass fatigue at limit load.

  9. Step 6, raising aspect ratio at the same area. AR = b²/S. Raising the span by 20 % (L = 18 m) at the same area raises AR by 1.2² = 1.44 and cuts the mean chord by 1.2. For the same total lift, uniform loading gives M = (lift per half-wing) × L/2, so M rises by 20 %. The box depth scales with chord, so h falls by 1.2. Analyze Describe how loads from the tail unit are transferred into the rear fuselage, and analyse why the rear fuselage is often critical for bending and fatigue. Use an aircraft of weight 500 kN at n = 2.5 with the wing lift 0.5 m behind the CG, a tail arm of 15 m, a tail-unit mass of 800 kg and a fin side load of 30 kN at 2.5 m above the fuselage axis (r = 1.2 m, skin 1.6 mm). Compare wing pitching moments of 150 kN·m (at VA) and 300 kN·m (at VD). 13 marks

Points to cover. Tailplane balancing load from pitch equilibrium; fin side load from rudder or sideslip; Loads enter through fittings and heavy frames; Rear-fuselage shear and moment grow forward from the tail; Fin load gives torsion T = F h, shear flow q = T/2A; Why critical: long arm, smaller section, cut-outs, load reversal and fatigue.

Model answer.

  1. How tail loads enter. The tailplane spars end at fittings on heavy frames in the rear fuselage (or, on a T-tail, on the fin spars, which pass the load down into the fuselage). The fin spars attach to two or three heavy frames. These frames spread the concentrated fitting loads into the skin as shear flow.
  2. Tailplane balancing load. P = −(n W a + M₀)/(lt − a), with n W a = 2.5 × 500 × 0.5 = 625 kN·m and lt − a = 14.5 m:
    CaseM₀ (kN·m)P (kN)Wing lift (kN)
    VA, n = 2.5150−53.45 (down)1303.4
    VD, n = 2.5300−63.79 (down)1313.8
  3. The higher speed corner gives the larger tail download, because the wing’s nose-down pitching moment grows with dynamic pressure. This is how the V-n corners size the tail.
  4. Rear-fuselage bending. Tail inertia n m g = 2.5 × 800 × 9.81 = 19.62 kN adds to the download. At a frame 10 m forward of the tail:
    VA case: M = (53.45 + 19.62) × 10 = 730.7 kN·m
VD case: M = (63.79 + 19.62) × 10 = 834.1 kN·m   (14 % larger)
  5. The moment is zero at the tail and grows linearly forward to the wing attachment. On a plain 1.2 m tube with 3.0 mm equivalent thickness (I = 0.01629 m⁴), the VD case gives σ = 834 100 × 1.2 ÷ 0.01629 = 61.4 MPa.
  6. Fin load. The side load bends the fuselage sideways and twists it:
    torque     T = F h = 30 × 2.5 = 75 kN·m
enclosed   A = π r² = π × 1.2² = 4.524 m²
shear flow q = T / (2A) = 75 000 / 9.048 = 8289 N/m
skin shear τ = q / t = 8289 / 0.0016 = 5.18 MPa
  7. Why the rear fuselage is critical. (1) The tail loads act on the longest lever arm on the aircraft, so modest forces make large moments. (2) The rear fuselage tapers, so its section (and I) is smaller just where the moment is applied. (3) It has cut-outs: rear doors, service panels, and on some aircraft an APU bay or a tail cone opening. (4) Loads reverse: balancing loads, gusts and manoeuvres swing the tail load up and down and the fin load left and right, so each skin panel sees alternating tension and compression. (5) Pressure cycles add hoop stress every flight, and the rear pressure bulkhead sits here. Fatigue cracks therefore start at fitting holes and frame-to-skin joints; damage-tolerance inspections concentrate on the rear pressure bulkhead and tail attachment frames.
  8. Closing check. Moments about the CG for the VD case: −1313.8 × 0.5 − 300 + 63.79 × 15 = −656.9 − 300 + 956.9 = 0.

Glossary

Buckling
Sudden sideways deflection of a thin member under compression at a load below its crushing strength.
Bulkhead
A transverse wall-like member that closes a section, carries concentrated load, or holds cabin pressure.
Doubler
An extra plate added around a cut-out or joint to restore the strength taken away.
Fail-safe design
Design in which a crack or failed member is stopped by other members until it is found in inspection.
Fatigue
Failure by repeated load cycles that start and grow a crack at a stress concentration.
Fitting
A local machined or forged component that connects two members and spreads a concentrated load.
Frame
A transverse ring in the fuselage that maintains the cross-section shape and supports stringers and skin.
Gust load
The extra lift created when the aircraft meets an upward or downward air current.
Honeycomb core
A core of hexagonal cells, made from foil or paper, whose walls carry shear between the faces.
Hoop stress
The circumferential tensile stress in a pressurised cylinder, equal to p·r/t.
Limit load
The maximum load expected in service, which the structure must carry without permanent damage.
Load factor
The ratio n = L/W of lift to weight, which measures how many times the aircraft feels heavier than its weight.
Load path
The continuous route by which a force travels from where it is applied to the support that reacts it.
Longeron
A heavy longitudinal fuselage member that acts as a main bending member.
Monocoque
Construction where the skin alone carries all loads, with no internal skeleton.
Neutral axis
The line in a beam section where bending stress is zero and about which the bending stresses balance.
Rib
A chordwise wing member that gives the aerofoil shape and transfers skin and stringer loads to the spars.
Sandwich structure
Two thin stiff faces bonded to a thick light core to obtain high bending stiffness at low weight.
Semi-monocoque
Construction with thin skin supported by frames, stringers and longerons so that each carries part of the load.
Spar cap
The upper or lower flange of a spar, farthest from the neutral axis, carrying the largest bending stress.
Stringer
A small longitudinal stiffener on the inside of the skin that takes axial stress and delays skin buckling.
Torsion box
A closed cell of spars and skin that resists twisting of a wing or tail surface.
Ultimate load
Limit load multiplied by the factor of safety, usually 1.5; the structure must not fail at this value.
V-n diagram
A graph of load factor against equivalent airspeed showing the flight envelope and the critical design cases.
