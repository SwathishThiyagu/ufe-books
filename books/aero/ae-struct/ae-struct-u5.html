5.1 Concept of shear flow and shear centre

A thin-walled beam carries a transverse shear force S not at one point but spread along its walls as a shear flow q, in N/mm. The resultant of that flow acts along one line, and the point where that line crosses the section's axis of symmetry, or in general the point every such line passes through, is the shear centre. A load whose line misses the shear centre twists the beam as well as bending it. Wing spars, ribs, channel frames and control-surface hinges are all placed with this in mind.

You need: the bending stress σ = M y/I and the second moment of area of a thin-walled section, from the topic on bending of thin-walled beams.

Shear flow is force per length of wall

Take a short element of a wall of thickness t (mm) carrying shear stress τ (N/mm²). Because the wall is thin, τ is taken as uniform through t, and the force carried along each millimetre of wall is q = τ t. This is the shear flow. It is a force per length, so a wall of length L carrying a constant q transmits a force q L.

q = τ t

q is the shear flow (N/mm); τ is the shear stress in the wall (N/mm²); t is the wall thickness (mm).

Where does q come from? Look at a beam carrying shear S. The bending moment changes along the span at the rate dM/dz = S, so the direct stress σ = M y/I on one face of a slice dz long is not the same as on the other. A piece of wall cut off between a free edge and a point s along the wall is pushed one way more than the other, and only a shear flow on the cut can hold it. So q depends on how fast σ changes along the beam, not on σ itself: at the root of a cantilever σ is largest, but if the shear is zero there, so is q.

q(s) = −(Sy/Ixx) ∫₀ˢ t y ds

q(s) is the shear flow at distance s along the wall from a free edge (N/mm), positive in the direction of increasing s; Sy is the shear force in the +y direction (N); Ixx is the second moment of area about the centroidal x axis (mm⁴); y is the signed y coordinate of the wall element from that axis (mm); t is the wall thickness (mm). This form needs a section symmetric about x; the general form is in the chapter on open section beams.

Three facts follow. At a free edge there is nothing to carry shear, so q = 0. Along a horizontal flange y is constant, so q grows linearly. Along a vertical web y changes, so q is parabolic and peaks where the web crosses the neutral axis. At a corner the flow turns and carries on: q is continuous.

The resultant and the shear centre

Each element q ds is a small force along the wall. Add them all and the result is one force equal to the applied shear. Its line of action lies in the plane of the section. Two different shear directions give two such lines; the point where they cross is the shear centre, and every transverse load through it bends the beam without twisting it.

  • The shear centre is a property of the section shape, not of the load.
  • A doubly symmetric section (I-section, circular tube, rectangular box of uniform wall) has its shear centre at the centroid.
  • A singly symmetric section has its shear centre on the axis of symmetry, but not in general at the centroid.
  • A section made of walls that meet at one point, such as an angle or a tee, has its shear centre at that point: every q ds passes through it, so their moment about it is zero.
  • A load through any other point equals the same load through the shear centre plus a torque S × (offset), which twists the beam.

The shear centre is also called the flexural centre. For thin-walled beams it coincides with the centre of twist, the point about which the section rotates under pure torque.

The channel: why the shear centre sits outside the web

A channel has a web of depth h and two flanges of width b on one side. Under a downward shear, the top flange flow runs one way and the bottom flange flow the other way; each flange carries a force F = qj b/2, where qj is the flow at the flange–web corner. The two flange forces form a couple F h. The web carries the vertical shear. For the resultant to have no twisting effect, the vertical force must act at a distance e from the web such that S e = F h, on the side away from the flanges.

e = t h² b²/(4 Ixx) = 3b²/(h + 6b)

e is the distance of the shear centre from the web mid-line, on the side away from the flanges (mm); h is the web depth and b the flange width, both on mid-lines (mm); t is the uniform thickness (mm). The second form uses Ixx = t h³/12 + t b h²/2, which neglects each flange's own second moment.

The centroid of the same channel lies on the flange side of the web, so the centroid and the shear centre lie on opposite sides of the web. A load through the centroid misses the shear centre by x̄ + e and twists the channel. A lipped channel adds short lips at the flange tips; their flows act against the flange couple and move the shear centre back towards the web. The method for any open wall is the same: start at each free edge with q = 0, build q along the wall, take moments of all the q ds about a convenient point, and equate that moment to S times the offset.

Worked example

Question. A channel has a web 120 mm deep and flanges 60 mm wide, all 3 mm thick (mid-line dimensions). It carries a vertical shear force S = 5 kN. Find (a) Ixx, (b) the shear flow at the flange–web corner and at the middle of the web, (c) the shear centre position, and (d) the torque if the load acts through the centroid instead.

  1. Web: t h³/12 = 3 × 120³/12 = 432,000 mm⁴. Flanges: 2 × (60 × 3) × 60² = 1,296,000 mm⁴. Ixx = 1,728,000 mm⁴.
  2. Top flange, from its free tip (y = +60 mm): q grows linearly with s, q = (S/Ixx) t y s in magnitude. At the corner (s = 60 mm): qj = 5000 × 3 × 60 × 60/1,728,000 = 31.25 N/mm.
  3. Web, from the corner down to mid-depth: the area above the neutral axis adds t (h/2)²/2 = 3 × 60²/2 = 5400 mm³ of first moment. qmid = 31.25 + (5000/1,728,000) × 5400 = 46.88 N/mm.
  4. Check the web total: the web carries ∫q ds = 2 × (31.25 × 60 + (2/3)(15.62) × 60) = 5,000 N = S. The flanges carry no vertical force.
  5. Each flange force: F = qj b/2 = 31.25 × 60/2 = 937.5 N. Couple: F h = 937.5 × 120 = 112,500 N·mm.
  6. Moments about the web mid-line: S e = F h, so e = 112,500/5000 = 22.5 mm. Formula check: 3b²/(h + 6b) = 10,800/480 = 22.5 mm.
  7. Centroid: x̄ = (2 × 180 × 30)/(2 × 180 + 360) = 15.0 mm from the web, on the flange side.
  8. A load through the centroid is 37.5 mm from the shear centre, so it adds a torque T = 5000 × 37.5 = 187,500 N·mm = 187.5 N·m.

Answer. Ixx = 1,728,000 mm⁴; q = 31.25 N/mm at the corners and 46.88 N/mm at mid-web; the shear centre is 22.5 mm outside the web, on the side away from the flanges; a load at the centroid adds 187.5 N·m of torque.

Exam traps

  • Taking the centroid as the shear centre. In the channel they are 37.5 mm apart, on opposite sides of the web.
  • Starting the integral at a corner. In an open section q = 0 only at a free edge.
  • Using the unsigned distance from the axis for y. y is a signed coordinate; below the neutral axis it is negative and the flow builds back down.
  • Placing the channel's shear centre on the flange side. It lies outside the web, on the side away from the flanges.

Understand Why is the shear flow zero at a free edge of an open wall? 3 marks

The edge has no neighbouring wall to deliver shear into it, and the complementary shear stress on the free face is zero, so τ and hence q = τ t vanish there.

Understand Why does shear flow depend on the shear force and not on the bending stress at a section? 3 marks

q balances the difference in direct stress between the two faces of a slice dz. That difference is (dM/dz) y dz/I = S y dz/I, so q is set by S. A section of large M but zero S carries no bending shear flow.

The load misses the shear centre by x̄ + e, so the rib twists as well as bends, adding torsional shear stress and stressing the attachments. In the worked channel the offset is 37.5 mm. 5.2 Shear of open section beams

An open section, such as a channel, Z, angle or lipped stringer, has free edges where the shear flow must be zero. Starting there and integrating along the wall gives the whole shear flow distribution; taking moments of it gives the shear centre. Below, the general integral is worked on a Z-section with Ixy ≠ 0 and on a channel stiffened by booms.

You need: shear flow and the shear centre from the first chapter, and Ixx, Iyy and Ixy of thin-walled sections from the topic on bending.

From a free edge to a shear flow

Cut a piece of wall between a free edge and a point at distance s along the wall mid-line. On the two faces of a slice dz long the direct stress differs by (∂σ/∂z) dz. The only force that can balance that difference is the shear flow on the cut, so ∂q/∂s = −t ∂σ/∂z. Using the unsymmetrical bending stress and dMx/dz = Sy, dMy/dz = Sx, and integrating from q = 0 at the free edge:

qs = −[(Sx Ixx − Sy Ixy)/D] ∫₀ˢ t x ds − [(Sy Iyy − Sx Ixy)/D] ∫₀ˢ t y ds,   D = Ixx Iyy − Ixy²

qs is the shear flow at s (N/mm), positive in the direction of increasing s; Sx and Sy are the shear force components (N); x and y are centroidal coordinates of the wall element (mm); Ixx, Iyy and Ixy are centroidal second moments (mm⁴); D is in mm⁸.

If the section is symmetric about x (Ixy = 0) and Sx = 0, this reduces to qs = −(Sy/Ixx) ∫ t y ds. The equation assumes the shear acts through the shear centre, so the section does not twist. Choosing the other free edge as the start reverses every sign but not the physical flow.

Working round a section, and finding the shear centre

  1. Find the centroid and Ixx, Iyy and Ixy using mid-line dimensions.
  2. Pick a free edge, the direction of s, and write x and y of each wall as functions of s.
  3. Integrate wall by wall. Carry the value at the end of one wall into the start of the next: q is continuous round a corner.
  4. Check that q returns to zero at the other free edge, and that the vertical walls add up to Sy.
  5. Take moments of all the q ds about a convenient point O and set them equal to the moment of the load placed at the unknown shear centre.

In a channel the two flange flows form a couple, which pushes the shear centre out beyond the web. A Z-section with equal flanges has point symmetry, so its shear centre is at the centroid. With Ixy ≠ 0 the x term in the formula is not zero, and q can change sign along a flange: a zero inside a wall is not a free edge.

Open sections with stiffening elements

A real stringer or spar has concentrated areas: spar caps, lips, attached stringers. Idealise each as a boom of area Br at (xr, yr) that carries all the direct stress, and let the thin walls between booms carry only shear. Walls of this kind are said to be ineffective in bending. The integral then contributes nothing between booms, so q is constant along each wall and jumps at each boom:

q₂ − q₁ = −[(Sx Ixx − Sy Ixy)/D] Br xr − [(Sy Iyy − Sx Ixy)/D] Br yr

q₁ and q₂ are the flows just before and just after boom r along the direction of s (N/mm); Br is the boom area (mm²); xr and yr its centroidal coordinates (mm). With Ixy = 0 and Sx = 0 it is q₂ − q₁ = −(Sy/Ixx) Br yr.

If the walls also carry direct stress, keep both: the integral for each wall plus the jump at each boom. The structural idealisation chapter shows how to choose B for a stringer and its skin.

Worked example

Question. A Z-section has a web 80 mm deep and two flanges 40 mm wide, all 2 mm thick; the top flange runs from the web to x = +40 mm, the bottom flange to x = −40 mm. It carries Sy = 3 kN (Sx = 0) through its shear centre. Find the shear flow at 10 mm steps from the top free edge, and the shear centre.

  1. Origin at the centroid (web mid-height). Ixx = 2 × 80³/12 + 2 × 80 × 40² = 341,333 mm⁴. Iyy = 2 × (2 × 40³/3) = 85,333 mm⁴. Ixy = 2 × 80 × 20 × 40 = 128,000 mm⁴ (flanges in the first and third quadrants). D = 1.274 × 10¹⁰ mm⁸.
  2. With Sx = 0: q = (Sy Ixy/D) ∫t x ds − (Sy Iyy/D) ∫t y ds = 0.030134 ∫t x ds − 0.020089 ∫t y ds (N/mm³).
  3. Top flange, s from the tip: x = 40 − s, y = 40, so ∫t x ds = 80s − s² and ∫t y ds = 80s. q = 0.030134(80s − s²) − 0.020089(80s). At s = 0, 10, 20, 30, 40: 0.000, 5.022, 4.018, −3.013, −16.071 N/mm.
  4. Web, s down from the top: y = 40 − s, so the web adds ∫t y ds = 80s − s². q = −16.071 − 0.020089(80s − s²). At s = 10 … 80: −30.134, −40.179, −46.205, −48.214, −46.205, −40.179, −30.134, −16.071 N/mm; the peak −48.214 N/mm is at the neutral axis.
  5. Bottom flange, s from the web: x = −s, y = −40. q = −16.071 − 0.030134s² + 0.020089(80s). At s = 10 … 40: −3.013, 4.018, 5.022, 0.000 N/mm. It returns to zero at the free tip, which checks the arithmetic.
  6. Resultants: the top flange gives ∫q ds = 0.030134 × 42,667 − 0.020089 × 64,000 = 0, and so does the bottom flange. The web gives ∫q ds = −3,000 N along +s (downwards), that is 3000 N upwards = Sy.
  7. Shear centre: the flanges carry no net force and the web lies on x = 0, so the moment of the flows about the centroid is zero. The shear centre is at the centroid, as point symmetry requires.

Answer. q runs 0, 5.02, 4.02, −3.01, −16.07 along the top flange, down to −48.214 N/mm at mid-web, and back to zero at the bottom tip; the shear centre is at the centroid.

Worked example 2

Question. A channel spar is idealised as four booms on a 200 mm deep web: 200 mm² at each flange tip (x = 80 mm) and 400 mm² at each web corner (x = 0), at y = ±100 mm. The walls carry shear only. It carries Sy = 12 kN through its shear centre. Find the shear flows and the shear centre.

  1. Ixx = Σ B y² = 2 × (200 + 400) × 100² = 12,000,000 mm⁴, and Sy/Ixx = 0.0010 N/mm³.
  2. Top flange, from the free tip boom 1: q₁₂ = −0.0010 × 200 × 100 = −20.0 N/mm (s runs from the tip to the corner).
  3. Web, after corner boom 2: q₂₃ = −20.0 − 0.0010 × 400 × 100 = −60.0 N/mm.
  4. Bottom flange, after corner boom 3 (y = −100): q₃₄ = −60.0 + 0.0010 × 400 × 100 = −20.0 N/mm. After tip boom 4: −20.0 + 20.0 = 0, the free edge. Check.
  5. Web force: 60 N/mm × 200 mm = 12,000 N = Sy. Each flange carries 20 × 80 = 1,600 N, in opposite directions: a couple of 1,600 × 200 = 320,000 N·mm.
  6. Moments about the web: Sy e = 320,000, so e = 26.67 mm from the web, on the side away from the flanges.

Answer. 20 N/mm in each flange, 60 N/mm in the web, zero beyond the tip booms; the shear centre is 26.67 mm outside the web.

Exam traps

  • Setting q = 0 at a web–flange corner. Only the free tips of an open section have q = 0.
  • Dropping Ixy on a Z or angle. Then the x term vanishes and the flange flows come out with the wrong shape.
  • Using |y| instead of the signed coordinate. Below the neutral axis y is negative and the integral starts to fall.

Analyze Why is the shear centre of an equal-flange Z at its centroid, while a channel's is outside the web? 4 marks

The Z has point symmetry about the centroid: each flange carries zero net force and the web passes through the centroid, so the flows have no moment about it. With no tip booms the flanges carry no flow (q₁₂ = 0), the couple vanishes, and the shear centre moves onto the web line. Stiffening the tips increases the flange flows and moves the shear centre outwards.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. The shear centre of an open stringer decides whether it buckles in pure bending or in combined bending and twist. Search for "flexural–torsional buckling of stringers".

5.3 Shear of closed section beams

A closed section, a wing box or a fuselage shell, has no free edge, so the shear flow has nowhere obvious to start. The method is to cut the loop once in imagination, find the open-section flow qb, and add a constant flow q₀ round the loop. For a load through the shear centre q₀ is fixed by the condition that the section does not twist. This chapter works the method on a rectangular box, then thickens one wall and shows how the shear centre moves towards it.

You need: the open-section shear flow from the chapter on open sections; the twist-rate formula used here is derived in the Bredt–Batho chapter.

The cut and the constant q₀

Make an imaginary cut in one wall. The section is now open, and the open-section integral gives a basic flow qb, zero at the cut. The real section has no cut, so the real flow is qb plus a constant q₀ that runs all the way round and represents the flow at the cut. q₀ is not found from force equilibrium: any constant flow round a closed loop has zero resultant force, only a moment 2Aq₀.

qs = qb + qs,0

qs is the actual shear flow (N/mm); qb is the open-section flow from the cut (N/mm); qs,0 is the constant flow at the cut (N/mm), positive in the same direction as s.

Two conditions can fix q₀. If the load position is known, take moments of q about any point and equate them to the moment of the load. If the shear centre is wanted, impose zero twist instead. The rate of twist of a closed cell is (1/2AG)∮q ds/t, so zero twist means:

∮ (qb + qs,0) ds/t = 0   →   qs,0 = −∮(qb/t) ds / ∮ ds/t

t is the wall thickness (mm); ds is a length of mid-line (mm); the integrals go once round the loop in the direction of s. With uniform t, qs,0 = −(1/L)∮qb ds, L being the perimeter.

Step by step

  1. Find the centroid and Ixx (and Iyy, Ixy if the section is unsymmetric), using mid-line dimensions.
  2. Choose the cut. A cut on an axis of symmetry is convenient, but any cut gives the same final q.
  3. Integrate qb = −(Sy/Ixx)∫t y ds from the cut, y being the signed coordinate, carrying the value round each corner.
  4. Evaluate ∮qb ds/t wall by wall and ∮ds/t, and find qs,0. Add it to every qb.
  5. Take moments of q about a convenient point O: the moment equals Sy times the distance from O to the shear centre.
  6. Check that the vertical walls add up to Sy and the horizontal walls to zero.

For a section symmetric about x, the shear centre lies on the x axis, and one load case (Sy) gives its position along it. An unsymmetric section needs a second case with Sx to find the other coordinate.

Symmetric and unsymmetric closed sections

In a symmetric box under vertical shear, both vertical walls carry flow in the same physical direction, opposing the load. Read round the loop the two values have opposite signs, only because s runs up one wall and down the other. The shear centre of a doubly symmetric box with uniform walls is at the centroid.

Make one wall thicker and the symmetry about the vertical axis is lost. The thick wall is stiffer in shear, so in the zero-twist solution it takes a larger share of the shear force, and the resultant, which is the shear centre, moves towards it. A wing box with a heavy front spar web therefore has its shear centre ahead of mid-box. The shear centre does not follow the centroid, so each must be found by its own calculation.

If the real load acts elsewhere, the section carries the zero-twist flow plus a Bredt–Batho torsion flow T/2A, where T is the load times its offset from the shear centre.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular box has mid-line width 200 mm, depth 100 mm and all walls 2 mm thick. It carries Sy = 10 kN through its shear centre. Cut the left wall at mid-height and take s up the left wall, along the top, down the right wall and back along the bottom. Find qb at the corners, qs,0, and the final flows.

  1. Ixx = 2 × (2 × 100³/12) + 2 × (200 × 2 × 50²) = 333,333 + 2,000,000 = 2,333,333 mm⁴. Sy/Ixx = 4.2857 × 10⁻³ N/mm³
  2. Left wall, up from the cut: ∫t y ds = 2 × 50²/2 = 2500 mm³, so qb at the top-left corner = −0.00429 × 2500 = −10.71 N/mm.
  3. Top wall (y = 50): adds 2 × 50 × 200 = 20,000 mm³. qb at the top-right corner = −96.43 N/mm.
  4. Right wall: the upper half adds +2500 and the lower half −2500, so qb at the bottom-right corner is again −96.43 N/mm. Bottom wall (y = −50) removes 20,000: −10.71 N/mm at bottom-left, and the last half-wall brings qb back to 0 at the cut.
  5. Uniform t, so qs,0 = −(1/L)∮qb ds. Integrating each wall: ∮qb ds = −0.00429 × (2 × 41,667 + 2 × 2,500,000 + 2,416,667) = −32,143 N. L = 600 mm. qs,0 = 53.57 N/mm.
  6. Final flows: top-left and bottom-left corners 42.86 N/mm; top-right and bottom-right −42.86 N/mm; mid-height of the left wall 53.57, of the right wall −53.57 N/mm along s.
  7. Read physically, both vertical walls carry flow upwards against the load; the top and bottom walls carry flows that reverse at mid-width. Moments confirm the shear centre at x = 100.0 mm, the mid-width, as symmetry requires.

Answer. qb = −10.71 and −96.43 N/mm at the corners; qs,0 = 53.57 N/mm; final corner flows ±42.86 N/mm; shear centre at mid-width.

Question. The same box has its left (front) wall thickened to 4 mm; the other walls stay 2 mm. Find the shear centre.

  1. Ixx = 4 × 100³/12 + 2 × 100³/12 + 2,000,000 = 2,500,000 mm⁴; Sy/Ixx = 0.0040 N/mm³. The cut and the path are as before.
  2. qb in units of k = Sy/Ixx: left-upper wall −2s² (t = 4); top −(5000 + 100s); right −(25,000 + 100s − s²); bottom −(25,000 − 100s); left-lower −(5000 − 200s + 2s²), returning to 0 at the cut.

Exam traps

  • Stopping at qb. The open-section flow is not the answer for a closed section; add qs,0.
  • Saying q = 0 at the cut. The real flow at the cut is qs,0.
  • Using ∮q ds = 0 when the walls differ in thickness. The condition is ∮q ds/t = 0.
  • Taking the shear centre at the centroid of an unsymmetric box. In the second example they are 6.9 mm apart.
  • Mixing the signs of qb and qs,0. Both must be positive in the same direction of s.

5.4 Torsion of closed sections: Bredt-Batho theory

Close a thin wall into a loop and twist it, and the torque is carried by a shear flow q that is the same in every wall. This is the Bredt–Batho result, q = T/2A. The stress in each wall is q/t, and the rate of twist depends on the enclosed area and on ∮ds/t. Wing boxes, fuselage barrels and tail booms are sized for torsion this way.

You need: shear flow q = τ t from the first chapter, and the shear modulus G.

Constant shear flow round a closed loop

Take a single-cell tube under a pure torque T, with no axial load. Cut out a small rectangle of wall bounded by two cross-sections and two lines along the beam. With no direct stress, axial equilibrium of the rectangle needs the shear flows on its two longitudinal edges to be equal. So q does not change along the wall: in a single closed cell under pure torque, q is constant all round, whatever the wall thickness.

Now take moments about any point O. A length ds of wall carries a force q ds, and its moment arm about O is the perpendicular distance p. But p ds is twice the area of the thin triangle from O to the element. Going round the loop, ∮p ds = 2A, so T = 2Aq.

q = T/(2A), τ = q/t = T/(2A t)

q is the shear flow (N/mm); T is the torque (N·mm); A is the area enclosed by the wall mid-line (mm²); t is the local wall thickness (mm); τ is the shear stress (N/mm²), largest where the wall is thinnest.

Use the area inside the mid-line, not the outer outline. The result assumes a thin wall, free warping of the cross-section ends and an unbuckled skin.

Rate of twist and the torsion constant

The section rotates by θ about its centre of twist. A wall element at perpendicular distance p from that centre moves a distance pθ along the wall, and the shear strain is γ = q/(G t). Summing the work of the shear flow round the loop (or the displacements, which gives the same result) leads to:

dθ/dz = (1/2A) ∮ q ds/(G t) = (T/4A²G) ∮ ds/t

dθ/dz is the rate of twist (rad/mm); G is the shear modulus (N/mm²); ∮ds/t is dimensionless (a length over a length) and is summed wall by wall when t changes.

J = 4A²/∮(ds/t),   dθ/dz = T/(G J),   θ = T L/(G J)

J is the torsion constant of the closed section (mm⁴); L is the length of the member (mm); θ is the total twist (rad).

For a circular tube of mean radius R and thickness t, A = πR² and ∮ds/t = 2πR/t, so J = 2πR³t, the polar second moment of a thin ring. The first form of dθ/dz, with q inside the integral, is the one to use for multi-cell sections and for the zero-twist condition of the closed-section shear chapter, where q is not constant.

Using it, and its limits

  1. Draw the mid-line and compute A inside it.
  2. Split the loop where t changes and add each length over its own thickness: ∮ds/t = Σ Li/ti.
  3. q = T/2A in every wall; τi = q/ti.
  4. J = 4A²/∮ds/t; dθ/dz = T/GJ; θ = T L/GJ.

The result becomes inaccurate rather than failing outright as the wall thickens, because the stress is no longer uniform through t. Where the cross-section is prevented from warping, at a root rib or a fuselage frame, direct stresses appear and the member is stiffer than the formula says. A wing box with more than one cell has a different q in each cell; the next chapter handles it.

Worked example

Question. A single-cell wing box has a rectangular mid-line 600 mm × 200 mm. The top and bottom skins are 1.5 mm thick and the two spar webs 3 mm. It carries T = 30 kN·m. With G = 27 GPa, find q, the stress in the skins and webs, J, the rate of twist and the twist over a 4 m length.

  1. A = 600 × 200 = 120,000 mm²; T = 30 × 10⁶ N·mm.
  2. q = T/2A = 30 × 10⁶/(2 × 120,000) = 125.0 N/mm, the same in all four walls.
  3. Skins: τ = 125.0/1.5 = 83.3 N/mm². Webs: τ = 125.0/3 = 41.7 N/mm². The thin skins carry the higher stress.
  4. ∮ds/t = 2 × 600/1.5 + 2 × 200/3 = 800 + 133.33 = 933.33.
  5. J = 4A²/∮ds/t = 4 × 120,000²/933.33 = 6.171 × 10⁷ mm⁴.
  6. dθ/dz = T/GJ = 30 × 10⁶/(27,000 × 6.171 × 10⁷) = 1.800 × 10⁻⁵ rad/mm = 0.0180 rad/m = 1.03°/m.
  7. Over 4 m: θ = 0.0180 × 4 = 0.0720 rad = 4.13°.

Answer. q = 125 N/mm; τ = 83.3 N/mm² in the skins and 41.7 N/mm² in the webs; J = 6.171 × 10⁷ mm⁴; 1.03° per metre, 4.13° over 4 m.

Exam traps

  • Using the outside outline for A. A is enclosed by the mid-line.
  • Taking ∮ds/t as perimeter over the thinnest wall. In the example that gives J = 5.40 × 10⁷ mm⁴ instead of 6.17 × 10⁷, so the twist comes out 14 per cent too large. Add each wall separately.
  • Assuming the stress is largest in the thick spar webs. q is constant, so τ = q/t is largest in the thinnest wall.
  • Applying q = T/2A to an open section. With no closed loop there is no enclosed area; open walls follow the St Venant law.
  • Leaving T in kN·m with A in mm². Convert T to N·mm first.

5.5 Single-cell and multi-cell structures

A wing box with a middle spar, or a fuselage with a floor, has two or more closed cells. Under torque each cell carries its own constant shear flow, the wall between two cells carries the difference, and every cell must twist at the same rate because they are joined. The torque equation plus one equal-twist equation per extra cell gives the flows. The same equations, with the open-section flow added, solve the cells under shear and locate the shear centre.

You need: Bredt–Batho torsion and the twist-rate integral from the previous chapter, and the cut-and-add method of the closed-section shear chapter.

Single cell and two cells

A single cell has one unknown flow, fixed by T = 2Aq. Two cells have two: q₁ circulating round cell 1 and q₂ round cell 2, both taken anticlockwise. The outer walls of cell 1 carry q₁ and those of cell 2 carry q₂. On the shared wall the two circulations run in opposite directions, so it carries q₁ − q₂ in the sense of cell 1.

T = 2A₁q₁ + 2A₂q₂

T is the torque (N·mm); A₁ and A₂ are the areas enclosed by cells 1 and 2 (mm²); q₁ and q₂ are the cell flows (N/mm).

dθ/dz = (1/2AR G) ∮_R q ds/t

dθ/dz is the rate of twist of cell R (rad/mm); the integral goes round cell R only, using the net flow in each wall, q₁ − q₂ in the shared wall; G is the shear modulus (N/mm²).

Because the cells are joined along their common wall and the ribs keep the section shape, every cell has the same rate of twist. That equal-twist condition is the second equation, and the one most often left out.

Setting up the equations

Write δ for ∮ds/t taken over a group of walls (dimensionless): δ₁ round all of cell 1, δ₂ round all of cell 2, and δ₁₂ for the shared wall alone. Then ∮₁q ds/t = δ₁q₁ − δ₁₂q₂ and ∮₂q ds/t = δ₂q₂ − δ₁₂q₁.

  1. Number the cells and take every cell flow anticlockwise.
  2. Compute AR, δR for each cell and δ for every shared wall.
  3. Write the rate of twist of each cell, (δR qR − Σ δRS qS)/(2AR G).
  4. Set the rates equal: n − 1 equations for n cells.
  5. Add the torque equation T = Σ2AR qR and solve.
  6. Report outer walls as qR and shared walls as differences.

The equations are linear, so they go straight into a matrix solver: a program that sets up the coefficients and solves them is a common exercise, and the second example below is one. Walls that carry only shear (ineffective in bending) still appear in δ; only booms and walls that carry direct stress appear in Ixx.

Multi-cell sections under shear

For a shear load, cut one wall in each cell to make the section open, find qb as for an open section, and add an unknown constant qs,0,R in each cell. There are then n + 1 unknowns: the n constants and the rate of twist. The equations are the n twist equations, with qb included in each integral, and one moment equation: the moments of all the flows about any point equal the moment of the applied load about that point. A constant flow q in cell R contributes 2AR q to that moment.

To find the shear centre, set the rate of twist to zero, solve the n twist equations for the constants, and the moment equation then gives the position of the load that causes no twist.

5.6 Structural idealization: booms and panels

A real wing box has a skin stiffened by many stringers, and integrating the shear flow round it by hand is long. Structural idealisation replaces it by booms, concentrated areas that carry all the direct stress, joined by thin panels that carry only shear. Each boom stands for a stringer plus the skin working with it. Done properly, the idealised section has the same second moment as the real one, and the shear flow becomes a short sum.

You need: the bending stress in thin-walled sections, and the shear flow jump at a boom from the chapter on open sections.

Booms carry direct stress, panels carry shear

In a stiffened skin most of the direct stress is carried by the stringers, spar caps and the strip of skin next to them. The skin between stringers is thin and carries little direct stress, but it carries shear well. The idealised section keeps that split: each stringer and its share of skin becomes a boom of area B at the stringer position, and each length of skin between booms becomes a panel of the real thickness t that carries a constant shear flow.

Panels that keep part of their skin in the booms are called effective in bending, through the boom areas. A wall given no boom contribution at all, such as a thin web between booms that already include the spar caps, is ineffective in bending: it carries shear only and adds nothing to I. Both kinds still enter ∮ds/t in twist calculations.

Effective boom area

Take a skin panel of width b and thickness t between booms 1 and 2, with direct stresses σ₁ and σ₂ at its edges and a linear variation between them. Replace it by two areas at its edges that give the same force and the same moment about each edge. That gives, for boom 1:

B₁ = (t b/6)(2 + σ₂/σ₁)

B₁ is the boom area at edge 1 contributed by this panel (mm²); t is the panel thickness and b its width (mm); σ₂/σ₁ is the ratio of the direct stresses at the far and near edges.

A boom collects one such term from every panel it touches, plus its own stringer or spar-cap area. Two ratios occur often. A skin panel between two booms at the same height under bending has σ₂/σ₁ = 1, so it contributes t b/2 to each. A spar web from the top boom to the bottom boom has σ₂/σ₁ = −1, so it contributes only t b/6 to each. For a stringer on a straight run between two equal panels of pitch p, the two terms add to t p.

  1. Mark every stringer and spar cap as a boom.
  2. For each boom, list the panels it touches, their widths and thicknesses.
  3. Find σ₂/σ₁ for each panel from the bending case (the ratio of the boom distances from the neutral axis for pure Mx).
  4. B = stringer area + Σ (t b/6)(2 + σ₂/σ₁) over the panels.
  5. Check: Σ B y² should equal the exact Ixx of the real section.

Properties, stresses and shear flows of the idealised section

Ixx = Σ Br yr²,   σr = Mx yr/Ixx

Ixx is the idealised second moment (mm⁴); Br and yr are the area (mm²) and distance from the neutral axis (mm) of boom r; σr is the boom stress (N/mm²); Mx is the bending moment (N·mm).

q₂ − q₁ = −(Sy/Ixx) Br yr

the jump in panel shear flow across boom r (N/mm) for a section symmetric about x; Sy is the shear force (N). Between booms q is constant.

A closed idealised box is solved exactly as in the closed-section chapter: cut a panel, sum the jumps, add qs,0. If the box is symmetric about the vertical axis and the load acts on that axis, the cut can be put on the axis of symmetry where q is zero, and no qs,0 is needed. The panel stress is τ = q/t.

5.7 Shear flow in tapered beams and wings

A wing gets shallower from root to tip. Its spar caps are then not parallel to the span, so the axial force in each cap has a component across the beam, and part of the shear force is carried by the caps instead of the web. The web shear flow is no longer S/h. This chapter finds the share the booms take, works it at three stations of a tapered spar, and shows that the web is often most loaded well outboard of the root.

You need: boom stresses and the shear flow sum of the idealisation chapter.

Boom forces have a component across the beam

Idealise the beam into booms carrying direct stress and panels carrying shear. At a section, boom r carries an axial force Pz,r = σr Br along the beam axis, with σr from the bending formula. If the boom is inclined to the z axis, the actual force runs along the boom, and it has components in the plane of the section:

Py,r = Pz,r (δyr/δz),   Px,r = Pz,r (δxr/δz),   Pr = Pz,r √(1 + (δxr/δz)² + (δyr/δz)²)

Pz,r is the component of the boom force along z (N), tension positive; δyr/δz and δxr/δz are the slopes of boom r in the yz and xz planes (dimensionless); Py,r and Px,r are the components across the section (N); Pr is the force along the boom.

Taper angles are small, so Pr ≈ Pz,r, but the components across the section are not small compared with the shear force. They carry part of Sy and Sx, so the webs and skins carry only the rest:

Sy,w = Sy − Σ Py,r,   Sx,w = Sx − Σ Px,r

Sy,w and Sx,w are the shear forces carried by the walls (N). The wall shear flows then follow from the idealised-section sum with Sy,w and Sx,w in place of Sy and Sx.

The two-boom tapered spar

Take a spar of two booms a distance h apart, with the neutral axis midway. Each boom is at y = ±h/2, so Ixx = 2B(h/2)² and Pz = M y B/Ixx = M/h: the boom area cancels. If the depth falls towards the tip at the rate h′ = −dh/dz (positive), each boom slopes towards the centre line at h′/2.

In the usual case, a cantilever whose depth is largest where the moment is largest, the tension boom and the compression boom both lean so that their components across the beam act with the web, against the load. Together they carry M h′/h, so:

Sw = S − M h′/h,   q = Sw/h

S is the shear force at the section (N); M the bending moment (N·mm); h the boom spacing (mm); h′ the rate at which the depth falls outboard (dimensionless); q the web shear flow (N/mm).

The same answer comes from boom equilibrium. The web flow equals the rate at which a boom loses force along the span, q = −dP/dz. With P = M/h, dP/dz = (dM/dz)/h − (M/h²)(dh/dz), and dM/dz = −S for z running outboard from the root of a tip-loaded cantilever. The first term alone is the uniform-beam flow S/h; the second is the taper correction.

Wings: taper, sweep and the shear centre

In a wing box with many booms, every boom whose height changes with z takes part of the shear. Compute Pz,r from the bending stress at the station, multiply by its slope, subtract the sum from Sy, and use the remainder in the ordinary shear flow calculation. If the booms taper in plan as well, the x slopes take part of Sx in the same way.

For a linear taper h′ is constant, but the correction M h′/h depends on M/h, which falls towards the tip. So the web flow often rises outboard even as the shear force stays constant: in the example the web flow at the tip is almost three times its root value. Sweep alone, a boom leaning only in plan at constant height, changes none of the Py terms; its Px components form a couple with the other booms and add a torque. Because the boom share depends on M while the web share depends on S, the line of action of the web resultant shifts with the ratio M/S, so a strongly tapered box has no fixed shear centre.

Worked example

Question. A cantilever spar 2 m long has two booms of 800 mm² each, 200 mm apart at the root and 120 mm at the tip, tapering linearly. A load of 20 kN acts at the tip. Find the boom forces, the share of the shear carried by the booms, and the web shear flow at the root, at mid-span and at the tip.

  2. Root: M = 40 kN·m, h = 200 mm. P = M/h = 40 × 10⁶/200 = 200,000 N (tension in the top boom, compression in the bottom). Boom stress 250 N/mm².
  3. Each boom carries P × 0.02 = 4,000 N across the beam; together 8,000 N. The web carries Sw = 20,000 − 8,000 = 12,000 N, and q = 12,000/200 = 60.0 N/mm. A uniform spar would give 100 N/mm.
  4. Check by q = −dP/dz: (−20,000)/200 − (40 × 10⁶/200²)(−0.04) = −100 + 40 = −60 N/mm, so q = 60 N/mm.
  5. Mid-span (1 m from the root): h = 160 mm, M = 20 kN·m, P = 125,000 N, booms carry 2 × 2,500 = 5,000 N, web 15,000 N, q = 93.75 N/mm.
  6. Tip: h = 120 mm, M = 0, so the booms carry nothing across the beam and q = 20,000/120 = 166.7 N/mm.

Answer. Web flow 60 N/mm at the root (booms take 8 kN), 93.75 N/mm at mid-span and 166.7 N/mm at the tip; root boom force 200 kN.

5.8 Combined open and closed section beams

Real wing sections mix closed cells with open parts: a torque box with an open trailing-edge section behind the rear spar, or a box with a cut-out for an undercarriage bay. The closed cell can carry a constant circulating flow; the open part cannot, because it has a free edge. The method cuts each closed cell once, finds the open-section flow everywhere, adds one constant per cell, and fixes it from moment equilibrium or from the twist condition.

You need: the cut-and-add method of the closed-section chapter, the boom jumps of the idealisation chapter, and the twist-rate integral.

Cutting a mixed section

A mixed section has one or more closed cells and at least one open wall ending in a free edge. The open wall starts from q = 0 at its free edge, like any open section. The closed cell has no free edge, so it is cut once in imagination. After the cuts the whole section is open, and the basic flow qb is found from the free edges and the cuts, using the Ixx of the whole section: every boom, open or closed, carries bending stress.

  1. Compute Ixx (and Iyy, Ixy if needed) for the whole section, open parts included.
  2. Cut each closed cell once. Start qb at every free edge and at every cut with zero.
  3. Sum the jumps at the booms (or integrate along walls that carry direct stress). Where an open wall joins a cell, its flow enters the cell at the junction boom.
  4. Add one unknown constant qs,0 to each closed cell. Open walls get no constant.
  5. Fix the constants: moment equilibrium if the load position is known, zero twist if the shear centre is wanted.

The equations

Sy xS = Σ (moments of qb about O) + Σ 2AR qs,0,R

xS is the distance of the load line from the moment point O (mm); AR is the area enclosed by cell R (mm²); the moment of a constant cell flow about any point is 2AR qs,0,R, anticlockwise positive.

dθ/dz = (1/2AR G) ∮_R (qb + qs,0,R) ds/t

the rate of twist (rad/mm), from cell R only; the open walls do not appear because they enclose no area.

The open walls do twist with the section; they just add almost nothing to the torsional stiffness, since their torsion constant is Σbt³/3 against the 4A²/∮ds/t of the cell. That is why the cell alone sets the rate of twist. With two cells sharing a wall, the twist integral for each cell uses the net flow in the shared wall, qb + qs,0,1 − qs,0,2, and the two rates are set equal, as in the multi-cell chapter.

When the load is not at the shear centre

If the load line is known, the moment equation gives the constant directly and the twist equation gives the rate of twist. If the shear centre is wanted, set the rate of twist to zero instead, solve for the constant, and put it into the moment equation to find xS. A load anywhere else twists the section by the amount the first approach gives. The trailing panels of a wing behind the rear spar move the shear centre slightly aft, because their small flows add a moment, but they hardly change the torsional stiffness.

5.9 Thin-webbed beams: Wagner's theory

Aircraft spar webs and fuselage shear webs are made so thin that they buckle in shear well below the design load. Buckling is not failure: the wrinkled web carries the load as a field of diagonal tension, held in place by the flanges and the vertical stiffeners. Wagner's theory of the complete tension field gives the stress in the web, the extra compression in the flanges, the load in the stiffeners and the angle of the wrinkles.

You need: shear flow in a web between two booms from the idealisation chapter, and the idea of plate buckling under shear.

A buckled web still carries shear

A flat web of depth d and thickness t between two flanges carries a shear W as a shear flow q = W/d, a stress τ = W/(t d). Pure shear is the same as tension on one 45° diagonal and equal compression on the other. A thin web cannot carry much compression: at a critical shear stress τcr it buckles into wrinkles along the tension diagonal. For a panel with simply supported edges, short side b and long side a:

τcr = ks π² E (t/b)²/[12(1 − ν²)],   ks ≈ 5.35 + 4(b/a)²

τcr is the shear buckling stress (N/mm²); E is Young's modulus (N/mm²); ν Poisson's ratio; t the thickness and b the shorter panel side (mm); a the longer side (mm); ks the shear buckling coefficient for simply supported edges.

For a 1 mm web τcr is a few N/mm², far below the working stress. After buckling the compression diagonal carries almost nothing, and the shear is carried by tension along the wrinkles at an angle α to the flanges. If the web is so thin that the buckling stress is negligible, the field is a complete tension field: that is Wagner's idealisation. A beam whose web carries the whole shear without buckling is a shear-resistant web beam, and its flows are those of the earlier chapters.

Wagner's complete tension field

Take a cantilever with tip load W, flanges a distance d apart, and vertical stiffeners at spacing b. Cut a vertical section through the web. The diagonal tension σt acts on a strip of width d sin α, so its vertical component is σt t d sin α cos α. That must equal W:

σt = 2W/(t d sin 2α)

σt is the diagonal tension stress (N/mm²); W is the shear force (N); t the web thickness and d the depth between flange centroids (mm); α the angle of the tension field to the flanges.

The horizontal component of the same field is W/tan α, a tension in the web that the two flanges must resist. Each takes half, as compression, on top of its bending load. At a distance z from the tip:

FT = W z/d − W/(2 tan α),   FB = −W z/d − W/(2 tan α)

FT and FB are the axial forces in the top and bottom flanges (N), tension positive, for a downward tip load with the top flange in tension; z is the distance from the load (mm).

On a horizontal cut the field pulls the flanges towards each other with a force σt t sin²α per unit length. The stiffeners hold them apart, so each stiffener is a strut:

P = σt t b sin²α = W b tan α/d

P is the compressive load in one stiffener (N); b is the stiffener spacing (mm).

The angle α and the flange bending

Statics does not give α. Wagner found it by minimising the strain energy of web, flanges and stiffeners: stiff flanges and stiffeners hold the field near 45°, flexible ones let it rotate. For a web of thickness t, flanges of area AF and stiffeners of area AS:

tan⁴α = (1 + t d/2AF)/(1 + t b/AS)

AF is the area of one flange (mm²); AS the area of one stiffener (mm²). Rigid flanges and stiffeners give tan α = 1, α = 45°.

Between stiffeners the field also pulls sideways on each flange, a distributed load σt t sin²α = W tan α/d per unit length. The flange acts as a continuous beam over the stiffeners and carries a secondary bending moment W b² tan α/(12d) at each stiffener and half that midway between. It must be designed for this as well as its axial load.

Real webs buckle at a non-zero τcr, so part of the shear is carried by the flat-web mechanism and part by tension. Semi-empirical incomplete tension field methods share the load between the two; Wagner's complete field is the limit, and it is conservative for the flanges and stiffeners.

Worked example

Question. A cantilever beam has flanges 400 mm apart, each 500 mm² in area, a web 1 mm thick, and vertical stiffeners of 200 mm² every 300 mm. It carries a tip load of 20 kN.   3. Angle: tan⁴α = (1 + 1 × 400/(2 × 500))/(1 + 1 × 300/200) = 1.4/2.5 = 0.56, so tan α = 0.8651 and α = 40.9°.

Exam traps

  • Calling web buckling failure. The buckled web carries the load in diagonal tension; failure is yielding of the web or failure of a flange or stiffener.
  • Forgetting the flange compression W/(2 tan α). Both flanges lose 11.6 kN of tension in the example: the compression flange is more loaded than bending alone says.
  • Using sin α cos α = 1 at 45°. It is 0.5; dropping it gives half the true σt, an unsafe error.

5.10 Torsion of thin-walled open sections

An open thin-walled section, an angle, channel, Z or stringer, has no closed loop for a shear flow to circulate round. It carries torque the way a flat strip does, with shear stress that changes sign through the thickness. Its torsion constant grows with the cube of the wall thickness, so it is very flexible in torsion. This chapter gives J = Σbt³/3, the surface stress and the rate of twist, and compares an open angle with a closed box made from the same strip.

You need: Bredt–Batho torsion of closed sections from the Bredt–Batho chapter, for the comparison.

Open walls twist strip by strip

Under a torque T, each wall of an open section behaves like a long thin rectangular strip. The shear stress runs along the wall, is zero on the mid-line and rises linearly to equal and opposite values at the two faces. The stresses on the two halves of the thickness form a couple with a lever arm of order t, which is why the stiffness is so small. Summing the strips, with the stress at the faces the same function of the rate of twist in every wall:

J = Σ b t³/3

J is the torsion constant of the open section (mm⁴); b is the length of one wall along its mid-line (mm); t is that wall's thickness (mm); the sum covers every wall. The 1/3 is the long-strip value; it is accurate when b is more than about ten times t.

Double every thickness and J rises eight times; double every length and it only doubles. An open section also warps: its cross-section does not stay plane. The formula assumes the warping is free; where a rib or a fixed end restrains it, axial stresses appear and the member is stiffer, which needs the theory of restrained torsion.

Stress and rate of twist

dθ/dz = T/(G J), θ = T L/(G J)

dθ/dz is the rate of twist (rad/mm); G is the shear modulus (N/mm²), about 27,000 for aluminium alloys and 79,000 for steel; L the length (mm); θ the total twist (rad).

τ = 2G n dθ/dz,   τmax = G t dθ/dz = T t/J

τ is the shear stress at a distance n from the mid-line of a wall (N/mm²); τmax is the stress at the faces of a wall of thickness t (mm). Every wall twists at the same rate, so τmax is largest in the thickest wall.

  • The largest stress is on the faces of the thickest wall, not the thinnest.
  • The stress is zero on the mid-line, so no constant shear flow exists: ∫τ dt across the wall is zero.
  • For a section built from one strip of uniform thickness, J = (total mid-line length) × t³/3, whatever its shape: an angle, a channel or a flat strip of the same length are equal in torsion.

Open angle against closed box

Take a 2 mm strip 120 mm long. Bent into a 60 mm × 60 mm angle it has J = 120 × 2³/3 = 320 mm⁴. Bent into a 30 mm × 30 mm square tube and closed with a seam, the same strip has A = 900 mm², ∮ds/t = 120/2 = 60 and J = 4A²/∮ds/t = 54,000 mm⁴, about 170 times stiffer, from identical material. The closed tube carries the torque as a constant shear flow at a lever arm of the whole section, the open angle only across the thickness.

This is why a wing torsion box is a closed cell between the spars, and why open stringers, which are stiff in bending, are never relied on for torque. A cut that opens a closed tube, a slot, an access hole without a doubler, turns it back into an open section and destroys its torsional stiffness.

Worked example

  4. (b) A = 30 × 30 = 900 mm², ∮ds/t = 120/2 = 60, J = 4 × 900²/60 = 54,000 mm⁴.
  5. q = T/2A = 10,000/1800 = 5.56 N/mm; τ = q/t = 2.78 N/mm².
  6. θ = 10,000 × 1000/(27,000 × 54,000) = 0.00686 rad = 0.393°.
  7. Ratio of stiffness: 54,000/320 = 169; ratio of stress: 62.5/2.78 = 22.5.

Exam traps

  • Applying Bredt–Batho to an open section. The 60 mm angle has no enclosed area; its J is 320 mm⁴.
  • Writing Σbt instead of Σbt³/3. The cube is the whole point.
  • Putting τmax in the thinnest wall. All walls twist at the same rate, so the thickest has the largest stress.
  • Leaving G in GPa and T in N·m. Convert both to N and mm; one slip alone makes θ 1000 times wrong.

Understand Why is an open section so much weaker in torsion than a closed one? 3 marks

Its stresses form a couple across the thickness t, a tiny lever arm, so J ∝ t³. A closed section carries a constant shear flow round the whole loop, at the scale of the section, so J ∝ A²t/s.

Apply A flat strip 100 mm × 3 mm, 1 m long, carries T = 50 N·m with G = 27 GPa. Find J, τmax and the twist. 4 marks

Apply Every wall of an open section is made three times thicker. How do J, τmax and the twist change for the same torque? 3 marks

Analyze Why does a wing torque box use a closed cell while its stringers are open sections? 5 marks

The closed cell has J = 4A²/∮ds/t, so a thin skin round a large area gives high torsional stiffness at low stress. Open stringers have J = Σbt³/3, so they would twist far too much; they are used for bending stiffness and to stabilise the skin, and the cell carries the torque.

Takeaways

  1. Shear flow q = τ t is set by the shear force, through the change of bending stress along the beam; it is zero at free edges and continuous round corners.
  2. The shear centre is a property of the section; a load anywhere else adds a torque equal to the load times its offset.
  3. A closed section is cut, solved as open, and given a constant qs,0 fixed by moments or by zero twist.
  4. Under pure torque one closed cell carries q = T/2A in every wall; several cells share the torque and twist at one rate.
  5. Booms carry direct stress and panels carry shear; a good idealisation reproduces the exact Ixx.
  6. In a tapered beam the inclined booms carry part of the shear, so the web carries S − M h′/h, not S.
  7. A buckled web carries shear as diagonal tension, which compresses the flanges, loads the stiffeners and bends the flanges between them.
  8. Open sections are a hundred or more times weaker in torsion than a closed section of the same material.

Understand Define shear flow and shear centre. Explain why a load through the shear centre bends a beam without twisting it, and derive the shear-centre position of a channel of uniform thickness. 10 marks

Points to cover. q = τ t and its origin in dM/dz; q(s) from a free edge; Resultant and shear centre; Offset load = load + torque; Channel: Ixx, qj, flange couple; e = 3b²/(h + 6b); Centroid against shear centre.

Model answer.

  1. Shear flow. In a thin wall the shear stress τ is taken as uniform through the thickness t, so the wall transmits a force per unit length q = τ t, in N/mm. It is set by the shear force: the bending stress changes along the beam at a rate set by dM/dz = S, and q balances that change on any piece of wall cut off from a free edge.
  2. For a section symmetric about x, measuring s from a free edge, with y the signed coordinate:
    q(s) = −(Sy/Ixx) ∫₀ˢ t y ds
q = 0 at a free edge; linear along a flange; parabolic along a web
  3. Shear centre. The flows q ds add up to a single force equal to the applied shear. Its line of action depends only on the section shape. The point through which the lines for every shear direction pass is the shear centre. A load through it produces bending only; a load elsewhere is equivalent to the same load at the shear centre plus a torque equal to the load times its offset, and the beam twists.
  4. Sketch: a channel with the web vertical and the flanges pointing right; the shear centre is drawn a distance e to the left of the web, on the horizontal axis of symmetry; flows run along the top flange towards the web, down the web and out along the bottom flange.
  5. Derivation of e for a channel with web h, flanges b and uniform t. With the flange second moment about its own axis neglected:
    Ixx = t h³/12 + 2 (b t)(h/2)² = t h²(h + 6b)/12
flange flow at the corner:  qj = (S/Ixx) t b (h/2)
flange force:  F = qj b/2 = S t b² h/(4 Ixx)
  6. The two flange forces are equal and opposite, a couple F h. Moments about the web mid-line:
    S e = F h   →   e = t h² b²/(4 Ixx) = 3b²/(h + 6b)
  7. Numbers: for h = 120 mm, b = 60 mm, t = 3 mm and S = 5 kN, Ixx = 1,728,000 mm⁴, qj = 31.25 N/mm, F = 937.5 N and e = 22.5 mm.
  8. Centroid against shear centre. The centroid of the same channel is x̄ = 15.0 mm from the web on the flange side. A load through the centroid is 37.5 mm from the shear centre and twists the channel with T = 5000 × 37.5 = 187,500 N·mm.
  9. Special cases: doubly symmetric sections have the shear centre at the centroid; singly symmetric ones on the axis of symmetry; sections whose walls meet at one point (angle, tee) at that point.
  10. Use in design: ribs, hinge brackets and control surfaces are arranged so that loads pass close to the shear centre, or the section is closed into a box, which can carry the resulting torque.
Understand Derive the expression for the shear flow in a thin-walled open section under shear forces Sx and Sy, and explain how the shear centre is found. Include sections with stiffening booms. 12 marks

Points to cover. Wall element equilibrium; ∂σ/∂z from Sx and Sy; General qs formula; Symmetric special case; Procedure and checks; Boom jumps; Moments for the shear centre.

Model answer.

  1. Set-up: a thin open wall of thickness t, mid-line coordinate s from a free edge, centroidal axes x and y, bending moments Mx and My, shear forces Sx and Sy with dMx/dz = Sy and dMy/dz = Sx. There is no axial load.
  2. Equilibrium of a wall element ds × dz along the beam: the direct stress σ on the two faces differs by (∂σ/∂z) dz and the shear flow on the two edges by (∂q/∂s) ds. With no axial load:
    ∂q/∂s + t ∂σ/∂z = 0
  3. The unsymmetrical bending stress gives ∂σ/∂z in terms of the shear forces:
    σ = [(My Ixx − Mx Ixy)/D] x + [(Mx Iyy − My Ixy)/D] y,   D = Ixx Iyy − Ixy²
∂σ/∂z = [(Sx Ixx − Sy Ixy)/D] x + [(Sy Iyy − Sx Ixy)/D] y
  4. Integrating from the free edge, where q = 0:
    qs = −[(Sx Ixx − Sy Ixy)/D] ∫₀ˢ t x ds − [(Sy Iyy − Sx Ixy)/D] ∫₀ˢ t y ds
  5. Special case: a section symmetric about x under Sy alone gives qs = −(Sy/Ixx)∫t y ds.
  6. Assumption: the shear acts through the shear centre, so the section does not twist; a load elsewhere adds a torsion flow.
  7. Procedure: (1) centroid and the three second moments; (2) pick a free edge and the direction of s; (3) write x(s) and y(s) for each wall; (4) integrate wall by wall, carrying the end value of one wall into the next; (5) check zero at the other free edge and that the walls sum to Sx and Sy.
  8. Worked check, the Z-section of the open-section chapter (web 80 mm, flanges 40 mm, t = 2 mm, Sy = 3 kN): Ixx = 341,333, Iyy = 85,333, Ixy = 128,000 mm⁴. The flow runs:
    top flange tip → corner:  0, 5.02, 4.02, −3.01, −16.07 N/mm
web peak at the neutral axis:  −48.21 N/mm
bottom flange back to 0 at its tip
  9. The sign changes inside each flange show the effect of Ixy; the web carries 3000 N and each flange zero net force.
  10. Stiffening elements: if booms carry the direct stress and the walls carry only shear, the integral is replaced by jumps:
    q₂ − q₁ = −[(Sx Ixx − Sy Ixy)/D] Br xr − [(Sy Iyy − Sx Ixy)/D] Br yr
  11. Shear centre: take moments of all the q ds about a convenient point (a corner where several walls meet removes them from the sum) and equate them to the moment of the shear force placed at the unknown shear centre, once for Sy (giving its x coordinate) and once for Sx (giving its y coordinate). For the stiffened channel of the chapter this gives 26.67 mm outside the web.
Understand State and derive the Bredt–Batho theory for torsion of a thin-walled closed tube: shear flow, shear stress, rate of twist and torsion constant. Apply it to a rectangular wing box. 12 marks

Points to cover. Assumptions; q constant; T = 2Aq; Twist rate and J; Circular tube check; Box numbers; Limits.

Model answer.

  1. Assumptions: thin closed wall, pure torque T, no axial stress, the cross-section keeps its shape and warps freely.
  2. Constant q. Cut a rectangular wall element with edges along the beam and round the section. With no direct stress, equilibrium along the beam requires the shear flows on its two longitudinal edges to be equal. So q is constant round the cell, even where t changes.
  3. Torque. A length ds at perpendicular distance p from any point O carries a force q ds with moment q p ds. Since p ds is twice the area of the thin triangle from O:
    T = ∮ q p ds = q ∮ p ds = 2 A q   →   q = T/(2A)
τ = q/t = T/(2 A t)
  4. A is the area enclosed by the mid-line; τ is largest where t is smallest.
  5. Rate of twist. The complementary energy of the wall per unit length is ∮q²/(2Gt) ds; equating its rate of change with the work of T per unit length, (1/2)T dθ/dz, or summing the displacements round the loop, gives:
    dθ/dz = (1/2A) ∮ q/(G t) ds = (T/4A²G) ∮ ds/t
J = 4A²/∮ds/t,   dθ/dz = T/(G J),   θ = T L/(G J)
  6. Check for a thin circular tube: A = πR², ∮ds/t = 2πR/t, J = 2πR³t, the polar second moment of a thin ring; τ = T/(2πR²t) agrees with T r/J at r = R.
  7. Numbers, the single-cell box of the Bredt–Batho chapter: mid-line 600 × 200 mm, skins 1.5 mm, spar webs 3 mm, T = 30 kN·m, G = 27 GPa. A = 120,000 mm², q = 30 × 10⁶/(2 × 120,000) = 125 N/mm.
  8. τ = 83.3 N/mm² in the skins and 41.7 N/mm² in the webs; ∮ds/t = 800 + 133.33 = 933.33; J = 6.171 × 10⁷ mm⁴.
  9. dθ/dz = 1.800 × 10⁻⁵ rad/mm = 1.03° per metre; over 4 m, 4.13°.
  10. Why the thin walls carry the stress: q is the same in every wall, so τ = q/t is largest where t is smallest; the designer therefore sizes the thinnest skin for strength and uses ∮ds/t, dominated by the thin walls, for stiffness.
  11. Check of the box numbers by the integral form: (1/2AG)∮q ds/t = 125 × 933.33/(2 × 120,000 × 27,000) = 1.800 × 10⁻⁵ rad/mm, the same rate as T/GJ.
  12. Limits: the theory loses accuracy as the wall thickens; warping restraint at a root rib stiffens the box; a buckled skin carries shear as a tension field; several cells need the multi-cell equations.
Understand Explain the structural idealisation of a stiffened wing section into booms and panels. Derive the effective boom area and show that the idealised section reproduces the bending stiffness. 10 marks

Points to cover. Why idealise; Booms and panels; Derivation of B; Stress ratios; Example and Ixx check; Use and limits; Walls effective and ineffective in bending.

Model answer.

  1. Why idealise: a stiffened skin has direct stress varying continuously round the section, and integrating the shear flow round dozens of stringers by hand is long. Most of the direct stress is in the stringers, spar caps and the skin next to them; the thin skin between them is good in shear.
  2. The idealised section: booms of area B at the stringer and spar-cap positions carry all the direct stress; panels of the real thickness t between booms carry only shear, constant along each panel.
  3. Derivation of the boom area. A skin panel of width b and thickness t has a linear direct stress from σ₁ at edge 1 to σ₂ at edge 2. Replace it by areas B₁ and B₂ at the edges that give the same moment about edge 2:
    σ₁ B₁ b = σ₂ t b (b/2) + (1/2)(σ₁ − σ₂) t b (2b/3)
B₁ = (t b/6)(2 + σ₂/σ₁)
  4. Each boom collects this term from every panel it touches plus its own stringer area.
  5. Ratios: a skin panel between two booms at the same height under bending has σ₂/σ₁ = 1 and gives t b/2 to each boom; a spar web from the top to the bottom boom has σ₂/σ₁ = −1 and gives t b/6; for pure axial load every ratio is 1.
  6. Example: a box 400 × 200 mm, spar caps 300 mm², mid-width stringers 150 mm², skins and webs 2 mm. Corner: 300 + (2 × 200/6) × 3 + (2 × 200/6) × 1 = 566.7 mm². Mid stringer: 150 + 2 × 200 = 550 mm².
  7. Check: Σ B y² = (4 × 566.7 + 2 × 550) × 100² = 3.3667 × 10⁷ mm⁴, exactly the Ixx of the real section (skins 1.6 × 10⁷, webs 2.667 × 10⁶, caps and stringers 1.5 × 10⁷). That is why the idealised section gives nearly the same stresses as the real one: it has the same bending stiffness by construction.
  8. Using it: boom stresses σ = M y/Ixx; panel flows from the jumps q₂ − q₁ = −(Sy/Ixx)B y at each boom, with qs,0 added in closed cells. For 15 kN in the example, each top and bottom skin panel carries 12.25 N/mm and each web 37.5 N/mm.
  9. Limits: the stress ratio depends on the load case (bending, axial, combined), so the booms change with it; the skin between stringers in compression may buckle and carry less than assumed; shear lag in wide skins puts more stress near the webs than the linear stress assumption.
  10. Walls effective and ineffective in bending: a wall whose skin is included in the booms carries shear only in the idealised model but is effective in bending through the booms; a wall left out of the booms is ineffective in bending. Both appear in ∮ds/t.

  6. Substituting: q₂ (2 × 35,343 × 0.6298 + 300,000) = 40 × 10⁶, so q₂ = 116.10 N/mm and q₁ = 73.12 N/mm.
  7. Shared spar: q₁ − q₂ = −42.98 N/mm, that is 42.98 N/mm in the sense of cell 2; τ = 17.2 N/mm².
  8. Outer walls: nose τ = 60.9 N/mm², cell 2 skins 77.4 N/mm², rear spar 58.1 N/mm².
  9. Torque shares: cell 1 carries 2A₁q₁ = 5.17 kN·m, cell 2 carries 34.83 kN·m.
  10. Rate of twist: (512.7 × 73.12 − 120 × 116.10)/(2 × 35,343 × 27,000) = 1.234 × 10⁻⁵ rad/mm = 0.707°/m; cell 2 gives the same.
  11. Check against one cell: had the rear cell alone carried the torque, q = 40 × 10⁶/300,000 = 133.3 N/mm. The nose cell takes a share, and the shared spar carries only the small difference.
  12. Physical check of the shared spar: it carries the difference of two nearly equal circulations, so it is lightly loaded in torsion; its stress of 17.2 N/mm² is far below the nose skin stress of 60.9 N/mm². In shear, by contrast, the spars carry most of the vertical load.
  13. Twist of the combined section compared with the nose cell alone: had the D-cell carried all 40 kN·m, q would be 566 N/mm and the nose skin stress 472 N/mm², far beyond the allowable. The second cell is what makes the torque box work.
  14. A matrix form, [K]{q} = {r}, with one equal-twist row and one torque row, is how a program would solve the same system for any number of cells.
Apply A wing box 600 mm × 200 mm has 400 mm² spar caps and 150 mm² stringers at quarter-widths of both skins; skins 1.5 mm, webs 2.5 mm. Idealise it, then find the boom stresses for Mx = 50 kN·m and the panel shear flows for Sy = 30 kN on the axis of symmetry. 13 marks

Points to cover. Stress ratios; Boom areas; Ixx check; Boom stresses; Jumps and symmetry; Panel and web flows; Checks.

Model answer.

  1. Data: a wing box 600 mm wide and 200 mm deep (mid-line). Spar caps 400 mm² at the four corners; stringers of 150 mm² at x = 150, 300 and 450 mm on both skins; skins 1.5 mm, spar webs 2.5 mm. Mx = 50 kN·m and Sy = 30 kN through the vertical axis of symmetry.
  2. Idealise: every skin panel is 150 mm wide with σ₂/σ₁ = 1; each web is 200 mm deep with σ₂/σ₁ = −1.
  3. Boom areas:
    corner: 400 + (1.5 × 150/6)(3) + (2.5 × 200/6)(1) = 400 + 112.5 + 83.3 = 595.8 mm²
stringer: 150 + 2 × (1.5 × 150/6)(3) = 375.0 mm²
  4. Second moment, checked against the real section:
    idealised: (4 × 595.8 + 6 × 375) × 100² = 4.6333 × 10⁷ mm⁴
real: skins 1.8 × 10⁷ + webs 3.333 × 10⁶ + caps 1.6 × 10⁷ + stringers 9 × 10⁶ = 4.6333 × 10⁷ mm⁴
  5. Boom stresses: σ = M y/I = 50 × 10⁶ × 100/4.6333 × 10⁷ = ±107.9 N/mm², the same in every boom on one skin.
  6. Shear: k = Sy/Ixx = 6.4748 × 10⁻⁴ N/mm³; each stringer jump is k × 375 × 100 = 24.28 N/mm and each corner jump k × 595.8 × 100 = 38.58 N/mm.
  7. Symmetry: the load is on the axis of symmetry and the middle stringer sits on it, so the panels either side of it carry equal and opposite flows of half its jump, 12.14 N/mm. No qs,0 is needed.
  8. Working outwards on the top skin, then down the webs:
    panel 300–450 mm: 12.14 N/mm
panel 450–600 mm: 12.14 + 24.28 = 36.42 N/mm
web: 36.42 + 38.58 = 75.00 N/mm
  9. The bottom skin mirrors the top. Check: 2 webs × 75.00 × 200 = 30,000 N = Sy; the web flow equals S/(2h) = 30,000/400 = 75 N/mm, as it must for a box symmetric about both axes.
  10. Stresses: web τ = 75.00/2.5 = 30.0 N/mm²; skin panels next to the spars τ = 36.42/1.5 = 24.3 N/mm².
  11. Sketch: booms as circles, panel flows as arrows: outward from the middle along the top, down both webs, inward along the bottom (for an upward lift on the box the senses reverse).
Apply A two-boom cantilever spar 3 m long tapers linearly from 300 mm deep at the root to 150 mm at the tip and carries 8 kN/m along its span. Find the boom force and the web shear flow at the root and at mid-span, allowing for the taper. 13 marks

Points to cover. Taper slope; S and M; Root: P, boom share, web flow; Mid-span; Check by dP/dz; Discussion.

Model answer.

  1. Data: cantilever spar 3 m long with two booms, depth 300 mm at the root and 150 mm at the tip, tapering linearly; a uniform load of 8 kN/m along the span. Find the boom force and the web shear flow at the root and at mid-span.
  2. Taper: the depth falls 150 mm over 3000 mm, h′ = 0.05; each boom slopes at 0.025.
  3. Loads at a distance x from the tip:
    S = w x,   M = w x²/2,   w = 8 N/mm
  4. Root (x = 3000 mm, h = 300 mm):
    S = 24,000 N,  M = 36,000,000 N·mm
P = M/h = 120,000 N in each boom
booms carry M h′/h = 36,000,000 × 0.05/300 = 6,000 N
web: Sw = 24,000 − 6,000 = 18,000 N,  q = 60.0 N/mm
  5. Without taper the root flow would be S/h = 80.0 N/mm: the taper relieves the web by 25 per cent.
  6. Mid-span (x = 1500 mm, h = 225 mm):
    S = 12,000 N,  M = 9,000,000 N·mm
P = 40,000 N
booms carry 9,000,000 × 0.05/225 = 2,000 N
web: 10,000 N,  q = 44.44 N/mm (S/h = 53.33)
  7. Check at the root by boom equilibrium, z outboard, dM/dz = −S:
    dP/dz = (dM/dz)/h − (M/h²)(dh/dz) = −24,000/300 − (36,000,000/90,000)(−0.05) = −60.0 N/mm
q = −dP/dz = 60.0 N/mm
  8. Why: the boom force acts along the inclined boom, so each boom has a component across the beam; for a beam deepest where the moment is largest both components act with the web against the load.
  9. With the uniform load the boom share falls faster than the shear towards the tip, so here the web flow is largest at the root; with a tip load, as in the chapter example, it is largest at the tip. Each station must be checked.
  10. General form for many booms: Sy,w = Sy − Σ Pz,r δyr/δz, with Pz,r = σr Br from the bending stress at the station; then the panel flows follow from the idealised-section sum with Sy,w in place of Sy.
  11. Boom stresses for 600 mm² booms: 200 N/mm² at the root and 66.7 N/mm² at mid-span, which shows that boom size is set at the root while the web may be critical elsewhere.
  12. Sign rule to remember: draw both boom forces as arrows along the booms (tension away from the section, compression towards it). If their components across the beam point the same way as the web shear resisting the load, subtract them from S; if they point the other way, add them.
  13. Assumptions: small taper angle (boom force along z ≈ boom force), booms carry all the direct stress, web carries only shear.

Analyze A wing section has one closed cell and two open trailing panels. Explain how many cuts and constants are needed and write the equations that fix them. Then find the flows, the rate of twist and the shear centre for the section given, with the load at 150 mm behind the front spar. 14 marks

Points to cover. Cuts and constants; Moment and twist equations; Ixx and qb; qs,0 from moments; Final flows; Twist; Shear centre.

Model answer.

  1. Set-up: one closed cell with booms 1 (0, 50), 2 (200, 50), 3 (200, −50), 4 (0, −50) mm of 600, 800, 800, 600 mm²; spar webs 2 mm, skins 1.5 mm; two open trailing panels from booms 2 and 3 to free booms 5 (300, 25) and 6 (300, −25) of 200 mm². Sy = 10 kN acting at x = 150 mm. G = 27 GPa.
  2. Cuts and constants: the trailing panels are open and start from zero at booms 5 and 6, so they need no cut and get no constant. The cell needs one cut (in the top skin 2–1) and one constant qs,0. In general: one cut and one constant per closed cell, none for open parts.
  3. Equations: with the load position known, moments fix qs,0 and the twist equation then gives the rate of twist:
    Sy xS = (moments of qb about O) + 2A qs,0
dθ/dz = (1/2AG) ∮ (qb + qs,0) ds/t   (cell walls only)
  4. Ixx = 7,250,000 mm⁴ (all six booms). Basic flows: trailing panels 6.90 N/mm; front spar −41.38 N/mm (downwards along s, so upwards physically); rear spar 62.07 N/mm upwards; skins zero.
  5. Moments of qb about the front-spar mid-point: 1,103,448 N·mm.
  6. Moment equation with the load at 150 mm: 10,000 × 150 = 1,103,448 + 40,000 qs,0, so qs,0 = 9.91 N/mm.
  7. Final flows: front spar 31.47 N/mm upwards, rear spar 71.98 N/mm upwards, skins 9.91 N/mm; trailing panels unchanged at 6.90 N/mm. Check: 100 × (31.47 + 71.98) − 2 × 172.4 = 10,000 N.
  8. Twist: ∮qb ds/t = 1,034.5, ∮ds/t = 366.67; dθ/dz = (1,034.5 + 9.91 × 366.67)/(2 × 20,000 × 27,000) = 4.324 × 10⁻⁶ rad/mm, anticlockwise, because the load is now behind the shear centre.
  9. Shear centre: setting dθ/dz = 0 gives qs,0 = −1,034.5/366.67 = −2.821 N/mm and xS = (1,103,448 + 40,000 × (−2.821))/10,000 = 99.1 mm. The load at 150 mm is 50.9 mm behind it, consistent with the sign of the twist.
  10. Two cells sharing a wall would add a second constant and an equal-twist equation; the shared wall carries qb + qs,0,1 − qs,0,2.
  11. Common errors: putting a constant into an open panel; leaving the open booms out of Ixx; using the twist equation of an open part.
Evaluate Evaluate open sections, single closed cells and multi-cell boxes for use as a wing torque box, using torsional stiffness, stress, fail-safety and weight. Support the argument with numbers. 14 marks

Points to cover. Criteria; Open section numbers; Closed cell numbers; Why closed wins; Multi-cell; Fail-safety and weight; Practical points and conclusion.

Model answer.

  1. Criterion: a torque box must carry the wing torque with low twist (aileron effectiveness, divergence and flutter margins) and acceptable shear stress, at low weight.
  2. Open section. J = Σbt³/3 depends on the cube of the thickness. For a 2 mm strip 120 mm long formed into a 60 mm angle under 10 N·m:
    J = 320 mm⁴,  τmax = 62.5 N/mm²,  twist 66.3° per metre
  3. Single closed cell. The same strip closed into a 30 mm square tube:
    J = 4A²/∮ds/t = 54,000 mm⁴,  τ = 2.78 N/mm²,  twist 0.393° per metre
stiffness ratio 169, stress ratio 22.5
  4. The closed cell wins because its shear flow acts round the whole enclosed area, with a lever arm of the section size, while the open section's stresses act across the thickness only.
  5. Multi-cell. Splitting a box with a middle spar barely changes the total enclosed area, so the stiffness changes little; the gain is in redundancy and in shorter, more stable skin panels. In the two-cell example of the multi-cell chapter, 20 kN·m twists the box 1.749° per metre and the shared wall carries only 17.4 N/mm.
  6. Fail-safety. If one cell of a multi-cell box is cracked, the remaining cell still carries torque by Bredt–Batho flow; a single cell cracked along its length becomes an open section and loses almost all its stiffness.
  7. Weight. For a given torque and allowable stress the closed cell needs t = T/(2Aτ), so its weight is set by the enclosed area, which is fixed by the aerofoil. The open section would need walls many times thicker to reach the same twist.
  8. Practical points: cut-outs for undercarriage or access turn a closed bay into an open one and need doublers or heavy ribs; open stringers are kept for bending stiffness and to stabilise the skin, not for torque.
  9. Shear centre: a closed box has its shear centre inside the box, near the line of the main loads; an open channel has it outside the web, so loads naturally miss it.
  10. Conclusion: the wing torque box should be closed, usually with two or three cells, with open stringers and ribs supporting the skin; open sections are evaluated for torsion only where they cannot be avoided (control-surface spars, stringer run-outs) and are kept short or closed locally.
Analyze Explain Wagner's complete tension field theory for a thin-webbed beam. For a cantilever with flanges 300 mm apart (350 mm² each), a 0.8 mm web, 150 mm² stiffeners at 250 mm and a 15 kN tip load, find α, σt, the flange loads 1 m from the tip, the stiffener load and the flange secondary moment. 13 marks

Points to cover. Buckling and diagonal tension; Buckling check; Angle; σt; Flange loads; Stiffener load; Secondary bending; Design consequences.

Model answer.

  1. Concept: a thin web loaded in shear has principal tension and compression on its two 45° diagonals. It buckles under the compression at τcr and carries further load as tension along the wrinkles, the diagonal tension field. Wagner's complete tension field assumes the buckling stress is negligible.
  2. Data: cantilever, flanges 300 mm apart (AF = 350 mm² each), web 0.8 mm, stiffeners of 150 mm² every 250 mm, tip load W = 15 kN, section 1 m from the tip; E = 70 GPa, ν = 0.33.
  3. Buckling check: ks = 5.35 + 4(250/300)² = 8.13; τcr = 8.13 × π² × 70,000 × (0.8/250)²/(12 × 0.891) = 5.38 N/mm², against a nominal τ = W/(t d) = 62.5 N/mm². The web buckles early, and the complete field is a fair model.
  4. Angle of the field:
    tan⁴α = (1 + t d/2AF)/(1 + t b/AS) = (1 + 240/700)/(1 + 200/150) = 0.5755
tan α = 0.8710,  α = 41.1°
  5. Diagonal tension, from vertical equilibrium of a vertical cut (σt t d sin α cos α = W):
    σt = 2W/(t d sin 2α) = 30,000/(0.8 × 300 × sin 82.1°) = 126.2 N/mm²
  6. Flange loads: bending W z/d plus half the horizontal field component W/tan α as compression in each:
    W z/d = 15,000 × 1000/300 = 50,000 N
W/(2 tan α) = 8,611 N
FT = 41,389 N (tension),  FB = −58,611 N (compression)
  7. Stiffener load, from the field pulling the flanges together:
    P = σt t b sin²α = W b tan α/d = 15,000 × 250 × 0.8710/300 = 10,887 N
stress 72.6 N/mm² in compression
  8. Flange secondary bending between stiffeners, under the distributed pull W tan α/d:
    M = W b² tan α/(12 d) = 226,820 N·mm at each stiffener,  113,410 N·mm midway
  9. Shear flow: the web still delivers q = W/d = 50 N/mm to the flanges on average; what changes is the route, through tension along the wrinkles instead of pure shear.
  10. Design consequences: the compression flange is loaded more than bending says; the stiffeners are struts and must be checked for column buckling; the flanges must carry the secondary bending; rivets along the flanges carry the field.
  11. If τcr is not small, part of the shear stays as flat-web shear, and incomplete tension field methods share the load; Wagner's field is then conservative for flanges and stiffeners.

Glossary

Basic shear flow
qb, the flow in a closed section after it has been cut, found as for an open section.
Boom
A concentrated area that carries all the direct stress of a stringer or spar cap and its share of skin.
Bredt–Batho theory
The theory of torsion of thin-walled closed sections: q = T/2A, constant round the cell.
Cell
One closed loop of walls in a section; a wing box with a middle spar has two.
Centre of twist
The point about which a section rotates under pure torque; it coincides with the shear centre.
Complete tension field
Wagner's idealisation of a buckled web that carries all its shear as diagonal tension.
Diagonal tension
Tension along the wrinkles of a buckled shear web, at angle α to the flanges.
Effective in bending
Said of a wall whose skin is counted in the booms or carries direct stress; one that is not is ineffective in bending.
Free edge
An unconnected edge of an open wall, where the shear flow is zero.
Idealisation
Replacing a stiffened section by booms carrying direct stress and panels carrying shear.
Panel
A length of skin or web between booms, carrying a constant shear flow in the idealised section.
Rate of twist
dθ/dz, the twist angle per unit length, in rad/mm or rad/m.
Shear buckling stress
τcr, the shear stress at which a flat web panel wrinkles.
Shear centre
The point through which a transverse load produces bending without twist; also called the flexural centre.
Shear flow
Shear force per unit length of wall, q = τ t, in N/mm.
Shear-resistant web
A web that carries its shear without buckling.
St Venant torsion
Torsion of a member whose cross-section warps freely; for open thin walls J = Σbt³/3.
Stiffener
A vertical member on a web that holds the flanges apart and anchors the tension field.
Taper
The change of depth or width of a beam along its span; it inclines the booms.
Torsion constant
J in dθ/dz = T/GJ; 4A²/∮ds/t for a closed cell, Σbt³/3 for an open section.
Warping
Axial displacement of points of a cross-section under torque, so that it does not stay plane.
