5.1 Concept of shear flow and shear centre

A thin-walled beam carries a transverse shear force S. That force does not sit at one point; it travels along the walls as a shear flow q, in N/mm. q equals shear stress τ times wall thickness t. The resultant of q acts through one point, the shear centre. If the load line misses that point, the beam twists as well as bends. Aircraft wings, fuselage frames and channel ribs are sized with this idea.

You need Unit 4 (second moments of area and unsymmetrical bending) and Unit 1 (wing and fuselage construction); see 5.2 for the full open-section calculation.

Shear flow is force per millimetre

Take a small element of a wall of thickness t (mm). The shear stress on it is τ (N/mm²). The force carried along one millimetre of wall length is q = τ t, in N/mm. This q is the shear flow. It is a force per length, not a stress and not a total force. For a vertical shear force S (N), q at a point depends on S (that is, on the rate at which the bending moment changes along the beam) and on the distance s along the wall measured from a free edge. At a free edge no shear can be carried, so q = 0 there. Moving along the wall, q builds up. A flange has nearly constant y, so q grows linearly along it and is largest at the web. A web has changing y, so q varies in a curve. At a junction the flow turns and continues.

q(s) = −(S_y / I_xx) ∫₀ˢ t y ds

q(s) is the shear flow in N/mm at distance s along the wall; S_y is the transverse shear force in N; I_xx is the second moment of area about the centroidal x-axis in mm⁴; t is the wall thickness in mm; y is the distance of the wall element from that x-axis in mm; s is the wall length measured from a free edge in mm. The sign gives direction; design uses the magnitude.

Resultant and shear centre

Every q element on the wall is a small force q ds. Adding these vectors round the section gives one resultant force. For a vertical shear S, the resultant is a vertical force of magnitude S whose line of action passes through the shear centre. For a singly symmetric section, the shear centre is where this line of action meets the axis of symmetry. A transverse load through the shear centre bends the beam without twisting. A load through any other point can be replaced by the same load at the shear centre plus a torque S × e, where e is the offset in mm. That torque twists a channel loaded at its centroid.

  • The shear centre is a property of the cross-section, not of the load.
  • For a doubly symmetric section, such as an I-section or a circular tube, the shear centre is at the centroid.
  • For a singly symmetric section, the shear centre lies on the axis of symmetry, but it may be offset along that axis from the centroid.
  • For a channel with flanges on one side of the web, the shear centre lies outside the web on the side away from the flanges.
  • Some syllabi call the shear centre the flexural centre; for the thin-walled beam model used here it is also the centre of twist.

Channel and lipped channel

For a plain channel of web depth h, flange width b and uniform thickness t, with flanges extending to one side, the offset e from the web centre line to the shear centre is given below. I_xx is the second moment about the horizontal centroidal axis. Measure e from the web centre line towards the side away from the flanges; a positive e in that direction means the shear centre lies outside the web, on the side opposite the flanges. The offset can be large even when the channel is small. A 120 mm deep, 60 mm wide, 3 mm thick channel gives e = 22.5 mm, while its centroid is only 15 mm from the web. Loading through the centroid therefore adds a torque. A lipped channel has small lips at the flange tips; the lips change I_xx and move the shear centre, and they also delay local buckling.

e = t h² b² / (4 I_xx) = 3b² / (h + 6b)

e is the shear-centre offset from the web centre line in mm; h is the web depth in mm; b is the flange width in mm; t is the uniform wall thickness in mm; I_xx is the second moment about the horizontal centroidal axis in mm⁴. The second form neglects the flange's own second moment.

The same integration method applies to any open wall: start at each free edge with q = 0, build q along the wall, take moments of q about a convenient point, and set the moment equal to S times the offset. The full distribution for an open section is worked in 5.2; closed cells are cut and balanced in 5.3.

Worked example

Question. A channel section has a web 120 mm deep, flanges 60 mm wide, uniform thickness 3 mm, and carries a vertical shear force S = 5 kN. Find the bending shear flow at the flange–web junction, and the distance e of the shear centre from the web centre line. Neglect the flange's own second moment about its centroid.

  1. Take x horizontal through mid-depth and y vertical. The web depth is h = 120 mm, flange width b = 60 mm, thickness t = 3 mm, and S = 5000 N.
  2. Second moment about the x-axis. Web: t h³/12 = 3 × 120³ / 12 = 432,000 mm⁴.
  3. Flanges: each area A_f = b t = 60 × 3 = 180 mm². Distance from x-axis is y = h/2 = 60 mm. Each A_f y² = 180 × 60² = 648,000 mm⁴. Two flanges give 1,296,000 mm⁴.
  4. Total I_xx = 432,000 + 1,296,000 = 1,728,000 mm⁴.
  5. At the top flange tip q = 0. At the web junction on the top flange, y = 60 mm. q_j = S t y b / I_xx = 5000 × 3 × 60 × 60 / 1,728,000 = 31.25 N/mm.
  6. Shear-centre offset: e = t h² b² / (4 I_xx) = 3 × 120² × 60² / (4 × 1,728,000) = 22.5 mm.
  7. The shear centre lies on the horizontal axis of symmetry, 22.5 mm from the web centre line on the side opposite the flanges.

Answer. The bending shear flow at the flange–web junction is 31.25 N/mm, and the shear centre is 22.5 mm from the web centre line on the side opposite the flanges.

Exam traps

  • Treating the centroid as the shear centre. In a channel they are far apart; a load at the centroid adds a torque S × e and twists the beam.
  • Using q = τ t with t in centimetres, or reporting q as a total force. q is in N/mm; multiply by a wall length to get force.
  • Starting the integration at a corner or junction instead of a free edge. For an open wall, q = 0 only at a free edge.
  • Taking the channel shear-centre offset towards the flanges. The shear centre lies outside the web on the side away from the flanges.
  • Confusing a singly symmetric section with a doubly symmetric one. The shear centre lies on the axis of symmetry, but not necessarily at the centroid.

The centroid is not the shear centre, so the load creates a torque. That torque twists the rib, adds unwanted shear flow, and can cause early buckling or distress at attachments. The load should pass through the shear centre, or the section should be made closed.

5.2 Shear of open section beams

An open thin-walled beam has a free edge where the shear flow must be zero. Moving along the wall, the shear flow grows because the bending stress changes along the beam. This chapter turns that idea into an integral, then into numbers for I, C, Z and lipped sections. The result gives the shear flow distribution and the shear centre, the point where a load causes no twist. Aircraft stringers, spar booms and fuselage frames are open or partly open sections, so this calculation is used in real stress offices.

You need: 5.1 for q and the shear centre, and Unit 4 for Ixx, Iyy and Ixy.

From a free edge to a shear flow

Cut the wall at a free edge and measure s along the wall centreline from that edge. Consider a small element of wall of length ds and thickness t. The direct stress σ changes along the beam axis z, so the element is not in equilibrium by itself. A shear flow q must act along the wall to balance the change. For bending about the x axis, dq/ds = −t ∂σ/∂z. With σ = Mx y/Ixx and dMx/dz = Sy, integrate from q = 0 at the free edge.

q = −(Sy/Ixx) ∫0s t y ds

q is the shear flow (N/mm); Sy is the shear force parallel to y (N); Ixx = ∫y² dA is the second moment about the centroidal x axis (mm⁴); t is the wall thickness (mm); y is the distance from the centroidal x axis (mm); s is the distance along the wall from the free edge (mm).

For an unsymmetrical section, the x coordinate also contributes. With centroidal axes, Sx and Sy as shear force components, and D = IxxIyy − Ixy², the same equilibrium gives the general open-section form. The sign of q follows the chosen direction of s. Starting at the other free edge reverses every sign but not the physical distribution.

q = −[(SxIxx − SyIxy)/D] ∫ t x ds − [(SyIyy − SxIxy)/D] ∫ t y ds

x and y are centroidal coordinates (mm); Iyy = ∫x² dA (mm⁴); Ixy = ∫xy dA (mm⁴); D = IxxIyy − Ixy² (mm⁸). Each integral runs from the same free edge.

Reading the distribution and finding the shear centre

In a symmetrical I-section under vertical shear, q is zero at both flange tips and largest in the web. A C-section has flange flows in opposite directions; their couple moves the shear centre away from the web. A Z-section is point-symmetric; its shear centre is at the centroid when the flanges are equal, but unequal flanges move it. A lipped channel adds short lips; the lip carries shear flow and shifts the shear centre further. If Ixy is nonzero, q can change sign along a flange, so a zero inside the wall is not a free edge.

  1. Choose one free edge and a positive direction for s. Mark where q = 0.
  2. Compute Ixx, Iyy and Ixy about the centroid.
  3. Integrate along the wall at short intervals, carrying q continuously through corners.
  4. Take moments of the shear flows about a convenient point O: MO = ∫(x dFy − y dFx).
  5. For vertical shear through the shear centre at x = ex, set MO = Syex and solve. For horizontal shear, use the corresponding y equation.

Closed loops cannot start at a free edge. They need a cut and a constant q0 round the loop; this is the method of 5.3.

Idealized stringer sections

A real wing section has stringers. Replace each stringer by a boom area Bi at (xi, yi), and let the skin carry only shear. For vertical shear and an open path, add the boom contributions crossed so far. This turns the integral into a short sum and is the form used in wing stress notes. The idealization itself is covered in 5.6.

q = qstart − (Sy/Ixx) Σ Bi yi

qstart is the shear flow at the start of the open path (N/mm); Bi is the boom area (mm²); yi is its centroidal height (mm); the sum is over booms already crossed. For unsymmetrical sections add the x term and use D.

Worked example

Question. A Z-section has a top flange 40 mm wide from x = 0 to 40 mm, a bottom flange 40 mm wide from x = −40 to 0, and a web 80 mm deep. All walls are 2 mm thick. It carries a vertical shear force Sy = 3 kN parallel to the web. Use thin-wall centreline dimensions. Compute the shear flow at 10 mm intervals from the top free edge round the section, sketch the distribution, and find the shear centre position.

  1. Take the origin at the centroid: web mid-height, x = 0, y positive upward. Ixx = 2×80³/12 + 2×(2×40)×40² = 85 333 + 256 000 = 341 333 mm⁴.
  2. Iyy = 2×2×40³/3 = 85 333 mm⁴. Ixy = 2×(2×40)×20×40 = 128 000 mm⁴. D = 341 333×85 333 − 128 000² = 1.2743×10¹⁰ mm⁸.
  3. Constants: Kx = SyIxy/D = 0.030134, Ky = SyIyy/D = 0.020089. At the web top, after the top flange, qstart = Kx(1600) − Ky(3200) = −16.071 N/mm.
  4. Top flange, s from the top tip: q = Kx(80s − s²) − Ky(80s). At s = 0, 10, 20, 30, 40 mm: q = 0, 5.022, 4.018, −3.013, −16.071 N/mm.
  5. Web, s downward from the web top: q = qstart − Ky(80s − s²). At s = 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80 mm: q = −30.134, −40.179, −46.205, −48.214, −46.205, −40.179, −30.134, −16.071 N/mm.
  6. Bottom flange, s leftward from the web bottom: q = qstart − Kxs² + Ky80s. At s = 10, 20, 30, 40 mm: q = −3.013, 4.018, 5.022, 0 N/mm.
  7. Full sequence from the top free edge, at 10 mm intervals: 0, 5.022, 4.018, −3.013, −16.071, −30.134, −40.179, −46.205, −48.214, −46.205, −40.179, −30.134, −16.071, −3.013, 4.018, 5.022, 0 N/mm. The sketch is the q against s plot.
  8. For the top flange ∫q ds = 0; for the bottom flange ∫q ds = 0; the web lies on x = 0. Thus the moment of all shear flows about the centroid is Mq = 0. For vertical shear through x = ex, Mq = Syex = 3000ex, so ex = 0 mm. Point symmetry gives ey = 0.

Answer. The shear flow sequence is 0, 5.022, 4.018, −3.013, −16.071, −30.134, −40.179, −46.205, −48.214, −46.205, −40.179, −30.134, −16.071, −3.013, 4.018, 5.022, 0 N/mm; the shear centre is at the centroid (0, 0).

Exam traps

  • Making q zero at the first flange tip you draw, not at the actual free edge. For a continuous Z wall the free edges are the two ends of the wall; the web-flange corner is not free.
  • Dropping Ixy for a Z or lipped section. A vertical shear then still has an x term, and q can reverse sign along a flange.
  • Reversing the sign of the integral. q = −(Sy/Ixx)∫t y ds: if s runs the other way, the sign of q flips. Keep one path and state it.
  • Using the centroid as the shear centre for every open section. C and lipped sections have offset shear centres.
  • Mixing units: S in N, t, y and s in mm, I in mm⁴, so q comes in N/mm. Using metres gives a factor-of-1000 error.

5.3 Shear of closed section beams

A closed section beam is a box: its wall forms one continuous loop, so shear flow has no free edge to start from. The trick is to cut the loop once in imagination, treat the result as an open section and compute the basic shear flow qb. The real section is qb plus a constant q0 round the loop. q0 is fixed by the twist condition: a shear force through the shear centre causes no twist. Aircraft fuselages, wing boxes and car bodies use this calculation to place the shear centre and avoid unwanted torsion.

You need Chapter 5.2 for qb of an open section and Chapter 5.4 for the zero-twist condition in Bredt form.

The cut and the constant q₀

In a closed thin-walled beam the wall forms one loop, so q cannot start from a free edge. Make an imaginary cut through one wall. The cut is a calculation device, not a physical slit. It turns the box into an open section, and 5.2 gives the basic shear flow qb in N/mm. Set qb = 0 at the cut and integrate round the mean line. Typical aircraft boxes use skins 1–3 mm thick and cell sizes 100–400 mm.

The real closed section carries q = qb + q0. q0 is a constant shear flow all round the loop, in N/mm. It replaces the connection removed by the cut. It is not found from statics of the cut section; it comes from compatibility.

q₀ = − [∮(qb/t) ds] / [∮(1/t) ds]

qb is basic shear flow (N/mm); t is wall thickness (mm); ds is element of mean-line length (mm); ∮ is one complete loop. For uniform t, q₀ = −(1/L)∮qb ds, where L is perimeter (mm).

For a shear load through the shear centre, the correct twist is zero: ∮(q/t)ds = 0. Substitute q = qb + q0 and solve. If t varies, keep t in both integrals. After q0 is added, check that the resultant of q equals the applied shear and acts through the shear centre. Torque-carrying closed sections are in 5.4.

Step-by-step for a closed box

  1. Choose any point on the mean line as the cut. Mark a positive direction round the loop.
  2. Compute Ix about the centroidal axis normal to the shear force, using mean-line dimensions.
  3. Start at the cut with qb = 0. Integrate qb = −(S/Ix)∫ t y ds along the path; y is distance from the centroidal axis.
  4. Integrate qb once round the loop. For uniform t, q0 = −(∮qb ds)/L.
  5. Add q0 to every qb. Check ∮(q/t)ds = 0.
  6. Take moments of q about a point O. The shear centre offset is e = MO/S.

The cut can be at a corner, mid-wall or on a flange. qb changes with the cut, but qb + q0 does not. At the cut the real shear flow is q0, not zero. For one axis of symmetry the shear centre lies on that axis; for two axes it is at the centroid. An unsymmetrical section needs Sx and Sy components and moments about two axes.

Symmetrical and unsymmetrical closed sections

Symmetrical boxes are the common wing-box and fuselage case. A vertical shear through the vertical symmetry axis gives q values equal and opposite on the two sides. q0 shifts the loop so twist is zero; the shear centre lies on the symmetry axis. One moment equation locates it along that axis.

An unsymmetrical closed section needs both shear components. Compute qb for Sx and Sy, add one q0 from the combined zero-twist condition, then take moments about two reference axes. The resultant of q is the shear force through the shear centre. If the real load is elsewhere, the offset times load is a torque; that torsion problem is in 5.4 and 5.5. The same moment check also confirms that the applied shear equals the resultant of q.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular closed box has mean-line width b = 200 mm, depth h = 100 mm and uniform skin thickness t = 2 mm. It carries a vertical shear force S = 10 kN through the mid-point of the left vertical wall. Taking a clockwise positive path from an imaginary cut at the mid-height of the left wall, find: (a) qb at the four corners; (b) q0; (c) the distance of the shear centre from the left wall. Use thin-walled theory.

  1. Centroid is at mid-depth. Ix = t h³/6 + b t h²/2 = 2×100³/6 + 200×2×100²/2 = 333,333 + 2,000,000 = 2,333,333 mm⁴. k = S/Ix = 10,000/2,333,333 = 4.286×10⁻³ N/mm³.
  2. Cut at mid-left. qb = 0 at the cut. On the upper left wall, ∫t y ds = 2∫₀⁵⁰ y dy = 2500 mm³. qb(top-left) = −k×2500 = −10.71 N/mm. By symmetry qb(bottom-left) = −10.71 N/mm.
  3. Top flange adds ∫t y ds = 2×50×200 = 20,000 mm³. qb(top-right) = −k(2500 + 20,000) = −96.43 N/mm. By symmetry qb(bottom-right) = −96.43 N/mm.
  4. Evaluate once round the loop: ∮qb ds = −3.214×10⁴ N. Perimeter L = 2(200 + 100) = 600 mm. q0 = −(−3.214×10⁴)/600 = +53.57 N/mm.
  5. Final q = qb + q0. Left corners: −10.71 + 53.57 = +42.86 N/mm. Right corners: −96.43 + 53.57 = −42.86 N/mm. Check: ∮(q/t)ds = 0.
  6. Take moments about mid-left wall O. Left wall has zero arm; top and bottom flange moments cancel. On the right wall, ∫q ds = q0×100 + ∫qb ds = 5357.1 − 10,357.1 = −5000 N. Moment magnitude = 200×5000 = 1.000×10⁶ N·mm. e = MO/S = 1.000×10⁶/10,000 = 100 mm.

Answer. qb = −10.71 N/mm at top-left and bottom-left, −96.43 N/mm at top-right and bottom-right; q0 = +53.57 N/mm; the shear centre lies 100 mm to the right of the left wall, at mid-depth.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting q0 and using the open-section qb as the real closed-section shear flow.
  • Assuming q = 0 at a chosen corner or cut in the real closed section; q0 shifts every value.
  • Mixing sign conventions: qb and q0 must use the same positive direction round the loop.
  • Using ∮q ds = 0 instead of ∮(q/t)ds = 0 when the wall thickness is not uniform.
  • Finding the shear centre from the centroid alone in an unsymmetrical closed section instead of taking moments of the q distribution.

5.4 Torsion of closed sections: Bredt-Batho theory

Close a thin wall into a loop and twist it. The torque is carried by a shear flow q that is the same all round the wall. This is the Bredt-Batho result. The wall stress depends on q and the local thickness. The twist rate depends on the area enclosed by the wall and on the integral of ds/t round the loop. Aircraft wing boxes and fuselage barrels use this idea. It turns a hard torsion problem into two or three numbers.

You need: shear flow q from 5.1, and the shaft torque-twist relation from Unit 2.

Constant shear flow round a closed loop

Take a single-cell tube. The mean line is the midline of the wall. It encloses area A (mm²). A pure torque T (N·mm) acts about the tube axis. In a thin closed wall the shear flow q (N/mm) is constant round the loop. There are no free edges, so the flow has nowhere to start or stop. It runs continuously round the wall.

Find q by taking moments about any point P inside the loop. On a length ds (mm) of wall the shear force is q ds. Its moment arm about P is r (mm). The small moment is dT = q r ds. The triangle with sides r and ds has area ½ r ds, so r ds = 2 dA. Integrate round the loop: T = q ∮ r ds = 2 A q. Hence q = T/(2A).

The wall shear stress is τ = q/t = T/(2At). t (mm) is the local wall thickness. τ (N/mm² or MPa) is largest where the wall is thinnest. A is the area enclosed by the mean line, not the outer contour. The formula assumes a thin wall, a closed loop, constant q, free warping and no buckling. Some syllabi call this Bredt's formula.

q = T/(2A), τ = q/t = T/(2At)

q is shear flow (N/mm); T is torque (N·mm); A is area enclosed by the mean line (mm²); t is wall thickness (mm); τ is wall shear stress (N/mm²).

Twist rate and equivalent torsion constant

The shear strain in the wall is γ = τ/G = q/(Gt). G (N/mm²) is the shear modulus. The twist per unit length dθ/dz (rad/mm) of a closed cell is the wall strain integrated round the loop, divided by twice the enclosed area: dθ/dz = (1/(2A))∮ γ ds. Put γ = q/(Gt) and q = T/(2A): dθ/dz = (T/(4A²G))∮(ds/t).

The integral ∮(ds/t) is taken along the mean line and is dimensionless when s and t use the same unit. For constant t and perimeter L, it is L/t. The equivalent torsion constant is J = 4A²/∮(ds/t). Then dθ/dz = T/(GJ), the same form as a solid shaft.

Check against a circular tube of mean radius R and thickness t. A = πR² and ∮ds/t = 2πR/t, so J = 2πR³t. The wall stress is τ = T/(2πR²t). The solid shaft formula τ = T r/J gives the same stress at r = R when J is taken as the polar moment of the thin ring. The symbols look alike; the meanings are different.

dθ/dz = (T/(4A²G))∮(ds/t),   J = 4A²/∮(ds/t),   dθ/dz = T/(GJ)

dθ/dz is twist per unit length (rad/mm); T is torque (N·mm); A is enclosed area (mm²); G is shear modulus (N/mm²); s is distance along the mean line (mm); t is wall thickness (mm); J is equivalent torsion constant (mm⁴).

How to use it, and when it fails

Use the formula in this order. Mark the mean line. Compute A from that line. Compute ∮(ds/t), splitting the loop into lengths of constant thickness. Find q = T/(2A). Find τ at each thickness. Find J and the twist rate. For a single cell the shear flow is the same in every wall.

  1. Draw the mean line through the middle of each wall.
  2. Measure A enclosed by the mean line.
  3. Split the loop where t changes and sum each length divided by its thickness.
  4. Use q = T/(2A) and τ = q/t.
  5. Use J = 4A²/∮(ds/t) and dθ/dz = T/(GJ).

The theory fails if the wall is not thin, if the loop is open, or if the wall buckles. An open section has a free edge and a different torsion law; see 5.10. A multi-cell box needs one shear flow per cell and equal twist between cells; see 5.5. Warping restraint and tapered walls need more than Bredt-Batho.

Worked example

Question. An elliptical wing box has a mean-line area A = 0.25 m², mean perimeter L = 1.8 m, skin thickness t = 2 mm and G = 27 GPa. It carries a torque T = 40 kN·m. Find the shear flow q, the shear stress τ in the skin, the equivalent torsion constant J, and the twist per metre of span.

  1. Convert: A = 0.25 m² = 0.25 × 10⁶ mm² = 2.50 × 10⁵ mm²; L = 1.8 m = 1800 mm; T = 40 kN·m = 40 × 10⁶ N·mm; G = 27 GPa = 27 000 N/mm².
  2. q = T/(2A) = (40 × 10⁶)/(2 × 2.50 × 10⁵) = (40 × 10⁶)/(5.00 × 10⁵) = 80 N/mm.
  3. τ = q/t = 80/2 = 40 N/mm² = 40 MPa.
  4. For constant t, ∮(ds/t) = L/t = 1800/2 = 900.
  5. J = 4A²/∮(ds/t) = 4(2.50 × 10⁵)²/900 = (2.50 × 10¹¹)/900 = 2.77777778 × 10⁸ mm⁴.
  6. dθ/dz = T/(GJ) = (40 × 10⁶)/(27 000 × 2.77777778 × 10⁸) = (40 × 10⁶)/(7.50 × 10¹²) = 5.3333 × 10⁻⁶ rad/mm.
  7. Per metre: dθ/dz = 5.3333 × 10⁻⁶ × 1000 = 0.005333 rad/m.

Answer. The shear flow is 80 N/mm, the skin shear stress is 40 MPa, the equivalent torsion constant is 277777778 mm⁴, and the twist is 0.005333 rad/m.

Exam traps

  • Using the outer contour area instead of the area enclosed by the mean line makes A too large and q and τ too small.
  • Applying Bredt-Batho to an open section: an open wall twists by the St Venant law, not by q = T/(2A); see 5.10.
  • Forgetting that q is constant only for a single closed cell under pure torque. A multi-cell section has a different q in each cell; see 5.5.
  • Taking ∮(ds/t) as perimeter/t when t changes: split the loop and add each length divided by its own thickness.
  • Mixing units: T in N·m with A in mm² gives q wrong by 1000; convert torque to N·mm first.

5.5 Single-cell and multi-cell structures

A wing torsion box is often split by a middle spar into two closed cells, and a fuselage is split by a floor beam the same way. Torque is shared between the cells, and because the cells are joined along a common wall they must twist at the same rate. One torque equation plus one equal-twist equation fix the two circulating flows q₁ and q₂, and from them the shear flow in every wall of the section.

You need: 5.4 for the single-cell torsion formula, and 5.3 for the cut-and-add-q₀ method used with a shear load.

Two cells share one wall and one twist rate

A single closed tube has one unknown shear flow q, fixed by one equation, T = 2Aq. A box with two cells has two unknowns. Call q₁ the flow circulating round cell 1 and q₂ the flow round cell 2, both in N/mm and both drawn in the same sense, say clockwise.

On the outer walls, cell 1 carries q₁ and cell 2 carries q₂. On the wall they share, the two circulations run in opposite directions along the same wall, so the net shear flow there is q₁ − q₂. A wing box with a middle spar and a fuselage with a floor beam both have exactly this layout.

T = 2A₁q₁ + 2A₂q₂

T is the applied torque in N·mm; A₁ and A₂ are the areas enclosed by cells 1 and 2 in mm²; q₁ and q₂ are the circulating shear flows in N/mm.

θ′ᵢ = (1 / (2AᵢG)) ∮ᵢ (q / t) ds

θ′ᵢ is the rate of twist of cell i in rad/mm; G is the shear modulus in N/mm²; t is wall thickness in mm; ds is an element of wall length in mm; the subscript i on the integral means the path goes round cell i only, using the net flow in any shared wall.

The cells are joined along the shared wall, so they cannot twist by different amounts: θ′₁ = θ′₂. That is the second equation. It is the one most answers leave out.

Setting up and solving the system

Write each cell's integral in terms of the flows. For cell i, ∮ᵢ(q/t)ds = Sᵢqᵢ − Σⱼ (Lᵢⱼ / tᵢⱼ) qⱼ, where Sᵢ = ∮ᵢ ds/t over the walls of cell i, a dimensionless number when ds and t are both in mm (500 for cell 1 and 400 for cell 2 in the example), Lᵢⱼ is the length of the wall shared by cells i and j, and tᵢⱼ is that wall's thickness. The minus sign is the difference rule again.

  1. Number the cells 1 to n and draw every flow q₁, q₂, … in one sense.
  2. Compute Sᵢ = ∮ ds/t for each cell over its own walls.
  3. Write θ′ᵢ = (Sᵢqᵢ − Σⱼ (Lᵢⱼ / tᵢⱼ) qⱼ) / (2AᵢG).
  4. Set θ′ᵢ = θ′ⱼ for each pair of neighbouring cells: n − 1 equations.
  5. Add the torque equation T = Σ 2Aᵢqᵢ: n equations for n unknowns.
  6. Solve, then report outer walls as qᵢ and a shared wall as qᵢ − qⱼ.

The system is linear, so a matrix solver does all the work: put the coefficients of q₁ … qₙ in a matrix K and the right-hand side in a vector r, then solve Kq = r. In MATLAB this is one backslash solve; on paper it is elimination. Some syllabi mark this step as a small programming exercise. The method does not change, only the solver does. A three-cell box adds one more flow and a second equal-twist condition.

Shear loads, shear centre and real sections

The same equations work when the section carries a transverse shear load instead of pure torque. Cut each cell to make the section open, get the basic flow q_b from 5.3, then add one constant per cell: q = q_b + q₀ᵢ. Equal twist relates q₀₁ and q₀₂, and a second condition — zero twist rate, or a known applied torque — closes the system.

Shear centre. Take the applied shear V through an arbitrary reference point, cut the cells and add q₀ᵢ, then choose the q₀ᵢ so that every cell has zero twist rate. Take moments of the resulting shear flows about the reference point; call the moment M. The load line of the shear flows, and hence the shear centre, lies at distance e = M/V from the reference point, on the side that makes the moments balance. Repeat for a second load direction if the shear centre position is needed in two coordinates.

Walls that take no direct stress. A plain web with no booms attached carries no bending stress, but it still carries shear, so its length and thickness still enter ∮ds/t. In the idealized section the booms carry only direct (bending) stress and are left out of the shear-carrying path; every skin, web and spar wall carries shear, so all of their lengths and thicknesses stay in ∮ds/t (see 5.6).

Where it appears. A wing box between front and rear spars, divided by a middle spar, is this problem exactly: the spars take the bending, the two cells share the torque from the aileron. A fuselage divided by a floor or cargo floor behaves the same way, with roof and floor twisting together under torsion.

5.6 Structural idealization: booms and panels

A real wing skin is stiffened by many stringers, so the direct stress varies continuously across the section and hand integration becomes long. Structural idealization replaces the section by a few booms that carry all the direct stress and thin panels that carry only shear. Each boom sits at a stringer position and is given an effective area that includes the skin working with it. The panels keep the real skin thickness. A hard integral becomes a short sum over booms. Shear-flow, shear-centre and torsion calculations for wing and fuselage boxes start this way.

You need 5.2 for the shear flow sum and Unit 4 for direct stress in bending.

Booms carry direct stress, panels carry shear

A wing box has skin, stringers, ribs and spars. Under bending, the stringers and the skin next to them carry almost all the direct stress. The thin skin between stringers carries very little direct stress but carries shear well.

The idealized section keeps this split. Each stringer plus its share of skin becomes one boom. A boom is a line area that carries only direct stress σ, in N/mm². The skin between booms becomes a panel of thickness t, in mm, that carries only shear flow q, in N/mm.

The boom has area B, in mm², and position (x, y) from a chosen origin. The panel has length b, in mm, and thickness t. The panel is treated as carrying a constant shear flow along its length. The whole section is then a ring of booms joined by panels.

This is also called the lumped-area method or stringer–skin idealization. It is used for wing boxes, fuselage frames and tail surfaces. The figure shows the chain from a real section to panel shear flows.

Effective boom area

B = Astringer + (t·b/6)(2 + σskin/σboom)

B is the effective boom area in mm²; Astringer is the stringer area in mm²; t is the skin thickness in mm; b is the sum of the two full skin widths working with the boom, b = b₁ + b₂, in mm; σskin is the direct stress in the skin and σboom the direct stress in the stringer, both in N/mm².

t·b is the skin area working with the boom; the factor 1/6 comes from the parabolic stress build-up in the skin. The term (2 + σskin/σboom) accounts for the skin on both sides of the stringer and for the skin working at a different stress from the stringer. When both work at the same stress the bracket is 3, so B = Astringer + t·b/2. For equal panels at pitch p, b = 2p, so B = Astringer + t·p.

A corner boom is shared by two panels. Use b = b₁ + b₂, so the skin contribution is (t/6)(b₁ + b₂)(2 + σskin/σboom). A boom on a straight run with skin on both sides uses b = b₁ + b₂, which is twice the pitch when the panels are equal.

  1. Mark every stringer position on the section.
  2. Give each boom b = b₁ + b₂: the two full skin widths working with it. For equal panels at pitch p, b = 2p.
  3. Choose the stress ratio. For a first check take σskin/σboom = 1.
  4. Compute B for every boom. For a corner boom use b₁ + b₂.
  5. Replace the real section by these booms and the skin panels.

Section properties and panel shear flow

Ixx = Σ B·y²

Ixx is the second moment of area about the horizontal centroidal axis in mm⁴; B is a boom area in mm²; y is the boom height from that axis in mm. The sum runs over all booms.

σ = M·y / Ixx

σ is the direct stress in a boom in N/mm²; M is the bending moment in N·mm; y is the boom height from the neutral axis in mm; Ixx is in mm⁴.

For a vertical shear force S, in N, cut the section at a panel and sum the boom terms from the cut to the point considered:

q = −(S/Ixx) Σ B·y

q is the shear flow in N/mm in the panel at the cut-off point; S is the vertical shear force in N; Ixx is in mm⁴; the sum is over the booms cut off between the free edge and that point.

The sum is over booms only, not over the panel. The panel thickness sets the shear stress, τ = q/t, but not q itself. In a closed box, add the constant q₀ from 5.3 so the twist is correct. In a symmetric box under vertical shear, q₀ is zero.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular wing box is 400 mm wide and 200 mm deep. It has four corner booms, each with stringer area 150 mm², and a skin 2 mm thick. Take σskin/σboom = 1. Find the effective boom areas, Ixx, and the panel shear flows for a vertical shear force S = 15 kN.

  1. Each corner boom is shared by a 400 mm flange panel and a 200 mm web panel. So b₁ = 400 mm and b₂ = 200 mm.
  2. Skin contribution = (t/6)(b₁ + b₂)(2 + σskin/σboom) = (2/6)(400 + 200)(2 + 1) = (0.3333)(600)(3) = 600 mm².
  3. Effective boom area B = Astringer + 600 = 150 + 600 = 750 mm².
  4. The four booms sit at y = +100 mm and y = −100 mm from the horizontal centroidal axis. Ixx = Σ B·y² = 4 × 750 × (100)² = 30,000,000 mm⁴ = 3.0 × 10⁷ mm⁴.
  5. Cut the top panel at its midpoint. Moving left to the top-left boom, the boom cut off has y = +100 mm. q = −(S/Ixx)B·y = −(15000/30000000)(750)(100) = −37.5 N/mm.
  6. The left web panel carries this q = −37.5 N/mm down to the bottom-left boom. That boom has y = −100 mm; its contribution (15000/30000000)(750)(100) = +37.5 N/mm cancels the first, so q after it is zero.
  7. Moving along the bottom panel gives q = 0, then the bottom-right boom adds +37.5 N/mm to the right web. The right web carries +37.5 N/mm. The top panel closes the loop at q = 0.
  8. The minus and plus signs appear because the loop is traced downwards on the left web and upwards on the right web. Both webs carry shear in the same direction, opposing the applied 15 kN, and 2 × 37.5 N/mm × 200 mm = 15 kN checks the balance.
  9. Magnitudes: each web panel carries 37.5 N/mm; the top and bottom panels carry zero.

Output

None

Answer. Each effective boom area is 750 mm², Ixx = 3.0 × 10⁷ mm⁴, and the vertical shear of 15 kN gives 37.5 N/mm in each web panel and zero in the top and bottom panels.

Exam traps

  • Counting the skin twice: the effective skin width belongs to the boom, but the panel still exists and still carries shear. Do not delete the panel.
  • Using one panel width for a corner boom shared by two panels. Use b₁ + b₂ for the two panels meeting at the corner.
  • Forgetting that the sum ΣB·y in the shear-flow formula runs over booms only. Adding panel areas to the sum gives a wrong q.
  • Mixing units: S in N, y in mm and I in mm⁴ give q in N/mm. Using S in kN gives a q that is 1000 times too small.
  • In a closed box, stopping at the basic q without adding q₀. The basic q alone does not satisfy zero twist.

Analyze In a symmetric four-boom box under vertical shear, why are the top and bottom panel shear flows zero? 5 marks

5.7 Shear flow in tapered beams and wings

A uniform beam's booms lose force from root to tip only because the bending moment falls. On a tapered beam the boom also slides inward as the depth shrinks, so its force changes for a second reason, and the inclined boom itself carries part of the transverse shear. The panel shear flow is then no longer S/h. That extra flow, the taper shear flow, decides how much shear a tapered spar web, a swept wing box or a tapering fuselage frame really carries.

You need: the boom-panel idealization from 5.6, the open-section shear flow rule from 5.2, and the bending stress M y/Ixx from Unit 4.

The boom force changes for two reasons

Idealize the section into booms that carry all the direct stress and thin panels that carry only shear (5.6). Boom i has area Ai and sits yi from the neutral axis. Its axial force is

Pi = σi Ai = Mx yi Ai / Ixx

Pi is the boom force in N, tension positive; Mx is the bending moment at the section in N·mm; yi is the boom distance from the neutral axis in mm; Ai is the boom area in mm²; Ixx is the second moment of area of the idealized section in mm⁴.

On a uniform beam only Mx varies with z, the distance along the span. On a tapered beam yi and Ixx change with z too, so dP/dz picks up extra terms from dyi/dz and dIxx/dz (in the two-boom spar these combine into one geometry term). Taper angles stay small, a few degrees, so cos of the slope is taken as 1.

One rule covers both effects. Cut the section and list the booms the cut removes. The shear flow in the panel at the cut is

q = −Σ (dPi/dz)

q is the shear flow at the cut in N/mm, positive in the direction of the cut; the sum runs over the booms removed by the cut; dPi/dz is the rate of change of boom force along the span in N/mm, and the minus sign means a boom that is losing force is handing that force to the panel.

This is the 5.2 integral written for booms. Freeze the geometry and it collapses to qb = −(Sx/Ixx)ΣAiyi, the ordinary bending flow. Whatever is left when the geometry moves is the taper flow.

Two-boom tapered spar

Take a spar of two equal booms, area A each, a distance h(z) apart, with the midpoint midway between them so each boom sits at y = ±h/2. Then Ixx = 2A(h/2)² = A h²/2.

Put that into the boom formula: P = M(h/2)A /(A h²/2) = M/h. The area cancels. The boom force depends only on the moment and the depth. Differentiate with respect to z:

dP/dz = (1/h)(dM/dz) − (M/h²)(dh/dz)

M is the bending moment in N·mm; h is the boom spacing in mm; z runs along the span in mm; dM/dz = −S, where S is the magnitude of the shear force in N, taken positive when the moment falls outward; dh/dz is the taper slope, dimensionless and negative when the depth shrinks outward.

Split the two terms. The first is the flow a uniform spar of the same depth would have: qb = −(1/h)(dM/dz) = S/h, in N/mm. The second is the taper flow, at the same cut and the same sign convention:

qtaper = (M/h²)(dh/dz)

qtaper is the extra shear flow in N/mm; the total in the panel is q = qb + qtaper.

When the depth and the moment both fall toward the tip, dh/dz is negative, so qtaper opposes qb and the web carries less than S/h. The reason is physical. The boom force acts along the inclined boom, so each boom contributes a transverse component P(dh/dz)/2. The two booms together carry P(dh/dz) of the applied shear and the web carries the rest.

Wings, swept boxes and the shear centre

A wing box tapers in depth from root to tip. For a linear taper dh/dz is the same at every station. qtaper = M h′/h² is usually largest inboard because M is largest there, but the h² denominator also matters for a strong taper.

Sweep alone does something different. A swept boom's force Pi = M yiAi/Ixx depends only on the vertical distance yi, so a boom that leans only in plan has the same yi and the same dPi/dz; sweep adds nothing to q = −ΣdPi/dz. What sweep does add is a chordwise component P dx/dz of the boom force. That produces a chordwise circulation of shear flow round the box and a torque about the span axis, which moves the line of action of the resultant, so the shear centre of 5.1 must be read with care. The vertical taper term comes only from the change of depth with z.

In fact a strongly tapered beam has no fixed shear centre. qb scales with S, but qtaper scales with M. The resultant position therefore depends on the ratio M/S at each station, which changes along the span and with the load case. A single shear centre is meaningful only when the taper term is small or happens to be proportional to S.

A tapering fuselage behaves the same way: stringers lean inward and their force has a transverse part. The examiner's check is always equilibrium: add the panel flows to the transverse boom components and match the applied shear force. Real sections that mix open flanges with closed cells are handled cell by cell in 5.8.

Worked example

Question. A cantilever spar of 2 m span is idealized as two booms, each of area 250 mm². The boom spacing tapers linearly from 200 mm at the root to 120 mm at the tip. A tip load of 20 kN produces a root bending moment of 40 kN·m. Find the taper shear flow and the bending shear flow in the web at the root, and the total shear flow there.

  1. Span L = 2000 mm; h = 200 mm at the root, 120 mm at the tip. Taper slope dh/dz = (120 − 200)/2000 = −0.04, with z outward.
  2. Ixx = A h²/2 = 250 × 200²/2 = 5.0 × 10⁶ mm⁴.
  3. Boom stress: σ = M(h/2)/Ixx = (40 × 10⁶ × 100)/(5.0 × 10⁶) = 800 N/mm².
  4. Boom force: P = σA = 800 × 250 = 200 000 N = 200 kN, the same as P = M/h = 40 × 10⁶/200.
  5. Shear force at the root: S = 20 kN, the tip load.
  6. Bending shear flow: qb = S/h = 20 000/200 = 100 N/mm.
  7. Taper shear flow: qtaper = (M/h²)(dh/dz) = (40 × 10⁶/200²)(−0.04) = −40 N/mm.
  8. Total: q = 100 − 40 = 60 N/mm in the web.
  9. Full derivative check: dP/dz = (1/h)(dM/dz) − (M/h²)(dh/dz) = (−20 000)/200 − 1000 × (−0.04) = −60 N/mm, so q = −dP/dz = 60 N/mm.
  10. Equilibrium check: web 60 × 200 = 12 kN; boom transverse components 2 × 200 000 × 0.02 = 8 kN; total 20 kN, matching the applied shear.

Answer. qb = 100 N/mm, qtaper = −40 N/mm (magnitude 40 N/mm, opposing the bending flow), total web shear flow q = 60 N/mm at the root.

5.8 Combined open and closed section beams

Real aircraft sections mix closed cells with open flanges. A wing box may have a closed torque cell and an open trailing-edge flange; a cut-out skin panel is open. The closed cell carries a constant shear flow q0 round its loop. The open flange has a free edge and carries only the basic flow qb. Cut each cell once, treat the skeleton as open, then use compatibility to find q0. This mixed case appears in wing ribs, fuselage frames and repair cut-outs. Only a few universities teach it.

You need: 5.3 for qb and q0, 5.2 for open walls, and 5.5 for cell compatibility.

Mixed sections are cut open cell by cell

A mixed section has one or more closed cells and at least one open wall. The open wall may be a flange, a stringer or a skin panel with a cut-out. The closed cell can carry a constant q0 round its loop. The open wall cannot: it has a free edge, so its shear flow starts at zero and follows qb from 5.2. The closed part also needs qb, found after cutting the cell.

  1. Cut every closed cell once at a chosen point. The section becomes an open skeleton.
  2. Find qb on the skeleton from the free edges, using the whole section second moment.
  3. Put one unknown q0 in each closed cell. The open walls get no q0.
  4. Impose zero twist on each cell, or equal twist on cells that share a wall.
  5. Add qb and q0 to get the final cell flows. Leave open walls as qb.

Compatibility fixes q0

q0 = −∮(qb/t) ds ÷ ∮(1/t) ds

qb is the basic shear flow on the cut cell in N/mm; t is wall thickness in mm; ds is an element of the cell perimeter; the integral goes once round the cell; q0 is in N/mm.

For one cell, zero twist gives ∮(qb+q0)/t ds = 0. For two cells i and j sharing a wall, write qi and qj for the redundant q0 flows of cells i and j. The shared wall carries qb+qi−qj. For cell i, θi = (1/(2AiG))[∮i(qb/t) ds + qi∮i(1/t) ds − qj∫shared(1/t) ds]. For cell j, θj = (1/(2AjG))[∮j(qb/t) ds + qj∮j(1/t) ds − qi∫shared(1/t) ds]. Set θi = θj. Ai and Aj are enclosed areas; G is shear modulus; qb is in N/mm, t in mm, ds in mm, and qi, qj are in N/mm. Under torque T, a single closed cell has Bredt flow q=T/(2A). An attached open boom carries direct stress from warping and adds no q0.

Worked example

Exam traps

  • Treating the mixed section as one closed tube and using Bredt for shear. The open flange has no enclosed area.
  • Leaving the open flange out of Ixx. Its direct stress changes qb everywhere.

  • Reversing the chosen cell loop and keeping the sign of q0. Reverse all signed cell flows with the loop.

5.9 Thin-webbed beams: Wagner's theory

A wing web is made thin to save weight, and a thin plate in shear buckles long before the beam fails. The wrinkled web cannot carry more than its buckling stress, so the extra shear travels as a diagonal tension band stretched between the booms. Wagner's theory turns that picture into two numbers: the diagonal tension factor k, which splits the applied shear between web and band, and the angle α of the band. It is the standard way to size spar webs, fuselage shear webs and any beam allowed to buckle below limit load.

You need: the boom–panel model and the shear flow q = S/d from 5.6, the shear flow idea itself from 5.1, and plate shear buckling from Unit 6, Plates, Buckling and Composite Structures.

What a buckled web does

A thin web carries shear as τ_av = S/(t d): S is the applied shear in N, t the web thickness in mm, d the web depth between boom centroids in mm. In the boom–panel model of 5.6 this is the shear flow q = S/d, in N/mm.

This holds only while the web stays flat. A plate in shear buckles at a critical stress τ_cr; Unit 6 gives the plate coefficients for a given panel shape. The web then wrinkles along one diagonal, close to 45° to the flanges, because pure shear has its principal tension on that diagonal.

Buckling is not failure. The wrinkled web keeps carrying shear but cannot climb much above τ_cr. The surplus travels as a diagonal tension band, which hands the load to the booms.

τ_av = S / (t · d)

τ_av is the nominal (pre-buckling) average shear stress in the web in N/mm²; S is the applied shear force in N; t is the web thickness in mm; d is the web depth between boom centroids in mm.

The diagonal tension band and the factor k

After buckling the web acts like diagonal straps in tension: the diagonal tension field, inclined at angle α to the booms. Wagner's theory describes it with one number, the diagonal tension factor k.

k = 1 − τ_cr / τ_av = (S − τ_cr · t · d) / S

k is the diagonal tension factor, a pure number between 0 and 1; τ_av = S/(t d) is the nominal web shear stress in N/mm²; τ_cr is the shear buckling stress of the web in N/mm².

The web stress is stuck at τ_cr, so the web carries S_w = τ_cr t d = (1 − k) S. The band carries the rest, ΔS = k S. A web that never buckles has k = 0: a shear-resistant web beam. A web with no shear capacity has k = 1: complete diagonal tension.

kHow the shear travels
0Web carries all of it; shear-resistant web beam
0 to 1Web at τ_cr, band carries the surplus
1Web carries nothing; complete diagonal tension

The band supplies ΔS through its tension σ_t: ΔS = σ_t t d sinα cosα. Since sinα cosα = ½ sin 2α, the product peaks at 0.5 at α = 45°, so a 45° band needs the least tension.

σ_t = ΔS / (t · d · sin α · cos α)

σ_t is the diagonal tension stress in the web in N/mm²; ΔS = k S is the shear carried by the band in N; α is the angle of the band to the booms in degrees; t and d are in mm.

Statics does not fix α. Compatibility between boom stretching and web stretching does: stiff booms hold the band near 45°, softer booms let it rotate towards the flange direction. Most aircraft webs sit between 30° and 45°.

Boom loads and the Wagner beam

The band is anchored to the booms. Over one bay of length L between ribs, each boom gains axial force P = ΔS L/d.

P = ΔS · L / d = k · S · L / d

P is the axial force added to one boom over the bay in N; L is the rib spacing (bay length) in mm; d is the web depth in mm; ΔS = k S is the band shear in N.

The total boom force still rises at S/d per unit length, because global equilibrium does not care how the shear is carried. Only the route changes: the web delivers (1 − k) S/d and the band k S/d. Add them once, never twice.

A beam of booms plus a web in diagonal tension is a Wagner beam; some syllabi call the method tension field theory. Wing and fuselage webs are often made thinner than the buckling limit on purpose: the booms and stringers are there anyway and can absorb the tension. Ribs and bulkheads anchor the field, and their spacing L fixes the boom force.

If the web stays below τ_cr at limit load, ignore all of this: use the shear flow of 5.2 and 5.3 and set k = 0.

Worked example

Question. A thin-webbed beam has a web 500 mm deep and 1.5 mm thick, and booms of area 300 mm² each. It carries 80 kN.   2. Web share: S_w = 60 N/mm² × 1.5 mm × 500 mm = 45 000 N = 45 kN.
  3. Band share: ΔS = 80 − 45 = 35 kN. Diagonal tension factor: k = 35/80 = 0.4375.
  4. sin 45° cos 45° = 0.5, so σ_t = 35 000 / (1.5 × 500 × 0.5) = 35 000/375 = 93.3 N/mm².

5.10 Torsion of thin-walled open sections

An open thin wall — an angle, tee, Z or channel — resists torque mainly by twisting each strip about its own centre line: St Venant torsion. Stiffness grows with the cube of wall thickness, so a 2 mm stiffener is very soft compared with a closed box. This chapter gives J = (1/3)Σbt³, surface shear stress and twist, and explains why a torsion box is a closed cell with open stringers riveted on.

You need Chapter 5.4 for the closed-box comparison and Unit 4 for the torque–twist relation.

Open walls twist strip by strip

An open section has no closed loop. A torque T (N·mm) cannot set up shear flow round a circuit, because none exists. Each strip twists about its mid-line. This is St Venant torsion.

Inside a strip shear stress is zero at mid-thickness and rises linearly to maxima at both surfaces. Those surface stresses form a couple over the thickness t, a short lever arm, so torsional stiffness is small.

J = (1/3) Σ b t³

J is the torsional constant (mm⁴); b is one wall length along its centre line (mm); t is that wall thickness (mm); the sum covers every wall.

The cube dominates. Doubling every t raises J eight times; halving t cuts J to one eighth. Adding width b helps only in proportion, so long thin walls are poor torsion members.

Open sections warp: angle legs sweep axially in opposite directions. The formula assumes free warping. A rib or fixed end restrains it, adds axial stress and stiffens the member; that is restrained torsion, not covered here.

Surface stress and rate of twist

τmax = T tmax / J

τmax is surface shear stress (N/mm²); T is torque (N·mm); tmax is the thickest wall (mm); J is the torsional constant (mm⁴). At distance n from mid-thickness, τ = T n / J.

θ = T L / (G J)

θ is total twist over length L (radians); L is length (mm); G is shear modulus (N/mm²), about 27 000 for aluminium and 79 000 for steel; J is the torsional constant (mm⁴). Twist per unit length is θ/L = T/(GJ) rad/mm.

  • τmax occurs in the thickest wall, not the thinnest. Stress is zero at mid-thickness, so a thick wall climbs further.
  • For an equal angle with legs b and thickness t, J = (2/3) b t³. Add flange and web separately for a tee. Open sections suit bending and buckling, not torsion.

A closed box versus the open angle

A 60 mm × 60 mm angle of 2 mm wall uses about 120 mm of strip along its centre line. Bend 232 mm of the same 2 mm strip into a square box with centre-line side 58 mm: the box uses about twice the strip. Its perimeter is s = 4 × 58 = 232 mm and enclosed area A = 58 × 58 = 3364 mm². Bredt–Batho theory (Chapter 5.4) gives J = 4A²t/s.

J = 4 A² t / s = 4 × 3364² × 2 / 232 = 390 224 mm⁴

A is area enclosed by the wall centre line (mm²); t is wall thickness (mm); s is centre-line perimeter (mm).

Now the whole perimeter carries constant shear flow q = T/(2A). At T = 200 N·m, L = 2 m, G = 27 GPa, the table compares the angle with the box.

  5. θ = TL/(GJ) = (200 000 × 2000)/(27 000 × 320) = 46.3 rad = 2653°, about 23 rad/m.
  6. Box: s = 4 × 58 = 232 mm, A = 58 × 58 = 3364 mm². J = 4A²t/s = 4 × 3364² × 2/232 = 390 224 mm⁴.
  7. Box twist: θ = 4 × 10⁸/(27 000 × 390 224) = 0.0380 rad = 2.18°.
  8. Compare: J ratio = 390 224/320 = 1219. Box stress τ = T/(2At) = 200 000/(2 × 3364 × 2) = 14.9 N/mm². Box uses about twice the strip.

Answer. Open angle: J = 320 mm⁴, τmax = 1250 N/mm², θ = 46.3 rad (2653°). Box: J = 390 224 mm⁴, θ = 0.0380 rad (2.18°), τ = 14.9 N/mm². At equal torque it is about 1219 times stiffer, with about twice the strip.

Exam traps

  • Feeding an open section to Bredt. For this angle the 60 mm × 60 mm outline gives 390 224 mm⁴ instead of 320 mm⁴ — wrong by about 1220.
  • Writing Σbt instead of Σbt³. The cube is the physics; the linear sum is a different quantity with different units.
  • Measuring b at the outer face, or counting a corner twice. Take b along the wall centre line; the error is small for 2 mm walls but visible at 4 mm.
  • Reporting τmax at mid-thickness or on the thinnest wall. It is zero at mid-thickness and largest on the thickest wall.
  • Unit slips: convert torque to N·mm (×1000) and G to N/mm² (×1000). Leaving G in GPa makes θ 1000 times too large (46 296 rad instead of 46.3 rad); leaving T in N·m makes it 1000 times too small (0.0463 rad = 46.3 mrad). Make both slips and the errors cancel, so the answer looks right in the wrong units. Always convert first.

Check yourself

Remember Write J for a thin-walled open section and define each symbol with its unit. 2 marks

J = (1/3) Σbt³, where b is wall length along its centre line (mm), t is wall thickness (mm), and J is in mm⁴. Sum over every wall.

Remember How does shear stress vary through the wall thickness of an open section under torque? 2 marks

It is zero at mid-thickness and rises linearly to equal and opposite maxima at both surfaces.

Understand Why does an open wall resist torque so poorly compared with a closed tube? 3 marks

The resisting couple inside a strip acts over t, a short lever arm, so stiffness scales as t³. In a closed tube shear flow runs round the full perimeter at large distance from the axis, and J depends on enclosed area squared.

Apply A flat strip 100 mm wide and 3 mm thick, 1 m long, carries T = 50 N·m with G = 27 GPa. Find J, τmax and twist. 4 marks

J = (1/3)(100)(3³) = 900 mm⁴. τmax = (50 000 × 3)/900 = 166.7 N/mm². θ = (50 000 × 1000)/(27 000 × 900) = 2.06 rad.

Apply The wall thickness of an open section is tripled everywhere. How do J, τmax and twist change at the same torque? 3 marks

J rises 27 times. τmax = Tt/J changes by 3/27 = 1/9, so falls to one ninth. Twist falls to 1/27.

Analyze A 200 mm length of the same 3 mm strip (twice the material of Q4) is closed into a square box with mean side 50 mm. Compare its twist with the open strip of Q4 at the same torque. 5 marks

Q4 strip: J = 900 mm⁴. Box: A = 50 × 50 = 2500 mm², s = 200 mm, J = 4 × 2500² × 3/200 = 375 000 mm⁴, ratio 375 000/900 = 417. Twist at equal torque falls from 2.06 rad to 0.00494 rad. The gain is at equal torque, not equal material; the box uses twice the material.

Understand What is warping of an open section, and when do the simple torsion formulas stop being accurate? 3 marks

Cross sections do not stay plane: points at leg tips move axially in opposite directions. The formulas assume free warping; a rib or fixed end restrains it, axial stresses appear and the member stiffens.

Analyze Why does a wing torsion box use a closed cell for torque while its stringers are open sections? 6 marks

The closed cell has J proportional to A²t/s, so a thin skin gives high stiffness, low stress and small twist. Open stringers have J proportional to bt³ and would twist too much, so they carry bending and delay skin buckling, not torque.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Real open-section members are restrained at ribs and fittings, so free-warping formulas over-predict twist. Vlasov theory adds a warping constant and a slower-decaying stress system; finite element codes use warping degrees of freedom. Search for non-uniform torsion of open thin-walled sections and warping constant Cw.

Takeaways

  1. Shear flow q is force per unit length of wall, so the wall thickness and the section shape decide both the stress and where the shear centre sits.
  2. A transverse load through the shear centre produces pure bending; a load anywhere else produces bending plus a twisting moment equal to the load times its offset from the shear centre.
  3. In an open section the shear flow is fully determined by statics alone, starting from zero at a free edge and integrating the direct stress gradient round the wall.
  4. A closed section carries an extra unknown constant q₀, so statics is not enough and a compatibility condition such as zero twist or a known shear centre is needed.
  5. Bredt-Batho theory makes the shear flow constant round a closed tube, and it depends on the enclosed area A, so a fat tube is far more efficient than a thin one.
  6. A multi-cell box is solved by cutting each cell, adding one q₀ per cell, and forcing all cells to twist at the same rate.
  7. Idealizing a wing into booms and panels replaces a difficult integral over a curved wall with a short sum over a few areas and lengths.
  8. A tapered beam needs an extra taper shear flow because the boom load changes along the span, and that change has a spanwise component.
  9. A buckled thin web is not a failed web: Wagner's diagonal tension field still carries shear, at the price of extra loads on the booms.
  10. An open thin wall resists torque only through St Venant torsion, with stiffness proportional to t³, so closing the same material into a box raises torsional stiffness by orders of magnitude.

Exam question bank

Remember and understand

Understand Define shear flow and shear centre. Explain, with a sketch, why a transverse load acting through the shear centre bends the beam without twisting it, and why a load offset from it adds a torque. 10 marks

Write q = τt with units N/mm and state that q acts along the wall centreline; Define shear centre as the point where the resultant of the shear flows acts, so the twisting moment about it is zero; Show that a load offset by e from the shear centre gives torque T = Pe, which the section must react by torsion; Sketch a channel section with the shear centre outside the web, on the opposite side to the flanges.

Understand Starting from a free edge, explain how the shear flow distribution in an open thin-walled section is obtained, and write the expression for q in terms of the applied shear force and the section second moments. 12 marks

State that q = 0 at a free edge because no shear can cross the free boundary; Write qs = −(SxIxx − SyIxy)/(IxxIyy − Ixy²)∫t x ds − (similar term in y) ds, and define Sx, Sy, Ixx, Iyy, Ixy, s; For a symmetric section simplify to q = −(Sy/Ixx)∫t y ds; Explain that the integral accumulates the direct stress gradient from the free edge; Note that the shear flow is statically determinate: no compatibility equation is needed.

Understand State and explain the Bredt-Batho theory for torsion of a closed thin-walled tube. Give the expressions for the shear flow, the shear stress, the rate of twist and the torsion constant. 12 marks

State the assumptions: thin wall, constant thickness round a loop, no warping restraint, shear flow uniform round the loop; Give q = T/(2A) and τ = T/(2At), defining A as the area enclosed by the wall centreline and t as thickness; Give dθ/dz = T/(GJ) with J = 4A²/∮(ds/t), and the total twist θ = TL/(GJ) over length L; Explain physically why the enclosed area, not the perimeter alone, dominates the stiffness.

Understand Explain structural idealization of a wing section into booms and panels. State what each part carries, and why the idealized section gives nearly the same answer as the real one. 10 marks

Replace each stringer plus its share of skin by a boom of area B carrying all direct stress; Replace the skin between booms by a panel carrying only shear flow, uniform along the panel; Justify the split: the skin is thin, so its direct stress resultant is small compared with the concentrated areas; Show the reduction of a wall integral to a sum over booms, and note that boom areas can include a correction for skin carrying direct stress.

Apply

Apply A thin-walled open channel section of uniform thickness t carries a vertical shear force S. Derive the shear flow round the section, sketch its distribution, and locate the shear centre. 13 marks

Take a cut at a free edge and integrate the direct stress gradient along the flanges and web; Evaluate q at each corner and along each wall, showing the parabolic variation in the web and linear variation in the flanges; Take moments of the shear flows about a convenient point in the web to find the line of action of the resultant; Report the shear centre position as a distance from the web, and check that it lies outside the section for a channel.

Apply A single-cell closed section is made by closing a channel with a thin wall. Using the cut-and-add method, find the basic shear flow qb, determine the constant q₀ from the condition that the section twists by the correct amount, and hence locate the shear centre. 14 marks

Cut the loop at a convenient point to make an open section; compute qb from the free-edge integration; Add a constant q₀ to all walls so the true shear flow is q = qb + q₀; Apply the condition of zero twist rate for a shear force through the shear centre: ∮(q/t)ds = 0, or the equivalent moment condition; Solve for q₀, then take moments of the resulting shear flows to find the shear centre; State the sign convention used for q and for s round the loop.

Apply A closed thin-walled tube of given mean perimeter, wall thickness and enclosed area carries a torque T. Find the shear flow, the maximum shear stress, the rate of twist and the total angle of twist over a given length. 12 marks

Compute A from the mean dimensions and apply q = T/(2A); Compute τ = q/t and identify where t is smallest for maximum stress; Compute J = 4A²/∮(ds/t), summing ds/t over the walls of differing thickness; Use θ = TL/(GJ) and dθ/dz = T/(GJ); state units of radians and G in N/mm².

Apply A two-cell wing box carries a pure torque T. Set up and solve the equations for the shear flows q₁ and q₂ in the two cells, and find the rate of twist. 14 marks

Write the torque equation T = 2A₁q₁ + 2A₂q₂ using the enclosed area of each cell; Write the equal-twist equation for the shared wall: (1/2A₁G)∮₁(q/t)ds = (1/2A₂G)∮₂(q/t)ds, with the shared wall contributing to both integrals; Solve the two simultaneous equations for q₁ and q₂, taking care with the sign of the shear flow in the common wall; Compute the common twist rate from either cell and check both give the same value.

Apply An idealized wing section has four booms of given areas and thin panels of given lengths. For a vertical shear force, compute the shear flow in each panel and the boom direct stresses. 13 marks

Confirm the section is open at a chosen free edge, so q starts at zero; Use qb = −(Sy/Ixx)Σ B y over the booms passed from the cut, summing contributions one boom at a time; Compute Ixx from the boom areas and their y distances, ignoring the thin panels; Tabulate the shear flow in each panel and check it returns to zero at the far free edge; Compute boom stresses from σ = Mxy/Ixx.

Apply A tapered beam or tapered wing carries a shear force and bending moment at one station. Derive and evaluate the additional taper shear flow caused by the spanwise component of the boom loads. 13 marks

Note that in a tapered beam the boom axial load P changes along the span, so the boom has a spanwise resultant component; Resolve this change of boom load into components along and normal to the beam axis; Add the normal component as an extra concentrated load at each boom and re-evaluate the shear flow it produces; Add this taper shear flow to the ordinary bending shear flow to get the total; Show that the taper contribution vanishes when the taper angle is zero.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze A section mixes one closed cell with an open flange outstand. Explain how many cuts and how many constants q₀ are needed, and write the compatibility conditions that determine them. 14 marks

Cut each closed cell once and also start the open part at its free edge; compute qb for the resulting open section; Introduce one constant q₀ per cell; the open part takes no constant; Write one equation per cell from the known twist rate (zero for a shear load through the shear centre, or T/(GJ) for a pure torque); Write the torque balance over all cells and the open walls to close the set; Solve the linear system, then superpose to get the true shear flow in every wall.

Evaluate Compare an open thin-walled section, a single closed box and a multi-cell box of the same material and roughly the same overall size for use as a wing torque box. Evaluate the torsional stiffness, the stress levels and the penalty of adding internal walls. 14 marks

Show that the open section resists torque only by St Venant torsion, with J = Σ(bt³/3), so stiffness scales with t³; Show that the closed single cell has J = 4A²/∮(ds/t), which scales with A² and is far larger for the same wall thickness; Explain that a multi-cell box spreads the shear flow over more walls, lowers the peak stress, but adds weight and manufacturing cost; Conclude why real wings use closed multi-cell boxes and why open sections appear only where torsion is small.

Analyze A thin web between two booms has buckled in shear. Using Wagner's diagonal tension field concept, explain how the web still carries shear and how the shear flows and boom loads are found. 13 marks

Describe the diagonal tension field: tension along one diagonal, compression along the other, at an angle α to the boom axis; State that the web carries shear by the diagonal stress field, so the effective shear flow depends on α; Write the equilibrium of a web panel to relate the applied shear flow to the diagonal tension and to the extra axial load transferred to the booms; Explain that the total boom load is the bending load plus the diagonal tension contribution, and that α is found from compatibility of the boom and web extensions; Note the design consequence: a buckled web is still a load path, but the booms must be sized for the extra axial load.

Evaluate Discuss the assumptions behind Bredt-Batho theory and behind boom-panel idealization, and evaluate the situations in which each one gives a misleading answer for a real aircraft wing. 13 marks

List Bredt-Batho assumptions: thin wall, closed loop, uniform shear flow, no warping restraint, elastic behaviour; List idealization assumptions: booms carry all direct stress, panels carry only shear, direct stress constant along a panel; Identify where they fail: thick or abrupt joints, cut-outs and access holes, restrained warping at wing root, post-buckled skin, tapering; Recommend when a finite element check or a more refined model is needed; Conclude that the closed-form results are design tools whose limits must be checked.

Glossary

Basic shear flow
The shear flow obtained in a closed section after it is cut open, before the constant loop shear flow is added.
Boom
A concentrated area in an idealized section that carries all the direct stress from bending.
Boom load
The axial force in a boom, equal to its direct stress times its area, used to find the taper shear flow.
Bredt-Batho theory
The thin-walled closed-tube torsion theory in which the shear flow is constant round the loop and equals T/(2A).
Closed section
A section whose wall centreline forms one or more closed loops, such as a box or a tube.
Compatibility condition
An equation on deformation, such as zero twist or equal twist in all cells, used to find the unknown constant shear flows.
Cut
An imaginary slit made in a closed loop so that the free-edge shear flow integration can be started.
Diagonal tension field
The pattern of diagonal tension and compression in a thin web that has buckled in shear, still able to carry load.
Enclosed area
The area bounded by the centreline of a closed wall, used in the Bredt-Batho formulas and in the torsion constant.
Flexural centre
Another name for the shear centre, stressing that it is the point where the bending shear flows have their resultant.
Multi-cell box
A closed section made of two or more adjacent closed loops sharing common internal walls, typical of a wing torsion box.
Open section
A section whose wall centreline does not close on itself, such as a channel, angle or Z-section.
Panel
A thin wall of an idealized section lying between two booms and carrying only shear flow.
Second moment of area
The geometric property I = ∫y²dA of a section about an axis, which sets the bending direct stress and the shear flow magnitude.
Shear centre
The point in a cross-section through which a transverse load must pass for the section to bend without twisting.
Shear flow
The shear force transmitted per unit length along a thin wall, equal to shear stress times wall thickness and measured in N/mm.
Shear resistant web
A web designed to carry shear without buckling, usually by being thick or closely stiffened.
St Venant torsion
Pure torsion of a bar with free warping, whose stiffness for a thin open wall is proportional to the cube of the wall thickness.
Taper
The gradual change of cross-section size along a beam or wing, measured as the angle between the outer surface and the beam axis.
Thin-walled section
A section whose wall thickness is small compared with its overall width and depth, so shear stress can be taken as uniform through the thickness.
Torsion constant
The geometric quantity J in T = GJ(dθ/dz), equal to 4A²/∮(ds/t) for a closed cell and Σ(bt³/3) for an open wall.
Twist rate
The angle of twist per unit length, dθ/dz = T/(GJ), in radians per millimetre.
Warping
Out-of-plane displacement of the cross-section when a non-circular section twists, which is restrained at a built-in end.
