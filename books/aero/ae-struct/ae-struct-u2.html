About this unit

The unit turns stored energy into a tool for finding deflections and redundant forces. It starts with the strain energy of a member under axial, bending and torsion load, then differentiates that energy to get a displacement (Castigliano’s theorem) and restates the same idea as virtual work with a unit load. 2.1 Strain energy in axial, bending and torsion loading

A stretched strut, a bent spar and a twisted control rod all store energy, like a wound spring. For a linearly elastic member the stored energy is one half the load times the deflection that load causes. Written in closed form for axial, bending and torsion loading, that single number gives deflections of trusses and beams without tracing the full elastic curve. Designers use it to compare competing layouts — a strut, a spar, a torque tube — long before any detail drawing exists.

You need: stress, strain, Young’s modulus, shear modulus, second moment of area, and the bending moment diagram of a beam.

Work done by the load is stored in the member

Load a member and it deflects. In a linearly elastic member the deflection grows in exact proportion to the load, and on removal the member springs back. The load has done work, and that work sits inside the material as strain energy U, measured in joules (1 J = 1 N·m).

Plot load against deflection. The graph is a straight line from the origin to the point (Δ, P). The work done is the area under that line.

U = ½ P Δ

P is the final load in newtons, Δ the deflection at that load in metres, U the stored strain energy in joules.

The ½ is there because the load does not start at P. It climbs from zero to P, so the average load along the way is P/2. A load of the same final size applied suddenly (constant force, no initial velocity) gives twice the deflection and stress, so it stores four times this energy. A true impact or dropped weight is a separate case. Exam questions assume gradual loading.

Strain energy is a scalar: no direction, never negative. It is quadratic in the loads, so energies of separate load cases do not simply add.

Typical questions: how much energy does this landing gear leg absorb? Does this spar store more energy than that one? Energy methods answer both without solving the deflection everywhere.

Three closed forms: axial, bending, torsion

Each mode is ½ × load × deformation, rewritten using section properties.

U = P² L / (2 A E)

P is the constant axial force (N), L the member length (m), A the cross-section area (m²), E Young's modulus (Pa); U comes out in joules.

U = ∫ M² dx / (2 E I)

M is the bending moment at station x (N·m), I the second moment of area about the neutral axis of bending (m⁴), x runs along the beam (m).

U = T² L / (2 G J)

T is the torque (N·m), G the shear modulus (Pa), J the polar second moment of area (m⁴). For a solid circular shaft of diameter d, J = πd⁴/32.

For a stepped bar or a shaft with several torques, split it into segments, take each segment as constant, and add: U = Σ Pᵢ²Lᵢ/(2AᵢEᵢ), and likewise for torsion. For a varying moment, integrate.

Standard beam results, resilience, and what is left out

Two beam answers are quoted so often that they are worth deriving once.

  1. Cantilever, tip load P, length L: measure x from the free end, so M(x) = Px.
  2. Then M²/(2EI) = P²x²/(2EI).
  3. Integrate from 0 to L: P²/(2EI) × L³/3 = P²L³/(6EI).
  4. Simply supported beam, central load P: with x measured from the left support, M = Px/2 (valid for 0 ≤ x ≤ L/2).
  5. Integrate 0 to L/2, then double: 2 × P²/(8EI) × (L/2)³/3 = P²L³/(96EI).

Both grow as P², so doubling the load quadruples the stored energy. Bending energy grows as L³ while axial energy grows only as L — a long beam is a spring, a long strut is not.

Modulus of resilience is strain energy per unit volume (J/m³); resilience itself is a total energy in joules. Proof resilience is the total energy stored at the yield load, the most the member can hold without permanent set. For axial loading the modulus of resilience is σy²/(2E).

Shear strain energy from the shear force V is deliberately left out here; it matters in short deep beams and thin webs. Torsion of non-circular and thin-walled sections needs shear flow and is treated with thin-walled sections.

Worked example

Question. (a) An aluminium alloy strut of length L = 1.2 m, cross-section area A = 250 mm² and E = 70 GPa carries a constant axial load P = 20 kN. Find its extension and the strain energy stored. (b) A steel cantilever 2 m long, E = 200 GPa, I = 8 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴, carries a tip load of 1 kN. Find its strain energy. Assume linear elasticity and gradually applied loads.

  1. Convert to base units: A = 250 mm² = 250 × 10⁻⁶ m², E = 70 GPa = 70 × 10⁹ Pa, P = 20 kN = 20 000 N.
  2. Extension of the strut: Δ = PL/(AE) = (20 000 × 1.2)/(250 × 10⁻⁶ × 70 × 10⁹) = 24 000/(1.75 × 10⁷) = 1.371 × 10⁻³ m = 1.371 mm.
  3. Energy from the area under the load-deflection line: U = ½PΔ = ½ × 20 000 × 1.371 × 10⁻³ = 13.71 J.
  4. Same answer from the formula: U = P²L/(2AE) = (20 000)² × 1.2/(2 × 250 × 10⁻⁶ × 70 × 10⁹) = 4.8 × 10⁸/(3.5 × 10⁷) = 13.71 J. Units: N²·m/(m²·N/m²) = N·m = J.
  5. Cantilever: U = P²L³/(6EI) = (1000)² × 2³/(6 × 200 × 10⁹ × 8 × 10⁻⁶) = 8 × 10⁶/(9.6 × 10⁶) = 0.83 J.
  6. Check the cantilever with the triangle rule: its tip deflection is Δ = PL³/(3EI) = 1000 × 8/(3 × 1.6 × 10⁶) = 1.667 × 10⁻³ m, so U = ½PΔ = ½ × 1000 × 1.667 × 10⁻³ = 0.833 J, the same as the formula.

Answer. The strut stores U = 13.71 J with an extension of 1.371 mm, and the cantilever stores U = 0.83 J.

Exam traps

  • Writing U = PΔ and dropping the ½. The load grew from zero, so the average is P/2; forgetting it doubles every answer.
  • Putting I into the torsion formula. J is polar: J = πd⁴/32 for a solid circle. It equals 2I only for a circular section, never in general.
  • Adding the strain energies of two separate load cases. Energy is quadratic in load, so two loads acting together give extra cross terms.
  • Feeding mm² or GPa straight into the formula. Convert A to m² (×10⁻⁶) and E to Pa (×10⁹), or you are out by 10³ or more.
  • Applying P²L/(2AE) to a stepped or tapered bar without splitting it into constant segments or integrating.

2.2 Castigliano's theorem

Castigliano's theorem turns a strain energy formula into a deflection. Write the strain energy U of a linear elastic body as a function of an applied load P, differentiate with respect to P, and the result is the displacement Δ at that load, along that load. It is the quickest route to the deflection of a beam, a frame or a truss once the strain energy is written down.

You need strain energy in axial, bending and torsion loading for U = P²L/(2AE), U = ∫M²/(2EI) dx and U = T²L/(2GJ).

Why the slope of U is a deflection

For a linear elastic body, ∂U/∂P = Δ at the load P, along P; ∂U/∂M = θ. This is Castigliano's second theorem.

∂U/∂P = Δ ∂U/∂M = θ

U is strain energy (J); P is load (N); Δ is displacement (m) along P; M is moment (N·m); θ is rotation (rad).

Load and deflection grow together, so U = ½PΔ. For an axial member, U = P²L/(2AE), so dU/dP = PL/(AE) = Δ.

An aluminium strut has L = 1.2 m, A = 250 mm², E = 70 GPa. With P in kN, U = 0.03429 P² J. At P = 20 kN, dU/dP = 0.06857 P = 1.371 J/kN, which is 1.371 mm. Directly, Δ = PL/(AE) = 1.371 × 10⁻³ m.

Read backwards, ∂U/∂Δ = P, Castigliano's first theorem.

The dummy load where no load acts

The theorem needs a load where you want the deflection. Usually there is none, so invent one.

  1. Apply a fictitious load Q at that point, along the wanted deflection. For rotation, use a fictitious moment Q.
  2. Write U with Q kept as a symbol.
  3. Differentiate ∂U/∂Q; the result contains Q.
  4. Set Q = 0 only after differentiating.

Set Q = 0 inside U first, and ∂U/∂Q = 0.

A reaction acts at a support; ∂U/∂R gives the support's own movement, zero on a rigid support. Used the other way round, ∂U/∂R = 0 is the compatibility equation that finds a redundant reaction R. For deflections, differentiate real loads or a dummy load at a free point.

Differentiate first, then integrate or sum

Writing U in full and then differentiating is slow. Differentiate under the integral sign instead. Each member or each slice of beam gives its own term.

Δ = Σ N (∂N/∂P) L/(AE)        Δ = ∫ M (∂M/∂P) dx/(EI)

N is the member force and ∂N/∂P its rate of change with the load P; M is the bending moment at x and ∂M/∂P its rate of change with P; L, A, E, I as before.

  • For a truss, ∂N/∂P is the member force caused by a load of 1 at the point, so the sum is exactly the unit load sum of the next chapter.
  • For a beam, ∂M/∂P is the moment caused by a load of 1 at the point, the m(x) of the unit load method.
  • With a dummy load Q, write M = M₀ + Qm, so ∂M/∂Q = m; then set Q = 0 and Δ = ∫ M₀ m dx/(EI).

Worked example

Question. A steel cantilever is 2 m long, E = 200 GPa, I = 8 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴, tip load P = 1 kN. Find U(P) and tip deflection.

  1. Measure x from the free end, so M = Px and U = ∫₀ᴸ P²x² dx/(2EI) = P²L³/(6EI).
  2. 6EI = 6 × 200 × 10⁹ × 8 × 10⁻⁶ = 9.6 × 10⁶ N·m², and L³ = 8 m³, so U = 8P²/(9.6 × 10⁶) = 8.333 × 10⁻⁷ P² J with P in newtons. At P = 1000 N, U = 0.833 J.
  3. Δ = ∂U/∂P = 2 × 8.333 × 10⁻⁷ P = 1.667 × 10⁻⁶ P m. At P = 1000 N, Δ = 1.667 × 10⁻³ m = 1.667 mm.
  4. Check: PL³/(3EI) = 1000 × 8/(3 × 1.6 × 10⁶) = 1.667 × 10⁻³ m.

Answer. U = 8.333 × 10⁻⁷ P² J (P in N), 0.833 J at 1 kN; tip deflection 1.667 mm downward.

Question. A stepped steel bar has E = 200 GPa and pull P = 50 kN. Segment 1: 0.4 m, 400 mm²; segment 2: 0.6 m, 200 mm². Find the extension.

Worked example 2

Question. A cantilever of length L = 3 m carries a uniformly distributed load w = 4 kN/m over its whole length; EI = 2400 kN·m². No load acts at the free end. Find the free-end deflection and the free-end rotation by Castigliano’s theorem with a dummy load and a dummy moment.

  1. Put a dummy downward load Q at the free end. Measure x from the free end: M = wx²/2 + Qx (hogging taken positive here), so ∂M/∂Q = x.
  2. Δ = ∫₀ᴸ M (∂M/∂Q) dx/(EI), then Q = 0: Δ = ∫₀³ (wx²/2)(x) dx/(EI) = wL⁴/(8EI).
  3. Numbers: wL⁴/(8EI) = 4 × 81/(8 × 2400) = 324/19 200 = 0.016875 m = 16.875 mm, downward.
  4. For rotation put a dummy moment C at the free end: M = wx²/2 + C, so ∂M/∂C = 1. With C = 0, θ = ∫₀³ (wx²/2) dx/(EI) = wL³/(6EI).
  5. θ = 4 × 27/(6 × 2400) = 108/14 400 = 0.0075 rad (0.43°), in the sense of the load.

Answer. Free-end deflection 16.875 mm downward and rotation 0.0075 rad; Q and C were set to zero only after differentiating.

Exam traps

  • Setting Q = 0 before differentiating makes ∂U/∂Q = 0.
  • Differentiating with respect to a reaction. It returns the support's own movement, zero on a rigid support; ∂U/∂R = 0 finds a redundant reaction.
  • Unit slips: J/N is m; J/kN is mm.
  • dU/dP of P²L/(2AE) is PL/(AE), never PL/(2AE).
  • Using U = ½PΔ with a deflection from a different load case.

2.3 Virtual work and unit load method

For a truss the work sum is a plain sum over members, with no integration along the members; for beams and frames the same principle becomes the integral ∫mM/(EI) dx. Put a unit load at the point where you want the deflection, in the direction you want it. That unit load creates its own member forces. The real loads create the real member forces. For each member, multiply the two forces by the member length and divide by axial stiffness. Add every member. The result is the wanted deflection. Aircraft trusses, roof trusses and crane jibs use this check.

You need Castigliano's theorem for the derivative idea and statics for joint equilibrium.

The principle

Virtual work compares two load cases on one truss. The real loads make member forces N. A unit load at the point and in the direction of the wanted deflection makes member forces n. The unit load is a probe: it does external work 1·Δ as it moves through the real deflection Δ. Inside each member it does internal work n·e through the real extension e. For a linear elastic member, e = NL/AE. Equating external and internal work gives the formula.

1 · Δ = Σ nNL/(AE)

1 is the unit load; Δ is the deflection at that point in the unit load's direction; N is the member force from the real loads; n is the member force from the unit load alone; L is the member length; A is the member cross-section area; E is Young's modulus; Σ runs over every member. Tension is positive for N and n; compression is negative.

Same sign gives positive Δ, in the unit load's direction. Opposite signs give negative Δ. Add every member; split a member with several segments into segments. The method is also called virtual work, the unit load method or the Maxwell–Mohr method.

The calculation table

  1. Solve the real truss by joint equilibrium for every member force N.
  2. Remove all real loads, apply a unit load at the point and in the direction wanted, and solve for every n.
  3. Tabulate each member: L, A, N, n, and nNL/A.
  4. Sum the last column and divide by E.

Use newtons and metres. Convert A from mm² by ×10⁻⁶; convert E from GPa by ×10⁹. If E is the same for all members, divide once at the end. A segmented member gets one row per segment. For beams the same recipe becomes ∫mM/(EI) dx (see deflection of beams using energy methods); a redundant truss uses the same sum to write its compatibility equation (see determinate and indeterminate trusses).

Worked example

Question. A plane truss has members AC and BC, with pin supports A and B on the same level and apex C above the midpoint. AB = 2 m and the apex height is 1.5 m. Each member has A = 200 mm² and E = 70 GPa. A vertical load of 10 kN acts downward at C. Find the vertical deflection of C by the unit load method.

  1. Half span = 1 m. Member length L = √(1² + 1.5²) = 1.8028 m. Height ratio = 1.5/1.8028 = 0.8321.
  2. At C, vertical equilibrium: 2N(0.8321) = 10 kN, so N = 10/(2 × 0.8321) = 6.009 kN. Compression, so N = −6009 N.
  3. Unit load of 1 kN downward at C, with no real loads: 2n(0.8321) = 1, so n = 0.6009. Compression, so n = −0.6009.
  4. AE = 200 × 10⁻⁶ × 70 × 10⁹ = 1.4 × 10⁷ N.
  5. Per member: nNL/AE = (−0.6009)(−6009)(1.8028)/(1.4 × 10⁷) = 4.65 × 10⁻⁴ m.
  6. Two members: Δ = 2 × 4.65 × 10⁻⁴ = 9.30 × 10⁻⁴ m = 0.93 mm.

Answer. The vertical deflection of C is 0.93 mm downward.

Exam traps

  • Mixing signs: call one compression and another tension in the same load case. Keep tension positive everywhere; write compression with a minus.
  • Finding n with the real loads still on the truss. n comes from the unit load alone.
  • Using millimetres for L and metres for Δ, or kN with mm². Convert to N and m first.
  • Forgetting a member or a segment. Every member with N and n contributes; a zero-force member gives a zero term, not a missing row.

2.4 Determinate and indeterminate trusses; method of joints

A plane truss is straight members joined at pins. If members plus reaction components equal twice the joints, joint equilibrium alone finds every force. If they are more, extra members are redundant: equilibrium leaves their forces free, and compatibility of deformation must supply one equation per redundant. Trusses appear in engine mounts, landing-gear bays and older wing ribs. The count decides what is solvable; the method of joints does the solving.

You need: equilibrium of concurrent forces and free body diagrams; the axial strain energy P²L/(2AE) from strain energy is used for the redundant truss.

Counting members, reactions and joints

Use a plane truss. Let m be members, r independent reaction components, j joints. Each pin joint gives ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0, so 2j equations. Unknowns: m member forces plus r reactions. The count is below.

D = m + r − 2j

m members; r reactions; j joints; D degree.

D = 0 means determinate: equilibrium gives every force. D > 0 means indeterminate to degree D: D extra equations are needed, one compatibility condition per redundant. D < 0 means a mechanism: the truss cannot carry general load. Count supports as pin r = 2 and roller r = 1. A fixed support has r = 3 but is rare in a plane truss. A roof truss with m = 7, r = 3, j = 5 has D = 7 + 3 − 10 = 0. This count is done before any force is calculated. It is necessary but not enough: the members must also be arranged so that no part can move. Two members that cross without a pin, or three reactions that are all parallel or all meet at one point, can give D = 0 and still leave a mechanism, so check the arrangement as well as the count.

Method of joints, tension positive

The method rests on the ideal truss: straight members joined by frictionless pins, loads applied only at the joints, and self-weight neglected or lumped at the joints. Each member is then a two-force member that carries pure tension or compression along its own axis. A member loaded between its joints also bends and needs beam analysis.

Assume tension positive: member force pulls its joint away. Negative is compression. Begin where at most two member forces are unknown.

  1. Find reactions from the whole truss as one free body.
  2. Choose a joint with at most two unknown member forces.
  3. Draw the joint alone. Replace each cut member by its force, arrow away for tension.
  4. Write ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0. Solve the two equations.
  5. Move to the next joint. Carry every solved force with its sign.
  6. Repeat until all member forces are found.

At an unloaded joint, two non-collinear members carry zero force. Three members with two collinear leave the third zero. Inspection works only when no external load or support reaction acts at that joint. The method gives forces, not deflections; deflections come from the unit load method.

If D > 0, one compatibility condition per redundant is needed. Remove a redundant, find the gap, close it; Section 3 does this for a three-bar truss. The method alone cannot start every joint in a redundant truss.

One redundant member: least work and checking a computer result

Call the force in the redundant member X. Cut it, and the truss left is determinate. Every member force is then N = N₀ + Xn, where N₀ comes from the real loads on the cut truss and n from a pair of unit pulls at the cut.

U = Σ N²L/(2AE)        ∂U/∂X = Σ N n L/(AE) = 0

U is the strain energy of all members including the redundant one; X is the redundant force; n is ∂N/∂X. The zero says the two faces of the cut do not separate.

  • Write N = N₀ + Xn for every member, the redundant itself having N = X and n = 1.
  • Set Σ (N₀ + Xn) n L/(AE) = 0 and solve for X.
  • Any member may be chosen as the redundant; the final forces are the same, only the arithmetic changes.

A matrix program gives every force and deflection at once. Check it by hand before trusting it: equilibrium at two or three joints, one member force by the method of joints, and one deflection by the unit load method. A small error in a support condition shows up at once in these checks.

Worked example

Question. A triangular truss has pin A and roller B on the same level, 4 m apart. Apex C is 2 m above midspan. A vertical load of 10 kN acts downward at C. Find the degree of indeterminacy and all member forces by the method of joints. Use tension positive.

  1. m = 3, r = 3 (pin 2, roller 1), j = 3; D = 3 + 3 − 6 = 0, and the triangle cannot fold, so the truss is determinate.
  2. Whole truss: ΣFy gives Ay + By = 10 kN. Symmetry gives Ay = By = 5 kN.
  3. Let θ be the angle each rafter makes with the bottom chord AB. Rafter AC rises 2 m over 2 m, length √(2² + 2²) = 2.828 m, so sin θ = cos θ = 2/2.828 = 0.7071.
  4. Joint A, vertical, tension positive: 5 kN + AC × 0.7071 = 0, so AC = −7.07 kN, compression.
  5. Joint A, horizontal: AB + (−7.07 kN × 0.7071) = 0, so AB = +5.00 kN, tension.
  6. By symmetry, BC = −7.07 kN, compression.
  7. Check at C: the two rafters push up with 2 × 7.07 kN × 0.7071 = 10 kN, balancing the load.

Answer. D = 0; AB = +5.00 kN tension, AC = BC = −7.07 kN (7.07 kN compression each).

Worked example 2

Question. Three aluminium bars meet at a pin D and hang it from a rigid frame. The middle bar is vertical and 2 m long; the outer bars are at 45° to it on either side, so each is 2√2 = 2.828 m long. All bars have A = 300 mm² and E = 70 GPa. A load P = 50 kN hangs from D. Find the force in each bar and the deflection of D.

  1. Count: m = 3, j = 4 (D and three frame pins), r = 6 (two at each frame pin). D = 3 + 6 − 8 = 1, so one bar is redundant. Choose the middle bar, force X.
  2. Cut truss: the two outer bars carry P alone. Vertical equilibrium at D: 2F cos 45° = P − X, so F = (P − X)/√2 in each outer bar.
  3. U = X² × 2/(2AE) + 2 × F² × 2.828/(2AE). Differentiate with ∂F/∂X = −1/√2: ∂U/∂X = [2X − 2 × 2.828 × F/√2]/AE = [2X − 4F]/AE.

  8. Compatibility check: each outer bar stretches 14 645 × 2.828/(2.1 × 10⁷) = 1.97 mm = 2.79 × cos 45°, the component of D’s movement along it.

2.5 Deflection of beams using energy methods

The unit-load method turns beam deflection into one integral. Put a unit load at the point and direction wanted. Find the moment m(x) it causes, combine with the real moment M(x): Δ = ∫₀ᴸ M m/(EI) dx. Aircraft stress offices use this to check wing spar or fuselage frame sag without solving the beam differential equation.

You need the unit-load recipe from virtual work and unit load method, and bending moment diagrams of beams.

One integral gives any deflection

Apply a unit load at the target point, in the target direction. Find m(x), the moment from that unit load alone, and M(x), the moment from real loads. Integrate their product along the beam.

1 · Δ = ∫₀ᴸ M m/(EI) dx

Δ is deflection at the chosen point in metres, in the unit-load direction; M(x) real bending moment in kN·m; m(x) moment from a 1 kN unit load in kN·m per kN; E Young's modulus in kN/m²; I second moment of area in m⁴; L span in metres.

Use kN and metres, so M m/(EI) is in metres. A downward unit load with a downward answer is positive. For rotation use a unit moment of 1 kN·m; the integral then gives slope in radians.

Split where the beam changes

For constant EI, 1/EI comes outside. For a stepped beam, or any change in I or loading, cut the beam and add pieces.

  1. Draw M(x); fix and write one sign convention.
  2. Remove real loads; apply the unit load at the target point and direction; draw m(x).
  3. Mark every x where I changes, or M or m changes form; these are segment limits.
  4. For each segment write M(x) and m(x), multiply, integrate, divide by that segment's EI.
  5. Add segments for total deflection.

Both diagrams are hogging: M = −2(3−x)² kN·m and m = −(3−x) kN·m per kN, so Mm is positive. Here w = 4 kN/m and EI = 2400 kN·m².

Check against known results

Compare with closed-form answers. Each assumes constant EI and gives deflection at the stated point.

  • Cantilever, uniformly distributed load w over the full length, free end: Δ = wL⁴/(8EI), downward.

Worked example

Question. A 3 m steel cantilever, fixed at x = 0, carries w = 4 kN/m. E = 200 GPa, I = 12 × 10⁻⁶ m⁴. Find free-end deflection by unit load and check.

2.6 Statically indeterminate beams

A beam with more reactions than equilibrium can solve is statically indeterminate. The force method turns one unknown reaction into the subject of a single equation. Remove that reaction and the beam becomes determinate; the real loads then push the freed point away from its support. Put the reaction back as an unknown force R; it pushes the point back. The two movements must cancel, and that one compatibility equation gives R. Equilibrium then gives every other reaction and the whole bending moment diagram. Aircraft frames, stiffened wing ribs and engine mount beams are built this way.

You need the unit-load integral from virtual work and unit load method and the beam deflection results from deflection of beams using energy methods.

The force method in five steps

It is also called the flexibility method. Count the reactions and the equilibrium equations. The difference is the degree of indeterminacy, and it equals the number of redundant reactions you must choose.

  1. Remove one reaction as the redundant R. The determinate beam left behind is the primary structure.
  2. Put only the real loads on it. Find Δload, the displacement at the freed point along R, using the unit-load integral.
  3. Put only a unit value of R (1 kN) at the same point. Find δ₁₁ there, in m per kN.
  4. The support does not move, so Δload + R δ₁₁ = 0. Solve for R.
  5. Use equilibrium for the other reactions, then draw the bending moment diagram.

Δload + R δ₁₁ = 0

Δload is the displacement at the freed point caused by the real loads, in m, positive along R; δ₁₁ is the displacement at the same point caused by a unit value of R, in m/kN; R is the redundant reaction in kN.

Fixed-fixed beam under a UDL

Span L, load w, constant EI. Take the two end moments as redundants. Symmetry gives MA = MB = M, so one unknown is left. The primary structure is the simply supported beam.

The UDL rotates each end by wL³/(24EI). The end moments rotate it back: ML/(3EI) from the moment at that end and ML/(6EI) from the far one. Zero end rotation gives wL³/(24EI) = ML/(2EI), so M = wL²/12. The moment is hogging at both ends and sagging wL²/24 at midspan, crossing zero at L(1 − 1/√3)/2 ≈ 0.211L from each end.

Sign discipline decides marks here. Pick one direction as positive for R and measure both displacements along it. Draw hogging above the axis and write it negative if sagging is positive.

Worked example

Question. A propped cantilever is fixed at A, propped at B, and is 4 m long. A uniformly distributed load w = 6 kN/m acts over the whole span; EI is constant. Find the prop reaction, the reaction at A, and the fixing moment at A, and sketch the bending moment diagram.

  1. Redundants: the fixed end gives three reactions and the prop one, four in all, against three equilibrium equations, so the degree of indeterminacy is 1. Choose R = prop reaction at B, upward.
  2. Primary structure: a cantilever fixed at A, carrying the UDL only.
  3. Deflection at B from the UDL, downward: wL⁴/(8EI) = 6 × 256/(8EI) = 192/EI m (EI in kN·m²).

  5. Compatibility, upward positive: −192/EI + R × 64/(3EI) = 0, so R = 192 × 3/64 = 9 kN upward (= 3wL/8).
  6. Vertical equilibrium: RA = wL − R = 24 − 9 = 15 kN upward.
  7. Fixing moment at A: MA = wL²/2 − RL = 48 − 36 = 12 kN·m hogging, which equals wL²/8.
  8. Moment at x from A, sagging positive: M(x) = −12 + 15x − 3x². Shear is zero at x = 15/6 = 2.5 m, where M = −12 + 37.5 − 18.75 = 6.75 kN·m sagging (= 9wL²/128).

2.7 Clapeyron's three-moment equation

A continuous beam runs over three or more supports. The bending moments at its supports are unknown, so equilibrium alone cannot find them. Clapeyron's three-moment equation links the moments at three consecutive supports in one line. Write it for each pair of spans and you get simultaneous equations for the whole beam. Aircraft floor beams, engine mounts and wing spar splices often use such continuous runs. Moment distribution reaches the same support moments by iteration.

You need statically indeterminate beams for releasing a redundant, and free bending moment diagrams of simply supported beams.

Three moments, one equation

Take three consecutive supports A, B, C. Span AB = L₁ and span BC = L₂, in metres. MA, MB, MC are the support moments, in kN·m. Use sagging positive and hogging negative. The free bending moment diagram is what the span would carry if it were a simple beam.

MA L₁ + 2MB(L₁ + L₂) + MC L₂ = −6(A₁x̄₁/L₁ + A₂x̄₂/L₂)

A₁, A₂ are the areas of the free bending moment diagrams of the two spans, in kN·m²; x̄₁, x̄₂ are the distances of their centroids from the outer supports A and C, in metres.

The right side is negative for downward loads. A simply supported end has M = 0; a fixed end has M unknown and needs one more equation.

The equation assumes the same EI along both spans, supports at one level with no settlement, and loads in the plane of the beam. A change of EI between spans or a sinking support adds terms to both sides.

Ready values and procedure

Two load cases cover most questions. For a uniform load w₁ on L₁ and w₂ on L₂, the right side becomes

−(w₁L₁³ + w₂L₂³)/4

w₁, w₂ are the uniform loads in kN/m on the two spans.

For a central point load P₁ on L₁ and P₂ on L₂, it becomes

−(3P₁L₁² + 3P₂L₂²)/8

P₁, P₂ are the point loads in kN at midspan of the two spans.

  1. Write the equation for each set of three consecutive supports.
  2. Put M = 0 at every simple end.
  3. Keep M unknown at every fixed or continuous support.
  4. Substitute the free-diagram area and centroid distance.
  5. Solve the simultaneous equations for the unknown support moments.

Measure x̄₁ from A and x̄₂ from C, not from B. A fixed end adds an unknown M. Treat the fixed end as a continuous support with an imaginary span of zero length and no load beyond it; writing the equation across that span gives the extra equation.

Worked example

Question. A continuous beam has two spans, AB = 6 m and BC = 4 m, with simple supports at A and C and a continuous support at B. A uniformly distributed load w = 10 kN/m acts on both spans. Find the bending moment at B.

  1. L₁ = 6 m, L₂ = 4 m, w = 10 kN/m. Simple ends give MA = 0 and MC = 0.
  2. For a simple span under UDL, A = wL³/12 and 6Ax̄/L = wL³/4. For AB: 10 × 6³ / 4 = 540 kN·m². For BC: 10 × 4³ / 4 = 160 kN·m².
  3. The equation becomes 2MB(6 + 4) = −(10 × 6³ + 10 × 4³)/4 = −(2160 + 640)/4 = −700.
  4. 20MB = −700, so MB = −35 kN·m.

Answer. MB = −35 kN·m, a hogging moment at support B.

Worked example 2

2.8 Continuous beams: moment distribution method

Moment distribution finds support moments of a continuous beam one joint at a time. Lock every joint, write fixed end moments, then unlock a joint: its unbalanced moment is shared by member stiffness, and half of each share is carried to a fixed far end. It checks computer results and shows how stiffness pulls moment toward the stiffer member.

You need statically indeterminate beams for compatibility, Clapeyron’s three-moment equation for a second route, and fixed end moments of single spans.

The idea and the sign convention

A continuous beam has more unknown support moments than equilibrium equations. The three-moment equation solves them together; moment distribution solves them one joint at a time. Imagine every internal joint is locked against rotation; simple supports remain pins. The loads produce fixed end moments (FEM) at member ends. These generally do not balance at a joint. Unlock the joint; it rotates until the moments at the joint sum to zero. The rotation spreads moment into the connected members. Re-lock the joint and move to the next joint. Repeat until carry-over is small. Use one sign convention: clockwise moments on member ends are positive. Hogging at both ends gives opposite signs, and sagging at both ends also gives opposite signs. Hogging at one end and sagging at the other give the same sign. Write the sign beside every FEM. The method is also called the Hardy Cross method.

Stiffness, distribution factor, carry-over

Member stiffness k is the moment at one end needed to rotate that end by one radian. For length L and flexural rigidity EI: k = 4EI/L with the far end fixed; k = 3EI/L with the far end pinned (held in position, free to rotate). At a joint, DFi = ki / Σk, where Σk is the sum of member stiffnesses there. Distribution factors at one joint sum to 1. A stiffer member takes a larger share. The propped cantilever FEM is wL²/8 at the fixed end for uniform load w over span L, and 3PL/16 for a central point load. The carry-over factor is ½ for a fixed far end and 0 for a pinned far end. Never carry over to a pinned end. Carry-over is why one pass may not be enough: a moment carried to a fixed end changes the balance there.

The examination table

  1. Columns at each joint: member, relative stiffness, DF, FEM, balance, carry over, final moment.
  2. k = 4EI/L with far end fixed, 3EI/L with far end pinned.
  3. DF = k/Σk at each joint; factors sum to 1.
  4. Write each FEM with its sign.
  5. Unbalanced U = sum of FEMs; apply balancing moment −U split by DF.
  6. Carry over ½ of each balance to a fixed far end; carry 0 to a pinned end.
  7. Repeat at other joints until carry-over is small.
  8. Add all contributions at each end for the final moment.
  9. Check: final moments at each joint sum to zero.

Takeaways

  1. A linear elastic member stores half the product of final load and deflection, U = ½PΔ, because the load grows from zero.
  2. Each loading type has one closed form: P²L/(2AE) for axial, ∫M²dx/(2EI) for bending, T²L/(2GJ) for torsion; they add for a member that carries several.
  3. Castigliano turns the derivative of energy into a deflection; a dummy load is set to zero only after differentiating.
  4. The unit load method is Castigliano with the derivative taken first: ∂M/∂P is the unit-load moment m.
  5. For a truss the unit load integral is a sum over members, Σ nNL/(AE); for a beam it is ∫Mm dx/(EI), split wherever EI or the moment law changes.
  6. Indeterminacy is not a dead end: remove a redundant, find the gap it leaves, and force the gap to close, by compatibility or by least work.
  7. Propped cantilever: R = 3wL/8, fixing moment wL²/8, peak sagging only 9wL²/128 because the fixing moment acts on every section.
  8. The three-moment equation links support moments span pair by span pair; moment distribution balances joint by joint and is the better hand method for four spans or more.

Model answer.

  1. Definition. When a load is applied gradually to an elastic body, the body deforms and the load does work. That work is stored in the material as strain energy U and is given back in full when the load is removed. Its unit is the joule, 1 J = 1 N·m.
  2. The work is a triangle. In a linearly elastic bar the extension grows in proportion to the load. At an intermediate load p the extension is δ, and a small increase dδ needs work p dδ.   3. U = ∫ p dδ, from δ = 0 to Δ, = area of triangle = ½ × P × Δ

Glossary

Axial rigidity
The product AE, resisting axial stretch of a bar.
Bending moment diagram
Plot of bending moment along a member, used inside the energy integrals.
Carry-over moment
Moment induced at the far end of a member when the near end is rotated.
Compatibility
Condition that displacements at a point must match the restraints actually present.
Complementary energy
The area above the load-deflection curve; for a linear elastic material it equals the strain energy.
Concentrated load
A load applied at a point, as opposed to a distributed load.
Determinate structure
Structure whose support reactions and member forces follow from equilibrium alone.
Distribution factor
Fraction of an unbalanced joint moment assigned to one member.
Fixed end moment
Moment developed at a clamped support of a single span under a given load.
Flexural rigidity
The product EI, resisting bending of a beam.
Free bending moment
Moment in a span as if it were simply supported, used with the three-moment equation.
Indeterminate structure
Structure with more unknowns than equilibrium equations, requiring compatibility.
Joint
Pin connection where truss members meet and where equilibrium is written.
Principle of superposition
For linear elastic structures, effects of separate loads add directly.
Redundant
An extra reaction or member force that is removed to make the structure determinate.
Stiffness factor
The moment needed to rotate a member end through one radian: 4EI/L with the far end fixed, 3EI/L with it pinned.
Strain energy
Energy absorbed by a body as it deforms elastically, recoverable on unloading.
Support moment
Bending moment at a continuous beam support, negative for hogging.
Sway
Lateral joint translation in a frame or truss that adds extra unknowns.
Torsional rigidity
The product GJ, resisting twist of a shaft.
Two-force member
Member loaded only at its ends by pins, so its force acts along its axis.
Unit load
A load of magnitude one applied in the direction of the displacement being sought.
Virtual work
Work done by a virtual force system moving through real displacements, or the reverse.
Zero-force member
Truss member carrying no force under the given load case, identified at a joint by inspection.
