2.1 Hydraulic systems: components, controllers and modes

A hydraulic system uses a confined liquid to carry force from a pump to the place where motion is needed. A pump draws fluid from a low-pressure reservoir and pressurises it, an accumulator stores a little of it under pressure for demand peaks, and valves send it to actuators, so a small pump can move a heavy surface. Aircraft use hydraulics for landing gear, brakes, flaps and flight controls because the fluid is stiff, the response is quick and the force per kilogram of equipment is high.

You need: the power-assisted and fully powered flight controls topic, for the surfaces these actuators move. Landing gear and brakes use this supply.

Pressure, force and supply layout

Pascal's law says pressure applied to a trapped liquid is transmitted equally in all directions. A pump creates pressure p; an actuator piston of area A receives force F. The working relation is:

F = p × A

F is force in newtons (N); p is pressure in pascals (Pa); A is piston area in square metres (m²).

Pressure is not force. At the same pressure, doubling the piston area doubles the force. A typical transport hydraulic system runs at 3000 psi, about 207 bar or 20.7 MPa. High pressure keeps the actuator and pipe sizes small, which saves weight.

In an open-centre system the pump output can return to the reservoir at low pressure when no user needs flow. In a closed-centre system the pump holds pressure and reduces its displacement when demand falls; a relief valve still protects the line. Transport aircraft usually use a closed-centre, variable-displacement arrangement so the pump does not waste power at idle.

The supply chain is reservoir → pumps → filter → accumulator → selector valve → actuator → return. The reservoir holds the fluid at low pressure, leaves room for expansion and lets air separate. On transport aircraft it is pressurised a little, by bleed air or by a bootstrap piston, so the pump inlet does not cavitate at altitude. The filter traps particles before they reach the valves.

Components in the flow path

An engine-driven pump is the main source in flight. An electric pump supplies ground checks, backup pressure or a second system. A hand pump gives a last-resort supply for a small essential load. The accumulator is a cylinder or sphere with hydraulic fluid on one side and nitrogen on the other, separated by a free piston, a bladder or a diaphragm. The nitrogen precharge is set on the ground; it is not the working pressure. The accumulator smooths pump pulses, covers short peak flows and gives a limited emergency supply.

A relief valve opens to return when pressure exceeds a set limit. A selector valve is commanded by the pilot and sends fluid to one side of the actuator. A servo valve moves a spool in proportion to an electrical or mechanical command for fine control. A hydraulic fuse closes when flow is much higher than normal, so a broken pipe does not empty the system. The actuator converts pressure into linear motion, and the return line carries low-pressure fluid back to the reservoir.

Filters sit on the pressure and return lines. A clogged filter opens a bypass valve so fluid can still flow, and a sensor warns the crew. Fluid quantity, pressure and temperature are watched because a leak often shows first as falling quantity, not as falling pressure. Transport aircraft use a fire-resistant phosphate-ester fluid (Skydrol is a common trade name); light aircraft and many military types use a red mineral-based fluid. The two must never be mixed, because each needs its own seal material.

Controllers and operating modes

Transport aircraft carry two or three independent systems, each with its own reservoir, pumps and users: green, blue and yellow on the A320 family, left, centre and right on many Boeing types. A power transfer unit (PTU) lets the pressure of one system drive a pump in another without mixing the fluids, and a ram air turbine can drive an emergency pump. A pump controller adjusts displacement or drive to hold pressure and unloads the pump when no user demands flow, which protects it from overheating. Mode logic selects which pumps and valves are active. The table is a common pattern; exact names and users differ by aircraft.

ModeSupplyTypical use
NormalMain engine-driven pump, electric pump on standbyAll hydraulic users
AlternateElectric pump or a second hydraulic systemEssential users only
EmergencyAccumulator, hand pump or another power sourceGear, brakes and minimum control

In normal mode the main pump supplies all users. In alternate mode a backup pump or a second system supplies essential users. In emergency mode the crew relies on stored pressure or a hand pump for a short list of loads. The crew monitors pressure, fluid quantity and temperature; a falling quantity with normal pressure points to a leak.

Worked example

Question. An actuator piston has diameter 5 cm and rod diameter 2 cm. The hydraulic system supplies 207 bar. Find the extension force and the retraction force.

  1. Convert pressure: 207 bar = 207 × 10⁵ Pa = 2.07 × 10⁷ Pa.
  2. Piston radius r = 0.05 m ÷ 2 = 0.025 m. Area A = πr² = π(0.025 m)² = 1.963 × 10⁻³ m².
  3. Extension force F = p × A = (2.07 × 10⁷ Pa)(1.963 × 10⁻³ m²) = 4.064 × 10⁴ N = 40.64 kN.
  4. Rod radius = 0.02 m ÷ 2 = 0.010 m. Rod area = π(0.010 m)² = 3.142 × 10⁻⁴ m².
  5. Annulus area = 1.963 × 10⁻³ − 3.142 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.649 × 10⁻³ m².
  6. Retraction force = (2.07 × 10⁷ Pa)(1.649 × 10⁻³ m²) = 3.414 × 10⁴ N = 34.14 kN.

Answer. The extension force is about 40.6 kN and the retraction force is about 34.1 kN.

Worked example 2

Question. A brake accumulator has a gas volume of 4.0 L and a nitrogen precharge of 1000 psi with no fluid in it. The system charges it to 3000 psi. The brakes stop working well below 1500 psi. Taking the pressures as absolute and the gas temperature as constant, find the fluid stored at 3000 psi and the fluid the brakes can use before the pressure falls to 1500 psi.

  1. Boyle’s law for the nitrogen: p₁V₁ = p₂V₂, so V₂ = p₁V₁ ÷ p₂.
  2. At 3000 psi the gas is squeezed to V = 1000 × 4.0 ÷ 3000 = 1.333 L.
  3. Fluid stored at 3000 psi = 4.0 − 1.333 = 2.667 L.
  4. At 1500 psi the gas has grown to V = 1000 × 4.0 ÷ 1500 = 2.667 L, so the fluid left is 4.0 − 2.667 = 1.333 L.
  5. Usable fluid between 3000 and 1500 psi = 2.667 − 1.333 = 1.333 L.
  6. Check: pV for the gas is 1000 × 4.0 = 3000 × 1.333 = 1500 × 2.667 = 4000 psi·L in every state.

Answer. The accumulator holds 2.67 L of fluid at 3000 psi, but only 1.33 L of it can be used before the pressure falls to 1500 psi. That is why an accumulator gives only a few brake applications after the pumps stop.

Exam traps

  • Do not treat the accumulator’s nitrogen precharge as the system working pressure. The precharge is set on the ground with no fluid in the accumulator; working pressure comes from the pump, and only part of the stored fluid is usable.
  • 3000 psi is about 207 bar, not 3000 kPa. 3000 psi is near 20.7 MPa.
  • Extension and retraction forces differ because the rod reduces the effective area on one side.
  • Open-centre and closed-centre describe idle flow behaviour, not whether the system is pressurised in flight.
  • A hydraulic fuse is a flow-limiting safety device, not an electrical fuse.

2.2 Pneumatic systems and typical air pressure system

Pneumatic power is compressed air or gas used to do work. On most aircraft it is bleed air, taken from an engine compressor and regulated, so the aircraft does not need a separate compressor for every job: that air drives cabin cooling and pressurisation, clears ice from the wing and intake, and spins the engine during start. A few aircraft also carry a high-pressure pneumatic power system of stored compressed air or nitrogen for gear, brakes and emergencies.

You need: hydraulic systems for how valves and actuators are driven, and cabin pressurisation and air cycle cooling for what happens to the air after it leaves the manifold.

Where the air comes from

An engine compressor raises the pressure and temperature of air before combustion. A small fraction of that air is taken out through a bleed port and sent to the aircraft. Pressure at the port depends on which compressor stage is tapped and on engine power. A low-pressure (LP) port gives cooler air at moderate pressure. A high-pressure (HP) port gives higher pressure but hotter air.

At low engine power the LP stage cannot supply enough pressure. The HP valve opens and HP air feeds the system. As power rises, LP pressure becomes enough; the HP valve closes and a check valve stops HP air from flowing backwards into the LP duct. So the source switches with power, and bleed is not always available from the HP stage.

Three sources feed the manifold: engine bleed (HP or LP), the auxiliary power unit (APU) on the ground and during engine start, and a ground cart connected at a ground service port. Before cooling, bleed air may sit at a few hundred kPa and a few hundred degrees Celsius.

Regulating and distributing the air

The first heat exchanger is the precooler. Fan air passes on one side and hot bleed on the other. The precooler lowers the bleed temperature, but the air leaving it is still hot enough to need heat protection on ducts. A fan-air valve controls the cooling flow.

Next is the pressure regulating and shut-off valve (PRV). The PRV regulates the downstream pressure to a design value, usually a few hundred kPa, and can close fully to shut off the bleed. It is controlled by a bleed monitoring computer that reads pressure and temperature sensors in the duct.

The regulated air enters a manifold: a network of ducts that runs along the fuselage. Check valves allow flow from a source into the manifold but block reverse flow, so a failed engine bleed cannot pressurise another engine's ducts. Isolation valves split the manifold into sections. If one section leaks, the crew can close an isolation valve and keep the other engine supplying the remaining users.

Protection is layered. For overpressure, the PRV closes and a relief valve opens if the PRV fails. For overheat, duct sensors trip a warning and close the affected valve. The crew sees the fault on the bleed panel or the electronic centralised aircraft monitor (ECAM), depending on the aircraft.

Users of the manifold

The manifold feeds several users, each through its own valve:

  • Air conditioning packs take bleed air and cool it; see air cycle cooling.
  • Wing and engine anti-ice use hot bleed air to keep surfaces above freezing; see ice and rain protection.
  • Engine starting uses bleed air to turn the air turbine starter; see engine starting.
  • Hydraulic reservoirs and potable water tanks are pressurised with bleed air, to stop pump cavitation and to deliver water.
  • On some types door seals are inflated with bleed air.

Cabin pressure is not a bleed user of its own: the packs feed the cabin and the outflow valve sets its pressure.

On a typical twin-engine aircraft, either engine can supply the whole manifold through the isolation valves. The APU supplies the manifold on the ground when engines are off; its air joins downstream of the precooler and the PRV, so no engine fan air is needed to cool it. This shared manifold is why a single bleed leak can affect several systems, and why isolation valves matter.

Ducts are sized from the mass flow they must carry, using the continuity equation:

ṁ = ρ × A × v

ṁ is mass flow in kg/s, ρ is air density in kg/m³, A is duct cross-section area in m², and v is mean velocity in m/s.

High-pressure pneumatic power systems

Some turboprops and older transports use a separate pneumatic power system at about 1000 to 3300 psi for landing gear, wheel brakes, nose-wheel steering and propeller braking. Many more aircraft carry only an emergency nitrogen or air bottle that can blow the gear down or apply the brakes once when the hydraulics have failed. A stored gas is light, does not catch fire and needs no return line, but it is compressible, so it suits on-off jobs rather than fine position control.

  • An engine-driven multi-stage piston compressor raises the air pressure; a relief valve protects each stage.
  • A moisture separator and a chemical dryer remove water, which would freeze in valves at altitude; a filter removes dirt.
  • Storage bottles hold the charge; a back-pressure valve keeps the compressor loaded until the bottles are charged.
  • A pressure-reducing valve gives each user the lower pressure it needs, and a shuttle valve switches a user, such as a brake, from its normal supply to the emergency bottle.

p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ (gas at constant temperature)

p is absolute pressure and V is gas volume; a bottle that discharges into empty actuators shares its gas over the larger volume, so its pressure falls in proportion.

Worked example

Question. A bleed duct carries 0.30 kg/s of air at 250 kPa and 200 °C. The duct area is 0.010 m². Take R = 287 J/(kg·K). Find the air density and the mean velocity.

  1. Convert temperature to kelvin: T = 200 + 273 = 473 K.
  2. Convert pressure to pascal: p = 250 kPa = 250 000 Pa.
  3. Apply the ideal-gas relation: ρ = p/(R × T) = 250 000/(287 × 473).
  4. Compute: 287 × 473 = 135 751, so ρ = 250 000/135 751 = 1.84 kg/m³.
  5. Use mass flow: ṁ = ρ × A × v, so v = ṁ/(ρ × A).
  6. Substitute: v = 0.30/(1.84 × 0.010) = 16.29 m/s.

Answer. The density is 1.84 kg/m³ and the mean velocity is 16.29 m/s.

Worked example 2

Question. An emergency gear-extension bottle holds 2.5 L of nitrogen at 200 bar and 15 °C. When it is fired, the gas fills the bottle plus the lines and actuators, which add 9.5 L. Each actuator needs at least 30 bar to push its leg into the downlock. Take R = 296.8 J/(kg·K) for nitrogen and the temperature as constant. Find the final pressure, say whether it is enough, and find the mass of gas in the bottle.

  1. Total volume after discharge: V₂ = 2.5 + 9.5 = 12.0 L.
  2. Boyle’s law: p₂ = p₁V₁ ÷ V₂ = 200 × 2.5 ÷ 12.0 = 41.7 bar.
  3. Compare: 41.7 bar is above the 30 bar the actuators need, a margin of 11.7 bar, so the bottle can extend the gear once.
  4. Mass: m = pV ÷ (RT) with p = 200 × 10⁵ Pa, V = 0.0025 m³ and T = 15 + 273.15 = 288.15 K.
  5. m = (2.0 × 10⁷ × 0.0025) ÷ (296.8 × 288.15) = 50 000 ÷ 85 523 = 0.585 kg.

Answer. The gas settles at 41.7 bar, enough for one extension with 11.7 bar to spare; the bottle holds 0.585 kg of nitrogen.

Exam traps

  • Do not write that bleed air normally comes from the HP stage. The HP port feeds the system only at low engine power; at cruise and climb power the LP port is enough and the HP valve stays closed.
  • Using gauge pressure instead of absolute pressure in ρ = p/(RT). The formula needs absolute pressure in Pa and absolute temperature in K.
  • Thinking the isolation valve is a check valve. A check valve is one-way and automatic; an isolation valve is opened or closed by command to split the manifold.
  • A duct overheat or leak closes the bleed and isolation valves for that section, not the pack valve alone; the pack valve only stops flow into one pack.
  • Use absolute pressure in p₁V₁ = p₂V₂ and in ρ = p/(RT); with gauge values the answer is wrong, most of all at low pressure.

2.3 Landing gear and brake systems

Landing gear carries the aircraft on the ground, and the brakes stop it. The gear uses an oleo strut: a cylinder of nitrogen gas acts as a spring, and oil forced through a small hole acts as a damper. The brakes turn the aircraft's kinetic energy into heat, so their design is really a heat problem. Every landing and every rejected takeoff depends on the two working together.

You need the hydraulic supply and actuator ideas from hydraulic systems.

Gear layout and the oleo strut

Landing gear is classified first by whether it moves. Fixed gear stays out, and light aircraft use it because it is simple and light. Retractable gear folds away in flight to cut drag, and it costs actuators, locks and doors. Gear is also classed by layout: nose wheel or tail wheel, and main gear with a single wheel, multiple wheels, or a bogie (a beam carrying two or more wheel pairs). A nose-wheel layout is stable on the ground; a tail-wheel layout is lighter but tends to swing.

Shock absorbers are classed by what stores the landing energy: a rigid gear leaves it to the tyres; light aircraft use a spring-steel leg or rubber bungee cords; almost every larger aircraft uses the oleo-pneumatic strut. The oleo strut does two jobs with one cylinder. Nitrogen above the oil is the spring: it compresses, stores the landing energy, then pushes the aircraft back up. Oil is the damper: as the strut closes, oil is forced through an orifice, often shaped by a metering pin, and the oil turns bounce energy into heat. Gas alone would bounce like a ball; oil alone would not spring back. A static seal joins parts that never move against each other; a dynamic seal rides on the sliding piston and keeps oil in and dirt out.

F = p × A

F is the upward force from the strut in newtons (N), p is the nitrogen pressure in pascals (Pa), and A is the piston area in square metres (m²). A larger piston or higher pressure carries a heavier aircraft.

Tyres are the only contact with the runway. They are tubeless on most aircraft, inflated with nitrogen to reduce fire risk, and checked cold because pressure rises with heat. Each tyre has a rated load, so a bogie spreads the aircraft weight over more tyres and lowers the runway strength needed.

Retraction, locks and the squat switch

A hydraulic actuator pushes the gear down or pulls it up; on some aircraft an electric motor drives the same linkage. Main legs usually swing sideways into the wing root or fuselage, or fore and aft into an engine nacelle; nose legs swing forward or aft. A side brace (drag brace) folds as the leg swings and is held straight by an over-centre lock when the leg is down. An uplock catches the gear in the up position so it cannot fall out, and a downlock holds the brace straight so the leg cannot fold on landing. The downlock matters most at touchdown: a gear that is down but not locked can collapse.

Sequence keeps the aircraft safe. The selector sends a command, the doors open, the gear moves, the doors close, and sensors confirm each position. A squat switch (weight-on-wheels switch) on the gear leg senses that the aircraft is on the ground and blocks retraction. Without it, a pilot could select gear up while parked. The same switch also arms other ground-only logic.

  • Uplock: holds gear up, released before extension.
  • Downlock: holds gear down, released before retraction.
  • Squat switch: says the aircraft is on the ground and stops retraction.
  • Proximity sensor: tells the cockpit the gear is actually locked, not just commanded.

Gear position indication and the logic that sequences gear with flaps are covered in landing gear and flap control. The pressure that drives the actuators comes from the hydraulic system.

Brakes: heat, discs and antiskid

A brake is an energy converter. It takes the aircraft's kinetic energy and turns it into heat in the discs. Single-disc brakes suit light aircraft; multi-disc brakes stack several discs to get more friction area in a small wheel. Carbon-carbon discs are used on large aircraft because they are light and survive very high temperatures. Steel discs are cheaper and work well at lower energy.

E = ½ × m × v²

E is kinetic energy in joules (J), m is aircraft mass in kilograms (kg), and v is landing speed in metres per second (m/s). Because v is squared, a small increase in speed raises brake energy a lot.

On light aircraft each brake pedal works a master cylinder, as in a car. On transports the pedals move a brake metering valve that admits system pressure to the brake pistons; an alternate hydraulic system and an accumulator back it up, and a shuttle valve connects the brake to whichever supply is live. Antiskid works like this: a wheel speed sensor measures each wheel. If a wheel slows too fast, it is about to skid, so the antiskid valve releases pressure for a moment and lets the wheel spin up again. This keeps friction near its best value and protects the tyre. Autobrake holds a selected deceleration after touchdown, and the parking brake holds the aircraft on accumulator pressure when the pumps are off.

Brake temperature is a real limit. Carbon discs can glow after a rejected takeoff, and fusible plugs in the wheel melt to let the tyre deflate before it can burst. Some syllabi list rocket separation mechanisms beside landing gear, because both are mechanisms driven by stored energy. A rocket stage is held by explosive bolts, frangible nuts or a clamp band (a Marman clamp) that a pyrotechnic charge releases; springs, gas pistons or small separation rockets then push the stages apart.

Worked example

Question. A 60 000 kg aircraft touches down at 250 km/h. Its four main-wheel brakes absorb 60 % of the kinetic energy, shared equally. Find the landing speed in m/s, the total kinetic energy, and the energy taken by each main brake.

  1. Convert speed: v = 250 / 3.6 = 69.44 m/s.
  2. Kinetic energy: E = ½ × 60 000 × (69.44)² = ½ × 60 000 × 4822.5 = 144.7 MJ.
  3. Brake share: E_brakes = 0.60 × 144.7 = 86.8 MJ. The other 40 % goes to reverse thrust, aerodynamic drag and rolling resistance.
  4. Each of four brakes: E_one = 86.8 / 4 = 21.7 MJ.

Answer. The landing speed is 69.4 m/s, the total kinetic energy is 145 MJ, and each main brake absorbs 21.7 MJ.

Worked example 2

Question. A 60 000 kg aircraft stands with 92 % of its weight on its two main legs. Each main oleo has a gas piston of diameter 0.20 m and a static gas volume of 4.0 L. Find the static gas pressure. At touchdown a strut is compressed until the gas volume is 1.6 L; taking the gas as isothermal for a first estimate, find the new pressure and the strut force.

  1. Load on one main leg: W = 0.46 × 60 000 × 9.81 = 270 756 N.
  2. Piston area: A = π × 0.10² = 0.031 42 m².
  3. Static pressure: p = W ÷ A = 270 756 ÷ 0.031 42 = 8.62 × 10⁶ Pa = 86.2 bar.
  4. Compression: p₂ = p₁V₁ ÷ V₂ = 86.2 × 4.0 ÷ 1.6 = 215.5 bar.
  5. Strut force: F = p₂ × A = 215.5 × 10⁵ × 0.031 42 = 677 kN, which is 2.5 times the static load.

Answer. Static pressure 86.2 bar; at 1.6 L the gas reaches 215.5 bar and the strut pushes with 677 kN. The gas spring stiffens as it closes, and a fast landing stroke is nearer adiabatic, which gives an even higher pressure.

Exam traps

  • Swapping the oleo roles: nitrogen gas is the spring, oil is the damper, not the other way round.
  • Forgetting that brake energy rises with the square of speed: a 10 % faster landing gives 1.1² = 1.21, about 21 % more heat.
  • Confusing uplock with downlock. The downlock holds the gear rigidly in the down position so the leg cannot fold at touchdown.
  • Thinking antiskid increases braking pressure. It releases pressure to stop a wheel skidding.
  • Using the tyre or strut gauge pressure as the force. Force is pressure times piston area, F = pA, and the gas pressure rises as the strut closes, so the static value is only the starting point.

2.4 Engine fuel and lubricating systems

The engine fuel system takes fuel from the tanks and meters the right flow to the combustor. The oil system pushes oil through the engine bearings and gears, removes their heat and carries wear debris to a filter and a chip detector. Every start, climb and cruise depends on both. Only a few universities teach this topic in detail, so keep the focus on the path, the jobs of the parts and the limits the crew watches.

You need: the engine control systems (FADEC) topic, for who commands the fuel flow.

Fuel path and metering

Fuel is stored in wing, centre and sometimes tail tanks. Vents keep the tanks near outside pressure, and crossfeed valves let either engine draw from either side to keep the aircraft balanced. A boost pump in each tank pushes fuel to the engine at low pressure, so it does not boil or form vapour at altitude. On the engine, a low-pressure pump stage sends it through the fuel-oil heat exchanger, where hot oil warms the fuel (so ice crystals melt) and the fuel cools the oil, and then through the low-pressure fuel filter. The high-pressure pump, driven by the accessory gearbox, raises the pressure for metering. The fuel metering unit, commanded by the FADEC (full authority digital engine control), sets the flow for the thrust demanded: FADEC is the computer, the metering unit is the valve it drives. A flow meter reports the flow to the cockpit, and a high-pressure shut-off valve, worked by the start lever and the fire handle, isolates the nozzles. The start that follows is in engine starting and ignition.

ṁ = ρ × V̇

ṁ is fuel mass flow in kg/s; ρ is density; V̇ is volume flow. With SI units, ρ in kg/m³ and V̇ in m³/s. With ρ in kg/L, V̇ must be in L/s.

  • The boost pump stops vapour lock; the HP pump makes metering pressure.
  • The filter bypass keeps fuel flowing if the filter blocks, and a pressure-drop switch warns the crew first.
  • The metering unit controls flow; it does not make pressure.

Oil system: heat and debris

A jet engine usually has a dry-sump oil system: oil is kept in a separate tank, not in the engine. A pressure pump sends oil through a filter and cooler to bearings and gears. Oil takes heat from the bearings and carries metal particles. Scavenge pumps return oil from the bearing chambers to the tank. The filter has a bypass: if it blocks, unfiltered oil still reaches the bearings. A chip detector is a magnetic plug that catches steel particles and warns the crew. Oil pressure and temperature limits are in the flight manual.

Q = ṁ × c × ΔT

Q is heat rate in kW; ṁ is oil mass flow in kg/s; c is specific heat in kJ/(kg·K); ΔT is oil temperature rise in K.

  • Wet sump stores oil in the engine sump; dry sump uses a tank and is common on jets.
  • The cooler may be the fuel-oil heat exchanger or a separate air-oil cooler.
  • A filter bypass warning means unfiltered oil is flowing.

Worked example

Question. An engine burns 1200 kg/h of fuel of density 0.8 kg/L. Find the volume flow in L/s. Separately, 0.5 kg/s of oil with c = 2.0 kJ/(kg·K) picks up 60 kW from the bearings. Find the oil temperature rise, and the outlet temperature if the oil enters the bearings at 80 °C.

  1. Fuel volume flow = mass flow ÷ density = 1200 kg/h ÷ 0.8 kg/L = 1500 L/h.
  2. Convert to seconds: 1500 L/h ÷ 3600 s/h = 0.4167 L/s, which rounds to 0.42 L/s.
  3. Oil heat rate Q = 60 kW = 60 kJ/s.
  4. ΔT = Q ÷ (ṁ × c) = 60 ÷ (0.5 × 2.0) = 60 ÷ 1.0 = 60 K.
  5. Outlet temperature = 80 + 60 = 140 °C.

Answer. Fuel volume flow is 0.42 L/s; the oil rises 60 K, from 80 °C to 140 °C.

Exam traps

  • Boost pump and HP pump do different jobs; do not swap their pressures or purposes.
  • Filter bypass keeps oil flowing but sends unfiltered oil to bearings; it is not a clean-oil path.
  • The fuel-oil heat exchanger heats fuel and cools oil; oil temperature rise uses oil flow and oil specific heat.
  • kg/h to L/s needs density first; dividing by 3600 alone gives wrong units.
  • Mixing up the fuel shut-off valves: the HP shut-off valve at the engine is closed by the start lever; the fire handle also closes the low-pressure (spar) valve at the tank, so a nacelle fire is not fed from the tank.

2.5 Engine starting and ignition systems

A jet engine cannot light its fuel at standstill: the compressor gives no airflow until something turns it. A starter spins the high-pressure spool until there is enough air for combustion; then ignition and fuel take over and the engine accelerates to idle on its own. Only a few universities teach this topic in detail, but the starting circuit, the exciter and the start sequence are classic exam questions.

You need: Chapter 2.2 for bleed air used by air starters, and Chapter 2.4 for the fuel that must be lit.

Starters: air turbine and electric

A starter is a motor that turns the HP compressor through the accessory gearbox. It supplies torque until the engine is well past self-sustaining speed, the N2 at which the engine can keep turning without help (N2 is HP spool speed as a percentage of its rated speed). The starter is cut out by N2, not by a clock.

An air turbine starter (ATS) is a small turbine driven by regulated bleed air. A start valve admits air from the APU, a running engine (cross-bleed start) or a ground cart. A reduction gear and an overrunning clutch drive the gearbox; at cut-out the start valve closes and the clutch lets the running engine overrun the starter, so the engine cannot overspeed it.

An electric starter is a DC series motor, chosen because it gives its highest torque at zero speed. On turboprops, helicopters and APUs it is often a starter-generator: one machine cranks the engine on 28 V DC and then becomes the DC generator. Large turbofans use air starters because an electric motor of the same power would be far too heavy.

Starting circuit and ignition

The electrical start circuit keeps a light start switch away from the starter’s current, which can reach several hundred amperes. The start switch energises a start relay; its heavy contacts (the starter contactor) connect the battery or ground power to the motor. A current limiter or a resistance in series softens the first surge, and a time relay or an N2 speed switch opens the contactor at cut-out. The same relay logic energises the ignition relay, so the exciters work only during the start.

An ignition exciter takes 28 V DC or 115 V AC and charges a storage capacitor to a few kilovolts. When the capacitor voltage reaches the breakdown of a sealed discharge gap, the stored energy is dumped into the igniter plug, a spark gap in the combustor liner, as one intense spark; most exciters give one or two sparks a second. The energy in each spark is set by the capacitor:

E = ½ × C × V²

E is the stored energy in joules (J), C is the capacitance in farads (F) and V is the charging voltage in volts (V).

Spark energies run from a few joules to about 20 J. The higher energy is used for starting and relight, because a cold, fast-moving mixture is hard to light; the lower energy is used for continuous ignition in flight, in heavy rain, turbulence, icing or on contaminated runways, so a flameout relights at once; it saves plug life. Two exciters and two plugs give redundancy. Piston engines are different: a magneto, an engine-driven generator with its own coil and contact breaker, makes the spark for each cylinder, and every engine has two magnetos.

Start sequence and abnormal starts

In an automatic start the FADEC runs the sequence. The values below are those of one common narrow-body auto-start (an A320-family aircraft with CFM56-5B engines); other engines use other numbers, so quote them only from that engine’s start schedule.

  1. START is selected; the start valve opens and the starter cranks N2.
  2. At about 16 % N2 the igniters are energised.
  3. At about 22 % N2 the HP fuel valve opens; light-off follows within seconds and EGT rises.
  4. Starter and combustion together accelerate the spool; at about 50 % N2 the start valve closes and the ignition switches off.
  5. The engine accelerates to idle, near 60 % N2, on its own.

Interlocks stop unsafe starts: the start valve will not open with low bleed pressure, fuel is inhibited below a minimum N2, and the igniters are on before fuel arrives, so fuel lights as soon as it enters. Early ignition is harmless; early or excess fuel is not.

  • Hot start: EGT passes the start limit, from too much fuel for the airflow (weak starter, tailwind, early fuel). Cut fuel at once.
  • Hung start: light-off occurs but N2 stops rising below idle, from low starter air or a weak battery. Abort before EGT climbs.
  • Wet start (no light-off): fuel enters but does not light. Cut fuel, then dry-motor (crank with fuel and ignition off) to clear it.

Worked example

Question. During an auto-start N2 rises at a steady 1.6 % per second while only the starter turns the engine. After light-off, starter and combustion together raise N2 at 2.4 % per second.   4. Cut-out: t = 13.75 + 11.67 = 25.42 s.
  5. Check the order: 10.0 s < 13.75 s < 25.4 s, so the igniters are already sparking when fuel arrives.

Answer. Ignition at 10.0 s, fuel and light-off at 13.75 s, starter cut-out at 25.4 s after cranking began.

Worked example 2

2.6 Cabin pressurisation and air cycle cooling

At 11 km the air outside is thin and cold: a crew cannot breathe it, because the lungs cannot take enough oxygen out of it. So the cabin is sealed and fed with conditioned air, while a valve at the rear lets the surplus out at a controlled rate. That valve, not the supply, sets the cabin pressure. On most airliners the supply is hot bleed air, so a pack cools it and then expands it through a turbine.

You need: the bleed air source and its regulation from Chapter 2.2.

Why the cabin must hold a pressure altitude

At 35 000 ft the air outside is at about 23.8 kPa, against 101.3 kPa at sea level. Oxygen is still 21 % of the air, so its partial pressure falls from about 21.3 kPa to 5.0 kPa, and the blood can no longer load enough of it. The result is hypoxia: dulled judgement first, then loss of consciousness.

Trapped gas is the second problem. By Boyle’s law pV is constant, so one litre of gas in the middle ear, a sinus or the gut at 101.3 kPa becomes 101.3 ÷ 23.8 = 4.3 litres at 35 000 ft, which causes pain and can damage tissue.

So the cabin is held at a cabin altitude: the altitude in the standard atmosphere whose pressure equals the cabin pressure. The usual limit is 8000 ft (2438 m), where the pressure is 75.3 kPa. The fuselage sets a maximum differential pressure, typically about 55–60 kPa (8–9 psi) on an airliner.

Δp = pcabin − pambient        F = Δp × A

Δp is the differential pressure in Pa; F is the outward force in N on a panel of area A in m².

At cruise the cabin sits near 8000 ft and Δp is large, so the hull carries a heavy steady load. If the cabin cannot hold its pressure, the cabin altitude rises and the crew must act; oxygen for that case is in aircraft oxygen systems.

The outflow valve sets the pressure

The fuselage is a pressure vessel with doors and many seals, and it is never airtight. Air enters from the packs at a controlled mass flow and leaves through the outflow valve, a motor-driven gate near the rear. Open it and cabin pressure falls; close it and pressure rises.

The cabin pressure controller reads cabin pressure from its own sensors, takes static pressure from the air data system, and drives the valve to follow a schedule. The crew selects a target cabin altitude and a rate of change, or lets the automatic schedule fly it.

  1. Ground: valve fully open, cabin at field elevation, no differential.
  2. Takeoff: the valve closes a little after liftoff, so the cabin does not bump.
  3. Climb: cabin altitude rises at a limited rate, a few hundred feet per minute, towards 8000 ft.
  4. Cruise: cabin altitude held constant.
  5. Descent: the cabin comes down so that it reaches field elevation at touchdown.
  6. Manual: the crew drives the valve directly if the automatic channels fail.

Three protections sit outside the controller. The positive pressure relief (safety) valve opens outward if Δp passes the structural limit. The negative pressure relief valve opens inward if outside pressure is higher than inside, for example in a fast descent with the outflow valve shut. A dump function drives the outflow valve fully open: in flight it clears smoke or fumes, and on the ground it brings the cabin to field pressure so the doors can be opened.

Air cycle cooling: expand the air and it cools

Bleed air reaches the pack at roughly 200 °C and 2 to 3 bar. The pack uses no refrigerant; it uses the air itself. Three effects share the work: heat transfer to ram air, compression and expansion. Air cycle cooling is light, needs no refrigerant, and the same air also pressurises and ventilates the cabin; its cost is the bleed air taken from the engines.

  • Flow control valve: meters bleed air and holds the pack to its design mass flow.
  • Primary heat exchanger: ram air from a scoop removes heat.
  • Compressor: raises the pressure again. The air gets hotter, but the higher pressure ratio lets the turbine extract more work and cool it further.
  • Secondary heat exchanger: ram air removes the compression heat, bringing the air back near ram-air temperature at a higher pressure.
  • Turbine: the air expands and does work on the wheel, which drives the compressor on the same shaft. It leaves very cold, near 0 °C or below.
  • Water separator: condensed water is spun or coalesced out, so no fog or ice reaches the cabin.

Why does expansion cool? In the turbine the air hands over energy as shaft work, so its temperature drops steeply; air forced through a plain hole keeps its energy and hardly cools. For an ideal (isentropic) turbine:

T₂ = T₁ × (p₂ ÷ p₁)^((γ − 1)/γ)

T is absolute temperature in K, p is absolute pressure, 1 is the turbine inlet, 2 the outlet, and γ = 1.4 for air. A real turbine of efficiency η gives T₂ = T₁ − η(T₁ − T₂s).

In the bootstrap cycle, the one above, the turbine drives the compressor. In the simple cycle there is no compressor: the turbine drives a fan that pulls cooling air through the heat exchanger. It gives less cooling and suits small aircraft and low demand.

The pack delivers air colder than needed. Hot trim air from the bleed is mixed back in through trim valves to hold each zone at its selected temperature. On the ground a fan on the air cycle machine shaft pulls ram air through the heat exchangers; in flight a modulating inlet flap sets the ram flow.

Worked example 2

Question. Air enters a pack’s cooling turbine at 300 kPa and 40 °C and leaves at 80 kPa. Take γ = 1.4. Find the outlet temperature for an ideal turbine and for a turbine of efficiency 0.80.

2.7 Vapour cycle cooling and cabin heating

An air cycle machine cools bleed air by expanding it, so it needs bleed pressure and a flow of ram air to dump heat. Parked on a hot stand there is no ram pressure, only what the pack fan can pull, so many aircraft and ground carts use a vapour cycle machine instead. It cools by boiling a refrigerant in an evaporator and condensing it in a condenser, like a domestic refrigerator. Cabin heating comes from trim bleed air, electric elements, a shroud on the exhaust or a small fuel-burning heater.

You need the air cycle machine and the bleed air supply first: see Chapter 2.6 and Chapter 2.2.

Why a vapour cycle machine exists

An air cycle machine is an open system. It takes hot bleed air, cools it in a heat exchanger, then expands it through a turbine. Its cooling power depends on two things it cannot control: bleed air pressure and the temperature of the ram air available to the heat exchanger.

Parked on a 42 °C afternoon in Chennai, both are against it: the ram air is hot and only the pack fan moves it, so the air cycle machine burns a lot of bleed air for little cooling.

A vapour cycle machine closes the loop. A fixed charge of refrigerant, R-134a for example, is sealed in the pipes and carried round and round. R-134a boils at about −26 °C at one atmosphere; in the evaporator the suction pressure is set so that it boils a few degrees above 0 °C, cold enough to cool the cabin air but warm enough that the water on the fins does not freeze. The machine needs only electric or shaft power for its compressor, plus a fan or ram air for the condenser.

You meet vapour cycle machines in ground cooling carts, in galley and cargo cooling, in helicopters and in many business jets and turboprops.

The four steps of the vapour compression cycle

Follow one kilogram of refrigerant round the loop. It changes from vapour to liquid and back, and that change is where the heat is carried.

  1. Compression. The compressor takes low-pressure, low-temperature vapour and squeezes it. Pressure and temperature rise above the outside air temperature, and shaft work is added.
  2. Condensation. The hot high-pressure vapour enters the condenser, a finned heat exchanger. Outside air (ram air in flight, a fan on the ground) takes heat out, and the vapour condenses into a high-pressure liquid.
  3. Expansion. The liquid passes through the expansion valve, a small orifice. Pressure drops sharply and temperature falls with it, giving a cold, low-pressure liquid-vapour mixture. No work is taken out; the process is a throttling.
  4. Evaporation. The cold mixture enters the evaporator in the cabin air duct. Cabin air blows over the fins and gives up heat; the refrigerant boils back to vapour and returns to the compressor.

COP = Qevap ÷ Wcomp

COP is the coefficient of performance, a pure number; Qevap is the heat absorbed from the cabin air in the evaporator, in kW; Wcomp is the shaft work put into the compressor, in kW.

Qcond = Qevap + Wcomp

Qcond is the heat rejected at the condenser, in kW. The condenser throws away both the cabin heat and the compressor work, so its heat duty is always larger than the evaporator’s.

A well-designed machine reaches a COP of 3 to 5. An air cycle machine at low altitude has a much lower COP, often below 1, because it compresses air that is then dumped overboard.

Advantages and disadvantages

  • Works on the ground: no bleed air and no ram air needed, just a small electric condenser fan. This is why ground carts use it.
  • Higher COP at low altitude: less shaft or electrical power per kW of cabin cooling than an air cycle machine.
  • The condenser needs good airflow. Dust, leaves and insects raise the condensing temperature and the COP falls fast.
  • Weight and complexity: a sealed compressor, receiver, pipework and refrigerant charge add mass.
  • Refrigerant handling: the charge must be recovered and recharged by trained staff; venting to atmosphere is illegal in most countries.
  • Leakage: joints and shaft seals leak slowly. A low charge shows as poor cooling, a cycling compressor and frost on the wrong pipe.

Cabin heating

A cabin at cruise is usually too cold, not too hot: the outside air is below −40 °C and the structure soaks to it. Without heat the flight deck is uncomfortable and the windscreens mist and ice.

Bleed-air trim is the method on large aircraft. Hot bleed air passes through a trim air valve and mixes with the cold air leaving the pack; a zone controller moves the valve to hold the selected temperature. The bleed comes from the pneumatic system.

Electric heaters are resistive elements in the air duct, switched by a relay or solid-state controller with an overheat switch. They suit small aircraft and helicopters with no bleed air, and supply foot and windscreen heat; windscreen heat is in ice and rain protection.

Exhaust muff heat is used on small piston-engine aircraft. A shroud around the muffler collects heated air, and a valve sends it to the cabin or overboard. A cracked muffler can leak carbon monoxide into the cabin, so a CO detector is the standard guard.

A combustion heater burns aircraft fuel in a sealed chamber; ventilating air passes over the outside of the chamber, so the combustion gas never mixes with cabin air. An igniter lights it, a fuel valve and thermostat set the heat, and an overheat switch and a combustion-air pressure switch shut it down. Some light twins and older transports use it.

Worked example

Question. A vapour cycle cabin cooling pack absorbs 12 kW from the cabin air. The compressor shaft takes 3 kW. (a) Find the coefficient of performance. (b) Find the heat rejected at the condenser. (c) Dirt on the condenser fins drops the COP to 3.5 with the same 3 kW of compressor work. What cooling does the pack now give?

  1. Write the data: Qevap = 12 kW, Wcomp = 3 kW.
  2. Part (a): COP = Qevap ÷ Wcomp = 12 ÷ 3 = 4.0. The COP has no unit.
  3. Part (b): apply the first law to the whole loop. Qcond = Qevap + Wcomp = 12 + 3 = 15 kW.
  4. The condenser must dump 15 kW, which is why it needs a large face area and a strong fan or ram air flow.
  5. Part (c): rearrange the COP definition, Qevap = COP × Wcomp = 3.5 × 3 = 10.5 kW.
  6. The pack now removes 10.5 kW instead of 12 kW for the same 3 kW, a loss of 1.5 kW (12.5 %), and the condenser rejects 10.5 + 3 = 13.5 kW at a higher temperature.

Answer. (a) COP = 4.0; (b) condenser heat rejected = 15 kW; (c) at COP 3.5 the pack cools 10.5 kW.

Exam traps

  • Swapping condenser and evaporator. The condenser is the hot one that rejects heat outside; the evaporator is the cold one inside the cabin duct.
  • Thinking COP above 1 breaks the first law. It does not: the extra heat at the condenser is the compressor work, and Qcond = Qevap + Wcomp always holds.
  • Using condenser heat in the COP. COP is Qevap ÷ Wcomp, never Qcond ÷ Wcomp.
  • Forgetting that the expansion valve does no work. It only drops pressure and temperature; the compressor is the only work input.
  • Treating the vapour cycle as an open system like the air cycle machine. It is closed: the same refrigerant charge circulates again and again.

Compressor, condenser, expansion valve, evaporator, then back to the compressor.

Understand Why does a vapour cycle machine cool better than an air cycle machine when the aircraft is parked on a hot day? 3 marks

It is a closed loop and needs no bleed air. The condenser only needs a small fan, so the machine is independent of ram air and bleed pressure. Its COP at low altitude is also higher, so the same power removes more heat.

Apply A pack absorbs 18 kW in the evaporator and the compressor shaft takes 4.5 kW. Find the COP and the condenser heat rejection. 4 marks

Analyze A vapour cycle pack shows COP = 4. A student claims this means the pack delivers four times the energy it consumes. Correct the statement. 5 marks

The pack does not create energy. It uses 3 kW of shaft work, for example, to move 12 kW of heat from the cabin to the outside air. The work is not the source of the heat; it is the cost of pumping it uphill in temperature. The first law still balances at the condenser: 12 kW in from the cabin plus 3 kW of work equals 15 kW out.

2.8 Aircraft oxygen systems

Above a safe cabin altitude the air holds too little oxygen for the body. The oxygen system raises the partial pressure of oxygen in the lungs by supplying stored gas, liquid oxygen, a chemical generator or, mostly in military aircraft, on-board generation. It protects the crew in a decompression and gives passengers time while the aircraft descends. Oxygen also makes oil and grease burn fiercely, so the system has its own safety rules.

You need: Chapter 2.6 for cabin altitude and Chapter 2.2 for bleed air.

Hypoxia and the need for oxygen

Hypoxia is a shortage of oxygen reaching the body tissues. It starts with headache and poor judgement, then leads to loss of consciousness. In an aircraft it is caused by low outside pressure: the fraction of oxygen in air stays near 0.21, but the total pressure falls, so the partial pressure of oxygen falls with it.

PO2 = FO2 × Pamb

PO2 is the partial pressure of oxygen in bar; FO2 is the oxygen fraction (0.21 in air); Pamb is ambient pressure in bar. At sea level Pamb ≈ 1.013 bar gives PO2 ≈ 0.213 bar. At 35,000 ft Pamb ≈ 0.24 bar gives PO2 ≈ 0.050 bar.

Cabin pressurisation keeps the cabin at a safe pressure altitude, often 6000 to 8000 ft. If pressurisation fails, the crew needs supplemental oxygen at once. The time of useful consciousness (TUC) is the time from losing oxygen to being unable to act usefully. Standard tables give about 3 to 5 minutes at 25 000 ft, 30 to 60 s at 35 000 ft and 15 to 20 s at 40 000 ft, so crew masks must be on within seconds.

Supplemental oxygen raises FO2 in the mask above 0.21, up to pure oxygen at high cabin altitude, so PO2 in the lungs stays near its sea-level value while Pamb is low. It buys the time needed to descend.

Supply types and components

Four supply types are common. High-pressure gaseous oxygen sits in cylinders at about 1,800 psi (124 bar). A filler valve fills the cylinder; a shut-off valve isolates it; a regulator reduces pressure; a mask delivers gas to the user. Liquid oxygen (LOX) stores more oxygen in less volume, but it must be kept cold and it boils off; it is used where weight and space matter. A chemical oxygen generator burns a sodium chlorate candle to release oxygen; it is simple and needs no pressure storage, but once started it cannot be stopped. Military aircraft often use an on-board oxygen generating system (OBOGS), which separates oxygen from engine bleed air with a molecular sieve.

Crew masks use a regulator. A demand regulator opens only when the user inhales. A diluter-demand regulator mixes cabin air with oxygen at low cabin altitude and goes to pure oxygen as cabin altitude rises. A pressure-demand regulator supplies oxygen slightly above cabin pressure so the mask stays sealed and the lungs get oxygen at very high cabin altitude or in smoke. Passenger masks are continuous-flow: a simple pressure reducer, or the chemical generator itself, feeds a fixed flow to each mask whether or not the passenger is breathing in. Most airliners supply passengers from chemical generators that run for roughly 12 to 22 minutes, long enough for an emergency descent.

Other parts are tubing, a pressure gauge, a relief valve and an overboard discharge disc that bursts if the cylinder is overpressured. The gauge reads cylinder pressure, not the mass of gas left. By Boyle’s law at constant temperature, the gas available at 1 bar is V₁ = Vw × (Pc − Pr) ÷ P₁, where Vw is the cylinder water volume in L, Pc the cylinder pressure in bar, Pr the reserve pressure that is not used, and P₁ = 1 bar.

Operation, mask drop-out and safety

Before flight the crew checks cylinder pressure and the mask seal. The crew dons masks when cabin altitude passes about 10 000 ft (the cabin altitude warning), and selects 100 % or emergency pressure for smoke. If cabin altitude passes about 14 000 ft, the passenger panels open automatically and the masks drop out; this automatic release is called mask drop-out. The passenger then pulls the mask down, which pulls a pin and starts the flow. Portable cylinders serve walk-around use.

  1. Check cylinder pressure and the mask seal before flight.
  2. Open the shut-off valve slowly and set the regulator to normal.
  3. Don the mask at the cabin altitude warning, about 10 000 ft.
  4. Above about 14 000 ft passenger masks drop out automatically; the passenger pulls one down to start the flow.

Oxygen is not fuel, but it makes fuel and oil burn fiercely. Fittings must be cleaned for oxygen service; no grease, oil or PTFE tape on threads. Open cylinder valves slowly to avoid a pressure surge and adiabatic heating. Keep cylinders away from heat and secure them so they do not become missiles. A chemical generator becomes hot; do not cover it. A LOX converter vents oxygen; keep it away from ignition sources. In a fire, shut off supply if possible and use the fire procedure in Chapter 2.9.

Worked example

Question. A passenger oxygen cylinder has a water volume of 50 L and is filled to 124 bar. It must be refilled when it falls to 10 bar, so gas below 10 bar is not counted. Twelve passengers each draw 4 L/min measured at 1 bar. Ignore temperature change. Find the usable gas, the total flow and how long the supply lasts.

  1. Usable gas at 1 bar: V₁ = Vw × (Pc − Pr) ÷ P₁ = 50 × (124 − 10) ÷ 1 = 5700 L.
  2. Total flow: 12 × 4 L/min = 48 L/min.
  3. Duration: 5700 ÷ 48 = 118.75 min.
  4. In hours: 118.75 ÷ 60 = 1.98 h, about 1 h 59 min.
  5. Check: 48 L/min × 118.75 min = 5700 L.

Answer. The cylinder gives 5700 L of usable gas at 48 L/min, which lasts about 119 minutes.

Exam traps

  • Reading cylinder pressure as quantity: the gas available is water volume × usable pressure, and the gauge pressure also changes with temperature even when no gas is used.
  • Using oil or grease on oxygen fittings: oxygen makes these burn fiercely, so oxygen service cleaning is mandatory.
  • Confusing diluter-demand with pressure-demand: a diluter-demand regulator mixes cabin air at low altitude; a pressure-demand regulator supplies oxygen above cabin pressure.
  • Assuming a chemical generator can be switched off: once the sodium chlorate candle starts, it supplies oxygen until it is finished.
  • Mixing up passenger and crew masks: passenger masks are continuous-flow and drop out automatically; crew masks use diluter-demand or pressure-demand regulators.

2.9 Fire detection, extinguishing and smoke detection

A fire protection system watches the engines, APU, cargo holds and lavatories for heat, flame or smoke, raises a cockpit warning, and lets the crew, or an automatic circuit, release a stored extinguishing agent into the affected zone. It exists because a fire in a hidden compartment can grow faster than anyone can see it. Fast detection, very few false alarms and a reliable discharge path are the design aims.

You need the engine and APU zone layout from engine fuel and lubricating systems, and circuit protection from the aircraft electrical systems topic.

What the system must do

Fire protection has two separate jobs. Detection watches a zone and decides that a fire exists. Extinguishing carries agent to that zone. The two jobs meet at the control unit and the cockpit. A smoke detector in a cargo hold does not put out a fire; it only starts the warning chain.

  • Fast response: the detector must signal within seconds of a flame or a temperature rise, before the fire spreads.
  • Low false alarms: a warning that cries wolf makes the crew ignore a real fire. The control unit uses a short confirmation delay and compares more than one sensor where possible.
  • Full zone coverage: no shadow area. A continuous loop or several spot detectors are placed so that any fire in the zone heats at least one sensor.
  • Testable and resettable: a test switch simulates a detector. After the test the system must return to normal without replacing parts.
  • Fault indication: a broken wire or a failed detector must show as a fault, not as a healthy zone or as a fire.
  • Survive the environment: vibration, heat, fuel, oil and cleaning fluids.

Detectors and the control unit

The detector is chosen for the zone. An engine nacelle has airflow and hot metal; a cargo hold has boxes and possible smouldering. One detector type does not fit all.

  • Thermocouple: several active junctions in the zone are wired against reference junctions kept cool. A fast temperature rise makes a voltage that trips a sensitive relay, so it detects the rate of rise, not a fixed temperature.
  • Bimetallic spot detector: two metals with different expansion bend a strip; at a set temperature it closes a contact. It protects one small point.
  • Continuous loop (resistance type): an Inconel tube with one centre wire (Fenwal) or two wires (Kidde) is packed with a eutectic salt or thermistor material whose resistance falls sharply when hot. A low resistance anywhere along the loop gives a warning.
  • Continuous loop (pneumatic type): a sealed tube holds a gas and a gas-charged core (the Lindberg or Systron-Donner type). Heat raises the pressure and closes a diaphragm switch: a general overheat raises the gas pressure, a local fire releases gas from the core.
  • Flame detectors: ultraviolet or infrared cells see the light of a flame within a second; they are paired or filtered so sunlight does not trip them.
  • Smoke detectors: an ionisation detector loses current when smoke enters its chamber; an optical detector sees light scattered by smoke onto a photocell. Both suit cargo holds and lavatories, where smoke arrives before flame.
  • Carbon monoxide detector: an electrochemical cell catches a slow, smouldering fire that makes little visible smoke.

The control unit supplies power, watches the loops and decides. It applies a short delay to reject a transient, drives the warning light and bell, and stores a fault code. The test switch injects a simulated detector signal. Crews test the loops before flight; the system then resets.

Engine and APU zones usually have two identical loops, A and B, in AND logic: both must sense a fire before the warning shows, which stops a damaged loop giving a false alarm. If one loop fails, the control unit shows a FAULT and switches to the healthy loop alone, so a single broken wire does not lose protection.

From warning to discharge

When the control unit confirms a fire, the cockpit gets a red fire light and a bell. On many types the pilot then pulls the fire handle: pulling closes the fuel (spar) valve, the hydraulic shut-off valve and the bleed valve, trips the generator and arms the squib circuit; rotating the handle then fires a squib. Other types use a guarded fire push-button and separate agent buttons. On the ground an APU fire usually shuts the APU down and fires its bottle automatically; in flight the crew uses the APU fire control.

A squib is a small explosive cartridge. It drives a cutter or opens the bottle valve. One squib gives one shot; after firing it is spent. Some installations carry two bottles, or a bottle with two squibs, so the crew can fire a second shot after the first has knocked the fire down.

Engine and APU bottles sit outside the fire zone: the APU bottle in the tail cone or aft bay, the engine bottles in the pylon, wing root or fuselage, with pipes to nozzles in the nacelle. Cargo holds are classed by how a fire is handled: in a Class C hold (the usual lower-deck hold) there are smoke detectors and a built-in extinguishing system, fired from the cockpit with a first high-rate discharge and then a metered flow that holds the concentration until landing; a Class E hold on a freighter has detection, and the crew shuts off the airflow to starve the fire. A lavatory waste bin has a small bottle with a fusible link that discharges by itself. Portable extinguishers in the cabin are for the crew.

The agent volume follows Va = C × Vz ÷ (1 − C), where Va is the volume of agent vapour needed in m³, C is the design concentration as a fraction and Vz is the zone volume in m³. The division by (1 − C) allows for the air the agent pushes out. The mass is m = Va × ρ, with ρ the agent vapour density.

Halon 1301 is a clean agent: it leaves no residue and breaks the chemical chain of combustion, so a few per cent stops a flame. It depletes ozone, so it is no longer made and aviation uses recycled stock while replacements are approved. Carbon dioxide is not used in occupied spaces because it can suffocate; water and dry powder suit hand-held cabin use.

Worked example

Question. A cargo compartment has a volume of 40 m³. The design agent concentration is 6 % by volume and the agent vapour density is 6 kg/m³. Find the agent vapour volume and mass, and the error if the simple form Va = C × Vz is used.

  1. Concentration as a fraction: C = 6 % = 0.06.
  2. Agent volume: Va = C × Vz ÷ (1 − C) = 0.06 × 40 ÷ 0.94 = 2.553 m³.
  3. Agent mass: m = Va × ρ = 2.553 × 6 = 15.3 kg.
  4. Simple form: 0.06 × 40 = 2.4 m³, so 2.4 × 6 = 14.4 kg.
  5. Difference: 15.3 − 14.4 = 0.9 kg, the simple form is 6 % low (1 − 0.94).

Answer. About 2.55 m³ of agent vapour, 15.3 kg of agent stored as liquid in the bottle; the simple form would give only 14.4 kg.

  • On fire-handle types the handle has two actions: pulling isolates and arms, rotating discharges. Writing only "pull to discharge" loses marks.
  • A squib is one-shot. A test does not fire the squib; it only tests the circuit.
  • Halon 1301 does not work mainly by removing oxygen. It breaks the chemical chain, so a low concentration can stop a flame.
  • Dual loops in AND logic need both loops to warn; that cuts false alarms, and a failed loop is dropped automatically with a FAULT light, so writing "one loop must fail before the other works" is wrong.

2.10 Landing gear and flap control, stall warning and protection

Landing gear and flaps must move only when the aircraft is in the right state, and the stall system watches angle of attack so it can warn, and if needed act, before lift is lost. Gear control commands up or down, checks the locks and shows the result; flap control drives the flaps to each selected step and stops them if the two wings disagree; the stall computer compares angle of attack with a limit that depends on the flap setting, shakes the stick as a warning and, on some types, pushes it as protection.

You need: landing gear and brakes for the gear structure, hydraulic systems for actuation, and the air data instruments topic for airspeed.

Gear selection, locking and warning

The gear lever sends an electrical command to the gear control unit (on the A320 family, two landing gear control and interface units working in turn). The unit opens a selector valve: for retraction it first opens the doors, unlocks the downlocks, retracts the legs into the uplocks, then closes the doors; extension runs the other way. Proximity sensors, which detect a metal target without touching it, tell the unit when each lock and door has reached position, and it moves to the next step only then.

A squat switch (weight-on-wheels sensor) on the shock strut senses that the strut is compressed. On the ground it blocks retraction, even if the lever is moved up, and it switches many systems between ground and flight logic. Position sensors drive the indication: three green lamps for down and locked, red for in transit or disagreeing with the lever, and nothing when the gear is up and locked. A press-to-test button checks the lamps.

The gear warning sounds when the gear is not down and locked and the throttles are near idle at low height, the usual sign of an approach. On many types it also sounds when the flaps are set for landing with the gear not down, and that warning cannot be silenced. High power with the gear up does not trigger it, so the crew must still check the green lamps.

If hydraulic pressure is lost, alternate extension releases the uplocks, by a cable, an electric motor or a gas bottle, and the gear falls under its own weight. The air load and assist springs push the legs into the downlocks.

Flap selection, asymmetry and load limiting

Trailing-edge Fowler flaps move aft and down, adding area and camber. The flap lever has detents such as UP, 1, 2, 3 and FULL. On a transport the lever position goes to a flap control computer, which runs a central power drive unit. That unit has two motors (two hydraulic, or one hydraulic and one electric) driving torque shafts that run along each wing to a ball-screw or rotary actuator at every flap track. Position pick-offs (synchros or rotary transducers) at the drive unit and at the wing tips report where the flaps are, and the computer stops the drive when they match the lever. On a light aircraft an electric motor, limit switches and a position indicator do the same job.

Both wings must move together. The computer compares the left and right wing-tip pick-offs: if they differ by more than a small threshold (an asymmetry), it stops the drive and sets the wing-tip brakes, which lock the torque shafts. A skew sensor finds one flap panel out of line with its neighbour on the same wing, for example after a track or actuator failure, and also stops the drive. A split flap would roll the aircraft, so stopping is safer than finishing the selection.

Load limiting protects the structure. At high airspeed the air load on extended flaps is large, so on some types the computer retracts the flaps one step automatically above the placard speed (flap load relief) and runs them out again when the speed falls. Limit switches also stop the drive at full up and full down.

Stall warning and protection

Angle of attack α is the angle between the wing chord line and the relative airflow. Lift rises with α until the wing stalls at the critical angle, so a stall depends on α, not on speed alone: an aircraft can stall at high speed in a steep turn. For level flight the stall speed is:

VS = √(2W ÷ (ρ S CLmax))

VS is the stall speed in m/s; W is the weight in N; ρ is the air density in kg/m³; S is the wing area in m²; CLmax is the maximum lift coefficient, which rises when flaps are extended. Certification rules require the stall warning to begin at least 5 kt or 5 % above the stall speed, whichever is greater.

On a light aircraft the warning comes from a small flapper switch on the wing leading edge: near the stall the stagnation point moves down and back, the airflow lifts the flapper and the switch sounds a horn. Some trainers use a reed horn instead, sounded by air sucked through a slot. Transports use an angle of attack vane on the fuselage side, a small weathervane whose rotary transducer sends α to the stall warning computer, which also receives flap and slat position and Mach number from the air data computer, because the stall angle changes with both.

Near the stall angle the computer drives the stick shaker, an electric motor turning an unbalanced weight on the control column, often with a horn or voice call. The shaker is a warning: it tells the crew to lower the nose.

Types whose stall is hard to recover, such as many T-tail jets, add a stick pusher: at a higher α an actuator pushes the column forward to reduce α even if the crew delays. Because a pusher acting wrongly is itself a hazard, it is inhibited on the ground by the squat switch, needs both α channels to agree, and can be cut out by the crew. Fly-by-wire aircraft go further: alpha protection limits α in the flight control laws, and alpha floor commands full thrust.

Worked example

Question. An airliner of mass 64 000 kg has a wing area of 122.6 m². At sea level (ρ = 1.225 kg/m³) its CLmax is 1.5 clean and 2.8 with full flap. Take g = 9.81 m/s² and 1 kt = 0.5144 m/s. Find the stall speed in each configuration, and the lowest speed at which the stall warning may start with full flap.

  1. Weight: W = 64 000 × 9.81 = 627 840 N.
  2. Clean: VS = √(2 × 627 840 ÷ (1.225 × 122.6 × 1.5)) = √(1 255 680 ÷ 225.28) = √5573.9 = 74.66 m/s = 145.1 kt.
  3. Full flap: VS = √(1 255 680 ÷ (1.225 × 122.6 × 2.8)) = √(1 255 680 ÷ 420.52) = √2986.0 = 54.64 m/s = 106.2 kt.
  4. Warning margin with full flap: 5 % of 106.2 = 5.3 kt, which is more than 5 kt, so the margin is 5.3 kt.
  5. Lowest warning speed: 106.2 + 5.3 = 111.5 kt.

Answer. The stall speed is 145.1 kt clean and 106.2 kt with full flap; the warning must start at 111.5 kt or above. The stall computer needs flap position because the same α limit does not fit both configurations.

Exam traps

  • Do not swap the stick shaker and stick pusher. The shaker warns; the pusher acts.
  • The gear warning needs gear not down plus throttles at idle, or landing flap; gear up at high power does not sound it.
  • A squat switch is not a lock switch. It senses weight on the strut; lock and door positions come from proximity sensors.
  • Asymmetry compares left with right wing; skew compares panels on the same wing. Both stop the drive.
  • Stall speed varies as √W, so a heavier aircraft stalls faster: 20 % more weight gives √1.2 = 1.095, about 9.5 % more speed.

2.11 Ice and rain protection systems

Ice on an aircraft destroys lift, adds weight and blinds the sensors that tell the crew their speed and height. Ice protection keeps the critical surfaces clear: hot air or electric heaters stop ice forming, and rubber boots or fluid remove it after it has formed. Rain protection keeps the windscreen clear so the runway stays visible. Most of it is switched, timed and monitored electrically.

You need: Chapter 2.2 for the bleed air that feeds thermal anti-icing.

Why ice is dangerous

Most airframe icing happens between 0 °C and about −20 °C in cloud or rain holding supercooled droplets: water still liquid below freezing. A droplet freezes as soon as it hits a surface at or below 0 °C, and wing leading edges, spinners, windscreens and probes are exactly that in flight.

  • Extra weight: a 3 mm layer over 12 m² of wing is 32.4 kg on one side.
  • Loss of lift: the rough leading edge breaks the smooth airflow, so CLmax falls and drag rises.
  • Earlier stall: lower CLmax and higher weight both raise the stall speed.
  • Tailplane: its thinner section and shorter chord make a thin layer a large fraction of the chord, so pitch control can be lost when the flaps go down.
  • Blind sensors: ice in a pitot head gives a false airspeed, and on a static port a false altitude as well.
  • Engine and propeller: ice shed into the engine damages blades and can cause a compressor stall; ice on a propeller causes vibration and thrust loss.
Vstall = √(2W ÷ (ρ S CLmax))

Vstall is the stall speed in m/s; W is the aircraft weight in N, including the ice; ρ is air density in kg/m³; S is wing area in m²; CLmax is the maximum lift coefficient, which falls when the leading edge is rough.

Ice also costs time: after freezing rain the aircraft needs fluid sprayed by a de-icing rig before departure, and the crew checks the fluid's holdover time.

Anti-icing, de-icing and the methods

Two words carry most of the marks. Anti-icing prevents ice forming on a surface: the skin is held above 0 °C, or coated with fluid that stops the droplet freezing. De-icing lets ice form and then removes it. One aircraft often uses both: hot air on the wing leading edges and a boot on the tail, or boots on the wing and electric heat on the probes.

Bleed air is not free. It comes from the engine compressor, so thrust falls and fuel burn rises while anti-icing is on, and the ducting, valves and regulators add weight and maintenance. Electric mats need generator capacity instead, so they suit small areas and short duty cycles.

Pneumatic boots: an older rule told crews to wait for 6 to 13 mm of ice before the first cycle, for fear of ice bridging (a thin layer stretching with the boot and staying on it). Tests on modern boots found bridging very rare, so current guidance for most types is to start the boots at the first sign of ice and keep them cycling on the timer, following the flight manual.

Protected areas, ice detection and rain removal

Protection is fitted where ice does the most harm, not over the whole airframe; weight and cost forbid that.

  • Wing leading edges, and the leading edges of the horizontal and vertical tail.
  • Engine inlet lips, the spinner and, on turboprops, the propeller blades (electric mats at the root).
  • Pitot heads, angle of attack vanes and the total air temperature probe, heated electrically; static ports on some types.
  • Windscreen, heated electrically for both ice and mist.
  • Water drain masts and lines, so they do not freeze.

Detection can be visual: a wing inspection light lets the crew see ice at night. Automatic ice detectors put a probe in the airstream, often a rod vibrating at a set frequency that falls as ice mass builds; at a set drop the detector lights an ICE annunciator, heats the probe clean and starts again. On some types it switches the anti-icing or boots on directly.

Rain removal works on the windscreen. Wipers are driven by an electric or hydraulic motor, and a spring holds the blade on the glass. Some jets use a rain-removal air curtain, a jet of hot bleed air blown across the screen, and many now rely on a permanent hydrophobic coating that makes water bead and blow away. The windscreen heater has an embedded temperature sensor and a controller that holds the glass warm, often on two power levels, with overheat protection so the layers do not delaminate.

Worked example

Question. A turboprop of mass 20 000 kg has a wing area of 61 m² and a clean CLmax of 1.6 at sea level (ρ = 1.225 kg/m³, g = 9.81 m/s²). Ice adds 100 kg and lowers CLmax to 1.25. Find the stall speed before and after, in m/s and knots (1 kt = 0.5144 m/s).

  1. Clean: W = 20 000 × 9.81 = 196 200 N; VS = √(2 × 196 200 ÷ (1.225 × 61 × 1.6)) = √(392 400 ÷ 119.56) = √3282.0 = 57.29 m/s = 111.4 kt.
  2. Iced: W = 20 100 × 9.81 = 197 181 N; VS = √(2 × 197 181 ÷ (1.225 × 61 × 1.25)) = √(394 362 ÷ 93.41) = √4221.8 = 64.98 m/s = 126.3 kt.
  3. Rise: 126.3 − 111.4 = 14.9 kt, or 13.4 %.
  4. Split the causes: the weight alone gives √(20 100 ÷ 20 000) = 1.0025, a 0.25 % rise; the CLmax loss gives √(1.6 ÷ 1.25) = 1.131, a 13.1 % rise.

Answer. The stall speed rises from 111.4 kt to 126.3 kt. Almost all of the rise comes from the lost lift, not from the weight of the ice.

Exam traps

  • Calling a pneumatic boot anti-icing. It is de-icing: it lets ice form and then cracks it off.
  • Listing only the wing when asked where protection is fitted. Tailplane, engine inlet, propeller, pitot and static heads, windscreen and drain masts all count.
  • Writing that boots must always wait for a thick layer. That is old guidance; modern boots are cycled from the first sign of ice unless the flight manual says otherwise.
  • Blaming the ice weight for the higher stall speed. A few kilograms of ice matter far less than the lost CLmax; and 3 mm is 0.003 m, not 0.03 m, in the mass sum.
  • Forgetting that bleed-air anti-icing steals compressor air, so thrust falls and fuel burn rises while the system is on.

Understand Why does a rough, iced leading edge raise the stall speed? 3 marks

Roughness makes the airflow separate earlier, so the maximum lift coefficient C_Lmax falls. In V_stall = √(2W ÷ (ρ S C_Lmax)) a smaller C_Lmax raises the stall speed. The added ice weight raises W, which raises it further.

The tail has a thinner section and shorter chord, so the same ice thickness is a larger fraction of its chord and spoils its flow sooner. When the flaps go down the tail must make more down-load at a larger negative angle, close to its own stall; if it stalls the nose pitches down at low height.

Analyze Compare bleed-air thermal anti-icing with electro-thermal mats for a twin turboprop on a regional route. 5 marks

At high speed the airflow can lift the blade off the glass and the arm loads are large. Heat, an air curtain or a hydrophobic coating clear the screen with no moving arm, so some aircraft rely on those.

Not for the exam. The 787 heats its wing leading edges electrically, trading bleed air for generator power. Research follows icephobic coatings and electro-expulsive systems that shatter ice with a short pulse. Search for "electro-expulsive de-icing" and "icephobic coating".

Takeaways

  1. A hydraulic system is the muscle of the aircraft: F = pA at about 3000 psi, with two or three independent systems and normal, alternate and emergency modes.
  2. An accumulator stores only a little usable fluid (Boyle’s law), enough for a few brake applications after the pumps stop.
  3. One regulated bleed-air manifold feeds the packs, anti-ice and engine start, so a bleed fault touches many systems; stored-gas bottles give one-shot emergency power.
  4. The oleo strut stores landing energy in nitrogen and turns the bounce into heat in the oil; brakes turn ½mv² into heat in the discs.
  5. Fuel is metered by a unit the FADEC commands; oil cools and lubricates the bearings and carries debris to the chip detectors.
  6. Starting ends well above self-sustaining speed; ignition is on before fuel, and a hot, hung or wet start is aborted by cutting fuel.
  7. The outflow valve, not the supply, sets cabin pressure; the air cycle turbine cools the air by taking work out of it, and a vapour cycle moves heat with a refrigerant.
  8. Oxygen keeps the oxygen partial pressure in the lungs up when the cabin is high, and must be on within the time of useful consciousness.
  9. Fire detection must be fast, cover the whole zone and almost never cry wolf; each bottle discharge is one-shot, so engines carry two shots.
  10. Gear, flap, stall and ice systems are electrical control and indication on hydraulic or electric drives: sensors, logic, lamps and warnings.

Model answer.

  1. Principle: compressed air from an engine compressor, regulated in pressure and temperature, is the aircraft’s source of pneumatic power. Taking air the engine has already compressed saves carrying a separate compressor for each job.
  2. Source selection: air is taken from a low-pressure (LP) stage at most powers. At low power the LP pressure is too low, so the HP valve opens and HP air feeds the system; a check valve stops HP air flowing back into the LP port. As power rises the HP valve closes.
  3. Temperature: the precooler, a heat exchanger cooled by engine fan air through a fan-air valve, brings the bleed down to about 200 °C before it enters the wing and fuselage ducts.
  4. Pressure: the pressure regulating and shut-off valve holds the downstream pressure to a design value of a few hundred kPa and closes fully on command or on a fault, under a bleed monitoring computer that reads duct pressure and temperature.
  5. Distribution: the regulated air enters a manifold. Check valves let each source feed it but stop reverse flow; isolation (cross-bleed) valves split it into left, centre and right parts. The APU and a ground cart join downstream of the engine precooler and regulator, so they work with the engines stopped.

Understand Why does an aircraft need cabin pressurisation, and how is cabin altitude controlled in normal operation? 13 marks

Points to cover. Above about 10 000 ft the partial pressure of oxygen falls and hypoxia sets in; Pressurisation keeps cabin altitude typically at or below 8000 ft; Conditioned air is fed in continuously and the outflow valve controls how much escapes; Cabin pressure controller schedules cabin altitude against aircraft altitude and limits the differential pressure; Differential pressure limited (about 8 to 9 psi in many transports) to protect the hull; Relief valves: positive pressure relief and negative pressure relief; Landing schedule brings the cabin to ambient before touchdown.

Model answer.

  1. Oxygen: air is about 21 % oxygen at every height, but its partial pressure falls with the total pressure: about 21.3 kPa at sea level and 5.0 kPa at 35 000 ft (23.8 kPa ambient). Above about 10 000 ft the blood starts to load too little oxygen and hypoxia sets in: poor judgement, then loss of consciousness.
  2. Time of useful consciousness, the time from losing oxygen to being unable to act usefully, falls from about 3 to 5 minutes at 25 000 ft to 30 to 60 s at 35 000 ft, so a cabin that stays at aircraft altitude is never acceptable on a jet.
  3. Trapped gas: by Boyle’s law a litre of gas in the ears, sinuses or gut at 101.3 kPa becomes 101.3 ÷ 23.8 = 4.3 L at 35 000 ft, which causes pain and injury.
  4. So the cabin is held at a cabin altitude, the standard-atmosphere height whose pressure equals the cabin pressure, normally at or below 8000 ft (75.3 kPa).
  5. Control: the packs feed conditioned air at a steady mass flow. The outflow valve, a motor-driven gate at the rear, sets how much leaves; closing it raises cabin pressure and opening it lowers it. The supply only has to exceed leakage plus outflow.
  6. The cabin pressure controller follows a schedule:
    PhaseOutflow valve and cabin
    GroundFully open; cabin at field pressure, no differential
    Take-off and climbCloses gradually; cabin climbs at a limited rate, a few hundred ft/min
    CruiseHolds the cabin altitude constant
    DescentOpens as needed so the cabin reaches field elevation at touchdown
    LandingFully open, so the doors can be opened
  7. Controller modes: automatic, where the controller takes the planned cruise altitude and the landing field elevation and works out the schedule; a standby or semi-automatic mode where the crew sets a cabin altitude and rate; and manual, where the crew drives the outflow valve motor directly with a switch while watching the cabin altitude, rate and Δp.
  8. Rate check: a cabin climbing to 8000 ft at 500 ft/min takes 8000 ÷ 500 = 16 minutes, which matches a normal airliner climb, so the cabin rate can stay within comfort limits.
  9. Altitude limit: at a maximum Δp of 55 kPa the cabin can be held at 75.3 kPa only while the ambient pressure is at least 75.3 − 55 = 20.3 kPa; above that the cabin altitude must rise with the aircraft.
  10. Limits: the hull is designed for a maximum differential pressure, about 55 to 60 kPa (8 to 9 psi) on airliners. A positive pressure relief valve opens outward above it; a negative pressure relief valve opens inward if outside pressure exceeds cabin pressure.

Why each discharge is one-shot: the agent is stored as a liquid under nitrogen pressure and released by a squib, a small explosive cartridge that ruptures a disc; the disc cannot be closed again, so the whole bottle empties in a second or two. A fast, full discharge reaches the design concentration before the airflow through the zone carries it away.
  6. Agent choice: Halon 1301 breaks the chemical chain of combustion, so about 5 to 6 % by volume puts out a fire and leaves no residue on engines or baggage. It depletes ozone and is no longer made, so the industry uses recycled stock while replacement agents are certified.
  7. Cargo classes: a Class C hold (the usual lower-deck hold) has smoke detection and a built-in system fired from the cockpit; a Class E hold on a freighter has detection, and the crew shuts off its airflow to starve the fire.
  8. Because one discharge may not be enough, engines usually have two bottles, either one per engine with cross-feed or two per engine, so the crew can fire a second shot if the warning stays on (typically after about 30 s). Cargo holds use a high-rate bottle followed by metered bottles to keep the concentration up for the rest of the flight.
  9. Agent for a 40 m³ cargo hold at 6 % by volume, agent vapour density 6 kg/m³:
    Va = C × Vz ÷ (1 − C) = 0.06 × 40 ÷ 0.94 = 2.553 m³
m = 2.553 × 6 = 15.3 kg
  10. Evaluation: the design trades weight against certainty. Bottles are heavy, so their number is kept small, while detection is duplicated because it is light; the result is a system where a single wire break or a single failed squib does not leave a zone unprotected.
  11. After a discharge the crew lands at the nearest suitable airport: the bottles cannot be recharged in flight, and a cargo fire is only held down, not always put out.
  12. Testing: the crew tests the loops and squib circuits before flight with a low current that proves continuity but cannot fire the squib; bottle pressure gauges or switches show a bottle that has leaked.
  13. False-alarm cost: an engine fire warning leads to an engine shutdown and a diversion; a cargo smoke warning leads to a discharge and an urgent landing. Each false alarm costs money and trust, so the AND logic and delays are as much part of safety as fast detection.
  14. Human side: the zone is named on the warning, the handle or button for that zone lights, and the checklist is short, so a crew under stress fires the right bottle into the right zone.
  15. Check: the simple form C × Vz would give 2.4 m³ and 14.4 kg, 0.9 kg less; a bottle sized that way would fall 6 % short of the design concentration.

Glossary

Air cycle machine
A compressor-turbine pair on one shaft that cools air by expanding it through the turbine.
Anti-skid system
A system that releases brake pressure when a wheel starts to skid, so the tyre keeps grip and does not burst.
Bleed manifold
The ducting that collects regulated bleed air from the engines and distributes it to the using systems.
Cabin rate
The rate of change of cabin pressure, normally limited to a few hundred feet per minute for passenger comfort.
Chip detector
A magnetic plug in the oil line that collects metal particles and warns of bearing or gear wear.
De-icing
Removing ice after it has formed, for example by inflating a pneumatic boot on the wing leading edge.
Differential pressure
The difference between cabin pressure and outside ambient pressure; the hull is sized for a fixed maximum.
Diluter-demand regulator
A crew mask regulator that opens on inhalation and mixes cabin air with oxygen, giving pure oxygen at high cabin altitude.
Downlock
A mechanical lock that holds the landing gear extended and carries the load off the actuator.
Exciter
The ignition unit that charges a capacitor and dumps its energy into the igniter plug as a spark.
Expansion valve
In a vapour cycle, a valve that drops the refrigerant pressure so it evaporates and absorbs heat.
Hydraulic fluid
A mineral or phosphate-ester based oil that carries pressure, lubricates moving parts and must not ignite easily.
Igniter plug
A plug that takes a high-energy pulse from the exciter and makes a spark in the combustor.
Oxygen partial pressure
The share of total gas pressure contributed by oxygen; the body needs a minimum value to stay conscious.
Packing
The seals in a hydraulic actuator that stop fluid leaking past the piston.
Power transfer unit
A hydraulic motor-pump pair that lets one system’s pressure drive a pump in another without mixing the fluids.
Precooler
A heat exchanger that cools hot engine bleed air with fan air before it enters the aircraft ducts.
Pressure relief valve
A valve that opens at a set pressure to return fluid to the reservoir and so protect the system from overpressure.
Proximity sensor
A sensor that detects a metal target without contact; it tells the gear control unit that a lock or door is in position.
Scavenge pump
A pump that returns used oil from the bearings and gearbox back to the oil tank.
Self-sustaining speed
The engine speed above which combustion keeps the engine accelerating without help from the starter.
Servo valve
A valve that positions its output in proportion to a small electrical or mechanical command signal.
Shock strut
The gas and oil leg of a landing gear that carries static weight and absorbs landing and taxi loads.
Squib
A small explosive cartridge that ruptures the disc of an extinguisher bottle when fired.
Stick pusher
An actuator that moves the control column forward automatically to break a stall.
Time of useful consciousness
The time from losing oxygen to being unable to act usefully; about 30 to 60 s at 35 000 ft.
Uplock
A hook on the landing gear that holds the retracted leg in place.
Water separator
A unit that removes condensed water from cooled air before it enters the cabin.
