2.1 Hydraulic systems: components, controllers and modes

A hydraulic system uses a confined liquid to carry force from a pump to a place where motion is needed. It stores fluid under pressure, sends it through valves, and lets a small pump move a heavy surface. Aircraft use it for landing gear, brakes, flaps and flight controls because it is stiff and quick. This chapter follows the fluid from reservoir to actuator and then looks at the controllers that pick the supply path and the operating mode.

You need Unit 1 for what the actuators move; see 2.3 for gear and brakes that use this supply.

Pressure, force and supply layout

Pascal's law says pressure applied to a trapped liquid is transmitted equally in all directions. A pump creates pressure p; an actuator piston of area A receives force F. The working relation is:

F = p × A

F is force in newtons (N); p is pressure in pascals (Pa); A is piston area in square metres (m²).

Pressure is not force. At the same pressure, doubling the piston area doubles the force. A typical transport hydraulic system runs at 3000 psi, about 207 bar or 20.7 MPa. High pressure keeps the actuator and pipe sizes small, which saves weight.

In an open-centre system the pump output can return to the reservoir at low pressure when no user needs flow. In a closed-centre system the pump holds pressure and reduces its displacement when demand falls; a relief valve still protects the line. Transport aircraft usually use a closed-centre, variable-displacement arrangement so the pump does not waste power at idle.

The supply chain is reservoir → pumps → accumulator → selector valve → actuator → return. The reservoir stores fluid and lets air and dirt separate; the filter traps particles before they reach the valves.

Components in the flow path

An engine-driven pump is the main source in flight. An electric pump supplies ground checks, backup pressure or a second system. A hand pump gives a last-resort supply for a small essential load. Some syllabi call the accumulator an oil-gas accumulator: a cylinder with hydraulic fluid on one side and nitrogen gas on the other, separated by a piston or bladder. The nitrogen precharge is set on the ground; it is not the working pressure. The accumulator smooths pump pulses, covers short peak flows and gives a limited emergency supply.

A relief valve opens to return when pressure exceeds a set limit. A selector valve is commanded by the pilot and sends fluid to one side of the actuator. A servo valve moves a spool in proportion to an electrical or mechanical command for fine control. A hydraulic fuse closes when flow is much higher than normal, so a broken pipe does not empty the system. The actuator converts pressure into linear motion, and the return line carries low-pressure fluid back to the reservoir.

Filters sit on the pressure and return lines. A clogged filter opens a bypass valve so fluid can still flow, and a sensor warns the crew. Fluid quantity, pressure and temperature are watched because a leak often shows first as falling quantity, not as falling pressure.

Controllers and operating modes

A pump controller adjusts displacement or drive to hold pressure and may unload the pump when no user demands flow. It protects the pump from overheating and overpressure. Mode logic selects which pumps and valves are active. The table is a common pattern; exact names and users differ by aircraft.

ModeSupplyTypical use
NormalMain engine-driven pump, electric pump on standbyAll hydraulic users
AlternateElectric pump or a second hydraulic systemEssential users only
EmergencyAccumulator, hand pump or another power sourceGear, brakes and minimum control

In normal mode the main pump supplies all users. In alternate mode a backup pump or a second system supplies essential users. In emergency mode the crew relies on stored pressure or a hand pump for a short list of loads. The crew monitors pressure, fluid quantity and temperature; a falling quantity with normal pressure points to a leak.

Worked example

Question. An actuator piston has diameter 5 cm and rod diameter 2 cm. The hydraulic system supplies 207 bar. Find the extension force and the retraction force.

  1. Convert pressure: 207 bar = 207 × 10⁵ Pa = 2.07 × 10⁷ Pa.
  2. Piston radius r = 0.05 m ÷ 2 = 0.025 m. Area A = πr² = π(0.025 m)² = 1.963 × 10⁻³ m².
  3. Extension force F = p × A = (2.07 × 10⁷ Pa)(1.963 × 10⁻³ m²) = 4.064 × 10⁴ N = 40.64 kN.
  4. Rod radius = 0.02 m ÷ 2 = 0.010 m. Rod area = π(0.010 m)² = 3.142 × 10⁻⁴ m².
  5. Annulus area = 1.963 × 10⁻³ − 3.142 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.649 × 10⁻³ m².
  6. Retraction force = (2.07 × 10⁷ Pa)(1.649 × 10⁻³ m²) = 3.414 × 10⁴ N = 34.14 kN.

Answer. The extension force is about 40.6 kN and the retraction force is about 34.1 kN.

Exam traps

  • Do not treat the accumulator's nitrogen precharge as the system working pressure; the precharge is a gas pressure behind a separator, and hydraulic pressure comes from the pump.
  • 3000 psi is about 207 bar, not 3000 kPa. 3000 psi is near 20.7 MPa.
  • Extension and retraction forces differ because the rod reduces the effective area on one side.
  • Open-centre and closed-centre describe idle flow behaviour, not whether the system is pressurised in flight.
  • A hydraulic fuse is a flow-limiting safety device, not an electrical fuse.

2.2 Pneumatic systems and typical air pressure system

Bleed air is compressed air taken from an engine compressor and used, after regulation, as the aircraft's clean source of pressure and heat. Instead of carrying a separate compressor for every job, the aircraft taps air the engine has already compressed. That air drives cabin cooling and pressurisation, clears ice from the wing and intake, and spins the engine during start. This chapter follows the air from the bleed port to the users, and names the valves and protections that keep pressure and temperature inside safe limits.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for how valves and actuators are driven, and Chapter 2.6 for what happens to the air after it leaves the manifold.

Where the air comes from

An engine compressor raises the pressure and temperature of air before combustion. A small fraction of that air is taken out through a bleed port and sent to the aircraft. This is cheaper than carrying a separate compressor for cabin air, and the air is already hot and dry. Pressure at the port depends on which compressor stage is tapped and on engine power. A low-pressure (LP) port gives cooler air at moderate pressure. A high-pressure (HP) port gives higher pressure but hotter air.

At low engine power the LP stage cannot supply enough pressure. The HP valve opens and HP air feeds the system. As power rises, LP pressure becomes enough; the HP valve closes and a check valve stops HP air from flowing backwards into the LP duct. So the source switches with power, and bleed is not always available from the HP stage.

Three sources feed the manifold: engine bleed (HP or LP), the auxiliary power unit (APU) on the ground and during engine start, and a ground cart connected at a ground service port. Before cooling, bleed air may sit at a few hundred kPa and a few hundred degrees Celsius. One kPa is 1000 Pa, and 100 kPa is about one atmosphere.

Regulating and distributing the air

The first heat exchanger is the precooler. Fan air passes on one side and hot bleed on the other. The precooler lowers the bleed temperature, but the air leaving it is still hot enough to need heat protection on ducts. A fan-air valve controls the cooling flow.

Next is the pressure regulating and shut-off valve (PRV). The PRV regulates the downstream pressure to a design value, usually a few hundred kPa, and can close fully to shut off the bleed. It is controlled by a bleed monitoring computer that reads pressure and temperature sensors in the duct.

The regulated air enters a manifold: a network of ducts that runs along the fuselage. Check valves allow flow from a source into the manifold but block reverse flow, so a failed engine bleed cannot pressurise another engine's ducts. Isolation valves split the manifold into sections. If one section leaks, the crew can close an isolation valve and keep the other engine supplying the remaining users.

Protection is layered. For overpressure, the PRV closes and a relief valve opens if the PRV fails. For overheat, duct sensors trip a warning and close the affected valve. The crew sees the fault on the bleed panel or the electronic centralised aircraft monitor (ECAM), depending on the aircraft.

Users of the manifold

The manifold feeds several users, each through its own valve:

  • Air conditioning packs take bleed air and cool it; the air cycle machine is covered in Chapter 2.6.
  • Wing and engine anti-ice use hot bleed air to keep surfaces above freezing; see Chapter 2.11.
  • Engine starting uses bleed air to turn the starter; see Chapter 2.5.
  • Hydraulic reservoirs and water tanks are pressurised with bleed air to prevent pump cavitation and to supply water.
  • Some door seals inflate with bleed air.

The packs supply conditioned air to the cabin; the outflow valve then controls cabin altitude. Cabin pressure is a downstream result of pack air, not a separate bleed-fed user.

On a typical twin-engine aircraft, either engine can supply the whole manifold through the isolation valves. The APU supplies the manifold on the ground when engines are off; its air joins downstream of the precooler and the PRV, so no engine fan air is needed to cool it. This shared manifold is why a single bleed leak can affect several systems, and why isolation valves matter.

ṁ = ρ × A × v

ṁ is mass flow in kg/s, ρ is air density in kg/m³, A is duct cross-section area in m², and v is mean velocity in m/s.

Worked example

Question. A bleed duct carries 0.30 kg/s of air at 250 kPa and 200 °C. The duct area is 0.010 m². Take R = 287 J/(kg·K). Find the air density and the mean velocity.

  1. Convert temperature to kelvin: T = 200 + 273 = 473 K.
  2. Convert pressure to pascal: p = 250 kPa = 250 000 Pa.
  3. Apply the ideal-gas relation: ρ = p/(R × T) = 250 000/(287 × 473).
  4. Compute: 287 × 473 = 135 751, so ρ = 250 000/135 751 = 1.84 kg/m³.
  5. Use mass flow: ṁ = ρ × A × v, so v = ṁ/(ρ × A).
  6. Substitute: v = 0.30/(1.84 × 0.010) = 16.29 m/s.

Answer. The density is 1.84 kg/m³ and the mean velocity is 16.29 m/s.

Exam traps

  • Bleed is always available: at low engine power only the HP stage can supply it; the HP valve closes again at high power. Do not assume the HP port is the normal source at cruise.
  • Confusing the precooler with the air cycle machine. The precooler is a fan-air heat exchanger before the manifold; the air cycle machine is inside the pack and cools by expansion.
  • Using gauge pressure instead of absolute pressure in ρ = p/(RT). The formula needs absolute pressure in Pa and absolute temperature in K.
  • Thinking the isolation valve is a check valve. A check valve is one-way and automatic; an isolation valve is opened or closed by command to split the manifold.
  • Forgetting that overheat protection closes the bleed valve, not the pack valve.

2.3 Landing gear and brake systems

Landing gear carries the aircraft on the ground, and the brakes stop it. The gear uses an oleo strut: a cylinder of nitrogen gas acts as a spring, and oil forced through a small hole acts as a damper. The brakes turn the aircraft's kinetic energy into heat, so their design is really a heat problem. Every landing and every rejected takeoff depends on this pair working together, from a light trainer at a small airfield to a widebody on a long runway.

You need the hydraulic supply and actuator ideas from Chapter 2.1.

Gear layout and the oleo strut

Landing gear is classified first by whether it moves. Fixed gear stays out, and light aircraft use it because it is simple and light. Retractable gear folds away in flight to cut drag, and it costs actuators, locks and doors. Gear is also classed by layout: nose wheel or tail wheel, and main gear with a single wheel, multiple wheels, or a bogie (a beam carrying two or more wheel pairs). A nose-wheel layout is stable on the ground; a tail-wheel layout is lighter but tends to swing.

The oleo strut does two jobs with one cylinder. Nitrogen gas above the oil is the spring: it compresses and stores the landing energy, then pushes the aircraft back up. Oil is the damper: as the strut moves, oil is forced through an orifice, and friction in the oil turns bounce energy into heat. Gas alone would bounce like a ball; oil alone would not spring back. A static seal joins two parts that do not move relative to each other. A dynamic seal rides on the sliding piston and keeps oil in and dirt out while the strut moves.

F = p × A

F is the upward force from the strut in newtons (N), p is the nitrogen pressure in pascals (Pa), and A is the piston area in square metres (m²). A larger piston or higher pressure carries a heavier aircraft.

Tyres are the only contact with the runway. They are tubeless on most aircraft, inflated with nitrogen to reduce fire risk, and checked cold because pressure rises with heat. Each tyre has a rated load, so a bogie spreads the aircraft weight over more tyres and lowers the runway strength needed.

Retraction, locks and the squat switch

A hydraulic actuator pushes the gear down or pulls it up; on some aircraft an electric motor drives the same linkage. The gear must be held in both end positions, not just moved there. An uplock catches the gear in the up position so it cannot fall out, and a downlock holds it down so it cannot fold on landing. The downlock is the one that matters at touchdown: a gear that is down but not locked will collapse.

Sequence keeps the aircraft safe. The selector sends a command, the doors open, the gear moves, the doors close, and sensors confirm each position. A squat switch (weight-on-wheels switch) on the gear leg senses that the aircraft is on the ground and blocks retraction. Without it, a pilot could select gear up while parked. The same switch also arms other ground-only logic.

  • Uplock: holds gear up, released before extension.
  • Downlock: holds gear down, released before retraction.
  • Squat switch: says the aircraft is on the ground and stops retraction.
  • Proximity sensor: tells the cockpit the gear is actually locked, not just commanded.

Gear position indication and the control logic that sequences gear with flaps are covered in Chapter 2.10. The hydraulic pressure that drives the actuators comes from Chapter 2.1.

Brakes: heat, discs and antiskid

A brake is an energy converter. It takes the aircraft's kinetic energy and turns it into heat in the discs. Single-disc brakes suit light aircraft; multi-disc brakes stack several discs to get more friction area in a small wheel. Carbon-carbon discs are used on large aircraft because they are light and survive very high temperatures. Steel discs are cheaper and work well at lower energy.

E = ½ × m × v²

E is kinetic energy in joules (J), m is aircraft mass in kilograms (kg), and v is landing speed in metres per second (m/s). Because v is squared, a small increase in speed raises brake energy a lot.

Hydraulic pressure from the brake system squeezes the discs through pistons. Antiskid works like this: a wheel speed sensor measures each wheel. If a wheel slows too fast, it is about to skid, so the antiskid valve releases pressure for a moment and lets the wheel spin up again. This keeps friction near its best value and protects the tyre. Autobrake selects a fixed deceleration level after touchdown, and the parking brake holds the aircraft with accumulator pressure when the pumps are off.

Brake temperature is a real limit. Carbon discs can glow after a rejected takeoff, and the wheel must be allowed to cool before the aircraft parks near fuel. Some syllabi list a rocket separation mechanism beside landing gear; that is stage separation on a booster, where clamps release and springs or small rockets push the stages apart. It is not an aircraft landing gear.

Worked example

Question. A 60 000 kg aircraft touches down at 250 km/h. Its four main-wheel brakes absorb 60 % of the kinetic energy, shared equally. Find the landing speed in m/s, the total kinetic energy, and the energy taken by each main brake.

  1. Convert speed: v = 250 / 3.6 = 69.44 m/s.
  2. Kinetic energy: E = ½ × 60 000 × (69.44)² = ½ × 60 000 × 4822.5 = 144.7 MJ.
  3. Brake share: E_brakes = 0.60 × 144.7 = 86.8 MJ.
  4. Each of four brakes: E_one = 86.8 / 4 = 21.7 MJ.

Answer. The landing speed is 69.4 m/s, the total kinetic energy is 145 MJ, and each main brake absorbs 21.7 MJ.

Exam traps

  • Swapping the oleo roles: nitrogen gas is the spring, oil is the damper, not the other way round.
  • Forgetting that brake energy rises with the square of speed, so a 10 % faster landing is about 21 % more heat.
  • Confusing uplock with downlock. The downlock holds the gear rigidly in the down position so the leg cannot fold at touchdown.
  • Thinking antiskid increases braking pressure. It releases pressure to stop a wheel skidding.
  • Treating the squat switch as a brake part. It is a weight-on-wheels switch that blocks gear retraction on the ground.

2.4 Engine fuel and lubricating systems

The fuel system takes fuel from the tanks and meters the right flow to the combustor. The oil system pushes oil through the engine bearings, removes heat and carries wear debris to a filter and chip detector. Every start, climb and cruise depends on both. Only a few universities teach this unit in detail, so keep the focus on the path and the limits. You meet these systems in pre-start checks, fuel-flow readings and oil pressure warnings.

You need: engine control basics from Unit 1.

Fuel path and metering

Fuel is stored in wing or fuselage tanks. A boost pump in the tank raises pressure so the fuel does not boil at altitude; this is low pressure. The fuel then passes a fuel-oil heat exchanger, where hot oil warms the fuel and the fuel cools the oil. A high-pressure pump driven by the engine gearbox raises pressure for the metering unit. A filter traps dirt. The metering unit, commanded by the FADEC (full authority digital engine control), sets the flow for the thrust demanded. FADEC is the control system; the metering unit is the valve it drives. A flow meter sends flow to the cockpit. Refuel and jettison lines let ground crew fill or dump fuel. Chapter 2.5 covers the start that follows.

ṁ = ρ × V̇

ṁ is fuel mass flow in kg/s; ρ is density; V̇ is volume flow. With SI units, ρ in kg/m³ and V̇ in m³/s. With ρ in kg/L, V̇ must be in L/s.

  • The LP boost pump stops vapour lock; the HP pump makes metering pressure.
  • The filter bypass keeps fuel flowing if the filter blocks.
  • The metering unit controls flow; it does not make pressure.

Oil system: heat and debris

A jet engine usually has a dry-sump oil system: oil is kept in a separate tank, not in the engine. A pressure pump sends oil through a filter and cooler to bearings and gears. Oil takes heat from the bearings and carries metal particles. Scavenge pumps return oil from the bearing chambers to the tank. The filter has a bypass: if it blocks, unfiltered oil still reaches the bearings. A chip detector is a magnetic plug that catches steel particles and warns the crew. Oil pressure and temperature limits are in the flight manual.

Q = ṁ × c × ΔT

Q is heat rate in kW; ṁ is oil mass flow in kg/s; c is specific heat in kJ/(kg·K); ΔT is oil temperature rise in K.

  • Wet sump stores oil in the engine sump; dry sump uses a tank and is common on jets.
  • The cooler may be the fuel-oil heat exchanger or a separate air-oil cooler.
  • A filter bypass warning means unfiltered oil is flowing.

Worked example

Question. An engine burns 1200 kg/h of fuel of density 0.8 kg/L. Find the volume flow in L/s. Separately, 0.5 kg/s of oil with c = 2.0 kJ/(kg·K) picks up 60 kW. Find the oil temperature rise.

  1. Fuel volume flow = mass flow ÷ density = 1200 kg/h ÷ 0.8 kg/L = 1500 L/h.
  2. Convert to seconds: 1500 L/h ÷ 3600 s/h = 0.4167 L/s, which rounds to 0.42 L/s.
  3. Oil heat rate Q = 60 kW = 60 kJ/s.
  4. ΔT = Q ÷ (ṁ × c) = 60 ÷ (0.5 × 2.0) = 60 ÷ 1.0 = 60 K.

Answer. Fuel volume flow is 0.42 L/s and oil temperature rise is 60 K.

Exam traps

  • Boost pump and HP pump do different jobs; do not swap their pressures or purposes.
  • Filter bypass keeps oil flowing but sends unfiltered oil to bearings; it is not a clean-oil path.
  • The fuel-oil heat exchanger heats fuel and cools oil; oil temperature rise uses oil flow and oil specific heat.
  • kg/h to L/s needs density first; dividing by 3600 alone gives wrong units.

2.5 Engine starting and ignition systems

An engine cannot light fuel at standstill. A starter spins the compressor until airflow supports combustion; then ignition and fuel take over. This chapter covers air turbine and electric starters, exciters, start sequencing and abnormal starts. Only a few universities teach this in detail.

You need: Chapter 2.2 for bleed air used by air starters, and Chapter 2.4 for the fuel that must be lit.

Starters: air turbine and electric

A starter is a motor that turns the HP compressor through the accessory gearbox. It supplies torque until self-sustaining speed. Cut-out is scheduled on N2, HP spool speed as a percentage of design speed, not fixed time.

An air turbine starter (ATS) is a small turbine driven by regulated bleed air. A start valve admits air from the APU, the other engine, or a ground cart. A reduction gear and clutch drive the gearbox; the clutch disengages at cut-out so the running engine cannot overspeed the starter. Cross-bleed starting takes air from a running engine; APU starting uses the auxiliary power unit.

An electric starter is a DC series motor, often a starter-generator: it cranks, then generates. A contactor and current limiter protect the supply. Electric starters suit small engines and APUs; large turbofans usually use air starters.

Ignition: exciters and igniter plugs

An exciter takes a low-voltage supply (28 V DC, or 115 V AC on some types, rectified inside). A capacitor stores charge, and a transformer raises it to a high-voltage pulse at the igniter plug. The plug is a spark gap in the combustor liner.

Low-energy ignition, a few joules, suits normal starts. High-energy ignition, tens of joules, gives a spark that survives rain, cold and lean mixtures. Two igniters give redundancy; some engines fire both for start.

Continuous ignition is selected in heavy rain, turbulence and on contaminated runways, so a flameout relights at once. It is also used in volcanic ash, though ash damages the engine. Igniters stay off in cruise to save plug life. The FADEC schedules ignition and fuel.

Low-energy systems use a smaller capacitor and cost less; high-energy systems are heavier but reliable in bad weather.

Start sequence and abnormal starts

The crew selects START. The start valve opens or the starter contactor closes, and the starter cranks N2. Ignition is energised at about 15 % N2. Fuel opens later, at about 20 % N2, when airflow can support a stable mixture. Light-off raises EGT and N2 accelerates. At cut-out, typically 25-40 % N2 on the engines these notes cover, the starter disengages. Cut-out is engine-specific and always above self-sustaining speed; quote it only from the start schedule. The engine then accelerates to idle. In the timeline, starter cranks 0-20 s, igniters at 15 s, fuel at 20 s, light-off near 22 s, idle about 40 s.

Interlocks stop unsafe starts. The start valve will not open with low bleed pressure or if the engine is already rotating above a threshold. Fuel is inhibited below minimum N2. Ignition must be on before fuel. Cut-out must occur above self-sustaining speed.

A hot start is EGT beyond the start limit, from excess fuel or late ignition. A hung start is N2 stalling below idle, from weak starter or low bleed. Dry motoring turns the engine with fuel and ignition off to clear vapour or cool a hot start.

Worked example

Question. A start reaches 60 % N2 in 40 s, linear. Find (a) average acceleration, (b) N2 at 20 s, (c) margin if measured cut-out N2 is 25 % and self-sustaining speed is 20 % N2.

  2. Predicted N2 at 20 s = 1.5 % N2/s × 20 s = 30 % N2.
  3. Measured cut-out N2 is 25 %; self-sustaining speed is 20 %.
  4. Margin = 25 - 20 = 5 percentage points. The measured spool is slower than predicted but still above self-sustaining speed.

Answer. Average acceleration is 1.5 % N2 per second; the linear prediction at 20 s is 30 % N2; measured cut-out N2 of 25 % leaves a 5 percentage-point margin over 20 % self-sustaining speed.

2.6 Cabin pressurisation and air cycle cooling

At 11 km the air outside is thin and cold. A crew cannot breathe it, and the lungs cannot pull enough oxygen out of it. So the cabin is sealed and fed with conditioned bleed air, while a valve at the rear lets the surplus out at a controlled rate. That valve, not the supply, sets the cabin pressure. The supply itself is hot, so a pack cools it and then expands it through a turbine. Every airliner uses this, from a Chennai–Delhi A320 to a long-haul 787.

You need: the bleed air source and its regulation from Chapter 2.2.

Why the cabin must hold a pressure altitude

At 35 000 ft (10 700 m) the air outside sits at about 23.8 kPa. Sea-level air is 101.3 kPa. Oxygen is 21% of dry air by volume at both heights, so the partial pressure of oxygen falls with the total pressure, from about 21.3 kPa to 5.0 kPa. Blood leaving the lungs cannot pick up enough oxygen at that level. The result is hypoxia: dulled judgement first, then loss of consciousness.

Trapped gas is the second problem. By Boyle's law, p × V is constant for a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature. One litre of gas in the middle ear, a sinus or the gut at 101.3 kPa becomes 101.3 ÷ 23.8 = 4.3 litres at 35 000 ft. The body cannot stretch that far. Ear and sinus pain follow, and tissue can be damaged.

So the cabin is held at a cabin altitude: the altitude in the standard atmosphere whose pressure equals the cabin pressure. The usual limit is 8000 ft (2438 m), where the pressure is 75.6 kPa. The fuselage sets a maximum differential pressure, typically about 55–60 kPa (8–9 psi) on an airliner.

Δp = pcabin − pambient        F = Δp × A

Δp is the differential pressure in Pa; pcabin is the pressure inside the cabin; pambient is the pressure outside at the aircraft's altitude; F is the outward force on a panel in N; A is the panel area in m².

Design lives between the two limits. At cruise the cabin sits near 8000 ft and Δp is large, so the structure carries a heavy steady load. If the aircraft climbs higher, or the cabin cannot hold its pressure, the cabin altitude rises and the crew must act. Oxygen for that case is Chapter 2.8.

The outflow valve sets the pressure

The fuselage is a pressure vessel with doors and many seals. It is not airtight, and it should not be. Air enters from the packs at a controlled mass flow and leaves through one large opening near the rear, the outflow valve. That valve is a motor-driven gate or butterfly. Open it and cabin pressure falls. Close it and pressure rises. The supply only has to exceed the natural leak plus the outflow.

The cabin pressure controller reads cabin pressure from its own sensors, takes static pressure from the air data system, and drives the valve to follow a schedule. The crew selects a target cabin altitude and a rate of change, or lets the automatic schedule fly it.

  1. Ground: valve fully open, cabin at field elevation, no differential, so the door can be opened safely.
  2. Takeoff: the valve closes progressively after liftoff, so the cabin does not bump.
  3. Climb: cabin altitude climbs at a limited rate, a few hundred feet per minute, to keep ears comfortable, until 8000 ft.
  4. Cruise: cabin altitude held constant while the aircraft climbs or steps.
  5. Descent: cabin altitude comes down so that at touchdown it equals field elevation, and the valve opens as outside pressure rises.
  6. Manual: the crew drives the valve directly if the automatic channel fails.

Three protections sit outside the controller. The positive pressure relief valve opens outward if Δp passes the structural limit. The negative pressure relief valve opens inward if outside pressure is higher than inside, for example after a rapid descent or a landing with the cabin still pressurised. A dump switch opens the valve fully on the ground, used for smoke removal.

Air cycle cooling: expand the air and it cools

Bleed air leaves the engine at roughly 200 °C and 2–3 bar. It must reach the cabin at a comfortable temperature, so a pack cools it. The pack uses no refrigerant; it uses the air itself. Three effects share the work: heat transfer to ram air, compression, and expansion.

  1. Flow control valve: meters bleed air and holds the pack to its design mass flow.
  2. Primary heat exchanger: ram air from a scoop removes heat.
  3. Compressor: the air is squeezed again. It leaves hotter, but at higher pressure.
  4. Secondary heat exchanger: ram air removes that heat, so the air leaves cooler than it did after the primary.
  5. Turbine: the air expands through a wheel and does work on it. The work drives the compressor on the same shaft. Air that has given up energy leaves the turbine very cold, near 0 °C or below.
  6. Water separator: condensed water is spun or coalesced out, so no fog or ice reaches the cabin.
  7. Cabin.

Why does expansion cool? Cooling means energy leaves the air. In the turbine the air pushes the blades and hands over energy as shaft work, so its temperature drops steeply. Simply forcing the same air through a hole gives almost no cooling, because the energy stays in the gas. That is why a pack has a turbine and not a nozzle.

In the bootstrap cycle the turbine drives the compressor through a shaft, which is the arrangement above. In the simple cycle there is no compressor: heat exchanger, then turbine. It gives less cooling and is used where bleed pressure is low and demand is small.

The pack delivers air colder than needed. A temperature control valve lets hot bleed air bypass the pack, and zone trim valves mix it into each cabin zone. Ram air flow comes from a fan on the turbine shaft on the ground, or from a modulating flap in flight.

Worked example

Question. An aircraft cruises at 35 000 ft, where the ambient pressure is 23.8 kPa. The cabin is held at 8000 ft, where the pressure is 75.6 kPa. (a) Find the differential pressure in kPa and in psi. (b) Find the outward force on a plug-type door of area 1.2 m². Take 1 psi = 6.895 kPa.

  1. List the data: p_cabin = 75.6 kPa, p_ambient = 23.8 kPa, A = 1.2 m².
  2. Differential pressure: Δp = 75.6 − 23.8 = 51.8 kPa.
  3. In psi: 51.8 ÷ 6.895 = 7.5 psi.
  4. Convert to base units: 51.8 kPa = 51 800 Pa.
  5. Force: F = Δp × A = 51 800 × 1.2 = 62 160 N.
  6. In kilonewtons: 62 160 ÷ 1000 = 62.2 kN.
  7. Comment: the door is held by stops, and this steady load passes into the fuselage frames and skins.

Answer. Differential pressure 51.8 kPa (7.5 psi); outward force on the door 62.2 kN.

Exam traps

  • Saying the pack compressor cools the air. It heats it. The turbine cools it, because the air gives up shaft work there.
  • Saying the supply pressure sets the cabin pressure. The outflow valve sets it; the supply only has to cover the leak plus the outflow.
  • Mixing cabin altitude with aircraft altitude. The cabin is at 8000 ft while the aircraft is at 35 000 ft, and the difference drives the door load.
  • Unit slips in Δp × A: kPa must become Pa (×1000) before multiplying by area in m², or the force comes out 1000 times too small.
  • Reversing the relief valves. The positive relief valve opens outward on excess Δp; the negative relief valve opens inward when outside pressure wins.

2.7 Vapour cycle cooling and cabin heating

An air cycle machine cools bleed air by expanding it, so it needs pressurised bleed air and ram air to dump heat. Parked on a hot stand there is no ram air and the bleed is already hot, so many aircraft carry a vapour cycle machine instead. It cools by boiling a refrigerant in an evaporator and condensing it in a condenser, like a domestic refrigerator. This chapter also covers cabin heating: electric elements, hot bleed air through a mixing valve, or a shroud around the exhaust muffler.

You need the air cycle machine and the bleed air supply first: see Chapter 2.6 and Chapter 2.2.

Why a vapour cycle machine exists

An air cycle machine is an open system. It takes hot bleed air, cools it in a heat exchanger, then expands it through a turbine. Its cooling power depends on two things it cannot control: bleed air pressure and the temperature of the ram air available to the heat exchanger.

Parked on the ground on a 42 °C afternoon in Chennai, both are against you. There is no ram air flow and the bleed is hot, so an air cycle machine on ground cart duty gives poor cooling for a lot of bleed air.

A vapour cycle machine closes the loop. A fixed charge of refrigerant, R-134a for example, is sealed inside pipes and carried round and round; it is not thrown away after each pass. R-134a boils at about −26 °C at sea-level pressure, so it easily becomes colder than the cabin it cools. The machine needs only electrical or shaft power for its compressor, plus a small fan or ram air to cool the condenser.

You meet vapour cycle machines as ground cooling carts, in cargo and galley cooling on some transport aircraft, and in flight deck cooling on a few types. The same four components sit in your kitchen refrigerator and in a split air conditioner.

The four steps of the vapour compression cycle

Follow one kilogram of refrigerant round the loop. It changes from vapour to liquid and back, and that change is where the heat is carried.

  1. Compression. The compressor takes low-pressure, low-temperature vapour and squeezes it. Pressure and temperature rise above the outside air temperature, and shaft work is added.
  2. Condensation. The hot high-pressure vapour enters the condenser, a finned heat exchanger. Outside air (ram air in flight, a fan on the ground) takes heat out, and the vapour condenses into a high-pressure liquid.
  3. Expansion. The liquid passes through the expansion valve, a small orifice. Pressure drops sharply and temperature falls with it, giving a cold, low-pressure liquid-vapour mixture. No work is taken out; the process is a throttling.
  4. Evaporation. The cold mixture enters the evaporator in the cabin air duct. Cabin air blows over the fins and gives up heat; the refrigerant boils back to vapour and returns to the compressor.

COP = Qevap ÷ Wcomp

COP is the coefficient of performance, a pure number; Qevap is the heat absorbed from the cabin air in the evaporator, in kW; Wcomp is the shaft work put into the compressor, in kW.

Qcond = Qevap + Wcomp

Qcond is the heat rejected at the condenser, in kW. The condenser throws away both the cabin heat and the compressor work, so it is always larger than the evaporator.

A well-designed machine reaches a COP of 3 to 5. An air cycle machine at the same low altitude sits near 1 or below, because it compresses air that is then dumped overboard.

Advantages and disadvantages

  • Works on the ground: no bleed air and no ram air needed, just a small electric condenser fan. This is why ground carts use it.
  • Higher COP at low altitude: less shaft or electrical power per kW of cabin cooling than an air cycle machine.
  • The condenser needs good airflow. Dust, leaves and insects raise the condensing temperature and the COP falls fast.
  • Weight and complexity: a sealed compressor, receiver, pipework and refrigerant charge add mass.
  • Refrigerant handling: the charge must be recovered and recharged by trained staff; venting to atmosphere is illegal in most countries.
  • Leakage: joints and shaft seals leak slowly. A low charge shows as poor cooling, a cycling compressor and frost on the wrong pipe.

Cabin heating

A cabin at cruise is usually too cold, not too hot. The structure soaks to the outside air temperature, the outside air is below −40 °C at altitude, and the air cycle machine delivers cold air. Without heat the flight deck becomes uncomfortable and windscreens mist and ice over.

Bleed-air trim is the method on large aircraft. Hot bleed air passes through a trim air valve and mixes with the cold air leaving the air cycle machine. A zone controller moves the valve to hold the selected cabin temperature. See Chapter 2.2 for where that bleed comes from.

Electric heaters are resistive elements wound into the air duct, switched by a relay or solid-state controller. They suit small aircraft and helicopters with no bleed air, and also supply foot heat and windshield heat. Windshield heating is covered in Chapter 2.11.

Exhaust muff heat is used on small piston-engine aircraft. A shroud around the muffler collects hot air, and a valve lets the pilot send it to the cabin or dump it overboard. The danger is carbon monoxide: a cracked muffler pushes exhaust gas into the cabin. A CO detector is the standard guard, and the heater valve is normally closed during take-off and climb.

Worked example

Question. A vapour cycle cabin cooling pack absorbs 12 kW from the cabin air. The compressor shaft takes 3 kW. (a) Find the coefficient of performance. (b) Find the heat rejected at the condenser. (c) The same compressor is used in a pack that must reach COP = 3.5. What evaporator load can it now carry?

  1. Write the data: Qevap = 12 kW, Wcomp = 3 kW.
  2. Part (a): COP = Qevap ÷ Wcomp = 12 ÷ 3 = 4.0. The COP has no unit.
  3. Part (b): apply the first law to the whole loop. Qcond = Qevap + Wcomp = 12 + 3 = 15 kW.
  4. The condenser must dump 15 kW, which is why it needs a large face area and a strong fan or ram air flow.
  5. Part (c): rearrange the COP definition, Qevap = COP × Wcomp = 3.5 × 3 = 10.5 kW.
  6. The pack cools less because the same 3 kW of shaft work now moves only 10.5 kW instead of 12 kW out of the cabin.

Answer. (a) COP = 4.0; (b) condenser heat rejected = 15 kW; (c) evaporator load at COP 3.5 = 10.5 kW.

Exam traps

  • Swapping condenser and evaporator. The condenser is the hot one that rejects heat outside; the evaporator is the cold one inside the cabin duct.
  • Thinking COP above 1 breaks the first law. It does not: the extra heat at the condenser is the compressor work, and Qcond = Qevap + Wcomp always holds.
  • Using condenser heat in the COP. COP is Qevap ÷ Wcomp, never Qcond ÷ Wcomp.
  • Forgetting that the expansion valve does no work. It only drops pressure and temperature; the compressor is the only work input.
  • Treating the vapour cycle as an open system like the air cycle machine. It is closed: the same refrigerant charge circulates again and again.

2.8 Aircraft oxygen systems

Above a safe cabin altitude the air holds too little oxygen for the body. The oxygen system raises the partial pressure of oxygen at the lungs by supplying stored gas, liquid oxygen, a chemical generator or, in military aircraft, on-board generation. It protects the crew during decompression and gives passengers time to descend. This chapter covers the need, the supply types, the regulator and mask parts, and the safety rules that keep oxygen from turning a small fire into a large one.

You need: Chapter 2.6 for cabin altitude and Chapter 2.2 for bleed air.

Hypoxia and the need for oxygen

Hypoxia is a shortage of oxygen reaching the body tissues. It starts with headache and poor judgement, then leads to loss of consciousness. In an aircraft it is caused by low outside pressure: the fraction of oxygen in air stays near 0.21, but the total pressure falls, so the partial pressure of oxygen falls with it.

PO2 = FO2 × Pamb

PO2 is the partial pressure of oxygen in bar; FO2 is the oxygen fraction (0.21 in air); Pamb is ambient pressure in bar. At sea level Pamb ≈ 1.013 bar gives PO2 ≈ 0.213 bar. At 35,000 ft Pamb ≈ 0.24 bar gives PO2 ≈ 0.050 bar.

Cabin pressurisation keeps the cabin at a safe pressure altitude, often about 6,000 to 8,000 ft. If pressurisation fails, or if the cabin altitude is deliberately high, the crew needs supplemental oxygen. The time of useful consciousness (TUC) is the time from oxygen loss to inability to act. It falls from many minutes at 25,000 ft to about 30–60 s at 35,000 ft and 15–20 s at 40,000 ft. Oxygen systems must supply a mask within that time.

Stored or chemical oxygen raises FO2 in the mask to nearly 1.0. This raises PO2 at the lungs even when Pamb is low. The system therefore buys time to descend to a safe cabin altitude.

Supply types and components

Four supply types are common. High-pressure gaseous oxygen sits in cylinders at about 1,800 psi (124 bar). A filler valve fills the cylinder; a shut-off valve isolates it; a regulator reduces pressure; a mask delivers gas to the user. Liquid oxygen (LOX) stores more oxygen in less volume, but it must be kept cold and it boils off; it is used where weight and space matter. A chemical oxygen generator burns a sodium chlorate candle to release oxygen; it is simple and needs no pressure storage, but once started it cannot be stopped. Military aircraft often use an on-board oxygen generating system (OBOGS), which separates oxygen from engine bleed air with a molecular sieve.

Regulators come in three forms. A demand regulator opens only when the user inhales. A diluter-demand regulator mixes cabin air with oxygen at low altitude and goes to pure oxygen as cabin altitude rises. A pressure-demand regulator supplies oxygen above cabin pressure so the mask stays sealed and the lungs get oxygen even in a rapid decompression. Crew masks are usually pressure-demand; passenger masks are usually continuous-flow.

Other components are tubing, a pressure gauge, a mask and a relief valve. The gauge reads cylinder pressure, not the mass of gas left. To find gas quantity at constant temperature: V1 = Vw × Pc ÷ P1. Here Vw is cylinder water volume in L (the internal volume filled with water), Pc is cylinder pressure in bar, P1 is the pressure at which you want the volume (1 bar), and V1 is gas volume at 1 bar.

Operation, mask drop-out and safety

Before flight the crew checks cylinder pressure and the mask seal. In flight the shut-off valve is open and the regulator is set to normal. The crew dons masks when cabin altitude passes the crew limit, often about 10,000 ft, and uses pressure-demand for smoke or rapid decompression. Passenger masks are in overhead panels. If cabin altitude rises above about 14,000 ft, the panels open automatically and the masks drop. The passenger pulls the mask to start flow; this is automatic mask drop-out. Portable cylinders are carried for walk-around use.

  1. Check cylinder pressure and mask seal before flight.
  2. Open the shut-off valve slowly and set the regulator to normal.
  3. Don the mask when cabin altitude passes the crew limit, often about 10,000 ft.
  4. If cabin altitude passes about 14,000 ft, passenger masks drop automatically; the passenger pulls to start flow.

Oxygen is not fuel, but it makes fuel and oil burn fiercely. Fittings must be cleaned for oxygen service; no grease, oil or PTFE tape on threads. Open cylinder valves slowly to avoid a pressure surge and adiabatic heating. Keep cylinders away from heat and secure them so they do not become missiles. A chemical generator becomes hot; do not cover it. A LOX converter vents oxygen; keep it away from ignition sources. In a fire, shut off supply if possible and use the fire procedure in Chapter 2.9.

  2. Total flow: 12 passengers × 4 L/min = 48 L/min.
  3. Duration: 6200 L ÷ 48 L/min = 129.166... min.

Exam traps

  • Reading cylinder pressure as quantity: pressure must be multiplied by water volume, and pressure changes with temperature even when gas remains.
  • Using oil or grease on oxygen fittings: oxygen makes these burn fiercely, so oxygen service cleaning is mandatory.
  • Confusing diluter-demand with pressure-demand: a diluter-demand regulator mixes cabin air at low altitude; a pressure-demand regulator supplies oxygen above cabin pressure.
  • Assuming a chemical generator can be switched off: once the sodium chlorate candle starts, it supplies oxygen until it is finished.
  • Mixing up passenger and crew masks: passenger masks are usually continuous-flow and drop automatically; crew masks are usually pressure-demand.

2.9 Fire detection, extinguishing and smoke detection

A fire detection system watches the engine, APU, cargo holds and toilets for heat, flame or smoke. It raises a cockpit warning, and the crew then releases a stored extinguishing agent into the affected zone. It exists because a fire in a hidden compartment can grow faster than the crew can see it. Fast detection, low false alarms and one reliable discharge path are the whole design aim. Every transport aircraft carries such a system; the same logic protects the APU and the cargo hold.

You need the engine and APU zone layout from engine fuel and lubricating systems, and basic circuit ideas from Unit 3.

What the system must do

Fire protection has two separate jobs. Detection watches a zone and decides that a fire exists. Extinguishing carries agent to that zone. The two jobs meet at the control unit and the cockpit. A smoke detector in a cargo hold does not put out a fire; it only starts the warning chain.

  • Fast response: the detector must signal within seconds of a flame or a temperature rise, before the fire spreads.
  • Low false alarms: a warning that cries wolf makes the crew ignore a real fire. The control unit uses a short confirmation delay and compares more than one sensor where possible.
  • Full zone coverage: no shadow area. A continuous loop or several spot detectors are placed so that any fire in the zone heats at least one sensor.
  • Testable and resettable: a test switch simulates a detector. After the test the system must return to normal without replacing parts.
  • Fault indication: a broken wire or a failed detector must show as a fault, not as a healthy zone or as a fire.
  • Survive the environment: vibration, heat, fuel, oil and cleaning fluids.

Detectors and the control unit

The detector is chosen for the zone. An engine nacelle has airflow and hot metal; a cargo hold has boxes and possible smouldering. One detector type does not fit all.

  • Thermocouple: a junction of two metals produces a small voltage when heated. The control unit reads the millivolts. It responds to a fire's heat, not to smoke.
  • Bimetallic spot detector: two metals with different expansion bend a strip. At a set temperature the strip closes a contact. It protects one small point.
  • Continuous loop: a tube runs around the zone. In a Kidde loop, heat melts a eutectic salt and the resistance between the central wire and the tube falls. In a Fenwal loop, heat raises the gas pressure inside the tube and operates a switch. The loop covers the whole zone and has no shadow.
  • Ultraviolet and infrared flame detectors: a UV cell sees the ultraviolet light of a flame; an IR sensor sees its infrared radiation. They respond in under a second. They are paired so that sunlight or a hot brake does not cause a false alarm.
  • Smoke detectors: an ionisation detector uses a radioactive source to ionise air; smoke reduces the tiny current. An optical detector shines light into a chamber; smoke scatters the light into a photocell. Both suit cargo holds and toilets, where smoke arrives before flame.
  • Carbon monoxide detector: an electrochemical cell gives a current proportional to CO. It catches a slow, smouldering fire that makes little visible smoke.

The control unit supplies power, watches the loops and decides. It applies a short delay to reject a transient, drives the warning light and bell, and stores a fault code. The test switch injects a simulated detector signal. Crews test the loops before flight; the system then resets.

Engine and APU zones usually have two identical loops, an A loop and a B loop. A fire sensed on either loop gives a warning. A break in one loop gives a fault, while the other loop still protects the zone. That is why the control unit can show FIRE, FAULT or normal, and why a single broken wire does not lose protection.

From warning to discharge

When the control unit confirms a fire, the cockpit gets a red fire light and a bell. The crew identifies the zone. For an engine or APU fire, the pilot pulls the fire handle. Pulling closes the fuel shut-off valve, the hydraulic shut-off valve and the bleed valve, and arms the squib circuit. Rotating the handle then fires the squib. On the ground an APU fire normally trips an automatic APU shutdown and, on many types, an automatic bottle discharge; in flight the crew uses the APU fire handle.

A squib is a small explosive cartridge. It drives a cutter or opens the bottle valve. One squib gives one shot; after firing it is spent. Some installations carry two bottles, or a bottle with two squibs, so the crew can fire a second shot after the first has knocked the fire down.

Engine and APU bottles are fixed in the aircraft structure, not inside the fire zone. The APU bottle sits in the tail cone or aft equipment bay; the engine bottles sit in the fuselage or wing root close to the engine, and pipes carry the agent to nozzles inside the nacelle. Cargo compartments have their own bottles, often several, released by a cargo fire switch. A lavatory has a small bottle with a fusible link: heat melts the link and the agent discharges without a crew action, and a manual port is provided. Portable extinguishers in the cabin are for the crew's immediate use.

The agent volume follows Va = C × Vz ÷ (1 − C), where Va is the volume the released vapour must occupy in the zone in m³, C is the design concentration as a fraction, and Vz is the zone volume in m³. The division by (1 − C) allows for the air that the agent displaces. The simpler Va = C × Vz is often used instead; it ignores that displaced air, and at 6 % the two forms differ by about 6 %. Mass then uses the agent vapour density.

Halon 1301 is a clean agent: it leaves no residue and breaks the combustion chain. It is also an ozone-depleting substance, so newer designs use HFC-125, HFC-227ea or FK-5-1-12. Water mist cools and displaces oxygen; dry powder suits flammable liquid fires; carbon dioxide is not used in occupied spaces because it can suffocate.

Worked example

Question. A cargo compartment has a volume of 40 m³. The design agent concentration is 6 % by volume. The agent vapour density is 6 kg/m³. Calculate the agent volume and the agent mass needed.

  1. Convert the concentration to a fraction: C = 6 % = 0.06.
  2. Agent volume: Va = C × Vz = 0.06 × 40 m³ = 2.4 m³.
  3. Agent mass: m = Va × ρ = 2.4 m³ × 6 kg/m³ = 14.4 kg.

Answer. The released agent must occupy 2.4 m³ of the zone (as vapour at zone conditions), which needs 14.4 kg of agent stored in the bottle.

  • Smoke detection is not suppression. A cargo smoke warning starts the chain; the bottles do the extinguishing.
  • The fire handle has two actions. Pulling isolates and arms; rotating discharges. Writing only 'pull to discharge' loses marks.
  • A squib is one-shot. A test does not fire the squib; it only tests the circuit.
  • Halon 1301 does not work mainly by removing oxygen. It breaks the chemical chain, so a low concentration can stop a flame.
  • Do not forget the loop test before flight. An untested loop can hide a broken wire.

2.10 Landing gear and flap control, stall warning and protection

Landing gear and flaps move only when the aircraft is in the right state, and the stall system watches angle of attack to warn or act before lift is lost. Gear control selects up or down travel, locks the leg in place and shows the crew the result. Flap control moves trailing-edge Fowler flaps in steps and stops a split or skew. The stall computer compares angle of attack, flap position and air data with a limit, then shakes the stick as a warning and pushes it as protection. These functions are used on every take-off, approach and landing.

You need: Chapter 2.3 for gear structure and brakes, Chapter 2.1 for hydraulic actuation, Unit 1 for flight controls and Unit 3 for air data.

Gear selection, locking and warning

The gear selector is a lever or switch in the cockpit. It sends an electrical command to the gear control unit. The unit opens the hydraulic path to the actuators; the hydraulics are in Chapter 2.1. Up selection retracts the leg. An uplock is a hook that holds the leg in the bay after pressure is removed. Down selection extends the leg. A downlock is a spring-loaded hook that holds the leg straight for landing. Lock switches tell the control unit that the hook has engaged.

A squat switch on the shock strut closes under aircraft weight. On the ground it prevents retraction and arms the warning. In flight it reports the air state. Position switches drive green lamps for down and locked and red lamps for in transit or unlocked. A press-to-test button checks the lamps.

The gear warning sounds when the gear is not down and locked and engine power is low. Power comes from the throttle setting. A low-power approach with the gear up gives a horn or voice message. High power with the gear up may inhibit the warning, so the crew must still check gear state. The warning depends on both gear position and power.

If hydraulic pressure is lost, alternate extension releases the uplocks and lets the gear fall. Springs, compressed gas or a hand crank may be used. Gravity and air load complete the downlock.

Flap selection, asymmetry and load limiting

Trailing-edge Fowler flaps increase wing camber and wing area. The flap selector has detents such as UP, 1, 2, 3 and FULL. Each detent sets a target position. A flap control unit commands the drive motor. Limit switches at each target stop the drive when the flap reaches the selected position.

Both wings must move together. A skew sensor detects one flap surface moving out of step with the other flap surface on the same wing, for example after a drive failure. An asymmetry detector compares left and right flap angles. If the difference exceeds a small threshold, the control unit stops both drives. A split flap would roll the aircraft, so stopping is safer than completing the selection.

Load limiting protects the structure. At high airspeed the air load on the flaps is large. The control unit may limit flap travel or prevent the next step until airspeed falls. Limit switches also stop the drive at full up and full down.

Stall warning and protection

Angle of attack α is the angle between the wing chord line and the relative airflow. Lift rises with α until the wing stalls at the critical angle. A stall depends on α, not on speed alone. A light aircraft can stall at high speed in a steep turn.

The angle of attack vane is a small weathercock vane on the fuselage side. It turns with the local airflow, and a rotary position transducer sends α to the stall warning computer. The computer also receives flap position and calibrated airspeed from the air data computer. It compares α with a limit that changes with flap setting.

At a set margin above the stall angle, the computer drives the stick shaker. An electric motor turns an unbalanced weight on the control column, and the column vibrates. A warning horn or voice call may sound at the same time. The shaker is a warning: it tells the crew to lower the nose.

If the crew does not respond, the stick pusher moves the column forward. An actuator pushes the control column or linkage. The pusher is protection: it reduces α even if the crew delays. It must be inhibited near the ground so it cannot push during take-off or landing. The alpha floor function in the autothrottle commands maximum thrust when α reaches a high limit; it is protection by thrust, not by pitch.

Worked example

Question. An aircraft has a stall speed of 120 kt at mass 50 000 kg. For a fixed configuration, stall speed varies as √mass. Find the stall speed at 40 000 kg. Set the low-speed stall warning at 1.15 times the stall speed and find the warning speed.

  1. Mass ratio = 40 000 ÷ 50 000 = 0.8.
  2. Stall speed ratio = √0.8 = 0.894427.
  3. New stall speed = 120 × 0.894427 = 107.331 kt, so 107.3 kt.
  4. Warning speed = 107.331 × 1.15 = 123.431 kt, so 123.4 kt.

Answer. The stall speed at 40 000 kg is 107.3 kt, and the warning speed at 1.15 times stall is 123.4 kt.

Exam traps

  • Do not swap the stick shaker and stick pusher. The shaker warns; the pusher acts.
  • The gear warning needs low power as well as gear up. Gear up alone does not always sound it.
  • A squat switch is not a gear position lock switch. It senses weight on the strut.
  • Flap asymmetry stops the drive; it does not continue to the selected detent.
  • Stall depends on angle of attack, not only on airspeed; a high-speed steep turn can stall.

2.11 Ice and rain protection systems

Ice on an aircraft destroys lift, adds weight and blinds the sensors that tell the crew their speed and height. Ice protection keeps the critical surfaces clear: hot air or electric heaters stop ice forming; rubber boots and fluid break it off after it has formed. Rain protection keeps the windscreen clear so the runway stays visible.

You need: Chapter 2.2 for the bleed air that feeds thermal anti-icing.

Why ice is dangerous

Clouds between 0 °C and about −20 °C carry supercooled droplets: water still liquid below freezing. A droplet freezes the moment it touches a surface at or below 0 °C. Wing leading edges, spinners, windscreens and probes are exactly that in flight, so ice builds within minutes.

  • Extra weight: a 3 mm layer over 12 m² of wing is 32.4 kg on one side, before any lift is lost.
  • Loss of lift: the rough, lumpy leading edge breaks smooth airflow, so the maximum lift coefficient Cₗₘₐₓ falls and drag rises.
  • Earlier stall: lower Cₗₘₐₓ and higher weight both push the stall speed up.
  • Tailplane trouble: the horizontal tail has a thinner section, so a thin ice layer there can take away pitch control just when the crew flares.
  • Blind sensors: ice in a pitot head gives false airspeed; ice on a static port gives false altitude as well. Unit 3 covers these instruments.
  • Engine and propeller: shed ice damages compressor blades or stalls the engine; ice on a propeller causes vibration and loss of thrust.
Vstall = √(2W ÷ (ρ S CLmax))

Vstall is the stall speed in m/s; W is the aircraft weight in N, including the ice; ρ is air density in kg/m³; S is wing area in m²; CLmax is the maximum lift coefficient, which falls when the leading edge is rough.

Ice also costs time: after freezing rain the aircraft needs fluid sprayed by a de-icing rig before departure, and the crew checks the fluid's holdover time.

Anti-icing, de-icing and the methods

Two words carry most of the marks. Anti-icing prevents ice forming on a surface: the skin is held above 0 °C, or coated with fluid that stops the droplet freezing. De-icing lets ice form and then removes it. One aircraft often uses both: hot air on the wing leading edges and a boot on the tail, or boots on the wing and electric heat on the probes.

Bleed air is not free. It comes from the engine compressor, so thrust falls and fuel burn rises while anti-icing is on, and the ducting, valves and regulators add weight and maintenance. Electric mats need generator capacity instead, so they suit small areas and short duty cycles.

Pneumatic boots need care. Cycled while the ice is only a fraction of a millimetre thick, the boot cannot crack it cleanly; the thin film stays and later ice bridges across the inflated shape. Boots are cycled only after a few millimetres have built up, with a timer doing it while the aircraft is in icing cloud.

Protected areas, ice detection and rain removal

Protection is fitted where ice does the most harm, not over the whole airframe; weight and cost forbid that.

  • Wing leading edges from root to tip, and the leading edges of the horizontal and vertical tail.
  • Engine inlet lips, the spinner, and on some types the first compressor stage.
  • Propeller blades, heated by electric mats at the root.
  • Pitot heads, static ports and the air temperature probe, all electrically heated, because no flight is safe on false air data.
  • Windscreen, heated electrically or by hot air, for both ice and mist.
  • Drain masts and drain lines, so the drains do not block or tear off.

Detection can be visual from the cockpit, which is why the ice inspection light sits on the fuselage side. Automatic detectors use a probe in the airstream: a vibrating rod whose frequency changes as ice mass builds, or an optical or conductivity sensor. The signal lights an annunciator, tells the crew when to select anti-icing, and on some aircraft starts the boot cycle. Unit 4 shows how such a discrete signal reaches the display.

Rain removal works on the windscreen alone. Wipers use an electric motor and linkage, and air pressure holds the blade on the glass. Rain repellent is a hydrophobic coating applied on the ground, or a fluid sprayed on, that makes water bead and blow away. Hot air or electric heat raises the glass temperature so rain evaporates and mist clears. Some fast aircraft carry no wiper at all and rely on heat and repellent, because the arm is a hazard at high speed.

  • Forgetting that bleed-air anti-icing steals compressor air, so thrust falls and fuel burn rises while the system is on.

Takeaways

  1. A hydraulic system is the muscle of the aircraft; its controller decides which pump supplies which service and in which mode.
  2. The accumulator absorbs demand peaks and buys a few seconds of pressure after a pump or engine failure.
  3. One hot bleed-air source fans out into air conditioning, pressurisation, anti-icing and engine starting, so a bleed fault touches many systems.
  4. The oleo strut turns landing energy into heat in the oil and gives it back slowly, which is why it has both a static and a dynamic rating.
  5. Fuel is metered by the fuel control unit, while oil removes bearing heat and carries wear debris to the filter and chip detector.
  6. Starting ends at self-sustaining speed; ignition must be live before fuel is admitted, or the engine floods.
  7. Once the hull is sealed, the outflow valve is the only pressure regulator that matters.
  8. Air cycle machines use bleed air itself as the working fluid; vapour cycle machines use a separate refrigerant and are more efficient on the ground.
  9. Oxygen is carried because no practical pressurisation keeps the oxygen partial pressure in the lungs high enough at very high cabin altitude.
  10. Fire detection must be fast, cover the whole zone and almost never cry wolf, and the extinguisher bottle gives one discharge only.

Understand Draw a bootstrap air cycle cooling system and explain how it takes hot bleed air down to cold cabin supply air. 13 marks

Bleed air enters through a flow control valve and is cooled in a precooler; Primary heat exchanger rejects heat to ram air; Compressor raises pressure and temperature; Secondary heat exchanger rejects more heat; Turbine expands the air, doing work and dropping its temperature; Water separator removes condensed moisture before the cabin; Bootstrap: the turbine drives the compressor through a common shaft, so the air is cooled further than in a simple cycle; Advantages: colder outlet, better cooling performance per kg of bleed air.

Understand List the main components of an aircraft hydraulic system and state the function of each. 13 marks

Reservoir stores fluid and allows for expansion and thermal growth; Pump (engine-driven or electric) supplies pressure, typically 3000 psi in transport aircraft; Filters keep contamination below the specified class; Accumulator stores pressure and damps pulses; Pressure relief valve protects the system from overpressure; Selector valve directs flow to the chosen service; Servo valve meters flow in proportion to an electrical or mechanical command; Actuators convert fluid pressure into linear or rotary motion; Return line and heat exchanger close the loop; System controller selects pump, isolates faults and reports status.

Understand Explain the working principle of a typical engine bleed-air pneumatic system and name the services it supplies. 13 marks

Bleed taken from LP or HP compressor stage depending on engine speed; Check valve and pressure regulating valve hold manifold pressure; Precooler uses fan air to limit bleed temperature; Manifold distributes air; isolation valves separate left and right sides; Services: air conditioning and pressurisation, wing and engine anti-icing, engine starting, hydraulic reservoir pressurisation, water system pressurisation; Overpressure and overtemperature shut-off protect the ducting; Cross-bleed start allows one engine to start the other.

Understand Why does an aircraft need cabin pressurisation, and how is cabin altitude controlled in normal operation? 13 marks

Above about 10 000 ft the partial pressure of oxygen falls and hypoxia sets in; Pressurisation keeps cabin altitude typically at or below 8000 ft; Conditioned air is fed in continuously and the outflow valve controls how much escapes; Cabin pressure controller schedules cabin altitude against aircraft altitude and limits the differential pressure; Differential pressure limited (about 8 to 9 psi in many transports) to protect the hull; Relief valves: positive pressure relief and negative pressure relief; Landing schedule brings the cabin to ambient before touchdown.

Understand State the requirements of an aircraft fire detection system and describe two detector types used in engine and cargo zones. 13 marks

Requirements: survive the environment, fast response, full zone coverage, low false alarms, fault indication, testable and resettable; Bimetallic spot detector: two bonded metals bend on heating and close a contact; simple and cheap; limited to spot coverage; Continuous loop: a tube or wire around the zone changes resistance when heated anywhere along it, giving full coverage; Thermocouple detector responds to rate of temperature rise rather than absolute temperature; Carbon monoxide detector used in cargo or cabin air to catch smouldering fires; Warning must reach the crew in the cockpit with a clear zone indication.

Apply

Apply An aircraft climbs from sea level to 12 500 m. Explain what the cabin pressure controller and outflow valve do during the climb and what the two relief valves protect against. 13 marks

Cabin altitude follows a schedule, not the aircraft altitude; Outflow valve closes as the aircraft climbs so less air escapes; Controller modulates the valve to hold the selected cabin rate of climb; Differential pressure rises and is limited to the structural maximum; Positive pressure relief valve opens if differential exceeds the limit; Negative pressure relief valve admits air if cabin pressure falls below ambient; Failure case: valve stuck open gives rapid depressurisation, stuck closed gives overpressure.

Apply One of three hydraulic systems fails in flight. Describe how the system controller reconfigures and which services are affected, using landing gear and brakes as examples. 13 marks

Each system has its own pump, reservoir and services; controllers isolate the failed system; Check valves stop fluid flowing back into the failed system; Accumulator gives a short reserve for one or two brake applications; Gear may need alternate extension (free fall or accumulator); Brake and anti-skid may revert to a degraded mode or alternate source; Warning and status shown to crew so they know which services remain; Modes of operation: normal, alternate, emergency or manual reversion.

Apply Describe the landing gear retraction sequence and explain the job of the uplock, downlock, squat switch, skew sensor and asymmetry detector. 13 marks

Squat switch confirms weight off wheels before retraction is allowed; Selector sends signal; hydraulic or electric actuator unlocks the downlock; Uplock catches the leg at the end of retraction and holds it against gravity and airloads; Downlock holds the leg when extended; a downlock warning sounds if it is not locked; Asymmetry detector compares left and right positions and stops the sequence on mismatch; Skew sensor detects a wheel turned out of line before retraction; Sequence valves and proximity sensors give position feedback to the cockpit indicator.

Apply Describe the engine start sequence from starter engagement to self-sustaining speed and explain the part played by the ignition system. 13 marks

Bleed air or electric power spins the starter through the accessory gearbox; N2 rises; fuel is admitted only above a minimum speed; Ignition is switched on before fuel so the mixture lights at once; Light-up raises exhaust gas temperature; the crew watch for a hot start or hung start; At self-sustaining speed the starter cuts out and the engine accelerates to idle; Ignition may be switched off after idle is stable, or left on in rain and icing; Wet start, hot start and hung start are the three abort cases.

Apply Trace fuel from the tank to the combustor and oil from the bearing back to the tank, and explain how each system reports a fault to the cockpit. 13 marks

Fuel: tank, boost pump, low pressure filter, fuel control unit meters by throttle, engine speed and air data, then burner nozzles; Fuel also cools oil and drives fuel-oil heat exchanger and some actuators; Oil: pressure pump feeds bearings and gears, scavenge pumps return oil to the tank; Oil gives cooling and carries wear debris to the filter and chip detector; Sensors: fuel flow, fuel pressure, oil pressure and oil temperature; Faults shown as warning and status, with chip detection indicating bearing wear.

Analyze and evaluate

Evaluate Evaluate the choice between hot bleed-air anti-icing and electro-thermal anti-icing, and say which you would choose for a high-bypass turbofan nacelle. Justify your answer. 16 marks

Anti-icing prevents ice; de-icing removes ice after it forms; Bleed air: uses hot air from the manifold, simple, proven, but costs engine power and raises fuel burn; Electro-thermal: pads or mats heat the skin electrically, no bleed penalty, but needs large electrical power and careful power management; Pneumatic boots de-ice by inflating to break the ice, used on turboprops; Chemical fluid systems give limited-duration protection; Choice justified with penalty on bleed extraction, engine efficiency and electrical load; Critical areas: wing leading edge, engine inlet lip, probes, windshield.

Analyze Analyse how stall warning and stall protection work together, and explain why warning must act before protection. 13 marks

Angle of attack vane measures local AoA and sends it to the stall computer; Stick shaker gives tactile warning at a margin before the stall angle; Stick pusher moves the column forward past a further threshold to force the nose down; Warning first gives the crew a chance to recover without automation taking control; Flap position, landing gear and Mach number adjust the thresholds (g stall and high speed stall); Squat switch and weight-on-wheels logic inhibit the protection on the ground; Failure of one channel must be shown to the crew.

Evaluate Evaluate the design of fire protection for engine, APU, cargo and toilet zones: why zone coverage and fault indication matter, and why the extinguisher gives only one discharge. 16 marks

Detectors must cover the whole zone, because a fire in a corner must still be seen; Fast response limits damage; low false alarm rate stops unnecessary engine shutdowns; Fault indication in the detector loop itself must be shown or a failed loop looks healthy; Engine fire: two loops for redundancy, both must agree to warn; Extinguisher bottles are limited in number and weight, so discharge is one-shot; Cargo smoke: detectors plus suppression; toilet: smoke detector and automatic bottle; After discharge the crew must land quickly because no second bottle may be available.

Glossary

Air cycle machine
A compressor-turbine pair on one shaft that cools air by expanding it through the turbine.
Anti-skid system
A system that releases brake pressure when a wheel starts to skid, so the tyre keeps grip and does not burst.
Bleed manifold
The ducting that collects regulated bleed air from the engines and distributes it to the using systems.
Cabin rate
The rate of change of cabin pressure, normally limited to a few hundred feet per minute for passenger comfort.
Chip detector
A magnetic plug in the oil line that collects metal particles and warns of bearing or gear wear.
De-icing
Removing ice after it has formed, for example by inflating a pneumatic boot on the wing leading edge.
Differential pressure
The difference between cabin pressure and outside ambient pressure; the hull is sized for a fixed maximum.
Diluter-demand regulator
A mask regulator that supplies oxygen only on inhalation and mixes it with cabin air as needed.
Downlock
A mechanical lock that holds the landing gear extended and carries the load off the actuator.
Expansion valve
In a vapour cycle, a valve that drops the refrigerant pressure so it evaporates and absorbs heat.
Hydraulic fluid
A mineral or phosphate-ester based oil that carries pressure, lubricates moving parts and must not ignite easily.
Igniter plug
A plug that takes a high-energy pulse from the exciter and makes a spark in the combustor.
Oxygen partial pressure
The share of total gas pressure contributed by oxygen; the body needs a minimum value to stay conscious.
Packing
The seals in a hydraulic actuator that stop fluid leaking past the piston.
Precooler
A heat exchanger that cools hot engine bleed air with fan air before it enters the aircraft ducts.
Pressure relief valve
A valve that opens at a set pressure to return fluid to the reservoir and so protect the system from overpressure.
Scavenge pump
A pump that returns used oil from the bearings and gearbox back to the oil tank.
Self-sustaining speed
The engine speed above which combustion keeps the engine accelerating without help from the starter.
Servo valve
A valve that positions its output in proportion to a small electrical or mechanical command signal.
Shock strut
The gas and oil leg of a landing gear that carries static weight and absorbs landing and taxi loads.
Stick pusher
An actuator that moves the control column forward automatically to break a stall.
Uplock
A hook on the landing gear that holds the retracted leg in place.
Water separator
A unit that removes condensed water from cooled air before it enters the cabin.
