5.1 Geometric concepts of navigation

An aircraft rarely knows its position directly. It measures one number, such as the time a radio pulse takes to come back or the direction a signal arrives from, and that number puts it somewhere on a line on the map: a line of position. Two lines that cross give a fix. This chapter sets up the geometry every later aid uses: the Earth model, the words for direction, the shapes of the lines and how the crossing angle sets the error.

You need basic trigonometry (sine and cosine rules). Height above the ground comes from the air data instruments: see Air data instruments: airspeed indicator and altimeter.

The surface you draw on

Navigation starts with a model of the Earth. The simplest is a sphere. A meridian is a half great circle from pole to pole; the equator is the great circle midway between the poles. Latitude φ is the angle north or south of the equator, 0° to 90°. Longitude λ is the angle east or west of the Greenwich meridian, 0° to 180°.

The real Earth is flattened at the poles, so satellite navigation uses the WGS-84 datum: an Earth-centred coordinate system plus a reference ellipsoid (semi-major axis a = 6 378 137 m, flattening f = 1/298.257223563). Satellite positions are points in space in that frame; only ground positions (latitude, longitude, height) are measured on the ellipsoid.

A great circle is the shortest path on the surface between two points; its plane passes through the Earth's centre. A rhumb line cuts every meridian at the same angle, so it can be flown on one constant track. It is longer than the great circle except along a meridian or the equator.

The nautical mile (nm) is 1852 m, first defined as one minute of arc of latitude. One knot (kt) is one nautical mile per hour. Aviation measures distance in nautical miles and speed in knots.

cos Δσ = sin φ₁ sin φ₂ + cos φ₁ cos φ₂ cos Δλ,    d = R · Δσ

Δσ is the angle between the two points seen from the Earth's centre, in radians; φ₁ and φ₂ are the two latitudes; Δλ is the difference of longitude; R ≈ 6371 km is the mean Earth radius; d is the great-circle distance in the units of R.

Five words for direction

Heading is where the nose points, measured clockwise from north. Always say whether it is true or magnetic.

Track is the direction the aircraft actually moves over the ground. Wind makes track differ from heading. Drift is the angle from heading to track, counted positive to the right: track = heading + drift. True airspeed is measured along the heading; ground speed along the track.

Bearing is the direction of one point from another, so always name both points. Over short distances the bearing of the station from the aircraft and of the aircraft from the station differ by 180°; on long great-circle legs the convergence of the meridians adds to that.

A radial is a line leaving a station, named by its magnetic bearing from that station. An aircraft on the 030 radial is somewhere on the line that leaves the station towards 030°. Its heading can be anything.

One measurement, one line

A line of position (LOP) is the set of all points where one measurement would give the same reading. The true position lies on it. Three shapes matter in this unit.

  • Circle. A range from a known station fixes distance but not direction, so the LOP is a circle centred on the station. DME and radar give circles; satellite ranges give spheres.
  • Straight line. A bearing from a known station fixes direction but not distance, so the LOP is a line leaving the station. VOR, ADF and the ILS localizer give such lines.
  • Hyperbola. The difference of the ranges to two stations is constant along a hyperbola that has the two stations as its foci.

Fix methods are named after what they measure: rho-theta (a range ρ and a bearing θ from one station), rho-rho (two ranges) and theta-theta (two bearings). Three lines almost never meet at one point; they form a small triangle called the cocked hat, and the fix is taken inside it.

The cut angle θ is the angle at which two LOPs cross. Each line has an error σ measured across the line (along the line the measurement says nothing). When the lines cross at a shallow angle, a small sideways shift of either line slides the crossing point a long way.

fix error ≈ σ ÷ sin θ

σ is the 1-sigma error of each line of position, measured at right angles to the line; θ is the cut angle. At θ = 90° the fix error equals σ; at θ = 30° it doubles.

Reference frames, and accuracy against repeatability

ECEF (Earth-centred, Earth-fixed): origin at the Earth's centre, x-axis through the equator at Greenwich, z-axis through the North Pole. It turns with the Earth, so a ground point keeps fixed coordinates; satellite orbits are broadcast in it.

Local NED (north, east, down) has its origin at the aircraft; heading and track live here. The inertial frame does not turn with the Earth, which turns at 15.04° per hour relative to it; gyros and accelerometers sense motion relative to this frame.

Accuracy is how close a reported position is to the true one. Repeatability is how close repeated reports are to each other. A system can be repeatable and wrong: a fixed bias moves every fix by the same 200 m. Calibration removes bias and averaging removes noise; neither does the other's job.

Worked example

Question. Two DME stations A and B are 130 nm apart. The aircraft reads 50 nm to A and 120 nm to B. (a) Find the cut angle at which the two range circles cross. (b) Find the aircraft's distance from A measured along AB, and its distance from the line AB. (c) Judge the fix.

  1. The two range circles cross at the aircraft. The angle between two circles at a crossing equals the angle between their radii there, so the cut angle is the angle at the aircraft in triangle A–aircraft–B, whose sides are 50, 120 and 130 nm.
  2. Cosine rule at the aircraft: cos θ = (50² + 120² − 130²) ÷ (2 × 50 × 120) = (2500 + 14 400 − 16 900) ÷ 12 000 = 0, so θ = 90°.
  3. Check: 50² + 120² = 16 900 = 130², so the triangle is right-angled at the aircraft.
  4. Along AB from A: x = (50² − 120² + 130²) ÷ (2 × 130) = 5000 ÷ 260 = 19.23 nm.
  5. Distance from AB: h = √(50² − 19.23²) = √(2130.2) = 46.15 nm. (Check with B: (130 − 19.23)² + 46.15² = 14400 = 120².)
  6. Two circles cross twice, once on each side of AB; the route or a VOR radial says which crossing is the aircraft.
  7. sin 90° = 1, so the fix error equals the error of one line: the best a two-range fix can give.

Answer. θ = 90°; the aircraft is 19.23 nm from A along AB and 46.15 nm off the line, on the side the route puts it. The fix is as good as a two-range fix gets.

Worked example 2

Question. Find the great-circle distance from Chennai (13.0° N, 80.2° E) to Delhi (28.6° N, 77.1° E) in kilometres and nautical miles, and the time at a ground speed of 450 kt.

  1. Δλ = 80.2° − 77.1° = 3.1°.
  2. sin 13.0° = 0.2250, sin 28.6° = 0.4787, cos 13.0° = 0.9744, cos 28.6° = 0.8780, cos 3.1° = 0.9985.
  3. cos Δσ = 0.2250 × 0.4787 + 0.9744 × 0.8780 × 0.9985 = 0.1077 + 0.8542 = 0.9619.
  4. Δσ = arccos 0.9619 = 15.86° = 0.2769 rad.
  5. d = 6371 km × 0.2769 = 1764 km; in nautical miles 1764 × 1000 ÷ 1852 = 953 nm.
  6. Check: one degree of great circle is 60 nm, and 15.86 × 60 = 952 nm, the same answer.
  7. Time = 953 ÷ 450 = 2.117 h = 127 min.

Answer. About 1764 km or 953 nm; at 450 kt it takes about 127 minutes (2 h 7 min).

Exam traps

  • Heading, track and bearing are three different things. A radial of 030 says where the aircraft is relative to the station; the nose may point anywhere.
  • Outbound on the 030 radial you track 030°; inbound on the same radial you track 210°. Forgetting the 180° flip costs marks.
  • In σ ÷ sin θ, σ is the error across each line, not along it. Along the line the measurement gives no information at all.
  • A small cocked hat does not prove a good fix. If all three lines share one bias, such as a common timing error, they make a tiny triangle around a wrong point.

5.2 Radio navigation: point-source and hyperbolic systems

A radio aid is a ground transmitter at a surveyed point, and the receiver on the aircraft turns its signal into one line of position. A point-source aid measures range or bearing to one station: the line is then a circle or a straight line through that station. A hyperbolic aid measures the difference in distance to two stations: the line is a hyperbola. This chapter covers both families — NDB/ADF, VOR, DME, TACAN, LORAN-C and Decca — and how such lines become a fix.

You need: 5.1 for lines of position, cut angle and the nautical mile.

Point-source aids: bearing or range from one station

A point-source aid has exactly one transmitter, at a surveyed position. The receiver measures one quantity about the line joining aircraft to station. Measure a bearing and the line of position is a straight line through the station. Measure a range and it is a circle centred on the station. One measurement gives one line, so a point-source aid alone cannot fix you.

The automatic direction finder (ADF) works with a non-directional beacon (NDB), a simple transmitter in the low and medium frequency bands, a few hundred kilohertz, radiating equally in all directions. The ADF antenna senses the direction the wavefront arrives from and shows a relative bearing, measured clockwise from the aircraft nose. Adding the heading gives the magnetic bearing to the station — the line of position. Long wavelengths bring trouble: the ionosphere tilts the wavefront after dark (night effect), coastlines refract it, and thunderstorms jam it. NDB bearings are therefore coarse.

The very high frequency omnidirectional range (VOR) transmits at 108–117.95 MHz. The station radiates two 30 Hz signals: a reference whose phase is the same in every direction, and a variable signal whose phase lags the reference by exactly the azimuth. The receiver compares the phases and reads a radial — the magnetic bearing of the aircraft measured from the station. Radial 090 means you are east of the station; your track to the station is then 270. A conventional VOR (CVOR) makes the variable signal with a radiation pattern rotated 30 times a second, electrically in modern stations (the earliest ones turned the antenna with a motor). A Doppler VOR (DVOR) keeps its antennas fixed and switches them round a wide ring, producing the same rotation as a Doppler shift. The wide aperture averages out reflections from buildings and terrain, so DVOR has much smaller site error.

Distance measuring equipment (DME) is two-way ranging at 962–1213 MHz. The aircraft interrogator sends a pulse pair; the ground transponder waits a fixed, known delay of about 50 µs and replies on another frequency. The receiver subtracts the delay and halves the round-trip time to get the slant range r. The ground distance is smaller, because the aircraft sits at height h:

d = √(r² − h²)

d is the ground range in metres or nautical miles; r is the measured slant range; h is the aircraft height above the transponder, in the same units as d.

The gap matters close in. From 10 km up, an aircraft 100 km out on the ground reads 100.5 km, half a percent long; at 20 km ground distance it reads 22.4 km, twelve percent long. Tactical air navigation (TACAN) is the military version in the 960–1215 MHz band, and one TACAN station gives both bearing and range, so a single transmitter yields a complete fix. Many civil VORs share a site with a DME or TACAN, forming a VORTAC.

The rho-theta fix and the cut angle

Pair a VOR with a co-located DME and one station gives both bearing and range: a rho-theta fix, ρ for range and θ for bearing. The receiver places the aircraft at distance d along radial θ from the surveyed station position. Airliners do this inside the flight management system and draw the result on the navigation display.

Any two-line fix is only as good as its cut angle — the angle at which the lines cross. Lines at 90° fix best; as the cut angle closes, a small measurement error slides the crossing point a long way. Cross-track error from a bearing error Δθ is r × Δθ with Δθ in radians: one degree at 100 nm is about 1.7 nm. Dividing the measurement error by sin(cut angle) gives the position error, and the same rule holds for hyperbolic lines.

Hyperbolic systems: measuring a difference

A hyperbolic aid measures no distance at all. A master and a secondary station transmit on one precisely kept schedule: the secondary fires a fixed, published delay after the master. The receiver times the two arrivals, removes that known delay, and converts the remaining time difference Δt to a range difference:

Δr = c·Δt

Δr is the range difference in metres; c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s is the speed of light; Δt is the measured time difference in seconds.

The aircraft never learns either range, only their difference — and that is enough. The points whose distances to two fixed stations differ by a constant form a hyperbola with the stations at its foci. So one pair of stations gives one hyperbola, one line of position. Take f as the half baseline: a = Δr/2 is the semi-major axis and b = √(f² − a²) is the semi-minor axis. Δr = 0 gives a = 0, and the hyperbola collapses to the straight perpendicular bisector of the baseline. The branch tells the side: the station whose signal arrives first is the nearer one.

A fix needs two hyperbolas, so three stations — one master measured against two secondaries. LORAN-C did this at 100 kHz using ground waves that follow the earth's curve, and its chains covered coastlines and continents to a few hundred metres. Decca compared the phase of continuous low-frequency waves over the North Sea and gave repeated positions to ships and helicopters. Omega, at very low frequency near 10–14 kHz, covered the globe with eight stations and was switched off in the 1990s. Satellite navigation reuses this geometry — see 5.6 — with the receiver's own clock offset carried as one more unknown.

Worked example

Question. After removing the secondary's fixed emission delay, a LORAN-C receiver finds that the secondary's pulse arrives 100 µs after the master's. The master–secondary baseline is 600 km. (a) Find the range difference Δr. (b) Find the semi-major and semi-minor axes of the hyperbola. (c) Which branch is the aircraft on?

  1. Δr = c·Δt = (3 × 10⁸ m/s) × (100 × 10⁻⁶ s) = 3.0 × 10⁴ m = 30.0 km.
  2. a = Δr/2 = 30.0/2 = 15.0 km.
  3. Half baseline f = 600/2 = 300.0 km. Then b = √(f² − a²) = √(300.0² − 15.0²) = √(90000 − 225) = √89775 = 299.6 km.
  4. The secondary's pulse arrives later, so the aircraft is 30.0 km farther from the secondary than from the master. It lies on the branch nearer the master, 15.0 km from the centre of the baseline where the hyperbola crosses it.

Answer. Δr = 30.0 km, a = 15.0 km, b = 299.6 km, and the aircraft is on the branch closer to the master station.

Worked example 2

Question. An aircraft at 9 km altitude reads a DME slant range of 150 km. What is its ground distance from the transponder?

  1. d = √(r² − h²) = √(150² − 9²).
  2. 150² = 22500 and 9² = 81, so d = √22419.
  3. d = 149.7 km.

Answer. The ground distance is 149.7 km.

Exam traps

  • One hyperbola is one line of position, not a fix. Two hyperbolas, hence three stations, are needed; a two-station reading fixes nothing.
  • DME reads slant range. Directly overhead at 10 km altitude the DME shows 10 km, not zero, and the ground distance is always smaller than the reading.
  • A VOR radial runs from the station to the aircraft. Radial 090 puts you east of the station and your track to the station is 270; swap the two and the fix is 180° wrong.
  • A hyperbolic receiver's clock need not be synchronised with the stations; only the stations must agree with each other, because a common clock offset cancels in the difference. Satellite navigation instead solves that offset as an extra unknown.
  • Watch units in Δr = c·Δt: microseconds, not milliseconds. A 100 ms difference would give 30 000 km, larger than the earth.

5.3 Landing aids: ILS and MLS

An instrument landing system (ILS) lets an aircraft fly a straight, accurate descent to a runway that the crew cannot see. Two ground transmitters each define one angle: the localizer says whether the aircraft is left or right of the extended centreline, and the glide path (glideslope) says whether it is above or below the descent slope. The crew, or the autopilot, keeps two needles centred. ILS is the world's standard precision approach aid and the basis of automatic landing; the microwave landing system (MLS) was designed to remove its limits, and satellite augmentation now offers a third way.

You need the geometry of lines of position and point-source aids and DME ranging.

Why ILS is needed, and its parts

Visual approaches stop when cloud and fog hide the runway. A non-precision aid such as a VOR gives only a track, not a slope, so the crew must stay high until they see the ground. ILS gives both lateral and vertical guidance down to a decision height of 200 ft or less, and with the right aircraft and crew to touchdown. That is its need: safe landings in low visibility, at a steady, predictable path that air traffic control can sequence.

Modulation depth is the guidance

Both transmitters radiate a carrier amplitude-modulated by two tones, 90 Hz and 150 Hz, from two overlapping antenna patterns. The receiver filters out the two tones and compares their depths of modulation m90 and m150 (each a fraction from 0 to 1). On the course line the two depths are equal.

DDM = m150 − m90

DDM is the difference in depth of modulation; positive means the 150 Hz tone is stronger. DDM = 0 on the course line, and the needle deflection is proportional to DDM.

Localizer sense: seen from an approaching aircraft, 90 Hz predominates to the left of the centreline and 150 Hz to the right. Full-scale deflection is DDM 0.155, at the edge of a course sector about 2.5° each side; the sector is set so it is 210 m (700 ft) wide at the threshold, so a long runway gets a narrower angle.

Glide path sense: 90 Hz predominates above the path and 150 Hz below it. Full-scale deflection is DDM 0.175, at about 0.7° above or below a 3° path, so the sector is about 1.4° thick. A receiver well below the path sees strong 150 Hz and shows "fly up".

Because guidance comes from a difference of two depths on one carrier, it does not depend on how strong the received signal is. Approach categories set how low the crew may go before seeing the runway:

Advantages and limits of ILS, and MLS

Advantages of ILS: one standard worldwide, so every airliner can use every ILS; a cheap, simple airborne receiver; guidance that grows more precise as the aircraft nears the runway; integrity monitoring on the ground; and support for automatic landing in CAT III.

Limits: one straight path per runway end; only 40 channels; beams bent by reflections from buildings, terrain and taxiing aircraft, so critical areas must be kept clear in low visibility; and false glide paths at steeper angles above the real one, which is why the glide path is captured from below.

The microwave landing system (MLS) works at 5031–5091 MHz with 200 channels. A narrow beam sweeps "to" and "fro" across the approach sector, and the receiver measures the time between the two passes: the angle follows from that time. Azimuth coverage is about ±40° and elevation up to 15°, so curved and segmented approaches are possible; precision DME (DME/P) gives range. MLS saw little civil use because ILS was already fitted and satellite methods arrived. Today GBAS (a ground station correcting GPS at one airport) and SBAS (corrections over a whole region) offer precision approaches; see satellite navigation.

Worked example

Question. An aircraft on a 3° ILS glide path is 5.0 nm (along the ground) from the point where the glide path meets the runway. (a) What height should it be at? (b) Its glide path receiver measures DDM 0.05 with 150 Hz stronger. Full-scale DDM 0.175 corresponds to 0.7°. How far off the path is it, on which side, and by how many feet?

  1. Range in feet: 1 nm = 6076 ft, so 5.0 nm = 30381 ft.
  2. Height on the path = range × tan 3° = 30381 × 0.05241 = 1592 ft (the rule of thumb is about 318 ft per nm).
  3. Fraction of full scale = 0.05 ÷ 0.175 = 0.2857.
  4. Angular deviation = 0.2857 × 0.7° = 0.20°.
  5. 150 Hz stronger on the glide path means below the path, so the needle says "fly up".
  6. The aircraft is on a 2.8° line instead of 3°: height = 30381 × tan 2.8° = 1486 ft.
  7. Height error = 1592 − 1486 = 106 ft low.

Answer. It should be at about 1592 ft; it is 0.20° below the path, about 106 ft low (at 1486 ft), and must fly up.

Worked example 2

Question. A localizer antenna stands 3300 m from the threshold, and the course sector is set to 210 m wide at the threshold. (a) Find the half-angle of the sector. (b) The receiver reads DDM 0.08 with 90 Hz stronger; full scale is DDM 0.155. Find the angular deviation, the side, and the sideways offset when the aircraft is 10 km from the antenna.

  1. Half the sector width at the threshold is 105 m, so the half-angle = arctan(105 ÷ 3300) = 1.82°.
  2. Fraction of full scale = 0.08 ÷ 0.155 = 0.516.
  3. Angular deviation = 0.516 × 1.82° = 0.94°.
  4. 90 Hz stronger on the localizer means left of the centreline (seen from the aircraft), so the needle shows the centreline to the right: fly right.
  5. Sideways offset at 10 km = 10 000 × tan 0.94° = 164 m.

Answer. The half-angle is 1.82°; the aircraft is 0.94° left of the centreline, about 164 m at 10 km, and must fly right.

Exam traps

  • Localizer: 90 Hz left, 150 Hz right (seen from the approaching aircraft). Glide path: 90 Hz above, 150 Hz below. Mixing the two senses gives the wrong side.
  • Full scale is about ±2.5° on the localizer but only about ±0.7° on the glide path; the glide path sector is 1.4° thick in total.
  • DDM is a difference of two modulation depths, not a ratio of two signal strengths.
  • Marker beacons (75 MHz) give distance check points, not guidance; they never define the centreline or the slope.
  • CAT IIIA and IIIB may have no decision height; only CAT IIIC has no RVR limit either.

5.4 Gyroscopes: principle, precession and nutation

A gyroscope is a rotor spinning fast about its axis. Left alone, the spin axis keeps pointing the same way in space, however the aircraft turns around it: that is rigidity. Push on the axis and it does not move the way you pushed; it turns at right angles, and that motion is precession. Rigidity gives the attitude and heading indicators a fixed reference, and precession against a spring gives the turn indicator its rate. This chapter works out both from H = Iω and covers nutation, TDF and SDF gyros, drift and the cockpit gyro instruments.

You need torque, moment of inertia and angular velocity from basic mechanics, and the frames of geometric concepts of navigation. The autopilot that uses gyro signals is in Autopilot and automatic flight control.

Rigidity, gimbals and degrees of freedom

A rotor spinning about its axis carries angular momentum H, a vector along the spin axis. With no external torque, H stays fixed in inertial space. That is rigidity in space. More mass far from the axis, or a faster spin, raises H and so rigidity.

H = I ω

H is angular momentum in kg·m²/s; I is the rotor's moment of inertia about its spin axis in kg·m²; ω is the spin rate in rad/s (rpm × 2π ÷ 60).

The rotor sits in gimbals: rings pivoted on bearings so that the case can turn without twisting the rotor. A rotor in two gimbals is free about both axes at right angles to its spin: it is a free gyro, or two-degree-of-freedom (TDF) gyro. (Some books also count the spin and say "three planes of freedom"; it is the same gyro.) A rotor in one gimbal can turn about one axis only: a single-degree-of-freedom (SDF) gyro. TDF gyros measure angles (attitude, heading); SDF gyros, restrained by a spring or a damper, measure rates.

If two gimbal axes line up, one freedom is lost and the gyro cannot follow a turn about the missing axis: gimbal lock. Caging locks the gimbals (not the rotor, which keeps spinning) to hold the axis square to the case before start or in aerobatics. Erection brings a vertical gyro's axis back to the local vertical slowly, with pendulous air vanes or small torque motors.

Torque turns into precession

A torque changes angular momentum: T = dH/dt. The change dH points along the torque vector T, not along the push. When T is at right angles to H, the length of H does not change; only its direction does, and the tip of H moves towards T.

Ω = T ÷ H = T ÷ (I ω)    (T at right angles to H)

Ω is the steady precession rate in rad/s; T is the torque component at right angles to the spin axis in N·m; H = Iω. A torque along the spin axis only speeds the rotor up or slows it down.

A practical rule gives the direction: picture the force applied at the rim of the rotor, then carry that point 90° round in the direction of spin. The rotor moves as if pushed there. A big H makes Ω small, which is why instrument rotors spin at 20 000 rpm and more.

Nutation, drift and the rate gyro

Apply a torque suddenly and the axis overshoots, tracing small fast loops around the steady precession path: nutation. For a symmetric rotor the loops repeat at about ωn = H ÷ It, where It is the moment of inertia about a gimbal axis; a fast rotor nutates fast and in tiny circles. Friction and damping in the gimbal bearings remove it, leaving pure precession.

Drift is any unwanted wandering of the spin axis. Real drift comes from torques the gyro should not feel: bearing friction, imbalance, gimbal friction. Apparent drift is not a motion of the gyro at all; the Earth turns beneath an axis that stays fixed in space. For a directional gyro, the apparent drift about the local vertical is 15.04 × sin φ °/h at latitude φ: 15.04 °/h at a pole, zero at the equator. Flying east or west adds transport wander, because the local north turns as longitude changes.

A rate gyro is an SDF gyro whose gimbal is held by a spring of stiffness k. When the case turns at rate Ω about the input axis, the gyro needs a torque HΩ to turn with it; the gimbal tilts until the spring supplies that torque. So the steady deflection measures the rate. With a viscous damper in place of the spring the deflection measures the integral of rate, which is angle: a rate-integrating gyro.

θ = H Ω ÷ k

θ is the gimbal deflection in rad; H is the rotor's angular momentum; Ω is the turn rate about the input axis in rad/s; k is the spring stiffness in N·m/rad.

Exam traps

  • Precession is at right angles to the push: carry the push 90° round in the direction of spin. Drawing the axis moving along the push loses the marks.
  • Convert rpm to rad/s before Ω = T/(Iω). Using rpm directly makes Ω come out 9.55 times too small (the wrong answer is the right one × 2π/60).

  • Apparent drift is the Earth turning under a fixed axis; real drift is from friction and imbalance. Only real drift is cured by a better gyro.
  • A turn coordinator shows rate of turn, not bank angle; it is an SDF rate gyro, not a vertical gyro.

5.5 Inertial sensors and inertial navigation systems

An inertial navigation system carries its own sensors and asks nothing of the outside world. Three accelerometers measure the push needed to hold a proof mass still, three gyros measure how fast the aircraft turns, and a computer integrates these to give velocity and position. Nothing is transmitted, so nothing can be jammed. The catch is drift: small sensor biases integrate into position errors that grow with time. INS is usually blended with GPS (see Kalman filter and GPS-INS integration).

You need gyroscopes for gyro behaviour, geometric concepts of navigation for the frames, and the accelerometer in Aircraft transducers: solenoids, pressure, temperature, strain, rotary position and accelerometers.

Accelerometers measure specific force, gyros measure rate

An accelerometer holds a small proof mass. When the case accelerates the mass lags; a strain gauge, capacitor or piezoelectric crystal senses the displacement. A force-rebalance accelerometer pushes the mass back to null with a coil and outputs the coil current, keeping the sensor linear and stiff.

The output is specific force f, not acceleration in space. At rest the accelerometer reads 1 g upward: the ground pushes the mass up. In free fall it reads zero, though it accelerates at 9.81 m/s². To get inertial acceleration, add gravity back.

a = f + g

a is inertial acceleration (m/s²); f is measured specific force (m/s²); g is gravitational acceleration (m/s²), 9.81 m/s² downward. At rest f is 9.81 m/s² upward, so a = 0.

Gyros are rate sensors. A ring laser gyro sends two beams round a triangular cavity in opposite directions; rotation makes their path lengths differ, and the beat frequency is proportional to rate (the Sagnac effect). A fibre-optic gyro does the same in a coil of fibre with no moving parts. MEMS gyros shake a tiny mass and read the Coriolis force. Output is angular rate ω in °/s or °/h; one integration gives angle.

Bias is the output at zero input; scale factor error is the slope error, in ppm or %. Gyro bias is quoted in °/h and accelerometer bias in mg, where 1 mg = 0.00981 m/s² and 1 µg = 9.81 × 10⁻⁶ m/s² (one millionth of a g).

Frames, mechanizations and the inertial chain

Besides the inertial, ECEF and NED frames, the INS uses the body frame bolted to the airframe: x forward, y right, z down. Vectors move between frames by a direction cosine matrix (DCM, a 3×3 matrix of direction cosines), or by a quaternion — four numbers with no singularity at 90° pitch. Attitude is the DCM from body to NED.

Two mechanizations exist. In a gimballed system the sensors sit on a platform that torque motors hold level and pointing north while the aircraft turns around it, so the sensors see almost no rotation. In a strapdown system the sensors are bolted to the airframe and see the full rotation rate, so the computer carries attitude continuously. Strapdown won — smaller, cheaper, more reliable — at the cost of computing power and a wider sensor range.

  1. Gyros give body rates ω; integrate them with the DCM to update body-to-NED attitude.
  2. Accelerometers give f in body axes; rotate f into NED with that same DCM.
  3. Add gravity: a = f + g, where g points down (third component +g in NED).
  4. Integrate a once for velocity, then again for position.
  5. Feed position back to the attitude step so the local level follows the Earth's curvature.

Alignment, Schuler tuning and error growth

Before use the INS must be aligned: its starting attitude must be found. Levelling uses the accelerometers — any tilt puts a horizontal component of gravity into f, so the pitch and roll that null it are correct. Gyrocompassing uses the gyros: the Earth turns at 15.04 °/h and its horizontal component points north. The heading at which the east-pointing gyro senses no Earth rate is north, found with no magnetic reference. Alignment needs a few minutes and the aircraft still.

A feedback from position to attitude is tuned to the Schuler period. A level platform carried over a round Earth behaves like a pendulum of length equal to the Earth's radius. Tuned to that natural period, a horizontal acceleration produces no steady tilt: the platform swings instead of drifting off level as the aircraft crosses the curved Earth.

T = 2π√(R/g) = 2π√(6.371 × 10⁶ / 9.81) = 5064 s = 84.4 min

T is the Schuler period (s); R = 6.371 × 10⁶ m is the Earth's radius; g = 9.81 m/s².

Errors after alignment grow by integration. In the first few minutes (well under 84.4 min ÷ 2π ≈ 13 min) the Schuler loop has not yet acted, and the open-loop forms hold: an accelerometer bias b gives velocity error bt and position error ½bt²; a gyro bias ε tilts the platform by εt, giving a false horizontal acceleration gεt, velocity error ½gεt² and position error gεt³/6.

Δp = ½bt² and Δp = gεt³/6

Δp is position error (m); b is accelerometer bias (m/s²); ε is gyro bias (rad/s); t is time since alignment (s); g = 9.81 m/s².

Over longer times the Schuler loop bounds the tilt. An accelerometer bias then gives a position error that oscillates with the 84.4 min period instead of growing as ½bt². A gyro bias still gives a position error that grows, but as a ramp with an 84.4 min ripple, not as t³:

Δp = R ε (t − sin(ωst) ÷ ωs),   ωs = √(g ÷ R)

Δp is the position error with the Schuler loop closed (m); R = 6.371 × 10⁶ m; ε is the gyro bias (rad/s); ωs = 1.241 × 10⁻³ rad/s is the Schuler frequency. For small t this reduces to gεt³/6; for long t it grows as Rεt.

So a gyro bias of 0.01 °/h costs about Rε = 0.31 m/s, roughly 0.6 nm every hour: the "nautical mile per hour" class of a navigation-grade INS. Gyro quality sets the long-term accuracy. The cure is an outside fix blended in by a Kalman filter (see Kalman filter and GPS-INS integration).

5.6 Satellite navigation: GPS and NAVSTAR

A satellite navigation receiver times coded signals from satellites whose orbits it knows, turns each travel time into a range, and solves the ranges together for its position. The receiver's own clock is cheap, so its offset is solved as a fourth unknown. GPS, the American system whose programme name is NAVSTAR, guides airliners, ships and phones, and its timing keeps telecom networks and power grids in step.

You need: 5.1 for frames and position fixing, and 5.2 for time-based ranging from a point source.

The NAVSTAR constellation and its orbits

GPS is the United States satellite navigation system; its original programme name is NAVSTAR. The baseline design places 24 satellites in 6 orbital planes, each plane inclined at 55° to the equator, at an altitude of about 20,200 km. More satellites are flown than the baseline, so a receiver on the ground usually sees 6 to 12 at a time.

Each satellite moves on a Keplerian ellipse described by six elements: semi-major axis a, eccentricity e, inclination i, right ascension of the ascending node Ω, argument of perigee ω, and the satellite's place on the ellipse. The broadcast ephemeris sends that last one as the mean anomaly at a reference time, plus small correction terms; the receiver works out the true anomaly from it and computes where the satellite was at the instant it transmitted.

T = 2π √(a³ / μ)

T is the orbital period in seconds, a is the semi-major axis in metres and μ = GM = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s² is the Earth's gravitational parameter (the universal constant G times the Earth's mass). With a ≈ 26 560 km, T = 2π √((2.656 × 10⁷)³ ÷ 3.986 × 10¹⁴) ≈ 43 078 s ≈ 11 h 58 min.

That period is half a sidereal day, so after one sidereal day (the Earth turning once) the satellite has made two orbits and is over the same ground point: the ground track and the sky geometry above a site repeat daily, about four minutes earlier each solar day.

PRN codes and the navigation message

All satellites share the same carrier frequencies: L1 at 1575.42 MHz, L2 at 1227.60 MHz and L5 at 1176.45 MHz. They are told apart by code. Each satellite carries its own pseudo-random noise (PRN) sequence, and a receiver separates them by correlating with the right copy — code division multiple access, CDMA.

Two codes ride on L1. The coarse/acquisition (C/A) code runs at 1.023 Mchip/s with 1023 chips, so it repeats every 1 ms. The precision P code runs at 10.23 Mchip/s and repeats weekly; it is encrypted as Y for authorised users. One C/A chip lasts 977.5 ns, 293 m of range; the receiver resolves a small fraction of a chip at the correlation peak.

The navigation message is a 50 bit/s data stream. It carries the satellite's ephemeris, the almanac (coarse orbits of all satellites), clock corrections, ionospheric model coefficients and health flags. A full message takes 12.5 minutes, but one ephemeris set is enough for a first fix.

Pseudorange and the four-unknown fix

ρ = c (treceive − ttransmit)

ρ is the pseudorange in metres, c = 299 792 458 m/s, treceive is the arrival time read on the receiver clock and ttransmit the transmission time read on the satellite clock. Both clocks are imperfect, so ρ is not the true geometric range.

For a satellite at (xs, ys, zs) and a receiver at (x, y, z) the geometric range is r = √((x − xs)² + (y − ys)² + (z − zs)²). The unknowns are x, y, z and the receiver clock offset δt. Each satellite gives one equation, ρ = r + c δt plus small propagation terms.

  1. Track four or more satellites and decode each ephemeris.
  2. Compute every satellite position at its transmission time.
  3. Measure code phase and form each pseudorange.
  4. Start from an approximate position and clock offset.
  5. Linearise, solve for the corrections, iterate until they are small.
  6. Output x, y, z, δt and the dilution of precision.

The equations are nonlinear, so the receiver linearises about its guess with a geometry matrix H (one row per satellite: the unit line-of-sight vector and a 1 for the clock), solves Δx = (HᵀH)⁻¹ Hᵀ Δρ, and repeats. The same (HᵀH)⁻¹ gives the dilution of precision (DOP): bunched satellites give a large DOP, which stretches every range error into a bigger position error.

Segments, other constellations and augmentation

GPS has three segments. The space segment is the satellites. The control segment is a master control station with monitor stations and upload antennas; it tracks the satellites, computes orbit and clock corrections, and uploads them. It also maintains the orbits: the Sun and Moon, the Earth's bulge and sunlight pressure slowly move each satellite out of its slot, so small thruster burns (station-keeping) put it back, with the satellite flagged unhealthy while it manoeuvres. The user segment is every receiver, from a phone to an aircraft GNSS unit.

GLONASS, the Russian Global Navigation Satellite System, flies 24 satellites in 3 planes at about 19 100 km (period about 11 h 15 min), inclined at 64.8° to serve high latitudes; it uses frequency division (FDMA, a different L1 channel near 1602 MHz for each satellite). Galileo (European Union) and BeiDou (China) are also global systems. NavIC is India's regional system: geostationary plus inclined geosynchronous satellites covering India and the surrounding region on L5 and S-band.

Augmentation improves accuracy and integrity. Satellite-based augmentation (SBAS) — GAGAN in India, WAAS in the United States, EGNOS in Europe — broadcasts corrections and integrity flags from a geostationary satellite over a whole region. Ground-based augmentation (GBAS) does the same at one airport for precision approach. Aircraft-based augmentation (ABAS) is receiver monitoring such as RAIM, which checks the ranges against each other.

  4. (b) Range error Δρ = c Δt = 2.99792458 × 10⁸ × 3 × 10⁻⁶ = 899.4 m.
  5. A fast receiver clock stamps each arrival 3 µs too late, so every measured travel time looks 3 µs too long and every pseudorange is about 899 m too long: the same error on all of them, which the fourth unknown absorbs.
  6. (c) The unknowns are x, y, z and δt, that is four. Each satellite supplies one equation, so four satellites give four equations. Three would do only if the receiver clock were perfect.

Answer. The signal takes 0.0674 s (67.4 ms); the 3 µs clock error adds about 899.4 m (roughly 0.9 km) to every pseudorange; and four satellites are needed because the receiver clock offset is the fourth unknown.

Worked example 2

Question. A one-dimensional model of the clock problem. Two beacons A and B, synchronised to each other, stand 12.000 km apart on a straight road. 5.7 Satellite signal errors: clocks and atmospheric effects

A satellite range is a travel time times the speed of light, so every timing error is a range error: 1 ns is 30 cm. The errors come from the satellite clock and orbit, the ionosphere and troposphere, and reflections and noise at the receiver, and each has its own cure. Only a few universities teach this topic, so the chapter is short.

You need satellite navigation: GPS and NAVSTAR for pseudorange and the navigation message, and geometric concepts of navigation for the cut-angle idea behind DOP.

Clock characteristics and orbits

Each satellite carries atomic clocks (rubidium and caesium), stable to about one part in 10¹³ over a day. The control segment measures each clock and broadcasts a correction polynomial:

δts = af0 + af1(t − toc) + af2(t − toc)²

δts is the satellite clock offset in s; af0 is the offset (s), af1 the drift (s/s) and af2 the drift rate (s/s²) at the reference time toc.

Relativity also moves the clock: weaker gravity makes it run fast and orbital speed makes it run slow, a net gain of about 38 µs a day, or 11.4 km of range. So the clock is set slightly low before launch (10.22999999543 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz), and the receiver adds a small term for the orbit's eccentricity.

Orbit (ephemeris) error is different: it is not subtracted from the range. The receiver uses the broadcast orbit to compute where the satellite was; the 1 to 2 m by which that position is wrong appears in the range along the line of sight.

The ionosphere: dispersive

The ionosphere holds free electrons from about 60 km to 1000 km. Its strength is the total electron content (TEC), the electrons in a 1 m² column along the path (1 TECU = 10¹⁶ electrons/m²). The code is delayed by

dI = 40.3 × TEC ÷ f²

dI is the extra range in metres; TEC in electrons/m²; f is the carrier frequency in Hz. At 10 TECU on L1 this is 1.6 m; daytime values near solar maximum are many times larger.

Because dI depends on frequency, two frequencies remove it. The ionosphere-free combination ρIF = (f₁²ρ₁ − f₅²ρ₅) ÷ (f₁² − f₅²) cancels the first-order delay exactly. Single-frequency receivers use the broadcast Klobuchar model, which removes about half. Near the magnetic equator (southern India) and the poles, scintillation, rapid fading of the signal, can break lock.

The troposphere, local errors and the budget

The troposphere is the neutral air below the tropopause (about 9 km over the poles, 16 to 17 km over the tropics). Its delay is non-dispersive, the same on every frequency, so two frequencies cannot remove it. The zenith delay is about 2.5 m (about 2.3 m dry, the rest from water vapour). At elevation E the slant delay is about dT ≈ dz ÷ sin E; models (Saastamoinen, Hopfield) remove most of it.

Multipath (late reflected copies) adds about 1 to 5 m; receiver noise is smaller. The root-sum-square of all residual range errors is the user equivalent range error (UERE). Geometry multiplies it: horizontal error ≈ HDOP × UERE, vertical error ≈ VDOP × UERE.

Worked example

Question. On L1 (1575.42 MHz) the ionospheric delay is 12.0 m. (a) Predict the delay on L5 (1176.45 MHz). (b) The zenith tropospheric delay is 2.5 m: find the slant delay at 10° elevation. (c) The remaining UERE is 5.0 m and HDOP is 1.8: find the horizontal error.

  1. (a) d ∝ 1/f², so d5 = d1 × (f₁/f₅)² = 12.0 × (1575.42 ÷ 1176.45)² = 12.0 × 1.7933 = 21.52 m. L5 is the lower frequency, so its delay is larger.
  2. (b) dT = 2.5 ÷ sin 10° = 2.5 ÷ 0.1736 = 14.4 m. It is the same on L1 and L5, so dual frequency does nothing to it.
  3. (c) σh = HDOP × UERE = 1.8 × 5.0 = 9.0 m.

Answer. (a) 21.5 m on L5; the ionosphere-free combination of the two ranges cancels both. (b) 14.4 m, which needs a model. (c) 9.0 m horizontal (1-sigma).

Exam traps

  • The 1/f² law is for the ionosphere only; the troposphere is non-dispersive, so L1/L5 does not remove it.
  • A lower frequency has a larger ionospheric delay: L5 gets more than L1, not less.

5.8 Kalman filter and GPS-INS integration

An inertial navigation system is smooth and continuous but drifts without limit. A GPS fix is bounded but noisy, and it disappears when the sky is blocked. A Kalman filter blends the two: it carries a number for how much each source is trusted at that instant and weights the correction accordingly. Estimate and uncertainty are updated in a two-step loop many times a second. Some syllabi call the subject multisensor navigation.

You need inertial navigation for the INS and its drifting error, and satellite navigation and satellite signal errors for the GPS fix and its error budget.

A filter, not a fix

The filter carries a state vector x: the quantities it wants to know. In a typical aviation system these are position, velocity, attitude, and the biases of the accelerometers and gyros.

It also carries a covariance matrix P: the uncertainty in x. The diagonal entries are variances, in m², (m/s)² and rad². The off-diagonal entries say how two errors move together — a position error caused by a velocity error, for instance.

P is the filter's own statement of how wrong it might be now: small P means the estimate is trusted, large P means it is not, and every correction is sized by it.

  1. Predict: push x and P forward using inertial data only.
  2. Update: compare with a GPS measurement and correct x and P.
  3. Repeat, many times a second, for the whole flight.

Predict, then update

The predict step carries the state forward on inertial data alone. F is the state transition matrix — the mechanics that turn position, velocity and attitude at one instant into the next. The control term is the IMU's measured acceleration and rotation, resolved into the navigation frame.

x = F x + B u

x is the state vector (n × 1); F is the state transition matrix (n × n); B u is the inertial input driving the state forward.

P = F P Fᵀ + Q

P is the state covariance; F P Fᵀ carries the old uncertainty forward; Q is the process noise covariance, the uncertainty the model itself adds.

Q says how much the model is distrusted. A flawless INS with no vibration and no bias drift would have Q = 0. Real Q is set larger when vibration, temperature swings or uncorrected bias are expected. Large Q makes the filter nervous: P grows quickly, so the next GPS fix is believed more.

The update step uses the GPS measurement z. H maps the state into the measurement — for a position fix it simply picks the position part of x.

y = z − H x

y is the innovation, measurement minus prediction; z is the GPS fix; H x is the predicted measurement.

The innovation has a sign. If the GPS says the aircraft is 50 m ahead of the prediction, the correction goes forward, not backward.

K = P Hᵀ (H P Hᵀ + R)⁻¹

K is the Kalman gain; R is the measurement noise covariance taken from the GPS error budget; K is small when P is small.

x ← x + K y, P ← (I − K H) P

I is the identity matrix; the covariance shrinks by the factor (I − K H) at every fix.

In one dimension K = P ÷ (P + R), always between 0 and 1. K near 1 means trust the GPS; K near 0 means trust the INS. It is recomputed at every fix, never tuned once and left.

Loosely, tightly and ultra-tight coupling

Loosely coupled. The receiver solves its own position and velocity, and hands them to the filter as z. Simple, receiver-independent, and needs four or more satellites. Most civil installations work this way.

Tightly coupled. Raw pseudoranges enter as z, and H predicts the range to each satellite from the blended position. Two or three satellites plus a good INS still constrain the state, because the INS fills the missing geometry. This helps under jamming and in urban canyons.

Ultra-tight. INS velocity is fed into the receiver's tracking loops, narrowing its frequency search so weaker signals can be tracked. Used in military and research receivers.

Coasting. When GPS drops, only predict runs. P grows step by step and the solution stays smooth but drifts. After minutes P is huge, so the returning fix produces a jump; good software limits the correction or declares the filter failed.

Feedback. The update also estimates accelerometer and gyro biases. Those estimates are fed back to reset the sensors, so the raw inertial data stops drifting. This closes the loop and is what makes long flights possible.

Worked example

Question. A one-dimensional GPS-INS filter has just finished a predict step. The INS solution is 1000 m along track with variance P = 100 m². At the same instant a GPS fix gives 1050 m with variance R = 25 m². Find the Kalman gain, the blended position and the new position variance, and say which source the filter trusted more.

  1. Gain in one dimension: K = P ÷ (P + R) = 100 ÷ (100 + 25) = 0.8.
  2. Innovation: y = z − x = 1050 − 1000 = 50 m, so the GPS is ahead of the prediction.
  3. Correction: K y = 0.8 × 50 = 40 m, applied forward.
  4. Blended position: x ← 1000 + 40 = 1040 m.
  5. New variance: P ← (1 − K) P = 0.2 × 100 = 20 m². The standard deviation σ (the square root of the variance, the typical size of the error) is √20 ≈ 4.47 m, smaller than the INS's 10 m and the GPS's 5 m.
  6. Weights are 0.8 on GPS and 0.2 on INS, matching R = 25 m² being four times smaller than P = 100 m².

Answer. K = 0.8, blended position 1040 m, new variance 20 m² (σ ≈ 4.47 m), which is better than either source alone.

Worked example 2

Question. In the same filter the variance just after a GPS update is P = 20 m². GPS is then lost for 30 s, and each one-second predict step adds Q = 4 m². When GPS returns, the INS predicts 3200 m and the fix reads 3230 m with R = 25 m². Find P before the update, K, the corrected position and the new P. Compare K with the gain had there been no outage.

  1. Each predict step adds Q (here F = 1), so after 30 steps P = 20 + 30 × 4 = 140 m² (σ = 11.8 m).
  2. K = P ÷ (P + R) = 140 ÷ (140 + 25) = 0.8485.
  3. Innovation y = 3230 − 3200 = +30 m; correction K y = 0.8485 × 30 = 25.5 m.
  4. Corrected position = 3200 + 25.5 = 3225.5 m.
  5. New P = (1 − K) P = 0.1515 × 140 = 21.2 m² (σ = 4.61 m).
  6. Without the outage P would have been 20 m², giving K = 20 ÷ 45 = 0.444: the filter would have trusted its INS more.

Answer. P = 140 m², K = 0.848, position 3225.5 m, new P = 21.2 m². After the outage the gain rises from 0.444 to 0.848, so the returning fix pulls the estimate most of the way to it.

Exam traps

  • K is not a fixed percentage or a tuned constant. It is recomputed from P and R at every step; writing 'K = 0.5 always' is the classic mistake.
  • The innovation is measurement minus prediction, z − H x. Reversing it pushes the estimate away from the fix instead of towards it.
  • Q and R are swapped under exam pressure. Q is process noise (how much the INS model is distrusted); R is measurement noise (the GPS error budget).
  • P grows during predict and shrinks during update. Forgetting the growth makes the filter overconfident and it stops listening to GPS.

5.9 Radar principles, surveillance and ADS-B

Radar sends out a radio pulse and listens for the echo. The delay gives range; the antenna direction gives bearing. Ground radars use the echo itself, and also interrogate each aircraft's transponder, to build a traffic picture. ADS-B goes further: the aircraft broadcasts its own satellite position twice a second and anyone in range can receive it. A tracker fuses all these plots into one picture for the controller and the cockpit. The same principles run the weather radar, the radio altimeter and collision avoidance.

You need geometric concepts of navigation for range and bearing as lines of position. The displays that show the traffic picture are in Electronic displays: PFD, MFD, EADI and EHSI.

A pulse radar, block by block

Radar means radio detection and ranging. A transmitter makes a short burst of radio energy, an antenna radiates it, and the receiver waits for the echo. The delay gives range and the antenna angle gives bearing, a circle and a line of position, so one radar fixes an aircraft by itself.

R = c · t ÷ 2

R is slant range in metres, c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s is the speed of light, and t is the echo delay in seconds measured from the transmitted pulse. The 2 appears because the pulse travels out and back.

  1. The transmitter generates a pulse of width τ at peak power Pt.
  2. The duplexer connects the antenna to the transmitter, then switches it to the receiver so the strong pulse cannot burn the receiver out.
  3. The antenna — a rotating dish or a fixed phased array — radiates the pulse and later collects the echo.
  4. The receiver amplifies the weak echo, and a threshold decides whether it is a real target or noise.
  5. The timer reads t, the computer computes R, and the antenna angle gives the bearing in degrees.
  6. The display plots one blip at that range and bearing.

Echo power drops as the fourth power of range: double the distance and only one-sixteenth comes back. That is why radar powers are measured in kilowatts, and why detection range is the hardest number to improve.

The radar equation, resolution and ambiguity

Rmax = [ Pt G² λ² σ ÷ ((4π)³ Pmin) ]1/4

Rmax is the maximum detection range in metres; Pt is the transmitted peak power in watts; G is the antenna gain, a pure number; λ is the wavelength in metres; σ is the radar cross-section of the target in square metres, set by shape and material rather than physical size; Pmin is the weakest echo the receiver can detect, in watts.

Pulse width and pulse repetition frequency set two different limits, and they are the pair students mix up.

ΔR = c τ ÷ 2      and      Ru = c ÷ (2 × PRF)

ΔR is the range resolution in metres and τ the pulse width in seconds; Ru is the maximum unambiguous range in metres and PRF the pulse repetition frequency in hertz, that is, how many pulses leave the antenna each second.

A short pulse separates targets in range; a low PRF gives a long unambiguous range. You cannot have both cheaply, so air-surveillance radars switch between several PRFs and stitch the answers together.

fd = 2 vr ÷ λ

fd is the Doppler shift in hertz, vr is the radial (closing) component of target speed in m/s, and λ is the wavelength in metres. The shift is a rise in frequency when the target closes. An aircraft flying across the beam has vr near zero and vanishes into clutter.

Primary, secondary and ADS-B

Primary surveillance radar (PSR) works on the skin echo. Metal reflects the pulse, so every aircraft in the beam appears whether its transponder works or not. PSR gives range and azimuth and nothing else — no identity, no height.

Secondary surveillance radar (SSR) is a conversation. The ground station interrogates at 1030 MHz and the transponder replies at 1090 MHz.

  • Mode A: identity only — the four-digit octal squawk the pilot sets.
  • Mode C: pressure altitude in 100 ft steps, sent along with the identity.
  • Mode S: a unique 24-bit aircraft address, so one aircraft can be selected and data-linked.

ADS-B — automatic dependent surveillance, broadcast — removes the interrogation. About twice a second the aircraft sends extended squitters on 1090 MHz: 112-bit messages that between them carry its GNSS position and altitude, its velocity, and its identity. Automatic because nobody asks; dependent because the position comes from the aircraft's own navigation system (5.6); broadcast because everyone in range receives it. ADS-B Out transmits. ADS-B In receives traffic and ground-uplinked radar tracks (TIS-B); on the UAT data link it also receives weather (FIS-B).

TCAS/ACAS is a separate cooperative system riding on the same transponders. It interrogates nearby aircraft directly and needs no ground station. TCAS II shows its resolution advisory as a commanded climb or descent rate on the flight director and vertical-speed indicator. The crew must respond within a few seconds; the autopilot is normally disconnected, not driven by the RA (see Autopilot and automatic flight control).

Radars carried on the aircraft

The radio (radar) altimeter is a frequency-modulated continuous-wave (FMCW) radar in the 4.2–4.4 GHz band. It sweeps its frequency up and down in a triangle and mixes the echo with what it is transmitting now; the echo left earlier, at a different frequency, so the mixer gives a beat frequency proportional to height. It reads height above the ground below from 0 to about 2500 ft and feeds TAWS (terrain awareness) and autoland.

Doppler navigation radar sends three or four narrow beams down and forward, back and to the sides (a Janus arrangement); the Doppler shift of each, fd = 2V cos γ ÷ λ with γ the angle between beam and velocity, gives ground speed and drift with no ground station. Weather radar, usually X-band near 9.3 GHz, paints rainfall in colours (green, yellow, red, magenta for heavier rain) from echo strength, and finds turbulence and windshear from the spread of Doppler shifts.

Mapping and multimode radar, mostly military, switches one set between ground mapping (sharpened by synthetic aperture processing), terrain following and air-to-air search.

Worked example

Question. An airport surveillance radar transmits at 2.8 GHz with PRF = 1000 Hz and a pulse width of 1 µs. (a) Find the maximum unambiguous range. (b) Find the range resolution. (c) Find the Doppler shift of an aircraft closing at 300 kt.

  1. Ru = c ÷ (2 × PRF) = (3 × 10⁸) ÷ (2 × 1000) = 150 000 m = 150 km.
  2. ΔR = c τ ÷ 2 = (3 × 10⁸ × 1 × 10⁻⁶) ÷ 2 = 150 m.
  3. Speed: 300 kt × 0.5144 = 154.3 m/s. Wavelength λ = c ÷ f = (3 × 10⁸) ÷ (2.8 × 10⁹) = 0.1071 m.
  4. fd = 2 vr ÷ λ = (2 × 154.3) ÷ 0.1071 = 2881 Hz ≈ 2.88 kHz, a rise in frequency because the target closes.

Answer. Unambiguous range 150 km, range resolution 150 m, Doppler shift about 2.88 kHz upward.

Worked example 2

Question. A radio altimeter sweeps ΔF = 100 MHz in a triangle at fm = 100 Hz, so fb = 4 ΔF fm R ÷ c. (a) Find the beat frequency at 500 ft. (b) What height gives a 20 kHz beat?

  1. 500 ft × 0.3048 = 152.4 m.
  2. fb = 4 × 10⁸ × 100 × 152.4 ÷ (3 × 10⁸) = 20320 Hz ≈ 20.3 kHz.
  3. (b) R = fb c ÷ (4 ΔF fm) = 20 000 × 3 × 10⁸ ÷ (4 × 10⁸ × 100) = 150 m = 492 ft.
  4. The beat is proportional to height: 133 Hz per metre here, so a counter on the beat frequency reads height directly.

Exam traps

  • PRF sets the unambiguous range and pulse width sets the range resolution — never the other way round.
  • Both formulas carry a factor 2 for the round trip, c τ ÷ 2 and c ÷ (2 × PRF). Drop it and every answer halves or doubles.
  • ADS-B is a broadcast, not an interrogation; nothing is sent to the aircraft to prompt it. SSR is the system that asks.
  • SSR asks at 1030 MHz and hears the reply at 1090 MHz. Reversing the pair is the standard one-mark trap.
  • More power buys detection range (radar equation), not resolution. Resolution is bought with a shorter pulse.

5.10 Terrain awareness and ground proximity warning systems

A serviceable aircraft, a healthy crew, and still it flies into a hill. That is controlled flight into terrain (CFIT), long one of the largest causes of deaths in jet aviation. The cure is a warning early enough to climb away. Terrain awareness and ground proximity warning systems — GPWS, TAWS, EGPWS — compare the aircraft's path with the ground, first only the ground below, later a stored map of the terrain ahead, and call "pull up" early enough to climb away.

You need the radio altimeter from radar principles, the ILS glide path from landing aids, the GNSS position from satellite navigation, and the barometric altimeter from Air data instruments: airspeed indicator and altimeter.

Why the ground kills: height above ground

In CFIT an airworthy aircraft, under control, is flown into ground, water or an obstacle the crew did not see in time, usually at night or in cloud after a small altitude or track error.

Two heights exist in the cockpit. The barometric altimeter shows height above a pressure datum and cannot know that a hill stands in the way. The radio altimeter times an echo and reads the height above the surface directly below, over roughly 0 to 2500 ft. That reading is radio altitude, RA.

Every reactive warning below is built from RA plus sink rate, airspeed, gear and flap position, and — in one mode only — the ILS glideslope. RA looks straight down, so the warning is late, typically 10 to 30 s before impact, and blind to a ridge 5 km ahead.

The seven GPWS modes

Classic GPWS is a set of seven modes. Each mode is an envelope: a limit that changes with RA, not a fixed number. Early units compared the envelope with barometric sink rate or the rate of change of RA; digital units added airspeed, gear, flap and ILS deviation.

Mode 1 works on barometric sink rate, gated by RA: the allowed sink rate falls as RA falls. Crossing the outer line gives the soft "SINK RATE"; crossing the inner one gives "PULL UP". Mode 2 watches how fast RA is falling, with separate clean and flap-down envelopes because an aircraft on final closes with the ground by design. Mode 3 guards take-off and go-around: losing height before a safe RA is reached gives "DON'T SINK". Mode 4 calls "TOO LOW GEAR", "TOO LOW FLAPS" or "TOO LOW TERRAIN" when the aircraft is low and not in landing configuration.

Mode 5 is the only reactive mode that needs a ground aid: it is armed when an ILS is tuned and the aircraft sits more than about 1.3 dots below the beam at low RA. It says the aircraft is low on the approach, nothing about terrain. Mode 6 is a reminder; Mode 7 gives windshear warning from the airspeed and vertical-path signature.

TAWS: looking ahead with a database

A terrain awareness and warning system (TAWS), also sold as enhanced GPWS, keeps the reactive modes and adds forward-looking terrain avoidance (FLTA). It carries a terrain and obstacle database (the highest elevation in each grid cell, plus known towers), a position from GNSS or the flight management system, and a computer that projects the track, speed and vertical path ahead. It also gives a premature descent alert (too low for the distance from the runway) and paints the terrain on the navigation display. Class A TAWS, for larger airliners, has FLTA, the premature descent alert, the display and the full reactive modes; Class B, for smaller aircraft, has FLTA, the premature descent alert and fewer reactive modes.

Caution is amber, on the display and as a soft voice, about 60 s before predicted impact: "TERRAIN AHEAD". Warning is red with the hard command "TERRAIN, PULL UP" at about 30 s. Alert times vary with manufacturer, speed and phase of flight.

Inhibits exist because a warning that cries wolf gets switched off. Mode 4 changes its envelope by design once the aircraft is in landing configuration, and a TERR INHIBIT switch lets the crew quiet nuisance alerts on a steep approach into a terrain-ringed airfield, flying the approach on the chart alone.

  • Database currency: a new tower, cable or building is invisible until the database is updated, and many wires and unlit obstacles are never mapped.
  • Position error: a GNSS outage leaving inertial-only position, a wrong datum, or a bad flight-management position puts the predicted path in the wrong place.
  • It warns; it does not fly. The escape is a maximum-performance climb the pilot must make.

Worked example

Question. An aircraft is at 5000 ft above sea level, descending at 1500 ft/min at a ground speed of 240 kt over ground at 1000 ft. A ridge whose top is at 3000 ft begins 8 nm ahead. FLTA gives a caution 60 s and a warning 30 s before predicted impact. (a) Will the path meet the ridge, and when? (b) Where and at what altitude do the caution and the warning come? (c) At the warning, what climb rate clears the ridge top by 500 ft if the climb starts at once, and if it starts 5 s later?

  1. Time to reach the ridge: 8 nm ÷ 240 kt = 0.0333 h = 120 s.
  2. Altitude then: 5000 − 1500 × (120 ÷ 60) = 2000 ft, which is below the 3000 ft top: the path hits the ridge face at 120 s.
  3. Caution, 60 s before: at t = 60 s the aircraft is at 5000 − 1500 × 1.0 = 3500 ft and 4.0 nm from the ridge. "CAUTION TERRAIN" (on some units "TERRAIN AHEAD").
  4. Warning, 30 s before: at t = 90 s it is at 2750 ft and 2.0 nm out. "TERRAIN, PULL UP".
  5. The reactive modes stay quiet all this time: over the 1000 ft ground the radio altitude at the warning is 1750 ft, and 1500 ft/min there is an ordinary approach sink rate.
  6. Escape: to cross the top at 3500 ft the aircraft must gain 750 ft in the 30 s left: 750 ÷ 0.5 min = 1500 ft/min.
  7. With a 5 s delay only 25 s remain: 750 ÷ (25 ÷ 60) = 1800 ft/min.

Answer. Yes: the path meets the ridge face 120 s ahead, at 2000 ft. Caution at 3500 ft, 4.0 nm out; warning at 2750 ft, 2.0 nm out. Clearing the top by 500 ft needs 1500 ft/min at once, or 1800 ft/min after a 5 s delay: every second of hesitation costs climb.

Question. The altimeter sub-scale is set to 1018 hPa when the local QNH is 1013 hPa. Near the surface 1 hPa is about 27 ft. What does the altimeter show, and which warnings does the error affect?

  1. The setting is 5 hPa too high, so the altimeter over-reads by 5 × 27 = 135 ft: the aircraft is 135 ft lower than it shows ("from high to low, look out below").
  2. The radio altimeter measures the ground directly and is unaffected, so the RA-gated reactive modes are unaffected.
  3. A constant offset does not change a rate, so the Mode 1 sink-rate comparison is unaffected.
  4. Any check that compares barometric altitude with terrain elevation is 135 ft optimistic; that is one reason TAWS also uses GNSS (geometric) altitude.

Answer. The altimeter shows about 135 ft too high. Radio-altitude modes and sink-rate comparisons are unaffected; barometric terrain-clearance checks are 135 ft optimistic.

Exam traps

  • GPWS is reactive and looks down with the radio altimeter; TAWS look-ahead is predictive and needs a terrain database plus a position. Do not swap the two.
  • Mode 1 (sink rate) uses barometric descent rate gated by RA; Mode 5 (glideslope) needs a tuned ILS and is below-path deviation in dots. Not the same warning.
  • Quote radio altitude, not barometric altitude, as the gating quantity of Modes 1 to 5. A barometric height of 900 ft over high ground may be 300 ft above the hill.
  • FLTA is the forward-looking terrain avoidance function; the premature descent alert is a separate TAWS alert for being too low for the distance to the runway.

Remember What does CFIT stand for, and what makes it different from an aircraft failure? 2 marks

Remember Which sensor feeds the reactive GPWS modes, and what quantity does it measure? 2 marks

The radio (radar) altimeter. It measures height above the surface directly below the aircraft, from about 0 to 2500 ft, and that reading is the radio altitude RA.

Understand Why does Mode 2 have separate flap-up and flap-down envelopes? 4 marks

On final approach with flaps down the aircraft closes with the ground by design. One clean-configuration limit would be too sensitive on approach or too slack en route; two envelopes keep the clean limit tight and the landing limit loose.

Apply An aircraft flies level at 180 kt ground speed, 300 ft below the top of a ridge 2.5 nm ahead. How long until predicted impact, and which FLTA alert is showing? 3 marks

Time = 2.5 ÷ 180 h = 50 s. That is less than 60 s but more than 30 s, so the amber caution is on, and the red warning comes 20 s later.

Analyze An aircraft flies towards a newly built radio mast that is not in its terrain database. Why does neither the look-ahead nor the reactive GPWS give a timely warning? 4 marks

Look-ahead compares the predicted path with stored terrain and obstacles; an unmapped mast has no entry, so the path looks clear. The reactive modes see only the surface directly below; a thin mast is a poor reflector and gives no clearance or sink-rate exceedance. Unmapped obstacles are the blind spot of both, which is why database updates and obstacle charts matter.

Analyze Why do crews inhibit some terrain alerts on steep approaches into airfields ringed by high ground? 4 marks

On a steep, curved approach the projected path meets terrain that the aircraft will in fact clear, so alerts fire again and again and would be ignored. Inhibiting keeps trust in the system; the cost is losing those alerts for that approach, so the crew flies the published procedure exactly.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Helicopter TAWS uses a shorter look-ahead because a helicopter can stop rather than climb. Synthetic vision draws the same terrain database on the flight display. Search for helicopter TAWS and synthetic vision.

Takeaways

  1. Every navigation system turns a measurement into a line of position; two lines of position make a fix, and a third checks it.
  2. A radio aid is either point source (range or bearing to one station) or hyperbolic (difference of ranges to two stations).
  3. ILS splits the job in two: the localizer owns left and right, the glideslope owns up and down, and the pilot flies the needles.
  4. A spinning mass holds its axis in inertial space, and a torque turns that axis at 90° to the torque at the rate Ω = T ÷ H: that is precession, the basis of every gyro instrument.
  5. An INS integrates its own accelerations, so it is smooth and self-contained; with Schuler tuning its errors swing with an 84.4 min period, but a gyro bias still makes the position error ramp up, about 0.6 nm an hour for 0.01 °/h.
  6. GPS measures travel time, not angle; four satellites are needed because the receiver clock offset is a fourth unknown.
  7. GPS error comes from four families — clocks and orbits, ionosphere, troposphere, local effects — and each family has its own cure.
  8. A Kalman filter weights the drifting INS and the noisy GPS by their covariances, giving a solution better than either alone.
  9. Radar gives range from echo travel time and bearing from antenna pointing; ADS-B shares position by broadcast instead of by echo.
  10. TAWS joins a reactive radio-altimeter layer with a look-ahead terrain-database layer to warn the crew early enough to climb.

Understand Distinguish point-source from hyperbolic radio navigation. Name one operational system of each and state exactly what the airborne receiver measures. 10 marks

Points to cover. Point source: one station, receiver measures range (DME) or bearing (VOR, ADF); Hyperbolic: two stations, receiver measures time difference which equals a constant difference of distances; Constant difference of distances is a hyperbola with the stations as foci; Three or more stations give a family of hyperbolas and hence a fix; Examples: DME and VOR as point source, Loran-C or Decca as hyperbolic; Rho-theta (VOR/DME) mixes one range and one bearing from the same station.

Model answer.

  1. Point-source aids use one ground station per line of position. The airborne set measures something about that station alone: a bearing to or from it, or a range from it. The LOP is a straight line or a circle centred on the station.

A time difference Δt is a range difference Δr = c Δt, and the points with a constant difference of distance to two fixed points form a hyperbola with the stations as foci.

Understand Explain the principle of a gyroscope and distinguish precession from nutation, stating where each matters in an aircraft instrument. 10 marks

Points to cover. A spinning mass with angular momentum along its spin axis; axis stays fixed in inertial space; Two degrees of freedom make a free (TDF) gyro for angles; a spring-restrained single-degree gyro measures rate, a damped one integrates rate; Precession: an applied torque turns the spin axis at 90° to the torque, at Ω = T ÷ H with H = Iω; Nutation: transient wobble of the axis after a sudden torque, damped out by the gimbal and the spin; Use in attitude indicator, heading indicator, turn coordinator and in inertial sensors; Gimbal lock and drift limits are practical consequences.

Model answer.

  1. Principle. A rotor spinning about its axis carries angular momentum H = Iω along the spin axis. Without torque H cannot change, so the axis keeps its direction in inertial space: rigidity. A torque T changes H at the rate T = dH/dt, and that change is at right angles to H.
  2. For a directional gyro rotor with I = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ kg·m² at 24 000 rpm:
    ω = 24 000 × 2π ÷ 60 = 2513.27 rad/s
H = Iω = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ × 2513.27 = 0.3016 kg·m²/s
  3. Precession. A steady torque at right angles to the spin axis does not tilt the axis the way the torque pushes. The axis turns about the third axis, at 90° to both the spin and the torque, at the steady rate Ω = T ÷ H. The direction is found by carrying the torque round 90° in the direction of spin. A large H makes Ω small, which is why instrument rotors spin at 20 000 rpm and more.
  4. Bearing friction of 5 × 10⁻⁶ N·m on that rotor:
    Ω = T ÷ H = 5 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 0.3016 = 1.658 × 10⁻⁵ rad/s
= 3.42 °/h of real drift
  5. Nutation. When a torque is applied suddenly, the axis does not settle at once into steady precession. It overshoots and traces small, fast loops around the precession path. For a symmetric rotor the loops repeat at about ωn = H ÷ It, where It is the moment of inertia about a gimbal axis. Gimbal friction and damping remove it, leaving pure precession.
  6. Taking It = 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ kg·m² for the same rotor (about half of I, as for a thin disc):
    ωn = 0.3016 ÷ (6.0 × 10⁻⁵) = 5027 rad/s, about 800 Hz
  7. Precession against nutation.
    PrecessionNutation
    CauseA steady torque at 90° to the spin axisA sudden torque or a shock
    MotionSteady turn of the axis at Ω = T ÷ HFast, small circles about the mean path at about H ÷ It
    LastsAs long as the torqueA short time, damped out
    Where it mattersUsed: rate gyro, erection, slaving; unwanted: real drift of the heading indicatorSettling of gyros after a jolt; a rate-gyro damper and stiff gimbals keep it small
  8. Use in instruments. The attitude indicator uses a two-degree-of-freedom vertical gyro, held to the vertical by an erection system that is itself a controlled precession. The heading indicator uses a directional gyro with a horizontal axis; its real drift (here 3.42 °/h) and the apparent drift from the Earth's rotation (15.04 × sin φ °/h, 3.38 °/h at 13° N) mean it is reset to the compass about every 15 minutes. The turn coordinator uses a spring-restrained single-degree rate gyro: the case turns at Ω, the gyro needs a torque HΩ to follow, and the spring deflects by θ = HΩ ÷ k.
  9. Practical limits: gimbal lock when two gimbal axes line up, caging to hold the gimbals square before start or in aerobatics, and real drift from friction and imbalance, which a large H keeps small.
  10. Check: in 15 minutes the heading indicator at 13° N drifts (3.42 + 3.38) × 0.25 = 1.7°, which matches the reset interval; and Ω × H = 1.658 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.3016 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ N·m, the torque we started with.

Analyze Classify the error sources that limit GPS accuracy and match each one with the correction technique used in practice. 12 marks

Points to cover. Satellite clock and orbit (ephemeris) errors, including the selective availability of the past; corrected by broadcast parameters and differential corrections; Ionosphere: dispersive, corrected by dual frequency, ionospheric models or SBAS; Troposphere: non-dispersive, corrected by a model using surface pressure, temperature and humidity; Receiver and antenna: multipath, noise, antenna phase centre; corrected by antenna siting, choke-ring antennas and filtering; Dilution of precision amplifies all of the above by poor satellite geometry; RAIM and augmentation systems check integrity, not just accuracy.

Model answer.

  1. GPS position error comes from errors in each pseudorange, multiplied by the geometry of the satellites. The range errors fall into four families: satellite clock and orbit, ionosphere, troposphere, and local effects at the receiver. Each family has a different physical cause, so each has a different cure.
  2. Clocks and orbits. The satellite clock and the broadcast orbit are never perfect; the residuals after the broadcast corrections are about a metre each. Until May 2000 selective availability added deliberate clock dithering of tens of metres; it is now off. Differential GPS and SBAS remove these errors because they are the same for every nearby user.
  3. Ionosphere. The delay is 40.3 × TEC ÷ f², so it depends on frequency: the ionosphere is dispersive. That is the key to its cure. A dual-frequency receiver measures the same range on L1 and L5 and solves for the delay; a single-frequency receiver uses the broadcast model, which removes only about half. SBAS (GAGAN, WAAS, EGNOS) broadcast a grid of measured delays. Example: 12.0 m on L1 becomes 12.0 × (1575.42 ÷ 1176.45)² = 21.5 m on L5, and the difference between them reveals both.
  4. Troposphere. The neutral air delays all frequencies equally (non-dispersive), so dual frequency does nothing. About 2.5 m at the zenith, it grows as 1 ÷ sin(elevation): 2.5 ÷ sin 10° = 14.4 m at 10°. It is corrected with a model using surface pressure, temperature and humidity, and by an elevation mask.
  5. Local effects. Multipath (reflections from the airframe or nearby buildings), receiver noise and antenna phase-centre variation are unique to each receiver, so differential corrections cannot remove them. Cures are good antenna siting, choke-ring antennas, narrow correlators and carrier smoothing.

Against a gently rising slope Mode 2 sees the closure and warns, typically 10 to 30 s before impact. The aircraft can be on a normal descent, in landing configuration, and still be flying at a mountain.

    caution, 60 s before impact: t = 60 s, 3500 ft, 4.0 nm from the ridge, "CAUTION TERRAIN"

  8. Limits of the look-ahead. The database must be current (a new tower or cable is invisible until added), position error or a wrong altimeter setting shifts the prediction, and steep approaches into terrain-ringed airfields can give nuisance alerts, which crews may inhibit only by procedure. The reactive modes stay as an independent backup that needs no database.
  9. Judgement. The reactive modes answer "is the ground below too close or coming up too fast?"; FLTA answers "will my path meet terrain ahead?". Only the second question protects against a steep face, and it gives a minute of warning instead of seconds, which is why the reactive modes had to be supplemented, not replaced.
  10. Check: at the warning the aircraft has 30 s and needs 750 ft, 750 ÷ 0.5 min = 1500 ft/min, exactly its present descent rate reversed; every second of delay raises the needed climb rate.
Evaluate Compare a VOR/DME area-navigation fix with an ILS approach, in terms of the lines of position used and the accuracy the operation demands. 12 marks

Points to cover. VOR/DME gives rho-theta: one bearing line and one range circle from the same station; DME alone gives a circle; a second DME gives a circle whose crossing is a fix, with poor geometry near the baseline; ILS gives two planes of constant angle, not range, so accuracy is angular and tightens as the aircraft nears the runway; En-route accuracy is of the order of a nautical mile; ILS has to keep the aircraft within metres of the centreline at the threshold; Both depend on correct receiver tuning, identification and monitoring; Why angular guidance is the right choice for the final approach.

Model answer.

  1. Both give a position from radio lines of position, but they are built for different jobs: VOR/DME area navigation keeps an aircraft near a route of its own choosing en route, while ILS keeps it on one fixed path down to a runway.
  2. VOR/DME lines of position. A VOR gives a bearing line (a radial) from the station; a DME gives a range circle. From one co-located VOR/DME the two cross at 90°, the rho-theta fix. An RNAV computer converts this to position and flies to waypoints that need not lie on any radial. A DME/DME fix uses two range circles from different stations, with a good cut only when the stations are well apart in bearing; near the baseline joining them the cut angle closes and the fix degrades.
  3. VOR/DME accuracy is angular for the bearing. A bearing error spreads with distance from the station:
    cross-track error = distance × tan(bearing error)
50 nm, 1°: 50 × tan 1° = 0.87 nm (1616 m)
50 nm, 2°: 50 × tan 2° = 1.75 nm
DME along-track error: about 0.2 nm, nearly constant with range
  4. So VOR/DME accuracy is of the order of a nautical mile at typical en-route distances. That is enough for en-route RNAV, whose specifications ask the aircraft to stay within 5 nm (RNAV 5) or 1 nm (RNAV 1) of the route 95% of the time.
  5. ILS lines of position. ILS measures two angles from the runway, not ranges: the localizer gives a vertical plane through the centreline and the glide path an inclined plane at 3°. Their intersection is the approach line. Range comes only from markers or DME, and it matters little, because the guidance is a line to follow, not a point to find.
  6. ILS accuracy tightens as the runway nears, because a fixed angle covers fewer metres closer in. With a localizer 3300 m from the threshold (half-angle 1.82°) and a 3° glide path:
    WhereLocalizer full scale (each side)Glide path full scale (above)
    10 km from the localizer318 mn/a
    5 nm from where the path meets the runwayn/a372 ft
    1 nm from where the path meets the runwayn/a74 ft
    Threshold105 m (210 m sector)path crosses at about 52 ft
  7. The final approach needs this: the aircraft must arrive within a few metres of the centreline and a few feet of the right height at the decision height, and an autoland must touch down on the centreline. A VOR bearing error that grows with distance from a station cannot give that; an angle measured from the runway itself gives errors that shrink to metres exactly where they matter.
  8. Side by side:

  9. Common requirements. Both depend on correct tuning and identification by Morse code, and on monitors; neither may be used if the ident is missing. Both suffer from site errors and reflections, which is why ILS critical areas are kept clear in low visibility.
  10. Judgement. Range-and-bearing fixing suits en-route flight, where any point may be a waypoint and a nautical mile is acceptable. Angular guidance from the runway suits the final approach, where only one path matters and the error must shrink to metres at the threshold. So angular guidance is the right choice for the last few miles.
  11. Check: the localizer full-scale offset grows in proportion to distance, 318 m at 10 km against 105 m at 3.3 km (ratio 3.03, as 10 ÷ 3.3); and the glide path band shrinks fivefold from 5 nm to 1 nm (372 ft to 74 ft).

Glossary

Accelerometer
A sensor that measures specific force along one sensitive axis, that is, acceleration minus gravity.
Accuracy
The closeness of a reported position to the true position, expressed in metres or nautical miles.
ADS-B
A surveillance service in which an aircraft broadcasts its identity, position and velocity, derived from its own navigation systems.
Bearing
The horizontal direction from the aircraft to a station, measured clockwise from north in degrees.
Dilution of precision
A factor that amplifies range errors into position errors because of the geometry of the visible satellites.
DME
Distance Measuring Equipment: an interrogator in the aircraft and a transponder on the ground that together give slant range in nautical miles from round-trip time.
ECEF
A Cartesian frame with its origin at the Earth's centre and axes fixed to the rotating Earth.
Ephemeris
The set of orbital parameters broadcast by a navigation satellite so a receiver can compute its position.
Fix
The point where two or more lines of position cross, taken as the aircraft's position.
Gimbal
A pivoted ring that lets an instrument or platform rotate about one axis independently of its mount.
Glideslope
The ILS ground beam that gives up-down guidance along the approach path, normally 3°.
Heading
The direction in which the aircraft nose points, measured clockwise from north in degrees.
Hyperbola
The curve traced by points whose distances to two fixed stations differ by a constant amount.
Kalman filter
A recursive estimator that combines a predicted state with a new measurement, weighting each by its covariance.
Line of position
The curve or straight line on which one measurement is constant, so it contains the true position.
Local NED
A navigation frame at the aircraft with axes pointing north, east and down.
Localizer
The ILS ground beam that gives left-right guidance relative to the runway centreline.
Nutation
A short-lived wobble of a gyroscope's spin axis about its steady direction after a disturbance.
Precession
The steady turning of a spinning axis at right angles to an applied torque, at a rate set by the torque and the spin momentum.
Pseudorange
The apparent satellite-to-receiver range obtained from signal travel time before the receiver clock offset is estimated and removed.
Radio altimeter
A frequency-modulated radar that measures height above the terrain directly beneath the aircraft.
Repeatability
The closeness of repeated readings of the same true value to each other, which can be good even when accuracy is poor.
Schuler tuning
Designing the inertial platform loop to have an 84.4 min period so that horizontal acceleration does not build a growing tilt error.
TAWS
Terrain Awareness and Warning System: equipment that alerts the crew to terrain or obstacle conflict, using radio altitude and a terrain database.
