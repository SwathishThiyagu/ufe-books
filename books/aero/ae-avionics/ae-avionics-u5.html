5.1 Geometric concepts of navigation

An aircraft rarely knows its position directly. It measures something small — the time a radio pulse takes to return, the angle a beam arrives from, the difference in distance to two stations — and turns that number into a line on a map. Every point on that line would have given the same reading. Cross two such lines and you have a fix. This chapter builds the geometry behind that: the surface, the words for direction, the shapes of the lines, and how the crossing angle sets the size of the error.

You need basic trigonometry and the air data instruments of Unit 3 (airspeed indicator and altimeter) for the third dimension.

The surface you draw on

Navigation starts with a model of the Earth. The simplest is a sphere. A meridian is a great circle through both poles; the equator is the great circle at right angles to it. Latitude φ is the angle from the equator, 0° to 90° north or south. Longitude λ is the angle east or west of Greenwich, 0° to 180°.

The real Earth is flattened at the poles, so a sphere would misplace a point by up to about 21 km. The standard shape is an ellipsoid of revolution called WGS-84, with semi-major axis a = 6 378 137 m and flattening f = 1/298.257223563. Satellite orbits and GPS coordinates are expressed on this ellipsoid, so it is not an academic detail.

A great circle is the shortest surface path between two points; its plane passes through the Earth's centre. A rhumb line keeps a constant track and cuts every meridian at the same angle. It is easier to fly but longer, except along a meridian or the equator.

The nautical mile is 1852 m, originally one minute of arc of latitude. One knot is one nautical mile per hour. Navigation distances are in nautical miles and speeds in knots.

d = R · Δσ,   cos Δσ = sin φ₁ sin φ₂ + cos φ₁ cos φ₂ cos Δλ

d is the great-circle distance in metres; R ≈ 6 371 000 m is a mean Earth radius; φ₁ and φ₂ are the two latitudes; Δλ is the difference in longitude; Δσ is the angular separation in radians.

Five words for direction

Heading is where the nose points, measured clockwise from north. It is an aircraft quantity, and you must say true or magnetic.

Track is where the aircraft actually moves over the ground. It is a ground quantity. Wind separates track from heading.

Bearing is the direction of one point from another. Name both points: the bearing of the station from the aircraft differs from the bearing of the aircraft from the station by 180°.

Radial is a line leaving a station outward, named by its magnetic bearing from that station. An aircraft on the 030 radial sits somewhere on the line that leaves the station at 030°. Its heading can be anything.

Drift is the angle between heading and track caused by wind, positive to the right. Track = heading + drift. Ground speed is measured along the track; true airspeed along the heading.

One measurement, one line

A line of position (LOP) is the set of all points on the Earth where one measurement would give the same reading. Three shapes matter.

  • Circle: a range from a known station fixes distance but not direction, so the LOP is a circle centred on the station. DME, GNSS and radar give circles.
  • Straight line: a bearing from a known station fixes direction but not distance, so the LOP is a line leaving the station. VOR, ADF and the ILS localizer give lines.
  • Hyperbola: the difference in range to two stations is constant along a curve with the stations as foci. That curve is a hyperbola.

Fix methods take their names from the measurements: rho-theta (one range and one bearing from the same station), rho-rho (two ranges, two circles) and theta-theta (two bearings, two lines).

Two LOPs cross at a point — the fix. Three lines almost never meet at one point; they form a small triangle called the cocked hat, and the fix is assumed inside it.

The cut angle θ is the angle at which the two LOPs cross. Position uncertainty is largest along each line, so the fix is tightest when the cut is near 90° and poor when the lines run nearly parallel.

fix error = σ ÷ sin θ

σ is the 1-sigma position error along each line of position; θ is the cut angle. At θ = 90° the fix error equals σ; at θ = 30° it doubles.

Reference frames, and two kinds of good

ECEF (Earth-centred, Earth-fixed): origin at the Earth's centre, x-axis through the equator at Greenwich, z-axis through the north pole, y-axis completing a right-handed set. The frame turns with the Earth, so a ground point has fixed coordinates. Satellite systems broadcast in ECEF.

Local NED: origin at the aircraft, axes north, east, down. This is the frame the pilot and the flight management system use; heading and track live here.

Inertial: fixed in space, not turning with the Earth. Gyros and accelerometers sense motion here. Earth rate is 15.041° per hour — small, but not zero over a long flight.

Accuracy is how close the reported position is to the true one. Repeatability is how close repeated measurements are to each other. A system can be repeatable and wrong: a fixed bias shifts every fix by the same 200 m. Calibration removes bias; averaging removes noise; neither removes the other.

Worked example

Question. Two DME stations A and B are 130 nm apart on the ground. The aircraft's corrected DME reads 50 nm to A and 120 nm to B. (a) Find the cut angle at which the two range circles cross. (b) Find the aircraft's distance from A measured along the line AB, and its perpendicular distance from AB. (c) Judge the quality of the fix.

  1. The three sides of the triangle A–aircraft–B are 50 nm, 120 nm and 130 nm.
  2. Cosine rule at the aircraft: cos θ = (50² + 120² − 130²) ÷ (2 × 50 × 120) = (2500 + 14400 − 16900) ÷ 12000 = 0 ÷ 12000 = 0, so θ = 90°.
  3. Check with Pythagoras: 50² + 120² = 2500 + 14400 = 16900 = 130². The triangle is right-angled at the aircraft, which confirms θ = 90°.
  4. Along-track offset from A: x = (50² − 120² + 130²) ÷ (2 × 130) = (2500 − 14400 + 16900) ÷ 260 = 5000 ÷ 260 = 19.23 nm.
  5. Perpendicular distance: h = √(50² − 19.23²) = √(2500 − 369.8) = √2130.2 = 46.15 nm.
  6. A 90° cut gives sin θ = 1, so the fix error equals the error along a single line — the best a two-range fix can achieve.

Answer. The circles cross at θ = 90°; the aircraft lies 19.23 nm from A along AB and 46.15 nm off that line, which is a well-conditioned two-range fix.

Exam traps

  • Bearing, heading and track are three different things. A radial of 030° says where the aircraft sits relative to the station; the nose may point anywhere.
  • Outbound on the 030 radial you track 030°; inbound on the same radial you track 210°. Forgetting the 180° flip costs marks.
  • A rhumb line is not a great circle. Constant heading is not the shortest path, and the gap widens with latitude.
  • A small cocked hat does not prove a good fix. If all three lines share one systematic bias — a common timing error, say — they make a tiny triangle around a wrong point.
  • 1 nm = 1852 m and 1 kt = 1 nm/h. Mixing knots with km/h gives a factor 1.852 error in every wind-triangle answer.

5.2 Radio navigation: point-source and hyperbolic systems

A radio aid is a ground transmitter at a surveyed point, and the receiver on the aircraft turns its signal into one line of position. A point-source aid measures range or bearing to one station: the line is then a circle or a straight line through that station. A hyperbolic aid measures the difference in distance to two stations: the line is a hyperbola. This chapter covers both families — NDB/ADF, VOR, DME, TACAN, LORAN-C and Decca — and how such lines become a fix.

You need: 5.1 for lines of position, cut angle and the nautical mile.

Point-source aids: bearing or range from one station

A point-source aid has exactly one transmitter, at a surveyed position. The receiver measures one quantity about the line joining aircraft to station. Measure a bearing and the line of position is a straight line through the station. Measure a range and it is a circle centred on the station. One measurement gives one line, so a point-source aid alone cannot fix you.

The automatic direction finder (ADF) works with a non-directional beacon (NDB), a simple transmitter in the low and medium frequency bands, a few hundred kilohertz, radiating equally in all directions. The ADF antenna senses the direction the wavefront arrives from and shows a relative bearing, measured clockwise from the aircraft nose. Adding the heading gives the magnetic bearing to the station — the line of position. Long wavelengths bring trouble: the ionosphere tilts the wavefront after dark (night effect), coastlines refract it, and thunderstorms jam it. NDB bearings are therefore coarse.

The very high frequency omnidirectional range (VOR) transmits at 108–117.95 MHz. The station radiates two 30 Hz signals: a reference whose phase is the same in all directions, and one whose phase rotates with azimuth. The receiver compares the phases and reads a radial — the magnetic bearing of the aircraft measured from the station. Radial 090 means you are east of the station; your track to the station is then 270. A conventional VOR (CVOR) rotates a loop or dipole physically. A Doppler VOR (DVOR) keeps its antennas fixed and switches them round a wide ring, producing the same rotation as a Doppler shift. The wide aperture averages out reflections from buildings and terrain, so DVOR has much smaller site error.

Distance measuring equipment (DME) is two-way ranging at 962–1213 MHz. The aircraft interrogator sends a pulse pair; the ground transponder waits a fixed, known delay of about 50 µs and replies on another frequency. The receiver subtracts the delay and halves the round-trip time to get the slant range r. The ground distance is smaller, because the aircraft sits at height h:

d = √(r² − h²)

d is the ground range in metres or nautical miles; r is the measured slant range; h is the aircraft height above the transponder, in the same units as d.

The gap matters close in. From 10 km up, an aircraft 100 km out on the ground reads 100.5 km, half a percent long; at 20 km ground distance it reads 22.4 km, twelve percent long. Tactical air navigation (TACAN) is the military version in the 960–1215 MHz band, and one TACAN station gives both bearing and range, so a single transmitter yields a complete fix. Many civil VORs share a site with a DME or TACAN, forming a VORTAC.

The rho-theta fix and the cut angle

Pair a VOR with a co-located DME and one station gives both bearing and range: a rho-theta fix, ρ for range and θ for bearing. The receiver places the aircraft at distance d along radial θ from the surveyed station position. Airliners do this inside the flight management system and draw the result on the navigation display.

Any two-line fix is only as good as its cut angle — the angle at which the lines cross. Lines at 90° fix best; as the cut angle closes, a small measurement error slides the crossing point a long way. Cross-track error from a bearing error Δθ is r × Δθ with Δθ in radians: one degree at 100 nmi is about 1.7 nmi. Dividing the measurement error by sin(cut angle) gives the position error, and the same rule holds for hyperbolic lines.

Hyperbolic systems: measuring a difference

A hyperbolic aid measures no distance at all. A master and a secondary station transmit pulses at the same instant, synchronised to each other. The receiver times the two arrivals, takes the difference Δt and converts it to a range difference:

Δr = c·Δt

Δr is the range difference in metres; c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s is the speed of light; Δt is the measured time difference in seconds.

The aircraft never learns either range, only their difference — and that is enough. The points whose distances to two fixed stations differ by a constant form a hyperbola with the stations at its foci. So one pair of stations gives one hyperbola, one line of position. Take f as the half baseline: a = Δr/2 is the semi-major axis and b = √(f² − a²) is the semi-minor axis. Δr = 0 gives a = 0, and the hyperbola collapses to the straight perpendicular bisector of the baseline. The branch tells the side: the station whose signal arrives first is the nearer one.

A fix needs two hyperbolas, so three stations — one master measured against two secondaries. LORAN-C did this at 100 kHz using ground waves that follow the earth's curve, and its chains covered coastlines and continents to a few hundred metres. Decca compared the phase of continuous low-frequency waves over the North Sea and gave repeated positions to ships and helicopters. Omega, at very low frequency near 10–14 kHz, covered the globe with eight stations and was switched off in the 1990s. Satellite navigation reuses this geometry — see 5.6 — with the receiver's own clock offset carried as one more unknown.

Worked example

Question. A LORAN-C receiver measures a time difference of 100 µs between the master station and one secondary. The master–secondary baseline is 600 km. (a) Find the range difference Δr. (b) Find the semi-major and semi-minor axes of the hyperbola. (c) Which branch is the aircraft on?

  1. Δr = c·Δt = (3 × 10⁸ m/s) × (100 × 10⁻⁶ s) = 3.0 × 10⁴ m = 30.0 km.
  2. a = Δr/2 = 30.0/2 = 15.0 km.
  3. Half baseline f = 600/2 = 300.0 km. Then b = √(f² − a²) = √(300.0² − 15.0²) = √(90000 − 225) = √89775 = 299.6 km.
  4. The secondary's pulse arrives later, so the aircraft is farther from the secondary. It lies on the branch nearer the master.

Answer. Δr = 30.0 km, a = 15.0 km, b = 299.6 km, and the aircraft is on the branch closer to the master station.

Worked example 2

Question. An aircraft at 9 km altitude reads a DME slant range of 150 km. What is its ground distance from the transponder?

  1. d = √(r² − h²) = √(150² − 9²).
  2. 150² = 22500 and 9² = 81, so d = √22419.
  3. d = 149.7 km.

Answer. The ground distance is 149.7 km.

Exam traps

  • One hyperbola is one line of position, not a fix. Two hyperbolas, hence three stations, are needed; a two-station reading fixes nothing.
  • DME reads slant range. Directly overhead at 10 km altitude the DME shows 10 km, not zero, and the ground distance is always smaller than the reading.
  • A VOR radial runs from the station to the aircraft. Radial 090 puts you east of the station and your track to the station is 270; swap the two and the fix is 180° wrong.
  • A hyperbolic receiver's clock need not be synchronised with the stations — only the stations must agree with each other, because a common clock offset cancels in the difference. In satellite navigation that offset is solved as an extra unknown.
  • Watch units in Δr = c·Δt: microseconds, not milliseconds. A 100 ms difference would give 30 000 km, larger than the earth.

5.3 Landing aids: ILS and MLS

An ILS lets an aircraft fly a straight descent to a runway even when the runway is not in sight. It broadcasts two radio beams: one tells the aircraft whether it is left or right of the centreline, the other whether it is above or below the correct slope. The crew follows two needles on the flight display. ILS is the world's standard precision approach aid; MLS was designed to remove its siting limits, and satellite augmentation is now replacing both.

You need 5.1 for lines of position and 5.2 for the point-source idea and DME ranging.

Two beams, two angles

ILS is a point-source aid: the runway-end equipment is the known point, and the aircraft measures angles from it. Two separate transmitters define the two angles. The localizer, at the far end of the runway, works in the VHF band 108–112 MHz. It has 40 channels, spaced 50 kHz, using the odd-tenth pattern (108.10, 108.15, 108.30, 108.35 MHz and so on). It defines the runway centreline: fly with zero left-right deviation and you track the centreline. The glideslope transmitter, beside the touchdown zone, works in the UHF band 329.15–335 MHz. Its 40 channels are paired with the localizer channels, spaced 150 kHz apart, so selecting one ILS frequency tunes both receivers. It defines the descent angle, usually 3° to the runway. Range comes from marker beacons at 75 MHz — outer, middle and inner — or from a DME paired with the ILS. Approach lighting adds the final visual segment. Full-scale needle deflection is 2.5° each side of the localizer centreline and 0.7° total for the glideslope, that is ±0.35°.

Modulation depth is the guidance

Both transmitters send an amplitude-modulated carrier. The modulation is at 90 Hz and 150 Hz. The receiver separates the two tones and compares their depths: m90 and m150, each a fraction from 0 to 1. On the localizer, 90 Hz is stronger to the left of the centreline and 150 Hz is stronger to the right. On the glideslope, 150 Hz is stronger above the path and 90 Hz is stronger below. Equal depths give DDM = 0, the on-path condition. Full-scale DDM is 0.155 on the localizer and 0.175 on the glideslope; at full scale the needle is at the edge of its scale. The pilot flies to keep both needles centred. A category I approach needs a decision height of 60 m (200 ft) and runway visual range of 550 m. CAT II needs 30 m (100 ft) and 300 m. CAT IIIA goes below 30 m or has no decision height and needs 200 m. IIIB needs 50 m. IIIC has no limits. The categories describe the whole system — aircraft, crew and ground equipment — not the receiver alone.

DDM = m150 − m90

m150 and m90 are the modulation depths at 150 Hz and 90 Hz, each from 0 to 1. Positive DDM means 150 Hz is stronger.

MLS and satellite augmentation

MLS was designed to overcome ILS limits. It works at about 5 GHz, 5031–5091 MHz, with 200 channels. Instead of a steady beam whose modulation depth is measured, MLS uses a time-referenced scanning beam: a narrow beam sweeps across the approach sector, and the receiver times the pass. Azimuth coverage is about ±60° and elevation coverage 0–20°. That wide sector allows curved and segmented approaches, not just a straight line. At 5 GHz the site is much less sensitive to buildings, terrain and ground reflections, and the antenna is small. MLS is paired with DME-P for precise range. ILS remains cheaper and is fitted at most runways. MLS saw limited civil uptake because ILS was already installed and satellite methods arrived. The modern replacement is satellite augmentation: GBAS corrects GPS locally for precision approach, and SBAS provides a wide-area correction. These are covered in 5.6.

Worked example

Question. An aircraft is on a 3° ILS glide path and the DME reads 5.0 nm to the threshold. (a) What height should the aircraft be at? (b) The glideslope receiver measures a DDM of 0.05 with 150 Hz stronger. The glideslope full-scale DDM is 0.175 at 0.35°. How far off path is the aircraft, and on which side?

  1. Convert range to feet: 1 nm = 6076 ft, so 5.0 nm = 5.0 × 6076 = 30,380 ft.
  2. Height = range × tan(3°). tan 3° = 0.05241. Height = 30,380 × 0.05241 = 1592 ft. Rounded, 1590 ft, or about 318 ft per nm.
  3. Convert DDM to a fraction of full scale: fraction = 0.05 ÷ 0.175 = 0.2857.
  4. Full-scale angle is 0.35°, so angular deviation = 0.2857 × 0.35° = 0.100°. Rounded, 0.10°.
  5. 150 Hz stronger means above the path on the glideslope, so the aircraft is above the 3° path.

Answer. The aircraft should be at about 1592 ft (roughly 1590 ft, or 318 ft per nm); it is 0.10° above the 3° path.

Exam traps

  • Do not carry the localizer sense across to the glideslope: on the localizer 90 Hz is left and 150 Hz is right; on the glideslope 150 Hz is above and 90 Hz is below.
  • Do not swap the bands: localizer 108–112 MHz VHF, glideslope 329.15–335 MHz UHF.
  • Full-scale deflection is 2.5° each side of the localizer centreline, but 0.7° total (±0.35°) on the glideslope.
  • CAT IIIA is either below 30 m or no decision height; only IIIC has no limits at all.
  • The marker beacon is at 75 MHz and gives range, not guidance; it does not define the centreline or slope.

5.4 Gyroscopes: principle, precession and nutation

A gyroscope is a spinning mass. Once spinning, its axis stays pointed in the same direction in inertial space unless a torque acts on it. Push the axis and it does not move the way you pushed; it moves at right angles, a motion called precession. Aircraft use this to sense attitude, heading and turn rate without looking outside. This chapter gives the angular momentum law behind rigidity, calculates precession from torque, explains the short wobble called nutation, lists the drift errors that remain, and names the three cockpit gyro instruments.

You need: torque, moment of inertia and angular velocity from basic rotational mechanics; the navigation frame is introduced in 5.1, and the autopilot that consumes gyro signals is in Unit 1, Flight Control Systems.

Rigidity in space and the gimbal

A rotor spinning about its axis carries angular momentum. The size is H = Iω. I is the moment of inertia in kg·m², ω is the spin rate in rad/s, and H is in kg·m²/s. With no external torque, H stays fixed in inertial space. That is rigidity in space: the spin axis keeps its direction while the aircraft turns around it.

H = Iω

H is angular momentum in kg·m²/s; I is moment of inertia in kg·m²; ω is spin rate in rad/s.

A free gyro has three degrees of freedom: spin about its own axis, and tilt about two perpendicular gimbal axes. A cockpit instrument uses two of these as its measurement axes and lets the third carry the spin. Two degrees of freedom are enough to report pitch and roll, or heading and bank, while the case moves with the aircraft.

Gimbals can line up. When two axes become parallel, one degree of freedom is lost and the gyro cannot respond in that direction. This is gimbal lock. Caging locks the rotor during violent manoeuvres or before start. Erection is the slow levelling of the gyro to the local vertical, done with pendulous vanes or small torque motors.

Torque turns into precession

A torque changes angular momentum: T = dH/dt. T is torque in N·m, H is angular momentum in kg·m²/s, and t is time in s. The change dH points along T. The tip of the H arrow moves toward T, so the spin axis swings at right angles to the push, not along it.

T = dH/dt and Ω = T/H = T/(Iω)

T is torque in N·m; H is angular momentum in kg·m²/s; t is time in s; Ω is steady precession rate in rad/s; I is moment of inertia in kg·m²; ω is spin rate in rad/s.

For a steady torque, the axis precesses at Ω = T/H. A large I or a fast spin gives a large H and a small Ω, so the axis resists the push. A rotor at 24,000 rpm is stiff for this reason. Direction follows the right-hand rule: curl the fingers from H toward T, and the thumb gives the precession direction in the sense of the spin.

Nutation, drift and real errors

Apply a torque suddenly and the axis overshoots before settling into steady precession. The short wobble is nutation. Some syllabi call nutation the wobble and precession the topple. Damping, such as viscous fluid or eddy currents, removes nutation; without damping it would ring for a long time.

Real drift comes from bearing friction, rotor imbalance and case motion. Apparent drift comes from the Earth turning under the gyro. About the local vertical, the rate is 15.04 × sin(φ) °/h, where φ is latitude. It is 15.04 °/h at the poles and zero at the equator. Flying east or west adds a transport rate because the aircraft changes longitude over the curved Earth.

Ωapp = 15.04 × sin(φ) °/h

Ωapp is apparent drift about the local vertical in degrees per hour; φ is latitude in degrees; 15.04 °/h is Earth's rotation rate.

Exam traps

  • Drawing precession along the push. The spin axis moves at right angles to the torque, in the direction that carries H toward T.
  • Treating nutation as the steady motion. Nutation is a short transient after a sudden torque; damping removes it, leaving steady precession.

  • Calling a turn coordinator an attitude indicator. It is a rate gyro: it shows rate of turn and roll, not a steady bank angle.

5.5 Inertial sensors and inertial navigation systems

An inertial navigation system carries its own sensors and asks nothing of the outside world. Three accelerometers measure the push needed to hold a proof mass still, three gyros measure how fast the aircraft turns, and a computer integrates these to give velocity and position. Nothing is transmitted, so nothing can be jammed. The catch is drift: small sensor biases integrate into large position errors, so the answer is good for minutes and wanders over hours. INS flies airliners, missiles, submarines and spacecraft, usually blended with GPS (see 5.8).

You need 5.4 for gyro behaviour, 5.1 for the frames, and the accelerometer material in Unit 4.

Accelerometers measure specific force, gyros measure rate

An accelerometer holds a small proof mass. When the case accelerates the mass lags; a strain gauge, capacitor or piezoelectric crystal senses the displacement (Unit 4). A force-rebalance accelerometer pushes the mass back to null with a coil and outputs the coil current, keeping the sensor linear and stiff.

The output is specific force f, not acceleration in space. At rest the accelerometer reads 1 g upward: the ground pushes the mass up. In free fall it reads zero, though it accelerates at 9.81 m/s². To get inertial acceleration, add gravity back.

a = f + g

a is inertial acceleration (m/s²); f is measured specific force (m/s²); g is gravitational acceleration (m/s²), 9.81 m/s² downward. At rest f is 9.81 m/s² upward, so a = 0.

Gyros are rate sensors. A ring laser gyro sends two beams round a triangular cavity in opposite directions; rotation makes their path lengths differ, and the beat frequency is proportional to rate (the Sagnac effect). A fibre-optic gyro does the same in a coil of fibre with no moving parts. MEMS gyros shake a tiny mass and read the Coriolis force. Output is angular rate ω in °/s or °/h; one integration gives angle.

Bias is the output at zero input; scale factor error is the slope error, in ppm or %. Gyro bias is quoted in °/h and accelerometer bias in mg, where 1 mg = 0.00981 m/s² and 1 µg = 9.81 × 10⁻⁶ m/s² (one millionth of a g).

Frames, mechanizations and the inertial chain

Four frames matter. The inertial frame does not rotate. The Earth-fixed (ECEF) frame rotates with the Earth. The local navigation frame has north, east and down (NED) axes at the aircraft. The body frame is bolted to the airframe: x forward, y right, z down. Vectors move between frames by a direction cosine matrix (DCM, a 3×3 matrix of direction cosines), or by a quaternion — four numbers with no singularity at 90° pitch. Attitude is the DCM from body to NED.

Two mechanizations exist. In a gimballed system the sensors sit on a platform that torque motors hold level and pointing north while the aircraft turns around it, so the sensors see almost no rotation. In a strapdown system the sensors are bolted to the airframe and see the full rotation rate, so the computer carries attitude continuously. Strapdown won — smaller, cheaper, more reliable — at the cost of computing power and a wider sensor range.

  1. Gyros give body rates ω; integrate them with the DCM to update body-to-NED attitude.
  2. Accelerometers give f in body axes; rotate f into NED with that same DCM.
  3. Add gravity: a = f + g, where g points down (third component +g in NED).
  4. Integrate a once for velocity, then again for position.
  5. Feed position back to the attitude step so the local level follows the Earth's curvature.

Alignment, Schuler tuning and error growth

Before use the INS must be aligned: its starting attitude must be found. Levelling uses the accelerometers — any tilt puts a horizontal component of gravity into f, so the pitch and roll that null it are correct. Gyrocompassing uses the gyros: the Earth turns at 15.04 °/h and its horizontal component points north. Rotating the heading until the sensed horizontal rate is zero finds north with no magnetic reference. Alignment needs a few minutes and the aircraft still.

A feedback from position to attitude is tuned to the Schuler period. A level platform carried over a round Earth behaves like a pendulum of length equal to the Earth's radius. Tuned to that natural period, a horizontal acceleration produces no steady tilt: the platform swings instead of drifting off level as the aircraft crosses the curved Earth.

T = 2π√(R/g) = 2π√(6.371 × 10⁶ / 9.81) = 5064 s = 84.4 min

T is the Schuler period (s); R = 6.371 × 10⁶ m is the Earth's radius; g = 9.81 m/s².

Errors after alignment grow by integration. An accelerometer bias b gives velocity error bt and position error ½bt². A gyro bias ε tilts the platform by εt, giving false horizontal acceleration gεt and position error gεt³/6. Gyro quality dominates: an accelerometer bias is painful, a gyro bias is fatal over hours.

Δp = ½bt² and Δp = gεt³/6

Δp is position error (m); b is accelerometer bias (m/s²); ε is gyro bias (rad/s); t is time since alignment (s); g = 9.81 m/s².

With the Schuler loop closed, an accelerometer bias gives a bounded oscillating position error instead of ½bt². A gyro bias still gives a position error that grows steadily with time (a ramp with an 84.4 min ripple); only the tilt stays bounded, so gyro bias remains the dominant long-term error. The t² and t³ formulas above describe the unaided open-loop case. The cure is external aid — a satellite fix, or a Kalman filter blending the two (see 5.8).

5.6 Satellite navigation: GPS and NAVSTAR

A satellite navigation receiver listens to coded radio signals from spacecraft whose orbits it already knows. It measures how long each signal took to arrive, turns each time into a range, and solves the ranges together to find its own position. The receiver's own clock is cheap, so its offset is solved as a fourth unknown. GPS — the American system whose programme name is NAVSTAR — guides airliners, ships, cars and phones, and its timing output also runs bank networks, UPI switches and power grids.

You need: 5.1 for frames and position fixing, and 5.2 for time-based ranging from a point source.

The NAVSTAR constellation and its orbits

GPS is the United States satellite navigation system; its original programme name is NAVSTAR. The baseline design places 24 satellites in 6 orbital planes, each plane inclined at 55° to the equator, at an altitude of about 20,200 km. More satellites are flown than the baseline, so a receiver on the ground usually sees 6 to 12 at a time.

Each satellite moves on a Keplerian ellipse described by six elements: semi-major axis a, eccentricity e, inclination i, right ascension of ascending node Ω, argument of perigee ω and true anomaly ν. The broadcast ephemeris lets the receiver rebuild this orbit and compute where the satellite was at the instant it transmitted.

T = 2π √(a³ / μ)

T is the orbital period in seconds, a is the semi-major axis in metres and μ = 3.986 × 10¹⁴ m³/s² is the Earth's gravitational constant. With a ≈ 26,560 km, T ≈ 11 h 58 min.

That period is half a sidereal day. After one sidereal day the Earth has turned once and the satellite has completed two orbits, so it sits over the same ground point again: the ground track repeats, and the sky geometry above a fixed site repeats too. Surveyors use this. A period of exactly 12 h solar would drift about four minutes a day and the track would not close.

PRN codes and the navigation message

All satellites share the same carrier frequencies: L1 at 1575.42 MHz, L2 at 1227.60 MHz and L5 at 1176.45 MHz. They are told apart by code. Each satellite carries its own pseudo-random noise (PRN) sequence, and a receiver separates them by correlating with the right copy — code division multiple access, CDMA.

Two codes ride on L1. The coarse/acquisition (C/A) code runs at 1.023 Mchip/s with 1023 chips, so it repeats every 1 ms. The precision P code runs at 10.23 Mchip/s and repeats weekly; it is encrypted as Y for authorised users. One C/A chip lasts 1/(1.023 × 10⁶) s ≈ 977.5 ns, which is 293 m of range, and the receiver resolves a small fraction of a chip by watching the correlation peak.

The navigation message is a 50 bit/s data stream. It carries the satellite's own ephemeris, the almanac of coarse orbits for the whole constellation, clock correction coefficients, ionospheric model coefficients and health flags. A full message takes 12.5 minutes, but one ephemeris set is enough for a first fix.

Pseudorange and the four-unknown fix

ρ = c (t_receive − t_transmit)

ρ is the pseudorange in metres, c = 299,792,458 m/s, t_receive is the arrival time read on the receiver clock and t_transmit is the transmission time read on the satellite clock. Both clocks are imperfect, so ρ is not the true geometric range.

For a satellite at (x_s, y_s, z_s) and a receiver at (x, y, z) the geometric range is r = √((x − x_s)² + (y − y_s)² + (z − z_s)²). The unknowns are x, y, z and the receiver clock offset δt. Each satellite gives one equation, ρ = r + c δt plus small propagation terms.

  1. Track four or more satellites and decode each ephemeris.
  2. Compute every satellite position at its transmission time.
  3. Measure code phase and form each pseudorange.
  4. Start from an approximate position and clock offset.
  5. Linearise, solve for the corrections, iterate until they are small.
  6. Output x, y, z, δt and the dilution of precision.

The equations are nonlinear, so the receiver linearises about its guess and builds the geometry matrix H, one row per satellite: a unit line-of-sight vector plus a 1 in the clock column. It then solves Δx = (HᵗH)⁻¹ Hᵗ Δρ and repeats. The same (HᵗH)⁻¹ gives the dilution of precision (DOP): large DOP means satellites are bunched together and every range error is stretched into a bigger position error.

Segments, other constellations and augmentation

GPS has three segments. The space segment is the satellites. The control segment is a master control station with monitor stations and upload antennas; it tracks the satellites, computes orbit and clock corrections, and uploads them. The user segment is every receiver, from a phone to an aircraft GNSS unit feeding the flight management system.

Other constellations work the same way with different details. GLONASS uses frequency division (FDMA, a different channel per satellite) and a 64.8° inclination that favours high latitudes. Galileo serves Europe, BeiDou serves China. NavIC is India's regional system: geostationary plus inclined geosynchronous satellites covering India and the surrounding region on L5 and S-band.

Augmentation improves accuracy and integrity. Satellite-based augmentation (SBAS) — GAGAN in India, WAAS in the United States, EGNOS in Europe — broadcasts corrections and integrity flags from a geostationary satellite over a whole region. Ground-based augmentation (GBAS) does the same from an airport for precision approach. Aircraft-based augmentation (ABAS) is receiver-side monitoring such as RAIM, which checks the measurements against each other.

Question. A GPS satellite is at a slant range of 20,200 km from a receiver. (a) How long does its signal take to arrive? (b) The receiver clock is fast by 3 µs; what range error does that add to every pseudorange? (c) Why does the receiver need four satellites rather than three?

5.7 Satellite signal errors: clocks and atmospheric effects

Satellite navigation works by timing radio signals, and every timing error becomes a position error. The error comes from four places: the satellite clock and orbit, the ionosphere, the troposphere, and local multipath and receiver noise. The ionosphere is dispersive, so two frequencies can remove it; the troposphere is not, so only a model helps. Only a few universities teach this at this depth. The error budget and dilution of precision turn these metres into the position uncertainty an aircraft must meet.

You need 5.6 for pseudorange and broadcast data, and 5.1 for dilution of precision.

Clock, orbit and the error budget

Each pseudorange is c times the measured travel time. Four corrections are applied before the fix: satellite clock, orbit, ionosphere and troposphere. The broadcast clock polynomial is δt_s = a_f0 + a_f1(t − t_oc) + a_f2(t − t_oc)², where δt_s is clock offset (s), a_f0 offset (s), a_f1 drift (s/s), a_f2 drift rate (s/s²), and t − t_oc age since reference. This removes most clock error. Relativistic and Sagnac corrections handle gravity, orbital speed and Earth rotation during signal travel. Ephemeris error is a few metres. The user equivalent range error (UERE) combines all range errors at the antenna. Position error = UERE × DOP; HDOP is horizontal dilution, VDOP vertical. RAIM checks redundant ranges; differential GPS removes common errors.

Ionosphere

The ionosphere is a shell of free electrons from about 60 km to 1000 km. Total electron content (TEC) is electrons in a 1 m² column; 1 TECU = 10^16 electrons/m². Group delay adds range d_I = 40.3 TEC / f², where d_I is metres, TEC electrons/m², f hertz. Delay grows as 1/f²: typically 5–15 m at zenith on L1 (1575.42 MHz), larger near the horizon and at solar maximum. The Klobuchar model removes about half. A dual-frequency L1/L5 receiver forms an ionosphere-free combination and removes the first-order term. Scintillation is rapid amplitude and phase fluctuation near the equator and poles, causing cycle slips and loss of lock.

Troposphere, multipath and local errors

The troposphere is the neutral atmosphere from the surface to about 8–12 km. Its delay is non-dispersive: nearly the same at L1 and L5, so dual frequency does not cancel it. Zenith delay is about 2.5 m, roughly 2.3 m dry plus 0.2 m wet. Slant delay is d_T ≈ d_z / sin E, where d_z is zenith delay and E is elevation; at 10°, d_T = 2.5 / 0.1736 = 14.4 m, reaching tens of metres near the horizon. Models such as Saastamoinen or Hopfield remove most of it. Multipath is reflected signal arriving after the direct ray; it is 1–5 m and reduced by a choke-ring antenna, ground plane, site choice and receiver design. Receiver noise is usually below a few metres.

Worked example

Question. An ionospheric delay of 12.0 m is measured on L1 at 1575.42 MHz. (a) Predict the delay on L5 at 1176.45 MHz. (b) The remaining user equivalent range error is 5.0 m and HDOP is 1.8. Find the one-sigma horizontal position error.

  1. Delay scales as 1/f², so d₂/d₁ = (f₁/f₂)².
  2. f₁/f₂ = 1575.42 / 1176.45 = 1.3391; squared = 1.7933.
  3. d₂ = 12.0 × 1.7933 = 21.52 m, rounded to 21.5 m. L5 is lower in frequency, so its delay is larger.
  4. σ_h = UERE × HDOP = 5.0 × 1.8 = 9.0 m.

Answer. The L5 delay is 21.5 m and the one-sigma horizontal position error is 9.0 m.

Exam traps

  • The 1/f² law applies to the ionosphere only; the troposphere is non-dispersive, so L1/L5 does not remove it.
  • A lower frequency sees a larger ionospheric delay: L5 gets more than L1, not less.
  • The satellite clock is corrected by the broadcast polynomial, not by the ionosphere or troposphere models.
  • DOP is a geometry multiplier, not a range error; a large HDOP magnifies a small UERE.
  • Dual frequency removes first-order ionosphere, not multipath, receiver noise or orbit error.

5.8 Kalman filter and GPS-INS integration

An inertial navigation system is smooth and continuous but drifts without limit. A GPS fix is bounded but noisy, and it disappears when the sky is blocked. A Kalman filter blends the two: it carries a number for how much each source is trusted at that instant and weights the correction accordingly. The estimate and its uncertainty are updated in a two-step loop that runs many times a second. Every airliner, drone and missile flying on mixed sensors uses this loop, and some syllabi call the whole subject multisensor navigation.

You need: Chapter 5.5 for the INS and its drifting error, and Chapters 5.6 and 5.7 for the GPS fix and its error budget.

A filter, not a fix

An INS integrates the accelerations its IMU measures, so its output is smooth but its error grows without limit. A GPS receiver gives a position that is bounded but noisy, and it can vanish for minutes. Neither alone is trustworthy all the time. A Kalman filter combines them instead of choosing between them.

The filter carries a state vector x: the quantities it wants to know. In a typical aviation system these are position, velocity, attitude, and the biases of the accelerometers and gyros.

It also carries a covariance matrix P: the uncertainty in x. The diagonal entries are variances, in m², (m/s)² and rad². The off-diagonal entries say how two errors move together — a position error caused by a velocity error, for instance.

P is the heart of the idea. It is the filter's own statement of how wrong it might be right now. Small P means the estimate is trusted; large P means it is not. Every correction applied later is sized by P.

  1. Predict: push x and P forward using inertial data only.
  2. Update: compare with a GPS measurement and correct x and P.
  3. Repeat, many times a second, for the whole flight.

Predict, then update

The predict step carries the state forward on inertial data alone. F is the state transition matrix — the mechanics that turn position, velocity and attitude at one instant into the next. The control term is the IMU's measured acceleration and rotation, resolved into the navigation frame.

x = F x + B u

x is the state vector (n × 1); F is the state transition matrix (n × n); B u is the inertial input driving the state forward.

P = F P Fᵀ + Q

P is the state covariance; F P Fᵀ carries the old uncertainty forward; Q is the process noise covariance, the uncertainty the model itself adds.

Q says how much the model is distrusted. A flawless INS with no vibration and no bias drift would have Q = 0. Real Q is set larger when vibration, temperature swings or uncorrected bias are expected. Large Q makes the filter nervous: P grows quickly, so the next GPS fix is believed more.

The update step uses the GPS measurement z. H maps the state into the measurement — for a position fix it simply picks the position part of x.

y = z − H x

y is the innovation, measurement minus prediction; z is the GPS fix; H x is the predicted measurement.

The innovation has a sign. If the GPS says the aircraft is 50 m ahead of the prediction, the correction goes forward, not backward.

K = P Hᵀ (H P Hᵀ + R)⁻¹

K is the Kalman gain; R is the measurement noise covariance taken from the GPS error budget; K is small when P is small.

x ← x + K y, P ← (I − K H) P

I is the identity matrix; the covariance shrinks by the factor (I − K H) at every fix.

In one dimension K = P ÷ (P + R), always between 0 and 1. K near 1 means trust the GPS; K near 0 means trust the INS. It is recomputed at every fix, never tuned once and left.

Loosely, tightly and ultra-tight coupling

Loosely coupled. The receiver solves its own position and velocity, and hands them to the filter as z. Simple, receiver-independent, and needs four or more satellites. Most civil installations work this way.

Tightly coupled. Raw pseudoranges enter as z, and H predicts the range to each satellite from the blended position. Two or three satellites plus a good INS still constrain the state, because the INS fills the missing geometry. This helps under jamming and in urban canyons.

Ultra-tight. INS velocity is fed into the receiver's tracking loops, narrowing its frequency search so weaker signals can be tracked. Used in military and research receivers.

Coasting. When GPS drops, only predict runs. P grows step by step and the solution stays smooth but drifts. After minutes P is huge, so the returning fix produces a jump; good software limits the correction or declares the filter failed.

Feedback. The update also estimates accelerometer and gyro biases. Those estimates are fed back to reset the sensors, so the raw inertial data stops drifting. This closes the loop and is what makes long flights possible.

Worked example

Question. A one-dimensional GPS-INS filter has just finished a predict step. The INS solution is 1000 m along track with variance P = 100 m². At the same instant a GPS fix gives 1050 m with variance R = 25 m². Find the Kalman gain, the blended position and the new position variance, and say which source the filter trusted more.

  1. Gain in one dimension: K = P ÷ (P + R) = 100 ÷ (100 + 25) = 0.8.
  2. Innovation: y = z − x = 1050 − 1000 = 50 m, so the GPS is ahead of the prediction.
  3. Correction: K y = 0.8 × 50 = 40 m, applied forward.
  4. Blended position: x ← 1000 + 40 = 1040 m.
  5. New variance: P ← (1 − K) P = 0.2 × 100 = 20 m², so σ = √20 ≈ 4.47 m.
  6. Weights are 0.8 on GPS and 0.2 on INS, matching R = 25 m² being four times smaller than P = 100 m².

Answer. K = 0.8, blended position 1040 m, new variance 20 m² (σ ≈ 4.47 m), which is better than either source alone.

Exam traps

  • K is not a fixed percentage or a tuned constant. It is recomputed from P and R at every step; writing 'K = 0.5 always' is the classic mistake.
  • The innovation is measurement minus prediction, z − H x. Reversing it sends the correction the wrong way and doubles the error.
  • Q and R are swapped under exam pressure. Q is process noise (how much the INS model is distrusted); R is measurement noise (the GPS error budget).
  • P grows during predict and shrinks during update. Forgetting the growth makes the filter overconfident and it stops listening to GPS.
  • The blend is not a plain average. With P = 100 m² and R = 25 m² the weights are 0.2 and 0.8, not 0.5 and 0.5.

5.9 Radar principles, surveillance and ADS-B

Radar sends out a radio pulse and listens for the echo. The delay gives range; the antenna direction gives bearing. Ground radars use the echo itself, and also interrogate each aircraft's transponder, to build a traffic picture. ADS-B goes further: the aircraft broadcasts its own satellite position twice a second and anyone in range can receive it. A tracker fuses all these plots into one picture for the controller and the cockpit. The same principles run the weather radar, the radio altimeter and collision avoidance.

You need: 5.1 for range and bearing as lines of position, and Unit 4 for the displays that show the traffic picture.

A pulse radar, block by block

Radar means radio detection and ranging. A transmitter makes a short burst of radio energy, an antenna radiates it, and the receiver waits for the echo. The delay gives range and the antenna angle gives bearing — the two lines of position from 5.1, so one radar fixes an aircraft by itself.

R = c · t ÷ 2

R is slant range in metres, c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s is the speed of light, and t is the echo delay in seconds measured from the transmitted pulse. The 2 appears because the pulse travels out and back.

  1. The transmitter generates a pulse of width τ at peak power P_t.
  2. The duplexer connects the antenna to the transmitter, then switches it to the receiver so the strong pulse cannot burn the receiver out.
  3. The antenna — a rotating dish or a fixed phased array — radiates the pulse and later collects the echo.
  4. The receiver amplifies the weak echo, and a threshold decides whether it is a real target or noise.
  5. The timer reads t, the computer computes R, and the antenna angle gives the bearing in degrees.
  6. The display plots one blip at that range and bearing.

Echo power drops as the fourth power of range: double the distance and only one-sixteenth comes back. That is why radar powers are measured in kilowatts, and why detection range is the hardest number to improve.

The radar equation, resolution and ambiguity

R_max = [ P_t G² λ² σ ÷ ( (4π)³ P_min ) ]^¼

R_max is the maximum detection range in metres; P_t is the transmitted peak power in watts; G is the antenna gain, a pure number; λ is the wavelength in metres; σ is the radar cross-section of the target in square metres, set by shape and material rather than physical size; P_min is the weakest echo the receiver can detect, in watts.

Pulse width and pulse repetition frequency set two different limits, and they are the pair students mix up.

ΔR = c τ ÷ 2 and R_u = c ÷ (2 × PRF)

ΔR is the range resolution in metres and τ the pulse width in seconds; R_u is the maximum unambiguous range in metres and PRF the pulse repetition frequency in hertz, that is, how many pulses leave the antenna each second.

A short pulse separates targets in range; a low PRF gives a long unambiguous range. You cannot have both cheaply, so air-surveillance radars switch between several PRFs and stitch the answers together.

f_d = 2 v_r ÷ λ

f_d is the Doppler shift in hertz, v_r is the radial (closing) component of target speed in m/s, and λ is the wavelength in metres. The shift is a rise in frequency when the target closes. An aircraft flying across the beam has v_r near zero and vanishes into clutter.

Primary, secondary and ADS-B

Primary surveillance radar (PSR) works on the skin echo. Metal reflects the pulse, so every aircraft in the beam appears whether its transponder works or not. PSR gives range and azimuth and nothing else — no identity, no height.

Secondary surveillance radar (SSR) is a conversation. The ground station interrogates at 1030 MHz and the transponder replies at 1090 MHz.

  • Mode A: identity only — the four-digit octal squawk the pilot sets.
  • Mode C: pressure altitude in 100 ft steps, sent along with the identity.
  • Mode S: a unique 24-bit aircraft address, so one aircraft can be selected and data-linked.

ADS-B — automatic dependent surveillance, broadcast — removes the interrogation. About twice a second the aircraft sends extended squitters on 1090 MHz: 112-bit messages that between them carry its GNSS position and altitude, its velocity, and its identity. Automatic because nobody asks; dependent because the position comes from the aircraft's own navigation system (5.6); broadcast because everyone in range receives it. ADS-B Out transmits. ADS-B In receives traffic and ground-uplinked radar tracks (TIS-B); on the UAT data link it also receives weather (FIS-B).

TCAS/ACAS is a separate cooperative system riding on the same transponders. It interrogates nearby aircraft directly and needs no ground station. TCAS II shows its resolution advisory as a commanded climb or descent rate on the flight director and vertical-speed indicator. The crew must respond within a few seconds; the autopilot is normally disconnected, not driven by the RA (Unit 1).

Radars carried on the aircraft

The radar altimeter is a small FMCW radar. It sweeps its frequency continuously and measures the beat between the transmitted and received waves; beat frequency rises with height above the ground below. It reads true height from 0 to about 2500 ft and feeds TAWS (5.10) and autoland.

Weather radar, usually X-band, paints rainfall rate from reflectivity and turbulence or windshear from Doppler on the navigation display.

Mapping and multimode radar is military: one hardware set switches between ground mapping, terrain following and air-to-air search. Only some syllabi list it.

Worked example

Question. A pulse radar works at PRF = 1000 Hz with a pulse width of 1 µs. (a) Find the maximum unambiguous range. (b) Find the range resolution. (c) Find the Doppler shift from an aircraft closing at 300 kt, if the radar transmits at 1030 MHz.

  1. Maximum unambiguous range: R_u = c ÷ (2 × PRF) = (3 × 10⁸ m/s) ÷ (2 × 1000 Hz) = 150 000 m = 150 km.
  2. Range resolution: ΔR = c τ ÷ 2 = (3 × 10⁸ m/s × 1 × 10⁻⁶ s) ÷ 2 = 150 m.
  3. Convert the speed: 300 kt × 0.5144 m/s per kt = 154.3 m/s.
  4. Wavelength: λ = c ÷ f = (3 × 10⁸ m/s) ÷ (1.03 × 10⁹ Hz) = 0.291 m.
  5. 1030 MHz is picked here only to practise the arithmetic; a primary surveillance radar transmits elsewhere in L-band, and 1030 MHz belongs to the SSR interrogation uplink.
  6. Doppler shift: f_d = 2 v_r ÷ λ = (2 × 154.3 m/s) ÷ 0.291 m = 1060 Hz ≈ 1.06 kHz. It is a rise in frequency because the target closes.

Answer. Unambiguous range 150 km, range resolution 150 m, and a closing Doppler shift of about 1.06 kHz.

Exam traps

  • PRF sets the unambiguous range and pulse width sets the range resolution — never the other way round.
  • Both formulas carry a factor 2 for the round trip, c τ ÷ 2 and c ÷ (2 × PRF). Drop it and every answer halves or doubles.
  • ADS-B is a broadcast, not an interrogation; nothing is sent to the aircraft to prompt it. SSR is the system that asks.
  • SSR asks at 1030 MHz and hears the reply at 1090 MHz. Reversing the pair is the standard one-mark trap.
  • More power buys detection range (radar equation), not resolution. Resolution is bought with a shorter pulse.

5.10 Terrain awareness and ground proximity warning systems

A serviceable aircraft, a healthy crew, and still it flies into a hill. That is controlled flight into terrain (CFIT), which has killed more people than any other cause in jet aviation. The cure is a warning that speaks before the ground arrives. Terrain awareness and ground proximity warning systems — GPWS, TAWS, EGPWS — compare the aircraft's path with the ground, first only the ground below, later a stored map of the terrain ahead, and call "pull up" early enough to climb away.

You need: the radar (radio) altimeter from 5.9, the glideslope of an ILS from 5.3, the GNSS position fix from 5.6, the position-error ideas of 5.7, and the barometric altimeter from Unit 3.

Why the ground kills: height above ground

Controlled flight into terrain (CFIT) is the accident in which an airworthy aircraft, flown by a qualified crew, is flown into ground, water or an obstacle the crew never saw in time. It happens at night, in cloud, over hilly ground, after a small altitude or track error.

Two heights exist in the cockpit. The barometric altimeter of Unit 3 shows height above a pressure datum and cannot know that a hill stands in the way. The radio altimeter times an echo and reads the height above the surface directly below, over roughly 0 to 2500 ft. That reading is radio altitude, RA.

Every reactive warning below is built from RA plus sink rate, airspeed, gear and flap position, and — in one mode only — the ILS glideslope. RA looks straight down, so the warning is late, typically 10 to 30 s before impact, and blind to a ridge 5 km ahead.

The seven GPWS modes

Classic GPWS is a set of seven modes. Each mode is an envelope: a limit that changes with RA, not a fixed number. Early units compared the envelope with barometric sink rate or the rate of change of RA; digital units added airspeed, gear, flap and ILS deviation.

Mode 1 works on barometric sink rate, gated by RA: a high sink rate is normal at 10 000 ft, so the allowed sink rate falls as RA falls. Crossing it gives the soft "SINK RATE"; a further crossing gives "PULL UP". Mode 2 has separate clean and flap-down envelopes, because an aircraft on final legitimately closes with the ground at 700 ft/min. Mode 3 guards the climb-out with gear still down. Mode 4 calls "TOO LOW GEAR" or "TOO LOW FLAPS" when the aircraft is low with the configuration wrong, then "TOO LOW TERRAIN".

Mode 5 is the only reactive mode that needs a ground aid: it is armed when an ILS is tuned and the aircraft sits more than about 1.3 dots below the beam at low RA. It says the aircraft is low on the approach, nothing about terrain. Mode 6 is a reminder; Mode 7 gives windshear warning from the airspeed and vertical-path signature.

TAWS: looking ahead with a database

A terrain awareness and warning system (TAWS), also sold as enhanced GPWS, keeps those seven modes and adds a forward look. It carries a digital terrain database — a grid of the highest elevation per cell plus known towers — a position from GNSS (5.6) or the flight management system, and a computer that projects the current track, speed and vertical path forward.

t = h ÷ (dh/dt)

t is the time to impact in seconds; h is the height above the terrain ahead in ft; dh/dt is the closing (descent) rate in ft/s. Use climb rate in the same form to check whether the aircraft can still out-climb the slope.

Caution is amber, on the display and as a soft voice, about 60 s before predicted impact: "TERRAIN AHEAD". Warning is red with the hard command "TERRAIN, PULL UP" at about 30 s. Alert times vary with manufacturer, speed and phase of flight.

Inhibits exist because a warning that cries wolf gets switched off. Gear down silences some Mode 1 and Mode 4 alerts; a TERR INHIBIT switch lets the crew quiet nuisance alerts on a steep approach into a terrain-ringed airfield, flying the approach on the chart alone.

  • Database currency: a new tower, cable or building is invisible until the database is updated, and many wires and unlit obstacles are never mapped.
  • Position error: a GNSS outage leaving inertial-only position, a wrong datum, or a bad flight-management position puts the predicted path in the wrong place.
  • It warns; it does not fly. The escape is a maximum-performance climb the pilot must make.

Worked example

Question. An aircraft is at 1800 ft above ground level, descending at 1800 ft/min towards level terrain. TAWS look-ahead is set to alert 30 s before predicted impact. (a) Find the time to impact now. (b) When does the alert come, and at what height? (c) The altimeter sub-scale is set 5 hPa wrong — how much barometric height error does that add?

  1. (a) Time to impact = height ÷ descent rate = 1800 ft ÷ 1800 ft/min = 1.0 min = 60 s.
  2. (b) 60 s is more than the 30 s threshold, so no alert yet. The alert comes after a further 30 s of descent.
  3. Height then = 1800 ft × (30 s ÷ 60 s) = 900 ft above ground level.
  4. (c) Near the surface 1 hPa ≈ 27 ft, so 5 hPa × 27 ft/hPa = 135 ft of barometric height error.
  5. The reactive modes read radio altitude and are unaffected; the look-ahead path uses barometric height, so the 135 ft matters there.

Answer. Time to impact is 60 s, the alert comes 30 s later at 900 ft above ground level, and a 5 hPa sub-scale error is about 135 ft of barometric height error.

Exam traps

  • GPWS is reactive and looks down with the radio altimeter; TAWS look-ahead is predictive and needs a terrain database plus a position. Do not swap the two.
  • Mode 1 (sink rate) uses barometric descent rate gated by RA; Mode 5 (glideslope) needs a tuned ILS and is below-path deviation in dots. Not the same warning.
  • Quote radio altitude, not barometric altitude, as the gating quantity of Modes 1 to 5. A barometric height of 900 ft over high ground may be 300 ft above the hill.
  • Inhibits are normal. A landing configuration or an inhibit switch suppresses some alerts, so "no warning" does not mean "no risk".
  • Barometric sub-scale error: 1 hPa near the surface is about 27 ft, so 5 hPa is about 135 ft.

Analyze An aircraft flies towards a newly built radio mast that is not in its terrain database. Why does neither the look-ahead nor the reactive GPWS give a timely warning? 4 marks

Look-ahead compares the predicted path with stored terrain and obstacle elevations; an unmapped mast has no entry, so it predicts clear ground and stays silent. The reactive modes measure only the height of the surface directly below: a thin mast is a poor reflector and a brief RA change is not a clearance or sink-rate exceedance, so they too stay silent. Unmapped obstacles are the classic blind spot of both systems; only rising ground around the mast would eventually trigger a reactive alert, and then too late.

Analyze Why do crews inhibit some terrain alerts on steep approaches into airfields ringed by high ground? 4 marks

The look-ahead projection assumes a normal climb-out and normal approach geometry. On a steep, curved approach the projected path meets terrain that the aircraft will in fact clear, so amber and red alerts fire repeatedly and would be ignored or disabled entirely. Inhibiting keeps the crew's trust in the system; the cost is that those alerts are gone for that approach.

Apply The altimeter sub-scale is set 5 hPa too low. Estimate the barometric height error and say which TAWS functions it affects. 2 marks

Near the surface 1 hPa ≈ 27 ft, so the error is 5 × 27 = 135 ft. It affects the barometric height used in the look-ahead path and in Mode 1's sink-rate comparison; it does not affect the radio-altimeter gating.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Helicopter versions (HTAWS) use a smaller look-ahead volume and steeper climb assumptions, because a helicopter can stop rather than climb. Synthetic vision shows the same terrain database on a head-down display, and enhanced vision adds an infrared or millimetre-wave image so an unmapped obstacle becomes visible. Database currency is the weak link: a new tower is invisible until the next update. Search next for "helicopter TAWS" and "terrain database obstacle data standard".

Takeaways

  1. Every navigation system turns a measurement into a line of position; two lines of position make a fix, and a third checks it.
  2. A radio aid is either point source (range or bearing to one station) or hyperbolic (difference of ranges to two stations).
  3. ILS splits the job in two: the localizer owns left and right, the glideslope owns up and down, and the pilot flies the needles.
  4. A spinning mass holds its axis in inertial space, and an applied torque moves that axis at 90° to the torque — that is precession, the basis of every gyro instrument.
  5. An INS integrates its own accelerations, so it is smooth and self-contained but its position error grows roughly with the square of time.
  6. GPS measures travel time, not angle; four satellites are needed because the receiver clock offset is a fourth unknown.
  7. GPS error comes from four families — clocks and orbits, ionosphere, troposphere, local effects — and each family has its own cure.
  8. A Kalman filter weights the drifting INS and the noisy GPS by their covariances, giving a solution better than either alone.
  9. Radar gives range from echo travel time and bearing from antenna pointing; ADS-B shares position by broadcast instead of by echo.
  10. TAWS joins a reactive radio-altimeter layer with a look-ahead terrain-database layer to warn the crew early enough to climb.

Exam question bank

Remember and understand

Understand Define a line of position. Show how a measured range to a station and a measured bearing to a station each produce one line of position on a chart, and how a fix is obtained. 10 marks

LOP defined as the locus of constant measurement; Range gives a circle centred on the station with radius equal to the range; Bearing gives a straight line radiating from the station (a great circle on the Earth); Two LOPs intersect at a fix; a third LOP shows the quality of the fix; Cocked hat is drawn around the crossing when errors are present.

Understand Distinguish point-source from hyperbolic radio navigation. Name one operational system of each and state exactly what the airborne receiver measures. 10 marks

Point source: one station, receiver measures range (DME) or bearing (VOR, ADF); Hyperbolic: two stations, receiver measures time difference which equals a constant difference of distances; Constant difference of distances is a hyperbola with the stations as foci; Three or more stations give a family of hyperbolas and hence a fix; Examples: DME and VOR as point source, Loran-C or Decca as hyperbolic; Rho-theta (VOR/DME) mixes one range and one bearing from the same station.

Remember List the ground and airborne components of ILS and state the function of each, giving the frequency band and the localizer and glideslope widths. 12 marks

Localizer in the 108–112 MHz band, 90 Hz and 150 Hz tones, full-scale deflection about 2.5° each side of centreline; Glideslope in the 328.6–335.4 MHz band, path normally 3°, full-scale deflection about 0.7° each side; Marker beacons at 75 MHz marking outer, middle and inner positions; Difference in depth of modulation drives the left-right and up-down needles; Airborne receiver, antenna, and display on the attitude director and HSI; Identification by Morse code; approach minima depend on the ILS category.

Understand Explain the principle of a gyroscope and distinguish precession from nutation, stating where each matters in an aircraft instrument. 10 marks

A spinning mass with angular momentum along its spin axis; axis stays fixed in inertial space; Two degrees of freedom needed for a free gyro; restraints give rate gyros and rate-integrating gyros; Precession: applied torque produces steady rotation of the spin axis at 90° to the torque, ωp = T/(I ωs); Nutation: transient wobble of the axis after a sudden torque, damped out by the gimbal and the spin; Use in attitude indicator, heading indicator, turn coordinator and in inertial sensors; Gimbal lock and drift limits are practical consequences.

Apply

Apply An aircraft flying at 30 000 ft reads a DME slant range of 40 NM to a station directly below it. Find the ground range and comment on the error when the aircraft is nearly overhead. 8 marks

Height in nautical miles: 30 000 / 6076 = 4.94 NM; Ground range = √(40² − 4.94²) = 39.7 NM; Slant range always exceeds ground range; As ground range falls the slant-range error grows, becoming large near overhead; Practical note: DME is not used for accurate overhead distance.

Apply An aircraft is established on a 3° glideslope, 5 NM from the runway threshold. Compute its height above the threshold and state what happens to the path if a 10 kt headwind develops while the needles stay centred. 8 marks

Height = 5 NM × tan 3° = 5 × 0.0524 = 0.262 NM; In feet: 0.262 × 6076 = about 1590 ft; Rule of thumb: about 318 ft per nautical mile; The beam is fixed in space; a headwind changes ground speed and rate of descent, not the geometry; Centring the needles keeps the aircraft on the same straight path.

Apply An accelerometer in an inertial system has a residual bias of 1 mg. Compute the velocity and position error after 100 s of pure inertial operation and explain the shape of the growth. 10 marks

1 mg = 9.81 × 10⁻³ m/s²; Velocity error = a t = 0.981 m/s; Position error = ½ a t² = 49 m; Velocity error grows with t, position error with t²; Sources: bias, scale factor, misalignment, and gravity error from tilt; Cures: factory calibration, temperature compensation, GPS updates.

Apply A satellite clock is in error by 10 ns. Compute the equivalent range error, and explain why a receiver needs four satellites rather than three. 8 marks

Range error = c Δt = 3 × 10⁸ × 10 × 10⁻⁹ = 3 m; Unknowns are x, y, z and the receiver clock offset, so four satellites are needed for four equations; Broadcast clock parameters correct the satellite clock but not the receiver clock; Pseudorange equation ρ = r + c b shows the clock offset entering every measurement; Geometry quality is described by dilution of precision.

Apply A primary surveillance radar receives an echo 100 µs after transmission and works at a pulse repetition frequency of 1 kHz. Find the target range and the maximum unambiguous range. 8 marks

Range = c t / 2 = 3 × 10⁸ × 100 × 10⁻⁶ / 2 = 15 000 m = 15 km; Factor of two because the pulse travels out and back; Maximum unambiguous range = c / (2 × PRF) = 150 km; Beyond that range echoes arrive after the next pulse and appear as second-trace returns; Range resolution depends on pulse width, not on PRF.

Apply A scalar Kalman filter has a predicted position variance P = 100 m² and a GPS measurement variance R = 25 m². Compute the Kalman gain, the updated variance and the weight given to each source. 8 marks

K = P / (P + R) = 100 / 125 = 0.8; Updated variance = (1 − K) P = 0.2 × 100 = 20 m²; Measurement is trusted with weight 0.8, prediction with weight 0.2; The update can never be worse than the better of the two inputs; In the vector case K is a matrix and P is a covariance matrix.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Explain why neither a stand-alone inertial navigation system nor a stand-alone GPS receiver is satisfactory for a long flight, and how loosely coupled GPS-INS integration solves both problems. 14 marks

INS: self-contained, high short-term accuracy, drifts without bound, error grows roughly as t²; GPS: bounded error, but noisy, low update rate, and can be lost or jammed; Fusion: filter estimates INS error states and feeds corrections back; GPS provides the long-term reference, INS bridges between updates and rejects noise; Feedback of corrections keeps the INS error small so the model stays linear; Bridging also gives continuity during a coasting period; describe the covariance behaviour.

Analyze Classify the error sources that limit GPS accuracy and match each one with the correction technique used in practice. 12 marks

Satellite clock and orbit (ephemeris) errors, including selective availability history — corrected by broadcast parameters and by differential corrections; Ionosphere: dispersive, corrected by dual frequency, ionospheric models or SBAS; Troposphere: non-dispersive, corrected by a model using surface pressure, temperature and humidity; Receiver and antenna: multipath, noise, cycle slips, antenna phase centre — corrected by siting, chokering and filtering; Dilution of precision amplifies all of the above by poor satellite geometry; RAIM and augmentation systems check integrity, not just accuracy.

Evaluate Describe the modes of a ground proximity warning system, and argue why the reactive modes had to be supplemented by a look-ahead terrain function. 12 marks

Reactive modes use radio altitude and barometric rate: excessive descent rate, excessive terrain closure, excessive descent after take-off, insufficient terrain clearance, excessive glideslope deviation; Warning envelopes widen as height above ground falls and as speed rises; Reactive warning fires only when the terrain is already under the aircraft; Look-ahead modes compare GPS or FMS position and projected path against a terrain and obstacle database; Database gives alerts tens of seconds earlier, plus a visual terrain display; Limits: database currency, position error, false alerts near steep terrain.

Evaluate Compare a VOR/DME area-navigation fix with an ILS approach, in terms of the lines of position used and the accuracy the operation demands. 12 marks

VOR/DME gives rho-theta: one bearing line and one range circle from the same station; DME alone gives a circle; a second DME gives a circle whose crossing is a fix, with poor geometry near the baseline; ILS gives two planes of constant angle, not range, so accuracy is angular and tightens as the aircraft nears the runway; En-route accuracy is of the order of a nautical mile; ILS has to keep the aircraft within metres of the centreline at the threshold; Both depend on correct receiver tuning, identification and monitoring; Why angular guidance is the right choice for the final approach.

Glossary

Accelerometer
A sensor that measures specific force along one sensitive axis, that is, acceleration minus gravity.
Accuracy
The closeness of a reported position to the true position, expressed in metres or nautical miles.
ADS-B
A surveillance service in which an aircraft broadcasts its identity, position and velocity, derived from its own navigation systems.
Bearing
The horizontal direction from the aircraft to a station, measured clockwise from north in degrees.
Dilution of precision
A factor that amplifies range errors into position errors because of the geometry of the visible satellites.
DME
Distance Measuring Equipment: an interrogator in the aircraft and a transponder on the ground that together give slant range in nautical miles from round-trip time.
ECEF
A Cartesian frame with its origin at the Earth's centre and axes fixed to the rotating Earth.
Ephemeris
The set of orbital parameters broadcast by a navigation satellite so a receiver can compute its position.
Fix
The point where two or more lines of position cross, taken as the aircraft's position.
Gimbal
A pivoted ring that lets an instrument or platform rotate about one axis independently of its mount.
Glideslope
The ILS ground beam that gives up-down guidance along the approach path, normally 3°.
Heading
The direction in which the aircraft nose points, measured clockwise from north in degrees.
Hyperbola
The curve traced by points whose distances to two fixed stations differ by a constant amount.
Kalman filter
A recursive estimator that combines a predicted state with a new measurement, weighting each by its covariance.
Line of position
The curve or straight line on which one measurement is constant, so it contains the true position.
Local NED
A navigation frame at the aircraft with axes pointing north, east and down.
Localizer
The ILS ground beam that gives left-right guidance relative to the runway centreline.
Nutation
A short-lived wobble of a gyroscope's spin axis about its steady direction after a disturbance.
Precession
The steady turning of a spinning axis at right angles to an applied torque, at a rate set by the torque and the spin momentum.
Pseudorange
The apparent satellite-to-receiver range obtained from signal travel time before the receiver clock offset is estimated and removed.
Radio altimeter
A frequency-modulated radar that measures height above the terrain directly beneath the aircraft.
Repeatability
The closeness of repeated readings of the same true value to each other, which can be good even when accuracy is poor.
Schuler tuning
Designing the inertial platform loop to have an 84.4 min period so that horizontal acceleration does not build a growing tilt error.
TAWS
Terrain Awareness and Warning System: equipment that alerts the crew to terrain or obstacle conflict, using radio altitude and a terrain database.
