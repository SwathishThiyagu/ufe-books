3.1 Power generation, distribution and protection

A generator is useful only when its voltage is held steady, its output is switched onto a busbar, and its faults are cleared before the wiring overheats. This chapter follows one generator channel from the engine shaft to the loads: DC generation, AC generation at 115 V 400 Hz from an integrated drive generator or at 360 to 800 Hz variable frequency, and the protection that makes the chain safe.

You need: first-year DC and AC circuit theory — Ohm's law, three-phase power and power factor — and 3.9 shows how the field current is actually controlled.

The DC channel: generator, cut-out, bus, with the regulator as control

Every load draws from a busbar, not directly from a generator. A generator converts engine shaft power into electrical power. Its voltage must stay inside a band because lamps, radios and instruments are designed for that band. One source of 28 V DC is a shunt or compound DC generator. A shunt generator has its field winding across the armature; output voltage rises with speed and field current. A compound generator adds a series field that lifts voltage as load current rises, so the regulator has less work to do.

The power path in a DC channel is: generator → reverse-current cut-out → busbar → loads. The reverse-current cut-out is a contactor that closes when generator voltage is above bus voltage. It opens when current tries to flow from the battery back into the generator. Without it, a stopped generator becomes a motor and drains the battery. This is a DC device, not an AC generator circuit breaker.

The voltage regulator is not in that power path. It is a control block: it senses bus voltage and controls the generator field current. It carries no load current, so it is drawn beside the generator, not between the generator and the busbar. How it senses and controls is in 3.9.

28 V DC is the long-standing low-voltage standard. The aircraft power-quality standard MIL-STD-704 gives the normal steady-state band at the equipment as 22 to 29 V for a 28 V DC system and 108 to 118 V for a 115 V AC system, so "28 V" is a nominal value. 270 V DC appears where high-power DC loads make the lower voltage wasteful; it is not a scaled 28 V system, because higher voltage changes insulation and arc behaviour.

AC generation: constant frequency and variable frequency

An AC generator's frequency follows its speed: f = p × n ÷ 120. Here f is frequency in hertz, p is the number of poles and n is speed in revolutions per minute. A four-pole machine at 12 000 rpm gives 400 Hz. Engine speed is not constant, so a 400 Hz supply needs a constant-speed drive. An integrated drive generator (IDG) puts that drive and an oil-cooled generator in one unit. It produces 115 V AC at 400 Hz. The generator control unit (GCU) is a control block: it regulates voltage by controlling field current and it protects the generator. The generator circuit breaker (GCB) is the switch that connects the generator to the AC bus. A transformer rectifier unit (TRU) then feeds the 28 V DC bus; TRUs are in 3.2.

Variable-frequency (VF) generation avoids the constant-speed drive. The generator runs at engine gearbox speed and its output varies from 360 Hz to 800 Hz. Loads are designed to accept that range or to rectify it first. VF saves weight and maintenance. Newer architectures use 235 V AC VF and 270 V DC; the A320 and B787 comparison is in 3.12.

The AC power path is: generator → GCB → AC bus → loads, with a TRU branch from the AC bus to the DC bus. The GCU sits outside this path. It senses generator voltage, current, frequency and field current, controls the field, and commands the GCB to trip or the drive to disconnect.

Protection at block level

Protection removes a fault before it damages wiring or the generator. The action matters as much as the name.

ProtectionWhat it protectsTypical action
Over-voltageLoads and insulationGCU removes field and trips GCB
Under-voltageLoads from sustained low voltageTime-delayed trip or transfer
Over-currentGenerator winding and feederInverse-time or instantaneous GCB trip
Over-excitationField winding and exciterReduce field; trip if it persists
Under/over-frequencyAC loads and generatorGCU trips GCB outside band
Differential or earth faultStator winding, internal faultTrip GCB and remove field
Drive disconnectIDG or drive jamMechanical disconnect, not an electrical trip

Over-voltage is the most damaging fault: a runaway field can drive a bus far above its band in a fraction of a second, so the trip is fast and inverse-time (the larger the excess, the quicker the trip). Under-voltage trips are delayed, so a short dip while a large motor starts does not drop the generator. A differential fault compares currents at the two ends of a winding; a difference means current is escaping through a fault, and the GCU trips at once. Over-excitation is different: the GCU first reduces field current, because opening the GCB alone does not remove the field. A seized IDG drive is handled by the drive disconnect, a mechanical action.

Worked example

Question. A twin-engine aircraft has two 28 V DC generators, each rated 200 A continuous. Non-essential loads draw 180 A and essential loads draw 60 A. One generator fails. (a) How much load must be shed so the surviving generator is not overloaded? (b) If all non-essential loads are dropped, what load does the surviving generator carry, and what fraction of its rating is that?

  1. Total load before failure = non-essential + essential = 180 A + 60 A = 240 A.
  2. Surviving generator capacity = 200 A.
  3. Minimum load to shed = 240 A − 200 A = 40 A.
  4. Dropping all non-essential load removes 180 A.
  5. Remaining load = 240 A − 180 A = 60 A, which is the essential load.
  6. Fraction of rating = 60 A ÷ 200 A = 0.30 = 30%.

Answer. At least 40 A must be shed; after all non-essential load is dropped the survivor carries 60 A, which is 30% of its 200 A rating.

Worked example 2

Question. A four-pole generator inside an IDG must supply 400 Hz. (a) At what speed must the constant-speed drive turn it? (b) A six-pole variable-frequency generator is geared straight to the engine and turns between 7200 rpm and 16 000 rpm across the flight. What frequency range does it give, and by what factor does its speed change?

  1. (a) From f = p × n ÷ 120, n = 120 × f ÷ p = 120 × 400 ÷ 4 = 12 000 rpm. The drive holds this speed whatever the engine does.
  2. (b) At 7200 rpm: f = 6 × 7200 ÷ 120 = 360 Hz.
  3. At 16 000 rpm: f = 6 × 16 000 ÷ 120 = 800 Hz.
  4. Speed ratio = 16 000 ÷ 7200 = 2.22, and the frequency changes by the same factor: 800 ÷ 360 = 2.22.

Answer. (a) 12 000 rpm. (b) 360 Hz to 800 Hz, a factor of 2.22, which is the variable-frequency band every load on such a bus must accept.

Exam traps

  • The GCB does not open for every fault. Over-excitation is first corrected by reducing field current; a seized IDG drive is cleared by the drive disconnect, a mechanical action.
  • 400 Hz from an IDG is constant frequency because a constant-speed drive holds generator speed. A 360-800 Hz variable-frequency supply is not a faulty 400 Hz supply; the range is its design.
  • Reverse-current cut-out is a DC device that stops the battery driving the generator as a motor. It is not the AC generator circuit breaker.
  • GCU and GCB are different: the GCU senses, regulates and decides; the GCB carries and breaks generator current.
  • Using f = p × n ÷ 120 with pole pairs instead of poles. p counts poles: four poles at 12 000 rpm give 400 Hz, not 800 Hz.

3.2 Inverters, transformer rectifier units and auto-transformers

An aircraft makes alternating current (AC) and direct current (DC) power, but each load needs one exact form. A converter changes AC to DC, DC to AC, or AC to a lower AC voltage. A transformer rectifier unit (TRU) turns 115 V, 400 Hz AC into 28 V DC. An inverter turns DC back into 115 V, 400 Hz AC for standby instruments. An auto-transformer taps one winding to make 26 V AC for lighting and synchro reference. Some syllabi cover this chapter only briefly.

You need: 3.1 for where the AC and DC buses come from.

Inverters and transformer rectifier units

An inverter changes direct current (DC) into alternating current (AC). A static inverter uses an SCR (silicon-controlled rectifier) or transistor bridge to switch the DC input into a stepped AC waveform, then a filter smooths it. Output is 115 V root mean square (rms), 400 Hz (hertz), single-phase or three-phase, for AC instruments, synchros (small AC angle sensors) and the AC standby bus. Frequency comes from the switching control, not from a shaft. On generator loss the battery can drive the inverter through the DC bus; see 3.4.

Older rotary inverters used a DC motor driving an AC generator. Static inverters are lighter, need no brushes and start faster.

A TRU changes 115 V line-to-neutral, 400 Hz AC into 28 V DC. It has a three-phase transformer and a full-wave diode bridge. Ratings are often 100 A to 200 A. The output feeds the DC bus and charges the battery; see 3.3. The losses, Pac − Pdc, appear as heat.

Aircraft AC voltage is quoted line-to-neutral. A 115 V system has VLL = 115 × √3 = 199.2 V, about 200 V. Use VLL = 200 V in the formula below.

Pdc = Vdc × Idc; Pac = Pdc / η; Iline = Pac / (√3 × VLL × cos φ)

Pdc is DC output power in W; Vdc is DC voltage in V; Idc is DC current in A; Pac is AC input power in W; η is efficiency as a decimal; Iline is line current in A; VLL is line-to-line AC voltage in V; for a 115 V aircraft AC system, VLL = 115 × √3 ≈ 200 V; cos φ is power factor.

Auto-transformers and current transformers

An auto-transformer is one winding with a tap. It steps 115 V AC down to 26 V AC for instrument lighting and synchro reference. The voltage ratio is V2 / V1 = N2 / N1, where V is voltage and N is turns. Part of the load current flows straight through the common section, so the winding carries only the difference of the two currents there and the unit is smaller and lighter than a two-winding transformer of the same rating. The price is no isolation: input and output share the winding, so a fault can pass across. Do not use it where isolation is required.

A current transformer (CT) senses load current. The cable carrying the load is the primary; the secondary gives a smaller current in a fixed ratio, for example 100 A to 5 A. A burden resistor turns secondary current into a voltage for indication or protection. The burden is the connected resistance; too large a burden distorts the signal. Never leave the secondary open while current flows, because a high voltage can appear.

Worked example

Question. A transformer rectifier unit is rated 200 A at 28 V DC and is 92 % efficient. It is fed from a 200 V line-to-line (115 V line-to-neutral) three-phase AC bus at unity power factor. Find the DC output power, the AC input power and the line current drawn.

  1. DC output power: Pdc = 28 V × 200 A = 5600 W.
  2. AC input power: Pac = Pdc / η = 5600 / 0.92 = 6086.96 W, about 6090 W.
  3. Line current: Iline = Pac / (√3 × 200 × 1) = 6086.96 / 346.41 = 17.57 A, about 17.6 A.

Answer. The TRU delivers 5600 W, draws about 6090 W from the AC bus, and takes about 17.6 A per line.

Worked example 2

Question. An auto-transformer steps the 115 V AC bus down to 26 V AC for a panel-lighting load of 2 A. Ignoring losses, find (a) the turns ratio, (b) the load power, (c) the current drawn from the 115 V bus and (d) the current in the common section of the winding.

  1. (a) Turns ratio N2 / N1 = V2 / V1 = 26 ÷ 115 = 0.226.
  2. (b) Load power = 26 V × 2 A = 52 W (VA, taking the lamps as resistive).
  3. (c) Input power equals output power, so I1 = 52 ÷ 115 = 0.452 A.
  4. (d) The common section carries the load current minus the input current: 2 − 0.452 = 1.548 A, flowing against the input current in that section.

Answer. Ratio 0.226, 52 W, 0.452 A from the bus and 1.548 A in the common section. A two-winding transformer would carry the full 2 A in its own secondary and 0.452 A in a separate primary, which is why the auto-transformer is lighter.

Exam traps

  • Treating an auto-transformer as an isolation transformer. It shares one winding, so a fault on the 115 V side can reach the 26 V side.
  • Using DC output power as AC input power, or dropping efficiency, when finding TRU line current.
  • Assuming inverter frequency follows battery voltage; the bridge control sets 400 Hz.
  • Leaving a CT secondary open, or choosing too large a burden.
  • Mixing 26 V AC instrument lighting with the 28 V DC bus.

3.3 Aircraft batteries: lead-acid and nickel-cadmium

When every generator is offline and no APU or ground power is connected, the battery is the source left; it carries the essential loads until another source comes on line. A 24 V lead-acid battery has 12 cells of about 2 V. A 24 V nickel-cadmium battery has 19 or 20 cells of about 1.2 V. Newer aircraft also fly lithium-ion batteries. Aircraft use batteries for engine and APU starting, the emergency DC bus and standby instruments. Some syllabi cover the chemistry and testing only briefly; the comparison is examined everywhere.

You need Chapter 3.1 and Chapter 3.2, because the battery sits on the DC bus and is charged through a TRU or dedicated charger.

Lead-acid: 12 cells, sulphuric acid, specific gravity

A lead-acid cell has a lead-dioxide positive plate, a sponge-lead negative plate and dilute sulphuric acid electrolyte. Each cell is about 2.1 V on open circuit and about 2 V on load; twelve in series make the 24 V battery. Specific gravity near 1.28 at full charge shows state of charge, because charging raises acid concentration. Read it after the battery rests or after mixing; water added during topping up lowers the reading until it mixes.

Lead-acid gives strong cranking current and is cheap, but heavy per ampere-hour. Deep discharge damages the plates. Capacity is in ampere-hours (Ah): Ah = I × t, where I is current in amperes and t is time in hours. C-rate = I ÷ rated Ah; a 40 Ah battery at 40 A is 1C, the 1-hour rate. Lead-acid voltage falls steadily during discharge, so the end point is easy to see.

Nickel-cadmium: 19 or 20 cells, flat voltage, thermal risk

A nickel-cadmium (NiCd) cell uses nickel hydroxide positive and cadmium negative plates in potassium hydroxide electrolyte. Sintered plates pack active material into a porous nickel matrix. Each cell gives about 1.2 V, so 20 cells give 24 V and 19 cells about 22.8 V; both are sold as 24 V batteries. Never use a 12-cell lead-acid charger setting on a NiCd battery.

NiCd has low internal resistance, good cold performance and long life. Its voltage stays almost flat until nearly discharged, so a simple voltage reading is a poor state-of-charge check. NiCd is prone to thermal runaway on a constant-voltage charge: as the cells warm, their voltage falls, so the charger pushes more current, which heats them further until electrolyte boils off or a cell vents. Battery temperature sensors and a charger that limits current stop this. NiCd also shows voltage depression, often called memory effect, after repeated shallow cycling; a full discharge and recharge in the battery shop restores most capacity.

Capacity check, charging and lithium-ion

A capacity check discharges the battery at constant current to a stated end voltage (about 1.0 V per NiCd cell), records the ampere-hours removed and compares them with the rated Ah; Worked example 2 does one. Lead-acid charges at constant current, then at constant voltage (float) to avoid overcharge. Nickel-cadmium is charged at constant current, stopped by voltage or temperature cut-off. Never leave a NiCd on a constant-voltage charge, for the reason above. Deep discharge below the end voltage damages both types.

Depth of discharge (DoD) = (Ah removed ÷ rated Ah) × 100 %. In Worked example 1, 20 Ah from a 40 Ah battery is 50 % DoD. The remaining 20 Ah at 20 A lasts 1 hour if rate effects are ignored; at high current the usable capacity is lower.

Lithium-ion cells are about 3.6 to 3.7 V each, so far fewer cells are needed for a 28 V DC system: the B787 main and APU batteries have eight cells in series. Lithium-ion stores more energy per kilogram than lead-acid or NiCd and keeps a flatter voltage, but a cell that is overcharged, over-discharged or damaged can go into thermal runaway and set its neighbours alight. So every lithium battery has a battery management system (BMS) that watches each cell voltage and temperature, balances the cells and cuts charging off. In January 2013 two B787 battery thermal-runaway events grounded the fleet for about three months; the fix added cell insulation, a sealed steel case and a vent line that takes any smoke overboard. Lithium iron phosphate cells, which are harder to drive into runaway, now replace NiCd in some emergency and standby batteries.

Worked example

Question. A 24 V lead-acid battery is rated 40 Ah at the 1-hour rate. It supplies 40 A for 30 minutes. (a) How many ampere-hours are used? (b) What is the depth of discharge? (c) If the load then drops to 20 A, how long will the remaining charge last?

  1. Convert time: 30 minutes = 0.5 hours.
  2. Ah used = I × t = 40 A × 0.5 h = 20 Ah.
  3. DoD = (20 Ah ÷ 40 Ah) × 100% = 50%.
  4. Remaining charge = 40 Ah − 20 Ah = 20 Ah.
  5. Time = 20 Ah ÷ 20 A = 1 h.

Answer. 20 Ah is used, the depth of discharge is 50%, and the remaining charge lasts 1 hour at 20 A, ignoring rate effects.

Worked example 2

Question. A 24 V, 40 Ah nickel-cadmium battery with 20 cells is given a capacity check. It is discharged at the 1-hour rate and reaches the end voltage of 1.0 V per cell after 51 minutes. (a) What is the discharge current and the end voltage of the battery? (b) What capacity did it deliver, as Ah and as a percentage of its rating?

  1. (a) The 1-hour rate of a 40 Ah battery is 40 A (1C).
  2. End voltage = 20 cells × 1.0 V = 20 V.
  3. (b) Time = 51 ÷ 60 = 0.85 h.
  4. Capacity delivered = 40 A × 0.85 h = 34 Ah.
  5. Percentage = 34 ÷ 40 × 100 = 85 %.

Answer. It was discharged at 40 A to 20 V and delivered 34 Ah, 85 % of its rating. The shop compares this with the limit in the battery's maintenance manual; a low result calls for a deep-cycle reconditioning and a second check.

Exam traps

  • Do not assume a 24 V NiCd battery has 12 cells; it has 19 or 20 cells.
  • Do not take a specific-gravity reading just after topping up with water; the acid is diluted until mixing.
  • Do not read 40 Ah as a continuous 40 A rating; it is 40 A for 1 hour at the 1-hour rate.
  • Do not call every NiCd capacity loss memory effect; ageing, dry-out and overcharge also reduce capacity, which is why a timed capacity check is used.
  • Do not use open-circuit voltage alone for NiCd state of charge; its curve is flat.

3.4 Emergency power: ram air turbine and ETOPS

When every main generator is lost, a small air-driven turbine, the ram air turbine (RAT), drops into the slipstream and powers the emergency buses. A permanent magnet generator (PMG) keeps the engine control alive, and the battery bridges the seconds before the RAT is on line. ETOPS rules set how far, in flying time, a twin may be from a suitable airport, and so how long the remaining sources must keep the essential loads running. Some syllabi cover this only briefly; the APU, the usual third source, is part of the same story.

You need 3.1 for main sources and 3.3 for battery capacity and usable charge.

The emergency supply chain

Main generators on the engines supply normal power. When all of them fail, the aircraft still needs flight instruments, one radio, the flight controls and engine control. These essential loads sit on the emergency (essential) AC bus and the essential DC bus, which are kept separate from the main buses so a main-bus fault cannot reach them. The changeover runs in a fixed order, automatically. First, protection opens the failed generators' breakers. At the same moment the essential DC bus falls back to the battery through a contactor, so the standby instruments never go dark. Then the RAT deploys and comes up to speed within a few seconds; its generator feeds the emergency AC bus, and an essential TRU (sized for the essential loads only) takes the essential DC bus back from the battery. Each source covers the gap until the next one is ready.

Ram air turbine, PMG and backup converters

Ram air turbine (RAT): a small two-bladed propeller on a strut is released into the airstream. It drives a generator directly, or drives a hydraulic pump whose fluid turns a hydraulic motor and generator; the A320 does it the second way, with an emergency generator of about 5 kVA. Deployment is automatic after loss of all AC power, or manual. The RAT works inside an airspeed band: below it the turbine turns too slowly to carry the load, so the crew keeps speed up; a governor on the blade pitch holds its speed against overspeed. It feeds the emergency AC bus, not the main buses.

Permanent magnet generator (PMG): a magnet rotor spins inside a fixed stator winding. There is no field winding, so its output cannot be switched off by removing field current; only stopping the shaft stops it. On an engine it supplies the electronic engine control (FADEC) with its own power, independent of the aircraft buses; the engine igniters are usually fed from the aircraft AC bus. Its frequency rises with shaft speed, so it is rectified inside the control unit. Backup converters let the battery feed the essential DC bus, and a static inverter makes AC for the AC standby bus from it.

ETOPS and its electrical consequence

ETOPS means Extended-range Twin-engine Operational Performance Standards. It sets the longest time a twin-engine aircraft may be, at its one-engine-inoperative cruise speed in still air, from a suitable alternate airport. Common approvals are 120 and 180 minutes, with longer ones for some types. The rule is about time, not distance: at 420 kt (knots), 120 minutes is 840 nautical miles (nm) and 180 minutes is 1260 nm. For the electrical system, ETOPS asks whether the remaining sources can carry the essential loads for the whole diversion after a further failure. The battery cannot do this alone; it covers only the gap until the APU generator or the RAT is on line. The time a battery lasts is t = Qusable ÷ I, where t is in hours, Qusable is the usable charge in ampere-hours and I is the current in amperes. Usable charge is less than rated capacity (see aircraft batteries).

The APU as a third source

The auxiliary power unit (APU) is a small gas turbine, usually in the tail cone, that drives its own generator. On the A320 that generator is rated 90 kVA, the same as one engine IDG, so it can replace a lost engine generator completely; on the B787 two APU starter-generators of 225 kVA each do the same job. The APU gives power on the ground before engine start and can be started in flight. On many ETOPS twins the APU generator is the third independent AC source, so ETOPS approval also checks that the APU starts reliably at altitude after a long cold soak, or the APU is run for the whole extended part of the flight. The APU is started from the battery (or, on the B787, from its own lithium-ion APU battery).

Worked example

Question. An aircraft is cleared for ETOPS 120, so it must stay within 120 minutes of a suitable alternate; at 420 kt that is 840 nm. Its essential DC bus draws 2.8 kW at 28 V. The battery is rated 40 Ah and only 50 % may be used. Find the bus current, usable charge and battery time, and say what this means for the diversion.

3.5 Internal and external lighting systems

An aircraft carries dozens of lamps, grouped by who uses them: the crew in the cockpit, the people in the cabin, and the outside world that must see the aircraft. Each group gets its own supply, its own switch and its own failure rule. Navigation lights tell another aircraft which way this one points; egress lights must work when everything else is dead. This chapter places each light, names its supply and control, and checks that an emergency pack has enough charge for its lamps.

You need: 3.1 for busbars and essential buses, and 3.3 for battery capacity ratings.

Three groups, three sets of rules

Every lamp belongs to one of three groups, decided by who needs it. The crew must read instruments and charts without losing night vision. Passengers need general light, a reading light and a clear path to the door. The outside world — another aircraft, a marshaller — must see where this aircraft is and which way it points.

The group decides the supply. Most lamps run on 28 V DC from a DC bus; some large cabin fittings and landing lights use AC. Emergency egress lights are the exception: they are fed from self-contained battery packs charged from an essential bus, so they still burn when every generator and every main bus is dead.

A rheostat dims a lamp by putting resistance in series with it. That wastes power as heat and shifts the colour. Pulse-width control switches the lamp fully on and off at high frequency and varies the on-time fraction; the eye sees only the average, and very little power is lost.

I = P ÷ V

I is the lamp current in amperes (A), P is the lamp power in watts (W) and V is the supply voltage in volts (V); lamps in parallel add their powers first. This holds for DC and for resistive AC lamps; for other AC loads divide by V × cos φ, where cos φ is the power factor.

Cockpit and cabin lighting

  • Flood lights: white lamps above and behind the crew, washing the whole panel; run dim at night.
  • Integral panel lights: built into panel and pedestal, back-lit through engraved legends so every label reads without glare.
  • Chart light: a focused swivel lamp over the chart table.
  • Dome light: general white light in the ceiling, for finding things rather than reading instruments.
  • Standby light: a small lamp on its own battery, so the crew is never in total darkness after a bus failure.

The cabin has ceiling and sidewall lights along the tube, a reading light in each passenger service unit, galley and lavatory lights, and illuminated signs. One cabin lighting control panel at the attendant station switches groups and sets brightness. Cabin lights are split across buses, so one failed bus darkens part of the tube, never all of it.

Emergency egress lighting and its packs

  • Exit signs at every door, lit red or white and marked EXIT.
  • Floor-proximity lights: a strip on or near the floor along the aisle, leading to each exit, so a person crawling under smoke can follow it.
  • External escape lights that illuminate the slide or overwing area once the door is open.
  • The emergency mode of the cockpit standby light.

The source is a set of self-contained battery packs spread through the cabin, each feeding a group of lamps. Each pack holds sealed cells, a charger, a changeover relay and the feeds to its lamps. In flight the pack floats on an essential bus, which keeps the cells charged, and an arming switch in the cockpit puts the system in standby. When the essential bus loses power, or the crew selects emergency, the relay drops and the pack feeds the lamps. Transport-aircraft rules ask for at least 10 minutes of emergency lighting, enough to evacuate. A pack is rated by both capacity in ampere-hours (Ah) and maximum discharge current in amperes (A); both limits must be checked against the connected lamps.

External lights

  • Navigation (position) lights: red on the left wingtip (port), green on the right wingtip (starboard), white at the tail. Red and green together mean the aircraft is coming towards you; red alone means its left side is in view. Each colour shows through a defined arc, about 110° for red and green and about 140° for white, so the lamps sit in shaped housings.
  • Anti-collision lights: a red beacon, rotating or flashing, on top and bottom of the fuselage, plus white strobes at the wingtips giving a short, very bright double flash. They go on before engine start.
  • Landing lights: high-intensity lamps in the wing root or on the nose gear, aimed forward. Taxi lights are wider and less intense.
  • Logo lights on the horizontal stabiliser light up the tail; wing or engine scan lights let the crew inspect leading edges for ice at night.

Landing lights are the largest lighting loads: a 600 W lamp on a 28 V supply draws 600 ÷ 28 = 21.4 A. Strobe tubes are bright enough to dazzle close-up, so they are often inhibited on the ground, which is control logic rather than a lamp job.

Worked example

Question. A cabin emergency lighting pack is rated 24 V, 4 Ah, with a maximum discharge current of 6 A. It must run 12 exit-sign lamps at 6 W each and 20 floor-proximity lights at 3 W each for 12 minutes. Find the total load power, the load current, the charge and energy taken, and the fraction of the pack rating used. Does the pack hold?

  1. Exit-sign load: P1 = 12 × 6 W = 72 W.
  2. Floor-proximity load: P2 = 20 × 3 W = 60 W.
  3. Total load power: P = 72 W + 60 W = 132 W.
  4. Load current: I = P ÷ V = 132 ÷ 24 = 5.5 A. This is below the 6 A maximum, so the current is within rating.
  5. Discharge time t = 12 minutes = 12 ÷ 60 h = 0.2 h.
  6. Charge taken: Q = I × t = 5.5 A × 0.2 h = 1.1 Ah. Energy taken: E = Q × V = 1.1 Ah × 24 V = 26.4 Wh.
  7. Fraction of rating: 1.1 ÷ 4 = 0.275, that is 27.5 %.
  8. Endurance at full load: 4 Ah ÷ 5.5 A = 0.727 h = 43.6 min, well over the 12 minutes needed. This is an upper bound, because usable capacity falls at high current, but even half of it would still exceed 12 minutes.

Answer. The pack holds: load current is 5.5 A against a 6 A limit, and 1.1 Ah of its 4 Ah capacity (27.5 %) is used in the 12 minutes, delivering 26.4 Wh of energy.

Exam traps

  • Swapping the navigation lights. Red is on the left (port) wingtip, green on the right (starboard), white at the tail. This is the commonest marking error.
  • Putting egress lights on a normal AC bus. They must work when every generator and main bus is lost, so they run from dedicated packs charged from an essential bus.
  • Confusing ampere-hours with amperes. A 4 Ah pack is not limited to 4 A; check capacity and maximum discharge current separately.
  • Forgetting the hour conversion. Twelve minutes is 0.2 h; a missing ÷60 gives an answer 60 times too large.
  • Treating a beacon and a strobe as the same lamp. The beacon is a rotating or flashing red anti-collision light; the strobe is a bright white double-flash tube at the wingtip.

3.6 Packaging, cooling and EMI/EMC requirements

An avionics box must work in a hot, vibrating bay full of electrical noise. Packaging fixes its shape and connectors so a line replaceable unit (LRU) can be swapped on the ramp. Cooling carries its heat to air or liquid. Electromagnetic interference and compatibility (EMI/EMC) rules limit what the box emits and what it must tolerate. Some syllabi leave this chapter out; the same ideas apply to every avionics box on every aircraft.

You need first-year heat transfer (thermal resistance); no earlier chapter is required, though 3.1 shows where the supply comes from.

Packaging: LRU, rack and connector

An LRU is a box that can be removed and replaced as one unit without opening it. It slides into a rack or a front-loading tray. Hold-down clamps lock it against vibration. Blind connectors at the rear mate when the tray is pushed home, so no hand tools are needed on the ramp.

Civil cases follow ARINC 404, whose air transport rack (ATR) sizes run from ¼ ATR to 1 ATR, or the newer ARINC 600, whose sizes are counted in modular concept units (MCU). These standards fix case dimensions, connector positions and the cooling-air interface, so boxes from different makers fit the same rack. Military (DOD) types use similar ideas with harsher environmental limits. Weight and volume matter because every kilogram costs fuel; access matters because a faulty box must come out in minutes. The connector carries power and signals. Keying prevents a wrong box from entering a tray. The case also provides the shield and the heat path, so its metalwork is part of both the electrical and thermal design.

Cooling: junction to air

Every watt lost as heat raises temperature. Natural convection suits small boxes. Larger bays use forced air, a cold plate, or a liquid loop. A cold plate is a metal plate with internal passages; the LRU mounts flat on it and heat flows by conduction. Liquid loops carry heat to a fuel-cooled or air-cooled heat exchanger. Cold-plate mounting needs a flat, clamped surface; a poor contact adds resistance.

Thermal resistance Rth is the temperature rise per watt, in kelvin per watt (K/W). For one path:

Tcase = Tair + P × Rth

Tcase is case temperature in °C; Tair is cooling-air inlet temperature in °C; P is heat in W; Rth is case-to-air resistance in K/W.

Inside the box, each hot component has its own junction-to-case resistance Rjc: Tj = Tcase + Pdevice × Rjc, where Pdevice is the heat of that one component, not of the whole LRU. The real limit is the hottest semiconductor junction, not the box surface. Less airflow raises Rth; in forced-air cooling halving the flow can come close to doubling it, so the same power gives a hotter case. Worked example 2 follows the heat from the case to a chip.

EMI/EMC: emissions and susceptibility

EMI is unwanted electromagnetic energy, carried as current along a wire or as a field through space, that disturbs equipment. EMC means the box works in that environment without disturbing others. Conducted emission travels along wires; radiated emission travels through space. Susceptibility is how easily the box is upset, by conducted or radiated energy.

Four controls work together. Suppress at the source with filters and ferrites. Route signals as screened twisted pairs and keep power cables away from signal cables. Screened means a conductive layer around the pair; twisted means the two wires swap positions along the run, so pickup cancels. Filter at the connector with feed-through capacitors. Shield the enclosure with conductive gaskets, then bond it to the airframe with a low-impedance path. Bonding is a direct metal connection; grounding is the reference return.

RTCA DO-160 covers environmental and EMI tests; MIL-STD-461 covers military EMI. The box must pass both emission and susceptibility limits. A shield with a poor bond is an antenna.

Worked example

Question. An LRU dissipates 120 W. Its cold plate has a thermal resistance of 0.15 K/W to the cooling air, and the inlet air is at 30 °C. (a) Find the case temperature. (b) The airflow is halved and the resistance doubles; the case limit is 70 °C. Is there still margin?

  1. Given P = 120 W, Rth = 0.15 K/W, Tair = 30 °C.
  2. Temperature rise = P × Rth = 120 × 0.15 = 18 K.
  3. Tcase = 30 + 18 = 48 °C.
  4. New Rth = 2 × 0.15 = 0.30 K/W; rise = 120 × 0.30 = 36 K.
  5. New Tcase = 30 + 36 = 66 °C. Margin = 70 − 66 = 4 K.

Answer. The case temperature is 48 °C; with halved airflow it is 66 °C, leaving 4 °C of margin.

Worked example 2

Question. In the 66 °C case of Worked example 1(b), a power transistor dissipates 5 W and has a junction-to-case resistance of 2.0 K/W. Its junction limit is 125 °C, and the designer wants at least 40 K of margin. Is the design acceptable?

  1. Use the device's own heat: Pdevice = 5 W (not the 120 W of the whole LRU).
  2. Rise from case to junction = 5 × 2.0 = 10 K.
  3. Tj = 66 + 10 = 76 °C.
  4. Margin = 125 − 76 = 49 K, which is more than the 40 K wanted.
  5. At full airflow the case is 48 °C, so Tj = 48 + 10 = 58 °C and the margin is 67 K.

Answer. Yes: the junction reaches 76 °C with halved airflow, a margin of 49 K; at full airflow it is 58 °C.

Exam traps

  • Bench pass does not guarantee bay pass: reduced airflow raises Rth and the case temperature.
  • Conducted emission leaves through wires; radiated emission leaves through air. Do not swap the words.
  • Temperature rise is P × Rth, not Rth ÷ P; use W and K/W to get K.
  • Shielding alone is not EMC; a poor bond, unfiltered connector or badly routed power cable still fails.
  • Using the whole LRU power with Rjc. The junction rise uses the heat of that one device.

3.7 Air data instruments: airspeed indicator and altimeter

An aircraft knows its speed and height by measuring pressure. A pitot tube faces the airflow and senses total pressure; a static port on the side senses the surrounding pressure. Their difference drives the airspeed indicator, while static pressure alone drives the altimeter. Both are simple mechanical computers, but their readings depend on calibration and on the pressure setting the pilot chooses, so reading them correctly is a core cockpit skill on every flight.

You need basic pressure and density ideas from physics; no earlier chapter is needed.

Pitot-static ports and the airspeed indicator

A pitot tube points into the relative airflow. Its open end brings air to rest and senses total pressure P0. A static port sits flush on the fuselage side and senses the surrounding static pressure P. The difference is impact pressure q:

q = P0 − P = ½ ρ V²

q is impact pressure in Pa; P0 is total pressure in Pa; P is static pressure in Pa; ρ is air density in kg/m³; V is the speed for which that impact pressure is produced at density ρ.

q = ½ρV² holds for incompressible flow, roughly below Mach 0.3 (about 200 kt at sea level); at higher speed the airspeed indicator needs a compressibility correction.

In the airspeed indicator, a thin capsule feels P0 on one side and P on the other. It expands with q, and a linkage moves the needle. The dial is calibrated at sea-level ISA density, ρ0 = 1.225 kg/m³. Rearranging q = ½ρV² gives V = √(2q/ρ). Because the dial is calibrated at ρ0, a given q always shows the same indicated speed. In thin air ρ is smaller, so the same q is produced by a higher true speed: TAS is greater than IAS, and the gap grows with altitude.

The dial shows indicated airspeed (IAS). Calibrated airspeed (CAS) is IAS corrected for position and installation error. True airspeed (TAS) is CAS corrected for air density, and at high Mach number also for compressibility. The coloured arcs are IAS limits: white for flap operation, green for normal, yellow for caution, and the red radial for never-exceed speed Vne.

Altimeter and pressure altitude

The altimeter uses a sealed aneroid capsule with a near vacuum inside. As static pressure falls, the capsule expands; as pressure rises, it compresses. A linkage turns the pointer. The static port is its only pressure input, so a blocked static line freezes the reading.

The subscale knob sets the reference pressure in the small window. Set QNH, the aerodrome pressure reduced to sea level, and on the ground the altimeter reads aerodrome elevation. Set 1013.25 hPa and it reads pressure altitude. Near sea level, h ≈ (Pref − P) × 27 ft/hPa; Pref is the set pressure, P is outside static pressure and h is height.

Pressure altitude is not true altitude. ISA pressure falls about 1 hPa per 27 ft near sea level and temperature falls 6.5 °C/km. In air colder than ISA, pressure levels are compressed and the aircraft is lower than the altimeter says. Flying from high pressure to low pressure also makes the altimeter overread.

Errors and blockage

No pressure instrument is perfect. Position error comes from airflow around the ports and changes with angle of attack and flap. Leaks make readings sluggish or wrong. Lag delays the response. Temperature error matters most for the altimeter. Blockage is the dangerous one.

  • Pitot blocked (and its drain blocked), static clear: in a climb the trapped P0 stays fixed while P falls, so q rises and the ASI overreads; in a descent it underreads. The ASI now behaves like an altimeter.
  • Static blocked, pitot clear: the altimeter freezes at the height of the blockage and the VSI reads zero. The ASI underreads in a climb (the trapped static pressure is too high) and overreads in a descent. Many aircraft have an alternate static source for this.
  • Pitot heat is the cure for ice in the pitot tube; it does not clear a blocked static port, so check the static drains.
  • Before flight, remove covers, check ports and drain holes, and cross-check the ASI at the runway.

Worked example

Question. (a) At sea level, where air density is 1.225 kg/m³, an aircraft has an impact pressure of 2500 Pa. Find the indicated airspeed in m/s and in knots. Use 1 m/s = 1.94384 kt. (b) The altimeter subscale is set to 1013.25 hPa and the static pressure outside is 950 hPa. Find the pressure altitude, using 27 ft per hPa.

  1. (a) Use q = ½ρV², so V = √(2q/ρ).
  2. 2q = 2 × 2500 = 5000 Pa.
  3. 2q/ρ = 5000 / 1.225 = 4081.63 m²/s².
  4. V = √4081.63 = 63.89 m/s ≈ 63.9 m/s.
  5. In knots: 63.89 × 1.94384 = 124.2 kt.
  6. (b) Pressure difference = 1013.25 − 950 = 63.25 hPa.
  7. Height = 63.25 × 27 = 1707.75 ft ≈ 1707.8 ft, about 1710 ft.

Answer. The airspeed is about 63.9 m/s (124 kt), and the pressure altitude is about 1708 ft (roughly 1710 ft).

Exam traps

  • Mixing up the ports: the pitot port feeds only the airspeed capsule, while the static port feeds the airspeed capsule, altimeter and vertical speed indicator.
  • Reading indicated airspeed as true airspeed. At altitude, the same IAS corresponds to a higher TAS because the air density is lower.
  • Forgetting that the altimeter reads height above the pressure level set in its subscale, not height above the ground or above sea level unless QNH is set correctly.
  • Thinking a blocked pitot always makes the ASI read zero. With static pressure still changing, a blocked pitot can make the ASI climb when the aircraft climbs.
  • Using 1013.25 hPa as QNH for landing. It gives pressure altitude; QNH must come from the aerodrome pressure report.

3.8 Engine instruments: tachometer and EGT

An engine instrument answers two questions from the cockpit: how fast is the engine turning, and how hot is its exhaust? A tachometer turns shaft speed into a voltage or a pulse frequency. An exhaust gas temperature (EGT) system turns exhaust heat into a small thermocouple voltage. Both signals are conditioned and shown as a number or a needle. Pilots use them at start, take-off and cruise to stay inside engine limits, and maintenance uses them to spot drift before a fault becomes a failure.

You need basic electrical measurement: voltage, current, frequency, and the millivolt as a small voltage; see 3.1 for aircraft supply and 3.13 for the other engine indicators.

Tachometer: speed becomes a signal

Engine speed is measured in revolutions per minute (rpm), but the cockpit usually shows a percentage. N1 is the speed of the fan or low-pressure spool. N2 is the speed of the core or high-pressure spool; a three-spool engine also shows N3. 100 % is a reference speed picked by the engine maker; it is not the same rpm on every engine.

A mechanical tachometer takes a flexible drive shaft from the engine gearbox to the indicator. In a drag-cup (eddy-current) indicator the shaft spins a magnet inside a light metal cup; eddy currents drag the cup round against a hairspring, and the cup turns the pointer in proportion to speed. This needs no electrical power, but the long shaft adds friction, lag and a path for oil leaks, so it is found only on light aircraft.

A DC tacho-generator is a small generator driven by the engine shaft. Its output voltage rises with speed: Vtacho = k × rpm. k is the generator constant in volts per rpm. The voltage is easy to measure, but brushes and commutator wear.

A magnetic or variable-reluctance pickup is better for modern engines. A coil sits close to a toothed wheel on the shaft. Each tooth changes the magnetic reluctance and induces one pulse. The indicator counts frequency, so there is no contact and no brush wear.

f = (rpm ÷ 60) × N

f is pulse frequency in hertz (pulses per second); rpm is shaft speed in revolutions per minute; N is the number of teeth on the wheel.

Exhaust gas temperature: heat becomes millivolts

An exhaust gas temperature (EGT) system uses a thermocouple. Two wires of different metals are joined at the hot junction, which sits in the exhaust. The other ends form the cold junction at the indicator or in a compensating circuit. A temperature difference between the junctions produces a small emf, called the Seebeck voltage. Type K uses chromel and alumel and has a sensitivity S of about 41 µV/°C over the working range (it varies a little with temperature). Several probes round the exhaust are wired in parallel, so the indicator shows their average.

VTC = S × (Thot − Tcold)

VTC is thermocouple voltage (µV); S is sensitivity (about 41 µV/°C for type K); Thot is hot-junction temperature (°C); Tcold is cold-junction temperature (°C).

The equation shows why the cold junction cannot be ignored: the sensor reports a difference, not an absolute temperature. Cold-junction compensation adds a voltage equal to S × Tcold (with Tcold measured from 0 °C), or holds the cold junction at a known temperature. The compensated signal goes to a high-gain amplifier, then to an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) or a moving-coil meter. An amplifier with gain G gives Vout = G × VTC.

The display shows EGT. Some engines call the same turbine measurement TGT, turbine gas temperature. Piston engines often show cylinder head temperature (CHT), which is a different place and a different limit.

Reading limits and cross-checks

Three readings matter. At start, EGT rises to a peak and then falls as the engine stabilises. A hot start crosses the start limit; the crew shuts down. At take-off, EGT and N1/N2 are read together. On a hot day the EGT limit may be reached before the thrust target, so the limit sets take-off power. In cruise, a slow EGT rise at the same N1, compared at the same outside temperature, altitude and bleed setting, can show a dirty compressor, worn turbine or a drifting sensor.

Do not mix N1 and N2. N1 is the fan or LP spool; N2 is the core or HP spool. Their 100% references are different speeds. A reading of 90% N2 is not 90% of N1, and it is not 90% of any universal rpm. To turn percent into rpm, use the reference speed in the engine manual.

The tachometer and EGT chains are separate until the display. A failed pickup usually gives no pulse or a noisy frequency; a failed thermocouple gives an open circuit or a fixed low reading. The cockpit may show both on one engine display, but the sensors, wires and conditioning are separate. Other engine indications are covered in 3.13; digital engine displays belong to avionics architecture and data buses.

3.9 Voltage regulators: vibrating-contact, carbon-pile and electronic

A generator's output voltage is not fixed. It rises as the shaft speeds up and falls as the load current rises. The cause is the field: generated emf depends on field flux and speed. A voltage regulator senses the output, compares it with a reference, and adjusts field current to hold voltage steady. Aircraft use three hardware forms: the vibrating-contact regulator, the carbon-pile regulator, and the electronic regulator. The first two are older; the electronic type is standard in generator control units.

You need: 3.1 for the generator, its field winding and the generator control unit.

The field is the control knob

Generated emf in a DC generator is E = k Φ ω. E is the generated emf in volts, k is a machine constant, Φ is the field flux per pole in webers, and ω is angular speed in radians per second. For a given field current, Φ is roughly constant. Then E rises in direct proportion to speed. At the terminals the voltage is lower than E by the armature resistance drop and by armature reaction. So terminal voltage falls as load current rises.

The regulator cannot change speed. It changes field current If. A small change in If gives a large change in Φ, and so in E. The loop is closed: sense terminal voltage, compare with a reference, drive the field. The quality measure is voltage regulation.

regulation = (Vno-load − Vfull-load) ÷ Vfull-load × 100

Vno-load is the terminal voltage at zero load in volts; Vfull-load is the terminal voltage at rated load in volts; the result is a percentage. Smaller is better.

Vibrating-contact and carbon-pile regulators

Vibrating-contact regulator. A solenoid coil is connected across the generator output. A spring holds a contact closed. While the contact is closed, full field current flows. When output voltage exceeds the setting, the solenoid pulls the contact open. A series resistance then enters the field circuit, so field current falls and voltage falls. The contact closes again. The cycle repeats many times a second, and the spring tension sets the regulated voltage. The average field current is set by the duty cycle D, the fraction of time the contact is closed. On light-aircraft DC generators it comes as a three-unit regulator: the voltage regulator, a current limiter (a second vibrating contact worked by a series coil, which caps the generator current) and a reverse-current cut-out. Its regulation is coarse, the contact arcs, so it wears and makes radio noise, and it needs periodic cleaning and adjustment.

Carbon-pile regulator. A stack of carbon discs sits between two plates. Its resistance falls as pressure rises. A solenoid works against a spring to vary the pressure, and the pile is in series with the field. Higher voltage pulls the solenoid, reduces pressure, raises pile resistance, and lowers field current. The control is smooth and has no arcing contacts, but the discs still wear slowly, shed carbon dust and drift with age and heat, so it needs periodic adjustment. The pile is also slow to respond. It was used on larger DC generators where smooth control mattered more than speed.

Electronic regulators and comparison

An electronic regulator uses a Zener diode as the reference. A sensing divider scales the output down. An error amplifier compares the scaled output with the Zener voltage. Its output drives a transistor, a thyristor, or a pulse-width modulator that switches the field. Pulse-width control gives a duty cycle D, and the average field current is D × Ifield(max). The loop is fast, has no moving parts and regulates most closely of the three. The same unit adds over-voltage and under-voltage cut-out, over-excitation limiting, and reverse-current protection. Cut-out removes the generator from the bus; it does not regulate voltage.

Worked example

Question. A 28 V DC generator has a field resistance of 10 Ω. The regulator inserts a 20 Ω series resistance when its contact opens. The average field current needed is 1.5 A. (a) Find the duty cycle D, the fraction of time the contact is closed. (b) With no load the output is 28.4 V and at full load 27.6 V; find the voltage regulation.

  1. Contact closed: field current Iclosed = 28 V ÷ 10 Ω = 2.80 A.
  2. Contact open: total field circuit resistance = 10 Ω + 20 Ω = 30 Ω. Iopen = 28 V ÷ 30 Ω = 0.933 A.
  3. Average field current = D Iclosed + (1 − D) Iopen. Put 1.50 A = D(2.80 A) + (1 − D)(0.933 A).
  4. Expand: 1.50 = 2.80D + 0.933 − 0.933D = 0.933 + 1.867D. Then D = (1.50 − 0.933) ÷ 1.867 = 0.304.
  5. Duty cycle = 0.304 × 100 = 30.4 %. This takes the field current as jumping between 2.80 A and 0.933 A; the field is inductive, so the real current rises and decays along curves and the true duty cycle differs a little, but the average-current method is what the exam expects.
  6. Regulation = (28.4 V − 27.6 V) ÷ 27.6 V × 100 = 0.8 ÷ 27.6 × 100 = 2.90 %.

Answer. Duty cycle is 30.4 % and voltage regulation is 2.9 %.

3.10 Wires, cables and circuit protection: fuses, current limiters and circuit breakers

Every wire is a resistor with a current limit. It carries its load current with a small voltage drop, and it survives a fault current only for a short time before insulation or copper fails. Protection devices—fuses, current limiters, limiting resistors and circuit breakers—break or limit that fault current before the wire is damaged. The device is chosen from the wire's derated rating, not from the load alone. This rule sets feeder sizes, breaker panels and reset actions on every aircraft electrical bus.

You need: 3.1 for the busbar and feeder arrangement that connects sources to loads.

Wire construction, gauge and derating

Aircraft wire uses stranded copper, not solid copper, because vibration and repeated bending crack a solid conductor. Strands are often silver plated for soldering and corrosion resistance, or nickel plated for hot engine and galley areas. Insulation is chosen by temperature and fire behaviour: PVC for cool protected areas, PTFE for high temperature, Kapton for thin lightweight harnesses, and glass braid where abrasion is severe. The gauge is given in AWG (American Wire Gauge). A lower AWG number means a thicker conductor and a higher current rating; 8 AWG is much thicker than 20 AWG.

The bare current rating of a wire is measured in free air. In an aircraft it must be derated for three effects: bundle size (many wires trapped in one loom cannot shed heat), altitude (thin air cools less), and ambient temperature (a hot bay adds heat). A wire that is safe at 60 A alone may be limited to 40 A in a dense hot bundle. Voltage drop is a second limit. Feeder drop is usually held to a few percent of bus voltage, so long runs need thicker wire even when heating is acceptable. The drop for a DC feeder with a return path is Vdrop = I × Rtotal and Rtotal = 2 × L × r. Here I is load current in A, L is one-way length in m, r is conductor resistance in Ω/m, and the factor 2 counts the return wire.

Wires are marked with circuit identity, clamped at fixed intervals, and bent no tighter than the stated bend radius. Sharp bends crush insulation and strand the copper.

Fuses, current limiters and breakers

A fuse is a one-shot device: a metal element melts when its inverse time-current curve says the current is too high for too long. At slight overload it takes minutes; at a short-circuit it takes milliseconds. A current limiter is a heavy-current fast-acting fuse used on a generator or main feeder. It clears a large fault before the downstream busbars see the full current. A limiting resistor is a resistor in series with a circuit to hold fault current or switch-on inrush current down. It does not break the circuit; it buys time for the breaker or fuse.

A circuit breaker can be reset. The thermal type uses a bimetallic strip heated by load current; the magnetic type uses a solenoid that pulls open on a high current; the thermal-magnetic type combines both, giving a delay on overload and fast action on short circuit. A trip-free breaker cannot be held closed on a fault: the handle moves but the contacts stay open until the mechanism resets. Remote-controlled breakers let the crew open and close a feeder from the cockpit.

Breakers are preferred in flight because a crew can reset one after a transient. That advantage is also a danger: repeated resetting of a thermal breaker into a real fault overheats the wire. A fuse is used where a one-shot clearance is acceptable and resetting is not wanted.

Coordination and selection

Protection is a race between two curves. The wire damage curve gives the time a conductor can carry a given current before its insulation or copper is damaged. The device curve gives the time the fuse or breaker takes to open at that current. At every fault current the device must open sooner than the wire is damaged, so on a time-current graph the device curve lies below the wire curve. At the normal load current the device must not open at all, so its curve rises steeply near its rating. The figure shows a 20 A breaker on a wire rated 25 A: at 40 A the breaker clears in 5.0 s while the wire would survive about 31 s; at 80 A the breaker clears in 1.0 s and the wire limit is about 5.2 s.

Selection starts with the load current, then checks the wire's derated rating, then the voltage drop. The breaker rating must not exceed the derated wire rating. If a 40 A load needs a 50 A breaker, the wire must have a derated rating of at least 50 A. A 50 A breaker on a wire rated 40 A protects the load but not the wire.

The fault path matters. A short at the end of a long feeder has enough resistance to reduce fault current, so the breaker may trip slowly. The return path, connectors and contactors add resistance. Coordination also covers the upstream device: the downstream breaker should clear a local fault before the generator current limiter opens. Busbar layouts and bus tie logic belong to 3.11; generator feeder settings belong to 3.1.

Worked example

Question. A 28 V DC feeder carries 40 A for 6 m from the bus to a load. The conductor is 8 AWG copper with a resistance of 2.06 mΩ per metre, and the return path doubles the length. (a) Find the voltage drop as a percentage of 28 V. (b) Find the power lost in the cable. (c) Suggest a breaker rating, stating the wire condition.

  1. One-way length L = 6 m. The return path makes total conductor length Ltotal = 2 × 6 = 12 m.
  2. Resistance per metre r = 2.06 mΩ/m = 0.00206 Ω/m. Total R = r × Ltotal = 0.00206 × 12 = 0.02472 Ω.
  3. Voltage drop Vdrop = I × R = 40 × 0.02472 = 0.9888 V, about 0.99 V.
  4. Percentage drop = (0.9888 ÷ 28) × 100 = 3.53 %. This is above a 2 % feeder limit, so 8 AWG is marginal for this 6 m, 40 A run. With 6 AWG (1.30 mΩ/m) the drop would be 40 × 12 × 0.00130 = 0.62 V, 2.2 %; with 4 AWG (0.815 mΩ/m) it is 0.39 V, 1.4 %.
  5. Power lost P = I² × R = 40² × 0.02472 = 1600 × 0.02472 = 39.552 W, about 39.6 W.
  6. Load current is 40 A, so a 50 A breaker is a practical next standard rating, provided the derated wire rating is at least 50 A. If the derated wire rating is only 40 A, the breaker must be 40 A or the wire made thicker.

Answer. Voltage drop 0.99 V (3.53 % of 28 V) and cable loss 39.6 W. The drop is above a 2 % feeder limit, so 8 AWG is marginal and 4 AWG meets it (1.4 %). For part (c), a 50 A breaker only if the derated wire rating is at least 50 A.

3.11 Bus systems: split, parallel and split-parallel; switches and relays

A busbar is a thick metal bar that takes power from a source and hands it to a group of loads. How generators are joined to the busbars — split, parallel or split-parallel — decides whether one fault darkens one bus or the whole aircraft. Switches make and break those joins by hand; relays and contactors make and break them electrically. Every aircraft from a two-seat trainer to a widebody uses one of these three layouts, and the choice is a trade between simplicity, fault containment and redundancy.

You need: 3.1 for generation and protection, 3.9 for the regulator that sets each generator's voltage, and 3.10 for the breakers that feed each load.

Busbar and the split bus

A busbar is a thick copper or aluminium bar. It collects power from one or more sources and hands it to a group of loads. Copper is common; aluminium saves weight on long runs. The bar is sized so its resistance, and the volt drop along it, stay small.

A split bus (also called an isolated bus) gives each generator its own bar. Generator 1 closes onto bus 1 through its generator circuit breaker (GCB), a heavy-duty contactor that the GCU closes and trips; Airbus calls the same device the generator line contactor. Generator 2 closes onto bus 2 through its own GCB. The two busbars never touch.

The gain is fault containment. A short on bus 1 trips GCB 1 and the aircraft loses bus 1 only. Nothing needs synchronising, because no two generators are ever connected to the same bar; a battery or a TRU may share a bus with a generator, but two generators never do. The wiring between the halves stays light and the control logic stays simple.

The price is that a generator failure strands its bus. Its DC loads can then be carried by the battery (see 3.3) or by a TRU fed from the other side's AC bus (see 3.2), but the AC loads of that side are lost unless a tie is closed. A single-engine aircraft is the simplest case: one generator, one main bus, a battery bus and an essential bus tapped off it. A light twin has one generator per engine on a common or split DC bus.

Parallel bus and load sharing

On a parallel bus all generators close onto one bar. Before a GCB closes, the incoming generator's voltage must match the bus; on AC, frequency and phase must match as well. That is the synchronism check, done by a synchroscope or by an automatic paralleling unit. Closing out of phase drives a large circulating current through both machines.

Once closed, the machines stay in step and share the load. Sharing is not arranged by setting exactly equal no-load voltages. Real machines have droop: terminal voltage falls as current rises.

V = V₀ − kI

V is the terminal voltage in volts, V₀ is the no-load voltage in volts, k is the droop coefficient in volts per ampere, and I is the generator current in amperes.

Two generators on one bus must sit at the same terminal voltage, so for equal no-load voltages the one with the smaller k takes the larger current. With unequal no-load voltages, solve V₀ − kI for both machines. Deliberate droop of a few percent from no-load to full load makes sharing predictable and stable. A load controller can also trim field currents to equalise the share. On AC systems the constant-speed drive holds frequency at 400 Hz, the engine governor sets kilowatt sharing, and the regulator sets reactive sharing.

Split-parallel bus

Split-parallel is the normal state of most twin-engine transports. The buses are split and each generator carries its own side, so fault containment is kept. A bus tie breaker, written BTB, sits between the two buses and stays open in normal flight.

When one generator drops offline its GCB opens and the BTB closes, so the surviving generator feeds both buses. Non-essential loads — galleys, cabin sockets, entertainment — must be shed first, because one generator is rarely rated for the whole aircraft load. A bus power control unit can do this automatically after a short delay.

Normal flight behaves like a split bus; a failure behaves like a parallel bus. An essential bus can also be fed from both sides through diodes, which pass current from either main bus into the essential bus but never from one main bus to the other, so the open BTB is not bypassed and the essential bus keeps power whichever side survives.

Switches, relays and contactors

Cockpit switches are worked by hand: the toggle switch, which stays where it is set; the push-button, which is often momentary and springs back; and the rotary selector, with many positions. Two other switches are worked by the aircraft itself: the microswitch, a tiny snap-action switch pressed by a lever or strut as a limit switch on a door or the landing gear, and the proximity switch, a non-contact sensor that detects a metal target and reports gear up or down.

A relay is an electrically operated switch. A small current in the coil makes a magnetic field that pulls the armature and moves the contacts. Normally open (NO) contacts close when the coil is energised; normally closed (NC) contacts open. A latching relay holds its last state with no coil current. A contactor is a heavy-duty relay for busbars and generators — same principle, bigger contacts, with arc quenching.

A contact rating must cover the making and breaking current, not only the steady current. A DC coil needs a suppression diode across it: when the coil is switched off, its inductance drives a large reverse voltage that would pit the controlling contacts. The diode gives that stored energy a path. A solid-state power controller (SSPC) replaces the relay with a MOSFET plus current sensing, so it can control and protect the wire in one device (see 3.10).

Worked example

Question. Two 28 V DC generators run in parallel on a 300 A bus load. Each has a no-load voltage of 28.5 V. Generator 1 droops 0.4 V per 100 A and generator 2 droops 0.6 V per 100 A. Find the current in each generator and the bus voltage.

  1. Convert each droop to volts per ampere: k₁ = 0.4 ÷ 100 = 0.004 V/A and k₂ = 0.6 ÷ 100 = 0.006 V/A.
  2. In parallel the terminal voltages are equal: 28.5 − 0.004 I₁ = 28.5 − 0.006 I₂.
  3. Cancel the 28.5 V on both sides: 0.004 I₁ = 0.006 I₂, so I₁ = 1.5 I₂.
  4. The load must balance the two currents: I₁ + I₂ = 300 A, so 1.5 I₂ + I₂ = 2.5 I₂ = 300 A, giving I₂ = 120 A.
  5. Then I₁ = 300 − 120 = 180 A.
  6. Bus voltage V = 28.5 − 0.004 × 180 = 28.5 − 0.72 = 27.78 V.
  7. Check on generator 2: 28.5 − 0.006 × 120 = 28.5 − 0.72 = 27.78 V. The two agree.

Answer. Generator 1 carries 180 A, generator 2 carries 120 A, and the bus voltage is 27.78 V.

Exam traps

  • Assuming parallel generators must have exactly equal no-load voltages. They share by droop, so equal set points with unequal slopes still give unequal currents.
  • Paralleling AC generators without a synchronism check. Closing out of phase gives a huge circulating current and can damage the drive.
  • Confusing the generator circuit breaker (GCB) with the bus tie breaker (BTB): the GCB joins one generator to its own bus, the BTB joins the two buses.
  • Treating a split bus as redundancy. It contains faults, but a lost generator leaves its DC loads on the battery or a TRU and its AC loads dead, until a tie is closed.
  • Leaving out the suppression diode across a DC relay coil, so the inductive kickback pits the controlling switch contacts.

Analyze Why does split-parallel operation give both fault containment and redundancy, which neither pure split nor pure parallel gives? 5 marks

In normal flight the tie is open, so a fault on one bus trips only that side's GCB: that is fault containment. After a generator failure the tie closes and the other generator feeds both sides: that is redundancy. Pure split has no backup for a lost generator; pure parallel lets one bus fault pull down every generator at once.

Not for the exam. 3.12 Power system architecture: A320 and B787 case studies

The A320 and the B787 sit at opposite ends of civil power-system design. The A320 keeps a fixed-frequency 115 V AC supply at 400 Hz and a 28 V DC supply from transformer rectifiers. The B787 is a more-electric, bleedless aircraft: 235 V AC at variable frequency and ±270 V DC. Higher voltage means less current for the same power, so thinner, lighter cables and lower copper loss. Comparing the two shows how voltage, frequency and conversion choices fit together into one architecture.

You need: 3.1 for generation and protection, 3.2 for conversion, and 3.11 for bus arrangements.

A320: fixed frequency, two voltage levels

The A320 has two engine-driven integrated drive generators (IDGs), each rated 90 kVA at 115/200 V, 400 Hz. The rating is in kVA, apparent power, not kW. Each IDG has a generator control unit (GCU) that regulates its field, watches for faults and closes its generator line contactor onto its own AC bus, AC BUS 1 or AC BUS 2. The buses are split in normal flight; a bus tie contactor joins them when one generator is off, so this is the split-parallel scheme of bus systems.

The APU generator, also 90 kVA, can replace either IDG on the ground or in flight, and ground power plugs in at the nose. Two transformer rectifiers (TR 1 and TR 2, 200 A each) make 28 V DC for DC BUS 1 and DC BUS 2, and an essential TR feeds the essential DC bus when needed. Two nickel-cadmium batteries start the APU and hold the essential buses for short gaps, and a static inverter makes AC for the essential AC bus from battery power. If all AC is lost, the RAT drives the blue hydraulic system, and a hydraulic-motor-driven emergency generator of about 5 kVA feeds the essential buses (see emergency power).

B787: variable frequency and more electric

The B787 takes no bleed air from its engines. Cabin pressurisation, wing ice protection, the brakes and several hydraulic pumps run on electricity instead, so it needs far more electrical power. Each engine carries two variable-frequency starter-generators (VFSGs), four in all, each rated 250 kVA. A starter-generator starts the engine as a motor and then generates. With no constant-speed drive, the output is 235 V AC at a frequency of about 360 Hz to 800 Hz that follows engine speed. Two APU starter-generators of 225 kVA each add backup, so the total installed generation is 4 × 250 + 2 × 225 = 1450 kVA. The four main generators alone give 1000 kVA, more than five times the 180 kVA of the A320's two IDGs.

Conversion is spread out. Auto-transformer rectifier units (ATRUs) turn 235 V AC into ±270 V DC for the big motor controllers (cabin compressors, hydraulic pumps, engine start), auto-transformer units (ATUs) make 115 V AC for older-style loads, and TRUs make 28 V DC. Small loads are switched by solid-state power controllers in remote power distribution units near the loads. Lithium-ion main and APU batteries start the APU and hold the DC buses, and the RAT powers the essential buses when all normal sources fail. Every load on the variable-frequency bus must accept the whole 360 to 800 Hz range; that is the key design rule of the aircraft.

P = 3 × Vph × I × cos φ

P is real power in watts; Vph is the phase (line-to-neutral) voltage, the 115 V or 235 V quoted for aircraft systems; I is the line current in amperes; cos φ is the power factor. The same power is √3 × VLL × I × cos φ, with VLL = √3 × Vph (200 V and 407 V).

At unity power factor I = P ÷ (3 Vph). Copper loss in a three-phase cable is 3 I2 R, where R is the resistance of one conductor. For the same power, raising the voltage lowers the current in proportion and the loss as 1 ÷ V2. That is why 235 V AC and ±270 V DC save copper weight, but they demand new insulation, connectors and arc protection.

Worked example

Question. A 100 kW balanced three-phase load at unity power factor is fed first from a 115 V (line-to-neutral) system and then from a 235 V (line-to-neutral) system, through the same cable. Find the line current in each case and the ratio of cable copper loss.

  1. Given: P = 100 kW = 100 000 W; Vph1 = 115 V; Vph2 = 235 V; cos φ = 1.

  3. Check with the line voltage: VLL = 1.732 × 115 = 199.2 V, and 100 000 ÷ (1.732 × 199.2) = 289.9 A, the same.
  4. At 235 V: I2 = 100 000 ÷ (3 × 235) = 100 000 ÷ 705 = 141.8 A.
  5. The cable is the same, so R is the same and the loss 3 I2 R scales with I2: ratio = (289.9 ÷ 141.8)2 = 2.0442 = 4.18.

Answer. The line current is 289.9 A at 115 V and 141.8 A at 235 V, so the 235 V system has 1/4.18 of the copper loss, or it can use a thinner, lighter cable for the same loss.

3.13 Engine indicators and fuel quantity indication

Engine indicators turn pressure, flow, torque and vibration into readings the crew can act on. Speed and exhaust temperature tell how fast and hot the engine runs; oil pressure, fuel pressure, fuel flow, torque and vibration tell whether it is healthy and how much fuel it is burning. Fuel quantity is the hardest indication because the fuel sits in a tank that tilts, heats and cools, and changes density. This chapter covers the sensors, the capacitance probe, and the cross-checks that keep the quantity figure honest.

You need: 3.8 for the engine indicator chain, and first-year capacitance, C = εA/d.

Pressure, flow, torque and vibration

A Bourdon tube is a curved metal tube that straightens as pressure inside rises. Its free end moves a linkage and the pointer. It needs no electricity, so light aircraft use it for oil and fuel pressure. Larger aircraft use a pressure transducer: a diaphragm or strain gauge bends and changes resistance or voltage.

IndicatorSensorWhat it shows
Oil pressureBourdon tube or transducerLubrication health
Fuel pressureBourdon tube or transducerPump and filter health
Fuel flowTurbine or vane flowmeter with density correctionkg per hour

Fuel flow is a rate, not a total. A turbine flowmeter has a rotor spun by the fuel; its speed, read as pulses by a pickup, follows the volume flow. To show kilograms per hour the system multiplies by the fuel density, so mass-flow meters either measure density or use a mass-sensing design. Torque is the useful output of a turboprop; a torque sensor measures gearbox twist or oil pressure in a torque piston and shows torque as a percentage. Shaft power is torque times shaft speed, P = T × ω, so the crew reads power from torque and propeller speed together. Vibration comes from an accelerometer on the casing; a rising level warns of imbalance or bearing damage.

Primary indicators are the ones needed to set power safely: N1 fan speed (or engine pressure ratio on some engines), exhaust gas temperature (EGT), and on many types N2 core speed. Secondary indicators, such as oil pressure, oil temperature, oil quantity, fuel flow and vibration, give health and trend. Starting indicators show the start sequence: starter valve open, ignition on, N2 rising, fuel on and light-off seen as an EGT rise, then starter cut-out at the set speed. The engine display groups these readings for the crew.

Fuel quantity: float and capacitance

On a light aircraft the tank has a float on an arm. The arm moves a rheostat, a variable resistor, and the current through it moves the gauge. It is simple and cheap. It also has moving parts, and the float can stick.

Transport aircraft use capacitance gauging. A probe in the tank is a capacitor: two plates, or two concentric tubes, with fuel or air between them. Fuel has a relative permittivity εr of about 2.0; air is 1.0. As fuel rises between the plates, it replaces air and the capacitance rises. A bridge circuit measures the capacitance and converts it to volume or mass.

C = ε0 εr A / d

C is capacitance in farads (F); ε0 = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m is the permittivity of free space; εr is the relative permittivity of the material between the plates; A is plate area in m²; d is plate separation in m.

A real probe is not fully full or fully empty. The fuel fills part of the height. Model it as two capacitors in parallel: the air-filled part and the fuel-filled part. Then C = Cempty + (Cfull − Cempty)(h/H), where h is fuel height and H is total height between the plates. For the example probe, Cempty is about 35 pF and Cfull about 71 pF.

The tank unit sends capacitance to a bridge. A compensator unit, a small probe kept fully flooded, measures the fuel's dielectric constant. The bridge uses it to correct the reading when fuel temperature or fuel type changes εr. To show kilograms, the system applies density: mass = volume × density. The refuelling panel lets the crew set a target in kilograms; valves close when the target is reached. Drip sticks are manual dipsticks used as an independent check.

Errors and cross-checks

Attitude changes the reading. In a climb, fuel runs to the rear of the tank; the probe sees a different local level from the true quantity. In a steep turn the same happens sideways. Transport systems use multiple probes and compensation, but a large attitude error can remain until the aircraft is level. Temperature changes both density and εr. A cold refuel gives more kilograms in the same volume; a warm tank gives fewer. Fuel type matters: different jet fuels have slightly different dielectric constants. The compensator corrects εr, but density still needs a temperature or density input.

Fuel flow integration is the cross-check. Total fuel used = fuel flow × time, summed over the flight. Subtract that from the starting quantity and compare with the gauge. If the gauge stays still while flow continues, the probe or bridge is suspect. If the gauge falls faster than flow used, a leak or a density error is possible. The check does not replace the gauge; it catches a stuck or biased indication.

mused = Σ (fuel flow × Δt)

mused is total fuel used in kilograms; fuel flow is in kilograms per second; Δt is each time step in seconds; the sum runs over the flight.

Worked example

Question. A capacitance fuel probe has parallel plates of area 0.02 m² separated by 5 mm. The relative permittivity is 1.0 for air and 2.0 for fuel; ε0 = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m. Fuel fills the plate height from the bottom. Find the capacitance when the tank is empty, half full and full. Give each answer in picofarads.

  1. Convert d = 5 mm = 0.005 m. Empty tank: only air between the plates. Cempty = ε0 A / d = (8.854 × 10⁻¹² × 0.02) / 0.005 = 3.5416 × 10⁻¹¹ F = 35.416 pF.
  2. Full tank: fuel replaces air, so multiply by εr = 2.0. Cfull = 2.0 × 35.416 = 70.832 pF.
  3. Half full: half the plate area sees air and half sees fuel, two capacitors in parallel. Chalf = (35.416 + 70.832) / 2 = 53.124 pF.
  4. Rounded to one decimal place: 35.4 pF, 53.1 pF and 70.8 pF. Each half of fuel height adds about 17.7 pF.

Answer. Cempty = 35.4 pF, Chalf = 53.1 pF, Cfull = 70.8 pF.

Worked example 2

Question. A flight starts with 12 000 kg of fuel. The fuel-flow totals show 2400 kg/h for the first 1.5 h and 2000 kg/h for the next 2 h. The quantity gauges then read 4100 kg. (a) How much fuel should remain? (b) What is the discrepancy, and what should the crew suspect?

  1. Fuel used in part 1 = 2400 kg/h × 1.5 h = 3600 kg.
  2. Fuel used in part 2 = 2000 kg/h × 2 h = 4000 kg.
  3. Total used = 3600 + 4000 = 7600 kg.
  4. Expected remaining = 12 000 − 7600 = 4400 kg.
  5. Discrepancy = 4400 − 4100 = 300 kg: the gauges show less than the flowmeters allow for.

Answer. (a) 4400 kg. (b) The gauges read 300 kg below the flow-based figure; a gauge falling faster than fuel is being burnt points to a leak, or to a density or probe error, so the crew runs the fuel-leak check before trusting either figure.

Exam traps

  • Capacitance is not proportional to tank volume in an irregular tank; it follows the wetted height and area between the plates.
  • Empty probe capacitance is not zero. Air gives about 35 pF in the example; zero would mean a short or an open circuit.
  • Do not mix fuel flow (kg/h) with fuel quantity (kg). The gauge shows total; flow must be integrated over time.
  • The dielectric constant of fuel is not fixed. Temperature and fuel type shift it, and the compensator exists for that; density also shifts.
  • A quantity gauge can be correct in litres and wrong in kilograms if density is wrong; read the units.
  • Reading a torque gauge as power. Power is torque times shaft speed, so the same torque at a lower propeller speed is less power.

Takeaways

  1. A generator is only useful once its voltage is regulated, its output is switched to a busbar, and its faults are cleared before wiring overheats.
  2. Converters exist because different loads need different forms of power: 115 V AC 400 Hz, 28 V DC, and on newer aircraft 235 V AC variable frequency and 270 V DC.
  3. The battery starts the engine or APU and bridges the gap between losing all generators and getting another source online.
  4. The ram air turbine and the permanent magnet generator are the last line of defence, and ETOPS rules decide how much time that line must buy.
  5. Aircraft lights divide by user (crew, cabin, outside world), and each group gets its own supply, control and failure requirement.

Model answer.

  1. Name the power path. On an AC transport aircraft the generator (an IDG at 115 V, 400 Hz, or a variable-frequency generator) feeds its generator circuit breaker (GCB), the GCB feeds the main AC busbar, and the busbar feeds each load through that load's own circuit breaker. A DC channel is the same with a reverse-current cut-out in place of the GCB. Draw it as a straight line: generator → GCB → busbar → load breaker → load.
  2. Place the control block. The generator control unit (GCU) is not in the power path and carries no load current. It senses generator voltage, current and frequency, controls the field current to hold the voltage at 115 V (or 28 V on a DC system), and commands the GCB to close or trip. Draw it to the side with a field-control line to the generator and a trip line to the GCB.
  3. Explain the busbar. The busbar is a thick copper or aluminium bar that collects the generator output and shares it among many load circuits. Its resistance is kept low so the voltage at every load is nearly the bus voltage. Grouping loads on buses (main, essential, battery) lets protection drop a group without losing the rest.
  4. State when the GCB closes. The GCU closes the GCB only when the generator voltage and frequency are inside their normal band, so a generator that has not built up, or is out of limits, is never connected. MIL-STD-704 sets the normal steady-state band at the equipment as 108 to 118 V for a 115 V AC system and 22 to 29 V for a 28 V DC system.
  5. Explain over-voltage protection. A runaway field (for example a failed regulator driving full field) can push the bus far above its band within a fraction of a second, damaging insulation and every connected load. The over-voltage unit therefore trips fast and on an inverse-time curve: the higher the excess, the quicker the trip. The GCU removes the field and opens the GCB together, because opening the GCB alone leaves an excited machine.
  6. Explain under-voltage protection. A generator whose voltage sags (a weak field, a drive fault, an overload) would drag the bus down and starve the loads. The under-voltage trip opens the GCB, but only after a time delay, so a short dip while a large motor starts does not drop the generator. The bus then transfers to another source through the bus tie.

V = 28.5 − 0.004 × 168 = 27.83 V   (check: 28.5 − 0.006 × 112 = 27.83 V)
  4. So the stiffer machine (generator 1) carries more: 168 A against 112 A, both under their 200 A ratings. A load controller would trim the fields to bring them nearer 140 A each.
  5. Split-parallel bus: the normal state of most twin transports. In normal flight the bus tie breaker (BTB) is open, so the system behaves as split, with fault containment and no sharing problem: generator 1 carries 150 A and generator 2 carries 130 A. When a generator fails, its GCB opens and the BTB closes, so the survivor feeds both buses, giving the redundancy of a parallel system.
  6. After one generator fails.
    load on the survivor = 150 + 130 = 280 A
rating = 200 A  →  shed at least 280 − 200 = 80 A
(shedding galleys and cabin loads first)

Evaluate Fuel quantity is often called the hardest cockpit measurement. Analyse why, and evaluate the correction methods used. A capacitance probe reads 35.4 pF empty and 70.8 pF full; and 10 000 litres of fuel at 0.80 kg/L is shown with a stale density of 0.78 kg/L: find the probe reading at half height and the mass error. 12 marks

Points to cover. Tank shape, attitude, slosh and acceleration; Capacitance follows wetted height; εr changes with fuel type and temperature; Density turns volume into mass; Compensator, densitometer, multiple probes and tank tables; Flow-integration cross-check and dripsticks; 53.1 pF; 200 kg (2.5 %) error.

Model answer.

  1. Why it is hard: the fuel lies in irregular wing tanks that flex, pitch, roll and accelerate, so the surface tilts and the local level at any one probe is not the true quantity; the fuel's dielectric constant and density change with fuel type and temperature; and the crew wants mass (kilograms, for range and weight) while the sensors see height or volume.
  2. The capacitance probe: two concentric tubes form a capacitor; fuel (εr about 2.0) rising between them replaces air (εr = 1.0), so C = Cempty + (Cfull − Cempty) × h ÷ H. It has no moving parts, unlike a float and rheostat, which can stick and gives only one point per tank.
  3. Probe at half height.
    C = 35.4 + (70.8 − 35.4) × 0.5 = 35.4 + 17.7 = 53.1 pF
  4. Correction 1, the dielectric: a compensator probe at the bottom of the tank is always fully wet and measures the fuel's own εr, so the bridge reads height correctly whatever the fuel.
  5. Correction 2, the density: mass = volume × density. A densitometer in the tank, or a density figure from refuelling, converts volume to mass. A wrong density gives a wrong mass even when the volume is right:
  6. Mass error from a stale density.
    true mass  = 10 000 L × 0.80 kg/L = 8000 kg
shown mass = 10 000 L × 0.78 kg/L = 7800 kg
error = 200 kg = 200 ÷ 8000 × 100 = 2.5 % (under-reading)
  7. Correction 3, attitude and shape: many probes per tank, spread out, plus a tank table that turns the set of probe heights into volume for the current pitch and roll; the fuel computer averages them and flags a disagreement.
  8. Cross-checks: fuel used from integrated fuel flow is subtracted from the start quantity and compared with the gauges; a gauge falling faster than the burn points to a leak or a probe or density fault. On the ground, dripsticks (magnetic level indicators) give an independent reading.
  9. Evaluation: the compensator removes the εr error and the multi-probe tank tables remove most of the attitude error, so the remaining weak point is density, a 2.5 % error in this example; that is why modern systems measure density in the tank rather than take a figure from the paperwork. Check: 53.1 pF is midway between 35.4 and 70.8 pF, as a half-full parallel-plate probe must be.
Evaluate Evaluate the consequences of fitting a circuit breaker larger than the wire's derated rating, and explain the role of current limiters and limiting resistors. Use a wire rated 25 A whose damage time is t = 30 000 ÷ (I² − 625) s, the 20 A breaker t = 6000 ÷ (I² − 400) s and a 50 A breaker t = 37 500 ÷ (I² − 2500) s, all at a 60 A fault. A limiting resistor of 0.5 Ω is put in series with a 28 V load: what is the largest inrush current? 12 marks

Points to cover. Oversized breaker: wire becomes the fuse, insulation chars, fire risk; At 60 A: wire 10.1 s, 20 A breaker 1.9 s, 50 A breaker 34.1 s; Below 50 A the large breaker never trips: sustained overload; Current limiter: slow-blow heavy-current fuse on feeders; Limiting resistor: 28 ÷ 0.5 = 56 A inrush cap; Undersizing also fails: nuisance trips.

Model answer.

  1. The rule: a breaker protects the wire, not the load. Its curve must lie below the wire damage curve at every current, and its rating must not exceed the wire's derated rating.
  2. Compare the times at a 60 A fault.
    DeviceWorkingTime at 60 A
    Wire damage (25 A wire)30 000 ÷ (3600 − 625)10.1 s
    20 A breaker6000 ÷ (3600 − 400)1.9 s
    50 A breaker37 500 ÷ (3600 − 2500)34.1 s
  3. With the 20 A breaker the fault clears in 1.9 s, well before the wire's 10.1 s: correct. With the 50 A breaker the wire is damaged at 10.1 s while the breaker would not open until 34.1 s, so the wire itself becomes the fuse: the insulation softens and chars, smoke and toxic fumes form in the bundle, neighbouring wires can be damaged, and an arc can start a fire.
  4. Worse, between 25 A and 50 A the 50 A breaker never trips at all, so a sustained overload of, say, 40 A cooks the wire indefinitely without any trip; this is the most dangerous case because nothing tells the crew.
  5. Current limiters: a current limiter is a heavy-current, slow-blow fuse used on generator and main feeders and bus ties. It carries large short overloads (motor starts) but melts on a sustained fault, so it isolates a faulted feeder before the main busbar sees the full fault current. It is not resettable and must be coordinated so the load breakers downstream clear local faults first.
  6. Limiting resistor.
    largest inrush = V ÷ R = 28 V ÷ 0.5 Ω = 56 A
(the load's own resistance would lower it further)
  7. A limiting resistor is put in series to hold down the inrush of a capacitive or motor load, or the fault current of a circuit, to a value a smaller breaker or contactor can handle; it limits, it does not break the circuit, and it is often bypassed by a contactor once the load is up to speed.
  8. Evaluation: oversizing a breaker removes the wire's protection and is a fire risk; undersizing it (below the load current) causes nuisance trips and repeated resets, which also heat the wire. The right breaker sits between the load current and the derated wire rating. Check: 30 000 ÷ 2975 = 10.08 s, 6000 ÷ 3200 = 1.875 s, 37 500 ÷ 1100 = 34.09 s.

Glossary

Anti-collision light
Flashing red beacon that warns ground crew and other traffic that the aircraft is about to move or is running.
APU
Auxiliary power unit: a small gas turbine in the tail that supplies electrical and pneumatic power on the ground and in flight if needed.
ATRU
Auto-transformer rectifier unit: an auto-transformer with a multi-pulse rectifier that turns 235 V AC into ±270 V DC on the B787.
Battery management system (BMS)
The electronics in a lithium-ion battery that watch each cell's voltage and temperature, balance the cells and stop charging when a limit is reached.
BITE
Built-in test equipment: self-check circuits inside a unit that report faults to the cockpit or a maintenance panel.
Bus tie
A contactor that joins two busbars, either to share load between generators or to let one generator feed both sides.
Capacitance probe
Fuel tank sensor whose capacitance changes as fuel rises between its plates; used to infer fuel level.
Circuit breaker
Resettable protection device that opens on overcurrent and can be reset by the crew after a fault is cleared.
Cold junction compensation
Correction applied for the temperature of the thermocouple's reference end, so the reading is the hot-end temperature only.
Contactor
A heavy-duty relay that makes and breaks a main power circuit under control of a switch or protection relay.
Current limiter
Slow-acting protective device for heavy feeder circuits; carries normal current indefinitely but opens on a large sustained fault.
Derating
Reducing a component's allowed current below its catalogue value to allow for high ambient temperature, altitude, or tightly bundled wires.
Droop
The fall of a generator's terminal voltage as its current rises, V = V₀ − kI; it sets how paralleled generators share load.
Dynamic pressure
The pressure produced by motion, equal to half the density times the square of the speed, written ½ρV².
Essential bus
A busbar reserved for loads that must keep running: flight instruments, radios, flight controls, and minimum lighting.
Excitation
Supplying the field current; a generator with no excitation produces no output.
Field current
The small DC current fed to a generator's field winding, which sets the strength of the magnetic field and hence the output voltage.
GCB
Generator circuit breaker: the heavy-duty contactor, commanded by the GCU, that connects a generator to its bus; Airbus calls it the generator line contactor.
Ground power unit
External cart or pit supply that feeds the aircraft busbars while the engines and APU are off.
HIRF
High-intensity radiated fields: strong external radio or radar energy the aircraft's wiring must not pick up as a false signal.
IDG
Integrated drive generator: an engine-driven generator with a built-in constant-speed drive so it always produces 400 Hz AC.
Load shedding
Automatically removing non-essential loads so a reduced power source can keep the essential ones running.
Pitot tube
Forward-facing open tube on the fuselage that senses total pressure for the airspeed indicator.
Rectifier
Circuit of diodes that converts alternating current into direct current.
Static pressure
The pressure of still air at the aircraft's altitude, sensed by ports on the fuselage side.
Tachometer
Instrument that displays engine shaft speed, usually as a percentage of the maximum rated speed.
Thermocouple
Junction of two unlike metals that produces a small voltage proportional to the temperature difference between its hot and cold ends.
TRU
Transformer rectifier unit; steps AC down and rectifies it to DC, typically giving 28 V DC from 115 V AC.
