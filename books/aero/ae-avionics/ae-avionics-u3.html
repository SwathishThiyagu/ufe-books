3.1 Power generation, distribution and protection

An aircraft generator is useless until its voltage is held steady, its output is switched onto a busbar, and its faults are cleared before wiring overheats. Follow one generator channel from the engine shaft to the loads. It covers DC generation with shunt and compound machines, AC generation at 115 V 400 Hz from an integrated drive generator and at 360-800 Hz variable frequency, and the 28 V and 270 V DC systems built on them. The chain is simple; the protection is what makes it safe.

You need: first-year DC and AC circuit theory — Ohm's law, three-phase power and power factor — and 3.9 shows how the field current is actually controlled.

The DC channel: generator, cut-out, bus, with the regulator as control

Every load draws from a busbar, not directly from a generator. A generator converts engine shaft power into electrical power. Its voltage must stay inside a band because lamps, radios and instruments are designed for that band. One source of 28 V DC is a shunt or compound DC generator. A shunt generator has its field winding across the armature; output voltage rises with speed and field current. A compound generator adds a series field that lifts voltage as load current rises, so the regulator has less work to do. The regulator is covered in 3.9.

The power path in a DC channel is: generator → reverse-current cut-out → busbar → loads. The reverse-current cut-out is a contactor that closes when generator voltage is above bus voltage. It opens when current tries to flow from the battery back into the generator. Without it, a stopped generator becomes a motor and drains the battery. This is a DC device, not an AC generator circuit breaker.

The voltage regulator is not in that power path. It is a control block: it senses bus voltage and controls the generator field current. It carries no load current, so it is drawn beside the generator, not between the generator and the busbar. How it senses and controls is in 3.9.

28 V DC is the long-standing low-voltage standard. 270 V DC appears where high-power DC loads make lower voltage wasteful; it is not a scaled 28 V system, because higher voltage changes insulation and arc behaviour.

AC generation: constant frequency and variable frequency

An AC generator's frequency follows its speed: f = p × n ÷ 120. Here f is frequency in hertz, p is the number of poles and n is speed in revolutions per minute. A four-pole machine at 12 000 rpm gives 400 Hz. Engine speed is not constant, so a 400 Hz supply needs a constant-speed drive. An integrated drive generator (IDG) puts that drive and an oil-cooled generator in one unit. It produces 115 V AC at 400 Hz. The generator control unit (GCU) is a control block: it regulates voltage by controlling field current and it protects the generator. The generator circuit breaker (GCB) is the switch that connects the generator to the AC bus. A transformer rectifier unit (TRU) then feeds the 28 V DC bus; TRUs are in 3.2.

Variable-frequency (VF) generation avoids the constant-speed drive. The generator runs at engine gearbox speed and its output varies from 360 Hz to 800 Hz. Loads are designed to accept that range or to rectify it first. VF saves weight and maintenance. Newer architectures use 235 V AC VF and 270 V DC; the A320 and B787 comparison is in 3.12.

The AC power path is: generator → GCB → AC bus → loads, with a TRU branch from the AC bus to the DC bus. The GCU sits outside this path. It senses generator voltage, current, frequency and field current, controls the field, and commands the GCB to trip or the drive to disconnect.

Protection at block level

Protection removes a fault before it damages wiring or the generator. The action matters as much as the name.

A differential fault compares currents at the two ends of a winding; a difference means current is escaping through a fault. An earth fault detects current returning through the airframe. Both act fast. Over-excitation is different: the GCU first reduces field current, because tripping the GCB alone does not remove the field. A seized IDG drive is handled by the drive disconnect, a mechanical action. So 'the contactor opens for every fault' is wrong.

Worked example

Question. A twin-engine aircraft has two 28 V DC generators, each rated 200 A continuous. Non-essential loads draw 180 A and essential loads draw 60 A. One generator fails. (a) How much load must be shed so the surviving generator is not overloaded? (b) If all non-essential loads are dropped, what load does the surviving generator carry, and what fraction of its rating is that?

  1. Total load before failure = non-essential + essential = 180 A + 60 A = 240 A.
  2. Surviving generator capacity = 200 A.
  3. Minimum load to shed = 240 A − 200 A = 40 A.
  4. Dropping all non-essential load removes 180 A.
  5. Remaining load = 240 A − 180 A = 60 A, which is the essential load.
  6. Fraction of rating = 60 A ÷ 200 A = 0.30 = 30%.

Answer. At least 40 A must be shed; after all non-essential load is dropped the survivor carries 60 A, which is 30% of its 200 A rating.

Exam traps

  • The GCB does not open for every fault. Over-excitation is first corrected by reducing field current; a seized IDG drive is cleared by the drive disconnect, a mechanical action.
  • 400 Hz from an IDG is constant frequency because a constant-speed drive holds generator speed. A 360-800 Hz variable-frequency supply is not a faulty 400 Hz supply; the range is its design.
  • Reverse-current cut-out is a DC device that stops the battery driving the generator as a motor. It is not the AC generator circuit breaker.
  • GCU and GCB are different: the GCU senses, regulates and decides; the GCB carries and breaks generator current.
  • Shedding exactly the calculated deficit may not match the load blocks available. Dropping a 180 A non-essential bus leaves 60 A on the survivor; state the block actually dropped.

3.2 Inverters, transformer rectifier units and auto-transformers

An aircraft makes alternating current (AC) and direct current (DC) power, but each load needs one exact form. A converter changes AC to DC, DC to AC, or AC to a lower AC voltage. A transformer rectifier unit (TRU) turns 115 V, 400 Hz AC into 28 V DC. An inverter turns DC back into 115 V, 400 Hz AC for standby instruments. An auto-transformer taps one winding to make 26 V AC for lighting and synchro reference. Only a few universities teach this chapter in detail.

You need: 3.1 for where the AC and DC buses come from.

Inverters and transformer rectifier units

An inverter changes direct current (DC) into alternating current (AC). A static inverter uses an SCR (silicon-controlled rectifier) or transistor bridge to switch the DC input into a stepped AC waveform, then a filter smooths it. Output is 115 V root mean square (rms), 400 Hz (hertz), single-phase or three-phase, for AC instruments, synchros (small AC angle sensors) and the AC standby bus. Frequency comes from the switching control, not from a shaft. On generator loss the battery can drive the inverter through the DC bus; see 3.4.

Older rotary inverters used a DC motor driving an AC generator. Static inverters are lighter, need no brushes and start faster.

A TRU changes 115 V line-to-neutral, 400 Hz AC into 28 V DC. It has a three-phase transformer and a full-wave diode bridge. Ratings are often 100 A to 200 A. The output feeds the DC bus and charges the battery; see 3.3. The losses, P_ac − P_dc, appear as heat.

Aircraft AC voltage is quoted line-to-neutral. A 115 V system has V_LL = 115 × √3 = 199.2 V, about 200 V. Use V_LL = 200 V in the formula below.

P_dc = V_dc × I_dc; P_ac = P_dc / η; I_line = P_ac / (√3 × V_LL × cos φ)

P_dc is DC output power in W; V_dc is DC voltage in V; I_dc is DC current in A; P_ac is AC input power in W; η is efficiency as a decimal; I_line is line current in A; V_LL is line-to-line AC voltage in V; for a 115 V aircraft AC system, V_LL = 115 × √3 ≈ 200 V; cos φ is power factor.

Auto-transformers and current transformers

An auto-transformer is one winding with tapped sections. It steps 115 V AC down to 26 V AC for instrument lighting and synchro reference. The voltage ratio is V₂ / V₁ = N₂ / N₁, where V is voltage and N is turns. It gives no isolation: input and output share the same winding, so a fault can pass across. Do not use it where isolation is required.

A current transformer (CT) senses load current. The cable carrying the load is the primary; the secondary gives a smaller current in a fixed ratio, for example 100 A to 5 A. A burden resistor turns secondary current into a voltage for indication or protection. The burden is the connected resistance; too large a burden distorts the signal. Never leave the secondary open while current flows, because a high voltage can appear.

Worked example

Question. A transformer rectifier unit is rated 200 A at 28 V DC and is 92 % efficient. It is fed from a 200 V line-to-line (115 V line-to-neutral) three-phase AC bus at unity power factor. Find the DC output power, the AC input power and the line current drawn.

  1. 1. DC output power: P_dc = 28 V × 200 A = 5600 W.
  2. 2. AC input power: P_ac = P_dc / η = 5600 / 0.92 = 6086.96 W, about 6090 W.
  3. 3. Line current: I_line = P_ac / (√3 × 200 × 1) = 6086.96 / 346.41 = 17.57 A, about 17.6 A.

Answer. The TRU delivers 5600 W, draws about 6090 W from the AC bus, and takes about 17.6 A per line.

Exam traps

  • Treating an auto-transformer as an isolation transformer. It shares one winding.
  • Using DC output power as AC input power, or dropping efficiency, when finding TRU line current.
  • Assuming inverter frequency follows battery voltage; the bridge control sets 400 Hz.
  • Leaving a CT secondary open, or choosing too large a burden.
  • Mixing 26 V AC instrument lighting with the 28 V DC bus.

3.3 Aircraft batteries: lead-acid and nickel-cadmium

With all generators offline, the battery is the only source; it carries essential loads until another source comes online. A 24 V lead-acid battery has 12 cells at about 2.1 V each. A 24 V nickel-cadmium battery has 19 or 20 cells at about 1.25 V each. Aircraft use these batteries for starting, emergency DC buses and standby instruments. Only a few universities teach the chemistry and testing detail; the comparison is standard.

You need Chapter 3.1 and Chapter 3.2, because the battery sits on the DC bus and is charged through a TRU or dedicated charger.

Lead-acid: 12 cells, sulphuric acid, specific gravity

A lead-acid cell has a lead-dioxide positive plate, a sponge-lead negative plate and dilute sulphuric acid electrolyte. Each cell gives about 2.1 V on load; twelve in series give 24 V. Specific gravity near 1.28 at full charge shows state of charge, because charging raises acid concentration. Read it after the battery rests or after mixing; water added during topping up lowers the reading until it mixes.

Lead-acid gives strong cranking current and is cheap, but heavy per ampere-hour. Deep discharge damages the plates. Capacity is in ampere-hours (Ah): Ah = I × t, where I is current in amperes and t is time in hours. C-rate = I ÷ rated Ah; a 40 Ah battery at 40 A is 1C, the 1-hour rate. The figure plots terminal voltage against capacity discharged at 1C. Lead-acid voltage falls steadily, so the end point is easy to see.

Nickel-cadmium: 19 or 20 cells, flat voltage, thermal risk

A nickel-cadmium (NiCd) cell uses nickel hydroxide positive and cadmium negative plates in potassium hydroxide electrolyte. Sintered plates pack active material into a porous nickel matrix. Each cell gives about 1.25 V, so 19 cells give 23.75 V and 20 cells give 25 V; both are called 24 V batteries. Never use a 12-cell lead-acid charger setting on a NiCd battery.

NiCd has low internal resistance, good cold performance and long life. Its voltage stays almost flat until nearly discharged, so a simple voltage reading is a poor state-of-charge check. The flat curve also means the end point must be detected carefully. NiCd is prone to thermal runaway: high temperature raises charging current, which raises temperature further and can vent electrolyte. It also shows voltage depression, often called memory effect, after repeated partial discharge and overcharge; a full slow discharge and recharge restores most capacity.

Capacity check, charging and lithium

A capacity check discharges the battery at constant current to a stated end voltage, records Ah removed, and compares it with rated Ah. A 40 Ah battery that gives only 30 Ah has 75% capacity. Lead-acid charges at constant current, then at constant voltage (float) to avoid overcharge. Nickel-cadmium is charged at constant current, stopped by voltage or temperature cut-off. Never leave a NiCd on a constant-voltage charge: its cell voltage falls as it heats, so current keeps rising. Deep discharge below the end voltage damages both types.

Depth of discharge (DoD) = (Ah removed ÷ rated Ah) × 100%. In the worked example, 20 Ah from a 40 Ah battery is 50% DoD. The remaining 20 Ah at 20 A lasts 1 hour if rate effects are ignored.

Some syllabi also list lithium-ion. It gives more energy per kilogram and a flatter voltage than lead-acid or NiCd, but it needs a battery management system because overcharge or cell damage can cause fire. That fire risk is why lithium needs stricter charge control and temperature monitoring.

  • Do not take a specific-gravity reading just after topping up with water; the acid is diluted until mixing.

3.4 Emergency power: ram air turbine and ETOPS

When every main generator is lost, a small air-driven generator called the ram air turbine (RAT) drops into the slipstream and feeds the emergency AC bus. A permanent magnet generator (PMG) supplies engine control power, and the battery bridges the gap until the RAT is online. ETOPS sets how long a twin must survive on one generator plus these backups. Only a few universities teach this at depth.

You need 3.1 for main sources and 3.3 for battery capacity and usable charge.

The emergency supply chain

Main generators on the engines supply normal power. When all fail, the aircraft still needs instruments, radios, flight controls and engine control. These essential loads sit on the emergency AC bus and the essential DC bus. The chain works in order. Protection removes the failed generators. The RAT deploys. Its generator feeds the emergency AC bus, which feeds an essential transformer rectifier (TRU). The TRU makes 28 V DC for the essential DC bus. The battery carries that DC bus for the few seconds before the RAT is online. The PMG supplies engine control power independently. The emergency AC bus is separate from the main AC buses, so a main-bus fault cannot reach emergency equipment. The essential TRU is sized for these loads only. A contactor connects the battery when the DC bus voltage falls. Each source covers the gap for the next. This order is automatic. The chain is tested.

Ram air turbine, PMG and backup converters

Ram air turbine (RAT): a small propeller on a strut is released into the airstream. It spins a generator of a few kVA (kilovolt-amperes). Deployment is automatic after loss of all AC power, or manual. The RAT has an airspeed band: below the lower limit it turns too slowly, above the upper limit it may overspeed. Inside the band its voltage and frequency still vary with airspeed. It feeds the emergency AC bus, not the main buses. Permanent magnet generator (PMG): a magnet rotor spins inside a fixed stator winding. There is no field winding, so the output cannot be switched off by removing field current; only stopping the shaft stops it. On an engine or APU it supplies control power to the electronic control unit, and in some APUs ignition power during start; on engines the igniters are usually fed from the aircraft AC bus. Its frequency rises with shaft speed, so it is rectified before use. Backup converters let the battery feed the essential DC bus; a static inverter makes AC for the AC standby bus.

ETOPS and its electrical consequence

ETOPS means Extended-range Twin-engine Operational Performance Standards. It sets the time a twin-engine aircraft may fly from a point on the route to a suitable alternate with one engine inoperative. Common limits are 60, 120 and 180 minutes. The rule is about time, not distance: at 420 kt (knots), 120 minutes is 840 nautical miles (nm), and 180 minutes is 1260 nm. For the electrical system, ETOPS asks whether the remaining generator, the APU start and the battery can carry the essential loads for the diversion time. The battery does not do this alone. It covers the gap until the APU generator or the RAT is online. ETOPS approval checks every source in the chain. Formula: t = Q_usable / I, where t is time in hours, Q_usable is usable charge in ampere-hours and I is current in amperes. Usable charge is less than rated capacity, as the battery chapter 3.3 shows.

Worked example

Question. An aircraft is cleared for ETOPS 120, so it must stay within 120 minutes of a suitable alternate; at 420 kt that is 840 nm. Its essential DC bus draws 2.8 kW at 28 V. The battery is rated 40 Ah and only 50 % may be used. Find the bus current, usable charge and battery time, and say what this means for the diversion.

  1. Distance = 420 kt × 2 h = 840 nm.
  2. Bus current I = P / V = 2800 W / 28 V = 100 A.
  3. Usable charge = 40 Ah × 0.50 = 20 Ah.
  4. Time t = Q_usable / I = 20 Ah / 100 A = 0.2 h.
  5. 0.2 h × 60 = 12 min.

Answer. 100 A, 20 Ah and 12 min; the RAT or APU must take over long before 120 minutes.

Exam traps

  • Thinking the battery covers the full ETOPS diversion; it only bridges until the RAT or APU generator is online.
  • Forgetting the RAT airspeed band; outside it the output is unusable.
  • Confusing the PMG with a wound-field generator; no field winding means it cannot be switched off electrically.
  • Using rated battery Ah instead of usable Ah; only a fraction may be discharged.

3.5 Internal and external lighting systems

An aircraft carries dozens of lamps, grouped by who uses them: the crew in the cockpit, the people in the cabin, and the outside world that must see the aircraft. Each group gets its own supply, its own switch and its own failure rule. Navigation lights tell another aircraft which way this one points; egress lights must work when everything else is dead. This chapter places each light, names its supply and control, and shows how to check that an emergency pack has enough energy for the lamps it feeds.

You need: 3.1 for busbars and essential buses, and 3.3 for battery capacity ratings.

Three groups, three sets of rules

Every lamp belongs to one of three groups, decided by who needs it. The crew must read instruments and charts without losing night vision. Passengers need general light, a reading light and a clear path to the door. The outside world — another aircraft, a marshaller — must see where this aircraft is and which way it points.

The group decides the supply. Most lamps are small and run on 28 V DC from a DC bus. Large cabin fittings and some landing lights need 115 V AC at 400 Hz from an AC bus, covered in 3.2. Emergency egress lights are the exception: each is fed from a self-contained battery pack charged from an essential bus, so it still burns when every generator and every main bus is dead.

A rheostat dims a lamp by putting resistance in series with it. That wastes power as heat and shifts the colour. Pulse-width control switches the lamp fully on and off at high frequency and varies the on-time fraction; the eye sees only the average, and very little power is lost.

I = P ÷ V

I is the lamp current in amperes (A), P is the lamp power in watts (W), V is the supply voltage in volts (V). Lamps in parallel add their powers first. For AC lamps use I = P ÷ (V × cos φ), where cos φ is the power factor.

Cockpit and cabin lighting

  • Flood lights: white lamps above and behind the crew, washing the whole panel; run dim at night.
  • Integral panel lights: built into panel and pedestal, back-lit through engraved legends so every label reads without glare.
  • Chart light: a focused swivel lamp over the chart table.
  • Dome light: general white light in the ceiling, for finding things rather than reading instruments.
  • Standby light: a small lamp on its own battery, so the crew is never in total darkness after a bus failure.

The cabin has ceiling and sidewall lights along the tube, a reading light in each passenger service unit, galley and lavatory lights, and illuminated signs. One cabin lighting control panel switches groups and sets brightness; some syllabi demonstrate this panel on a cabin-lighting training rig. Cabin lights are split across buses, so one failed bus darkens part of the tube, never all of it.

Emergency egress lighting and its packs

  • Exit signs at every door, lit red or white and marked EXIT.
  • Floor-proximity lights: a strip about 0.3 m above the floor leading to each exit, so a person crawling under smoke can follow them.
  • External escape lights that illuminate the slide or overwing area once the door is open.
  • The emergency mode of the cockpit standby light.

The source is a set of self-contained battery packs spread through the cabin. Each pack holds sealed cells, a charger, a changeover relay and the feeds to its lamps. In normal flight the pack floats on an essential bus, which keeps the cells charged, and an arming switch in the cockpit puts the system in standby. When the essential bus loses power, or the crew selects emergency, the relay drops and the pack feeds the lamps. Endurance is short, of the order of 10 to 15 minutes — enough to evacuate. A pack is rated by both capacity in ampere-hours (Ah) and maximum discharge current in amperes (A); both limits must be checked against the connected lamps.

External lights

  • Navigation (position) lights: red on the left wingtip (port), green on the right wingtip (starboard), white at the tail. Red and green together mean the aircraft is coming towards you; red alone means its left side is in view. Each colour shows through a defined arc, about 110° for red and green and about 140° for white, so the lamps sit in shaped housings.
  • Anti-collision lights: a red beacon, rotating or flashing, on top and bottom of the fuselage, plus white strobes at the wingtips giving a short, very bright double flash. They go on before engine start.
  • Landing lights: high-intensity lamps in the wing root or on the nose gear, aimed forward. Taxi lights are wider and less intense.
  • Logo lights on the horizontal stabiliser light up the tail; wing or engine scan lights let the crew inspect leading edges for ice at night.

Landing and taxi lights may be 115 V AC or 28 V DC by type; a 600 W lamp on 115 V draws about 5.2 A. Strobe tubes are bright enough to hurt close-up, so they are often inhibited on the ground, which is control logic rather than a lamp job.

Worked example

Question. A cabin emergency lighting pack is rated 24 V, 4 Ah, with a maximum discharge current of 6 A. It must run 12 exit-sign lamps at 6 W each and 20 floor-proximity lights at 3 W each for 12 minutes. Find the total load power, the load current, the charge and energy taken, and the fraction of the pack rating used. Does the pack hold?

  1. Exit-sign load: P1 = 12 × 6 W = 72 W.
  2. Floor-proximity load: P2 = 20 × 3 W = 60 W.
  3. Total load power: P = 72 W + 60 W = 132 W.
  4. Load current: I = P ÷ V = 132 ÷ 24 = 5.5 A. This is below the 6 A maximum, so the current is within rating.
  5. Discharge time t = 12 minutes = 12 ÷ 60 h = 0.2 h.
  6. Charge taken: Q = I × t = 5.5 A × 0.2 h = 1.1 Ah. Energy taken: E = Q × V = 1.1 Ah × 24 V = 26.4 Wh.
  7. Fraction of rating: 1.1 ÷ 4 = 0.275, that is 27.5 %.
  8. Endurance at full load: 4 Ah ÷ 5.5 A = 0.727 h ≈ 43.6 min, well over the 12 minutes needed. This is an upper bound; at 5.5 A the usable capacity is below 4 Ah, so true endurance is shorter, but still far above 12 minutes.

Answer. The pack holds: load current is 5.5 A against a 6 A limit, and 1.1 Ah of its 4 Ah capacity (27.5 %) is used in the 12 minutes, delivering 26.4 Wh of energy.

Exam traps

  • Swapping the navigation lights. Red is on the left (port) wingtip, green on the right (starboard), white at the tail. This is the commonest marking error.
  • Putting egress lights on a normal AC bus. They must work when every generator and main bus is lost, so they run from dedicated packs charged from an essential bus.
  • Confusing ampere-hours with amperes. A 4 Ah pack is not limited to 4 A; check capacity and maximum discharge current separately.
  • Forgetting the hour conversion. Twelve minutes is 0.2 h; a missing ÷60 gives an answer 60 times too large.
  • Treating a beacon and a strobe as the same lamp. The beacon is a rotating or flashing red anti-collision light; the strobe is a bright white double-flash tube at the wingtip.

3.6 Packaging, cooling and EMI/EMC requirements

An avionics box must work in a hot, vibrating bay full of electrical noise. Packaging fixes its shape and connectors so a line replaceable unit (LRU) can be swapped on the ramp. Cooling carries heat to air or liquid. Electromagnetic interference/electromagnetic compatibility (EMI/EMC) limits what the box emits and tolerates. Only a few universities teach this chapter. The same ideas appear on every aircraft.

You need first-year heat transfer (thermal resistance); no earlier chapter is required, though 3.1 shows where the supply comes from.

Packaging: LRU, rack and connector

An LRU is a box that can be removed and replaced as one unit without opening it. It slides into a rack or a front-loading tray. Hold-down clamps lock it against vibration. Blind connectors at the rear mate when the tray is pushed home, so no hand tools are needed on the ramp.

Civil cases follow ARINC 404 or ARINC 600. These standards fix case dimensions and connector positions, so boxes from different makers fit the same rack. Military (DOD) types use similar ideas with harsher environmental limits. Weight and volume matter because every kilogram costs fuel; access matters because a faulty box must come out in minutes. The connector carries power and signals. Keying prevents a wrong box from entering a tray. The case also provides the shield and the heat path, so its metalwork is part of both the electrical and thermal design.

Cooling: junction to air

Every watt lost as heat raises temperature. Natural convection suits small boxes. Larger bays use forced air, a cold plate, or a liquid loop. A cold plate is a metal plate with internal passages; the LRU mounts flat on it and heat flows by conduction. Liquid loops carry heat to a fuel-cooled or air-cooled heat exchanger. Cold-plate mounting needs a flat, clamped surface; a poor contact adds resistance.

Thermal resistance Rth is the temperature rise per watt, in kelvin per watt (K/W). For one path:

Tcase = Tair + P × Rth

Tcase is case temperature in °C; Tair is cooling-air inlet temperature in °C; P is heat in W; Rth is case-to-air resistance in K/W.

Inside the box add junction-to-case resistance: Tj = Tcase + P × Rjc. The limit is the hottest semiconductor junction, not the box surface. Halving airflow raises Rth, often doubling it, so the same power gives a hotter case.

EMI/EMC: emissions and susceptibility

EMI is unwanted electrical energy. EMC means the box works in that environment without disturbing others. Conducted emission travels along wires; radiated emission travels through air. Susceptibility is how easily the box is disturbed, by conducted or radiated paths.

Four controls work together. Suppress at the source with filters and ferrites. Route signals as screened twisted pairs and keep power cables away from signal cables. Screened means a conductive layer around the pair; twisted means the two wires swap positions along the run, so pickup cancels. Filter at the connector with feed-through capacitors. Shield the enclosure with conductive gaskets, then bond it to the airframe with a low-impedance path. Bonding is a direct metal connection; grounding is the reference return.

RTCA DO-160 covers environmental and EMI tests; MIL-STD-461 covers military EMI. The box must pass both emission and susceptibility limits. A shield with a poor bond is an antenna.

Worked example

Question. An LRU dissipates 120 W. Its cold plate has a thermal resistance of 0.15 K/W to the cooling air, and the inlet air is at 30 °C. (a) Find the case temperature. (b) The airflow is halved and the resistance doubles; the case limit is 70 °C. Is there still margin?

  1. Given P = 120 W, Rth = 0.15 K/W, Tair = 30 °C.
  2. Temperature rise = P × Rth = 120 × 0.15 = 18 K.
  3. Tcase = 30 + 18 = 48 °C.
  4. New Rth = 2 × 0.15 = 0.30 K/W; rise = 120 × 0.30 = 36 K.
  5. New Tcase = 30 + 36 = 66 °C. Margin = 70 − 66 = 4 K.

Answer. The case temperature is 48 °C; with halved airflow it is 66 °C, leaving 4 °C of margin.

Exam traps

  • Bench pass does not guarantee bay pass: reduced airflow raises Rth and the case temperature.
  • Conducted emission leaves through wires; radiated emission leaves through air. Do not swap the words.
  • Temperature rise is P × Rth, not Rth ÷ P; use W and K/W to get K.
  • Shielding alone is not EMC; a poor bond, unfiltered connector or badly routed power cable still fails.

3.7 Air data instruments: airspeed indicator and altimeter

An aircraft knows its speed and height by measuring pressure. A pitot tube faces the airflow and senses total pressure; a static port on the side senses the surrounding pressure. Their difference drives the airspeed indicator, while static pressure alone drives the altimeter. Both are simple mechanical computers, but their readings depend on calibration and on the pressure setting the pilot chooses. Pilots use them every take-off, climb, cruise and landing, from a short circuit to a long-haul cruise, so reading them correctly is a core cockpit skill.

You need basic pressure and density ideas from physics; no earlier chapter is needed.

Pitot-static ports and the airspeed indicator

A pitot tube points into the relative airflow. Its open end brings air to rest and senses total pressure P0. A static port sits flush on the fuselage side and senses the surrounding static pressure P. The difference is impact pressure q:

q = P0 − P = ½ ρ V²

q is impact pressure in Pa; P0 is total pressure in Pa; P is static pressure in Pa; ρ is air density in kg/m³; V is the speed for which that impact pressure is produced at density ρ.

q = ½ρV² holds for incompressible flow, roughly below Mach 0.3 (about 200 kt at sea level); at higher speed the airspeed indicator needs a compressibility correction.

In the airspeed indicator, a thin capsule feels P0 on one side and P on the other. It expands with q, and a linkage moves the needle. The dial is calibrated at sea-level ISA density, ρ0 = 1.225 kg/m³. Rearranging q = ½ρV² gives V = √(2q/ρ). Because the dial is calibrated at ρ0, a given q always shows the same indicated speed. In thin air ρ is smaller, so the same q is produced by a higher true speed: TAS is greater than IAS, and the gap grows with altitude.

The dial shows indicated airspeed (IAS). Calibrated airspeed (CAS) is IAS corrected for position and installation error. True airspeed (TAS) is CAS corrected for air density, and at high Mach number also for compressibility. The coloured arcs are IAS limits: white for flap operation, green for normal, yellow for caution, and the red radial for never-exceed speed Vne.

Altimeter and pressure altitude

The altimeter uses a sealed aneroid capsule with a near vacuum inside. As static pressure falls, the capsule expands; as pressure rises, it compresses. A linkage turns the pointer. The static port is its only pressure input, so a blocked static line freezes the reading.

The subscale knob sets the reference pressure in the small window. Set QNH, the aerodrome pressure reduced to sea level, and on the ground the altimeter reads aerodrome elevation. Set 1013.25 hPa and it reads pressure altitude. Near sea level, h ≈ (Pref − P) × 27 ft/hPa; Pref is the set pressure, P is outside static pressure and h is height.

Pressure altitude is not true altitude. ISA pressure falls about 1 hPa per 27 ft near sea level and temperature falls 6.5 °C/km. In air colder than ISA, pressure levels are compressed and the aircraft is lower than the altimeter says. Flying from high pressure to low pressure also makes the altimeter overread.

Errors and blockage

No pressure instrument is perfect. Position error comes from airflow around the ports and changes with angle of attack and flap. Leaks make readings sluggish or wrong. Lag delays the response. Temperature error matters most for the altimeter. Blockage is the dangerous one. Some pressure-sensing chains also appear in fuel quantity indication, see 3.13.

  • Pitot blocked, static clear: in a climb the trapped P0 stays fixed while P falls, so q rises and the ASI overreads; in a descent it underreads.
  • Static blocked: the ASI, altimeter and VSI lose their true source; the ASI may behave like an altimeter, the altimeter freezes, and the VSI reads zero.
  • Pitot heat is the cure for ice in the pitot tube; it does not clear a blocked static port, so check the static drains.
  • Before flight, remove covers, check ports and drain holes, and cross-check the ASI at the runway.

Worked example

Question. (a) At sea level, where air density is 1.225 kg/m³, an aircraft has an impact pressure of 2500 Pa. Find the indicated airspeed in m/s and in knots. Use 1 m/s = 1.94384 kt. (b) The altimeter subscale is set to 1013.25 hPa and the static pressure outside is 950 hPa. Find the pressure altitude, using 27 ft per hPa.

  1. (a) Use q = ½ρV², so V = √(2q/ρ).
  2. 2q = 2 × 2500 = 5000 Pa.
  3. 2q/ρ = 5000 / 1.225 = 4081.63 m²/s².
  4. V = √4081.63 = 63.89 m/s ≈ 63.9 m/s.
  5. In knots: 63.89 × 1.94384 = 124.2 kt.
  6. (b) Pressure difference = 1013.25 − 950 = 63.25 hPa.
  7. Height = 63.25 × 27 = 1707.75 ft ≈ 1707.8 ft, about 1710 ft.

Answer. The airspeed is about 63.9 m/s (124 kt), and the pressure altitude is about 1708 ft (roughly 1710 ft).

Exam traps

  • Mixing up the ports: the pitot port feeds only the airspeed capsule, while the static port feeds the airspeed capsule, altimeter and vertical speed indicator.
  • Reading indicated airspeed as true airspeed. At altitude, the same IAS corresponds to a higher TAS because the air density is lower.
  • Forgetting that the altimeter reads height above the pressure level set in its subscale, not height above the ground or above sea level unless QNH is set correctly.
  • Thinking a blocked pitot always makes the ASI read zero. With static pressure still changing, a blocked pitot can make the ASI climb when the aircraft climbs.
  • Using 1013.25 hPa as QNH for landing. It gives pressure altitude; QNH must come from the aerodrome pressure report.

3.8 Engine instruments: tachometer and EGT

An engine instrument answers two questions from the cockpit: how fast is the engine turning, and how hot is its exhaust? A tachometer turns shaft speed into a voltage or a pulse frequency. An exhaust gas temperature (EGT) system turns exhaust heat into a small thermocouple voltage. Both signals are conditioned and shown as a number or a needle. Pilots use them at start, take-off and cruise to stay inside engine limits, and maintenance uses them to spot drift before a fault becomes a failure.

You need basic electrical measurement: voltage, current, frequency, and the millivolt as a small voltage; see 3.1 for aircraft supply and 3.13 for the other engine indicators.

Tachometer: speed becomes a signal

Engine speed is measured in revolutions per minute (rpm), but the cockpit usually shows a percentage. N1 is the speed of the fan or low-pressure spool. N2 is the speed of the core or high-pressure spool. 100% is a reference speed picked by the engine manufacturer; it is not the same rpm on every engine.

A mechanical tachometer uses a flexible drive shaft from the engine gearbox to the indicator. The indicator is a small generator or a drag-cup mechanism. This is direct and needs no power, but the long shaft adds friction, lag and a path for oil leaks.

A DC tacho-generator is a small generator driven by the engine shaft. Its output voltage rises with speed: V_tacho = k × rpm. k is the generator constant in volts per rpm. The voltage is easy to measure, but brushes and commutator wear.

A magnetic or variable-reluctance pickup is better for modern engines. A coil sits close to a toothed wheel on the shaft. Each tooth changes the magnetic reluctance and induces one pulse. The indicator counts frequency, so there is no contact and no brush wear.

f = (rpm ÷ 60) × N

f is pulse frequency in hertz (pulses per second); rpm is shaft speed in revolutions per minute; N is the number of teeth on the wheel.

Exhaust gas temperature: heat becomes millivolts

An exhaust gas temperature (EGT) system uses a thermocouple. Two wires of different metals are joined at the hot junction, which sits in the exhaust. The other ends form the cold junction at the indicator or in a compensating circuit. A temperature difference between the junctions produces a small emf, called the Seebeck voltage. Type K uses chromel and alumel and has a sensitivity S of about 41 microvolt per degree Celsius.

V_TC = S × (T_hot − T_cold)

V_TC is thermocouple voltage (µV); S is sensitivity (about 41 µV/°C for type K); T_hot is hot-junction temperature (°C); T_cold is cold-junction temperature (°C).

The equation shows why the cold junction cannot be ignored: the sensor reports a difference, not an absolute temperature. Cold-junction compensation adds a voltage equal to S × T_cold, or holds the cold junction at a known temperature. The compensated signal goes to a high-gain amplifier, then to an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) or a moving-coil meter. An amplifier with gain G gives V_out = G × V_TC.

The display shows EGT. Some engines call the same turbine measurement TGT, turbine gas temperature. Piston engines often show cylinder head temperature (CHT), which is a different place and a different limit.

Reading limits and cross-checks

Three readings matter. At start, EGT rises to a peak and then falls as the engine stabilises. A hot start crosses the start limit; the crew shuts down. At take-off, EGT and N1/N2 are read together. On a hot day the EGT limit may be reached before the thrust target, so the limit sets take-off power. In cruise, a slow EGT drift at the same N1 can show a dirty compressor or a drifting sensor.

Do not mix N1 and N2. N1 is the fan or LP spool; N2 is the core or HP spool. Their 100% references are different speeds. A reading of 90% N2 is not 90% of N1, and it is not 90% of any universal rpm. To turn percent into rpm, use the reference speed in the engine manual.

The tachometer and EGT chains are separate until the display. A failed pickup usually gives no pulse or a noisy frequency; a failed thermocouple gives an open circuit or a fixed low reading. The cockpit may show both on one engine display, but the sensors, wires and conditioning are separate. Other engine indications are covered in 3.13; see Unit 4, Avionics Architecture, Displays and Data Buses, for digital display and data buses.

Worked example

Question. A magnetic pickup faces a gear with 30 teeth on the HP shaft of a turbofan. The indicator reads 9000 rpm. (a) Find the pulse frequency in hertz.   • Assuming the cold junction is always at 0 °C; in an aircraft it sits near the instrument or in a compensating circuit and must be compensated.

3.9 Voltage regulators: vibrating-contact, carbon-pile and electronic

A generator's output voltage is not fixed. It rises as the shaft speeds up and falls as the load current rises. The cause is the field: generated emf depends on field flux and speed. A voltage regulator senses the output, compares it with a reference, and adjusts field current to hold voltage steady. Aircraft use three hardware forms: the vibrating-contact regulator, the carbon-pile regulator, and the electronic regulator. The first two are older; the electronic type is standard in generator control units.

You need: 3.1 for the generator, its field winding and the generator control unit.

The field is the control knob

Generated emf in a DC generator is E = k Φ ω. E is the generated emf in volts, k is a machine constant, Φ is the field flux per pole in webers, and ω is angular speed in radians per second. For a given field current, Φ is roughly constant. Then E rises in direct proportion to speed. At the terminals the voltage is lower than E by the armature resistance drop and by armature reaction. So terminal voltage falls as load current rises.

The regulator cannot change speed. It changes field current I_f. A small change in I_f gives a large change in Φ, and so in E. The loop is closed: sense terminal voltage, compare with a reference, drive the field. The quality measure is voltage regulation.

regulation = (V_no-load − V_full-load) ÷ V_full-load × 100

V_no-load is the terminal voltage at zero load in volts; V_full-load is the terminal voltage at rated load in volts; the result is a percentage. Smaller is better.

Vibrating-contact and carbon-pile regulators

Vibrating-contact regulator. A solenoid coil is connected across the generator output. A spring holds a contact closed. While the contact is closed, full field current flows. When output voltage exceeds the setting, the solenoid pulls the contact open. A series resistance then enters the field circuit, so field current falls and voltage falls. The contact closes again. The cycle repeats many times a second. The average field current is set by the duty cycle D, the fraction of time the contact is closed. Accuracy is about ±1.5 %. The contact arcs, so it wears and makes radio noise. Maintenance is needed.

Carbon-pile regulator. A stack of carbon discs sits between two plates. Its resistance falls as pressure rises. A solenoid varies the pressure, and the pile is in series with the field. Higher voltage pulls the solenoid, reduces pressure, raises pile resistance, and lowers field current. There are no contacts, so there is no wear and no arcing. The pile is slow and its resistance changes with temperature, so accuracy is limited. It was used where smooth control mattered more than speed.

Electronic regulators and comparison

An electronic regulator uses a Zener diode as the reference. A sensing divider scales the output down. An error amplifier compares the scaled output with the Zener voltage. Its output drives a transistor, a thyristor, or a pulse-width modulator that switches the field. Pulse-width control gives a duty cycle D, and the average field current is D × I_field(max). The loop is fast and has no moving parts. Accuracy is better than ±0.5 %. The same unit adds over-voltage and under-voltage cut-out, over-excitation limiting, and reverse-current protection. Cut-out removes the generator from the bus; it does not regulate voltage.

Question. A 28 V DC generator has a field resistance of 10 Ω. The regulator inserts a 20 Ω series resistance when its contact opens. The average field current needed is 1.5 A.   1. Contact closed: field current I_closed = 28 V ÷ 10 Ω = 2.80 A.
  2. Contact open: total field circuit resistance = 10 Ω + 20 Ω = 30 Ω. I_open = 28 V ÷ 30 Ω = 0.933 A.
  3. Average field current = D I_closed + (1 − D) I_open. Put 1.50 A = D(2.80 A) + (1 − D)(0.933 A).

3.10 Wires, cables and circuit protection: fuses, current limiters and circuit breakers

Every wire is a resistor with a current limit. It carries its load current with a small voltage drop, and it survives a fault current only for a short time before insulation or copper fails. Protection devices—fuses, current limiters, limiting resistors and circuit breakers—break or limit that fault current before the wire is damaged. The device is chosen from the wire's derated rating, not from the load alone. This rule sets feeder sizes, breaker panels and reset actions on every aircraft electrical bus.

You need: 3.1 for the busbar and feeder arrangement that connects sources to loads.

Wire construction, gauge and derating

Aircraft wire uses stranded copper, not solid copper, because vibration and repeated bending crack a solid conductor. Strands are often silver plated for soldering and corrosion resistance, or nickel plated for hot engine and galley areas. Insulation is chosen by temperature and fire behaviour: PVC for cool protected areas, PTFE for high temperature, Kapton for thin lightweight harnesses, and glass braid where abrasion is severe. The gauge is given in AWG (American Wire Gauge). A lower AWG number means a thicker conductor and a higher current rating; 8 AWG is much thicker than 20 AWG.

The bare current rating of a wire is measured in free air. In an aircraft it must be derated for three effects: bundle size (many wires trapped in one loom cannot shed heat), altitude (thin air cools less), and ambient temperature (a hot bay adds heat). A wire that is safe at 60 A alone may be limited to 40 A in a dense hot bundle. Voltage drop is a second limit. Feeder drop is usually held to a few percent of bus voltage, so long runs need thicker wire even when heating is acceptable. The drop for a DC feeder with a return path is V_drop = I × R_total and R_total = 2 × L × r. Here I is load current in A, L is one-way length in m, r is conductor resistance in Ω/m, and the factor 2 counts the return wire.

Wires are marked with circuit identity, clamped at fixed intervals, and bent no tighter than the stated bend radius. Sharp bends crush insulation and strand the copper.

Fuses, current limiters and breakers

A fuse is a one-shot device: a metal element melts when its inverse time-current curve says the current is too high for too long. At slight overload it takes minutes; at a short-circuit it takes milliseconds. A current limiter is a heavy-current fast-acting fuse used on a generator or main feeder. It clears a large fault before the downstream busbars see the full current. A limiting resistor is a resistor in series with a circuit to hold fault current or switch-on inrush current down. It does not break the circuit; it buys time for the breaker or fuse.

A circuit breaker can be reset. The thermal type uses a bimetallic strip heated by load current; the magnetic type uses a solenoid that pulls open on a high current; the thermal-magnetic type combines both, giving a delay on overload and fast action on short circuit. A trip-free breaker cannot be held closed on a fault: the handle moves but the contacts stay open until the mechanism resets. Remote-controlled breakers let the crew open and close a feeder from the cockpit.

Breakers are preferred in flight because a crew can reset one after a transient. That advantage is also a danger: repeated resetting of a thermal breaker into a real fault overheats the wire. A fuse is used where a one-shot clearance is acceptable and resetting is not wanted.

Coordination and selection

Protection is a race between two curves. The wire damage curve gives the time a conductor can carry a given fault current before its insulation or copper is damaged. The device curve gives the time the fuse or breaker takes to open at that current. For every fault current the device curve must lie below the wire damage curve, so the device opens first. Separately, the device must not open at the normal load current. The figure shows this: at 40 A the 20 A breaker clears in about 5 s while the wire damage limit is about 31 s. At 80 A the breaker clears in about 1.25 s and the wire limit is about 7.8 s.

Selection starts with the load current, then checks the wire's derated rating, then the voltage drop. The breaker rating must not exceed the derated wire rating. If a 40 A load needs a 50 A breaker, the wire must have a derated rating of at least 50 A. A 50 A breaker on a wire rated 40 A protects the load but not the wire.

The fault path matters. A short at the end of a long feeder has enough resistance to reduce fault current, so the breaker may trip slowly. The return path, connectors and contactors add resistance. Coordination also covers the upstream device: the downstream breaker should clear a local fault before the generator current limiter opens. Busbar layouts and bus tie logic belong to 3.11; generator feeder settings belong to 3.1.

Worked example

Question. A 28 V DC feeder carries 40 A for 6 m from the bus to a load. The conductor is 8 AWG copper with a resistance of 2.06 mΩ per metre, and the return path doubles the length. (a) Find the voltage drop as a percentage of 28 V. (b) Find the power lost in the cable. (c) Suggest a breaker rating, stating the wire condition.

  1. One-way length L = 6 m. The return path makes total conductor length L_total = 2 × 6 = 12 m.
  2. Resistance per metre r = 2.06 mΩ/m = 0.00206 Ω/m. Total R = r × L_total = 0.00206 × 12 = 0.02472 Ω.
  3. Voltage drop V_drop = I × R = 40 × 0.02472 = 0.9888 V, about 0.99 V.
  4. Percentage drop = (0.9888 ÷ 28) × 100 = 3.53 %. This is above the usual few-percent feeder limit, so 8 AWG is marginal for this 6 m 40 A run.
  5. Power lost P = I² × R = 40² × 0.02472 = 1600 × 0.02472 = 39.552 W, about 39.6 W.
  6. Load current is 40 A, so a 50 A breaker is a practical next standard rating, provided the derated wire rating is at least 50 A. If the derated wire rating is only 40 A, the breaker must be 40 A or the wire made thicker.

Answer. Voltage drop 0.9888 V (3.53 % of 28 V), cable loss 39.552 W. The drop is above the usual few-percent feeder limit, so 8 AWG is marginal here. For part (c), a 50 A breaker only if the derated wire rating is at least 50 A.

3.11 Bus systems: split, parallel and split-parallel; switches and relays

A busbar is a thick metal bar that takes power from a source and hands it to a group of loads. How generators are joined to the busbars — split, parallel or split-parallel — decides whether one fault darkens one bus or the whole aircraft. Switches make and break those joins by hand; relays and contactors make and break them electrically. Every aircraft from a two-seat trainer to a widebody uses one of these three layouts, and the choice is a trade between simplicity, fault containment and redundancy.

You need: 3.1 for generation and protection, 3.9 for the regulator that sets each generator's voltage, and 3.10 for the breakers that feed each load.

Busbar and the split bus

A busbar is a thick copper or aluminium bar. It collects power from one or more sources and hands it to a group of loads. Copper is common; aluminium saves weight on long runs. The bar is sized so its resistance, and the volt drop along it, stay small.

A split bus (also called an isolated bus) gives each generator its own bar. Generator 1 closes onto bus 1 through its generator circuit breaker, written GCB — a heavy-duty contactor built for busbar currents. Generator 2 closes onto bus 2 through its own GCB. The two busbars never touch.

The gain is fault containment. A short on bus 1 trips GCB 1 and the aircraft loses bus 1 only. Nothing needs synchronising, because no two generators are ever connected together. The wiring between the halves stays light and the control logic stays simple.

The price is that a generator failure strands its bus. Bus 1 then runs on the battery (see 3.3) or its DC loads are fed from the other side through a transformer rectifier (see 3.2). A TRU fed from the other side's AC bus can keep DC loads alive, but it cannot replace an AC generator or supply AC loads. A small single-engine aircraft is effectively split, with one generator, one bus and an essential bus tapped off it.

Parallel bus and load sharing

On a parallel bus all generators close onto one bar. Before a GCB closes, the incoming generator's voltage must match the bus; on AC, frequency and phase must match as well. That is the synchronism check, done by a synchroscope or by an automatic paralleling unit. Closing out of phase drives a large circulating current through both machines.

Once closed, the machines stay in step and share the load. Sharing is not arranged by setting exactly equal no-load voltages. Real machines have droop: terminal voltage falls as current rises.

V = V₀ − kI

V is the terminal voltage in volts, V₀ is the no-load voltage in volts, k is the droop coefficient in volts per ampere, and I is the generator current in amperes.

Two generators on one bus must sit at the same terminal voltage, so for equal no-load voltages the one with the smaller k takes the larger current. With unequal no-load voltages, solve V₀ − kI for both machines. Deliberate droop of a few percent from no-load to full load makes sharing predictable and stable. A load controller can also trim field currents to equalise the share. On AC systems the constant-speed drive holds frequency at 400 Hz, the engine governor sets kilowatt sharing, and the regulator sets reactive sharing.

Split-parallel bus

Split-parallel is the normal state of most twin-engine transports. The buses are split and each generator carries its own side, so fault containment is kept. A bus tie breaker, written BTB, sits between the two buses and stays open in normal flight.

When one generator drops offline its GCB opens and the BTB closes, so the surviving generator feeds both buses. Non-essential loads — galleys, cabin sockets, entertainment — must be shed first, because one generator is rarely rated for the whole aircraft load. A bus power control unit can do this automatically after a short delay.

Normal flight behaves like a split bus; a failure behaves like a parallel bus. A single-engine aircraft cannot do this. An essential bus can also be fed from both sides through diodes, so it never loses power whichever side survives.

Switches, relays and contactors

A switch is operated by hand. Manual cockpit types are the toggle switch, which stays where it is set; the push-button, which is momentary and springs back; and the rotary selector, with many positions. Two other devices are switched by movement rather than by hand: the microswitch, a tiny snap-action device actuated by a lever or strut as a limit switch on a door or gear; and the proximity switch, a non-contact sensor that detects a metal target and reports gear up or down.

A relay is an electrically operated switch. A small current in the coil makes a magnetic field that pulls the armature and moves the contacts. Normally open (NO) contacts close when the coil is energised; normally closed (NC) contacts open. A latching relay holds its last state with no coil current. A contactor is a heavy-duty relay for busbars and generators — same principle, bigger contacts, with arc quenching.

A contact rating must cover the making and breaking current, not only the steady current. A DC coil needs a suppression diode across it: when the coil is switched off, its inductance drives a large reverse voltage that would pit the controlling contacts. The diode gives that stored energy a path. A solid-state power controller (SSPC) replaces the relay with a MOSFET plus current sensing, so it can control and protect the wire in one device (see 3.10).

Worked example

Question. Two 28 V DC generators run in parallel on a 300 A bus load. Each has a no-load voltage of 28.5 V. Generator 1 droops 0.4 V per 100 A and generator 2 droops 0.6 V per 100 A. Find the current in each generator and the bus voltage.

  1. Convert each droop to volts per ampere: k₁ = 0.4 ÷ 100 = 0.004 V/A and k₂ = 0.6 ÷ 100 = 0.006 V/A.
  2. In parallel the terminal voltages are equal: 28.5 − 0.004 I₁ = 28.5 − 0.006 I₂.
  3. Cancel the 28.5 V on both sides: 0.004 I₁ = 0.006 I₂, so I₁ = 1.5 I₂.
  4. The load must balance the two currents: I₁ + I₂ = 300 A, so 1.5 I₂ + I₂ = 2.5 I₂ = 300 A, giving I₂ = 120 A.
  5. Then I₁ = 300 − 120 = 180 A.
  6. Bus voltage V = 28.5 − 0.004 × 180 = 28.5 − 0.72 = 27.78 V.
  7. Check on generator 2: 28.5 − 0.006 × 120 = 28.5 − 0.72 = 27.78 V. The two agree.

Answer. Generator 1 carries 180 A, generator 2 carries 120 A, and the bus voltage is 27.78 V.

Exam traps

  • Assuming parallel generators must have exactly equal no-load voltages. They share by droop, so equal set points with unequal slopes still give unequal currents.
  • Paralleling AC generators without a synchronism check. Closing out of phase gives a huge circulating current and can damage the drive.
  • Confusing the generator circuit breaker (GCB) with the bus tie breaker (BTB): the GCB joins one generator to its own bus, the BTB joins the two buses.
  • Treating a split bus as redundancy. It contains faults, but a lost generator leaves its bus on the battery or a TRU, not on the other generator.
  • Leaving out the suppression diode across a DC relay coil, so the inductive kickback pits the controlling switch contacts.

3.12 Power system architecture: A320 and B787 case studies

The A320 and the B787 sit at opposite ends of civil power-system design. The A320 keeps a fixed-frequency 115 V AC (alternating current) supply at 400 Hz and a 28 V DC (direct current) supply from transformer rectifier units. The B787 uses a more-electric, bleedless layout: 235 V AC at variable frequency and 270 V DC. The reason for the higher voltage is cable weight and load growth. For the same power, a higher voltage needs less current, so thinner cables and lower copper loss. These two aircraft show how architecture choices follow from voltage, frequency and conversion.

You need: 3.1 for generation and protection, 3.2 for conversion, and 3.11 for bus arrangements.

A320: fixed frequency, two voltage levels

A320: fixed frequency, two voltage levels. The A320 has two engine-driven integrated drive generators. An integrated drive generator (IDG) is a generator with a built-in constant-speed drive, so the generator turns at a speed that gives 115 V AC at 400 Hz even when the engine speed changes. Each IDG is rated 90 kVA. The rating is in kVA, apparent power, not kW. A generator control unit (GCU) regulates the generator field, monitors faults and controls the generator contactor that connects the IDG to its AC bus. A contactor is a heavy-duty relay. A bus tie contactor can join the two main AC buses when one generator is off.

An auxiliary power unit (APU) generator of similar size supplies AC power on the ground and in flight as a backup. In an all-generator failure, a ram air turbine (RAT) drives a small emergency generator for essential loads; see 3.4. Transformer rectifier units (TRUs) convert 115 V AC to 28 V DC for DC buses and battery charging. Nickel-cadmium batteries supply DC power before engine start and bridge short interruptions. The result is a conventional split bus system, with AC and DC essential buses kept alive by contactor logic.

B787: variable frequency and more electric

B787: variable frequency and more electric. The B787 removes engine bleed air for cabin pressurisation and uses electrical power for more loads. Each engine carries two large starter-generators, each rated 250 kVA. A starter-generator is a machine that starts the engine and then generates electricity. It produces 235 V AC at variable frequency, about 360 Hz to 800 Hz, as engine speed changes. No constant-speed drive is fitted, so the AC bus frequency is not fixed.

Two APU starter-generators supply ground power and backup. Transformer rectifier units convert 235 V AC to 270 V DC. Lithium-ion batteries provide emergency and ground power. The RAT supplies essential buses when all normal sources fail. Every load on the variable-frequency bus must accept the full 360-800 Hz range; this is the key design rule. 3.13 Engine indicators and fuel quantity indication

Engine indicators turn pressure, flow, torque and vibration into readings the crew can act on. Speed and exhaust temperature tell how fast and hot the engine runs; oil pressure, fuel pressure, fuel flow, torque and vibration tell whether it is healthy and how much fuel it is burning. Fuel quantity is the hardest indication because the fuel sits in a tank that tilts, heats and cools, and changes density. This chapter covers the sensors, the capacitance probe, and the cross-checks that keep the quantity figure honest.

You need: 3.8 for the engine indicator chain, and first-year capacitance, C = εA/d.

Pressure, flow, torque and vibration

A Bourdon tube is a curved metal tube that straightens as pressure inside rises. Its free end moves a linkage and the pointer. It needs no electricity, so light aircraft use it for oil and fuel pressure. Larger aircraft use a pressure transducer: a diaphragm or strain gauge bends and changes resistance or voltage.

IndicatorSensorWhat it shows
Oil pressureBourdon tube or transducerLubrication health
Fuel pressureBourdon tube or transducerPump and filter health
Fuel flowTurbine or paddlewheelkg per hour
TorqueTorque sensorShaft power, turboprop
VibrationAccelerometerImbalance or bearing damage

Fuel flow is a rate, not a total. A turbine flowmeter has a rotor spun by the fuel; its speed follows flow. A paddlewheel meter gives pulses as blades pass a pickup. The display converts pulses to kilograms per hour. Torque is the useful output of a turboprop; a torque sensor measures gearbox twist or reaction and shows percentage or shaft power. Vibration comes from an accelerometer on the casing; a rising level warns of imbalance or bearing damage.

Primary indicators are the ones needed to set power safely: N1 fan speed, exhaust gas temperature (EGT), and on some types N2 core speed. Secondary indicators — oil pressure, oil temperature, fuel flow, vibration — give health and trend. Starting indicators are separate: starter valve open, ignition on, and starter cut-out at the right speed. The engine display groups these readings for the crew.

Fuel quantity: float and capacitance

On a light aircraft the tank has a float on an arm. The arm moves a rheostat, a variable resistor, and the current through it moves the gauge. It is simple and cheap. It also has moving parts, and the float can stick.

Transport aircraft use capacitance gauging. A probe in the tank is a capacitor: two plates, or two concentric tubes, with fuel or air between them. Fuel has a relative permittivity εr of about 2.0; air is 1.0. As fuel rises between the plates, it replaces air and the capacitance rises. A bridge circuit measures the capacitance and converts it to volume or mass.

C = ε0 εr A / d

C is capacitance in farads (F); ε0 = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m is the permittivity of free space; εr is the relative permittivity of the material between the plates; A is plate area in m²; d is plate separation in m.

A real probe is not fully full or fully empty. The fuel fills part of the height. Model it as two capacitors in parallel: the air-filled part and the fuel-filled part. Then C = C_empty + (C_full − C_empty)(h/H), where h is fuel height and H is total height between the plates. For the example probe, C_empty is about 35 pF and C_full about 71 pF.

The tank unit sends capacitance to a bridge. A compensator unit, a small probe kept fully flooded, measures the fuel's dielectric constant. The bridge uses it to correct the reading when fuel temperature or fuel type changes εr. To show kilograms, the system applies density: mass = volume × density. The refuelling panel lets the crew set a target in kilograms; valves close when the target is reached. Drip sticks are manual dipsticks used as an independent check.

Errors and cross-checks

Attitude changes the reading. In a climb, fuel runs to the rear of the tank; the probe sees a different local level from the true quantity. In a steep turn the same happens sideways. Transport systems use multiple probes and compensation, but a large attitude error can remain until the aircraft is level. Temperature changes both density and εr. A cold refuel gives more kilograms in the same volume; a warm tank gives fewer. Fuel type matters: different jet fuels have slightly different dielectric constants. The compensator corrects εr, but density still needs a temperature or density input.

Fuel flow integration is the cross-check. Total fuel used = fuel flow × time, summed over the flight. Subtract that from the starting quantity and compare with the gauge. If the gauge stays still while flow continues, the probe or bridge is suspect. If the gauge falls faster than flow used, a leak or a density error is possible. The check does not replace the gauge; it catches a stuck or biased indication.

m_used = Σ (fuel flow × Δt)

m_used is total fuel used in kilograms; fuel flow is in kilograms per second; Δt is each time step in seconds; the sum runs over the flight.

Worked example

Question. A capacitance fuel probe has parallel plates of area 0.02 m² separated by 5 mm. The relative permittivity is 1.0 for air and 2.0 for fuel; ε0 = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m. Fuel fills the plate height from the bottom. Find the capacitance when the tank is empty, half full and full. Give each answer in picofarads.

  1. Convert d = 5 mm = 0.005 m. Empty tank: only air between the plates. C_empty = ε0 A / d = (8.854 × 10⁻¹² × 0.02) / 0.005 = 3.5416 × 10⁻¹¹ F = 35.416 pF.
  2. Full tank: fuel replaces air, so multiply by εr = 2.0. C_full = 2.0 × 35.416 = 70.832 pF.
  3. Half full: half the plate area sees air and half sees fuel, two capacitors in parallel. C_half = (35.416 + 70.832) / 2 = 53.124 pF.
  4. Rounded to one decimal place: 35.4 pF, 53.1 pF and 70.8 pF. Each half of fuel height adds about 17.7 pF.

Answer. C_empty = 35.4 pF, C_half = 53.1 pF, C_full = 70.8 pF.

Exam traps

  • Capacitance is not proportional to tank volume in an irregular tank; it follows the wetted height and area between the plates.
  • Empty probe capacitance is not zero. Air gives about 35 pF in the example; zero would mean a short or an open circuit.
  • Do not mix fuel flow (kg/h) with fuel quantity (kg). The gauge shows total; flow must be integrated over time.
  • The dielectric constant of fuel is not fixed. Temperature and fuel type shift it, and the compensator exists for that; density also shifts.
  • A quantity gauge can be correct in litres and wrong in kilograms if density is wrong; read the units.

Takeaways

  1. A generator is only useful once its voltage is regulated, its output is switched to a busbar, and its faults are cleared before wiring overheats.
  2. Converters exist because different loads need different forms of power: 115 V AC 400 Hz, 28 V DC, and on newer aircraft 235 V AC variable frequency and 270 V DC.
  3. The battery starts the engine or APU and bridges the gap between losing all generators and getting another source online.
  4. The ram air turbine and the permanent magnet generator are the last line of defence, and ETOPS rules decide how much time that line must buy.
  5. Aircraft lights divide by user (crew, cabin, outside world), and each group gets its own supply, control and failure requirement.
  6. An avionics box must be packaged to a standard form, cooled by a defined path, and designed to meet interference limits, or it will not be fitted to an aircraft.
  7. The airspeed indicator reads total minus static pressure; the altimeter reads static pressure alone.
  8. Voltage regulators all do one thing: compare output with a reference and move field current to close the gap.
  9. Protection is chosen from the derated rating of the wire, not from the load current, otherwise the wire becomes the fuse.
  10. Bus architecture decides how much aircraft is lost when one generator or one bus fails.

Exam question bank

Long answers here are marked on the chain from source to load: generation, regulation, conversion, distribution, protection, and the failure case that each architecture is designed to survive.

Remember and understand

Understand Explain how a generator's output reaches an aircraft load. Cover the busbar, the generator contactor, and over-voltage and under-voltage protection. 12 marks

Generator output rectified or regulated to the bus voltage; Generator contactor connects output to the busbar only when voltage and frequency are inside limits; Over-voltage trip opens the contactor before loads are damaged; Under-voltage trip prevents a failed generator from dragging the bus down; Busbar feeds each load through its own fuse or circuit breaker.

Understand Compare lead-acid and nickel-cadmium aircraft batteries: construction, cell voltage, charge and discharge behaviour, and where each is used. 10 marks

Lead-acid: about 2 V per cell, lead and lead-dioxide plates, dilute sulphuric acid; NiCd: about 1.2 V per cell, nickel hydroxide and cadmium plates, alkaline electrolyte; NiCd has lower internal resistance, so higher cranking current; Lead-acid is cheaper and simpler but heavier for the same capacity; NiCd tolerates deep discharge better; both need venting and temperature control.

Remember Describe the internal and external lighting of a transport aircraft. State the supply and control for each group. 12 marks

Exterior: navigation red/green/white, anti-collision beacons, strobes, landing and taxi lights; Flight compartment: panel flood, integral post lights, map light, dimming control; Cabin: ceiling and sidewall lighting, emergency floor-path lighting on its own battery supply; Emergency exit signs and floor path lights must survive loss of main power; Dimming by variable resistance or pulse-width control.

Understand Explain the construction and working of a vibrating-contact voltage regulator, and state its main drawbacks. 10 marks

Electromagnet coil across the generator output pulls an armature; Armature carries a contact that opens and closes the field circuit; Field current is chopped; average field current falls as voltage rises; Spring tension sets the regulated voltage; Drawbacks: contact wear, radio interference from arcing, coarse regulation.

Understand Explain the pitot-static system and how the airspeed indicator and the altimeter use it. 12 marks

Pitot tube faces the airflow and senses total pressure; Static ports on the fuselage side sense static pressure; Airspeed indicator capsule sees total pressure inside and static outside; deflection gives dynamic pressure; Airspeed is computed from dynamic pressure and air density; Altimeter is an evacuated capsule seeing static pressure only; a stack and linkage convert pressure to height; Blocked pitot or static ports give specific false readings.

Apply

Apply An aircraft has a 28 V DC system with a 100 A generator. Explain how a TRU and an inverter are used to feed a 115 V AC 400 Hz load and a 28 V DC load, and why both converters are needed. 12 marks

TRU: transformer steps AC down, rectifier gives DC; used where main supply is AC; Inverter: DC to AC at fixed frequency for AC instruments; Loads dictate the conversion, not the source; Conversion losses and heat must be allowed for; In modern architecture many small converters replace one large unit.

Apply A 28 V DC feeder carries 40 A continuously and passes through a zone where the ambient temperature derates the wire to 70 percent of its rated current. Explain how you would choose the protection device. 13 marks

Find the wire's rated current from the table for that gauge; Apply temperature and bundle derating factors; Protection device rating must be at or below the derated wire rating, not the 40 A load; Check the time-current curve: fault current must clear before wire damage; Check voltage drop over the run and short-circuit withstand.

Apply Describe how the ram air turbine is deployed and what it feeds, and explain how ETOPS rules use the available emergency power time. 13 marks

Deployment on loss of AC bus or by pilot action; automatic on some types; Governed to constant frequency, usually 115 V AC 400 Hz; Feeds essential AC and, through a TRU, essential DC; Load shedding leaves only flight controls, essential instruments, one radio; ETOPS diversion time is set by the certified time with this power available; Pilot must descend to a lower altitude where the RAT is effective.

Apply Explain how a tachometer and an EGT indicator turn engine speed and temperature into cockpit readings. 10 marks

Tachometer: small generator or magnetic pick-up gives a voltage or pulse rate proportional to shaft speed; Indicator is a voltmeter calibrated in percent RPM; EGT: thermocouples in the exhaust stream generate a small millivolt signal; Cold junction compensation and amplification in the indicator; Indicator scale in degrees Celsius; limits marked in red.

Apply Describe the packaging, cooling and EMI requirements for a line-replaceable avionics unit, and name the standards used. 13 marks

ARINC 600 or ARINC 404 case, front-loading, standard connectors; DOD / MIL standards for shock, vibration, humidity and altitude; Cooling by forced air through the case, or conduction to a cold plate; Heat load must be removed within the bay's cooling budget; EMI: shielding, filtering, grounding, bonding; emission and susceptibility limits; Lightning and HIRF protection as separate requirements.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare the A320 and B787 electrical architectures and evaluate why the B787 moved to 235 V AC variable frequency and 270 V DC. 16 marks

A320: 115 V AC 400 Hz from IDGs, 28 V DC from TRUs, batteries for emergency; B787: 235 V AC variable frequency from large starter-generators, 270 V DC from TRUs; Higher voltage means lower current for the same power, so lighter cables; Variable frequency removes the constant-speed drive, saving weight and maintenance; Trade-off: new protection devices, arc-fault detection, higher insulation demands.

Analyze Explain split, parallel and split-parallel bus systems. Analyse what happens to the aircraft in each when one generator fails in flight. 14 marks

Split: each generator feeds its own bus; a fault is confined to one side; Parallel: generators share the bus; load sharing needs equalising circuits; Split-parallel: parallel on ground for starting, split in flight; On failure in split, only the loads on that bus are lost; essential loads transfer to the healthy bus; In parallel, one fault can trip the whole bus unless bus-tie protection acts; Trade-off between load sharing efficiency and fault containment.

Evaluate Fuel quantity measurement is called the hardest cockpit parameter. Analyse why, and evaluate the correction methods used. 12 marks

Tank moves, fuel sloshes, aircraft pitches and rolls; Capacitance probes measure fuel level but dielectric constant changes with fuel type and temperature; Density changes mean volume measured is not mass; Compensator probe and temperature correction; multiple probes averaged; Attitude and acceleration corrections; dipstick check as fallback; Compare with a simple float gauge and state why it is inadequate.

Evaluate Evaluate the consequences of fitting a fuse or circuit breaker larger than the wire's derated rating, and explain the role of current limiters and limiting resistors. 12 marks

Wire becomes the fusible element; insulation chars and fire risk rises; Short circuit may not be cleared before damage; Current limiter: high rupturing capacity device for heavy feeder faults; Limiting resistor reduces fault current to a level a smaller breaker can clear; Coordination: device time-current curve must lie below the wire damage curve; Undersizing also fails: nuisance trips and load loss.

Glossary

Anti-collision light
Flashing red beacon that warns ground crew and other traffic that the aircraft is about to move or is running.
APU
Auxiliary power unit: a small gas turbine in the tail that supplies electrical and pneumatic power on the ground and in flight if needed.
BITE
Built-in test equipment: self-check circuits inside a unit that report faults to the cockpit or a maintenance panel.
Bus tie
A contactor that joins two busbars, either to share load between generators or to let one generator feed both sides.
Capacitance probe
Fuel tank sensor whose capacitance changes as fuel rises between its plates; used to infer fuel level.
Circuit breaker
Resettable protection device that opens on overcurrent and can be reset by the crew after a fault is cleared.
Cold junction compensation
Correction applied for the temperature of the thermocouple's reference end, so the reading is the hot-end temperature only.
Contactor
A heavy-duty relay that makes and breaks a main power circuit under control of a switch or protection relay.
Current limiter
Slow-acting protective device for heavy feeder circuits; carries normal current indefinitely but opens on a large sustained fault.
Derating
Reducing a component's allowed current below its catalogue value to allow for high ambient temperature, altitude, or tightly bundled wires.
Dynamic pressure
The pressure produced by motion, equal to half the density times the square of the speed, written ½ρV².
Essential bus
A busbar reserved for loads that must keep running: flight instruments, radios, flight controls, and minimum lighting.
Excitation
Supplying the field current; a generator with no excitation produces no output.
Field current
The small DC current fed to a generator's field winding, which sets the strength of the magnetic field and hence the output voltage.
Ground power unit
External cart or pit supply that feeds the aircraft busbars while the engines and APU are off.
HIRF
High-intensity radiated fields: strong external radio or radar energy the aircraft's wiring must not pick up as a false signal.
IDG
Integrated drive generator: an engine-driven generator with a built-in constant-speed drive so it always produces 400 Hz AC.
Load shedding
Automatically removing non-essential loads so a reduced power source can keep the essential ones running.
Pitot tube
Forward-facing open tube on the fuselage that senses total pressure for the airspeed indicator.
Rectifier
Circuit of diodes that converts alternating current into direct current.
Static pressure
The pressure of still air at the aircraft's altitude, sensed by ports on the fuselage side.
Tachometer
Instrument that displays engine shaft speed, usually as a percentage of the maximum rated speed.
Thermocouple
Junction of two unlike metals that produces a small voltage proportional to the temperature difference between its hot and cold ends.
TRU
Transformer rectifier unit; steps AC down and rectifies it to DC, typically giving 28 V DC from 115 V AC.
