6.1 Flight management systems

The flight management system (FMS) is the on-board computer that holds the flight plan, predicts time and fuel, and sends steering commands to the autopilot. It exists because a crew cannot track dozens of waypoints, winds and fuel figures by hand while flying. The FMS blends sensor positions, works through the route leg by leg, and produces lateral (LNAV) and vertical (VNAV) guidance. It is used on almost every airliner and many business jets. Only a few universities teach it inside a guidance unit; most place it in flight control syllabi.

You need: autopilot and automatic flight control from Unit 1; inertial sensors and INS from Unit 5; and IMA and LRUs from Unit 4.

Four jobs in one box

An FMS is a line replaceable unit (LRU): a box a mechanic can swap on the ramp. It does four jobs. Navigation blends positions from the Global Positioning System (GPS), inertial navigation systems (INS) and radio aids into one best position. Flight planning stores the route as a chain of legs between waypoints (named points) and airways (named routes). Performance holds aircraft speeds, altitude limits and fuel flow, then predicts time and fuel for each leg. Guidance turns the plan into commands: LNAV steers left and right; VNAV controls climb, cruise and descent. The figure shows the flow. The FMS core receives sensors and both databases, then sends guidance to the autopilot. The autopilot and flight director (the cockpit steering display) fly the command; they do not create the plan.

Databases, pages and alerts

The navigation database holds places: waypoints, airways, radio navaids, airports and runway data. It is updated every 28 days, like chart cycles. The performance database holds aircraft numbers: take-off speeds, climb speeds, cruise Mach and fuel flow at each altitude. A crew loads the route through the multipurpose control and display unit (MCDU), a keypad and screen. Typical pages are INIT (initial position and weight), RTE (origin, destination, waypoints), LEGS (leg-by-leg list), PERF (speeds and fuel) and PROGRESS (current position, time, fuel). If the route is invalid — a waypoint not in the database, a gap called a discontinuity, or a leg that cannot be flown — the FMS shows an alert such as ROUTE DISCONTINUITY or NOT IN DATABASE. The crew must fix it before LNAV can follow the plan.

Worked example

Question. An aircraft must fly 900 NM at a true airspeed (TAS, speed through the air) of 450 kt into a 50 kt headwind. Its fuel flow is 2400 kg/h. Find the ground speed, the time and the fuel burn, as the performance page would show before departure. (1 NM is one nautical mile; 1 kt is one nautical mile per hour.)

  1. Ground speed = TAS − headwind = 450 kt − 50 kt = 400 kt. Ground speed is speed over the ground.
  2. Time = distance ÷ ground speed = 900 NM ÷ 400 kt = 2.25 h = 2 h 15 min.
  3. Fuel burn = fuel flow × time = 2400 kg/h × 2.25 h = 5400 kg.

Answer. Ground speed is 400 kt, time is 2.25 h (2 h 15 min) and fuel burn is 5400 kg.

Exam traps

  • FMS versus autopilot: the FMS computes the steering command; the autopilot and flight director fly it.
  • Navigation database holds places; performance database holds aircraft numbers. Do not look for fuel flow in the navigation database.
  • Headwind subtracts from TAS; tailwind adds. Using TAS instead of ground speed gives too short a time.
  • A route discontinuity is a missing leg, not a failed sensor. The crew must close it.
  • LNAV is lateral guidance only; VNAV is vertical. Neither one is the autopilot.

6.2 Guidance systems and taxonomy of guidance laws

A missile cannot steer by looking at a target; it needs a number. The guidance law is the rule that turns what the seeker or radar knows about the target into one acceleration command, written a_c or n_c, and the autopilot and fins then make the airframe produce it. Every guidance law in use sits in one of four families: command, homing, navigation and modern. Knowing the family tells you where the computer sits, what it measures, and what can go wrong. Air-to-air, anti-tank and surface-to-air missiles all run on these laws.

You need: the autopilot idea from Unit 1, Flight Control Systems, since that is the block which receives the guidance command.

The guidance loop

Guidance is a loop, not a one-shot sum. Five blocks sit in it, and the last one feeds the first.

  1. Seeker or radar measures the target: range R in metres, closing speed, and the angle of the line of sight (LOS), the straight line from missile to target.
  2. The guidance law turns those measurements into one number, the commanded lateral acceleration a_c, in m/s² or in g, where 1 g = 9.81 m/s².
  3. The autopilot compares a_c with the acceleration the airframe is actually pulling and issues fin deflection commands (see 6.7).
  4. Fin servos move the surfaces, the airframe makes lift, and the missile turns.
  5. The seeker measures again. The loop repeats many times a second.

This is feedback control at the outer level. Its bandwidth is low, a few hertz, because the missile body turns far slower than a fin servo. A command that changes faster than the airframe can follow is wasted, and it can excite the body. Guidance laws are therefore designed together with the autopilot, not separately.

Two consequences matter. A command can never be better than the measurement behind it: a noisy LOS rate gives a noisy a_c. And whichever family of law is used, the output is always the same kind of signal, an acceleration command, so the autopilot does not care which law produced it.

The four families

Two different questions split the laws. Command and homing answer: where does the guidance computer sit? Navigation and modern answer: what rule does it run? The four names are the labels in everyday use, even though they mix the two questions.

  • Command guidance: the computer stays on the ground, on the launcher or in the launching aircraft, and the missile carries a receiver and an autopilot. In MCLOS (manual command to line of sight) an operator watches missile and target and steers with a joystick. In SACLOS (semi-automatic) the operator only holds a crosshair on the target and the computer works out the command. In beam riding the missile flies inside a radar or laser beam aimed at the target and corrects itself whenever it drifts off the beam centre. Details in 6.3.
  • Homing guidance: the computer travels inside the missile and the seeker looks at the target itself. Active homing means the missile carries both transmitter and receiver, so it is fire-and-forget. Semi-active means another radar, on the ground or in the launching aircraft, illuminates the target and the missile only receives the reflection. Passive means the missile transmits nothing and listens for heat, reflected light or the target's own radar.
  • Navigation guidance: the rule is a statement about geometry and can run on the ground or on board. Pursuit points the velocity vector at the target. CLOS keeps the missile on the line from launcher to target. Proportional navigation commands acceleration proportional to the rate at which the LOS is rotating, and is the workhorse of homing missiles. The mathematics is in 6.4 and 6.5.
  • Modern guidance: optimal laws minimise a cost such as miss distance plus control effort. Differential-game laws treat the target as an opponent who is also optimising. Sliding-mode and H-infinity laws hold stability when the model is wrong. Estimation-based laws fuse seeker and inertial data, often with a Kalman filter, before the law runs. Optimal and robust are the two names most syllabi use.

Classical and modern: two cuts of one set

Some syllabi call pursuit, LOS, CLOS and PN the classical guidance laws and put everything else under modern guidance. That is a division by date and by mathematics, not by hardware. A PN law from the 1950s and a sliding-mode law from the 2010s may run inside the same homing missile, one after the other as the missile flies.

Classical laws assume a target that does not dodge and measurements that are clean. Against a turning tank or a jinking aircraft the miss distance grows unless the missile has acceleration margin to spare. Modern laws attack that gap: they model the target's manoeuvre, or minimise a cost, or drive the error to zero in finite time in spite of the model.

For the exam, keep three labels apart: the family (command, homing, navigation, modern), where the computer sits, and the rule it runs. A semi-active homing missile flying proportional navigation carries all three labels at once.

Worked example

Question. An anti-tank missile is 4 km from its target and closing at 800 m/s. The seeker sees the target 200 m to one side of the missile's present velocity vector. Treating the correction as a constant lateral acceleration starting from zero sideways velocity, find the time to go and the average lateral acceleration needed to remove the error.

  1. Time to go: t = R ÷ v = 4000 m ÷ 800 m/s = 5 s.
  2. With zero initial sideways velocity, the sideways displacement is y = ½ a t².
  3. Rearrange for acceleration: a = 2y ÷ t² = (2 × 200 m) ÷ (5 s)² = 400 m ÷ 25 s² = 16 m/s².
  4. Convert to g: 16 m/s² ÷ 9.81 m/s² per g = 1.63 g.
  5. So the guidance law must call for about 1.6 g of lateral acceleration for 5 s. Whichever family produces that command, the autopilot and fins must deliver this number.

Answer. Time to go 5 s, average lateral acceleration 16 m/s², which is about 1.63 g.

Exam traps

  • Mixing the two divisions. Command and homing say where the computer sits; pursuit, CLOS and PN say what rule it runs. A homing missile can and usually does run PN.
  • Calling semi-active homing 'command guidance' because the launcher radar does the illuminating. Illuminating is not commanding: the missile still computes its own steering, on board.
  • Unit slips with g. Writing 16 g instead of 16 m/s², or 1.63 m/s² instead of 1.63 g. Divide or multiply by 9.81 and state which unit you mean.
  • Assuming modern always beats classical. A well-tuned PN law with enough acceleration margin often beats an optimal law built on a wrong target model.
  • Thinking CLOS is homing. In CLOS the computer tracks both missile and target from the launch site, so the hardware is command guidance even though the rule is geometric.

6.3 Command and homing guidance

Command guidance keeps the guidance computer on the ground or launcher. A tracker watches target and missile, the computer works out a correction, and a radio or wire link sends it to the missile. Homing guidance moves the computer into the missile nose: a seeker looks at the target and steers without help from the launcher. Both exist because a ground computer can be large and cheap, while a missile seeker gives the final metres of accuracy when the link is jammed or the range is short.

You need Chapter 6.2 for the guidance-law taxonomy.

Command guidance: the loop stays outside

In command guidance the guidance computer stays on the ground, on a ship or in the launcher. A tracker—a radar or an optical sight—measures the target and the missile. The computer compares the two lines of sight and turns the difference into a steering command. An uplink carries that command to the missile, where a receiver moves the fins. The missile's position goes back to the tracker, so the loop closes outside the missile.

  • MCLOS, manual command to line of sight. The operator keeps a sight on the target and steers the missile with a joystick; the computer only passes the stick command to the fins. Cheap, but the operator must see both target and missile, and accuracy falls as range grows.
  • SACLOS, semi-automatic command to line of sight. The operator keeps the sight on the target only; the computer measures the missile's offset from the sight line and flies it back. Workload drops, so accuracy improves at longer range.
  • Beam riding. The launcher points a radar or laser beam at the target. The missile sits inside the beam and senses its own offset from the beam centre; the correction is generated on board or by the beam. The operator only holds the beam, but the beam must stay on the target until impact.

A radio uplink can be jammed; a wire link is hard to jam but limits range and lays a cable. Command guidance suits short-range anti-tank missiles and some surface-to-air missiles because the heavy computer stays on the ground.

Homing guidance: the seeker in the nose

Homing guidance puts a seeker in the missile nose. The seeker measures the direction of the target and its angular motion; the missile's own computer steers to null the line-of-sight rate, so the missile stays on a collision course. The three types differ in where the energy comes from.

  • Active. The missile carries both transmitter and receiver. It sends a radar pulse and listens for the echo. Fire-and-forget: the launcher can leave after launch. Cost and size are high, and the missile's small radar has short range.
  • Semi-active. The launcher illuminates the target; the missile carries only a receiver and homes on the reflected energy. The missile is cheaper and lighter than active, but the launcher must keep pointing at the target until impact.
  • Passive. The missile listens to energy the target itself emits—radar transmissions, infrared heat, or jamming signals (home-on-jam). It carries no transmitter and needs no illumination from the launcher. It works only against a target that emits, and flares or decoys can pull it away.

The trap is the name. Semi-active is not passive: a semi-active missile has a receiver and no transmitter, but the launcher still lights the target. Passive uses only the target's own radiation.

Choosing a guidance type

Range alone does not decide. A command link adds delay, so a fast crossing target needs a quick tracker and a quick operator. A homing seeker removes link delay but adds missile cost and size. Pick command guidance when the launcher can stay and the missile must be cheap. Pick homing when the final metres need the missile's own eye and the launcher must leave. 6.4 Pursuit, LOS and CLOS guidance

Pure pursuit aims the missile straight at the target. CLOS keeps it on the launcher-to-target line. Both need only the target's direction, so they are cheap. Both fail to hold the line of sight steady, the real condition for a hit. Early infrared missiles, anti-tank beam riders and short-range surface-to-air systems use them.

You need: Chapter 6.2 for guidance families and Chapter 6.3 for the guidance computer.

Line of sight and its rate

The line of sight (LOS) is the straight line from missile to target. Its length R is the range in metres. Its direction changes as either vehicle moves.

The LOS rate λ̇ (lambda dot) is how fast that direction turns, in degrees per second. It is the most useful number in guidance.

If the line joining two vehicles points the same way at every instant, the LOS rate is zero and both are on a collision course: the constant-bearing course. Each runs along a fixed straight line and they meet at the same point at the same time.

Intercept condition: λ̇ = 0

λ̇ is the LOS rate in degrees per second. Zero means the LOS direction is frozen.

Any law that does not drive the LOS rate to zero either misses or needs an enormous turn at the end.

Pure and deviated pursuit

Pure pursuit makes the missile's velocity vector point at the target: heading equals LOS direction, lead angle zero.

From behind, this is a tail chase. The missile keeps curving because the target slides sideways, and the LOS rate never reaches zero. Lateral demand is small early and blows up near the end, when a fixed sideways offset must be removed in little time.

Head-on, the path is nearly straight and the demand stays moderate. From the side it curves clearly, though less than in a tail chase, and the terminal demand still rises as the range closes.

Deviated pursuit fixes a lead angle θ greater than zero, aiming ahead. The collision-course lead angle comes from the sine rule.

sin θ = (Vt ÷ Vm) sin β

θ is lead angle between missile velocity and LOS, in degrees; Vt target speed, m/s; Vm missile speed, m/s; β angle between target velocity and LOS, in degrees.

A fixed θ works only while Vt and β stay constant, which they rarely do. Deviated pursuit is a snapshot, not a guidance law.

CLOS and beam riding

Command to line of sight (CLOS) keeps the missile on the launcher-to-target line. A tracker at the launcher measures the angle between missile and that line, and the computer sends yaw and pitch commands to cancel the error.

A small angular error at the launcher turns into a linear miss that grows with range, because the same fraction of a degree spans more metres the further out the missile is. The swinging line also demands more lateral acceleration from the missile as range opens.

CLOS suits short-range work: shoulder-launched surface-to-air missiles and ship point defence.

Beam riding is the radar form of CLOS. The launcher points a narrow beam at the target and the missile flies down the beam centre, with a receiver at its tail watching the beam. No target tracker travels in the missile, so it is cheap. The beam widens with distance, limiting range.

Some syllabi call beam riding a separate law; it is CLOS with the beam as reference line.

Worked example

Question. A missile flies at 600 m/s. The target flies at 300 m/s and its velocity makes 60° with the line of sight. Find the lead angle for a collision course, and state what pure pursuit would use instead.

  1. Data: Vm = 600 m/s, Vt = 300 m/s, β = 60°.
  2. Speed ratio: Vt ÷ Vm = 0.5. sin 60° = 0.866.
  3. sin θ = 0.5 × 0.866 = 0.433; θ = arcsin 0.433 = 25.7°.
  4. Pure pursuit uses θ = 0; the path curves and lateral demand peaks near the end.

Answer. The collision-course lead angle is 25.7° ahead of the line of sight, against 0° for pure pursuit.

Exam traps

  • Pure pursuit is not a collision course. Only a constant-bearing course has zero LOS rate; in pure pursuit the LOS keeps turning.
  • λ̇ is an angular rate in degrees per second, not a linear speed in metres per second.
  • β is measured from LOS to target velocity, not from missile heading or launcher.
  • CLOS error grows with range because the tracker resolves angles only to a fixed small value: the same fraction of a degree spans a larger linear miss as range opens (and the missile must accelerate harder to stay on the swinging line).
  • Beam riding is CLOS with the beam as reference. Do not label it homing.

6.5 Proportional navigation and its variants

Proportional navigation (PN) is a homing law that commands missile acceleration proportional to the rate at which the line of sight rotates. Zero line-of-sight rate means the missile is on a collision course. PN needs only angle measurements from a seeker, not target range or target speed. It is used in air-to-air, surface-to-air and anti-ship missiles and in guided bombs. This chapter gives the base law, the main variants, and how each behaves against a target that manoeuvres.

You need: Chapter 6.2 for guidance-law families and Chapter 6.4 for line-of-sight geometry and why pursuit and CLOS leave a LOS rate.

Collision course and line-of-sight rate

Draw the missile M and target T. The line joining them is the line of sight (LOS). The angle λ is the LOS direction from a fixed reference. The LOS rate λ̇ = dλ/dt is how fast that line rotates, in rad/s. Range R is the distance M to T. Closing velocity Vc = −dR/dt is positive when the range shrinks, in m/s. A seeker measures λ directly; Vc may come from a radar or from an estimate.

If λ̇ = 0 while R decreases, the LOS keeps one direction. Missile and target stay on a straight line, so a hit follows. This is a collision course: constant bearing, decreasing range. Any nonzero λ̇ means the missile is sliding off that line. The seeker measures λ and λ̇; PN uses λ̇ as its error signal. The guidance computer is inside the missile because this is homing guidance, as Chapter 6.3 explains.

Base law: a = N Vc λ̇

a = N Vc λ̇

a is the commanded acceleration perpendicular to the LOS, in m/s²; N is the effective navigation ratio, dimensionless; Vc is closing velocity in m/s; λ̇ is LOS rate in rad/s.

The command is proportional to the rotation rate. If the LOS rotates faster, the missile turns harder. The sign of λ̇ sets the direction. At λ̇ = 0 the command is zero, so the missile does not turn away from a collision course.

Why this drives λ̇ to zero: a turn perpendicular to the LOS changes the missile velocity direction so as to reduce LOS rotation. That is negative feedback. The gain N sets how strongly the loop reacts. Typical N is 3 to 5. Below about 2 the loop can fail to converge; above 5 the response is fast but sensitive to seeker noise and fin saturation. N carries no units, so the units of a come from Vc λ̇: (m/s)(1/s) = m/s².

Use rad/s, not deg/s. One degree per second equals π/180 = 0.01745 rad/s. A seeker that reports 1 °/s has λ̇ = 0.01745 rad/s.

Ideal PN (IPN) keeps the exact geometry without small-angle approximations and is used as a benchmark for what PPN loses.

Manoeuvring targets and the choice of N

Against a non-manoeuvring target, PPN drives λ̇ near zero with bounded acceleration. Miss distance is small if N is in the usual range and the seeker and fins are fast enough. The missile does not need target range, only closing speed.

Against a manoeuvring target, PPN leaves a steady-state miss unless N is large. The target's turn creates LOS rotation; PPN reacts only after λ̇ appears, so it always lags. APN adds (N/2) aT⊥, which is nonzero even when λ̇ is zero, so the command anticipates the target turn.

Higher N lowers miss distance but raises acceleration demand at launch and makes the missile more sensitive to seeker noise, target glint and fin saturation. Lower N is gentler but slower. The usual design range is 3 to 5. The fin servos and airframe must produce the commanded acceleration; see Chapter 6.6.

Worked example

Question. An air-to-air missile closes on a target at Vc = 900 m/s. The missile speed is Vm = 600 m/s. The seeker measures a LOS rate of 0.02 rad/s. The navigation ratio is N = 4. Take g = 9.81 m/s².   3. The PPN acceleration is perpendicular to the LOS.
  4. Convert to g: 72 / 9.81 = 7.3 g.
  5. Write the TPN law: aTPN = N Vm λ̇.

Exam traps

  • Feeding λ̇ in degrees per second instead of rad/s: the command comes out 57.3 times too large.
  • Putting Vm into the PPN formula or Vc into the TPN formula. PPN uses closing speed; TPN uses missile speed.
  • Thinking N has units or that a larger N is always better. N is dimensionless, usually 3 to 5; larger N raises noise sensitivity and fin saturation.
  • Dropping the sign of λ̇. The sign sets the turn direction, and a sign error doubles the miss instead of removing it.
  • Adding the whole target acceleration in APN instead of (N/2) times its component normal to the LOS.

6.6 Missile airframe, control types and fin actuation servos

A guidance law gives an acceleration command. Something physical must turn it into a sideways force: the airframe, its fins, and the small servo behind each fin. This chapter covers the missile body, the three ways to steer it — aerodynamic fins, thrust vector or reaction jets, and skid-to-turn versus bank-to-turn — and the fin actuation chain that turns milliamps of command into hundreds of newtons of rod force. Every homing missile is built around this chain. Only a few universities teach this depth.

You need: Unit 2 for hydraulic and pneumatic supply, Unit 3 for actuators, and Chapter 6.5 for the acceleration command.

Airframe and lift

A missile airframe has a nose dome that lets the seeker look out, a body carrying the motor and warhead, and fins. Fins near the nose are canards; tail fins are the more common choice. Both make lift.

Lift comes from two sources. The body makes lift when it flies at an angle of attack α, the angle between the body axis and the airflow. The fins add lift in proportion to their deflection δ, the angle between fin and body.

L = q S C_L

L is lift in newtons; q = ½ρv² is dynamic pressure in pascals, with ρ the air density in kg/m³ and v the speed in m/s; S is a reference area in m²; C_L is the dimensionless lift coefficient. At v = 0 the fins do nothing.

Propulsion, briefly: a solid rocket motor for short range, a ramjet for long cruise, and thrust vector control during boost while speed is still low.

Control types and the turn convention

Aerodynamic fin control works inside the atmosphere, where q is high enough for useful lift and hinge moment.

Thrust vector control (TVC) tilts the motor thrust itself; reaction jets at nose or tail push the body directly. Both work outside the atmosphere, where q ≈ 0 and fins are dead weight, and during boost.

Skid-to-turn (STT) pitches and yaws the body independently with two fin pairs, without rolling. It is quick and suits a cruciform airframe. Bank-to-turn (BTT) rolls the missile first so its lift vector lies in the turn plane, then pulls one elevator. BTT gives more lift per degree of angle of attack and keeps a ramjet inlet in clean flow, but the roll costs time. See Chapter 6.7.

Fin actuation servos

Three technologies: electric, where a brushless motor drives the fin through a gearbox — clean but moderate torque; electro-hydraulic, where a pump or accumulator supplies oil and a servo valve meters it into a ram — highest torque for its size, used on large fins; pneumatic, where hot gas from a gas generator drives the ram — cheap and short-lived.

  1. Acceleration command arrives from the autopilot.
  2. Servo amplifier takes voltage in and gives current out.
  3. Torque motor moves the servo valve flapper.
  4. Valve meters oil into the ram; the push rod moves.
  5. Position sensor on the fin feeds deflection back to the amplifier.

Two limits set the response. Torque limit is the largest hinge torque the actuator can hold; it caps deflection at high q. Rate limit is the largest slew rate in °/s; it caps how fast the fin arrives. A rate-limited servo answers a step with a ramp.

The actuator must beat the hinge moment — the aerodynamic torque on the fin about its hinge — plus inertia and friction. Gyros (rate, free, dynamically tuned) sense the resulting motion; their physics is in Unit 5.

Worked example

Question. A fin needs 30 N·m of hinge torque and must slew 2° in 20 ms. The push rod acts 25 mm from the hinge. Find the rod force, the slew rate in °/s and rad/s, and the actuator power.

  1. Lever arm r = 25 mm = 0.025 m.
  2. Rod force F = torque ÷ r = 30 ÷ 0.025 = 1200 N.
  3. Slew rate = 2° ÷ 0.02 s = 100 °/s.
  4. In radians: 100 × π/180 = 1.745 rad/s.
  5. Power = torque × angular rate = 30 × 1.745 = 52.4 W.

Answer. Rod force 1200 N, slew rate 100 °/s (1.745 rad/s), actuator power 52.4 W.

Exam traps

  • Hinge moment is the aerodynamic torque on the fin; actuator torque is what the servo supplies. Dropping the lever arm turns 30 N·m into a wrong 30 N.
  • Power needs rad/s. Using 100 °/s gives 3000 W, 57 times too large.
  • A torque-limited servo saturates and lags; a rate-limited servo ramps. Different step responses, and examiners ask which you have.
  • Fins cannot steer at launch because q ≈ 0. That phase belongs to TVC or reaction jets.
  • STT does not roll the airframe; BTT does.

6.7 Roll and lateral autopilots; bank-to-turn guidance

A missile rarely flies straight at its target. It must hold a stable roll attitude, turn a guidance command into sideways acceleration, and sometimes roll its whole body so its lift points the right way. The roll autopilot holds the airframe about its long axis. The lateral autopilot turns an acceleration demand into fin motion. Bank-to-turn guidance combines them: roll to a computed bank angle, then pull. These loops sit between the guidance law and the fins, and they decide whether a hit is smooth or wasted.

You need: Chapter 6.6 for the airframe and fin servos, and Unit 1 for autopilot basics.

Roll autopilot: hold the long axis

The roll autopilot controls rotation about the missile's long axis. The controlled variable is roll angle φ, measured from a reference such as wings level. Its derivative is roll rate p = dφ/dt. A rate gyro measures p. A free or vertical gyro, or an attitude reference, measures φ. The autopilot forms a command from the attitude error and adds roll-rate damping.

δa = Kφ(φcmd − φ) − Kp p

δa is the aileron or roll-fin deflection, in radians; φ is the measured roll angle, in radians; φcmd is the commanded roll angle, in radians; p is roll rate, in radians per second; Kφ is the attitude gain, in radians of fin per radian of roll error; Kp is the rate gain, in radians of fin per radian per second.

The negative sign on Kp opposes roll rate. Without it, the missile overshoots the commanded bank. Wings-level mode is φcmd = 0. Gain scheduling with Mach and altitude keeps the loop response similar as dynamic pressure changes. Modern missiles use ring laser or fibre optic gyros, which have no spinning mass and low drift; dynamically tuned gyros, which do have a small spinning rotor, are also used. The roll loop must be faster than the lateral loop, so bank angle is ready before lift is applied.

Lateral autopilot: acceleration to fin

The lateral autopilot converts a guidance acceleration command into yaw motion. Its outer loop uses an accelerometer to measure lateral acceleration ay. The error is e = acmd − ay, where acmd is the commanded lateral acceleration and ay is the measured value, both in m/s². The outer loop turns e into a yaw-rate command rcmd; an integral term removes steady error. The inner loop uses a yaw rate gyro. It compares r with rcmd and commands the rudder or yaw fins. A simple inner law is δr = Kr(rcmd − r). δr is rudder deflection in radians, Kr is the rate gain, and r is measured yaw rate in rad/s. Rate feedback damps the short-period yaw oscillation.

Gains are scheduled with Mach and altitude. At low dynamic pressure the fins are weak and need larger deflection for the same acceleration. At high dynamic pressure the same deflection gives more acceleration, so gains fall. The accelerometer and rate gyro are the transducers described in Unit 4. In a skid-to-turn missile this loop works with pitch and yaw without rolling. In a bank-to-turn missile the same acceleration demand is split: the roll autopilot sets the lift direction, and the in-plane loop sets angle of attack.

Bank-to-turn: roll, then pull

Bank-to-turn guidance uses one lift plane. The guidance law produces a commanded acceleration vector. Let atotal be its magnitude. Before rolling, the vector has an in-plane part ain = atotal cos φ and a sideways part aside = atotal sin φ, where φ is the required bank angle. The roll autopilot rolls the airframe by φ until the lift plane contains the commanded vector. The roll puts both parts into the lift plane, so the wing must give lift aL = atotal. The turn itself comes from angle of attack, not from the roll. Roll only points the lift vector.

sin φ = aside ÷ atotal; ain = atotal cos φ; aL = atotal

φ is the required bank angle, in radians; atotal is the magnitude of the commanded acceleration, in m/s²; aside is the component normal to the current lift plane, in m/s²; ain is the in-plane component before rolling, in m/s²; aL is the lift acceleration required after rolling, equal to atotal, in m/s².

  1. Take the guidance acceleration vector; find its magnitude atotal and the component aside normal to the current lift plane.
  2. Compute the bank command φ = asin(aside ÷ atotal).
  3. Roll the airframe with the roll autopilot until φ is reached and settled.
  4. Command angle of attack so the lift in the body plane equals atotal.

Compare with skid-to-turn. STT keeps the airframe unrolled and uses separate pitch and yaw fins. It reverses direction quickly and avoids roll cross-coupling. It uses two lifting planes and usually more drag. BTT uses one lifting surface, so it can have less trim drag and longer range. It needs a high roll rate to move the lift plane, suffers inertia cross-coupling between roll and yaw, and reverses a turn slowly because the body must roll first. The roll loop bandwidth must sit well above the lateral loop, otherwise the lift vector is not steady when the pull starts.

Worked example

Question. A bank-to-turn missile must accelerate at 8 g in a direction that lies 4 g to the side of its lift plane. Find the bank angle and the lift the wing must give.

  1. Given: atotal = 8 g, aside = 4 g. g is the acceleration due to gravity, 9.81 m/s², so 8 g = 78.5 m/s².
  2. sin φ = aside ÷ atotal = 4 ÷ 8 = 0.5.
  3. φ = asin(0.5) = 30.0°.
  4. Lift after the roll: aL = atotal = 8 g = 78.5 m/s². Before rolling, the in-plane component was ain = atotal cos φ = 8 × cos 30° = 8 × 0.8660 = 6.928 g, about 6.93 g.
  5. The roll autopilot must settle at 30° before the lateral autopilot pulls the full 8 g of lift.

Answer. The bank angle is 30.0° and the wing must give 8 g of lift, about 78.5 m/s².

Exam traps

  • Roll alone does not turn a BTT missile. The turn comes from the angle of attack applied after the roll; roll only aims the lift vector.
  • Do not use a_L = a_total sin φ. After the roll the lift is the full command a_total; its components along the old lift plane and across it are a_total cos φ and a_total sin φ.
  • The roll loop must be faster than the lateral loop. If both have similar bandwidth, the lift vector moves while the missile is pulling.
  • BTT is not automatically better. It saves drag with one lifting plane but needs high roll rate and suffers inertia cross-coupling; STT reverses faster.
  • Bank angle sign matters: φ must point the lift toward the commanded acceleration, not away from it.

6.8 Spacecraft systems: power, thermal and attitude control

A spacecraft runs its own power station, its own thermal control and its own pointing system, with no pilot and no maintenance visit. The solar array must make electricity in sunlight, the battery must carry the load through eclipse, heat can leave only by radiation, and the payload must be pointed to within arcseconds. Only a few universities teach this inside an avionics unit; space-systems courses treat it as core material.

You need Unit 3 for power generation and batteries, and Unit 4 for spacecraft avionics architecture.

Electrical power: make it, store it, regulate it

The power system has three blocks: the solar array, the battery, and the power conditioning and distribution unit (PCDU). The array makes direct current while the Sun is visible. The battery carries the whole load during eclipse. The PCDU converts, regulates and switches that power out to every load on the bus.

Two regulation schemes are common. A shunt regulator dumps surplus array current into a resistor or a switch when the battery is full; it is simple and cheap. Maximum power point tracking (MPPT) moves the array's operating voltage so it stays on its peak power curve as temperature and illumination change; it extracts more energy but costs more mass and money.

A regulated bus holds a fixed voltage such as 28 V or 50 V, so payloads see a clean supply. An unregulated bus follows the battery voltage and is lighter, but every load must tolerate the swing.

E_ecl = P_load × t_ecl ÷ 60      C = E_ecl ÷ (DoD × η_dis)      P_arr = (P_load + E_ecl × 60 ÷ t_sun) ÷ η_arr

E_ecl is the eclipse energy in Wh; P_load is the payload plus bus load in W; t_ecl is the eclipse time in minutes; C is the battery capacity in Wh; DoD is the allowed depth of discharge (0 to 1); η_dis is the discharge efficiency; t_sun is the sunlight time in minutes; η_arr is the array and harness efficiency; P_arr is the array output at beginning of life in W.

  1. Energy out in eclipse = load power × eclipse time.
  2. Battery capacity = eclipse energy ÷ (depth of discharge × discharge efficiency).
  3. Recharge power = eclipse energy ÷ sunlight time.
  4. Array output = (load power + recharge power) ÷ array efficiency.

Thermal control: dumping heat by radiation

There is no air in space, so a spacecraft loses heat by radiation only. A radiator of area A at absolute temperature T dumps heat at the rate given by the Stefan-Boltzmann law.

Q = ε σ A T⁴

Q is the heat dumped in W; ε is the emissivity, a number between 0 and 1; σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/(m²·K⁴) is the Stefan-Boltzmann constant; A is the radiating area in m²; T is the absolute temperature in K.

Multi-layer insulation (MLI) is a blanket of many thin aluminised sheets. It blocks radiation and conduction together and keeps a box warm with almost no heater power. Radiators are coated white or fitted with optical solar reflectors so their emissivity stays high while they absorb little sunlight.

Passive control uses MLI, surface coatings, radiators and heat pipes — sealed tubes that carry heat from a hot box to a radiator by evaporating a fluid at the hot end. Active control adds heaters, louvers and pumped loops. A 200 W box at 300 K with ε = 0.9 needs A = 200 ÷ (0.9 × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 300⁴) = 0.48 m².

Attitude control: holding a pointing

Spin stabilisation spins the whole craft about its axis of largest moment of inertia. Like a top it resists disturbance and needs no wheels, but the payload sweeps round with the spin, so it suits simple spin-stabilised satellites. Three-axis control holds the body fixed in inertial space.

  • Reaction wheel: a disc whose speed is changed by a motor. The craft turns the other way to conserve angular momentum, using no propellant.
  • Momentum wheel: runs at a constant bias speed and gives gyroscopic stiffness.
  • Control moment gyro (CMG): a gimballed spinning wheel, so a small wheel makes a large torque.
  • Thruster: fires gas for big slews and for unloading wheels, at the cost of propellant.
  • Magnetorquer: a coil that pushes against the Earth's magnetic field, a small torque in low Earth orbit only.

Sensors are the sun sensor (coarse, two-axis), the star tracker (arcsecond class), the Earth sensor (infrared horizon) and the gyro (rate, short term). Disturbance torques come from gravity gradient, solar radiation pressure, residual atmosphere and the magnetic field.

τ = K_p θ + K_d θ̇

τ is the commanded control torque in N·m; θ is the attitude error in rad; θ̇ is the rate error in rad/s; K_p is the proportional gain in N·m/rad; K_d is the derivative gain in N·m·s/rad. The derivative term adds damping so the craft does not oscillate.

A wheel absorbs disturbance momentum over time and its speed keeps rising until it saturates. Thrusters or magnetorquers then dump that stored momentum; this is desaturation.

Worked example

Question. A satellite carries a 500 W payload in a 96 min orbit with a 35 min eclipse. The battery may be taken to 90% depth of discharge at 90% discharge efficiency. The array works for the remaining 61 min and 10% of its output is lost in the array and harness. Find the battery capacity and the array output at beginning of life.

  1. Sunlight time t_sun = 96 − 35 = 61 min.
  2. Eclipse energy E_ecl = P_load × t_ecl ÷ 60 = 500 × 35 ÷ 60 = 291.67 Wh.
  3. Battery capacity C = E_ecl ÷ (DoD × η_dis) = 291.67 ÷ (0.9 × 0.9) = 291.67 ÷ 0.81 = 360.08 Wh.
  4. Recharge power P_rec = E_ecl ÷ (t_sun ÷ 60) = 291.67 ÷ (61 ÷ 60) = 291.67 ÷ 1.0167 = 286.89 W.
  5. Array must supply load plus recharge = 500 + 286.89 = 786.89 W.
  6. With 10% loss, P_arr = 786.89 ÷ 0.9 = 874.32 W.

Answer. Battery capacity 360.08 Wh (about 360 Wh) and array output 874.32 W (about 875 W) at beginning of life.

Exam traps

  • Sizing the array for the payload alone and forgetting the recharge energy; the array must also refill the battery.
  • Using the full 96 min orbit period instead of the 35 min eclipse when sizing the battery, which triples the capacity.
  • Putting degrees Celsius into εσAT⁴; the Stefan-Boltzmann law needs kelvin, and a Celsius value can give a negative or wildly wrong area.
  • Multiplying by depth of discharge and efficiency instead of dividing; capacity is always larger than the eclipse energy.
  • Assuming reaction wheels alone can hold attitude for years; disturbance torques saturate them and they must be desaturated.

Takeaways

  1. A guidance law is only a mapping from measured target information to an acceleration command; everything else is hardware that must deliver that command.
  2. The four guidance families are command, homing, navigation (terrain or stellar referenced) and hybrid; the choice is set by where the computer sits and what sensor it can see.
  3. Pursuit and CLOS look simple but leave a non-zero LOS rate at intercept, so the miss distance grows quickly against a crossing target.
  4. Proportional navigation drives the LOS rate to zero by commanding a = N·V_c·λ̇, which is why it is the default law in most missiles.
  5. The PN variants differ only in the reference frame of the measurement and in whether target acceleration is estimated: PPN, TPN, APN, TPN, GPN, IPN.
  6. The autopilot, fin servo and airframe dynamics sit inside the guidance loop, so slow or saturating fins turn a good law into a large miss distance.
  7. A bank-to-turn airframe turns by rolling first, which gives it more lift for a given angle of attack but slows the response and couples the axes.
  8. A spacecraft cannot refuel or open a window, so power, thermal and attitude control must all work from stored energy and radiation alone.

Exam question bank

Long answers lean on the classical guidance laws — deriving and comparing the PN variants, LOS-rate reasoning, miss-distance argument — plus the roll/lateral autopilot and fin-servo chain, with the FMS and spacecraft subsystems usually asked as shorter parts.

Remember and understand

Understand Define a guidance law and describe the four families of guidance laws, giving one practical example of each. 8 marks

Definition: rule mapping target information to an acceleration command; Family 1: command guidance — computer on ground or launcher; Family 2: homing guidance — computer inside missile, seeker data; Family 3: navigation guidance — inertial, stellar, terrain reference; Family 4: hybrid and modern laws — combined or optimal; One real example per family, with the sensor each needs.

Understand Compare command guidance with homing guidance: block diagrams, information flow, accuracy, jamming and cost. 8 marks

Command: target tracked by ground radar, missile tracked too, steering sent by radio uplink; Homing: seeker in the missile, no uplink needed after launch; Command needs a datalink and a ground radar; homing needs an expensive seeker; Homing accuracy falls with seeker noise and radome error; command accuracy falls with tracking error and link delay; Command suits short-range or beam-riding systems; homing suits fire-and-forget; Jamming and countermeasure behaviour of each.

Understand State the main functions of a flight management system and trace how a flight plan entered by the crew becomes a steering command at the autopilot. 10 marks

Navigation: position from IRS, GPS, radio navaids, Kalman blending; Flight planning: route from navigation database, waypoints, legs, constraints; Performance: fuel, time, climb and descent profile prediction; Guidance: cross-track and vertical deviation turned into roll and pitch commands; Autopilot and flight director follow the command; autothrottle follows the speed target; Crew interface, datalink uplink of routes, alerting.

Understand Explain pursuit guidance and CLOS guidance with sketches. Why does each of them fail to bring the line-of-sight rate to zero against a crossing target? 10 marks

Pursuit: missile velocity vector points at the target at every instant; Pure pursuit gives a tail chase with ever-growing lateral demand; lag pursuit and lead pursuit as variants; CLOS: missile held on the launcher–target line by a beam or radio command; Both leave a non-zero LOS rate for a crossing target, so the missile must keep turning; Acceleration demand grows without limit near intercept; miss distance grows with target speed; Effect of missile speed advantage and of range.

Understand Describe the power, thermal and attitude control subsystems of a spacecraft and explain why each must work without a pilot. 10 marks

Power: solar array, battery, shunt or converter regulation, distribution and load switching; Thermal: insulation blankets, coatings, radiators, heaters, heat pipes, temperature limits of units; Attitude: sensors (sun, star, horizon, gyro), actuators (reaction wheels, thrusters, magnetic torquers), control loop; Why autonomy: light-time delay, no atmosphere, no maintenance, long mission life; Fault detection and safe modes; Momentum dumping and orbit keeping.

Apply

Apply A missile closing on its target at V_c = 900 m/s measures a line-of-sight rate of 0.02 rad/s. Using pure proportional navigation with N = 4, find the commanded lateral acceleration in m/s² and in g. 13 marks

State the law a = N·V_c·λ̇ with every symbol defined; Substitute: a = 4 × 900 × 0.02 = 72 m/s²; Convert: 72 / 9.81 ≈ 7.3 g; Direction: normal to the LOS, in the sense that reduces λ̇; Comment on saturation if the airframe cannot supply 7.3 g; Comment on the effect of raising N to 5.

Apply A target manoeuvres normal to the line of sight at 6 g. With N = 4, compute the extra acceleration that augmented proportional navigation demands over pure PN, and explain why the extra term is needed. 13 marks

APN law: a = N·V_c·λ̇ + (N/2)·a_T; Extra term = (N/2)·a_T = 2 × 6 g = 12 g; Pure PN only reacts after λ̇ has already built up; Against a manoeuvring target PN leaves a steady-state lag and a residual miss; Cost: target acceleration a_T must be estimated, usually from seeker data; TPN and other variants that avoid needing a_T.

Apply A bank-to-turn missile needs a lateral acceleration of 10 g. Its pitch demand is 6 g and its yaw demand is 8 g. Find the roll angle command and the total normal acceleration, and explain the steps the autopilot takes. 13 marks

Resolve the demand into body pitch and yaw components; Magnitude = √(6² + 8²) = 10 g; roll angle φ = arctan(8/6) ≈ 53°; Roll autopilot drives the airframe to φ using roll fins or ailerons; Then only pitch command is applied; the lift vector does the turning; Advantages: one strong lift direction, less drag, better for ramjet intakes; Penalties: roll must finish before the turn, cross-coupling, slower response.

Apply A guidance command from the seeker must reach the fin as a deflection angle in a few milliseconds. Describe the fin actuation servo chain, and explain how servo bandwidth, gear ratio, deadband and rate limit affect the guidance loop. 10 marks

Chain: command → servo amplifier → motor (electric, hydraulic or pneumatic) → gearbox → fin; Position feedback from a potentiometer or resolver; rate feedback for damping; Bandwidth must be well above the airframe natural frequency and the guidance loop crossover; Gear ratio trades fin torque against fin speed; Deadband causes limit cycles and a steady miss; rate limit causes phase lag under large commands; Stall and hinge moment limits under high dynamic pressure.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare pursuit, CLOS and proportional navigation in terms of the line-of-sight rate each produces, the acceleration demanded and the miss distance against a crossing, manoeuvring target. 13 marks

Define λ̇ and the collision-course condition λ̇ = 0; Pursuit: λ̇ stays finite, demand rises near intercept, tail chase against a receding target; CLOS: missile forced onto the launcher–target line; demand grows with missile range from the line; PN: command proportional to λ̇, so λ̇ is driven to zero; finite and bounded demand; Plot or sketch of acceleration against time for the three laws; Effect of target manoeuvre: all three degrade, PN least.

Evaluate A seeker measures LOS rate with noise, the radome adds a boresight error that grows with look angle, and the fin servo has finite bandwidth. Analyse how these three effects together set the miss distance of a PN homing missile. 16 marks

Guidance loop as a feedback loop with seeker, filter, autopilot, servo, airframe and kinematics; Seeker noise passed through the loop; too high an N amplifies noise into fin demand; Radome slope error creates a parasitic feedback loop that can destabilise the flight path; Servo lag and rate limit add phase lag, reducing gain and phase margin; Miss distance grows with target manoeuvre, with heading error and with loop lag; Design measures: proper N, lead filters, radome compensation, higher servo bandwidth, head pointing.

Evaluate Evaluate bank-to-turn against skid-to-turn for an air-to-air missile. Which would you choose for a long-range ramjet missile and why? 13 marks

STT: separate pitch and yaw fins, airframe stays level, fast independent response; BTT: roll first, then pull; lift vector aligned with the turn; BTT gives more normal force per angle of attack because the whole body lifts; BTT reduces drag and suits ramjet intake orientation and antenna alignment; BTT costs time for the roll and creates roll–yaw–pitch coupling; Choice: BTT for long range with air-breathing propulsion; STT for agility at short range.

Evaluate Classical PN uses only LOS rate. Modern guidance laws use optimal control, differential game or sliding-mode ideas. Evaluate what these laws gain and what they demand from the on-board systems. 13 marks

Classical laws: PPN, TPN, APN, GPN, IPN — simple, need LOS rate and closing velocity; Optimal laws: minimise a cost of miss distance plus control effort; need target model and time-to-go; Differential game laws: assume a smart evading target; give a worst-case guaranteed miss; Sliding-mode and robust laws: tolerate uncertainty but demand fast switching and care with fin rate limits; Costs: more computation, better estimation (time-to-go, target acceleration), more sensors; Practical outcome: PN variants still dominate; modern laws used where a large miss margin is needed.

Analyze An aircraft loses GPS in flight. Analyse how the flight management system keeps navigation and guidance working, and what the crew and autopilot lose. 13 marks

FMS position normally blended from IRS, GPS and radio navaids by a Kalman filter; Without GPS, IRS-only position drifts with time; radio navaid updates restore it; Navigation database and flight plan remain valid; waypoint sequencing continues; Required navigation performance may no longer be met, so the route or approach must change; Autopilot can still fly headings, tracks and barometric vertical paths; Crew alerting, reversion to conventional navaids, and the case for GNSS-independent backup.

Glossary

Augmented proportional navigation
PN with an added term proportional to target acceleration to counter manoeuvres.
Autopilot
Control loop that holds or commands the vehicle's attitude and rate of turn.
Bank-to-turn
Control in which the missile rolls first so its lift vector points in the direction of the turn.
Beam riding
Guidance in which the missile stays inside a radar or laser beam pointed at the target.
CLOS
Command to line-of-sight; the missile is forced to stay on the line from launcher to target.
Closing velocity
The rate at which the missile–target range decreases, in m/s, written V_c.
Collision course
A geometry in which the line-of-sight rate stays zero, so missile and target meet if neither manoeuvres.
Command guidance
Guidance in which the computer stays on the ground or launcher and sends steering commands to the missile.
Fin actuation servo
Motor, gearbox and feedback loop that move a fin to the angle the guidance command asks for.
Flight management system
On-board computer that holds the flight plan, predicts time and fuel, and gives steering commands to the autopilot.
Guidance
The process of computing and issuing the steering commands that take a vehicle to a target or along a path.
Homing guidance
Guidance in which the seeker and computer are inside the missile, so it steers itself after launch.
Line of sight
The straight line joining the missile or launcher to the target at any instant.
Line-of-sight rate
The angular speed at which the line of sight rotates, measured in rad/s.
Miss distance
The closest approach between missile and target, the main measure of guidance quality.
Momentum dumping
Removing excess momentum stored in reaction wheels, usually with thrusters or magnetic torquers.
Navigation constant
The dimensionless gain N in proportional navigation, typically between 3 and 5.
Proportional navigation
Guidance that commands acceleration proportional to the line-of-sight rate, a = N·V_c·λ̇.
Pursuit
Guidance that keeps the missile velocity vector pointed at the target's current position.
Radiator
Surface on a spacecraft that dumps waste heat to space by radiation.
Radome slope error
Apparent shift of target angle caused by the nose dome, which feeds back into the guidance loop as a false LOS rate.
Reaction wheel
Spinning wheel used to exchange angular momentum with a spacecraft so the body turns.
Skid-to-turn
Control in which pitch and yaw fins turn the missile while the body stays nearly level.
Star tracker
Camera that compares star patterns with a catalogue to give the spacecraft's attitude.
Thermal control
The set of coatings, insulation, heaters and radiators that keep units inside their temperature limits.
Waypoint
A named point with known coordinates that the flight plan passes through.
