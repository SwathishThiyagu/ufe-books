6.1 Flight management systems

A flight management system (FMS) is the computer that holds an airliner’s planned route, knows where the aircraft is along it, predicts the time and fuel to every waypoint, and steers the autopilot along the route sideways (LNAV, lateral navigation) and up and down (VNAV, vertical navigation). Only a few universities teach the FMS in this unit, so the chapter is short.

You need: Unit 5, Kalman filter and GPS-INS integration (where the position comes from), and Unit 4, Electronic displays (the navigation display that shows the route).

Parts of an FMS

The flight management computer (FMC) does the work; the crew talks to it through the MCDU (multifunction control and display unit), a screen with a keyboard. The navigation database holds waypoints, airways, radio aids and procedures and is replaced every 28 days (the AIRAC cycle). The performance database holds the drag, thrust and fuel-flow model of the type. Airliners carry two FMCs that cross-check each other (Figure 6.1.1).

The four jobs

  1. Navigation: blend the inertial reference system (IRS) with GPS or DME for the best position.
  2. Flight planning: store the route as legs between waypoints.
  3. Performance: choose speeds and predict time and fuel. The cost index, CI = cost of time ÷ cost of fuel, sets the trade: CI = 0 flies for least fuel, a high CI flies faster.
  4. Guidance: compare the position with the active leg and send roll commands (LNAV) and pitch and thrust commands (VNAV) to the autopilot; chapter 6.3 builds the loops that obey them.

The predictions

For each leg, ground speed GS = TAS ± wind component, where TAS is the true airspeed and a headwind subtracts. Time = distance ÷ GS, and fuel = fuel flow × time, recomputed whenever the measured wind changes.

The top of descent (TOD) is where the descent must start. On a path of angle γ the distance needed is d = Δh ÷ tan γ, where Δh is the height to lose. For a 3° path tan 3° = 0.0524, which gives the pilots’ rule of about 3 NM per 1,000 ft (1 NM = 1,852 m; 1 ft = 0.3048 m). A real FMS builds an idle-thrust path from its model, so its TOD differs a little.

Worked example

Question. A route has legs of 120, 380 and 450 NM, flown at TAS 450 kt into a 30 kt headwind with a fuel flow of 2,400 kg/h. Cruise is at 35,000 ft and the approach begins at 2,000 ft. Find (a) the time, (b) the fuel, treating it all as cruise, and (c) the 3° top-of-descent distance, compared with the rule of thumb.

  1. Distance: 120 + 380 + 450 = 950 NM. Ground speed: 450 − 30 = 420 kt.
  2. Time: 950 ÷ 420 = 2.262 h = 2 h 15.7 min (legs 17.1, 54.3 and 64.3 min).
  3. Fuel: 2,400 kg/h × 2.262 h = 5,429 kg.
  4. Height to lose: Δh = 35,000 − 2,000 = 33,000 ft = 33,000 × 0.3048 = 10,058 m.
  5. Descent distance: d = 10,058 ÷ 0.0524 = 191,926 m; 191,926 ÷ 1,852 = 103.6 NM.
  6. Rule of thumb: 3 × 33 = 99 NM, 4.6 NM short, because tan 3° gives 3.14 NM per 1,000 ft.

Answer. 2 h 15.7 min, about 5,430 kg of fuel, top of descent 103.6 NM before the approach point.

Exam traps

  • Dividing distance by TAS instead of ground speed; here that hides 9 minutes of headwind.
  • Using the cruise altitude instead of the height to lose, or mixing feet with nautical miles.
  • Writing that the FMS flies the aircraft. It commands; the autopilot and autothrottle move the surfaces and the thrust levers.

6.2 Guidance, navigation and control: the GNC loop and types of guidance

Every vehicle that steers itself answers three questions many times a second. Where am I? That is navigation. Where should I be, and which way must I head to get there? That is guidance. How do I move the controls so the vehicle does it? That is control. Joined in a loop, the three are called GNC, and the same loop runs autopilots, launch vehicles, spacecraft and drones. Some syllabi call this topic the guidance system.

You need: Unit 5, Inertial sensors and inertial navigation systems, and Satellite navigation: GPS; chapter 6.1 for the FMS route.

Three jobs

Navigation estimates the vehicle’s state: position, velocity and attitude, with their likely errors. Its inputs are sensors: an inertial unit, GPS, radio aids, air data, or star trackers in space. Guidance compares that state with the mission (a route, a waypoint, a glide path, a docking port) and produces commands: a heading, a bank angle, an altitude, a speed, or a deviation to remove. Control turns the commands into actuator movements (elevator, ailerons, rudder, thrust, thrusters, reaction wheels) and keeps the vehicle stable while doing so.

A phone map on a drive to Chennai Central makes the split plain. The blue dot is navigation. The route line and “turn left in 200 m” are guidance. Your hands on the wheel are control. A wrong blue dot gives a wrong instruction, however good the driver.

Closed loops, inner and outer

Some syllabi begin with the types of control systems. An open-loop system acts without measuring the result, like a washing-machine timer. A closed-loop (feedback) system measures its output, compares it with the command and acts on the error. GNC is closed-loop at every level (Figure 6.2.1).

The loops are nested. The control loop is inner and fast: it holds attitude and rotation rates, correcting many times a second. The guidance loop is outer and slower: a heading or a position changes slowly, so it updates the attitude command only every second or so. The inner loop is kept several times faster so the two do not fight.

Types of guidance

Where guidance gets its information sorts it into broad families; some syllabi call this the taxonomy of guidance. The table gives each family with civil examples.

A homing sensor is active when it sends out its own energy and measures the return (a docking lidar or radar) and passive when it uses light or radio that the destination already gives off or reflects (a camera seeing a painted pattern on a landing pad).

Real missions combine the families by phase. An airliner departs on a stored route (preprogrammed), takes radar vectors in a busy terminal area (command) and lands on the ILS (beam). A docking spacecraft is commanded from the ground through days of phasing orbits, then switches to its own relative navigation and homes on the port over the last few hundred metres; ISRO’s SpaDeX mission docked two small satellites this way in January 2025.

Guidance is only as good as navigation

Guidance acts on the estimated state, so every navigation error passes straight into the path flown. A navigation-grade inertial system typically drifts about 1 NM per hour, which is why the FMS keeps updating it from GPS or DME (Unit 5) and why a homing sensor takes over in the last metres of a docking.

Worked example 1

Question. A fixed-wing survey drone maps a farm near Coimbatore. Its navigation puts it at the origin of a local north-east grid, and the next waypoint is 400 m north and 300 m east. The drone heads 020° at 10 m/s in still air. (a) What distance and bearing does guidance compute? (b) What is the heading error and which way must control turn? (c) How long does the leg take once on course? (d) If the GPS position is 5 m wrong sideways, how large can the bearing error be?

  1. Distance = √(400² + 300²) = √(160,000 + 90,000) = √250,000 = 500 m.
  2. Bearing = tan⁻¹(east ÷ north) = tan⁻¹(300 ÷ 400) = tan⁻¹ 0.75 = 36.9°. Guidance outputs “desired heading 036.9°”.
  3. Heading error = 36.9° − 20.0° = +16.9°; positive means the waypoint is to the right, so control banks right.
  4. Time = 500 m ÷ 10 m/s = 50 s.
  5. A 5 m sideways error seen from 500 m changes the bearing by at most tan⁻¹(5 ÷ 500) = 0.57°. From 50 m the same error gives 5.7°, so near a waypoint the drone declares arrival by distance (say within 10 m) instead of chasing the bearing.

Answer. 500 m on 036.9°; turn right through 16.9°; 50 s; at most 0.57° of bearing error.

Worked example 2

Question. An airliner flies a 2 h 30 min leg on inertial navigation alone, drifting 1 NM per hour. (a) How large can the position error be at the end? (b) Can it fly a route that requires staying within 1 NM of the path? (c) With GPS updates every second, how far does the inertial position drift between updates?

  1. Error = 1 NM/h × 2.5 h = 2.5 NM at the end.
  2. The error passes 1 NM after 1 h, so the aircraft cannot meet a 1 NM route on inertial alone; it needs GPS or DME updating.
  3. Drift rate = 1,852 m ÷ 3,600 s = 0.51 m/s, so 0.51 m per one-second update, far below any route limit.

Answer. 2.5 NM; no; about 0.5 m between GPS updates.

Exam traps

  • Mixing guidance with navigation. A GPS receiver on its own is navigation; it says nothing about where to go.
  • Writing that guidance moves the control surfaces. Guidance produces commands; control moves the surfaces.
  • Treating the ILS as navigation only. It gives the aircraft its offset from the beam (navigation information), which the autopilot uses as its guidance reference; say both.
  • Saying homing loses accuracy near the end. Its accuracy improves as the range falls.

6.3 Aircraft autopilots: pitch, roll and heading hold

An autopilot is the control part of GNC for an aircraft: a set of feedback loops that move the elevator, ailerons and rudder through servos to hold what the pilot or the FMS selects, such as a pitch attitude, an altitude, a bank angle or a heading. Fast inner loops hold attitude; slower outer loops hold the flight path by giving the inner loops their commands. Some syllabi call the lateral half the roll and lateral autopilot.

You need: Unit 1, Autopilot and automatic flight control (modes and the mode control panel); Unit 5, Gyroscopes (how attitude is sensed); chapter 6.2 for inner and outer loops.

Building blocks

The attitude and heading reference system (AHRS) or the IRS gives pitch θ, roll (bank) φ and heading ψ, with the body rates p (roll), q (pitch) and r (yaw). The air data computer gives altitude h, airspeed and vertical speed. A flight control computer works out each command, and a servo actuator drives the surface through a clutch and a torque limit, so the pilot can always overpower it. Most loops use a gain on the error plus a rate term for damping.

Longitudinal loops: pitch hold and altitude hold

Pitch attitude hold: δe = Kθ(θc − θ) − Kq·q. Here δe is the elevator command (nose-up positive), θc the commanded pitch, Kθ the degrees of elevator per degree of pitch error, q the pitch rate and Kq the rate gain. The rate term damps the short-period motion; without it a stiff Kθ makes the nose bob.

Altitude hold wraps an outer loop round it: θc = θtrim + Kh(hc − h), limited to a few degrees. Here hc is the selected altitude and Kh the degrees of pitch per metre of error. If the aircraft sinks below hc, θc rises, the aircraft climbs and the error shrinks. At constant angle of attack the climb angle follows the pitch change, so ḣ ≈ V·Kh·(hc − h) with Kh in radians per metre and V the true airspeed in m/s. The error therefore dies away exponentially with time constant τh = 1 ÷ (V Kh). The autothrottle holds speed meanwhile; without it, the climb back to altitude bleeds speed.

Lateral loops: roll hold and heading hold

Roll attitude hold: δa = Kφ(φc − φ) − Kp·p, where δa is the aileron command, φc the commanded bank and p the roll rate. Heading hold (HDG) adds the outer loop φc = Kψ(ψc − ψ), capped at a bank limit, typically 25° to 30°. In a level coordinated turn the turn rate is

ψ̇ = g tan φ ÷ V        (ψ̇ in rad/s, g = 9.81 m/s², V true airspeed in m/s)

For small bank this gives ψ̇ ≈ (g ÷ V)Kψ(ψc − ψ), so the heading error also dies away exponentially, with τψ = V ÷ (g Kψ).

The rudder channel has two jobs: a yaw damper that feeds back yaw rate to damp the Dutch roll, and turn coordination that keeps sideslip near zero. In NAV or LNAV mode the FMS cross-track error replaces the heading error at the outer loop: the lateral loops stay the same, only the source of the command changes (Figure 6.3.1).

The standard-rate turn

A standard-rate (rate one) turn is 3°/s, a full circle in 2 minutes. From the turn-rate formula the bank needed is tan φ = Vω ÷ g, with ω = 3°/s = 0.05236 rad/s. The turn radius is R = V ÷ ω and the load factor n = 1 ÷ cos φ. Pilots use the rule bank ≈ TAS (kt) ÷ 10 + 7.

Worked example 1

Question. (a) Find the bank angle, turn radius and load factor for a standard-rate turn at 120 kt TAS, and check with the rule of thumb. (b) The same autopilot at 250 kt TAS has a 25° bank limit. What happens, and what turn rate and radius result?

  1. V = 120 × 1,852 ÷ 3,600 = 61.73 m/s; ω = 0.05236 rad/s.
  2. tan φ = 61.73 × 0.05236 ÷ 9.81 = 0.3295, so φ = 18.2°.
  3. R = 61.73 ÷ 0.05236 = 1,179 m; n = 1 ÷ cos 18.2° = 1.053.
  4. Rule: 120 ÷ 10 + 7 = 19°, within 1° of the exact value.
  5. At 250 kt: V = 128.6 m/s; tan φ = 128.6 × 0.05236 ÷ 9.81 = 0.6865, so φ = 34.5°, above the 25° limit.
  6. The autopilot holds 25°: ω = 9.81 × tan 25° ÷ 128.6 = 9.81 × 0.4663 ÷ 128.6 = 0.03557 rad/s = 2.04°/s; R = 128.6 ÷ 0.03557 = 3,616 m.

Answer. (a) 18.2°, 1,179 m, n = 1.053. (b) The bank is capped at 25°, so the turn is slower than standard: 2.04°/s with a radius of 3.6 km.

Worked example 2

Question. At 120 kt (61.73 m/s) the heading-hold gain is Kψ = 0.5° of bank per degree of heading error, with a 25° bank limit. (a) The pilot selects a heading 30° to the right: find the commanded bank and the initial turn rate. (b) Find τψ and the time for the error to fall to 1°. (c) In altitude hold with Kh = 0.1° of pitch per metre, a downdraft leaves the aircraft 30 m low. Find the pitch command above trim, the initial climb rate, τh and the time to be within 1 m.

  1. φc = 0.5 × 30 = 15°, inside the 25° limit.
  2. ψ̇ = 9.81 × tan 15° ÷ 61.73 = 9.81 × 0.2679 ÷ 61.73 = 0.04258 rad/s = 2.44°/s.
  3. τψ = 61.73 ÷ (9.81 × 0.5) = 12.6 s. Kψ is degrees per degree, so it needs no conversion.
  4. The error falls as 30° × e−t/12.6; it reaches 1° when t = 12.6 × ln 30 = 12.6 × 3.40 = 42.8 s.
  5. θc − θtrim = 0.1 × 30 = 3°; initial climb rate ḣ = 61.73 × sin 3° = 3.23 m/s (636 ft/min).
  6. Kh = 0.1°/m = 0.001745 rad/m; τh = 1 ÷ (61.73 × 0.001745) = 9.28 s; within 1 m after 9.28 × ln 30 = 31.6 s.

Answer. (a) 15° bank, 2.44°/s. (b) τψ = 12.6 s, 42.8 s to within 1°. (c) +3° pitch, 3.23 m/s climb, τh = 9.28 s, 31.6 s to within 1 m.

Exam traps

  • Putting knots and degrees per second straight into tan φ = Vω ÷ g. Convert TAS to m/s and ω to rad/s first.
  • Forgetting the bank limit: at high speed a standard-rate turn needs more bank than the autopilot allows, so it turns more slowly.
  • Converting Kψ (degrees per degree, already a ratio) but forgetting to convert Kh (degrees per metre) to radians per metre.
  • Drawing the outer loop driving the servo. The outer loop only commands the inner loop.
  • Altitude hold without the autothrottle: the climb back to altitude costs speed.

Check yourself

Remember Which sensors feed pitch-attitude hold, and which feeds altitude hold? 2 marks

Pitch hold: pitch angle and pitch rate from the AHRS or IRS. Altitude hold: altitude from the air data computer.

Understand Why does pitch-attitude hold feed back pitch rate as well as pitch angle? 3 marks

Angle feedback alone acts like a spring and lets the nose oscillate. Rate feedback opposes the motion like a damper, so the attitude settles without bobbing.

Understand Why does heading hold limit the bank angle? 2 marks

A large heading error would otherwise command a steep bank, raising load factor and stall speed; the limit keeps turns comfortable and safe.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Real autopilots change their gains with airspeed and altitude (gain scheduling), because the same elevator movement does far more at high dynamic pressure. Many modern designs replace separate pitch and thrust loops with a total energy control system, which uses thrust for total energy and pitch for the split between height and speed. 6.4 Approach and landing guidance: coupled ILS approach and autoland

In a coupled approach the autopilot, not the pilot’s hands, flies the ILS localizer and glide slope down to the decision height; autoland carries on through the flare, touchdown and rollout. Guidance (the beams), navigation (deviations and radio altitude) and control (chapter 6.3) meet here with the least room for error. No syllabus lists this topic in the unit; it is added here to complete the subject.

You need: Unit 5, Landing aids: ILS and MLS (how the beams are formed); chapter 6.3 for the loops.

The path in space

The glide slope is usually 3° and is placed so that the path crosses the runway threshold about 50 ft (15 m) up, the threshold crossing height (TCH). At a distance d before the threshold, the height above it is

h = d × tan 3° + 15 m        (tan 3° = 0.0524)

The rate of descent is ground speed × tan 3°. With ground speed in knots this is close to 5 × GS in ft/min, and the path drops about 300 ft per NM (318 ft exactly).

The ILS reports an angle off the path, not metres. The same angle is fewer metres near the runway, because the linear error is d × tan ε for an angular error ε. So the beam grows more sensitive in metres as the aircraft closes in, and a gain that is right at 4 NM would make the autopilot over-correct and oscillate near the threshold. Autopilots therefore reduce their beam gains with radio altitude (gain scheduling).

The coupled approach, step by step

  1. Arm: the crew tunes the ILS (or the FMS does), flies a heading to join the localizer and arms approach mode (APP).
  2. Localizer capture and track: when the localizer deviation becomes small, the lateral outer loop switches from heading to localizer; bank commands now remove the lateral deviation.
  3. Glide slope capture from below: the aircraft meets the glide slope from below, because false glide paths exist at steeper angles above the true one. The vertical outer loop switches from altitude hold to glide-slope track, and pitch commands remove the vertical deviation.
  4. Track: the autothrottle holds the approach speed while monitors compare the channels and the beam signals.
  5. Decision height: the pilot must see the required runway lights or markings by DH; if not, the pilot presses TO/GA and the autopilot flies the go-around.

Decision height (DH) is the height above the threshold at which the pilot must see the runway environment or go around; for CAT II and III it is read on the radio altimeter. Runway visual range (RVR) is how far down the runway the pilot can see the lights.

Autoland

Below CAT II limits the pilot cannot see enough to land by hand, so the system lands. Heights vary by type.

  1. Land mode: somewhere between a few hundred and 1,500 ft radio altitude, the system checks every channel and annunciates what it can do.
  2. Align (decrab): in a crosswind the aircraft has been crabbing, nose into wind; just before touchdown the rudder yaws the nose onto the runway line while the ailerons keep the wings nearly level.
  3. Flare: from about 50 ft the pitch loop drops the glide slope and follows a flare law that commands sink rate in proportion to height, ḣ = −h ÷ τ, where τ is the flare time constant. The sink rate falls as the wheels near the runway.
  4. Retard: thrust goes to idle close to the ground (some types call “RETARD” at 10 ft).
  5. Rollout: rudder and then nose-wheel steering follow the localizer along the centreline while the autobrake slows the aircraft.

Two words describe how failures are handled. A fail-passive system causes no significant path change when one part fails, but it disconnects and the pilot must take over at once; it usually means one autopilot and suits CAT IIIA with a DH. A fail-operational system completes the landing after one failure; it needs at least two autopilots engaged and allows CAT IIIB landings with no DH. Below 200 ft the radio altimeter is the key sensor for both.

Worked example 1

Question. An aircraft flies a 3° ILS at a ground speed of 140 kt; TCH = 15 m (50 ft). Find (a) its height above the threshold 4 NM out, with a rule-of-thumb check, (b) its rate of descent, and (c) the distance and time before the threshold at which it reaches a CAT I DH of 200 ft and a CAT II DH of 100 ft.

  1. d = 4 × 1,852 = 7,408 m; h = 7,408 × 0.0524 + 15 = 388.2 + 15 = 403.2 m = 1,323 ft. Rule: 4 × 300 + 50 = 1,250 ft.
  2. GS = 140 × 1,852 ÷ 3,600 = 72.0 m/s; rate of descent = 72.0 × 0.0524 = 3.77 m/s = 743 ft/min. Rule: 5 × 140 = 700 ft/min.
  3. CAT I: height above the TCH = 200 − 50 = 150 ft = 45.7 m; d = 45.7 ÷ 0.0524 = 872 m (0.47 NM); time = 872 ÷ 72.0 = 12.1 s.
  4. CAT II: 100 − 50 = 50 ft = 15.2 m; d = 15.2 ÷ 0.0524 = 291 m; time = 291 ÷ 72.0 = 4.0 s.

Answer. (a) 1,323 ft; (b) 743 ft/min; (c) CAT I: 872 m and 12.1 s before the threshold; CAT II: 291 m and only 4.0 s, which is why low categories need certified automatic systems and trained crews.

Worked example 2

Question. (a) An aircraft is 0.2° below the glide path. How many metres low is it at 4 NM and at 0.5 NM? (b) An exponential flare with τ = 4 s starts at 50 ft. Find the commanded sink rate at 50, 20 and 10 ft and compare it with the glide-slope sink rate of example 1.

  1. tan 0.2° = 0.00349. At 4 NM: 7,408 × 0.00349 = 25.9 m low.
  2. At 0.5 NM: 926 × 0.00349 = 3.2 m low. The same beam error is eight times smaller in metres, so the beam gain must come down near the runway.
  3. Flare, ḣ = −h ÷ 4: at 50 ft, 12.5 ft/s (750 ft/min); at 20 ft, 5.0 ft/s; at 10 ft, 2.5 ft/s.
  4. Glide-slope sink = 3.77 m/s = 12.4 ft/s, so the flare starts at almost the same sink rate (no jolt). A pure exponential never reaches the ground, so real flare laws aim a little below the runway.

Answer. (a) 25.9 m and 3.2 m. (b) 12.5, 5.0 and 2.5 ft/s; the flare blends smoothly from the 12.4 ft/s of the glide slope.

Exam traps

  • Measuring DH above sea level. It is height above the threshold, read on the radio altimeter for CAT II and III.
  • Forgetting the 50 ft threshold crossing height when finding where the path reaches a given height.
  • Swapping fail-passive and fail-operational. Fail-passive disconnects safely; fail-operational carries on and lands.
  • Joining the glide slope from above, where false glide paths lie.

Check yourself

Remember State the decision height and RVR for CAT I and CAT II. 2 marks

CAT I: DH 200 ft, RVR 550 m. CAT II: DH below 200 ft but not below 100 ft, RVR not less than 300 m.

Remember List the phases of an autoland in order. 2 marks

Localizer and glide-slope track, land mode check, align (decrab), flare, retard, touchdown, rollout.

Understand Why is the glide slope joined from below? 2 marks

False glide paths lie at steeper angles above the true one; from below, the true 3° path is met first.

Understand Why must the autopilot’s beam gain fall as the aircraft nears the runway? 3 marks

The beam measures angle; a fixed angle is fewer metres close in, so a fixed gain gives larger commands per metre of error and the aircraft would over-correct and oscillate.

Apply Find the height above the threshold 3 NM out on a 3° glide path with a 15 m TCH. 2 marks

5,556 × 0.0524 + 15 = 291.2 + 15 = 306.2 m = 1,005 ft.

Analyze Fog at an airport leaves an RVR of 100 m. Which category of approach is needed, what kind of autoland system, and what must be in place on the ground? 5 marks

CAT IIIB. A fail-operational autoland with at least two autopilots and radio altimeters. On the ground: a CAT III ILS with its critical areas kept clear under low-visibility procedures, centreline and touchdown-zone lights, RVR measurement, and a crew trained and approved for CAT III.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. The GBAS landing system (GLS) gives ILS-like guidance from GPS corrected by a station at the airport, and satellite-based augmentation, such as India’s GAGAN, supports LPV approaches with vertical guidance where no ILS exists. Head-up displays with enhanced vision let crews hand-fly some low-visibility approaches. Search next for GLS, LPV approach and enhanced flight vision systems.

6.5 Spacecraft systems: power, thermal and attitude control

A spacecraft must make its own electricity, hold its own temperature and point the right way, alone and for years. Three subsystems do this: power (solar array, battery and regulation), thermal control, and attitude determination and control. Only a few universities teach this topic in the unit, so the chapter is short; some syllabi call the last part attitude stabilisation.

Power

In low Earth orbit (LEO) a satellite spends part of every orbit in Earth’s shadow. The array must run the loads in sunlight and recharge the battery; the battery runs the loads through eclipse. With load P, eclipse time Te, sunlight time Td, and path efficiencies Xe ≈ 0.6 (through the battery) and Xd ≈ 0.8 (direct):

P_array = (P·Te/Xe + P·Td/Xd) ÷ Td
Battery capacity C = P·Te ÷ (DoD × η)

DoD is the allowed depth of discharge and η the discharge efficiency. A LEO battery cycles about 5,500 times a year, so lithium-ion cells are kept to a shallow DoD, often 20 to 30 %. Multi-junction solar cells convert about 30 % of the 1,361 W/m² of sunlight.

Thermal and attitude

In vacuum, heat leaves only by radiation. A radiator of area A, emissivity ε and absolute temperature T rejects Q = εσAT⁴, with σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W/m²K⁴. Multilayer insulation, coatings and heat pipes do most of the work; heaters and louvres trim it.

Attitude is sensed by sun sensors, Earth sensors, magnetometers, gyros and star trackers (Unit 5 covers gyros). Spin stabilisation uses the gyroscopic stiffness of a spinning body. Three-axis stabilisation holds every axis with reaction wheels: a wheel speeds up to soak up a disturbance torque T from gravity gradient, solar pressure, drag or magnetism, storing momentum H = T × t. A steady torque saturates the wheel, so magnetorquers or thrusters dump the momentum. The whole set is a GNC loop (chapter 6.2) in space.

Worked example

Question. A satellite in a 500 km LEO has a 95 min orbit with 35 min of eclipse and a steady 500 W load. Take Xe = 0.6, Xd = 0.8, DoD = 30 %, η = 0.9, cells of 30 % efficiency with a 0.9 assembly factor facing the Sun. Find (a) the battery capacity, (b) the array power and area, (c) the radiator area to reject 450 W at 290 K with ε = 0.85, and (d) how long a 0.4 N·m·s reaction wheel lasts against a steady 2 × 10⁻⁵ N·m disturbance.

  1. Eclipse energy: 500 × 35 ÷ 60 = 291.7 Wh; C = 291.7 ÷ (0.30 × 0.9) = 1,080 Wh.
  2. Array: (500 × 35 ÷ 0.6 + 500 × 60 ÷ 0.8) ÷ 60 = (29,167 + 37,500) ÷ 60 = 1,111 W.
  3. Output per m² = 1,361 × 0.30 × 0.9 = 367.5 W/m²; area = 1,111 ÷ 367.5 = 3.02 m².
  4. Radiator: εσT⁴ = 0.85 × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 290⁴ = 0.85 × 401.0 = 340.9 W/m²; A = 450 ÷ 340.9 = 1.32 m².
  5. Momentum per orbit: 2 × 10⁻⁵ × 5,700 s = 0.114 N·m·s; 0.4 ÷ 0.114 = 3.5 orbits to saturation.

Answer. 1,080 Wh; 1,111 W from 3.02 m²; 1.32 m² of radiator; the wheel saturates in 3.5 orbits, so momentum is dumped every orbit or two.

Exam traps

  • Putting °C into T⁴. Use kelvin: 17 °C is 290 K.
  • Sizing the battery from power instead of eclipse energy, or forgetting the DoD.
  • Thinking reaction wheels remove momentum. They only store it; magnetorquers or thrusters remove it.

Check yourself

Remember Name four attitude sensors and two attitude actuators. 2 marks

Sensors: sun sensor, Earth sensor, magnetometer, gyro, star tracker. Actuators: reaction wheels, magnetorquers, thrusters.

Understand Why is the depth of discharge kept low for a LEO battery? 2 marks

It cycles about 5,500 times a year; shallow cycles let lithium-ion cells survive that many without losing too much capacity.

Understand Why must momentum be dumped from reaction wheels? 2 marks

A steady disturbance keeps adding momentum; the wheel speeds up until it reaches its limit and can no longer absorb torque.

Apply Find the radiator area to reject 300 W at 300 K with ε = 0.8. 2 marks

0.8 × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × 300⁴ = 367.4 W/m²; A = 300 ÷ 367.4 = 0.82 m².

Analyze To save mass, a designer raises the DoD from 30 % to 60 % in the worked example. What changes, and why is it risky? 4 marks

Capacity halves to 540 Wh, saving battery mass. But each of about 5,500 yearly cycles is twice as deep, so the cells fade faster and may not carry the eclipse load at end of life.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. CubeSats now carry complete attitude units, with tiny star trackers and wheels, in a box smaller than a 10 cm cube. Larger satellites use solar array drives to track the Sun and electric propulsion for orbit raising. Search next for CubeSat ADCS, momentum management and spacecraft thermal design.

  1. Navigation estimates the state, guidance turns the state and the mission into commands, and control moves the actuators; the loops are nested, with control the fastest.
  2. Guidance falls into broad families (preprogrammed, command, beam and homing), and real missions switch family by phase of flight.
  3. The FMS does navigation, flight planning, performance and guidance; it commands the autopilot and autothrottle but moves nothing itself.
  4. Time and fuel use ground speed, not TAS; top of descent is Δh ÷ tan γ, about 3 NM per 1,000 ft on a 3° path.
  5. An autopilot holds attitude in fast inner loops with rate damping, and altitude or heading in slower outer loops with time constants 1 ÷ (V Kh) and V ÷ (g Kψ).
  6. A standard-rate turn needs tan φ = Vω ÷ g; at high speed the bank limit makes the autopilot turn more slowly.
  7. On a 3° ILS, h = d tan 3° + 15 m; decision heights fall within a kilometre of the threshold, beam gains are reduced close in, and autoland adds flare, retard and rollout with fail-passive or fail-operational redundancy.
  8. A spacecraft’s array must earn the eclipse energy too; its battery is sized by eclipse energy and depth of discharge, its radiator by εσAT⁴, and its reaction wheels store momentum that magnetorquers must dump.

Most syllabi that teach this unit ask for the autopilot loops (pitch, roll and lateral, heading) with block diagrams, the guidance system and its types, and the functions of the flight management system. Some also list gyroscopes and the types of control systems here; gyroscopes are in Unit 5, and fly-by-wire, fly-by-light and IMA are in Units 1 and 4.

Analyze Compare preprogrammed, command, beam and homing guidance for (a) the last 300 m of a spacecraft docking and (b) the last 2 NM of an airliner approach. Choose a family for each and justify the choice. 13 marks

Points to cover. Glossary

AHRS
Attitude and heading reference system: gives pitch, roll, heading and body rates.
AIRAC cycle
The 28-day cycle on which navigation databases are replaced worldwide.
Autoland
Automatic landing through flare, touchdown and rollout.
Beam guidance
Guidance along a path marked in space by a ground transmitter, such as the ILS.
Command guidance
Guidance in which an outside station decides and sends the commands.
Cost index
Ratio of time cost to fuel cost that sets the FMS speeds.
Decision height (DH)
Height above the threshold at which the pilot must see the runway or go around.
Depth of discharge (DoD)
Fraction of battery capacity used in one discharge.
Fail-operational
A system that completes its task after one failure.
Fail-passive
A system that fails without a dangerous deviation but stops working.
Flare
The final pitch-up that cuts the sink rate before touchdown.
Flight management system (FMS)
Computer that plans the route, predicts time and fuel and steers the autopilot.
Gain scheduling
Changing a controller’s gains with flight condition, such as height or airspeed.
Glide slope
The ILS vertical beam, usually 3°.
GNC
Guidance, navigation and control: the loop by which a vehicle steers itself.
Homing
Guidance using an on-board sensor that senses the destination directly.
Inner loop
The fast loop that holds attitude and rates.
LNAV, VNAV
Lateral and vertical navigation modes in which the FMS steers the autopilot.
Localizer
The ILS lateral beam aligned with the runway centreline.
Magnetorquer
Coil that pushes against Earth’s magnetic field to dump wheel momentum.
MCDU
Multifunction control and display unit: the crew’s keyboard and screen for the FMS.
Outer loop
The slower loop that holds a path quantity by commanding the inner loop.
Preprogrammed guidance
Following a path stored before the start with the vehicle’s own navigation.
Reaction wheel
Flywheel whose speed changes to turn a spacecraft or absorb torque.
RVR
Runway visual range: how far along the runway the lights can be seen.
Standard-rate turn
A turn at 3°/s, 360° in two minutes.
Top of descent (TOD)
The point where the descent from cruise must begin.
Yaw damper
Rudder loop on yaw rate that damps the Dutch roll.
