1.1 Need for control systems; conventional flight controls

An aircraft left alone does not hold an altitude, a heading or a bank angle. It drifts with every gust and every change of thrust. A flight control system is the chain that lets a pilot, or later a computer, demand a motion and get it: a cockpit control moves, linkage carries the movement to a surface, the surface deflects into the airflow, airloads appear, and the aircraft rotates about one of its axes. The same chain runs from a Cessna 172R to an airliner; only the source of the force changes, which is the step taken in power-assisted and fully powered controls.

Six freedoms, three axes, three primary surfaces

An aircraft has six degrees of freedom: three translations (fore-aft, lateral, vertical) and three rotations. A degree of freedom is one independent way the body can move. A flight control system commands the rotations directly; translation follows from attitude and thrust.

  1. Roll — rotation about the longitudinal axis, nose to tail. The ailerons move in opposite senses: the up-going aileron cuts lift on its wing and the down-going one raises it, so the aircraft banks.
  2. Pitch — rotation about the lateral axis, wingtip to wingtip. The elevator sits on the horizontal tail; trailing edge up pushes the tail down and the nose up.
  3. Yaw — rotation about the vertical axis. The rudder sits on the vertical tail; trailing edge left pushes the tail right and the nose left.

The ailerons also cause adverse yaw: the down-going aileron makes more drag and pulls the nose away from the turn. The rudder corrects it. Together the aileron, elevator and rudder are the primary flight controls.

A pilot commands a deflection, not a force. At speed a few degrees of surface produce a large moment; the linkage gearing sets how many millimetres of stick travel give one degree of surface. That ratio is the first design choice.

Secondary controls work on the wing or on the surface, not on the axis directly

  • Flaps: trailing-edge surfaces on the wing. Extending them raises lift and drag, so the aircraft can fly slower for take-off and landing.
  • Slats: leading-edge surfaces that extend to delay stall at a high angle of attack.
  • Spoilers: upper-surface wing panels that lift up to spoil lift and add drag. They are used for descent, as a speedbrake, and on some aircraft to help roll.
  • Trim tabs: small hinged tabs on a primary surface. Moving the tab changes the hinge moment, so the surface stays deflected with little or no stick force. The tab moves the surface; the surface moves the aircraft.
  • Throttle: not a surface, but part of the control chain because it sets thrust.

Some syllabi group these as auxiliary controls. Notice the difference: a primary control changes an axis of motion, while a secondary control changes the wing's lift or the surface's hinge moment.

The conventional chain, part by part

The Cessna 172R, a four-seat single-engine trainer, has fully mechanical controls: a control wheel (yoke) on a column, two rudder pedals, a throttle, a mixture control and a manual elevator trim wheel. No hydraulic boost sits between pilot and surface. Follow one demand in a light aircraft of this kind: pull the column back; a short push-pull rod turns a bell crank under the floor; two cables run aft over pulleys; a turnbuckle in each cable sets its tension; at the tail the cable pulls the elevator horn, and the elevator rotates trailing edge up about its hinge.

The chain is sized by its gearing G: the stick travel per unit of surface rotation. If friction is small, the work the pilot puts in equals the work done on the surface, F × (stick travel) = H × (surface rotation), so the stick force follows directly from the hinge moment.

F = H ÷ G

F is the stick force in N; H is the aerodynamic hinge moment in N·m; G is the gearing in metres of stick travel per radian of surface rotation (multiply mm per degree by 57.3 and divide by 1000). Small gearing (few mm per degree) makes the aircraft twitchy and the forces high; large gearing makes the response sluggish and the forces light.

Worked example

Question. The elevator of a light aircraft moves 25° when the control column travels 120 mm from neutral. The elevator horn arm is 80 mm and the rod or cable pulls at right angles to it. The pilot holds the column with 60 N. Ignoring friction, find (a) the gearing in mm per degree and in m per radian, (b) the hinge moment the pilot can hold, (c) the force in the rod at the horn and the mechanical advantage of the linkage.

  1. (a) Gearing = column travel ÷ elevator angle = 120 mm ÷ 25° = 4.8 mm per degree.
  2. In radians: 25° = 25 × π/180 = 0.4363 rad, so G = 0.120 m ÷ 0.4363 rad = 0.2750 m/rad.
  3. (b) With no friction, F × travel = H × rotation, so H = F × G = 60 N × 0.2750 m/rad = 16.50 N·m.
  4. (c) The horn tip moves along an arc of 80 mm × 0.4363 rad = 34.9 mm while the column moves 120 mm.
  5. Rod force = H ÷ horn arm = 16.50 N·m ÷ 0.080 m = 206.3 N. If the rod pulled at an angle θ to the horn, only F sin θ would turn the surface.
  6. Mechanical advantage = rod force ÷ pilot force = 206.3 ÷ 60 = 3.44. Check: it equals the travel ratio 120 mm ÷ 34.9 mm = 3.44, and the work is the same at both ends: 60 N × 120 mm = 206.3 N × 34.9 mm = 7200 N·mm.
  7. In a steady hold the rod moment balances the aerodynamic hinge moment; the pilot must push a little harder only to accelerate the surface or to beat friction.

Exam traps

  • Putting roll on the rudder. The rudder yaws the aircraft; the ailerons roll it, and they also cause adverse yaw.
  • Saying large gearing makes the controls heavy. F = H ÷ G: many millimetres of stick per degree give light forces and a sluggish response; few millimetres per degree give heavy forces and a twitchy one.
  • Mixing the axes: roll is about the longitudinal axis, pitch about the lateral axis, yaw about the vertical axis.
  • Using millimetres in a hinge-moment sum. Convert the horn arm to metres (80 mm = 0.080 m), so the answer is in N·m, not N·mm.
  • Thinking ailerons turn the aircraft directly. They bank it; the horizontal component of lift does the turning.

1.2 Push-pull rod and cable-pulley systems

A pilot's command must travel from the stick or pedal to a surface far away. Two mechanical ways do this. A push-pull rod is a tube that carries the command in both directions, push and pull, and needs bell cranks and guides to route it. A cable system carries only pull, so it always runs as a matched pair: one cable pulls the surface one way while the other slackens. Rods suit short, straight runs; cables suit long, curved runs. Both are still found on light aircraft and as the mechanical backbone of power-assisted airliners.

You need the chapter on conventional flight controls for the cockpit controls and the surfaces they move.

Push-pull rod: a tube that takes both directions

A push-pull rod is a thin metal tube with an end fitting at each end. The fitting ends in a ball-jointed rod end: a spherical bearing inside a housing. The ball lets the rod tilt a few degrees, so the rod carries only an axial push or pull and no bending moment passes into the tube; any side load must be taken by guides or idler levers. A rod carries tension when pulled and compression when pushed. Tension is easy; compression can make a long thin tube buckle sideways. For a long tube the limit is set by Euler buckling, not by material strength.

Pcr = π²EI/(KL)²

Pcr is the critical buckling load in N; E is Young's modulus in Pa; I is the second moment of area of the tube section in m⁴; L is the unsupported length in m; K is the end-condition factor, 1 for pin-ended rods. A guide that holds the rod sideways halves the unsupported length and so multiplies Pcr by four.

σ = F/A

σ is axial stress in Pa; F is axial force in N; A is cross-section area in m².

A bell crank is a two-armed lever on a pivot. It redirects the rod and can change the ratio of motion by unequal arm lengths. An idler lever only supports and redirects; it does not change the ratio. Guides and idler levers break a long run into short unsupported lengths, which raises the buckling load. Every joint adds backlash, a small lost motion. Temperature changes the rod length, so rigging must leave adjustment.

Cable and pulley: pull only, so always in pairs

Aircraft control cable is stranded steel: many thin wires twisted into strands, strands into a cable. It is flexible and strong in tension. It cannot push. So the surface is driven by a closed loop of two cables. When the pilot moves the control, one cable pulls the horn and the other cable goes slack. The slack cable is kept under pre-tension so it follows without lost motion when the direction reverses.

Pulleys redirect the cable. Fairleads are small guides that keep the cable in line through structure. At a pressurised bulkhead, a pressure seal lets the cable pass without leaking cabin air. A turnbuckle is a threaded link that shortens or lengthens the cable to set tension. A tension regulator is a spring-loaded device that keeps tension nearly constant as the aircraft expands with temperature and the cable stretches. Rigging pins lock the surfaces and controls at neutral while tensions are set.

A flexible push-pull cable is different: a stranded inner core slides inside a flexible conduit. It can follow a curved route and carries push and pull, but with more friction than a rod. It is common for throttle, mixture and other secondary controls.

ΔL = FL/(AE)

ΔL is cable stretch in m; F is tension in N; L is cable length in m; A is the metal cross-section area in m²; E is Young's modulus in Pa.

Backlash, friction and the comparison

Backlash, also called lost motion, is the small movement at a joint before the load is carried. Rods have backlash at each ball joint and bell crank bearing. Cables have stretch and pulley clearance. Backlash makes the control feel loose and can cause flutter if it grows.

Friction rises with every pulley and fairlead, so a long cable run can feel heavy. Rods have less friction but need straight lines and strong supports. A rod can jam if the structure bends into its path; a cable can jump a pulley or wear through a fairlead. Maintenance checks tension, pulley freedom, cable wear and rod-end play.

Where the mechanical system stops

As aircraft grow faster and larger, the airload on a surface, and so the hinge moment, grows beyond a pilot's strength. Friction and stretch in a long cable run make the response imprecise. Rods add weight and need many bell cranks. The mechanical system still gives the command, but it needs help to supply the force. That help is the subject of the chapter on powered flight controls.

Worked example

Question. A rudder cable is 3 m long, with a metal cross-section of 6 mm², steel with E = 200 GPa. When the pilot pushes the pedal hard the cable carries 1.5 kN. (a) Find the stretch. (b) A push-pull rod of the same 3 m length is an aluminium-alloy tube, 20 mm outside and 18 mm inside diameter, E = 70 GPa, carrying the same 1.5 kN in compression. Find the stress and the Euler load with pinned ends. (c) How many equal bays, separated by guides, are needed for a buckling load of at least 1.5 times the working load?

  1. (a) Convert: A = 6 mm² = 6 × 10⁻⁶ m²; E = 200 × 10⁹ Pa; F = 1500 N; L = 3 m. Then AE = 6 × 10⁻⁶ × 200 × 10⁹ = 1.2 × 10⁶ N.
  2. ΔL = FL ÷ (AE) = 1500 × 3 ÷ (1.2 × 10⁶) = 3.75 × 10⁻³ m = 3.75 mm.
  3. (b) Tube area A = π/4 × (20² − 18²) = π/4 × 76 = 59.7 mm². Stress σ = 1500 N ÷ 59.7 mm² = 25.1 N/mm² = 25.1 MPa, far below the yield stress of the alloy.
  4. Second moment of area I = π/64 × (20⁴ − 18⁴) = π/64 × 55 024 = 2701 mm⁴.
  5. Euler load over the full 3 m: Pcr = π² × 70 000 N/mm² × 2701 mm⁴ ÷ (3000 mm)² = 207 N. The rod would buckle at one-seventh of the working load.
  6. (c) Required Pcr = 1.5 × 1500 = 2250 N. Since Pcr grows with 1/L², the bay length must be at most 3000 × √(207 ÷ 2250) = 911 mm.
  7. Three bays of 1000 mm give 207 × 3² = 1866 N, not enough; four bays of 750 mm give 207 × 4² = 3317 N, which passes. So three guides are needed along the run.

Exam traps

  • Drawing one cable that both pushes and pulls the surface. A cable can only pull, so the surface needs a second cable pulling the other way.
  • Checking a push-pull rod in compression by stress alone. A long thin rod fails by buckling at a load far below its stress limit.
  • Forgetting pre-tension in the return cable. A slack cable gives lost motion and a delayed response when the control reverses.
  • Confusing a bell crank with an idler lever. A bell crank redirects and can change the motion ratio; an idler only supports and redirects.
  • Mixing mm² and m² in ΔL = FL/(AE). Using 6 instead of 6 × 10⁻⁶ gives a stretch a million times too large.

1.3 Power-assisted and fully powered flight controls

As an aircraft grows bigger and faster, the air load on a flap or elevator grows far faster than a pilot's arm. Hydraulics supplies the missing force. In a power-assisted system the mechanical link of rods and cables stays and a hydraulic boost acts in parallel with it. In a fully powered system the link is deleted and actuators alone move the surface. All large transports and most fighters are fully powered, while smaller types often stay power-assisted. This chapter shows why the force outgrows the pilot, how the boost loop closes, and what redundancy an irreversible system needs.

You need: the chapter on push-pull rods and cables for the mechanical link that the boost sits in parallel with.

Why the pilot's arm runs out

The air load is spread over the whole control surface. Each small element of load sits at some distance behind the hinge, and the sum of load times that distance is the hinge moment M, which the pilot's linkage must carry.

M = q S c Ch, q = ½ ρ V²

M is the hinge moment in N·m; q is the dynamic pressure in Pa; ρ is air density in kg/m³; V is true airspeed in m/s; S is the surface area in m²; c is its chord (mean width) in m; and Ch is the dimensionless hinge moment coefficient, set by shape and deflection.

Two things make M grow fast. Speed enters as V², so doubling speed quadruples the moment. Size is worse: area S grows with the square of a length L and chord c grows with L, so M grows with L³. An aircraft twice as long needs about eight times the hinge moment at the same Ch and speed. A trainer at 60 m/s and an airliner at 250 m/s differ by (250/60)² ≈ 17 in q alone.

A pilot can pull a few hundred newtons for a moment and much less for a long hold. Through a linkage of gearing G the pilot can hold a hinge moment F × G (see conventional flight controls); with F = 200 N and G = 0.30 m/rad that is only 60 N·m. A larger G lowers the force but makes the stick travel impossibly long. Once M reaches a few hundred N·m, a pure rod-and-cable link cannot do the job. Hydraulics closes the gap.

Power-assisted (boost) systems

A power-assisted system — also called boosted, servo-assisted or partial power — keeps the mechanical link and adds a hydraulic actuator in parallel with it. The pilot's command still travels down the rods or cables; the actuator only shares the load.

  1. The pilot moves the column, and the input linkage shifts the spool of a servo valve.
  2. The open valve meters hydraulic fluid at system pressure into one side of the actuator.
  3. The piston drives the surface, and its force is summed with the pilot's own rod at a summing lever.
  4. A follow-up linkage from the surface back to the valve returns the spool to neutral when the surface reaches the demanded angle, which stops the motion.
  5. With the valve closed and the pilot's hand off the control, trapped fluid locks the actuator and the surface holds its position.

The follow-up linkage is position feedback. Without it the valve would stay open and the surface would run to its stop. The boost ratio is the total hinge moment divided by the share the pilot's linkage carries, so a ratio of 30 means the pilot supplies 1/30 of the moment and the hydraulics supply about 97%. Because part of the load still comes back up the linkage, the pilot keeps a natural feel of the air.

If the hydraulics fail, the pilot can still fly. This is manual reversion. A bypass valve, or an open-centre servo valve, lets fluid move freely between the two sides of the actuator, so the surface is no longer locked. The pilot's rods and cables then drive it directly, with much higher stick forces and a restricted speed. Several smaller transports, turboprops, trainers and business jets use boosted controls for exactly this reason.

Fully powered and power-actuated systems

A fully powered system — also called irreversible or all-powered — deletes the manual path. The pilot's input only positions a servo valve, or tells a flight control computer what is wanted. Actuators alone move the surface.

Irreversible means the surface cannot drive back. Fluid trapped by a closed servo valve locks the actuator, so an air load cannot push the piston and cannot move the pilot's control. That is good: a gust does not snatch the stick. It is also the catch: with the link gone, the pilot has no natural feel at all, and an artificial feel system becomes compulsory (see the chapter on Q-feel and servo tabs).

  • Redundancy: two or three actuators per surface, fed by separate hydraulic systems, so one system failure still leaves control.
  • Jamming: a seized actuator can hold a surface hard over. Separate actuators, jam detection and sometimes a breakable connection limit the damage.
  • Rate limiting: flow, not pressure, sets how fast the surface moves. At high q the surface may lag the command and the lag can feed a pilot-induced oscillation.
  • Dissimilarity: channels are deliberately not identical, for example hydraulic on one and electric on another, so one fault type cannot take all of them.

Power-actuated systems go further. An electro-hydrostatic actuator carries its own small pump, driven by an electric motor bolted at the surface, and draws only electrical power and a little fluid. This removes long runs of high-pressure pipe, which leak and are heavy, but the electrical generation must grow to match (see aircraft electrical power generation).

Worked example

Question. An elevator has area S = 4 m², chord c = 0.5 m and hinge moment coefficient Ch = 0.15 at 100 m/s in air of density 1.225 kg/m³. (a) Find the hinge moment. (b) The pilot gives 200 N through a linkage of gearing 0.30 m/rad. Find the boost ratio needed. (c) The actuator works on a 0.10 m arm at 3000 psi (20.7 MPa). Find the piston area for the hydraulic share. (d) Find the hinge moment at 200 m/s.

  1. Dynamic pressure: q = ½ ρ V² = 0.5 × 1.225 × 100² = 6125 Pa.
  2. (a) Hinge moment: M = q S c Ch = 6125 × 4 × 0.5 × 0.15 = 1837.5 N·m.
  3. (b) Pilot share: F × G = 200 N × 0.30 m/rad = 60 N·m. Boost ratio = 1837.5 ÷ 60 = 30.6, so the hydraulics supply 1837.5 − 60 = 1777.5 N·m, 96.7% of the moment.
  4. (c) Actuator force = 1777.5 N·m ÷ 0.10 m = 17 775 N. Piston area = force ÷ pressure = 17 775 ÷ (20.7 × 10⁶) = 8.59 × 10⁻⁴ m² = 8.59 cm², a bore of about 33 mm. A real actuator is made larger to leave margin for the pressure drop across the valve.
  5. (d) At 200 m/s: q = 0.5 × 1.225 × 200² = 24 500 Pa, four times larger, so M = 24 500 × 4 × 0.5 × 0.15 = 7350 N·m.

Answer. The hinge moment is 1837.5 N·m; a boost ratio of 30.6 leaves the pilot 60 N·m; the actuator needs about 8.6 cm² of piston at 20.7 MPa; at 200 m/s the same surface needs 7350 N·m.

Exam traps

  • Saying a fully powered aircraft needs no feel system. It is the exact opposite: with the link gone, artificial feel is the only thing giving the pilot any stick force.
  • Calling a boosted system irreversible. A power-assisted system keeps the mechanical link and can revert to manual, with heavy forces.
  • Using V instead of V² in q, or dropping the chord c. Treat c and Ch as separate factors; Ch is dimensionless, c is in metres.
  • Thinking the follow-up linkage adds feel. It is position feedback that closes the loop and stops the surface at the commanded angle.
  • Believing higher hydraulic pressure gives a faster surface. Pressure sets the force; flow sets the rate.

1.4 Q-feel system and servo tabs

An irreversible powered control has no natural feel: the actuator swallows the whole hinge moment and the stick is disconnected from the airloads. Q-feel puts feel back artificially, so stick force grows with dynamic pressure q = ½ρV² — light at low speed, firm at high speed. Tabs do the same aerodynamically: a small tab with a small hinge moment drives or lightens a large surface. Nearly every large transport has artificial feel of some kind, and tabs remain common on manual and boosted surfaces. Some syllabi treat this topic only briefly, inside powered controls.

You need: the chapter on powered flight controls for the irreversible powered system that has lost its natural feel.

Why feel must follow dynamic pressure

In an irreversible powered control the actuator carries the whole hinge moment. Airloads no longer push back on the stick, so feel must be made on purpose. A plain spring is the obvious answer and the wrong one: the response changes with speed, the spring does not.

q = ½ ρ V²

q is dynamic pressure in Pa (N/m²); ρ is air density in kg/m³, about 1.225 at sea level in the standard atmosphere; V is true airspeed in m/s.

Hinge moment grows roughly with q times deflection. At 250 m/s, q is about eleven times its value at 75 m/s. A fixed spring would make the aircraft razor-sharp at high speed and sluggish at low speed. Q-feel makes the spring rate a function of q, so the stick force for a given stick movement follows the square of airspeed, as natural feel would on a manual aircraft.

The Q-feel chain: pitot to stick

  1. Sensing: pitot minus static gives impact pressure, read by a capsule or the air data computer.
  2. Feel computer: scales q into a gradient, e.g. 3 N per kilopascal.
  3. Bellows: q moves a spring cartridge anchor in the stick circuit, stiffening it as q rises.
  4. Spring gradient: stick travel x gives force F = k(q)·x.
  5. Bob weight: a mass on the linkage adds force with load factor n — feel per g that no speed sensor gives.
  6. Trim release: unloads the feel unit so the pilot can shift the neutral point; on release the present position is zero force.

The unit is called artificial or synthetic feel. It only loads the stick, never the surface.

Servo tabs and the tab family

A tab is a small aerofoil hinged at the trailing edge of a main surface. Its chord is short, so its own hinge moment is small, but its lift acts on a long arm behind the surface hinge. A tab can therefore move something much bigger than itself.

In a servo tab system the pilot's linkage drives only the tab; the main surface is free to float on its hinge. The pilot moves the tab, say trailing edge up; the airflow pushes the tab down, and that force on a long arm behind the surface hinge swings the main surface trailing edge down, the opposite way to the tab. A small stick force moves a large surface, so a large manual aircraft can be flown by tab. The servo tab is a driving device.

TabMovesJob
Servoopposite to surfacedrives the surface
Geared, balanceopposite to the surfacecuts hinge moment (assists the pilot)

The anti-servo tab is the mirror image: geared to move with the surface, it increases hinge moment and resists the pilot. That resistance is feel, and it damps the surface against gusts. An all-moving tailplane (stabilator) pivots near its own aerodynamic centre, so its hinge moment is small and the stick would feel too light; light aircraft with a stabilator, such as the Piper PA-28 Cherokee, carry an anti-servo tab for that reason. Memory line: a servo tab saves muscle, an anti-servo tab gives back information.

Worked example

Question. A Q-feel system gives 3 N of stick force per kilopascal of dynamic pressure above the trim value. The aircraft is trimmed (zero stick force) at 75 m/s. Find the stick force needed to hold the same stick displacement at 150 m/s and at 250 m/s, in air of density 1.225 kg/m³.

  1. At 75 m/s: q₁ = ½ × 1.225 × 75² = 3445 Pa = 3.445 kPa. This is the trim point, where the stick force is zero.
  2. At 150 m/s: q₂ = ½ × 1.225 × 150² = 13 781 Pa = 13.781 kPa.
  3. Rise above trim: q₂ − q₁ = 13.781 − 3.445 = 10.336 kPa, so F = 3 × 10.336 = 31.0 N.
  4. At 250 m/s: q₃ = ½ × 1.225 × 250² = 38 281 Pa = 38.281 kPa; rise 38.281 − 3.445 = 34.836 kPa, so F = 3 × 34.836 = 104.5 N.
  5. Check: q rose 4 times from 75 to 150 m/s because q carries V²; the force does not rise 4 times because it is counted from the trim value.

Answer. The pilot needs 31.0 N at 150 m/s and 104.5 N at 250 m/s, both measured from zero force at the 75 m/s trim.

Exam traps

  • Swapping servo and anti-servo: the servo tab moves opposite to the surface and drives it; the anti-servo tab moves with the surface and adds feel.
  • Matching airspeed to density wrongly: use local density with true airspeed, or sea-level density with equivalent airspeed.
  • Leaving q in pascals when the gradient is per kilopascal — divide by 1000 first.
  • Thinking Q-feel changes the aircraft's response. It changes only pilot force; the surface still deflects as commanded.
  • Confusing the q-bellows (feel per unit speed) with the bob weight (feel per g).

1.5 Digital fly-by-wire and fly-by-light

Fly-by-wire (FBW) replaces the mechanical link between cockpit and surface with wires, sensors and computers. The pilot moves a sidestick or column; gyros, accelerometers and air data measure the aircraft; redundant flight control computers use control laws to command hydraulic or electro-hydrostatic actuators. Fly-by-light (FBL) keeps the same chain but carries the signals over optical fibre. FBW is used on modern airliners and fighters because it saves weight, removes rigging and friction, and allows envelope protection and relaxed static stability.

You need: the chapter on powered flight controls for powered actuation and the chapter on Q-feel and servo tabs for artificial feel.

The signal chain

In a mechanical system the pilot's hand supplies the command and the rod or cable carries it. In FBW the command becomes an electrical signal. A sidestick or column has position sensors; rate gyros measure body angular rates p, q and r (roll, pitch and yaw rates in degrees per second); accelerometers measure normal acceleration nz in g; air data gives airspeed V, dynamic pressure q̄ in Pa (written with a bar so it is not confused with the pitch rate q) and angle of attack α. These signals enter the flight control computer (FCC). The pilot never pushes the surface directly; the FCC decides how much surface deflection gives the commanded motion.

  1. Pilot input: sidestick position gives a demand, for example a pitch rate in degrees per second.
  2. Sensors: gyros, accelerometers and air data measure the actual motion and air state.
  3. FCC: three or four independent lanes compute the same command; a voter compares lanes and rejects a disagreeing lane.
  4. Actuator: the FCC sends a command to a servo actuator, usually powered by hydraulic pressure; an electro-hydrostatic actuator has its own electric pump.
  5. Surface: the actuator moves the aileron, elevator or rudder; the resulting motion is fed back by the sensors.

Control laws and modes

A control law is the formula or table that turns pilot and sensor inputs into actuator commands. A rate command law commands pitch rate, roll rate or yaw rate; the FCC compares commanded rate with measured rate and drives the surface until the error is small. For pitch, the C* (“C-star”) law blends pitch rate and normal acceleration: at low speed the pitch-rate part dominates, so it feels like a rate command; at high speed the normal-acceleration part dominates, so it feels like a load-factor (g) command. This gives consistent handling across the flight envelope.

  • Normal mode (normal law on Airbus types): all protections active: stall, overspeed, bank and load-factor limits.
  • Alternate mode: some protections lost; control laws simplified.
  • Direct mode: pilot command goes to the actuator with minimal feedback; no envelope protection.

FBW saves weight by deleting cables, pulleys, turnbuckles and rigging. It removes friction and backlash. The FCC can add envelope protection, carefree handling, gust alleviation and relaxed static stability, which allows a smaller tail. The cost is dependence on electric power, computers and validated software; lightning and electromagnetic interference (EMI) need protection.

Digital lanes and fly-by-light

Early FBW was analog. Digital FBW samples signals and runs software, so it can self-check, schedule gains by software, run built-in test (BIT), and grow by software change. Redundancy is by lanes, not by making one lane perfect. With three lanes, two good lanes outvote one bad lane. After that first failure only two remain, and two lanes that disagree cannot tell which one is wrong, so the system can only disconnect or drop to a backup. With four lanes a second failure can still be outvoted. The worked example puts numbers on this.

P(all k lanes fail) = pk

p is the failure probability of one lane in one flight hour; k is the number of independent lanes.

P(m or more of n fail) = Σk=m…n C(n, k) pk (1 − p)n−k ≈ C(n, m) pm

C(n, k) is the number of ways to choose k lanes out of n; the approximation holds when p is small. Majority voting in a triplex is lost when m = 2 of n = 3 lanes fail.

Fly-by-light replaces the copper signal wires with optical fibre. It is immune to EMI, has no ground loops, cannot cause a spark at a fuel tank, is light and has high bandwidth. Problems are mechanical: fibre has a minimum bending radius, every connector loses optical power, and the optical power budget must close. FBL still uses hydraulic or electric power at the actuator.

Worked example

Question. A digital FBW computer has independent lanes, each failing with probability p = 1 × 10⁻⁴ in a flight hour. (a) For three lanes, find the probability that all three fail in one hour. (b) Majority voting needs two good lanes out of three. Find the probability of losing the majority (two or more lanes failed). (c) For four lanes, find the probability that three or more fail. Compare each with a target of 1 × 10⁻⁹ per hour.

Python program.

from math import comb

p = 1e-4   # failure probability of one lane in one flight hour

def at_least(m, n):
    """probability that m or more of n independent lanes fail"""
    return sum(comb(n, k) * p**k * (1 - p)**(n - k) for k in range(m, n + 1))

print(f"triplex, all 3 fail:      {p**3:.2e}")
print(f"triplex, 2 or more fail:  {at_least(2, 3):.2e}")
print(f"quadruplex, 3 or more:    {at_least(3, 4):.2e}")
  1. Independence means the probabilities multiply. Let p = 1 × 10⁻⁴.
  2. (a) P(all 3) = p³ = (1 × 10⁻⁴)³ = 1 × 10⁻¹² per hour.
  3. (b) Two or more of three fail: P = C(3,2) p² (1 − p) + p³ = 3 × 10⁻⁸ × 0.9999 + 10⁻¹² = 3.00 × 10⁻⁸ per hour.
  4. (c) Three or more of four fail: P = C(4,3) p³ (1 − p) + p⁴ = 4 × 10⁻¹² × 0.9999 + 10⁻¹⁶ = 4.00 × 10⁻¹² per hour.
  5. Compare with 1 × 10⁻⁹: the triplex all-fail value (10⁻¹²) passes, but the triplex loses its majority 30 times more often than the target. The quadruplex keeps a voting majority to 4 × 10⁻¹², which passes. This is why many FBW computers are quadruplex, or triplex with a dissimilar backup.

Output

triplex, 2 or more fail:  3.00e-08
quadruplex, 3 or more:    4.00e-12

Answer. (a) 1.00 × 10⁻¹² per hour; (b) 3.00 × 10⁻⁸ per hour, which misses a 10⁻⁹ target; (c) 4.00 × 10⁻¹² per hour, which meets it.

1.6 Autopilot and automatic flight control

An autopilot closes the control loop automatically. Sensors measure the aircraft's actual attitude, rate or path. A computer compares that with the commanded value and sends a correction. Servos move the surfaces or the throttles. The pilot selects a mode and a target; the machine holds it. This frees attention for navigation and monitoring, and it is the basis of every automatic flight control system (AFCS), from a light aircraft wing leveller to an airliner autoland.

You need: the powered actuators of the chapter on powered flight controls and the computer-driven control laws of the chapter on digital fly-by-wire, because the autopilot commands those actuators and uses those computers.

The parts of the system

An automatic flight control system (AFCS) is a closed loop. The pilot or a navigation receiver gives a command. Sensors measure the actual aircraft state. A computer subtracts actual from commanded to get an error, shapes a correction, and sends it to a servo. The servo moves a control surface or a thrust lever. The aircraft responds, the sensors see the new state, and the loop repeats.

The sensors are gyros for attitude and rate, an inertial measurement unit (IMU) for acceleration and rate, air data for airspeed and altitude, and a radio altimeter for height above the ground during approach. The computer is usually a flight control computer with redundancy. The servo is an electro-hydraulic or electro-mechanical actuator. The mode selector tells the computer what to hold; the disconnect button removes servo power and returns control to the pilot.

The inner loop is fast and holds attitude or rate: pitch rate, roll rate and yaw damping. The outer loop is slower and holds a path: altitude, heading, speed or a radio navigation course. The outer loop sends its target to the inner loop, as a heading select mode asks the roll channel for a bank angle.

δa = Kp e − Kr p (roll); δe = Kp e − Kr q (pitch)

For the roll channel: δa is the aileron command in degrees; p is the roll rate in degrees per second, positive right. For the pitch channel: δe is the elevator command in degrees, positive nose-up; q is the pitch rate in degrees per second, positive nose-up. e is the error between commanded and actual attitude in degrees. Kp is the proportional gain in degrees per degree. Kr is the rate gain in degrees per degree per second; it has units of seconds. The minus sign damps the motion.

Modes and the two loops

Pitch hold and roll hold keep the current attitude. Altitude hold uses the outer loop to command small pitch changes. Heading select commands a bank angle until the heading error is zero. VOR/LOC and glide-slope coupling let the autopilot follow a radio beam: localizer for lateral, glide slope for vertical. Approaches are classed Category I, II or III by decision height and runway visual range; autoland, with redundant sensors, computers and servos, is used mainly for Category II and III.

Some autopilots add low-speed protection: when speed falls toward the stall they warn, change mode or disconnect. Disengagement logic removes the autopilot when the pilot presses the disconnect button, when a sensor fails, or when the servo is forced against a limit. Most systems sound an aural warning at disconnect.

Rate feedback exists because proportional control alone overshoots. If the aircraft is rolling toward the target, the proportional term still commands a large correction. The rate term subtracts a command proportional to how fast the aircraft is already moving. The result is a smaller command near the target and a smooth stop.

Many Cessna 172R aircraft carry a single-axis autopilot that works as a wing leveller: one servo on the ailerons and a turn coordinator as its rate sensor. The turn coordinator is primarily a rate-of-turn instrument; its canted gyro also responds to roll rate, and the wing leveller uses that roll-rate signal. It does not measure bank angle. If a wing drops, the servo moves the ailerons to level the wings. It cannot hold altitude or fly a heading by itself; the pilot still controls pitch and power.

Active control technology goes further, using the same surfaces for load alleviation and stability augmentation; see the chapter on active control technology.

Auto-throttle

The auto-throttle system (ATS) moves the thrust levers. It does not move the elevator. In speed hold it compares actual speed with the selected speed and moves the levers to reduce the error. In Mach hold it does the same with Mach number. Take-off/go-around (TOGA) moves the levers to the take-off or go-around thrust limit.

Auto-throttle helps in gusty air because the thrust changes quickly while the pilot keeps a steady attitude. On approach it holds speed while the autopilot follows the glide slope, so the pilot monitors rather than chases the throttles. If speed is low, the auto-throttle adds thrust while the autopilot keeps holding the path with pitch.

  • Lower workload: the pilot monitors speed instead of adjusting thrust levers through every gust.
  • Precise speed on approach, which keeps the landing distance predictable.
  • Fuel saving in cruise, by holding the planned speed or Mach number.
  • Engine protection: the commanded thrust stays inside the limits the engine control enforces.
  • Quick, repeatable go-around thrust through the TOGA mode.

Worked example

Question. A roll channel uses 3° of aileron per degree of roll error and 0.8° of aileron per degree per second of roll rate, with the rate term subtracted. 1.7 Active control technology

Active control technology (ACT) makes the flight control computers do more than follow the stick: they shape airframe loads and supply stability. Relaxing static stability lets the centre of gravity move aft, so the tail pushes down less and trim drag falls. Load alleviation cuts gust and manoeuvre peaks, so the wing can be lighter. Some syllabi call such an aircraft a control configured vehicle; some leave the topic out. Load alleviation flies on several modern airliners, and relaxed stability is standard on modern fighters.

You need: the chapter on digital fly-by-wire for the fly-by-wire chain and the chapter on autopilots for closed-loop thinking.

Relaxed static stability

A conventional aircraft is stable in pitch because its centre of gravity (CG) lies ahead of the neutral point, the point where a change in angle of attack gives no pitching moment. The gap is the static margin. A positive margin means the tail must push down in steady flight.

SM = (xnp − xcg) ÷ c̄

SM is the static margin (dimensionless); xnp is the neutral point position, xcg the centre of gravity position, both measured along the chord from the same datum in metres; c̄ is the mean aerodynamic chord in metres. SM > 0 means statically stable.

That download is lift the wing makes and the tail throws away, and it grows with the margin. ACT lets the designer shrink the margin and push the CG aft, because the computer now supplies pitch stability artificially. Download falls, trim drag falls, fuel burn improves, and the tail can be smaller.

The price: the aircraft cannot be flown without the computer, since the bare airframe diverges slowly in pitch. Losing the computer is a design case that must be shown safe. This is relaxed static stability (RSS). In a transport the margin is cut to near zero to save trim drag. In a fighter such as the F-16 the margin is made negative on purpose, so the aircraft pitches into a turn more readily, and the computer keeps it controllable.

Load alleviation and ride quality

The same loop does load alleviation. Gust load alleviation (GLA) senses vertical acceleration with accelerometers near the wing tips and in the fuselage. A band-pass filter keeps only the band of frequencies at which gusts shake the aircraft and bend the wing. It rejects the steady 1 g part, so a steady climb passes through untouched, and it rejects high-frequency sensor noise. The computer then deflects ailerons and spoilers to unload the wing as the gust arrives, cutting the peak bending moment at the wing root.

Manoeuvre load alleviation (MLA) does the same for the pilot's pull: when pitch rate shows a manoeuvre starting, the surfaces shift lift inboard and the root bending peak falls.

Flutter suppression uses the sensing too. Flutter is an unstable oscillation from bending and torsion coupling. The surface is driven so its aerodynamic force is in phase with the flutter mode's velocity (90° out of phase with its displacement), which adds damping. Ride smoothing cuts the response in turbulence. Envelope limiting caps the pilot's demand so angle of attack α, load factor n and dynamic pressure q stay inside the envelope — carefree handling.

Hardware and the certification argument

The chain is short: accelerometers and rate gyros (see aircraft transducers) feed the computers, which shape the command and pass it to the same actuators that fly the surface. The band-pass filter is what separates ACT from the basic control law. Actuator rate limits matter: a surface that cannot move fast enough lags the command, adds phase, and can drive a mode unstable instead of damping it.

Certification rests on two demonstrations. The function must be shown not to make any load worse anywhere in the envelope, because a wrong deflection at the wrong moment is itself a load case. Its failure must be shown safe, with the airframe surviving the function switched off.

The prize is structural mass: where gust load sizes the wing root and the wing is certified to the reduced peak load, the bending material there shrinks, and that saving is carried for the life of the aircraft.

Worked example

Question. A gust gives a peak limit load factor of 2.5 g. With gust load alleviation the peak falls to 2.0 g. 1.8 Engine control systems

The engine is a control loop of its own. The thrust lever states a demand; the engine control system sets fuel flow and engine geometry to meet it while keeping spool speeds, exhaust gas temperature and surge margin inside limits. Older engines use hydro-mechanical fuel control units; modern ones use a Full Authority Digital Engine Control (FADEC). The same loop accepts thrust demand from the auto-throttle and returns engine data to the cockpit. Some syllabi teach it with flight controls, because it is a clear example of a fast inner loop nested under the auto-throttle.

You need: the chapter on autopilots for auto-throttle, and the closed-loop idea from the chapter on powered flight controls and the chapter on digital fly-by-wire.

Hydro-mechanical engine control

An engine control loop has one thrust-setting variable and several limits. On most turbofans the thrust parameter is N1, the low-pressure or fan spool speed as a percentage of a reference. Some engines use N2, the high-pressure spool speed, or the engine pressure ratio (EPR) as the thrust-setting parameter; the rest of the loop is the same. EGT is the exhaust gas temperature; TGT (turbine gas temperature) is the same kind of measurement taken closer to the turbine, so its limiting value differs from the EGT limit. P1 and T1 are inlet pressure and temperature, because air density changes how much fuel a lever setting should receive. Fuel flow is in kg/s, and oil pressure is a health input.

An engine control acts on three things: fuel flow (the fuel metering valve), air flow (variable stator vanes and compressor bleed valves, which keep the compressor away from surge) and, on some engines, the exhaust (nozzle area on afterburning military engines, and the thrust reverser). A hydro-mechanical fuel control unit (FCU) does the fuel part with cams, bellows, levers and a flyweight speed governor: the lever sets the governed speed, and the governor meters fuel to hold it. During a rapid lever movement an acceleration schedule caps the fuel flow, because too much fuel at once would push the compressor into surge or over-heat the turbine; a deceleration schedule keeps a minimum flow so the flame does not go out. Limiters cut fuel before EGT or spool speed passes its limit. The FCU is reliable but hard to retune, and it has no software and no built-in test. Ignition and the thrust reverser are separate systems on such an engine.

FADEC and ratings

A FADEC (Full Authority Digital Engine Control) is an electronic engine control with two channels, one active and one standby. It has its own power supply, built-in test, and no full-authority mechanical backup. It reads throttle lever angle, N1, N2, EGT, fuel flow, P1, T1 and oil pressure, then drives the fuel metering valve, stator vanes, bleed valves and ignition. It also runs auto-start and sends engine data to the cockpit and the auto-throttle.

The lever asks for a rating, not a fuel flow. Typical ratings are take-off, maximum continuous, climb and cruise. For each rating the FADEC computes a target N1 from the inlet conditions P1 and T1 and holds it by trimming fuel. The target is set as a corrected speed, because the fan and compressor behave the same at the same N1 ÷ √θ.

N1c = N1 ÷ √θ, θ = T1 ÷ 288.15 K

N1c is the corrected fan speed in % (what the rating fixes); N1 is the physical fan speed in %; θ is the inlet temperature ratio; T1 is the inlet total temperature in kelvin; 288.15 K is the sea-level standard temperature.

On a hot day the same corrected speed needs a higher physical N1 and runs a higher EGT. When a physical limit is reached, FADEC holds the limit and the thrust falls, so the same lever position gives different thrust on a hot afternoon and a cold morning. In automatic flight the flight control system sends thrust demand to the auto-throttle, which moves the lever; FADEC still decides the fuel.

Worked example

Question. A FADEC sets the take-off rating as a corrected fan speed N1c = 97.0 %. The physical N1 limit is 100.0 %. Find the physical N1 the FADEC commands at an inlet temperature of (a) 15 °C, (b) −10 °C and (c) 40 °C. (d) At 40 °C, what corrected speed can the engine actually reach?

Takeaways

  1. An aircraft does not hold an attitude or path by itself, so a control chain must carry the cockpit demand to a surface: command, linkage or signal, surface, airload, motion.
  2. Gearing links force and moment: with no friction, stick force F = H ÷ G, so the pilot’s 60 N through 0.2750 m/rad holds only 16.5 N·m.
  3. Rods carry push and pull but are sized by buckling; cables carry pull only, so they run in matched pairs with turnbuckles and tension regulators.
  4. Hinge moment H = q S c Ch grows with V² and with the cube of size, so above a certain size and speed hydraulics supplies the force and the pilot only the command.
  5. An irreversible powered control must be given artificial feel: Q-feel makes stick force rise with dynamic pressure, a bob weight adds feel per g, and tabs do it aerodynamically on manual surfaces.
  6. Fly-by-wire replaces the linkage with sensors, computers and control laws, giving weight saving and envelope protection; it pays with redundant lanes, power and validated software.
  7. Lane counting is a probability argument: a triplex loses its voting majority at about 3p², a quadruplex at about 4p³.
  8. An autopilot closes the same loop with sensors and servos: a fast inner loop holds attitude and rate, a slower outer loop holds heading, altitude or a radio path; the auto-throttle closes the speed loop on thrust.
  9. Active control technology uses the same computers to supply stability (relaxed static stability) and to cut gust and manoeuvre loads, allowing a smaller tail and a lighter wing.
  10. Engine control is its own fast loop: the FADEC holds a corrected fan speed for the selected rating and every limit, so the same lever gives less thrust on a hot day.

Evaluate Evaluate fly-by-light against fly-by-wire. State the physical reasons for its advantages and the practical reasons it is not universal. 13 marks

Points to cover. FBW signal path is electrical wire; FBL uses optical fibre; Advantages: immune to EMI and lightning-induced currents, no ground loops, no spark, light, high bandwidth; Problems: connectors and splices, bend radius, optical power budget, actuators still need power; Optical-to-electrical interfaces add parts; Where FBL suits; one production example.

Model answer.

  1. What changes. Fly-by-light (FBL) keeps the FBW architecture (sensors, computers, control laws, powered actuators) and replaces only the signal wires with optical fibre. The actuators still need hydraulic or electrical power.
  2. Why the advantages are physical. A fibre carries light in glass, not current in a conductor. So a changing magnetic field (EMI, high-intensity radiated fields, lightning) induces no current in it; there are no ground loops; a damaged fibre cannot spark near fuel; and light pulses carry far higher data rates than a copper pair.
  3. Weight. A FBW aircraft needs heavy shielding and lightning protection on its signal wiring; fibre needs none for its signal, so the harness is lighter.
  4. Why it is not universal: the optical power budget. Example with assumed values: transmitter −10 dBm, receiver sensitivity −30 dBm (20 dB available); 6 connectors at 0.75 dB; 200 m of fibre at 3 dB/km; 3 dB margin for ageing.
    connectors  6 × 0.75   = 4.5 dB
fibre       0.2 × 3    = 0.6 dB
margin                 = 3.0 dB
total                  = 8.1 dB  ≤ 20 dB available: link closes
  5. Reading the budget. Most loss is at connectors, not in the fibre, so every extra break in the run for maintenance costs budget; a dirty or damaged connector can lose several dB and close the link.
  6. Other practical problems. Fibre has a minimum bend radius, which limits routing through tight structure; repair needs special splicing tools and training; every computer and actuator needs an optical-to-electrical interface, which adds parts and failure points; and decades of certified copper-wire FBW give designers confidence that FBL has yet to build.
  7. Comparison.
    Fly-by-wireFly-by-light
    Signal mediumcopper wireoptical fibre
    EMI and lightningshielding and protection neededimmune in the signal path
    Bandwidthmoderatevery high
    Routing and repairflexible, standard toolsbend radius, special splicing
    Experience baselargesmall
  8. Where FBL suits. Aircraft that face strong EMI or carry fibre near fuel: it flies in production on the Kawasaki P-1 maritime patrol aircraft and on research aircraft.
  9. Evaluation. FBL is better physics for the signal path; FBW wins today on cost, repair and certification experience. As composite airframes (which give less natural screening than aluminium) and higher data rates spread, the balance moves toward fibre.
  10. Closing check. 4.5 + 0.6 + 3.0 = 8.1 dB of loss against 20 dB available leaves 11.9 dB spare; six more connectors (another 4.5 dB) would still close, which shows connectors, not length, are the limit.
Analyze An aircraft on approach shows a slow pitch oscillation after the autopilot was engaged. Analyse the possible causes in the control loop and the checks you would make. 12 marks

Points to cover. Identify loop elements: sensor, comparator, control law gain and damping, servo, surface, aircraft dynamics; Sensor fault or lag; lost rate signal; Gain too high for the present q̄; gain schedule fault; glide-slope gain not reduced near the runway; Servo rate limit or backlash; actuator hysteresis; Aircraft changes: speed, configuration, CG; Tests: disengage, fly manually, check signals, gain schedule and servo rigging.

Model answer.

  1. The loop to analyse. Pitch channel δe = Kp e − Kr q (e the pitch-attitude error, q the pitch rate), inside an outer loop that holds the glide slope. An oscillation means the loop has too little damping: too much gain, too little rate feedback, or extra lag somewhere.
  2. Possible causes, element by element.
    ElementPossible faultWhy it gives a slow pitch oscillation
    Pitch-rate gyrofailed to zero or wrong signthe Kr q damping term disappears or reverses
    Attitude or air-data sensorlag or noisethe error arrives late, so corrections arrive late
    Gain schedulegains not reduced as q̄ risesthe surface is more effective at higher q̄, so the loop gain grows
    Glide-slope couplinggain not reduced as the aircraft nears the runwaythe beam narrows, so the same angle error means a smaller height error and the loop over-reacts
    Servo and linkagerate limit, backlash or hysteresisa dead zone or lag gives a steady limit cycle
    Aircraftflap change, speed change, aft CGthe airframe’s own pitch damping and stability change
  3. Effect of gain, in numbers. If gains set for q̄ = 6125 Pa (100 m/s) are used at 150 m/s, q̄ is (150 ÷ 100)² = 2.25 times larger, so the elevator’s effect, and the loop gain, are 2.25 times what the designer intended: enough to turn a well-damped loop into an oscillating one.
  4. Effect of losing rate feedback. In the roll example of the autopilot chapter the rate term was 1.6° out of 12°; with it gone the command near the target stays large, the aircraft overshoots, and the proportional term reverses it, giving a sustained oscillation. The pitch channel behaves the same way.
  5. Checks, in order. (1) Disengage the autopilot and fly manually: if the oscillation stops, the fault is in the autopilot loop, not the airframe. (2) Compare the pitch-rate and attitude signals with a second source (standby instruments, the other channel). (3) Check the gain schedule against airspeed and radio altitude in the maintenance data. (4) Check the elevator servo for rate limiting, backlash and correct rigging and clutch setting. (5) Check the weight and balance for an aft CG and the configuration (flap setting) when the oscillation began.
  6. Distinguishing the causes. An oscillation that grows as the aircraft nears the runway points to the glide-slope gain; one that appears only at higher speed points to the gain schedule; a small steady oscillation of fixed size points to servo backlash; one from the moment of engagement points to a sensor or sign fault.
  7. Closing check. (150 ÷ 100)² = 2.25 confirms how strongly an unscheduled gain grows with speed, and every cause in the table acts through the same law δe = Kp e − Kr q, so the checks follow the loop element by element.

Glossary

Actuator
The hydraulic or electric ram that converts a command signal into a force and stroke on a control surface.
Aileron
A wing trailing-edge surface deflected differentially to produce roll; one goes up while the other goes down.
Auto throttle
A servo system that drives the throttles automatically to hold a selected speed, thrust or Mach number.
Autopilot
A system that holds or tracks an attitude, heading, altitude or path by closing the control loop through sensors and servos.
Backlash
Lost motion between linked parts, so a small control input produces no output; it adds free play and can cause flutter.
Bell crank
A pivoted two-armed lever that changes the direction of a rod or cable run, and its motion ratio when the arms differ.
Bob weight
A mass on the elevator linkage that adds stick force in proportion to load factor, so the pilot feels each g.
Control horn
The lever arm on a control surface to which the push-pull rod or cable is attached, setting the mechanical advantage.
Control surface
A movable aerofoil whose deflection changes the force or moment on the aircraft, such as an aileron, elevator, rudder or flap.
Corrected speed
A spool speed divided by √θ, the square root of the inlet temperature ratio, so engine behaviour can be compared across temperatures.
Direct law
A degraded FBW mode where pilot inputs pass to the surfaces with simple gearing and no envelope protection.
Dynamic pressure
q = ½ρV², in pascals when ρ is kg/m³ and V is m/s; it sets the aerodynamic force on a surface and hence the feel force.
Electro-hydraulic servo valve
A valve that meters hydraulic flow in proportion to a small electrical current, giving fast, precise actuator control.
Elevator
The pitch control surface hinged to the horizontal tailplane, moving the tail lift thus the pitching moment.
Envelope protection
Control-law limiting that prevents the aircraft exceeding angle of attack, load factor, pitch or bank limits whatever the pilot asks.
FADEC
Full Authority Digital Engine Control: a redundant digital computer that schedules engine fuel and geometry within all limits.
Fly-by-wire
A control system in which pilot input is converted to electrical signals and computers command the actuators.
Gearing
The ratio of stick travel to surface rotation, in mm per degree or m per radian; it sets both the response and the stick force.
Load alleviation
Deflecting surfaces automatically to reduce wing or tail bending loads during gusts and manoeuvres.
LRU
Line Replaceable Unit: a box that can be swapped on the flight line without recalibration or deep maintenance.
Mechanical advantage
The ratio of output force to input force of a linkage, the inverse of the ratio of their displacements.
Relaxed static stability
A design where the aircraft is deliberately made slightly unstable and stabilised continuously by the flight control system.
Rudder
The yaw control surface on the vertical fin, producing side force and yaw, also used to coordinate turns.
Servo
A device that compares a command with a measured position and drives an output until the error is removed.
Stabilator
A one-piece horizontal tail that pivots as a whole, used instead of a fixed horizontal stabilizer with a separate elevator.
Static margin
The distance from the centre of gravity aft to the neutral point, as a fraction of the mean aerodynamic chord; positive means statically stable.
Throttle lever angle
The position of the cockpit thrust lever, the command input to the engine control loop, usually given in degrees.
Trim
A small steady deflection or force that holds the aircraft in the desired state without continuous pilot input.
Trim tab
A small hinged tab on a primary surface, set by the pilot so its hinge moment holds the surface deflected with no stick force.
