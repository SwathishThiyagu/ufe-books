1.1 Need for control systems; conventional flight controls

An aircraft left alone does not hold an altitude, a heading or a bank angle. It drifts with every gust and every change of thrust. A flight control system is the chain that lets a pilot, or later a computer, demand a motion and get it: a cockpit control moves, linkage carries the movement to a surface, the surface deflects into the airflow, airloads appear, and the aircraft rotates about one of its axes. The same chain runs from a Cessna 172R to an airliner, though the force source changes. See 1.3 for that step.

Six freedoms, three axes, three primary surfaces

An aircraft has six degrees of freedom: three translations (fore-aft, lateral, vertical) and three rotations. A degree of freedom is one independent way the body can move. A flight control system commands the rotations directly; translation follows from attitude and thrust.

  1. Roll — rotation about the longitudinal axis, nose to tail. The ailerons move in opposite senses: the up-going aileron cuts lift on its wing and the down-going one raises it, so the aircraft banks.
  2. Pitch — rotation about the lateral axis, wingtip to wingtip. The elevator sits on the horizontal tail; trailing edge up pushes the tail down and the nose up.
  3. Yaw — rotation about the vertical axis. The rudder sits on the vertical tail; trailing edge left pushes the tail right and the nose left.

The ailerons also cause adverse yaw: the down-going aileron makes more drag and pulls the nose away from the turn. The rudder corrects it. Some syllabi call the aileron, elevator and rudder the primary controls.

A pilot commands a deflection, not a force. At speed a few degrees of surface produce a large moment; the linkage gearing sets how many millimetres of stick travel give one degree of surface. That ratio is the first design choice.

Secondary controls work on the wing or on the surface, not on the axis directly

  • Flaps: trailing-edge surfaces on the wing. Extending them raises lift and drag, so the aircraft can fly slower for take-off and landing.
  • Slats: leading-edge surfaces that extend to delay stall at a high angle of attack.
  • Spoilers: upper-surface wing panels that lift up to spoil lift and add drag. They are used for descent, as a speedbrake, and on some aircraft to help roll.
  • Trim tabs: small hinged tabs on a primary surface. Moving the tab changes the hinge moment, so the surface stays deflected with little or no stick force. The tab moves the surface; the surface moves the aircraft.
  • Throttle: not a surface, but part of the control chain because it sets thrust.

Some syllabi group these as auxiliary controls. Notice the difference: a primary control changes an axis of motion, while a secondary control changes the wing's lift or the surface's hinge moment.

The conventional chain, part by part

The Cessna 172R is a full mechanical single: a yoke on a column, two rudder pedals, a throttle lever and a manual trim wheel. No hydraulic boost sits between pilot and surface. Follow one demand: pull the yoke back, the column rotates, a rod moves forward, a bell crank changes the direction, cables run aft through pulleys, a turnbuckle sets tension, and a horn on the elevator converts cable pull into surface rotation about the hinge.

Worked example

Question. The elevator of a light aircraft moves 25° when the control column travels 120 mm from neutral. (a) Find the gearing in mm of column travel per degree of elevator. (b) The pilot pulls with 60 N and the linkage gives a mechanical advantage of 4 from grip to elevator push rod; find the push-rod force. (c) The elevator horn arm is 80 mm; find the hinge moment the rod produces.

  1. Gearing = column travel ÷ elevator angle = 120 mm ÷ 25° = 4.8 mm per degree.
  2. Gearing and mechanical advantage are linked: gearing = mechanical advantage × horn arm (with consistent units). The numbers here are chosen for practice and need not match exactly.
  3. Push-rod force = pilot force × mechanical advantage = 60 N × 4 = 240 N.
  4. Assume the push rod acts at right angles to the horn arm; at an angle θ the moment is F × arm × sin θ. Convert the horn arm to metres: 80 mm = 0.08 m.
  5. Hinge moment = push-rod force × horn arm = 240 N × 0.08 m = 19.2 N·m.
  6. In a steady hold the rod moment equals the aerodynamic hinge moment; to move the surface the rod moment must be larger by the amount needed to accelerate the surface and its linkage.

Exam traps

  • Putting roll on the rudder. The rudder yaws the aircraft; the ailerons roll it, and they also cause adverse yaw.
  • Saying a trim tab moves the aircraft. The tab moves the surface, and the surface moves the aircraft.
  • Mixing the axes: roll is about the longitudinal axis, pitch about the lateral axis, yaw about the vertical axis.
  • Using millimetres in a hinge-moment sum. Convert the horn arm to metres, so the answer is in N·m, not N·mm.
  • Thinking ailerons turn the aircraft directly. They bank it; the horizontal component of lift does the turning.

1.2 Push-pull rod and cable-pulley systems

A pilot's command must travel from the stick or pedal to a surface far away. Two mechanical ways do this. A push-pull rod is a tube that carries the command in both directions, push and pull, and needs bell cranks and guides to route it. A cable system carries only pull, so it always runs as a matched pair: one cable pulls the surface one way while the other slackens. Rods suit short, straight runs; cables suit long, curved runs. Both are still found on light aircraft and as the mechanical backbone of power-assisted airliners.

You need Chapter 1.1 for the cockpit controls and the surfaces they move.

Push-pull rod: a tube that takes both directions

A push-pull rod is a thin metal tube with an end fitting at each end. The fitting ends in a ball-jointed rod end: a spherical bearing inside a housing. The ball lets the rod tilt a few degrees without bending the tube, and it removes side loads. A rod carries tension when pulled and compression when pushed. Tension is easy; compression can make a long thin tube buckle sideways. The limit is set by Euler buckling, not by material strength.

P_cr = π²EI/(KL)²

P_cr is the critical buckling load in N; E is Young's modulus in Pa; I is the second moment of area of the tube section in m⁴; L is the unsupported length in m; K is the end-condition factor, about 1 for pin-ended rods.

σ = F/A

σ is axial stress in Pa; F is axial force in N; A is cross-section area in m².

A bell crank is a two-armed lever on a pivot. It redirects the rod and can change the ratio of motion by unequal arm lengths. An idler lever only supports and redirects; it does not change the ratio. Guides and idler levers break a long run into short unsupported lengths, which raises the buckling load. Every joint adds backlash, a small lost motion. Temperature changes the rod length, so rigging must leave adjustment.

Cable and pulley: pull only, so always in pairs

Aircraft control cable is stranded steel: many thin wires twisted into strands, strands into a cable. It is flexible and strong in tension. It cannot push. So the surface is driven by a closed loop of two cables. When the pilot moves the control, one cable pulls the horn and the other cable goes slack. The slack cable is kept under pre-tension so it follows without lost motion when the direction reverses.

Pulleys redirect the cable. Fairleads are small guides that keep the cable in line through structure. At a pressurised bulkhead, a pressure seal lets the cable pass without leaking cabin air. A turnbuckle is a threaded link that shortens or lengthens the cable to set tension. A tension regulator is a spring-loaded device that keeps tension nearly constant as the aircraft expands with temperature and the cable stretches. Rigging pins lock the surfaces and controls at neutral while tensions are set.

A flexible push-pull cable is different: a stranded inner core slides inside a flexible conduit. It can follow a curved route and carries push and pull, but with more friction than a rod. It is common for throttle, mixture and other secondary controls.

ΔL = FL/(AE)

ΔL is cable stretch in m; F is tension in N; L is cable length in m; A is the metal cross-section area in m²; E is Young's modulus in Pa.

Backlash, friction and the comparison

Backlash, also called lost motion, is the small movement at a joint before the load is carried. Rods have backlash at each ball joint and bell crank bearing. Cables have stretch and pulley clearance. Backlash makes the control feel loose and can cause flutter if it grows.

Friction rises with every pulley and fairlead, so a long cable run can feel heavy. Rods have less friction but need straight lines and strong supports. A rod can jam if the structure bends into its path; a cable can jump a pulley or wear through a fairlead. Maintenance checks tension, pulley freedom, cable wear and rod-end play.

Where the mechanical system stops

As aircraft grow faster and larger, the airload on a surface, and so the hinge moment, grows beyond a pilot's strength. Friction and stretch in a long cable run make the response imprecise. Rods add weight and need many bell cranks. The mechanical system still gives the command, but it needs help to supply the force. That help is the subject of Chapter 1.3.

Worked example

Question. A rudder cable is 3 m long, of cross-section 6 mm², steel with E = 200 GPa, and the turnbuckle pre-tensions it to 1.5 kN. (a) Find the stretch. (b) A push-pull rod of the same length with cross-section 50 mm² carries the same 1.5 kN in compression; find the stress.

  1. Convert: cable area A_c = 6 mm² = 6 × 10⁻⁶ m²; E = 200 GPa = 200 × 10⁹ Pa; F = 1.5 kN = 1500 N; L = 3 m.
  2. Use ΔL = FL/(A_c E).
  3. A_c E = 6 × 10⁻⁶ × 200 × 10⁹ = 1.2 × 10⁶ N.
  4. ΔL = 1500 × 3 / (1.2 × 10⁶) = 4500 / (1.2 × 10⁶) = 3.75 × 10⁻³ m = 3.75 mm.
  5. Rod area A_r = 50 mm² = 50 × 10⁻⁶ m².
  6. σ = F/A_r = 1500 / (50 × 10⁻⁶) = 30 × 10⁶ Pa = 30 MPa.
  7. 30 MPa is far below steel yield, so the rod does not fail by stress; it fails by buckling if the unsupported length is too large.

Answer. (a) Stretch = 3.75 mm. (b) Compressive stress = 30 MPa, so the rod's real limit is buckling, not stress.

Exam traps

  • Drawing one cable that both pushes and pulls the surface. A cable can only pull, so the surface needs a second cable pulling the other way.
  • Checking a push-pull rod in compression by stress alone. A long thin rod fails by buckling at a load far below its stress limit.
  • Forgetting pre-tension in the return cable. A slack cable gives lost motion and a delayed response when the control reverses.
  • Confusing a bell crank with an idler lever. A bell crank redirects and can change the motion ratio; an idler only supports and redirects.
  • Mixing mm² and m² in ΔL = FL/(AE). Using 6 instead of 6 × 10⁻⁶ gives a stretch a million times too large.

1.3 Power-assisted and fully powered flight controls

As an aircraft grows bigger and faster, the air load on a flap or elevator grows far faster than a pilot's arm. Hydraulics supplies the missing force. In a power-assisted system the mechanical link from Chapter 1.2 stays and a hydraulic boost acts in parallel with it. In a fully powered system the link is deleted and actuators alone move the surface. All large transports and most fighters are fully powered, while smaller types often stay power-assisted. This chapter shows why the force outgrows the pilot, how the boost loop closes, and what redundancy an irreversible system needs.

You need: Chapter 1.2 for the mechanical link that the boost sits in parallel with.

Why the pilot's arm runs out

The air load is spread over the whole control surface. Each small element of load sits at some distance behind the hinge, and the sum of load times that distance is the hinge moment M, which the pilot's linkage must carry.

M = q S c C_h, q = ½ ρ V²

M is the hinge moment in N·m; q is the dynamic pressure in Pa; ρ is air density in kg/m³; V is true airspeed in m/s; S is the surface area in m²; c is its chord (mean width) in m; and C_h is the dimensionless hinge moment coefficient, set by shape and deflection.

Two things make M grow fast. Speed enters as V², so doubling speed quadruples the moment. Size is worse: area S grows with the square of a length L and chord c grows with L, so M grows with L³. An aircraft twice as long needs about eight times the hinge moment at the same C_h and speed. A trainer at 60 m/s and an airliner at 250 m/s differ by (250/60)² ≈ 17 in q alone.

A pilot can hold roughly 150–200 N of steady stick force, and perhaps 350 N in a short sharp pull. With a stick arm near 0.3 m, that is only tens of newton-metres. Once M reaches a few hundred N·m, a pure rod-and-cable link cannot do the job. Hydraulics closes the gap.

Power-assisted (boost) systems

A power-assisted system — also called boosted, servo-assisted or partial power — keeps the mechanical link and adds a hydraulic actuator in parallel with it. The pilot's command still travels down the rods or cables; the actuator only shares the load.

  1. The pilot moves the column, and the input linkage shifts the spool of a servo valve.
  2. The open valve meters hydraulic fluid at system pressure into one side of the actuator.
  3. The piston drives the surface, and its force is summed with the pilot's own rod at a summing lever.
  4. A follow-up linkage from the surface back to the valve returns the spool to neutral when the surface reaches the demanded angle, which stops the motion.
  5. With the valve closed and the pilot's hand off the control, trapped fluid locks the actuator and the surface holds its position.

The follow-up linkage is position feedback. Without it the valve would stay open and the surface would run to its stop. The boost ratio is the total hinge moment divided by the share the pilot's linkage carries, so a ratio of 30 means the pilot supplies 1/30 of the moment and the hydraulics supply about 97%. Ratios of about 20 to 40 are common. Because part of the load still comes back up the linkage, the pilot keeps a natural feel of the air.

If the hydraulics fail, the pilot can still fly. This is manual reversion. A bypass valve, or an open-centre servo valve, lets fluid move freely between the two sides of the actuator, so the surface is no longer locked. The pilot's rods and cables then drive it directly, with much higher stick forces and a restricted speed. Several smaller transports, turboprops, trainers and business jets use boosted controls for exactly this reason.

Fully powered and power-actuated systems

A fully powered system — also called irreversible or all-powered — deletes the manual path. The pilot's input only positions a servo valve, or tells a flight control computer what is wanted. Actuators alone move the surface. All large transports and most fighters are built this way, while smaller types often stay power-assisted.

Irreversible means the surface cannot drive back. Fluid trapped by a closed servo valve locks the actuator, so an air load cannot push the piston and cannot move the pilot's control. That is good: a gust does not snatch the stick. It is also the catch: with the link gone, the pilot has no natural feel at all, and an artificial feel system becomes compulsory (see Chapter 1.4).

  • Redundancy: two or three actuators per surface, fed by separate hydraulic systems, so one system failure still leaves control.
  • Jamming: a seized actuator can hold a surface hard over. Separate actuators, jam detection and sometimes a breakable connection limit the damage.
  • Rate limiting: flow, not pressure, sets how fast the surface moves. At high q the surface may lag the command and the lag can feed a pilot-induced oscillation.
  • Dissimilarity: channels are deliberately not identical, for example hydraulic on one and electric on another, so one fault type cannot take all of them.

Power-actuated systems go further. An electro-hydrostatic actuator carries its own small pump, driven by an electric motor bolted at the surface, and draws only electrical power and a little fluid. This removes long runs of high-pressure pipe, which leak and are heavy, but the electrical generation must grow to match (see Unit 3, Aircraft Electrical Systems and Instruments).

Exam traps

  • Saying a fully powered aircraft needs no feel system. It is the exact opposite: with the link gone, artificial feel is the only thing giving the pilot any stick force.
  • Calling a boosted system irreversible. A power-assisted system keeps the mechanical link and can revert to manual, with heavy forces.
  • Using V instead of V² in q, or dropping the chord c. Treat c and C_h as separate factors; C_h is dimensionless, c is in metres.
  • Thinking the follow-up linkage adds feel. It is position feedback that closes the loop and stops the surface at the commanded angle.
  • Believing higher hydraulic pressure gives a faster surface. Pressure sets the force; flow sets the rate.

1.4 Q-feel system and servo tabs

An irreversible powered control has no natural feel: the actuator swallows the whole hinge moment and the stick is disconnected from the airloads. Q-feel puts feel back artificially, so stick force grows with dynamic pressure q = ½ρV² — light at low speed, firm at high speed. Tabs do the same aerodynamically: a small tab with a small hinge moment drives or lightens a large surface. Nearly every large transport has artificial feel of some kind, and tabs remain common on manual and boosted surfaces.

You need: Chapter 1.3 for the irreversible powered system that has lost its natural feel.

Why feel must follow dynamic pressure

In an irreversible powered control the actuator carries the whole hinge moment. Airloads no longer push back on the stick, so feel must be made on purpose. A plain spring is the obvious answer and the wrong one: the response changes with speed, the spring does not.

q = ½ ρ V²

q is dynamic pressure in Pa (N/m²); ρ is air density in kg/m³, about 1.225 at sea level in the standard atmosphere; V is true airspeed in m/s.

Hinge moment grows roughly with q times deflection. At 250 m/s, q is about eleven times its value at 75 m/s. A fixed spring would make the aircraft razor-sharp at high speed and sluggish at low speed. Q-feel makes the spring rate a function of q, so stick force follows the square of airspeed and the aircraft feels the same at every speed.

The Q-feel chain: pitot to stick

  1. Sensing: pitot minus static gives impact pressure, read by a capsule or the air data computer.
  2. Feel computer: scales q into a gradient, e.g. 3 N per kilopascal.
  3. Bellows: q moves a spring cartridge anchor in the stick circuit, stiffening it as q rises.
  4. Spring gradient: stick travel x gives force F = k(q)·x.
  5. Bob weight: a mass on the linkage adds force with load factor n — feel per g that no speed sensor gives.
  6. Trim release: unloads the feel unit so the pilot can shift the neutral point; on release the present position is zero force.

The unit is called artificial or synthetic feel. It only loads the stick, never the surface.

Servo tabs and the tab family

A tab is a small aerofoil hinged at the trailing edge of a main surface. Its chord is short, so its own hinge moment is small, but its lift acts on a long arm behind the surface hinge. A tab can therefore move something much bigger than itself.

The servo tab is linked to the fixed structure, not the surface. When the pilot moves the surface, the linkage swings the tab the opposite way; the tab's lift presses the trailing edge round and drags the surface with it. A small stick force moves a large surface, so a manual aircraft can be flown by tab. The servo tab is a driving device.

1.5 Digital fly-by-wire and fly-by-light

Fly-by-wire (FBW) replaces the mechanical link between cockpit and surface with wires, sensors and computers. The pilot moves a sidestick or column; gyros, accelerometers and air data measure the aircraft; redundant flight control computers use control laws to command hydraulic or electro-hydrostatic actuators. Fly-by-light (FBL) keeps the same chain but carries the signals over optical fibre. FBW is used on modern airliners and fighters because it saves weight, removes rigging and friction, and allows envelope protection and relaxed static stability.

You need: Chapter 1.3 for powered actuation and Chapter 1.4 for artificial feel.

The signal chain

In a mechanical system the pilot's hand supplies the command and the rod or cable carries it. In FBW the command becomes an electrical signal. A sidestick or column has position sensors; rate gyros measure body angular rates p, q and r (roll, pitch and yaw rates in degrees per second); accelerometers measure normal acceleration nz in g; air data gives airspeed V, dynamic pressure qbar and angle of attack α. These signals enter the flight control computer (FCC). The pilot never pushes the surface directly; the FCC decides how much surface deflection gives the commanded motion.

  1. Pilot input: sidestick position gives a demand, for example a pitch rate in degrees per second.
  2. Sensors: gyros, accelerometers and air data measure the actual motion and air state.
  3. FCC: three or four independent lanes compute the same command; a voter compares lanes and rejects a disagreeing lane.
  4. Actuator: the FCC sends a command to a servo actuator, usually powered by hydraulic pressure; an electro-hydrostatic actuator has its own electric pump.
  5. Surface: the actuator moves the aileron, elevator or rudder; the resulting motion is fed back by the sensors.

Control laws and modes

A control law is the formula or table that turns pilot and sensor inputs into actuator commands. A rate command law commands pitch rate, roll rate or yaw rate; the FCC compares commanded rate with measured rate and drives the surface until the error is small. For pitch, the C* law blends pitch rate and normal acceleration: at low speed it behaves like a rate command, at high speed it limits load factor. This gives consistent handling across the flight envelope.

  • Normal mode: all protections active: stall, overspeed, bank and load-factor limits.
  • Alternate mode: some protections lost; control laws simplified.
  • Direct mode: pilot command goes to the actuator with minimal feedback; no envelope protection.

FBW saves weight by deleting cables, pulleys, turnbuckles and rigging. It removes friction and backlash. The FCC can add envelope protection, carefree handling, gust alleviation and relaxed static stability, which allows a smaller tail. The cost is dependence on electric power, computers and validated software; lightning and electromagnetic interference (EMI) need protection.

Digital lanes and fly-by-light

Early FBW was analog. Digital FBW samples signals and runs software, so it can self-check, schedule gains by software, run built-in test (BIT), and grow by software change. Redundancy is by lanes, not by making one lane perfect. With three lanes, two can outvote one; with four, a failed lane can be isolated. The worked example shows why: three lanes at 1 × 10⁻⁴ failures per hour give 1 × 10⁻¹² per hour for all three failing, below the usual 1 × 10⁻⁹ per hour catastrophic target.

P(all k lanes fail) = pk

p is the failure probability of one lane in one flight hour; k is the number of independent lanes.

Fly-by-light replaces the copper signal wires with optical fibre. It is immune to EMI, has no ground loops, cannot cause a spark at a fuel tank, is light and has high bandwidth. Problems are mechanical: fibre has a minimum bending radius, every connector loses optical power, and the optical power budget must close. FBL still uses hydraulic or electric power at the actuator.

1.6 Autopilot and automatic flight control

An autopilot closes the control loop automatically. Sensors measure the aircraft's actual attitude, rate or path. A computer compares that with the commanded value and sends a correction. Servos move the surfaces or the throttles. The pilot selects a mode and a target; the machine holds it. This frees attention for navigation and monitoring, and it is the basis of every automatic flight control system (AFCS), from a light aircraft wing leveller to an airliner autoland.

You need: the powered actuators of Chapter 1.3 and the computer-driven control laws of Chapter 1.5, because the autopilot commands those actuators and uses those computers.

The parts of the system

An automatic flight control system (AFCS) is a closed loop. The pilot or a navigation receiver gives a command. Sensors measure the actual aircraft state. A computer subtracts actual from commanded to get an error, shapes a correction, and sends it to a servo. The servo moves a control surface or a thrust lever. The aircraft responds, the sensors see the new state, and the loop repeats.

The sensors are gyros for attitude and rate, an inertial measurement unit (IMU) for acceleration and rate, air data for airspeed and altitude, and a radio altimeter for height above the ground during approach. The computer is usually a flight control computer with redundancy. The servo is an electro-hydraulic or electro-mechanical actuator. The mode selector tells the computer what to hold; the disconnect button removes servo power and returns control to the pilot.

The inner loop is fast and holds attitude or rate: pitch rate, roll rate and yaw damping. The outer loop is slower and holds a path: altitude, heading, speed or a radio navigation course. The outer loop sends its target to the inner loop, as a heading select mode asks the roll channel for a bank angle.

δa = Kp e − Kr p (roll); δe = Kp e − Kr q (pitch)

For the roll channel: δa is the aileron command in degrees; p is the roll rate in degrees per second, positive right. For the pitch channel: δe is the elevator command in degrees, positive nose-up; q is the pitch rate in degrees per second, positive nose-up. e is the error between commanded and actual attitude in degrees. Kp is the proportional gain in degrees per degree. Kr is the rate gain in degrees per degree per second; it has units of seconds. The minus sign damps the motion.

Modes and the two loops

Pitch hold and roll hold keep the current attitude. Altitude hold uses the outer loop to command small pitch changes. Heading select commands a bank angle until the heading error is zero. VOR/LOC and glide-slope coupling let the autopilot follow a radio beam: localizer for lateral, glide slope for vertical. Autoland adds redundant sensors, computers and servos, and is classified as Category I, II or III by decision height and runway visual range.

Low-speed protection and upset protection watch for stall or unusual attitude and push the aircraft back to a safe state. Disengagement logic removes the autopilot when the pilot presses the disconnect button, when a sensor fails, or when the servo is forced against a limit. Some systems give an aural warning before disconnect.

Rate feedback exists because proportional control alone overshoots. If the aircraft is rolling toward the target, the proportional term still commands a large correction. The rate term subtracts a command proportional to how fast the aircraft is already moving. The result is a smaller command near the target and a smooth stop.

On a Cessna 172R, a simple single-axis wing leveller uses one servo on the ailerons and a turn coordinator as its rate sensor. The turn coordinator is primarily a rate-of-turn instrument; its canted gyro also responds to roll rate, and the wing leveller uses that roll-rate signal. It does not measure bank angle. If a wing drops, the servo moves the ailerons to level the wings. It cannot hold altitude or fly a heading by itself; the pilot still controls pitch and power.

Active control technology goes further, using the same surfaces for load alleviation and stability augmentation; see Chapter 1.7.

Auto-throttle

The auto-throttle system (ATS) moves the thrust levers. It does not move the elevator. In speed hold it compares actual speed with the selected speed and moves the levers to reduce the error. In Mach hold it does the same with Mach number. Take-off/go-around (TOGA) moves the levers to the take-off or go-around thrust limit.

Auto-throttle helps in gusty air because the thrust changes quickly while the pilot keeps a steady attitude. On approach it holds speed while the autopilot follows the glide slope, so the pilot monitors rather than chases the throttles. It is not a pitch control. If speed is low, the auto-throttle adds thrust; the autopilot continues to hold the selected path with pitch. A descent to regain speed belongs only to a speed-on-pitch mode, not the usual auto-throttle split.

The same loop structure applies: error, gain, actuator. A speed error of 5 kt might produce a small lever movement; a large error produces a larger movement. Rate of change of speed can be fed back to damp the response, just as rate feedback damps the roll channel.

Worked example

Question. A roll channel uses 3° of aileron per degree of roll error and 0.8° of aileron per degree per second of roll rate, with the rate term subtracted. At one instant the aircraft is 4° left of the commanded bank and rolling right at 2°/s. Find the aileron command and the share removed by rate feedback.

  1. Set e = +4° for the 4° left bank error, because the correction needed is to the right. Set p = +2°/s for the right roll rate. A positive result means right aileron.
  2. Proportional command = Kp × e = 3 × 4 = 12° of aileron.
  3. Rate share = Kr × p = 0.8 × 2 = 1.6° of aileron.
  4. Aileron command δ = Kp e − Kr p = 12 − 1.6 = 10.4° of right aileron.
  5. Rate share as a percentage of the proportional command = 1.6 ÷ 12 × 100 = 13.3 %. The rate term removes about 13 % of the proportional command and damps the roll.

Answer. The aileron command is 10.4° of right aileron; rate feedback removes 1.6°, about 13.3 % of the 12° proportional command.

  • An autopilot without servos is only a computer. The servo is the part that moves the surface or lever.
  • The rate term is subtracted algebraically when p is positive in the direction of the error; if the aircraft is rolling away from the target, p has the opposite sign and the rate term adds to the correction. Adding rate feedback with the wrong sign makes the response less damped and increases overshoot.
  • A single-axis wing leveller holds wings level; it does not hold altitude or heading by itself.

1.7 Active control technology

Active control technology (ACT) makes the flight control computers do more than follow the stick: they shape airframe loads and supply stability. Relaxing static stability lets the centre of gravity move aft, so the tail pushes down less and trim drag falls. Load alleviation cuts gust and manoeuvre peaks, so the wing can be lighter. Some syllabi call such an aircraft a control configured vehicle. Airlines fly it on Airbus and Boeing types; fighters depend on it.

You need: Chapter 1.5 for the fly-by-wire chain and Chapter 1.6 for closed-loop thinking.

Relaxed static stability

A conventional aircraft is stable in pitch because its centre of gravity (CG) lies ahead of the neutral point, the point where a change in angle of attack gives no pitching moment. The gap is the static margin. A positive margin means the tail must push down in steady flight.

SM = (x_np − x_cg) / c̄

SM is the static margin (dimensionless); x_np is the neutral point position, x_cg the centre of gravity position, both measured along the chord from the same datum in metres; c̄ is the mean aerodynamic chord in metres. SM > 0 means statically stable.

That download is lift the wing makes and the tail throws away, and it grows with the margin. ACT lets the designer shrink the margin and push the CG aft, because the computer now supplies pitch stability artificially. Download falls, trim drag falls, fuel burn improves, and the tail can be smaller.

The price: the aircraft cannot be flown without the computer, since the bare airframe diverges slowly in pitch. Losing the computer is a design case that must be shown safe. This is relaxed static stability (RSS) — not a licence to make an aircraft unstable for manoeuvrability.

Load alleviation and ride quality

The same loop does load alleviation. Gust load alleviation (GLA) senses vertical acceleration with accelerometers near the wing tips and in the fuselage. A band-pass filter keeps only the gust band, roughly 1–3 Hz, where the wing and the gust response live. It rejects the steady 1 g part, so a steady climb passes through untouched, and it rejects high-frequency sensor noise. The computer then deflects ailerons and spoilers to unload the wing as the gust arrives, cutting the peak bending moment at the wing root.

Manoeuvre load alleviation (MLA) does the same for the pilot's pull: when pitch rate shows a manoeuvre starting, the surfaces shift lift inboard and the root bending peak falls.

Flutter suppression uses the sensing too. Flutter is an unstable oscillation from bending and torsion coupling. The surface is driven so its aerodynamic force is in phase with the flutter mode's velocity (90° out of phase with its displacement), which adds damping. Ride smoothing cuts the response in turbulence. Envelope limiting caps the pilot's demand so angle of attack α, load factor n and dynamic pressure q stay inside the envelope — carefree handling.

Hardware and the certification argument

The chain is short: accelerometers and rate gyros (see Unit 4, Aircraft transducers) feed the computers, which shape the command and pass it to the same actuators that fly the surface. The band-pass filter is what separates ACT from the basic control law. Actuator rate limits matter: a surface that cannot move fast enough lags the command, adds phase, and can drive a mode unstable instead of damping it.

Certification rests on two demonstrations. The function must be shown not to make any load worse anywhere in the envelope, because a wrong deflection at the wrong moment is itself a load case. Its failure must be shown safe, with the airframe surviving the function switched off.

The prize is structural mass: if the wing is certified to the reduced peak load, the bending material at the root shrinks, and that saving is carried for the life of the aircraft.

Worked example

Question. A gust gives a peak limit load factor of 2.5 g. With gust load alleviation the peak falls to 2.0 g. The wing must be designed to 1.5 times the limit load. (a) Find both ultimate load factors. (b) Find the percentage reduction in design load.

  1. Factor of safety 1.5, so ultimate = 1.5 × limit.
  2. (a) Without ACT: 1.5 × 2.5 = 3.75 g. With ACT: 1.5 × 2.0 = 3.0 g.
  3. (b) Reduction = 0.75 / 3.75 = 20 %.
  4. Bending material at the root scales with design load, so about 20 % less material.

Answer. Ultimate load factor is 3.75 g without ACT and 3.0 g with it, a 20 % cut in design load.

  • GLA needs a band-pass filter, not a plain high-pass or low-pass: the high-pass would pass sensor noise, the low-pass would follow a steady climb and fight it.
  • Ultimate load is 1.5 × limit load. Cutting the limit peak by 20 % cuts the ultimate peak by 20 %, not 30 %.
  • GLA acts on gusts, MLA on the pilot's manoeuvre. Examiners swap the names.
  • An active function that saturates its actuator can raise a load, so actuator rate limits are part of the load case.

1.8 Engine control systems

The engine is a control loop of its own. The thrust lever states a demand; the engine control system sets fuel flow and engine geometry to meet it while keeping spool speeds, exhaust gas temperature and surge margin inside limits. Older engines use hydro-mechanical fuel control units; modern ones use a Full Authority Digital Engine Control (FADEC). The same loop accepts thrust demand from the auto-throttle and returns engine data to the cockpit. Only a few universities teach this inside the flight control unit, because it is the clearest example of a fast inner loop nested under the aircraft's outer loop.

You need: Chapter 1.6 for auto-throttle, and the closed-loop idea from Chapter 1.3 and Chapter 1.5.

Hydro-mechanical engine control

An engine control loop has one thrust-setting variable and several limits. On most turbofans the thrust parameter is N1, the low-pressure or fan spool speed as a percentage of a reference. Some engines use N2, the high-pressure spool speed, or the engine pressure ratio (EPR) as the thrust-setting parameter; the rest of the loop is the same. EGT is the exhaust gas temperature; TGT (turbine gas temperature) is the same kind of measurement taken closer to the turbine, so its limiting value differs from the EGT limit. P1 and T1 are inlet pressure and temperature, because air density changes how much fuel a lever setting should receive. Fuel flow is in kg/s, and oil pressure is a health input.

The fuel control outputs are the fuel metering valve, variable stator vanes and bleed valves. Ignition and the thrust reverser are separate systems on a hydro-mechanical engine; on a FADEC engine the ignition is driven by the FADEC. A hydro-mechanical fuel control unit (FCU) compares the throttle lever's demand with a speed governor. Acceleration enrichment adds extra fuel during a rapid lever movement, and limiters reduce fuel before EGT or spool speed exceeds its schedule. The FCU is a closed-loop controller made of cams, bellows and levers: reliable, but hard to retune. It has no software and no built-in test.

FADEC and ratings

A FADEC (Full Authority Digital Engine Control) is an electronic engine control with two channels, one active and one standby. It has its own power supply, built-in test, and no full-authority mechanical backup. It reads throttle lever angle, N1, N2, EGT, fuel flow, P1, T1 and oil pressure, then drives the fuel metering valve, stator vanes, bleed valves and ignition. It also runs auto-start and sends engine data to the cockpit and the auto-throttle.

The lever asks for a rating, not a fuel flow. Typical ratings are take-off, maximum continuous and cruise. For each rating FADEC schedules N1 against P1 and T1, with EGT as the limit. If EGT reaches its ceiling, FADEC trims fuel back even though the lever asks for more. The same lever position therefore gives different thrust on a hot afternoon and a cold morning. In automatic flight the flight control system sends thrust demand to the auto-throttle, which moves the lever; FADEC still decides the fuel.

Worked example

Question. In cruise a FADEC meters fuel at 0.12 kg/s for 3 hours. (a) Find the fuel mass used. (b) Convert it to litres at a density of 0.8 kg/L. (c) Find the fuel cost at ₹90 per litre.

  2. For 3 h: 432 × 3 = 1296 kg.
  3. Volume = 1296 ÷ 0.8 = 1620 L.
  4. Cost = 1620 × 90 = ₹1,45,800.

Answer. The fuel used is 1296 kg, or 1620 L, costing ₹1,45,800.

  • A rating is a thrust schedule, not a fixed fuel flow.

Key terms and formulas

TermMeaning or formulaChapter
Flight control chainThe path command → linkage → surface deflection → airload → aircraft motion; break any link and the aircraft stops obeying the pilot.1.1
Hinge momentThe aerodynamic torque about a control surface's hinge line, in newton-metres (N·m); it is what the pilot or actuator must overcome.1.1
Push-pull rodA stiff tube that carries a command in compression as well as tension, so it needs guides and lost-motion-free joints.1.2
Cable-pulley systemSteel cables in matched pairs running over pulleys; each cable carries only tension, so a return path is compulsory.1.2
TurnbuckleAn adjustable threaded link that sets and holds cable tension so both cables of a pair stay taut.1.2
Bell crankA pivoted lever that changes the direction of a rod or cable run and can also change the mechanical advantage.1.2
Power-assisted controlA system that keeps the mechanical link and adds a hydraulic boost, so the pilot feels a reduced share of the hinge moment.1.3
Fully powered controlA system where actuators alone move the surface; the mechanical link is gone and artificial feel must be added.1.3
Irreversible controlA powered control whose actuator will not back-drive the cockpit, so surface loads are never felt by the pilot directly.1.3
Q-feel systemAn artificial feel unit that makes stick force proportional to dynamic pressure q = ½ρV², imitating the feel of a mechanical aircraft.1.4
Servo tabA small tab whose deflection creates a hinge moment that moves the main surface, so the pilot moves the tab and the tab moves the surface.1.4
Anti-servo tabA tab geared to move the same way as the surface, giving a stabilator positive stick force and preventing over-control.1.4
Fly-by-wire (FBW)A system where pilot inputs become electrical signals to computers that command hydraulic or electric actuators.1.5
Control lawThe software rule that converts pilot demand and sensor data into surface commands, shaping gain with speed, altitude and configuration.1.5

Fly-by-light (FBL)FBW whose signal path is optical fibre instead of wire, giving immunity to electromagnetic interference and no conductive path for lightning.1.5

Takeaways

  1. An aircraft is not naturally stable in the sense the pilot wants, so a control chain must connect cockpit demand to aerodynamic response.
  2. Rods carry push and pull; cables carry pull only, so cable systems always run in matched pairs with turnbuckles to keep tension.
  3. Mechanical systems are simple and cheap but heavy, friction-ridden and hard to route through a large fuselage.
  4. Above a certain size and speed the hinge moment exceeds human strength, so hydraulics supplies the force and the pilot supplies only the command.
  5. An irreversible powered system must be given artificial feel, by a Q-feel unit or by tabs, or the pilot loses all sense of airspeed.
  6. Fly-by-wire replaces mechanical linkage with computers and control laws, giving weight saving, envelope protection and carefree handling at the cost of redundancy.
  7. An autopilot is the same control loop as the pilot's hand but closed by sensors and servos, and the auto throttle closes the same loop on the engine.
  8. Active control technology turns the control surfaces into load and stability devices, allowing a smaller tail and a lighter wing.
  9. Engine control is a separate loop: from throttle demand to fuel flow and geometry, now run digitally by FADEC inside every limit.

Exam question bank

Long answers here are usually built around one chain — command, linkage or signal, actuator, surface, airload — plus the reasons a given system replaced the previous one, so draw the block diagram and then justify each block.

Remember and understand

Understand Explain why an aircraft needs a flight control system at all. Describe the chain from cockpit input to aircraft motion and name the quantity each link must handle. 10 marks

Aircraft does not hold a wanted attitude or path by itself; disturbances and trim changes act continuously; Chain: pilot command → linkage or signal → surface deflection → change in local angle of attack → aerodynamic force and hinge moment → aircraft motion; Feedback to the pilot through feel, or through sensors in a powered system; Distinguish control (short-term attitude) from trim and from stability.

Understand Compare the push-pull rod system with the cable-pulley system for primary flight controls: construction, load paths, friction, weight and maintenance. 13 marks

Rod: tube in tension and compression, needs guides and bell cranks, high stiffness, low stretch, heavier over long runs; Cable: tension only, always in matched pairs, pulleys, turnbuckles, fairleads, follow-up on both sides; Friction and backlash: pulleys and swaging add compliance; rods add many joints; Routing through fuselage and wing, thermal expansion, preload, inspection for fraying or rod end wear; Which suits a small trainer and which suits a large transport.

Remember List the main components of a conventional flight control system and state the function of each. 10 marks

Cockpit controls: stick or wheel, pedals, throttle; Linkage: rods, cables, pulleys, bell cranks, quadrants, turnbuckles; Surface attachments: horns, hinges, control surface itself; Feel and trim devices: trim tabs, bungees, downsprings, Q-feel unit; Stops, locks and gust locks; position indicators.

Understand Explain the Q-feel system. Why is it needed, how does it work, and how does it produce stick force proportional to dynamic pressure? 13 marks

Irreversible powered control gives no hinge moment feedback to the pilot; q = ½ρV²; at higher speed and altitude-corrected speed the aircraft responds more sharply, so feel must rise too; Q-feel unit uses airspeed or impact pressure to load a spring or bungee in the linkage; Result: stick force per unit deflection rises with q, restoring natural handling and preventing over-stress; Compare with aerodynamic feel from a tab.

Remember What is a servo tab? Explain how a small tab force moves a large surface, and how the anti-servo tab differs. 8 marks

Tab hinged at the trailing edge of the main surface; Pilot moves the tab; tab deflection creates a hinge moment about the main surface hinge; Main surface follows, so pilot force depends on tab size and gearing, not on surface area; Anti-servo tab moves same direction as surface, adding feel and damping on stabilators; Trim tab is set to a fixed deflection to relieve steady stick force.

Understand Describe a digital fly-by-wire system. Explain the signal path and the role of control laws and redundancy. 14 marks

Pilot input transducers, air data and inertial sensors feed the flight control computers; Computers run control laws; output drives electro-hydraulic or electro-mechanical actuators; Control law gives gain scheduling with q and configuration, envelope protection, turn coordination; Redundancy: multiple computers, lanes, dissimilar hardware and software, majority voting, reversion modes; Advantages: weight, no rigging or friction, envelope protection; disadvantages: cost, software certification, power dependence.

Understand Explain the operation of an autopilot and of an auto throttle, and state the advantages of each. 13 marks

Autopilot: sensor measures attitude or path, comparator forms error against command, computer shapes correction, servo moves surface; Inner loops (pitch rate, roll rate) and outer loops (altitude, heading); PID shaping for damping; Modes: wing level, heading hold, altitude hold, glide slope and localiser tracking, autoland; Auto throttle: servo drives throttle levers to hold speed, thrust or Mach; coupled to autopilot in approach; Advantages: reduced workload, fuel saving, precise tracking, consistent approach speed, better ride.

Understand Explain full authority digital engine control (FADEC). How does it replace a hydro-mechanical fuel control unit? 13 marks

Engine loop: throttle lever angle in, fuel flow and geometry (VSV, VBV, bleed) out, thrust produced; Sensors: N1, N2, EGT, fuel flow, inlet pressure and temperature, PLA; Hydro-mechanical unit uses cams and bellows to schedule fuel against speed and pressure; FADEC: digital schedule with full authority, limits on N and EGT, start and reverse scheduling, built-in test; Advantages: fuel efficiency, precise limits, condition monitoring; needs redundant channels and power.

Apply

Apply A transport aircraft of 60 t has a control surface with hinge moment coefficient 0.08, area 2.4 m² and chord 0.5 m. Estimate the hinge moment at 250 m/s at sea level and decide whether a human could move it without hydraulic boost. Show each step. 12 marks

q = ½ρV² with ρ = 1.225 kg/m³ gives about 38 kPa; Hinge moment = Ch × q × S × c; Substitute and get the number with units (N·m); Compare with a realistic stick force through the linkage and with a human limit of a few hundred newtons; Conclude that full power or power assist is needed; state assumptions about deflection.

Apply A small trainer has irreversible controls only on the elevator. Explain how you would add artificial feel and how you would trim it, using only tabs and bungees. 10 marks

Elevator feel: bungee or spring cartridge in the linkage, plus a downspring if needed for static stability; Anti-servo tab on stabilator to give positive stick force gradient; Trim: trim tab deflection relieves steady stick force, or an adjustable spring in the circuit; Check that force per unit speed rises and that trim authority covers the CG range; State the consequence of no feel: over-control and structural risk.

Apply Design the redundancy scheme for a digital fly-by-wire flight control computer of a civil transport and justify the voting scheme you choose. 14 marks

Three or four computers, two lanes each; dissimilar software and hardware; Sensor lanes duplicated; comparison, threshold and majority vote (2-out-of-3 or 3-out-of-4); Fail-passive first, then fail-operational after further failures; Reversion modes, direct law versus normal law, degradation announcement; Power supply redundancy, built-in test, maintenance significance; Certification argument and probability of loss of control.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare mechanical, power-assisted, fully powered and fly-by-wire flight control systems on weight, feel, redundancy, maintenance and envelope protection. Which suits a 2-seat trainer and which suits a large transport? 15 marks

Weight trend with aircraft size and run length; Feel: natural, boosted, artificial by Q-feel, artificial by control law; Redundancy: none, simple, duplicated hydraulics, multi-computer systems; Maintenance: rigging, backlash, hydraulic leaks versus software and LRU replacement; Envelope protection only in FBW; cost and certification burden; Recommendation map: trainer to mechanical or simple powered; transport to FBW.

Evaluate Discuss active control technology. How do load alleviation, flutter suppression, gust alleviation and relaxed static stability change the aircraft design, and what risks does each introduce? 15 marks

Gust and manoeuvre load alleviation reduces wing bending moment, allowing a lighter wing; Flutter suppression extends the speed envelope with a smaller tail; Relaxed static stability allows a smaller tail and better cruise efficiency at the cost of continuous computer involvement; Ride smoothing for passenger comfort; Risks: dependence on the control system, failure cases, software maturity, pilot training; Carefree handling as an operational benefit.

Evaluate Evaluate fly-by-light against fly-by-wire. State the physical reasons for its advantages and the practical reasons it is not universal. 13 marks

FBW signal path is electrical wire; FBL uses optical fibre; Advantages: no electromagnetic interference, no conductive path for lightning or high intensity radiated fields, lower cable weight, higher bandwidth; Problems: optical connectors and splices are delicate, bend radius limits routing, actuators still need electrical power, optical to hydraulic interface adds complexity; Weight of copper against weight of fibre and terminations; Where FBL suits: military and high-interference environments.

Analyze An aircraft on approach shows a slow pitch oscillation after the autopilot was engaged. Analyse the possible causes in the control loop and the checks you would make. 12 marks

Identify loop elements: sensor, comparator, control law gain and damping, servo, surface, aircraft dynamics; Sensor error or lag; altitude or pitch sensor noise; Control law gain too high for current q; scheduled gain fault; Servo rate limit or backlash; actuator hysteresis; Aircraft handling changes with speed and configuration; CG shift; Test: disengage, check in manual, check gain schedule and servo rigging.

Evaluate Modern aircraft group flight control, autopilot, engine and navigation functions into integrated modular avionics. Discuss the benefits and the new failure modes this creates. 14 marks

LRUs replaced by common computing modules hosting software partitions; Benefits: weight, power, cost, fewer connectors, easier upgrade, shared maintenance data; Partitioning and robust time and space separation; certification of shared resources; New failure modes: common mode failure in shared modules, network and bus failures, software complexity, thermal loading; Redundancy and grace degradation; maintenance and built-in test implications; Flight management system and autopilot coupling through a shared data bus.

Glossary

Actuator
The hydraulic or electric ram that converts a command signal into a force and stroke on a control surface.
Aileron
A wing trailing-edge surface deflected differentially to produce roll; one goes up while the other goes down.
Auto throttle
A servo system that drives the throttles automatically to hold a selected speed, thrust or Mach number.
Autopilot
A system that holds or tracks an attitude, heading, altitude or path by closing the control loop through sensors and servos.
Backlash
Lost motion between linked parts, so a small control input produces no output; it adds free play and can cause flutter.
Control horn
The lever arm on a control surface to which the push-pull rod or cable is attached, setting the mechanical advantage.
Control surface
A movable aerofoil whose deflection changes the force or moment on the aircraft, such as an aileron, elevator, rudder or flap.
Direct law
A degraded FBW mode where pilot inputs pass to the surfaces with simple gearing and no envelope protection.
Dynamic pressure
q = ½ρV², in pascals when ρ is kg/m³ and V is m/s; it sets the aerodynamic force on a surface and hence the feel force.
Electro-hydraulic servo valve
A valve that meters hydraulic flow in proportion to a small electrical current, giving fast, precise actuator control.
Elevator
The pitch control surface hinged to the horizontal tailplane, moving the tail lift thus the pitching moment.
Envelope protection
Control-law limiting that prevents the aircraft exceeding angle of attack, load factor, pitch or bank limits whatever the pilot asks.
FADEC
Full Authority Digital Engine Control: a redundant digital computer that schedules engine fuel and geometry within all limits.
Fly-by-wire
A control system in which pilot input is converted to electrical signals and computers command the actuators.
Load alleviation
Deflecting surfaces automatically to reduce wing or tail bending loads during gusts and manoeuvres.
LRU
Line Replaceable Unit: a box that can be swapped on the flight line without recalibration or deep maintenance.
Mechanical advantage
The ratio of output force to input force of a linkage, the inverse of the ratio of their displacements.
Relaxed static stability
A design where the aircraft is deliberately made slightly unstable and stabilised continuously by the flight control system.
Rudder
The yaw control surface on the vertical fin, producing side force and yaw, also used to coordinate turns.
Servo
A device that compares a command with a measured position and drives an output until the error is removed.
Stabilator
A one-piece horizontal tail whose whole surface moves about a pivot, used instead of a separate fin and elevator.
Throttle lever angle
The position of the cockpit thrust lever, the command input to the engine control loop, usually given in degrees.
Trim
A small steady deflection or force that holds the aircraft in the desired state without continuous pilot input.
Trim tab
A small hinged tab set to a fixed deflection that produces a steady hinge moment to relieve stick force in cruise.
