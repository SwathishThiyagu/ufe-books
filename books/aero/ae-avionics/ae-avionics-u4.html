4.1 Civil, military, spacecraft and UAV avionics

Avionics is aircraft electronics, but the word covers different design goals. A civil airliner needs safety, regularity and certification. A fighter needs mission computing, survivability and weapons control. A spacecraft needs autonomy, telemetry and fault recovery far from repair. A UAV needs small size, low weight and a datalink to a remote pilot. The same building blocks recur: sensors, processing, displays, data buses, power and cooling. Their balance changes with the platform. This chapter compares the four families and shows why one avionics template cannot serve all.

You need: Unit 1 flight control basics help but are not required; Section 4.2 sets the architecture vocabulary.

From separate boxes to integrated avionics

Early aircraft carried separate radios, a gyro horizon, an altimeter and an airspeed indicator. Each box had its own wire, power and case. Integration began when digital computers could combine air data, attitude and navigation. The cockpit became fewer glass displays fed by shared computers; displays and data buses are covered in 4.2 and 4.3. A useful framework is six layers: sensors, processing, displays, data buses, power and cooling. Sensors include air data probes, inertial units, radar and electro-optical turrets. Processing turns measurements into flight guidance, mission tracks or telemetry frames. Displays present state to a crew or remote pilot. Data buses carry words with timing and error checks, such as ARINC 429 and MIL-STD-1553B (4.7). Power and cooling set what can run. Civil avionics adds certification: a failure must be shown to be extremely improbable if its effect is catastrophic. ADS-B Out is a civil example: the aircraft broadcasts position and identity at 1090 MHz for surveillance; signal details are in Unit 5. Military and spacecraft systems reuse the same layers but change the priorities.

Civil and military avionics

Civil avionics optimises safety, regularity and cost. The environment is a pressurised cabin, repeated take-off and landing, lightning and icing. Redundancy is formal: duplicate or triple channels, cross-checks and dispatch rules. Certification follows failure consequence; software assurance is tied to the effect of a failure (4.9). A B737 broadcasting ADS-B Out at 1090 MHz is civil surveillance; it helps controllers see the aircraft, but it is not a navigation sensor (Unit 5).

Military avionics optimises mission success and survivability. The environment adds vibration, high g, radar illumination and electromagnetic jamming. Redundancy may be battle damage tolerance and graceful degradation, not only dispatch. Mission computing fuses radar, EO/IR, SIGINT, communications and stores management. Stores management controls weapons release; it is not a sensor. Electronic warfare (EW) detects, locates or counters threats. HUD and helmet-mounted displays keep symbols in the pilot's line of sight (4.5). MIL-STD-1553B is a common military bus. Certification is by military airworthiness and mission requirements, not civil passenger rules. The payload list matters: radar, EO/IR, SIGINT, communications, stores and science packages each need power, cooling and data paths, but sensor physics is outside this chapter.

Spacecraft and UAV avionics

Spacecraft avionics optimises autonomy and telemetry. A satellite cannot be repaired; it must survive launch vibration, vacuum, thermal cycling and radiation. Processing must detect faults and recover, often with redundant computers and watchdogs. Guidance computes attitude and orbit corrections; a 3-axis satellite uses reaction wheels or thrusters to hold attitude. Telemetry packs housekeeping and science data into frames; the ground segment may see the spacecraft only in short passes. Power is solar arrays plus batteries; cooling is by radiation, not air. Redundancy is sparing and fault recovery, not a quick return to a gate.

UAV avionics optimises size, weight and power. A 25 kg UAV may carry an autopilot, GNSS/INS, a datalink and a small EO/IR payload. Waypoint following is run by the autopilot; the remote pilot sends commands over a datalink. Datalink loss changes design: the UAV must loiter, return or land by itself. Redundancy is limited by mass and cost, so the design uses careful failure modes and geofencing. Treating UAV avionics as small civil avionics is a trap: limited weight, power and datalink loss change the architecture. Payloads range from EO/IR and communications to radar and SIGINT; each adds power and cooling load. See 4.8 for high-bandwidth links and 4.10 for sensors.

4.2 Avionics system architecture, IMA and LRUs

An avionics architecture is a plan of where computing happens and how data reaches the cockpit. Older aircraft use federated architecture: one box, one function. Newer aircraft use integrated modular avionics (IMA): a shared cabinet hosts several functions as separated software partitions. In both layouts the unit of design is the line replaceable unit (LRU), a box a technician can change on the flight line. This chapter compares the two layouts and then covers the built-in test equipment that keeps both of them maintainable.

You need Chapter 4.1 for the platform types that shape these choices, and Chapter 4.7 for how the buses themselves work.

LRUs and federated architecture

A line replaceable unit (LRU) is a box that can be removed and replaced on the flight line. The definition is practical, not electrical: the swap needs hand tools and a short test, no hangar and no recalibration of the whole aircraft. Each LRU has a part number, a connector list and a self-test that runs when power is applied.

Hold four things in view for any avionics layout: sensors (air data probes, inertial measurement units), computers (the boxes that process), data buses (the wires or fibres that carry messages) and displays (what the crew sees). Power and cooling cut across all four; see Unit 3 for generation, distribution, packaging and cooling.

In a federated architecture each LRU owns exactly one function. The air data computer computes airspeed and altitude. The inertial reference unit gives attitude and heading. The autopilot computer moves the control surfaces. Signals travel on dedicated point-to-point wires between boxes, so each function has its own private path.

  • Fault isolation is simple: a fault leads to one box, the box is swapped, the aircraft flies.
  • Each box is certified on its own, with no shared software to argue about.
  • Cost: weight, volume, power and connector count multiply with every added function.
  • Spares: many part numbers must be stocked at every station on the network.

IMA: shared cabinets, modules and partitions

Integrated modular avionics (IMA) replaces many single-function boxes with a few cabinets. A cabinet holds modules — processor modules, input/output modules and power supply modules — that plug into a backplane, a printed board carrying power and data between them. The wiring inside the cabinet is fixed; the function lives in software loaded onto a module.

Each function runs as a partition. ARINC 653 is the standard that defines this arrangement: a partition gets a fixed time slot on the processor and its own memory area, so one function cannot read another's data or take its processor time. Software partitioning detail belongs to Chapter 4.9.

Redundancy does not disappear. A cabinet can hold a standby processor module; if the active module fails, the standby takes the partition over. Because the hardware is now shared, a shared failure is possible, so designers add redundancy rather than remove it. The modules feed the cockpit through switched buses such as AFDX (Chapter 4.8).

Some syllabi call the same idea integrated avionics. The name covers the whole aircraft-level change: shared cabinets, shared buses and shared displays.

BITE, maintenance data and the trade-off

Every LRU and every IMA module carries built-in test equipment (BITE). BITE is circuitry and software inside the box that injects test signals and checks the response. It runs at power-up, continuously in flight, and on command from a maintenance panel. It stores fault codes in non-volatile memory.

Those codes travel over a data bus to a central maintenance computer, then to a maintenance recorder or a ground link. A technician on the apron reads them and goes straight to the suspect box, so a fault is found in minutes instead of hours of probing. This matters on a tight turnaround, where a delay in finding the fault costs the schedule.

Δm = Σ mfed − mIMA

mfed is the mass of one replaced federated LRU in kg, summed over all replaced units; mIMA is the total mass of the cabinet with its modules in kg; Δm is the mass saved in kg. The same subtraction in watts gives the power saved.

  • Mass, volume, power and connector count fall, because there are fewer boxes and fewer wires.
  • Wiring falls again when shared buses replace point-to-point links.
  • Certification work rises: every hosted function must be shown to be isolated from its neighbours.
  • The shared cabinet is a common point, so its failure must be covered by redundancy.

Exam traps

  • IMA does not remove redundancy. Shared hardware makes a common failure possible, so designers add standby modules; an answer that says IMA saves weight by deleting duplicate boxes is wrong.
  • An LRU is the box, not a part inside it. A relay or a pressure switch inside a black box is not an LRU; the line replaceable unit is the box the technician unbolts.
  • BITE tests the unit it sits in and reports codes. It does not repair anything and it does not prove the whole system healthy, because the fault may lie in wiring or in a neighbour.
  • ARINC 653 is a software partitioning standard, not a bus standard. Do not confuse it with ARINC 429 or 664, which describe how bits travel on the wire.
  • Federated architecture is not obsolete. Light aircraft, helicopters and many military types still use it because its certification path is short and its fault isolation is obvious.

Each function runs as a partition. The partition gets a fixed time slot on the processor, enforced by a scheduler, and its own memory area, enforced by memory protection. Apply Six federated LRUs, each of mass 3 kg and drawing 30 W, are replaced by one IMA cabinet of mass 4 kg drawing 70 W. Find the mass and power saved. 5 marks

Federated mass = 6 × 3 = 18 kg; mass saved = 18 − 4 = 14 kg. Federated power = 6 × 30 = 180 W; power saved = 180 − 70 = 110 W.

Analyze Why does a shared IMA cabinet need more redundancy, not less, than a federated layout? 5 marks

In a federated layout a single box failure removes one function only. In a cabinet, one failed module or one failed backplane can remove several functions at once, so the failure is common-mode. The architecture must therefore replicate modules and often replicate cabinets, and route partitions to a standby, so the number of failure paths is greater even though the number of boxes is smaller.

Analyze BITE reports no fault, yet the crew reports a wrong reading on a display. Give two reasons why the report and the reading can disagree. 4 marks

First, BITE tests the unit it lives in and its own interfaces, so a fault in the wiring, a connector or a neighbouring unit is invisible to it. Second, BITE compares against stored limits and may pass a unit that is inside tolerance but drifting, or it may not test the specific path the crew uses, such as a scaling table applied downstream.

4.3 Analog and glass cockpits

A cockpit is the crew's office. In an analog cockpit, each quantity gets its own dial, moved by a sensor through wires or mechanical linkages. In a glass cockpit, a few screens replace many dials. Computers draw pages on those screens from the same sensor data. The change raises information density: the crew sees more useful data in less space. It also adds dependence on power, cooling and software. Civil airliners, military transports and business jets all use glass today; older aircraft remain analog or hybrid.

You need: Chapter 4.2 on avionics architecture, IMA and LRUs, because displays are fed by the architecture.

Analog cockpit: one dial per job

An analog cockpit uses direct-reading dials. A dial is a round instrument with a pointer. The pointer moves because a sensor sends a signal, or because a mechanical linkage turns it. The airspeed indicator, altimeter, vertical speed indicator, attitude indicator, heading indicator, engine tachometer and fuel gauge each have their own case and their own place on the panel.

  • Each dial is single-function: it shows one quantity and nothing else.
  • Wiring is point-to-point: a sensor wire goes to its dial, not to a shared network.
  • Warnings are dedicated: a red lamp, a horn or a stick shaker for one condition.
  • Part count is high: many dials, many connectors, many lamps.
  • Mechanical linkages are common in older aircraft: cables, rods and pulleys move pointers or control surfaces.

The analog cockpit is robust in a simple way. A failed dial does not blank the others. A dial with a direct mechanical link can still read after an electrical failure. The cost is space and crew workload. Twenty or more dials spread across the panel, and the crew must scan them one by one. Adding a new function means adding another instrument.

Glass cockpit: fewer screens, more pages

A glass cockpit replaces most dials with electronic displays. Early glass used cathode-ray tubes (CRTs); modern glass uses liquid-crystal displays (LCDs). A display is a screen, not a sensor. It receives digital data from a symbol generator. The symbol generator is a computer that turns sensor and system data into pixels: lines, scales, numbers and colours.

N_slots = D × P

N_slots is the total number of page slots available across displays; D is the number of displays; P is the number of pages each display can show at different times. A page slot is one display showing one selected page. Displays may share the same page definitions, so the number of distinct pages can be smaller than N_slots.

  1. Sensors measure air data, attitude, engine and system values.
  2. The symbol generator receives those values over data buses.
  3. The generator draws the selected page on the selected display.
  4. The crew changes pages with a control panel or a cursor.
  5. A central warning computer collects failures and shows them on a shared warning page.

A page is a screen layout. One page can contain several dial functions: a primary flight page might show airspeed, attitude, altitude and heading together. One display can show a primary flight display page, then a navigation page, then an engine page. The same display is reconfigurable: it is not tied to one quantity. Page count and function count are separate counts. Four displays with six pages each give 4 × 6 = 24 page slots over time, while 24 dial functions split evenly need six functions per display at once, arranged on one or more pages.

A hybrid cockpit sits between the two. It keeps some analog dials and adds one or two CRT or LCD screens. Many older airliners were upgraded this way: the centre panel got a screen, while the primary dials stayed. A hybrid is not a failed glass cockpit; it is a deliberate mix chosen for cost, certification or training.

Why glass needs power, cooling and software assurance

Glass improves information density but moves the failure points. A dial needs a sensor and a wire. A glass display needs a sensor, a data bus, a symbol generator, a display, electrical power and cooling. If power is lost, several screens can go blank together. If cooling fails, an LCD can overheat and dim or shut down. If software has a defect, a page can show a wrong scale or a stale value.

  • Power: displays and symbol generators draw electrical power; emergency power must keep the essential pages alive.
  • Cooling: CRT and LCD displays produce heat; avionics bays need airflow or liquid cooling.
  • Software assurance: the symbol generator is software-controlled; its development follows a software assurance standard such as DO-178B (DO-178C for newer designs).
  • Data validity: a display is only as good as its sensor and bus data; a valid-looking page can still be wrong.

The trap is the phrase 'glass cockpit'. It does not mean paperless, and it does not mean sensorless. The screens still need valid sensor data and a working symbol generator. The crew still needs checklists, and the aircraft still needs air data probes, gyros or inertial sensors, and engine sensors. The display is the last step in a chain, not the whole chain. For the page formats themselves, see Chapter 4.4.

4.4 Electronic displays: PFD, MFD, EADI and EHSI

A glass cockpit gives the crew a few screens instead of many dials (see 4.3). The primary flight display, or PFD, shows attitude, airspeed, altitude, vertical speed and heading — the data needed to fly. The multifunction display, or MFD, is a selectable screen for navigation, weather or systems pages. The older names EADI and EHSI describe the attitude and navigation halves of the same idea. Engine and systems pages appear on EICAS or ECAM. These formats turn raw sensor and computer data into a standard picture a pilot can read in seconds.

You need: the glass cockpit layout and its data flow (Chapter 4.3), and air data and engine instruments (Unit 3).

PFD: the screen you fly by

The primary flight display (PFD) is the screen in the pilot's forward view that carries the data needed to control the aircraft. Its centre is an attitude picture: sky above, ground below, a white horizon line between them. A flight director draws command bars that show where to pitch and roll to follow the selected mode, such as heading hold or an altitude capture.

Around the attitude picture sit the other primary values. Airspeed runs as a tape on the left, altitude as a tape on the right, vertical speed as a needle or scale at the far right, and heading as a tape or compass arc at the bottom. Selected values, such as the target altitude, appear as small bugs on the tapes.

The layout follows the pilot's scan. The eyes move from attitude to speed to altitude in a short loop, so these sit close together. Colour carries meaning: blue or cyan for sky, brown for ground, red for a warning that needs immediate action, amber for a caution, green for normal or engaged, magenta for a selected target. A red cross or red text over a field means the data is invalid. One PFD replaces five or more mechanical instruments — attitude indicator, airspeed indicator, altimeter, vertical speed indicator and heading indicator — and saves panel area.

MFD and the older names EADI and EHSI

The multifunction display (MFD) is a general-purpose screen. The crew picks a page with bezel keys or a cursor: navigation map, weather radar, traffic, terrain, engine and systems synoptics, or checklists. The same hardware can show any page, which is why one MFD can replace several dedicated panels.

The older names come from the first colour CRT units that replaced mechanical dials. The electronic attitude director indicator (EADI) was the attitude indicator plus flight director on one tube; it could also carry approach deviation and radio altitude. The electronic horizontal situation indicator (EHSI) was the compass card plus course and deviation display, with map, arc and plan modes.

On a modern flight deck the EADI's job is the PFD's attitude area, and the EHSI's job is done by the navigation display (ND), which on many types is a page of the MFD. The names survive in old aircraft documentation and in syllabi. They describe functions, not extra boxes.

EICAS and ECAM: engine and systems pages

Engines and systems need their own permanent pages because a failure there is not visible from attitude alone. Boeing calls its format the engine indication and crew alerting system (EICAS). Airbus calls its equivalent the electronic centralised aircraft monitor (ECAM). EICAS and ECAM are both engine-indication and crew-alerting displays; some notes call them engine and system monitors.

EICAS shows engine parameters such as N1 (fan speed), N2 (core speed), exhaust gas temperature and fuel flow, plus alert messages in a fixed order of severity. ECAM shows engine parameters and system synoptics — hydraulic, electrical, fuel, pressurisation — with a procedure for the current failure. Alert levels rise from advisory (typically cyan/blue) to caution (amber) to warning (red); a warning usually brings an aural alert too. White is used for memo or status text, not for the advisory level.

Failure flags, colour rules and reversion

Failure flags stop a broken screen from misleading the crew. When a computer or sensor fails, the affected field is replaced by a red X, a red box or a status message such as ATT (attitude) failure. The crew then flies on standby instruments or on the remaining valid data. No displayed value is trusted without a validity check.

  • Red cross or red text over a field: data invalid, do not use.
  • Amber message: caution, crew action needed soon.
  • Red message with an aural warning: act now.
  • Green: normal or engaged.

Reversion means switching a display unit to another format when a unit fails. A reversion panel or a soft key lets a remaining screen show the lost page, and on some types the PFD can be moved to the MFD position. Certification fixes the minimum set of screens that must survive, and standby instruments remain the last layer. See 4.6 for how these choices affect crew workload.

4.5 Head-up and helmet-mounted displays

A head-up display (HUD) puts flight symbols in the pilot's forward view, on a glass combiner, while the pilot looks through it at the runway. A helmet-mounted display (HMD) puts symbols on a small display fixed to the helmet, so they follow the head. Both keep critical data in the line of sight and cut the look-down time to the instrument panel. HUDs are common on civil aircraft for low-visibility approach; HMDs are common on military aircraft for cueing, night vision and weapon aiming. The key optical idea is collimation: symbols appear focused at infinity.

You need: electronic display formats from Chapter 4.4 and the glass-cockpit background from Chapter 4.3.

How a HUD makes an image at infinity

A HUD keeps the pilot's eyes on the outside world. The symbol generator is a computer that builds the HUD picture from air data, inertial data and guidance. The projector turns that picture into light and sends it through collimating optics. Collimating optics make the rays from each picture point parallel. The combiner is a partially reflective glass plate in front of the pilot. It reflects the projected light to the eye and still lets the outside scene pass through.

The pilot sees two images together: the real runway and the virtual symbols. Because the rays are parallel, the symbols appear focused at infinity, the same distance as the outside scene. The pilot can read the symbols and watch the runway without changing focus. A screen sits at a short distance and forces the eye to refocus every time the pilot looks from the screen to the outside world. This is the main reason a HUD is not a small screen pasted on glass. The optical path is symbol generator → projector → collimating optics → combiner → pilot eye.

tan θ = h / d

θ is the angle of the combiner above the pilot's forward line of sight (an elevation angle); h is the height of the combiner above the eye line; d is the forward distance from the eye to the combiner.

HUD symbology, civil and military use

HUD symbology has two groups. Conformal symbols are drawn in the same direction as the outside world. The flight path vector, pitch ladder and runway centreline are examples. They appear to sit on the real scene. Non-conformal symbols sit in fixed positions on the combiner. Airspeed, altitude, heading, vertical speed and guidance commands are examples. The pilot reads them without looking down.

Civil aircraft use HUD mainly for low-visibility takeoff and approach. The HUD shows guidance, speed and attitude while the pilot looks at the runway. Military aircraft use HUD for weapon cueing, target designation, terrain following and low-level flight. Some military HMDs add off-boresight cueing: the pilot looks at a target and presses a button, and the system marks that line of sight.

Two practical limits matter. Too many symbols cause clutter, and too bright a display hides the outside scene. Brightness must adjust to daylight, night and cloud. These limits belong to human factors, covered in Chapter 4.6.

Helmet-mounted displays

An HMD fixes a small display to the helmet. It can be monocular, one eye, or binocular, both eyes. A head tracker measures the helmet's orientation, usually with magnetic, optical or inertial sensors. The symbol generator uses that orientation to place symbols correctly. The helmet branch can share the same symbol generator and projector electronics as a HUD.

HMDs are used for cueing and night vision. In a fighter, the pilot can look off-axis, and the helmet shows a target cue or missile seeker direction. In a helicopter, the HMD can show terrain and obstacle warnings while the pilot looks out. Night-vision HMDs combine an image intensifier or thermal camera with symbols. Civil head-worn displays are appearing for taxi and maintenance, but the main use is military.

An HMD is harder than a HUD. The display must be light, close to the eye and safe in a crash. The head tracker must be fast and accurate; delay makes symbols swim when the head moves. The eye relief and exit pupil must be large enough for the pilot to see the whole displayed image, even with the helmet moving slightly on the head. These ergonomic details are in Chapter 4.6.

Worked example

Question. A HUD combiner is 75 mm above the pilot's eye and 500 mm forward. Find the elevation angle θ of the combiner above the pilot's forward line of sight.

  2. Forward distance d = 500 mm.

  • The combiner is a beam splitter, not a small screen.
  • The look-down angle uses vertical height over forward distance, not over horizontal sideways distance.
  • An HMD head tracker is not the display; tracker delay makes symbols lag behind the head.
  • Civil HUD use is mainly low-visibility approach; military HUD and HMD use is mainly cueing and mission data.

4.6 Cockpit design and human-machine interface

A cockpit is a human-machine interface (HMI): the place where a crew senses aircraft state, decides, and acts. It exists because displays, controls and warnings must match human limits of attention, memory and reaction time. Good design makes the normal flow smooth and the abnormal case obvious. It is used in every aircraft, from a two-seat trainer to a long-haul jet. Only a few universities teach this topic in depth.

You need: Chapter 4.4 for display formats and Chapter 4.5 for eyes-out displays.

The cockpit as a human-machine interface

The crew works in a loop: sense, decide, act. The HMI supplies the sense and act parts. Displays show state. Controls change state. Warnings interrupt when state is unsafe. Automation carries long tasks. The pilot stays at the top because the crew remains responsible.

Information hierarchy means importance decides position. Primary flight data — attitude, airspeed, altitude, heading — sits in the forward view. Navigation and systems data sit on multi-function displays (MFDs) and need a deliberate look. Menus hold less urgent data. The more often a value is used and the less time the crew has to react, the nearer it must be to the eyes.

Controls follow the same logic. Frequent controls sit on the stick or throttle, or near the pilot's hand. Infrequent controls sit on panels. Every control must show its effect: a switch position, a light, or a display echo.

Colour, alerts and master caution

Colour carries meaning only if used consistently. Common civil convention: red for warning, amber for caution, green for normal, white for advisory, magenta for the active route. Colour must never be the only cue. A shape, text or sound must repeat it, because colour vision varies and sunlight washes out screens.

Alerts come in levels. A warning needs immediate action (fire, stall). A caution needs timely attention (low fuel, system fault). An advisory is information (a mode change). Master warning and master caution lights gather the first alert so the crew looks at the right system page. Aural alerts use different tones; the sound must stand out from cockpit noise.

Usability rules of the FAR/CS 25.1302 kind ask that a crew can detect, understand and respond to every alert, without hazard from the design itself. The alert must say what is wrong and what action is required. A master caution that flashes for every trivial fault trains the crew to ignore it.

Controls, workload and automation mode awareness

Workload is not constant. Take-off and landing are high-workload; cruise is low. The HMI must reduce peak workload and keep the crew monitoring in low workload. Error and fatigue make this harder: people skip checks, misread similar values, and trust a silent automation. Design uses checklists, standard callouts, and clear failure messages.

Automation mode awareness means the crew always knows what the automation is doing and what it will do next. The flight mode annunciator (FMA) shows active and armed modes. A silent mode change is a design fault. Mode confusion — the crew thinks the aircraft is in one mode but it is in another — is a known accident factor.

One design checklist:

  • Is the most urgent information nearest the eyes?
  • Does every alert name the fault and the action?
  • Is colour backed by text or shape?
  • Do controls show their state?
  • Is the automation mode always visible?
  • Can the crew recover from a mistake without a hidden mode?

Worked example

Question. A pilot scans 6 displays with 0.4 s dwell per display. Find one full scan time. If a warning must be seen within 2 s, is one full scan enough?

  1. Scan time = number of displays × dwell per display = 6 × 0.4 s = 2.4 s.
  2. Warning reaction budget = 2 s.
  3. Compare: 2.4 s > 2 s, so one full scan is not enough.
  4. Therefore the warning needs a separate salient channel: master warning light and aural alert.

Answer. One full scan takes 2.4 s; it is not enough for a 2 s warning budget, so the warning must be separate and salient.

Exam traps

  • More information on screen is not better HMI; clutter raises scan time and hides faults.
  • Red, amber and green are not decoration; using red for normal values destroys warning meaning.
  • A master caution for every minor fault causes alarm fatigue; crews ignore real alerts.
  • Mode confusion is not only pilot error; a silent or hidden mode change is a design failure.

4.7 Data buses: ARINC 429 and MIL-STD-1553B

A data bus is a shared set of wires and rules that carries digital words between avionics units. It replaces bundles of point-to-point wires with a defined word format, timing and error check. ARINC 429 is the civil one-way bus; MIL-STD-1553B is the military command/response bus. Both are still found in aircraft and helicopters, often beside newer Ethernet. This chapter shows how each word is built, how fast it moves, and why direction and topology change the design.

You need: Chapter 4.2, LRUs and IMA, for what an LRU is and why units exchange data.

ARINC 429: one-way civil bus

ARINC 429 is a one-way serial bus used on civil aircraft. One transmitter drives a twisted pair, and up to 20 receivers listen. The pair carries a balanced differential signal: one wire goes positive while the other goes negative, and the receiver reads the difference. This rejects common-mode noise from nearby cables and motors.

Every transmission is a 32-bit word. Bits 1–8 are the label, an octal code that names the parameter. Bits 9–10 are the SDI, the source/destination identifier, used when two similar units send the same label. Bits 11–29 carry the data. Bits 30–31 are the SSM, the sign/status/matrix, which says whether the data is valid, No Computed Data, a test value, or a sign. Bit 32 is odd parity over the other 31 bits.

T_word = n / R

T_word is one word time in seconds; n is the word length in bits (32 for ARINC 429); R is the bit rate in bits per second.

ARINC 429 has two standard speeds: high speed at 100 kbps and low speed at 12.5 kbps. The bus is simple, deterministic and well understood. Its weakness is wiring: each direction needs its own pair, because a 429 pair has only one transmitter. One pair can carry many parameters as a stream of labelled words; a dedicated transmitter is a design choice made when a parameter needs a high, independent update rate.

MIL-STD-1553B: command/response bus

MIL-STD-1553B is a bidirectional serial bus used on military aircraft, helicopters and spacecraft. It runs at 1 Mbps and uses Manchester II encoding: each bit is a low-to-high or high-to-low transition in the middle of the bit period. The transition carries the clock, so no separate clock wire is needed.

A 1553B word is 20 bits: 3 sync bits, 16 information bits and 1 parity bit. There are three word types. A command word comes from the bus controller and gives the remote terminal address, transmit/receive bit, subaddress and word count. A data word carries 16 data bits. A status word comes back from the remote terminal and reports address, message error, busy, subsystem flag and terminal flag.

One bus controller controls all traffic; up to 31 remote terminals answer. The controller sends a command, the addressed terminal replies with status, data moves, and a final status may confirm. 1553B is usually dual redundant: two independent buses, A and B, so one cable or transceiver failure does not stop the exchange. The controller decides which bus is active.

Choosing between them

ARINC 429 suits civil systems that send many separate parameters from one unit to many listeners: air data computers to displays, inertial reference units to flight management, radio altimeters to warning computers. It is one-way, so a reply needs a second 429 channel. 1553B suits military systems that need many units on one shared bus and need confirmation that a command was received. It is command/response, not a free-running broadcast.

Worked example

Question. An ARINC 429 transmitter runs at 100 kbps and sends a 32-bit word. A MIL-STD-1553B bus runs at 1 Mbps and sends a 20-bit word. Find each word time. For a 1553B transmit message (remote terminal to controller) with one command, one data and one status word, find total time ignoring gaps.

  1. ARINC 429: R1 = 100,000 bit/s and n1 = 32 bits. T1 = n1 / R1 = 32 / 100,000 = 0.00032 s = 0.32 ms.
  2. MIL-STD-1553B: R2 = 1,000,000 bit/s and n2 = 20 bits. T2 = n2 / R2 = 20 / 1,000,000 = 0.00002 s = 20 µs.
  3. A transmit message is command + status + data words, so count = 1 command + 1 status + 1 data = 3 words. Tmsg = 3 × 20 µs = 60 µs.
  4. A receive message would be command + status + data + final status, so the same one data word would give 4 words = 80 µs.

4.8 AFDX and fibre-optic communication

Older buses such as ARINC 429 are slow one-way links. A modern aircraft needs dozens of computers to exchange large blocks of data with a guaranteed delay. AFDX — Avionics Full-Duplex Switched Ethernet, standardised as ARINC 664 part 7 — meets this with 100 Mbit/s switched Ethernet plus virtual links that reserve bandwidth. Fibre-optic cable carries data as light, giving high bandwidth, low weight and no pickup of electrical interference. Both appear in airliner avionics bays and in fly-by-light research.

You need: Chapter 4.7 for how a data bus defines words, timing and error checks.

Why a switched network replaced bundles of wires

ARINC 429 is a one-way link: one transmitter, up to twenty receivers, 100 kbit/s. An airliner has dozens of computers that must exchange large blocks of data. Wiring each pair separately needs hundreds of cables and adds weight. AFDX replaces the bundle with a switched network.

AFDX means Avionics Full-Duplex Switched Ethernet; the standard is ARINC 664 part 7. Every link runs at 100 Mbit/s in both directions at once. Two kinds of equipment sit on the network. An end system is the AFDX interface inside one avionics computer: it builds frames for its host, sends them, and delivers frames addressed to it. A switch reads the destination of each frame and forwards it only to the port that leads to the receiver.

Each link is full duplex and point-to-point, so there are no collisions. A port never hears another port's traffic while it transmits. Heavy traffic between two computers does not slow a third pair, provided the reserved bandwidths fit on each link. The price is that the whole traffic pattern must be defined before flight and loaded as a configuration table.

The same ARINC 664 part 7 network is used on large Airbus and Boeing types to carry flight control, display and maintenance data. Office Ethernet cannot promise a delay; AFDX can, and that is why aviation adopted it.

Virtual links, BAG and redundancy management

A virtual link (VL) is a one-way logical path from one source end system to one or more destination end systems. A 16-bit VL ID in each frame identifies it. Frames of many VLs share the same physical switches, but each VL has capacity reserved for it in advance.

Two numbers define a VL. BAG, the bandwidth allocation gap, is the minimum time between the first bits of two consecutive frames of that VL. Allowed values are powers of two from 1 ms to 128 ms. Lmax is the largest frame, in bytes, that the VL may send. From these two numbers the reserved bandwidth follows.

reserved bandwidth = 8 × Lmax ÷ BAG

Lmax is the maximum frame size of the VL in bytes; the 8 converts bytes to bits; BAG is the bandwidth allocation gap in seconds; the result is in bit/s. The source paces itself to send at most one frame of size Lmax per BAG.

Switches reserve that share on every link the VL crosses, so the worst-case delay of a frame can be computed before the aircraft flies. Certification needs exactly that: a bound, not an average.

Redundancy management: each end system normally connects to two independent networks, A and B, with separate switches and wiring. Every frame is sent on both. Each frame carries a sequence number from 0 to 255 that counts per VL. The receiver checks the Ethernet CRC and the sequence number, keeps the first valid copy, and discards the duplicate or any out-of-order frame. One failed switch or one cut cable costs nothing.

Fibre-optic communication

A fibre-optic link turns electrical data into light, carries the light in glass, and turns it back. Three parts: a light source driven by the transmitter circuit, the fibre, and a detector that converts light back into current.

The source is an LED or a laser diode. The fibre is a glass core inside a cladding of lower refractive index. Light in the core that strikes the core–cladding boundary at an angle greater than the critical angle undergoes total internal reflection: it reflects back into the core instead of escaping, so it stays guided along the fibre. The detector is a PIN photodiode, or an avalanche photodiode where more sensitivity is needed.

Two kinds of fibre. Multimode fibre has a wide core, about 50 or 62.5 µm, and carries many light paths; it is cheap and suits short runs inside the aircraft, commonly at 850 nm. Single-mode fibre has a core near 9 µm and one path; with a laser at 1300 or 1550 nm it reaches far with low loss.

  • Advantage — bandwidth: Gbit/s on one strand.
  • Advantage — weight: one fibre replaces a bundle of copper cables, and weight costs fuel.
  • Advantage — immunity to EMI: the signal is light and the fibre is dielectric, so there is no conduction, no ground loop and no spark risk near a fuel tank.
  • Disadvantage — connectors must be polished and cleaned; a dirty connector loses more signal than a kilometre of fibre.
  • Disadvantage — tight bends leak light, so a minimum bend radius is specified.
  • Disadvantage — connectors, splices and test sets cost more, and a fibre cannot deliver power to a remote sensor.

Aircraft that signal flight control surfaces over fibre are called fly-by-light; see Flight Control Systems.

Worked example

Question. A camera sends images over one AFDX virtual link. Its configuration table entry gives BAG = 2 ms and Lmax = 1000 bytes. (a) Find the reserved bandwidth in bit/s and in Mbit/s. (b) Find the maximum number of frames per second the link may carry.

  1. Convert the frame size to bits: 1000 bytes × 8 = 8000 bits.
  2. Convert the BAG to seconds: 2 ms ÷ 1000 = 0.002 s.
  3. Reserved bandwidth = 8 × Lmax ÷ BAG = 8000 bits ÷ 0.002 s = 4 000 000 bit/s.
  4. In Mbit/s: 4 000 000 ÷ 1 000 000 = 4 Mbit/s.
  5. Frame rate limit = 1 ÷ BAG = 1 ÷ 0.002 s = 500 frames per second.
  6. Check: 500 frames/s × 8000 bits = 4 000 000 bit/s, which matches step 3.

Answer. The virtual link reserves 4 000 000 bit/s, that is 4 Mbit/s, and may carry at most 500 frames per second.

Exam traps

  • AFDX is not office Ethernet: no CSMA/CD, no random access. Every link is full duplex and switched, and the traffic pattern is fixed in a configuration table before flight.
  • Unit slips in 8 × Lmax ÷ BAG: Lmax is in bytes, so multiply by 8; BAG is listed in milliseconds, so divide by 1000. Writing BAG as 2 instead of 0.002 s makes the answer 1000 times too small (4000 bit/s instead of 4 000 000 bit/s).
  • BAG is the minimum spacing between two frames of one VL. It is not the frame transmission time and not the maximum end-to-end latency.
  • Fibre removes electrical interference, not the need for redundancy. Switches, connectors and fibres still fail, so networks A and B both stay.
  • Lmax is a maximum, not an average. Reserved bandwidth must cover the worst case, so always use Lmax.

Check yourself

Remember What does the bandwidth allocation gap (BAG) of a virtual link define, and what values may it take? 2 marks

The minimum time between the first bits of two consecutive frames of that virtual link. Allowed values are powers of two from 1 ms to 128 ms.

Remember Name the light source, the medium and the detector in a fibre-optic link. 2 marks

Source: LED or laser diode. Medium: glass fibre with core and cladding. Detector: PIN photodiode or avalanche photodiode.

Understand Why does an AFDX network have no collisions, when older shared Ethernet did? 3 marks

Every link is a full-duplex point-to-point connection into a switch. A station transmits on one pair and receives on another, and the switch buffers and forwards frames, so two stations never share one transmission medium.

Understand AFDX uses ordinary Ethernet frames. Why can its delay still be guaranteed? 3 marks

Each VL has a fixed Lmax and BAG in a configuration table, and switches reserve that rate on every link it crosses. Worst-case queueing delay is therefore computable before flight, unlike best-effort office Ethernet.

Apply A virtual link has BAG = 8 ms and Lmax = 1500 bytes. Find its reserved bandwidth. 4 marks

1500 bytes × 8 = 12 000 bits. BAG = 0.008 s. 12 000 ÷ 0.008 = 1 500 000 bit/s = 1.5 Mbit/s.

Apply How long does one 1500-byte frame occupy a 100 Mbit/s AFDX link? 4 marks

1500 × 8 = 12 000 bits. 12 000 ÷ 100 000 000 = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ s = 120 µs. 120 µs is for the payload bits only; headers, preamble and interframe gap add a few more microseconds.

Analyze An engineer argues: 'We use fibre everywhere, so the second AFDX network can be removed.' Give two reasons why this is wrong. 5 marks

Fibre removes electrical interference and ground loops, but it does not stop broken fibres, dirty or loose connectors, failed switches or switch power loss; redundancy covers those failures, not only EMI. Also, certification of a single-channel design would need a quantified failure rate showing the loss of the network is extremely improbable, which a single path cannot demonstrate.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Deterministic Ethernet has spread well past aviation: the ideas inside ARINC 664 part 7 reappear in IEEE 802.1 time-sensitive networking, now used in cars and factory control. Researchers work on gigabit AFDX and on optical backbones with optical switches inside the aircraft. Fly-by-light, where even flight control signalling is optical, has flown in demonstrators but not in service airliners. Search next for 'ARINC 664 part 7', 'AFDX virtual link BAG', 'time-sensitive networking' and 'fly-by-light'.

4.9 Avionics software: RTCA DO-178B and V-model

Every line of code in a flight computer is a safety decision. A wrong altitude on a display, a missed spoiler command, a badly tuned fly-by-wire gain — each can end a flight. RTCA DO-178B is the industry guide that makes a software team prove its work: that requirements were written, that code matches them, and that tests show the code does what the requirements say. Regulators accept it as one acceptable way to show airborne software is safe, so most civil avionics suppliers follow it closely.

You need Chapter 4.2 on IMA and partitioning, plus some familiarity with software testing. Only a few universities teach this topic.

The V-model: decompose down, verify up

RTCA DO-178B, Software Considerations in Airborne Systems and Equipment Certification (1992), is industry guidance, not law. Regulators accept it as one acceptable means of showing that airborne software is safe; its European twin is EUROCAE ED-12B. It describes a life cycle shaped like a V.

The left arm decomposes. Aircraft requirements become high-level requirements (HLR): what the software must do, with no design. HLRs become low-level requirements (LLR): algorithms, data flows, logic. LLRs become source code.

The right arm verifies. Unit tests check code against LLRs. Integration tests check that modules work together. Validation checks the finished software against the aircraft requirements. Reviews and analyses count as verification too; DO-178B never says test only.

Traceability holds the V together, in both directions. Every HLR traces down to LLRs and to at least one test. Every LLR traces down to source code and to at least one test. A requirement with no test is a gap. Every test of a required behaviour must trace to a requirement; robustness tests of abnormal inputs are expected and are not traced to requirements. Auditors read the trace first.

Software levels A to E

The software level comes from the failure condition of the function, decided in the system safety assessment — not from the size or difficulty of the code.

LevelFailure conditionEffect on the aircraft
ACatastrophicPrevents continued safe flight and landing
BHazardousLarge reduction in safety margins
CMajorSignificant reduction in safety margins
DMinorSlight reduction in safety margins
ENo safety effectNone

Objectives scale with the level: level A carries the full set, level D only a small subset, level E none. At level A the structural coverage objective demands MC/DC (modified condition/decision coverage): every condition inside a decision must be shown to change that decision on its own, so a condition hidden by short-circuit evaluation is caught.

Plans, standards, configuration management and quality assurance records are objectives in their own right: who wrote the code, which version flew, who checked it.

Worked example

Question. A spoiler control function is classified catastrophic, so its software is level A. Its requirements database holds 40 low-level requirements. A traceability review finds tests traced to 38 of them. (a) Find the requirement-to-test traceability percentage. (b) State how many gaps remain. (c) State whether the certification evidence is complete.

  1. Level: catastrophic failure condition gives software level A.
  2. Total low-level requirements = 40; traced to tests = 38.
  3. Traceability = 38 ÷ 40 × 100 = 0.95 × 100 = 95%.
  4. Gaps = 40 − 38 = 2 requirements with no traced test.
  5. 95% is below 100%, so the evidence is incomplete; each gap must be closed by a traced test, or the requirement removed with justification, before certification.

Answer. Traceability is 95%, with 2 requirements untraced, so the evidence is incomplete and the gaps must be closed.

Exam traps

  • DO-178B prescribes no language, compiler or tool; it asks for evidence that objectives are met. A new compiler is allowed if you can show it does no harm.
  • Level follows the failure condition of the function, not the complexity, size or cost of the code.
  • Verification asks 'did we build it right' (code against requirements); validation asks 'did we build the right thing' (software against aircraft requirements).
  • Traceability is bidirectional. Answers that only trace requirement to test lose marks.
  • DO-178B is guidance accepted by regulators, not law; do not write that it is a legal requirement.

Upward trace proves every line of code exists for a stated requirement; downward trace proves every requirement has code and a test, so nothing is missing and nothing is extra.

Nothing is banned. The team must meet the level A objectives for the code, including structural coverage to MC/DC, and supply evidence that the generator and tools do not insert errors.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. DO-178C replaced DO-178B in 2011 and adds supplements: DO-330 for tool qualification, DO-331 for model-based development, DO-332 for object-oriented software and DO-333 for formal methods. 4.10 Aircraft transducers: solenoids, pressure, temperature, strain, rotary position and accelerometers

A transducer converts a physical quantity into an electrical signal, or an electrical signal into motion. A sensor measures; a solenoid acts. Aircraft use transducers for engine control, air data, fuel quantity, flight controls and health monitoring. The electrical form matters because it can be wired, multiplexed and checked. This chapter covers solenoids, pressure, temperature, strain, rotary position and acceleration, with the common signal forms and typical aircraft uses.

You need: basic electrical quantities and the signal-flow view from 4.2; the detailed conditioning circuits come in 4.11.

From physical quantity to signal

An aircraft cannot read pressure, heat or position directly. A transducer converts one physical quantity, the measurand, into an electrical signal; the reverse direction is actuation. A sensor is a transducer used for measurement. A solenoid is a transducer used for action. Active transducers generate a signal from the physical effect, such as a thermocouple. Passive transducers change resistance or capacitance and need excitation. Output may be voltage, current, resistance, frequency or a switch state. Avionics uses transducers in engine control, air data, fuel quantity, flight controls and health monitoring. Electrical output is easy to wire, multiplex and check. Signal conditioning and conversion follow in 4.11.

Solenoids and pressure

A solenoid is a coil with a movable iron plunger. Current in the coil creates a magnetic field and pulls the plunger against a spring. An on/off solenoid gives two states: energised or not. It opens a fuel shut-off valve or selects a hydraulic line. A proportional solenoid gives force roughly proportional to coil current; it trims a pressure valve. It is an actuator, not a measuring sensor.

Pressure is force per area, P = F/A, in pascal (Pa); 1 bar = 100000 Pa. A Bourdon tube is a curved flattened tube that straightens as pressure rises and moves a linkage. A diaphragm is a flexible disc that deflects under pressure. A strain-gauge pressure transducer bonds gauges to a diaphragm; pressure bends the diaphragm, the gauges stretch, and resistance changes. Absolute pressure uses a vacuum reference; gauge pressure uses local ambient. Aircraft use these for pitot-static air data, hydraulic pressure, fuel pressure and cabin pressure.

Temperature and strain

An RTD is a metal resistor, often platinum, whose resistance rises with temperature.

RT = R0[1 + α(T − T0)]

RT is resistance at temperature T in Ω; R0 is resistance at T0; α is the temperature coefficient in 1/°C; T and T0 are in °C.

It is stable and linear over a moderate range, but needs excitation current and can self-heat. A thermocouple joins two dissimilar metals. The hot junction produces a Seebeck emf proportional to the temperature difference between hot and cold junctions. It measures difference, not absolute temperature, unless cold-junction compensation is added. A thermistor is a semiconductor resistor with a large, nonlinear resistance change; it is sensitive but limited in range. Aircraft use thermocouples for exhaust gas temperature, RTDs for oil temperature and thermistors for cabin temperature.

A strain gauge is a thin conductor or foil bonded to a structure. When the structure stretches, the gauge lengthens and its resistance rises.

GF = (ΔR/R) / ε

GF is gauge factor, dimensionless; ΔR is resistance change in Ω; R is unstrained resistance in Ω; ε = ΔL/L is strain, dimensionless.

A bridge circuit converts the small ΔR into a voltage. Strain gauges measure wing bending, landing-gear loads and pressure-diaphragm deflection.

Rotary position and acceleration

A potentiometer uses a wiper on a resistive track. It gives a voltage divider output proportional to angle; simple, but the wiper wears. An LVDT has an AC-excited primary and two secondaries with a movable core. The secondary difference gives amplitude and phase proportional to displacement. An RVDT is the rotary form. A resolver has sine and cosine windings and gives robust angular information; aircraft use resolvers and RVDTs in flight-control and engine actuators.

An accelerometer has a proof mass. Acceleration moves the mass; piezoelectric material gives a charge, or a MEMS capacitor changes. Specify range in g, sensitivity in mV/g and number of axes. Aircraft use accelerometers in inertial systems, flight-data recording and stall or load monitoring. See Unit 5 for inertial navigation.

Worked example

Question. A 350 Ω strain gauge has gauge factor 2 and is strained by 500 µε. Find the fractional resistance change and the change in resistance.

  1. Strain is given in microstrain: ε = 500 µε = 500 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.0005.
  2. Use GF = (ΔR/R)/ε, so ΔR/R = GF × ε = 2 × 0.0005 = 0.001.
  3. The fractional change is 0.001, which is 0.1 %.
  4. ΔR = (ΔR/R) × R = 0.001 × 350 Ω = 0.35 Ω.

Answer. The fractional resistance change is 0.001 and the resistance change is 0.35 Ω.

Exam traps

  • A solenoid is an actuator, not a sensor; a transducer can do both.
  • A thermocouple gives a temperature difference, not absolute temperature, without cold-junction compensation.
  • Microstrain means 10⁻⁶: 500 µε is 0.0005, not 500.
  • LVDT and RVDT outputs are AC and need demodulation; they are not simple DC potentiometers.
  • Pressure readings must state absolute or gauge; the wrong reference shifts every value.

Its emf depends on the difference between hot and cold junction temperatures. To report absolute hot-junction temperature, the cold-junction temperature must be measured and added as compensation.

4.11 Avionics electronics: diodes, transistors, op-amps, signal conditioning, ADC and DAC, microcontrollers

Avionics electronics is the layer between a transducer and a computer. A pressure sensor gives a few millivolts; an ADC needs volts and a clean signal. Diodes protect, transistors switch and amplify, op-amps condition, ADCs digitise, DACs drive actuators, and a microcontroller runs the loop. The same chain appears in an air-data computer, a fuel-quantity unit or a flap controller. This chapter follows one signal from bridge to actuator and gives the numbers an exam asks for.

You need: Chapter 4.10 on transducers and basic electronics (voltage, current, resistance).

Diodes and transistors: protection and control

A diode is a two-terminal one-way valve. Current flows from anode to cathode when the anode is about 0.7 V higher for silicon. Reverse bias blocks current until breakdown. In avionics a diode does three jobs. A rectifier turns AC into DC in a transformer rectifier unit. A flyback diode sits across a relay or solenoid coil; when coil current stops, the stored energy circulates through the diode instead of punching a voltage spike into the transistor. A clamping diode limits an input to the supply rails.

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) has base, collector and emitter. In the active region it amplifies: a small base current controls a larger collector current. The DC current gain is β = IC / IB, where IC is collector current in amperes and IB is base current in amperes. As a switch, the BJT is driven into saturation so VCE is small and power loss is low. A MOSFET is a voltage-controlled switch with gate, drain and source; it carries the high current of a fuel pump, landing-light relay or flap motor, and the microcontroller drives its gate through a resistor.

  • A silicon diode drop of 0.7 V is a loss in low-voltage circuits.
  • A relay coil needs a flyback diode; without it the transistor sees a large inductive kick.
  • A MOSFET gate needs a defined pull-down so it does not switch on when the microcontroller resets.

Op-amps and signal conditioning

An operational amplifier (op-amp) is a high-gain differential amplifier. Ideal rules: no current enters the input terminals, and the op-amp drives its output so the two input voltages are equal when negative feedback is present. These two rules solve most circuits.

Inverting amplifier: input through Rin to the '-' input, feedback Rf from output to '-', '+' grounded. Gain A = -Rf / Rin. Non-inverting: input to '+', feedback divider Rf from output to '-', Rin from '-' to ground. Gain A = 1 + Rf / Rin. A buffer is the non-inverting circuit with Rf replaced by a wire (Rf = 0) and Rin removed, so A = 1 + 0 = 1; it gives a high input impedance and a low output impedance.

A = -Rf / Rin   and   A = 1 + Rf / Rin

A is voltage gain (no unit); Rf is feedback resistance in ohms; Rin is input resistance in ohms.

A bridge sensor (strain gauge, pressure capsule) gives a differential voltage of a few millivolts on top of a common-mode voltage. A differential amplifier amplifies the difference and rejects the common part. The circuit has two input resistors R1 and R3 and two feedback resistors R2 and R4, with R1 = R3 = Rin and R2 = R4 = Rf; then gain = Rf / Rin. All four equal is the special case gain = 1. In practice a resistor mismatch reduces common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR). An instrumentation amplifier uses three op-amps to give high input impedance and a set gain.

Signal conditioning means making a sensor signal fit for the ADC. Amplification raises millivolts to volts. Filtering removes 50 Hz mains pickup, vibration noise and aliasing before sampling. Isolation (optocoupler or transformer) stops a ground loop or a high common-mode voltage from damaging the computer. Linearisation corrects a thermocouple or pressure sensor curve with a polynomial or lookup table.

ADC, DAC and the microcontroller

An analog-to-digital converter (ADC) turns a voltage into a code. For an N-bit ADC with reference Vref, the resolution (smallest step) is Q = Vref / 2N, where Q is in volts, Vref is the full-scale reference in volts and N is the number of bits. The output code is floor(Vin / Q), where Vin is the input voltage in volts. The code saturates at 2N − 1; inputs at or above Vref clip to full scale. With this floor rule the quantisation error is between 0 and −Q, that is, up to one step low. If the converter rounds to the nearest step, the error is up to ±Q/2. State which convention a question uses. Sampling must be at least twice the highest signal frequency (Nyquist); an anti-alias filter before the ADC enforces this.

Q = Vref / 2N   and   code = floor(Vin / Q)

Q is resolution in volts; Vref is reference voltage in volts; N is ADC bits; Vin is input voltage in volts; floor rounds down to an integer code.

A digital-to-analog converter (DAC) does the reverse: code k gives Vout = k Q. It drives a valve, a servo amplifier or a lamp dimmer. The DAC output needs a buffer to supply current; a bare DAC pin cannot drive a solenoid.

A microcontroller is a single chip with a CPU, flash program memory, RAM, digital input/output pins, timers and serial ports (UART, SPI, I²C, CAN; see Chapter 4.7). In avionics it runs a periodic loop: read the ADC, filter and scale the value, compare with a limit, update outputs, send a status word on the bus. Timers generate the sample clock and pulse-width modulation (PWM) for actuator drive. Watchdog timers reset the chip if the loop hangs. The chain is: sensor → bridge → amplifier → filter → ADC → microcontroller → DAC → actuator.

Worked example

Question. An 8-bit ADC has a reference voltage of 5.000 V. (a) Find the resolution in volts and millivolts. (b) Find the output code for an input of 1.000 V.

  1. N = 8 bits, Vref = 5.000 V. Number of codes = 28 = 256.
  2. Q = Vref / 2N = 5.000 V / 256 = 0.01953125 V.
  3. In millivolts: 0.01953125 V × 1000 mV/V = 19.53125 mV ≈ 19.53 mV.
  4. Code = floor(Vin / Q) = floor(1.000 V / 0.01953125 V) = floor(51.2) = 51.
  5. Check: code 51 represents 51 × 0.01953125 V = 0.99609375 V, below 1.000 V; code 52 gives 1.015625 V, above. So 51 is correct.

Answer. Resolution is 0.01953125 V (19.53 mV) and the code for 1.000 V is 51.

Exam traps

  • An 8-bit ADC has 256 codes but 255 steps between them. For an ideal binary ADC with code 0 at 0 V, use Q = Vref / 2N, not Vref / 255.
  • Inverting op-amp gain has a minus sign; non-inverting gain has a plus one. Writing A = Rf/Rin for the non-inverting circuit is a common slip.
  • A flyback diode must be reverse-biased in normal operation: cathode to the positive supply. Reversing it shorts the supply when the coil is energised.
  • A BJT used as a switch must be driven into saturation. A small base current leaves it in the active region, where it dissipates heat and may fail.
  • A differential amplifier rejects common-mode voltage only as well as its resistor matching allows. Gain formulas ignore CMRR; exam questions often add a common-mode term.

Check yourself

Remember What is the function of a flyback diode across a relay coil? 2 marks

It gives the coil current a path when the driving transistor turns off, so the inductive voltage spike is clamped and the transistor is protected.

A buffer has very high input impedance, so it does not load the bridge, and low output impedance, so it can drive the ADC input and any filter without loss.

Analyze A non-inverting op-amp has Rf = 9 kΩ and Rin = 1 kΩ. Find the gain and the output for a 0.2 V input. If Rf drifts to 10 kΩ, what changes? 4 marks

CPU, program memory (flash), RAM, digital input/output pins, timers and serial ports; any four.

Sampling at frequency fs cannot represent signals above fs/2. Higher frequencies fold back as false low frequencies. The anti-alias filter removes them before sampling.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Radar and flight-control computers use radiation-hard or radiation-tolerant processes, and FPGAs implement many high-speed ADC interfaces. Takeaways

  1. The platform drives the avionics: airliners buy safety and schedule regularity, fighters buy mission capability, spacecraft buy autonomy, UAVs buy low size, weight and power.
  2. Federated architecture is simple to certify but heavy; IMA saves weight and space by sharing modules and separating functions with partitions.
  3. The glass cockpit did not remove information, it regrouped it, so the PFD carries control data and the MFD carries navigation and systems data.
  4. HUD and HMD keep symbols in the outside view so the pilot does not lose sight of the runway or the target while reading numbers.

  6. AFDX turns Ethernet into a deterministic bus by policing each virtual link's BAG and Lmax at the switch.
  7. DO-178B ties the amount of software verification to the consequence of failure, and the V-model makes each requirement traceable to a test.
  8. Transducers and the signal chain in front of the ADC decide measurement accuracy as much as the computer does, so bridge excitation, filtering and resolution all matter.

Understand Describe the standard content of the primary flight display and the multi-function display on a modern airliner, and explain why the information is split that way. 13 marks

PFD: attitude with pitch ladder and bank scale, airspeed tape, altitude tape, vertical speed, heading, flight path vector, autopilot and flight director modes; PFD: localiser and glide slope scales for approach, radio height; MFD: navigation map with waypoints and weather, engine and fuel pages, system synoptics, checklists; Reason for the split: control information always in the same place for scan; reference and systems information on demand; Colour and shape coding conventions and the use of the attitude as the central reference.

Understand Explain the ARINC 429 word format and transmission scheme. State the two bit rates and how a receiver knows a word is valid. 13 marks

One-way simplex transmission, one transmitter to up to many receivers, twisted shielded pair; 32-bit word: label, source/destination identifier, data field, sign/status matrix, parity; Odd parity bit and the parity check at the receiver; Bit rates 12.5 kbit/s and 100 kbit/s, self-clocking with a tri-state return-to-zero waveform; Gap between words and how a receiver times out on a silent label; Limitation: no acknowledgement, so redundancy and multiple labels are used for integrity.

Understand Describe the message formats and operation of MIL-STD-1553B, including the roles of bus controller, remote terminal and bus monitor. 13 marks

1 Mbit/s, Manchester II encoded, bidirectional shielded twisted pair, transformer coupled stubs; Word types: command word, data word, status word, each 20 bits with parity; Message types: controller to terminal, terminal to controller, terminal to terminal, mode code; Bus controller owns the schedule; remote terminals reply within a fixed response time; bus monitor listens only; Terminal address field, subaddress, word count, and the status word bits for fault reporting; Why command/response suits military systems: deterministic timing and central fault detection.

Apply

Apply An aircraft is being retrofitted from ARINC 429 to AFDX for a new display system. Using BAG and Lmax, explain how you would size the virtual links and what guarantee the switch gives. 13 marks

Virtual link defined by source, one or more destinations, a BAG (1 to 128 ms, powers of 2) and a maximum frame size Lmax; Calculate the worst-case bandwidth of a link as Lmax bytes plus protocol overhead divided by BAG; Sum of virtual link bandwidths on a port must stay within the link capacity with a margin; Switch uses a traffic policing and scheduling algorithm so each frame arrives within a bounded latency; Consequence: deterministic delivery for flight-critical data even though the medium is Ethernet; Comparison with the 429 one-way label scheme and the wiring reduction gained.

Apply A pressure sensor gives a 0 to 50 mV differential output with a 2 kΩ source impedance. Explain the signal chain to produce a clean digital reading in the avionics computer. 13 marks

Instrumentation amplifier for high input impedance, high common-mode rejection and fixed gain; Bridge or sensor excitation and the effect of excitation drift on the result; Filtering: anti-alias low-pass filter before sampling; ADC resolution and reference; sample rate above twice the highest signal frequency; Quantisation error and the effect of noise on the least significant bit; Conversion to engineering units with calibration coefficients held in non-volatile memory; BITE limits and a validity flag sent with the value.

Apply Explain how a head-up display produces a collimated image that stays aligned with the runway, and why alignment accuracy is critical. 13 marks

Symbols generated by a CRT or LCD, magnified and collimated by a lens group so rays leave parallel; Combiner glass reflects the symbols while letting the outside view through; Focus at infinity means the pilot needs no refocusing between symbols and the scene; Alignment to the aircraft reference system through attitude and heading inputs and boresighting; Conformal symbology: flight path vector and runway box drawn where they are in the real world; Error sources: mounting, latency of the attitude data, and the consequence of parallax or misalignment.

Apply Map a flight-control computer software development onto the V-model for a function whose failure is catastrophic, and list the objectives that this level adds. 16 marks

Left side: system requirements, high-level software requirements, low-level requirements, design, code; Right side: unit tests against low-level requirements, integration tests against high-level requirements, system tests against system requirements; Bidirectional traceability between every requirement and its test, plus a verification review; Level A adds independence, structural coverage of every statement and decision, and independence between developer and verifier; Configuration index and change control for every artefact; Achieving no unintended function, robustness under abnormal inputs.

Analyze and evaluate

Evaluate Compare ARINC 429, MIL-STD-1553B and AFDX as backbone buses for a transport aircraft, and justify a choice for flight-critical data against passenger-cabin data. 16 marks

429: simplex, low speed, simple and cheap, no acknowledgement, but a wiring explosion with many labels; 1553B: 1 Mbit/s, command/response, deterministic, strong fault detection, but military heritage and a single point of schedule control; AFDX: switched full-duplex Ethernet, virtual links with BAG and Lmax, redundancy management, higher bandwidth; Compare bandwidth, determinism, weight, cost, fault detection, certification evidence; Choice: AFDX for flight-critical with duplicated networks, a separate simpler bus for cabin and utility systems; Role of network redundancy and integrity checking in the final argument.

Evaluate Evaluate the human-factors advantages and risks of the glass cockpit compared with the analog cockpit, and show how cockpit design compensates for the risks. 13 marks

Advantages: fewer instruments, integrated data, reconfigurable pages, fewer mechanical failures, automatic alerting; Risks: loss of a display concentrates information, over-reliance, mode confusion, colour and clutter issues, automation complacency; Compensation: display redundancy, reversionary modes, standby attitude airspeed and altitude instruments; Standard layout so the scan pattern is fixed; consistent colour conventions; aural plus visual warnings; Alert ranking and the caution-warning-advisory hierarchy; Design review against human limits: workload, reaction time, legibility, glare, reach.

Analyze An engine indication system must measure turbine temperature, shaft speed, oil pressure and engine vibration. Analyse the transducer choice for each and the signal-conditioning problems involved. 13 marks

Temperature: thermocouple with cold-junction compensation or a resistive sensor; small signal needs amplification; Speed: magnetic pickup or variable-reluctance sensor giving frequency, or a tachometer generator giving voltage; Pressure: strain gauge or piezoresistive sensor in a bridge, needing excitation and bridge balancing; Vibration: accelerometer, charge or voltage output, wide band, needs anti-alias filtering; Environment: temperature drift, electromagnetic interference, isolation of grounds, sensor excitation stability; Health monitoring: BITE limits, sensor validity and how a failed sensor is declared rather than trusted.

Analyze Assess the fire and electrical interference risks of copper cabling in an aircraft and analyse how fibre-optic links in an AFDX-style network reduce them. 13 marks

Copper: electromagnetic interference pickup and emission, crosstalk, a short circuit can pass current into a fuel zone, lightning and high-intensity radiated field coupling; Fibre: dielectric, so no current path and no antenna effect, immune to interference, no crosstalk between fibres; Total internal reflection in a core with lower-index cladding carries the light; Single-mode small core for long distance, multimode larger core for shorter runs and cheaper connectors; Costs: connector cleanliness, bend radius, repair complexity, source and detector ageing; Point that fibre removes the interference path but not the need for redundancy and arc or fire protection in the power system.

Evaluate A display function is hosted on an IMA module. Evaluate the design choices needed to keep it safe when it shares a module with a lower-criticality function. 16 marks

Assign a software level from the failure consequence, and note that the higher level dominates the platform; Time and memory partitioning by a certified operating system, with a fixed schedule and protected memory; Budgets: processor time, memory, input-output and bus bandwidth per partition; Health monitoring and partition restart; how an overrun is detected and contained; Data integrity on virtual links between partitions, and the use of a separate network for the display; Evidence for certification: partition analysis, timing analysis, fault injection testing; Evaluate the trade: weight and cost saving against the extra verification of the shared platform.

Glossary

Accelerometer
A transducer that produces a signal proportional to acceleration along a defined axis.
ADC
Analog-to-digital converter; a circuit that converts a voltage into a number with a stated resolution and reference.
AFDX
Avionics full-duplex switched Ethernet; a switched network with guaranteed virtual links for aircraft data.
Architecture
The arrangement of computers, sensors, buses and displays in a system and the rules by which they exchange data.
ARINC 429
A simplex digital bus standard using 32-bit words over a twisted pair at 12.5 or 100 kbit/s.
Avionics
The electronic systems fitted to an aircraft or spacecraft for communication, navigation, display, control and monitoring, and the study of those systems.
Backplane
The fixed wiring and connector assembly at the back of a rack that links the modules plugged into it.
BAG
Bandwidth allocation gap; the shortest permitted interval between frames of one virtual link.
Built-in test equipment
Circuits and software inside a unit that test it, report its health and help isolate a fault.
Collimation
Optical processing that makes the rays from an image parallel, so the image appears at infinity.
DAC
Digital-to-analog converter; it converts a number into a voltage or current, often to drive an actuator.
DO-178B
Guidance for airborne software that sets assurance objectives according to the consequence of a software failure.
EADI
Electronic attitude director indicator; the earlier name for the attitude and flight-director part of a PFD.
EHSI
Electronic horizontal situation indicator; the navigation display showing heading, track, course and map.
EICAS
Engine indication and crew alerting system; it shows engine parameters and ranked warning and caution messages.
End system
The AFDX interface inside an avionics unit that puts data into and takes data out of the switched network.
Federated architecture
An architecture in which each function has its own dedicated box and its own wiring to the others.
Glass cockpit
A cockpit in which electronic multi-function displays replace most separate mechanical instruments.
Head-up display
A display that collimates symbols so they appear at infinity and overlays them on the outside view.
Helmet-mounted display
A display mounted on the pilot's helmet that moves with the head and shows mission or flight symbols.
Integrated modular avionics
An architecture in which a few shared computing modules and a common network host many software functions as isolated partitions.
Line replaceable unit
An aircraft box that can be removed and replaced as a whole at the aircraft, without internal adjustment.
Lmax
The maximum payload, in bytes, that a virtual link may place in one frame.
MFD
Multi-function display; a screen that can show navigation, weather, engine and system pages on selection.
MIL-STD-1553B
A military command/response serial bus at 1 Mbit/s with a bus controller, remote terminals and a bus monitor.
Module
A plug-in card or unit inside a rack that provides processing, input-output or power conversion.
Multimode fibre
An optical fibre with a larger core carrying several modes, used for shorter links.
Operational amplifier
A high-gain differential amplifier that uses external feedback to set gain, offset or filtering.
Partition
A protected allocation of processor time, memory and input-output that isolates one hosted function from another.
PFD
Primary flight display; the screen presenting attitude, airspeed, altitude, heading and approach guidance.
Signal conditioning
Amplifying, filtering, isolating and scaling a sensor signal so a converter can read it accurately.
Single-mode fibre
An optical fibre with a core small enough to carry one propagation mode, used for long runs.
Strain gauge
A resistive foil bonded to a structure whose resistance changes with the strain the structure experiences.
Total internal reflection
Complete reflection of light at the core-cladding boundary when the ray strikes above the critical angle.
Transducer
A device that converts one form of energy, such as pressure or movement, into an electrical signal or the reverse.
V-model
A development life cycle in which each design stage is verified by a matching test stage on the other arm.
Virtual link
A defined logical data path in AFDX with a fixed maximum frame size and a fixed minimum frame interval.
