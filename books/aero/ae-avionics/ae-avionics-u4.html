4.1 Civil, military, spacecraft and UAV avionics

Avionics means aviation electronics: every electronic system on an aircraft or spacecraft that senses, computes, communicates, navigates, controls or displays. An airliner, a fighter, a satellite and a small drone all use the same building blocks: sensors, computers, data buses, displays and controls, power and cooling. What changes is the priority. An airliner must be safe and on time, a fighter must win its mission and survive, a spacecraft must look after itself far from any repair, and a UAV must do its job on a few kilograms and a few hundred watts.

How aircraft electronics evolved

Aircraft electronics grew in four steps, each set by the parts available.

  • Valve era (1920s to 1950s): radios used vacuum tubes (valves). Instruments were mechanical or pneumatic, each standing alone.
  • Transistor era (1950s and 1960s): transistors made radios and autopilots smaller, cooler and more reliable; analog computers ran autopilots, and inertial navigation appeared on long-range aircraft.
  • Digital era (1970s and 1980s): microprocessors arrived, and digital data buses replaced bundles of wires: MIL-STD-1553 for military aircraft (first issued in 1973) and ARINC 429 for airliners. Screens began to replace dials in the early 1980s, and the Airbus A320 (1988) brought digital fly-by-wire to airliners.
  • Integrated era (1990s onward): functions began to share computers: the Boeing 777 (1995) used a shared cabinet, and the Airbus A380 (2007) and Boeing 787 (2011) use integrated modular avionics on switched Ethernet.

Military aircraft followed the same path, ending in systems that fuse radar, infrared and radio data into one picture.

One way to organise all of this is by function. Some syllabi call it the organisation framework of avionics:

  • Communication, navigation and surveillance (CNS): radios, navigation receivers, transponders and ADS-B.
  • Flight control and flight management: autopilot, fly-by-wire computers, flight management system.
  • Displays and crew interface: screens, head-up display, controls, warnings.
  • Aircraft systems management: engine, fuel, electrical, hydraulic and maintenance computers.
  • Mission systems (military and special-purpose aircraft): radar, electro-optical sensors, electronic warfare, weapons.

Civil and military avionics

Civil avionics is designed for safety, regularity (on-time departures) and low cost of ownership. Certification is the main driver. Every failure condition is classified by its effect, and the more severe the effect, the less probable it must be. The usual targets for large aeroplanes are: catastrophic not more than 10⁻⁹ per flight hour (called extremely improbable), hazardous not more than 10⁻⁷, and major not more than 10⁻⁵. No single computer is that reliable, so civil designs use two or three independent channels that check each other.

Military avionics is designed for mission success and survivability. Its electronics meet high g, vibration, heat and jamming, and are built to military environmental and interference standards (MIL-STD-810 for environment, MIL-STD-461 for electromagnetic interference). Typical functions are radar, electronic warfare, electro-optical and infrared (EO/IR) sensors and stores management (control of weapons carriage and release). Redundancy here also means graceful degradation: a damaged aircraft keeps flying with fewer functions.

Spacecraft and UAV avionics

Spacecraft avionics is designed for autonomy. A satellite cannot be repaired, and the ground may hear it only during short passes. Radiation can flip a memory bit (a single-event upset) or slowly damage parts, so the on-board computer runs fault detection, isolation and recovery (FDIR): it spots a fault, switches to a spare unit and keeps the craft safe. The main blocks are on-board data handling, telemetry, tracking and command (TT&C) links, attitude and orbit control, and solar-array and battery power.

UAV avionics is designed for size, weight and power (SWaP). A small UAV carries an autopilot, GNSS and inertial sensors, a command-and-control (C2) datalink to the ground control station and a payload link. Every watt and gram comes out of endurance. The datalink can be lost, so the UAV must have a pre-planned lost-link behaviour (loiter, return home or land) and a geofence.

Worked example

Question. A flight-control computer channel has a failure rate λ = 2 × 10⁻⁴ per flight hour. Loss of all channels is catastrophic, so the target is 10⁻⁹ per flight hour. Assume the channels fail independently and treat a one-hour flight. (a) Find the probability that one channel fails. (b) Find the probability that both channels of a duplex system fail. (c) Repeat for a triplex system. (d) Which layout meets the target?

  1. One channel: P₁ = 1 − e−λt with t = 1 h. Since λt = 2 × 10⁻⁴ is small, P₁ ≈ λt = 2 × 10⁻⁴ (the exact value is 1.9998 × 10⁻⁴).
  2. Duplex: the system is lost only if both independent channels fail, so P₂ = P₁² = (2 × 10⁻⁴)² = 4 × 10⁻⁸.
  3. Triplex: P₃ = P₁³ = (2 × 10⁻⁴)³ = 8 × 10⁻¹².
  4. Compare with 10⁻⁹: P₁ = 2 × 10⁻⁴ and P₂ = 4 × 10⁻⁸ are both larger, so they fail the target. P₃ = 8 × 10⁻¹² is smaller, so triplex passes with a factor of 10⁻⁹ ÷ (8 × 10⁻¹²) = 125 to spare.
  5. Caution: this assumes independent failures. A common cause, such as the same software bug in all three channels or one shared power supply, defeats the squaring and cubing. That is why designers add dissimilar hardware or software and separate power.

Worked example 2

Question. A survey UAV has 2.0 kWh of usable battery energy. Propulsion takes 400 W, the autopilot 10 W, the datalink 30 W and the camera payload 60 W. (a) Find the endurance. (b) The designer adds two more autopilot channels of 10 W each for redundancy. Find the new endurance and the minutes lost.

  1. Total power before: 400 + 10 + 30 + 60 = 500 W.
  2. Endurance before: 2000 Wh ÷ 500 W = 4.0 h.
  3. Total power after: 500 + 2 × 10 = 520 W.
  4. Endurance after: 2000 Wh ÷ 520 W = 3.846 h.
  5. Time lost: 4.0 − 3.846 = 0.154 h = 0.154 × 60 = 9.2 minutes, before counting the extra mass of the two boxes.

Answer. Endurance falls from 4.0 h to 3.85 h, a loss of about 9.2 minutes per flight. On a UAV, redundancy is paid for in endurance, which is why UAV designs use fewer channels and careful lost-link behaviour.

Exam traps

  • Calling ADS-B a navigation system. ADS-B Out broadcasts the aircraft's own position and identity for surveillance; the position comes from GNSS.
  • Listing weapons as sensors. Stores are a payload controlled by stores management; radar, EO/IR and SIGINT are the sensing payloads.
  • Squaring a failure probability without saying the channels must be independent. A common-mode fault makes the duplex system no better than one channel.
  • Treating UAV avionics as small airliner avionics. SWaP limits and datalink loss change the whole architecture.

4.2 Avionics system architecture, IMA and LRUs

An avionics architecture is the plan of where computing happens and how data moves between sensors, computers and displays. Older aircraft use a federated architecture: one box per function, each with its own wiring. Newer aircraft use integrated modular avionics (IMA): a few shared cabinets host many functions as protected software partitions. In both, the hardware is packaged as line replaceable units (LRUs) that a technician can swap on the apron, and built-in test tells the technician which one to swap.

You need: Civil, military, spacecraft and UAV avionics for the building blocks and the redundancy targets.

LRUs and the federated architecture

A line replaceable unit (LRU) is a sealed box that can be removed and replaced on the flight line with hand tools and a short functional test, without a hangar visit. Each LRU has a part number, a connector, a mounting tray in the avionics bay and its own self-test. Inside it are shop replaceable units (circuit cards) that only a workshop opens.

In a federated architecture each function owns its own LRU. The air data computer works out airspeed and altitude, the inertial reference unit gives attitude and heading, and the autopilot computer sends commands to the servo actuators that move the control surfaces. Boxes exchange data on dedicated links, usually ARINC 429 on civil aircraft and MIL-STD-1553B on military ones (see Data buses: ARINC 429 and MIL-STD-1553B).

  • Strength: a fault stays inside one box, so it is easy to find and easy to certify.
  • Strength: each supplier designs and qualifies its box alone.
  • Weakness: every new function adds a box with its own power supply, case, cooling and wiring, so mass, power and connector count grow with the number of functions.
  • Weakness: an airline must stock many different spare part numbers.

IMA: shared cabinets, modules and partitions

Integrated modular avionics (IMA) replaces many single-function boxes with a few cabinets or racks. A cabinet holds plug-in modules: core processing modules, input/output modules and power supply modules, joined by a backplane (a board that carries power and data between them). Remote data concentrators near the sensors turn analog signals into digital words, so long analog wire runs disappear. Examples are the shared cabinets of the Boeing 777, the IMA of the Airbus A380 and A350, and the Common Core System of the Boeing 787.

Each hosted function runs in a partition. ARINC 653 is the software standard that defines it. A partition gets a fixed window of processor time in a repeating major frame, and its own memory area protected by hardware. One function therefore cannot overwrite another function's data or steal its time, even if it crashes. This is called robust partitioning, and it is what allows a less critical function to share a processor with a critical one.

U = (Σ tᵢ) ÷ TMF × 100 %

U is the processor utilisation in per cent; tᵢ is the time window of partition i in ms; TMF is the major frame in ms. Each partition runs once per major frame, so its rate is 1 ÷ TMF.

Redundancy does not go away with IMA. One module may now carry several functions, so one module fault is a common-mode fault. Designers place the two copies of a critical function on modules in different cabinets with separate power, and keep standby modules.

BITE, maintenance and the trade-off

Built-in test equipment (BITE) is the circuitry and software inside each LRU or module that checks it. Power-up BITE runs when power is applied, continuous BITE runs in flight, and initiated BITE runs on command from a maintenance terminal. Fault codes are stored in non-volatile memory and sent to a central maintenance computer, which can also downlink them before landing, so a technician can meet the aircraft with the right spare.

Worked example

Question. An aircraft carries 8 federated LRUs, each of mass 2 kg and drawing 40 W. An IMA cabinet that hosts all eight functions has a total mass of 5 kg and draws 90 W. Find the mass and power saved, and express each as a percentage of the federated value.

  1. Federated mass: 8 × 2 kg = 16 kg. Federated power: 8 × 40 W = 320 W.
  2. Mass saved: Δm = 16 − 5 = 11 kg.
  3. Power saved: ΔP = 320 − 90 = 230 W.
  4. Mass saving: 11 ÷ 16 × 100 = 68.75 %.
  5. Power saving: 230 ÷ 320 × 100 = 71.875 %, about 71.9 %.

Answer. The cabinet saves 11 kg (68.75 %) and 230 W (about 71.9 %). Less power also means less heat for the avionics cooling system to remove.

Worked example 2

Question. An IMA core module runs ARINC 653 partitions in a 50 ms major frame: flight management 20 ms, display management 12 ms, fuel quantity 8 ms and maintenance 5 ms. (a) Find the utilisation and the spare time. (b) At what rate does each partition run? (c) Can a new 8 ms cabin function be added to this module?

  1. Sum of windows: 20 + 12 + 8 + 5 = 45 ms.
  2. Utilisation: U = 45 ÷ 50 × 100 = 90 %. Spare time: 50 − 45 = 5 ms.
  3. Each partition runs once per major frame: rate = 1 ÷ 0.050 s = 20 Hz.
  4. New function: it needs 8 ms but only 5 ms is free, and 45 + 8 = 53 ms is more than 50 ms. It does not fit.
  5. Options: host it on another module, or shorten another window only if that function's own timing analysis still passes.

Exam traps

  • Writing that IMA removes redundancy. Shared hardware creates common-mode faults, so critical functions are split across modules and cabinets.
  • Calling a circuit card an LRU. The LRU is the box swapped on the apron; cards inside it are shop replaceable units.
  • Confusing ARINC 653 (partitioning in software) with ARINC 429 or ARINC 664 (data on a wire).
  • Adding partition windows and forgetting that the total must fit inside one major frame.
  • Saying BITE proves the system healthy. It tests its own box; wiring and neighbouring units can still be at fault.

4.3 Analog and glass cockpits

An analog cockpit gives every quantity its own dial, driven by its own sensor. A glass cockpit draws the same quantities on a few screens, from computers that read the sensors over data buses. Glass saves panel space, weight and crew workload and can show pictures no dial can, such as a moving map. In exchange it depends on electrical power, cooling and software, so a glass cockpit is designed around what happens when a screen, a computer or the power fails.

You need: Avionics system architecture, IMA and LRUs for how sensors and computers are connected.

The analog cockpit: one dial per job

The flight instruments of an analog cockpit are the basic six, set out in the basic T: the attitude indicator at the top centre, the airspeed indicator to its left, the altimeter to its right, and the heading indicator below the attitude indicator. The turn coordinator and the vertical speed indicator fill the two lower corners. Three are pitot-static instruments (airspeed, altitude, vertical speed) and three are gyroscopic (attitude, heading, turn). Engine dials sit beside them: tachometer, exhaust gas temperature, oil pressure and temperature, fuel quantity. How these instruments work is covered in Air data instruments and Engine instruments: tachometer and EGT.

  • Each dial shows one quantity and has its own case, lighting and wiring.
  • A failed dial blanks only its own quantity, and a pneumatic or vacuum dial still works after an electrical failure.
  • The pilot must scan a wide panel and combine the readings in the head.
  • Every new function means another instrument and more panel space.

Examples: the Cessna 172 with its six-pack, the Boeing 727 and the early Boeing 747, where a flight engineer watched a separate panel of system dials.

The glass cockpit: screens, symbol generators and pages

An electronic flight instrument system (EFIS) has three parts: display units (the screens), display computers or symbol generators that turn sensor data into pictures, and control panels on which the crew selects modes and ranges. Early screens were colour cathode-ray tubes (CRTs); modern ones are active-matrix liquid-crystal displays (LCDs), which are lighter, cooler and readable in sunlight. Data arrives on buses from the air data and inertial units, the flight management system and the engine computers.

A page is a screen layout. One screen can show the primary flight page, a navigation map, an engine page or a system synoptic, chosen by the crew or brought up automatically when a fault appears. Because the drawing is software, one screen replaces several dials and the same screens can show weather radar, traffic and terrain.

What glass depends on

A dial needs a sensor and a wire. A glass picture needs a sensor, a bus, a display computer, a screen, electrical power and cooling. Each link can fail, and one failed computer or power bus can blank several screens at once. Glass cockpits answer this in three ways.

  • Reversion: if a screen fails, its page moves to another screen, automatically or by a switch. On many airliners the primary flight page moves to the navigation screen next to it.
  • Standby instruments: a small standby display (or standby dials) with its own sensors and its own battery shows attitude, airspeed and altitude when everything else is lost.
  • Load shedding: on battery power the electrical system switches off everything except the essential screens and sensors, so the battery lasts long enough to land (see Emergency power: ram air turbine and ETOPS).

Software defects and bad data are the other risk: a screen can show a confident-looking wrong value. So each value carries a validity status, and invalid data is replaced by a failure flag instead of being drawn (see Electronic displays).

Worked example

Question. A light aircraft has a glass cockpit and a 24 V, 10 Ah battery of which 80 % can be used. With the alternator failed, the loads are: primary display 60 W, multifunction display 60 W, attitude and air data unit 20 W, standby display 15 W and radios 40 W. (a) Find the usable battery energy. (b) Find the battery time with all loads on. (c) The pilot sheds the multifunction display and the radios. Find the new battery time.

  1. Battery energy: 24 V × 10 Ah = 240 Wh. Usable: 0.80 × 240 = 192 Wh.
  2. All loads: 60 + 60 + 20 + 15 + 40 = 195 W.
  3. Time with all loads: 192 Wh ÷ 195 W = 0.985 h = 0.985 × 60 = 59 minutes.
  4. After shedding: 60 + 20 + 15 = 95 W.
  5. Time after shedding: 192 Wh ÷ 95 W = 2.02 h = 121 minutes.
  6. Check: 95 W × 2.02 h = 192 Wh, the usable energy.

Answer. Usable energy 192 Wh; about 59 minutes with every load on, and about 121 minutes (2.0 h) after shedding the multifunction display and radios. Load shedding doubles the time available to reach an airfield.

Exam traps

  • Writing that a glass cockpit removes sensors. The pitot-static system, gyros or inertial sensors and engine sensors are all still there; only the indicator changed.
  • Calling a hybrid cockpit a failed upgrade. It is a deliberate mix of dials and screens.
  • Forgetting the standby instruments in a glass cockpit answer. Examiners expect them with their own battery.
  • Comparing only normal operation. Compare failure modes: one dial lost versus one screen or one power bus lost.
  • Using 24 V × 10 Ah = 240 W. Volts times ampere-hours gives watt-hours, energy, not power.

4.4 Electronic displays: PFD, MFD, EADI and EHSI

A glass flight deck uses a few standard screen formats. The primary flight display (PFD) carries everything needed to fly the aircraft: attitude, airspeed, altitude, vertical speed and heading. The navigation display or multifunction display (MFD) carries the map, weather and traffic. Engine and system screens, called EICAS on Boeing aircraft and ECAM on Airbus aircraft, show engine values and crew alerts. The older names EADI and EHSI are the first-generation screens whose jobs the PFD and the navigation display took over.

You need: Analog and glass cockpits.

The primary flight display

The PFD sits straight in front of each pilot. Its centre is the attitude picture: blue sky above, brown ground below, a horizon line, a pitch ladder in degrees and a fixed aircraft symbol. Flight director bars on it show the pitch and roll that the selected mode is asking for.

  • Airspeed tape on the left, with the current speed in a box, the selected speed as a bug and the speed limits as coloured bands. A speed trend arrow grows from the box and points to the speed the aircraft will have in 10 seconds at the present acceleration.
  • Altitude tape on the right, with the selected altitude above it and the barometric setting below it.
  • Vertical speed scale at the far right, in feet per minute.
  • Heading as a tape or compass arc along the bottom.
  • Flight mode annunciator (FMA) across the top: the active and armed autopilot, autothrust and flight director modes.
  • Glideslope and localiser deviation scales and radio altitude appear during an approach.

One PFD therefore replaces the attitude indicator, airspeed indicator, altimeter, vertical speed indicator and heading indicator, plus the flight director and approach indicators, and puts them where the pilot's scan runs: attitude, speed, attitude, altitude.

EADI, EHSI and the navigation display

The first electronic flight instrument systems, on the Boeing 757 and 767 and the Airbus A310 in the early 1980s, used two colour CRTs per pilot. The electronic attitude director indicator (EADI) drew the attitude sphere, flight director bars, ILS deviation, radio altitude and a fast-or-slow speed pointer. The electronic horizontal situation indicator (EHSI) drew a compass rose with the selected course, a course deviation indicator (CDI), distance and wind, and could switch between map, VOR, approach and plan modes.

On today's flight decks the EADI's job is done by the attitude area of the PFD, which also took over the airspeed and altitude dials. The EHSI's job is done by the navigation display (ND), which adds the flight-plan route, weather radar, traffic (TCAS) and terrain. On some types the ND is one page of a multifunction display that can also show checklists or system pages. EADI and EHSI are therefore names of functions, still used in older aircraft manuals and in syllabi.

EICAS and ECAM: engine and system monitor displays

Engines and systems need their own screens. Boeing's engine indication and crew alerting system (EICAS) shows primary engine values (thrust setting as N1 or EPR, and exhaust gas temperature) and the crew alert messages on the upper screen, and secondary engine values (N2, fuel flow, oil pressure and temperature, vibration) or system synoptics on the lower screen. Airbus's electronic centralised aircraft monitor (ECAM) uses an engine and warning display above a system display. When a fault occurs, the warning display lists the failure and the actions, and the system display brings up the synoptic of the system concerned. Some syllabi call these electronic systems monitor displays.

Worked example

Question. A PFD shows an indicated airspeed of 250 kt. The aircraft is decelerating at a steady 1.2 kt/s. The speed trend arrow shows the speed predicted 10 s ahead. (a) Where does the tip of the arrow point? (b) Does it point up or down the tape? (c) The selected speed bug is at 240 kt. How long until the aircraft reaches it?

  1. Speed change in 10 s: Δv = a × t = −1.2 kt/s × 10 s = −12 kt.
  2. Predicted speed: 250 − 12 = 238 kt, so the arrow tip points at 238 kt.
  3. The speed is falling, so the arrow points down the tape, towards lower numbers.
  4. Time to the bug: (250 − 240) ÷ 1.2 = 8.3 s.
  5. Check: 8.3 s is less than 10 s, so the arrow tip (238 kt) has already passed the 240 kt bug, which tells the pilot the bug will be reached within the next 10 s.

Answer. The trend arrow points down to 238 kt; the aircraft reaches the 240 kt bug in about 8.3 s.

Worked example 2

4.5 Head-up and helmet-mounted displays

A head-up display (HUD) projects flight symbols onto a glass combiner in front of the pilot, so the pilot reads speed, attitude and guidance while looking at the runway or the target. The symbols are collimated: they appear at infinity, at the same focus as the outside world. A helmet-mounted display (HMD) puts the symbols on the helmet visor or an eyepiece, and a head tracker keeps them pointing at the right place wherever the pilot looks. Civil aircraft use HUDs mainly for low-visibility approach and landing; military aircraft use HUDs and HMDs for weapon aiming, cueing and night flying.

You need: Electronic displays: PFD, MFD, EADI and EHSI for the symbols the HUD repeats.

How a HUD puts symbols at infinity

A HUD has four parts. The HUD computer (symbol generator) builds the picture from air data, inertial and guidance data. The display source (a CRT in older units, now an LCD with LED backlight or a micromirror projector) draws the symbols brightly. The collimating optics are lenses or curved mirrors with the source at their focal plane, so the rays from each symbol point leave parallel. The combiner is a glass plate with a coating that reflects the display colour (usually green) and transmits the rest of the outside light.

Parallel rays look as if they come from a very distant object, so the symbols form a virtual image at infinity. The pilot's eye can stay focused on the runway and still see sharp symbols. A panel display 0.75 m away needs a focus change of 1 ÷ 0.75 = 1.33 dioptres every time the eyes move between it and the outside, and the eyes also have to move down. The HUD removes both.

Fighters use a refractive HUD: the optics sit under the glareshield and the combiner stands above them. Airliners use an overhead (reflective) HUD: a projector above the pilot's head and a combiner that folds down in front of the eyes. The eye box (head motion box) is the space in which the pilot's eyes can move and still see all the symbols; the exit aperture of the optics limits the instantaneous field of view.

IFOV = 2 arctan(D ÷ 2L)

IFOV is the instantaneous field of view in degrees; D is the diameter of the exit aperture (collimating lens) in mm; L is the distance from the eye to that aperture in mm.

Conformal symbols and the flight path vector

Conformal symbols are drawn where the real thing is in the outside world: the horizon line, the pitch ladder, the runway outline and the flight path vector (FPV), which marks where the aircraft is actually going. Non-conformal symbols sit at fixed places: airspeed, altitude, heading and mode labels. Conformal symbols are only useful if they are exactly aligned (boresighted) with the aircraft axes; a misaligned FPV points the pilot at the wrong spot on the runway.

γ = arctan(Vv ÷ Vg)

γ is the flight path angle below the horizon; Vv is the vertical speed and Vg the ground speed, both in the same units (ft/min here, with 1 kt = 101.27 ft/min).

On a stable approach the pilot places the FPV on the touchdown zone. A HUD with guidance lets a suitably certified airliner land in fog with the pilot flying by hand, and an enhanced vision system can add an infrared image of the runway behind the symbols.

Helmet-mounted displays

An HMD puts a small display and optics on the helmet: one eye (monocular, like the helicopter sight of the AH-64 Apache) or both eyes (binocular). A head tracker measures where the head points, by magnetic, optical or inertial sensing or a mix. The computer uses that direction to draw conformal symbols and to cue sensors and missiles off-boresight: the pilot looks at a target, presses a switch, and the seeker or radar slews to that line of sight. Night-vision HMDs add an image intensifier or a thermal image. The F-35 has no HUD at all; its HMD carries every head-up symbol.

The hard parts are mass (a heavy helmet strains the neck under g and in an ejection), fit, and tracker latency. If the computer draws a symbol for where the head was a few milliseconds ago, the symbol trails the real target whenever the head turns. The error is the head rate times the delay, so fast trackers and head-motion prediction are needed.

Worked example

Question. An airliner flies an approach at a ground speed of 140 kt with a vertical speed of 750 ft/min down. 4.6 Cockpit design and human-machine interface

The cockpit is a human-machine interface (HMI): the place where the crew senses the aircraft's state, decides and acts. Good cockpit design fits displays, controls and alerts to human limits of attention, memory and reaction time, so that normal flying is easy and a failure is obvious. Some syllabi leave this chapter out, so it is kept short.

You need: Electronic displays: PFD, MFD, EADI and EHSI.

Layout: the most urgent data nearest the eyes

The crew works in a loop: sense, decide, act. Displays feed the sensing, controls carry the action and alerts interrupt when something is wrong. Information hierarchy decides position: primary flight data sits in the primary field of view straight ahead, navigation and system pages sit beside it, and rarely used data sits on overhead panels or in menus. Controls follow the same rule. Fighters put the most used switches on the stick and throttle (HOTAS). Every control must show its state, by its position, a light or a display echo.

Airbus uses a dark cockpit philosophy: with every system normal, no overhead light is on, so any light that comes on means a change. Certification asks the same of all large aeroplanes: under CS 25.1302 and the matching FAA rule, the crew must be able to detect, understand and act on the information each system gives, without the design itself causing errors.

Alerts, colour and mode awareness

Alerts come in levels. A warning needs immediate action (fire, stall, terrain) and comes with a red master warning light and an aural alert. A caution needs timely action (a generator failure) and comes in amber with a master caution light. Lower levels inform without demanding action. Colour must never be the only cue: a word, a shape or a sound must carry the same meaning, because sunlight washes out colours and some pilots have weak colour vision. A master caution that flashes for trivial faults teaches the crew to ignore it.

Mode awareness means the crew always knows what the automation is doing and what it will do next. The flight mode annunciator shows active modes in one colour and armed modes in another. A silent mode change, or two modes that look alike on the screen, is a design fault that has contributed to accidents.

Worked example

Question. A pilot scans six display areas in a fixed loop, spending 0.4 s on each. A new caution appears on one area only, and the crew must notice it within 1.5 s.   1. Full scan: 6 × 0.4 s = 2.4 s.

  4. The average (0.83 s) meets 1.5 s but the worst case (2.0 s) does not, and the design must meet the worst case.
  5. Fix: a master caution light in the primary field of view and an aural chime catch the eye wherever it is.

4.7 Data buses: ARINC 429 and MIL-STD-1553B

A data bus is a set of wires plus rules (word format, timing, addressing and error check) that lets avionics boxes exchange digital data. ARINC 429 is the civil bus: one transmitter sends 32-bit labelled words to up to 20 receivers, in one direction only. MIL-STD-1553B is the military bus: one bus controller commands up to 31 remote terminals on a shared, dual-redundant cable at 1 Mbit/s. Both are decades old and still fitted, because they are simple, predictable and easy to certify.

You need: Avionics system architecture, IMA and LRUs.

ARINC 429: one transmitter, many listeners

ARINC 429 runs on a shielded twisted pair. The signal is bipolar return-to-zero: for a 1 the line goes to HI (+10 V between the wires) for the first half of the bit and returns to NULL (0 V); for a 0 it goes to LO (−10 V) and returns to NULL. Every bit returns to zero, so the receiver finds the clock from the data itself.

Only one transmitter may drive a pair, and up to 20 receivers listen. A box that must reply needs its own transmitter and its own pair, so a two-way link needs two pairs. One pair still carries many parameters: the transmitter sends a stream of words, each tagged with a label, and each receiver keeps the labels it needs. Two speeds are used: high speed at 100 kbit/s and low speed at 12 to 14.5 kbit/s (12.5 kbit/s is common). Words are separated by a gap of at least 4 bit times.

MIL-STD-1553B: command and response

MIL-STD-1553B runs at 1 Mbit/s on a shielded twisted pair, with each box joined to the main cable by a transformer-coupled stub. Bits are Manchester II coded: a 1 is a high-to-low change in the middle of the bit and a 0 is low-to-high, so the clock travels with the data. The bus is normally dual redundant: cables A and B, and the controller retries on the other if a message fails.

One bus controller (BC) starts every transfer. Up to 31 remote terminals (RTs) speak only when commanded. A bus monitor listens and records traffic but never replies. Every word is 20 bit times: a 3-bit sync, 16 bits and an odd parity bit.

  • Command word (from the BC): RT address (5 bits), transmit/receive bit, subaddress (5 bits) and word count (5 bits, 1 to 32 data words).
  • Data word: 16 bits of data.
  • Status word (from the RT): its own address plus flags such as message error, busy, subsystem flag and terminal flag.

In a receive transfer (BC to RT) the BC sends the command and the data words, and the RT answers with its status word. In a transmit transfer (RT to BC) the BC sends the command, and the RT answers with its status word followed by the data. Both carry one command, one status and n data words. The RT must reply within 4 to 12 µs. An RT-to-RT transfer uses two commands and two status words.

4.8 AFDX and fibre-optic communication

Modern airliners have dozens of computers that exchange large blocks of data with a guaranteed delay. AFDX (Avionics Full-Duplex Switched Ethernet, standardised as ARINC 664 Part 7) does this with ordinary Ethernet frames on 100 Mbit/s full-duplex links through switches, plus virtual links that reserve bandwidth before flight. Every frame travels on two independent networks at once. Fibre-optic cable carries data as light, which gives high bandwidth, low mass and immunity to electrical interference.

You need: Data buses: ARINC 429 and MIL-STD-1553B.

Why switched Ethernet, and why not office Ethernet

ARINC 429 carries 100 kbit/s one way, and an airliner would need hundreds of such pairs. AFDX replaces them with a switched network. An end system is the AFDX interface inside an avionics computer: it builds frames for its host and receives frames addressed to it. A switch forwards each frame only towards its destinations. Every link is point-to-point and full duplex (separate transmit and receive pairs), so frames never collide. AFDX is used on the Airbus A380, A350 and A400M and the Boeing 787.

Office Ethernet is best effort: a busy switch may delay or drop a frame, and nobody can say how long a frame will take. Certification needs a bound. AFDX gets one by fixing all traffic in a configuration table loaded before flight. Each switch polices every flow against the table and discards frames that break it, so one babbling computer cannot flood the network.

Virtual links, BAG and redundancy

A virtual link (VL) is a one-way logical path from one source end system to one or more destinations, named by a 16-bit VL identifier carried in the frame's destination address. Many VLs share the same cables and switches, but each has its bandwidth reserved by two numbers.

  • BAG (bandwidth allocation gap): the minimum time between the starts of two frames on that VL. It is a power of two from 1 ms to 128 ms.
  • Lmax: the largest frame the VL may send, from 64 to 1518 bytes.

BVL = 8 × Lmax ÷ BAG

BVL is the reserved bandwidth in bit/s; Lmax is in bytes (×8 gives bits); BAG is in seconds. The VL may send at most 1 ÷ BAG frames per second. On the wire each frame also carries 20 bytes of preamble and interframe gap.

The end system also limits jitter, the extra delay a frame meets while waiting behind frames of other VLs at the output, to 500 µs. With every VL's Lmax and BAG known, the worst-case delay through each switch can be computed before flight.

Redundancy: each end system has two ports, one on network A and one on network B, with separate switches, cables and power. Every frame is sent on both, carrying a per-VL sequence number (one byte). The receiver checks each copy, keeps the first valid one and drops the duplicate, so a failed switch or a cut cable costs no data.

Fibre-optic communication

A fibre link has a light source (an LED or a laser diode), the fibre and a photodetector (a PIN or avalanche photodiode). The fibre is a glass core inside a cladding of slightly lower refractive index. Light that meets the core-cladding boundary at more than the critical angle θc is totally reflected back into the core and stays guided: this is total internal reflection. Light entering the fibre end within the acceptance angle is guided; the sine of that angle is the numerical aperture (NA).

sin θc = n₂ ÷ n₁   and   NA = √(n₁² − n₂²)

n₁ is the core index and n₂ the cladding index; θc is measured from the normal to the boundary; NA = sin of the acceptance half-angle in air.

Multimode fibre (core 50 or 62.5 µm, usually at 850 nm) is cheap and easy to connect, and suits the short runs inside an aircraft. Single-mode fibre (core about 9 µm, at 1310 or 1550 nm) carries one path with very low loss over long distances.

  • Advantages: very high bandwidth; much lighter than copper bundles; immune to electromagnetic interference and lightning pickup; no sparks near fuel; no ground loops.
  • Disadvantages: connectors must be clean and precisely aligned; tight bends leak light, so a minimum bend radius applies; repair needs special tools; a fibre cannot carry power to a sensor.

A link works if the received power stays above the receiver sensitivity with a safety margin. The power budget adds every loss in decibels: fibre loss per km times length, plus connector and splice losses. Flight control over fibre is called fly-by-light (see Digital fly-by-wire and fly-by-light).

4.9 Avionics software: RTCA DO-178B and V-model

RTCA DO-178B, Software Considerations in Airborne Systems and Equipment Certification (1992), is the guidance that certification authorities accept for airborne software; its European twin is EUROCAE ED-12B, and DO-178C replaced it in 2011. It does not prescribe a language or a method. It sets objectives, more of them the worse the effect of a failure, and asks for evidence that each was met. The development it describes is usually drawn as a V. Some syllabi leave this chapter out, so it is kept short.

You need: Civil, military, spacecraft and UAV avionics for failure conditions.

Software levels A to E

The system safety assessment classifies the worst effect of each function's failure, and that sets the software level. The size or difficulty of the code plays no part.

Statement coverage means every line ran at least once in testing. Decision coverage adds that every decision took both outcomes, true and false. Modified condition/decision coverage (MC/DC) adds that every condition inside a decision was shown to change the outcome on its own, with the other conditions held fixed. For a decision with n conditions MC/DC needs at least n + 1 tests, far fewer than the 2n of exhaustive testing.

The V-model and traceability

The left arm of the V goes down from system requirements to high-level software requirements (what the software must do), then to low-level requirements (the design: algorithms, data, logic), then to source code at the bottom. The right arm goes up, checking each level against the one opposite it: unit tests against low-level requirements, software integration tests against the high-level requirements, and hardware and software integration on the target computer against the system requirements. Reviews and analyses also count as verification.

Traceability ties the two arms together in both directions. Each high-level requirement traces to low-level requirements and to its tests; each low-level requirement traces to code and to tests. Downward trace shows nothing was forgotten; upward trace shows no code exists without a requirement. Robustness tests, which feed abnormal inputs, are also required. Planning, configuration management and quality assurance records are objectives in their own right: they show which version flew and who checked it.

4.10 Aircraft transducers: solenoids, pressure, temperature, strain, rotary position and accelerometers

A transducer converts one form of energy into another. A sensor converts a physical quantity (pressure, temperature, strain, position, acceleration) into an electrical signal; an actuator such as a solenoid converts an electrical signal into force and motion. Every engine, fuel, flight-control and air data system starts with transducers, and the accuracy of the whole system is set here, before any computer sees the number.

You need: Ohm's law. The electronics that amplify and digitise these signals are in Avionics electronics.

Transducers and solenoids

An active transducer makes its own signal (a thermocouple, a piezoelectric crystal); a passive one changes a resistance, capacitance or inductance and needs an excitation supply (a strain gauge, an RTD, an LVDT). The output may be a voltage, a current, a resistance, a frequency or an AC amplitude and phase.

A solenoid is a coil wound round a movable iron plunger. Current through the coil makes a magnetic field that pulls the plunger in against a return spring. On/off solenoids open fuel shut-off valves and select hydraulic lines; latching solenoids hold their position with no current; proportional solenoids give a force that follows the coil current and are used in pressure-control valves. When the current stops, the coil's stored energy produces a voltage spike, so the driver needs a flyback diode.

F = B²A ÷ (2μ₀)

F is the pull on the plunger face in N; B is the flux density in the air gap in T; A is the pole-face area in m²; μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m. The pull rises steeply as the gap closes, because B rises.

Pressure

Pressure is force per area, in pascals (1 bar = 100 kPa). Absolute pressure is measured against a vacuum, gauge pressure against the local atmosphere and differential pressure between two ports: an altimeter needs absolute static pressure, an airspeed indicator the difference between pitot and static pressure.

  • Bourdon tube: a curved flattened tube that straightens as pressure rises; used for high pressures such as hydraulics.
  • Capsule and bellows: thin corrugated shells that expand with pressure; an evacuated (aneroid) capsule drives the altimeter.
  • Diaphragm with strain gauges: pressure bends a thin diaphragm, and gauges bonded to it change resistance. Silicon diaphragms with diffused (piezoresistive) gauges are the usual electronic pressure sensor.
  • Capacitive: the diaphragm is one plate of a capacitor, and its deflection changes the capacitance.

Temperature and strain

A resistance temperature detector (RTD) is a platinum wire or film whose resistance rises almost linearly with temperature: RT = R₀(1 + αT), with R₀ = 100 Ω at 0 °C and α = 0.00385 /°C for the common Pt100. It is accurate and stable and suits oil, fuel and air temperatures. A thermistor is a semiconductor bead whose resistance falls steeply and non-linearly with temperature (NTC); it is sensitive but has a narrow range.

A thermocouple is two different metals joined at the hot end. The Seebeck effect gives an emf that depends on the temperature difference between the hot junction and the cold (reference) junction where the wires meet the copper of the instrument. To get the hot temperature, the instrument measures the cold-junction temperature and adds the emf the thermocouple would give at that temperature: cold-junction compensation. Exhaust gas temperature (EGT) is measured with chromel-alumel (type K) thermocouples, several probes round the exhaust wired in parallel to give an average (see Engine instruments: tachometer and EGT). The emf is not exactly proportional to temperature, so real indicators use the type K table.

GF = (ΔR ÷ R) ÷ ε

GF is the gauge factor (about 2 for metal foil); ΔR is the change of resistance and R the unstrained resistance in Ω; ε = ΔL ÷ L is the strain (1 µε = 10⁻⁶).

A strain gauge is a foil grid bonded to a part; when the part stretches, the grid lengthens and its resistance rises. The change is tiny, so gauges sit in a Wheatstone bridge excited by a steady voltage. With four active gauges (two stretched, two compressed) the output is Vout = Vex × ΔR ÷ R, four times one gauge's output, and temperature effects cancel because all four arms change together.

Rotary position, speed and acceleration

  • Potentiometer: a wiper on a resistive track gives a voltage proportional to angle; simple, but the wiper wears.
  • Synchro and resolver: rotating transformers. A synchro has three stator windings 120° apart; a resolver has two at 90°, giving outputs proportional to sin θ and cos θ, so θ = arctan(sine output ÷ cosine output). Both are rugged and used on flight-control and engine actuators.
  • LVDT and RVDT: an AC-excited primary and two secondaries around a movable core (linear) or rotor (rotary). The difference of the secondaries has an amplitude that follows the displacement and a phase that shows its direction, so it needs a phase-sensitive demodulator.
  • Speed: a variable-reluctance probe facing a toothed wheel gives one pulse per tooth, f = n × N ÷ 60 for n teeth at N rpm; this measures the engine shaft speeds N1 and N2.
  • Accelerometers: a proof mass on a spring moves by x = m a ÷ k. Piezoelectric types measure vibration (they cannot hold a steady reading); capacitive MEMS types are small and cheap; servo (force-balance) types hold the mass still with a coil and give the most accurate output, for inertial navigation.

4.11 Avionics electronics: diodes, transistors, op-amps, signal conditioning, ADC and DAC, microcontrollers

Avionics electronics is the layer between the transducers and the computers. A bridge gives a few millivolts; an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) wants volts in a clean, limited band; a microcontroller turns the numbers into decisions; and a transistor or a digital-to-analog converter (DAC) drives the actuator. The same chain sits inside an air data computer, a fuel-quantity unit and a flap controller.

You need: Aircraft transducers for the bridge output used here.

Diodes and transistors

A diode conducts from anode to cathode once the forward voltage is about 0.7 V (silicon) and blocks the other way. In aircraft it is used as a rectifier (transformer rectifier units turn 115 V AC into 28 V DC), as a flyback diode across a relay or solenoid coil, and as a Zener or transient-voltage suppressor that clamps spikes from switching and lightning.

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) has base, collector and emitter. In the active region it amplifies: IC = β IB, where β is the current gain. As a switch it is driven into saturation with more base current than IC ÷ β, so VCE falls to about 0.2 V and little power is lost. A MOSFET is switched by its gate voltage and draws almost no gate current; power MOSFETs switch pumps, lights and motors, and are the switches inside solid-state power controllers.

Op-amps and signal conditioning

An operational amplifier is a very high-gain differential amplifier. With negative feedback two rules solve most circuits: no current flows into the inputs, and the output makes the two input voltages equal.

Inverting: A = −Rf ÷ Rin     Non-inverting: A = 1 + Rf ÷ Rin

A is the voltage gain; Rf is the feedback resistor from the output to the inverting input; Rin goes from the inverting input to the signal (inverting) or to ground (non-inverting). A voltage follower (output tied to the inverting input, no resistors) has A = 1, very high input resistance and low output resistance.

A differential amplifier uses one op-amp and four resistors; with R₁ = R₃ and R₂ = R₄ it amplifies the difference of its inputs by R₂ ÷ R₁ and rejects the voltage common to both. A bridge output is a small difference riding on half the excitation. The instrumentation amplifier does this with three op-amps: two input followers with gain and a difference stage. Its gain is G = 1 + 2R ÷ RG, set by one resistor RG.

Signal conditioning then means: amplify to fill the ADC range; filter (an anti-alias low-pass filter removes noise above half the sampling rate; a first-order RC filter has cut-off fc = 1 ÷ (2πRC)); isolate where grounds differ; clamp the inputs; and linearise in software.

ADC and DAC

Q = Vref ÷ 2N     code = floor(Vin ÷ Q), at most 2N − 1

Q is the resolution (one step) in volts; Vref is the reference voltage; N is the number of bits; Vin is the input; floor rounds down. With this rounding-down rule the quantisation error is between 0 and one step; a converter that rounds to the nearest step has an error of at most ±Q ÷ 2.

Converter types: successive approximation (SAR), the usual general-purpose type, tries one bit at a time from the most significant, so an N-bit conversion takes N steps; flash uses 2N − 1 comparators for the fastest conversion; sigma-delta oversamples and filters to give 16 to 24 bits for slow signals such as pressure and temperature; dual-slope suits meters. The sampling rate must be more than twice the highest frequency present (the Nyquist rule), or higher frequencies fold back as false low ones (aliasing).

A DAC does the reverse: Vout = k × Vref ÷ 2N for code k. A DAC output needs a buffer or power amplifier to drive a servo valve coil.

Microprocessors and microcontrollers

A microprocessor is a CPU on a chip; memory and input/output are separate chips. A microcontroller puts the CPU, flash program memory, RAM, timers, an ADC, digital input/output and serial ports (UART, SPI, I²C, CAN) on one chip, so a sensor or actuator controller needs few other parts. A typical loop runs at a fixed rate: read the ADC, filter and scale, check limits, update the outputs, send the value on the bus and kick the watchdog timer, which resets the chip if the loop ever stops.

  • Writing Q = Vref ÷ 2N. It is Vref divided by 2 to the power N: 5 V ÷ 2⁸ = 19.5 mV.
  • Using A = Rf ÷ Rin for a non-inverting amplifier. It is 1 + Rf ÷ Rin.
  • Fitting the flyback diode the wrong way round. Cathode to the positive supply; reversed, it shorts the supply whenever the coil is on.
  • Giving a BJT switch exactly IC ÷ β of base current. It sits in the active region and overheats; drive it into saturation.

Takeaways

  1. The platform sets the priorities: airliners buy safety and regularity, fighters mission and survivability, spacecraft autonomy, UAVs low size, weight and power.
  2. Independent channels multiply reliability (2 × 10⁻⁴ becomes 8 × 10⁻¹² for three), but only if common causes are designed out.
  3. IMA saves mass, power and wiring by sharing modules, and keeps functions apart with time and memory partitions that must fit inside the major frame.
  4. Glass cockpits regroup the basic six onto the PFD and add maps and alerts, at the price of dependence on power, computers and software, so reversion, standby instruments and load shedding matter.
  5. HUD symbols are collimated to infinity and conformal symbols must be boresighted; HMDs need fast head trackers.
  6. ARINC 429 is one-way and labelled (36 bit times per word with the gap); 1553B is command/response at 20 µs per word; AFDX reserves 8 × Lmax ÷ BAG per virtual link on two parallel networks.
  7. DO-178B sets the effort by the failure condition, and the V-model traces every requirement to code and tests both ways.
  8. Accuracy is set at the transducer and the conditioning chain: bridge, instrumentation amplifier, anti-alias filter and ADC resolution must be designed together.

Understand Trace the evolution of aircraft electronic systems from the valve era to integrated modular avionics, and compare the design drivers of civil and military avionics. A flight-control channel has a failure rate of 1 × 10⁻⁴ per flight hour; show how many independent channels meet a 10⁻⁹ per flight hour target. 13 marks

Points to cover. Four eras: valves, transistors, digital buses and screens, integration; Organisation framework by function; Civil drivers: safety targets, regularity, cost; Military drivers: mission, survivability, environment, EW; Redundancy calculation against 10⁻⁹ with the independence caveat.

Model answer.

  1. Valve era (1920s to 1950s). Radios used vacuum tubes; instruments were mechanical or pneumatic and stood alone. Each function had its own box and its own wires.
  2. Transistor era (1950s and 1960s). Transistors made radios, autopilots and fire-control computers smaller, cooler and more reliable. Analog computers ran control loops and inertial navigation reached long-range aircraft.
  3. Digital era (1970s and 1980s). Microprocessors put functions into software. Digital buses replaced wire bundles: MIL-STD-1553 (first issued 1973) on military aircraft and ARINC 429 on airliners. Screens began replacing dials in the early 1980s, engine and alerting screens let two pilots do a flight engineer's job, and the A320 (1988) brought digital fly-by-wire to airliners.
  4. Integrated era (1990s onward). Functions share computers: a shared cabinet on the Boeing 777 (1995) and integrated modular avionics on switched Ethernet on the A380 (2007) and 787 (2011). Military aircraft moved to integrated systems that fuse radar, infrared and radio data into one picture.
  5. Organisation framework. Avionics is grouped by function: communication, navigation and surveillance; flight control and flight management; displays and crew interface; aircraft systems management; and, on military aircraft, mission systems (radar, EO/IR, electronic warfare, stores management).
  6. Civil and military drivers compared.
    DriverCivilMilitary
    Main goalsafety, regularity, low cost of ownershipmission success, survivability
    Safety rulefailure probability by severity: catastrophic ≤ 10⁻⁹, hazardous ≤ 10⁻⁷, major ≤ 10⁻⁵ per flight hourmilitary airworthiness plus mission reliability
    Environmentpressurised cabin, lightning, icinghigh g, vibration, heat, jamming (MIL-STD-810, MIL-STD-461)
    Redundancyidentical or dissimilar duplex and triplex channelsgraceful degradation after damage
    Typical busesARINC 429, AFDXMIL-STD-1553B, higher-speed links
  7. Redundancy calculation. With λ = 1 × 10⁻⁴ per flight hour and t = 1 h, one channel fails with P₁ ≈ λt = 1 × 10⁻⁴.

    simplex  P = 1 × 10⁻⁴      fails 10⁻⁹
duplex   P = (10⁻⁴)² = 1 × 10⁻⁸   fails 10⁻⁹ (10 times too high)

  10. Spacecraft and UAV contrast. Spacecraft avionics adds radiation tolerance and fault detection, isolation and recovery because no repair is possible; UAV avionics trades redundancy against size, weight and power and needs lost-link behaviour, so the same redundancy calculation gives a different answer once endurance is counted.
  11. Closing check. 10⁻¹² ÷ 10⁻⁹ = 10⁻³, so the triplex system is a thousand times better than required; the duplex system at 10⁻⁸ is ten times worse. Three channels is the minimum.

Model answer.

  1. Topology. One transmitter drives a shielded twisted pair and up to 20 receivers listen. A reply needs a second pair with its own transmitter. One pair carries many parameters, each tagged by a label.
  2. Signalling. Bipolar return-to-zero: a 1 is +10 V (HI) for the first half bit then NULL, a 0 is −10 V (LO) then NULL. The return to NULL in every bit lets the receiver recover the clock.     BitsFieldMeaning
    1–8Labelparameter code in octal, sent first
    9–10SDIwhich of two similar sources sent it

    32Parityodd parity over the word
  4. Validity. The receiver accepts a word only if the parity is odd, the label is one it expects, the SSM says normal operation, and the word arrives within its expected refresh time; otherwise the value is flagged invalid and the display shows a failure flag.

    15 × 25 + 30 × 5 = 375 + 150 = 525 words/s

  10. BNR data. In binary (BNR) words the value is a two's complement fraction of a stated range, with bit 29 as the sign and bit 28 as the most significant bit. Points to cover. Ideal op-amp rules; Inverting and non-inverting gains with outputs; Differential amplifier: gain and common-mode rejection; BJT in saturation: IC, IB, RB; Flyback diode orientation and purpose.

Model answer.

  1. Ideal rules. With negative feedback, no current enters the inputs and the output drives the two inputs to the same voltage.
  2. Inverting amplifier (signal through Rin to the − input, Rf from output to −, + grounded).
    A = −Rf ÷ Rin = −47 ÷ 4.7 = −10
Vout = −10 × 0.25 = −2.5 V
  3. Non-inverting amplifier (signal to +, Rf from output to −, Rin from − to ground).
    A = 1 + Rf ÷ Rin = 1 + 10 = 11
Vout = 11 × 0.25 = 2.75 V
  4. A voltage follower is the non-inverting circuit with the output tied straight to the − input: gain 1, very high input resistance, used as a buffer.
  5. Differential amplifier (R1 = R3 = 10 kΩ, R2 = R4 = 100 kΩ).
    gain = R2 ÷ R1 = 10
Vout = 10 × (2.512 − 2.500) = 10 × 0.012 = 0.12 V
common-mode part 2.506 V is rejected (as well as the resistor match allows)
  6. This is the job a bridge needs: a small difference on top of a large common voltage. The three-op-amp instrumentation amplifier adds high input resistance and one-resistor gain setting.
  7. Relay driver.
    IC = 28 V ÷ 140 Ω = 0.20 A
minimum IB = 0.20 ÷ 50 = 4 mA; ×2 for saturation → 8 mA
RB = (3.3 − 0.7) ÷ 0.008 = 325 Ω → standard 330 Ω gives 2.6 ÷ 330 = 7.9 mA
  8. In saturation VCE is about 0.2 V, so the transistor dissipates about 0.2 × 0.20 = 0.04 W. The pin must be able to source about 8 mA; if not, a MOSFET (driven by gate voltage, almost no gate current) is used instead.
  9. Flyback diode. Fitted across the coil, cathode to +28 V, anode to the collector. When the transistor turns off, the coil current (0.20 A) flows round through the diode instead of producing a spike of L di/dt that would break down the transistor. Fitted backwards it would short the supply each time the coil is on.
  10. Closing check. −10 × 0.25 = −2.5 V; 11 × 0.25 = 2.75 V; 10 × 0.012 = 0.12 V; 330 Ω × 7.9 mA = 2.6 V.
Apply Explain how temperature is measured on an engine and in its oil system with thermocouples, RTDs and thermistors. Four type K EGT probes read 700, 720, 690 and 730 °C. An EGT thermocouple gives 28.0 mV with the cold junction at 35 °C (take 41 µV/°C). A Pt100 (α = 0.00385 /°C) in the oil reads 138.5 Ω. Find the average EGT, the compensated EGT and the oil temperature. 13 marks

Points to cover. Seebeck effect, hot and cold junctions; Cold-junction compensation; Parallel probes give an average; RTD law and thermistor contrast; Calculations and the error without compensation.

Model answer.

  1. Thermocouple. Two dissimilar metals (chromel and alumel for type K) joined at the hot end give an emf that depends on the difference between the hot junction and the cold junction, where the wires meet the instrument's copper. It is active (needs no supply), rugged and works to over 1000 °C, so it suits exhaust gas temperature.
  2. Cold-junction compensation. The instrument measures its own terminal temperature and adds the emf the thermocouple would give at that temperature. Without it the reading is low by the cold-junction temperature and drifts as the instrument warms.
  3. Averaging. Several probes round the exhaust are wired in parallel; with equal loop resistances the indicator sees the average emf, so a hot or cold streak at one probe does not dominate.
  4. Average EGT.
    (700 + 720 + 690 + 730) ÷ 4 = 2840 ÷ 4 = 710 °C
  5. Compensated EGT.
    ΔT = 28.0 × 10⁻³ ÷ 41 × 10⁻⁶ = 682.9 °C
Thot = 682.9 + 35 = 717.9 °C
without compensation the gauge would show 682.9 °C, 35 °C low
  6. The 41 µV/°C sensitivity is a classroom approximation; real indicators use the type K reference table because the emf is not exactly linear.
  7. RTD. A platinum element whose resistance rises almost linearly: RT = R₀(1 + αT). It is passive (needs a small excitation current), accurate and stable from about −200 to 600 °C, and suits oil, fuel and air temperatures.
  8. Oil temperature.
    T = (RT ÷ R₀ − 1) ÷ α = (138.5 ÷ 100 − 1) ÷ 0.00385 = 0.385 ÷ 0.00385 = 100.0 °C
  9. Thermistor. A semiconductor bead whose resistance falls steeply and non-linearly with temperature (NTC). It is very sensitive but has a narrow range, so it is used for cabin and equipment-bay temperatures, not EGT.
  10. Choosing the sensor. EGT needs a thermocouple (range, ruggedness, no supply); oil and fuel temperatures need the accuracy and stability of an RTD; a thermistor fits where a cheap, sensitive sensor over a narrow range is enough. The RTD is read by passing a small known current and measuring the voltage, kept small to avoid self-heating.
  11. Closing check. 41 µV × 682.9 = 28.0 mV; 100 × (1 + 0.00385 × 100) = 138.5 Ω.
Apply Explain how a head-up display produces a collimated image, the difference between conformal and non-conformal symbols, and why boresight alignment matters. On an approach at 150 kt ground speed and 800 ft/min descent, the HUD aperture is 130 mm at 550 mm from the eye. Find the flight path angle and the instantaneous field of view, and the symbol error of an HMD with 20 ms latency at 150°/s head rate. 13 marks

Points to cover. Parts: computer, source, collimating optics, combiner; Image at infinity; no refocus between symbols and scene; Conformal and non-conformal symbols, FPV; γ, IFOV and HMD latency calculations; Boresight and civil and military uses.

Model answer.

  1. Parts. The HUD computer builds the symbols from air data, inertial and guidance data; the display source draws them; the collimating optics have the source at their focal plane so each point leaves as parallel rays; the combiner reflects the display colour to the eye and passes the outside scene.
  2. Collimation. Parallel rays form a virtual image at infinity, the same focus as the runway, so the pilot reads symbols without refocusing or looking down. A panel at 0.75 m would need 1.33 dioptres of refocus each time.
  3. Symbols. Conformal: horizon, pitch ladder, runway outline and flight path vector, drawn on the real objects. Non-conformal: airspeed, altitude, heading and mode labels at fixed places.
  4. Flight path angle.
    Vg = 150 × 101.27 = 15 190 ft/min
γ = arctan(800 ÷ 15 190) = arctan(0.0527) = 3.01° below the horizon
  5. Instantaneous field of view.
    IFOV = 2 arctan(130 ÷ (2 × 550)) = 2 arctan(0.1182) = 2 × 6.74° = 13.48°
half-angle 6.74° > 3.01°, so the FPV is in view
  6. Boresight. The HUD must be aligned with the aircraft axes; if it is off by even a fraction of a degree, the FPV points at the wrong place on the runway and a hand-flown low-visibility landing is guided to the wrong spot. HUDs are aligned on installation and checked against the aircraft datum.
  7. HMD latency.
    error = 150 °/s × 0.020 s = 3.0°
for 0.5°: latency ≤ 0.5 ÷ 150 = 3.3 ms, or head-motion prediction
  8. Uses. Civil: low-visibility approach and landing, often with an enhanced vision image. Military: weapon aiming, off-boresight cueing through the HMD, night flying; the F-35 uses its HMD in place of a HUD.
  9. HMD versus HUD. The HMD keeps symbols in view wherever the head turns, which a fixed HUD cannot, but it adds helmet mass, needs a head tracker of low latency and must stay safe in an ejection. Conformal HMD symbols are only as good as the tracker.
  10. Limits of a HUD. Too many symbols clutter the view, and brightness must follow the outside light so the symbols neither vanish against a bright sky nor hide the runway at night.
  11. Closing check. tan 3.01° = 0.0526 and 15 190 × 0.0526 = 799 ft/min ≈ 800.

Evaluate Compare ARINC 429, MIL-STD-1553B and AFDX as data networks for a transport aircraft, with numbers for their data throughput, and justify a choice for flight-critical data and for passenger-cabin data. 16 marks

Points to cover. Topology, direction, speed, coding of each; Throughput: 429 word, 1553B 32-word message, AFDX 1518-byte frame; Determinism and redundancy of each; Weight and wiring; Choice for flight-critical and cabin data with reasons.

Model answer.

  1. Main features.
    FeatureARINC 429MIL-STD-1553BAFDX
    Topologyone transmitter, ≤ 20 receiversbus, one BC, ≤ 31 RTsswitched star, full duplex
    Directionone wayhalf duplex, commandedboth ways at once
    Bit rate100 or 12.5 kbit/s1 Mbit/s100 Mbit/s
    Determinismfixed transmit scheduleBC scheduleBAG and Lmax policed per VL
    Redundancyduplicate units and busescables A and Bnetworks A and B, sequence numbers
  2. ARINC 429 useful data rate.
    19 data bits per 36 bit times at 100 kbit/s
19 ÷ 0.36 ms = 52.8 kbit/s of data per pair
  3. 1553B, one 32-word transmit message (8 µs response, 4 µs gap).
    time = 20 + 8 + 20 + 32 × 20 + 4 = 692 µs
data = 32 × 16 = 512 bits → 512 ÷ 692 µs = 0.74 Mbit/s
  4. AFDX, 1518-byte frames back to back (20 bytes overhead each).
    wire time = 1538 × 8 ÷ 10⁸ = 123.0 µs
frame rate ≈ 1518 × 8 ÷ 123.0 µs = 98.7 Mbit/s of frame bits
  5. Throughput comparison. AFDX carries roughly 130 times the useful rate of a 1553B bus and about 1900 times one ARINC 429 pair, and it does so in both directions on every link.
  6. Wiring. ARINC 429 needs a pair for every transmitter, so a large aircraft has hundreds of pairs; 1553B shares one dual cable among many terminals; AFDX uses one link pair per end-system port per network, through switches, which cuts weight sharply on a big aircraft.
  7. Flight-critical data. AFDX is the choice for the backbone: bandwidth reserved per virtual link, worst-case delay computable before flight, switches that police faulty senders, and two independent networks. Simple sensors and older LRUs stay on ARINC 429, joined through gateways or remote data concentrators. 1553B is a military bus; on a civil transport its certification base and supply chain are weaker, though it remains a fair choice on military transports.
  8. Cabin data. In-flight entertainment and passenger connectivity need high, bursty bandwidth but no flight-safety guarantee. They go on ordinary Ethernet, kept separate from the AFDX domain by a one-way or filtered gateway, so cabin traffic or a cyber attack from a passenger device cannot reach flight-critical networks.
  9. Judgement. The right answer is a layered network, not a single bus: AFDX core, ARINC 429 at the edges, and a separated open network for the cabin.
  10. Certification and cost. ARINC 429 parts are cheap, simple and proven, so an LRU that only broadcasts a few values stays on 429. AFDX switches and end systems cost more and need a network-wide configuration and timing analysis, which pays off only when many computers exchange a lot of data.
  11. Error detection. ARINC 429 and 1553B check each word with one odd parity bit, which catches any single-bit error but not every multi-bit error; ARINC 429 adds the SSM status and 1553B adds the status word and retries. AFDX frames carry the 32-bit Ethernet CRC, and the sequence numbers reject duplicates and out-of-order frames, so its integrity checking is far stronger.
  12. Latency. One ARINC 429 word takes 0.36 ms at high speed; a 1553B message of 4 data words takes about 132 µs plus its wait in the BC's schedule; an AFDX frame waits at most one BAG at the source plus the computed switch delays. For a fly-by-wire loop running at 50 to 100 Hz, all three can be fast enough; the difference is how many signals each can carry at that rate.
  13. Closing check. 98.7 ÷ 0.74 = 133 and 98 700 ÷ 52.8 = 1869, consistent with the ratios used.
Evaluate A display function whose loss is hazardous is hosted on an IMA module beside a cabin function with no safety effect. Evaluate the software levels, the partitioning needed, and the verification effort. Map its development onto the V-model, give an MC/DC test set for the decision (a or b) and c if it were level A, and assess a traceability review that finds 114 of 120 low-level requirements traced to tests. 16 marks

Points to cover. Levels from failure conditions: hazardous B, no effect E; Robust partitioning; module and OS at the higher level; V-model mapping and two-way traceability; Coverage by level; MC/DC set of n + 1 tests; Traceability percentage and gaps; verdict.

Model answer.

  1. Levels. Software level follows the failure condition: hazardous gives level B, no safety effect gives level E. The display partition must meet the level B objectives (65 in DO-178B, with decision coverage); the cabin partition has no objectives.
  2. Partitioning. The two may share the module only if ARINC 653 robust partitioning holds: the cabin partition gets a fixed time window and protected memory, so it cannot overrun its slot, write into the display partition's memory or crash the module. The operating system, the module hardware and the partition configuration must be assured to the higher level, B, because their failure could take the display down.
  3. V-model mapping.
    Left arm (define)Right arm (verify)
    System requirements for the display functionHardware/software integration tests on the IMA module
    High-level software requirements (what to show, timing)Software integration tests
    Low-level requirements (algorithms, data)Unit tests, with structural coverage
    Source code (bottom of the V)reviews and analyses of code
  4. Traceability runs both ways: down, so every requirement has code and tests; up, so no code exists without a requirement. Robustness tests with abnormal inputs are added.
  5. MC/DC set for (a or b) and c, if level A.
    TestabcOutcomeShows
    T1101truea (with T2), c (with T4)
    T2001falsea, b
    T3011trueb (with T2)
    T4100falsec (with T1)
  6. Each pair differs in one condition only and flips the outcome, so 4 tests (n + 1 for n = 3) replace the 8 of exhaustive testing. At level B decision coverage would need only two tests with opposite outcomes, such as T1 and T2.
  7. Traceability review.
    traced = 114 ÷ 120 × 100 = 95 %
gaps = 120 − 114 = 6 requirements without a traced test
  8. Verdict. The evidence is incomplete. Each of the 6 requirements needs a test traced to it, or the requirement must be shown unnecessary and removed through the change process, before the level B objectives are met. 95 % is not a pass mark; the target is 100 %.
  9. Evaluation. Hosting a level E function beside a level B one saves a box, but it adds the cost of assuring the platform and the partitioning to level B. The saving is worth it when many functions share the module; for a single small cabin function, a separate module may be simpler.
  10. Independence and process. At levels A and B some verification must be done by people independent of the authors, and the plans, configuration management records and quality assurance records must show exactly which version of the code and data was verified and loaded. On IMA the configuration tables (partition windows, memory maps, virtual links) are themselves verified data items.
  11. Structural coverage by level. Level A: MC/DC; level B: decision coverage; level C: statement coverage; level D: none. Coverage analysis also finds dead code (no requirement) and missing tests (requirement without test), which links back to the traceability review.
  12. Closing check. (a or b) and c: T1 = (1 or 0) and 1 = 1; T2 = (0 or 0) and 1 = 0; T3 = (0 or 1) and 1 = 1; T4 = (1 or 0) and 0 = 0. 114 + 6 = 120.

Glossary

Aliasing
High frequencies appearing as false low frequencies when sampling is too slow.
Avionics
All the electronic systems of an aircraft or spacecraft that sense, compute, communicate, navigate, control or display.
BAG
Bandwidth allocation gap: the minimum time between frame starts on one virtual link.
Basic T
The standard layout of the six flight instruments with the attitude indicator at the top centre.
BITE
Built-in test equipment: self-test inside a box that stores and reports fault codes.
Bus controller
The one 1553B terminal that starts every transfer.
Cold-junction compensation
Adding the emf for the reference-junction temperature to a thermocouple reading.
Combiner
The partly reflecting glass of a HUD that overlays symbols on the outside view.
Conformal symbol
A HUD symbol drawn where the real object is, such as the horizon or flight path vector.
ECAM
Electronic centralised aircraft monitor (Airbus).
EFIS
Electronic flight instrument system: display units, display computers and control panels.
EICAS
Engine indication and crew alerting system (Boeing).
FDIR
Fault detection, isolation and recovery: on-board logic that finds a fault and switches to a spare.
Federated architecture
One dedicated box per function with point-to-point wiring.
Head tracker
The sensor that measures where an HMD wearer's head points.
Instrumentation amplifier
A three-op-amp differential amplifier with high input resistance and gain set by one resistor.
Integrated modular avionics
Shared cabinets of plug-in modules hosting many functions as protected software partitions.
Label
The 8-bit octal code at the start of an ARINC 429 word that names the parameter.
Line replaceable unit
A box that can be removed and replaced on the flight line.
LVDT
Linear variable differential transformer: an AC position sensor with a movable core.
Major frame
The repeating cycle in which every ARINC 653 partition gets its time window.
Manchester II
A code with a transition in the middle of every bit, so the clock travels with the data.
MC/DC
Modified condition/decision coverage: each condition shown to change the decision on its own.
Payload
What a platform carries to do its job: sensors, communication relays, weapons, science instruments or passengers.
Remote terminal
A 1553B terminal that replies only when commanded.
Reversion
Moving a failed screen's page to another screen.
Speed trend arrow
A PFD arrow pointing to the airspeed expected in 10 seconds.
SSM
Sign/status matrix: bits 30 and 31 of an ARINC 429 word.
SWaP
Size, weight and power: the budget that limits UAV and military avionics.
Total internal reflection
Reflection of light at a boundary to a lower-index medium beyond the critical angle; it guides light in a fibre.
Traceability
Links from each requirement to its design, code and tests, in both directions.
Virtual link
A one-way AFDX path with a reserved bandwidth set by BAG and Lmax.
Watchdog timer
A timer that resets a microcontroller if its software stops responding.
Wheatstone bridge
Four resistive arms with excitation across one diagonal and output across the other.
