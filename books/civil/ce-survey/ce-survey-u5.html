5.1 Total station: components, working and errors

A total station is an electronic theodolite with an EDM and a processor in one instrument. It measures horizontal angle, vertical angle and slope distance to a prism at one setup, then computes coordinates. It exists because separate theodolite, tape and calculator work is slow and error-prone on a busy site. You see it in topographic surveys, road and building setting out, cadastral work, tunnel control and deformation monitoring. Some syllabi call it an electronic tacheometer.

You need: EDM from Unit 1 and the theodolite from Unit 4; setting out in 5.6 reverses the coordinate step.

Parts and what each does

A total station does three jobs in one body: an electronic theodolite measures angles, an EDM measures distance to a reflector prism, and a processor combines them. The telescope rotates about a horizontal axis (the trunnion axis); the whole upper assembly, carrying that axis, rotates about the vertical axis of the instrument. The tribrach levels the instrument and centres it over the station mark. A display and keypad run menus; a battery powers the set; storage holds raw angles, distances and coordinates. Angles are quoted in degrees, minutes and seconds: 1° = 60′ (minutes of arc) = 3600″ (seconds of arc).

  • Horizontal and vertical angle encoders read the circles electronically, often to 1″ or better.
  • The EDM unit measures the phase difference of a modulated light beam sent to the prism and back; it gives slope distance S.
  • The reflector prism returns the beam. Its prism constant is the small offset between the optical centre and the mechanical mounting, entered with its sign.
  • An optical or laser plummet centres the instrument over the ground mark.
  • The processor applies the prism constant and atmospheric correction, then computes horizontal distance and coordinates.

d = S sin z

d is horizontal distance in metres; S is the corrected slope distance in metres (after prism constant); z is the zenith angle in degrees, measured from the vertical.

A motorized total station turns under keypad control. A robotic total station follows the prism and lets one person survey alone.

Working at the station

  1. Set the tripod over the station, mount the instrument, and level with the foot screws.
  2. Centre the station mark under the plummet while keeping the bubble centred. Forced centring is better on control surveys.
  3. Enter station coordinates, height of instrument HI, and target height HT.
  4. Sight the backsight, enter its azimuth or coordinates, and set the horizontal circle. This orientation fixes the direction of the survey.
  5. Turn to the target. Read horizontal angle β clockwise from the backsight, zenith angle z, and slope distance S.
  6. Correct the slope distance: S_c = S + k, where k is the prism constant in metres with its sign. Compute d = S_c sin z and ΔV = S_c cos z. The target reduced level is RLtarget = RLstation + HI + ΔV − HT.
ER = EP + d sin A     NR = NP + d cos A

E and N are easting and northing in metres; P is the station; R is the target; d is horizontal distance in metres; A is the azimuth of the line in degrees, clockwise from north, given by A = azimuth of backsight + β; β is the clockwise horizontal angle turned from the backsight.

The processor repeats this for every shot. Stakeout reverses it: enter a design point and the display shows the angle to turn and the distance to measure. Field work covers traversing, topographic detail, as-built surveys and setting out. Prismless EDM suits short ranges to walls or rock faces. Transfer data by USB or Bluetooth.

Errors and checks

Every error enters the coordinates through an angle or a distance.

  • Collimation error: the line of sight is not square to the horizontal axis. It makes horizontal angles wrong; check by reversing the telescope.
  • Vertical index error: the vertical circle zero is mis-set. It tilts heights and horizontal distance through z; check face left and face right.
  • Circle eccentricity: the circle centre is not on the axis. The error varies with angle; modern instruments correct it.
  • Prism constant wrong: every slope distance shifts by the full prism constant k; horizontal distance shifts by k sin z and the height difference by k cos z. A 30 mm slip ruins stakeout even when it looks small.
  • Centring, HI and HT errors: a wrong station position, height of instrument or target height shifts coordinates or levels. Measure HI and HT with a tape.
  • Atmosphere and refraction: temperature and pressure change light speed; enter corrected values. Hot ground bends the line of sight near the surface.
  • Booking errors: wrong point name, angle mode or sign. Record raw data and check the first few coordinates.

For monitoring, repeat the same setup and check fixed reference points.

Worked example

Question. At station P (E 1000.000 m, N 2000.000 m), backsight Q has azimuth 30°00′00″. A total station reads a clockwise horizontal angle β = 75°30′00″ from PQ to R, a slope distance of 120.000 m and a zenith angle of 85°00′00″. Find the horizontal distance PR, the azimuth of PR, and the coordinates of R.

  1. Data: EP = 1000.000 m, NP = 2000.000 m, backsight azimuth = 30°00′00″, β = 75°30′00″, S = 120.000 m, z = 85°00′00″.
  2. Prism constant is zero here, so S_c = S = 120.000 m. Horizontal distance: d = S_c sin z = 120.000 × sin 85° = 120.000 × 0.996195 = 119.543 m.
  3. Azimuth of PR: A = 30°00′00″ + 75°30′00″ = 105°30′00″ = 105.5°.
  4. Easting difference: ΔE = d sin A = 119.543 × 0.963630 = 115.196 m.
  5. Northing difference: ΔN = d cos A = 119.543 × (−0.267238) = −31.947 m.
  6. Coordinates of R: ER = 1000.000 + 115.196 = 1115.196 m; NR = 2000.000 − 31.947 = 1968.053 m.

Answer. PR = 119.543 m, azimuth PR = A = 105°30′00″, and R = (E 1115.196 m, N 1968.053 m).

Exam traps

  • Using slope distance S as horizontal distance; you must multiply by sin z.
  • Forgetting the prism constant, or entering it with the wrong sign, so every distance is offset.
  • Ignoring height of instrument HI or target height HT when reducing levels.
  • Turning the wrong way: β is clockwise from the backsight, and A is clockwise from north.
  • Skipping the atmospheric correction on long sights, then blaming the instrument for a small scale error.

5.2 GPS and GNSS: principles and positioning methods

GNSS finds a receiver position from the travel times of radio signals from several satellites whose positions are known. GPS is the United States system; GNSS is the family that also includes GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou and NavIC. A surveyor uses GNSS for control points, stake-out, machine guidance and monitoring. The receiver measures a range to each satellite, reads the satellite position from the navigation message, and solves for latitude, longitude, height and its own clock error. One setup can give centimetre positions when two receivers share carrier-phase observations.

You need: datums and coordinate systems from Unit 1, control surveys from Unit 4, and the total station from 5.1 for comparison.

Segments, signals and pseudorange

The space segment is the satellite constellation. Each satellite carries atomic clocks, radios and a processor. It broadcasts a navigation message that contains its broadcast ephemeris: the orbital parameters a receiver uses to compute the satellite position at the signal transmission time. The control segment tracks every satellite from monitor stations, computes orbit and clock corrections at a master control station, and uploads them. The user segment is the receiver and its antenna.

Each signal has three parts. The carrier is the radio wave, such as L1 or L5. The code is a known binary pattern that lets the receiver align its copy with the incoming signal. The navigation message carries orbit, clock and health data. Code alignment gives a pseudorange: a range that is biased by clock errors. Carrier phase counts cycles, precise to millimetres, but starts with an unknown whole number of cycles, called the ambiguity.

ρ = c(tr − ts) + c δtr

ρ is the measured pseudorange in metres; c = 299,792,458 m/s; tr is the true reception time in system (GPS) time in seconds, equal to the receiver clock reading minus δtr; ts is the satellite transmission time in system time in seconds; δtr is the receiver clock bias in seconds, positive when the receiver clock is ahead. In words, measured pseudorange = true range + c δtr = c(tr − ts) + c δtr, so a receiver clock that is ahead makes every pseudorange too long, and the bias is subtracted when correcting.

Four satellites give four pseudoranges. The unknowns are receiver x, y, z and δtr. Three satellites give three equations for four unknowns, so the receiver cannot solve. Satellite spread in the sky then decides how range error becomes position error; see 5.3.

Positioning methods

Absolute positioning uses one receiver. Single-point positioning (SPP) uses code pseudoranges and broadcast ephemeris. Accuracy is a few metres. Relative positioning uses two or more receivers. The base sits on a known point; the rover works nearby. Differencing cancels common errors: satellite clock, most ionosphere and troposphere delay, and orbit error.

DGPS corrects code ranges. The base compares measured and known ranges and sends corrections; the rover applies them. This gives metre-level results. RTK uses carrier phase. The base and rover track the same satellites; the baseline is solved to centimetre level once the integer ambiguities are fixed. Network RTK uses a network of permanent stations, called CORS, to model corrections over an area; the rover receives a service message.

Survey modes: static, fast static and kinematic. Static keeps receivers still for long sessions for long baselines and high accuracy. Fast static shortens the occupation with dual-frequency data. Kinematic moves the rover, with ambiguity fixed at the start. Code is robust and instant; phase is precise but needs ambiguity resolution.

Receivers, coordinates and errors

A receiver has an antenna, a radio-frequency front end, channels, a processor and storage. Channels track several satellites at once. Accuracy classes run from hand-held metre, to mapping sub-metre, to survey centimetre with RTK. GPS is one GNSS; a multi-constellation receiver tracks GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou and NavIC. More satellites help in cities and under trees, but geometry still matters.

GPS coordinates are expressed in WGS84, a global geocentric datum. Receivers from other constellations transform their positions into WGS84, so GNSS output is normally in WGS84. Survey work often uses a local datum and a map projection. UTM is a common working frame: zones, easting, northing, metres. A datum transformation converts WGS84 coordinates to the local grid. Projection mathematics is in Unit 1 and Unit 7; do not repeat it here.

Errors. Satellite clock and ephemeris errors come from the broadcast data. Ionosphere delay varies with electron content; dual-frequency data remove most of it. Troposphere delay depends on pressure, temperature and humidity; models reduce it. Multipath is signal arrival after reflection from buildings or ground. Receiver noise is internal. Corrections come from differencing, models, dual frequency, choke-ring antennas and good site selection.

Other constellations: GLONASS (Russia), Galileo (Europe), BeiDou (China), NavIC (India). GPS modernization adds new civil signals such as L2C, L5 and L1C and new satellites. Multi-frequency, multi-constellation receivers are now normal.

Worked example

Question. A GNSS receiver clock is ahead by 10 µs. It measures pseudoranges 20,200,000.000 m, 21,000,000.000 m, 19,800,000.000 m and 22,500,000.000 m. Take c = 299,792,458 m/s. Find the common range error and the corrected pseudoranges.

  1. Convert the clock bias: 10 µs = 10 × 10⁻⁶ s = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ s.
  2. Common range error = c × bias = 299,792,458 × 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 2997.92458 m ≈ 2997.925 m.
  3. A receiver clock ahead makes each measured pseudorange too long by this amount. Corrected range = measured − error.
  4. 20,200,000.000 − 2997.925 = 20,197,002.075 m.
  5. 21,000,000.000 − 2997.925 = 20,997,002.075 m.
  6. 19,800,000.000 − 2997.925 = 19,797,002.075 m.
  7. 22,500,000.000 − 2997.925 = 22,497,002.075 m.

Answer. The common range error is about 2997.925 m; corrected pseudoranges are 20,197,002.075 m, 20,997,002.075 m, 19,797,002.075 m and 22,497,002.075 m.

Exam traps

  • Three satellites are not enough. The receiver clock bias is a fourth unknown; four satellites give four equations.
  • A receiver clock ahead gives pseudoranges that are too long; subtract the common error. Do not add it.
  • Code pseudorange is metre-level and instant; carrier phase is millimetre-level but has an integer ambiguity.
  • WGS84 and UTM are not the same: WGS84 is a datum; UTM is a projected grid in metres.
  • Convert µs to s before multiplying by c; 1 µs = 10⁻⁶ s.

5.3 Satellite geometry and accuracy measures

A GNSS receiver computes position from ranges to satellites. The ranges are not perfect; each carries a small error. The spread of satellites across the sky decides how much those range errors grow into position errors. Satellites bunched in one part of the sky give a weak fix. Satellites spread widely give a strong fix. Surveyors measure this spread with DOP, the dilution of precision, and plan observation windows with it. The same numbers help compare a GNSS fix with a total station fix on a project.

You need: 5.2 for how ranges give position, and Unit 4, Weights and adjustment, for covariance and error propagation.

Satellite geometry and DOP

Each satellite has an elevation angle above the horizon and an azimuth angle clockwise from north. A skyplot draws azimuth around a circle and elevation as distance from the centre: centre is zenith, outer edge is horizon. Good geometry means satellites are spread in azimuth and include both high and low elevations. A satellite directly overhead adds little horizontal strength; two satellites close together add almost the same information. Range distance itself does not improve geometry.

Let u_i = (u_x, u_y, u_z) be the unit vector from receiver to satellite i. For four unknowns, x, y, z and receiver clock, the design matrix H has one row per satellite, [-u_x, -u_y, -u_z, 1]. The covariance of the position solution is σ_r² (HᵀH)⁻¹, where σ_r is the 1-sigma range error in metres. DOP factors are square roots of diagonal terms of Q = (HᵀH)⁻¹.

σ_position = DOP × σ_range

σ_position is the 1-sigma position error in metres; DOP is the chosen dilution factor; σ_range is the 1-sigma range error in metres.

  • GDOP = √trace(Q), using x, y, z and clock.
  • PDOP uses x, y, z. HDOP uses x, y. VDOP uses z. TDOP uses clock.
  • If satellites lie in one direction, HᵀH is nearly singular and DOP becomes very large.
  • DOP depends on angular spread, not signal strength.

Accuracy measures: RMS, CEP, 2DRMS

Precision is repeatability; accuracy is closeness to true coordinates. DOP predicts precision from geometry only. A bias such as an ionospheric delay shifts every fix and is not reduced by good DOP. Report both.

  • RMS: for n horizontal errors e_i, RMS = √(Σe_i²/n). It includes bias if present.
  • DRMS = √(σ_x²+σ_y²), the radial 1-sigma horizontal error.
  • CEP is the radius of a circle containing 50% of horizontal fixes. CEP is not 95%.
  • 2DRMS = 2 × DRMS, commonly quoted as about 95% horizontal confidence for circular normal errors.
  • Vertical error uses VDOP × σ_range; it is often larger than horizontal error in GPS.

A confidence level states the probability contained. A 95% horizontal radius is about 2DRMS. A 95% vertical interval uses 1.96 σ_v, where σ_v is the vertical 1-sigma error. For survey control, compare measured check points, not only DOP.

Error sources, mask angle and planning

Range errors enter every satellite. Some cancel in the position solution; some do not.

  • Ionosphere: free-electron delay, frequency dependent; dual-frequency helps.
  • Troposphere: dry and wet delay; models reduce it.
  • Multipath: reflected signals near buildings and trees; site and antenna choice help.
  • Clock: satellite and receiver clock errors; the clock term is estimated, and TDOP applies.
  • Ephemeris: satellite position error.
  • User dynamics: antenna motion, vibration and poor tracking at the survey pole.

DOP does not fix any of these. It only scales random range noise. A low PDOP with large multipath still gives a wrong fix. Planning uses a mask angle, the lowest elevation accepted; common values are 10° to 15°. A high mask removes noisy low satellites but may remove spread and raise DOP. Compute PDOP for the day and choose an observation window with low PDOP and enough satellites. In a city, plan around buildings. Multi-GNSS (GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou and NavIC) adds satellites and usually lowers DOP. GPS modernization adds signals. See 5.2 for positioning.

Worked example

Question. Two satellites are used in a 2D fix. From receiver R at (0,0), satellite A is at (0,10000) m and satellite B is at (10000,0) m. The unit vectors to A and B are (0,1) and (1,0). Form H and compute HDOP. What happens if B is at (0,20000) m instead?

  1. Receiver R = (0,0). Satellite A = (0,10000) m, so u_A = (0,1). Satellite B = (10000,0) m, so u_B = (1,0).
  2. For the 2D unknown (x,y), use rows from the unit vectors. Rows are written here with +u instead of -u as in Section 1; the sign flip does not change HᵀH, so HDOP is the same. H = [[0,1],[1,0]].
  3. HᵀH = [[1,0],[0,1]] = I.
  4. (HᵀH)⁻¹ = I. HDOP = √(trace(I)) = √(1+1) = √2 = 1.414.
  5. If B is at (0,20000) m, u_B = (0,1). Then H = [[0,1],[0,1]] and HᵀH = [[0,0],[0,2]].
  6. The determinant is 0 × 2 − 0 × 0 = 0, so HᵀH is singular. HDOP is infinite: there is no horizontal spread between the two directions.

Answer. For the spread pair, HDOP = 1.414; for the collinear pair, HDOP is infinite.

Exam traps

  • Treating high elevation as good geometry. DOP depends on how the line-of-sight directions are spread, not on elevation alone, not on signal strength and not on range distance.
  • Confusing DOP with accuracy. DOP scales random range noise; it does not remove ionosphere, multipath or ephemeris bias.
  • Saying CEP is 95%. CEP is about 50%; 2DRMS is the usual about-95% horizontal measure.
  • Forgetting the clock term. GDOP and TDOP include clock; HDOP and VDOP do not.
  • Assuming more satellites always lower DOP. A new satellite in the same cluster may barely change the geometry.

Analyze A skyplot shows all satellites north and low. What happens to HDOP and VDOP, and what planning step helps? 5 marks

North-only directions give weak east-west strength, so HDOP is large. Low elevation raises noise (more atmosphere and multipath). Here every satellite has almost the same low elevation, so the vertical and clock terms cannot be separated well and VDOP is also poor. The planning step is to wait for an observation window when satellites are spread in azimuth and in elevation, and to keep the mask angle low (10°–15°) so the low satellites that add spread are not rejected. A high mask angle here would remove the only satellites present.

Low DOP only means good geometry. A bias such as multipath at the site or an uncorrected atmospheric delay can shift all fixes. A wrong antenna height or a bad coordinate datum can also cause a constant offset.

Understand Why can a very high mask angle worsen a GNSS survey? 3 marks

It rejects low-elevation satellites. That can remove the only satellites giving horizontal spread, so PDOP or HDOP rises even though multipath and atmospheric noise fall.

5.4 LiDAR and laser scanning

LiDAR means light detection and ranging. It measures distance from the two-way travel time of a laser pulse. A scanner steers many pulses across a scene. The ranges, beam angles and sensor position give a 3D point cloud. LiDAR maps terrain under trees, checks power lines, measures forests and buildings, and records as-built corridors. It works at night and gives its own light. It sees the ground through small gaps in leaves. The output is dense and georeferenced.

You need: the range-and-time idea from 5.2 GPS and GNSS and basic remote sensing terms from Unit 6.

Range from travel time

LiDAR stands for light detection and ranging. It finds the slant range to a surface from the round-trip time of a short laser pulse. One pulse gives one point. A scanner sends pulses in a fixed pattern, so many points form a point cloud.

R = c t / 2

R is slant range in metres; c is the speed of light in vacuum, 299,792,458 m/s; t is two-way travel time in seconds. The factor 2 is because the pulse travels to the surface and back.

The main parts are a laser, a scanner mirror, a detector, a timer, a GNSS receiver and an IMU. The mirror deflects the pulse. The detector catches the return. The timer records t. GNSS gives position and IMU gives orientation. Each point stores x, y, z and intensity. Intensity is the strength of the return, not height.

  1. Emit a short pulse from the laser.
  2. Deflect it by the rotating or oscillating mirror.
  3. Detect the returned light.
  4. Stop the timer and compute R = c t / 2.
  5. Combine R with the beam direction and the GNSS/IMU pose to get x, y, z.
  6. Repeat for the next angle.

Platforms and returns

Airborne LiDAR flies on an aircraft or helicopter. It maps large terrain and forest areas. Terrestrial laser scanning stands on a tripod and scans buildings, slopes or plant. Mobile mapping puts scanners on a vehicle for road corridors. 5.5 UAV and drone surveying covers small drones that may carry LiDAR or cameras.

A pulse can hit leaves before ground. A discrete-return system records one or more returns, such as first return from canopy and last return from ground. A waveform system records the full return shape. Classification labels points as ground, vegetation, building or wire. Ground points give a bare-earth DEM. All points give a DSM, which includes trees and roofs.

Georeferencing turns ranges and angles into map coordinates. The scanner pose from GNSS and IMU is combined with each range and angle. In mobile mapping the pose changes every moment, so timing between scanner and GNSS/IMU must be tight.

Errors and uses

Range errors come from timer resolution and pulse shape. Angular errors come from mirror encoding. GNSS and IMU errors shift or rotate the whole cloud. Scan geometry causes spacing to grow with range. Dark or wet surfaces return weak light. Fog, dust and rain scatter the beam. Intensity is not elevation.

  • Terrain: bare-earth DEM under vegetation.
  • Power lines: clearance checks and sag.
  • Forestry: canopy height and biomass.
  • Buildings: facades and as-built records.
  • Corridors: roads, rail and pipelines.
  • Change detection: repeat scans of a site.

Classification and point cloud analysis sit in a GIS or CAD workflow. See Unit 7, Geographic Information Systems, for raster and vector analysis. Image classification belongs to Unit 6.

A scan is not a map until points are classified and checked against control. Check known points on the ground before using a DEM for volumes or design.

Worked example

Question. A LiDAR pulse returns after 2.00 µs. The scanner steps by 0.100° between pulses. Take c = 299,792,458 m/s. Find the range and the point spacing on a surface perpendicular to the beam at that range.

  1. t = 2.00 µs = 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ s.
  2. R = c t / 2 = 299,792,458 × 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ / 2 = 299.792458 m.
  3. θ = 0.100° = 0.100 × π / 180 = 0.001745329 rad.
  4. s = R θ = 299.792458 × 0.001745329 = 0.5232 m.
  5. Rounded: R = 299.792 m and s = 0.523 m.

Answer. The range is 299.792 m and the point spacing at that range is 0.523 m.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the divide by 2: t is the two-way time, out and back.
  • Using intensity as elevation: intensity is return strength, not height.
  • Confusing DEM and DSM: a DEM is bare earth, a DSM includes trees and roofs.
  • Using degrees directly in s = R θ: convert the step angle to radians first.
  • Calling a GNSS/IMU error a range error: it shifts or rotates the whole point cloud.

5.5 UAV and drone surveying

A UAV (uncrewed aerial vehicle), or drone, is a small aircraft that flies a planned mission and carries a camera or scanner. A drone survey takes many overlapping photos, matches the same ground points in them, and builds a map and an elevation model. It exists because one pilot can cover a site in minutes at centimetre-level detail. Users apply it to topographic mapping, construction progress, landslide and flood mapping, and agriculture.

You need: 5.2 GNSS for positions, 5.4 LiDAR for point clouds, and Unit 6 remote sensing for image basics.

Drone types and payloads

A multirotor has four or more rotors. It takes off and lands vertically, hovers, and flies slowly. It suits 10–50 ha, gives about 20–40 minutes of flight, takes off and lands vertically, and can hover over one spot; a fixed-wing generally carries more payload and flies longer but cannot hover. A fixed-wing drone has wings and must keep moving. It covers hundreds of hectares in one flight and is good for long strips, but it cannot hover and needs a launch and landing area. A hybrid VTOL (vertical take-off and landing) combines both.

  • RGB camera: cheap photos for an orthomosaic and surface model.
  • Multispectral camera: several bands for crop stress and vegetation indices.
  • LiDAR scanner: direct ranges and a point cloud that can see ground under gaps in trees.
  • GNSS tag: each photo gets a position; RTK (real-time kinematic) GNSS gives 1–3 cm.
  • GCP (ground control point): a marked point on the ground with known coordinates, used to check or correct the model.

Flight planning: GSD and overlap

GSD (ground sample distance) is the ground size of one image pixel. A smaller GSD means finer detail. Use flying height above ground, not above mean sea level.

GSD = p × H ÷ f

GSD is ground sample distance in metres per pixel; p is pixel pitch (pixel size) in metres; H is flying height above ground in metres; f is focal length in metres.

Overlap is the shared part of two photos. Along-track overlap is between successive photos in a strip; side overlap is between adjacent strips. Survey flights use about 70–80% along-track and 60–70% side overlap. Overlap creates common points for matching.

With 80% along-track overlap, each new photo adds 20% new ground. Spacing = footprint × (1 − overlap fraction). Number of images = strip length ÷ spacing + 1. The footprint is the ground length covered by one photo.

Structure from motion and products

SfM (structure from motion) is a photogrammetric method that recovers 3D structure and camera positions from overlapping images.

  1. Detect keypoints (corners and blobs) in every image.
  2. Match the same keypoint across overlapping images.
  3. Calibrate the camera: focal length, principal point, lens distortion.
  4. Build tie points: matched points seen in many images.
  5. Run bundle adjustment: solve camera positions and 3D point positions together by minimising reprojection error.
  6. Build a dense cloud from all pixels, then a mesh and products.

Products: an orthomosaic is a seamless top-down map made from rectified images. A DEM (digital elevation model) is an elevation grid. A DSM includes buildings and trees; a DTM is bare ground after ground points are classified. Resolution is close to GSD. High overlap and sharp photos matter more than a high megapixel count for matching. RTK positions help bundle adjustment, but GCPs still catch drift. The dense cloud carries colour from RGB images or direct elevation from LiDAR. Some syllabi call this drone photogrammetry.

Rules and uses

In India, the Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA) regulates drones. Outline: register the drone, fly only in permitted zones, and hold a remote pilot certificate. Red zones are no-fly; airports and sensitive areas need clearance. Keep line of sight and follow height limits. Permits depend on weight class and zone; green zones need less paperwork. Do not fly over people or moving traffic without permission.

Uses: topographic mapping, construction monitoring, landslide and flood mapping, agriculture, and stockpile volumes. A construction team flies weekly and compares DEMs to measure cut and fill. A landslide map uses a DSM before and after rain. A farm uses multispectral images to spot water stress. For setting out, see 5.6.

Worked example

Question. A drone camera has pixel size 2.4 µm and focal length 8.8 mm. It flies at 120 m above ground. Find the GSD. For a 3.0 km strip and a 150 m along-track footprint with 80% overlap, find the number of images.

  1. Convert units: pixel pitch p = 2.4 µm = 2.4 × 10⁻⁶ m; focal length f = 8.8 mm = 0.0088 m; height H = 120 m.
  2. GSD = p × H ÷ f = (2.4 × 10⁻⁶ m × 120 m) ÷ 0.0088 m = 0.0327 m.
  3. GSD = 0.0327 m = 3.27 cm per pixel.
  4. Along-track spacing = 150 m × (1 − 0.80) = 150 m × 0.20 = 30 m.
  5. Strip length = 3.0 km = 3000 m.
  6. Number of images = 3000 m ÷ 30 m + 1 = 100 + 1 = 101.

Answer. GSD is 0.0327 m (3.27 cm), along-track spacing is 30 m, and the strip needs 101 images.

Exam traps

  • Using flying height above mean sea level instead of above ground. A site at 500 m elevation with 120 m above ground is 620 m above sea level.
  • Treating GSD as contour interval. GSD is pixel size on the ground; contour interval is vertical spacing between contours.
  • Reading 80% overlap as 80% new area. It leaves 20% new ground, so spacing is 0.20 × footprint.
  • Assuming RTK GNSS removes the need for GCPs. GCPs check datum, camera model and systematic drift.
  • Mixing the orthomosaic with a single photo or with the DEM. The orthomosaic is a 2D map; the DEM is elevation.

5.6 Construction surveys and setting out

Setting out turns a design into marks on the ground. It reverses a topographical survey: control points already have known coordinates, and the crew places a new point at its design coordinates by angle and distance. A foundation, column, road centreline or drain must sit where the drawing says, not where the ground suggests. Total station stakeout and GNSS stakeout are the two common methods. Only a few universities teach this as a separate topic; most fold it into engineering surveying.

You need 5.1 Total station and the control ideas of Unit 4, Horizontal and vertical control.

What setting out does

Setting out, also called layout surveying, places a designed point on the ground from known control. A control point is a fixed station with known coordinates and level. The design gives the target as coordinates (E, N), where E is easting and N is northing, or as an offset from a reference line. A reference line is a straight baseline between two known points, such as a building grid line or road centreline.

Horizontal control fixes easting and northing; vertical control fixes reduced level. The crew sets the instrument on a control station and orients it to a backsight, a second known station. The instrument now has a known zero direction. The crew turns the computed angle and measures the computed distance.

The coordinate method stores control and target coordinates in the total station. The angle-distance method computes azimuth and distance first. GNSS stakeout works in open sky; a total station is better near walls or under trees. See 5.1 and 5.2.

From coordinates to a peg, then a check

ΔE = E_target − E_station; ΔN = N_target − N_station; α = atan2(ΔE, ΔN); D = √(ΔE² + ΔN²); β = α_target − α_backsight

ΔE and ΔN are easting and northing differences in metres; α is azimuth clockwise from north, with atan2 taking easting first; D is horizontal distance; β is the setting-out angle from the backsight, reduced to 0° to 360°.

Field steps: set on the control station, sight the backsight, set that direction as β = 0°, turn β clockwise, measure D, and drive the peg. Check from another control station; one setup cannot reveal an orientation blunder.

Construction surveys repeat this at each stage: site clearing, foundation corners, columns, road centreline, drainage gradients, and deformation monitoring. Tolerances come from the project specification. An as-built check re-measures the finished work independently and records actual coordinates. GNSS stakeout is fast in open areas; total station stakeout is stronger in urban or covered sites.

Worked example

Question. Control station A is (E 1000.000 m, N 1000.000 m) with backsight azimuth 0°00′00″. A building corner C is to be set at (E 1025.000 m, N 1040.000 m). Find the setting-out angle from the backsight and the horizontal distance AC.

  1. ΔE = 1025.000 − 1000.000 = +25.000 m.
  2. ΔN = 1040.000 − 1000.000 = +40.000 m.
  3. Both positive: C is north-east of A, so α lies between 0° and 90°.
  4. D = √(25.000² + 40.000²) = √(625 + 1600) = √2225 = 47.1699 m ≈ 47.170 m.
  5. α = atan2(25.000, 40.000) = 32.00538° = 32°00′19″.
  6. β = α − backsight azimuth = 32.00538° − 0° = 32.00538° clockwise.

Answer. Turn 32.00538° clockwise from the backsight and measure 47.170 m horizontally from A to peg C.

Exam traps

  • Swapping easting and northing in atan2. Use atan2(ΔE, ΔN), not atan2(ΔN, ΔE).
  • Using a magnetic bearing as a grid azimuth. Magnetic bearings carry declination and local attraction; setting out needs project or grid azimuth.
  • Forgetting the backsight. β = target azimuth − backsight azimuth, reduced to 0–360°.
  • Laying out slope distance as horizontal distance. Reduce EDM slope distance before marking.
  • Checking only from the setup that set the peg. Re-measure from another control point to catch orientation and coordinate blunders.

5.7 Hydrographic and underground surveys

Hydrographic surveying measures depth and position in water bodies. Underground surveying carries surface coordinates and direction below ground. Both extend control beyond dry land: one through water, one down a shaft. An echo sounder turns two-way sound travel time into depth. Tide reduction converts that depth to chart datum. GNSS or a total station fixes each sounding. Underground, a shaft transfers coordinates and azimuth to a tunnel traverse. Only a few universities teach this as a full topic. Ports, dredging, dams and metro tunnels use it.

You need 5.1 total station, 5.2 GNSS and Unit 3 levelling for heights and control.

Depth from a sound pulse

An echo sounder has a transducer under the boat. It sends a short sound pulse down and receives the echo from the bed. The timer records the two-way travel time t. The pulse goes down and back, so one-way path is half the total. A transducer converts electrical energy to sound and back. The bed gives a strong echo.

d = c t / 2

d is depth below the transducer in metres, c is sound speed in the water in m/s, and t is the two-way travel time in seconds. In sea water c is about 1500 m/s. It varies with temperature, salinity and pressure, so use a measured value.

  1. Fix the boat position at every sounding.
  2. Measure t and read c.
  3. Compute d.
  4. Record depth, position and tide height.

This is the same travel-time idea as 5.4 LiDAR, with sound in water instead of light in air. Sounding lines are spaced so the bed is covered.

Position, tide and the chart

Bathymetry is water depth measurement. Depth needs a position. A sounding plan sets parallel runs across the area and cross lines to check coverage. GNSS fixes the boat; a total station fixes near-shore marks. Each sounding is stored as a triple: position, depth, time. The sounding plan spacing depends on map scale and the least depth expected.

Chart datum is a low-water reference, close to lowest astronomical tide. A chart shows depth below datum, not below the moving surface. Tide height h is water level above chart datum at sounding time. Reduced depth D = d + h. If the tide is below chart datum, h is negative. A tide gauge or predicted tide gives h.

A bathymetric chart plots reduced depth at position, with shoreline and symbols. Depth contours join equal reduced depths. Cross lines expose gaps and position errors. Dredging, navigation and reservoir capacity use the chart. The chart is final.

Shaft transfer and tunnel traverse

Underground survey begins at surface control. GNSS or a total station gives coordinates and height at the shaft collar. Two plumb wires or a laser transfer a vertical line down the shaft. A gyrotheodolite, or the two wires, gives azimuth: the horizontal angle of a line from north. Check the transfer before extending the traverse.

An underground traverse starts at the shaft station. A total station measures angles and distances along the tunnel and carries coordinates to the face. The surveyor sets out the centreline and grade, the designed slope. Two headings meet at breakthrough; breakthrough error is the difference between the faces. Azimuth transfer error grows with distance, so check with cross headings.

Levels transfer by tape or laser; check against a surface benchmark. Traverse adjustment uses the same closing error ideas as Unit 2. Mine and tunnel work call this mine surveying or underground surveying. The surface benchmark controls tunnel level.

Worked example

Question. An echo sounder records a two-way travel time of 0.040 s. Sound speed in water is 1500 m/s. The tide is 1.20 m above chart datum. Find the depth below the water surface and the reduced depth below chart datum.

  1. One-way time = t / 2 = 0.040 / 2 = 0.020 s.
  2. Depth d = c × one-way time = 1500 × 0.020 = 30.0 m.
  3. Reduced depth D = d + h = 30.0 + 1.20 = 31.2 m.
  4. The tide is positive because it is above chart datum.

Answer. Depth below the water surface is 30.0 m; reduced depth below chart datum is 31.2 m.

Exam traps

  • Using t instead of t/2 gives double the depth.
  • Subtracting the tide when it is above chart datum; D = d + h.
  • Mixing sound speed units, for example 1500 m/s with t in milliseconds.
  • Calling chart datum the sea surface; it is a fixed low-water reference.

Takeaways

  1. A total station is one instrument that measures angle and distance and turns them into coordinates, but only after prism constant, atmospheric correction and two-face collimation checks are applied.
  2. GNSS position comes from travel time: range = c × time, and four unknowns (x, y, z and receiver clock) need four satellites.
  3. Code pseudorange gives metre-level accuracy; carrier-phase RTK gives centimetre-level accuracy but needs a base station or correction service within about 10–20 km.
  4. DOP converts range error into position error, so good satellite spread and a clear sky matter more than the price of the receiver.
  5. LiDAR ranges directly by laser time of flight and produces a dense 3D point cloud day or night, limited by timing resolution, scan angle and the GNSS/IMU solution.
  6. Drone mapping is photogrammetry at scale: planned overlap plus known camera positions plus SfM produce an orthomosaic and DEM whose detail is fixed by the GSD.
  7. Setting out reverses surveying: compute bearing and distance from control to the design point, guide the operator to it, then check by an independent method.
  8. Under water and underground the same control logic applies, but sound replaces light and plumb wires or gyros replace line of sight.

ρi = √((xi − x)² + (yi − y)² + (zi − z)²) + c × δt, with satellite position from ephemeris and δt the receiver clock offset; Four unknowns x, y, z and δt, so four satellites are the minimum; Linearise by Taylor series about an approximate position and solve by least squares; Geometry matrix gives the covariance and hence DOP; Mention ionospheric and tropospheric delay, and how dual frequency or models reduce the ionospheric part; Accuracy statement: single point a few metres; RTK a few centimetres.

Apply Describe the procedure for a hydrographic survey of a reservoir, from horizontal control to the plotting of the bed contour map. 13 marks

Horizontal control along the bank by total station traverse or GNSS; mark ranges or use DGPS/RTK on the boat; Vertical control: gauge or tide staff, water level at the time of sounding; Echo sounder with transducer draft, bar check before and after the survey, sound velocity correction; Line spacing and run lines; record time, position and depth for each sounding; Reduce soundings to chart datum: reduced level = water level − depth; Plot soundings, draw bed contours, compute capacity by cross sections or by a surface model; list errors.

Apply For a topographic survey of a 200 hectare site you may use a total station, RTK GNSS or a drone. Compare the three for accuracy, production rate, cost and checking, and recommend one with reasons. 12 marks

Accuracy: total station 5 mm to 10 mm at the point, RTK about 20 mm to 30 mm horizontal, drone 20 mm to 50 mm with control points; Production: total station about 200 to 500 points a day, RTK 500 to 1500 points a day, drone 200 ha in a couple of hours of flying; Cost in rupees: capital, daily hire, processing software, and manpower; Limitation: total station needs line of sight and a helper, RTK fails under canopy, drone needs open sky and permission; Checks: known baseline for total station, repeated point and DOP for RTK, independent checkpoints for the drone; Recommend a drone flight with GNSS control points, with total station used for hidden and boundary detail.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare the error sources and the final accuracy of a total station closed traverse with an RTK GNSS control survey, and state which method should control the other and why. 13 marks

Total station errors: angular seconds accumulate with distance, EDM scale and atmospheric error parts per million, centering and target height; GNSS errors: orbit and clock, ionosphere, troposphere, multipath, DOP, antenna phase centre; Error propagation: traverse misclosure in mm against distance; GNSS relative accuracy of 10 mm + 1 ppm; Angular control must come from GNSS or a known azimuth because a traverse drifts in orientation; Total station is better in canopy, near structures and for short hidden lines; Practice: GNSS fixes the control points, total station fills detail and sets out; check both against a common baseline.

Evaluate Evaluate drone photogrammetry for computing earthwork volume of a road embankment against total station cross sections. Discuss GSD, surface extraction, vegetation and the acceptable tolerance. 13 marks

GSD at 100 m flying height with a 10 mm pixel and 8 mm lens is about 3 cm, so surface detail is at that order; Surface from SfM gives a dense point cloud but follows vegetation and can hide ground under canopy; Total station cross sections are sparse but accurate at the points measured; Volume error depends mainly on the ground model and the formation level, not on point density alone; Cross-check on a known test patch; a few per cent is realistic; Decide against the specification, usually 5 % for earthwork, and use the same datum and boundary for all methods.

Analyze Explain how horizontal control and azimuth are transferred underground through a vertical shaft, and analyse the errors and the checks used in tunnel surveying. 13 marks

Two plumb wires hung in the shaft, coordinates transferred by joining them to surface control, geometry of the triangle made as flat as possible; Azimuth transfer by gyro-theodolite, or by two shafts with a connecting traverse underground; Level transfer by tape and water level or by a deep staff; Errors: wire swing, refraction and air current in the shaft, small angle at the wires, gyro drift; Checks: closing the traverse between two shafts, comparing gyro and computed azimuth, repeat measurements on different days; Tolerance for tunnel breakthrough and the need for frequent check surveys.

Evaluate Evaluate the limits of GNSS in an urban site and under tree canopy, and discuss remedies and the regulatory limits on drone surveys in India. 12 marks

Multipath, signal blockage, high DOP and few satellites in an urban canyon; canopy attenuates and scatters the signal; Remedies: raise mask angle carefully, use multi-constellation and dual frequency, network RTK with a short baseline, total station for shadowed points, ground control points for drone work; Best practice: fix control in open sky and carry it by total station; Drone regulation in India: DGCA rules require registration, a remote pilot certificate, permitted zones and no-fly zones near airports and defence areas; Accuracy statement and check against a known point; State when the survey must be refused or replanned.

Glossary

Bathymetry
The measurement and charting of water depth and the shape of the water bed.
Carrier phase
The measured phase of the satellite carrier wave, tracked in cycles and used for high-precision relative positioning.
DEM
Digital Elevation Model, a grid of ground surface elevations with buildings and vegetation removed.
DOP
Dilution of Precision, a geometry factor that multiplies range error to give position error.
Echo sounder
A device that measures depth by timing a sound pulse sent to the bed and back.
EDM
An instrument that measures distance from the phase or travel time of a modulated signal sent to a reflector and back.
Electronic theodolite
A theodolite whose horizontal and vertical circle readings are produced electronically by encoders and shown on a display.
Ephemeris
The broadcast orbit parameters that let a receiver compute a satellite's position at a given time.
GNSS
Any global satellite navigation system that provides position and time from ranging signals, such as GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou or NavIC.
Ground control point
A point on the ground whose coordinates are known and which is identifiable in the images, used to georeference and check a survey.
GSD
Ground Sample Distance, the ground dimension represented by one pixel of an image.
Ionospheric delay
The extra signal delay caused by free electrons in the ionosphere, reduced by dual-frequency observation or by a model.
LiDAR
A ranging method that uses laser pulses and their two-way travel time to build a dense three-dimensional point cloud.
Mask angle
The elevation below which a receiver ignores satellites, usually 10° to 15°.
Orthomosaic
A seamless, geometrically corrected image map made by joining many photographs.
Overlap
The proportion of one photograph repeated in the adjacent photograph or strip, needed for matching and stereo.
Point cloud
A large set of measured points with three-dimensional coordinates describing a surface or object.
Prism constant
The fixed offset added to a measured distance to allow for the light path inside the reflector assembly.
Pseudorange
A range obtained from signal travel time multiplied by the speed of light, biased by clock and atmospheric errors.
Resection
Determining the occupied station's coordinates from measurements to known points, used in a free station setup.
RTK
Real-Time Kinematic positioning, in which carrier-phase corrections from a base station give centimetre accuracy in the field.
Setting out
The field operation of fixing on the ground a point or line whose position is already defined on a drawing.
Structure from motion
A computer-vision photogrammetric method that recovers camera positions and scene points from many overlapping images.
Total station
An integrated electronic theodolite, distance meter and computer that measures angles and distances and computes coordinates.
