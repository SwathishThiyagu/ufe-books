1.1 Principles and classification of surveying; plane and geodetic

Surveying finds the relative positions of points on or near the earth and draws them on a map or plan. It exists because a road, a plot boundary or a dam must be placed in correct relation to everything around it. Surveys are classified by purpose, by instrument, and by whether the earth’s curvature is included. This chapter fixes the vocabulary and the two working principles used in every later survey.

The object and two principles

Surveying is the art and science of determining the relative positions of points on or near the surface of the earth, and of representing them on a map or plan at a chosen scale. The object is not only to measure a distance but to fix a point in relation to a known framework.

Two principles govern field work. First, work from the whole to the part. Establish a broad, accurate control network first—a set of well-fixed reference points—then fill in details from that control. Errors stay small and local instead of accumulating across the site. Second, fix a new point by at least two independent measurements. One distance from one known point places the new point on a circle around that point. A second distance from another known point cuts that circle at two points; a rough sketch of the site, or a third measurement, decides which of the two is the real point. Two angles from two known points also fix it. Surveyors call fixing by distances trilateration. A single measurement is never enough for position.

Direct distance work is covered in chain and tape surveying and EDM.

Classify by purpose

Classification by purpose answers a simple question: what is the survey for?

  • Topographic surveying maps natural and man-made features and the relief of the ground. It supports route selection, drainage and site planning; the map is read with map scales and topographic sheets.
  • Cadastral surveying fixes land parcel boundaries, ownership and area. Revenue offices use it for land records and taxation.
  • Engineering surveying supplies design data and sets out works such as roads, bridges and pipelines during construction.
  • Control surveying builds the accurate framework of points on which other surveys depend.
  • Hydrographic surveying maps water bodies, depths and shores for navigation, dredging and flood work.
  • Astronomical surveying determines latitude, longitude or azimuth from stars; it is now rare in routine work but explains older control.

A project can need several classes at once. A highway may start with control, use topographic survey for alignment, cadastral survey for land, and engineering survey for setting out.

Classify by instrument

The instrument names the main quantity measured or the main tool used. The name also hints at what the survey can deliver: distances alone, bearings alone, angles with distances, or coordinates directly.

  • Chain or tape surveying measures distances directly and builds a framework of lines and offsets; it cannot measure angles well.
  • Compass surveying measures magnetic bearings of lines, so it handles traverses where angles are needed but precision is moderate.
  • Theodolite surveying measures horizontal and vertical angles precisely. It is the base of triangulation and tacheometry.
  • Total station surveying combines an electronic distance meter with angle measurement and computes coordinates in the field.
  • GNSS surveying (Global Navigation Satellite System) uses satellite signals to give position; it is fast for control and detail where sky view is open.

Modern classes such as UAV (drone) and LiDAR (laser scanning) surveying are now named separately; how they work is taught with modern surveying (total station, GNSS, LiDAR and drones). A surveyor chooses the simplest instrument that meets the required precision.

Plane and geodetic surveying

This split depends on one question: does the curvature of the earth matter at the precision you need?

In plane surveying, the earth is treated as flat. Horizontal distances and angles are taken on a plane, and ordinary plane geometry is used. This is valid for most engineering works: a building site, a town map, a canal alignment. A common working limit is an area up to about 250 km². It is a guide, not a law; the decision also depends on the precision required and the length of individual sights.

In geodetic surveying, the curvature is included. The earth is represented by a spheroid, a flattened ellipsoid that approximates the earth, and positions are computed on that curved surface. This is needed for large areas, long baselines, national control and precise maps. A 600 km baseline is geodetic work. Plane formulas would bend the answer by many metres.

For a sight of length d, the earth’s curvature lifts the far point by about:

c = d²/(2R)

c is the curvature correction in the same unit as d; d is the horizontal sight length; R is the mean earth radius, about 6371 km.

The correction grows with the square of the sight: about 0.08 m at 1 km, 1.96 m at 5 km and 7.85 m at 10 km, which no engineer can ignore. Datum details are in datums and coordinate systems.

Worked example

Question. A town area is 12 km × 8 km. (a) Is a plane survey acceptable? (b) For a 600 km long geodetic baseline, which survey is needed? (c) Estimate the curvature correction for a 10 km sight with R = 6371 km.

  1. Area = 12 × 8 = 96 km². 96 km² is below the working guide of about 250 km², so plane surveying is acceptable for the town map.
  2. 600 km is far beyond the plane limit and is a long baseline; geodetic surveying is needed.
  3. d = 10 km = 10,000 m; R = 6371 km = 6,371,000 m. c = d²/(2R) = (10,000)²/(2 × 6,371,000) m = 100,000,000/12,742,000 m = 7.848 m ≈ 7.85 m.

Answer. The town may be surveyed as a plane survey (96 km²); the 600 km baseline needs geodetic surveying; and the 10 km curvature correction is about 7.85 m.

Exam traps

  • Reversing the first principle: work from the whole to the part, not from the part to the whole. Starting with detail makes errors grow.
  • Treating the 250 km² limit as a legal rule. It is a guide; precision and sight length decide.
  • Mixing topographic and cadastral: topographic shows features and relief; cadastral shows parcel boundaries and ownership.
  • Putting d and R in different units in c = d²/(2R). Convert both to metres, or both to kilometres, before dividing.
  • Calling any large survey geodetic without checking curvature and precision; and calling any small survey plane when a long precise sight may still need curvature.

1.2 Measurements, errors, accuracy and precision

Every reading a surveyor takes is an approximation. The tape is a few millimetres long, the bubble sits slightly off centre, the observer reads 4.62 where the mark is at 4.63. This chapter names those imperfections, measures them, and shows how to report a result honestly. You will learn what error and correction mean, why errors are gross, systematic or random, and why accuracy and precision are two different things.

You need principles and classification of surveying; plane and geodetic for what a survey measures and when earth curvature matters.

True value, error and correction

The true value is the quantity as it really is: the distance between two pegs, the angle between two lines. Nobody knows it exactly. We adopt a value from a higher-order survey or a calibration certificate and call that the true value X. The observed value x is what the instrument or the surveyor gives.

e = x − X

e is the error, in the same unit as the measurement; x is the observed value; X is the true or accepted value.

A tape reads 100.05 m on a line whose true length is 100.00 m. The error is +0.05 m: the reading is larger than the truth, so the tape is shorter than the length marked on it and it over-reads. A tape that is too long gives readings smaller than the truth.

c = −e = X − x

c is the correction, added to the observed value; e is the error. Here c = −0.05 m.

Sign matters more than anything else here. An error of +0.05 m and a correction of +0.05 m are opposite instructions; using the wrong one puts the answer twice as far from the truth as doing nothing. Tape corrections are built in chain and tape surveying; the accuracy statement of an EDM is in electronic distance measurement (EDM).

Report only the significant figures the instrument supports. A tape read to the nearest centimetre gives 100.03 m, not 100.0300 m.

Gross, systematic and random errors

Gross errors, or blunders, are mistakes: reading 4.62 as 4.92, booking a backsight in the foresight column, dropping a whole 30 m tape length. They are large, one-off and unpredictable, and statistics cannot find them. Only checks can: measure from both ends, add the parts and compare with the whole, keep a second observer. A suspected blunder is rejected and the measurement repeated.

Systematic errors repeat with the same sign and about the same size under the same conditions. They push every reading the same way, so they bias the result. A steel tape standardised at 20 °C and used at 38 °C reads short on every span of the day. They come in three groups.

  • Instrumental: the tape is too long or too short, its zero mark is worn, an EDM carries a constant offset, a level bubble is out of adjustment.
  • Personal: the eye is not square to the scale (parallax), the bubble is judged centred when it is not, one observer always reads a little late.
  • Natural: temperature, humidity and pressure of the air, refraction, and wind.

Because the sign and size repeat, averaging does not remove a systematic error. It must be measured by standardising the tape against a known baseline and taken out by a correction: standardisation, temperature, tension, sag, slope and alignment for taping; constant plus parts per million for an EDM.

Random errors are the small scatter in any reading: the bubble never sits in exactly the same place, the pull on the tape is not steady, the mark is estimated to the nearest half millimetre. Their sign and size cannot be predicted, but over many readings they partly cancel — repeating is the cure for random error and no cure at all for bias.

Accuracy, precision and repeated readings

Accuracy is how close a measured value, or the mean of a set, lies to the true value. Precision is how close repeated readings lie to one another. Accuracy is about bias; precision is about scatter.

Readings of 100.02, 100.04, 100.03, 100.05 and 100.01 m on a line of true length 100.00 m are precise: they span 4 cm from lowest to highest and their standard deviation is about 1.6 cm. They are not accurate: every one lies above the truth, a systematic error of about +0.03 m. A single reading of 100.00 m from a carelessly held tape is accurate by luck and not precise at all. Four combinations exist: accurate and precise (the goal), precise but not accurate (calibrate and correct), accurate but not precise (repeat and average), neither (start again).

Three numbers describe a set of n repeated readings x₁ to xₙ.

x̄ = (x₁ + x₂ + … + xₙ) / n

x̄ (x-bar) is the mean, in m; n is the number of readings.

dᵢ = xᵢ − x̄

dᵢ is the deviation of reading i from the mean, in m. The deviations of a set always add to zero.

s = √( Σdᵢ² / (n − 1) )

s is the sample standard deviation, in m; Σdᵢ² is the sum of squared deviations, in m²; n − 1 is used because the mean already used one piece of information. The uncertainty of the mean is about s/√n, so four times as many readings halve it.

Results built from measurements carry their errors along. For a sum or difference the absolute errors combine in quadrature; for a product or quotient the relative errors do.

S = a ± b:  eS = √(ea² + eb²)    P = a × b or a ÷ b:  eP/P = √((ea/a)² + (eb/b)²)

ea and eb are the errors of a and b; eS is the error of the sum; eP is the error of the product. Both assume the errors are independent and random.

Worked example

Question. A distance is measured five times: 100.02, 100.04, 100.03, 100.05 and 100.01 m. The true value is 100.00 m. Find the mean, the error of the mean, and the sample standard deviation. State whether the set is accurate, precise, or both.

  1. Sum the readings: 100.02 + 100.04 + 100.03 + 100.05 + 100.01 = 500.15 m, so x̄ = 500.15 / 5 = 100.03 m.
  2. Error of the mean: e = 100.03 − 100.00 = +0.03 m, so the correction is c = −0.03 m.
  3. Deviations from the mean: −0.01, +0.01, 0.00, +0.02, −0.02 m. They add to zero, as a check. Their squares: 0.0001, 0.0001, 0.0000, 0.0004, 0.0004 m², so Σdᵢ² = 0.0010 m².
  4. s² = 0.0010 / (5 − 1) = 0.00025 m², so s = √0.00025 = 0.016 m.
  5. Uncertainty of the mean: s/√n = 0.016 / √5 = 0.007 m. The bias of 0.03 m is about four times this, so the set is precise but not accurate: a systematic error dominates.

Answer. Mean = 100.03 m, error of the mean = +0.03 m (correction −0.03 m), sample standard deviation = 0.016 m; the set is precise but not accurate.

Exam traps

  • Correction is the negative of the error. Adding the error instead of subtracting it doubles the mistake — a favourite one-mark deduction.
  • Low scatter is not proof of correctness: in the worked example the readings are tight but the mean is 30 mm high.
  • Averaging kills random error, not systematic error. More readings shrink s/√n and leave the bias untouched.
  • Dividing Σdᵢ² by n instead of n − 1 makes s too small; and the squares are in m², so the standard deviation comes back in m.
  • A blunder is rejected and re-measured, never averaged in.

1.3 Scales, maps and topographic sheets

A map is a drawing of the ground pulled down to fit on a sheet of paper. The shrinking factor is the scale, written as the ratio of a distance on the map to the same distance on the ground. Without a scale a map is only a sketch. With one, a ruler on the sheet gives real distances in metres and kilometres. Engineers read it on topographic sheets, village plans and highway drawings, and everyone meets it on a phone map, where the bar at the corner changes as you zoom.

You need principles and classification of surveying; plane and geodetic for what a survey produces, and measurements, errors, accuracy and precision for why every measured length carries error.

Scale is a ratio, not a length

Scale compares a length on the paper with the same length on the ground. Write it as a fraction: one unit on the map stands for M units on the ground.

RF = map distance ÷ ground distance = 1 / M

RF is the representative fraction, a pure number; map distance is the length measured on the sheet; ground distance is the horizontal length on the ground in the same unit; M is the denominator, the number of ground units that one map unit represents.

These are horizontal distances. A road that climbs 100 m while running 1 km along the slope has a horizontal length of about 995 m, and 995 m is what the map shows. Plans record horizontal positions, never slope lengths.

The two lengths are in the same unit, so the units cancel and an RF carries no unit at all. A scale of 1:25,000 means 1 cm on the map is 25,000 cm on the ground, which is 250 m. It does not mean 25,000 m.

Large scale means a large fraction. 1:1,000 is larger than 1:50,000, because 1/1000 is greater than 1/50,000. A large-scale map covers a small area with much detail; a small-scale map covers a district or a state with little detail.

Drawing the scale on the sheet

A printed RF is useless to a reader holding only a divider. So a map also carries a drawn bar, built to the same ratio, which can be measured against directly.

  1. Fix the RF and the longest ground distance the bar must read, say 5 km.
  2. Length of scale = RF × that distance. For RF 1:50,000 and 5 km: 5 × 100,000 ÷ 50,000 = 10 cm.
  3. Draw a line 10 cm long and place zero a little in from the left end.
  4. Divide the part to the right of zero into equal major divisions; divide the single left division into 10 sub-divisions.
  5. Label the major divisions outwards from zero and name the units.

That is a plain scale. It reads to one sub-division, usually a tenth of a major division.

A diagonal scale reads finer. Ten parallel lines span the height of one sub-division, and a diagonal across them splits that sub-division into ten equal parts by similar triangles. A plain scale reads 1/10 of a major division; a diagonal scale reads 1/100 of a major division, that is 1/10 of a sub-division.

A vernier scale reaches the same fineness with a short auxiliary scale drawn beside the main one. The vernier division that lines up with a main division gives the fraction directly, which saves space on a crowded sheet.

A graphical, or comparative, scale is a bar drawn for two unit systems, kilometres below and miles above, so a reader switches units without arithmetic. It also survives copying: if the sheet is photographed and shrunk, a printed RF becomes wrong, but the bar shrinks with the drawing and stays true.

Reading a topographic sheet

A topographic sheet shows relief, drainage, transport and settlement, and its margin carries the information needed to read the body of the map.

  • Title: the sheet name and its number in the national index.
  • Scale: the RF together with a drawn bar.
  • Contour interval: the vertical step between neighbouring contours. Contours are only a layer here; reading them is taught with levelling and contouring.
  • Grid: reference lines printed on the sheet with ticks in the margin, so any point can be quoted as a grid reference.
  • North direction: true, magnetic and grid north on one diagram, with the magnetic declination for the year of the edition.
  • Legend, edition, year of survey, and the datum to which spot heights and benchmarks refer.

Sheet numbering follows a hierarchy. A large index sheet is cut into smaller sheets, each cut again, and every piece gets a letter or number, so the full code of a sheet says where in the country it lies. The margin usually lists the numbers of the adjoining sheets too.

The scale decides what the sheet can answer. A 1:1,000 plan of a campus shows a footpath; a 1:50,000 sheet shows the highway crossing the district. Flood and landslide maps are drawn on the same kind of sheet, and their worth depends on the scale being stated, because the area of the zone is scaled off the paper with a planimeter or a grid.

Worked example

Question. Two points are 6.4 cm apart on a map whose representative fraction is 1:25,000. (a) Find the horizontal distance between them on the ground, in km. (b) A road is 3.5 km long on the ground. Find its length on the same map, in cm.

  1. RF = 1/25,000, so ground distance = map distance × 25,000, with both lengths in the same unit.
  2. (a) Ground distance = 6.4 cm × 25,000 = 160,000 cm.
  3. Convert: 160,000 cm ÷ 100 = 1,600 m; 1,600 m ÷ 1,000 = 1.6 km.
  4. (b) Convert the road to centimetres first: 3.5 km = 3.5 × 100,000 = 350,000 cm.
  5. Map length = 350,000 cm ÷ 25,000 = 14 cm.

Answer. (a) The points are 1.6 km apart on the ground; (b) the road is 14 cm long on the map.

Exam traps

  • Writing 1 cm = 25,000 m for RF 1:25,000. It is 25,000 cm, that is 250 m; the denominator counts the same unit as the map length.
  • Calling 1:50,000 a large scale because 50,000 is a big number. The larger the denominator, the smaller the scale.
  • Using a slope length as if it were horizontal. Maps carry horizontal distances, so a slope distance measured on the ground and entered as a map length overestimates the plan length. Reduce slope to horizontal before applying the RF.
  • Mixing kilometres and centimetres in one line. Put both lengths in the same unit before multiplying or dividing by M; the order of converting and computing does not matter, the matching of units does.
  • Using the printed RF on an enlarged or reduced photocopy. The copy has a new scale, so the printed RF is now wrong; the drawn bar grew or shrank with the sheet and still reads true.

1.4 Datums and coordinate systems

A datum is the reference surface and origin that gives coordinates meaning. Latitude, longitude and height are numbers until you say which surface they are measured from. A datum names the ellipsoid, how it sits on the earth, and the zero for heights. Surveyors, map makers and GNSS users rely on datums to combine measurements taken on different days and by different instruments. Without a datum, a coordinate is just a pair of numbers; with one, it points to a place on the ground.

You need: principles and classification of surveying; plane and geodetic for why earth curvature matters, and scales, maps and topographic sheets for how map positions are shown.

Geoid, ellipsoid and datum

The geoid is the equipotential surface of earth's gravity field that best fits mean sea level, continued under the continents. Mean sea level is the average sea surface height recorded by tide gauges over a long period; it is not a flat plane. The geoid is lumpy because mass inside the earth is uneven: mountains, ocean trenches and dense rock pull the surface up or down by tens of metres.

An ellipsoid is a smooth mathematical surface made by rotating an ellipse about its short axis. It is fixed by two numbers: the semi-major axis a (equatorial radius) and the flattening f. The ellipsoid is simple enough for formulas, so survey computations use it instead of the geoid.

A datum pairs an ellipsoid with a position and orientation on the earth. A horizontal datum fixes latitude and longitude; a vertical datum fixes heights. WGS84 is a global datum used by GNSS. The older Indian datum, built on the Everest ellipsoid with its origin at Kalianpur, is a local example; the newer open series topographic maps of India are on WGS84. A datum is not a coordinate system; it is the surface that a coordinate system refers to.

Heights, benchmarks and orthometric height

Orthometric height H is the height of a point above the geoid, measured along the plumb line (the local vertical). It is the height that water levels and construction work use. A benchmark is a permanent mark, often a brass plate on a wall or pillar, whose reduced level (height above the vertical datum) is known from levelling. In India the benchmarks of the national levelling network carry reduced levels above mean sea level, and the spot heights on topographic sheets use the same vertical datum. How a level is carried from a benchmark, booked and reduced is taught with levelling.

GNSS receivers give ellipsoidal height h, measured above the ellipsoid. The geoid is not the ellipsoid, so the two heights differ by the geoid undulation N: the height of the geoid above the ellipsoid at that point.

H = h − N

H is orthometric height in metres; h is ellipsoidal height in metres; N is geoid undulation in metres, positive when the geoid lies above the ellipsoid.

If N is negative, the geoid lies below the ellipsoid and H is larger than h. A level survey and a GNSS survey at the same point will not agree until this correction is applied.

Geographic, projected and Cartesian coordinates

Geographic coordinates give a point by latitude φ, longitude λ (angle east or west of the prime meridian, which is 0° longitude) and a height. Latitude φ is the angle, measured at the point, between the normal to the ellipsoid and the equatorial plane, positive north; this is geodetic latitude, the latitude a map or GNSS receiver reports on that datum. Latitude and longitude need a horizontal datum; height needs a vertical datum. A point at 13.08° N, 80.27° E is a geographic coordinate, not a distance on the ground.

Projected coordinates flatten the ellipsoid onto a map plane using a map projection. The Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) system divides the earth into 60 zones, each 6° of longitude wide, and gives easting and northing in metres. Easting is measured from the zone's central meridian with a false easting of 500 000 m added, so a point on the central meridian has easting 500 000 m, points to the west have less, points to the east have more, and every easting in the zone stays positive. Northing is measured from the equator, with a false northing of 10 000 000 m added in the southern hemisphere so northings stay positive there. UTM coordinates must state the zone and its hemisphere letter, for example 44N (zone 44, northern hemisphere), which is not the same thing as latitude 44° N. The mathematics of projections is taught with map projections in GIS. To find the zone of a longitude λ east, take zone = whole part of (λ + 180)/6, plus 1; its central meridian is 6 × zone − 183 degrees.

Cartesian coordinates use three perpendicular axes. Earth-centred, earth-fixed (ECEF) coordinates X, Y, Z have their origin at the earth's centre; the Z axis points near the north pole. GNSS computes in ECEF and then converts to latitude, longitude and height. When two sources use different datums, a datum transformation converts coordinates from the first datum to the second before they are mixed. Without that step, a shift of many metres can appear.

Worked example

Question. A GNSS receiver on a survey pillar records ellipsoidal height h = 52.3 m. A geoid model gives the geoid undulation there as N = −82.0 m. (a) Find the orthometric height H. (b) A second point is reported as 13.08° N, 80.27° E on WGS84. Name the type of coordinates, find its UTM zone and central meridian, and say whether its easting is more or less than 500 000 m.

  1. Write the height relation: H = h − N.
  2. Substitute: H = 52.3 − (−82.0). Subtracting a negative adds, so H = 52.3 + 82.0 = 134.3 m. The geoid lies 82.0 m below the ellipsoid here, so the point is higher above the geoid than above the ellipsoid.
  3. (b) Latitude and longitude on a stated datum are geographic coordinates.
  4. UTM zone = whole part of (80.27 + 180)/6, plus 1 = whole part of 43.38, plus 1 = 44. Central meridian = 6 × 44 − 183 = 81° E.
  5. The point is 81 − 80.27 = 0.73° west of the central meridian, so its easting is less than the false easting: below 500 000 m (it is still positive).

Answer. H = 134.3 m. The second point is in geographic coordinates; it lies in UTM zone 44N, central meridian 81° E, and its easting is less than 500 000 m because it is west of that meridian.

Exam traps

  • Sign of N: use H = h − N, never H = h + N. With N = −82.0 m and h = 52.3 m the wrong formula gives H = −29.7 m, a point below mean sea level, while the correct one gives H = 134.3 m. A negative N increases H.
  • Ellipsoidal height h and orthometric height H are not the same. h comes from GNSS; H is the height above the geoid.
  • Latitude and longitude need a horizontal datum; a height needs a vertical datum. A coordinate pair alone does not fix a height.
  • UTM easting and northing are meaningless without the zone and hemisphere.
  • When mixing coordinates from two datums, transform first. Do not average raw coordinates from WGS84 and a local datum.

1.5 Chain and tape surveying

Chain and tape surveying fixes points by measuring straight distances with a chain or steel tape, then locates detail by a short offset from those lines. It needs no angle instrument — only a tape, ranging rods, arrows and a cross-staff — so it is cheap, quick and easy to check. The result is a skeleton of lines: a base line, tie lines and check lines. It suits house plots and small sites, and gives a first pass before a total station or EDM takes over.

You need: measurements, errors, accuracy and precision for error types and scales, maps and topographic sheets for how field distances become a map.

Instruments and ranging

A chain is 100 or 150 links of galvanised steel wire, each link 0.2 m, with a brass handle at each end; a metric chain is 20 m or 30 m. A tape is a continuous strip — steel for routine work (10–50 m), invar where a base line must be precise because invar barely expands with heat, linen for rough work.

  • Ranging rods: 2 m or 3 m poles painted in 0.2 m red and white bands, so a station can be sighted far away.
  • Arrows (chain pins): 400 mm steel pins, ten to a set, dropped at every chain length.
  • Cross-staff: a staff with two sighting slits at right angles, for setting out a perpendicular from the chain line.
  • Optical square: two mirrors fixed at 45°, doing the same job with better accuracy.
  • Pegs and plumb bob: transfer a point from tape to ground.

Ranging is fixing intermediate points on the straight line between two stations. On level ground with both ends visible, one person stands behind a station and sights along the line, signalling the rodman to move left or right until the rod hides the far station. That is direct ranging. When the ends cannot see each other, two rodmen use indirect or reciprocal ranging: each can see one end station and lines in the other's rod towards it, and they repeat this in turns until both rods sit on the straight line between the two stations. On sloping ground the tape is held horizontal and stepped down in short lengths, or the slope length is measured and corrected (see below).

Base line, tie line, check line and offsets

Reconnaissance comes first: walk the site, note buildings, trees and the road, and sketch an index map showing the stations. Three kinds of line then build the framework.

  • Base line: longest line, through the middle on flattest ground, measured with greatest care because every other length is tied to it.
  • Tie line (subsidiary line): joins a survey station to a point on a main line, turning one long offset into two short ones.
  • Check line: joins two points already fixed. Its length can be computed from the triangles and also measured; the difference is the error of the whole job.

An offset is a short measurement from the chain line out to an object — fence corner, tree, manhole. Book two numbers: chainage (distance along the line from the start) and offset length. A perpendicular offset is at 90°, set with a cross-staff or optical square. An oblique offset is at any angle, used when a right angle is awkward. Keep offsets short. The longer the offset, the more a small error in setting the right angle moves the plotted point; the limiting length of an offset is the one at which that shift would just show on the plan, about 0.25 mm at the plotting scale.

  1. Open field book so ruled page faces you; vertical line divides chainage from offset.
  2. Enter chainage left of line and offset length right, same row.
  3. Work up the page from bottom, chainage increasing.
  4. Never erase: strike out a wrong entry and rewrite it, so the original survives.

Tape corrections

Every tape has built-in error, and field conditions differ from the day it was standardized. Write correction c as the amount added to measured length M to get true length: true = M + c. Some books define c as measured − true, and then every sign flips; fix the convention first.

c = (L′ − L) / L × M

standardization: L′ true length between end marks (m), L nominal length (30 m), M measured length (m). Tape too long gives positive c.

c = M α (T − T₀)

temperature: α is the coefficient of expansion, about 11 to 12 × 10⁻⁶ per °C for steel (use the value the question gives), T field temperature (°C), T₀ standardization temperature (°C). Hotter than T₀ means tape expanded and reading is short, so c is positive.

c = (P − P₀) M / (A E)

tension: P applied pull (N), P₀ standard pull (N), A tape cross-section (mm²), E Young's modulus, 2.0 × 10⁵ N/mm² for steel. Harder pull stretches tape, reading short, c positive.

c = − w² l³ / (24 P²)

sag, for one span: w weight per metre (N/m), l unsupported span (m), P pull (N). For n equal spans multiply by n. A sagging tape bows below straight line and reads long, so c is negative.

c = √(M² − h²) − M

slope and alignment: h level difference between ends (m), or distance tape is held off the line (m). Measured value is always longer, so c negative. Total correction is sum of all terms.

Worked example

Question. A 30 m steel tape was standardized at 20 °C. It is used at 35 °C to measure a line, and the reading is 30.000 m. The coefficient of thermal expansion of steel is 11.2 × 10⁻⁶ per °C. (a) Find the temperature correction and the true length. (b) A separate slope measurement between two stations is 20.000 m along the slope, with a level difference of 1.200 m between the ends. Find the horizontal distance and the slope correction.

  1. Temperature difference: ΔT = 35 − 20 = 15 °C.
  2. Temperature correction: ct = M α ΔT = 30.000 × 11.2 × 10⁻⁶ × 15 = 0.00504 m = +5.04 mm.
  3. True length = M + ct = 30.000 + 0.00504 = 30.00504 m ≈ 30.005 m.
  4. Horizontal distance = √(M² − h²) = √(20.000² − 1.200²) = √(400.000 − 1.440) = √398.560 = 19.964 m.
  5. Slope correction = horizontal − measured = 19.964 − 20.000 = −0.036 m.

Answer. Temperature correction = +5.04 mm, giving a true length of 30.005 m; the horizontal distance is 19.964 m, a slope correction of −0.036 m.

Exam traps

  • Sign convention: c = true − measured here. Books using measured − true flip every sign; state your convention before adding.
  • In the standardisation, temperature and tension formulas M is the measured length of the whole line, not the 30 m tape. In the sag formula l is one unsupported span: a 30 m tape supported every 10 m has three spans with l = 10 m, so the sag correction is 3 × w²(10)³/(24P²).
  • Units in tension formula: pull N, area mm², E N/mm². Mixing mm² with m² throws answer out by 10⁶.
  • Slope and alignment corrections are always negative; slope or off-line distance is longer than horizontal.
  • If check line disagrees, re-measure; never stretch offsets to close the figure.

1.6 Electronic distance measurement (EDM)

EDM measures a distance by sending an electromagnetic wave to a target and comparing either travel time or phase with the returning wave. Tape is slow and unsafe over long or busy ground; EDM gives a fast electronic reading that is stored and processed. Surveyors use it for control, setting out, boundaries, roads and construction. Accuracy is quoted as a constant part plus a part per million of the distance, so long lines have larger uncertainty.

You need: measurements, errors, accuracy and precision and chain and tape surveying.

Pulse and phase measurement

An electromagnetic wave travels through air at speed v, close to 3×10⁸ m/s. A pulse EDM sends a short burst, starts a clock, receives the echo and measures total travel time Δt. The one-way distance is half the path length.

d = (v Δt) / 2

d is the one-way distance in m; v is the wave speed in air in m/s; Δt is the total there-and-back travel time in s.

Most survey EDM uses phase difference. The transmitter sends a continuous wave modulated at frequency f. The returning wave has phase lag Δφ. The instrument measures only the fractional cycle. It repeats at several frequencies to find N, the whole number of cycles.

d = (λ / 2)(N + Δφ / (2π))

d is the one-way distance; λ = v/f is the modulation wavelength in air; N is the whole number of cycles in the double path; Δφ is the phase difference in radians.

λ = v/f is the modulation wavelength in air. Fine frequency gives resolution; coarse frequency gives the metre range. One frequency alone cannot give an unambiguous long distance. Pulse and phase both need the wave speed in air, so atmospheric correction applies to both.

Range, accuracy and accessories

EDM instruments are also grouped by the carrier wave. Microwave instruments carry the signal on radio waves; they reach tens of kilometres and need a second active unit at the far end, not a prism. Electro-optical instruments use infrared or visible light returned by a glass prism; almost all total stations are of this kind. A laser carrier also lets the instrument measure without a prism, from the faint diffuse return of a wall or rock.

Typical prism EDM range is a few hundred metres to several kilometres. Reflectorless EDM reflects from the object surface and works from a few metres to a few hundred metres; range depends on colour and roughness. Display resolution is often 1 mm or 0.1 mm; resolution is not accuracy.

σd (mm) = ±(a (mm) + b (ppm) × d (km))

σd is the expected error in mm; a is the constant part in mm; b is the distance-dependent part in ppm; d is the measured distance in km.

The constant part covers instrument zero error and phase resolution. Centring adds a separate field error. The ppm part covers atmospheric and frequency uncertainty. For ±(3 mm + 3 ppm), at 1 km the ppm part is 3 mm; the total is 6 mm.

  • Prism: a retroreflector returns the beam parallel; its prism constant is a small offset, such as 0 or −30 mm, entered in the instrument.
  • Tribrach: levels and centres the instrument over the station with an optical plummet.
  • Thermometer and barometer: give temperature and pressure for atmospheric correction.
  • Target plate: helps aim in reflectorless mode.
  • EDM without a prism: uses diffuse reflection; no prism constant, shorter range, surface-dependent accuracy.

Operation and main errors

Operation: set the instrument on the station with a tribrach and level it; set the prism on the target station; enter prism constant, temperature and pressure; aim at the prism; press measure; record slope distance. Reduce the slope distance S to horizontal with the vertical angle α read at the same time: horizontal distance = S cos α. A total station is EDM plus electronic angle measurement; its parts and errors are taught with the total station in modern surveying.

Prism constant error: a wrong constant shifts every distance by the same amount. Atmospheric error: wave speed depends on air density, so temperature and pressure mistakes produce ppm errors. Cyclic error: a small periodic error with phase, often a few millimetres, from signal leakage. Centring error: instrument or target not over the mark; random, and larger in effect on short lines. Frequency error and resolution also contribute.

The worked example below combines the constant and ppm parts. In field practice, record temperature and pressure at the instrument, not hours later. Check the prism constant whenever the prism type changes; a 30 mm mistake is impossible to miss on a short line but easy to blame on the instrument.

Worked example

Question. An EDM has accuracy ±(3 mm + 3 ppm). It measures a slope distance of 850.000 m. Find the expected error and express the distance with its uncertainty.

  1. Write the distance in km and in mm: 850.000 m = 0.850 km = 850000 mm.
  2. Find the distance-dependent part in mm: b × d = 3 ppm × 0.850 km = 3 × 0.850 = 2.55 mm. Equivalently, 3×10⁻⁶ × 850000 mm = 2.55 mm.
  3. The constant part is a = 3 mm.
  4. Add the two parts: total error = 3 mm + 2.55 mm = 5.55 mm, quoted as 5.6 mm.
  5. Convert to metres for the uncertainty: 5.55 mm = 0.00555 m ≈ 0.006 m.

Answer. The distance part is 2.55 mm and the total expected error is 5.55 mm, quoted as 5.6 mm. The distance is 850.000 m ± 0.006 m.

Worked example 2

Question. A phase EDM measures with two modulation frequencies. The coarse one has λ/2 = 1000 m and gives a phase difference of 152.3°; the fine one has λ/2 = 10 m and gives 110.52°. (a) Find the distance. (b) The prism constant of −30 mm was not entered; correct the distance. (c) The vertical angle to the prism is +3°00′. Find the horizontal distance.

  1. Coarse distance: 1000 × 152.3/360 = 1000 × 0.4231 = 423.1 m. It is good only to about a metre, but it has no ambiguity within 1000 m.
  2. Fine fraction: 110.52/360 = 0.3070, so the fine reading is 10 × 0.3070 = 3.070 m past a whole number of 10 m units.
  3. Whole number of fine units: N = (423.1 − 3.07)/10 = 42.0, so N = 42.
  4. Distance: d = (λ/2)(N + Δφ/2π) = 10 × (42 + 0.3070) = 423.070 m.
  5. Prism constant: add −0.030 m, so the slope distance is 423.070 − 0.030 = 423.040 m.
  6. Horizontal distance: 423.040 × cos 3° = 423.040 × 0.99863 = 422.460 m.

Answer. The slope distance is 423.070 m as read, 423.040 m after the prism constant, and the horizontal distance is 422.460 m.

Exam traps

  • ppm is parts per million, not millimetres per metre. 3 ppm is 3 mm per km, not 3 mm per m.
  • Add the constant part and the ppm part after scaling. Do not multiply 3 ppm by the constant 3 mm.
  • The prism constant has a sign and differs by prism type. A wrong value shifts every distance by the same amount.
  • EDM measures slope distance. Without a vertical angle reduction it is not horizontal distance.
  • Reflectorless mode is short-range and surface-dependent; it does not remove atmospheric correction or give the same accuracy as a prism.

Understand State the two basic principles of surveying and explain why surveying proceeds from the whole to the part. 10 marks

Model answer.

  1. What surveying must achieve. Surveying fixes the relative positions of points and draws them to scale. Two working rules make this possible with ordinary instruments: work from the whole to the part, and fix every new point by at least two independent measurements from points already fixed.
  2. First principle: whole to part. First fix a few widely spaced control stations with the best method and the most care (a closed traverse, a triangulation net or GNSS), and check that the frame closes. Then fix secondary stations from the frame, and only then pick up detail (buildings, fences, trees) from the nearest stations by short offsets or short radial shots.
  3. Why the order matters: errors stay local. Suppose a 2 km boundary is built from twenty 100 m lines laid one after another, each with a random error of ±0.02 m. The errors add in quadrature, so the far end is uncertain by about √20 × 0.02 m, and every bearing error swings the line further. Worked from a control frame, each short line is held between two fixed points and its error cannot travel past them.
    part to whole:  end error ≈ √n × e = √20 × 0.02 = 0.089 m (and growing with every extra line)
whole to part:  error of a detail point ≈ error of one short line ≈ 0.02 m
  4. The frame also gives checks. A closed traverse must close; a check line measured across a chain-survey triangle must agree with the plotted length. A detail survey that starts from the parts has nothing to close on, so a blunder spreads unseen through everything that follows.
  5. Second principle: two independent measurements. From two fixed points A and B, a new point P can be fixed by two distances (trilateration, chain triangles), a chainage and a perpendicular offset, two angles (intersection), an angle and a distance (radiation, as a total station does), or two oblique offsets (tie measurements). Worked case: A and B are 50 m apart, AP = 30 m and BP = 40 m.

Understand Explain what a datum is. Describe the geoid, the reference ellipsoid and the relation between ellipsoidal height, orthometric height and geoid undulation. 12 marks

Points to cover. Datum = reference surface + origin + orientation + (for modern datums) an ellipsoid; Geoid: mean sea level surface extended under land; ellipsoid: mathematical approximation; h = H + N, define each symbol and its units; Why a coordinate without a datum is meaningless; mention WGS84 as a geocentric datum.

Model answer.

  1. Datum. A datum is the reference against which positions and heights are given: a reference surface, its size and shape, and how it is placed and oriented on the earth (origin and axes). A horizontal datum carries latitude and longitude; a vertical datum carries heights.
  2. Geoid. The geoid is the equipotential surface of the earth's gravity that best fits mean sea level, continued under the land. Water does not flow along it, and a levelling instrument's bubble is set perpendicular to it. It is uneven, rising and falling by tens of metres because the mass inside the earth is uneven.
  3. Reference ellipsoid. An ellipsoid of revolution, defined by its semi-major axis a and flattening f, is smooth enough for formulas. WGS84, the datum of GPS, uses a = 6 378 137 m and f = 1/298.257 223 563. A local datum such as the older Indian datum fits an ellipsoid (Everest) closely to one region instead of to the whole earth.
  4. Three heights. h, the ellipsoidal height, is measured from the ellipsoid along its normal (what GNSS gives). H, the orthometric height, is measured from the geoid along the plumb line (what levelling gives). N, the geoid undulation, is the height of the geoid above the ellipsoid. They are related by h = H + N, so H = h − N, all in metres.
  5. Worked case. A GNSS receiver gives h = 52.3 m where a geoid model gives N = −82.0 m. A second point has h = 100.0 m and N = +30.0 m.
    point 1:  H = h − N = 52.3 − (−82.0) = 134.3 m   (geoid below the ellipsoid: H > h)
point 2:  H = h − N = 100.0 − 30.0  = 70.0 m    (geoid above the ellipsoid: H < h)
  6. Why a coordinate needs its datum. The same pillar has different latitude and longitude on WGS84 and on a local datum, and different heights above the ellipsoid and above the geoid. Coordinates from two datums are transformed to one before they are combined; heights from GNSS are turned into levelled heights with a geoid model.
  7. The ellipsoid in numbers. From a and f the semi-minor axis and the eccentricity follow:
    b = a (1 − f) = 6 378 137 × (1 − 1/298.257 223 563) = 6 356 752.3 m
e² = 2f − f² = 0.006 694 38
so the polar radius is 21.4 km shorter than the equatorial radius
  8. Vertical datum. Orthometric heights start from mean sea level found from years of tide gauge records and carried inland by precise levelling to a network of benchmarks. A height quoted "above mean sea level" refers to that datum; a GNSS height above the ellipsoid does not, until N is subtracted.
  9. Height differences over a site. Over a few kilometres N changes slowly, so GNSS height differences are almost levelled differences, but not quite. Two points 2 km apart:
    point A: h = 52.3 m, N = −82.0 m → H = 134.3 m
point B: h = 54.1 m, N = −82.3 m → H = 136.4 m
Δh = 1.8 m, but ΔH = 2.1 m: ignoring the 0.3 m change in N gives a 0.3 m error in the rise
  10. Check. Substitute back: H + N = 134.3 + (−82.0) = 52.3 m = h, and 70.0 + 30.0 = 100.0 m = h. The relation holds for both signs of N.

    Too shortreads too much: correction negativelays out too little: add tape lengths
    Too longreads too little: correction positivelays out too much: take tape lengths off
  9. Setting out with the same tape. To set out a true 1200 m at 35 °C with this tape, each tape length really covers 29.985 04 m, so the reading to lay out is larger:
    reading = 1200 × 30 / 29.985 04 = 1200.60 m
  10. Areas with a wrong tape. Every length is wrong by the same factor, so an area is wrong by the factor squared. A plot found to be 4.00 ha with the short tape at its standard temperature is really
    4.00 × (29.98/30)² = 4.00 × 0.998 667 = 3.995 ha
  11. Size of the net correction. −0.800 + 0.2016 = −0.5984 m in 1200 m is 1 in 2005. Left out, it would be a systematic error of 0.6 m that no amount of repetition would show.
  12. The value of α. 11.2 × 10⁻⁶ per °C is the usual figure for a steel tape (11 to 12 × 10⁻⁶); an invar tape has about one thirtieth of it and is used on base lines where temperature cannot be measured well.
  13. Check. Work it the other way: the tape's real length at 35 °C is (30 − 0.02) × (1 + 11.2 × 10⁻⁶ × 15) = 29.98 × 1.000168 = 29.985 04 m. Forty such lengths are 40 × 29.985 04 = 1199.40 m, the same answer.

Analyze Compare chain and tape surveying with EDM and total station work for a topographic survey of a 5 hectare site. Judge which you would choose and justify. 14 marks

Points to cover. Chain and tape: cheap, no power, accurate for short distances, slow, limited by ground and obstructions, errors accumulate with many offsets; EDM and total station: measures slope distance and angles, reduces to coordinates in the field, longer range, needs calibration and atmospheric correction; Compare initial cost, speed, accuracy per point, manpower, data handling; Justify a choice with the site size, terrain and required accuracy; note that a control framework is needed either way; Mention GNSS as the control option.

Model answer.

  1. The site. 5 hectares is 50 000 m², for example 250 m × 200 m. Its perimeter is 2 × (250 + 200) = 900 m and its diagonal √(250² + 200²) = 320.2 m.
  2. Chain and tape. A 30 m tape covers the perimeter in 900/30 = 30 tape lengths and the diagonal in 11. It needs no power and little money, and it is accurate on short level lines. It cannot measure angles, so detail is fixed by offsets from lines, and every building, hedge or slope across a line is an obstacle. The errors are systematic per tape length (temperature, pull, sag, slope) and must be corrected line by line.
  3. Accuracy on the diagonal. Careful steel taping at 1 in 5000 gives 320.2/5000 = 0.064 m on the 320 m diagonal. An EDM of ±(3 mm + 3 ppm) gives 3 + 3 × 0.320 = 4.0 mm on the same line.
  4. EDM and total station. One set-up near the middle reaches every corner (the farthest is about 160 m away), measures angle and slope distance together and stores coordinates directly. Obstacles matter only if they block the line of sight. It needs calibration, batteries, the atmospheric and prism corrections, and a trained observer.
  5. Comparison.
    ItemChain and tapeTotal station
    Cost of equipmentlowhigh
    Distances on this site30 tape lengths round the boundaryone shot per point
    Error on the 320 m diagonalabout 64 mm at 1 in 5000about 4 mm
    Angles and heightsnot measuredmeasured with each point
    Obstaclesblock chainingblock only the sight
    Datafield book, plotted by handstored coordinates, plotted by software
  6. Judgement. For a topographic survey, which needs both position and height of many points, the total station is the better choice: it gives about sixteen times better accuracy on the long line and heights without a separate levelling. Chain and tape remains sensible for a small, level, open plot where only a boundary plan is wanted, or as a check on a few total station distances.
  7. Control in either case. Both methods need a frame: a few stations fixed and checked first. GNSS can fix two or three of them on the national datum so the plan can be joined to other maps.
  8. Heights. A topographic survey needs heights. With chain and tape they come from a separate levelling on a grid: a 25 m grid over 250 m × 200 m has (250/25 + 1) × (200/25 + 1) = 11 × 9 = 99 points, each a staff reading. A total station gives the height of every detail point with its position, from the vertical angle and slope distance.
  9. Height from one total station shot. Instrument height 1.50 m on a station of reduced level 20.000 m; slope distance 120.000 m at a vertical angle of +2°00′; prism height 1.80 m:
    V = 120.000 × sin 2° = 4.188 m;  H = 120.000 × cos 2° = 119.927 m
RL of point = 20.000 + 1.50 + 4.188 − 1.80 = 23.888 m
  10. Risks of each. Chain and tape: slope and temperature corrections forgotten, a tape length dropped from the count, offsets too long. Total station: wrong prism constant or atmospheric setting, a flat battery, a wrong station or backsight entered, so that hundreds of points are wrong together; always re-observe a known point at the end.
  11. A mixed plan for the 5 ha site. Two or three GNSS control points, a short total station traverse round them, total station detail and heights, and a few tape checks between hard detail such as building corners.
  12. Check. 64 mm/4.0 mm = 16, the factor quoted; and 250 × 200 = 50 000 m² = 5 ha, the area given.
Evaluate A student claims that averaging many readings removes all error. Evaluate this claim using the ideas of gross, systematic and random errors. 10 marks

Points to cover. Averaging reduces only random errors, roughly by 1 over square root of n; Systematic errors survive averaging; they shift the mean and need a formula or calibration; Gross errors distort the mean badly; they must be found and removed first; Conclusion: accuracy needs correction plus averaging, not averaging alone; Give a tape example: a tape 0.05 m too long stays wrong however many times it is used.

Model answer.

  1. The claim. "Averaging many readings removes all error." It is true only for one of the three kinds of error.
  2. Random errors are reduced. If one reading scatters with s = 0.02 m, the mean of n readings scatters with s/√n.
    n = 4:   0.02/√4  = 0.010 m
n = 16:  0.02/√16 = 0.005 m   (four times the readings, half the error)
  3. Systematic errors survive. A 30 m tape that is 0.05 m too long reads a true 300 m line as 300 × 30/30.05 = 299.50 m every time. Averaging a hundred such readings still gives 299.50 m.
    each reading:  300 × 30 / 30.05 = 299.50 m
mean of any number of readings: 299.50 m   (error −0.50 m stays)
  4. Gross errors corrupt the mean. Five readings: 299.51, 299.49, 299.50, 299.52 and 329.50 m (a whole tape length dropped from the count in the last).
    mean = (299.51 + 299.49 + 299.50 + 299.52 + 329.50)/5 = 1527.52/5 = 305.50 m
without the blunder: 1198.02/4 = 299.505 m
  5. So the correct order is: find and remove blunders by checks (the 329.50 m reading is 30 m from the rest); remove systematic errors by calibration and correction (here, the tape correction of +0.50 m found by comparing the tape on a standard baseline); then average what is left to reduce random error.
  6. Verdict. The claim is false. Averaging improves precision, not accuracy. A precise mean can still be wrong by the whole systematic error, and one blunder can move it by metres.
  7. Why random errors cancel. Random errors are equally likely to be positive or negative and small ones are more common than large ones, so in a sum they partly cancel. Their total grows only as √n while the sum of the readings grows as n, so the error of the mean falls as 1/√n.
  8. Spotting the blunder by the scatter. The four good readings have mean 299.505 m and s = 0.013 m. The fifth is 30 m away, more than two thousand times s:
    deviations +0.005, −0.015, −0.005, +0.015;  Σd² = 0.0005
s = √(0.0005/3) = 0.013 m;   3s = 0.039 m
329.50 − 299.505 = 30.0 m  ≫ 0.039 m  → reject
  9. Surveying errors that averaging leaves alone. Tape temperature and sag; a wrong EDM prism constant; collimation error of a level (removed by equal back and fore sight lengths, not by repetition); local attraction on a compass. Each has the same sign every time, so each needs a correction or a field method that cancels it.
  10. Diminishing returns. Going from 16 to 64 readings halves the random error again, from 0.005 m to 0.0025 m, at four times the work; past a point the systematic error left over is larger than the random part, and more readings buy nothing.
  11. Check. Corrected result: 299.505 + 0.50 = 300.005 m, within the 0.005 m random uncertainty of the true 300 m.
Analyze Explain why a latitude and longitude pair without a stated datum, and a height without a stated reference surface, are of limited use in engineering work. 12 marks

Points to cover. Same physical point gets different coordinates on different datums; shifts can be tens to hundreds of metres; Datum transformation converts coordinates between datums; parameters must be stated; Height may be ellipsoidal (GNSS) or orthometric (levelling); they differ by the geoid undulation N; Engineering uses: setting out, GIS overlay, flood levels; a wrong datum gives a wrong position or level; Recommend recording datum, epoch and projection with every coordinate set.

Model answer.

  1. A coordinate is a measurement from a reference. Latitude is the angle between the ellipsoid normal and the equatorial plane; longitude is the angle from the prime meridian; both are defined only on a stated ellipsoid placed in a stated way, that is, on a datum.
  2. Different datums, different numbers. The same pillar has one latitude and longitude on WGS84 and different ones on the older Indian datum on the Everest ellipsoid, because the two ellipsoids differ in size, shape and position. The difference is large enough to put a point in the wrong plot or on the wrong side of a road, so coordinates are transformed to one datum before they are combined, with the transformation stated.
  3. Heights need a surface too. GNSS gives h above the ellipsoid; levelling gives H above the geoid. They differ by the geoid undulation N, which can be tens of metres.
    h = 52.3 m, N = −82.0 m   →   H = h − N = 134.3 m
using h as if it were H puts the point 82.0 m too low
  4. Projected coordinates need their zone. In UTM a point at 13.08° N, 80.27° E is in zone 44 (central meridian 81° E). Its easting is below the false easting of 500 000 m because it is 0.73° west of the central meridian. The same easting and northing quoted without "zone 44N" could be a point in any of the 60 zones.
  5. Engineering consequences. Setting out: a building set out from GNSS coordinates on one datum and a drawing on another lands in the wrong place. Flood work: a flood level given above mean sea level but compared with GNSS heights is wrong by N. GIS: two layers on different datums do not overlay.
  6. Good practice. Record with every coordinate set the datum, the projection and zone, the vertical datum (or the geoid model used), the units and the date of observation.
  7. Seconds into metres. One second of latitude is R × 4.848 × 10⁻⁶ = 30.9 m on the ground; one second of longitude at latitude 13° is 30.9 × cos 13° = 30.1 m. A datum difference of only a few seconds is therefore a shift of about a hundred metres.
    1″ of latitude = 6 371 000 × 4.848 × 10⁻⁶ = 30.9 m
3″ = 93 m
  8. Decimal places matter too. 13.08° N, 80.27° E is given to 0.01°, so each value could be off by 0.005° = 0.005 × 111.3 km = 557 m. A position for setting out needs about seven decimal places of a degree (about 1 cm), and the datum, before the digits mean anything.
  9. Example of a misfit. A pipeline drawing in a local datum and a contractor's GNSS on WGS84 can disagree by tens to hundreds of metres in position and by N in height; the pipe gradient worked from mixed heights can even reverse.
  10. Check. Zone = whole part of (80.27 + 180)/6, plus 1 = 43 + 1 = 44; central meridian = 6 × 44 − 183 = 81°.
Evaluate Assess the sources of error in an EDM observation and suggest practical steps to keep a total station traverse within 1 in 10000 closing accuracy. 14 marks

Points to cover. Instrument errors: zero error, scale error, prism constant, phase resolution; Atmospheric errors: temperature and pressure affect wave speed; enter corrected values or let the instrument do it; Pointing and centring errors, prism alignment, height of instrument and target; Reflectorless measurement: weaker return, shorter range, larger error, careful on dark or inclined surfaces; Practise: forced centring, measure each distance both ways, close the traverse, adjust by Bowditch or transit rule, apply curvature and refraction to long lines.

Model answer.

  1. Target. A closed traverse of six legs of 200 m has a perimeter of 1200 m. Closing to 1 in 10 000 allows a closing error of 1200/10 000 = 0.12 m.
  2. Random distance error. An EDM of ±(3 mm + 3 ppm) gives 3 + 3 × 0.2 = 3.6 mm on each leg; six independent legs together give about √6 × 3.6 mm.

    sideways shift = 200 m × 5″ / 206 265 = 0.0048 m = 4.8 mm

Glossary

Astronomical surveying
Surveying in which positions are fixed from observations of stars, giving latitude, longitude and azimuth independent of ground control.
Atmospheric correction
A correction applied to EDM distances for the actual temperature and pressure, since the speed of light in air depends on both.
Base line
The longest and most carefully measured line of a chain survey, from which the framework of the survey is built.
Check line
A line measured in the field only to verify that the plotted framework is correct.
Contour interval
The constant vertical distance between two successive contour lines on a map.
Control survey
A survey that establishes a network of points of known high accuracy to which all detail surveys are referred.
Datum transformation
The set of parameters and formulas used to convert coordinates from one geodetic datum to another.
EDM
An instrument that measures distance by transmitting an electromagnetic signal to a reflector or target and measuring phase or time of travel.
Error
The difference between a measured value and the true value of the quantity.
Field book
The ruled notebook in which all field measurements and sketches are recorded at the time of survey.
Geodetic surveying
Surveying in which the curvature of the earth is taken into account and computations are made on a reference ellipsoid.
Geographic coordinates
Latitude, longitude and height that define a position on a stated reference surface.
Geoid undulation
The height of the geoid above (or below) the reference ellipsoid at a place, symbol N, in metres.
Hydrographic surveying
Surveying of water bodies to fix depths, shore lines and underwater features for navigation and dredging.
Map
A scaled, conventional representation of features of the ground seen from above on a horizontal plane.
Mean sea level
The average height of the sea surface over a long period, used as the zero for heights.
Modulation frequency
The frequency at which the carrier wave of an EDM is varied; it sets the unit of length used to resolve the phase difference.
Perpendicular offset
An offset measured at 90 degrees to the survey line, usually with a cross staff or optical square.
Projected coordinates
Plane coordinates, such as easting and northing in UTM, obtained by mathematically projecting the ellipsoid onto a flat surface.
Random error
A small error of unpredictable sign and size, caused by imperfect observation, which is reduced by repeated measurement and averaging.
Representative fraction
The scale written as a ratio 1:M, where one unit on the map stands for M of the same units on the ground.
Residual
The difference between an observed value and its adjusted or most probable value.
Surveying
The art and science of determining the relative positions of points on or near the earth's surface and representing them to a convenient scale on a map or plan.
