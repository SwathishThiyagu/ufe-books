2.1 Meridians, bearings and azimuths

A survey line has no meaning until its direction is fixed against a north. Surveyors choose a meridian as the reference north, then record every line as an angle from it. The same direction can be written as a whole circle bearing, a reduced bearing, or an azimuth; all three describe one arrow. This chapter shows how to switch between those forms, how to get the reverse direction, and how to move between magnetic and true north. These conversions run through compass work, traverses, and setting out on site.

You need the foundations of surveying: angles, scales, maps and topographic sheets.

Meridians: the reference north

Direction needs a reference. A meridian is the line through the observer and a chosen north. Four meridians are used. The true meridian (geographic meridian) follows Earth's axis and passes through the geographic poles. The magnetic meridian is shown by a freely suspended compass needle and points to magnetic north. A grid meridian is a line parallel to the central meridian of a map projection; it is a drawing convention, not a physical north. An arbitrary meridian is any convenient line chosen for one small survey.

A bearing is the angle between a survey line and a stated meridian, and may be written whole circle or reduced. An azimuth is always measured clockwise from a north reference, usually true north, so it is the same as the whole circle bearing from that north. Always state the north used, because a bearing without a meridian is incomplete.

Whole circle and reduced bearings

A whole circle bearing (WCB) is measured clockwise from north, from 0° to 360°. A reduced bearing (RB) or quadrantal bearing is measured from the nearer end of the meridian, north or south, toward east or west. Its value is between 0° and 90°, written with N or S first and E or W last.

RB = θ in NE; RB = 180° − θ in SE; RB = θ − 180° in SW; RB = 360° − θ in NW

θ is the whole circle bearing in degrees; NE means 0° < θ < 90°, SE means 90° < θ < 180°, SW means 180° < θ < 270°, NW means 270° < θ < 360°.

RB is the reduced bearing in degrees from 0° to 90°, with the quadrant letters chosen first.

The rule is mechanical. In the north-east quadrant the WCB is the RB. In the south-east quadrant subtract the WCB from 180°. In the south-west quadrant subtract 180° from the WCB. In the north-west quadrant subtract the WCB from 360°. Reverse the rules when the RB is given. Keep degrees and minutes; 1° = 60′. A result above 90° inside an RB means the quadrant rule is wrong.

Fore bearings, back bearings, angles and declination

The fore bearing (FB) is measured from the station to the next point. The back bearing (BB) is measured from that next point back to the station. The two differ by 180°: BB = FB + 180° if FB < 180°, and BB = FB − 180° if FB ≥ 180°. Add or subtract 180° so the result stays between 0° and 360°. At a station B, the angle between lines BA and BC uses bearing BA (the back bearing of AB) and bearing BC (the fore bearing of BC).

An interior angle is inside the closed figure; a deflection angle is measured from the prolongation of the previous line to the next line, left or right. Magnetic declination is the angle between the true meridian and the magnetic meridian. It is east when magnetic north lies east of true north; then true bearing = magnetic bearing + declination. It is west when magnetic north lies west of true north; then true bearing = magnetic bearing − declination. With 5°30′ east declination, magnetic north lies 5°30′ east of true north, so every magnetic bearing reads smaller than the true bearing by that amount; the magnetic needle points to the right of true north.

Declination changes slowly with time, so maps state its year. Isogonic lines join places of equal declination; the agonic line has zero declination. Local iron and current are separate errors; see compass surveying and local attraction.

Worked example

Question. Convert WCB 145° and WCB 250° into reduced bearings and give the back bearing of each. A line has magnetic bearing 40°00′ and the declination at that place is 5°30′ east. Find its true bearing and the true bearing of the opposite direction.

  1. For WCB 145°: it lies between 90° and 180°, so the quadrant is SE. RB = 180° − 145° = 35°. The letters are S and E, so RB = S 35° E.
  2. Back bearing of 145°: 145° < 180°, so BB = 145° + 180° = 325°.
  3. For WCB 250°: it lies between 180° and 270°, so the quadrant is SW. RB = 250° − 180° = 70°. The letters are S and W, so RB = S 70° W.
  4. Back bearing of 250°: 250° > 180°, so BB = 250° − 180° = 70°.
  5. Declination is 5°30′ east, so add it. True bearing = 40°00′ + 5°30′ = 45°30′.
  6. The opposite direction differs by 180°: 45°30′ + 180° = 225°30′ true.

Answer. The reduced bearings are S 35° E and S 70° W, their back bearings are 325° and 70°, and the true bearings are 45°30′ and 225°30′.

Exam traps

  • The reference letter comes first: S 35° E is correct; E 35° S is not.
  • An RB number must lie between 0° and 90°. If a rule gives 120°, you used the wrong quadrant.
  • For back bearing, add 180° only when FB < 180°; otherwise subtract 180°. Do not produce 500°.
  • Declination east is added to magnetic bearing, west is subtracted. Reversing the sign rotates every line.
  • When adding minutes, carry 60′ as 1°. 40°50′ + 5°30′ = 46°20′, not 45°80′.

2.2 Compass surveying and local attraction

A compass turns direction into a number from the earth's magnetic field, so the number is only as good as the field at that spot. Iron objects, steel tapes, phones and cables carrying current bend the needle; bearings still look reasonable while wrong. This chapter reads a bearing, builds the two checks that expose a bent needle, finds clean stations and corrects the rest.

You need: meridians, bearings and azimuths for whole circle bearings, back bearings and interior angles, and the foundations of surveying for angular units, error and precision.

The compass, its least count and its adjustments

A compass needle is a small magnet on a sharp pivot. It swings until it lies along the magnetic meridian, and a graduated ring shows how far the line of sight is from that meridian. Read the ring and you have the magnetic bearing of the line.

The plain (surveyor's) compass fixes the ring to the box and moves the needle inside it; you read quadrantal bearings, 0° to 90°. The prismatic compass carries the ring on the needle and puts a prism at the eye, so graduations and object are seen together. It reads whole circle bearings, 0° to 360° clockwise from magnetic north, with least count 30′ (half a degree), the smallest change the ring can show.

Temporary adjustments, repeated at every station:

  1. Fix the compass on its tripod and centre it over the station with a plumb bob.
  2. Level the box, so the needle swings freely.
  3. Release the needle, let it settle, and tap the glass to overcome friction.
  4. Adjust the prism until graduations are sharp and the ranging rod is bisected by the sighting hair.
  5. Let the needle rest, read the ring, and book the station, line, and fore or back bearing.

Compass traverse and its two checks

A compass traverse measures the bearing of each line in turn. In a closed traverse the last station is the first, as round a plot boundary. In an open traverse the line ends at a point not yet fixed, as along a road centre line; it has no closing check, so tie it to known points.

At each station read two bearings: the fore bearing (FB) of the line ahead, and the back bearing (BB) of the line behind. Both describe the same line from opposite ends, so they must differ by half a circle.

FB = BB ± 180°

FB is the fore bearing at the station, in degrees clockwise from magnetic north; BB is the back bearing of the same line at the far station; use + when BB < 180° (answer stays below 360°) and − when BB > 180°. A difference other than 180° means local attraction at one of the two stations.

The second check works only on a closed polygon and uses the interior angles.

Σ interior angles = (2n − 4) × 90°

n is the number of sides of the closed polygon and the sum is in degrees: 180° for a triangle, 360° for a quadrilateral, 540° for a pentagon.

An interior angle at a station comes from the two bearings there: take their difference, and choose the value inside the polygon. If the measured sum is S, the angular error is e = S − (2n − 4) × 90°. Correct every angle by −e/n. If e does not divide evenly, give the odd minutes to the angles at stations with the shortest sights, which are the least reliable.

Local attraction: cause, detection, correction

The needle obeys every magnetic field, not only the earth's. A steel tape, an iron railing, a truck, iron ore, a key, or a cable carrying current adds its own field. The needle then points somewhere other than magnetic north. Every bearing at that station is turned by the same small angle, from a few minutes to a few degrees, and the next clean station reads normally.

Detection is a test on a line, not on one station. If the fore and back bearings of a line differ by exactly 180°, both its stations are free from local attraction. Start from such a line and walk through the traverse; the first station you cannot vouch for is the one to correct.

Method 1, interior angles. Compute the interior angle at every station from the observed bearings, check the sum against (2n − 4) × 90°, share the error, then work out corrected bearings outward from a line known to be true. Local attraction cancels in the angles because it enters both bearings at a station by the same amount.

Method 2, direct from an unaffected line. Take the correct bearing of a line at a free station, add or subtract 180° for its correct back bearing, and compare that with the observed back bearing at the far station. The difference is the correction at that station. Apply it to every bearing taken there, move to the next line, and repeat. The corrections must still agree when you return to the start.

Errors and precautions

  • Needle not free: a blunt pivot or grit makes it stick; tap the glass and see if it returns to the same reading.
  • Compass not level: a tilted needle rubs and drags; level the box each time.
  • Sighting: the ranging rod must stand vertical and be bisected by the hair; read the ring square through the prism.
  • Magnetic declination: the angle between true north and magnetic north changes with place and slowly with time, so note the date and place of the survey; defined in meridians, bearings and azimuths.
  • Keep tapes, keys, phones, iron pegs and magnets away from the compass, never stand over it with a steel chain, and always book both fore and back bearings.

Worked example

Question. The observed bearings of a closed compass traverse ABCD are: AB 46°00′, BA 226°00′, BC 130°00′, CB 310°00′, CD 220°30′, DC 40°00′, DA 309°30′, AD 130°00′. Find the stations free from local attraction, the correction at each affected station, and all corrected bearings.

  1. Test each line. AB − BA = 46°00′ − 226°00′ = −180°00′; magnitude exactly 180°, so A and B are free.
  2. BC − CB = 130°00′ − 310°00′ = −180°00′; B is free, so BC = 130°00′ is correct and correct CB = 310°00′, equal to observed CB. Station C is free.
  3. C is free, so CD = 220°30′ stands. Correct DC = 220°30′ − 180°00′ = 40°30′. Observed DC is 40°00′, so D reads 30′ too small: correction at D = +30′.
  4. Apply +30′ at D: DC = 40°30′, DA = 310°00′. Check: correct AD at A = 310°00′ − 180°00′ = 130°00′, equal to observed AD. The loop closes.
  5. Interior angles from corrected bearings: A = AD − AB = 84°00′; B = BA − BC = 96°00′; C = CB − CD = 89°30′; D = (DC + 360°00′) − DA = 40°30′ + 360°00′ − 310°00′ = 90°30′.
  6. Check: 84°00′ + 96°00′ + 89°30′ + 90°30′ = 360°00′ = (2 × 4 − 4) × 90°. No angle adjustment is needed.

Answer. Stations A, B and C are free; D carries +30′. Corrected bearings: AB 46°00′, BA 226°00′, BC 130°00′, CB 310°00′, CD 220°30′, DC 40°30′, DA 310°00′, AD 130°00′; interior angles 84°00′, 96°00′, 89°30′, 90°30′ sum to 360°00′.

Exam traps

  • Taking the first station as free. Only a line whose fore and back bearings differ by 180° proves two stations free.
  • Applying a station correction to bearings taken elsewhere. A correction belongs to the station where the needle was read.
  • Sign slip in FB = BB ± 180°: add when BB < 180°, subtract when BB > 180°.
  • Mixing local attraction with magnetic declination. Declination is between true and magnetic north; local attraction changes station to station.
  • Reporting the exterior angle when the interior was asked. If the bearing difference exceeds 180°, the interior angle is 360° minus that difference.

2.3 Plane table surveying

Plane table surveying is a field method that draws the map on the drawing board while the surveyor stands on the ground. The board is levelled, centred over the station and oriented to north or to a known line. An alidade sights each object and its straight edge draws a ray on the sheet. Distances or intersecting rays fix the points. The surveyor leaves the field with a finished plot, not a field book. It suits large-scale maps of small areas, reconnaissance, and quick checks.

You need meridians, bearings and azimuths for meridians and bearings, compass surveying and local attraction for the trough compass, and the foundations of surveying for scale and plotting.

Equipment on the board

The plane table is a drawing board on a tripod. The board carries the paper on which the map is drawn. The alidade is a straightedge with a sighting device. A plain alidade has two vanes. A telescopic alidade has a small telescope and a bubble; it sights farther and can read vertical angles. The spirit level checks that the board is horizontal. The trough compass is a long narrow compass used to set magnetic north. The plumbing fork is a U-shaped metal piece; with a plumb bob it places a plotted point exactly over the ground station. A chain or tape measures distances. A pencil, eraser and scale are also needed. The key idea is that the map is drawn in the field. Each sight line becomes a ray on the sheet. No separate field book is needed.

Setting up in order

  1. Fix the tripod. Spread the legs, press the shoes into the ground, and clamp the board.
  2. Level the board. Place the spirit level in two directions at right angles. Adjust the tripod legs until the bubble stays central.
  3. Centre the table. Put the plumbing fork on the sheet so its upper point is over the station point. The plumb bob must hang over the ground mark. Shift the board, not the paper, until it does.
  4. Orient the table. Orientation means turning the table until every plotted line is parallel to its ground line.
  5. For magnetic orientation, place the trough compass and turn the board until the needle lies along the north line on the sheet.
  6. For backsight orientation, place the alidade along a plotted line pq and sight the ground point Q. Turn the table until the line of sight cuts Q. Check with a second plotted line.
  7. Magnetic orientation is quick but local attraction can spoil it; see compass surveying and local attraction. The backsight is better when a good plotted line exists.
  8. Orientation must be repeated at every new station. Levelling and centring are also checked after each move.

Four methods

  • Radiation: one station O. Rays are drawn to every object. Each distance is measured with chain or tape and plotted along its ray. Use it for small open areas, such as a building site or a playground.
  • Intersection (triangulation): two or more stations. A ray to the object is drawn from each station. The crossing of the rays fixes the object, with no distance measurement. Use it for inaccessible points, such as a tree across a river or a temple spire.
  • Traversing: the table moves from station to station along a line. At each station it is oriented and the next side is drawn and measured. Use it for roads, canals and narrow strips.
  • Resection: the table stands at an unknown point and sights known points. The rays locate the station itself. Use it when the station is inconvenient to reach or when known points are visible.

Resection, three-point problem and checks

Resection fixes an unknown station P from known points A, B and C. The table stands at P. The plotted points are a, b and c. The alidade is placed on a and sighted to A, and a ray is drawn. The same is done from b to B and c to C. If the three rays meet at one point p, that point is the station on the sheet. In practice the orientation is not known, so the rays form a small triangle of error. Trial and error turns the table a little, draws the three rays again, and repeats until the triangle shrinks to a point. Bessel's solution is a construction that gives the correct orientation from the plotted triangle without many guesses. Lehmann's rules use the position of P relative to ABC and the triangle of error to say which way to turn the table. If P lies on the circumcircle of ABC, the resection is indeterminate — the rays stay concurrent whatever the orientation — so the danger circle must be avoided. The two-point problem locates the station from two known points, using an auxiliary station that is set up and plotted first. It is less common than the three-point problem. Some syllabi ask for the full Bessel construction and all Lehmann cases; here the idea is enough.

  • Advantages: the plot is made in the field, mistakes are seen at once, and no field book is needed.
  • Disadvantages: the table is heavy, wind and rain disturb it, the sheet gets crowded, and precision is lower than a theodolite traverse.
  • Common errors: poor centring, board not level, orientation not repeated, alidade not on the exact point, and rays too short.
  • Precautions: clamp the tripod, check levelling and centring after every move, orient every station, use a fine pencil, and draw rays long enough to intersect cleanly.

Worked example

Question. The line pq is already plotted on the sheet with magnetic bearing 210°. The table is set up at p and the trough compass needle along pq reads 195°. (a) By how much and in which direction must the table be turned? (b) What will the needle read along pq after correct orientation?

  1. Plotted magnetic bearing of pq: θ_plotted = 210°.
  2. Observed magnetic bearing of pq from the needle: θ_observed = 195°.
  3. Correction Δθ = θ_plotted − θ_observed = 210° − 195° = 15°.
  4. Δθ is positive, so pq is 15° short of its plotted bearing. Turn the table clockwise by 15°.
  5. New reading = θ_observed + Δθ = 195° + 15° = 210° = θ_plotted, so orientation is correct.

Answer. The table must be turned 15° clockwise, and the needle will then read 210° along pq.

Exam traps

  • Orientation is not centring. Centring puts the station point over the ground mark; orientation turns the table to the correct north or backsight. Repeat orientation at every station.
  • Never rotate the plotted line to match the needle. The line is the record; turn the board.
  • A compass reading lower than the plotted bearing means the table is turned anticlockwise relative to correct; correct by turning clockwise by the difference. Check the sign with a sketch.
  • In the three-point problem, a station on the circumcircle of the three known points is the danger circle; the resection is indeterminate. Choose other points.
  • Radiation measures distance along each ray; intersection needs no distance. Mixing them gives a wrong plot.

2.4 Traverse surveying: latitudes and departures

A traverse is a chain of straight lines measured between stations. Each line is fixed by its length and its bearing. Latitude and departure split that line into a north-south part and an east-west part. The split turns slanted lines into signed numbers that add like vectors. Surveyors use it to carry coordinates along roads, canals, boundaries and control networks, and to check that a closed traverse returns to its starting point.

You need meridians, bearings and azimuths for whole circle bearings and the foundations of surveying for trigonometric ratios and coordinate axes.

Traverse, stations and types

A traverse is a survey made of connected straight lines. The ends of the lines are traverse stations. For every line the surveyor records the length and the bearing. The chain of lines is the traverse.

A closed traverse starts and ends at the same station. It encloses an area, so it suits a plot boundary or a lake. An open traverse starts at a known station and ends at an unknown point. Roads, canals and pipelines use it because the route does not return. A link traverse starts at one known station and ends at a different known station. It joins two control points.

In all three types, the field record is a traverse table. Each row is one line: from station, to station, length and bearing. Two computed columns are added next: latitude and departure. These columns turn each slanted line into a north-south step and an east-west step, so the steps can be added and checked.

A compass gives the bearing of every line directly. A theodolite or total station traverse measures the angle at each station instead, so the bearings are worked out from one known bearing. For a traverse run clockwise with interior angles: bearing of the next line = bearing of the previous line + 180° − interior angle at the station (add or subtract 360° to stay between 0° and 360°). For example, if AB is 45° and the interior angle at B is 90°, then BC = 45° + 180° − 90° = 135°.

Latitude and departure from a bearing

Take a line of length l metres. Its whole circle bearing is θ, measured clockwise from north. Latitude is the north-south component. Departure is the east-west component.

Latitude = l cos θ Departure = l sin θ

l is the line length in metres; θ is the whole circle bearing in degrees, clockwise from north; latitude is positive north and negative south; departure is positive east and negative west.

The sign follows the quadrant. In the north-east quadrant both are positive. In the south-east, latitude is negative and departure positive. In the south-west, both are negative. In the north-west, latitude is positive and departure negative.

A reduced bearing α is the acute angle from the nearer north or south end of the meridian, measured towards east or west. Reduced bearings give the same numbers if you keep the signs separate. For α (0° to 90°), latitude is ± l cos α and departure is ± l sin α. Choose + or − from the north/south and east/west words. Do not put a reduced bearing into l cos θ and l sin θ: θ must be the whole circle bearing, otherwise the signs come out wrong. A line at 135° has latitude 180 cos 135° = −127.28 m and departure 180 sin 135° = +127.28 m.

Traverse table, coordinates and the closure checks

A traverse table has one row per line. The columns are line, length l, whole circle bearing θ, latitude l cos θ and departure l sin θ. Add the signed latitudes and the signed departures in separate columns.

Coordinates come from those sums. Start at a station with known coordinates (E₀, N₀). For each line, add its departure to easting and its latitude to northing:

  1. Enext = E_previous + departure
  2. Nnext = N_previous + latitude

These are consecutive coordinates. They depend on the starting station. Independent coordinates are tied to a survey grid, so every station has one fixed pair.

For a closed traverse, the return to the first station gives two checks:

Σ latitude = 0 Σ departure = 0

Σ latitude is the algebraic sum of all signed latitudes in metres; Σ departure is the algebraic sum of all signed departures in metres.

If either sum is not zero, the traverse has a misclosure: the amount by which the sums fail to be zero. Adjusting it is the job of closing error and traverse adjustment. An open traverse has no such check because it does not return to the start. Area from the coordinates is in area computation by coordinates.

Worked example

Question. A closed traverse ABCD has AB = 200 m at 45°, BC = 180 m at 135°, CD = 160 m at 210° and DA = 226 m at 303°20′. Compute the latitude and departure of every line and the two sums. Does the traverse close exactly?

  1. Change DA to decimal degrees: 303°20′ = 303 + 20/60 = 303.3333°.
  2. Use θ as the whole circle bearing. For each line: latitude = l cos θ and departure = l sin θ.
  3. AB: latitude = 200 cos 45° = +141.42 m; departure = 200 sin 45° = +141.42 m.
  4. BC: latitude = 180 cos 135° = −127.28 m; departure = 180 sin 135° = +127.28 m.
  5. CD: latitude = 160 cos 210° = −138.56 m; departure = 160 sin 210° = −80.00 m.
  6. DA: latitude = 226 cos 303.3333° = +124.19 m; departure = 226 sin 303.3333° = −188.82 m.
  7. Σ latitude = 141.42 − 127.28 − 138.56 + 124.19 = −0.23 m.
  8. Σ departure = 141.42 + 127.28 − 80.00 − 188.82 = −0.12 m.

Answer. Σ latitude = −0.23 m and Σ departure = −0.12 m; neither is zero, so the traverse does not close exactly.

Exam traps

  • Using a reduced bearing in l cos θ and l sin θ. The formula needs the whole circle bearing θ; a reduced bearing gives the wrong sign in the south or west half.
  • Forgetting that latitude is north-south and departure is east-west. Swapping them turns every coordinate step by 90°.
  • Dropping the sign when adding. Latitudes and departures are signed vector components, not plain lengths, so −127.28 m must stay negative in the sum.
  • Applying the zero-sum check to an open traverse. Only a closed traverse must return to the start; an open traverse has no such condition.
  • Treating a nonzero sum as proof that the fieldwork is wrong. It is a misclosure, adjusted as shown in closing error and traverse adjustment; the sum itself must still be computed and reported.

2.5 Closing error and traverse adjustment

A closed traverse should return to its start. In practice it lands a small distance away. That gap is the closing error. Surveyors measure it, compare it with the perimeter, and spread it over the lines by a fixed rule so the traverse closes exactly. The same arithmetic turns field angles and distances into one consistent set of coordinates for maps, land records, road alignment and GIS layers.

You need traverse surveying: latitudes and departures for latitudes, departures and coordinates, and compass surveying and local attraction for angular misclosure.

The closing error and what it tells you

Run a closed traverse from A through B, C, D and back toward A. The computed last point is A′, not exactly A. From A to A′ the displacement has two parts: the sum of latitudes ΣL and the sum of departures ΣD. In a perfect close both are zero.

e = √((ΣL)² + (ΣD)²)

e is the closing error in metres; ΣL is the algebraic sum of latitudes in metres, north positive and south negative; ΣD is the algebraic sum of departures in metres, east positive and west negative.

tan θ = |ΣD| ÷ |ΣL|

θ is the acute angle from the north–south line. Choose the quadrant from the signs: ΣL positive and ΣD positive gives north-east; ΣL negative and ΣD positive gives south-east; both negative gives south-west; ΣL positive and ΣD negative gives north-west.

With ΣL = −0.23 m and ΣD = −0.12 m, tan θ = 0.12/0.23 = 0.522, so θ = 27°33′. Both sums are negative, so A′ lies south-west of A. The bearing from A to A′ is 180° + 27°33′ = 207°33′.

Size alone is not enough. A 0.4 m error in a 100 m loop is serious; in a 10 km loop it is small. Compare with relative precision: P ÷ e, where P = Σlᵢ is the perimeter in metres and e is the closing error in metres. Write it as 1 in P/e. A rough compass traverse is accepted at a much lower precision (1 in a few hundred to about 1 in 1000) than a theodolite or total station traverse (1 in several thousand or better). Always use the limit set for the job.

Angular and linear misclosure; Bowditch rule

Two different misclosures exist. Angular misclosure compares the measured angles with geometry. For a closed polygon of n sides, the interior angles must sum to (2n − 4) × 90°. For n = 5 the sum is 540°. The observed sum minus this value is the angular misclosure.

Correct large angular misclosure first. Otherwise every bearing, and so every latitude and departure, carries the same error. Linear misclosure is e above, in metres, computed after the latitudes and departures.

The Bowditch rule, also called the compass rule, spreads the linear misclosure in proportion to line length. For line i of length lᵢ:

CLi = −ΣL × (lᵢ ÷ P)    and    CDi = −ΣD × (lᵢ ÷ P)

CLi is the correction to latitude in metres; CDi is the correction to departure in metres; ΣL and ΣD are the misclosures in metres; lᵢ is the length of line i in metres; P is the perimeter in metres.

The minus sign is essential: corrections act opposite to the misclosure. Add CLi to the latitude of line i and CDi to its departure. Then add the adjusted latitudes and departures cumulatively from station to station to get coordinates. The new sums must be zero, apart from rounding.

Why Bowditch for compass work? A compass bearing is less precise than a theodolite angle, and its effect on a line's latitude and departure grows with the line's length. Distance error also grows with length. The rule puts more correction on longer lines. That is a reasonable simple model, not a physical law.

The transit rule is the alternative: correction to latitude proportional to |latitude|, and to departure proportional to |departure|, not to length. It suits a theodolite traverse, where angles are more precise than distances. The equal rule gives every line the same correction. Least-squares adjustment is the rigorous method; it is taught with weights and adjustment of observations in control surveys.

Omitted measurements

Some syllabi add omitted measurements. One length or one bearing of a closed traverse is missing. Use ΣL = 0 and ΣD = 0. If line i has unknown length l and bearing θ, the known lines give ΣLknown and ΣDknown. The components of the missing line are therefore l cos θ = −ΣLknown and l sin θ = −ΣDknown.

Square and add: l = √(ΣLknown² + ΣDknown²). Divide: tan θ = ΣDknown ÷ ΣLknown. Take the quadrant from the signs of the components of the missing line, that is from −ΣLknown (its latitude) and −ΣDknown (its departure), not from the signs of the sums themselves.

Example: in the traverse of the worked example below, suppose the length and bearing of DA were not recorded. The three known lines give ΣLknown = 141.42 − 127.28 − 138.56 = −124.42 m and ΣDknown = 141.42 + 127.28 − 80.00 = +188.70 m. So DA has latitude +124.42 m and departure −188.70 m: north-west. l = √(124.42² + 188.70²) = 226.03 m; tan α = 188.70/124.42 = 1.517, α = 56°36′, so the bearing is 360° − 56°36′ = 303°24′. The missing line absorbs the whole misclosure, which is why an omitted measurement leaves no check.

Check by recomputing both sums. Two omitted quantities, one on each of two lines, can still be solved from ΣL = 0 and ΣD = 0: two unknown lengths give two linear equations, and two unknown bearings give two simultaneous equations for the directions. If three or more quantities are missing, the two closing equations are not enough. You need extra field data or least squares. Do not guess.

Exam traps

  • Dividing the misclosure by the number of lines in the Bowditch rule. The denominator is the perimeter P = Σlᵢ; a longer line must receive a larger share.

2.6 Area computation by coordinates

Every closed boundary has corners. Once each corner has an easting and a northing, the area inside comes from one sum of cross products. You walk around the boundary in order, multiply each easting by the change in northing, and add. The method is used for land records, plot layout, earthwork and GIS. It needs no drawing and no scale. The only rules are: keep the corners in order, finish the loop, and remember that the raw sum is twice the area.

You need traverse surveying: latitudes and departures for coordinates and closing error and traverse adjustment for adjusted coordinates; the foundations of surveying cover area units.

The cross-product sum

Let every corner have coordinates (E, N): E is easting, the distance east of the origin, and N is northing, the distance north. Both in metres. Number the corners 1 to n in order around the boundary. For corner i, write Ei and Ni. The next corner is i+1; the previous is i−1. At the ends wrap around: corner n+1 is corner 1, and corner 0 is corner n.

2A = Σ Ei (Ni+1 − Ni−1)

A is the area in square metres; Ei is the easting of corner i; Ni+1 and Ni−1 are the northings of the next and previous corners; the sum runs once around all n corners.

The same area has a second form. It pairs each corner with the next one directly:

2A = Σ (Ei Ni+1 − Ei+1 Ni)

Ei, Ni are the coordinates of corner i; Ei+1, Ni+1 are those of the next corner; the last pair uses corner 1 after corner n.

Both formulas return twice the area, not the area. A table keeps the work visible. For the first form, write one row per corner with columns E, N, next N, previous N, and the product E × (next N − previous N). Add the product column. Divide the total by 2. The result is the area, provided the coordinates and the order are correct.

  1. List the corners in boundary order.
  2. For each row, read the next and previous northings.
  3. Multiply the easting by their difference.
  4. Add all rows and halve the total.

Order, sign and units

Take the corners in one direction around the boundary, clockwise or anticlockwise. Do not jump across the plot, skip a corner or repeat the first corner at the end. A jump makes the figure cross itself like a bow-tie. The formula then adds some outside parts and subtracts inside parts, so the number is not the plot area.

The sign of the sum fixes the direction. With eastings to the right and northings up, an anticlockwise walk gives a positive sum; a clockwise walk gives a negative sum. Area is never negative: take the absolute value. The sign is information about the direction, not a reason to drop the result.

Keep both coordinates in the same unit before multiplying. If E and N are in metres, the sum is in square metres and A = S/2 is in square metres, where S is the raw sum and A is the area. Convert at the end. 1 hectare = 10 000 m². 1 acre = 4046.86 m². For example, 550 m² is 550 ÷ 10 000 = 0.055 ha.

Double meridian distance (DMD) is the older name for the same job done from latitudes and departures. The double area is the sum of DMD × latitude for every line, with signs; halve it for the area. The coordinate formula is the shorter modern route.

Where the coordinates come from

The coordinates may come from a closed traverse after adjustment, from a total station, or from a GNSS receiver. In all three cases the arithmetic is the same. The coordinates must refer to one consistent grid and one consistent unit. An adjusted traverse is preferred because the closing error has already been spread. Coordinate computation is the step that produces these E and N values; the area step starts only after they are fixed.

CAD and GIS compute the same sum in an instant. A polygon area command uses this cross-product rule, not a planimeter or a grid count. For a surveyor, the formula is still needed to check the software, to show the method in an exam, and to understand why the order of points matters. It is used in land records, plot layout, land acquisition, and any map overlay that reports an area; the same sum runs inside GIS overlay tools. One wrong point moves one product and changes the area, so check the order and the unit before trusting the total.

Worked example

Question. Find the area of the plot ABCD with corners A(10, 10), B(30, 10), C(40, 30) and D(20, 40), eastings and northings in metres, taken in order around the boundary. Check the answer with the other form of the formula.

  1. Write the coordinates in order. A: E = 10 m, N = 10 m. B: E = 30 m, N = 10 m. C: E = 40 m, N = 30 m. D: E = 20 m, N = 40 m. There are n = 4 corners.
  2. At A, next is B with N = 10 m, previous is D with N = 40 m. Product = 10 × (10 − 40) = −300 m².
  3. At B, next is C with N = 30 m, previous is A with N = 10 m. Product = 30 × (30 − 10) = 600 m².
  4. At C, next is D with N = 40 m, previous is B with N = 10 m. Product = 40 × (40 − 10) = 1200 m².
  5. At D, next is A with N = 10 m, previous is C with N = 30 m. Product = 20 × (10 − 30) = −400 m².
  6. Sum S = −300 + 600 + 1200 − 400 = 1100 m². This S is twice the area, so area A = 1100 ÷ 2 = 550 m².
  7. Convert to hectares: 550 ÷ 10 000 = 0.055 ha.
  8. Cross-product check: (10 × 10 − 30 × 10) + (30 × 30 − 40 × 10) + (40 × 40 − 20 × 30) + (20 × 10 − 10 × 40) = (−200) + 500 + 1000 + (−200) = 1100 m², the same.

Answer. The area of plot ABCD is 550 m², which is 0.055 ha.

Takeaways

  1. A bearing is meaningless until you say which north it was measured from, so always state true, magnetic, grid or arbitrary.
  2. The same direction can be written as a whole-circle bearing, a reduced bearing, a back bearing or an interior angle; converting correctly is a skill worth drilling.
  3. Magnetic declination changes with place and time, so a compass bearing must carry its date and location to be useful.
  4. Local attraction is detected by comparing fore and back bearings; the stations that agree are free of it and become the reference.
  5. Plane table surveying trades precision for speed: it plots directions directly but cannot measure distances you did not tape.
  6. Latitude and departure turn a traverse into simple vector sums, which is why every adjustment rule works on these two columns.
  7. A closed traverse almost never closes by itself; the closing error is evidence of small systematic and random errors, not of one blunder.
  8. Bowditch's rule suits compass traverses, where bearings are as uncertain as lengths; the transit rule suits theodolite traverses where angles are better than lengths.
  9. The coordinate or shoelace method gives the area of any closed polygon from its corner coordinates, with no scaling or planimeter.
  10. Sign conventions for latitude and departure must be fixed before any table is made, or the whole adjustment goes wrong.

  2. True meridian. The line through the station and the geographic north and south poles. It is fixed by astronomical observation (or from GNSS) and does not change with time, so true bearings are permanent and are used for property boundaries and maps.
  3. Magnetic meridian. The direction in which a freely suspended, balanced magnetic needle comes to rest at the station. It points to magnetic north, which differs from true north by the magnetic declination, and it drifts slowly year by year.
  4. Grid meridian. A line parallel to the central meridian of the map projection (the grid lines running north on the sheet). It is the north of a projected coordinate system; away from the central meridian it differs slightly from true north.
  5. Arbitrary meridian. Any convenient fixed line, such as the line from a station to a temple spire or a corner of a building, used as north in a small local survey. It is easy to set up again but means nothing outside that survey.
  6. Why a bearing needs its meridian. One line has a different bearing from each meridian. Example: a line with magnetic bearing 40°00′ where the declination is 5°30′ east has a true bearing of 45°30′; on a sheet whose grid north is 0°45′ west of true north at that place, its grid bearing is 46°15′.
    MeridianBearing of the same line
    Magnetic40°00′
    True40°00′ + 5°30′ = 45°30′
    Grid (grid north 0°45′ west of true)45°30′ + 0°45′ = 46°15′
    Arbitrary (line to a spire at true 12°00′)45°30′ − 12°00′ = 33°30′
  7. Consequence. A field book entry such as "AB 40°00′" is incomplete: the same number set out from true north instead of magnetic north turns the line by 5°30′, which moves its far end by 100 × tan 5°30′ = 9.6 m at 100 m. The book must say which north (and, for magnetic, the date and place).
  8. True meridians converge. True meridians meet at the poles, so they are not parallel. Two stations 1 km apart east–west at latitude 13° lie 1/(111.32 cos 13°) = 0.00922° of longitude apart, and their true meridians differ in direction by 0.00922° × sin 13° = 0.00207°, about 7.5″. This is negligible in a compass survey but matters in long theodolite and GNSS work, which is why grid meridians, all parallel, are used for coordinates.
  9. Which meridian in practice. Magnetic for compass surveys, because the needle gives it at once; true for property boundaries, maps and long routes, because it does not change; grid for coordinate work with total stations and GNSS on a projected sheet; arbitrary for a small site plan that will never be joined to a map.
  10. Check. Each bearing in the table differs from the magnetic value only by the angle between its meridian and magnetic north (5°30′, 6°15′ and −6°30′), so all four describe the same ground line.
Understand Explain magnetic declination and isogonic lines. How is a magnetic bearing converted to a true bearing when the declination is 3°15′ east? 8 marks

Points to cover. Declination is the angle between true and magnetic meridians at a place; East declination: true bearing = magnetic bearing + declination; West declination: true bearing = magnetic bearing − declination; Isogonic lines join places of equal declination; the agonic line is the zero-declination line; Worked conversion with 3°15′ east, giving a numerical answer; Declination changes with time, so the year of observation must be stated.

Model answer.

  1. Declination. Magnetic declination is the horizontal angle between the true meridian and the magnetic meridian at a place. It is east when magnetic north lies east of true north, and west when it lies west.
  2. Why it varies. The earth's magnetic field is not aligned with its axis and changes slowly, so declination differs from place to place and changes with time (secular variation), with small daily changes as well. A declination is therefore quoted with its place and year.
  3. Isogonic and agonic lines. An isogonic line joins places with the same declination; an agonic line joins places with zero declination, where the needle points to true north. Maps of these lines let a surveyor read the declination for a site.
  4. Rule. With east declination the needle points east of true north, so the magnetic bearing of every line is smaller than its true bearing:
    east declination:  true bearing = magnetic bearing + declination
west declination:  true bearing = magnetic bearing − declination
  5. Worked conversion, declination 3°15′ east.
    Magnetic bearingWorkingTrue bearing
    112°30′112°30′ + 3°15′115°45′

    S 40°00′ W (WCB 220°00′)220°00′ + 3°15′223°15′ = S 43°15′ W
  6. The reverse. A true bearing of 115°45′ set out with a compass at the same place is laid off as 115°45′ − 3°15′ = 112°30′ magnetic.
  7. Change with time. If a map gives 3°15′ E in 2016 with an annual change of 2′ westward, then in 2026 the declination is 3°15′ − 10 × 2′ = 2°55′ E, and the 112°30′ line would read 115°45′ − 2°55′ = 112°50′ on a compass that year.
  8. Finding the declination at a site. (1) Read it from an isogonic chart or a geomagnetic model for the place and date. (2) Observe the true bearing of a line by the sun or Polaris and read the same line with the compass. Secular variation (over years, the largest), annual variation (a few minutes over the year), diurnal variation (a few minutes over the day) and irregular changes during magnetic storms. A compass survey repeated years later must be brought to the declination of the new date before old and new bearings are compared, or the same boundary will appear to have turned.

Remember List the four methods of plane table surveying and state one situation where each is preferred. 8 marks

Points to cover. Radiation: small area surveyed from a single station; Intersection or triangulation: distant or inaccessible objects, no chaining possible; Traversing: long narrow strips such as roads and canals; Resection: the table is set up at an unknown point and located by sights to known points; One practical situation for each method.

Model answer.

  1. Common start. In every method the table is set up, levelled, centred over the station with the plumbing fork, and oriented (by trough compass or by backsighting a plotted line) before rays are drawn.
  2. Radiation. From one station O, rays are drawn with the alidade to every object and the distance to each is taped and scaled along its ray. Preferred for a small open area visible from one point, such as a playground or a building site with all corners within one tape length or two.
  3. Intersection (graphic triangulation). From the two ends of a measured base line, rays are drawn to each object; the crossing of the rays fixes it and no distance to the object is measured. Preferred for inaccessible or distant points: a tree across a river, the far bank of a stream, a spire.
  4. Traversing. The table is moved from station to station along the route; at each station it is oriented by backsighting the previous station, the next line is drawn and its length taped. Preferred for long narrow strips: a road, a canal, a stream course.
  5. Resection. The table stands at a point not yet plotted, and its position on the sheet is found by sighting points already plotted (two-point or three-point problem). Preferred when the table must be set up at a convenient spot that was not occupied before, for example to pick up detail hidden from the existing stations.

    Radiationoneto every pointsmall open area
    Intersectiontwo (base line)base line onlyinaccessible points
    Traversingmany, along the routeevery traverse lineroads, canals
    Resectionthe unknown stationnone needed for Plocating a new set-up
  7. Two-point and three-point problems. In resection the three-point problem uses three well-spaced plotted points sighted from the new station; the tracing-paper method or Lehmann's trial method (shrinking the triangle of error) fixes the station. The two-point problem uses only two plotted points and an auxiliary station, so it takes longer and is used only when a third point is not visible.
  8. Scale and orientation in numbers. At 1:500 a taped distance of 42.5 m is drawn 42.5/500 = 0.085 m = 8.5 cm along the ray. An orientation error of 1° moves a point 50 m away sideways by 50 × tan 1° = 0.87 m on the ground, which is 1.75 mm on the sheet, seven times the 0.25 mm plotting accuracy. Understand Describe the checks used to test a compass traverse before adjustment. 8 marks

Points to cover. Difference between fore bearing and back bearing should be 180°; difference from 180° indicates local attraction or a reading blunder; Sum of interior angles should equal (2n − 4) × 90° for a closed polygon; Sum of deflection angles should be 360°; Sum of latitudes and sum of departures of a closed traverse should each be zero; Any large single mismatch points to a recording blunder rather than to local attraction.

Model answer.

  1. Why check before adjusting. Adjustment spreads small errors; it must never hide a blunder or local attraction. Four checks are made on the field data first.
  2. 1. Fore and back bearings. For every line, FB − BB = ±180°. A line that passes proves both its stations free from local attraction. Example: AB 46°00′ and BA 226°00′ differ by 180°00′, so A and B are free; CD 220°30′ and DC 40°00′ differ by 180°30′, so C or D is affected.
  3. 2. Sum of interior angles. For a closed polygon of n sides the interior angles sum to (2n − 4) × 90°. Local attraction does not affect an included angle (both bearings at a station are turned equally), so this check tests reading and booking.
    n = 4:  (2 × 4 − 4) × 90° = 360°
measured 84°00′ + 96°00′ + 89°30′ + 91°00′ = 360°30′  →  error +30′, correction −7′30″ each
  4. 3. Sum of deflection angles. For a closed traverse the algebraic sum of the deflection angles (right positive, left negative) is 360°.
  5. 4. Sums of latitudes and departures. For a closed traverse ΣL = 0 and ΣD = 0. The closing error e = √(ΣL² + ΣD²) and the relative precision P/e must meet the limit.
    ΣL = −0.23 m, ΣD = −0.12 m, P = 766 m
e = √(0.0529 + 0.0144) = 0.259 m,  precision 766/0.259 = 1 in 2950
  6. Reading the checks. Many small mismatches suggest ordinary random error; one large mismatch suggests a blunder. A closing error that lies parallel to one line suggests a wrong length in that line; a bearing blunder moves the end at right angles to the line.
  7. 5. Closing on known points. When the traverse runs between two stations whose coordinates are known, ΣL must equal Nend − Nstart and ΣD must equal Eend − Estart. Example: from (500.00 E, 800.00 N) to (742.30 E, 655.10 N), the sums should be ΣL = −144.90 m and ΣD = +242.30 m.
  8. 6. Field checks on lengths and directions. Measure each line in both directions (the two lengths should agree to about 1 in 500 for chained lines); sight a distant prominent object, such as a chimney, from several stations and see that its bearings plot to one point; take a tie line across the figure between two stations and compare its measured length with the length computed from the traverse.

  10. Check. The angle-sum correction −7′30″ × 4 = −30′ removes the +30′ exactly.

Apply Explain the radiation, intersection and traversing methods of plane table surveying with a sketch of each, and state the field checks used. 13 marks

Points to cover. Radiation: single station, rays to all objects, distances scaled; suit small areas; Intersection: two or more stations, rays drawn from each, object at the crossing; suit distant objects; Traversing: table shifted along the route, each leg plotted; suit roads and canals; Checks: two-point and three-point problem for orientation, closing the traverse on the starting station; Limitations: weather, weight of the board, scale distortion on large areas.

Model answer.

  1. Sketches. Radiation, intersection and traversing.
  2. Setting up. In all three, level the board, centre it over the station with the plumbing fork, and orient it (trough compass, or backsight along a plotted line) before drawing rays.
  3. Radiation. Set up at O, near the middle of the area, and mark o on the sheet. Sight each object with the alidade pivoted on o, draw a ray, tape the distance and scale it along the ray. Example: a corner 36.0 m away at 1:500 is plotted 36.0/500 = 7.2 cm from o. Suits a small area seen from one station.
  4. Intersection. Measure a base line AB and plot it as ab. Set up at A, orient along AB, and draw rays to every object; move to B, orient by backsighting A, and draw rays to the same objects. Each crossing fixes an object without taping to it. Example: a 60 m base at 1:500 is 12 cm on the sheet. Suits inaccessible points; keep the angle of cut between about 30° and 150°.
  5. Traversing. Set up at A, orient, draw the ray to B and plot the taped length; move to B, orient by backsighting A, draw the ray to C, and so on. Detail is picked up from each station by radiation or intersection. Suits roads and canals.
  6. Field checks. Draw a check ray to a third point from each station (it should pass through the plotted point); in intersection, fix one point from three stations, which should meet at one point; in traversing, close on the starting station or on a known point, and distribute any small closing error graphically in proportion to distance; re-check orientation by sighting a distant plotted point before leaving each station.
  7. Limitations. The board is heavy and awkward in wind and rain, the sheet is exposed, and the accuracy is graphic: about 0.25 mm on paper, which is 0.125 m on the ground at 1:500.
  8. Equipment. Drawing board on a tripod, alidade (plain or telescopic) with a fiducial edge, plumbing fork with plumb bob, spirit level, trough compass, drawing sheet fixed to the board, and a tape for distances.
  9. Orientation and centring in numbers. An orientation error of 1° moves a point 50 m away by 50 × tan 1° = 0.87 m, which is 1.75 mm at 1:500, so backsighting is preferred to the trough compass. A centring error e on the ground shifts the plotted point by e/500 at 1:500; keeping that under 0.25 mm needs e under 0.25 mm × 500 = 0.125 m, which the plumbing fork easily gives.
  10. Intersection worked. Base AB = 60 m. At A the ray to a tree T makes 62° with AB; at B it makes 55° with BA. The angle at T is 180° − 62° − 55° = 63°, a good cut. The sine rule gives the distances the rays should show when scaled:
    AT = 60 sin 55° / sin 63° = 60 × 0.81915 / 0.89101 = 55.16 m  → 11.03 cm at 1:500
BT = 60 sin 62° / sin 63° = 60 × 0.88295 / 0.89101 = 59.46 m  → 11.89 cm at 1:500
  11. Traversing: closing on the sheet. If a plane table traverse of 60 cm total length on the sheet closes with a 4.0 mm gap, each station is moved parallel to the gap by 4.0 mm × (distance along the traverse)/60 cm; a station 36 cm along moves 4.0 × 36/60 = 2.4 mm, and the last station moves the full 4.0 mm onto the start.
  12. Choosing among them. On one survey they are often combined: a traverse gives the frame of stations, radiation picks up the nearby detail from each, and intersection fixes the points that cannot be reached.
  13. Check. At 1:500, 1 cm on paper is 5 m on the ground: 7.2 cm × 5 = 36.0 m, matching the taped distance in the radiation example.

    deflection = 300°00′ − 236°40′ = 63°20′, positive, so 63°20′ right

  7. Convention. Whole circle bearings run from 0° to 360° clockwise from north; a reduced bearing is always 90° or less and names the meridian end (N or S) first.

Analyze Compare plane table surveying with compass traversing for a small topographic survey. Under what field conditions would you choose each? 13 marks

Points to cover. Plane table plots in the field, so no field book and no later plotting errors; Compass traverse records angles and distances; plotting happens later in the office with instruments; Plane table is fast for small-scale detail work but awkward on steep or windy ground; Compass work is affected by local attraction; plane table alidade is not magnetic unless a compass box is used; Accuracy: compass traverse with a tape gives better distances; plane table orientation errors accumulate; Choose plane table for rapid small-area mapping, compass for a long traverse needing recorded data.

Model answer.

  1. What each records. The plane table draws the map in the field: directions are rays on the sheet and distances are scaled on them. The compass traverse records bearings and lengths in a field book, and the plan is plotted (or the coordinates computed) later.
  2. Comparison.
    PointPlane tableCompass traverse
    Outputfinished plan on sitefield book, plotted later
    Detaileasy: fixed while seenneeds offsets and sketches
    Precision of directiongraphic, about 0.25 mm on paperleast count 30′ (prismatic)
    Magnetic troubleonly if oriented by compasslocal attraction at every station
    Weatherpoor in wind and rainlittle affected
    Record for laternone beyond the sheetnumbers that can be recomputed
    Equipmentheavy board and tripodlight
  3. Size of the errors. A 30′ error in a compass bearing moves the end of a 100 m line sideways by 100 × tan 0°30′; a plotting error of 0.25 mm at 1:1000 is 0.25 m on the ground.
    100 × tan 0°30′ = 100 × 0.00873 = 0.87 m
0.25 mm × 1000 = 0.25 m
  4. When to choose the plane table. A small, open, fairly flat area that needs a plan with plenty of detail quickly (a campus block, a village site at 1:500 or 1:1000), in fair weather, where the absence of a numerical record does not matter.
  5. When to choose the compass traverse. A long or wooded route (a forest track, a stream), rough ground or bad weather, or where the measurements must be kept and computed later; avoid it near railways, steel structures and power lines.
  6. Combining them. A compass traverse can give the frame, and the plane table can fill in detail from its stations.
  7. Errors peculiar to each. Plane table: orientation, centring, the alidade edge not passing through the station point, paper shrinkage and the instability of the board. Compass traverse: local attraction, a sluggish or bent needle, reading the ring to only 15′ or 30′, change of declination during the work, and plotting errors when the book is drawn up.
  8. A small topographic survey, in numbers. A site of about 200 m × 150 m mapped at 1:500 with some 60 detail points:
    plane table: plotting accuracy 0.25 mm × 500 = 0.125 m
compass: 30′ error at 150 m = 150 × tan 0°30′ = 1.31 m sideways
compass traverse of 700 m at 1 in 300 to 1 in 600: closing error 2.33 m to 1.17 m

    Dense trees, long narrow routecompasslight, needs only the next station in sight
    Near steel, rails or power linesplane table oriented by backsightno needle at each station

    Data must be kept and recomputedcompassthe field book is a numerical record
  10. Recommendation for the example site. A 200 m × 150 m open block with 60 detail points is a plane table job: two or three stations by radiation and intersection, the plan finished on site in about a day, and every point within 0.125 m. If the block were wooded or the work had to continue in the rains, a compass traverse round it with offsets to the detail would be chosen instead, accepting metre-level error.
  11. Check. On a 100 m line, the compass error (0.87 m) is larger than the plane-table plotting error at 1:1000 (0.25 m), so for short-range detail the plane table is the more precise of the two; over long routes the traverse keeps its edge because its errors can be checked and adjusted.
Evaluate Compare Bowditch's rule and the transit rule for adjusting a closed traverse, using the traverse AB 200 m at 45°, BC 180 m at 135°, CD 160 m at 210°, DA 226 m at 303°20′ (ΣL = −0.23 m, ΣD = −0.12 m). State which rule suits which instrument and why. 13 marks

Points to cover. Bowditch: corrections proportional to line length, assumes angular and linear errors are equally likely; Transit rule: corrections proportional to the magnitude of latitude or departure, assumes angular work is more precise; Bowditch suits compass traverses where bearings and lengths are of similar reliability; Transit rule suits theodolite traverses with taped or EDM lengths; Both remove the closing error but redistribute it differently; large errors should not be blindly adjusted; Guard against using adjustment to hide a blunder: first check the angle sum and the fore–back bearing difference.

Glossary

Adjusted coordinates
The station coordinates recomputed after traverse adjustment so that the figure closes exactly.
Alidade
A straight-edge ruler with a sight, used on the plane table to draw rays in the sighted direction.
Bearing of closing error
The direction of the gap between the start and the computed end, found from the total latitude and total departure errors.
Closed traverse
A traverse that returns to its starting station (a loop) or ends on another station of known position (a link traverse), so its closure can be checked.
Coordinate
The ordered pair of northing and easting that fixes a point's position relative to a chosen origin.
Grid meridian
The north direction defined by the vertical grid lines of a map projection, used for grid bearings.
Included angle
The angle at a traverse station between the two lines meeting there, measured either inside or outside the figure.
Local attraction
A local magnetic disturbance that deflects the compass needle away from the magnetic meridian.
Magnetic needle
A small magnetised steel needle pivoted at its centre that aligns itself with the local magnetic field.
Meridian
A reference direction line from the station to some north, from which bearings are measured clockwise.
Open traverse
A traverse that starts at a known point and ends at an unknown one, so no closure check is possible.
Orientation
Setting the plane table so that lines already drawn on the sheet point in the same true directions as on the ground.
Perimeter
The total length of all lines of the traverse, used as the denominator in Bowditch's distribution.
Plane table
A drawing board on a tripod, used with an alidade to plot survey lines directly in the field.
Precision of a traverse
The perimeter divided by the closing error, quoted as 1 in P/e and used to judge the quality of the work.
Prismatic compass
A survey compass with a prism eyepiece that lets the observer read the graduated ring while sighting the object.
Reduced bearing
A bearing written as an acute angle from the nearer end of the north–south line, with a quadrant letter such as N 30° E.
Resection
Fixing the table position at an unknown point by sighting two or three known points already plotted on the sheet.
Shoelace formula
The cross-product summation that gives the area of a closed polygon from the coordinates of its corners.
Surveyor's compass
A plain compass with a graduated ring and two sight vanes, read by eye from above the needle.
Three-point problem
A resection method that locates the table station from the angles subtended by three known points, solved graphically or by the trial-and-error method.
Traverse
A series of connected survey lines whose lengths and directions are measured to fix the positions of successive stations.
Two-point problem
A resection method that orients the table using two known points and an arbitrarily chosen auxiliary station.
Whole-circle bearing
A bearing measured clockwise from north through the full 0° to 360° range, also called the azimuth.
