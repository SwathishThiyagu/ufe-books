6.1 Electromagnetic radiation and the spectrum

Remote sensing measures a target without touching it by recording electromagnetic radiation. The sun, the earth and a radar transmitter all send this energy as coupled electric and magnetic fields. Wavelength fixes frequency and photon energy. That relation decides which sensor can see a crop, a warm roof or a cloud, and it shapes every later step: atmospheric passage, surface reflection, and the digital number stored in an image.

You need: Unit 1 for units, scale and the meaning of a measurement, and basic physics of waves; see 6.2 for what happens to the energy after it leaves the source.

Remote sensing and the wave picture

Remote sensing measures an object from a distance, without contact. A drone camera and a satellite sensor both do this. A passive system records radiation already present: reflected sunlight or the earth's own heat. An active system sends its own pulse and records the echo; radar and LiDAR are active.

Electromagnetic radiation (EMR) is a pair of fields, one electric and one magnetic, oscillating at right angles and travelling forward together. In vacuum they move at c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s; in air we use the same value. The wave repeats over a distance called the wavelength λ, in metres (m), micrometres (µm) or nanometres (nm). The number of repeats per second is frequency f, in hertz (Hz); 1 Hz is one cycle per second.

c = f λ

c is the speed of light in m/s; f is frequency in Hz; λ is wavelength in m. A longer wavelength means a lower frequency because c is fixed.

A photon is one quantum of EMR. Its energy is Ep = h f = h c ÷ λ, where Ep is photon energy in joules (J), h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s is Planck's constant, f is frequency in Hz, c is speed of light in m/s and λ is wavelength in m. Remote sensing also quotes energy in electron volts (eV), with 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

The electromagnetic spectrum

The electromagnetic spectrum is the full range of EMR, ordered by wavelength. Band names are conventions, not sharp walls. The table lists bands used in remote sensing.

Gamma rays and X-rays are ionising and have very short wavelengths. They are not used to image the earth's surface from orbit. The visible band, 0.4–0.7 µm, is the small part the human eye sees. Near infrared and short-wave infrared are reflected sunlight bands used for crops, moisture and minerals. Thermal infrared is emitted by the surface itself, and microwave can pass through cloud.

Two natural sources drive most passive remote sensing. The sun radiates roughly as a blackbody near 5800 K; its spectrum peaks near 0.5 µm, inside the visible band. The earth is much cooler, near 300 K, and its thermal emission peaks near 10 µm. Planck's law gives the smooth curve of blackbody radiance against wavelength. We use only its shape here: a peak, a steep fall on the short-wave side and a slower fall on the long-wave side. Solar-reflected sensing uses visible to short-wave infrared. Thermal sensing uses the 3–15 µm window.

Radiometric quantities and photon energy

Radiometry names the energy at each stage. Radiant energy Q is the total energy carried by radiation, in joules (J). Radiant flux Φ is energy per unit time, in watts (W); 1 W = 1 J/s. Irradiance Ee is radiant flux falling on a surface per unit area, in W/m². Radiance Le is flux leaving or arriving at a surface in a given direction, per unit projected area and per unit solid angle, in W m⁻² sr⁻¹. The steradian (sr) is the SI unit of solid angle. A satellite sensor measures radiance; irradiance describes the light arriving at the ground.

Ep(eV) = 1.24 ÷ λ(µm)

Ep is photon energy in electron volts; λ is wavelength in micrometres. The constant 1.24 comes from h c ÷ (1 eV).

Photon energy falls as wavelength rises. Green light at 0.55 µm has photons of about 2.25 eV. A thermal infrared photon at 10 µm has about 0.124 eV, roughly eighteen times weaker. This does not mean the infrared beam carries less power: power also depends on how many photons arrive per second. The skin feels warmth because many low-energy photons are absorbed and turned into heat.

Worked example

Question. Green light has wavelength λ = 0.55 µm. Find its frequency and its photon energy in joules and in electron volts. Use c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s and h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.

  1. Convert the wavelength to metres: λ = 0.55 µm = 0.55 × 10⁻⁶ m = 5.5 × 10⁻⁷ m.
  2. Frequency: f = c ÷ λ = 3 × 10⁸ ÷ (5.5 × 10⁻⁷) = 5.45 × 10¹⁴ Hz.
  3. Photon energy in joules: Ep = h f = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 5.45 × 10¹⁴ = 3.61 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  4. Photon energy in electron volts: Ep = 3.61 × 10⁻¹⁹ ÷ 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 2.25 eV. The shortcut 1.24 ÷ 0.55 gives the same value.

Answer. The frequency is 5.45 × 10¹⁴ Hz and the photon energy is 3.61 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, equal to 2.25 eV.

Exam traps

  • Wavelength and frequency move in opposite directions. A longer wavelength means lower frequency and lower photon energy, so an infrared photon is weaker than a visible photon even though infrared feels hot on the skin.
  • The wave equation is c = f λ, not c = f ÷ λ. Rearrange to f = c/λ and λ = c/f. Check units: Hz × m = m/s.
  • A micrometre is 10⁻⁶ m, not 10⁻³ m. Write 0.55 µm as 0.55 × 10⁻⁶ m before dividing. Mixing µm with m is the most common numerical slip.
  • Flux and irradiance are different. Flux is in watts; irradiance is in watts per square metre. A stronger lamp gives more flux, but the irradiance at a surface also depends on area and distance.
  • Near infrared around 0.7–1.4 µm is mostly reflected sunlight. Thermal infrared around 10 µm is emitted by the target. Do not call a 10 µm image a reflected infrared image.

6.2 Energy interactions with atmosphere and earth surfaces

Sunlight leaves the sun as broad-spectrum energy, crosses the atmosphere, strikes a surface, and travels back up to a sensor. On both trips the atmosphere scatters and absorbs part of it. At the surface the energy splits into reflected, absorbed and transmitted fractions. The sensor therefore records radiance — light arriving at the instrument — and not the reflectance of the ground. This chapter follows one beam along that path, quantifies the losses, and ends with the formula that converts measured radiance back to reflectance. Every comparison of two dates and every classification depends on that conversion.

You need Chapter 6.1 for wavelength, irradiance and radiance.

The path: sun, atmosphere, ground, atmosphere, sensor

Sunlight arrives at the top of the atmosphere as irradiance E, measured in watts per square metre (W/m²). The atmosphere removes part of it on the way down. The ground receives the rest and splits it into reflected, absorbed and transmitted fractions. On the way up the atmosphere removes more, and adds light of its own that never touched the ground. The sensor at the top records radiance L, in W m⁻² sr⁻¹ — power per unit area per unit solid angle, that is, the light arriving from one direction.

Two consequences follow. First, L is not a property of the ground alone; it depends on the sun, the atmosphere and the viewing direction. Second, a number that describes the ground must be recovered by undoing the atmospheric part. That number is reflectance.

The two trips are not equal. The downward beam is nearly parallel, so it crosses the shortest possible path. The upward beam is scattered into a wide cone and meets more air. A sensor looking straight down at noon sees the least atmosphere; a sensor looking obliquely sees much more, which is one reason off-nadir images look hazier.

Scattering: three regimes, set by particle size

Scattering redirects energy without absorbing it. The size of the particle compared with the wavelength decides the behaviour.

  • Rayleigh scattering — nitrogen and oxygen molecules, far smaller than any visible wavelength. Scattered intensity goes as 1/λ⁴. Blue light at 0.45 µm is scattered (0.70 ÷ 0.45)⁴ ≈ 5.8 times more strongly than red light at 0.70 µm. That is why the sky is blue and the direct sun looks slightly yellow, and why blue bands of satellite images are full of haze.
  • Mie scattering — aerosols such as dust, smoke, sea salt and pollen, roughly 0.1 µm to a few µm, close to the wavelength. It is only weakly wavelength dependent and is forward-peaked. It produces the grey-white veil over a city.
  • Non-selective scattering — cloud droplets and coarse dust, much larger than the wavelength. All wavelengths scatter about equally, so fog and cloud look white and hide the ground in every band.

Absorption and the atmospheric windows

Absorption converts radiation into heat. Only certain wavelengths are absorbed, because a molecule absorbs only photons whose energy matches its own transitions: water vapour near 1.4, 1.9 and 2.7 µm; carbon dioxide near 2.7 and 4.3 µm; ozone in the ultraviolet and near 9.6 µm; oxygen in narrow bands near 0.69 and 0.76 µm. Between these bands the atmosphere is largely transparent. The gaps are the atmospheric windows, and every optical sensor is built inside one.

Energy balance at the surface and the radiance-to-reflectance link

ρ + α + τ = 1

ρ is reflectance, α is absorptance and τ is transmittance — each the fraction of the energy arriving at the surface that is reflected, absorbed or passed through.

For an opaque surface such as soil or rock, τ = 0 and ρ + α = 1. Albedo is the reflectance averaged over the whole solar spectrum, weighted by the energy in each wavelength. Fresh snow has an albedo near 0.8–0.9 and stays cool; a dark asphalt road near 0.1 and heats up.

Reflection is specular or diffuse. A calm water surface is a mirror: almost all energy leaves at one angle equal to the incidence angle. A rough field scatters in all directions. A surface that scatters equally in every direction, so that the same radiance is seen from any viewpoint, is called Lambertian. Most natural land surfaces are approximately Lambertian.

ρ = π L / E

ρ is reflectance (dimensionless); L is the radiance leaving the surface in W m⁻² sr⁻¹; E is the irradiance falling on it in W m⁻²; π ≈ 3.1416 carries the geometry, because a constant radiance integrated over a hemisphere gives π L.

Path radiance. The sensor also receives light scattered into its line of sight that never reached the ground. Call it L_p; the measured radiance is L_target + L_p. A hazy image therefore looks brighter and flatter, and a reflectance computed from it without correction is too high. Removing L_p is atmospheric correction, handled in Chapter 6.7.

Worked example

Question. A flat, uniform field is imaged from directly above on a clear day. The irradiance reaching the field is E = 500 W/m² and the sensor measures a radiance L = 25 W m⁻² sr⁻¹ from it. (a) Treating the field as Lambertian, find its reflectance. (b) The same field is imaged again when haze cuts the irradiance at the surface to 250 W/m², and the measured radiance falls to 12.5 W m⁻² sr⁻¹. Find the reflectance again and comment. Take π = 3.1416.

  1. Write the working formula for a Lambertian surface: ρ = π L / E.
  2. Substitute part (a): ρ = 3.1416 × 25 ÷ 500.
  3. Numerator: 3.1416 × 25 = 78.54, in W m⁻² sr⁻¹, since π is dimensionless.
  4. Divide: 78.54 ÷ 500 = 0.15708.
  5. So ρ = 0.157, that is 15.7 per cent reflected. The remaining 84.3 per cent is absorbed or transmitted by the soil.
  6. Substitute part (b): ρ = 3.1416 × 12.5 ÷ 250.
  7. Numerator: 3.1416 × 12.5 = 39.27 W m⁻² sr⁻¹; divide: 39.27 ÷ 250 = 0.15708.
  8. Both L and E halved, so the ratio is unchanged. The radiance fell because the illumination fell, not because the field changed.

Answer. The field's reflectance is 0.157, or 15.7 per cent, in both cases; the radiance halved only because the irradiance halved.

Exam traps

  • Radiance is not reflectance. Radiance changes with sun angle, haze and season; reflectance does not. Convert digital numbers to reflectance before comparing two dates.
  • Rayleigh scattering goes as 1/λ⁴, not 1/λ, and short wavelengths scatter most. The sky is blue because blue is scattered out of the beam, not because blue is absorbed.
  • Albedo is a broadband average, never the reflectance of a single band. Quoting a one-band value as albedo loses marks.
  • Path radiance always adds to the signal. An uncorrected hazy image gives a reflectance that is too high, never too low.
  • For an opaque surface τ = 0, so ρ + α = 1. Writing ρ + α + τ = 1 and then setting τ = 1 reverses the physics.

6.3 Spectral reflectance of vegetation, soil and water

A spectral signature is the curve of reflectance against wavelength for a surface. Vegetation, soil and water absorb and scatter light differently, so each has a different curve. A sensor records radiance in a few bands; compare those values with known curves and you can tell crop from bare field from pond. This is the basis of land-cover mapping, crop monitoring and water mapping from satellites. The curve is measured as reflectance, a ratio from 0 to 1 or a percentage, so it is independent of the sun's brightness at the moment of measurement.

You need Chapter 6.2 for reflectance and atmospheric effects, and Chapter 6.1 for band wavelengths.

The spectral signature

Reflectance ρ(λ) is the fraction of light arriving at a surface that the surface sends back at wavelength λ. Wavelength λ is the distance between wave crests, measured here in micrometres (µm). Reflectance has no unit; write it as 0 to 1 or as a percentage. A spectral signature is a plot of ρ against λ from 0.4 µm to 2.5 µm. The visible part is 0.4–0.7 µm. The reflective infrared runs from 0.7 to 2.5 µm. It is split into the near-infrared (NIR), about 0.7–1.3 µm, and the shortwave infrared (SWIR), about 1.3–2.5 µm.

Why a curve and not one number: every material absorbs some wavelengths and scatters others. Absorption by pigments, water or minerals makes dips. Scattering by leaf cells, sand grains or suspended particles makes broad highs. The shape, not one band, identifies the surface. A satellite with a few bands samples this curve at fixed wavelengths; the sampled values are the measurements you classify in Chapter 6.7.

Vegetation: pigments and the red edge

Chlorophyll absorbs blue light near 0.45 µm and red light near 0.67 µm for photosynthesis. It absorbs green near 0.55 µm less strongly, so a leaf has a small green peak: reflectance near 12% in the figure, against about 5% in blue and red. The green peak is small. The big feature is the red edge: between about 0.67 µm and 0.75 µm reflectance rises steeply, from 5% to 50% in the figure.

From 0.7 to 1.1 µm, on the near-infrared plateau, a healthy leaf is bright. The cause is internal cell structure: light enters the leaf, bounces between cell walls and air spaces, and much of it leaves again. Pigments do not absorb NIR. A dense crop can reflect 40–50% or more. Beyond 1.1 µm, leaf water absorbs; bands near 1.4 µm and 1.9 µm are deep dips, and the curve falls to 25% at 1.6 µm and 18% at 2.2 µm in the figure.

Stressed or sparse vegetation has lower NIR and higher red, so the red edge weakens. This is why vegetation indices use red and NIR together.

Soil, water, snow and NDVI

Soil has a smooth curve that rises slowly from visible to shortwave infrared: 10% at 0.45 µm, 18% at 0.67 µm and 34% at 2.2 µm in the figure for dry soil. There are no sharp pigment bands. Moisture lowers the whole curve because water absorbs; organic matter darkens it; iron oxides raise red and lower blue; texture sets brightness. Dry sand is bright; wet clay is dark.

Clear water is brightest in blue (8% at 0.45 µm in the figure), falls through green and red, and is almost black in NIR (1% beyond 1.1 µm). Water absorbs NIR strongly, so ponds and rivers are dark in NIR images. Turbidity from suspended sediment raises visible reflectance, especially red. Algae add chlorophyll, which can create a green peak and red absorption. Snow is very bright in the visible and drops in shortwave infrared because ice absorbs there.

NDVI = (ρNIR − ρred) ÷ (ρNIR + ρred)

ρNIR is the near-infrared reflectance and ρred is the red reflectance; both are unitless fractions from 0 to 1. NDVI runs from −1 to +1.

High NIR and low red give values near +1 for green vegetation. Soil gives a low positive value; water gives zero or negative. Other indices are SAVI, EVI, NDWI and NDBI. A spectral library stores measured curves with labels and metadata; matching compares an image spectrum to library entries.

Worked example

Question. A crop pixel has red reflectance 0.10 and near-infrared reflectance 0.50. A bare soil pixel nearby has red reflectance 0.24 and near-infrared reflectance 0.32. Compute NDVI for both pixels. State which pixel is vegetation and why.

  1. Write the formula: NDVI = (ρ_NIR − ρ_red) ÷ (ρ_NIR + ρ_red). The reflectances are unitless.
  2. Crop numerator: 0.50 − 0.10 = 0.40. Crop denominator: 0.50 + 0.10 = 0.60. Crop NDVI = 0.40 ÷ 0.60 = 0.6667, about 0.67.
  3. Soil numerator: 0.32 − 0.24 = 0.08. Soil denominator: 0.32 + 0.24 = 0.56. Soil NDVI = 0.08 ÷ 0.56 = 0.1429, about 0.14.
  4. Compare: the crop value 0.67 lies high on the scale, towards +1, because NIR is high and red is low; 0.67 is a typical dense-vegetation value. The soil value 0.14 is low.

Answer. Crop NDVI is about 0.67 and soil NDVI is about 0.14; the crop pixel is vegetation because high NIR with low red gives a value high on the scale, towards +1; 0.67 is typical of dense vegetation.

Exam traps

  • The green peak is not the brightest part of vegetation. NIR reflectance is much higher, so a false-colour image shows crops in red, not green.
  • Water is dark in NIR because water absorbs NIR strongly; it is not black in the visible. Clear water still reflects more blue than red.
  • NDVI is a ratio, not a percentage. Do not write 0.67 as 67% unless you multiply by 100, and do not swap NIR and red.
  • Wet soil is darker than dry soil, not brighter. Moisture absorption lowers the whole soil curve.
  • Snow is bright in visible bands but drops in shortwave infrared. A bright visible pixel is not automatically cloud or sand.

6.4 Remote sensing platforms and sensors

A remote sensing system is a platform carrying a sensor. The platform fixes the height, the orbit and the viewing time. The sensor fixes the bands and the angular field of view of one detector; the platform height turns that angle into a ground pixel size, p = θH. Orbit and sensor geometry decide what the image looks like: a weather satellite sees one hemisphere all day, while a polar satellite repeats a narrow strip at the same local solar time. This chapter classifies platforms and sensors, explains whiskbroom and pushbroom scanning, and links the choice to Indian missions such as Resourcesat, Cartosat, RISAT and INSAT.

You need: 6.1 for the spectrum and 6.2 for atmospheric windows that decide which bands reach the sensor.

Platforms and orbits

A platform is the carrier that holds the sensor. Platforms are classed by height.

  • Ground: tripod, mast or crane. It gives millimetre to centimetre detail over a small site, as in a laboratory or a bridge inspection.
  • Airborne: UAV at a few hundred metres, or aircraft at 1–10 km. It covers a site on demand for crop health, construction progress and disaster response. For UAV hardware, see Unit 5.
  • Space: satellite at 400–36,000 km. It gives repeated wide coverage for national mapping and weather.

Orbit decides the viewing pattern. A sun-synchronous orbit is near-polar, with inclination about 98°. Inclination is measured from the equatorial plane, from 0° to 180°. Values above 90° mean the orbit moves against Earth's rotation (retrograde); sun-synchronous orbits are retrograde, so about 98°. The satellite crosses a place at the same local solar time on each pass, so shadows and sun angle match between dates. Landsat, Sentinel-2 and Resourcesat use this. A geostationary orbit is circular over the equator at 35,786 km, with a period of one sidereal day. The satellite stays over one longitude and sees the same hemisphere continuously. INSAT and GOES use this for weather. A repeat cycle is the number of days between identical ground tracks; a short cycle gives frequent temporal coverage.

Sensors and scanning geometry

Sensors are passive or active. A passive sensor measures natural radiation: reflected sunlight in optical bands, or emitted thermal infrared. It needs daylight for reflected bands and cannot see through cloud in the visible. An active sensor carries its own transmitter and measures the return. SAR sends microwaves and records amplitude and phase; it works through cloud and at night. LiDAR sends laser pulses and measures range. A microwave radiometer is passive; it measures weak natural microwave emission.

Imaging geometry has three common forms. A framing camera takes a whole two-dimensional picture at once, like a phone camera. A whiskbroom scanner has a mirror that sweeps across the flight track while the platform moves along it. A pushbroom scanner has a fixed linear array of detectors across the track; the platform's motion along the track builds the image. The swath is the ground width covered by one complete cross-track scan: one sweep of the mirror for a whiskbroom, the full detector line for a pushbroom. A multispectral sensor has several narrow bands; a panchromatic band is one wide band used for sharpening.

p = S / N and θ = p / H

p is the ground pixel size in metres; S is the swath width in metres; N is the number of detectors in the linear array; θ is the detector field of view in radians; H is the platform height in metres.

Indian systems show the choices. Resourcesat satellites are sun-synchronous and carry multispectral sensors such as LISS-3, LISS-4 and the wide AWiFS. Worked example

Question. A pushbroom sensor flies at a height of 600 km. Its linear array has 6000 detectors and images a swath 60 km wide on the ground. (a) Find the ground pixel size. (b) Find the field of view of one detector in radians and microradians. (c) Find the pixel size if the same array flies at 300 km.

  1. Convert the swath to metres: S = 60 km = 60,000 m. The number of detectors is N = 6000.
  2. Ground pixel size: p = S / N = 60,000 m / 6000 = 10.0 m.
  3. Detector field of view: θ = p / H = 10.0 m / 600,000 m = 1.67 × 10⁻⁵ rad. This is rounded to three significant figures.
  4. In microradians: θ = 1.67 × 10⁻⁵ × 10⁶ = 16.7 µrad.
  5. At height 300 km: p = θ H = 1.67 × 10⁻⁵ × 300,000 m = 5.0 m (to three significant figures).

Answer. The ground pixel is 10.0 m, the detector field of view is 1.67 × 10⁻⁵ rad (16.7 µrad), and at 300 km the pixel is 5.0 m. These are rounded to three significant figures.

  • Sun-synchronous is near-polar and gives the same local solar time; geostationary is over the equator at 35,786 km and gives a continuous view of one hemisphere. The examiner's favourite question asks which orbit gives repeatable sun angle.
  • SAR is active, not passive. Thermal infrared is passive, even though it works at night. A sensor is active only if it carries its own transmitter.
  • Use metres in p = S / N and θ = p / H. A swath in kilometres must be multiplied by 1000 before dividing by the detector count.
  • Panchromatic is one wide band, not a set of narrow multispectral bands. It gives fine spatial detail but cannot separate crop type by spectral curve.

6.5 Spatial, spectral, radiometric and temporal resolution

Every remote sensing image has four limits: spatial, spectral, radiometric and temporal resolution. Spatial fixes the ground size of a pixel, spectral fixes the number and width of wavelength bands, radiometric fixes the number of discrete levels, and temporal fixes the revisit time. These limits decide what a sensor can see and how often. They are used to choose imagery for crop monitoring, urban mapping, disaster response and water studies. Each finer limit is bought at a cost in another.

You need Chapter 6.4 for platform height, swath and detector arrays, and Chapter 6.1 for wavelength.

Spatial resolution is the ground pixel size

An image is a grid of picture elements, pixels. Each pixel stores a digital number (DN), an integer code for the radiance received from its ground area. Spatial resolution is the ground size of one pixel. A smaller ground pixel size is a finer spatial resolution; 5 m is finer than 30 m. The sensor height and its instantaneous field of view (IFOV) set it.

GSD = H × θ

GSD is the ground sampling distance in metres; H is the sensor height above ground in metres; θ is the instantaneous field of view in radians.

A sensor at H = 700 km with θ = 10 μrad has GSD = 700 000 m × 10 × 10⁻⁶ = 7 m. A larger IFOV or a higher platform gives a coarser pixel.

When one pixel covers two covers, such as field and road, it is a mixed pixel. Its DN is roughly the area-weighted average of the two radiances. The pixel then belongs fully to neither class. Finer pixels reduce mixing but cost data volume and often a narrower swath. The figure shows nine 10 m pixels averaged into one 30 m pixel.

Spectral and radiometric resolution

Spectral resolution describes the number and width of wavelength bands. A panchromatic sensor has one broad band and collects much energy. A multispectral sensor has a few bands, for example visible and near infrared. A hyperspectral sensor has many narrow, contiguous bands. Narrow bands help separate covers with similar spectral signatures, such as two crops.

Radiometric resolution is the number of discrete levels used to record radiance. An n-bit sensor gives 2ⁿ levels. An 8-bit sensor gives 256 levels with DN from 0 to 255. A 12-bit sensor gives 4096 levels with DN from 0 to 4095. More bits mean finer steps between levels, not a larger pixel.

ΔL = (Lmax − Lmin) ÷ (2ⁿ − 1)

ΔL is the quantisation step in radiance units; Lmax and Lmin are the radiance limits of the sensor; n is the number of bits per pixel; 2ⁿ is the number of levels.

A smaller ΔL means higher radiometric resolution. More spectral bands split the incoming energy into smaller portions, so each band has less signal and a lower signal-to-noise ratio unless the sensor is larger or integration time is longer.

Temporal resolution and the trade-offs

Temporal resolution is the revisit time, also called the repeat cycle: the time between two images of the same ground point. A geostationary sensor stares at the same area and repeats in minutes. A polar-orbiting sensor moves in a swath and repeats in days. Wide-swath sensors revisit more often than narrow-swath sensors at the same altitude.

The four resolutions compete. A finer GSD needs a smaller detector footprint on the ground or a longer focal length, which usually narrows the swath and lengthens revisit. More bands divide the same optical energy, so each band is noisier. Higher radiometric resolution increases data volume. Good design picks the combination the task needs: crop mapping wants spectral and temporal detail; a city map wants fine spatial detail.

Ground sampling distance is not the whole story. Real image quality also depends on the modulation transfer function (MTF), which measures how well contrast survives at fine detail, and on signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Only a few universities teach MTF and SNR as resolution measures; see Chapter 6.7 for how resampling changes pixel size.

Worked example

Question. A 30 m sensor images a 30 m × 30 m block. The same block is seen by a 10 m sensor as nine pixels with digital numbers 10, 10, 10, 10, 70, 70, 10, 70, 70. Find the single digital number of the block in the 30 m image and the fraction of the block that is bright cover.

  1. Each 10 m pixel covers 100 m²; nine cover 900 m², equal to one 30 m pixel of 30 m × 30 m.
  2. Sum the DNs: 10 + 10 + 10 + 10 + 70 + 70 + 10 + 70 + 70 = 330.
  3. Mean DN = 330 ÷ 9 = 36.666..., which rounds to 37.
  4. The bright pixels are four of nine: 4 ÷ 9 = 0.4444 = 44.4 per cent.
  5. The coarse DN 37 is between 10 and 70, so the 30 m pixel is mixed; it belongs fully to neither cover.

Answer. The 30 m sensor records digital number 37, and 44.4 per cent of the block is bright cover.

Exam traps

  • Finer spatial resolution means a smaller ground pixel size. 5 m is finer than 30 m, not coarser.
  • Higher radiometric resolution means more bits and more levels, not a bigger pixel or more bands.
  • Spectral resolution is about band number and width, not about how small a pixel is.
  • A mixed pixel is not a broken measurement; it is the average of covers inside the pixel.
  • Temporal resolution is revisit time, not the time taken to process an image.

6.6 Visual image interpretation

Visual image interpretation is the reading of an image by a trained eye before any software is used. The interpreter pools tone and colour, shape, size, pattern, texture, shadow, site and association to name each object and draw its boundary. It exists because a single pixel or a single band cannot say whether a grey patch is a roof, a dry field or a shadow. It is used to make land-use maps, check crop area, map forests and wetlands, assess flood or fire damage, and update topographic sheets from new imagery.

You need Unit 1 for scales and map measurement, and 6.3 and 6.5 for signatures and pixel size.

Elements of visual interpretation

Start with the recorded appearance, then add context. Each element answers a different question about the same target.

  • Tone is the brightness of the target in the recorded band; colour is its hue when three bands are shown. Deep water is dark; dry sand is bright.
  • Shape is the outline of the object. A rectangular field, a straight canal and a winding river have different shapes. Size is the outline measured against the image scale; it separates a house from a shed.
  • Pattern is the arrangement of discrete objects, such as an orchard grid or terrace lines. Texture is the frequency of tone change inside a patch; smooth water and rough canopy differ.
  • Shadow is the dark area cast by an object. Its direction and length give cues to height and sun position, but it hides the ground inside it.
  • Site is where the object sits relative to terrain, drainage and transport. Association is what normally lies beside it, such as a car park beside a stadium.

No single element decides a class. A dark rectangle may be a roof or a pond; shape, texture and association settle the name.

Keys, image types and timing

An interpretation key is a written or pictorial table that links a land cover to its expected appearance: tone, shape, texture, season and usual neighbours. The interpreter compares the image with the key, then checks doubtful patches in the field.

  • Black-and-white panchromatic image: one broad band, fine detail, no colour.
  • True colour image: red, green and blue bands shown as red, green and blue. Colour looks natural.
  • False-colour infrared composite: near-infrared, red and green bands shown as red, green and blue. Healthy vegetation is bright red because it reflects near-infrared strongly.
  • Thermal image: emitted heat, shown as brightness. Warm roofs and hot sand are bright; it can be taken at night.

Aerial photographs come from aircraft: large scale, high detail, flown on demand, but limited repeat coverage. Satellite images come from fixed orbits: repeated coverage, several spectral bands, usually smaller scale. The two are compared, not ranked.

Season changes the target. A kharif crop field and a rabi crop field look different; a deciduous forest loses leaves. Sun angle changes shadow length and brightness. A low sun gives long shadows and strong relief; a high sun gives short shadows and flatter tone.

Multi-date visual comparison places two or more images of the same area side by side. The interpreter looks for new edges, lost patches and colour or tone changes. Images from the same season are preferred, so that crop stage is not mistaken for real change.

Image scale and ground measurement

Scale relates a distance on the image to the same distance on the ground. A representative fraction 1:50,000 means one unit on the image represents 50,000 of the same unit on the ground.

S = dp ÷ Dg = 1 : M

S is the scale ratio; dp is the photo distance; Dg is the ground distance in the same unit; M is the denominator of the representative fraction.

At 1:50,000, one centimetre on the image represents 50,000 cm on the ground, which is 500 m. Measure several known distances and average them if the photo is tilted or the terrain is steep.

  • Linear scale factor is 1/50,000.
  • Area scale factor is the square of the linear factor. At 1:50,000, one square centimetre represents 500 m × 500 m = 250,000 m² = 0.25 km².
  • Multiply a measured photo area by the area represented by one square centimetre to get ground area.

Worked example

Question. An aerial photograph has scale 1:50,000. A forest patch measures 6.4 cm² on the photo. Two field corners are 4.2 cm apart on the same photo. Find the ground distance between the corners and the forest area in km².

  1. Scale 1:50,000 means 1 cm on the photo represents 50,000 cm on the ground.
  2. Convert to metres: 50,000 cm ÷ 100 = 500 m. So 1 cm on the photo represents 500 m on the ground.
  3. Ground distance between the corners = 4.2 cm × 500 m/cm = 2100 m = 2.1 km.
  4. For area, 1 cm² on the photo represents 500 m × 500 m = 250,000 m².
  5. Convert to square kilometres: 250,000 m² ÷ 1,000,000 = 0.25 km² per cm².
  6. Forest area = 6.4 cm² × 0.25 km²/cm² = 1.6 km².

Answer. The ground distance is 2100 m and the forest area is 1.6 km².

Exam traps

  • Shadow is not a dark surface. It hides the ground it falls on, so confirm the class from the sun-facing side or from another date.
  • Pattern is the arrangement of separate objects; texture is the fine change of tone inside one patch. An examiner may swap the two words.
  • Area scales as the square of the linear scale. At 1:50,000, 1 cm² is 0.25 km², not 0.5 km².
  • Season and sun angle can create false change. Two images of the same field may differ because one is kharif and the other is rabi, or because one has long shadows.
  • The representative fraction has no unit. Convert photo and ground distances to the same unit before dividing, and do not mix centimetres with kilometres.

6.7 Digital image processing: enhancement and classification

A satellite does not hand you a map. It hands you a grid of digital numbers. Digital image processing turns that raw grid into a corrected image, stretches and filters it so the eye can read it, and then asks a computer to sort every pixel into a class — water, crop, built-up. A final error matrix says how much to trust the labels. District crop surveys, urban growth studies and flood damage maps all depend on this chain.

You need the spectral signatures of 6.3, the resolutions of 6.5, and the visual keys of 6.6, which become the training classes.

Correcting the numbers first

A digital image is a grid of square pixels. Each pixel holds a digital number (DN) — a whole number stored at the radiometric resolution of the sensor, 0 to 255 for an 8-bit image. One grid is one band. A multispectral image stacks several bands pixel for pixel, so band 3 and band 4 at row 120, column 45 describe the same patch of ground.

Radiometric correction fixes brightness. Sensor calibration removes detector drift using known on-board references. Sun angle correction divides reflectance by the cosine of the solar zenith angle, because a low winter sun or a slope tilted away receives less energy and would look falsely dark. Haze correction subtracts path radiance, the light scattered into the sensor by the atmosphere, which lifts even the darkest pixel above zero.

Geometric correction fixes position. Ground control points (GCPs) are features — a road junction, a tank bund, a bridge — whose image coordinates and map coordinates are both known. A transformation is fitted through them and the image is resampled onto the map grid. Nearest neighbour copies the closest original DN and keeps values intact. Bilinear interpolation takes the four nearest input pixels and averages them with weights that fall with distance, so the result looks smoother but invents new DN values.

Enhancement: stretch, filter, composite

A histogram counts how many pixels carry each DN. A raw scene often fills only a narrow slice of the range because of haze or weak contrast, so it looks grey and flat. A linear contrast stretch pulls the used range out to the full display range.

DN(out) = (DN(in) − min) ÷ (max − min) × 255

DN(in) is the input digital number; min and max are the lowest and highest values kept, read from the histogram; DN(out) is the stretched value, clipped to 0–255; 255 is the top of an 8-bit display.

Histogram equalisation is a stronger, non-linear stretch. It reassigns values so every brightness level holds roughly the same number of pixels. Detail in dark forest opens up, but noise in a clear reservoir can be exaggerated.

A spatial filter slides a small kernel, usually 3 × 3, across the image. A low-pass kernel gives every cell the weight 1/9; the output is the average of the nine cells in the window, the centre pixel included, which smooths noise and blurs edges. A high-pass kernel puts a large positive weight at the centre and negative weights around it; flat ground cancels to near zero and only edges survive, so the image looks sharpened.

A false colour composite loads three bands into the red, green and blue channels of the display. The usual choice sends near-infrared to red, red to green and green to blue. Healthy vegetation reflects strongly in the near-infrared, so it appears bright red, while water stays dark.

Features: ratios, NDVI and PCA

A band ratio divides one band by another, pixel by pixel. Dividing cancels much of the illumination difference between a sunlit slope and a shaded one, so the ratio shows the material rather than the light falling on it.

NDVI = (ρ(NIR) − ρ(red)) ÷ (ρ(NIR) + ρ(red))

ρ(NIR) and ρ(red) are reflectance or DN in the near-infrared and red bands; NDVI runs from −1 to +1; healthy crops approach 0.8, bare soil about 0.2, water and shadow are negative, and thick cloud stays close to zero.

Principal component analysis (PCA) rotates the bands into new uncorrelated axes called components, ordered by variance. The first component carries most of the brightness, later ones carry subtle spectral differences. Only a few universities teach the eigenvector working in full; know what the method is for.

Vegetation indices drive crop monitoring, drought watch and forest cover work. Ratios of red and near-infrared also separate iron-rich rock from vegetation in geological mapping.

Classification and accuracy

In supervised classification the analyst draws training areas for each class, using the visual keys from 6.6. Software computes the mean and covariance of every class. The maximum likelihood rule then assigns each pixel to the class with the highest probability, assuming the class values follow a normal distribution.

In unsupervised classification there are no training areas. The k-means algorithm picks k clusters, seeds a mean for each, assigns every pixel to the nearest mean, recomputes the means, and repeats until the means stop moving. The analyst then names the clusters — water, crop, built-up — by looking at them on the image.

Supervised gives named classes directly and needs ground knowledge; unsupervised finds groups the analyst may not have expected and needs labelling afterwards. After classification, a majority filter removes isolated single pixels, and the classes are labelled on the map.

Accuracy is judged against test pixels checked on the ground, listed in an error matrix of classes mapped against classes true. Overall accuracy is the diagonal sum divided by the total. Kappa removes the agreement that chance alone would produce.

κ = (p(o) − p(e)) ÷ (1 − p(e))

p(o) is the observed agreement, the fraction of test pixels on the diagonal; p(e) is the agreement expected by chance from the row and column totals; κ = 1 is perfect agreement, κ = 0 is chance level.

Worked example

Question. One band has digital numbers from 40 to 90. It is stretched linearly to the full 0–255 range. Write the stretch formula, find the output for input DN 40, 65 and 90, and state what happens to a pixel with DN 30.

  1. Read the ends from the histogram: min = 40 and max = 90, so the range is 90 − 40 = 50.
  2. Write the stretch: DN(out) = (DN(in) − 40) ÷ 50 × 255, which is 5.1 × (DN(in) − 40).
  3. Input 40: (40 − 40) ÷ 50 × 255 = 0 ÷ 50 × 255 = 0.
  4. Input 65: (65 − 40) ÷ 50 × 255 = 25 ÷ 50 × 255 = 0.5 × 255 = 127.5; rounded for an 8-bit store, 128.
  5. Input 90: (90 − 40) ÷ 50 × 255 = 50 ÷ 50 × 255 = 255.
  6. Input 30: (30 − 40) ÷ 50 × 255 = −10 ÷ 50 × 255 = −51, which is outside the display range and is clipped to 0.

Answer. The stretch gives DN 40 → 0, DN 65 → 127.5 (stored as 128) and DN 90 → 255; any DN below 40 clips to 0.

Exam traps

  • High overall accuracy does not remove class confusion. Read the error matrix by row and column. A class can be 95 per cent correct on the diagonal (user's accuracy) and yet have a low producer's accuracy, because many of its own ground pixels were labelled as another class. Check both directions before trusting any single class.
  • Radiometric correction is about brightness, geometric correction about position. Marks are lost by swapping the two.
  • A contrast stretch changes only the display values, not the stored reflectance. Do not claim it corrects the image.
  • NDVI is (NIR − red) ÷ (NIR + red). Reversing the order gives a negative index and a wrong map, and water genuinely has a negative NDVI.

Radiance (L)Radiant flux leaving a unit area into a unit solid angle, in W·m⁻²·sr⁻¹; what the sensor actually measures6.2
Spectral signatureThe curve of reflectance against wavelength for a surface; its shape identifies the material6.3

Takeaways

  1. Every remote sensing measurement begins with electromagnetic energy, and wavelength alone fixes both the frequency and the photon energy.
  2. Between the sun and the sensor the atmosphere scatters and absorbs energy, so the sensor records radiance that has been altered twice.
  3. The atmosphere is transparent only in certain windows, which is why sensors are built for specific wavelength bands.
  4. Reflectance is a property of the surface; radiance is what reaches the sensor, and the two differ by atmospheric effects and illumination geometry.
  5. Vegetation, soil and water each have a distinct reflectance-versus-wavelength curve, and the differences between the curves allow identification.
  6. A remote sensing system is a platform plus a sensor; orbit choice fixes repeat time and viewing geometry, and sensor geometry fixes image shape.
  7. The four resolutions limit what an image can show, and improving one usually costs another.
  8. Visual interpretation combines tone, shape, size, pattern, texture, shadow and association with site knowledge before any software is used.
  9. Digital processing turns raw digital numbers into corrected, enhanced images and then into labelled classes, with an accuracy table to judge the labels.

Exam question bank

Long answers are expected to run in one line from electromagnetic radiation and its interaction with the atmosphere and target, through sensors, platforms and the four resolutions, to visual interpretation and digital classification, using spectral signature curves as the connecting thread.

Remember and understand

Understand Explain the wave and particle models of electromagnetic radiation. Show how wavelength fixes frequency and photon energy, and name the spectral regions used in optical remote sensing with their approximate wavelength ranges. 13 marks

Oscillating electric and magnetic fields at right angles, travelling at c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s; ν = c ÷ λ and Q = hν = hc ÷ λ, with h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s; Inverse relation: longer wavelength means lower frequency and lower photon energy; Regions: visible 0.4–0.7 μm, near-infrared 0.7–1.3 μm, shortwave infrared 1.3–3 μm, thermal infrared 8–14 μm, microwave 1 mm–1 m; Wave model explains reflection, refraction, diffraction; particle model explains absorption and emission; Why band choice follows the physics: thermal bands detect emission, microwave penetrates cloud.

Understand Describe how the atmosphere modifies incoming and outgoing electromagnetic radiation. Explain Rayleigh, Mie and non-selective scattering, absorption, and the idea of atmospheric windows. 13 marks

Scattering and absorption act on the downward solar path and again on the upward path to the sensor; Rayleigh: particles much smaller than λ, intensity varies as 1 ÷ λ⁴, strong in blue, causes haze; Mie: particles about the size of λ (dust, smoke, aerosols), affects longer wavelengths; Non-selective: particles much larger than λ (water droplets, cloud), scatters all visible wavelengths equally, gives white cloud; Absorption by ozone, carbon dioxide and water vapour in specific bands; Atmospheric windows: visible, 1.5–1.8 μm, 2.0–2.4 μm, 8–14 μm, and microwave; Effect on image: reduced contrast, bluish haze, need for atmospheric correction.

Understand Draw and explain the spectral reflectance curves of healthy vegetation, dry soil and clear water over the visible to shortwave infrared range. State how the curves are used to identify these surfaces. 13 marks

Vegetation: chlorophyll absorption in blue and red, small green peak near 0.55 μm, red edge at 0.68–0.75 μm, high near-infrared plateau from leaf cell structure, water absorption dips near 1.4 and 1.9 μm; Soil: smooth, gradually rising curve, no sharp features; brightness depends on moisture, organic matter, iron, texture; Water: low reflectance in visible, falls further into near-infrared; turbid water rises in visible; clear water almost black in near-infrared; Snow: very high reflectance in visible, drops in shortwave infrared; Curve shape as a signature; band ratios separate the surfaces; Physical reasons behind each feature, not just shape.

Understand Classify remote sensing platforms and sensors. Explain passive and active sensors, geostationary and sun-synchronous orbits, and the difference between whiskbroom and pushbroom scanning. 13 marks

Platforms: ground-based, airborne (aircraft, UAV), spaceborne (satellite, space station); Passive sensors use reflected or emitted natural radiation; active sensors carry their own transmitter, e.g. radar, LiDAR; Geostationary orbit: about 36,000 km above the equator, same period as the Earth, fixed view, coarse resolution, continuous monitoring; Sun-synchronous near-polar orbit at about 700–900 km, fixed local solar time, global coverage, repeat cycle of days; Whiskbroom: single detector with a rotating mirror scans across track, dwell time limits radiometric precision; Pushbroom: linear detector array images a full line, longer dwell time, no moving mirror, geometric distortions differ; Examples of sensor types: panchromatic, multispectral, hyperspectral, thermal, SAR, LiDAR.

Understand Explain the four resolutions of a remote sensing image with suitable examples, and describe the trade-offs between them. 13 marks

Spatial resolution: ground size of one pixel, in metres; 5.8 m on one well-known Indian imaging satellite versus 10 m to 30 m on open missions; Spectral resolution: number, width and placement of bands; panchromatic single wide band versus hyperspectral with hundreds of narrow bands; Radiometric resolution: bits per pixel, 8 bits gives 256 levels, 11 bits gives 2048 levels; Temporal resolution: repeat cycle in days; geostationary gives minutes, polar orbiting gives days, some high-resolution sensors can be tasked; Trade-offs: finer spatial resolution usually means narrower swath and longer repeat; more bands mean lower signal per band; Application-driven choice.

Apply

Apply A vegetation index image is to be prepared from a sensor whose red band is 0.63–0.69 μm and near-infrared band is 0.76–0.90 μm. Compute NDVI at two pixels: pixel A with red reflectance 0.05 and near-infrared reflectance 0.45; pixel B with red 0.20 and near-infrared 0.30. Interpret both values and state two limitations of NDVI. 13 marks

Formula: NDVI = (ρ_NIR − ρ_red) ÷ (ρ_NIR + ρ_red); Pixel A: (0.45 − 0.05) ÷ (0.45 + 0.05) = 0.40 ÷ 0.50 = 0.80, dense healthy vegetation; Pixel B: (0.30 − 0.20) ÷ (0.30 + 0.20) = 0.10 ÷ 0.50 = 0.20, sparse or stressed cover, or mixed soil; Range check −1 to +1; water and cloud give negative or near-zero values; Limitations: saturates over dense canopy, affected by soil background and atmosphere, needs red and near-infrared bands; Why the ratio reduces illumination differences.

Apply An image of 8000 columns by 8000 rows is recorded at 11-bit radiometric resolution in four bands. Calculate the number of grey levels, the raw storage in megabytes, and the ground area covered if the pixel size is 5.8 m. Comment on how storage changes if the data are stored as 8-bit. 13 marks

11 bits gives 2¹¹ = 2048 grey levels; Total pixels = 8000 × 8000 = 6.4 × 10⁷; Bits = 6.4 × 10⁷ × 11 × 4 = 2.816 × 10⁹ bits, divide by 8 for bytes and by 10⁶ for megabytes ≈ 352 MB; At 8 bits: 6.4 × 10⁷ × 1 × 4 bytes = 2.56 × 10⁸ bytes ≈ 256 MB; Ground area = 6.4 × 10⁷ × (5.8)² m² ≈ 2.15 × 10⁹ m² ≈ 2153 km²; Comment: fewer bits save space but quantise brightness and may lose detail in dark targets.

Apply You must monitor paddy crop growth every week over a district of about 5000 km². Choose the platform type, orbit, band set and resolution, and justify each choice. Then state how you would convert the images into a crop-area map. 13 marks

Platform: spaceborne, since the area is large and weekly coverage is needed; Orbit: sun-synchronous polar, fixed local time for comparable illumination; swath wide enough to cover the district in one or two passes; Bands: red and near-infrared for NDVI, plus shortwave infrared for moisture; possibly green for visual composites; Spatial resolution: 10 m to 30 m adequate for field-level crop mapping; finer resolution increases data volume without need; Temporal resolution: repeat cycle of about 5 days or better, or a constellation, to get cloud-free weekly observations; Processing: geometric and atmospheric correction, NDVI time series, supervised classification on training fields, accuracy check on ground truth; Mention cloud cover as the main operational risk in the monsoon.

Apply A raw image has digital numbers between 40 and 120 in an 8-bit file. Explain how a linear contrast stretch, a histogram equalisation and a band ratio would each change the image, and state which you would use to bring out a faint drainage pattern and why. 13 marks

Raw data occupy a narrow part of the 0–255 range, so the image looks flat and dark; Linear stretch maps input minimum and maximum to 0 and 255, output = (DN − min) × 255 ÷ (max − min); preserves relative brightness; Histogram equalisation redistributes values so each grey level is equally frequent; boosts local contrast but changes relative brightness; Band ratio divides one band by another, cancelling illumination differences and highlighting absorption features; For faint drainage: contrast stretch first, since the pattern is a tonal and textural feature and relative brightness matters; histogram equalisation may amplify noise; Principal component analysis and edge enhancement as further options.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze A sensor at altitude records radiance, but the analyst wants surface reflectance. Analyse the factors that separate the two, and evaluate how atmospheric correction and ground reference data improve the estimate. 13 marks

Radiance is at-sensor flux per unit area per unit solid angle; reflectance is a surface property; Path radiance from scattering adds a constant bias; absorption removes signal in some bands; Illumination geometry: solar zenith and azimuth angle, topographic slope and aspect change the incident flux; Adjacency effect and mixed pixels from finite sensor footprint; Correction methods: dark object subtraction, radiative transfer models, empirical line calibration against bright and dark targets; Residual error remains; indices such as NDVI and band ratios reduce but do not remove it; Evaluate: relative correction is enough for classification, absolute reflectance needed for multi-date quantitative work.

Evaluate Compare visual image interpretation with digital classification for mapping land use. Evaluate when a trained interpreter still beats software, and how the two can be combined. 13 marks

Visual: uses tone, shape, size, pattern, texture, shadow, site and association; needs experience and site knowledge; slow over large areas; reproducible only with effort; Digital: uses pixel values and statistics; fast, repeatable, handles many bands; needs training data and ground truth; suffers from mixed pixels and spectral confusion; Visual wins on shape and context reasoning, e.g. separating a road from a canal of the same tone, and on small areas with fine detail; Digital wins on large areas, multi-band and multi-date data, and quantitative indices; Hybrid: digital classification aided by on-screen visual editing, image segmentation into objects then classification; Accuracy assessment by confusion matrix, overall accuracy and kappa coefficient.

Evaluate Two images of the same coastal city, five years apart, are to be used to map urban growth. Analyse the requirements on resolution, registration and season, and evaluate the sources of error in the resulting change map. 13 marks

Requirements: same or similar sensor and bands, similar spatial resolution, images from the same season and near-same time of day to match sun angle and phenology; Accurate geometric registration to within a fraction of a pixel, otherwise edges produce false change; Radiometric normalisation between dates, since sensor gain and atmosphere differ; Methods: image differencing, band ratio, principal component analysis, post-classification comparison; Errors: misregistration, mixed pixels along boundaries, phenology and tide differences, cloud and shadow, classification error propagating into the change map; Evaluate: post-classification comparison gives a clear change matrix but doubles the classification error; differencing is more sensitive but harder to label; Validate with high-resolution imagery or field check.

Evaluate A newly designed satellite must serve both agriculture monitoring and urban mapping. Evaluate how the choice of orbit, swath, band set and spatial resolution constrains each other, and propose a reasonable design with justification. 13 marks

Sun-synchronous polar orbit gives a fixed local time, useful for both applications, with a repeat cycle of a few days; Narrow swath gives fine pixels but long revisit; wide swath gives frequent coverage but coarse pixels; Agriculture needs red and near-infrared for vegetation indices and frequent revisit, moderate resolution is acceptable; Urban mapping needs fine spatial resolution and panchromatic sharpening, but that pushes data volume and cost up; Design compromise: multispectral at moderate resolution plus a panchromatic band, off-nadir tasking for rapid revisit; Signal-to-noise falls as pixels shrink; radiometric resolution must be adequate; Conclusion: state the trade-off explicitly and defend the chosen numbers.

Glossary

Absorption
Transfer of radiation energy into a medium, such as a gas molecule or a leaf, so that it is not returned to the sensor.
Albedo
Fraction of incident solar radiation reflected by a surface over all wavelengths, a single broadband number.
Atmospheric window
A wavelength interval where absorption is weak, allowing the sensor to see the ground.
Band
A narrow wavelength interval recorded as one image channel by a sensor.
Contrast stretch
Point operation that maps a narrow range of digital numbers onto the full display range to improve visibility.
Digital number
Integer value stored for each pixel, proportional to radiance after sensor calibration scaling.
Emission
Radiation given out by a body because of its temperature, described by Planck's law.
False-colour composite
Image made by assigning three different wavelength bands to red, green and blue display channels; colours are not natural.
Geostationary orbit
Circular equatorial orbit at about 36,000 km altitude where the satellite keeps pace with Earth's rotation and appears fixed.
Instantaneous field of view
Angular cone seen by one detector at one instant; its projection on the ground sets the pixel size.
Kappa coefficient
Measure of classification agreement after removing the agreement expected by chance alone.
LiDAR
Active system that sends laser pulses and times the return to measure distance and build a three-dimensional surface model.
Nadir
The point on the ground directly below the sensor; the viewing direction straight down.
Pixel
One picture element, the smallest cell of a raster image, holding a single digital number per band.
Radiance
Radiant flux per unit projected area per unit solid angle reaching the sensor, in W·m⁻²·sr⁻¹.
Reflectance
Dimensionless ratio of reflected to incident flux at a surface, ranging from 0 to 1.
SAR
Synthetic Aperture Radar, an active microwave sensor that uses platform motion to build a fine-resolution image and sees through cloud.
Scale
Ratio of image distance to corresponding ground distance, e.g. 1:25,000.
Scattering
Redirection of radiation by particles or molecules without absorption, producing haze and path radiance.
Swath width
Ground width covered by the sensor in one sweep or one pass.
Texture
Frequency of tonal change in a group of pixels, giving a rough or smooth appearance used in visual interpretation.
Transmittance
Fraction of incident radiation that passes through a medium, the complement of absorption and scattering losses.
Unsupervised classification
Grouping pixels into statistically similar clusters without training samples, after which the analyst names the clusters.
Whiskbroom scanner
Sensor that sweeps a single detector across the track using a rotating mirror while the platform moves forward.
