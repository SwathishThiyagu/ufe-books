6.1 Electromagnetic radiation and the spectrum

Remote sensing measures a target without touching it by recording electromagnetic radiation: reflected sunlight, the earth's own heat, or the echo of a radar pulse. Wavelength fixes frequency and photon energy, and that decides which sensor can see a crop, a warm roof or a cloud.

You need: units and the meaning of a measurement, and the basic physics of waves.

Remote sensing and the wave picture

Remote sensing measures an object from a distance, without contact. A drone camera and a satellite sensor both do this. A passive system records radiation already present: reflected sunlight or the earth's own heat. An active system sends its own pulse and records the echo; radar and LiDAR are active.

Electromagnetic radiation (EMR) is a pair of fields, one electric and one magnetic, oscillating at right angles and travelling forward together. In vacuum they move at c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s; in air we use the same value. The wave repeats over a distance called the wavelength λ, in metres (m), micrometres (µm) or nanometres (nm). The number of repeats per second is frequency f, in hertz (Hz); 1 Hz is one cycle per second.

c = f λ

c is the speed of light in m/s; f is frequency in Hz; λ is wavelength in m. A longer wavelength means a lower frequency because c is fixed.

A photon is one quantum of EMR. Its energy is Ep = h f = h c ÷ λ, where Ep is photon energy in joules (J), h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s is Planck's constant, f is frequency in Hz, c is speed of light in m/s and λ is wavelength in m. Remote sensing also quotes energy in electron volts (eV), with 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

The electromagnetic spectrum is the full range of EMR, ordered by wavelength. Band names are conventions, not sharp walls. The table lists bands used in remote sensing.

Gamma rays and X-rays are ionising and have very short wavelengths. They are not used to image the earth's surface from orbit. The visible band, 0.4–0.7 µm, is the small part the human eye sees. Near infrared and short-wave infrared are reflected sunlight bands used for crops, moisture and minerals. Mid and thermal infrared are dominated by energy the surface emits itself, and microwave can pass through cloud.

Two natural sources drive most passive remote sensing. The sun radiates roughly as a blackbody near 5800 K and the earth near 300 K. Planck's law gives the smooth curve of blackbody radiance against wavelength: a peak, a steep fall on the short-wave side and a slower fall on the long-wave side. Wien's displacement law gives the peak: λmax = 2898 ÷ T, with λmax in µm and T the absolute temperature in kelvin. The sun peaks near 0.50 µm, inside the visible band; the earth peaks near 9.7 µm. Solar-reflected sensing therefore uses visible to short-wave infrared. Thermal sensing works mainly in the 8–14 µm atmospheric window; a second window near 3–5 µm carries reflected sunlight and emitted heat together (see energy interactions).

Radiometric quantities and photon energy

Radiometry names the energy at each stage. Radiant energy Q is the total energy carried by radiation, in joules (J). Radiant flux Φ is energy per unit time, in watts (W); 1 W = 1 J/s. Irradiance Ee is radiant flux falling on a surface per unit area, in W/m². Radiance Le is flux leaving or arriving at a surface in a given direction, per unit projected area and per unit solid angle, in W m⁻² sr⁻¹. The steradian (sr) is the SI unit of solid angle. A satellite sensor measures radiance; irradiance describes the light arriving at the ground.

Ep(eV) = 1.24 ÷ λ(µm)

Ep is photon energy in electron volts; λ is wavelength in micrometres. The constant 1.24 comes from h c ÷ (1 eV).

Photon energy falls as wavelength rises. Green light at 0.55 µm has photons of about 2.25 eV. A thermal infrared photon at 10 µm has about 0.124 eV, roughly eighteen times weaker. This does not mean the infrared beam carries less power: power also depends on how many photons arrive per second. The skin feels warmth because many low-energy photons are absorbed and turned into heat.

Worked example

Question. Green light has wavelength λ = 0.55 µm. Find its frequency and its photon energy in joules and in electron volts. Use c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s and h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.

  1. Convert the wavelength to metres: λ = 0.55 µm = 0.55 × 10⁻⁶ m = 5.5 × 10⁻⁷ m.
  2. Frequency: f = c ÷ λ = 3 × 10⁸ ÷ (5.5 × 10⁻⁷) = 5.45 × 10¹⁴ Hz.
  3. Photon energy in joules: Ep = h f = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 5.45 × 10¹⁴ = 3.61 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  4. Photon energy in electron volts: Ep = 3.61 × 10⁻¹⁹ ÷ 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 2.25 eV. The shortcut 1.24 ÷ 0.55 gives the same value.

Answer. The frequency is 5.45 × 10¹⁴ Hz and the photon energy is 3.61 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, equal to 2.25 eV.

Worked example 2

Question. Use Wien's displacement law to find the peak wavelength of (a) the sun at 5800 K, (b) the earth's surface at 300 K and (c) a forest fire at 800 K. Say in which band a sensor should look for each, and compare the photon energy at the two extreme peaks.

  1. Write the law: λmax = 2898 ÷ T, with T in kelvin and λmax in µm.
  2. Sun: λmax = 2898 ÷ 5800 = 0.4997 µm ≈ 0.50 µm, inside the visible band 0.4–0.7 µm.
  3. Earth: λmax = 2898 ÷ 300 = 9.66 µm, inside the 8–14 µm thermal window.
  4. Fire: λmax = 2898 ÷ 800 = 3.62 µm, inside the 3–5 µm mid-infrared window. That is why fire-detection channels sit near 3.7–4 µm.
  5. Photon energies at the peaks: 1.24 ÷ 0.50 = 2.48 eV for the sun and 1.24 ÷ 9.66 = 0.128 eV for the earth, a ratio of 2.48 ÷ 0.128 = 19.4.
  6. Check: the temperatures are in the ratio 5800 ÷ 300 = 19.3, and λmax × T = 0.4997 × 5800 = 2898 for the sun and 9.66 × 300 = 2898 for the earth, as the law requires.

Answer. The sun peaks at 0.50 µm (visible), the earth at 9.66 µm (thermal, 8–14 µm) and a fire at 3.62 µm (mid infrared, 3–5 µm). A photon at the sun's peak carries about 19 times the energy of one at the earth's peak.

Exam traps

  • Wavelength and frequency move in opposite directions. A longer wavelength means a lower frequency and a lower energy per photon. Photon energy is not beam power: a warm surface sends very many weak infrared photons, so the infrared beam can still carry a lot of power.
  • The wave equation is c = f λ, not c = f ÷ λ. Rearrange to f = c ÷ λ and λ = c ÷ f. Check units: Hz × m = m/s.
  • A micrometre is 10⁻⁶ m, not 10⁻³ m. Write 0.55 µm as 0.55 × 10⁻⁶ m before dividing. Mixing µm with m is the most common numerical slip.
  • Irradiance is flux divided by area. The same 100 W spread over 2 m² gives 50 W/m², over 4 m² only 25 W/m²; a larger area lowers the irradiance, it never raises it.
  • Near infrared, 0.7–1.3 µm, is mostly reflected sunlight. Thermal infrared, 8–14 µm, is emitted by the target. Do not call a 10 µm image a reflected infrared image; and in Wien's law use kelvin, never degrees Celsius.

6.2 Energy interactions with atmosphere and earth surfaces

Sunlight crosses the atmosphere, strikes a surface and travels back up to a sensor. On both trips the atmosphere scatters and absorbs part of it, and at the surface the energy splits into reflected, absorbed and transmitted fractions. The sensor therefore records radiance, not the reflectance of the ground; this chapter follows one beam along that path and converts the measured radiance back to reflectance.

You need: wavelength, irradiance and radiance from electromagnetic radiation and the spectrum.

The path: sun, atmosphere, ground, atmosphere, sensor

Sunlight arrives at the top of the atmosphere as irradiance E, in watts per square metre (W/m²). The atmosphere removes part of it on the way down; the ground reflects, absorbs or transmits the rest. On the way up the atmosphere removes more and adds light of its own. The sensor records radiance L, in W m⁻² sr⁻¹: power per unit area per unit solid angle, the light arriving from one direction.

Two consequences follow. First, L is not a property of the ground alone; it depends on the sun, the atmosphere and the viewing direction. Second, a number that describes the ground must be recovered by undoing the atmospheric part. That number is reflectance.

The air path grows with the angle from the vertical. A sensor looking straight down, with a high sun, sees the least atmosphere; an oblique view crosses much more air, which is one reason off-nadir images look hazier.

Scattering: three regimes, set by particle size

Scattering redirects energy without absorbing it. The size of the particle compared with the wavelength decides the behaviour.

  • Rayleigh scattering: nitrogen and oxygen molecules, far smaller than any visible wavelength. Scattered intensity goes as 1/λ⁴. Blue light at 0.45 µm is scattered (0.70 ÷ 0.45)⁴ ≈ 5.8 times more strongly than red light at 0.70 µm. That is why the sky is blue and the direct sun looks slightly yellow, and why blue bands of satellite images are full of haze.
  • Mie scattering: aerosols such as dust, smoke, sea salt and pollen, roughly 0.1 µm to a few µm, close to the wavelength. It is only weakly wavelength dependent and is forward-peaked. It produces the grey-white veil over a city.
  • Non-selective scattering: cloud droplets and coarse dust, much larger than the wavelength. All wavelengths scatter about equally, so fog and cloud look white and hide the ground in every band.

Absorption and the atmospheric windows

Absorption converts radiation into heat. Only certain wavelengths are absorbed, because a molecule absorbs only photons whose energy matches its own transitions: water vapour near 1.4, 1.9 and 2.7 µm; carbon dioxide near 2.7 and 4.3 µm; ozone in the ultraviolet and near 9.6 µm; oxygen in narrow bands near 0.69 and 0.76 µm. Between these bands the atmosphere is largely transparent. The gaps are the atmospheric windows, and every optical sensor is built inside one.

Energy balance at the surface and the radiance-to-reflectance link

ρ + α + τ = 1

ρ is reflectance, α is absorptance and τ is transmittance, each the fraction of the energy arriving at the surface that is reflected, absorbed or passed through.

For an opaque surface such as soil or rock, τ = 0 and ρ + α = 1. Albedo is the reflectance averaged over the whole solar spectrum, weighted by the energy in each wavelength. Fresh snow has an albedo near 0.8–0.9 and stays cool; a dark asphalt road near 0.1 and heats up.

Reflection is specular or diffuse. A calm water surface is a mirror: almost all energy leaves at one angle equal to the incidence angle. A rough field scatters in all directions. A surface that scatters equally in every direction, so that the same radiance is seen from any viewpoint, is called Lambertian. Most natural land surfaces are approximately Lambertian.

ρ = π L / E

ρ is reflectance (dimensionless); L is the radiance leaving the surface in W m⁻² sr⁻¹; E is the irradiance falling on it in W m⁻²; π ≈ 3.1416 carries the geometry, because a constant radiance integrated over a hemisphere gives π L.

Path radiance. The sensor also receives light scattered into its line of sight that never reached the ground. Call it Lp. On the way up the ground-leaving radiance is also cut by the upward transmittance T (a fraction, 0.9 means 90 percent gets through), so the sensor reads L = T Lg + Lp, where Lg = ρE ÷ π is the radiance leaving the ground. Solving for reflectance gives ρ = π (L − Lp) ÷ (T E). Removing Lp and T is atmospheric correction (see digital image processing).

Worked example

Question. A flat, uniform field is imaged from directly above. (a) On a clear day the irradiance at the field is E = 500 W/m² and the sensor reads L = 25.0 W m⁻² sr⁻¹; take T = 1 and Lp = 0 and find the reflectance. (b) On a hazy day the irradiance at the field is 400 W/m², the upward transmittance is T = 0.9 and the haze adds path radiance Lp = 4.0 W m⁻² sr⁻¹. The field has not changed. What does the sensor read? (c) What reflectance do you get if you ignore the haze, and what if you correct for it? Take π = 3.1416.

  1. (a) Clear day: ρ = π L ÷ E = 3.1416 × 25.0 ÷ 500 = 78.54 ÷ 500 = 0.157, that is 15.7 percent.
  2. (b) Ground-leaving radiance on the hazy day: Lg = ρ E ÷ π = 0.157 × 400 ÷ 3.1416 = 20.0 W m⁻² sr⁻¹. It is lower than 25.0 only because less light reached the field.
  3. After the climb through the haze: T Lg = 0.9 × 20.0 = 18.0 W m⁻² sr⁻¹.
  4. Add the path radiance: L = 18.0 + 4.0 = 22.0 W m⁻² sr⁻¹. The sensor reads 22.0, not half of 25.0, because the haze adds light of its own.
  5. (c) Ignoring the haze: ρ = π L ÷ E = 3.1416 × 22.0 ÷ 400 = 69.12 ÷ 400 = 0.173, too high by 0.016 (10 percent of the true value).
  6. Removing only Lp: 3.1416 × 18.0 ÷ 400 = 0.141, now too low, because the upward loss is still in the number.
  7. Full correction: ρ = π (L − Lp) ÷ (T E) = 3.1416 × 18.0 ÷ (0.9 × 400) = 56.55 ÷ 360 = 0.157, the clear-day value.

Answer. The reflectance is 0.157 on both days. The hazy-day sensor reads 22.0 W m⁻² sr⁻¹; ignoring the haze gives 0.173, and only removing both the path radiance and the upward loss gives back 0.157.

Exam traps

  • Radiance is not reflectance. Radiance changes with sun angle, haze and viewing direction even when the ground is the same; reflectance changes only when the surface itself changes, such as a growing crop, wet soil or fresh snow. Convert to reflectance before comparing two dates.
  • Rayleigh scattering goes as 1/λ⁴, not 1/λ, and short wavelengths scatter most. The sky is blue because blue is scattered out of the beam, not because blue is absorbed.
  • Albedo is a broadband average, never the reflectance of a single band. Quoting a one-band value as albedo loses marks.
  • Path radiance adds to the signal, so an uncorrected hazy image gives a reflectance that is too high (0.173 against 0.157 in the worked example). Subtracting Lp but forgetting the transmittance overshoots the other way.
  • For an opaque surface τ = 0, so ρ + α = 1. Do not set τ = 1: that would describe a perfectly transparent surface that reflects and absorbs nothing.

6.3 Spectral reflectance of vegetation, soil and water

A spectral signature is the curve of reflectance against wavelength for a surface. Vegetation, soil and water absorb and scatter light differently, so each has a different curve. A sensor records radiance in a few bands; compare those values with known curves and you can tell crop from bare field from pond. This is the basis of land-cover mapping, crop monitoring and water mapping from satellites. The curve is measured as reflectance, a ratio from 0 to 1 or a percentage, so it is independent of the sun's brightness at the moment of measurement.

You need: reflectance and atmospheric effects from energy interactions, and band wavelengths from the spectrum.

The spectral signature

Reflectance ρ(λ) is the fraction of light arriving at a surface that the surface sends back at wavelength λ. Wavelength λ is the distance between wave crests, measured here in micrometres (µm). Reflectance has no unit; write it as 0 to 1 or as a percentage. A spectral signature is a plot of ρ against λ from 0.4 µm to 2.5 µm. The visible part is 0.4–0.7 µm. The reflective infrared runs from 0.7 to 2.5 µm. It is split into the near-infrared (NIR), about 0.7–1.3 µm, and the shortwave infrared (SWIR), about 1.3–2.5 µm.

Why a curve and not one number: every material absorbs some wavelengths and scatters others. Absorption by pigments, water or minerals makes dips. Scattering by leaf cells, sand grains or suspended particles makes broad highs. The shape, not one band, identifies the surface. A satellite with a few bands samples this curve at fixed wavelengths; the sampled values are the measurements you classify in digital image processing.

Vegetation: pigments and the red edge

Chlorophyll absorbs blue light near 0.45 µm and red light near 0.67 µm for photosynthesis. It absorbs green near 0.55 µm less strongly, so a leaf has a small green peak: reflectance near 12% in the figure, against about 5% in blue and red. The big feature is the red edge: between about 0.68 µm and 0.75 µm reflectance rises steeply, from about 5% to 45% in the figure.

From about 0.75 to 1.3 µm, on the near-infrared plateau, a healthy leaf is bright. The cause is internal cell structure: light enters the leaf, bounces between cell walls and air spaces, and much of it leaves again. Pigments do not absorb NIR. A dense crop can reflect 40–50% or more. Beyond about 1.3 µm leaf water takes over: bands near 1.4 µm and 1.9 µm are deep dips, and the curve is near 30% at 1.6 µm and 18% at 2.2 µm in the figure.

Stressed or sparse vegetation has lower NIR and higher red, so the red edge weakens. This is why vegetation indices use red and NIR together.

Soil, water, snow and NDVI

Soil has a smooth curve that rises slowly from visible to shortwave infrared: 10% at 0.45 µm, 18% at 0.66 µm and 33% at 2.2 µm in the figure for dry soil. There are no sharp pigment bands. Moisture lowers the whole curve because water absorbs; organic matter darkens it; iron oxides raise red and lower blue; texture sets brightness. Dry sand is bright; wet clay is dark.

Clear water is brightest in blue (8–9% at 0.40–0.45 µm in the figure), falls through green and red, and is almost black in NIR (2% at 0.83 µm, 1% at 1.1 µm). Water absorbs NIR strongly, so ponds and rivers are dark in NIR images. Turbidity from suspended sediment raises visible reflectance, especially red. Algae add chlorophyll, which can create a green peak and red absorption. Snow is very bright in the visible and drops in shortwave infrared because ice absorbs there.

NDVI = (ρNIR − ρred) ÷ (ρNIR + ρred)

ρNIR is the near-infrared reflectance and ρred is the red reflectance; both are unitless fractions from 0 to 1. NDVI runs from −1 to +1.

High NIR and low red give values near +1 for green vegetation. Soil gives a low positive value; water gives zero or negative. Other indices are SAVI, EVI, NDWI and NDBI. A spectral library stores measured curves with labels and metadata; matching compares an image spectrum to library entries.

Worked example

Question. Read the band values plotted as dots in the figure: in the red band (0.63–0.69 µm) vegetation 0.05, dry soil 0.18, clear water 0.04; in the NIR band (0.76–0.90 µm) vegetation 0.50, dry soil 0.22, clear water 0.02. (a) Compute NDVI for each cover. (b) The same crop on a slope facing away from the sun receives half the light, so both its band values halve. Find its NDVI. (c) Say what the results show.

  1. Formula: NDVI = (ρNIR − ρred) ÷ (ρNIR + ρred); reflectances are unitless.
  2. Vegetation: (0.50 − 0.05) ÷ (0.50 + 0.05) = 0.45 ÷ 0.55 = 0.818.
  3. Dry soil: (0.22 − 0.18) ÷ (0.22 + 0.18) = 0.04 ÷ 0.40 = 0.100.
  4. Clear water: (0.02 − 0.04) ÷ (0.02 + 0.04) = −0.02 ÷ 0.06 = −0.333.
  5. (b) Shaded crop: red 0.025, NIR 0.25. NDVI = (0.25 − 0.025) ÷ (0.25 + 0.025) = 0.225 ÷ 0.275 = 0.818, the same as in sun. The halving cancels between top and bottom.
  6. (c) The crop sits in the upper part of the −1 to +1 range, as dense green vegetation does (typical values 0.6–0.9); soil is just above zero; water is negative because it reflects more red than NIR. The ratio keeps the crop at 0.818 whether it is lit or shaded, which a single NIR value (0.50 against 0.25) cannot do.

Answer. NDVI is 0.818 for vegetation, 0.100 for dry soil and −0.333 for clear water, and stays 0.818 for the shaded crop. The sign and size of NDVI separate the three covers, and the ratio removes the illumination change.

Exam traps

  • The green peak is not the brightest part of vegetation. NIR reflectance is much higher, so a false-colour image shows crops in red, not green.
  • Water is dark in NIR because water absorbs NIR strongly; it is not black in the visible. Clear water still reflects more blue than red.
  • NDVI is a ratio, not a percentage. Do not write 0.67 as 67% unless you multiply by 100, and do not swap NIR and red.
  • Wet soil is darker than dry soil, not brighter. Moisture absorption lowers the whole soil curve.
  • Snow is bright in visible bands but drops in shortwave infrared. A bright visible pixel is not automatically cloud or sand.

6.4 Remote sensing platforms and sensors

A remote sensing system is a platform carrying a sensor. The platform fixes the height, the orbit and the viewing time. The sensor fixes the bands and the angular field of view of one detector; the platform height turns that angle into a ground pixel size, p = θH. Orbit and sensor geometry decide what the image looks like: a weather satellite sees one hemisphere all day, while a polar satellite repeats a narrow strip at the same local solar time. This chapter classifies platforms and sensors, explains whiskbroom and pushbroom scanning, and links the choice to Indian missions such as Resourcesat, Cartosat, RISAT and INSAT.

You need: the spectrum from electromagnetic radiation and the atmospheric windows from energy interactions.

Platforms and orbits

A platform is the carrier that holds the sensor. Platforms are classed by height.

  • Ground: tripod, mast or crane. It gives millimetre to centimetre detail over a small site, as in a laboratory or a bridge inspection.
  • Airborne: UAV at a few hundred metres, or aircraft at 1–10 km. It covers a site on demand for crop health, construction progress and disaster response. UAV hardware and flight planning belong to drone surveying.
  • Space: satellite at 400–36,000 km. It gives repeated wide coverage for national mapping and weather.

Orbit decides the viewing pattern. A sun-synchronous orbit is near-polar, with inclination about 98°. Inclination is measured from the equatorial plane, from 0° to 180°. Values above 90° mean the orbit moves against Earth's rotation (retrograde); sun-synchronous orbits are retrograde, so about 98°. The satellite crosses a place at the same local solar time on each pass, so shadows and sun angle match between dates. Landsat, Sentinel-2 and Resourcesat use this. A geostationary orbit is circular over the equator at 35,786 km, with a period of one sidereal day. The satellite stays over one longitude and sees the same hemisphere continuously. INSAT and GOES use this for weather. A repeat cycle is the number of days between identical ground tracks; a short cycle gives frequent temporal coverage.

T = 2π √(a³ ÷ μ)

T is the orbital period in seconds; a = R + h is the orbit radius in km, with R = 6378 km the earth's equatorial radius and h the height; μ = 398 600 km³/s² is the earth's gravitational constant. A higher orbit has a longer period.

Sensors and scanning geometry

Sensors are passive or active. A passive sensor measures natural radiation: reflected sunlight in optical bands, or emitted thermal infrared. It needs daylight for reflected bands and cannot see through cloud in the visible. An active sensor carries its own transmitter and measures the return. SAR sends microwaves and records amplitude and phase; it works through cloud and at night. LiDAR sends laser pulses and measures range. A microwave radiometer is passive; it measures weak natural microwave emission.

Imaging geometry has three common forms. A framing camera takes a whole two-dimensional picture at once, like a phone camera. A whiskbroom scanner has a mirror that sweeps across the flight track while the platform moves along it. A pushbroom scanner has a fixed linear array of detectors across the track; the platform's motion along the track builds the image. The swath is the ground width covered by one complete cross-track scan: one sweep of the mirror for a whiskbroom, the full detector line for a pushbroom. A multispectral sensor has several narrow bands; a panchromatic band is one wide band used for sharpening.

p = S / N and θ = p / H

p is the ground pixel size in metres; S is the swath width in metres; N is the number of detectors in the linear array; θ is the detector field of view in radians; H is the platform height in metres.

Indian systems show the choices. Resourcesat satellites are sun-synchronous and carry multispectral sensors such as LISS-3, LISS-4 and the wide AWiFS. The same ground point can therefore be seen by a polar land sensor once every few days and by a weather satellite every half hour. The trade-offs between pixel size, bands and revisit are in the four resolutions.

Worked example

Question. A pushbroom sensor flies at a height of 600 km. Its linear array has 6000 detectors and images a swath 60 km wide on the ground. (a) Find the ground pixel size. (b) Find the field of view of one detector in radians and microradians. (c) Find the pixel size if the same array flies at 300 km.

  1. Convert the swath to metres: S = 60 km = 60,000 m. The number of detectors is N = 6000.
  2. Ground pixel size: p = S / N = 60,000 m / 6000 = 10.0 m.
  3. Detector field of view: θ = p / H = 10.0 m / 600,000 m = 1.67 × 10⁻⁵ rad. This is rounded to three significant figures.
  4. In microradians: θ = 1.67 × 10⁻⁵ × 10⁶ = 16.7 µrad.
  5. At height 300 km: p = θ H = 1.67 × 10⁻⁵ × 300,000 m = 5.0 m (to three significant figures).

Answer. The ground pixel is 10.0 m, the detector field of view is 1.67 × 10⁻⁵ rad (16.7 µrad), and at 300 km the pixel is 5.0 m. These are rounded to three significant figures.

Worked example 2

Question. A land satellite flies a circular sun-synchronous orbit at h = 705 km. (a) Find its orbital period and the number of orbits per day. (b) Find how far apart, at the equator, two successive ground tracks fall. (c) With a 185 km swath, why can it not image every point each day?   • Panchromatic is one wide band, not a set of narrow multispectral bands. It gives fine spatial detail but cannot separate crop type by spectral curve.

6.5 Spatial, spectral, radiometric and temporal resolution

Every remote sensing image has four limits: spatial, spectral, radiometric and temporal resolution. Spatial fixes the ground size of a pixel, spectral fixes the number and width of wavelength bands, radiometric fixes the number of discrete levels, and temporal fixes the revisit time. These limits decide what a sensor can see and how often. They are used to choose imagery for crop monitoring, urban mapping, disaster response and water studies. Each finer limit is bought at a cost in another.

You need: platform height, swath and detector arrays from platforms and sensors.

Spatial resolution is the ground pixel size

An image is a grid of picture elements, pixels. Each pixel stores a digital number (DN), an integer code for the radiance received from its ground area. Spatial resolution is the ground size of one pixel. A smaller ground pixel size is a finer spatial resolution; 5 m is finer than 30 m. The sensor height and its instantaneous field of view (IFOV) set it.

GSD = H × θ

GSD is the ground sampling distance in metres; H is the sensor height above ground in metres; θ is the instantaneous field of view (IFOV) of one detector element, in radians. The exact form is GSD = 2H tan(θ ÷ 2); for the microradian angles of satellite detectors it equals Hθ to many decimal places, but it fails for wide angles.

A sensor at H = 700 km with θ = 10 μrad has GSD = 700 000 m × 10 × 10⁻⁶ = 7 m. A larger IFOV or a higher platform gives a coarser pixel.

When one pixel holds two land covers, such as part field and part road, it is a mixed pixel. Its DN is roughly the area-weighted average of the two radiances. The pixel then belongs fully to neither class. Finer pixels reduce mixing but cost data volume and often a narrower swath. The figure shows nine 10 m pixels averaged into one 30 m pixel.

Spectral and radiometric resolution

Spectral resolution describes the number and width of wavelength bands. A panchromatic sensor has one broad band and collects much energy. A multispectral sensor has a few bands, for example visible and near infrared. A hyperspectral sensor has many narrow, contiguous bands. Narrow bands help separate covers with similar spectral signatures, such as two crops.

Radiometric resolution is the number of discrete levels used to record radiance. An n-bit sensor gives 2ⁿ levels. An 8-bit sensor gives 256 levels with DN from 0 to 255. A 12-bit sensor gives 4096 levels with DN from 0 to 4095. More bits mean finer steps between levels, not a larger pixel.

ΔL = (Lmax − Lmin) ÷ (2ⁿ − 1)

ΔL is the quantisation step in radiance units; Lmax and Lmin are the radiance limits of the sensor; n is the number of bits per pixel. 2ⁿ levels have 2ⁿ − 1 steps between them (256 levels, DN 0 to 255, have 255 steps), which is why the range divides by 2ⁿ − 1.

A smaller ΔL means higher radiometric resolution. More spectral bands split the incoming energy into smaller portions, so each band has less signal and a lower signal-to-noise ratio unless the sensor is larger or integration time is longer.

Temporal resolution and the trade-offs

Temporal resolution is the revisit time, also called the repeat cycle: the time between two images of the same ground point. A geostationary sensor stares at the same area and repeats in minutes. A polar-orbiting sensor moves in a swath and repeats in days. Wide-swath sensors revisit more often than narrow-swath sensors at the same altitude.

The four resolutions compete. A finer GSD needs a smaller detector footprint on the ground or a longer focal length, which usually narrows the swath and lengthens revisit. More bands divide the same optical energy, so each band is noisier. Higher radiometric resolution increases data volume. Good design picks the combination the task needs: crop mapping wants spectral and temporal detail; a city map wants fine spatial detail.

Ground sampling distance is not the whole story. Real image quality also depends on the modulation transfer function (MTF), which measures how well contrast survives at fine detail, and on signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Only a few universities teach MTF and SNR as resolution measures; see digital image processing for how resampling changes pixel size.

Worked example

Question. A 30 m sensor images a 30 m × 30 m block. The same block is seen by a 10 m sensor as nine pixels with digital numbers 10, 10, 10, 10, 70, 70, 10, 70, 70. Find the single digital number of the block in the 30 m image and the fraction of the block that is bright cover.

  1. Each 10 m pixel covers 100 m²; nine cover 900 m², equal to one 30 m pixel of 30 m × 30 m.

  3. Mean DN = 330 ÷ 9 = 36.666..., which rounds to 37.
  4. The bright pixels are four of nine: 4 ÷ 9 = 0.4444 = 44.4 per cent.

Answer. The 30 m sensor records digital number 37, and 44.4 per cent of the block is bright cover.

Worked example 2

Question. A sensor band records radiance from 0 to 120 W m⁻² sr⁻¹ µm⁻¹. Two neighbouring fields give 60.05 and 60.25 in the same units. Find the quantisation step and the DN of each field (round to the nearest level) for (a) an 8-bit and (b) a 12-bit version of the sensor. Which version can tell the fields apart?

  1. Step: ΔL = (Lmax − Lmin) ÷ (2ⁿ − 1).
  2. (a) 8 bits: ΔL = 120 ÷ 255 = 0.471. Field 1: 60.05 ÷ 0.471 = 127.6 → DN 128. Field 2: 60.25 ÷ 0.471 = 128.0 → DN 128.
  3. (b) 12 bits: ΔL = 120 ÷ 4095 = 0.0293. Field 1: 60.05 ÷ 0.0293 = 2049.2 → DN 2049. Field 2: 60.25 ÷ 0.0293 = 2056.0 → DN 2056.
  4. The difference 60.25 − 60.05 = 0.20 is less than half of one 8-bit step (0.471), so both fields land on DN 128. At 12 bits the same 0.20 spans 0.20 ÷ 0.0293 = 6.8 steps, and the DNs differ by 7.
  5. Check: DN × ΔL returns the radiance to within half a step: 128 × 0.471 = 60.2; 2049 × 0.0293 = 60.04 and 2056 × 0.0293 = 60.24.

Answer. The 8-bit step is 0.471 and both fields read DN 128; the 12-bit step is 0.0293 and the fields read 2049 and 2056. Only the 12-bit sensor separates them.

  • Higher radiometric resolution means more bits and more levels, not a bigger pixel or more bands.
  • Spectral resolution is about band number and width, not about how small a pixel is.
  • A mixed pixel is not a broken measurement; it is the average of covers inside the pixel.
  • Temporal resolution is revisit time, not the time taken to process an image. And a radiance difference smaller than one quantisation step can vanish: two fields 0.20 apart both read DN 128 on an 8-bit sensor with a 0.471 step.

6.6 Visual image interpretation

Visual image interpretation is the reading of an image by a trained eye before any software is used. The interpreter pools tone and colour, shape, size, pattern, texture, shadow, site and association to name each object and draw its boundary. It exists because a single pixel or a single band cannot say whether a grey patch is a roof, a dry field or a shadow. It is used to make land-use maps, check crop area, map forests and wetlands, assess flood or fire damage, and update topographic sheets from new imagery.

You need: map scales and topographic sheets, the spectral signatures and pixel size.

Elements of visual interpretation

Start with the recorded appearance, then add context. Each element answers a different question about the same target.

  • Tone is the brightness of the target in the recorded band; colour is its hue when three bands are shown. Deep water is dark; dry sand is bright.
  • Shape is the outline of the object. A rectangular field, a straight canal and a winding river have different shapes. Size is the object's measured length, width or area, read against the image scale; it separates a house from a shed.
  • Pattern is the arrangement of discrete objects, such as an orchard grid or terrace lines. Texture is the frequency of tone change inside a patch; smooth water and rough canopy differ.
  • Shadow is the dark area cast by an object. Its direction and length give cues to height and sun position, but it hides the ground inside it.
  • Site is where the object sits relative to terrain, drainage and transport. Association is what normally lies beside it, such as a car park beside a stadium.

No single element decides a class. A dark rectangle may be a roof or a pond; shape, texture and association settle the name.

Keys, image types and timing

An interpretation key is a written or pictorial table that links a land cover to its expected appearance: tone, shape, texture, season and usual neighbours. The interpreter compares the image with the key, then checks doubtful patches in the field.

  • Black-and-white panchromatic image: one broad band, fine detail, no colour.
  • True colour image: red, green and blue bands shown as red, green and blue. Colour looks natural.
  • False-colour infrared composite: near-infrared, red and green bands shown as red, green and blue. Healthy vegetation is bright red because it reflects near-infrared strongly.
  • Thermal image: emitted heat, shown as brightness. Warm roofs and hot sand are bright; it can be taken at night.

Aerial photographs come from aircraft: large scale, high detail, flown on demand, but limited repeat coverage. Satellite images come from fixed orbits: repeated coverage, several spectral bands, usually smaller scale. The two are compared, not ranked.

Season changes the target. A kharif crop field and a rabi crop field look different; a deciduous forest loses leaves. Sun angle changes shadow length and brightness. A low sun gives long shadows and strong relief; a high sun gives short shadows and flatter tone.

Multi-date visual comparison places two or more images of the same area side by side. The interpreter looks for new edges, lost patches and colour or tone changes. Images from the same season are preferred, so that crop stage is not mistaken for real change.

Image scale and ground measurement

Scale relates a distance on the image to the same distance on the ground. A representative fraction 1:50,000 means one unit on the image represents 50,000 of the same unit on the ground.

S = dp ÷ Dg = 1 : M

S is the scale ratio; dp is the photo distance; Dg is the ground distance in the same unit; M is the denominator of the representative fraction.

At 1:50,000, one centimetre on the image represents 50,000 cm on the ground, which is 500 m. On a vertical photo of flat ground one scale holds everywhere. Over hills the scale changes with ground height, and on a tilted photo it changes across the frame, so measure with a scale found near the object or work on a rectified image.

  • Linear scale factor is 1/50,000.
  • Area scale factor is the square of the linear factor. At 1:50,000, one square centimetre represents 500 m × 500 m = 250,000 m² = 0.25 km².
  • Multiply a measured photo area by the area represented by one square centimetre to get ground area.

S = f ÷ (H − h)

S is the scale of a vertical photo; f is the camera focal length; H is the flying height above datum; h is the ground height above datum; f and H − h in the same unit. Higher ground is closer to the camera, so it appears at a larger scale.

Shadow also gives height. If a shadow is s long on the ground and the sun is at elevation angle e above the horizon, the object is s × tan e high, provided the shadow falls on level ground.

Worked example

Question. An aerial photograph has scale 1:50,000. A forest patch measures 6.4 cm² on the photo. Two field corners are 4.2 cm apart on the same photo. Find the ground distance between the corners and the forest area in km².

  1. Scale 1:50,000 means 1 cm on the photo represents 50,000 cm on the ground.
  2. Convert to metres: 50,000 cm ÷ 100 = 500 m. So 1 cm on the photo represents 500 m on the ground.
  3. Ground distance between the corners = 4.2 cm × 500 m/cm = 2100 m = 2.1 km.
  4. For area, 1 cm² on the photo represents 500 m × 500 m = 250,000 m².
  5. Convert to square kilometres: 250,000 m² ÷ 1,000,000 = 0.25 km² per cm².
  6. Forest area = 6.4 cm² × 0.25 km²/cm² = 1.6 km².

Answer. The ground distance is 2100 m and the forest area is 1.6 km².

Worked example 2

Question. A vertical air photo is taken with a 152 mm camera from 3200 m above datum over flat ground at 160 m above datum. (a) Find the photo scale. (b) A tower's shadow is 1.5 mm long on the photo and the sun was 40° above the horizon. Find the shadow length on the ground and the tower's height.

  1. (a) Height of the camera above the ground: H − h = 3200 − 160 = 3040 m.
  2. Scale: S = f ÷ (H − h) = 0.152 m ÷ 3040 m = 1 ÷ 20 000, that is 1:20 000. Both lengths are in metres before dividing.
  3. (b) Ground shadow: 1.5 mm × 20 000 = 30 000 mm = 30 m.

  • Shadow is not a dark surface. It hides the ground it falls on, so read the class from the lit side of the object, which faces the sun, or from another date. Shadows always point away from the sun.
  • Pattern is the arrangement of separate objects; texture is the fine change of tone inside one patch. An examiner may swap the two words.

6.7 Digital image processing: enhancement and classification

A satellite hands you a grid of digital numbers, not a map. Digital image processing corrects that grid, stretches and filters it so the eye can read it, and then sorts every pixel into a class such as water, crop or built-up. An error matrix then says how far to trust the labels.

You need: the spectral signatures, the four resolutions and the keys of visual image interpretation, which become the training classes.

Correcting the numbers first

A digital image is a grid of pixels, each holding a digital number (DN) at the sensor's radiometric resolution, 0 to 255 for an 8-bit image. One grid is one band; a multispectral image stacks several bands pixel for pixel, so all bands at row 120, column 45 describe the same patch of ground.

Radiometric correction fixes brightness. Calibration turns DNs into radiance and removes detector drift; sun-angle correction divides by the cosine of the solar zenith angle, so a low winter sun does not make the ground look falsely dark; haze correction subtracts the path radiance that lifts even the darkest pixel above zero.

Geometric correction fixes position. Ground control points (GCPs) are features, such as a road junction, a tank bund or a bridge, whose image and map coordinates are both known. A transformation is fitted through them and the image is resampled onto the map grid. Nearest neighbour copies the closest original DN and keeps values intact. Bilinear interpolation takes the four nearest input pixels and averages them with weights that fall with distance, so the result looks smoother but invents new DN values.

Enhancement: stretch, filter, composite

A histogram counts how many pixels carry each DN. A raw scene often fills only a narrow slice of the range because of haze or weak contrast, so it looks grey and flat. A linear contrast stretch pulls the used range out to the full display range.

DN(out) = (DN(in) − min) ÷ (max − min) × 255

DN(in) is the input digital number; min and max are the lowest and highest values kept, read from the histogram; DN(out) is the stretched value, clipped to 0–255; 255 is the top of an 8-bit display.

Histogram equalisation is a stronger, non-linear stretch. It reassigns values so every brightness level holds roughly the same number of pixels. Detail in dark forest opens up, but noise in a clear reservoir can be exaggerated.

A spatial filter slides a small kernel, usually 3 × 3, across the image. A low-pass kernel gives every cell the weight 1/9, so the output is the average of the nine cells in the window, centre included: noise is smoothed and edges blur. A high-pass kernel has a large positive centre and negative neighbours: flat ground cancels to near zero and edges stand out.

A false colour composite shows near-infrared as red, red as green and green as blue, so healthy vegetation appears bright red and water dark.

Features: ratios, NDVI and PCA

A band ratio divides one band by another, pixel by pixel. Dividing cancels much of the illumination difference between a sunlit slope and a shaded one, so the ratio shows the material rather than the light falling on it.

NDVI = (ρ(NIR) − ρ(red)) ÷ (ρ(NIR) + ρ(red))

ρ(NIR) and ρ(red) are reflectance or DN in the near-infrared and red bands; NDVI runs from −1 to +1; healthy crops approach 0.8, bare soil about 0.1 to 0.2, water and shadow are negative, and thick cloud stays close to zero.

Principal component analysis (PCA) rotates the bands into new uncorrelated axes called components, ordered by variance. The first component carries most of the brightness; later ones carry subtle spectral differences. Know what the method is for; the eigenvector working is rarely asked.

Classification and accuracy

In supervised classification the analyst draws training areas for each class, using the visual keys of visual image interpretation. Software computes the mean and covariance of every class. The maximum likelihood rule then assigns each pixel to the class with the highest probability, assuming the class values follow a normal distribution.

In unsupervised classification there are no training areas. The k-means algorithm picks k clusters, seeds a mean for each, assigns every pixel to the nearest mean, recomputes the means, and repeats until the means stop moving. The analyst then names the clusters (water, crop, built-up) by looking at them on the image.

Supervised classification gives named classes directly but needs ground knowledge; unsupervised finds groups the analyst may not have expected and needs labelling afterwards. A majority filter then removes isolated single pixels.

Accuracy is judged against test pixels checked on the ground, listed in an error matrix (also called a confusion matrix) of mapped classes in rows against reference classes in columns. Overall accuracy is the diagonal sum divided by the total. User's accuracy of a class is its diagonal cell ÷ its row total: how often a pixel mapped as that class really is it. Producer's accuracy is the diagonal cell ÷ its column total: how much of the real class was found. Kappa removes the agreement that chance alone would produce.

κ = (p(o) − p(e)) ÷ (1 − p(e))

p(o) is the observed agreement, the fraction of test pixels on the diagonal; p(e) is the agreement expected by chance from the row and column totals; κ = 1 is perfect agreement, κ = 0 is chance level.

Worked example

Question. One band has digital numbers from 40 to 90. It is stretched linearly to the full 0–255 range. Write the stretch formula, find the output for input DN 40, 65 and 90, and state what happens to a pixel with DN 30.

  1. Ends from the histogram: min = 40, max = 90, so the range is 90 − 40 = 50. The stretch is DN(out) = (DN(in) − 40) ÷ 50 × 255 = 5.1 × (DN(in) − 40).
  2. Input 40: 5.1 × 0 = 0. Input 90: 5.1 × 50 = 255.
  3. Input 65: 5.1 × 25 = 127.5; an 8-bit store holds whole numbers, so it is stored as 128.
  4. Input 30: 5.1 × (−10) = −51, outside the display range, so it is clipped to 0.

Answer. The stretch gives DN 40 → 0, DN 65 → 127.5 (stored as 128) and DN 90 → 255; any DN below 40 clips to 0.

Worked example 2

Question. A land-cover map is checked at 300 test pixels. Rows are the mapped class, columns the class found on the ground. Water row: 48, 2, 0. Crop row: 3, 120, 27. Built-up row: 1, 18, 81 (columns in the order water, crop, built-up). Find the overall accuracy, the user's and producer's accuracy of each class, and kappa.

  1. Row totals: water 48 + 2 + 0 = 50, crop 3 + 120 + 27 = 150, built-up 1 + 18 + 81 = 100. Column totals: water 48 + 3 + 1 = 52, crop 2 + 120 + 18 = 140, built-up 0 + 27 + 81 = 108. Grand total 300.
  2. Overall accuracy po = (48 + 120 + 81) ÷ 300 = 249 ÷ 300 = 0.830.
  3. User's accuracy (diagonal ÷ row): water 48 ÷ 50 = 0.960, crop 120 ÷ 150 = 0.800, built-up 81 ÷ 100 = 0.810.
  4. Producer's accuracy (diagonal ÷ column): water 48 ÷ 52 = 0.923, crop 120 ÷ 140 = 0.857, built-up 81 ÷ 108 = 0.750.
  5. Chance agreement pe = (50 × 52 + 150 × 140 + 100 × 108) ÷ 300² = (2600 + 21 000 + 10 800) ÷ 90 000 = 34 400 ÷ 90 000 = 0.382.
  6. κ = (0.830 − 0.382) ÷ (1 − 0.382) = 0.448 ÷ 0.618 = 0.725.
  7. Reading: 27 real built-up pixels were mapped as crop, so built-up has the lowest producer's accuracy (0.750) even though 81 percent of what the map calls built-up is right.

Answer. Overall accuracy 0.830; user's accuracy 0.960, 0.800, 0.810 and producer's accuracy 0.923, 0.857, 0.750 for water, crop and built-up; κ = 0.725. The crop and built-up confusion is the weak point.

Exam traps

  • High overall accuracy does not remove class confusion. A class can be right 95 percent of the time where the map shows it (user's accuracy) and still miss many of its real pixels (low producer's accuracy). Read both before trusting a class.
  • Radiometric correction is about brightness, geometric correction about position. Marks are lost by swapping the two.
  • A contrast stretch changes only the display values, not the stored reflectance. Do not claim it corrects the image.
  • NDVI is (NIR − red) ÷ (NIR + red). Reversing the order gives a negative index and a wrong map, and water genuinely has a negative NDVI.
  • User's accuracy divides by the row (mapped) total and producer's accuracy by the column (reference) total. Swapping them reverses the meaning: in the worked example built-up is 0.810 one way and 0.750 the other.

Takeaways

  1. Every remote sensing measurement begins with electromagnetic energy, and wavelength alone fixes both the frequency and the photon energy.
  2. Between the sun and the sensor the atmosphere scatters and absorbs energy, so the sensor records radiance that has been altered twice.
  3. The atmosphere is transparent only in certain windows, which is why sensors are built for specific wavelength bands.
  4. Reflectance is a property of the surface; radiance is what reaches the sensor, and the two differ by atmospheric effects and illumination geometry.
  5. Vegetation, soil and water each have a distinct reflectance-versus-wavelength curve, and the differences between the curves allow identification.
  6. A remote sensing system is a platform plus a sensor; orbit choice fixes repeat time and viewing geometry, and sensor geometry fixes image shape.
  7. The four resolutions limit what an image can show, and improving one usually costs another.
  8. Visual interpretation combines tone, shape, size, pattern, texture, shadow and association with site knowledge before any software is used.
  9. Digital processing turns raw digital numbers into corrected, enhanced images and then into labelled classes, with an accuracy table to judge the labels.

Understand Explain the wave and particle models of electromagnetic radiation. Show how wavelength fixes frequency and photon energy, and name the spectral regions used in optical remote sensing with their approximate wavelength ranges. 13 marks

Points to cover. Oscillating electric and magnetic fields at right angles, travelling at c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s; f = c ÷ λ and Ep = h f = h c ÷ λ, with h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s; Inverse relation: longer wavelength means lower frequency and lower photon energy; Regions: visible 0.4–0.7 µm, near infrared 0.7–1.3 µm, short-wave infrared 1.3–3 µm, thermal infrared 8–14 µm, microwave 1 mm–1 m; Wave model explains reflection, refraction, diffraction and polarisation; particle model explains absorption, emission and detection; Why band choice follows the physics: Wien peaks, thermal bands detect emission, microwave penetrates cloud.

Model answer.

  1. Wave model. Electromagnetic radiation (EMR) is an electric field and a magnetic field oscillating at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel. In vacuum, and very nearly in air, it travels at c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. Wavelength λ is the distance between crests; frequency f is the number of crests passing a point each second. They are tied by c = f λ.
  2. Particle model. EMR also arrives as photons, packets of energy Ep = h f = h c ÷ λ, with h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s. In electron volts this is Ep = 1.24 ÷ λ, with λ in µm (1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J).

Understand Describe how the atmosphere modifies incoming and outgoing electromagnetic radiation. Explain Rayleigh, Mie and non-selective scattering, absorption, and the idea of atmospheric windows. 13 marks

Points to cover. Scattering and absorption act on the downward solar path and again on the upward path to the sensor; Rayleigh: particles much smaller than λ, intensity varies as 1 ÷ λ⁴, strong in blue, causes haze; Mie: aerosols about the size of λ (dust, smoke, sea salt), weakly wavelength dependent, forward-peaked, grey-white urban haze; Non-selective: particles much larger than λ (water droplets, cloud), scatters all visible wavelengths equally, gives white cloud; Absorption by ozone, carbon dioxide and water vapour in specific bands; Atmospheric windows: visible, 0.8–1.3, 1.5–1.8, 2.0–2.4, 3–5 and 8–14 µm, and microwave; Effect on image: reduced contrast, bluish haze, need for atmospheric correction.

Model answer.

  1. Two passes. Sunlight crosses the atmosphere on the way down to the ground and the reflected light crosses it again on the way up to the sensor. On both passes the atmosphere scatters and absorbs, and on the way up it also adds path radiance Lp, light scattered into the sensor's view that never touched the ground.
  2. Rayleigh scattering is caused by gas molecules (N₂, O₂) much smaller than the wavelength. Its strength varies as 1 ÷ λ⁴. Blue at 0.45 µm is scattered (0.70 ÷ 0.45)⁴ = 5.86 times more than red at 0.70 µm and (0.85 ÷ 0.45)⁴ = 12.7 times more than near infrared at 0.85 µm. Hence the blue sky, the red low sun, and haze that is worst in blue bands.
  3. Mie scattering is caused by aerosols about the size of the wavelength (0.1 µm to a few µm): dust, smoke, sea salt, pollen. It depends only weakly on wavelength, is strongest in the forward direction and gives the grey-white veil over a city.
  4. Non-selective scattering is caused by particles much larger than the wavelength, such as cloud droplets and coarse dust. All visible and near-infrared wavelengths scatter about equally, so cloud and fog look white and hide the ground in every optical band.
  5. Absorption turns radiation into heat at wavelengths matching molecular transitions: water vapour near 1.4, 1.9 and 2.7 µm; carbon dioxide near 2.7 and 4.3 µm and beyond 14 µm; ozone in the ultraviolet and near 9.6 µm; oxygen in narrow bands near 0.69 and 0.76 µm.

Analyze A sensor at altitude records radiance, but the analyst wants surface reflectance. Analyse the factors that separate the two, and evaluate how atmospheric correction and ground reference data improve the estimate. 13 marks

Points to cover. Radiance is at-sensor flux per unit area per unit solid angle; reflectance is a surface property; Path radiance from scattering adds a constant bias; absorption removes signal in some bands; Illumination geometry: solar zenith and azimuth angle, topographic slope and aspect change the incident flux; Adjacency effect and mixed pixels from finite sensor footprint; Correction methods: dark object subtraction, radiative transfer models, empirical line calibration against bright and dark targets; Residual error remains; indices such as NDVI and band ratios reduce but do not remove it; Evaluate: relative correction is enough for classification, absolute reflectance needed for multi-date quantitative work.

Model answer.

  1. Definitions. Radiance L is the power per unit area per unit solid angle arriving at the sensor, in W m⁻² sr⁻¹; it depends on the sun, the atmosphere and the view. Reflectance ρ is the fraction of incoming light the surface returns, a property of the ground alone.
  2. The link for a Lambertian surface: L = T ρ E ÷ π + Lp, so ρ = π (L − Lp) ÷ (T E), where E is the irradiance at the ground, T the upward transmittance and Lp the path radiance.
  3. Factors that separate them: (1) path radiance from Rayleigh and Mie scattering, an additive bias largest in blue; (2) absorption by water vapour, ozone and CO₂, which removes signal in some bands; (3) illumination: E = E0 cos θz, so the same field receives cos 30° = 0.866 of the overhead value at a 30° solar zenith angle but only cos 60° = 0.50 at 60°; slope and aspect change the local angle further; (4) sensor calibration: DNs must first be turned into radiance with the gain and offset; (5) adjacency and mixed pixels, where light from neighbouring bright surfaces spills in.
  4. Worked case. E = 400 W/m², T = 0.9, Lp = 4.0. A field reading L = 22.0 gives an uncorrected ρ = π × 22.0 ÷ 400 = 0.173 and a corrected ρ = π × 18.0 ÷ 360 = 0.157: the error is 10% of the true value.
  5. Dark object subtraction: the darkest pixels in a band (deep clear water, deep shadow) should have reflectance near zero, so their radiance is taken as Lp. If the darkest water reads 4.0, subtract 4.0 from the whole band. Simple and image-based, but it ignores T and assumes a truly dark object exists.
  6. Empirical line with ground reference data: measure two targets in the field with a spectrometer, a dark one (ρ = 0.05) and a bright one (ρ = 0.45), and read their image radiance, here 9.73 and 55.57. A straight line through them: slope = (55.57 − 9.73) ÷ (0.45 − 0.05) = 114.6, intercept = 9.73 − 114.6 × 0.05 = 4.0. The field then gives ρ = (22.0 − 4.0) ÷ 114.6 = 0.157. The ground data fix both T E ÷ π (the slope) and Lp (the intercept) at once.
  7. Radiative transfer models (6S, MODTRAN) compute T and Lp from the sun and view angles and the aerosol and water-vapour amounts; they need no ground targets but need good atmospheric data.
  8. Evaluation. For a single-date classification a relative correction (or none) is often enough, because all classes are shifted alike. For multi-date change detection or for physical quantities (NDVI trends, water quality, albedo) absolute surface reflectance is needed, and ground reference targets are the best check. Indices and ratios reduce, but do not remove, illumination and haze effects.
  9. Check. The empirical line reproduces the model: 114.6 = T E ÷ π = 0.9 × 400 ÷ 3.1416 = 114.6, and the intercept 4.0 equals Lp.

Glossary

Absorption
Transfer of radiation energy into a medium, such as a gas molecule or a leaf, so that it is not returned to the sensor.
Albedo
Fraction of incident solar radiation reflected by a surface over all wavelengths, a single broadband number.
Atmospheric window
A wavelength interval where absorption is weak, allowing the sensor to see the ground.
Band
A narrow wavelength interval recorded as one image channel by a sensor.
Contrast stretch
Point operation that maps a narrow range of digital numbers onto the full display range to improve visibility.
Digital number
Whole number stored for each pixel of a band; it is related to radiance by the sensor's calibration gain and offset.
Emission
Radiation given out by a body because of its temperature, described by Planck's law.
Error matrix
Table of mapped classes against reference classes at test pixels; it gives overall, user's and producer's accuracy and kappa.
False-colour composite
Image made by assigning three different wavelength bands to red, green and blue display channels; colours are not natural.
Geostationary orbit
Circular equatorial orbit at about 36,000 km altitude where the satellite keeps pace with Earth's rotation and appears fixed.
Instantaneous field of view
Angular cone seen by one detector at one instant; its projection on the ground sets the pixel size.
Kappa coefficient
Measure of classification agreement after removing the agreement expected by chance alone.
LiDAR
Active system that sends laser pulses and times the return to measure distance and build a three-dimensional surface model.
Nadir
The point on the ground directly below the sensor; the viewing direction straight down.
Path radiance
Radiance scattered into the sensor's line of sight by the atmosphere without touching the ground; it brightens hazy images.
Pixel
One picture element, the smallest cell of a raster image, holding a single digital number per band.
Radiance
Radiant flux per unit projected area per unit solid angle reaching the sensor, in W·m⁻²·sr⁻¹.
Reflectance
Dimensionless ratio of reflected to incident flux at a surface, ranging from 0 to 1.
SAR
Synthetic Aperture Radar, an active microwave sensor that uses platform motion to build a fine-resolution image and sees through cloud.
Scale
Ratio of image distance to corresponding ground distance, e.g. 1:25,000.
Scattering
Redirection of radiation by particles or molecules without absorption, producing haze and path radiance.
Swath width
Ground width covered by the sensor in one sweep or one pass.
Texture
Frequency of tonal change in a group of pixels, giving a rough or smooth appearance used in visual interpretation.
Transmittance
