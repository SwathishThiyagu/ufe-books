3.1 Levelling terms: datum, benchmark, reduced level

Levelling finds how much higher or lower one point is than another. A level cannot measure a height above the sea directly: it gives a horizontal line of sight, and two staff readings taken from one set-up give the height difference between the two staff points. Heights become useful only when every difference is tied to one agreed zero surface, the datum, through a point whose height is already known, the benchmark. The height of any point above the datum is its reduced level. Road gradients, drain slopes, canal beds, plinth levels and contour maps are all built from these three ideas.

Surfaces and lines

A level surface is everywhere at right angles to gravity, like the surface of still water; because the earth is round, it is curved. A level line is a line on a level surface, so it is curved too.

A horizontal plane at a point is the flat plane that touches the level surface at that point. A horizontal line is a straight line in that plane, and a vertical line is the direction of a plumb bob, at right angles to the level surface. With the bubble centred, a level's line of collimation (through the cross-hairs and the objective's centre) is horizontal. Over sights of about 100 m the horizontal and level lines differ by under a millimetre; longer sights need the curvature and refraction correction.

The datum is the level surface from which heights are measured. National maps use mean sea level (MSL), the average sea level over a long period of tide readings; the Survey of India publishes benchmark heights above it. A small site may use an arbitrary datum (say, a gate pillar called 100.000 m); such heights compare with each other but not with a map until the datums are connected.

Benchmarks and reduced levels

The reduced level (RL) of a point is its height above the datum, in metres, usually written to three decimals (millimetres). Some syllabi call it the elevation of the point. A point below the datum has a negative RL; for example the floor of a deep sump on an arbitrary datum of 0.000 m at the road can have RL −2.350 m. Always write the datum beside a height, because a height without its datum is not an RL.

A benchmark (BM) is a fixed, well-marked point whose RL is known. Four kinds are named in the syllabi:

  • GTS benchmark: a mark of the Great Trigonometrical Survey across the country, with its RL above MSL published by the Survey of India; the most reliable starting height.
  • Permanent benchmark: a mark fixed by a department such as PWD or the railways on a culvert, pier or milestone, its RL found from a GTS benchmark.
  • Arbitrary benchmark: a point given an assumed RL (for example 100.000 m) on a small job where no permanent benchmark is near; all heights are then relative.
  • Temporary benchmark (TBM): a point fixed for one job, such as a peg in concrete or a mark on a kerb, used to close the day's work and to restart from the next day.

A station is the point where the staff is held and a reading is taken. The levelling staff is a graduated rod held vertically on the station; the reading where the horizontal cross-hair cuts it is the height of the line of collimation above the foot of the staff.

The readings and the height chain

Each set-up of the level has one backsight (BS): the first reading, taken on a point of known RL. It may have several intermediate sights (IS): readings on points whose RL is wanted. It ends with one foresight (FS): the last reading before the instrument is moved. A change point (CP), also called a turning point, is a firm station that takes a foresight from one set-up and then a backsight from the next, so the height chain carries across the move.

The height of instrument (HI), also called the height of collimation, is the RL of the line of collimation for one set-up. It is a reduced level, not the height of the tripod above the ground.

HI = RL of the backsight station + BS;   RL of any station = HI − its staff reading

All values are in metres. The BS and the reading on the new station must come from the same set-up, because each set-up has its own HI.

Combining the two gives RL(new) = RL(known) + BS − FS. A positive BS − FS means the new point is higher than the known point. A large reading means low ground, because more of the staff stands below the line of sight.

Worked example

Question. A level is set up near a road culvert. The permanent benchmark on the culvert has RL 120.450 m above MSL. The staff readings are: 1.865 m on the BM, 0.635 m on the plinth of a building, 2.410 m on the road edge, and 1.250 m on a peg to be used as a TBM (the last reading before the level is moved). (a) Name each reading. (b) Find the HI and the RL of the plinth, the road edge and the TBM. (c) The site drawings had used an arbitrary datum, calling the plinth 100.000 m. Convert the arbitrary RLs 101.385 m and 98.640 m to MSL.

  1. (a) 1.865 m on the BM is the backsight (first reading, on a known RL). 0.635 m and 2.410 m are intermediate sights. 1.250 m on the peg is the foresight, because it is the last reading before the level moves.
  2. (b) HI = RL of BM + BS = 120.450 + 1.865 = 122.315 m.
  3. RL of plinth = HI − 0.635 = 122.315 − 0.635 = 121.680 m.
  4. RL of road edge = 122.315 − 2.410 = 119.905 m. The large reading tells us the road is the lowest point seen.
  5. RL of TBM = 122.315 − 1.250 = 121.065 m.
  6. Check: RL(TBM) − RL(BM) should equal BS − FS. 121.065 − 120.450 = 0.615 m and 1.865 − 1.250 = 0.615 m. They agree.
  7. (c) The plinth is 100.000 m on the arbitrary datum and 121.680 m on MSL, so the MSL value = arbitrary value + 21.680 m.
  8. 101.385 m arbitrary → 101.385 + 21.680 = 123.065 m above MSL; 98.640 m arbitrary → 98.640 + 21.680 = 120.320 m above MSL.

Answer. HI = 122.315 m; RL of plinth 121.680 m, road edge 119.905 m, TBM 121.065 m (all above MSL). The arbitrary heights 101.385 m and 98.640 m are 123.065 m and 120.320 m above MSL.

Exam traps

  • Calling the HI the height of the tripod. HI is the RL of the line of sight: 122.315 m in the example, not about 1.5 m.
  • Mixing readings of two set-ups in RL = RL(known) + BS − FS. The BS and the FS must be read with the instrument in the same place.
  • Thinking a larger reading means a higher point. It is the opposite: a larger staff reading means lower ground.
  • Leaving out the datum. Write “RL 121.680 m above MSL” or “on an arbitrary datum”; two heights on different datums cannot be subtracted.

3.2 Levels and their temporary and permanent adjustments

A level is a telescope that can be made to look along a horizontal line, mounted so that it can turn in a horizontal circle. Every reading in levelling assumes that the line of sight is truly horizontal when the bubble is centred. Two kinds of adjustment make that true. Temporary adjustments are done at every set-up: setting up, levelling up and removing parallax. Permanent adjustments are done only when a test shows the instrument's own lines are out of their proper relation; the most important is the two-peg test for the line of collimation.

You need: backsight, foresight, height of instrument and reduced level from Levelling terms: datum, benchmark, reduced level.

Types of level and their parts

  • Dumpy level: telescope fixed rigidly to the vertical spindle, bubble tube on top; levelled with three foot screws.
  • Tilting level: a tilting screw tilts the telescope slightly, so the main bubble is centred quickly for each sight.
  • Automatic level: a hanging prism compensator keeps the line of sight horizontal once a circular bubble is centred; the common site instrument today.
  • Digital level: an automatic level that reads a bar-coded staff and stores the readings. Older texts also describe the wye (Y) level.

The main parts of a dumpy level are the telescope (objective lens, eyepiece, and the diaphragm carrying the cross-hairs), the focusing screw, the bubble tube (a curved vial whose axis is horizontal when the bubble is central), the levelling head with three foot screws, and the tripod. The levelling staff is usually a 4 m folding or telescopic staff graduated every 5 mm, read to the millimetre.

Three lines matter. The vertical axis is the axis the instrument turns about. The axis of the bubble tube is the tangent to the vial's curve at its centre. The line of collimation joins the intersection of the cross-hairs to the optical centre of the objective. For a correct dumpy level: (1) the bubble axis is perpendicular to the vertical axis, (2) the line of collimation is parallel to the bubble axis, and (3) the horizontal cross-hair lies in a plane perpendicular to the vertical axis.

Temporary adjustments at every set-up

  1. Setting up. Plant the tripod firmly and bring the head roughly level by moving the legs.
  2. Levelling up. With the telescope parallel to two foot screws, turn them together (thumbs both inward or both outward) until the bubble is central; the bubble follows the left thumb. Turn through 90° over the third screw and centre the bubble with it alone. Repeat until the bubble stays central in every direction.
  3. Focusing the eyepiece on the cross-hairs against a bright background.
  4. Focusing the objective on the staff with the focusing screw.
  5. Removing parallax. If the cross-hair seems to move on the staff when the eye moves, the image is not in the plane of the cross-hairs; refocus until it does not.

Permanent adjustments and the two-peg test

Permanent adjustments correct the instrument itself, in this order:

  • Bubble tube adjustment (bubble axis perpendicular to the vertical axis). Level up carefully, then turn the telescope through 180°. If the bubble moves off centre, bring it halfway back with the capstan screws at one end of the bubble tube and the other half with the foot screws. Repeat until the bubble stays central in all directions.
  • Cross-hair ring adjustment (horizontal hair perpendicular to the vertical axis). A point sighted at one end of the horizontal hair must stay on it as the telescope turns; if not, rotate the diaphragm ring.
  • Collimation adjustment (line of collimation parallel to the bubble axis), by the two-peg test. If the line of sight tilts by α, a reading at distance D is wrong by D tan α; equal sights cancel this, so the test uses unequal sights.

The two-peg test uses two pegs A and B about 100 m apart:

  1. Set up exactly midway at C and read both staffs. The two readings carry equal error, so their difference is the true difference of level.
  2. Move the level to a point D on the line BA produced, a short distance beyond A, and read both staffs again.
  3. Let e be the error per metre of sight. Then (reading on B − DB·e) − (reading on A − DA·e) = true difference. Solve for e.
  4. Compute the correct reading on B, (reading on B) − DB·e. With the level still at D, move the horizontal cross-hair with the diaphragm screws (dumpy level), or bring the line of sight to the correct reading with the tilting screw and then re-centre the bubble with its capstan screws (tilting level). On an automatic level the reticule is adjusted.
e = [(b₂ − a₂) − (b₁ − a₁)] ÷ (DB − DA);   α = tan⁻¹ e

a₁, b₁ are the readings on A and B from the midpoint; a₂, b₂ the readings from D; DA, DB the distances from D to A and B in metres. A positive e means the line of sight points upward (readings too large).

The sensitivity of a bubble tube is the angle the line of sight tilts when the bubble moves one division. Read a staff at distance D, move the bubble n divisions with a foot screw and read again; if the reading changes by s, the sensitivity is α = s ÷ (nD) radians, or 206 265 × s ÷ (nD) seconds.

Worked example

Question. Two pegs A and B are 100 m apart. With the level midway, the readings are 1.625 m on A and 2.115 m on B. The level is then set up at D, 10 m from A on the line BA produced (so DB = 110 m), and the readings are 1.435 m on A and 1.943 m on B. (a) Find the true difference of level between A and B. (b) Is the line of collimation inclined upward or downward, and by what angle? (c) What should the staff on B read when the instrument at D is in adjustment? (d) Check your answer.

  1. (a) From the midpoint the errors cancel: true difference = b₁ − a₁ = 2.115 − 1.625 = 0.490 m. B reads more, so B is 0.490 m lower than A.
  2. (b) Apparent difference from D = b₂ − a₂ = 1.943 − 1.435 = 0.508 m.
  3. The extra 0.508 − 0.490 = 0.018 m builds up over the extra sight length DB − DA = 110 − 10 = 100 m, so e = 0.018 ÷ 100 = 0.00018 m per metre.
  4. e is positive, so the far reading is too large: the line of sight is inclined upward. α = tan⁻¹(0.00018) = 0.00018 rad × 206 265 = 37″ (about 37 seconds).
  5. (c) Correct reading on B = 1.943 − 110 × 0.00018 = 1.943 − 0.0198 = 1.923 m.
  6. Correct reading on A = 1.435 − 10 × 0.00018 = 1.435 − 0.0018 = 1.433 m.
  7. (d) Check: 1.923 − 1.433 = 0.490 m, the true difference. Adjust until B reads 1.923 m with the bubble central.

Answer. True difference 0.490 m (B lower). The line of collimation is inclined upward by about 37″ (0.018 m in 100 m). With the level at D, the staff on B should read 1.923 m.

Exam traps

  • Taking the difference from the second set-up as the true one. Only the midpoint readings give the true difference, because equal sights cancel the collimation error.
  • Correcting the whole bubble error with the capstan screws. In the bubble tube adjustment, half the error is the instrument's (capstan screws) and half is the levelling (foot screws).
  • Doing the two-peg test with parallax or a moving bubble. Temporary adjustments come first, or a correct instrument gets “adjusted” wrongly.
  • Naming the wrong order. Permanent adjustments of a dumpy level: bubble tube first, then the cross-hair ring, then the line of collimation.

3.3 Differential, fly and profile levelling

Every levelling method repeats one step: from a set-up, the backsight on a known point and the foresight on a new point give the new point's RL. The methods differ in how the steps are strung together and what the run is for. Differential levelling carries a height to a distant point through change points. Fly levelling is a quick run of backsights and foresights to bring a height to the work, and check levelling closes the run back on a known benchmark to measure the error. Profile levelling takes many intermediate sights along and across a line, so that a road, canal or pipeline can be drawn as a section and its cutting and filling worked out.

You need: HI = RL + BS and RL = HI − reading from Levelling terms, and an adjusted level from Levels and their adjustments.

Simple, differential, fly and check levelling

Simple levelling uses one set-up: the difference of level between two points both visible from one station is BS − FS. When the points are far apart, the ground is too steep for the 4 m staff, or obstacles block the view, the run is split by change points. This is differential levelling (compound levelling): each change point is read as a foresight, the level moves on, and the same point is read as a backsight.

ΣBS − ΣFS = RL(last point) − RL(first point)

ΣBS is the sum of all backsights and ΣFS the sum of all foresights in the run, in metres. This checks the arithmetic of the run; it cannot detect a misread staff.

Fly levelling is a quick differential run with long, balanced sights and no intermediate sights, to bring a benchmark's height to a site. Check levelling tests earlier work: it ends back on the starting benchmark or on another benchmark of known RL. The closing error e is the observed RL of the closing benchmark minus its known RL.

The permissible closing error is written e = C√K mm, with K the run length in km and C set by the class of work (a few mm for precise levelling, more for ordinary work). An acceptable e is shared out: correction at a point = −e × (set-ups up to it ÷ total set-ups), or in proportion to distance. A larger e means the run is repeated.

Profile levelling and cross-sections

Profile levelling (longitudinal sectioning) finds ground RLs along the centre line of a road, railway, canal or sewer, pegged every 20 m or 30 m and at changes of slope. Each set-up has a backsight, intermediate sights on the pegs in view, and a foresight on a change point. RL plotted against chainage, with an exaggerated vertical scale, is the longitudinal section (L-section).

Cross-sections are levelled at right angles to the centre line at each peg, at set offsets left and right. The engineer draws the formation line (the proposed finished level) on the L-section. At each peg, depth of cutting = ground RL − formation RL when positive; depth of filling = formation RL − ground RL when the ground is below formation. Formation RL at chainage x on a falling gradient of 1 in n is RL₀ − x ÷ n.

Errors in levelling and precautions

  • Instrumental: collimation error (cancelled by equal sights), a sluggish bubble, a loose tripod head, a staff of wrong length.
  • Natural: curvature and refraction on long sights, wind, heat shimmer, the tripod or a change point sinking.
  • Personal: bubble off centre while reading, parallax, a tilted staff (always reads too large), misreading the metre, booking in the wrong column.
  • Precautions: sights under about 100 m and balanced; check the bubble at every reading; rock the staff and take the smallest reading; change points on firm, sharp objects; book before moving and check on site.

Worked example

Question. Profile levelling was done along the centre line of a proposed road from a BM of RL 85.250 m. The readings were: BS 1.420 on the BM; IS 1.865 at chainage 0, 2.310 at 20 m and 2.945 at 40 m; FS 3.280 at 60 m (change point); BS 0.865 at 60 m; IS 1.540 at 80 m and 2.105 at 100 m; FS 2.660 at 120 m.   1. Set-up 1: HI = 85.250 + 1.420 = 86.670 m. RLs: ch 0 = 86.670 − 1.865 = 84.805; ch 20 = 84.360; ch 40 = 83.725; ch 60 (FS) = 86.670 − 3.280 = 83.390 m.

  4. Formation falls 1 in 50, that is 0.400 m per 20 m: 84.200, 83.800, 83.400, 83.000, 82.600, 82.200 and 81.800 m at chainages 0 to 120.
  5. Ground − formation at each peg: ch 0: 84.805 − 84.200 = 0.605 m cut; ch 20: 0.560 m cut; ch 40: 0.325 m cut; ch 60: 0.390 m cut; ch 80: 0.115 m cut; ch 100: 82.150 − 82.200 = −0.050 m, so 0.050 m fill; ch 120: 81.595 − 81.800 = −0.205 m, so 0.205 m fill.

Worked example 2

Question. Check levelling was run from BM A (RL 100.000 m) to a temporary benchmark T and back to A, with four set-ups of roughly equal length, 1.6 km in all. Set-up 1: BS 1.325 on A, FS 2.040 on CP1. Set-up 2: BS 0.985 on CP1, FS 1.760 on T. Set-up 3: BS 2.115 on T, FS 1.090 on CP2. Set-up 4: BS 1.905 on CP2, FS 1.428 on A. The specification allows 12√K mm. Find the closing error, decide whether the work is accepted, and give the corrected RL of T.

  1. RL of CP1 = 100.000 + 1.325 − 2.040 = 99.285 m. RL of T = 99.285 + 0.985 − 1.760 = 98.510 m.
  2. RL of CP2 = 98.510 + 2.115 − 1.090 = 99.535 m. RL of A on return = 99.535 + 1.905 − 1.428 = 100.012 m.
  3. Closing error e = 100.012 − 100.000 = +0.012 m = 12 mm. Check: ΣBS − ΣFS = 6.330 − 6.318 = +0.012 m.
  4. Permissible error = 12√1.6 = 12 × 1.265 = 15.2 mm. Since 12 mm < 15.2 mm, the work is accepted.
  5. Correction per set-up = −12 ÷ 4 = −3 mm. T is reached after 2 set-ups, so its correction is −6 mm: RL of T = 98.510 − 0.006 = 98.504 m.

Answer. Closing error +12 mm, within the permitted 15.2 mm, so the run is accepted; the corrected RL of T is 98.504 m.

Exam traps

  • Ending a profile run with an intermediate sight. The last reading of every set-up, including the final one, is a foresight.
  • Thinking the check ΣBS − ΣFS = last RL − first RL proves the field work. It only checks the arithmetic; a misread staff still balances. Closing on a known benchmark is the field check.
  • Applying the whole closing error to the last point. It is shared in proportion to set-ups or distance, so T in the example gets −6 mm, not −12 mm.
  • Getting cut and fill the wrong way round: cut when the ground is above formation, fill when it is below.

3.4 Booking and reduction of levels

A level gives staff readings, not heights. The level book records each reading in its proper column, and reduction turns the readings into reduced levels. There are two standard methods. The height of collimation (HI) method finds the RL of the line of sight once per set-up and subtracts each reading from it. The rise and fall method compares each reading with the one before it from the same set-up. Both end with arithmetic checks that must be run on site, while a wrong page can still be re-read.

You need: backsight, intermediate sight, foresight and change point from Levelling terms, and the field patterns of Differential, fly and profile levelling.

The level book and the height of collimation method

Each line of the level book is one staff station. The columns are Station, BS, IS, FS, then either HI (height of collimation method) or Rise and Fall (rise and fall method), then RL and Remarks. A change point takes one line with its FS and its BS side by side, because both readings are on the same point. A staff held upside down against the underside of a beam, a bridge soffit or a ceiling is an inverted staff; its reading is booked with a minus sign, because the point is above the line of sight.

  1. Write the BM and its RL. HI of set-up 1 = RL of BM + BS.
  2. For every IS and the FS of that set-up: RL = HI − reading. An inverted reading −r gives RL = HI + r.
  3. At a change point, the RL found from its FS plus the new BS gives the next HI: HI(new) = RL(CP) + BS(new).
  4. Continue to the last FS.
Check 1:  ΣBS − ΣFS = last RL − first RL
Check 2 (full):  Σ(each HI × number of IS and FS reduced from it) − ΣIS − ΣFS = sum of all RLs except the first

Check 1 tests only the BS, FS and change-point arithmetic. Check 2 also tests every intermediate subtraction; without it, a slip in an IS line passes unnoticed.

The rise and fall method

Within one set-up the line of sight is fixed, so a smaller reading means the staff stands on higher ground. Compare each reading with the previous reading from the same set-up (the previous reading of a change-point line is its BS, for the next comparison):

previous reading − present reading = + (a rise)  or  − (a fall);   RL = previous RL + rise, or previous RL − fall

Rise and fall are booked as positive numbers in their own columns; the heading carries the sign. An inverted reading is used with its minus sign, so 0.580 followed by −1.725 is a rise of 0.580 − (−1.725) = 2.305 m.

Check:  ΣBS − ΣFS = ΣRise − ΣFall = last RL − first RL

Every IS enters a rise or a fall, so this one three-way check tests all the arithmetic.

Comparing the two methods

  • HI method: fewer calculations with many intermediate sights, so it suits profiles, contouring and setting out.
  • Rise and fall: more arithmetic, but one check covers every reading, so it suits fly and check levelling.
  • Neither check finds a misread staff: the level book balances with wrong readings. Only closing on a known benchmark does.
  • To complete a page with missing entries, use the column relations: rise or fall from two readings, a reading from a rise or fall, and a missing BS from the final RL and Check 1.

3.5 Curvature and refraction; reciprocal levelling

On a long sight the horizontal line of sight rises away from the curved level surface, so the far staff reads too high; this is curvature. The air bends the ray slightly back towards the earth, so the staff reads a little lower; this is refraction. Curvature is the larger, so the combined correction is always subtracted from a long-sight reading. Where a long sight cannot be avoided, for example across a river or valley, reciprocal levelling cancels both errors, and the instrument's collimation error with them, by reading from both banks. Only a few syllabi list this topic by name, but every standard textbook covers it and it is a regular exam problem.

You need: the level line and horizontal line from Levelling terms and the collimation error of Levels and their adjustments.

Curvature, refraction and the combined correction

Let the instrument be at A and the staff at B, a horizontal distance d away, both on the same level surface. The horizontal line from A meets the staff at B at a height c above the level line. With R the earth's radius, (R + c)² = R² + d², and since c is tiny compared with 2R, c = d² ÷ 2R. With R = 6370 km, d in kilometres and c in metres:

Cc = d² ÷ (2R) = 0.0785 d²   (subtract)
Cr = (1/7) Cc = 0.0112 d²   (add)
C = Cc − Cr = 0.0673 d²   (subtract)

d is the sight length in km; the corrections are in metres. The refraction factor 1/7 is an average for daytime conditions; it varies with temperature and pressure, which is why long sights near the ground at midday are unreliable.

At 100 m (d = 0.1 km) C is under 1 mm, which is why ordinary levelling ignores it. At 800 m it is 0.0673 × 0.64 = 0.043 m, far too large to ignore. Equal backsight and foresight lengths also cancel C, just as they cancel collimation error, because both readings are too high by the same amount. Rearranged, d = √(h ÷ 0.0673) = 3.855√h km gives the distance to the visible horizon from an eye h metres above the level surface.

Reciprocal levelling

Across a river the instrument cannot stand midway, so the sights cannot be balanced. Reciprocal levelling uses two set-ups instead:

  1. Set the level very close to A (a few metres). Read the staff on A (a near reading, almost error-free) and on B (a far reading).
  2. Take the level across and set it very close to B. Read B (near) and A (far).
  3. Let h be the true fall from A to B and e the total error of a far reading (curvature − refraction + collimation). From near A: rB − rA = h + e. From near B: r′B − r′A = h − e.
  4. Add and subtract the two equations.
h = ½[(rB − rA) + (r′B − r′A)];   e = ½[(rB − rA) − (r′B − r′A)]

rA, rB: readings with the level near A; r′A, r′B: readings with the level near B. h positive means A is higher than B. e minus 0.0673 d² gives the collimation error over the far sight.

The method assumes refraction is the same for both far sights. Taking the two sets at about the same time, ideally with two levels read at once, or averaging several sets, keeps that assumption reasonable.

Question. Reciprocal levelling across a river 800 m wide between A (RL 150.000 m) and B gave: level near A, staff on A 1.428 m and on B 2.365 m; level near B, staff on B 1.815 m and on A 0.980 m.   3. RL of B = 150.000 − 0.886 = 149.114 m.
  4. Error in one far reading e = ½(0.937 − 0.835) = 0.051 m.
  5. Combined curvature and refraction for d = 0.8 km: C = 0.0673 × 0.8² = 0.043 m.
  6. Collimation error over 800 m = 0.051 − 0.043 = 0.008 m (readings too high, so the line of sight points upward), an angle of 0.008 ÷ 800 × 206 265 ≈ 2″.

Exam traps

  • Entering d in metres in 0.0673 d². For 800 m, d = 0.8 km and C = 0.043 m; with d = 800 the answer is 43 000 m.
  • Adding the combined correction. Curvature makes the far reading too large, so C is subtracted; only the refraction part on its own is additive.
  • Taking 0.0785 d² as the net correction. That is curvature alone; with refraction the net is 0.0673 d².
  • Mixing the order of subtraction in the two set-ups. Use (reading on B − reading on A) in both, or the two errors do not cancel.
  • Claiming reciprocal levelling removes refraction always. It cancels only the part that is the same for both far sights.

3.6 Contours: characteristics, methods and uses

A contour is an imaginary line on the ground joining points of equal reduced level; the shoreline of a still lake is one. A map with contours drawn at equal height steps shows the third dimension on flat paper: where the lines crowd, the ground is steep; where they spread, it is gentle. Contour maps are used to choose sites, align roads and canals at a fixed gradient, mark catchments, find the area flooded behind a dam and estimate earthwork. Contouring needs levelling, so it builds directly on the booking and reduction methods.

You need: RLs from the height of collimation method in Booking and reduction of levels.

Contour interval and horizontal equivalent

The contour interval (CI), also called the vertical interval, is the constant difference in RL between successive contours on a map, for example 1 m or 2 m. The horizontal equivalent (HE) is the horizontal distance between two successive contours at a place; it changes from place to place with the slope.

HE = CI ÷ tan θ = CI × n   (ground gradient 1 in n)

θ is the ground slope angle and n the gradient expressed as 1 vertical in n horizontal. Halving the gradient (1 in 25 instead of 1 in 50) halves the HE, so the contours come closer.

The CI depends on the purpose, the map scale, the steepness of the ground (steep ground needs a larger CI or the lines merge) and the time and money available: typically 0.25 m to 1 m on a 1:500 or 1:1000 site plan, and 10 m or 20 m on a 1:50 000 sheet.

Characteristics of contours

  • All points on one contour have the same RL. Every contour closes on itself, within the map or beyond its edge.
  • Contours close together mean steep ground; widely spaced means gentle ground; equally spaced means a uniform slope; straight, parallel and equally spaced lines mean a plane surface.
  • Closed contours with higher values inside show a hill; with lower values inside, a depression (pond or pit).
  • Across a valley, contours form V or U shapes pointing uphill, because the valley floor rises upstream. Across a ridge, they form V or U shapes pointing downhill.
  • Two contours of different RL do not cross or meet, except at a vertical cliff (where they merge) or an overhanging cliff (where they cross, the hidden one dashed).
  • The direction of steepest slope, which water follows, is at right angles to the contours.

Methods of contouring

Direct method. Points on each contour are found in the field. From the HI, a contour of RL H needs the staff reading HI − H; the staffman moves up or down the slope until the reading matches, and the point is pegged and fixed in plan. Accurate but slow; suits small areas.

Indirect method. Spot levels are taken and contours interpolated between them. The points can be a grid of squares (block levelling, 5 m to 20 m sides), cross-sections along a route, radial lines from a station on a hill, or tacheometric points on rough ground.

x = L × (H − HA) ÷ (HB − HA)

Interpolation by calculation: a contour of RL H crosses the line from A (RL HA) to B (RL HB) at x from A, with L the plan distance AB, assuming a uniform slope. Estimation by eye and the graphical method (a tracing of parallel lines) are faster but rougher.

Uses of contour maps

  • Sections and intervisibility: a line across the map gives the ground profile and shows whether two points see each other.
  • Route location at a fixed gradient: for a gradient of 1 in n, set dividers to HE = CI × n at map scale and step from contour to contour; the points joined form the grade contour.
  • Catchment: the ridge line around a valley, traced across the contours, bounds the catchment area.
  • Reservoir and earthwork: the area inside the contour at full supply level is the submerged area, and contour areas give capacity and cut-and-fill volumes, as worked in Areas, volumes and reservoir capacity from contours.

3.7 Areas, volumes and reservoir capacity from contours

Each closed contour on a map encloses an area at a known level. Treat the ground between two contours as a slice with those two areas as its faces, add the slices, and you have the volume of earth or water below any level. This is how the capacity of a reservoir is worked out before a dam is built, and how a capacity–elevation curve is drawn for its operation. Only a few universities teach this as a separate topic; it completes the uses of contours.

You need: contour interval and the uses of contour maps from Contours: characteristics, methods and uses.

Areas enclosed by contours

The area inside each contour is measured on the map with a planimeter (mechanical or digital), by counting squares on a transparent grid, or from the coordinates of points along the line (the coordinate method of area computation). A map area is converted to ground area with the scale: at 1:s, 1 cm² of map is (s cm)² of ground. At 1:5000, 1 cm² = 5000² cm² = 2500 m² = 0.25 ha, where 1 hectare = 10 000 m².

Volumes between contours

Let A₁, A₂, …, An be the areas of n successive contours at a contour interval h.

Trapezoidal (end-area) rule:  V = h [ (A₁ + An)/2 + A₂ + A₃ + … + An−1 ]
Prismoidal rule (n odd):  V = (h/3) [ A₁ + An + 4(A₂ + A₄ + …) + 2(A₃ + A₅ + …) ]

V in m³ when areas are in m² and h in m. The trapezoidal rule takes the area to change linearly with height in each slice. The prismoidal rule treats each pair of slices as a prismoid whose middle area is the contour between them; it needs an odd number of contours (an even number of slices). If the number is even, use it on all but the last slice and add that slice by the trapezoidal rule.

Below the lowest contour the basin narrows to its deepest point. That last bit is taken as a cone or pyramid: V₀ = A₁ × h₀ ÷ 3, where h₀ is the height of the lowest contour above the deepest point.

Reservoir capacity and the capacity curve

The capacity at a level is the total volume of water below it, measured from the deepest point, so it is the running sum of the slices from the bottom upward. Plotting capacity (and area) against elevation gives the capacity–elevation curve, which is read at any water level during operation. Water below the sill of the lowest outlet is dead storage: it cannot be released and is left for silt. The storage between the outlet sill and the full reservoir level (full supply level, FSL) is the live storage, the water that can be used.

Worked example

Question. A reservoir site is contoured on a 1:5000 map at a 2 m interval. The planimeter areas inside the contours are: 100 m, 1.40 cm²; 102 m, 4.40; 104 m, 8.20; 106 m, 12.80; 108 m, 18.40; 110 m, 24.40; 112 m, 31.40 cm². The deepest point of the valley is at RL 99.2 m, the outlet sill is at 102.0 m and the full reservoir level is 110.0 m. Find the capacity at each contour, the dead and live storage, and compare the trapezoidal and prismoidal answers for the live storage.

  1. Ground areas (1 cm² = 2500 m²): 3500, 11 000, 20 500, 32 000, 46 000, 61 000 and 78 500 m² (0.35 to 7.85 ha).
  2. Bottom cone, 99.2 to 100 m: V₀ = 3500 × 0.8 ÷ 3 = 933 m³.
  3. Slices by the trapezoidal rule, V = 2 × (Alower + Aupper)/2: 100–102 m 14 500; 102–104 m 31 500; 104–106 m 52 500; 106–108 m 78 000; 108–110 m 107 000; 110–112 m 139 500 m³.
  4. Running sums give the capacity: 100 m, 933; 102 m, 15 433; 104 m, 46 933; 106 m, 99 433; 108 m, 177 433; 110 m, 284 433; 112 m, 423 933 m³.
  5. Dead storage = capacity at the sill (102 m) ≈ 15 430 m³. Live storage (trapezoidal) = 284 433 − 15 433 = 269 000 m³.
  6. Prismoidal rule from 102 m to 110 m (five contours, an odd number): V = (2/3)[11 000 + 61 000 + 4(20 500 + 46 000) + 2(32 000)] = (2/3)(402 000) = 268 000 m³.
  7. The two differ by 1000 m³, under 0.4 %; the prismoidal value is the better estimate for a basin that widens smoothly.

Answer. Capacity rises from 933 m³ at 100 m to 284 433 m³ at the full reservoir level of 110 m (and 423 933 m³ at 112 m). Dead storage ≈ 15 430 m³; live storage 269 000 m³ by the trapezoidal rule and 268 000 m³ by the prismoidal rule.

Exam traps

  • Using the map area directly. 1.40 cm² on a 1:5000 map is 3500 m², not 1.40 m²; the scale is squared for areas.
  • Applying the prismoidal rule to an even number of contours. It needs an odd number; add a leftover slice by the trapezoidal rule.
  • Measuring capacity from the lowest contour. The volume below it (the bottom cone, 933 m³ here) belongs in the capacity.
  • Calling capacity at FSL the usable water. Usable (live) storage excludes the dead storage below the outlet sill.
  • Unit slips with hectares: 1 ha = 10 000 m², so 1 ha × 1 m = 10 000 m³.

Analyze Silt raises the bed of the reservoir in the example by 1.5 m over 20 years. Which storage is lost first, and why does the capacity curve need re-surveying? 4 marks

Silt settles in the deepest part first, so dead storage below 102 m (about 15 430 m³) is filled first, then live storage. The areas at the lower contours shrink, so the old capacity curve overstates the water at every level until a fresh survey redraws it.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Today reservoir capacity comes from a digital elevation model of the basin, built from drone or LiDAR survey above water and an echo-sounder (bathymetric) survey below it, and GIS computes the area and volume at any level in seconds. Repeated bathymetric surveys measure sedimentation and update the operating curves. Search for reservoir sedimentation survey, area–capacity curve and bathymetric survey.

Takeaways

  1. A level measures only differences of height; the datum enters through a benchmark of known RL.
  2. Within one set-up, RL = HI − reading, and a larger reading means lower ground; an inverted reading is negative.
  3. Temporary adjustments are made at every set-up; the two-peg test checks that the line of collimation is horizontal when the bubble is central.
  4. Equal backsight and foresight lengths cancel collimation error and curvature and refraction.
  5. The arithmetic checks test only arithmetic; closing the run on a known benchmark is the only test of the field work.
  6. Use the full HI check or the rise and fall method when intermediate sights matter.
  7. On long sights subtract 0.0673 d²; across a river use reciprocal levelling.
  8. Close contours mean steep ground; a valley's V points uphill; contours are interpolated from spot levels assuming a uniform slope.
  9. Contour areas give volumes by the trapezoidal or prismoidal rule; capacity is the running sum from the deepest point, and live storage excludes the dead storage below the outlet.

Understand Define datum, benchmark, reduced level, backsight, foresight, intermediate sight, change point and height of instrument. Explain, with a sketch, how a reduced level is carried from a GTS benchmark to a distant point. 13 marks

Points to cover. Datum and MSL; level line versus horizontal line; Four types of benchmark; BS, IS, FS, CP and HI defined; Sketch of set-ups and the height chain; HI = RL + BS; RL = HI − reading; ΣBS − ΣFS check.

Model answer.

  1. Datum: the level surface from which heights are measured; in India mean sea level (MSL), to which the Survey of India's benchmarks are referred. A level line lies on a level surface and is curved; a horizontal line is straight and touches the level line at one point. A levelled instrument's line of sight is a horizontal line.
  2. Reduced level (RL): the height of a point above the datum, in metres to three decimals, positive above and negative below the datum.
  3. Benchmark: a fixed point of known RL. GTS benchmarks are fixed by the national survey with published RLs; permanent benchmarks are fixed by departments on culverts, piers and milestones; arbitrary benchmarks are given an assumed RL on small jobs; temporary benchmarks are set for one job, to close and restart work.
  4. Backsight (BS): the first reading after setting up, on a point of known RL. Foresight (FS): the last reading before the level is moved. Intermediate sight (IS): any reading between them. Change point (CP): a firm point that takes an FS from one set-up and a BS from the next. Height of instrument (HI): the RL of the line of collimation for a set-up.
  5. Sketch (side view): the GTS BM, the level at set-up 1 with its horizontal line of sight, the staff on CP1, set-up 2, and the staff on the distant point P. Each set-up has its own HI.
  6. Procedure: set up between the BM and CP1 with roughly equal sights; HI₁ = RL(BM) + BS₁; RL(CP1) = HI₁ − FS₁. Move the level; HI₂ = RL(CP1) + BS₂; RL(P) = HI₂ − FS₂. Repeat for as many set-ups as needed.
  7. Worked numbers: BM 120.450 m, BS 1.865 → HI 122.315 m; FS 1.250 on a TBM → RL 121.065 m. The TBM is then the start of the next day's work.
  8. Check: ΣBS − ΣFS = RL(last) − RL(first). Here 1.865 − 1.250 = 0.615 = 121.065 − 120.450. The run should finally close on a second known benchmark so that the field work, not just the arithmetic, is checked.
Understand Describe the temporary adjustments of a dumpy level. State the fundamental lines and the permanent adjustments, and explain the principle of the two-peg test. 13 marks

Points to cover. Setting up, levelling up (two screws, then third), eyepiece, objective, parallax; Vertical axis, bubble axis, line of collimation and required relations; Bubble tube, cross-hair ring and collimation adjustments in order; Two-peg test: midpoint gives true difference; second set-up finds the error.

Model answer.

  1. Setting up: plant the tripod legs firmly and bring the head roughly level by moving the legs; fix the instrument.
  2. Levelling up: turn the telescope parallel to two foot screws and turn them together, thumbs both inward or both outward, until the bubble is central (it follows the left thumb). Turn the telescope through 90° over the third screw and centre the bubble with it alone. Repeat until the bubble stays central in every direction.
  3. Focusing the eyepiece on the cross-hairs against a bright background; focusing the objective on the staff; removing parallax by refocusing until the cross-hair does not move on the staff when the eye moves.
  4. Fundamental lines: the vertical axis, the axis of the bubble tube and the line of collimation. Required: bubble axis ⟂ vertical axis; line of collimation ∥ bubble axis; horizontal cross-hair ⟂ vertical axis.
  5. Bubble tube adjustment: level up, turn through 180°; if the bubble runs, correct half with the capstan screws of the tube and half with the foot screws; repeat.
  6. Cross-hair ring adjustment: a point at one end of the horizontal hair must stay on the hair as the telescope turns; if not, rotate the diaphragm ring.
  7. Collimation adjustment by the two-peg test. Pegs A and B about 100 m apart. With the level midway, both sights are equal, so their errors are equal and b₁ − a₁ is the true difference.
  8. Set up at D close to one peg (on the line produced) and read both again. With e the error per metre: (b₂ − DBe) − (a₂ − DAe) = b₁ − a₁, so e = [(b₂ − a₂) − (b₁ − a₁)] ÷ (DB − DA).
  9. Example: b₁ − a₁ = 2.115 − 1.625 = 0.490 m; from D (10 m beyond A, 110 m from B) b₂ − a₂ = 1.943 − 1.435 = 0.508 m; e = 0.018 ÷ 100 = 0.00018 per m, upward (about 37″). Correct reading on B = 1.943 − 110 × 0.00018 = 1.923 m.
  10. Adjust: on a dumpy level move the diaphragm until B reads 1.923 m with the bubble central; on a tilting level set 1.923 m with the tilting screw and re-centre the bubble with its capstan screws. Check: corrected readings 1.923 − 1.433 = 0.490 m, the true difference.
Understand State the characteristics of contours. Describe the direct and indirect methods of contouring and list the uses of a contour map. 13 marks

Points to cover. Contour, contour interval, horizontal equivalent; Six or more characteristics with valley and ridge; Direct method with the staff reading HI − H; Indirect: grid, cross-sections, radial, tacheometric; interpolation; Uses: sections, intervisibility, grade contour, catchment, reservoir, earthwork.

Model answer.

  1. A contour joins points of equal RL. The contour interval (CI) is the constant RL step between contours; the horizontal equivalent (HE) is the plan distance between two contours, HE = CI ÷ tan θ = CI × n for a gradient of 1 in n.
  2. Characteristics: every point on a contour has the same RL; every contour closes, on or off the map; close spacing means steep ground, wide spacing gentle ground, equal spacing a uniform slope; contours of different RL never cross except at an overhang and merge only at a vertical cliff; closed loops with higher values inside are hills, with lower values inside depressions; across a valley the V points uphill, across a ridge downhill; the steepest slope (water flow) is at right angles to the contours.
  3. Direct method: set up the level, take a BS on a BM and find HI. For a contour of RL H the staff must read HI − H. Example: HI 101.235 m → 1.235 m for the 100 m contour, 0.735 m for 100.5 m. The staffman moves until the reading matches; the point is pegged and fixed in plan. Accurate but slow; for small, open sites.
  4. Indirect method: spot levels are taken and contours are interpolated. Layouts: a grid of squares (block levelling, 5 to 20 m) on small gentle sites; cross-sections along a route; radial lines from a central station on hills; tacheometric points on rough ground.
  5. Interpolation by calculation: x = L (H − HA) ÷ (HB − HA). Example: A 102.6 m and B 105.1 m, 30 m apart; the 104 m contour is 30 × 1.4 ÷ 2.5 = 16.8 m from A. Estimation and the graphical method are quicker and rougher.
  6. Choice of CI: purpose, map scale, steepness of the ground, time and cost; 0.25 to 1 m on site plans, 10 to 20 m on 1:50 000 sheets.
  7. Uses: drawing a longitudinal or cross-section along any line; testing intervisibility; locating a route at a fixed gradient by stepping the HE with dividers (1 in 25 with a 2 m CI → 50 m, 20 mm at 1:2500); marking the catchment; finding the submerged area and capacity of a reservoir; estimating cut and fill; choosing sites for buildings, dams and bridges.
Understand Explain simple, differential, fly, check and profile levelling. What are the sources of error in levelling and the precautions against them? 13 marks

Points to cover. Simple: one set-up; differential: change points; Fly: quick BS/FS run; check: closes on a known BM; permissible error C√K; Profile: L-section and cross-sections, IS at pegs; Instrumental, natural and personal errors; Precautions.

Model answer.

  1. Simple levelling: both points seen from one set-up; difference = BS − FS.
  2. Differential (compound) levelling: used when points are far apart, at very different heights or hidden; the run is split by change points read first as FS then as BS. Check ΣBS − ΣFS = last RL − first RL.
  3. Fly levelling: a quick differential run with long, balanced sights and only BS and FS, to carry a benchmark's height to the site.
  4. Check levelling: a run made to test earlier work; it closes on the starting BM or on another BM. Closing error e = observed − known RL; permissible e = C√K mm (K in km), C set by the class of work. Example: run of 1.6 km closing +12 mm with 12√K allowed: limit 15.2 mm, accepted; corrections −3 mm per set-up.
  5. Profile levelling: RLs along a centre line at 20 m or 30 m pegs (intermediate sights) plotted as a longitudinal section with an exaggerated vertical scale; cross-sections at right angles at each peg. The formation line drawn on it gives depths of cut and fill.
  6. Instrumental errors: line of collimation not horizontal with the bubble central; sluggish bubble; loose tripod head; staff of wrong length or worn foot.
  7. Natural errors: curvature and refraction on long sights (0.0673 d²); wind shaking the staff; heat shimmer; settlement of the tripod or of a change point.
  8. Personal errors: bubble not central while reading; parallax; staff not vertical (reads too large); misreading metres or decimetres; booking in the wrong column or row.
  9. Precautions: sights under about 100 m and balanced; bubble checked at each reading; staff rocked and the least reading taken; change points on firm, sharp objects; readings booked at once and the arithmetic check done on site; every run closed on a known benchmark.
  10. Sketch for the answer: a longitudinal section with the ground line, the formation line, and cut shaded where the ground is above it and fill where it is below; with the HI method each peg's RL is HI − IS.
  11. Typical class of work: the constant C in C√K is a few millimetres for precise levelling and larger for ordinary engineering levelling; the specification of the job fixes it.

The level is then set up at D on the line AB produced, 12 m beyond B, and the readings are 1.540 m on A and 1.094 m on B. Find the true difference of level, the collimation error and its direction, and the readings on A and B for a correct line of sight from D. 13 marks

Points to cover. True difference from the midpoint; Apparent difference from D; e from the difference over DA − DB; Direction and angle; Corrected readings and check.

Model answer.

  1. From the midpoint the errors cancel: b₁ − a₁ = 1.326 − 1.784 = −0.458 m, so B is 0.458 m higher than A.
  2. From D: DA = 132 m, DB = 12 m. Apparent b₂ − a₂ = 1.094 − 1.540 = −0.446 m.
  3. Using (b₂ − 12e) − (a₂ − 132e) = −0.458: −0.446 + 120e = −0.458, so e = −0.012 ÷ 120 = −0.0001 m per metre.
  4. Negative e means the readings are too small: the line of sight is inclined downward. Angle = 0.0001 × 206 265 ≈ 21″.
  5. Correct reading on A = 1.540 − 132 × (−0.0001) = 1.540 + 0.0132 = 1.553 m. Correct reading on B = 1.094 + 12 × 0.0001 = 1.095 m.
  6. Check: 1.095 − 1.553 = −0.458 m, the true difference.
  7. Adjustment: with the level at D and the bubble central, raise the line of sight (diaphragm on a dumpy level, or tilting screw then bubble capstan screws on a tilting level) until A reads 1.553 m; repeat the test to confirm.

  4. Error in one far reading e = ½(1.735 − 1.525) = 0.105 m.

  6. Collimation error over 1.2 km = 0.105 − 0.097 = 0.008 m, readings too high, so the line of sight points upward by 0.008 ÷ 1200 × 206 265 ≈ 1.4″.
  7. Why the method works: near readings carry almost no error; the far reading carries +e from both sides, so the mean of the two apparent differences cancels e (curvature, refraction and collimation), provided refraction is the same for both far sights.
  8. Visible horizon from h = 9 m: d = √(9 ÷ 0.0673) = 3.855 × √9 = 11.6 km.
  9. Check: C at 11.6 km = 0.0673 × 11.6² = 9.06 m ≈ 9 m, the height of the station.
  10. Sketch: the valley with A and B; the level a few metres from A reading A (near) and B (far), then a few metres from B reading B (near) and A (far); the far sights both run 1.2 km.
  11. Sign check: B lower means the readings on B exceed those on A from both set-ups, as found (1.735 and 1.525 are both positive).
  12. Practical note: refraction varies with temperature near the ground, so the two sets should be taken at about the same time of day (ideally simultaneously with two levels) and several sets averaged.
  13. Comparison: without reciprocal levelling, the single set-up near A would give B lower by 1.735 m, an error of 0.105 m; applying C alone would leave 0.008 m of collimation error.
  14. Summary: RL of B 310.820 m; far-sight error 0.105 m, made up of 0.097 m curvature and refraction and 0.008 m collimation (about 1.4″ upward). The result does not depend on the instrument height at either bank, because each set-up's two readings are taken with the same line of sight.

Analyze A 1.2 km road is to be levelled for its longitudinal section. Heights must be brought from a GTS benchmark (RL 54.215 m) 0.6 km away to a site TBM, checked, and then used for the profile. A check run of six set-ups (2.25 km) gave the TBM as 57.880 m after three set-ups and closed on the GTS benchmark at 54.199 m. With a permitted error of 12√K mm, evaluate the run, correct the TBM, and justify the methods and booking you would use for the whole job. 15 marks

Points to cover. Fly levelling out, check levelling back; Closing error and permitted limit; Distribution of correction; Profile with HI booking; check run with rise and fall; Field precautions and plan.

Model answer.

  1. Closing error e = observed − known = 54.199 − 54.215 = −0.016 m (−16 mm).
  2. Permitted error = 12√2.25 = 12 × 1.5 = 18 mm. Since 16 mm < 18 mm, the run is accepted.
  3. Correction = +16 mm shared over six set-ups = +2.67 mm per set-up. The TBM was reached after three set-ups: correction = +3 × 2.67 = +8 mm. Corrected RL of TBM = 57.880 + 0.008 = 57.888 m.
  4. Method to the site: fly levelling (backsights and foresights only, balanced sights of 50 to 80 m) from the GTS BM to the TBM, then check levelling back to the BM or on to a second benchmark, so that field mistakes show as a misclosure; the arithmetic check alone cannot show them.
  5. Booking for the check run: rise and fall, because its check ΣBS − ΣFS = ΣRise − ΣFall = last − first covers every reading.
  6. Profile: pegs every 20 m or 30 m on the 1.2 km centre line and at changes of slope; from each set-up a BS on the TBM or a CP, intermediate sights on every peg in view, and an FS on a CP. Booking by the height of collimation method, because there are many intermediate sights; apply Check 1 and the full HI check. Close the profile run on the TBM or a second TBM.
  7. Cross-sections at each peg at set offsets to give the road width for earthwork.
  8. Precautions: test the level by the two-peg test before starting; keep sights under about 100 m and balanced; change points on firm plates; avoid midday shimmer; staff vertical and read without parallax.
  9. Result: TBM 57.888 m (accepted, error −16 mm within 18 mm). Had the error exceeded 18 mm, the run would be repeated, not adjusted.
  10. Check of the correction sum: the six set-up corrections (+2.67 mm each) add to +16 mm, cancelling the closing error exactly.
  11. If the set-ups were of very unequal length, the correction would be shared in proportion to distance instead: correction at a point = −e × (distance to it ÷ total distance).
  12. Record keeping: book the TBM's corrected RL (57.888 m) with its description and sketch, so later work starts from the adjusted value.

Glossary

Backsight
First reading of a set-up, on a point of known RL.
Benchmark
Fixed point of known RL: GTS, permanent, arbitrary or temporary.
Block levelling
Spot levels on a grid of squares for indirect contouring.
Change point
Point read as a foresight and then as a backsight.
Check levelling
Run that closes on a known benchmark to test earlier work.
Closing error
Observed minus known RL of the closing benchmark.
Contour interval
Constant RL difference between successive contours.
Datum
Level surface from which heights are measured, usually mean sea level.
Dead storage
Water below the lowest outlet of a reservoir.
Fly levelling
Quick run of backsights and foresights to carry a height.
Foresight
Last reading before the level is moved.
Formation level
Proposed finished level of a road, canal or drain.
Grade contour
Line on a map rising or falling at a fixed gradient across the contours.
Height of instrument
RL of the line of collimation for one set-up.
Horizontal equivalent
Plan distance between successive contours at a place.
Horizontal line
Straight line touching the level line at a point.
Intermediate sight
Any reading between the backsight and the foresight.
Inverted staff
Staff held upside down against a soffit; reading booked negative.
Level line
Line on a level surface; curved with the earth.
Line of collimation
Line through the cross-hairs and the optical centre of the objective.
Live storage
Water between the outlet sill and the full reservoir level.
Longitudinal section
Ground profile along a centre line plotted against chainage.
Mean sea level
Average height of the sea over a long period of tide readings; the national datum.
Parallax
Apparent movement of the cross-hair on the staff when the eye moves.
Reciprocal levelling
Two set-ups near each end of a long sight to cancel its errors.
Reduced level
Height of a point above the datum.
Sensitivity
Angle the line of sight tilts for one division of bubble movement.
Two-peg test
Test for the parallelism of the line of collimation and the bubble axis.
