7.1 GIS definition, components and history

A GIS, or Geographic Information System, is an organised set of five parts: hardware, software, data, people and methods. It captures, stores, manages, analyses and displays data that carries a location, so questions of the form "where is what, and what lies near it" get an answer. A printed map can only show; a decision needs two or more layers compared through location. GIS is used wherever place decides the answer: a metro alignment, a rural school site, a drought relief list, a water network.

You need: map scale and topographic sheets, and the idea of a remote sensing image.

Definition and the five parts

A GIS is a system that captures, stores, manages, analyses and displays spatially referenced data, data tied to a place on the earth, so that people can answer a question and take a decision. Each record carries a position: coordinates, or a named area such as a ward or a survey number.

The system has five parts. Drop one and it stops being a GIS.

  • Hardware: computers, servers, phones, GNSS receivers, scanners and plotters.
  • Software: the program that holds layers and runs overlay, buffer and query.
  • Data: the layers (points, lines, areas, images) with their attributes. Usually the costliest part of a project.
  • People: the surveyor who captures, the analyst who queries, the engineer who decides.
  • Methods (procedures): the written rules: datum and projection, scale, checking, acceptable error.

Four functions run through these parts. Input turns a paper map, a total station file, a GNSS track or a satellite image into a layer. Storage and management keep the layers in a database along with topology, the recorded rules that say which line meets which, and which area lies inside which. Manipulation and analysis combine layers by buffer, overlay and query (the operations are worked out in spatial analysis and overlay). Output gives the answer as a map, a table or a report.

Some syllabi write Geographical Information System, others Geospatial Information System; the meaning is the same. In an exam, stress the last word, System: the system includes people and written methods as well as the computer, which is why a mapping program alone is not a GIS.

History, Indian use, and the questions it answers

The first computer-based GIS was built in the 1960s for the Canada Land Inventory under Roger Tomlinson, to hold land capability, land use and ownership over large areas and to answer where land suited what. In the same decade a graphics laboratory at Harvard produced early computer mapping programs. These ran on mainframes, and one overlay could take hours.

Through the 1970s and 1980s GIS moved to minicomputers and then to desktop personal computers. Vector storage with topology became standard and commercial packages appeared. The 1990s made GNSS a routine input. After 2000 it moved to the web, then to the cloud, so a phone can send a position and receive an answer from a server.

In India, ISRO and NRSC run Bhuvan, a national geoportal that serves satellite imagery and thematic layers, and Survey of India supplies digital topographic data. State remote sensing centres apply the same layers to district planning. Everyday use is quieter: a cab app matching the nearest driver, a delivery route, a crop-insurance claim checked against a field boundary.

  • Drought management: rainfall, soil, crop and groundwater layers together mark the villages needing relief first.
  • Rural school planning: child population, walking distance and existing buildings overlay to fix a new site.
  • Building attributes: roof footprints and heights read from imagery into a layer with attributes.
  • Zonal planning: remote sensing land-use classes combined with administrative boundaries to fix zones.
  • Civil engineering: alignments, cut and fill, utility clashes and site progress tracked as layers.

Worked example

Question. A city engineer must list every street within 200 m of a proposed metro station that has no water main, and find how much new pipe that means. All layers are in metres on one projection. The street layer, after a distance query, gives for each street its shortest distance from the station and whether a main runs along it: Gandhi Road 0 m, main; Market Street 80 m, no main; Temple Street 150 m, main; Lake Road 190 m, no main; Mill Street 240 m, no main; School Lane 320 m, main. The streets are straight. Find the buffer area, the answer list and the length of new pipe inside the buffer.

  1. Input and check (data, methods): the station point, street lines and water mains must share one datum, projection and units (see map projections). Layers that do not line up give a wrong answer silently.
  2. Buffer the station at R = 200 m (analysis): area = πR² = π × 200² = 125 664 m² = 12.57 ha.
  3. Select the streets that cross the buffer: shortest distance less than 200 m. Gandhi Road (0), Market Street (80), Temple Street (150) and Lake Road (190) pass; Mill Street (240) and School Lane (320) do not.
  4. Remove the streets that carry a main (an attribute test on the selected set): Gandhi Road and Temple Street go. The answer list is Market Street and Lake Road.
  5. Length of a straight street inside a circle of radius R at distance d from the centre: 2√(R² − d²). Market Street: 2√(200² − 80²) = 2√33 600 = 366.6 m. Lake Road: 2√(200² − 190²) = 2√3900 = 124.9 m.
  6. New pipe inside the buffer = 366.6 + 124.9 = 491.5 m, about 492 m. The table and the map of these two streets are the output; the engineer, the people component, chooses the pipe route.

Answer. The buffer covers 125 664 m² (12.57 ha). Market Street and Lake Road are inside it with no main, needing about 492 m of new pipe (367 m + 125 m).

Exam traps

  • Writing 'GIS = Google Maps', 'GIS = a satellite image' or 'GIS = mapping software'. The examiner wants a system of five components and four functions, with analysis named as the difference.
  • Listing hardware and software only. People and methods are components too, and data is normally the largest cost.
  • Calling remote sensing a part of GIS, or GIS a part of remote sensing. Remote sensing is a data source; GIS is the system that analyses it.
  • Naming GPS or GNSS as a component. It is an input device and a source of positions, not one of the five parts.
  • History answers that give a date with no name, or a name with no work. Roger Tomlinson and the Canada Land Inventory is the pair expected.

7.2 Spatial and attribute data

Every GIS feature carries two kinds of information. Spatial data says where it is: coordinates in a stated system, plus shape, size, orientation and stored neighbour relations. Attribute data says what it is: a table of properties such as type, depth or ward. A key joins the two halves. This split lets a city ask 'which bore wells are deeper than 60 m' and also 'which wells lie inside ward 7'. The same design supports land records, pipeline maintenance, disaster maps and bus route planning.

You need: the five parts of a GIS from GIS components, and coordinates and map scales.

Spatial data: the where

Spatial data is the where half. It stores a feature's position in a stated coordinate system, such as easting and northing in metres. Easting is distance east of the origin; northing is distance north. Without the stated system and units the numbers mean nothing. Spatial data also stores shape, size and orientation: a point has no length, a line has length, a polygon has area and a perimeter.

The same object can change primitive with scale. A town is a point on a national map and a polygon on a district map. A road is a line at city scale and a polygon in a land acquisition plan. GIS distinguishes continuous fields from discrete objects. Elevation and temperature are fields: a value exists everywhere. Wells and parcels are objects: each has a boundary or a point location.

Topology is the stored set of neighbour relations. Adjacency means two polygons share a boundary. Containment means one feature lies inside another, such as a well inside ward 7. Connectivity means lines meet at nodes, as pipes do in a water network. Topology answers many location questions quickly and helps catch slivers after overlay. The physical storage of geometry is covered in raster and vector models.

Attribute data and measurement scales

Attribute data is the what half. It is a table: each record is one feature and each field is one property. Standard tables have features as rows and fields as columns, as in the figure. Field values follow a measurement scale, which fixes the arithmetic that is valid.

  • Nominal: labels only. Soil type, road class and well type allow equality and counting, not averaging.
  • Ordinal: ordered labels. Condition rank 1 to 5 allows sorting and a median, but gaps between ranks need not be equal.
  • Interval: equal gaps, no true zero. Temperature in °C allows addition, subtraction and a mean; 20 °C is not twice 10 °C.
  • Ratio: equal gaps and a true zero. Depth in m, length in m and population allow all arithmetic and ratios.

A number can still be nominal. Ward 7 is a label, not a quantity; averaging wards 7 and 9 to get 8 is meaningless. Depth 80 m is ratio because 0 m is a real absence of depth and 80 m is twice 40 m. Marking the scale prevents wrong operations before any query is written.

Keys, joins, queries and metadata

A primary key is a field with a unique value for each record. It links the attribute row to the geometry. A join merges two tables on a shared key. A one-to-one join matches one row to one row; a one-to-many join matches one row to many. A well table can join to a ward boundary table on the ward number. Many wells lie in one ward, so from wells to ward the join is many-to-one. The key must have the same type on both sides: text '07' will not join to numeric 7 until one side is cleaned.

An attribute query tests fields: 'all bore wells deeper than 60 m'. A spatial query tests geometry or location: 'all wells inside ward 7'. Both can run together, but they are different operations. Metadata records source, date, scale, accuracy and owner. A layer without metadata cannot be judged fit for a new use. See spatial analysis and overlay for combining such layers.

Worked example

Question. Four tube wells are surveyed. Well 1: easting 412 m, northing 1330 m, open, depth 40 m. Well 2: 415 m, 1332 m, open, 55 m. Well 3: 418 m, 1328 m, bore, 80 m. Well 4: 421 m, 1331 m, bore, 65 m. Ward 7 is the polygon with easting 400 to 416 m and northing 1320 to 1340 m; ward 9 lies east of it. (a) State the measurement scale of depth and of type. (b) Run the attribute query depth > 60 m. (c) Run the spatial query "inside ward 7" on the coordinates. (d) Store the result as a ward field and name the key and cardinality of a join to the ward boundary table.

  1. (a) Depth has a true zero (0 m means no depth) and equal metre steps, so it is a ratio scale: 80 m is twice 40 m. Type (open, bore) has no order, so it is nominal.
  2. (b) Test the depth field: Well 1, 40 m, false; Well 2, 55 m, false; Well 3, 80 m, true; Well 4, 65 m, true. Wells 3 and 4 pass. No coordinate was used.
  3. (c) Test the geometry: a point is inside the rectangle if 400 ≤ E ≤ 416 and 1320 ≤ N ≤ 1340. Well 1 (412, 1330): inside. Well 2 (415, 1332): inside. Well 3 (418, 1328): E = 418 > 416, outside. Well 4 (421, 1331): outside. Wells 1 and 2 are in ward 7. No attribute was used. (For a ward of any shape the GIS runs a point-in-polygon test instead of the two inequalities.)
  4. (d) Writing the result into each well record gives a ward field: 7, 7, 9, 9. The join key to the ward boundary table is the ward number. Many wells share one ward, so the join from wells to wards is many-to-one.

Answer. Depth is ratio and type nominal. The attribute query depth > 60 m returns Wells 3 and 4; the spatial query inside ward 7 returns Wells 1 and 2. The ward number is the key, and the wells-to-ward join is many-to-one.

Exam traps

  • Calling the easting and northing rows 'attributes'. They are the spatial half; the properties below them are the attribute half.
  • Joining ward '07' to numeric 7 without converting one side. Join keys must have the same type and a clean value.
  • Treating ward 7 as a ratio value and averaging ward numbers. Ward is a nominal label, not a quantity.
  • Mixing an attribute query with a spatial query. "Depth > 60 m" tests a field; "inside ward 7" tests coordinates against a boundary. Once the answer is stored as a ward field, "ward = 7" is an attribute query that copies an earlier spatial result.
  • Assuming a feature has one fixed primitive. A town or road can be a point, a line or a polygon at different scales.

7.3 Raster and vector data models

A GIS stores the same ground reality in one of two data models. The vector model records points, lines and polygons by coordinates; the raster model records a grid of cells, each with one value. The choice changes boundary sharpness, storage size, overlay speed and network analysis. A land parcel boundary, a pipeline and a well fit vector; an elevation surface or a satellite image fits raster. This chapter shows how each model is built, how storage is estimated, and when to choose one over the other.

You need: the split between geometry and attributes from spatial and attribute data, and image resolution from remote sensing.

Vector model: points, arcs, polygons

The vector model stores each feature as a geometric object with coordinates. A point is one coordinate pair (x, y); a well or a survey station is a point. A line, also called an arc, is an ordered chain of vertices joined by straight segments; a road or a pipeline is an arc. A polygon is a closed chain of vertices; a land parcel or a lake is a polygon. Vertices are the stored coordinate pairs used to build arcs and polygons.

Nodes are special points where arcs meet or end. The model stores topology, the recorded set of neighbours and connections, not just the drawing. An arc-node list names each arc and the nodes at its two ends. A polygon-arc list names each polygon and the arcs that bound it. Topology lets the GIS find shared boundaries, detect gaps and overlaps, and trace a network without rebuilding geometry.

Some syllabi call this the feature-based or object-based model. Its strengths are sharp boundaries, small storage for sparse data, exact lengths and areas, and good handling of networks and parcels. Its costs are complex overlay, poor fit for continuous fields, and topology maintenance after edits.

Raster model: one value per cell

The raster model divides area into rows and columns of square cells, also called pixels. It records an origin (coordinate of a corner), a cell size in ground units, the number of rows and columns, and one value per cell. Some syllabi call this the cell-based or grid-based model. The value can be elevation for a DEM (digital elevation model), a land-cover class code, or one band of a photograph. A 20 m cell covers 20 × 20 = 400 m², whatever value it carries.

Raster analysis is simple because cells line up. Overlay compares cell to cell, and map algebra applies one arithmetic or logical rule to every cell. Continuous surfaces fit naturally, and remote sensing images arrive as rasters. The costs are blocky boundaries, large storage, and a fixed resolution: a 30 m grid cannot show a 5 m road.

Bytes = rows × columns × bits per cell ÷ 8

rows and columns are the grid dimensions; bits per cell is the storage for one value (8 bits for a class code, 32 bits for floating elevation). For 1000 rows, 1000 columns and 8 bits: 1000 × 1000 × 8 ÷ 8 = 1 000 000 bytes, about 1 MB before compression.

A TIN (triangulated irregular network) is a vector surface of triangles. Rasterising converts vector to raster; vectorising converts raster to vector; both lose or invent detail at the cell size.

Choosing between them

Use vector when boundaries must be exact, features are sparse, or the question is about a network. Use raster when the variable changes continuously, when data come from an image, or when many layers must be combined cell by cell.

Worked example

Question. An elevation raster has 3 rows and 4 columns. The cell size is 20 m. Values in metres are row 1: 22, 25, 31, 34; row 2: 24, 29, 35, 38; row 3: 27, 33, 41, 45. (a) Find the area of one cell. (b) Find the total raster area. (c) Count cells at or above 35 m. (d) Find the area of that high ground. (e) State which model fits a well point, a pipeline line and the full elevation surface.

  1. One cell is a square 20 m by 20 m. Area = 20 × 20 = 400 m².
  2. The grid has 3 rows × 4 columns = 12 cells. Total area = 12 × 400 = 4 800 m².
  3. Read values ≥ 35: row 1 none; row 2 has 35 and 38; row 3 has 41 and 45. Count = 4 cells.
  4. High ground area = 4 × 400 = 1 600 m².
  5. A well is a point, a pipeline is an arc, so both fit vector. The elevation surface is continuous and gridded, so it fits raster.

Answer. One cell is 400 m², the grid is 4 800 m², four cells are at or above 35 m, high ground is 1 600 m², and point and line features fit vector while the elevation surface fits raster.

Worked example 2

Question. A straight 10 km pipeline is stored as a vector line with a vertex every 10 m, each vertex holding two 8-byte coordinates. The same 10 km × 10 km area is also stored as a raster of 5 m cells with 1 byte per cell to mark pipe or no pipe. Compare the storage.

  1. Vector: vertices = 10 000 ÷ 10 + 1 = 1001. Bytes = 1001 × 2 × 8 = 16 016 bytes, about 16 kB.
  2. Raster: cells = (10 000 ÷ 5) × (10 000 ÷ 5) = 2000 × 2000 = 4 000 000. Bytes = 4 000 000 × 8 ÷ 8 = 4 000 000 bytes = 4 MB.
  3. Ratio: 4 000 000 ÷ 16 016 = 250. The raster stores every empty cell; the vector stores only the line.
  4. Precision: the raster places the pipe only to within a 5 m cell (up to 2.5 m off its true line); the vector keeps the surveyed coordinates.

Answer. The vector line needs about 16 kB and the raster 4 MB, 250 times more, and the raster also blurs the pipe position to the 5 m cell.

Exam traps

  • Raster is not always a photograph. A DEM is a raster of numbers. Vector is not always more accurate; accuracy comes from the source survey and coordinate precision.
  • Raster area is cell count × cell area, with edge cells counted whole. Converting a raster class to polygons keeps that same stepped area; a smooth boundary drawn by eye over the class gives a different number.
  • Do not confuse cell size with map scale. A 20 m cell stays 20 m on the ground, but its printed size changes.
  • Do not forget topology maintenance: editing an arc changes both polygons that share it.
  • The storage formula uses bits per cell; divide by 8 before calling the result bytes.

7.4 Data input, editing and verification

A GIS layer is only as good as the data pushed into it. Data input turns a paper toposheet, a total station file, a satellite image or a spreadsheet into points, lines and polygons in the chosen model. Editing then removes the geometric and attribute errors that digitising always creates. Verification proves the layer against independent evidence, control-point residuals and a field sample, before any analysis is trusted. Real projects spend more time here than on analysis: a city corporation digitising property boundaries, or a highway authority importing total station files, will both live with these errors for years.

You need: the two data models from raster and vector models, spatial versus attribute data from spatial and attribute data, map scales, and total station and GNSS capture.

Sources, input methods and georeferencing

Every layer starts as something else: a paper toposheet, a total station or GNSS file, a satellite image, a scanned revenue record, a spreadsheet, or crowd-picked points. None of these is a clean layer. Input is the work of converting each source into features with coordinates and attributes.

  • Manual digitising: the sheet is fixed on a digitising table and the cursor is traced along each feature; the table sends coordinates to the software.
  • On-screen (heads-up) digitising: a scanned or georeferenced image sits behind the cursor and you click each vertex.
  • Scanning and vectorising: software thins scanned lines to centrelines, then you correct the result.
  • COGO entry: bearings, distances and offsets from survey records are typed in, and the software computes coordinates.
  • Direct import: total station and GNSS files carry point number, easting, northing, elevation and a feature code.
  • Image interpretation: features are identified and traced on a rectified satellite or aerial image.

A scanned sheet has pixel coordinates, not ground coordinates. Georeferencing fixes that. Pick control points whose ground coordinates are known, such as grid intersections on a toposheet, or points surveyed with GNSS. Fit a transformation from pixel position to easting and northing: an affine fit has six unknowns, so three points fix it exactly and four or more give a least-squares fit whose residuals show the error; a second-order fit needs at least six points and absorbs scanner distortion. At each control point compare the transformed position with the known position; the difference is the residual, in metres, split into easting and northing.

RMS = √( (Σeᵢ² + Σnᵢ²) ÷ n )

eᵢ, nᵢ are the easting and northing residuals at control point i, in metres; n is the number of control points; RMS is the combined root-mean-square error in metres. It equals √(s²ₑ + s²ₙ), where s²ₑ = Σeᵢ²/n and s²ₙ = Σnᵢ²/n are the mean squared residuals in each axis.

Compare the RMS with the tolerance the scale allows. A 1:50 000 toposheet supports about 12.5 m on the ground (0.25 mm at map scale × 50 000). If RMS exceeds tolerance, improve or re-survey the control and refit; do not simply accept the picture.

Editing, topology and attribute entry

Switch on snapping before digitising, not after. Within the snap tolerance a new vertex takes an existing node's coordinate exactly, so two lines share one point instead of two points a hair apart.

  • Add or delete vertices to reshape a line.
  • Split a line where the feature changes; merge two lines that are the same road or boundary.
  • Trim overshoots, lines that run past the line they should touch, and extend undershoots, which stop short.
  • Close polygons that fail to close on their last vertex.
  • Delete duplicate nodes: two vertices at one coordinate.
  • Remove slivers: thin polygons formed when two boundaries that should coincide are digitised a few metres apart. A sliver holds a strip that belongs to neither neighbour: where the two lines leave a gap, the strip is missing from both parcels; where they overlap, it is counted twice.

Topology is the stored record of which lines meet, which lines bound which polygon, and which polygons are adjacent. Build it, then test it. This finds what a tidy screen hides: dangles (an endpoint touching nothing), unclosed polygons, and crossings with no node. Repair, then rebuild before attributes go in.

Each attribute field has a type (integer, real, text or date) and a domain, the allowed values such as a fixed land-use code list. Each record needs a unique key so the feature and its record join one to one (see spatial and attribute data). Duplicate records break every count and every area total.

Verification, quality and manipulation

Verification asks whether the layer matches the ground, not whether it looks neat. Five checks cover it.

  • Positional accuracy: control-point residuals, plus independently surveyed check points not used in the fit.
  • Attribute accuracy: field-sample the parcels. 46 correct out of 50 sampled gives 92 %; always report the sample size.
  • Logical consistency: no dangles, no unclosed polygons, no polygon with two owners, no negative length.
  • Completeness: every source feature is present; count features per sheet against the source.
  • Edge matching: a road crossing a sheet boundary must have identical coordinates on both sheets. If it stops 30 m short of its neighbour, the join is wrong.

Metadata records source, date, scale, datum, projection, units, transformations applied, RMS and known gaps. Without it no later user can judge the layer (see map projections).

Manipulation reshapes the layer for use: transformation (reprojection), rubber sheeting (warping a layer to fit control points), edge matching, clipping to a boundary, format conversion between shapefile, GeoJSON and GeoPackage, and merging adjacent sheets. Record each operation and verify the layer again afterwards; overlay analysis is covered in spatial analysis and overlay.

Worked example

Question. A 1:50 000 toposheet is georeferenced on four control points. The residuals, written as (easting, northing) in metres, are: P1 (+2.0, +1.0), P2 (−1.0, +2.0), P3 (+1.0, −2.0), P4 (−2.0, −1.0). The project tolerance is 5 m. (a) Compute the RMS error. (b) State whether the layer is accepted. (c) Cleaning finds 7 dangles, 3 slivers and 2 duplicate nodes. State the repair order before attribute entry and why.

  1. Square the easting residuals: 2.0² = 4.0, (−1.0)² = 1.0, 1.0² = 1.0, (−2.0)² = 4.0. Sum = 10.0 m².
  2. Mean squared easting: s²ₑ = 10.0 ÷ 4 = 2.5 m².
  3. Square the northing residuals: 1.0² = 1.0, 2.0² = 4.0, (−2.0)² = 4.0, (−1.0)² = 1.0. Sum = 10.0 m², so s²ₙ = 10.0 ÷ 4 = 2.5 m².
  4. RMS = √(2.5 + 2.5) = √5 = 2.236 m ≈ 2.24 m. The same value comes from √((10.0 + 10.0) ÷ 4).
  5. 2.24 m is below the 5 m tolerance, and also below the 12.5 m the 1:50 000 scale allows (0.25 mm × 50 000). Point by point, every residual is √(2.0² + 1.0²) = 2.24 m, so no single corner is bad either; the layer is accepted.
  6. Repair order: delete the 2 duplicate nodes first, then fix the 7 dangles by extending undershoots and trimming overshoots, then delete the 3 slivers and rebuild topology. A sliver has no real owner, so attributes attached to it before repair would be counted twice or lost.

Answer. RMS error = 2.24 m, below the 5 m tolerance, so the layer is accepted; the 2 duplicate nodes, 7 dangles and 3 slivers are repaired and topology rebuilt before attributes are entered.

Exam traps

  • Judging quality by how tidy the screen looks. Dangles, slivers and duplicate nodes look harmless and still break area and overlay results.
  • Mixing digitising precision with positional accuracy: storing coordinates to four decimals does not move the point closer to the ground.
  • Quoting attribute accuracy without the sample size, or sampling only the parcels you already believe are correct.
  • Editing a layer but forgetting to rebuild topology, so adjacency and polygon areas stay stale.
  • Accepting a georeference on RMS alone when one corner has a 15 m residual. Look at the residuals point by point.

7.5 Map projections and coordinate systems

A GIS layer is only useful if its numbers land on the right spot. Latitude and longitude describe a place on a curved earth; easting and northing describe it on a flat grid. A projection converts one to the other, and a datum says which earth the numbers belong to. Every layer must state its datum, projection and units, or features from different sources will not line up. District GIS, cadastral maps and utility networks all depend on this.

You need: datums and coordinate systems from the foundations of surveying, and how coordinates ride with the geometry in raster and vector models.

Ellipsoid, geoid and datum

A position on the earth is a latitude (angle north or south of the equator) and a longitude (angle east or west of the prime meridian), both in degrees measured on an ellipsoid, a smooth mathematical surface close to the real earth. The geoid is a different surface: mean sea level, continued under the land. A datum fixes one chosen ellipsoid to the earth with a stated origin and orientation, so a coordinate names one place and no other. WGS84 is the global datum of GNSS. Older Indian topographic sheets use the Everest ellipsoid fixed by an older Indian datum; the newer open series maps use WGS84.

Degrees do not give flat-plane distance. One degree of longitude is about 111 km at the equator and shrinks to zero at the poles, so geometry in degrees is distorted. A projection turns latitude and longitude into easting and northing in metres on a plane. No projection keeps every property: it can keep shape (conformal), keep area (equal-area), keep one distance, or keep directions from one point. Pick by the job.

Projection families and the UTM grid

Cylindrical projections wrap a cylinder around the earth; conic projections use a cone; azimuthal projections use a plane touching at one point. Transverse Mercator is a cylinder turned sideways, touching along a meridian, so it suits a north-south strip of country.

UTM divides the world into 60 zones, each 6° of longitude wide, numbered from zone 1 at 180° W. Zone n runs from 180° + 6(n − 1) to 180° + 6n degrees. So zone number = floor((longitude + 180) ÷ 6) + 1. Each zone has a central meridian. There the scale factor is 0.9996: a 1000 m distance on the ellipsoid measures 999.6 m on the grid, 0.4 m short per kilometre. About 180 km either side the factor is 1, and further out it exceeds 1. A distance measured on the ground is first reduced to the ellipsoid (multiply by R ÷ (R + h) for a site h metres up) and then multiplied by the scale factor. A false easting of 500 000 m is added so eastings never go negative; a false northing of 10 000 000 m does the same for northings in the southern hemisphere. India falls roughly in zones 42 to 47.

Software reprojects on the fly, converting a layer into the display projection. That fixes the projection only. A wrong datum still shifts features, by a hundred metres or more between WGS84 and an old Indian datum. Every sheet's margin names its datum and projection, so read the margin rather than assume.

Worked example

Question. A point near Chennai is at 80° E, 13° N. Find (a) its UTM zone number, (b) the zone's central meridian, (c) how far west of that meridian the point lies, and (d) the grid length of a 1000 m line measured on the ground at 100 m above the ellipsoid near the central meridian. Take R = 6 378 000 m.

  1. (a) Zone number = floor((longitude + 180) ÷ 6) + 1 = floor((80 + 180) ÷ 6) + 1 = floor(43.33) + 1 = 44.
  2. (b) Zone 44 runs from −180 + 6 × 43 = 78° E to 84° E. Its central meridian is 78° + 3° = 81° E.
  3. (c) The point is 81° − 80° = 1° west of it. On the ground that is about 111.3 km × cos 13° = 111.3 × 0.974 = 108.4 km, so its easting is roughly 500 000 − 0.9996 × 108 400 ≈ 391 600 m.
  4. (d) Reduce to the ellipsoid: 1000 × 6 378 000 ÷ (6 378 000 + 100) = 999.984 m. Apply the scale factor: 999.984 × 0.9996 = 999.584 m.
  5. Check: the scale factor alone gives 999.600 m; the 100 m height takes off another 0.016 m. The false easting keeps every easting in the zone positive.

Answer. Zone 44, central meridian 81° E, point about 1° (108 km) west of it with easting near 391 600 m; the 1000 m ground line becomes 999.584 m on the grid.

Exam traps

  • Feeding degrees into software that expects metres, or the reverse.
  • Assuming two layers with the same numeric range share a datum; equal numbers are not equal places.
  • Believing on-the-fly reprojection repairs a wrong datum. It repairs the projection only.
  • Choosing a projection before deciding which property (area, shape or distance) the job needs.
  • Forgetting the false easting and northing. Without them, points west of the central meridian get negative eastings and southern-hemisphere points get negative northings.
  • Applying 0.9996 straight to a taped ground distance. Reduce it to the ellipsoid first, then apply the scale factor.

7.6 Spatial analysis and map overlay

Spatial analysis asks where features are close, overlap or score high. A GIS earns its cost by combining layers so one output answers a sitting question that no single layer answers. You select by location, buffer, overlay polygons, or run raster map algebra. Only a few universities teach this depth; most ask for the overlay idea and one weighted score. Uses include school siting, flood and landslide maps, drought planning, building-attribute extraction and construction monitoring.

You need: raster and vector storage from raster and vector models, clean topology from data input and editing, and a shared datum and projection from map projections.

Queries, buffers and vector overlay

An attribute query selects rows by values: wards with population > 5000. A spatial query selects by geometry: wards containing a school point. Point-in-polygon tests whether a point lies inside a polygon and copies the polygon's attributes (such as its ward number) onto the point; it can also summarise the points inside each polygon. A buffer is a polygon at a stated distance d around a feature; dissolve removes shared boundaries between overlapping buffers.

Vector overlay: clip cuts input to a boundary and keeps input attributes; erase removes the inside and keeps input attributes on the remainder; union keeps all areas and both attribute sets where present; intersect keeps only common area and both attribute sets; identity keeps all input geometry and adds second-layer attributes where covered; symmetric difference keeps area in either but not both, with each side's own attributes; spatial join transfers attributes by location without changing geometry.

Area rules: intersect area ≤ smaller input. Union area is not A + B; it is A + B − intersection. The intersection is never larger than the smaller input, and it equals the smaller input when that layer lies wholly inside the other. A flood layer intersected with a ward layer is no larger than the smaller of the two.

Raster map algebra and weighted overlay

Raster map algebra works on grids. Local computes each output cell from the cells at the same position in one or more input rasters. Focal uses a neighbourhood, such as 3 × 3, for slope. Zonal summarises cells inside polygons. Global uses the whole grid. Reclassification converts raw values into ordered scores, usually 1 to 5; state the class breaks, because a score of 5 is not comparable across layers otherwise. Weighted overlay gives S = Σ wi si, where si is the score of layer i, wi its weight, and Σ wi = 1; S is the suitability index. From a DEM, slope = arctan(√((∂z/∂x)² + (∂z/∂y)²)), where ∂z/∂x is the rise in elevation per metre eastwards and ∂z/∂y per metre northwards; on a grid of cell size c, ∂z/∂x = (zeast − zwest) ÷ 2c. This slope is in radians; multiply by 180/π for degrees. Slope in percent is the tangent of the angle times 100. Aspect is the compass azimuth of steepest descent: aspect = atan2(−∂z/∂x, −∂z/∂y), where the first argument is the east component and the second is the north component. atan2 returns an angle from −180° to 180°; add 360° if negative. It is undefined when both horizontal derivatives are zero. z is elevation.

Spatial correlation and uses

Spatial correlation asks whether two layers move together. High rainfall with low crop yield is negative; high ward population with high school admissions is positive. Plot the paired values and use Pearson or Spearman correlation to test whether two layers move together; use Moran's I to test whether one variable is clustered or dispersed in space (a bivariate form exists for two variables). Correlation is not cause. Uses: drought management (rainfall, soil, crop), rural school planning, building-attribute extraction, zonal planning with remote sensing, flood and landslide mapping, construction monitoring.

Worked example

Question. Three villages are candidates for a new school. Child population within 2 km has weight 0.6; distance from the nearest existing school has weight 0.4. Both are scored 1 to 5. Village A scores 5 and 2; B scores 4 and 4; C scores 2 and 5. Find each suitability index, the best village, and the area and 30 m cell count of a 2 km buffer.

  1. Weights: w1 = 0.6 for population, w2 = 0.4 for distance; w1 + w2 = 1.0.
  2. A: 0.6 × 5 + 0.4 × 2 = 3.0 + 0.8 = 3.8.
  3. B: 0.6 × 4 + 0.4 × 4 = 2.4 + 1.6 = 4.0.
  4. C: 0.6 × 2 + 0.4 × 5 = 1.2 + 2.0 = 3.2.
  5. Buffer area = π × d² = π × 2000² = 12 566 371 m² = 12.57 km².
  6. One 30 m cell = 30 × 30 = 900 m²; cells = 12 566 371 ÷ 900 = 13 962.6, about 13 963 cells.

Answer. S = 3.8 for A, 4.0 for B and 3.2 for C, so village B is chosen; the 2 km buffer is 12.57 km², about 13 963 cells of 30 m.

Worked example 2

Question. A 3 × 3 window of a DEM with 30 m cells has these neighbours of the centre cell: east 104 m, west 96 m, north 110 m, south 98 m. Find the slope in degrees and percent and the aspect.

  1. East-west gradient: ∂z/∂x = (104 − 96) ÷ (2 × 30) = 8 ÷ 60 = 0.1333.
  2. North-south gradient: ∂z/∂y = (110 − 98) ÷ 60 = 12 ÷ 60 = 0.2000.
  3. Gradient size: √(0.1333² + 0.2000²) = √(0.01778 + 0.04000) = √0.05778 = 0.2404.
  4. Slope = arctan 0.2404 = 13.5°; in percent, 0.2404 × 100 = 24.0 %.
  5. Aspect = atan2(−∂z/∂x, −∂z/∂y) = atan2(−0.1333, −0.2000) = −146.3°; add 360° to get 213.7°.
  6. Check: the ground rises to the north and east, so it must face (slope down towards) the south-west; 213.7° lies between 180° (south) and 270° (west), as expected.

Answer. Slope 13.5° (24.0 %), aspect 213.7°, facing south-west.

Exam traps

  • Adding union areas as A + B; the overlap must be subtracted.
  • Calling a buffer distance an area; for a point, buffer area is πd².
  • Using weights that do not sum to 1, or comparing score 5 across layers with unstated class breaks.
  • Mixing overlay attribute rules: clip keeps only input attributes, intersect keeps both.
  • Reading spatial correlation as proof of cause.
  • Reporting slope from the formula in radians, or aspect without turning a negative atan2 result into 0° to 360°. In the second worked example −146.3° must be written 213.7°.

Raster: grid of cells, one value per cell, cell size sets resolution; Vector: points, lines, polygons with coordinates plus attribute records; Storage: raster grows with area/cell size squared; vector compact for simple features; Precision: vector boundaries exact; raster boundaries stepped; Analysis: raster fast for continuous surfaces and map algebra; vector better for networks and boundaries; Topology and network analysis natural in vector; Examples: elevation, rainfall, land cover as raster; cadastral parcels, pipelines, roads as vector; Conversion is possible both ways with loss.

Model answer.

  1. Vector model: points (x, y), lines (ordered vertices) and polygons (closed rings), each with an attribute record; topology records which lines meet and which polygons share an edge.
  2. Raster model: a grid of square cells with an origin, a cell size, rows and columns, and one value per cell (elevation, class code or image band).
  3. Storage, with numbers. A 10 km pipeline with a vertex every 10 m needs 1001 vertices × 16 bytes = 16 016 bytes (16 kB) as a vector. A 5 m raster of the 10 km × 10 km area needs 2000 × 2000 cells × 1 byte = 4 000 000 bytes (4 MB), 250 times more, because every empty cell is stored. Raster storage grows with (area ÷ cell size²): halving the cell size multiplies it by 4.
  4. Precision. A vector boundary is as exact as its surveyed coordinates. A raster boundary follows whole cells: with 20 m cells a boundary can be up to 10 m (half a cell) from its true line, and an area is counted in 400 m² steps: on a 3 × 4 grid of 20 m cells, the four cells at or above 35 m give exactly 4 × 400 = 1600 m², whatever the true contour encloses.
  5. Analysis speed and type. Raster overlay is cell by cell, so map algebra (slope, suitability, flood depth) is simple and fast. Vector overlay must intersect lines and rebuild topology, which is heavier, but buffers, network tracing and shortest paths are natural in vector.
  6. Comparison:
    PointVectorRaster
    storagesmall for sparse featuresfixed by rows × columns × bits
    boundariessharpstepped, set by cell size

  8. Conversion. Rasterising draws lines into cells and loses exact position; vectorising traces cell edges and produces stepped or smoothed lines. Both lose detail at the cell size.

Understand Explain datum, projection and coordinate system. Why must every GIS layer record all three and its units? 10 marks

Points to cover. Datum: ellipsoid plus its placement, defines the reference frame, e.g. WGS84; Projection: equations mapping latitude/longitude to flat x, y, with unavoidable distortion of shape, area, distance or direction; Coordinate system: the numbered plane, e.g. UTM easting/northing in metres within a named zone; Same point can have different numbers under different datums, a shift of a hundred metres or more between some older Indian and global frames; Mismatched layers produce apparent offsets that look like digitising errors; Best practice: store datum, projection, units and zone as layer metadata; reproject before overlay.

Model answer.

  1. Datum. A reference ellipsoid (a smooth mathematical surface close to the earth) fixed to the earth with a stated origin and orientation, so latitude and longitude name one place. WGS84 is the global GNSS datum; older Indian sheets use the Everest ellipsoid with an older Indian datum.
  2. Projection. Equations that turn latitude and longitude on the curved ellipsoid into x and y on a plane. No projection keeps shape, area, distance and direction all at once; transverse Mercator (used by UTM) keeps shape.
  3. Coordinate system. The numbered plane that results, with origin, axes and units, such as UTM zone 44 N in metres: zone = floor((80 + 180) ÷ 6) + 1 = 44 for a point at 80° E; central meridian 81° E; scale factor 0.9996 there; false easting 500 000 m.
  4. Units. Degrees and metres are not interchangeable: 1° of longitude is about 111 km at the equator but 111.3 × cos 13° = 108.4 km near Chennai, so geometry in degrees is distorted.
  5. What goes wrong without them. A layer on a different datum sits a hundred metres or more off; at 1:50 000 the allowed error is only 0.25 mm × 50 000 = 12.5 m, so the shift is ten times the tolerance and looks like a digitising error. A layer in degrees loaded as metres lands near the origin of the grid; a missing zone puts features 6° (about 650 km) away.
  6. Best practice: record datum, projection, zone and units in each layer's metadata (a registry code such as EPSG:32644 says WGS84 / UTM zone 44N in one number), reproject layers to one system before overlay, and transform datums with stated parameters, because on-the-fly reprojection fixes the projection only.
  7. Worked conversion. A point at 80° E, 13° N lies 1° west of the central meridian, about 108.4 km on the ground. Multiplied by 0.9996 and subtracted from the false easting, it gives an easting of roughly 500 000 − 108 400 = 391 600 m, with the northing measured from the equator (about 1 437 km). The same point read in degrees on another layer would be (80, 13), nowhere near (391 600, 1 437 000), which is why the units must be stated.
  8. Check. Zone 44 covers 78° to 84° E, so 80° E is inside it; 0.9996 × 1000 = 999.6 m on the grid for a 1000 m ellipsoid distance at the central meridian.

Suppose 18 villages fall inside and 11 of them below 10 m: those 11 are ranked by population for evacuation, and the road layer routes relief around flooded links.
  3. Scenario testing: raise the flood level to 12 m and rerun; the list changes in minutes, which no paper map allows.
  4. Infrastructure case, road corridor. Reclassify terrain slope, land cost, settlements and forest to cost scores 1–5 and combine with weights, for example 0.3, 0.3, 0.2, 0.2. A cell with scores 2, 4, 1, 5 costs 0.6 + 1.2 + 0.2 + 1.0 = 3.0. A least-cost path across the cost surface gives the corridor; changing the forest weight shows how sensitive the alignment is.
  5. Where the value lies: layers combined through location, rules applied the same way everywhere, quick reruns, and conflicts (a corridor through a protected forest, a relief route over a breached bridge) found before field work.
  6. Limits: the output is no better than the inputs (an old DEM or census misleads); weights are judgement and can steer the answer; uncertainty is usually invisible on a clean map; a GIS cannot weigh social and legal issues it holds no layer for.
  7. Cost and benefit: one GIS run replaces many field visits for screening, but the short list still needs a field check.
  8. Evaluation: the statement is true when the data quality, the weights and the assumptions are stated with the map, and the model is used to compare options; without them the same software produces decorative maps that only look like decisions.
  9. Check. 80 + 3.14 = 83.1 km²; 0.3 + 0.3 + 0.2 + 0.2 = 1.0; 0.3 × 2 + 0.3 × 4 + 0.2 × 1 + 0.2 × 5 = 3.0.

Glossary

Accuracy
How close a measured value is to the true value, stated as a number in metres or as a class error rate.
Attribute table
A table of rows (features) and columns (attributes) linked to geometry by a key field.
Buffer
A polygon at a fixed distance from a feature, used for proximity queries and protected margins.
DEM
Digital Elevation Model, a raster of ground elevation values used for slope, contour and volume work.
Error propagation
The way input errors combine and grow through successive analysis steps so the output is less reliable than any single input.
Georeferencing
Assigning real-world coordinates to a scanned map, image or drawing so that it can sit correctly with other layers.
GIS
An organised system of hardware, software, data, people and methods for capturing, storing, analysing and displaying spatially referenced data.
GNSS
Global Navigation Satellite System, the family of satellite positioning systems that includes GPS, giving position on a datum such as WGS84.
Ground control point
A point with known coordinates, measured by total station or GNSS, used to georeference or to check imagery.
Ground sample distance
The ground size represented by one image pixel in a UAV or aerial photograph, usually given in centimetres.
Key field
A column whose value is unique and is used to join an attribute table to a geometry table.
Layer
One thematic set of spatial data, such as roads, within a GIS project.
Metadata
Data about data: source, date, datum, projection, units, scale and accuracy of a layer.
Orthomosaic
A geometrically corrected image mosaic in which every pixel is placed at its true map position.
Overlay analysis
Combining layers of the same area to obtain a new layer carrying the attributes of the inputs.
Precision
How closely repeated measurements agree with each other, independent of accuracy.
Rasterisation
Converting vector features to a grid of cells, with attribute value assigned to every cell.
Resolution
The smallest feature a dataset can show, expressed as raster cell size in metres or as map scale.
Sliver polygon
A thin false polygon created when two boundary lines that should coincide do not; its strip belongs to neither neighbour.
Spatial query
A question answered by testing geometry, e.g. list the plots within 100 m of a canal or the wells inside ward 7.
Structure from motion
A photogrammetric method that reconstructs camera positions and a 3D surface from many overlapping images.
Topology
The set of rules that record how features connect and share boundaries, e.g. adjacent polygons share one edge, not two.
Vectorisation
Converting a raster or scanned map into points, lines and polygons.
Zonal analysis
Computing statistics of one layer within the zones of another, e.g. mean rainfall per watershed.
