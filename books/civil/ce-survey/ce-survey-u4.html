4.1 Theodolite: parts and adjustments

A theodolite reads two angles at once: one horizontal, one vertical. It can do that only if three axes inside it stay square to each other. Those axes cannot be machined perfect and they drift with jolts and temperature, so the instrument carries screws and bubbles to restore the geometry. Temporary adjustments are made at every station and take a few minutes. Permanent adjustments are made rarely, by a trained hand, and only when a field test fails.

You need: measurement of lengths and angles, and the least count of a vernier, from Unit 1, Measurements, errors, accuracy and precision.

Three axes, three right angles

A theodolite measures a horizontal angle between two directions and a vertical angle above or below the horizon. It does this with two graduated circles. The horizontal circle lies in a horizontal plane and is read through verniers. The vertical circle turns with the telescope and gives the elevation.

The instrument is built around three axes. The vertical axis is the spindle inside the tribrach; levelling makes it truly vertical, along gravity. The horizontal axis, also called the trunnion axis, carries the telescope, so the telescope swings up and down about it. The line of collimation is the straight line from the centre of the objective through the crossing point of the cross hairs to the target.

Adjustment means restoring three right angles and two settings that go with them: the line of collimation square to the horizontal axis, the horizontal axis square to the vertical axis, and the plate level axis square to the vertical axis, with the altitude level parallel to the line of collimation and the vertical-circle index at zero. When all hold, a vertical angle means what it claims.

Face left is the position with the vertical circle on the observer's left when the telescope points forward. Face right is the reverse. Transiting is turning the telescope end for end about the horizontal axis, through 180°. Changing face means transiting and then swinging the upper part 180° about the vertical axis until the same target is sighted again; the vertical circle is now on the other side. A swing alone does not change face. On a correct instrument, face left and face right readings of the same target differ by exactly 180°.

Parts and what each one does

The moving parts sit on two concentric spindles. The inner spindle carries the upper plate and the telescope; the outer spindle carries the lower plate and the horizontal circle, and is fixed to the tribrach.

LC = (value of one main-scale division) ÷ (number of vernier divisions)

LC is the least count, the smallest angle the vernier can read. On a common 20″ theodolite the horizontal circle is graduated to 20′ and the vernier has 60 divisions, so LC = 20′ ÷ 60 = 1200″ ÷ 60 = 20″. One minute is 60 seconds of arc.

Temporary adjustment: at every station

Temporary adjustment is done at every setup. It makes the vertical axis vertical, puts the instrument over the mark and removes parallax. Nothing is corrected permanently.

  1. Stand the tripod over the station mark, legs firm and about shoulder width, head roughly level.
  2. Look through the optical plummet and slide the instrument on the tribrach head until the mark sits under the cross.
  3. Centre the plate level bubble with two foot screws, turning both thumbs towards each other or both away from each other, choosing the direction that moves the bubble to the centre.
  4. Turn the instrument through 90° and centre the bubble with the third foot screw. Repeat in the first position until the bubble stays central all round.
  5. Focus the eyepiece on the cross hairs against a bright sky until the hairs are sharp.
  6. Sight the target and turn the focusing screw until the image is sharp.
  7. Move your eye up and down behind the eyepiece. If the image slides over the hairs, there is parallax; refocus until it stops.

Check setting over again after levelling; the two disturb each other slightly. On sloping ground keep one leg downhill. Centre the bubble with the foot screws only, never by shifting the legs.

Permanent adjustment: six tests, six corrections

Permanent adjustment is done rarely and in a fixed order: plate level, diaphragm, telescope level, collimation, spindle, vertical-circle index. Each test assumes the ones before it hold, so a fault left in the first test spoils every test after it.

The plate level test runs by transiting. If the bubble wanders, the level axis and the vertical axis are not square, and the drift is twice the error. The collimation test compares face left and face right on a distant, well-defined point; the mean is free of the error, so field work on a suspect instrument is booked on both faces.

Worked example

Question. A 20″ vernier theodolite is levelled on a station and the plate level bubble is centred. The telescope is transited 180° about the horizontal axis and the bubble stands 4 divisions off centre. One division is 30″. (i) Find the error between the plate level axis and the vertical axis. (ii) State the correction.

  1. Transiting reverses the level relative to the vertical axis, so the bubble shows twice the axis error.
  2. Bubble drift = 4 × 30″ = 120″.
  3. Axis error = 120″ ÷ 2 = 60″, that is 2 divisions.
  4. Correction: bring the bubble back 2 divisions (60″) with the capstan screws of the plate level. This tilts the level tube, not the instrument.
  5. Re-level with the foot screws, transit again, and repeat until the bubble stays centred in both positions.

Answer. The plate level axis is out of square by 60″ (2 divisions); bring the bubble back 2 divisions with the plate level capstan screws, re-level and repeat until it stays centred.

Exam traps

  • Correcting the whole bubble drift. Transiting shows twice the axis error, so 4 divisions off means a 2-division error.
  • Mixing temporary and permanent adjustment. Temporary is every setup; permanent is rare and follows a failing test.
  • Confusing transiting with changing face. Transiting turns the telescope end for end about the horizontal axis; changing face is transiting plus swinging the upper part 180° to re-sight the target on the other face.
  • Expecting one test to reveal everything. Each test checks one axis relation, in order.
  • Unit slips: 20″ is twenty seconds of arc, not minutes or millimetres, and 1′ = 60″.

4.2 Measurement of horizontal and vertical angles

Horizontal and vertical angles are the two numbers a theodolite exists to produce. A horizontal angle is the turn between two sight lines projected on a horizontal plane. It fixes direction in a traverse, a triangulation net or a building layout. A vertical angle is the rise or fall of a sight line above or below the horizontal; with a distance it gives a height. The instrument reads a graduated circle through verniers. Both faces are observed and averaged, so instrument errors cancel instead of entering the answer.

You need Chapter 4.1 for the axes, clamps, tangent screws and the meaning of face left and face right.

Horizontal angle by the ordinary method

A horizontal angle at station O between targets A and B is the angle between the vertical planes O-A and O-B. It is measured in the horizontal plane. The horizontal circle is graduated clockwise in degrees. Two verniers, A and B, sit 180° apart. Reading both and averaging removes eccentricity of the centres.

  1. Set up the theodolite over O, level it, focus the telescope and eyepiece. Work face left.
  2. Clamp the lower plate. Release the upper clamp, turn to A, bisect, and clamp the upper clamp. Set the circle near zero with the upper tangent screw.
  3. Read both verniers. For each vernier, read the degrees on the main scale at the mark just before that vernier's zero, and read minutes and seconds from the vernier divisions. Do this for A and for B separately. Then reduce B by 180° and average it with A.
  4. Release the upper clamp, turn clockwise to B, bisect, and read both verniers. Angle AOB = mean reading at B − mean reading at A. If A was set to zero, it is the mean reading at B.
  5. Transit the telescope, set face right, and repeat. The mean of the face-left and face-right angles is the result. The mean of the faces is free of collimation error. Half the difference between the face-left and face-right angles is the error collimation caused in this angle, about c(sec h₂ − sec h₁), where c is the instrument's collimation error and h₁, h₂ are the altitudes of the two sights; it measures the instrument's collimation error only when the two sights are steep and differ in altitude.

θ = ½[A + (B ± 180°)]

θ is the mean horizontal reading in degrees; A and B are the two vernier readings in degrees. Use +180° when B is less than 180°, and −180° when B is greater.

Repetition and the direction method

Repetition turns the same angle n times on the circle without resetting. Each turn uses a different part of the graduation, so a graduation error at one place tends to cancel. The final reading divided by n gives the angle.

  1. Set zero on A as before. Turn to B and read R1.
  2. Clamp the upper plate to the lower plate. Release the lower clamp, turn the whole instrument back to A. The reading does not change.
  3. Clamp the lower plate. Release the upper clamp, turn to B again. The reading is now R2 = 2θ. Repeat n times to get Rn.
  4. Compute θ = (Rn − R0)/n. R0 is the starting reading; if R0 = 0, θ = Rn/n.

θ = (Rn − R0) / n

θ is the required angle; Rn is the final reading after n repetitions; R0 is the initial reading; n is the number of repetitions.

The direction method, also called reiteration, is used when several directions are observed from one station. Set zero on a reference direction. Observe each direction in clockwise order on both faces. Close the horizon by sighting the reference again. The final reading should be 360° from the start. Any difference e is the closing error. Apply the correction −e/m cumulatively to the observed directions, starting with 0 at the reference and adding −e/m at each step, so the closing sight of the reference gets −e. Do not subtract a flat −e/m from every direction.

correction at k-th direction = −k e / m

e is the closing error in seconds, final reading minus 360°; m is the number of observed directions including the reference; k is the direction count from the first reference. k = 0 at the reference, k = 1 at the next observed direction, and k = m at the closing sight of the reference. The correction at k = m is −e.

Vertical angles and the errors that remain

A vertical angle is measured in a vertical plane. It is the angle between the line of sight and the horizontal (altitude), or between the line of sight and the zenith (zenith distance). Elevation is above the horizontal and positive. Depression is below and negative.

The vertical circle is fixed to the telescope. Its zero may be at the horizon or at the zenith. On an altitude graduation, 0° is horizontal and 90° is zenith. On a zenith-distance graduation, 0° is zenith and 90° is horizontal. To find the graduation of the vertical circle, sight a nearly horizontal object on face left. If it reads about 0° the circle is graduated as altitude; if it reads about 90° it is graduated as zenith distance.

α = 90° − z

α is altitude, positive above the horizon and negative below; z is zenith distance in degrees. z = 90 gives α = 0; z = 0 gives α = +90°; z = 120° gives α = −30°.

Sight the target on face left and read the vertical circle. Transit, set face right, sight the same target, and read again. Reduce each reading to the same quadrant. The mean is α = ½(α_L + α_R). The half difference is the index error. A depression must carry a minus sign; a zenith reading above 90° already tells you it is a depression. A vertical angle with a horizontal distance gives a height; see Chapter 4.3.

Most angle errors are removed by both faces. The common ones are:

  • Eccentricity of centres: the centre of the graduated horizontal circle does not coincide with the vertical axis about which the instrument turns, so each vernier reads a slightly wrong angle. Reading both verniers, which sit 180° apart, and averaging removes it.
  • Collimation: the line of sight is not perpendicular to the horizontal axis. The mean of faces removes it.
  • Vertical axis not truly vertical: the whole circle is tilted, so angles between steep sights are wrong and the error does not reverse when the telescope is transited. The mean of faces does not remove it. Level the instrument carefully, check the plate and altitude bubbles, and keep sights short and near the horizontal.
  • Graduation error: circle divisions are imperfect. Repetition and changing circle positions reduce it.
  • Bisection and refraction: a blurred target or a sight close to hot ground bends the line. Use short sights, set targets high, avoid midday shimmer.
  • Index error of the vertical circle: zero is off the true horizon or zenith. The mean of faces removes it.

Worked example

Question. A horizontal angle AOB is measured by the repetition method with a 20″ theodolite, starting from a zero reading. After 4 repetitions the vernier reads 148°24′40″. Face left gives 37°06′20″ and face right gives 37°06′00″. Find the angle by repetition, the mean of faces, and the half difference.

  1. Convert the total reading: 148°24′40″ = 148×3600 + 24×60 + 40 = 534280″.
  2. Divide by the number of repetitions: θ = 534280″ / 4 = 133570″ = 37°06′10″.
  3. Convert face readings: face left = 37°06′20″ = 133580″; face right = 37°06′00″ = 133560″.
  4. Mean of faces = (133580″ + 133560″) / 2 = 133570″ = 37°06′10″.
  5. Half difference = (133580″ − 133560″) / 2 = 10″. This is the error collimation caused in this particular angle; it measures the instrument's collimation error only when the two sights are steep and differ in altitude.

Answer. Angle AOB = 37°06′10″ (133570″); the face mean is also 37°06′10″, and the half difference is 10″, the collimation effect in this angle.

Exam traps

  • Face right read at face value: the face-right reading of a direction is about 180° from its face-left reading. Reduce the face-right mean reading by 180° (add 180° if it is less than 180°) and only then average it with the face-left value. Reducing vernier B by 180° is a separate rule, used within each face.
  • A depression entered as positive: the sign carries into trigonometric levelling and reverses the height.
  • Using a zenith distance as an altitude: α = 90° − z, not z itself.
  • Forgetting R0 in repetition: θ = (Rn − R0)/n, not Rn/n, when the start is not zero.
  • Averaging A and B without the 180° correction: the mean is meaningless.

4.3 Trigonometric levelling: heights and distances

Trigonometric levelling finds a height from a vertical angle and a horizontal distance. A theodolite gives the angle; a chain, tape or EDM gives the distance. The base of the object need not be touched. Surveyors use it for a gopuram, a chimney, a bridge pier or a hill top. Only a few universities teach this chapter.

You need Chapters 4.1 and 4.2 for setting up and reading vertical angles on both faces, and Unit 3 for curvature, refraction and reduced levels.

One angle, one distance

Set the instrument at A. Sight the top T. D is the horizontal distance from A to the vertical line through T, in metres. α is the vertical angle, positive for elevation and negative for depression. hi is the instrument height at A (m); ht is the target height above the point sighted (m).

ΔH = D tan α

ΔH is the height of T above the instrument axis (m); D is the horizontal distance (m); α is the vertical angle, signed in degrees.

ΔH gives height above the instrument axis. Then RL_T = RL_A + hi + D tan α − ht. For an accessible base, D is measured to the base. If the base is level with A and the target is the top, object height is D tan α + hi. Never use a slant distance in D tan α.

Base inaccessible and corrections

Single plane: P, Q and the object lie in one vertical plane. With Q farther and instrument axes at the same level, H = D tan α₁ = (D+s) tan α₂, so D = s tan α₂/(tan α₁ − tan α₂). D is from P to the object (m), s is PQ (m), α₁ and α₂ are vertical angles from P and Q. If the axes differ by e = axis at Q − axis at P (m), D = (s tan α₂ + e)/(tan α₁ − tan α₂). Then find H and add instrument height.

Double plane: P and Q are off the object's vertical plane. Measure horizontal angle θ₁ at P between PQ and the object, and θ₂ at Q between QP and the object. Then D_P = s sin θ₂/sin(θ₁+θ₂) and D_Q = s sin θ₁/sin(θ₁+θ₂). Compute two heights and average.

For long sights apply the combined curvature and refraction correction C = 0.0673 D² m, with D in km. Unit 3 derives it and gives its sign. At 100 m it is 0.0007 m and is ignored in building work.

Worked example

Question. Two instrument stations P and Q are 60.000 m apart, in the same vertical plane as the top of a tower, with Q farther away. The vertical angles to the top are 12°30′ from P and 9°18′ from Q. The instrument height is 1.45 m at both stations, and the target is the top itself. Find the horizontal distance from P and the height of the top above the ground at P. tan 12°30′ = 0.22169 and tan 9°18′ = 0.16376.

  1. Difference of tangents = 0.22169 − 0.16376 = 0.05793.
  2. D = s tan α₂/(tan α₁ − tan α₂) = 60.000 × 0.16376 / 0.05793 = 169.6 m.
  3. Height above the instrument axis = D tan α₁ = 169.6 × 0.22169 = 37.60 m.
  4. The target is the top, so ht = 0.
  5. Height above ground at P = 37.60 + 1.45 = 39.05 m.

Answer. The horizontal distance from P is 169.6 m and the top is 39.05 m above the ground at P.

Exam traps

  • Using a slant distance in D tan α. D is horizontal, not along the line of sight.
  • Stopping at D tan α. That is height above the instrument axis; add hi and subtract ht.
  • Taking a depression angle as positive. Use α negative, or subtract D tan |α|.
  • In the single-plane formula, swapping α₁ and α₂. D is from the nearer station and the numerator uses the farther angle.
  • Putting D in metres into 0.0673 D². That correction uses D in kilometres.

4.4 Tacheometry: stadia, subtense and tangential methods

Tacheometry measures a distance from an angle, not from a chain. A staff or bar of known length stands at the far station; the theodolite measures the small angle it subtends, and that angle fixes the horizontal distance and the elevation in the same sighting. The method is fast enough for contouring and for detail across a river, a railway line or a crowded Chennai street where a tape cannot go. Stadia, subtense and tangential are three ways to get the known length and the angle.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for vertical angles, Chapter 4.1 for the instrument, Unit 3 for reduced levels, and Unit 1 for the idea of a subtended angle and electronic distance measurement.

The angle carries the distance

A length held at the far station subtends an angle at the instrument. For a small angle, the length equals distance times angle in radians, so distance is length divided by angle. Tacheometry uses this one relation to avoid chaining. A graduated staff is held at the staff station, and the telescope reads the length between two marks; that length is the staff intercept s, in metres.

The stadia system adds two horizontal hairs to the telescope, one above and one below the centre hair. The separation of these outer hairs is the stadia interval i, in metres at the diaphragm. With f the focal length of the object glass and d the distance from the object glass to the vertical axis, the lens geometry gives D = K s + C. K = f/i is the stadia constant, usually 100. C = f + d is the additive constant, usually between 0 and 0.3 m. An anallatic lens makes C zero, so the field reduction is only D = 100 s.

D = K s + C

D is the horizontal distance from the instrument axis to the staff, in metres; K = f/i is the stadia constant; s is the staff intercept, in metres; C = f + d is the additive constant, in metres.

  • Fixed hairs: the outer hairs are permanent, and K and C are instrument constants.
  • Movable hairs: one hair is moved until the intercept is a round number; the stadia constant is then read from a scale.

Inclined line of sight

When the line of sight is inclined at a vertical angle θ above the horizontal, the staff is still held vertical. The intercept s on the staff is not perpendicular to the line of sight. Its component perpendicular to the sight is s cos θ. The inclined distance from the instrument to the staff is therefore K(s cos θ) + C. Projecting that distance on the horizontal and vertical gives two equations.

D = K s cos²θ + C cosθ

D is horizontal distance in metres; θ is the vertical angle; s is the staff intercept on a vertical staff, in metres; K and C are the stadia constants.

V = K s sinθ cosθ + C sinθ

V is the vertical component from the instrument axis to the mid hair, in metres, positive upward; other symbols as above.

The cos²θ is the common mistake. The first cosθ turns the vertical staff intercept into a length perpendicular to the sight; the second cosθ turns the inclined distance into a horizontal distance. If the staff is held normal to the line of sight, s is already perpendicular, so D = (K s + C) cosθ and V = (K s + C) sinθ. The normal staff gets one cosθ only. Holding a staff normal is awkward on a sloping sight, so the vertical staff is standard.

The method is used for contouring and for detail that cannot be chained: across a river, a railway yard, or a crowded street. One sighting gives both D and V, and the reduced level follows with the mid-hair reading.

Subtense and tangential methods

A subtense bar is a horizontal bar of fixed length s, commonly 2.000 m, set at the far station. The theodolite at the instrument station measures the whole angle θ between the two ends of the bar. The triangle gives D = s / (2 tan(θ/2)). For small θ, tan(θ/2) ≈ θ/2, so D ≈ s/θ, with θ in radians. The angle is the whole angle subtended by the bar, not half. A two-instrument version puts a short base at the instrument station; two theodolites at its ends sight the same distant target, and the triangle gives the distance.

The tangential method uses two vertical angles to two targets on the same vertical line. Let α₁ be the angle to the upper target, α₂ to the lower, and s the vertical distance between the targets. The horizontal distance is D = s / (tan α₁ − tan α₂). Use algebraic signs when one angle is a depression. The method suits a long sight where the staff reading is unreliable because of refraction or because the target is on a boat or a bridge.

D = s / (tan α₁ − tan α₂)

D is horizontal distance in metres; s is the vertical separation of the two targets in metres; α₁ and α₂ are the vertical angles to the upper and lower targets, with signs.

A total station measures D directly by EDM, so tacheometry is now a check or a fallback. See Unit 5 for that hardware.

4.5 Triangulation and trilateration

A control frame fixes a few points so that every later survey can hang on them. In triangulation you measure one side with great care, observe the angles of a chain of triangles, and compute the other sides by the sine rule. Trilateration measures all three sides by electronic distance measurement and finds the angles by the cosine rule. Both give coordinates over hills, rivers and city blocks where a traverse is slow. Road alignments, canal routes and mapping projects start from such a frame.

You need Chapter 4.1 and Chapter 4.2 for angles on both faces, and the azimuth and coordinate ideas from Unit 2.

One measured side, many computed sides

A triangulation net is a chain of triangles joined side to side. One side, the base AB, is measured with great care. All angles are observed, typically to about 20″. In triangle ABC the sine rule gives the other sides:

a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C

a = BC is the side opposite angle A; b = AC is opposite B; c = AB is opposite C; A, B, C are the interior angles in degrees.

Thus BC = AB sin A / sin C and AC = AB sin B / sin C. The computed side and its azimuth carry the coordinates forward: ΔE = d sin α and ΔN = d cos α, where d is the side, α its azimuth, and ΔE, ΔN are the easting and northing differences.

The base fixes scale; the angles fix shape. A base error scales every computed length, so the base gets the best instrument and the most corrections. Angles are observed on both faces with a theodolite, as in Chapter 4.2.

Control is graded. Primary or first-order triangulation has the highest accuracy and longest sides. Secondary or second-order is shorter and looser. Tertiary or third-order is shorter still. A base net is a strong figure around the base; the main chain carries the frame forward; subsidiary triangles fill gaps; a satellite or eccentric station is occupied off-centre and its angles are reduced to the centre.

Shape, layout and field work

A well-conditioned triangle has no angle below 30° or above 120°. The best shape is near 60° in all three angles. Near 60° the sine changes slowly for a given angle error, so the computed side is least sensitive. A thin triangle with a very acute angle is weak: its small sine magnifies angle error. Strength of figure compares layouts by how much angle error grows into side error; the lower the growth, the stronger the figure.

Reconnaissance comes first. Walk the area, choose stations that are intervisible, mark them permanently, and avoid lines that graze the ground or pass too close to buildings. A station signal must be visible from both ends. Its height must clear grass, traffic and heat shimmer. A tower or scaffold raises the instrument or signal above obstruction. Refraction bends the line of sight near the ground; keep the line well above the surface and observe on both faces.

The usual layout is a base net, a main chain of triangles, subsidiary triangles and, where a station cannot be occupied, a satellite station. A check base at the far end, or a closing azimuth, reveals accumulated error. The check is part of the design, not an afterthought.

Base measurement, trilateration and coordinates

The base is measured by tape or EDM. A tape needs standardisation, temperature, tension, sag and slope corrections. An EDM needs atmospheric and prism corrections. The corrected length is reduced to mean sea level:

L₀ = L · R/(R + H) ≈ L(1 − H/R)

L is the measured length at height H above mean sea level; R is the earth's radius, about 6,371,000 m; L₀ is the length at mean sea level; H and R use the same unit.

Trilateration measures all three sides of each triangle by EDM. The angles follow from the cosine rule:

cos A = (b² + c² − a²)/(2bc)

a, b, c are the sides opposite angles A, B, C respectively.

No horizontal angle is observed. The distances must be accurate and reduced to the same surface or projection. Modern total stations make trilateration fast; triangulation remains useful where long sights and angles are easier than long distance measurement.

Coordinates start from the base. If AB has length c and azimuth α_AB, then E_B = E_A + c sin α_AB and N_B = N_A + c cos α_AB. The angle at A gives the azimuth of AC, and the computed side AC gives C. A closing side or azimuth checks the whole chain.

Worked example

Question. In a triangulation triangle ABC, AB = 500.000 m is the base. The observed angles are A = 62°18′ and B = 57°42′. Find C, BC and AC. Use sin 62°18′ = 0.88539, sin 57°42′ = 0.84526 and sin 60°00′ = 0.86603.

  1. C = 180° − (62°18′ + 57°42′) = 180° − 120°00′ = 60°00′.
  2. Write the sine rule: BC/sin A = AB/sin C, so BC = AB sin A / sin C.
  3. BC = 500.000 × 0.88539 / 0.86603 = 511.18 m.
  4. AC/sin B = AB/sin C, so AC = AB sin B / sin C.
  5. AC = 500.000 × 0.84526 / 0.86603 = 488.02 m.
  6. BC + AC = 511.18 + 488.02 = 999.20 m.

Answer. So C = 60°00′, BC = 511.18 m and AC = 488.02 m; the sum of the two computed sides is 999.20 m.

Exam traps

  • In the sine rule, pair each side with the angle opposite it. BC goes with A, AC with B, AB with C. Pairing BC with B is the commonest error.
  • A thin triangle is weak, not strong. An angle below 30° makes the computed opposite side very sensitive to angle error.
  • Forget the check base and closing azimuth and a small angle mistake passes unnoticed. Design one check into every chain.
  • Mix units in the mean-sea-level reduction and the answer shifts. Use R = 6,371,000 m, not 6371 km with H in metres.
  • In trilateration, apply the cosine rule to the angle between the two sides you name in the denominator. Swapping a and b gives the wrong angle.

4.6 Horizontal and vertical control

A control survey fixes a small number of points to a much higher accuracy than the detail survey that follows. Horizontal control gives X and Y; vertical control gives Z. Everything else — a topographic sheet, a road centre line, a pipeline, a drone map — hangs on these points. An error in the detail stays local, but an error in control spreads into all later work. So control is observed first, checked, adjusted, and only then used as the frame for detail.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for horizontal and vertical angles, Chapter 4.5 for triangulation and trilateration, Unit 3 for differential levelling and booking, and Chapter 4.3 for trigonometric levelling.

Control is a sparse frame of known points

A control point is a point on the ground whose position is known to a stated accuracy and which is marked so that it can be found again. Its position is three numbers: X and Y in the chosen plane datum, and Z, the reduced level above the datum. A control network is a set of such points, tied together by observations and adjusted as one piece.

The detail survey that follows — compound walls, kerbs, trees, contours — is hung on the control. That is why control comes first. An error in a detail point stays at that one spot; an error in a control point is carried into every detail point fixed from it. Two parties working from the same control also join without a seam, which matters when a road surveyed by one consultant meets a drain surveyed by another.

Control is sparse on purpose: few points, each observed longer, with better instruments and repeated measurements. Density rises as the map gets finer. A 1:25 000 topo sheet can live with points a few kilometres apart; a layout plan for a housing colony needs points close enough to set out from, often a few hundred metres apart.

Control is graded by order. First order is the highest: tightest permitted misclosure, widest spacing. Second and third order fill in, and minor control is fixed from them for the detail survey. Some syllabi quote a permitted relative closing error of about 1 in 5000 for an ordinary minor traverse, and an order of magnitude tighter for first-order work.

Horizontal control: angles, sides or satellites

Horizontal control fixes X and Y. Four methods are in use.

  • Triangulation: one base line measured with great care, every other side computed from well-shaped observed angles (Chapter 4.5).
  • Trilateration: every side measured directly by EDM, angles optional.
  • Traverse: angles and sides measured along a route; suits corridors such as a road, a canal or a pipeline, where a chain of triangles is awkward.
  • GNSS: satellite ranges give X, Y and Z, and stations need not see each other; the standard choice for new work.

A control network is not trusted until it proves itself. The standard checks are: closing the horizon, where all angles around one station must sum to 360°; closing on a known point or a known side, where the computed value must match the fixed value within the permitted misclosure; a check base measured a second time by an independent method; and loop closure in a traverse, where the coordinates must return to the starting point. Traverse closing error and its adjustment are in Unit 2, and Chapter 4.7 shows how an accepted misclosure is distributed.

Vertical control, benchmarks and ground control points

Vertical control fixes Z. The instruments are the level and the staff, and the method is differential levelling (Unit 3). A run starts at a benchmark of known reduced level and closes back on it or on another benchmark. For a loop that returns to the same mark, the closing error is ΣBS − ΣFS, and the run is accepted only if it is within the permitted misclosure, written c√K mm, where K is the loop length in kilometres and c is a constant set by the order — about 4 for precise work, 12 for ordinary and 24 for rough.

Where the ground is too rough, or the point is a hilltop that cannot be reached, trigonometric levelling is used (Chapter 4.3). It is faster and works uphill, but it is weaker than a levelling run, so it serves lower-order vertical control or fills in between benchmarks.

A benchmark must be permanent and findable. A brass bolt in a culvert, a chisel mark on a gate pillar, a concrete block with a painted number: each needs a description — street or village, a sketch, and distances to two or three nearby objects. Without that record the mark is useless once the paint fades.

Ground control points (GCPs) are the same idea for photogrammetry, UAV flights and GIS base mapping: points with known X, Y and Z that show clearly in the imagery and georeference the whole block. Setting out hangs on the same frame — a building corner, a road chainage, a pipeline centre line is set out from control points, never from a nearby tree.

Worked example

Question. A levelling loop starts at benchmark A with RL 250.000 m, runs 6 km, and closes back on A. The field book gives ΣBS = 12.824 m and ΣFS = 12.798 m. (a) Find the misclosure. (b) Decide whether to accept the run if the permitted misclosure is 12√K mm, with K in km. (c) State the correction per kilometre.

  1. For a loop that returns to its starting benchmark, the net rise must be zero. Net rise = ΣBS − ΣFS, where ΣBS is the sum of all backsights and ΣFS the sum of all foresights, both in metres.
  2. ΣBS − ΣFS = 12.824 − 12.798 = +0.026 m.
  3. Convert to millimetres: 0.026 × 1000 = 26 mm. The plus sign means the run finishes 26 mm high: the computed RL at A is 250.026 m against the known 250.000 m.
  4. Permitted misclosure = 12√K mm, with K = 6 km: 12 × √6 = 12 × 2.4495 = 29.39 mm.
  5. Compare: 26 mm < 29.39 mm, so the run is accepted.
  6. Distribute the 26 mm with the opposite sign over the 6 km: 26 ÷ 6 = 4.33 mm per km, subtracted from the reduced level at each point in proportion to its distance along the run.

Answer. Misclosure +26 mm, permitted 29.39 mm, so the run is accepted; the 26 mm is distributed at 4.33 mm per km with the opposite sign.

Exam traps

  • Treating the detail survey as control. Control is observed first, to a tighter standard, and detail hangs on it; the reverse order cannot be repaired later.
  • Ignoring a misclosure that is inside the limit. It must be distributed, not dropped; a run outside the limit is repeated with better setups, not adjusted.
  • Sign slip in a closed loop: misclosure is ΣBS − ΣFS, so a positive value means the run finished high. The correction carries the opposite sign.
  • Unit slip in c√K mm: K must be in kilometres and the answer comes out in millimetres. Feeding K in metres inflates the limit by about 32 times.
  • Mixing up order and density. Higher order means a smaller permitted misclosure and fewer, more widely spaced points — not more points.

4.7 Weights and adjustment of observations

Observations never agree exactly. Some come from short levelling runs, some from long ones, some from angles turned many times. A weight is a number that says how much each observation deserves to be trusted. The most probable value makes the weighted sum of squared residuals least. This is least-squares adjustment, used in control networks, traverse closes and GNSS baselines. Only a few universities teach this chapter.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for repeated angle observations, Chapter 4.6 for control runs, and Unit 1 for random and systematic error.

Weights measure trust

The true value is the exact number we cannot know. The observed value is what the instrument gives. Error = observed − true value; residual = observed − adjusted value. A weight w is a relative number: w = k/σ², where σ² is the error variance and k is any constant. A small variance gives a large weight.

For n equally precise observations of one quantity, their mean has weight n times one observation, and standard error σ/√n. A levelled height difference over a run of length L has weight proportional to 1/L, because random error grows as √L. A taped distance over length L also has weight proportional to 1/L. An angle turned n times has weight proportional to n. Weights may be scaled; only ratios matter. For a sum or difference, variances add; for a product of independent observations, the relative variances add: (σxy/xy)² ≈ (σx/x)² + (σy/y)².

Least squares and normal equations

Choose the unknown x so that S = Σ wᵢ (lᵢ − x)² is least. Set dS/dx = 0. For direct observations of one quantity, the normal equation is x Σwᵢ = Σwᵢlᵢ, so x = Σwᵢlᵢ / Σwᵢ, the weighted mean.

For two unknowns x and y, write each observation as aᵢx + bᵢy = lᵢ. Minimising Σ wᵢ (aᵢx + bᵢy − lᵢ)² gives two normal equations: (Σw a²)x + (Σw ab)y = Σw a l and (Σw ab)x + (Σw b²)y = Σw b l. Solve them simultaneously.

After adjustment, vᵢ = lᵢ − adjusted value. With relative weights, σ₀ = √(Σw v²/(n−u)), where u is the number of unknowns. For a weighted mean u = 1, so the denominator is n−1; for two unknowns u = 2, so it is n−2. An observation of weight wᵢ has standard error σ₀/√wᵢ, and the weighted mean has σ₀/√Σw. Probable error PE = 0.6745σ. A residual larger than 3σ is doubtful; recheck it.

Worked example

Question. The difference of level between two points is measured by three levelling runs: h₁ = 12.436 m over 2 km, h₂ = 12.448 m over 3 km and h₃ = 12.440 m over 5 km. Find the most probable difference of level.

  1. Weights ∝ 1/L; for L = 2, 3, 5 km take 1/2, 1/3, 1/5.
  2. Scale by 30: w₁ = 15, w₂ = 10, w₃ = 6; Σw = 31.
  3. Numerator = 15×12.436 + 10×12.448 + 6×12.440 = 186.540 + 124.480 + 74.640 = 385.660 m.
  4. Mean = 385.660/31 = 12.44065 ≈ 12.441 m.
  5. Residuals v: −0.005, +0.007, −0.001 m.
  6. Σwv² ≈ 0.000871 m², small; no run is rejected.

Answer. The most probable difference of level is 12.441 m.

Exam traps

  • Do not set w ∝ L for a levelling run. A long run has larger variance, so w ∝ 1/L.
  • Weight uses variance σ², not σ. Doubling σ quarters the weight.
  • Error is measured from the true value; residual is measured from the adjusted value. They are not the same.
  • Probable error is 0.6745σ, not σ; the 3σ rule uses the standard error.
  • In a weighted mean divide by Σw, not by the number of observations.

4.8 Simple, transition and vertical curves

Road and rail alignments cannot turn at a sharp point. A curve joins two straights while keeping the change of direction smooth, and chainage carries distance along it. The simple circular curve fixes the geometry; a transition spiral lets curvature and superelevation grow from zero; a vertical parabola joins gradients. Every point on these curves is set out from the tangent using an offset or a deflection angle computed from the radius. Surveyors stake these points for construction, and the same geometry is checked on drawings before a paver or a rail welding gang reaches site.

You need: chainage and offsets from Unit 1, deflection angles from Chapter 4.2, and fast detail setting out from Chapter 4.4.

Simple circular curve: elements and chainage

Two straights meet at the intersection point I with deflection angle Δ, the angle by which the forward direction turns. A circular arc of radius R joins them. The tangent length T runs from I back to the first tangent point T₁; the curve length L_c runs along the arc; the long chord LC joins T₁ and T₂; E is the external distance from I to the crown; M is the mid-ordinate from the long chord to the crown.

T = R tan(Δ/2), L_c = πRΔ/180, LC = 2R sin(Δ/2), E = R(sec(Δ/2) − 1), M = R(1 − cos(Δ/2))

T is tangent length in m; R radius in m; Δ deflection angle in degrees; L_c curve length (arc) in m; LC long chord in m; E external distance in m; M mid-ordinate in m; sec θ = 1/cos θ.

Chainage follows the route. Chainage of T₁ = chainage of I − T; chainage of T₂ = chainage of I + T; crown chainage = T₁ + L_c/2. A surveyor stakes T₁ and T₂ before any offset.

Degree of curve by the arc definition: D is the central angle subtended by a 100 m arc, so R = 5729.58/D. D is an angle in degrees; R is a length in metres. A smaller D is a flatter curve.

Setting out by offsets and deflection angles

Offsets from the tangent are measured perpendicular from the straight at distance x from T₁: y = R − √(R² − x²) ≈ x²/(2R). The approximation is the parabola used for small x. Offsets from the long chord use x measured from the chord midpoint: y = √(R² − x²) − √(R² − (LC/2)²).

Rankine's deflection method: set the theodolite at T₁, set zero on I, and turn a deflection δ for each point. For a chord c from T₁, δ = c/(2R) radians = 1718.87 c/R minutes. The final deflection to T₂ is Δ/2; the procedure is in Chapter 4.2. A total station computes coordinates of each point and stakes them; the tape and offset become a check. See Unit 5.

For a circular curve, the curve lies wholly between the two tangents, so every offset is measured inward from the straight.

Transition curve: from straight to circle

Curvature cannot jump from zero on the straight to 1/R on the circle. Centrifugal acceleration V²/R (force per unit mass of the vehicle) and superelevation would jump at the tangent point. A transition curve makes curvature grow linearly with distance l: at distance l the radius is R_l, so R_l l = R L_s. At l = 0 it is straight; at l = L_s the radius is R. The clothoid (spiral) does this; for small l its offset is the cubic parabola y = l³/(6 R L_s). A lemniscate is an alternative. The figure uses R = 150 m and transition length L_s = 50 m.

R_l l = R L_s, L_s = V³/(C R), s = L_s²/(24 R)

R_l is radius at distance l in m; R circular radius in m; L_s transition length in m; V speed in m/s; C rate of change of radial acceleration in m/s³; s shift in m; V²/R is the centrifugal acceleration (force per unit mass) in m/s².

Cant gives the same length: equilibrium superelevation e = V²/(gR), with g = 9.81 m/s², and the cant is ramped at a permitted rate. e = V²/(gR) is a ratio with no unit; the outer edge is raised by e × carriageway width, or by e × gauge for rail. Shift s moves the circular arc inward. With equal transitions, tangent length from I to the first tangent point = (R + s) tan(Δ/2) + L_s/2. A sharper curve, a smaller R, needs a longer transition.

Vertical curve: summit and valley

A vertical curve joins two gradients g₁ and g₂, written as decimals with sign. Values of g are taken algebraically with sign, so +0.02 means a 2% rise and −0.01 means a 1% fall. A summit falls (g₂ < g₁); a valley rises (g₂ > g₁). A parabola gives constant rate of change of grade.

L_v = |N| / r, c = N x² / (2 L_v)

N = g₂ − g₁ is the algebraic change of grade; r is permitted grade change per metre; c is tangent correction in m; x is distance from start of vertical curve in m; L_v vertical curve length in m.

Length by rate of change of grade is L_v = |N|/r. A second check comes from stopping sight distance: a summit must let the driver see an object over the crest; a valley must keep headlights on the road. At distance x from the start, RL on curve = RL of tangent at that chainage + c. At the start c = 0 and it grows with x; the sign follows N. Set out by chainage and level.

Worked example

Question. A circular curve of radius R = 300 m deflects an alignment by Δ = 40°. The intersection point is at chainage 2450.000 m. Find T, L_c, long chord, E, M, chainages of T₁ and T₂, and the transition length for design speed 80 km/h with C = 0.3 m/s³.

  1. Half the deflection angle = Δ/2 = 20°.
  2. T = R tan(Δ/2) = 300 tan 20° = 109.19 m.
  3. L_c = πRΔ/180 = π × 300 × 40/180 = 209.44 m.
  4. Long chord = 2R sin(Δ/2) = 2 × 300 sin 20° = 205.21 m.
  5. E = R(sec(Δ/2) − 1) = 300(sec 20° − 1) = 19.25 m.
  6. M = R(1 − cos(Δ/2)) = 300(1 − cos 20°) = 18.09 m.
  7. T₁ = 2450.000 − 109.19 = 2340.81 m; T₂ = 2450.000 + 109.19 = 2559.19 m.
  8. V = 80/3.6 = 22.22 m/s; transition length = V³/(C R) = 22.22³/(0.3 × 300) = 121.93 m.

Answer. T = 109.19 m, L_c = 209.44 m, LC = 205.21 m, E = 19.25 m, M = 18.09 m, T₁ = 2340.81 m, T₂ = 2559.19 m, transition length = 121.93 m.

Exam traps

  • Using Δ where Δ/2 belongs. T uses tan(Δ/2), LC uses sin(Δ/2), E uses sec(Δ/2), and M uses cos(Δ/2).
  • Mixing degree of curve with radius. D is an angle in degrees; R is a length in metres; for a 100 m arc R = 5729.58/D.
  • Reversing the transition rule. A sharper curve has smaller R and needs a longer transition, because L_s = V³/(C R).
  • Finding T₁ and T₂ with arc length. Tangent points come from the straight tangent length T, not from L_c.
  • Dropping the sign in a vertical curve. Use N = g₂ − g₁ algebraically; a summit gives negative correction and a valley gives positive correction.

Takeaways

  1. A theodolite is only as good as its three mutually perpendicular axes; every adjustment, temporary or permanent, is an attempt to restore them.
  2. Repeating a horizontal angle on both faces removes collimation error, index error and the effect of an inclined horizontal axis.
  3. One vertical angle plus one distance gives a height, so trigonometric levelling reaches towers, chimneys and hilltops that a level cannot.
  4. In stadia work with a vertical staff, both the intercept and the formula change with the angle of sight; the correcting terms tan θ and sec θ must not be dropped.
  5. A triangulation scheme is judged by the shape of its triangles, not by the number of stations.
  6. Trilateration has largely replaced chain triangulation now that EDM and total stations give long distances directly.
  7. Control is deliberately made more accurate than the detail survey, so that detail errors do not accumulate into the framework.
  8. Weights convert observations of different quality into a single consistent estimate by the principle of least squares.
  9. A curve is set out by computing offsets or deflection angles from the radius and the chainage; the tangent point is the anchor for every calculation.

Glossary

Additive constant
The fixed distance from the instrument axis to the principal focus in the stadia formula, made zero by an analytic lens.
Altitude
The vertical angle of a line of sight measured from the horizontal plane, positive upward.
Anallatic point
The external point about which the angle subtended by the staff intercept is constant, giving direct proportionality of distance to intercept.
Base line
The single side of a triangulation network measured directly and with the highest precision available.
Base net
A small well-shaped figure attached to the base line, used to expand it into the main triangulation.
Control point
A fixed station whose position or level is known to a higher accuracy than the detail survey around it.
Curvature and refraction correction
Combined correction of about 0.0673 D² metres (D in km) applied to long sights in levelling.
Degree of curve
The central angle subtended by a fixed chord, usually 30 m, used to state curvature in some countries.
Eccentricity of the verniers
A condition where the centre of the graduated circle and the axis of rotation do not coincide, removed by averaging opposite vernier readings.
Intersection angle
The angle between the two straights meeting at a curve's point of intersection, equal to the total deflection of the curve.
Normal equation
One of the set of equations formed in least squares adjustment that gives the most probable unknowns.
Reduced level
The height of a point above the adopted datum, usually mean sea level.
Reiteration method
Measuring several directions in turn from a common zero and reducing them to the first direction.
Repetition method
Turning the same angle several times on the same plate setting and dividing the accumulated reading by the number of turns.
Residual
The difference between an observed value and its adjusted or most probable value.
Staff intercept
The difference between the readings of the upper and lower stadia hairs on a staff.
Strength of figure
An index of how much angular error is magnified into side error in a triangulation network.
Subtense method
Tacheometry using a fixed-length bar held at the far station, with distance from the angle it subtends.
Tacheometry
The branch of surveying in which distance and elevation are obtained from the angle subtended by a known length, without chaining.
Tangent screw
A slow-motion screw giving small controlled rotation after the clamp is tightened.
Theodolite
A precision instrument for measuring horizontal and vertical angles with a telescope that can be transited in a vertical plane.
Transit
To turn the telescope through 180° in the vertical plane, reversing the face of the instrument.
Traverse
A control method in which successive station-to-station distances and angles are measured and coordinates are computed from them.
Variance
The square of the standard error; the quantity whose inverse gives the weight of an observation.
Vertical circle
The graduated circle fixed to the telescope's horizontal axis and read to give altitude or zenith angles.
