4.1 Theodolite: parts and adjustments

A theodolite measures two angles from one set-up: a horizontal angle between two directions, and a vertical angle above or below the horizontal. It gives true angles only if the lines inside it keep their proper relations. Temporary adjustments make the instrument ready at every station. Permanent adjustments restore those relations when a field test shows one has drifted.

You need: the level, its bubble and the idea of a line of collimation from the topic on levels and their adjustments in levelling (helpful, not essential).

The axes and the parts

A transit (vernier) theodolite is built round four lines. The vertical axis is the axis the instrument turns about in azimuth; levelling makes it vertical. The horizontal axis (trunnion axis) is the axis the telescope tilts about. The line of collimation (line of sight) joins the intersection of the cross-hairs to the optical centre of the objective. The axis of the plate level is the tangent to the curve of its vial at the centre of the bubble's run. The axis of the altitude level sets the zero of the vertical circle.

The least count of a vernier is the value of one main-scale division divided by the number of vernier divisions. A circle graduated every 20′ with a 60-division vernier reads to 20′ ÷ 60 = 20″ (a 20″ theodolite). Two verniers 180° apart are read and averaged to remove eccentricity of the circle.

Face left means the vertical circle is on the observer's left when sighting; face right, on the right. Transiting turns the telescope through 180° about the horizontal axis. Swinging turns it about the vertical axis. Changing face is transiting and then swinging through 180° to sight the same point again, so the vertical circle moves to the other side. The mean of a face-left and a face-right observation removes the collimation, trunnion-axis and vertical-index errors.

Temporary adjustments at every station

  1. Setting up and centring. Set the tripod over the station, head roughly level, and bring the plumb bob (or optical plummet) over the peg by moving the legs, then the shifting head.
  2. Levelling up. Turn the upper plate until one plate level is parallel to two foot screws. Turn those two screws together, thumbs both moving inward or both outward; the bubble moves the way the left thumb moves. Turn through 90° so the level lies over the third screw and centre the bubble with that screw alone. Repeat until the bubble stays central in every position.
  3. Focusing the eyepiece on the cross-hairs against the sky or a sheet of paper.
  4. Focusing the objective on the target with the focusing screw.
  5. Removing parallax. If the cross-hairs seem to move over the target when the eye moves, the image is not in the plane of the cross-hairs; refocus the objective until it does not.

Centring is checked again after levelling, because levelling moves the head slightly. Modern telescopes focus by moving a lens inside the tube (internal focusing); older ones move the objective on a slide (external focusing).

Permanent adjustments

For a correct theodolite: (1) the plate level axis is perpendicular to the vertical axis; (2) the vertical cross-hair lies in a plane perpendicular to the horizontal axis; (3) the line of collimation is perpendicular to the horizontal axis; (4) the horizontal axis is perpendicular to the vertical axis; (5) the altitude level axis is parallel to the line of collimation, and the vertical circle reads zero when the line of sight is horizontal. The tests are made in that order, because each assumes the earlier relations hold.

  1. Plate level test. Level up, then swing the upper plate through 180° about the vertical axis. If the bubble runs n divisions off centre, the error is half of that. Bring the bubble halfway back with the capstan screws at one end of the vial and the other half with the foot screws. Repeat until the bubble stays central in all positions.
  2. Cross-hair ring (vertical hair) test. Sight a well-defined point with one end of the vertical hair and tilt the telescope slowly with the vertical tangent screw. If the point leaves the hair, rotate the diaphragm ring slightly.
  3. Collimation test (horizontal). On level ground set up at O. Face left, sight a point A about 100 m away, transit and mark B about 100 m beyond. Change face, sight A again, transit and mark C beside B. If B and C coincide the line of sight is square to the horizontal axis. If not, BC is four times the error; move the vertical hair with the diaphragm screws until it cuts a point D at one quarter of BC from C.
  4. Spire (horizontal axis) test. Sight a high point such as a spire on face left, depress the telescope and mark a point near the ground; repeat on face right. Two marks mean the horizontal axis is not perpendicular to the vertical axis; one end of it is raised or lowered with the screw in the standard until both faces give one mark.
  5. Vertical circle index and altitude level test. With the altitude bubble centred, the vertical circle should read 0° (or 90° on a zenith circle) when the line of sight is horizontal. Sight a well-defined point on both faces and read the vertical angle. If the two angles differ, keep the hair on the point, set the vernier to the mean angle with the clip (index) screw, and then centre the altitude bubble with its capstan screws.

Worked example

Question. A 20″ transit theodolite is being tested on a level site. (a) After careful levelling the plate bubble runs 4 divisions off centre when the upper plate is swung through 180°. Each division of the vial is 20″. Find the error and state how it is corrected. (b) In the collimation test, with the instrument at O, the marks B and C made 100 m from O on face left and face right are 24 mm apart. Find the collimation error in seconds and say where the vertical hair must be set.

  1. (a) Swinging through 180° reverses the tilted vial about the vertical axis, so the bubble shows twice the error: 4 divisions run means an error of 4 ÷ 2 = 2 divisions.
  2. In angle: 2 × 20″ = 40″ between the plate level axis and the plane perpendicular to the vertical axis.
  3. Correction: bring the bubble back 2 divisions (halfway) with the capstan screws of the plate level, and the remaining 2 divisions with the foot screws. Level again and swing; repeat until the bubble stays central.
  4. (b) Each face throws the line of sight off by 2c after transiting (c on the backsight and c after the transit), and the two faces throw it to opposite sides, so BC = 4c × OB.
  5. c = BC ÷ (4 × OB) = 0.024 ÷ (4 × 100) = 0.00006 rad.
  6. In seconds: 0.00006 × 206 265 = 12.4″, about 12″.
  7. Set the vertical hair on D, one quarter of BC from C: 24 ÷ 4 = 6 mm from C towards B. Repeat the test until B and C coincide.

Answer. (a) The plate level is out by 2 divisions (40″); correct half with the capstan screws and half with the foot screws. (b) Collimation error about 12″ (0.00006 rad); set the vertical hair 6 mm from C towards B.

Exam traps

  • Transiting the telescope in the plate level test. The plate bubble is tested by swinging the instrument through 180° about the vertical axis; transiting does not move the plate level.
  • Correcting the whole bubble run with the capstan screws. Only half the run is the instrument's error; the other half is taken out with the foot screws.
  • Moving the hair to the midpoint of BC in the collimation test. BC is four times the error, so the hair goes one quarter of BC from C.
  • Thinking two faces cure everything. The face mean removes collimation, trunnion-axis and index errors, but not a vertical axis that is not vertical; only careful levelling removes that.

The spire test (horizontal axis not perpendicular to the vertical axis). Its effect on a direction grows with tan of the vertical angle, so it is small on flat sights and large on steep ones. A collimation error would grow with sec of the vertical angle and show even on flat sights.

4.2 Measurement of horizontal and vertical angles

A horizontal angle fixes direction: it is what a traverse, a triangulation net and a building layout are built from. A vertical angle fixes slope: with a distance it gives a height. The theodolite reads both on graduated circles, and the way the readings are taken decides how many of the instrument's errors cancel. Reading both verniers removes eccentricity, observing on both faces removes collimation, trunnion-axis and index errors, and repeating the angle on different parts of the circle reduces graduation error.

You need: the parts of the theodolite, face left and face right, and the temporary adjustments from Theodolite: parts and adjustments.

Horizontal angle by the ordinary method

The horizontal angle PQR at station Q is the angle between the vertical planes through QP and QR; the theodolite measures it on the horizontal circle whatever the heights of P and R. The circle is graduated clockwise, so an angle is always measured by turning clockwise from the first station to the second.

  1. Set up over Q and make the temporary adjustments. Work on face left.
  2. With the upper clamp, set vernier A to 0°00′00″ and clamp. Release the lower clamp, sight P and bisect it with the lower tangent screw. Read both verniers.
  3. Release the upper clamp, turn clockwise to R, clamp and bisect with the upper tangent screw. Read both verniers.
  4. Angle = mean reading at R − mean reading at P. Vernier B reads 180° more; drop the 180° before averaging its minutes and seconds with those of A.
  5. Change face and repeat on face right. The result is the mean of the two faces.

To read a vernier, take the degrees and minutes at the main-scale mark just before the vernier's zero, then find the vernier line that coincides with a main-scale line and add its value. On a 20″ instrument the vernier is figured in minutes and 20″ steps.

Repetition and the direction (reiteration) method

In the repetition method the angle is added on the circle several times without reading in between, and the total is divided by the number of repetitions. Readings accumulate on different parts of the circle, so graduation errors average out, and the vernier's least count is divided by n. The usual set is three repetitions on face left, then three on face right.

  1. Set 0°00′00″, sight P with the lower clamp and lower tangent screw, read both verniers.
  2. Release the upper clamp, sight R with the upper motion. This is the first repetition; a rough reading checks the total later.
  3. Release the lower clamp (the reading stays on the circle), turn back to P and bisect with the lower motion.
  4. Release the upper clamp, turn to R and bisect with the upper motion. The circle now shows twice the angle. Repeat until n repetitions; read both verniers.
  5. Angle = (final mean reading − initial reading) ÷ n, adding 360° to the final reading for each complete turn of the circle.

In the direction method (reiteration), several stations A, B, C, D are sighted in turn clockwise from one reference, and the readings are booked as directions; each angle is the difference of two directions. The round ends on the reference again (closing the horizon). The closing error e (final reading − first reading, on a full circle) is shared equally among the m angles of the round, so the corrections to the directions are cumulative: 0 at the reference, −e/m at the first station, −2e/m at the next, and −e on the closing sight. The round is repeated on the other face and with the circle set to different starting values.

Vertical angles

A vertical angle α is the angle between the line of sight and the horizontal in a vertical plane: an angle of elevation (positive) or depression (negative). The zenith distance z is measured from the vertical; α = 90° − z. Older vernier theodolites have a vertical circle reading 0°–90° in four quadrants, with the 0° at the horizontal; most electronic instruments read zenith distance from 0° to 360°.

Face left:  αL = 90° − zL;   face right:  αR = zR − 270°
α = ½(αL + αR);   index error = ½[(zL + zR) − 360°]

zL, zR are the zenith readings on face left and face right of the same point. On a perfect instrument zL + zR = 360°. The index error is subtracted from zL to correct a single face-left reading.

To observe, centre the altitude bubble (or let the compensator settle), bisect the point with the horizontal hair on face left, read the vertical circle, then repeat on face right. The mean of the faces removes the index error and the error of an altitude level not parallel to the line of sight.

Worked example

Question. With a 20″ theodolite at Q, the angle PQR is measured by three repetitions on each face, starting from 0°00′00″ on P. After three repetitions the verniers read: face left, A 128°33′40″ and B 308°34′20″; face right, A 128°34′40″ and B 308°35′20″. Find the angle on each face and the final angle.

  1. Face left: drop the 180° from vernier B, so B gives 128°34′20″. Mean of the verniers = ½(128°33′40″ + 128°34′20″) = 128°34′00″.
  2. Face left angle = (128°34′00″ − 0°00′00″) ÷ 3 = 42°51′20″.
  3. Face right: vernier B gives 308°35′20″ − 180° = 128°35′20″. Mean = ½(128°34′40″ + 128°35′20″) = 128°35′00″.
  4. Face right angle = 128°35′00″ ÷ 3 = 42°51′40″.
  5. Final angle = ½(42°51′20″ + 42°51′40″) = 42°51′30″.
  6. The faces differ by 20″, small enough for a 20″ instrument used three times (each repetition result is read to 20″ ÷ 3 ≈ 7″); a difference of a minute or more would mean a slipped clamp or a bad bisection.

Answer. Face left 42°51′20″, face right 42°51′40″; the angle PQR = 42°51′30″.

Worked example 2

Question. A theodolite with a zenith-reading vertical circle sights the top of a mast. Face left reads 84°12′40″ and face right reads 275°47′40″. Find the vertical angle, the index error, and the corrected face-left reading.

  1. Face left: αL = 90° − 84°12′40″ = +5°47′20″ (elevation).
  2. Face right: αR = 275°47′40″ − 270° = +5°47′40″.
  3. Mean vertical angle α = ½(5°47′20″ + 5°47′40″) = +5°47′30″.
  4. Check by the sum: zL + zR = 84°12′40″ + 275°47′40″ = 360°00′20″. Index error = ½(20″) = +10″.
  5. Corrected face-left zenith reading = 84°12′40″ − 10″ = 84°12′30″, giving α = 90° − 84°12′30″ = 5°47′30″, the same as the face mean.

Answer. Vertical angle +5°47′30″ (elevation); index error +10″; corrected face-left zenith distance 84°12′30″.

Exam traps

  • Averaging vernier B without dropping 180°. B reads about 180° more than A; only its minutes and seconds are averaged with A.
  • Dividing by n without subtracting the initial reading, or forgetting the 360° added each time the circle passes zero in repetition.
  • Applying the same correction to every direction after closing the horizon. The corrections grow step by step, from 0 at the reference to −e at the closing sight.
  • Taking a zenith reading as the vertical angle. α = 90° − z on face left; a z above 90° means a depression.
  • Expecting changing face to remove the error of a vertical axis that is not vertical, or refraction. Neither reverses with the face.

Analyze An angle measured once on each face gives 72°15′40″ and 72°16′20″. A second set by six repetitions gives 72°16′05″. Which value would you adopt, and why? 4 marks

The repetition value, 72°16′05″, or a weighted mean leaning towards it. The single-face values carry the full least count error and one graduation error each; six repetitions spread the reading over the circle and divide the reading error by six. The 40″ gap between the single faces also suggests a bisection or clamp slip.

Analyze Is the repetition method or the direction method better at a triangulation station with five stations to be observed? Justify. 4 marks

The direction method. With five stations, repetition must measure each angle separately, which is slow and repeats each pointing many times. The direction method observes all five in one round, gives every angle from two directions, checks itself by closing the horizon, and is repeated on both faces with different initial settings to beat graduation error.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Electronic theodolites read angles to 1″ or better and store directions in sets, so the direction method on both faces with several circle settings is done in minutes. Software checks each set against the others and flags a pointing that disagrees. Search for rounds of angles, electronic theodolite set collection and angular closure.

4.3 Trigonometric levelling: heights and distances

Trigonometric levelling finds a height from a vertical angle and a horizontal distance, instead of from staff readings. It reaches points a level cannot: the top of a chimney or a temple tower, a hill station across a valley, or a pier in a river. When the base of the object can be reached, one angle and a taped distance do it. When it cannot, two instrument stations and a measured base give the distance as well. Few syllabi list it by name.

You need: vertical angles on both faces from Measurement of horizontal and vertical angles, and reduced levels, benchmarks and the curvature and refraction correction from levelling.

Base of the object accessible

Set the theodolite at a station P, a horizontal distance D from the foot of the object, and sight its top T with a vertical angle α. The height of T above the instrument's horizontal axis is D tan α. To tie it to the datum, take a staff reading on a benchmark with the telescope horizontal: the RL of the instrument axis (its height of collimation) is RL of BM + that reading.

h = D tan α;   RL of T = RL of instrument axis + D tan α

D is the horizontal distance in metres (not the slope distance along the sight). α is positive for elevation and negative for depression. If the sight is taken to a target fixed a height t above the point wanted, subtract t.

For sights beyond about 300 m add the combined curvature and refraction correction, 0.0673 d² m with d in km, to the computed height (the earth falls away below the horizontal line of sight, so the point is higher than D tan α shows). At 165 m it is under 2 mm and is ignored.

Base inaccessible: stations in one vertical plane

When the foot of the object cannot be reached, D cannot be taped. Choose two stations P and Q on one straight line towards the object, with P nearer and Q a measured distance s farther away, so that P, Q and the top T lie in one vertical plane (the single plane method). Measure α₁ at P and α₂ at Q to T, and find the RL of the instrument axis at each station from a benchmark.

h₁ = D tan α₁ and h₂ = (D + s) tan α₂, with h₁ − h₂ = d
D = (s tan α₂ + d) ÷ (tan α₁ − tan α₂)

D is the horizontal distance from P to the vertical line through T. h₁ and h₂ are the heights of T above the instrument axis at P and at Q. d = (RL of axis at Q) − (RL of axis at P), positive when the axis at Q is higher. When both axes are at one level, d = 0 and D = s tan α₂ ÷ (tan α₁ − tan α₂).

The RL of T is found from P (axis RL + h₁) and checked from Q (axis RL + h₂). Because the equation forces the two answers to agree, this check catches arithmetic slips only; an error in s or in an angle passes unseen. So the base is taped twice and both angles are read on both faces.

Base inaccessible: stations not in one plane

Often no straight line towards the object is clear. In the double plane method P and Q are any two stations a measured distance s apart from which the top T is visible. At P measure the horizontal angle θ₁ between Q and T and the vertical angle α₁ to T; at Q measure θ₂ between P and T and α₂. The triangle PQT′ (T′ the point vertically below T) is solved in plan by the sine rule.

DP = s sin θ₂ ÷ sin(θ₁ + θ₂);   DQ = s sin θ₁ ÷ sin(θ₁ + θ₂)
RL of T = RL of axis at P + DP tan α₁ = RL of axis at Q + DQ tan α₂

DP and DQ are the horizontal distances from P and from Q to T′. θ₁ and θ₂ are the horizontal angles at P and Q inside triangle PQT′. The two RLs of T are computed separately; if they agree within the expected error, their mean is taken.

The angle at T′ is 180° − (θ₁ + θ₂); a very small angle there makes D sensitive to angle errors, so the stations are chosen to give a triangle with angles between about 30° and 120°.

Worked example

Question. To find the RL of the top T of a water tower whose base is fenced off, stations P and Q are set out on a straight line towards it, Q being 60.000 m farther from the tower than P. A staff on a benchmark of RL 100.000 m reads 1.625 m with the telescope horizontal at P and 1.385 m at Q. The vertical angles to T are 12°30′ at P and 9°18′ at Q. Find the horizontal distance from P to the tower and the RL of T. Take tan 12°30′ = 0.22169 and tan 9°18′ = 0.16376.

  1. RL of the instrument axis at P = 100.000 + 1.625 = 101.625 m; at Q = 100.000 + 1.385 = 101.385 m.
  2. d = axis at Q − axis at P = 101.385 − 101.625 = −0.240 m (the axis at Q is lower).
  3. D = (s tan α₂ + d) ÷ (tan α₁ − tan α₂) = (60.000 × 0.16376 − 0.240) ÷ (0.22169 − 0.16376).
  4. Numerator = 9.8256 − 0.240 = 9.5856 m; denominator = 0.05793. D = 9.5856 ÷ 0.05793 = 165.47 m.
  5. From P: h₁ = D tan α₁ = 165.47 × 0.22169 = 36.683 m, so RL of T = 101.625 + 36.683 = 138.308 m.
  6. Check from Q: h₂ = (165.47 + 60.000) × 0.16376 = 225.47 × 0.16376 = 36.923 m, so RL of T = 101.385 + 36.923 = 138.308 m. The two agree.
  7. Curvature and refraction at 0.165 km: 0.0673 × 0.165² = 0.002 m, negligible here.

Answer. The tower is 165.47 m from P, and its top is at RL 138.308 m (36.683 m above the axis at P).

Question. The same top is observed by the double plane method from two stations P and Q 80.000 m apart, not in line with the tower. At P the horizontal angle QPT′ is 62°00′ and the vertical angle to T is 18°30′00″; at Q the angle PQT′ is 54°00′ and the vertical angle is 17°12′20″. The axis RLs are 101.625 m at P and 101.385 m at Q. Find the RL of T from each station. Take sin 54° = 0.80902, sin 62° = 0.88295, sin 116° = 0.89879, tan 18°30′ = 0.33460 and tan 17°12′20″ = 0.30966.

  1. θ₁ + θ₂ = 62°00′ + 54°00′ = 116°00′; the angle at T′ is 64°00′, a well-shaped triangle.
  2. DP = 80.000 × 0.80902 ÷ 0.89879 = 72.01 m; DQ = 80.000 × 0.88295 ÷ 0.89879 = 78.59 m.
  3. From P: h = 72.01 × 0.33460 = 24.094 m, RL of T = 101.625 + 24.094 = 125.719 m.
  4. From Q: h = 78.59 × 0.30966 = 24.336 m, RL of T = 101.385 + 24.336 = 125.721 m.
  5. The two differ by 2 mm, well within the reading accuracy, so the mean is taken: 125.720 m.

Exam traps

  • Using the slope distance in D tan α. D is the horizontal distance; a taped or EDM slope distance is first reduced with cos α.
  • Forgetting the instrument axis. D tan α is the height above the axis, so the axis RL (benchmark RL + staff reading) is added.
  • Swapping the angles in the single plane formula. α₁ is from the nearer station P and is the larger angle; the formula gives D from P.
  • Getting the sign of d wrong. d is the axis RL at the far station minus that at the near station; here it was −0.240 m.
  • Using the opposite angle in the double plane method. DP uses sin θ₂, the angle at the other station.

4.4 Tacheometry: stadia, subtense and tangential methods

Tacheometry finds a horizontal distance and a difference of height from readings at the instrument, without taping. A theodolite with two extra hairs reads a length on a staff, and that length grows in proportion to the distance. It is fast on broken ground and for the many points of a contour survey, at an accuracy of about 1 in 500 to 1 in 1000. Three systems are taught: the stadia method with fixed hairs, the subtense method with a bar of known length, and the tangential method with two vanes on a staff.

You need: vertical angles from Measurement of horizontal and vertical angles and heights from Trigonometric levelling: heights and distances.

The stadia principle and its constants

The diaphragm of a tacheometer carries a stadia hair above and below the central hair, a fixed distance i apart. Rays through the two hairs leave the objective and spread in a narrow wedge, so the length s they cut on a vertical staff, the staff intercept (top hair reading − bottom hair reading), is proportional to the distance. From the lens formula, with the line of sight horizontal:

D = (f ÷ i) s + (f + d) = K s + C

D is the horizontal distance from the instrument's vertical axis to the staff, in m. f is the focal length of the objective, i the stadia hair interval, d the distance from the objective to the vertical axis. K = f ÷ i is the multiplying constant, made 100 by the maker. C = f + d is the additive constant, about 0.3 to 0.45 m in an external-focusing telescope.

An anallatic lens fitted between the objective and the eyepiece of an external-focusing telescope makes C zero, so D = 100 s directly. A modern internal-focusing telescope has C so small (a few centimetres) that it is taken as zero. The constants of an instrument are found in the field by taping two or more distances on level ground, reading the intercepts, and solving D₁ = K s₁ + C and D₂ = K s₂ + C together.

Inclined sights with the staff vertical

On sloping ground the line of sight is inclined at θ. With the staff held vertical (the usual practice, because a staff is easy to plumb), the intercept s is not square to the line of sight; its square-on part is s cos θ. The inclined distance to the staff is K s cos θ + C, and its horizontal and vertical components give:

D = K s cos²θ + C cos θ;   V = K s sin θ cos θ + C sin θ = ½ K s sin 2θ + C sin θ
RL of staff station = RL of instrument axis ± V − h

θ is the vertical angle of the central hair's sight. V is the vertical height of the central hair's point on the staff above (+, elevation) or below (−, depression) the instrument axis. h is the central hair reading. The RL of the instrument axis is the station RL plus the height of the axis above the peg.

With the staff held normal to the sight instead, D = (K s + C) cos θ + h sin θ, a form rarely used now.

Subtense and tangential methods

In the subtense method a horizontal bar of known length (2 m, with targets at its ends) is set up at the far station, square to the line of sight, using a small sighting device on the bar. The theodolite measures the horizontal angle θ subtended by the two targets. Because the angle is measured in the horizontal plane, the result is already a horizontal distance, whatever the slope. The method needs only angles at the instrument and no staff reading.

Subtense:  D = (b ÷ 2) cot(θ ÷ 2) ≈ b ÷ θ (θ in radians)
Tangential:  D = s ÷ (tan α₁ − tan α₂)

b is the bar length (2 m) and θ the horizontal angle between its targets. In the tangential method, s is the vertical distance between two vanes on the staff, and α₁ and α₂ are the vertical angles to the upper and lower vanes (both elevations here; a depression is entered as a negative angle).

In the tangential method the telescope has no stadia hairs. The central hair is set on two vanes fixed a known distance apart on the staff and the two vertical angles are read. It suits long sights where stadia hairs cannot be read finely, but is slower.

4.5 Triangulation and trilateration

Triangulation fixes a network of control stations by measuring one side, the base, with great care, and then only angles. Every triangle of the network is solved by the sine rule, so each new side becomes the known side of the next triangle, and coordinates follow from the sides and their bearings. Trilateration does the reverse: electronic distance measurement gives all three sides of each triangle, and the angles are computed. Both build the frame on which detail surveys, road and canal alignments and large-scale mapping are hung.

You need: horizontal angles by the direction method from Measurement of horizontal and vertical angles, and whole-circle bearings and latitudes and departures from traversing.

Principle, figures and classification

A triangulation system is a series of triangles joined side to side. If one side and the three angles of a triangle are known, the other two sides follow from the sine rule: a ÷ sin A = b ÷ sin B = c ÷ sin C, each side opposite its angle. The measured side is the base line; a second base, or a check base, at the far end of the chain tests the whole computation.

The common figures are a chain of single triangles (quick, but with no check beyond the sum of each triangle's angles), braced quadrilaterals (four stations with both diagonals observed, the strongest and most used for a long, narrow strip), and centred polygons (a central station surrounded by a ring of triangles, for wide areas). A triangle is well-conditioned when no angle is below about 30° or above 120°; the ideal shape is an isosceles triangle with base angles of 56°14′, which makes side errors least for a given angle error. Strength of figure is a number computed from the angles of a layout that compares how much angle error grows into side error; a lower value means a stronger figure.

OrderBase lineTriangle sidesAverage triangle closureMaximum closure
Primary (first order)8 to 12 km30 to 150 kmunder 1″3″
Secondary (second order)1.5 to 5 km8 to 65 km3″8″
Tertiary (third order)0.5 to 3 km1.5 to 10 km6″12″

Typical values quoted in Indian textbooks. First-order angles are observed in many sets with a 1″ instrument, so the mean is good to about a second; lower orders accept a few seconds. The sum of the angles of a triangle on the earth exceeds 180° by the spherical excess, about 1″ for every 196 km² of triangle area, so it matters only in primary work.

Field work: stations, signals and the base

  1. Reconnaissance. Study maps, walk the ground, choose stations that are intervisible, give well-conditioned triangles, and are easy to reach and to find again. Lines must clear the ground and buildings, keeping well above hot surfaces to limit refraction.
  2. Station marks and signals. Mark each station with a buried mark and a reference sketch. Set up a signal (pole, beacon or lamp) over it for sighting from other stations, and a tower or scaffold where the ground does not give intervisibility.
  3. Angles. Observe directions at every station on both faces and with several initial settings of the circle; reduce each triangle so that its angles sum to 180° (plus the spherical excess), sharing the error equally when the angles have equal weight.
  4. Base measurement. Measure the base by EDM or a standardised steel or invar tape on a cleared alignment. Apply the corrections for standardisation, temperature, pull, sag and slope, and finally reduce the length to mean sea level.
  5. Satellite station. When the instrument cannot stand on a station (a steeple or a tank), observe from a nearby satellite station and reduce the angles to the true station by the small triangles between them.
Reduction to mean sea level:  L₀ = L × R ÷ (R + H) ≈ L − L H ÷ R

L is the measured horizontal length at mean height H above mean sea level, R the earth's radius (about 6370 km), and L₀ the length at mean sea level. H and R in the same unit.

Trilateration and computing coordinates

In trilateration every side of every triangle is measured by EDM and reduced to the same surface. The angles are then computed by the cosine rule, cos A = (b² + c² − a²) ÷ (2bc), and used to carry bearings. It is fast where long lines can be measured electronically, and its scale is strong; but a single triangle has no check (any three lengths that obey the triangle rule fit together), so braced quadrilaterals with both diagonals measured are used.

Coordinates are carried through the net like a traverse. From a station A of known easting and northing (EA, NA) and a side AB of length l and whole-circle bearing β, the departure is l sin β and the latitude is l cos β.

EB = EA + l sin β;   NB = NA + l cos β

β is the whole-circle bearing of AB measured clockwise from north. The bearing of the next side is found from the adjusted angle at the station. A station reached by two routes through the net must get the same coordinates from both, within rounding.

Exam traps

  • Pairing a side with the wrong angle in the sine rule. BC is opposite A and AC opposite B, so BC = AB sin A ÷ sin C.

4.6 Horizontal and vertical control

A control survey fixes a few points to a higher accuracy than the detail survey that follows. Horizontal control gives each point its easting and northing; vertical control gives its reduced level. Walls, roads, contours and drone maps are then hung on these points. An error in a detail point stays at that point, but an error in a control point is copied into everything fixed from it, so control is observed first, checked against a known value, adjusted, and only then used.

You need: triangulation and computing coordinates from Triangulation and trilateration, and benchmarks and closing errors from levelling.

Working from the whole to the part

The rule of surveying is to work from the whole to the part: first a sparse frame of strong control over the whole area, then denser and weaker control inside it, and detail last. Errors made in the small work then stay inside the frame instead of building up across the site. Two parties working from the same control also meet without a gap or an overlap.

Control is graded by order. National (first-order) control is fixed by the national survey with the best instruments and the strongest figures; second- and third-order points densify it; minor control on a site is fixed from those. Higher order means a smaller permitted error and wider spacing. Specifications differ, but as a guide a first-order traverse closes to about 1 in 25 000 or better and an ordinary theodolite traverse for an engineering site to about 1 in 5000 to 1 in 10 000.

Each control point is a permanent mark (a bolt in concrete, a pillar, a mark on a culvert) with a description: a sketch and measured distances to two or three nearby features, so it can be found and used again. Ground control points for drone and aerial mapping are control points that show clearly in the images and fix the photographs to the ground.

Methods of horizontal control

  • Triangulation: one measured base and observed angles; strong over hills and open country with long sights.
  • Trilateration: all sides measured by EDM; strong in scale, used with braced figures.
  • Traversing: a chain of measured lines and the angles between them; suits roads, canals, pipelines and built-up areas where a network of triangles cannot be seen through.
  • GNSS: satellite positioning at each station; stations need a clear sky but not a sight of each other, and give height as well.

A closed traverse either returns to its start (a loop) or ends on another known station, so its error can be found; an open traverse has no check and is not used for control. For a closed loop of n stations, the interior angles must sum to (2n − 4) × 90°. The angular error is shared equally among the angles; the whole-circle bearings are then computed from a known starting bearing (bearing of next line = bearing of previous line + 180° − interior angle, for a loop observed clockwise round the inside).

Departure = l sin β;   Latitude = l cos β
Closing error e = √[(ΣDep)² + (ΣLat)²];   relative precision = 1 in (Σl ÷ e)
Bowditch correction to a line = −(ΣDep or ΣLat) × l ÷ Σl

l is a line's length and β its whole-circle bearing. In a closed loop the departures and the latitudes must each sum to zero; what is left is the closing error. Bowditch's (compass) rule shares the error in proportion to the length of each line; it suits traverses where angles and distances are of similar precision. The transit rule shares it in proportion to each line's departure or latitude instead.

Vertical control

Vertical control is carried from benchmarks of the national levelling network by differential levelling in closed loops or between two benchmarks, with balanced sights. The closing error is the computed RL of the closing benchmark minus its known RL (for a loop, ΣBS − ΣFS, the sum of all backsights minus the sum of all foresights, which should be zero). It is accepted if it is within the permitted value C√K mm, K being the length of the run in km and C a constant set by the specification (a few millimetres for precise levelling, larger for ordinary work), and is then distributed in proportion to distance.

Where levelling is impractical (hill tops, across a gorge) trigonometric heights from reciprocal vertical angles give lower-order vertical control. GNSS gives heights above the ellipsoid, which must be turned into reduced levels with a geoid model before they can be used with benchmark heights.

  • Starting detail before the control is closed and adjusted. An error found later cannot be removed from the detail already fixed.
  • Using (n − 2) × 180° for the wrong set of angles. It is the sum of interior angles; exterior angles of a loop sum to (2n + 4) × 90°.
  • Distributing the closing error with the same sign as the error. The correction has the opposite sign, so the sums become zero.
  • Quoting the closing error without the length. 0.05 m is excellent on 935 m and poor on 50 m; the relative precision 1 in (Σl ÷ e) is what is compared with the specification.
  • Adjusting a traverse whose closing error is beyond the limit. It is remeasured, not adjusted; adjustment shares only small random errors.

4.7 Weights and adjustment of observations

No two measurements of the same quantity agree exactly, and a control network has more measurements than the unknowns it fixes. Adjustment chooses the one set of values that fits all the observations best, giving more say to the better ones. The better ones are those with larger weights, and the rule that fixes the best values is least squares: the sum of the weighted squares of the residuals is made a minimum. Few syllabi list this topic by name, but every triangle closure and traverse adjustment in a control survey uses it.

You need: triangle closure and traverse misclosure from Triangulation and trilateration and Horizontal and vertical control.

Errors, residuals and weights

Errors are of three kinds. Mistakes (a misread vernier, a wrong station sighted) are removed by checks and repeated observations, never by adjustment. Systematic errors follow a law and keep their sign (a tape that is too long, collimation error, curvature); they are removed by corrections or by the method of observing. Accidental (random) errors are what remains: small, equally likely positive or negative, and they are what adjustment deals with.

The true error of an observation is its value minus the true value, which is never known. The most probable value is the best estimate of the true value from the observations, and a residual v is an observation minus the most probable value. The standard error σ measures the spread of an observation; the probable error is 0.6745σ, the error that half the observations exceed.

The weight w of an observation is a number proportional to its trustworthiness, w ∝ 1 ÷ σ². Only ratios of weights matter. The laws of weights used in surveying follow from it:

  • The mean of n equally good observations has n times the weight of one, and standard error σ ÷ √n.
  • An angle measured n times (repetitions or sets) has weight n.
  • A levelled height difference over a run of K km has weight 1 ÷ K, because its variance grows in proportion to the length; the same holds for a taped line.
  • If a quantity is multiplied by a constant c, its weight is divided by c²; the weight of a sum or difference of two quantities is 1 ÷ (1/w₁ + 1/w₂).

The principle of least squares

The most probable values are those that make Σwv² a minimum. For repeated measurements of one quantity, setting the derivative of Σw(l − x)² to zero gives the weighted mean. With more unknowns, each observation is written as an observation equation in the unknowns; minimising Σwv² gives one normal equation per unknown, and the normal equations are solved together.

Weighted mean:  x = Σwl ÷ Σw
Standard error of unit weight:  σ₀ = √[Σwv² ÷ (n − u)];   of the mean:  σx = σ₀ ÷ √Σw

l are the observations, w their weights, v = l − x the residuals, n the number of observations and u the number of unknowns (1 for a single quantity, so n − u = n − 1). σ₀ is the standard error of an observation of weight 1.

To form a normal equation for an unknown, multiply each observation equation by its weight and by the coefficient of that unknown in it, and add. For a levelling net with two unknown RLs x and y fixed from a benchmark of RL 100.000 m by the equal-weight observations x − 100.000 = 2.512, y − x = 1.836 and y − 100.000 = 4.352, the normal equations are 2x − y = 100.676 and −x + 2y = 106.188, giving x = 102.513 m and y = 104.351 m; the loop's 4 mm misclosure is shared as 1.3 mm on each line.

Two simple results are used constantly. In a triangle whose angles have weights w₁, w₂, w₃, the closing error is distributed in proportion to 1 ÷ w (inversely as the weights), with the opposite sign; with equal weights it is shared equally. At a station where angles are measured and their sum is also measured, the discrepancy is shared in the same way.

Judging the result

After adjustment, look at each residual. Under random errors about 68 % of residuals lie within ±σ and nearly all within ±3σ, so a residual larger than about 3σ points to a mistake: that observation is checked or repeated, not simply adjusted. A value of σ₀ far above what the instruments should give also says that something systematic was missed.

Worked example

Question. The angles of a triangle are measured with different numbers of repetitions, giving weights: A = 48°18′32″ (weight 1), B = 63°27′46″ (weight 2) and C = 68°14′04″ (weight 3). Find the most probable values of the angles.

  1. Sum = 48°18′32″ + 63°27′46″ + 68°14′04″ = 180°00′22″, so the error is +22″ (spherical excess negligible).
  2. Corrections are in proportion to 1 ÷ w: 1, 1/2 and 1/3. Their sum is 11/6.
  3. Correction to A = −22″ × 1 ÷ (11/6) = −12″; to B = −22″ × (1/2) ÷ (11/6) = −6″; to C = −22″ × (1/3) ÷ (11/6) = −4″. Total −22″.
  4. Adjusted angles: A = 48°18′20″, B = 63°27′40″, C = 68°14′00″, summing to 180°00′00″.
  5. The weakest angle (A, weight 1) takes the largest share, as it should.

Answer. A = 48°18′20″, B = 63°27′40″, C = 68°14′00″.

Worked example 2

Question. The difference of level between two benchmarks is found by three levelling runs: 12.436 m over 2 km, 12.448 m over 3 km and 12.440 m over 5 km. Find the most probable difference, the residuals, the standard error of a 1 km run and the standard error of the result.

  1. Weights ∝ 1 ÷ K: w = 1/2, 1/3, 1/5 = 0.5000, 0.3333, 0.2000; Σw = 1.0333 (a 1 km run has weight 1).
  2. Σwl = 0.5 × 12.436 + 0.3333 × 12.448 + 0.2 × 12.440 = 6.2180 + 4.1493 + 2.4880 = 12.8553. x = 12.8553 ÷ 1.0333 = 12.4406 m. (Using weights 15, 10, 6 gives 385.660 ÷ 31 = 12.4406 m, the same.)
  3. Residuals v = l − x: −4.6 mm, +7.4 mm, −0.6 mm.
  4. Σwv² = 0.5 × 21.2 + 0.3333 × 54.8 + 0.2 × 0.4 = 10.6 + 18.3 + 0.1 = 28.9 mm².
  5. σ₀ = √(28.9 ÷ (3 − 1)) = √14.5 = 3.8 mm, the standard error of a 1 km run.
  6. σx = 3.8 ÷ √1.0333 = 3.7 mm. The largest residual, 7.4 mm on a 3 km run, is under 3σ for that run (3 × 3.8 × √3 = 19.7 mm), so no run is rejected.

Answer. Most probable difference 12.441 m (12.4406 m); residuals −4.6, +7.4 and −0.6 mm; σ for a 1 km run 3.8 mm; standard error of the result 3.7 mm.

Exam traps

  • Weighting a levelling run in proportion to its length. A longer run is less reliable, so w ∝ 1 ÷ K.
  • Weight from σ instead of σ². Halving the standard error makes the weight four times, not twice.
  • Distributing a triangle error in proportion to the weights. It goes in proportion to 1 ÷ w; the best angle gets the smallest correction.
  • Dividing Σwv² by n. The divisor is n − u, the number of redundant observations.
  • Adjusting a mistake. A residual of several σ means a blunder to be found and remeasured, not shared out.

4.8 Simple, transition and vertical curves

Roads, railways and canals change direction and gradient through curves. A simple circular curve joins two straights with an arc of one radius. A transition curve placed between a straight and the arc lets the curvature, and the superelevation that balances it, grow gradually instead of jumping. A vertical curve, usually a parabola, joins two gradients at a summit or a valley. Each is computed, then pegged at regular chainages.

You need: horizontal angles from Measurement of horizontal and vertical angles, chainage along a line, and reduced levels from levelling.

Simple circular curve: elements and chainages

Two straights meet at the point of intersection I; the deflection angle Δ is the angle through which the forward straight turns from the back straight. An arc of radius R touches the back straight at the first tangent point T₁ and the forward straight at T₂. The degree of curve D is the angle at the centre subtended by a 30 m arc, so R = 1718.9 ÷ D (D in degrees).

Tangent length T = R tan(Δ/2);   length of curve l = πRΔ ÷ 180
Long chord = 2R sin(Δ/2);   apex (external) distance E = R[sec(Δ/2) − 1];   mid-ordinate M = R[1 − cos(Δ/2)]
Chainage of T₁ = chainage of I − T;   chainage of T₂ = chainage of T₁ + l

R in m, Δ in degrees. T is measured along each straight from I to the tangent point. E is from I to the middle of the curve, and M from the middle of the long chord to the curve. The chainage of T₂ is reached along the curve, so it is found by adding l to T₁, not T to I.

Setting out a circular curve

Pegs go at full chainages (every 20 m or 30 m), with a sub-chord at each end. The methods are:

  • Linear methods (tape only, for short curves): offsets from the long chord, y = √(R² − x²) − (R − M) with x from the midpoint of the chord; or offsets from the tangent, y = R − √(R² − x²), about x² ÷ (2R) for small x, with x measured along the tangent from T₁.
  • Rankine's method of tangential angles (theodolite and tape): set the theodolite at T₁ on 0°00′00″ towards I. For a chord c the tangential angle is δ = 1718.9 c ÷ R minutes. Turn the total deflection (the running sum of δ) and measure the chord from the previous peg to cut the line of sight. The total deflection to T₂ must equal Δ/2.
  • Two-theodolite method (no taping, rough ground): instruments at T₁ and T₂ turn the total deflections and the two lines of sight cross at each peg. A total station sets out each peg from its computed coordinates.

Transition curves

Entering a circular curve straight from a tangent, a vehicle would meet the full radial acceleration V² ÷ R and superelevation at once. A transition curve is a spiral whose radius falls from infinity at the tangent to R at the circular arc, so that the radial acceleration grows at a steady rate. For the ideal (clothoid) spiral the radius r at distance l along it satisfies r l = R Ls; for setting out, its offsets from the tangent are close to the cubic parabola y = x³ ÷ (6 R Ls).

Length by rate of change of radial acceleration:  Ls = V³ ÷ (c R)
Shift S = Ls² ÷ (24 R);   total tangent length = (R + S) tan(Δ/2) + Ls ÷ 2;   equilibrium superelevation e = V² ÷ (g R)

V is the design speed in m/s, c the permitted rate of change of radial acceleration in m/s³ (about 0.3 to 0.6), R the radius in m, Ls the transition length in m. The shift S moves the circular arc in to make room for the transitions. e is a ratio (rise per unit width); codes cap it, and tyre friction carries the rest.

Vertical curves

Two gradients g₁ and g₂ (as decimals, + rising, − falling in the direction of chainage) meet at the vertical point of intersection. A summit curve has g₂ < g₁; a valley (sag) curve has g₂ > g₁. A parabola gives a constant rate of change of gradient, and its offsets from the tangent are simple to compute.

Length Lv = (g₂ − g₁) ÷ r;   level on the curve at distance x from its start = tangent level + (g₂ − g₁) x² ÷ (2 Lv)

r is the permitted rate of change of gradient per metre (sight distance may govern instead). Tangent level = start level + g₁ x. On a summit (g₂ − g₁) is negative and the curve lies below the tangent. The highest or lowest point is at x = −g₁ Lv ÷ (g₂ − g₁).

Worked example

Question. Two straights meet at I, chainage 2450.000 m, with a deflection angle of 40°.   3. (b) Chainage of T₁ = 2450.000 − 109.19 = 2340.81 m. Chainage of T₂ = 2340.81 + 209.44 = 2550.25 m (not 2450.000 + 109.19).
  4. (c) First sub-chord to chainage 2360: c₁ = 2360 − 2340.81 = 19.19 m. Then nine full chords of 20 m reach 2540, and the last sub-chord is 2550.25 − 2540 = 10.25 m.

Takeaways

  1. A theodolite gives true angles only when its lines keep their relations; temporary adjustments are made at every station, permanent ones only after a failed test.
  2. Reading both verniers removes eccentricity; the mean of both faces removes collimation, trunnion-axis and index errors, but not a tilted vertical axis.

Remember and understand

Understand Name the fundamental lines of a transit theodolite and the relations between them in a correctly adjusted instrument. Describe the permanent adjustments in the order in which they are made, giving the test and the correction for each. 13 marks

Points to cover. Five lines and five relations; Plate level test by swinging 180°; Cross-hair ring test; Collimation test with marks B, C and D; Spire test; Vertical index and altitude level.

Model answer.

  1. Fundamental lines: the vertical axis (about which the instrument turns), the horizontal or trunnion axis (about which the telescope tilts), the line of collimation (through the intersection of the cross-hairs and the optical centre of the objective), the axis of the plate level, and the axis of the altitude level.
  2. Required relations: (1) the plate level axis is perpendicular to the vertical axis; (2) the vertical cross-hair lies in a plane perpendicular to the horizontal axis; (3) the line of collimation is perpendicular to the horizontal axis; (4) the horizontal axis is perpendicular to the vertical axis; (5) the altitude level axis is parallel to the line of collimation, and the vertical circle reads zero when the line of sight is horizontal.
  3. Order: the adjustments are made in the order 1 to 5, because each test assumes the earlier relations already hold. A collimation test made with a faulty plate level, for example, would mix two errors.
  4. Plate level adjustment. Test: level up carefully, then swing the upper plate through 180° about the vertical axis. If the bubble stays central the level is in adjustment. If it runs n divisions, the error is n/2, because swinging reverses the tilted vial. Correction: bring the bubble back halfway (n/2) with the capstan screws at one end of the vial and the other half with the foot screws; repeat until it stays central in all positions. Example: a run of 4 divisions of 20″ is an error of 40″.
  5. Cross-hair ring adjustment. Test: sight a fine point with one end of the vertical hair and tilt the telescope with the vertical tangent screw; the point must stay on the hair. Correction: loosen two adjacent capstan screws of the diaphragm and rotate the ring until it does.
  6. Collimation adjustment (line of sight perpendicular to the horizontal axis). Test on level ground: set up at O, sight A about 100 m away on face left, transit and mark B about 100 m beyond O; change face, sight A again, transit and mark C beside B. Each face throws the line 2c off the straight, to opposite sides, so BC = 4c × OB. Correction: move the vertical hair with the side capstan screws until it cuts D, one quarter of BC from C; repeat. Example: BC = 24 mm at 100 m gives c = 0.024 ÷ 400 = 0.00006 rad ≈ 12″, and D is 6 mm from C.
  7. Horizontal axis adjustment (spire test). Test: set up near a tall building, sight a high point such as a spire on face left, depress the telescope and mark a point P near ground level; change face and repeat to get Q. If P and Q differ, the horizontal axis is not perpendicular to the vertical axis. Correction: sight the midpoint of PQ, raise the telescope (it will miss the spire) and raise or lower one end of the trunnion axis with the screw in the standard until the hair cuts the spire.
  8. Vertical index and altitude level adjustment. Test: centre the altitude bubble, sight a fine point on both faces and read the vertical angles. If they differ, the index is in error by half the difference. Correction: keep the hair on the point, set the vernier to the mean angle with the clip screw, which moves the bubble off; re-centre the altitude bubble with its own capstan screws. Repeat until both faces agree.

Summary of what remains: after these adjustments small residual errors are still eliminated in practice by observing on both faces and reading both verniers; only a vertical axis that is not truly vertical cannot be eliminated by changing face, so levelling up is done with special care before steep sights.

Understand Derive the distance equation D = K s + C for a stadia tacheometer with a horizontal line of sight, and explain the meaning of K and C. Explain how the constants are found in the field and how an anallatic lens affects C. 13 marks

Points to cover. Stadia hairs and intercept; Lens formula and similar triangles; K = f ÷ i and C = f + d; Field determination from two taped distances; Anallatic lens and internal focusing.

Model answer.

  1. The diaphragm carries two stadia hairs, a fixed distance i apart, above and below the central hair. Rays through the outer hairs cut a vertical staff at two points; the difference of the readings is the staff intercept s.
  2. Let f be the focal length of the objective, u the distance from the optical centre of the objective to the staff, and v the distance from the objective to the image (the plane of the diaphragm). The image of the intercept s is the hair interval i.
  3. By similar triangles through the optical centre, s ÷ i = u ÷ v. By the lens formula, 1/u + 1/v = 1/f.
  4. Multiplying the lens formula by u f: f + f u/v = u, so u = f + f (u/v) = f + f (s/i) = (f/i) s + f.
  5. The distance D from the vertical axis of the instrument to the staff is u + d, where d is the distance from the objective's optical centre to the vertical axis. So D = (f/i) s + (f + d) = K s + C.
  6. K = f/i is the multiplying constant; the maker spaces the hairs so that K = 100. C = f + d is the additive constant, about 0.3 to 0.45 m in an external-focusing telescope, where the objective is moved out on a slide to focus.
  7. Field determination: on level ground, tape two or more distances from the instrument, for example D₁ = 50.000 m and D₂ = 100.000 m, and read the intercepts, say s₁ = 0.496 m and s₂ = 0.996 m. Then 100.000 = 0.996K + C and 50.000 = 0.496K + C. Subtracting, 50.000 = 0.500K, so K = 100, and C = 50.000 − 49.600 = 0.400 m. With more distances, the constants are found as the best fit (least squares) and the readings are repeated.
  8. Anallatic lens: an extra convex lens fixed between the objective and the eyepiece of an external-focusing telescope, placed so that the apex of the cone of stadia rays falls exactly at the vertical axis. Then C = 0 and D = K s directly, so the field reduction is simpler.
  9. Internal-focusing telescopes focus by moving a concave lens inside a tube of fixed length; their additive constant is only a few centimetres and is taken as zero, so most modern tacheometers behave as anallatic.
  10. On an inclined sight to a vertical staff the same constants are used in D = K s cos²θ + C cos θ and V = ½ K s sin 2θ + C sin θ; with the constants above, s = 1.250 m and θ = 8°12′ give D = 122.85 m and V = 17.70 m.
  11. Check of the field values: K s + C for the 50 m staff gives 100 × 0.496 + 0.400 = 50.000 m, as taped.

  2. Personal errors: centring error (most serious on short sights: a 5 mm centring error on a 50 m sight changes the direction by up to 5 ÷ 50 000 rad ≈ 21″), imperfect levelling, inaccurate bisection of a thick or tilted target, parallax, misreading the vernier or booking wrongly, and using the wrong tangent screw. Remedies: careful centring with an optical plummet, sighting the foot of the ranging rod or a fine target, removing parallax, reading both verniers and both faces, and following a fixed routine.
  3. Natural errors: horizontal refraction where a line grazes hot ground or a wall; heat shimmer at midday; wind vibrating the instrument; uneven heating of the tripod; settlement of the tripod on soft ground. Remedies: keep lines well above the ground, observe early or late in the day, shade the instrument, use a firm set-up, and observe quickly. None of these reverses on changing face.
  4. Effect on steep sights, collimation: for a collimation error c = 20″, a sight at h = 0° is in error by 20″, but at h = 30° by 20 × sec 30° = 23.1″, and at h = 60° by 40″. An angle between a flat sight and a 60° sight carries 40 − 20 = 20″ of error.
  5. Effect on steep sights, horizontal axis: for i = 20″, a flat sight has no error (tan 0° = 0), a 30° sight 20 × 0.577 = 11.5″, and a 60° sight 34.6″. This is why the spire test uses a steep sight.
  6. Vertical axis tilt of 20″ has the same order of effect as the horizontal axis error on steep sights, but it does not change sign on changing face, so the face mean keeps it in full. Only levelling up carefully removes it.
  7. Analysis: errors that change sign with the face (collimation, horizontal axis, index) cancel in the face mean; errors that do not (vertical axis, refraction, centring) must be prevented at the set-up. Errors that vary round the circle (graduation, eccentricity) are averaged by reading two verniers and by repetition on different parts of the circle.
  8. Assessing the result: the spread between faces and between sets, the closure of the horizon at a station, and finally the angular misclosure of a traverse or triangle show whether the remaining random error is within the specification.
  9. Conclusion: observe on both faces, read both verniers, use several sets with different initial settings, level and centre with care, and keep sights clear of the ground; then only small random errors remain, and these are shared by adjustment.
Evaluate Compare triangulation, trilateration, traversing and GNSS as methods of horizontal control. Recommend, with reasons, a scheme for controlling a 20 km highway corridor through mixed open country and a town. 15 marks

Points to cover. What each method measures and how it is checked; Strength, speed and terrain limits; Accuracy measures and closing checks; Recommended mixed scheme; Vertical control for the same corridor.

Model answer.

  1. Triangulation: measures one base and all angles; sides by the sine rule. Strong in open, hilly country with long intervisible lines; needs towers where the ground is flat; scale depends on the base; checks come from triangle closures, a check base and redundant figures.
  2. Trilateration: measures all sides by EDM; angles by the cosine rule. Strong in scale and fast with modern EDM; a single triangle has no internal check, so braced figures with extra lines are needed; still needs intervisibility.
  3. Traversing: measures lines and the angles between consecutive lines; follows a corridor and works through towns where long lines are blocked. Its errors grow with the number of stations, so it must close on itself or on known points; checks are the angular closure, (2n − 4) × 90° for a loop, and the linear misclosure, compared as 1 in (Σl ÷ e).
  4. GNSS: measures positions from satellites; stations need a clear sky, not a view of each other. Gives E, N and ellipsoidal height in one session; accuracy of a few millimetres to centimetres with static or network methods; weak under trees, in narrow streets and near tall buildings.
  5. Comparison:
    MethodMain measurementBest terrainMain check
    TriangulationAngles + baseOpen, hillyTriangle closure, check base
    TrilaterationDistancesOpen, intervisibleRedundant lines
    TraverseAngles + distancesCorridors, townsAngular and linear closure
    GNSSSatellite rangesOpen skyRepeat sessions, closed baselines
  6. Recommendation for a 20 km corridor: a primary frame of GNSS stations in pairs every 2 to 3 km along the route, each pair intervisible so that it also gives a bearing; fixed in static sessions from national reference stations and adjusted by least squares.
  7. Within the open stretches, densify with total-station traverses between GNSS pairs (link traverses that start and close on known coordinates and bearings), with legs of 150 to 300 m.
  8. Through the town, where sky is blocked, run a total-station traverse along the street between GNSS pairs on either side of the town, closing to better than 1 in 20 000, and adjust by Bowditch's rule or least squares.
  9. Triangulation in the classical sense would be slow over 20 km of flat country and needs towers; it is used only where a river or valley must be crossed, as a braced quadrilateral across the gap measured by both angles and distances.
  10. Vertical control: carry levels by double-run differential levelling along the corridor between national benchmarks, with benchmarks every 250 to 500 m, accepting a closure of C√K mm; GNSS heights alone are not used for drainage gradients unless converted with a good geoid model and checked on benchmarks.
  11. Conclusion: GNSS for the frame, traverses for density and for the town, levelling for heights; every part closed on something known.
Evaluate Justify the use of a transition curve between a straight and a circular curve. For a road curve of radius 450 m and deflection angle 36°, with a design speed of 90 km/h, find the length of transition by the rate of change of radial acceleration (0.5 m/s³) and by the rate of applying superelevation (maximum 0.07, carriageway 7.5 m, raised at 1 in 150). Adopt a length and find the shift and the total tangent length. 14 marks

Points to cover. Why: radial acceleration and cant applied gradually; Ideal spiral r l = R Ls; Length by c; Equilibrium e, the cap, and length by cant; Shift and tangent length.

Model answer.

  1. Need for a transition: on a straight the radius is infinite and the radial acceleration zero; on the circular arc it is V² ÷ R. Without a transition the full acceleration, and the superelevation that balances it, would appear at the tangent point at once, giving a jerk and making the outer edge of the road rise abruptly. A transition increases the curvature steadily, so the acceleration grows at a controlled rate and the superelevation can be run in over its length.
  2. Other benefits: the vehicle follows the natural path of a driver turning the wheel steadily, the widening of the carriageway on the curve can be applied gradually, and the appearance of the road is smoother.
  3. Ideal transition: the radius r at a distance l from the tangent satisfies r l = R Ls, a clothoid; for setting out, the offsets are y = x³ ÷ (6 R Ls) (the cubic parabola).
  4. Length by the rate of change of radial acceleration: V = 90 ÷ 3.6 = 25.0 m/s. Ls = V³ ÷ (c R) = 15 625 ÷ (0.5 × 450) = 69.4 m.
  5. Equilibrium superelevation: e = V² ÷ (g R) = 625 ÷ (9.81 × 450) = 0.142. This exceeds the maximum of 0.07, so e = 0.07 is provided and the rest (0.142 − 0.07 = 0.072) is carried by side friction between tyre and road, which is well within what a dry road gives.
  6. Length by superelevation: the outer edge rises by e × width = 0.07 × 7.5 = 0.525 m. At a rate of 1 in 150 this needs 0.525 × 150 = 78.8 m.
  7. Adopt the larger value, rounded up: Ls = 80 m.
  8. Shift S = Ls² ÷ (24 R) = 6400 ÷ 10 800 = 0.59 m. The circular arc is moved inward by this amount to make room for the transitions.
  9. Total tangent length from I to the start of the transition = (R + S) tan(Δ/2) + Ls ÷ 2 = (450 + 0.59) × tan 18° + 40 = 450.59 × 0.32492 + 40 = 146.41 + 40 = 186.41 m.
  10. Check of the arc left between the transitions: each transition turns the direction through Ls ÷ (2R) rad = 80 ÷ 900 = 0.0889 rad = 5.09°, so the two together take 10.19° of the 36°, leaving 25.81° for the circular arc, a length of 450 × 0.4505 = 202.7 m. The arc is long enough, so the design works.
  11. Evaluation: governed here by superelevation, not by comfort; on a sharper curve or at a higher speed the comfort criterion would govern. A transition of 80 m costs little extra land and greatly improves safety at 90 km/h.

Glossary

Additive constant
C = f + d in the stadia formula; zero with an anallatic lens.
Anallatic lens
A lens in an external-focusing telescope that makes the additive constant zero.
Base line
The side of a triangulation network measured directly, with every correction.
Bowditch rule
Shares a traverse's closing error in proportion to the lengths of the lines.
Braced quadrilateral
Four triangulation stations with both diagonals observed.
Changing face
Transiting and swinging through 180° so the vertical circle is on the other side.
Closing error
The amount by which a traverse or levelling run fails to close; √(ΣDep² + ΣLat²) for a traverse.
Closing the horizon
Observing round a station back to the first direction, to check the sum of angles.
Deflection angle
The angle through which the forward straight turns from the back straight.
Degree of curve
The angle at the centre subtended by a 30 m arc or chord; R = 1718.9 ÷ D.
Departure
The east–west component of a line, l sin β.
Face left
The observation with the vertical circle on the observer's left.
Latitude
The north–south component of a line, l cos β.
Least count
The smallest reading a vernier gives: one main-scale division ÷ number of vernier divisions.
Least squares
The rule that the most probable values make Σwv² a minimum.
Line of collimation
The line through the intersection of the cross-hairs and the optical centre of the objective.
Most probable value
The best estimate of a quantity from its observations, given their weights.
Normal equation
One equation per unknown obtained by minimising Σwv².
Residual
An observation minus the most probable value.
Shift
The inward movement of a circular arc to fit transition curves; Ls² ÷ 24R.
Staff intercept
The difference between the top and bottom stadia hair readings.
Subtense bar
A horizontal bar of known length set square to the line of sight for measuring distance by angle.
Transiting
Turning the telescope through 180° about the horizontal axis.
Trilateration
Control by measuring all sides of a network's triangles.
Weight
A number proportional to an observation's reliability, w ∝ 1 ÷ σ².
Well-conditioned triangle
A triangle with no angle under about 30° or over 120°.
Zenith distance
The angle from the vertical to the line of sight; α = 90° − z.
