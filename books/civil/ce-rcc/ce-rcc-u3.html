3.1 Behaviour of RC beams in shear

Shear in an RC beam arrives as diagonal tension inside the web. Bending and shear act together; at the neutral axis the principal tensile stress equals the shear stress, acting at about 45° to the axis. Concrete is weak in tension, so a fine diagonal crack opens first. After that the beam stays up only because four things share the work: uncracked concrete above the crack, aggregate interlock, dowel action of the bars, and stirrups. Every shear rule in IS 456 comes from this picture.

You need Unit 1 for characteristic strength and partial safety factors, Unit 2 for the singly reinforced section and effective depth; stirrup design continues in 3.2 Design of shear reinforcement.

Shear arrives as diagonal tension

A beam section carries two stresses at once. Bending gives a horizontal stress σ, compression on top and tension at the bottom. Shear force V gives a shear stress τ, equal on horizontal and vertical planes. Combined, they produce a principal tensile stress inclined to the axis.

σ₁ = −σ/2 + √((σ/2)² + τ²)

σ₁ is the principal tensile stress at a point (MPa); σ is the bending stress there, positive in compression (MPa), so compression on top makes σ positive and the term −σ/2 reduces the tension; τ is the shear stress from τ = VQ/(Ib) (MPa); V is the shear force at the section (N); Q is the first moment of area of the part above the point about the neutral axis (mm³); I is the second moment of area (mm⁴); b is the width at that point (mm).

At the neutral axis σ = 0, so σ₁ = τ. This peak tension acts on a plane at 45° to the beam axis: diagonal tension. Concrete cracks when the principal tension passes its direct tensile strength, roughly 10% of f_ck.

Near the support the bending moment is small, so the crack is steep: a web-shear crack. Where the moment is also large, an existing flexural crack bends over into the diagonal: a flexure-shear crack. Both are diagonal tension.

Four things carry the shear after cracking

An unreinforced beam splits and drops as soon as the first diagonal crack opens. A reinforced beam survives because four mechanisms take the shear.

  • Uncracked concrete above the diagonal crack: the compression zone stays whole and carries roughly 20 to 40% of the shear, acting as the chord of a truss.
  • Aggregate interlock: the two rough faces of a narrow crack rub and pass shear as friction. It works only while the crack is narrow, roughly under 0.3 mm.
  • Dowel action: the longitudinal bars crossing the crack act as pins and transfer shear by bending locally. Bar yielding or splitting along the bars kills it.
  • Stirrups: vertical or inclined steel crosses the crack and takes tension directly. Before cracking it does little; after cracking it is the only dependable part.

The truss analogy assembles these parts. The compression zone is the top chord, the tension steel is the bottom chord, the concrete between diagonal cracks is a set of struts at about 45°, and the stirrups are vertical ties. Load travels down the struts and up the ties, so the beam behaves like a truss with a cracked web.

A stirrup works best when it crosses a crack at right angles. A vertical stirrup meets a 45° crack at 45°, so only the component of its tension acting across the crack, T sin 45° ≈ 0.707 T, resists the crack, where T is the stirrup tension in N. An inclined stirrup at 45° crosses the crack at 90°, all of its tension acts across the crack, and it is √2 times as effective for the same area. The design formula is in 3.2.

Failure modes, τ_v, τ_c and the critical section

  • Diagonal tension (web shear) failure: a sudden diagonal crack splits the web where no stirrup crosses it. Brittle, no warning.
  • Shear-compression failure: the diagonal crack grows into the compression zone above the support or under the load, and concrete crushes after the stirrups yield. This one gives warning.
  • Web crushing: the diagonal strut crushes in a thin web under heavy shear. Widening the web or raising the concrete grade is the only cure; extra stirrups do not help.

τ_v = V_u / (b_w × d)

τ_v is the nominal shear stress (N/mm², that is MPa); V_u is the factored shear force at the section (N); b_w is the width of the web (mm), equal to the full width b for a rectangular beam; d is the effective depth (mm).

The concrete's own share is τ_c, read from IS 456 Table 19. It rises with concrete grade and with p_t = 100 A_st/(b_w d), the percentage of tension steel, where A_st is the area of tension steel in mm². More steel means a stiffer section, a narrower crack and better aggregate interlock. For M25 with p_t ≈ 0.9%, τ_c = 0.62 MPa, interpolated from Table 19.

The upper limit τ_c,max depends only on the grade: 2.8 MPa for M20, 3.1 MPa for M25, 3.5 MPa for M30. Three checks follow. If τ_v ≤ τ_c, minimum stirrups are enough. If τ_c < τ_v ≤ τ_c,max, design stirrups for V_us = (τ_v − τ_c) b_w d, where V_us is the shear carried by the shear reinforcement in N. If τ_v > τ_c,max, enlarge the section or raise the concrete grade.

The shear used is not the reaction at the support, but the shear at the critical section, a distance d from the face of the support. Closer than that, the load arches directly into the support through a short concrete strut, the full diagonal crack cannot form, and the shear there is not critical.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular RC beam has width b = 300 mm, overall depth D = 600 mm and effective depth d = 550 mm. The tension steel is A_st = 1485 mm², so p_t = 100 × 1485/165,000 = 0.9%. The factored shear at the critical section is V_u = 180 kN. Concrete is M25, with design shear strength τ_c = 0.62 MPa and maximum shear stress τ_c,max = 3.1 MPa. Find τ_v and state whether shear reinforcement is required.

  1. Write the data: b_w = 300 mm, d = 550 mm, V_u = 180 kN, τ_c = 0.62 MPa, τ_c,max = 3.1 MPa.
  2. Convert the shear to newtons: V_u = 180 × 10³ = 180,000 N.
  3. Compute the concrete area resisting shear: b_w d = 300 × 550 = 165,000 mm².
  4. Nominal shear stress: τ_v = V_u/(b_w d) = 180,000/165,000 = 1.0909 MPa, about 1.09 MPa.
  5. Compare with the concrete strength: τ_c = 0.62 MPa < τ_v = 1.09 MPa, so concrete alone cannot carry the shear and stirrups are needed.
  6. Compare with the upper limit: τ_v = 1.09 MPa < τ_c,max = 3.1 MPa, so the web will not crush and the section size is kept.
  7. Shear to be carried by the stirrups: V_us = (τ_v − τ_c) b_w d = (1.0909 − 0.62) × 165,000 = 77,700 N = 77.7 kN, using the unrounded τ_v.

Output

1.09
77.7

Answer. τ_v = 1.09 MPa; since 0.62 < 1.09 < 3.1 MPa, the section is safe against web crushing but concrete alone is not enough, so vertical stirrups must carry V_us = 77.7 kN.

Exam traps

  • Using the maximum shear at the support face instead of V_u at the critical section, d away. This over-designs and loses marks.
  • Treating τ_c as fixed. It depends on concrete grade and p_t = 100 A_st/(b_w d); the same M25 section gives a different τ_c when the tension steel changes.
  • Mixing units: with V_u in kN, τ_v = V_u × 10³/(b_w d) in N/mm². Forgetting 10³ gives an answer 1000 times too small.
  • Thinking τ_c,max is added to τ_c, or that more stirrups rescue a section with τ_v > τ_c,max. Beyond τ_c,max only a bigger section or a higher grade helps.
  • Using overall depth D instead of effective depth d in τ_v = V_u/(b_w d).

3.2 Design of shear reinforcement

Concrete alone carries only a limited diagonal tension. When the factored shear V_u exceeds the concrete's shear strength, the beam needs shear reinforcement. Vertical stirrups, or bent-up bars, carry the excess. The designer computes the nominal shear stress τ_v, checks it against the concrete's upper limit τ_c,max, subtracts the concrete contribution V_uc, and sizes stirrups from V_us = 0.87 f_y A_sv d / s_v. The result is a spacing s_v that is practical, safe and within code limits. Every beam in a building or bridge uses this check.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for τ_v and τ_c, and Unit 2 for the beam section and effective depth.

The idea: concrete plus steel

Shear in a beam is diagonal tension. Chapter 3.1 explains how cracks form and how concrete, aggregate interlock, dowel action and stirrups share the force. The code gives the concrete a design shear strength τc, which depends on the concrete grade and the percentage of tension steel. For a rectangular section, the concrete contribution is Vuc = τc bw d. Here bw is the web width in mm and d is the effective depth in mm. Vuc is in N when τc is in N/mm² (MPa). Divide by 1000 to get kN.

τv = Vu / (bw d); Vuc = τc bw d; Vus = Vu − Vuc

τv is the nominal shear stress in MPa; Vu is the factored shear in N; bw is the web width in mm; d is the effective depth in mm; τc is the concrete design shear strength in MPa; Vuc is the concrete shear capacity in N; Vus is the shear carried by steel in N.

The applied factored shear is Vu. It produces the nominal shear stress τv = Vu / (bw d). Keep Vu in N in this formula. If τv exceeds τc,max, the web is too thin and must be enlarged; stirrups cannot repair a crushed web. If Vu is greater than Vuc, the excess Vus = Vu − Vuc is carried by shear reinforcement. If Vu is not greater than Vuc, nominal stirrups are still provided.

Some syllabi call the whole procedure 'design for shear'. They also call the two strengths 'design shear strength with and without shear reinforcement'. The first is τc; the second is τc plus the steel term. Torsion adds another shear stress; it is handled in Chapter 3.3.

Sizing vertical stirrups

Vertical stirrups cross the diagonal crack and carry Vus. Use two-legged stirrups in narrow beams and four-legged stirrups in wide beams. Asv is the total area of all stirrup legs that cross the crack. For two-legged 8 mm stirrups, Asv = 2 × 50.3 = 100.6 mm².

  1. Compute Vu from the load combination and find τv = Vu/(bwd).
  2. Check τv ≤ τc,max. If it fails, increase the section; do not proceed with stirrup design.
  3. Find Vuc = τc bw d and the steel demand Vus = Vu − Vuc.
  4. Choose the stirrup diameter and number of legs, then get Asv.
  5. Compute sv = 0.87 fy Asv d / Vus. Use Vus in N; if Vus is in kN, multiply it by 1000.
  6. Round sv down to a practical value and check the minimum steel and maximum spacing rules.
Vus = 0.87 fy Asv d / sv; sv = 0.87 fy Asv d / Vus

Vus is the shear carried by stirrups in N; fy is the characteristic yield strength of steel in MPa; Asv is the total area of stirrup legs in mm²; d is the effective depth in mm; sv is the stirrup spacing in mm. The factor 0.87 is 1/1.15, the steel partial safety factor.

The spacing formula comes from Vus = 0.87 fy Asv d / sv. fy is in MPa, Asv in mm², d in mm and sv in mm. Stirrup legs must be anchored around the top bars; anchorage is covered in Chapter 3.5.

Minimum steel, maximum spacing and bent-up bars

Even when Vus is zero or small, the code requires minimum shear reinforcement. It controls cracking, holds the longitudinal bars and gives warning before failure. The usual requirement is Asv/(bw sv) ≥ 0.4/(0.87 fy). The left side is the stirrup area per unit length. The right side is the minimum ratio for the steel grade.

Asv/(bw sv) ≥ 0.4/(0.87 fy)

Asv is the total stirrup leg area in mm²; bw is the web width in mm; sv is the spacing in mm; fy is the steel yield strength in MPa. The ratio is dimensionless.

Maximum spacing is the lesser of 0.75d and 300 mm in the general case. Near curtailment points the rules are tighter; see Chapter 3.6. Detailing rules for cover, bar spacing and stirrup shape are in Chapter 3.7.

  • Bent-up bars inclined at α can carry 0.87 fy Asv sin α. The code limits the share they take, and their anchorage must be checked.
  • Vertical stirrups are preferred in thin webs and in regions of high shear.
  • If the calculated spacing is 257 mm, round down to 250 mm, not up to 260 mm.

Worked example

Question. Design vertical stirrups for a rectangular beam with b = 300 mm, d = 550 mm and factored shear V_u = 180 kN. Use M25 concrete with τ_c = 0.62 MPa and τ_c,max = 3.1 MPa. Use Fe415 steel and two-legged 8 mm stirrups. Find the required spacing and state the spacing to be provided.

  1. Compute the nominal shear stress: τ_v = V_u/(b d) = 180 × 10³ / (300 × 550) = 1.09 MPa.
  2. Check the upper limit: τ_v = 1.09 MPa ≤ τ_c,max = 3.1 MPa. The web is adequate.
  3. Find the concrete shear capacity: V_uc = τ_c b d / 1000 = 0.62 × 300 × 550 / 1000 = 102.3 kN.
  4. Find the shear to be carried by stirrups: V_us = V_u − V_uc = 180 − 102.3 = 77.7 kN.
  5. Area of two-legged 8 mm stirrups: A_sv = 2 × 50.3 = 100.6 mm².
  6. Required spacing: s_v = 0.87 f_y A_sv d / V_us = 0.87 × 415 × 100.6 × 550 / (77.7 × 10³) = 257.1 mm.
  7. Maximum spacing = min(0.75 × 550, 300) = min(412.5, 300) = 300 mm. The calculated 257.1 mm is below this limit.
  8. Round down to a practical spacing: provide 250 mm. This is below 257.1 mm and below 300 mm, so it is safe.

Answer. Provide two-legged 8 mm stirrups at 250 mm centres; τ_v = 1.09 MPa is below τ_c,max = 3.1 MPa and the calculated spacing 257.1 mm is rounded down to 250 mm.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting minimum shear reinforcement when V_us is small or zero. The code still requires stirrups.
  • Checking only τ_v ≤ τ_c,max and not providing steel for V_u > V_uc. The first check protects the web; the second gives the steel.
  • Mixing N and kN in the spacing formula. V_us in kN must be multiplied by 1000 before dividing.
  • Using τ_c,max as the concrete design strength for V_uc. τ_c,max is only the upper crush limit; V_uc uses τ_c.
  • Rounding the stirrup spacing up. Always round down, and also satisfy the maximum spacing limit.

3.3 Design for torsion

A beam can twist as well as bend and shear. Torsion is the twisting moment Tu. It adds shear stresses all around the section, so a beam designed only for bending and shear may crack on the side face. IS 456 avoids a full torsion theory: it converts Tu into an equivalent shear Ve and an equivalent moment Mt. The beam is then designed for Ve with closed stirrups and for Mt with longitudinal bars. This chapter shows the conversion, the units and the checks, using a 300 mm × 600 mm beam carrying 30 kN·m.

You need Chapter 3.1 for diagonal tension, Chapter 3.2 for stirrup design, and Unit 2 for flexural steel.

Torsion as circulating shear

Torsion is twisting. A torque Tu, in kN·m, makes shear stresses circulate around a beam section. These stresses are largest near the perimeter and zero at the centre. A thin-walled open section, such as an I-section, twists easily; a closed box or a closed stirrup cage resists the circulation. A solid rectangle is not an open section. Two cases matter. Equilibrium torsion is needed for static equilibrium: a cantilever slab twisting its edge beam, or a curved bridge girder. The full Tu must be designed for. Compatibility torsion comes from restraint: a beam monolithic with a slab cannot twist freely, so the slab attracts torsion. After cracking, the beam loses torsional stiffness and the moment redistributes; many codes allow a reduced design value. Do not mix the two. The diagonal tension background is in Chapter 3.1.

Equivalent shear and moment

IS 456 does not ask you to solve a full torsion problem. It replaces Tu by two ordinary actions: an equivalent shear Ve and an equivalent moment Mt. The section is then designed as a beam in shear and bending.

Ve = Vu + 1.6 Tu / b

Vu is factored shear (kN); Tu is torsional moment (kN·m); b is breadth (m); Ve is equivalent shear (kN). If b is in mm, use Ve = Vu + 1600 Tu / b.

Mt = Tu(1 + D/b) / 1.7

D is overall depth; b and D are in the same units, so D/b is dimensionless; Mt is in kN·m when Tu is in kN·m. A deep beam gets a larger Mt.

  1. Convert Tu, b and D to consistent units.
  2. Compute Ve and Mt.
  3. Check τve = Ve/(b d) ≤ τc,max from IS 456 Table 20.
  4. Design closed stirrups for Ve.
  5. Design longitudinal bars for Mu + Mt.

The figure shows this flow for Vu = 120 kN and Tu = 30 kN·m.

Closed stirrups and corner bars

Transverse steel for torsion must be closed stirrups, with ends properly anchored. Ordinary open stirrups do not enclose the concrete and cannot resist the circulating shear. Use the shear route in Chapter 3.2 with Ve in place of Vu. For a two-legged closed stirrup, Asv is the area of both legs; spacing sv follows Vus = 0.87 fy Asv d / sv. Also limit sv to the least of x1, (x1 + y1)/4 and 300 mm, where x1 and y1 are the short and long dimensions of the closed stirrup. Place longitudinal bars for Mt at the corners of the stirrups, at least one bar in each corner, so the torsion steel forms a closed cage. If τve exceeds τc,max, the concrete cannot take the diagonal compression; enlarge the section or increase concrete grade. Do not merely add stirrups. Curtailment and anchorage of these bars follows Chapter 3.5 and Chapter 3.6.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular beam has b = 300 mm, D = 600 mm, effective depth d = 550 mm, factored shear Vu = 120 kN and factored torsional moment Tu = 30 kN·m at a section. Find Ve and Mt. State what each reinforcement is designed for.

  1. Convert b to metres: b = 0.3 m. The ratio D/b = 600/300 = 2.0.
  2. Equivalent shear: Ve = 120 + 1.6 × 30 / 0.3 = 120 + 160 = 280 kN. Using b in mm: Ve = 120 + 1600 × 30 / 300 = 280 kN.
  3. Equivalent moment: Mt = 30 × (1 + 2.0) / 1.7 = 30 × 3 / 1.7 = 52.94 ≈ 52.9 kN·m.
  4. Check equivalent shear stress: τve = 280 × 103 / (300 × 550) = 1.70 N/mm2. For M25 concrete, τc,max = 3.1 N/mm2 (IS 456 Table 20). Since 1.70 N/mm2 is less than 3.1 N/mm2, the section is adequate in diagonal compression.
  5. Design closed stirrups for Ve = 280 kN. Design longitudinal corner bars for Mu + Mt = Mu + 52.9 kN·m.

Answer. Ve = 280 kN and Mt = 52.9 kN·m, so design stirrups for 280 kN and longitudinal bars for Mu + 52.9 kN·m.

Exam traps

  • Using Tu in kN·m with b in mm and forgetting the 1000 factor; or mixing metres and millimetres in D/b.
  • Treating compatibility torsion as equilibrium torsion and reducing a moment that statics requires.
  • Designing open stirrups or forgetting corner bars; torsion needs a closed cage.
  • Skipping τve ≤ τc,max and adding stirrups to a section that is too small.
  • Designing longitudinal steel for Mu alone and forgetting to add Mt.

3.4 Combined bending, shear and torsion

When bending, shear and torsion act together, their stresses add. IS 456 avoids a complex interaction formula: it turns factored torque Tu into an equivalent moment Mt and an equivalent shear Ve. Flexural steel is designed for Mu + Mt, stirrups for Ve, and the section is checked for equivalent shear stress. Edge beams, spandrel beams, curved girders and eccentrically loaded beams need this. Only a few universities teach it as a separate advanced chapter.

You need: Chapter 3.3 for Mt and Ve, Chapter 3.2 for stirrups, and Unit 2 for flexural design.

Equivalent loads

Torsion creates shear stresses around the perimeter. Bending shear also creates shear stress. IS 456 replaces torque by two ordinary actions, so familiar flexure and shear design can be used.

Mt = Tu (1 + D/b) / 1.7

Tu is factored torque in kN·m; b is width in mm; D is overall depth in mm; Mt is equivalent moment in kN·m.

Ve = Vu + 1.6 Tu / b

Vu is factored shear in kN; b is width in m when Tu is in kN·m; Ve is equivalent shear in kN.

The factors 1.7 and 1.6 are calibrated. This is not a simple addition of actions. After design, check τve = Ve/(b d) ≤ τc,max. Here τve is equivalent shear stress in MPa, b and d are in mm, Ve is in N, and τc,max comes from IS 456 Table 20. If τve is close to τc,max, deepen the beam rather than crowd stirrups.

Design sequence

  1. Find factored Mu, Vu, Tu. Use Tu in kN·m and b in m for Ve.
  2. Compute Mt and Ve. Design longitudinal steel for Md = Mu + Mt by Unit 2; put bars at corners and side faces.
  3. Design closed stirrups for Ve, not Vu, using Chapter 3.2.
  4. Check τve = Ve/(b d) ≤ τc,max. If it fails, enlarge the section or raise concrete grade.
  5. Detail corner bars, side-face steel and loops as in Chapter 3.7.

Torsion shear reverses around the perimeter, so closed stirrups and corner bars are essential. The equivalent method is conservative; it lets a designer use ordinary beam charts. Torsion still controls detailing. Torsion cracks spiral around the beam, so longitudinal bars need end anchorage and closed stirrups need proper hooks. Some syllabi call this design for combined bending, shear and torsion.

Worked example

Question. A beam has b = 300 mm, D = 600 mm, d = 550 mm, Mu = 150 kN·m, Vu = 120 kN and Tu = 30 kN·m. Take M25 concrete with τc,max = 3.1 MPa. Find Md, Ve and τve, and check the section.

Answer. Md = 202.94 kN·m, Ve = 280 kN and τve = 1.70 MPa; τve < τc,max = 3.1 MPa.

  • Designing stirrups only for Vu. Use Ve.
  • Swapping b and D in the Mt formula.
  • Skipping the τc,max check after torsion is added.
  • Adding Mu and Mt with different units.

3.5 Bond, anchorage and development length

A reinforced concrete beam works only if steel and concrete move together. Bond is the shear stress at their interface that transfers force from concrete to a bar. Without bond the bar would slide out and the section would fail before the steel yields. Development length L_d is the embedded length needed to build up the bar force safely. Designers check it at supports, at curtailment points and wherever a bar must carry its full tension. This chapter covers bond stress, IS 456 development length, anchorage and the support check.

You need: shear at supports from Chapter 3.1 and Chapter 3.2; partial safety factors from Unit 1; bar stress from Unit 2.

What bond is and why bars are anchored

Bond is the shear stress transferred across the steel–concrete interface. When a bar is pulled, the concrete grips its surface and the force travels from steel into concrete over a length. Two names are used. Flexural bond is the change of bar force along a beam because the bending moment changes. Anchorage bond is the transfer at an end, where a bar must be held so it does not pull out. Both are the same physical action.

  • Adhesion: chemical grip between concrete and steel.
  • Friction: resistance to sliding along the bar surface.
  • Mechanical interlock: ribs on deformed bars bear against concrete.

Deformed bars rely mostly on interlock. Plain bars rely on adhesion and friction, so they need hooks or bends. Average bond stress over an embedded length L is τ_bd = F/(π φ L). F is the bar force in N, φ is the bar diameter in mm, L is the embedment in mm, and τ_bd is the bond stress in N/mm². Real bond stress is not uniform: it is high near a crack and low elsewhere. IS 456 uses an average design bond stress that depends on concrete grade and bar surface. For M25 concrete, IS 456 gives τ_bd = 1.4 N/mm² for plain bars in tension. For deformed bars in tension the value is 1.6 times larger: τ_bd = 1.4 × 1.6 = 2.24 N/mm².

Bond failure can be pull-out, where the bar slides, or splitting, where the concrete splits along the bar because cover is small. Splitting is why cover, spacing and transverse steel matter. A bar near a surface has less concrete to confine it. Some syllabi call this topic bond failure and strength.

Development length from bond stress

At ultimate, the force in a tension bar is F = σ_s π φ²/4, where σ_s is the bar stress and φ is the diameter. Put this into the bond equation over the development length L_d: τ_bd = (σ_s π φ²/4)/(π φ L_d). Cancel π and one φ: τ_bd = σ_s φ/(4 L_d). Rearrange:

Ld = φ σs / (4 τbd)

Ld is the development length in mm; φ is the bar diameter in mm; σs is the bar stress to develop in N/mm², taken as 0.87 fy in tension; τbd is the design bond stress in N/mm²; fy is the characteristic yield strength of steel in N/mm².

The factor 0.87 is the code's ultimate steel stress for a tension bar. For Fe415 steel, σ_s = 0.87 × 415 = 361.05 N/mm². A smaller bar gives a smaller L_d because L_d is directly proportional to φ. Four 10 mm bars have total area 4 × 78.54 = 314.16 mm², equal to one 20 mm bar, and their total perimeter is 4 × π × 10 = 125.7 mm versus π × 20 = 62.8 mm for the single 20 mm bar. So the group develops in a shorter length. Deformed bars have a higher τ_bd than plain bars, so their L_d is shorter.

  1. Choose the bar diameter φ, steel grade f_y and concrete grade.
  2. Read the design bond stress τ_bd for that concrete grade and bar type.
  3. Compute σ_s = 0.87 f_y for a tension bar.
  4. Compute L_d = φ σ_s/(4 τ_bd) in mm. Round upward for detailing.
  5. Check that the available embedment on the correct side of the critical section is at least L_d.

Anchorage at supports and curtailment points

At a simple support the bending moment is near zero, but the bar still carries force from the adjacent span. IS 456 limits the bar diameter so that L_d does not exceed M1/V + L0. M1 is the moment of resistance of the section at the support in N·mm, V is the shear at that section in N, and L0 is the anchorage length beyond the face of the support in mm. The term M1/V is the distance over which the bar force can be built up by the moment gradient. L0 is the physical length inside the support. Use the same units throughout.

At least one-third of the total tension steel required for positive moment at midspan should extend into the support. This keeps the support section tough and provides anchorage. A standard hook or bend gives extra anchorage by bearing and friction. IS 456 allows a hook to count for plain bars; for deformed bars, the straight length is usually enough. Do not add a hook to L_d unless the code permits it.

At a curtailment point, a bar that is no longer needed for bending must still be developed. Extend the bar beyond the theoretical cut-off by L_d. Without this extension the bar can slip at the section where it is still stressed. Curtailment rules and lap splices are covered in Chapter 3.6. Buildable anchorage details are in Chapter 3.7.

Typical checks are beam bottom bars entering a column, slab bars at a support, and footing starter bars. In each case the question is the same: is there enough embedded length on the tension side of the critical section to develop the bar force without pull-out or splitting?

Worked example

Question. Find the development length of a 16 mm Fe415 deformed bar in M25 concrete in tension. Take τ_bd = 2.24 N/mm² and σ_s = 0.87 f_y. State the check needed at a simple support.

  1. Data: φ = 16 mm, f_y = 415 N/mm², τ_bd = 2.24 N/mm².
  2. Bar stress: σ_s = 0.87 × 415 = 361.05 N/mm².
  3. Use L_d = φ σ_s/(4 τ_bd).
  4. L_d = 16 × 361.05/(4 × 2.24) = 5776.8/8.96 = 644.73 mm.
  5. Round to 645 mm, or 0.645 m.
  6. Support check: L_d ≤ M1/V + L0, and at least one-third of the midspan positive moment steel must extend into the support.

Answer. L_d ≈ 644.7 mm (0.645 m), and this length must satisfy L_d ≤ M1/V + L0 at the simple support.

Exam traps

  • Using f_y instead of 0.87 f_y in L_d. The code stress at ultimate is 0.87 f_y in tension.
  • Using the bond stress for plain bars with deformed bars, or the reverse. Deformed bars have higher τ_bd and shorter L_d.
  • Forgetting that L_d must be available on the correct side of the critical section, not just somewhere near the support.
  • Treating the support width as L_d. The simple-support check uses M1/V + L0, not support width alone.
  • Confusing development length with lap length. A lap splice transfers force between two bars and is covered in Chapter 3.6.

3.6 Lap splices and curtailment of bars

Reinforcement is supplied in fixed lengths, so bars must be joined; a lap splice overlaps two bars so force passes from one to the other through bond. Curtailment stops bars where bending moment has fallen, saving steel, but only after development length is satisfied. Both ideas protect the same thing: every bar force must have a safe path into concrete. They appear in beam schedules, bar bending schedules and site checks. Only a few universities teach this as a separate chapter.

You need: Chapter 3.5 for Ld and Unit 2 for the bending moment envelope.

Lap splices

What a lap is. A lap splice overlaps two bars over a length Llap. Force leaves the ending bar through bond into concrete and enters the continuing bar over the same overlap. A 16 m beam cannot use one 12 m stock bar, so a joint is needed. The lap needs concrete around both bars and ties to hold them. Some syllabi call this splicing of reinforcement. A lap joins bars within a span; it does not replace end anchorage at a support.

Llap ≥ max(Ld, 30φ)

Llap is the overlap length in mm; Ld is the development length from Chapter 3.5 in mm; φ is the bar diameter in mm. 30φ is the flexural tension minimum.

In flexural tension, Llap is the larger of Ld and 30φ. In compression the demand is lower: a compression lap is at least max(compression Ld, 24φ). When diameters differ, use the larger bar diameter, which gives the greater development length.

Stagger adjacent laps. If all laps fall at one section, that section has many interrupted bars. Keep clear spacing and do not crowd a lap with other steel.

Curtailment of bars

Theoretical cut-off. Bending moment falls from midspan toward a support. Extra bars can stop where the remaining bars alone carry the design moment. Find that point from the bending moment envelope, which covers all load arrangements. It is the theoretical cut-off.

Shift by max(Ld, 12φ). At the theoretical cut-off the bar still carries force. That force needs bond length. Move the actual cut-off by at least max(Ld, 12φ) toward the low-moment end, so the bar continues that length beyond the theoretical point. A cut exactly at zero moment is wrong: zero moment does not mean zero bar force.

Cut in steps. Curtail one pair, check the remaining section, then the next. Each step changes steel area, anchorage and shear. Provide stirrups near a cut-off because the tensile force changes abruptly. The figure shows a 6 m beam with two full-length bars and two bars from 1.5 m to 4.5 m. Detail the final shape as in Chapter 3.7.

Worked example

Question. A 6 m simply supported beam has four 16 mm bars at midspan. Two bars are curtailed 1.5 m from each support, leaving a 3 m central length. For a 16 mm bar, Ld = 1.03 m. Check a 1.2 m lap when the flexural tension minimum is 30φ. State the extension beyond the theoretical cut-off.

  1. φ = 16 mm. Minimum lap = 30φ = 30 × 16 = 480 mm = 0.48 m.
  2. Ld = 1.03 m = 1030 mm.
  3. Required lap = max(1030, 480) = 1030 mm = 1.03 m.
  4. Provided lap = 1.2 m = 1200 mm. 1200 > 1030, so the lap is adequate; spare = 170 mm = 0.17 m.
  5. At a cut-off the extension is max(Ld, 12φ) = max(1030, 12 × 16) = max(1030, 192) = 1030 mm = 1.03 m. With the actual end at 1.5 m from the support, the theoretical point can be at 1.5 + 1.03 = 2.53 m from the support.

Answer. The 1.2 m lap is adequate (required 1.03 m), and each curtailed bar needs max(Ld, 12φ) = 1.03 m of extension beyond its theoretical cut-off.

Exam traps

  • Cutting a bar at the theoretical point of zero moment instead of shifting the cut-off by Ld.
  • Using a lap shorter than Ld, or forgetting the 30φ minimum in flexural tension.
  • Placing all laps at one section instead of staggering them.
  • Treating a compression lap with the tension minimum, or using the smaller bar diameter when bars differ.

3.7 Detailing of reinforcement

Detailing turns the steel calculated for bending, shear and torsion into a drawing a site team can build. Every bar gets a diameter, position, length, shape and anchorage. The drawing must keep cover, bar spacing, stirrup spacing, hooks, bends, development length and lap lengths within code limits. A correct design with poor detailing can crack, spall or fail at a lap. Some syllabi call this 'detailing requirements'. It is used on every beam, slab and column drawing and in the bar bending schedule that the contractor uses to cut and bend steel.

You need: Chapter 3.2 for stirrups, 3.3 for torsion steel, 3.5 for Ld and 3.6 for laps and curtailment.

Cover and clear spacing

Cover is concrete between the surface and the steel. It protects steel from water and fire and gives bond. Nominal cover is the cover written on the drawing, measured to the outer surface of the outermost steel, usually the stirrup. Clear cover is the least actual concrete thickness to a bar surface. For a main bar inside a stirrup, clear cover = nominal cover + stirrup diameter. Do not use nominal cover as clear cover in spacing checks.

For n bars in one layer in a beam of width b, the clear spacing s is s = (b − 2c − 2φs − nφ)/(n − 1). Here b is the beam width in mm, c is nominal cover to the outside of the stirrup in mm, φs is stirrup bar diameter in mm, φ is main bar diameter in mm and n is the number of bars. The minimum clear spacing is the greatest of φ, 25 mm and (maximum aggregate size + 5 mm).

When bars are in two layers, the vertical clear gap between layers must be at least the greater of 15 mm and the nominal maximum size of coarse aggregate. Keep the largest bars at the corners. If spacing fails, increase width, use a larger bar with fewer bars, or put bars in two layers with a clear gap meeting that limit. The worked example below checks a 300 mm beam with four 16 mm bars.

Stirrups, hooks and bends

A stirrup is a closed tie. Its spacing is closest near the support, where shear is high, and may increase toward mid-span as shear falls. Show the first stirrup at 50 mm from the support face unless the drawing gives another value. At a curtailment point, keep stirrups close on both sides because a stopped bar needs anchorage and the section is disturbed.

Hooks transfer force into concrete. A 90° bend alone can open under load; use a 135° hook for stirrups in beams, with the hook enclosing a longitudinal bar. Bend bars around a mandrel. Sharp bends crush concrete inside the bend and crack the bar. Code rules give a minimum internal bend radius as a multiple of bar diameter, larger for larger bars. Do not rebend a bar at the same place on site.

The drawing shows stirrup diameter, shape, hook length and spacing mark. A common mark is '8 φ @ 150 c/c near support, 8 φ @ 200 c/c at mid-span'. Define φ as bar diameter and c/c as centre-to-centre. The schedule gives each stirrup's cutting length: centreline perimeter plus hook allowance. Check that the stirrup fits inside cover and around the main bars.

Anchorage, laps and the bar schedule

A bar force cannot stop at a section. It must be carried into concrete by development length Ld, or into another bar by a lap. Ld comes from Chapter 3.5. A tension lap is at least the greater of Ld and 30φ unless a code rule says otherwise; a compression lap is shorter. Show every lap length as a dimension on the drawing, not as a note 'lap'. Stagger laps: do not put all laps at one section.

Curtailment stops a bar where bending moment has fallen. The bar must extend beyond the theoretical cut-off by Ld, and enough bars must continue to the support. Keep some bottom steel continuous into the support as the code requires in a beam. For a cantilever, top steel must develop into the support. The drawing marks the cut-off point and the extension.

  1. Draw in this order: (1) mark beam size, cover and stirrup; (2) place main bars to scale and check clear spacing; (3) mark stirrup spacing zones; (4) dimension Ld, laps and curtailment; (5) write bar marks; (6) fill the bar bending schedule.

A bar bending schedule lists bar mark, diameter, shape, number, cutting length and weight. Weight per metre = φ²/162 kg/m for φ in mm. For a 16 mm bar this is about 1.58 kg/m. The BBS and drawing must agree: every bar on the drawing appears in the schedule with a length and a position. The contractor cuts and bends from the BBS, so one missing lap length becomes a site error.

Worked example

Question. A beam is 300 mm wide with four 16 mm bars in one layer, 8 mm stirrups and 25 mm nominal cover to the outside of the stirrup. Check the clear spacing between bars and state if the layout is valid.

  1. Width inside stirrups = b − 2c − 2φs = 300 − 2×25 − 2×8 = 234 mm.
  2. Total width of four main bars = nφ = 4×16 = 64 mm.
  3. Clear spacing = (234 − 64)/(4 − 1) = 170/3 = 56.7 mm.
  4. For normal 20 mm aggregate, minimum clear spacing = max(φ, 25 mm, aggregate + 5 mm) = max(16, 25, 25) = 25 mm.
  5. 56.7 mm is greater than 25 mm, so the bars fit with a clear gap.

Answer. The clear spacing is 56.7 mm, which is greater than the 25 mm minimum, so the layout is valid.

Exam traps

  • Using nominal cover as clear cover. If the drawing says 25 mm nominal and the stirrup is 8 mm, the clear cover to the main bar is 33 mm, not 25 mm.
  • Forgetting the stirrup diameter in spacing: subtract 2c and 2φs before dividing by (n − 1).
  • Showing a lap as a note without its length. A lap has a dimension and a stagger.
  • Curtailing a bar exactly at the theoretical cut-off. Extend it by Ld beyond that point.
  • Mixing weight formula units: φ²/162 gives kg/m only when φ is in mm.

Takeaways

  1. Shear cracks are diagonal tension cracks; the beam survives them through concrete in compression, aggregate interlock, dowel action and stirrups.
  2. Design shear is checked at d from the support face, using τ_v against τ_c, and stirrups take only what concrete cannot.
  3. Stirrups must be provided even when the concrete alone is strong enough, to hold the beam together and control crack width.
  4. There is a hard ceiling τ_c,max; if τ_v crosses it, only a larger section works.
  5. Torsion is handled by converting the torque into an equivalent shear and an equivalent moment, so the same shear and flexural design tools apply.
  6. Bond is the only reason steel and concrete act together; without enough embedment the bar pulls out instead of yielding.
  7. Development length depends on bar diameter and bar stress, not on the length of the beam or the size of the load.
  8. Curtailment and splicing are where real beams fail: the moment diagram, the development length and the code's extension rules must all be satisfied at the cut.

Remember and understand

Understand Explain how a reinforced concrete beam without any shear reinforcement resists shear. Name each contribution and say which one is lost once a wide crack opens. 12 marks

Uncracked stage: shear stress τ = VQ/(Ib), principal tension at 45°; Crack forms when principal tensile stress exceeds concrete tensile strength; Compression zone above the crack carries shear; Aggregate interlock across crack faces; Dowel action of longitudinal bars crossing the crack; Stirrups, where present, act as the fourth mechanism; Aggregate interlock and dowel action drop sharply as crack width grows.

Understand Define nominal shear stress and design shear strength of concrete. Explain why τ_v is compared with τ_c and what the designer must do if τ_v exceeds τ_c,max. 10 marks

τ_v = V_u/(b d), with terms defined and units; τ_c from the code table, function of grade of concrete and p_t = 100A_st/(b d); Explain that τ_c is the concrete contribution, so the excess goes to stirrups; τ_c,max is the crushing limit of the diagonal strut; If τ_v > τ_c,max, redesign the section (increase b or D); more stirrups do not help.

Understand Explain what bond is between reinforcing steel and concrete, where it comes from, and why a bar must be embedded a development length before it can carry its full force. 12 marks

Bond as shear at the steel–concrete interface; Sources: adhesion, friction, mechanical interlock of ribs; Flexural bond versus anchorage bond; τ_bd = F/(π φ L) derived from force balance; Setting τ_bd equal to the permissible value gives L_d = φ σ_s/(4 τ_bd); L_d independent of the load, depends on bar diameter and stress; Consequence: a small bar needs a shorter embedment than a large one at the same stress.

Understand State the code rules for lap length in tension and in compression, and explain why laps are staggered rather than bunched at one section. 8 marks

Lap length is at least the development length L_d; Longer minimum for direct tension than for flexural tension; Compression laps smaller than tension laps; Laps staggered so that a large fraction of the steel is not spliced at one section; Reason: a spliced section is weaker in bond and concentrates cracking; Small bars lap better than large bars; large bars need mechanical couplers or welding.

Apply

Apply A simply supported beam of width 250 mm and effective depth 450 mm carries a factored shear of 150 kN at the support. Take f_ck = 25 N/mm², f_y = 415 N/mm², and tension steel of three 20 mm bars. Design the vertical stirrups and give the spacing layout along the span. 14 marks

Compute τ_v = V_u/(b d) and p_t = 100A_st/(b d); Read τ_c and τ_c,max from the code tables and check the section; V_us = V_u − τ_c b d; Choose two-legged 8 mm stirrups, A_sv = 2 × 50.3 mm²; s_v = 0.87 f_y A_sv d / V_us; Check s_v against minimum shear reinforcement and against the maximum spacing limit; Vary spacing in zones from the shear force diagram; round down to a practical value; Sketch the layout with the first stirrup close to the support.

Apply A beam of width 300 mm and overall depth 600 mm carries, at a section, a factored shear of 180 kN and a factored torque of 40 kN m. Compute the equivalent shear and equivalent moment and explain how the stirrups and longitudinal steel are then designed. 14 marks

V_e = V_u + 1.6 T_u/b with consistent units; M_t = T_u (1 + D/b)/1.7; Design stirrups for V_e using the usual shear route; Total longitudinal steel from M_u + M_t acting together; Check the section against the torsional shear limit; Provide side face reinforcement when the depth is large; Note that torsion steel must be closed links anchored properly.

Apply Show, with numbers, how to check the development length at a simple support and decide whether a standard 90° bend is required. 10 marks

Bar force at the section from the bending moment; L_d = φ σ_s/(4 τ_bd) or the code value 0.87 f_y φ/(4 τ_bd); Available embedment from the support face, counting cover and the anchorage value of the bend (8φ for 90°); Compare and add a bend or increase the embedment if short; Mention that at least one third of the positive steel must reach the support.

Apply The design gives V_us = 90 kN at a section of a beam with d = 500 mm, f_y = 415 N/mm², using two-legged 10 mm stirrups. Find the required spacing and verify the minimum shear reinforcement requirement. 8 marks

A_sv = 2 × 78.5 = 157 mm²; s_v = 0.87 f_y A_sv d / V_us; Compare with minimum A_sv/(b s_v) ≥ 0.4/(0.87 f_y); Compare with the maximum spacing limit related to d; State the adopted spacing and why it is rounded down.

Apply Prepare a reinforcement detailing scheme for a rectangular beam carrying combined bending, shear and torsion, showing the stirrups, torsion links, longitudinal bars and cover. 10 marks

Closed stirrups anchored around the longitudinal bar; Spacing from shear design, and the extra torsional requirement; Longitudinal steel placed near the corners of the links; Side face reinforcement for deep webs; Nominal cover from the exposure class; Bar spacing rules for crack control and concreting; Section and side elevation drawing conventions.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze IS 456 does not design a beam in torsion by torsional theory; it uses equivalent shear and equivalent moment. Explain why, and give two situations where this simplification can mislead the designer. 13 marks

True torsional stress distribution is complex and depends on compatibility; Equivalent shear and moment reduce torsion to familiar design tools; Result is conservative for ordinary beams; Where it is unsafe or inadequate: equilibrium torsion where the member must carry the torque for stability; Thin-walled or flanged members where the shear flow path differs; Interaction with bending at the section is not captured fully; Secondary effects such as crack width and serviceability need separate thought.

Evaluate A contractor cuts off half of the bottom bars exactly where the bending moment diagram shows they are no longer needed. Analyse what can go wrong, and state what the code requires instead. 13 marks

Moment at the section is not the whole story: bond and shear interact; A bar must be developed before it carries its force, so the cut point moves towards midspan; Code requires the bar to extend beyond the theoretical cut-off by at least d or 12φ; Limits on how much steel may be curtailed or stopped at one section; Diagonal tension cracks shift the point of maximum bar force; Sudden loss of steel causes a brittle failure near the support region; Correct procedure: shift the moment diagram, then cut in steps.

Analyze Discuss how shear span-to-depth ratio, concrete grade and stirrup spacing change the mode of shear failure of a beam. 12 marks

Small shear span: arch action and diagonal compression, crushing type failure; Large shear span: beam action, diagonal tension failure; Very slender: flexural failure before shear; Higher concrete grade raises both τ_c and τ_c,max; Closer stirrups change brittle diagonal tension failure into a ductile, yielding mode; Minimum stirrups prevent sudden failure without shear reinforcement; Link to the code requirement of links at all sections.

Evaluate Assess how far detailing — cover, spacing, anchorage and laps — decides the real safety of a beam. Use the interaction of bond and shear in your answer. 13 marks

Calculated steel cannot work without adequate cover and anchorage; Thin cover corrodes, low cover loses fire resistance; Crowded bars prevent concrete from reaching the steel; Bond and shear act together: diagonal cracks cut the embedment short; Anchorage at supports is where shear is highest; Poor laps behave like short development lengths and fail in pull-out; Detailing is design, not drafting.

Glossary

Anchorage
Additional hold given to a bar end by a bend, hook or plate beyond the straight embedment.
Anchorage bond
Bond needed to hold the ends of a bar so that its force is transferred to the concrete.
Bent-up bar
Longitudinal tension bar bent up near the support to act as inclined shear reinforcement.
Bond
Transfer of force between a bar and the surrounding concrete by adhesion, friction and mechanical interlock.
Characteristic strength
Value of strength below which not more than five per cent of test results are expected to fall; f_ck for concrete, f_y for steel.
Clear cover
Actual distance from the concrete surface to the nearest steel surface measured on site.
Compatibility torsion
Torsion arising from the need to keep adjoining parts together, which may be reduced by cracking and redistribution.
Curtailment
Stopping some bars where the bending moment has fallen, subject to development and extension rules.
Effective depth
Distance from the extreme compression fibre to the centroid of the tension steel, symbol d, in mm.
Equilibrium torsion
Torsion that is required for the member to stay in equilibrium, so it cannot be redistributed or reduced.
Exposure class
Classification of the environment (mild, moderate, severe and so on) that fixes the minimum cover and concrete grade.
Flexural bond
Bond needed because the bending moment, and therefore the bar force, changes from section to section.
Flexural shear crack
Inclined crack that starts as a vertical flexural crack and bends over towards the load as shear stress opens it.
Hook
Curved end given to a bar to provide anchorage; a standard semicircular hook has an anchorage value of about 16φ.
Lap length
Length over which two bars overlap to transfer force, never less than the development length.
Limit state of collapse
Condition just before the structure fails, checked with factored loads and partial safety factors.
Shear span
Distance from a point load to the nearer support; the shear span-to-depth ratio decides the type of shear failure.
Shifted moment diagram
Moment diagram moved horizontally by the effective depth to account for diagonal tension when bars are curtailed.
Side face reinforcement
Steel placed along the two faces of a deep web, required when the overall depth exceeds the code value, to control cracking.
Stirrup
Closed vertical or inclined link placed around the longitudinal bars to carry shear and to hold the cage together.
Torsion
Twisting action on a member, measured as a torque T about the longitudinal axis in N mm.
Torsional shear stress
Shear stress circulating around the section, added to the ordinary shear stress when a torque acts.
Web-shear crack
Inclined crack that forms in the web of a beam where the principal tensile stress is highest, often before flexural cracking.
