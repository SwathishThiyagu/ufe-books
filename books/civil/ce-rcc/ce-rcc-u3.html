3.1 Behaviour of RC beams in shear

Shear in a reinforced concrete beam shows itself as diagonal tension in the web. Bending and shear stress combine into a principal tension inclined to the beam axis, and because concrete is weak in tension, inclined cracks open long before the steel yields. After cracking, the beam carries shear through the uncracked concrete above the crack, friction across the crack, dowel action of the main bars and the stirrups. Every shear rule in IS 456 comes from this picture.

You need: the singly reinforced section, effective depth d and the limit state of collapse (limit state design of beams). The stirrup design itself is in Design of shear reinforcement.

Diagonal tension in an uncracked beam

Take a point inside a beam. Bending gives a horizontal normal stress σ there (compression above the neutral axis, tension below it). The shear force V gives a shear stress τ, equal on the horizontal and the vertical plane through the point. The two combine into a principal tensile stress σ1 acting on an inclined plane.

σ1 = σ/2 + √((σ/2)² + τ²)     tan 2θ = 2τ/σ

σ1 is the principal tensile stress; σ is the bending stress, positive in tension (N/mm²); τ = V Q/(I b) is the shear stress, with Q the first moment of the area beyond the point about the neutral axis (mm³), I the second moment of area (mm⁴) and b the width (mm); θ is the angle of σ1 to the beam axis.

At the soffit τ = 0, so σ1 is horizontal and the crack is vertical. At the neutral axis σ = 0, so σ1 = τ at θ = 45°. A crack that starts vertical at the soffit therefore bends over towards the load as it climbs: a flexure-shear crack, the usual shear crack. In a thin web near a support an inclined web-shear crack can open first at the neutral axis. Concrete cracks when σ1 reaches its tensile strength, only about 2 to 3 N/mm² for M25.

How a cracked beam carries shear

Cut the beam along an inclined crack and look at the piece between the crack and the support. The shear on that piece is held by four forces.

V = Vcz + Va + Vd + Vs

Vcz is the shear in the uncracked compression zone; Va is the vertical part of aggregate interlock, friction between the rough crack faces; Vd is the dowel force in the main bars crossing the crack; Vs is the sum of the stirrup forces crossing the crack.

Aggregate interlock works only while the crack is narrow, and dowel action is limited by the thin cover under the bars. Stirrups do almost nothing before cracking; after it they are the part that can be designed, and by holding the crack narrow they keep Va and Vd working too.

The truss analogy puts these together: the compression zone is the top chord, the tension steel the bottom chord, the concrete between cracks forms 45° struts and the stirrups are the vertical ties. A stirrup inclined at 45° against the crack crosses it at 90° and works better than a vertical one, but vertical stirrups are easier to fix and also hold the main bars.

Modes of shear failure

Without enough shear steel, the failure mode depends mainly on a/d, where a is the shear span (point load to support).

  • a/d below about 1 (deep beams): arch action; the strut crushes or splits, or the tie bars lose anchorage.
  • a/d about 1 to 2.5: shear-compression; the concrete above the inclined crack crushes.
  • a/d about 2.5 to 6: diagonal tension; the crack runs through suddenly and the beam splits. The most brittle mode.
  • a/d above about 6: flexure governs.
  • Thin webs under heavy shear: web crushing of the struts, which stirrups cannot prevent.

From behaviour to the code check

τv = Vu / (b d)

τv is the nominal shear stress (N/mm²); Vu is the factored shear force at the section (N); b is the width of the beam, or the web width bw of a flanged beam (mm); d is the effective depth (mm).

τc is the design shear strength of concrete (IS 456 Table 19). It rises with the grade and with pt = 100 Ast/(b d), where Ast is the tension steel (mm²) continuing at least d beyond the section. τc,max (Table 20) caps τv even with shear steel, to stop web crushing: 2.8 N/mm² for M20, 3.1 for M25, 3.5 for M30.

So there are three cases: τv ≤ τc needs only minimum stirrups; τc < τv ≤ τc,max needs stirrups designed for Vus = Vu − τc b d; τv > τc,max needs a bigger section or a higher grade.

Where the support reaction compresses the beam end, load within d of the support face goes straight into the support, so IS 456 takes the section at d from the face as critical and designs the zone nearer the support for that shear.

Worked example

Question. An uncracked M25 beam 300 mm × 600 mm carries M = 60 kN·m and V = 120 kN at a section. Treating it as a plain concrete section, find σ, τ, σ1 and its direction 150 mm below the neutral axis, and σ1 at the neutral axis. Compare with the flexural strength 0.7√fck.

  1. I = b D³/12 = 300 × 600³/12 = 5.4 × 10⁹ mm⁴, with the neutral axis at mid-depth.
  2. Bending stress at y = 150 mm (tension side): σ = M y/I = 60 × 10⁶ × 150/(5.4 × 10⁹) = 1.67 N/mm².
  3. Area below the point: 300 × 150 = 45,000 mm² with centroid 225 mm from the axis, so Q = 1.0125 × 10⁷ mm³ and τ = V Q/(I b) = 120 × 10³ × 1.0125 × 10⁷/(5.4 × 10⁹ × 300) = 0.75 N/mm².
  4. σ1 = 0.833 + √(0.833² + 0.75²) = 0.833 + 1.121 = 1.95 N/mm²; tan 2θ = 1.5/1.667 = 0.90, so θ = 21.0°. The crack would form at right angles to σ1, at 69° to the axis.
  5. At the neutral axis: σ1 = τmax = 1.5 V/(b D) = 1.5 × 120,000/180,000 = 1.00 N/mm² at 45°.
  6. Flexural strength of M25: 0.7 √25 = 3.5 N/mm². Both values are below it, so the beam is still uncracked.

Answer. σ = 1.67 N/mm² (tension), τ = 0.75 N/mm², σ1 = 1.95 N/mm² at 21° to the axis (a crack would form at 69°); at the neutral axis σ1 = 1.00 N/mm² at 45°. Both are below 3.5 N/mm², so no crack yet.

Worked example 2

Question. The same beam, now cracked, has effective depth d = 550 mm and three 25 mm bars in tension continuing past the section. The factored shear at the critical section is Vu = 180 kN; concrete is M25. Find τv, pt, τc (IS 456 Table 19) and τc,max, decide which case applies, and find the shear the stirrups must carry.

  1. Ast = 3 × π × 25²/4 = 1473 mm²; τv = 180 × 10³/(300 × 550) = 1.09 N/mm²; pt = 100 × 1473/165,000 = 0.89 %.
  2. Table 19, M25: τc = 0.57 at pt = 0.75 and 0.64 at 1.00, so τc = 0.57 + (0.89 − 0.75)/0.25 × 0.07 = 0.61 N/mm². Table 20: τc,max = 3.1 N/mm².
  3. τc = 0.61 < τv = 1.09 < τc,max = 3.1 N/mm²: the section is adequate, but shear reinforcement must be designed.
  4. Concrete share: τc b d = 0.61 × 165,000 = 100.65 kN. Stirrup share: Vus = 180 − 100.65 = 79.35 kN.

Answer. τv = 1.09 N/mm², pt = 0.89 %, τc = 0.61 N/mm², τc,max = 3.1 N/mm²; the middle case applies, and the stirrups must carry Vus = 79.35 kN (designed in the next chapter).

Exam traps

  • Writing that the crack is along the principal tension. It opens at right angles to σ1: 45° at the neutral axis, but 69° at the point of worked example 1.
  • Treating τc as fixed for a grade. It also depends on pt, counting only bars that continue at least d past the section.
  • Thinking more stirrups cure web crushing. Above τc,max only a bigger section or a higher grade helps.
  • Using D instead of d in τv = Vu/(b d), or leaving Vu in kN instead of N.

3.2 Design of shear reinforcement

Once the factored shear is more than the concrete can carry, the rest goes to shear reinforcement: vertical stirrups, inclined stirrups or bent-up bars. IS 456 gives one route. Find τv, check it against τc,max, take the concrete share τc b d, and size the stirrups for the remainder Vus from the truss formula Vus = 0.87 fy Asv d/sv. Then check the minimum steel and the maximum spacing, and change the spacing along the span as the shear falls. Every beam on every drawing goes through this check.

You need: τv, τc, τc,max and the critical section from Behaviour of RC beams in shear.

The design route

  1. At the critical section find τv = Vu/(b d) and check τv ≤ τc,max; if not, enlarge the section.
  2. Read τc for pt (bars continuing d past the section). If τv ≤ τc, provide minimum stirrups; otherwise Vus = Vu − τc b d.
  3. Choose the stirrup size and legs (Asv = total leg area), find sv, round it down and check the minimum steel and maximum spacing.
  4. Find where the shear falls enough for a wider spacing, and draw the zones.
Vus = 0.87 fy Asv d / sv     so     sv = 0.87 fy Asv d / Vus

Vus is the shear carried by vertical stirrups (N); fy is the stirrup steel strength (N/mm²) and 0.87 = 1/1.15; Asv is the total area of the stirrup legs (mm²), 2 × 50.3 = 100.5 mm² for two-legged 8 mm stirrups; d is the effective depth and sv the spacing (mm).

The formula is the truss analogy in one line. A 45° crack spans a length d along the beam, so it crosses d/sv stirrups, and each one yields at 0.87 fy Asv. Some syllabi call τc the 'design shear strength without shear reinforcement' and τc,max the limit 'with shear reinforcement'. τc,max is never subtracted from Vu; only τc b d is.

Minimum steel and maximum spacing

Asv/(b sv) ≥ 0.4/(0.87 fy)     so     sv ≤ 0.87 fy Asv/(0.4 b)

b is the beam width (mm); fy is the stirrup steel strength (N/mm²), not taken greater than 415 N/mm² in this rule; the other symbols are as above.

Minimum stirrups are required even when τv ≤ τc. They hold the cage together, stop a sudden diagonal tension failure if the concrete is weaker than assumed, and give warning. IS 456 waives them only where τv is less than half of τc and in members of minor importance such as lintels.

Maximum spacing of vertical stirrups is the least of 0.75 d and 300 mm; for stirrups inclined at 45° it is the lesser of d and 300 mm. The idea is that every possible 45° crack must cross at least one stirrup, and in practice two.

Design aids such as SP 16 tabulate τc against pt, and Vus/d = 0.87 fy Asv/sv for each stirrup size and spacing. For two-legged 8 mm Fe415 stirrups at 100 mm, Vus/d = 0.87 × 415 × 100.5/100 = 363 N/mm, that is 3.63 kN/cm. Divide the required Vus/d into it to get the spacing directly.

Bent-up bars, inclined stirrups and slabs

  • Inclined stirrups or a series of bent-up bars at angle α to the beam axis: Vus = 0.87 fy Asv d (sin α + cos α)/sv. At α = 45°, sin α + cos α = 1.414.
  • A single bent-up bar, or a group bent up at the same section: Vus = 0.87 fy Asv sin α.
  • Bent-up bars may carry at most half of the total shear reinforcement; stirrups must carry the rest.
  • Solid slabs: τc is multiplied by a factor k from 1.00 (slabs 300 mm or thicker) to 1.30 (150 mm or thinner), and τv must not exceed half of τc,max. Slabs are usually made thick enough to need no shear steel at all.

Worked example

Question. Continue the beam of the previous chapter: b = 300 mm, d = 550 mm, M25, three 25 mm bars, Vu = 180 kN at the critical section, τc = 0.61 N/mm² and Vus = 79.35 kN. Design two-legged 8 mm Fe415 vertical stirrups.

  1. Check the section: τv = 1.09 N/mm² ≤ τc,max = 3.1 N/mm². Adequate.
  2. Stirrup area: Asv = 2 × π × 8²/4 = 2 × 50.27 = 100.5 mm².
  3. Spacing for strength: sv = 0.87 × 415 × 100.5 × 550/(79.35 × 10³) = 19,957,000/79,350 = 251.5 mm.
  4. Spacing for minimum steel: sv ≤ 0.87 × 415 × 100.5/(0.4 × 300) = 36,290/120 = 302.4 mm.
  5. Maximum spacing: least of 0.75 × 550 = 412.5 mm and 300 mm, so 300 mm.
  6. The strength value 251.5 mm governs. Round down: provide 250 mm.
  7. Check: with sv = 250 mm, Vus = 0.87 × 415 × 100.5 × 550/250 = 79.8 kN ≥ 79.35 kN. Safe.

Answer. Two-legged 8 mm Fe415 stirrups at 250 mm centres (strength needs 251.5 mm; minimum steel allows 302.4 mm and the code maximum is 300 mm).

Worked example 2

Question. A simply supported beam with the same section (b = 300 mm, d = 550 mm, M25, three 25 mm bars running full length) has a clear span of 6 m and carries a factored load wu = 80 kN/m including self-weight. Design two-legged 8 mm Fe415 stirrups and give the spacing zones over half the span.

  1. Shear at the support face: V = 80 × 6/2 = 240 kN. At the critical section, d = 0.55 m from the face: Vu = 240 − 80 × 0.55 = 196 kN.
  2. τv = 196 × 10³/165,000 = 1.19 N/mm² < τc,max = 3.1 N/mm². τc = 0.61 N/mm² as before, so the concrete carries τc b d = 100.65 kN.
  3. Vus = 196 − 100.65 = 95.35 kN. Spacing: sv = 0.87 × 415 × 100.5 × 550/(95.35 × 10³) = 209.3 mm. Provide 200 mm.
  4. Capacity with the widest spacing allowed, 300 mm: Vus = 0.87 × 415 × 100.5 × 550/300 = 66.5 kN, so the beam with stirrups at 300 mm carries 100.65 + 66.5 = 167.2 kN.
  5. Where does the shear fall to 167.2 kN? 240 − 80 x = 167.2 gives x = 0.91 m from the face. Round up the close zone to 1.0 m.
  6. Zone 1, from the face to 1.0 m: 8 mm stirrups at 200 mm, the first one 50 mm from the face. Zone 2, from 1.0 m to midspan: 8 mm stirrups at 300 mm (minimum steel and maximum spacing are both met).
  7. Check zone 1: capacity at 200 mm = 100.65 + 0.87 × 415 × 100.5 × 550/200/1000 = 100.65 + 99.8 = 200.4 kN ≥ 196 kN.

Answer. Two-legged 8 mm stirrups at 200 mm for the first 1.0 m from each support face, then at 300 mm over the middle 4.0 m. The change point is where V has fallen to 167.2 kN, 0.91 m from the face.

Exam traps

  • Subtracting τc,max b d instead of τc b d. τc,max is only the ceiling on τv; the concrete share is τc b d.
  • Leaving Vus in kN in sv = 0.87 fy Asv d/Vus. 79.35 kN must be 79,350 N, or the spacing comes out 1000 times too large.
  • Taking Asv as the area of one leg. For two-legged 8 mm stirrups Asv = 100.5 mm², not 50.3 mm².
  • Rounding the spacing up (251.5 mm to 260 mm) or forgetting the 0.75 d and 300 mm limits. Always round down and apply all three limits.
  • Leaving out stirrups where τv < τc. Minimum shear reinforcement is still required, except where τv < τc/2 or in lintels.

3.3 Design for torsion

A beam twists when a load acts off its axis: an edge beam carrying a cantilever slab or a canopy, a curved balcony beam, a lintel under a sunshade. The twisting moment Tu sets up shear stress that circulates round the section, so one side face sees bending shear plus torsion shear. After cracking the torque is carried by a cage of closed stirrups and corner bars, with the concrete between spiral cracks acting as struts. IS 456 does not ask for this space truss to be analysed. It turns Tu into an equivalent shear for the stirrups and an equivalent moment for the longitudinal bars, so the familiar shear and flexure checks do the work.

You need: τv, τc and τc,max from Behaviour of RC beams in shear and the stirrup formula from Design of shear reinforcement.

Equilibrium torsion and compatibility torsion

Equilibrium torsion is torque the member must carry to keep the structure standing. An edge beam holding a cantilever canopy is the classic case: if the beam could not resist the twist, the canopy would fall. IS 456 requires such members to be designed for the full torque.

Compatibility torsion comes only because members are cast together. A secondary beam framing into a main beam rotates at its end and twists the main beam, but if the main beam cracked and softened in torsion, the secondary beam would simply carry its load as a simply supported beam. IS 456 says no specific design for torsion is needed in such indeterminate cases, provided the torsional stiffness is neglected when the internal forces are calculated; the shear reinforcement is taken to control the torsional cracking.

The exam test: release the twist in your mind. If the structure still stands, it is compatibility torsion; if something collapses, it is equilibrium torsion.

How a beam carries torque

In an uncracked solid rectangle the torsional shear stress is zero at the centre and largest at the middle of the long faces. It acts in a closed loop round the section. On one side face it adds to the bending shear stress; on the opposite face it subtracts. The principal tension is at 45° on every face, so the cracks wind round the beam as a spiral.

After cracking, the inside of the section does little. The torque is carried by a thin outer tube, modelled as a space truss: closed stirrups are the hoops, longitudinal bars at the corners are the stringers, and the concrete between the spiral cracks forms struts at about 45°. Two facts follow. Torsion needs transverse steel and longitudinal steel together, and the stirrups must be closed, because the shear flow runs round all four faces. An open U stirrup has no top leg to carry it.

A beam under bending and torsion can also fail by skew bending: a spiral crack on three faces and crushing along the fourth. This is why IS 456 adds a torsion term to the bending moment, and why, when the torque is large, steel is needed on the compression face too.

The IS 456 method

Ve = Vu + 1.6 Tu/b     τve = Ve/(b d) ≤ τc,max

Ve is the equivalent shear (N); Vu the factored shear (N); Tu the factored torque (N·mm); b the breadth (mm); τve the equivalent shear stress (N/mm²); d the effective depth (mm). With Tu in kN·m and b in m, 1.6 Tu/b comes out in kN.

If τve exceeds τc,max, the section must be enlarged. If τve ≤ τc, minimum shear reinforcement is enough. If τve > τc, both longitudinal and transverse torsion steel are designed.

Asv = Tu sv/(b1 d1 × 0.87 fy) + Vu sv/(2.5 d1 × 0.87 fy)
but not less than (τve − τc) b sv/(0.87 fy)

Asv is the area of the two legs of a closed stirrup (mm²); sv its spacing (mm); b1 and d1 the centre-to-centre distances between the corner bars across the width and the depth (mm).

Mt = Tu (1 + D/b)/1.7     Me1 = Mu + Mt     Me2 = Mt − Mu (if Mt > Mu)

Mt is the equivalent moment from torsion; Mu the factored moment; D the overall depth, so D/b has no unit; Me1 is designed on the flexural tension face, Me2 on the compression face (see Combined bending, shear and torsion).

  • Spacing of torsion stirrups: not more than x1, (x1 + y1)/4 and 300 mm, where x1 and y1 are the short and long dimensions of the stirrup.
  • At least one longitudinal bar in each corner of the stirrups, and extra side bars when a dimension exceeds 450 mm.
  • Stirrups closed, with ends anchored by 135° hooks round a corner bar.

Worked example

Question. An edge beam 300 mm × 600 mm (d = 550 mm) carries a factored shear Vu = 120 kN and a factored torque Tu = 30 kN·m from a cantilever canopy. Concrete is M25 and steel Fe415. The bottom steel is four 20 mm bars (pt = 0.76 %); top corner bars are 12 mm; nominal cover is 30 mm and the stirrups are 10 mm. Check the section and design the closed stirrups.

  1. The canopy would fall if the beam could twist freely, so this is equilibrium torsion.
  2. Ve = 120 + 1.6 × 30/0.3 = 280 kN; τve = 280 × 10³/(300 × 550) = 1.70 N/mm² < τc,max = 3.1 N/mm², so the section is adequate.
  3. Table 19, M25, pt = 0.76 %: τc = 0.57 N/mm². Since τve > τc, torsion steel is needed.
  4. Corner bar centres are 30 + 10 + 10 = 50 mm (bottom) and 30 + 10 + 6 = 46 mm (top) from the faces, so b1 = 300 − 100 = 200 mm and d1 = 600 − 50 − 46 = 504 mm.
  5. Two-legged 10 mm stirrups: Asv = 157.1 mm², so 0.87 fy Asv = 0.87 × 415 × 157.1 = 56,720 N.
  6. Strength: sv = 56,720/(30 × 10⁶/(200 × 504) + 120 × 10³/(2.5 × 504)) = 56,720/(297.6 + 95.2) = 144.4 mm.
  7. Lower limit: sv ≤ 56,720/((1.697 − 0.57) × 300) = 167.8 mm, with the unrounded τve = 1.697 N/mm².
  8. Spacing limits: stirrup centreline 230 mm × 530 mm, so x1 = 230, (x1 + y1)/4 = 190 and 300 mm. The strength value governs: provide 10 mm closed stirrups at 140 mm.

Answer. Ve = 280 kN, τve = 1.70 N/mm² (below 3.1, above τc = 0.57); two-legged 10 mm closed stirrups at 140 mm centres (strength needs 144.4 mm; the other limits are 167.8, 190, 230 and 300 mm).

Exam traps

  • Using open U stirrups for torsion. The shear flow goes round all four faces, so the stirrups must be closed and hooked round a corner bar.
  • Mixing units in Ve = Vu + 1.6 Tu/b. With Tu = 30 kN·m and b = 0.3 m the term is 160 kN; with b written as 300 it is 0.16, which is wrong unless Tu is in kN·mm.
  • Taking b1 and d1 as the beam size. They are the distances between the centres of the corner bars: 200 mm and 504 mm here, not 300 and 600 mm.
  • Designing compatibility torsion as if it were equilibrium torsion, or the reverse. Ask whether the structure would still stand if the member could twist freely.
  • Designing the stirrups and forgetting that torsion also needs longitudinal steel (Mt added to Mu).

Understand Why must torsion reinforcement include both closed stirrups and longitudinal bars? 3 marks

Apply b = 250 mm, D = 500 mm, d = 460 mm, Vu = 80 kN, Tu = 20 kN·m, M25. Find Ve and τve and check the section. 4 marks

3.4 Combined bending, shear and torsion

Edge beams, spandrel beams, ring beams and beams carrying sunshades feel bending, shear and torsion at the same section. IS 456 handles the three together with two equivalent actions. The equivalent moment Me1 = Mu + Mt sizes the bars on the tension face, and Me2 = Mt − Mu on the compression face when torsion is large. The equivalent shear Ve checks the section and, with Tu and Vu, sizes the closed stirrups. Only a few syllabi list this as a topic of its own; it is where the torsion and the shear chapters come together into one complete beam design.

You need: Ve, Mt and the torsion stirrup formula from Design for torsion, and the singly reinforced beam design (limit state design of beams).

How the three actions interact

Torsion pulls on all four corner bars: the spiral struts of the space truss need a longitudinal tie. On the bottom face this tension adds to the bending tension, which is why Mt is added to Mu. On the top face bending causes compression, which cancels some of the torsion tension. If Mt is larger than Mu, the top face ends up in net tension, which is the case IS 456 covers with Me2.

Shear and torsion shear add on one side face and subtract on the other. The equivalent shear Ve = Vu + 1.6 Tu/b is the side that adds, so checking τve against τc,max protects the worst face against web crushing.

Me1 = Mu + Tu (1 + D/b)/1.7          Me2 = Mt − Mu (when Mt > Mu)

Me1 is the equivalent moment for the flexural tension face (kN·m); Me2 acts in the opposite sense and is designed on the flexural compression face (kN·m); D/b is the overall depth over the breadth, no unit.

Design sequence

  1. Find the factored Mu, Vu and Tu at the section, and decide that the torsion is equilibrium torsion.
  2. Find Ve and τve = Ve/(b d). If τve > τc,max, enlarge the section.
  3. Find Mt and Me1. Check Me1 ≤ Mu,lim = 0.138 fck b d² (Fe415); if larger, design a doubly reinforced section.
  4. Find Ast for Me1 from M = 0.87 fy Ast d (1 − Ast fy/(b d fck)), or from the SP 16 flexure table using Me1/(b d²).
  5. If Mt > Mu, find the steel for Me2 on the other face.
  6. Read τc for the steel provided; if τve > τc, design closed stirrups from the torsion formula, check the (τve − τc) b sv/(0.87 fy) lower limit and the spacing limits x1, (x1 + y1)/4 and 300 mm.
  7. Detail: a bar in every corner, side-face bars when D exceeds 450 mm (0.1 % of the web area, shared between the two faces, at 300 mm or less), 135° hooks, and full anchorage of the longitudinal bars at the supports.

The factors 1.6 and 1.7 are calibrated against tests on rectangular beams, not derived from statics; use them as given, with consistent units.

Exam traps

  • Designing the bottom steel for Mu alone. The tension face is designed for Me1 = Mu + Mt.
  • Forgetting Me2 near supports or wherever Mu is small. When Mt > Mu, the top face is in tension and its bars must be designed and anchored as tension bars.

  • Leaving out the side-face bars of a beam deeper than 450 mm under torsion.
  • Writing Mt = Tu (1 + b/D)/1.7. The ratio is D/b, overall depth over breadth: 2.0 here, not 0.5.

3.5 Bond, anchorage and development length

Steel and concrete act as one material only because of bond, the shear stress along the surface of each bar that passes force between the bar and the concrete around it. A bar cannot reach its design stress at the point where it starts; the force has to be built up by bond over a length. That length is the development length Ld, and it decides how far bars must run past a critical section, into a support, round a hook or beyond a cut-off. Most anchorage failures in real buildings are failures to provide it.

You need: the design stress 0.87 fy of steel (design philosophies and partial safety factors) and the moment of resistance of a singly reinforced section (limit state design of beams).

What bond is

  • Adhesion: the chemical grip of hardened cement paste on the steel. It breaks at a very small slip.
  • Friction: resistance to sliding once the bar starts to move, helped by shrinkage of the concrete onto the bar.
  • Mechanical bearing: the ribs of a deformed bar bear on the concrete like small keys. This is the main source of bond for the deformed (TMT) bars used today.

Flexural bond is needed wherever the bar force changes because the moment changes; anchorage bond is needed at a bar end, where the whole force must pass into the concrete. IS 456 does not check local flexural bond stress in the limit state method; it requires Ld on both sides of every section where a bar is fully stressed, plus the check at supports and points of inflection.

Bond can fail in two ways. In pull-out, the bar slides and shears the concrete between its ribs; this needs good confinement and is the case the bond stresses of IS 456 assume. In splitting, the wedging action of the ribs splits the cover along the bar. Splitting governs when the cover is thin or the bars are crowded, which is why cover, spacing and stirrups matter for bond.

Development length

A bar of diameter φ carries the force σs π φ²/4. Over an embedded length Ld the bond force is τbd π φ Ld. Setting the two equal and cancelling π φ gives the formula.

Ld = φ σs/(4 τbd)

Ld is the development length (mm); φ is the bar diameter (mm); σs is the stress to be developed, 0.87 fy at the limit state of collapse (N/mm²); τbd is the design bond stress (N/mm²).

IS 456 gives τbd for plain bars in tension: 1.2 N/mm² for M20, 1.4 for M25, 1.5 for M30, 1.7 for M35 and 1.9 for M40 and above. For deformed bars these values are increased by 60 %, and for bars in compression by a further 25 %. For bundled bars, Ld of each bar is increased by 10 % for two bars in contact, 20 % for three and 33 % for four. So the M25 value for deformed bars in tension is 1.4 × 1.6 = 2.24 N/mm².

Anchorage at supports and hooks

  • Deformed bars may be used without hooks if Ld is available. Plain bars in tension normally get hooks.
  • Anchorage value of a bend: 4φ for each 45° of bend, up to 16φ. A 90° bend is worth 8φ; a standard U-type hook is worth 16φ. The anchorage value counts as part of Ld.
  • In compression, hooks and bends add nothing; only the straight projected length counts.
  • At a simple support, at least one third of the positive moment steel must run into the support for a length Ld/3 (one quarter in continuous beams).
Ld ≤ 1.3 M1/V + L0

Checked at simple supports and points of inflection; it limits the bar diameter. M1 is the moment of resistance with all bars reaching the section stressed to 0.87 fy (N·mm); V is the shear there (N); L0 is the anchorage beyond the centre of the support plus the anchorage value of any hook (mm), or at a point of inflection the greater of d and 12φ. Use 1.3 when a compressive reaction confines the bar ends, otherwise 1.0.

Near a support the shear is large, so the bar force climbs quickly from zero; M1/V is roughly the distance over which the moment reaches M1. A bar too thick for that distance pulls out before reaching its full force. The cure is smaller bars, or a bend or hook to increase L0.

3.6 Lap splices and curtailment of bars

Bars come in stock lengths of about 12 m, so long members need joints; the usual joint is a lap splice, two bars overlapped so the force passes from one to the other through the concrete. Bars are also stopped (curtailed) where the bending moment has fallen enough for fewer bars to do the work. Both are safe only if every bar has its development length and the joints and cut-offs are placed where the code allows. Only a few syllabi list this as a separate topic, but every detailing question uses it.

You need: Ld and the anchorage values of hooks from Bond, anchorage and development length, and the moment of resistance of a beam section (limit state design of beams).

Lap splices

  • Flexural tension: lap length = the greater of Ld and 30φ. Direct tension (ties): the greater of 2 Ld and 30φ. The straight length of a lap must be at least 15φ or 200 mm.
  • Compression: lap length = Ld in compression, but not less than 24φ.
  • Increase the tension lap by 1.4 for a bar at the top of a section as cast with cover less than 2φ, or at a corner with cover less than 2φ, or where adjacent laps are closer than 75 mm or 6φ; by 2.0 when both conditions apply.
  • Bars of different diameters: the lap length is based on the smaller bar, because the joint can never pass more force than the smaller bar carries.
  • Laps are not used for bars larger than 36 mm; such bars are welded or joined with mechanical couplers.

Place laps away from the sections of maximum stress: in a flexural member, not where the moment is more than half the moment of resistance. Splice not more than half the bars at one section, and stagger the laps; laps count as staggered when their centres are at least 1.3 times the lap length apart. Bunched laps make one weak, crowded section that cracks first and is hard to concrete. Some syllabi call this topic 'splicing of reinforcement'.

Curtailment of bars

Plot the moment diagram and the moment of resistance MR of the bars that continue. The theoretical cut-off point of the stopped bars is where the moment falls to that MR: beyond it they are not needed for flexure. They cannot stop there, because an inclined crack starting there shifts the bar force towards the support.

  • Extend every curtailed bar beyond its theoretical cut-off by the greater of d and 12φ (not needed at a simple support or at the free end of a cantilever).
  • Every bar must also have Ld measured from the section where it is fully stressed, and the continuing bars must have Ld beyond the theoretical cut-off.
  • A bar should not stop in a tension zone unless one of three conditions holds: the shear at the cut-off is not more than two thirds of the shear capacity including the stirrups; or extra stirrups are provided over 0.75 d from the cut-off; or, for bars up to 36 mm, the continuing bars give twice the area needed there and the shear is not more than three quarters of the capacity.
  • At a simple support at least one third of the positive steel (one quarter in a continuous beam) runs into the support for Ld/3.
  • At a continuous support at least one third of the negative steel runs beyond the point of inflection by the greatest of d, 12φ and one sixteenth of the clear span.

3.7 Detailing of reinforcement

Detailing turns the calculated steel into bars a site team can cut, bend, place and concrete: each bar's diameter, position, length, shape and anchorage, drawn and listed in a bar bending schedule. The code's detailing rules on cover, spacing, minimum and maximum steel, stirrup hooks and side-face bars exist because a correct design with poor detailing still cracks, corrodes or fails at a joint. Some syllabi call this 'design requirements as per the current code' or 'detailing requirements'.

You need: development length, laps and curtailment from Bond, anchorage and development length and Lap splices and curtailment of bars.

Cover

Nominal cover is the design depth of concrete over all steel, including the stirrups, written on the drawing. It protects the steel from corrosion and fire and gives the bars room to bond. IS 456 sets it by exposure: 20 mm mild, 30 mm moderate, 45 mm severe, 50 mm very severe, 75 mm extreme (for mild exposure it may be 5 mm less for main bars up to 12 mm). Fire resistance may need more. Longitudinal bars in columns need at least 40 mm, and not less than the bar diameter.

Because the nominal cover is measured to the stirrup, the main bar sits deeper: its cover is the nominal cover plus the stirrup diameter. Effective depth d is measured to the centroid of the main bars: d = D − cover − stirrup diameter − φ/2 for one layer.

Bar spacing and amount of steel

  • Horizontal clear distance between bars: at least the diameter of the larger bar and the nominal maximum aggregate size plus 5 mm (25 mm for 20 mm aggregate).
  • Two or more layers: bars vertically in line, with a vertical clear gap of at least 15 mm, two thirds of the maximum aggregate size and the largest bar diameter.
  • Maximum clear distance between tension bars in beams, for crack control: 180 mm for Fe415 and 150 mm for Fe500 when no moment is redistributed.
  • Tension steel: at least 0.85 b d/fy and not more than 0.04 b D. Compression steel: not more than 0.04 b D, held by stirrups.
  • Side-face bars: 0.1 % of the web area, shared between the two faces at 300 mm or less, when the web is deeper than 750 mm; in beams under torsion when a dimension exceeds 450 mm.

s = (b − 2c − 2φs − nφ)/(n − 1)

s is the clear spacing between n bars of diameter φ in one layer (mm); b is the beam width (mm); c is the nominal cover (mm); φs is the stirrup diameter (mm).

Stirrups, hooks and the bar bending schedule

A stirrup is anchored when its end is bent at least 90° round a bar of at least its own diameter and continued 8φ, or bent 135° and continued 6φ, or bent 180° and continued 4φ. Torsion stirrups and stirrups in earthquake zones use the 135° hook, so they cannot open when the cover spalls. Bars are bent round a mandrel to the bend radii of the bending code and SP 34, the handbook on concrete reinforcement and detailing; a bend that is too tight crushes the concrete inside it and can crack the bar.

  1. Draw the section to scale: outline, cover, stirrup, main bars; check clear spacing.
  2. Draw the elevation: bar marks, cut-off points, laps and their lengths, anchorage at the supports.
  3. Mark the stirrup zones: '10 φ @ 140 c/c', where φ is the bar diameter and c/c means centre to centre, with the first stirrup 50 mm from the face.
  4. Write the bar bending schedule: bar mark, diameter, shape, number, cutting length and weight. Weight per metre = φ²/162 kg/m with φ in mm.

The schedule and the drawing must agree bar for bar; the fabricator works only from the schedule. Show every lap and anchorage as a dimension, never as a note 'lap as required'.

Exam traps

  • Measuring the nominal cover to the main bar. It is measured to the outermost steel, the stirrup; the main bar cover is 30 + 10 = 40 mm here.
  • Forgetting the stirrup diameter in the clear-spacing formula: subtract 2c and 2φs before dividing by n − 1.
  • Taking 15 mm as the gap between two layers of 20 mm bars. The gap is the greatest of 15 mm, two thirds of the aggregate size and the bar diameter: 20 mm here.
  • Hooking a torsion stirrup at 90°. Use 135° hooks with 6φ extension, round a corner bar.
  • Using φ²/162 with φ in cm or getting kg instead of kg/m: a 16 mm bar weighs 1.58 kg per metre.

Analyze On site the stirrups are made 10 mm smaller all round than drawn. Which checks change and how? 4 marks

Torsion cracks spiral round all faces and the cover can spall at the corners. A 90° hook lies in the cover and opens when the cover goes; a 135° hook is bent back into the core round the corner bar, so the hoop stays closed.

Takeaways

  1. Shear cracks are diagonal tension cracks. After cracking the beam carries shear through the compression zone, aggregate interlock, dowel action and stirrups.
  2. Check τv = Vu/(b d) at d from the support face: below τc give minimum stirrups, up to τc,max design stirrups for Vu − τc b d, above τc,max enlarge the section.
  3. Stirrups follow from Vus = 0.87 fy Asv d/sv; round the spacing down and respect the minimum steel and the 0.75 d and 300 mm limits.
  4. Equilibrium torsion must be designed; compatibility torsion may be ignored if torsional stiffness is ignored in the analysis.
  5. Torsion needs closed stirrups and longitudinal corner bars: Ve checks the section, Tu and Vu size the stirrups, Me1 = Mu + Mt sizes the bars, and Me2 appears when Mt > Mu.
  6. Development length Ld = φ σs/(4 τbd) depends only on the bar, the steel and the concrete; use the deformed-bar bond stress for TMT bars.
  7. At simple supports check Ld ≤ 1.3 M1/V + L0 and run a third of the bars into the support for Ld/3.
  8. Laps are the greater of Ld and 30φ in flexural tension, based on the smaller bar, staggered and away from high moment; curtailed bars run d or 12φ past the theoretical cut-off.
  9. Detailing (cover, spacing, hooks, side-face bars and the bar bending schedule) decides whether the calculated steel can actually work.

Understand Explain how a reinforced concrete beam resists shear before and after diagonal cracking. Name each contribution after cracking and explain the modes of shear failure. 12 marks

Model answer.

  1. Before cracking. At any point the bending stress σ and the shear stress τ = V Q/(I b) combine into a principal tension σ1 = σ/2 + √((σ/2)² + τ²) at an angle θ with tan 2θ = 2τ/σ. At the soffit τ = 0, so σ1 is horizontal; at the neutral axis σ = 0, so σ1 = τ at 45°.

  3. Cracking. Concrete cracks when σ1 reaches its tensile strength (about 2 to 3 N/mm² in direct tension for M25).   4. After cracking the shear is carried by four parts, V = Vcz + Va + Vd + Vs: the uncracked compression zone above the crack (Vcz); aggregate interlock, friction between the rough crack faces (Va); dowel action of the main bars crossing the crack (Vd); and the stirrups crossing the crack (Vs).

Analyze and evaluate

Evaluate IS 456 designs a beam for torsion through equivalent shear and equivalent moment rather than torsion theory. Explain why, and discuss where this simplification can mislead a designer. 13 marks

Points to cover. Cracked torsion is a space truss; the analysis is complex; Equivalent actions reuse shear and flexure tools; Factors calibrated on rectangular beams; Where it misleads: compatibility torsion, Me2 near supports, flanged and thin-walled sections, detailing; Numerical illustration.

Model answer.

  1. Why. After cracking a beam in torsion behaves as a space truss: closed stirrups as hoops, corner bars as stringers and spiral concrete struts. An exact design needs the shear-flow area, strut angle and interaction with bending and shear. IS 456 replaces this with two familiar actions: Ve = Vu + 1.6 Tu/b for the web and the stirrups, and Mt = Tu (1 + D/b)/1.7 added to Mu for the longitudinal bars. The designer then uses the ordinary shear check and flexure tables (such as SP 16).
  2. The factors 1.6 and 1.7 are calibrated against tests on solid rectangular beams, and the method keeps the key physics: torsion needs both transverse and longitudinal steel, and the web can crush (τve ≤ τc,max).
  3. Illustration, edge beam 300 × 600 mm, d = 550 mm, Mu = 150 kN·m, Vu = 120 kN, Tu = 30 kN·m:
    QuantityValue
    Ve120 + 160 = 280 kN
    τve1.70 N/mm² (τc,max = 3.1)
    Mt52.94 kN·m
    Me1202.94 kN·m, Ast = 1157 mm²
    stirrups10 mm closed @ 140 (strength 144.4 mm)
  4. Where it can mislead (1): compatibility torsion. Applied blindly, it designs torsion that IS 456 allows to be neglected, or worse, a designer releases torsion that is actually equilibrium torsion (an edge beam carrying a canopy). The method does not classify the torsion; the designer must.
  5. (2) Near supports Mu is small. In the example, at Mu = 30 kN·m, Mt = 52.94 > Mu, so Me2 = 22.94 kN·m puts the top face in tension. A designer who checks only midspan, or stops the top bars as hangers, misses this.
  6. (3) Flanged, hollow or thin-walled sections: the calibration is for solid rectangles. Using b of the web alone ignores the flanges' torsional resistance in some cases and overstates it in others; box girders need the thin-walled tube model.
  7. (4) Detailing is not in the formulas: closed stirrups with 135° hooks, a bar in each corner, side bars beyond 450 mm depth, and anchorage of longitudinal bars at supports. A section that passes the numbers but has open stirrups will fail by spiral cracking.
  8. Evaluation: the method is adequate and conservative for ordinary rectangular beams under equilibrium torsion, provided the designer classifies the torsion, checks Me2 wherever Mu is small and details a closed cage. Check from the illustration: 280 × 10³/(300 × 550) = 1.70 N/mm² < 3.1, so the section works.

Evaluate Evaluate how far detailing (cover, spacing, anchorage, laps and stirrup hooks) decides the real safety of a beam. Use the interaction of bond and shear in your answer. 13 marks

Points to cover. Calculated steel works only if it can develop its force; Cover: durability, fire, splitting; Spacing: concreting and cracks; Anchorage at supports where shear is highest; Laps and cut-offs; Hooks and closed stirrups; Numbers from the examples.

Model answer.

  1. A design gives areas of steel; detailing decides whether each bar can actually reach 0.87 fy. Every rule exists because of a failure that the strength calculation does not see.
  2. Cover: nominal cover (30 mm for moderate exposure, to the stirrups) protects against corrosion and fire, and resists splitting of the concrete along deformed bars. Thin cover means rust, spalling and a splitting bond failure at a lower load.
  3. Spacing: the clear gap must be at least the bar size and aggregate + 5 mm so concrete can surround each bar (46.7 mm in the edge beam is fine), and at most 180 mm for Fe415 to control crack width. Crowded bars leave voids and lose bond.
  4. Bond and shear interact at supports. Near a support the shear is largest and an inclined crack moves the bar force towards the support, so the bars need anchorage exactly where the moment is small. In the support example, two 20 mm bars needed Ld = 806 mm ≤ 1.3 M1/V + L0 = 964 mm; with 25 mm bars, Ld = 1007 mm passed that check but the length into the support (Ld/3 = 336 mm > 270 mm) failed without a bend.
  5. Laps and cut-offs: a lap shorter than max(Ld, 30φ) behaves like a short anchorage and pulls out; bunched laps create a weak section. A bar stopped at its theoretical cut-off is overstressed by the shift from inclined cracks; the extension d or 12φ (450 mm in the 6 m beam) covers this.
  6. Hooks and stirrups: a stirrup is anchored only with 90° + 8φ, 135° + 6φ or 180° + 4φ. Under torsion or cyclic load the 135° hook keeps the hoop closed after the cover spalls. An open or poorly hooked stirrup carries none of the Vus assumed in design.

Glossary

Aggregate interlock
Shear passed across a narrow crack by friction between its rough faces.
Anchorage bond
Bond needed at a bar end, where the whole bar force passes into the concrete.
Bar bending schedule
List of every bar with its mark, diameter, shape, number, cutting length and weight.
Bent-up bar
A main bar bent up near a support to act as inclined shear reinforcement; it may carry at most half of the shear steel's share.
Bond
Transfer of force between a bar and the concrete by adhesion, friction and the bearing of ribs.
Compatibility torsion
Torque that arises only because members are monolithic; IS 456 lets it be neglected if torsional stiffness is ignored in the analysis.
Dowel action
Shear carried by the main bars bending locally where they cross a crack.
Effective depth
Distance from the compression face to the centroid of the tension steel, d.
Equilibrium torsion
Torque the member must carry to keep the structure in equilibrium; it must be designed for.
Flexural bond
Bond needed where the bar force changes along the beam because the moment changes.
Flexure-shear crack
A crack that starts vertical at the soffit and turns inclined towards the load where both moment and shear are large.
Lap splice
Overlap of two bars that passes force from one to the other through the concrete.
Mechanical coupler
A threaded or swaged sleeve that joins two bars end to end, used for large bars and crowded sections.
Nominal cover
Design cover to all steel including links, set by the exposure class.
Shear span
Distance from a point load to the nearer support, a; the ratio a/d decides the mode of shear failure.
Side-face reinforcement
Longitudinal bars on the side faces of deep webs, 0.1 % of the web area, at 300 mm or less.
Skew bending
Failure of a beam under bending and torsion along a spiral crack on three faces with crushing on the fourth.
Space truss
Model of a cracked beam in torsion: closed stirrups as hoops, corner bars as stringers, spiral concrete struts.
Splitting
Bond failure in which the wedging of the ribs splits the cover along the bar.
Standard hook
A U-type bar end with an anchorage value of 16φ; a 90° bend is worth 8φ.
Stirrup
A closed or open link round the main bars that carries shear and holds the cage together.
Theoretical cut-off point
Where the moment falls to the moment of resistance of the bars that continue.
Truss analogy
Model of a cracked beam as a truss with concrete struts, stirrup ties and the main steel and compression zone as chords.
Web crushing
Crushing of the inclined concrete struts in a thin web under heavy shear, prevented by the τc,max limit.
Web-shear crack
An inclined crack that opens first inside a thin web near the neutral axis, close to a support.
