5.1 Classification of columns; effective length and slenderness

A column is a member that carries load mainly in compression. Before any steel is chosen, the designer sorts it: by the way its bars are held, by the way it is loaded, and above all by how slender it is. Slenderness depends on two things, the end restraint, which gives the effective length, and the size of the section. A short column fails by crushing of its materials; a slender column also bows sideways, and that bow adds a moment the designer must include.

You need the limit state of collapse in compression and the stress block (see Stress block parameters for limit state of collapse), and the idea of a moment of resistance from beam design.

Types of columns

Columns are grouped in four independent ways. A designer states all four before starting.

  • By lateral steel. A tied column has longitudinal bars held by closed ties at a pitch along the height. A helically reinforced (spiral) column has a continuous helix that confines the core, so it carries about 5 % more load and fails in a more ductile way. A composite column has a steel section inside the concrete.
  • By loading. An axially loaded column carries load through its centroid (only the minimum eccentricity applies). A column with uniaxial bending carries load plus moment about one axis. A column with biaxial bending has moments about both axes, as at the corner of a frame.
  • By shape. Square, rectangular, circular, and L or T shaped columns at wall corners.
  • By slenderness. Short or slender, set by the slenderness ratios below. A pedestal is a very short compression member whose effective length is not more than three times its least lateral dimension; it may be of plain concrete.

The frame matters too. A braced column belongs to a frame whose sideways movement is held by shear walls, bracing or a lift core. An unbraced column must resist sway by its own bending. The same column is usually short in a braced frame and may be slender in an unbraced one.

Effective length from end restraints

The unsupported length l is the clear height between the floor and the underside of the beam (or slab) that holds the column sideways. The length that buckles is the effective length le = K l, where K depends on how much each end can rotate and move. IS 456 Table 28 gives K for ideal end conditions; the recommended design values are a little larger than the theoretical ones because real joints are never fully fixed.

End conditionsTheoretical KDesign K (IS 456)
Fixed at both ends, no sway0.500.65
Fixed at one end, pinned at the other, no sway0.700.80
Pinned at both ends, no sway1.001.00
Fixed at both ends, one end free to sway1.001.20
Fixed at one end, free at the other (flagpole)2.002.00

For a column in a real frame, K comes from the stiffness of the beams meeting at each end (the charts in IS 456 Annex E). A braced frame gives K between 0.65 and 1.0; a sway frame gives K above 1.0.

Slenderness ratio, the limit of 12 and the minimum eccentricity

lex/D < 12 and ley/b < 12  →  short column

D is the section depth for bending about the major (x) axis and b the width for bending about the minor (y) axis, both in mm; lex and ley are the effective lengths for buckling about those axes, in mm. If either ratio is 12 or more, the column is slender.

Check both axes. The minor axis usually decides, because b is the smaller dimension. IS 456 also caps the unsupported length at 60 times the least lateral dimension, and for a column free at one end at 100b²/D. A slender column is not forbidden; it simply needs an additional moment for the sideways bow (IS 456 clause 39.7):

May = (Pu b / 2000) × (ley/b)²,  reduced by k = (Puz − Pu)/(Puz − Pb) ≤ 1

Pu is the factored axial load; Puz = 0.45fckAc + 0.75fyAsc is the pure axial capacity; Pb is the axial load at the balanced point (concrete crushing and tension steel yielding together). Max is the same expression with D and lex. Work it only about an axis whose ratio is 12 or more.

Even a short column is never loaded exactly through its centroid. IS 456 clause 25.4 therefore requires every column to be designed for a minimum eccentricity about each axis in turn:

emin = l/500 + D/30, not less than 20 mm

l is the unsupported length in mm and D is the lateral dimension in the direction of the eccentricity, in mm. The design moment is then at least Pu × emin.

Worked example

Question. A braced tied column has section b × D = 300 mm × 450 mm and unsupported length 3.6 m, effectively pinned at both ends for buckling about either axis. Find the slenderness ratios, classify the column, and find the minimum eccentricity about each axis.

  1. End conditions: pinned at both ends, no sway, so K = 1.0 and lex = ley = 1.0 × 3600 = 3600 mm.
  2. Major axis (bending in the direction of D = 450 mm): lex/D = 3600/450 = 8.0, which is less than 12.
  3. Minor axis (direction of b = 300 mm): ley/b = 3600/300 = 12.0. IS 456 calls a column short only when the ratio is less than 12, so a ratio of exactly 12.0 makes the column slender about the minor axis.
  4. Classification: slender about the minor axis only. The additional moment is needed about y; about x the column behaves as short.
  5. Minimum eccentricity about y (across b): 3600/500 + 300/30 = 7.2 + 10.0 = 17.2 mm, below the 20 mm floor, so emin,y = 20.0 mm.
  6. Minimum eccentricity about x (across D): 3600/500 + 450/30 = 7.2 + 15.0 = 22.2 mm, above 20 mm, so emin,x = 22.2 mm.

Answer. lex/D = 8.0 and ley/b = 12.0: the column is slender about the minor axis (12 is not less than 12). emin = 22.2 mm about x and 20.0 mm about y.

Worked example 2

Question. The same column carries Pu = 1200 kN and has 6 bars of 16 mm (3 on each 450 mm face), 8 mm ties, 40 mm clear cover, M20 concrete and Fe415 steel. Find the additional moment about the minor axis and its reduced value.

  1. Additional moment: May = (Pu b/2000)(ley/b)² = (1200 × 300/2000) × 12.0² = 180 × 144 = 25920 kN·mm = 25.92 kN·m.
  2. Steel: Asc = 6 × 201.1 = 1206 mm² (0.89 % of Ag = 135 000 mm²). Ac = 135000 − 1206 = 133794 mm².
  3. Puz = 0.45 × 20 × 133794 + 0.75 × 415 × 1206 = 1204.1 + 375.5 = 1579.6 kN.
  4. Balanced point about y: cover to bar centre d′ = 40 + 8 + 8 = 56 mm, so d = 300 − 56 = 244 mm and xu,b = 0.0035/(0.0035 + 0.0038) × 244 = 117.0 mm (0.0038 = 0.87fy/Es + 0.002 = 0.0018 + 0.002, the limiting tension strain for Fe415). Concrete force 0.36 × 20 × 450 × 117.0 = 379.0 kN; compression bars (strain 0.00182) add about 188.1 kN; tension bars at 0.87fy take 217.7 kN. Pb = 349.4 kN.
  5. Reduction factor: k = (1579.6 − 1200)/(1579.6 − 349.4) = 379.6/1230.2 = 0.309.
  6. Reduced additional moment: 0.309 × 25.92 = 8.00 kN·m. Compare the minimum-eccentricity moment Puemin,y = 1200 × 0.020 = 24.0 kN·m: the column is designed about y for its first-order moment (at least 24.0 kN·m) plus 8.00 kN·m.

Answer. May = 25.92 kN·m before reduction; with Puz = 1580 kN and Pb = 349 kN, k = 0.31 and the additional moment is 8.0 kN·m.

Exam traps

  • Calling a ratio of exactly 12 short. IS 456 says short only when both ratios are less than 12.
  • Checking only the major axis. The minor axis has the smaller dimension and usually the larger ratio.
  • Using the clear height in place of le, or the theoretical K (0.5) in place of the design K (0.65) for a fixed–fixed column.
  • Forgetting the 20 mm floor on emin, or using D for both axes. Each axis uses its own dimension.
  • Adding the full additional moment without the factor k. When Pu is well above Pb, k cuts Ma sharply.

5.2 Axially loaded short columns

A short column under axial load is the simplest compression member: concrete and longitudinal steel shorten together and share the load until the concrete crushes. IS 456 turns this into one line of algebra, so the design is quick, but three details decide whether the answer is right: the concrete area must exclude the steel, the steel must stay between 0.8 % and 6 % of the section, and the ties or helix must follow the code. Most interior columns of an ordinary building are designed this way, and circular columns with a helix are used where extra strength and ductility are wanted.

You need the column types and the minimum eccentricity from Classification of columns; effective length and slenderness.

The axial load formula and when it applies

At failure the concrete in a short column is at its design compressive strength and the steel has yielded. IS 456 clause 39.3 takes the concrete stress as 0.4fck and the steel stress as 0.67fy, values that already include a reduction for the minimum eccentricity:

Pu = 0.4 fck Ac + 0.67 fy Asc,  with Ac = Ag − Asc

Pu is the factored axial load in N; fck and fy are the characteristic strengths of concrete and steel in N/mm²; Ag is the gross area, Asc the area of longitudinal steel and Ac the net concrete area, all in mm².

The formula is valid only when emin does not exceed 0.05 times the lateral dimension. If emin is larger, design the column for Pu with the moment Puemin as in Uniaxial and biaxial bending; interaction diagrams. Solving the formula for the steel gives Asc = (Pu − 0.4fckAg)/(0.67fy − 0.4fck).

Rules for longitudinal bars and ties (IS 456 clause 26.5.3)

  • Longitudinal steel: at least 0.8 % and at most 6 % of Ag (4 % is a practical upper limit where bars are lapped).
  • At least 4 bars in a rectangular column and 6 in a circular column; bar diameter at least 12 mm; spacing along the perimeter not more than 300 mm.
  • Nominal cover to the longitudinal bars at least 40 mm (and not less than the bar diameter).
  • Tie diameter: not less than one quarter of the largest longitudinal bar, and not less than 6 mm.
  • Tie pitch: not more than the least of (i) the least lateral dimension, (ii) 16 times the smallest longitudinal bar diameter and (iii) 300 mm.
  • Every corner bar sits in a tie corner. A bar more than 75 mm from a restrained bar needs its own link, so the middle bar of a face gets an extra link.

Design steps: (1) write Pu, fck, fy and Ag; (2) check that the column is short and emin ≤ 0.05D; (3) find Asc from the formula and raise it to 0.8 % if smaller; (4) choose bars and check the capacity with the bars provided; (5) choose the ties.

Helically reinforced circular columns

A closely spaced helix confines the concrete core. When the core is fully confined the column keeps carrying load after the cover spalls. IS 456 clause 39.4 allows 1.05 times the tied-column strength if the helix is heavy enough:

Vhelix/Vcore ≥ 0.36 (Ag/Ak − 1) fck/fy

Vhelix and Vcore are the volumes of helix steel and of core concrete per unit height; Ak = πDk²/4 is the core area, Dk being the core diameter measured to the outside of the helix.

The helix pitch must be not more than 75 mm and not more than Dk/6, and not less than 25 mm or 3 times the helix bar diameter. A circular column with ordinary circular ties is designed like a tied column, without the factor 1.05.

Worked example

Question. Design a braced short tied column of 400 mm × 400 mm, unsupported length 3.0 m, for a factored axial load of 2000 kN. Use M20 concrete and Fe415 steel.

  1. Slenderness: even with K = 1, 3000/400 = 7.5 < 12, so the column is short. emin = 3000/500 + 400/30 = 6.0 + 13.3 = 19.3 mm → 20 mm (floor). 0.05D = 0.05 × 400 = 20 mm, so emin ≤ 0.05D and the axial formula applies.
  2. Ag = 400 × 400 = 160 000 mm². Steel limits: 0.008 × 160 000 = 1280 mm² to 0.06 × 160 000 = 9600 mm².
  3. Required steel: Asc = (2 000 000 − 0.4 × 20 × 160 000)/(0.67 × 415 − 0.4 × 20) = (2 000 000 − 1 280 000)/270.05 = 2666 mm².
  4. Try 8 bars of 20 mm: 2513 mm², less than 2666; capacity 1958.7 kN < 2000 kN, not enough. Use 6 bars of 25 mm: Asc = 6 × 490.9 = 2945 mm² (1.84 %), 3 bars on each of two opposite faces.
  5. Capacity with the bars provided: 0.4 × 20 × (160000 − 2945) + 0.67 × 415 × 2945 = 1256.4 + 818.9 = 2075.4 kN ≥ 2000 kN. Safe.
  6. Ties: diameter ≥ max(25/4 = 6.25 mm, 6 mm), so use 8 mm. Pitch ≤ least of 400 mm, 16 × 25 = 400 mm and 300 mm, so 300 mm. Each middle bar is about 140 mm from the corner bars (more than 75 mm), so it gets an extra 8 mm link.

Answer. 6 bars of 25 mm (2945 mm², 1.84 %) with 8 mm ties at 300 mm c/c and a link on each middle bar; capacity 2075 kN against 2000 kN.

Worked example 2

Question. A circular column of 400 mm diameter with a helix carries a factored axial load of 1800 kN. Use M25 concrete, Fe415 steel, 40 mm clear cover to the helix and an 8 mm helix. Design the longitudinal steel and the helix pitch.

  1. Ag = π × 400²/4 = 125664 mm². With the helix, Pu = 1.05(0.4fckAc + 0.67fyAsc), so the tied strength needed is 1800/1.05 = 1714.3 kN.
  2. Asc = (1714286 − 0.4 × 25 × 125664)/(278.05 − 10) = 457649/268.05 = 1707 mm². Minimum 0.8 % = 1005 mm².
  3. Provide 6 bars of 20 mm (the minimum number for a circular column): 1885 mm² (1.50 %). Capacity = 1.05 × (10 × 123779 + 278.05 × 1885) = 1850.0 kN ≥ 1800 kN.
  4. Core: Dk = 400 − 2 × 40 = 320 mm, Ak = π × 320²/4 = 80425 mm², Ag/Ak = 1.5625. Required ratio = 0.36 × (1.5625 − 1) × 25/415 = 0.01220.
  5. Helix volume per turn: 50.27 × π × (320 − 8) = 49269 mm³ (8 mm bar on a centre-line diameter of 312 mm). For pitch s, 49269/(80425 s) ≥ 0.01220 gives s ≤ 50.2 mm.

Exam traps

  • Using Ag for the concrete term. That counts the concrete displaced by the bars and gives less steel than needed.
  • Using the axial formula when emin > 0.05D (for example a 300 mm side with emin = 20 mm). Such a column must be checked for Pu with Puemin.
  • Quoting a fourth limit "48 × tie diameter" for the tie pitch. IS 456:2000 gives only three: least lateral dimension, 16 × smallest bar and 300 mm.
  • Taking the tie diameter from the smallest bar. It is one quarter of the largest bar, and never below 6 mm.
  • Applying the factor 1.05 to a circular column with plain ties, or measuring the core diameter to the centre of the helix instead of its outside.

5.3 Uniaxial and biaxial bending; interaction diagrams

Most columns carry a moment as well as an axial load: from beams on one side only, from wind, or simply from the minimum eccentricity. Load and moment cannot be checked separately, because a little axial compression actually helps a section resist bending while a lot of it hurts. The interaction diagram draws this as one curve of Pu against Mu for a given section and steel; a load case is safe if its point lies inside. SP 16 prints these curves in non-dimensional form, and for moments about both axes IS 456 adds a simple load-contour check.

You need the axial formula and bar rules from Axially loaded short columns, and strain compatibility with the stress block 0.36fckb xu acting at 0.42xu (see Stress block parameters for limit state of collapse).

How the interaction diagram is built

Pick a depth xu of the neutral axis. Strain is 0.0035 at the compressed face and varies linearly (when xu is larger than D, the code instead fixes 0.002 at 3D/7 from the compressed face). Each bar gets its strain, then its stress from the design stress–strain curve of the steel. Adding the concrete force and the bar forces gives Pu; taking their moments about the centroid gives Mu. Repeating for many xu traces the curve.

  • Pure axial load (xu → ∞): Puz = 0.45fckAc + 0.75fyAsc. The design cap for any column is the axial formula 0.4fckAc + 0.67fyAsc, which already allows for emin.
  • Balanced point: the tension steel reaches 0.87fy/Es + 0.002 (0.0038 for Fe415) as the concrete reaches 0.0035. The moment capacity is near its largest here.
  • Compression failure above the balanced load: more Pu means less Mu. Tension failure below it: more Pu means more Mu, because compression delays yielding of the tension steel.
  • Pure bending (Pu = 0): the section behaves as a doubly reinforced beam.

SP 16 plots Pu/(fckbD) against Mu/(fckbD²) with one curve per value of p/fck (p is the steel percentage), for Fe250, Fe415 and Fe500, for d′/D = 0.05, 0.10, 0.15 and 0.20, and for bars on two faces or spread round four faces. Circular columns have their own charts. The design procedure is: compute the two non-dimensional numbers, read p/fck, and find Asc = p b D/100.

Biaxial bending: the load-contour check

A corner column bends about both axes. IS 456 clause 39.6 uses the load-contour method (often called Bresler's method):

(Mux/Mux1)αn + (Muy/Muy1)αn ≤ 1.0

Mux, Muy are the factored moments about x and y (each at least Puemin); Mux1, Muy1 are the uniaxial moment capacities about x and y at the same Pu; αn = 1.0 when Pu/Puz ≤ 0.2, 2.0 when Pu/Puz ≥ 0.8, and linear in between: αn = 1 + (Pu/Puz − 0.2)/0.6.

  1. Find Pu, Mux and Muy; raise each moment to at least Puemin.
  2. Assume bars spread round four faces; take a trial p (often 1.5 to 2.5 %).
  3. Read or compute Mux1 and Muy1 at Pu.
  4. Compute Puz and αn.
  5. Check the load contour; if the sum exceeds 1, increase p and repeat.

Circular columns

A circular section with equally spaced bars is symmetric about every diameter, so moments about two axes combine into one: Mu = √(Mux² + Muy²), checked as uniaxial bending. Use at least 6 bars. The capacity changes slightly with how the bars sit relative to the bending axis; design charts use the weaker orientation.

Worked example

Question. A braced short column 300 mm × 500 mm, 3.5 m long, carries Pu = 1200 kN and Mu = 150 kN·m about the major axis. Use M20, Fe415, 8 mm ties, 40 mm cover and 20 mm bars on the two 300 mm faces. Find the steel and check it by strain compatibility.

  1. Minimum eccentricity: 3500/500 + 500/30 = 23.7 mm, so Puemin = 1200 × 0.0237 = 0.0 kN·m < 150 kN·m. Design for 150 kN·m.
  2. d′ = 40 + 8 + 10 = 58 mm, d′/D = 0.116 (between the 0.10 and 0.15 charts). Pu/(fckbD) = 1 200 000/(20 × 300 × 500) = 0.40; Mu/(fckbD²) = 150 × 10⁶/(20 × 300 × 500²) = 0.10.
  3. Strain compatibility gives the steel that makes the curve pass through this point: Asc = 2097 mm², p = 1.40 %, p/fck = 0.070. The SP 16 charts are drawn from the same theory, so a chart reading lands at about p/fck ≈ 0.07.
  4. Provide 8 bars of 20 mm, four on each 300 mm face: Asc = 2513 mm² (1.68 %).
  5. Check at Pu = 1200 kN: the neutral axis that gives this load is xu = 398.9 mm. Concrete: C = 0.36 × 20 × 300 × 398.9 = 861.6 kN at 0.416xu = 165.9 mm from the top.
  6. Top bars (depth 58 mm): strain 0.0035 × (398.9 − 58)/398.9 = 0.00299, stress 353.8 − 8.9 (concrete displaced) = 344.9 N/mm², force 433.4 kN. Bottom bars (depth 442 mm): strain -0.00038, stress -75.6 N/mm² (tension), force -95.0 kN.
  7. Sum: 861.6 + 433.4 − 95.0 = 1200.0 kN ✓. Moment about the centroid: 861.6 × (250 − 165.9)/1000 + (433.4 + 95.0) × 192/1000 = 72.4 + 101.5 = 173.9 kN·m ≥ 150 kN·m.

Answer. 8 bars of 20 mm (2513 mm², 1.68 %); at Pu = 1200 kN the section resists 174 kN·m against 150 kN·m. Ties 8 mm at 300 mm c/c with links on the middle bars.

Worked example 2

Question. A braced short corner column 400 mm × 400 mm, 3.0 m long, carries Pu = 1500 kN, Mux = 120 kN·m and Muy = 90 kN·m. Use M25 and Fe415 with 8 equal bars (3 on each face). Check 8 bars of 20 mm, and redesign if needed.

  1. emin = 3000/500 + 400/30 = 19.3 mm → 20 mm, so the minimum moment is 1500 × 0.020 = 30 kN·m, below both applied moments.
  2. Trial 8 bars of 20 mm: Asc = 2513 mm² (1.57 %). By symmetry Mux1 = Muy1; at Pu = 1500 kN strain compatibility gives 136.7 kN·m.
  3. Puz = 0.45 × 25 × (160 000 − 2513) + 0.75 × 415 × 2513 = 2554 kN. Pu/Puz = 0.587, so αn = 1 + (0.587 − 0.2)/0.6 = 1.646.
  4. Check: (120/136.7)1.646 + (90/136.7)1.646 = 0.807 + 0.503 = 1.31 > 1. Not safe.
  5. Try 8 bars of 25 mm: Asc = 3927 mm² (2.45 %), d′ = 60.5 mm. Mux1 = Muy1 = 183.5 kN·m at 1500 kN; Puz = 2978 kN; Pu/Puz = 0.504; αn = 1.506.

Exam traps

  • Reading Mux1 at zero axial load. It must be the capacity at the design Pu.
  • Taking αn as a constant 1.5 (or any guess). It comes from Pu/Puz, between 1.0 and 2.0.
  • Writing Puz with Ag. It is 0.45fckAc + 0.75fyAsc, with the net concrete area.
  • Saying moment capacity always falls as axial load rises. Below the balanced load it rises.
  • Checking a circular column separately about x and y. Combine the moments as √(Mux² + Muy²) first.

Understand Why does a small axial compression increase the moment capacity of a column section? 3 marks

5.4 Wall footings

A wall footing, or strip footing, is a long reinforced concrete slab under a load-bearing wall. It widens the wall's narrow base so the soil pressure stays below the safe bearing capacity. Each metre of wall is designed as a pair of cantilevers sticking out on both sides of the wall: the width comes from the soil, the depth from one-way shear and bending, and the steel from bending across the width. Only a few syllabi list it as a separate topic, but where it appears it is a standard long design question. You see it under brick compound walls, load-bearing houses and the walls of small sheds.

You need singly reinforced section design and the shear strength τc of IS 456 Table 19 (see Singly reinforced rectangular beams and Design of shear reinforcement).

Width, pressure and the critical sections

Take 1 m length of wall. With service load P (kN/m, wall plus what it carries) and safe bearing capacity qs (kN/m²), the width is B = (P + footing weight)/qs; the footing weight is often taken as 10 % of P at first and checked later. The soil pushes up on the slab with the net factored pressure wu = 1.5P/B. The footing's own weight is left out of wu: it sits directly on the soil and causes no bending.

Mu = wu lm²/2 per metre

lm is the distance from the free edge to the critical section for moment. IS 456 clause 34.2.3.2 puts that section at the face of a concrete wall, but halfway between the centre-line and the face of a masonry wall, so for a brick wall lm = (B − t)/2 + t/4, where t is the wall thickness.

The reason for the masonry rule: a brick wall is not monolithic with the footing, so it does not stiffen the slab right up to its face. One-way shear is checked at distance d (the effective depth) from the face of the wall, with Vu = wu((B − t)/2 − d). Punching shear does not arise for a continuous wall.

Depth, steel and detailing

  1. Depth for bending: d ≥ √(Mu/(0.138fck × 1000)) for Fe415. Footings are usually made deeper than this so that one-way shear passes without stirrups (IS 456 expects footings to carry shear by concrete alone in ordinary cases).
  2. Main steel across the width from Mu = 0.87fyAstd(1 − fyAst/(fckbd)), not less than 0.12 % of the gross section for Fe415.
  3. Development length: each bar must extend Ld = 0.87fyφ/(4τbd) beyond the critical section for moment. For M20 and Fe415, τbd = 1.2 × 1.6 = 1.92 N/mm², so Ld = 47φ.
  4. Distribution steel along the wall: 0.12 % of the gross section.
  5. Thickness at the edge at least 150 mm (IS 456 clause 34.1.2); the top may slope from the wall to the edge to save concrete. Clear cover 50 mm, on a 75 to 100 mm bed of lean concrete.

Worked example

Question. A 230 mm brick wall carries a service load of 160 kN per metre. The safe bearing capacity is 150 kN/m². Design the footing in M20 concrete with Fe415 bars.

  1. Width: allow 10 % for self-weight, B = 1.1 × 160/150 = 1.17 m. Adopt B = 1.20 m.
  2. Net factored pressure: wu = 1.5 × 160/1.20 = 200 kN/m². Projection from the wall face: (1.20 − 0.23)/2 = 0.485 m.
  3. Critical section for moment (masonry wall): 0.485 + 0.23/4 = 0.5425 m from the free edge. Mu = 200 × 0.5425²/2 = 29.43 kN·m per metre. (Taken at the face, as for a concrete wall, it would be only 23.52 kN·m.)
  4. Depth for bending: d = √(29.43 × 10⁶/(0.138 × 20 × 1000)) = 103.3 mm. Adopt D = 250 mm with 10 mm bars: d = 250 − 50 − 5 = 195 mm.
  5. Steel: Ast = 438 mm²/m (minimum 0.0012 × 1000 × 250 = 300 mm²/m). Provide 10 mm at 175 mm c/c: 449 mm²/m, pt = 0.230 %.
  6. One-way shear at d from the face: x = 0.485 − 0.195 = 0.290 m, Vu = 200 × 0.290 = 58.0 kN/m, τv = 58000/(1000 × 195) = 0.297 N/mm². τc for pt = 0.23 % (M20) = 0.344 N/mm² > τv. Safe.
  7. Development length of 10 mm bars: 47 × 10 = 470 mm. Available beyond the critical section: 542.5 − 50 = 492.5 mm ≥ 470 mm. (A 12 mm bar would need 564 mm, more than available, unless bent up.)
  8. Distribution: 0.0012 × 1000 × 250 = 300 mm²/m; provide 8 mm at 160 mm c/c (314 mm²/m).
  9. Soil check with the real self-weight: (160 + 0.25 × 1.2 × 25)/1.2 = 139.6 kN/m² ≤ 150. Safe.

Exam traps

  • Taking the moment at the face of a brick wall. For masonry the section is halfway between the centre-line and the face (t/4 inside the face), which gives the larger moment.
  • Putting the footing's self-weight into wu. It is counted for bearing, not for bending or shear.
  • Using the service pressure (here 133 kN/m²) with factored moments.
  • Adding stirrups to a thin footing that fails in shear. Make the footing deeper instead.
  • Forgetting the development length: a large bar can fail to anchor in a short projection.

5.5 Isolated footings: square, rectangular and circular

An isolated footing is a slab under a single column. It spreads the column load over enough soil to keep the pressure safe, and it bends upward like an upside-down cantilever slab on every side of the column. Its plan comes from the soil; its depth usually comes from shear, either one-way shear across the width or punching shear round the column; and its steel comes from bending at the column face. Square and rectangular pads are the commonest; a column moment makes the pressure uneven, and a sloped top saves concrete in large pads. Every framed building in India stands on footings like these.

You need the wall-footing steps of Wall footings, and the shear strength τc and development length Ld (see Design of shear reinforcement and Bond, anchorage and development length).

Plan size and soil pressure

Size the plan with service loads: A = (P + Wf)/qs, where P is the column load, Wf the footing weight (start with 10 % of P) and qs the safe bearing capacity. A square pad has side √A; a circular pad has diameter √(4A/π). For a rectangular column, make the projections equal on all sides (L − a = B − b, where a × b is the column), so that both directions carry similar moments.

With a column moment M (service), the pressure varies across the length: p = (P + Wf)/A ± M/Z, with Z = BL²/6 for bending along L. Keep pmax ≤ qs and pmin ≥ 0, which means e = M/P ≤ L/6. An alternative is to shift the footing by e so its centroid sits under the resultant, which makes the pressure uniform again for that load case.

Design for bending, shear and anchorage

Use the net factored pressure wu = Pu/A (or the trapezoid from Pu and Mu); the footing weight is left out. IS 456 clause 34 then fixes four checks:

  • Bending at the column face, in each direction, on the full width: Mu = wuB × (projection)²/2.
  • One-way shear at distance d from the column face, across the full width: τv = Vu/(Bd) ≤ τc (Table 19, with the pt provided).
  • Punching shear on a perimeter d/2 from the column faces: Vu = Pu − wu(a + d)(b + d), b0 = 2(a + b + 2d), τv = Vu/(b0d) ≤ ks × 0.25√fck, with ks = 0.5 + b/a ≤ 1.
  • Anchorage: bars extend Ld beyond the column face; column bars extend Ld (in compression) into the footing; bearing under the column is limited to 0.45fck√(A1/A2) with √(A1/A2) ≤ 2.

In a rectangular pad the long bars are spread evenly across B. The short bars are not: IS 456 clause 34.3.1(b) puts 2/(β + 1) of them in a central band of width B, where β = L/B, and the rest evenly in the two end strips. Minimum steel is 0.12 % of the gross section in each direction for Fe415.

Sloped footings

A large pad can be thick at the column and taper to an edge of at least 150 mm (IS 456 clause 34.1.2). The slope is kept gentle enough to cast without a top shutter, and a flat ledge of about 75 mm is left round the column for its formwork. Every check is then made with the depth at its own section: bending at the column face uses the full depth there, but only the concrete above the neutral axis counts in compression, so the narrow flat top limits the compression width; one-way shear at d from the face uses the reduced depth and the trapezoidal section at that line; punching uses the depth at d/2 from the face.

Worked example

Question. A 300 mm × 500 mm column carries a service load of 1200 kN. The safe bearing capacity is 200 kN/m². Design a rectangular footing with M20 concrete and Fe415 bars.

  1. Plan: A = 1.1 × 1200/200 = 6.60 m². Equal projections need L − B = 0.5 − 0.3 = 0.2 m, so B(B + 0.2) = 6.60, B = 2.47 m. Adopt L × B = 2.7 m × 2.5 m = 6.75 m²; both projections are (2.7 − 0.5)/2 = (2.5 − 0.3)/2 = 1.10 m.
  2. Net factored pressure: wu = 1.5 × 1200/6.75 = 266.67 kN/m².
  3. Moment per metre at either face: 266.67 × 1.10²/2 = 161.33 kN·m/m; d for bending = √(161.33 × 10⁶/2760) = 242 mm. Punching alone would need d ≈ 440 mm; one-way shear needs more. Adopt D = 600 mm, d = 600 − 50 − 16 = 534 mm (average of the two layers).

  5. Punching: Vu = 1800 − 266.67 × (0.5 + 0.534)(0.3 + 0.534) = 1570.0 kN, b0 = 2(500 + 300 + 2 × 534) = 3736 mm, τv = 0.787 N/mm². ks = 0.5 + 300/500 = 1.1 → 1.0; allowed 0.25√20 = 1.118 N/mm². Safe.

5.6 Combined footings

When two columns stand close together, or one sits near a boundary, their separate footings would overlap or spill outside the plot. A combined footing carries both columns on one slab. Its plan follows one rule: put the centroid of the slab under the resultant of the two column loads, so that the soil pressure is uniform. Lengthwise the slab then behaves like an upside-down beam loaded by the soil and supported at the columns; crosswise it is a short cantilever under each column. Two-column footings are common at lift wells, expansion joints and plot edges in city buildings.

You need the isolated-footing checks of Isolated footings, and shear and bending diagrams for beams.

Fixing the plan

  1. Add the service loads, P = P1 + P2, and find the area A = P/qs (plus about 10 % for self-weight when qs is a gross value).
  2. Locate the resultant from column 1: x̄ = P2s/(P1 + P2), where s is the column spacing.
  3. Choose the length L so the centre of the slab is at x̄: the projection beyond column 1 is a1 = L/2 − x̄, and beyond column 2 is a2 = L − s − a1. Then B = A/L (or fix B and find L = A/B).
  4. If a boundary limits a1 (say a1 = 0), L follows from L = 2x̄; if even that is impossible, use a trapezoidal footing or a strap footing.

With the centroid under the resultant, the factored soil pressure is uniform: qu = (Pu1 + Pu2)/(LB), and the line load along the footing is w = quB.

Longitudinal shear and bending

Draw the shear force and bending moment diagrams of a beam of length L carrying the upward line load w and the two downward column loads. Measuring x from the end beyond column 1:

V(x) = w x − Pu1[x > a1] − Pu2[x > a1 + s];  M(x) = w x²/2 − Pu1(x − a1) − Pu2(x − a1 − s)

a term in brackets counts only once x has passed that column; M positive means tension at the bottom.

  • Under each column (the cantilevers) the moment is sagging: tension at the bottom.
  • Between the columns the soil pushes the slab up between two supports, so it hogs: tension at the top. The largest hogging moment is where V = 0, at x = Pu1/w.
  • Depth is set by one-way shear at d from a column face (and checked for punching under the heavier column); footings carry shear by concrete, so a deeper slab is the usual answer. Steel: bottom bars for the largest sagging moment, top bars for the hogging moment, each at least 0.12 % of the gross section.

Transverse design under each column

Across the width the column load spreads out to the edges, so a strip under each column works as a cantilever of length (B − b)/2 from the column face, loaded by Pu/B per metre of width. Codes do not fix the width of this strip; a common choice is the column width plus d on each side. Its bottom bars sit above the longitudinal bottom bars, so their effective depth is one bar diameter less. Elsewhere, nominal transverse steel (0.12 %) is enough.

5.7 Cantilever retaining walls

A cantilever retaining wall holds back a bank of earth, for a basement, a road cut or a raised plot. It is an inverted T: a vertical stem fixed into a base slab, with the part of the slab under the retained soil called the heel and the part in front called the toe. The soil pushes the stem sideways; the soil standing on the heel holds the whole wall down. The wall is first checked as a rigid block against overturning, sliding and bearing, and only then are the stem, heel and toe designed as cantilevers. Only a few syllabi include it in this course, and walls up to about 6 m are of this type.

You need cantilever slab design (see Cantilever and continuous slabs) and the soil pressure ideas used in Wall footings.

Earth pressure and stability

For dry, level, cohesionless backfill the active pressure grows linearly with depth (Rankine): p = Kaγz, with Ka = (1 − sin φ)/(1 + sin φ), where γ is the soil unit weight and φ its angle of friction. Over the full height H, measured to the underside of the base, the thrust is Pa = ½KaγH² per metre, acting at H/3 above the base.

  • Overturning about the toe: IS 456 clause 20.1 asks that 0.9 × restoring moment ≥ 1.4 × overturning moment from the earth pressure, which is a factor of safety of 1.4/0.9 ≈ 1.55. Many textbooks quote 2.0 for overturning; use what your examiner expects, and state it.
  • Sliding: clause 20.2 asks the same on forces: 0.9 × μΣW ≥ 1.4 × Pa, where μ is the base friction coefficient (about 0.5 for most soils). A shear key under the base adds passive resistance when this fails.
  • Bearing: the resultant cuts the base at x = (MR − MO)/ΣW from the toe; e = B/2 − x must not exceed B/6, and pmax = (ΣW/B)(1 + 6e/B) ≤ qs.

Rules of thumb fix a first trial: base width about 0.5 to 0.6H, toe about B/3, base and stem thickness about H/12 to H/10.

Designing the parts

  • Stem: a vertical cantilever of height h (above the base) under the triangular pressure, Mu = 1.5Kaγh³/6 at its base. Main bars on the soil face, curtailed upward; distribution and nominal bars on the front face.
  • Heel: the soil and slab weight press down more than the soil reaction pushes up, so it bends downward from the stem: main bars at the top.
  • Toe: the soil pressure pushes it up: main bars at the bottom.
  • Shear in each part is carried by the concrete; anchor the stem bars into the base for their full development length.

Exam traps

  • Putting the thrust at H/2. The pressure is triangular, so its resultant is at H/3.
  • Leaving out the soil on the heel. It is usually the largest stabilising weight.
  • Putting the heel bars at the bottom or the toe bars at the top. Heel bends down (top tension), toe bends up (bottom tension).
  • Using factored loads in the stability checks without the 0.9 and 1.4 of clause 20.
  • Taking H as the stem height in the thrust. Earth pressure acts down to the underside of the base.

5.8 Water tanks

A water tank must hold water without leaking, and that changes the design aim. A beam may crack finely and still be safe; a tank wall with the same cracks would weep. So tanks are designed to keep concrete tension low or crack widths tiny under the working load, with low steel stresses and small, closely spaced bars. Water pressure rises with depth, a circular wall resists it by hoop tension, and a rectangular wall resists it by bending. Overhead tanks, underground sumps and treatment tanks across Indian towns are designed this way, to IS 3370.

You need hydrostatic pressure and the working stress idea (see Working stress method and permissible stresses), and cantilever slab bending from Cantilever retaining walls.

Water pressure and the design aim

p = γw h

p is the water pressure in kN/m² at depth h (m) below the water surface; γw is taken as 10 kN/m³ in design (9.81 kN/m³ for fresh water).

The pressure diagram on a wall is a triangle, zero at the surface and γwH at the base. IS 3370 (Part 2):2009 limits the calculated crack width of liquid-retaining faces to 0.2 mm. It also gives a simpler route: crack widths are taken as acceptable if the steel stress at working load does not exceed 130 N/mm² for high-yield deformed bars (115 N/mm² for plain bars). For the walls to be uncracked, the concrete tensile stress is checked against limits such as 1.5 N/mm² in direct tension and 2.0 N/mm² in bending for M30.

Minimum steel in each direction is 0.35 % of the concrete section for deformed bars, reducible to 0.24 % for tanks with no dimension over 15 m. Bar spacing should not exceed 300 mm or the wall thickness. Walls thinner than 200 mm may have all their steel in one layer. Tanks need two load cases: full (water pushing out) and, for underground tanks, empty (soil and ground water pushing in, and uplift).

Circular tanks with a flexible base: hoop tension

Cut a 1 m high ring at depth h in half. The water pushes on the projected area 2r × 1 with force 2pr; the two cut faces pull back with 2T. So the ring tension is:

T = p r = γw h r  (kN per metre height)

r is the internal radius in m. The hoop steel is As = T/σst with σst = 130 N/mm²; the concrete check is T/(1000t + (m − 1)As) ≤ σct, where t is the wall thickness in mm and m = 280/(3σcbc) is the modular ratio (9.33 for M30, σcbc = 10 N/mm²).

With a flexible (sliding) joint at the base, the wall carries pure hoop tension; the rings are heaviest at the bottom and can be reduced higher up. With a fixed base the wall also bends vertically near the bottom and needs vertical bars for that moment, from coefficient tables in IS 3370 (Part 4).

Rectangular tanks and detailing

A long wall of a rectangular tank, fixed at the base and free at the top, is a vertical cantilever under triangular pressure: M = γwH³/6 per metre at the base, with tension on the water face. Short walls span both ways between their neighbours and the base and are designed with plate coefficients. The base slab carries the water weight to the ground or, in an elevated tank, spans to the staging.

  • Use bars of 8 to 16 mm at 100 to 200 mm, lapped in a staggered pattern.
  • Use dense concrete of low water–cement ratio, cured well; M30 is the usual grade for liquid-retaining RC.
  • Seal construction joints with water bars; test the tank by filling it before finishes.

Worked example

Question. A circular tank of 6 m internal diameter holds water 3.5 m deep and has a flexible base joint. Design the wall at the base in M30 concrete with Fe415 bars, using σst = 130 N/mm² and σct = 1.5 N/mm².

  1. Pressure at the base: p = 10 × 3.5 = 35 kN/m². Hoop tension T = p r = 35 × 3 = 105 kN/m.
  2. Hoop steel: As = 105000/130 = 808 mm²/m. Provide 12 mm rings at 130 mm c/c: 870 mm²/m.
  3. Thickness for no cracking: 105000/(1000t + 8.33 × 808) ≤ 1.5 gives t ≥ 63 mm. That is far less than practical; adopt t = 150 mm (a common rule is 30H + 50 = 155 mm, H in m).
  4. Check: 105000/(1000 × 150 + 8.33 × 870) = 0.67 N/mm² ≤ 1.5. Safe.
  5. Vertical steel: minimum 0.24 % of 1000 × 150 = 360 mm²/m (tank under 15 m); spacing not over 150 mm, so 8 mm at 130 mm c/c (387 mm²/m). With t < 200 mm both layers may be in one face.
  6. Higher up: at 2.0 m depth (1.5 m below the top) T = 10 × 2.0 × 3 = 60 kN/m and As = 462 mm²/m, so 12 mm rings could open to 245 mm, but they are limited by the 150 mm spacing rule to 150 mm (754 mm²/m).

Answer. Wall 150 mm thick; hoop steel 12 mm at 130 mm c/c at the base (870 mm²/m against 808); concrete tension 0.67 N/mm²; vertical 8 mm at 130 mm c/c.

Exam traps

  • Using the diameter in T = pr. Use the radius.
  • Using the average pressure for the base ring. Each ring takes the pressure at its own depth.
  • Designing a tank wall at the limit state of collapse only. The working steel stress (130 N/mm²) or the 0.2 mm crack width governs.
  • Taking M = γwH³/2 for a cantilever wall. Triangular load gives H³/6 (45 kN·m/m for 3 m), uniform load would give pH²/2.
  • Forgetting the empty-tank case of an underground sump: outside soil pressure and flotation.

Takeaways

  1. Classify every column first: both slenderness ratios below 12 make it short; a ratio of 12 or more adds the additional moment about that axis.
  2. Every column carries at least Puemin; the axial formula 0.4fckAc + 0.67fyAsc is only for emin ≤ 0.05D.
  3. Ties follow three pitch limits: least lateral dimension, 16 times the smallest bar, 300 mm.
  4. A load–moment point is safe inside the interaction curve; moment capacity peaks near the balanced load.
  5. Biaxial bending uses capacities at the design Pu and an exponent αn set by Pu/Puz.
  6. Footings are sized with service loads and designed with net factored pressure; their own weight causes no bending.
  7. In footings one-way shear usually sets the depth; punching is checked at d/2 from the column.
  8. A combined footing puts its centroid under the resultant, then needs top steel between the columns and transverse steel under each.
  9. A retaining wall is checked as a rigid block (overturning, sliding, bearing) before its stem, heel and toe are designed.
  10. Water tanks are designed for watertightness: low steel stress, small bars at close spacing, and enough concrete to limit tension.

  7. Bearing: x = (198.1 − 64.0)/131.0 = 1.023 m from the toe; e = 1.2 − 1.023 = 0.177 m ≤ B/6 = 0.400 m. pmax = (131.0/2.4)(1 + 6 × 0.177/2.4) = 78.7 kN/m²; pmin = 30.5 kN/m². Both between 0 and 150.
  8. Stem (for completeness): Mu = 1.5 × (1/3) × 18 × 3.7³/6 = 76.0 kN·m/m; with d = 194 mm, Ast = 1253 mm²/m on the soil face.
  9. Why first: the stem, heel and toe moments depend on the base width, the toe length and the pressures under the base. If the wall overturns, slides or overloads the soil, the base must be resized, and every member moment changes. So the rigid-body checks fix the geometry; member design follows.
  10. Reading the result: friction alone gives a plain factor of 1.50, but IS 456 asks for 0.9 × resistance ≥ 1.4 × force, which is the same as a plain factor of 1.4/0.9 = 1.56. The wall is therefore short of resistance even though it would pass the older factor of 1.5. Other remedies are a wider heel (more soil weight on the base) or a rougher, deeper founding level; the key is cheapest because it adds passive resistance without changing the stem or the bearing pressures much.

Evaluate A circular water tank of 10 m internal diameter holds water 4 m deep and rests on a flexible base joint. Design the wall (M30, Fe415), giving the hoop steel at 1 m intervals, and explain why the steel stress is kept low. 13 marks

Points to cover. Pressure and hoop tension T = γwhr at each level; Steel at 130 N/mm² (IS 3370 Part 2); Thickness for no cracking with σct = 1.5 N/mm² and practical minimum; Minimum steel and spacing rules; Vertical steel; Why low steel stress.

Model answer.

  1. Data: internal diameter 10 m (r = 5 m), water depth 4 m, flexible base joint, M30 and Fe415. Working-stress values from IS 3370 (Part 2): σst = 130 N/mm² for deformed bars; σct = 1.5 N/mm² in direct tension; σcbc = 10 N/mm², so m = 280/(3 × 10) = 9.33 and m − 1 = 8.33. γw = 10 kN/m³.
  2. Hoop tension at depth h: T = 10 × h × 5 = 50h kN/m. At the base T = 200 kN/m.
  3. Thickness: no-crack check 200000/(1000t + 8.33 × 1538) ≤ 1.5 gives t ≥ 121 mm. The rule 30H + 50 = 170 mm and two layers of bars with 45 mm cover lead to t = 200 mm.
  4. Hoop steel (As = T/130, two layers because t ≥ 200 mm, spacing ≤ 200 mm = t):

  5. Minimum steel: tank under 15 m, so 0.24 % of 1000 × 200 = 480 mm²/m in each direction; the top metre is governed by it.
  6. Concrete tension at the base: 200000/(1000 × 200 + 8.33 × 1616) = 0.94 N/mm² ≤ 1.5. Safe.
  7. Vertical steel: the flexible base means no vertical bending, so the minimum 480 mm²/m: 8 mm bars at 200 mm c/c on each face (503 mm²/m). Base slab on the ground: minimum steel both ways, with a water bar at the wall joint.
  8. Why low stress: steel strain at working load sets the crack width. At 130 N/mm² the strain is 130/200 000 = 0.00065, and with small bars at close spacing the cracks stay within 0.2 mm, the limit IS 3370 sets for liquid-retaining faces. At a limit-state stress of 0.87 × 415 = 361 N/mm² the cracks would be nearly three times wider and the tank would leak.

Glossary

Balanced point
The load at which concrete reaches 0.0035 strain as the tension steel reaches its limiting strain; the interaction curve is widest near it.
Braced column
A column in a frame held against sideways movement by walls, bracing or a core, so its effective length is at most its clear length.
Combined footing
One footing carrying two or more columns.
Core diameter
The diameter of a helically reinforced column measured to the outside of the helix.
Critical section
The section where a check is made: the face for bending, d from the face for one-way shear, d/2 for punching.
Heel and toe
The parts of a retaining wall base behind and in front of the stem.
Helix
A continuous spiral of bar round the core of a circular column that confines the concrete.
Hoop tension
The ring tension in a circular tank wall caused by water pressure.
Interaction diagram
The curve of axial load against moment capacity for one section and steel arrangement.
Lateral tie
A closed loop of small bar round the longitudinal bars that stops them buckling and holds them in place.
Load contour
The curve of Mux against Muy at a fixed axial load, approximated by the exponent αn.
Modular ratio
m = 280/(3σcbc), used to add the steel to the concrete area in working-stress checks.
Net factored pressure
The upward soil pressure from the factored column load only, used for footing design.
Pedestal
A compression member whose effective length is not more than three times its least lateral dimension.
Safe bearing capacity
The pressure the soil can carry with an adequate margin against shear failure and excess settlement.
Shear key
A downstand under a retaining wall base that adds passive resistance against sliding.
Sloped footing
A footing whose top slopes from the column to a thinner edge to save concrete.
SP 16
The Bureau of Indian Standards handbook of design aids for reinforced concrete, with charts and tables for beams, slabs and columns.
Strap footing
Two pads joined by a stiff beam so that an eccentric pad at a boundary is balanced by an inner one.
Unbraced (sway) column
A column whose ends can move sideways; its effective length is more than its clear length.
Unsupported length
The clear height of a column between the floor and the underside of the beam or slab that holds it sideways.
Water bar
A strip of rubber or PVC cast across a construction joint to stop water seeping through.
