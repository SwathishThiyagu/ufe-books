5.1 Classification of columns; effective length and slenderness

A column carries load mainly in compression. Its behaviour changes if it can bend sideways between its ends. Two ideas decide that behaviour: how the ends are restrained, which gives the effective length, and how thin the column is, which gives the slenderness ratio. Together they sort a column into short or slender. Short columns are designed for axial load plus a small minimum eccentricity; slender columns need extra moments. This classification comes first in every column design, before steel is chosen in 5.2 or moments are checked in 5.3.

You need Unit 1 for limit state philosophy and material behaviour, and the bending picture of Unit 2.

Tied and spiral columns; braced and unbraced frames

A column is a vertical compression member. Two independent groupings matter before any steel is designed. The first is how the longitudinal bars are held: by ties or by a spiral. The second is how the frame resists sideways movement: braced or unbraced.

A tied column has vertical bars and separate lateral ties. A tie is a closed loop of small diameter bar, spaced along the height. Ties hold the bars in position and stop them buckling outward. Most building columns are tied columns.

A spiral column has one continuous helical spiral in place of ties. The spiral confines the concrete core. A confined core carries more load and deforms more before failure, so spiral columns are used in bridge piers and heavy industrial work. The design formulas differ, but the classification in this chapter is the same.

A braced column belongs to a frame with a lateral support system — shear walls, bracing or a stiff core — so the frame cannot sway. An unbraced column relies on its own frame action to resist sway. Braced frames give smaller effective lengths. Unbraced frames give larger ones. The same section can be short in a braced frame and slender in an unbraced frame.

Effective length from end restraints

The clear height between supports is the unsupported length l. It is not the length that controls buckling. A fixed end resists rotation; a pinned end does not. The effective length is l_eff = K l, where K is the effective length factor and l is the unsupported length.

End conditionsK
Both ends pinned1.0
Both ends fixed0.65
One fixed, one pinned0.80
One fixed, one free2.0

For a braced frame, K is usually between 0.65 and 1.0. For an unbraced frame, K is greater than 1.0. Use the value from the frame analysis or the code table for the actual end restraints; the four values above are the standard ideal cases.

Slenderness ratio and the 12 limit

The slenderness ratio about an axis is λ = l_eff / d. Here l_eff is the effective length about that axis and d is the lateral dimension perpendicular to that axis. For a rectangular section b × D, bending about the major axis has d = D; bending about the minor axis has d = b. Check both axes.

λ = l_eff / d

λ is the slenderness ratio (dimensionless); l_eff is the effective length about the axis, in mm; d is the lateral dimension perpendicular to that axis, in mm.

A column is short when both ratios are less than 12. It is slender when either ratio is 12 or more. The number 12 is a design line. Below it, the sideways deflection under load adds a second-order moment small enough to neglect. At or above it, that extra moment must be added, and the column is designed as slender. Some syllabi call the slenderness ratio the column slenderness.

Even a short column is not loaded perfectly axially. Construction tolerances and accidental load positions give a minimum eccentricity: e_min = l/500 + D/30, and not less than 20 mm. l is the unsupported length in mm. D is the lateral dimension in the direction of bending, in mm. e_min is in mm. The 20 mm floor prevents an unrealistically small design moment. Use the larger of the calculated value and 20 mm.

e_min = max( l/500 + D/30 , 20 mm )

e_min is the minimum eccentricity in mm; l is the unsupported length in mm; D is the lateral dimension in the direction of bending in mm; max( , ) takes the larger value.

Worked example

Question. A tied column has section b × D = 300 mm × 450 mm and unsupported length 3.6 m. Both ends are pinned in a braced frame. Find the effective length, the slenderness ratios about both axes, the minimum eccentricity about each axis, and classify the column as short or slender.

  1. Both ends pinned gives K = 1.0. The unsupported length is l = 3.6 m = 3600 mm. The effective length is l_eff = K l = 1.0 × 3600 = 3600 mm = 3.6 m.
  2. About the major axis, the lateral dimension is D = 450 mm. The slenderness ratio is λ_major = l_eff / D = 3600 / 450 = 8.0.
  3. About the minor axis, the lateral dimension is b = 300 mm. The slenderness ratio is λ_minor = l_eff / b = 3600 / 300 = 12.0.
  4. λ_minor = 12.0 is not less than 12, so the column is slender (borderline).
  5. Minimum eccentricity about the minor axis, lateral dimension in the direction of bending = b = 300 mm: e_min = 3600/500 + 300/30 = 7.2 + 10 = 17.2 mm. The floor is 20 mm, so e_min = 20.0 mm.
  6. Minimum eccentricity about the major axis, lateral dimension in the direction of bending = D = 450 mm: e_min = 3600/500 + 450/30 = 7.2 + 15 = 22.2 mm. This is above the floor, so e_min = 22.2 mm. The e_min formula takes the section dimension in the direction of bending, whichever axis is being checked.

Answer. Effective length = 3.6 m; slenderness ratios = 8.0 and 12.0; minimum eccentricities = 20.0 mm and 22.2 mm; the column is slender (borderline).

Exam traps

  • Using the unsupported length l in place of l_eff. The 12 limit applies to λ = l_eff / d, not to the clear height.
  • Checking only the major axis. The minor axis often has the larger ratio because its lateral dimension d is smaller.
  • Putting the wrong dimension in the denominator. For each axis, use the section dimension perpendicular to that axis; do not use the least lateral dimension for both axes.
  • Forgetting the 20 mm floor. l/500 + D/30 can come out below 20 mm, and then 20 mm governs.
  • Treating a spiral column automatically as slender. Ties and spirals describe construction; short or slender comes from the slenderness ratios.

5.2 Axially loaded short columns

A short column under axial load carries its load through concrete and longitudinal steel acting together. Design chooses steel area so the factored load stays below the section capacity, then adds ties to hold the bars straight and prevent buckling. The idea exists because columns are the one member whose failure can bring down a whole frame. You meet it in every RCC building: a G+3 apartment, a school hall, a factory shed. The arithmetic is short, but the concrete area must exclude the steel.

You need: Unit 1 for limit state and partial safety factors, and 5.1 to confirm the column is short.

Why the axial formula works

An axially loaded column carries load along its centroidal axis. In a short column, buckling does not govern; the section fails when concrete and steel reach their design strengths. The limit state of collapse in compression gives a simple addition of concrete capacity and steel capacity. The code permits this axial formula when the minimum eccentricity is at most 0.05 times the lateral dimension. If eccentricity is larger, the column bends and 5.3 applies.

Pu = 0.4 fck Ac + 0.67 fy Asc

Pu is factored axial load in N; fck is concrete cube strength in N/mm²; fy is steel yield strength in N/mm²; Ac = Ag − Asc is concrete area in mm²; Ag is gross area in mm²; Asc is steel area in mm².

The stress 0.4 fck is the design compressive strength of concrete in a column. It is lower than the 0.446 fck used for beam flexure. The steel stress is 0.67 fy. Steel must be between 0.8% and 6% of Ag. The minimum steel resists accidental bending and shrinkage; the maximum avoids congestion and keeps concrete carrying most of the load.

Design steps for a tied short column

Start with the factored load and the chosen section. Confirm from 5.1 that the column is short and that the minimum eccentricity e_min is within the axial limit. For the worked example, e_min = 20 mm on the 300 mm side, greater than 0.05 × 300 = 15 mm. Strictly, the section must then be designed for the minimum eccentricity moment; the axial formula is used here as the usual exam simplification, and the small moment is covered by the design margin. In practice such columns are checked with 5.3.

  1. Write Pu in N. Note fck, fy and Ag = b × D in mm².
  2. Find the steel needed from Asc = (Pu − 0.4 fck Ag) ÷ (0.67 fy − 0.4 fck). This comes from substituting Ac = Ag − Asc in the axial formula.
  3. Apply the limits: Asc must be at least 0.008 Ag and at most 0.06 Ag. Raise a small value to the minimum; if the required value exceeds 6%, enlarge the section.
  4. Choose bars: pick a diameter and a whole number of bars so the provided area is just above the required area. Check the percentage again.
  5. Check capacity with the provided area: Ac = Ag − Asc, then Pu,cap = 0.4 fck Ac + 0.67 fy Asc. It must be at least Pu.
  6. Design ties: diameter ≥ max(6 mm, largest longitudinal bar diameter ÷ 4). Spacing ≤ min(least lateral dimension, 16 × smallest longitudinal bar diameter, 40 × tie diameter, 300 mm). Use a convenient diameter and spacing within these limits.

Keep cover as required for durability. Cover is not part of the strength formula, but it fixes the bar positions. For a tied column, the ties are the only lateral restraint, so their spacing and diameter are not optional.

Where the axial formula stops

The axial formula is a strength check, not a slenderness check. A stocky column can still fail by buckling if it is slender; that is why 5.1 comes first. The formula also assumes the load is axial with only the minimum eccentricity. Real frames push moments into columns from beam loads, wind and earthquakes. Those moments need the interaction diagram of 5.3, even when the axial load is high. In ordinary gravity frames, many inner columns are close to axial, so this chapter covers a large share of columns in a building. Corner and edge columns usually carry moment and need the next chapter. Always recheck capacity with the bars actually provided, because rounding to a standard diameter changes the area. A small change in bar diameter can move capacity by tens of kilonewtons.

Worked example

Question. Design the longitudinal steel and ties for a short tied rectangular column 300 mm × 400 mm carrying a factored axial load of 1200 kN. Use M20 concrete and Fe415 steel. Take cover 40 mm.

  1. Pu = 1200 kN = 1,200,000 N. fck = 20 N/mm², fy = 415 N/mm². Ag = 300 × 400 = 120,000 mm². Minimum Asc = 0.008 × 120,000 = 960 mm². Maximum Asc = 0.06 × 120,000 = 7200 mm². For the 300 mm side, e_min = 20 mm is greater than 0.05 × 300 = 15 mm. Strictly, the section must then be designed for the minimum eccentricity moment; the axial formula is used here as the usual exam simplification, and the small moment is covered by the design margin. In practice such columns are checked with 5.3.
  2. Substitute Ac = Ag − Asc in Pu = 0.4 fck Ac + 0.67 fy Asc: 1,200,000 = 0.4×20×(120,000 − Asc) + 0.67×415×Asc. This gives 1,200,000 = 960,000 + 270.05 Asc.
  3. Asc needed = (1,200,000 − 960,000) ÷ 270.05 = 888.72 mm². This is below 960 mm², so the minimum governs. Required Asc = 960 mm².
  4. Try 4 bars of 20 mm: Asc = 4 × π × 20² ÷ 4 = 1256.64 mm². Percentage = 1256.64 ÷ 120,000 × 100 = 1.047%, between 0.8% and 6%.
  5. Capacity check: Ac = 120,000 − 1256.64 = 118,743.36 mm². Pu,cap = 0.4×20×118,743.36 + 0.67×415×1256.64 = 949,946.9 + 349,407.9 = 1,299,354.8 N = 1299.35 kN. This exceeds 1200 kN, so safe.
  6. Ties: diameter ≥ max(6 mm, 20 ÷ 4 = 5 mm) = 6 mm; use 8 mm. Spacing ≤ min(300 mm, 16 × 20 = 320 mm, 40 × 8 = 320 mm, 300 mm) = 300 mm. Use 8 mm ties at 300 mm c/c. Cover 40 mm.

Answer. Use 4 bars of 20 mm (Asc = 1256.64 mm², 1.047% of Ag) with 8 mm ties at 300 mm c/c; the section capacity is 1299.35 kN, greater than the factored load of 1200 kN.

Exam traps

  • Using Ag instead of Ac = Ag − Asc in the concrete term. This overcounts concrete and gives a lower, unsafe steel area.
  • Stopping at the calculated Asc when it is below 0.8% of Ag. The minimum steel must be enforced.
  • Borrowing 0.446 fck from beam flexure for a column. The axial column formula uses 0.4 fck.
  • Taking tie diameter as one-quarter of the smallest bar. The rule is one-quarter of the largest longitudinal bar, and never less than 6 mm.
  • Using 16 times the largest bar, or forgetting the 40 × tie diameter and 300 mm caps, for tie spacing. The spacing limits are the least lateral dimension, 16 times the smallest longitudinal bar, 40 times the tie diameter, and 300 mm; the smallest governs.

5.3 Uniaxial and biaxial bending; interaction diagrams

A column that carries axial load and bending must be checked as one combined problem. The interaction diagram plots factored axial load Pu against factored moment Mu. Every point inside the curve is a safe load-moment pair for that section and steel. For bending about both axes, two uniaxial capacities are used in a load contour check. Designers use this for building columns, bridge piers and industrial frames, where loads are rarely perfectly centred.

You need: 5.1 for short and slender columns, 5.2 for axial capacity, and Unit 1 for partial safety factors and stress block parameters.

The interaction diagram

Pu is the factored axial load in kN. Mu is the factored moment in kN·m about the axis being checked. A short column section has a limit curve in the Pu-Mu plane. The curve is the interaction diagram.

The curve has three landmarks. At pure compression, Mu is zero and Pu is largest. At the balanced point, concrete reaches its limit strain and steel yields at the same time. At pure bending, Pu is zero and the section carries only Mu. Between these points the curve bends inward. Any load-moment pair inside the curve is safe; any pair outside is unsafe.

Design charts, such as SP 16, give Mux1 for a chosen section, steel percentage and concrete grade at a given Pu. Read Mux1 on the horizontal axis and Pu on the vertical axis. The chart already uses factored loads and limit state material strengths. Do not mix service loads with chart values.

emin = l/500 + D/30, with emin ≥ 20 mm

emin is the minimum eccentricity in mm; l is the unsupported length in mm; D is the lateral dimension in the plane of bending in mm.

Real columns are never perfectly axial. The minimum moment is Pu emin. Add it to the calculated moment in that axis before reading the chart. This covers unavoidable load eccentricity and construction tolerance.

Biaxial bending and the load contour

Biaxial bending means moment about both principal axes at once. Corner columns and columns in frames under wind or earthquake commonly have Mux and Muy together. A uniaxial check on each axis alone is not enough.

(Mux/Mux1)αn + (Muy/Muy1)αn ≤ 1

Mux and Muy are applied factored moments about the x and y axes in kN·m. Mux1 and Muy1 are the uniaxial moment capacities about the same axes at the same Pu, in kN·m. αn is the load contour exponent, usually between 1 and 2.

For a fixed Pu, the safe biaxial capacity is a curve in the Mux-Muy plane. The exponent αn shapes this curve. αn near 1 gives a straight line; αn near 2 gives a rounded contour. At low axial load use a value nearer 1; at high axial load use a value nearer 2, or take the value from the code or chart.

The method is often called the Bresler load contour method. Use Mux1 and Muy1 read at the same Pu. Do not use the pure moment capacity or the capacity at a different axial load.

  1. Compute factored Pu and the factored moments Mux and Muy, including the minimum eccentricity moment in each axis.
  2. Choose a trial section and longitudinal steel. Read Mux1 and Muy1 from SP 16 at the same Pu.
  3. Check each axis alone: Mux ≤ Mux1 and Muy ≤ Muy1.
  4. Check the biaxial load contour with the specified αn.
  5. If the contour sum is greater than 1, increase the section or the steel and repeat.

Charts, circular columns and slenderness

Rectangular column charts are normalized. The axes are Pu/(fck b D) and Mu/(fck b D²), and the curve parameter is p/fck, where fck is the concrete grade in N/mm², b and D are the section dimensions in mm, and p is the steel percentage. Choose the chart for the concrete grade and bar layout, then convert back using the actual b and D. Circular column charts use the ratio of steel area to gross area; a symmetric circular section has the same uniaxial moment capacity about any diameter. Check steel percentage limits and tie detailing from Unit 3.

If one moment dominates, compare each applied moment with its uniaxial capacity first. If either ratio exceeds 1, the section fails before the biaxial check. The contour check then decides the combined case.

Slender columns add second-order moment. Effective length and slenderness are in 5.1. A short column ignores second-order effects, but it does not ignore minimum eccentricity.

Worked example

Question. A 300 mm × 400 mm short column has 4 bars of 20 mm diameter, M20 concrete and Fe415 steel. It carries Pu = 800 kN, Mux = 90 kN·m and Muy = 40 kN·m. From SP 16 charts, Mux1 = 130 kN·m and Muy1 = 100 kN·m at Pu. Check the section for biaxial bending using αn = 1.5.

  1. Write the data: b = 300 mm, D = 400 mm, Pu = 800 kN, Mux = 90 kN·m, Muy = 40 kN·m, Mux1 = 130 kN·m, Muy1 = 100 kN·m, αn = 1.5.
  2. Assume the given moments already include the minimum eccentricity moment. If not, add Pu emin to each axis first.
  3. Steel area: Asc = 4 × π × 20²/4 = 1256.6 mm². This is about 1.05% of 300 × 400 mm², which matches the chart steel percentage.
  4. Check the x-axis ratio: Mux/Mux1 = 90/130 = 0.692.
  5. Raise to αn: 0.6921.5 = 0.576.
  6. Check the y-axis ratio: Muy/Muy1 = 40/100 = 0.400.
  7. Raise to αn: 0.4001.5 = 0.253.
  8. Add: 0.576 + 0.253 = 0.829.
  9. 0.829 ≤ 1.0, so the section passes the biaxial load contour check.

Answer. The column is safe for biaxial bending; the interaction sum is 0.829 ≤ 1.0.

Exam traps

  • Adding the two moment ratios linearly when αn is not 1. The code check uses each ratio raised to αn.
  • Reading Mux1 or Muy1 at a different Pu, or using service moments instead of factored moments.
  • Forgetting the minimum eccentricity moment Pu emin before the chart is read.
  • Using the uniaxial capacity about the wrong axis, or swapping b and D in the chart.
  • Treating a slender column's first-order moment as final; second-order effects belong with effective length in 5.1.

5.4 Wall footings

A wall footing, also called a strip footing, spreads a long wall load over soil. The wall sits on a reinforced concrete slab that projects on both sides. Its width comes from service load divided by safe bearing capacity. Its thickness comes from bending at the wall face and one-way shear at distance d from that face. Only a few universities teach this. You meet it under compound walls, load-bearing brick buildings and boundary walls, where a narrow strip is cheaper than many isolated pads.

You need: beam bending from Unit 2, shear from Unit 3, and one-way slab detailing from Unit 4.

Width and bending

A wall footing is a long reinforced concrete slab under a wall. The wall load P is in kN per metre length. The safe bearing capacity q_safe is in kN/m². Choose the width from service load: B = P / q_safe. Include footing self-weight; if it is not included, add about 10% to P. B is in metres. The wall thickness t_w is in metres. The slab projects a = (B − t_w)/2 on each side. This projection is the cantilever that bends the slab. A 230 mm wall on B = 1.2 m gives a = 0.485 m. The factored soil pressure is w_u = 1.5 P / B in kN/m². Use w_u for strength checks, never the service pressure. The maximum moment per metre length is at the wall face: M_u = w_u a² / 2. M_u is in kN·m per metre. The free edge has zero moment. For M20 concrete and Fe415 steel, the limiting depth is d_req = √(M_u × 10⁶ / (0.138 f_ck b)). Use b = 1000 mm. Adopt overall depth D with cover 50 mm and bar diameter φ: d = D − 50 − φ/2. Keep edge thickness at least 150 mm. Main bars run across the width; find A_st as for a singly reinforced beam (see Unit 2).

Shear and detailing

Check one-way shear at a section d from the wall face. The cantilever length beyond it is a − d. Then V_u = w_u (a − d) per metre. The shear stress is τ_v = V_u × 10³ / (1000 d), in N/mm². Compare with τ_c from IS 456 Table 19 at p_t = 100 A_st /(1000 d). If τ_v exceeds τ_c, increase D; shear reinforcement is not provided in footings. Distribution steel runs along the wall length. Provide 0.12% of the gross section: A_st,dist = 0.0012 × 1000 × D, in mm² per metre. For D = 200 mm, use 8 mm bars at 200 mm c/c. Cover is 50 mm on the soil face. Punching shear does not govern a long strip; see isolated footings for that check. A uniform 200 mm slab suits light walls.

Worked example

Question. A 230 mm brick wall carries a service load of 180 kN per metre length; this value includes the footing self-weight. The safe bearing capacity of the soil is 150 kN/m². Design the width and thickness of a wall footing using M20 concrete and Fe415 steel. Take cover 50 mm.

  1. Service width: B = 180 / 150 = 1.20 m. Projection: a = (1.20 − 0.23) / 2 = 0.485 m.
  2. Factored soil pressure: w_u = 1.5 × 180 / 1.20 = 225 kN/m².
  3. Moment at wall face: M_u = 225 × 0.485² / 2 = 26.46 kN·m/m.
  4. Required effective depth: d_req = √(26.46 × 10⁶ / (0.138 × 20 × 1000)) = 97.9 mm.
  5. Adopt D = 250 mm and 10 mm bars: d = 250 − 50 − 5 = 195 mm.
  6. Steel from beam formula: A_st = 392 mm²/m. Provide 10 mm @ 140 mm c/c = 561 mm²/m; p_t = 0.29%.
  7. Shear at d from face: x = 0.485 − 0.195 = 0.290 m; V_u = 225 × 0.290 = 65.3 kN/m; τ_v = 65.3 × 10³/(1000 × 195) = 0.33 N/mm². τ_c ≈ 0.38 N/mm² for p_t = 0.29%. τ_v < τ_c, so no shear reinforcement is needed.
  8. Distribution steel: 0.0012 × 1000 × 250 = 300 mm²/m. Provide 8 mm @ 160 mm c/c = 314 mm²/m.

Exam traps

  • Using service soil pressure with a factored moment. Multiply the wall load by 1.5 first.
  • Taking the bending moment at the footing edge. The critical section is at the wall face, because that section carries the full projection a as its lever arm; at every section closer to the free edge the remaining projection, and hence the moment, is smaller.
  • Using overall depth D in place of effective depth d in bending and shear.
  • Confusing distribution steel along the wall with main steel across the width; they run at right angles.

5.5 Isolated footings: square, rectangular and circular

An isolated footing carries one column and spreads its load over enough soil to keep bearing pressure safe. The pad may be square, rectangular or circular in plan. It is sized from service load and safe bearing capacity, then designed for factored bending, one-way shear and punching shear. You see these footings under building columns, compound-wall piers and small industrial sheds.

You need: 5.2 for column load and bars, 5.4 for bearing pressure, and Unit 3 for shear and development length.

Size from bearing

Start with service load P in kN and safe bearing capacity qs in kN/m². The plan area must satisfy A ≥ P/qs. For a square footing, side L = √A. For a rectangular footing, choose L and B so LB ≥ A, often with L/B between 1 and 2. For a circular footing, diameter D = √(4A/π). Round up to a practical size, usually a multiple of 50 mm.

Then add the footing self weight and check p = (P + Wf)/A ≤ qs, where Wf is self weight in kN. A quick first guess is Wf = 0.1P. Use service loads for soil pressure and factored loads for concrete design.

For an eccentric column load, pressure is not uniform: pmax = P/A + M/Z and pmin = P/A − M/Z, where M is service moment in kN·m and Z is section modulus in m³. Keep pmax ≤ qs and pmin ≥ 0 so the soil does not lift. For a rectangular pad of sides L and B, bending about the axis parallel to B gives Z = BL²/6 in m³; use the axis about which the moment acts.

A ≥ P/qs; L = √A; D = √(4A/π)

P is service column load in kN; qs is safe bearing capacity in kN/m²; A is plan area in m²; L is square side and D is circular diameter, both in m.

Bending at the column face

Use factored column load Pu = 1.5P for concrete design. The net upward pressure is wu = Pu/A in kN/m². For a square pad of side L and a square column of side c, the cantilever projection is l = (L − c)/2. Bending is checked at the column face, not at the pad edge. The moment per metre width is Mu = wul²/2. For a rectangular pad, take b as the column side parallel to L and c as the column side parallel to B, so lx = (L − b)/2 and ly = (B − c)/2 give moments in the two directions. For a circular pad, a simple first design treats a unit-width radial strip as a cantilever of length (D − dc)/2, where dc is column diameter.

Find effective depth from the limiting moment: Mu,lim = 0.138 fckb d² for Fe415 steel, with b = 1000 mm. So d = √(Mu × 10⁶/(0.138 fck × 1000)). Use 0.133 for Fe500 and 0.148 for Fe250. Then find steel from Mu = 0.87fyAstd(1 − fyAst/(fckbd)). Provide at least 0.12% of gross area for Fe415, and 0.15% for mild steel. Check both directions. The longer projection gives the larger moment per metre, so the direction of the longer side of the pad usually needs more steel; the shorter direction needs less.

wu = Pu/A; Mu = wul²/2; d = √(Mu × 10⁶/(0.138 fck × 1000))

Pu is factored column load in kN; A is plan area in m²; wu = Pu/A is in kN/m²; l is the projection in m; Mu = wul²/2 is in kN·m per metre width (multiply by 10⁶ to get N·mm); fck is in N/mm²; b is taken as 1000 mm and d comes out in mm.

One-way shear, punching and anchorage

One-way shear is checked on a vertical section at distance d from the column face. For a square footing, Vu = wuL(l − d), with l = (L − c)/2. The stress is τv = Vu/(B d) ≤ τc, where B is the width at that section and τc is the concrete shear strength from Unit 3. If τv exceeds τc, increase the depth.

Punching shear is checked on a perimeter at d/2 from the column face. For a square column of side c, perimeter b0 = 4(c + d) and inside area = (c + d)². The net punching force is Vu = Pu − wu(c + d)². The stress is τv = Vu/(b0d) ≤ ksτc, where ks = 0.5 + βc ≤ 1 and βc is short side/long side of the column. For a square column, ks = 1. For a circular column, b0 = π(dc + d).

Column bars must extend into the footing for development length Ld. For M20 concrete and Fe415 steel, Ld ≈ 47φ in tension. Provide at least that length below the column face. A sloped footing saves concrete, but its depth changes, so repeat bending, one-way shear and punching checks at each critical section.

b0 = 4(c + d); Vu = Pu − wu(c + d)²; τv = Vu/(b0d) ≤ ksτc

b0 is punching perimeter in mm; c is square column side in mm; d is effective depth in mm; Vu is net punching force in N; wu is net upward pressure; ks is 0.5 + βc but not more than 1.

Worked example

Question. An isolated square footing supports a 400 mm square column with a service load of 1000 kN. The safe bearing capacity is 200 kN/m². Use M20 concrete and Fe415 steel. Find the footing size and the depth required for bending at the column face. Take cover 50 mm and assume 16 mm bars.

  1. 1. Plan area: A = 1000/200 = 5.0 m². Side = √5.0 = 2.24 m. Adopt L = 2.3 m, so actual area = 5.29 m².
  2. 2. Self-weight check: assume 300 mm thickness. Wf = 0.3 × 5.29 × 25 = 39.7 kN. p = (1000 + 39.7)/5.29 = 196.5 kN/m² < 200 kN/m². Safe.
  3. 3. Factored load: Pu = 1.5 × 1000 = 1500 kN. Net pressure: wu = 1500/5.29 = 283.55 kN/m².
  4. 4. Projection: l = (2.3 − 0.4)/2 = 0.95 m. Mu = 283.55 × 0.95²/2 = 127.95 kN·m per metre width.
  5. 5. For M20 and Fe415: d = √(127.95 × 10⁶/(0.138 × 20 × 1000)) = √46359 = 215.3 mm. Adopt d = 220 mm.
  6. 6. Overall depth with 50 mm cover and 16 mm bars: D = 220 + 50 + 8 = 278 mm. Adopt 300 mm.

Exam traps

  • Punching shear is checked on the perimeter at d/2 from the column face, not on the column face. The column-face perimeter gives a much larger stress and is wrong.
  • Use service load and safe bearing capacity for sizing. Use factored load and net upward pressure for bending and shear. Mixing them is the commonest error.
  • Do not include the footing self weight in the net upward pressure for bending; it is balanced by soil reaction under the whole pad. Do include it in the bearing pressure check.
  • Effective depth is not overall depth. Subtract cover and half the bar diameter.
  • In a rectangular footing the two directions have different projections, so they have different moments per metre.

5.6 Combined footings

Two close columns need one footing, not two. Isolated footings would overlap, or one would sit on softer ground. A combined footing spreads both loads over one base. Its plan is fixed by one rule: the centroid of the base area must lie under the resultant of the two loads, so soil pressure stays uniform. The footing is then designed as a beam along the columns, plus a transverse strip under each column. Boundary columns, twin columns and lift shafts use this form.

You need: isolated footings for bearing pressure, one-way shear and punching shear, and Unit 2 for singly reinforced beam design.

Fix the plan: centroid under the resultant

A combined footing puts two column loads on one base. Pressure is uniform only when the resultant of the two loads passes through the centroid of the base area. If it does not, the base tilts, pressure becomes trapezoidal, and one end may exceed the safe bearing capacity.

  1. Add service loads: P = P1 + P2 in kN. Use service loads because q_safe in kN/m² is a working-load value.
  2. Area A = P / q_safe in m². Add about 10% to P for self weight unless q_safe already covers it.
  3. Choose width B, then length L = A / B, both in m.
  4. Take moments about column 1: x̄ = P2 s / (P1 + P2), where s is spacing in m.
  5. Place the base centroid at x̄ from column 1. The base runs L/2 each side, so projection beyond column 1 is a1 = L/2 − x̄, and beyond column 2 is a2 = L − s − a1.
  6. Both projections must be positive. A negative a1 means L is too short for this load pattern.
x̄ = (P1 × 0 + P2 × s) / (P1 + P2),   q = (P1 + P2) / (B L) ≤ q_safe

x̄ is distance of resultant from centre of column 1, in m; P1 and P2 are service loads in kN; s is spacing in m; q is uniform soil pressure in kN/m²; B and L are base width and length in m.

Upward pressure and longitudinal bending

Multiply service loads by 1.5 for dead plus live to get Pu1 and Pu2. Factored soil pressure is q_u = (Pu1 + Pu2)/(B L) in kN/m², acting upward.

Along its length the footing is a beam of width B and overall depth D. Soil gives upward line load w = q_u B in kN/m; the columns are downward point loads.

M(x) = w x²/2 − Pu1 (x − a1) − Pu2 (x − a1 − s)

M(x) is bending moment at x metres from left end, in kN·m; w = q_u B is in kN/m; a1 is left projection in m; s is spacing in m; a term is dropped while its bracket is negative.

The footing bends opposite to a normal beam. Under projections it sags: tension at bottom, so bottom bars run under each column. Between columns it hogs: tension at top, so top bars are needed. Leaving out top bars is the classic error.

Design the section as a rectangular beam of width B and depth D using Unit 2. Depth is usually fixed by punching shear first, then checked in bending.

Transverse bending, one-way shear and punching

Across its width the footing works like a small isolated footing under each column. The strip under a column cantilevers c = (B − b)/2 on each side, where b is column size in m.

M_t = q_u c² / 2

M_t is transverse moment per metre of footing length, in kN·m/m; c = (B − b)/2 is projection beyond column face in m; q_u is factored soil pressure in kN/m².

Transverse bars sit at the bottom under each column, over a band about the column width plus one effective depth each side.

Check one-way shear on a plane at d from the column face across width B; shear is soil pressure outside that plane. Check punching shear on a perimeter at d/2 from the face, with b0 = 4(b + d) for a square column and Vu = q_u × (area outside perimeter). Both follow Unit 3; the heavier column governs.

5.7 Cantilever retaining walls

A cantilever retaining wall holds back soil. The stem is vertical and fixed into a base slab. The heel of the slab carries soil; the toe sticks out in front. Soil pushes the stem, which bends as a cantilever. The whole wall must not tip, slide or sink. These walls appear at basements, road embankments, ramps and plot boundaries. The wall is checked for stability first, then stem, heel and toe are designed.

You need cantilever bending and shear from Unit 2, footing sizing by bearing pressure from 5.5, and bar detailing from Unit 3.

Parts and soil pressure

The stem is the vertical wall. The base slab lies under it. The slab behind the stem is the heel; the part in front is the toe. Soil on the heel stabilizes the wall. The stem acts as a vertical cantilever fixed to the base; heel and toe act as horizontal cantilevers fixed to the stem.

For dry, level, cohesionless backfill, pressure grows linearly with depth. Ka = (1 − sin φ)/(1 + sin φ) = tan²(45° − φ/2), where φ is the soil friction angle.

P = ½ Ka γ H²

P is active thrust in kN per metre length; Ka is active pressure coefficient (no unit); γ is soil unit weight in kN/m³; H is retained height in m.

Pressure at depth z is p = Ka γ z, so at the base p = Ka γ H. The diagram is a triangle. Thrust is its area, at H/3 above the base.

Stability checks before strength

Check the wall as a rigid body under working loads before steel design.

  1. Sum vertical loads: stem, base slab, soil on heel. Take moments about the toe.
  2. Overturning: resisting moment ÷ overturning moment (P × H/3). Keep about 2.0 unless your syllabus or code says otherwise.
  3. Sliding: μ ΣV ÷ P, about 1.5, with μ ≈ tan φ. A shear key helps if this fails.
  4. Bearing: e = M/ΣV about base centre; pmax = (ΣV/B)(1 + 6e/B). Keep pmax below safe bearing capacity and e ≤ B/6, so no soil tension.

ΣV is total vertical load in kN per metre, B is base width in m, M is net moment about base centre in kN m, and e is eccentricity in m.

Designing the stem, heel and toe

Multiply earth pressure effects by 1.5. Design each part as a unit-width cantilever slab.

  • Stem: base moment 1.5 × P × H/3; base shear 1.5 × P. Main steel on soil face; nominal steel on other face.
  • Heel: net downward load (soil plus slab self weight minus upward soil reaction) makes it a cantilever fixed at the back face of the stem. Tension is at the top, so main steel at top of heel; nominal steel at bottom.
  • Toe: net upward soil pressure (minus toe self weight) makes it a cantilever fixed at the front face of the stem. Tension is at the bottom, so main steel at bottom of toe.
  • Check one-way shear at the stem face and anchor stem bars into the base slab for full development length.

Worked example

Question. A wall retains 4 m of soil, γ = 18 kN/m³, φ = 30°, Ka = 1/3. Find base pressure, thrust per metre, resultant height and factored stem base moment.

  1. Ka = tan²(45° − 30°/2) = tan²30° = 1/3.
  2. Base pressure p = Ka γ H = (1/3) × 18 × 4 = 24 kN/m².
  3. Thrust P = ½ Ka γ H² = 0.5 × (1/3) × 18 × 4² = 48 kN per metre.
  4. Resultant height = H/3 = 4/3 = 1.333 m above base.
  5. Mu = 1.5 × P × H/3 = 1.5 × 48 × 1.333 = 96 kN m per metre.

Answer. Base pressure 24 kN/m²; thrust 48 kN per metre at 1.33 m above base; factored stem base moment 96 kN m per metre.

Exam traps

  • Putting the resultant at H/2; a triangle's centroid is at H/3.
  • Writing thrust as Ka γ H and forgetting the ½.
  • Omitting soil on the heel from stabilizing moment; it is often largest.
  • Checking overturning and sliding with factored loads; stability is working-load.
  • Allowing e > B/6; widen base or shift stem instead.

5.8 Water tanks

A water tank is a concrete box that must hold a liquid without leaking, not just without collapsing. Water pushes sideways on every wall, and the pressure grows with depth. The wall must be strong enough for that push and also tight enough that cracks stay hairline for the life of the tank. You meet this in overhead tanks on apartment roofs, underground sumps, elevated service reservoirs and sewage treatment tanks. Strength alone is not the design aim here: crack control and durability decide the steel.

You need: serviceability and crack control from Unit 2, detailing rules from Unit 3, and the cantilever bending idea from cantilever retaining walls.

Water pushes sideways, and cracks leak

Water in a tank pushes horizontally on the wall. At depth h below the free surface the pressure is p = γwh. The pressure is zero at the top and largest at the base, so the load diagram is a triangle, never a rectangle. Design each ring of the wall for the pressure at its own level; the base gets the maximum.

p = γw h

p is the horizontal water pressure in kN/m² at depth h; γw is the unit weight of water, taken as 10 kN/m³ in design (9.81 kN/m³ more precisely); h is the depth below the water surface in metres.

A tank is not a beam. A cracked beam still carries its load; a cracked tank leaks, because water passes through a crack a fraction of a millimetre wide. So design has two targets at once: enough steel for strength, and low steel stress with closely spaced small bars so cracks stay narrow. Working stress design is popular for tank walls for exactly this reason — the permissible stress is set low on purpose, not because we distrust limit state theory.

Two load cases matter. Tank full: pressure on walls and base, the case that sizes the hoop or bending steel. Tank empty: soil and ground water pushing in from outside, which matters for underground tanks. An empty underground tank can also float if uplift exceeds its own weight plus overburden.

Circular tanks: hoop tension

A circular wall is pushed outwards, and it resists by stretching as a ring. That stretch is hoop tension, a direct pull, not a bending moment. To find it, cut the ring across a diameter and take half the ring, 1 m high, as a free body. Water pushes on the projected area 2r × 1 m with total force p × 2r. Two hoop forces T pull back at the cut. So 2T = 2p r, giving T = p r.

T = p r    and    Ast = T × 1000 / σst

T is the hoop tension in kN per metre height of wall; p is the water pressure at that level in kN/m²; r is the internal radius in m; Ast is the hoop steel in mm² per metre height; σst is the permissible tensile stress in steel in N/mm²: about 130 for direct tension (hoop tension) and about 150 for bending, for Fe 415; the 150 used in the example is illustrative; the 1000 converts kN to N.

The hoop bars are horizontal rings. They sit near the water face, with adequate cover, because the water face is where a crack would leak, and the outer face carries only minimum steel. Keep the spacing small, roughly 100 to 150 mm, so that even a shrinkage crack stays narrow.

Vertical steel depends on the base joint. If the wall is free at the top and the base joint lets the wall slide, hoop tension alone governs. If the base is rigidly held, the wall also bends vertically near the base and vertical steel must carry that moment. The base slab then takes the water weight and spans between the walls.

Rectangular tanks and the base slab

In a long rectangular tank each wall spans vertically between the base slab and the top. An open-top tank is a vertical cantilever fixed at the base and free at the top. The triangular pressure gives a base moment M = γwh³/6 per metre width. For h = 3 m this is 10 × 27/6 = 45 kNm per metre width. Main steel goes on the water face at the base and on the outer face higher up. Short walls instead act as panels supported on three or four edges, bending in two directions, and then need steel both ways on both faces.

  1. Fix the geometry: diameter or plan size, and the water depth.
  2. Compute p = γwh at the base and at each level you are designing.
  3. Find hoop tension per metre height (circular) or the bending moment per metre width (rectangular).
  4. Pick a bar diameter and a spacing that gives the required area with spacing under about 150 mm.
  5. Check the base slab, the uplift case, and the joint details.

Liquid tightness is won in the detailing. Use minimum steel on both faces, bars of 8 to 12 mm, spacing 150 to 200 mm, staggered laps, cover of about 40 to 50 mm on the water face, and a low water-cement ratio with good curing. Put water stops at construction joints. A tank that is strong but badly detailed fails its leak test.

Worked example

Question. A circular water tank has an internal diameter of 6 m and holds water 3 m deep. Find the hoop tension per metre height at the base. Design the hoop steel using 150 N/mm² as an illustrative permissible tensile stress for this problem, and 10 mm bars, and state the spacing and the steel provided.

  1. Internal radius r = 6/2 = 3.0 m.
  2. Pressure at the base p = γwh = 10 × 3 = 30 kN/m².
  3. Hoop tension T = p r = 30 × 3 = 90 kN per metre height = 90 000 N per metre height.
  4. Steel area Ast = T/σst = 90 000/150 = 600 mm² per metre height.
  5. Area of one 10 mm bar = π × 10²/4 = 78.5 mm².
  6. Spacing = 1000 × 78.5/600 = 130.9 mm, so provide 130 mm centres.
  7. Steel provided = 1000 × 78.5/130 = 604 mm² per metre, which is more than 600 mm². Safe.

Answer. Hoop tension at the base is 90 kN per metre height; the hoop steel is 600 mm² per metre, provided as 10 mm bars at 130 mm centres (604 mm²/m).

Exam traps

  • Using the average pressure for the base hoop tension. With average pressure the answer halves to 45 kN/m, which is wrong; each level uses the pressure at that level.
  • Putting the diameter into T = p r instead of the radius. That doubles every answer downstream.
  • Designing only for strength and ignoring crack control. A tank that passes the strength check but has 200 mm spacing and high steel stress will leak.
  • Unit slips: T comes out in kN per metre, so multiply by 1000 before dividing by N/mm²; and Ast is mm² per metre height, not the total steel in the wall.
  • For a rectangular wall with triangular water pressure, using the uniform-load moment p h²/2 instead of γwh³/6. For h = 3 m the correct base moment is 45 kNm per metre; the uniform-load formula gives 30 × 9/2 = 135 kNm per metre, three times too large. Check which load shape you assumed.

Analyze The hoop of a circular tank is in direct tension all round. Why then is the main hoop steel placed near the water face? 5 marks

The whole section is in tension, so the section needs steel on both faces for crack control. But the crack that leaks is the one that reaches the water face, so the main area is placed there with adequate cover, and the outer face keeps the minimum.

Analyze Two tanks hold 3 m of water: a 6 m diameter circular tank and a 3 m high rectangular tank. Why does the circular wall carry the same water so much more cheaply? 5 marks

The circular wall carries the pressure as hoop tension, a direct pull spread evenly through the section. The rectangular wall must bend as a vertical cantilever, so the same water creates 45 kNm per metre of bending moment, needing a thicker wall and much heavier steel.

Remember Which load case governs an underground tank that may stand empty? 2 marks

The empty condition with soil and ground water pushing in from outside, together with the uplift check: if the tank's weight plus overburden is less than the upward water force, it can float.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Many large circular tanks are prestressed: wire wound round the outside puts the concrete into compression before the water arrives, so the wall never goes into tension and never cracks (see Unit 6). Big tanks are analysed by finite element methods or by standard coefficient tables for walls with different edge conditions. Water stops, pour sequence and curing decide whether a tank passes its leak test as much as the steel does. Takeaways

  1. A column is short or slender by its slenderness ratio le/D, not by its height alone; the end conditions fix le through k.
  2. For a short axially loaded column, choose the steel percentage so that 0.4fckAc + 0.67fyAsc is at least Pu, then provide ties at the codal pitch.
  3. A column with moment is safe when its (Pu, Mu) point lies inside the interaction diagram; biaxial bending uses the two-axis interaction check.
  4. A wall footing gets its width from the soil bearing pressure and its depth from bending and one-way shear at the section d from the wall face.
  5. An isolated footing is sized by bearing pressure and its depth is usually fixed by punching shear, not by bending.
  6. A combined footing for two columns is first made statically neutral by putting its centroid under the resultant load, then designed as a beam in the long direction and as a cantilever in the transverse direction.
  7. A cantilever retaining wall must first pass the three stability checks — overturning, sliding and bearing — and only then are the stem, heel and toe designed as cantilevers.
  8. In a water tank, crack control and durability can decide the thickness and steel just as much as strength does.

Glossary

Active earth pressure
The horizontal push of soil on a wall that has moved slightly away from it, Pa = ½KaγH² per metre length, in kN/m.
Balanced failure
The state where concrete crushing and steel yielding occur together; it marks the peak of the interaction diagram's moment axis.
Braced column
A column whose ends are held against relative lateral translation by bracing or by a stiff core, so k is small and le is less than the unsupported length in the sway sense.
Combined footing
A single footing supporting two or more columns, used when columns are close or the property line prevents separate footings.
Crack width
The calculated width of a flexural crack at the tension face, in mm, limited by the code to keep the member durable and watertight.
Critical section
The section where a particular check is made: at the face for bending, at d for one-way shear, at d/2 around the column for punching.
Development length
The embedded length of a bar needed to transfer its force to the concrete by bond, in mm.
Distribution steel
Steel placed at right angles to the main steel to control cracking and to hold the main bars in position.
Eccentricity of footing load (e)
e = M/ΣV; the footing is fully in compression only when e ≤ B/6, otherwise the pressure diagram is partial.
Factor of safety against overturning
Ratio of the restoring moment about the toe to the overturning moment from the earth pressure; it must exceed the codal minimum.
Factor of safety against sliding
Ratio of the resisting horizontal force (base friction plus passive pressure) to the active thrust.
Helical reinforcement
Continuous spiral of steel in a circular column that confines the core and increases ductility and load capacity.
Lateral tie
Closed loop of steel around the longitudinal bars that prevents their buckling and confines the concrete.
Levelling course
A thin layer of lean concrete (PCC) under a footing that gives a clean, level surface for the reinforcement.
Net upward soil pressure
The factored soil reaction used to design a footing, equal to the column load divided by the footing area, in kN/m².
One-way shear
Shear on a vertical plane across the full width of a slab-like member, checked at a distance d from the support or column face.
Puz
The pure axial capacity of a column section with zero moment, used to pick αn in the biaxial bending check.
Radius of gyration (i)
i = √(I/A), in mm, used to express slenderness for non-rectangular sections.
Safe bearing capacity
The net soil pressure that can be applied with an adequate factor of safety against shear failure and settlement, in kN/m².
Stability of a retaining wall
The condition that the wall does not overturn, slide or overload the soil under the base.
Surcharge
An extra vertical load on the soil behind a wall, such as a road or stored material, which adds to the earth pressure.
Unbraced column
A column in a frame that is free to sway sideways, so the effective length is greater than the unsupported length and k exceeds 1.
