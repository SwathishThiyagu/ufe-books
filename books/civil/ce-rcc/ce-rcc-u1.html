1.1 Properties of concrete and reinforcing steel

Concrete is strong in compression and weak in tension. Steel is strong in both. Put steel bars where concrete would crack, and the two materials carry a beam together. That partnership only works if you know how each material behaves when squeezed and stretched. Their stress–strain curves supply every number a design method needs: strength, stiffness, and the strain at which each material gives up. This chapter reads those two curves, defines fck and fy, and adds the time-dependent effects — creep and shrinkage — that change the picture over the life of a structure.

You need: stress, strain and Hooke's law from engineering mechanics.

Concrete: cube strength and the stress–strain curve

Concrete is cement, fine aggregate, coarse aggregate and water. The cement paste hardens and glues the aggregates into a stony mass. Its useful property is compressive strength. Its tensile strength is only about one-tenth of that, so reinforcing steel is placed to take the tension.

Strength is measured on a 150 mm cube cured 28 days and crushed in a compression testing machine. The grade is written M20, M25 and so on: M stands for mix, and the number is the characteristic cube strength fck in N/mm² at 28 days. IS 456 asks for M20 as the minimum grade for reinforced concrete in mild exposure. Moderate exposure needs M25, and the requirement rises with the severity of exposure.

fck is a characteristic value: only 5% of well-made test cubes are expected to fall below it. A standard cylinder, 150 mm diameter and 300 mm high, gives about 0.8 times the cube strength, because the platens of the machine restrain a cube and make it look stronger. The 5% rule is what 'characteristic' means: the value is a statistical lower bound, not the average strength of the cubes.

The stress–strain curve in compression is not a straight line. Stress climbs steeply, bends over, peaks near a strain of 0.002 at a stress of the order of the cylinder strength, then falls as the concrete cracks. IS 456 keeps 0.0035 as the maximum useful compressive strain in bending. Stiffness is quoted as one secant modulus from zero to the service stress:

Ec = 5000 √fck

Ec is the short-term modulus of elasticity of concrete in N/mm²; fck is the characteristic cube strength in N/mm². For M25, Ec = 5000 × 5 = 25,000 N/mm².

Reinforcing steel: grades and the yield plateau

Steel bars are hot-rolled and ribbed so that concrete grips them. They are graded by characteristic yield strength fy in N/mm²: Fe415, Fe500 and Fe550. Fe is the chemical symbol for iron. Fe415 means a reinforcing bar with characteristic yield strength fy = 415 N/mm²; it does not mean the design stress is 415 N/mm².

Every grade shares one elastic modulus, Es = 2 × 10⁵ N/mm², about eight times the modulus of concrete. The curve is a straight line from zero to yield, so the yield strain is fy/Es. For Fe415: 415 ÷ 200,000 = 0.002075, about 0.00208.

At yield the curve turns flat. This is the yield plateau: strain grows while stress stays almost constant. After yield the steel strain-hardens up to an ultimate tensile strength that lies above the yield stress, the margin shrinking as the grade rises, then it necks and snaps. The plateau is the reason steel is safe in a beam — the member sags visibly before it breaks.

For design, IS 456 replaces the test curve with a design curve: a straight line from zero to a stress of 0.87fy at strain 0.87fy/Es, then a flat line at 0.87fy. Strain hardening is ignored. The 0.87 already contains the partial safety factor of 1.15 on fy. Bars must also be ductile, so each grade carries a minimum elongation at fracture. Fe415 is more ductile than Fe550. A higher grade gives more strength and needs less bar area, at the cost of that ductility.

Creep, shrinkage and durability

Two effects change concrete with time. Creep is the slow gain in strain under a sustained load; the creep coefficient φ is creep strain divided by elastic strain. IS 456 gives φ = 2.2 for load first applied at 28 days, falling to about 1.6 if the load is applied at one year. Shrinkage is drying contraction with no load on the member, of the order of 0.0003 strain. Both are largely irreversible and both add deflection to a slab over the years.

Durability closes the list. Dense, low-permeability concrete keeps water and chloride away from the steel. Cover, water–cement ratio and minimum grade are fixed by the exposure class: a coastal site such as Chennai needs more cover and a lower water–cement ratio than a dry inland site, because chlorides break down the passive film on the bars, the steel rusts, swells and splits the concrete.

These numbers set the limits for every method in this unit. The stress block is built from the 0.0035 and 0.002 strains. Partial safety factors are applied to fck and fy, never to Ec. The balanced section of Unit 2 is the one where steel yields at the same instant the concrete reaches 0.0035.

  • Peak compressive stress at strain ≈ 0.002.
  • Ultimate compressive strain in bending = 0.0035.
  • Creep coefficient φ = 2.2 for load first applied at 28 days; about 1.6 at one year.
  • Drying shrinkage ≈ 0.0003 strain.
  • Steel modulus Es = 2 × 10⁵ N/mm² for all grades.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported slab uses M25 concrete and Fe415 steel. (a) Estimate the short-term modulus of the concrete. (b) Find the strain at which the steel first yields, taking Es = 2 × 10⁵ N/mm². (c) State the compressive strain in the concrete at peak stress.

  1. Characteristic cube strength of M25 concrete: fck = 25 N/mm².
  2. Modulus: Ec = 5000 √fck = 5000 × √25 = 5000 × 5 = 25,000 N/mm².
  3. Characteristic yield strength of Fe415 steel: fy = 415 N/mm².
  4. Yield strain: εy = fy / Es = 415 ÷ 200,000 = 0.002075, about 0.00208.
  5. Concrete reaches peak stress at a strain of about 0.002; its ultimate useful strain in bending is 0.0035.
  6. Check the ratio: Ec/Es = 25,000 ÷ 200,000 = 0.125, so concrete is about one-eighth as stiff as steel.

Answer. Ec = 25,000 N/mm², steel yield strain = 0.002075 (about 0.00208), and concrete peak-stress strain ≈ 0.002.

Exam traps

  • Do not treat the cube strength 25 N/mm² as the modulus; Ec is about 25,000 N/mm², a thousand times larger. The unit is N/mm² in both cases, which hides the slip.
  • 415 N/mm² is the characteristic yield strength of Fe415, not the design strength. The design value comes from dividing the characteristic value by a partial safety factor, as the limit state chapter shows.
  • Cylinder strength is about 0.8 times cube strength. Mixing the two in one calculation is a common examiner's trap and gives answers that are 25% off.
  • Concrete has no straight elastic portion. Ec = 5000√fck is a secant modulus at service stress, so Hooke's law must not be used up to the peak.
  • 0.0035 is the ultimate compressive strain in bending, not the strain at peak stress; 0.002 is the peak.

1.2 Working stress method and permissible stresses

Working stress method (WSM) checks a reinforced concrete section at service loads. The section is assumed elastic: plane sections remain plane, concrete is linear in compression, concrete carries no tension, and steel and concrete act together. Stresses computed from these assumptions must stay below permissible values. WSM is used for simple beams, older structures, and preliminary checks. It is also called the elastic method or service load method.

You need Chapter 1.1 for stress–strain behaviour and modular ratio.

Service loads, elastic stresses and permissible limits

WSM starts with service loads: the loads expected during normal use, without load factors. Elastic analysis gives concrete compressive stress σcbc and steel tensile stress σst. A permissible stress is the code limit for that stress at service. For M20 concrete in bending, σcbc = 7 N/mm². For Fe415 steel in tension, σst = 230 N/mm².

  • Plane sections remain plane, so strain varies linearly with distance from the neutral axis.
  • Concrete in compression is linear elastic: stress is proportional to strain.
  • Concrete tension is ignored; steel carries all tension after cracking.
  • Steel and concrete at the same level have the same strain. The modular ratio m converts steel area into equivalent concrete area.
m = Es / Ec = 280 / (3σcbc)

m is the modular ratio; Es is Young's modulus of steel; Ec is an effective modulus of concrete; σcbc is the permissible concrete compressive stress in N/mm².

For M20 with σcbc = 7 N/mm², m = 280/(3×7) = 13.33. This value accounts for creep under long-term compression.

Balanced section: k, j and Q

In a balanced section, concrete and steel reach their permissible stresses at the same moment. Let x be the neutral axis depth and d the effective depth. The neutral axis depth factor is k = x/d. Strain compatibility and the modular ratio give the balanced value of k.

k = mσcbc / (mσcbc + σst)

k is the neutral axis depth factor; m is the modular ratio; σcbc is the permissible concrete compressive stress; σst is the permissible steel tensile stress.

j = 1 − k/3

j is the lever arm factor; the lever arm is z = jd.

MR = 0.5σcbc b x z = Q b d²

MR is the moment of resistance in Nmm; b is the beam width in mm; d is the effective depth in mm; Q is the moment factor in N/mm², with Q = 0.5σcbc k j.

For M20 concrete and Fe415 steel, m = 13.33, k = 0.2887, j = 0.9038 and Q = 0.913 N/mm². Rounded, k = 0.289 and j = 0.904. A section with less steel than the balanced value is governed by steel; a section with more steel is governed by concrete.

Design of a singly reinforced rectangular beam

WSM design fixes a rectangular section and tension steel so that elastic stresses stay within permissible values.

  1. Find the service bending moment M from the applied loads.
  2. Choose width b and effective depth d. Effective depth is overall depth minus cover minus half the bar diameter.
  3. Compute m, k, j and Q for the chosen concrete and steel.
  4. Find the balanced moment capacity MR = Q b d². If M is greater, increase the depth.
  5. Find required steel area Ast = M / (σst j d).
  6. Choose bars giving at least this area, then check cover and spacing.
  7. Check shear and bond separately; flexure design alone is not enough.

The method is simple and conservative because permissible stresses are well below strength. It does not predict collapse or ductility. Full beam design, flanged sections and doubly reinforced sections belong to Unit 2; shear belongs to Unit 3.

Worked example

Question. A singly reinforced rectangular beam has b = 230 mm and d = 450 mm. Use M20 concrete with σcbc = 7 N/mm² and Fe415 steel with σst = 230 N/mm². Find the balanced moment of resistance and the balanced steel area.

  1. Modular ratio: m = 280 / (3 × 7) = 13.33.
  2. Neutral axis depth factor: k = mσcbc / (mσcbc + σst) = (13.33 × 7) / (13.33 × 7 + 230) = 93.33 / 323.33 = 0.2887, about 0.289.
  3. Lever arm factor: j = 1 − k/3 = 1 − 0.2887/3 = 0.9038, about 0.904.
  4. Moment factor: Q = 0.5σcbc k j = 0.5 × 7 × 0.2887 × 0.9038 = 0.913 N/mm².
  5. Balanced moment: MR = Q b d² = 0.913 × 230 × 450² = 42.5 × 10⁶ Nmm = 42.5 kNm.
  6. Balanced steel area: Ast = MR / (σst j d) = 42.5 × 10⁶ / (230 × 0.9038 × 450) = 454 mm².

Answer. The balanced moment of resistance is 42.5 kNm and the balanced steel area is 454 mm².

Exam traps

  • Do not use fy = 415 N/mm² as σst. WSM uses the permissible stress, here 230 N/mm² for Fe415.
  • Concrete carries no tension in WSM. All tensile force is taken by steel.
  • The formula m = 280/(3σcbc) uses σcbc, not σst. Both stresses must be in N/mm².
  • Balanced section means concrete and steel reach permissible stress together. A real section may be under-reinforced or over-reinforced.
  • WSM uses service loads with no load factors or partial safety factors. Limit state design uses factored loads.

1.3 Ultimate load method

The ultimate load method, also called ultimate load design or the load factor method, designs a reinforced concrete section to resist factored ultimate loads at collapse. It multiplies service loads by one global load factor, analyses the member for those ultimate loads, and checks that the section's ultimate strength is at least the required ultimate moment. Engineers used it as a step beyond working stress design because concrete and steel behave inelastically near failure. Current Indian practice uses limit state design, but the ultimate load method explains the collapse side of that idea clearly.

You need: Chapter 1.2 for service loads and permissible stresses, and Chapter 1.1 for the stress–strain limits behind ultimate strength.

The idea of a global load factor

The working stress method in Chapter 1.2 checks whether stresses at service loads stay below permissible values. The ultimate load method asks a different question: will the member stand when the loads reach their factored ultimate value? Service loads are the loads expected in normal use. Dead load comes from the member itself and the finishes; live load comes from people, furniture or stored goods. The global load factor γf is one number applied to the total service load. It lumps together load variation, material strength, construction quality and the consequence of failure. The method is also called ultimate load design or the load factor method.

wu = γf(wD + wL)

wu is the ultimate load per metre, γf is the global load factor, wD is the service dead load per metre and wL is the service live load per metre.

The required ultimate moment is found by analysing the member under wu. For a linear elastic analysis, Mu,req = γf Mservice. Mservice is the bending moment from unfactored service loads. The result is one design load and one design moment. There is no separate factor for dead load, live load, concrete or steel. That simplicity is the method's attraction and its weakness.

Collapse resistance and the check

Ultimate strength is the load-carrying capacity just before failure. The collapse moment Mu is the bending moment a section can carry at that stage. It is calculated from the ultimate strength of the materials: the concrete strength is taken as the cube strength fck through an ultimate stress block whose maximum stress is a fraction of fck, and steel at its yield strength fy; no material safety factor is applied to either. The section is then checked as Mu ≥ Mu,req. Mu is the available collapse resistance; Mu,req is the moment from factored ultimate loads.

The check covers collapse only. It does not directly check service deflection, crack width or durability. A beam may pass Mu ≥ Mu,req and still sag too much or crack visibly under normal service loads. It also gives no separate measure of ductility. If the section is over-reinforced, collapse can be sudden and brittle.

  • Advantage: it targets the real failure condition instead of an elastic stress limit.
  • Advantage: it uses the inelastic strength of concrete and steel, so it can be less conservative than working stress design.
  • Advantage: one global factor and one check make hand calculations short.
  • Limitation: service deflection and cracking are not checked.
  • Limitation: one factor cannot separate the variability of dead load, live load, concrete and steel.
  • Limitation: brittle failure remains a risk unless detailing and steel limits are enforced.

Why limit state design replaced it

Working stress design came first. Ultimate load design followed as engineers recognised that concrete and steel do not fail at elastic stress limits. Limit state design later kept the collapse check but added serviceability checks. It also replaced the single global factor with partial safety factors. Dead and live loads get different factors because their variability differs. Concrete and steel get separate material factors. This gives the designer control over each uncertainty.

The ultimate load method therefore survives as a teaching idea and as the collapse half of limit state design. It is not the current method for ordinary reinforced concrete design in Indian practice; Chapter 1.4 sets out the two-limit-state approach. Chapter 1.6 shows how characteristic values become design values. Use the ultimate load method to understand ultimate load, ultimate strength and the collapse check. Do not use it as a complete design method for a real beam.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported reinforced concrete beam spans 6.0 m and carries a service dead load of 12 kN/m and a service live load of 8 kN/m. Use a global load factor of 1.5. Find the required ultimate moment Mu,req.

  1. Total service load w = wD + wL = 12 + 8 = 20 kN/m.
  2. Global load factor γf = 1.5 (given). Ultimate load wu = γf w = 1.5 × 20 = 30 kN/m.
  3. For a simply supported beam, Mu,req = wu L² / 8, where L = 6.0 m.
  4. Mu,req = 30 × 6.0² / 8 = 30 × 36 / 8 = 135 kNm.

Answer. The required ultimate moment is Mu,req = 135 kNm.

Exam traps

  • Ultimate load and ultimate strength are not the same: the load acts on the member, while the strength resists it. The check compares a moment capacity with a moment demand.
  • Apply the global factor once to the service loads. The materials already enter at their ultimate values, fck and fy, with no material factor. Do not add partial safety factors on top; that is limit state design, not the ultimate load method.
  • Do not mix this global load factor with the partial safety factors of Chapter 1.6. Numerically similar factors can mean different things.
  • ULM has no deflection or crack-width check. Passing Mu ≥ Mu,req does not prove serviceability.
  • Keep units consistent: w in kN/m with L in m gives M in kNm. The L² term is easy to forget.

1.4 Limit state philosophy: collapse and serviceability

Limit state design checks a structure twice: once at the edge of collapse, and once at normal service. A limit state is a condition beyond which the structure no longer satisfies a design requirement. The collapse limit state protects life; the serviceability limit state protects comfort, appearance and durability. IS 456 uses this philosophy for reinforced concrete. It is used for beams, slabs, columns, footings and water tanks, wherever a designer must show both safety and service performance.

You need: working stress method and ultimate load method from earlier chapters, plus material stress–strain behaviour.

What a limit state means

A limit state is a condition beyond which the structure no longer satisfies a design requirement. The requirement can be safety, or it can be service performance. Limit state design therefore asks two separate questions. Will the structure survive the worst credible load? Will it behave well under everyday load?

The first question is the limit state of collapse. The structure must not fail by flexure, shear, compression or torsion. The second is the limit state of serviceability. The structure must not deflect too much or crack too widely. The collapse check uses factored design loads and design material strengths. The serviceability check uses service loads, that is characteristic loads with a load factor of 1.0, so it represents everyday behaviour.

The method is probabilistic in spirit. Loads vary from one day to another. Concrete strength varies from one batch to another. Steel strength varies from one heat to another. Characteristic values are chosen from statistical data. Partial safety factors then cover unfavourable variation, modelling error and consequence of failure. The calculation itself is deterministic. The probabilistic idea sits behind the factors, not inside every beam formula. Characteristic values and partial safety factors are covered separately.

Collapse checks

The core inequality is Sd ≤ Rd. Here Sd is the design action effect: bending moment, shear force, axial force or torsion produced by design loads. Rd is the design resistance of the section using design material strengths. The inequality must hold for every collapse mode.

In flexure, the section must carry the design bending moment. Yielding of tension steel before concrete crushing is wanted, because it gives warning. An over-reinforced section crushes suddenly in compression and must be avoided.

In shear, diagonal cracking can lead to a brittle failure. Design provides stirrups and checks concrete shear capacity. In compression, short columns must carry axial load and moment without crushing. Slender columns add buckling effects. In torsion, twisting moments combine with shear and bending, so the section is checked for the combined action.

A collapse check is not a prediction of the exact failure load. It is a code-defined verification at factored loads. The detailed beam checks are in Unit 2, shear and torsion in Unit 3, and columns in Unit 5.

Sd ≤ Rd

Sd is the design action effect from design loads (moment, shear, axial force or torsion); Rd is the design resistance using design material strengths.

Serviceability and why LSM is preferred

Serviceability checks use service loads, not factored collapse loads. Deflection is checked because a floor that sags visibly can crack partitions, disturb finishes and feel unsafe. Cracking is checked because wide cracks let water and air reach steel, and because appearance matters. IS 456 gives a total deflection limit of L/250 for a beam or slab. Here L is the span and the limit is in the same length unit as the deflection.

Other deflection limits apply after partitions and finishes are added. They are tighter than L/250, so a section that just passes total deflection may still fail the later stage. Crack width limits depend on exposure. Unit 2 covers the calculation methods.

The working stress method checks only elastic stresses at service load. It does not check collapse directly. The ultimate load method checks collapse but can miss service problems. Limit state design checks both, and it uses separate partial safety factors for loads and materials. That is why it is preferred in IS 456. Some syllabi call this the limit state philosophy or the limit state method.

Δmax ≤ L/250

Δmax is the calculated maximum total deflection under service loads; L is the span, in the same length unit as Δmax.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported reinforced concrete beam has a span of 6.0 m. The calculated total deflection under service loads is 28 mm. Check the total deflection against the IS 456 limit of span/250. State whether the serviceability limit is satisfied.

  1. Span L = 6.0 m = 6.0 × 1000 = 6000 mm.
  2. Total deflection limit = L/250 = 6000/250 = 24 mm.
  3. Actual total deflection Δ_max = 28 mm.
  4. Compare: 28 mm > 24 mm. The limit is exceeded.
  5. The serviceability limit of total deflection is not satisfied. The section needs revision, such as increased depth, or deflection must be reduced.

Answer. The allowable total deflection is 24 mm; the actual 28 mm exceeds it, so the serviceability limit is not satisfied.

Exam traps

  • Serviceability is not optional. A beam can pass collapse and still fail the deflection or crack check.
  • L/250 is the total deflection limit. Do not use it for deflection after partitions or finishes; those limits are tighter.
  • Limit state design is not the same as ultimate load method. LSM adds serviceability checks and uses partial safety factors for both loads and materials.
  • Do not compare characteristic loads directly with characteristic strengths. Design values come from partial safety factors; see partial safety factors.
  • Shear and torsion collapse can be brittle. A flexure-only check does not prove safety.

1.5 Characteristic strength and characteristic loads

A concrete mix or a steel bar does not have one fixed strength. Test results scatter. Limit state design starts from a cautious value near the weak end of that scatter: the characteristic strength. Loads also scatter, so design uses a characteristic load near the high end. These two numbers are the raw inputs; partial safety factors later turn them into design values (Chapter 1.6). The idea exists because safety must be based on measured variability, not on a single average.

You need: Chapter 1.4 for the limit state idea and Chapter 1.1 for the material behaviour.

Scatter is real: the lower tail for strength

Characteristic strength is the strength value below which not more than 5% of test results fall. For concrete, fck is the 28-day cube strength measured on 150 mm cubes. The grade M25 means fck = 25 N/mm². For steel, fy is the characteristic yield strength; Fe 415 means fy = 415 N/mm².

Test results from one mix form a distribution. The mean μ is the average. The standard deviation σ measures the spread: a large σ means the mix is less consistent. If the distribution is close to normal, the 5% lower fractile lies about 1.65 standard deviations below the mean.

fck = μ − 1.65σ

μ is the mean cube strength in N/mm²; σ is the standard deviation in N/mm²; fck is the characteristic cube strength in N/mm². The multiplier 1.65 is the normal-distribution 5% fractile, rounded.

Why 1.65? For a normal curve, the area below μ − 1.65σ is about 5%. Design only needs the weak tail, because a few low cubes should not make the whole structure unsafe. The mean would put about half the tests below the design starting point, which is not cautious enough.

Some syllabi call these characteristic values. The same statistical idea is used for steel, where tensile tests give a lower 5% fractile. The stress–strain curves in Chapter 1.1 use these strengths as their labels.

Characteristic loads use the upper tail

Loads also scatter. The weight of a slab, the crowd on a balcony, the wind on a hoarding, and the water in a tank all vary. Characteristic load Qk is a load value with a small probability, usually 5%, of being exceeded in service. For a normal load model the 5% upper fractile is Qk = μQ + 1.65σQ, where μQ is the mean load and σQ is its standard deviation in the same units.

Notice the sign. Concrete and steel use the lower tail: μ − 1.65σ. Loads use the upper tail: μ + 1.65σ. A load that is too high is the unsafe case; a material that is too weak is the unsafe case.

Dead load is often calculated from dimensions and density, so its scatter is small. Live load has larger scatter. Wind and earthquake loads are treated with separate statistical models and return periods. The common thread is the same: pick a fractile, not the average.

Chapter 1.6 applies partial safety factors to these characteristic values.

One rule, two directions

Read fck = 25 N/mm² as: 'if we test many cubes from this concrete, about 5 in 100 may be weaker than 25 N/mm².' Read Qk = 120 kN as: 'in service, the load is expected to exceed 120 kN only about 5% of the time.'

The characteristic value is not the design value. Design values are fck/γm and γfQk; the factors γm and γf are in Chapter 1.6. This separation is deliberate: one number describes the material or load; another number describes the required safety.

Standard grades are convenient labels for fck. If a calculation gives 25.4 N/mm², M25 is chosen, not M30. Rounding down keeps the specified grade on the safe side of the calculated fractile.

1.6 Partial safety factors and design values

Partial safety factors turn characteristic values into design values. A characteristic load or strength is a statistical value, not a guarantee for every site or every batch. Design multiplies the load by a load factor γf and divides the strength by a material factor γm. The result is a design load and a design strength. Limit state design compares these two, so the structure has a known margin against collapse and still behaves at service. Every beam, slab and column check in later units uses these values.

You need: characteristic strength and characteristic loads from Chapter 1.5.

What the two factors do

Design starts from a characteristic load Fk and a characteristic strength fk. The real load may be larger; the real strength may be smaller. Two separate factors cover the two uncertainties. Some syllabi call these numbers design values.

Fd = γf Fk

Fd is design load in kN or kN/m; γf is the partial safety factor for loads, greater than 1; Fk is characteristic load in the same unit.

fd = fk ÷ γm

fd is design strength in N/mm²; γm is the partial safety factor for material, greater than 1; fk is characteristic strength, fck for concrete or fy for steel.

The load factor is greater than 1, so it increases the action. The material factor is greater than 1, so it reduces the resistance. Do not exchange them. A load is never divided by γm in this method, and a strength is never multiplied by γf.

IS 456 uses γf = 1.5 on dead and live loads in the common gravity combination. It uses γm = 1.5 for concrete and γm = 1.15 for steel. Steel gets the smaller factor because mill rolling controls its yield strength more closely than site concrete controls its cube strength.

Common load combinations

Loads act together. A load combination is a set of loads that can act at the same time. The code gives the combinations, and each combination has one factored total. For dead load DL and live load LL, the usual gravity combination is:

Fd = 1.5(DL + LL)

DL is characteristic dead load and LL is characteristic live load, both in kN or kN/m; Fd is design load in the same unit.

When wind WL acts, wind is combined with dead load. A common set is:

  • 1.5(DL + WL) for wind with full dead load.
  • 0.9DL + 1.5WL when wind uplift reduces dead load.
  • 1.2(DL + LL + WL) when dead, live and wind act together.

The 0.9 factor is a load factor below 1 for a case where dead load helps stability; it is not a strength factor. Use the combination that gives the largest effect at the section you are checking. This chapter gives common combinations only; the code table is the final reference.

Design strength in concrete and steel

For concrete in flexure, IS 456 writes the design compressive strength as:

fcd = 0.67 fck ÷ γm = 0.446 fck

fcd is design compressive strength in N/mm²; fck is characteristic cube strength in N/mm²; γm = 1.5 for concrete; 0.67 converts cube strength to the design value used in the member.

The number 0.446 is just 0.67 ÷ 1.5. It is a design strength, not a characteristic strength. For M25, 0.446 × 25 = 11.15 N/mm². The exact 0.67 × 25 ÷ 1.5 gives 11.17 N/mm².

For steel, the design yield strength is:

fyd = fy ÷ γm = fy ÷ 1.15 = 0.87fy

fyd is design yield strength in N/mm²; fy is characteristic yield strength in N/mm²; γm = 1.15 for steel.

For Fe500, fyd = 500 ÷ 1.15 = 434.78 N/mm². These design values go into the limit state check. Beam design using them comes in Unit 2. For the full concrete stress block, see Chapter 1.7.

Worked example

Question. For M25 concrete and Fe500 steel, find the design compressive strength of concrete in flexure and the design yield strength of steel. Use γm = 1.5 for concrete and γm = 1.15 for steel.

  1. Write the characteristic values: fck = 25 N/mm² and fy = 500 N/mm².
  2. For concrete, use fcd = 0.67 fck ÷ γm. First compute 0.67 × 25 = 16.75 N/mm².
  3. Divide by 1.5: 16.75 ÷ 1.5 = 11.1667 N/mm², which rounds to 11.17 N/mm².
  4. The rounded IS form is 0.446 fck. Compute 0.446 × 25 = 11.15 N/mm².
  5. For steel, use fyd = fy ÷ γm. Compute 500 ÷ 1.15 = 434.7826 N/mm².
  6. Round the steel value to two decimal places: 434.78 N/mm².

Answer. Design compressive strength is about 11.17 N/mm² (11.15 N/mm² with 0.446fck), and design yield strength is about 434.78 N/mm².

Exam traps

  • Do not multiply a characteristic load by γm or divide a characteristic strength by γf. Loads are multiplied by γf; strengths are divided by γm.
  • Do not use one material factor for both materials. Concrete uses γm = 1.5; steel uses γm = 1.15.
  • 0.446fck is a design strength, not the characteristic cube strength. It already includes the 0.67 factor and γm = 1.5.
  • For wind uplift, do not use 1.5DL with 1.5WL as the only check. The 0.9DL + 1.5WL combination can govern when dead load stabilises the structure.
  • Keep units consistent. Concrete and steel strengths are in N/mm², which is the same as MPa; loads may be in kN or kN/m.

1.7 Stress block parameters for limit state of collapse

At collapse, a reinforced concrete section is idealised by a rectangular stress block. IS 456 replaces the curved concrete compression stress with an average stress 0.36fck over the compression depth xu. This gives one force C and one lever arm z, so moment capacity is C z. The idea makes flexural design quick and consistent. It is used for singly reinforced beams and slabs. Only a few universities teach the parameters in this detail.

You need: characteristic strength from 1.5 and design values from 1.6.

The equivalent rectangular block

At the limit state of collapse in flexure, concrete near the top fibre is at its design compressive strength. The real stress distribution is curved. IS 456 permits an equivalent rectangular block. Here b is the section width in mm, d is the effective depth in mm, and xu is the neutral axis depth in mm. The equivalent block is uniform at 0.36fck over depth xu, where fck is the characteristic cube strength in N/mm². The real curved distribution that it replaces has its peak 0.446fck (= 0.67fck/1.5) at the extreme compression fibre, and this shape is what fixes the centroid at 0.42xu. The numbers 0.36 and 0.42 are the stress block parameters. 0.36 is the average stress coefficient; 0.42 fixes the centroid. The 0.36fck is the average intensity over the compression zone, while the 0.42xu position of the resultant is fixed by the real curved shape; together they are the code's idealisation, not two independent facts about a uniform stress.

C = 0.36 fck b xu

C is compressive force in N; fck is characteristic concrete strength in N/mm²; b is width in mm; xu is neutral axis depth in mm.

Force, lever arm and moment

Compression C acts at 0.42xu from the top fibre. Steel tension T acts at the steel centroid, at effective depth d. The lever arm is z = d − 0.42xu. For horizontal equilibrium, T = C. The design steel force is T = 0.87fy Ast, where fy is characteristic yield strength in N/mm² and Ast is tension steel area in mm². The moment capacity about either force is Mu = C z. In a singly reinforced rectangular section at collapse, these equations give the whole flexural check: C from concrete, z from geometry, and Ast from force balance.

z = d − 0.42xu

z is lever arm in mm; d is effective depth in mm; xu is neutral axis depth in mm.

Mu = C z = 0.36 fck b xu (d − 0.42xu)

Mu is moment capacity in Nmm; C is compressive force in N; z is lever arm in mm.

Worked example

Question. A singly reinforced rectangular beam has b = 230 mm, d = 450 mm, M25 concrete and Fe415 steel. Take xu = 0.48d. Find C, z, Mu and Ast at collapse.

  1. 0.48 is the limiting xu/d for Fe415 steel, so the section is at Mu,lim. xu = 0.48d = 0.48 × 450 = 216 mm.
  2. C = 0.36 × 25 × 230 × 216 = 447,120 N.
  3. z = 450 − 0.42 × 216 = 450 − 90.72 = 359.28 mm.
  4. Mu = 447,120 × 359.28 = 160,641,273.6 Nmm ≈ 160.6 kNm.
  5. Ast = C/(0.87fy) = 447,120/(0.87 × 415) = 447,120/361.05 = 1238.388 mm² ≈ 1238 mm² (about 1240 mm²; provide 4 bars of 20 mm, giving 1257 mm²).

Answer. C = 447,120 N, z = 359.28 mm, Mu ≈ 160.6 kNm and Ast ≈ 1238 mm² (about 1240 mm²; provide 4 bars of 20 mm).

Exam traps

  • Writing C = 0.446fck b xu, that is, using the peak stress over the full depth xu, overestimates compression; 0.446fck is only the peak of the real distribution, not its average.
  • Writing z = d − 0.42d or z = 0.58d; the correct term is 0.42xu, so z = d − 0.42xu.
  • Adding C and T instead of equating them; at collapse T = C.
  • Applying another partial safety factor to 0.36 or 0.87; these coefficients already carry material safety.
  • Measuring xu or 0.42xu from the steel instead of the extreme compression fibre.

Takeaways

  1. Concrete is strong in compression and weak in tension, while steel is strong in both and ductile, so reinforced concrete combines them.
  2. The concrete stress–strain curve is nonlinear, so design uses characteristic strength and an idealised stress block.
  3. The working stress method is elastic and service-load based; it is simple but often conservative.
  4. The ultimate load method checks collapse using factored loads but does not explicitly check serviceability.
  5. Limit state design checks both collapse and serviceability using partial safety factors.
  6. Characteristic values are statistical fractiles, not simple averages.
  7. Partial safety factors cover load uncertainty, material variability and the consequences of failure.
  8. The IS 456 stress block gives concrete force 0.36 fck b xu and lever arm d − 0.42 xu for flexure.
