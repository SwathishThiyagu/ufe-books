1.1 Properties of concrete and reinforcing steel

Concrete is strong in compression and weak in tension; steel is strong in tension and ductile, so steel bars are placed where concrete would crack. Every design method starts from how each material behaves when squeezed or stretched: its strength, its stiffness and the strain at which it gives up. This chapter reads the stress–strain curves of concrete and reinforcing steel as IS 456:2000 idealises them, defines fck and fy, and adds the slow effects, creep and shrinkage, that change a member over its life.

You need: stress, strain, Hooke's law and the second moment of area from engineering mechanics.

Concrete: grade, strength and stiffness

Concrete is cement, sand, coarse aggregate and water hardened into a stony mass. Its tensile strength is only about a tenth of its compressive strength, so steel is placed wherever tension occurs.

Strength is measured by crushing 150 mm cubes after 28 days of curing. The grade M20, M25, M30 names the characteristic cube strength fck in N/mm²: M25 means fck = 25 N/mm². "Characteristic" means that not more than 5 % of the test results are expected to fall below this value (see characteristic strength and loads). A cylinder 150 mm across and 300 mm tall breaks at about 0.8 times the cube strength, because the steel platens of the machine hold the short cube and make it look stronger.

IS 456 sets a minimum grade for reinforced concrete by exposure: M20 for mild exposure, M25 for moderate, M30 for severe, M35 for very severe and M40 for extreme.

Two more properties come from fck. The short-term modulus of elasticity is Ec = 5000 √fck, and the flexural tensile strength (modulus of rupture) is fcr = 0.7 √fck, both in N/mm².

The real compressive curve climbs, bends over, peaks near a strain of 0.002 and then falls as the concrete crushes. For design IS 456 replaces it with an idealised curve: a parabola from zero to a strain of 0.002, then a flat line up to the ultimate strain 0.0035. In the member the flat part is at 0.67fck; with the material factor 1.5 it becomes 0.67fck/1.5 = 0.446fck, the design curve used at collapse (see stress block parameters).

Reinforcing steel: grades and design curves

Bars are graded by their characteristic yield strength fy in N/mm². Mild steel Fe250 is a plain bar with a sharp yield point; high yield strength deformed (HYSD) bars, Fe415, Fe500 and Fe550, carry ribs that grip the concrete. "Fe" is the chemical symbol for iron (Latin ferrum); Fe415 means fy = 415 N/mm², not a design stress of 415 N/mm². For bars with no sharp yield point, fy is the 0.2 % proof stress: the stress that leaves a permanent strain of 0.002 when the load is removed.

All grades have the same modulus, Es = 2 × 10⁵ N/mm², about eight times the modulus of M25 concrete. Higher grades need less bar area but are less ductile: the minimum elongation at fracture is 14.5 % for Fe415 and 12 % for Fe500.

IS 456 gives two design curves, both with a design yield stress fyd = fy/1.15 = 0.87fy.

  • Mild steel (Fe250): a straight line up to 0.87fy = 217.5 N/mm² at a strain of 217.5 / 200,000 = 0.00109, then flat.
  • HYSD bars (Fe415, Fe500): straight only up to 0.8 × 0.87fy. Beyond it the curve bends, and 0.87fy is reached at an extra inelastic strain of 0.002, that is at a total strain of 0.87fy/Es + 0.002.

SP 16, the design aid to IS 456, tabulates this curve: for Fe415 it runs from 288.7 N/mm² at a strain of 0.00144 to 360.9 N/mm² at 0.00380. So Fe415 reaches its full design stress only at a strain of 0.0038. Fe500 reaches it at 0.87 × 500 / 200,000 + 0.002 = 0.00418.

Creep, shrinkage and durability

Creep is the slow growth of strain under a load that stays on. The creep coefficient θ is the final creep strain divided by the elastic strain. IS 456 gives θ = 2.2 when the load is first applied at 7 days, 1.6 at 28 days and 1.1 at one year: the older the concrete when loaded, the less it creeps. Creep is handled through an effective modulus Ece = Ec/(1 + θ).

Shrinkage is the drying contraction of concrete with no load on it; IS 456 suggests a total shrinkage strain of 0.0003 in the absence of test data. Both add to the deflection of a slab over the years.

Durability is the ability of concrete to protect its steel from water and chlorides, which make bars rust, swell and split the cover. IS 456 fixes the nominal cover by exposure: 20 mm for mild, 30 mm for moderate, 45 mm for severe, 50 mm for very severe and 75 mm for extreme exposure, along with a maximum water–cement ratio and a minimum cement content.

The strains 0.002 and 0.0035 build the stress block, and Ec, θ and the shrinkage strain give the deflections checked at the limit state of serviceability.

Worked example

Question. A beam 230 mm wide and 450 mm deep overall is made of M25 concrete and reinforced with Fe415 bars. (a) Find the short-term modulus Ec and the flexural tensile strength fcr. (b) Find the bending moment at which the uncracked beam first cracks; ignore the steel. (c) Find the strain at which the bars reach their design stress 0.87fy, and compare it with the strain of Fe250.

  1. Grade M25 gives fck = 25 N/mm², so √fck = 5.
  2. Ec = 5000 × 5 = 25,000 N/mm² and fcr = 0.7 × 5 = 3.5 N/mm².
  3. Section modulus of the gross section: Z = b D²/6 = 230 × 450² / 6 = 7.7625 × 10⁶ mm³ (I = 1.7466 × 10⁹ mm⁴ divided by 225 mm).
  4. Cracking moment Mcr = fcr × Z = 3.5 × 7.7625 × 10⁶ = 27.17 × 10⁶ Nmm = 27.2 kNm.
  5. Fe415 design stress 0.87 × 415 = 361.05 N/mm². Elastic part of the strain = 361.05 / 200,000 = 0.0018; add the inelastic 0.002: total 0.0038.
  6. Fe250: 0.87 × 250 = 217.5 N/mm² at 217.5 / 200,000 = 0.00109, with no inelastic part.

Answer. Ec = 25,000 N/mm², fcr = 3.5 N/mm²; the beam cracks at about 27.2 kNm; Fe415 reaches 0.87fy at a strain of 0.0038, against 0.00109 for Fe250.

Worked example 2

Question. M25 concrete in a column, loaded at 28 days, carries a sustained stress of 6 N/mm². Find the elastic, creep and total long-term strains (with shrinkage) and the effective modulus. How does the creep change if the load comes on at 7 days?

  1. Ec = 25,000 N/mm² (first worked example). Elastic strain = 6 / 25,000 = 0.00024.
  2. Loading at 28 days: θ = 1.6. Creep strain = 1.6 × 0.00024 = 0.000384.
  3. With shrinkage 0.0003: total = 0.00024 + 0.000384 + 0.0003 = 0.000924, almost four times the elastic strain.
  4. Effective modulus Ece = Ec/(1 + θ) = 25,000 / 2.6 = 9615 N/mm².
  5. At 7 days θ = 2.2: creep strain 2.2 × 0.00024 = 0.000528, 37.5 % more; older concrete creeps less.

Answer. Elastic 0.00024, creep 0.000384, total 0.000924; Ece = 9615 N/mm²; 0.000528 creep if loaded at 7 days.

Exam traps

  • Ec is not fck. For M25, fck = 25 N/mm² while Ec = 25,000 N/mm², a thousand times larger; both are in N/mm², which hides the slip.
  • The design stress of Fe415 is 0.87 × 415 = 361 N/mm², not 415 N/mm²; see partial safety factors.
  • For HYSD bars the design stress is reached at a strain of 0.87fy/Es + 0.002 (0.0038 for Fe415), not at 0.87fy/Es (0.0018). Using 0.0018 gives a wrong limiting depth of neutral axis.
  • Creep coefficients are 2.2, 1.6 and 1.1 for loading at 7 days, 28 days and one year; using 2.2 at 28 days overstates long-term deflection by 3.2/2.6 = 1.23 times.

1.2 Working stress method and permissible stresses

The working stress method (WSM), also called the elastic method or the modular ratio method, checks a reinforced concrete section under the loads it actually carries in use, with no load factors. Concrete and steel are treated as elastic materials, and the stresses computed from that model must stay below permissible stresses: fixed fractions of the material strengths. IS 456:2000 keeps it in Annex B, and it is still used for water tanks, quick checks and old structures. This chapter gives its assumptions, permissible stresses and design constants, and designs a singly reinforced beam.

You need: Ec, Es and the strength grades from properties of concrete and reinforcing steel.

Assumptions and permissible stresses

  • Plane sections remain plane, so strain varies linearly with distance from the neutral axis.
  • Concrete in compression and steel are both linear elastic: stress is proportional to strain.
  • Concrete carries no tension; once it cracks, the steel takes all the tension.
  • Steel and the concrete around it have the same strain (perfect bond), so a steel area Ast acts like a concrete area m Ast.

The modular ratio m converts steel to equivalent concrete. IS 456 fixes m = 280/(3σcbc), where σcbc is the permissible compressive stress in bending, instead of the short-term Es/Ec; this partly allows for creep. For M20, m = 280/(3 × 7) = 13.33, whereas the short-term ratio would be 200,000/22,361 = 8.9.

For M20, σcbc = 7 N/mm² is about a third of fck; for Fe415, σst = 230 N/mm² is about 0.55fy. That margin is the factor of safety of the method.

Neutral axis, balanced section and the constants k, j, Q

For a section of width b and effective depth d (top fibre to the centroid of the tension steel), equating the first moments of the compression concrete and the transformed steel about the neutral axis gives the actual neutral axis depth x:

b x²/2 = m Ast (d − x)

b and d in mm, Ast in mm², x in mm. Solve the quadratic for x.

The critical (balanced) neutral axis depth xc = k d is where concrete reaches σcbc and steel reaches σst at the same moment. From the linear strain diagram, the steel stress divided by m and the concrete stress are in the ratio (d − x) : x, which gives

k = mσcbc / (mσcbc + σst),   j = 1 − k/3,   Q = ½ σcbc k j

k is the neutral axis depth factor, j d = z is the lever arm (compression acts at x/3 from the top), and Q in N/mm² is the moment factor, so that the balanced moment of resistance is MR = Q b d².

Because mσcbc = 280/3 = 93.33 for every grade, k depends only on the steel: 0.289 for Fe415 and 0.400 for Fe250. With M20 and Fe415, j = 0.904 and Q = 0.5 × 7 × 0.289 × 0.904 = 0.913 N/mm²; with M25 and Fe415, Q = 1.109 N/mm².

Compare the actual x with xc:

  • x < xc: under-reinforced. Steel reaches σst first, so MR = σst Ast (d − x/3).
  • x = xc: balanced. Both limits are reached together, MR = Q b d².
  • x > xc: over-reinforced. Concrete reaches σcbc first, so MR = ½ σcbc b x (d − x/3).

Design of a singly reinforced beam by WSM

A singly reinforced beam has tension steel only; the design aims close to balanced, which uses both materials fully.

  1. Find the service load, including self-weight (25 kN/m³ for reinforced concrete), and the service moment M.
  2. Pick the width b (often the wall thickness, 230 mm, or 250 mm) and compute m, k, j and Q.
  3. Find the required effective depth d = √(M/(Q b)). Choose the overall depth D = d + effective cover, rounded up.
  4. Find the steel area Ast = M/(σst j d) and choose bars; check the minimum tension steel 0.85 b d/fy.
  5. Check the actual stresses with the bars provided: solve for x, then σcbc = M/(½ b x (d − x/3)) and σst = M/(Ast (d − x/3)); both must not exceed the permissible values.
  6. Check shear, bond and deflection separately.

The method predicts service stresses well, but it cannot say how far the member is from collapse; that gap led to the ultimate load method.

Worked example

Question. Design a simply supported rectangular beam of effective span 5 m by the working stress method. Besides its self-weight it carries a uniformly distributed service load of 12 kN/m (finishes and live load). Use M20 concrete and Fe415 steel, a width of 250 mm and an effective cover of 40 mm. Check the stresses with the bars provided.

  1. Permissible stresses: σcbc = 7 N/mm², σst = 230 N/mm²; m = 280/(3 × 7) = 13.33.
  2. Constants: k = 93.33/323.33 = 0.2887, j = 1 − 0.2887/3 = 0.9038, Q = 0.5 × 7 × 0.2887 × 0.9038 = 0.913 N/mm².
  3. Try D = 500 mm (span/10): self-weight 0.25 × 0.50 × 25 = 3.125 kN/m, w = 15.125 kN/m, M = 15.125 × 5²/8 = 47.27 kNm.
  4. Required depth d = √(47.27 × 10⁶ / (0.913 × 250)) = √207,100 = 455 mm. Provided d = 500 − 40 = 460 mm ≥ 455 mm. Keep D = 500 mm.
  5. Ast = M/(σst j d) = 47.27 × 10⁶ / (230 × 0.9038 × 460) = 494 mm² (minimum 0.85bd/fy = 236 mm²). Provide 2 bars of 16 mm and 1 of 12 mm: 515 mm².
  6. Actual neutral axis: 250x²/2 = 13.33 × 515 × (460 − x) gives x = 133.9 mm (xc = 0.2887 × 460 = 132.8 mm, so the section is just over-reinforced).
  7. Lever arm z = 460 − 133.9/3 = 415.4 mm. Concrete stress σcbc = 47.27 × 10⁶ / (0.5 × 250 × 133.9 × 415.4) = 6.80 N/mm² ≤ 7. Steel stress σst = 47.27 × 10⁶ / (515 × 415.4) = 220.9 N/mm² ≤ 230.

Answer. A 250 mm × 500 mm beam (d = 460 mm) with 2 bars of 16 mm and 1 bar of 12 mm (515 mm²); under 47.3 kNm the stresses are 6.80 N/mm² in concrete and 221 N/mm² in steel, both within the permissible values.

Question. A beam 230 mm wide with an effective depth of 450 mm has 2 bars of 16 mm as tension steel. M20 concrete, Fe415 steel. Find its moment of resistance by WSM and the concrete stress when that moment acts.

Exam traps

  • WSM uses the permissible stresses σcbc and σst, never fck or fy: for Fe415 the steel stress is 230 N/mm², not 415 or 361 N/mm².
  • The IS 456 modular ratio is 280/(3σcbc) = 13.33 for M20, not Es/Ec = 8.9; using 8.9 moves the neutral axis up and gives a different k, j and Q.
  • In an over-reinforced section the moment of resistance comes from the concrete, ½ σcbc b x (d − x/3); writing σst Ast (d − x/3) for it overstates the capacity.
  • In WSM the compression acts at x/3 below the top, so z = d − x/3; the 0.42xu of the limit state stress block does not belong here.

1.3 Ultimate load method

The ultimate load method (ULM), also called ultimate strength design or the load factor method, designs a section for collapse instead of for service stresses. The service loads are multiplied by a load factor to get the ultimate loads, and the section is made strong enough to carry them when its concrete and steel have reached their real, inelastic strengths. It came after the working stress method because tests showed that elastic stresses say little about the load at which a beam actually fails. Today it survives as the collapse half of limit state design.

You need: the working stress method and the stress–strain curves in properties of concrete and reinforcing steel.

Load factor and ultimate load

Service loads are the loads expected in normal use: dead load wD from the member, finishes and walls, and live load wL from people, furniture and stored goods. A load factor γf is the number by which a service load is multiplied to get the ultimate load at which collapse is checked:

wu = γf (wD + wL)

wu is the ultimate load per metre, γf the load factor (for example 1.5), and wD, wL the service dead and live loads per metre, all in kN/m.

Different factors can be put on dead and live load, because live load is less certain, but the idea is the same: the whole margin of safety sits on the load side. The required ultimate moment follows from ordinary analysis: for a linear analysis Mu,req = γf Mservice, where Mservice is the moment from the service loads.

Ultimate strength of a section

The ultimate moment of resistance Mu is the largest moment the section can carry before it fails. It is found from the real behaviour at failure: the concrete compression zone has the curved stress distribution of the stress–strain curve, and the tension steel has yielded and carries fy. No material factor is used; the materials enter at their characteristic strengths.

For a rectangular section use the idealised concrete curve without the material factor: a parabola to a strain of 0.002 and a flat top at 0.67fck to 0.0035. Its average stress over the compression depth xu is 0.67 × 17/21 = 0.542fck, and its resultant acts at 0.416xu below the top (the same shape as the limit state stress block, where the derivation is shown). Equilibrium gives

0.542 fck b xu = fy Ast,   Mu = fy Ast (d − 0.416 xu)

b is the width and d the effective depth in mm, Ast the tension steel area in mm², xu the depth of the neutral axis at failure in mm.

The section is safe when Mu ≥ Mu,req. The ratio Mu/Mservice is the real factor of safety against collapse, which WSM never computes.

Merits, drawbacks and why limit state design replaced it

  • Merit: it designs for the actual failure condition and uses the reserve of strength beyond the elastic range, so sections are smaller and cheaper than by WSM.
  • Merit: the load factor is a direct, understandable margin against collapse, and it treats dead and live load as loads, not as stresses.
  • Drawback: nothing in the method checks service behaviour. Slender sections made with high-strength steel can pass the collapse check and still deflect and crack badly under everyday load.
  • Drawback: one factor on the load cannot separate the scatter of concrete strength from that of steel strength.
  • Drawback: an over-reinforced section can fail suddenly by crushing; the method needs extra rules to keep failures ductile.

Limit state design kept the collapse check of ULM, split the single factor into partial safety factors on loads and on each material, and added explicit serviceability checks for deflection and cracking. IS 456 adopted the limit state method in its 1978 revision, and the 2000 edition retains it as the main method; see limit state philosophy and partial safety factors.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported beam of span 6.0 m carries a service dead load of 12 kN/m and a service live load of 8 kN/m. Using the ultimate load method with a load factor of 1.5 on the total load, find the service moment and the required ultimate moment Mu,req.

  1. Total service load w = wD + wL = 12 + 8 = 20 kN/m.
  2. Service moment Mservice = w L²/8 = 20 × 6.0²/8 = 90 kNm.
  3. Ultimate load wu = 1.5 × 20 = 30 kN/m.
  4. Required ultimate moment Mu,req = wu L²/8 = 30 × 36/8 = 135 kNm, which is 1.5 × 90.

Answer. Mservice = 90 kNm and Mu,req = 135 kNm.

Worked example 2

Question. The beam of the first worked example is 300 mm wide with an effective depth of 500 mm and has 4 bars of 20 mm Fe415 as tension steel; the concrete is M20. Find its ultimate moment of resistance Mu by the ultimate load method (no material factors), check it against Mu,req = 135 kNm, and find the real factor of safety against collapse.

  1. Ast = 4 × 314.2 = 1257 mm². Steel force at failure T = fy Ast = 415 × 1257 = 521,500 N.
  2. Neutral axis: 0.542 × 20 × 300 × xu = 521,500, so xu = 521,500 / 3254 = 160.3 mm.
  3. Check that the steel has yielded: steel strain = 0.0035 × (500 − 160.3)/160.3 = 0.0074, more than the yield strain 415/200,000 = 0.0021. The section is under-reinforced and fails in a ductile way.
  4. Lever arm z = 500 − 0.416 × 160.3 = 433.3 mm. Mu = 521,500 × 433.3 = 226.0 × 10⁶ Nmm = 226 kNm.
  5. Check: Mu = 226 kNm ≥ Mu,req = 135 kNm, so the section is safe at collapse.
  6. Factor of safety against collapse = Mu/Mservice = 226/90 = 2.5.

Answer. Mu = 226 kNm ≥ 135 kNm, so the beam passes; it would collapse at about 2.5 times its service load. ULM says nothing about its deflection at 90 kNm.

Exam traps

  • Mu (the capacity of the section) and Mu,req (the demand from ultimate loads) are different numbers; the check is Mu ≥ Mu,req. Writing Mu = 135 kNm for the demand hides which side is which.
  • Apply the load factor once, to the loads. In ULM the materials enter at fck and fy with no material factor; dividing by 1.5 and 1.15 as well turns it into limit state design and double-counts safety.
  • The concrete stress at failure is not fck: the flat top of the curve is 0.67fck, because concrete in a member is weaker than a test cube.
  • Passing the ULM check does not prove the beam serviceable; the method has no deflection or crack check.
  • Keep units: w in kN/m and L in m give M in kNm; forgetting the square on L gives a moment six times too small for a 6 m span.

1.4 Limit state philosophy: collapse and serviceability

A limit state is a condition beyond which a structure, or one of its members, no longer does what it was designed to do. Limit state design, the method of IS 456:2000, checks every member against two families of limit states: collapse, which protects life, and serviceability, which protects use, appearance and durability. Each check uses its own loads and its own material values, chosen from statistics and partial safety factors. This chapter sets out the philosophy as the code states it, the limits it puts on deflection and cracking, and why it is preferred to the working stress and ultimate load methods.

You need: the working stress method and the ultimate load method.

The limit state philosophy

The aim of design, in the words of most codes, is a structure that will, with an acceptable probability, stay fit for use during its life, sustain all the loads that are likely to occur, and resist misuse and fire well enough. Limit state design turns this into a set of checks: for each way a structure can stop doing its job, show that the chance of reaching that limit is small enough.

The chance is controlled in two steps. First, loads and strengths are described by characteristic values, statistical fractiles of their scatter (see characteristic strength and loads). Second, these are turned into design values by partial safety factors: loads are multiplied by γf, strengths divided by γm (see partial safety factors). The calculation itself is then deterministic; the probability sits behind the numbers.

The basic inequality for every limit state is

design action effect Sd ≤ design resistance Rd

Sd is the moment, shear, axial force or torsion caused by the design loads; Rd is the resistance of the section computed from design strengths. For a serviceability limit state the "resistance" is a limit such as span/250 or a crack width of 0.3 mm.

Limit state of collapse

The limit states of collapse are reached when the member fails or becomes unstable. IS 456 lists:

  • Flexure: the section fails in bending. The stress block gives its resistance; tension steel should yield before the concrete crushes, so the failure gives warning.
  • Compression: a column crushes, alone or combined with bending; slender columns add buckling moments.
  • Shear: diagonal tension cracks; this is brittle, so stirrups are provided.
  • Torsion: twisting, combined with shear and bending, in edge beams and curved beams.

Other collapse limit states, such as loss of equilibrium (overturning or sliding of the whole structure) and failure under fire, are checked under the relevant rules. For collapse the loads are factored, usually γf = 1.5 for dead plus live load, and the strengths are design strengths with γm = 1.5 for concrete and 1.15 for steel.

Limit state of serviceability

Serviceability limit states are checked at service loads (γf = 1.0, and 0.8 on live and wind load when both act with dead load) and with γm = 1.0. IS 456 gives these limits for beams and slabs:

  • Deflection: the final deflection, including creep and shrinkage, measured from the level of the supports, must not exceed span/250. The deflection after the partitions and finishes are built must not exceed span/350 or 20 mm, whichever is less.
  • Cracking: the surface crack width should not exceed 0.3 mm in general. Where cracking is harmful because the member is always exposed to moisture or is in contact with soil or ground water, 0.2 mm is suggested; in a particularly aggressive environment, such as the severe exposure class, the limit is 0.004 times the nominal cover.
  • Others: vibration, durability (cover, grade and water–cement ratio for the exposure) and fire resistance.

In everyday design deflection is controlled by limiting the span to effective depth ratio (basic value 20 for simply supported beams, 7 for cantilevers and 26 for continuous beams, modified for steel stress and compression steel), and cracking by rules for bar spacing and cover. Those rules come with the design of beams and slabs.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported beam of span 6.0 m supports a floor with brick partitions. By calculation, its final total deflection is 22 mm and the part of the deflection that occurs after the partitions are built is 19 mm. Check both deflection limits of IS 456 and state what you would change.

Exam traps

  • Serviceability is checked at service loads (γf = 1.0) with γm = 1.0, not at factored loads. Using 1.5 times the load in a deflection check overstates it by half.
  • Span/250 is the limit on the final deflection; the stricter limit after partitions is span/350 or 20 mm, whichever is less. For a 6 m span that is 17.1 mm, not 20 mm.
  • A section that passes the flexure check can still fail in shear: shear is a separate, brittle collapse limit state.
  • Limit state design is not "ultimate load method with a different factor": it adds serviceability checks and separate factors for each load and each material.
  • The probabilistic basis is in the choice of characteristic values and factors; the design calculation itself does not compute a probability.

1.5 Characteristic strength and characteristic loads

No two cubes from the same concrete break at the same load, and no two days put the same load on a floor. Limit state design therefore does not start from a single "strength" or "load" but from a cautious value at the unsafe end of each scatter: the characteristic strength near the weak end of the test results, and the characteristic load near the heavy end of the loads. Partial safety factors are applied to these values later. This chapter shows how the characteristic values are found from a mean and a standard deviation, and how the same rule is used backwards to set the target strength of a concrete mix.

You need: the idea of a limit state from limit state philosophy, and mean and standard deviation from statistics.

Characteristic strength: the lower 5 % of the results

IS 456 defines the characteristic strength of a material as the strength below which not more than 5 % of the test results are expected to fall. For concrete it is fck, the 28-day strength of 150 mm cubes; grade M25 means fck = 25 N/mm². For steel it is fy, the yield or 0.2 % proof stress; Fe415 means fy = 415 N/mm².

Test results from one mix cluster around their mean μ, the average. The standard deviation σ measures how widely they spread: a well-controlled site has a small σ. Cube strengths follow a normal (bell-shaped) distribution closely, and for a normal distribution 5 % of the values lie more than 1.645σ below the mean. IS 456 rounds 1.645 to 1.65:

fck = μ − 1.65 σ

μ is the mean cube strength and σ the standard deviation, both in N/mm².

Why not use the mean? Half of all the concrete would then be weaker than the value assumed in design. Using the 5 % value means only about one cube in twenty is weaker, and the partial safety factor covers that remaining risk. The same mean with a larger σ gives a lower fck, so good quality control is rewarded with a higher grade.

Target mean strength for a mix

A mix designer works the rule backwards. To be sure that a mix will be at least M25, it must be proportioned for a target mean strength above 25 N/mm²:

target mean strength = fck + 1.65 s

s is the standard deviation expected on that site. When no site data exist, IS 456 gives assumed values: 3.5 N/mm² for M10 and M15, 4.0 N/mm² for M20 and M25, and 5.0 N/mm² for M30 to M50 (Table 8, for good site control).

For M25 the target is 25 + 1.65 × 4 = 31.6 N/mm²; for M30 it is 30 + 1.65 × 5 = 38.25 N/mm². The mix is designed for the target, so that its weakest 5 % still reaches the grade.

Characteristic loads: the upper 5 %

For loads the unsafe direction is upward. IS 456 defines the characteristic load as the load that has a 95 % probability of not being exceeded during the life of the structure. If a load is described by a mean μQ and a standard deviation σQ,

Qk = μQ + 1.65 σQ

Qk is the characteristic load; μQ and σQ are in the same unit, kN or kN/m².

Enough statistics rarely exist for real loads, so IS 456 takes the loads of IS 875 (dead, imposed, wind, snow) and IS 1893 (earthquake) as the characteristic loads. Dead load, worked out from sizes and densities, has little scatter; imposed load, from people and goods, has much more; wind and earthquake loads are set for a chosen return period.

ValueMeaningTail used

fycharacteristic yield strength of steellower 5 %
Qkcharacteristic loadupper 5 %: μ + 1.65σ

Characteristic values describe the material or the load; they carry no extra safety. The design values come from them through the partial safety factors.

Worked example

Question. Thirty cube tests at 28 days on the concrete of a building site give a mean strength of 32 N/mm² and a standard deviation of 4 N/mm². Find the characteristic strength and the grade the concrete may be called.

  4. Check the meaning: about 5 % of the cubes, 1 or 2 of 30, are expected below 25.4 N/mm².

Answer. fck = 25.4 N/mm², so the concrete is M25 (not M30).

Exam traps

  • fck is the 5 % value, not the mean. A mix with a mean of 32 N/mm² is M25, not M30.
  • Strengths use the minus sign, μ − 1.65σ; loads use the plus sign, μ + 1.65σ. A high load and a low strength are the unsafe cases.
  • The target mean strength is fck + 1.65s, above the grade. Designing a mix for a mean of exactly 25 N/mm² gives an fck of about 25 − 6.6 = 18.4 N/mm² when s = 4.
  • Characteristic values carry no factor of safety; the design values are 0.67fck/γm, fy/γm and γf Qk.
  • Round a calculated fck down to a grade: 25.4 N/mm² is M25, never M30.

1.6 Partial safety factors and design values

Characteristic values say what the loads and strengths are likely to be; partial safety factors add the margin. The load is multiplied by a factor γf and the strength divided by a factor γm, and the results are the design load and the design strength used in every limit state check. The factors are "partial" because each covers only its own share of the uncertainty: one for each kind of load in each combination, one for concrete and one for steel. This chapter gives the IS 456 values, shows how the load combinations are formed, and derives the design strengths 0.446fck and 0.87fy.

You need: characteristic strength and characteristic loads.

Design values

Fd = γf Fk,   fd = fk / γm

Fk is a characteristic load and Fd the design load (kN, kN/m or kNm); fk is a characteristic strength (fck or fy) and fd the design strength (N/mm²); γf and γm are the partial safety factors for loads and for materials.

γf covers loads larger than the characteristic value, errors in analysis and the seriousness of failure. γm covers strength in the structure lower than in the test specimen, workmanship and the approximations of the design formulae. Both are at least 1 for the collapse limit state, so the load goes up and the strength goes down. The two must not be swapped: a load is never divided by γm and a strength is never multiplied by γf.

Load combinations and γf (IS 456 Table 18)

Loads that can act together form a load combination. IS 456 gives γf for each combination and each limit state (DL dead load, IL imposed or live load, WL wind or earthquake load):

CombinationCollapse: DLCollapse: ILCollapse: WLServiceability: DL, IL, WL
DL + IL1.51.5—1.0, 1.0, —
DL + WL1.5 or 0.9—1.51.0, —, 1.0
DL + IL + WL1.21.21.21.0, 0.8, 0.8

Wind and earthquake can act in either direction, so each combination with WL is taken with +WL and with −WL. The factor 0.9 is used on dead load when dead load helps resist the wind, as in uplift or overturning: a structure lighter than expected is then the unsafe case. The design value is the most severe effect from all the combinations at each section, and sometimes a combination reverses the sign of the moment.

For materials IS 456 uses γm = 1.5 for concrete and 1.15 for steel at the limit state of collapse, and 1.0 for both at serviceability. Steel gets the smaller factor because it is made and tested in a mill, while concrete is batched, placed and cured on site. For accidental loads the collapse values are relaxed to 1.2 for concrete and 1.0 for steel.

Design strengths of concrete and steel

Concrete in a member is weaker than the same concrete in a cube: the cube is small, short and held by the platens, and the member is large and loaded for longer. IS 456 takes the strength in the member as 0.67fck. Dividing by γm:

design compressive strength = 0.67 fck / 1.5 = 0.4467 fck ≈ 0.446 fck

IS 456 writes 0.446fck; for M25 that is 11.15 N/mm² (11.17 N/mm² without rounding). This is the peak of the design stress block, not its average.

fyd = fy / 1.15 = 0.87 fy

For Fe415, fyd = 0.87 × 415 = 361 N/mm²; for Fe500, 0.87 × 500 = 435 N/mm² (500/1.15 = 434.8 N/mm² exactly).

These are the numbers behind the stress block parameters (0.36 and 0.42) and behind every beam, slab and column formula of limit state design.

1.7 Stress block parameters for limit state of collapse

At the limit state of collapse in flexure, the compressive stress in the concrete is not uniform: it follows the design stress–strain curve, a parabola and then a flat top at 0.446fck. Integrating that shape over the compression depth gives two numbers, the stress block parameters: the total force C = 0.36fck b xu and its position, 0.42xu below the top. With them a beam's moment of resistance is one force times one lever arm. Only a few universities derive the parameters in their own right; most use them directly in beam design.

You need: the concrete and steel design curves in properties of concrete and reinforcing steel and 0.446fck and 0.87fy from partial safety factors.

Assumptions and the strain diagram

IS 456 analyses a section at collapse in flexure with these assumptions: plane sections remain plane; the strain in the extreme compression fibre is 0.0035; the tensile strength of concrete is ignored; the stress in concrete follows the design curve (parabola to a strain of 0.002, then flat at 0.446fck); and the strain in the tension steel at failure is not less than fy/(1.15Es) + 0.002, that is 0.87fy/Es + 0.002.

Let xu be the depth of the neutral axis at collapse. Strain is zero there and 0.0035 at the top, so the strain is 0.002 at a height of (0.002/0.0035) xu = 4/7 xu above the neutral axis, that is 3/7 xu = 0.429xu below the top. Above that level the concrete stress is the flat 0.446fck; below it the stress is a parabola that falls to zero at the neutral axis.

Deriving 0.36 and 0.42

Per unit width, the compression force is the area of the stress diagram, written in terms of the peak stress 0.446fck:

rectangle: (3/7) xu × 0.446fck;  parabola: (2/3) × (4/7) xu × 0.446fck
total: (3/7 + 8/21) × 0.446fck xu = (17/21) × 0.446fck xu = 0.361fck xu ≈ 0.36fck xu

The parabola's area is two-thirds of the rectangle that encloses it.

The centroid of the rectangle is (3/14) xu below the top. The centroid of the parabolic part is 3/8 of its depth below its flat (vertex) end, that is 3/7 xu + (3/8)(4/7) xu = (9/14) xu below the top. Taking moments about the top:

depth of C = [(3/7)(3/14) + (8/21)(9/14)] / (17/21) × xu = 0.416 xu ≈ 0.42 xu

So C = 0.36fck b xu acts at 0.42xu below the top, and the lever arm to the steel is z = d − 0.42xu.

The stress block is therefore not a uniform stress of 0.36fck; 0.36fck is the stress the force would have if it were spread evenly over xu, and 0.42 locates where it really acts. Equilibrium (C = T) and moments give

0.36 fck b xu = 0.87 fy Ast,   Mu = 0.87 fy Ast (d − 0.42 xu)

b and d in mm, Ast in mm², fck and fy in N/mm²; Mu in N mm. Eliminating xu gives the IS 456 form Mu = 0.87 fy Ast d (1 − Ast fy/(b d fck)).

Worked example

Question. A singly reinforced beam is 230 mm wide with an effective depth of 450 mm and has 3 bars of 16 mm of Fe415 in M25 concrete. Find xu, check whether it is under-reinforced, and find its moment of resistance Mu.

  1. Ast = 3 × 201.1 = 603 mm². Steel force T = 0.87 × 415 × 603 = 217,700 N.
  2. xu = 0.87 fy Ast/(0.36 fck b) = 217,700/(0.36 × 25 × 230) = 217,700/2070 = 105.2 mm.
  3. xu,max = 0.48 × 450 = 216 mm. Since 105.2 < 216 the section is under-reinforced.
  4. Concrete force C = 0.36 × 25 × 230 × 105.2 = 217,800 N, equal to T (check).
  5. Lever arm z = 450 − 0.42 × 105.2 = 405.8 mm. Mu = 217,700 × 405.8 = 88.4 × 10⁶ N mm = 88.4 kNm.
  6. Check with the IS 456 form: 0.87 × 415 × 603 × 450 × (1 − 603 × 415/(230 × 450 × 25)) = 97.97 × 10⁶ × 0.903 = 88.5 kNm. The two agree within 0.2 % (0.42 is a rounded 0.416).

Answer. xu = 105.2 mm < 216 mm, under-reinforced; Mu ≈ 88.4 kNm.

Exam traps

  • The force is 0.36fck b xu, not 0.446fck b xu: 0.446fck is the peak stress of the block, and using it as the average overstates C by about 24 %.
  • The lever arm is d − 0.42xu, not d − 0.42d and not d − xu/3 (the WSM value).
  • Steel strain at 0.87fy for HYSD bars is 0.87fy/Es + 0.002 (0.0038 for Fe415). Leaving out the 0.002 gives xu,max/d = 0.66 for Fe415, which allows over-reinforced, brittle sections.
  • Do not divide 0.36 or 0.87 by another partial safety factor; both already include γm.
  • If the computed xu exceeds xu,max, the singly reinforced formula Mu = 0.87fy Ast(d − 0.42xu) no longer holds; redesign the section or add compression steel.

Takeaways

  1. Concrete is graded by its characteristic cube strength; its design curve is a parabola to a strain of 0.002 and flat at 0.446fck to 0.0035.
  2. HYSD steel reaches its design stress 0.87fy only at a strain of 0.87fy/Es + 0.002 (0.0038 for Fe415); this strain sets the limiting neutral axis depth.
  3. Creep (θ = 2.2, 1.6, 1.1 at 7 days, 28 days, one year) and shrinkage (0.0003) add long-term deflection, not strength.
  4. The working stress method keeps elastic service stresses under permissible values, with m = 280/(3σcbc) and the constants k, j and Q.
  5. The ultimate load method puts all the safety on the load through one factor and designs for collapse, but checks nothing at service.
  6. Limit state design checks collapse with factored loads and design strengths, and serviceability with service loads, using characteristic values and partial safety factors.
  7. Characteristic strength is μ − 1.65σ and characteristic load is μ + 1.65σ; the partial factors γf (1.5, 1.2, 0.9) and γm (1.5, 1.15) turn them into design values.
  8. The stress block gives C = 0.36fck b xu at 0.42xu; with xu,max/d = 0.48 for Fe415 the limiting moment is 0.138fck b d².

Understand Compare the working stress method, the ultimate load method and the limit state method. State the advantages of the limit state method over the other two. 13 marks

Points to cover. WSM: service loads, elastic behaviour, permissible stresses, modular ratio; ULM: load factor, inelastic strength at failure, no serviceability check; LSM: limit states of collapse and serviceability, characteristic values, partial safety factors; Comparison table; Numerical illustration of economy; Advantages of LSM.

Model answer.

  1. Working stress method (WSM): the member is analysed under service loads with no load factor. Concrete and steel are assumed elastic, concrete carries no tension, and steel is converted to concrete with the modular ratio m = 280/(3σcbc). Computed stresses must not exceed permissible stresses, for example σcbc = 7 N/mm² for M20 and σst = 230 N/mm² for Fe415. Safety is the ratio of strength to permissible stress.
  2. Ultimate load method (ULM): service loads are multiplied by a load factor (for example 1.5) and the section is designed to fail only at that load, using the real inelastic strength of concrete and steel with no material factor. Safety is the load factor.
  3. Limit state method (LSM): a limit state is a condition beyond which the member no longer serves its purpose. The member is checked for the limit states of collapse (flexure, compression, shear, torsion) with factored loads and design strengths, and for the limit states of serviceability (deflection, cracking) with service loads. Loads and strengths start from characteristic values (5 % fractiles) and are modified by partial safety factors: γf = 1.5 for dead plus live load, γm = 1.5 for concrete and 1.15 for steel.
  4. Comparison:

  5. Numerical illustration: a balanced section 230 mm wide with d = 450 mm in M20 and Fe415. WSM: MR = 0.913 × 230 × 450² = 42.5 kNm at service.   6. Advantage 1: LSM checks both safety and serviceability; WSM never computes the collapse load and ULM never checks deflection or cracking.
  7. Advantage 2: separate factors for each load and each material place safety where the uncertainty is: a larger factor on concrete (site-made) than on steel (factory-made), and a dead load factor of 0.9 where dead load helps stability.
  8. Advantage 3: it uses the inelastic reserve of the materials, so sections are smaller and cheaper, as the 2.0 ratio above shows.
  9. Advantage 4: its characteristic values rest on the measured scatter of strengths and loads, so the margin of safety is rational and can be calibrated.
  10. Limitations: more calculation and more checks; reliance on the code's factors; and the serviceability checks become essential because LSM sections are slimmer.
  11. Conclusion: IS 456:2000 uses LSM as its main method and keeps WSM in Annex B for special cases such as liquid-retaining structures and quick checks.
Understand Define characteristic strength and characteristic load. Explain their statistical basis, and explain how partial safety factors convert them into design values, with the IS 456 values. 13 marks

Points to cover. Scatter of test results, normal distribution, mean and standard deviation; fck = μ − 1.65σ; target mean strength fck + 1.65s; IS 456 assumed s; Characteristic load: 95 % probability of not being exceeded; Qk = μ + 1.65σ; IS 875 loads; Design load γf Fk and design strength fk/γm; IS 456 Table 18 values; γm 1.5 and 1.15; Design strengths 0.446fck and 0.87fy.

Model answer.

  1. Strengths and loads scatter. Thirty cubes from one mix give thirty different strengths, roughly in a normal (bell) distribution with a mean μ and a standard deviation σ. Design must not use the mean, because half the concrete would be weaker than assumed.
  2. Characteristic strength (IS 456): the strength below which not more than 5 % of test results are expected to fall. For a normal distribution the 5 % point is 1.645σ below the mean, rounded to 1.65: fck = μ − 1.65σ. Example: μ = 32 N/mm², σ = 4 N/mm² gives fck = 32 − 6.6 = 25.4 N/mm², so M25.
  3. Used backwards for mix design, the target mean strength is fck + 1.65s. IS 456 Table 8 gives assumed s of 3.5 N/mm² (M10, M15), 4.0 N/mm² (M20, M25) and 5.0 N/mm² (M30 to M50). For M25: 25 + 1.65 × 4 = 31.6 N/mm².
  4. Characteristic load (IS 456): the load with a 95 % probability of not being exceeded during the life of the structure: Qk = μQ + 1.65σQ (the upper tail, because a high load is unsafe). Example: μQ = 2.4 kN/m², σQ = 0.6 kN/m² gives Qk = 3.39 kN/m². Since data are seldom available, the loads of IS 875 and IS 1893 are taken as characteristic loads.

Partial safety factors turn characteristic values into design values: design load Fd = γf Fk and design strength fd = fk/γm. γf allows for loads above the characteristic value, errors in analysis and the consequence of failure; γm for lower strength in the structure than in test specimens and for workmanship.

Understand Explain the limit state philosophy as given in IS 456. Describe the limit states of collapse and of serviceability, with the code's limits on deflection and crack width. 13 marks

Points to cover. Definition of limit state and aim of design; Characteristic values and partial safety factors; Sd ≤ Rd; Collapse: flexure, compression, shear, torsion; factored loads and design strengths; Serviceability: deflection span/250 and span/350 or 20 mm; cracks 0.3 mm, 0.2 mm, 0.004 × cover; Loads and factors used for each; Example check.

Model answer.

  1. A limit state is a condition beyond which a structure or a member no longer satisfies a design requirement. The aim of design is a structure that, with an acceptable probability, remains fit for use during its life, carries all loads likely to occur, and resists misuse and fire adequately.
  2. The probability is controlled through characteristic values (5 % fractiles of strength, 95 % non-exceedance of load) and partial safety factors. For every limit state the check is design action Sd ≤ design resistance Rd.
  3. Limit states of collapse: flexure (bending failure, with tension steel yielding before the concrete crushes for warning), compression (column crushing, with bending and slenderness), shear (diagonal tension, brittle, needing stirrups) and torsion (twisting with shear and bending). Loss of equilibrium of the structure as a whole and failure in fire are also limit states of safety.
  4. For collapse, loads are factored (γf = 1.5 on DL + IL; 1.2 on each of DL, IL and WL; 1.5 or 0.9 on DL with 1.5 WL) and strengths are design values with γm = 1.5 for concrete and 1.15 for steel.
  5. Limit states of serviceability are checked with γf = 1.0 (0.8 on IL and WL when they act together with DL) and γm = 1.0.
  6. Deflection: the final deflection due to all loads, including creep and shrinkage, measured from the supports, must not exceed span/250; the deflection after partitions and finishes are built must not exceed span/350 or 20 mm, whichever is less. In design this is normally ensured by span/effective depth ratios: 7 for cantilevers, 20 for simply supported and 26 for continuous members, with modification factors.
  7. Cracking: surface crack width generally not more than 0.3 mm; 0.2 mm where cracking is harmful because the member is always wet or in contact with soil or ground water; and 0.004 times the nominal cover in particularly aggressive (severe) environments.
  8. Other serviceability limits: vibration, durability (minimum grade, cover, water–cement ratio for the exposure) and fire resistance (minimum dimensions and cover).
  9. Example: a 6 m beam with a calculated final deflection of 22 mm and 19 mm after partitions:

Evaluate A 7 m simply supported floor beam passes the collapse checks, but within two years it has sagged visibly and the partitions under it have cracked. Its calculated short-term deflection under the sustained load is 12 mm, the load came on at 28 days, and the shrinkage deflection is estimated at 4 mm. Evaluate the design with the limit state philosophy and recommend the checks and remedies. 13 marks

Points to cover. Collapse and serviceability are separate limit states; Long-term deflection with creep coefficient 1.6 and shrinkage; Compare with span/250 and span/350 or 20 mm; Causes: slenderness, early loading, high steel stress, cracking; Checks the designer missed; Remedies.

Model answer.

  1. Passing the collapse checks shows only that the beam will not fail at 1.5 times the loads. Sagging and cracked partitions are failures of the limit state of serviceability, which has to be checked separately at service loads with γf = 1.0 and γm = 1.0.
  2. Long-term deflection: creep multiplies the deflection under sustained load by about (1 + θ). For loading at 28 days θ = 1.6 (IS 456), so 12 × (1 + 1.6) = 31.2 mm. Adding shrinkage 4 mm gives a final deflection of about 35.2 mm. (This simple factor is an estimate; IS 456 Annex C uses an effective modulus Ec/(1 + θ) in the same way.)
  3. Compare with the limits for L = 7000 mm:
    limitvalueestimateresult
    final, span/25028 mm35.2 mmfails by 7.2 mm
    after partitions, min(span/350, 20 mm)20 mmcreep 19.2 mm + shrinkage 4 mm = 23.2 mm (partitions built soon after loading)fails
  4. The deflection after partitions counts the part that happens after they are built; if the partitions went up soon after the load, nearly all the creep (1.6 × 12 = 19.2 mm) and shrinkage happen after them, which is enough to crack brick walls.
  5. Likely causes: a slim section (a high span/effective depth ratio, possibly above the basic 20 times its modification factor); loading at an early age (had the load come on at 7 days θ would be 2.2 and the creep worse; at one year 1.1); high-grade steel used at a high service stress; and cracking, which lowers the stiffness well below that of the gross section.
  6. Checks the designer should have made: the span/effective depth ratio with the modification factor for steel stress and compression steel; or a full deflection calculation with the cracked moment of inertia, creep and shrinkage; the crack width (0.3 mm general) and bar spacing; and the timing of the partitions.
  7. Remedies for this beam: prop it and build the partitions after more of the creep has occurred; add a steel or concrete jacket or external plates to stiffen it; or provide flexible joints at the top of the partitions. For new designs: deeper sections, compression steel (it reduces creep deflection), later striking of formwork and loading, and good curing to reduce shrinkage.
  8. Evaluation: the design is unsafe for use though safe against collapse. Limit state design exists to catch exactly this case, which neither the working stress method (which does not compute deflection) nor the ultimate load method (which ignores service behaviour) would have flagged.

Glossary

Balanced section
A section in which concrete and steel reach their limits (permissible stresses in WSM, design strains in LSM) at the same time.
Characteristic load
The load with a 95 % probability of not being exceeded during the life of the structure.
Characteristic strength
The strength below which not more than 5 % of test results are expected to fall.
Creep
The slow increase of strain in concrete under a sustained stress.
Creep coefficient
Final creep strain divided by elastic strain; 1.6 for concrete loaded at 28 days.
Design strength
Characteristic strength divided by the partial safety factor for the material (0.446fck, 0.87fy).
Effective depth
Distance from the extreme compression fibre to the centroid of the tension steel, d.
HYSD bar
High yield strength deformed bar (Fe415, Fe500, Fe550), ribbed for bond, with yield taken as the 0.2 % proof stress.
Lever arm
Distance between the compression force C and the tension force T in a section.
Limit state
A condition beyond which a structure or member no longer satisfies a design requirement.
Limit state of collapse
Failure of a member by flexure, compression, shear or torsion, checked with factored loads.
Limit state of serviceability
Loss of fitness for use by deflection, cracking or vibration, checked with service loads.
Load factor
The factor by which a service load is multiplied to get the ultimate load.
Modular ratio
In WSM, m = 280/(3σcbc), the factor that converts a steel area to an equivalent concrete area.
Neutral axis
The line in a section where the bending strain is zero.
Over-reinforced section
A section with more steel than the balanced amount; concrete fails first, suddenly.
Partial safety factor
A factor applied to a characteristic load (γf) or strength (γm) to obtain its design value.
Permissible stress
The largest stress allowed at service load in the working stress method.
Proof stress (0.2 %)
The stress that leaves a permanent strain of 0.002 after unloading; the yield strength of bars with no sharp yield point.
Shrinkage
Contraction of concrete as it dries, with no load; about 0.0003 strain.
Stress block
The distribution of compressive stress in concrete at collapse, summarised by 0.36fck b xu acting at 0.42xu.
Target mean strength
The mean strength a mix is designed for, fck + 1.65s, so that its 5 % value reaches the grade.
Ultimate load method
Design for collapse under service loads times a load factor, with materials at their full strength.
Under-reinforced section
A section with less steel than the balanced amount; steel yields first and the failure gives warning.
Working stress method
Elastic design that keeps service-load stresses within permissible stresses.
