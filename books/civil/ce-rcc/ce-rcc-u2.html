2.1 Singly reinforced rectangular beams

A singly reinforced rectangular beam is a concrete beam with steel only on the tension side. At collapse, the concrete in compression carries a resultant force and the steel carries tension. The neutral axis depth xu follows from balancing the concrete compression against the steel tension, together with the moment equation. Strain compatibility fixes the limiting depth xu,max that keeps the section ductile. The two forces are then used to get the moment. This is the standard IS 456 limit state flexure calculation for building beams, footings and many water-retaining members. It is the first flexure design most students meet.

You need: Unit 1 on stress block parameters, characteristic strength and partial safety factors.

The collapse mechanism

At ultimate limit state, a cracked beam is treated as a concrete compression zone and a steel tension tie. Plane sections remain plane, so strains vary linearly with distance from the neutral axis. Concrete has almost no tensile strength after cracking, so concrete below the neutral axis is ignored. IS 456 replaces the curved concrete stress distribution by an equivalent block. Its resultant compression is C = 0.36 fck b xu, acting at 0.42 xu from the compression face. The steel force is T = 0.87 fy Ast. Here b is width in mm, d is effective depth in mm from the compression face to the tension steel centroid, D is overall depth in mm, xu is neutral axis depth in mm, fck is concrete characteristic cube strength in N/mm², fy is steel yield strength in N/mm², and Ast is tension steel area in mm². The lever arm is z = d − 0.42 xu. Equilibrium C = T gives the steel area once xu is known. The steel is called the tension steel and its area is Ast. When only tension steel is provided, the section is singly reinforced.

C = 0.36 fck b xu    T = 0.87 fy Ast    z = d − 0.42 xu

C is concrete compression in N; T is steel tension in N; fck is in N/mm²; b and xu are in mm; fy is in N/mm²; Ast is in mm²; d and z are in mm.

Mu = 0.36 fck b xu (d − 0.42 xu) = 0.87 fy Ast z

Mu is the factored moment in N·mm; other symbols are as above. Divide by 10⁶ to get kN·m.

Limits and design sequence

To keep the section ductile, xu must not exceed xu,max. IS 456 gives xu,max/d = 0.53 for Fe250, 0.48 for Fe415 and 0.46 for Fe500. The limiting moment is Mu,lim = 0.36 fck b xu,max (d − 0.42 xu,max). If Mu ≤ Mu,lim, the section can be singly reinforced. If Mu > Mu,lim, compression steel is needed; see 2.3. Minimum tension steel is 0.85 b d / fy. Maximum tension steel is 0.04 b D. The minimum prevents sudden brittle failure. The maximum avoids congestion and over-reinforcement. Design aids in 2.5 tabulate the same equation.

  1. Fix b, D, d, fck, fy and the factored moment Mu in N·mm.
  2. Find xu,max from xu,max/d: 0.53 for Fe250, 0.48 for Fe415, 0.46 for Fe500.
  3. Compute Mu,lim = 0.36 fck b xu,max (d − 0.42 xu,max).
  4. If Mu ≤ Mu,lim, solve Mu = 0.36 fck b xu (d − 0.42 xu) for the smaller root xu.
  5. Compute Ast = Mu / [0.87 fy (d − 0.42 xu)].
  6. Check 0.85 b d / fy ≤ Ast ≤ 0.04 b D; then choose bars and detail.

After the steel area

After Ast is known, choose bars so the provided area is at least the required area. Keep clear cover and bar spacing as required for durability and placing. The chosen bars fix the actual d; if it differs much from the assumed d, repeat the calculation. Check xu/d ≤ xu,max/d. This confirms an under-reinforced section that yields before concrete crushes; the failure modes are compared in 2.2. A beam must also pass serviceability checks for deflection and crack width at service loads; see 2.6. Shear, torsion and bond are checked separately in Unit 3. For a one-way slab the same flexure equation appears in Unit 4, with b taken as 1000 mm.

Worked example

Question. Design the tension steel for a singly reinforced rectangular beam: b = 230 mm, overall depth D = 450 mm, effective depth d = 400 mm, fck = 25 N/mm², fy = 415 N/mm², factored moment Mu = 120 kN·m. Check that the section is singly reinforced and give a bar arrangement.

  1. Data: Mu = 120 kN·m = 120 × 10⁶ N·mm; b = 230 mm; d = 400 mm; D = 450 mm; fck = 25 N/mm²; fy = 415 N/mm².
  2. xu,max = 0.48 d = 0.48 × 400 = 192 mm for Fe415.
  3. Mu,lim = 0.36 fck b xu,max (d − 0.42 xu,max) = 0.36 × 25 × 230 × 192 × (400 − 80.64) = 126.93 × 10⁶ N·mm = 126.93 kN·m.
  4. Since 120 kN·m ≤ 126.93 kN·m, the section is singly reinforced.
  5. Solve 120 × 10⁶ = 0.36 × 25 × 230 × xu × (400 − 0.42 xu). This gives 869.4 xu² − 828000 xu + 120 × 10⁶ = 0. The smaller root is xu = 178.31 mm; the other root is greater than d and is not valid.
  6. Check xu/d = 178.31/400 = 0.446 ≤ 0.48, so the section is under-reinforced.
  7. z = d − 0.42 xu = 400 − 0.42 × 178.31 = 325.11 mm.
  8. Ast = Mu / (0.87 fy z) = 120 × 10⁶ / (0.87 × 415 × 325.11) = 1022.3 mm².
  9. Minimum Ast = 0.85 b d / fy = 0.85 × 230 × 400 / 415 = 188.4 mm². Maximum Ast = 0.04 b D = 0.04 × 230 × 450 = 4140 mm². The value 1022.3 mm² lies between them.
  10. Provide 3 bars of 22 mm: area = 3 × π/4 × 22² = 1140.4 mm², which is greater than 1022.3 mm².

Answer. The section is singly reinforced: xu = 178.31 mm and Ast = 1022.3 mm²; provide 3 bars of 22 mm giving 1140.4 mm², with Mu,lim = 126.93 kN·m.

Exam traps

  • Using overall depth D in place of effective depth d in the moment equation and in the lever arm. The steel centroid sets d; D is only for the maximum steel check.
  • Using fy directly in place of 0.87 fy. 0.87 fy is the design strength of the steel. The partial safety factor 1.15 on fy gives 1/1.15 ≈ 0.87, so use 0.87 fy in T and in the moment equation.
  • Forgetting the xu ≤ xu,max check. Too much tension steel makes an over-reinforced section that crushes before yielding.
  • Mixing kN·m and N·mm. Use 1 kN·m = 10⁶ N·mm before substituting into any formula.
  • Taking b as a large flange width for a rectangular beam. Flange width belongs to T and L beams; see 2.4.

2.2 Balanced, under-reinforced and over-reinforced sections

A reinforced concrete beam can fail in three ways. The tension steel may yield first and the beam bends and warns before collapse. The concrete may crush first in a sudden brittle failure. Or steel and concrete may reach their limits together. The amount of tension steel relative to the balanced steel decides which mode occurs. Designers use this classification to keep beams ductile, so a warning comes before failure. The same idea appears in every singly reinforced beam check and in the design aids for flexure.

You need: Chapter 2.1 and the stress–strain curves of Unit 1.

Neutral axis and the three modes

Let b be the width and d the effective depth of a rectangular beam. Let xu be the depth of the neutral axis from the extreme compression fibre. Concrete crushes at the ultimate strain εcu = 0.0035. Plane sections remain plane, so the steel strain εs follows from strain compatibility:

εs = εcu (d − xu) / xu

εs is the tensile strain in the steel; εcu is the concrete ultimate strain, 0.0035; d is the effective depth; xu is the neutral axis depth.

A smaller xu puts the steel farther from the neutral axis in strain terms and raises εs. Steel yields when εs reaches εy = fy/Es, where fy is the yield strength and Es = 200000 N/mm² is Young's modulus of steel.

The limiting neutral axis depth xu,max is the depth at which concrete crushing and steel yielding occur together. IS 456 gives xu,max/d = 0.53 for Fe250, 0.48 for Fe415 and 0.46 for Fe500. These values come from εcu = 0.0035 paired with the code limiting steel strain εs,lim = 0.002 + 0.87 fy/Es, which is slightly above the yield strain fy/Es; at xu = xu,max the steel has already yielded.

If xu < xu,max, the steel yields before the concrete crushes. This is an under-reinforced section. If xu = xu,max, both happen together. This is a balanced section. If xu > xu,max, the concrete crushes before the steel yields. This is an over-reinforced section.

Some syllabi call these tension-controlled, balanced and compression-controlled sections. The names change; the comparison xu against xu,max does not.

Balanced steel and limiting percentage

At the balanced condition, concrete compression equals steel tension:

0.36 fck b xu,max = 0.87 fy Ast,lim

fck is the characteristic concrete strength in N/mm²; fy is the steel yield strength in N/mm²; b is the beam width in mm; xu,max is the limiting neutral axis depth in mm; Ast,lim is the balanced tension steel area in mm².

The factor 0.36 fck is the design average stress over the rectangular compression block. The factor 0.87 fy is the design tensile stress in steel. Solve for the balanced steel area and the limiting steel percentage:

Ast,lim = 0.36 fck b xu,max / (0.87 fy)

Ast,lim is the balanced steel area in mm²; other symbols are as defined above.

pt,lim = 100 Ast,lim / (b d) = 100 × 0.36 fck (xu,max/d) / (0.87 fy)

pt,lim is the limiting steel percentage; d is the effective depth in mm.

A provided percentage pt = 100 Ast/(b d) below pt,lim means Ast < Ast,lim, so the section is under-reinforced. A value above pt,lim means over-reinforced. Values for fck = 30 N/mm²:

Classifying a section

  1. Read b, d, fck, fy and Ast.
  2. Pick xu,max/d for the actual fy.
  3. Compute xu,max = (xu,max/d)d.
  4. Compute Ast,lim from the balanced force equation.
  5. Compute pt,lim and pt.
  6. Compare. If Ast < Ast,lim or pt < pt,lim, under-reinforced; equal, balanced; greater, over-reinforced.

The comparison is about the neutral axis, not about Ast alone. A beam with 800 mm² of steel can be under-reinforced in a large section and over-reinforced in a very small one. Always compare with Ast,lim for the same b, d, fck and fy.

In a real check, the strain diagram gives xu. For an under-reinforced section, steel has yielded, so the tension force is 0.87 fyAst and xu follows from 0.36 fckb xu = 0.87 fyAst. For an over-reinforced section, that equation is not valid because steel stress is below 0.87 fy. Use strain compatibility there. The classification by Ast against Ast,lim avoids this trap.

IS 456 design practice keeps sections under-reinforced. An under-reinforced beam deflects, cracks and gives warning before collapse. An over-reinforced beam can crush suddenly with little warning. Balanced is a limit, not a normal design target. If capacity is short, add compression steel as in Chapter 2.3 rather than make the section over-reinforced. Design aids in Chapter 2.5 tabulate pt,lim.

Worked example

Question. A singly reinforced rectangular beam has b = 300 mm, d = 500 mm, fck = 30 N/mm², fy = 500 N/mm² and Ast = 2500 mm². Compute Ast,lim, pt,lim and pt, and classify the section.

  1. For Fe500, xu,max/d = 0.46. So xu,max = 0.46 × 500 = 230 mm.
  2. At the balanced condition, C = T: 0.36 fck b xu,max = 0.87 fy Ast,lim.
  3. C = 0.36 × 30 × 300 × 230 = 745200 N = 745.2 kN.
  4. Ast,lim = 745200 / (0.87 × 500) = 745200 / 435 = 1713.1 mm².
  5. pt,lim = 100 × 1713.1 / (300 × 500) = 1.142%.
  6. pt = 100 × 2500 / (300 × 500) = 1.667%.
  7. Ast = 2500 mm² > Ast,lim = 1713.1 mm², and pt = 1.667% > pt,lim = 1.142%. The section is over-reinforced.

Answer. Ast,lim = 1713.1 mm², pt,lim = 1.142%, pt = 1.667%; the section is over-reinforced.

Exam traps

  • Calling a section under-reinforced because Ast is small, without comparing Ast with Ast,lim or xu with xu,max.
  • Using xu,max/d = 0.48 for Fe500 steel instead of 0.46.
  • Comparing pt with a fixed 1% or 0.75% instead of pt,lim for the actual fck and fy.
  • Using 0.36 fckb xu = 0.87 fyAst to find xu for an over-reinforced section, where steel has not yielded.
  • Confusing overall depth D with effective depth d when computing pt.

2.3 Doubly reinforced beams

A singly reinforced beam has a ceiling: the concrete compression block cannot grow beyond xu,max, so its moment capacity stops at Mu,lim. When the factored moment exceeds this, or when floor height restricts the beam depth, we add compression steel Asc near the top and extra tension steel Ast2 near the bottom. The pair forms an internal couple that carries the excess moment. This is common in building frames where beam depths are fixed by floor heights.

You need: Chapter 2.1, Chapter 2.2, and the Unit 1 stress block.

When and why compression steel is added

A singly reinforced beam reaches its ceiling when the neutral axis depth xu equals xu,max. The concrete compression block cannot take more, so the moment capacity stops at Mu,lim. If the factored moment Mu is larger, or if the depth cannot be increased, steel is placed near the compression face. This is compression steel, area Asc, at effective cover d′. Extra tension steel Ast2 is added at the bottom.

The section is kept at xu = xu,max to use the full concrete capacity. The compression force is C = 0.36 fck b xu + fsc Asc. The tension force is T = 0.87 fy Ast. Here fck is concrete strength, b is width, fy is steel yield strength, and fsc is the stress in compression steel.

Mu = Mu,lim + fsc Asc (d − d′)

Mu is the factored design moment in N·mm; Mu,lim is the limiting moment of the singly reinforced section; fsc is the compressive stress in compression steel in N/mm²; Asc is the compression steel area in mm²; d is effective depth; d′ is effective cover to the centre of compression steel.

The excess moment is ΔM = Mu − Mu,lim. Equilibrium gives Ast2 = Asc fsc/(0.87 fy). The compression steel stress fsc comes from strain compatibility: εsc = 0.0035(xu − d′)/xu. Use fsc = 0.87 fy only when εsc is at least the yield strain of the steel. Otherwise fsc = Es εsc, with Es = 200000 N/mm².

Some syllabi call this compression reinforcement. It is common in building frames where beam depth is fixed by floor height, and in beams that must control deflection.

Design procedure

  1. Find Mu,lim = 0.36 fck b xu,max (d − 0.42 xu,max) with xu,max from the steel grade.
  2. If Mu ≤ Mu,lim, design a singly reinforced beam; stop.
  3. If Mu > Mu,lim or depth is restricted, set xu = xu,max.
  4. Compute ΔM = Mu − Mu,lim.
  5. Compute εsc = 0.0035(xu − d′)/xu and fsc = min(0.87 fy, Es εsc).
  6. Compute Asc = ΔM/[fsc(d − d′)].
  7. Compute Ast2 = Asc fsc/(0.87 fy) and Ast,lim = 0.36 fck b xu/(0.87 fy).
  8. Take Ast = Ast,lim + Ast2; check 0.04 bD limits and minimum steel; select bars.

Checks, limits and economy

Compression steel only earns its place if it can yield or carry useful stress. With d′/d around 0.05–0.1, εsc usually exceeds yield and fsc = 0.87 fy. For large d′, fsc falls and Asc rises. IS 456 limits both Asc and Ast to 0.04 bD of the gross section. Tension steel must also meet the minimum area for the beam. If ΔM is very large, a deeper beam or a flanged section may be cheaper than heavy doubly reinforced steel; see Chapter 2.4. Compression steel reduces long-term deflection and cracking, but the beam still needs the serviceability checks of Chapter 2.6.

Worked example

Question. Design the compression and tension steel for a rectangular beam with b = 250 mm, d = 500 mm, d′ = 50 mm, fck = 25 N/mm², fy = 415 N/mm², and factored moment Mu = 250 kN·m. Take Mu,lim = 215.57 kN·m.

  1. xu,max/d = 0.48 for Fe 415, so xu = 0.48 × 500 = 240 mm. Check Mu,lim = 0.36 × 25 × 250 × 240 × (500 − 0.42 × 240) = 215.57 kN·m.
  2. Mu = 250 kN·m > Mu,lim, so the beam is doubly reinforced. Excess moment ΔM = 250 − 215.57 = 34.43 kN·m = 34.43 × 106 N·mm.
  3. Strain in compression steel: εsc = 0.0035 × (240 − 50)/240 = 0.002771. Yield strain for Fe 415 = 0.87 × 415/200000 = 0.001805. Since εsc > yield strain, compression steel yields.
  4. fsc = 0.87 × 415 = 361.05 N/mm².
  5. Asc = ΔM/[fsc(d − d′)] = 34.43 × 106/[361.05 × (500 − 50)] = 211.93 mm².
  6. Extra tension steel Ast2 = Asc fsc/(0.87 fy) = 211.93 × 361.05/361.05 = 211.93 mm².
  7. Limiting tension steel Ast,lim = 0.36 fck b xu/(0.87 fy) = 0.36 × 25 × 250 × 240/361.05 = 1495.64 mm².
  8. Total tension steel Ast = Ast,lim + Ast2 = 1495.64 + 211.93 = 1707.56 mm² (using unrounded values).
  9. Provide 4 bars of 25 mm diameter: area = 4 × 491 = 1964 mm² > 1707.56 mm². Provide 2 bars of 16 mm at top: area = 402 mm² > 211.93 mm².
  10. Check 0.04 bD: with overall depth D ≈ 550 mm, 0.04 bD = 0.04 × 250 × 550 = 5500 mm², both steel areas are below the limit.

Answer. Asc = 211.93 mm² and Ast = 1707.56 mm²; provide 2–16 mm compression bars and 4–25 mm tension bars.

Exam traps

  • Adding Asc but forgetting Ast2: the compression steel force must be balanced by extra tension steel; otherwise the section is not in equilibrium.
  • Taking fsc = 0.87fy always. For large d′ or small xu, εsc may be below yield; use fsc = Es εsc.
  • Using total Ast in the concrete block term. The concrete contribution is fixed by xu,max and Ast,lim; the extra steel only carries ΔM.
  • Unit slip: Mu in kN·m must be converted to N·mm before dividing by fsc(d − d′) in N/mm² and mm.
  • Setting xu = xu,max when Mu < Mu,lim; this overestimates concrete capacity and gives wrong steel.

2.4 Flanged beams: T and L sections

A slab cast monolithically with a beam acts as a flange in compression. The result is a T-section when slab lies on both sides and an L-section at an edge. The flange adds a wide concrete compression area, so the beam carries more moment than a rectangular beam of the same web width. Shear lag means only an effective width bf is used. Designers meet this in building floors, where beams support slabs. The chapter shows how to find bf, test whether the neutral axis lies in the flange, and compute tension steel.

You need Chapter 2.1 for the rectangular stress block and Unit 4 for slab behaviour.

Slab and beam act together

Concrete cast on top of a beam does not stop at the beam face. It continues into the slab. When the beam bends sagging, the slab near the beam is in compression. A T-beam has slab on both sides; an L-beam has slab on one side. The slab is the flange and the beam below is the web.

The flange gives a wide compression area. A flanged beam carries more moment than a rectangular beam of web width bw for the same depth and steel. The flange helps only in compression. If the slab lies on the tension side, as over a support in a continuous beam, ignore it.

The slab and beam must be cast monolithically. A precast slab simply resting on a beam does not form a T-section unless shear connection is provided.

Effective flange width from IS 456

Use effective flange width bf. Shear lag makes stress fall away from the web, so only a limited slab width is effective. Symbols: bw is web breadth; Df is flange thickness; bf is effective flange width; L0 is distance between points of zero bending moment. For a simply supported beam, L0 is the effective span.

IS 456 gives upper limits. For a T-beam: bf ≤ bw + 2Df + L0/6, and bf ≤ bw + clear spacing between beams. For an L-beam: bf ≤ bw + Df + L0/12, and bf ≤ bw + half the clear spacing to the next beam. The smaller value governs.

The spacing limit exists because the slab can be shared only up to midspan between beams; beyond that it belongs to the next beam. For continuous T-beams, L0 is the distance between points of contraflexure, not always the full span. A T-section and an L-section are both flanged beams; the slab is the flange and the rib is the web.

Never take the whole slab width. A wide bf looks safe but gives wrong steel and unsafe checks.

Neutral axis and design steel

Begin by assuming the neutral axis lies in the flange: xu ≤ Df. Then concrete compression is a rectangle of width bf, and the section is a singly reinforced rectangular beam of width bf.

Mu = 0.36 fck bf xu (d − 0.42 xu)

Mu is factored moment in N·mm; fck concrete strength in N/mm²; bf effective flange width in mm; xu neutral axis depth in mm; d effective depth in mm.

Solve the quadratic for xu. If xu ≤ Df, the assumption holds. Tension steel Ast = 0.36 fck bf xu / (0.87 fy), where fy is steel yield strength. Also check xu ≤ xu,max; for Fe 415 steel, xu,max/d = 0.48.

If xu > Df, the neutral axis is in the web. Split concrete force into web and flange overhangs. Web force Cw = 0.36 fck bw xu, lever arm d − 0.42 xu. Flange overhang force Cf = 0.36 fck (bf − bw) Df. When 0.42xu ≥ Df, its lever arm is d − Df/2. Then Mu = Cw(d − 0.42xu) + Cf(d − Df/2), and Ast = (Cw + Cf)/(0.87 fy). If 0.42xu < Df, the rectangular stress block does not cover the full flange depth. Replace Df by 0.42xu in Cf, so Cf = 0.36 fck (bf − bw)(0.42xu), and the lever arm becomes d − 0.42xu/2 = d − 0.21xu. If 0.42xu ≥ Df, use Df itself with lever arm d − Df/2.

If xu exceeds xu,max, add compression steel as in Chapter 2.3 or increase the depth. Design aids in Chapter 2.5 speed the rectangular step.

Worked example

Question. A T-beam has bf = 1000 mm, bw = 300 mm, Df = 100 mm, d = 500 mm, fck = 25 N/mm², fy = 415 N/mm² and Mu = 250 kN·m. Check whether xu lies within the flange and find Ast.

  1. Convert moment: Mu = 250 kN·m = 250 × 10⁶ N·mm.
  2. Assume xu ≤ Df = 100 mm. Use rectangular width bf = 1000 mm.
  3. Write Mu = 0.36 fck bf xu (d − 0.42 xu). Substitute: 250 × 10⁶ = 0.36 × 25 × 1000 × xu × (500 − 0.42 xu).
  4. Simplify: 250 × 10⁶ = 9000 xu (500 − 0.42 xu). Rearrange: 3780 xu² − 4,500,000 xu + 250,000,000 = 0.
  5. Solve: xu = [4,500,000 − √(4,500,000² − 4 × 3780 × 250,000,000)] / (2 × 3780) = 58.42 mm. The other root is above d, so reject it.
  6. Check assumption: xu = 58.42 mm < Df = 100 mm. The neutral axis lies inside the flange.
  7. Find steel: Ast = 0.36 fck bf xu / (0.87 fy) = 0.36 × 25 × 1000 × 58.42 / (0.87 × 415) = 1456.32 mm².
  8. Check ductility: xu/d = 58.42/500 = 0.117 < 0.48 for Fe 415. The section is under-reinforced.
  9. Provide 4 bars of 22 mm diameter: area = 4 × 380.13 = 1520.5 mm², which is more than 1456.32 mm².

Answer. xu = 58.42 mm lies within the 100 mm flange, so design as a rectangular beam of width 1000 mm; Ast = 1456.32 mm².

Exam traps

  • Using web width bw when the neutral axis lies inside the flange; the correct width is bf.
  • Taking the whole slab width as bf and forgetting the IS 456 limits from span and clear spacing.
  • Using the flange when the slab is in tension, such as over a support in a continuous beam.
  • In a T-section stress block, counting the full width bf in the flange overhang term instead of (bf − bw), which double-counts the web.
  • Forgetting to check xu ≤ xu,max; a flanged beam can still be over-reinforced even when the flange is in compression.

2.5 Design aids for flexure

Design aids are printed tables and charts that give the tension steel for a singly reinforced beam directly. You enter with Mu/bd² and the two grades, and read pt, the steel percentage. Some universities allow them in the exam hall. Only a few teach them formally; twenty minutes of practice is enough.

You need the moment capacity equation from Chapter 2.1 and the ductility limit on xu/d from Chapter 2.2.

The table that solves the quadratic

Moment capacity comes from the steel force and its lever arm. Written in terms of steel area, the equation is quadratic.

Mu = 0.87 fy Ast (d − 0.42 xu),   with   xu = 0.87 fy Ast / (0.36 fck b)

Mu is the factored moment in N·mm; fy the steel strength in N/mm²; Ast the tension steel area in mm²; d the effective depth in mm; b the width in mm; fck the concrete cube strength in N/mm²; xu the neutral axis depth in mm.

Solving it for every beam is slow and easy to mis-key. The aid solves it once over a grid. One table serves one pair of grades: enter with Mu/bd² in N/mm², read pt as a percentage of b d. Charts plot the same grid as curves.

  • Rectangular, singly reinforced section: no compression steel, no flange.
  • IS 456 stress block: 0.36 fck acting over the full compression depth xu, with its resultant at 0.42 xu below the extreme compression fibre, giving the lever arm d − 0.42 xu.
  • Under-reinforced section: the table stops at pt,lim.

Reading it correctly

  1. Put the factored moment in N·mm: 120 kN·m becomes 120 × 10⁶ N·mm.
  2. Divide by b d², with b and d in mm.
  3. Open the table for your fck and fy and find the nearest row.
  4. Read pt as a percentage, not a fraction.
  5. Compute Ast = pt b d / 100.
  6. Compare pt with pt,lim.

Ast = pt b d / 100

Ast is the tension steel area in mm²; pt the percentage read from the aid; b the width in mm; d the effective depth in mm.

Your value usually falls between two rows; interpolate linearly. A chart read to two significant figures is as good. Use effective depth, not overall depth: a 450 mm deep beam with 25 mm cover and one layer of 20 mm bars has d = 415 mm.

Tables stop at pt,lim because beyond it xu/d crosses its limit — 0.53 for Fe250, 0.48 for Fe415, 0.46 for Fe500 — and concrete crushes before the steel yields. For M25 with Fe415, pt,lim = 1.20 %. A demand above that needs a deeper or wider beam, or compression steel as in Chapter 2.3.

Worked example

Question. A singly reinforced beam has b = 250 mm, d = 400 mm and Mu = 120 kN·m. Concrete is M25, steel Fe415. Use design aid tables to find pt and Ast, and choose the bars.

  1. Mu = 120 × 10⁶ N·mm; b d² = 4.00 × 10⁷ mm³; Mu/bd² = 3.00 N/mm².
  2. M25–Fe415 table: pt = 1.00 %, so Ast = 1.00 × 250 × 400 / 100 = 1000 mm².
  3. 16 mm bar = 201 mm²; 1000/201 = 4.98, so 5 bars give 1005 mm², pt = 1.005 %.
  4. xu = 0.87 × 415 × 1005 / (0.36 × 25 × 250) = 161 mm; xu/d = 0.40 ≤ 0.48, so under-reinforced.

Answer. Ast = 1000 mm², provided as 5 bars of 16 mm (1005 mm²); pt = 1.005 % is below pt,lim = 1.20 %.

Exam traps

  • pt is a percentage: pt = 1.00 means 1 % of b d, so Ast = b d/100, not b d. Dropping the 100 makes the steel a hundred times too large.
  • Mu inside Mu/bd² must be in N·mm. A moment left in kN·m gives a ratio a million times too small.
  • Using overall depth D instead of effective depth d. The tables are built on the lever arm to the steel centroid.
  • Reading the Fe500 table for Fe415 steel can return a pt about 15–20 % too low; an M30 table for M25 changes pt by only a few per cent.
  • Using the aid for a T-beam or a doubly reinforced beam. Those tables know nothing of a flange or of compression steel.

2.6 Serviceability: deflection and crack control

A beam can be strong enough at collapse and still be unfit for use if it sags or cracks too much. Serviceability checks answer a different question: at the loads people actually apply, does the beam remain usable? IS 456 sets deflection and crack limits for this. Deflection is checked under service loads, not factored loads. The usual quick check is a span/depth rule with modification factors; crack control comes from bar spacing, cover and distribution steel. Some syllabi call this deflection control and crack control.

You need Chapter 2.1 for beam behaviour and the serviceability limit state from Unit 1.

Service loads and why deflection matters

Limit state of serviceability asks whether the beam remains usable under the loads that actually act. These are service loads: characteristic loads with a partial safety factor of 1.0. Factored loads belong to the collapse check, not here. Excessive deflection damages partitions, finishes and ceilings, causes ponding on roofs and makes floors feel shaky. IS 456 gives two deflection limits for beams. Both are checked under service loads.

SituationLimit
Total deflectionspan/250
After partitions or finishesspan/350 or 20 mm, whichever is less

Short-term deflection is the immediate elastic sag. Long-term deflection grows with time because concrete creeps under sustained load and shrinks as it dries. A beam that looks fine on the day of casting can sag more after a year, so the long-term value is the one compared with the limits.

Deflection check: limits and span/depth method

The rigorous route computes short-term deflection from elastic theory on a cracked section, then adds creep and shrinkage. The simplified route, used in most designs, checks span to effective depth. The basic ratio depends on support type. Modification factors adjust for tension steel, compression steel and flanged action.

L/d ≤ (L/d)basic × kt × kc × kf

L is the effective span; d is the effective depth; (L/d)basic is the code basic ratio for the support type; kt is the modification factor for tension steel; kc is the modification factor for compression steel; kf is the modification factor for a flanged section.

kt is largest for lightly reinforced sections, about 2.0 at a tension steel percentage near 0.2%, and falls as the tension steel percentage increases. A lower steel percentage gives a shallower neutral axis and lower curvature at the same service steel stress, so the deflection is smaller and a larger span/depth is permitted. Heavy tension steel reduces kt. kc accounts for compression steel, which reduces long-term deflection. kf accounts for the extra stiffness of a T or L flange in compression. Use the factors from IS 456 tables or charts with the actual steel percentage and stress. If the check fails, increasing depth is the most effective change.

The cracking moment and transformed-section calculation behind the rigorous route are in Chapter 2.7.

Crack control by detailing

Flexural cracks appear where concrete tension exceeds its strength. IS 456 controls them mainly by detailing: limit bar spacing, provide adequate cover, and distribute steel across the tension zone. Small diameter bars at close spacing produce many fine cracks instead of a few wide ones. Cover protects steel and controls the crack width at the surface; too little cover lets a crack open widely.

Deep beams need side face reinforcement to control cracks on the vertical faces. Slabs use distribution steel at right angles to the main bars. For normal exposure, a surface crack width of about 0.3 mm is the usual limit; aggressive exposure needs stricter control. The detailed crack-width formula and shear cracks are in Unit 3.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported beam has span 6.0 m and computed total deflection 22 mm. The partition is installed after construction and computed deflection after partition is 19 mm. Check the IS 456 deflection limits.

  1. Convert span to millimetres: L = 6.0 m × 1000 = 6000 mm.
  2. Total deflection limit = L/250 = 6000/250 = 24.0 mm.
  3. Compare total deflection: 22.0 mm ≤ 24.0 mm, so the total deflection check passes.
  4. After-partition limit = L/350 = 6000/350 = 17.14 mm; the 20 mm cap is larger, so the limit is 17.14 mm.
  5. Compare after-partition deflection: 19.0 mm > 17.14 mm, so the after-partition check fails.

Answer. Total deflection is within span/250, but deflection after partition exceeds span/350 or 20 mm; the beam needs revision by increasing depth or reducing deflection.

Exam traps

  • Using factored loads for deflection or crack checks. Serviceability uses service loads, not design loads for collapse.
  • Comparing after-partition deflection with span/250. The stricter limit is span/350 or 20 mm, whichever is less.
  • Forgetting the 20 mm cap in the after-partition limit. For spans greater than 7 m, L/350 exceeds 20 mm, so the 20 mm cap governs; for shorter spans L/350 is smaller and governs.
  • Using the basic span/depth ratio without modification factors for tension steel, compression steel or flanged sections.
  • Treating a beam that passes collapse design as automatically serviceable. Deflection and cracking are separate limit states.

2.7 Cracking moment and transformed sections

Concrete cracks when the tensile stress at its extreme fibre reaches a small fraction of its compressive strength. Before that point the beam is uncracked and behaves elastically. The modular ratio m lets you replace steel bars by an equivalent area of concrete, so one neutral axis and one second moment of area give the stresses. The same transformed section gives the cracking moment Mcr, the bending moment at which the first flexural crack appears. Deflection checks, crack initiation and the switch to a cracked-section analysis all use Mcr.

You need: concrete properties and limit state philosophy (see Unit 1, Design Philosophies and Material Behaviour) and the singly reinforced beam neutral-axis idea in Chapter 2.1.

Elastic behaviour before cracking

At loads below Mcr the beam is uncracked and behaves elastically. Plane sections stay plane, so concrete and steel at the same level have the same strain. Stress is E times strain. Steel has Es = 200,000 N/mm²; concrete has Ec = 5000√fck N/mm², where fck is characteristic cube strength in N/mm². The modular ratio is m = Es/Ec. For M25, fck = 25 N/mm², Ec = 25,000 N/mm², m = 8.00. Steel stress is m times the concrete stress at the same level. The value of m is short-term; creep under sustained load raises the effective m. This chapter uses the short-term value. The transformed section replaces steel by an equivalent concrete area so that one elastic analysis gives both materials' stresses. Cracking moment uses this uncracked section, not the cracked section of Chapter 2.6. Under normal service loads a reinforced beam is already cracked; the uncracked transformed section gives Mcr, not the service stresses. A small tensile stress can crack concrete even when the steel is nowhere near yield.

Transformed section for an uncracked beam

Steel displaces concrete. In the uncracked section, tension steel of area Ast sits at effective depth d. Replace it by (m−1)Ast of concrete at the same level. The mAst supplies the steel force; the −Ast removes the concrete that is not there. Use (m−1)Ast, not mAst. For b width, D overall depth and ybar from the compression face:

ybar = [bD(D/2) + (m − 1)Ast d] / [bD + (m − 1)Ast]

ybar is distance from compression face in mm; b is width in mm; D is overall depth in mm; d is effective depth in mm; Ast is tension steel area in mm²; m is modular ratio.

The second moment about the neutral axis is

I = bD³/12 + bD(ybar − D/2)² + (m − 1)Ast(d − ybar)²

I is second moment of area about the neutral axis in mm⁴; other symbols are as above.

Here b and D are in mm, Ast in mm², d and ybar in mm, and I in mm⁴. Concrete stress at distance y from the neutral axis is σc = M y / I, where M is bending moment in N·mm. Steel stress before cracking is σs = m M (d−ybar)/I. After cracking, concrete tension is ignored; the cracked transformed section uses mAst and a new neutral axis. That section gives steel stress after cracking. It is not used for Mcr.

Cracking moment

The first flexural crack forms when the tensile stress at the extreme tension fibre reaches the flexural tensile strength fr = 0.7√fck N/mm². This is a mean value, not a design strength. The cracking moment is Mcr = fr I / yt. I and ybar come from the uncracked transformed section. yt is the distance from the neutral axis to the extreme tension fibre; for a bottom-tension beam yt = D − ybar. With fr in N/mm², I in mm⁴ and yt in mm, Mcr is in N·mm; divide by 10⁶ for kN·m. Use Mcr to decide whether a service moment causes cracking. If Mservice > Mcr, use the cracked section for deflection and steel stress, as in Chapter 2.6. If Mservice ≤ Mcr, the section is uncracked and the transformed uncracked section applies. Mcr is not the ultimate moment and must not be compared directly with a factored design moment.

Mcr = fr I / yt

Mcr is cracking moment in N·mm; fr = 0.7√fck is flexural tensile strength in N/mm²; I is uncracked transformed second moment of area in mm⁴; yt is distance from neutral axis to extreme tension fibre in mm.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular beam has b = 300 mm, D = 500 mm, d = 450 mm, Ast = 1200 mm² and fck = 25 N/mm². Take Es = 200,000 N/mm² and Ec = 5000√fck. Find the modular ratio m and the uncracked transformed-section cracking moment Mcr.

  1. Ec = 5000√25 = 25,000 N/mm². m = Es/Ec = 200,000/25,000 = 8.00.
  2. Gross concrete area Ac = bD = 300 × 500 = 150,000 mm². Its centroid is D/2 = 250 mm from the compression face.
  3. Net transformed steel area As' = (m − 1)Ast = (8 − 1) × 1200 = 8400 mm² at d = 450 mm.
  4. Total transformed area A = 150,000 + 8400 = 158,400 mm². ybar = (150,000 × 250 + 8400 × 450) / 158,400 = 41,280,000 / 158,400 = 260.61 mm.
  5. Concrete term: bD³/12 = 300 × 500³ / 12 = 3.125 × 10⁹ mm⁴. Shift term: 150,000(260.61 − 250)² = 16.87 × 10⁶ mm⁴. Steel term: 8400(450 − 260.61)² = 301.31 × 10⁶ mm⁴. I = 3.125 × 10⁹ + 16.87 × 10⁶ + 301.31 × 10⁶ = 3.443 × 10⁹ mm⁴.
  6. fr = 0.7√25 = 3.5 N/mm². yt = D − ybar = 500 − 260.61 = 239.39 mm.
  7. Mcr = fr I / yt = 3.5 × 3.443 × 10⁹ / 239.39 = 50.34 × 10⁶ N·mm = 50.34 kN·m.

Answer. m = 8.00 and Mcr ≈ 50.34 kN·m.

Exam traps

  • Using mAst instead of (m − 1)Ast for tension steel in an uncracked transformed section. The −Ast removes concrete displaced by the bar.
  • Using the cracked transformed-section I to compute Mcr. Mcr belongs to the uncracked section; the cracked section is for post-cracking steel stress.
  • Taking yt as D/2 or D. Find the neutral axis of the transformed section first; for bottom tension yt = D − ybar.
  • Forgetting units: fr in N/mm², I in mm⁴, yt in mm give Mcr in N·mm. Divide by 10⁶ for kN·m.
  • Treating fr = 0.7√fck as a design strength and comparing Mcr directly with factored Mu. Mcr is a mean cracking value, not a limit-state strength.

Takeaways

  1. In limit state design, the factored moment is resisted by an internal couple between the concrete compression block and the tension steel.
  2. The neutral axis depth follows from horizontal equilibrium and strain compatibility, and it must not exceed xu,max for a ductile section.
  3. The steel percentage relative to the balanced value decides whether steel yields first, concrete crushes first, or both occur together.
  4. When Mu exceeds Mu,lim or depth is restricted, compression steel and extra tension steel form a second internal couple.
  5. A monolithic slab can act as a compression flange only over a limited effective width.
  6. Design aids replace repetitive quadratic solutions with direct tables and charts, but the designer still checks ductility and detailing.
  7. Strength checks alone are not enough; deflection and crack width must satisfy serviceability limits at service loads.
  8. Before cracking, a transformed section gives elastic stresses and the cracking moment Mcr.

Glossary

Balanced steel percentage
The steel percentage that makes steel yield exactly when concrete crushes.
Characteristic strength
The strength value below which not more than 5% of test results are expected to fall.
Crack width
The width of a visible crack at the concrete surface, checked for durability and appearance.
Cracking moment
The bending moment at which the extreme concrete fibre reaches its flexural tensile strength.
Deflection
Vertical movement of a beam under load, checked against span limits.
Design aid
A table or chart that gives steel percentage or moment capacity directly.
Design value
The value obtained by dividing characteristic strength by the partial safety factor for material.
Doubly reinforced beam
A beam with both compression steel and tension steel designed to resist moment.
Effective flange width
The limited slab width assumed to act with the beam web in compression.
Factored load
A load obtained by multiplying a characteristic load by the partial safety factor for loads.
Flange
The horizontal slab part of a T- or L-beam that provides compression area.
Flanged beam
A beam in which part of a monolithic slab acts as a compression flange.
Lever arm
The perpendicular distance between the compression and tension resultants in a section.
Limit state
A condition beyond which a structure no longer satisfies its design requirements.
Limiting moment of resistance
The maximum moment a singly reinforced section can carry when the neutral axis is at xu,max.
Modular ratio
The ratio of modulus of elasticity of steel to that of concrete.
Over-reinforced section
A section in which concrete crushes before tension steel yields.
Partial safety factor
A multiplier that accounts for uncertainty in loads or material strength.
Serviceability
The ability of a structure to remain usable under service loads without excessive deflection or cracking.
Singly reinforced beam
A beam with tension steel only and no designed compression steel.
Stress block
An idealised shape of concrete stress at collapse used to compute compression force.
Transformed section
An elastic section in which steel area is replaced by an equivalent concrete area.
Under-reinforced section
A section in which tension steel yields before concrete reaches crushing strain.
Web
The vertical part of a flanged beam below the slab flange.
xu,max
The limiting neutral axis depth for a ductile section with a given steel grade.
