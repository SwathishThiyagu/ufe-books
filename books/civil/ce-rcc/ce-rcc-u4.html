4.1 One-way slabs

A one-way slab bends mainly in the short direction. Take a 1 m wide strip along the short span and design it as a beam. This works when the long span ly is at least twice the short span lx. The strip carries the full factored load w in kN/m. Use Mu = wl²/8, Vu = wl/2, then find steel from Mu/bd²fck. Add distribution steel at right angles and check shear, deflection and cracking. This is the common floor slab in buildings.

You need Unit 1 (partial safety factors, stress block) and Unit 2 (singly reinforced flexure, Mu/bd²fck); see 4.2 for the two-way case.

Why the short span carries the load

A slab is a plate supported on edges. Under load it curves in both directions. The stiffness of a unit-width strip against bending varies as 1/l⁴, where l is the span of that strip. If the long span ly is twice the short span lx, the short strip is 2⁴ = 16 times stiffer. The short direction then carries about 16/17 ≈ 94% of the load. IS 456 treats a slab as one-way when ly/lx ≥ 2. Some syllabi call this simply a slab spanning one way.

The design takes a 1 m wide strip spanning the short direction. This strip is a beam of width b = 1000 mm and depth h equal to the slab thickness. The effective depth is d = h - clear cover - φ/2, where φ is the main bar diameter in mm. A load of w kN/m² on the slab becomes a line load of w kN/m on the strip. The unit is kN per metre of strip, not kN total.

A simply supported strip uses Mu = wl²/8 and Vu = wl/2. Continuous slabs use code coefficients that allow for pattern loading (see 4.3). A cantilever slab has hogging moment at its support. This chapter uses the simply supported case. If ly/lx < 2, the slab bends in both directions and must be designed as a two-way slab (see 4.2).

Design steps for a 1 m strip

  1. Pick a trial thickness h. For a simply supported one-way slab, basic span/effective depth is 20 for mild steel. For Fe415 multiply by 0.8, giving about 16. Use a round value such as 100 mm, 110 mm or 120 mm.
  2. Find the effective span l. Use clear span plus effective depth, or centre-to-centre of supports, whichever is less. For small bearings many designs take l equal to clear span.
  3. Build the load per m²: self weight = 25h (h in m), floor finish, and live load. Total service load = DL + LL. Factored load w = 1.5(DL + LL).
  4. Compute moment and shear per metre strip: Mu = wl²/8 and Vu = wl/2.
  5. Compute d = h - clear cover - φ/2 with b = 1000 mm. Check Mu ≤ 0.138 fck b d² for Fe415.
  6. Find steel area Ast from the flexure formula. Take the larger of Ast required and the minimum steel.
  7. Minimum steel is 0.12% of b h for Fe415 (0.15% for mild steel).
  8. Fix spacing: main bars ≤ min(3d, 300 mm); distribution bars ≤ min(5d, 450 mm). Distribution steel area ≥ 0.12% b h.

Mu = w l² / 8

w is the factored load in kN/m on the 1 m strip; l is the effective span in m; Mu is in kN·m per metre width.

Mu = 0.87 fy Ast d (1 - fy Ast / (fck b d))

fy is steel strength in N/mm²; Ast is steel area in mm²; d is effective depth in mm; b = 1000 mm; fck is concrete strength in N/mm².

Checks and detailing

Shear check: τv = Vu/(b d). The design shear strength τc is read from IS 456 Table 19 using pt = 100 Ast/(b d). A slab has no shear reinforcement, so it must satisfy τv ≤ k·τc, where k is the depth factor from IS 456 clause 40.2.1.1 (about 1.30 for h ≤ 150 mm, falling to 1.00 for h ≥ 300 mm). Using k = 1 is always safe. If the check fails, increase the thickness h.

Deflection check: basic span/depth is 20 for simply supported, 26 for continuous, and 7 for cantilever. Multiply by the modification factor for tension steel. The modified ratio must be at least l/d. A 120 mm slab on a 3 m span has l/d ≈ 31.6; low steel gives a modification factor that can make it acceptable. Always check.

Crack control: keep main bar spacing ≤ 300 mm and distribution bar spacing ≤ 450 mm. Provide nominal cover of 20 mm for mild exposure (this may be taken as 15 mm when the main bar is 12 mm or smaller) and 30 mm for moderate exposure, as per IS 456 Table 16. Distribution steel runs at right angles to the main steel. It holds the main bars during casting, resists shrinkage and temperature stresses, and spreads local loads.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported one-way slab has a clear span of 3.0 m between 230 mm walls; the effective span is 3.0 m. Live load 3.0 kN/m², floor finish 1.0 kN/m², thickness 120 mm, M25 concrete, Fe415 steel, clear cover 20 mm. Find the factored moment and the main and distribution steel.

  1. Thickness h = 120 mm = 0.12 m. Self weight = 0.12 × 25 = 3.0 kN/m². Floor finish = 1.0 kN/m². Live load = 3.0 kN/m². Total service load = 3.0 + 1.0 + 3.0 = 7.0 kN/m².
  2. Factored load w = 1.5 × 7.0 = 10.5 kN/m². On a 1 m strip, w = 10.5 kN/m.
  3. Effective span by the Section 2 rule: clear span + d = 3.0 + 0.095 = 3.095 m (d = 95 mm from step 4), and centre-to-centre of supports = 3.0 + 0.230 = 3.23 m. The lesser is 3.095 m. This example takes l = 3.0 m only as a conservative simplification, which slightly over-designs the slab. Then Mu = wl²/8 = 10.5 × 3.0² / 8 = 11.8125 kN·m per m, and Vu = wl/2 = 10.5 × 3.0 / 2 = 15.75 kN per m.
  4. Effective depth: clear cover 20 mm, assume 10 mm bar. d = 120 - 20 - 10/2 = 95 mm. b = 1000 mm.
  5. Limiting moment: Mu,lim = 0.138 fck b d² = 0.138 × 25 × 1000 × 95² = 31.14 × 10⁶ N·mm = 31.14 kN·m. Mu = 11.8125 < 31.14, so the section is under-reinforced.
  6. Steel: R = Mu/(b d²) = 11.8125×10⁶ / (1000×95²) = 1.309 N/mm². Ast = (0.5 fck/fy)[1 - √(1 - 4.6 Mu/(fck b d²))] b d = (0.5×25/415)[1 - √(1 - 4.6×11.8125×10⁶/(25×1000×95²))] × 1000×95 = 368.2 mm²/m.
  7. Minimum steel = 0.12% of b h = 0.0012 × 1000 × 120 = 144.0 mm²/m. Required 368.2 > 144.0, so use 368.2 mm²/m.
  8. Spacing of 10 mm bars: area of one bar = 78.54 mm². Spacing = 1000 × 78.54 / 368.2 = 213.3 mm. Use 200 mm c/c, which provides 392.7 mm²/m.
  9. Distribution steel = 144.0 mm²/m. Use 8 mm bars, area 50.27 mm². Spacing = 1000 × 50.27 / 144.0 = 349 mm. Maximum allowed = min(5d, 450) = min(475, 450) = 450 mm. Use 250 mm c/c, which provides 201.1 mm²/m.

Answer. The factored load is 10.5 kN/m², Mu = 11.8125 kN·m per metre, Vu = 15.75 kN per metre, main steel 368.2 mm²/m (use 10 mm @ 200 mm c/c), distribution steel 144.0 mm²/m (use 8 mm @ 250 mm c/c).

Exam traps

  • Writing the total load in kN instead of kN/m. On a 1 m strip, a load of 10.5 kN/m² becomes 10.5 kN/m.
  • Using the full slab width (say 3 m) in Mu/bd². Take b = 1000 mm for a 1 m strip.
  • Forgetting distribution steel. It is at least 0.12% of b h for Fe415 and runs at right angles to the main steel.
  • Using the wrong cover when finding d. Take clear cover, not effective cover, and subtract half the bar diameter.

4.2 Two-way slabs with various edge conditions

A two-way slab bends in both directions because its long span is not much larger than its short span. When ly/lx ≤ 2, load travels to all four supports and the design needs two sets of moments and steel. IS 456 Table 26 supplies coefficients for nine edge conditions, so the same panel gives different moments when edges are continuous or free. This chapter shows how to choose the case, compute Mx and My, place steel in both directions, and add corner torsion steel. It is used for floor panels in houses, offices and hospitals.

You need Chapter 4.1 for slab action and Unit 2 for flexure design.

Why ly/lx decides two-way action

A slab is a plate supported on four edges. Let lx be the effective short span and ly the effective long span. Measure each between beam centrelines, or as clear span plus effective depth, whichever is smaller. The ratio ly/lx decides the action. If ly/lx > 2, most load travels to the two long edges and the slab is one-way; design it as 1 m wide beams (Chapter 4.1). If ly/lx ≤ 2, load travels to all four edges and the panel is two-way.

Rankine–Grashoff explains why. Assume the central strips deflect equally. Let w be total factored load per m², and wx and wy the parts carried in the short and long directions. Then wx lx⁴ = wy ly⁴ and wx + wy = w, so wx = w ly⁴/(lx⁴ + ly⁴) and wy = w lx⁴/(lx⁴ + ly⁴). For lx = 4 m and ly = 5 m, wx = 0.71w and wy = 0.29w. The short span carries more because it is stiffer. IS 456 Table 26 coefficients start from this split and correct for continuity, corner torsion and the real support pattern.

Factored load means 1.5 times the service dead plus live load for the usual limit-state combination. Effective span, not clear span, enters both the ratio and the moment formula.

Table 26 coefficients and the nine edge cases

Table 26 gives four dimensionless coefficients for each case: αx(-) and αx(+) for the short direction at a continuous edge and at midspan; αy(-) and αy(+) for the long direction. Negative means hogging at support; positive means sagging at midspan. Then Mx(-) = αx(-) w lx², Mx(+) = αx(+) w lx², My(-) = αy(-) w ly², My(+) = αy(+) w ly².

Mx = αx w lx², My = αy w ly²

Mx and My are factored moments per metre width in the short and long directions (kN·m/m); αx and αy are Table 26 coefficients; w is factored load (kN/m²); lx and ly are effective spans (m).

Read the row by edge condition. The nine distinct cases in IS 456 Table 26 are: (1) interior panel, all four edges continuous; (2) one short edge discontinuous; (3) one long edge discontinuous; (4) two adjacent edges discontinuous; (5) two short edges discontinuous; (6) two long edges discontinuous; (7) three edges discontinuous with one long edge continuous; (8) three edges discontinuous with one short edge continuous; (9) four edges discontinuous, that is all edges simply supported. Some syllabi name a case by the edges that are discontinuous; match the pattern carefully.

The middle row is the linear interpolation for two adjacent edges discontinuous at ly/lx = 1.25. Interpolate between table rows when the ratio falls between them.

Steel in both directions, torsion steel and checks

Design each direction as a 1 m wide rectangular beam. For the short span use d_x = D - cover - φ/2, where D is slab thickness and φ is bar diameter. For the long span use d_y = d_x - φ because long-span bars lie above short-span bars. This keeps the larger effective depth for the larger short-span moment. Compute Ast from Unit 2 flexure, or use the design aids. Minimum steel is 0.12% of bD for Fe415; maximum spacing is min(3d, 300 mm).

At a corner where both edges are discontinuous, the corner tends to lift. Provide torsion steel top and bottom, each layer 0.75 times the steel for the maximum midspan moment, in both directions, extending lx/5 from the corner. At a corner where only one of the two meeting edges is continuous, provide half the torsion steel, that is 0.375 times the steel for the maximum midspan moment, again in four layers over lx/5. At continuous edges, top steel must resist the hogging moment and extend into the support.

Checks: one-way shear. A safe upper bound is Vu = w lx/2 at the long edge, which assumes the whole load is carried in the short direction. The exact share is wx lx/2 with wx from the Rankine–Grashoff split. Then τv = Vu/(b d) ≤ τc; deflection by span/depth; crack control by spacing. Yield line collapse is treated in Chapter 4.6; flat slabs in Chapter 4.5.

Worked example

Question. A 140 mm thick slab covers an effective panel of 4.0 m × 5.0 m. Two adjacent edges are discontinuous and the other two are continuous over beams. Live load 4.0 kN/m², floor finish 1.0 kN/m², M25 concrete, Fe415 steel, cover 20 mm. Find the factored moments in both directions and the steel.

  1. Effective spans: lx = 4.0 m, ly = 5.0 m, so ly/lx = 1.25 < 2. The panel is two-way.
  2. Loads: self weight = 0.140 × 25 = 3.50 kN/m²; finish = 1.00; live = 4.00. Service load = 8.50 kN/m². Factored w = 1.5 × 8.50 = 12.75 kN/m².
  3. Edge case: two adjacent edges discontinuous. From Table 26 at ly/lx = 1.25: αx(-) = 0.063, αx(+) = 0.0475, αy(-) = 0.037, αy(+) = 0.0265.
  4. Moments: Mx(-) = 0.063 × 12.75 × 4.0² = 12.85 kN·m/m; Mx(+) = 0.0475 × 12.75 × 16 = 9.69 kN·m/m; My(-) = 0.037 × 12.75 × 5.0² = 11.79 kN·m/m; My(+) = 0.0265 × 12.75 × 25 = 8.45 kN·m/m.
  5. Effective depths: assume 8 mm bars. d_x = 140 - 20 - 4 = 116 mm. d_y = 116 - 8 = 108 mm.
  6. Steel: with M25, Fe415 and b = 1000 mm, Ast = 0.5(fck/fy)[1 - √(1 - 4.6M/(fck b d²))] b d. For Mx(-), 4.6M/(fck b d²) = 0.176, giving Ast = 322 mm²/m. Similarly Mx(+) = 240 mm²/m, My(-) = 318 mm²/m, My(+) = 224 mm²/m.
  7. Minimum steel = 0.12% × 1000 × 140 = 168 mm²/m. All required areas exceed the minimum.
  8. Provide 8 mm @ 150 mm c/c for Mx(-) (335 mm²/m); 8 mm @ 200 mm c/c for Mx(+) (251 mm²/m); 8 mm @ 150 mm c/c for My(-) (335 mm²/m); 8 mm @ 200 mm c/c for My(+) (251 mm²/m). Long-span bars sit above short-span bars.
  9. Corner torsion at the corner with both edges discontinuous: 0.75 × 240 = 180 mm²/m top and bottom each way. Provide 8 mm @ 250 mm c/c (201 mm²/m) for a length lx/5 = 0.8 m. At the two mixed corners (one edge continuous, one discontinuous), provide half: 0.5 × 180 = 90 mm²/m, for example 8 mm @ 250 mm c/c top and bottom each way over 0.8 m.
  10. Checks: Vu = w lx/2 = 25.5 kN/m is a safe upper bound that assumes all load travels in the short direction; the exact share is wx lx/2 ≈ 0.71 × 12.75 × 2.0 = 18.1 kN/m. Use Vu = 25.5 kN/m for design; τv = 25.5 × 10³/(1000 × 116) = 0.22 MPa. With pt ≈ 0.29%, τc is above 0.22 MPa, so shear is safe. Check deflection and development length by IS 456.

Answer. The factored moments are Mx(-) = 12.85, Mx(+) = 9.69, My(-) = 11.79 and My(+) = 8.45 kN·m/m; steel required is 322, 240, 318 and 224 mm²/m, provided as 8 mm @ 150, 200, 150 and 200 mm c/c.

Exam traps

  • Using clear spans instead of effective spans to get ly/lx, which can move the panel to the wrong table row.
  • Reading the coefficient row for the wrong edge case, especially confusing one short edge discontinuous with one long edge discontinuous.
  • Swapping αx and αy, or pairing αy with lx². Mx always uses lx² and My always uses ly².
  • Forgetting that long-span steel is placed above short-span steel, so d_y is smaller than d_x.
  • Skipping torsion steel at a corner where both edges are discontinuous, or extending it less than lx/5.

4.3 Cantilever and continuous slabs

A cantilever slab is fixed at one edge and free at the other: it carries a hogging moment at the support, so its main steel goes at the top. A continuous slab runs over a row of beams, and the code gives its design moments from a table of coefficients instead of analysing every live-load pattern. A sunshade and a corridor floor are the two cases. Both are one-way slabs designed by limit state.

You need: the one-way slab strip and limit state flexure design from Chapter 4.1 and Unit 2.

Cantilever slab: moment at the face, steel at the top

A cantilever slab is fixed at one edge and free at the other: a chajja, a shop canopy, a small balcony. It bends down under load, so the top fibre stretches. Tension is at the top, so the main steel lies near the top face and runs back into the slab behind the support.

Design a 1 m wide strip. A load of w kN/m² on that strip is the same number in kN/m, because the strip is 1 m wide. Self weight is 25t kN/m², where t is the thickness in metres and 25 kN/m³ is the unit weight of reinforced concrete.

Mu = wu l² / 2

Mu is the hogging moment at the face of the support, per metre width, in kN·m/m; wu is the total factored load in kN/m² (the same number in kN/m on a 1 m strip); l is the clear projection from the face of the support to the free edge, in metres.

  1. Take l from the face of the support to the free edge, never to the beam centre. IS 456 uses clear projection plus half the effective depth as effective length; the face still governs the moment.
  2. Add self weight, finish and live load; multiply by 1.5 for wu.
  3. Compute Mu = wu l²/2.
  4. Choose the thickness so l/d stays within the basic 7 for a cantilever, adjusted by the modification factor for the steel area.
  5. Find Ast for Mu; check it against 0.12 per cent of the gross area for Fe415 steel.
  6. Place the bars at the top, spacing not more than the smaller of 3d and 300 mm.
  7. Run the top bars into the back span at least the development length Ld beyond the face.
  8. Check shear at a distance d from the face, where V = wu (l − d).

Continuous slabs and the code moment coefficients

A slab cast over a row of parallel beams is continuous over the supports. An exact analysis needs several arrangements of live load, one per critical section. IS 456 gives coefficient tables for continuous beams, which a 1 m strip of slab uses directly.

Mu = α wu l²

Mu is the design moment at the section considered, per metre width, in kN·m/m; α is the coefficient read from the code table for that section and span type; wu is the total factored load in kN/m²; l is the effective span in metres.

  • Three or more spans.
  • Spans differ by not more than 15 per cent of the longest span.
  • The load is uniformly distributed over the whole slab.
  • The cross-section is the same in all spans.
  • No concentrated load the tables do not cover.

The tables already contain pattern loading. The largest sagging moment in a span comes with live load on that span and on alternate spans, dead load everywhere. The largest hogging moment at a support comes with live load on the two spans on either side of it. Designing each section for the worse case gives the moment envelope.

Top steel near a support follows that envelope. The hogging moment falls away from the support, so part of the top steel can be curtailed, keeping at least half running into the span and carrying every curtailed bar beyond the cut-off point by the anchorage length.

Where the two cases bite in detailing

In a cantilever the support is the whole design. The top bars must be anchored into the back span for a full development length, because bond over that length carries the hogging moment at the face. If the projection is short and the back span narrow, bend the bars down into the beam. Add distribution steel at right angles to the main bars.

In a continuous slab the steel follows the envelope: bottom bars at midspan, top bars over every support, heavier next to an end support. Top bars over an interior support usually run from the two adjacent spans and must reach far enough on both sides for bond.

Worked example

Question. A cantilever slab projects 1.5 m clear from the face of a beam. Live load 3.0 kN/m², floor finish 1.0 kN/m², thickness 150 mm, M25 concrete, Fe415 steel, clear cover 20 mm, 10 mm main bars. Find the hogging moment at the support and the main steel. Then state how a three-span continuous slab of 3.0 m each, carrying the same loads, is designed by the coefficient method.

  1. Self weight = 25 × 0.150 = 3.75 kN/m². Total service load w = 3.75 + 1.0 + 3.0 = 7.75 kN/m².
  2. wu = 1.5 × 7.75 = 11.625 kN/m², which is 11.625 kN/m on a 1 m strip.
  3. l = 1.5 m to the face. Mu = 11.625 × 1.5²/2 = 13.078 kN·m per metre.
  4. d = 150 − 20 − 5 = 125 mm. Mu/(fck b d²) = 13.078 × 10⁶/(25 × 1000 × 125²) = 0.0335, so the section is under-reinforced.
  5. Solving Mu = 0.87 fy Ast d (1 − Ast fy/(b d fck)) gives Ast = 301.9 mm²/m. Minimum steel = 0.12% of 1000 × 150 = 180 mm²/m, so 301.9 mm²/m governs.
  6. Provide 10 mm bars at 250 mm centres: 314.16 mm²/m, below the smaller of 3d = 375 mm and 300 mm. Capacity is 13.59 kN·m/m, greater than 13.078.
  7. Shear at d from the face: V = 11.625 × (1.5 − 0.125) = 15.98 kN/m, τv = 15.98 × 10³/(1000 × 125) = 0.128 MPa. The code shear strength for M25 at this steel is higher, so no shear reinforcement is needed.
  8. Development length Ld = φ × 0.87 fy/(4 τbd) = 10 × 361/(4 × 2.24) = 403 mm into the back span beyond the face.
  9. l/d = 1500/125 = 12 against a basic 7 for a cantilever; even with the modification factor the limit is near 10, so 150 mm is shallow and 175 mm is the right choice.
  10. Continuous slab: wu l² = 11.625 × 3.0² = 104.6 kN·m per metre. Check the three-span and 15 per cent conditions, read α at each section, and take Mu = α × 104.6 kN·m/m.
  11. Design the steel at each section as in Chapter 4.1, keep top steel over every support, and curtail it where the envelope falls, carrying each curtailed bar beyond the cut-off by the anchorage length.

Answer. Mu = 13.078 kN·m per metre at the face; Ast = 301.9 mm²/m, so 10 mm bars at 250 mm centres at the top (314.16 mm²/m). At d from the face V = 15.98 kN/m and τv = 0.128 MPa, so no shear reinforcement is needed. The 1.5 m cantilever is shallow at 150 mm and better at 175 mm.

Exam traps

  • Measuring the cantilever span to the centre of the support instead of the face. The hogging moment is critical at the face.
  • Putting the main steel of a cantilever at the bottom. Tension is at the top, so bottom steel is wasted and the slab cracks at the support.
  • Using the code coefficients with only two spans, with spans differing by more than 15 per cent of the longest, or under a heavy concentrated load.
  • Adding separate pattern-load cases on top of the coefficients. They already include pattern loading; the design moments are the envelope.
  • Curtailing top steel in a continuous slab without the anchorage length beyond the cut-off, so the bar pulls out before it can develop its force.

Check yourself

Remember Where is the main steel of a cantilever slab placed, and why? 2 marks

Near the top face. The hogging moment at the support puts the top fibre in tension.

Remember Which code tables give the moment coefficients for continuous beams and slabs? 2 marks

The bending moment coefficient tables of IS 456, one for three spans and one for four or more; a 1 m strip of slab uses them directly.

Understand Why does the code require the spans to be nearly equal before the coefficients may be used? 3 marks

The coefficients come from the analysis of equal spans. A much longer span attracts a different share of moment, and the tabulated values no longer bound the real moments.

Apply A cantilever slab projects 1.2 m clear, thickness 120 mm, floor finish 1.0 kN/m², live load 2.5 kN/m². Find the factored hogging moment at the face and the minimum steel. 4 marks

Self weight = 25 × 0.12 = 3.0 kN/m²; w = 6.5 kN/m²; wu = 9.75 kN/m²; Mu = 9.75 × 1.2²/2 = 7.02 kN·m/m. Minimum steel = 0.12% × 1000 × 120 = 144 mm²/m.

Apply For a four-span continuous slab, which spans carry live load to give the largest sagging moment in the second span? 4 marks

Live load on the second span and on the fourth, with dead load on all spans. Alternate loading lets the loaded span sag without the neighbours holding it up.

Analyze Why does the largest hogging moment at a support occur when live load acts only on the two spans next to that support? 5 marks

Those two spans bend down and rotate the slab over the support, pulling the top fibre there. Loading the further spans would stiffen the region and reduce the hogging.

Analyze A designer measures a 1.5 m cantilever to the centre of a 300 mm wide beam and designs for the larger moment. Is the design safe and economical? 6 marks

Safe in flexure, because the moment is overestimated, but wasteful in steel; it also puts the anchorage check on the wrong section, and the true force at the face may not be developed if the bar is cut short.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. In practice a continuous slab is modelled in a frame analysis program with pattern loading, and the code coefficients act as a check on the output. Flat slabs need the frame method or the direct design method, and yield line theory gives the collapse load for irregular panels.

4.4 Staircases: dog-legged and other types

A staircase carries people between floors, and each sloping flight behaves as a slab bending along the slope. In the dog-legged form two flights run side by side in opposite directions, joined by a half landing at mid-height. The flight is designed as a simply supported one-way slab — the waist slab — spanning between landings, using the same flexure, shear and deflection checks as one-way slabs, plus one extra step: the sloping dead load is converted to load per metre of horizontal run.

You need: one-way slabs for the b d, Ast and shear routine, and cantilever and continuous slabs for support conditions at the landings.

Riser, tread, going and the dog-legged plan

The riser is the vertical height of one step; the tread its horizontal depth; the going is the tread without the nosing. A comfortable stair keeps 2 × riser + tread between 600 mm and 640 mm, so a 150 mm riser suits a 300 mm tread.

Number of risers = floor-to-floor height ÷ riser. A 3000 mm floor with 150 mm risers needs 20 risers, so 10 per flight and a 1500 mm rise per flight. In a dog-legged stair the two flights run parallel in opposite directions with a half landing at mid-height; there is no gap between the flights, so it fits a narrow rectangle. Most syllabi ask for this type.

  • Open well stair: a gap (well) between the flights, used where a lift or more light is wanted.
  • Straight flight: one long flight between floors; simple to design, needs a long plan.
  • Stringer beam stair: steps span between two sloping beams, so the steps carry the load, not a waist slab.
  • Spiral and cantilever tread stairs: treads cantilever from a central column or a wall; only a few universities teach these in detail.

The flight as a sloping one-way slab

The waist slab has thickness t measured perpendicular to the slope. Its weight per metre of horizontal run exceeds 25 t, because the slab is longer than its horizontal span. That correction is the slope factor:

slope factor = √(1 + (R/T)²)

R is the riser in mm, T the tread in mm. For R = 150 mm and T = 300 mm it is √(1 + 0.25) = 1.118.

Loads per metre width on the horizontal run: waist slab 25 t × slope factor; steps as a triangle, 25 × R/2 (R in metres); finish × slope factor; live load as given on plan. The effective span l is the horizontal distance between landing supports: clear span + d or centre-to-centre, whichever is smaller. Then Mu = wu l²/8 and V = wu l/2.

Design steps and checks

  1. Fix riser, tread, number of risers and flight rise; compute the slope factor.
  2. Take a 1 m strip, compute wu per metre of horizontal run, and Mu = wu l²/8.
  3. d = t − cover − half the bar diameter; find Mu/(bd²) and pt; Ast = pt b d/100.
  4. Check minimum steel 0.12% of b t for Fe415 (0.15% for Fe250); spacing ≤ 3d or 300 mm.
  5. Shear: τv = V/(bd) ≤ k τc; a slab this deep needs no stirrups.
  6. Deflection: span/d ≤ 20 × modification factor (simply supported).
  7. Distribution steel 0.12% of gross area across the slope, 8 mm bars.

The landing slab is designed separately as a short-span one-way slab spanning transversely between the floor beams or walls. Main flight bars run along the slope and are anchored into the landing; distribution bars run across. Cover 20 mm.

Worked example

Question. Design the waist slab of a dog-legged staircase. Floor-to-floor height 3.0 m, riser 150 mm, tread 300 mm, so 10 risers per flight. Waist slab 150 mm thick, landing 1.2 m wide, effective span of each flight 3.3 m, live load 3.0 kN/m², floor finish 1.0 kN/m², M25 concrete, Fe415 steel, cover 20 mm.

  1. Rise per flight = 10 × 0.150 = 1.5 m. Slope factor = √(1 + (150/300)²) = 1.118.
  2. Loads per metre of horizontal run: waist slab 25 × 0.150 × 1.118 = 4.19 kN/m; steps 25 × 0.150/2 = 1.88 kN/m; finish 1.0 × 1.118 = 1.12 kN/m; live 3.00 kN/m. Total w = 10.19 kN/m.
  3. wu = 1.5 × 10.19 = 15.28 kN/m. Mu = 15.28 × 3.3²/8 = 20.80 kN·m.
  4. d = 150 − 20 − 6 = 124 mm. Mu/(bd²) = 20.80 × 10⁶/(1000 × 124²) = 1.353.
  5. pt = 0.401%. Ast = 0.401 × 1000 × 124/100 = 498 mm².
  6. 12 mm bars at 220 mm give 113.1 × 1000/220 = 514 mm² > 498 mm². Minimum 0.12% of 1000 × 150 = 180 mm², satisfied.
  7. V = 15.28 × 3.3/2 = 25.2 kN. τv = 25.2 × 10³/(1000 × 124) = 0.203 N/mm², less than τc ≈ 0.44 N/mm² for pt = 0.40 and M25; with k = 1.30 for a 150 mm slab, k τc ≈ 0.57 N/mm². Safe.
  8. Deflection: span/d = 3300/124 = 26.6 < 20 × 1.6 = 32 with the modification factor for pt ≈ 0.40 and fs ≈ 233 N/mm². Safe.
  9. Distribution steel = 0.0012 × 1000 × 150 = 180 mm². Use 8 mm at 250 mm (201 mm²).

Answer. Provide a 150 mm waist slab with 12 mm main bars at 220 mm along the slope and 8 mm distribution bars at 250 mm across, for Mu = 20.80 kN·m and Ast = 498 mm².

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the slope factor understates the waist slab weight by about 12% for a 150/300 stair.
  • Using the sloping length (about 3.7 m in the example, i.e. 3.3 × 1.118) as the effective span instead of the horizontal distance between supports. The extra length is only for bar cutting and anchorage.
  • Applying the slope factor to the live load. Live load is specified on plan area, so it is used as it is; the factor applies only to the sloping waist slab and finishes.
  • Taking the step load as 25 × R per metre instead of 25 × R/2. The steps form a triangle over the horizontal run, not a rectangle.
  • Designing the landing slab as a continuation of the flight. It is a separate short-span slab spanning transversely between the floor beams.

4.5 Flat slabs

Flat slab floors carry the floor load directly to columns, with no beams between columns. The slab is thicker or locally thickened near columns, where the load concentrates. This saves headroom, gives a clean ceiling and suits offices and car parks. Design of a regular panel uses the direct design method: one total static moment for the panel, split into negative and positive parts, then shared between column and middle strips. Punching shear around the column is the special check.

You need: Chapter 4.2 and flexure from Unit 2.

The slab goes straight to the columns

A flat slab is a reinforced concrete slab supported directly on columns, with no beams between the columns. The floor load travels from the slab to the columns and then to the footings. A flat soffit gives more clear height, simpler formwork and free passage for services. Office floors, shopping areas and car parks use this form when the plan is regular.

Near a column the load is concentrated. Two local thickenings help. A capital is a widened top of the column. A drop is a thickened part of the slab around the column. Both spread the load into the slab and reduce the shear stress at the column. A slab without either is called a flat plate; it is used for light loads.

The special danger is punching shear. The column can punch a plug out of the slab. The slab is checked for shear on a critical perimeter close to the column face, usually at half the effective depth from the face. If the check fails, the slab needs a capital, a drop or shear reinforcement. Punching reinforcement design is outside this chapter; column and footing design is in Unit 5.

Direct design method for one panel

For a regular panel of equal spans, the direct design method finds one total static moment for the whole panel in one direction. Use the factored load. Use the clear span between column faces, not the centre-to-centre span. The same calculation is repeated in the other direction with the spans swapped.

M₀ = w l₂ lₙ² / 8

M₀ is the total static moment in kN·m for one panel and one direction; w is the factored load in kN/m²; l₂ is the panel width transverse to the direction of bending in m; lₙ is the clear span between column faces in m.

The total moment is split by the code proportions. At the support, 65 percent is negative moment. At midspan, 35 percent is positive moment. The negative moment is shared as 75 percent to the column strip and 25 percent to the middle strip. The positive moment is shared as 60 percent to the column strip and 40 percent to the middle strip.

Moment at sectionColumn strip shareMiddle strip share
Negative at support75%25%
Positive at midspan60%40%

The column strip is the band along the column line. Its total width is half the panel width, l₂/2, divided equally on the two sides of the line. The middle strip is the remaining half. To get a design moment per metre, divide the strip moment by the strip width in metres. For a 6.0 m panel, the column strip is 3.0 m wide and the middle strip is 3.0 m wide.

Worked example

Question. A flat slab floor has panels 6.0 m × 6.0 m centre to centre on 400 mm square columns. The slab is 180 mm thick. Live load is 4.0 kN/m² and floor finish is 1.0 kN/m². Use M25 concrete and Fe415 steel. By the direct design method, find M₀, the negative and positive moments, and the design moment per metre in the column strip and middle strip.

  1. Clear span lₙ = 6.0 − 0.4 = 5.6 m. Panel width l₂ = 6.0 m.
  2. Slab self weight = 0.180 × 25 = 4.5 kN/m². Total dead load = 4.5 + 1.0 = 5.5 kN/m². Service load = 5.5 + 4.0 = 9.5 kN/m².
  3. Factored load w = 1.5 × 9.5 = 14.25 kN/m².
  4. M₀ = w l₂ lₙ² / 8 = 14.25 × 6.0 × 5.6² / 8 = 335.2 kN·m ≈ 335 kN·m.
  5. Negative moment = 0.65 × 335.2 = 217.9 kN·m ≈ 218 kN·m. Positive moment = 0.35 × 335.2 = 117.3 kN·m ≈ 117 kN·m.
  6. Column strip width = l₂/2 = 3.0 m. Middle strip width = 3.0 m.
  7. Negative moment in column strip per metre = 0.75 × 217.9 / 3.0 = 54.5 kN·m/m. Negative moment in middle strip per metre = 0.25 × 217.9 / 3.0 = 18.2 kN·m/m.
  8. Positive moment in column strip per metre = 0.60 × 117.3 / 3.0 = 23.5 kN·m/m. Positive moment in middle strip per metre = 0.40 × 117.3 / 3.0 = 15.6 kN·m/m.

Answer. M₀ = 335 kN·m; negative = 218 kN·m and positive = 117 kN·m; column strip moments are 54.5 and 23.5 kN·m/m, middle strip moments are 18.2 and 15.6 kN·m/m (negative and positive respectively).

Exam traps

  • Using the centre-to-centre span in place of the clear span lₙ makes M₀ too high. lₙ runs between column faces.
  • Reporting a whole-panel moment as a per-metre moment: divide the strip moment by the strip width in metres.
  • Swapping the 65/35 split or the 75/25 and 60/40 strip shares reverses the design.
  • The column strip is not the full panel: its total width is about half the panel width, l₂/2, split equally on the two sides of the column line.
  • Punching shear is checked near the column face, not at midspan; a slab safe in flexure can still fail by punching.

4.6 Yield line theory

Yield line theory finds the load at which a reinforced concrete slab collapses by bending. It assumes straight hinge lines divide the slab into flat plates that rotate. Each line carries the plastic moment Mp. Engineers use it for irregular slabs, flat slabs and staircases. Only a few universities teach it.

You need moment of resistance from Unit 2 and two-way slab behaviour from Chapter 4.2.

Yield line and plastic moment

A yield line is a line along which the slab has yielded in bending and can rotate at constant moment. It is a plastic hinge spread along a line. The line forms where the elastic moment first reaches Mp and then spreads. The moment per metre width is Mp, the plastic moment of resistance, in kNm/m. Mp is the full plastic capacity after yielding, not the elastic moment. For a singly reinforced section, Mp = 0.87 fy Ast (d − 0.42 xu). fy is steel yield strength in N/mm², Ast is steel area per metre width in mm²/m, d is effective depth in mm, and xu is neutral axis depth in mm. Also xu = 0.87 fy Ast / (0.36 fck b), where fck is concrete strength in N/mm² and b = 1000 mm for a one-metre strip. The analysis assumes rigid-perfectly plastic plates and small virtual displacements δ, an imagined deflection used only to compare work.

Virtual work on a square slab

Virtual work has two sides. External work is wu times the volume swept by the plates: for area A and centroid deflection δc, it is wu A δc, where wu is the uniform load in kN/m². Internal work is Σ Mp × length × θ, where θ is the relative rotation across a yield line in radians. Use the angle between the two plates meeting at the line, not the rotation of one plate about its support.

For a square slab of side L, simply supported on four edges, assume yield lines from the centre to the four corners. Four triangular plates form. Let the centre move down by δ. Each triangle has area L²/4 and centroid deflection δ/3, so external work = 4 × wu × (L²/4) × (δ/3) = wu L² δ/3. Each diagonal line has length L/√2. The relative rotation across a diagonal is θ = 2√2 δ/L. Internal work = 4 × Mp × (L/√2) × (2√2 δ/L) = 8 Mp δ. Equating gives wu L² δ/3 = 8 Mp δ, so wu = 24 Mp / L².

Upper bound and admissible patterns

Yield line theory is an upper bound method. Any assumed pattern that is geometrically possible gives a collapse load at or above the true load. The true collapse load is the lowest value from all admissible patterns. A pattern that is not the lowest gives a load higher than the true collapse load, so the design is unsafe. Only the lowest wu from all admissible patterns is acceptable; a missed pattern can overestimate strength and lead to under-design. For a rectangular slab, try lines that divide it into plates. For a cantilever slab, the yield line runs parallel to the free edge, at the fixed edge. In general, a free edge carries no moment, so the yield line pattern must run from the free edge back to the supported edges or to other yield lines. A slab with a hole needs extra lines around the hole. Check that every plate can rotate without stretching. The method gives only the collapse load, so serviceability checks are separate, as in Chapter 4.1.

Worked example

Question. A square slab 4.0 m × 4.0 m is simply supported on all four edges and reinforced with 10 mm bars at 150 mm centres both ways at an effective depth of 100 mm. M25 concrete, Fe415 steel. Find Mp per metre width and use yield line theory to find the uniform collapse load wu.

  1. Bar area = π × 10² / 4 = 78.54 mm². Steel per metre width = 78.54 × 1000 / 150 = 523.6 mm²/m.
  2. fck = 25 N/mm², fy = 415 N/mm², b = 1000 mm, d = 100 mm.
  3. Neutral axis: xu = 0.87 × 415 × 523.6 / (0.36 × 25 × 1000) = 21.01 mm. The limit is xu,max/d = 0.53 for Fe250, 0.48 for Fe415 and 0.46 for Fe500 (IS 456); here Fe415 gives xu,max = 0.48 × 100 = 48 mm, so the section is under-reinforced.
  4. Lever arm = d − 0.42 xu = 100 − 0.42 × 21.01 = 91.18 mm.
  5. Mp = 0.87 × 415 × 523.6 × 91.18 = 17.24 × 10⁶ Nmm/m = 17.24 kNm/m.
  6. wu = 24 Mp / L² = 24 × 17.24 / 4.0² = 25.86 kN/m².

Answer. Mp = 17.24 kNm/m and the uniform collapse load wu = 25.86 kN/m².

Exam traps

  • Using support rotation θ = 2δ/L instead of relative rotation θ = 2√2 δ/L reduces the internal work, and hence wu, by a factor of 1/√2 (to about 71% of the correct value).
  • The lowest wu is correct: the method is an upper bound.
  • Mp is per metre width; do not multiply by the full slab width unless the yield line length requires it.
  • Keep Mp in kNm/m and L in m; using mm gives a factor 1000 error.
  • A yield line is a collapse mechanism, not a serviceability crack; serviceability is checked separately.

Takeaways

  1. One-way slabs are designed as 1 m wide beams in the short span; two-way slabs need coefficients in both directions when long span is less than twice short span.

Glossary

Aspect ratio
Ratio of long span to short span of a slab panel.
Cantilever
Member fixed at one end and free at the other.
Clear span
Distance between inner faces of supports.
Collapse load
Load at which a slab mechanism forms.
Column strip
Flat-slab strip beside the column centreline that carries a major share of moment.
Continuous slab
Slab running over three or more supports, with continuity reducing positive moments.
Dog-legged staircase
Stair with two flights and a half-space landing between floors.
Edge condition
Support condition that decides whether an edge is continuous or discontinuous for coefficient selection.
Effective depth
Distance from extreme compression fibre to centroid of tension reinforcement.
Effective span
Design span used in calculations, based on clear span and support conditions.
Flat slab
Slab supported directly by columns without beams.
Going
Horizontal distance between successive risers.
Hogging moment
Bending moment that causes tension at the top of a slab or beam.
Landing
Level platform between flights or at floor level.
Middle strip
Flat-slab strip between column strips.
One-way slab
Slab that bends only in one direction and is designed as a 1 m wide beam.
Pattern loading
Arrangement of live load on selected spans to produce critical design moments.
Riser
Vertical height of one step.
Sagging moment
Bending moment that causes tension at the bottom of a slab or beam.
Slab
Flat plate member carrying transverse load mainly by bending.
Tread
Horizontal top surface of one step.
Two-way slab
Slab that bends in two directions because long span is less than twice short span.
Upper bound
Collapse load from an assumed mechanism that is not lower than the true collapse load.
Virtual work
Energy balance between external load work and internal work at yield lines.
Waist slab
Inclined slab forming the structural spine of a staircase flight.
Yield line
Line of plastic hinging in a slab at collapse.
