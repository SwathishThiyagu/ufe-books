About this unit

Slabs are most of the concrete in a building, so their design decides much of its steel and cost. The unit starts with the one-way slab, a 1 m strip designed as a beam, and moves to two-way panels whose moments come from IS 456 coefficient tables. Cantilever and continuous slabs add hogging moments and code moment coefficients, and the staircase flight is a one-way slab on a slope. Flat slabs drop the beams and carry the slab straight on columns, and yield line theory closes the unit with the collapse load of any slab shape.

4.1 One-way slabs

A one-way slab bends in one direction only, across its short span. It happens when the slab rests on two opposite edges, or on four edges with the long span ly more than twice the short span lx. The design takes a strip 1 m wide across the short span and designs it as a beam 1000 mm wide. Floors of corridors, verandahs and long narrow rooms are one-way slabs.

You need the limit state design of singly reinforced beams (the stress block, xu,max, the Ast formula) and the IS 456 shear strength τc.

When a slab is one-way

Think of a slab on four edges as two sets of crossing strips that must deflect the same amount where they cross. A simply supported strip deflects in proportion to l⁴, so the shorter strip is much stiffer and takes most of the load. With ly = 2lx the short strip is 2⁴ = 16 times stiffer and carries 16/17 ≈ 94 % of the load.

IS 456 therefore designs a slab with ly/lx > 2 as one-way: only the short-span strips are designed for bending, and the long direction gets the minimum steel. A slab on two opposite supports is one-way whatever the ratio. When ly/lx ≤ 2 the slab is two-way; see Two-way slabs with various edge conditions.

A load of w kN/m² on the slab is a line load of w kN/m on a 1 m strip, the same number. Then b = 1000 mm and the beam depth is the slab thickness D. The effective depth is d = D − nominal cover − φ/2, where φ is the main bar diameter.

The IS 456 rules a slab design uses

  • Effective span (cl. 22.2): the smaller of clear span + d and the centre-to-centre distance of the supports.
  • Deflection (cl. 23.2): span/effective depth must not exceed the basic value (20 simply supported, 26 continuous, 7 cantilever) times the Fig. 4 modification factor. The factor depends on the steel percentage pt and the service stress fs = 0.58 fy × (Ast required/Ast provided); near pt = 0.3 % it is about 1.5, so start with l/d ≈ 28.
  • Minimum steel (cl. 26.5.2.1): 0.12 % of bD for Fe415 bars, 0.15 % for mild steel, for main and distribution steel. Bar diameter at most D/8.
  • Maximum spacing (cl. 26.3.3): main bars the smaller of 3d and 300 mm; distribution bars the smaller of 5d and 450 mm.
  • Nominal cover (Table 16): 20 mm for mild exposure (15 mm allowed for bars of 12 mm or less), 30 mm for moderate.
  • Shear (cl. 40.2.1.1): no stirrups are needed if τv ≤ kτc, with τc from Table 19 and the depth factor k = 1.30 for D ≤ 150 mm, falling to 1.00 at 300 mm.
Mu = wu l² / 8,   Vu = wu l / 2

wu is the factored load, 1.5 × (dead + live), in kN/m on the strip; l is the effective span in m.

Ast = (0.5 fck / fy) [1 − √(1 − 4.6 Mu /(fck b d²))] b d

This is the Annex G equation Mu = 0.87 fy Ast d (1 − Ast fy/(b d fck)) solved for Ast, valid while Mu ≤ Mu,lim = 0.138 fck b d² (Fe415). The flexure tables of SP 16 give the same pt from Mu/bd².

Distribution steel runs along the long span, on top of the main bars, which stay in the outer layer to keep the larger d. It holds the main bars during concreting, controls shrinkage and temperature cracks, and spreads a point load to several main bars. If the deflection check fails, increase D and repeat the design.

Worked example

Question. Design a simply supported one-way slab for a room 3.0 m × 7.5 m (clear) with 230 mm walls on all sides. Live load 3 kN/m², floor finish 1 kN/m², M20, Fe415, mild exposure (20 mm cover, 10 mm bars).

  1. Type: ly/lx = 7.5/3.0 = 2.5 > 2, so the slab spans one way, across 3.0 m.
  2. Trial depth: l ≈ 3.1 m, d ≈ 3100/28 = 111 mm, D ≈ 111 + 25 = 136 mm. Adopt D = 140 mm, d = 140 − 20 − 5 = 115 mm.
  3. Effective span: 3.0 + 0.115 = 3.115 m, or centre to centre 3.230 m. Take l = 3.115 m.
  4. Loads: self weight 0.140 × 25 = 3.50, finish 1.00, live 3.00; w = 7.50 kN/m², wu = 1.5 × 7.50 = 11.250 kN/m².
  5. Mu = 11.250 × 3.115²/8 = 13.65 kN·m/m; Vu = 11.250 × 3.115/2 = 17.52 kN/m. Mu,lim = 0.138 × 20 × 1000 × 115² = 36.50 kN·m, larger, so under-reinforced.
  6. Steel: 4.6 Mu/(fck b d²) = 4.6 × 13.65 × 10⁶/(20 × 1000 × 115²) = 0.2373; Ast = (0.5 × 20/415)(1 − √(1 − 0.2373)) × 1000 × 115 = 351 mm²/m, above the minimum 0.12 % × 1000 × 140 = 168 mm²/m.
  7. 10 mm bars (78.54 mm²): 1000 × 78.54/351 = 224 mm. Provide 10 mm @ 220 mm c/c = 357 mm²/m (220 < 3d and 300 mm).
  8. Distribution: 168 mm²/m with 8 mm bars: 1000 × 50.27/168 = 299 mm. Provide 8 mm @ 290 mm c/c (173 mm²/m).
  9. Shear: τv = 17.52 × 10³/(1000 × 115) = 0.152 N/mm². pt = 100 × 357/(1000 × 115) = 0.31 %, τc = 0.389 N/mm² (Table 19), kτc = 1.30 × 0.389 = 0.506 N/mm² > τv. Safe.
  10. Deflection: fs = 0.58 × 415 × 351/357 = 237 N/mm²; with pt = 0.31 % the factor is about 1.49. Allowed l/d = 20 × 1.49 = 29.8; actual 3115/115 = 27.1. Safe. (A 130 mm slab, d = 105 mm, fails: l/d = 29.6 against about 27.8.)

Answer. D = 140 mm; main steel 10 mm @ 220 mm c/c (357 mm²/m) across the 3.0 m span at the bottom; distribution steel 8 mm @ 290 mm c/c along the long span. Mu = 13.65 kN·m/m; shear and deflection pass.

Exam traps

  • Using the clear span or the wall centre lines without comparing: the effective span is the smaller of the two rules, here 3.115 m, not 3.0 m (unsafe) or 3.23 m.
  • Writing the strip load in kN: 11.25 kN/m² on a 1 m strip is 11.25 kN/m, and b = 1000 mm, not the room width.
  • Checking deflection with the basic ratio 20 alone, or with the steel first assumed. The limit is 20 × the factor for the steel actually provided, here 20 × 1.49 = 29.8.
  • Spacing distribution bars at 3d or placing them below the main bars. Their limit is the smaller of 5d and 450 mm, and they go on top.

4.2 Two-way slabs with various edge conditions

A two-way slab rests on all four edges and has ly/lx ≤ 2, so it bends in both directions and sends load to all four supports. IS 456 gives its design moments as coefficients times wu lx²: Table 26 for panels whose corners are held down (cast with beams or continuous slabs), Table 27 for simply supported panels whose corners can lift. The edge condition, continuous or discontinuous, picks the row. Most room floors in RC framed buildings are two-way slabs.

You need One-way slabs (effective span, minimum steel, the Ast formula).

How a two-way panel shares its load

Let lx be the short effective span and ly the long one. Take the two central strips crossing at mid-panel. If both deflect the same, wx lx⁴ = wy ly⁴ with wx + wy = w, so the short span takes wx = w ly⁴/(lx⁴ + ly⁴). This is the Rankine–Grashoff split. It ignores the twisting stiffness of the slab, so IS 456 uses coefficients that allow for it, for continuity, and for corners that are held down.

A continuous edge is one where the slab carries on over the beam into the next panel; it has a hogging (negative) moment and needs top steel. A discontinuous edge is the outer edge of the floor or an edge on a wall; the slab is treated as simply supported there. Table 26 lists nine cases: interior panel; one short edge discontinuous; one long edge discontinuous; two adjacent edges discontinuous; two short edges discontinuous; two long edges discontinuous; three edges discontinuous with one long edge continuous; three edges discontinuous with one short edge continuous; four edges discontinuous.

  • Each direction is split into a middle strip (3/4 of the width) and two edge strips (1/8 each). The table moments apply to the middle strip; edge strips get minimum steel.
  • Short-span bars go in the outer layer, so dy = dx − φ. Mid-span bars run to within 0.25l of a continuous edge and 0.15l of a discontinuous edge; top bars over a continuous edge run 0.15l into the span, half of them 0.3l.
  • At a discontinuous edge, top steel of half the mid-span steel, extending 0.1l, takes care of the partial fixity.
  • Torsion steel at a corner where both edges are discontinuous: four layers (top and bottom, both ways), each 3/4 of the steel required for the largest mid-span moment, over lx/5 from the corner. Half of it where only one edge is continuous; none where both are continuous. Table 27 panels have no torsion steel: their corners are free to lift.
  • Deflection (cl. 24.1): for lx ≤ 3.5 m and live load ≤ 3 kN/m², lx/D ≤ 35 (simply supported) or 40 (continuous), times 0.8 for Fe415: 28 and 32. Otherwise check by cl. 23.2 as for one-way slabs.

Exam traps

  • Writing My = αy w ly². IS 456 uses lx² in both directions; with ly² the long-span moments of the worked example come out 65 % too large.
  • Reading the column for ly/lx but taking αy from it too: αy is a single value per case, in the last column.
  • Swapping the layers. Short-span bars go outermost (larger d); dy = dx − φ.
  • Giving torsion steel to every corner. Full steel only where both edges are discontinuous, half where one is continuous, none where both are continuous, and none in Table 27 panels.
  • Using clear spans for ly/lx. Use effective spans; the ratio picks the column.

4.3 Cantilever and continuous slabs

A cantilever slab is fixed along one edge and free at the other: a balcony, a chajja over a window, a canopy. Its moment is hogging at the support, so the main steel is at the top and must be anchored well behind the support. A continuous slab runs over three or more supports; IS 456 gives its design moments and shears as coefficients (Tables 12 and 13) that already allow for live load on some spans and not others. Both are designed as 1 m strips, like a one-way slab.

You need One-way slabs (effective span, Ast, minimum steel, deflection factor).

Cantilever slabs

Under load a cantilever bends down and its top fibre stretches, so the tension steel sits near the top face. The moment is largest at the face of the support: for a uniform factored load wu over the clear projection l and a line load Pu at the free edge (a railing or parapet),

Mu = wu l² / 2 + Pu l

Mu in kN·m per metre at the face of the support; l is the clear projection in m; Pu in kN/m.

  • Effective span for deflection (cl. 22.2 c): the length to the face of the support plus d/2. The basic span/d is 7, times the Fig. 4 factor. Cantilevers are usually decided by this check.
  • Shear is checked at a distance d from the face: Vu = wu(l − d) + Pu.
  • The top bars run back into the slab or beam behind the support by at least the development length Ld = φσs/(4τbd), with τbd = 1.2 × 1.6 = 1.92 N/mm² for deformed bars in M20: Ld = 47φ for Fe415.
  • The back span or the supporting beam must hold the cantilever down: a balcony on a beam twists that beam, so the beam needs torsion design.
  • Distribution steel (minimum steel) runs along the support, tied under the top main bars.

Continuous slabs and the moment coefficients

In a slab over many beams the worst moment at each section comes from a different live-load pattern: the largest sagging moment in a span when that span and alternate spans carry live load; the largest hogging moment at a support when the two spans next to it are loaded, with the rest following alternately. Dead load is always everywhere. For uniform loads cl. 22.5 lets the designer skip this and use coefficients, if there are at least three spans that differ by no more than 15 % of the longest.

Exam traps

  • Putting cantilever main steel at the bottom. The tension is at the top; bottom bars there do nothing and the slab cracks at the support.
  • Stopping the top bars at the face of the support. They need Ld = 470 mm (47φ) beyond the face, or the bar pulls out before it yields.
  • Leaving out the railing or parapet load at the tip: it has the full lever arm l.
  • Using the coefficients for two spans, unequal spans (more than 15 % apart) or heavy point loads. Analyse those with pattern loading.
  • Using the same coefficient for dead and live load. Live load gets the larger value (1/10 against 1/12 at the end span) because it may be on some spans only.

4.4 Staircases: dog-legged and other types

A staircase carries people from floor to floor, and each sloping flight is a slab that spans along the slope or across it. In the dog-legged stair, the commonest in Indian houses and offices, two flights run side by side in opposite directions with a half landing between them. Its flight is designed as an inclined one-way slab, the waist slab, after the sloping load is converted to load per metre of horizontal run. Only a few syllabi list staircases, but where they do, a full dog-legged design is a standard long question.

You need One-way slabs; the landing that spans across the stair is designed the same way.

Parts and types of staircases

The riser R is the height of one step and the tread T its horizontal width; the going is the horizontal length of a flight (number of treads × T). A comfortable step keeps 2R + T between about 550 and 700 mm; 160 mm and 300 mm give 620 mm. The waist is the thickness of the sloping slab measured at right angles to its soffit (cl. 33.3).

  • By plan shape: straight flight; quarter-turn; dog-legged (two parallel flights, no gap between them, half landing); open well (a gap or well between the flights, often round a lift); bifurcated; spiral and helical.
  • Spanning across (transversely): each step is a small cantilever from a wall or a central spine beam, or spans between two side walls or two stringer beams. The steps carry the load; the waist is thin.
  • Spanning along the slope (longitudinally): the waist slab with its steps spans between landings or beams at the top and bottom of the flight. The dog-legged stair is usually designed this way.
  • Tread-riser (folded plate) stairs have no waist; each tread and riser act together as a folded slab.

Effective span and loads (cl. 33)

  • Supported at the top and bottom by beams parallel to the risers: span = centre-to-centre of those beams.
  • Spanning onto the edge of a landing slab that itself spans parallel to the risers: span = going + at each end half the landing width or 1 m, whichever is smaller.
  • Landing slab spanning in the same direction as the flight: the two act as one slab between the supporting beams or walls, the going measured horizontally.
  • Where flights or landings cross at right angles (open wells), a load on the common area may be taken half in each direction.

slope factor = √(R² + T²) / T

The waist of thickness t weighs 25t × √(R² + T²)/T kN per m² of plan, because the sloping slab is longer than its plan. The steps add the triangle 25 × R/2 (R in m) per m² of plan. Live load and floor finish are taken on plan.

The flight is then a simply supported one-way slab on its horizontal effective span: Mu = wu l²/8, steel along the slope in the bottom of the waist, distribution bars across, and the usual shear and deflection checks. Where the soffit has a re-entrant corner, at the top of a flight that meets a landing, a bottom bar bent round that corner would pull out through the cover when it takes tension. So the flight bars run straight on into the top of the landing and the landing bars run straight on into the flight, crossing, each with its development length.

Worked example

Question. Design the flight of a dog-legged staircase: floor-to-floor height 3.2 m, riser 160 mm, tread 300 mm, landings 1.25 m wide at both ends spanning across the stair hall onto walls. Live load 3 kN/m², finish 1 kN/m², M20, Fe415, 20 mm cover, 12 mm bars.

  1. Steps: 3200/160 = 20 risers, 10 per flight; 9 treads, so the going is 9 × 0.300 = 2.7 m. 2R + T = 620 mm, comfortable.
  2. Effective span (landings span parallel to the risers): 2.7 + 2 × smaller of (1.25/2, 1 m) = 2.7 + 2 × 0.625 = 3.95 m.
  3. Waist: trial t = 180 mm, d = 180 − 20 − 6 = 154 mm. Slope factor = √(160² + 300²)/300 = 1.1333.
  4. Loads per m² of plan: waist 25 × 0.180 × 1.1333 = 5.100; steps 25 × 0.160/2 = 2.00; finish 1.00; live 3.00; w = 11.100 kN/m², wu = 16.650 kN/m². (Taking the flight load over the landing ends, which weigh only 8.5 kN/m², is on the safe side.)
  5. Mu = 16.650 × 3.95²/8 = 32.47 kN·m/m; Vu = 16.650 × 3.95/2 = 32.88 kN/m. Mu,lim = 0.138 × 20 × 1000 × 154² = 65.5 kN·m, so under-reinforced.
  6. Steel: 4.6 Mu/(fck b d²) = 0.3149; Ast = 639 mm²/m. 12 mm bars (113.1 mm²): 1000 × 113.1/639 = 177 mm; provide 12 mm @ 170 mm c/c (665 mm²/m).

  • Forgetting the slope factor: the waist load would be 25 × 0.180 = 4.50 instead of 5.10 kN/m², about 13 % low.
  • Using the sloping length as the span. The span is horizontal: here 3.95 m, not the sloping length of the going.
  • Applying the slope factor to the live load. Live load is given on plan and is used as it is.
  • Taking the steps as 25R per m² instead of the triangle 25R/2.
  • Bending the flight bottom bars round the re-entrant corner of the soffit at the upper landing; under tension they straighten and spall the cover. Run them straight on and cross them with the landing bars.

4.5 Flat slabs

A flat slab is a slab carried straight on columns, with no beams. The floor load goes from the slab into the columns, so the slab is often thickened round each column with a drop, and the column top may be widened into a capital. Flat slabs give a flat ceiling, a lower storey height and simple formwork, which suits offices, malls, hospitals and car parks. IS 456 clause 31 designs a regular layout by the direct design method and checks punching shear round every column.

You need Two-way slabs with various edge conditions (two-way bending) and the shear strength of slabs.

Parts and proportions (cl. 31.1, 31.2)

  • Flat plate: a slab of uniform thickness on plain columns, for light loads and short spans.
  • Drop: a thickening of the slab round the column, rectangular in plan and at least one-third of the panel length in each direction. It adds depth where the hogging moment and shear are largest.
  • Column head (capital): a flared top of the column. Only the part inside a 90° cone or pyramid fitting within the column and head counts in design.
  • Column strip: the band along a column line, 0.25l₂ wide on each side of the line but not more than 0.25l₁ (l₁ is the span being designed, l₂ the span across it). The middle strip is the rest of the panel width.
  • Thickness: at least 125 mm. The span/d rules of cl. 23.2 apply directly to slabs with drops; without drops they are multiplied by 0.9. Use the longer span.

Direct design method (cl. 31.4, 31.5)

The method may be used when there are at least three continuous spans each way, panels are rectangular with long/short span ≤ 2, successive spans differ by no more than one-third of the longer, columns are offset by at most 10 % of the span, and the live load is not more than three times the dead load. Then each direction is designed for the total static moment of one panel:

M₀ = W lₙ / 8

W = wu l₂ lₙ is the total design load on the panel width l₂ over the clear span lₙ (kN). lₙ runs between the faces of the columns or capitals, but is not taken less than 0.65l₁. M₀ is in kN·m for the whole panel width.

End spans use other fractions that depend on how stiff the outer column is, and the column strip takes all of the negative moment at an exterior support. Divide each strip moment by the strip width to get a moment per metre, then design the steel as for a one-way slab, with the depth of the drop at the column. Layouts that fail the conditions are designed by the equivalent frame method (cl. 31.5).

Punching shear (cl. 31.6)

A column can punch through the slab along a truncated cone or pyramid. The critical section is a perimeter at d/2 from the face of the column, capital or drop, and the shear on it is the load on the panel outside that perimeter. The stress must not exceed ks τc, where τc = 0.25√fck and ks = 0.5 + βc ≤ 1 (βc = short side/long side of the column). Above that, up to 1.5 times it, shear reinforcement is needed; beyond that the slab must be made deeper or given a drop or capital. When a drop is used, check a second perimeter at d/2 outside the drop, where the slab is thinner.

Worked example

4.6 Yield line theory

Yield line theory finds the load at which a reinforced concrete slab collapses. At collapse the under-reinforced slab cracks and yields along straight lines, the yield lines, which cut it into flat pieces that rotate like a mechanism. Equating the work done by the load to the work absorbed in the yield lines gives the collapse load. It suits slabs of any shape and support, where tables do not exist. Only a few syllabi teach it.

You need the moment of resistance of an under-reinforced section and Two-way slabs with various edge conditions.

Yield lines and the moment m

Along a yield line the steel has yielded and the slab rotates at a constant moment m per metre of line, the moment of resistance of the section (kN·m/m). For an under-reinforced section m = 0.87 fy Ast (d − 0.42xu), with xu = 0.87 fy Ast/(0.36 fck b). If the steel per metre is the same both ways (isotropic slab), m is the same for a line in any direction.

  • Yield lines are straight and end at a slab boundary or meet other yield lines.
  • A supported edge is an axis of rotation for the piece next to it; a column is a point the axis passes through.
  • A yield line between two pieces passes through the point where their axes of rotation meet.
  • Sagging yield lines (positive) form in the span, hogging ones (negative) along continuous or fixed edges.

The work equation and the upper bound

Give the mechanism a small deflection δ at its deepest point. External work = Σ w × (area of each piece) × (deflection of its centroid). Internal work = Σ m × (projected length of the yield lines of a piece on its axis) × (its rotation θ). Setting them equal gives w. For a square slab of side L simply supported on all edges, the yield lines are the two diagonals: each of the four triangles has area L²/4, centroid deflection δ/3, rotation θ = δ/(L/2) and projected length L. So w L² δ/3 = 4 × m × L × 2δ/L = 8mδ, and wu = 24m/L².

Every possible mechanism gives a load at or above the true collapse load: the method is an upper bound. A wrong pattern overestimates the strength and is unsafe, so try the possible patterns (or vary the pattern dimension) and take the smallest load. Elastic coefficient methods such as Table 26 are lower bound methods and err on the safe side.

Worked example

Question. A 4 m × 4 m slab, simply supported on all four edges, has 10 mm bars @ 150 mm both ways at an average d = 100 mm. M20, Fe415. Find m and the collapse load. Then find the collapse load of a 4 m × 6 m slab with the same steel.

  1. Ast = 1000 × 78.54/150 = 523.6 mm²/m; xu = 0.87 × 415 × 523.6/(0.36 × 20 × 1000) = 26.26 mm (under xu,max = 48 mm).
  2. m = 0.87 × 415 × 523.6 × (100 − 0.42 × 26.26) = 16.82 kN·m/m.
  3. Square slab: wu = 24 × 16.82/4² = 25.23 kN/m².
  4. Rectangle: the pattern has a ridge along the middle of the long direction and corner lines meeting it at a distance b from each short edge. Trying b (m): 1.00 → 22.08; 1.50 → 19.06; 2.00 → 18.02; 2.38 → 17.84; 3.00 → 18.22 kN/m².
  5. The smallest is at b ≈ 2.38 m: wu = 17.84 kN/m². The closed form 24m/(lx²(√(3 + a²) − a)²) with a = lx/ly = 0.667 gives the same, 17.84 kN/m².

Answer. m = 16.82 kN·m/m; collapse loads 25.23 kN/m² (square) and 17.84 kN/m² (4 m × 6 m). A guess of b = 1.0 m would have claimed 22.08 kN/m², 24 % too high.

Exam traps

  • Taking the largest load from several patterns. The method is an upper bound: the smallest load governs.
  • Using the rotation of only one piece across a diagonal yield line and the true line length. Either use relative rotation 2√2δ/L with length L/√2, or projected length L with each piece's own rotation 2δ/L: both give 8mδ.
  • Mixing units: m in kN·m/m, spans in m, w in kN/m².
  • Treating the collapse load as a service load. It is the factored (ultimate) load; deflection and cracking are checked separately.

Remember and understand

Understand Explain how a slab supported on four edges shares its load between the two directions. State the IS 456 rules that separate one-way and two-way slabs, and explain how edge conditions, corners and strips enter the design of a two-way slab. 13 marks

Points to cover. Crossing strips with equal deflection; stiffness ∝ 1/l⁴; Rankine–Grashoff formula with a numerical case; One-way if ly/lx > 2 or supported on two edges; Table 26 for restrained panels (nine cases), Table 27 for simply supported; M = α w lx² in both directions; Middle and edge strips, torsion steel, layer order.

Model answer.

  1. Load sharing. Think of the slab as two families of strips, one across each span, crossing at right angles. At each crossing point both strips must deflect the same amount. A simply supported strip under uniform load deflects 5wl⁴/(384EI), so for the same deflection the load each strip takes is inversely proportional to l⁴: the shorter strip is much stiffer and takes most of the load.
  2. Rankine–Grashoff. With wx on the short span and wy on the long span:
    wx lx⁴ = wy ly⁴,   wx + wy = w
wx = w ly⁴/(lx⁴ + ly⁴),   wy = w lx⁴/(lx⁴ + ly⁴)
  3. For lx = 4 m and ly = 5 m: wx = 625/(256 + 625) w = 0.709w and wy = 0.291w. For ly = 2lx: wx = 16/17 = 0.941w. So once the long span is twice the short one, the long direction carries only about 6 % of the load.
  4. IS 456 rules. A slab supported on two opposite edges, or on four edges with ly/lx > 2, is designed as one-way: 1 m strips across the short span, with only distribution (minimum) steel along the long span. With ly/lx ≤ 2 it is two-way and is designed by Annex D. Spans are effective spans (cl. 22.2).
  5. Coefficients. The simple split ignores the twisting stiffness of the slab and continuity. IS 456 therefore gives moment coefficients, Mx = αx w lx² and My = αy w lx² (both with lx²). Table 27 applies to simply supported panels whose corners can lift; its values equal the Rankine–Grashoff moments. Table 26 applies to panels whose corners are held down, in nine cases from interior panel to four edges discontinuous; each case has a negative coefficient for the continuous edges and a positive one for mid-span. αx varies with ly/lx; αy is one value per case.
  6. Edge conditions. A continuous edge, where the slab carries on into the next panel, develops a hogging moment and needs top steel; the more edges are continuous, the smaller the mid-span moments. A discontinuous edge is treated as simply supported, with nominal top steel (half the mid-span steel over 0.1l) for partial fixity.
  7. Strips and corners. Each direction is divided into a middle strip (3/4 of the width), which takes the table moments, and two edge strips (1/8 each) with minimum steel. Short-span bars go in the outer layer, so dy = dx − φ. Column strips carry top bars over the columns (12 mm @ 140) extending into the span by at least 0.3lₙ, and bottom bars (10 mm @ 240) through the span; middle strips carry lighter top and bottom mesh for 14.3 and 12.3 kN·m/m, not less than the minimum 0.12 % × 1000 × 200 = 240 mm²/m. At least two bottom bars of the column strip in each direction should pass through the column core so that the slab hangs on the column if punching ever starts.
  10. Answer. M₀ = 241.4 kN·m; column strip 42.8 and 18.4 kN·m/m, middle strip 14.3 and 12.3 kN·m/m (negative, positive); punching 1.08 < 1.12 N/mm².

Analyze and evaluate

Evaluate A 4.0 m square slab, simply supported on four walls with corners free to lift, carries wu = 12 kN/m². Design it by IS 456 Table 27 (d = 100 mm both ways, M20, Fe415), then find its collapse load by yield line theory and comment on the margin. 13 marks

Points to cover. Table 27 coefficient at ly/lx = 1; Design moment and steel; Moment of resistance m of the steel provided; Yield line pattern and work equation; Collapse load and ratio to wu; Lower bound and upper bound methods.

Model answer.

  1. Design moment. Table 27 at ly/lx = 1: αx = αy = 0.062. M = 0.062 × 12 × 4.0² = 11.90 kN·m/m in each direction.
  2. Steel. Ast = (0.5 × 20/415)(1 − √(1 − 4.6 × 11.90 × 10⁶/(20 × 1000 × 100²))) × 1000 × 100 = 356 mm²/m. Provide 10 mm @ 220 mm c/c both ways (357 mm²/m). (For simplicity the same d is used both ways; in practice the upper layer has d − φ.)
  3. Moment of resistance. xu = 0.87 × 415 × 357/(0.36 × 20 × 1000) = 17.9 mm; m = 0.87 × 415 × 357 × (100 − 0.42 × 17.9) = 11.92 kN·m/m.
  4. Mechanism. Isotropic steel and a square slab: yield lines along the two diagonals, four triangular pieces rotating about the walls. Give the centre a deflection δ.
  5. Work equation.
    External work = 4 × w × (L²/4) × δ/3 = w L² δ/3
Internal work  = 4 × m × L × (2δ/L) = 8 m δ
w L² δ/3 = 8 m δ   →   w = 24 m / L²
  6. Collapse load. w = 24 × 11.92/4.0² = 17.88 kN/m², which is 1.49 times the design load 12 kN/m².
  7. Comment. Table 27 takes M = 0.062wL², close to wL²/16 (the Rankine–Grashoff value), while the mechanism needs only m = wL²/24. So a coefficient design has a margin of about 24 × 0.062 = 1.49 on the collapse load, before any extra from rounding the bar spacing: 1.49 here. The coefficient method is a lower bound (it satisfies equilibrium and never asks more than the capacity), while yield line theory is an upper bound; the true collapse load lies between 12 and 17.9 kN/m². Because the corners are free to lift, corner levers form at collapse and lower the yield line load a little, so the real margin is somewhat less than 1.49, but well above 1.
  8. Check of the steel. The capacity of the provided steel, m = 11.92 kN·m/m, almost exactly equals the design moment 11.90 kN·m/m, because 10 mm @ 220 mm gives 357 mm²/m against 356 needed. The collapse load scales with m, so the rounding adds almost nothing here and the whole margin comes from the method itself.
  9. Judgement. The margin is not wasted: it also covers the deflection and cracking that the collapse method ignores. Yield line theory is valuable when a slab is irregular or an existing slab must be checked for a new load, not as a way to cut the steel of a routine panel.
Analyze A slab 3.6 m × 6.6 m (effective spans), simply supported on walls with corners free to lift, carries wu = 10.5 kN/m² (D = 150 mm, dx = 125 mm, dy = 115 mm, M20, Fe415). A designer treats it as one-way. Compare that design with the two-way design by Table 27 and decide which is right. 13 marks

Points to cover. ly/lx and the IS 456 rule; Table 27 coefficients by interpolation; Two-way Mx, My and steel; One-way M and steel; Rankine–Grashoff share; Verdict on safety and economy.

Model answer.

  1. Classification. ly/lx = 6.6/3.6 = 1.833 ≤ 2, so IS 456 treats the slab as two-way. The designer's one-way assumption ignores the long-direction bending.
  2. Table 27. Between 1.75 (αx = 0.113, αy = 0.037) and 2.0 (0.118, 0.029): at 1.833, αx = 0.113 + 0.005 × 0.333 = 0.1147 and αy = 0.037 − 0.008 × 0.333 = 0.0343.
  3. Moments and steel.
    DesignShort span M (kN·m/m)Ast short (mm²/m)Long span MAst long
    Two-way, Table 270.1147 × 10.5 × 3.6² = 15.603680.0343 × 10.5 × 3.6² = 4.67115 → minimum 180
    One-way10.5 × 3.6²/8 = 17.01404not designeddistribution, minimum 180
  4. Short span. The one-way moment is 1.09 times the two-way value, so its short-span steel is 10 % more than needed. That is on the safe side.
  5. Long span. The two-way moment 4.67 kN·m/m needs only 115 mm²/m, less than the minimum 180 mm²/m; the one-way design also gives the long direction the minimum (as distribution steel). So the long direction is covered either way here, but only because the slab is near the limit of 2. At ly/lx = 1.3, αy = 0.055 against αx = 0.093: the long-direction moment is then too big for the minimum steel and a one-way design would be unsafe there.
  6. Load sharing check. Rankine–Grashoff: the short span takes 6.6⁴/(3.6⁴ + 6.6⁴) = 91.9 % of the load, so a one-way design ignores about 8 % of the load in the long direction. Table 27 gives αx = 0.1147, close to 0.919/8 = 0.1148, as expected since its values are the Grashoff ones.
  7. Torsion and corners. The corners are free to lift, so no torsion steel is needed in either design; if they were held down (Table 26) the coefficients would be smaller still and torsion steel would be required.
  8. A quick way to see the same result: the share of the load carried along the long span falls as (lx/ly)⁴. At 3.6 m × 6.6 m it is only about 8 %; at a ratio of 1.3 it would be about a quarter of the load. The code limit of 2 is where this share becomes small enough to be covered by distribution steel.
  9. Verdict. The two-way design is the code design and the more economical one (368 against 404 mm²/m in the short span). The one-way design is safe for this slab only because ly/lx is close to 2; as a method for a panel with ly/lx ≤ 2 it is wrong.

Glossary

Column head
A flared top of a column under a flat slab.
Column strip
In a flat slab, the band 0.25l₂ wide on each side of a column line.
Continuous edge
A slab edge where the slab carries on into the next panel, giving hogging moment.
Development length
The length a bar must be embedded to develop its design stress by bond, Ld = φσs/(4τbd).
Discontinuous edge
A slab edge treated as simply supported, such as an outer wall.
Distribution steel
Minimum steel at right angles to the main bars of a one-way slab.
Dog-legged staircase
Two parallel flights running in opposite directions with a half landing and no gap between them.
Drop
A thickening of a flat slab round a column, at least one-third of the panel length each way.
Edge strip
The outer 1/8 of a two-way panel width, given minimum steel.
Effective depth
Distance from the compression face to the centroid of the tension steel.
Effective span
The span used in design, fixed by cl. 22.2 of IS 456.
Flat slab
A slab carried directly on columns without beams.
Going
The horizontal length of a stair flight.
Hogging moment
A moment that puts the top of the slab in tension.
Middle strip
The central part of a slab panel that takes the design moments; 3/4 of the width in a two-way slab.
Modification factor
The Fig. 4 multiplier on the basic span/depth ratio for the tension steel provided.
One-way slab
A slab that bends in one direction, designed as strips across its short span.
Pattern loading
Live load placed on selected spans to produce the largest moment at a section.
Punching shear
Shear failure of a slab round a column along a truncated cone or pyramid.
Riser
The vertical height of one step.
Slope factor
√(R² + T²)/T, converting the sloping waist weight to load per m² of plan.
Torsion reinforcement
Top and bottom mesh at held-down corners of a two-way slab that resists twisting.
Total static moment
M₀ = W lₙ/8, the total moment of a flat slab panel in one direction.
Tread
The horizontal width of one step.
Two-way slab
A slab on four edges with ly/lx ≤ 2 that bends in both directions.
Upper bound
A load at or above the true collapse load, given by any valid mechanism.
Waist slab
The sloping slab that carries the steps of a flight, its thickness measured normal to the soffit.
Yield line
A line along which a slab yields and rotates at a constant moment per metre at collapse.
