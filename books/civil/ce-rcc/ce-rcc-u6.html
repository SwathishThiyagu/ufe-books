6.1 Basic principles and advantages of prestressing

Prestressed concrete squeezes the concrete before the load arrives. A stretched high-strength steel tendon pulls back on the ends of the beam and puts the whole section into compression. Bending from load then has to eat through that compression before any tension appears, so cracks start much later, or never. The same trick lets a beam be shallower and span farther than ordinary reinforced concrete. You meet it in long bridge girders, thin floor slabs, water tanks, pipes, railway sleepers and precast roof units across India.

You need: concrete properties and characteristic strength from Unit 1, the simple bending stress f = My/I from Unit 2, and a first look at high-strength steel from 6.2.

Prestress in one idea

Concrete is strong in compression and weak in tension. A plain concrete beam cracks when the bending tension reaches roughly one-tenth of its compressive strength. Ordinary reinforced concrete accepts this: the concrete cracks, and steel bars inside carry the tension that the concrete cannot.

Prestressing reverses the order. Before the load comes, the concrete is deliberately put into compression. Later bending tension must first cancel that compression before it can crack anything.

The force comes from a tendon: a cable of high-strength steel wires, strands or bars, stretched by a hydraulic jack. The jacking force P, in kN or N, is the force in the tendon at the moment the jack stretches it.

When the jack is released, the tendon tries to shrink back. Its ends are held by anchorages bearing on the concrete, so it drags the concrete with it and the section is squeezed. That hand-over of force from jack to concrete is called transfer. The force left after transfer is smaller than P; the losses are covered in 6.5.

Where the tendon sits matters. The eccentricity e is the distance from the centroidal axis of the section to the tendon, in mm. The tendon pulls with P at depth e; the concrete pushes back with P along the axis; the two form a couple that resists bending. That is the internal couple of a prestressed member.

Mr = P × e

Mr is the moment resisted by the prestress couple, in N·mm; P is the tendon force in N; e is the eccentricity in mm, measured from the centroidal axis to the tendon.

Adding the stresses: decompression and cracking

At any fibre, the stress is the sum of three parts: the axial squeeze P/A, the bending P·e·y/I caused by the eccentric tendon, and the bending M·y/I from the applied load. Take compression as positive throughout.

f = P/A ± P·e·y/I ± M·y/I

f is the stress at the fibre in N/mm²; P the tendon force in N; A the concrete area in mm²; e the eccentricity in mm; y the distance from the centroidal axis to the fibre in mm; I the second moment of area in mm⁴; M the applied bending moment in N·mm. Choose each sign by looking at the fibre: a tendon below the axis adds compression at the bottom and takes it away at the top.

As load grows, the stress at the tension face falls. The load at which that stress just becomes zero is the decompression load. Beyond it, tension starts to build; the concrete cracks only when that tension reaches its own tensile strength. So the whole range from zero load to cracking stays uncracked, and an uncracked section is stiff and barely deflects.

The couple P × e already acts as a built-in hogging moment before any load arrives. This is why a prestressed beam usually bows upward, a camber, when it is taken off the bed. Choosing a tendon profile so that this upward effect cancels part of the downward load is the load-balancing idea of 6.4.

Why prestress is used

  • Smaller, lighter sections for the same span, which cuts dead load and foundation cost.
  • Longer spans are possible: a prestressed girder can cross a gap that would need a much deeper reinforced concrete girder.
  • The section stays uncracked under service load, which suits water tanks, pipes and other liquid-retaining structures.
  • High-strength steel is used efficiently, close to its design strength, because crack width does not limit it the way it limits ordinary rebar (see 6.2).
  • Factory precasting gives good control, quick erection and less site labour: bridge girders, sleepers, electric poles and floor units are common examples.
  • Camber under prestress offsets part of the later sag, so deflections stay small.

The cost is skill and hardware: jacks, anchorages, ducts, high-strength steel, and a strong concrete mix. A heavy force enters at a small anchorage and spreads out, so the end block needs its own reinforcement (6.7). For short spans and light loads, ordinary reinforced concrete is still cheaper.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular beam 200 mm wide and 400 mm deep carries a straight tendon with jacking force P = 400 kN. (a) Find the stress when the tendon is concentric. (b) Find the top and bottom fibre stresses when the tendon is placed 100 mm below the centroidal axis. (c) For that eccentric tendon, a sagging moment later causes 8 N/mm² bending tension at the bottom fibre — find the net bottom stress, and compare with the concentric case.

  1. Area of the section: A = 200 × 400 = 80,000 mm².
  2. Concentric tendon: f = P/A = 400,000 / 80,000 = 5 N/mm² compression, uniform over the section.
  3. Second moment of area: I = b·D³/12 = 200 × 400³ / 12 = 1.0667 × 10⁹ mm⁴; distance to the extreme fibre y = 400/2 = 200 mm.
  4. Prestress bending from eccentricity: P·e·y/I = 400,000 × 100 × 200 / (1.0667 × 10⁹) = 8 × 10⁹ / (1.0667 × 10⁹) = 7.5 N/mm².
  5. Bottom fibre: 5 + 7.5 = 12.5 N/mm² compression.
  6. Top fibre: 5 − 7.5 = −2.5, that is 2.5 N/mm² tension, present even before any load.
  7. Apply the load: bottom stress = 8 − 12.5 = −4.5, so 4.5 N/mm² compression; the bottom never goes into tension.
  8. Concentric comparison: bottom stress = 8 − 5 = 3 N/mm² tension, so the concentric beam would crack while the eccentric one does not.

Exam traps

  • Prestress does not make concrete strong in tension. It only adds compression; if the load's tension exceeds the pre-compression plus the concrete's own tensile strength, the section still cracks and must be checked.
  • Sign slips. With compression positive, a tendon below the axis adds compression at the bottom and reduces it at the top, which can leave the top in tension under prestress alone. Always draw the stress diagram at top and bottom.
  • The jacking force is not the force that stays in the tendon. Transfer, friction, anchorage slip, creep, shrinkage and relaxation all reduce it (see 6.5). Design with the effective force.
  • Eccentricity e is measured from the centroidal axis, never from the top or bottom face. Measuring from the face gives a wrong P·e·y/I and wrong stresses.
  • Prestressing is not the same thing as using high-strength concrete. Strong concrete is needed because the prestress is large, but the tensile force is carried by the tendon.

6.2 High-strength concrete and high-tensile steel

Prestressing works only if the two materials pull their weight. The concrete must carry a large permanent compression without crushing or splitting, and the steel must hold a high tensile stress without yielding. That is why prestressed concrete uses high-strength concrete of grade M40 and above together with high-tensile steel in the form of wires, strands or bars. The pair makes long spans, thin sections and crack-free service possible in bridges, water tanks and railway sleepers.

You need Unit 1 on stress-strain behaviour of concrete and steel, and 6.1 for why prestress keeps concrete in compression.

Why stronger materials are needed

A tendon of area Ap stressed to fp applies a force P = Ap fp to the concrete. Here Ap is the tendon area in mm² and fp is the tensile stress in the steel in N/mm². P is in newtons when the units are mm² and N/mm². Prestressing forces are large, often hundreds of kilonewtons.

Ordinary mild steel yields at about 250 N/mm². A tendon at that stress would need a huge area to give the same force, so the section becomes heavy and uneconomical. High-tensile steel carries 1500 N/mm² or more, so a small tendon gives a large force. High-strength concrete then carries the resulting compression, bond and bearing stresses.

P = Ap fp

P is the prestressing force in N; Ap is the tendon cross-section area in mm²; fp is the tensile stress in the tendon in N/mm².

High-strength concrete

Concrete grade M40 means a characteristic cube compressive strength of 40 N/mm² at 28 days. The test uses 150 mm cubes. Prestressed work commonly starts at M40 and goes higher; a few codes allow M30 for lightly stressed members, but the trend is strong concrete.

High strength comes from a low water-cement ratio, good grading, adequate cement and careful compaction and curing. The tensile strength also rises with grade, but it remains a small fraction of the cube strength. Concrete is still weak in tension, so prestress must keep tensile stresses low or absent.

Ec = 5000√fck

Ec is the modulus of elasticity of concrete in N/mm²; fck is the characteristic cube compressive strength in N/mm².

For M40, Ec ≈ 5000√40 ≈ 31,600 N/mm². A stiffer concrete deflects less and holds the tendon force with less shortening. Higher strength also gives better resistance to creep and shrinkage, which reduces losses later.

Bearing and bond govern many details. At an anchorage, the jack force enters through a small plate, so local bearing stress is high. In pre-tensioning, the force transfers from steel to concrete by bond over a short transmission length. Strong concrete resists splitting, crushing and slip in both cases. See 6.7 for end-block design.

High-tensile steel

High-tensile steel comes as wire, strand or bar. A wire is a single small-diameter product, plain or indented. A strand is a group of wires twisted together. The common 7-wire strand has one central wire and six outer wires wound helically around it. A bar is a solid alloy-steel product, sometimes threaded for anchorage. Wires and strands commonly reach 1500 to 1900 N/mm²; bars are usually lower.

The stress-strain curve has no clear yield plateau. It is nearly straight at working stress, then bends gradually, reaches a peak and fails at a small strain. There is no yield point like mild steel. Design therefore uses the 0.1% proof stress fp0.1: draw a line parallel to the linear part, offset by 0.1% strain, and read the stress where it meets the curve. This is the stress that leaves a permanent strain of 0.1% after unloading.

For a typical strand, the 0.1% proof stress is about 85 to 90 percent of the ultimate tensile strength fpu. fpu is the maximum stress on the stress-strain curve. A designer checks the tendon stress against the proof stress, not against 250 N/mm², and not against the ultimate strength without a material factor.

Relaxation is another property of high-tensile steel. When a tendon is held at constant length, its stress slowly falls with time. This is relaxation, not creep: creep is strain change under constant stress; relaxation is stress change under constant strain. Low-relaxation strands reduce this loss. The calculation belongs to 6.5.

Worked example

Question. A pre-tensioned tendon has cross-section area Ap = 100 mm² and is stressed to fp = 1600 N/mm². (a) Find the prestressing force P. (b) Find the area of mild steel needed to carry the same force at a permissible stress of 250 N/mm². (c) State one practical conclusion.

  1. Use P = Ap fp.
  2. P = 100 mm² × 1600 N/mm² = 160,000 N.
  3. Convert to kilonewtons: P = 160,000 / 1000 = 160 kN.
  4. For mild steel, As = P / fs = 160,000 N / 250 N/mm² = 640 mm².
  5. The high-tensile tendon needs 100 mm², while mild steel needs 640 mm² for the same force.

Answer. The prestressing force is 160 kN, and the equivalent mild-steel area is 640 mm².

Exam traps

  • Using 250 N/mm² as the design strength of high-tensile steel. It has no clear yield point; use the 0.1% proof stress.
  • Reading M40 as 40 N/mm² tensile strength. M40 is characteristic cube compressive strength.
  • Mixing units: mm² × N/mm² gives N, not kN. Divide by 1000 only at the end.
  • Saying high-tensile steel is ductile because it is strong. It has low elongation and fails with little warning.
  • Confusing relaxation with creep. Relaxation is stress loss at constant strain; creep is strain growth at constant stress.

6.3 Pre-tensioning and post-tensioning systems

Pre-tensioning stretches the tendon first and casts concrete around it; post-tensioning casts a duct first and stretches the tendon after the concrete hardens. The order decides how force enters the concrete: bond in pre-tensioning, an end anchorage in post-tensioning. Factory-made sleepers, pipes and short-span slabs use pre-tensioning. Site-built beams, slabs and bridge girders use post-tensioning. Only a few universities teach this detail; the main split is simple. Read the timeline as two ways to reach the same goal: concrete in compression before load arrives.

You need: 6.1 for why prestress works and 6.2 for the strong materials it needs.

The order of work

Pre-tensioning follows a fixed order. Workers stress the tendon between abutments of a stressing bed. They fix formwork around it, cast concrete, and cure it. They release the tendon. The tendon tries to shorten, concrete grips it, and compression enters by bond along the transfer length. No end anchorage remains. The transfer length is the short distance over which bond builds up. It depends on tendon surface and concrete strength.

Post-tensioning reverses the order. Workers fix a duct and cast concrete. After the concrete hardens, they pass the tendon through the duct and stress it with a jack. The jack reacts against the concrete face through an anchorage. Force enters at the ends. Grouting fills the duct later.

The figure puts both orders on one timeline. Pre-tensioning stresses first; post-tensioning casts first and stresses later. Release and stressing are the moments when force moves from jack to concrete.

Pre-tensioning: long-line and mould methods

Long-line method: one long stressing bed holds many identical units in a line. A straight tendon runs from abutment to abutment. Each unit is cast in its own mould on the same line. One jacking operation stresses all units. The method suits factory production of sleepers, pipes, planks and poles. The tendon profile is usually straight, so eccentricity is small.

Mould method: each unit has its own mould and stressing equipment. The tendon is stressed against the mould or a small bed. The method suits large units or curved tendon profiles. It needs more jacks and handling.

In both methods, release is the key step. The tendon is cut or slowly released, and bond develops over the transfer length. Good compaction and a clean tendon surface shorten that length. End slip means bond has failed. Slow release is better than a sudden cut because it avoids shock. A sudden cut can damage the bond.

Post-tensioning: ducts, anchorages, grouting

Post-tensioning needs hardware. A duct is a thin corrugated metal or plastic tube cast into the concrete. It reserves a hole for the tendon. The tendon is a wire, strand or bar. At each end, an anchorage grips the tendon and bears on the concrete. Common grips are wedges for strands, button heads for wires, and nuts for threaded bars. A coupler joins two tendon lengths; it is not an anchorage.

After stressing, the duct is grouted. Grout is a cementitious mix pumped into the duct. It protects steel from corrosion and, in a bonded system, lets the tendon act with the concrete. An unbonded tendon is greased and plastic-sheathed; it is anchored at the ends and never grouted. Unbonded tendons are common in slabs.

Common systems are Freyssinet, Magnel, BBRV, VSL and Lee-McCall. They differ in grip, anchorage shape and number of wires per tendon. The transfer point is the exam favourite. Post-tensioning transfers force by end anchorage at stressing. Grouting gives bond only later.

Worked example

Question. A post-tensioned beam has 4 tendons, each jacked to 150 kN. Find total jacking force. The first tendon loses 3% when the second is stressed. Find its force after that step.

  1. n = 4 tendons; P = 150 kN each.
  2. Total = n × P = 4 × 150 = 600 kN.
  3. Remaining fraction = 1 − 0.03 = 0.97.
  4. First tendon force = 150 × 0.97 = 145.5 kN.

Answer. Total jacking force = 600 kN; first tendon force after the second is stressed = 145.5 kN.

Exam traps

  • Post-tensioning transfers force by bond at the ends: false, the anchorage does it; grout bonds later.
  • Grouting stresses the tendon: false, grout protects and bonds an already stressed tendon.
  • A coupler is an anchorage: false, it joins tendon lengths.
  • Pre-tensioning release needs no bond: false, bond develops exactly at release.
  • Long-line uses one mould per unit: false, many units share one bed.

6.4 Stress, strength and load balancing concepts

A prestressed beam carries three stresses at once: axial compression from the tendon, bending from the tendon's eccentricity, and bending from external load. The design question is what stress remains at the top and bottom fibres. This chapter sets one sign convention, adds the three parts, and defines decompression and cracking moments. It then turns the tendon's upward force into an equivalent load. Load balancing chooses a tendon profile that cancels part of the applied load, so the member bends less. These ideas are used in every prestressed beam check.

You need: bending stress from Unit 2, Limit State Design of Beams, and the basic prestressing idea from 6.1.

One sign convention, three stress parts

Use compression positive for every stress in this chapter. Let y be the distance of a fibre from the centroid, positive upward. Let e be the tendon eccentricity below the centroid, positive downward. Let M be a sagging external moment, positive. Then the stress at that fibre is:

f = P/A − P e y/I + M y/I

f is stress at the fibre (N/mm², compression positive); P is tendon force (N); A is concrete area (mm²); e is tendon eccentricity below centroid (mm); y is fibre distance from centroid, positive upward (mm); I is second moment of area (mm⁴); M is sagging moment (Nmm).

The term P/A is uniform axial compression. The term P e y/I is bending from the tendon; its sign changes above and below the centroid. The term M y/I is ordinary load bending. For a symmetric section, Z = I/(D/2), so top and bottom stresses are:

f_top = P/A − P e/Z + M/Z ; f_bottom = P/A + P e/Z − M/Z

Z is section modulus (mm³); D is overall depth (mm); top and bottom are at y = +D/2 and y = −D/2.

Check one fibre at a time. Write the sign of each term before adding. The worked example uses this order.

Transfer, service, decompression and cracking

At transfer, only the prestress force and the member self-weight act. At service, effective prestress and full load act. Use the same sign convention in both stages; only P and M change. Losses that reduce P are covered in 6.5.

Decompression means the stress at an extreme tension fibre has just reached zero. For a tendon below the centroid under sagging load, the bottom fibre is the one that decompresses. Set f_bottom = 0:

M_dec = Z_b (P/A + P e/Z_b)

M_dec is decompression moment (Nmm); Z_b is bottom section modulus (mm³). For a symmetric section Z_b = Z.

Cracking starts when that fibre reaches the concrete flexural tensile strength, not zero stress. The cracking moment is:

M_cr = M_dec + f_cr Z_b

M_cr is cracking moment (Nmm); f_cr is modulus of rupture of concrete (N/mm²).

A beam with M below M_dec is uncracked and the elastic formulas hold. Between M_dec and M_cr the bottom is in tension but uncracked. Above M_cr a crack forms and the gross-section stress formula no longer gives the true tension.

Load balancing with a parabolic tendon

A curved tendon pushes on the concrete along its length. For a parabolic tendon with sag e over span L, the upward equivalent load is:

w_p = 8 P e / L²

w_p is upward equivalent load per unit length (N/mm if P is N and L is mm); P is tendon force (N); e is tendon sag at midspan (mm); L is span (mm).

Load balancing chooses e so that w_p cancels part or all of the downward load w. Full balance needs:

e = w L²/(8P)

w is downward load per unit length, using the same units as w_p.

If w_p = w, the beam carries no bending; only P/A remains. If w_p is smaller, the residual downward load w − w_p causes bending. This is a design aid: pick a tendon profile, compute w_p, and check the remaining stresses by superposition. A balanced profile often gives smaller depth and less deflection. The idea is used in long-span bridge girders and transferred to continuous members through concordant cables in 6.8.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported rectangular beam has b = 250 mm, D = 600 mm, A = 150,000 mm², I = 4.5 × 10⁹ mm⁴ and Z = 15 × 10⁶ mm³. The tendon force at transfer is P = 600 kN at eccentricity e = 150 mm below the centroid. A sagging moment M = 120 kNm acts. Using compression positive, find the top and bottom stresses at transfer, the stress change from M, and the net top and bottom stresses.

  1. Axial stress: P/A = 600,000 N ÷ 150,000 mm² = 4 N/mm² compression.
  2. Prestress bending magnitude: P e/Z = 600,000 N × 150 mm ÷ 15,000,000 mm³ = 6 N/mm². The tendon is below the centroid, so this gives tension at top and compression at bottom.
  3. At transfer with M = 0: top = 4 − 6 = −2 N/mm², that is 2 N/mm² tension; bottom = 4 + 6 = 10 N/mm² compression.
  4. Load bending: M/Z = 120,000,000 Nmm ÷ 15,000,000 mm³ = 8 N/mm². Sagging gives compression at top and tension at bottom.
  5. Net top = −2 + 8 = 6 N/mm² compression. Net bottom = 10 − 8 = 2 N/mm² compression.

Answer. At transfer the top fibre has 2 N/mm² tension and the bottom has 10 N/mm² compression; after the 120 kNm sagging moment the net stresses are 6 N/mm² compression at top and 2 N/mm² compression at bottom.

Exam traps

  • Mixing signs halfway through the calculation. Decide compression positive at the start and keep it for top, bottom, prestress and load terms.
  • Using the jacking force at service. The service calculation needs effective prestress after losses; losses are treated in 6.5.
  • Drawing the tendon on the wrong side of the centroid. For e below the centroid, the prestress term is tension at top, not compression.
  • Using the gross-section Z after the section has cracked. The formula f = P/A ± P e y/I ± M y/I assumes an uncracked elastic section.
  • Treating w_p as a real downward load. For a sagging parabolic tendon the equivalent load acts upward and must be given the correct sign in the load balance.

6.5 Losses of prestress

A tendon is jacked to a chosen force, but that force does not stay. The jacking force P0 is measured at the jack. The concrete shortens, the duct rubs, the anchor beds in, and time brings creep, shrinkage and relaxation. Each effect removes a small part of P0. The remaining force is the effective prestress P_eff. Design for service uses P_eff, not P0. The idea matters on every post-tensioned slab, bridge girder and water tank where the designer must know the force that actually presses the concrete.

You need 6.1, 6.2, 6.3 and 6.4.

What a loss is

A loss is a reduction in tendon force after jacking. Immediate losses happen during transfer or stressing. Time-dependent losses grow over months. The total loss ΔP is the sum of all force losses, in kN.

The effective prestress is:

P_eff = P0 − ΔP_es − ΔP_fr − ΔP_slip − ΔP_cr − ΔP_sh − ΔP_rel

P_eff is the effective prestress force after all losses (kN); P0 is the jacking force (kN); ΔP_es is elastic shortening loss, ΔP_fr is friction loss, ΔP_slip is anchorage slip loss, ΔP_cr is creep loss, ΔP_sh is shrinkage loss and ΔP_rel is relaxation loss, all in kN.

For a preliminary estimate, take each loss as a percentage of P0 and add the percentages. That estimate assumes each loss is independent of the others. It is not exact, because losses interact. State the assumption when you use it.

Immediate losses

Elastic shortening happens when the tendon compresses the concrete. In a pre-tensioned member the concrete shortens at transfer. The tendon shortens with it. The stress loss is

Δfp,es = (Es / Ec) fcp

Δfp,es is tendon stress loss (N/mm²); Es is steel modulus (N/mm²); Ec is concrete modulus (N/mm²); fcp is concrete compressive stress at tendon level (N/mm²). Force loss ΔPes = Aps Δfp,es / 1000 (kN), with Aps the tendon area (mm²) and Δfp,es in N/mm².

In a post-tensioned member jacked one tendon at a time, earlier tendons lose force when later tendons compress the concrete. Jack all tendons together and this loss is small.

Friction in a post-tensioned duct has two parts. Curvature friction depends on the angle change. Wobble friction depends on length. The force at distance x from the jack is

Px = P0 e−μα − kx

Px is tendon force at x (kN); P0 is jacking force (kN); μ is curvature friction coefficient (dimensionless); α is total angle change from jack to x (radians); k is wobble coefficient (per metre); x is distance from jack (m); e is 2.718.

Use radians in μα. A 5° angle is 0.087 rad. The loss is P0 − Px.

At the anchor, the wedge or plate beds into the concrete by a slip δ. For a simple estimate, ignore friction and take the whole tendon of length L to shorten uniformly by δ. The stress loss along the tendon is

Δfp,slip = (Es δ) / L

Δfp,slip is tendon stress loss (N/mm²); Es is steel modulus (N/mm²); δ is anchorage slip (mm); L is full tendon length (mm); friction is ignored. Force loss ΔPslip = Aps Δfp,slip / 1000 (kN), with Aps in mm² and Δfp,slip in N/mm².

With friction, the slip is absorbed over a shorter affected length ls found from the friction slope. The tendon strain varies from a maximum at the anchor to zero at the end of ls. The loss at the anchor is about

Δfp,slip = (2 Es δ) / ls

Δfp,slip is tendon stress loss at the anchor (N/mm²); Es is steel modulus (N/mm²); δ is anchorage slip (mm); ls is affected length over which slip is absorbed (mm). The loss falls linearly to zero at the end of ls. The simple estimate with L understates the anchor loss when friction is significant.

Time-dependent losses

Creep is slow shortening of concrete under sustained compression. The tendon follows the concrete and loses stress:

Δfp,cr = (Es / Ec) φ fcp

Δfp,cr is creep stress loss (N/mm²); φ is creep coefficient (dimensionless); other symbols as before.

Shrinkage is drying shrinkage of concrete. It pulls the tendon inward. The stress loss is

Δfp,sh = εsh Es

Δfp,sh is shrinkage stress loss (N/mm²); εsh is shrinkage strain (dimensionless); Es is steel modulus (N/mm²).

Relaxation is loss of stress in steel held at constant strain. It depends on the steel type and initial stress. Low-relaxation strand loses less than ordinary stress-relieved strand. Use the manufacturer's value or the code table. A typical low-relaxation value is about 2% of the initial prestress.

Add all force losses to get P_eff. Check service stresses with P_eff, as in 6.4. Design checks that use effective force come in 6.6.

Worked example

Question. A post-tensioned beam is jacked to P0 = 1000 kN. For a preliminary estimate take friction 4%, anchorage slip 3%, elastic shortening 2%, creep 6%, shrinkage 3% and relaxation 2%, each as a percentage of P0. Find the total loss and the effective prestress.

  1. List the percentage losses: friction 4%, anchorage slip 3%, elastic shortening 2%, creep 6%, shrinkage 3%, relaxation 2%.
  2. Add the percentages: 4 + 3 + 2 + 6 + 3 + 2 = 20%. This assumes each loss is a percentage of P0 and losses are added directly.
  3. Total loss ΔP = 0.20 × 1000 kN = 200 kN.
  4. Effective prestress P_eff = P0 − ΔP = 1000 − 200 = 800 kN.

Answer. Total loss is 20% or 200 kN; effective prestress is 800 kN.

Exam traps

  • Adding percentages of P0 is a preliminary estimate. Real losses interact; do not present the sum as exact.
  • Using degrees in the friction term μα. Convert to radians: 1° = π/180 rad.
  • Swapping creep and relaxation. Creep is concrete shortening; relaxation is steel stress loss at constant strain.
  • Using P0 in service stress checks. Use P_eff after all losses.
  • Forgetting that anchorage slip loss depends on tendon length L; a short tendon loses more stress for the same slip.

6.6 Flexural design of prestressed members

A prestressed beam must pass two different tests. Before it carries service load, the jacking force and self-weight must not overstress the concrete. Under full load, the effective prestress and the loads must keep tension within the chosen class. At collapse, the tendon stress rises far above the effective prestress, and the section must have enough ultimate moment. Design therefore chooses a section and tendon, checks transfer and service stresses, then checks ultimate flexure. The same sequence is used for a roof beam, a bridge girder or a precast slab.

You need: 6.4 for stress calculation and 6.5 for effective prestress, plus the limit state flexure stress block from Unit 2.

Service stress checks set the section

Every prestressed flexural member is checked at two stages. At transfer, when the tendon is released, the force is the initial prestress Pi and the only load is self-weight. At service, the force is the effective prestress Pe = Pi minus losses, and the load is the full service load. Extreme fibre stresses are found by adding axial, prestress bending and load bending.

f = P/A ± P e/Z ± M/Z

f is stress in N/mm², compression positive; P is prestress force in N; A is concrete area in mm²; e is tendon eccentricity from the centroid in mm; Z = I/y is the section modulus in mm³; M is bending moment in Nmm; the ± signs follow the fibre and the tendon side.

At transfer the concrete is young, so the code allows a lower compressive stress, often written as a fraction of the cube strength at transfer fci. At service the limit is a fraction of the characteristic cube strength fck. These limits stop crushing and excessive creep.

Tension is controlled by choosing a behaviour class. Type 1: no tension at service, used for liquid-retaining tanks and aggressive exposure. Type 2: tension allowed but below the modulus of rupture, so no visible crack, used for bridge decks. Type 3: cracks allowed but width limited by ordinary reinforcement, used in partially prestressed members (see 6.9). Some syllabi and IS 1343 call these Class 1, Class 2 and Class 3. Section size is often governed by the transfer check, not by ultimate strength: a beam strong enough at collapse may still crush at release because Pi acts on a small concrete area.

Ultimate moment with bonded tendons

At ultimate, the concrete cracks and the tendon stress is no longer Pe/Ap. For bonded tendons the strain is compatible with the surrounding concrete, so the tendon stress rises to fpb. Codes give fpb from tables using the ultimate tendon strength fpu, concrete strength fck and effective prestress. For a rectangular section with only bonded tendons, horizontal equilibrium gives:

0.36 fck b xu = Ap fpb

fck is characteristic cube strength in N/mm²; b is section width in mm; xu is neutral axis depth in mm; Ap is tendon area in mm²; fpb is tendon stress at ultimate in N/mm².

Mu = Ap fpb (d − 0.42 xu)

Mu is ultimate moment in Nmm; d is effective depth to the tendon centroid in mm; the lever arm is d − 0.42 xu. Compare Mu with the factored design moment.

For a flanged section, if xu lies within the flange, use b = bf. If xu is below the flange, C = 0.36 fck bw xu + 0.45 fck (bf − bw) Df, where bw is web width in mm, bf is flange width in mm and Df is flange thickness in mm; equate this C to Ap fpb. Mild steel can be added: 0.36 fck b xu = Ap fpb + 0.87 fy As, with its moment 0.87 fy As (ds − 0.42 xu), where ds is effective depth to the rebar. Shear and deflection are separate checks: shear reinforcement from Unit 3, deflection from Unit 2 serviceability. Flexural design only fixes the section and tendon for moment.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular pretensioned beam has width b = 300 mm, effective depth d = 700 mm and bonded tendon area Ap = 500 mm². The tendon stress at ultimate is fpb = 1500 N/mm² and the concrete cube strength is fck = 50 N/mm². Find the neutral axis depth xu and the ultimate moment Mu.

  1. Tension at ultimate: T = Ap fpb = 500 × 1500 = 750,000 N.
  2. Concrete compression: C = 0.36 fck b xu = 0.36 × 50 × 300 × xu = 5400 xu N.
  3. Equate T and C: 5400 xu = 750,000, so xu = 138.9 mm.
  4. Lever arm: d − 0.42 xu = 700 − 0.42 × 138.9 = 700 − 58.3 = 641.7 mm.
  5. Ultimate moment: Mu = 750,000 × 641.7 = 481.25 × 10⁶ Nmm = 481.25 kNm.

Answer. xu = 138.9 mm and Mu = 481.25 kNm, about 481 kNm.

Exam traps

  • Using the effective prestress force Pe as the ultimate capacity. Ultimate tension is Ap fpb, and fpb is much larger than Pe/Ap.
  • Forgetting that transfer uses Pi and self-weight only, while service uses Pe and full load. Losses change the force and the stress sign.
  • A sign slip: a tendon below the centroid causes hogging, so it adds compression at the bottom and tension at the top. Locate the tendon side before adding moments.
  • Using the full flange width bf in 0.36 fck b xu when xu is below the flange. Below the flange, compression uses the web plus the flange overhang.
  • Unit slip: f in N/mm² and dimensions in mm give M in Nmm. Divide by 10⁶ for kNm; a missing factor of 10⁶ makes the answer look 1000 times too large.

6.7 Anchorage zone (end block) design

At a post-tensioned anchorage, a jack force of hundreds or thousands of kilonewtons enters the concrete through a small steel plate. The force cannot stay concentrated: it spreads out into the end block and then into the full beam. This spreading produces transverse tension behind the plate, called bursting. A separate local effect, called spalling, appears in the concrete around the plate at the loaded face, because the stiff plate restrains the lateral expansion of the concrete beneath it; this tension acts on planes parallel to the loaded face, just outside the plate. Both must be dealt with, but they have different causes. The end block must have enough bearing area and transverse reinforcement to carry these local effects. The same idea appears at every post-tensioned anchor in a bridge, a parking slab or a metro viaduct segment.

You need: 6.1 and 6.3; post-tensioning introduces the concentrated anchorage force.

Two anchorage zones

An anchorage zone is the region near a post-tensioning anchor where the tendon force enters the concrete. It has two parts. The primary zone is the small block immediately behind the bearing plate. Here the force is still concentrated, bearing stress is high, and spalling tension can appear near the loaded face. The secondary zone is the larger end block where the force spreads from the plate area to the full concrete section. This spreading produces transverse bursting tension. The primary zone is controlled mainly by bearing plate size and local mesh; the secondary zone is controlled by closed stirrups or similar transverse steel. Pre-tensioned members transfer force by bond over a length, so this end-block problem belongs mainly to post-tensioning, discussed in 6.3.

Bearing stress and plate size

The first check is bearing. A plate of area Aplate presses on concrete with average stress σb = P / Aplate. P is the anchorage force after losses, in newtons; Aplate is the net plate area in mm²; σb is bearing stress in N/mm². Use the same force units throughout. A 1000 kN force is 1,000,000 N, not 1000 N. The plate must be large enough that σb does not exceed the permitted bearing stress for the concrete. Concrete under a plate is confined by the surrounding concrete, so its bearing strength is higher than its cube strength, but this benefit is limited. A thin plate can bend or punch into concrete, so plate thickness and stiffness matter too. If σb is too high, enlarge the plate or use a stronger concrete; adding stirrups alone does not fix bearing.

σb = P / Aplate

σb is average bearing stress in N/mm²; P is anchorage force in N; Aplate is plate area in mm².

Bursting force and transverse steel

Behind the plate the force spreads outward, like water from a narrow nozzle. The spreading lines of force pull the concrete sideways and create transverse tension. For a square or rectangular plate, an approximate bursting force is Fbst ≈ 0.25 P (1 − a/b). Here P is the anchorage force after losses, the jack force reduced by losses, and the same value is used for both the bearing check and the bursting check. a is the plate width in the direction considered, and b is the end-block width in the same direction; a/b is dimensionless. The force Fbst is in the same units as P. A wider plate gives a smaller 1 − a/b and less bursting. For a rectangular plate, repeat the bursting check in both directions, taking a and b in the same direction each time, and provide stirrups for the bursting force found in each direction. The bursting force is not flexural steel demand; it is a splitting force inside the end block. Provide closed stirrups, U-bars or spirals around the tendon, spread over the bursting zone, with area Ast ≈ Fbst / (0.87 fy), with Fbst in N, fy in N/mm² and Ast in mm². Convert Fbst from kN to N before using the formula: 166.7 kN = 166,700 N. Place several small layers rather than one heavy layer, and anchor them well. Near the surface, add a light mesh for spalling.

  1. Place closed stirrups around the duct, starting close to the plate.
  2. Spread them over the bursting zone, roughly one member depth.
  3. Add surface mesh or small U-bars for spalling near the loaded face.
  4. Keep the bearing plate concentric with the tendon.

Worked example

6.8 Concordant cables and linear transformation

A continuous prestressed beam is statically indeterminate. Prestress can then produce reactions at supports even when no external load acts. These secondary reactions shift the pressure line and complicate design. A concordant cable is a tendon profile that produces no such reactions: the pressure line coincides with the cable. Linear transformation is the rule that lets you move a cable profile by adding a straight line without changing the equivalent loads. Designers use it to adjust tendon eccentricity for cover, anchorage or stress while keeping the load balancing and reactions unchanged.

You need: load balancing and equivalent loads from 6.4, and continuous beam bending from Unit 2.

Pressure line and concordant cable

A simply supported prestressed beam is statically determinate. The beam can bend freely under prestress, so the supports feel no vertical force from the tendon. Every cable in such a beam is concordant.

A continuous beam is statically indeterminate. The middle support restrains the beam, so prestress can create reactions at the supports. These reactions are called secondary reactions. They produce a secondary bending moment Ms in addition to the primary moment P e.

The pressure line is the locus of the resultant compressive force in the concrete. Its eccentricity from the centroid is ep = M / P, where M is the total moment from prestress (primary plus secondary), P is the prestress force, and ep is measured from the centroid, positive downward. In a concordant cable, ep(x) = e(x) at every section, so Ms = 0 and the support reactions from prestress are zero.

A straight cable at constant eccentricity is the simplest concordant cable in a two-span beam. It gives a constant moment P e and zero shear, so the supports feel no vertical force. A concentric straight cable with e = 0 is a trivial case. A curved cable can also be concordant if its equivalent loads are self-equilibrated. The test is zero secondary reactions, not zero eccentricity.

Concordant profiles are useful because the designer can analyse prestress alone without computing secondary moments. They also avoid unexpected reactions at supports. In practice many tendons are not concordant, so secondary reactions must be calculated.

Linear transformation

A linear transformation of a cable profile means adding a straight line: enew(x) = e(x) + a + b x. Here x is the distance along the beam (m), a is a constant with units of length (m), and b is dimensionless — it is the slope of the added line, in m/m. The added term has zero curvature, d²(a + b x)/dx² = 0.

w_p = −P d²e/dx²

w_p is the equivalent upward load per unit length (N/m); P is the prestress force (N); e is the tendon eccentricity, positive downward (m); x is the distance along the beam (m).

A sagging parabola has negative second derivative, so wp is positive, i.e. upward. Because the added straight line has zero second derivative, it does not change wp.

In a continuous beam, the reactions from prestress come from the complete equivalent load system: the distributed loads wp, the anchorage forces and moments at the ends, and the support conditions. A linear transformation does change the anchorage forces and moments, but that change is self-equilibrated: zero net force and zero net moment. A self-equilibrated change adds no support reaction. So the reactions depend only on wp and the support conditions, and wp is unchanged. Therefore a linear transformation of a concordant cable is also concordant. This is the theorem of linear transformation.

What changes is the eccentricity and the primary moment P e at every section. Stresses and cover change. Linear transformation is not free for stress design; it only preserves the equivalent loads and the reactions. Designers use it to shift a tendon up or down at a support, to satisfy anchorage or cover, without changing the load balancing.

Two-span example and limits

Take a two-span beam with equal spans L. A straight tendon at constant eccentricity e = 100 mm is concordant. The moment is P × 0.100 at every section, the shear is zero, and the middle support feels no vertical force from prestress.

Add a straight line et = k x/L, with k in mm and x measured from the left end. The beam is 2L long, so at the right end x = 2L. The new profile is a straight inclined tendon: at the left end e = 100 mm, at the right end e = 100 + 2k mm. The curvature is still zero, so wp = 0 and the reactions are still zero. The moment becomes linear. The cable remains concordant.

A parabolic tendon in each span is different. Its curvature is not zero, so it produces an equivalent load wp. That load generally needs support reactions, so the cable is not concordant unless the equivalent loads are self-equilibrated. Linear transformation still leaves wp unchanged, so it cannot turn a non-concordant cable into a concordant one. It only preserves whatever reactions already exist.

In post-tensioned continuous slabs and beams, designers often choose a concordant profile or accept secondary moments. The equivalent load method from 6.4 gives wp; the linear transformation rule then lets the tendon be moved for practical detailing without recomputing that load.

Exam traps

  • Concordant does not mean straight or concentric. Concordant means zero secondary reactions; a curved cable can be concordant if its equivalent loads are self-equilibrated, and a straight concentric cable is only a trivial case.
  • Adding a straight line does not change w_p. Do not recompute w_p from the new midspan eccentricity; use the curvature, which comes only from the parabolic part.
  • Secondary reactions are not external loads. They are caused by restraint in a continuous beam. A simply supported beam has none.
  • Watch the sign in w_p = −P d²e/dx². With e positive downward, a sagging parabola has e'' negative and gives upward w_p.
  • The pressure line equals the cable only when the cable is concordant. In general e_p = M/P includes the secondary moment, so the pressure line is not the tendon profile.

6.9 Partial prestressing

Partial prestressing is a deliberate mix of prestressed tendons and ordinary reinforcing bars. The tendons apply compression and carry part of the bending moment; the bars carry the rest and control cracks. It exists because full prestressing can be costly and can make a member too cambered or brittle, while ordinary reinforced concrete needs larger sections and shows wider cracks under heavy load. You meet it in bridge decks, long-span floors, water tanks and precast panels where a small, controlled tension under service load is acceptable.

You need: 6.6; Unit 2 reinforced concrete flexure and serviceability.

What partial prestressing is

A partially prestressed member has both prestressing steel (tendons) and non-prestressed reinforcement (ordinary bars). The two work together. Prestressing steel is high-tensile wire, strand or bar; ordinary steel is a ribbed bar such as Fe 415 or Fe 500. The tendons are tensioned to a force P, and the bars are placed like normal RC steel. Under service load the section may be uncracked, cracked, or on the point of cracking. A fully prestressed member is usually designed to keep concrete in compression or at zero tension. Ordinary RC has no prestress at all. Partial prestressing sits between them.

λ = Mp / Mu

λ is the prestress contribution ratio; Mp is the moment resisted by the prestress effect; Mu is the total required moment. Some texts call this the degree of prestressing.

Full prestressing gives small deflection and no tension, but it needs a large force, many tendons and strong anchorages. The member may camber upward and have little ductility. Ordinary RC is simple and cheap but cracks and deflects. Partial prestressing uses tendons for a large share of the moment and bars for the peak and for crack control. It reduces the prestress force, the number of tendons, the camber and the end-block reinforcement. It is not a lower grade of full prestressing; it is a different design choice.

The spectrum runs from full prestressing with no tension, through partial prestressing with tendons plus rebar, to ordinary RC with no prestress. The figure shows this order.

Design checks: service and ultimate

The design idea is to split the required moment. Choose the tendon force and profile so prestress supplies Mp, and let ordinary bars supply Ms. A first estimate uses Mu = Mp + Ms. Exact ultimate analysis uses strain compatibility because the tendon stress at failure is not simply its initial stress, and the two steel types may have different lever arms.

As = Ms / (0.87 fy z)

As is the area of ordinary tension bars in mm²; Ms is the moment carried by those bars in N mm; fy is the yield strength of the ordinary steel in N/mm²; z is the lever arm in mm.

  1. For the service stress check, use the effective prestress after losses to find the service-load prestress moment.
  2. For the ultimate-strength estimate, take the tendon stress at the ultimate limit state (or assume an ultimate tendon stress and confirm it by strain compatibility) and find the ultimate prestress moment Mp.
  3. Subtract this ultimate Mp from the total factored moment to get Ms = Mu - Mp; both terms must be at the same ultimate limit state.
  4. Find the ordinary steel area from As = Ms / (0.87 fy z), then choose bars.
  5. Check service stresses and crack width using the effective prestress after losses.
  6. Check ultimate strength by strain compatibility, and check ductility and anchorage.

Both checks matter. A section can be strong at ultimate and still fail serviceability by wide cracks or excessive tension. A section can look safe at service and still be brittle at ultimate. Partial prestressing therefore needs the service stress check, the crack-width check and the ultimate strength check.

Economy and common uses

Partial prestressing is common where full prestressing is too expensive or too stiff. Examples are bridge decks, precast floor slabs, water tanks, piles and parking structures. In a long-span floor, tendons can carry the main moment and ordinary bars can handle peak moments near supports and control cracking. Fewer tendons mean fewer anchorages, less end-block reinforcement and simpler detailing. The builder can use conventional rebar skills for part of the steel.

Economy comes from balance, not from saving steel alone. The section must still satisfy service stress, crack width, ultimate strength and deflection. A cheap design that cracks badly will need repair, and a design with too much prestress may need extra camber control. The engineer chooses the prestress contribution ratio to suit span, load, crack limits and construction method.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported beam must resist a factored moment Mu = 300 kN m. The design uses partial prestressing: prestress is taken to contribute Mp = 180 kN m and ordinary Fe 415 bars carry the remaining Ms. Take the lever arm for the ordinary bars as z = 600 mm and use 0.87 fy in the steel force. Find the area of ordinary tension reinforcement As. State one serviceability check that must still be made.

  1. Remaining moment for ordinary bars: Ms = Mu - Mp = 300 - 180 = 120 kN m.
  2. Convert to N mm: Ms = 120 × 10⁶ N mm.
  3. Steel design stress: 0.87 fy = 0.87 × 415 = 361.05 N/mm².
  4. As = Ms / (0.87 fy z) = 120 × 10⁶ / (361.05 × 600).
  5. As = 553.9 mm², so provide about 554 mm²; for example, two 20 mm bars give 628 mm².
  6. Serviceability check: check tensile stress and crack width under service load; also confirm ultimate strength by strain compatibility.

Answer. The ordinary tension reinforcement required is As = 554 mm², and the member must still be checked for service tensile stress and crack width.

Exam traps

  • Treating partial prestressing as a lower grade of full prestressing. It is a deliberate mix, and crack width and ultimate strength both need checks.
  • Adding the prestress moment and the rebar moment without checking strain compatibility. The tendon stress at ultimate is not simply the initial prestress, and the lever arms may differ.
  • Using the gross concrete section for service stresses after the section has cracked. A cracked transformed section is needed for realistic tension and crack checks.
  • Forgetting losses of prestress in the service check. The jacking force is not the effective force that remains.
  • Mixing units: kN m must become N mm before dividing by stresses in N/mm².

Takeaways

  1. Prestressing turns concrete's weakness in tension into an advantage by keeping the section in compression under service load.
  2. Large forces are needed, so prestressed work demands high-strength concrete and high-tensile steel together.
  3. Pre-tensioning relies on bond; post-tensioning relies on end anchorages and grout.
  4. The jacking force is not the design force; losses must be estimated and subtracted to get effective prestress.
  5. Stresses at a section are the sum of axial, prestress bending and load bending terms, f = P/A ± P e y/I ± M y/I.
  6. Load balancing converts a tendon profile into an upward load and makes deflection control intuitive.
  7. Flexural design has two checks: no cracking or limited tension at service, and adequate moment at ultimate.
  8. The end block always needs transverse reinforcement because the anchorage force bursts outward as it spreads.
  9. In continuous beams, cable profile choice controls whether prestress creates reactions, and hence secondary moments.
  10. Partial prestressing is often the economical answer where full prestress would be wasteful or crack control is not critical.

Glossary

Anchorage
The end device, such as a wedge and plate, that holds the stressed tendon against the concrete.
Bursting stress
Transverse tensile stress set up behind an anchorage as the concentrated force spreads into the section.
Concordant cable
A tendon whose profile lies along the pressure line, producing no reactions at the supports.
Cracking moment
The bending moment at which the extreme fibre tensile stress reaches the tensile strength of concrete.
Creep
Time-dependent shortening of concrete under sustained compressive stress.
Decompression
The load at which the stress at the extreme tensile fibre just becomes zero.
Duct
A thin metal or plastic sheath cast in concrete to leave a hole for a post-tensioning tendon.
Eccentricity
The perpendicular distance between the tendon centroid and the concrete centroidal axis.
Effective prestress
The force that remains in the tendon after all immediate and time-dependent losses.
End block
The region near an anchorage, about one beam depth long, designed for the spreading force.
Grouting
Filling the duct with cement grout after stressing to give bond and protect the tendon from corrosion.
Jacking force
The force read at the jack when the tendon is tensioned, before any losses.
Linear transformation
A straight-line shift of a tendon profile that leaves the equivalent loads and pressure line unchanged.
Load balancing
A design method in which the upward force from a curved tendon is balanced against part of the applied load.
Partial prestressing
A design in which tendons and ordinary reinforcement together provide the required strength and crack control.
Pressure line
The line of action of the resultant compressive force at each section of a prestressed member.
Prestressed concrete
Concrete in which an internal compressive stress has been introduced, usually by stretched steel, to oppose the tensile stress from loads.
Relaxation
Gradual loss of stress in a tendon held at constant length.
Secondary moment
The moment produced in a continuous member by the reactions that a non-concordant tendon creates at the supports.
Shrinkage
Shortening of concrete due to drying, independent of applied stress.
Spalling stress
Transverse tensile stress close to the loaded face, acting near the anchorage plate edges.
Strand
Several wires twisted together, commonly seven wires, used as a prestressing tendon.
Tendon
A group of high-tensile wires, strands or bars used to apply the prestressing force.
Transfer
The operation of releasing the prestress from the jack or bed into the concrete.
