6.1 Basic principles and advantages of prestressing

Prestressed concrete is concrete squeezed on purpose before it carries load. A stretched high-strength steel tendon puts it into compression, and the bending from the load must use up that stored compression before tension can appear, so cracks come much later or never. The same idea lets a beam be shallower and span farther than reinforced concrete: bridge girders, metro viaduct segments, railway sleepers, poles, water tanks and hollow-core slabs.

You need the properties of concrete and reinforcing steel (characteristic strength, modulus of elasticity) and the bending stress f = My/I from limit state design of beams.

Prestress in one idea

Concrete is strong in compression and weak in tension. Its flexural tensile strength, the modulus of rupture, is only fcr = 0.7√fck by IS 456: about 4.4 N/mm² for M40 concrete against a cube strength of 40 N/mm². Reinforced concrete accepts this: the concrete cracks and bars carry the tension.

Prestressing changes the order: the concrete is compressed first, and the load must cancel that compression before it can cause tension.

The force comes from a tendon, a high-tensile steel wire, strand or bar stretched by a hydraulic jack. The jacking force P0 is the tendon force while the jack holds it. Transfer is the moment that force is handed to the concrete. In a pre-tensioned member the tendon is cut or released from its bed and grips the concrete by bond; in a post-tensioned member the jack is removed and the tendon bears on the concrete through end anchorages. The force left in the tendon later is smaller than P0 because of losses of prestress.

The eccentricity e is the distance from the centroidal axis of the concrete section to the tendon, in mm. A concentric tendon (e = 0) gives the same compression P/A at every fibre. A tendon below the axis also bends the section upward with a prestress moment P × e, adding compression at the bottom, where the load will cause tension.

Adding the stresses

At any fibre the stress is the sum of three parts: the axial squeeze P/A, the bending P·e/Z caused by the eccentric tendon, and the bending M/Z from the load. In this unit compression is positive and tension negative. For the top and bottom fibres of a rectangular section, Z = bD²/6:

ftop = P/A − P·e/Z + M/Z
fbottom = P/A + P·e/Z − M/Z

f is the fibre stress in N/mm² (compression positive); P is the tendon force in N; A is the concrete area in mm²; e is the eccentricity below the centroid in mm; Z = I/y is the section modulus in mm³ for that fibre; M is the sagging moment from the load in N·mm. The signs above hold for a tendon below the centroid and a sagging moment.

As load grows, the bottom stress falls. The moment that brings the bottom stress exactly to zero is the decompression moment. Beyond it the bottom goes into tension, and it cracks when that tension reaches fcr. The upward bending P × e also gives a prestressed beam a small upward camber before load. Making a curved tendon's upward push cancel part of the load is load balancing.

Why prestress is used

  • The section stays uncracked, or has only fine cracks, at service load: good for durability, water tanks, pipes and members in coastal air.
  • Smaller and lighter sections for the same span, so less dead load on columns and foundations; long spans become practical.
  • High-strength steel and concrete are used at high stresses, so less material carries the same load.
  • Deflection is small because the section is uncracked and the camber offsets part of the sag.
  • Better shear and fatigue resistance: the compression reduces diagonal tension and the steel stress changes little under repeated load.

The cost is skill and hardware: jacks, anchorages, ducts, grout, strong concrete and supervision. The large force entering at a small anchorage needs its own steel in the anchorage zone. For short spans and light loads, ordinary reinforced concrete is usually cheaper. The method was made practical by Eugène Freyssinet in France around 1928, once high-tensile steel was available to survive the long-term losses.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular beam 200 mm wide and 400 mm deep carries a straight tendon with force P = 400 kN. (a) Find the stresses when the tendon is concentric. (b) Find the top and bottom stresses when the tendon is 100 mm below the centroid. (c) A sagging moment of 56 kN·m is then applied. Find the top and bottom stresses for both tendon positions, and say which beam cracks if the concrete is M40. (d) Find the moment that brings the bottom stress to zero in each case.

  1. Section: A = 200 × 400 = 80,000 mm²; Z = bD²/6 = 200 × 400²/6 = 5.333 × 10⁶ mm³.
  2. (a) Concentric: P/A = 400,000/80,000 = 5 N/mm² compression at every fibre.
  3. (b) Prestress bending: P·e/Z = 400,000 × 100/(5.333 × 10⁶) = 7.5 N/mm². Top = 5 − 7.5 = −2.5 N/mm², that is 2.5 N/mm² tension; bottom = 5 + 7.5 = 12.5 N/mm² compression.
  4. (c) Load bending: M/Z = 56 × 10⁶/(5.333 × 10⁶) = 10.5 N/mm², compression at the top and tension at the bottom.
  5. Eccentric tendon: top = −2.5 + 10.5 = 8 N/mm² compression; bottom = 12.5 − 10.5 = 2 N/mm² compression. No tension anywhere.
  6. Concentric tendon: top = 5 + 10.5 = 15.5 N/mm²; bottom = 5 − 10.5 = −5.5 N/mm², that is 5.5 N/mm² tension.
  7. Cracking check: fcr = 0.7√40 = 4.43 N/mm². The concentric beam's bottom tension 5.5 N/mm² exceeds it, so that beam cracks; the eccentric beam is still in compression.
  8. (d) Bottom stress zero when M/Z equals the bottom prestress: eccentric M = 12.5 × 5.333 × 10⁶ = 66.7 kN·m; concentric M = 5 × 5.333 × 10⁶ = 26.7 kN·m.

Answer. Concentric: 5 N/mm² everywhere. Eccentric: 2.5 N/mm² tension at the top and 12.5 N/mm² compression at the bottom before load; under 56 kN·m it has 8 N/mm² (top) and 2 N/mm² (bottom), both compression, while the concentric beam has 5.5 N/mm² bottom tension and cracks. The same 400 kN placed 100 mm low lifts the zero-tension moment from 26.7 to 66.7 kN·m, 2.5 times.

Exam traps

  • Prestress does not make concrete strong in tension. It only stores compression; if the load's tension exceeds that compression plus fcr, the section still cracks.
  • Sign slips. Keep compression positive all the way. A tendon below the axis gives tension at the top before load (2.5 N/mm² in the worked example), and that top tension at transfer is a real check, not a rounding error.
  • Eccentricity is measured from the centroid of the concrete section, never from the top or bottom face.
  • The jacking force is not the force that stays. Use the force after losses for the service check.

6.2 High-strength concrete and high-tensile steel

Prestressing works only if both materials can take very high stress. The concrete must carry a large permanent compression without crushing or splitting, and the steel must hold a high tensile stress for decades while the concrete slowly shortens. That is why prestressed concrete pairs high-strength concrete, usually M40 and above, with high-tensile steel wires, strands and bars of about 1500 to 1900 N/mm² strength. The pair makes long spans, thin sections and crack-free service possible.

You need the properties of concrete and reinforcing steel (stress-strain curves, Ec = 5000√fck) and the idea of stored compression.

Why ordinary steel fails as a tendon

A tendon of area Ap stressed to fp applies a force P = Ap fp. After transfer the concrete keeps shortening: at once by elastic shortening, then for years by creep and shrinkage. The bonded tendon shortens with it, and every bit of shortening strain ε removes Esε of tendon stress. In a typical beam these three effects together remove 150 to 250 N/mm².

Δfp = m fc + φ m fc + εsh Es

Δfp is the tendon stress lost in N/mm²; m = Es/Ec is the modular ratio; fc is the concrete stress at tendon level in N/mm²; φ is the creep coefficient; εsh is the shrinkage strain; Es is the steel modulus, about 195,000 to 200,000 N/mm².

Mild steel Fe 250 can be stretched to perhaps 200 N/mm². A loss of 150 to 250 N/mm² wipes out almost all of it, which is why the early attempts with ordinary steel failed. High-tensile steel jacked to about 1400 N/mm² loses the same 150 to 250 N/mm² and keeps most of its force. The loss is fixed by the concrete, so the only way to keep the force is to start from a very high steel stress.

High-strength concrete

Grade M40 means a characteristic cube strength fck of 40 N/mm² at 28 days. IS 1343 asks for at least M40 in pre-tensioned work and permits M30 in post-tensioned work; M40 to M60 is common in bridges and precast plants. The strength at the age of transfer, fci, matters too, because the full prestress often arrives when the concrete is only a few days old.

Stronger concrete helps in five ways. It carries higher compression, so the section can be smaller. Its modulus Ec = 5000√fck is higher (31,600 N/mm² for M40 against 22,400 for M20), so elastic shortening is smaller. It creeps and shrinks less, so the time-dependent losses are smaller. It takes the high bearing stress under anchorage plates. And it gives better bond, so pre-tensioned strands transfer their force over a shorter length. Its flexural tensile strength 0.7√fck also rises with grade (4.4 N/mm² for M40), though it stays a small fraction of the cube strength.

High-tensile steel

Prestressing steel comes in three forms. A wire is a single cold-drawn wire, 3 to 8 mm, plain or indented (IS 1785 covers plain hard-drawn wire). A strand is a group of wires twisted together; the common 7-wire strand has six wires wound round a straight centre wire. Low-relaxation 7-ply strand (IS 14268) of 12.7 mm nominal size has an area of about 98.7 mm² and a breaking load of about 184 kN, so fpu ≈ 1860 N/mm². A bar is a solid alloy-steel bar, 20 to 40 mm, often threaded for nut anchorages (IS 2090); bars are weaker, around 1000 N/mm².

The stress-strain curve has no yield plateau. It is nearly straight up to about 70 to 80 percent of the strength, then bends gradually and breaks at a small total strain of a few percent. Because there is no yield point, the code uses a proof stress: the stress that leaves a small specified permanent strain after unloading, read with an offset line parallel to the straight part. The jacking stress is capped at about three-quarters of fpu so the tendon stays in the near-elastic range.

Relaxation is the slow loss of stress in steel held at constant length; it grows with the initial stress and the temperature. It is not creep: creep is strain growing under constant stress, relaxation is stress falling under constant strain. Low-relaxation strand, made by stretching the strand while it is heated, loses roughly half as much as ordinary stress-relieved strand. The numbers belong to losses of prestress.

Worked example

Question. A pre-tensioned beam of M40 concrete has a concrete stress at tendon level of fc = 8 N/mm² after transfer. Take Es = 195 kN/mm², creep coefficient φ = 1.6 and shrinkage strain 300 × 10⁻⁶. (a) Find the stress lost by elastic shortening, creep and shrinkage. (b) Compare what is left in a mild-steel tendon stretched to 200 N/mm² and in a 1860 N/mm² strand jacked to 0.75 fpu. (c) With a further relaxation loss of 35 N/mm², how many 12.7 mm strands (98.7 mm² each) give an effective force of 900 kN?

  1. Modulus of concrete: Ec = 5000√40 = 31,623 N/mm². Modular ratio m = 195,000/31,623 = 6.17.
  2. Elastic shortening: m fc = 6.17 × 8 = 49.3 N/mm².
  3. Creep: φ m fc = 1.6 × 6.17 × 8 = 78.9 N/mm².
  4. Shrinkage: εsh Es = 300 × 10⁻⁶ × 195,000 = 58.5 N/mm².
  5. (a) Total = 49.3 + 78.9 + 58.5 = 186.8 N/mm².
  6. (b) Mild steel: 200 − 186.8 = 13.2 N/mm² left, only 7 percent of the initial stress. Strand: jacking stress 0.75 × 1860 = 1,395 N/mm²; 1,395 − 186.8 = 1,208.2 N/mm² left, 87 percent.
  7. (c) Effective strand stress = 1,395 − 186.8 − 35 = 1,173.2 N/mm². Area needed = 900,000/1,173.2 = 767 mm². Strands = 767/98.7 = 7.77, so provide 8 strands (789.6 mm², effective force 926 kN).

Answer. Losses 49.3 + 78.9 + 58.5 = 186.8 N/mm². A mild-steel tendon would keep only 13.2 N/mm² (7 percent), useless as prestress; the strand keeps 1,208.2 N/mm² (87 percent). 8 strands of 12.7 mm give the 900 kN effective force.

Exam traps

  • Using 250 N/mm² or the yield stress of mild steel as if it applied to prestressing steel. High-tensile steel has no yield point; the code works with fpu and a proof stress.
  • Reading M40 as a tensile strength. It is the characteristic cube compressive strength at 28 days; the flexural tensile strength of M40 is only about 4.4 N/mm².
  • Writing that strong concrete "resists" shrinkage. Higher grades simply creep and shrink less, so the losses are smaller.
  • Confusing relaxation with creep: relaxation is stress loss in the steel at constant strain; creep is strain growth in the concrete at constant stress.
  • Mixing units: mm² × N/mm² gives N. Divide by 1000 only at the end to get kN.

6.3 Pre-tensioning and post-tensioning systems

There are two ways to stretch the tendon. In pre-tensioning the tendon is stretched first and the concrete is cast around it; in post-tensioning the concrete is cast first with a duct inside, and the tendon is stretched after the concrete has hardened. The order decides how the force enters the concrete: by bond along the tendon in pre-tensioning, through end anchorages in post-tensioning. Only a few universities teach the systems in detail, so this chapter is short.

You need the idea of transfer and the wires and strands used as tendons.

Pre-tensioning

The tendons are stretched between two heavy abutments at the ends of a casting bed and held by grips. Moulds are set round them, concrete is cast and cured, often with steam so that it gains strength in a day. When the concrete reaches its transfer strength, the tendons are released slowly from the abutments or cut. Each tendon tries to shorten, the concrete grips it, and the force passes into the concrete by bond over a short transmission length at each end. No anchorage is left in the member.

In the long-line method (Hoyer system) one bed, often 100 m or more, holds many units end to end, and one stressing operation serves them all: railway sleepers, poles, piles and hollow-core slabs are made this way. The tendons are usually straight; they can be deflected (harped) at hold-down points to follow the moment, but sharp curves are not possible. It needs a factory, and units must be transportable.

Post-tensioning

A duct (corrugated steel or plastic sheath) is fixed in the formwork to the planned profile, and the concrete is cast. After it hardens, the tendon is threaded through, stressed by a jack that pushes against the concrete face, and locked by an anchorage that bears on the concrete through a plate. The duct is then filled with cement grout, which protects the steel and bonds it to the concrete. Curved profiles are easy, which suits continuous beams, long bridge girders and site-cast slabs.

Systems differ in how they grip the tendon. Freyssinet uses a conical male wedge in a female cone to grip a ring of wires or strands. Magnel-Blaton grips pairs of wires in sandwich plates with flat wedges. Gifford-Udall anchors each wire with split cone wedges in a barrel. Lee-McCall stresses threaded high-tensile bars and locks them with nuts. Modern multi-strand systems use a wedge plate with one tapered three-piece wedge per strand.

6.4 Stress, strength and load balancing concepts

A prestressed beam can be analysed in three ways, and all three give the same stresses. The stress concept treats the concrete as an elastic material carrying two forces, prestress and load, and adds their stresses. The strength concept treats prestressed concrete like reinforced concrete: the tendon is the tension and the concrete the compression of an internal couple. The load-balancing concept turns the curved tendon into an upward load that cancels part of the real load. Each one is the quickest tool for a different question.

You need the fibre stress formula and simple beam moments (M = wL²/8 at midspan).

Stress concept: add the stresses

Treat the uncracked section as elastic. With compression positive, a tendon at eccentricity e below the centroid and a sagging moment M, the extreme fibre stresses are:

ftop = P/A − P e/Zt + M/Zt
fbot = P/A + P e/Zb − M/Zb

P is the prestress in N; A the concrete area in mm²; e the eccentricity in mm; Zt, Zb the section moduli for the top and bottom fibres in mm³ (equal for a rectangle, bD²/6); M the moment in N·mm; f in N/mm².

Two stages are checked with this formula. At transfer the force is the initial prestress Pi and the only load is the self-weight, which acts as soon as the beam cambers off its soffit. At service the force is the effective prestress Pe after losses and the moment is from the full load. The moment that makes fbot = 0 is the decompression moment Mdec = Zb(P/A + P e/Zb); cracking needs a further fcr Zb, with fcr = 0.7√fck. Between Mdec and the cracking moment the bottom is in tension but uncracked, and the formula still holds.

Strength concept: the internal couple

Think of the tendon force P as the tension T and the resultant concrete compression C = P as the other half of a couple. With no load, C sits at the tendon. A load moment M must be resisted by the couple, so C moves up by a lever arm

a = M/P

a is the distance in mm from the tendon to the line of action of C; M is the load moment in N·mm; P the prestress in N.

The line traced by C along the span is the pressure line, or C-line. Once its eccentricity ep from the centroid is known, the stresses are simply P/A ± P ep/Z. In prestressed concrete the steel force hardly changes with load, while the lever arm does; in reinforced concrete the lever arm hardly changes, while the steel force does. If the C-line stays inside the middle third of the section (the kern), no tension appears.

Load-balancing concept

A curved tendon presses up on the concrete along its whole length. For a parabolic tendon with sag e measured from the chord joining its two end points, over a span L, the upward load is uniform:

wp = 8 P e/L²

wp is the upward load in kN/m when P is in kN and e and L are in m. At the ends the tendon also pushes horizontally with P and vertically with its slope component, which goes straight into the supports.

If wp equals the downward load w, the beam carries only the axial P/A: no bending, no deflection. If wp is smaller, only the unbalanced load w − wp bends the beam, and its stresses and deflection are found as for an ordinary beam. Designers often balance the permanent load, so the beam stays level for most of its life. For a tendon whose ends sit at the centroid, the sag equals the midspan eccentricity.

Worked example

Question. A simply supported beam of span 10 m has a section 250 mm × 600 mm of M40 concrete. Find the midspan top and bottom stresses by (a) the stress concept, (b) the strength concept and (c) load balancing. (d) Find the midspan deflection and the sag that would balance the whole load.

  1. Section: A = 150,000 mm², Z = 250 × 600²/6 = 15 × 10⁶ mm³, I = 250 × 600³/12 = 4.5 × 10⁹ mm⁴.
  2. Load: self-weight 0.25 × 0.6 × 25 = 3.75 kN/m; total w = 3.75 + 6 = 9.75 kN/m; M = wL²/8 = 9.75 × 10²/8 = 121.875 kN·m.

  4. (b) Lever arm a = M/P = 121.875 × 10⁶/600,000 = 203.1 mm above the tendon, so C is 203.1 − 150 = 53.1 mm above the centroid. P ep/Z = 600,000 × 53.125/(15 × 10⁶) = 2.125. Top = 4 + 2.125 = 6.125; bottom = 4 − 2.125 = 1.875 N/mm².

Exam traps

  • Mixing signs halfway through. Fix compression positive at the start and keep it for every term and both fibres.
  • Ignoring the self-weight at transfer. It acts as soon as the beam cambers up, and it reduces the top tension.
  • Using the eccentricity at midspan as the sag when the tendon ends are not at the centroid. The sag is measured from the chord joining the tendon ends.
  • Treating wp as a downward load. A sagging tendon pushes up on the concrete.
  • Using the gross-section formula after the section has cracked. It holds up to the cracking moment, not beyond.

6.5 Losses of prestress

A tendon is jacked to a chosen force, but that force does not stay. The concrete shortens, the tendon rubs on its duct, the wedges draw in, and over the years the concrete creeps and shrinks while the steel relaxes. Each effect removes part of the jacking force P0; what is left is the effective prestress Pe, typically 75 to 85 percent of P0. The service checks of every prestressed member use Pe, so the losses must be worked out, not guessed.

You need the properties of the two materials (Es, Ec, creep, shrinkage, relaxation) and the two stressing systems.

Each loss is a loss of tendon stress Δf in N/mm²; multiply by Ap in mm² for a force in N, then divide by 1000 for kN. Everything below is for a bonded tendon whose strain follows the concrete at its level.

Immediate losses

Elastic shortening. At transfer the concrete at tendon level is compressed to fc and shortens by fc/Ec; the tendon shortens with it.

Δfes = m fc,   fc = Pi/A + Pi e²/I

m = Es/Ec; fc is the concrete stress at tendon level in N/mm² (the self-weight term Mge/I can be subtracted when the question gives it); Pi in N, e in mm, I in mm⁴.

In a post-tensioned member stressed with one jack, the first tendon loses force as each later tendon squeezes the concrete, and the last loses nothing. With n tendons the average loss is (n − 1)/(2n) of m fc, which many textbooks round to half.

Friction. A tendon pulled through a curved duct presses on the duct wall and rubs (curvature friction, coefficient μ); small unintended wiggles of the duct add wobble friction (coefficient k per metre):

Px = P0 e−(μα + kx)

Px is the force at x metres from the jack; α is the total angle change of the tendon over that length in radians; for a parabola of sag e and span L, α = 8e/L over the full span; e in the exponent is the base 2.718. μ and k come from the code or the duct supplier.

Anchorage slip. At lock-off the wedges draw in by δ, usually 3 to 6 mm. If friction is negligible the whole tendon length L shortens uniformly and Δf = Esδ/L. With friction, the draw-in is resisted by reverse friction and is used up over a setting length ls from the anchor: ls = √(EsApδ/p), where p is the friction loss of force per unit length. The force loss is 2pls at the anchor, falling to zero at ls. The uniform formula then badly understates the loss at the jack end.

Time-dependent losses

Δfcr = φ m fc    Δfsh = εsh Es

φ is the creep coefficient (ultimate creep strain divided by elastic strain); εsh is the shrinkage strain after transfer. Textbook problems on IS 1343 commonly take φ = 2.2, 1.6 and 1.1 for loading at 7 days, 28 days and one year; εsh = 300 × 10⁻⁶ for pre-tensioning and 200 × 10⁻⁶/log10(t + 2) for post-tensioning, t being the age in days at transfer. The 2012 code gives fuller tables by humidity and member size.

Relaxation is taken from the code table or the maker's test as a percentage of the initial stress; it rises steeply with initial stress, which is one reason the jacking stress is capped. Add all the stress losses, subtract from the initial stress, and multiply by Ap to get Pe. A rough first design may take total losses as 15 to 20 percent for post-tensioning and 20 to 25 percent for pre-tensioning, but an exam problem expects each loss computed.

Worked example

Question. A pre-tensioned beam 200 mm × 300 mm has 320 mm² of wires stressed to 1200 N/mm², at 50 mm below the centroid. Es = 210 kN/mm², Ec = 35 kN/mm², creep coefficient 1.6, shrinkage strain 300 × 10⁻⁶ and relaxation 3 percent of the initial stress. Ignore the self-weight. Find each loss, the total percentage loss and the effective force.

  1. A = 60,000 mm², I = 200 × 300³/12 = 4.5 × 10⁸ mm⁴, Pi = 320 × 1200 = 384,000 N, m = 210/35 = 6.
  2. Stress at tendon level: fc = 384,000/60,000 + 384,000 × 50²/(4.5 × 10⁸) = 6.4 + 2.133 = 8.533 N/mm².
  3. Elastic shortening: 6 × 8.533 = 51.2 N/mm².
  4. Creep: 1.6 × 6 × 8.533 = 81.9 N/mm².
  5. Shrinkage: 300 × 10⁻⁶ × 210,000 = 63 N/mm².
  6. Relaxation: 0.03 × 1200 = 36 N/mm².
  7. Total = 51.2 + 81.9 + 63 + 36 = 232.1 N/mm², that is 19.3 percent.

Answer. Losses: elastic shortening 51.2, creep 81.9, shrinkage 63 and relaxation 36 N/mm², total 232.1 N/mm² (19.3 percent); Pe = 309.7 kN from 384 kN.

Exam traps

  • Using degrees in μα. Convert to radians; α for a parabola over the full span is 8e/L.

  • Using P0 in the service check. Use Pe after all losses.

6.6 Flexural design of prestressed members

Flexural design is a set of checks at two moments in a member's life. At transfer the full initial prestress meets young concrete and only the self-weight resists it; at service the prestress has dropped by its losses and the full load acts. The fibre stresses at both stages must stay inside the code limits, and the section must also have enough ultimate moment, when the tendon stress rises far above the effective prestress.

You need the stress concept, the loss ratio η = Pe/Pi, and the limit state stress block 0.36 fck b xu with lever arm d − 0.42 xu.

The four stress conditions

Let fct and ftt be the permissible compression and tension at transfer, and fcw and ftw those at service (all as positive numbers). For a beam with the tendon below the centroid, the four conditions at the critical section are:

Transfer, top:   Pi/A − Pie/Zt + Mg/Zt ≥ −ftt
Transfer, bottom: Pi/A + Pie/Zb − Mg/Zb ≤ fct
Service, top:    Pe/A − Pee/Zt + Mt/Zt ≤ fcw
Service, bottom:  Pe/A + Pee/Zb − Mt/Zb ≥ −ftw

Mg is the self-weight moment, Mt = Mg + Mq the total service moment and Mq the live-load moment, in N·mm. Exam questions usually give the permissible stresses.

The tension limit at service depends on the type of member in IS 1343. Type 1: no tensile stress at service (liquid-retaining structures, severe exposure). Type 2: tension allowed but below the cracking stress, so no visible cracks. Type 3: cracks allowed, with the width limited, which is partial prestressing. Some syllabi write Class 1, 2 and 3 for the same idea.

Choosing the section and the eccentricity

Combining the transfer and service conditions at each fibre eliminates P and e and gives the smallest section moduli that can work:

Zt ≥ (Mq + (1 − η)Mg)/(fcw + η ftt)
Zb ≥ (Mq + (1 − η)Mg)/(η fct + ftw)

η is the loss ratio Pe/Pi, often 0.8 to 0.85; the other symbols are as above. The live-load moment, not the dead load, drives the section size, because the dead load is present at transfer to cancel part of the prestress.

With a section chosen, the transfer-top condition gives the largest eccentricity and the service-bottom condition the smallest. Plotting all four conditions as lines of 1/P against e is Magnel's graphical method.

Ultimate moment of resistance

At collapse the bonded tendon strains with the cracked concrete around it, so its stress rises from the effective value (about 0.6 fpu) towards its strength. The tendon strain is the effective prestrain plus the strain of the concrete at tendon level: εp = fpe/Es + 0.0035(d − xu)/xu. When that strain is well past the elastic range, the tendon reaches its design stress fpb = 0.87 fp. With the limit state stress block of IS 456:

0.36 fck b xu = fpb Ap,   Mu = fpb Ap(d − 0.42 xu)

fck in N/mm²; b the width and d the depth to the tendon centroid in mm; Ap in mm²; Mu in N·mm. IS 1343 also gives a table of fpb/0.87fp and xu/d against Apfp/(b d fck) for rectangular sections, which reduces fpb when that ratio is large.

Flanged sections are treated as reinforced T-beams. Compare Mu with the factored moment 1.5(Mg + Mq).

6.7 Anchorage zone (end block) design

At a post-tensioned anchorage, a force of hundreds or thousands of kilonewtons enters the concrete through a steel plate a few hundred millimetres square. The force cannot stay concentrated; within a length about equal to the depth of the member it spreads out until the stress is the ordinary P/A ± Pe/Z of the whole section. The region where it spreads is the end block or anchorage zone. The spreading pulls the concrete apart sideways, so every end block needs bearing area and transverse steel, and its design is a standard exam question.

You need post-tensioning anchorages and the force after jacking from losses of prestress.

How the force spreads

Follow the lines of compression from a small plate on the end face. They start bunched under the plate, fan out, and become parallel and evenly spaced about one end-block depth in from the face. Where the lines bend outward, their change of direction needs a tensile force at right angles to them, across the member: this is bursting tension. It is zero at the face, peaks at roughly 0.3 to 0.5 of the block depth behind it, and dies away by the end of the zone. The stress pattern was worked out by Magnel, Guyon and Rowe; a strut-and-tie model shows the same thing as two inclined struts held together by a transverse tie.

Close to the loaded face, around the edges of the plate and in the corners between widely spaced anchorages, there is a second, local tension called spalling tension. It is caused by the concrete next to the plate being dragged along by the deformation under it. It can break off the corners and cover, so a light mesh is placed right behind the face. Some books call the small region just under the plate the primary (local) zone and the larger spreading region the secondary (general) zone.

Bearing stress under the plate

The first check is that the concrete under the plate does not crush. The concrete round the loaded area confines it, so the permitted bearing stress rises with the ratio of the area available for spreading to the loaded area. IS 1343 limits it to:

fb = 0.48 fci √(Abr/Apun) ≤ 0.8 fci

fci is the cube strength at transfer in N/mm²; Apun is the punching area, the contact area of the anchorage plate in mm²; Abr is the bearing area, the largest area of the end face that is similar to and concentric with Apun, in mm². The actual stress is Pk/Apun, where Pk is the jacking force in N.

If the bearing stress is too high, enlarge the plate, use a stronger concrete at transfer, or spread the anchorages; stirrups do not cure a bearing failure.

Bursting force and its steel

IS 1343 gives the total bursting force across each principal direction of a square end block loaded by a square plate, as a fraction of Pk depending on the ratio of plate size 2ypo to block size 2yo in that direction:

2ypo/2yo0.30.40.50.60.7
Fbst/Pk0.230.200.170.140.11

The table is the straight line Fbst/Pk = 0.32 − 0.3(2ypo/2yo). A rectangular end block is checked in each direction separately, with the plate and block sizes in that direction. The steel is designed at 0.87 fy:

Ast = Fbst/(0.87 fy)

Fbst in N and fy in N/mm² give Ast in mm². Use closed links, mesh or a spiral round each anchorage.

  1. Check the bearing stress first.
  2. Find Fbst in both directions from the table.
  3. Provide closed links or mesh for the larger demand in each direction, spread through the bursting zone, from close behind the plate to about one end-block depth in.
  4. Add a surface mesh just behind the face for spalling, and lap the links with the beam stirrups beyond the zone.

Worked example

Question. A post-tensioned beam has an end block 400 mm wide and 500 mm deep. A single tendon with jacking force 1000 kN is anchored at the centre through a 200 mm × 200 mm plate. fci = 40 N/mm² and Fe 415 links are used. Check the bearing stress and design the bursting reinforcement.

  1. Bearing: Apun = 200 × 200 = 40,000 mm². The largest concentric square on a 400 mm wide face is 400 × 400, so Abr = 160,000 mm²; √(Abr/Apun) = 2.
  2. Permitted fb = 0.48 × 40 × 2 = 38.4, but not more than 0.8 × 40 = 32 N/mm², so fb = 32 N/mm². Actual = 1,000,000/40,000 = 25 N/mm² ≤ 32: safe.

6.8 Concordant cables and linear transformation

In a simply supported beam the tendon can be placed anywhere and the supports never notice: the beam is free to camber. A continuous beam is not free. Its intermediate supports stop the camber, so the prestress itself pushes and pulls on the supports and sets up extra secondary moments. Then the line of the concrete compression, the pressure line, no longer follows the tendon. A concordant cable is a profile that causes no secondary moments, and linear transformation is the rule that lets the designer move a tendon over the intermediate supports without moving the pressure line.

You need the pressure line and equivalent loads and the analysis of a propped or continuous beam by compatibility (deflection of a removed support set back to zero).

Primary, secondary and resultant moments

Take the prestress P with eccentricity e(x) below the centroid. The primary moment is P·e(x): the moment the tendon would cause if the beam were free. In a statically determinate beam it is the whole story, so every cable in a simply supported beam is concordant.

In a continuous beam the primary moment tries to bend the beam into a curve the supports do not allow. The supports react with secondary reactions, a set of forces that are in equilibrium on their own, and these cause secondary moments Ms. Because the reactions act only at supports, Ms is linear between supports. The resultant moment is the sum, and the pressure line sits at

ep(x) = e(x) − Ms(x)/P

ep is the eccentricity of the resultant concrete compression below the centroid in mm (negative means above); e is the tendon eccentricity; Ms is the secondary moment, sagging positive, in N·mm; P in N. The fibre stresses due to prestress are P/A ± P ep/Z.

A cable is concordant when Ms = 0 everywhere, so the pressure line coincides with the tendon. A useful test: any profile drawn to the shape of the bending moment diagram of some real load on the continuous beam, at any scale, is concordant.

Linear transformation

Move the tendon at the intermediate supports up or down, keep its eccentricity at the two end supports, and keep its shape (curvature) inside every span. Within each span the change is a straight line, so the profile has been changed by a linear transformation.

A straight-line change has no curvature, so the equivalent load of the tendon inside each span is unchanged. The only new loads are the kink forces exactly over the intermediate supports, which go straight into the supports without bending the beam. The resultant moment, and therefore the pressure line, is unchanged: whatever the transformation adds to the primary moment it takes away again as secondary moment. This is the theorem of linear transformation, usually credited to Guyon.

Two practical results follow. A linearly transformed concordant cable is still concordant only if the transformation is zero; any other transformation makes it non-concordant, with the same pressure line. And a non-concordant cable can always be transformed into the concordant one that runs along its own pressure line, which is what the worked example does. The designer is therefore free to choose the tendon position over the supports for cover, duct space and friction, while the concrete stresses stay those of the pressure line.

Two-span beam with a straight tendon

A straight tendon at constant eccentricity e in a two-span beam is the standard exam case, and it is not concordant. Freed from the middle support, the beam would camber up uniformly under the constant moment P e. Compatibility at the middle support (upward camber of the 2L beam, M0(2L)²/8EI, equal to the deflection of a central force R, R(2L)³/48EI) gives R = 3Pe/L downward there and 1.5Pe/L upward at each end. The secondary moment at the middle support is 1.5Pe, so the resultant there is 0.5Pe of the opposite sense: the pressure line rises from e below the centroid at the ends to e/2 above it at the middle support.

Worked example

Question. A two-span continuous beam, each span 10 m, of section 300 mm × 600 mm, has a straight tendon with P = 1000 kN at 100 mm below the centroid over its full length. (a) Find the secondary reactions and the secondary and resultant moments at the middle support. (b) Locate the pressure line. (c) Find the concrete stresses due to prestress at the middle support. (d) Find a concordant cable by linear transformation and show that the stresses do not change.

  1. Primary moment M0 = P e = 1000 × 0.1 = 100 kN·m along the whole beam, cambering it up.
  2. (a) Compatibility: R = 3M0/L = 3 × 100/10 = 30 kN downward at the middle support; 15 kN upward at each end (they balance, as secondary reactions must).
  3. Secondary moment at the middle support = 15 × 10 = 150 kN·m (sagging). Resultant = 150 − 100 = 50 kN·m sagging.
  4. (b) ep = e − Ms/P: at the ends 100 − 0 = 100 mm below; at the middle support 100 − 150 × 10⁶/(1000 × 10³) = 100 − 150 = −50 mm, that is 50 mm above the centroid; linear in between, ep = 100 − 15x mm with x in m from an end.
  5. (c) A = 180,000 mm², Z = 300 × 600²/6 = 18 × 10⁶ mm³. P/A = 5.556; P ep/Z = 1,000,000 × 50/(18 × 10⁶) = 2.778. With C above the centroid: top = 5.556 + 2.778 = 8.333, bottom = 5.556 − 2.778 = 2.778 N/mm².
  6. (d) Transform the tendon linearly: keep 100 mm below at the ends and raise it by 150 mm at the middle support, so e(x) = 100 − 15x mm in each span. It now lies on the pressure line.
  7. Check: its primary moment at the middle support is 1000 × (−0.05) = −50 kN·m, that is −50 kN·m sagging, equal to the old resultant, so Ms = 0: the new cable is concordant. The kink of 2 × 0.015 rad over the support pushes 1000 × 0.03 = 30 kN straight into the support.
  8. Stresses at the middle support with the new cable: P/A ± P × 50/Z = 8.333 and 2.778 N/mm², the same as in (c).

Answer. The straight cable is not concordant: it causes 30 kN down at the middle support and 15 kN up at each end, a secondary moment of 150 kN·m and a resultant of 50 kN·m at the middle support, so the pressure line runs from 100 mm below at the ends to 50 mm above at the middle. Moving the tendon there gives a concordant cable with the same stresses, 8.33 and 2.78 N/mm².

Exam traps

  • Saying a straight tendon with constant eccentricity is concordant in a continuous beam. It is not: it causes secondary reactions and moments (it is concordant only if e = 0).
  • Saying linear transformation leaves the reactions unchanged. It leaves the pressure line and the resultant moment unchanged; the secondary moments change.
  • Applying the transformation as one straight line over the whole beam and moving the end eccentricities. The ends stay fixed; the change is linear within each span.
  • Putting the secondary moment in as a curve. Secondary reactions act only at supports, so Ms is straight between supports.
  • Using e instead of ep for the stresses in a continuous beam. The concrete sees the pressure line.

6.9 Partial prestressing

Partial prestressing is the middle ground between full prestressing and ordinary reinforced concrete. The member carries both tendons and untensioned bars; the tendons supply enough prestress to keep it uncracked under its permanent load, and under the full service load fine, controlled cracks are allowed, which the bars keep narrow. It uses fewer tendons, less camber and simpler end blocks than full prestressing, and gives more ductility. You meet it in building floor beams, parking decks, many bridge decks and precast members where hairline cracks under peak load are acceptable.

You need the Type 1, 2 and 3 members and the ultimate moment, and crack control from serviceability of reinforced concrete beams.

What partial prestressing is

A fully prestressed member (Type 1 or Type 2) has no tension, or tension below the cracking stress, under the full service load. A partially prestressed member (Type 3) is allowed to crack under the full service load, with the crack width limited; IS 1343 limits it to 0.2 mm for ordinary exposure and 0.1 mm for aggressive exposure. The cracks close again when the live load goes, because the prestress pulls them shut. Reinforced concrete is the other end of the scale, with no prestress at all.

Two numbers describe where a member sits on that scale. The degree of prestressing compares the decompression moment with the service moment, λ = Mdec/Ms: λ ≥ 1 is full prestressing, 0 < λ < 1 partial, λ = 0 reinforced concrete. The partial prestressing ratio compares the strengths of the two steels at ultimate:

PPR = Ap fpb/(Ap fpb + As 0.87fy)

Ap fpb is the tendon force and As 0.87fy the force in the untensioned bars at the ultimate limit state, both in N. PPR = 1 for full prestressing, 0 for reinforced concrete.

Why use it

  • Full prestressing designed for a live load that rarely acts gives a large permanent camber, which can grow with creep and crack floor finishes.
  • Fewer tendons mean fewer anchorages, ducts and end-block links, and a cheaper, quicker job.
  • The untensioned bars add ductility and warning before failure, which full prestressing with few, highly stressed tendons can lack.
  • The bars can be placed where they are most useful: over supports, at openings, and for crack control near the faces.

The price is that the member is cracked under peak load: its stiffness is lower then, the tendon stress varies more, which matters for fatigue in bridges, and the crack width must be checked. Partial prestressing is not a weak form of full prestressing; it is a deliberate choice for members whose live load is a large, occasional part of the total.

Design checks

  1. Choose the prestress to balance the permanent load or to give decompression under the quasi-permanent load, using the effective force after losses.
  2. At transfer, check the stresses with Pi as for any prestressed member.
  3. At service, find the tension under the full load; if it exceeds the cracking stress, the member is Type 3 and the crack width must be checked on the cracked section (IS 1343 gives limiting hypothetical tensile stresses as a simpler check).
  4. At ultimate, put both steels in the force balance: 0.36 fck b xu = Ap fpb + As 0.87fy, and take moments about the compression with each steel at its own depth.
  5. Check that both steels have yielded, using strain compatibility, and check deflection and shear.

Exam traps

  • Calling partial prestressing a lower grade of full prestressing. It is a deliberate choice, with its own checks for crack width and fatigue.
  • Adding the tendon and bar moments without checking that both steels have yielded. Use strain compatibility; the lever arms are different.
  • Using the gross section for stresses after the beam has cracked. Crack width and stiffness need the cracked section.
  • Forgetting losses in the service check: the degree of prestressing uses Pe, not the jacking force.
  • Mixing up the two ratios: λ is a service quantity (moments), PPR an ultimate one (steel forces).

Takeaways

  1. Prestress stores compression in the concrete; the load must cancel it before tension, and then exceed 0.7√fck before a crack.
  2. Elastic shortening, creep and shrinkage take 150 to 250 N/mm² out of any bonded tendon, so only high-tensile steel near 1400 N/mm² keeps a useful force; strong concrete keeps those losses small.
  3. Pre-tensioning passes the force by bond in a factory bed; post-tensioning passes it through anchorages, with ducts, grout and curved profiles.
  4. The stress, strength and load-balancing concepts are three views of one beam and give the same fibre stresses.
  5. Losses are computed one by one: elastic shortening, friction and draw-in at once, creep, shrinkage and relaxation over years; service checks use Pe.
  6. Flexural design checks four fibre stresses at transfer (Pi, Mg) and service (Pe, Mt), then the ultimate moment with the tendon near 0.87fp.
  7. An end block needs a bearing check and closed links for the bursting force read from 2ypo/2yo, plus a spalling mesh at the face.
  8. In continuous beams prestress causes secondary moments; a concordant cable causes none, and linear transformation moves a tendon without moving the pressure line.

  10. Service check (Pe = 592.2 × 1100 = 651.4 kN at e = 750 − 425 = 325 mm, service load 16.375 kN/m):

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze A post-tensioned floor beam shows fine flexural cracks at midspan under service load, although the design calculation gave a small compression at the bottom fibre. Analyse the possible causes, show by numbers how sensitive the bottom stress is to the losses, and state what you would check on site. Take a 300 mm × 700 mm section, Pi = 1250 kN at e = 200 mm and a service moment of 300 kN·m, designed with η = 0.85. 13 marks

Points to cover. Bottom stress for the design η; Sensitivity to the real losses; Other causes: weaker concrete, restraint and temperature, overload, misplaced tendon; Site checks: jacking records, extensions, cores, tendon profile, grout; Remedies.

Model answer.

  1. Section: A = 210,000 mm², Z = 24.5 × 10⁶ mm³; M/Z = 300 × 10⁶/(24.5 × 10⁶) = 12.245 N/mm². Bottom stress = Pe/A + Pee/Z − M/Z.

  4. Losses larger than assumed: friction higher than the design μ and k (a badly aligned or dented duct), a larger draw-in than allowed for, early stressing of young concrete (more creep and shrinkage), or relaxation of ordinary instead of low-relaxation strand.
  5. Tension the calculation left out: restraint of shrinkage by stiff columns or walls, and temperature gradients (sun on the slab) can add 1 to 2 N/mm² of tension. Example: η = 0.65 gives −1.74; adding 1.5 N/mm² of restraint tension and a 10 percent overload (1.22 N/mm²) gives −4.47 N/mm², beyond fcr = 4.43 N/mm²: cracks.
  6. Other causes: concrete weaker than specified (lower fcr and Ec), the tendon placed higher than drawn (smaller e), heavier finishes or partitions than assumed, or a strand that slipped or broke during stressing.
  7. Site checks: jacking pressure and gauge calibration records; measured against computed extensions (a short extension shows high friction); lift-off tests at the anchorages to measure the force left; cover meter or GPR scans for the tendon height; cores and rebound tests for concrete strength; grout inspection for voids; crack-width measurement and monitoring with temperature.
  8. Remedies: if cracks are fine (under 0.2 mm) and stable, record and monitor, since the member may still satisfy Type 3; otherwise add external prestress or bonded plates, and seal the cracks against corrosion.
  9. Reading the crack pattern first: vertical cracks at midspan, widest at the soffit and closing upward, point to flexure (too little prestress or too much moment); cracks running along the tendon line point to a duct, grout or bursting problem; inclined cracks near the supports point to shear. Map the cracks, their widths and their change with load and temperature before choosing tests.
  10. Order of investigation: drawings and stressing records first (cheap), then non-destructive checks of cover, tendon height and concrete strength, then lift-off tests and, if needed, a load test with deflection and crack-width readings. Each step narrows the cause before any repair is chosen.

Evaluate For a building floor beam 350 mm × 750 mm of M40 concrete, span 10 m, dead load 10 kN/m (including self-weight) and live load 15 kN/m, compare a fully prestressed design (no tension under full load) with a partially prestressed design (decompression under dead load, bars for strength). Tendons at e = 275 mm (dp = 650 mm), 12.7 mm strands at an effective 1116 N/mm², bars Fe 500 at ds = 700 mm. Discuss which you would choose. 14 marks

Points to cover. Prestress needed for each design and number of strands; Service stresses and degree of prestressing; Ultimate strength; bars needed for the partial design; Camber and deflection under dead load; Economy, crack control, ductility; recommendation.

Model answer.

  1. Section: A = 262,500 mm², Z = 350 × 750²/6 = 32.81 × 10⁶ mm³, Z/A = 125 mm, e = 275 mm. Moments: Md = 10 × 10²/8 = 125 kN·m; Ml = 15 × 10²/8 = 187.5 kN·m; total 312.5 kN·m. Each strand gives 98.7 × 1116 = 110.15 kN.
  2. Full prestressing (bottom stress ≥ 0 at 312.5 kN·m): Pe ≥ M/(Z/A + e) = 312.5 × 10⁶/400 = 781.2 kN → 7.09 → 8 strands (881.2 kN).
  3. Partial prestressing (decompression under dead load): Pe ≥ 125 × 10⁶/400 = 312.5 kN → 3 strands (330.4 kN). Under full load the bottom stress is 1.259 + 2.769 − 9.524 = −5.5 N/mm², more tension than fcr = 4.43: it cracks under full live load (Type 3), λ = 0.42.
  4. Ultimate (Mu required = 1.5 × 312.5 = 468.75 kN·m). Full: T = 8 × 98.7 × 1618.2 = 1,277.7 kN, xu = 253.5 mm, Mu ≈ 694 kN·m, far more than needed (index 0.16, so the code table would trim fpb slightly).
  5. Partial: strands give Tp = 479.1 kN; solving 0.36 × 40 × 350 xu = Tp + Ts with Mu = Tp(650 − 0.42xu) + Ts(700 − 0.42xu) = 468.75 kN·m gives Ts = 296.3 kN, xu = 153.9 mm, As = 296,306/435 = 681 mm²: provide 3 bars of 18 mm (763 mm²).

The partial design balances the dead load, keeps the floor level, is cheaper and more ductile.

Glossary

Anchorage
The device of wedges, plates or nuts that holds a stressed tendon against the concrete.
Bursting tension
Transverse tension inside the end block caused by the spreading of the anchorage force.
Concordant cable
A cable in a continuous member that causes no secondary moments, so the pressure line follows it.
Cracking moment
The moment at which the tension face reaches the modulus of rupture.
Creep
Growth of strain in concrete under sustained stress.
Decompression moment
The moment at which the stress at the tension face just falls to zero.
Draw-in (anchorage slip)
The movement of the wedges into the anchorage at lock-off, which shortens the tendon.
Duct
A sheath cast into the concrete to leave a passage for a post-tensioned tendon.
Eccentricity
Distance from the centroid of the concrete section to the tendon.
Effective prestress
The force left in the tendon after all losses; used for service checks.
End block
The anchorage zone near a post-tensioned anchorage, about one member depth long, where the force spreads.
Grouting
Pumping cement grout into the duct after stressing, for bond and corrosion protection.
Jacking force
The tendon force while the jack holds it, before any loss.
Linear transformation
Moving a cable at the intermediate supports without changing its end positions or its curvature in each span; the pressure line stays put.
Load balancing
Choosing a tendon profile whose upward push cancels part or all of the downward load.
Loss ratio
η = Pe/Pi, typically 0.75 to 0.85.
Partial prestressing
Prestressing that allows cracks of limited width under full service load, with untensioned bars added (Type 3).
Pressure line
The line of action of the resultant compression in the concrete along the member.
Prestressed concrete
Concrete in which compression has been introduced before loading, by stretched high-tensile steel, to counter the tension the loads will cause.
Relaxation
Loss of stress in steel held at constant length.
Secondary moment
The moment in a continuous member caused by the support reactions that prestress induces.
Setting length
The length from the anchor over which draw-in is used up by reverse friction.
Shrinkage
Shortening of concrete as it dries, independent of load.
Spalling tension
Local tension near the loaded face round the plate edges and between anchorages.
Strand
Wires twisted together; the 7-wire strand has six wires round a centre wire.
Tendon
A wire, strand, bar or group of them that carries the prestressing force.
Transfer
The handing of the prestress to the concrete: release from the bed in pre-tensioning, lock-off at the anchorage in post-tensioning.
Transmission length
The length over which a pre-tensioned tendon passes its force to the concrete by bond.
