1.1 Origin of bioelectric potentials: resting and action potentials

A living cell is a tiny battery. Its membrane separates charged ions, so the inside sits at a small negative voltage compared with the outside. This resting voltage is near −70 mV. When a stimulus pushes the voltage past a threshold, the cell fires a brief positive spike called an action potential. Every bioelectric recorder — ECG, EEG, EMG — measures these voltages after they have spread through tissue. The cell model explains why the signals exist, why they are small, and why they are brief. This is the electrophysiology behind every recorder.

You need: school-level idea of a cell membrane and ions, plus the units below; electrode theory is in <a href='#electrode-theory-surface-needle-and-microelectrodes'>electrode theory and amplification in <a href='#bio-amplifiers-and-data-acquisition-systems'>bio-amplifiers.

The membrane is a battery

Every cell is wrapped in a lipid membrane. The membrane is a thin insulator. Ion channels are selective pores. The main ions are Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻ and large negatively charged proteins. The sodium-potassium pump uses energy from ATP to move three Na⁺ ions out and two K⁺ ions in for each cycle. This pump builds and maintains the concentration gradients: Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are high outside; K⁺ and the large negative proteins are high inside.

At rest, K⁺ leak channels are more open than Na⁺ leak channels. K⁺ leaves down its gradient, carrying positive charge out. That makes the inside negative. Na⁺ leaks in and the pump removes it, so the gradients do not run down.

Vm = Vinside − Voutside

Vm is membrane voltage; Vinside is the voltage inside the cell; Voutside is the voltage outside. A negative Vm means the inside is negative with respect to the outside.

For one ion, the Nernst equation gives the equilibrium voltage: the membrane voltage at which that ion's concentration push is exactly balanced by the electric pull, so it stops moving on balance.

Eion = (61.5 mV ÷ z) × log₁₀([ion]out ÷ [ion]in)   at 37 °C

Eion is the equilibrium (Nernst) voltage, inside with respect to outside, in mV; z is the ion's charge (+1 for Na⁺ and K⁺, −1 for Cl⁻); [ion]out and [ion]in are the concentrations outside and inside in mmol/L. 61.5 mV is 2.303 RT/F at body temperature.

The Goldman equation combines the ions, each weighted by how easily it crosses the membrane (its permeability P). At rest the membrane is most permeable to K⁺, so the resting potential lies close to EK; the small Na⁺ leak pulls it a little less negative. In a typical neuron the result is about −70 mV inside. Other cells differ: heart muscle rests near −90 mV.

Vm = 61.5 mV × log₁₀[(PK[K⁺]out + PNa[Na⁺]out + PCl[Cl⁻]in) ÷ (PK[K⁺]in + PNa[Na⁺]in + PCl[Cl⁻]out)]

PK, PNa and PCl are the relative permeabilities (only their ratio matters). Cl⁻ is negative, so its inside and outside concentrations swap places.

Action potential: threshold, spike and reset

An action potential is a rapid change in Vm that travels along a nerve or muscle fibre. It starts only when a stimulus depolarises the membrane to threshold. Threshold is a voltage, not a time; a common value is about −55 mV inside. Depolarisation means making Vm less negative. A stimulus below threshold produces a small local change that dies away. At threshold, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open.

Na⁺ enters, so Vm rises quickly toward positive values. This is the depolarisation phase. The peak is often near +30 mV. At the peak, Na⁺ channels inactivate and voltage-gated K⁺ channels open. K⁺ leaves the cell, carrying positive charge out, so Vm falls. This is repolarisation. K⁺ channels close slowly, so Vm often goes below the resting value. That undershoot is hyperpolarisation. The pump and leak channels then restore the resting state.

After a spike the membrane has a refractory period. During the absolute refractory period no new action potential can start. During the relative refractory period a stronger-than-normal stimulus can start one. The action potential is all-or-none: once threshold is crossed, the height and shape are fixed for that cell. A stronger stimulus does not make a taller spike; it makes more spikes per second. This is why nerve signals carry information in timing and frequency.

Reading the waveform and units

A plot of Vm against time shows rest as a flat line, a sharp rise at threshold, a peak, a fall and a small undershoot. The x-axis is time in milliseconds (ms); 1 ms = 0.001 s. The y-axis is membrane voltage in millivolts (mV); 1 mV = 0.001 V. A typical spike lasts 1–2 ms in a nerve, longer in some muscle cells.

The figure marks rest at −70 mV, threshold at −55 mV, the peak at +30 mV and the undershoot to about −80 mV. Notice that threshold is above rest: it is a depolarised value. The spike amplitude, from rest to peak, is 100 mV; measured from threshold it would be only 85 mV. Recorders do not see the cell directly. An electrode converts ionic current to electronic current (<a href='#electrode-theory-surface-needle-and-microelectrodes'>electrode theory), and a bio-amplifier raises the small differential voltage (<a href='#bio-amplifiers-and-data-acquisition-systems'>bio-amplifiers). The ECG, EEG and EMG chapters apply this cell source at body-surface scale.

Worked example

Question. A neuron has resting potential −70 mV inside, threshold −55 mV inside, and peak +30 mV inside. (a) Find the depolarisation needed to reach threshold. (b) Find the spike amplitude, from the resting potential to the peak.

  1. Depolarisation to threshold is threshold minus resting voltage: (−55 mV) − (−70 mV) = 15 mV.
  2. Spike amplitude is peak minus resting voltage: (+30 mV) − (−70 mV) = 100 mV.
  3. The two minus signs in each subtraction matter: subtracting a negative voltage adds its magnitude.

Answer. The depolarisation needed is 15 mV, and the spike amplitude from rest to peak is 100 mV.

Worked example 2

Question. A neuron has these concentrations (mmol/L), inside and outside: K⁺ 140 and 5, Na⁺ 15 and 145, Cl⁻ 10 and 110. Take 61.5 mV for 2.303 RT/F at 37 °C. (a) Find the Nernst voltages EK, ENa and ECl. (b) With relative permeabilities PK : PNa : PCl = 1 : 0.04 : 0.45, find the resting potential from the Goldman equation.

  1. EK = 61.5 × log₁₀(5 ÷ 140) = 61.5 × (−1.447) = −89.1 mV.
  2. ENa = 61.5 × log₁₀(145 ÷ 15) = 61.5 × 0.985 = +60.6 mV.
  3. ECl: z = −1, so ECl = −61.5 × log₁₀(110 ÷ 10) = −61.5 × 1.041 = −64.1 mV.
  4. Goldman numerator (outside K⁺ and Na⁺, inside Cl⁻): 1 × 5 + 0.04 × 145 + 0.45 × 10 = 5 + 5.8 + 4.5 = 15.3.
  5. Goldman denominator (inside K⁺ and Na⁺, outside Cl⁻): 1 × 140 + 0.04 × 15 + 0.45 × 110 = 140 + 0.6 + 49.5 = 190.1.
  6. Vm = 61.5 × log₁₀(15.3 ÷ 190.1) = 61.5 × log₁₀(0.0805) = 61.5 × (−1.094) = −67.3 mV.
  7. Check: −67.3 mV lies between EK (−89.1 mV) and ENa (+60.6 mV) and close to EK, because K⁺ has the highest permeability.

Answer. EK = −89.1 mV, ENa = +60.6 mV, ECl = −64.1 mV; the Goldman resting potential is −67.3 mV, close to the usual −70 mV.

Exam traps

  • Sign slip: resting potential is negative inside; writing +70 mV reverses the cell.
  • Threshold is a depolarised voltage near −55 mV, not the resting voltage; a stimulus below threshold does not fire.
  • All-or-none: a stronger stimulus increases firing rate, not spike height.
  • Unit slip: mV and ms are thousandths; 100 mV = 0.1 V, and 2 ms = 0.002 s.
  • Spike amplitude is peak minus rest (100 mV here), not peak minus threshold (85 mV). Peak-to-peak runs from the highest to the lowest point, so for this trace it includes the undershoot and is 110 mV.

1.2 Electrode theory: surface, needle and microelectrodes

An electrode is the boundary where ion current in the body becomes electron current in a wire, through a double layer of separated charge at the metal surface. That layer adds a DC half-cell potential and an impedance of its own. Whether a millivolt ECG or a microvolt EEG reaches the amplifier cleanly depends on how well both are handled. This chapter covers the interface, its equivalent circuit, and the three families used in practice: surface, needle and microelectrodes.

You need resting and action potentials for where the bio-potential comes from, and the idea of impedance as resistance and capacitance combined.

Ionic current becomes electronic current

Nerve and muscle currents are carried by ions, mainly Na⁺, K⁺ and Cl⁻, in salt water. Wires carry electrons. The electrode converts one into the other at its metal surface by a chemical reaction that gives an electron to an ion or takes one from it.

The reaction leaves electrons on the metal and a sheet of ions in the liquid just outside it. These opposite charge sheets form the double layer, which stores charge like a capacitor. Its steady voltage, measured against a standard hydrogen electrode, is the half-cell potential of that metal–electrolyte pair.

A recording uses two electrodes, each with its own half-cell potential. Their difference reaches the amplifier as an offset even when the tissue is silent. A matched pair gives a few millivolts; a mismatched pair gives tens to hundreds of millivolts, far more than a 1 mV ECG. This is the electrode offset potential.

When current flows, the half-cell potential shifts from equilibrium. That shift is polarisation, and a strongly polarised electrode drifts with current and time, ruining a long recording. Ag/AgCl in a chloride gel is almost non-polarisable: Ag ⇌ Ag⁺ + e⁻ reverses easily, so the offset stays small and steady. It is the standard for ECG, EEG and EMG.

Equivalent circuit and impedance matching

Model one electrode as a DC source Ehc in series with a resistance Rs, shunted by a capacitance Cd and a leakage resistance Rd. Rs is the gel and skin path, often tens of kΩ on unprepared skin. Cd is the double layer and makes electrode impedance fall as frequency rises. Rd is the path by which charge crosses the interface through the reaction.

Vin = Vbio × Zin ÷ (Zin + Ze)

Vbio is the bio-potential generated by the tissue in volts; Ze is the electrode impedance at that frequency in ohms; Zin is the amplifier input impedance in ohms; Vin is the voltage actually present at the amplifier terminals.

To keep the loss under 1%, Zin must be at least 100 times Ze. A gelled surface electrode sits at a few kΩ, so a 1 MΩ input is plenty. A microelectrode at 10 MΩ needs 1 GΩ or more, which is why microelectrode amplifiers are built differently (see bio-amplifiers and data acquisition).

Impedance is frequency dependent, so a mismatch shows up differently in the ECG and EEG bands. And the two electrodes of a differential pair should have equal impedance: unequal impedance turns part of the 50 Hz common-mode pick-up into a differential voltage, which the amplifier then amplifies as if it were signal, so the effective rejection falls. Abrade the skin and use matched electrodes.

Surface electrodes

Surface electrodes sit on the skin and record the summed activity of many cells below. Four shapes cover most clinical use.

  • Plate or disc: an Ag/AgCl snap on a foam pad with gel, pre-gelled and disposable, stuck on the chest for ECG.
  • Reusable plate: metal or rubber held by a strap; gel is applied each time and the electrode is cleaned between patients.
  • Suction: a metal cup pulled on by a rubber bulb; fast to attach on a limb or foetal scalp, easily dislodged.
  • Floating: the metal is recessed behind a gel column, so the double layer does not move when the metal or skin moves; motion artefact drops, which suits Holter and stress testing.

Disposable costs more per electrode but saves setup time and gives repeatable impedance. Reusable is cheaper per use, but the gel dries and the offset drifts over a long session.

Unipolar or bipolar. A unipolar arrangement uses one active electrode against a reference, either a distant electrode or an average of several such as the Wilson central terminal, and sees a wide region. A bipolar arrangement uses two similar electrodes close together, sees a small region, and rejects what is common to both. Most ECG chest leads are unipolar against the Wilson terminal; limb leads I, II and III are bipolar pairs (see ECG recording).

Needle and microelectrodes

Needle electrodes pass through the skin into muscle. A monopolar needle is a fine stainless steel wire insulated except at the tip, used with a surface reference; the pickup region is wide. A concentric needle has a hollow cannula with a fine wire inside: wire active, cannula reference, so the pickup volume is small and sharp. Concentric needles record single motor units, which clinical EMG needs (see EEG and EMG recording).

Microelectrodes record from one cell. A glass micropipette is a capillary pulled to a 0.1–1 µm tip and filled with 3 M KCl; current crosses the tip by ion movement, so tip resistance is high, roughly 10–100 MΩ, and a larger tip gives lower resistance. A metal microelectrode is tungsten or platinum–iridium etched to a point and insulated except at the tip; its resistance is about 1–10 MΩ, suited to extracellular spike recording.

High tip resistance costs bandwidth and signal. Tip resistance with cable capacitance forms a low-pass filter, and the divider above throws signal away unless Zin is very large. Keep the lead short and put the first amplifier stage close to the electrode. Every electrode is also a conductive path into the body, and leakage current through an intracardiac electrode can cause microshock; its limits and tests are covered under electrical safety and testing.

Worked example

Question. A glass microelectrode records a cell and has a tip resistance of 10 MΩ. The amplifier input impedance is 1 GΩ. What fraction of the bio-potential reaches the amplifier input, and what percentage is lost in the electrode?

  1. Write the data with units: Ze = 10 MΩ = 10 × 10⁶ Ω and Zin = 1 GΩ = 1 × 10⁹ Ω.
  2. Electrode and input form a divider, Vin = Vbio × Zin ÷ (Zin + Ze); the fraction arriving is Zin ÷ (Zin + Ze).
  3. Add them: Zin + Ze = 1 × 10⁹ + 10 × 10⁶ = 1.01 × 10⁹ Ω.
  4. Divide: 1 × 10⁹ ÷ 1.01 × 10⁹ = 0.9901, so 99.01% arrives.
  5. The lost share is 1 − 0.9901 = 0.0099, that is 0.99%.

Answer. 0.9901 of the bio-potential (99.01%) reaches the amplifier and 0.99% is lost across the 10 MΩ tip resistance.

Exam traps

  • Do not treat the electrode offset as real bio-potential. It is a DC half-cell potential and must be blocked by the amplifier or cancelled by matched electrodes.
  • A good electrode does not have zero half-cell potential. Matching the two electrodes is what cancels it; a mismatched pair leaves a standing DC that can saturate the amplifier.
  • High electrode impedance also raises thermal noise and, if the two electrodes differ, converts common-mode mains into a differential voltage, so CMRR falls.
  • Microelectrode tip resistance is the pipette's own series resistance, not the cell membrane resistance. Candidates confuse the two in numerical problems.
  • Unipolar and bipolar describe the arrangement, not the shape. The same disc is unipolar in one lead and part of a bipolar pair in another; read which one the question describes.

1.3 Physiological transducers and load cells

A physiological transducer turns a body variable — force, pressure, temperature or light — into an electrical signal that a recorder can read. The load cell is the common force version: a metal element bends under load, strain gauges change resistance, and a Wheatstone bridge turns that tiny change into millivolts. You meet it in weighing scales, hospital beds, infusion pumps and gait force plates. This chapter explains how to choose a transducer, compares six transducer families, and follows one quarter-bridge load cell from 1000 µε to 2.5 mV.

You need basic circuit laws and the Wheatstone bridge; bio-signal origin is in resting and action potentials and electrode matching in electrode theory.

Selection criteria

Selection criteria. A physiological transducer must not disturb the body and must give a usable electrical copy of the variable. Its range is the span of input over which it meets its stated specification; the overload limit, the input it survives without damage, is usually higher. Sensitivity is output change per unit input, for example 2 mV per 100 g. Linearity says the sensitivity stays constant over the range. Hysteresis is the difference between readings when you increase and then decrease the input; a good transducer has little hysteresis. Accuracy is closeness to the true value. Resolution is the smallest change it can show. Loading effect is the disturbance the transducer itself causes: a stiff force sensor takes load from tissue, a cold probe cools skin. Biocompatibility means the material touching the patient is safe and cleanable. Range and accuracy should match the clinical question; an extra digit of resolution is useless if hysteresis is large. Some syllabi call this physiological transducers and selection criteria.

Transducer families

The main families differ in what changes. A resistive transducer changes resistance with strain, temperature or light; the strain gauge is one. A capacitive transducer changes capacitance when plates move or the dielectric changes; it suits small displacement and pressure. An inductive transducer changes inductance through a moving core, useful for displacement. A piezoelectric crystal gives charge when squeezed; it measures dynamic force and vibration, not steady weight because charge leaks away. A photoelectric transducer changes current with light; it appears in pulse oximetry and optical counting. An electrochemical transducer converts a chemical concentration into voltage or current; glucose sensors use it, covered under biosensors. Temperature sensors are a short comparison: a thermistor is resistive and sensitive but nonlinear; an RTD is resistive and stable; a thermocouple is a small voltage from two metals and needs cold-junction correction. Choose the family by the variable, the bandwidth and the loading the body can tolerate.

Strain gauges and load cells

A strain gauge is a thin metal or semiconductor foil bonded to a spring element. When the element stretches, the foil lengthens and narrows, so its resistance rises.

GF = (ΔR / R) / ε

GF is the gauge factor, dimensionless; ΔR is the change in resistance in Ω; R is the unstrained resistance in Ω; ε is strain, the fractional change in length, dimensionless.

Metal foil gauges have GF near 2; semiconductor gauges are higher but more temperature-sensitive. The gauges sit in a Wheatstone bridge of four arms. In a quarter bridge one arm is an active gauge and the other three are fixed resistors. In a half bridge two gauges are active, one in tension and one in compression. In a full bridge all four are active.

ΔV = Vex × GF × ε / 4

ΔV is the bridge output in V for a quarter bridge; Vex is the excitation voltage in V; GF is the gauge factor; ε is strain.

A half bridge gives twice this output, a full bridge four times. A load cell has a machined beam, column or diaphragm; gauges are bonded and sealed; a full bridge is usual. Its output is quoted in mV/V, so it scales with excitation. Load cells weigh patients, medicines and food, and force platforms use them in gait and balance studies (see biomechanical measurement). The next stage is the bio-amplifier and ADC (see bio-amplifiers and data acquisition).

Worked example

Question. A quarter-bridge load cell uses 350 Ω strain gauges, excitation 5 V and gauge factor 2.0. The strain is 1000 µε (1×10⁻³). Find the bridge output in volts and millivolts.

  1. Given: Vex = 5 V, GF = 2.0, ε = 1000 µε.
  2. Convert strain: 1000 µε = 1000 × 10⁻⁶ = 1 × 10⁻³.
  3. Use the quarter-bridge formula: ΔV = Vex × GF × ε / 4.
  4. Substitute: ΔV = 5 × 2.0 × 1 × 10⁻³ / 4 = 0.010 / 4 = 0.0025 V.
  5. Convert to millivolts: 0.0025 V × 1000 = 2.5 mV.

Answer. The bridge output is 0.0025 V, which is 2.5 mV.

Worked example 2

Question. A hospital bed scale uses a full-bridge load cell rated 2 mV/V at its 200 kg capacity, excited by 10 V. The bridge output goes to an amplifier of gain 200 and a 12-bit ADC with a 5 V range. A patient of 75 kg lies on the bed. Find the bridge output, the amplifier output, the ADC count and the weight that one count stands for.

  1. Full-scale bridge output = rated output × excitation = 2 mV/V × 10 V = 20 mV at 200 kg.
  2. The output is proportional to load: at 75 kg it is 20 mV × 75 ÷ 200 = 7.5 mV.
  3. Amplifier output = 200 × 7.5 mV = 1.5 V. At full load it would be 200 × 20 mV = 4.0 V, inside the 5 V ADC range.
  4. ADC count = 1.5 V ÷ 5 V × 4096 = 1228.8, so the ADC reads 1228 (it truncates).
  5. One count is 5 V ÷ 4096 = 1.22 mV at the ADC. Since 4.0 V stands for 200 kg, one count stands for 200 kg × 1.22 mV ÷ 4.0 V = 0.061 kg, about 61 g.

Answer. Bridge output 7.5 mV, amplifier output 1.5 V, ADC reading 1228 counts, and a resolution of about 61 g per count.

Exam traps

  • In a quarter bridge only one gauge is active, so its output is one quarter of a full bridge for the same strain; a half bridge is one half.
  • Convert microstrain to strain before using the formula: 1000 µε = 1×10⁻³, not 1000.
  • A load cell's millivolt output depends on excitation voltage; doubling Vex doubles the output, so a reading without Vex is incomplete.
  • High sensitivity is not the same as high accuracy; hysteresis, linearity and loading effect can dominate.
  • Piezoelectric sensors measure dynamic force, not steady weight; do not use them for a static load cell question.

1.4 Bio-amplifiers and data acquisition systems

Biopotentials at the skin are tiny: about 1 mV for ECG, tens of microvolts for EEG. They sit on a much larger shared voltage, mostly 50 Hz mains and electrode offset. A bio-amplifier amplifies the difference between two electrodes and rejects the shared part. Its output goes to a data acquisition system (DAS), which samples at fixed instants and converts each sample to a number for storage, filtering and display.

You need: resting and action potentials for signal sizes, electrode theory for electrode impedance and half-cell potential, and basic op-amp ideas (virtual short, negative feedback).

Small difference on a large shared voltage

Each electrode carries the biopotential, a half-cell offset up to a few hundred mV DC, and 50 Hz mains pickup. ECG is about 1 mV, EEG 10 to 100 µV, EMG a few mV. Mains pickup can exceed the signal a hundredfold.

The wanted part is Vd = V1 − V2. The shared part is Vcm = (V1 + V2)/2. A single-ended amplifier of gain G gives G(Vcm + Vd/2), so interference buries the signal. A differential amplifier subtracts first, cancelling the shared part. CMRR measures how well.

Ad = Vout / Vd,   Acm = Vout / Vcm,   CMRR = Ad / Acm,   CMRR(dB) = 20 log₁₀(Ad / Acm)

Ad is differential gain (no unit); Acm is common-mode gain (no unit); CMRR is the common-mode rejection ratio as a plain number; CMRR(dB) is the same ratio in decibels. 20 is used because both are amplitude ratios.

CMRR matters more than raw gain because the shared part is much bigger. Typical ECG front-end targets:

From a resistor-matched difference amplifier to the instrumentation amplifier

A one-op-amp differential amplifier has input impedance set by its input resistors, often near 10 kΩ, far below electrode impedance. Its CMRR depends on resistor matching: 0.1 % resistors give only about 50 dB, short of the 80 to 120 dB an ECG front end needs.

The instrumentation amplifier fixes both with three op-amps. Two input buffers sense V1 and V2, giving input impedance above 10⁹ Ω. One external resistor Rg sets gain. A third stage subtracts the buffer outputs. On-chip trimmed resistors make 100 dB CMRR routine.

G = 1 + 2R / Rg

G is differential gain of the input stage (no unit); R is the feedback resistor inside each buffer, in ohms; Rg is the single external gain-setting resistor, in ohms. Lower Rg gives higher gain.

Input offset voltage is the small DC difference between op-amp inputs, and gain multiplies it. A 2 mV offset at gain 1000 becomes 2 V and saturates the amplifier. Keep first-stage gain modest, remove offset with a high-pass filter, then add gain later.

Noise is also multiplied by gain. Op-amp noise is quoted in nV/√Hz, so total noise grows with √bandwidth; a 150 Hz ECG channel is quieter than a 10 kHz one. Gain-bandwidth product limits bandwidth: a 1 MHz op-amp gives 1 kHz at gain 1000.

An isolation barrier (transformer or optocoupler) separates the patient circuit from mains. The driven right leg circuit senses common-mode voltage at the inputs, inverts and amplifies it, and drives it back through the right-leg electrode. Effective CMRR rises by 20 to 40 dB.

Filters: three jobs, three filters

The high-pass removes DC electrode offset (hundreds of mV) and baseline wander from breathing and sweat. Diagnostic ECG uses 0.05 Hz, monitoring 0.5 Hz. Too high a corner sags the ST segment, a diagnostic error.

The low-pass removes EMG noise and high-frequency interference: diagnostic ECG 150 Hz, monitor 40 Hz, EEG 70 Hz, EMG 500 Hz. It is also the anti-alias filter, so it must be analog and before the sampler.

The notch at mains frequency (50 Hz in India) removes hum that CMRR missed. A narrow notch with Q 20 to 30 rings after sharp edges and can distort QRS, so some recorders let the user switch it off.

Order: modest gain, high-pass, more gain, low-pass, notch, ADC driver. No stage should saturate.

DAS: sample, hold, convert, store

A DAS is all hardware and software between filtered analog voltage and stored number: multiplexer, sample-and-hold, analog-to-digital converter (ADC), microcontroller (MCU), storage and display. The multiplexer lets one ADC serve many channels, e.g. eight EEG electrodes; it needs settling time so charge does not leak between channels.

The sample-and-hold closes a switch, charges a capacitor to the input, then opens. The ADC converts a steady value, and the sampling instant is fixed, keeping the time grid even.

LSB = VFS / 2ⁿ

LSB is the smallest voltage step the ADC can show, in volts; VFS is the full-scale input range, in volts; n is resolution in bits. The input-referred step is LSB divided by amplifier gain.

A 12-bit ADC with a 3.3 V range has LSB = 3.3/4096 = 0.806 mV. At gain 1000 one count is 0.806 µV at the electrode, well below ECG size. Real resolution is often poorer than n bits because of noise and reference drift; the useful figure is effective number of bits.

Sampling rate follows Nyquist: fs ≥ 2 × the highest frequency present. In practice 2 to 5 times that is common: ECG 500 samples per second for a 150 Hz band, EEG 250 to 500 for 70 Hz, EMG 1 to 2 thousand for 500 Hz. Anything above fs/2 folds back as a false lower frequency, and no digital filter can separate it, so the analog low-pass comes first. The driver reads the ADC over a serial bus, fills a ring buffer, applies digital filtering and calibration from a 1 mV test pulse, checks lead-off, and streams time-stamped frames to display and storage.

Worked example

Question. A bio-amplifier has differential gain 1000 and CMRR 80 dB. The differential input is 1 mV and common-mode input is 50 mV. Find output signal, output interference, and interference as a percentage of signal.

  1. Convert CMRR: CMRR = 10^(80/20) = 10⁴.
  2. Signal output = 1000 × 1 mV = 1 V.
  3. Acm = Ad / CMRR = 1000 / 10⁴ = 0.1.
  4. Interference output = 0.1 × 50 mV = 5 mV.
  5. Interference = (5 mV / 1000 mV) × 100 = 0.5 %.

Answer. Output signal is 1 V; output interference is 5 mV, which is 0.5 % of the signal.

Exam traps

  • A CMRR of 80 dB is a ratio of 10⁴, not 80. Convert with CMRR = 10^(dB/20) before computing common-mode output.
  • Voltage gain dB uses 20 log₁₀(A); CMRR dB uses 20 log₁₀(Ad/Acm). Both are amplitude ratios; 10 is only for power.
  • The high-pass removes electrode offset and baseline wander, not 50 Hz mains. Only the notch, or high CMRR with driven right leg, removes mains hum.
  • The anti-alias filter must be analog and before sample-and-hold. Once aliased, no digital filter can separate it from a real signal.
  • ADC resolution is VFS/2ⁿ, not VFS/(2ⁿ − 1). 12 bits at 3.3 V gives 0.806 mV per count.

1.5 ECG recording system and vector cardiography

The heart makes about 1 mV at the skin. The ECG recording system picks this up with electrodes, amplifies it with a differential bio-amplifier, filters it, and draws it on a grid with fixed voltage and time scales. Every clinic, ICU, ambulance and home Holter uses this chain. Vectorcardiography draws the same activity as a loop in space, showing the heart's electrical axis more directly than twelve separate traces.

You need: resting and action potentials on cardiac potentials, electrode theory on electrode impedance, and bio-amplifiers and data acquisition on bio-amplifiers.

Twelve views of one signal: the standard leads

A lead is a view: a fixed pair of electrodes with defined polarity, so the same heart looks different from different angles. A bipolar lead measures voltage between two active electrodes. A unipolar lead measures one exploring electrode against a common reference built from the others.

The three standard limb leads use right arm (RA), left arm (LA) and left leg (LL). Lead I = LA − RA, II = LL − RA, III = LL − LA. Adding I and III gives LL − RA, which is lead II. This is Einthoven's law: II = I + III, a check on any limb-lead recording.

RA, LA and LL sit at the corners of an equilateral triangle with the heart at its centre, the Einthoven triangle. Each lead therefore looks along a fixed axis in the frontal plane.

The recording chain and its two scales

The signal path is fixed: electrodes, lead selector, buffer, bio-amplifier, filters, ADC, display. The buffer has input impedance above 10 MΩ so electrode impedance does not divide down the signal. The bio-amplifier is differential, gain near 1000, common-mode rejection ratio above 100 dB to kill 50 Hz mains pickup. Filters remove baseline drift below 0.05 Hz and muscle noise above 150 Hz. A 12-bit ADC converts the trace for storage and display.

Two scales make the paper readable. Voltage: 1 mV = 10 mm at standard gain (10 mm/mV). Time: standard paper speed 25 mm/s, so 1 mm = 0.04 s and a large 5 mm square = 0.2 s. At 60 beats per minute the RR interval is 1 s = 25 mm.

time = length / speed   and   heart rate = 60 / RR

time is in s; length is the distance on paper in mm; speed is the paper speed in mm/s (25 or 50); RR is the interval between two QRS complexes in s; heart rate is in beats per minute. At 25 mm/s, heart rate = 1500 / (RR in mm).

  1. Switch on, let it settle, press the 1 mV button and confirm the calibration bar is exactly 10 mm tall.
  2. Select the lead, check the trace is free of 50 Hz fuzz, record.
  3. Measure widths in mm, divide by paper speed to get seconds.
  4. Measure heights in mm, divide by 10 to get mV.

The calibration pulse is the machine's honesty check. On maintenance rounds, wipe electrodes, inspect the patient cable for breaks, and confirm the bar is 10 mm on every channel.

Vectorcardiography (VCG) records the same dipole but keeps its direction. The Frank lead system uses seven electrodes (five around the chest, one on the back of the neck, one on the left leg), plus a ground electrode if used, to produce three orthogonal components X, Y and Z: X to the left (positive at the left), Y head to foot (positive at the foot), Z front to back (positive at the back). Plotting X against Y gives the frontal loop, X against Z the horizontal loop, Y against Z the sagittal loop. At each instant the tip of the heart vector traces a path, so one QRS complex becomes one closed QRS loop. The P loop is small, the QRS loop large, the T loop intermediate. Cardiologists read the loop's rotation direction, area and the magnitude and angle of the maximum vector.

  • Paper speed sets time and calibration sets voltage. A trace can look normal and still be wrong if the 1 mV bar is not 10 mm tall.
  • Einthoven's law is II = I + III. Reversing signs, or writing I = II + III, is the most common slip.

  • aVR is normally negative for P, QRS and T because the heart vector points away from the right arm. A positive aVR is a finding, not a normal variant.
  • VCG is not a different signal. It uses orthogonal Frank leads X, Y and Z on the same electrical activity, so a normal ECG and a normal VCG must agree.

1.6 EEG and EMG recording

EEG and EMG look similar on a screen but come from different scales. EEG records summed postsynaptic potentials of cortical neurons, often 10–100 microvolts (µV), through scalp electrodes. EMG records action potentials of muscle fibres, often 0.1–5 millivolts (mV), through surface or needle electrodes. EMG is typically 20–50 times larger than EEG (10–500 times across the full ranges), so each branch needs its own gain, band and filter. EEG uses gain near 10⁴ and a 0.5–70 Hz band; EMG uses lower gain, often 10²–10³, and a 20–500 Hz band. Clinics use these recordings for brain state, muscle activation and fatigue checks. Remember 1 mV = 1000 µV; check units before choosing gain.

You need: resting and action potentials for resting and action potentials, electrode theory for electrode impedance, and bio-amplifiers and data acquisition for differential gain and CMRR.

EEG: microvolt cortical rhythms

Cortical neurons communicate through postsynaptic potentials. A single potential is tiny, but millions of aligned pyramidal cells sum at the scalp. The result is the electroencephalogram (EEG), a random-looking wave of 10–100 µV. EEG is used for sleep staging, anaesthesia depth and seizure monitoring; seizure detail is in multichannel EEG and evoked potentials.

Scalp electrodes follow the 10–20 system. Names mark the brain region: Fp frontopolar, F frontal, C central, P parietal, O occipital, T temporal. Odd numbers are left, even numbers right, and z is midline. Fp1, F3, C3, P3 and O1 form a left-side chain; Fp2, F4, C4, P4 and O2 mirror it on the right. A1 and A2 are earlobe references.

A referential montage connects each active electrode to one common reference, such as an earlobe or the average of all electrodes. It shows activity spread over the scalp. A bipolar montage connects adjacent electrodes, for example Fp1–F3. It rejects common noise and sharpens local differences, but it needs more channels.

  • Delta: below 4 Hz; deep sleep (slow eye movements can imitate it).
  • Theta: 4–8 Hz; drowsiness.
  • Alpha: 8–13 Hz; relaxed wakefulness with eyes closed.
  • Beta: 13–30 Hz; active thinking and some drugs.
  • Gamma: above 30 Hz; small, and easily confused with muscle activity.

EEG gain is about 10⁴ because 50 µV × 10⁴ = 0.5 V, a useful ADC range. The band 0.5–70 Hz passes delta to beta and removes slow sweat drift and high-frequency muscle. A 50 Hz notch removes mains interference in India. Sampling at 250 Hz gives a Nyquist frequency of 125 Hz, above the 70 Hz band. An anti-aliasing low-pass filter set near 70–100 Hz still goes before the ADC, so noise and muscle activity above 125 Hz never fold into the EEG band.

Eye blinks appear as slow frontal waves, jaw clench adds EMG, loose electrodes give pops, and sweat drifts the baseline. Checking electrode impedance and asking the patient to relax the jaw removes many faults.

EMG: millivolt muscle activity

EMG records the electrical activity of muscle fibres. A motor unit is one motor neuron and all the muscle fibres it drives. When the neuron fires, the fibre action potentials sum into a motor unit action potential (MUAP). Surface electrodes on the skin over the muscle belly and a tendon record many MUAPs together. Needle electrodes enter the muscle and pick up single units with better selectivity, but they are invasive.

Surface EMG is in the millivolt range, roughly 0.1–5 mV; needle EMG can reach several millivolts. A smaller gain than EEG is enough, often 10²–10³. The band is 20–500 Hz because MUAP edges are fast. The amplifier still needs high common-mode rejection ratio (CMRR), and a 50 Hz notch removes mains. Sample above 1 kHz so the Nyquist frequency is above 500 Hz.

Raw EMG is zero-mean and oscillatory, so one number needs a processing chain. Filter first with the band-pass and 50 Hz notch filters. Then rectify: take the absolute value |x(t)|. After that choose one amplitude measure. Low-pass smooth the rectified signal to get the mean absolute (averaged rectified) value, or compute RMS over a window directly from the filtered or rectified signal. Do not apply RMS after smoothing; RMS already does the averaging.

xRMS = √( (1/T) ∫₀ᵀ x²(t) dt )

x(t) is the filtered EMG voltage in volts; T is the averaging window in seconds; xRMS is the root mean square in volts.

In a sustained contraction, fatigue shifts the EMG spectrum downward: the median frequency falls. Amplitude often rises first as more motor units are recruited, then falls as fibres tire. The frequency shift is the more reliable fatigue sign.

Checks and maintenance

The two branches must not share one gain and one filter. EEG at 50 µV through EMG gain 10³ gives only 0.05 V, a small fraction of the ADC range, so resolution is poor and the gain error shows. EMG at 2 mV through EEG gain 10⁴ gives 20 V, which clips the amplifier. Use separate plug-in modules or software presets; label them clearly. Run a short routine before trusting a trace.

  • Check each electrode impedance before recording; a high impedance picks up 50 Hz and movement.
  • Ask the patient to blink, clench and relax; identify artefacts in the record.
  • Test the 50 Hz notch and the calibration pulse at the start and end of a session.
  • Record a known calibration signal before each session; a gain error changes every later reading.
  • For EMG, mark the contraction level and joint angle; RMS depends on both.
  • For EEG, keep the reference consistent; changing reference changes the waveform.

Multichannel EEG and epilepsy needs are in multichannel EEG and evoked potentials; biofeedback uses the same front end in biofeedback instrumentation.

Worked example

Question. An EEG amplifier has gain 10,000 and input 50 µV. What is the output? If the EEG is sampled at 250 Hz, what is the Nyquist frequency?

  1. Convert the input: 50 µV = 50 × 10⁻⁶ V = 5 × 10⁻⁵ V.
  2. Find the output: Vout = Vin × A = 5 × 10⁻⁵ V × 10,000 = 0.5 V.
  3. Find the Nyquist frequency: fN = fs ÷ 2 = 250 Hz ÷ 2 = 125 Hz.
  4. Check the band: 125 Hz is above the 70 Hz EEG upper limit, so the sampled EEG does not alias.

Worked example 2

Question. Four successive samples of a filtered surface EMG are 0.4, −0.8, 1.2 and −0.4 mV. (a) Find the mean absolute value and the RMS of this window. (b) During a 60 s held contraction the median frequency falls from 120 Hz to 84 Hz. Find the percentage fall and say what it shows.

  1. Rectify: |x| = 0.4, 0.8, 1.2, 0.4 mV. Mean absolute value = (0.4 + 0.8 + 1.2 + 0.4) ÷ 4 = 2.8 ÷ 4 = 0.70 mV.
  2. Square: 0.16, 0.64, 1.44, 0.16 mV². Mean square = 2.40 ÷ 4 = 0.60 mV².
  3. RMS = √0.60 = 0.775 mV. RMS is larger than the mean absolute value because squaring weights the big 1.2 mV sample more.
  4. Fall in median frequency = (120 − 84) ÷ 120 × 100 = 36 ÷ 120 × 100 = 30 %.
  5. A 30 % downward shift of the spectrum is the classic sign of muscle fatigue: conduction along the tiring fibres slows, so power moves to lower frequencies.

Answer. Mean absolute value 0.70 mV and RMS 0.775 mV; the median frequency falls by 30 %, which shows fatigue.

Exam traps

  • µV and mV are not interchangeable. EEG is 10–100 µV; surface EMG is 0.1–5 mV. A 1000× slip changes the gain choice.
  • Using EEG gain 10⁴ on EMG clips the amplifier; using EMG gain 10³ on EEG gives a small output and poor ADC resolution.
  • A bipolar montage does not mean a reference to earth. It measures the difference between two scalp electrodes, so common noise cancels.
  • Nyquist frequency is half the sampling rate, not the sampling rate itself. Sampling at 250 Hz gives 125 Hz, not 250 Hz. A high Nyquist frequency is not enough by itself; an anti-aliasing low-pass filter before the ADC stops higher-frequency noise folding back.
  • Fatigue is not only a smaller signal. The median frequency falls, and amplitude can rise first before it falls.

1.7 Nerve conduction velocity measurement

Nerve conduction velocity (NCV) is the speed of an action potential along a peripheral nerve, reported in metres per second (m/s). The measurement exists because a damaged nerve conducts slowly, and a doctor needs a number, not just a feeling that the limb is weak. The test stimulates the same nerve at two skin sites and records the response at each. The time gap between the two responses removes fixed delays at the nerve ending and muscle. NCV is used for carpal tunnel syndrome, ulnar nerve problems, diabetic neuropathy and nerve injury after fractures. It is a standard part of nerve conduction studies.

You need: EEG and EMG recording for the origin and recording of muscle and nerve signals, and electrode theory for surface electrode behaviour.

What NCV measures

Nerve conduction velocity (NCV) is the speed at which an action potential travels along a peripheral nerve. The unit is metres per second (m/s). The instrument stimulates the nerve at two points on the skin and records the response from a muscle or from the nerve itself. Some syllabi call the whole test nerve conduction studies (NCS); this chapter covers the velocity measurement.

For a motor nerve, the recording electrodes sit over the muscle belly and tendon. The stimulator gives a short current pulse through surface electrodes. The muscle response is the compound muscle action potential (CMAP), a sum of many muscle fibre action potentials, usually a few millivolts (mV). For a sensory nerve, the recording electrodes sit over the nerve trunk. The response is the sensory nerve action potential (SNAP), a sum of sensory axon potentials, usually a few microvolts (μV). The SNAP needs more amplification and averaging because it is small.

Latency is the time from the stimulus to the start of the response, measured in milliseconds (ms). Amplitude is the height of the response. Latency tells when the signal arrives; amplitude tells how many fibres responded. Velocity uses latency and distance, not amplitude. Surface electrodes are safe and repeatable; needle recording is used only in special cases. Needle EMG analysis is in EEG and EMG recording. Stimulators used for therapy are covered under electrotherapy and nerve stimulators.

The latency-difference formula

Stimulate the nerve at a distal site, far from the muscle. The latency you see at the recording electrode includes three parts: conduction from the distal stimulus site to the nerve terminal, the delay at the neuromuscular junction, and the time for muscle fibres to start their action potential. Call this common delay D. It is not nerve conduction along the long segment you want to measure.

Now stimulate the same nerve at a proximal site. The new latency equals D plus the time to travel the extra distance d between the two stimulation sites. Subtract the distal latency from the proximal latency. D cancels:

Δt = tproximal − tdistal;  v = d / Δt

tdistal and tproximal are the latencies from stimulus to response onset at the distal and proximal sites, in seconds (or milliseconds); Δt is their difference in the same unit; d is the distance between the two stimulation sites in metres (or millimetres); v is the nerve conduction velocity in m/s (or mm/ms). Because 1 mm/ms = 1 m/s, many machines report millimetres and milliseconds directly.

  1. Place the recording electrode over the muscle for a motor study, or over the nerve trunk for a sensory study.
  2. Stimulate the distal site. Increase the stimulus until the response stops growing; this is supramaximal, so all fibres fire.
  3. Record the distal latency and amplitude.
  4. Stimulate the proximal site with the limb in the same position. Record the proximal latency and amplitude.
  5. Measure d with a tape along the nerve path between the two stimulation points.
  6. Compute Δt and v = d / Δt. Repeat and average; note skin temperature.

Distance, temperature and the trace

Distance is measured between the two cathode positions, along the line of the nerve, with the joint at the same angle for both stimuli. A 10 mm error in a 200 mm segment changes v by about 5 percent; this is a common reason for a wrong answer. Record distance in millimetres.

Temperature changes velocity. A cool limb conducts more slowly because ion channel kinetics and membrane properties change. Warm the limb, measure skin temperature, and compare with the other side. The latency difference method removes the fixed distal delay, but cooling still changes the speed of the measured segment. Motor and sensory fibres also conduct at different speeds: sensory fibres are often faster, and the lab reports them separately.

The CMAP or SNAP amplitude is read peak-to-peak or from baseline to the first negative peak, depending on the lab. Low amplitude with near-normal velocity points to axonal loss. Slow velocity with a preserved amplitude points to demyelination. Mixed pictures occur. This interpretation belongs to medicine. The instrument's job is to give a clean latency, a correct distance and a stable amplitude.

The stimulator and the recorder

The nerve stimulator is a constant-current pulse source, so the current stays set even when skin resistance changes. A typical unit gives single rectangular pulses of 0.1–0.2 ms (up to 1 ms), 0–100 mA, at about 1 pulse per second. Its output is isolated from mains and earth, and each pulse also starts the recorder sweep, so latency is read from the stimulus mark.

The recorder is a bio-amplifier with high CMRR and a wide band, about 2 Hz to 10 kHz for motor studies. The stimulus artefact is large, so the amplifier must recover within a fraction of a millisecond; a ground electrode between the stimulating and recording sites keeps the artefact small. A muscle stimulator uses the same isolated output with longer pulses and pulse trains.

Worked example

Question. A technician tests a motor nerve. The distance between the distal stimulation site and the proximal stimulation site is 200 mm. The distal latency is 2 ms and the proximal latency is 6 ms. Find the latency difference and the motor nerve conduction velocity. Give the velocity in m/s.

  1. Write the given values: d = 200 mm, tdistal = 2 ms, tproximal = 6 ms.
  2. Find the latency difference: Δt = tproximal − tdistal = 6 ms − 2 ms = 4 ms.
  3. Use the formula v = d / Δt. Keep mm and ms together: v = 200 mm / 4 ms = 50 mm/ms.
  4. Convert to m/s. Since 1 mm/ms = 1 m/s, v = 50 m/s.
  5. Check with SI units: d = 0.200 m and Δt = 0.004 s, so v = 0.200 / 0.004 = 50 m/s. The answer agrees.

Answer. The latency difference is 4 ms and the motor nerve conduction velocity is 50 m/s.

Exam traps

  • Using the proximal latency alone gives v = d / tproximal = 200 / 6 = 33.3 m/s, which is too low. Use the latency difference Δt.
  • Mixing units: 200 mm / 4 ms is 50 mm/ms, which equals 50 m/s. Do not divide millimetres by seconds without converting.
  • Measuring distance from the stimulation site to the recording electrode instead of between the two stimulation sites gives a meaningless velocity.
  • A cool limb slows conduction. Record skin temperature and compare with the other side before trusting a low velocity.
  • CMAP or SNAP amplitude does not enter v = d / Δt. A small amplitude changes the diagnosis, not the velocity arithmetic.

1.8 Heart rate monitor, Holter monitor and ECG troubleshooting

A heart-rate monitor turns an ECG into a beat count and a number on a screen. It detects each QRS complex, times the RR interval, and displays beats per minute. A Holter monitor records the same ECG for a whole day so a doctor can replay it later. Both depend on clean electrodes, a stable amplifier, and quick fault finding. You meet them in bedside monitors, 24-hour cardiac checks, and home ECG patches.

You need: resting and action potentials for the action potential and ECG recording for ECG leads and display.

Heart-rate monitor: from QRS to bpm

A heart-rate monitor starts with surface ECG electrodes. The bio-amplifier removes common-mode voltage and raises the millivolt ECG to a usable level. The QRS detector then marks the sharp ventricular depolarisation. It does not count every P wave or T wave. From each mark, an RR timer measures the time to the next mark.

  1. Band-pass filter the ECG to about 5–15 Hz, the usual QRS band, to keep QRS energy and reject baseline wander and high-frequency noise.
  2. Use slope or threshold detection to find a QRS. A short refractory window, such as 200 ms, stops double counting.
  3. Time the interval between two QRS marks; this is the RR interval.
  4. Convert RR to rate and update the display and alarm limits.

HRinst = 60 ÷ RR

HRinst is instantaneous heart rate in beats per minute; RR is the interval between two QRS complexes in seconds; 60 changes seconds to minutes.

Averaged heart rate is more stable for display: HRavg = NQRS ÷ tmin. Here NQRS is the number of QRS complexes and tmin is the total time in minutes. A monitor sounds an alarm when the rate crosses limits set by the clinician. Arrhythmia alarms and foetal monitoring are covered with arrhythmia monitoring.

Holter monitor: a full-day ECG record

A Holter monitor is a small battery recorder worn for 24 h or more. Chest electrodes feed the same kind of ECG amplifier, but the output goes to memory instead of only a screen. Older recorders used cassette tape; modern ones use flash memory. The record holds every ECG sample with a time stamp, so short events are not missed.

The patient keeps a diary and may press an event button when symptoms occur. At replay, software scans the whole record. It finds QRS complexes, plots heart rate against time, and shows full ECG around events. A doctor reads the replay to link symptoms with the rhythm. Average heart rate for the record uses total QRS complexes divided by total minutes.

  • Cassette or flash memory stores the full ECG; flash is common now because it has no moving parts.
  • Replay software marks QRS events, trends RR intervals and finds pauses or rate changes.
  • Event markers and diary times guide the doctor to the important strips.

  • 50 Hz interference gives a thick fuzzy trace at mains frequency; baseline wander is a slow swing from respiration or sweat; muscle artefact is high-frequency spikes. Do not label all three as the same fault.
  • A Holter total of 108,000 QRS complexes over 24 h is an average rate, not the peak rate. Do not report it as a maximum.

1.9 Multichannel EEG, epilepsy, evoked potentials and MEG

Multichannel EEG records many scalp voltages at once, so a clinician can see where brain activity rises, falls or synchronises. Epilepsy shows up as spikes and seizures; evoked potentials are tiny stimulus-locked responses pulled out by averaging. MEG measures the magnetic field of the same currents with SQUID sensors. These tools map brain function non-invasively in hospitals and research labs, and they share one problem: a microvolt or femtotesla signal buried in noise.

You need: Chapter 6 for EEG bandwidth and gain, Chapter 4 for differential amplifiers, and Chapter 2 for electrode impedance.

10–20 electrodes and montages

The 10–20 system places scalp electrodes at fixed fractions of the head. Common sites are Fp1, Fp2, C3, C4, O1, O2 and midline Fz, Cz, Pz. Odd numbers sit on the left, even numbers on the right, and z means midline. Denser caps use 64, 128 or 256 electrodes for finer spatial maps.

Each electrode feeds one channel of a multichannel amplifier. Gain is typically 10⁴ to 10⁵ and CMRR above 80 dB, because the EEG is only tens of microvolts riding on a larger common-mode voltage. Routine bandwidth is about 0.5–70 Hz.

A montage is the chosen pairing of electrodes. A referential montage compares each active electrode with one reference, such as linked ears or Cz. A bipolar montage subtracts neighbouring electrodes, for example Fp1–F3 then F3–C3. Referential shows the field at one site; bipolar rejects shared noise but can cancel a broad field. The display stacks channels, with voltage up and time across.

Epilepsy in the EEG

A seizure is a sudden, synchronous discharge of cortical neurons. On EEG it appears as spikes, sharp waves or spike-and-wave complexes. A spike lasts about 20–70 ms and a sharp wave 70–200 ms; both stand out from background, and a slow wave often follows. Absence seizures give a regular 3 Hz spike-and-wave pattern. Focal seizures give spikes in one or a few channels.

Interictal spikes occur between seizures and help locate the focus. Ictal EEG is recorded during the event, often with video. The reader compares channels and montages to separate a true cortical spike from an eye blink or electrode artefact. Spike amplitude is tens to hundreds of microvolts, larger than normal background. At a paper speed of 30 mm/s, one page shows about 10 s.

Evoked potentials and averaging

An evoked potential (EP) is a small, time-locked brain response to a stimulus. Visual EP (VEP): flash or reversing checkerboard on a screen, recorded over the occiput, with a positive peak near 100 ms called P100. Brainstem auditory EP (BAEP): click through earphones, waves I–V in the first 10 ms. Somatosensory EP (SSEP): electrical pulse to a nerve, such as the median nerve, with N20 near 20 ms. Latency is the time from stimulus to a peak, in ms. Amplitude is peak-to-peak, in µV.

The EP is buried in ongoing EEG. Averaging many stimulus-locked trials keeps the EP because it has fixed phase, and averages random noise toward zero. Artefact rejection removes trials with blinks or movement before averaging. A 50 Hz notch filter removes mains interference.

  1. Place electrodes using the 10–20 sites.
  2. Give stimuli at a fixed rate.
  3. Record EEG and reject bad trials.
  4. Average the remaining trials.
  5. Measure latency and amplitude from the averaged wave.

SNRN = SNR1 × √N

SNR1 is the signal-to-noise ratio in one trial (dimensionless); N is the number of averaged trials (no unit); SNRN is the ratio after averaging.

MEG and SQUID sensors

Magnetoencephalography (MEG) measures the magnetic field produced by the same cortical currents that make the EEG. The field is tiny, on the order of 10–1000 fT (femtotesla; 1 fT = 10⁻¹⁵ T). A SQUID (superconducting quantum interference device) converts this flux into a voltage. It works at cryogenic temperature, so the sensors sit in a helmet cooled by liquid helium.

MEG is not distorted by the skull and scalp, so it localises sources well; temporal resolution matches EEG. Arrays have 100–300 sensors. It is used in presurgical mapping and research. EEG and MEG are often recorded together and mapped onto an MRI.

EEG maintenance and troubleshooting

Before every recording, check each electrode impedance (below about 5 kΩ, and the electrodes similar to each other) and apply the calibration signal. A 50 µV calibration pulse should give the same deflection on every channel, about 7 mm at the usual sensitivity of 7 µV/mm, with the same decay set by the high-pass filter. Then record a short biological check: eyes open, eyes closed (alpha should appear over the occiput), blink and clench.

1.10 EEG and EMG biofeedback instrumentation

Biofeedback instrumentation turns a small EEG or EMG voltage into a light or tone the patient can see or hear. The patient then tries to change the signal, for example to relax a muscle or to increase alpha waves. The machine does not cure by itself; it gives fast, clear information. Clinics use it for relaxation training, stress management, rehabilitation and some attention training. Some syllabi call this EEG biofeedback and EMG biofeedback.

You need: EEG and EMG recording for EEG and EMG signal bands and electrode placement, and bio-amplifiers and data acquisition for bio-amplifier gain and common-mode rejection.

The feedback loop from electrode to patient

The chain starts at the patient. Surface electrodes pick up the tiny voltage. A high-gain amplifier raises it without adding much noise. A band-pass filter keeps only the EEG or EMG band. A rectifier changes negative and positive swings into one polarity by taking the absolute value. A smoother, a low-pass filter or averager, gives a steady envelope. A comparator compares this envelope with a set threshold. The output drives a tone or lamp. The patient hears or sees the result and tries again. This closed loop is the biofeedback mechanism.

Vout = G × Vin;  Venv = low-pass average of |Vout|;  C = 1 if Venv ≥ T, else 0

Vin is the electrode signal in µV; G is the amplifier gain (no unit); Vout is the amplifier output; Venv is the envelope, the output of a low-pass smoother with a time constant of about 0.1–1 s; T is the threshold, in the same unit as Venv (mV in practice); C is the comparator output, 1 for tone on and 0 for tone off. For a sine wave the average of the rectified signal is 0.90 × its RMS value.

  1. Clean skin and attach electrodes over the target muscle or scalp site.
  2. Amplify the small differential voltage with high common-mode rejection.
  3. Filter to the chosen band and add a 50 Hz notch where needed.
  4. Rectify, then smooth to form an envelope.
  5. Compare the envelope with threshold T and drive the tone or light.
  6. Adjust T as the patient improves, so the task stays possible.

EEG alpha training and EMG relaxation training

EEG alpha training uses scalp electrodes, often over the occipital region. The alpha rhythm lies in the 8–13 Hz band. The filter passes this band and rejects slower and faster activity. The threshold is set so the tone is on when alpha activity is above a chosen level. The patient sits quietly, often with eyes closed, and learns to keep the tone on. This is sometimes called alpha biofeedback. It is training, not a cure.

EMG relaxation training uses surface electrodes over a muscle such as the frontalis or forearm. The filter passes the EMG band, about 20–500 Hz, and rejects 50 Hz mains interference. The rectified smoothed signal is higher when more muscle fibres fire. The threshold is set just below the current resting level. The tone sounds when EMG is above threshold and stops when the patient relaxes. The silence is the reward. A physiotherapist may use this for tension headache or relaxation practice. The patient must understand the link between effort and tone.

Threshold choice decides the training. When the tone is the reward, as in alpha training, too high a threshold gives no reward and too low a threshold gives reward all the time. When silence is the reward, as in EMG relaxation, the rule turns round: too low a threshold keeps the tone on and the patient never succeeds. A good setting gives success often enough to keep the patient trying but not so often that the signal has no meaning.

Artefacts, thresholds and safety

Mains interference is a common fault. If an EMG unit also passes 50 Hz, the tone follows the mains hum and rewards interference, not muscle activity. Use a notch filter, short leads and good electrode contact. Motion, sweat and ECG pickup can also raise the envelope. Check the raw waveform before trusting the tone.

A comparator can chatter when the signal sits near threshold. Add hysteresis: turn on at a higher threshold Thigh and off at a lower threshold Tlow; between the two the output keeps its last state. Smoothing also reduces chatter. Feedback delay must be short. If the tone lags by seconds, the patient cannot link action to result.

Safety is still needed because electrodes touch the patient. Use an isolated patient circuit, low leakage current and a battery or medical-grade supply. Biofeedback is sensing only, not stimulation, but electrode impedance and patient current still need checks. Most exams ask for the block diagram, one threshold calculation and the artefact list.

Worked example

Question. An EMG relaxation unit is tested with a 100 Hz sine of 20 µV RMS at its input. The amplifier gain is 1000. The full-wave rectifier and smoother give the average rectified value, which for a sine is 0.90 × RMS. The comparator turns the tone on at 15 mV and off at 12 mV. (a) Find the amplifier output and the envelope. (b) Is the tone on? (c) The test input is lowered to 12 µV RMS, as when the patient relaxes. What does the tone do?

  1. Amplifier output: Vout = 1000 × 20 µV = 20,000 µV = 20 mV RMS. The 100 Hz test tone lies inside the 20–500 Hz EMG band, so the filter passes it.
  2. Envelope: Venv = 0.90 × 20 mV = 18.0 mV.
  3. Compare with the on level: 18.0 mV ≥ 15 mV, so C = 1 and the tone is on.
  4. Relaxed input: Vout = 1000 × 12 µV = 12 mV RMS, so Venv = 0.90 × 12 = 10.8 mV.
  5. 10.8 mV is below the 12 mV off level, so C = 0 and the tone stops. An envelope between 12 and 15 mV would leave the tone in its last state, so it cannot chatter.

Answer. Output 20 mV RMS and envelope 18.0 mV, so the tone is on; at 12 µV the envelope is 10.8 mV, below the 12 mV off level, so the tone goes off and the silence rewards relaxation.

Exam traps

  • Using gain without unit conversion: 20 µV × 1000 is 20 mV, not 20 V.
  • Letting 50 Hz through an EMG channel; the display then follows mains hum, not muscle activity.
  • Setting a threshold without smoothing; the comparator chatters when the envelope sits near T.
  • Confusing the EEG alpha band (8–13 Hz) with the EMG band (about 20–500 Hz).
  • Forgetting patient isolation: a sensing circuit still needs low leakage current and safe electrode current.

1.11 Biomechanical measurement: pedobarograph, gait and limb position

Biomechanical measurement turns body force, foot pressure and limb position into numbers. A pedobarograph maps pressure under the foot. Gait analysis times the walk cycle and measures step and stride. Limb position sensors record joint angle, and velocity and acceleration are obtained from these signals or from inertial sensors. Clinics use these numbers to fit insoles, check recovery after injury and design prostheses. Some syllabi call the whole area biomechanics measurement. The same ideas appear in sports labs and in any instrument that must show how a body moves, not just how its nerves and muscles fire.

You need: physiological transducers and load cells, and the basic mechanics of force, pressure, velocity and acceleration.

Pedobarograph: pressure map of the foot

A pedobarograph measures pressure between the foot and the floor. A platform version has a flat sensor array; the patient walks across it. An insole version puts thin sensors inside the shoe and records many steps. Each sensor cell changes resistance or capacitance with load, and the instrument converts its voltage to pressure in kPa. Peak pressure is the highest cell pressure at an instant. Contact area is the number of loaded cells multiplied by one cell's area.

P = F / A

P is pressure in pascal (Pa); F is the force on one sensor cell in newton (N); A is that cell's area in square metre (m²). 1 kPa = 1000 Pa.

Calibration uses known loads applied over a known area. Put a known mass on the platform, or press the whole platform with a plate of known area, and record the output voltage at several loads. The load becomes a pressure P = mg / A in Pa, where m is the mass in kg, g is 9.81 m/s² and A is the loaded area in m². Fit a straight line to voltage against pressure; the slope is sensitivity in mV/kPa and the intercept is zero offset. If only the force is known and the area is not, report the slope in mV/N instead. A pressure map is not a force plate. A force plate gives total ground reaction force with load cells; a pedobarograph gives the spatial pattern of pressure.

Clinics use peak pressure to find hot spots under a diabetic foot and contact area to check load sharing. Insoles are trimmed where pressure is high. Platform data is clean but short; insole data is long but needs careful calibration.

Gait cycle and walking numbers

One gait cycle starts when a foot touches the floor and ends when the same foot touches again. The stance phase is the time the foot is on the ground. The swing phase is the time the foot is in the air. In normal walking stance is about 60% of the cycle and swing about 40%; running changes this.

Step length is the distance between successive contacts of opposite feet. Stride length is the distance between successive contacts of the same foot. In a symmetric gait one stride is two steps. Cadence is steps per minute. Stride frequency is strides per second.

v = Lstep × cstep

v is walking speed in m/s; Lstep is step length in m; cstep is cadence in steps/s (cadence in steps/min divided by 60).

Speed also equals stride length times stride frequency. A walker with step length 0.7 m and cadence 120 steps/min has cadence 2 steps/s and speed 1.4 m/s. The figure shows a 1000 ms cycle with stance 0 to 600 ms and swing 600 to 1000 ms. Notice the cycle is 1.0 s, so stance is 0.6 s and swing is 0.4 s.

Limb position: angle, velocity and acceleration

A goniometer measures joint angle. A simple electrogoniometer has a potentiometer or strain gauge across the joint; its voltage changes as the angle changes. It is calibrated at known angles, for example 0° and 90°, to get a volts-per-degree slope.

An accelerometer measures acceleration. It also senses gravity, which on a limb is about 9.81 m/s², far larger than the movement signal. A gyroscope measures angular velocity. An inertial measurement unit (IMU) packs both.

Remove gravity before integrating. Using the sensor's orientation, find the component of gravity along its measuring axis, gaxis, and subtract it: amotion = ameasured − gaxis. Only this motion term is integrated, once for velocity and twice for position. Integrating the raw reading gives a completely wrong velocity and position.

amotion = ameasured − gaxis,  v = Δx / Δt,  a = Δv / Δt

ameasured is the raw accelerometer reading in m/s²; gaxis is the part of gravity (9.81 m/s²) along the sensor's measuring axis, found from its orientation; amotion is the acceleration due to movement in m/s²; Δx is change in position in m; Δt is time interval in s; v is velocity in m/s; Δv is change in velocity in m/s; a is acceleration in m/s².

After gravity is removed, drift still comes from sensor bias and noise, and each integration adds more. So IMU position drifts. Camera systems avoid drift by tracking reflective markers on the limb. From marker positions x(t), velocity is v = Δx/Δt and acceleration is a = Δv/Δt.

Use goniometers for joint angle, IMUs for free walking outside a lab, and cameras for a reference in a gait lab. Sample fast enough: a walking gait has useful detail up to about 10 Hz to 20 Hz, so 100 samples/s gives margin.

Worked example

Question. A walker has step length 0.7 m and cadence 120 steps/min. Find walking speed. If stance is 60% of a 1.0 s gait cycle, find stance and swing times. Assume symmetric gait.

  1. Cadence is given in steps/min; convert to steps/s: c = 120 / 60 = 2.0 steps/s.
  2. Each step advances 0.7 m; speed = step length × cadence = 0.7 m × 2.0 steps/s = 1.4 m/s.
  3. One gait cycle is 1.0 s. Stance fraction is 60% = 0.60. Stance time = 0.60 × 1.0 s = 0.60 s.
  4. Swing time = cycle time − stance time = 1.0 s − 0.60 s = 0.40 s.
  5. Check: stance + swing = 0.60 s + 0.40 s = 1.0 s.

Answer. Speed is 1.4 m/s (cadence 2.0 steps/s), stance time is 0.60 s and swing time is 0.40 s.

Worked example 2

Question. A pedobarograph platform has square sensor cells 5 mm on a side. Under the heel of a standing 70 kg patient the most loaded cell carries 12.5 N, and 480 cells are loaded in all. Find the peak pressure, the contact area and the mean pressure under the foot. Take g = 9.81 m/s².

  1. Cell area A = 5 mm × 5 mm = 25 mm² = 25 × 10⁻⁶ m² = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ m².
  2. Peak pressure = F ÷ A = 12.5 N ÷ 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ m² = 500,000 Pa = 500 kPa.
  3. Contact area = 480 × 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ m² = 0.012 m², that is 120 cm².
  4. Body weight = m g = 70 × 9.81 = 686.7 N. Mean pressure = 686.7 N ÷ 0.012 m² = 57,225 Pa = 57.2 kPa.
  5. Check: the peak cell is about 9 times the mean (500 ÷ 57.2 = 8.7), the hot spot a clinician looks for under the heel.

Answer. Peak pressure 500 kPa, contact area 0.012 m² (120 cm²) and mean pressure 57.2 kPa.

Exam traps

  • Cadence is steps/min, not strides/min. One stride is two steps.
  • Step length is not stride length; for a symmetric gait stride length = 2 × step length.
  • Pressure is force per area. Peak pressure is not total force; a small area gives high pressure with modest force.
  • In a gait cycle, stance plus swing = 100%. Normal walking is about 60% stance and 40% swing, not 50/50.
  • Inertial sensor position comes from double integration and drifts; one clean short trial does not prove long-term accuracy.

Takeaways

  1. Every bioelectric signal starts as an ion movement across a membrane, so the source voltage is always a chemical battery of a few tens of millivolts.
  2. An electrode is an ionic-to-electronic converter; its half-cell potential and its impedance decide how clean the recorded signal will be.
  3. A transducer must be chosen by range, sensitivity, linearity, impedance and safety for the specific body variable, not by one single best type.
  4. A bio-amplifier's job is to multiply the difference between two electrodes and throw away the interference both share, which is why CMRR and input impedance matter more than plain gain.
  5. The ECG recorder is a calibrated voltmeter and clock: standard leads, 1 mV calibration pulse, 25 mm/s paper speed and 10 mm/mV sensitivity.
  6. EEG needs microvolt gain in a 0.5–70 Hz band while EMG needs millivolt gain up to about 500 Hz; one amplifier setting cannot serve both.
  7. Nerve conduction velocity turns two latency readings at two stimulation sites into a number in metres per second that separates normal nerve from demyelination.
  8. Most ECG faults are found by checking, in order, the patient, the electrode, the cable and amplifier, and lastly the mains interference path.
  9. Averaging and filtering pull evoked potentials out of background EEG because the response repeats while the noise does not.
  10. Biofeedback works only when the measured signal is clean and the feedback reaches the patient within a fraction of a second.

Understand Describe the electrode–electrolyte interface, half-cell potential and why electrode impedance must match the amplifier. 12 marks

Points to cover. Ionic current in tissue, electronic current in wire, conversion at the interface; Double layer model and its capacitance; Half-cell potential; offset from two different metals; need for Ag/AgCl pairs; Polarisation, non-polarisable electrodes, electrode jelly and skin preparation; Typical surface electrode impedance 1–10 kΩ; causes of drift and motion artefact; Why amplifier input impedance must be many times the electrode impedance.

Model answer.

  1. Ionic to electronic current. In tissue and gel the current is carried by ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻); in the lead wire by electrons. At an Ag/AgCl electrode the reactions Ag ⇌ Ag⁺ + e⁻ and Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ ⇌ AgCl hand charge across the boundary.
  2. Double layer. The reaction leaves a sheet of charge on the metal and an opposite sheet of ions in the electrolyte a fraction of a nanometre away. Two charge sheets that close together form a capacitance Cd, and the charge-transfer path through the reaction is a resistance Rd in parallel with it.
  3. Half-cell potential. The double layer holds a steady voltage, measured against the standard hydrogen electrode; for Ag/AgCl it is about +0.22 V. Two identical electrodes give equal and opposite half-cell potentials, so only their small difference, a few millivolts, reaches the amplifier as offset. Two different metals can leave hundreds of millivolts, far more than a 1 mV ECG.
  4. Polarisation. When current flows, the half-cell potential shifts (overpotential). A polarisable electrode such as bare stainless steel behaves like a capacitor and drifts; Ag/AgCl in chloride gel is almost non-polarisable because its reaction reverses easily, so the offset stays small and steady. Skin preparation and gel lower the skin resistance and reduce motion artefact.
  5. Equivalent circuit. Each electrode is Ehc in series with Rs (gel and skin) and the parallel pair Rd ∥ Cd. As frequency rises the reactance of Cd falls, so impedance falls; a gelled surface electrode is about 1–10 kΩ in the ECG band, unprepared skin tens of kΩ or more.

Model answer.

  3. Instrumentation amplifier. Glossary

10–20 system
Standard spacing of EEG electrodes by percentages of the skull landmarks, giving named positions such as Fz, C3 and O2.
Active electrode
Electrode placed over the source of interest; the other is the reference or indifferent electrode.
ADC
Analogue-to-digital converter; its resolution in bits and its sampling rate set the quality of the stored signal.
Ag/AgCl electrode
Silver wire coated with silver chloride, used as a stable, low-offset, non-polarisable biopotential electrode.
Aliasing
False low-frequency signal created when the sampling rate is below twice the highest signal frequency.
Biopotential
Voltage produced by living tissue, such as the membrane voltage of a nerve or muscle cell or the body-surface copy of many such cells.
Bipolar electrode
Electrode pair that records the difference between two nearby active sites.
Capacitive transducer
Sensor that converts displacement or pressure into a change of capacitance.
Depolarisation
Loss of the negative resting voltage as positive ions enter the cell.
Fatigue (EMG)
Fall in the median frequency and rise in amplitude of the EMG spectrum as a muscle is held contracted.
Gauge factor
Ratio of fractional resistance change to fractional length change in a strain gauge, about 2 for metal foil.
Inductive transducer
Sensor that converts displacement into a change of inductance, for example by moving a core in a coil.
Isolation amplifier
Circuit that passes the signal across an optical or transformer barrier so no mains fault current can reach the patient.
Latency
Time from a stimulus to the start of the evoked or muscle response, measured in milliseconds.
Load cell
Force transducer made of a metal element with bonded strain gauges wired as a Wheatstone bridge.
Motion artefact
False signal caused by movement of the electrode on the skin, which changes the half-cell potential.
Notch filter
Narrow band-stop filter, usually set at 50 Hz in India, that removes mains interference with little loss elsewhere.
Photoelectric transducer
Sensor that converts light intensity into current or voltage, used for pulse and blood-oxygen measurement.
Polarisation
Build-up of charge at an electrode that opposes current flow and distorts low-frequency signals.
Refractory period
Short time after an action potential when the cell cannot fire again, which limits the maximum firing rate.
Repolarisation
Return of the membrane voltage to its resting negative value after an action potential.
Sodium–potassium pump
Active transport that pushes Na⁺ out and K⁺ in to keep the concentration gradients that make the resting potential.
SQUID
Superconducting quantum interference device, the extremely sensitive magnetic sensor used in MEG.
Unipolar (monopolar) electrode
Active electrode referred to one distant common reference, so the signal contains more of the remote activity.
