2.1 Blood pressure measurement: direct and indirect

Blood pressure is the pressure of blood inside an artery, quoted as two numbers: systolic, the peak during a heartbeat, and diastolic, the trough between beats. Their difference is the pulse pressure, and the mean arterial pressure (MAP) is the average that pushes blood to the organs. These numbers detect hypertension, guide treatment in shock or on a ventilator, and check that a drug is working. The same pressure can be read by a sensor placed inside the artery (direct) or by a cuff wrapped round the arm (indirect).

You need Unit 1: physiological transducers, the strain-gauge bridge, bio-amplifiers and recording systems.

Direct measurement: sensor inside the artery

A cannula is inserted into the radial artery at the wrist, sometimes the brachial or femoral artery. It connects to a stiff saline-filled catheter that ends at a strain-gauge transducer. The transducer has a diaphragm with a strain gauge bridge bonded to it: pressure bends the diaphragm, the bridge changes resistance, a bridge amplifier converts that to a voltage, and a monitor draws the pulse waveform. This is the reference method. It gives a beat-by-beat waveform, not just two numbers, so it is used in intensive care and during surgery.

  1. Flush the catheter with saline and remove all bubbles.
  2. Zero the transducer at the level of the right atrium, roughly the mid-axillary line, with the tap open to air.
  3. Turn the tap to the artery and check the waveform on the monitor.
  4. Read systolic and diastolic from the waveform; in a ventilated patient read at end-expiration.

The catheter and transducer form a second-order system with a natural frequency and a damping ratio. If the damping ratio is too low, the trace rings or overshoots after each systolic peak, and systolic pressure reads falsely high. If the natural frequency is too low, the trace cannot follow the fast systolic upstroke, the peaks are blunted and systolic pressure reads low. A bubble in the line adds compliance, lowers the natural frequency and flattens the trace, so the systolic peak is lost. Only a few universities examine this dynamic response.

MAP = D + (S − D) / 3

MAP is mean arterial pressure in mmHg; S is systolic pressure in mmHg; D is diastolic pressure in mmHg. The formula holds at normal heart rates.

The cuff and Korotkoff sounds

An inflatable rubber bladder inside a cloth cuff is wrapped round the upper arm and pumped above systolic pressure. The brachial artery is squeezed shut and flow stops. Air is let out slowly. When cuff pressure falls just below systolic, blood squirts through the partly open artery as a turbulent jet, the artery wall vibrates, and a stethoscope over the artery below the cuff hears the sound. These are Korotkoff sounds, named after the Russian physician who described them.

Phase V is diastolic in most adults. In some patients the sounds never disappear: children, pregnancy and aortic regurgitation are common reasons. If sounds are still heard when the cuff pressure reaches zero, phase IV (muffling) is taken as diastolic instead.

The mercury manometer is a vertical glass tube; cuff pressure pushes mercury up a calibrated scale. It is accurate but is being withdrawn because mercury is toxic. The aneroid manometer uses a Bourdon tube or bellows moving a needle, and it needs regular calibration. In the palpatory method the operator feels the radial pulse return as the cuff falls; this gives systolic pressure only, and it is used when sounds are hard to hear. A Doppler probe over the artery detects the frequency shift from moving blood and is used in infants and in peripheral arteries.

Oscillometric method and sources of error

Automated oscillometric monitors use the same cuff but no microphone. A pressure sensor watches the small pressure pulses that the artery pushes into the cuff as it opens and closes with each beat. The size of these oscillations rises to a maximum and then falls. The cuff pressure at maximum oscillation is taken as MAP; the machine then computes systolic and diastolic from fixed ratios of the oscillation amplitude. The method needs no trained ear, works through thin clothing, and is used in home monitors and ward machines.

  • Cuff too narrow: reads falsely high. The bladder should be about 40 percent of the arm circumference in width and about 80 percent of the arm circumference in length, so that it wraps most of the way round the arm.
  • Arm below heart level: reads high, roughly 0.8 mmHg for every centimetre of drop.
  • Deflation faster than 3 mmHg per second: underestimates systolic and overestimates diastolic.
  • Talking, a full bladder, caffeine or anxiety all raise the reading. A rise seen only in the clinic, with normal home readings, is the white-coat effect.

Worked example

Question. A 12 cm cuff on an arm of 28 cm circumference is inflated to 160 mmHg and let down at 3 mmHg/s. Korotkoff sounds appear at 128 mmHg, become muffled at 100 mmHg and disappear at 82 mmHg. (a) Give systolic, diastolic and mean arterial pressure. (b) Express systolic pressure in kPa, using 1 mmHg = 0.1333 kPa. (c) An arterial catheter in the same patient reads 132/86 mmHg. Why is it different?

  1. Systolic pressure is the cuff pressure at the first Korotkoff sound: S = 128 mmHg. Diastolic pressure is taken at phase V, where sounds disappear: D = 82 mmHg. Phase IV at 100 mmHg is only noted, not used as diastolic.
  2. Pulse pressure = S − D = 128 − 82 = 46 mmHg.
  3. Mean arterial pressure = D + (S − D)/3 = 82 + 46/3 = 82 + 15.3 = 97.3 mmHg.
  4. Convert systolic pressure: 128 × 0.1333 = 17.1 kPa.
  5. The cuff reads pressure transmitted through skin, fat and the artery wall, and it marks systolic at the first sound rather than at the true peak. Systolic pressure also rises as the pulse wave travels from the aorta to the arm (peripheral amplification), so the direct radial reading is usually a few mmHg higher than the cuff reading.

Answer. Systolic 128 mmHg, diastolic 82 mmHg, pulse pressure 46 mmHg, MAP 97.3 mmHg, and systolic pressure 17.1 kPa; the indirect cuff value is slightly lower because of peripheral amplification and because it depends on cuff width, arm position and deflation rate.

Exam traps

  • Writing phase IV (muffling, 100 mmHg) as diastolic when the examiner expects phase V (disappearance, 82 mmHg).
  • Forgetting that a cuff that is too narrow reads falsely high, and one that is too wide reads falsely low.
  • Zeroing the transducer at the shelf level instead of at the right atrium, which adds a hydrostatic error.
  • Mixing units: 1 mmHg = 0.1333 kPa, so a pressure in kPa is about 7.5 times smaller than the same pressure in mmHg.
  • Assuming an oscillometric machine finds systolic at the first oscillation; it takes the cuff pressure at maximum oscillation as MAP.

2.2 Blood flow and cardiac output: dye and thermal dilution

Cardiac output is the volume of blood the heart pushes out in one minute. Doctors need it to judge whether a sick heart is pumping enough, and to guide drugs and fluids. Dilution methods measure it without opening the chest: inject a known amount of indicator into the blood, then record how the indicator concentration changes downstream. The faster the blood flows, the more the indicator is spread out and the smaller the concentration peak becomes. This chapter covers two hospital standards: dye dilution with indocyanine green, and thermal dilution with cold saline through a pulmonary artery catheter.

You need: the heart rate and cardiac cycle ideas from Unit 1, and the transducer and bio-amplifier basics from Unit 1; the pressure measurement in 2.1 shows the same idea of a sensor on a catheter.

Cardiac output and the dilution principle

Cardiac output (CO) is the blood volume the heart ejects per minute, in litres per minute (L/min). At rest an adult heart moves about 5 L/min; during exercise it can rise several times. CO = heart rate × stroke volume, but measuring stroke volume directly is hard, so dilution methods infer the flow from how blood carries an injected indicator.

The Fick principle is the reference method: oxygen consumption divided by the arteriovenous oxygen difference gives CO. It needs blood gas analysis, covered in Unit 3. Dilution methods use the same conservation idea with an indicator instead of oxygen.

Inject a known mass I (mg) of indicator into a vein. Downstream, a sensor records concentration c(t) in mg/L against time t in seconds. If blood flow is Q in L/s and the indicator mixes completely, then I = Q ∫ c(t) dt. The integral A = ∫ c(t) dt has units mg·s/L and is the area under the concentration-time curve. Rearranged and converted to minutes:

CO = 60 I / A

CO is cardiac output in L/min; I is the mass of indicator injected in mg; A = ∫ c(t) dt is the area under the first-pass concentration-time curve in mg·s/L; 60 converts seconds to minutes.

The method assumes complete mixing, no indicator loss before the sensor, and steady flow during the measurement. Those assumptions are the reason the recording technique matters.

Dye dilution with indocyanine green

Dye dilution uses indocyanine green (ICG), a green dye that stays in the blood vessels and is removed by the liver. A typical dose is 5 mg, injected rapidly into a central vein. Arterial blood is drawn through a densitometer, a small cuvette that measures light absorption at the dye's wavelength. The recorder plots dye concentration against time. The curve rises as the first bolus passes the sampling site, peaks within a few seconds, then falls.

After about 20–30 s some dye returns through the circulation and creates a second, smaller hump called recirculation. That hump is not part of the first pass. To find the true first-pass area, plot the falling part of the curve on semilog paper, fit a straight line, and extend it below the recirculation hump. The area under the first-pass curve — the measured part plus the extrapolated part — is A. Equivalently, take the area under the original curve up to the start of recirculation, then add the area under the extrapolated line from that time onward.

  1. Inject a known mass I of ICG into a central vein.
  2. Draw arterial blood through a densitometer and record c(t) for 30–40 s.
  3. Identify the first-pass peak and the start of recirculation.
  4. Extrapolate the downslope on semilog paper to remove the recirculation hump.
  5. Measure A by planimetry or by numerical integration.
  6. Compute CO = 60 I / A.

ICG is relatively safe, but it contains iodine, so allergy matters. The method is accurate when the curve is clean. The area is what matters; the height alone is not CO.

Thermal dilution and the Swan-Ganz catheter

Thermal dilution replaces the dye with cold fluid and the densitometer with a thermistor. A Swan-Ganz catheter floats through the right heart into the pulmonary artery. It has a proximal port in the right atrium and a thermistor near its tip in the pulmonary artery. A known volume, commonly 10 mL, of cold saline or dextrose is injected into the right atrium. The thermistor records the small fall and recovery of blood temperature as the cold bolus passes.

The recorder plots temperature change ΔT (°C) against time (s). The area under this curve, AT = ∫ ΔT dt, has units °C·s. The monitor computes:

CO = K / AT

CO is cardiac output in L/min; AT = ∫ ΔT dt is the area under the temperature-time curve in °C·s; K is the computation constant supplied with the catheter, in the units the monitor expects.

K bundles the injectate volume, its specific heat and density, the blood temperature, and catheter factors. Enter the correct K for the injectate volume and temperature; a wrong K gives a wrong CO even when the curve is perfect.

Iced injectate (0–4 °C) gives a larger temperature difference from blood, so a larger ΔT and a better signal-to-noise ratio. Room-temperature injectate is also used and is more comfortable, but the constant changes. Thermal dilution is safer than dye: no dye reaction, no liver clearance problem, and it can be repeated every few minutes. The main risks are those of the catheter itself, not the indicator.

Worked example

Question. 5 mg of indocyanine green is injected and the arterial concentration curve is recorded. The computed area under the first pass is 60 mg·s/L. (a) Find the cardiac output. (b) What would it be if the area were 50 mg·s/L?

  1. Write the Stewart-Hamilton equation: CO = 60 I / A.
  2. For part (a), substitute I = 5 mg and A = 60 mg·s/L. Check units: mg ÷ (mg·s/L) = L/s; multiplying by 60 s/min gives L/min.
  3. CO = 60 × 5 / 60 = 5 L/min.
  4. For part (b), keep I = 5 mg and use A = 50 mg·s/L.
  5. CO = 60 × 5 / 50 = 6 L/min.
  6. A smaller area means the blood carried the dye away faster, so the cardiac output is higher.

Answer. (a) The cardiac output is 5 L/min. (b) With a first-pass area of 50 mg·s/L, the cardiac output is 6 L/min.

Exam traps

  • Dropping the factor 60 in CO = 60 I / A. With I = 5 mg and A = 60 mg·s/L, dropping 60 gives 0.083 L/s (not L/min), which is the same as 5 L/min.
  • Including the recirculation hump in A. That makes A too large and CO too small, because CO is inversely proportional to the area.
  • Mixing time units: the area must be in mg·s/L before multiplying by 60. If the curve is integrated in mg·min/L, do not multiply by 60 again.
  • Using the wrong computation constant K for thermal dilution when the injectate volume or temperature is changed. The curve can be correct while CO is wrong.
  • Confusing the dye curve, with concentration in mg/L, and the thermal curve, with temperature change in °C. Their areas have different units and different constants.

2.3 Heart sounds: stethoscope and phonocardiography

Blood hitting a closing valve makes a short, low thump. A stethoscope lets a doctor hear this directly; a phonocardiograph records it as a waveform, the PCG, beside the ECG. The ECG says when the heart electrically fired; the PCG says when the valves actually shut. Clinicians use heart sounds at every bedside examination and in foetal monitoring. Engineers use them to test microphones, filters and timing circuits. This chapter explains where S1 and S2 come from, how the stethoscope couples sound to the ear, and how the PCG signal chain times sounds against the ECG.

You need Unit 1's ECG recording, bio-amplifiers and filter basics (see Unit 1, Bioelectric Signals, Electrodes and Recorders).

What makes the sounds, and when

A heart sound is a short vibration from the heart and great vessels. S1 and S2 come from a valve closing; S3 and S4 come from blood entering the ventricle. The leaflet stops, the blood column behind it is checked, and a pressure wave runs through the chest wall. Valve opening is normally quiet. Systole is the phase when the ventricles contract; diastole is when they relax and fill.

S1 is the first sound. It comes from closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves at the start of ventricular systole. Its two parts are M1 and T1. S2 comes from closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves at the end of systole; its parts are A2 and P2. S3 is a low sound in early diastole, when blood rushes into the ventricle. S4 is a low sound in late diastole, when the atrium contracts. S3 is common in children and young adults and is often harmless. S4 can be normal in children and athletes, but in an older adult it usually means a stiff ventricle and suggests disease.

The energy of S1 and S2 lies mostly at low frequencies. The heart-sound band is often taken as about 20 Hz to 600 Hz. Turbulent flow adds higher frequencies than the low rumble of S3 and S4, but murmurs vary: aortic stenosis and regurgitation are relatively high-pitched, while mitral stenosis is low-pitched and needs the bell. S3 and S4 sit at the very low end, below about 50 Hz. That is why a stethoscope needs two different chest pieces.

Stethoscope: bell, diaphragm and tubing

A stethoscope has a chest piece, tubing and earpieces. It does not amplify sound in the electronic sense; it couples the small pressure changes at the chest wall to the ear canal with as little loss as possible. The ear then does the hearing.

The bell is an open cup. It senses low-frequency pressure changes and is used for S3, S4 and low murmurs such as mitral stenosis. Press it lightly. Pressing hard stretches the skin under it and turns the skin into a diaphragm, so low sounds fade. The diaphragm is a stretched membrane. It responds to higher-frequency movement and is used for S1, S2 and higher murmurs such as aortic regurgitation. Press it firmly.

Tubing matters. A long, narrow or thin-walled tube attenuates high frequencies and adds resonances. Keep the tube short, wide and thick-walled, and seal the earpieces in the ear. A noisy room raises the noise floor, so quiet low sounds are lost first. A stethoscope is a coupling device, not a gain device.

Phonocardiograph: recording the sound with time reference

A phonocardiograph records the heart sound as a waveform. The chain is: chest piece, microphone, preamplifier, band-pass filter, amplifier, recorder or display. An air-coupled microphone has a small air chamber closed by a membrane; chest vibration moves the membrane and changes the air pressure on a capacitive or electret element. A contact microphone, often piezoelectric, sits directly on the chest. The preamplifier needs high input impedance so it does not load the microphone.

The band-pass filter keeps the heart-sound band and rejects baseline wander below about 25 Hz and high-frequency noise above its upper cutoff. The worked example uses 25 Hz to 400 Hz. A narrow upper cutoff makes a clean trace but can hide a high-frequency murmur. The amplifier raises the signal to a level the recorder can handle.

A simultaneous ECG channel is the time reference. The R wave marks electrical activation. S1 and S2 follow the R wave by short, repeatable delays that are measured on each trace; the S2 delay in particular lengthens as the heart rate falls. On the trace, a systolic murmur lies between S1 and S2; a diastolic murmur lies between S2 and the next S1. A2 and P2 can separate on inspiration, giving a split S2. Heart rate is found from the RR interval: heart rate in beats per minute = 60 / RR, with RR in seconds. Foetal phonocardiography uses the same idea to separate the baby's sounds from the mother's; see Chapter 2.4.

Worked example

Question. A phonocardiogram is recorded with a 25-400 Hz filter together with lead II ECG. S1 begins 0.05 s after the R wave, S2 begins 0.35 s after the same R wave, and the next R wave comes 0.80 s later. (a) Find the systolic interval. (b) Find the heart rate and the diastolic interval. (c) Would a 600 Hz murmur appear on this trace?

  1. (a) The systolic interval is the time from S1 onset to S2 onset: 0.35 s − 0.05 s = 0.30 s.
  2. (b) The RR interval is 0.80 s. Heart rate = 60 / RR = 60 / 0.80 = 75 beats per minute.
  3. Diastolic interval = RR − systolic interval = 0.80 s − 0.30 s = 0.50 s.
  4. (c) The filter upper cutoff is 400 Hz. A 600 Hz component lies above this cutoff and is heavily attenuated, so it does not show on the trace.

Answer. The systolic interval is 0.30 s, the heart rate is 75 beats per minute, the diastolic interval is 0.50 s, and the 600 Hz murmur is largely removed by the 400 Hz upper cutoff.

Exam traps

  • Saying S2 is caused by valve opening. S1 and S2 both come from valve closure; S2 is the aortic and pulmonary valves shutting at the end of systole.
  • Pressing the bell hard and still expecting low sounds. Heavy pressure turns the skin into a diaphragm and removes S3 and S4.
  • Putting a systolic murmur after S2. Systole lies between S1 and S2; diastole lies between S2 and the next S1.
  • Using a PCG upper cutoff that is too low. A clean-looking trace can hide a high-frequency murmur above the cutoff.
  • Using RR in milliseconds in heart rate = 60/RR. Either convert RR to seconds, or use 60000/RR with RR in milliseconds.

2.4 Foetal monitoring and arrhythmia monitoring

Two bedside monitors watch a rhythm. A foetal monitor separates the baby's heartbeat from the mother's and draws it against contractions. An arrhythmia monitor finds each QRS complex, measures the RR interval, converts it to rate, and alarms when the interval leaves set limits. Both are used in labour rooms, coronary care units and general wards. This chapter shows how each monitor separates the wanted signal from the unwanted one, and how a single missed beat can make a rate report look dangerously slow.

You need: Chapter 2.3 for heart sounds and phonocardiography, and Unit 1 for ECG recording and the heart rate monitor.

Foetal heart rate from the abdomen

The foetal heart is small and sits inside a moving mother. Two abdominal methods find its rate. An ultrasound Doppler transducer sends a continuous wave into the abdomen. Echoes from moving foetal heart walls and valves shift in frequency. The demodulated Doppler envelope repeats at the foetal heart rate. A band-pass filter removes slow maternal movement and fast noise.

Abdominal ECG uses electrodes on the mother's abdomen. The record contains maternal QRS complexes and foetal QRS complexes. The maternal complex is larger and regular. Subtraction estimates a maternal QRS template and subtracts it. Logic cancellation blanks the maternal QRS windows and keeps foetal peaks outside them. The cleaned signal gives foetal QRS times.

f_HR = 60 / T_f

f_HR is the foetal heart rate in beats per minute; T_f is the time between two foetal beats in seconds. A rate of 140 beats per minute gives T_f = 60/140 = 0.429 s.

  1. Place the transducer or abdominal electrodes.
  2. Record the mixed signal for several beats.
  3. Mark maternal QRS complexes from the large regular peaks.
  4. Cancel them by subtraction or logic gating.
  5. Detect foetal peaks and measure T_f.
  6. Display rate and trace.

Scalp electrode, contractions and the CTG trace

A direct foetal scalp electrode is a small clip placed on the foetal scalp after the membranes have ruptured and the cervix is dilated. It records the foetal ECG directly. There is no maternal QRS, so the signal is clean, but the method is invasive.

An intrauterine pressure catheter lies in the uterus and measures pressure. The cardiotocograph (CTG) prints foetal heart rate and uterine pressure together. The baseline is the average foetal rate over about 10 minutes, normally 110 to 160 beats per minute. Beat-to-beat variability is the small change from one foetal beat to the next, normally about 5 to 25 beats per minute. An acceleration is a brief rise. A deceleration is a fall.

Foetal phonocardiography uses a microphone on the abdomen. A filter selects the foetal heart sound band and rejects maternal sounds. It is passive and simple, but noise from mother and labour ward limits it.

Arrhythmia monitor: QRS to alarm

An arrhythmia monitor watches one ECG lead. A QRS detector first removes baseline drift and T waves with a band-pass filter, often around 5 to 15 Hz. It then finds sharp peaks. For each beat it measures the RR interval, the time from one R wave to the next.

R_inst = 60 / RR

R_inst is the instantaneous heart rate in beats per minute; RR is the interval between two adjacent R waves in seconds.

A monitor stores limits. Typical adult settings are tachycardia above 100 to 120 beats per minute, bradycardia below 50 to 60 beats per minute, and a pause when RR exceeds 1.5 to 2 s. Asystole is no QRS for a longer set time. The comparator drives an audible alarm and a message.

Artefact rejection checks QRS shape, a refractory period, and the previous RR. A very short RR after a normal beat may be noise. A missed beat is the dangerous error. If true RR is 800 ms and one QRS is missed, measured RR becomes 1600 ms. The reported rate is 60/1.60 = 37.5 beats per minute instead of 75. One missed beat doubles one interval and halves the reported rate.

2.5 Spirometry and pulmonary function measurement

A spirometer measures how much air moves in and out of the lungs and how fast. The volume-time curve and the flow-volume loop separate a blocked airway from a stiff lung. Clinics use spirometry to diagnose asthma and COPD, to check lung function before surgery, and to screen workers exposed to dust.

You need: school physics only; the idea of volume and flow is built here. Residual volume and airway resistance come in Chapter 2.8.

Primary volumes and capacities

Spirometer measures the volume of air a person moves in and out. The subject breathes through a mouthpiece with the nose clipped; the instrument draws volume against time. The slope of that curve is flow.

Four primary volumes: tidal volume VT is one quiet breath, about 0.5 L; inspiratory reserve volume IRV is extra air after a quiet breath in, about 3.0 L; expiratory reserve volume ERV is extra air after a quiet breath out, about 1.1 L; residual volume RV is air left after the hardest breath out, about 1.2 L. RV never leaves the lungs.

Capacities: IC = VT + IRV; VC = IRV + VT + ERV; FRC = ERV + RV; TLC = VC + RV. A spirometer measures VT, IRV and ERV, so it gives IC and VC. It cannot measure RV or FRC, because that air stays inside.

Spirometer types and the BTPS correction

Four designs are common. The water-sealed bell floats over water and writes on a drum; accurate but slow. The dry rolling seal uses a piston and rolling seal; easy to clean and common in clinics. The turbine has a vane that spins with flow; rotation gives flow, and integrated flow gives volume; hand-held units use it. The pneumotachograph measures the small pressure drop across a mesh, and flow is proportional to that drop; see Chapter 2.6.

A 3 L calibration syringe is pushed through before a session. The spirometer should read 3.000 L within its tolerance, often ±3%. This catches leaks and drift.

Lung gas is at 37 °C, at atmospheric pressure, and saturated with water vapour; this is BTPS. A spirometer in a cool room measures ATPS, a smaller volume. Volumes are corrected to BTPS before comparison with predicted values; the factor is about 1.07 to 1.10 at room temperature.

Forced vital capacity and the spirogram

The forced vital capacity (FVC) manoeuvre is the standard test. The subject sits, wears a nose clip, seals the lips, breathes in fully to TLC, then blows out as hard and fast as possible for at least 6 s until no more air comes out. The spirogram is the volume-time curve of that blow.

FEV1 is the volume exhaled in the first second. FVC is the total exhaled volume. The FEV1/FVC ratio is FEV1 ÷ FVC, dimensionless. Normal is above 0.70; some laboratories use 0.75.

An obstructive pattern (asthma, COPD) has low FEV1 and ratio below 0.70, because narrowed airways slow the air out; FVC may be normal or low. A restrictive pattern (pulmonary fibrosis) has low FVC and low FEV1, but ratio normal or high, because the stiff lung is small yet the airway is open.

FEF25-75 is the average flow over the middle half of FVC, from 25% to 75% of volume; it falls early in small-airway disease but is effort-dependent. Peak expiratory flow rate PEFR is the highest flow, often in L/min. The flow-volume loop plots flow against volume; obstruction gives a scooped expiratory limb and low peak. Helium dilution and nitrogen washout measure RV and FRC; Chapter 2.8 explains them.

Worked example

  3. The patient's ratio 0.40 is below 0.70, and FEV1 is low at 1.2 L. This is an obstructive pattern.
  4. Peak flow = 400 L/min ÷ 60 s/min = 6.666... L/s ≈ 6.67 L/s.

Exam traps

  • Using FEV1 alone: a low FEV1 occurs in both obstruction and restriction; only the FEV1/FVC ratio separates them.
  • Expecting a spirometer to measure residual volume or FRC: that air never leaves the lungs, so only helium dilution, nitrogen washout or a plethysmograph can find it (Chapter 2.8).
  • Forgetting BTPS: comparing an ATPS volume with a BTPS predicted value underreads the lung volume by about 8%.
  • Unit slip: PEFR is often in L/min while FEV1 and FVC are in litres; divide by 60 to get L/s.
  • Calling any low FVC restrictive: obstruction can also reduce FVC; check the ratio and the flow-volume shape.

2.6 Pneumotachometer; intra-alveolar and thoracic pressures

Air moves because a pressure difference pushes it. A pneumotachometer measures that push as a small drop across a known resistance, so the drop becomes flow, and flow integrated over time becomes volume. Pressure sensors inside the alveoli and in the oesophagus show the effort the breathing muscles make. These measurements appear in pulmonary function labs, sleep studies, ventilators and exercise testing, where a doctor needs to separate a blocked airway from a stiff lung.

You need: Chapter 2.5 for lung volumes, and Unit 1 for the differential pressure transducer and carrier amplifier.

Flow from a known resistance

The pneumotachometer is a deliberate resistance in the breathing path. Air crosses a fine mesh or a bundle of small tubes, and pressure taps before and after it feed a differential pressure transducer. For smooth, laminar flow the drop follows Poiseuille's law: it is proportional to flow. One calibration number, the resistance, then links pressure drop and flow.

ΔP = R × Q

ΔP is the pressure drop in cm H2O; R is the resistance in cm H2O per L/s; Q is the flow in L/s.

A Fleisch pneumotachograph uses many parallel capillaries. The parallel paths keep resistance low and keep flow laminar. A screen pneumotachograph uses a woven wire mesh: cheaper, but it clogs more easily. A hot-wire pneumotachograph heats a thin wire and measures how much the flow cools it; it has no mesh and no simple pressure-drop law.

  1. Set zero with no flow and the mouthpiece blocked.
  2. Apply a known steady flow. If 0.5 L/s gives 0.75 cm H2O, sensitivity is 0.75/0.5 = 1.5 cm H2O per L/s.
  3. Keep flow inside the laminar range; turbulence makes the drop rise faster than flow.
  4. Integrate the flow signal over time to get volume, and correct any slow zero drift.

Pressures inside and around the alveoli

Intra-alveolar pressure (P_alv) is the pressure inside the air sacs. With a shutter closed and no flow, mouth pressure equals alveolar pressure; otherwise a small catheter can reach it. Intrapleural pressure (P_pl) is the pressure in the thin space around the lung. It is estimated with a small balloon in the oesophagus, because oesophageal pressure tracks pleural pressure.

Transpulmonary pressure is the distending pressure across the lung. Transthoracic pressure is the pressure across the chest wall, from pleural space to body surface. Pressures use cm H2O; 1 cm H2O = 0.098 kPa. At rest, intrapleural pressure is about −5 cm H2O and alveolar pressure is about 0 to ±1 cm H2O.

P_tp = P_alv − P_pl

P_tp is transpulmonary pressure in cm H2O; P_alv is intra-alveolar pressure in cm H2O; P_pl is intrapleural pressure in cm H2O.

Airway resistance (R_aw) links flow and pressure. With expiratory flow taken as positive, P_alv = P_mouth + R_aw × Q, where P_mouth is mouth pressure. During inspiration the same magnitude appears with the opposite sign, so alveolar pressure falls below mouth pressure. A blocked airway raises R_aw and needs a larger pressure difference for the same flow. Airway resistance measurement is covered in Chapter 2.8.

Calibration, integration and drift

The differential transducer measures only the small drop across the mesh, so it must have good sensitivity and a stable zero. The carrier amplifier boosts the transducer output. Calibration is done with a syringe that delivers a known volume or with a steady reference flow. The flow signal is then integrated to give volume.

V = ∫ Q dt

V is volume in L; Q is flow in L/s; t is time in s.

Integration accumulates any offset. A zero error of 0.01 L/s for 60 s adds 0.6 L of false volume. Modern instruments reset the integrator at the start of each breath or subtract a measured zero. The same flow signal can drive a volume display and a flow-volume loop. Check linearity with a calibrated syringe before a test, because a clogged mesh changes the resistance and the sensitivity.

Worked example

Question. A Fleisch pneumotachograph is calibrated: a steady flow of 0.5 L/s gives a differential pressure of 0.75 cm H2O. (a) Find its sensitivity in cm H2O per L/s. (b) The flow is integrated over a 1 s inspiration with average flow 0.5 L/s; find the tidal volume in L. (c) For a steady expiratory flow of 0.5 L/s, airway resistance 2 cm H2O per L/s and mouth pressure 0 cm H2O, find the alveolar pressure.   2. (b) Volume = average flow × time = 0.5 L/s × 1 s = 0.5 L.
  3. (c) Take expiratory flow as positive: P_alv = P_mouth + R_aw × Q = 0 + 2 × 0.5 = 1 cm H2O.
  4. Transpulmonary pressure P_tp = P_alv − P_pl = 1 − (−5) = 6 cm H2O.

Exam traps

  • Gauge pressure versus absolute pressure: airway and pneumotachometer pressures are usually gauge pressures, measured relative to atmosphere. Mixing them with absolute pressure gives wrong alveolar or transpulmonary values.
  • Sign convention: expiratory flow positive gives P_alv = P_mouth + R_aw × Q; inspiratory flow reverses the sign. A sign slip changes −1 cm H2O into +1 cm H2O.
  • Linear only while laminar: the calibrated resistance is invalid when flow becomes turbulent, so high-flow readings need a different curve or a different sensor.
  • Unit slip: 1 cm H2O is about 0.098 kPa, not 1 kPa, and not 1 mmHg. Convert before comparing with kPa or mmHg.
  • Intra-alveolar is not intrapleural: alveolar pressure is inside the air sac; intrapleural pressure is in the space around the lung. Transpulmonary pressure is their difference.

2.7 Oximetry, impedance pneumography and apnea monitors

Oximetry reads oxygen saturation from the colour of pulsating blood. Two light-emitting diodes, red at about 660 nm and infrared at about 940 nm, shine through a finger. A photodiode measures what passes. The pulsatile part is separated from the steady tissue part, and their ratio gives SpO₂. Impedance pneumography watches breathing from the small rise in chest impedance as air enters the lungs. An apnoea monitor alarms when that change stops for 10-20 s. These three measurements sit together at the bedside.

You need: breathing volumes and flow from Chapter 2.5 and Chapter 2.6, and bridge circuits and bio-amplifiers from Unit 1.

Pulse oximetry: two wavelengths and one ratio

Pulse oximetry rests on the Beer–Lambert law: absorbance is proportional to concentration and path length; the light that gets through falls exponentially with both.

A = ε c d

A is absorbance (no unit); ε is the molar absorption coefficient at that wavelength, in litre per mole per centimetre (L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹); c is concentration (moles per litre); d is path length (centimetres). The units cancel, so A has no unit.

In a finger, light crosses skin, bone, venous blood and arterial blood. The steady part is DC. Each heartbeat pushes arterial blood into the finger, so the light path through arterial blood lengthens and shortens; this small changing part is AC. The AC is the pulsatile arterial signal.

Oxygenated and reduced haemoglobin absorb differently. At red 660 nm, reduced haemoglobin absorbs more. At infrared 940 nm, oxygenated haemoglobin absorbs more. Two wavelengths therefore separate oxygen saturation from the total amount of blood and tissue in the path. A red LED and an infrared LED are switched on alternately, often several hundred times per second. One photodiode on the far side measures the transmitted light for each wavelength.

  1. Switch the red and infrared LEDs alternately.
  2. Convert the photodiode current to a voltage and separate the two wavelength channels.
  3. Split each channel into DC by low-pass filtering and AC by band-pass filtering at the heart rate.
  4. Compute the ratio R from the two channels.
  5. Read SpO₂ from an empirical calibration table or curve.
R = (ACred / DCred) ÷ (ACIR / DCIR)

ACred and ACIR are the pulsatile amplitudes in the red and infrared channels (same light units); DCred and DCIR are the steady levels (same units); R is dimensionless.

R rises as saturation falls. The machine converts R to SpO₂ through an empirical calibration curve, built by measuring R in volunteers at known saturation. In one common table, R = 0.55 reads about 98%.

What pulse oximetry cannot tell you

SpO₂ is a percentage: oxygenated haemoglobin as a share of oxygenated plus reduced haemoglobin in arterial blood. Carboxyhaemoglobin and methaemoglobin are not counted as reduced haemoglobin, which is why they push the reading in the wrong direction. It is not a measure of breathing. A patient on oxygen can have a normal SpO₂ while breathing badly and retaining carbon dioxide. Carbon dioxide is seen by capnography or blood gas; apnoea is seen by chest movement or airflow. Saturation alone cannot detect either. This is the central limit of pulse oximetry.

  • Motion: probe movement changes the light path; the AC looks like a pulse and R becomes wrong.
  • Low perfusion: a cold finger, shock or low blood pressure makes the arterial AC small; noise dominates.
  • Nail polish and ambient light: dark polish absorbs red and infrared; strong room light adds a steady offset.
  • Carboxyhaemoglobin: carbon monoxide poisoning makes haemoglobin absorb red light like oxygenated haemoglobin, so SpO₂ reads falsely high.
  • Venous pulsation and severe anaemia can also mislead; the displayed value is SpO₂, not arterial SaO₂ from a blood sample.

Some syllabi call oximetry an extra bedside measurement rather than a core one; with ECG, blood pressure and respiration it completes the set.

Impedance pneumography and apnoea monitors

Impedance pneumography uses the chest as a conductor. Air is a poor conductor, so when the lungs fill, transthoracic impedance rises. Two or four electrodes are placed on the chest. A small alternating current, about 50 kHz, is passed between a pair of electrodes; another pair measures the voltage. The current is kept small for safety. The impedance change is roughly 1 Ω per litre of air in a typical adult.

A bridge circuit converts the small impedance change into a voltage. A band-pass amplifier keeps the breathing frequency and rejects drift and the ECG. The output is a respiration waveform: up on inspiration, down on expiration. Chest movement and the cardiac stroke add artefacts; the cardiac stroke is a small bump at the heart rate on top of the breathing wave.

S = ΔZ / ΔV

S is sensitivity (ohms per litre); ΔZ is the impedance change (ohms); ΔV is the volume change (litres). A 0.5 Ω change with a 0.5 L breath gives S = 1 Ω per litre.

An apnoea monitor watches this waveform. It sounds an alarm when no impedance change larger than a set amount occurs for 10-20 s. It is used for neonates in hospital and at home. A threshold that is too low alarms on cardiac artefact; too high misses shallow breathing.

Worked example

Question. A finger probe gives red channel DC = 100 units and AC = 2 units, infrared channel DC = 110 units and AC = 4 units. (a) Compute R. (b) The calibration table maps R = 0.55 to about 98%; which way does R move if saturation falls? (c) An apnoea monitor shows a 0.5 Ω impedance change with a 500 mL breath; find its sensitivity.

  1. Red channel ratio: AC/DC = 2/100 = 0.020 (dimensionless).
  2. Infrared channel ratio: AC/DC = 4/110 = 0.03636 (dimensionless).
  3. R = 0.020 / 0.03636 = 0.55 (dimensionless).
  4. Less oxygenated blood absorbs more red light, so the red AC/DC term grows; the infrared term falls; R rises and the table reads a lower SpO₂.
  5. S = ΔZ / ΔV = 0.5 Ω / 0.5 L = 1 Ω per litre.

Answer. R = 0.55, so the table reads about 98%; a fall in saturation raises R; the monitor sensitivity is 1 Ω per litre.

Exam traps

  • A normal SpO₂ does not prove good breathing. A patient on oxygen can hypoventilate and retain carbon dioxide while the saturation looks fine.
  • R rises as saturation falls. Do not invert the ratio or read the calibration table backwards.
  • AC is the pulsatile arterial part; DC is the steady tissue, bone and venous part. Swapping them destroys the measurement.
  • The figure 1 Ω per litre is a typical adult value, not a fixed constant. Electrode position, chest shape and lung volume change it.
  • Carboxyhaemoglobin and nail polish can make SpO₂ falsely high or low. Motion and low perfusion make the ratio noisy, not just the waveform ugly.

2.8 Whole-body plethysmograph, residual volume and airway resistance

A whole-body plethysmograph is a sealed box, about 600 L, in which the subject sits and breathes through a shutter. Small pressure swings in the box and at the mouth let the instrument find the gas volume in the chest by Boyle's law. That gives residual volume, the air left after a full breath out, which a spirometer cannot measure. With the shutter open, the same box estimates alveolar pressure and so airway resistance. Clinics use it for asthma, COPD and unexplained breathlessness.

You need: Chapter 2.5 for lung-volume names and Chapter 2.6 for flow, pressure and airway resistance.

The sealed box and two pressure signals

The box is rigid and sealed, so the gas inside has a fixed mass. The subject sits inside and breathes through a mouthpiece. A shutter can close the mouthpiece. Two pressure transducers record the signals: one measures box pressure, Pbox; the other measures mouth pressure, Pmouth. Box pressure swings are tiny, about 0.05 cm H2O, while mouth swings are about 10 cm H2O in the same pant. cm H2O is a pressure unit; 1 cm H2O = 98.1 Pa.

The subject pants at about 1 Hz, one cycle per second, against the closed shutter. Fast small swings keep box gas adiabatic: no heat is exchanged with the walls, so box gas follows P Vγ = constant, where γ (gamma) ≈ 1.4 for air. Lung gas stays isothermal, meaning at constant temperature, because it is in close contact with blood and tissue, so it follows Boyle's law, P1V1 = P2V2. The box pressure records how the chest volume changes; the mouth pressure records the alveolar pressure swing.

Thoracic gas volume and residual volume

With the shutter closed at the end of a quiet breath out, the lung volume is the functional residual capacity, FRC. The panting compresses and expands that trapped gas. The ratio of the two pressure swings gives the trapped volume:

Vtg ≈ Vbox × (ΔPbox / ΔPmouth)   (exam approximation)

Vtg is thoracic gas volume in litres; Vbox is the sealed box volume in litres; ΔPbox and ΔPmouth are the peak-to-peak pressure swings in the same pressure unit, usually cm H2O. The exact relation carries the adiabatic factor for box gas, about 1.4: Vtg ≈ Vbox × ΔPbox/(1.4 × ΔPmouth).

Using the exam approximation, a 600 L box with a 0.05 cm H2O box swing and a 10 cm H2O mouth swing gives Vtg = 600 × 0.05/10 = 3.0 L. At that moment Vtg equals FRC. A spirometer can measure expiratory reserve volume, ERV, the extra air pushed out after a quiet breath out, and vital capacity, VC, the largest breath. It cannot measure what stays inside. Subtract:

RV = FRC − ERV; TLC = RV + VC

RV is residual volume; FRC is functional residual capacity, equal to Vtg when the shutter closes at end-expiration; ERV is expiratory reserve volume; TLC is total lung capacity; VC is vital capacity. All volumes in litres.

Residual volume is the air left after a full forced breath out; total lung capacity is the sum of residual volume and vital capacity. Lung gas is reported at BTPS: body temperature, ambient pressure, saturated with water vapour. At 37°C water vapour pressure is 47 mmHg. If a question gives barometric pressure PB, the full Boyle's-law correction is Vtg × (PB − 47)/PB; at PB = 760 mmHg the factor is about 0.938. Exam sums often use the simple ratio, as in the worked example.

Airway resistance with the shutter open

The shutter opens. The subject breathes through a pneumotachometer, which measures airflow from a small pressure drop across a known resistance. Alveolar pressure cannot be measured directly, so the box pressure is used as a surrogate. A calibration factor k converts box pressure swing to alveolar pressure: PA = k × ΔPbox. The factor is found with a known resistance or a mouth pressure reference.

Airway resistance is the pressure difference from alveolus to mouth divided by flow:

Raw = (PA − Pmouth) / V̇

Raw is airway resistance in cm H2O per L/s; PA is alveolar pressure in cm H2O; Pmouth is mouth pressure in cm H2O; V̇ (V-dot) is airflow in L/s.

A flow of 2 L/s with a 4 cm H2O alveolar-to-mouth difference gives Raw = 4/2 = 2 cm H2O per L/s. Normal airway resistance is roughly 0.6 to 2.4 cm H2O per L/s. Values above this suggest obstruction. Conductance is 1/Raw. Specific airway conductance divides conductance by Vtg, so it is less dependent on lung size; only a few universities ask for it. Units matter: write cm H2O per L/s, not cm H2O/L.

Worked example

Question. A subject sits in a 600 L plethysmograph. With the shutter closed he pants; mouth pressure swings by 10 cm H2O and box pressure by 0.05 cm H2O. (a) Find the thoracic gas volume. (b) His expiratory reserve volume is 1.2 L and slow vital capacity is 4.0 L; find residual volume and total lung capacity. (c) With the shutter open, a flow of 2 L/s gives a box pressure swing equal to 4 cm H2O of alveolar pressure; find airway resistance.

  1. Write the closed-shutter exam approximation: Vtg = Vbox × ΔPbox/ΔPmouth. Put Vbox = 600 L, ΔPbox = 0.05 cm H2O, ΔPmouth = 10 cm H2O.
  2. Vtg = 600 × 0.05/10 = 600 × 0.005 = 3.0 L. This is FRC because the shutter was closed at end-expiration.
  3. Residual volume = FRC − ERV = 3.0 − 1.2 = 1.8 L.
  4. Total lung capacity = RV + VC = 1.8 + 4.0 = 5.8 L.
  5. Airway resistance = pressure difference / flow = 4/2 = 2.0 cm H2O per L/s. This is within the normal range of about 0.6 to 2.4 cm H2O per L/s, toward its upper end. Obstruction is suggested only when Raw rises above about 2.4 cm H2O per L/s.

Answer. Vtg = 3.0 L (FRC), residual volume = 1.8 L, total lung capacity = 5.8 L, and airway resistance = 2.0 cm H2O per L/s, within the normal range toward its upper end; obstruction is suggested only above about 2.4 cm H2O per L/s.

Exam traps

  • Treating ΔPbox as ΔPmouth: box swings are about 0.05 cm H2O, mouth swings about 10 cm H2O; the ratio has no units and must be applied to Vbox.
  • Forgetting BTPS and water vapour: lung gas is at body temperature, pressure and saturation; subtract 47 mmHg water vapour pressure before using Boyle's law if the question gives barometric pressure.
  • Mixing FRC and residual volume: Vtg at shutter closure is FRC, not residual volume; residual volume = FRC − ERV.
  • Using airway-resistance units as cm H2O/L instead of cm H2O per L/s.
  • Assuming box pressure is alveolar pressure in open-shutter resistance: use the calibration factor between box pressure and alveolar pressure.

Check yourself

Remember What does a whole-body plethysmograph measure when the shutter is closed? 2 marks

It measures thoracic gas volume at the current lung volume, usually FRC, from Boyle's law using box and mouth pressure swings.

Understand Why is lung gas treated as isothermal but box gas as adiabatic during panting? 3 marks

Lung gas exchanges heat with blood and tissue and has a large surface, so its compression is near isothermal. Box gas has little time and small surface for heat exchange during about 1 Hz panting, so its compression is near adiabatic.

Apply A 500 L box gives ΔPbox = 0.06 cm H2O for ΔPmouth = 12 cm H2O. Find Vtg. 4 marks

Vtg = 500 × 0.06/12 = 2.5 L.

Apply Vtg = 3.5 L, ERV = 1.1 L, VC = 4.2 L. Find RV and TLC. 5 marks

RV = 3.5 − 1.1 = 2.4 L; TLC = 2.4 + 4.2 = 6.6 L.

Analyze A patient has normal VC but a high RV/TLC ratio. Which measurement did spirometry miss and how does plethysmography catch it? 4 marks

Spirometry misses RV because RV cannot be exhaled. Plethysmography measures FRC as Vtg, subtracts ERV to get RV, so it shows hyperinflation when RV/TLC is high.

Apply Flow is 1.5 L/s and the alveolar-to-mouth pressure difference is 3 cm H2O. Find airway resistance. 3 marks

Raw = 3/1.5 = 2 cm H2O per L/s.

Analyze Why can box pressure be used for airway resistance only after a calibration factor, while mouth pressure is used directly? 5 marks

Box pressure is a surrogate for alveolar pressure; it depends on box volume, gas compression and subject movement. A calibration relates box swing to alveolar pressure. Mouth pressure is measured at the mouth, so the pressure difference is direct.

Takeaways

  1. Blood pressure is always a difference between arterial pressure and the atmosphere, so zeroing the sensor to air is part of every measurement.
  2. The cuff method reads pressure indirectly from Korotkoff sounds; the catheter method reads it directly but needs a sterile artery puncture.
  3. Cardiac output is found from dilution by dividing a known amount of indicator by the area under its downstream concentration-time curve.
  4. Heart sounds are low-frequency events timed to valve closure, and the phonocardiograph is only useful when the sound channel is displayed beside an ECG.
  5. A foetal monitor must reject the mother's much larger ECG or heartbeat before it can count the baby's rate.
  6. Spirometry reports volume and flow under BTPS, and the FEV1/FVC ratio separates an obstructed airway from a stiff lung.
  7. Air flow is measured as a pressure drop; lung and chest pressures are measured to judge how hard the breathing muscles are working.
  8. A pulse oximeter and an impedance apnea monitor are cheap, non-invasive guards for a patient on a hospital bed, and both can be fooled by movement.
  9. A body plethysmograph uses Boyle's law inside a sealed box to find residual volume and airway resistance without asking the patient to cooperate fully.

Remember and understand

Understand Define systolic, diastolic and mean arterial pressure. Describe the auscultatory method of indirect blood pressure measurement and explain what produces each phase of the Korotkoff sounds. 13 marks

Pressure is measured relative to atmosphere, in mmHg; Cuff placed on upper arm, inflated above systolic to stop flow; Cuff deflated slowly at 2-3 mmHg per second; Turbulent flow under the cuff produces phase 1 tapping sound = systolic; Sound muffling (phase 4) and disappearance (phase 5) = diastolic; Cuff must be at heart level to avoid hydrostatic error; MAP = diastolic + (systolic - diastolic)/3; Sources of error: cuff size, observer hearing, deflation rate.

Remember Explain the origin of the four heart sounds and describe a phonocardiograph recording system, including the choice between the bell and diaphragm of a stethoscope. 12 marks

S1 from mitral and tricuspid closure at start of systole; S2 from aortic and pulmonary closure at end of systole; S3 early diastolic filling sound, S4 late diastolic atrial sound; Microphone, low-noise amplifier, band-pass filter of roughly 20-600 Hz; Simultaneous ECG for timing; Bell picks up low frequencies, diaphragm filters them out for higher murmurs; Systole is the interval between S1 and S2, diastole between S2 and the next S1.

Understand Describe the dye dilution and thermal dilution methods of cardiac output measurement. Give the working equation and list the assumptions. 13 marks

Indicator injected upstream, concentration sensed downstream; Stewart-Hamilton: CO = amount injected / (mean concentration x time base); Dye such as indocyanine green, measured by densitometry at the ear or artery; Thermal dilution uses cold saline and a thermistor on a pulmonary artery catheter; Curve must have a clean exponential decay, no recirculation during measurement; Indicator must mix completely and be lost only by flow; Thermal method needs correction for heat loss to catheter wall; Sources of error: injection loss, recirculation, unstable flow.

Understand Draw and explain a normal forced expiratory spirogram. Define FVC, FEV1, FEV1/FVC and FEF25-75, and state how the shape separates obstruction from restriction. 13 marks

Volume-time curve: steep first second, then plateau; FVC = total volume exhaled after full inspiration; FEV1 = volume in first second; Normal FEV1/FVC about 0.75-0.80 in adults; Obstruction: low ratio because FEV1 falls more than FVC; Restriction: normal or high ratio but both FEV1 and FVC reduced; FEF25-75 is the average flow over the middle half of the breath, sensitive to small-airway disease; Flow-volume loop shapes: scooped in obstruction, narrowed in restriction.

Apply

Apply An indicator dilution curve is recorded after injecting 5 mg of dye. The mean concentration over the curve is 2.5 mg per litre and the curve base lasts 12 s. Calculate cardiac output in litres per minute and comment on whether the value is physiological. 10 marks

CO = amount / (mean concentration x time); Convert time to minutes: 12 s = 0.2 min; CO = 5 / (2.5 x 0.2) = 10 L/min; Resting adult value is about 5 L/min, so 10 L/min is high; High output suggests exercise, sepsis, anaemia or a left-to-right shunt; Check injection loss, curve extrapolation and calibration.

Apply A spirometer calibrated at ATPS reads 3.00 L for a forced vital capacity at 25 °C and 750 mmHg. Describe how the reading is converted to BTPS and explain why the correction is needed. Also list the checks a 3 L calibration syringe is used for. 10 marks

BTPS = body temperature 37 °C, ambient pressure, water vapour saturated; Gas expands as it warms from 25 °C to 37 °C; BTPS factor from standard tables, roughly 1.08 at this temperature and pressure; Corrected FVC approximately 3.24 L; Reason: the lung reports volumes at body conditions, so clinic readings must match; Syringe used to check linearity, leaks, and volume accuracy of the sensor; Also check that a known 3 L push reads about 3 L on the display.

Apply A screen pneumotachometer has a resistance of 2 cm H₂O per litre per second and shows a pressure drop of 6 cm H₂O. Find the flow. Explain how the signal is made linear and how volume is obtained from it. 10 marks

Flow = pressure drop / resistance = 6/2 = 3 L/s; Flow is proportional to pressure drop only when the flow profile is laminar (Poiseuille); Capillary or screen element keeps flow laminar; Two symmetric pressure taps allow bidirectional measurement; Flow is electronically integrated to give volume; Drift of the integrator needs a reset or a high-pass zero; Gas condition affects viscosity, so BTPS correction applies.

Apply A two-wavelength pulse oximeter measures the ratio of ratios R = 0.6. Explain how R is formed, how it is converted to SpO2, and describe three situations in which the reading becomes unreliable. 10 marks

Red and infrared LEDs alternately illuminate the finger; Photodiode current has a steady and a pulsatile part; Ratio uses the pulsatile (AC) divided by steady (DC) part at each wavelength; R = AC/DC at red divided by AC/DC at infrared; R is mapped to SpO2 through a calibration curve from volunteer studies; Unreliable with poor perfusion, motion artefact, nail polish or bright light; Carboxyhaemoglobin and methaemoglobin read falsely high or low.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare direct and indirect methods of blood pressure measurement. Discuss which is chosen in an intensive care unit, in a home UPI-era health check, and during exercise testing, giving reasons. 13 marks

Direct: catheter in artery with strain-gauge or fluid-coupled transducer, continuous waveform, gold standard; Direct risks: infection, thrombosis, bleeding, needs trained staff; Indirect: cuff with auscultation, oscillometry or ultrasound, non-invasive, intermittent; Indirect errors: cuff size, arrhythmia, calcified arteries, observer bias; ICU uses direct arterial line for beat-to-beat values and drug titration; Home and clinic use automated oscillometric cuff devices; Exercise testing needs fast repeated readings, so automated cuffs or finger methods are used; Conclusion: choice depends on need for continuity, accuracy and risk.

Analyze Describe a foetal monitoring system based on abdominal ECG and phonocardiography. Explain how the foetal signal is separated from the maternal signal and how the arrhythmia monitor in the same unit measures RR intervals. 14 marks

Abdominal electrodes pick up both maternal and foetal ECG; Foetal R wave is smaller; maternal complexes are removed by subtraction or gating; Foetal heart rate computed from foetal RR interval, about 110-160 beats per minute; Ultrasound Doppler and foetal phonocardiography are alternatives; Cardiotocograph plots foetal rate against uterine contractions; Arrhythmia monitor measures each RR interval and compares with running average; Alarm when rate crosses limits or RR changes abruptly; Artefacts: movement, electrode loss, missed R waves; need blanking and averaging.

Evaluate A body plethysmograph is used to measure thoracic gas volume and airway resistance. Explain the principle with Boyle's law, derive the volume equation, and discuss why the panting manoeuvre is used. 14 marks

Patient sits in a sealed box and breathes through a shutter and mouthpiece; Shutter closes and the patient pants against it; Alveolar gas is compressed and rarefied, mouth pressure tracks alveolar pressure; Box pressure changes oppositely as box volume changes; Boyle's law: P1V1 = P2V2 applied to the gas in the chest and to the box; Thoracic gas volume obtained, residual volume = thoracic gas volume minus expiratory reserve volume; Shutter open, flow and mouth pressure give airway resistance from pressure-flow slope; Panting keeps gas exchange and temperature effects small.

Evaluate Design a bedside monitoring chain for a post-operative patient that continuously shows ECG, SpO2, blood pressure and breathing. State what each channel measures, how alarms are set, and how artefacts are reduced. 16 marks

ECG electrodes with high common-mode rejection amplifier and driven right leg; SpO2 finger probe with ratio-of-ratios processing; Blood pressure by automated cuff, or arterial line if continuous values are needed; Respiration from chest impedance bands between ECG electrodes; Alarm limits set from the patient's baseline with delay to avoid false calls; Artefacts: movement, mains interference, electrode drying, poor perfusion; Use of filters, averaging, lead-off detection and signal quality indicators; Isolation barrier for patient safety and defibrillation protection.

Evaluate Compare the pneumotachometer, the spirometer and the impedance method for measuring ventilation. Discuss accuracy, patient effort and suitability for long-term apnea monitoring. 13 marks

Pneumotachometer gives true flow from a pressure drop, needs a mouthpiece and is accurate but interferes with breathing; Spirometer integrates flow or volume directly, used for forced manoeuvres in the clinic; Impedance method is non-contact apart from surface electrodes, gives relative change not absolute volume; Impedance is best for apnea alarm, not for lung function numbers; Movement and cardiac pulsation corrupt the impedance signal; Choice depends on whether absolute volume or a trend is needed; A 3 L syringe check is standard for spirometer and pneumotachograph chains.

Glossary

Apnea
A pause in breathing, commonly alarming when it lasts longer than about 10 to 20 seconds.
Arrhythmia
Any abnormal rhythm, detected by comparing each RR interval with set limits.
ATPS
Ambient temperature and pressure, saturated with water vapour; conditions at the spirometer itself.
Auscultation
Listening to body sounds, here the Korotkoff sounds over an artery below a cuff.
Bell
The cup-shaped chest piece of a stethoscope, best for low-frequency sounds such as S3 and S4.
Body plethysmograph
A sealed box in which pressure changes around a breathing subject give lung volume and airway resistance.
Boyle's law
At constant temperature, pressure times volume of a fixed amount of gas stays constant.
Cardiac output
Volume of blood pumped by the heart per minute, about 5 L/min at rest in an adult.
Diaphragm
The flat chest piece of a stethoscope, which filters low frequencies and highlights murmurs.
Diastole
The part of the cardiac cycle when the ventricles relax and fill.
Dry rolling seal
A spirometer design in which a piston rolls on a flexible seal with low friction, giving an accurate volume signal.
Expiratory reserve volume
Volume that can still be breathed out after a normal quiet expiration.
Fick principle
Blood flow equals the amount of a substance taken up per minute divided by the arteriovenous concentration difference.
Foetal heart rate
The baby's heart rate, normally about 110 to 160 beats per minute.
Helium dilution
Method that finds lung volume from the dilution of a known amount of helium in a closed circuit.
Indocyanine green
A dye used for dilution cardiac output, measured optically because it absorbs near 800 nm.
Intra-alveolar pressure
Pressure inside the air sacs, equal to atmospheric when no air is moving.
Oscillometry
Automated cuff method that reads pressure from the size of the pressure pulsations in the cuff.
Peak expiratory flow rate
The highest flow, in litres per minute, reached during a forced expiration.
Photoplethysmography
Optical recording of the pulsatile blood volume in a finger or earlobe.
RR interval
Time between two successive R waves of the ECG, in milliseconds; inverse gives heart rate.
Spirometer
Instrument that records the volume of air breathed in and out and the speed of the breath.
Strain-gauge transducer
A diaphragm whose resistance changes when pressure bends it, used in direct blood pressure lines.
Systole
The part of the cardiac cycle when the ventricles contract and eject blood.
