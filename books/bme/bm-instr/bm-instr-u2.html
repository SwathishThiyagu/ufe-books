2.1 Blood pressure measurement: direct and indirect

Blood pressure is the pressure of blood inside an artery, quoted as two numbers: systolic, the peak during a heartbeat, and diastolic, the trough between beats. Their difference is the pulse pressure, and the mean arterial pressure (MAP) is the average that pushes blood to the organs. These numbers detect hypertension, guide treatment in shock or on a ventilator, and check that a drug is working. The same pressure can be read by a sensor placed inside the artery (direct) or by a cuff wrapped round the arm (indirect).

You need: physiological transducers, the strain-gauge bridge and bio-amplifiers (see Physiological transducers and load cells, and Bio-amplifiers and data acquisition systems).

Direct measurement: sensor inside the artery

A cannula is inserted into the radial artery at the wrist, sometimes the brachial or femoral artery. It connects to a stiff saline-filled catheter that ends at a strain-gauge transducer. The transducer has a diaphragm with a strain gauge bridge bonded to it: pressure bends the diaphragm, the bridge changes resistance, a bridge amplifier converts that to a voltage, and a monitor draws the pulse waveform. This is the reference method. It gives a beat-by-beat waveform, not just two numbers, so it is used in intensive care and during surgery.

  1. Flush the catheter with saline and remove all bubbles.
  2. Zero the transducer at the level of the right atrium, roughly the mid-axillary line, with the tap open to air.
  3. Turn the tap to the artery and check the waveform on the monitor.
  4. Read systolic and diastolic from the waveform; in a ventilated patient read at end-expiration.

The catheter and transducer form a second-order system with a natural frequency and a damping ratio. If the damping ratio is too low, the trace rings or overshoots after each systolic peak, and systolic pressure reads falsely high. If the natural frequency is too low, the trace cannot follow the fast systolic upstroke, the peaks are blunted and systolic pressure reads low. A bubble in the line adds compliance, lowers the natural frequency and flattens the trace, so the systolic peak is lost.

MAP = D + (S − D) / 3

MAP is mean arterial pressure in mmHg; S is systolic pressure in mmHg; D is diastolic pressure in mmHg. The formula holds at normal heart rates.

The cuff and Korotkoff sounds

An inflatable rubber bladder inside a cloth cuff is wrapped round the upper arm and pumped above systolic pressure. The brachial artery is squeezed shut and flow stops. Air is let out slowly. When cuff pressure falls just below systolic, blood squirts through the partly open artery as a turbulent jet, the artery wall vibrates, and a stethoscope over the artery below the cuff hears the sound. These are Korotkoff sounds, named after Nikolai Korotkov, the Russian surgeon who described them in 1905.

Phase V is diastolic in most adults. In some patients the sounds never disappear: children, pregnancy and aortic regurgitation are common reasons. If sounds are still heard when the cuff pressure reaches zero, phase IV (muffling) is taken as diastolic instead.

The mercury manometer is a vertical glass tube; cuff pressure pushes mercury up a calibrated scale. It is accurate but is being withdrawn because mercury is toxic. The aneroid manometer uses a Bourdon tube or bellows moving a needle, and it needs regular calibration. In the palpatory method the operator feels the radial pulse return as the cuff falls; this gives systolic pressure only, and it is used when sounds are hard to hear. A Doppler probe over the artery detects the frequency shift from moving blood and is used in infants and in peripheral arteries.

Oscillometric method and sources of error

Automated oscillometric monitors use the same cuff but no microphone. A pressure sensor watches the small pressure pulses that the artery pushes into the cuff as it opens and closes with each beat. The size of these oscillations rises to a maximum and then falls. The cuff pressure at maximum oscillation is taken as MAP; the machine then finds systolic and diastolic from fixed ratios of the oscillation amplitude, for example systolic where the amplitude has risen to about half of its maximum and diastolic where it has fallen to about 0.8 of it. Each maker uses its own ratios, tested against the auscultatory method. The method needs no trained ear, works through thin clothing, and is used in home monitors and ward machines.

  • Cuff too narrow: reads falsely high. The bladder should be about 40 percent of the arm circumference in width and about 80 percent of the arm circumference in length, so that it wraps most of the way round the arm.
  • Arm below heart level: reads high, roughly 0.8 mmHg for every centimetre of drop.
  • Deflation faster than 3 mmHg per second: underestimates systolic and overestimates diastolic.
  • Talking, a full bladder, caffeine or anxiety all raise the reading. A rise seen only in the clinic, with normal home readings, is the white-coat effect.

Worked example

Question. A 12 cm wide cuff on an arm of 28 cm circumference is inflated to 160 mmHg and let down at 3 mmHg/s. Korotkoff sounds appear at 128 mmHg, become muffled at 100 mmHg and disappear at 82 mmHg. (a) Is the cuff the right size? (b) Give systolic, diastolic, pulse and mean arterial pressure, and systolic pressure in kPa (1 mmHg = 0.1333 kPa). (c) How long after deflation starts is the first sound heard? (d) A radial artery catheter in the same patient reads 134/80 mmHg. Why is it different, and does the mean pressure change?

  1. (a) Bladder width ÷ arm circumference = 12 ÷ 28 = 0.43, close to the 40 % rule, so the cuff is the right size.
  2. (b) Systolic is the cuff pressure at the first Korotkoff sound: S = 128 mmHg. Diastolic is taken at phase V, where the sounds disappear: D = 82 mmHg. Phase IV at 100 mmHg is noted, not used.
  3. Pulse pressure = S − D = 128 − 82 = 46 mmHg. MAP = D + (S − D)/3 = 82 + 46/3 = 82 + 15.3 = 97.3 mmHg.
  4. Systolic in kPa = 128 × 0.1333 = 17.1 kPa.
  5. (c) The cuff falls from 160 to 128 mmHg, a drop of 32 mmHg, at 3 mmHg/s: 32 ÷ 3 = 10.7 s. It reaches 82 mmHg after 78 ÷ 3 = 26 s, so the whole reading takes about half a minute.
  6. (d) Radial catheter MAP = 80 + (134 − 80)/3 = 80 + 18 = 98 mmHg, within 1 mmHg of the cuff value. As the pulse wave travels from the aorta to the wrist, reflected waves make the peak taller and the trough slightly lower (peripheral amplification). So the radial line reads systolic higher and diastolic a little lower than a cuff on the upper arm, while the mean pressure stays almost the same.

Answer. The cuff is the right size (0.43 of the arm). Systolic 128 mmHg (17.1 kPa), diastolic 82 mmHg, pulse pressure 46 mmHg, MAP 97.3 mmHg; the first sound comes 10.7 s after deflation starts. The radial line differs because of peripheral amplification, but its MAP of 98 mmHg matches the cuff.

Exam traps

  • Writing phase IV (muffling, 100 mmHg) as diastolic when the examiner expects phase V (disappearance, 82 mmHg).
  • Forgetting that a cuff that is too narrow reads falsely high, and one that is too wide reads falsely low.
  • Zeroing the transducer at the shelf level instead of at the right atrium, which adds a hydrostatic error.
  • Mixing units: 1 mmHg = 0.1333 kPa, so a pressure in kPa is about 7.5 times smaller than the same pressure in mmHg.
  • Assuming an oscillometric machine finds systolic at the first oscillation; it takes the cuff pressure at maximum oscillation as MAP.

2.2 Blood flow and cardiac output: dye and thermal dilution

Cardiac output is the volume of blood the heart pushes out in one minute. Doctors need it to judge whether a sick heart is pumping enough, and to guide drugs and fluids. Dilution methods measure it without opening the chest: inject a known amount of indicator into the blood, then record how the indicator concentration changes downstream. The faster the blood flows, the more the indicator is spread out and the smaller the concentration peak becomes. This chapter covers two hospital standards: dye dilution with indocyanine green, and thermal dilution with cold saline through a pulmonary artery catheter.

You need: the cardiac cycle and the transducer and bio-amplifier basics; the pressure measurement in 2.1 shows the same idea of a sensor on a catheter.

Cardiac output and the dilution principle

Cardiac output (CO) is the blood volume the heart ejects per minute, in litres per minute (L/min). At rest an adult heart moves about 5 L/min; during exercise it can rise several times. CO = heart rate × stroke volume, but measuring stroke volume directly is hard, so dilution methods infer the flow from how blood carries an injected indicator.

The Fick principle is the reference method: oxygen consumption divided by the arteriovenous oxygen difference gives CO. It needs blood oxygen measurement (see Blood gas analyzers and pH measurement). Dilution methods use the same conservation idea with an indicator instead of oxygen.

Inject a known mass I (mg) of indicator into a vein. Downstream, a sensor records concentration c(t) in mg/L against time t in seconds. If blood flow is Q in L/s and the indicator mixes completely, then I = Q ∫ c(t) dt. The integral A = ∫ c(t) dt has units mg·s/L and is the area under the concentration-time curve. Rearranged and converted to minutes:

CO = 60 I / A

CO is cardiac output in L/min; I is the mass of indicator injected in mg; A = ∫ c(t) dt is the area under the first-pass concentration-time curve in mg·s/L; 60 converts seconds to minutes.

The method assumes complete mixing, no indicator loss before the sensor, and steady flow during the measurement. Those assumptions are the reason the recording technique matters.

Dye dilution with indocyanine green

Dye dilution uses indocyanine green (ICG), a green dye that stays in the blood vessels and is removed by the liver. A typical dose is 5 mg, injected rapidly into a central vein. Arterial blood is drawn through a densitometer, a small cuvette that measures light absorption at the dye's wavelength. The recorder plots dye concentration against time. The curve starts to rise a few seconds after injection, when the first bolus reaches the sampling site, peaks a few seconds later, then falls.

After about 20–30 s some dye returns through the circulation and creates a second, smaller hump called recirculation. That hump is not part of the first pass. To find the true first-pass area, plot the falling part of the curve on semilog paper, fit a straight line, and extend it below the recirculation hump. The area under the first-pass curve — the measured part plus the extrapolated part — is A. Because the extrapolated part is an exponential, its area is simply the concentration where extrapolation starts multiplied by the time constant τ of the decay.

Some ICG preparations contain sodium iodide, so ask about iodine allergy. The method is accurate when the curve is clean. The area is what matters; the height alone is not CO.

Thermal dilution and the Swan-Ganz catheter

Thermal dilution replaces the dye with cold fluid and the densitometer with a thermistor. A Swan-Ganz catheter floats through the right heart into the pulmonary artery. It has a proximal port in the right atrium and a thermistor near its tip in the pulmonary artery. A known volume, commonly 10 mL, of cold saline or dextrose is injected into the right atrium. The thermistor records the small fall and recovery of blood temperature as the cold bolus passes.

The recorder plots the temperature fall ΔT (°C) against time (s). The area under this curve, AT = ∫ ΔT dt, has units °C·s. Heat takes the place of the dye mass in the dilution equation:

CO = 60 × VI × (TB − TI) × K1 / AT

CO is cardiac output in L/min; VI is the injectate volume in litres; TB and TI are the blood and injectate temperatures in °C; K1 = (density × specific heat of injectate) ÷ (density × specific heat of blood), about 1.08 for 5 % dextrose; AT is in °C·s; 60 converts L/s to L/min.

Monitors fold everything except AT into one computation constant K, so CO = K / AT; the constant also corrects for the warming of the injectate inside the catheter. Enter the K for the injectate volume and temperature actually used: a wrong K gives a wrong CO even when the curve is perfect.

Iced injectate (0–4 °C) gives a larger temperature difference from blood and so a better signal-to-noise ratio; room-temperature injectate is easier to handle but needs its own constant. Thermal dilution needs no dye and can be repeated every few minutes; its risks are those of the catheter itself.

Worked example

Question. 5 mg of indocyanine green is injected. The arterial densitometer gives these concentrations (mg/L) every 2 s from 4 s to 16 s: 0, 3.0, 7.5, 6.0, 4.2, 3.0, 2.1. After 16 s recirculation lifts the curve. (a) Find the first-pass area, extrapolating the decay beyond 16 s. (b) Find the cardiac output. (c) What would an operator get by also counting a recirculation area of 20 mg·s/L?

  1. (a) Trapezoids from 4 s to 16 s, each 2 s wide: A₁ = 2 × [(0 + 2.1)/2 + 3.0 + 7.5 + 6.0 + 4.2 + 3.0] = 2 × (1.05 + 23.7) = 49.5 mg·s/L.
  2. The tail falls from 3.0 to 2.1 mg/L in 2 s, a ratio of 0.70, so on semilog paper it is a straight line with time constant τ = 2 / ln(3.0/2.1) = 2 / 0.357 = 5.61 s.
  3. Area under the extrapolated tail from 16 s on: A₂ = c(16) × τ = 2.1 × 5.61 = 11.8 mg·s/L.
  4. First-pass area A = A₁ + A₂ = 49.5 + 11.8 = 61.3 mg·s/L.
  5. (b) CO = 60 I / A = 60 × 5 / 61.3 = 4.90 L/min. Units: mg ÷ (mg·s/L) = L/s, times 60 s/min gives L/min.
  6. (c) With the recirculation area added, A = 81.3 mg·s/L and CO = 300 / 81.3 = 3.69 L/min, about 25 % too low.

Answer. A = 61.3 mg·s/L and CO = 4.9 L/min; counting recirculation would wrongly give 3.7 L/min.

Worked example 2

Question. 10 mL of 5 % dextrose at 2 °C is injected into the right atrium of a patient whose blood is at 37 °C. The thermistor curve has area AT = 4.5 °C·s. Take K1 = 1.08. (a) Find the cardiac output. (b) The next injection uses injectate at 22 °C. What area should the curve have if the output is unchanged, and why is iced injectate preferred?

  1. (a) VI = 10 mL = 0.010 L; TB − TI = 37 − 2 = 35 °C.
  2. CO = 60 × 0.010 × 35 × 1.08 / 4.5 = 22.68 / 4.5 = 5.04 L/min.
  3. (b) Now TB − TI = 37 − 22 = 15 °C. For the same CO, AT = 60 × 0.010 × 15 × 1.08 / 5.04 = 1.93 °C·s.
  4. The area falls in the ratio 15/35 = 0.43, so the curve is less than half as tall while the noise from breathing and blood temperature drift stays the same. Iced injectate gives the better signal-to-noise ratio.

Answer. CO = 5.0 L/min; with 22 °C injectate the area would be only 1.93 °C·s, so iced injectate gives a cleaner reading.

Exam traps

  • Dropping the factor 60 in CO = 60 I / A. With I = 5 mg and A = 60 mg·s/L, dropping 60 gives 0.083 L/s (not L/min), which is the same as 5 L/min.
  • Including the recirculation hump in A. That makes A too large and CO too small, because CO is inversely proportional to the area.
  • Mixing time units: the area must be in mg·s/L before multiplying by 60. If the curve is integrated in mg·min/L, do not multiply by 60 again.
  • Using the wrong computation constant K for thermal dilution when the injectate volume or temperature is changed. The curve can be correct while CO is wrong.

2.3 Heart sounds: stethoscope and phonocardiography

Blood hitting a closing valve makes a short, low thump. A stethoscope lets a doctor hear this directly; a phonocardiograph records it as a waveform, the PCG, beside the ECG. The ECG says when the heart electrically fired; the PCG says when the valves actually shut. Clinicians use heart sounds at every bedside examination and in foetal monitoring. Engineers use them to test microphones, filters and timing circuits. This chapter explains where S1 and S2 come from, how the stethoscope couples sound to the ear, and how the PCG signal chain times sounds against the ECG.

You need: ECG recording, bio-amplifiers and filter basics (see ECG recording system and vector cardiography, and Bio-amplifiers and data acquisition systems).

What makes the sounds, and when

A heart sound is a short vibration from the heart and great vessels. S1 and S2 come from a valve closing; S3 and S4 come from blood entering the ventricle. The leaflet stops, the blood column behind it is checked, and a pressure wave runs through the chest wall. Valve opening is normally quiet. Systole is the phase when the ventricles contract; diastole is when they relax and fill.

S1 is the first sound. It comes from closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves at the start of ventricular systole. Its two parts are M1 and T1. S2 comes from closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves at the end of systole; its parts are A2 and P2. S3 is a low sound in early diastole, when blood rushes into the ventricle. S4 is a low sound in late diastole, when the atrium contracts. S3 is common in children and young adults and is often harmless. S4 can be normal in children and athletes, but in an older adult it usually means a stiff ventricle and suggests disease.

Most of the energy of S1 and S2 lies below about 200 Hz, and S3 and S4 sit at the very low end, below about 50 Hz. Murmurs come from turbulent flow and spread higher, to several hundred hertz, but they vary: aortic stenosis and regurgitation are relatively high-pitched, while mitral stenosis is a low-pitched rumble that needs the bell. That is why a stethoscope needs two chest pieces, and why a phonocardiograph offers more than one filter band.

Stethoscope: bell, diaphragm and tubing

A stethoscope has a chest piece, tubing and earpieces. It does not amplify sound in the electronic sense; it couples the small pressure changes at the chest wall to the ear canal with as little loss as possible. The ear then does the hearing.

The bell is an open cup. It senses low-frequency pressure changes and is used for S3, S4 and low murmurs such as mitral stenosis. Press it lightly. Pressing hard stretches the skin under it and turns the skin into a diaphragm, so low sounds fade. The diaphragm is a stretched membrane. It responds to higher-frequency movement and is used for S1, S2 and higher murmurs such as aortic regurgitation. Press it firmly.

Tubing matters. A long, narrow or thin-walled tube attenuates high frequencies and adds resonances. Keep the tube short, wide and thick-walled, and seal the earpieces in the ear. A noisy room raises the noise floor, so quiet low sounds are lost first. A stethoscope is a coupling device, not a gain device.

Phonocardiograph: recording the sound with time reference

A phonocardiograph records the heart sound as a waveform. The chain is: chest piece, microphone, preamplifier, band-pass filter, amplifier, recorder or display. An air-coupled microphone has a small air chamber closed by a membrane; chest vibration moves the membrane and changes the air pressure on a capacitive or electret element. A contact microphone, often piezoelectric, sits directly on the chest. The preamplifier needs high input impedance so it does not load the microphone.

The band-pass filter keeps the heart-sound band and rejects baseline wander below about 25 Hz and high-frequency noise above its upper cutoff. The worked example uses 25 Hz to 400 Hz. A narrow upper cutoff makes a clean trace but can hide a high-frequency murmur. The amplifier raises the signal to a level the recorder can handle.

A simultaneous ECG channel is the time reference. The R wave marks electrical activation. S1 and S2 follow the R wave by short, repeatable delays that are measured on each trace; the S2 delay in particular lengthens as the heart rate falls. On the trace, a systolic murmur lies between S1 and S2; a diastolic murmur lies between S2 and the next S1. A2 and P2 can separate on inspiration, giving a split S2. Heart rate is found from the RR interval: heart rate in beats per minute = 60 / RR, with RR in seconds. Foetal phonocardiography uses the same idea to separate the baby's sounds from the mother's; see Chapter 2.4.

Worked example

Question. A phonocardiogram is recorded with a 25-400 Hz filter together with lead II ECG. S1 begins 0.05 s after the R wave, S2 begins 0.35 s after the same R wave, and the next R wave comes 0.80 s later. (a) Find the systolic interval. (b) Find the heart rate and the diastolic interval. (c) Would a 600 Hz murmur appear on this trace?

  1. (a) The systolic interval is the time from S1 onset to S2 onset: 0.35 s − 0.05 s = 0.30 s.
  2. (b) The RR interval is 0.80 s. Heart rate = 60 / RR = 60 / 0.80 = 75 beats per minute.
  3. Diastolic interval = RR − systolic interval = 0.80 s − 0.30 s = 0.50 s.
  4. (c) The filter upper cutoff is 400 Hz. A 600 Hz component lies above this cutoff and is heavily attenuated, so it does not show on the trace.

Answer. The systolic interval is 0.30 s, the heart rate is 75 beats per minute, the diastolic interval is 0.50 s, and the 600 Hz murmur is largely removed by the 400 Hz upper cutoff.

Exam traps

  • Saying S2 is caused by valve opening. S1 and S2 both come from valve closure; S2 is the aortic and pulmonary valves shutting at the end of systole.
  • Pressing the bell hard and still expecting low sounds. Heavy pressure turns the skin into a diaphragm and removes S3 and S4.
  • Putting a systolic murmur after S2. Systole lies between S1 and S2; diastole lies between S2 and the next S1.
  • Using a PCG upper cutoff that is too low. A clean-looking trace can hide a high-frequency murmur above the cutoff.
  • Using RR in milliseconds in heart rate = 60/RR. Either convert RR to seconds, or use 60000/RR with RR in milliseconds.

2.4 Foetal monitoring and arrhythmia monitoring

Two bedside monitors watch a rhythm. A foetal monitor separates the baby's heartbeat from the mother's and draws it against contractions. An arrhythmia monitor finds each QRS complex, measures the RR interval, converts it to rate, and alarms when the interval leaves set limits. Both are used in labour rooms, coronary care units and general wards. This chapter shows how each monitor separates the wanted signal from the unwanted one, and how a single missed beat can make a rate report look dangerously slow.

You need: 2.3 for heart sounds and phonocardiography, and ECG recording and the heart rate monitor (see Heart rate monitor, Holter monitor and ECG troubleshooting).

Foetal heart rate from the abdomen

The foetal heart is small and sits inside a moving mother. Two abdominal methods find its rate. An ultrasound Doppler transducer, usually 1 to 2.5 MHz, sends a continuous wave into the abdomen. Echoes from the moving foetal heart walls and valves come back shifted in frequency by fd = 2 f0 v cos θ / c, where f0 is the transmitted frequency, v the speed of the moving wall, θ the angle between beam and motion and c ≈ 1540 m/s in tissue. The shift is in the audible range, so the monitor can play it as a sound. The demodulated Doppler envelope repeats at the foetal heart rate; a band-pass filter removes slow maternal movement and fast noise.

Abdominal ECG uses electrodes on the mother's abdomen. The record contains maternal QRS complexes and foetal QRS complexes. The maternal complex is larger and regular. Subtraction estimates a maternal QRS template and subtracts it. Logic cancellation blanks the maternal QRS windows and keeps foetal peaks outside them. The cleaned signal gives foetal QRS times.

FHR = 60 / Tf

FHR is the foetal heart rate in beats per minute; Tf is the time between two foetal beats in seconds. A rate of 140 beats per minute gives Tf = 60/140 = 0.429 s.

  1. Place the transducer or abdominal electrodes.
  2. Record the mixed signal for several beats.
  3. Mark maternal QRS complexes from the large regular peaks.
  4. Cancel them by subtraction or logic gating.
  5. Detect foetal peaks and measure Tf.
  6. Display rate and trace.

Scalp electrode, contractions and the CTG trace

A direct foetal scalp electrode is a small clip placed on the foetal scalp after the membranes have ruptured and the cervix is dilated. It records the foetal ECG directly. There is no maternal QRS, so the signal is clean, but the method is invasive.

An intrauterine pressure catheter lies in the uterus and measures pressure. The cardiotocograph (CTG) prints foetal heart rate and uterine pressure together. The baseline is the average foetal rate over about 10 minutes, normally 110 to 160 beats per minute. Beat-to-beat variability is the small change from one foetal beat to the next, normally about 5 to 25 beats per minute. An acceleration is a brief rise. A deceleration is a fall.

Foetal phonocardiography uses a microphone on the abdomen. A filter selects the foetal heart sound band and rejects maternal sounds. It is passive and simple, but noise from mother and labour ward limits it.

Arrhythmia monitor: QRS to alarm

An arrhythmia monitor watches one ECG lead. A QRS detector first removes baseline drift and T waves with a band-pass filter, often around 5 to 15 Hz. It then finds sharp peaks. For each beat it measures the RR interval, the time from one R wave to the next.

Rinst = 60 / RR

Rinst is the instantaneous heart rate in beats per minute; RR is the interval between two adjacent R waves in seconds.

A monitor stores limits. Typical adult settings are tachycardia above 100 to 120 beats per minute, bradycardia below 50 to 60 beats per minute, and a pause when RR exceeds 1.5 to 2 s. Asystole is no QRS for a longer set time. The comparator drives an audible alarm and a message.

Artefact rejection checks QRS shape, a refractory period and the previous RR; a very short RR after a normal beat may be noise. Ectopic beats are flagged by timing and shape: a premature ventricular contraction comes early (for example at less than 80 % of the running RR average), has a wide QRS of 0.12 s or more, and is followed by a compensatory pause. A missed beat is the dangerous error. If the true RR is 800 ms and one QRS is missed, the measured RR becomes 1600 ms and the reported rate is 60/1.60 = 37.5 beats per minute instead of 75.

2.5 Spirometry and pulmonary function measurement

A spirometer measures how much air moves in and out of the lungs and how fast. The volume-time curve and the flow-volume loop separate a blocked airway from a stiff lung. Clinics use spirometry to diagnose asthma and COPD, to check lung function before surgery, and to screen workers exposed to dust.

You need: school physics only; the idea of volume and flow is built here. Residual volume and airway resistance come in Chapter 2.8.

Primary volumes and capacities

A spirometer measures the volume of air a person moves in and out. The subject breathes through a mouthpiece with the nose clipped, and the instrument plots volume against time. The slope of that curve is flow.

Four primary volumes: tidal volume VT is one quiet breath, about 0.5 L; inspiratory reserve volume IRV is extra air after a quiet breath in, about 3.0 L; expiratory reserve volume ERV is extra air after a quiet breath out, about 1.1 L; residual volume RV is air left after the hardest breath out, about 1.2 L. RV never leaves the lungs.

Capacities: IC = VT + IRV; VC = IRV + VT + ERV; FRC = ERV + RV; TLC = VC + RV. A spirometer measures VT, IRV and ERV, so it gives IC and VC. It cannot measure RV or FRC, because that air stays inside.

Spirometer types and the BTPS correction

Four designs are common. In the water-sealed bell a light bell floats over water and a counterweight keeps the pressure inside close to atmospheric; the bell rises by Δh when the subject breathes out into it, so the volume is V = A × Δh, with A the inside cross-section of the bell, and a pen writes the movement on a rotating drum (kymograph). It is accurate but slow. The dry rolling seal uses a light piston on a rolling seal; it is easy to clean and common in clinics. In the turbine a vane spins at a speed proportional to flow, and counting its turns gives volume; hand-held units use it. The pneumotachograph measures the small pressure drop across a mesh, and flow is proportional to that drop; see 2.6.

A 3 L calibration syringe is pushed through before a session. The spirometer should read 3.000 L within its tolerance, often ±3%. This catches leaks and drift.

Lung gas is at 37 °C, at atmospheric pressure, and saturated with water vapour; this is BTPS. A spirometer in a cooler room measures the same gas at ATPS (ambient temperature and pressure, saturated), where it takes up less room. Volumes are corrected to BTPS before comparison with predicted values:

VBTPS = VATPS × [(PB − Pw) / (PB − 47)] × [310 / (273 + t)]

PB is barometric pressure in mmHg; Pw is the saturated water vapour pressure at the spirometer temperature t (22.4 mmHg at 24 °C, 23.8 mmHg at 25 °C); 47 mmHg is the vapour pressure at 37 °C; 310 K is body temperature. At room temperature the factor is about 1.07 to 1.10.

Forced vital capacity and the spirogram

The forced vital capacity (FVC) manoeuvre is the standard test. The subject sits, wears a nose clip, seals the lips, breathes in fully to TLC, then blows out as hard and fast as possible for at least 6 s until no more air comes out. The spirogram is the volume-time curve of that blow.

FEV1 is the volume exhaled in the first second. FVC is the total exhaled volume. The FEV1/FVC ratio is FEV1 ÷ FVC, dimensionless. A ratio below 0.70 is the usual fixed cut-off for obstruction; many laboratories now compare the ratio with the lower limit of normal for the patient's age, sex and height instead, because the ratio falls with age.

An obstructive pattern (asthma, COPD) has low FEV1 and a ratio below 0.70, because narrowed airways slow the air out; FVC may be normal or low. A restrictive pattern (pulmonary fibrosis) has low FVC and low FEV1 but a normal or high ratio, because the stiff lung is small yet the airways are open. Spirometry can only suggest restriction; a low total lung capacity confirms it, and that needs the body box or a gas method.

FEF25-75 is the average flow over the middle half of FVC, from 25% to 75% of volume; it falls early in small-airway disease but is effort-dependent. Peak expiratory flow rate PEFR is the highest flow, often in L/min. The flow-volume loop plots flow against volume; obstruction gives a scooped expiratory limb and low peak. Helium dilution and nitrogen washout measure RV and FRC; 2.8 explains them with the body box.

Worked example

Question. A healthy man gives FVC = 4.0 L and FEV1 = 3.2 L. A patient with asthma gives FVC = 3.0 L and FEV1 = 1.2 L. (a) Compute FEV1/FVC for each, as a decimal and a percentage. (b) Classify the patient using the 0.70 cutoff. (c) The patient's peak flow is 400 L/min; convert it to L/s.

  4. Peak flow = 400 L/min ÷ 60 s/min = 6.666... L/s ≈ 6.67 L/s.

Exam traps

  • Using FEV1 alone: a low FEV1 occurs in both obstruction and restriction. The FEV1/FVC ratio separates the two patterns, and a true restriction still needs a low TLC to confirm it.
  • Expecting a spirometer to measure residual volume or FRC: that air never leaves the lungs, so only helium dilution, nitrogen washout or a plethysmograph can find it (Chapter 2.8).
  • Forgetting BTPS: comparing an ATPS volume with a BTPS predicted value underreads the lung volume by about 8%.
  • Unit slip: PEFR is often in L/min while FEV1 and FVC are in litres; divide by 60 to get L/s.
  • Calling any low FVC restrictive: obstruction can also reduce FVC; check the ratio and the flow-volume shape.

2.6 Pneumotachometer; intra-alveolar and thoracic pressures

Air moves because a pressure difference pushes it. A pneumotachometer measures that push as a small drop across a known resistance, so the drop becomes flow, and flow integrated over time becomes volume. The pressures that drive the lung, inside the air sacs and in the space around the lung, cannot be reached with a probe, so they are found indirectly: from mouth pressure when the airway is briefly shut, and from a balloon in the oesophagus. These measurements appear in pulmonary function labs, ventilators and exercise testing, where a doctor needs to separate a blocked airway from a stiff lung.

You need: 2.5 for lung volumes, and the differential pressure transducer and carrier amplifier (see Physiological transducers and load cells).

Flow from a known resistance

The pneumotachometer is a deliberate resistance in the breathing path. Air crosses a fine mesh or a bundle of small tubes, and pressure taps before and after it feed a differential pressure transducer. For smooth, laminar flow the drop follows Poiseuille's law: it is proportional to flow. One calibration number, the resistance of the head, then links pressure drop and flow.

ΔP = Rpt × Q

ΔP is the pressure drop in cm H₂O; Rpt is the resistance of the pneumotachograph head in cm H₂O per L/s, its calibration factor; Q is the flow in L/s.

A Fleisch pneumotachograph uses many parallel capillaries: the parallel paths keep the total resistance low and the flow in each tube laminar. A screen pneumotachograph uses a woven wire mesh; it is cheaper but clogs with moisture more easily, so the head is often heated. A hot-wire flowmeter heats a thin wire and measures how much the flow cools it; it has no mesh and no simple pressure-drop law.

  1. Set zero with no flow through the head.
  2. Apply known steady flows across the working range. If 0.5 L/s gives 0.75 cm H₂O, Rpt = 0.75/0.5 = 1.5 cm H₂O per L/s; equal ratios at 0.5, 1 and 2 L/s show the head is linear.
  3. Keep flow inside the laminar range; turbulence makes the drop rise faster than flow.
  4. Integrate the flow signal to get volume, and check the volume with a 3 L syringe pushed at slow and fast speeds.

Pressures inside and around the alveoli

Intra-alveolar pressure (Palv) is the pressure inside the air sacs. No sensor can sit in an air sac, so it is found indirectly. In the interrupter method a fast shutter closes the mouthpiece for about 0.1 s; flow stops, pressure equalises along the airway, and the mouth pressure just after closure equals the alveolar pressure just before it. The body plethysmograph gives it from the box pressure (see 2.8).

Intrapleural pressure (Ppl) is the pressure in the thin space between lung and chest wall. It is estimated with a thin balloon catheter in the lower oesophagus, because oesophageal pressure follows pleural pressure. Three differences are used: transpulmonary pressure, across the lung, Palv − Ppl; chest-wall (transthoracic) pressure, Ppl − Pbs, where Pbs is body-surface (atmospheric) pressure; and transrespiratory pressure, Palv − Pbs, across the whole system. Pressures are in cm H₂O; 1 cm H₂O = 0.098 kPa. At rest, intrapleural pressure is about −5 cm H₂O and alveolar pressure swings about ±1 cm H₂O.

Ptp = Palv − Ppl

Ptp is transpulmonary pressure, Palv intra-alveolar pressure and Ppl intrapleural pressure, all in cm H₂O and all measured from the same reference.

Airway resistance (Raw) links flow and the pressure that drives it. With expiratory flow taken as positive, Palv = Pmouth + Raw × Q; during inspiration alveolar pressure falls below mouth pressure. So Raw = (Palv − Pmouth) / Q, with Q read by the pneumotachometer just before the interruption. A narrowed airway raises Raw and needs a larger pressure difference for the same flow.

Calibration, integration and drift

The differential transducer measures only the small drop across the head, often under 2 cm H₂O, so it needs good sensitivity and a stable zero. A carrier amplifier excites the transducer and demodulates its output into a voltage proportional to ΔP. The flow signal is then integrated to give volume.

V = ∫ Q dt

V is volume in L; Q is flow in L/s; t is time in s.

Integration accumulates any offset: a zero error of 0.01 L/s for 60 s adds 0.6 L of false volume. Modern instruments reset the integrator at the start of each breath or subtract a measured zero. The same flow signal drives the volume display and the flow-volume loop. A clogged or wet mesh raises Rpt, so the head is recalibrated with known flows, and the volume with a syringe, before each session.

Exam traps

  • Intra-alveolar is not intrapleural: alveolar pressure is inside the air sac and swings around zero; intrapleural pressure is around the lung and stays below atmospheric in quiet breathing.

2.7 Oximetry, impedance pneumography and apnea monitors

Oximetry reads oxygen saturation from the colour of pulsating blood. Two light-emitting diodes, red at about 660 nm and infrared at about 940 nm, shine through a finger. A photodiode measures what passes. The pulsatile part is separated from the steady tissue part, and their ratio gives SpO₂. Impedance pneumography watches breathing from the small rise in chest impedance as air enters the lungs. An apnoea monitor alarms when that change stops for 10-20 s. These three measurements sit together at the bedside.

You need: breathing volumes and flow from 2.5 and 2.6, and bridge circuits and bio-amplifiers (see Bio-amplifiers and data acquisition systems).

Pulse oximetry: two wavelengths and one ratio

Pulse oximetry rests on the Beer–Lambert law: absorbance is proportional to concentration and path length; the light that gets through falls exponentially with both.

A = ε c d

A is absorbance (no unit); ε is the molar absorption coefficient at that wavelength, in litre per mole per centimetre (L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹); c is concentration (moles per litre); d is path length (centimetres). The units cancel, so A has no unit.

In a finger, light crosses skin, bone, venous blood and arterial blood. The steady part is DC. Each heartbeat pushes arterial blood into the finger, so the light path through arterial blood lengthens and shortens; this small changing part is AC. The AC is the pulsatile arterial signal.

Oxygenated and reduced haemoglobin absorb differently. At red 660 nm, reduced haemoglobin absorbs more. At infrared 940 nm, oxygenated haemoglobin absorbs more. Two wavelengths therefore separate oxygen saturation from the total amount of blood and tissue in the path. A red LED and an infrared LED are switched on alternately, often several hundred times per second. One photodiode on the far side measures the transmitted light for each wavelength.

  1. Switch the red and infrared LEDs alternately.
  2. Convert the photodiode current to a voltage and separate the two wavelength channels.
  3. Split each channel into DC by low-pass filtering and AC by band-pass filtering at the heart rate.
  4. Compute the ratio R from the two channels.
  5. Read SpO₂ from an empirical calibration table or curve.
R = (ACred / DCred) ÷ (ACIR / DCIR)

ACred and ACIR are the pulsatile amplitudes in the red and infrared channels (same light units); DCred and DCIR are the steady levels (same units); R is dimensionless.

R rises as saturation falls. Each maker converts R to SpO₂ with its own empirical curve, built by measuring R in volunteers breathing low-oxygen mixtures while arterial samples are analysed. Textbooks often use the straight-line approximation SpO₂ ≈ 110 − 25R (in %), which gives 100 % at R = 0.4 and 85 % at R = 1.0; use it unless the question gives a table.

What pulse oximetry cannot tell you

SpO₂ is a percentage: oxygenated haemoglobin as a share of oxygenated plus reduced haemoglobin in arterial blood. Carboxyhaemoglobin and methaemoglobin are left out of this share, but they still absorb red and infrared light, so they push the reading the wrong way: carbon monoxide makes SpO₂ read falsely high, and methaemoglobin drives it towards about 85 %. SpO₂ is not a measure of breathing either. A patient on oxygen can have a normal SpO₂ while breathing badly and retaining carbon dioxide; carbon dioxide is seen by capnography or a blood gas, and apnoea by chest movement or airflow.

  • Motion: probe movement changes the light path; the AC looks like a pulse and R becomes wrong.
  • Low perfusion: a cold finger, shock or low blood pressure makes the arterial AC small; noise dominates.
  • Nail polish and ambient light: dark polish absorbs red and infrared; strong room light adds a steady offset.
  • Carboxyhaemoglobin: carbon monoxide poisoning makes haemoglobin absorb red light like oxygenated haemoglobin, so SpO₂ reads falsely high.
  • Venous pulsation and severe anaemia can also mislead; the displayed value is SpO₂, not arterial SaO₂ from a blood sample.

Impedance pneumography and apnoea monitors

Impedance pneumography uses the chest as a conductor. Air is a poor conductor, so when the lungs fill, transthoracic impedance rises. A small alternating current, tens of kilohertz (50 kHz is typical) and well under a milliampere, is passed through the chest. With two electrodes the same pair carries the current and senses the voltage, as ECG monitors do with their own electrodes; with four, one pair carries the current and a separate pair senses the voltage, which removes the electrode-skin impedance from the reading. The breathing change is small, of the order of 1 Ω per litre on a base impedance of a few hundred ohms, and it depends on electrode position and build, so the method gives timing and trends, not litres.

A bridge circuit converts the small impedance change into a voltage. A band-pass amplifier keeps the breathing frequency and rejects drift and the ECG. The output is a respiration waveform: up on inspiration, down on expiration. Chest movement and the cardiac stroke add artefacts; the cardiac stroke is a small bump at the heart rate on top of the breathing wave.

S = ΔZ / ΔV

S is sensitivity (ohms per litre); ΔZ is the impedance change (ohms); ΔV is the volume change (litres). A 0.5 Ω change with a 0.5 L breath gives S = 1 Ω per litre.

An apnoea monitor watches this waveform. It sounds an alarm when no impedance change larger than a set amount occurs for a set time, usually 10 to 20 s (often 20 s for newborns), and many also alarm on a falling heart rate. It is used for neonates in hospital and at home. A threshold that is too low alarms on cardiac artefact; too high misses shallow breathing. Other apnoea sensors are a thermistor at the nostril, warmed by each breath out, a pressure-sensitive pad under the baby, and capnography.

Worked example

Question. A finger probe gives red channel DC = 100 units and AC = 2 units, infrared channel DC = 110 units and AC = 4 units. (a) Compute R. (b) Estimate SpO₂ with SpO₂ ≈ 110 − 25R, and say which way R moves if saturation falls. (c) An apnoea monitor shows a 0.5 Ω impedance change with a 500 mL breath; find its sensitivity.

  1. Red channel ratio: AC/DC = 2/100 = 0.020 (dimensionless).
  2. Infrared channel ratio: AC/DC = 4/110 = 0.03636 (dimensionless).
  3. R = 0.020 / 0.03636 = 0.55 (dimensionless).
  4. SpO₂ ≈ 110 − 25 × 0.55 = 110 − 13.75 = 96.25 ≈ 96 %. Less oxygenated blood absorbs more red light, so the red AC/DC term grows and the infrared term falls; R rises and the reading falls.
  5. S = ΔZ / ΔV = 0.5 Ω / 0.5 L = 1 Ω per litre.

Answer. R = 0.55, so SpO₂ is about 96 %; a fall in saturation raises R; the monitor sensitivity is 1 Ω per litre.

Exam traps

  • A normal SpO₂ does not prove good breathing. A patient on oxygen can hypoventilate and retain carbon dioxide while the saturation looks fine.
  • R rises as saturation falls. Do not invert the ratio or read the calibration table backwards.
  • AC is the pulsatile arterial part; DC is the steady tissue, bone and venous part. Swapping them destroys the measurement.
  • The figure 1 Ω per litre is a typical adult value, not a fixed constant. Electrode position, chest shape and lung volume change it.
  • Carboxyhaemoglobin makes SpO₂ falsely high; dark nail polish and methaemoglobin make it unreliable. Motion and low perfusion make the ratio noisy, not just the waveform ugly.

2.8 Whole-body plethysmograph, residual volume and airway resistance

A whole-body plethysmograph is a rigid sealed box, about 600 L, in which the subject sits and breathes through a mouthpiece with a shutter. When the subject pants against the closed shutter, the trapped lung gas is squeezed and stretched; Boyle's law turns the tiny box-pressure swing and the larger mouth-pressure swing into the volume of gas in the chest. That gives functional residual capacity and residual volume, which a spirometer cannot measure. With the shutter open, the same box gives alveolar pressure and so airway resistance.

You need: Chapter 2.5 for lung-volume names and Chapter 2.6 for flow, pressure and airway resistance.

The sealed box and two pressure signals

The box is rigid and sealed, so the gas inside has a fixed mass. Two transducers record the signals: one measures mouth pressure, Pmouth, at the mouthpiece; the other measures box pressure, Pbox. A pneumotachometer in the mouthpiece measures flow. A small piston pump on the box wall is pushed in and out by a known volume to calibrate the box: it gives Kbox, the box-volume change per unit of box pressure, for example 400 mL per cm H₂O.

With the shutter closed the subject pants gently at about 1 Hz. Breathing-muscle effort compresses the lung gas a little on each push and expands it on each pull; the chest grows and shrinks by the same tiny volume ΔV, and the box gas around the subject changes volume by the same ΔV the other way, so Pbox swings. Because the airway is shut there is no flow, so the mouth pressure equals the alveolar pressure. Panting is used because it is fast: the lung gas stays at body temperature and saturated (isothermal), and the box gas has no time to exchange heat with the walls, which the piston calibration already allows for.

Thoracic gas volume, FRC and residual volume

Apply Boyle's law to the trapped lung gas. Only the dry gas obeys it, because the water vapour pressure stays at 47 mmHg at 37 °C however hard the gas is squeezed. If the lung volume V changes by ΔV while alveolar pressure changes by ΔPmouth, P V = (P + ΔP)(V − ΔV), which for small changes gives:

Vtg = ΔV × (PB − PH₂O) / ΔPmouth,   ΔV = Kbox × ΔPbox

Vtg is the thoracic gas volume; ΔV is the lung volume change read from the calibrated box; PB is barometric pressure and PH₂O = 47 mmHg, both converted to cm H₂O (760 mmHg = 1033 cm H₂O, 47 mmHg = 64 cm H₂O, so PB − PH₂O ≈ 970 cm H₂O at sea level); ΔPmouth and ΔPbox are the peak-to-peak swings in cm H₂O.

If the shutter closes at the end of a quiet breath out, Vtg is the functional residual capacity, FRC. A spirometer in the same sitting gives the expiratory reserve volume ERV and the vital capacity VC, and the rest follows: RV = FRC − ERV and TLC = RV + VC. The ratio RV/TLC, normally about 0.25 to 0.35 in young adults, rises with air trapping in COPD and asthma.

The gas methods measure only gas that talks to the airway. In helium dilution the subject rebreathes from a closed spirometer of volume V₁ holding helium at fraction C₁; helium is not absorbed, so after mixing C₁V₁ = C₂(V₁ + FRC) and FRC = V₁(C₁ − C₂)/C₂, with carbon dioxide absorbed and oxygen added as it is used. In nitrogen washout the subject breathes pure oxygen and all exhaled gas is collected until the nitrogen is gone; FRC = (volume collected × its nitrogen fraction) ÷ the starting alveolar nitrogen fraction, about 0.80. Gas trapped behind closed airways is missed by both but is counted by the box, so in COPD the box FRC is the larger.

Airway resistance with the shutter open

With the shutter open the subject keeps panting, now through the pneumotachometer. To drive air out, alveolar pressure must rise above mouth pressure; that compresses the alveolar gas slightly, and the box sees it as a pressure swing. The closed-shutter run has already shown how many cm H₂O of alveolar pressure go with each unit of box pressure, so the box swing in the open run can be turned into alveolar pressure without any probe in the lung:

Raw = (ΔPmouth / ΔPbox)shutter closed × (ΔPbox / V̇)shutter open

Raw is airway resistance in cm H₂O per L/s; the first ratio is the slope of the closed-shutter trace; the second is the box swing per unit flow V̇ (in L/s) in the open-shutter trace. The box units cancel.

Normal adult airway resistance is roughly 0.6 to 2.4 cm H₂O per L/s; higher values suggest obstruction. Conductance is Gaw = 1/Raw. Because airways widen as the lung fills, resistance falls at larger lung volumes, so reports also give specific values that remove that effect: sRaw = Raw × Vtg and sGaw = Gaw / Vtg. Write resistance as cm H₂O per L/s, not cm H₂O/L.

Worked example

Question. A subject sits in a body box whose piston calibration gives Kbox = 400 mL per cm H₂O. Barometric pressure is 760 mmHg. With the shutter closed at the end of a quiet breath out, the subject pants: mouth pressure swings by 9.7 cm H₂O and box pressure by 0.075 cm H₂O. Spirometry gives ERV = 1.2 L and VC = 4.0 L. With the shutter open, a flow swing of 1.0 L/s goes with a box swing of 0.0116 cm H₂O. Find (a) the thoracic gas volume, (b) RV and TLC, (c) airway resistance and specific airway conductance.

  1. (a) Lung volume change: ΔV = Kbox × ΔPbox = 400 × 0.075 = 30 mL.
  2. Dry-gas pressure: PB − PH₂O = (760 − 47) mmHg = 713 mmHg × 1.36 = 970 cm H₂O.
  3. Vtg = ΔV × 970 / ΔPmouth = 30 mL × 970 / 9.7 = 3000 mL = 3.0 L. The shutter closed at end-expiration, so this is FRC.
  4. (b) RV = FRC − ERV = 3.0 − 1.2 = 1.8 L; TLC = RV + VC = 1.8 + 4.0 = 5.8 L; RV/TLC = 1.8/5.8 = 0.31, normal.
  5. (c) Closed-shutter slope: ΔPmouth/ΔPbox = 9.7/0.075 = 129.3. Open-shutter: ΔPbox/V̇ = 0.0116 per L/s.
  6. Raw = 129.3 × 0.0116 = 1.50 cm H₂O per L/s, inside the normal range of about 0.6 to 2.4.
  7. Gaw = 1/1.50 = 0.667 L/s per cm H₂O; sGaw = 0.667/3.0 = 0.22 per s per cm H₂O.

Answer. Vtg = FRC = 3.0 L; RV = 1.8 L, TLC = 5.8 L; Raw = 1.50 cm H₂O per L/s (normal) and sGaw = 0.22 per s per cm H₂O.

Worked example 2

Question. Helium dilution in the same subject: a closed spirometer holds 5.0 L of gas with 10.0 % helium. After a few minutes of rebreathing from FRC the helium reads 6.25 %. Find FRC and compare it with the box value.

  1. Helium is conserved: C₁V₁ = C₂(V₁ + FRC), so FRC = V₁(C₁ − C₂)/C₂.
  2. FRC = 5.0 × (10.0 − 6.25)/6.25 = 5.0 × 3.75/6.25 = 3.0 L.
  3. The helium value equals the box value, 3.0 L, so no gas is trapped behind closed airways. In COPD the box would read higher.

Answer. FRC = 3.0 L by helium dilution, the same as the plethysmograph, so there is no trapped gas.

Exam traps

  • Using the box swing as if it were a volume: ΔPbox becomes ΔV only through the piston calibration Kbox.
  • Forgetting water vapour: Boyle's law uses PB − 47 mmHg, about 970 cm H₂O at sea level, not 1033 cm H₂O.
  • Mixing FRC and residual volume: Vtg at an end-expiration shutter closure is FRC; RV = FRC − ERV.
  • Expecting helium dilution to agree in COPD: gas trapped behind closed airways never meets the helium, so the gas method reads lower than the box.
  • Writing airway resistance in cm H₂O/L instead of cm H₂O per L/s.

Takeaways

  1. Blood pressure is a pressure above atmosphere, so a catheter transducer is zeroed to air at heart level; the cuff method reads it indirectly from Korotkoff sounds (phase I systolic, phase V diastolic) or from the peak of cuff oscillations (MAP).
  2. Dilution gives cardiac output as indicator dose ÷ area under the first-pass curve: CO = 60 I / A for dye, and the same idea with heat for thermodilution; recirculation must be cut off by extrapolating the exponential decay.
  3. Heart sounds are low-frequency events from valve closure (S1, S2) and ventricular filling (S3, S4); a phonocardiogram is timed against the ECG R wave to find systole and diastole.
  4. A foetal monitor must cancel the mother's larger ECG or use Doppler before it can count the baby's rate; an arrhythmia monitor turns each RR interval into a rate, and one missed QRS halves the rate it reports.
  5. Spirometry gives volumes and flows (FVC, FEV1, their ratio) corrected from ATPS to BTPS; it cannot measure residual volume or FRC.
  6. A pneumotachometer turns a small laminar pressure drop into flow and integrates it to volume; alveolar and pleural pressures are found indirectly, by brief interruption and by an oesophageal balloon.
  7. A pulse oximeter forms the ratio of ratios R from red and infrared AC/DC signals; it reports saturation, not ventilation, and is fooled by carbon monoxide, motion and poor perfusion.
  8. Impedance pneumography and apnoea monitors watch the timing of breathing, not its volume, and alarm after a set silent time of 10 to 20 s.
  9. The body box applies Boyle's law to the gas trapped in the chest during panting to give FRC, RV and airway resistance; helium dilution and nitrogen washout give FRC from gas balance but miss trapped gas.

Understand Define systolic, diastolic and mean arterial pressure. Describe the auscultatory method of indirect blood pressure measurement and explain what produces each phase of the Korotkoff sounds. 13 marks

Points to cover. Pressure is measured relative to atmosphere, in mmHg; Cuff placed on upper arm, inflated above systolic to stop flow; Cuff deflated slowly at 2-3 mmHg per second; Turbulent flow under the cuff produces phase 1 tapping sound = systolic; Sound muffling (phase 4) and disappearance (phase 5) = diastolic; Cuff must be at heart level to avoid hydrostatic error; MAP = diastolic + (systolic - diastolic)/3; Sources of error: cuff size, observer hearing, deflation rate.

Model answer.

  1. Definitions. Blood pressure is the pressure in an artery above atmospheric, in mmHg. Systolic pressure S is the peak in each beat, just after the ventricles eject; diastolic pressure D is the lowest value, just before the next beat. Mean arterial pressure is the time-average that perfuses the organs: MAP ≈ D + (S − D)/3 at normal heart rates, because diastole lasts about twice as long as systole.
  2. Equipment. A cloth cuff holds an inflatable bladder about 40 % of the arm circumference wide and 80 % long; a bulb and valve inflate and deflate it; a mercury or aneroid manometer shows cuff pressure; a stethoscope (or a microphone in automated units) sits over the brachial artery below the cuff. The cuff is at heart level, with the arm supported.
  3. Procedure. (1) Inflate about 30 mmHg above the pressure at which the radial pulse disappears, so the artery is fully shut and silent. (2) Deflate at 2–3 mmHg per second. (3) Note the cuff pressure at the first clear tapping sound (systolic) and where the sounds vanish (diastolic). (4) Deflate fully and wait before repeating.
  4. Korotkoff phases and their cause. When cuff pressure falls just below systolic, the artery opens for a moment at the peak of each beat; blood squirts through the narrowed segment as a turbulent jet and the wall snaps open, which is heard as a tap. As the cuff falls further the artery stays open for longer in each beat and the sound changes:

Remember Explain the origin of the four heart sounds and describe a phonocardiograph recording system, including the choice between the bell and diaphragm of a stethoscope. 12 marks

Points to cover. S1 from mitral and tricuspid closure at start of systole; S2 from aortic and pulmonary closure at end of systole; S3 early diastolic filling sound, S4 late diastolic atrial sound; Microphone, low-noise amplifier, band-pass filter of about 20–500 Hz with selectable bands; Simultaneous ECG for timing; Bell picks up low frequencies, diaphragm filters them out for higher murmurs; Systole is the interval between S1 and S2, diastole between S2 and the next S1.

Model answer.

  1. Origin of the four sounds. S1 is closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves at the start of ventricular systole (components M1, T1); it is the longer, lower sound. S2 is closure of the aortic and pulmonary valves at the end of systole (A2, P2); it is shorter and sharper, and splits on inspiration because P2 is delayed. S3 is a low sound in early diastole as blood rushes into the ventricle; it is normal in children and young adults. S4 is a low sound in late diastole when the atrium contracts into a stiff ventricle; it usually indicates disease in an adult.
  2. Frequency content. S1 and S2 have most energy below about 200 Hz; S3 and S4 are below about 50 Hz; murmurs, from turbulence, reach several hundred hertz. A recording system therefore needs a band of about 20–500 Hz with selectable sub-bands.
  3. Stethoscope: bell and diaphragm. The stethoscope couples chest-wall vibration to the ear; it does not amplify. The bell is an open cup, held lightly, that passes low frequencies: use it for S3, S4 and the rumble of mitral stenosis. Pressing it hard stretches the skin into a membrane and the low sounds fade. The diaphragm is a stiff membrane, pressed firmly, that suppresses low frequencies and brings out S1, S2 and high-pitched murmurs such as aortic regurgitation. Short, wide, thick-walled tubing keeps losses small.

Understand Describe the dye dilution and thermal dilution methods of cardiac output measurement. Give the working equation and list the assumptions. 13 marks

Points to cover. Indicator injected upstream, concentration sensed downstream; Stewart-Hamilton: CO = amount injected / (mean concentration x time base); Dye such as indocyanine green, measured by densitometry at the ear or artery; Thermal dilution uses cold saline and a thermistor on a pulmonary artery catheter; Recirculation removed by extrapolating the exponential decay on semilog paper; Indicator must mix completely and be lost only by flow; Thermal method needs correction for heat loss to catheter wall; Sources of error: injection loss, recirculation, unstable flow.

Model answer.

  1. Principle. A known amount of indicator is injected upstream and mixes with all the blood passing; downstream its concentration c(t) is recorded. Mass balance: everything injected passes the sensor once, so I = Q ∫c dt. Hence Q = I / ∫c dt, and in L/min, CO = 60 I / A, where A is the area under the first-pass curve in mg·s/L. Older books write this as CO = I / (c̄ × t), with c̄ the mean concentration over the curve duration t.
  2. Dye dilution. Indocyanine green (about 5 mg) is injected quickly into a central vein. Arterial blood is drawn through a densitometer cuvette that reads optical absorption near 800 nm. The curve appears after a few seconds, peaks, falls almost exponentially, and then a second hump appears as dye recirculates. The downslope is plotted on semilog paper and extended as a straight line; the area under the measured part plus the extrapolated tail is A.
  3. Worked numbers (dye). Samples every 2 s from 4 to 16 s: 0, 3.0, 7.5, 6.0, 4.2, 3.0, 2.1 mg/L.
    A₁ (trapezoids)  = 2 × [(0 + 2.1)/2 + 3.0 + 7.5 + 6.0 + 4.2 + 3.0] = 49.5 mg·s/L
τ                = 2 / ln(3.0/2.1) = 5.61 s
A₂ (tail)        = 2.1 × 5.61 = 11.8 mg·s/L
CO               = 60 × 5 / (49.5 + 11.8) = 4.90 L/min
  4. Thermal dilution. A Swan-Ganz catheter floats through the right heart into the pulmonary artery. A known volume of cold 5 % dextrose or saline (10 mL at 0–4 °C) is injected through the proximal port in the right atrium; a thermistor near the tip records the fall in blood temperature. The equation replaces dye mass with heat: CO = 60 VI(TB − TI)K1 / ∫ΔT dt, with K1 ≈ 1.08 for dextrose. For 10 mL at 2 °C, blood at 37 °C and an area of 4.5 °C·s: CO = 60 × 0.010 × 35 × 1.08 / 4.5 = 5.04 L/min.
  5. Assumptions. (1) The indicator mixes completely with all the flow before the sensor. Points to cover. Model answer.

  1. Flow. For a linear pneumotachometer ΔP = R × Q, so Q = ΔP / R = 1.5 / 0.5 = 3 L/s.
  2. Construction. A screen pneumotachometer holds a fine wire mesh across the breathing tube; a Fleisch head uses a bundle of parallel capillaries. Pressure taps on each side of the element feed a differential pressure transducer, whose bridge output is amplified by a carrier amplifier.
  3. Why it is linear. Poiseuille's law says the pressure drop across a tube is proportional to flow while flow is laminar.     V = ∫ Q dt
example: Q = 3 L/s held for 0.5 s → V = 1.5 L

At 3 L/s the element adds 1.5 cm H₂O of resistance, small beside the normal airway resistance of about 1–2 cm H₂O per L/s times the same flow, so the subject barely notices it.   10. Safety. Isolated (type CF) applied parts limit patient leakage current to about 10 µA, and defibrillation-proof inputs with series resistors and voltage clamps protect the amplifiers.
  11. Evaluation. Alarm fatigue is the main real-world failure: limits based on the patient's baseline, short alarm delays and combined alarms (low SpO₂ with apnoea) keep the alarms meaningful. The design meets the brief: continuous ECG, SpO₂ and breathing, with intermittent or continuous pressure as the patient needs.
Evaluate Compare the pneumotachometer, the spirometer and the impedance method for measuring ventilation. Discuss accuracy, patient effort and suitability for long-term apnea monitoring. 13 marks

Points to cover. Pneumotachometer gives true flow from a pressure drop, needs a mouthpiece and is accurate but interferes with breathing; Spirometer integrates flow or volume directly, used for forced manoeuvres in the clinic; Impedance method needs only surface electrodes and gives a relative change, not absolute volume; Impedance is best for apnea alarm, not for lung function numbers; Movement and cardiac pulsation corrupt the impedance signal; Choice depends on whether absolute volume or a trend is needed; A 3 L syringe check is standard for spirometer and pneumotachograph chains.

Model answer.

  1. What each measures. The spirometer measures volume (bell, rolling seal) or integrates flow (turbine) at the mouth; the pneumotachometer measures flow as a pressure drop across a laminar resistance and integrates it to volume; impedance pneumography measures the change in transthoracic impedance as the lungs fill.
  2. Comparison.

  3. Accuracy in numbers. A spirometer reads 3.0 ± 0.09 L on the calibration syringe. A pneumotachometer with Rpt = 1.5 cm H₂O per L/s reads 0.5 L/s from 0.75 cm H₂O, but a 0.01 L/s zero offset integrates to 0.6 L per minute, so it needs per-breath reset. An impedance signal of 0.5 Ω for a 0.5 L breath can drift by tens of percent when the patient turns over.
  4. Patient effort. Spirometry needs a cooperative subject who can perform a maximal blow; it is impossible in an infant or an unconscious patient. A pneumotachometer needs only a sealed mouthpiece or mask, so it suits ventilated patients but not a sleeping baby at home. Impedance needs nothing beyond electrodes, often the ECG electrodes already in place.
  5. Suitability for long-term apnoea monitoring. Impedance is the practical choice: unobtrusive, cheap, and the alarm needs only the timing of breaths (no change above threshold for 10–20 s). Its weaknesses are obstructive apnoea, where the chest keeps moving against a closed airway and the impedance still changes, and cardiac artefact, which can look like shallow breathing. Adding SpO₂ and heart rate, or a nasal thermistor, covers these gaps.
  6. Calibration in common. The 3 L syringe checks spirometer and pneumotachometer volume, emptied at several speeds to check the pneumotachometer's linearity; the impedance channel is checked by its own test resistance and is never used for litres.
  7. Ventilators. In intensive care the pneumotachometer sits in the ventilator circuit at the patient Y-piece, measuring both directions; heated heads prevent condensation, and the integrated volume gives tidal volume and minute ventilation alarms. This is where the pneumotachometer, not the spirometer, does long-term monitoring.
  8. Conclusion. Use the spirometer for diagnostic lung volumes, the pneumotachometer for flow in the laboratory and on ventilators, and impedance for continuous rate and apnoea watching.

Glossary

Apnea
A pause in breathing, commonly alarming when it lasts longer than about 10 to 20 seconds.
Arrhythmia
Any abnormal rhythm, detected by comparing each RR interval with set limits.
ATPS
Ambient temperature and pressure, saturated with water vapour; conditions at the spirometer itself.
Auscultation
Listening to body sounds, here the Korotkoff sounds over an artery below a cuff.
Bell
The cup-shaped chest piece of a stethoscope, best for low-frequency sounds such as S3 and S4.
Body plethysmograph
A sealed box in which pressure changes around a breathing subject give lung volume and airway resistance.
Boyle's law
At constant temperature, pressure times volume of a fixed amount of gas stays constant.
Cardiac output
Volume of blood pumped by the heart per minute, about 5 L/min at rest in an adult.
Diaphragm
The flat chest piece of a stethoscope, which filters low frequencies and highlights murmurs.
Diastole
The part of the cardiac cycle when the ventricles relax and fill.
Dry rolling seal
A spirometer design in which a piston rolls on a flexible seal with low friction, giving an accurate volume signal.
Expiratory reserve volume
Volume that can still be breathed out after a normal quiet expiration.
Fick principle
Blood flow equals the amount of a substance taken up per minute divided by the arteriovenous concentration difference.
Foetal heart rate
The baby's heart rate, normally about 110 to 160 beats per minute.
Helium dilution
Method that finds lung volume from the dilution of a known amount of helium in a closed circuit.
Indocyanine green
A dye used for dilution cardiac output, measured optically because it absorbs near 800 nm.
Intra-alveolar pressure
Pressure inside the air sacs, equal to atmospheric when no air is moving.
Oscillometry
Automated cuff method that reads pressure from the size of the pressure pulsations in the cuff.
Peak expiratory flow rate
The highest flow, in litres per minute, reached during a forced expiration.
Photoplethysmography
Optical recording of the pulsatile blood volume in a finger or earlobe.
RR interval
Time between two successive R waves of the ECG, in milliseconds; inverse gives heart rate.
Spirometer
Instrument that records the volume of air breathed in and out and the speed of the breath.
Strain-gauge transducer
A diaphragm whose resistance changes when pressure bends it, used in direct blood pressure lines.
Systole
The part of the cardiac cycle when the ventricles contract and eject blood.
