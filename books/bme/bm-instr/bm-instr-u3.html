3.1 Spectrophotometry and colorimetry

A coloured or ultraviolet-absorbing solution takes light out of a beam in proportion to how much substance it contains. Shine a known light intensity through it, measure what survives, and the Beer–Lambert law turns that measurement into concentration. Hospital laboratories use this daily for haemoglobin, bilirubin, glucose, creatinine and total protein. The instrument is a spectrophotometer; a colorimeter is its simpler cousin with a filter instead of a monochromator. This chapter explains the light measurement, the law, the instrument chain and the practical limits.

You need: concentration in mol/L, the base-10 logarithm, and the idea that a solution has a colour because it absorbs some wavelengths. The enzyme reactions that create colours for glucose are in Unit 4, Biosensors.

Light, transmittance and absorbance

Visible light runs from about 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red); ultraviolet (UV) is the band below 400 nm. A solution looks coloured because it absorbs some visible wavelengths and lets others pass. A clear solution can still absorb UV. The laboratory uses both bands: visible for coloured reaction products, UV for proteins and nucleic acids.

Let I₀ be the light intensity entering the solution and I the intensity leaving it. Transmittance T is the fraction that survives. T has no unit and lies between 0 and 1; multiplying by 100 gives percentage transmittance, %T. Absorbance A is defined so that it grows as light is lost. The minus sign makes A positive when I is less than I₀. A is also called optical density (OD), though some older books use OD loosely.

T = I / I₀    and    A = −log₁₀ T = log₁₀(I₀ / I)

T is transmittance (no unit); I₀ is the incident light intensity; I is the transmitted light intensity; A is absorbance (no unit).

Absorbance is the useful quantity because it is linear in concentration. Transmittance is not. If a solution absorbs half the light, T = 0.50 and A = 0.30. If concentration doubles inside the linear range, T falls to 0.25 but A rises to 0.60, exactly double.

The Beer–Lambert law

The law states that absorbance is proportional to concentration and to the light path. ε is molar absorptivity, a property of the substance at a stated wavelength and temperature. It is the absorbance of a 1 mol/L solution in a 1 cm cuvette. Because ε depends on wavelength, the instrument must be set to the same wavelength for standards and samples.

A = ε c l

A is absorbance (no unit); ε is molar absorptivity in L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹; c is concentration in mol/L; l is the light path through the solution in cm.

A blank cuvette sets the reference. Fill it with the solvent or with reagent minus sample, set A = 0. This makes I₀ the light through the blank, so absorbance from the sample's colour alone is measured. Without a blank, the cuvette, solvent and reagent absorbances are wrongly added to the sample.

For routine work, prepare standards of known concentration, read their absorbances, and plot A against c. The line should pass through the origin with slope εl. Read the unknown from the line. The linear range is the concentration band where this line is straight. At high A (roughly above 1.5) stray light and molecular interactions bend the line; at very low A, noise dominates. Dilute and repeat if a sample reads outside the range.

Inside a spectrophotometer

A single-beam spectrophotometer has six parts in a row. The source gives polychromatic light. A tungsten-halogen lamp covers the visible band; a deuterium lamp covers UV. The monochromator, a prism or a diffraction grating, selects a narrow band of wavelengths. The cuvette holds the blank or sample. It is glass or plastic for visible light, but quartz for UV because ordinary glass absorbs UV. The photodetector converts transmitted light into current; a photodiode is common, and a photomultiplier tube (PMT) is used when light is very weak. The amplifier scales the signal, and the readout shows A and %T.

A colorimeter, also called a filter photometer, is the same chain with a coloured glass or interference filter in place of the monochromator. It works at a few fixed wavelengths, is cheaper and simpler, and suits a single routine test. A spectrophotometer can scan or select any wavelength in its range, so it is more flexible. Some syllabi call the whole topic photometry or colorimetry.

  1. Switch on and let the lamp warm up.
  2. Select the wavelength.
  3. Fill the blank cuvette and set A = 0 or %T = 100.
  4. Wipe the sample cuvette and insert it.
  5. Read A or %T. Handle cuvettes by the frosted sides, not the clear faces, and keep the light path free of bubbles and fingerprints.

Clinical uses and fluorescence

In the clinical laboratory, spectrophotometry measures haemoglobin as cyanmethaemoglobin, bilirubin, glucose through an enzyme-produced colour, creatinine through the Jaffe reaction, and total protein by a colour reaction. Each test fixes a wavelength where the product absorbs strongly and the reagents absorb little.

Fluorescence spectroscopy, which some syllabi add, is a related method. The sample is excited at one wavelength and the emitted light is measured at a longer wavelength. It is more sensitive than absorption because the detector sees emitted light against a dark background, but it needs a fluorophore and careful correction for quenching.

An automated biochemical analyser uses the same Beer–Lambert law: it pipettes sample and reagent, incubates, reads absorbance in a flow-through or cuvette photometer, and computes concentration. See 3.5 Automated biochemical analysers. A flame photometer also measures light, but it measures emission from excited atoms, not absorption; see 3.2 Flame photometer.

Worked example

Question. A 0.20 mmol/L standard of a coloured compound in a 1.00 cm cuvette reads A = 0.30. (a) Find ε. (b) A patient sample in the same cuvette reads A = 0.45; find its concentration. (c) What is the transmittance of the standard?

  1. Convert the standard concentration to mol/L: c = 0.20 mmol/L = 0.20 × 10⁻³ mol/L = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L.
  2. Find ε from A = ε c l: ε = A / (c l) = 0.30 / (2.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L × 1.00 cm) = 1500 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹.
  3. For the sample: c = A / (ε l) = 0.45 / (1500 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ × 1.00 cm) = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L.
  4. Convert to mmol/L: 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L × 10³ mmol/mol = 0.30 mmol/L.
  5. Find the standard transmittance: T = 10^(−A) = 10^(−0.30) = 0.50, that is 50 %.

Answer. ε = 1500 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹; the sample concentration is 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L = 0.30 mmol/L; the standard transmittance is 0.50 (50 %).

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the blank: reagent colour and cuvette absorption then add to the sample and give a falsely high absorbance.
  • Mixing up absorbance and transmittance: A is logarithmic, so doubling concentration doubles A but squares T: if A goes from 0.30 to 0.60, T falls from 0.50 to 0.25. The halving seen in one example is not a general rule. Both relations hold only inside the linear range.
  • Using a glass cuvette for UV work: glass absorbs UV strongly below about 340 nm, so very little light reaches the detector and the reading becomes noisy and distorted by stray light. If the sample cell is glass while the blank is quartz, the extra absorption is added to the sample and A reads falsely high. Use quartz, or UV-transparent plastic, for both blank and sample in UV work.
  • Assuming the calibration stays straight at high absorbance: above about A = 1.5, stray light bends the line; dilute the sample and repeat.
  • Changing the light path: A = ε c l, so a 0.5 cm cuvette halves A; standards and samples must use the same path length.

Analyze A sample reads A = 2.0. Why is this value unreliable, and what should the technician do? 4 marks

A = 2.0 is above the linear range; stray light and molecular interactions make the Beer–Lambert plot bend, so concentration read from a calibration line is wrong. Dilute the sample by a known factor, re-read, and multiply the result by that factor.

Analyze What is the purpose of a blank cuvette, and what happens if it is omitted? 4 marks

The blank contains solvent or reagent without sample. It sets I₀ and makes A = 0 for everything except the sample's colour. If omitted, absorbance from the cuvette, solvent and reagent adds to the sample reading and the concentration is overestimated.

Understand State one difference between a colorimeter and a spectrophotometer. 3 marks

A colorimeter uses a filter to select a few fixed wavelengths, while a spectrophotometer uses a prism or diffraction grating and can select or scan many wavelengths.

Apply Inside the linear range, the concentration of a coloured solution is tripled. What happens to A and to T? 3 marks

A becomes three times its old value: A_new = 3A_old, because A = εcl. Transmittance becomes the cube of its old value: T_new = 10^(−3A_old) = (10^(−A_old))³ = (T_old)³, that is T_old × T_old × T_old.

3.2 Flame photometer

A flame photometer measures sodium, potassium, lithium, calcium or barium by the light their atoms emit in a hot flame. A diluted sample is sprayed into the flame. Atoms absorb heat, become excited, and fall back emitting light at fixed wavelengths. A filter selects one wavelength and a photodetector measures the brightness. The method exists because these elements are hard to measure by simple colour reactions and are common in clinical samples: sodium and potassium in serum and urine, and lithium in patients on lithium therapy. It is fast, cheap and needs only a small sample.

You need: 3.1 for the source–monochromator–detector–readout chain and for calibration curves.

Atoms emit their own light

An alkali metal atom in a hot flame takes in thermal energy and reaches an excited state. It stays there for a very short time, about a nanosecond, and then falls back. On falling back it emits light at wavelengths fixed by its electron levels. The wavelength is a fingerprint of the element. Sodium gives a strong yellow line at 589 nm, potassium a line at 766 nm, and lithium a line at 671 nm. The symbol nm means nanometre, 10⁻⁹ m. The brightness of the emitted line depends on how many atoms of that element are in the flame. More atoms give more light. This is emission, not absorption: the instrument does not shine its own light through the sample. Only elements that are easily excited in a flame work well: sodium, potassium, lithium, calcium and barium. Clinical use is mostly sodium and potassium in serum and urine, and lithium for patients on lithium therapy.

From diluted sample to reading

The sample must first be diluted because serum contains about 140 mmol/L sodium and 4 mmol/L potassium, which is too high for the linear range. A dilution of 1:50 or 1:100 is common. A nebulizer turns the diluted sample into a fine spray. The spray enters an air–acetylene or air–LPG flame at about 1700–2000 °C. Water evaporates, salts break into atoms, and some atoms become excited. The light from the flame passes through a filter or monochromator that selects one line: 589 nm for sodium, 766 nm for potassium, or 671 nm for lithium. A photodetector converts the selected light into a current. An amplifier increases the signal, and a readout displays a number.

R = k C_d

R is the readout in arbitrary units; C_d is the diluted concentration in mmol/L; k is a constant found by calibration.

The chain follows the same source–monochromator–detector–readout idea as 3.1, but the source is the flame itself.

Calibration, dilution and errors

Calibration uses a blank and at least one standard of known concentration, run under the same flame. For a one-point calibration:

C_serum = C_std × (R_sample / R_std) × D

C_serum is the original serum concentration in mmol/L; C_std is the standard concentration in mmol/L; R_sample and R_std are the readouts for sample and standard in the same units; D is the dilution factor.

For 1:50 dilution D = 50; for 1:100, D = 100. An internal lithium standard is often added to every sample and standard. The instrument reads the sodium-to-lithium ratio. Flame flicker, viscosity differences and small changes in spray rate affect both lines, so the ratio cancels much of the error. Common interferences are other ions, flame drift, a blocked capillary, and too high a concentration. At high concentration, atoms near the cool edge of the flame absorb light from excited atoms, so the curve bends downward; this is self-absorption. A blocked capillary gives a low, noisy signal. An atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AAS) measures light absorbed from a separate hollow-cathode lamp. A flame photometer measures emitted light. Both need calibration, and both are used for sodium and potassium, but the measurement principle is opposite.

Worked example

Question. A serum sample is diluted 1:50 and run in a flame photometer. The readout is 40 units. A 2.0 mmol/L sodium standard gives 32 units under the same flame. Find the sodium concentration in the diluted sample and in the original serum.

  1. A 1:50 dilution means one part serum is made up to 50 parts with water, so the dilution factor is D = 50.
  2. For the same flame, readout is proportional to diluted concentration: R_sample / R_std = C_d / C_std.
  3. C_d = C_std × (R_sample / R_std) = 2.0 mmol/L × (40 / 32) = 2.5 mmol/L.
  4. The original serum is 50 times more concentrated: C_serum = C_d × D = 2.5 mmol/L × 50 = 125 mmol/L.

Answer. The diluted sample contains 2.5 mmol/L sodium, so the original serum contains 125 mmol/L.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the dilution factor: 2.5 mmol/L is the diluted concentration; the serum value is 125 mmol/L in the worked example.
  • Calling the measurement absorption, or applying the Beer–Lambert law directly to the emitted flame light. A flame photometer measures emission.
  • Using the wrong filter: sodium is read at 589 nm, potassium at 766 nm, and lithium at 671 nm.
  • Calibrating only at high concentration, where self-absorption bends the curve downward and makes the sample look lower than it is.
  • Ignoring the internal standard and flame drift, then reporting a false potassium result.

3.3 Blood gas analyzers and pH measurement

A blood gas analyser measures the three variables that set the acid–base state of arterial blood: pH, the partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO₂) and the partial pressure of oxygen (pO₂). Three electrodes sit in one chamber held at 37 °C. A glass pH electrode, a Severinghaus pCO₂ electrode and a Clark pO₂ electrode produce electrical signals, and the analyser calculates bicarbonate and base excess from them. It is used in emergency rooms, intensive care and operation theatres, where a patient in ketoacidosis or on a ventilator needs the result within minutes.

You need electrode theory and the Nernst equation from Unit 1, and the readout and calibration ideas are close to those in 3.1.

pH and the glass electrode

pH tells how acidic a solution is. It is defined as pH = −log₁₀ a(H⁺), where a(H⁺) is the hydrogen ion activity in mol/L. In dilute blood, activity is close to concentration, so pH ≈ −log₁₀[H⁺]. Arterial blood normally lies between 7.35 and 7.45; below 7.35 is acidaemia and above 7.45 is alkalaemia.

pH = −log₁₀ a(H⁺)

a(H⁺) is the hydrogen ion activity in mol/L; [H⁺] is the hydrogen ion concentration in mol/L.

The measurement is potentiometric. The measuring electrode is a glass bulb with a thin hydrated layer. H⁺ ions exchange at the glass surface and create a potential that depends on pH. The reference electrode is Ag/AgCl in fixed KCl; it gives a constant potential. The analyser measures the voltage between the two electrodes. The glass electrode has very high resistance, so the amplifier must have high input impedance; otherwise the reading loads the cell and falls.

E = E⁰ + (2.303RT/nF) log₁₀ a

E is the electrode potential in volts; E⁰ is the standard potential; R is 8.314 J/(mol·K); T is absolute temperature in K; n is the number of electrons per ion (n = 1 for H⁺); F is 96485 C/mol; a is activity.

For H⁺, the practical form is E = E⁰ − (2.303RT/F) pH. At 37 °C the slope 2.303RT/F is about 61.5 mV per pH unit. One pH unit changes the cell voltage by about 61.5 mV; 0.1 pH unit changes it by about 6.15 mV.

pCO₂, pO₂ and derived values

pCO₂ is the partial pressure of carbon dioxide in arterial blood, in mmHg. The Severinghaus pCO₂ electrode is a pH electrode behind a CO₂-permeable membrane. A thin film of sodium bicarbonate solution sits between the membrane and the glass. CO₂ diffuses in, forms carbonic acid, and lowers the pH of the film. The pH electrode reads that fall; after calibration the signal is displayed as pCO₂.

pO₂ is the partial pressure of oxygen, also in mmHg. The Clark pO₂ electrode is a polarographic cell. A platinum cathode is held at about −0.65 V against an Ag/AgCl anode, and an oxygen-permeable membrane covers the cell. Oxygen crosses the membrane and is reduced at the cathode: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻. The current is proportional to pO₂.

Bicarbonate is calculated, not measured, from the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation. Base excess is the amount of acid needed to bring pH back to 7.40 at pCO₂ 40 mmHg; normal is −2 to +2 mmol/L. Total CO₂ = [HCO₃⁻] + 0.03 × pCO₂, in mmol/L.

pH = 6.1 + log₁₀([HCO₃⁻] / (0.03 × pCO₂))

6.1 is the pKa of carbonic acid at 37 °C; [HCO₃⁻] is in mmol/L; pCO₂ is in mmHg; 0.03 is the CO₂ solubility in mmol/(L·mmHg).

Running a sample and reading the pattern

  1. Take arterial blood into a heparinised syringe; the radial artery is common.
  2. Remove air bubbles, cap the syringe and label it.
  3. Run the sample within minutes; if delayed, keep it on ice and correct for temperature.
  4. Calibrate with two gas mixtures of known pCO₂ and pO₂ and with pH buffers; run quality control serum.
  5. Read pH, pCO₂ and pO₂; check the calculated HCO₃⁻ and base excess.

Room air has pO₂ about 150 mmHg and pCO₂ about 0 mmHg. An air bubble therefore raises pO₂ and lowers pCO₂. The chamber is held at 37 °C because the electrodes are calibrated at that temperature; a cool sample gives a falsely low pCO₂ and a falsely high pH. Some syllabi call this an ABG (arterial blood gas) analyser.

Worked example

Question. A blood gas analyser reports pH 7.32, pCO₂ 30 mmHg and HCO₃⁻ 15 mmol/L at 37 °C. Check the bicarbonate value with the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation and classify the acid–base state.

  1. Use Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH = 6.1 + log₁₀([HCO₃⁻] / (0.03 × pCO₂)).
  2. Substitute [HCO₃⁻] = 15 mmol/L and pCO₂ = 30 mmHg.
  3. 0.03 × 30 = 0.90 mmol/L.
  4. 15 ÷ 0.90 = 16.7 (unitless ratio).
  5. log₁₀ 16.7 = 1.22.
  6. pH = 6.1 + 1.22 = 7.32.
  7. pH 7.32 is below 7.35, so there is acidaemia. HCO₃⁻ 15 mmol/L is below 22 mmol/L, so the cause is metabolic. pCO₂ 30 mmHg is below 35 mmHg, so the lungs are compensating.

Answer. pH = 7.32, so the sample shows metabolic acidosis with respiratory compensation.

Exam traps

  • Mixing mmHg and kPa. Normal pCO₂ is 35–45 mmHg, which is about 4.7–6.0 kPa; do not put a kPa number into a formula written for mmHg.
  • Forgetting the 37 °C condition. A cool sample gives a falsely low pCO₂ and a falsely high pH, because the electrode slope and the blood equilibria change with temperature.
  • Leaving an air bubble in the syringe. Room air raises pO₂ and lowers pCO₂, so the printout can look like hyperoxia with respiratory alkalosis.
  • Treating the printed HCO₃⁻ as a measured value. It is calculated from pH and pCO₂, so an error in pCO₂ moves the HCO₃⁻ value.
  • Reversing the sign of base excess. A positive base excess means metabolic alkalosis; a negative base excess means metabolic acidosis.

3.4 Blood cell counters

A blood cell does not conduct electricity well. Pull it through a small hole filled with salt solution and it pushes electrolyte out of the way. The resistance across the hole rises for a moment, so a constant current makes a small voltage pulse. Count the pulses and you have the number of cells. Measure their height and you have cell size. This is the Coulter principle. It turned the slow manual blood count into a routine automated test printed on every complete blood count report.

You need: 3.1 for a calibrated readout, and Unit 1 for amplifiers and transducers.

Blood cells and the manual count

Blood carries three formed elements. Red cells, or erythrocytes, carry oxygen. White cells, or leucocytes, defend the body. Platelets, or thrombocytes, help clotting. Adult reference ranges are wide. Red cells are about 4.5–5.5 × 1012 per litre. White cells are 4–11 × 109 per litre. Platelets are 150–400 × 109 per litre. The five white cell classes are neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and basophils.

Manual counting uses a haemocytometer: a glass slide with a ruled Neubauer grid and a chamber 0.1 mm deep. One large square is 1 mm × 1 mm × 0.1 mm, so its volume is 0.1 µL. Blood is diluted in an isotonic diluent so cells stay round and separate. The diluted sample fills the chamber by capillary action. A technician counts cells under a microscope in a known number of squares.

  1. Dilute blood by a known factor D. For red cells D is often 1:200; for white cells 1:20.
  2. Fill the Neubauer chamber and let cells settle.
  3. Count N cells in a known volume V of diluted sample, in µL.
  4. Use the formula below and report per litre.

cells per litre = (N × D × 106) / V

N is the number of cells counted; D is the dilution factor; V is the counted volume in µL of diluted sample; 106 converts cells per µL to cells per litre.

The Coulter principle

A Coulter counter pulls diluted blood through a single aperture, about 100 µm across, in a glass tube. Two electrodes sit on either side of the aperture. A constant current flows through the electrolyte between them. A cell is a poor conductor compared with the electrolyte. As it enters the aperture it displaces conducting liquid, so resistance rises. The current source holds current constant, so the resistance change appears as a voltage pulse of a few millivolts.

The pulse is amplified and fed to threshold discriminators. A small pulse is a platelet. A medium pulse is a red cell. A larger pulse is a white cell. The thresholds are set from known size ranges and calibration particles. A scaler counts the pulses above each threshold. The same pulses feed a size histogram. The pulse height follows cell volume, so the histogram shows the population distribution.

ΔV = I × ΔR

ΔV is the pulse height in volts; I is the constant current in amperes; ΔR is the rise in resistance in ohms when a cell displaces electrolyte.

Two cells passing together make one pulse, not two. This coincidence loss makes a concentrated sample read low. Dilution to about 1:500 for red cells keeps the chance small. The aperture must also be clean; a clog blocks current and gives false counts.

Automatic counters and the white cell differential

A modern analyser combines impedance with flow cytometry. The diluted sample is forced through a narrow stream so cells pass one at a time through a laser beam. Forward scatter at a small angle gives cell size. Side scatter at 90° gives internal granularity. Fluorescence from a stained cell gives a chemical label. A detector array records all three for each cell.

A three-part differential groups white cells into lymphocytes, monocytes and granulocytes. A five-part differential reports neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and basophils. Impedance alone separates by size only, so eosinophils overlap neutrophils. Optical scatter and cytochemical or fluorescent staining resolve them. The analyser plots a scattergram and flags abnormal populations for a manual film review.

Semi-automatic counters need the operator to dilute the sample and press a button. Fully automatic counters aspirate blood, dilute, lyse red cells for the white count, count platelets, and print a complete blood count. They also measure haemoglobin by photometry, as in 3.1. Results are reported per litre, with reference ranges by age and sex.

Worked example

Question. A blood sample is diluted 1:500. While 0.2 mL of the diluted suspension passes the aperture, 2.0 × 106 pulses are counted. Find the red cell count per litre and per µL.

  1. Counted pulses N = 2.0 × 106.
  2. Volume of diluted suspension counted V = 0.2 mL.
  3. Cells per mL of diluted sample = N / V = 2.0 × 106 / 0.2 mL = 1.0 × 107 per mL.
  4. Cells per mL of blood = 1.0 × 107 × 500 = 5.0 × 109 per mL.
  5. Cells per litre = 5.0 × 109 × 1000 = 5.0 × 1012 per litre.
  6. Per µL is the same as per 10-6 L: 5.0 × 106 per µL, that is 5.0 million per µL.

Answer. The red cell count is 5.0 × 1012 per litre, or 5.0 million per µL.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the dilution factor D. Multiply by it once; the diluted count is not the blood count.
  • Using the blood volume instead of the diluted volume in the formula. V is the volume actually counted in the chamber or aperture.
  • Reporting per millilitre when the report asks per litre. Multiply per mL by 1000.
  • Believing a high count from a clogged aperture. Coincidence biases counts low; a clog can bias them high.
  • Setting thresholds by eye. Platelets and small red cells overlap; set thresholds with calibration particles.

3.5 Automated biochemical analysers

An automated biochemical analyser pipettes sample and reagent, incubates at 37 °C, reads absorbance and computes concentration for hundreds of tests an hour. A hospital laboratory may receive 500 samples before noon, each needing several tests; a bench cuvette cannot keep up. The chemistry is still the Beer–Lambert law; only pipetting, timing and washing are mechanised.

You need: Read Chapter 3.1 for the Beer–Lambert law, the cuvette photometer and calibration.

The fluid path and the blocks

A sample probe dips into the tube, senses liquid level and aspirates 2–20 µL of serum. A second probe adds 200–300 µL of reagent. Capacitive sensing: the probe tip is one plate of a capacitor, and capacitance changes when the tip touches serum. Resistive sensing: the probe and grounded tube form a circuit whose conductance changes when the tip enters conducting serum. Either change stops the downward drive and flags a short sample.

Two pumps move liquids. A peristaltic pump squeezes a flexible tube with rollers; it is cheap but less precise. A syringe pump drives a piston and delivers microlitre volumes accurately.

The mixture enters a cuvette in a wheel held at 37 °C for a fixed incubation time. Light from the lamp passes through the filter wheel, which selects the wavelength — common ones are 340, 405, 505, 546, 578 and 600 nm — then through the cuvette. A photodiode after the cuvette measures transmitted light. A wash station aspirates the used mixture and rinses the cuvette.

Continuous flow and discrete analysers

Continuous-flow or air-segmented analysers push sample and reagent as one stream broken by air bubbles. The bubbles scrub the tube wall and keep samples apart as the stream passes one detector. Every sample gets the same timing. Very few remain installed.

Discrete analysers give each test its own cuvette. In a random-access analyser any test can be ordered on any sample at any time, and an emergency sample can jump the queue; most large Indian laboratories use these. A batch analyser runs one test on a long row of samples and is cheaper for one high-volume assay.

Throughput is quoted in tests per hour. 600 tests per hour means one result every 3600 s ÷ 600 = 6 s on average. This is cycle time, not the incubation time of one test, which is longer and overlaps other tests. A probe washed between samples still carries a trace of previous reagent, so reagent carryover adds a small false rise to the next result. Daily quality control uses control sera of known value on a chart with ±2 SD limits.

Reading the result: end point, kinetic, bichromatic

A reagent starts the reaction and absorbance changes. In an end-point assay the reaction runs to completion and the analyser reads the final absorbance once. The computer converts it with a stored calibration curve:

c(sample) = c(std) × (A(sample) ÷ A(std))

c(sample) is the unknown concentration in mmol/L; c(std) is the calibrator value in mmol/L; A(sample) and A(std) are net absorbances, meaning measured absorbance minus reagent blank and cuvette blank: A = A(measured) − A(blank).

In a kinetic assay the analyser takes several readings while the reaction runs at a steady rate and computes ΔA/min, the change in absorbance per minute. Enzyme tests are usually read as a rate, because activity is proportional to ΔA/min; some fixed-time methods read one absorbance after a set incubation, but a true rate method cannot be read as an end point.

Bichromatic measurement reads the cuvette at the main wavelength and again at a reference wavelength, then subtracts. Turbidity such as lipaemia, and a scratched or dirty cuvette, add nearly the same absorbance at both wavelengths, so the subtraction removes most of that. Haemolysis is not cancelled this way: free haemoglobin absorbs differently at the two wavelengths. Modern analysers measure a haemolysis index and flag the sample.

Worked example

Question. An end-point glucose assay at 505 nm uses a 1 cm cuvette. A 5.0 mmol/L calibrator gives net A = 0.28; the sample gives net A = 0.42. Find glucose and the average cycle time at 600 tests per hour.

  1. Use c(sample) = c(std) × (A(sample) ÷ A(std)); A means net absorbance.
  2. c = 5.0 mmol/L × (0.42 ÷ 0.28) = 5.0 × 1.50 = 7.5 mmol/L.
  3. Cycle time = 3600 s ÷ 600 = 6 s on average.

Answer. Glucose is 7.5 mmol/L; average cycle time is 6 s.

Exam traps

  • Using single-point calibration outside the linear range: the curve flattens, and a straight-line ratio under-reads the true concentration.
  • Reading a kinetic assay as an end point: absorbance is still changing; enzyme tests need ΔA/min.
  • Forgetting reagent carryover: a trace of previous reagent raises the next result; use an effective wash and check with a blank after a high-reagent sample, not by a reagent blank.
  • Mixing results from analysers with different wavelengths or path lengths: the same concentration gives a different absorbance.

3.6 Chromatography and electrophoresis

Chromatography and electrophoresis both separate the molecules of a mixture. Chromatography uses a moving mobile phase and a stationary phase; each solute divides between them differently and so travels at its own speed. Electrophoresis uses an electric field: a charged molecule feels a force qE and drifts at a speed set by its charge and friction. Clinical labs use these methods for glycated haemoglobin, drug levels, urine steroids, amino acids, serum proteins, haemoglobin variants and DNA. Only a few universities teach this chapter; others meet the ideas in analyser specifications.

You need: 3.1 for UV absorbance and the densitometer, and 3.7 for a clear serum before the run.

Chromatography: moving and stationary phases

A mixture is placed at the start of a column or sheet. The mobile phase is a gas or liquid that flows and carries solutes. The stationary phase is a solid or liquid film that does not move. Each solute divides between the phases. A solute that spends more time in the stationary phase is retarded more and leaves later. Separation is by partition (dissolves in both phases) or adsorption (sticks to the solid).

Retention time tR is time from injection to a peak maximum. Two solutes separate when their retention times differ and peaks do not overlap too much. Thin-layer chromatography uses a coated plate and a rising solvent; paper chromatography uses water held in paper fibres; column chromatography packs the stationary phase in a tube.

Gas chromatography (GC) uses a carrier gas and a heated column; a flame ionisation detector burns organic molecules and measures ion current. HPLC uses small packed particles, so liquid is pumped at high pressure; a UV detector at 280 nm is common for proteins. Clinical uses: glycated haemoglobin, drug levels, urine steroids, amino acids.

Electrophoresis: speed set by charge and friction

An electrophoresis tank applies an electric field E (V/m) across a buffer-soaked support. A molecule with charge q (C) feels force qE. It accelerates until friction balances that force, then drifts at speed v (m/s).

v = μE = qE / f

v is drift speed (m/s); μ is mobility (m²/V·s); E is field strength (V/m); q is charge (C); f is friction factor (N per m/s).

The sign of the charge follows buffer pH relative to the protein's isoelectric point pI. At pH 8.6 serum proteins are net negative and migrate to the anode. Mobility depends on charge, size and shape.

Paper and cellulose acetate carry serum proteins and haemoglobin variants. Agarose gel is used for DNA, negative from phosphate groups. SDS-PAGE binds SDS, masks protein charge and makes all proteins negative, so they separate by molecular weight: smaller moves faster. Isoelectric focusing uses a pH gradient; a protein stops at its pI, where net charge is zero. Capillary electrophoresis uses a narrow tube and high voltage.

Serum protein electrophoresis on cellulose acetate at pH 8.6 gives albumin, α₁, α₂, β and γ bands. Albumin is fastest and most abundant. A densitometer scans the stained strip and reports each band as a percentage of total protein.

Worked example

Question. Serum protein electrophoresis on cellulose acetate at pH 8.6 is run on a sample with total protein 70 g/L. The densitometer reads albumin 60 %, α₁ 4 %, α₂ 9 %, β 12 % and γ 15 %. Calculate the albumin and γ-globulin concentrations in g/L.

  1. Check the fractions: 60 + 4 + 9 + 12 + 15 = 100 %.
  2. Albumin fraction = 60 % = 0.60.
  3. Albumin concentration = 0.60 × 70 g/L = 42 g/L.
  4. γ-globulin fraction = 15 % = 0.15.
  5. γ-globulin concentration = 0.15 × 70 g/L = 10.5 g/L.

Answer. Albumin is 42 g/L and γ-globulin is 10.5 g/L.

  • At pH 8.6 serum proteins are net negative and move to the anode; albumin is fastest, γ slowest.
  • Retention time belongs to chromatography; migration distance or mobility belongs to electrophoresis.
  • SDS-PAGE reports molecular weight, not net charge, because SDS masks the protein's own charge.
  • In HPLC the small packed particles are the stationary phase; high pressure is needed for flow, not heating.
  • In isoelectric focusing the protein stops at pH = pI because net charge is zero, even though the field remains on.

3.7 Centrifugation

A centrifuge spins tubes around a spindle. Denser particles move outward and settle as a pellet; the liquid left above is the supernatant. The outward force is not set by rpm alone. It grows with the square of the speed and with the radius, so one tube gives different forces on different rotors. Laboratories use this to get serum or plasma from blood, urine sediment for microscopy, and separated blood components for transfusion.

You need: drag on a particle moving through a fluid, and the blood fractions from 3.4.

Force, radius and speed

Only a few universities teach this chapter, but every laboratory protocol needs the calculation. A particle in a spinning tube is pushed outward by a centrifugal field. The field is quoted as relative centrifugal force (RCF): the number of times it is stronger than gravity, g = 9.81 m/s². RCF depends on the radius r, measured in cm from the spindle to the tube bottom, and on the rotor speed in revolutions per minute (rpm).

RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × r × (rpm)²

r is the radius from spindle to tube bottom in cm; rpm is the rotor speed; RCF is a multiple of g.

In SI units RCF = ω²r/g, where ω is angular speed in rad/s and r is in m. Convert rpm to ω with ω = 2π(rpm)/60. Doubling rpm gives four times RCF.

Stokes' law gives the settling speed v = d² Δρ ω²r/(18η), where d is particle diameter, Δρ is particle density minus fluid density, and η is fluid viscosity. The sedimentation coefficient is s = v/(ω²r) = d²Δρ/(18η). It is quoted in Svedberg units: 1 S = 10⁻¹³ s. Denser or larger particles have larger s and pellet first.

Rotors and routine use

  • Fixed-angle rotor: tubes sit at a slant; the pellet collects on the outer wall. Good for high-speed spins.
  • Swing-out or horizontal rotor: buckets swing flat; the pellet collects at the tube bottom and is easy to see.
  • Bench and microcentrifuge: small volumes; refrigerated versions remove motor heat.
  • High-speed and ultracentrifuge: higher RCF; an ultracentrifuge spins under vacuum to reduce heating.
  • Preparative centrifugation collects a pellet. Analytical centrifugation measures the moving boundary during the spin.
  • Density-gradient separation: Ficoll-Hypaque layers lymphocytes at an interface.

For serum, clotted blood is spun at about 2000–3000 rpm for 10 min; anticoagulated blood gives plasma. Urine is spun briefly and the sediment is examined under the microscope. Blood banks separate red cells, platelets and plasma.

  • Balance opposite tubes by weight; imbalance at high speed damages the rotor.
  • Close and lock the lid; never open while the rotor turns.
  • Use sealed cups and check tubes for cracks; a broken tube makes an aerosol.

Worked example

Question. A tube is held at a radius of 10 cm in a rotor spinning at 4000 rpm. Find the relative centrifugal force. What is the RCF at 8000 rpm?

  1. Write the formula: RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × r × (rpm)².
  2. Put r = 10 cm and rpm = 4000: RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 × (4000)².
  3. (4000)² = 1.6 × 10⁷.
  4. RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 × 1.6 × 10⁷ = 1788.8.
  5. At 8000 rpm, (8000)² = 6.4 × 10⁷, so RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 × 6.4 × 10⁷ = 7155.2.

Answer. The RCF is 1788.8 × g at 4000 rpm and 7155.2 × g at 8000 rpm.

Exam traps

  • Using the tube diameter instead of the radius from the spindle to the tube bottom.
  • Quoting rpm as if it were a force: two rotors at the same rpm give different RCF values.
  • Forgetting to square the speed: doubling rpm gives four times RCF, not twice.
  • Swapping pellet and supernatant: the pellet is the solid at the wall or bottom.
  • Skipping the balance step: an unbalanced high-speed rotor can break tubes and release aerosol.

3.8 Radioactivity measurement: GM counter and scintillation

Some patients need a radioactivity measurement: a thyroid uptake study, a hormone assay, a sample in a hot lab. Ionising radiation is invisible, so we detect what it does — it knocks electrons off atoms. In a gas it makes ion pairs, and the Geiger–Müller tube turns each event into one large pulse. In a crystal it makes a flash of light, and a photomultiplier turns that flash into a pulse whose height follows the radiation energy.

You need the photodetector and photomultiplier idea from 3.1.

Radiation units and the Geiger–Müller tube

Activity is decays per second, in becquerel (Bq): 1 Bq = 1 decay per second. The older curie (Ci) is 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq. Absorbed dose is energy deposited per kilogram of the material absorbing it, in gray (Gy): 1 Gy = 1 J/kg. Equivalent dose weights absorbed dose by the harm of each radiation type, in sievert (Sv); for gamma rays, 1 Gy gives 1 Sv.

The GM tube is a metal cylinder with a thin window, filled with argon at low pressure plus a little halogen or alcohol vapour, the quench gas. A fine axial wire is the anode at a few hundred volts positive; the wall is the cathode.

A gamma ray or beta particle ionises argon. Electrons drift to the wire, gain energy in the steep field near it and ionise more argon, so an avalanche builds and the tube discharges briefly. One event gives one pulse of about 1 V, whatever energy the radiation carried.

The quench gas absorbs the discharge photons and stops the avalanche, so the tube recovers. Count rate against voltage is flat over the Geiger plateau, usually 350–500 V, and the tube runs mid-plateau. Every pulse is the same size, so the tube counts radiation but cannot name the isotope. After each pulse the tube is blind for a dead time of 100–200 µs, which limits high count rates.

Scintillation counter and the gamma spectrum

A thallium-activated sodium iodide crystal, NaI(Tl), absorbs a gamma ray and emits a light flash whose brightness follows the energy deposited. A photomultiplier tube (PMT) has a photocathode that turns the flash into electrons and a dynode chain that multiplies them about a million times, giving an electrical pulse.

Pulse height follows gamma energy. A multichannel analyser sorts pulses by height into channels and plots counts against energy: the gamma spectrum. The photopeak position identifies the isotope, which the GM tube cannot do.

Tritium and carbon-14 beta particles are too weak to reach a crystal, so the sample is dissolved in a liquid scintillation cocktail and flashes are counted from inside. Clinical uses include radioimmunoassay with iodine-125 and thyroid uptake studies.

Safety is lead shielding, short time near the source and distance, since dose falls with the square of distance. Staff wear film or thermoluminescent badges to record dose.

Worked example

Question. A Geiger–Müller counter gives 12 000 counts in 5.0 min with a source present and 200 counts in 5.0 min with the source removed. The counting efficiency is 20 %. Find the net count rate and the activity of the source.

  1. Both counts are taken over the same time: t = 5.0 min = 300 s.
  2. Gross count rate = 12 000 / 300 s = 40.0 counts per second; background rate = 200 / 300 s = 0.67 counts per second.
  3. Net counts = 12 000 − 200 = 11 800, so net count rate = 11 800 / 300 s = 39.33 counts per second.
  4. Activity A = net count rate / efficiency = 39.33 / 0.20 = 196.67 Bq.

Answer. The net count rate is about 39.3 counts per second and the source activity is about 197 Bq, roughly 2.0 × 10² Bq.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting to subtract the background count, or reporting raw counts instead of counts per second.
  • Claiming the GM pulse height tells you the radiation energy — it does not; energy information comes from detectors whose pulse height follows deposited energy, such as the scintillation counter, proportional counter or a semiconductor detector.
  • Mixing the units: curie is activity (1 Ci = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq), gray is absorbed dose, sievert is equivalent dose.
  • Treating dead time as microseconds of no consequence: at 5000 counts per second with 200 µs dead time the tube is effectively saturated.
  • Setting the GM voltage anywhere on the plateau without checking it is a flat region — below it counts are lost, above it the tube discharges continuously.

Geiger–Müller tubeGas-filled detector that gives one large voltage pulse per ionising event, without energy information.3.8
Scintillation counterScintillator converts radiation to light flashes; a photomultiplier converts them to pulses whose height follows energy.3.8

Takeaways

  1. Beer–Lambert law links absorbance, concentration, and path length, so a photometer can read concentration from light transmission.
  2. A flame photometer measures light emitted by excited alkali metal atoms and uses calibration to report sodium or potassium concentration.
  3. A blood gas analyser combines a glass pH electrode, a Severinghaus pCO₂ electrode, and a Clark pO₂ electrode in one temperature-controlled chamber.
  4. Bicarbonate and base excess are calculated from measured pH and pCO₂ rather than measured directly by the electrodes.
  5. A Coulter counter counts resistance pulses to count cells and uses pulse height to estimate cell size.
  6. An automated biochemical analyser is a robot that repeats photometric measurement many times per hour with pipetting, incubation, and washing steps.
  7. Chromatography separates by partition between mobile and stationary phases, while electrophoresis separates charged molecules by movement in an electric field.
  8. Centrifugal force grows with the square of rotor speed and with radius, so RCF must be calculated for each rotor and speed.
  9. A Geiger–Müller tube gives a simple count per event, while a scintillation counter gives pulse height related to radiation energy.

Remember and understand

Understand State the Beer–Lambert law, define every term, and explain why absorbance is used instead of transmitted intensity for concentration measurement. 12 marks

Write A = εcl and define A, ε, c, l; Explain A = log₁₀(I₀/I) and the linear relation to concentration; State assumptions: monochromatic light, dilute solution, no scattering; Give one clinical example such as glucose or haemoglobin measurement.

Understand Draw and explain the construction and working of a flame photometer for estimating sodium and potassium in serum. 13 marks

Label nebulizer, burner, flame, monochromator or filter, detector, and readout; Explain atomisation, excitation, and emission at fixed wavelengths; Describe calibration with standards and use of lithium internal standard; Mention interference from other alkali metals and flame instability.

Understand Describe the three electrode systems of a blood gas analyser and state what each measures. 14 marks

Glass pH electrode: potential changes with H⁺ activity; Severinghaus electrode: CO₂ changes internal bicarbonate pH, giving pCO₂; Clark electrode: O₂ reduction at cathode gives current proportional to pO₂; Explain common temperature control, membranes, electrolytes, and calibration.

Understand Explain the Coulter principle and how a blood cell counter obtains both cell count and cell size. 12 marks

Describe aperture, electrolyte, electrodes, and current path; Cell displaces electrolyte and raises resistance; one pulse per cell; Pulse count gives cell number; pulse height gives volume; Mention dilution, threshold setting, and coincidence error.

Understand Compare chromatography and electrophoresis as separation methods, naming the property each uses. 10 marks

Chromatography uses partition between mobile and stationary phases; Electrophoresis uses charge and size in an electric field; Compare mobile phase, support medium, detection, and typical clinical uses; Give examples such as HbA1c by chromatography and serum protein electrophoresis.

Apply

Apply A solution of concentration 0.20 mmol L⁻¹ gives absorbance 0.40 in a 1.0 cm cuvette. Calculate ε and the concentration when absorbance is 0.30 under the same conditions. 10 marks

Use ε = A/(cl) = 0.40/(0.20 × 1.0) = 2.0 L mmol⁻¹ cm⁻¹; Use c = A/(εl) = 0.30/2.0 = 0.15 mmol L⁻¹; Show unit cancellation and state proportionality; Note that the same wavelength and cuvette are assumed.

Apply A 1:50 diluted serum sample gives 60 units on a flame photometer; a 3.0 mmol L⁻¹ sodium standard gives 50 units. Find serum sodium concentration and discuss one interference. 10 marks

Use ratio c_diluted = 3.0 × 60/50 = 3.6 mmol L⁻¹; Multiply by dilution factor: 3.6 × 50 = 180 mmol L⁻¹; State that potassium or ionisation interference can bias emission; Suggest internal standard or standard-addition check.

Apply For a blood sample at 37 °C, pH = 7.30 and pCO₂ = 45 mmHg. Using the Henderson–Hasselbalch relation, calculate bicarbonate in mmol L⁻¹. 12 marks

Use [HCO₃⁻] = 0.03 × pCO₂(mmHg) × 10^(pH − 6.1); Substitute: 0.03 × 45 × 10^1.20 = 21.4 mmol L⁻¹; State that low bicarbonate with low pH suggests metabolic acidosis; Mention that base excess is a calculated acid–base index.

Apply A 1:500 diluted blood sample is drawn through a Coulter aperture; 0.100 mL gives 15,000 pulses. Calculate cells per µL in whole blood and state one coincidence limitation. 10 marks

Diluted count = 15,000 cells in 0.100 mL = 150,000 cells/mL; Multiply by 500: 75,000,000 cells/mL whole blood; Convert to 75,000 cells/µL; Coincidence error counts two cells as one at high concentration.

Apply An automated analyser takes 18 s per test channel and runs 20 channels in parallel. Calculate theoretical tests per hour and explain why carryover and reagent dead volume reduce this figure. 10 marks

Per channel: 3600/18 = 200 tests per hour; Twenty channels: 200 × 20 = 4000 tests per hour theoretical; Carryover needs wash steps between samples; Reagent dead volume, calibration, and quality control reduce throughput.

Apply A centrifuge rotor has radius 10 cm and spins at 3000 rpm. Calculate RCF in ×g and explain why a 5 cm rotor at the same rpm gives a different force. 10 marks

Use RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × r × (rpm)²; For r = 10 cm: RCF ≈ 1006 ×g; For r = 5 cm: RCF ≈ 503 ×g; RCF depends on radius as well as speed.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze A bench spectrophotometer and an automated analyser use the same Beer–Lambert law but give different serum glucose values. Analyse possible causes and state checks. 14 marks

Check wavelength, path length, blank, and calibration; Check reagent age, temperature, incubation time, and sample volume; Consider carryover, flow-cell dirt, turbidity, and lipaemia; Verify with controls, duplicate samples, and manual comparison.

Evaluate A clinical laboratory reports very low sodium by flame photometry and a high red cell count by Coulter counter on the same diluted sample. Analyse whether dilution, calibration, or coincidence can explain both, and outline verification steps. 14 marks

Check shared diluent, dilution factor, and pipetting error; For sodium, check flame calibration and potassium interference; For cell count, check aperture blockage, coincidence, and threshold; Repeat with fresh dilutions, controls, and an independent method.

Evaluate Compare a Geiger–Müller counter and a scintillation detector for measuring a low-activity radioimmunoassay label; choose one and justify by efficiency, energy information, and quenching. 12 marks

GM tube gives one large pulse per event and little energy information; Scintillation counter gives pulse height related to energy and higher efficiency for gamma; Quenching and background affect low-activity counting; Choose scintillation for low activity and energy discrimination; choose GM for simple surveys.

Glossary

Absorbance
A logarithmic measure of how much light a sample absorbs, equal to log₁₀(I₀/I).
Analyte
The substance being measured in a sample.
Base excess
The calculated amount of acid or base needed to return blood pH to 7.40 at pCO₂ 40 mmHg.
Blank
A reference solution without the analyte, used to set the zero absorbance point.
Buffer
A solution that resists large pH changes when small amounts of acid or base are added.
Calibration curve
A plot of instrument signal against known concentrations, used to read an unknown.
Chromatogram
The record of detector signal versus time from a chromatographic separation.
Coincidence error
Counting error in a cell counter when two cells pass the aperture together and are counted as one.
Colorimetry
Measurement of colour intensity to estimate the concentration of a coloured substance.
Cuvette
A transparent cell that holds the sample in a photometer.
Electrophoretic mobility
Migration velocity of a charged molecule per unit electric field.
Henderson–Hasselbalch equation
pH = 6.1 + log([HCO₃⁻]/(0.03 × pCO₂)) for blood bicarbonate, with pCO₂ in mmHg.
Internal standard
A fixed amount of a substance added to samples and standards to correct for instrument variation.
Mobile phase
The moving liquid or gas that carries solutes through a chromatographic system.
Monochromator
A device that selects a narrow band of wavelengths from a light source.
Nebulizer
A device that turns a liquid sample into a fine spray for introduction into a flame.
Pellet
The sediment packed at the bottom of a centrifuge tube.
pH
The negative logarithm of hydrogen ion activity, used to express acidity or alkalinity.
Photodetector
A device that converts light intensity into an electrical signal.
Photomultiplier tube
A sensitive light detector that converts weak light flashes into measurable electrical pulses.
Relative centrifugal force
Centrifugal force expressed as a multiple of gravitational acceleration g.
Scintillator
A material that emits light when ionising radiation deposits energy in it.
Stationary phase
The fixed phase in chromatography through which the mobile phase moves.
Supernatant
The liquid left above the sediment after centrifugation.
