3.1 Spectrophotometry and colorimetry

A coloured or ultraviolet-absorbing solution takes light out of a beam in proportion to how much substance it contains. Shine a known light intensity through it, measure what survives, and the Beer–Lambert law turns that measurement into concentration. Hospital laboratories use this daily for haemoglobin, bilirubin, glucose, creatinine and total protein. The instrument is a spectrophotometer; a colorimeter is its simpler cousin with a filter instead of a monochromator. This chapter explains the light measurement, the law, the instrument chain and the practical limits.

You need: concentration in mol/L, the base-10 logarithm, and the idea that a solution has a colour because it absorbs some wavelengths. The enzyme reactions that make the colour in a glucose test are met again with enzyme-based biosensors.

Light, transmittance and absorbance

Visible light runs from about 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red); ultraviolet (UV) is the band below 400 nm. A solution looks coloured because it absorbs some visible wavelengths and lets others pass. A clear solution can still absorb UV. The laboratory uses both bands: visible for coloured reaction products, UV for proteins and nucleic acids.

Let I₀ be the light intensity entering the solution and I the intensity leaving it. Transmittance T is the fraction that survives. T has no unit and lies between 0 and 1; multiplying by 100 gives percentage transmittance, %T. Absorbance A is defined so that it grows as light is lost. The minus sign makes A positive when I is less than I₀. Older books call A the optical density (OD).

T = I / I₀    and    A = −log₁₀ T = log₁₀(I₀ / I)

T is transmittance (no unit); I₀ is the incident light intensity; I is the transmitted light intensity; A is absorbance (no unit).

Absorbance is the useful quantity because it is linear in concentration. Transmittance is not. If a solution absorbs half the light, T = 0.50 and A = 0.30. If concentration doubles inside the linear range, T falls to 0.25 but A rises to 0.60, exactly double.

The Beer–Lambert law

The law states that absorbance is proportional to concentration and to the light path. ε is molar absorptivity, a property of the substance at a stated wavelength and temperature. It is the absorbance of a 1 mol/L solution in a 1 cm cuvette. Because ε depends on wavelength, the instrument must be set to the same wavelength for standards and samples.

A = ε c l

A is absorbance (no unit); ε is molar absorptivity in L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹; c is concentration in mol/L; l is the light path through the solution in cm.

A blank cuvette sets the reference. Fill it with the solvent or with reagent minus sample, set A = 0. This makes I₀ the light through the blank, so absorbance from the sample's colour alone is measured. Without a blank, the cuvette, solvent and reagent absorbances are wrongly added to the sample.

For routine work, prepare standards of known concentration, read their absorbances, and plot A against c. The line should pass through the origin with slope εl. Read the unknown from the line. The linear range is the concentration band where this line is straight. At high A (roughly above 1.5) stray light and molecular interactions bend the line; at very low A, noise dominates. Dilute and repeat if a sample reads outside the range.

Inside a spectrophotometer

A single-beam spectrophotometer has six parts in a row. The source gives polychromatic light. A tungsten-halogen lamp covers the visible band; a deuterium lamp covers UV. The monochromator, a prism or a diffraction grating, selects a narrow band of wavelengths. The cuvette holds the blank or sample. It is glass or plastic for visible light, but quartz for UV because ordinary glass absorbs UV. The photodetector converts transmitted light into current; a photodiode is common, and a photomultiplier tube (PMT) is used when light is very weak. The amplifier scales the signal, and the readout shows A and %T.

A colorimeter, also called a filter photometer, is the same chain with a coloured glass or interference filter in place of the monochromator. It works at a few fixed wavelengths, is cheaper and simpler, and suits a single routine test. A spectrophotometer can scan or select any wavelength in its range, so it is more flexible. Some syllabi call the whole topic photometry or colorimetry.

  1. Switch on and let the lamp warm up.
  2. Select the wavelength.
  3. Fill the blank cuvette and set A = 0 or %T = 100.
  4. Wipe the sample cuvette and insert it.
  5. Read A or %T. Handle cuvettes by the frosted sides, not the clear faces, and keep the light path free of bubbles and fingerprints.

Clinical uses and fluorescence

In the clinical laboratory, spectrophotometry measures haemoglobin as cyanmethaemoglobin, bilirubin, glucose through an enzyme-produced colour, creatinine through the Jaffe reaction, and total protein by a colour reaction. Each test fixes a wavelength where the product absorbs strongly and the reagents absorb little.

Fluorescence spectroscopy, which some syllabi add, is a related method. The sample is excited at one wavelength and the emitted light is measured at a longer wavelength. The detector is placed at 90° to the exciting beam, so it sees the emitted light against a dark background. This makes fluorescence far more sensitive than absorption, but the sample must contain a fluorescent molecule (a fluorophore), and other substances can quench, that is weaken, the emission.

An automated biochemical analyser uses the same Beer–Lambert law: it pipettes sample and reagent, incubates, reads absorbance in a flow-through or cuvette photometer, and computes concentration. See 3.5 Automated biochemical analysers. A flame photometer also measures light, but it measures emission from excited atoms, not absorption; see 3.2 Flame photometer.

Worked example

Question. A 0.20 mmol/L standard of a coloured compound in a 1.00 cm cuvette reads A = 0.30. (a) Find ε. (b) A patient sample in the same cuvette reads A = 0.45; find its concentration. (c) What is the transmittance of the standard?

  1. Convert the standard concentration to mol/L: c = 0.20 mmol/L = 0.20 × 10⁻³ mol/L = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L.
  2. Find ε from A = ε c l: ε = A / (c l) = 0.30 / (2.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L × 1.00 cm) = 1500 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹.
  3. For the sample: c = A / (ε l) = 0.45 / (1500 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ × 1.00 cm) = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L.
  4. Convert to mmol/L: 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L × 10³ mmol/mol = 0.30 mmol/L.
  5. Find the standard transmittance: T = 10−A = 10−0.30 = 0.50, that is 50 %.

Answer. ε = 1500 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹; the sample concentration is 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L = 0.30 mmol/L; the standard transmittance is 0.50 (50 %).

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the blank: reagent colour and cuvette absorption then add to the sample and give a falsely high absorbance.
  • Mixing up absorbance and transmittance: A is a logarithm, so doubling the concentration doubles A but squares T. From A = 0.10 to 0.20, T goes from 0.79 to 0.63, not to 0.40; from A = 0.30 to 0.60, T goes from 0.50 to 0.25. Both relations hold only inside the linear range.
  • Using a glass cuvette for UV work: glass absorbs UV strongly below about 340 nm, so very little light reaches the detector and the reading becomes noisy and distorted by stray light. If the sample cell is glass while the blank is quartz, the extra absorption is added to the sample and A reads falsely high. Use quartz, or UV-transparent plastic, for both blank and sample in UV work.
  • Assuming the calibration stays straight at high absorbance: above about A = 1.5, stray light bends the line; dilute the sample and repeat.
  • Changing the light path: A = ε c l, so a 0.5 cm cuvette halves A; standards and samples must use the same path length.

Understand Why is absorbance linear in concentration while transmittance is not? 3 marks

Absorbance is defined as a logarithm, so the multiplicative loss of light becomes an additive quantity proportional to the number of absorbing molecules. Transmittance falls exponentially with concentration, so a plot of T against c is a curve.

A = −log₁₀(0.25) = 0.602. The absorbance is 0.602.

Apply A substance has ε = 2000 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹. In a 1.00 cm cuvette its absorbance is 0.60. Find the concentration in mol/L. 3 marks

c = A/(ε l) = 0.60/(2000 × 1.00) = 3.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol/L.

Analyze A sample reads A = 2.0. Why is this value unreliable, and what should the technician do? 4 marks

A = 2.0 is above the linear range; stray light and molecular interactions make the Beer–Lambert plot bend, so concentration read from a calibration line is wrong. Dilute the sample by a known factor, re-read, and multiply the result by that factor.

Analyze What is the purpose of a blank cuvette, and what happens if it is omitted? 4 marks

The blank contains solvent or reagent without sample. It sets I₀ and makes A = 0 for everything except the sample's colour. If omitted, absorbance from the cuvette, solvent and reagent adds to the sample reading and the concentration is overestimated.

Understand State one difference between a colorimeter and a spectrophotometer. 3 marks

A colorimeter uses a filter to select a few fixed wavelengths, while a spectrophotometer uses a prism or diffraction grating and can select or scan many wavelengths.

Apply Inside the linear range, the concentration of a coloured solution is tripled. What happens to A and to T? 3 marks

A becomes three times its old value, because A = εcl. T becomes the cube of its old value, because T = 10−A: for example T = 0.50 (A = 0.30) becomes 10−0.90 = 0.125.

3.2 Flame photometer

A flame photometer measures sodium, potassium, lithium, calcium or barium by the light their atoms emit in a hot flame. A diluted sample is sprayed into the flame. Atoms absorb heat, become excited, and fall back emitting light at fixed wavelengths. A filter selects one wavelength and a photodetector measures the brightness. The method exists because these elements are hard to measure by simple colour reactions and are common in clinical samples: sodium and potassium in serum and urine, and lithium in patients on lithium therapy. It is fast, cheap and needs only a small sample.

You need: 3.1 for the source–monochromator–detector–readout chain and for calibration curves.

Atoms emit their own light

An alkali metal atom in a hot flame takes in thermal energy and reaches an excited state. It stays there for a very short time, of the order of 10 nanoseconds, and then falls back. On falling back it emits light at wavelengths fixed by its electron levels. The wavelength is a fingerprint of the element. Sodium gives a strong yellow line at 589 nm, potassium a line at 766 nm, and lithium a line at 671 nm. The symbol nm means nanometre, 10⁻⁹ m. The brightness of the emitted line depends on how many atoms of that element are in the flame. More atoms give more light. This is emission, not absorption: the instrument does not shine its own light through the sample. Only elements that are easily excited in a flame work well: sodium, potassium, lithium, calcium and barium. Clinical use is mostly sodium and potassium in serum and urine, and lithium for patients on lithium therapy.

From diluted sample to reading

The sample must first be diluted because serum contains about 140 mmol/L sodium and 4 mmol/L potassium, which is too high for the linear range. A dilution of 1:50 to 1:200 is common, made in a diluent that already holds a fixed amount of lithium (the internal standard, below). A nebulizer turns the diluted sample into a fine spray, and only the finest droplets reach the flame.

The flame is air with LPG (propane or butane), at about 1900 °C, or air with acetylene, at about 2200–2300 °C. The cooler LPG flame is enough to excite sodium, potassium and lithium, and it excites few other elements, which keeps interference low. Water evaporates, salts break into atoms, and a small fraction of the atoms become excited.

The light from the flame passes a lens and an interference filter (or a monochromator) that selects one line: 589 nm for sodium, 766 nm for potassium, or 671 nm for lithium. Each line has its own filter and photodetector, so the channels are read together. The potassium line at 766 nm lies at the red edge of the visible band, where many detectors chosen for the yellow sodium line are weak, so that channel needs a red-sensitive detector such as a silicon photodiode. An amplifier raises each signal and the readout displays a number.

R = k Cd

R is the readout in arbitrary units; Cd is the diluted concentration in mmol/L; k is a constant found by calibration.

The chain follows the same source–monochromator–detector–readout idea as 3.1, but the source is the flame itself and no lamp shines through the sample.

Calibration, dilution and errors

Calibration uses a blank (diluent alone) and at least one standard of known concentration, run under the same flame. For a one-point calibration:

Cserum = Cstd × (Rsample / Rstd) × D

Cserum is the original serum concentration in mmol/L; Cstd is the standard concentration in mmol/L, made up at the diluted strength; Rsample and Rstd are the readouts for sample and standard in the same units; D is the dilution factor.

For 1:50 dilution D = 50; for 1:100, D = 100. With an internal standard, the instrument reads ratios instead of single lines: sodium-to-lithium on the sodium channel and potassium-to-lithium on the potassium channel. Lithium is used because healthy serum has almost none of it and its line at 671 nm is clear of the other two. Flame flicker, viscosity differences and small changes in spray rate change all the lines by the same fraction, so each ratio cancels much of the error. The ratio then takes the place of R in the formula. When lithium itself is to be measured, for a patient on lithium therapy, the lithium internal standard cannot be used, and lithium is read directly against lithium standards or with another element as the internal standard.

Common errors are other ions, flame drift, a blocked capillary and too high a concentration. At high concentration, atoms near the cool edge of the flame absorb light from excited atoms, so the curve bends downward; this is self-absorption. A blocked capillary gives a low, noisy signal. An atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AAS) measures light absorbed from a separate hollow-cathode lamp, while a flame photometer measures emitted light. Both need calibration and both can measure sodium and potassium, but the measurement principles are opposite.

Worked example

Question. A serum sample is diluted 1:50 and run in a flame photometer. The readout is 40 units. A 2.0 mmol/L sodium standard gives 32 units under the same flame. Find the sodium concentration in the diluted sample and in the original serum, and say whether the value is normal.

  1. A 1:50 dilution means one part serum is made up to 50 parts with diluent, so the dilution factor is D = 50.
  2. For the same flame, readout is proportional to diluted concentration: Rsample / Rstd = Cd / Cstd.
  3. Cd = Cstd × (Rsample / Rstd) = 2.0 mmol/L × (40 / 32) = 2.0 × 1.25 = 2.5 mmol/L.
  4. The original serum is 50 times more concentrated: Cserum = Cd × D = 2.5 mmol/L × 50 = 125 mmol/L.
  5. The serum sodium reference range is about 135–145 mmol/L, so 125 mmol/L is low (hyponatraemia). A low result is repeated with a fresh dilution before it is reported.

Answer. The diluted sample contains 2.5 mmol/L sodium, so the original serum contains 125 mmol/L, which is below the normal range.

Worked example 2

Question. Serum is diluted 1:50 in a diluent that contains lithium. On the potassium channel the potassium-to-lithium ratio is 0.42. A 0.10 mmol/L potassium standard made in the same diluent gives a ratio of 0.50. (a) Find the serum potassium. (b) The spray rate then falls by 10 %. What happens to the potassium signal and to the ratio?

  1. The ratio takes the place of R in the one-point formula: Cd = Cstd × (ratiosample / ratiostd).
  2. Cd = 0.10 mmol/L × (0.42 / 0.50) = 0.10 × 0.84 = 0.084 mmol/L in the diluted sample.
  3. Cserum = 0.084 mmol/L × 50 = 4.2 mmol/L. The reference range is about 3.5–5.0 mmol/L, so this is normal.
  4. If the spray rate falls by 10 %, fewer atoms of both elements reach the flame, so the potassium signal and the lithium signal both fall to 0.90 of their old values.
  5. The ratio is (0.90 × K signal) / (0.90 × Li signal) = the same 0.42, so the result stays 4.2 mmol/L. Without the internal standard the reading would fall by 10 %, to about 3.8 mmol/L.

Answer. (a) Serum potassium = 4.2 mmol/L. (b) Both signals fall by 10 %, the ratio stays 0.42 and the result stays 4.2 mmol/L; a single-line reading would have dropped to about 3.8 mmol/L.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the dilution factor: 2.5 mmol/L is the diluted concentration; the serum value is 125 mmol/L in the worked example.
  • Calling the measurement absorption, or applying the Beer–Lambert law directly to the emitted flame light. A flame photometer measures emission.
  • Using the wrong filter: sodium is read at 589 nm, potassium at 766 nm, and lithium at 671 nm.
  • Calibrating only at high concentration, where self-absorption bends the curve downward and makes the sample look lower than it is.
  • Ignoring the internal standard and flame drift, then reporting a false potassium result; the potassium channel also reads a ratio to lithium.

3.3 Blood gas analyzers and pH measurement

A blood gas analyser measures three quantities in arterial blood: pH and the partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO₂), which together set the acid–base state, and the partial pressure of oxygen (pO₂), which reports how well the blood is oxygenated. Three electrodes sit in one chamber held at 37 °C. A glass pH electrode, a Severinghaus pCO₂ electrode and a Clark pO₂ electrode produce electrical signals, and the analyser calculates bicarbonate and base excess from them. It is used in emergency rooms, intensive care and operation theatres, where a patient in ketoacidosis or on a ventilator needs the result within minutes.

You need electrode theory and the Nernst equation, and the readout and calibration ideas are close to those in 3.1.

pH and the glass electrode

pH tells how acidic a solution is. It is defined as pH = −log₁₀ a(H⁺), where a(H⁺) is the hydrogen ion activity in mol/L. In dilute blood, activity is close to concentration, so pH ≈ −log₁₀[H⁺]. Arterial blood normally lies between 7.35 and 7.45; below 7.35 is acidaemia and above 7.45 is alkalaemia.

pH = −log₁₀ a(H⁺)

a(H⁺) is the hydrogen ion activity in mol/L; [H⁺] is the hydrogen ion concentration in mol/L.

The measurement is potentiometric. The measuring electrode is a glass bulb with a thin hydrated layer. H⁺ ions exchange at the glass surface and create a potential that depends on pH. The reference electrode is Ag/AgCl in fixed KCl; it gives a constant potential. The analyser measures the voltage between the two electrodes. The glass electrode has very high resistance, so the amplifier must have high input impedance; otherwise the reading loads the cell and falls.

E = E⁰ + (2.303RT/nF) log₁₀ a

E is the electrode potential in volts; E⁰ is the standard potential; R is 8.314 J/(mol·K); T is absolute temperature in K; n is the number of electrons per ion (n = 1 for H⁺); F is 96485 C/mol; a is activity.

For H⁺, the practical form is E = E⁰ − (2.303RT/F) pH. At 37 °C the slope 2.303RT/F is about 61.5 mV per pH unit. One pH unit changes the cell voltage by about 61.5 mV; 0.1 pH unit changes it by about 6.15 mV.

pCO₂, pO₂ and derived values

pCO₂ is the partial pressure of carbon dioxide in arterial blood, in mmHg. The Severinghaus pCO₂ electrode is a pH electrode behind a CO₂-permeable membrane. A thin film of sodium bicarbonate solution sits between the membrane and the glass. CO₂ diffuses in, forms carbonic acid, and lowers the pH of the film. The pH electrode reads that fall; after calibration the signal is displayed as pCO₂.

pO₂ is the partial pressure of oxygen, also in mmHg. The Clark pO₂ electrode is a polarographic cell. A platinum cathode is held at about −0.65 V against an Ag/AgCl anode, and an oxygen-permeable membrane covers the cell. Oxygen crosses the membrane and is reduced at the cathode: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻. The current is proportional to pO₂.

Bicarbonate is calculated, not measured, from the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation. Base excess is the amount of strong acid, in mmol per litre of blood, needed to bring the blood back to pH 7.40 at pCO₂ 40 mmHg and 37 °C; a negative value (a base deficit) means base, not acid, would be needed. Normal is −2 to +2 mmol/L. Total CO₂ = [HCO₃⁻] + 0.03 × pCO₂, in mmol/L.

pH = 6.1 + log₁₀([HCO₃⁻] / (0.03 × pCO₂))

6.1 is the pKa of carbonic acid at 37 °C; [HCO₃⁻] is in mmol/L; pCO₂ is in mmHg; 0.03 is the CO₂ solubility in mmol/(L·mmHg).

Running a sample and reading the pattern

  1. Take arterial blood into a heparinised syringe; the radial artery is common.
  2. Remove air bubbles, cap the syringe and label it.
  3. Run the sample within about 30 minutes; with a longer delay the white cells keep using oxygen, so pO₂ falls and pCO₂ rises.
  4. Calibrate with two gas mixtures of known pCO₂ and pO₂ and with pH buffers; run quality control serum.
  5. Read pH, pCO₂ and pO₂; check the calculated HCO₃⁻ and base excess.

Room air has pO₂ about 150 mmHg and pCO₂ about 0 mmHg. An air bubble therefore raises pO₂ and lowers pCO₂. The chamber holds the sample at 37 °C, so the printed values are values at 37 °C whatever the sample temperature was on arrival. If the patient is colder, for example at 33 °C during cooling after a cardiac arrest, more CO₂ and O₂ stay dissolved in the cooler blood, so the true pCO₂ and pO₂ inside the patient are lower and the true pH is higher than the 37 °C readings. The analyser gives corrected values when the patient temperature is entered. The instrument is often called an ABG (arterial blood gas) analyser.

Worked example

Question. A blood gas analyser reports pH 7.32, pCO₂ 30 mmHg and HCO₃⁻ 15 mmol/L at 37 °C. Check the bicarbonate value with the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation, classify the acid–base state, and check the compensation with Winter's formula.

  1. Use Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH = 6.1 + log₁₀([HCO₃⁻] / (0.03 × pCO₂)).
  2. Substitute [HCO₃⁻] = 15 mmol/L and pCO₂ = 30 mmHg.
  3. 0.03 × 30 = 0.90 mmol/L.
  4. 15 ÷ 0.90 = 16.7 (unitless ratio).
  5. log₁₀ 16.7 = 1.22.
  6. pH = 6.1 + 1.22 = 7.32.
  7. pH 7.32 is below 7.35, so there is acidaemia. HCO₃⁻ 15 mmol/L is below 22 mmol/L, so the cause is metabolic. pCO₂ 30 mmHg is below 35 mmHg, so the lungs are compensating.
  8. Winter's formula gives the pCO₂ expected when the lungs compensate fully for a metabolic acidosis: expected pCO₂ = 1.5 × HCO₃⁻ + 8 = 1.5 × 15 + 8 = 30.5 ± 2 mmHg, that is 28.5 to 32.5 mmHg.
  9. The measured 30 mmHg lies inside that band, so the compensation is appropriate and there is no second, respiratory disorder.

Answer. The calculated pH is 7.32, matching the printout, so the sample shows metabolic acidosis with appropriate respiratory compensation (expected pCO₂ 28.5–32.5 mmHg, measured 30 mmHg).

Exam traps

  • Mixing mmHg and kPa. Normal pCO₂ is 35–45 mmHg, which is about 4.7–6.0 kPa; do not put a kPa number into a formula written for mmHg.
  • Forgetting what the 37 °C means. The analyser warms every sample to 37 °C and reports at 37 °C. For a patient at 33 °C the true pCO₂ and pO₂ are lower and the true pH higher; enter the patient temperature to get the corrected values.
  • Leaving an air bubble in the syringe. Room air raises pO₂ and lowers pCO₂, so the printout can look like hyperoxia with respiratory alkalosis.
  • Treating the printed HCO₃⁻ as a measured value. It is calculated from pH and pCO₂, so an error in pCO₂ moves the HCO₃⁻ value.
  • Reversing the sign of base excess. A positive base excess means metabolic alkalosis; a negative base excess means metabolic acidosis.

Understand Why does a glass pH electrode need an amplifier with very high input impedance? 3 marks

The glass bulb has very high resistance. If the amplifier draws current, the cell voltage is loaded and falls. A high input impedance means almost no current flows, so the measured voltage stays close to the true electrode potential.

Apply Calculate pH from [HCO₃⁻] = 24 mmol/L and pCO₂ = 40 mmHg at 37 °C. 3 marks

pH = 6.1 + log₁₀(24 / (0.03 × 40)). 0.03 × 40 = 1.20 mmol/L. 24 / 1.20 = 20. log₁₀ 20 = 1.30. pH = 6.1 + 1.30 = 7.40.

Analyze A syringe has a small air bubble and the sample is run late. What happens to pO₂, pCO₂ and pH, and why? 4 marks

Room air has pO₂ near 150 mmHg and pCO₂ near 0 mmHg, so the bubble lets oxygen in and carbon dioxide out: pO₂ rises, pCO₂ falls and pH rises. The delay acts the other way: white cells keep using oxygen and making carbon dioxide, so pO₂ falls and pCO₂ rises. The two errors do not cancel in any predictable way, so the result cannot be trusted; take a fresh sample without air and run it promptly.

The bicarbonate layer is a thin pH-sensitive solution. CO₂ crosses the membrane, forms carbonic acid and changes the pH of this layer. The glass pH electrode then reads that pH change, which is calibrated as pCO₂.

ΔpH = 7.40 − 7.20 = 0.20. Voltage change = 61.5 × 0.20 = 12.3 mV. With E = E⁰ − 61.5 × pH, the cell voltage rises by about 12.3 mV.

pH 7.50 is above 7.45, so there is alkalaemia. pCO₂ 30 mmHg is below 35 mmHg, so the primary change is respiratory. HCO₃⁻ 23 mmol/L is within 22–26 mmol/L. This is respiratory alkalosis.

3.4 Blood cell counters

A blood cell does not conduct electricity well. Pull it through a small hole filled with salt solution and it pushes electrolyte out of the way. The resistance across the hole rises for a moment, so a constant current makes a small voltage pulse. Count the pulses and you have the number of cells. Measure their height and you have cell size. This is the Coulter principle. It turned the slow manual blood count into a routine automated test printed on every complete blood count report.

You need: 3.1 for a calibrated readout, and bio-amplifiers for the pulse amplifier.

Blood cells and the manual count

Blood carries three formed elements. Red cells, or erythrocytes, carry oxygen. White cells, or leucocytes, defend the body. Platelets, or thrombocytes, help clotting. Adult reference ranges are wide. Red cells are about 4.5–5.5 × 1012 per litre. White cells are 4–11 × 109 per litre. Platelets are 150–400 × 109 per litre. The five white cell classes are neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and basophils.

Manual counting uses a haemocytometer: a glass slide with a ruled Neubauer grid and a chamber 0.1 mm deep. One large square is 1 mm × 1 mm × 0.1 mm, so its volume is 0.1 µL. Blood is diluted in an isotonic diluent so cells stay round and separate. The diluted sample fills the chamber by capillary action. A technician counts cells under a microscope in a known number of squares.

  1. Dilute blood by a known factor D. For red cells the dilution is 1:200, so D = 200; for white cells it is 1:20, so D = 20, in a fluid that lyses the red cells.
  2. Fill the Neubauer chamber and let cells settle.
  3. Count N cells in a known volume V of diluted sample, in µL.
  4. Use the formula below and report per litre.

cells per litre = (N × D × 106) / V

N is the number of cells counted; D is the dilution factor; V is the counted volume in µL of diluted sample; 106 converts cells per µL to cells per litre.

The Coulter principle

A Coulter counter pulls diluted blood through a single aperture, about 100 µm across, in a glass tube. Two electrodes sit on either side of the aperture. A constant current flows through the electrolyte between them. A cell is a poor conductor compared with the electrolyte. As it enters the aperture it displaces conducting liquid, so resistance rises. The current source holds current constant, so the resistance change appears as a voltage pulse of a few millivolts.

The pulse is amplified and fed to threshold discriminators. In the red cell channel, where whole blood is diluted about 1:50 000, pulses equal to cell volumes of about 2–20 fL (1 fL = 10−15 L) are counted as platelets and larger pulses, from about 36 fL upward, as red cells. White cells are counted on a separate portion of the sample, diluted about 1:500 in a reagent that lyses the red cells, because whole blood holds about a thousand red cells for every white cell. The thresholds are set with calibration particles of known size. A scaler counts the pulses in each band, and the same pulses feed a size histogram: pulse height follows cell volume, so the histogram shows the spread of cell sizes.

ΔV = I × ΔR

ΔV is the pulse height in volts; I is the constant current in amperes; ΔR is the rise in resistance in ohms when a cell displaces electrolyte.

Two cells passing together make one pulse, not two. This coincidence loss makes a concentrated sample read low. Diluting the red cell channel about 1:50 000 keeps the chance small. The aperture must also be clean; a partial clog changes the pulse heights and adds noise pulses.

Automatic counters and the white cell differential

A modern analyser combines impedance with flow cytometry. The diluted sample is forced through a narrow stream so cells pass one at a time through a laser beam. Forward scatter at a small angle gives cell size. Side scatter at 90° gives internal granularity. Fluorescence from a stained cell gives a chemical label. A detector array records all three for each cell.

A three-part differential groups white cells into lymphocytes, monocytes and granulocytes. A five-part differential reports neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, eosinophils and basophils. Impedance alone separates by size only, so eosinophils overlap neutrophils. Optical scatter and cytochemical or fluorescent staining resolve them. The analyser plots a scattergram and flags abnormal populations for a manual film review.

Semi-automatic counters need the operator to dilute the sample and press a button. Fully automatic counters aspirate blood, split it into a red cell and platelet dilution and a separate lysed white cell dilution, count both, and print a complete blood count. They also measure haemoglobin by photometry, as in 3.1. Results are reported per litre, with reference ranges by age and sex.

Worked example

Question. A Coulter counter meters 0.50 mL of each dilution through its aperture. The red cell channel, diluted 1:50 000, gives 48 000 pulses above the red cell threshold. The white cell channel, diluted 1:500 with the red cells lysed, gives 7000 pulses. Find the red and white cell counts per litre and say whether they are normal.

  1. Use cells per litre = (N / V) × D × 1000, with V in mL: N / V is cells per mL of dilution, × D gives cells per mL of blood, × 1000 gives cells per litre.
  2. Red cells: N / V = 48 000 / 0.50 mL = 96 000 per mL of dilution.
  3. Per mL of blood: 96 000 × 50 000 = 4.8 × 109 per mL; per litre: 4.8 × 109 × 1000 = 4.8 × 1012 per litre.
  4. White cells: N / V = 7000 / 0.50 mL = 14 000 per mL of dilution.
  5. Per mL of blood: 14 000 × 500 = 7.0 × 106 per mL; per litre: 7.0 × 106 × 1000 = 7.0 × 109 per litre.
  6. Compare with the reference ranges: red cells 4.5–5.5 × 1012 per litre and white cells 4–11 × 109 per litre. Both counts are normal.

Answer. Red cells 4.8 × 1012 per litre (4.8 million per µL) and white cells 7.0 × 109 per litre (7000 per µL); both are within the reference ranges.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the dilution factor D. Multiply by it once; the diluted count is not the blood count.
  • Using the blood volume instead of the diluted volume in the formula. V is the volume actually counted in the chamber or aperture.
  • Reporting per millilitre when the report asks per litre. Multiply per mL by 1000.
  • Trusting a count from a partly clogged aperture. Coincidence biases counts low; debris in a partial clog adds noise pulses and changes pulse heights, so the analyser watches the aperture current and the time taken to meter the volume.
  • Setting thresholds by eye. Platelets and small red cells overlap; set thresholds with calibration particles.

3.5 Automated biochemical analysers

An automated biochemical analyser pipettes sample and reagent, incubates at 37 °C, reads absorbance and computes concentration for hundreds of tests an hour. A hospital laboratory may receive 500 samples before noon, each needing several tests; a bench cuvette cannot keep up. The chemistry is still the Beer–Lambert law; only pipetting, timing and washing are mechanised.

You need: Read Chapter 3.1 for the Beer–Lambert law, the cuvette photometer and calibration.

The fluid path and the blocks

A sample probe dips into the tube, senses liquid level and aspirates 2–20 µL of serum. A second probe adds 200–300 µL of reagent. Capacitive sensing: the probe tip is one plate of a capacitor, and capacitance changes when the tip touches serum. Resistive sensing: the probe and grounded tube form a circuit whose conductance changes when the tip enters conducting serum. Either change stops the downward drive and flags a short sample.

Two pumps move liquids. A peristaltic pump squeezes a flexible tube with rollers; it is cheap but less precise. A syringe pump drives a piston and delivers microlitre volumes accurately.

The mixture enters a cuvette in a wheel held at 37 °C for a fixed incubation time. Light from the lamp passes through the filter wheel, which selects the wavelength — common ones are 340, 405, 505, 546, 578 and 600 nm — then through the cuvette. A photodiode after the cuvette measures transmitted light. A wash station aspirates the used mixture and rinses the cuvette.

Continuous flow and discrete analysers

Continuous-flow or air-segmented analysers push sample and reagent as one stream broken by air bubbles. The bubbles scrub the tube wall and keep samples apart as the stream passes one detector. Every sample gets the same timing. Very few remain installed.

Discrete analysers give each test its own cuvette. In a random-access analyser any test can be ordered on any sample at any time, and an emergency sample can jump the queue. A batch analyser runs one test on a long row of samples and is cheaper for one high-volume assay.

Throughput is quoted in tests per hour. 600 tests per hour means one result every 3600 s ÷ 600 = 6 s on average. This is cycle time, not the incubation time of one test, which is longer and overlaps other tests. A probe washed between samples still carries a trace of previous reagent, so reagent carryover adds a small false rise to the next result. Daily quality control uses control sera of known value on a chart with ±2 SD limits.

Reading the result: end point, kinetic, bichromatic

A reagent starts the reaction and absorbance changes. In an end-point assay the reaction runs to completion and the analyser reads the final absorbance once. The computer converts it with a stored calibration curve:

c(sample) = c(std) × (A(sample) ÷ A(std))

c(sample) is the unknown concentration in mmol/L; c(std) is the calibrator value in mmol/L; A(sample) and A(std) are net absorbances, meaning measured absorbance minus reagent blank and cuvette blank: A = A(measured) − A(blank).

In a kinetic assay the analyser takes several readings while the reaction runs at a steady rate and computes ΔA/min, the change in absorbance per minute. Enzyme tests are usually read as a rate, because activity is proportional to ΔA/min; some fixed-time methods read one absorbance after a set incubation, but a true rate method cannot be read as an end point. Many enzyme tests follow NADH at 340 nm, where its molar absorptivity is 6220 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹; one unit (U) of enzyme converts 1 µmol of substrate per minute.

activity (U/L) = (ΔA/min) × Vt × 106 / (ε × l × Vs)

ΔA/min is the size of the absorbance change per minute; Vt is the total volume in the cuvette and Vs the sample volume, in the same units; ε is in L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹; l is the path in cm; 106 turns mol into µmol.

Bichromatic measurement reads the cuvette at the main wavelength and again at a reference wavelength, then subtracts. Turbidity such as lipaemia, and a scratched or dirty cuvette, add nearly the same absorbance at both wavelengths, so the subtraction removes most of that. Haemolysis is not cancelled this way: free haemoglobin absorbs differently at the two wavelengths. Modern analysers measure a haemolysis index and flag the sample.

Worked example

Question. An end-point glucose assay at 505 nm uses a 1 cm cuvette. A 5.0 mmol/L calibrator gives net A = 0.28; the sample gives net A = 0.42. Find glucose and the average interval between results at 600 tests per hour.

  1. Use c(sample) = c(std) × (A(sample) ÷ A(std)); A means net absorbance, after the reagent blank is subtracted.
  2. c = 5.0 mmol/L × (0.42 ÷ 0.28) = 5.0 × 1.50 = 7.5 mmol/L.
  3. Average interval = 3600 s ÷ 600 = 6 s. This is the rate at which results come out, not the time one test takes; each test incubates for minutes while others run beside it.

Answer. Glucose is 7.5 mmol/L; results come out every 6 s on average.

Worked example 2

Question. An ALT test follows NADH at 340 nm in a 1 cm cuvette. The analyser mixes 20 µL of serum with 200 µL of reagent and reads A at 1, 2 and 3 minutes: 1.250, 1.200 and 1.150. Find the enzyme activity in U/L.

  1. The readings fall by 0.050 each minute, so ΔA/min = 0.050. The fall is steady, so the reaction is in its linear phase and the rate can be used.
  2. Total volume Vt = 20 + 200 = 220 µL; sample volume Vs = 20 µL; Vt / Vs = 11.
  3. Activity = 0.050 × 11 × 106 / (6220 × 1) = 550 000 / 6220 = 88.4 U/L.
  4. Check the units: (1/min) ÷ (L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ × cm) = mol L⁻¹ min⁻¹; × 106 gives µmol L⁻¹ min⁻¹, which is U/L.

Answer. ALT activity is about 88 U/L, roughly twice the upper reference limit of about 40 U/L.

Worked example 2

Question. To measure sample carryover, a high glucose sample is run three times (H1 = 30.0, H2 = 30.2, H3 = 29.8 mmol/L) and then a low sample three times (L1 = 5.30, L2 = 5.12, L3 = 5.10 mmol/L). Find the carryover.

  1. L3 is the low result once any carryover has been washed out; H3 is the high result.
  2. Carryover = (L1 − L3) / (H3 − L3) × 100 %.
  3. L1 − L3 = 5.30 − 5.10 = 0.20 mmol/L; H3 − L3 = 29.8 − 5.10 = 24.7 mmol/L.
  4. Carryover = 0.20 / 24.7 × 100 % = 0.81 %.

Answer. The carryover is 0.81 %: a low sample run straight after a very high one reads about 0.2 mmol/L high. The laboratory compares this with its own limit.

Exam traps

  • Using single-point calibration outside the linear range: the curve flattens, and a straight-line ratio under-reads the true concentration.
  • Reading a kinetic assay as an end point: absorbance is still changing; enzyme tests need ΔA/min.
  • Forgetting carryover: a trace of a very high sample left on the probe raises the next result. A reagent blank does not correct it; thorough washing does, and it is measured with a high-then-low run (worked example 3).
  • Mixing results from analysers with different wavelengths or path lengths: the same concentration gives a different absorbance.

3.6 Chromatography and electrophoresis

Chromatography and electrophoresis both separate the molecules of a mixture. Chromatography uses a moving mobile phase and a stationary phase; each solute divides between them differently and so travels at its own speed. Electrophoresis uses an electric field: a charged molecule feels a force qE and drifts at a speed set by its charge and friction. Clinical labs use these methods for glycated haemoglobin, drug levels, urine steroids, amino acids, serum proteins, haemoglobin variants and DNA.

You need: 3.1 for UV absorbance and the densitometer, and 3.7 for a clear serum before the run.

Chromatography: moving and stationary phases

A mixture is placed at the start of a column or sheet. The mobile phase is a gas or liquid that flows and carries solutes. The stationary phase is a solid or liquid film that does not move. Each solute divides between the phases. A solute that spends more time in the stationary phase is retarded more and leaves later. Separation is by partition (dissolves in both phases) or adsorption (sticks to the solid).

In column methods (gas chromatography and HPLC) a peak is described by its retention time tR, the time from injection to the peak maximum; under fixed conditions each compound has its own tR. On a plate or a paper sheet there is no injection: the solvent rises for a fixed time, and each spot is described by its retardation factor Rf. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) uses a coated plate and a rising solvent; paper chromatography uses water held in paper fibres; column chromatography packs the stationary phase in a tube.

Rf = distance moved by the spot ÷ distance moved by the solvent front

both distances are measured from the starting line, in the same units; Rf has no unit and lies between 0 and 1.

Gas chromatography (GC) uses a carrier gas and a heated column; a flame ionisation detector burns organic molecules and measures ion current. HPLC uses small packed particles, so liquid is pumped at high pressure; a UV detector is common, at 280 nm for proteins and at about 415 nm for haemoglobin in glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) tests. Clinical uses: glycated haemoglobin, drug levels, urine steroids, amino acids.

Electrophoresis: speed set by charge and friction

An electrophoresis tank applies an electric field E (V/m) across a buffer-soaked support. A molecule with charge q (C) feels force qE. It accelerates until friction balances that force, then drifts at speed v (m/s).

v = μE = qE / f

v is drift speed (m/s); μ is mobility (m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹); E is field strength (V/m); q is charge (C); f is the friction factor (N·s/m).

The sign of the charge follows buffer pH relative to the protein's isoelectric point pI. At pH 8.6 serum proteins are net negative and migrate to the anode. Mobility depends on charge, size and shape.

Paper and cellulose acetate carry serum proteins and haemoglobin variants. Agarose gel is used for DNA, negative from phosphate groups. In SDS-PAGE the detergent SDS binds to the proteins, masks their own charge and makes them all negative, so they separate by molecular weight: smaller moves faster. Isoelectric focusing uses a pH gradient; a protein stops at its pI, where net charge is zero. Capillary electrophoresis uses a narrow tube and high voltage.

Serum protein electrophoresis on cellulose acetate at pH 8.6 gives albumin, α₁, α₂, β and γ bands. Albumin is fastest and most abundant. A densitometer scans the stained strip and reports each band as a percentage of total protein.

3.7 Centrifugation

A centrifuge spins tubes around a spindle. Particles denser than the liquid move outward and settle as a pellet; the liquid left above is the supernatant. Particles lighter than the liquid, such as fat droplets, move inward and float. The outward force is not set by rpm alone. It grows with the square of the speed and with the radius, and the same tube sits at a different radius in a different rotor, so the same rpm gives a different force. Laboratories use this to get serum or plasma from blood, urine sediment for microscopy, and separated blood components for transfusion.

You need: drag on a particle moving through a fluid, and the blood fractions from 3.4.

Force, radius and speed

Every laboratory protocol needs this calculation. A particle in a spinning tube is pushed outward by a centrifugal field. The field is quoted as relative centrifugal force (RCF): the number of times it is stronger than gravity, g = 9.81 m/s². RCF depends on the radius r, measured in cm from the spindle to the tube bottom, and on the rotor speed in revolutions per minute (rpm).

RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × r × (rpm)²

r is the radius from spindle to tube bottom in cm; rpm is the rotor speed; RCF is a multiple of g.

In SI units RCF = ω²r/g, where ω is angular speed in rad/s and r is in m. Convert rpm to ω with ω = 2π(rpm)/60. Doubling rpm gives four times RCF.

A particle in the tube moves outward until the drag of the liquid balances the push of the field. Stokes' law then gives its settling speed. The sedimentation coefficient s is the speed per unit field, s = v/(ω²r) = d²Δρ/(18η). It is quoted in Svedberg units, 1 S = 10⁻¹³ s. Denser or larger particles have larger s and pellet first; a particle with Δρ below zero moves inward.

v = d² Δρ ω² r / (18 η)

v is the settling speed in m/s; d is the particle diameter in m; Δρ is particle density minus liquid density in kg/m³; ω is the angular speed in rad/s; r is the radius in m; η is the liquid viscosity in Pa·s. Under gravity alone, ω²r is replaced by g.

Rotors and routine use

  • Fixed-angle rotor: tubes sit at a slant; the pellet collects on the outer wall. Good for high-speed spins.
  • Swing-out or horizontal rotor: buckets swing flat; the pellet collects at the tube bottom and is easy to see.
  • Bench and microcentrifuge: small volumes; refrigerated versions remove motor heat.
  • High-speed and ultracentrifuge: higher RCF; an ultracentrifuge spins under vacuum to reduce heating.
  • Preparative centrifugation collects a pellet. Analytical centrifugation measures the moving boundary during the spin.
  • Density-gradient separation: Ficoll-Hypaque layers lymphocytes at an interface.

For serum, clotted blood is spun at about 1000–2000 × g for 10 min; on a rotor of 15 cm radius that is about 2400–3500 rpm (worked example 2). Anticoagulated blood spun the same way gives plasma. Urine is spun briefly and the sediment is examined under the microscope. Blood banks separate red cells, platelets and plasma.

  • Balance opposite tubes by weight; imbalance at high speed damages the rotor.
  • Close and lock the lid; never open while the rotor turns.
  • Use sealed cups and check tubes for cracks; a broken tube makes an aerosol.

Worked example

Question. A tube is held at a radius of 10 cm in a rotor spinning at 4000 rpm. Find the relative centrifugal force. What is the RCF at 8000 rpm?

  1. Write the formula: RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × r × (rpm)².
  2. Put r = 10 cm and rpm = 4000: RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 × (4000)².
  3. (4000)² = 1.6 × 10⁷.
  4. RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 × 1.6 × 10⁷ = 1788.8.
  5. At 8000 rpm, (8000)² = 6.4 × 10⁷, so RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × 10 × 6.4 × 10⁷ = 7155.2.

Answer. The RCF is 1788.8 × g at 4000 rpm and 7155.2 × g at 8000 rpm.

Worked example 2

Question. A serum protocol asks for 1500 × g. (a) What speed is needed on a swing-out rotor with r = 15 cm? (b) The same tubes are put in a small rotor with r = 7.5 cm at that speed. What RCF do they get?

  2. 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × 15 = 1.677 × 10⁻⁴; 1500 / 1.677 × 10⁻⁴ = 8.94 × 10⁶.

Answer. (a) About 3000 rpm (2990 rpm) on the 15 cm rotor. (b) Only 750 × g on the 7.5 cm rotor, so the protocol must be set by RCF, not by rpm.

Worked example 2

Question. Treat a red cell as a sphere of diameter 8 µm and density 1100 kg/m³ in plasma of density 1025 kg/m³ and viscosity 1.2 × 10⁻³ Pa·s. Find its settling speed (a) under gravity and (b) at 1500 × g.

  2. (a) Under gravity: v = d²Δρg/(18η) = 6.4 × 10⁻¹¹ × 75 × 9.81 / (18 × 1.2 × 10⁻³) = 4.71 × 10⁻⁸ / 0.0216 = 2.18 × 10⁻⁶ m/s.

3.8 Radioactivity measurement: GM counter and scintillation

Some patients need a radioactivity measurement: a thyroid uptake study, a hormone assay, a sample in a hot lab. Ionising radiation is invisible, so we detect what it does — it knocks electrons off atoms. In a gas it makes ion pairs, and the Geiger–Müller tube turns each event into one large pulse. In a crystal it makes a flash of light, and a photomultiplier turns that flash into a pulse whose height follows the radiation energy.

You need the photodetector and photomultiplier idea from 3.1.

Radiation units and the Geiger–Müller tube

Activity is decays per second, in becquerel (Bq): 1 Bq = 1 decay per second. The older curie (Ci) is 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq. Absorbed dose is energy deposited per kilogram of the material absorbing it, in gray (Gy): 1 Gy = 1 J/kg. Equivalent dose is absorbed dose multiplied by a radiation weighting factor for the harm each radiation type does, in sievert (Sv), which is also 1 J/kg; the factor is 1 for gamma rays and beta particles and 20 for alpha particles, so for gamma rays 1 Gy gives 1 Sv.

The GM tube is a metal cylinder with a thin window, filled with argon at low pressure plus a little halogen or alcohol vapour, the quench gas. A fine axial wire is the anode at a few hundred volts positive; the wall is the cathode.

A gamma ray or beta particle ionises argon. Electrons drift to the wire, gain energy in the steep field near it and ionise more argon, so an avalanche builds and the tube discharges briefly. One event gives one pulse of about 1 V, whatever energy the radiation carried.

The quench gas absorbs the discharge photons and stops the avalanche, so the tube recovers. Count rate against voltage is flat over the Geiger plateau, usually 350–500 V, and the tube runs mid-plateau. Every pulse is the same size, so the tube counts radiation but cannot name the isotope. After each pulse the tube is blind for a dead time τ of 100–200 µs, so some events are lost at high count rates. The true rate is found from the observed rate:

n = m / (1 − mτ)

n is the true count rate and m the observed count rate, in counts per second; τ is the dead time in s; mτ is the fraction of time the tube is dead.

Scintillation counter and the gamma spectrum

A thallium-activated sodium iodide crystal, NaI(Tl), absorbs a gamma ray and emits a light flash whose brightness follows the energy deposited. A photomultiplier tube (PMT) has a photocathode that turns the flash into electrons and a dynode chain that multiplies them about a million times, giving an electrical pulse.

Pulse height follows gamma energy. A multichannel analyser sorts pulses by height into channels and plots counts against energy: the gamma spectrum. The photopeak position identifies the isotope, which the GM tube cannot do.

Tritium and carbon-14 beta particles are too weak to reach a crystal, so the sample is dissolved in a liquid scintillation cocktail and flashes are counted from inside. Clinical uses include radioimmunoassay with iodine-125 and thyroid uptake studies.

Safety is lead shielding, short time near the source and distance: the dose rate from a small source falls as 1/(distance)², so doubling the distance cuts it to a quarter. Staff wear film or thermoluminescent badges to record dose.

Worked example

Question. A Geiger–Müller counter gives 12 000 counts in 5.0 min with a source present and 200 counts in 5.0 min with the source removed. The counting efficiency is 20 %. Find the net count rate and the activity of the source.

  1. Both counts are taken over the same time: t = 5.0 min = 300 s.
  2. Gross count rate = 12 000 / 300 s = 40.0 counts per second; background rate = 200 / 300 s = 0.67 counts per second.
  3. Net counts = 12 000 − 200 = 11 800, so net count rate = 11 800 / 300 s = 39.33 counts per second.
  4. Activity A = net count rate / efficiency = 39.33 / 0.20 = 196.67 Bq.

Answer. The net count rate is about 39.3 counts per second and the source activity is about 197 Bq.

Worked example 2

Question. A GM counter with a dead time of 200 µs records 1000 counts per second from a sample. Find the true count rate and the fraction of events lost.

  1. m = 1000 s⁻¹ and τ = 200 µs = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ s.
  2. mτ = 1000 × 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.20, so the tube is dead for 20 % of each second.
  3. n = m / (1 − mτ) = 1000 / (1 − 0.20) = 1000 / 0.80 = 1250 counts per second.
  4. Lost events = 1250 − 1000 = 250 per second, which is 250 / 1250 = 20 % of the true rate.

Answer. The true rate is 1250 counts per second; 20 % of the events are lost in the dead time.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting to subtract the background count, or reporting raw counts instead of counts per second.
  • Claiming the GM pulse height tells you the radiation energy — it does not; energy information comes from detectors whose pulse height follows deposited energy, such as the scintillation counter, proportional counter or a semiconductor detector.
  • Mixing the units: curie is activity (1 Ci = 3.7 × 10¹⁰ Bq), gray is absorbed dose, sievert is equivalent dose.
  • Treating dead time as microseconds of no consequence: at 5000 counts per second with 200 µs dead time the tube is effectively saturated.
  • Setting the GM voltage anywhere on the plateau without checking it is a flat region — below it counts are lost, above it the tube discharges continuously.

Analyze Why is the GM tube operated in the middle of the plateau and not at its lower edge? 5 marks

Mid-plateau a small drift in the high-voltage supply barely changes the count rate. At the lower edge the avalanches are incomplete, so counts depend strongly on voltage; above the plateau the tube discharges continuously and counts falsely.

Analyze A source gives about 5000 counts per second and the tube dead time is 200 µs. What goes wrong? 4 marks

Dead-time fraction = observed rate × dead time = 5000 s⁻¹ × 200 × 10⁻⁶ s = 1.0. The tube can never deliver more than 1/τ = 5000 counts per second, so at this rate it is saturated: it is dead between pulses and the true rate is much higher than 5000 per second. Dilute the sample or apply a dead-time correction.

Takeaways

  1. The Beer–Lambert law A = εcl makes absorbance, not transmittance, proportional to concentration; a blank sets A = 0 and a standard read at the same wavelength and path gives the unknown by ratio.
  2. A flame photometer measures light emitted by sodium, potassium and lithium atoms in a flame; serum is diluted 1:50 to 1:200 in a lithium diluent, and the Na/Li and K/Li ratios cancel flame and spray changes.
  3. A blood gas analyser holds a glass pH electrode, a Severinghaus pCO₂ electrode and a Clark pO₂ electrode at 37 °C; pH and pCO₂ set the acid–base state, pO₂ reports oxygenation.
  4. Bicarbonate and base excess are calculated from pH and pCO₂ with the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation; Winter's formula checks whether the breathing has compensated a metabolic acidosis.
  5. A Coulter counter counts resistance pulses as cells pass a 100 µm aperture and sizes them by pulse height; red cells and platelets are counted at about 1:50 000, white cells on a separate lysed dilution near 1:500.
  6. An automated analyser repeats photometry hundreds of times an hour: end-point tests are read by ratio to a calibrator, enzyme tests as ΔA/min, and carryover and bichromatic correction are checked on purpose.
  7. Chromatography separates by partition between a mobile and a stationary phase (retention time on columns, Rf on plates); electrophoresis separates charged molecules by mobility μ = v/E.
  8. Centrifugal force grows with radius and with the square of speed, RCF = 1.118 × 10⁻⁵ × r × (rpm)², so protocols are set in × g, not rpm.
  9. A Geiger–Müller tube gives one equal pulse per event and loses counts in its dead time; a scintillation counter gives pulses whose height follows energy, so it can identify an isotope.

Model answer.

  1. Statement. When monochromatic light passes through a dilute solution, the absorbance is proportional to the concentration of the absorbing substance and to the length of the light path: A = εcl.
  2. Terms.

  3. Where it comes from. Each thin slice of solution removes the same fraction of the light that reaches it, and that fraction is proportional to the number of molecules in the slice. Losing a fixed fraction per slice makes the intensity fall exponentially with path and with concentration: I = I₀ × 10−εcl. Taking the logarithm turns this into the straight line A = εcl.
  4. Transmittance. T = I/I₀ is the fraction of light that gets through, so T = 10−A. T is not linear in concentration: doubling c doubles A but squares T. For A = 0.30, T = 0.50; for A = 0.60, T = 0.25, not 0.00.
  5. Why absorbance is used. (1) A plot of A against c is a straight line through the origin with slope εl, so one standard and a blank are enough for a ratio calculation. (2) Equal steps in concentration give equal steps in A at any level, so errors are easy to read. Monochromatic light, because ε changes with wavelength; a dilute, clear solution, because at high concentration molecules interact and above about A = 1.5 stray light bends the line; no scattering or fluorescence; the same cuvette path and temperature for standard and sample.

Understand Draw and explain the construction and working of a flame photometer for estimating sodium and potassium in serum. 13 marks

Points to cover. Label nebulizer, burner, flame, monochromator or filter, detector, and readout; Explain atomisation, excitation, and emission at fixed wavelengths; Describe calibration with standards and use of lithium internal standard; Mention interference from other alkali metals and flame instability.

Model answer.

  1. Principle. Atoms of sodium, potassium and lithium in a hot flame absorb heat, rise to an excited state and fall back within about 10 ns, emitting light at wavelengths fixed by their electron levels: 589 nm (Na), 766 nm (K) and 671 nm (Li). The brightness of each line is proportional to the number of atoms of that element in the flame, and so to its concentration in the sprayed solution.
  2. Block diagram (draw left to right).
    diluted serum ─> nebulizer ─> burner + flame ─> lens ─> filter 589 / 766 / 671 nm ─> photodetector ─> amplifier ─> readout
                  ^      ^

  3. Sample preparation. Serum holds about 140 mmol/L sodium and 4 mmol/L potassium, far above the linear range, so it is diluted 1:50 to 1:200 in a diluent that already contains a fixed amount of lithium.
  4. Nebulizer and burner. Compressed air draws the diluted sample up a fine capillary and breaks it into a spray; large drops fall to a drain and only the fine mist is carried with the fuel gas into the burner. Fuel is LPG (propane or butane) with air, giving about 1900 °C; air–acetylene gives about 2200–2300 °C. The cooler flame excites the alkali metals but few other elements.
  5. Atomisation and excitation. In the flame the water evaporates, the salt particles melt and vaporise, and the molecules split into free atoms. A small, steady fraction of these atoms is excited at any instant, and their emission is the signal.

Understand Explain the Coulter principle and how a blood cell counter obtains both cell count and cell size. 12 marks

The record of detector signal versus time from a chromatographic separation.
Coincidence error
Counting error in a cell counter when two cells pass the aperture together and are counted as one.
Colorimetry
Measurement of colour intensity to estimate the concentration of a coloured substance.
Cuvette
A transparent cell that holds the sample in a photometer.
Electrophoretic mobility
Migration velocity of a charged molecule per unit electric field.
Henderson–Hasselbalch equation
pH = 6.1 + log([HCO₃⁻]/(0.03 × pCO₂)) for blood bicarbonate, with pCO₂ in mmHg.
Internal standard
A fixed amount of a substance added to samples and standards to correct for instrument variation.
Mobile phase
The moving liquid or gas that carries solutes through a chromatographic system.
Monochromator
A device that selects a narrow band of wavelengths from a light source.
Nebulizer
A device that turns a liquid sample into a fine spray for introduction into a flame.
Pellet
The sediment packed at the bottom of a centrifuge tube.
pH
The negative logarithm of hydrogen ion activity, used to express acidity or alkalinity.
Photodetector
A device that converts light intensity into an electrical signal.
Photomultiplier tube
A sensitive light detector that converts weak light flashes into measurable electrical pulses.
Relative centrifugal force
Centrifugal force expressed as a multiple of gravitational acceleration g.
Scintillator
A material that emits light when ionising radiation deposits energy in it.
Stationary phase
The fixed phase in chromatography through which the mobile phase moves.
Supernatant
The liquid left above the sediment after centrifugation.
