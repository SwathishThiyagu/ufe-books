4.1 Biosensor concept, components and classification

A biosensor is an analytical device in which a biological recognition layer sits in close contact with a transducer. The bioreceptor binds or reacts with one analyte; the transducer converts that event into current, voltage, light or mass change; electronics amplify, filter and display a number. It exists because a plain pH electrode cannot pick glucose out of blood. It is used in glucose strips, pregnancy tests, DNA probes, food checks and water monitoring. No reading is trusted until the device is calibrated.

You need: school chemistry, Ohm's law, and the basics of electrode theory and bio-amplifiers.

The biosensor chain

A biosensor is an analytical device. A biological recognition element, or bioreceptor, sits in close contact with a transducer. The bioreceptor is selective: an enzyme catalyses one reaction, an antibody binds one antigen, a DNA probe pairs with one sequence. The transducer converts binding or reaction into current, voltage, light or mass change that rises with analyte concentration: in proportion for an amperometric sensor, with the logarithm for a potentiometric one. The analyte is the substance measured, such as glucose in blood.

A plain chemical sensor is not a biosensor. A glass pH electrode or ion-selective electrode has no biological layer. Its selectivity comes from a glass or ion-selective membrane, not from a biomolecule. A pH electrode responds to H⁺, and an ion-selective electrode to the ion its membrane selects; neither can pick glucose out from other sugars.

The chain is analyte → bioreceptor → transducer → signal conditioning → display. Signal conditioning means amplification and filtering. Display reads mg/dL or mmol/L. The bioreceptor must touch the transducer, or product diffuses away and signal falls. The transducer may be an electrode, field-effect transistor, optical fibre, piezoelectric crystal or thermistor.

Classification and generations

Two classifications matter. First, by bioreceptor: enzyme (catalyses a reaction), antibody (binds antigen), nucleic acid (DNA or RNA probe), cell (whole cell or tissue), and aptamer (short synthetic nucleic acid). Some syllabi call antibodies, aptamers and DNA probes affinity elements. See bio-recognition elements.

Second, by transducer: amperometric (current at fixed potential), potentiometric (voltage at near-zero current), conductometric (conductance), optical (absorbance or fluorescence), piezoelectric (mass shifts frequency) and calorimetric (heat). Each has different electronics; see electrochemical transducers and ISFET.

Enzyme biosensors come in three generations. The first uses a natural acceptor, usually oxygen, and measures oxygen used or hydrogen peroxide made. The second uses a small redox mediator, a molecule that shuttles electrons between enzyme and electrode; it lowers operating potential and interference. The third uses direct electron transfer from enzyme to electrode, with no mediator; only some enzymes allow this.

Calibration, characteristics and standard addition

Desired characteristics: reliability (same sample, same answer), simplicity, cost in rupees per test, sensitivity, selectivity, dynamic range (lowest to highest usable concentration), response time (time to 90% of final reading), drift, and operating conditions such as pH, temperature and ionic strength. Sensitivity is the slope of the calibration curve: signal per unit concentration for a linear sensor (µA per mmol/L), or signal per tenfold change in concentration for a potentiometric sensor (mV per decade). Selectivity is response to the analyte over similar molecules. Drift is slow output change at fixed sample.

A calibration curve plots signal against known concentration. Fit signal = S c + b, where S is slope, c concentration, b intercept. Blank noise σ is the standard deviation of repeated blank readings. Signal-to-background ratio is analyte signal divided by blank signal; higher is better. A positive control has known analyte; a negative control has matrix with no analyte. Handle blood and serum as infectious: gloves, no mouth pipetting, sharps bin.

LOD = 3σ/S

LOD is detection limit in concentration units; σ is standard deviation of blank readings in signal units; S is sensitivity, the calibration slope in signal units per concentration unit.

Matrix is everything except the analyte; it can suppress or enhance signal. Standard addition handles this: measure sample, add a known small volume of standard, measure again, and use the increase. Correct for the added volume, because it dilutes the original. Applications include medicine (glucose strips, DNA probes), agriculture (pesticides, soil nitrate), bioproduction (fermentation) and environment (water checks). The market is dominated by disposable glucose strips, a few tens of rupees each. Complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) arrays put many sensors on one chip and read many analytes at once; cross-talk and calibration are the limits. See blood glucose sensors.

Worked example

Question. A 5.00 mL diluted serum sample gives a current of 4.0 µA. You add 0.100 mL of a 100 mmol/L glucose standard and the current rises to 7.0 µA. Find the glucose concentration in the cell, the sensitivity, and the original undiluted concentration in mmol/L and mg/dL. The serum was diluted 1:4 with diluent (one part serum plus four parts diluent), a dilution factor of 5.

  1. Spike: 0.100 mL × 100 mmol/L = 0.0100 mmol = 10.0 µmol.
  2. Let c be sample concentration in the cell in mmol/L and k the sensitivity in µA per mmol/L. Before the spike: kc = 4.0 µA.
  3. After the spike, volume = 5.00 + 0.100 = 5.10 mL. Total analyte = 5.00c + 10.0 µmol, so cell concentration = (5c + 10)/5.10 mmol/L. Then k(5c + 10)/5.10 = 7.0 µA.
  4. Divide by kc = 4.0: (5c + 10)/(5.10c) = 1.75. Expand: 5c + 10 = 8.925c. c = 10/(8.925 − 5) = 2.5478 mmol/L ≈ 2.55 mmol/L.
  5. Sensitivity: k = 4.0/2.5478 = 1.570 µA per (mmol/L) ≈ 1.57.
  6. Dilution factor 5: original = 5 × 2.5478 = 12.739 mmol/L ≈ 12.74 mmol/L. In mg/dL: 12.739 × 18 = 229.3 mg/dL, about 229 mg/dL.

Answer. Cell glucose = 2.55 mmol/L; sensitivity = 1.57 µA per (mmol/L); original serum = 12.74 mmol/L = 229.3 mg/dL (about 229 mg/dL), a diabetic value.

Worked example 2

Question. Five readings of the blank in the same cell are 0.12, 0.10, 0.14, 0.11 and 0.13 µA. Using the sensitivity found in worked example 1, find the detection limit.

  1. Mean of the blank = (0.12 + 0.10 + 0.14 + 0.11 + 0.13)/5 = 0.60/5 = 0.12 µA.
  2. Deviations from the mean: 0, −0.02, +0.02, −0.01, +0.01 µA; their squares add to 0.0004 + 0.0004 + 0.0001 + 0.0001 = 0.0010.
  3. Standard deviation σ = √(0.0010/(5 − 1)) = √0.00025 = 0.0158 µA.
  4. LOD = 3σ/S = 3 × 0.0158 / 1.57 = 0.0302 mmol/L.

Answer. The detection limit is about 0.030 mmol/L in the cell, far below the 2.55 mmol/L measured, so the sample reading is well clear of the noise.

Exam traps

  • A plain glass pH electrode or ion-selective electrode is not a biosensor; it has no biological recognition layer.
  • Sensitivity is the slope, in µA per mmol/L or mV per decade. Detection limit is a concentration, found from 3σ/S. Do not report one when asked for the other.
  • In standard addition, the added standard changes the total volume; ignoring the dilution gives a high result.
  • First generation uses oxygen, second uses a redox mediator, third uses direct electron transfer. Do not mix the order.
  • Optical and piezoelectric biosensors are not electrochemical; do not call every biosensor amperometric.

4.2 Bio-recognition elements: enzymes, antibodies, nucleic acids

A biosensor must pick one molecule out of a blood sample full of others, and the bio-recognition element does that picking. Enzymes catalyse one substrate, antibodies bind one antigen, nucleic acid probes pair with one complementary sequence. The element's kinetics or binding constant sets the sensor's range and detection limit. Glucometer strips, pregnancy tests and DNA test cards all rest on this layer. This chapter covers the three families, the numbers that describe them, and how they are fixed to a transducer.

You need: 4.1 for the component chain.

Enzymes: catalysis sets the range

Most enzymes are protein catalysts; a few are RNA. It binds one substrate at its active site, converts it, and is released unchanged, so one molecule cycles many times. Activity is quoted in international units: 1 U = 1 µmol of substrate converted per minute. Glucose oxidase, used on every glucose strip, oxidises β-D-glucose and is nearly blind to other sugars.

v = Vmax S / (Km + S)

v is the rate (µmol min⁻¹ per mg of enzyme); Vmax is the rate when every site is occupied; S is substrate concentration; Km is the substrate concentration giving v = Vmax/2.

Km measures how tightly the enzyme holds its substrate: a small Km means tight binding and early saturation. At S = 19 Km, v = 0.95 Vmax; above that, doubling S barely changes v, so the sensor cannot resolve two high concentrations. Well below Km the rate is nearly proportional to S, v ≈ (Vmax/Km) S, and that straight part is the useful range. Glucose oxidase in solution has a Km for glucose reported in the region of 20–40 mmol/L, above most blood glucose values. On an electrode the enzyme is immobilised, substrate must diffuse through the layer, and the apparent Km usually rises; a diffusion-limiting membrane is often added on purpose to stretch the straight part.

On a glucose electrode the enzyme reaction runs to completion by itself. The electrode oxidises the product, hydrogen peroxide or a reduced mediator, to turn each enzyme turnover into electrons that can be counted as a current.

Antibodies and nucleic acids: binding sets selectivity

An antibody is not a catalyst. It binds an antigen and stays bound; it is neither consumed nor turned over. Affinity is described by the dissociation constant Kd = [antibody][antigen]/[complex]; smaller Kd means tighter binding. A whole IgG has two sites, so multivalent avidity can be far stronger than one site's Kd suggests. Detection is label-free when binding itself changes mass or charge on the transducer, and labelled when a fluorescent or enzyme tag reports the event.

θ = [Ag] / (Kd + [Ag])

θ is the fraction of antibody sites occupied; [Ag] is the free antigen concentration; Kd is the dissociation constant, in the same units. At [Ag] = Kd, half the sites are filled.

A nucleic acid probe is a short single strand, for example a 20-base oligomer. It finds its target by Watson–Crick pairing: A with T, G with C. Hybridisation strengthens as G+C content rises (three hydrogen bonds per G–C pair against two for A–T) and as salt concentration rises, which shields the negative phosphate backbones; it weakens as temperature rises. The melting temperature Tm is where half the duplexes have separated. Whole cells or tissue slices can also be the recognition layer: their own receptors and enzymes respond to the analyte, as in whole-cell sensors for toxicity or biochemical oxygen demand.

Immobilisation methods: adsorption is simple but leaks; covalent coupling is stable; gel entrapment holds whole cells and enzymes; self-assembled monolayers of thiols on gold give an ordered, reproducible surface.

Worked example

Question. A glucose oxidase layer has Vmax = 120 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹ and, in solution, Km = 25 mmol/L (a value inside the reported range). (a) Find v at S = 5.0 mmol/L. (b) Show that S = 25 mmol/L gives half of Vmax. (c) Find the concentration giving 95 % of Vmax. (d) At S = 20 mmol/L, compare the actual rate with the straight-line rate (Vmax/Km) S, first for Km = 25 mmol/L and then for an immobilised layer with apparent Km = 60 mmol/L.

  1. Write Michaelis–Menten: v = Vmax S / (Km + S).
  2. (a) v = 120 × 5.0 / (25 + 5.0) = 600 / 30 = 20.0 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹.
  3. (b) v = 120 × 25 / (25 + 25) = 3000 / 50 = 60.0 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹, exactly Vmax/2, which is the definition of Km.
  4. (c) 95 % of Vmax needs S = 19 Km = 19 × 25 = 475 mmol/L, far above any blood value.
  5. (d) The ratio of actual to straight-line rate is Km/(Km + S). For Km = 25: 25/45 = 0.56, so the response at 20 mmol/L is 44 % below the straight line.
  6. For apparent Km = 60: 60/80 = 0.75, only 25 % below. Raising the apparent Km straightens the response over 0–20 mmol/L, at the cost of a smaller signal.

Answer. (a) 20.0 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹; (b) 60.0 = Vmax/2; (c) 475 mmol/L; (d) 56 % of the straight-line rate with Km 25 mmol/L, 75 % with apparent Km 60 mmol/L.

Worked example 2

Question. An antibody on a sensor has Kd = 1.0 nmol/L for its antigen. Find the fraction of sites occupied at antigen concentrations of 0.10, 1.0 and 9.0 nmol/L, and say what this means for the measuring range.

  1. Use θ = [Ag] / (Kd + [Ag]).
  2. [Ag] = 0.10 nmol/L: θ = 0.10 / 1.10 = 0.091, about 9 %.
  3. [Ag] = 1.0 nmol/L: θ = 1.0 / 2.0 = 0.50, half the sites.
  4. [Ag] = 9.0 nmol/L: θ = 9.0 / 10.0 = 0.90.
  5. Going from 1.0 to 9.0 nmol/L, a ninefold rise, only moves θ from 0.50 to 0.90; the signal changes little above a few times Kd.

Answer. θ = 0.091, 0.50 and 0.90. The useful range is roughly 0.1 to 10 times Kd, here about 0.1–10 nmol/L; a tighter antibody (smaller Kd) moves the range lower.

Exam traps

  • An antibody is not a catalyst: it binds without turnover, so Vmax and Km do not apply to it.
  • Km is a concentration in mmol/L, not a rate; the rate units belong to Vmax.
  • Smaller Kd means tighter binding; a large Kd is the weak antibody.
  • The Km measured in free solution is not the apparent Km of the immobilised layer.

4.3 Electrochemical transducers and enzyme-based biosensors

An electrochemical transducer turns a chemical event into an electrical signal. In an enzyme biosensor, the enzyme consumes its target and makes a product; the electrode oxidises or reduces that product, and the current or voltage carries the concentration. Amperometric enzyme electrodes are the workhorse of blood glucose strips and many laboratory analysers. Potentiometric and conductometric versions cover pH, urea and electrolyte tests. This chapter shows the three-electrode cell, the glucose oxidase chain, the read-out electronics, and how planar electrodes are made.

You need 4.1 for the biosensor chain and bio-amplifiers and data acquisition systems for the op-amp and the converter.

The three-electrode cell and the three measurement modes

An electrochemical cell needs two electrodes, but a biosensor usually uses three. The working electrode (WE) is where the reaction of interest happens; it is platinum, gold or carbon. The reference electrode (RE) is Ag/AgCl in 3 M KCl. It holds a fixed potential and passes almost no current. The counter electrode (CE), often platinum, carries the current that balances the WE. A potentiostat keeps the WE at a set potential against the RE by driving the CE.

The three measurement modes differ in what is held and what is read. In amperometric mode the potential is fixed and current is measured; current rises with the flux of electroactive product. In potentiometric mode the current is held at zero and voltage is measured; the voltage follows the Nernst equation. In conductometric mode a small AC voltage is applied and conductance is measured.

ModeHeldMeasuredTypical use
AmperometricFixed potentialCurrentGlucose, lactate
PotentiometricZero currentVoltagepH, urea, K⁺
ConductometricSmall AC voltageConductanceUrea, cell culture

E = E° + (RT / nF) ln a

E is the cell potential in volts; E° is the standard potential; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is absolute temperature in K; n is the number of electrons; F = 96485 C/mol; a is the dimensionless activity of the ion (its concentration relative to the standard state, approximately the concentration in mol/L for dilute solutions), so the logarithm has no units. At 25 °C the slope is about 59 mV per decade for n = 1.

Why three electrodes? In a two-electrode cell, current through the reference electrode polarises it and the set potential drifts. The potentiostat draws almost no current through the RE, so the setpoint stays stable. That stability is why pH meters and urea sensors can trust a millivolt reading.

The glucose oxidase amperometric chain

Glucose oxidase (GOx) is the enzyme. Its reaction is glucose + O₂ → gluconolactone + H₂O₂. GOx has a flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) cofactor that takes two electrons from glucose. The enzyme is selective because other sugars fit the active site poorly. The enzyme layer is immobilised over the working electrode.

In the common amperometric route, hydrogen peroxide is oxidised at the WE held at about +0.7 V vs Ag/AgCl: H₂O₂ → O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻. Two electrons arrive per glucose molecule. The current is proportional to the glucose flux reaching the enzyme layer. A planar strip uses this chemistry; see 4.5 for the disposable version.

A second option measures the oxygen consumed with a Clark electrode. Oxygen is reduced at about −0.6 V vs Ag/AgCl. As glucose rises, oxygen falls, so the current falls. That sign is easy to miss. Mediators such as ferrocene and ferricyanide shuttle electrons from GOx to the electrode, so the electrode can run at +0.2 to +0.4 V instead of the +0.7 V needed to oxidise H₂O₂ directly. Working at the lower potential reduces, but does not remove, the false current from easily oxidised interferents such as ascorbic acid and uric acid.

Faraday’s law, read-out electronics and planar fabrication

I = n F J A

I is current in A; n is electrons per molecule (2 for glucose); F = 96485 C/mol; J is flux in mol cm⁻² s⁻¹; A is electrode area in cm².

The transimpedance amplifier converts current to voltage. It is an op-amp with feedback resistor Rf. With Rf between 100 kΩ and 1 MΩ, a 1 µA current gives 0.1 V to 1 V (Vout = −I × Rf for the inverting connection; only the magnitude is used in read-out). Choose Rf so that the largest expected current still gives an output inside the amplifier's swing. An RC low-pass filter removes noise, an ADC digitises the voltage, and the display shows mmol/L. The full data-acquisition hardware is the same as for bio-amplifiers.

Temperature changes enzyme activity and electrode slope. A temperature sensor and a Wheatstone bridge provide on-chip compensation. Resistors, diodes and transistors appear here only as circuit parts; their device physics is not the subject of this chapter. Fabrication starts with photolithography to define the pattern, sputtering to deposit Pt or Au, and lift-off to remove unwanted metal. Micromachining shapes wells and channels. Self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) anchor the enzyme and reduce fouling.

Worked example

Question. A planar glucose electrode has area A = 0.020 cm². Glucose reaches the enzyme layer at a steady flux J = 5.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ mol cm⁻² s⁻¹. Each glucose yields n = 2 electrons, and F = 96485 C/mol. (a) Find the current. (b) If the transimpedance amplifier has Rf = 1.0 MΩ, find Vout. (c) Explain why Rf = 100 kΩ is a better choice for a 3 V supply.

  1. Write Faraday’s law for an electrode: I = n F J A.
  2. Compute nF = 2 × 96485 = 192970 C/mol.
  3. Multiply by flux: 192970 × 5.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 9.65 × 10⁻⁵ A per cm².
  4. Multiply by area: 9.65 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.020 = 1.93 × 10⁻⁶ A = 1.93 µA.
  5. With Rf = 1.0 MΩ: Vout = 1.93 × 10⁻⁶ A × 1.0 × 10⁶ Ω = 1.93 V.
  6. With Rf = 100 kΩ: Vout = 1.93 × 10⁻⁶ A × 1.0 × 10⁵ Ω = 0.193 V.
  7. 1.93 V is not too close to the 3 V rail for the op-amp itself, but it leaves little room for the output to rise when glucose (and so current) increases above the calibration point. With Rf = 100 kΩ the quiescent output is 0.193 V, so the amplifier stays in range over a wider glucose span before it saturates.

Answer. The current is 1.93 µA; with 1.0 MΩ Vout is 1.93 V, and with 100 kΩ it is 0.193 V, so 100 kΩ is the practical choice.

Worked example 2

Question. A urea electrode uses urease over an ammonium-ion electrode, which follows an ideal 59.16 mV per decade at 25 °C (n = 1). A 1.0 mmol/L urea standard reads −120.0 mV and a patient sample reads −80.0 mV. Find the urea concentration.

  1. Urease turns urea into ammonium, and the electrode potential rises by 59.16 mV for each tenfold rise in ammonium, so E = Estd + 59.16 log₁₀(c/cstd).
  2. ΔE = −80.0 − (−120.0) = +40.0 mV.
  3. Decades = 40.0 / 59.16 = 0.676.
  4. c/cstd = 100.676 = 4.75, so c = 4.75 × 1.0 = 4.75 mmol/L.

Answer. Urea is about 4.8 mmol/L. A real electrode is calibrated with two or more standards, because its slope is often below the ideal 59.16 mV.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting that a measured current needs a stated reference electrode and applied potential. A bare current value is not a calibrated measurement.
  • Mixing up the sign: oxidation at the working electrode gives an anodic positive current, while the Clark oxygen sensor current falls as glucose rises.
  • Calling the counter electrode the reference electrode. The RE passes almost no current; the CE carries the current.
  • Using n = 1 for the peroxide route. One glucose gives two electrons at the working electrode, so n = 2.
  • Using area in m² with flux in mol cm⁻² s⁻¹. Convert to cm² first, or the current will be off by 10⁴.

4.4 ISFET and IMFET

An ion-sensitive field-effect transistor, or ISFET, is a MOSFET with no metal gate. The sample and an ion-selective membrane sit where the gate was, and bound ions change the surface charge. That change shifts the threshold voltage, so a chemical concentration appears as an electrical signal. ISFETs measure pH in small volumes; enzyme-coated EnFETs measure penicillin, glucose or urea; antibody-coated IMFETs detect antigens without a label. They are used in portable meters, lab-on-chip systems and implantable chemical sensing.

Structure and the charge-to-current path

An ISFET is a MOSFET (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor) with the metal gate removed. Source and drain are n⁺ (heavily doped n-type) regions in p-silicon, with a channel between them. The gate stack is a thin silicon dioxide layer plus silicon nitride (Si₃N₄) or tantalum oxide (Ta₂O₅), exposed to the sample electrolyte. A reference electrode, usually Ag/AgCl, sits in the sample and completes the circuit. For ion selectivity, an ion-selective membrane covers the gate. The threshold voltage VT is the gate voltage at which the channel just forms; drain current ID flows from drain to source when VT is exceeded.

Ions bind to surface sites such as SiOH or TaOH. The bound charge changes the surface potential. The surface potential adds in series with the applied gate voltage, and the electrolyte double-layer capacitance divides with the gate capacitance, so only part of the surface potential appears across the channel and shifts VT. At fixed gate voltage, ID changes. The site-binding model describes the surface charge against pH; only its name is needed here. The device is small, fast and batch-fabricated, and its low output impedance suits electrical readout.

pH ISFET, EnFET and the Nernst slope

At 25 °C the pH response follows the Nernst slope: 59.16 mV per pH unit. Run the ISFET at constant drain current with a feedback gate. The feedback adjusts the gate voltage VG to hold ID fixed. VG then follows the surface potential, so the ISFET becomes a pH meter with an electrical output. If the gate voltage is held fixed instead, the drain current changes; in the subthreshold region the change is set by the subthreshold swing, typically 60–80 mV per decade of current. Temperature must be known because the Nernst slope is proportional to absolute temperature.

ΔVG = (2.303RT/F) × ΔpH = 59.16 mV × ΔpH at 25 °C

ΔVG is the gate-voltage shift; ΔpH is the pH change; R is 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is absolute temperature in K; F is 96485 C mol⁻¹; the sign is set by surface charge and feedback polarity.

An enzyme field-effect transistor (EnFET) is the same device with an enzyme layer over the gate. The enzyme reaction changes local pH or charge. Penicillinase on penicillin produces penicilloic acid; glucose oxidase on glucose produces gluconic acid; urease on urea produces ammonia and raises pH. The enzyme layer sets selectivity, and its kinetics set range and detection limit (see <a href='#bio-recognition-elements-enzymes-antibodies-nucleic-acids'>4.2 and <a href='#blood-glucose-sensors-and-diabetes-management'>4.5).

IMFET and the Debye-length limit

An immunologically sensitive field-effect transistor (IMFET) is an ISFET with an antibody immobilised on the gate or in a membrane over it. Antigen binding adds charge near the surface. If that charge lies within the Debye length, it changes the surface potential and shifts VT. Detection is label-free: no fluorescent or radioactive tag is needed. The Debye length is the distance over which counterions screen charge; it is about 1 nm in physiological salt, so charge farther away is screened. Undiluted serum therefore gives a weak direct signal; dilute the sample or use a membrane that brings binding close to the surface. A neutral antigen gives little direct charge signal; a charged sandwich label can help but is no longer strictly label-free.

Readout uses a constant-current or constant-voltage circuit. A differential pair has one sensing ISFET and one reference ISFET without antibody, or with inactive antibody. Subtraction removes common drift, temperature and light effects. CMOS (complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor) integration puts many ISFETs and amplifiers on one chip for multi-ion or multi-antibody arrays.

Fabrication and practical limits

Fabrication follows standard CMOS steps.

  1. Oxidation grows a thick field oxide on p-silicon.
  2. Photolithography and etching open the source and drain windows.
  3. Diffusion or ion implantation forms the n⁺ source and drain; annealing follows.
  4. A second lithography step opens the gate region, and a thin gate oxide (SiO₂) is grown there.
  5. The sensing layer, Si₃N₄ by LPCVD or Ta₂O₅, is deposited over the gate oxide.
  6. Contact holes are opened and aluminium or gold is deposited for the source and drain contacts.
  7. Encapsulation with epoxy, polyimide or silicon nitride covers everything except the gate window; an ion-selective or enzyme membrane is added last.

Practical limits are drift, hysteresis, light sensitivity and reference-electrode instability. Drift is a slow change in VT from hydration or ion migration. Hysteresis makes the reading depend on the previous pH. Light creates carriers in silicon and shifts the current. The reference electrode's drift adds directly to VG, so it must be stable. Temperature control matters because the Nernst slope changes with T. The gate window is the only place where the gate insulator touches the sample; everything else is sealed by epoxy, polyimide or nitride.

Worked example

Question. A pH ISFET has a Nernst slope of 59.16 mV per pH unit at 25 °C and a subthreshold swing of 70 mV per decade. The sample pH falls from 7.4 to 7.1. (a) Find the gate-voltage shift at constant drain current with a feedback gate. (b) Find the drain-current ratio at constant gate voltage. (c) Explain why an IMFET signal from one electronic charge per bound antigen needs a differential pair and averaging.

  1. ΔpH = 7.1 − 7.4 = −0.3 pH units.
  2. |ΔVG| = 59.16 mV/pH × 0.3 pH = 17.748 mV ≈ 17.7 mV. pH falling raises the surface potential, so the feedback gate moves down by 17.7 mV to hold ID.
  3. At constant VG, use S = 70 mV/decade: ID ratio = 10|ΔV|/S = 1017.7/70 = 100.253 = 1.79.
  4. So ID rises by about 1.8 times at fixed VG; the sign follows the surface-charge direction.
  5. For (c), q = 1.602 × 10−19 C and ΔV = q/C. A 10 µm × 10 µm gate with 10 nm of SiO₂ (relative permittivity 3.9) has C = 8.854 × 10−12 × 3.9 × 10−10 / 10−8 = 0.35 pF, so ΔV ≈ 1.602 × 10−19 / 3.5 × 10−13 = 0.46 µV. A smaller gate has a smaller C and a larger shift, up to a few millivolts for nanoscale devices. One charge on a normal gate is far below drift and noise; a reference ISFET subtracts common drift, and averaging many bound antigens and many readings reduces random noise.

Answer. The constant-current gate-voltage shift is 17.7 mV downward, the constant-voltage drain current rises by a factor of about 1.79, and one bound charge shifts a 10 µm gate by only about 0.5 µV.

Exam traps

  • Sign slip: at constant current the feedback gate moves to compensate; do not say the drain current changes by 59 mV/pH.
  • Mixing readouts: the Nernst slope applies to gate voltage at constant current; the subthreshold swing applies to current at constant gate voltage.
  • Ignoring Debye screening in IMFET: undiluted serum screens charge beyond about 1 nm; binding must be close to the surface.
  • Treating ISFET as a glass electrode: it has no glass bulb and needs a stable reference electrode; reference drift adds to VG.
  • Forgetting drift, hysteresis, light and temperature: all shift VT or the slope; a single calibration point is not enough.

4.5 Blood glucose sensors and diabetes management

Blood glucose sensors answer one question many times a day: how much glucose is in this drop of blood, or in the fluid under the skin? The disposable strip is the amperometric enzyme electrode made cheap enough to throw away. Continuous monitors turn the same chemistry into a 24-hour trace. People with diabetes use both to dose insulin, catch hypoglycaemia and see how food and exercise move their glucose. This chapter shows how the strip works, what the numbers mean, and where the errors come from.

You need the amperometric cell from 4.3 and calibration ideas from 4.1.

The strip is a disposable amperometric cell

A glucose strip is a plastic rectangle with a capillary channel. Touch the channel to a blood drop and suction fills it. At the far end sit a working electrode and a reference electrode, printed on the plastic. Dried glucose oxidase (GOx) and a mediator cover the working electrode.

The meter applies a fixed potential and reads current after a few seconds. Current is proportional to glucose. Fill-detection contacts tell the meter the channel is full, so the timer starts only then. This is the amperometric enzyme electrode from 4.3, made disposable.

GOx oxidises glucose to gluconolactone. In early strips oxygen accepted the electrons, and the meter measured either the hydrogen peroxide produced or the oxygen consumed. That made the reading depend on blood oxygen, which changes in illness, at altitude and in poor circulation. A mediator such as ferricyanide (Fe(CN)₆³⁻) or an osmium complex now carries electrons from the enzyme to the electrode. The mediator is oxidised at a lower potential, so fewer substances interfere and the strip reads faster.

Cut-offs, units and the plasma question

Most clinics use these cut-offs: fasting plasma glucose 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or more; 2-hour OGTT 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or more; random glucose 200 mg/dL or more with symptoms; HbA1c 6.5% or more. Hypoglycaemia is below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L). The two units convert as below.

glucose (mmol/L) = glucose (mg/dL) ÷ 18

glucose (mg/dL) is the concentration in milligrams per decilitre; glucose (mmol/L) is the same concentration in millimoles per litre.

A meter reads whole blood from a finger. A laboratory usually reports plasma. Plasma glucose is about 11% higher than whole blood, because red cells have less water per unit volume and glucose dissolves in water. Many meters report plasma-equivalent values, but haematocrit still shifts the result: anaemia can read high and polycythaemia can read low. Compare a meter with a lab value only after allowing for this.

HbA1c is not a strip reading. It is a lab measure of average glucose over the past two to three months.

CGM, error sources and the dosing loop

A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) puts a glucose oxidase wire under the skin and measures interstitial fluid. It sends a current every 1–5 minutes. Interstitial glucose lags blood glucose by several minutes, longer when glucose is changing fast. Calibrate with a finger-stick reading when the trace is steady.

Accuracy is reported as MARD, the mean absolute relative difference between sensor and reference readings. Lower is better; many sensors today are below 10%. The trend arrow shows direction and speed, so a reading of 110 mg/dL falling fast is not the same as 110 mg/dL steady.

Error sources: haematocrit; ascorbic acid and uric acid, which oxidise at the electrode and add current; temperature, which changes enzyme rate and diffusion; altitude; expired strips; and reuse. A compression low happens at night when the body presses on the sensor, reducing local blood flow; it reads low without real hypoglycaemia. Confirm with a finger-stick.

The management loop is simple. Self-monitoring gives the number. Correction dose = (current glucose − target glucose) ÷ insulin sensitivity factor (ISF). Meal dose = carbohydrate grams ÷ carbohydrate ratio (CR). A strip costs roughly ₹10–₹50 in India, so cost matters. Reusing a strip or sharing a meter is unsafe: blood-borne infection and strip degradation both follow.

Worked example

Question. A meter reads 212 mg/dL before lunch. The person's insulin sensitivity factor (ISF) is 1 unit per 50 mg/dL and the target is 120 mg/dL. (a) Convert the reading to mmol/L.   1. (a) 212 ÷ 18 = 11.8 mmol/L.

  3. (c) Meal = 40 ÷ 10 = 4.0 units. Total = 4.0 + 1.84 = 5.84 units, given as 5.8 units.

Worked example 2

Question. A CGM is checked against laboratory plasma glucose at four times. Sensor: 100, 150, 80 and 210 mg/dL. Reference: 110, 140, 90 and 200 mg/dL. Find the MARD.

  • Thinking the mediator is the enzyme. Glucose oxidase is the enzyme; ferricyanide or an osmium complex is the electron shuttle.
  • Treating a CGM reading as blood glucose. Interstitial fluid lags blood, and a compression low can look like hypoglycaemia.
  • Reusing strips or sharing meters. Both are unsafe and also corrupt the reading.

4.6 DNA biosensors and sequence electrodes

A DNA biosensor answers one narrow question: does this sample contain a strand with a known sequence? It holds the complementary strand, the probe, on an electrode and lets the target hybridise to it. The duplex formed is then read as a current or an impedance change. The same trick serves pathogen detection, mutation screening, forensic samples and food authenticity checks, and it is the reason a single wrong base can be caught.

You need: 4.2 for nucleic acid probes and hybridisation, and 4.3 for electrode transduction.

The probe electrode

The sensor carries the complementary strand, the probe, fixed on the transducer. The probe is a single synthetic strand of 15–30 bases. Shorter than 15 and the duplex is too weak, so near-matches bind too; longer than 30 and one wrong base changes the stability too little to see.

Anchoring uses a thiol group (–SH) at the probe's 5' end. Clean gold dipped in probe solution forms Au–S bonds, and the strands pack into a self-assembled monolayer (SAM): one molecule thick, held by the sulphur head, bases free in the buffer.

  1. Polish the gold electrode, rinse with water and dry.
  2. Immerse in 0.1–1 µM thiolated probe in buffer for 1–16 hours.
  3. Rinse, then backfill with 1 mM 6-mercapto-1-hexanol for about one hour.
  4. Rinse, store wet at 4 °C and use within days.

The backfill thiol, MCH, fills bare gold between probes. It stands the probes upright instead of letting them lie flat, spaces them so a target can reach in, and blocks the metal so redox molecules in solution cannot touch it and add background current. Many probes also carry a block of ten thymine bases (T10) between the thiol anchor and the sequence, a linker that holds the probe away from the gold; the MCH backfill is still done.

Hybridisation, Tm and stringency

Sample DNA arrives double-stranded. Heat it to 95 °C for five minutes and cool it fast on ice; the strands separate and stay apart, and these single strands are added to the electrode. Bases then pair by Watson–Crick rules: A with T through two hydrogen bonds, G with C through three. A G+C-rich duplex is therefore more stable than an A+T-rich one of the same length.

Tm = 2(A + T) + 4(G + C)

A, T, G, C are the counts of each base in the probe (not percentages); Tm is the melting temperature in °C, at which half the duplex has separated.

Salt matters. Na⁺ ions shield the negative phosphate backbones, which otherwise repel each other, so low salt destabilises the duplex. Formamide, a denaturant, lowers Tm and lets an assay run cooler. The Wallace rule above is a counting rule for perfect-match probes of about 14–20 bases in high-salt buffer; for longer probes, locked nucleic acids or mixed solvents, use a nearest-neighbour calculation.

The wash gives selectivity. After hybridisation, wash the electrode at Tm − 5 °C in the same buffer. A perfectly matched target stays bound; a target with one internal mismatch has a Tm about 5 °C lower, so at the wash temperature about half of it has separated and the wash carries those strands away. This strictness is stringency, set by temperature and salt together. Wash too cold or too salty and mismatches survive, giving a false positive.

Reading the duplex: label-free and labelled

A duplex changes the electrode surface, and several transducers can report it.

  • Impedance: the negative backbone of the duplex repels ferri/ferrocyanide ions, so charge-transfer resistance rises as duplex forms.
  • ISFET: the extra surface charge shifts the threshold voltage (see 4.4).
  • Surface plasmon resonance: the added mass changes the refractive index at a gold film, read as an angle shift.

Labelled routes give a bigger signal for extra steps. A redox-active intercalator such as methylene blue is added after hybridisation; it binds only duplex DNA, so more duplex means more bound dye and a larger current after rinsing. A ferrocene tag on the target, or on a signalling probe, is present all the time, but it is held at the electrode and gives current only when the target is captured in a duplex; unbound tagged strands are washed away. An enzyme label such as horseradish peroxidase turns one binding event into many electrons through substrate turnover. The sandwich format uses a capture probe on the electrode plus a labelled detector probe binding a second site on the target, which adds specificity but needs a longer target.

The stem-loop (E-DNA) probe is a neat alternative. Its two ends are self-complementary and pair with each other, holding a redox tag close to the electrode so current flows freely. Target binding opens the stem, the tag swings away and the current falls. That is a signal-off sensor: probe loss or damage lowers the current exactly as target does, so always normalise.

In clean buffer a label-free electrode reaches picomolar. A real sample — blood, sputum, a food rinse — holds femtomolar target among far more non-target DNA, so most assays amplify first with PCR or an isothermal method such as LAMP or RPA.

Uses and controls

Pathogen detection covers tuberculosis, dengue and Salmonella in food and water. Mutation screening covers known single-base changes, where the stringency wash is the entire method. Forensic work uses short mitochondrial or repeat-region sequences from tiny degraded samples. Food authenticity checks identify species in meat and fish.

Every assay runs three electrodes: a blank, a perfect-match positive and a single-mismatch negative. A strong current alone proves nothing; it must exceed the mismatch signal by a margin you have calibrated (4.1).

4.7 Quantum-dot and nanomaterial biosensors

At the nanoscale, a particle has a large surface for its volume, and its optical and electronic behaviour can depend on its size. A few molecules binding to a quantum dot or a gold nanoparticle can change light emission, colour, or current. This makes nanomaterial biosensors sensitive, and some of them, such as gold-nanoparticle strips read by eye, need no optics at all; quantum-dot assays still need a light source and a detector. They appear in rapid antigen strips, research assays, and point-of-care tests. The same physics also sets their limits: charge screening, toxicity, and batch-to-batch variation.

You need 4.2 for bio-recognition elements and 4.4 for the FET and Debye-length idea.

Quantum dots: size sets colour

A quantum dot is a semiconductor crystal whose core is 2–10 nm across. A common label uses a CdSe core with a ZnS shell about 1–2 nm thick. The core absorbs light and emits light; the shell passivates surface states and reduces leaching. In a crystal this small, the electron and hole are confined. Their allowed energies become discrete. The band gap rises as the dot shrinks. This is quantum confinement. A small dot emits blue; a larger dot emits red. Absorption is broad because many transitions above the band edge are allowed. Emission is narrow because it comes from the lowest confined state. The dot is also photostable, so it can be excited for long times without bleaching like many dyes.

Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) is the transfer of excitation energy from an excited donor, here the dot, to a nearby acceptor dye without any light being emitted in between. The acceptor quenches the donor, and the efficiency E of the transfer depends very steeply on the donor–acceptor distance:

E = 1 / (1 + (r / R0)6)

E is the fraction of donor energy transferred; r is the donor–acceptor distance in nm; R0 is the Förster radius in nm, the distance where E = 0.5: about 2–6 nm for dye–dye pairs and about 4–7 nm for a quantum-dot donor with a dye acceptor.

As a label, the dot is coated with antibody or DNA, and binding is detected by its emission. As a FRET donor, the dot passes energy to an acceptor dye on the target. The sixth power makes E fall steeply: a small increase in r turns transfer off. This reports binding and unbinding without a wash step. Cadmium is toxic. The ZnS shell reduces exposure, but broken dots and waste need controlled disposal.

Gold nanoparticles and the red line

A gold nanoparticle of 20–40 nm has a strong optical resonance called localised surface plasmon resonance, LSPR. Light makes conduction electrons oscillate together. Dispersed particles look red because they absorb and scatter around the green. When particles aggregate, their plasmons couple, and the colour shifts to blue-purple. This colour change needs no instrument. It is the basis of the red test line in a lateral flow strip: gold–antibody conjugate binds target, the complex is captured at a line, and accumulated gold looks red. Gold also works as an electrochemical label. The gold is oxidised to Au3+, and the current gives the amount bound.

  • Dispersed 20–40 nm gold: red.
  • Aggregated gold: blue-purple.
  • Lateral flow line: accumulated gold, read by eye.
  • Electrochemical gold label: oxidation current.

Nanomaterial FETs, magnetic particles, conducting polymers

A carbon nanotube, a graphene sheet, or a silicon nanowire can be the channel of a field-effect transistor. A charged molecule bound near the channel changes its conductance. The device measures this change as a current or threshold shift. The limit is the Debye length: in salty serum, mobile ions screen charge within about 1 nm. Only binding inside that layer changes the signal. This is the same limit as the IMFET in 4.4.

λD ≈ 0.304 / √I nm

λD is the Debye length in nm for a 1:1 salt in water at 25 °C; I is the ionic strength in mol/L. Serum, I ≈ 0.15 mol/L, gives about 0.8 nm.

  • Magnetic nanoparticles: superparamagnetic, bind target, magnet pulls them out for separation or preconcentration.
  • Conducting polymers: polypyrrole and PEDOT are nanostructured platforms that anchor biomolecules and carry charge.
  • Silicon nanowires: high surface-to-volume ratio, so small charge changes give large conductance changes in low-salt buffer.

These platforms are chosen for surface area and for easy electrical readout. They do not remove the need for calibration, clean samples, or a recognition element that binds only the target.

Worked example

Question. A quantum-dot donor has Förster radius R0 = 5.0 nm. An acceptor dye sits on a target. Use E = 1 / (1 + (r / R0)6). (a) Bound target holds the acceptor at r = 4.0 nm. Find E and the percentage of donor energy transferred. (b) After the target leaves, r = 8.0 nm. Find E. (c) Find the ratio of bound to unbound donor quenching and explain why a wash step may not be needed.

  1. Bound: r / R0 = 4.0 / 5.0 = 0.80. Sixth power = 0.806 = 0.262. Denominator = 1 + 0.262 = 1.262. E = 1 / 1.262 = 0.792, about 0.79, so 79% of donor energy is transferred.
  2. Unbound: r / R0 = 8.0 / 5.0 = 1.60. Sixth power = 1.606 = 16.78. Denominator = 1 + 16.78 = 17.78. E = 1 / 17.78 = 0.0563, about 0.056, so 5.6%.
  3. Ratio = 0.792 / 0.0563 = 14.1, about 14. A 4 nm movement changes transfer from strong to weak. Donor emission returns when the target leaves, so binding is seen without a wash step.

Answer. Bound: E = 0.79 (79%); unbound: E = 0.056 (5.6%); ratio ≈ 14.

Worked example 2

Question. With the same quantum-dot donor (R0 = 5.0 nm), donor emission falls from 1000 counts without acceptor to 300 counts when the target is bound. Find the transfer efficiency and the donor–acceptor distance.

  1. E = 1 − (donor emission with acceptor ÷ donor emission without) = 1 − 300/1000 = 0.70.
  2. Rearrange E = 1/(1 + (r/R0)6): (r/R0)6 = 1/E − 1 = 1/0.70 − 1 = 0.4286.
  3. r/R0 = 0.42861/6 = 0.868.
  4. r = 0.868 × 5.0 = 4.34 nm.

Answer. E = 0.70 and r ≈ 4.3 nm, slightly inside R0, as expected for an efficiency above 0.5.

Worked example 2

Question. A graphene FET antibody sensor is used in serum (ionic strength 0.15 mol/L) and in serum diluted 100-fold in water. Find the Debye length in each and say whether an antigen charge 3 nm from the channel can be sensed.

  1. Serum: λD = 0.304 / √0.15 = 0.304 / 0.387 = 0.785 nm.
  2. Diluted 100-fold: I = 0.15 / 100 = 0.0015 mol/L, so λD = 0.304 / √0.0015 = 0.304 / 0.0387 = 7.85 nm.
  3. Diluting 100-fold makes the Debye length √100 = 10 times longer.
  4. A charge 3 nm away is almost four Debye lengths out in serum (3/0.785 = 3.8), so it is screened; in the diluted sample it is well inside 7.85 nm and changes the channel.

Answer. λD is 0.79 nm in serum and 7.9 nm after 100-fold dilution; the antigen is sensed only in the diluted sample, at the price of a 100 times lower antigen concentration.

Exam traps

  • Saying a quantum dot changes colour because it is bigger and scatters more light. The cause is quantum confinement of the electron and hole, which raises the band gap as the dot shrinks.
  • Treating FRET efficiency as linear in distance. E falls with the sixth power of r, so small distance changes give large signal changes.
  • Reversing the gold colours: dispersed 20–40 nm gold is red; aggregated gold is blue-purple.
  • Forgetting Debye screening: a nanowire or graphene FET in undiluted serum detects only charge within about 1 nm of the surface, not every bound molecule.
  • Confusing Förster radius R0 with the particle radius. R0 is the donor–acceptor distance where FRET efficiency is 0.5.

Takeaways

  1. A biosensor is only a system: recognition element, transducer and electronics, and no reading is trusted until it is calibrated against standards.
  2. Selectivity comes from the biological element; the usable range and detection limit come from its binding or catalytic kinetics.
  3. Electrochemical transducers are popular because they are cheap, small and give a direct electrical signal that a simple circuit can digitise.
  4. In an ISFET the sample and a reference electrode take the place of the metal gate, so the measured quantity is a shift in threshold voltage: about 59 mV per pH at 25 °C, read at constant drain current.
  5. The disposable glucose strip is the most widely used biosensor in the world, and the continuous monitor turns the same chemistry into a time trace.
  6. DNA biosensors trade the enzyme's amplification for sequence specificity, and read the amount of duplex formed by current or impedance.
  7. At the nanoscale, high surface area amplifies small binding events, but charge screening by salt limits what can be detected electrically.
  8. Every number on a biosensor report comes from calibration: sensitivity is the slope, the detection limit is 3σ/S, and standard addition corrects for the sample matrix.

Understand Explain how a biosensor differs from an ordinary chemical or physical sensor, and why the biological element is the source of selectivity. 8 marks

Points to cover. Physical sensor responds to a bulk property; chemical sensor responds to a class of species; Biological element gives molecular recognition through shape and charge complementarity; Enzyme active site, antigen–antibody fit, base pairing; Selectivity measured against interfering species; Selectivity does not fix range or LOD; kinetics do.

Model answer.

  1. Physical sensor. Responds to a bulk physical quantity: a thermistor to temperature, a strain gauge to force. It has no chemical selectivity at all.
  2. Chemical sensor. Responds to a class of chemical species through a non-biological layer: a glass electrode to H⁺, a valinomycin membrane to K⁺, a metal-oxide film to reducing gases. Its selectivity comes from the glass, the membrane or the oxide.
  3. Biosensor. Adds a biological recognition element. A glass electrode cannot tell glucose from fructose, because neither changes H⁺ directly; glucose oxidase over an electrode can, because only glucose fits its active site well.
  4. Why the biological element gives selectivity. Biological molecules recognise shape and charge together. (1) The enzyme active site holds the substrate by several weak bonds at fixed positions, so a sugar with one hydroxyl group in the wrong place binds poorly. (2) An antibody's binding pocket matches the shape and charge of one epitope, with Kd often in the nanomolar range. (3) A DNA probe pairs base by base, A with T and G with C, so one wrong base lowers Tm by about 5 °C.

Points to cover. Model answer.

  1. Rate equation. v = Vmax S / (Km + S). Vmax is the rate with every active site busy; Km is the substrate concentration at which v = Vmax/2.
  2. Origin in one line. Enzyme and substrate form a complex that either falls apart or turns into product; at steady state the fraction of enzyme in the complex is S/(Km + S).
  3. Two limits. For S ≪ Km, v ≈ (Vmax/Km) S: first order, straight in S, the useful measuring region. For S ≫ Km, v ≈ Vmax: zero order, the signal no longer changes with S.

Understand Describe the structure and principle of an ISFET, and explain how it differs from a MOSFET. 10 marks

Points to cover. MOSFET structure: source, drain, gate oxide, metal gate; ISFET: metal gate replaced by ion-selective membrane, sample and reference electrode; Surface charge at the sensing layer shifts the threshold voltage; Nernstian response, about 59 mV per pH unit at 25 °C; Difference: input is ionic activity in solution, not a gate voltage; Drift and reference electrode issues.

Model answer.

  1. MOSFET. n⁺ source and drain in p-silicon, a thin SiO₂ gate oxide, and a metal gate. A gate voltage above the threshold VT forms an n-channel and drain current ID flows.
  2. ISFET. The metal gate is removed. A sensing layer of Si₃N₄ or Ta₂O₅ is deposited on the gate oxide, the sample electrolyte covers it, and a Ag/AgCl reference electrode in the sample sets the gate voltage. Everything except the gate window is sealed with epoxy or polyimide.
  3. Comparison.
    FeatureMOSFETISFET
    gatemetal on oxidereference electrode, electrolyte, sensing layer on oxide
    inputgate voltageion activity in the sample (pH)
    what shiftsnothing; VT fixedVT moves with surface potential
    packagefully sealedgate window open to liquid
    extra partsnonestable reference electrode, temperature sensor
  4. Principle. H⁺ ions bind to SiOH or TaOH sites on the sensing layer. The bound charge changes the surface potential, which adds in series with the applied gate voltage and so shifts the effective VT. The ideal shift is the Nernst slope, 2.303RT/F = 59.16 mV per pH at 25 °C.
  5. Readout. At constant drain current, a feedback loop moves VG to cancel the shift, so VG follows pH: a fall from 7.4 to 7.1 moves VG by 59.16 × 0.3 = 17.7 mV. At constant VG in the subthreshold region with a swing of 70 mV per decade, ID changes by 1017.7/70 = 1.79 times.
  6. Variants. An enzyme layer makes an EnFET (urease, penicillinase, glucose oxidase); an antibody layer makes an IMFET, limited by Debye screening to binding within about 1 nm in serum.
  7. Practical problems. Drift as the layer hydrates, hysteresis, light sensitivity of the silicon, temperature (61.55 mV per pH at 37 °C) and above all the reference electrode, whose drift adds straight to VG. A differential pair with a reference FET cancels common drift.
  8. Advantages over a glass electrode. Small, robust, batch-made on a wafer, fast, and low output impedance, so it suits catheters and arrays.
  9. Fabrication in one line. The ISFET is made with ordinary CMOS steps: field oxide, source and drain diffusion, thin gate oxide, sensing-layer deposition, contacts, then encapsulation that leaves only the gate window open.
  10. Check. 17.7/70 = 0.253 and 100.253 = 1.79.

  3. Electron route. Ferricyanide mediator, not oxygen: reduced GOx passes electrons to Fe(CN)₆³⁻, and the electrode reoxidises Fe(CN)₆⁴⁻. This removes the oxygen dependence of the first-generation H₂O₂ route.

  5. Electrodes and immobilisation. Screen-printed carbon working electrode, Ag/AgCl reference, and a fill-detect pair at the far end of the capillary. GOx and mediator are dried in a hydrophilic polymer film over the working electrode; cross-linking with glutaraldehyde or entrapment keeps them in place.

Apply An ISFET with a Nernstian slope of 59 mV per pH unit is used for a urea sensor. Calculate the threshold shift for a pH change of 0.05 and explain two reasons a measured shift may not match this figure. 10 marks

Points to cover. ΔV = 59 × 0.05 = 2.95 mV; Sign follows direction of pH change; Reason 1: membrane drift and hydration over hours; Reason 2: reference electrode instability or junction potential; Reason 3: incomplete urease conversion at low urea or short residence time; Calibration and blank correction before any reading is quoted.

Model answer.

  1. Principle. Urease hydrolyses urea: CO(NH₂)₂ + H₂O → 2NH₃ + CO₂. Ammonia takes up H⁺, so the local pH at the gate rises, and the pH ISFET under the enzyme layer reads that rise.
  2. Threshold shift. ΔV = 59 mV per pH × 0.05 pH = 2.95 mV.
  3. Sign. The pH rises, so H⁺ leaves the surface sites, the surface potential falls and, at constant drain current, the feedback gate voltage moves in the opposite direction to the shift for a pH fall: if a fall of 0.3 pH moved VG down by 17.7 mV, a rise of 0.05 moves it up by 2.95 mV.
  4. Effect of temperature.
    TemperatureSlope 2.303RT/FShift for 0.05 pH
    25 °C59.16 mV2.96 mV
    37 °C61.55 mV3.08 mV
  5. Reason 1: drift. The sensing layer hydrates over hours and VT creeps, often by tenths of a millivolt to a few millivolts per hour. Against a 2.95 mV signal that is a large error unless a reference FET without urease subtracts it.
  6. Reason 2: reference electrode. The Ag/AgCl potential and its liquid junction drift with chloride and temperature; any change adds directly to VG.
  7. Reason 3: buffer and enzyme. The sample buffer soaks up part of the ammonia, so the pH change depends on buffer capacity as much as on urea; and at low urea or a short contact time the urease conversion is incomplete. The slope of an EnFET is therefore set by calibration with urea standards, not by the Nernst value.
  8. Reason 4: sub-Nernstian surface. Si₃N₄ often gives 45–56 mV per pH rather than 59, so the measured shift would be 2.3–2.8 mV.
  9. Practice. Calibrate the EnFET with two or three urea standards in the same buffer, run a reference FET, control temperature, and quote readings only after the blank has been subtracted.
  10. Two-point calibration. If 5 and 20 mmol/L urea standards give 2.4 and 6.0 mV, the working slope is (6.0 − 2.4)/15 = 0.24 mV per mmol/L over that range; samples are read from this line, not from the Nernst value.
  11. Worked contrast. A pH fall of 0.30 at constant drain current moved VG by 17.7 mV; the urea signal here is six times smaller, which is why an EnFET is always read differentially against a reference FET without enzyme.
  12. Check. 59 × 0.05 = 2.95 and 61.55 × 0.05 = 3.08.
Apply Design a DNA biosensor to detect a known single-base mutation. Describe the probe, its attachment, the hybridisation step, the signal readout, the controls and the stringency wash. 12 marks

Points to cover. Short probe matched to the mutant sequence, 15–30 bases; Thiol probe on gold through a self-assembled monolayer, backfilled to control spacing; Hybridisation in buffer at chosen salt and temperature; Readout: redox label, intercalator current, or impedance change; Positive control target, negative control mismatched target, blank; Stringency wash: raised temperature or lowered salt melts mismatched duplex; Single-base discrimination demonstrated by comparing signals.

Model answer.

  1. Goal. Tell a sample with a known single-base mutation from the normal (wild-type) sequence.
  2. Probe. A 20-base synthetic strand complementary to the mutant sequence, with the mutation near the middle, where one mismatch destabilises the duplex most. Example: A = 5, T = 5, G = 5, C = 5 gives Tm = 2(10) + 4(10) = 60 °C by the Wallace rule. A T10 linker and a 5' thiol are added.
  3. Attachment. Clean gold electrode in 0.1–1 µM thiolated probe for 1–16 h; Au–S bonds form a self-assembled monolayer. Backfill with 1 mM 6-mercapto-1-hexanol for about 1 h so the probes stand up and bare gold is blocked.
  4. Sample preparation. Amplify the region by PCR or an isothermal method, heat to 95 °C for 5 min to separate the strands, and cool fast on ice.
  5. Hybridisation. Apply the single strands in buffer with enough Na⁺ to shield the backbones, at 55 °C (Tm − 5) for this probe.
  6. Stringency wash. Same temperature and salt, or slightly hotter or lower in salt. The mutant target forms a perfect duplex (Tm 60 °C) and stays; the wild-type target has one mismatch (Tm about 55 °C), so about half of it has separated at 55 °C and is washed away; a slightly hotter wash removes more.
  7. Readout by impedance in ferri/ferrocyanide.
    ElectrodeRct (kΩ)Change from 1.20 kΩ
    blank1.265 %
    wild type (mismatch)1.5630 %
    mutant (match)3.00150 %
  8. Alternative readouts. Methylene blue intercalator current after the wash, a ferrocene-tagged signalling probe, or an E-DNA stem-loop whose current falls on binding.
  9. Controls. A blank electrode, a positive control (synthetic mutant target), a negative control (synthetic wild-type target) on the same chip, and a calibrated decision threshold between them, for example 75 %.
  10. Decision. A sample giving 150 % is mutant; 30 % is wild type; a value near 90 % suggests a heterozygous sample with both strands and is repeated.
  11. Why the mutation sits in the middle. A mismatch at the end of the probe leaves most of the duplex intact and changes Tm by only 1–2 °C; in the middle it splits the duplex into two short halves and lowers Tm by about 5 °C, which the wash can use.
  12. Check. Match-to-mismatch ratio = 150/30 = 5.0, enough to separate the two with a single threshold.
Apply A home test uses gold nanoparticles with an antibody sandwich for a cardiac marker. Explain how the LSPR signal arises, why nanoparticles amplify small binding events, and what limits the test. 10 marks

Points to cover. LSPR absorption depends on particle size, shape and spacing; Binding of antigen and second antibody layer changes local refractive index; Shift in wavelength or colour change read by eye or simple optics; Optical readout avoids charge screening by salt; Limits: antibody cross-reactivity, matrix effects in whole blood, batch-to-batch particle size, quantitation accuracy.

Model answer.

  1. Format. A lateral-flow strip: sample pad, conjugate pad with gold nanoparticles carrying antibody 1, a nitrocellulose membrane with a test line of antibody 2 and a control line of anti-species antibody, and an absorbent pad that pulls the liquid along.
  2. How LSPR arises. Light drives the conduction electrons of a 20–40 nm gold particle to oscillate together. At about 520 nm this oscillation resonates, so the particle absorbs and scatters green light strongly and looks red. The peak depends on size, shape and spacing: aggregated particles couple and shift the absorption to longer wavelengths, so the colour moves to blue-purple.
  3. Signal at the line. The cardiac marker (for example troponin) is sandwiched between gold–antibody 1 and the fixed antibody 2, so gold piles up at the test line and the line turns red. The control line captures excess conjugate whatever the sample, showing the strip has run.
  4. Why nanoparticles amplify. Each bound antigen brings a whole particle whose extinction is enormous for its size: one 40 nm gold particle absorbs about as strongly as many thousands of dye molecules. A modest number of binding events therefore gives a line visible by eye, with no instrument.
  5. Optical advantage. The readout is colour, not charge, so it is not limited by Debye screening (about 0.8 nm in serum); the antigen can bind anywhere on the antibody.
  6. Binding limit (illustrative numbers). Troponin I has a mass of about 24 kDa. A level of 50 ng/L is 50 × 10⁻⁹ / 24 000 = 2.1 × 10⁻¹² mol/L. With an assumed Kd of 0.1 nmol/L, the fraction of capture sites filled is θ = 2.1 × 10⁻¹² / (1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ + 2.1 × 10⁻¹²) = 0.020, about 2 %. That is why home cardiac tests are qualitative and only detect clearly raised levels.
  7. Limits. Cross-reactivity of the antibodies; whole-blood matrix (red cells filtered out by the pad, viscosity changing the flow); batch-to-batch particle size changing the colour; the high-dose hook effect, where a very high antigen level saturates both antibodies separately and the line fades; and poor quantitation by eye. A small reader that measures line intensity improves the last point.
  8. Clinical caution. A negative home test does not rule out a heart attack; chest pain still needs hospital assessment.
  9. Check. 2.1 × 10⁻¹²/1.02 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 0.0206.

Analyze A continuous glucose monitor reads 4.2 mmol/L while the fingerstick reads 6.0 mmol/L on day 6 of wear, with the gap widening after meals. Analyse the likely causes and state how calibration and alarms should be handled. 13 marks

Points to cover. Interstitial fluid lag behind blood, exaggerated by rising or falling glucose; Sensor drift and enzyme decay over wear time; Pressure-induced changes in local perfusion at night; Calibration: fingerstick calibration points, time limits on calibration validity; Trend arrows rather than single points; alarms set on rate of change; Accuracy expressed as mean absolute relative difference against reference; Rule that a reading is confirmed with a fingerstick before dosing.

Model answer.

  1. The gap. Sensor 4.2 against finger-stick 6.0 mmol/L: an absolute difference of 1.8 mmol/L, and a relative difference of 1.8/6.0 = 30 %, about three times a typical MARD of under 10 %.
  2. Cause 1: physiological lag. The sensor reads interstitial fluid, which follows blood by about 5–15 min. After a meal glucose rises; if it rises at 0.15 mmol/L per minute, a 12 min lag alone puts the sensor 0.15 × 12 = 1.8 mmol/L low. This fits the gap that widens after meals.
  3. Cause 2: sensor ageing. On day 6 the enzyme has partly lost activity and the membrane has fouled with protein, so the sensitivity has fallen. If the sensitivity has dropped by 10 %, a true 6.0 reads 5.4 even without lag.
  4. Cause 3: local effects. Pressure on the sensor (a compression low, mostly at night) cuts local blood flow; inflammation around the wire consumes glucose. These give falsely low readings independent of meals.
  5. Separating the causes.
    CheckLagAgeingCompression
    gap when glucose steadysmallpersistsonly while pressed
    gap after mealslarge, then closespersistsunrelated
    repeat finger-stick in 20 minagrees betterstill lowagrees after moving
  6. Calibration. Calibrate only when the trend arrow is flat (glucose changing under about 0.05 mmol/L per minute), never right after a meal or insulin. A calibration taken at this moment would wrongly raise the sensor gain by 6.0/4.2 = 1.43 and then over-read for hours. If the steady-state gap stays above about 20 %, replace the sensor.
  7. Alarms. The sensor is near the low alarm (3.9 mmol/L) while blood is normal, so false low alarms will follow. Use rate-of-change and predictive alarms, and confirm any low or any dosing decision with a finger-stick.
  8. Accuracy reporting. MARD over paired readings; for example 9.09, 7.14, 11.11 and 5.00 % average to 8.1 %. Accuracy is worst in the low range, where a fixed error is a larger share.
  9. Conclusion. The widening gap after meals points to lag on top of day-6 sensitivity loss; act on finger-sticks until the trace is steady and agrees.
  10. Day-to-day practice. Most current sensors are factory calibrated; when they allow a finger-stick calibration, it should be entered only with a flat trend arrow and clean, dry fingers, because a sugary finger reads falsely high.
  11. Check. 0.15 × 12 = 1.8 mmol/L, the whole gap.
Evaluate Compare an enzyme electrode, an ISFET and a nanomaterial FET as the basis of a portable urea meter. Judge which you would choose for a clinic and give reasons. 13 marks

Points to cover. Enzyme electrode: urease converts urea to ammonium, amperometric or potentiometric readout, cheap and proven; ISFET: label-free, small, but drift and reference electrode problems; Nanomaterial FET: high sensitivity, but Debye screening in high-salt samples and reproducibility issues; Criteria: detection limit, linear range in urine, drift, sterility, cost per test, calibration burden; Recommendation with a stated trade-off; Controls and calibration plan for the chosen device.

Model answer.

  1. Need. A clinic urea meter for serum (normal about 2.5–7.8 mmol/L, up to 50 or more in kidney failure), simple to run, low cost per test, and stable day to day.
  2. Enzyme electrode. Urease over an ammonium ion-selective electrode (potentiometric) or a pH electrode. Proven, cheap, tolerant of serum. Ideal slope 59.16 mV per decade: a reading 40.0 mV above a 1.0 mmol/L standard gives 1040.0/59.16 = 4.75 mmol/L. Drawbacks: the logarithmic response compresses high values, K⁺ and Na⁺ interfere with an ammonium membrane, and the enzyme needs cold storage.
  3. ISFET (EnFET). Urease on a pH ISFET. Very small, batch-made, fast, suits arrays. Drawbacks: drift of the sensing layer, reference electrode stability, light and temperature sensitivity, and a signal of only millivolts (2.95 mV for 0.05 pH) that depends on the sample buffer capacity.
  4. Nanomaterial FET. Graphene or nanowire channel with urease or a receptor. Very sensitive in low-salt buffer, but in serum the Debye length is about 0.8 nm, so the sample must be diluted about 100 times (Debye length 7.9 nm), and device-to-device reproducibility is still poor.

For a clinic, the urease enzyme electrode: it measures undiluted serum over the whole clinical range, its behaviour and failure modes are well known, and two-point calibration each day is enough. The EnFET is the choice when many analytes must share one tiny chip; the nanomaterial FET is still a research device for this job.
  7. Calibration and controls for the chosen device. Two standards bracketing the range (for example 2.5 and 25 mmol/L), checked every morning; a normal and a high control serum per batch; temperature at 37 °C or compensated; a slope below about 50 mV per decade flags a tired membrane.
  8. Trade-off stated. The choice gives up miniaturisation for robustness and serum compatibility.
  9. Running cost. An electrode with a replaceable urease membrane serves many tests, so the cost per test falls to that of the standards and controls; single-use FET chips must be made very cheaply to compete.

Analyze Explain how Debye screening restricts label-free electrical detection of charged molecules, and analyse three ways to overcome it. 12 marks

Points to cover. Debye length about 0.7–0.8 nm in 150 mM salt; charge beyond it is screened from the electrode; Consequence: large targets and whole cells give weak signal in serum; Strategy: dilute the sample to lengthen Debye length; Strategy: move the binding event close to the surface, using short probes or fragmented targets; Strategy: use an optical or mass readout that ignores charge screening; State cost of each strategy: loss of sample realism or added steps.

Model answer.

  1. What screening is. Ions in a salt solution crowd around any surface charge with opposite sign, so the electric field of a charge dies away over a short distance, the Debye length λD. A FET or electrode senses only charge inside this layer.
  2. Size. For a 1:1 salt at 25 °C, λD ≈ 0.304/√I nm with I in mol/L.
  3. Debye length against ionic strength.
    MediumI (mol/L)λD (nm)
    serum, 150 mM salt0.150.304/0.387 = 0.79
    serum diluted 10×0.0150.304/0.122 = 2.5
    serum diluted 100×0.00150.304/0.0387 = 7.9
    1 mM buffer0.0010.304/0.0316 = 9.6
  4. Consequence. An IgG antibody is about 10–15 nm long. An antigen captured at its tips sits several nanometres from the gate, that is several Debye lengths away in serum, and its charge is almost fully screened. Large proteins, whole cells and long DNA give weak or no direct signal in undiluted samples.
  5. Strategy 1: dilute or desalt. Diluting 100 times lengthens λD ten times, to 7.9 nm. Cost: the target is also diluted 100 times, so the detection limit worsens, and low ionic strength can change binding.
  6. Strategy 2: bring binding closer. Use small receptors instead of whole antibodies: antibody fragments (Fab, about 5 nm), aptamers or short DNA probes, so the bound charge lies within a nanometre or two. Cost: new receptor chemistry and possibly lower affinity.
  7. Strategy 3: change the readout. Use an optical (SPR, fluorescence, gold colour) or mass (quartz crystal) readout that does not depend on charge, or a polymer layer such as PEG that raises the effective screening length at the surface. Cost: more optics or added layers and steps.
  8. Other options. High-frequency impedance readout, or a labelled sandwich that brings a charged or redox label to the surface; both add steps.
  9. Judgement. Strategy 2 keeps the sample realistic and the device simple, so it is the usual first choice; dilution is the quickest laboratory fix.
  10. Worked check of strategy 2. If a 5 nm Fab fragment replaces a 12 nm IgG, the bound antigen moves from about 12 nm to about 5 nm from the gate; in tenfold diluted serum (λD 2.5 nm) that is two Debye lengths instead of about five, so much more of its charge is felt.
  11. Check. √0.0015 = 0.0387, and 0.304/0.0387 = 7.85 nm, ten times 0.785 nm.
Evaluate Judge labelled versus label-free DNA detection for a fever panel that must run without a thermocycler. 10 marks

Points to cover. Labelled route: redox tag, intercalator or fluorophore; higher signal, extra washing steps; Sandwich format for longer targets and extra specificity; Label-free route: impedance or FET; simpler chemistry, more sensitive to matrix; E-DNA stem-loop gives reagentless, reusable sensing but single-step kinetics; Throughput, cost per test in rupees, shelf life, need for controls; Verdict tied to the stated requirement of no thermocycler.

Model answer.

  1. Requirement. Detect several fever pathogens (for example dengue, malaria, typhoid) from a blood sample without a thermocycler. Amplification must therefore be isothermal: LAMP at about 60–65 °C or RPA at about 37–42 °C, both run on a simple heater.
  2. Labelled route. A redox tag (ferrocene, methylene blue), an intercalator or a fluorophore reports the duplex. Signal is large and specific; a sandwich with a capture probe and a labelled detector probe adds a second sequence check. Cost: extra reagents and wash steps, and labels with limited shelf life.
  3. Label-free route. Impedance or FET readout of the duplex itself. Simpler chemistry and fewer steps, but the signal is small, it is sensitive to the sample matrix and non-specific adsorption, and FETs suffer Debye screening (0.8 nm in serum).
  4. E-DNA stem-loop. Reagentless and reusable: binding opens the stem and the current falls. It is signal-off, so probe loss looks like a positive unless the current is normalised.

  6. Amplification makes the difference. LAMP makes about 10⁹ copies in under an hour, so the target is far above the picomolar limit of either readout, and specificity rather than sensitivity decides. LAMP also makes non-specific products, which a sandwich with a second probe rejects better than a single label-free probe.
  7. Controls. Each panel carries a blank, a positive control for each pathogen and a human-gene control that proves the sample was amplified.

Glossary

Affinity constant
Measure of how tightly an antibody binds its antigen; tighter binding lowers the detection limit but slows release.
Amperometric sensor
Sensor that holds the electrode at a fixed potential and measures the resulting current.
Analyte
The specific substance the biosensor is built to measure.
Biocompatibility
Ability of an implanted sensor to stay in the body without harming tissue or being fouled by it.
Biofouling
Build-up of protein and cells on a sensor surface that blocks the analyte and reduces signal over time.
Charge screening
Reduction of the effect of a surface charge by ions in the surrounding solution.
Enzyme activity
Amount of substrate converted per unit time under stated conditions, usually in units per milligram.
First-generation biosensor
Enzyme sensor that measures the natural substrate or product, such as oxygen or hydrogen peroxide.
Impedance spectroscopy
Technique that measures how the electrode–solution interface resists and stores charge over a range of frequencies.
Ion-selective membrane
Thin film that lets one ion pass or binds it, producing a charge at the surface of an ISFET or electrode.
Lateral flow strip
Paper-based test where labelled particles move by capillary action and are captured on a test line.
MARD
Mean absolute relative difference, the usual accuracy measure for a continuous glucose monitor.
Mediator
Small redox molecule that shuttles electrons from an enzyme to the electrode, allowing a lower operating potential.
Nafion membrane
Permselective polymer film that lets small molecules through while blocking anions such as ascorbate.
Nernst equation
Relation giving electrode potential as a function of ion activity, about 59 mV per decade at 25 °C for a one-electron change.
Potentiometric sensor
Sensor that measures voltage at almost zero current, usually with an ion-selective membrane.
Probe
Short single-stranded nucleic acid of known sequence fixed on the sensor surface.
Redox label
Electroactive group such as ferrocene or methylene blue whose proximity to the electrode changes the measured current.
Reference electrode
Electrode of known, stable potential, usually Ag/AgCl, against which the sensing electrode is measured.
Sandwich assay
Format where a capture probe binds the target and a second labelled probe binds a different site on the same target.
Second-generation biosensor
Enzyme sensor that uses a mediator or redox dye to transfer electrons instead of relying on oxygen.
Stringency
Combination of temperature, salt and detergent in a wash that removes mismatched duplexes while keeping perfect matches.
Third-generation biosensor
Sensor in which the enzyme transfers electrons directly to the electrode without a mediator.
Working electrode
Electrode where the reaction of interest is driven and the current is measured.
