4.1 Biosensor concept, components and classification

A biosensor is an analytical device in which a biological recognition layer sits in close contact with a transducer. The bioreceptor binds or reacts with one analyte; the transducer converts that event into current, voltage, light or mass change; electronics amplify, filter and display a number. It exists because a plain pH electrode cannot pick glucose out of blood. It is used in glucose strips, pregnancy tests, DNA probes, food checks and water monitoring. No reading is trusted until the device is calibrated.

You need: school chemistry, Ohm's law, and the electrode and amplifier basics from Unit 1.

The biosensor chain

A biosensor is an analytical device. A biological recognition element, or bioreceptor, sits in close contact with a transducer. The bioreceptor is selective: an enzyme catalyses one reaction, an antibody binds one antigen, a DNA probe pairs with one sequence. The transducer converts binding or reaction into current, voltage, light or mass change proportional to analyte concentration. The analyte is the substance measured, such as glucose in blood.

A plain chemical sensor is not a biosensor. A glass pH electrode or ion-selective electrode has no biological layer. Its selectivity comes from a glass or ion-selective membrane, not from a biomolecule. It measures H⁺; it cannot separate glucose from other sugars.

The chain is analyte → bioreceptor → transducer → signal conditioning → display. Signal conditioning means amplification and filtering. Display reads mg/dL or mmol/L. The bioreceptor must touch the transducer, or product diffuses away and signal falls. The transducer may be an electrode, field-effect transistor, optical fibre, piezoelectric crystal or thermistor.

Classification and generations

Two classifications matter. First, by bioreceptor: enzyme (catalyses a reaction), antibody (binds antigen), nucleic acid (DNA or RNA probe), cell (whole cell or tissue), and aptamer (short synthetic nucleic acid). Some syllabi call antibodies, aptamers and DNA probes affinity elements. See bio-recognition elements.

Second, by transducer: amperometric (current at fixed potential), potentiometric (voltage at near-zero current), conductometric (conductance), optical (absorbance or fluorescence), piezoelectric (mass shifts frequency) and calorimetric (heat). Each has different electronics; see electrochemical transducers and ISFET.

Enzyme biosensors come in three generations. The first uses a natural acceptor, usually oxygen, and measures oxygen used or hydrogen peroxide made. The second uses a small redox mediator, a molecule that shuttles electrons between enzyme and electrode; it lowers operating potential and interference. The third uses direct electron transfer from enzyme to electrode, with no mediator; only some enzymes allow this.

Calibration, characteristics and standard addition

Desired characteristics: reliability (same sample, same answer), simplicity, cost in rupees per test, sensitivity, selectivity, dynamic range (lowest to highest usable concentration), response time (time to 90% of final reading), drift, and operating conditions such as pH, temperature and ionic strength. Sensitivity is the slope of the calibration curve: signal per unit concentration. Selectivity is response to the analyte over similar molecules. Drift is slow output change at fixed sample.

A calibration curve plots signal against known concentration. Fit signal = S c + b, where S is slope, c concentration, b intercept. Blank noise σ is the standard deviation of repeated blank readings. Signal-to-background ratio is analyte signal divided by blank signal; higher is better. A positive control has known analyte; a negative control has matrix with no analyte. Handle blood and serum as infectious: gloves, no mouth pipetting, sharps bin.

LOD = 3σ/S

LOD is detection limit in concentration units; σ is standard deviation of blank readings in signal units; S is sensitivity, the calibration slope in signal units per concentration unit.

Matrix is everything except the analyte; it can suppress or enhance signal. Standard addition handles this: measure sample, add a known small volume of standard, measure again, and use the increase. Correct for the added volume, because it dilutes the original. Applications include medicine (glucose strips, DNA probes), agriculture (pesticides, soil nitrate), bioproduction (fermentation) and environment (water checks). The market is dominated by disposable glucose strips, a few tens of rupees each. Complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) arrays put many sensors on one chip and read many analytes at once; cross-talk and calibration are the limits. See blood glucose sensors.

Worked example

Question. A 5.00 mL diluted serum sample gives a current of 4.0 µA. You add 0.100 mL of a 100 mmol/L glucose standard and the current rises to 7.0 µA. Find the glucose concentration in the cell, the sensitivity, and the original undiluted concentration in mmol/L and mg/dL. The serum was diluted 1:4 with diluent (one part serum plus four parts diluent), a dilution factor of 5.

  1. Spike: 0.100 mL × 100 mmol/L = 0.0100 mmol = 10.0 µmol.
  2. Let c be sample concentration in the cell in mmol/L and k the sensitivity in µA per mmol/L. Before the spike: kc = 4.0 µA.
  3. After the spike, volume = 5.00 + 0.100 = 5.10 mL. Total analyte = 5.00c + 10.0 µmol, so cell concentration = (5c + 10)/5.10 mmol/L. Then k(5c + 10)/5.10 = 7.0 µA.
  4. Divide by kc = 4.0: (5c + 10)/(5.10c) = 1.75. Expand: 5c + 10 = 8.925c. c = 10/(8.925 − 5) = 2.5478 mmol/L ≈ 2.55 mmol/L.
  5. Sensitivity: k = 4.0/2.5478 = 1.570 µA per (mmol/L) ≈ 1.57.
  6. Dilution factor 5: original = 5 × 2.5478 = 12.739 mmol/L ≈ 12.74 mmol/L. In mg/dL: 12.739 × 18 = 229.3 mg/dL, about 229 mg/dL.

Answer. Cell glucose = 2.55 mmol/L; sensitivity = 1.57 µA per (mmol/L); original serum = 12.74 mmol/L = 229.3 mg/dL (about 229 mg/dL), a diabetic value.

Exam traps

  • A plain glass pH electrode or ion-selective electrode is not a biosensor; it has no biological recognition layer.
  • Sensitivity is the slope, in µA per mmol/L or mV per decade. Detection limit is a concentration, found from 3σ/S. Do not report one when asked for the other.
  • In standard addition, the added standard changes the total volume; ignoring the dilution gives a high result.
  • First generation uses oxygen, second uses a redox mediator, third uses direct electron transfer. Do not mix the order.
  • Optical and piezoelectric biosensors are not electrochemical; do not call every biosensor amperometric.

4.2 Bio-recognition elements: enzymes, antibodies, nucleic acids

A biosensor must pick one molecule out of a blood sample full of others, and the bio-recognition element does that picking. Enzymes catalyse one substrate, antibodies bind one antigen, nucleic acid probes pair with one complementary sequence. The element's kinetics or binding constant sets the sensor's range and detection limit. Glucometer strips, pregnancy tests and DNA test cards in Indian labs all rest on this layer. This chapter covers the three families, the numbers that describe them, and how they are fixed to a transducer.

You need: 4.1 for the component chain.

Enzymes: catalysis sets the range

An enzyme is a protein catalyst. It binds one substrate at its active site, converts it, and is released unchanged, so one molecule cycles many times. Activity is quoted in international units: 1 U = 1 µmol of substrate converted per minute. Glucose oxidase, used on every glucose strip, oxidises β-D-glucose and is nearly blind to other sugars.

v = Vmax·S/(Km + S)

v is the rate (µmol min⁻¹ per mg of enzyme); Vmax is the rate when every site is occupied; S is substrate concentration; Km is the substrate concentration giving v = Vmax/2.

Km measures how tightly the enzyme holds its substrate: small Km means tight binding and early saturation. At S = 19·Km, v = 0.95·Vmax. Above that, doubling S barely changes v, so the sensor cannot resolve two high concentrations; the useful range ends well below 19·Km. On an electrode the enzyme is immobilised, substrate must diffuse through the layer, and the apparent Km usually rises.

An unfavourable equilibrium is pulled forward by removing product continuously. An enzyme electrode does exactly this by oxidising the product at the electrode surface.

Antibodies and nucleic acids: binding sets selectivity

An antibody is not a catalyst. It binds an antigen and stays bound; it is neither consumed nor turned over. Affinity is described by the dissociation constant Kd = [antibody][antigen]/[complex]; smaller Kd means tighter binding. A whole IgG has two sites, so multivalent avidity can be far stronger than one site's Kd suggests. Detection is label-free when binding itself changes mass or charge on the transducer, and labelled when a fluorescent or enzyme tag reports the event.

A nucleic acid probe is a short single strand, for example a 20-base oligomer. It finds its target by Watson–Crick pairing: A with T, G with C. Hybridisation strengthens as G+C content rises (three hydrogen bonds per G–C pair against two for A–T) and as salt concentration rises, which shields the negative phosphate backbones; it weakens as temperature rises. The melting temperature Tm is where half the duplexes have separated. Cells and chemoreception serve as recognition layers in whole-cell sensors.

Immobilisation methods: adsorption is simple but leaks; covalent coupling is stable; gel entrapment holds whole cells and enzymes; self-assembled monolayers of thiols on gold give an ordered, reproducible surface.

Worked example

Question. A glucose-oxidase electrode has Vmax = 120 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹ and Km = 5.0 mmol/L. (a) Find v at S = 2.0 mmol/L. (b) Show that S = 5.0 mmol/L gives half of Vmax. (c) Find the concentration giving 95% of Vmax. (d) On the immobilised strip the apparent Km is 12 mmol/L; state the new half-saturation level.

  1. Write Michaelis–Menten: v = Vmax·S/(Km + S).
  2. At S = 2.0 mmol/L: numerator 120 × 2.0 = 240; denominator 5.0 + 2.0 = 7.0; v = 240/7.0 = 34.3 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹.
  3. At S = 5.0 mmol/L: v = 120 × 5.0/10.0 = 60.0 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹, exactly Vmax/2, which is the definition of Km.
  4. 95% of Vmax needs S = 19·Km = 19 × 5.0 = 95 mmol/L.
  5. On the surface the half-saturation point moves to the apparent Km = 12 mmol/L, so the same enzyme saturates later.

Answer. (a) 34.3 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹, (b) 60.0 µmol min⁻¹ mg⁻¹ = Vmax/2, (c) 95 mmol/L, (d) 12 mmol/L.

Exam traps

  • An antibody is not a catalyst: it binds without turnover, so Vmax and Km do not apply to it.
  • Km is a concentration in mmol/L, not a rate; the rate units belong to Vmax.
  • Smaller Kd means tighter binding; a large Kd is the weak antibody.
  • The Km measured in free solution is not the apparent Km of the immobilised layer.

4.3 Electrochemical transducers and enzyme-based biosensors

An electrochemical transducer turns a chemical event into an electrical signal. In an enzyme biosensor, the enzyme consumes its target and makes a product; the electrode oxidises or reduces that product, and the current or voltage carries the concentration. Amperometric enzyme electrodes are the workhorse of blood glucose strips and many laboratory analysers. Potentiometric and conductometric versions cover pH, urea and electrolyte tests. This chapter shows the three-electrode cell, the glucose oxidase chain, the read-out electronics, and how planar electrodes are made.

You need 4.1 for the biosensor chain and Unit 1 for op-amps and data acquisition.

The three-electrode cell and the three measurement modes

An electrochemical cell needs two electrodes, but a biosensor usually uses three. The working electrode (WE) is where the reaction of interest happens; it is platinum, gold or carbon. The reference electrode (RE) is Ag/AgCl in 3 M KCl. It holds a fixed potential and passes almost no current. The counter electrode (CE), often platinum, carries the current that balances the WE. A potentiostat keeps the WE at a set potential against the RE by driving the CE.

The three measurement modes differ in what is held and what is read. In amperometric mode the potential is fixed and current is measured; current rises with the flux of electroactive product. In potentiometric mode the current is held at zero and voltage is measured; the voltage follows the Nernst equation. In conductometric mode a small AC voltage is applied and conductance is measured.

ModeHeldMeasuredTypical use
AmperometricFixed potentialCurrentGlucose, lactate
PotentiometricZero currentVoltagepH, urea, K⁺
ConductometricSmall AC voltageConductanceUrea, cell culture

E = E° + (RT / nF) ln a

E is the cell potential in volts; E° is the standard potential; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is absolute temperature in K; n is the number of electrons; F = 96485 C/mol; a is the dimensionless activity of the ion (its concentration relative to the standard state, approximately the concentration in mol/L for dilute solutions), so the logarithm has no units. At 25 °C the slope is about 59 mV per decade for n = 1.

Why three electrodes? In a two-electrode cell, current through the reference electrode polarises it and the set potential drifts. The potentiostat draws almost no current through the RE, so the setpoint stays stable. That stability is why pH meters and urea sensors can trust a millivolt reading.

The glucose oxidase amperometric chain

Glucose oxidase (GOx) is the enzyme. Its reaction is glucose + O₂ → gluconolactone + H₂O₂. GOx has a flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) cofactor that takes two electrons from glucose. The enzyme is selective because other sugars fit the active site poorly. The enzyme layer is immobilised over the working electrode.

In the common amperometric route, hydrogen peroxide is oxidised at the WE held at about +0.7 V vs Ag/AgCl: H₂O₂ → O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻. Two electrons arrive per glucose molecule. The current is proportional to the glucose flux reaching the enzyme layer. A planar strip uses this chemistry; see 4.5 for the disposable version.

A second option measures the oxygen consumed with a Clark electrode. Oxygen is reduced at about −0.6 V vs Ag/AgCl. As glucose rises, oxygen falls, so the current falls. That sign is easy to miss. Mediators such as ferrocene and ferricyanide shuttle electrons from GOx to the electrode, so the electrode can run at +0.2 to +0.4 V instead of the +0.7 V needed to oxidise H₂O₂ directly. Working at the lower potential reduces, but does not remove, the false current from easily oxidised interferents such as ascorbic acid and uric acid.

Faraday’s law, read-out electronics and planar fabrication

I = n F J A

I is current in A; n is electrons per molecule (2 for glucose); F = 96485 C/mol; J is flux in mol cm⁻² s⁻¹; A is electrode area in cm².

The transimpedance amplifier converts current to voltage. It is an op-amp with feedback resistor Rf. With Rf between 100 kΩ and 1 MΩ, a 1 µA current gives 0.1 V to 1 V (Vout = −I × Rf for the inverting connection; only the magnitude is used in read-out). Choose Rf so that the largest expected current still gives an output inside the amplifier's swing. An RC low-pass filter removes noise, an ADC digitises the voltage, and the display shows mmol/L. Full data-acquisition hardware belongs to Unit 1.

Temperature changes enzyme activity and electrode slope. A temperature sensor and a Wheatstone bridge provide on-chip compensation. Resistors, diodes and transistors appear here only as circuit parts; their device physics is not the subject of this chapter. Fabrication starts with photolithography to define the pattern, sputtering to deposit Pt or Au, and lift-off to remove unwanted metal. Micromachining shapes wells and channels. Self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) anchor the enzyme and reduce fouling.

Worked example

Question. A planar glucose electrode has area A = 0.020 cm². Glucose reaches the enzyme layer at a steady flux J = 5.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ mol cm⁻² s⁻¹. Each glucose yields n = 2 electrons, and F = 96485 C/mol. (a) Find the current. (b) If the transimpedance amplifier has Rf = 1.0 MΩ, find Vout. (c) Explain why Rf = 100 kΩ is a better choice for a 3 V supply.

  1. Write Faraday’s law for an electrode: I = n F J A.
  2. Compute nF = 2 × 96485 = 192970 C/mol.
  3. Multiply by flux: 192970 × 5.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 9.65 × 10⁻⁵ A per cm².
  4. Multiply by area: 9.65 × 10⁻⁵ × 0.020 = 1.93 × 10⁻⁶ A = 1.93 µA.
  5. With Rf = 1.0 MΩ: Vout = 1.93 × 10⁻⁶ A × 1.0 × 10⁶ Ω = 1.93 V.
  6. With Rf = 100 kΩ: Vout = 1.93 × 10⁻⁶ A × 1.0 × 10⁵ Ω = 0.193 V.
  7. 1.93 V is not too close to the 3 V rail for the op-amp itself, but it leaves little room for the output to rise when glucose (and so current) increases above the calibration point. With Rf = 100 kΩ the quiescent output is 0.193 V, so the amplifier stays in range over a wider glucose span before it saturates.

Answer. The current is 1.93 µA; with 1.0 MΩ Vout is 1.93 V, and with 100 kΩ it is 0.193 V, so 100 kΩ is the practical choice.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting that a measured current needs a stated reference electrode and applied potential. A bare current value is not a calibrated measurement.
  • Mixing up the sign: oxidation at the working electrode gives an anodic positive current, while the Clark oxygen sensor current falls as glucose rises.
  • Calling the counter electrode the reference electrode. The RE passes almost no current; the CE carries the current.
  • Using n = 1 for the peroxide route. One glucose gives two electrons at the working electrode, so n = 2.
  • Using area in m² with flux in mol cm⁻² s⁻¹. Convert to cm² first, or the current will be off by 10⁴.

4.4 ISFET and IMFET

An ion-sensitive field-effect transistor, or ISFET, is a MOSFET with no metal gate. The sample and an ion-selective membrane sit where the gate was, and bound ions change the surface charge. That change shifts the threshold voltage, so a chemical concentration appears as an electrical signal. ISFETs measure pH in small volumes; enzyme-coated EnFETs measure penicillin, glucose or urea; antibody-coated IMFETs detect antigens without a label. They are used in portable meters, lab-on-chip systems and implantable chemical sensing.

You need: <a href='#electrochemical-transducers-and-enzyme-based-biosensors'>4.3 for potentiometric transduction, and the MOSFET ideas of gate, threshold voltage and subthreshold swing from basic electronics.

Structure and the charge-to-current path

An ISFET is a MOSFET (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor) with the metal gate removed. Source and drain are n⁺ (heavily doped n-type) regions in p-silicon, with a channel between them. The gate stack is a thin silicon dioxide layer plus silicon nitride (Si₃N₄) or tantalum oxide (Ta₂O₅), exposed to the sample electrolyte. A reference electrode, usually Ag/AgCl, sits in the sample and completes the circuit. For ion selectivity, an ion-selective membrane covers the gate. The threshold voltage V_T is the gate voltage at which the channel just forms; drain current I_D flows from drain to source when V_T is exceeded.

Ions bind to surface sites such as SiOH or TaOH. The bound charge changes the surface potential. The surface potential adds in series with the applied gate voltage, and the electrolyte double-layer capacitance divides with the gate capacitance, so only part of the surface potential appears across the channel and shifts V_T. At fixed gate voltage, I_D changes. The site-binding model describes the surface charge against pH; only its name is needed here. The device is small, fast and batch-fabricated, and its low output impedance suits electrical readout.

pH ISFET, EnFET and the Nernst slope

At 25 °C the pH response follows the Nernst slope: 59.16 mV per pH unit. Run the ISFET at constant drain current with a feedback gate. The feedback adjusts the gate voltage V_G to hold I_D fixed. V_G then follows the surface potential, so the ISFET becomes a pH meter with an electrical output. If the gate voltage is held fixed instead, the drain current changes; in the subthreshold region the change is set by the subthreshold swing, typically 60–80 mV per decade of current. Temperature must be known because the Nernst slope is proportional to absolute temperature.

ΔVG = (2.303RT/F) × ΔpH = 59.16 mV × ΔpH at 25 °C

ΔVG is the gate-voltage shift; ΔpH is the pH change; R is 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is absolute temperature in K; F is 96485 C mol⁻¹; the sign is set by surface charge and feedback polarity.

An enzyme field-effect transistor (EnFET) is the same device with an enzyme layer over the gate. The enzyme reaction changes local pH or charge. Penicillinase on penicillin produces penicilloic acid; glucose oxidase on glucose produces gluconic acid; urease on urea produces ammonia and raises pH. The enzyme layer sets selectivity, and its kinetics set range and detection limit (see <a href='#bio-recognition-elements-enzymes-antibodies-nucleic-acids'>4.2 and <a href='#blood-glucose-sensors-and-diabetes-management'>4.5).

IMFET and the Debye-length limit

An immunologically sensitive field-effect transistor (IMFET) is an ISFET with an antibody immobilised on the gate or in a membrane over it. Antigen binding adds charge near the surface. If that charge lies within the Debye length, it changes the surface potential and shifts V_T. Detection is label-free: no fluorescent or radioactive tag is needed. The Debye length is the distance over which counterions screen charge; it is about 1 nm in physiological salt, so charge farther away is screened. Undiluted serum therefore gives a weak direct signal; dilute the sample or use a membrane that brings binding close to the surface. A neutral antigen gives little direct charge signal; a charged sandwich label can help but is no longer strictly label-free.

Readout uses a constant-current or constant-voltage circuit. A differential pair has one sensing ISFET and one reference ISFET without antibody, or with inactive antibody. Subtraction removes common drift, temperature and light effects. CMOS (complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor) integration puts many ISFETs and amplifiers on one chip for multi-ion or multi-antibody arrays.

Fabrication and practical limits

Fabrication follows standard CMOS steps.

  1. Oxidation grows the gate oxide on p-silicon.
  2. Deposit the Si₃N₄ (LPCVD) or Ta₂O₅ sensing layer on the gate oxide before photolithography defines the gate windows.
  3. Photolithography defines source, drain and gate windows.
  4. Etching opens those windows.
  5. Diffusion or ion implantation forms the n⁺ source and drain; annealing follows.
  6. Metallisation deposits aluminium or gold contacts.
  7. Encapsulation covers everything except the gate window with epoxy, polyimide or silicon nitride; the ion-selective membrane is added last.

Practical limits are drift, hysteresis, light sensitivity and reference-electrode instability. Drift is a slow change in V_T from hydration or ion migration. Hysteresis makes the reading depend on the previous pH. Light creates carriers in silicon and shifts the current. The reference electrode's drift adds directly to V_G, so it must be stable. Temperature control matters because the Nernst slope changes with T. The gate window is the only place where the gate insulator touches the sample; everything else is sealed by epoxy, polyimide or nitride.

Worked example

Question. A pH ISFET has a Nernst slope of 59.16 mV per pH unit at 25 °C and a subthreshold swing of 70 mV per decade. The sample pH falls from 7.4 to 7.1.   3. At constant V_G, use S = 70 mV/decade: I_D ratio = 10^(|ΔV|/S) = 10^(17.7/70) = 10^0.253 = 1.79.
  4. So I_D rises by about 1.8 times at fixed V_G; the sign follows the surface-charge direction.
  5. For (c), q = 1.602×10−19 C, and ΔV = q/C. For a 10 µm × 10 µm gate with 10 nm oxide, C ≈ 0.35 pF, so ΔV ≈ 0.5 µV. Smaller gates give larger shifts, up to a few millivolts for nanoscale devices. Exam traps

  • Sign slip: at constant current the feedback gate moves to compensate; do not say the drain current changes by 59 mV/pH.
  • Mixing readouts: the Nernst slope applies to gate voltage at constant current; the subthreshold swing applies to current at constant gate voltage.
  • Ignoring Debye screening in IMFET: undiluted serum screens charge beyond about 1 nm; binding must be close to the surface.
  • Treating ISFET as a glass electrode: it has no glass bulb and needs a stable reference electrode; reference drift adds to V_G.
  • Forgetting drift, hysteresis, light and temperature: all shift V_T or the slope; a single calibration point is not enough.

4.5 Blood glucose sensors and diabetes management

Blood glucose sensors answer one question many times a day: how much glucose is in this drop of blood, or in the fluid under the skin? The disposable strip is the amperometric enzyme electrode made cheap enough to throw away. Continuous monitors turn the same chemistry into a 24-hour trace. People with diabetes use both to dose insulin, catch hypoglycaemia and see how food and exercise move their glucose. This chapter shows how the strip works, what the numbers mean, and where the errors come from.

You need the amperometric cell from 4.3 and calibration ideas from 4.1.

The strip is a disposable amperometric cell

A glucose strip is a plastic rectangle with a capillary channel. Touch the channel to a blood drop and suction fills it. At the far end sit a working electrode and a reference electrode, printed on the plastic. Dried glucose oxidase (GOx) and a mediator cover the working electrode.

The meter applies a fixed potential and reads current after a few seconds. Current is proportional to glucose. Fill-detection contacts tell the meter the channel is full, so the timer starts only then. This is the amperometric enzyme electrode from 4.3, made disposable.

GOx oxidises glucose to gluconolactone. In early strips oxygen accepted the electrons, and the meter measured either the hydrogen peroxide produced or the oxygen consumed. That made the reading depend on blood oxygen, which changes in illness, at altitude and in poor circulation. A mediator such as ferricyanide (Fe(CN)₆³⁻) or an osmium complex now carries electrons from the enzyme to the electrode. The mediator is oxidised at a lower potential, so fewer substances interfere and the strip reads faster.

Cut-offs, units and the plasma question

Most clinics use these cut-offs: fasting plasma glucose 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or more; 2-hour OGTT 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or more; random glucose 200 mg/dL or more with symptoms; HbA1c 6.5% or more. Hypoglycaemia is below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L). The two units convert as below.

glucose (mmol/L) = glucose (mg/dL) ÷ 18

glucose (mg/dL) is the concentration in milligrams per decilitre; glucose (mmol/L) is the same concentration in millimoles per litre.

A meter reads whole blood from a finger. A laboratory usually reports plasma. Plasma glucose is about 11% higher than whole blood, because red cells have less water per unit volume and glucose dissolves in water. Many meters report plasma-equivalent values, but haematocrit still shifts the result: anaemia can read high and polycythaemia can read low. Compare a meter with a lab value only after allowing for this.

HbA1c is not a strip reading. It is a lab measure of average glucose over the past two to three months.

CGM, error sources and the dosing loop

A continuous glucose monitor (CGM) puts a glucose oxidase wire under the skin and measures interstitial fluid. It sends a current every 1–5 minutes. Interstitial glucose lags blood glucose by several minutes, longer when glucose is changing fast. Calibrate with a finger-stick reading when the trace is steady.

Accuracy is reported as MARD, the mean absolute relative difference between sensor and reference readings. Lower is better; many sensors today are below 10%. The trend arrow shows direction and speed, so a reading of 110 mg/dL falling fast is not the same as 110 mg/dL steady.

Error sources: haematocrit; ascorbic acid and uric acid, which oxidise at the electrode and add current; temperature, which changes enzyme rate and diffusion; altitude; expired strips; and reuse. A compression low happens at night when the body presses on the sensor, reducing local blood flow; it reads low without real hypoglycaemia. Confirm with a finger-stick.

The management loop is simple. Self-monitoring gives the number. Correction dose = (current glucose − target glucose) ÷ insulin sensitivity factor (ISF). Meal dose = carbohydrate grams ÷ carbohydrate ratio (CR). A strip costs roughly ₹10–₹50 in India, so cost matters. Reusing a strip or sharing a meter is unsafe: blood-borne infection and strip degradation both follow.

4.6 DNA biosensors and sequence electrodes

A DNA biosensor answers one narrow question: does this sample contain a strand with a known sequence? It holds the complementary strand, the probe, on an electrode and lets the target hybridise to it. The duplex formed is then read as a current or an impedance change. The same trick serves pathogen detection, mutation screening, forensic samples and food authenticity checks, and it is the reason a single wrong base can be caught.

You need: 4.2 for nucleic acid probes and hybridisation, and 4.3 for electrode transduction.

The probe electrode

The sensor carries the complementary strand, the probe, fixed on the transducer. The probe is a single synthetic strand of 15–30 bases. Shorter than 15 and the duplex is too weak, so near-matches bind too; longer than 30 and one wrong base changes the stability too little to see.

Anchoring uses a thiol group (–SH) at the probe's 5' end. Clean gold dipped in probe solution forms Au–S bonds, and the strands pack into a self-assembled monolayer (SAM): one molecule thick, held by the sulphur head, bases free in the buffer.

  1. Polish the gold electrode, rinse with water and dry.
  2. Immerse in 0.1–1 µM thiolated probe in buffer for 1–16 hours.
  3. Rinse, then backfill with 1 mM 6-mercapto-1-hexanol for about one hour.
  4. Rinse, store wet at 4 °C and use within days.

The backfill thiol, MCH, fills bare gold between probes. It stands the probes upright instead of letting them lie flat, spaces them so a target can reach in, and blocks the metal so redox molecules in solution cannot touch it and add background current. A block of ten thymine bases in the probe spaces chemically instead.

Hybridisation, Tm and stringency

Sample DNA arrives double-stranded. Heat it to 95 °C for five minutes and cool it fast on ice; the strands separate and stay apart, and these single strands are added to the electrode. Bases then pair by Watson–Crick rules: A with T through two hydrogen bonds, G with C through three. A G+C-rich duplex is therefore more stable than an A+T-rich one of the same length.

Tm = 2(A + T) + 4(G + C)

A, T, G, C are the counts of each base in the probe (not percentages); Tm is the melting temperature in °C, at which half the duplex has separated.

Salt matters. Na⁺ ions shield the negative phosphate backbones, which otherwise repel each other, so low salt destabilises the duplex. Formamide, a denaturant, lowers Tm and lets an assay run cooler. The Wallace rule above is a counting rule for perfect-match probes of about 14–20 bases in high-salt buffer; for longer probes, locked nucleic acids or mixed solvents, use a nearest-neighbour calculation.

The wash gives selectivity. After hybridisation, wash the electrode at Tm − 5 °C in the same buffer. A perfectly matched target stays bound; a target with one internal mismatch has a Tm about 5 °C lower, melts off and is carried away. This strictness is stringency, set by temperature and salt together. Wash too cold or too salty and mismatches survive, giving a false positive.

Reading the duplex: label-free and labelled

A duplex changes the electrode surface, and several transducers can report it.

  • Impedance: the negative backbone of the duplex repels ferri/ferrocyanide ions, so charge-transfer resistance rises as duplex forms.
  • ISFET: the extra surface charge shifts the threshold voltage (see 4.4).
  • Surface plasmon resonance: the added mass changes the refractive index at a gold film, read as an angle shift.

Labelled routes give a bigger signal for extra steps. A redox-active intercalator such as methylene blue is added after hybridisation; it binds only duplex DNA, so more duplex means more bound dye and a larger current after rinsing. A ferrocene-tagged target carries its own electroactive group only when it hybridises. An enzyme label such as horseradish peroxidase turns one binding event into many electrons through substrate turnover. The sandwich format uses a capture probe on the electrode plus a labelled detector probe binding a second site on the target, which adds specificity but needs a longer target.

The stem-loop (E-DNA) probe is a neat alternative. Its two ends are self-complementary and pair with each other, holding a redox tag close to the electrode so current flows freely. Target binding opens the stem, the tag swings away and the current falls. That is a signal-off sensor: probe loss or damage lowers the current exactly as target does, so always normalise.

In clean buffer a label-free electrode reaches picomolar. A real sample — blood, sputum, a food rinse — holds femtomolar target among far more non-target DNA, so most assays amplify first with PCR or an isothermal method such as LAMP or RPA.

Uses and controls

Pathogen detection covers tuberculosis, dengue and Salmonella in food and water. Mutation screening covers known single-base changes, where the stringency wash is the entire method. Forensic work uses short mitochondrial or repeat-region sequences from tiny degraded samples. Food authenticity checks identify species in meat and fish.

Every assay runs three electrodes: a blank, a perfect-match positive and a single-mismatch negative. A strong current alone proves nothing; it must exceed the mismatch signal by a margin you have calibrated (4.1).

4.7 Quantum-dot and nanomaterial biosensors

At the nanoscale, a particle has a large surface for its volume, and its optical and electronic behaviour can depend on its size. A few molecules binding to a quantum dot or a gold nanoparticle can change light emission, colour, or current. This makes nanomaterial biosensors sensitive without complex optics. They appear in rapid antigen strips, research assays, and point-of-care tests. The same physics also sets their limits: charge screening, toxicity, and batch-to-batch variation.

You need 4.2 for bio-recognition elements and 4.4 for the FET and Debye-length idea.

Quantum dots: size sets colour

A quantum dot is a semiconductor crystal 2–10 nm across. A common label uses a CdSe core with a ZnS shell. The core absorbs light and emits light; the shell passivates surface states and reduces leaching. In a crystal this small, the electron and hole are confined. Their allowed energies become discrete. The band gap rises as the dot shrinks. This is quantum confinement. A small dot emits blue; a larger dot emits red. Absorption is broad because many transitions above the band edge are allowed. Emission is narrow because it comes from the lowest confined state. The dot is also photostable, so it can be excited for long times without bleaching like many dyes.

E = 1 / (1 + (r / R0)6)

E is the fraction of donor energy transferred; r is the donor–acceptor distance in nm; R0 is the Förster radius in nm, the distance where E = 0.5, usually 2–6 nm for such pairs.

As a label, the dot is coated with antibody or DNA. Binding is detected by its emission. As a FRET donor, the dot transfers energy to a nearby acceptor dye. The sixth power makes E fall steeply: a small increase in r turns transfer off. This reports binding and unbinding without a wash step. Cadmium is toxic. The ZnS shell reduces exposure, but broken dots and waste need controlled disposal.

Gold nanoparticles and the red line

A gold nanoparticle of 20–40 nm has a strong optical resonance called localised surface plasmon resonance, LSPR. Light makes conduction electrons oscillate together. Dispersed particles look red because they absorb and scatter around the green. When particles aggregate, their plasmons couple, and the colour shifts to blue-purple. This colour change needs no instrument. It is the basis of the red test line in a lateral flow strip: gold–antibody conjugate binds target, the complex is captured at a line, and accumulated gold looks red. Gold also works as an electrochemical label. The gold is oxidised to Au3+, and the current gives the amount bound.

  • Dispersed 20–40 nm gold: red.
  • Aggregated gold: blue-purple.
  • Lateral flow line: accumulated gold, read by eye.
  • Electrochemical gold label: oxidation current.

Nanomaterial FETs, magnetic particles, conducting polymers

A carbon nanotube, a graphene sheet, or a silicon nanowire can be the channel of a field-effect transistor. A charged molecule bound near the channel changes its conductance. The device measures this change as a current or threshold shift. The limit is the Debye length: in salty serum, mobile ions screen charge within about 1 nm. Only binding inside that layer changes the signal. This is the same limit as the IMFET in 4.4.

  • Magnetic nanoparticles: superparamagnetic, bind target, magnet pulls them out for separation or preconcentration.
  • Conducting polymers: polypyrrole and PEDOT are nanostructured platforms that anchor biomolecules and carry charge.
  • Silicon nanowires: high surface-to-volume ratio, so small charge changes give large conductance changes in low-salt buffer.

These platforms are chosen for surface area and for easy electrical readout. They do not remove the need for calibration, clean samples, or a recognition element that binds only the target.

Worked example

Question. A quantum-dot donor has Förster radius R0 = 5.0 nm. An acceptor dye sits on a target. Use E = 1 / (1 + (r / R0)6). (a) Bound target holds the acceptor at r = 4.0 nm.   2. Unbound: r / R0 = 8.0 / 5.0 = 1.60. Sixth power = 1.606 = 16.78. Denominator = 1 + 16.78 = 17.78. E = 1 / 17.78 = 0.0563, about 0.056, so 5.6%.
  3. Ratio = 0.792 / 0.0563 = 14.1, about 14. A 4 nm movement changes transfer from strong to weak. Donor emission returns when the target leaves, so binding is seen without a wash step.

Exam traps

  • Saying a quantum dot changes colour because it is bigger and scatters more light. The cause is quantum confinement of the electron and hole, which raises the band gap as the dot shrinks.
  • Treating FRET efficiency as linear in distance. E falls with the sixth power of r, so small distance changes give large signal changes.
  • Reversing the gold colours: dispersed 20–40 nm gold is red; aggregated gold is blue-purple.
  • Forgetting Debye screening: a nanowire or graphene FET in undiluted serum detects only charge within about 1 nm of the surface, not every bound molecule.
  • Confusing Förster radius R0 with the particle radius. R0 is the donor–acceptor distance where FRET efficiency is 0.5.

PotentiometryMeasurement of voltage at near-zero current; voltage follows the Nernst equation, about 59 mV per decade at 25 °C4.3
ImmobilisationFixing the enzyme or antibody onto the electrode surface by adsorption, covalent bonding, cross-linking or entrapment4.3
ISFETIon-sensitive field-effect transistor: a MOSFET whose metal gate is replaced by ion-selective membrane plus sample, so surface charge shifts threshold voltage4.4
Threshold voltage VTGate voltage at which the MOSFET channel just forms; in an ISFET it moves with the charge on the membrane surface4.4
IMFETImmunologically sensitive FET: antibody layer on the gate; antigen binding changes charge and shifts VT4.4
Continuous glucose monitor (CGM)Needle enzyme electrode left in interstitial fluid for days, giving a reading every few minutes, with a lag of roughly 5–15 minutes behind blood4.5
HybridisationPairing of a single-stranded probe with its complementary target strand to form a double helix4.6
Self-assembled monolayer (SAM)One-molecule-thick ordered film, typically an alkanethiol on gold, used to attach probes and block bare surface4.6
E-DNA stem-loop probeHairpin DNA with a redox tag at one end; hybridisation opens the stem, moves the tag away from the electrode and lowers the current4.6

Quantum dotSemiconductor nanocrystal (for example CdSe) whose emission colour is set by particle size, used as an optical or electrochemical label4.7
Localised surface plasmon resonance (LSPR)Size- and shape-dependent light absorption of metal nanoparticles that shifts when molecules bind to their surface4.7

Takeaways

  1. A biosensor is only a system: recognition element, transducer and electronics, and no reading is trusted until it is calibrated against standards.
  2. Selectivity comes from the biological element; the usable range and detection limit come from its binding or catalytic kinetics.
  3. Electrochemical transducers are popular because they are cheap, small and give a direct electrical signal that a simple circuit can digitise.
  4. In an ISFET the sample itself acts as part of the gate, so the measured quantity is a shift in threshold voltage, not a current through a membrane.
  5. The disposable glucose strip is the most widely used biosensor in the world, and the continuous monitor turns the same chemistry into a time trace.
  6. DNA biosensors trade the enzyme's amplification for sequence specificity, and read the amount of duplex formed by current or impedance.
  7. At the nanoscale, high surface area amplifies small binding events, but charge screening by salt limits what can be detected electrically.

Remember and understand

Remember Define a biosensor. Name its three essential components and state the function of each, with one medical and one environmental example. 8 marks

Definition linking biological recognition to a measurable signal proportional to one analyte; Bio-recognition element: enzyme, antibody, nucleic acid, cell or receptor; Transducer: electrochemical, optical, piezoelectric or thermal; Electronics: amplification, digitisation, display; Example: glucose strip (medical); pesticide or BOD sensor (environment).

Understand Explain how a biosensor differs from an ordinary chemical or physical sensor, and why the biological element is the source of selectivity. 8 marks

Physical sensor responds to a bulk property; chemical sensor responds to a class of species; Biological element gives molecular recognition through shape and charge complementarity; Enzyme active site, antigen–antibody fit, base pairing; Selectivity measured against interfering species; Selectivity does not fix range or LOD; kinetics do.

Understand Classify biosensors by bio-recognition element and by transducer type, giving one example of each class. 10 marks

Understand Explain Michaelis–Menten kinetics and show how Km and Vmax set the useful measuring range of an enzyme electrode. 12 marks

Rate equation and meaning of Vmax and Km; First-order region at low substrate, zero-order at high substrate; Linear range sits where signal is proportional to concentration; Immobilisation lowers activity and can shift apparent Km; Diffusion limitation in a thick film widens or narrows the range; Link to detection limit and saturation point.

Understand Describe the structure and principle of an ISFET, and explain how it differs from a MOSFET. 10 marks

Apply A glucose sensor gives readings of 12, 26, 51, 98 and 190 nA for standards of 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 mmol/L. Find the sensitivity and, if the blank gives 3.0 ± 0.6 nA, the limit of detection. 10 marks

Apply Design a disposable amperometric glucose sensor strip for a home meter. Choose the enzyme, the immobilisation method, the electrode material, the applied potential and the calibration scheme, and justify each choice. 12 marks

Apply An ISFET with a Nernstian slope of 59 mV per pH unit is used for a urea sensor. Calculate the threshold shift for a pH change of 0.05 and explain two reasons a measured shift may not match this figure. 10 marks

Apply A home test uses gold nanoparticles with an antibody sandwich for a cardiac marker. Explain how the LSPR signal arises, why nanoparticles amplify small binding events, and what limits the test. 10 marks

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze A continuous glucose monitor reads 4.2 mmol/L while the fingerstick reads 6.0 mmol/L on day 6 of wear, with the gap widening after meals. Analyse the likely causes and state how calibration and alarms should be handled. 13 marks

Interstitial fluid lag behind blood, exaggerated by rising or falling glucose; Sensor drift and enzyme decay over wear time; Pressure-induced changes in local perfusion at night; Calibration: fingerstick calibration points, time limits on calibration validity; Trend arrows rather than single points; alarms set on rate of change; Accuracy expressed as mean absolute relative difference against reference; Rule that a reading is confirmed with a fingerstick before dosing.

Evaluate Compare an enzyme electrode, an ISFET and a nanomaterial FET as the basis of a portable urea meter. Judge which you would choose for a clinic and give reasons. 13 marks

Enzyme electrode: urease converts urea to ammonium, amperometric or potentiometric readout, cheap and proven; ISFET: label-free, small, but drift and reference electrode problems; Nanomaterial FET: high sensitivity, but Debye screening in high-salt samples and reproducibility issues; Criteria: detection limit, linear range in urine, drift, sterility, cost per test, calibration burden; Recommendation with a stated trade-off; Controls and calibration plan for the chosen device.

Analyze Explain how Debye screening restricts label-free electrical detection of charged molecules, and analyse three ways to overcome it. 12 marks

Debye length about 0.7–0.8 nm in 150 mM salt; charge beyond it is screened from the electrode; Consequence: large targets and whole cells give weak signal in serum; Strategy: dilute the sample to lengthen Debye length; Strategy: move the binding event close to the surface, using short probes or fragmented targets; Strategy: use an optical or mass readout that ignores charge screening; State cost of each strategy: loss of sample realism or added steps.

Evaluate Judge labelled versus label-free DNA detection for a fever panel that must run without a thermocycler. 10 marks

Labelled route: redox tag, intercalator or fluorophore; higher signal, extra washing steps; Sandwich format for longer targets and extra specificity; Label-free route: impedance or FET; simpler chemistry, more sensitive to matrix; E-DNA stem-loop gives reagentless, reusable sensing but single-step kinetics; Throughput, cost per test in rupees, shelf life, need for controls; Verdict tied to the stated requirement of no thermocycler.

Glossary

Affinity constant
Measure of how tightly an antibody binds its antigen; tighter binding lowers the detection limit but slows release.
Amperometric sensor
Sensor that holds the electrode at a fixed potential and measures the resulting current.
Analyte
The specific substance the biosensor is built to measure.
Biocompatibility
Ability of an implanted sensor to stay in the body without harming tissue or being fouled by it.
Biofouling
Build-up of protein and cells on a sensor surface that blocks the analyte and reduces signal over time.
Charge screening
Reduction of the effect of a surface charge by ions in the surrounding solution.
Enzyme activity
Amount of substrate converted per unit time under stated conditions, usually in units per milligram.
First-generation biosensor
Enzyme sensor that measures the natural substrate or product, such as oxygen or hydrogen peroxide.
Impedance spectroscopy
Technique that measures how the electrode–solution interface resists and stores charge over a range of frequencies.
Ion-selective membrane
Thin film that lets one ion pass or binds it, producing a charge at the surface of an ISFET or electrode.
Lateral flow strip
Paper-based test where labelled particles move by capillary action and are captured on a test line.
MARD
Mean absolute relative difference, the usual accuracy measure for a continuous glucose monitor.
Mediator
Small redox molecule that shuttles electrons from an enzyme to the electrode, allowing a lower operating potential.
Nafion membrane
Permselective polymer film that lets small molecules through while blocking anions such as ascorbate.
Nernst equation
Relation giving electrode potential as a function of ion activity, about 59 mV per decade at 25 °C for a one-electron change.
Potentiometric sensor
Sensor that measures voltage at almost zero current, usually with an ion-selective membrane.
Probe
Short single-stranded nucleic acid of known sequence fixed on the sensor surface.
Redox label
Electroactive group such as ferrocene or methylene blue whose proximity to the electrode changes the measured current.
Reference electrode
Electrode of known, stable potential, usually Ag/AgCl, against which the sensing electrode is measured.
Sandwich assay
Format where a capture probe binds the target and a second labelled probe binds a different site on the same target.
Second-generation biosensor
Enzyme sensor that uses a mediator or redox dye to transfer electrons instead of relying on oxygen.
Stringency
Combination of temperature, salt and detergent in a wash that removes mismatched duplexes while keeping perfect matches.
Third-generation biosensor
Sensor in which the enzyme transfers electrons directly to the electrode without a mediator.
Working electrode
Electrode where the reaction of interest is driven and the current is measured.
