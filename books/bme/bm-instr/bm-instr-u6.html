6.1 Audiometers: basic and pure-tone

An audiometer is a calibrated tone generator with headphones. It sends a pure tone of known frequency and known level to one ear, and the tester finds the softest level the person hears. That level is the hearing threshold. The result is plotted as hearing level in dB HL against frequency, giving an audiogram. Clinics use it to detect hearing loss, to fit hearing aids, and to screen workers exposed to loud machinery or schoolchildren. This chapter covers pure-tone audiometry and a short look at speech audiometry.

You need the amplifier and filter ideas of bio-amplifiers, and see macro- and micro-shock for current paths through the body and electrical safety and testing for equipment safety.

Air conduction, bone conduction and the audiogram

A pure-tone audiometer has an oscillator with a frequency selector, an amplifier, an attenuator, a tone interrupter switch, a masking-noise generator, the earphones and bone vibrator, and a patient response button that lights a lamp. The oscillator makes a sine wave from 125 Hz to 8 kHz. The attenuator sets the level in 5 dB steps, usually from −10 to 120 dB HL. The interrupter presents the tone for 1–2 s without a click. The earphone delivers the tone to one ear. Air conduction tests the whole path: outer ear, middle ear and inner ear.

Bone conduction uses a small vibrator pressed on the mastoid bone behind the ear. The sound reaches the inner ear through the skull and skips the outer and middle ear. Comparing air and bone thresholds separates conductive loss (outer or middle ear) from sensorineural loss (inner ear or nerve).

The result is an audiogram. Frequency is on the horizontal axis from 125 Hz to 8 kHz. Hearing level is on the vertical axis in dB HL, marked 0 to 100; 0 is normal and larger numbers mean worse hearing. In the figure, a normal ear sits at 0 dB HL at every frequency. The patient's thresholds are 20 dB HL at 250 Hz, 35 dB at 500 Hz, 45 dB at 1 kHz, 55 dB at 2 kHz and 60 dB at 4 kHz. A clinical audiogram spaces the frequencies an octave apart and puts 0 dB HL at the top, so a worse threshold is plotted lower. Right-ear air conduction is marked O, left-ear X, and bone conduction < and >.

Hearing level uses a reference pressure that changes with frequency and with the earphone. 0 dB HL is the median threshold of healthy young ears. Its sound pressure is the reference equivalent threshold sound pressure level (RETSPL) of that earphone: at 1 kHz it is close to 0 dB SPL, 20 µPa, for an insert earphone, and about 7 dB SPL, 45 µPa, for a TDH-39 supra-aural earphone. At low frequencies the RETSPL is much higher, so 0 dB HL is not the same sound pressure level (SPL) everywhere.

LHL = 20 log10(p / pref(f))

LHL is hearing level in dB HL; p is the sound pressure at the ear; pref(f) is the normal threshold pressure at frequency f for that earphone; for an insert earphone at 1 kHz pref ≈ 20 µPa.

The Hughson-Westlake staircase

The Hughson-Westlake method is a staircase. It finds the lowest level heard, not the first level heard.

  1. Explain the test and place the headphones. Test the better ear first.
  2. Start at 1 kHz with a tone at 30 dB HL for about one second.
  3. If the first tone is not heard, raise it to 50 dB HL, then in 10 dB steps until it is heard.
  4. After every tone that is heard, drop by 10 dB and present again.
  5. After every miss, raise by 5 dB and present again.
  6. The threshold is the lowest level heard on two of three ascending runs.
  7. Test 2, 4 and 8 kHz, retest 1 kHz as a check, then 500 and 250 Hz.

Masking stops the non-test ear from hearing the tone by bone conduction. Feed narrow-band noise, centred on the test frequency, to the non-test ear. A supra-aural earphone tone crosses the skull about 40 dB weaker (the interaural attenuation), so mask for air conduction when the test-ear threshold is 40 dB or more above the non-test ear's bone threshold; a bone vibrator shakes the whole skull, so mask for bone conduction whenever an air-bone gap over 10 dB is to be trusted. Masking noise goes to the non-test ear, never the test ear.

Speech audiometry and calibration

Speech audiometry asks what the ear does with words, not just tones. The speech reception threshold (SRT) is the level at which the person repeats half of a list of two-syllable words correctly. It is also called the speech recognition threshold. SRT should agree with the pure-tone average of 500 Hz, 1 kHz and 2 kHz within about 10 dB. A word recognition (discrimination) score uses a fixed list of one-syllable words at a comfortable level, often 30–40 dB above the SRT, and reports the percentage repeated correctly. The speech audiometer is the same instrument with a recorded-speech or microphone input, a VU meter to set the input level, the calibrated attenuator, and earphones or a loudspeaker in a sound-treated room.

Calibration keeps the numbers honest. A coupler and a sound-level meter set the reference equivalent threshold sound pressure level at each frequency for the earphone model. A daily biological check on a known normal ear catches drift. Because the RETSPL belongs to one earphone model, an audiometer is recalibrated whenever its earphones are changed.

Worked example

Question. A pure-tone audiometer with insert earphones is calibrated so that 0 dB HL at 1 kHz equals a sound pressure of 20 µPa. A patient hears the 1 kHz tone only when the dial is at 60 dB HL. (a) What pressure reached the ear? (b) What pressure would a TDH-39 supra-aural earphone, whose 1 kHz RETSPL is 7 dB SPL, give at the same dial setting?

  1. Write the hearing-level formula: L = 20 log10(p / p0).
  2. Put L = 60 dB and p0 = 20 µPa.
  3. Divide both sides by 20: 60 / 20 = 3.
  4. Take the antilog: p / p0 = 103 = 1000.
  5. Multiply: p = 20 µPa × 1000 = 20,000 µPa.
  6. Convert: 20,000 µPa = 0.02 Pa = 20 mPa.
  7. (b) The supra-aural earphone adds its 7 dB reference: 60 + 7 = 67 dB SPL.
  8. p = 20 µPa × 1067/20 = 20 µPa × 2239 = 44 770 µPa = 44.8 mPa.

Answer. (a) 20 mPa, one thousand times the 20 µPa reference; (b) 44.8 mPa, because the same 60 dB HL sits on a 7 dB higher reference for the supra-aural earphone.

Exam traps

  • dB HL is not dB SPL. A reading of 40 dB HL means 40 dB above the normal threshold at that frequency, not a fixed pressure, because the reference pressure changes with frequency.
  • Air conduction and bone conduction are not two names for the same test. Bone conduction skips the outer and middle ear, so an air-bone gap points to a conductive component.
  • Masking noise is delivered to the non-test ear. Sending it to the test ear raises the threshold and gives a false reading.
  • The Hughson-Westlake rule is 10 dB down after a heard tone and 5 dB up after a miss. Reversing these steps gives the wrong threshold.
  • SRT and the word recognition score are different numbers. SRT is the 50% speech level; the word score is a percentage at a chosen comfortable level.

6.2 Endoscopes: types and uses

An endoscope lets a doctor look inside a body cavity through a natural opening or a small cut. It pipes light in along one bundle of glass fibres and brings the picture back along a second, ordered bundle, or along a tiny camera chip at the tip. Rigid tubes with glass rod lenses serve joints and the abdomen; flexible fibre-optic and video scopes reach the stomach and the colon. The same instrument carries a channel for forceps, suction and water, so the doctor can see, sample and treat in one pass.

You need physiological transducers, because the chip at a video scope's tip is a CCD or CMOS image sensor of that family.

The cavity problem and the rigid scope

An open cavity is dark and closed. To see inside without cutting it wide open, a doctor needs three things at once: light delivered into the cavity, an image carried back out, and a slim path for tools. An endoscope supplies all three. Every design, from a steel tube to a pill-sized camera, splits into the same parts: a lamp outside the body, an illumination path, an imaging path, a working channel, and controls at the handle.

The rigid endoscope is a straight metal tube, a few millimetres across, holding a relay of glass rod lenses. Each rod lens forms a real image of the previous one, so the picture is passed down the tube like a baton and arrives at an eyepiece or a camera head. In this rod-lens design long glass rods fill most of the tube and the thin air gaps act as the lenses, so light travels mostly through glass, fewer surfaces are needed and less light is lost. The optical result is a bright, sharp, wide view. The mechanical limit is the straight line: a rigid scope looks only where the tube already points.

Rigid scopes go where a straight path exists: through the abdominal wall (laparoscopy), into a knee or shoulder joint (arthroscopy), through the urethra into the bladder (cystoscopy). A few carry a prism at the tip to look 30° or 70° sideways while the tube itself stays straight.

How a flexible scope keeps the picture

Light travels along a glass fibre by total internal reflection. The fibre is a core of glass of refractive index n₁ wrapped in a cladding of slightly lower index n₂. A ray that meets the core–cladding boundary at more than the critical angle from the normal, that is, a ray running nearly along the fibre, is totally reflected and travels on.

sin θc = n₂ / n₁

θc is the critical angle, measured from the normal to the fibre wall; n₁ is the refractive index of the core; n₂ is the refractive index of the cladding, with n₂ < n₁. For n₁ = 1.50 and n₂ = 1.47, sin θc = 0.98 and θc ≈ 78°.

A flexible endoscope uses two separate bundles. The illumination bundle is incoherent: its fibres are packed at random and their only job is to pour light onto the tissue. The image bundle is coherent: fibre number 1 sits at the same corner of the bundle face at the handle as at the tip. Because the order never changes, a bright point at the tip leaves at one known point at the eyepiece, and a picture survives.

An objective lens at the tip focuses the tissue onto the face of the image bundle, and an eyepiece or camera magnifies what comes out. What the doctor sees is a mosaic of fibres. Two points closer together than one fibre width fall on the same fibre and merge, so many thin fibres give a finer picture but less light per fibre.

To aim the tip, two or four thin cables run down the shaft; a knob at the handle pulls one pair against the other and bends a springy section at the tip.

Video endoscopes, channels, cleaning and uses

The video endoscope removes the eyepiece. A CCD or CMOS sensor 1–2 mm across sits at the tip behind the objective lens, turns the image into an electrical signal, and wires carry it up the shaft to a processor and a monitor. The whole team watches one screen, and the image can be frozen, recorded and printed. A light bundle usually remains, so the lamp and its heat stay outside the body; some newer scopes use tiny LEDs at the tip instead.

A working channel runs the length of the shaft, typically 1.2 mm to 3.8 mm wide. Biopsy forceps, a snare or a laser fibre pass through it; the same channel gives suction, a water jet for washing, and air to inflate the cavity. Tip angulation and the channel together make the instrument a treatment tool, not just a camera.

The cleaning method is decided by what the instrument can survive. A rigid all-metal scope can be steam sterilised in an autoclave at 134 °C. A flexible scope cannot: heat and water ruin the fibre bundles, the seals and the sensor. It is leak-tested, cleaned, then disinfected chemically, for example in 2% glutaraldehyde for about 20 minutes, and hung to dry. A video scope's handle and connector are kept out of the liquid.

Worked example

Question. The image bundle of a flexible endoscope holds 10,000 fibres. Each fibre has a core diameter of 30 µm. (a) Find the light-carrying area of one fibre core. (b) Find the total core area of the bundle in mm². (c) Give one reason against simply using thicker fibres.

  1. Core radius r = d / 2 = 30 µm / 2 = 15 µm.
  2. Area of one core A₁ = π r² = π × (15 µm)² = π × 225 µm² = 706.9 µm², that is about 707 µm².
  3. Total core area A = N × A₁ = 10,000 × 706.9 µm² = 7.069 × 10⁶ µm².
  4. Convert units: 1 mm² = 1000 µm × 1000 µm = 10⁶ µm², so A = 7.069 × 10⁶ / 10⁶ mm² = 7.07 mm².
  5. Part (c): a thicker core carries more light per fibre, but each fibre then covers a larger spot on the image face. Two points closer than one fibre spacing (the core plus its cladding, a little over 30 µm here) land on the same fibre and merge, so the picture becomes coarser.

Answer. One core carries about 707 µm² of light-carrying area, and the 10,000-fibre bundle gives a total core area of 7.07 mm²; thicker cores brighten the image but blur it.

Exam traps

  • Swapping the two bundles: the incoherent bundle only carries light, the coherent bundle carries the image because its fibres keep the same order at both ends.
  • Placing the lamp at the tip of a fibre-optic scope. Its lamp stays outside and light comes down the light bundle; only cable-free designs such as the capsule, and some modern video scopes, carry small LEDs at the tip.
  • Treating a bigger fibre core as better resolution. A bigger core carries more light but resolves less, because resolution is set by the spacing of cores, core plus cladding.
  • Assuming every endoscope can be autoclaved. Steam at 134 °C destroys fibre bundles, seals and sensors, so flexible scopes are disinfected chemically instead.
  • Forgetting that a rigid scope sees only along a straight line, which is why it cannot do gastroscopy or colonoscopy.

6.3 Dental X-ray machine

A dental X-ray machine is a small X-ray tube with a pointed cone. It makes one short exposure of a tooth onto film or a digital sensor. The tube has a fixed anode, a tungsten target and oil cooling. A step-up transformer gives the high voltage, and a timer switches it on for a set time. Dentists use it for periapical and bitewing views, and for panoramic imaging. Its design is a lesson in keeping dose low.

You need: basic X-ray production and the inverse-square law from a first-year physics course.

Tube, transformer and timer

The tube is a stationary-anode type. A tungsten target sits in a vacuum glass envelope, and insulating oil around it carries heat away. Electrons boiled off the heated filament are accelerated across the tube and hit the target. Most of their energy becomes heat; under 1 % becomes X-rays, mostly braking radiation (bremsstrahlung) with a continuous spectrum, plus the characteristic lines of tungsten. The tube sits in a lead-lined head filled with oil, with one window for the beam. Many dental heads are self-rectified: the tube itself conducts only on the half-cycle when the anode is positive. Newer units use a high-frequency inverter that gives an almost steady tube voltage and a shorter exposure. A step-up transformer raises the mains voltage to the tube voltage, set in kilovolt peak (kVp). The tube current, in milliamperes (mA), sets how many electrons flow. A timer closes the circuit for the exposure time in seconds (s). Dental units typically run at 60 to 70 kVp and 7 to 10 mA for 0.1 to 1 s, giving a few milliampere-seconds (mAs). Exposure quantity is mAs = mA × s. The timer must be accurate because a long exposure raises dose. The exposure button is a dead-man switch on a coiled cord long enough for the operator to stand 2 m away; releasing it stops the exposure at once, and a light and a sound show that X-rays are on.

Filtration, collimation and the cone

Inherent filtration comes from the tube wall and oil; added filtration is an aluminium sheet. Total filtration for a dental unit running at 70 kVp or below is at least 1.5 mm aluminium equivalent; units that work above 70 kVp need at least 2.5 mm aluminium. It removes soft X-rays that would only add skin dose. A collimator or lead diaphragm limits the beam to the cone opening. The cone, or position-indicating device, aims the beam at the tooth and sets the target-to-skin distance. A focal spot under 1 mm keeps the image sharp. Film speed or a digital sensor sets the dose needed for a good image.

I₂/I₁ = (d₁/d₂)²

I₁ and I₂ are beam intensities at distances d₁ and d₂ from the target; dose follows the same ratio.

The cone also sets the beam size. The beam may be no wider than 6 cm at the skin, and rectangular collimation to the size of the sensor cuts the dose further. The focus-to-skin distance must be at least 20 cm for units above 60 kVp; a longer cone also makes the rays more nearly parallel, so the image is less magnified.

Views and operator protection

An intra-oral periapical film shows the whole tooth and root apex. A bitewing shows crowns and crestal bone of upper and lower teeth in one bite. A panoramic unit (orthopantomogram, OPG) rotates a narrow beam and detector around the head to make one wide image of the jaws; it is not an intra-oral exposure. The operator stands at least 2 m away, behind a lead screen if possible, and wears a lead apron if staying in the room. A lead apron and thyroid collar protect the patient.

Image receptors and keeping the dose low

Film is the oldest receptor; faster film (speed group E or F) needs less exposure. Digital receptors replace it: a wired CCD or CMOS sensor in a thin plastic case gives the image on screen in seconds, and a phosphor storage plate is read by a laser scanner afterwards. Both need less exposure than film, and the image can be enhanced, so retakes fall.

  • Use the fastest receptor and the lowest exposure that gives a diagnostic image.
  • Collimate to the sensor size and keep the long cone.
  • Hold the sensor with a holder, never with the patient's or operator's finger.
  • Cover the patient with a lead apron and thyroid collar.
  • Avoid retakes by correct aiming and keeping the patient still.
  • Stand at least 2 m away, out of the beam, and wear a dose badge.

Worked example

Question. A dental unit is set to 70 kVp, 7 mA and 0.6 s. (a) Find the exposure in mAs. (b) The cone is changed from 20 cm to 40 cm target-to-skin distance. By what factor does the skin dose change?

  1. Exposure: mAs = mA × s = 7 mA × 0.6 s = 4.2 mAs.
  2. Distance ratio: d₂/d₁ = 40 cm / 20 cm = 2.
  3. Inverse-square factor: (d₂/d₁)² = 2² = 4.
  4. New skin dose = old dose / 4 = 0.25 of the old dose.

Answer. The exposure is 4.2 mAs, and the skin dose falls by a factor of 4 to one quarter of its old value.

Worked example 2

Question. The dental unit of the first example runs at 70 kVp and 7 mA for 0.6 s with a tungsten target (Z = 74). (a) Find the highest photon energy and the shortest wavelength in the beam. (b) Taking the input power as kVp × mA, find the electrical energy of one exposure. (c) With an X-ray efficiency of about 1.1 × 10⁻⁹ × Z × V, find how much of that energy leaves as X-rays and how much becomes heat.

  1. An electron accelerated through 70 kV gains 70 keV, so no photon can carry more: Emax = 70 keV.
  2. Shortest wavelength λmin = hc ÷ E = 1.24 nm·keV ÷ 70 keV = 0.0177 nm (17.7 pm).
  3. Electrical energy = 70 000 V × 0.007 A × 0.6 s = 294 J (an upper estimate, because the voltage is a peak value).
  4. Efficiency = 1.1 × 10⁻⁹ × 74 × 70 000 = 0.0057, that is 0.57 %.
  5. X-ray energy = 0.0057 × 294 J = 1.7 J; heat in the target = 294 − 1.7 = 292 J.
  6. So 99.4 % of the energy heats the anode, which is why even a short exposure needs a copper-backed target in oil, and why the timer limits the number of exposures per minute.

Answer. Emax = 70 keV and λmin = 0.0177 nm; about 294 J goes in, of which only 1.7 J (0.57 %) leaves as X-rays and 292 J becomes heat.

Exam traps

  • kVp sets penetration and contrast; mA sets quantity. Do not use one for the other.
  • mAs = mA × s; mA alone is not exposure.
  • Doubling cone distance cuts dose by 4, not 2 (inverse square).
  • Timer is in seconds; 0.6 s is not 0.6 ms.

6.4 Physiological effects of electricity; macro- and micro-shock

Electricity harms the body in proportion to the current that flows through it, not the voltage printed on the casing. Skin resistance turns a voltage into a current through V = I R. A tingle through dry skin and a fatal shock through wet skin can come from the same socket. The same rule explains why a few tens of microamps pushed straight into the heart by a catheter can be more dangerous than a hundred times that current through intact skin. These two cases are called macro-shock and micro-shock, and hospital electrical design is built around the second.

You need first-year circuit ideas, V = I R and series and parallel resistances, and the electrode-to-skin behaviour from electrode theory.

The body as a resistor: current does the damage

A person becomes part of a circuit when two points of the body touch a live conductor and a return path. The current through the body follows Ohm's law. The damage depends on that current, its path and how long it flows; the voltage on the casing only sets how much current the resistance will pass.

Skin is the main resistor. Dry, intact skin gives about 10 kΩ hand to hand; wet or broken skin gives about 1 kΩ or less. Inside the body the resistance falls to a few hundred ohms, roughly 300 Ω to 500 Ω. Contact area, calluses and sweat all move these numbers.

I = V / R

I is the body current in amperes, V is the touch voltage in volts, and R is the resistance of the current path in ohms. For milliamps, I(mA) = 1000 × V / R.

Path matters because current density matters. A hand-to-hand or hand-to-foot path crosses the heart; a finger-to-finger path mostly does not. The same current squeezed through a small contact point heats tissue and causes a burn.

  • Dry skin, hand to hand: about 10 kΩ.
  • Wet skin, hand to hand: about 1 kΩ.
  • Internal path from a catheter: a few hundred ohms, with no skin in the way.

The effects ladder at mains frequency

At 50 Hz or 60 Hz the effects rise in a fixed order as current increases. The values below are for a current path through the trunk and a shock lasting about one second.

  • 1 mA: faint tingle, the perception level.
  • 10 mA to 15 mA: let-go level. The hand grips the conductor and cannot release it, because the flexor muscles are stronger than the extensors.
  • 20 mA to 30 mA: respiratory tetanus. The chest muscles clamp and breathing stops while the current flows.
  • About 100 mA: ventricular fibrillation, the usual cause of death in an electric shock.
  • Several amperes: deep burns and cardiac arrest; the heart may be clamped rather than fibrillating.

Why is 50/60 Hz the worst frequency? Nerve and muscle membranes are most easily driven by alternating currents in the tens of hertz. At direct current, or at radio frequencies, a much larger current is needed for the same excitation. Mains frequency sits in that worst band.

Duration changes the outcome. A 100 mA shock for a second is dangerous; the same current for a few milliseconds may not fibrillate. Short contacts still burn, because heating goes as I²R at the contact point.

Macro-shock versus micro-shock

Macro-shock means current enters through intact skin. Skin resistance limits the current, so the source must be tens or hundreds of volts to push a dangerous current. The ICU example below is a macro-shock calculation.

Micro-shock means an insulated conductor, a saline-filled catheter or a pacing lead carries current directly to the heart. Skin resistance is absent. About 100 µA, and in some studies as little as 20 µA, can fibrillate the heart. The 10 µA patient-leakage limit applies to type CF parts, the ones allowed to touch the heart, and leaves a safety margin; type B and BF parts, which touch only intact skin, may leak up to 100 µA.

Micro-shock also depends on timing. The heart is most vulnerable during the T wave, the repolarisation phase, roughly the last third of each beat. A small pulse landing in that window can start fibrillation; the same pulse outside the window often cannot.

That is why an intracardiac lead needs its own protection: isolation, current-limiting resistors and careful earthing. The three defences and the four tests are in Electrical safety and testing.

Worked example

Question. A patient in an ICU touches the metal casing of a faulty monitor at 230 V. (a) The hand-to-hand path through dry skin is 10 kΩ. (b) The same path through wet skin is 1 kΩ. (c) Later, a saline-filled catheter gives a 500 Ω path to the heart, and a 0.1 V offset appears across it. Find the body current in each case and compare it with the 100 mA fibrillation level and the 10 µA patient-leakage limit for cardiac-connected (type CF) equipment.

  1. (a) Dry skin: I = V / R = 230 V / 10,000 Ω = 0.023 A = 23 mA.
  2. 23 mA is above the 10–15 mA let-go level and below the 100 mA fibrillation level. The shock is painful and may freeze the muscles, but it usually does not fibrillate the heart.
  3. (b) Wet skin: I = 230 V / 1000 Ω = 0.230 A = 230 mA.
  4. 230 mA is 2.3 times the 100 mA fibrillation level, so a shock lasting about a second is likely to fibrillate.
  5. (c) Catheter: I = 0.1 V / 500 Ω = 0.0002 A = 200 µA.
  6. 200 µA is twenty times the 10 µA limit for cardiac-connected equipment and above the roughly 100 µA that can fibrillate the heart directly, so it is a micro-shock hazard even though the voltage is only 0.1 V.

Answer. 23 mA through dry skin, 230 mA through wet skin, and 200 µA through the catheter; the wet-skin and catheter cases exceed their danger levels.

Exam traps

  • Saying high voltage is dangerous and stopping there. A 230 V source is harmless through 100 kΩ and lethal through 1 kΩ; the current decides.
  • Writing micro-shock limits in milliamps. The intracardiac danger level is tens to about a hundred microamps, a thousand to several thousand times smaller than the 100 mA macro-shock level (20 µA is 5000 times smaller).
  • Mixing up let-go and fibrillation. Let-go is 10–15 mA; fibrillation is near 100 mA for a one-second shock.
  • Ignoring duration and timing. A short pulse, or a pulse outside the T wave, is far less likely to fibrillate than a one-second shock.
  • Treating body resistance as one fixed number. It changes tenfold between dry and wet skin and drops again once a conductor is inside the body.

6.5 Electrical safety and testing

Electrical safety means a patient or nurse cannot get a dangerous current from a mains-powered device. Three defences stop current reaching a body: a protective earth wire, isolation, and double insulation. Four tests prove those defences: visual inspection, earth continuity, insulation resistance, and leakage current. The same checks are used before a ventilator enters an ICU, after a monitor is repaired, and during periodic testing in a hospital. This chapter shows the classes, the leakage paths, the teaching limits, and the single-fault test that matters most.

You need macro- and micro-shock: current, not voltage, damages tissue, and micro-shock reaches the heart through a catheter.

Defences and equipment classes

Mains has two wires, live and neutral, and a third protective earth wire. Class I equipment connects its metal enclosure to protective earth. If a live wire touches the enclosure, current flows down the earth wire and the fuse or breaker trips. Equipotential bonding joins all metal parts that a person can touch, so no dangerous voltage difference exists between them.

Class II equipment has double insulation: basic insulation plus a second layer, or reinforced insulation, so no protective earth is needed. Class III equipment uses a safety extra-low voltage supply, usually below 25 V AC or 60 V DC, and needs no earth either.

An applied part touches the patient. Type B is body type and may be earthed or not. Type BF is body floating: the patient circuit is isolated from earth. Type CF is cardiac floating and is isolated enough for direct cardiac contact. The F means floating, so a fault current has no easy path through the patient to earth.

An isolation transformer separates the device supply from the earthed mains, so the secondary has no reference to earth. A single contact with one live conductor then cannot drive current through the body to earth. An ELCB or GFCI compares live and neutral currents; if the difference exceeds a set value, often 30 mA for household protection, it opens the circuit.

Leakage currents and limits

Enclosure leakage current flows from the metal case to earth through a person touching the case. Patient leakage current flows from an applied part, through the patient, to earth. They are measured separately because the patient path is far more dangerous.

Iearth ≤ 5 mA in normal condition; Ienclosure ≤ 100 µA in normal condition and ≤ 500 µA in single fault; Ipatient ≤ 100 µA for B or BF; Ipatient ≤ 10 µA for CF

Iearth is protective-earth-conductor leakage; Ienclosure is case-to-earth leakage; Ipatient is patient leakage; B, BF and CF are applied-part types. The patient limits shown are normal-condition limits; single-fault limits are higher.

The analyser does not use a plain ammeter. It passes the leakage through a measuring device of 1 kΩ with a frequency-weighting network, which stands in for the body, and reads the voltage across it: 1 mV across 1 kΩ is 1 µA.

The earth wire resistance limit is ≤ 0.1 Ω from the earth pin to the enclosure, 0.2 Ω with a detachable mains cord; routine hospital tests that include the cord commonly accept up to 0.3 Ω. Insulation resistance is measured at 500 V and must be ≥ 1 MΩ. Earth leakage must be ≤ 5 mA. Enclosure leakage must be ≤ 100 µA in normal condition and ≤ 500 µA in single fault. Patient leakage must be ≤ 100 µA for type B or BF and ≤ 10 µA for type CF in normal condition. Single-fault limits are higher because the device must remain safe while the fault is present, but a device that fails only in single fault is still removed.

For micro-shock, use isolated patient inputs, keep the patient circuit away from earthed metal, and during catheter procedures ask staff to work with one hand in the pocket. That stops a second hand from touching an earthed surface and completing a current path through the heart.

The test sequence

A safety analyser is a meter with a mains outlet, a test load and switches for normal and single-fault conditions. Start with the device off and unplugged for inspection.

  1. Visual inspection: check cords, plugs, labels, fuses, cracks and liquid spills.
  2. Earth continuity: measure from the earth pin to exposed metal; the limit is 0.1 Ω.
  3. Insulation resistance: join live and neutral, apply 500 V DC to earth, and require at least 1 MΩ.
  4. Leakage current: run the device and measure enclosure and patient leakage in normal condition.
  5. Single-fault leakage: repeat with the protective earth open, then with the neutral open. The limit is higher, but a failure still means removal.
  6. Functional test: confirm the device works, then release it.

The single-fault test is the one examiners ask about. Open the protective earth and measure leakage; then open the neutral. Testing only in normal condition misses the most dangerous faults. A broken earth wire or a broken neutral may leave a device looking normal while its leakage path is changed.

Worked example

Question. A technician tests a class I ventilator. Earth wire resistance reads 0.35 Ω, insulation resistance 50 MΩ, and earth leakage current 0.9 mA with the machine running. The limits are 0.1 Ω, 1 MΩ and 5 mA. Which test fails, and what action follows?

  1. Earth continuity: measured 0.35 Ω, limit 0.1 Ω. Since 0.35 > 0.1, this test fails.
  2. Insulation resistance: measured 50 MΩ, limit 1 MΩ. Since 50 ≥ 1, this test passes.
  3. Earth leakage: measured 0.9 mA, limit 5 mA. Since 0.9 ≤ 5, this test passes.
  4. Only earth continuity fails, so the leakage result is not trusted until the protective earth path is repaired.

Answer. Only the earth continuity test fails: 0.35 Ω is above the 0.1 Ω limit, while 50 MΩ passes the 1 MΩ limit and 0.9 mA passes the 5 mA limit. Remove the ventilator, inspect and repair the power-cord earth pin, then repeat the tests.

Worked example 2

Question. An ECG monitor with a type CF applied part is put on a safety analyser. The voltage across the 1 kΩ measuring device is 8 mV for patient leakage in normal condition and 62 mV with the protective earth opened. Find the two currents, compare them with the type CF limits, and say whether the monitor could be used on a ward patient with skin electrodes only, where a type BF limit would apply.

  1. Normal condition: I = V ÷ R = 8 mV ÷ 1 kΩ = 8 µA.
  2. Type CF normal-condition limit is 10 µA; 8 ≤ 10, so it passes.
  3. Single fault: I = 62 mV ÷ 1 kΩ = 62 µA.
  4. Type CF single-fault limit is 50 µA; 62 > 50, so it fails.
  5. The type BF limits are 100 µA and 500 µA, which 8 µA and 62 µA would meet, but the monitor is labelled CF and must meet CF limits; it is removed from service and repaired, not relabelled.

Answer. 8 µA passes the 10 µA CF limit, but 62 µA in single fault fails the 50 µA CF limit, so the monitor is withdrawn for repair.

Exam traps

  • Testing only in normal condition: a device can pass normal leakage and fail with the earth open. The single-fault test is the real protection.
  • Mixing resistance units: 0.1 Ω is an earth-wire resistance, while 1 MΩ is an insulation resistance. Do not compare them as if they were the same test.
  • Rejecting class II equipment for missing a protective earth: class II uses double insulation and does not need an earth wire, but it must still pass insulation and enclosure leakage.
  • Comparing patient leakage with earth leakage: 100 µA is the patient limit for B or BF; 5 mA is the earth-conductor leakage limit; the enclosure (case) leakage limit is 100 µA in normal condition. Do not use the wrong number.
  • Reversing type CF and type BF limits: type CF is stricter at 10 µA, not looser than the 100 µA limit for B or BF.

6.6 Medical device regulations

A medical device may be sold only after its risk class decides how much evidence it needs and which licence it must hold. The rule chain asks: does it enter the body, how long does it stay, and how much energy or fluid does it deliver? India uses four classes, A to D, under the Medical Devices Rules, 2017, run by the Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation (CDSCO). The US uses three classes, with 510(k) substantial-equivalence review and premarket approval. Europe uses the CE mark.

You need electrical safety and testing, because the electrical safety standard appears inside the regulatory file.

Risk class decides the evidence

Classification predicts harm, not quality. A tongue depressor rests in the mouth for a few seconds and does nothing else. A pacemaker sits in the heart for years and can deliver current. The same failure is not equally dangerous, so the evidence cannot be equal.

Indian rules use a rule chain, close to the international (IMDRF) rules. Ask three questions in order. Does the device enter the body? How long does it stay? Does it deliver energy or medicine? Duration has three bands: transient under 60 minutes, short-term from 60 minutes to 30 days, long-term over 30 days. The answers send the device to class A, B, C or D. Class A is low risk. Class D is high risk. Higher class means more documents: risk analysis, bench data, clinical evaluation, and often clinical investigation. The class also decides who licenses the device. A sterile syringe and an implantable pacemaker can both be single-use, but they do not carry the same risk.

6.7 Psychophysiological measurement: polygraph, BSR and GSR

Emotional arousal changes the skin, breathing and pulse. A polygraph records these channels together on one time base and marks the instant each question is asked. The skin channel is electrodermal activity: basal skin resistance (BSR) is the resting level, and the galvanic skin response (GSR) is the change after a stimulus. Clinics use GSR in biofeedback and autonomic testing; forensic teams use the polygraph as an arousal recorder. The device does not see lies. It sees sweat, movement and breathing, and the examiner interprets them.

You need surface electrodes and bio-amplifiers, and the small-current rule from macro- and micro-shock.

Basal skin resistance and the GSR

Skin is not a fixed resistor. Eccrine sweat glands, dense on the palm and fingers, are controlled by the sympathetic nervous system. When arousal rises, the ducts fill with sweat. Salt water conducts, so skin conductance rises and skin resistance falls. BSR is the resting resistance before a stimulus, measured across two palm or finger electrodes. Typical values run from tens of kilo-ohms to about one megaohm; a dry palm is nearer the high end.

A short sound, a question or a sudden pain produces the GSR: resistance falls, reaches a low point after a latency of about 1 to 3 s, then recovers slowly over several seconds. The response is a change from BSR, not a new baseline. The older name psychogalvanic reflex is still seen. Endosomatic measurement records the small potential the skin itself generates; exosomatic measurement applies an external current and reads the change. Most instruments are exosomatic.

R = V / I

R is skin resistance in ohms, V is the voltage across the electrode pair in volts, and I is the applied current in amperes.

Constant-current measurement and artefacts

In the constant-current method a source pushes a fixed small current through the electrodes, often around 10 µA. The voltage across the pair is measured. By R = V / I, a fall in resistance appears as a fall in voltage. A bridge circuit can also be used: the skin electrode pair forms one arm, and the bridge is balanced at BSR; the out-of-balance voltage is the GSR. Whatever the circuit, keep the current small, clean the skin, and use a matched pair of Ag/AgCl electrodes with a thin isotonic electrolyte layer, such as 0.05 M NaCl paste or gel. Bare dry metal on skin polarises under the small DC current, so the voltage drifts and the BSR wanders. A current that is safe on intact dry skin can be dangerous if it reaches the heart through a catheter; see macro- and micro-shock.

Electrodes go on the palm, at the thenar and hypothenar sites, or on the volar surface of two fingers, and the resistance is measured between this pair. If the amplifier needs a ground or reference electrode, place it on a neutral site such as the forearm; it carries no part of the measured resistance. Record BSR first, then wait for a stable trace. Movement, deep breath, a cough, room temperature and a sweaty hand all move the trace. Chart speed sets the time scale: faster paper spreads a 1 to 3 s response over more millimetres for reading latency. Mark stimulus onset on the same chart.

Polygraph channels and the legal limit

Polygraph is a multi-channel chart recorder. A pneumograph uses a belt around the chest and another around the abdomen; each belt is a strain gauge or a bellows that turns girth change into a voltage, so the record shows rate and depth of breathing. A blood-pressure cuff on the arm, or a photoplethysmograph (PPG) on a finger, shows cardiovascular change. PPG detects the pulse by the light absorbed by blood. Some systems add an EMG channel on the forearm or jaw to catch muscle tension. Signal conditioners amplify and filter each channel; a question marker writes a line or code on the same record.

Read the chart as a pattern: a question that produces a GSR together with a breath hold or a pulse rise is called a reaction. A reaction is not proof of a lie. The polygraph measures arousal, and fear, anger, illness or a cold room can produce the same pattern. In India, courts treat a polygraph result as corroborative only; it does not by itself convict or acquit. Consent and procedure belong to forensic practice, not to the instrument.

Artefacts: movement of the cuff, a loose belt, electrode pop, 50 Hz pickup, and sweat running between electrodes. A slow baseline drift is normal. Check the marker alignment before scoring.

Worked example

Question. A constant-current GSR meter passes 10 µA through a pair of palm electrodes. The voltage across them is 1.5 V while the subject rests and falls to 1.2 V just after a question. Find the basal skin resistance, the resistance after the question, the fall in resistance and the percentage fall. State why the meter reading must be inverted for a resistance plot.

  1. Write the current in amperes: I = 10 µA = 10 × 10−6 A.
  2. At rest, RBSR = V / I = 1.5 / (10 × 10−6) = 150 000 Ω = 150 kΩ.
  3. After the question, R = 1.2 / (10 × 10−6) = 120 000 Ω = 120 kΩ.
  4. Fall = 150 kΩ − 120 kΩ = 30 kΩ.
  5. Percentage fall = (30 / 150) × 100 = 20 per cent.
  6. The meter reads voltage, which falls when resistance falls; to plot resistance, invert the voltage axis or use the computed R.

Answer. BSR is 150 kΩ, the response lowers it to 120 kΩ, a fall of 30 kΩ or 20 per cent; the voltage trace must be inverted to read as resistance.

Exam traps

  • Writing that skin resistance rises with arousal. Sweat raises conductance, so resistance falls; BSR is the resting level and the response is the change from it.
  • Calling the GSR response the baseline. BSR is the resting value; GSR is the transient fall after a stimulus.
  • Expecting an immediate response. Latency is about 1 to 3 s, and recovery is slower; a marker placed too early misreads latency.
  • Reading the voltage trace as resistance without inverting. In a constant-current meter a lower voltage means lower resistance, so the resistance plot is upside down.
  • Saying the polygraph detects lies. It records arousal; the interpretation is separate and, in Indian courts, corroborative only.

6.8 Ophthalmic instruments: tonometers, slit lamp and auto refractometer

An ophthalmic instrument measures one property of the eye with a calibrated optical or mechanical method. A tonometer measures intra-ocular pressure, the fluid pressure inside the eye. A slit lamp builds a bright optical section so a clinician can see cornea, anterior chamber and lens layer by layer. An auto refractometer measures the lens power needed to focus the eye. These three measurements support glaucoma screening, eye examination and spectacle prescription in eye clinics.

You need macro- and micro-shock for patient-safe low-current design, and basic lens optics (focal length, dioptre) from first-year physics.

Tonometry: pressure from force and area

Intra-ocular pressure (IOP) is the pressure of the aqueous humour inside the eyeball. It is measured in millimetres of mercury (mmHg). Normal is about 10 to 21 mmHg. High pressure damages the optic nerve in glaucoma, so tonometry is a screening test.

Indentation tonometry uses a Schiotz tonometer. A known weight pushes a plunger into the cornea. The scale reading is not pressure directly; a conversion table for that weight and instrument gives mmHg. Corneal stiffness affects the reading, so the table is a calibration, not a pure physics answer.

Applanation tonometry flattens a small area. The Imbert-Fick rule is:

P = F / A

P is pressure in pascals (Pa); F is force in newtons (N); A is flattened area in square metres (m²).

A Goldmann tonometer uses a prism on the slit lamp. The prism flattens a circle of diameter 3.06 mm, so A = π(1.53 mm)² = 7.35 mm². The clinician puts one drop of local anaesthetic and fluorescein in the eye. Under blue light the tear film is stained, and two green arcs show the flattened circle. The force knob is turned until the arcs just touch. Force in millinewtons (mN) divided by this fixed area gives pressure. The diameter 3.06 mm is chosen because there the outward push of corneal stiffness and the inward pull of the tear film cancel, and a force of 1 gram (9.81 mN) gives exactly 10 mmHg, so the knob is read in grams and multiplied by 10.

Non-contact tonometry uses a short air puff. The puff flattens the cornea and an optical detector times the flattening. It needs no drop and no contact, so it suits screening. It is less reliable with an irregular cornea.

Calibration checks: zero the instrument, check the prism or probe, and test against a known calibration verifier. A drifted tonometer gives a wrong glaucoma decision.

Slit lamp: an optical section of the eye

The slit lamp is a biomicroscope: a microscope and a lamp on one movable base. The lamp arm carries a bright source and an adjustable slit aperture. The microscope arm carries the objective and eyepieces. A joystick moves the instrument in x, y and z. The patient rests chin and forehead on supports.

With wide diffuse light you scan lids, conjunctiva, cornea, iris and lens. With a narrow slit at an angle you get an optic section: a thin illuminated slice through transparent tissue. The cornea appears as a curved band, the anterior chamber as dark space, and the lens shows layers. Moving the slit shows depth, thickness, deposits and cells.

The slit lamp is a platform, not one test. With a gonioscopy lens it views the drainage angle; with a fundus lens it gives a magnified view of the retina and optic disc. Those lenses belong to ophthalmology courses. The main safety point is light exposure and patient comfort; the instrument is non-contact except for the lens.

Auto refractometer: measuring the focus

Refraction is the measurement of the eye's focusing error; the lens that corrects it is the prescription, written as sphere, cylinder and axis. Sphere (S) is spherical power in dioptres (D), positive for hyperopia and negative for myopia. Cylinder (C) is astigmatic power. Axis, in degrees from 0 to 180, is the meridian along which the cylinder has no power; its power acts at 90° to the axis.

An auto refractometer projects an infra-red target into the eye. Infra-red is invisible and keeps the pupil wide. Light reflects from the retina and passes back through the eye's optics. A Shack-Hartmann sensor uses a lenslet array: each small lens makes a spot. A perfect eye gives a regular grid; a defocused or astigmatic eye shifts the spots. The computer fits these shifts to sphere, cylinder and axis. Some instruments use a knife-edge or a moving pattern instead.

The result is objective. The patient then reads a chart for visual acuity, which is sharpness, not refraction. A person who reads 6/6 with glasses on may still have a strong prescription in those glasses. The clinician fine-tunes the auto refractometer reading by subjective refraction.

Uses: busy clinics, screening camps, before cataract surgery, and children who cannot cooperate with a manual test. Calibration uses a model eye of known power.

Worked example

Question. A Goldmann prism flattens a circle of diameter 3.06 mm when the force knob is set to 20 mN. Find the intra-ocular pressure in mmHg.

  1. Diameter d = 3.06 mm, so radius r = d/2 = 1.53 mm.
  2. Area A = πr² = π × (1.53 mm)² = 7.35 mm² = 7.35 × 10⁻⁶ m².
  3. Force F = 20 mN = 20 × 10⁻³ N.
  4. P = F/A = 20 × 10⁻³ N / 7.35 × 10⁻⁶ m² = 2721 Pa.
  5. 1 mmHg = 133.3 Pa, so P = 2721 / 133.3 = 20.4 mmHg.

Answer. The intra-ocular pressure is 20.4 mmHg, inside the normal range of about 10 to 21 mmHg.

Worked example 2

Question. An auto refractometer prints −2.00 DS / −0.75 DC × 180 for a right eye. Find (a) the power needed in the 180° and 90° meridians, (b) the spherical equivalent and (c) the far point of the eye in each meridian.

  1. The cylinder has no power along its axis, 180°, so that meridian needs only the sphere: −2.00 D.
  2. At 90° the cylinder adds its full power: −2.00 + (−0.75) = −2.75 D.
  3. Spherical equivalent = sphere + cylinder ÷ 2 = −2.00 − 0.375 = −2.375 D, written −2.38 D.
  4. A myopic eye focuses only up to its far point, at 1 ÷ |power| metres: 1 ÷ 2.00 = 0.50 m in the 180° meridian.
  5. In the 90° meridian 1 ÷ 2.75 = 0.364 m, so vertical lines and horizontal lines blur at different distances: that is astigmatism.

Answer. −2.00 D at 180° and −2.75 D at 90°, spherical equivalent −2.38 D, far points 0.50 m and 0.36 m.

Exam traps

  • mmHg is not Pa: 1 mmHg = 133.3 Pa. Convert before comparing with a pascal answer, and keep mN to N straight (20 mN = 0.020 N).
  • Imbert-Fick assumes a thin, dry, flexible cornea. The tear film pulls the prism in, so the Goldmann design fixes the area and relies on prism calibration.
  • A Schiotz scale reading is not mmHg. The conversion table depends on the weight used and the instrument.
  • Refraction is not visual acuity. A 6/6 eye with glasses can still have a -2.00 D prescription.
  • The slit lamp is not a tonometer. It is a viewing platform; pressure comes from a tonometer, often mounted on it.

Takeaways

  1. A pure-tone audiometer does one job: it finds the softest tone a person can hear at each frequency, and the answer is written in dB HL with 0 dB set to the normal ear.
  2. The audiogram separates conductive loss from nerve loss by comparing air conduction with bone conduction; a gap between them means the outer or middle ear is at fault.
  3. An endoscope needs one bundle to carry light in and a second, ordered bundle (or a camera chip) to carry the image out; if the image fibres lose their order, the picture becomes a blur of light.
  4. A dental X-ray set uses low kVp, a small focal spot, a narrow collimator cone and a short exposure, so the dose per film is a few microsieverts.
  5. Tissue is damaged by current, not by voltage; dry skin makes the body a poor conductor, but a catheter to the heart removes that protection.
  6. Macroshock needs about 100 mA through the skin to fibrillate the heart, while microshock can do it with about 100 µA or less, which is why cardiac-connected equipment has the tightest leakage limits.
  7. Electrical safety rests on three defences, earthing, isolation and double insulation, and is verified by four tests, inspection, earth continuity, insulation resistance and leakage current.
  8. A device's risk class decides its regulatory path: the higher the class, the more bench data, animal or clinical evidence and third-party audit it needs before a licence.
  9. A polygraph records skin resistance, breathing and pulse together and marks the moment of each question; it measures arousal, not truth, and courts treat it with caution.
  10. Each ophthalmic instrument produces one number: the tonometer gives pressure in mmHg, the auto refractometer gives lens power in dioptres, and the slit lamp gives a magnified optical section rather than a number.

Glossary

Air-bone gap
The difference in dB between air-conduction and bone-conduction thresholds at one frequency; a gap points to a conductive loss.
ALARA
As low as reasonably achievable, the rule that every radiation exposure is kept to the minimum that still gives the needed information.
Audiogram
A graph of hearing threshold in dB HL against frequency in hertz, drawn separately for each ear.
CDSCO
The Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation, the Indian authority that licenses medical devices and sets their risk classes.
Cine or video endoscope
An endoscope with a small solid-state camera chip at the tip instead of an image bundle, sending a video signal up the cable.
Class I equipment
Equipment protected by basic insulation plus a protective earth conductor connected to exposed metal.
Dioptre
The unit of lens power, equal to the reciprocal of the focal length in metres; a 2 D lens focuses parallel light at 0.5 m.
Double insulation
Basic insulation plus supplementary insulation with no reliance on earth, used in Class II equipment.
Focal spot
The small area on the X-ray anode where electrons strike and X-rays are produced; a smaller spot gives a sharper image.
Intraocular pressure
The fluid pressure inside the eyeball, normally about 10 to 21 mmHg, raised in glaucoma.
ISO 13485
The international standard for a quality management system in the design and manufacture of medical devices.
Isolation transformer
A transformer whose primary and secondary are not electrically connected, used to break the path from mains earth to the patient.
Keratometry
Measurement of the curvature of the front surface of the cornea, usually given as the radius in millimetres and power in dioptres in two meridians.
kVp
Peak kilovoltage across an X-ray tube, which decides the penetrating power of the beam.
Let-go current
The largest current at which a person can still release a gripped conductor, about 10 to 16 mA at mains frequency.
Masking
A noise presented to the non-test ear so that a loud tone in the test ear is not heard by the other cochlea.
Patient leakage current
Current that flows from the applied part of equipment through the patient to earth, measured under normal and single-fault conditions.
Premarket notification
A regulatory submission, commonly called 510(k) in the United States, showing a moderate-risk device is substantially equivalent to one already on the market.
Residual current device
A breaker that trips when the current in the live wire and the neutral wire differ by a small set value, indicating current is leaking to earth.
Skin conductance
The reciprocal of skin resistance, measured in microsiemens and preferred in modern work because it varies more linearly with sweat gland activity.
Total internal reflection
Light travelling in a fibre core strikes the cladding at a shallow angle and is reflected back completely, so it stays inside the fibre.
Type CF applied part
A cardiac-floating applied part, isolated from earth and from other circuits so that it is safe for direct connection to the heart.
Ventricular fibrillation
Chaotic uncoordinated contraction of the heart muscle, fatal within minutes without defibrillation, and the main electrical hazard of shock.
Working channel
The hollow tube running the length of a flexible endoscope through which biopsy forceps, snares or suction are passed.
