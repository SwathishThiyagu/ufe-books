5.1 Cardiac pacemakers and defibrillators

A pacemaker is a small implanted generator that gives a timed electrical pulse to the heart when the heart's own conduction fails. The pulse starts a contraction; the muscle does the pumping. A defibrillator gives one large shock to stop chaotic fibrillation, so a normal rhythm can restart. Pacemakers run for years on a lithium-iodine cell. Defibrillators store energy in a capacitor and release it through paddles or implanted leads. Both are used in emergency care, cardiology wards and implanted therapy.

You need resting and action potentials and ECG recording, and arrhythmia monitoring. The circuit that keeps the ECG monitor alive during a shock is covered with defibrillator protection and cardiac ablation.

Pacemaker: a timed trigger

An internal pacemaker is implanted under the skin, usually below the collarbone. A lead runs through a vein to the right ventricle; its tip electrode touches the heart muscle. An external pacemaker does the same job from outside the body. In an emergency it paces through large skin pads on the chest; because the current must cross the chest wall it needs about 40–200 mA in pulses of 20–40 ms, far more than an implanted lead, and the chest muscles twitch. After heart surgery a temporary external box drives a wire passed through a vein or sewn onto the heart. The generator has a cell, a timing circuit, an output stage and a sense amplifier.

The lead may be unipolar or bipolar. In unipolar pacing, current flows from the tip electrode to the metal case of the generator, so the path is long. In bipolar pacing, current flows between two close electrodes on the lead tip; the path is short, so it is less affected by muscle noise. Bipolar leads are common today.

Fixed-rate mode fires at a set rate, for example 70 pulses per minute (ppm), whatever the heart does. Demand mode, also called R-wave inhibited, listens for the heart's own QRS complex. If a beat is sensed within the timing window, the output is inhibited; if no beat appears, the pacemaker fires. This avoids competition with the heart's own rhythm.

The output stage charges a small capacitor from the cell between beats and empties it into the lead through a coupling capacitor, so each pulse is a short rectangle of about 2.5–5 V (programmable), a few milliamperes into a lead impedance of about 500 Ω. The coupling capacitor blocks any steady current, which would corrode the electrode and injure the tissue at the tip. A three-letter code names the type: chamber paced, chamber sensed, response to a sensed beat. VOO is fixed-rate ventricular pacing, VVI is ventricular demand pacing and DDD paces and senses both atrium and ventricle.

Batteries. The lithium-iodine cell has been the standard since the 1970s: about 2.8 V, 1–2.5 Ah, very low self-discharge and a slow, predictable voltage fall near the end of life, so the pacemaker signals that replacement is due months ahead. Earlier implants used mercury-zinc cells (1.35 V each), which lasted only about 2–3 years and released hydrogen gas; a few used nuclear cells heated by plutonium-238, and rechargeable nickel-cadmium cells were tried and dropped. External pacemakers use ordinary replaceable batteries, such as a 9 V cell, because the box stays outside the body.

  • Pacing rate is counted in pulses per minute; 60 ppm means one pulse every 1 s.
  • Pulse width is the duration of each pulse, usually 0.5–2 ms. A short pulse excites the muscle and keeps average current low.
  • A lithium-iodine cell gives about 2.8 V and a capacity in ampere-hours (Ah).
life (years) = Qbatt / (Iavg × 8760)

Qbatt is cell capacity in Ah, Iavg is average current in A, and 8760 is hours per year.

Defibrillator: one large shock

Ventricular fibrillation is chaotic electrical activity; the heart quivers and does not pump. A defibrillator charges a capacitor and discharges it through the chest or implanted leads for a few milliseconds. This large current stuns the heart muscle, so all cells recover together and a normal rhythm can restart.

An AC defibrillator applied mains current from a step-up transformer, about 250–750 V at 50 Hz for 0.25–1 s, across the heart. It needed mains power, damaged more muscle and could not be made portable, so the DC defibrillator replaced it. A DC defibrillator charges a capacitor and then empties it in one short pulse. External units use paddles or adhesive pads on the chest, one below the right collarbone beside the sternum and one over the apex, or one in front and one on the back. Internal defibrillation uses small paddles placed directly on the heart during open-chest surgery, at about 5–50 J, and an implanted cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) shocks through its own leads at up to about 35–40 J.

The DC defibrillator circuit. A step-up transformer, or a DC-to-DC converter in a battery unit, raises the voltage; a rectifier and a current-limiting resistor charge the storage capacitor, typically 16–32 µF, to as much as 5 kV. A high-voltage charge–discharge switch first connects the capacitor to the charger and then, when both paddle buttons are pressed, to the patient. In the classic Lown circuit a series inductor of about 50–100 mH shapes the discharge into a damped half-sine pulse about 5 ms long instead of a sharp spike. The stored energy is shared between the chest, about 50 Ω, and the internal resistance of the inductor, switch and cables, so the delivered energy is less than the stored energy. Biphasic units reverse the current partway with a bridge of electronic switches and adjust the pulse to the measured chest impedance.

Waveforms are monophasic or biphasic. Monophasic sends current in one direction; external settings go up to 360 J. Biphasic reverses current partway through; a typical external setting is 200 J. Biphasic is common because it often defibrillates at lower energy.

Unsynchronised defibrillation fires at once, for ventricular fibrillation. Synchronised cardioversion times the shock to the R wave, for rhythms such as atrial fibrillation; it avoids firing in the vulnerable T wave. The protection circuit that saves the ECG amplifier from this shock is in defibrillator protection and cardiac ablation.

E = ½ C V²

E is stored energy in joules, C is capacitance in farads, and V is capacitor voltage in volts. Charge is Q = C V, in coulombs.

Worked example

Question. A DC defibrillator charges a 32 µF capacitor to 5000 V. Find the energy stored and the charge on the capacitor.

  1. Given C = 32 µF = 32×10⁻⁶ F and V = 5000 V.
  2. Stored energy E = ½ C V² = 0.5 × 32×10⁻⁶ F × (5000 V)².
  3. (5000 V)² = 25×10⁶ V². So E = 0.5 × 32×10⁻⁶ × 25×10⁶ J = 400 J.
  4. Charge Q = C V = 32×10⁻⁶ F × 5000 V = 0.16 C.
  5. Not all of it reaches the patient. The capacitor discharges through the internal resistance of the inductor, switch and cables in series with the chest. With 10 Ω inside the machine and 50 Ω of chest, the patient receives 50 ÷ (50 + 10) = 0.83 of the energy: 0.83 × 400 J = 333 J. The other 67 J heats the internal resistance.

Answer. Energy stored = 400 J; charge on the capacitor = 0.16 C; with 10 Ω of internal resistance about 333 J reaches a 50 Ω chest.

Worked example 2

Question. An implanted pacemaker paces at 70 ppm with pulses of 2.5 V and 0.5 ms into a lead impedance of 500 Ω. Its sensing and timing circuits draw a steady 10 µA. The lithium-iodine cell has a usable capacity of 1.2 Ah. Find the pulse current, the charge per pulse, the average current and the battery life in years.

  1. Pulse current I = V ÷ R = 2.5 V ÷ 500 Ω = 5 mA.
  2. Charge per pulse q = I × t = 5 × 10⁻³ A × 0.5 × 10⁻³ s = 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ C = 2.5 µC.
  3. Pulses per second = 70 ÷ 60 = 1.167, so the pacing current averages 2.5 µC × 1.167 per second = 2.92 µA.
  4. Total average current Iavg = 2.92 µA + 10 µA = 12.92 µA.
  5. Charge used per year = 12.92 × 10⁻⁶ A × 8760 h = 0.1132 Ah.
  6. Life = 1.2 Ah ÷ 0.1132 Ah per year = 10.6 years. The steady circuit drain uses more charge than the pulses themselves.

Answer. Pulse current 5 mA, 2.5 µC per pulse, average current about 12.9 µA and a battery life of about 10.6 years.

Exam traps

  • Do not mix energy, charge and voltage: E = ½CV² gives joules, Q = CV gives coulombs, V is volts. The capacitance must be in farads, not microfarads.
  • A pacemaker does not pump blood. It only triggers a contraction; the heart muscle does the pumping.
  • Unsynchronised defibrillation is not synchronised cardioversion. Cardioversion times the shock to the R wave; defibrillation fires immediately.
  • Fixed-rate pacing fires at the set rate regardless of intrinsic beats; demand mode inhibits when a beat is sensed.
  • External defibrillation uses more energy than internal defibrillation because the current path is longer and loss is greater.

5.2 Heart-lung machine

The heart-lung machine, also called the cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) circuit, does the work of the heart and lungs for a short time. It drains blood from the right atrium, adds oxygen and removes carbon dioxide, then returns blood to the aorta. The surgeon can then stop the heart, open it, and repair valves or grafts while the body still gets oxygenated blood. It is used in coronary artery bypass grafting, valve replacement and some congenital heart repairs. This chapter follows the blood path and the safety devices that keep it safe.

You need cardiac output, blood flow and blood pressure measurement, and pacemakers and defibrillators for the heart's electrical rhythm.

The bypass loop

The CPB circuit is a set of tubes, a pump, an oxygenator and safety sensors. Blood leaves the patient through a venous cannula, a wide plastic tube placed in the right atrium or a large vein. It falls by gravity into a venous reservoir. The reservoir holds a store of blood, lets bubbles rise out, and gives the pump a steady supply. A level sensor watches the blood level; if the reservoir runs low, it alarms and can stop the pump. From the reservoir, a roller pump pushes blood through a membrane oxygenator. The oxygenator is the artificial lung: gas flows on one side of a thin membrane, blood on the other, so oxygen enters and carbon dioxide leaves. A heat exchanger then cools or warms the blood. The arterial cannula returns blood to the aorta. The circuit is primed with fluid before bypass, because air in the line can kill.

An arterial filter sits near the arterial end in practice. It traps bubbles and small particles before blood re-enters the aorta. A cardioplegia line is a separate small limb that gives a cold potassium-rich solution to stop the heart and protect it.

Pumps, flows and pressures

Two pump types are common. A roller pump has rollers that squeeze a plastic tube closed and push blood forward. It is occlusive: it can keep pushing against a blocked line and build dangerous pressure, so a pressure limit and alarm are needed. A centrifugal pump spins a disc or cone and throws blood outward. It is non-occlusive: if the line blocks, flow falls, and if the reservoir empties, it can run dry. Flow is set by body size, not by a single number. The usual index is 2.2–2.4 L/min per m² of body surface area (BSA). A 1.7 m² adult therefore gets about 3.7–4.1 L/min. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) during bypass is kept around 50–80 mmHg, lower than normal because the blood is non-pulsatile and diluted. The circuit is filled with priming fluid, about 1–2 L in an adult, which dilutes the blood. Heparin is given before cannulation to stop clotting in the tubes. Ventilation is stopped while the patient is on bypass and restarted when bypass is reduced and the lungs take over gas exchange again (see ventilator types and modes).

BSA = √(height × weight ÷ 3600)

height is in cm, weight in kg, and BSA is in m² (Mosteller formula).

Flow = flow index × BSA

flow index is 2.2–2.4 L/min/m² and BSA is in m².

Safety and the team

Bypass is safe only because several alarms watch it. A bubble detector on the arterial line uses ultrasound to spot air; it stops the pump before air reaches the brain. A level sensor on the reservoir stops the pump if blood runs low. A low-pressure alarm warns of a disconnected or leaking line or falling flow; a high-pressure alarm warns of a clamped, kinked or blocked line. A backup battery keeps the pump running during a power cut, a real risk wherever the mains supply is unreliable. The perfusionist runs the machine, checks blood gases and flow, and talks to the surgeon. Heparin is reversed with protamine at the end. The heart is restarted after the cross-clamp is removed, and bypass is slowly reduced. The circuit is not a treatment by itself; it is a temporary support.

Worked example

Question. A patient is 170 cm tall and weighs 60 kg. Find the body surface area (BSA) and the pump flow at a flow index of 2.4 L/min/m². State the flow to two decimal places.

  1. Use the Mosteller formula: BSA = √(height × weight ÷ 3600), with height in cm and weight in kg.
  2. Insert the values: BSA = √(170 × 60 ÷ 3600) = √(10200 ÷ 3600) = √2.8333.
  3. Take the square root: BSA = 1.6833 m², about 1.68 m².
  4. Find flow: Flow = flow index × BSA = 2.4 L/min/m² × 1.6833 m² = 4.0399 L/min.
  5. Round to two decimal places: flow = 4.04 L/min. The perfusionist sets the pump near 4 L/min and checks that mean arterial pressure stays around 60 mmHg.

Answer. BSA is about 1.68 m² and pump flow is about 4.04 L/min.

Exam traps

  • The oxygenator is an artificial lung, not only an oxygen adder. It must remove carbon dioxide too; if CO₂ builds up, blood pH falls, which the perfusionist sees on the blood gas results.
  • Do not set a fixed 5 L/min for every adult. Flow is indexed to body surface area, 2.2–2.4 L/min/m², so a small adult gets less flow.
  • A roller pump is occlusive and can build pressure against a blocked line; a centrifugal pump is non-occlusive and can run dry. They need different alarms and limits.
  • MAP during bypass is about 50–80 mmHg, lower than the usual non-bypass value of about 70–100 mmHg. A low reading can be normal for bypass.
  • Heparin is given before bypass and reversed with protamine; it is not the cardioplegia drug. Cardioplegia is a cold potassium-rich solution given through a separate line.

5.3 Ventilators: types and modes

A ventilator pushes a breath into the lungs and lets it out. It is used when a patient cannot breathe enough, in the ICU, operation theatre or ambulance. Positive-pressure ventilators blow gas in; negative-pressure ventilators, such as the iron lung, lower pressure around the chest so air is sucked in. Every breath has three events: trigger, limit and cycle. The variable that ends inspiration often appears in the mode name, but not always. Pressure control is time-cycled and is named for its pressure limit, not its cycle. This idea sorts most ventilator vocabulary.

You need spirometry, lung volumes and the intra-alveolar and thoracic pressures from respiratory measurement, because ventilator pressures act across the same lung mechanics.

Breath phases and ventilator types

Negative-pressure ventilators lower the pressure around the chest. The iron lung is the classic example: the patient lies in a chamber, chamber pressure falls, and air enters the lungs because thoracic pressure is now higher than chamber pressure. Positive-pressure ventilators do the opposite: they push gas into the airway through an endotracheal tube, tracheostomy or tight-fitting mask. Invasive ventilation uses a tube; non-invasive ventilation uses a mask. Every positive-pressure breath has three separate events. The trigger starts inspiration; it can be a set time, a drop in airway pressure, or a measured patient flow. The limit caps inspiration; it can be a set flow or a set pressure. The cycle ends inspiration; it can be volume, pressure, time, flow or the patient. The trigger, limit and cycle need not use the same variable. In pressure-control mode the limit is pressure, but the cycle is usually time. A fourth variable, the baseline, sets the pressure or flow to which the patient exhales; PEEP is a raised baseline.

The five cycling variables

Five cycling variables are named in syllabi. A volume-cycled breath ends after a set tidal volume; the ventilator measures volume and stops inspiration, so pressure rises if compliance falls. A pressure-cycled breath ends when airway pressure reaches a set value; the volume delivered then depends on compliance and resistance, so it can fall if the lung stiffens. A time-cycled breath ends after a set inspiratory time; infant ventilators often use this. A flow-cycled breath ends when inspiratory flow falls to a set fraction of its peak; pressure support uses this, because flow slows as the lung fills. A patient-cycled breath ends when the patient exhales or a demand valve closes; CPAP uses this. The figure places these five under one question.

Modes and settings

Modes combine trigger, limit, cycle and settings. Continuous mandatory ventilation (CMV) gives set breaths at a set rate; the patient cannot trigger extra breaths. Assist-control gives the set rate, but a patient trigger also receives the full set tidal volume. Synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation (SIMV) gives set mandatory breaths and allows spontaneous breaths between them; pressure support can assist those spontaneous breaths. Pressure support is patient-triggered, pressure-limited and flow-cycled. Pressure control is time-cycled and pressure-limited. Volume control is volume-cycled. CPAP is continuous positive airway pressure; the patient breathes spontaneously against a constant pressure. PEEP is positive end-expiratory pressure; it keeps small airways and alveoli open at end expiration and can be added to any mode. Settings: tidal volume 6–8 mL/kg of predicted body weight (PBW); PBW is from height, not scale weight. Respiratory rate is breaths per minute. FiO₂ is the fraction of inspired oxygen, from 0.21 in room air to 1.0. I:E ratio is inspiratory time divided by expiratory time, often 1:2 to 1:3. PEEP is often started at 5 cm H₂O. Alarm limits watch high pressure, low volume and apnea. Dry gas needs a humidifier; see humidifiers, nebulizers and inhalers.

Worked example

Question. A male patient is 175 cm tall. Find the tidal volume range at 6–8 mL/kg of predicted body weight. PBW = 50 + 0.91 × (height in cm − 152.4) for men and 45.5 + 0.91 × (height in cm − 152.4) for women; use the male formula here.

  1. Height above 152.4 cm = 175 − 152.4 = 22.6 cm.
  2. PBW = 50 + 0.91 × 22.6 = 50 + 20.566 = 70.566 kg; round to 70.6 kg.
  3. Low tidal volume = 6 mL/kg × 70.6 kg = 423.6 mL; round to 424 mL.
  4. High tidal volume = 8 mL/kg × 70.6 kg = 564.8 mL; round to 565 mL.

Answer. Set tidal volume between about 424 mL and 565 mL.

Worked example 2

Question. A volume-controlled ventilator is set to a rate of 15 breaths/min, an I:E ratio of 1:2, a tidal volume of 500 mL with constant inspiratory flow, and PEEP 5 cmH₂O. The monitor shows a peak pressure of 28 cmH₂O and a plateau pressure of 20 cmH₂O. Find the inspiratory and expiratory times, the minute ventilation, the inspiratory flow, the static compliance and the airway resistance.

  1. One breath lasts 60 ÷ 15 = 4 s.
  2. I:E = 1:2 splits it into 3 parts: Ti = 4 ÷ 3 = 1.33 s and Te = 2 × 4 ÷ 3 = 2.67 s.
  3. Minute ventilation = tidal volume × rate = 0.5 L × 15 = 7.5 L/min.
  4. Inspiratory flow = 0.5 L ÷ 1.33 s = 0.375 L/s = 22.5 L/min.
  5. Static compliance = Vt ÷ (plateau − PEEP) = 500 mL ÷ (20 − 5) cmH₂O = 33.3 mL/cmH₂O, lower than the 50–100 of a healthy ventilated adult, so the lungs are stiff.
  6. Airway resistance = (peak − plateau) ÷ flow = (28 − 20) ÷ 0.375 = 21.3 cmH₂O per L/s, about twice the usual value for an adult tube, so look for secretions or a kinked or narrow tube.

Answer. Ti = 1.33 s, Te = 2.67 s, minute ventilation 7.5 L/min, flow 0.375 L/s (22.5 L/min), compliance 33.3 mL/cmH₂O and resistance 21.3 cmH₂O per L/s.

Exam traps

  • Pressure control is usually time-cycled, not pressure-cycled; the name comes from the pressure limit.
  • Use predicted body weight from height for tidal volume, not the weight on the weighing scale.
  • CPAP is not a full mandatory breath; the patient does the work while pressure stays positive.
  • PEEP is end-expiratory pressure, not peak inspiratory pressure; a high peak pressure alarm is different.
  • Pressure-cycled ventilation gives a variable tidal volume when lung compliance changes; volume-cycled ventilation keeps volume but pressure may rise.

5.4 Humidifiers, nebulizers and inhalators

Dry medical gas from a cylinder or pipeline holds almost no water. The airway lining needs water and heat; without them it crusts, bleeds and narrows. A humidifier adds water as vapour and never carries a drug. A nebulizer makes fine droplets and can carry a drug. An inhaler delivers a measured drug dose. These devices are used in oxygen therapy, asthma, COPD, intensive care and at home.

You need spirometry and lung volumes, and ventilator types and modes for the ventilator circuit.

Why dry gas hurts the airway

Normal breathing conditions air in the nose. By the time gas reaches the carina it is close to body temperature, 37 °C, and nearly saturated with water vapour. Saturated gas at 37 °C holds 44 mg of water per litre. Absolute humidity is the mass of water vapour in a litre of gas, written mg/L. The saturation value changes with temperature. The point in the airway where gas reaches 37 °C and full saturation is the isothermic saturation boundary. It sits below the carina in normal breathing. Dry oxygen moves this boundary deeper. The lower airway then gives up its own water and heat. Mucus thickens, cilia stop moving, and the patient may wheeze or bleed.

Mw = AH × Q

Mw is water mass per minute (mg/min); AH is the absolute humidity of saturated gas at 37 °C (44 mg/L); Q is gas flow (L/min). For completely dry gas, the water to be added equals the full saturated value, AH = 44 mg/L. For partly humid gas, subtract the humidity already present before multiplying by Q.

Humidifiers: adding vapour

Humidifiers add water as vapour. They do not put drug into the gas. A bubble-through humidifier passes gas through a bottle of sterile water; gas leaves with vapour. It is simple and cheap, but bubbling makes noise and the water path can become contaminated. A pass-over humidifier flows gas over a wet surface or wick and adds vapour without bubbling. A heated humidifier warms a chamber, often to 32–37 °C, so the gas can carry more water. A heated-wire circuit warms the tube to stop vapour condensing as rainout. The heater control uses a temperature sensor at the chamber or near the patient. A heat-and-moisture exchanger (HME), also called an artificial nose, traps heat and water from exhaled gas on a hydrophobic membrane or foam and returns some to the next breath. It needs no water supply and no power. It suits short transport or a patient whose upper airway is bypassed by an endotracheal tube or tracheostomy. It is not needed when the natural upper airway is working. It is not good for long ventilation with high humidity, thick secretions or low tidal volume, because it adds dead space and resistance. The worked example shows why a heated chamber must be refilled: 10 L/min of dry oxygen needs about 26 g of water each hour at full saturation.

Too much water is also a hazard. Rainout in the tubing can run into the patient or block the tube, wet flow sensors make the ventilator read wrong volumes, a chamber set above 37 °C can scald the airway, and small infants can take in too much water. A warm water reservoir also grows bacteria, so sterile water, closed refill systems and regular circuit changes are used.

Nebulizers, inhalers and oxygen delivery

Nebulizers make droplets, not just vapour, so they can carry a drug. A jet nebulizer uses compressed air or oxygen through a narrow venturi; the low pressure draws liquid up a tube, and a baffle breaks it into droplets. The mass median aerodynamic diameter (MMAD) is the droplet size at which half the mass is in smaller droplets. For lower-airway deposition, MMAD is usually 1–5 µm. Smaller droplets may be exhaled like a gas; larger droplets hit the mouth and throat. An ultrasonic nebulizer uses a piezoelectric crystal vibrating at high frequency to throw a fountain of fine droplets. It is quiet and needs no gas flow, but it can heat the liquid and is less useful for some drugs. A mesh nebulizer pushes liquid through a vibrating plate with tiny holes, giving fine droplets with low residual volume. Inhalers give a measured dose. A metered-dose inhaler (MDI) has a pressurised canister with drug and propellant; one press releases a fixed dose. A spacer holds the cloud so the patient can breathe it in slowly; it cuts drug deposited in the mouth and throat. A dry-powder inhaler (DPI) holds drug powder; the patient's breath breaks the powder into particles. It needs no propellant but needs enough inspiratory flow. Oxygen delivery devices: a nasal cannula gives low-flow oxygen through two prongs; a simple face mask gives a higher flow; a venturi mask uses a jet and air entrainment to give a fixed inspired oxygen fraction (FiO₂), such as 24%, 28% or 35%. Humidification is added when flow is high, when the gas is dry, or when the upper airway is bypassed.

Worked example

Question. A patient breathes 10 L/min of completely dry oxygen. Saturated gas at 37 °C holds 44 mg of water per litre. (a) How much water must be added per minute to saturate the gas? (b) How much per hour, in grams?

  1. Identify the flow Q = 10 L/min and the final absolute humidity AH = 44 mg/L.
  2. Water per minute = AH × Q = 44 mg/L × 10 L/min = 440 mg/min.
  3. Water per hour = 440 mg/min × 60 min/h = 26,400 mg/h.
  4. Convert milligrams to grams: 26,400 mg ÷ 1000 mg/g = 26.4 g/h.

Answer. The gas needs 440 mg of water per minute, which is 26.4 g per hour.

Worked example 2

Question. Room air at 25 °C and 50 % relative humidity is given to a patient at 10 L/min through a tracheostomy, so the nose does not condition it. Exam traps

  • An HME is not a heated humidifier. It recycles exhaled heat and water and has no water chamber.

5.5 Diathermy: short-wave, microwave and ultrasonic

Diathermy means heating deep tissue on purpose without cutting the skin. A short-wave unit passes a radio-frequency current through the body at 27.12 MHz. A microwave unit beams 2450 MHz energy into tissue. An ultrasonic unit sends 0.8–1 MHz sound waves through a coupling gel. The tissue absorbs the energy and becomes warm. This relieves pain, relaxes muscle spasm and speeds repair in physiotherapy. Surgical diathermy uses the same radio-frequency idea to cut and coagulate, but that is a separate application, not therapy.

You need: basic AC circuits — frequency f in hertz, wavelength λ = c ÷ f with c the speed of light, and impedance — plus the idea that current needs a complete path.

Short-wave diathermy at 27.12 MHz

A short-wave diathermy unit takes 50 Hz mains, rectifies it, and feeds an oscillator. The oscillator runs at 27.12 MHz. A power amplifier raises the signal to a useful output, commonly a few hundred watts at most. A matching network tunes the amplifier to the patient, because the body is not a fixed 50 ohm load. The output then reaches the applicator: two insulated plates, or a coil. In capacitive coupling the plates sit on either side of the limb, so the tissue between them is the dielectric (the material between the plates) and it heats. In inductive coupling a coil is placed near the body and its changing magnetic field induces small circulating currents, called eddy currents. Some units allow both.

Tissue heats by two losses. Dielectric loss: the alternating electric field makes polar water molecules rotate. Their collisions with neighbours turn field energy into heat. Resistive loss: ions in tissue drift with the field and collide. Both depend on frequency and on tissue composition. Muscle has more water and ions than fat, so it usually absorbs more short-wave energy.

The body is not heated by a travelling beam at this frequency. The free-space wavelength, about 11 m, is far longer than the limb, so the tissue sits in the near field between the plates or under the coil. Tissue absorbs relatively little of a 27.12 MHz field per centimetre, so the heating reaches deep muscle. As frequency rises, absorption per centimetre rises and the heating stays nearer the surface. 27.12 MHz is a reserved industrial–scientific–medical frequency, not any random short-wave radio channel. 13.56 MHz is also used.

Microwave and ultrasonic diathermy

Microwave diathermy uses 2450 MHz. A microwave oscillator, such as a magnetron, feeds a reflector applicator. The applicator directs a beam into the tissue, and the water in tissue absorbs it. The wavelength in air is about 12.2 cm, but tissue absorbs strongly, so useful heating is only a few centimetres deep. Keep the applicator at the stated distance. Do not treat over the eye or testis, where little tissue protects the organ. Microwaves are convenient for superficial joints and muscles.

Ultrasonic diathermy uses a piezoelectric transducer (a crystal that vibrates when driven by a voltage) at 0.8–1 MHz. The transducer converts electrical drive into mechanical vibration. A coupling gel removes air, because air reflects ultrasound. The beam enters tissue and is absorbed; the patient feels warmth. 1 MHz reaches several centimetres. 3 MHz is used for more superficial tissue. Bone and air interfaces absorb or reflect strongly, so avoid directing the beam across a bone edge or through air.

All three methods raise tissue temperature. On short-wave and microwave units the dial sets output power in watts and a timer sets the minutes. On an ultrasound unit the dial sets intensity, the power per square centimetre of the head, usually 0.1–3 W/cm²; total power is intensity times the effective radiating area (ERA) of the head, and pulsed mode, for example 20 % on, lowers the average heating. The operator starts low and asks the patient about warmth and pain. Skin sensation is the safety sensor; if it is impaired, the patient cannot warn of a burn.

Dose, contraindications and surgical diathermy

Dose is not just a number on the dial. It is power in watts applied for a time in minutes, and it depends on the applicator, the body part and the patient's sensation. A physiotherapy session may last 10–20 minutes, but the setting is adjusted to a comfortable warmth. Burns can occur at high power, with poor coupling, with wrinkled towels or with a metal implant that concentrates the field.

Contraindications include metal implants in the field, pregnancy over the abdomen or pelvis, ischaemic tissue (poor blood supply), bleeding tissue and impaired sensation. A cardiac pacemaker or other electronic implant is also a reason to avoid short-wave and microwave diathermy, because the field can interfere with the device. Do not treat over the eye, testis or growing bone with microwave or ultrasound unless specifically trained.

Surgical diathermy, or electrosurgery, uses radio-frequency current for cutting and coagulation (clotting). A small active electrode creates high current density, so tissue vaporises and the cut seals small vessels. A large return plate spreads the current. It is not therapy and not the same as heating a joint. The same RF principle appears in cardiac ablation, where a catheter burns a small patch of heart muscle; see defibrillator protection and cardiac ablation.

5.6 Electrotherapy and nerve stimulators

A nerve or muscle fires when its membrane is pushed past a threshold by charge. Electrotherapy passes a controlled current pulse through skin electrodes to do that on purpose. The same idea relieves pain (TENS), retrains a weak muscle (EMS/NMES), contracts a paralysed muscle (FES), or helps a surgeon find a motor nerve with a hand-held probe. The instrument is a pulse generator with a constant-current output; its dose is amplitude, pulse width and frequency.

You need the action potential, nerve conduction and EMG recording, and the current limits from electrical safety.

The pulse and its parameters

Tissue is an electrolyte. Current enters through a metal electrode, crosses skin, runs along nerve and muscle, and leaves through a return electrode. A nerve responds to the charge crossing its membrane in a short time, not to the average current over a minute. So the stimulator makes short pulses with a gap between them.

Four numbers describe the pulse. Amplitude is peak current, usually 0–100 mA for surface stimulation. Pulse width is how long one pulse lasts, usually 50–400 µs. Frequency is pulses per second, usually 1–150 Hz. Charge per pulse is amplitude × width.

Q = I × t

Q is charge per pulse in coulombs (C); I is the set current in amperes (A); t is the pulse width in seconds (s).

A fifth number is the duty cycle: the fraction of time the current is on. With width t and frequency f, D = t × f, and average current is I × D. A 20 mA pulse of 200 µs at 100 Hz has only 0.4 mA average current, but the nerve sees the 20 mA peak. The peak excites; the average is safe for skin.

D = t × f

D is duty cycle (a fraction, no unit); t is pulse width in seconds (s); f is pulse frequency in hertz (Hz).

Electrodes are the other half of the circuit. Charge density = charge per pulse ÷ electrode area, in coulombs per square centimetre (C/cm²), or more conveniently microcoulombs per square centimetre (µC/cm²). For a biphasic pulse, use the charge of one phase. Too much charge density burns skin. Modern units use biphasic pulses — a positive pulse followed by an equal negative pulse — so net charge per cycle is zero and electrochemical damage is reduced.

Forms of electrotherapy

TENS (transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation) puts electrodes over the painful area or the nerve supplying it. It excites large sensory fibres (Aβ) that carry touch and pressure. Gate-control theory says their signals close a 'gate' in the spinal cord, so smaller pain fibres (Aδ and C) cannot pass theirs up. TENS relieves pain without a muscle contraction. Typical settings: 10–100 Hz, 50–200 µs, comfortable tingling.

EMS (electrical muscle stimulation) and NMES (neuromuscular electrical stimulation) act on motor nerves: the nerve fires and the muscle contracts. They re-educate a muscle after injury or surgery, when the patient cannot contract it voluntarily. FES (functional electrical stimulation) times the same pulses to a function, such as lifting a foot during walking in foot-drop, or cycling in spinal-cord injury.

Interferential therapy (IFT) uses two currents at about 4 kHz that differ by 1–150 Hz. They cross in tissue and produce a low-frequency beat at the difference frequency. The high carrier penetrates skin with less discomfort; the beat does the stimulation. Russian current is a 2.5 kHz sine wave switched on and off in bursts at 50 Hz — typically 10 ms on and 10 ms off, repeated 50 times a second, so the burst duty cycle is 50 %. It is used for muscle strengthening.

Older names are still asked. Galvanic current is steady direct current (DC), used for iontophoresis or wound healing. Faradic current is a short interrupted DC pulse that excites motor nerves. Both are less used now, but exams still compare them with TENS.

Nerve and muscle stimulators

A stimulator has five blocks, shown in the figure. A battery powers an oscillator that sets the frequency (1–150 Hz). A pulse shaper sets the width (50–400 µs) and makes the pulse rectangular or biphasic. The output stage is a constant-current source: it changes its voltage to push the set current through the electrode–skin resistance, which varies from person to person and with sweat. Skin electrodes carry the current to the nerve or muscle.

A nerve stimulator for surgery is a hand-held probe with a fine tip. The surgeon touches it near a suspected motor nerve; a small current (often 0.5–2 mA) makes the muscle twitch. That identifies the nerve before it is cut or avoided. The return electrode is a large pad on the skin. The same probe can test a nerve after repair.

A surface stimulator for TENS or EMS is a small battery box with two or four channels, each with an independent amplitude control. Output is constant current up to about 100 mA into 500–1000 Ω. The pulse is usually biphasic and charge-balanced. The device limits charge per pulse and average current to keep skin safe; the safety limits are covered under electrical safety. Nerve conduction velocity measurement uses the same kind of stimulator, but it is a recording test, not therapy.

Worked example

Question. A TENS unit is set to amplitude 20 mA, pulse width 200 µs and frequency 100 Hz. The pulse is rectangular and monophasic for this calculation. Exam traps

  • Confusing pulse frequency with the internal carrier: Russian current has a 2.5 kHz carrier in 50 Hz bursts; interferential therapy has a 4 kHz carrier with a 1–150 Hz beat.
  • Assuming more current is always better. Peak current excites the nerve, but charge density at the electrode decides whether skin burns.
  • Mixing TENS and EMS. TENS acts on sensory fibres for pain and normally causes no twitch; EMS acts on motor fibres to make a muscle contract.
  • Using mixed units in duty cycle. 200 µs × 100 Hz = 0.02, not 20. Convert width to seconds first.
  • Thinking nerve conduction velocity measurement is electrotherapy. It uses a stimulator, but it is a recording test.

5.7 Lithotripsy

A kidney stone is a hard mineral mass, often 5–10 mm across, that forms in the kidney and blocks the flow of urine. Lithotripsy breaks it into small pieces with focused shock waves, and the pieces leave the body in the urine. No cut, no open surgery. The common form is extracorporeal shock-wave lithotripsy (ESWL); extracorporeal means outside the body. It is the first treatment offered for most kidney and upper-ureter stones. Harder or deeper stones are broken from inside with a laser or an ultrasonic probe.

You need only the school physics of waves and focusing: a wave carries energy, and a curved surface can bring it to a point.

How a shock wave breaks a stone

A shock wave is a single pressure pulse, not a continuous sound. Its pressure rises to a peak in a few nanoseconds, falls, swings below zero briefly, then settles. In water the peak is tens of megapascals — hundreds of times atmospheric pressure. It carries energy, not electric charge.

Two effects break the stone. The stone is stiffer and denser than the water and tissue around it: an acoustic impedance mismatch. And the compressive pulse passes into the stone, reaches the far face, and reflects back as a tensile (pulling) wave. Brittle mineral is weak in tension, so cracks open at the back face.

Cavitation helps. The negative part of the wave pulls dissolved gas into tiny bubbles; they collapse violently and hammer the surface. The aim is fragments of 1–2 mm, which travel down the ureter with the urine; pieces of 4–5 mm or more can lodge in the ureter and block it.

Generating and focusing the shock wave

The classic source is electrohydraulic. A spark gap sits under water at the first focus F1 of an ellipsoid reflector. A capacitor of about 80 nF charged to 15–25 kV discharges across the gap, the water flashes into plasma, a bubble grows and collapses, and an acoustic pulse spreads in all directions.

On an ellipsoid, the distance from focus F1 to any point on the surface plus the distance from that point to focus F2 is constant. Every ray leaving F1 therefore travels the same path length to reach F2, so all rays arrive together and add in phase. The stone is placed at F2, where the pressure is many times the pressure near the spark.

PF1 + PF2 = 2a

P is any point on the ellipsoidal reflector; F1 is the spark-gap focus; F2 is the focus where the stone sits; 2a is the constant path length, in metres.

An electromagnetic source fires a pulse of current through a flat coil behind a metal membrane. The membrane is driven away from the coil and launches an acoustic pressure pulse into the water, and a lens or a cylindrical reflector focuses it. No electrodes wear out, so every firing of thousands is the same. A piezoelectric source mounts several hundred ceramic crystals on a spherical bowl and fires them together; the bowl curvature is the focus. The wide bowl spreads the energy over a large area of skin, so the skin and its nerves receive little and the treatment hurts less; the energy becomes intense only in the small focal zone, which must therefore be aimed precisely.

Coupling matters more than any other detail. Air reflects almost all acoustic energy, so the path from source to skin must be water or gel. Old machines used a water bath the patient sat in; modern ones use a water-filled cushion or a gel pad pressed to the flank. A trapped air bubble blocks the wave and burns the skin.

One session, and breaking the stone from inside

  1. Locate the stone by X-ray or ultrasound and bring it onto the shock-wave axis.
  2. Give analgesia or sedation; the patient lies on the table over the water cushion.
  3. Couple with gel and start at low energy, raising it in steps.
  4. Fire 2000–3000 shocks at 60–120 shocks per minute, checking position on the image.
  5. Stop when the fragments are small enough to pass.
  6. Advise plenty of water; fragments leave over days to weeks.

Rate matters. Firing quickly leaves no time for the cavitation bubbles of one shock to dissolve, so the next shock hits a bubble cloud that scatters it. Slower firing, 60 to 120 shocks per minute, gives better fragmentation and less tissue bruising, even though the session runs longer.

ESWL fails on very hard stones such as cystine, on stones above about 2 cm where the fragment load is too heavy, in obese patients where the skin-to-stone distance is long, and on stones low in the ureter where pelvic bone blocks the path. A ureteric stent, a thin plastic tube left in the ureter, keeps urine flowing so a column of fragments cannot block the kidney.

Intracorporeal lithotripsy breaks the stone from inside the body. A ureteroscope passes up the urethra and bladder to the stone. A holmium:YAG laser sends 2.1 µm light pulses down a thin silica fibre and drills the stone; an ultrasonic probe vibrates at about 20–25 kHz, breaks the stone and can suck the dust out through a hollow core. Laser suits hard stones, ultrasonic suits large soft ones.

Worked example

Question. An electrohydraulic lithotripter charges an 80 nF capacitor to 20 kV and discharges it across the spark gap. One session delivers 2500 shocks. Find the electrical energy per shock and the total energy delivered in the session.

  1. Capacitor energy E = ½CV², with C = 80 nF = 80 × 10⁻⁹ F and V = 20 kV = 20 × 10³ V.
  2. V² = (20 × 10³)² = 4 × 10⁸ V².
  3. E = 0.5 × 80 × 10⁻⁹ F × 4 × 10⁸ V² = 16 J per shock.
  4. Total energy = 16 J × 2500 = 40 000 J = 40 kJ.
  5. Only a small part of each 16 J reaches the stone as acoustic energy; most is lost in the spark, the water path and the reflector.

Answer. 16 J per shock, and 40 kJ (40 000 J) for the whole session.

Worked example 2

Question. An ellipsoidal reflector has a semi-major axis a = 150 mm and a semi-minor axis b = 90 mm. Find the distance from the centre to each focus, the distance between the spark at F₁ and the stone at F₂, the path length of every reflected ray, and the time a reflected pulse takes to reach the stone through water at 1500 m/s.

  2. Distance F₁F₂ = 2c = 240 mm, so the stone sits 240 mm from the spark.
  3. Every reflected path PF₁ + PF₂ = 2a = 300 mm, whatever point P of the reflector the ray strikes.
  4. Travel time = 0.300 m ÷ 1500 m/s = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ s = 0.2 ms for every reflected ray, so they all arrive together.
  5. The direct, unreflected wave covers only 240 mm and arrives in 0.16 ms, 0.04 ms earlier, but it is weak because it is not focused.

Answer. c = 120 mm, F₁F₂ = 240 mm, every reflected path is 300 mm and every reflected ray arrives after 0.2 ms.

Exam traps

  • The word shock misleads. This is an acoustic pressure pulse; no current flows through the patient. The 20 kV stays inside the water bath or the coil circuit.
  • Label the foci correctly. F₁ is the near focus, inside the reflector, where the spark fires; F₂ is the far focus, outside the reflector at a fixed distance, where the stone is placed. The table is moved until the stone sits at F₂.
  • In ½CV² put C in farads and V in volts. Substituting 20 for 20 kV makes the energy a million times too small.
  • ESWL is not for every stone: stones above about 2 cm, cystine stones and stones low in the ureter usually need another method.
  • A ureteric stent does not treat the stone; it only keeps urine flowing while the fragments pass.

5.8 Cryogenic techniques

Cold below about −40 °C destroys living tissue. A cryoprobe, a metal tip cooled by liquid nitrogen or by an expanding gas, is pressed on or pushed into a lesion, and the ice that forms kills the cells. Surgeons use it on warts and skin lesions, on small kidney and liver tumours, and inside the heart to stop atrial fibrillation. The same cold, applied more gently with a protective chemical, preserves blood, semen and tissue for years inside a dewar. This chapter covers the physics, the probe, the freeze–thaw cycle and the safety rules.

You need sensible heat (Q = mcΔT), latent heat (Q = mL) and specific heat capacity from basic heat transfer; compare with diathermy, which adds heat instead of removing it.

How cold kills a cell

Ice does not form inside the cell first. It forms in the fluid between cells. Pure water joins the ice, and the fluid left behind becomes a concentrated salt solution. The cell loses water, shrinks, and its proteins are damaged. If cooling is fast, ice also forms inside the cell, and those sharp crystals cut the membranes and organelles.

Thaw is the second half of the kill. Ice melts, water rushes back into the shrunken cell, and a membrane already weakened by the first freeze breaks. A slow thaw builds bigger crystals and does more damage than a fast thaw.

Q = m c ΔT₁ + mw L + m cf ΔT₂

Q is the heat removed, in joules (J); m is the mass of tissue, in kg; c is the specific heat of unfrozen tissue, about 3.5 kJ/kg·K; ΔT₁ is the fall from body temperature to freezing, in K; mw is the mass of water that freezes, in kg; L is the latent heat of fusion of water, 334 kJ/kg; cf is the specific heat of frozen tissue, about 1.8 kJ/kg·K; ΔT₂ is the fall below freezing, in K. Tissue freezes near 0 °C (in fact a degree or two below), and not all of its water freezes, so the latent term is an upper estimate.

A single freeze is unreliable. Surgeons therefore use the freeze–thaw–freeze cycle.

  1. Freeze until the ice ball reaches a few millimetres beyond the edge of the lesion: the edge of the ice is near 0 °C, and the lethal −40 °C line lies a few millimetres inside it.
  2. Hold for a few minutes so the cold soaks in.
  3. Let it thaw slowly. Do not assist the thaw.
  4. Freeze a second time, then let it thaw finally.

Below about −40 °C almost no cell survives. Above that temperature some cells recover and the lesion regrows. That −40 °C line inside the tissue, not the tip temperature, is the real target.

Cryogens and the cryoprobe

There are two ways to make a cold tip. One is to pump a cryogen, a very cold liquid, through a chamber at the tip. The other is to let a compressed gas expand through a small nozzle. Both pull heat out of the tissue by conduction.

Liquid nitrogen boils at −196 °C at one atmosphere. In a closed probe it flows into a small chamber at the metal tip, boils there, and the vapour leaves through the handle. The probe does not store cold, it only carries it, so the flow must be steady.

The Joule–Thomson effect is the cooling that happens when a compressed gas is forced through a narrow orifice and expands. No moving parts and no separate refrigerator are needed. A gas cools this way only below its inversion temperature; argon and nitrous oxide are below theirs at room temperature, whereas helium warms on expansion.

A thermocouple at the tip measures temperature. A small heater wire lets the surgeon warm the tip on demand, so it releases from the tissue without tearing it. That is called an active thaw.

Where cryogenic technique is used

Skin. Warts, verrucae, actinic keratosis and small skin cancers are frozen with a spray or a cotton swab for 10–30 s, twice, with a thaw between. The blister that forms lifts the dead tissue off.

Tumours. In cryoablation one or more probes are pushed into a kidney, liver, prostate or lung tumour under ultrasound or CT guidance. The ice ball is visible on the image. Two cycles are used, and the ice must cover the tumour plus a margin.

Heart. A cryoballoon freezes the tissue around the pulmonary veins, where atrial fibrillation often starts. defibrillator protection and cardiac ablation compares this with radio-frequency ablation.

Storage. Semen, embryos, stem cells, skin and rare blood units are kept frozen for years; ordinary donated red cells are not frozen but kept at about 4 °C for up to about six weeks. A cryoprotectant such as glycerol or DMSO is added first, so water leaves the cells before freezing. The sample is cooled slowly, about 1 °C per minute, and stored in a dewar, a vacuum flask, either in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C or in its vapour at about −150 °C. Thawing is fast, in a 37 °C water bath.

Safety with cryogens

Liquid nitrogen is safe to use only when the cold, the gas and the pressure it makes are all controlled.

  • Cold burns and frostbite: never touch an uninsulated probe; wear a face shield and loose gloves.
  • Asphyxiation: one litre of liquid nitrogen gives about 700 litres of gas at room temperature, which pushes oxygen out of a closed room without any smell.
  • Vent the boil-off: use dewars in a ventilated space with an oxygen monitor; never seal a dewar.
  • Pressure: fit relief valves on any vessel that can be closed, because trapped liquid expands about 700 times when it warms.

Worked example

Question. During cryosurgery a 1 g piece of tissue must be cooled from 37 °C to −40 °C, the lethal temperature. The tissue is 80 % water by mass and freezes at about 0 °C. Take the specific heat of tissue as 3.5 kJ/kg·K above freezing and 1.8 kJ/kg·K when frozen, and the latent heat of fusion of water as 334 kJ/kg. (a) Find the total heat removed. (b) Find the minimum mass of liquid nitrogen needed, if each kilogram of nitrogen absorbs 199 kJ as it evaporates.

  1. Convert the mass: m = 1 g = 0.001 kg.
  2. Cooling to freezing: Q₁ = m c ΔT₁ = 0.001 kg × 3500 J/kg·K × 37 K = 129.5 J.
  3. Freezing the water: mw = 0.8 × 0.001 kg = 0.0008 kg, so Q₂ = mw L = 0.0008 kg × 334 000 J/kg = 267.2 J.
  4. Cooling the frozen tissue from 0 °C to −40 °C: Q₃ = m cf ΔT₂ = 0.001 kg × 1800 J/kg·K × 40 K = 72 J.
  5. Total heat: Q = 129.5 + 267.2 + 72 = 468.7 J. The latent term is the largest; it is an upper estimate, because some water stays unfrozen.
  6. Nitrogen needed: 468.7 J ÷ 199 000 J/kg = 2.355 × 10⁻³ kg = 2.36 g.
  7. This is the ideal minimum. A real probe also cools the handle, the lines and the tissue around the lesion, so several times as much nitrogen is used.

Answer. The heat removed is 468.7 J for one gram of tissue, and the minimum liquid nitrogen needed is 2.36 g if no cold is wasted.

Exam traps

  • Cryotherapy removes heat; diathermy adds heat. They are opposite treatments with similar-sounding names.
  • A −196 °C tip does not mean −196 °C in the tissue. The ice front must pass −40 °C at the far edge of the lesion, so probe placement and imaging decide success.
  • One long freeze is weaker than two shorter freezes with a slow thaw between them. A quick thaw with no second freeze under-kills and the lesion returns.
  • Unit slips: latent heat is 334 kJ/kg = 334 000 J/kg, not 334 J/kg, and specific heat is per kg, not per gram. A stray factor of 1000 is the commonest arithmetic error here.
  • Oxygen deficiency has no warning smell. Nitrogen gas is colourless and odourless, so a leaking dewar in a small room causes dizziness and collapse before anyone notices.

5.9 Defibrillator protection circuit and cardiac ablation catheter

A defibrillator pushes up to 5000 V across the chest while the ECG electrodes are still stuck on it, so the ECG amplifier needs a protection network that swallows the shock and then recovers. Separately, some arrhythmias come from one small patch of heart muscle, and a cardiac ablation catheter burns that patch with radio-frequency (RF) current delivered through a 4 mm tip electrode. This chapter shows how the protection circuit works, why it protects the amplifier and not the patient, and how the ablation catheter is mapped, powered and kept at a safe tip temperature.

You need the bio-amplifier and ECG recording, the shock energies from pacemakers and defibrillators, and the current limits from electrical safety.

The problem: a shock arrives at the ECG electrodes

The ECG amplifier reads a signal of about 1 mV at the skin. Its input stage is a low-noise differential amplifier built from devices that tolerate a few volts at most. A defibrillator delivers 200 J to 360 J through paddles on the chest, with a peak voltage of up to about 5000 V. The paddles sit on the same chest as the ECG electrodes, so a large part of that voltage appears across the electrode leads and reaches the amplifier input.

Without protection the amplifier dies in one shock. The input devices break down, a track may fuse, and the monitor is lost for the rest of the arrest — exactly when the team needs to see the rhythm. The protection network sits between the electrodes and the amplifier and does three jobs in order: it limits the current, shunts the excess to a common point, and clamps whatever is left to the supply rails.

The network protects the amplifier, not the patient. The patient is protected by placing the paddles well away from the electrodes, by good electrode contact, and by the current-limiting design of defibrillator-proof leads. Mixing up these two protections is a standard exam error.

How the protection network works

  1. Series resistor at each electrode. Each lead carries a resistor of tens of kilo-ohms. With 5000 V across the pair, this resistor alone limits the lead current to a fraction of an ampere — for example 5000 V ÷ 40 kΩ = 125 mA with 20 kΩ in each of two leads — instead of amperes.
  2. Neon lamp or spark gap across the inputs. At normal ECG voltages the neon lamp is an open circuit. When the voltage across the neon lamp reaches about 60–100 V (a few hundred volts if the shunt is a spark gap), the gas ionises and the lamp fires, becoming a low-resistance shunt that carries the shock current. In this circuit the lamp is a shunt, not a pilot light.
  3. Clamp diodes at the amplifier input. One diode per input goes to the positive supply and one to the negative supply. Any leftover voltage beyond the rails turns a diode on and is diverted to the supply.
  4. Recovery. After the shock the amplifier is saturated and the lamp is still ionised, so the trace sits off-scale. The channel takes a fraction of a second to a few seconds to settle and show a clean ECG again.
  • Defibrillator-proof leads carry the series resistors inside the lead or its connector, so the current is limited before it travels down the lead. The shunt and clamp diodes are in the monitor's input circuit, so the protection only works when such a lead is used with a monitor built for it.
  • Every extra component adds noise and leakage current, so the designer keeps the protection as small as the shock voltage allows.
  • Screened leads reduce the 50 Hz pickup that the series resistors would otherwise make worse.

Cardiac ablation: burning the faulty patch

Some arrhythmias come from one small region of muscle — an extra pathway between atrium and ventricle, a slow pathway near the AV node, or a focus firing inside a pulmonary vein. Drugs may fail or be needed lifelong. Ablation destroys that few-millimetre patch on purpose, so the abnormal circuit cannot conduct.

  1. Mapping. Catheters are passed through a vein to the heart and local electrical signals are recorded to locate the focus. The same catheters pace the heart to test the circuit.
  2. Positioning. A steerable ablation catheter, with a 4 mm tip electrode, is bent by pull wires at the handle until the tip touches the target under X-ray and signal guidance.
  3. Delivery. An RF generator feeds the tip at about 500 kHz, 20 W to 50 W. Current spreads into the tissue, heats it resistively, and the tip is held at 60 °C to 70 °C by a thermocouple that throttles the generator. The circuit is completed by a large dispersive pad on the patient's back or thigh; current flows from the small tip, through the tissue, to this pad, so the heating is concentrated at the tip.
  4. Check. The lesion is a few millimetres deep. The team retests the circuit; if the arrhythmia can still be started, more lesions are placed.

Cryoablation is the alternative: a balloon or tip cooled by expanding gas freezes the same tissue, which is useful near the AV node because cold injury is partly reversible and the effect can be tested before it is permanent. Complications are serious: perforation of the heart wall leading to tamponade, thromboembolism, damage to the AV node needing a pacemaker, and radiation dose to staff and patient from long screening.

Worked example

Question. An RF ablation generator delivers 50 W into heart tissue that looks like 100 Ω at the catheter tip, at a frequency of 500 kHz. Find the current and the voltage at the tip, and check the power.

  1. Data: power P = 50 W, tissue impedance R = 100 Ω, frequency f = 500 kHz (not needed for the calculation).
  2. Power and resistance give voltage: P = V² ÷ R, so V = √(P × R) = √(50 × 100) = √5000 = 70.7 V.
  3. Power and resistance give current: P = I² × R, so I = √(P ÷ R) = √(50 ÷ 100) = √0.5 = 0.707 A.
  4. Check: I × V = 0.707 A × 70.7 V = 50 W, which matches the generator setting.
  5. Meaning: 70.7 V at 500 kHz sits at the tip. Ablation RF also reaches the ECG leads as interference. Only a small part of this RF voltage couples into the ECG leads, far below the neon firing voltage, so the shunt stays off; the series resistors with the input capacitance form a low-pass filter, and the amplifier's filters remove the rest of the 500 kHz pickup.

Answer. The generator drives 0.707 A at 70.7 V into the tissue, which is the set 50 W.

Exam traps

  • Saying the protection circuit protects the patient. It protects the ECG amplifier; the patient is protected by paddle placement and by lead design.
  • Calling the neon lamp an indicator. In this circuit it is the shunt that fires at roughly 60–100 V and carries the shock current.
  • Mixing the two voltage levels: an ablation tip runs at about 70 V, a defibrillator at up to 5000 V. Both can meet the same electrode lead.
  • Writing that ablation kills tissue with a shock. It heats tissue with continuous RF current at about 500 kHz, 20–50 W, holding the tip at 60–70 °C.
  • Forgetting recovery time. A protected amplifier is still saturated for a moment after the shock, so the first part of the trace is unusable.

    Energy5–50 J on the heart; ICD up to about 35–40 J120–200 J biphasic, up to 360 J monophasic
    Powermains unit in theatre; ICD runs on its own lithium cellmains with battery backup, or battery in an AED
  5. AC versus DC operation. The early AC defibrillator applied mains through a step-up transformer: about 250–750 V at 50 Hz for 0.25–1 s. The DC defibrillator stores energy in a capacitor and releases it in one pulse:
    32 µF at 5000 V: E = ½ × 32 × 10⁻⁶ × 5000² = 400 J stored
with 10 Ω inside and 50 Ω chest: 50 / 60 × 400 = 333 J delivered
  6. DC replaced AC because it defibrillates with less muscle damage and fewer arrhythmias, its energy can be set and measured, and it can run from a battery. "AC-powered" today means only that the charger runs from mains.
  7. Pulse energy and shock energy. A defibrillation shock of 200 J is about 200 ÷ 6.25 × 10⁻⁶ = 3.2 × 10⁷ times a pacing pulse: pacing excites a small patch and lets the beat spread, while defibrillation must depolarise most of the ventricle at once.

Analyze Analyze why dry oxygen injures the airway and how humidifier or nebulizer settings correct this. Include hazards of over-humidification and infection. 13 marks

Points to cover. Dry gas damages cilia and mucosa; Water vapour versus droplet size; Humidifier settings and nebulizer output; Condensation, infection and water overload; Effect on ventilator readings.

Model answer.

  1. Why dry gas injures the airway. Normally the nose warms and wets inspired air, so that below the carina it is at 37 °C and saturated (44 mg/L), the isothermic saturation boundary. Dry oxygen, or any gas given through a tube or tracheostomy, pushes that boundary deep into the lungs, and the lower airway gives up its own water and heat.
  2. The chain of damage:
    StageEffect
    Mucus driesbecomes thick and sticky
    Cilia slow and stopsecretions are not cleared
    Mucosa inflamesbleeding, ulcers, airway narrowing
    Secretions plug the airwayscollapse of lung units, infection, blocked tube
  3. How much water is missing:
    dry oxygen, 10 L/min: 44 mg/L × 10 = 440 mg/min = 26.4 g/h
room air 25 °C, 50 % RH: 0.5 × 23 = 11.5 mg/L present
  deficit 44 − 11.5 = 32.5 mg/L → 325 mg/min = 19.5 g/h
  4. Water vapour versus droplets. A humidifier adds vapour, single water molecules that stay in the gas and reach every airway. A nebulizer adds droplets; droplets of 1–5 µm settle in the bronchi and alveoli, larger ones rain out in the tubing and throat, and droplets do not raise the humidity of the gas much. So humidity is corrected with a humidifier, and drugs are given with a nebulizer.
  5. Humidifier settings:
    PatientDeviceTarget
    Nasal cannula up to 4 L/minusually none neededthe nose still works
    High-flow oxygen or maskheated humidifiernear 37 °C at the patient
    Intubated or tracheostomyheated humidifier with heated-wire circuit33–44 mg/L, 34–41 °C at the patient end
    Short transportHMEreturns about 70 % of exhaled moisture
  6. Nebulizer settings. A jet nebulizer is driven by 6–8 L/min, gives MMAD 1–5 µm and leaves about 1 mL unused in the cup. In a ventilator circuit it goes in the inspiratory limb, and an HME must be removed while it runs, because the HME traps the aerosol and its resistance rises.
  7. Hazards of over-humidification and infection:
    HazardCausePrevention
    Rainoutwarm saturated gas cools in the tubingheated-wire circuit, water traps below the patient
    Water overloaddroplets plus vapour in small infantshumidifier set to saturation only
    Scaldchamber above 37–41 °Ctemperature probe at the patient end with an alarm
    Infectionwarm water reservoirsterile water, closed refill, circuit changes
  8. Effect on ventilator readings. A jet nebulizer adds its 6–8 L/min to the breath, so the delivered and exhaled volumes read high and the patient may not trigger; condensation in the expiratory filter raises resistance; water on a flow sensor makes the volume readings wrong; water sloshing in the tubing can trigger extra breaths.
  9. Closing check: 44 × 10 × 60 / 1000 = 26.4 g/h; (44 − 11.5) × 10 × 60 / 1000 = 19.5 g/h.
Evaluate Evaluate the hazards of short-wave diathermy in a patient with a metallic implant or cardiac pacemaker. What precautions would you take? 13 marks

Points to cover. Radio-frequency current heats tissue and metal; Implant heating and pacemaker interference; Electrode placement and tuning; Patient screening and monitoring; When diathermy is contraindicated.

Model answer.

  1. How short-wave diathermy heats. A 27.12 MHz field between capacitor plates, or from an induction coil, drives dielectric and resistive losses in the tissue. The wavelength, 3 × 10⁸ ÷ 27.12 × 10⁶ = 11.06 m, is far longer than the body, so the patient sits in the near field and anything conducting in that field also carries current.
  2. Hazard 1: a metallic implant. Metal conducts far better than tissue, so the field lines crowd onto it and the current density is highest at its ends and edges:
    EffectResult
    Current concentrates at the metal surfacedeep burn next to the implant
    The patient feels warmth only in the skinthe deep burn gives no warning
    Metal jewellery, zips, hearing aids, metal couchlocal burns at the skin
  3. Hazard 2: a cardiac pacemaker:
    EffectResult
    RF picked up by the lead and sensed as beatsoutput inhibited: asystole in a pacemaker-dependent patient
    RF current induced in the leadheating of the tissue at the lead tip, raising the pacing threshold
    Strong field on the generatorreset, reprogramming or permanent damage
  4. How big the dose is:
    short-wave: 200 W for 10 min → E = 200 × 600 = 120 kJ
ultrasound alternative: 1.5 W/cm² × 5 cm² = 7.5 W → 2250 J in 5 min
  5. Electrode placement and tuning. The plates are spaced evenly from the skin with dry towels, so the field is even; the cables are kept apart and away from the patient; the circuit is tuned to resonance with the patient so the power is delivered smoothly, and sweat is wiped away because water concentrates the field.
  6. Precautions:
    StepAction
    Screeningask about pacemakers, ICDs, cochlear implants, nerve stimulators, metal plates, joint replacements, IUDs, pregnancy; read the notes
    Preparationremove jewellery, hearing aids and metal; use a wooden couch and chair
    Sensation testtest hot and cold sensation over the area first
    Monitoringstart low, ask about warmth every few minutes, use a timer, keep the call bell in the hand
    Distancekeep the unit away from other patients' electronic devices; staff stand about 1–2 m away
  7. When diathermy is contraindicated. Any patient with a pacemaker, ICD or other implanted stimulator: do not use short-wave or microwave diathermy anywhere on the body, because the field and the leads extend widely. A metal implant in or near the treated area: do not treat that area with short-wave or microwave. Also pregnancy (abdomen and pelvis), loss of sensation, poor circulation, bleeding, malignancy and active infection in the field.
  8. Evaluation. The precautions reduce but do not remove the risk, because the hazard comes from physics, not from operator error. Where a contraindication exists, choose another treatment: ultrasound away from the device, hot packs, exercise or TENS with the pacemaker team's agreement.
  9. Closing check: 3 × 10⁸ / 27.12 × 10⁶ = 11.06 m; 200 W × 600 s = 120 kJ; 1.5 × 5 = 7.5 W.
Evaluate Explain how an ablation catheter and its radio-frequency generator treat supraventricular tachycardia. Relate its safety principles to defibrillator protection. 15 marks

Points to cover. Arrhythmia focus mapping; Catheter electrode and RF generator; Controlled heating and lesion formation; Impedance, temperature and power limits; Protection of ECG amplifiers from high energy; Patient and operator safety.

Model answer.

  1. Supraventricular tachycardia. Most SVT is a re-entry circuit with a small critical part: a slow pathway near the AV node (AV nodal re-entry), an accessory pathway between atrium and ventricle (as in Wolff–Parkinson–White), or a single atrial focus. Destroying a few millimetres of that tissue cures it.
  2. Focus mapping. Catheters passed from the femoral vein are placed at the high right atrium, the His bundle, the coronary sinus and the right ventricle. Their signals show the order of activation; programmed pacing starts the tachycardia so the earliest signal, the target, can be found. A 3D mapping system shows the activation as a colour map of the chamber.
  3. Catheter and generator:
    PartDesignValue
    Ablation cathetersteerable by pull wires, about 7 F (2.33 mm), with a thermocouple in the tip4 mm tip electrode
    RF generatorcontinuous sine wave, unmodulated500 kHz, 20–50 W
    Dispersive padlarge pad on the back or thighabout 100 cm²
    Controlspower, tip temperature, impedance and time limits60–70 °C, 30–60 s per lesion
  4. Power at the tip (50 W into 100 Ω):
    V = √(P R) = √(50 × 100) = 70.7 V;  I = √(P / R) = √0.5 = 0.707 A
check: 0.707 × 70.7 = 50 W
  5. Why the heating stays at the tip. The same current crosses a small tip and a large pad:
    tip area ≈ π × 2.33 × 4 + π × 2.33² / 4 = 29.3 + 4.3 = 33.6 mm²
tip current density = 707 mA / 33.6 mm² = 21 mA/mm²
pad: 100 cm² = 10 000 mm², about 300 times larger, so about 1/300 of the density
  6. Controlled heating and lesion formation. Resistive heating is greatest in the thin tissue layer touching the tip; heat conducts outward and tissue above about 50 °C dies, making a lesion a few millimetres deep. The generator throttles power to hold the tip at 60–70 °C; above about 100 °C blood boils and forms a coagulum on the tip.
  7. Impedance as a safety signal. A fall of 5–10 Ω during delivery shows the tissue is heating. A sudden rise shows coagulum on the tip, so the generator cuts out. Each burn is applied for 30–60 s and the circuit is retested; if the tachycardia can no longer be started, the ablation is done. Near the AV node, cryoablation may be used because its effect can be tested and reversed before it is permanent.
  8. Relation to defibrillator protection:
    IdeaAblationDefibrillator protection
    Limit the energypower, temperature and time limits in the generatorseries resistors limit current: 5000 V / 40 kΩ = 125 mA
    Divert what is not wanteddispersive pad spreads the return currentneon lamp shunts the shock, diodes clamp the rest
    Protect the recordingfilters remove 500 kHz from the intracardiac and surface ECGthe network stops 5000 V reaching the amplifier
    Accept a recovery timesignals are noisy during deliverytrace saturated for up to a few seconds
  9. The lab must also be ready to defibrillate, because pacing the circuit can start atrial or ventricular fibrillation; the ECG amplifiers in the recording system therefore need the same defibrillation protection.
  10. Patient and operator safety. Patient: full pad contact, since a half-detached pad concentrates the current and burns the skin; watch for perforation and tamponade, AV block and clots; low leakage currents through the catheters, because a direct path to the heart makes microshock possible. Operator: lead aprons, thyroid shields and pulsed low-dose fluoroscopy against the X-ray dose.
  11. Closing check: √(50 × 100) = 70.7 V; 0.707 A × 70.7 V = 50 W; 10 000 / 33.6 = 298, about 300.

Glossary

Cardiac ablation catheter
A catheter that delivers radio-frequency energy to destroy an arrhythmia-causing patch of heart muscle.
Cardiopulmonary bypass
A heart-lung machine circuit that pumps and oxygenates blood while the heart is stopped.
Charge per pulse
Current multiplied by pulse width, Q = I × t, in coulombs.
Cryosurgery
Destruction of tissue by local freezing below about −40 °C.
Defibrillator
A device that delivers a large controlled shock to stop ventricular fibrillation.
Defibrillator protection circuit
A circuit that protects an ECG amplifier from a defibrillator shock.
Diathermy
Deep heating of tissue by radio-frequency current, microwaves or ultrasound.
EMS
Electrical muscle stimulation, a general term for current pulses that cause muscle contraction.
Faradic current
A short interrupted current used to stimulate nerve and muscle.
FES
Functional electrical stimulation, NMES timed to help a useful movement.
Galvanic current
A steady direct current used in some electrotherapy treatments.
Humidifier
A device that adds water vapour to dry medical gas.
Inhaler
A device that delivers a measured drug dose to the airway.
Interferential therapy
Two crossed medium-frequency currents that produce a low difference frequency in tissue.
Lithotripsy
Breaking a kidney stone with focused shock waves.
Microwave diathermy
Diathermy using microwave energy, commonly 2.45 GHz.
Nebulizer
A device that turns liquid into fine droplets for inhalation.
NMES
Neuromuscular electrical stimulation, used to make muscles contract.
Pacemaker
A device that gives a small timed electrical pulse to the heart when natural conduction fails.
Pulse width
The on-time of one electrical pulse.
Russian current
A medium-frequency alternating current used for muscle strengthening.
Short-wave diathermy
Diathermy using radio-frequency current, commonly 27.12 MHz.
TENS
Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, used mainly for pain relief.
Ultrasound diathermy
Deep heating by high-frequency sound waves passed into tissue.
Ventilator
A machine that moves a breath into the lungs and lets it out.
