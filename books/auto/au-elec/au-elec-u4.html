4.1 Emission norms: Euro and Bharat Stage standards

An emission standard is a legal upper limit on what a vehicle may emit per kilometre of a fixed driving cycle: a mass in g/km for carbon monoxide (CO), hydrocarbons (HC), nitrogen oxides (NOx) and particulate matter (PM), and a count per km for particle number (PN). The limits exist because these gases and particles harm health and air. Each new stage lowers the ceilings, and India follows the European (Euro) ladder under the name Bharat Stage (BS). The tighter the ceiling, the more exactly the engine must meter fuel and time the spark, which is why the rest of this unit is about electronic control.

You need: the idea of rich and lean mixtures from engine fuelling and exhaust emissions. The lambda sensor itself is covered under Exhaust gas oxygen (lambda) sensors.

Limits per kilometre, for the whole vehicle

The regulated pollutants are CO, HC, NOx, PM and PN. CO is poisonous. HC and NOx form smog in sunlight. PM and PN are fine particles that reach deep into the lungs. A car limit is written in g/km: grams of that pollutant emitted for each kilometre driven. PN is written as particles per km.

The test runs the complete vehicle on a fixed driving cycle on a chassis dynamometer (rollers under the driven wheels), with the exhaust collected and analysed. So the result includes the engine, the catalyst and the gearing: it is a vehicle limit, not an engine limit. Heavy-duty engines are approved on an engine dynamometer instead, and their limits are in g/kWh of engine work. The two units cannot be compared.

A vehicle passes only if every pollutant is at or below its own ceiling. One failure fails the vehicle, however good the others are. Some diesel limits are written as a combined HC + NOx figure: add the two measured values before you compare.

The Euro ladder and the Bharat Stage steps

Euro 1 to Euro 6 is a ladder: each stage lowers the ceilings. India based Bharat Stage I to BS VI on that ladder, with its own driving cycle and dates. BS IV applied across the country from April 2017. India then skipped BS V and moved straight to BS VI in April 2020. With that change, the sulphur limit in petrol and diesel fell from 50 ppm (parts per million) to 10 ppm. Low sulphur keeps the catalyst active and lets a diesel particulate filter regenerate cleanly. A second BS VI phase from April 2023 added on-road (real driving) emission checks and stricter on-board diagnostics.

A stage is a type-approval limit for a vehicle family, not a quality mark on one engine. Homologation is the official approval test that a new model must pass before it can be sold.

What each step demands from the engine is the link to the rest of this unit. A petrol engine meets the limits with a three-way catalyst, which works only when the electronic control unit (ECU) holds the mixture at λ = 1, the chemically correct air-fuel ratio. That needs closed-loop electronic fuel control and precise spark timing, both run by the ECU (see concept of electronic engine control). A diesel runs lean (λ well above 1), so it cannot use a three-way catalyst; it cuts NOx with exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) and selective catalytic reduction (SCR), and traps soot in a particulate filter. Some syllabi call this topic emission legislation. Only a few universities teach it in depth.

Worked example

Question. A petrol car on the test cycle emits 0.82 g/km CO, 0.07 g/km HC and 0.11 g/km NOx. Use the petrol limits in the table: BS IV CO 1.0, HC 0.10, NOx 0.08 g/km; BS VI CO 1.0, HC 0.10, NOx 0.06 g/km. (a) Express each result as a percentage of its limit. (b) Does the car pass BS IV? BS VI? (c) By how much must NOx fall to pass BS VI?

  1. CO: 0.82 ÷ 1.0 × 100 = 82 % of the limit under both stages. HC: 0.07 ÷ 0.10 × 100 = 70 % under both.
  2. NOx against BS IV: 0.11 ÷ 0.08 × 100 = 137.5 % of the limit, that is 0.03 g/km (37.5 %) over.
  3. NOx against BS VI: 0.11 ÷ 0.06 × 100 = 183.3 % of the limit, that is 0.05 g/km (83.3 %) over.
  4. Pass needs every pollutant at or below 100 %. CO and HC pass, NOx fails, so the car fails both BS IV and BS VI.
  5. To pass BS VI, NOx must fall from 0.11 to 0.06 g/km: a cut of 0.05 g/km, which is 0.05 ÷ 0.11 × 100 = 45.5 % of the present NOx.

Answer. CO is at 82 % and HC at 70 % of their limits, but NOx is at 137.5 % of the BS IV limit and 183.3 % of the BS VI limit, so the car fails both stages. NOx must fall by 0.05 g/km (45.5 %) to pass BS VI.

Exam traps

  • Mixing units: g/km is a vehicle limit from a chassis dynamometer; g/kWh is an engine limit from an engine test.
  • Comparing HC and NOx separately when the stage gives a combined HC + NOx limit: add them first.
  • Writing that BS VI followed BS V. India skipped BS V and went from BS IV (2017) to BS VI (April 2020).
  • Treating PM and PN as the same. PM is a mass in g/km; PN is a count of particles per km.
  • Calling a vehicle a pass because most pollutants pass. One pollutant over its ceiling fails the vehicle.

4.2 Engine fuelling, exhaust emissions and fuel economy

An engine burns a mixture of petrol and air, and the air-fuel ratio decides how completely the fuel burns and what leaves the tailpipe: carbon monoxide (CO), unburnt hydrocarbons (HC) and nitrogen oxides (NOx). No single ratio is best everywhere, so the controller moves between rich, stoichiometric and lean as the engine starts, warms, cruises and accelerates. This chapter links the mixture to the pollutants, the oxidising and three-way catalysts, exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) and fuel economy in km/L, L/100 km and grams of CO₂ per km.

You need: the legal ceilings from emission norms, and first-year chemistry of complete and incomplete combustion.

Air-fuel ratio, lambda and the pollutants

Petrol needs about 14.7 kg of air for every 1 kg of fuel to burn completely. This is the stoichiometric air-fuel ratio, AFRst. The actual air-fuel ratio AFR is air mass divided by fuel mass. Lambda (λ), the excess-air ratio, compares the two.

λ = AFR ÷ AFRst = AFR ÷ 14.7

AFR is the actual ratio in kg of air per kg of petrol; AFRst = 14.7 for petrol; λ has no unit. λ = 1 is stoichiometric, λ < 1 is rich (too little air for the fuel) and λ > 1 is lean (excess air).

Each pollutant follows λ in its own way.

  • CO forms when carbon burns with too little oxygen, so it rises steeply on the rich side and is very low lean.
  • HC is fuel that escaped the flame: quenched at the cold cylinder wall, hidden in crevices round the piston rings, or never burnt. It is high when rich, lowest slightly lean, and rises again very lean, when the flame starts to misfire.
  • NOx forms when nitrogen and oxygen combine at high flame temperature. It needs both heat and spare oxygen, so it is low rich, peaks slightly lean (about λ = 1.05 to 1.1) and falls again very lean because the flame is cooler.
  • In a diesel, PM (soot) comes from fuel-rich pockets inside the spray, even though the average diesel mixture is lean.

Catalytic converters and exhaust gas recirculation

A catalytic converter is a ceramic or metal honeycomb in the exhaust, coated with a porous washcoat that carries precious metals (platinum and palladium for oxidation, rhodium for NOx reduction). An oxidising (two-way) catalyst uses spare oxygen in the exhaust to burn CO and HC. It lowers CO and HC but cannot remove NOx.

  • Oxidation: 2CO + O2 → 2CO2; for a hydrocarbon CnHm: CnHm + (n + m/4) O2 → n CO2 + (m/2) H2O.
  • Reduction (three-way only): 2NO + 2CO → N2 + 2CO2.

A three-way catalyst does both jobs at once, but only in a narrow window around λ = 1, about 1 percent wide. Lean of the window the spare oxygen leaves NOx unreduced; rich of it there is too little oxygen to burn all the CO and HC. Conversion efficiency is (pollutant in − pollutant out) ÷ pollutant in.

The catalyst must also be hot. Its light-off temperature, where conversion reaches about half, is about 250 to 350 °C. In the first minute after a cold start the catalyst is below light-off, so that minute can carry much of a short trip's emissions. The ECU therefore retards the spark to send hotter exhaust to the catalyst (see spark timing control), and some engines pump secondary air into the exhaust.

EGR, exhaust gas recirculation, sends a metered part of the exhaust back to the intake. The inert gas dilutes the charge and lowers peak flame temperature, so NOx falls. At part load, moderate EGR has little effect on fuel economy and can improve it slightly, because the engine needs less throttling (lower pumping loss). Too much EGR slows the burn, raises HC and causes misfire. The ECU schedules EGR by speed and load and shuts it at idle and full load.

Spark timing, fuel economy and CO₂

Spark timing also moves the emissions. Retarding the spark from the best-torque point lowers peak temperature, so NOx falls, and leaves the exhaust hotter, which burns more HC in the exhaust. The cost is torque, so fuel use per km rises.

Fuel economy has two common forms. km per litre is higher-is-better; litres per 100 km is lower-is-better. They are reciprocal: L/100 km = 100 ÷ (km/L). A car giving 14.7 km/L uses 100 ÷ 14.7 = 6.8 L/100 km.

CO₂ is not a product of bad combustion: almost all the carbon in the fuel leaves as CO₂ when the burn is good. So CO₂ per km is fixed by fuel used per km. Burning 1 kg of petrol makes about 3.16 kg of CO₂; with a density of 0.74 kg/L that is about 2.34 kg of CO₂ per litre. Cutting CO₂ means cutting fuel.

The controller chooses a compromise at every operating point. Steady cruise runs at λ = 1 in closed loop, so the three-way catalyst can clean all three pollutants (see electronic fuel control). Full load runs slightly rich (λ about 0.85 to 0.9) for power and to cool the exhaust valves and catalyst. Cold start runs rich until the fuel evaporates well. Deceleration cuts fuel completely. The effect of each choice on torque and fuel use is measured as in torque, power and BSFC.

Worked example

Question. A car uses 6.8 L of petrol to cover 100 km. Petrol density is 0.74 kg/L and burning 1 kg of petrol makes 3.16 kg of CO₂. Find (a) the economy in km/L, (b) the fuel mass per km, (c) the CO₂ in g/km, and (d) λ when the air-fuel ratio drops to 13.2:1 during hard acceleration.

  1. (a) km/L = 100 ÷ 6.8 = 14.71 km/L.
  2. (b) Fuel mass per 100 km = 6.8 L × 0.74 kg/L = 5.032 kg, so fuel mass per km = 5.032 ÷ 100 = 0.05032 kg/km.
  3. (c) CO₂ per km = 0.05032 kg/km × 3.16 = 0.1590 kg/km = 159.0 g/km.
  4. (d) λ = 13.2 ÷ 14.7 = 0.898, about 0.90. Since λ < 1 the mixture is rich: CO and HC rise and NOx falls while it lasts.

Answer. 14.71 km/L; 0.0503 kg of petrol per km; 159 g CO₂/km; at 13.2:1, λ = 0.90 (rich).

Question. On the test cycle a petrol car makes, before the catalyst, CO 8.0 g/km, HC 1.2 g/km and NOx 1.0 g/km. With the catalyst hot and λ held at 1, conversion is CO 90 %, HC 92 %, NOx 95 %. Exam traps

  • Treating λ and AFR as the same number. λ has no unit; AFR is kg of air per kg of fuel. λ = 1 means AFR = 14.7 for petrol, not 1:1.
  • Believing a three-way catalyst cleans any mixture. Outside its window near λ = 1, CO and HC (rich) or NOx (lean) pass through.
  • Forgetting light-off. A cold catalyst does almost nothing even at λ = 1; about 250 to 350 °C is needed.
  • Saying an oxidising catalyst removes NOx. It only burns CO and HC; NOx needs the reducing step of a three-way catalyst.
  • Listing CO₂ with the products of poor combustion. CO₂ comes from good combustion and is fixed by the fuel burnt; CO and HC come from poor combustion.

4.3 Engine performance variables: torque, power and BSFC

Torque, power and brake specific fuel consumption (BSFC) are the numbers a test cell measures to judge every fuel and spark choice the ECU makes. Torque is the twisting effort at the crankshaft. Power is torque multiplied by angular speed. BSFC is the mass of fuel spent for each kilowatt-hour of work delivered at the crankshaft, in g/kWh. Together they show where an engine is strong and where it is economical, and they become the targets stored in the ECU maps.

You need: the effect of a rich mixture on torque and fuel from engine fuelling and exhaust emissions, and work, power and energy from first-year physics.

Torque, speed and brake power

Torque T is the twisting effort the crankshaft delivers, in N·m. A dynamometer loads the engine, holds its speed steady and measures T. Turning the shaft through one radian at torque T does T joules of work, so shaft power is T × ω, where ω is the angular speed in rad/s. With speed N in rev/min, ω = 2πN ÷ 60.

P = 2πNT ÷ 60

P is brake power in W; T is brake torque in N·m; N is speed in rev/min; 2π ÷ 60 converts rev/min to rad/s.

At 3000 rpm and 150 N·m, P = 2π × 3000 × 150 ÷ 60 = 47.1 kW. At 4000 rpm the torque has fallen to 145 N·m, yet P = 60.7 kW. Past the torque peak, speed rises faster than torque falls, so power keeps climbing until torque falls steeply near the top of the speed range.

Brake mean effective pressure (BMEP) lets engines of different sizes be compared. It is the brake work per cycle divided by the total swept volume Vd. A four-stroke makes one cycle every two revolutions, so the work per cycle is 4πT and BMEP = 4πT ÷ Vd (for a two-stroke, 2πT ÷ Vd). With T in N·m and Vd in m³, BMEP is in Pa; 1 bar = 10⁵ Pa.

BSFC and brake thermal efficiency

A fuel meter gives the fuel mass flow ṁf, usually in kg/h. Dividing it by brake power gives BSFC.

BSFC = ṁf × 1000 ÷ P

BSFC is in g/kWh; ṁf is in kg/h; P is in kW; 1000 converts kg to g.

A small BSFC is good. A modern petrol engine reaches about 230 to 260 g/kWh at its best point; a diesel reaches about 200 g/kWh or less, because its higher compression ratio and lean running extract more work from each gram. Fuel consumption in kg/h (or L/h) alone cannot compare engines, because it does not say how much work the fuel bought.

One kWh is 3600 kJ. The fuel burnt to make it holds BSFC × CV kJ, where CV is the calorific value in MJ/kg, which is the same number as kJ/g. Their ratio is the brake thermal efficiency ηbth.

ηbth = 3600 ÷ (BSFC × CV)

ηbth is a fraction; BSFC in g/kWh; CV in MJ/kg; 3600 is kJ per kWh.

Mechanical efficiency and the BSFC map

Indicated power is the power the gas delivers to the pistons. Brake power is what is left at the crankshaft after friction and pumping. Friction power = indicated power − brake power, and mechanical efficiency ηm = brake power ÷ indicated power, about 0.8 to 0.9 for a petrol engine at full load, falling at light load and at high speed.

A BSFC map draws lines of equal BSFC on a chart of speed (x) against torque or BMEP (y). Its lowest-BSFC island sits at moderate speed, about 2000 to 3000 rpm, and fairly high load.

  • At high speed, friction and pumping grow, so more fuel only keeps the engine turning.
  • At light load, the fixed losses are shared over little brake power, and throttling adds pumping loss, so BSFC is poor.
  • At full load, the ECU enriches the mixture to protect components, and the extra fuel raises BSFC again.

Measuring torque, power, BSFC and emissions at every speed and load on a test bed is called engine mapping. The ECU fuel and spark tables are filled from these maps (see concept of electronic engine control).

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder 1.6 L petrol engine runs at 3000 rev/min and 150 N·m. Fuel flow is 11.5 kg/h; petrol CV is 43 MJ/kg. Mechanical efficiency is 0.85. Find the brake power, BMEP, BSFC, brake thermal efficiency, indicated power and friction power.

  1. ω = 2π × 3000 ÷ 60 = 314.16 rad/s, so P = T × ω = 150 × 314.16 = 47 124 W = 47.12 kW.
  2. BMEP = 4πT ÷ Vd = 4π × 150 ÷ 0.0016 m³ = 1 178 097 Pa = 11.78 bar.
  3. BSFC = 11.5 × 1000 ÷ 47.12 = 244.04 g/kWh.
  4. ηbth = 3600 ÷ (244.04 × 43) = 3600 ÷ 10 494 = 0.3431, that is 34.31 %.
  5. Indicated power = 47.12 ÷ 0.85 = 55.44 kW; friction power = 55.44 − 47.12 = 8.32 kW.

Answer. Brake power 47.12 kW, BMEP 11.78 bar, BSFC 244.04 g/kWh, brake thermal efficiency 34.31 %, indicated power 55.44 kW and friction power 8.32 kW.

Exam traps

  • Putting rev/min straight into P = Tω; convert first with ω = 2πN ÷ 60.
  • Forgetting the 1000 in BSFC: kg/h ÷ kW gives kg/kWh, a number 1000 times smaller than g/kWh.
  • Taking BMEP = T ÷ Vd. For a four-stroke it is 4πT ÷ Vd; leaving out 4π gives a value about 12.6 times too small.
  • Confusing the torque peak with the power peak; power peaks at a higher speed.
  • Using CV in kJ/kg in η = 3600 ÷ (BSFC × CV); it must be in MJ/kg (= kJ/g).

4.4 Concept of electronic engine control

An electronic control unit (ECU) is a small computer that runs the engine. Many times a second it reads its sensors, looks up calibrated tables called maps, and drives the injectors, the ignition coils and the valves. It exists because a three-way catalyst needs the air-fuel ratio held within about one percent of λ = 1 for the whole life of the car, through wear, heat and altitude, which no carburettor or mechanical distributor can do. Diesel engines also have an ECU, but they run lean (λ well above 1), meter the injected fuel directly and clean the exhaust without a three-way catalyst.

You need: the legal limits from emission norms, the air-fuel compromise from engine fuelling and exhaust emissions, and the targets in torque, power and BSFC.

Why a computer, and what a loop means

Here λ (the excess-air ratio) = actual air-fuel ratio ÷ stoichiometric air-fuel ratio; λ = 1.05 means 5 percent more air than the fuel needs. The three-way catalyst converts CO, HC and NOx at once only in a band about one percent wide around λ = 1. A carburettor meters fuel through fixed jets and a venturi; its mixture drifts with jet wear, fuel temperature, air density and altitude, and it cannot hold one percent over ten years and 150 000 km. The ECU can, because it measures and corrects.

Open loop means the ECU computes an output from its stored calibration alone. It looks up a number and fires the injector for that long, with no sensor confirming the result; it trusts that fuel pressure, airflow and injector flow are as calibrated.

Closed loop means a sensor measures the result and the ECU corrects the output. The exhaust gas oxygen (EGO, or lambda) sensor is the measuring element. A zirconia switching sensor gives about 0.1 V when the exhaust is lean and about 0.8 to 0.9 V when rich, and crosses over within a very small change of λ near 1; the ECU treats about 0.45 V as the switch point. It compares the reading with its target and adds a correction called a trim. An electronic table lookup alone is still open loop; only the measurement closes the loop.

The figure is the block diagram of an engine management system. Sensors on the left report speed, load, temperatures and exhaust oxygen. The ECU conditions and digitises them, runs the maps, and drives the actuators on the right. The exhaust gas reaching the oxygen sensor closes the fuel loop. Over the CAN bus the engine ECU shares data with the other ECUs of the car, such as the transmission, ABS and body controllers (see In-vehicle networks and CAN).

Inside the ECU: signals, maps and interpolation

Signal path. A sensor output is rarely ready for a processor. Signal conditioning scales it into the input range of the analogue-to-digital converter (ADC), filters noise and clamps voltage spikes. The ADC turns the voltage into a count: a 10-bit ADC with a 5 V reference gives 2¹⁰ = 1024 steps, so one count is 5 ÷ 1024 ≈ 4.9 mV. Pulse signals such as crank and camshaft position go to timer inputs that measure the time between teeth.

The processor runs its program from flash memory and keeps working values in RAM. Learned trims are kept in non-volatile memory (EEPROM or flash) in most modern ECUs; older ones kept them in keep-alive RAM, lost when the battery is disconnected. Driver stages are power switches, usually low-side MOSFETs that ground one end of an injector or coil, with a clamp that absorbs the inductive kick when the switch opens.

Calibration grid. The central object is a two-dimensional table, the map. One axis is engine speed in rpm; the other is load, either manifold absolute pressure (MAP) in kPa or air mass per stroke in mg. Each cell stores one calibrated value: injector open time, spark advance, target λ, EGR valve position or idle air position. The values come from engine mapping on a dynamometer and from road tests; filling them is called calibration.

y = y₁ + (y₂ − y₁) × (x − x₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁)

y is the value wanted (for example injector open time in ms); x is the measured speed or load; (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) are the two neighbouring calibrated cells.

Between cells, the ECU interpolates linearly along each axis. When both speed and load fall between cells it uses the four surrounding cells: first along one axis, then along the other.

Worked example

Question. In the injection-time map of a warm engine, the column for 2000 rpm holds 4.0 ms at 40 kPa manifold pressure and 6.0 ms at 50 kPa. The ECU reads 46 kPa. Find the injector pulse width, and the fraction of one four-stroke cycle it takes.

  1. 46 kPa lies between the calibrated cells at 40 kPa and 50 kPa in the 2000 rpm column, so interpolate along the load axis.
  2. Pulse width = 4.0 + (6.0 − 4.0) × (46 − 40) ÷ (50 − 40) = 4.0 + 2.0 × 0.6 = 5.2 ms.
  3. One revolution at 2000 rpm takes 60 ÷ 2000 = 0.030 s. A four-stroke cycle is two revolutions: 0.060 s = 60 ms.
  4. Fraction of the cycle = 5.2 ÷ 60 = 0.0867, that is 8.7 %.

Answer. The injector is open for 5.2 ms, which is 8.7 % of the 60 ms four-stroke cycle.

Exam traps

  • Calling a map-based output closed loop. A table lookup is open loop even though it is electronic; the loop closes only when a sensor measures the result.
  • Interpolating along the wrong axis. Use the two cells that bracket the measured load in the same speed column.
  • Mixing milliseconds and percent. Pulse width is a time in ms; duty is that time divided by the cycle time, and a four-stroke cycle is two revolutions.
  • Treating λ = 1 as the target everywhere. Full load runs rich in open loop, and cranking and warm-up ignore the cold λ sensor.
  • Thinking limp-home is normal running. It replaces a failed sensor with a substitute value and limits the engine; it keeps the car moving, not clean.

4.5 Electronic fuel control and petrol fuel injection

Electronic fuel control works out the air entering the engine, computes the fuel mass that air needs, and holds each injector open for the time that delivers it. An exhaust oxygen sensor reports what actually burned, and the ECU trims the pulse to keep λ = 1. Every petrol car sold under today's emission norms runs this loop.

You need: open and closed loop from concept of electronic engine control. The sensors (manifold absolute pressure, air flow, throttle, temperature and oxygen) and the stepper and solenoid actuators are covered under Automotive sensors and actuators.

From the carburettor to petrol injection

A carburettor speeds the incoming air through a narrow throat, the venturi; the pressure drop there draws fuel through a fixed jet. Nothing measures the result, so on a hot afternoon, a cold start or a sudden throttle opening the mixture drifts away from the ratio the catalyst needs. The emission norms tighten that ratio at every stage.

Electronic control of carburetion was the stop-gap: a solenoid valve bled air into the carburettor fuel circuits, moved by the ECU from the oxygen-sensor signal. It held λ near 1 at steady cruise, but cold starts, transients and the uneven split of mixture between cylinders stayed poor.

Petrol injection replaced the jet with a solenoid valve opened for a computed time. An electric pump in the tank sends fuel through a filter to a fuel rail; a pressure regulator holds the rail about 3 to 4 bar above the manifold pressure for port injection, so the flow depends only on open time.

  • Throttle-body (single-point) injection: one or two injectors above the throttle. Cheap, but slow on transients and uneven between cylinders.
  • Port (multi-point) fuel injection: one injector per cylinder in the intake port, sprayed at the back of the intake valve. The common layout.
  • Gasoline direct injection: the injector sprays into the cylinder at about 50 to 200 bar. It cools the charge and allows stratified lean running, but makes more fine particles.

Port injectors can fire all at once once per revolution (simultaneous), in two groups (group), or each just before its own intake stroke (sequential, which needs a camshaft sensor to know the cylinder). Sequential gives the best emissions.

The injector and the fuel calculation

Inside the injector a coil pulls an armature and needle off its seat against a spring; fuel at rail pressure sprays through the nozzle. When the current stops the spring closes the needle. The static flow is the fuel the injector passes when held fully open, quoted at a stated pressure. Because the needle takes a fraction of a millisecond to open, the ECU adds a dead time (about 0.5 to 1 ms, longer at low battery voltage) to every pulse.

  • Saturated drive: battery voltage across a high-resistance coil (about 12 to 16 Ω) for the whole pulse. Simple and cheap.
  • Peak-and-hold drive: for a low-resistance coil, a high current (about 4 A) opens the needle fast, then the driver drops to about 1 A to hold it. Faster opening, cooler coil.

The speed-density calculation turns manifold pressure into an open time in four steps.

  1. Air density from manifold absolute pressure p and intake temperature T: ρ = p ÷ (R T), with R = 287 J/(kg·K).
  2. Air mass flow: ṁair = ηv × ρ × Vd × N ÷ 2.
  3. Fuel per injection: (ṁair ÷ AFR) ÷ (injections per second).
  4. Open time = fuel per injection ÷ injector flow, plus the dead time.

Here ηv is volumetric efficiency, the air actually trapped divided by the swept volume's worth at manifold density, read from a map (typically 0.8 to 0.9 at full load; tuned intakes can push it slightly above 1). Vd is the total swept volume in m³, N is speed in rev/s, and ÷ 2 is because a four-stroke fills each cylinder once every two revolutions. AFR is 14.7 at λ = 1. Engines with a hot-film mass airflow (MAF) sensor skip the first two steps: they read ṁair directly.

The base pulse is then corrected: extra fuel when the coolant is cold, a short extra pulse when the throttle opens suddenly (fuel wets the port walls), and a longer dead time at low battery voltage.

Closed loop, fuel trim and the actuators

The switching oxygen sensor says only rich (high voltage) or lean (low voltage), not by how much. So the ECU ramps the pulse leaner until the sensor flips lean, then ramps richer until it flips rich, and so on: the mixture hunts around λ = 1 about once or twice a second. That hunt suits the catalyst, which stores a little oxygen on lean swings and releases it on rich swings.

Short-term fuel trim (STFT) is that live correction, a percentage added to or taken from the base pulse. When STFT stays biased one way, the ECU learns the offset into a long-term fuel trim (LTFT) table against speed and load, so the next start begins from the corrected value. LTFT hides slow drift, such as a dirty air-flow sensor or a weak pump, until it reaches its limit and sets a fault code.

  • EGR valve: a stepper motor, or a vacuum diaphragm switched by a solenoid, opens a passage from exhaust to intake to cut NOx.
  • Idle air stepper motor: a pintle moves to bypass air around the closed throttle, setting idle speed when cold or loaded by the air-conditioner or alternator.
  • EVAP purge solenoid: opens to draw petrol vapour from the charcoal canister into the intake. That vapour is extra fuel, so the sensor reads rich and the ECU shortens the injector pulse to keep λ = 1.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder 1.6 L petrol engine with sequential port injection runs at 3000 rpm at full throttle. The manifold absolute pressure is 100 kPa, intake air is at 27 °C and volumetric efficiency is 0.85. The air-fuel ratio is 14.7:1. Each injector flows 3.0 g/s when fully open, and the dead time is 0.9 ms. Find the air mass flow, the fuel per injection, the commanded pulse width and the injector duty.

  1. Air density ρ = p ÷ (RT) = 100 000 ÷ (287 × 300) = 1.161 kg/m³; take 1.16 kg/m³.
  2. N = 3000 ÷ 60 = 50 rev/s. Swept volume filled per second = Vd × N ÷ 2 = 0.0016 × 25 = 0.040 m³/s.
  3. ṁair = 0.85 × 1.16 × 0.040 = 0.03944 kg/s = 39.44 g/s.
  4. Fuel flow = 39.44 ÷ 14.7 = 2.683 g/s.
  5. Each injector fires once per cycle: 25 times a second, so 4 × 25 = 100 injections a second. Fuel per injection = 2.683 ÷ 100 = 0.02683 g = 26.83 mg.
  6. Open time for that fuel = 0.02683 ÷ 3.0 = 0.00894 s = 8.94 ms. Commanded pulse = 8.94 + 0.9 = 9.84 ms.
  7. One cycle at 3000 rpm = 2 × 60 ÷ 3000 = 40 ms, so duty = 9.84 ÷ 40 = 24.6 %.

Answer. Air flow 39.44 g/s, fuel 26.83 mg per injection, commanded pulse 9.84 ms (8.94 ms of flow plus 0.9 ms dead time), duty 24.6 %.

Exam traps

  • Using the full swept volume every revolution: a four-stroke fills each cylinder once every two revolutions, so use Vd × N ÷ 2.
  • Using an ambient density at part throttle: speed density needs the manifold density from MAP and intake temperature.
  • Confusing milliseconds with milligrams: pulse width is a time, fuel per injection is a mass; the injector flow converts one into the other.
  • Forgetting the dead time: the commanded pulse is the flow time plus the opening delay.
  • Thinking the oxygen sensor sets the mixture: it only trims a base pulse from the map, and it is cold and ignored at start-up.

4.6 Ignition and spark timing control

The spark must arrive at a crank angle, so the ECU works in crank degrees. It switches the coil on for a dwell time that stores enough energy, switches it off to fire the plug a set number of degrees before top dead centre (TDC), and retards that angle when the knock sensor hears knock. Spark advance decides torque, fuel economy, emissions and piston survival.

You need: maps and closed loop from concept of electronic engine control, the load signal from electronic fuel control, and the coil, plug and ignition layouts from Electronic and programmed ignition systems and Distributorless and direct ignition systems.

Dwell time and dwell angle

The coil stores energy only while its primary circuit is switched on. The current rises towards a limit set by the winding resistance and by a current limit in the driver, typically a few amperes. The stored energy is ½LI², where L is the primary inductance in henry and I the current in ampere. Switching off collapses the field, the secondary winding produces the high voltage and the plug arcs.

Dwell time td is how long, in milliseconds, the ECU keeps the primary on before the break. For a given coil it depends mainly on battery voltage: a low battery makes the current rise more slowly, so the ECU lengthens the dwell from a voltage table. This is constant-energy ignition: the same current at every speed.

Dwell angle θd is the same interval in crank degrees. The crank turns N × 360 ÷ 60 000 degrees per millisecond.

θd = 0.006 × N × td

θd is the dwell angle in crank degrees; N is engine speed in rpm; td is the dwell time in ms; 0.006 = 360 ÷ 60 000.

At 3000 rpm one millisecond is 18 degrees, so 3.0 ms is 54 degrees. At 6000 rpm the same 3.0 ms is 108 degrees. The dwell time stays fixed while the dwell angle grows with speed, so the ECU starts charging earlier as the engine speeds up. The limit is the gap between sparks of one coil: when a single coil serves four cylinders, sparks are 180 degrees apart, and the dwell plus the spark itself must fit inside that gap. Coil-on-plug systems fire each coil once every 720 degrees and have room to spare.

Spark advance, MBT and the knock limit

The mixture takes a few milliseconds to burn. For best torque the pressure peak should come about 10 to 15 degrees after TDC, so the plug must fire before TDC. Spark advance θadv is the crank angle before TDC (BTDC) at which the plug fires.

More advance starts the burn earlier and moves the pressure peak towards TDC, so torque rises, until a point past which it falls again because the piston works against rising pressure. MBT (minimum advance for best torque) is the smallest advance that gives the maximum torque. More advance only adds NOx and knock.

Knock is the unburnt end gas ahead of the flame igniting by itself, giving a sharp pressure spike that rings the block and, if it lasts, cracks pistons. The knock limit is the most advance the engine tolerates at that speed and load. It falls with low-octane petrol, hot intake air, hot coolant and carbon deposits.

The ECU fires at θspark = min(θMBT, θknock − Δ), all in degrees BTDC, where Δ is a 1 to 3 degree safety margin. At low speed and high load the knock limit usually lies below MBT, so the engine runs retarded and gives up some torque.

The spark map and its corrections

Base advance comes from a two-dimensional map with engine speed on one axis and load (air per cycle, from the air-flow or manifold-pressure sensor) on the other: the same kind of map and interpolation as the fuel table.

  • Light load, 1500 to 2500 rpm: about 30 to 45 degrees BTDC. The charge is thin and burns slowly; knock is unlikely.
  • Wide-open throttle: about 10 to 20 degrees BTDC. The dense charge burns fast and knocks easily.
  • Idle: about 5 to 15 degrees BTDC, nudged to hold idle speed.

The map holds steady warm values only. Typical corrections are added to the advance angle:

Closed-loop knock control and misfire detection

The knock sensor is a piezoelectric accelerometer bolted to the block. Knock rings the block at a frequency set by the bore, typically 5 to 15 kHz. The ECU band-pass filters the signal, looks only in a window of crank angle after each spark, and compares the signal with a speed-dependent threshold.

  1. Knock seen: retard 2 to 4 degrees at once, for that cylinder only where the ECU knows which cylinder fired.
  2. No knock: creep the advance back slowly, about a degree after a set number of clean firings.
  3. Knock returns: retard again. The timing settles just under the knock limit and hunts there.
  4. Knock persists at the retard limit: enrich the mixture, cut turbo boost, or both.

Fast retard and slow advance keep the loop stable: one heavy knock can damage the piston, while a slow return costs only a little torque for a few seconds.

Misfire shows up as a crank that does not speed up. The crank sensor gives a pulse every few degrees, and the ECU times each segment to find the angular acceleration after each firing. A firing cylinder speeds the crank; a dead one does not. Comparing each cylinder with the average gives a misfire count, which sets a diagnostic code (see On-board diagnostics: OBD-II) and can cut that cylinder's injector to keep raw fuel out of the catalyst.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder engine with one coil for all four cylinders needs a 3.0 ms dwell, and the ECU wants the spark at 25 degrees BTDC. (a) At 3000 rpm, find the dwell angle and the crank angle at which charging must start. (b) Repeat at 6000 rpm with the same dwell time. (c) Sparks are 180 degrees apart. Find the time left between the end of one spark event and the next dwell at 6000 rpm if the spark lasts 1.5 ms, and say what happens at 8000 rpm.

  1. (a) 3000 rpm: 0.006 × 3000 = 18 degrees per ms. Dwell angle = 3.0 × 18 = 54 degrees. Charging starts 54 degrees before the spark: 25 + 54 = 79 degrees BTDC.
  2. (b) 6000 rpm: 0.006 × 6000 = 36 degrees per ms. Dwell angle = 3.0 × 36 = 108 degrees. Charging starts at 25 + 108 = 133 degrees BTDC.
  3. (c) The 180-degree gap at 6000 rpm lasts 180 ÷ 36 = 5.0 ms. Dwell 3.0 ms + spark 1.5 ms = 4.5 ms, leaving 0.5 ms spare. It fits.
  4. At 8000 rpm: 0.006 × 8000 = 48 degrees per ms, so the gap is 180 ÷ 48 = 3.75 ms, less than the 4.5 ms needed. The ECU must shorten the dwell (to about 2.25 ms), giving a weaker spark. Wasted-spark and coil-on-plug layouts avoid this by giving each coil a longer gap.

Answer. At 3000 rpm the dwell is 54 degrees and charging starts at 79 degrees BTDC; at 6000 rpm it is 108 degrees, starting at 133 degrees BTDC, and dwell plus spark (4.5 ms) fits the 5.0 ms gap. At 8000 rpm the gap is only 3.75 ms, so one coil can no longer give the full dwell.

Exam traps

  • Adding milliseconds to degrees. Convert first: degrees = 0.006 × N × ms.
  • Treating dwell angle as constant. In constant-energy ignition the dwell time is held; the angle grows with speed.
  • Calling advance a time. 25 degrees BTDC is about 1.4 ms at 3000 rpm and 0.7 ms at 6000 rpm.
  • Saying the ECU always runs at MBT. It runs at min(MBT, knock limit − margin).
  • Mixing the two loops: knock control changes the spark; misfire detection counts and reports (and may cut fuel), it does not change the advance.

    fuel per injection = 26.83 mg

Understand Explain the working of a three-way catalytic converter and why the engine must be held near λ = 1 for it to work. 12 marks

Points to cover. Construction: honeycomb, washcoat, platinum, palladium, rhodium; Oxidation of CO and HC and reduction of NOx happen together; Narrow window around λ = 1; rich leaves CO and HC, lean leaves NOx; Closed-loop control with the oxygen sensor hunts across λ = 1; oxygen storage; Light-off temperature and the cold-start problem.

Model answer.

  1. Construction. A ceramic (or metal) honeycomb of many thin channels sits in a steel shell in the exhaust. The channel walls carry a porous washcoat (mainly alumina, with ceria for oxygen storage) that spreads the precious metals: platinum and palladium speed oxidation, rhodium speeds the reduction of NOx. The honeycomb gives a large surface with little back pressure.
  2. Reactions. Three conversions happen on the same surface:
    CO oxidation:  2CO + O2 → 2CO2
HC oxidation:  CnHm + (n + m/4) O2 → n CO2 + (m/2) H2O
NOx reduction: 2NO + 2CO → N2 + 2CO2
  3. Why λ = 1. The oxidation needs oxygen; the reduction needs a gas with no spare oxygen (CO, HC and hydrogen take the oxygen away from NO). Only near the stoichiometric mixture, λ = 1, does the exhaust carry both in balance. Lean of the window (λ > 1) the spare oxygen leaves NOx unreduced; rich of it (λ < 1) there is too little oxygen to burn all the CO and HC. The window is only about 1 % wide in λ.
  4. How the ECU holds it. The switching oxygen sensor upstream of the catalyst reads about 0.8 to 0.9 V rich and 0.1 V lean. The ECU ramps the injector pulse leaner until the sensor flips lean, then richer until it flips rich, so the mixture hunts around λ = 1 about once or twice a second. Ceria in the washcoat stores oxygen on the lean swings and gives it back on the rich swings, which smooths the hunt. A second sensor after the catalyst checks that this storage still works.
  5. Numbers (engine fuelling example). Engine-out CO 8.0, HC 1.2, NOx 1.0 g/km, catalyst hot:
    PollutantAt λ = 1 (conversion)Tailpipe (g/km)BS VI limit (g/km)
    CO90 %8.0 × 0.10 = 0.801.0
    HC92 %1.2 × 0.08 = 0.0960.10
    NOx95 %1.0 × 0.05 = 0.050.06
    NOx at λ = 1.0260 %1.0 × 0.40 = 0.400.06
  6. A 2 % lean drift lifts NOx from 0.05 to 0.40 g/km, 6.7 times the limit. That is the whole case for closed-loop electronic fuel control.
  7. Light-off. The catalyst converts little until it reaches its light-off temperature, about 250 to 350 °C. After a cold start the first minute can carry much of a short trip's emissions, so the ECU retards the spark to send hot exhaust to the catalyst, some engines add secondary air, and catalysts are mounted close to the engine.
  8. Limits of the device. A three-way catalyst cannot work on a diesel or a lean-burn engine (always λ > 1), lead and high sulphur poison it, and overheating from misfire (raw fuel burning in the catalyst) can melt the honeycomb.

Model answer.

Nothing measures what burned, so any error in air measurement, fuel pressure or injector flow passes straight to the exhaust.

Model answer.

Analyze Compare carburetion with electronic petrol fuel injection on mixture accuracy, cylinder-to-cylinder distribution, emissions, cold starting and transient response. 13 marks

Points to cover. Carburettor meters by venturi depression; drifts with temperature, wear and altitude; Injection meters by open time per cylinder from measured air; Port injection gives equal distribution; transients need wall-wetting compensation; Cold start and altitude from maps and sensors; Why BS VI can not be met with a carburettor.

Model answer.

  1. How each meters fuel. A carburettor accelerates air through a venturi; the pressure drop draws fuel through a fixed jet, so fuel follows air only as well as the venturi curve allows. Electronic injection measures or computes the air (speed density or mass airflow), computes the fuel and opens an injector for a calculated time at a regulated pressure (about 3 to 4 bar above manifold pressure).
  2. Comparison:

    Altituderichens as air thinsMAP and air temperature correct it automatically
    Distributionone mixture shared through the manifold; end cylinders differone injector per cylinder, equal fuel
    Cold startchoke: crude, rich, high HCcoolant-temperature tables, tapered enrichment
    Transientsaccelerator pump; flat spotsacceleration enrichment and wall-wetting model

    Emissionscannot feed a three-way catalystλ = 1 for a three-way catalyst
    Cost, simplicitycheap, no electronicssensors, ECU, pump, injectors
  3. Mixture accuracy in numbers. The engine fuelling example shows what a 2 % lean drift does at the catalyst: tailpipe NOx rises from 0.05 to 0.40 g/km, 6.7 times the BS VI limit. A carburettor routinely drifts by more than 2 % with temperature and altitude, so it cannot hold the window.
  4. Electronic carburetion. Fitting a feedback solenoid to a carburettor gave λ near 1 at steady cruise and was a stop-gap; it could not fix distribution, cold start or transients, so it gave way to injection.
  5. Fuel economy and power. Injection needs no venturi, so the intake can be larger (higher volumetric efficiency, more power), and the fuel cut and precise cruise mixture save fuel. The fuel-control example shows the ECU computing 26.83 mg per injection to within the injector's resolution.
  6. Evaluation. For any engine sold under BS VI, injection is the only practical choice: the limits need closed-loop λ control, catalyst heating and on-board diagnostics, none of which a carburettor can provide. The carburettor survives only where emission rules are loose, such as some small off-road engines.
  7. Check. The comparison rests on one number: the three-way catalyst window, about 1 % in λ, is narrower than a carburettor's drift.
Evaluate Evaluate the effect of EGR on NOx, knock, combustion stability and fuel consumption, and explain how the ECU meters EGR. 13 marks

Points to cover. NOx forms at high peak temperature; EGR dilutes the charge and lowers it; Moderate EGR reduces throttling loss and knock, allowing more advance; Too much EGR slows the burn, raises HC, causes misfire; Little or none at idle and full load; Metering: stepper or vacuum valve, position feedback, MAP check; cooled EGR.

Model answer.

  1. Why NOx forms. NOx forms when nitrogen and oxygen combine at the highest flame temperatures (above about 2000 K), and its rate rises steeply with temperature. Cutting peak temperature by even a little cuts NOx a lot.
  2. What EGR does. Exhaust gas is mostly N2, CO2 and H2O, which do not burn. Mixed into the charge, it absorbs heat (CO2 and H2O have high heat capacity) and dilutes the oxygen, so the flame is cooler and slower. Typical part-load EGR rates of 10 to 20 % cut engine-out NOx substantially.
  3. Effects, evaluated:
    EffectModerate EGR at part loadToo much EGR
    NOxfalls stronglyfalls further, but…
    Knockreduced (cooler end gas), so more advance is allowed–
    Burn rateslower; ECU adds advance (the EGR correction in the spark table)very slow; partial burns
    Combustion stabilityacceptablemisfire, rough running, HC rises
    Fuel consumptionlittle change or slightly better: less throttling, less heat lossworse
    COlittle changerises with partial burns
  4. Why fuel use need not rise. To make the same torque with exhaust in the charge, the throttle must open wider, so manifold pressure rises and the pumping loss falls. Cooler gas also loses less heat to the walls. These gains roughly offset the slower burn, so the net effect at part load is small or slightly positive.
  5. Where EGR is shut. At idle the burn is already unstable, so EGR would cause misfire. At full load every bit of oxygen is wanted for power. During cold start the burn is too slow. The ECU therefore schedules EGR from a speed-load map, with zero in these regions.
  6. Metering. The EGR valve is either a stepper-motor or DC-motor valve (position sensed by a potentiometer) or a vacuum diaphragm switched by a solenoid. The ECU commands a position from its map and checks the result: the valve position sensor, and a rise in manifold pressure when the valve opens (or a change in mass air flow, which drops because exhaust replaces fresh air). A stuck valve sets a diagnostic code.
  7. Cooled EGR. Passing the exhaust through a coolant-cooled heat exchanger makes it denser and cooler, so more can be added for the same volumetric efficiency penalty and knock falls further; downsized turbo petrol engines and all modern diesels use it.
  8. Verdict. EGR is the cheapest in-cylinder NOx control for a petrol engine and costs little fuel when it is metered by the ECU and kept away from idle and full load. Its limit is combustion stability, so the calibration finds the largest rate that still burns smoothly at each point.
Evaluate A car has a fault in the manifold absolute pressure sensor. Analyse how the ECU detects the fault and what fallback strategies keep the car running. 14 marks

Points to cover. Circuit checks: signal out of range high or low (open, short to ground or supply); Plausibility: compare with throttle angle, speed, a model or the air-flow sensor; key-on reading against barometric pressure; Fallback: substitute load from throttle and speed (alpha-N) or a default value; Open-loop fuel, safe spark advance, EGR off, limited speed; Warning lamp, fault code, freeze-frame data read over the diagnostic link.

Model answer.

  1. The sensor. A MAP sensor is a piezoresistive bridge on a silicon diaphragm with a reference vacuum on one side. It gives a voltage that rises with absolute pressure, typically about 0.5 V at very low pressure to 4.5 V near atmospheric, from a 5 V reference. The ECU uses it as its main load signal for fuel (speed density) and spark.
  2. Circuit checks (electrical faults):
    ReadingLikely faultDetection
    Below about 0.2 Vopen signal wire or short to groundout of range low for a set time
    Above about 4.8 Vshort to the 5 V reference or open groundout of range high
    Frozen valueblocked hose or failed elementno change while the throttle moves
  3. Plausibility checks (signal in range but wrong). With the key on and the engine stopped, the MAP must read barometric pressure (about 100 kPa at sea level); a reading of 60 kPa is implausible. At idle with the throttle closed a healthy engine pulls about 30 to 40 kPa; at wide-open throttle about atmospheric. The ECU also compares MAP with a model built from throttle angle and speed, and, if fitted, with the mass air-flow sensor. A persistent mismatch is a fault even though the voltage is in range.
  4. Fallback load. Once a fault is confirmed, the ECU stops using the sensor and computes a substitute load from throttle angle and engine speed (the alpha-N method, using a stored table), or at worst a fixed default value. Air density is then estimated from the intake temperature and the last known barometric pressure.
  5. What changes in the strategies:
    SystemFallback
    Fuelbase pulse from the substitute load; closed-loop λ trim kept if the oxygen sensor is healthy, which corrects much of the load error
    Sparka safe, retarded map so that knock is unlikely
    EGRswitched off (its check uses MAP)
    Engine speedlimited; boost (on a turbo) reduced
    Driverwarning lamp on; the car can be driven to a workshop (limp-home)
  6. Recording. The ECU stores a diagnostic trouble code (for example, MAP circuit high) with freeze-frame data: speed, coolant temperature, throttle angle and trims at the moment of the fault. A technician reads them through the on-board diagnostic connector, which talks to the ECU over the CAN bus.
  7. Evaluation. Detection must avoid false alarms, so each check needs the fault to persist for a set time or number of cycles. The fallback trades emissions and performance for safety: the car keeps moving, the engine is protected by the retarded spark, and the oxygen sensor still limits the mixture error. A speed-density engine without a backup load signal is the worst case, which is why many engines cross-check MAP against the throttle.

  4. ADC and timer inputs. A 10-bit ADC with a 5 V reference resolves 5 ÷ 1024 = 4.9 mV per count. Timer inputs time the gap between crank teeth to get speed and angle to a fraction of a degree.
  5. Processor and memory. The microcontroller runs the program from flash memory, keeps live values in RAM and stores learned trims and fault codes in EEPROM or flash. Each cycle it interpolates the maps: in the concept example, 2000 rpm and 46 kPa give a 5.2 ms base pulse. It adds corrections and works out when, in crank degrees, to switch each output.

The on-board diagnostic system monitors sensors, catalyst, EGR and misfire, stores fault codes with freeze-frame data, lights the warning lamp, and switches to limp-home values when a sensor fails. A scan tool reads all this through the diagnostic connector.

Glossary

Air-fuel ratio
Mass of air divided by mass of fuel entering the engine; about 14.7 for complete combustion of petrol.
BMEP
Brake mean effective pressure: brake work per cycle divided by swept volume.
Brake thermal efficiency
Brake power divided by the heat energy supplied by the fuel per second.
Calibration
Filling the ECU tables from dynamometer and road tests so the engine meets emission, power and driveability targets.
Controller area network (CAN)
A two-wire broadcast bus with message identifiers and arbitration that lets vehicle ECUs share data.
Conversion efficiency
The fraction of a pollutant entering the catalyst that it removes.
Crank position sensor
An inductive or Hall-effect sensor reading a toothed wheel to give crank angle and speed.
Dead time
The opening delay of an injector, about 0.5 to 1 ms, added to every pulse and longer at low battery voltage.
Diagnostic trouble code
A stored code identifying which monitored circuit or system has failed.
Dwell
The time (or crank angle) the ignition coil primary is switched on before the spark.
EGR valve
A valve that admits a metered fraction of exhaust gas into the intake to lower combustion temperature.
Engine control unit (ECU)
The computer that reads the sensors, runs the maps in real time and drives the actuators.
Engine map
A two-dimensional table of calibrated values indexed by engine speed and load.
Excess-air ratio (λ)
Actual air-fuel ratio divided by the stoichiometric ratio; 1 means chemically correct.
Fuel injector
A solenoid valve that sprays fuel; the quantity is set by its open time and the pressure difference across it.
Fuel trim
The ECU's correction to injection time from the oxygen sensor: short-term (live) and long-term (learned).
Homologation
The official approval test a new vehicle model must pass before it can be sold.
Knock
Self-ignition of the end gas ahead of the flame, giving a sharp pressure rise and a metallic ringing.
Light-off temperature
The catalyst temperature at which conversion reaches about half; about 250 to 350 °C.
Limp-home mode
A fallback in which the ECU uses substitute values and limits the engine so the car can reach a workshop.
Manifold absolute pressure
Pressure in the intake manifold measured from vacuum; the ECU's main load signal in speed density.
MBT
Minimum spark advance for best torque.
Pulse width
The time in milliseconds the ECU holds an injector open.
Spark advance
The crank angle before top dead centre at which the spark occurs.
Stoichiometric mixture
The mixture in which all the fuel can burn using all the oxygen supplied.
Three-way catalyst
A converter that oxidises CO and HC and reduces NOx together when the mixture stays near λ = 1.
Volumetric efficiency
Air actually trapped in the cylinder divided by the air that would fill the swept volume at manifold conditions.
