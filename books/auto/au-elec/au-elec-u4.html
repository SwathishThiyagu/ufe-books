4.1 Emission norms: Euro and Bharat Stage standards

An emission standard is a legal upper limit on pollutant output per kilometre of a fixed driving cycle: mass in g/km for CO, HC, NOx and PM, and count in particles/km for PN. It exists because carbon monoxide, hydrocarbons, nitrogen oxides, particulates and particle number harm health and air. Because the test runs the whole vehicle, the limit covers the vehicle, not the engine alone. Each new stage lowers the ceiling, and India follows the Euro ladder as Bharat Stage. This chapter shows how limits are written and checked, and why tighter limits push the engine into precise electronic control.

You need: exhaust species and air-fuel ratio from 4.2, and the lambda sensor from Unit 3.

Limits per kilometre, not per engine

An emission standard is a legal ceiling on pollutants per kilometre. The regulated pollutants are carbon monoxide (CO), hydrocarbons (HC), oxides of nitrogen (NOx), particulate matter (PM) and particle number (PN). CO is poisonous. HC and NOx help form smog. PM and PN reach deep into the lungs. A petrol-car limit is written in g/km; PN is in particles/km. The test runs the whole vehicle on a fixed driving cycle on a chassis dynamometer. The result is the vehicle's tailpipe output, not the engine alone. Some stages set a combined HC + NOx limit, so you cannot compare HC and NOx as two separate numbers. An engine test may use g/kWh, a different unit for a steady load. The limit is met only if every pollutant is at or below its ceiling; one failure fails the vehicle. For example, a car may pass CO and HC and still fail on NOx.

Euro ladder, Bharat Stage steps, and the 2020 jump

Euro 1 to Euro 6 is a ladder: each stage lowers the ceilings. India based its Bharat Stage I to BS VI limits on the Euro ladder, with some India-specific differences in test requirements and timelines. India skipped BS V and moved from BS IV to BS VI in April 2020. With that fuel change, sulphur in petrol fell from 50 ppm (parts per million) to 10 ppm. Lower sulphur protects the catalyst and allows a diesel particulate filter to work. A stage number is not a quality mark on the engine; it is a type-approval limit for a vehicle family. Homologation means the official approval test. The tighter ceilings force electronic engine control: in a petrol engine the ECU holds λ = 1, the chemically correct air-fuel ratio, for the three-way catalyst, and sets spark timing. A diesel engine runs lean (λ > 1) and cuts NOx with exhaust gas recirculation (EGR), a lean NOx trap or selective catalytic reduction (SCR). The lambda sensor and closed-loop control are in 4.5; EGR and the three-way catalyst are in 4.6. Some syllabi call this chapter 'emission legislation' or 'homologation limits'. Only a few universities teach this chapter in depth.

Worked example

Question. A petrol car on the test cycle emits 0.82 g/km CO, 0.07 g/km HC and 0.11 g/km NOx. Limits: BS IV CO 1.0, HC 0.10, NOx 0.08 g/km; BS VI CO 1.0, HC 0.10, NOx 0.06 g/km. (a) Pass BS IV? (b) Pass BS VI? (c) NOx exceedance in g/km and percent for each.

  1. CO margin: (1.0 − 0.82)/1.0 × 100 = 18%. HC margin: (0.10 − 0.07)/0.10 × 100 = 30%. Both are under both stages.
  2. NOx vs BS IV: 0.11 − 0.08 = 0.03 g/km over; 0.03/0.08 × 100 = 37.5%. Overall fail BS IV.
  3. NOx vs BS VI: 0.11 − 0.06 = 0.05 g/km over; 0.05/0.06 × 100 = 83.33%. CO and HC still pass, but overall fail BS VI.

Answer. The car fails both stages overall: CO passes by 18%, HC by 30%, NOx exceeds BS IV by 0.03 g/km (37.5%) and BS VI by 0.05 g/km (83.33%).

Exam traps

  • Mixing units: g/km is a vehicle limit from a chassis dynamometer; g/kWh is an engine limit from a steady test.
  • Comparing HC and NOx separately when the stage gives a combined HC + NOx limit.
  • Thinking BS VI followed BS V in India. India skipped BS V.
  • Treating PM mass and PN as the same. PM is mass; PN is particle count.
  • Calling a vehicle a pass because CO and HC pass while NOx is over the ceiling.

4.2 Engine fuelling, exhaust emissions and fuel economy

An engine burns a mixture of petrol and air. The air-fuel ratio sets how completely that burn uses the fuel. It also sets how much carbon monoxide, unburnt hydrocarbon and nitrogen oxide leave the tailpipe. The controller therefore cannot choose one ratio for all conditions: it must move between rich, stoichiometric and lean as the engine warms, accelerates or cruises. This chapter links combustion, the three-way catalyst, exhaust gas recirculation and fuel economy in litres per 100 km, km per litre and grams of carbon dioxide (CO2) per km.

You need 4.1 for the legal ceilings and first-year chemistry of complete and incomplete combustion; the oxygen sensor itself is covered in Unit 3, Exhaust gas oxygen (lambda) sensors.

Combustion and lambda

Petrol needs about 14.7 kg of air for every 1 kg of fuel to burn completely. This is the stoichiometric air-fuel ratio, AFRst. The actual air-fuel ratio AFR is air mass divided by fuel mass. Lambda λ is AFR divided by AFRst. At λ = 1 the mixture is stoichiometric. λ < 1 is rich: not enough air for all the fuel. λ > 1 is lean: excess air.

The pollutants follow λ. CO (carbon monoxide) forms when fuel burns with too little oxygen, so CO rises sharply rich. HC (unburnt hydrocarbon) comes from flame quench at the cold cylinder wall, gas trapped in crevices and unburnt mixture; it too is high when rich. NOx (nitrogen oxides) forms when nitrogen and oxygen combine at high flame temperature and stay hot long enough. It is low rich, peaks slightly lean, then falls as the flame cools. Diesel PM (particulate matter) comes from fuel-rich spots inside a spray, even when the average diesel mixture is lean.

The figure plots relative CO, HC and NOx against λ. At λ = 1.0 it shows CO 35, HC 45 and NOx 60. At λ = 1.05 NOx reaches 85 while CO falls to 22. Only a narrow band near λ = 1 suits a three-way catalyst.

λ = AFR ÷ AFRst = AFR ÷ 14.7

AFR is actual air mass per fuel mass in kg air per kg petrol; AFRst is 14.7 kg air per kg petrol; λ is dimensionless, with λ = 1 stoichiometric, λ < 1 rich and λ > 1 lean.

Catalysts and exhaust gas recirculation

An oxidising catalyst uses exhaust oxygen to burn CO to CO2 and HC to CO2 and water. It lowers CO and HC but cannot remove NOx. A three-way catalyst also reduces NOx to nitrogen. It needs the exhaust to swing slightly rich and slightly lean around λ = 1: rich spells feed the reduction, lean spells feed the oxidation. Outside that window one pollutant always runs away.

The catalyst must be hot. Light-off temperature is about 250 to 350 °C. Below light-off conversion is poor, so the first minute after a cold start can carry much of a trip's emissions. The controller runs rich and retards spark to heat the catalyst, then returns to λ = 1.

EGR, exhaust gas recirculation, sends part of the exhaust back to the intake. The inert gas dilutes the charge and lowers peak combustion temperature, so NOx falls. At part load EGR has little effect on fuel economy and can slightly improve it by reducing pumping losses and allowing more spark advance. Excessive EGR slows combustion, can misfire and then does cost fuel. The ECU schedules EGR by speed and load and switches it off at idle and full load.

  • Oxidation: 2CO + O2 → 2CO2; for a hydrocarbon CnHm: CnHm + (n + m/4) O2 → n CO2 + (m/2) H2O.
  • Three-way: oxidation plus NOx reduction, but only inside the λ window.

Fuel economy and CO2

Fuel economy has two common forms. km per litre is higher-is-better; litres per 100 km is lower-is-better. They are reciprocal: L/100 km = 100 ÷ (km/L). A car giving 14.7 km/L uses 100 ÷ 14.7 = 6.8 L/100 km.

The CO2 mass is fixed by the carbon content of the fuel; almost all of that carbon leaves as CO2, so CO2 scales with fuel burnt, while CO and HC come from incomplete combustion. Burning 1 kg of petrol makes about 3.16 kg of CO2. For grams of CO2 per km, take fuel mass per km in kg, multiply by 3.16, then multiply by 1000. Fuel economy and CO2 are two views of the same fuel flow.

The controller chooses a compromise at every operating point. Rich mixtures can give more power and lower NOx, but raise CO, HC and fuel use. Lean mixtures can save fuel at light load, but raise NOx and may misfire. EGR cuts NOx; at part load it has little effect on fuel economy and can slightly improve it by reducing pumping losses, but excessive EGR slows combustion, can misfire and then does cost fuel. The three-way catalyst forces steady cruise close to λ = 1, the only point where all three pollutants can be cleaned at once.

Worked example

Question. A car uses 6.8 L of petrol to cover 100 km. Petrol density is 0.74 kg/L and burning 1 kg of petrol makes about 3.16 kg of CO2. Find the fuel mass per 100 km, the fuel mass per km, the CO2 in g/km, and λ when the air-fuel ratio drops to 13.2:1 under acceleration.

  1. Fuel mass per 100 km = volume × density = 6.8 L × 0.74 kg/L = 5.032 kg per 100 km, rounded to 5.03 kg.
  2. Fuel mass per km = 5.032 kg ÷ 100 = 0.05032 kg/km, rounded to 0.0503 kg/km.
  3. CO2 mass per km = 0.05032 kg/km × 3.16 kg CO2 per kg fuel = 0.1590112 kg/km.
  4. In grams: 0.1590112 kg/km × 1000 g/kg = 159.0112 g/km, rounded to 159 g/km.
  5. At AFR = 13.2:1, λ = 13.2 ÷ 14.7 = 0.8979, rounded to 0.90. This is rich because λ < 1.

Answer. The car uses 5.03 kg of petrol per 100 km, or 0.0503 kg/km, and emits 159 g CO2/km. At 13.2:1 the mixture is rich with λ = 0.90.

Exam traps

  • Treating λ and AFR as the same number. λ is dimensionless; AFR is kg air per kg fuel. λ = 1 means AFR = 14.7 for petrol, not 1:1.
  • Believing a three-way catalyst cleans any mixture. Outside its narrow window near λ = 1, CO or NOx runs away.
  • Forgetting light-off. A cold catalyst at 20 °C does almost nothing even at λ = 1; about 250 to 350 °C is needed.
  • Thinking rich is free NOx control. Rich lowers NOx but raises CO, HC and fuel use, and can overheat the catalyst.
  • Mixing CO2 with regulated pollutants. CO2 is a greenhouse gas tied to fuel carbon, not a CO/HC/NOx limit.

4.3 Engine performance variables: torque, power and BSFC

Torque is crankshaft twist. Power is torque × angular speed. BSFC is fuel mass to deliver each kWh at the crankshaft, in g/kWh. A large slow engine has big torque and modest power; a small fast engine reverses this. A badly matched engine can be thirsty at both low and high speed.

You need the rich-mixture torque/fuel trade-off from 4.2 and work, power and energy basics.

Torque, speed and brake power

Torque T is crankshaft twisting effort, in N·m. A dynamometer holds speed steady and measures it. Turning the shaft one radian delivers T joule, so shaft power is T × ω, where ω is angular speed in rad/s.

P = 2πNT/60

P is brake power in W; T is brake torque in N·m; N is speed in rev/min; 2π/60 converts rev/min to rad/s.

Brake power is power left at the crankshaft after friction and pumping losses. At 3000 rpm and 150 N·m, P = 2π×3000×150÷60 = 47.1 kW. At 4000 rpm torque is 145 N·m but P = 60.7 kW. Speed multiplies torque; beyond the torque peak speed still rises faster than torque falls, so power climbs.

BMEP is brake work per cycle divided by swept volume of all cylinders. For a four-stroke, BMEP = 4πT ÷ V_d; for a two-stroke, BMEP = 2πT ÷ V_d. T is N·m, V_d is m³, BMEP is Pa. T ÷ V_d is only proportional to BMEP, but that is enough to compare engines of different sizes.

BSFC and brake thermal efficiency

A fuel flow meter gives fuel mass flow ṁ_f, usually kg/h. BSFC is that flow divided by brake power.

BSFC = ṁ_f × 1000 ÷ P

BSFC is in g/kWh; ṁ_f is in kg/h; P is in kW; 1000 converts kg to g.

Small BSFC is good. Petrol engines reach about 240–260 g/kWh at best point. Diesels reach 200 g/kWh or below because higher compression extracts more work.

One kWh delivered is 3600 kJ. Fuel burnt to make it holds BSFC × CV kilojoule, where CV is calorific value.

η_bth = 3600 ÷ (BSFC × CV)

η_bth is a fraction; BSFC is in g/kWh; CV is in MJ/kg (= kJ/g); 3600 is kJ per kWh.

At 244.04 g/kWh and 43 MJ/kg, η_bth = 3600 ÷ 10 494 = 0.3431, or 34.3 percent. About two-thirds leaves as heat.

The BSFC map and mechanical efficiency

A BSFC map plots contours of BSFC against speed (x) and brake torque or load (y). Its low-BSFC island sits at moderate speed, about 2000–3000 rpm, and high load near wide-open throttle.

  • Friction and pumping grow with speed, so at high rpm more fuel just turns the engine.
  • At light load fixed losses are shared over little brake power, so BSFC is poor.
  • At full load enrichment protects components, and extra fuel raises BSFC again (see 4.2).

Indicated power is gas power inside cylinders. Brake power survives after friction and pumping. Friction power = indicated − brake. Mechanical efficiency η_m = P_brake ÷ P_indicated; petrol engines run about 0.85, falling at high speed.

These variables set the ECU fuel and spark tables in 4.4.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder petrol engine runs at 3000 rev/min and 150 N·m. Fuel flow is 11.5 kg/h; petrol CV is 43 MJ/kg. Find brake power, BSFC and brake thermal efficiency.

  1. ω = 2π×3000/60 = 314.16 rad/s.
  2. P = Tω = 150×314.16 = 47.124 kW.
  3. BSFC = 11.5×1000 ÷ 47.124 = 244.04 g/kWh.
  4. η_bth = 3600 ÷ (244.04×43) = 0.3431 = 34.31%.

Answer. 47.12 kW, 244.04 g/kWh, 34.31%.

Exam traps

  • Putting rev/min straight into P = Tω; convert with ω = 2πN/60.
  • Forgetting 1000 in BSFC; kg/h ÷ kW gives kg/kWh, 1000 times too small.
  • Confusing torque peak with power peak; power peaks at higher speed.
  • Wrong CV unit in η = 3600/(BSFC×CV); use MJ/kg (= kJ/g).

4.4 Concept of electronic engine control

An electronic engine control unit (ECU) is a small computer that runs the engine. It reads sensors, looks up calibrated tables called maps, and drives injectors, the ignition coil and valves in a repeating loop. It exists because a three-way catalyst needs the air-fuel ratio held within about one percent of λ = 1 for a whole vehicle life, through wear, heat and altitude. No carburettor or mechanical distributor can do that. Petrol engines use this stoichiometric loop. Diesel engines also have an ECU, but they always run lean (λ well above 1) and control injected fuel quantity directly, cleaning the exhaust with an oxidation catalyst, a particulate filter and selective catalytic reduction instead of a three-way catalyst.

You need: 4.1 for the legal limit, 4.2 for the air-fuel compromise, and 4.3 for the targets the maps hold.

Why a computer, and what a loop means

Petrol burns cleanly in a three-way catalyst only when the exhaust stays within a narrow band around λ = 1. Here λ (the excess-air ratio) = actual air-fuel ratio ÷ stoichiometric air-fuel ratio. λ = 1 means the mixture is exactly stoichiometric; λ = 1.05 means 5 percent more air than stoichiometric. The catalyst converts carbon monoxide, hydrocarbons and nitrogen oxides at once in that band; outside it, conversion of at least one pollutant collapses. The band is about one percent wide in λ. A carburettor meters fuel through fixed jets and a venturi. Its mixture drifts with jet wear, fuel pressure, air density, altitude and engine temperature. It cannot hold one percent over ten years and 150 000 km. The electronic control unit (ECU) can, because it measures and corrects.

Open loop means the ECU computes an output from stored calibration alone. It looks up a number and fires the injector for that long, with no sensor confirming the result. The assumption is that fuel pressure, airflow and injector flow are exactly as calibrated.

Closed loop means a sensor measures the result and the ECU corrects the output. The exhaust oxygen (lambda) sensor produces a voltage that switches sharply from lean to rich at λ = 1. A zirconia switching sensor gives about 0.1 V when the exhaust is lean and about 0.8 to 0.9 V when rich, crossing over within a very small change of λ around 1; the ECU treats roughly 0.45 V as the switch point. The ECU compares the measured mixture with its target and adds a correction, called a trim. Electronic hardware alone does not make a loop; a table lookup is still open loop until a measurement closes it.

Inside the ECU: signals, maps and interpolation

Signal path. A sensor output is rarely ready for a processor. Signal conditioning scales it into the analogue-to-digital converter's (ADC) input range, filters radio-frequency noise, and protects against voltage spikes. The ADC turns the voltage into a count. A 10-bit ADC with a 5 V reference gives 2¹⁰ = 1024 steps, so one count is 5 ÷ 1024 ≈ 4.9 mV.

The processor runs a program stored in flash memory, uses RAM for temporary values, and keeps adaptive trims in non-volatile memory (EEPROM or flash) in most modern ECUs; older designs hold them in keep-alive RAM, which loses the learned trims when the battery is disconnected. Driver stages are power switches, usually low-side n-channel MOSFETs that ground one end of an injector or coil; a flyback diode catches the inductive kick when the switch opens.

Calibration grid. The central object is a two-dimensional table, also called a map. One axis is engine speed in rpm, the other is load — manifold absolute pressure (MAP) in kPa, or air mass per stroke in mg. Each cell stores one calibrated value: injector open time, spark advance, idle valve position, EGR lift, target lambda. Values come from bench and vehicle tests, not from a formula.

y = y₁ + (y₂ − y₁) × (x − x₁) ÷ (x₂ − x₁)

y is the value wanted (for example injector open time in ms); x is the measured speed or load; (x₁, y₁) and (x₂, y₂) are the two neighbouring calibrated cells.

At a speed and load between cells, the ECU interpolates linearly along each axis. The full set of cells is the engine map. A three-dimensional surface uses the same idea with four surrounding cells.

Worked example

Question. In the injection-duration map for a warm engine, the column for 2000 rpm holds 4.0 ms at 40 kPa manifold pressure and 6.0 ms at 50 kPa. The ECU reads 46 kPa. Find the injector pulse width, and the fraction of one engine cycle it takes.

  1. The measured load 46 kPa lies between the calibrated cells 40 kPa and 50 kPa at 2000 rpm, so interpolate along the load axis.
  2. Pulse width = 4.0 + (6.0 − 4.0) × (46 − 40) ÷ (50 − 40) ms.
  3. Pulse width = 4.0 + 2.0 × 6 ÷ 10 = 4.0 + 1.2 = 5.2 ms.
  4. A four-stroke cycle takes two revolutions. One revolution takes 60 ÷ 2000 s = 0.030 s, so one cycle is 0.060 s = 60 ms.
  5. Fraction of cycle = 5.2 ÷ 60 = 0.0867, which is 8.7 percent.

Answer. The injector is open for 5.2 ms, which is 8.7 percent of the 60 ms four-stroke cycle.

Exam traps

  • Calling a map-based output 'closed loop'. A lookup from a table is open loop even though it is electronic; the loop closes only when a sensor measures the result and the ECU corrects for it.
  • Interpolating along the wrong axis. The rpm column and the load row are different axes; use the two cells that bracket the measured load at the same speed.
  • Mixing milliseconds and percent. Pulse width is an open time in ms; duty is that time divided by the cycle time, and a four-stroke cycle is two revolutions, not one.
  • Treating λ = 1 as the target everywhere. At full load the ECU deliberately runs rich in open loop to protect exhaust valves, and during cranking and warm-up the lambda sensor is not used.
  • Thinking limp-home is normal closed loop. Limp-home replaces a failed sensor with a fixed substitute value and limits engine output; it keeps the car moving, not emissions compliant.

The catalyst converts CO, HC and NOx together only in a narrow band around λ = 1. If the mixture drifts rich, CO and HC conversion falls; if it drifts lean, NOx conversion falls. One percent is the practical width of that window over the life of the vehicle.

Apply A warm-engine injection map at 3000 rpm stores 3.5 ms at 30 kPa and 5.5 ms at 40 kPa. The ECU reads 36 kPa. Find the pulse width and the percentage of one four-stroke cycle. 4 marks

Pulse width = 3.5 + (5.5 − 3.5) × (36 − 30) ÷ (40 − 30) = 3.5 + 1.2 = 4.7 ms. One cycle at 3000 rpm = 2 × 60 ÷ 3000 = 0.040 s = 40 ms. Fraction = 4.7 ÷ 40 = 11.75 percent.

Analyze The lambda sensor fails on a highway cruise. What does the ECU do, and which control mode is active? 3 marks

The ECU detects the missing or stuck sensor signal and enters limp-home. It discards closed-loop trim, substitutes a fixed or mapped pulse width, and may limit rpm. The engine runs open loop with poorer emission control but keeps the vehicle moving.

Apply An injector is open for 6 ms in a four-stroke cycle at 2500 rpm. Find the cycle time and the duty percentage. 4 marks

Cycle time = 2 × 60 ÷ 2500 = 0.048 s = 48 ms. Duty = 6 ÷ 48 = 0.125 = 12.5 percent.

Analyze Why does the ECU need two different ways to measure air, speed density and mass airflow? 4 marks

Speed density is cheap and robust because it uses MAP and a volumetric-efficiency map, but it is sensitive to camshaft changes and altitude unless calibrated. Mass airflow measures air directly and handles those changes, but the hot-film sensor is costlier and can be contaminated. Manufacturers choose by engine type, cost and required accuracy.

Understand What is deceleration fuel cut and why does it save fuel and emissions? 3 marks

When the throttle is closed and engine speed is above about 1500 rpm, the ECU switches the injectors off. The engine is driven by the wheels, so no fuel is needed. Fuel and emissions fall to zero during the cut; injection resumes near idle to prevent a stall.

4.5 Electronic fuel control and petrol fuel injection

Electronic fuel control estimates or measures the air entering the engine, computes the fuel mass needed, and holds each injector open for the time that delivers it. An oxygen sensor in the exhaust reports what actually burned, and the controller trims the pulse. Every modern petrol car runs this loop.

You need: 4.4 for open and closed loop control, and Unit 3 for the MAP (manifold absolute pressure), throttle and oxygen sensors and for stepper and solenoid actuators.

Why the carburettor had to go

A carburettor lets incoming air speed up through a narrow throat called a venturi; the pressure drop there sucks fuel out through a jet. The jet is a fixed hole, so fuel flow follows air flow only as well as the venturi curve allows, and nothing measures the result. On a hot afternoon, on a cold start, or when the throttle snaps open, the mixture drifts away from the ratio the catalyst needs. Emission norms tighten that ratio at every stage (see emission norms and Bharat Stage standards), and a fixed jet cannot follow.

Electronic carburetion was the stop-gap. A solenoid valve bled air into the carburettor's fuel passages, and the controller moved it from the exhaust oxygen signal. It held λ = 1 at steady cruise, but fuel was still atomised by venturi vacuum and cylinder-to-cylinder distribution stayed uneven.

Petrol injection replaced the jet with a solenoid valve per cylinder, opened for a computed time. Three layouts exist.

  • Throttle-body injection (TBI): one or two injectors above the throttle. Cheap, but slow to respond to transients.
  • Port fuel injection (PFI): one injector per cylinder in the intake port above the valve. The fuel has a whole intake stroke to evaporate. This is the common layout.
  • Direct injection (GDI): the injector sprays into the cylinder at 100 bar or more. It cools the charge and allows lean stratified running, but it makes fine soot particles.

The injector and the fuel calculation

Inside the injector is a solenoid: a coil, an armature and a needle held on its seat by a spring. Current in the coil pulls the needle up, fuel at rail pressure sprays, and when the current stops the spring closes the valve in about a millisecond.

The static flow rating is the mass of fuel the injector passes per second when held fully open, in g/s or cc/min, always quoted at a stated pressure, usually 3 to 4 bar for port injection. Flow rises roughly as the square root of pressure. Two drive methods are used.

  • Saturated drive: battery voltage is held across the coil for the whole pulse. Simple and cheap; the needle opens slowly, so it suits small injectors.
  • Peak-and-hold drive: about 4 A for the first millisecond pulls the needle open fast, then the driver drops to about 1 A to hold it. Large injectors need this, and the coil runs cooler.

The controller turns an air mass into a pulse in four steps.

  1. Estimate the trapped air mass flow from speed density.
  2. Divide by the air-fuel ratio to get fuel mass flow.
  3. Divide by the number of injections per second to get fuel per injection.
  4. Divide by the injector flow to get the open time.

m_air = η_v × ρ × V_d × (N ÷ 2)

m_air is air mass flow in kg/s; η_v is volumetric efficiency, the air actually trapped divided by the swept volume's worth, typically 0.8 to 0.9 at full load, and slightly above 1 is possible with tuned intake and exhaust; ρ is intake air density in kg/m³, from the MAP (manifold absolute pressure) sensor and the intake temperature sensor; V_d is total engine displacement in m³; N is engine speed in revolutions per second. The ÷2 is because a four-stroke inducts air only every second revolution.

t_inj = (m_air ÷ AFR) ÷ (n_cyl × N ÷ 2) ÷ Q_inj

t_inj is injector open time in s; AFR is the air-fuel ratio by mass, 14.7 for petrol at λ = 1; n_cyl is the number of cylinders; Q_inj is the injector static flow in kg/s.

Many engines measure the air directly with a hot-wire or hot-film air-flow meter (MAF) instead of speed density. The fuel calculation, the trim loop and the fault reasoning stay the same once the air mass flow is known.

Closed loop, fuel trim and the actuators

The narrowband oxygen sensor is a zirconia cell whose voltage switches sharply as the exhaust crosses λ = 1: high when rich, low when lean (see Unit 3). It does not say how rich or how lean. The controller therefore steps the pulse up and down and watches for the switch to reverse. That hunt around λ = 1 is closed loop.

Short-term fuel trim (STFT) is the instant correction, a percentage added to or taken from the base pulse, usually limited to about ±25%. When STFT stays biased, the ECU learns the offset into a long-term fuel trim (LTFT) table, so the next time the engine runs at that speed and load it starts from the corrected value. LTFT hides a dirty air-flow sensor or a clogged injector until it hits its limit and sets a fault code.

Three actuators work alongside the loop.

  • EGR valve: a stepper motor, or a vacuum diaphragm switched by a solenoid, opens a passage from exhaust to intake. It dilutes the charge and lowers peak flame temperature, which cuts NOx.
  • Idle air stepper motor: a pintle moves in and out to bypass air around the closed throttle. It sets idle speed hot or cold, and lifts it when the alternator or the air-conditioner loads the engine.
  • EVAP purge solenoid: opens to draw petrol vapour stored in the canister into the intake. That vapour is extra fuel, so the oxygen sensor reads rich and the ECU shortens the injector pulse to keep λ = 1.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder 1.6 L petrol engine runs at 3000 rpm with volumetric efficiency 0.85. Intake air density is 1.16 kg/m³ and the air-fuel ratio is 14.7:1. Exam traps

  • Using the full displacement per revolution: a four-stroke inducts air into only half its cylinders per revolution, so divide V_d by 2.
  • Mixing densities: speed density needs the intake manifold density from MAP and intake temperature, not an ambient bench figure, and η_v must not be dropped.
  • Confusing milliseconds with milligrams: pulse width is time, fuel per injection is mass; the injector flow converts one to the other.
  • Thinking the oxygen sensor sets the mixture: it only trims a base pulse that comes from the calibration map, and it is cold and inactive during a cold start.
  • Swapping the trims: STFT is the instant correction, LTFT is the value learned and stored against speed and load.

4.6 Ignition and spark timing control

The spark must arrive at a crank angle, not an instant, so the engine control unit works in degrees. It charges the coil for a fixed dwell time, fires the plug a set number of degrees before top dead centre, and retards that angle when the knock sensor complains. Spark advance decides torque, fuel economy, emissions and piston survival. Every petrol car on Indian roads runs this loop thousands of times a minute, at little extra cost once the ECU exists for fuelling.

You need: 4.4 for maps and closed loop, 4.5 for load and shared corrections, and Unit 2 for how the coil and plug work.

Dwell time and dwell angle

The coil stores energy only while its primary circuit is closed. Current rises towards a limit set by winding resistance, typically 4 A to 6 A. Stored energy is ½LI², where L is primary inductance in henry and I is current in ampere. Opening the circuit collapses the field; the secondary makes high voltage and the plug arcs.

Dwell time td is how long, in milliseconds, the ECU holds the primary closed before the break. It is fixed for a coil and battery voltage. A low battery makes current rise more slowly, so the ECU lengthens dwell.

Dwell angle θd is the same interval in crank degrees. The ECU works in degrees because the spark must land at a crank angle.

θ<sub>d</sub> = t<sub>d</sub> × N × 360 ÷ 60 000 = 0.006 × N × t<sub>d</sub>

θd is dwell angle in crank degrees; td is dwell time in milliseconds; N is engine speed in rpm; 360 is degrees in one revolution and 60 000 is milliseconds in one minute.

At 3000 rpm one millisecond is 18 crank degrees, so 3.0 ms spans 54 degrees. At 6000 rpm one millisecond is 36 degrees; the ECU shortens dwell to 2.0 ms. The window must fit between sparks.

Spark advance, MBT and the knock limit

The mixture needs time to burn. The flame starts at the plug, crosses the chamber, and for best torque the pressure peak should fall about 10 to 15 degrees after top dead centre. So the plug fires before TDC. Spark advance θadv is the crank angle in degrees before top dead centre (BTDC) at which the plug fires.

More advance starts the burn earlier and pulls the pressure peak towards TDC, so torque rises; past a point the gain stops. Minimum advance for best torque (MBT) is the smallest advance giving maximum torque. Beyond MBT, extra advance adds heat and knock and buys nothing.

Knock is unburnt end gas ahead of the flame front igniting by itself, giving a sharp pressure spike that can crack a piston. The knock limit is the most advance the engine survives at that speed and load. It drops with low-octane petrol, hot intake air, hot coolant and carbon deposits.

The ECU fires at θspark = min(θMBT, θknock − Δ), where θspark is firing advance, θMBT is MBT advance, θknock is knock-limit advance, all in degrees BTDC, and Δ is a 1 to 3 degree safety margin. At low speed and high load the knock limit usually sits below MBT, so the engine runs retarded (less advance) and gives up torque.

The spark map and its corrections

Base advance comes from a two-dimensional map, with engine speed on one axis and load on the other. Load is air trapped per cycle, measured by the mass air flow sensor or computed from manifold pressure as in 4.5. This is the map lookup of 4.4 applied to one output.

  • Light load, 1500 to 2500 rpm: 30 to 45 degrees BTDC. Charge dilute, burn slow, knock unlikely.
  • Wide open throttle: 10 to 20 degrees BTDC. Dense charge burns fast and knocks easily.
  • Idle: 5 to 15 degrees BTDC, nudged every firing to hold idle speed.

The map holds steady-state values only. Typical corrections, added as angles before charging:

CorrectionAdvance changeReason
Coolant coldadd 5 to 15 degreesCold charge burns slowly
Intake air hotretard 2 to 6 degreesHot air raises knock risk
EGR (exhaust gas recirculation) flowingadd 3 to 8 degreesInert gas slows the burn
Knock heardretard 2 to 4 degrees per stepClosed loop below knock limit
Low-octane fuelretardKnock limit falls

Corrections are summed and clamped to a safe band for that speed and load. The same sensor corrections appear in fuel control, where they change injector open time instead of coil timing.

Closed-loop knock control and misfire detection

The knock sensor is a piezo accelerometer bolted to the block (Unit 3). It rings at 5 to 15 kHz when the block rings from knock. The ECU band-pass filters around the knock frequency, rectifies, and compares the peak with a speed-dependent threshold.

  1. Knock seen: retard by 2 to 4 degrees, for that cylinder if coils are per cylinder.
  2. No knock: advance slowly, about 0.5 to 1 degree per firing.
  3. Knock returns: retard again. Timing settles just under the knock limit and hunts.
  4. Knock persists at full retard: enrich the mixture, reduce boost, or both.

Fast retard and slow advance keep the loop stable. Retard is immediate because knock damages metal in seconds; advance is slow because a few degrees too far is heard after the next firing.

Misfire shows up as a crank that does not speed up. The crank sensor gives a pulse every few degrees, and the ECU times each segment to get angular acceleration. A firing cylinder accelerates the crank; a dead one does not. Comparing each cylinder with the average gives a misfire index, which sets the on-board diagnostic (OBD-II) code (Unit 6) and protects the catalytic converter from raw fuel.

Worked example

Question. At 3000 rpm the ECU wants the spark 25 degrees before top dead centre and a dwell of 3.0 ms. (a) How many crank degrees is 3.0 ms at this speed? (b) At what crank angle before TDC must the coil start charging? (c) At 6000 rpm the coil needs only 2.0 ms: find the new dwell angle and the new start angle.

  1. 3000 rpm = 50 rev/s; 50 × 360 = 18 000 crank degrees/s; 18 000 ÷ 1000 = 18 degrees/ms.
  2. (a) Dwell angle = 3.0 × 18 = 54 degrees.
  3. (b) Charging starts 54 degrees before the spark, and the spark is 25 degrees BTDC: 25 + 54 = 79 degrees BTDC.
  4. 6000 rpm = 100 rev/s; 100 × 360 = 36 000 degrees/s; 36 degrees/ms.
  5. (c) Dwell angle = 2.0 × 36 = 72 degrees; charging starts at 25 + 72 = 97 degrees BTDC.

Answer. The 3.0 ms dwell is 54 degrees and charging starts 79 degrees BTDC; at 6000 rpm the 2.0 ms dwell is 72 degrees and charging starts 97 degrees BTDC.

Exam traps

  • Adding milliseconds to degrees. Convert first: degrees = ms × N × 0.006.
  • Treating dwell angle as constant. Dwell time is set by the coil; angle grows with speed.
  • Calling advance a time. 25 degrees BTDC is about 1.4 ms at 3000 rpm.
  • Saying the ECU always runs at MBT. It runs at min(MBT, knock limit − margin).
  • Mixing loops: knock control retards spark; misfire detection only reports.

Takeaways

  1. Each new emission stage lowers the legal ceiling per kilometre, and only close mixture and spark control can meet it.
  2. Air-fuel ratio is a compromise: rich mixtures give power and cool the exhaust, lean mixtures give economy but raise NOx, and λ = 1 is the only point where a three-way catalyst works on all three pollutants at once.
  3. Torque is the twisting effort, power is torque multiplied by speed, and BSFC tells you how much fuel each unit of that power costs.
  4. An ECU replaces mechanical feel with stored maps indexed by speed and load, so the same engine can hold different compromises at every operating point.
  5. Electronic fuel control estimates trapped air per cylinder, converts it to injector open time, then trims that time using the exhaust oxygen sensor.
  6. Spark timing and dwell are set digitally, and the knock sensor lets the ECU run close to the knock limit without damaging the engine.
  7. Sensors, actuators, the ECU and the in-vehicle network together form the engine management system that diagnostics, traction control and cruise control all read from.

Glossary

Air-fuel ratio
Mass of air divided by mass of fuel entering the engine, about 14.7 for complete combustion of petrol.
Brake thermal efficiency
Brake power divided by the heat energy supplied by the fuel.
Calibration
The process of filling those tables by testing the engine on a dynamometer and choosing values that meet emission and driveability targets.
Closed-loop trim
Correction the ECU applies to injection time from the oxygen sensor, kept as short-term and long-term learning.
Controller area network (CAN)
A two-wire broadcast bus with message identifiers and arbitration that lets vehicle modules share data.
Crank angle sensor
A sensor, usually magnetic or Hall effect, that reports crankshaft position and speed to the ECU.
Dead time
The short part of an injector pulse, roughly 0.5–1 ms, in which the injector has opened but fuel flow has not reached full rate.
Diagnostic trouble code
A stored code that identifies which monitored circuit or system has failed.
Dwell
Time the ignition coil primary is energised before the spark.
EGR valve
A valve that admits a controlled fraction of exhaust gas into the inlet to lower combustion temperature.
Engine control unit
The computer that runs the maps in real time and drives the actuators.
Engine map
A two-dimensional table of calibrated control values indexed by speed and load.
Excess air ratio (λ)
Actual air-fuel ratio divided by the stoichiometric ratio; 1 means chemically correct.
Fuel injector
An electrically opened valve that sprays atomised fuel; the quantity delivered is set by open time and pressure difference.
Hall effect
A voltage appears across a current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field; used in position and speed sensors.
Homogeneous charge
A charge in which fuel vapour and air are fully mixed before ignition.
Knock
Uncontrolled auto-ignition of the end gas after the spark, producing a sharp pressure rise and metallic sound.
Limp-home mode
A fallback strategy in which the ECU uses substitute values so the car can be driven slowly to a workshop.
Manifold absolute pressure
Pressure inside the inlet manifold measured against vacuum, used with speed to estimate trapped air.
Pulse width
The time in milliseconds an injector stays open, commanded by the ECU.
Spark advance
Crank angle before top dead centre at which the spark occurs, chosen from a speed-load map.
Stoichiometric mixture
The mixture strength at which all fuel can burn using all the oxygen supplied.
Three-way catalyst
A converter that oxidises CO and HC and reduces NOx together when the mixture stays near stoichiometric.
Volumetric efficiency
Ratio of air actually trapped in the cylinder to the swept volume filled at inlet conditions.
