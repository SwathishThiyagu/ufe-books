2.1 Ignition fundamentals and coil ignition system

A petrol engine needs a spark across a plug gap at the end of compression, at roughly 10–20 kV. The battery-coil ignition system makes that spark from a 12 V battery. Current flows in a low-voltage primary winding; a contact breaker opens the circuit; the collapsing magnetic field induces a high voltage in a secondary winding. A distributor sends this pulse to the correct cylinder. The same mechanism advances or retards the spark as speed and load change. It is also called the Kettering system or contact-point ignition, and it was the standard car ignition for decades.

You need Unit 1 for the battery, the ignition switch and high-voltage wiring, and the idea that a changing current makes a changing magnetic field.

Four jobs, one coil

An ignition system has four jobs. It must generate a voltage high enough to jump the plug gap: about 10–20 kV normally, more under heavy compression. It must send that pulse to the cylinder ready to fire. It must fire at the correct crank angle, a little before top dead centre (TDC), because the flame takes time to build pressure. It must vary that angle with speed and load: more speed needs more advance, heavy load needs less.

The arrangement that does all four with one coil is the battery-and-coil system, also called the Kettering system or contact-point ignition. A 12 V battery, an ignition switch and a ballast resistor feed the primary winding. The ballast resistor limits primary current; many circuits bypass it during cranking. A cam-driven contact breaker opens the primary circuit. A secondary winding sits on the same core. A distributor with a rotor and cap routes the secondary pulse to each plug in the firing order. The coil stores energy, the points time the pulse, and the distributor sends it out.

How the collapse makes high voltage

When the points close, current builds in the primary winding. This winding has a few hundred turns of thick wire, so its resistance is low and the current can reach several amperes. The current sets up a magnetic field in the iron core.

At the firing point, a cam lobe opens the points. Primary current falls to zero and the magnetic field collapses. A changing flux Φ through a winding induces an emf in every turn:

e = −N dΦ/dt

e is induced emf in volts (V); N is turns; Φ is magnetic flux in webers (Wb); t is time in seconds (s). The minus sign is Lenz's law: the emf opposes the change.

The same core links primary and secondary, so the changing flux induces an emf in both. This is mutual induction. The secondary has many thousands of turns of fine wire. Its emf is roughly e₂ = (N₂/N₁) × e₁ when both windings link the same flux. The speed of the collapse matters more than the turns ratio alone: a fast fall gives a large dΦ/dt. The secondary pulse goes through the distributor cap and rotor to one plug lead, then across that plug gap.

Dwell angle, dwell time and the capacitor

Dwell is the time the points stay closed. The coil needs enough dwell to build current; too little gives a weak spark, too much heats the coil. Dwell angle is the angle the distributor cam turns while the points are closed. Data books may quote it in distributor degrees or crank degrees. On a four-stroke engine the cam turns at half engine speed, so one distributor degree is two crank degrees.

Dwell time is the same interval in seconds:

t_dwell = (θ_dwell / 360°) × (1 / f_cam)

θ_dwell is dwell angle in cam degrees; f_cam is cam revolutions per second.

For a multi-cylinder engine use the angle between sparks: dwell angle in crank degrees = dwell fraction × θ_spark.

A capacitor of about 0.2–0.3 μF sits across the points. When the points start to open, the primary current would jump the gap as an arc. The arc burns the points and keeps current flowing, so the field collapses slowly. The capacitor gives the current a short path and absorbs the energy. The primary circuit then oscillates and the current falls quickly, giving a high dΦ/dt and a high secondary voltage. The capacitor also stops arcing, so the points last.

Advance curves: centrifugal and vacuum

The spark must occur earlier as speed rises: flame speed is roughly fixed while the piston moves faster. It must occur earlier at light load, because a lean mixture burns slowly. At heavy load, a rich mixture burns faster and too much advance can cause knock.

Centrifugal advance uses two weights on a plate driven by the distributor shaft. As speed rises, the weights swing outward and turn the cam relative to the shaft, so the points open earlier. Vacuum advance uses a diaphragm linked to the inlet manifold. Light load means high vacuum; the diaphragm pulls the breaker plate round and adds advance. Heavy load means low vacuum, and a spring removes the advance.

The result is an advance curve: spark advance in crank degrees before TDC against engine speed, with a vacuum correction for load. Base timing is set at idle with the vacuum pipe disconnected. The engine ECU in Chapter 2.3 replaces weights and diaphragms with a lookup table, but the two needs remain.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder, four-stroke engine runs at 3000 rpm. The contact breaker dwell is 60% of the spark interval. Find the spark frequency, the spark interval, the dwell angle in crank degrees and the dwell time.

  1. Engine speed = 3000 rpm = 3000/60 = 50 rev/s.
  2. A four-stroke cycle is 720° of crank. Four cylinders fire once each per cycle, so one spark every 720/4 = 180° of crank, i.e. two sparks per revolution.
  3. Spark frequency f = 50 × 2 = 100 sparks/s.
  4. Spark interval T = 1/f = 1/100 = 0.010 s = 10 ms.
  5. Dwell angle = 0.60 × 180° = 108° of crank. On the distributor cam this is 108/2 = 54°.
  6. Dwell time = 0.60 × 10 ms = 6 ms.

Answer. Spark frequency = 100 sparks/s; spark interval = 10 ms; dwell angle = 108° of crank (54° of distributor); dwell time = 6 ms.

Exam traps

  • Using rpm/60 as the spark frequency and forgetting the ×2 for a four-stroke four-cylinder engine.
  • Taking 60% of 360° instead of 60% of the angle between two sparks (180° of crank here).
  • Thinking the coil steps up a steady 12 V like a transformer. It needs the primary current to change, and the fast collapse makes the high voltage.
  • Saying the capacitor increases the spark energy directly. It suppresses arcing at the points, speeds the collapse and protects the points.
  • Confusing spark advance with firing order or with camshaft timing: advance means firing earlier before TDC.

2.2 Spark plugs and spark pulse generation

A spark plug seals a small gap inside the combustion chamber and fires a spark across that gap. The spark is timed shortly before top dead centre on the compression stroke. The voltage needed to break the gap is not fixed. It rises with gap size and cylinder pressure, and falls when the electrodes are hot. The ignition coil must supply that voltage with enough stored energy. If the coil cannot, the engine misfires, especially under full load. This chapter explains plug construction, heat range, fouling and the high-voltage pulse path.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the coil, primary current and the collapsing-field idea.

Construction and heat range

The plug is a metal shell around a ceramic insulator. A terminal at the top takes the high-tension cable. The insulator is alumina ceramic; it holds the centre electrode and carries heat from the tip to the shell. The centre electrode runs through the insulator. Its tip faces one or more ground electrodes fixed to the shell. The shell screws into the cylinder head and gives the earth return. A suppressor seal is a small resistor near the terminal. It limits radio-frequency noise and seals combustion gas. A copper core in the centre electrode carries heat away; platinum or iridium tips resist erosion.

Heat range is the plug's ability to shed heat from its tip to the shell. It is set by the length of the ceramic nose. A hot plug has a longer nose: the tip runs hotter, so it burns off carbon and oil. It suits a cold engine or a lightly loaded engine that would otherwise stay below self-cleaning temperature. A cold plug has a shorter nose: the tip runs cooler and avoids pre-ignition. It suits a hot engine or a high-power engine. The range number alone is not enough; numbering systems run in opposite directions, so match the plug to the engine maker's specification.

The gap is the shortest distance between the centre and ground electrodes. It is set to 0.7–1.1 mm. A gap that is too small gives a weak, short spark; too large a gap raises the firing voltage beyond the coil. Carbon or oil fouling builds a conductive bridge across the insulator or gap. That bridge leaks current before the spark can form.

Firing voltage and the high-voltage circuit

The gas in the plug gap is an insulator until free electrons gain enough energy to ionise it. The voltage at which this avalanche starts is the firing voltage, Vf. Vf rises when the gap is wider, because the electric field is weaker for the same voltage. It rises with cylinder pressure, because denser gas has more molecules to ionise. It falls when the electrodes are hot, because a hot surface emits electrons more easily. Typical values are Vf = 10–35 kV, gap = 0.7–1.1 mm and spark duration = 1–2 ms. A high-compression engine at full load sits near the top of that band.

V₂ = n V₁

V₂ is the ideal secondary voltage in volts (V); n is the turns ratio N₂/N₁; V₁ is the primary voltage induced at current break in volts (V), not the battery voltage.

The coil must make V₂ larger than Vf. The coil stores energy in its magnetic field. At current break the primary voltage rises to V₁, not 12 V, and the secondary gives V₂. A 1:100 coil with V₁ = 250 V gives 25 kV ideal. The high-voltage path is secondary → HT cable → distributor cap → rotor → plug cable → terminal → centre electrode → gap → ground electrode → shell → engine block → earth. Each joint adds loss: rotor gap, cable resistance, suppressor cap, dirt on the cap. The plug sees less than the coil's ideal V₂.

Pulse energy, fouling and misfire

Pulse energy follows the formula below. A coil with 8 mH and 6 A stores 144 mJ. That energy sustains the spark for about 1–2 ms. Too little energy gives a short, weak spark that cannot ignite a lean or turbulent mixture. Too much energy erodes electrodes. The coil therefore needs both enough turns ratio for voltage and enough stored energy for duration.

E = ½ L I²

E is stored energy in joules (J); L is primary inductance in henries (H); I is primary current in amperes (A).

A fouled plug can fire at low pressure and fail at high pressure. At idle the cylinder pressure is low, so Vf is low. At full load the pressure is high, so Vf rises. If the coil's V₂ is already near the limit, the extra few kilovolts decide between spark and misfire. The same logic explains why a worn plug with an enlarged gap misfires first under load; the gap raises Vf. See Chapter 2.3 for how the ECU controls dwell and timing.

Worked example

Question. A coil has primary inductance 8 mH and the primary current reaches 6 A before the points open. Find the energy stored. The coil has a 1:100 turns ratio and the primary voltage at break is 250 V. Find the secondary voltage and check whether it fires a 0.9 mm gap on full load using the full-load line.

  1. Convert inductance: L = 8 mH = 8 × 10−3 H. Primary current I = 6 A.
  2. Stored energy: E = ½ L I² = 0.5 × 8 × 10−3 × 6² = 0.144 J = 144 mJ.
  3. Turns ratio n = N₂/N₁ = 100. Primary induced voltage at break V₁ = 250 V.
  4. Ideal secondary voltage: V₂ = n V₁ = 100 × 250 V = 25,000 V = 25 kV.
  5. Gap = 0.9 mm = 9 units of 0.1 mm, so x = 9. From the full-load line, Vreq = 10 + 2.2 × 9 = 29.8 kV.
  6. Compare: V₂ = 25 kV is less than Vreq = 29.8 kV by 4.8 kV. The coil cannot fire this gap at full load, so the engine misfires under load.

Answer. Stored energy is 144 mJ; ideal secondary voltage is 25 kV; the full-load requirement at 0.9 mm is 29.8 kV, so the coil is short by 4.8 kV and the engine misfires under load.

Exam traps

  • Do not multiply the turns ratio by 12 V. The secondary voltage is n times the primary voltage induced at current break; the battery voltage is not present across the primary at that instant.
  • Do not read the plot's x-axis as millimetres. It is in 0.1 mm steps, so 0.9 mm is x = 9, not x = 0.9.
  • Higher compression raises firing voltage. It does not lower it, because denser gas has more molecules to ionise.
  • A hot plug is not a plug for a hot engine. Hot range runs hotter for a cold or lightly loaded engine; cold range runs cooler for a hot, high-power engine.
  • Suppressor caps and resistor plugs reduce radio noise; they add resistance and secondary loss, not spark energy.

2.3 Electronic and programmed ignition systems

An electronic ignition system replaces the contact breaker with a sensor and a transistor. The transistor switches the coil primary current, and an electronic module or the engine ECU decides when to start that current and when to stop it. Programmed ignition goes further: it stores spark advance and dwell in look-up maps indexed by engine speed and manifold pressure (or air flow), then adds corrections for coolant temperature, knock and starting. You meet it on almost every petrol car and motorcycle sold today.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the coil and primary circuit, Chapter 2.2 for spark voltage, and crank position sensors from Unit 3.

From points to a transistor switch

The contact breaker is a mechanical switch driven by the camshaft. A rubbing block opens the points, the primary current falls, and the coil makes the high-voltage pulse. Three problems appear as engines get faster. The points burn because they interrupt several amperes many times a second. The dwell angle drifts as the rubbing block wears, so coil current changes. At high speed the points may not stay closed long enough to build full current.

Electronic ignition removes the rubbing block. A magnetic reluctance or Hall-effect pick-up on the crankshaft or distributor sends a pulse each time a trigger wheel tooth passes. The pulse tells the module the crank angle. The module drives a power transistor, often called the igniter, in series with the coil primary. The transistor does the switching; the pick-up only reports position. A small signal from the pick-up controls a large current in the coil.

Dwell control is the next step. Dwell is the time the primary current is allowed to flow before the spark. In a constant-dwell system the module keeps the dwell time nearly fixed, so the coil gets the same charging time at every speed. In a constant-energy system the module measures battery voltage and coil current and adjusts dwell to reach a target energy before each spark. At low battery voltage it lengthens dwell; at very high speed the time between sparks becomes too short, so it must shorten dwell and accept less than the target energy.

f<sub>spark</sub> = (N/60) × (z/2);  T<sub>spark</sub> = 1/f<sub>spark</sub>;  t<sub>dwell</sub> = D × T<sub>spark</sub>

fspark is sparks per second; N is engine speed in rev/min; z is number of cylinders; Tspark is the time between sparks in seconds; D is dwell as a fraction of the spark period; tdwell is dwell time in seconds. The z/2 factor applies to a four-stroke engine, which sparks once per two revolutions per cylinder.

At 5000 rpm a four-cylinder four-stroke engine makes 166.7 sparks/s, so each spark period is 6 ms. A dwell fraction of 0.60 then gives 3.6 ms of primary current.

Programmed ignition: the advance map

Programmed ignition, also called electronic spark advance or digital ignition, stores the spark timing in a look-up table inside the ECU. The table has engine speed on one axis and manifold pressure or air flow on the other. Each cell holds a spark advance in degrees before top dead centre (BTDC). The ECU reads the cell for the current speed and load, then adds corrections. Coolant temperature correction gives extra advance when the engine is cold or reduces it while warming. Knock correction retards the spark in small steps when the knock sensor reports detonation, then returns advance slowly. Starting correction fixes a safe advance during cranking.

The chain has seven blocks. A crank sensor gives speed and crank angle. A knock sensor gives a retard request. The ECU holds the map and calculates dwell and timing. The ignition driver, or igniter, switches the primary current. The coil stores energy and makes the high voltage, typically around 25 kV. The distributor sends that voltage to the correct plug in the firing order. On a four-cylinder engine the order may be 1-3-4-2. Later systems remove the distributor, as Chapter 2.4 explains.

The ECU sends a dwell pulse to the igniter. The leading edge starts primary current; the trailing edge stops it and fires the spark. The time between the two edges is the dwell time. The angle between the trailing edge and top dead centre is the spark advance. Both are computed, so the same engine can use different values for idle, cruise and full load.

Control methods at block level

Four control methods appear in order of increasing freedom. Mechanical control uses centrifugal and vacuum advance; it cannot correct for temperature. Electronic control replaces points with a transistor but may still use a fixed advance curve. Mapped control uses a table in memory, so the advance can follow any shape the calibrator chooses. Closed-loop knock control adds feedback: the knock sensor listens for detonation, the ECU retards timing, and then advances it back until knock reappears. This lets the engine run near the knock limit without damage.

The driver sees the result as smooth idle, clean acceleration and no pinging under load. The service technician sees it as a scan tool data stream: rpm, manifold pressure, coolant temperature, calculated advance, knock retard and dwell. A faulty crank sensor usually stops the spark; a faulty knock sensor usually forces a default retard.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder four-stroke petrol engine runs at 5000 rpm. The ignition system holds a dwell of 60% of the spark period. A knock sensor requests 4° of spark retard. Find the spark rate in sparks per second, the spark period, the dwell time, and the time taken by 4° of crank rotation.

  1. Engine speed N = 5000 rev/min. Number of cylinders z = 4. For a four-stroke engine, sparks per second f = (N/60) × (z/2) = (5000/60) × (4/2) = 83.333 × 2 = 166.667 sparks/s.
  2. Spark period T = 1/f = 1/166.667 s = 0.006 s = 6 ms.
  3. Dwell fraction D = 0.60. Dwell time t = D × T = 0.60 × 6 ms = 3.6 ms.
  4. One revolution takes 60/5000 s = 0.012 s = 12 ms. That covers 360°. So 4° of crank takes 4 × 12/360 ms = 0.133 ms.

Answer. Spark rate is 166.7 sparks/s, spark period is 6 ms, dwell time is 3.6 ms, and 4° of crank rotation takes 0.133 ms.

Exam traps

  • Treating dwell as a fixed angle at all speeds. With points, the dwell angle is fixed, so the dwell time shrinks as speed rises and the coil may not reach full current. An electronic constant-dwell system fixes the dwell time instead, and a constant-energy system varies the dwell time so each spark reaches a target coil energy.
  • Mixing spark advance with dwell. Advance is when the spark occurs relative to top dead centre; dwell is the charging time before that spark.
  • Forgetting the z/2 factor for a four-stroke engine. Using N/60 alone gives one spark per revolution, not one spark per two revolutions per cylinder.
  • Reading the map axis as accelerator pedal position. Many advance maps use manifold pressure or air flow, not pedal angle.
  • Assuming knock retard is permanent. The ECU retards timing only while knock is present, then advances back toward the mapped value.

2.4 Distributorless and direct ignition systems

Distributorless ignition removes the rotating distributor and gives each coil its own job. In the common waste-spark form, one coil serves a pair of cylinders and fires both plugs together: one spark occurs near the end of compression, the useful one, and the other occurs in the exhaust stroke and is wasted. Direct ignition goes further: one coil per cylinder, mounted on or near the plug, fires only on compression. The engine control unit (ECU) decides timing from crank and cam position. Only a few universities teach this as a separate topic, so treat it as an advanced extension of Chapter 2.3.

You need: Chapter 2.3 for ECU switching and the coil driver, plus basic crank and cam sensor ideas from Unit 3.

Waste-spark distributorless ignition

Waste-spark ignition uses one coil for two cylinders whose pistons rise and fall together but whose strokes are opposite. In a four-cylinder engine with firing order 1-3-4-2, the pairs are 1&4 and 2&3. The ECU charges the coil primary, interrupts it at the calculated angle, and the secondary sends high voltage to both plugs. One plug is near the end of compression and lights the mixture. The other is in exhaust and its spark is wasted, though it still loads the coil. Each coil fires twice in the 720° cycle, which is two crank revolutions. Coil 1&4 fires at cylinder 1 compression and 360° later at cylinder 4 compression. Coil 2&3 fires at 180° and 540°. The figure shows this order. Waste spark needs no distributor cap, rotor or long high-voltage cables, so it is cheap and common on small engines.

Direct ignition: one coil per cylinder

Direct ignition gives every cylinder its own coil, mounted on the plug (coil-on-plug) or close to it (coil-near-plug). The ECU fires that coil only on the compression stroke. A crank sensor and a trigger wheel give speed and crank angle within one revolution. A trigger wheel is a toothed disc whose teeth pass a sensor; a missing tooth or a special tooth marks a reference. The crank alone cannot separate compression from exhaust because the two revolutions look identical. A cam sensor turns at half crank speed and adds the phase. With both signals the ECU knows the 720° position and can fire each coil once per cycle. This is why direct ignition needs a cam sensor for true sequential operation. The high-voltage path is short, so radio interference and cable leakage fall. Timing is more accurate because there is no rotor gap or mechanical advance to wear.

Service and timing checks

The service picture changes. There is no rotor to burn or cap contact to erode, and no distributor shaft play to upset timing. A misfire on one cylinder points to that cylinder's coil, plug or short high-voltage boot; a paired misfire on a waste-spark engine points to the shared coil. The ECU can cut fuel to a cylinder and watch crank speed, or measure coil current, to identify a dead coil. Replacement coils are often smart coils with built-in igniters, triggered by a low-voltage signal. Keep the plug gap and heat range correct: a wide gap raises the voltage demand and can cause flashover inside a worn coil boot. For the driver circuit inside the ECU see Unit 4; for sensor physics see Unit 3.

Worked example

Question. A 4-cylinder engine with firing order 1-3-4-2 runs at 4000 rpm on a waste-spark system with coils paired 1&4 and 2&3. Find the sparks per second per coil and the crank angle of each spark in the 720° cycle.

  1. Engine speed = 4000 rpm ÷ 60 = 66.67 rev/s.
  2. A 4-stroke cycle needs 2 revolutions, so total sparks per second = 66.67 × 2 = 133.3.
  3. Each of the two coils fires half the sparks: 133.3 ÷ 2 = 66.7 sparks per second per coil.
  4. One 720° cycle lasts 2 ÷ 66.67 = 0.030 s = 30 ms.
  5. Cylinder 1 fires at 0°, cylinder 3 at 180°, cylinder 4 at 360° and cylinder 2 at 540°. Coil 1&4 sparks at 0° and 360°; coil 2&3 sparks at 180° and 540°.

Answer. Each coil sparks 66.7 times per second, the engine produces 133.3 sparks per second, the 720° cycle lasts 30 ms, and the sparks occur at 0° (cylinder 1), 180° (cylinder 3), 360° (cylinder 4) and 540° (cylinder 2).

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the 720° cycle: a crank sensor alone cannot separate compression from exhaust.
  • Thinking a waste-spark coil fires once per revolution; a paired coil fires twice in 720°.
  • Pairing cylinders by neighbouring numbers instead of by equal piston position and firing order.
  • Ignoring the wasted spark when calculating coil energy: both plugs load the same secondary.
  • Calling coil-near-plug the same as coil-on-plug; one has a short high-voltage cable.

2.5 Lighting technology, light sources and lamps

A lamp turns electrical power into light. Its efficacy is the light output per watt, in lumens per watt (lm/W). Halogen, gas-discharge and LED sources differ mainly in that number, in colour and in how fast they start. Car makers choose among them for headlamps, tail lamps and indicators. This chapter compares the sources and the lamp units built around them. Only a few universities teach this topic.

You need: P = V I from Unit 1, Batteries, Starting and Charging Systems.

Photometric units and efficacy

Light is measured in four linked units. Luminous flux Φ is total light output, in lumens (lm). Luminous intensity I is flux per solid angle, in candela (cd = lm/sr). Illuminance E is flux falling on a surface, in lux (lux = lm/m²). Luminance L is intensity per unit area, in cd/m²; it is what the eye sees as the brightness of a surface. Efficacy η is flux per electrical watt: η = Φ / P, in lm/W. Here Φ is luminous flux in lm, P is electrical power in W. A 55 W halogen bulb giving 1500 lm has η = 1500/55 = 27.3 lm/W. Spreading the same 1500 lm over a wider beam does not make the lamp brighter on the road; it lowers illuminance at each point.

Light sources

Filament lamps use incandescence: current heats a tungsten wire to about 2700 K, and it glows. Plain tungsten gives roughly 10–15 lm/W. A halogen lamp adds iodine or bromine vapour; the halogen cycle returns evaporated tungsten to the filament, so the bulb runs hotter and lasts longer, at about 20–30 lm/W. Gas-discharge lamps strike an arc through gas. HID xenon lamps use a xenon fill and metal salts; they give about 80 lm/W and a bluish-white colour, but need a ballast and take seconds to reach full output. Solid-state lamps use electroluminescence: electrons recombine with holes in a semiconductor junction and release photons. LEDs give about 90–110 lm/W, start instantly and last long. Laser headlamps use blue lasers and a phosphor to make a very small, very bright source.

Lamp units and demands

A bare source is not a headlamp. A reflector collects the light and shapes it into a beam. A projector unit uses an ellipsoidal reflector, a lens and a cut-off shield; the shield creates the sharp horizontal cut-off that keeps light out of oncoming drivers' eyes. A lens, or the reflector surface, spreads or focuses the beam. The beam pattern is asymmetric. In left-hand-drive countries, where traffic keeps right, the beam is low and short on the left, towards oncoming traffic, and rises on the right to light the near-side kerb without glare. In India, where traffic keeps left, the pattern is mirrored: the dip is on the right and the rise on the left. Demands: enough flux on the road, no glare above the cut-off, short warm-up, long life, vibration resistance and low cost. Halogen is cheap and instant; HID gives more light but needs a ballast and warm-up; LED is efficient and durable but needs heat management and careful optics.

Worked example

Question. A halogen headlamp uses a 55 W bulb giving 1500 lm. An LED headlamp gives 2000 lm for 20 W. The system runs at 13.2 V while charging. Find the efficacy of each and the current each draws.

  2. LED efficacy = 2000 lm / 20 W = 100.0 lm/W.
  3. Halogen current = 55 W / 13.2 V = 4.17 A.
  4. LED current = 20 W / 13.2 V = 1.52 A.

Exam traps

  • A higher-lumen lamp spread over a wider beam can put less light on the road; compare illuminance at the same distance.
  • HID xenon is not an LED: it needs a ballast and warm-up, and its colour comes from the arc, not a phosphor.
  • Halogen efficacy is 20–30 lm/W, not 80; 80 lm/W is HID.

2.6 Front, rear, interior and special-purpose lighting

A car must show its size and intention to other road users, and light the road for its driver. Lamps are grouped by function: front, rear, interior and special purpose. Each group has a beam pattern, colour rule, switch and legal limit. Only a few universities teach this grouping in depth; the electrical content is the same circuit path — battery, fuse, switch or relay, lamp, earth. You meet it when a Chennai bus flashes its hazard lamps at a breakdown.

You need: Chapter 2.5 for lamp types, ratings and efficacy.

Front lighting

Front lamps do two jobs: light the road and signal. Headlamps have main beam (high beam) for empty roads and dip beam (low beam) for traffic. Dip beam is aimed down and left in India, giving an asymmetric cut-off: the near kerb is lit, the oncoming driver is not dazzled. Aiming screws or a motor set the beam; see Chapter 2.7. The dip cut-off is checked at a test lane; a high aim fails the inspection.

Daytime running lamps (DRLs) are low-power white lamps that switch on with the engine. Fog lamps are wide and low for mist; front fog lamps are white or selective yellow. Position lamps mark the car's width at night. To the front, lamps are white or amber, and the only amber lamps at the front are the direction indicators. A relay is used because two 55 W headlamps draw about 9.2 A at 12 V, too much for a small dash switch. This is why a headlamp relay clicks when you switch on.

Rear and interior lighting

Rear lamps signal from behind. Tail lamps are red and come on with the position lamps. Stop lamps are brighter red and work only when the brake pedal closes its switch; a typical stop lamp is 21 W. Reversing lamps are white and switch on with reverse gear. Direction indicators are amber and flash at about 90 per minute. Rear fog lamps are red and much brighter than tail lamps; use them only in poor visibility. The number-plate lamp is white and lights the plate without glare.

Interior lamps are convenience loads: dome, map, vanity, ambient, boot and glovebox lamps. Many use a door switch to earth, so opening a door completes the circuit. Dimming uses pulse-width modulation (PWM): a switch turns the lamp on and off quickly, and the eye sees average brightness. All these lamps return to the body earth. A failed earth makes several lamps glow dimly through each other.

Special-purpose lamps and the circuit

Special lamps warn or work. Hazard warning lamps flash all four indicators; the hazard switch has its own supply so it works with the ignition off. Beacons and flashing lamps on ambulances, police and maintenance vehicles use a separate flasher or LED driver. Work lamps on trucks and tractors are white and aimed at the job. School-bus lamps include red flashing warning lamps and a stop-arm lamp.

The circuit path is fixed: battery → fuse → lighting switch → relay for high current → lamp → earth return (figure). The fuse protects the wiring, not the lamp. The flasher unit is a thermal or electronic switch that opens and closes the indicator circuit; it sets the flash rate and blinks the dash tell-tale. Electric horns sit on the same auxiliary family: battery, fuse, relay and horn, switched by the horn button. Front and rear lamps are in parallel, so one failure does not stop the others, and the total current adds.

Worked example

Question. Two 55 W headlamps, two 5 W tail lamps, two 5 W number-plate lamps and 3 W of instrument illumination are on together. The battery is at 12.6 V. Find total current and choose the next standard fuse above it with margin.

  • Putting a white lamp at the rear or a red lamp at the front; colour rules are legal.
  • Treating the flasher unit as a fuse; it sets the 90 per minute rate.

2.7 Adaptive lighting systems

A fixed headlamp points where the car points. When the road bends, the beam keeps straight and the inside of the corner stays dark. An adaptive front lighting system (AFS) moves the lamp instead: a swivel motor turns it left and right with the steering, and a level motor tilts it up and down so the beam stays on the road when the boot is loaded. Sensors feed an ECU, and the ECU commands two small motors. Only a few universities teach this topic.

You need: the lamp and beam ideas from Chapter 2.5 and Chapter 2.6, and steering angle sensing from Unit 3.

Swivel and level: two motions

A fixed headlamp is aimed once at the factory and bolted. An adaptive front lighting system (AFS) moves the lamp while you drive, in two independent motions.

Swivel is rotation about a vertical axis, called yaw. The lamp turns left or right with the steering wheel, so the beam enters the bend instead of lighting the hedge.

Levelling is rotation about a horizontal axis, called pitch. The lamp tips up or down so the cut-off line stays on the road. Load causes the problem: passengers in the back and a full boot lift the nose, and a fixed beam climbs into oncoming eyes. Braking dive and acceleration squat do the same briefly.

The two motions are separate. A car may have levelling alone, common with high-intensity discharge lamps because a bright arc must not glare. Swivel needs a steering angle sensor and speed. Most premium cars have both; some have swivel only.

Inputs, ECU and motors

Four inputs feed the AFS electronic control unit (ECU). The steering angle sensor reports steering wheel angle and rate. Wheel speed sensors report vehicle speed over the CAN bus. Ride height sensors link body to axle at front and rear; their difference gives pitch. A forward camera sees oncoming and leading vehicles.

The ECU computes target swivel and pitch angles, then commands two motors. A stepper motor moves in fixed steps and the ECU counts them; a DC motor uses a position sensor, often a potentiometer, to close the loop. The ECU compares commanded and measured angle and corrects.

Swivel is capped near 15°. A larger angle would send the beam across the centre line into oncoming traffic, so the command saturates. Speed shapes the command: a large angle at walking pace, a small one on the highway. On a fault the lamp parks at centre and dips, so the driver still has a low beam.

A matrix headlamp replaces the single source with many switchable LED segments. The camera locates a vehicle, the ECU blanks the segments aimed at it, and the rest stay on high beam. Adaptive driving beam is the function; matrix LED is one way to build it.

Worked example

Question. A boot load sinks the rear of the car by 20 mm (the mass itself is not needed). The wheelbase (rear axle to front axle) is 2.0 m. Find the pitch angle and the levelling correction. The steering wheel is turned 90°, and the swivel command is 0.15 × steering wheel angle, capped at 15°. Find the swivel angle.

  1. Drop = 20 mm = 0.020 m; wheelbase = 2.0 m.
  2. tan θ = 0.020/2.0 = 0.010, so θ = 0.57°.
  3. The rear sinks, so the beam axis tilts up by 0.57°; the level motor must tilt it down by 0.57°.
  4. Swivel command = 0.15 × 90° = 13.5°. This is below the 15° cap, so the lamp turns 13.5°.

Answer. Pitch correction 0.57° downward; swivel 13.5°.

Exam traps

  • Levelling corrects pitch (up-down) for load; swivelling corrects yaw (left-right) for steering. A car can have one without the other.
  • Check which angle the gain is defined on: here it is the steering wheel angle; if the gain is given on road wheel angle, divide by the steering ratio first.
  • Levelling direction: a sinking rear raises the beam, so the motor tilts the lamp down, not up.
  • Ignoring the swivel cap: at about 15° the command saturates, so more steering gives no more swivel.
  • Matrix LED is not a brighter lamp: segments switch off, so light on the road can fall.

2.8 Instrument clusters, dashboard and head-up displays

The instrument cluster is the small computer sitting in front of the driver. It receives frames from other ECUs on the CAN bus and pulses from sensors wired to it directly, then turns that data into a needle angle, a digit, a warning lamp or an image floating on the windscreen. It exists because the driver must read speed, engine speed, fuel, temperature and faults in one glance without looking away from the road. Every car, bus and truck on an Indian road has one.

You need: CAN frames from Unit 6, CAN and CAN FD protocols and wheel-speed sensing from Unit 3, crankshaft position and engine speed sensors.

The cluster as a node on the bus

Behind the dashboard sits a small computer with its own microcontroller, memory and display driver. It is a node on the CAN bus. It reads frames from the engine ECU, the ABS ECU and the body controller, and it also reads a few sensors wired to it directly, such as the fuel-level float.

The cluster drives four things: stepper motors that move the needles, an LCD or TFT panel, LED telltales and the backlight. The main gauges are the speedometer in km/h, the tachometer in rpm (shown in thousands), the fuel gauge from E to F, and the coolant temperature gauge from C to H. The odometer is not a gauge; it is a stored count of distance held in the cluster's memory.

Telltales use fixed colours. Red means stop — low oil pressure, brake fault, handbrake on. Amber means caution — check engine, ABS, low fuel. Green and blue mean status — indicators, high beam. The driver information display adds the trip meter, range, outside temperature and door-open warnings.

The cluster also stores diagnostic trouble codes and lights a lamp when one is set; a scan tool reads the full code. At key-on it runs a self-test: every telltale lights, and every needle sweeps full scale and back. A needle that does not sweep has a failed motor or driver.

Speed from wheel pulses

The speedometer does not use engine rpm. A sensor at the gearbox output or at each wheel sends one electrical pulse for every tooth or magnet that passes it. The number of pulses per kilometre, K, is a constant stored in the cluster for a given tyre and sensor.

The distance per pulse is d = C / N metres, where C is the tyre rolling circumference in metres and N is the number of pulses per wheel revolution. Equivalently, d = 1000 / K when K is in pulses per km.

The cluster counts n whole pulses in a fixed window of T seconds, then computes the speed.

v = 3.6 × n × d / T

v is speed in km/h; n is the number of pulses counted in the window; d is the distance per pulse in metres; T is the window length in seconds; 3.6 converts m/s to km/h.

A short window reacts quickly but is coarse at low speed, because few pulses arrive. A long window is smooth but lags. Many clusters use a longer window below about 30 km/h, or measure the time between pulses instead. Tyre wear, pressure, load and a change of tyre size all change C, so the reading drifts. The same wheel-speed pulses feed the ABS and ESP, which is why one faulty wheel sensor can light the ABS lamp and disturb the speedometer together.

Needles, panels and the head-up display

A modern needle is driven by a small stepper motor through a reduction gear. The driver chip sends a number of steps, not a voltage, so the cluster can command any angle repeatably. The motor holds its position by magnetic detent when power is removed. Older clusters used a crossed-coil movement, where two coils at right angles pulled a magnet and the needle angle depended on the ratio of their currents.

A segmented LCD shows fixed digits cheaply. A dot-matrix LCD or a colour TFT gives free-form text, menus, a reversing camera image or a map. The backlight is a string of white LEDs, dimmed by pulse-width modulation. The dimming command comes from a light sensor on the dash, the headlamp switch, or a button the driver presses; on the bus it is a short CAN frame.

A head-up display (HUD) throws light from a projector in the dash onto a combiner — a small glass screen above the cluster — or onto a special wedge-shaped layer inside the windscreen. The driver sees a virtual image that appears to float ahead of the car.

Virtual image distance is how far ahead the image seems to be. For a windscreen HUD it is typically 2 to 2.5 m in front of the driver's eye. The image is deliberately focused far, not at the glass. A driver looking at the road is focused at 20 m or more. If the HUD image sat at 0.5 m, the eye would have to re-focus and converge for near vision on every glance, taking about 0.2 s and losing the road picture. At 2.5 m the eye stays almost at road focus. The HUD shows speed, the speed limit, a navigation arrow and ADAS warnings — short items read in one glance, not maps. Brightness must follow daylight; too bright at night causes glare.

Worked example

Question. A speedometer receives N = 4 pulses per wheel revolution. The tyre rolling circumference is C = 1.90 m. The cluster counts whole pulses in a window of T = 0.5 s. Find (a) the distance per pulse, (b) the pulse frequency at 100 km/h, (c) the number of pulses counted in one window, and (d) the speed the cluster displays from that count.

  1. Distance per pulse: d = C / N = 1.90 / 4 = 0.475 m per pulse.
  2. Convert the true speed: 100 km/h = 100 / 3.6 = 27.78 m/s.
  3. Wheel revolutions per second = 27.78 / 1.90 = 14.62 rev/s.
  4. Pulse frequency f = 14.62 × 4 = 58.48 pulses/s.
  5. Pulses in T = 0.5 s: n = 58.48 × 0.5 = 29.24. The counter keeps whole pulses, so n = 29.
  6. Displayed speed: v = 3.6 × n × d / T = 3.6 × 29 × 0.475 / 0.5 = 99.18 km/h.

Answer. d = 0.475 m, f = 58.48 pulses/s, count = 29, displayed speed = 99.18 km/h — about 0.8 km/h low because the fractional pulse is dropped.

Exam traps

  • Thinking the speedometer is driven by engine rpm. It is driven by wheel or gearbox pulses, so a change of tyre size or final drive changes the reading.
  • Mixing pulses per kilometre with pulses per revolution. K is per km, N is per wheel turn; convert between them with the rolling circumference.
  • Dropping the factor 3.6 between m/s and km/h, which makes the answer 3.6 times wrong.
  • Using the tyre's nominal radius instead of the loaded rolling circumference. Load and pressure change it, and that changes the stored distance per pulse.
  • Forgetting that the cluster counts whole pulses. The window value rounds down, so the displayed speed is slightly low; averaging several windows or timing the pulse period removes the error.

2.9 Wipers, keyless entry and immobilisers

Wipers clear the screen with a small permanent-magnet motor that has two speeds and a park switch; a washer pump and a rain sensor look after the rest. Keyless entry is a short radio conversation between a fob and the car, and the immobiliser is a second, stricter conversation that decides whether the engine may fire at all. These are the body electronics a driver touches every day: the blade that returns to the bottom of the glass, the door that unlocks as the handle is pulled, and the engine that stays silent for the wrong key.

You need Unit 1 for the battery and starting circuit, and Chapter 2.1 for what a spark needs; body-network frames are in Unit 6.

The wiper motor, its brushes and the park switch

A windscreen wiper is a permanent-magnet DC motor turning a crank. The field does not come from a winding: two curved ferrite magnets in the body give a steady flux. The armature carries the windings, and current reaches them through carbon brushes pressing on a commutator. A worm gearbox cuts the motor's few thousand rev/min to about 40 to 60 sweeps per minute at the blade, and a crank-and-link mechanism turns rotation into the back-and-forth arc.

Most wiper motors have three brushes. Two are the normal supply and earth. The third sits about 30° away and feeds the armature at a shifted angle, which distorts the field and weakens the flux per pole. A weaker field lets the armature spin faster at the same 12 V. So one brush pair gives low speed and the third brush gives high speed — the speed change comes from brush position, not from a higher voltage.

The park switch stops the blade at the bottom of the glass. It is a cam-driven contact inside the gearbox, not the stalk switch the driver moves.

  1. Driver switches the stalk off; the direct supply to the motor is removed.
  2. A second path through the park-switch contact keeps the motor running.
  3. The cam holds that contact closed over most of the circle.
  4. At the park notch the contact opens and the supply is gone.
  5. The switch then shorts the armature to earth, braking it so the blade cannot coast past the bottom.

Intermittent wipe, washer pump and rain sensor

Light rain does not need continuous wiping. The intermittent relay powers the motor for one wipe, waits, then repeats. The wait is set by an RC timer: a capacitor charges through a resistor until its voltage crosses a threshold; the relay then fires and the capacitor resets. A potentiometer on the stalk varies the resistance, so the driver picks the delay. A wiper ECU does the same job with a counter in software.

T ≈ R × C

T is the delay in seconds, R is the timing resistance in ohms, C is the timing capacitance in farads. R = 100 kΩ with C = 100 µF gives T = 100 000 × 0.0001 = 10 s.

The washer pump is a small permanent-magnet motor driving a centrifugal impeller, drawing 2 to 5 A for a second or two. A wipe-after-wash timer runs the blades two or three strokes after the pump stops, so the fluid is spread and then cleared.

A rain sensor shines infrared light into the glass at an angle. The light normally reflects inside the glass by total internal reflection and comes back to a photodiode. Water on the outside lets some light escape, so the photodiode current falls; the electronics read that as rain and step through intermittent, low and high speed.

Remote entry, passive entry and the immobiliser

Remote keyless entry (RKE) is a one-way radio link. Pressing the fob sends a frame: a serial number identifying the fob, a command (lock, unlock, boot) and a rolling code. The receiver, usually in the body control module, demodulates the frame at 315 MHz or 433 MHz and passes it on.

A fixed code can be recorded and replayed by a thief. A rolling code changes on every press: fob and receiver hold the same counter and the same secret key, and the transmitted number is a keyed hash of the counter. The receiver accepts a few values ahead of its own counter, so a fob pressed by accident out of range still works; beyond that window the two are out of step and the fob must be resynchronised.

Passive keyless entry removes the button. Aerials in the door handles and cabin transmit a low-frequency wake-up, about 125 kHz. The fob answers on UHF with its identity. If the answer is valid and the fob is outside the car, the door unlocks when the handle is touched; if the fob is inside, the start button is allowed.

The immobiliser is a second, stricter check. The key holds a transponder — a glass capsule with a chip and a coil, no battery. A coil around the lock barrel drives 125 kHz into it and powers the chip by induction. The car sends a challenge; the chip computes a response with its secret key and returns it. On a match the immobiliser ECU sends a start enable to the engine ECU over the bus. Without it the engine ECU will not drive the coils or open the injectors: the engine cranks but never fires.

Central locking motors do the lock and unlock work that the RKE frame commands, driven by the body control module after a valid frame.

Worked example

Question. A wiper arm is 500 mm long from pivot to tip and sweeps through 120°. It completes 45 wipes per minute, where one wipe is one out-and-back sweep. The intermittent relay times its delay with a 100 kΩ resistor and a 100 µF capacitor. Find (a) the average speed of the wiper tip and (b) the intermittent delay.

  1. Convert the sweep angle to radians: θ = 120 × π/180 = 2.094 rad.
  2. Arc travelled in one one-way sweep: s = rθ = 0.500 m × 2.094 = 1.047 m.
  3. One wipe is out and back: 2 × 1.047 = 2.094 m.
  4. Wipe rate = 45/60 = 0.75 wipes per second.
  5. Average tip speed = 0.75 s⁻¹ × 2.094 m = 1.5708 m/s. The instantaneous tip speed is not constant: it is zero at the ends of the arc and peaks near mid-sweep at about 1.6 times the average.
  6. Delay T = R × C = 100 000 Ω × 100 × 10⁻⁶ F = 10.0 s.

Answer. The average tip speed is 1.5708 m/s (about 1.57 m/s), with the instantaneous speed peaking near mid-sweep at about 2.5 m/s, and the intermittent delay is 10.0 s.

Worked example 2

Question. A rain sensor's photodiode delivers 5.0 µA when the glass is dry and 2.0 µA when it is wet. The sense resistor is 100 kΩ. Find the change in the voltage seen by the comparator.

  1. Dry voltage: V = I × R = 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ A × 100 000 Ω = 0.50 V.
  2. Wet voltage: V = I × R = 2.0 × 10⁻⁶ A × 100 000 Ω = 0.20 V.
  3. Change = 0.50 − 0.20 = 0.30 V.

Answer. The comparator input falls by 0.30 V when rain lands on the glass.

Exam traps

  • The immobiliser usually does not cut the starter. It stops the spark and the injectors, so the engine cranks normally and never fires.
  • A rolling code out of step fails even with the correct key: many presses far from the car push the fob counter past the receiver's acceptance window.
  • Wiper high speed comes from the third brush position, not from 24 V or a bigger motor; the supply stays 12 V.
  • RC delay comes out in seconds only with R in ohms and C in farads: 100 kΩ with 100 µF is 10 s, not 10 ms.
  • The park switch is inside the gearbox cam, not the stalk switch the driver moves — examiners ask for both.

Check yourself

Remember What is the job of the park switch in a wiper motor? 2 marks

It keeps the motor supplied through a cam contact until the blade reaches the bottom notch, then opens and brakes the armature so the blade stops in the park position.

Understand Explain why a third brush gives a higher wiper speed. 3 marks

The third brush feeds armature current at a shifted angle, which weakens the flux per pole. A weaker field raises the speed at the same applied voltage, so no extra supply is needed.

T = R × C = 47 000 × 220 × 10⁻⁶ = 10.34 s.

Analyze A car with the correct key cranks but does not fire. Give two faults consistent with this. 5 marks

The coil around the lock barrel is open, so the transponder is never powered; or the immobiliser ECU is not sending the start enable, for example after a bus fault or an unmatched key. Either way spark and injection stay disabled.

Understand How does passive keyless entry differ from remote keyless entry? 3 marks

RKE needs a button press on a one-way UHF link. PKE wakes the fob with a low-frequency aerial in the car, the fob replies on UHF, and the door unlocks on touching the handle; starting is allowed if the fob is inside.

Analyze Why is a rolling code safer than a fixed code? 4 marks

A recorded fixed code can be replayed later. A rolling code is a keyed hash of a counter, so an old recording is behind the receiver's counter and is rejected.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Production wipers now sit on the LIN bus, so the body controller sends a speed or a delay and the motor module does the rest. Phone-as-key systems replace the UHF fob with Bluetooth Low Energy or ultra-wideband ranging, which measures distance so a relay attack fails. Immobiliser functions live inside larger secure elements and are developed to ISO 26262 and AUTOSAR requirements. Takeaways

  1. A coil ignition system makes high voltage only because the primary current is cut quickly, so the collapsing field links the many secondary turns.
  2. The voltage a plug needs rises with gap size and with compression pressure, so a worn plug or a lean, hot engine misfires first at high load.
  3. Electronic ignition replaces the contact breaker with a transistor, and the ECU then owns dwell and timing as mapped values.
  4. Distributorless and direct ignition push the coil to the plug, removing rotor, cap and long high-tension leads with their losses.
  5. Compare lamps by efficacy in lm/W and by colour, then by life and cost over the whole vehicle life.
  6. Each lighting group has its own beam pattern, colour, switching circuit and legal limit, not just its own bulb.
  7. Adaptive lighting is a closed loop: sensors measure steering angle and vehicle pitch, and a motor moves the beam.
  8. The cluster and the immobiliser are bus nodes, so diagnosis means looking at frames and codes, not only at wires.

Glossary

Ballast resistor
Resistor in series with the coil primary that limits current when running and is bypassed during cranking.
CAN bus
Two-wire differential serial bus on which ECUs exchange short frames with identifiers and data bytes.
Centrifugal advance
Speed-dependent weights and springs that rotate the contact plate to advance timing with engine speed.
Condenser
Capacitor across the contact breaker that absorbs arcing and speeds up the collapse of the primary current.
Contact breaker
Cam-driven mechanical switch in older systems that interrupts the primary current.
Diagnostic trouble code
Stored code that names a fault the ECU has detected, read through the diagnostic connector.
Distributor
Rotating switch that sends the coil's high voltage to each plug in firing order.
Fog lamp
Low, wide-beam lamp used in poor visibility; rear fog lamps must be red and are limited in intensity.
Head-up display
Optical unit that projects speed and warnings onto the windscreen in the driver's line of sight.
Headlamp levelling
Manual or automatic adjustment of headlamp aim to compensate for vehicle load.
Ignition switch
Key-operated multi-position switch that connects battery, ignition, starter and accessory circuits.
Immobiliser
System that prevents engine start unless the correct coded key is present.
Knock sensor
Piezoelectric sensor on the block that detects detonation vibration so the ECU can retard timing.
Lumens
Unit of luminous flux, the total light output of a source.
Park position
Rest position of the wiper blade at the bottom of the screen, held by a switch inside the motor gearbox.
Projector headlamp
Lamp using a small arc or LED source, a reflector and a lens to form a sharp cut-off beam.
Rain sensor
Optical sensor on the windscreen that detects water and switches the wipers automatically.
Resistor spark plug
Plug with a built-in resistor that suppresses radio-frequency interference from the spark.
Rotor arm
Rotating contact inside the distributor cap that bridges the coil lead to one plug terminal at a time.
Stepper motor
Motor that moves in fixed angular steps, used for needles and for headlamp levelling.
Telltale
Symbol lamp in the cluster that reports a system state or fault to the driver.
Vacuum advance
Diaphragm unit driven by inlet manifold depression that adds advance at light load.
Wasted spark
Spark delivered to a cylinder on its exhaust stroke by a coil shared with its pair.
