2.1 Ignition fundamentals and coil ignition system

A petrol engine needs a spark across the plug gap a little before the end of compression, and the gap needs roughly 10–20 kV to break down. The battery-and-coil ignition system makes that spark from a 12 V battery. Current builds in a low-voltage primary winding, a contact breaker opens the circuit, and the collapsing magnetic field induces a high voltage in a secondary winding. A distributor sends the pulse to the right cylinder. It is also called the Kettering system or contact-point ignition.

You need: the battery, ignition switch and wiring of the vehicle power supply, and the idea that a changing current makes a changing magnetic field.

Four jobs, one coil

An ignition system has four jobs: make a voltage high enough to jump the plug gap; send it to the cylinder that is ready; fire at the right crank angle, a little before top dead centre (TDC), because the flame takes time to build pressure; and move that angle with speed and load.

The battery-and-coil system does all four. A 12 V battery, the ignition switch and a ballast resistor feed the coil's primary winding. It limits the running current and is bypassed during cranking, when the starter pulls the battery down to about 9–10 V. A cam-driven contact breaker (the points) completes the primary circuit to earth. A secondary winding sits on the same iron core. A distributor with a rotor and cap routes the secondary pulse to each plug in the firing order, and each plug returns the current to earth through the engine block.

How the collapse makes high voltage

When the points close, current builds in the primary winding, a few hundred turns of thick wire, and sets up a magnetic field in the iron core. At the firing point a cam lobe opens the points, the current falls to zero and the field collapses. A changing flux Φ induces an emf in every turn:

e = −N dΦ/dt

e is the induced emf in volts (V), N the number of turns, Φ the magnetic flux in webers (Wb) and t the time in seconds (s). The minus sign is Lenz's law: the emf opposes the change.

The same core links both windings, so the collapse induces an emf in both. This is mutual induction. The primary emf at the break is a few hundred volts; the secondary has many thousands of turns of fine wire, about 100 times the primary turns, so its emf is roughly e₂ = (N₂/N₁) × e₁, which is 10 kV or more. The speed of the collapse matters as much as the turns ratio: a fast fall gives a large dΦ/dt.

Dwell, coil current and the capacitor

Dwell is the period for which the points stay closed and current flows in the primary. The dwell angle is the angle the cam turns in that period; data books quote it in distributor (cam) degrees or crank degrees. On a four-stroke engine the cam turns at half engine speed, so one cam degree is two crank degrees. A four-stroke engine of z cylinders sparks once every 720°/z of crank, and the time between sparks is:

Ts = 120 ÷ (N × z)

Ts is the spark interval in seconds, N the engine speed in rev/min and z the number of cylinders. If the points are closed for a fraction D of each interval, the dwell angle is θd = D × 720°/z crank degrees and the dwell time is td = D × Ts.

The coil current does not jump to its full value. The primary has inductance L and resistance R, so the current rises along a curve:

i = (V/R) × (1 − e−t/τ), τ = L/R

i is the primary current in amperes after the points have been closed for t seconds, V the supply voltage, R the total primary resistance in ohms, L the primary inductance in henries and τ the time constant in seconds. The energy stored at the break is E = ½ L i².

With points the dwell angle is fixed by the cam, so at high speed the dwell time shrinks and the spark weakens (worked example 2).

A capacitor of about 0.2–0.3 µF, often called the condenser, sits across the points. Without it the current would arc across the opening points, burning them and slowing the collapse. The capacitor takes the current for the first instant, so the points open cleanly, the current falls fast, and the secondary voltage is high.

Advance curves: centrifugal and vacuum

The flame takes roughly the same time to burn, while the piston moves faster as speed rises, so the spark must come earlier at high speed. A thin, diluted part-load charge burns slowly and also needs more advance; at full load the dense charge burns faster, and too much advance causes knock.

Centrifugal advance uses two weights on a plate driven by the distributor shaft. As speed rises the weights swing out against springs and turn the cam ahead of the shaft, so the points open earlier. Vacuum advance uses a diaphragm fed from a small port just above the closed throttle plate. At idle the port sees almost no vacuum; at part-throttle cruise it sees high vacuum and the diaphragm turns the breaker plate against the cam rotation, adding advance. At full throttle the vacuum is low and a spring removes that advance.

Base (static) timing is set at idle with the vacuum pipe disconnected and plugged, using a timing light on the pulley marks. Total advance = base + centrifugal + vacuum. The ECU of electronic and programmed ignition replaces both with a stored map.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder, four-stroke engine runs at 3000 rpm. The contact-breaker dwell is 60% of the spark interval. Find the spark frequency, the spark interval, the dwell angle in crank and cam degrees, and the dwell time.

  1. Engine speed = 3000 rpm = 3000/60 = 50 rev/s.
  2. A four-stroke cycle is 720° of crank and four cylinders fire once each per cycle, so one spark every 720°/4 = 180° of crank, two sparks per revolution.
  3. Spark frequency f = 50 × 2 = 100 sparks/s.
  4. Spark interval Ts = 120 ÷ (3000 × 4) = 0.010 s = 10 ms (the same as 1/f).
  5. Dwell angle = 0.60 × 180° = 108° of crank. The cam turns at half speed, so this is 108/2 = 54° of cam.
  6. Dwell time td = 0.60 × 10 ms = 6 ms.

Answer. Spark frequency 100 sparks/s; spark interval 10 ms; dwell angle 108° of crank (54° of cam); dwell time 6 ms.

Worked example 2

Question. The same engine's coil has primary inductance L = 8 mH, and the coil with its ballast resistor has R = 3.0 Ω on a 12 V supply. Find the current at the break and the energy stored at 3000 rpm (dwell time 6 ms) and at 6000 rpm, where the same 60% dwell lasts only 3 ms.

  1. Final current V/R = 12 ÷ 3.0 = 4.0 A. Time constant τ = L/R = 0.008 ÷ 3.0 = 2.67 ms.
  2. At 3000 rpm: t/τ = 6 ÷ 2.67 = 2.25; e−2.25 = 0.105; i = 4.0 × (1 − 0.105) = 3.58 A.
  3. Energy E = ½ L i² = 0.5 × 0.008 × 3.58² = 0.0512 J = 51.2 mJ.
  4. At 6000 rpm: t/τ = 3 ÷ 2.67 = 1.125; e−1.125 = 0.325; i = 4.0 × (1 − 0.325) = 2.70 A.
  5. Energy E = 0.5 × 0.008 × 2.70² = 0.0292 J = 29.2 mJ.
  6. Ratio 29.2 ÷ 51.2 = 0.57: doubling the speed leaves only 57% of the spark energy, because the fixed dwell angle now lasts half the time.

Answer. 3.58 A and 51.2 mJ at 3000 rpm; 2.70 A and 29.2 mJ at 6000 rpm, a 43% loss of spark energy.

Exam traps

  • Using rpm/60 as the spark frequency and forgetting the ×2 for a four-cylinder four-stroke engine.
  • Taking 60% of 360° instead of 60% of the angle between two sparks (180° of crank here), or mixing cam degrees with crank degrees.
  • Saying the capacitor adds spark energy. It stops the points arcing, which makes the collapse fast.
  • Taking vacuum advance from the manifold below the throttle. The port sits just above the throttle plate, so there is almost no vacuum advance at idle.

2.2 Spark plugs and spark pulse generation

A spark plug carries the coil's high voltage into the combustion chamber, seals the chamber, and fires a spark across a small gap shortly before top dead centre on the compression stroke. The voltage needed to break the gap is not fixed: it rises with gap size and cylinder pressure, and falls when the electrodes are hot. The coil must offer more voltage than the plug needs, with enough stored energy to keep the spark alive. If it cannot, the engine misfires, first under full load. This chapter covers plug construction, heat range, the firing voltage and the path the high-voltage pulse takes.

You need: the coil, dwell and stored energy from ignition fundamentals.

Construction and heat range

The plug is a steel shell around a ceramic insulator. A terminal at the top takes the high-tension cable. The insulator is alumina ceramic; it holds the centre electrode and carries heat from the tip to the shell. The centre electrode tip faces one or more earth electrodes welded to the shell. The shell screws into the cylinder head and gives the earth return. In a resistor plug a resistor of a few kilohms, set in a conductive glass seal below the terminal, cuts radio-frequency noise; the glass seal also stops combustion gas leaking up the centre. A copper core in the centre electrode carries heat away, and platinum or iridium tips resist erosion.

Heat range is the plug's ability to shed heat from its tip to the shell. It is set mainly by the length of the insulator nose. A hot plug has a long nose: heat has a long path, so the tip runs hotter and burns off carbon and oil. It suits a lightly loaded engine whose plug would otherwise stay below the self-cleaning temperature, about 450 °C. A cold plug has a short nose: the tip runs cooler, so it does not glow and cause pre-ignition in a hot, high-power engine. Makers number heat ranges in opposite directions, so always fit the plug the engine maker lists.

The gap is the shortest distance between the centre and earth electrodes, set to about 0.7–1.1 mm. Too small a gap gives a short, weak spark that may not light the mixture; too large a gap raises the firing voltage beyond what the coil can give. Carbon or oil fouling builds a conducting layer on the insulator nose. That layer leaks the coil's energy to earth before the voltage can rise high enough to break the gap.

Firing voltage and the high-voltage circuit

The gas in the gap is an insulator until free electrons gain enough energy to ionise it. The voltage at which this avalanche starts is the firing voltage Vf. It rises when the gap is wider, because the field is weaker for the same voltage. It rises with cylinder pressure, because denser gas means the electrons collide before they gain enough energy. It falls when the electrodes are hot, because a hot surface releases electrons more easily, and it is higher with worn, rounded electrodes. Typical values are 10–20 kV at part load and up to 30–35 kV at full load on a high-compression or turbocharged engine.

For one high-compression engine the required voltage follows roughly straight lines (Figure 2.2.1):

Vf ≈ 10 + 22 g (full load)

Vf is the firing voltage in kV and g the gap in mm. At light load the same engine needs only Vf ≈ 5 + 12 g. These lines are for this example engine; every engine has its own.

The coil's available voltage is what it can deliver into an open circuit. At the break the primary emf rises to a few hundred volts (not 12 V), and the secondary gives roughly V₂ = n V₁, where n = N₂/N₁ is the turns ratio and V₁ the primary emf. A 1:100 coil with V₁ = 250 V gives 25 kV. The difference between available and required voltage is the voltage reserve; the engine runs only while the reserve is positive.

The high-voltage path is coil secondary → king lead → distributor cap centre → rotor → rotor gap → cap segment → plug lead → terminal → centre electrode → gap → earth electrode → shell → cylinder head → back to the coil through the engine earth. Each part adds loss: the rotor gap, lead resistance, a suppressor, and dirt or moisture tracking on the cap. The plug therefore sees less than the coil's ideal V₂.

The spark pulse: firing line, spark line, ringing

An oscilloscope on the secondary shows three stages after each break, and a service technician reads them. First the voltage shoots up the firing line until the gap breaks down, in a few microseconds. Then it drops to a low, roughly flat spark line of 1–2 kV while the arc burns for about 1–2 ms, using the stored energy. When the energy can no longer hold the arc, the spark goes out and the coil rings down in a few damped oscillations. A high firing line means a wide gap or an open lead; a short spark line means too little energy or a lean mixture.

E = ½ L I²

E is the energy stored in the coil in joules (J), L the primary inductance in henries (H) and I the primary current at the break in amperes (A).

Too little energy gives a short spark that cannot light a lean or turbulent mixture; too much erodes the electrodes. A fouled or worn plug fires at idle and fails at full load: the cylinder pressure, and so Vf, rises with load, and the reserve runs out. The ECU of electronic and programmed ignition sets the dwell so the coil reaches its current at every speed.

Worked example

Question. An electronic-ignition coil has primary inductance 8 mH and the primary current reaches 6 A at the break. Its turns ratio is 1:100 and the primary emf at the break is 250 V. (a) Find the energy stored. (b) Find the ideal secondary voltage. (c) Using the full-load line Vf = 10 + 22 g, decide whether it fires a 0.9 mm gap at full load. (d) Find the largest gap it can fire at full load.

  1. L = 8 mH = 0.008 H and I = 6 A.
  2. E = ½ L I² = 0.5 × 0.008 × 6² = 0.144 J = 144 mJ.
  3. V₂ = n V₁ = 100 × 250 V = 25 000 V = 25 kV.
  4. Required at 0.9 mm: Vf = 10 + 22 × 0.9 = 10 + 19.8 = 29.8 kV.
  5. Reserve = 25 − 29.8 = −4.8 kV. The reserve is negative, so this plug misfires at full load (at light load it needs only 5 + 12 × 0.9 = 15.8 kV and fires).
  6. Largest gap: 25 = 10 + 22 g, so g = 15 ÷ 22 = 0.68 mm.

Answer. (a) 144 mJ. (b) 25 kV. (c) The 0.9 mm gap needs 29.8 kV, 4.8 kV more than the coil gives, so it misfires at full load. (d) A gap of 0.68 mm or less fires.

Exam traps

  • Multiplying the turns ratio by 12 V. The secondary voltage is n times the primary emf at the break, a few hundred volts.
  • Saying hot electrodes need more voltage. A hot surface frees electrons more easily, so the firing voltage falls; pressure and gap raise it.
  • A hot plug is not a plug for a hot engine. A hot plug suits a cool-running, lightly loaded engine; a cold plug suits a hot, high-power engine.
  • Mixing available voltage (what the coil can give) with required voltage (what the plug needs). The engine runs on the difference, the reserve.
  • Resistor plugs and suppressors cut radio noise; they add resistance in the secondary, not spark energy.

2.3 Electronic and programmed ignition systems

An electronic ignition system replaces the contact breaker with a sensor and a transistor. The transistor switches the coil's primary current, and an ignition module or the engine ECU decides when to start that current and when to stop it. Programmed ignition goes further: it stores the spark advance in a map indexed by engine speed and load (manifold pressure or air flow), then corrects the mapped value for coolant temperature, knock and starting. You meet it on almost every petrol car and motorcycle sold today.

You need: dwell, the coil current curve and advance from ignition fundamentals.

From points to a transistor switch

The contact breaker has three problems as engines get faster. The points burn, because they break a few amperes many times a second. The dwell angle drifts as the rubbing block wears, so the coil current changes. And at high speed the fixed dwell angle lasts too short a time to build full current, and the points may bounce.

Electronic ignition removes the rubbing block. An inductive (magnetic reluctance) or Hall-effect pick-up in the distributor or at the crankshaft sends a pulse each time a trigger-wheel tooth passes. The pulse tells the module the crank angle. The module drives a power transistor, often called the igniter, in series with the coil primary. The pick-up only reports position; a small signal from it controls the large coil current.

Early modules kept the points' behaviour. In a constant-dwell system the dwell angle is fixed, as with points, so the dwell time shrinks as speed rises. A constant-energy system uses a low-inductance, low-resistance coil that reaches its current quickly. It measures the coil current and switches the transistor on just early enough to reach a set current, about 7–8 A, before each spark; a current-limiting circuit then holds that current until the break. So the dwell time stays nearly constant and the dwell angle grows with speed. It also lengthens the dwell when the battery voltage is low. At very high speed the dwell angle reaches its limit and the energy finally falls.

td = D × Ts

td is the dwell time in seconds, D the dwell as a fraction of the spark interval and Ts = 120 ÷ (N × z) the spark interval in seconds, with N the speed in rev/min and z the number of cylinders of a four-stroke engine.

Programmed ignition: the advance map

Programmed ignition, also called electronic spark advance or digital ignition, stores the spark timing in a table in the ECU memory. One axis is engine speed, the other is load (manifold pressure or air flow per stroke). Each cell holds an advance in degrees before TDC (BTDC); a typical map has 16 speeds × 16 loads, and between cells the ECU interpolates. Drawn in three dimensions the table looks like a rough landscape, the ignition map. Unlike centrifugal weights and a vacuum diaphragm, the map can take any shape the calibrator finds best on the test bed.

The ECU then corrects the mapped value. Coolant temperature: extra advance on a cold engine. Knock: the ECU retards the spark in small steps when the knock sensor reports detonation, then advances it back slowly. Starting: a fixed safe advance during cranking. It also sets the dwell from battery voltage and speed, using a second, smaller map.

The chain has these blocks: a crank sensor (speed and crank angle) with a reference mark for TDC; a manifold pressure or air-flow sensor (load); coolant temperature and knock sensors; the ECU with its maps; the igniter; the coil; and, on older systems, a distributor sending the high voltage to the plugs in the firing order. Later systems drop the distributor (see distributorless and direct ignition).

The ECU sends a dwell pulse to the igniter. The rising edge starts the primary current; the falling edge stops it and fires the spark. The time between the two edges is the dwell time, and the angle between the falling edge and TDC is the spark advance. Because both are computed from crank-sensor teeth, the timing stays correct for the life of the engine and needs no adjustment.

Control methods at block level

Four control methods appear in order of increasing freedom. Mechanical control uses centrifugal and vacuum advance and cannot correct for temperature. Electronic control replaces the points with a transistor but may keep a mechanical advance curve. Mapped control reads a table, so the advance can follow any shape. Closed-loop knock control adds feedback: it retards when knock appears and advances back until knock just reappears, so the engine runs close to its knock limit without damage.

A service technician sees the result as a scan-tool data stream: rpm, manifold pressure, coolant temperature, calculated advance, knock retard and dwell. A failed crank sensor usually stops the spark altogether; a failed knock sensor usually makes the ECU fall back to a safe, retarded map.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder four-stroke petrol engine runs at 5000 rpm on a constant-dwell system with dwell of 60% of the spark interval. The knock sensor requests 4° of retard. Find the spark rate, the spark interval, the dwell time and the time taken by 4° of crank rotation.

  1. Spark rate f = (5000/60) × (4/2) = 83.33 × 2 = 166.7 sparks/s.
  2. Spark interval Ts = 120 ÷ (5000 × 4) = 0.006 s = 6 ms.
  3. Dwell time td = 0.60 × 6 ms = 3.6 ms.
  4. One revolution takes 60/5000 s = 12 ms for 360°, so 4° takes 4 × 12/360 = 0.133 ms.

Answer. 166.7 sparks/s; spark interval 6 ms; dwell time 3.6 ms; the 4° retard moves the spark 0.133 ms later.

Worked example 2

Question. A constant-energy system's coil needs 3.0 ms of current to reach its limited current of 7.5 A. For a four-cylinder four-stroke engine find the dwell as a fraction of the spark interval and as a crank angle at 1000 rpm and at 6000 rpm.

  1. Spark interval at 1000 rpm: Ts = 120 ÷ (1000 × 4) = 0.030 s = 30 ms.
  2. Fraction = 3.0 ÷ 30 = 0.10; the angle between sparks is 720°/4 = 180°, so the dwell angle = 0.10 × 180° = 18° of crank.
  3. Spark interval at 6000 rpm: Ts = 120 ÷ (6000 × 4) = 0.005 s = 5 ms.
  4. Fraction = 3.0 ÷ 5 = 0.60; dwell angle = 0.60 × 180° = 108° of crank.
  5. Check: 18° at 1000 rpm lasts 18/360 × 60 ms = 3.0 ms, and 108° at 6000 rpm lasts 108/360 × 10 ms = 3.0 ms.

Answer. At 1000 rpm the dwell is 10% of the interval, 18° of crank; at 6000 rpm it is 60%, 108° of crank. The dwell time stays 3.0 ms, so every spark gets the same 7.5 A.

Exam traps

  • Swapping the two systems. Constant dwell keeps the dwell angle (as points did), so dwell time falls with speed; constant energy keeps the coil current, so the dwell angle rises with speed.
  • Mixing spark advance with dwell. Advance is when the spark occurs relative to TDC; dwell is the charging period before it.
  • Forgetting the z/2 factor for a four-stroke engine. N/60 alone gives one spark per revolution.
  • Reading the map's load axis as accelerator pedal position. Advance maps use manifold pressure or air flow.
  • Thinking knock retard is permanent. The ECU retards only while knock is present, then creeps back toward the mapped value.

2.4 Distributorless and direct ignition systems

Distributorless ignition removes the rotating distributor and gives each coil its own cylinders. In the waste-spark form one coil serves a pair of cylinders and fires both plugs together: one plug is near the end of compression and lights the charge, the other is on its exhaust stroke and the spark is wasted. Direct ignition goes further: one coil per cylinder, on or near the plug, fired only on compression. The ECU times every spark from crank and cam sensors. Only a few universities teach this as a separate topic; it extends electronic and programmed ignition.

Waste-spark distributorless ignition

Waste spark uses one double-ended coil for two cylinders whose pistons rise and fall together but are 360° apart in the cycle. In a four-cylinder engine with firing order 1-3-4-2 the pairs are 1 & 4 and 2 & 3. The two ends of the secondary go to the two plugs, and the current runs from one plug to earth, through the block, and back up through the other plug, so the two sparks are in series and of opposite polarity.

When cylinder 1 is near the end of compression, cylinder 4 is near the end of exhaust. The compressed gas in cylinder 1 needs most of the voltage; the hot, low-pressure exhaust gas in cylinder 4 breaks down at only a few kilovolts. One revolution later the roles swap. So each coil fires once every crank revolution, twice in the 720° cycle, and the ECU needs only the crank sensor: a spark on every revolution always lands on the right stroke of one of the two cylinders.

Waste spark needs no cap, rotor or long leads, so it is cheap and common on small engines. Its costs are a plug that sparks twice as often, so it wears faster, and timing that can be trimmed only pair by pair.

Direct ignition: one coil per cylinder

Direct ignition gives every cylinder its own coil, mounted on the plug (coil-on-plug) or close to it with a short lead (coil-near-plug). The ECU fires each coil only on its compression stroke. A crank sensor reading a trigger wheel gives speed and angle; a missing tooth marks a reference position. But one crank revolution looks exactly like the next, so the crank alone cannot tell compression from exhaust. A cam sensor, turning at half crank speed, gives one pulse per 720° and tells the ECU which revolution it is in. That is why direct ignition needs a cam sensor and waste spark does not.

The high-voltage path is a few centimetres long, so lead losses and radio interference fall. Each cylinder's timing can be trimmed by its own knock signal, and a coil has a whole cycle to recharge, so dwell is never short. Many coils are "smart coils" with the igniter built in, triggered by a low-voltage signal from the ECU.

Service and fault finding

There is no rotor to burn, cap to track, or distributor shaft play to upset timing, and the timing cannot be adjusted by hand. A misfire on one cylinder of a direct system points to that cylinder's coil, plug or boot; a misfire on both cylinders of a pair on a waste-spark engine points to the shared coil. The ECU watches crank speed for the small slow-down after a dead cylinder, and some ECUs check the coil's primary current, to name the cylinder in a fault code. Keep the plug gap correct: a wide gap raises the voltage demand and can cause flashover inside a worn coil boot.

Worked example

Question. A four-cylinder engine with firing order 1-3-4-2 runs at 4000 rpm on waste spark, with coils paired 1 & 4 and 2 & 3. Find the length of one cycle, the firings per second of each coil, the useful sparks per second, and the crank angle of each firing with the cylinder on compression.

  1. Speed = 4000 ÷ 60 = 66.67 rev/s. One 720° cycle is 2 revolutions, so it lasts 2 ÷ 66.67 = 0.030 s = 30 ms.
  2. Each coil fires once per revolution, so 66.67 firings per second per coil, 133.3 for the two coils together.
  3. Each firing gives one useful spark (compression) and one wasted spark (exhaust), so the engine gets 133.3 useful sparks per second, and every plug sparks 66.67 times per second.
  4. The cylinders fire 180° apart in the order 1-3-4-2: cylinder 1 at 0°, 3 at 180°, 4 at 360°, 2 at 540°.
  5. Coil 1 & 4 fires at 0° (1 on compression, 4 on exhaust) and 360° (4 on compression, 1 on exhaust); coil 2 & 3 fires at 180° (3 compression) and 540° (2 compression). The firings are 180° × 30 ms/720° = 7.5 ms apart.

Answer. Cycle 30 ms; 66.7 firings per second per coil; 133.3 useful sparks per second; firings every 7.5 ms at 0° (cylinder 1), 180° (3), 360° (4) and 540° (2).

Exam traps

  • Saying a waste-spark coil fires once per cycle. It fires once every revolution, twice per 720°, and each firing sparks both of its plugs.
  • Thinking direct ignition can run on the crank sensor alone. It needs the cam sensor to tell compression from exhaust; waste spark does not.
  • Pairing cylinders by neighbouring numbers. Pair the cylinders whose pistons move together and are 360° apart in the firing order: 1 & 4, 2 & 3.
  • Forgetting that the two waste-spark plugs are in series and of opposite polarity; the exhaust-stroke plug takes only a few kilovolts.
  • Calling coil-near-plug the same as coil-on-plug; coil-near-plug keeps a short high-voltage lead.

2.5 Lighting technology, light sources and lamps

A lamp turns electrical power into light, and its efficacy is the light out per watt in, in lumens per watt (lm/W). Filament, halogen, gas-discharge and LED sources differ in that number, in colour, in how fast they start and in how long they last. A headlamp also has to meet strict technical demands: light far down the road without dazzling anyone. This chapter covers the photometric units, how each source makes light, how lighting developed, and the lamp units built round the sources. Only a few universities teach this topic.

Photometric units and efficacy

Light is measured in four linked units. Luminous flux Φ is the total light output, in lumens (lm). Luminous intensity I is flux per unit solid angle in one direction, in candela (cd = lm/sr). Illuminance E is the flux falling on a surface, in lux (lx = lm/m²). Luminance is intensity per unit projected area of a source or surface, in cd/m²; it is what the eye sees as brightness. Efficacy is η = Φ/P, with P the electrical power in W.

On the road, illuminance falls with the square of distance from the lamp:

E = I ÷ d²

E is the illuminance in lux on a surface facing the lamp, I the intensity of the beam in that direction in candela, and d the distance in metres.

Spreading the same flux over a wider beam lowers the intensity in each direction, so a lamp with more lumens does not always light the road better. Lamp makers quote a bulb's power and flux at its test voltage, 13.2 V for a 12 V lamp.

Light sources and their physics

Incandescence: current heats a tungsten filament until it glows, about 2700 K in a vacuum bulb, giving 10–15 lm/W. Most of the power leaves as infrared heat. A halogen lamp adds iodine or bromine to the fill gas. Evaporated tungsten combines with the halogen near the hot quartz glass and is carried back to the filament, the halogen cycle, so the bulb stays clear and the filament can run hotter, about 3000–3200 K, at about 20–30 lm/W.

Gas discharge: a high-voltage pulse, about 20 kV from the ballast, strikes an arc through xenon gas between two electrodes; metal salts then vaporise and give most of the light. A 35 W HID (xenon) lamp gives about 3000 lm, 80–90 lm/W, bluish-white, but needs the ballast and a few seconds to reach full output. Electroluminescence: in an LED, electrons and holes recombine at a semiconductor junction and release photons; a blue chip with a phosphor coat gives white light. LEDs give about 90–110 lm/W at the lamp, start instantly, last the life of the car and need a constant-current driver and a heat sink. A laser headlamp aims blue laser diodes at a phosphor to make a very small, very bright spot, used to extend the high beam.

Development, technical demands and lamp units

Car lamps went from acetylene and oil lamps to vacuum filament bulbs, then to two-filament bulbs giving main and dipped beam from one lamp. Halogen bulbs arrived in the 1960s, xenon HID in the early 1990s, full-LED headlamps in the late 2000s, and laser high beams in the mid-2010s. Each step gave more light for less power.

The technical demands on a headlamp are: enough light far down the road; a dipped beam with a sharp cut-off so no light rises into oncoming eyes; fixed colours; resistance to vibration, heat and water; long life; and type approval of the lamp and bulb together. The dipped beam is asymmetric. In India, where traffic keeps left, the cut-off rises on the left to light the kerb and road signs, and stays low on the right, towards oncoming traffic; in right-hand-traffic countries it is mirrored.

A source is shaped into a beam by a lamp unit. A reflector lamp uses a shaped mirror, with a clear or fluted lens; a free-form reflector divides the mirror into facets that each place light in one part of the pattern. A projector lamp uses an ellipsoidal reflector, a shield and a lens: the shield cuts off the upper light at the focus, and the lens images its edge onto the road as the sharp cut-off line.

Worked example

Question. A halogen headlamp uses a 55 W bulb giving 1500 lm. An LED headlamp gives 2000 lm for 20 W. Both are rated at the 13.2 V test voltage. Find the efficacy of each and the current each draws.

  1. Halogen efficacy = 1500 lm ÷ 55 W = 27.3 lm/W.
  2. LED efficacy = 2000 lm ÷ 20 W = 100 lm/W.
  3. Halogen current = 55 W ÷ 13.2 V = 4.17 A.
  4. LED current = 20 W ÷ 13.2 V = 1.52 A.
  5. The LED gives 2000 − 1500 = 500 lm (33%) more flux for 35 W and 2.65 A less.

Answer. Halogen 27.3 lm/W at 4.17 A; LED 100 lm/W at 1.52 A: 33% more light for 2.65 A less current.

Worked example 2

Question. The brightest point of a dipped beam has an intensity of 20 000 cd. Find the illuminance on a sign facing the car at 25 m and at 50 m.

  1. At 25 m: E = I ÷ d² = 20 000 ÷ 25² = 20 000 ÷ 625 = 32 lx.
  2. At 50 m: E = 20 000 ÷ 50² = 20 000 ÷ 2500 = 8 lx.
  3. Doubling the distance cuts the illuminance to a quarter: 32 ÷ 4 = 8 lx.

Answer. 32 lx at 25 m and 8 lx at 50 m.

Exam traps

  • Lumen, candela and lux are not interchangeable: lumen is total output, candela is output in one direction, lux is light falling on a surface.
  • Forgetting the square in E = I ÷ d²: at twice the distance the illuminance is a quarter, not a half.
  • HID xenon is not an LED: it is an arc lamp that needs a high-voltage ballast and a warm-up.
  • Halogen efficacy is 20–30 lm/W, not 80; about 80–90 lm/W is HID.
  • Reversing the asymmetric dip for India: the cut-off rises on the left (kerb side) and stays low on the right.

2.6 Front, rear, interior and special-purpose lighting

A car must light the road for its driver and show its size and intentions to everyone else. Its lamps fall into four groups: front, rear, interior and special purpose. Each lamp has a colour rule, a switch and a legal limit, and every one sits in the same kind of circuit: battery, fuse, switch or relay, lamp, earth. Only a few universities teach this grouping in depth, but the circuit sums are standard exam questions.

You need: lamp powers and the 13.2 V rating from lighting technology.

Front lighting

Headlamps give a main (high) beam for empty roads and a dipped (low) beam for traffic. In India the dipped beam's asymmetric cut-off rises on the left, lighting the kerb while keeping light out of oncoming drivers' eyes; the main beam has no cut-off. Aim is set with screws or a levelling motor (see adaptive lighting) and checked against a screen at a test centre.

Position lamps (side lamps) mark the car's width at night. Daytime running lamps (DRLs) are white lamps that come on with the engine and make the car easier to see by day. Front fog lamps are white or selective yellow, mounted low with a wide, flat beam under the fog. Front direction indicators are amber. A headlamp relay carries the lamp current so the stalk switch carries only the relay coil's small current, and many cars fuse the left and right headlamps separately so one blown fuse never leaves the car dark.

Rear and interior lighting

Tail lamps are red and come on with the position lamps, typically 5 W each. Stop lamps are a brighter red, typically 21 W each plus a high-mounted centre stop lamp, and are switched by the brake pedal switch. Reversing lamps are white and switched by the gearbox in reverse. Rear direction indicators are amber and flash at 60–120 flashes a minute, about 90 typically. The rear fog lamp is red, about as bright as a stop lamp, and used only in poor visibility. The number-plate lamp is white so the plate can be read.

Interior lamps, the dome, map, vanity, boot and glovebox lamps, are convenience loads. A door switch usually switches the earth side: opening the door earths the lamp and completes the circuit. A body module may fade the lamp out by pulse-width modulation (PWM), switching it on and off so fast that the eye sees only the average. Instrument and switch lamps are dimmed the same way. A failed earth can make lamps on the same earth glow dimly through each other's filaments.

Special-purpose lamps and the lamp circuit

Special-purpose lamps warn or work. Hazard warning lamps flash all the indicators together; the hazard switch has a permanent battery feed so it works with the ignition off. Ambulances, police and breakdown vehicles carry flashing beacons with their own driver. Trucks and tractors carry white work lamps aimed at the job, and some cars have cornering lamps that light the side of the road when the indicator is on at low speed.

The lamp circuit is the same every time: battery → fuse → switch or relay → lamp → earth return (Figure 2.6.1). The fuse protects the wire, not the lamp, so it is sized to the wire and to the load current with a margin. The lamps are in parallel, so one failure leaves the others lit and the currents add. The indicators have their own path: ignition feed → hazard switch → flasher unit → indicator switch → left or right lamps; the flasher sets the rate, and an electronic flasher flashes faster if a lamp fails, warning the driver.

I = ΣP ÷ V

I is the total current in amperes, ΣP the sum of the lamp powers in watts at their rated voltage and V that voltage in volts. A filament lamp at a higher voltage draws more current, but less than in proportion, because its hot filament has a higher resistance.

Worked example

Question. Two 55 W headlamps, two 5 W tail lamps, two 5 W number-plate lamps and 3 W of instrument lamps are on together, all rated at 13.2 V and all on one fuse. Find the current of each group and the total, and choose a standard fuse (5, 7.5, 10, 15, 20 A) with at least 25% margin.

  1. Headlamps: 2 × 55 = 110 W, I = 110 ÷ 13.2 = 8.33 A.
  2. Tail lamps: 2 × 5 = 10 W, I = 10 ÷ 13.2 = 0.76 A. Number-plate lamps: also 10 W, 0.76 A.
  3. Instrument lamps: 3 ÷ 13.2 = 0.23 A.
  4. Total power 110 + 10 + 10 + 3 = 133 W; total current I = 133 ÷ 13.2 = 10.08 A (8.33 + 0.76 + 0.76 + 0.23 = 10.08 A).
  5. With 25% margin: 1.25 × 10.08 = 12.6 A. The next standard fuse above that is 15 A, and the wire must be rated for at least 15 A.

Answer. Total 10.08 A (headlamps 8.33 A, tail 0.76 A, plate 0.76 A, instruments 0.23 A); a 15 A fuse.

Exam traps

  • Treating a 12 V filament lamp as a constant-power load at other voltages. Its current rises with voltage, but less than in proportion; an estimate with the cold-rated resistance at 13.8 V errs on the high side, which is the safe side for a fuse.
  • Forgetting the stop lamps: two 21 W lamps add 42 W (3.2 A at 13.2 V) only while the brake pedal is pressed.
  • Swapping main and dipped beam: only the dipped beam has the asymmetric cut-off.
  • Breaking the colour rules: white or selective yellow at the front, red at the rear, amber only for indicators, white for reversing and number-plate lamps.
  • Treating the flasher unit as a fuse. It sets the flash rate, about 90 a minute; with an electronic flasher a faster flash means a lamp has failed.

2.7 Adaptive lighting systems

A fixed headlamp points where the car points. On a bend the beam goes straight on and the inside of the curve stays dark; with a loaded boot the nose lifts and the dipped beam climbs into oncoming eyes. An adaptive front lighting system (AFS) moves or reshapes the beam instead: a swivel motor turns the lamp with the steering, a levelling motor tilts it to hold the cut-off on the road, and the ECU picks a beam pattern to suit the road. Only a few universities teach this topic.

You need: the dipped-beam cut-off from front, rear, interior and special-purpose lighting.

Levelling, swivel and beam modes

Levelling is rotation about a horizontal axis (pitch). Passengers in the back and a full boot push the rear down and tilt the whole body nose-up; braking dive and acceleration squat do the same briefly. The level motor tilts the lamp down by the same angle so the cut-off stays where it was set. Headlamp aim is given as a slope: an aim of −1% means the cut-off falls 10 cm for every 10 m ahead. Lamps with a very bright dipped beam, such as xenon HID, must have automatic levelling and headlamp cleaning by law.

Swivel, or dynamic bending light, is rotation about a vertical axis (yaw). The lamp turns into the bend with the steering, typically up to about 15° each way, so the beam lights the road the car is about to use. Static cornering light is a separate lamp, or an extra LED segment, that switches on at low speed when the indicator is on or the steering is turned hard, lighting the side of a junction.

Many AFS lamps also change the beam pattern with speed: a wide, short town light; the normal country light; a longer, slightly raised motorway light at high speed; and an adverse-weather light that cuts light just ahead of the car, where wet roads glare back.

Inputs, ECU and motors

The AFS ECU reads the steering angle sensor (wheel angle and rate), the vehicle speed sent over the CAN bus by the ABS ECU from its wheel-speed sensors, front and rear ride-height sensors (the difference gives pitch) and, on newer cars, a forward camera that sees oncoming and leading vehicles.

It computes target swivel and pitch angles and commands two small motors per lamp. A stepper motor moves in fixed steps and the ECU counts them from a reference stop; a DC motor carries a position sensor, often a potentiometer, so the ECU can close the loop. The swivel command shrinks with speed: large at junction speed, small on the highway, and it saturates at its limit so the beam never sweeps across the centre line. On a fault the lamps park straight ahead and slightly low, so the driver keeps a legal dipped beam.

θ = tan⁻¹(Δh ÷ b)

θ is the pitch angle of the body, Δh the drop of the rear axle in metres with the front axle at its normal height, and b the wheelbase in metres. Every point of the body, including the lamp, tilts by the same θ.

A matrix headlamp builds the high beam from many LED segments. The camera finds other vehicles and the ECU switches off only the segments aimed at them, keeping high beam everywhere else. This function is called adaptive driving beam (ADB); matrix LED is one way to build it.

Worked example

Question. A car with a wheelbase of 2.5 m has its headlamps aimed at −1.0%. Loading the boot lowers the rear axle by 20 mm while the front stays put. (a) Find the pitch angle. (b) Find the new aim and how far the cut-off rises at 25 m. (c) The swivel command is 0.15 × steering-wheel angle, capped at 15°. Find the swivel for steering-wheel angles of 90° and 120°.

  1. Δh = 20 mm = 0.020 m, b = 2.5 m, so tan θ = 0.020 ÷ 2.5 = 0.008 and θ = 0.46°.
  2. The nose tilts up by 0.46°, which is a slope of 0.008 = 0.8%. The aim goes from −1.0% to −1.0 + 0.8 = −0.2%: the beam now falls only 2 cm per 10 m.
  3. At 25 m the cut-off rises by 25 × 0.008 = 0.20 m. The level motor must tilt the lamp down by 0.46° to bring the aim back to −1.0%.
  4. At 90°: 0.15 × 90° = 13.5°, below the cap, so the lamp turns 13.5°.
  5. At 120°: 0.15 × 120° = 18° is above the cap, so the command saturates at 15°.

Answer. (a) 0.46°. (b) The aim becomes −0.2% and the cut-off rises 0.20 m at 25 m, so the level motor tilts the lamp 0.46° down. (c) 13.5° at 90°; 15° (capped) at 120°.

Exam traps

  • Levelling corrects pitch (up and down) for load; swivel corrects yaw (left and right) for steering. A car can have one without the other.
  • Using the distance from the rear axle to the lamp in the pitch angle. The body turns about the front axle, so use the wheelbase; the lamp's position does not change the angle.
  • Getting the direction wrong: a sinking rear tilts the beam up, so the motor tilts the lamp down.
  • Check which angle a swivel gain is defined on. If it is given per degree of road-wheel angle, divide the steering-wheel angle by the steering ratio first.
  • Ignoring the cap: beyond about 15° more steering gives no more swivel.

2.8 Instrument clusters, dashboard and head-up displays

The instrument cluster is the small computer in front of the driver. It takes frames from other ECUs on the CAN bus and signals from a few sensors wired to it directly, and turns them into needle angles, digits, warning lamps and, on some cars, an image that seems to float beyond the windscreen. It exists so the driver can read speed, engine speed, fuel, temperature and faults in one glance without looking away from the road for long.

The cluster and its gauges

Behind the dashboard sits a microcontroller with memory, a CAN transceiver and display drivers. It reads frames from the engine ECU, the ABS ECU and the body controller, and a few sensors directly. It drives stepper motors for the needles, an LCD or TFT panel, LED warning lamps (telltales) and the backlight. The main gauges are the speedometer (km/h), the tachometer (rpm, shown in thousands), the fuel gauge (marked E to F) and the coolant temperature gauge (marked C to H, with no unit). The odometer is a stored count of distance in non-volatile memory.

The fuel sender is a float on an arm that moves a wiper over a resistor track in the tank, so the resistance changes with the fuel level. The coolant sender is an NTC thermistor, whose resistance falls as the coolant heats. Older clusters fed these senders through a thermal gauge: a heater wound on a bimetal strip bends the strip and moves the needle in proportion to the current. A voltage stabiliser fed the gauges a steady voltage below the battery's, so the reading did not change with charging voltage, and the bimetal's slow response stopped the needle swinging as fuel sloshed. A modern cluster reads the sender's voltage with its analogue-to-digital converter and damps the reading in software instead.

Telltales use fixed colours. Red means stop: low oil pressure, brake fault, charging fault. Amber means check soon: engine management, ABS, low fuel. Green and blue mean a function is on: indicators, main beam. At key-on the cluster lights every telltale and sweeps each needle to full scale and back as a self-test.

Speed from pulses

The speedometer does not use engine rpm. A sensor at a wheel or at the gearbox output sends one pulse for every tooth or magnet that passes it. If the sensor sees N pulses per wheel revolution and the tyre rolls C metres per revolution, each pulse is d = C ÷ N metres. A gearbox-output sensor turns i times per wheel revolution, where i is the final-drive ratio, so then d = C ÷ (N × i). Clusters store the pulses per kilometre, K = 1000 ÷ d.

v = 3.6 × n × d ÷ T

v is the displayed speed in km/h, n the whole number of pulses counted in a gate time of T seconds, d the distance per pulse in metres, and 3.6 converts m/s to km/h.

Counting whole pulses means one pulse is the smallest step: 3.6 × d ÷ T km/h. A short gate reacts quickly but is coarse; a long gate is fine but lags. So clusters average several gates or time the period between pulses at low speed. Tyre wear, pressure and a change of tyre size change C. A smaller tyre gives more pulses per kilometre than the stored K, so the speedometer reads high; changing the final-drive ratio alters the reading only when the sensor is on the gearbox. The same wheel pulses feed the ABS, so one failed wheel sensor can light the ABS lamp and upset the speed together.

Needles, displays and the head-up display

A modern needle is driven by a small stepper motor through a reduction gear. The driver chip sends steps, not a voltage, so any angle can be repeated exactly. The motor has no return spring: at key-off the cluster steps every needle back to its zero stop, and at key-on it may drive against the stop to find zero. Older clusters used an air-cored (crossed-coil) gauge: two coils at right angles pulled a magnet, and the needle angle depended on the ratio of the two currents.

A segmented LCD shows fixed digits cheaply; a colour TFT shows menus, a camera image or a map. The backlight is a string of white LEDs dimmed by PWM, following the headlamp switch, a light sensor or the driver's dimmer.

A head-up display (HUD) projects from a unit in the dashboard onto a small combiner glass or onto a wedge-shaped layer in the windscreen. The driver sees a virtual image that seems to float about 2–2.5 m ahead. At that distance the eye needs much less refocusing from the road than for a gauge 0.7 m away, so a glance costs less time. The HUD shows short items, speed, the limit, a navigation arrow and driver-assistance warnings, and its brightness follows daylight so it never dazzles at night.

Worked example

Question. A wheel speed sensor gives N = 4 pulses per wheel revolution and the tyre rolling circumference is C = 1.90 m. The cluster counts whole pulses in a gate of T = 0.5 s. 2.9 Wipers, keyless entry and immobilisers

Wipers clear the screen with a small permanent-magnet motor that has two speeds and a park switch; a washer pump squirts fluid and the horn warns other road users. Keyless entry is a short radio conversation between a fob and the car, and the immobiliser is a second, stricter conversation that decides whether the engine may run at all. These are the body electrics a driver uses every day.

The wiper motor, its brushes and the park switch

A windscreen wiper motor is a permanent-magnet DC motor: two ferrite magnets give the field, and current reaches the armature through carbon brushes on a commutator. A worm gearbox cuts the motor's few thousand rev/min to roughly 40–70 wipes a minute, and a crank and link rods turn the rotation into the back-and-forth sweep of the arms.

Most wiper motors have three brushes. The common brush and the low-speed brush sit opposite each other. The high-speed brush is set closer to the common brush, so fewer armature conductors lie in series between the two brushes in use. Those fewer conductors generate less back emf at a given speed, so more current flows and the motor speeds up until the back emf balances the supply again. The supply stays 12 V; the speed changes only because a different brush is used.

The park switch is a cam-driven contact in the gearbox, not the stalk switch. When the driver switches off mid-sweep, the motor keeps running through the park contact, which the cam holds closed over most of the turn. At the park position the cam opens that contact and closes another that shorts the armature: the spinning motor acts as a generator into a short circuit, brakes hard, and the blades stop at the bottom of the screen.

Intermittent wipe, washers and horns

The intermittent relay runs the motor for one wipe, waits, then repeats. In the simplest timer a capacitor charges through a resistor, and the relay fires when the capacitor voltage reaches about 63% of the supply. The delay is then one time constant:

T = R × C

T is the delay in seconds, R the timing resistance in ohms and C the capacitance in farads. A potentiometer on the stalk changes R so the driver chooses the delay; a body ECU does the same job with a software timer.

The washer system has a plastic reservoir, a small permanent-magnet motor driving a centrifugal pump that draws a few amperes, tubes with a non-return valve so the jets prime at once, and jets or fan nozzles aimed at the screen. A wipe-after-wash timer runs the blades two or three more strokes after the pump stops. Headlamp washers use a second, stronger pump. A rain sensor shines infrared light into the screen; dry glass reflects it back to a photodiode by total internal reflection, and water on the outside lets light escape, so the photodiode current falls and the wipers start.

An electric horn is a vibrating-diaphragm buzzer. Current through a coil pulls an iron armature fixed to a steel diaphragm; the movement opens a pair of contacts in series with the coil, the pull stops, the diaphragm springs back, the contacts close, and the cycle repeats a few hundred times a second. A tone disc on the diaphragm or a spiral trumpet (windtone horn) shapes the sound; an adjusting screw sets the contact gap and so the current and tone, and a capacitor or resistor across the contacts cuts arcing. Cars usually fit a high-note and a low-note horn together, switched by a horn relay so the horn button carries only the relay coil current.

Remote entry, passive entry and the immobiliser

Remote keyless entry (RKE) is a one-way radio link. A press of the fob sends a frame at 315 MHz or 433 MHz holding the fob's identity, the command (lock, unlock, boot) and a rolling code. The receiver in the body control module checks it and, if it is valid, drives the central-locking motors. Fob and receiver share a secret key and a counter; each press advances the fob's counter and sends a keyed code made from it. The receiver accepts codes a little ahead of its own counter, so presses out of range are tolerated, and rejects any old code, so a recorded press cannot be replayed.

Passive keyless entry removes the button: aerials in the door handles send a low-frequency wake-up at about 125 kHz, the fob answers on UHF, and the door unlocks when the handle is touched; with the fob inside, the start button works.

The immobiliser is a separate check. The key holds a transponder, a chip and a coil with no battery. A coil round the lock barrel or start button powers it at 125 kHz, sends a challenge, and the chip returns a response computed with its secret key. Only on a match does the immobiliser send a start enable to the engine ECU; without it the ECU will not fire the injectors or the coils, so the engine cranks but never runs.

Worked example

Question. A wiper arm is 500 mm from pivot to tip and sweeps 120°. It makes 45 wipes a minute, one wipe being out and back. The intermittent timer uses 100 kΩ and 100 µF. Exam traps

  • High wiper speed comes from the third brush, which leaves fewer conductors and less back emf between the brushes in use; the supply stays 12 V.
  • The park switch is a cam contact in the gearbox, and it also brakes the motor by shorting the armature; examiners ask for both.
  • R × C gives seconds only with R in ohms and C in farads: 100 kΩ with 100 µF is 10 s, not 10 ms.
  • The immobiliser does not usually stop the starter. It withholds the start enable, so the engine cranks but the injectors and coils never fire.
  • Treating keyless entry and the immobiliser as one system. The fob's rolling code opens the doors; the transponder challenge and response allows the engine to start.

  1. A coil ignition makes high voltage only because the primary current is cut quickly: the collapsing flux induces a large emf in the many secondary turns, and the capacitor stops the points arcing so the collapse is fast.

  4. Electronic ignition swaps the points for a pick-up and a transistor; constant-energy dwell keeps the coil current, and programmed ignition reads the advance from a speed-load map with corrections.

Model answer.

  1. Draw the circuit. The primary circuit runs battery → ignition switch → ballast resistor → coil primary → contact breaker → earth, with a capacitor across the points. The secondary circuit runs coil secondary → distributor rotor and cap → plug lead → plug → earth.
  2. Name the parts and their jobs. The battery supplies 12 V; the ignition switch connects the system; the ballast resistor limits running current and is bypassed during cranking; the coil has a primary of a few hundred thick turns and a secondary of about 100 times as many fine turns on one iron core; the cam-driven points switch the primary; the capacitor (0.2–0.3 µF) sits across the points; the distributor routes each pulse to the plug in the firing order; the centrifugal and vacuum units move the timing.

By e = −N dΦ/dt an emf of a few hundred volts appears in the primary, and the secondary, with about 100 times the turns, gives roughly 100 × that, 10–25 kV or more.
  5. Role of the capacitor. At the instant of opening the capacitor takes the primary current, so no arc forms between the separating contacts. The current therefore stops fast, dΦ/dt is large, the secondary voltage is high, and the points do not burn.
  6. Breakdown and spark. The secondary voltage rises until the plug gap ionises (the firing voltage, about 10–20 kV), then falls to a spark-line voltage of 1–2 kV while the arc burns for 1–2 ms using the stored energy. The arc lights the compressed charge a little before TDC.

  8. Timing. Centrifugal weights advance the spark as speed rises; a vacuum diaphragm fed from a port above the throttle adds advance at part-throttle cruise. Total advance = base + centrifugal + vacuum.

Understand What decides the voltage needed to fire a spark plug? Explain the effect of electrode gap, compression pressure and electrode temperature, and why misfire appears first at high load. 10 marks

Points to cover. Breakdown needs a field strong enough to ionise the gap; define firing voltage; Wider gap: more voltage; Higher pressure: more voltage; Hot electrodes: less voltage; worn, rounded electrodes: more; Available voltage against required voltage: the reserve; high load uses it up.

Model answer.

  1. Define the firing voltage. The gas in the gap is an insulator. When the voltage rises, the few free electrons are accelerated by the field and, if they gain enough energy between collisions, knock more electrons off gas molecules. The avalanche turns the gap into a conducting arc. The voltage at which this happens is the firing (breakdown) voltage Vf.
  2. Electrode gap. For a given voltage the field is E = V ÷ g, so a wider gap g needs a proportionally higher voltage to reach the same field. Worn plugs have wider gaps, so they need more voltage. For the example engine of the book, Vf ≈ 10 + 22 g kV at full load.
  3. Compression pressure. Denser gas means the electrons travel a shorter distance between collisions, so they need a stronger field to gain enough energy. Full load and high compression ratios raise the pressure at the spark, so Vf rises. At light load the same engine needs only about 5 + 12 g kV.
  4. Electrode temperature and shape. A hot surface frees electrons more easily, so Vf falls as the electrodes heat up; a cold engine needs more voltage. Sharp edges concentrate the field and lower Vf; worn, rounded electrodes raise it. Fouling deposits leak charge away before the voltage builds.
  5. Mixture. A lean mixture needs a slightly higher voltage and a longer, stronger spark to light.
  6. Pressure and gap together. For a uniform field the breakdown voltage depends on the product of pressure and gap (Paschen's law), which is why a plug that fires on the bench at 1 bar can still miss in the engine at 10–15 bar: the engine multiplies the pressure several times over.
  7. Polarity. Coils are wired so the centre electrode is negative, because the hotter centre electrode gives off electrons more easily, which lowers the firing voltage; reversed coil leads raise the voltage the plug needs.
  8. Why misfire appears first at high load. Take a coil that gives 25 kV and a 0.9 mm gap:
    ConditionRequired VfReserve
    Light load5 + 12 × 0.9 = 15.8 kV25 − 15.8 = +9.2 kV: fires
    Full load10 + 22 × 0.9 = 29.8 kV25 − 29.8 = −4.8 kV: misfires

Understand Explain how an electronic ignition system uses a pick-up and a switching transistor in place of contact-breaker points, and how constant-energy dwell control works. 13 marks

Points to cover. Problems of the points; Pick-up types: inductive, Hall effect; Igniter transistor switches the coil current; Constant dwell against constant energy with current limiting; Advantages.

Model answer.

  1. State the problems of the points. They break a few amperes many times a second, so they burn; the rubbing block wears, so the dwell drifts; at high speed the fixed dwell angle gives too little charging time; and the contacts can bounce.
  2. Pick-up (position sensor). An inductive pick-up has a permanent magnet and coil facing a toothed reluctor; each tooth changes the magnetic path and induces an alternating voltage whose size grows with speed. A Hall-effect pick-up has a Hall chip and magnet separated by a rotating vane; it gives a clean square wave whose size does not depend on speed, so it works down to cranking speed. Either gives one signal per cylinder per cycle at a known crank angle.
  3. Igniter. The pick-up signal goes to the ignition module, which drives a power transistor (often a Darlington or IGBT) in series with the coil primary. Transistor on = current builds (dwell); transistor off = the break, and the coil fires exactly as with points. The pick-up only reports position; it carries no coil current.
  4. Constant dwell. Early modules copied the points: the transistor conducts for a fixed fraction of each spark interval, so the dwell angle is fixed and the dwell time falls with speed. At 6000 rpm a 60% dwell on a four-cylinder engine gives only 0.60 × 5 ms = 3 ms.
  5. Constant energy. A low-resistance, low-inductance coil reaches its current quickly. The module senses the primary current with a small resistor and switches on just early enough to reach a set current, say 7.5 A, before each spark; a current-limiting stage then holds 7.5 A until the break. It also lengthens the dwell when the battery voltage is low.

Analyze Analyse why ignition timing must be advanced as engine speed rises and retarded as load rises, and how knock limits the advance in a programmed ignition system. 13 marks

Points to cover. Burn takes roughly a fixed time; crank angle per ms grows with speed; Peak pressure should come about 15° after TDC; Light load burns slowly: more advance; full load burns fast and knocks: less; Knock sensor, retard in steps, creep back; Map with corrections.

Model answer.

  1. The goal. The charge should burn so that peak cylinder pressure comes about 10–15° after TDC; earlier and the piston is pushed against while still rising, later and the gas expands before it has burned. The spark must be timed to start the burn early enough.
  2. Speed. The flame needs roughly the same time from spark to peak pressure, say 2.0 ms, while the crank turns faster. Crank angle covered in 2.0 ms is 2.0 ms × N/60 × 360°:
    SpeedAngle in 2.0 msSpark for peak at 15° ATDC
    1500 rpm2.0 × 25 rev/s × 360° = 18°18° − 15° = 3° BTDC
    4500 rpm2.0 × 75 rev/s × 360° = 54°54° − 15° = 39° BTDC
  3. In real engines turbulence rises with speed and shortens the burn a little, so the advance grows less steeply than this simple model, and flattens at high speed. That is the shape of a centrifugal advance curve.
  4. Load. At light load the throttle is nearly closed: the charge is thin and diluted with left-over exhaust gas, so it burns slowly and needs more advance (vacuum advance). At full load the dense, undiluted charge burns fast, so less advance is needed; extra advance would only raise pressure and temperature before TDC.
  5. Knock. Too much advance at high load makes the unburned end gas ahead of the flame self-ignite. The resulting pressure waves ring the block at a few kilohertz, hammer the piston and overheat it. The best-torque advance is often beyond the knock limit at full load, so the knock limit sets the advance there.
  6. Knock control. A piezoelectric knock sensor on the block picks up the ringing; the ECU looks for it in a window after each spark and, if found, retards that cylinder by a few degrees, then advances again in small steps until knock just returns. The engine thus runs at the knock limit of the fuel actually in the tank and adapts to heat and altitude.
  7. Programmed ignition. The ECU stores the best advance against speed and load in a map, then adds corrections for coolant temperature, cranking and knock. Compared with weights and a diaphragm, the map can follow any shape and never wears.
  8. Effects of too much or too little: too much advance gives knock and high NOx; too little gives low power, high exhaust temperature that can overheat the catalyst, and higher fuel use.
  9. Check: the advance for 1500 rpm (3° BTDC) and 4500 rpm (39° BTDC) differ by 36°, the 54° − 18° extra crank angle covered in the same 2.0 ms.

For a cheap car already built with halogen optics, keep halogen bulbs (or change the whole approved lamp unit), because LED bulbs in halogen housings make glare and are not legal.
  12. Check: the LED pair's 3.03 A against the halogen pair's 8.33 A is a 5.3 A cut, the same 70 W ÷ 13.2 V used in the fuel estimate.
Analyze Analyse how a modern instrument cluster works as a node on the CAN bus and how it measures speed, and what happens when a frame stops arriving. 13 marks

Points to cover. Microcontroller, CAN transceiver, stepper drivers, display, telltales; Speed from wheel or gearbox pulses: d = C ÷ N, v = 3.6 n d ÷ T; Frames from engine, ABS, body ECUs; update rates; timeout; Default behaviour and fault codes; Contrast with the older directly wired cluster.

Model answer.

  1. Hardware. The cluster has a microcontroller with memory, a CAN transceiver, stepper-motor drivers for the needles, an LCD or TFT display, LED telltales and a PWM backlight. A few inputs are wired directly: fuel sender, sometimes the speed sensor, the handbrake and fluid-level switches.
  2. Frames it receives. The engine ECU sends engine speed and coolant temperature; the ABS ECU sends vehicle speed from its wheel sensors; the body module sends lamp, door and indicator states. Each frame has an identifier (which also sets its priority), up to 8 data bytes and a check sum, and is repeated every 10–100 ms depending on how fast it changes.
  3. Speed from pulses (wheel sensor, N = 4, C = 1.90 m, gate 0.5 s, true 100 km/h):

  4. Gauge drive. The microcontroller turns each value into a needle angle with a stored curve and steps the motor; it damps the fuel reading so sloshing does not swing the needle, and lights telltales from status bits in the frames.
  5. A frame stops arriving. The cluster watches the time since the last frame of each kind. If, say, the engine-speed frame (normally every 10 ms) is missing for a set timeout, perhaps several hundred milliseconds, it treats the value as invalid rather than showing a frozen number.
  6. Default behaviour: the affected needle drops to zero or the display shows dashes; the matching warning lamp is lit (for example the engine or ABS lamp); a fault code such as "lost communication with engine ECU" is stored in memory; other gauges with good frames carry on. The cluster may sound a chime.
  7. Diagnosis. A technician reads the stored codes through the diagnostic connector, then checks which ECU stopped talking: if several frames from one ECU are missing, the fault is that ECU or its supply; if frames from many ECUs are missing, the bus wiring, its two 120 Ω terminations or a short is suspect.
  8. Contrast with a directly wired cluster: each gauge had its own wire and sender, so a broken wire affected one gauge only and the gauge simply read zero, but the wiring was heavy and the cluster could not store faults.
  9. Check: 29 pulses × 0.475 m = 13.78 m in 0.5 s = 27.55 m/s = 99.2 km/h, 0.8 km/h below the true 100 km/h because the 0.24 part pulse was dropped.
Evaluate Evaluate adaptive front lighting: what it gains the driver, what it costs, and where it can fail. Use a car with a 2.5 m wheelbase and a 20 mm rear drop, and a swivel law of 0.15 × steering-wheel angle capped at 15°, to support your answer. 16 marks

Points to cover. Functions: levelling, dynamic bending, static cornering, town/country/motorway/adverse-weather, ADB; Numbers: 0.46° pitch, +0.20 m at 25 m; 13.5° and 15° swivel; Gains: see further on bends, less glare; Costs: motors, sensors, ECU, calibration, repair; Failure modes and fail-safe; verdict.

Model answer.

  1. What AFS does. It levels the beam for pitch (load, braking, acceleration); swivels it with the steering (dynamic bending light); switches a cornering lamp at junction speed (static cornering light); reshapes the beam for town, country, motorway and wet roads; and, with a camera, keeps the high beam on while blanking other road users (adaptive driving beam).

  4. Gains for the driver. On a bend a fixed beam lights the verge; a 13.5° swivel puts light on the lane ahead, so the driver sees an obstacle earlier. A 0.20 m rise at 25 m without levelling is enough to dazzle oncoming drivers, so levelling protects them and keeps the driver's own range from shrinking under braking dive. Motorway light lengthens the beam at speed; adverse-weather light cuts glare from wet roads; ADB lets the driver use the high beam far more of the time.
  5. Gains for others. Less glare for oncoming and leading drivers, the main reason very bright lamps must have automatic levelling.
  6. Costs. Two motors per lamp, ride-height sensors, steering-angle input, an ECU or software in the lamp module, a camera for ADB, more complex type approval, calibration after a wheel-alignment or windscreen change, and expensive repair after a small front-end crash, since the lamp unit holds motors and electronics.
  7. Failure modes. A stuck swivel motor leaves the beam pointing sideways; a drifting height sensor (a bent link after a bump) aims the beam wrongly all the time; a camera that misses a vehicle lets ADB dazzle it; software errors can make the beam twitch over bumps; water in a connector can disable a whole lamp.
  8. Fail-safe design. On any fault the lamps park straight ahead and slightly low, the high-beam assist switches off, and a warning lamp and fault code are set, so the driver always has a legal dipped beam. The swivel command is always capped (15° here) so a wrong steering signal cannot sweep the beam into oncoming traffic.
  9. Checks in service. The technician reads codes, calibrates the height sensors to the unladen car, sets the basic aim (−1.0% here) on a screen, and tests the swivel range against the specification.
  10. Verdict. For a low-cost car, automatic levelling is the most valuable part, because it protects other road users and is required anyway with bright lamps; swivel and ADB give real gains on dark, winding roads but add cost and failure points that mainly suit higher-priced cars. As LED matrix lamps get cheaper, beam shaping in software is replacing moving motors, removing many of the mechanical failures.
  11. Check: tilting the lamp down 0.46° cancels the 0.20 m rise at 25 m (25 × tan 0.46° = 0.20 m), and the swivel law reaches its cap at 15 ÷ 0.15 = 100° of steering-wheel angle.

Glossary

Adaptive front lighting
System that levels, swivels and reshapes the headlamp beam to suit load, steering and speed.
Ballast resistor
Resistor in series with the coil primary that limits current when running and is bypassed during cranking.
Candela
Unit of luminous intensity, light per unit solid angle in one direction.
Centrifugal advance
Speed-sensitive weights and springs that turn the cam ahead of the shaft to advance the spark with engine speed.
Coil-on-plug
Direct ignition with a separate coil mounted on each plug.
Condenser
Capacitor of about 0.2–0.3 µF across the contact-breaker points that stops arcing and speeds the collapse of the field.
Contact breaker
Cam-driven mechanical switch in older systems that makes and breaks the coil primary current.
Distributor
Rotating switch, driven at camshaft speed, that sends the coil's high voltage to each plug in firing order.
Dwell angle
Cam or crank angle for which the primary current flows before each spark.
Electric horn
Self-interrupting electromagnetic buzzer whose vibrating diaphragm makes the warning sound.
Firing voltage
Voltage at which the plug gap breaks down and the spark starts.
Flasher unit
Electronic or thermal switch that flashes the indicators at about 90 a minute.
Fuel sender
Float-driven variable resistor in the tank that tells the gauge the fuel level.
Hall-effect pick-up
Sensor that gives a square-wave signal as a vane interrupts a magnetic field, independent of speed.
Head-up display
Optical unit that shows a virtual image of speed and warnings about 2–2.5 m ahead of the driver.
Igniter
Power transistor stage that switches the coil primary current on the command of a pick-up or the ECU.
Ignition switch
Key or button switch that connects the ignition, starter and accessory circuits.
Immobiliser
System that enables fuel and spark only after a coded transponder key answers correctly.
Knock sensor
Piezoelectric sensor on the block that detects detonation so the ECU can retard the timing.
Lumen
Unit of luminous flux, the total light output of a source.
Lux
Unit of illuminance, one lumen per square metre falling on a surface.
Park switch
Cam contact in the wiper gearbox that runs the motor to the park position and then brakes it.
Projector headlamp
Lamp using an ellipsoidal reflector, a shield and a lens to form a sharp cut-off beam.
Resistor spark plug
Plug with a built-in resistor that suppresses radio-frequency interference from the spark.
Rolling code
Code from a key fob that changes on every press so a recording cannot be replayed.
Telltale
Symbol lamp in the cluster that reports a system state or fault to the driver.
Vacuum advance
Diaphragm unit fed from a port just above the throttle plate that adds advance at part-throttle cruise.
Washer pump
Small motor-driven centrifugal pump that sprays screen wash through jets.
Waste spark
Spark delivered to a cylinder on its exhaust stroke by a coil shared with its pair.
