6.1 Vehicle networking and need for data buses

A modern car has tens of electronic control units (ECUs), and most of them need data that another ECU measures. Running one copper wire for every signal between every pair of units makes a harness that is heavy, costly and full of connectors that can fail. A data bus solves this: all the units share one pair of wires and send their data as short numbered messages. This chapter explains how data moves between automotive electronic systems, why one signal is used by many systems at once, and what a vehicle bus must guarantee before it can carry engine, brake or door data.

You need the vehicle power supply, wire sizing and circuit diagrams, and the idea of a sensor that gives an electrical signal.

Data transfer between ECUs: wires, signals and messages

An electronic control unit (ECU) is a small computer: a microcontroller with memory, input circuits for sensors, output drivers for actuators and a communication interface. A node is anything that sends or receives on a network; every ECU is a node, and so is the diagnostic socket when a scan tool is plugged in. A signal is one quantity, such as engine speed or a door-open switch. A message (or frame) is one packet on the bus; one message usually carries several signals packed side by side.

In the older point-to-point method, each signal has its own wire from the unit that measures it to every unit that uses it. If engine speed is needed by the instrument cluster, the gearbox ECU and the air-conditioning ECU, three wires carry the same value. With a serial data bus, the engine ECU puts engine speed into one message, sends it once, and every unit that needs it reads it. Sending many signals over one medium in turn is called multiplexing.

L = n(n − 1) / 2

L is the number of links needed to join every one of n units directly to every other one. A bus needs one shared pair of wires and one transceiver (the transmit-receive circuit) per unit, so its cost grows only as n.

Inside one ECU, chips talk over short board-level links, which some syllabi call communication protocols in embedded systems: UART (start and stop bits; also the basis of LIN), SPI (a fast clocked link to memory or driver chips) and I²C (two wires shared by sensors). They work over centimetres; a vehicle bus must work over metres of harness, in electrical noise, between units from different suppliers.

Cross-system functions and shared signals

A cross-system function is a vehicle feature that no single ECU can perform alone. It is the strongest reason for networking. Some everyday examples:

  • Traction control: the ABS ECU sees a driven wheel spinning faster than the others and sends a torque-reduction request; the engine ECU retards ignition or closes the throttle.
  • Wheel speed: measured once by the ABS ECU, it is used by the speedometer, the odometer, the navigation unit, the electric power steering (lighter steering at low speed) and the gearbox ECU.
  • Automatic gear shift: the gearbox ECU asks the engine ECU for a short torque dip during each shift, so the shift is smooth.
  • Crash unlock: the airbag ECU sends a crash message; the body ECU unlocks the doors and switches on the hazard lamps.

Car makers group ECUs into domains by the kind of data they exchange: powertrain (engine, gearbox), chassis (ABS, ESP, steering, suspension), body and comfort (doors, seats, lamps, climate), infotainment (radio, navigation, phone, displays) and diagnostics. Each domain has its own bus, chosen for its needs, and a gateway ECU joins the domains and forwards only the messages that must cross.

Requirements for a vehicle bus

A vehicle bus is judged on a short list of requirements. The examiner expects you to name them and to say which systems need which.

  • Data rate (bandwidth): the bits per second the bus can carry, from about 20 kbit/s for a door module to 100 Mbit/s for a camera.
  • Latency: the delay from a unit deciding to send a value to the receiver having it, in milliseconds or microseconds.
  • Determinism: the worst-case delay is known and guaranteed, not just small on average. Brakes and steering need this; a seat motor does not.
  • Reliability: errors must be detected (check sums, acknowledgement) and a faulty node must not block the others.
  • Fault tolerance: some functions must keep working after a broken wire or a failed node.
  • EMC: the bus must not radiate interference and must survive ignition and motor noise.
  • Cost per node, sleep and wake-up: cheap enough for dozens of small units, and able to sleep in a parked car and wake on a message.
  • Expandability and diagnosis: new units can be added without rewiring, and a workshop tester can reach every unit.

Because the bus is shared, its time is a limited resource. The bus load U is the fraction of time the bus is busy:

U = (Σ bits per message ÷ period of that message) ÷ bit rate

the sum runs over every periodic message; bits per message is the frame length on the wire; the period is how often the message is sent (s); the bit rate is in bit/s. A common design target is to keep U below about 60 to 70 per cent, so that retries after errors and messages added later still fit.

Worked example

Question. A car has 12 ECUs, and each must exchange data with every other ECU. (a) How many links does full point-to-point wiring need, and how many line driver circuits at the link ends? (b) How many transceivers does one shared bus need? (c) How many fewer driver circuits is that?

  1. Number of units: n = 12.
  2. Links for full point-to-point wiring: L = n(n − 1) / 2 = 12 × 11 / 2 = 132 / 2 = 66 links.
  3. Each link has a driver at each end: 2L = 2 × 66 = 132 driver circuits.
  4. A bus needs one transceiver per unit: 12 transceivers, on one shared twisted pair.
  5. Saving: 132 − 12 = 120 driver circuits, and 66 separate cable runs become one.

Answer. Point-to-point wiring needs 66 links with 132 line drivers; one bus needs 12 transceivers, 120 fewer.

Worked example 2

Question. A powertrain CAN bus runs at 500 kbit/s. Twenty messages are sent every 10 ms and eight more every 20 ms. Each message takes 125 bits on the wire. (a) Find the bus load. (b) Can four new messages of 125 bits every 10 ms be added if the load must stay under 70 per cent?

  1. Bits per second from the 10 ms group: 20 × 125 bits ÷ 0.010 s = 2500 ÷ 0.010 = 250 000 bit/s.
  2. Bits per second from the 20 ms group: 8 × 125 bits ÷ 0.020 s = 1000 ÷ 0.020 = 50 000 bit/s.
  3. Total demand: 250 000 + 50 000 = 300 000 bit/s.
  4. Bus load: U = 300 000 ÷ 500 000 = 0.60, that is 60 per cent.
  5. New messages: 4 × 125 ÷ 0.010 = 50 000 bit/s, so the demand becomes 350 000 bit/s.
  6. New load: 350 000 ÷ 500 000 = 0.70, exactly 70 per cent, which is not under the limit.

Answer. The load is 60 per cent now; the four new messages would raise it to 70 per cent, so they do not fit under the 70 per cent limit. Send them every 20 ms (load 65 per cent) or move some messages to another bus.

Exam traps

  • Counting each pair twice: 12 units need 66 point-to-point links, not 132. The 132 is the number of link ends.
  • A signal is not a message: one message usually carries several signals, so "one wire per signal" becomes "one message for several signals", not one message per signal.
  • High bandwidth is not determinism. A fast bus can still have an unknown worst-case delay if messages queue; determinism means the worst case is guaranteed.
  • Bus load uses the period of each message, not the number of messages alone: 20 messages every 10 ms load the bus twice as much as 20 messages every 20 ms.
  • A gateway is not a junction box: it is an ECU that decides which messages cross, and it can change their rate or format.

6.2 Network topology, OSI model and classification of bus systems

Before choosing a bus, an engineer asks four questions: how are the nodes wired together (topology), who is allowed to start a message (network organization), how is a clash on the wire avoided (control mechanism), and how fast and how safe must it be (class). The OSI reference model gives a common language for the answers, because it splits communication into seven layers and lets us say exactly which jobs a bus such as CAN does and which it leaves to software. This chapter gives the topologies, the access methods, the seven layers and the bus classes, with the numbers an examiner asks for.

You need vehicle networking and the need for data buses: nodes, messages, bus load and the requirements for a bus.

Network topology

Topology is the shape of the wiring between nodes. Five shapes appear in cars:

A bus needs the least cable and is easy to extend; a star needs the most but isolates faults; a ring suits a continuous stream such as audio but needs every node working.

Network organization and control mechanisms

Network organization says who may start a transfer. In master-slave organization one master starts every exchange and the slaves only answer (LIN). In multi-master organization any node may start (CAN). Software then follows a client-server pattern (a tester asks, an ECU answers) or a producer-consumer pattern (a node broadcasts a value and every interested node takes it).

Addressing says how a receiver knows a message is for it. Subscriber-oriented addressing puts the address of the receiving node in the message. Message-oriented addressing gives the content a number (CAN's identifier means "engine speed", not "for the cluster"), and each node filters the numbers it wants. Transmission-oriented addressing identifies a message by its time slot (FlexRay's static segment).

The control mechanism, or bus access method, decides who may use the shared wire at a given moment:

  • Centralised, deterministic: master-slave polling. The master's schedule fixes every slot, so latency is known (LIN).
  • Decentralised, deterministic: time-division multiple access (TDMA), where each node owns fixed time slots (FlexRay static segment), or token passing, where only the node holding the token may send.
  • Random access: carrier sense multiple access (CSMA). A node sends when the bus is idle. In CSMA/CD (collision detection, early office Ethernet) a collision destroys both frames and both retry. In CAN's version, often called CSMA/CR (collision resolution), the clash is settled bit by bit so the higher-priority frame survives undamaged.

Worked example

Question. A scan tool asks for the vehicle identification number (VIN). The engine ECU's reply is 20 bytes long: 3 bytes of service and record number, then the 17 VIN characters. It travels on classic CAN using ISO-TP. How many CAN frames does the ECU send, and how many bytes does each carry? Which OSI layers are at work?

  1. A single frame holds at most 7 data bytes; 20 > 7, so the reply must be segmented.
  2. First frame: it carries the total length (20) in its header and the first 6 data bytes. Bytes left: 20 − 6 = 14.
  3. The scan tool answers with one flow control frame that says "send the rest".
  4. Consecutive frame 1 carries 7 bytes: 14 − 7 = 7 left. Consecutive frame 2 carries the last 7 bytes: 7 − 7 = 0 left.
  5. ECU frames: 1 first frame + 2 consecutive frames = 3 frames, carrying 6 + 7 + 7 = 20 bytes.
  6. Layers: the VIN request and reply are layer 7 (an OBD service); the cutting into first and consecutive frames is layer 4; each frame's arbitration and CRC are layer 2; the voltages on the twisted pair are layer 1.

Answer. The ECU sends 3 frames (6 + 7 + 7 data bytes) after the tester's flow control frame; layers 7, 4, 2 and 1 are used, and layers 3, 5 and 6 are empty.

Worked example 2

Question. Find the raw data rate each function needs and name its bus class. (a) A door module sends 8 frames of 100 bits every 100 ms. (b) The engine and gearbox exchange 30 messages of 125 bits every 20 ms. (c) A rear camera sends 640 × 480 pixels at 16 bits per pixel, 30 pictures per second, uncompressed.

  1. (a) 8 × 100 bits ÷ 0.100 s = 800 ÷ 0.100 = 8000 bit/s = 8 kbit/s. Below 10 kbit/s: Class A; a 19.2 kbit/s LIN bus is 42 per cent loaded (8 ÷ 19.2 = 0.417).
  2. (b) 30 × 125 bits ÷ 0.020 s = 3750 ÷ 0.020 = 187 500 bit/s = 187.5 kbit/s. Between 125 kbit/s and 1 Mbit/s: Class C; a 500 kbit/s CAN bus is 37.5 per cent loaded.
  3. (c) Bits per picture = 640 × 480 × 16 = 307 200 × 16 = 4 915 200 bits.
  4. Rate = 4 915 200 × 30 = 147 456 000 bit/s ≈ 147.5 Mbit/s.
  5. This is above 100 Mbit/s, so 100BASE-T1 cannot carry it uncompressed; it needs a multimedia-class link such as 1000BASE-T1 Ethernet, or compression.

Answer. (a) 8 kbit/s, Class A (LIN). (b) 187.5 kbit/s, Class C (500 kbit/s CAN). (c) 147.5 Mbit/s, the multimedia class above 1 Mbit/s (gigabit Ethernet or compressed video).

Exam traps

  • CAN is not a seven-layer stack. The CAN standard covers layers 1 and 2 only; transport (ISO-TP) and application (UDS, OBD) are separate standards.
  • Master-slave is an organization, not a topology. LIN is master-slave on a bus topology; a star is a wiring shape.
  • CAN does not use CSMA/CD. In CSMA/CD a collision destroys both frames; in CAN the higher-priority frame wins the clash without damage.
  • SAE defines Classes A, B and C. Name C+ or D as a textbook extension for buses above 1 Mbit/s, and say which buses you mean.
  • A single ring is not fault tolerant: one broken fibre in a MOST ring stops the whole ring.

6.3 CAN and CAN FD protocols

CAN (Controller Area Network) is the bus most ECUs in a car use: a two-wire, multi-master broadcast bus in which every node hears every frame and each frame carries a number that names its content. When two nodes start together, a dominant 0 overrides a recessive 1, so the lower identifier wins and its frame goes on undamaged. Classic CAN carries 0 to 8 data bytes at up to 1 Mbit/s; CAN FD (flexible data rate) keeps the arbitration and adds a faster data phase and up to 64 bytes.

You need topology, OSI layers and bus classes.

The two wires and bit-wise arbitration

Each node has a CAN controller that builds and checks frames and a transceiver that drives the twisted pair, CAN_H and CAN_L. In high-speed CAN (ISO 11898-2), a recessive bit (logic 1) leaves both wires at about 2.5 V, so the difference is 0 V. A dominant bit (logic 0) drives CAN_H to about 3.5 V and CAN_L to about 1.5 V, a difference of about 2 V. A 120 Ω resistor at each end of the trunk stops reflections; seen from the wires, the two are in parallel, so a healthy bus measures 60 Ω between CAN_H and CAN_L with the power off.

If one node drives dominant while another leaves the bus recessive, the bus is dominant. The wire acts like an AND gate on the logic levels (wired-AND). Arbitration uses this:

  1. The bus is idle (recessive). Every node with a frame waiting sends the start-of-frame bit, a dominant 0.
  2. Each node sends its identifier, most significant bit first, and reads the bus after every bit.
  3. A node that sent recessive 1 but reads dominant 0 has lost. It stops sending at once and becomes a receiver of the other frame.
  4. The node that never saw a difference wins and finishes its frame. The loser tries again when the bus is next idle.

No bit of the winning frame is damaged, so this is non-destructive arbitration, and a lower identifier means a higher priority: brake and engine messages get low identifiers.

Bit stuffing keeps edges on the wire for synchronisation: after five equal bits in a row the transmitter adds one bit of the opposite value, and every receiver removes it. Stuffing covers the bits from SOF to the end of the CRC sequence.

Each node checks for five errors: bit (a sender reads back a different bit, outside arbitration), stuff (six equal bits), CRC, form (a fixed bit such as a delimiter is wrong) and ACK (no receiver acknowledged). A node that finds one sends an error frame of six dominant bits, so every node discards the frame, and the sender repeats it. Each node counts its errors in a transmit error counter (TEC) and a receive error counter (REC). It is error active while both are 127 or less, error passive when either exceeds 127 (it may only send a recessive error flag), and bus-off when TEC exceeds 255 (it stops sending altogether). This fault confinement keeps one faulty node from blocking the bus.

SP = (1 + PROP + PHASE1) ÷ N

SP is the sample point as a fraction of one bit; a bit has N time quanta: one sync quantum, PROP for propagation delay, PHASE1 and PHASE2 either side of the sample point, N = 1 + PROP + PHASE1 + PHASE2. About 75 to 87.5 per cent is usual.

CAN FD: same arbitration, faster data

CAN FD keeps the identifier and arbitration rules, so priority works exactly as before, and changes the control field. RTR becomes RRS, always dominant, because CAN FD has no remote frames; IDE stays. Then come FDF (FD format, recessive in an FD frame), a reserved bit, BRS (bit rate switch: recessive means the data phase runs at the fast rate) and ESI (error state indicator: shows whether the sender is error passive).

DLC 0 to 8 means that many bytes; 9 to 15 mean 12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 48 and 64 bytes. The CRC is 17 bits up to 16 bytes and 21 bits above. A typical setting is arbitration at 500 kbit/s and a data phase at 2 Mbit/s. A classic controller cannot share a bus with FD frames: when it reads the recessive FDF bit where it expects the dominant r0, it raises a form error and destroys the FD frame. Every controller on an FD bus must therefore be FD-capable; the wires, transceivers rated for the fast rate and 120 Ω terminations stay the same.

Worked example

Question. Nodes A and B start arbitration at the same moment. A sends standard identifier 0x123 and B sends 0x121. Which node wins, at which identifier bit, and what does the loser do?

  1. Write the identifiers in decimal: 0x123 = 1×256 + 2×16 + 3 = 291; 0x121 = 256 + 32 + 1 = 289.
  2. Write the 11 bits, most significant first (bit 10 down to bit 0): A = 001 0010 0011, B = 001 0010 0001.
  3. Bits 10 down to 2 are the same in both (001 0010 00), so both nodes read back what they sent.
  4. At bit 1, A sends recessive 1 and B sends dominant 0. The bus reads 0.
  5. A sent 1 but read 0, so A has lost. It stops sending and receives B's frame. Bit 0 is 1 in both; only B is still sending.
  6. B finishes its frame undamaged. A sends its frame when the bus is next idle.

Answer. B (0x121, decimal 289) wins at identifier bit 1, because it has a dominant 0 there and A has a recessive 1; A becomes a receiver and retries after B's frame.

Worked example 2

Question. A standard CAN data frame carries 8 data bytes on a 500 kbit/s bus. (a) How many bits does it have without stuffing, counting the 3-bit interframe space? (b) The worst case adds one stuff bit for every 4 bits after the first in the stuffed region; how many bits is that? (c) How long does the frame occupy the bus in each case? (d) What is the useful data rate with no stuffing?

  1. Fixed bits from SOF to EOF: 1 + 11 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 4 + 15 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 7 = 44 bits, plus 8 × 8 = 64 data bits: 108 bits. With the interframe space: 108 + 3 = 111 bits.
  2. Stuffed region (SOF to end of CRC): 1 + 11 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 4 + 64 + 15 = 98 bits. Worst-case stuff bits = (98 − 1) ÷ 4 = 24.25, so 24 bits.
  3. Worst-case length: 111 + 24 = 135 bits.
  4. Bit time at 500 kbit/s = 1 ÷ 500 000 = 2 µs. No stuffing: 111 × 2 µs = 222 µs. Worst case: 135 × 2 µs = 270 µs.
  5. Useful data rate with no stuffing: 64 data bits every 222 µs = 64 ÷ 0.000222 = 288 288 bit/s, about 288 kbit/s, which is 64 ÷ 111 = 57.7 per cent of 500 kbit/s.

Answer. (a) 111 bits; (b) 135 bits; (c) 222 µs and 270 µs; (d) about 288 kbit/s of data, 57.7 per cent of the bit rate.

Exam traps

  • The lower identifier wins, because dominant is 0 and recessive is 1. Writing "the higher priority number wins" loses the mark.
  • CAN FD switches only the data phase to the fast rate (BRS); arbitration and ACK stay at the nominal rate, so priority works as in classic CAN.
  • In CAN FD a DLC above 8 is a code, not a byte count: 9 means 12 bytes, 10 means 16 and 15 means 64.
  • Bus-off follows TEC above 255; error passive follows TEC or REC above 127. Do not swap the two limits.

A frame of up to 64 bytes has many more bits that can be hit by noise, so a 17- or 21-bit CRC keeps the chance of an undetected error low. A classic node reads the recessive FDF bit as a form error and sends an error frame, destroying every FD frame.

6.4 LIN, FlexRay and automotive Ethernet

A car needs more than one kind of bus. A door switch sends a few bits now and then; steering and damping need messages at exactly known times; cameras and maps need very high bandwidth. LIN (Local Interconnect Network) is a cheap single-wire master-slave bus for body switches and motors. FlexRay is a dual-channel, time-triggered bus for chassis and x-by-wire control. Automotive Ethernet is a switched, single-pair network for cameras, infotainment and the backbone. This chapter shows how each one works, with frame timing and rates you can calculate, and where each fits beside CAN.

You need CAN and CAN FD for the comparison, and the bus classes from topology, OSI model and classification.

LIN: one wire, one master

LIN links small body modules: window lifters, mirrors, seat motors and climate flaps. It uses one signal wire plus ground, at battery voltage: the master has a 1 kΩ pull-up resistor and each slave about 30 kΩ, and a node pulls the wire low for a dominant 0. A cluster has one master (usually a body ECU that is also on CAN) and up to 15 slaves, at 1 to 20 kbit/s; 19.2 kbit/s is common.

The master owns a schedule table, a fixed list of frames and the time slot for each. For every slot it sends a header; the one node that publishes that frame (a slave, or the master itself) sends the response, and any node that needs the data reads it. Only the master starts frames, so there are no collisions and the timing is known.

  • Break: at least 13 dominant bits and a recessive delimiter. It marks the start of a frame, so every slave knows a header is coming. (Waking a sleeping cluster is a separate wake-up pulse.)
  • Sync: the byte 0x55 (alternating bits), from which slaves measure the bit time.
  • Protected identifier (PID): a 6-bit frame identifier (0 to 63) plus 2 parity bits, P0 = ID0 ⊕ ID1 ⊕ ID2 ⊕ ID4 and P1 = NOT(ID1 ⊕ ID3 ⊕ ID4 ⊕ ID5). It names the frame, not a slave.
  • Data: 1 to 8 bytes in the response.
  • Checksum: the classic checksum covers the data bytes; the enhanced checksum (LIN 2.x) covers the PID as well. Diagnostic frames 0x3C and 0x3D always use the classic one.

T = (34 + 10 × (n + 1)) × τ

T is the nominal frame time; 34 bits is the nominal header (break and delimiter 14, sync 10, PID 10); every byte on the wire takes 10 bits (start bit, 8 data bits, stop bit); n data bytes plus 1 checksum byte make the response; τ is one bit time (1 ÷ bit rate).

Tₘₐₓ = 1.4 × T

Tₘₐₓ is the longest time the frame may take; the 40 per cent margin allows for gaps between bytes. The schedule slot must be at least Tₘₐₓ.

FlexRay: fixed slots on two channels

FlexRay is for systems where a late message is itself a fault: active suspension, chassis control, brake-by-wire and steer-by-wire. It has two independent channels, A and B, each at up to 10 Mbit/s. A node may send the same frame on both channels for redundancy or different frames to double the rate. The wiring can be a passive bus, an active star (a star coupler that cuts off a faulty branch) or a mix.

Time is divided into repeating communication cycles, typically 1 to 5 ms long and numbered 0 to 63. Every node keeps a synchronised clock. Each cycle has four parts:

  • Static segment: time-division multiple access (TDMA). Equal slots, each owned by one node, repeat in the same order every cycle. No arbitration and no collisions, so the worst-case delay of a static message is one cycle.
  • Dynamic segment: flexible TDMA (FTDMA) on minislots. A slot counter steps through the minislots; a node whose turn comes and has data starts its frame and stretches its slot to the frame length, which leaves less time for later numbers. A node with nothing to send uses just one short minislot. Low numbers therefore have priority.
  • Symbol window: carries short symbols such as the collision avoidance symbol (CAS) and the media access test symbol (MTS). Start-up frames travel in static slots, and wake-up uses its own wake-up pattern.
  • Network idle time (NIT): no traffic; nodes correct their clocks.

A FlexRay frame has a 5-byte header (frame ID equal to the slot number, payload length, header CRC, cycle count), 0 to 254 payload bytes and a 24-bit CRC.

Automotive Ethernet: switched and service-ready

Office Ethernet uses two pairs (100BASE-TX) or four pairs (1000BASE-T) and heavy connectors. Automotive Ethernet sends full duplex over one unshielded twisted pair: 100BASE-T1 at 100 Mbit/s and 1000BASE-T1 at 1 Gbit/s, using echo cancellation so both ends can talk at once. Each link joins exactly two devices, so the network is a star of switches with no collisions. A switch forwards a frame only to the port that leads to its destination, so many transfers run in parallel. 10BASE-T1S (10 Mbit/s) is the exception: several nodes can share one short multidrop pair, which suits zonal links that used to be CAN.

Ethernet carries camera and radar streams, infotainment, software downloads and the backbone. VLAN tags keep safety, body and infotainment traffic apart on one switch. Above IP, SOME/IP (scalable service-oriented middleware over IP) lets an ECU call a service by name, and DoIP (diagnostics over IP) speeds up flashing. Plain Ethernet is not deterministic; AVB/TSN (audio video bridging, time-sensitive networking) adds clock synchronisation and scheduled traffic for bounded latency.

Worked example

Question. A LIN window-lifter slave sends a response of 8 data bytes at 19.2 kbit/s. (a) Find the nominal and maximum frame times. (b) The master's schedule has five frames, each given a 10 ms slot. Is 10 ms long enough for this frame, and how often is each frame sent?

  1. Bit time: τ = 1 ÷ 19 200 s = 52.08 µs.
  2. Bits on the wire: header 34 + response 10 × (8 + 1) = 34 + 90 = 124 bits.
  3. Nominal frame time: 124 × 52.08 µs = 6458 µs = 6.46 ms.
  4. Maximum frame time: 1.4 × 6.46 ms = 9.04 ms.
  5. 9.04 ms is less than the 10 ms slot, so the slot is long enough.
  6. Schedule cycle: 5 slots × 10 ms = 50 ms, so each frame is sent every 50 ms, 20 times a second.

Answer. Nominal 6.46 ms, maximum 9.04 ms; a 10 ms slot fits, and each of the five frames repeats every 50 ms.

Worked example 2

Question. A 1000-byte block (a camera thumbnail, say) must cross the car. Ignoring protocol overhead, how long does it take on 19.2 kbit/s LIN, 500 kbit/s CAN, one 10 Mbit/s FlexRay channel and 100 Mbit/s Ethernet?

  2. LIN: 8000 ÷ 19 200 = 0.4167 s = 416.7 ms.
  3. CAN: 8000 ÷ 500 000 = 0.016 s = 16 ms.

Exam traps

  • LIN is not multi-master: only the master sends headers. A slave sends only a response, and only for the frame identifiers it publishes.
  • The LIN header names a frame, not a slave. One frame can be read by many nodes, and the master can publish a frame itself.
  • FlexRay is not purely event-driven: the static segment is TDMA and gives the guarantee; only the dynamic segment is FTDMA.
  • Automotive Ethernet uses one pair, not the two pairs of 100BASE-TX or the four of 1000BASE-T. In a LIN frame time every byte is 10 bits, and the checksum byte counts too.

6.5 MOST and coupling of networks through gateways

Infotainment traffic is different from control traffic: audio and video must flow without a break, at megabits per second, between the head unit, amplifier, navigation, phone and displays. MOST (Media Oriented Systems Transport) was built for this. It is a ring, usually of plastic optical fibre, in which a timing master sends frames at the audio sample rate and every device takes its stream out of the frame and puts its own in. The ring is joined to the car's CAN, LIN and FlexRay buses by a gateway. This chapter explains MOST, the ways networks are coupled and what a central gateway does, with channel and delay numbers worked out.

You need the ring topology and OSI layers from topology, OSI model and classification, and the CAN frame time from CAN and CAN FD.

MOST: a synchronous ring for media

In a MOST network up to 64 devices are joined in a ring: each device receives from the one before and sends to the one after. MOST25 uses plastic optical fibre (POF) with a 1 mm core and a red LED: light, cheap and immune to electrical noise. MOST50 and MOST150 can also run on copper wires.

One device, usually the head unit, is the timing master. It sends frames round the ring without a pause at the system frequency, 44.1 kHz (the CD sample rate) or 48 kHz. Every other device is a timing slave: it recovers the clock from the arriving frames, takes the bytes meant for it, puts its own bytes into the free places and passes the frame on. Because every device runs on the master's clock, audio arrives one sample per frame with no buffering and no lost samples.

At the application layer each device is described by function blocks (for example an amplifier block or a tuner block) that other devices control with short control messages. Because the ring passes through every device, one broken fibre or one dead device stops the whole ring; MOST therefore has a ring break diagnosis that finds the position of the break, and the gateway reports it to the workshop tester.

The MOST25 frame and its three channels

A MOST25 frame is 64 bytes (512 bits): 1 byte of header (preamble and boundary descriptor), 60 bytes of data, 2 bytes of control data and 1 byte of trailer (frame control and parity). The 60 data bytes are split, by the boundary descriptor, between two channels:

  • Synchronous channel: byte positions reserved for one stream for as long as it plays. A 16-bit stereo audio stream sends one sample of each side per frame, so it needs 4 bytes in every frame.
  • Asynchronous channel: the rest of the 60 bytes, shared by packet data such as map tiles, address books and pictures, which can wait a little.
  • Control channel: the 2 control bytes of each frame. A control message is spread over a block of 16 frames, and the channel carries commands ("amplifier, volume up"), status and network management.

R = bytes per frame × 8 × f

R is the data rate of a channel in bit/s; f is the frame rate, equal to the system frequency (44 100 frames per second at 44.1 kHz). The whole 64-byte frame gives 64 × 8 × 44 100 = 22.58 Mbit/s, often quoted as about 23 Mbit/s for MOST25.

MOST50 doubles the frame to 1024 bits and MOST150 makes it 3072 bits, about 150 Mbit/s at 48 kHz; MOST150 also adds a channel that carries Ethernet packets and one for isochronous video. Many new cars now use Ethernet with AVB/TSN for media instead.

Coupling networks: repeaters, bridges, routers and gateways

Two networks can be joined at different OSI layers. A repeater (layer 1) only regenerates the signal, so both sides are one network. A bridge (layer 2) joins two segments of the same protocol and passes only frames that must cross. A router (layer 3) chooses a path between networks by address. A gateway works up to layer 7 and joins networks with different protocols, rates and frame formats. In a car, the buses are of different kinds, so they are coupled through gateways, most often one central gateway with a port on every domain bus.

The central gateway does six jobs:

  1. Message routing: a routing table says which identifiers received on one bus are sent on which other buses; the rest are dropped.
  2. Signal routing: it unpacks signals from incoming frames and packs them into new frames for the other bus, for example engine speed and coolant temperature into one body-bus frame for the cluster.
  3. Rate adaptation: it buffers frames between a 500 kbit/s bus and a 125 kbit/s bus, or between CAN and MOST, and may send a fast-changing signal less often on the slow side.
  4. Filtering and protection: it blocks frames that must not cross, so the infotainment side can never send a brake or steering message.
  5. Diagnostic routing: the OBD socket is wired to the gateway, which passes the tester's requests to every ECU on every bus.
  6. Network management: it wakes the buses when a door opens or a key is used and lets them sleep when the car is parked.

A networked car of today is an example: a 500 kbit/s powertrain CAN (engine, gearbox), a chassis CAN or FlexRay (ABS and ESP, steering, suspension), a 125 kbit/s body CAN with LIN sub-buses (doors, seats, climate), a MOST ring or Ethernet for infotainment, and the OBD socket, all meeting at the gateway. A frame that crosses waits for its turn in the gateway, so the delay of a crossing message is the sum of its frame time on the input bus, the gateway's processing time, any wait for the output bus and its frame time on the output bus.

Worked example

Question. A MOST25 ring runs at 44.1 kHz. The head unit plays a 16-bit stereo radio stream to the amplifier, and the DVD player sends 6-channel 16-bit surround sound. (a) What is the rate of one stereo stream and how many bytes per frame does it need? (b) How many bytes do the two streams use, and what asynchronous rate is left in the 60 data bytes? (c) How many stereo streams could the frame carry at most? (d) What is the control channel rate?

  1. (a) Stereo rate = 44 100 samples/s × 16 bits × 2 sides = 1 411 200 bit/s = 1.41 Mbit/s. Per frame: 2 sides × 16 bits = 32 bits = 4 bytes.
  2. (b) Surround: 6 channels × 2 bytes = 12 bytes per frame. Both streams: 4 + 12 = 16 synchronous bytes.
  3. Bytes left for the asynchronous channel: 60 − 16 = 44 bytes. Rate = 44 × 8 × 44 100 = 15 523 200 bit/s ≈ 15.5 Mbit/s.
  4. (c) Most stereo streams: 60 ÷ 4 = 15 if every data byte were synchronous.
  5. (d) Control channel: 2 bytes × 8 × 44 100 = 705 600 bit/s = 705.6 kbit/s.

Answer. (a) 1.41 Mbit/s, 4 bytes per frame; (b) 16 bytes used, about 15.5 Mbit/s left for packet data; (c) 15 stereo streams at most; (d) 705.6 kbit/s for control.

Worked example 2

Question. The central gateway forwards a standard 8-data-byte frame (111 bits with the interframe space, no stuffing) from the 500 kbit/s powertrain CAN to the 125 kbit/s body CAN. Its processing takes 0.20 ms and the body bus is free. (a) Find the delay from the start of the frame on the powertrain bus to the end of its copy on the body bus. (b) The gateway forwards 10 such frames every 20 ms. What load do they put on the body bus?

  1. Input frame time: 111 bits ÷ 500 000 bit/s = 0.000222 s = 0.222 ms.
  2. Output frame time: 111 bits ÷ 125 000 bit/s = 0.000888 s = 0.888 ms.
  3. Total delay = 0.222 + 0.20 + 0 + 0.888 = 1.31 ms.
  4. Bits per second on the body bus: 10 × 111 bits ÷ 0.020 s = 1110 ÷ 0.020 = 55 500 bit/s.
  5. Load: 55 500 ÷ 125 000 = 0.444, that is 44.4 per cent of the body bus from forwarded frames alone.

Answer. (a) 1.31 ms; (b) 44.4 per cent, so the gateway must forward only the frames the body bus really needs, or send them less often.

Exam traps

  • MOST is not arbitrated like CAN. Synchronous streams own fixed byte positions in every frame; only the asynchronous and control channels are shared.
  • One broken fibre stops the whole MOST ring, not just the next device; this is why the ring break diagnosis exists.
  • A gateway is not a repeater or a bridge: it translates between different protocols and decides which signals cross, up to OSI layer 7.
  • Forwarding is not free: the crossing message pays its frame time on both buses plus the gateway's processing, and the slow bus pays its load.
  • The MOST frame rate equals the audio sample rate (44.1 or 48 kHz), so a stream uses bytes per frame, not bits per second, in the frame budget.

6.6 On-board diagnostics: OBD-II

OBD-II (on-board diagnostics, second generation) is the standard that lets one scan tool plug into any compliant car and read its emissions-related faults and live engine data. It fixes the 16-pin connector and its pins, the bus protocols, the request services, the parameter numbers and the format of the fault codes. It became law for petrol cars in the USA from 1996 and in Europe (as EOBD) from 2001; in India the BS-VI Phase 2 rules of April 2023 brought full OBD-II monitoring. A workshop uses it to find why the malfunction lamp is on, to read sensor values while the engine runs and to check that the engine computer has finished its self-tests.

Fault codes, monitors and the lamp

A diagnostic trouble code (DTC) is a letter and four digits. The letter is the system: P powertrain, C chassis, B body, U network. The first digit is 0 for a generic code defined by the standard and 1 for a maker-specific one (2 and 3 are split between the two). For P codes the second digit is the subsystem (1 and 2 fuel and air metering, 3 ignition and misfire, 4 emission controls, 5 speed and idle control, 6 computer and outputs, 7 and 8 transmission), and the last two digits name the fault. P0301 is a generic misfire in cylinder 1. On the bus each DTC is two bytes: the top 2 bits give the letter (00 P, 01 C, 10 B, 11 U), the next 2 bits the first digit, and the remaining three groups of 4 bits the last three digits.

Monitors are self-tests the ECU runs: misfire, fuel system and comprehensive components run all the time; catalyst, oxygen sensor, oxygen sensor heater, evaporative system and EGR run only when their conditions are met during a drive cycle (cold start, warm-up, steady cruise, idle). The readiness status (service 01, PID 01) shows which monitors have completed since the codes were last cleared. When a monitor first fails, the ECU stores a pending code. Most faults are two-trip faults: if the same fault is found in the next drive cycle, the code is confirmed, a freeze frame is stored and the malfunction indicator lamp (MIL) comes on. A misfire bad enough to damage the catalyst lights the MIL at once, and flashes it. After three good drive cycles in a row the lamp goes off, and the code is erased after about 40 warm-up cycles without the fault. Service 04 clears codes, but a fault that is still there returns, and a permanent code stays until its monitor passes.

Worked example

Question. A scan tool sends 01 0C (live engine speed). The ECU replies 41 0C 1A F0. Find the engine speed.

  1. The reply starts 41 (service 01 + 0x40) and 0C (the PID), so the data bytes are A = 0x1A and B = 0xF0.
  2. A = 0x1A = 1 × 16 + 10 = 26; B = 0xF0 = 15 × 16 + 0 = 240.
  3. Raw count = 256A + B = 256 × 26 + 240 = 6656 + 240 = 6896.
  4. Each count is 0.25 rpm, so speed = 6896 ÷ 4 = 1724 rpm.

Answer. The engine is running at 1724 rpm.

Worked example 2

Question. Service 03 on a CAN car returns 43 02 03 01 01 33. A freeze frame for the first code gives PID 05 = 0x7B, PID 0D = 0x3E and PID 04 = 0x59. Decode the codes and the freeze frame.

6.7 MISRA coding and ISO 26262 functional safety

Networked ECUs run software that can brake, steer or deploy an airbag, so a software fault can hurt people. Two standards deal with this. MISRA C is a set of coding guidelines that removes the risky parts of the C language, so embedded code behaves predictably and can be checked by tools. ISO 26262 is the functional-safety standard for road vehicles: it starts from a hazard, assigns an ASIL (automotive safety integrity level) from severity, exposure and controllability, and then demands documented evidence through a safety lifecycle. MISRA keeps the code clean; ISO 26262 shows the whole item is safe enough. Some syllabi cover this only briefly.

You need the idea of determinism and fault confinement from CAN and CAN FD, and some C programming.

MISRA C: guidelines and deviations

MISRA C:2012 sorts its guidelines as mandatory (never broken), required (broken only with a formal deviation) and advisory. A deviation is a written, reviewed permit with a reason and a risk analysis, not a silent exception. Typical rules: no recursion (stack depth must be provable); no use of malloc, calloc, realloc or free at all (Rule 21.3); no implicit conversion that can lose a value; braces round every if, else and loop body; pointer arithmetic only inside an array; every switch has a default; check every error return. A static-analysis tool checks the source, and the team fixes each violation or records a deviation.

Passing MISRA gives disciplined code; it is not proof of functional safety. Code generated from models must pass the same checks, or the generator must be qualified as a tool.

ISO 26262: hazard, ASIL, safety goal and evidence

ISO 26262:2018 has 12 parts and follows a V-model: requirements down the left side, implementation at the bottom, and verification and validation back up the right. Part 3 is the concept phase and part 6 covers software. The item definition fixes what is inside: which ECUs, sensors, actuators and functions. HARA (hazard analysis and risk assessment) studies each hazardous event and rates severity S0 to S3, exposure E0 to E4 and controllability C0 to C3. The standard's table gives the ASIL; for the classes S1 to S3, E1 to E4 and C1 to C3, it matches a simple sum: S + E + C = 10 gives ASIL D, 9 gives C, 8 gives B, 7 gives A, and 6 or less gives QM (quality management, no ASIL requirement).

Each hazardous event gets a safety goal that inherits its ASIL, such as "no unintended full braking at speed". The technical safety concept names safety mechanisms: plausibility checks, watchdog timers, redundant sensors, end-to-end protection of bus messages and a safe state. ASIL decomposition may split a requirement into two independent ones of lower ASIL, each still tagged with the original, for example ASIL B(D) + ASIL B(D), but the independence must be shown.

The fault tolerant time interval (FTTI) is the minimum time from a fault to a possible hazardous event. The safety mechanism must detect the fault and reach the safe state within it. Hardware is judged by architectural metrics computed from failure rates and the diagnostic coverage of the safety mechanisms: the single-point fault metric SPFM = 1 − (λSPF + λRF) ÷ λ, where λSPF is the rate of single-point faults, λRF the rate of residual faults and λ the total failure rate of the safety-related hardware, and the latent fault metric (LFM) for hidden faults. ASIL D asks for SPFM of at least 99 per cent.

Worked example

Question. A brake-by-wire safety goal has FTTI = 200 ms. Sensor sampling takes 20 ms, a two-frame confirmation takes 40 ms and the actuator needs 30 ms to reach the safe state. How much time is left for the detection logic, and by when must the fault be flagged?

  1. Budget: detection time = FTTI − sampling − confirmation − actuation.
  2. Delays other than detection: 20 + 40 + 30 = 90 ms.
  3. Detection time: 200 − 90 = 110 ms.
  4. The fault must be flagged by 20 + 40 + 110 = 170 ms after it occurs.
  5. The actuator then has 200 − 170 = 30 ms, so the safe state is reached at 200 ms, just inside the FTTI.

Answer. The detection logic has 110 ms; the fault must be flagged within 170 ms, and the actuator uses the last 30 ms.

Worked example 2

Question. HARA rates unintended steering at motorway speed as S3 (life-threatening), E4 (high exposure) and C3 (hard to control), and a second hazard as S1 (light injuries), E4 and C2. Find the ASILs.

  1. Steering: S + E + C = 3 + 4 + 3 = 10, so ASIL D, the highest level.
  2. Second hazard: 1 + 4 + 2 = 7, so ASIL A.
  3. If the steering controllability were C2 instead, the sum would be 9 and the ASIL C.

Answer. Unintended steering is ASIL D; the second hazard is ASIL A.

Exam traps

  • MISRA compliance alone does not give ISO 26262 compliance: MISRA is coding evidence, while ISO 26262 also needs HARA, safety goals, a safety concept, metrics and verification.
  • ASIL D is the highest level, A the lowest; QM is not "ASIL 0" but means ordinary quality management with no ASIL requirement.
  • FTTI is not the detection time: it must hold sensing, confirmation, detection and actuation together.
  • ASIL decomposition does not lower the safety goal: each part keeps the original tag, such as B(D), and independence must be proved.
  • MISRA C:2012 bans the heap functions of stdlib.h outright (Rule 21.3); "no allocation after start-up" is a deviation some projects write, not the rule.

6.8 V2X communication, connected vehicles and infotainment

V2X (vehicle-to-everything) is radio communication between a vehicle and the things around it: other vehicles, roadside units, pedestrians' phones and the cellular network, plus, for an electric car, the power grid. It exists because radar and cameras see only in a line of sight and cannot know what another driver intends; a V2V message can say "I am braking hard" before the brake lights ahead are visible, or warn of a car about to cross a blind junction. The same connected electronics bring live traffic, emergency calls, over-the-air updates and phone projection into the cabin, which is why connected vehicles and infotainment are taught together.

You need gateways, which keep connected units away from safety buses, and the idea of latency from vehicle networking.

V2X links and safety messages

The vehicle carries an on-board unit (OBU), a radio with a small computer. A roadside unit (RSU) is a fixed radio box at a junction, toll plaza or gantry, linked to traffic signals and to a back-end server. The links are:

  • V2V, vehicle to vehicle: position, speed and braking shared directly between OBUs.
  • V2I, vehicle to infrastructure: OBU to RSU, for signal timing, road works and tolling.
  • V2P, vehicle to pedestrian: usually a phone app or a wearable tag; important where two-wheelers and walkers share the road.
  • V2N, vehicle to network: through the cellular network to cloud services.
  • V2G, vehicle to grid: an electric vehicle that charges, or sends power back, under control messages from the grid operator. Here the main flow is power; the messages only manage it.

V2V, V2I and V2P can work directly, with no SIM or tower; only V2N needs cellular coverage. A safety message carries position, speed, heading, brake status and a time stamp, about ten times a second. From these the receiving OBU predicts paths and warns of: forward collision (the car ahead brakes hard), intersection movement (a car about to cross your path at a blind junction), an approaching emergency vehicle, a road hazard (crash, pothole, oil), and GLOSA (green light optimal speed advisory), which suggests a speed that reaches the next signal on green. GLOSA advises the driver; it does not change the signal.

d = vr × (tw + Δt)

d is the distance (m) at which a warning must reach the driver; vr is the closing speed (m/s); tw is the warning time the driver needs (s); Δt is the radio and processing latency (s).

Radio choices, the telematics unit and cloud services

Two radio families compete. IEEE 802.11p (DSRC in the USA, ITS-G5 in Europe) is Wi-Fi adapted for cars in the 5.9 GHz band; it works ad hoc, with no base station or subscription, and is mature. C-V2X (cellular V2X, first LTE-V2X and now 5G NR-V2X) has a sidelink, a direct device-to-device mode (PC5) that works without a tower, plus the normal network link for V2N. Both send small, frequent, signed messages; they differ in radio design and in who builds the roadside network.

The telematics control unit (TCU) is the car's cellular gateway: a modem, a GNSS receiver for position and time, and an embedded SIM. It carries live traffic, remote diagnostics, emergency call after a crash, stolen-vehicle tracking and over-the-air (OTA) updates, where the maker sends new software over the cellular link. The TCU reaches the in-car buses only through the gateway, which keeps cloud traffic away from the powertrain and chassis domains. Some cars combine the V2X OBU and the TCU in one box.

Infotainment, security and connected autonomy

The head unit is the cabin computer behind the central screen. It plays audio and video, runs navigation (GNSS plus map data, with live traffic from the TCU), handles hands-free calls over Bluetooth, and projects a phone with Android Auto or Apple CarPlay: the phone runs the apps, and the head unit gives the screen, touch and speakers. A head-up display (HUD) can show speed, a turn arrow or a V2X warning on the windscreen; its optics belong with the instrument cluster and head-up displays. Media travels from the head unit to amplifier and displays over MOST or Ethernet.

Connection widens the attack surface: the cellular link, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, USB, the OTA server and the diagnostic socket. A false message could fake a brake warning or unlock a door. V2X messages are therefore signed: each carries a digital signature and a certificate from a public-key infrastructure (PKI), so a receiver can check that a real OBU sent it and that it was not altered. A signature proves origin and integrity; it does not hide the content. Short-lived pseudonym certificates protect drivers' privacy, and certificates of misbehaving units can be revoked.

A connected autonomous vehicle uses V2X as one more sensor beside radar (range and speed), camera (lanes, signs) and lidar (a 3-D point cloud). V2X adds what is out of sight and what others intend. The fusion software must weigh a late or false message against its own sensors, never trust it blindly.

Worked example

Question. Two cars approach a blind junction with a closing speed of 40 m/s. The driver needs a warning 2.5 s before the possible collision, and the V2X link plus processing adds 100 ms. At what distance must the warning be sent, and how much of that distance is due to latency?

  1. Warning distance without latency: vr × tw = 40 m/s × 2.5 s = 100 m.
  2. Latency in seconds: Δt = 100 ms = 0.100 s.
  3. Extra distance closed during the latency: 40 m/s × 0.100 s = 4 m.
  4. Required distance: d = 40 × (2.5 + 0.1) = 40 × 2.6 = 104 m.

Answer. The warning must be sent when the cars are 104 m apart; 4 m of that is caused by the 100 ms latency.

Worked example 2

Question. An RSU at a signal tells an approaching car that the light, 250 m ahead, turns green in 20 s and stays green for 15 s. The speed limit is 50 km/h. What speed range should GLOSA advise so that the car arrives on green without stopping?

  1. The car must arrive no earlier than 20 s and no later than 20 + 15 = 35 s from now.
  2. Highest useful speed: 250 m ÷ 20 s = 12.5 m/s = 12.5 × 3.6 = 45 km/h (faster would arrive on red).
  3. Lowest speed: 250 m ÷ 35 s = 7.14 m/s = 7.14 × 3.6 = 25.7 km/h (slower would miss the green).
  4. Both are below the 50 km/h limit, so the whole range is allowed.

Answer. GLOSA should advise about 26 to 45 km/h; any steady speed in that range reaches the signal while it is green.

Exam traps

  • V2X is radio communication, not a sensor that measures anything itself; a V2X car still needs radar and cameras, and V2X adds what they cannot see.
  • V2V, V2I and V2P do not need cellular coverage; only V2N and cloud services stop when there is no tower.
  • 802.11p and C-V2X are different radios: 802.11p is ad hoc Wi-Fi at 5.9 GHz; C-V2X has a direct sidelink and a network mode.
  • A signature is not encryption: it proves who sent the message and that it was not changed, but anyone can still read it.
  • Convert milliseconds to seconds before multiplying by m/s: 100 ms is 0.1 s, so the latency adds 4 m, not 4000 m.

6.9 VANET architecture, applications and routing

A VANET (vehicular ad hoc network) is a wireless network that moving vehicles form among themselves. Links appear as cars come into radio range and vanish as they part; roadside units (RSUs) can add fixed access points, but vehicles can still pass messages car to car where there is none. Because cars move fast but only along roads, and every car knows its own position from satellite navigation, a VANET routes packets by position instead of by stored routes. Some syllabi cover this only briefly.

You need the V2X links, OBUs and RSUs from V2X communication.

Ad hoc networks and the three VANET domains

An ad hoc network has no fixed infrastructure: every node forwards packets for the others, so distant nodes reach each other in several hops. A VANET is a special ad hoc network whose nodes are vehicles, each with an on-board unit (OBU). Its architecture has three domains. The in-vehicle domain is the car's own ECUs and buses, joined to the OBU through the gateway. The ad hoc domain is OBU-to-OBU and OBU-to-RSU radio. The infrastructure domain is the RSUs' wired back-haul, the internet and the servers behind it.

Applications, characteristics and challenges

Road crashes kill about 1.19 million people a year worldwide, according to the WHO's 2023 global status report, most of them in low- and middle-income countries such as India. Safety applications aim at this: collision warning, hazard and crash warning, intersection warning and emergency-vehicle warning. Non-safety applications include traffic information, electronic tolling, parking, fleet tracking and infotainment. Safety messages are short, repeated and urgent; non-safety messages can wait.

VANETs differ from general mobile ad hoc networks (MANETs): nodes move fast, so links last seconds; motion follows roads, so it is predictable; vehicles have plenty of battery power and space; position and time come from GNSS; and the network can be huge in a jam yet broken into pieces on an empty highway at night. Their challenges are frequent link breaks, the broadcast storm (many cars rebroadcasting the same alert and jamming the channel), GNSS errors in tunnels and between tall buildings, security against fake messages, and drivers' privacy.

Routing in a VANET

Topology-based routing finds and stores a path. AODV (ad hoc on-demand distance vector) floods a route request and keeps the route found; DSR (dynamic source routing) puts the whole path in each packet. At road speeds the links break before a route can be repaired, so these work poorly. Position-based routing needs only the positions of the neighbours and of the destination. GPSR (greedy perimeter stateless routing) first uses greedy forwarding: send the packet to the neighbour closest to the destination. If no neighbour is closer than the current node (a void), it switches to perimeter mode: face routing with the right-hand rule on a planarized graph of the radio neighbours (a Gabriel or relative neighbourhood graph), until it reaches a node closer than where greedy forwarding stopped. Delivery is guaranteed only if that graph is connected.

Geocast sends a message to every vehicle inside a target area, such as 1 km behind a crash; it suppresses the broadcast storm by letting only the farthest receiver rebroadcast, or by random waiting. Where no next car exists, store-carry-forward keeps the packet in a car until another car or an RSU comes into range.

Worked example

Question. A packet at a car at (0, 0) m must reach a destination at (100, 0) m. The car's neighbours are A at (40, 10), B at (70, −20) and C at (20, 30). Which neighbour does greedy forwarding choose?

  1. For each neighbour (x, y), distance to the destination d = √((100 − x)² + (0 − y)²).
  2. A: √(60² + 10²) = √(3600 + 100) = √3700 = 60.8 m.
  3. B: √(30² + 20²) = √(900 + 400) = √1300 = 36.1 m.
  4. C: √(80² + 30²) = √(6400 + 900) = √7300 = 85.4 m.
  5. The current car is 100 m from the destination; B is the closest neighbour and is closer than 100 m, so greedy mode continues.

Answer. Greedy forwarding sends the packet to B, 36.1 m from the destination.

Worked example 2

Question. Radio range is 250 m. (a) Two cars on opposite carriageways each drive at 120 km/h. For how long can they exchange messages as they pass? (b) Two cars in the same lane drive at 100 km/h and 80 km/h, starting side by side. How long until the link breaks?

  1. (a) Closing speed = 120 + 120 = 240 km/h = 240 ÷ 3.6 = 66.7 m/s.
  2. They are in range from 250 m before meeting to 250 m after: 500 m of relative travel. Time = 500 ÷ 66.7 = 7.5 s.
  3. (b) Relative speed = 100 − 80 = 20 km/h = 20 ÷ 3.6 = 5.56 m/s.
  4. The link breaks when the gap reaches 250 m: 250 ÷ 5.56 = 45 s.

Answer. (a) about 7.5 s; (b) about 45 s. Links with oncoming traffic are short, so a route through them must be rebuilt every few seconds.

Exam traps

  • VANET nodes are not energy-limited and do not move randomly; MANET assumptions of battery limits and random motion do not hold.
  • Greedy forwarding picks the neighbour closest to the destination, not the one closest to the sender or the farthest away.
  • Perimeter mode uses a planarized graph of radio neighbours, not the road map; delivery is guaranteed only if that graph is connected.
  • Flooding a hazard alert is not free: unchecked rebroadcasting causes the broadcast storm.

6.10 DSRC, IEEE 802.11p and WAVE protocol stack

DSRC (dedicated short-range communications) is the radio service for road safety in the 5.9 GHz band. IEEE 802.11p is its lower layers: Wi-Fi changed for fast-moving cars, with 10 MHz channels and no joining handshake before sending. WAVE (wireless access in vehicular environments, the IEEE 1609 family) sits above 802.11p and handles channel switching, short-message networking and security. Together they let a car broadcast a basic safety message about ten times a second to every car and roadside unit within a few hundred metres. Some syllabi cover this stack only briefly.

You need V2X communication and the routing ideas of VANETs.

The band and IEEE 802.11p

In the USA the DSRC band was 5.850 to 5.925 GHz, seven 10 MHz channels: one control channel (CCH), channel 178, and six service channels (SCH). In 2020 the US regulator gave the lower 45 MHz to unlicensed Wi-Fi and the upper 30 MHz (5.895 to 5.925 GHz) to cellular V2X, so new US systems are moving away from DSRC. Europe's ITS-G5, built on the same 802.11p radio, uses channels between 5.855 and 5.925 GHz.

The 802.11p physical layer uses OFDM, like 802.11a, but with the clock halved: 10 MHz channels instead of 20 MHz, so every symbol is twice as long (8 µs). The longer guard time tolerates the echoes and Doppler shift of a moving car, and the data rates halve to 3 to 27 Mbit/s; 6 Mbit/s is the usual rate for safety messages. The MAC runs outside the context of a basic service set (OCB): a car does not scan, join, authenticate or exchange keys with an access point before sending. It listens for a free channel and broadcasts. EDCA (enhanced distributed channel access) gives four access categories, so safety frames wait a shorter time before sending than ordinary data. The radio is half duplex: it cannot send and receive on one channel at once.

WAVE: the IEEE 1609 layers above 802.11p

  • IEEE 1609.2, security: signs messages with certificates from a public-key infrastructure, and can encrypt them, so a receiver can check the sender and that the message was not changed. It sits beside the other layers and serves them all.
  • IEEE 1609.3, networking services: WSMP (WAVE short message protocol) carries small safety packets with very little header; WAVE service advertisements tell cars which service is on which channel; ordinary internet traffic uses IPv6 with UDP or TCP.
  • IEEE 1609.4, multi-channel operation: a 100 ms sync interval, timed from GNSS, is split into a CCH interval and an SCH interval, normally 50 ms each with a short guard time at each switch. Every single-radio car is on the control channel at the same time, for safety messages and advertisements, and goes to a service channel for other data.
  • IEEE 1609.1, resource manager: an application-level service that let a remote application use an OBU's resources; it was little used and later withdrawn, so most stacks leave it out.

The common safety message is the basic safety message (BSM) of SAE J2735: position, speed, heading, acceleration, brake status and vehicle size, sent about ten times a second, typically 200 to 400 bytes with its security data.

Worked example

Question. A 300-byte BSM is sent at 6 Mbit/s. Ignoring the preamble and MAC header, how long is one transmission, and how many such messages fit into one 50 ms control-channel interval if 10 per cent of the interval is lost to guard time and contention? How many cars, each sending one BSM per 100 ms sync interval, could share the channel at best?

  1. Bits: 300 × 8 = 2400 bits.
  2. Airtime: 2400 ÷ (6 × 10⁶) = 0.0004 s = 0.4 ms.
  3. Usable part of the CCH interval: 50 ms × (1 − 0.10) = 45 ms.
  4. Messages: 45 ÷ 0.4 = 112.5, so 112 whole messages fit.
  5. Each car sends one BSM per 100 ms, which is one per CCH interval, so at best 112 cars can share the channel.

Answer. One BSM takes 0.4 ms; 112 fit in a 45 ms usable interval, so about 112 cars at best; preambles, headers and collisions lower this in a real jam.

Exam traps

  • 802.11p uses 10 MHz channels, not the 20 MHz of 802.11a, so its symbols are twice as long and its rates are half.
  • WAVE is not another name for 802.11p: 802.11p is only the MAC and physical layer; WAVE is the IEEE 1609 layers above it.
  • The 100 ms sync interval is the channel-switching rhythm of 1609.4; it does not mean only one BSM fits in it.
  • 3 to 27 Mbit/s is the raw physical rate; delivered throughput in a crowded channel is much lower.

Takeaways

  1. A bus replaces n(n − 1)/2 point-to-point links with one shared pair; the price is that bandwidth, latency, determinism and bus load become design limits.
  2. Describe any network by topology, organization, access method, OSI layers and class; vehicle buses define only layers 1 and 2 and leave transport and application to other standards.
  3. In CAN the dominant 0 wins, so the lowest identifier wins arbitration with no lost bits; an 8-byte frame takes 222 to 270 µs at 500 kbit/s.
  4. CAN FD keeps arbitration and speeds up only the data phase, carrying up to 64 bytes; a classic node cannot stay on an FD bus.
  5. LIN is cheap and polled, FlexRay is time-triggered on two channels, and Ethernet is switched and fast; each fills a cost, speed and timing niche.
  6. MOST carries media in a ring clocked by a timing master at 44.1 or 48 kHz; streams take fixed bytes in every frame.
  7. The central gateway routes, repacks, filters and times signals between all buses and the OBD socket, and every crossing adds delay.
  8. OBD-II fixes the socket, services, PIDs and DTC format; scale every PID with its formula and remember the two-trip rule for the MIL.
  9. MISRA C makes the code analysable; ISO 26262 turns hazards into ASILs, safety goals and evidence; neither replaces the other.
  10. V2X, VANET routing and DSRC timing are all numbers questions: warning distance, contact time, greedy distances and BSM airtime.

Exam question bank

Understand Explain why modern vehicles exchange data over buses instead of point-to-point wiring. Describe cross-system functions and the requirements a vehicle bus system must meet, with a calculation. 13 marks

Points to cover. Point-to-point links grow as n(n − 1)/2; Bus: one shared pair, one transceiver per ECU, numbered messages; Cross-system functions: traction control, shared wheel speed, gear-shift torque dip, crash unlock; Domains and the gateway; Requirements: data rate, latency, determinism, reliability, fault tolerance, EMC, cost, sleep and wake, expandability, diagnosis; Bus load U = Σ(bits ÷ period) ÷ bit rate with a margin.

Model answer.

  1. Definitions. An ECU (electronic control unit) is a small computer that reads sensors and drives actuators. A signal is one quantity (engine speed); a message or frame is one packet on a bus that carries several signals. In point-to-point wiring each signal has its own wire from the unit that measures it to every unit that uses it; on a serial data bus the measuring unit sends the value once and every unit that needs it reads it (multiplexing).
  2. Why point-to-point wiring fails. To join n units directly to each other needs L = n(n − 1)/2 links, each with a driver at both ends. A bus needs one twisted pair and one transceiver per unit:
    ECUs nPoint-to-point links n(n − 1)/2Line drivers 2LBus transceivers
    615306
    10459010
    126613212
  3. So with 10 ECUs the bus saves 45 − 1 = 44 cable runs and 90 − 10 = 80 driver circuits. Fewer wires mean a lighter, cheaper harness with fewer connectors to corrode, and a new unit can be added by tapping the existing pair. Draw the two sketches: a hexagon of six ECUs with 15 crossing lines, and the same six ECUs hanging from one pair with a 120 Ω terminator at each end.
  4. Cross-system functions. Many features need more than one ECU, and only a network makes them practical: (a) traction control, where the ABS ECU sees a spinning wheel and asks the engine ECU to cut torque; (b) wheel speed measured once by the ABS ECU and used by the speedometer, navigation, electric power steering and gearbox; (c) a torque dip requested by the gearbox ECU during each shift; (d) crash unlock, where the airbag ECU tells the body ECU to unlock the doors and flash the hazard lamps; (e) adaptive cruise, where the radar ECU commands engine torque and brake pressure.
  5. Domains. ECUs are grouped into powertrain, chassis, body and comfort, infotainment and diagnostics; each domain gets a bus suited to it, and a gateway joins them and forwards only the messages that must cross.
  6. Requirements for a bus system: (1) data rate, from about 20 kbit/s for doors to 100 Mbit/s for cameras; (2) latency, the delay from send to receive; (3) determinism, a guaranteed worst case, needed by brakes and steering; (4) reliability: CRC, acknowledgement and fault confinement so one bad node cannot block the bus; (5) fault tolerance after a broken wire; (6) electromagnetic compatibility; (7) low cost per node; (8) sleep and wake-up so a parked car draws little current; (9) expandability; (10) diagnostic access from the workshop socket.
  7. Bus load calculation. A 500 kbit/s powertrain bus carries 25 messages of 125 bits, each every 10 ms:
    bits per second = 25 × 125 ÷ 0.010 = 312 500 bit/s
U = 312 500 ÷ 500 000 = 0.625 = 62.5 %
  8. 62.5 per cent is inside a design limit of about 70 per cent but leaves little room. Adding five more such messages gives 30 × 125 ÷ 0.010 = 375 000 bit/s, U = 75 per cent, over the limit, so the designer would lengthen some periods, move messages to another bus or use CAN FD.
  9. Check and conclusion. 12 × 11/2 = 66 and 25 × 125 = 3125 bits per 10 ms agree with the figures above. A bus trades a heavy harness for a shared resource, so its rate, latency, determinism and load must be designed, and the gateway keeps the domains apart.

Understand Describe the CAN data frame field by field and explain bit-wise arbitration, bit stuffing and error handling. Calculate the length and duration of an 8-byte frame at 500 kbit/s. How does CAN FD differ? 13 marks

Points to cover. Physical layer: CAN_H and CAN_L, dominant and recessive levels, 120 Ω terminations; Frame: SOF, identifier, RTR, IDE, r0, DLC, data, CRC, ACK, EOF, interframe space; Wired-AND arbitration, lowest identifier wins, non-destructive; Bit stuffing after five equal bits; Five error types, error frame, TEC and REC limits, bus-off; Frame length 111 bits, worst case 135; 222 to 270 µs; CAN FD: RRS, FDF, BRS, ESI, DLC codes to 64 bytes, longer CRC.

Model answer.

  1. Physical layer. Each node has a controller and a transceiver on a twisted pair, CAN_H and CAN_L, terminated by 120 Ω at each end (60 Ω measured across the pair). Recessive (logic 1): both wires about 2.5 V. Dominant (logic 0): CAN_H about 3.5 V, CAN_L about 1.5 V. A dominant bit from any node overrides recessive bits from all others (wired-AND).
  2. Frame fields (draw them as a strip):
    FieldBitsJob
    SOF1dominant start bit; synchronises all nodes
    Identifier11 (29 extended)content and priority
    RTR1data or remote frame
    IDE, r01 + 1standard format; reserved
    DLC40 to 8 data bytes
    Data0 to 64signals
    CRC + delimiter15 + 1error check
    ACK slot + delimiter1 + 1receivers that found the frame correct drive the slot dominant
    EOF7recessive end
    Interframe space3gap
  3. Arbitration. All waiting nodes send SOF together and then their identifiers, most significant bit first, reading the bus after each bit. A node that sends recessive 1 but reads dominant 0 has lost; it stops at once and becomes a receiver. Example: 0x123 (001 0010 0011) against 0x121 (001 0010 0001): the bits agree down to bit 2; at bit 1, 0x123 sends 1 and 0x121 sends 0, so 0x121 wins. Because nothing of the winner is damaged, the scheme is non-destructive and the lowest identifier has the highest priority.
  4. Bit stuffing. After five equal bits the transmitter adds one opposite bit, from SOF to the end of the CRC; receivers remove it. This keeps edges for resynchronisation.
  5. Error handling. Each node checks for bit, stuff, CRC, form and ACK errors. On an error it sends an error frame of six dominant bits; all nodes discard the frame and the sender repeats it. Error counters (TEC, REC) rise on errors and fall on good frames: error active up to 127, error passive above 127, bus-off when TEC exceeds 255. This fault confinement stops a faulty node from blocking the bus.
  6. Frame length and duration for 8 data bytes at 500 kbit/s:
    fixed bits SOF..EOF = 1+11+1+1+1+4+15+1+1+1+7 = 44
with data:       44 + 64 = 108 bits;  + 3 IFS = 111 bits
stuffed region   1+11+1+1+1+4+64+15 = 98 bits
worst stuffing   (98 − 1) ÷ 4 = 24.25 → 24 bits  → 135 bits
bit time         1 ÷ 500 000 = 2 µs
duration         111 × 2 = 222 µs ;  135 × 2 = 270 µs
data efficiency  64 ÷ 111 = 57.7 %
  7. CAN FD. Arbitration and ACK stay the same. The RTR position becomes RRS (always dominant, no remote frames); then FDF (recessive: FD format), a reserved bit, BRS (recessive: switch the data phase to the fast rate, for example 2 Mbit/s) and ESI (error state of the sender). DLC 9 to 15 codes 12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 48 and 64 bytes. The CRC is 17 bits up to 16 bytes and 21 bits above. Classic controllers cannot share an FD bus, because they see the recessive FDF bit as a form error and destroy the frame.
  8. Check: 108 bits from SOF to EOF plus the 3-bit gap gives 111; at 2 µs a bit, 222 µs, so a fully loaded 500 kbit/s bus could carry at most about 4500 such frames per second.
Understand Compare LIN, FlexRay and automotive Ethernet on topology, data rate, access method, determinism and typical use, with a frame-time calculation for LIN. 13 marks

Points to cover. LIN: one wire, master and up to 15 slaves, 1 to 20 kbit/s, schedule table, header and response; LIN frame: break, sync, PID with parity, data, checksum; T = (34 + 10(n + 1))τ, Tₘₐₓ = 1.4T; FlexRay: two channels at 10 Mbit/s, bus or star, static TDMA and dynamic minislots, symbol window, NIT; Ethernet: 100BASE-T1 and 1000BASE-T1 on one pair, switched star, VLAN, SOME/IP, TSN, 10BASE-T1S; Comparison table and where each is used.

Model answer.

  1. LIN. One signal wire and ground at battery voltage; a master and up to 15 slaves at 1 to 20 kbit/s (often 19.2 kbit/s). The master's schedule table fixes which frame is sent in each slot. For each slot the master sends a header (break of at least 13 dominant bits, sync byte 0x55, protected identifier of 6 ID bits plus 2 parity bits) and the publishing node sends the response (1 to 8 data bytes and a checksum). No arbitration is needed because only the master starts frames.
  2. LIN frame time for 2 and 8 data bytes at 19.2 kbit/s (τ = 52.08 µs):
    n = 2:  bits = 34 + 10 × 3 = 64    T = 64 × 52.08 µs = 3.33 ms   Tₘₐₓ = 1.4 × 3.33 = 4.67 ms
n = 8:  bits = 34 + 10 × 9 = 124   T = 124 × 52.08 µs = 6.46 ms  Tₘₐₓ = 1.4 × 6.46 = 9.04 ms
  3. So a 10 ms slot is enough even for the longest frame, and a schedule of five 10 ms slots repeats each frame every 50 ms.
  4. FlexRay. Two independent channels, A and B, each 10 Mbit/s, wired as a bus, an active star or a mix. Time is split into communication cycles (typically 1 to 5 ms): a static segment of equal slots, each owned by one node (TDMA); a dynamic segment of minislots in which a node with data stretches its slot (FTDMA, lower numbers first); a symbol window for symbols such as CAS and MTS; and network idle time for clock correction. A static message has a worst-case delay of one cycle and can be sent on both channels for redundancy.
  5. Automotive Ethernet. 100BASE-T1 (100 Mbit/s) and 1000BASE-T1 (1 Gbit/s) send full duplex over one unshielded twisted pair, so the harness is light; office Ethernet needs two pairs (100BASE-TX) or four (1000BASE-T). Each link joins two devices, so the network is a star of switches with no collisions. VLANs separate traffic, SOME/IP gives service calls, DoIP speeds diagnostics, and AVB/TSN adds time sync and scheduled traffic for bounded delay. 10BASE-T1S lets several nodes share one short pair.
  6. Comparison:

    Topologybus, one wirebus or star, 2 channelsswitched star, one pair per link
    Rateup to 20 kbit/s10 Mbit/s per channel100 Mbit/s or 1 Gbit/s
    Accessmaster pollsTDMA + minislotsswitched full duplex
    Determinismfixed by scheduleguaranteed in static segmentbest effort, bounded with TSN
    Usedoors, mirrors, seatssuspension, steer- and brake-by-wirecameras, infotainment, backbone
    Cost per nodelowesthighmedium to high
  7. Time to move a 1000-byte block (8000 bits), ignoring overhead:
    LIN 19.2 kbit/s:  8000 ÷ 19 200 = 416.7 ms

  8. Conclusion: the three are not rivals but layers of one network. LIN keeps cheap modules off CAN, FlexRay gives guaranteed timing for x-by-wire, and Ethernet carries bulk data; gateways join them. Check: 124 bits at 52.08 µs is 6.458 ms, and 416.7 ms ÷ 0.08 ms ≈ 5200 times between LIN and Ethernet.
Understand Explain the MOST bus: its topology, the role of the timing master, the frame structure and its channels. Then explain how the networks of a car are coupled through gateways, with an example of a networked vehicle. 13 marks

Points to cover. MOST ring of up to 64 devices, plastic optical fibre, red LED; MOST50 and MOST150 also on copper; Timing master sends frames at 44.1 or 48 kHz; slaves recover the clock; MOST25 frame 64 bytes: 60 data bytes split synchronous and asynchronous, 2 control bytes; Channel rates from bytes per frame × 8 × f; Repeater, bridge, router, gateway by OSI layer; Central gateway jobs and crossing delay; Example vehicle network.

Model answer.

  1. MOST (Media Oriented Systems Transport) is a bus for infotainment: audio, video, navigation and phone. Up to 64 devices form a ring; each receives from the previous device and sends to the next. MOST25 uses plastic optical fibre with a 1 mm core and a red LED, which is light, cheap and immune to electrical noise; MOST50 and MOST150 can also use copper. Draw a ring of head unit, amplifier, navigation and rear display with arrows in one direction.
  2. Timing master. One device, usually the head unit, sends frames continuously at the system frequency, 44.1 kHz or 48 kHz. All other devices are timing slaves: they recover the clock from the frames, take out their bytes, insert their own and pass the frame on. So every stream is locked to one clock and audio arrives one sample per frame without buffering. A single broken fibre stops the ring, so MOST has a ring break diagnosis.
  3. Frame and channels (MOST25). One frame is 64 bytes = 512 bits: a header byte, 60 data bytes, 2 control bytes and a trailer byte. The boundary descriptor splits the 60 data bytes into a synchronous channel (byte positions reserved for each stream, such as 4 bytes for 16-bit stereo audio) and an asynchronous channel for packet data such as map tiles. The 2 control bytes form the control channel for commands and status; a control message is spread over 16 frames.
  4. Channel budget at 44.1 kHz with a stereo radio stream and a mono navigation voice:
    gross rate       64 × 8 × 44 100 = 22.58 Mbit/s (about 23 Mbit/s)
stereo 16-bit    2 × 2 bytes = 4 bytes/frame → 4 × 8 × 44 100 = 1.411 Mbit/s
mono 16-bit      2 bytes/frame → 705.6 kbit/s
synchronous used 4 + 2 = 6 bytes; asynchronous left 60 − 6 = 54 bytes
asynchronous     54 × 8 × 44 100 = 19.05 Mbit/s
control channel  2 × 8 × 44 100 = 705.6 kbit/s
  5. MOST50 uses 1024-bit frames and MOST150 3072-bit frames (about 150 Mbit/s at 48 kHz, with an Ethernet channel).
  6. Coupling of networks, by OSI layer: a repeater (layer 1) regenerates the signal; a bridge (layer 2) joins segments of one protocol; a router (layer 3) chooses paths between networks; a gateway (up to layer 7) joins different protocols, rates and formats. Car buses differ, so they are coupled by gateways, usually one central gateway.
  7. Gateway jobs: (1) message routing by an identifier table; (2) signal routing, unpacking signals and repacking them into new frames; (3) rate adaptation and buffering between fast and slow buses; (4) filtering, so infotainment can never send chassis frames; (5) diagnostic routing from the OBD socket to every ECU; (6) network management, waking and sleeping the buses.
  8. Crossing delay, 111-bit frame from 500 kbit/s powertrain CAN to 125 kbit/s body CAN with 0.20 ms processing:
    in   111 ÷ 500 000 = 0.222 ms
out  111 ÷ 125 000 = 0.888 ms
total = 0.222 + 0.20 + 0.888 = 1.31 ms
  9. Example of a networked vehicle: powertrain CAN at 500 kbit/s (engine, gearbox), chassis CAN or FlexRay (ABS and ESP, steering, suspension), body CAN at 125 kbit/s with LIN sub-buses (doors, seats, climate), a MOST ring or Ethernet for infotainment, and the OBD socket, all on the central gateway. Draw this star of buses round the gateway with the MOST ring on one side. Check: 6 + 54 = 60 data bytes, and 0.222 + 0.888 + 0.20 = 1.31 ms.
Remember Explain the OBD-II system: the connector and its pins, the communication protocols, the diagnostic services and PIDs, the DTC format, the readiness monitors and the MIL. Decode an engine-speed reply. 13 marks

Points to cover. Emissions law; J1962 16-pin socket within reach of the driver; Pins 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 10, 14, 15, 16; Five protocols; CAN with 11- or 29-bit IDs at 250 or 500 kbit/s; Services 01 to 0A; PIDs and scaling; DTC: letter, generic or maker digit, subsystem, fault number; two-byte coding; Monitors, drive cycle, pending code, two-trip rule, MIL, clearing.

Model answer.

  1. Purpose. OBD-II is an emissions-diagnostics standard: one socket, one set of messages and one code format, so any scan tool can read any compliant car. It became law for petrol cars in the USA from 1996 and in Europe as EOBD from 2001; in India BS-VI Phase 2 (April 2023) brought full OBD-II monitoring.
  2. Connector (SAE J1962): 16 pins in two rows of eight, within reach of the driver. Draw it with these pins labelled:
    PinFunction
    2 / 10SAE J1850 bus + / −
    4 / 5chassis ground / signal ground
    6 / 14CAN high / CAN low
    7 / 15K-line / L-line (ISO 9141-2, ISO 14230)
    16battery positive, fused
  3. Protocols. Five were allowed: SAE J1850 PWM and VPW, ISO 9141-2, ISO 14230 (KWP2000) and ISO 15765-4 (CAN). CAN is required on US cars from model year 2008, with 11-bit or 29-bit identifiers at 500 or 250 kbit/s (most cars: 11-bit, 500 kbit/s).
  4. Services (the reply carries the service number + 0x40):
    ServiceJob
    01live data
    02freeze frame
    03stored (confirmed) codes
    04clear codes, lamp off
    05oxygen sensor tests (not on CAN)
    06monitor test results
    07pending codes
    08control of a test

    0Apermanent codes
  5. PIDs. Each live value has a parameter identifier and a scaling formula: PID 04 load = A × 100 ÷ 255 %, PID 05 coolant = A − 40 °C, PID 0C speed = (256A + B) ÷ 4 rpm, PID 0D vehicle speed = A km/h.
  6. Decode: request 01 0C, reply 41 0C 0C 80.
    41 = 01 + 40, 0C = PID → A = 0x0C = 12, B = 0x80 = 128
count = 256 × 12 + 128 = 3072 + 128 = 3200
speed = 3200 ÷ 4 = 800 rpm (a normal idle)
  7. DTC format. A letter and four digits: P powertrain, C chassis, B body, U network; first digit 0 generic, 1 maker-specific; for P codes the second digit is the subsystem (3 ignition and misfire) and the last two the fault. P0301 is a generic misfire in cylinder 1. On the bus a DTC is two bytes: the top 2 bits give the letter, the next 2 the first digit, then three 4-bit digits; bytes 03 01 decode to P0301.
  8. Monitors and lamp. Continuous monitors (misfire, fuel system, components) and non-continuous ones (catalyst, oxygen sensor and heater, evaporative system, EGR) run during a drive cycle; readiness status shows which have completed. A first failure stores a pending code (service 07). If the fault returns in the next drive cycle (two-trip rule), the code is confirmed, a freeze frame is stored and the MIL comes on; a catalyst-damaging misfire flashes it at once. Three good drive cycles turn the lamp off. Service 04 clears codes and resets readiness, but an unrepaired fault returns, and permanent codes (0A) stay until the monitor passes.
  9. Check: 0x0C80 = 3200 and 3200 ÷ 4 = 800 rpm; the pin list has ground (4, 5), power (16) and CAN (6, 14) as the examiner expects.

This supports ISO 26262 part 6 by making the code analysable and its timing predictable, which the FTTI argument needs. Tools such as the compiler and code generator must be qualified.
  8. What each part proves: ISO 26262 provides the structure (HARA, safety goal, concept, metrics, verification, safety case and independent assessment); FlexRay provides the timing and communication redundancy; MISRA C provides code-level evidence. None is sufficient alone: MISRA code on CAN without a timing proof fails the FTTI argument, and FlexRay with badly written code can still fail.
  9. Verdict. The combination can support an ASIL D claim, provided the FTTI margin is increased, the SPFM stays above 99 per cent and the independence of the two channels is demonstrated. Check: 3 + 4 + 3 = 10, 5 + 10 + 15 + 20 = 50 ms and 1 − 3/400 = 99.25 per cent.

Glossary

Arbitration
The bit-wise contest at the start of a CAN frame that the lowest identifier wins.
ASIL
Automotive safety integrity level, A (lowest) to D (highest), from hazard analysis.
Automotive Ethernet
Switched Ethernet on one twisted pair (100BASE-T1, 1000BASE-T1) for cameras and backbones.
Bit stuffing
Adding one opposite bit after five equal bits so that receivers stay synchronised.
Bridge
A device that joins two segments of the same protocol at the data link layer.
BSM
Basic safety message: position, speed, heading and braking, sent about ten times a second.
Bus
A single shared medium on which several nodes exchange messages.
Bus load
The fraction of time a bus is busy carrying frames.
Bus-off
The CAN state of a node whose transmit error counter has passed 255; it stops sending.
CAN FD
CAN with a faster data phase and up to 64 data bytes, keeping CAN's arbitration.
CSMA
Carrier sense multiple access: a node sends when it finds the medium idle.
Determinism
A guarantee that the worst-case message delay is known in advance.
Dominant bit
The CAN logic 0, which overrides a recessive bit on the bus.
DSRC
Dedicated short-range communications, the 5.9 GHz radio service for road safety.
DTC
Diagnostic trouble code: a letter and four digits naming a fault.
ECU
Electronic control unit: a small computer that reads sensors, drives actuators and talks on the bus.
FlexRay
A dual-channel, time-triggered bus at 10 Mbit/s per channel for chassis and x-by-wire systems.
FTTI
Fault tolerant time interval: the minimum time from a fault to a possible hazardous event.
Gateway
An ECU that joins buses of different protocols and decides which signals cross, up to OSI layer 7.
ISO-TP
The transport protocol (ISO 15765-2) that cuts long diagnostic messages into CAN frames.
LIN
Local Interconnect Network: a single-wire master-slave bus up to 20 kbit/s for body modules.
MIL
Malfunction indicator lamp, the engine warning lamp lit for confirmed emissions faults.
Minislot
A short time unit of the FlexRay dynamic segment that a node stretches when it sends.
MISRA C
Coding guidelines that remove risky constructs from embedded C.
MOST
Media Oriented Systems Transport: a synchronous ring, often optical, for audio, video and infotainment.
Multiplexing
Sending many signals over one medium in turn, instead of one wire per signal.
Node
Any device on a network that can send or receive, such as an ECU or the diagnostic socket.
OBD-II
The standard 16-pin socket, protocols, services and fault codes for emissions diagnostics.
OBU and RSU
On-board unit (the V2X radio in a car) and roadside unit (a fixed V2X radio).
OSI reference model
A seven-layer model of communication, from the physical layer to the application layer.
PID
Parameter identifier: the number of one live value in OBD-II service 01.
Recessive bit
The CAN logic 1, the idle level that any dominant bit overrides.
Schedule table
The LIN master's fixed list of frames and their time slots.
Signal
One quantity, such as engine speed, carried inside a message; one message usually holds several signals.
TDMA
Time-division multiple access: each node owns fixed time slots.
Timing master
The MOST device that generates the frames and the clock for the whole ring.
Topology
The wiring shape of a network: bus, star, ring, daisy chain or a hybrid.
V2X
Vehicle-to-everything radio communication: V2V, V2I, V2P, V2N and V2G.
VANET
Vehicular ad hoc network: a wireless network formed by moving vehicles.
WAVE
Wireless access in vehicular environments, the IEEE 1609 layers above 802.11p.
