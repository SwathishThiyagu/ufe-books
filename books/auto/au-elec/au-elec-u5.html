5.1 Electronic transmission control

An automatic gearbox still has gears; electronics decide when to change and how hard to press the clutches. The transmission control unit (TCU) reads throttle position, vehicle speed and engine speed, looks up the best gear in a shift map, and then commands solenoid valves that set hydraulic pressure. That pressure fills a clutch pack, so the new gear engages in a fraction of a second without a jolt. The same loop runs automated manuals, dual-clutch boxes, belt CVTs and conventional automatics. This chapter shows the loop, the hydraulics, and the four layouts.

You need: Unit 4 for throttle, engine torque and power, and Unit 3 for speed and position sensors.

The shift map and the shift schedule

The TCU needs two numbers before it can choose a gear: throttle opening α (percent, 0 means closed, 100 means wide open) and vehicle speed v (km/h, from the output shaft sensor or the wheel speed sensors).

Plot α on the vertical axis and v on the horizontal axis. Draw one curve for each shift. The region to the right of the 2→3 curve means the car is fast enough for third. The region to the left means drop back to second. The two boundaries are deliberately separated, so the box does not jump 2–3–2–3 at one steady speed. That separation is hysteresis, the same trick a thermostat uses.

Light throttle keeps the curves at low speed, so the car upshifts early and the engine stays slow and economical. Heavy throttle pushes the curves to high speed, so the engine is held near its power peak. Pressing the accelerator past a set fraction forces a downshift called kickdown. The family of curves for every gear is the shift schedule; the table stored inside the TCU is the shift map.

Every decision rests on one relation:

n_engine = n_wheel × i_gear × i_final

n_engine is engine speed in rpm; n_wheel is wheel speed in rpm; i_gear is the selected gear ratio; i_final is the final drive ratio. Both ratios are dimensionless.

An overdrive gear has i_gear below 1, so it lowers engine speed. A low gear has i_gear above 1 and multiplies torque.

Hydraulics, pressure and shift quality

A shift is hydraulic work. The TCU cannot push a clutch; it can only change a current.

  1. Pressure stage. A regulator holds line pressure, the supply for the whole hydraulic circuit. A variable-force solenoid trims it. Line pressure must rise with engine torque, because a clutch carrying more torque needs more clamping force or it slips.
  2. Fill stage. A shift solenoid opens and oil fills the piston of the on-coming clutch until the plates touch. This travel is the clutch fill; its time depends on oil temperature, oil age and clearance.
  3. Torque stage. The off-going clutch releases while the on-coming clutch takes the load. Too early and the engine flares; too late and the car jerks.
  4. Torque reduction. The TCU asks the engine controller for a short torque cut, retarded spark or a small throttle trim, just as the ratio changes. The shift finishes faster and the passengers feel less of a step.
  5. Learning. Speed sensors on the input and output shafts measure the real shift time. If it is longer than target, the TCU lengthens the fill pulse or raises pressure on the next shift. That is adaptive learning, and it repeats for the life of the box.

Four layouts, one control loop

The same loop drives four different mechanisms.

  • AMT (automated manual). A normal manual gearbox with a robot that works the clutch and the gear lever. Cheap and efficient, but torque is interrupted, so the car nods.
  • DCT (dual-clutch transmission). Two clutches, one for odd gears and one for even. The next gear is already engaged, so the shift is a clutch-to-clutch handover with no torque break.
  • CVT (continuously variable transmission). A steel belt runs between two pulleys whose widths are set by hydraulic pressure. There are no steps; the TCU chooses the engine speed it wants.
  • Conventional automatic. Planetary gearsets with clutch packs, usually behind a torque converter.

The torque converter is an engine-driven impeller throwing oil at a turbine on the gearbox input, so the car can creep at idle with no mechanical link. It multiplies torque while the speed difference is large. At cruising speed a lock-up clutch closes inside the converter, tying input to engine and removing the slip loss. The lock-up clutch is a plain on/off clutch; the shift clutches do the ratio work.

Worked example

Question. A car runs at 60 km/h in top gear. Wheel radius r = 0.30 m, final drive ratio 3.50, top gear ratio 0.70. Find (a) wheel speed in rpm, (b) engine speed in top gear, (c) engine speed after a downshift to a direct gear of ratio 1.00, and (d) the rise in engine speed.

  1. Convert speed to SI: v = 60 ÷ 3.6 = 16.67 m/s.
  2. Wheel circumference = 2πr = 2 × π × 0.30 = 1.885 m.
  3. Wheel rpm = (v ÷ circumference) × 60 = (16.67 ÷ 1.885) × 60 = 8.842 × 60 = 530.5 rpm.
  4. Engine speed in top gear = 530.5 × 3.50 × 0.70 = 530.5 × 2.45 = 1299.8 rpm, about 1300 rpm.
  5. Engine speed in the direct gear = 530.5 × 3.50 × 1.00 = 1856.8 rpm, about 1857 rpm.
  6. Rise = 1857 − 1300 = 557 rpm.

Answer. Wheel speed 530.5 rpm, engine speed 1300 rpm in top gear and 1857 rpm in the direct gear, a rise of 557 rpm.

Exam traps

  • Leaving out the final drive when converting wheel speed to engine speed. Engine rpm needs both ratios: gear ratio and final drive.
  • Assuming a shift happens at a fixed vehicle speed. The map also reads throttle, so a light throttle upshifts early and a heavy one holds the gear.
  • Getting the ratio direction wrong. i_gear above 1 reduces speed and multiplies torque; an overdrive below 1 does the opposite.
  • Confusing the lock-up clutch inside the torque converter with the shift clutches. Lock-up only removes converter slip; the shift clutches change the ratio.
  • Mixing rpm and rad/s, or forgetting the ×60 and ÷60 in the wheel-speed conversion.

Clutch torque capacity grows with clamping force, and clamping force comes from hydraulic pressure. If engine torque exceeds capacity, the clutch slips, burns and the shift time drifts.

The TCU moves the pulley ratio continuously instead of stepping gears, so it can hold the engine at the speed of best torque or best fuel consumption while vehicle speed rises.

5.2 Cruise control and adaptive cruise control

Cruise control keeps a car at a speed the driver chooses, without the driver holding the pedal. It measures actual speed, compares it with the set speed, and moves the throttle through a controller. Adaptive cruise control (ACC) adds a forward sensor: it also measures the gap to the car ahead and slows or brakes to keep a safe time gap. Used on highways, in stop-and-go traffic, and in many new cars sold in India with radar or camera systems.

You need Unit 4 for how throttle changes engine torque, Unit 3 for radar and speed sensors, and 5.1 for how a controller commands an actuator.

Basic cruise control

Basic cruise control holds a driver-chosen speed. The driver presses SET above a minimum speed, often around 30 km/h. The controller stores the set speed v_set. RESUME brings back the stored speed after a cancel. CANCEL or a tap on the brake ends control. COAST lowers the stored speed without using the pedal. Actual speed v comes from wheel speed sensors.

e = v_set − v; u = Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt

e is speed error in m/s; v_set is set speed in m/s; v is actual speed in m/s; u is throttle command (0 to 100 %); Kp is proportional gain; Ki is integral gain; t is time in s.

The P term gives an immediate response. The I term sums error over time. On a hill, a plain P controller leaves a steady error: uphill needs more throttle, and the only way a P controller makes more throttle is to keep a positive error. The integral keeps adding while error exists, until error returns to zero. On downhill, the integral unwinds. If the car still goes faster than set, basic cruise cannot brake; it has throttle authority only.

Adaptive cruise control

ACC adds a forward sensor, usually a 77 GHz radar or a camera, and a gap loop. Radar measures distance d in metres to the lead car and relative speed. Target selection picks the nearest car in the host lane with the smallest time gap; the camera helps identify lanes.

t_gap = d ÷ v;   d_target = t_target × v;   e_gap = d − d_target;   closing rate = v − v_lead

t_gap is time gap in s; d is measured distance to lead car in m; v is host speed in m/s; t_target is selected time gap in s; d_target is desired distance in m; e_gap is gap error in m; v_lead is lead car speed in m/s.

Time gap is in seconds, not metres. The driver selects t_target, often 1.5 to 2.5 s. At a higher speed the same time gap needs a larger distance, so d_target grows with v. A positive e_gap means the host is farther than target; a negative e_gap means it is closer and must slow. Closing rate is positive when the gap is shrinking.

The controller combines speed error and gap error. If the gap is large, it follows set speed. As the gap falls, it reduces speed; if needed it applies the service brakes through ESP (5.4). Basic cruise has no brake authority. A car cutting in makes d drop suddenly; the controller brakes, but if the cut-in is too close the driver must brake. Stop-and-go ACC can brake to zero and restart. The brake pedal always cancels or overrides; the accelerator can also override. Radar processing is in 5.8; PI tuning is in 5.9.

Worked example

Question. A host car is set to 25 m/s. Its 77 GHz radar sees a lead car in the same lane travelling at 20 m/s at a distance of 60 m. The driver has selected a 2.0 s time gap. (a) Find the present time gap. (b) Find the target distance. (c) Find the gap error. (d) Find the closing rate. (e) At the present speeds, how long before the gap would become zero if nothing changed? (f) What average deceleration removes the 5 m/s closing speed over that time?

  1. Present time gap: t_gap = d ÷ v_host = 60 m ÷ 25 m/s = 2.4 s.
  2. Target distance: d_target = t_target × v_host = 2.0 s × 25 m/s = 50 m.
  3. Gap error: e_gap = d − d_target = 60 m − 50 m = +10 m. The host is 10 m farther than target.
  4. Closing rate: v_host − v_lead = 25 m/s − 20 m/s = 5 m/s. Positive, so the gap is shrinking.
  5. Time to zero gap at present speeds: t = d ÷ closing rate = 60 m ÷ 5 m/s = 12 s. The target gap itself is reached earlier, in (60 − 50) ÷ 5 = 2 s.
  6. Average deceleration: a = closing rate ÷ t = 5 m/s ÷ 12 s = 0.4167 ≈ 0.42 m/s².

Answer. The time gap is 2.4 s, target distance 50 m, gap error +10 m, closing rate 5 m/s, time to zero gap 12 s, and average deceleration 0.42 m/s².

Exam traps

  • Basic cruise control cannot brake; it only moves the throttle. On a long downhill the car can exceed set speed.
  • Time gap is in seconds, not metres. The target distance changes with speed: 2.0 s is 50 m at 25 m/s but 40 m at 20 m/s.
  • Set speed is not actual speed while the loop is catching up. The error is v_set − v, not v_set alone.
  • Gap error sign matters. Positive e_gap means the host is farther than target, not closer.
  • The brake pedal always overrides ACC. ACC brake force comes through the service brakes and ESP, not from a separate pedal.

5.3 Electronic and adaptive power steering

Electric power steering (EPS) replaces the hydraulic pump, belt and fluid with an electric motor that pushes the steering only when the driver asks. A torque sensor measures how hard the driver turns; a speed signal says how fast the car is going; the ECU (electronic control unit) looks up an assist map and sets motor current. Parking needs a large assist, highway driving a small one. EPS is common on new cars in India, from hatchbacks to SUVs, and it provides the steering actuator for lane-keeping in ADAS.

You need: torque and angle sensors and the Hall effect from Unit 3; the 12 V supply and motor current from Unit 1.

From hydraulic to electric: where the motor sits

Hydraulic power steering always drives a pump from the engine belt. The pump circulates oil even when the wheel is straight, so it costs fuel. A rotary valve sends pressure to one side of the rack when the driver turns. EPS removes the pump, the belt and the fluid. An electric motor provides torque only when the driver moves the wheel, so straight-line running draws almost no steering power.

There are three common layouts. In column EPS the motor sits on the steering column and drives it through a worm gear; this is compact and suits small cars. In pinion EPS the motor turns a second pinion on the rack, while the driver turns the first pinion; this is the dual-pinion layout. In rack EPS the motor turns a ball screw that pushes the rack directly, giving the largest assist for heavier cars.

The motor does not drive the rack directly. A reduction gear multiplies its torque. If the gear ratio is N, the assist torque at the pinion is:

T<sub>assist</sub> = N × k<sub>t</sub> × I<sub>m</sub>

Tassist is assist torque at the pinion in N·m; N is the reduction ratio, unitless; kt is the motor torque constant in N·m per ampere; Im is motor current in A.

The driver's torque and the assist add at the pinion: Tpinion = Tdriver + Tassist. Tdriver is the torque the driver applies through the steering wheel, in N·m.

Torque and angle sensing

The torque sensor is the heart of EPS. It uses a torsion bar: a thin shaft between the steering wheel input and the pinion output. When the driver turns, the bar twists by a small angle. The twist angle is proportional to driver torque. A Hall-effect or inductive pick-up measures the relative angle between the two ends, without touching them. Zero twist means zero driver torque, so the ECU can set zero assist.

A separate steering angle sensor measures how far the wheel is turned from straight ahead, usually over several turns lock to lock. It uses geared magnets and Hall sensors, or an optical code disc. The angle signal is needed for return-to-centre, for damping, and by ESP. The speed signal arrives from the vehicle network; see Unit 6.

The sensor pair must be plausible. A torque sensor signal outside its range, or a signal that does not match the angle sensor, sets a fault code. The ECU then reduces assist or shuts the motor down, because a wrong torque reading could give a large assist in the wrong direction.

The assist map, damping and adaptive ratio

The assist map is a look-up table. One axis is driver torque, the other is vehicle speed. The output is target assist torque, or directly a target motor current. At parking speed the map allows a large assist, perhaps 8 N·m at the pinion, so a small driver torque moves the tyres on dry tarmac. At 120 km/h the map allows only a small assist, perhaps 2 N·m, so the driver still feels the road. More assist is not better at speed; it makes the wheel numb and hides the limit of grip.

Three corrections sit on top of the map. Return-to-centre adds a small torque that brings the wheel back after a low-speed turn, so the driver does not have to unwind it by hand. Damping opposes fast steering motion; it is computed from the rate of change of angle and prevents overshoot when the driver releases the wheel. Friction compensation adds a small current to overcome the column, gear and rack friction, so the steering feels light and consistent.

Fail-safe is mechanical. If the motor, sensor or ECU fails, a relay or clutch disconnects the motor. The column and rack still connect the wheel to the road, so the driver steers manually with higher effort. The ECU also limits motor current and temperature; a stalled motor at full lock must not burn.

Adaptive or variable-ratio steering goes further. An active steering system fits a planetary gear and a second motor in the column. At low speed it adds angle, so a small turn of the wheel gives a large road-wheel angle; at high speed it subtracts angle, so the car is less twitchy. The same ECU changes both ratio and assist with speed. Some syllabi call the whole area adaptive power steering.

Worked example

Question. An EPS assist map asks for 8 N·m of assist torque at the pinion during parking and 2 N·m at 120 km/h. The reduction gear ratio is 10:1 and the motor torque constant is 0.05 N·m per ampere. Find the motor torque and motor current at both conditions.

  1. At parking, the assist torque needed at the pinion is Tassist,p = 8 N·m.
  2. Motor torque = Tassist,p ÷ N = 8 N·m ÷ 10 = 0.8 N·m.
  3. Motor current = motor torque ÷ kt = 0.8 N·m ÷ 0.05 N·m/A = 16 A.
  4. At 120 km/h, the assist torque needed at the pinion is Tassist,h = 2 N·m.
  5. Motor torque = 2 N·m ÷ 10 = 0.2 N·m.
  6. Motor current = 0.2 N·m ÷ 0.05 N·m/A = 4 A.
  7. The 4 A at highway speed keeps feel in the wheel instead of making it numb.

Answer. The motor current is 16 A at parking and 4 A at 120 km/h, from motor torques of 0.8 N·m and 0.2 N·m.

Exam traps

  • More assist is not always better: assist must fall as vehicle speed rises, otherwise the steering feels numb and the driver loses road feel.
  • Steering torque is the driver's input measured by the torsion bar; assist torque is the motor's output at the pinion. They are not the same quantity.
  • The reduction ratio multiplies motor torque, so assist torque is larger than motor torque by the factor N. Students often divide by N when they should multiply.
  • The torque sensor measures twist across the torsion bar; the steering angle sensor measures absolute wheel position. Swapping their roles breaks return-to-centre and ESP.
  • Fail-safe does not mean the motor keeps assisting. It means the motor is disconnected and the driver still has a mechanical path to the road wheels.

5.4 Anti-lock braking and traction control

Anti-lock braking (ABS) and traction control (TCS) both keep a tyre near the slip where it grips best. ABS does this by building, holding and releasing brake pressure many times a second. TCS does it by reducing engine torque or braking a spinning drive wheel. The same wheel speed sensors and hydraulic modulator serve both. You meet them on cars, two-wheelers and trucks, especially in rain, on gravel and during panic stops.

You need: wheel speed sensing from Unit 3, the hydraulic and electrical supply from Unit 1, and the pressure modulator idea used again in ESP.

Slip ratio and the friction peak

Let v be the vehicle's forward speed in m/s, ω the wheel's angular speed in rad/s, and r the wheel radius in m. The wheel's contact patch tries to move at ωr. Slip ratio is λ = (v − ωr)/v. λ is dimensionless. Free rolling gives ωr = v, so λ = 0. A locked wheel has ω = 0, so λ = 1. Under braking, slip rises from 0 towards 1.

λ = (v − ωr) / v

v is vehicle forward speed (m/s); ω is wheel angular speed (rad/s); r is wheel radius (m); λ is slip ratio (dimensionless).

The tyre's friction coefficient μ depends on λ. μ is the ratio of friction force to normal load, so it has no unit. A typical dry-asphalt curve rises steeply, peaks near λ = 0.15 and then falls as the wheel locks. Wet road has a lower peak. Figure 1 shows both curves. If λ is held near the peak, the tyre gives more braking force than a locked tyre. That is the whole aim of ABS. The controller does not need the exact peak; it watches wheel speed, estimates λ, and keeps it in a narrow band, often about 0.10 to 0.20.

For a drive wheel, wheel spin makes ωr larger than v. The same formula gives a negative λ. TCS works on the magnitude of that drive slip and limits it near the friction peak.

ABS hardware and the pressure cycle

Each wheel carries a toothed ring and a wheel speed sensor. An inductive sensor makes an AC voltage whose frequency follows tooth passage; a Hall sensor gives a clean square wave. The ABS electronic control unit (ECU) compares the four wheel speeds. A wheel dropping faster than the others is close to locking.

The hydraulic modulator sits between the master cylinder and each brake. For each wheel it has an inlet valve, an outlet valve and a low-pressure accumulator; a return pump sends fluid back to the master cylinder. The ECU runs three phases:

  1. Build: inlet valve open, outlet valve closed; brake pressure rises.
  2. Hold: both valves closed; pressure is trapped.
  3. Release: inlet closed, outlet open; fluid escapes to the accumulator and the return pump runs.

The ECU repeats build-hold-release about 5 to 15 times per second. This is fast enough to hold λ near the peak but slow enough for the hydraulics. Electronic brake distribution (EBD) uses the same hardware to apportion rear brake force and reduce rear lock before ABS acts. On loose gravel or fresh snow, a locked wheel can push a wedge of material and stop sooner. ABS prevents that wedge, so the stop can be longer. That is not a fault; it is the price of steering control.

Traction control

TCS uses the same wheel speed sensors. It compares a driven wheel with a non-driven wheel or with the vehicle speed. If a drive wheel spins, λ is negative and its magnitude rises past the grip peak. TCS then acts in this order, as needed:

  • Reduce throttle opening through the electronic throttle, or cut fuel and spark on older systems.
  • Retard ignition timing to cut engine torque quickly.
  • Brake the spinning wheel through the ABS modulator.

Braking one spinning wheel also helps an open differential. An open differential gives equal torque to both wheels, so a wheel on ice limits the torque to the gripped wheel. Braking the spinning wheel creates a reaction torque; the differential then sends more torque to the wheel with grip. This is the differential-brake idea. Some systems also reduce the throttle limit for a short time after the spin ends, so the tyre does not break away again. TCS and ESP share sensors and brakes, but TCS controls drive slip while ESP controls yaw.

Worked example

Question. A car travels at v = 20 m/s. Its wheel radius is r = 0.30 m. (a) The wheel turns at ω = 60 rad/s. Compute the slip ratio λ and state whether ABS holds pressure if its target band is 0.10 ≤ λ ≤ 0.20. (b) Under hard braking the wheel drops to ω = 40 rad/s. Compute λ and state the ABS action.

  1. Contact-patch speed: ωr = 60 × 0.30 = 18 m/s.
  2. Slip ratio: λ = (20 − 18) / 20 = 2 / 20 = 0.10. This lies in the target band, so ABS holds pressure.
  3. For ω = 40 rad/s: ωr = 40 × 0.30 = 12 m/s.
  4. λ = (20 − 12) / 20 = 8 / 20 = 0.40. This is past the peak, so ABS releases pressure until slip returns near 0.15.

Answer. λ = 0.10 at 60 rad/s (ABS holds), and λ = 0.40 at 40 rad/s (ABS releases).

Exam traps

  • Slip ratio uses vehicle forward speed v as the reference; it is dimensionless, so do not add m/s.
  • λ = 0 is free rolling and λ = 1 is a locked wheel. For a spinning drive wheel the formula gives a negative λ; TCS uses the magnitude.
  • ABS can lengthen a stop on loose gravel or fresh snow because a locked wheel builds a material wedge.
  • Build opens the inlet valve; release opens the outlet valve and runs the return pump. Do not swap them.
  • The pressure cycle rate is about 5 to 15 per second (Hz), not per minute.

5.5 Electronic stability program (ESP)

Electronic stability program (ESP) is an active safety system that keeps a car pointing where the driver steers during a skid. It computes the yaw rate the steering and speed ask for, measures the yaw rate the car actually has, and brakes one wheel to create a correcting yaw moment. It also reduces engine torque when drive force is feeding the slide. You meet ESP in cars, SUVs and electric vehicles on wet roads, gravel and emergency swerves. Some syllabi call it ESC or VDC, for vehicle dynamics control.

You need: 5.4 for wheel slip and brake-pressure modulation, and Unit 3 for yaw rate and lateral acceleration sensors.

Desired yaw rate from the steering

Yaw rate r is how fast the car turns about a vertical axis through its centre of gravity, measured in rad/s. The driver asks for yaw by turning the steering wheel. At road-wheel steering angle δ rad and speed v m/s, a simple bicycle model gives a reference yaw rate. It is called a bicycle model because it lumps the two front wheels into one and the two rear wheels into one.

rdes = vδ / (L + K v²)

rdes is the desired yaw rate in rad/s; v is vehicle speed in m/s; δ is road-wheel steering angle in rad; L is wheelbase in m; K is the understeer term in s²/m, so K v² is in m.

Large K means the car understeers more, so it needs more steering for the same yaw. ESP compares rdes with the measured rmeas. The error e = rdes − rmeas has a sign. Positive e means the car yaws less than asked, which is understeer. Negative e means it yaws more than asked, which is oversteer. The reference is also capped by tyre grip, so ESP never asks for a yaw rate that friction cannot support.

Sensors and the brake hardware

Five signals do most of the work. A yaw rate sensor, usually a small vibrating MEMS gyro, measures r. A lateral acceleration sensor measures sideways acceleration ay in m/s² and helps check grip and sensor plausibility. A steering angle sensor at the column measures δ. Four wheel-speed sensors, shared with ABS, give vehicle speed and wheel slip. A brake pressure sensor tells the hydraulic unit what pressure it has built.

The ESP hydraulic unit has a pump and solenoid valves. It can raise, hold or release brake pressure at one wheel while leaving the others alone. This is the same modulator family used by ABS and traction control. ABS stops wheel lock under braking; TCS stops drive-wheel spin; ESP adds yaw correction. ESP can also send a torque-reduction request to the engine or motor controller over CAN.

An off switch lets the driver disable ESP, for example to rock a car out of snow or run on a dynamometer. The switch does not add grip; it only stops automatic intervention.

Understeer and oversteer corrections

In understeer the front tyres lose grip first. Actual yaw rate is lower than desired, so the nose runs wide. ESP brakes the rear inner wheel. That braking force acts behind the centre of gravity on the inside of the turn, and it creates a yaw moment that turns the nose into the corner. In the worked example the car is understeering, so the rear inner wheel is the correct choice.

In oversteer the rear tyres lose grip first. Actual yaw rate is higher than desired, so the rear slides out and the nose tucks in. ESP brakes the front outer wheel. That braking force acts ahead of the centre of gravity on the outside of the turn, and it creates an outward yaw moment that reduces the extra yaw. Braking the wrong wheel makes the skid worse, so this is the first thing an examiner tests.

Engine torque reduction backs up the brakes. On driven wheels, less torque means less wheelspin and more lateral grip left for steering. ESP cannot exceed tyre grip. On ice it can only help the car do what the tyres allow; it cannot create friction. It also cannot beat physics at high speed.

Worked example

Question. A car has wheelbase L = 2.6 m and understeer term K = 0.0025 s²/m. It travels at v = 20 m/s with road-wheel steering angle δ = 0.05 rad. The yaw rate sensor reads rmeas = 0.200 rad/s. (a) Find the desired yaw rate in rad/s and °/s. (b) Find the yaw error. (c) State whether the car understeers or oversteers and which wheel ESP should brake.

  1. Compute K v² = 0.0025 × 20² = 0.0025 × 400 = 1.0 m.
  2. Denominator L + K v² = 2.6 + 1.0 = 3.6 m.
  3. Numerator v δ = 20 × 0.05 = 1.0 m·rad/s.
  4. rdes = 1.0 / 3.6 = 0.2778 rad/s ≈ 0.278 rad/s.
  5. In degrees per second: rdes = 0.2778 × 180/π = 15.915 °/s.
  6. Measured: rmeas = 0.200 rad/s = 0.200 × 180/π = 11.459 °/s.
  7. Error e = rdes − rmeas = 0.2778 − 0.200 = 0.0778 rad/s ≈ 0.078 rad/s = 4.456 °/s.
  8. e is positive, so actual yaw is less than desired: understeer. Brake the rear inner wheel.

Answer. Desired yaw rate is 0.278 rad/s (15.915 °/s), measured is 0.200 rad/s (11.459 °/s), error is 0.078 rad/s (4.456 °/s); the car understeers, so ESP brakes the rear inner wheel.

Exam traps

  • Understeer is corrected by braking the rear inner wheel; oversteer is corrected by braking the front outer wheel. Reversing them is the classic wrong answer.
  • Use radians in r = vδ/(L + K v²). Convert degrees to rad first; °/s is only for display.
  • ESP cannot raise tyre grip. It only uses the grip available; a car on ice still slides.
  • ESP is not ABS or TCS. ABS stops wheel lock; TCS stops drive-wheel spin; ESP corrects yaw.
  • Positive error rdes − rmeas means understeer, not oversteer. Keep the sign.

5.6 Active suspension control

Active suspension uses a force actuator at each corner to push or pull the body, so the body stays flat while the wheels follow the road. A passive suspension has fixed springs and dampers. A semi-active suspension varies damper force. A fully active suspension adds a hydraulic or electric actuator that can both push and pull. Luxury cars and some electric vehicles use it. Only a few universities teach this chapter.

You need basic spring–mass–damper vibration from a mechanics course, and see 5.5 for yaw and roll control using brakes.

Three layouts

Passive suspension: springs and dampers have fixed rates. The body and wheel move as the road dictates.

Semi-active suspension: the damper is adjustable. A solenoid or motor changes the damping coefficient many times a second. It can only dissipate energy, so its force always opposes the relative velocity across the damper (body minus wheel). It cannot inject energy to hold the body flat over a bump.

Fully active suspension: a hydraulic or electric actuator at each corner supplies force in both directions. It can hold the body flat over a bump while the wheel moves. The actuator adds energy, so it needs a pump or a high-current electric drive.

Sensors and skyhook law

Sensors: a body accelerometer at each corner measures body motion; a wheel accelerometer measures wheel hop; a ride-height sensor measures the gap between body and wheel.

The corner accelerometer signal is integrated to give body velocity, with a high-pass filter to stop drift, and that velocity feeds the skyhook law.

The skyhook law imagines a damper hung from a fixed sky. The controller measures body velocity and asks the actuator for a force that opposes it.

F = −c_sky × v_body

F is actuator force in newtons (N); c_sky is skyhook gain in N·s/m; v_body is body velocity in m/s, positive upward. The minus sign means force opposes body velocity. Magnitude is F = c_sky × |v_body|.

For a semi-active damper, the controller applies F only if F × v_rel < 0, where v_rel is the relative velocity across the damper (body minus wheel) in m/s. Otherwise it commands minimum damping, and the body is not held.

The controller computes F and commands damper current or actuator pressure. A larger body velocity asks for a larger force.

Roll, pitch and limits

Roll control: in a turn the body leans outward. The controller raises force on the outer corners and lowers it on the inner corners. The left–right force difference creates a roll moment that cancels the lean.

Pitch control: braking dips the nose; accelerating lifts it. Front–rear force distribution creates a pitch moment.

Bandwidth: the loop must respond faster than body motion. It must also cover wheel hop at about 10–15 Hz, so actuator and controller bandwidth is usually well above 20 Hz, not just a few times the 1–2 Hz body ride frequency. Power cost is real: a fully active system draws kilowatts, while a semi-active damper draws tens of watts.

Worked example

Question. A corner skyhook gain is c_sky = 5000 N·s/m. The body moves upward at v_body = 0.08 m/s. The damper gives 800 N per ampere and saturates at 1.0 A (800 N). (a) Find the force magnitude. (b) Find the current. (c) Repeat for v_body = 0.16 m/s and state the damper limit.

  1. Use magnitude F = c_sky × |v_body| = 5000 × 0.08 = 400 N.
  2. Current I = F ÷ 800 = 400 ÷ 800 = 0.5 A.
  3. For 0.16 m/s: F2 = 5000 × 0.16 = 800 N.
  4. I2 = 800 ÷ 800 = 1.0 A. This is exactly the rated damper limit. A larger body velocity cannot be met; the force stays clipped at 800 N.

Answer. Force 400 N, current 0.5 A; at 0.16 m/s force 800 N, current 1.0 A, exactly the rated damper limit (clipped at 800 N for larger velocity).

Exam traps

  • Skyhook damps the body, not the wheel; the wheel still follows the road.
  • Sign slip: F = −c_sky × v_body. Reversing the sign adds force in the direction of motion.
  • Semi-active can only dissipate energy; its force always opposes relative velocity across the damper and cannot hold the body flat over a bump.
  • Gain units: c_sky is N·s/m, not N/m (spring rate) or N·s (impulse).

5.7 Airbags and passive safety systems

Passive safety saves you without driver action: structure and restraints. The crumple zone folds so occupants lose speed over a longer distance and time. The seat belt holds them; a pretensioner removes slack and a load limiter caps chest force. The airbag is a supplement, fired by the crash sensor within milliseconds. Every new car sold in India today carries this chain, from a small hatchback upward. Buses and trucks rely mostly on the structure and on belts, without airbags.

You need: Unit 3 for accelerometers and Unit 1 for the electrical supply and the squib circuit.

The crash pulse and the crumple zone

During a crash the car does not stop instantly. The front structure folds over a designed length: the crumple zone. Car deceleration against time is the crash pulse. Its area is the speed change; its length is the time restraints get.

a = v² ÷ (2s), t = v ÷ a

a is the average deceleration in m/s², v is the impact speed in m/s, s is the crush distance in m, and t is the stopping time in s.

At 50 km/h (13.9 m/s) with 0.6 m crush: a = 13.9² ÷ (2 × 0.6) = 161 m/s², about 16.4 g, where g = 9.81 m/s², and t = 13.9 ÷ 161 = 0.086 s, or 86 ms. Without crush, the same speed change over a few millimetres would take under a millisecond and give about two thousand g. Even a few centimetres of crush, as in a rigid pole hit, still gives several hundred g over a few milliseconds. That gap is the point of passive safety.

The seat belt is the primary restraint. It spreads load over the pelvis and rib cage. A pretensioner, fired by the crash signal, pulls slack out in about 10 ms. A load limiter then lets the belt pay out at a few kN, so the chest avoids the peak. Belt first, bag second.

Inside the airbag ECU: MEMS accelerometer and safing sensor

The airbag ECU (electronic control unit) sits under the centre console or the floor. Its main sensor is a MEMS accelerometer: a silicon proof mass on thin springs, whose capacitance changes as the mass moves (see Unit 3, Automotive Sensors and Actuators). The output is a voltage proportional to deceleration along the car's axis, calibrated in g.

The ECU samples this about every millisecond, filters it, compares it with a stored threshold, and often integrates it into a speed change Δv. The threshold must be crossed and held for a few milliseconds, or nothing fires.

A second, independent sensor sits in series in the firing circuit: the safing sensor. It is a mechanical switch (a ball held by a magnet that closes at a few g) or a second accelerometer. The squib, a tiny heater inside the inflator, gets current only when the accelerometer and the safing sensor agree.

  1. Key on: self-test. Squib continuity is checked; an open circuit stores a fault code and lights the warning lamp.
  2. Crash: signal sampled, filtered, compared with the threshold curve.
  3. Threshold crossed and held, safing sensor closed: fire decision.
  4. A current pulse of a few amperes for a couple of milliseconds is sent to the squib.
  5. The same event unlocks the doors, switches on the hazard lamps and cuts the fuel pump.

A capacitor inside the ECU holds enough charge to fire the bag even if the battery cable breaks in the crash (see Unit 1, Batteries, Starting and Charging Systems).

Inflator, venting and the other airbags

The squib's pulse ignites a solid propellant, which decomposes in about 20 to 30 ms into a large volume of nitrogen-rich gas; older units used sodium azide, newer ones guanidine salts.

The bag is woven nylon, folded behind a cover with a hidden seam. Gas fills it in about 25 ms after the fire signal, so it is full around 50 ms after impact. Gas then escapes through vent holes and through the fabric, so the bag softens before the occupant arrives. The bag alone cannot stop the body; it spreads the load over a larger area and keeps the head off the steering wheel and the A-pillar, while the belt does the stopping. This is the ride-down phase.

  • Driver bag in the steering wheel; passenger bag in the dashboard.
  • Side thorax bags in the seat or door; curtain bags along the roof rail for the head.
  • Knee bags under the dashboard, so the legs do not slide forward.

A side impact leaves very little crush distance, so side and curtain bags fire earlier, within 5 to 10 ms. Curtains stay inflated for several seconds in case the car rolls over. Rollover sensing uses a roll-rate sensor plus lateral accelerometers.

Occupant detection, child seats and the deployment threshold

Not every bump fires the bag. Each maker stores a deployment threshold, roughly a speed change in the region of 15 to 25 km/h for a frontal impact. A parking knock must not fire it: that would be costly and could injure a small person sitting close.

The full name is supplemental restraint system, SRS, and the word supplemental is literal. A belted occupant moves late and meets a bag that is already full and venting. An unbelted occupant moves early and far, and can meet the bag while its cover and fabric are still opening at high speed. That is how the device meant to protect can injure an unbelted or out-of-position person.

Occupant detection completes the rule. A pressure bladder or a capacitive mat in the passenger seat classifies it as empty, child seat, small adult or adult. If a child seat is detected, the passenger bag is suppressed. A rear-facing child seat must never sit in front of an active passenger bag.

A belt switch in each buckle drives the reminder lamp and tells the ECU which deployment path to use.

5.8 Advanced driver assistance systems (ADAS)

ADAS is a stack of sensors, perception, fusion, planning and actuation that warns the driver or acts for a moment. It exists because a machine can sense some hazards earlier than a tired driver, but it must work at a defined level of automation. You meet it in automatic emergency braking, lane keeping, blind spot warning, adaptive cruise and parking assist. This chapter is tagged advanced; only a few universities teach ADAS as a full chapter.

You need 5.2 for adaptive cruise, 5.3 for steering angle, and 5.7 for passive safety as the last resort.

The five-layer stack and SAE levels

ADAS is not one box. Sensors give raw data. Perception turns pixels and echoes into objects, lanes and signs. Fusion combines the sensors. Planning chooses a path and speed. Actuation commands brakes, steering and throttle. The figure runs from raw data at the bottom to decision at the top.

SAE levels run from 0 to 5. Level 0 has no automation. Level 1 assists one task, such as adaptive cruise. Level 2 handles steering and speed together, but the driver still monitors the road and remains responsible. Level 3 is conditional, Level 4 is high and Level 5 is full automation. Most ADAS here is Level 1 or 2.

A warning only alerts. An intervening function moves a control. Lane departure warning alerts; lane keep steers. The same stack serves both warning and action. Only a few universities teach this as a full chapter; the layers matter more than the brand names.

Sensors, fusion and functions

Every sensor is a trade-off. Camera: detail, low cost, weak in glare and fog. Radar: range and speed in bad weather, poor shape. Lidar: 3D cloud, higher cost, weak in dust. Ultrasonic: short range, cheap, good for parking.

Fusion combines them because one weakness is another strength. A camera sees lane lines that radar cannot; radar sees a stopped car that a camera misses in low sun. Fusion needs time alignment and one vehicle coordinate frame.

Common functions: AEB brakes for a collision; lane departure warning alerts; lane keep steers; blind spot detection watches the side; adaptive cruise holds a gap (see 5.2); traffic sign recognition reads signs; parking assist uses ultrasonics; driver monitoring watches eyes and head. AEB, lane keep and adaptive cruise act; the rest mostly warn or assist. Driver monitoring is becoming common in Indian cars with camera-based attention checks.

AEB, delay and the safety limit

AEB shows the chain. Perception finds the object and estimates range and closing speed. Planning decides a crash is likely. Actuation builds brake pressure. Time passes in sensing, decision and brake build, so AEB cannot beat physics.

s = v t_d + v²/(2a)

s is stopping distance (m), v is speed (m/s), t_d is delay (s), a is braking deceleration (m/s²).

At low speed AEB can stop the car. At high speed it may only reduce impact speed. The numbers decide the limit, not a fixed claim. The driver must still steer and brake in a Level 2 car.

A warning-only system does not brake; an intervening system does. Passive safety, such as the airbag in 5.7, is the last resort after AEB fails. Functional safety asks what happens if a sensor is wrong or a brake command is late; formal analysis is in Unit 6. Fusion reduces false alarms and missed stops.

Worked example

Question. A car at 50 km/h approaches a stopped car. AEB delay is 0.5 s, then braking is 8 m/s². Use v = 13.9 m/s. Find delay distance, braking distance, total distance, and the margin at 25 m, 30 m and 18 m.

  1. Delay: 13.9 × 0.5 = 6.95 m.
  2. Braking: 13.9² ÷ (2 × 8) = 12.08 m.
  3. Total: 6.95 + 12.08 = 19.03 m.
  4. Margins: 25 − 19.03 = 5.97 m; 30 − 19.03 = 10.97 m; 18 − 19.03 = −1.03 m.
  5. Stop is possible at 25 m and 30 m; at 18 m it is not.

Answer. Delay distance 6.95 m, braking distance 12.08 m, total 19.03 m; margins 5.97 m, 10.97 m and −1.03 m, so only the first two detections allow a stop.

Exam traps

  • Level 2 is not self-driving; the driver still monitors the road and remains responsible.
  • Lane departure warning alerts; lane keep steers. A warning is not an intervention.
  • Convert km/h to m/s by dividing by 3.6. Mixing units changes stopping distance by a factor of 3.6 or about 13.
  • Delay distance is v t_d, not v²/(2a). Do not count the delay twice.
  • A negative margin means the crash is unavoidable, not that the brakes failed.

5.9 PI and PD controllers for vehicle speed control

A vehicle speed loop compares reference and measured speed and commands tractive force. A PI controller removes steady-state speed error; a PD controller adds damping. The vehicle is modelled as a first-order lag, so two gains and one time constant describe the loop. Cruise control, electric-vehicle torque control and ADAS speed tracking use this. Only a few universities teach the tuning method here.

You need: Chapter 5.2 for the cruise loop, and first-order lag plus Laplace transforms from a control course.

Plant, PI law and lambda tuning

m dv/dt = F − b v

m is mass (kg); v is speed (m/s); F is tractive force (N); b is resistance (N per m/s).

Rearranged, τ dv/dt + v = K F, with τ = m/b in s and K = 1/b in (m/s)/N. In Laplace form, V(s)/F(s) = K/(τs + 1).

Error e = v_ref − v, with v_ref the wanted speed. PI law: u = Kp e + Ki ∫e dt, where u is commanded force (N), Kp is N per (m/s), and Ki is N/m (newton per metre of integrated speed error; Ki = Kp/Ti). Proportional action alone leaves an offset. At steady state Kp(v_ref − v) = b v, so e_ss = b v_ref/(Kp + b). For Kp much larger than b this is close to b v_ref/Kp. The integral supplies the force and drives e to zero.

Lambda tuning chooses closed-loop time constant λ: Kp = τ/(K λ), Ti = τ, Ki = Kp/Ti. Smaller λ gives faster response but larger force, so check actuator saturation. A reference model filters v_ref, often as 1/(λs + 1), before the loop sees it. On a real vehicle with a rate-limited actuator this slows the demanded force and limits saturation and overshoot; in the ideal first-order loop modelled here the loop already has no overshoot, so the main gain is a smooth force command.

PD damping and practical derivative

PD law u = Kp e + Kd de/dt, where Kd is N per (m/s²). The derivative predicts error growth and opposes fast speed change, adding damping. With constant v_ref, de/dt = −dv/dt, so derivative on measurement is u = Kp e − Kd dv/dt. This avoids a kick when v_ref steps.

Measured speed carries sensor noise. A raw derivative amplifies it, so filter the derivative with time constant Tf, or use a small Kd. The derivative path becomes s/(Tf s + 1): a first-order lag with time constant Tf (in s) on the derivative. PI plus reference model is common for speed; PD alone cannot remove offset because at steady state de/dt = 0. Full Bode and root-locus design belongs to a control course.

For a step v_ref, the reference filter slows the demanded force before the PI block. The loop sees a gentle ramp, not a jump. On real vehicles this limits actuator saturation and overshoot; with the ideal lambda-tuned loop the response is already first order, so the main benefit is a smooth force command, not lower overshoot.

Worked example

Question. A 1500 kg vehicle has resistance b = 100 N per m/s. (a) Find K and τ. (b) Choose λ = 3 s and find Kp, Ti, Ki. (c) With v_ref = 30 m/s, find the steady error if only proportional action is used.

  1. 1500 dv/dt = F − 100v, so V(s)/F(s) = 1/(1500s + 100) = 0.01/(15s + 1).
  2. K = 0.01 (m/s)/N, τ = 15 s.
  3. Kp = τ/(K λ) = 15/(0.01 × 3) = 500 N per (m/s).
  4. Ti = τ = 15 s; Ki = Kp/Ti = 500/15 = 33.3 N/m.
  5. At steady state with proportional-only control, 500(30 − v) = 100v, so v = 25 m/s and e = 30 − 25 = 5 m/s. The shortcut b v_ref/Kp = 3000/500 = 6 m/s is only an upper bound, good when Kp is much larger than b. The integral term supplies the needed force and e falls to zero.

Answer. K = 0.01 (m/s)/N, τ = 15 s, Kp = 500 N/(m/s), Ki = 33.3 N/m, and proportional-only offset = 5 m/s (exact; the shortcut 3000/500 = 6 m/s is an upper bound).

Exam traps

  • Error sign: e = v_ref − v, not v − v_ref.
  • Kp alone leaves a steady offset; the integral term removes it.
  • Derivative on noisy measured speed amplifies noise; filter it.
  • Units: Kp N per m/s; Ki N/m; Kd N per (m/s²).
  • Too small λ demands huge force and saturates the actuator.

Takeaways

  1. A TCU chooses the shift point from a map and then shapes clutch pressure, so shift quality is a hydraulic control problem.
  2. Cruise control is one closed loop on throttle; adaptive cruise control adds a second loop on gap that may also use the brakes.
  3. Electric power steering replaces hydraulic pressure with motor current, and the assist map is the heart of its design.
  4. ABS holds wheel slip near the peak of the friction curve by cycling brake pressure; traction control does the same job with drive torque.
  5. ESP compares the yaw rate the driver asked for with the yaw rate the car has, then brakes one wheel to correct the difference.
  6. An active suspension drives a force actuator at each corner so the body stays flat while the wheels follow the road.
  7. An airbag is a supplement to the seat belt, fired by a crash sensor within milliseconds of a severe impact.
  8. ADAS is a stack: sense, fuse, plan, act, at a defined level of automation.
  9. PI control removes steady-state speed error, PD control adds damping, and two gains plus a plant model are all a speed loop needs.

Glossary

Assist map
The lookup table that turns measured steering torque and vehicle speed into a motor current for power steering.
CAN bus
The shared two-wire serial network over which controllers such as ESP, EPS and the engine ECU exchange signals.
Dead band
A small range of error around the set point inside which the controller deliberately does nothing, to stop actuator chatter.
Electronic throttle
A throttle plate driven by a motor under ECU command, with pedal position as a request rather than a direct link.
Friction compensation
A control term that adds a small torque in the direction of motion to overcome stiction in the steering or pedal mechanism.
Integral windup
The unwanted growth of an integral term while the actuator is saturated, which causes overshoot when the error finally reverses.
Oversteer
The condition where the car turns more than the steering asks for, so the rear swings out.
Quarter-car model
A two-mass model of one corner of a car, with the sprung body mass on a spring and damper above the unsprung wheel mass on the tyre stiffness.
Return-to-centre
An EPS function that steers the wheels back towards straight ahead at low speed when the driver lets go.
Seat belt pretensioner
A device that pulls the belt tight within milliseconds of a crash, before the airbag is needed.
Sensor fusion
The process of merging measurements from several sensors into one consistent description of the surroundings.
Shift map
The stored set of upshift and downshift speeds against throttle opening that fixes when the gearbox changes gear.
Skyhook damping
A suspension control law that damps the body against a notional fixed reference while leaving the wheels free.
Slip ratio
The fractional difference between vehicle speed and wheel rim speed, λ = (v − ωr)/v, which sets how much grip a tyre gives.
Squib
The small electric detonator inside the airbag module that ignites the gas generator on command.
Steady-state error
The error that remains a long time after a step change when the controller has no integral action to remove it.
Time headway
The gap to the vehicle ahead divided by your speed, in seconds; ACC controls this rather than distance.
Time-to-collision
The gap divided by the closing speed, in seconds; a measure used to warn or brake before a rear-end hit.
Torsion bar
A calibrated bar that twists in proportion to the torque applied through it, used in EPS to measure steering torque.
Understeer
The condition where the car turns less than the steering asks for, so the front wheels run wide.
Variable-ratio steering
A steering system whose ratio of road-wheel angle to steering-wheel angle changes with steering angle, so parking needs fewer turns.
Yaw moment
The turning moment about the vertical axis made by a single wheel's brake force acting at a distance from the centre of gravity.
Yaw rate
The speed at which the car turns about its vertical axis, measured by a yaw rate sensor in degrees per second.
