5.1 Electronic transmission control

An automatic gearbox still has gears; electronics decide when to change and how hard to press the clutches. The transmission control unit (TCU) looks up the best gear in a shift map from throttle opening and vehicle speed, uses engine and shaft speeds to time the shift, and then commands solenoid valves that set hydraulic pressure. That pressure fills a clutch pack, so the new gear engages in a fraction of a second without a jolt. The same loop runs automated manuals, dual-clutch boxes, belt CVTs and conventional automatics. This chapter shows the loop, the hydraulics, and the four layouts.

You need: throttle, engine torque and power from electronic engine control, and speed and position sensors from automotive sensors and actuators.

The shift map and the shift schedule

The TCU needs two numbers before it can choose a gear: throttle opening α (percent, 0 means closed, 100 means wide open) and vehicle speed v (km/h, from the output shaft sensor or the wheel speed sensors).

Plot α on the vertical axis and v on the horizontal axis. Draw one curve for each shift. The region to the right of the 2→3 curve means the car is fast enough for third. The region to the left means drop back to second. The two boundaries are deliberately separated, so the box does not jump 2–3–2–3 at one steady speed. That separation is hysteresis, the same trick a thermostat uses.

Light throttle keeps the curves at low speed, so the car upshifts early and the engine stays slow and economical. Heavy throttle pushes the curves to high speed, so the engine is held near its power peak. Pressing the accelerator past a set fraction forces a downshift called kickdown. The family of curves for every gear is the shift schedule; the table stored inside the TCU is the shift map.

Every decision rests on one relation:

nengine = nwheel × igear × ifinal

nengine is engine speed in rpm; nwheel is wheel speed in rpm; igear is the selected gear ratio; ifinal is the final drive ratio. Both ratios are dimensionless.

An overdrive gear has igear below 1, so it lowers engine speed. A low gear has igear above 1 and multiplies torque.

Hydraulics, pressure and shift quality

A shift is hydraulic work. The TCU cannot push a clutch; it can only change a current.

  1. Pressure stage. A regulator holds line pressure, the supply for the whole hydraulic circuit. A variable-force solenoid trims it. Line pressure must rise with engine torque, because a clutch carrying more torque needs more clamping force or it slips.
  2. Fill stage. A shift solenoid opens and oil fills the piston of the on-coming clutch until the plates touch. This travel is the clutch fill; its time depends on oil temperature, oil age and clearance.
  3. Torque stage. The off-going clutch releases while the on-coming clutch takes the load. If the off-going clutch lets go before the on-coming clutch can carry the torque, the engine flares (revs up with no load); if both clutches hold together for too long, they fight each other (tie-up) and the car jerks.
  4. Torque reduction. The TCU asks the engine controller for a short torque cut, retarded spark or a small throttle trim, just as the ratio changes. The shift finishes faster and the passengers feel less of a step.
  5. Learning. Speed sensors on the input and output shafts measure the real shift time. If the engine flared, the on-coming clutch was late, so the TCU lengthens its fill pulse or raises its pressure on the next shift; if the shift was harsh from tie-up, it shortens the fill or lowers the pressure. That is adaptive learning, and it repeats for the life of the box.

Four layouts, one control loop

The same loop drives four different mechanisms.

  • AMT (automated manual). A normal manual gearbox with a robot that works the clutch and the gear lever. Cheap and efficient, but torque is interrupted, so the car nods.
  • DCT (dual-clutch transmission). Two clutches, one for odd gears and one for even. The next gear is already engaged, so the shift is a clutch-to-clutch handover with no torque break.
  • CVT (continuously variable transmission). A steel belt runs between two pulleys whose widths are set by hydraulic pressure. There are no steps; the TCU chooses the engine speed it wants.
  • Conventional automatic. Planetary gearsets with clutch packs, usually behind a torque converter.

The torque converter is an engine-driven impeller throwing oil at a turbine on the gearbox input, so the car can creep at idle with no mechanical link. It multiplies torque while the speed difference is large. At cruising speed a lock-up clutch closes inside the converter, tying input to engine and removing the slip loss. Many TCUs run the lock-up clutch with a small controlled slip (partial lock-up), which damps engine torsional vibration at low engine speed; the shift clutches do the ratio work.

Worked example

Question. A car runs at 60 km/h in top gear with 30 % throttle. Wheel radius r = 0.30 m, final drive ratio 3.50, top gear ratio 0.70. The driver presses the pedal to 90 % and the TCU kicks down to the direct gear (ratio 1.00). Find (a) the wheel speed in rpm, (b) the engine speed in top gear, (c) the engine speed after the kickdown, and (d) the rise in engine speed.

  1. Convert speed to SI: v = 60 ÷ 3.6 = 16.67 m/s.
  2. Wheel circumference = 2πr = 2 × π × 0.30 = 1.885 m.
  3. Wheel rpm = (v ÷ circumference) × 60 = (16.67 ÷ 1.885) × 60 = 8.842 × 60 = 530.5 rpm.
  4. Engine speed in top gear = 530.5 × 3.50 × 0.70 = 530.5 × 2.45 = 1299.8 rpm, about 1300 rpm.
  5. Engine speed in the direct gear = 530.5 × 3.50 × 1.00 = 1856.8 rpm, about 1857 rpm.
  6. Rise = 1857 − 1300 = 557 rpm.
  7. Why the shift happens: on the shift map of Figure 5.1.1 the point (60 km/h, 30 %) lies right of the top-to-direct downshift line, but (60 km/h, 90 %) lies left of it, so the TCU selects the lower gear and the engine moves towards its power range.

Answer. Wheel speed 530.5 rpm; engine speed 1300 rpm in top gear and 1857 rpm after the kickdown to direct gear, a rise of 557 rpm at the same road speed.

Exam traps

  • Leaving out the final drive when converting wheel speed to engine speed. Engine rpm needs both ratios: gear ratio and final drive.
  • Assuming a shift happens at a fixed vehicle speed. The map also reads throttle, so a light throttle upshifts early and a heavy one holds the gear.
  • Getting the ratio direction wrong. igear above 1 reduces speed and multiplies torque; an overdrive below 1 does the opposite.
  • Confusing the lock-up clutch inside the torque converter with the shift clutches. Lock-up only removes converter slip; the shift clutches change the ratio.
  • Mixing rpm and rad/s, or forgetting the ×60 and ÷60 in the wheel-speed conversion.

5.2 Cruise control and adaptive cruise control

Cruise control keeps a car at a speed the driver chooses, without the driver holding the pedal. It measures actual speed, compares it with the set speed, and moves the throttle through a controller. Adaptive cruise control (ACC) adds a forward sensor: it also measures the gap to the car ahead and slows or brakes to keep a safe time gap. Used on highways, in stop-and-go traffic, and in many new cars sold in India with radar or camera systems.

You need: how throttle changes engine torque (electronic engine control), radar and wheel speed sensors (automotive sensors and actuators), and electronic transmission control for how a controller commands an actuator.

Basic cruise control

Basic cruise control holds a driver-chosen speed. The driver presses SET above a minimum speed, often around 30 km/h. The controller stores the set speed vset. RESUME brings back the stored speed after a cancel. CANCEL or a tap on the brake ends control. Holding COAST closes the throttle so the car slows; when the button is released, the speed reached is stored as the new vset. Actual speed v comes from wheel speed sensors.

e = vset − v; u = Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt

e is speed error in m/s; vset is set speed in m/s; v is actual speed in m/s; u is throttle command (0 to 100 %); Kp is proportional gain in % per (m/s); Ki is integral gain in % per m, since ∫e dt is in m; t is time in s.

The P term gives an immediate response. The I term sums error over time. On a hill, a plain P controller leaves a steady error: uphill needs more throttle, and the only way a P controller makes more throttle is to keep a positive error. The integral keeps adding while error exists, until error returns to zero. On downhill, the integral unwinds. If the car still goes faster than set, basic cruise cannot brake; it has throttle authority only.

Adaptive cruise control

ACC adds a forward sensor, usually a 77 GHz radar or a camera, and a gap loop. Radar measures distance d in metres to the lead car and relative speed. Target selection picks the nearest car in the host's own lane, the in-path target; the camera and the yaw rate help decide which lane each car is in.

tgap = d ÷ v; dtarget = ttarget × v; egap = d − dtarget; closing rate = v − vlead

tgap is time gap in s; d is measured distance to lead car in m; v is host speed in m/s; ttarget is selected time gap in s; dtarget is desired distance in m; egap is gap error in m; vlead is lead car speed in m/s.

Time gap is in seconds, not metres. The driver selects ttarget, often 1.5 to 2.5 s. At a higher speed the same time gap needs a larger distance, so dtarget grows with v. A positive egap means the host is farther than target; a negative egap means it is closer and must slow. Closing rate is positive when the gap is shrinking.

The controller combines speed error and gap error. If the gap is large, it follows set speed. As the gap falls, it reduces speed; if needed it applies the service brakes through the ESP hydraulic unit. Basic cruise has no brake authority. A car cutting in makes d drop suddenly; the controller brakes, but if the cut-in is too close the driver must brake. Stop-and-go ACC can brake to zero and restart. The brake pedal always cancels or overrides; the accelerator can also override. Sensor fusion is in ADAS; PI tuning is in PI and PD controllers.

Worked example

Question. A host car is set to 25 m/s. Its 77 GHz radar sees a lead car in the same lane travelling at 20 m/s at a distance of 60 m. The driver has selected a 2.0 s time gap. (a) Find the present time gap. (b) Find the target distance. (c) Find the gap error. (d) Find the closing rate. (e) At the present speeds, how long before the gap would become zero if nothing changed? (f) What constant deceleration of the host, starting now, brings it down to the lead car's 20 m/s exactly when the gap reaches the 50 m target, and how long does that take?

  1. Present time gap: tgap = d ÷ vhost = 60 m ÷ 25 m/s = 2.4 s.
  2. Target distance: dtarget = ttarget × vhost = 2.0 s × 25 m/s = 50 m.
  3. Gap error: egap = d − dtarget = 60 m − 50 m = +10 m. The host is 10 m farther than target.
  4. Closing rate: vhost − vlead = 25 m/s − 20 m/s = 5 m/s. Positive, so the gap is shrinking.
  5. Time to zero gap at present speeds: t = d ÷ closing rate = 60 m ÷ 5 m/s = 12 s. This is the time-to-collision, TTC. The target gap itself would be reached in (60 − 50) ÷ 5 = 2 s.
  6. The closing speed must fall from 5 m/s to 0 while the gap shrinks by 10 m (from 60 m to 50 m). In relative motion, a = closing² ÷ (2 × 10 m) = 5² ÷ 20 = 1.25 m/s², and the time is 5 ÷ 1.25 = 4 s.
  7. Check: in 4 s the host covers 25 × 4 − ½ × 1.25 × 4² = 90 m and the lead car 20 × 4 = 80 m, so the gap becomes 60 − (90 − 80) = 50 m, and the host speed is 25 − 1.25 × 4 = 20 m/s.

Answer. Time gap 2.4 s, target distance 50 m, gap error +10 m, closing rate 5 m/s, time to zero gap (TTC) 12 s; a steady 1.25 m/s² of braking for 4 s settles the host at 20 m/s and 50 m behind.

Exam traps

  • Basic cruise control cannot brake; it only moves the throttle. On a long downhill the car can exceed set speed.
  • Time gap is in seconds, not metres. The target distance changes with speed: 2.0 s is 50 m at 25 m/s but 40 m at 20 m/s.
  • Set speed is not actual speed while the loop is catching up. The error is vset − v, not vset alone.
  • Gap error sign matters. Positive egap means the host is farther than target, not closer.
  • The brake pedal always overrides ACC. ACC brake force comes through the service brakes and ESP, not from a separate pedal.

5.3 Electronic and adaptive power steering

Electric power steering (EPS) replaces the hydraulic pump, belt and fluid with an electric motor that pushes the steering only when the driver asks. A torque sensor measures how hard the driver turns; a speed signal says how fast the car is going; the ECU (electronic control unit) looks up an assist map and sets motor current. Parking needs a large assist, highway driving a small one. EPS is common on new cars in India, from hatchbacks to SUVs, and it provides the steering actuator for lane-keeping in ADAS.

You need: torque and angle sensors and the Hall effect (automotive sensors and actuators), and the 12 V supply and motor currents (vehicle electrical systems).

From hydraulic to electric: where the motor sits

Conventional hydraulic power steering drives its pump from the engine belt; electro-hydraulic systems keep the hydraulic rack but drive the pump with an electric motor. The pump circulates oil even when the wheel is straight, so it costs fuel. A rotary valve sends pressure to one side of the rack when the driver turns. EPS removes the pump, the belt and the fluid. An electric motor provides torque only when the driver moves the wheel, so straight-line running draws almost no steering power.

There are three common layouts. In column EPS the motor sits on the steering column and drives it through a worm gear; this is compact and suits small cars. In pinion EPS the motor turns a second pinion on the rack, while the driver turns the first pinion; this is the dual-pinion layout. In rack EPS the motor turns a ball screw that pushes the rack directly, giving the largest assist for heavier cars.

The motor does not drive the rack directly. A reduction gear multiplies its torque. If the gear ratio is N, the assist torque at the pinion is:

Tassist = N × kt × Im

Tassist is assist torque at the pinion in N·m (gear losses ignored); N is the reduction ratio, unitless; kt is the motor torque constant in N·m per ampere; Im is motor current in A.

The driver's torque and the assist add at the pinion: Tpinion = Tdriver + Tassist. Tdriver is the torque the driver applies through the steering wheel, in N·m.

Torque and angle sensing

The torque sensor is the heart of EPS. It uses a torsion bar: a thin shaft between the steering wheel input and the pinion output. When the driver turns, the bar twists by a small angle. The twist angle is proportional to driver torque. A Hall-effect or inductive pick-up measures the relative angle between the two ends, without touching them. Zero twist means zero driver torque, so the ECU can set zero assist.

A separate steering angle sensor measures how far the steering wheel is turned from straight ahead, over several turns lock to lock; dividing by the steering ratio gives the road-wheel angle. It uses geared magnets and Hall sensors, or an optical code disc. The angle signal is needed for return-to-centre, for damping, and by ESP. The speed signal arrives over the CAN bus from the ABS unit.

The sensor pair must be plausible. A torque sensor signal outside its range, or a signal that does not match the angle sensor, sets a fault code. The ECU then reduces assist or shuts the motor down, because a wrong torque reading could give a large assist in the wrong direction.

The assist map, damping and adaptive ratio

The assist map is a look-up table. One axis is driver torque, the other is vehicle speed. The output is target assist torque, or directly a target motor current. At parking speed the map allows a large assist, perhaps 40 N·m at the pinion, so a small driver torque moves the tyres on dry tarmac. At 120 km/h the map allows only a small assist, perhaps 4 N·m, so the driver still feels the road. More assist is not better at speed; it makes the wheel numb and hides the limit of grip.

Three corrections sit on top of the map. Return-to-centre adds a small torque that brings the wheel back after a low-speed turn, so the driver does not have to unwind it by hand. Damping opposes fast steering motion; it is computed from the rate of change of angle and prevents overshoot when the driver releases the wheel. Friction compensation adds a small current to overcome the column, gear and rack friction, so the steering feels light and consistent.

Fail-safe is mechanical. If the motor, sensor or ECU fails, a relay or clutch disconnects the motor. The column and rack still connect the wheel to the road, so the driver steers manually with higher effort. The ECU also limits motor current and temperature; a stalled motor at full lock must not burn.

Adaptive or variable-ratio steering goes further. An active steering system fits a planetary gear and a second motor in the column. At low speed it adds angle, so a small turn of the wheel gives a large road-wheel angle; at high speed it subtracts angle, so the car is less twitchy. The same ECU changes both ratio and assist with speed. Some syllabi call the whole area adaptive power steering.

Worked example

Question. A car with column EPS is parked. Turning the tyres needs 44 N·m at the pinion; the driver gives 4 N·m and the assist map asks for the other 40 N·m. At 120 km/h the map asks for only 4 N·m of assist. The worm reduction is 16 : 1, the motor torque constant is 0.05 N·m per ampere and the pinion radius is 8 mm. Ignore gear losses. Find (a) the rack force at parking and (b) the motor torque and motor current at both conditions.

  1. Pinion torque at parking: Tpinion = Tdriver + Tassist = 4 + 40 = 44 N·m.
  2. Rack force = pinion torque ÷ pinion radius = 44 N·m ÷ 0.008 m = 5500 N, or 5.5 kN.
  3. The worm multiplies motor torque by 16 on its way to the pinion, so the motor needs Tassist ÷ N = 40 ÷ 16 = 2.5 N·m.
  4. Motor current at parking: Im = 2.5 N·m ÷ 0.05 N·m/A = 50 A.
  5. At 120 km/h: motor torque = 4 ÷ 16 = 0.25 N·m.
  6. Motor current at 120 km/h: Im = 0.25 ÷ 0.05 = 5 A.
  7. Check with the formula: N × kt × Im = 16 × 0.05 × 50 = 40 N·m and 16 × 0.05 × 5 = 4 N·m. The 50 A parking peak sets the size of the EPS fuse and cable; at speed the current, and the assist, are a tenth of that.

Answer. Rack force 5.5 kN at parking; motor torque 2.5 N·m and current 50 A at parking, 0.25 N·m and 5 A at 120 km/h.

Exam traps

  • More assist is not always better: assist must fall as vehicle speed rises, otherwise the steering feels numb and the driver loses road feel.
  • Steering torque is the driver's input measured by the torsion bar; assist torque is the motor's output at the pinion. They are not the same quantity.
  • Going from the motor to the pinion, multiply by N; going back from the assist the map asks for to the motor torque, divide by N, as the worked example does. Using the wrong direction makes the current 256 times wrong for a 16 : 1 gear.
  • The torque sensor measures twist across the torsion bar; the steering angle sensor measures absolute wheel position. Swapping their roles breaks return-to-centre and ESP.
  • Fail-safe does not mean the motor keeps assisting. It means the motor is disconnected and the driver still has a mechanical path to the road wheels.

5.4 Anti-lock braking and traction control

Anti-lock braking (ABS) and traction control (TCS) both keep a tyre near the slip where it grips best. ABS does this by building, holding and releasing brake pressure many times a second. TCS does it by reducing engine torque or braking a spinning drive wheel. The same wheel speed sensors and hydraulic modulator serve both. You meet them on cars, two-wheelers and trucks, especially in rain, on gravel and during panic stops.

You need: wheel speed sensors (automotive sensors and actuators), and the pressure modulator idea used again in ESP.

Slip ratio and the friction peak

Let v be the vehicle's forward speed in m/s, ω the wheel's angular speed in rad/s, and r the wheel radius in m. The tread of the wheel moves at ωr relative to the axle. Slip ratio is λ = (v − ωr)/v. λ is dimensionless. Free rolling gives ωr = v, so λ = 0. A locked wheel has ω = 0, so λ = 1. Under braking, slip rises from 0 towards 1.

λ = (v − ωr) / v

v is vehicle forward speed (m/s); ω is wheel angular speed (rad/s); r is wheel radius (m); λ is slip ratio (dimensionless).

The tyre's friction coefficient μ depends on λ. μ is the ratio of friction force to normal load, so it has no unit. A typical dry-asphalt curve rises steeply, peaks near λ = 0.15 to 0.2 and then falls as the wheel locks. Wet road has a lower peak at a slightly smaller slip. Figure 5.4.1 shows both curves. If λ is held near the peak, the tyre gives more braking force than a locked tyre. That is the whole aim of ABS. The controller does not need the exact peak; it watches wheel speed, estimates λ, and keeps it in a narrow band, often about 0.10 to 0.20.

For a drive wheel, wheel spin makes ωr larger than v. The same formula gives a negative λ. TCS works on the magnitude of that drive slip and limits it near the friction peak.

ABS hardware and the pressure cycle

Each wheel carries a toothed ring and a wheel speed sensor. An inductive sensor makes an AC voltage whose frequency follows tooth passage; a Hall sensor gives a clean square wave. The ABS electronic control unit (ECU) compares the four wheel speeds. A wheel dropping faster than the others is close to locking.

The hydraulic modulator sits between the master cylinder and each brake. For each wheel it has an inlet valve, an outlet valve and a low-pressure accumulator; a return pump sends fluid back to the master cylinder. The ECU runs three phases:

  1. Build: inlet valve open, outlet valve closed; brake pressure rises.
  2. Hold: both valves closed; pressure is trapped.
  3. Release: inlet closed, outlet open; fluid escapes to the accumulator and the return pump runs.

The ECU repeats build-hold-release about 5 to 15 times per second. This is fast enough to hold λ near the peak but slow enough for the hydraulics. Electronic brake distribution (EBD) uses the same hardware to apportion rear brake force and reduce rear lock before ABS acts. On loose gravel or fresh snow, a locked wheel can push a wedge of material and stop sooner. ABS prevents that wedge, so the stop can be longer. That is not a fault; it is the price of steering control.

Traction control

TCS uses the same wheel speed sensors. It compares a driven wheel with a non-driven wheel or with the vehicle speed. If a drive wheel spins, λ is negative and its magnitude rises past the grip peak. TCS has three levers, from slow and smooth to fast and local:

  • Close the electronic throttle: smooth, but slow, because the intake manifold must empty before torque falls.
  • Retard the ignition or cut fuel to some cylinders: engine torque falls within a few revolutions.
  • Brake the spinning wheel through the ABS modulator: the fastest, and it acts on one wheel only.

Braking one spinning wheel also helps an open differential. An open differential gives equal torque to both wheels, so a wheel on ice limits the torque to the gripped wheel. Braking the spinning wheel creates a reaction torque; the differential then sends more torque to the wheel with grip. This is the differential-brake idea. Some systems also reduce the throttle limit for a short time after the spin ends, so the tyre does not break away again. TCS and ESP share sensors and brakes, but TCS controls drive slip while ESP controls yaw.

Worked example

Question. A car brakes hard from v = 20 m/s. Its wheel radius is r = 0.30 m and its ABS target band is 0.10 ≤ λ ≤ 0.20. Find the slip ratio λ and the ABS action when the front-left wheel turns at (a) ω = 63 rad/s, (b) ω = 57 rad/s and (c) ω = 40 rad/s. (d) Read the dry-asphalt friction at each slip from Figure 5.4.1 and compare it with a locked wheel.

  1. (a) Tread speed ωr = 63 × 0.30 = 18.9 m/s, so λ = (20 − 18.9) ÷ 20 = 1.1 ÷ 20 = 0.055.
  2. 0.055 is below the band: the wheel is under-braked, so ABS builds pressure (inlet open, outlet closed).
  3. (b) ωr = 57 × 0.30 = 17.1 m/s, so λ = (20 − 17.1) ÷ 20 = 2.9 ÷ 20 = 0.145.
  4. 0.145 is inside the band, near the peak: ABS holds pressure (both valves closed).
  5. (c) ωr = 40 × 0.30 = 12 m/s, so λ = (20 − 12) ÷ 20 = 8 ÷ 20 = 0.40.
  6. 0.40 is past the peak and the wheel is heading for lock: ABS releases pressure (outlet open) until λ falls back into the band.
  7. (d) On the dry curve μ ≈ 0.91 at λ = 0.055, 1.17 at 0.145 and 1.07 at 0.40, against 0.76 for a locked wheel (λ = 1). Holding the band gives about 54 % more braking force than locking, and the wheel still steers.

Answer. λ = 0.055 (build), 0.145 (hold) and 0.40 (release); friction about 0.91, 1.17 and 1.07 against 0.76 when locked.

Exam traps

  • Slip ratio uses vehicle forward speed v as the reference; it is dimensionless, so do not add m/s.
  • λ = 0 is free rolling and λ = 1 is a locked wheel. For a spinning drive wheel the formula gives a negative λ; TCS uses the magnitude.
  • ABS can lengthen a stop on loose gravel or fresh snow because a locked wheel builds a material wedge.
  • Build opens the inlet valve; release opens the outlet valve and runs the return pump. Do not swap them.
  • The pressure cycle rate is about 5 to 15 per second (Hz), not per minute.

5.5 Electronic stability program (ESP)

Electronic stability program (ESP) is an active safety system that keeps a car pointing where the driver steers during a skid. It computes the yaw rate the steering and speed ask for, measures the yaw rate the car actually has, and brakes one wheel to create a correcting yaw moment. It also reduces engine torque when drive force is feeding the slide. You meet ESP in cars, SUVs and electric vehicles on wet roads, gravel and emergency swerves. Some syllabi call it ESC (electronic stability control) or VDC (vehicle dynamics control).

You need: anti-lock braking and traction control for wheel slip and brake-pressure modulation, and yaw rate and lateral acceleration sensors (automotive sensors and actuators).

Desired yaw rate from the steering

Yaw rate r is how fast the car turns about a vertical axis through its centre of gravity, measured in rad/s. The driver asks for yaw by turning the steering wheel. At road-wheel steering angle δ rad and speed v m/s, a simple bicycle model gives a reference yaw rate. It is called a bicycle model because it lumps the two front wheels into one and the two rear wheels into one.

rdes = vδ / (L + K v²)

rdes is the desired yaw rate in rad/s; v is vehicle speed in m/s; δ is road-wheel steering angle in rad; L is wheelbase in m; K is the understeer term in s²/m, so K v² is in m.

Large K means the car understeers more, so it needs more steering for the same yaw. ESP compares rdes with the measured rmeas. Count yaw rate and steering angle as positive in the direction of the turn, left or right; then the sign of the error e = rdes − rmeas tells the story. Positive e means the car yaws less than asked, which is understeer. Negative e means it yaws more than asked, which is oversteer. The reference is also capped by tyre grip: in a steady turn the lateral acceleration is v × r and cannot exceed μg, so rdes is held below μg ÷ v. ESP never asks for a yaw rate that friction cannot support.

Sensors and the brake hardware

Five signals do most of the work. A yaw rate sensor, usually a small vibrating MEMS gyro, measures r. A lateral acceleration sensor measures sideways acceleration ay in m/s² and helps check grip and sensor plausibility. A steering angle sensor at the column measures the steering-wheel angle; dividing it by the steering ratio, about 15 : 1, gives the road-wheel angle δ. Four wheel-speed sensors, shared with ABS, give vehicle speed and wheel slip. A brake pressure sensor tells the hydraulic unit what pressure it has built.

The ESP hydraulic unit has a pump and solenoid valves. It can raise, hold or release brake pressure at one wheel while leaving the others alone. This is the same modulator family used by ABS and traction control. ABS stops wheel lock under braking; TCS stops drive-wheel spin; ESP adds yaw correction. ESP can also send a torque-reduction request to the engine or motor controller over CAN.

An off switch lets the driver disable ESP, for example to rock a car out of snow or run on a dynamometer. The switch does not add grip; it only stops automatic intervention.

Understeer and oversteer corrections

In understeer the front tyres lose grip first. Actual yaw rate is lower than desired, so the nose runs wide. ESP brakes the rear inner wheel. That braking force acts behind the centre of gravity on the inside of the turn, and it creates a yaw moment that turns the nose into the corner. The size of the correction is the yaw moment Mz = Fx × (t ÷ 2), where Fx is the brake force at that wheel in N and t ÷ 2 is half the track width in m. In the worked example the car is understeering, so the rear inner wheel is the correct choice.

In oversteer the rear tyres lose grip first. Actual yaw rate is higher than desired, so the rear slides out and the nose tucks in. ESP brakes the front outer wheel. That braking force acts ahead of the centre of gravity on the outside of the turn, and it creates an outward yaw moment that reduces the extra yaw. Braking the wrong wheel makes the skid worse, so this is the first thing an examiner tests.

Engine torque reduction backs up the brakes. On driven wheels, less torque means less wheelspin and more lateral grip left for steering. ESP cannot exceed tyre grip. On ice it can only help the car do what the tyres allow; it cannot create friction. It also cannot beat physics at high speed.

Worked example

Question. A car in a left turn has wheelbase L = 2.6 m, track width 1.5 m and understeer term K = 0.0025 s²/m. It travels at v = 20 m/s with road-wheel steering angle δ = 0.05 rad. The yaw rate sensor reads rmeas = 0.200 rad/s. (a) Find the desired yaw rate in rad/s and °/s. (b) Find the yaw error. (c) State whether the car understeers or oversteers and which wheel ESP should brake. (d) ESP brakes that wheel with 2000 N; find the yaw moment, and check the desired yaw rate against the grip limit for μ = 0.9.

  1. Compute K v² = 0.0025 × 20² = 0.0025 × 400 = 1.0 m.
  2. Denominator L + K v² = 2.6 + 1.0 = 3.6 m.
  3. Numerator v δ = 20 × 0.05 = 1.0 m·rad/s.
  4. rdes = 1.0 / 3.6 = 0.2778 rad/s ≈ 0.278 rad/s.
  5. In degrees per second: rdes = 0.2778 × 180/π = 15.915 °/s.
  6. Measured: rmeas = 0.200 rad/s = 0.200 × 180/π = 11.459 °/s.
  7. Error e = rdes − rmeas = 0.2778 − 0.200 = 0.0778 rad/s ≈ 0.078 rad/s = 4.456 °/s.
  8. e is positive, so actual yaw is less than desired: understeer. In a left turn the rear inner wheel is the left rear; brake it.
  9. Yaw moment: Mz = 2000 N × (1.5 ÷ 2) m = 2000 × 0.75 = 1500 N·m, turning the nose into the turn.
  10. Grip limit: μg ÷ v = 0.9 × 9.81 ÷ 20 = 0.441 rad/s. The desired 0.278 rad/s is below it, so the road can support it and rdes is not capped.

Exam traps

  • Understeer is corrected by braking the rear inner wheel; oversteer is corrected by braking the front outer wheel. Reversing them is the classic wrong answer.
  • Use radians in r = vδ/(L + K v²). Convert degrees to rad first; °/s is only for display.
  • ESP cannot raise tyre grip. It only uses the grip available; a car on ice still slides.
  • ESP is not ABS or TCS. ABS stops wheel lock; TCS stops drive-wheel spin; ESP corrects yaw.
  • Positive error rdes − rmeas means understeer, not oversteer. Keep the sign.

5.6 Active suspension control

Active suspension uses a force actuator at each corner to push or pull the body, so the body stays flat while the wheels follow the road. A passive suspension has fixed springs and dampers. A semi-active suspension varies damper force. A fully active suspension adds a hydraulic or electric actuator that can both push and pull. Luxury cars and some electric vehicles use it. Only a few universities teach this chapter.

You need: basic spring–mass–damper vibration from a mechanics course, and ESP for yaw and roll control using brakes.

Three layouts

Passive suspension: springs and dampers have fixed rates. The body and wheel move as the road dictates.

Semi-active suspension: the damper is adjustable. A solenoid or motor changes the damping coefficient many times a second. It can only dissipate energy, so its force always opposes the relative velocity across the damper (body minus wheel). It cannot inject energy to hold the body flat over a bump.

Fully active suspension: a hydraulic or electric actuator at each corner supplies force in both directions. It can hold the body flat over a bump while the wheel moves. The actuator adds energy, so it needs a pump or a high-current electric drive.

Sensors and skyhook law

Sensors: a body accelerometer at each corner measures body motion; a wheel accelerometer measures wheel hop; a ride-height sensor measures the gap between body and wheel.

The corner accelerometer signal is integrated to give body velocity, with a high-pass filter to stop drift, and that velocity feeds the skyhook law.

The skyhook law imagines a damper hung from a fixed sky. The controller measures body velocity and asks the actuator for a force that opposes it.

F = −csky × vbody

F is actuator force in newtons (N); csky is skyhook gain in N·s/m; vbody is body velocity in m/s, positive upward. The minus sign means force opposes body velocity. Magnitude is F = csky × |vbody|.

For a semi-active damper, the controller applies F only if F × vrel < 0, where vrel is the relative velocity across the damper (body minus wheel) in m/s. Then it sets the damping c = −F ÷ vrel, kept between the damper's minimum and maximum. Otherwise it commands minimum damping, and the body is not held.

The controller computes F and commands damper current or actuator pressure. A larger body velocity asks for a larger force.

Roll, pitch and limits

Roll control: in a turn the body leans outward. The controller raises force on the outer corners and lowers it on the inner corners. The left–right force difference creates a roll moment that cancels the lean.

Pitch control: braking dips the nose; accelerating lifts it. Front–rear force distribution creates a pitch moment.

Bandwidth: the loop must respond faster than body motion. It must also cover wheel hop at about 10–15 Hz, so actuator and controller bandwidth is usually well above 20 Hz, not just a few times the 1–2 Hz body ride frequency. Power cost is real: a fully active system draws kilowatts, while a semi-active damper draws tens of watts.

Worked example

Question. One corner uses skyhook control with csky = 5000 N·s/m. At one instant the body moves up at vbody = 0.08 m/s. (a) Find the skyhook force. (b) On a fully active corner, an electromagnetic actuator gives 250 N per ampere; find its current. (c) On a semi-active corner, the damping can be set from cmin = 500 to cmax = 4000 N·s/m, with c = 500 + 3500 × I for a valve current I from 0 to 1.0 A. Find the damping and current when the wheel moves down at 0.12 m/s. (d) Repeat (c) when the wheel moves up at 0.18 m/s.

  1. (a) F = −csky × vbody = −5000 × 0.08 = −400 N: 400 N downward on the body.
  2. (b) Fully active: I = 400 ÷ 250 = 1.6 A, in the direction that pulls the body down.
  3. (c) Relative velocity vrel = vbody − vwheel = 0.08 − (−0.12) = +0.20 m/s; the damper is extending. Test: F × vrel = −400 × 0.20 = −80, which is negative, so a damper can make this force.
  4. Damping needed: c = −F ÷ vrel = 400 ÷ 0.20 = 2000 N·s/m, inside 500 to 4000. Current I = (2000 − 500) ÷ 3500 = 0.429 A.
  5. (d) vrel = 0.08 − 0.18 = −0.10 m/s; the damper is being compressed. Test: F × vrel = −400 × (−0.10) = +40, which is positive: a damper being compressed pushes the body up, never down, so the skyhook force cannot be made.
  6. Command minimum damping: c = 500 N·s/m, I = 0 A. The damper force is −500 × (−0.10) = +50 N, upward but small, which is the least harm it can do until vrel changes sign.

Answer. Skyhook force 400 N downward; 1.6 A in the fully active actuator; semi-active 2000 N·s/m at 0.429 A when the wheel moves down, and minimum damping (0 A, +50 N) when the wheel moves up.

Exam traps

  • Skyhook damps the body, not the wheel; the wheel still follows the road.
  • Sign slip: F = −csky × vbody. Reversing the sign adds force in the direction of motion.
  • Semi-active can only dissipate energy; its force always opposes relative velocity across the damper and cannot hold the body flat over a bump.
  • Gain units: csky is N·s/m, not N/m (spring rate) or N·s (impulse).
  • The semi-active test uses vrel, not vbody. The same upward body velocity can be reachable or not, depending on whether the wheel moves down or up at that instant.

5.7 Airbags and passive safety systems

Passive safety saves you without driver action: structure and restraints. The crumple zone folds so occupants lose speed over a longer distance and time. The seat belt holds them; a pretensioner removes slack and a load limiter caps chest force. The airbag is a supplement, fired by the crash sensor within milliseconds. New cars sold in India must now carry front airbags for the driver and the front passenger, from a small hatchback upward. Buses and trucks rely mostly on the structure and on belts.

You need: accelerometers (automotive sensors and actuators) and the vehicle electrical supply for the squib circuit.

The crash pulse and the crumple zone

During a crash the car does not stop instantly. The front structure folds over a designed length: the crumple zone. Car deceleration against time is the crash pulse. Its area is the speed change; its length is the time restraints get.

a = v² ÷ (2s), t = v ÷ a

a is the average deceleration in m/s², v is the impact speed in m/s, s is the crush distance in m, and t is the stopping time in s.

At 50 km/h (13.9 m/s) with 0.6 m crush: a = 13.9² ÷ (2 × 0.6) = 161 m/s², about 16.4 g, where g = 9.81 m/s², and t = 13.9 ÷ 161 = 0.086 s, or 86 ms. Without crush, the same speed change over a few millimetres would take under a millisecond and give about two thousand g. Even a few centimetres of crush, as in a rigid pole hit, still gives several hundred g over a few milliseconds. That gap is the point of passive safety.

The seat belt is the primary restraint. It spreads load over the pelvis and rib cage. A pretensioner, fired by the crash signal, pulls slack out in about 10 ms. A load limiter then lets the belt pay out at a few kN, so the chest avoids the peak. Belt first, bag second.

Inside the airbag ECU: MEMS accelerometer and safing sensor

The airbag ECU (electronic control unit) sits under the centre console or the floor. Its main sensor is a MEMS accelerometer: a silicon proof mass on thin springs, whose capacitance changes as the mass moves. The output is a voltage proportional to deceleration along the car's axis, calibrated in g.

The ECU samples this about every millisecond, filters it, compares it with a stored threshold, and often integrates it into a speed change Δv. The threshold must be crossed and held for a few milliseconds, or nothing fires.

A second, independent sensor sits in series in the firing circuit: the safing sensor. It is a mechanical switch (a ball held by a magnet that closes at a few g) or a second accelerometer. The squib, a tiny heater inside the inflator, gets current only when the accelerometer and the safing sensor agree.

  1. Key on: self-test. Squib continuity is checked; an open circuit stores a fault code and lights the warning lamp.
  2. Crash: signal sampled, filtered, compared with the threshold curve.
  3. Threshold crossed and held, safing sensor closed: fire decision.
  4. A current pulse of a few amperes for a couple of milliseconds is sent to the squib.
  5. The same event unlocks the doors, switches on the hazard lamps and cuts the fuel pump.

A capacitor inside the ECU holds enough charge to fire the bag even if the battery cable breaks in the crash.

Inflator, venting and the other airbags

The squib's pulse ignites a solid propellant, which decomposes in about 20 to 30 ms into a large volume of nitrogen-rich gas; older units used sodium azide, newer ones guanidine salts.

The bag is woven nylon, folded behind a cover with a hidden seam. Gas fills it in about 25 ms after the fire signal, so it is full around 50 ms after impact. Gas then escapes through vent holes and through the fabric, so the bag softens before the occupant arrives. The bag alone cannot stop the body; it spreads the load over a larger area and keeps the head off the steering wheel and the A-pillar, while the belt does the stopping. This is the ride-down phase.

  • Driver bag in the steering wheel; passenger bag in the dashboard.
  • Side thorax bags in the seat or door; curtain bags along the roof rail for the head.
  • Knee bags under the dashboard, so the legs do not slide forward.

A side impact leaves very little crush distance, so side and curtain bags fire earlier, within 5 to 10 ms. Curtains stay inflated for several seconds in case the car rolls over. Rollover sensing uses a roll-rate sensor plus lateral accelerometers.

Occupant detection, child seats and the deployment threshold

Not every bump fires the bag. Each maker stores a deployment threshold, roughly a speed change in the region of 15 to 25 km/h for a frontal impact. A parking knock must not fire it: that would be costly and could injure a small person sitting close.

The full name is supplemental restraint system, SRS, and the word supplemental is literal. A belted occupant moves late and meets a bag that is already full and venting. An unbelted occupant moves early and far, and can meet the bag while its cover and fabric are still opening at high speed. That is how the device meant to protect can injure an unbelted or out-of-position person.

Occupant detection completes the rule. A pressure bladder or a capacitive mat in the passenger seat classifies it as empty, child seat, small adult or adult. If a child seat is detected, the passenger bag is suppressed. A rear-facing child seat must never sit in front of an active passenger bag.

A belt switch in each buckle drives the reminder lamp and tells the ECU which deployment path to use.

5.8 Advanced driver assistance systems (ADAS)

ADAS is a stack of sensors, perception, fusion, planning and actuation that warns the driver or takes over braking, steering or speed control. It exists because a machine can sense some hazards earlier than a tired driver, but it must work at a defined level of automation. You meet it in automatic emergency braking, lane keeping, blind spot warning, adaptive cruise and parking assist. This chapter is tagged advanced; only a few universities teach ADAS as a full chapter.

You need 5.2 for adaptive cruise, 5.3 for steering angle, and 5.7 for passive safety as the last resort.

The five-layer stack and SAE levels

ADAS is not one box. Sensors give raw data. Perception turns pixels and echoes into objects, lanes and signs. Fusion combines the sensors. Planning chooses a path and speed. Actuation commands brakes, steering and throttle. Figure 5.8.1 draws the chain from sensors on the left to actuation on the right, with the stopping distance of the worked example below it.

SAE levels run from 0 to 5. Level 0 has no automation. Level 1 assists one task, such as adaptive cruise. Level 2 handles steering and speed together, but the driver still monitors the road and remains responsible. Level 3 is conditional, Level 4 is high and Level 5 is full automation. Most ADAS here is Level 1 or 2.

A warning only alerts. An intervening function moves a control. Lane departure warning alerts; lane keep steers. The same stack serves both warning and action. The layers matter more than the brand names.

Sensors, fusion and functions

Every sensor is a trade-off. Camera: detail, low cost, weak in glare and fog. Radar: range and speed in bad weather, poor shape. Lidar: 3D cloud, higher cost, weak in dust. Ultrasonic: short range, cheap, good for parking.

Fusion combines them because one weakness is another strength. A camera sees lane lines that radar cannot; radar keeps measuring range and closing speed in rain, fog and low sun that blind a camera, while the camera can tell a stopped car from a bridge or sign that radar sees only as a stationary echo. Fusion needs time alignment and one vehicle coordinate frame.

Common functions: AEB brakes for a collision; lane departure warning alerts; lane keep steers; blind spot detection watches the side; adaptive cruise holds a gap (see adaptive cruise control); traffic sign recognition reads signs; parking assist uses ultrasonics; driver monitoring watches eyes and head. AEB, lane keep, adaptive cruise and parking assist act on the brakes, steering or throttle; lane departure warning, blind spot detection and traffic sign recognition mostly warn, though some blind spot systems also brake or steer. Driver monitoring is becoming common in Indian cars with camera-based attention checks.

AEB, delay and the safety limit

AEB shows the chain. Perception finds the object and estimates range and closing speed. Planning decides a crash is likely. Actuation builds brake pressure. Time passes in sensing, decision and brake build, so AEB cannot beat physics.

s = v td + v²/(2a)

s is stopping distance (m), v is speed (m/s), td is delay (s), a is braking deceleration (m/s²).

A quicker measure is time-to-collision, TTC = d ÷ vrel, the gap divided by the closing speed; AEB warns first and then brakes as TTC falls. At low speed AEB can stop the car. At high speed it may only reduce impact speed. The numbers decide the limit, not a fixed claim. The driver must still steer and brake in a Level 2 car.

A warning-only system does not brake; an intervening system does. Passive safety, such as the airbag, is the last resort after AEB fails. Functional safety asks what happens if a sensor is wrong or a brake command is late; ISO 26262 functional safety answers it formally. Fusion reduces false alarms and missed stops.

Worked example

Question. A car at 50 km/h (use v = 13.9 m/s) approaches a stopped car. The AEB delay from first detection to full braking is 0.5 s, then braking is 8 m/s². Find the delay distance, braking distance and total distance; the TTC and the margin when the stopped car is first detected at 25 m, 30 m and 18 m; and the impact speed in the 18 m case.

  1. Delay: 13.9 × 0.5 = 6.95 m.
  2. Braking: 13.9² ÷ (2 × 8) = 12.08 m.
  3. Total: 6.95 + 12.08 = 19.03 m.
  4. Margins: 25 − 19.03 = 5.97 m; 30 − 19.03 = 10.97 m; 18 − 19.03 = −1.03 m.
  5. TTC at 25 m = 25 ÷ 13.9 = 1.80 s (2.16 s at 30 m, 1.29 s at 18 m). A stop is possible from 25 m and 30 m; from 18 m it is not.
  6. Impact speed from 18 m: braking starts after 6.95 m, so it has 18 − 6.95 = 11.05 m. v² = 13.9² − 2 × 8 × 11.05 = 193.21 − 176.80 = 16.41, so v = 4.05 m/s (14.6 km/h), down from 50 km/h.

Answer. Delay 6.95 m, braking 12.08 m, total 19.03 m; margins 5.97 m (25 m), 10.97 m (30 m) and −1.03 m (18 m), so only the first two allow a stop; from 18 m the car still hits at 4.05 m/s (14.6 km/h).

5.9 PI and PD controllers for vehicle speed control

A vehicle speed loop compares reference and measured speed and commands tractive force. A PI controller removes steady-state speed error; a PD controller adds damping. The vehicle is modelled as a first-order lag, so two gains and one time constant describe the loop. Cruise control, electric-vehicle torque control and ADAS speed tracking use this. Only a few universities teach the tuning method here.

You need: Chapter 5.2 for the cruise loop, and first-order lag plus Laplace transforms from a control course.

Plant, PI law and lambda tuning

m dv/dt = F − b v

m is mass (kg); v is speed (m/s); F is tractive force (N); b is resistance (N per m/s).

Rearranged, τ dv/dt + v = K F, with τ = m/b in s and K = 1/b in (m/s)/N. In Laplace form, V(s)/F(s) = K/(τs + 1).

Error e = vref − v, with vref the wanted speed. PI law: u = Kp e + Ki ∫e dt, where u is commanded force (N), Kp is N per (m/s), and Ki is N/m (newton per metre of integrated speed error; Ki = Kp/Ti). Proportional action alone leaves an offset. At steady state Kp(vref − v) = b v, so ess = b vref/(Kp + b). For Kp much larger than b this is close to b vref/Kp. The integral supplies the force and drives e to zero.

Lambda tuning chooses the closed-loop time constant λ and cancels the plant pole with the PI zero: Kp = τ/(K λ), Ti = τ, Ki = Kp/Ti. Smaller λ gives faster response but larger force, so check actuator saturation. A reference model filters vref, often as 1/(λs + 1), before the loop sees it. On a real vehicle with a rate-limited actuator this slows the demanded force and limits saturation and overshoot; in the ideal first-order loop modelled here the loop already has no overshoot, so the main gain is a smooth force command.

PD damping and practical derivative

PD law u = Kp e + Kd de/dt, where Kd is N per (m/s²). The derivative predicts error growth and opposes fast speed change, adding damping. With constant vref, de/dt = −dv/dt, so derivative on measurement is u = Kp e − Kd dv/dt. This avoids a kick when vref steps.

Measured speed carries sensor noise. A raw derivative amplifies it, so filter the derivative with time constant Tf, or use a small Kd. The derivative path becomes s/(Tf s + 1): a first-order lag with time constant Tf (in s) on the derivative. PI plus reference model is common for speed; PD alone cannot remove offset because at steady state de/dt = 0. Full Bode and root-locus design belongs to a control course.

For a step in vref, the reference filter turns the jump into a smooth rise before the PI block sees it. Worked example 2 puts numbers on it: the 2500 N jump in force disappears and the peak acceleration falls from 1.67 to 0.61 m/s².

Worked example

Question. A 1500 kg vehicle has resistance b = 100 N per m/s. (a) Find K and τ. (b) Choose λ = 3 s and find Kp, Ti, Ki. (c) With vref = 30 m/s, find the steady error if only proportional action is used.

  1. 1500 dv/dt = F − 100v, so V(s)/F(s) = 1/(1500s + 100) = 0.01/(15s + 1).
  2. K = 0.01 (m/s)/N, τ = 15 s.
  3. Kp = τ/(K λ) = 15/(0.01 × 3) = 500 N per (m/s).
  4. Ti = τ = 15 s; Ki = Kp/Ti = 500/15 = 33.3 N/m.
  5. Proportional only: at steady state the force Kp e must balance the resistance b v, so 500(30 − v) = 100v, v = 15000 ÷ 600 = 25 m/s and e = 5 m/s. This is ess = b vref ÷ (Kp + b) = 3000 ÷ 600. With PI the integral supplies the 2500 N at zero error, so the error falls to zero.

Answer. K = 0.01 (m/s)/N, τ = 15 s, Kp = 500 N/(m/s), Ti = 15 s, Ki = 33.3 N/m; proportional-only offset 5 m/s (the car settles at 25 m/s).

Worked example 2

Question. The car of worked example 1 cruises at 25 m/s under the PI loop (Kp = 500 N/(m/s), Ti = 15 s), so the integral holds the 2500 N that resistance needs. The driver raises vref to 30 m/s in one step. (a) Find the force and acceleration just after the step. (b) Find the speed after 3 s and 9 s. (c) Repeat with a reference model 1/(3s + 1) on vref, and compare the peak acceleration.

  1. With Ti = τ the PI zero cancels the plant pole: loop gain = Kp K ÷ (τ s) = 500 × 0.01 ÷ (15 s) = 1 ÷ (3 s), so the closed loop is 1 ÷ (3s + 1), first order with λ = 3 s.
  2. (a) Just after the step e = 5 m/s while the integral still holds 2500 N: F = 2500 + 500 × 5 = 5000 N. Net force 5000 − 100 × 25 = 2500 N, so a = 2500 ÷ 1500 = 1.67 m/s².
  3. (b) The first-order rise is v(t) = 25 + 5(1 − e−t/3): v(3) = 25 + 5(1 − e⁻¹) = 25 + 3.16 = 28.16 m/s; v(9) = 25 + 5(1 − e⁻³) = 29.75 m/s.
  4. (c) The filtered reference rises as 25 + 5(1 − e−t/3), so the error and the force do not jump at t = 0. Speed now follows two lags in series: v(t) = 25 + 5[1 − (1 + t/3)e−t/3].
  5. v(3) = 25 + 5(1 − 2e⁻¹) = 25 + 1.32 = 26.32 m/s; v(9) = 25 + 5(1 − 4e⁻³) = 29.00 m/s.
  6. Acceleration dv/dt = 5(t/9)e−t/3 peaks at t = 3 s: 5 ÷ (3e) = 0.61 m/s², against 1.67 m/s² at once without the model. The peak force falls from 5000 N to about 3550 N.

Answer. Without the model: 5000 N and 1.67 m/s² at once, 28.16 m/s at 3 s and 29.75 m/s at 9 s. With it: no jump, 26.32 m/s at 3 s, 29.00 m/s at 9 s, peak acceleration 0.61 m/s² and peak force about 3550 N. Neither overshoots.

Exam traps

  • Error sign: e = vref − v, not v − vref.
  • Kp alone leaves a steady offset; the integral term removes it.
  • Derivative on noisy measured speed amplifies noise; filter it.
  • Units: Kp N per m/s; Ki N/m; Kd N per (m/s²).
  • Too small λ demands huge force and saturates the actuator.

Evaluate Evaluate the design choices in an airbag system: where the crash sensors sit, how the firing threshold is set, how fast the bag must inflate, and why the seat belt must still be worn. 10 marks

Points to cover. Sensors: front accelerometers, side pressure or acceleration sensors, rollover sensing; Threshold: severe enough deceleration within a short window, with a safing sensor to prevent false fire; Timing: full inflation in tens of milliseconds, before the occupant moves far; Bag vents energy, belt carries the steady restraint; unbelted occupant needs different tuning; Hazards: out-of-position occupants, child seats, bag replacement after firing.

Model answer.

  1. Where the sensors sit: the main accelerometer is in the airbag ECU near the centre of the car, where it feels the whole crash pulse; front upfront sensors in the crush zone see a severe frontal crash early; side sensors (accelerometers in the pillars or pressure sensors in the doors) are needed because a side impact gives only a few milliseconds; a roll-rate sensor with lateral accelerometers detects rollover. Evaluation: central plus satellite sensors give both early and reliable detection.
  2. How the threshold is set: the ECU filters the acceleration, integrates it to a speed change Δv and fires only when Δv and its rate cross a stored curve for a few milliseconds, with an independent safing sensor agreeing. Frontal thresholds are roughly a speed change of 15 to 25 km/h. Evaluation: too low a threshold fires on kerb strikes and parking knocks; too high misses real crashes; the safing sensor guards against a single fault.
  3. How fast the bag must inflate: for a 50 km/h barrier crash with 0.6 m of crush, a = 13.9² ÷ (2 × 0.6) = 161 m/s² (16.4 g) and the car stops in 2 × 0.6 ÷ 13.9 = 86 ms. The bag must be full before the head of the belted occupant reaches it; with the threshold crossed near 10 ms, firing near 25 ms and about 25 ms to fill, it is full near 50 ms, well inside the pulse. Side bags have only 5 to 10 ms.
  4. Venting: the bag vents through holes and fabric so it softens as the occupant arrives; it spreads the load over the head and chest, while the belt with pretensioner and load limiter carries the main restraint force through the pelvis and rib cage.
  5. Why the belt must still be worn: SRS means supplemental. An unbelted occupant moves early and far and can meet the bag while it is still opening at high speed; the belt also keeps the occupant in place in side impacts, rollovers and second impacts where the frontal bag does not help.
  6. Hazards and their design answers: out-of-position occupants (seat sensors, two-stage inflators, depowered bags); rear-facing child seats (passenger occupant detection and bag suppression); a fired module must be replaced, and the ECU stores the crash record and must usually be replaced too.
  7. Overall evaluation: the design trades early firing (needs sensors near the impact and low thresholds) against false firing (needs safing and held thresholds); the numbers above show the bag must work inside the 86 ms crash pulse, which only a belted occupant matches well.
  8. Check: t = 2s ÷ v = 1.2 ÷ 13.9 = 0.086 s agrees with v ÷ a = 13.9 ÷ 161; a bag full at 50 ms is well inside it.
Evaluate Evaluate PI versus PD control for vehicle speed. Explain why practical speed loops use PI and where the damping that PD would give is obtained instead. 12 marks

Points to cover. PD acts on the error's rate; in speed loops it amplifies sensor noise and can fight the throttle's own lag; PI gives zero steady-state error against grade and headwind, which matters more than extra damping; Damping comes from the plant's inertia and from rate limits and feed-forward on the throttle map; Where PD does appear: inner torque loops and active suspension damping; Practical gain limits, anti-windup and override by the driver.

Model answer.

  1. Plant: m dv/dt = F − bv; for m = 1500 kg, b = 100 N/(m/s) the speed loop sees a first-order lag with τ = 15 s. The controller must hold speed against resistance, grade and wind, and follow a new set speed comfortably.
  2. PD law: F = Kpe + Kd de/dt. At steady state de/dt = 0, so PD is the same as P: the speed settles where Kpe = bv. With Kp = 500 and vref = 30 m/s, v = 25 m/s and e = 5 m/s, and every 2 % of grade (294 N) adds 294 ÷ 600 = 0.49 m/s more. A driver notices a 5 m/s (18 km/h) offset at once.
  3. PI law: F = Kpe + Ki∫e dt. The integral keeps growing while any error remains, so at steady state e = 0 against any constant resistance, grade or wind; this is the main requirement of cruise control.
  4. Noise: the wheel speed signal has noise from tooth spacing and road roughness. With a 10 ms sample, noise of 0.05 m/s between samples looks like 0.05 ÷ 0.01 = 5 m/s² of acceleration; with Kd = 200 N/(m/s²) that is 1000 N of chattering force, the same size as the force the loop needs. A filter s/(Tfs + 1) helps but adds lag.
  5. Lag: the throttle and engine respond with their own delay; derivative action pushes hard on the first sign of change and can fight that delay, giving a jerky response. The speed loop does not need it, because the plant is first order and has no oscillation of its own to damp.
  6. Where the damping comes from instead: the car's inertia and resistance (τ = 15 s) already make the loop well damped; lambda tuning (Ti = τ) gives a first-order, non-overshooting response; a reference model 1/(λs + 1) or a rate limit on vref smooths the force demand (no 2500 N jump for a 5 m/s step); and feed-forward from the throttle map and road grade gives fast response without high gain.
  7. Where PD does appear: in inner loops with oscillating dynamics, such as a torque loop with driveline shuffle, an electronic throttle position loop, or active suspension damping, where the derivative term is the damping force itself.
  8. Practical points for the PI: anti-windup when the throttle is fully open or closed; gain limits so the force step stays inside the actuator; and override by the driver on the brake or accelerator.
  9. Summary:

Glossary

Assist map
The lookup table that turns measured steering torque and vehicle speed into a motor current for power steering.
Bicycle model
A vehicle model that lumps the two front wheels into one and the two rear wheels into one, used to compute the reference yaw rate.
CAN bus
The shared two-wire serial network over which controllers such as ESP, EPS and the engine ECU exchange signals.
Dead band
A small range of error around the set point inside which the controller deliberately does nothing, to stop actuator chatter.
Electronic throttle
A throttle plate driven by a motor under ECU command, with pedal position as a request rather than a direct link.
Friction compensation
A control term that adds a small torque in the direction of motion to overcome stiction in the steering or pedal mechanism.
Integral windup
The unwanted growth of an integral term while the actuator is saturated, which causes overshoot when the error finally reverses.
Lock-up clutch
The clutch inside a torque converter that ties the turbine to the impeller at cruising speed, removing converter slip; often run with a small controlled slip.
Oversteer
The condition where the car turns more than the steering asks for, so the rear swings out.
Quarter-car model
A two-mass model of one corner of a car, with the sprung body mass on a spring and damper above the unsprung wheel mass on the tyre stiffness.
Return-to-centre
An EPS function that steers the wheels back towards straight ahead at low speed when the driver lets go.
Safing sensor
A second, independent crash sensor in series with the airbag firing circuit; the squib fires only when it agrees with the main accelerometer.
Seat belt pretensioner
A device that pulls the belt tight within milliseconds of a crash, before the airbag is needed.
Sensor fusion
The process of merging measurements from several sensors into one consistent description of the surroundings.
Shift map
The stored set of upshift and downshift speeds against throttle opening that fixes when the gearbox changes gear.
Skyhook damping
A suspension control law that damps the body against a notional fixed reference while leaving the wheels free.
Slip ratio
The fractional difference between vehicle speed and wheel rim speed, λ = (v − ωr)/v, which sets how much grip a tyre gives.
Squib
The small electric detonator inside the airbag module that ignites the gas generator on command.
Steady-state error
The error that remains a long time after a step change when the controller has no integral action to remove it.
Time headway
The gap to the vehicle ahead divided by your speed, in seconds (also called the time gap); ACC controls this rather than distance.
Time-to-collision
The gap divided by the closing speed, in seconds; a measure used to warn or brake before a rear-end hit.
Torsion bar
A calibrated bar that twists in proportion to the torque applied through it, used in EPS to measure steering torque.
Understeer
The condition where the car turns less than the steering asks for, so the front wheels run wide.
Variable-ratio steering
Steering whose ratio of road-wheel angle to steering-wheel angle is not fixed: a variable-ratio rack changes it with steering angle, and active steering (a planetary gear and motor in the column) changes it with vehicle speed.
Yaw moment
The turning moment about the vertical axis made by a single wheel's brake force acting at a distance from the centre of gravity.
Yaw rate
The speed at which the car turns about its vertical axis, measured by a yaw rate sensor in degrees per second.
