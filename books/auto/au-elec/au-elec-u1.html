1.1 Evolution of electronics in automobiles

A car in 1960 used electricity mainly for starting, ignition and lamps. A car today uses electronics for engine control, braking, steering, comfort and safety. This chapter traces that change as a cause-and-effect chain: bigger electrical loads demanded better charging; emission and safety laws demanded precise control; precise control demanded sensors, actuators and networks. The chain explains why the 12 V battery, alternator, ECU and CAN bus sit together in one system. It also prepares you to study batteries, starting and charging in the rest of this unit.

You need: basic DC circuits from first-year physics (voltage, current, resistance and power); for the battery itself see Chapter 1.4.

From hand crank to a regulated 12 V bus

Early petrol cars were started by hand. The driver turned a crank, which rotated the crankshaft until the engine fired. A magneto, a small generator driven by the engine, made the high-voltage spark. The only electrical load was ignition, so a battery was optional. Hand cranking was dangerous: a mis-timed kick could break a wrist.

Battery-coil ignition changed the pattern. A battery supplied current to an ignition coil, the coil stepped voltage up, and a distributor sent the pulse to each spark plug. In 1912 the electric self-starter (on the Cadillac, designed by Charles Kettering) made starting an electric motor job, so the car needed a rechargeable battery, and a belt-driven dynamo charged it. The system voltage was 6 V at first. As lamps, wipers and radios were added, current grew and the voltage drop in the cables became a problem. From the mid-1950s car makers moved to 12 V systems: the battery is nominally 12 V (about 12.6 V at rest) and the charging system holds the bus near 14 V while the engine runs.

The dynamo had a commutator and brushes, and its output was limited at low engine speed. From 1960 the alternator began to replace it: a rotating field winding induces three-phase AC in stationary stator coils, and a diode bridge rectifies the AC to DC. It charges even at idle. The voltage regulator holds the bus near 14 V by adjusting field current. See Chapter 1.6 and Chapter 1.7 for the circuits and curves.

Emission laws pull electronics into the engine

From the 1970s, first the United States and Japan and then Europe limited carbon monoxide (CO), hydrocarbons (HC) and nitrogen oxides (NOx) from petrol engines. The European steps are numbered Euro I to Euro 6, and India adopted matching Bharat Stage (BS) norms a few years behind each Euro step, then jumped from BS IV straight to BS VI in April 2020. The table gives the years; the limits and test cycles themselves come with the emission norms in electronic engine control. Here the point is cause and effect: a carburettor meters fuel by mechanical jets and cannot hold a precise air-fuel ratio as speed, load and temperature change. A three-way catalytic converter needs the ratio held near the chemically correct value, so mechanical fuelling was not enough.

Electronic fuel injection (EFI) replaced the carburettor on new petrol cars. An electronic control unit (ECU) reads sensors: air flow or manifold pressure, crank position, throttle position, coolant temperature and exhaust oxygen. It calculates injector pulse width and ignition timing, then drives injectors and the ignition coil. The same ECU can diagnose faults and store codes. Sensors and actuators, and fuel and spark control, are later topics of this subject. Electronic ignition first removed contact points, which wore and needed gap setting. A reluctor or Hall sensor triggered a transistor, giving a stable spark.

The result is a feedback loop. The oxygen sensor measures leftover oxygen in exhaust; the ECU trims fuel until the mixture returns to target. This loop cuts emissions and holds fuel economy. It also makes the engine dependent on electrical power and signal quality.

Networks, assistance and the system map

As ECUs multiplied, separate wires for every signal became heavy and hard to diagnose. A data bus lets many controllers share a pair of wires. CAN (Controller Area Network), developed by Bosch in the 1980s and first used in a production car in 1991, is the common bus in cars; it carries short messages with identifiers and error checks. CAN frames and protocols come with in-vehicle networks. The bus joins engine, transmission, brake and body controllers. On-board diagnostics (OBD-II) at first allowed several protocols; CAN became the required one for new cars in the United States from the 2008 model year.

Safety systems followed the same path. Anti-lock braking, electronic stability program and airbags need fast sensing and actuation; adaptive cruise control and automatic emergency braking add radar, camera and fusion. These are advanced driver assistance systems (ADAS), covered with vehicle motion control and safety. Electric and hybrid traction adds a high-voltage battery and inverter beside the 12 V bus; the separation and safety devices are in Chapter 1.11.

One system map keeps the mechanical and electrical sides together. The engine block holds the cylinders; the cylinder head holds valves and camshaft; pistons connect through connecting rods to the crankshaft. The four-stroke cycle needs spark and fuel at the right instant, so crank and cam sensors feed the ECU. The drivetrain takes torque through the transmission, drive shaft and differential to the wheels. Suspension, brakes and steering are controlled or assisted electrically. None of these parts is redesigned here; the electrical system serves them.

Worked example

Question. A 1970 car has a 35 A dynamo; a 2020 car has a 120 A alternator. Both are regulated at 14.0 V. Compare the maximum electrical power available.

  1. Use P = V × I, where P is power in watts, V is the regulated bus voltage in volts, and I is current in amperes.
  2. 1970 car: P₁ = 14.0 V × 35 A = 490 W.
  3. 2020 car: P₂ = 14.0 V × 120 A = 1680 W.
  4. Increase = P₂ − P₁ = 1680 W − 490 W = 1190 W.
  5. Ratio = P₂ ÷ P₁ = 1680 W ÷ 490 W = 3.428..., about 3.4.

Answer. The 2020 alternator can supply 1680 W against 490 W, an increase of 1190 W, about 3.4 times the 1970 value.

Worked example 2

Question. A car of the 1950s has 300 W of lamps and accessories. The feed cable and its connections have a resistance of 0.01 Ω. Compare a 6 V system with a 12 V system: find the current, the voltage drop in the cable, the drop as a percentage of the system voltage, and the power lost in the cable.

  1. 6 V system: I = P ÷ V = 300 W ÷ 6 V = 50 A.
  2. Cable drop: ΔV = I × R = 50 A × 0.01 Ω = 0.5 V, which is 0.5 ÷ 6 × 100 = 8.3 % of the supply.
  3. Cable loss: P = I²R = 50² × 0.01 = 25 W.
  4. 12 V system: I = 300 W ÷ 12 V = 25 A; ΔV = 25 × 0.01 = 0.25 V, which is 0.25 ÷ 12 × 100 = 2.1 %.
  5. Cable loss: P = 25² × 0.01 = 6.25 W.
  6. Doubling the voltage halved the current, halved the drop in volts, cut the percentage drop to a quarter and cut the cable loss to a quarter (loss goes with I²).

Answer. At 6 V the load draws 50 A, drops 0.5 V (8.3 %) and wastes 25 W in the cable; at 12 V it draws 25 A, drops 0.25 V (2.1 %) and wastes 6.25 W, a quarter of the loss.

Exam traps

  • The evolution was driven by emission limits, safety rules and customer loads, not by a wish for more gadgets.
  • A dynamo and an alternator are not the same: the dynamo uses a commutator to make DC; the alternator makes AC and rectifies it with diodes.
  • 12 V is a nominal battery voltage; a charging system runs near 14.0 V. Do not use 12 V when a question gives the regulated bus.
  • Bharat Stage and Euro norms are matching regulatory steps, not two different physical processes; India skipped the Euro V equivalent and went from BS IV to BS VI.
  • Power is volts times amperes; energy is power times time. A 120 A alternator does not store 120 Ah; the battery rating is separate.

1.2 Vehicle electrical systems: power supply, wire sizing and circuit diagrams

A car's electrical system is a small power grid on wheels: the alternator generates, the battery stores, and dozens of loads draw from one 12 V bus through copper wires. Each wire must be thick enough to keep its voltage drop and temperature within limits, and no thicker, because a harness is heavy and costly. Circuit diagrams are the map a technician uses to follow every path from the battery to a load and back.

You need: Ohm's law (V = I × R) and power (P = V × I) from first-year physics, and the history of the 12 V system in Chapter 1.1.

Generator, store and loads: the vehicle power supply

Every conventional vehicle electrical system has three parts. The alternator generates electrical energy while the engine runs. The battery stores energy for starting and for times when the alternator cannot keep up. The loads (also called consumers) use the energy. All of them hang on one bus: a positive network fed from the battery and alternator, and a return through the steel body and engine block, called chassis ground or terminal 31.

Loads are grouped by how long they draw current, because that decides how much charge they take from the battery:

  • Permanent loads work even when parked: clock, alarm, ECU standby current. Each draws milliamperes, but over weeks they can flatten a battery.
  • Continuous loads run with the engine: ignition, injection, fuel pump, engine ECU.
  • Long-time loads run for minutes or hours: headlamps, blower, rear-window heater, wipers, radio.
  • Short-time loads work for seconds: starter, horn, power windows, brake lamps.

The charge balance compares the charge the alternator puts into the battery with the charge the loads take out. In slow night traffic the alternator at idle may give less current than the loads need, so the battery makes up the difference; on an open road the alternator covers the loads and recharges it. A design must keep the balance positive over a typical trip, or the battery goes flat after a week of short drives.

Most cars use one 12 V battery. Vehicles with large accessory loads (ambulances, caravans, buses) may use a two-battery system: a starter battery kept for cranking and a supply battery for the other loads, joined through a relay or isolator so the loads can never drain the starter battery. Heavy trucks use 24 V, two 12 V batteries in series, which halves the current for the same power. Loads on the bus are in parallel, so their currents add.

Dimensioning of wires

A wire is chosen by three tests: the voltage drop at full load must stay within a permitted value; the wire's temperature must stay within the limit of its insulation, which depends on the current and the bundle it sits in; and it must be mechanically strong enough for vibration and handling, so very thin wires are avoided even for tiny currents. For long feeds, such as those to the rear lamps, the voltage drop usually decides.

The resistance of a wire is R = ρl ÷ A. Putting it into the voltage drop ΔU = I × R and solving for the cross-section gives the sizing formula:

A = (I × ρ × l) ÷ ΔU

A is the conductor cross-section in mm²; I is the load current in A; ρ is the resistivity of copper, about 0.0172 Ω·mm²/m at 20 °C (it rises about 0.4 % per kelvin, so a hot wire drops more); l is the wire length in m; ΔU is the permitted voltage drop in V.

Here l is the length that carries the current. With a chassis return only the feed counts, because the steel body has almost no resistance; with a separate return wire, as sensors use, l is twice the route length. Round the result up to the next standard size: 0.35, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.5, 2.5, 4, 6, 10, 16, 25, 35 or 50 mm². Starter cables are 16 to 50 mm²; most lamp and motor feeds 0.75 to 2.5 mm².

Reading circuit diagrams and symbols

A circuit diagram (schematic) shows how each circuit works: current paths run as straight vertical lines from the positive supply at the top to ground (terminal 31) at the bottom, and parts are drawn where the circuit is easiest to follow, not where they sit in the car. A wiring diagram shows the real positions of the parts and the harness routes, with wire colours and sizes. A terminal diagram lists every connector pin, and a location diagram shows where each part is fitted.

Standard symbols are drawn for the battery, fuse, switch, relay coil and contact, lamp, motor, diode, resistor, connector and ground. Wires carry a code such as 1.5 RD/WH: 1.5 mm², red with a white stripe. Terminals carry the DIN 72552 numbers that are stamped on the parts themselves:

Worked example

Question. Two 55 W low-beam headlamps are fed through one copper wire 4.0 m long from the relay to the lamps, with the return through the body. Take the nominal 12 V for the current. The permitted voltage drop on this feed is 0.3 V. Copper resistivity is 0.0172 Ω·mm²/m. (a) Find the load current. (b) Find the minimum cross-section and choose a standard size. (c) Find the actual voltage drop with the chosen wire.

  1. Load current: the lamps are in parallel, so P = 2 × 55 W = 110 W and I = P ÷ V = 110 W ÷ 12 V = 9.17 A.
  2. Only the feed carries resistance, because the body is the return, so l = 4.0 m.
  3. A = (I × ρ × l) ÷ ΔU = (9.17 A × 0.0172 Ω·mm²/m × 4.0 m) ÷ 0.3 V = 0.631 ÷ 0.3 = 2.10 mm².
  4. Round up to the next standard size: 2.5 mm². (1.5 mm² is smaller than 2.10 mm², so it would break the voltage-drop limit.)
  5. Resistance of the chosen wire: R = ρl ÷ A = 0.0172 × 4.0 ÷ 2.5 = 0.0275 Ω.
  6. Actual drop: ΔU = I × R = 9.17 A × 0.0275 Ω = 0.25 V, which is below the permitted 0.3 V.

Answer. The current is 9.17 A; the minimum section is 2.10 mm², so a 2.5 mm² wire is chosen, giving a drop of 0.25 V (1.5 mm² would give 9.17 × 0.0172 × 4.0 ÷ 1.5 = 0.42 V, too much).

Worked example 2

Question. On a rainy night in slow traffic a car's loads are: engine management and fuel pump 15 A, low beams 9.2 A, side and tail lamps 4 A, wipers 5 A, blower 12 A, rear-window heater 12 A, radio 2 A. At idle the alternator gives 45 A. (a) Find the battery discharge current. (b) Find the charge lost in 30 min. (c) On the highway the alternator gives 90 A with the same loads. If the battery needs 1.1 Ah of charge for each 1 Ah taken out, how long does the recharge take?

  1. Total load: 15 + 9.2 + 4 + 5 + 12 + 12 + 2 = 59.2 A.
  2. Battery discharge current at idle = load − alternator = 59.2 A − 45 A = 14.2 A.
  3. Charge lost in 30 min: Q = I × t = 14.2 A × 0.5 h = 7.1 Ah.
  4. Charge to put back: 7.1 Ah × 1.1 = 7.8 Ah.
  5. Spare alternator current on the highway: 90 A − 59.2 A = 30.8 A, all of which can flow into the battery.
  6. Recharge time: t = 7.8 Ah ÷ 30.8 A = 0.25 h ≈ 15 min.

Answer. The battery discharges at 14.2 A and loses 7.1 Ah in half an hour; on the highway the 30.8 A surplus puts back the 7.8 Ah in about 15 minutes, so the trip keeps a positive charge balance only if the highway part lasts that long.

  • A wire that passes the voltage-drop test can still overheat in a hot, packed harness; temperature is a separate test.

1.3 Electromagnetic compatibility and interference suppression

Electromagnetic compatibility (EMC) means that every electrical part of a vehicle works properly in the electrical noise made by the others, and does not make enough noise itself to upset them or nearby receivers. A car is a hard place for this: sparks of many kilovolts, sparking motor brushes and switched coils sit a few centimetres from ECUs that read millivolt sensor signals and radios that pick up microvolt broadcasts. Interference suppression keeps the noise down at its source, blocks its path and hardens the receiver.

You need: the 12 V supply and the circuit diagrams of Chapter 1.2, and the idea of inductance and capacitance from first-year physics.

Source, coupling path and sink

Every EMC problem has three parts. The source makes the disturbance. The coupling path carries it. The sink (the victim) is the part that misbehaves. EMC has two sides: emission, how much noise a part sends out, and immunity (also called susceptibility), how much noise a part can take without a fault.

  • Sources inside the vehicle: the ignition spark (a very fast current pulse that radiates up to hundreds of megahertz); commutator motors, whose brushes spark; the alternator diodes; relays, solenoids and injectors, which give spikes when switched off; PWM drivers; and the load dump, a surge when the battery is disconnected while the alternator is charging.
  • Sources outside: broadcast and mobile-phone transmitters, radar, high-voltage power lines, and electrostatic discharge from a person's hand.
  • Sinks: the radio and other receivers (long, medium and FM bands, digital radio, GPS), ECU inputs and sensor lines, and the data buses that join the ECUs.

A disturbance on the supply line is called a transient. Switching off an inductive load can put a negative spike of a hundred volts or more on its wire. A load dump in a 12 V system, if it is not suppressed, can raise the bus to around a hundred volts for a few hundred milliseconds, enough to destroy electronics. International test standards describe these pulses so that every supplier tests against the same shapes: ISO 7637-2 for conducted transients, ISO 16750-2 for the load dump, CISPR 25 for radio emissions from components, and the ISO 11452 series for immunity to radiated fields.

How interference travels

There are four coupling paths, and the remedy depends on which one carries the noise.

  1. Conducted (galvanic) coupling: two circuits share a wire or ground point. Current from a noisy load flows through the shared resistance and the voltage drop appears in the quiet circuit. A long common ground return is the usual culprit.
  2. Capacitive coupling: two wires run side by side, and a fast voltage change on one pushes current into the other through the small capacitance between them. It grows with dv/dt, the rate of change of voltage.
  3. Inductive coupling: a changing current makes a changing magnetic field, which induces a voltage in any nearby loop. It grows with di/dt and with the loop area.
  4. Radiated coupling: at high frequency a wire acts as an aerial. A harness wire about a quarter of a wavelength long (75 cm at 100 MHz) radiates and receives well.

XC and XL are the reactances of a capacitor and an inductor in ohms (Ω); f is the frequency in hertz (Hz); C is capacitance in farads (F); L is inductance in henries (H). A capacitor is a low impedance to high-frequency noise and an inductor (choke) is a high impedance, which is why both are used to keep noise off the supply.

Interference suppression

Suppression works best at the source, then on the path, last at the sink.

  • At the source: a freewheeling diode, an RC network or a varistor across every relay and solenoid coil; capacitors across DC motor brushes and at the alternator B+ terminal; chokes in motor supply lines; resistor spark plugs and suppression resistors in plug caps or HT leads, which slow the spark current pulse; and Zener power diodes in the alternator rectifier, which clamp the load-dump surge.
  • On the path: keep wires short; route power wires away from signal wires; use twisted pairs (the CAN bus) so that induced voltages cancel; use screened cables for aerials and sensitive sensors; bond the engine, body and exhaust together with short, wide ground straps; and bring sensor grounds to one clean point instead of the nearest bolt.
  • At the sink: filter capacitors and ferrites on ECU inputs, metal housings, software that ignores implausible values.

A vehicle is tested twice: each component on a bench to CISPR 25 limits, and the whole vehicle in a screened, absorber-lined room.

Worked example

Question. A 12 V relay coil has a resistance of 85 Ω and an inductance of 0.20 H. It is switched off by a transistor in the ECU. (a) Find the coil current and the energy stored in the coil. (b) Without suppression, the transistor would try to stop this current in 2 µs. Estimate the voltage the coil would try to produce. (c) A freewheeling diode (0.7 V drop) is fitted across the coil. Find the time constant of the current decay and the time for the current to fall to 10 % of its value.

  1. Coil current: I = V ÷ R = 12 V ÷ 85 Ω = 0.141 A.
  2. Stored energy: W = ½ L I² = 0.5 × 0.20 H × (0.141 A)² = 1.99 × 10⁻³ J ≈ 2.0 mJ.
  3. Induced voltage if the current stops in 2 µs: V = L × di/dt = 0.20 H × 0.141 A ÷ (2 × 10⁻⁶ s) = 14,100 V. In practice the transistor breaks down long before, or an arc forms, and that spike is the interference.
  4. With the diode, the coil current keeps flowing round the loop through the diode, so the coil voltage is clamped at about 0.7 V and the transistor sees only 12 V + 0.7 V = 12.7 V.
  5. The current then decays through the coil's own resistance with time constant τ = L ÷ R = 0.20 H ÷ 85 Ω = 2.35 ms.
  6. Time to fall to 10 %: t = τ × ln 10 = 2.35 ms × 2.303 = 5.4 ms.

Answer. The coil carries 0.141 A and stores about 2.0 mJ; unsuppressed it would try to make about 14 kV, but with the diode the transistor sees only 12.7 V and the current dies away with τ = 2.35 ms, reaching 10 % after about 5.4 ms (so the relay releases a few milliseconds later than without the diode).

Worked example 2

Question. A radiator fan motor is suppressed with an LC low-pass filter in its supply: a 5 µH choke in series and a 1 µF capacitor to ground. (a) Find the cut-off frequency. (b) Find the reactance of the choke and the capacitor at 1 MHz, in the medium-wave radio band. (c) An ideal two-element LC filter falls by 40 dB per decade above cut-off. Estimate the attenuation at 1 MHz.

  1. Cut-off: fc = 1 ÷ (2π√(LC)) = 1 ÷ (2π × √(5 × 10⁻⁶ × 1 × 10⁻⁶)) = 1 ÷ (2π × 2.236 × 10⁻⁶) = 71.2 kHz.
  2. Choke at 1 MHz: XL = 2πfL = 2π × 10⁶ × 5 × 10⁻⁶ = 31.4 Ω, a high series impedance for the noise.
  3. Capacitor at 1 MHz: XC = 1 ÷ (2πfC) = 1 ÷ (2π × 10⁶ × 10⁻⁶) = 0.159 Ω, a near short to ground for the noise.
  4. Decades above cut-off: log₁₀(1 MHz ÷ 71.2 kHz) = log₁₀(14.05) = 1.148.
  5. Attenuation ≈ 40 dB × 1.148 = 45.9 dB, so the noise voltage is cut to about 1/200 of its value (10^(45.9/20) = 197).
  6. The motor's own 12 V DC supply passes untouched, because at 0 Hz the choke is a short wire and the capacitor is an open circuit.

Answer. The cut-off is 71.2 kHz; at 1 MHz the choke is 31.4 Ω and the capacitor 0.159 Ω, and the filter cuts the noise by about 46 dB (about 200 times) while letting the DC through.

Exam traps

  • EMC is two-sided: low emission and enough immunity. Answers that only describe suppression of the ignition miss half the topic.
  • A freewheeling diode must be fitted with its cathode to the positive end of the coil. Fitted the other way, it shorts the supply when the relay is switched on.
  • A freewheeling diode slows the release of a relay. Where fast release matters, a Zener or resistor is added in series with the diode.
  • Capacitive coupling grows with dv/dt and inductive coupling with di/dt; mixing them up gives the wrong remedy (screening against electric fields, twisting and small loops against magnetic fields).
  • Resistor spark plugs and suppression leads reduce radio interference, not spark energy needed for ignition; replacing them with plain plugs is a common cause of radio noise.

1.4 Battery design, lead-acid construction and working

A cell stores chemical energy and gives it back as electricity. The lead-acid cell does this with two lead-based plates in dilute sulfuric acid. It is cheap, it can push out a huge burst of current for cranking, and it survives hundreds of charge and discharge cycles. Six such cells in one plastic box make the 12 V battery of every petrol and diesel car. This chapter opens the box: the plates, the separator, the acid and the reaction that runs one way on discharge and back on charge.

You need: the battery's place among vehicle loads from Chapter 1.1, and basic oxidation and reduction from school chemistry.

One cell, then a battery of six

A cell is one complete chemical unit: two plates with the electrolyte between them. A battery is one or more cells joined and sold as one package. A car battery holds six cells inside a single case.

In series the same current flows through every cell and the cell voltages add. A fully charged cell rests at about 2.1 V on open circuit, so six cells give 6 × 2.1 V = 12.6 V; the "12 V" on the label is nominal.

V_battery = n × V_cell

n is the number of cells in series (6 in an automotive battery); V_cell is the open-circuit voltage of one fully charged cell, about 2.1 V; V_battery is the battery terminal voltage at rest, in volts.

The series connection gives the 12.6 V the starter needs (see Chapter 1.9); the large plate area and the few milliohms of internal resistance let the pack push out hundreds of amperes, with the terminal voltage sagging by a volt or two.

Inside a cell: plates, separator, acid and case

A cell is a stack of alternating plates. The negative plate is a lead grid filled with spongy lead (Pb): grey, soft and very porous, so the acid touches a huge surface area. The positive plate is a similar grid whose active material is lead dioxide (PbO2), a dark brown powder. The grid itself is only the conductor; it carries current to the plate lug.

Plates of one polarity are welded to a strap to form a plate group; a positive and a negative group interleave with separators to form the plate block of one cell, and cell connectors pass through the partitions to join the cells in series. More or larger plates give more ampere-hours; the cell voltage stays about 2.1 V whatever the size.

Two plate families exist. Plante plates are formed by converting the lead plate itself into active material through repeated cycling. They are thick and hold less energy per kilogram, but they last long, so they suit standby work. Pasted plates are made by spreading a paste of lead oxides onto a cast lead-alloy grid and then forming it by charging. They store more energy per kilogram and are what nearly every vehicle battery uses.

A porous separator lies between each pair of opposite plates. It lets ions pass but stops the plates touching, which would short the cell. Wood, rubber, glass fibre and plastics such as polyethylene have all been used.

The electrolyte is dilute sulfuric acid (H2SO4 in water). When fully charged its specific gravity is about 1.26 to 1.28. Specific gravity is the density of the liquid divided by the density of water, so 1.28 means the acid is 1.28 times as dense as water.

Everything sits in one compartment of a hard rubber or polypropylene case. Partitions divide the case into six cells. The cover carries the terminals — the positive post is deliberately made larger than the negative — and vented caps that let gas escape and water be added.

Discharge and charge: one reaction, run both ways

Discharge turns chemical energy into electrical energy. At the negative plate, lead gives up electrons and becomes lead sulfate. At the positive plate, lead dioxide takes electrons and also becomes lead sulfate.

Pb + HSO<sub>4</sub><sup>−</sup> → PbSO<sub>4</sub> + H<sup>+</sup> + 2e<sup>−</sup>   (negative plate, discharge)

Pb is spongy lead; HSO4− is the bisulfate ion from the acid; PbSO4 is lead sulfate; e− is an electron released to the external circuit. This is oxidation.

PbO<sub>2</sub> + HSO<sub>4</sub><sup>−</sup> + 3H<sup>+</sup> + 2e<sup>−</sup> → PbSO<sub>4</sub> + 2H<sub>2</sub>O   (positive plate, discharge)

PbO2 is lead dioxide; H+ is the hydrogen ion; H2O is water. This is reduction, taking the two electrons the negative plate released.

Pb + PbO<sub>2</sub> + 2H<sub>2</sub>SO<sub>4</sub> → 2PbSO<sub>4</sub> + 2H<sub>2</sub>O   (overall cell, discharge)

the left side is the charged state (lead, lead dioxide, sulfuric acid); the right side is the discharged state (lead sulfate on both plates plus water).

Both plates end up coated with lead sulfate. The acid is a reactant, not just a conducting liquid: it is consumed while water is produced, so the electrolyte weakens and its specific gravity falls. That fall is what a hydrometer reads (see Chapter 1.5), and what battery state-of-charge sensing, a sensors topic, also estimates. A useful rule links the two: the open-circuit voltage of a rested cell is about the specific gravity plus 0.84, so 1.28 gives about 2.12 V.

Charging reverses every term: 2PbSO4 + 2H2O → Pb + PbO2 + 2H2SO4. The positive plate stays positive; the charger pushes current the other way, so the reactions run backwards and the acid is restored. The charger must apply more than 12.6 V to drive current in (see Chapter 1.6).

Voltage, gassing and the faults that age a battery

Open-circuit cell voltage stays near 2.1 V for most of the discharge and then falls steeply at the end. Under load the terminal voltage is lower by I × Rinternal, where I is the current drawn in amperes and Rinternal is the internal resistance in ohms.

Above about 2.3 to 2.4 V per cell (roughly 13.8 to 14.4 V for the whole battery) the charging current starts splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen. This is gassing, not boiling. Water is lost, the level drops, and the gas mixture is explosive, so vents stay clear and sparks stay away.

  • Sulfation: lead sulfate left standing crystallises. Hard crystals do not convert back on charge, so capacity is lost permanently. Storing a battery discharged is the usual cause.
  • Stratification: acid is denser than water and sinks, so the bottom of the cell is stronger than the top and the plates work unevenly. Driving, and charging, stirs the liquid.
  • Self-discharge: a battery standing idle loses charge by internal leakage, a few percent a month, faster when warm.

Worked example

Question. A fully charged lead-acid cell rests at 2.1 V on open circuit. (a) Find the open-circuit voltage of an automotive battery that contains six such cells in series. (b) Later, one cell develops an internal short circuit and behaves as a plain conductor. Find the new battery voltage.

  1. The six cells are in series, so the voltages add: V_battery = n × V_cell.
  2. Healthy case: n = 6 and V_cell = 2.1 V, so V_battery = 6 × 2.1 V = 12.6 V.
  3. A shorted cell contributes 0 V and no longer reacts, so only 5 cells are active: n = 5.
  4. Faulty case: V_battery = 5 × 2.1 V = 10.5 V.

Answer. The battery reads 12.6 V when healthy and 10.5 V with one cell shorted.

Worked example 2

Question. A 12 V, 60 Ah battery has an electrolyte specific gravity of 1.28 when fully charged. Use the rule cell voltage ≈ SG + 0.84.   1. Full: cell voltage = 1.28 + 0.84 = 2.12 V; battery = 6 × 2.12 V = 12.72 V.

  3. Where 3.66 g/Ah comes from: 1 Ah = 3600 C = 0.0373 mol of electrons (3600 ÷ 96,485), and the reaction uses one H₂SO₄ per electron: 0.0373 × 98.1 g = 3.66 g.

Exam traps

  • Gassing is not boiling. It is the electrolysis of water and it starts above about 2.3 to 2.4 V per cell (roughly 13.8 to 14.4 V for the whole battery).

1.5 Battery ratings, testing and maintenance-free batteries

A battery label is a promise about charge and current. Ampere-hour (Ah) says how much charge it stores; reserve capacity says how long it can run essential loads; cold cranking amps (CCA) says how much current it can push into a cold engine. Tests compare that promise with the battery's real condition. Ratings guide battery selection; tests decide replacement. You meet them at every service bay, battery shop and vehicle assembly line.

You need: Chapter 1.4 for cell construction and discharge.

What the ratings mean

Ampere-hour (Ah) is stored charge. A 60 Ah battery at the 20 h rate can deliver 60 Ah when discharged over 20 h. The test current is constant.

Ah = I × t

I is constant current in A; t is discharge time in h; Ah is ampere-hour capacity.

At the 20 h rate, I = Ah ÷ 20 h. So 60 Ah gives 3 A. The test ends at 10.5 V (1.75 V per cell) at a stated temperature, usually about 25 °C. A battery discharged faster gives fewer ampere-hours, because the acid cannot reach the inner pores of the plates in time, so a rating always names its rate.

Reserve capacity (RC) is the time in minutes a fully charged battery at about 25 °C can supply 25 A before terminal voltage falls to 10.5 V. It answers: if the alternator fails, how long will lights and wipers run?

Cold cranking amps (CCA) is the current a battery can deliver at −18 °C for 30 s while keeping at least 7.2 V across its terminals (the SAE definition). Cranking amps (CA) uses the same 30 s and 7.2 V, but at 0 °C. European and Japanese standards use other times and end voltages, so compare ratings measured to the same standard.

Ah is not starting current. A high-Ah battery may have low CCA; a small, high-CCA battery may have modest Ah. Read both.

Tests that reveal health

Open-circuit voltage (OCV) is measured after the battery rests, with no load. A full 12 V battery reads about 12.6 V to 12.8 V, 12.2 V about half charge, and 12.0 V about a quarter charge; below roughly 11.9 V it is deeply discharged.

A hydrometer measures specific gravity (SG) of the electrolyte. Full is about 1.265; 1.120 is nearly empty. Correct for temperature. Sealed batteries have no caps, so use OCV or a conductance tester.

  1. Load test: apply half the CCA or three times the Ah for 15 s; voltage should stay above about 9.6 V at room temperature.
  2. Conductance test: inject a small AC signal and read the estimated CCA; fast, safe and needs no full discharge.
  3. Charging-system test: run the engine at fast idle and measure the voltage across the battery terminals; about 13.8 V to 14.4 V shows the alternator is charging.

Safety: a charging battery makes hydrogen gas, which explodes with a spark. Wear eye protection, keep flames away, and vent the area. Acid burns skin. Disconnect the negative terminal first and reconnect it last.

A load test heats the battery; let it rest before repeating. A conductance test can pass a battery that later fails under real cold cranking, so use it as a quick screen, not a guarantee. The charging circuit itself is covered in Chapter 1.6.

Maintenance-free designs

A maintenance-free battery limits water loss so it needs no periodic topping-up. Flooded maintenance-free types are vented through small vents. Only valve-regulated types (AGM, gel) carry a pressure-relief valve.

Lead-calcium grids replace the antimony of conventional grids with calcium (hybrid designs keep a little antimony in one plate). Calcium reduces gassing and self-discharge, so less water is lost. Calcium also makes the battery less tolerant of deep discharge.

VRLA means valve-regulated lead-acid. The valve releases gas only if pressure rises too far. In absorbed glass mat (AGM), a glass mat holds the electrolyte like a sponge, so it cannot spill. Oxygen made at the positive plate diffuses to the negative plate and recombines to water. This gas recombination keeps water inside.

An enhanced flooded battery (EFB) is a flooded battery built for start-stop. It has better deep-cycle life than a plain flooded battery but costs less than AGM. Start-stop needs many engine restarts, so the battery cycles more.

  • Use the specified type: AGM charging voltages differ from flooded.
  • Never open a VRLA battery; its valve is a safety device, not a service cap.

Battery versions, substitutes and special cases

Starter batteries come in several versions. The conventional flooded battery with lead-antimony grids needs topping-up. The low-maintenance and maintenance-free flooded batteries use lead-calcium grids. The enhanced flooded battery (EFB) and the AGM battery serve start-stop cars, and the gel battery, with the acid set into a gel by silica, resists deep cycling. Heavy-duty batteries for trucks, buses and tractors have thicker plates and plates fixed against vibration, and deep-cycle batteries for caravans and boats are built to be discharged far more often than a starter battery.

A substitute (replacement) battery must match the original in voltage, technology, size and terminal layout, and its CCA and Ah must be at least the original values. An AGM battery is replaced only by an AGM battery, because its charging voltage and its gas recombination differ; on cars with a battery sensor the new battery must be registered so that charging is set for a new one. Lithium-iron-phosphate 12 V starter batteries and supercapacitor packs exist as substitutes in racing and some new vehicles, but they need a matching charging strategy.

Special cases include vehicles that stand for long periods (fit a battery isolator or a maintenance charger, because standby loads drain the battery), hot climates (water loss and grid corrosion rise with temperature, so maintenance-free types and a lower charging voltage help), very cold climates (choose by CCA), and two-battery systems that keep a starter battery separate from a supply battery.

1.6 Charging system and charging circuit

The charging system keeps the 12 V bus alive while the engine runs. Belts spin an alternator, which makes three-phase AC; a rectifier turns it into DC. A voltage regulator adjusts field current so the bus stays near 14 V, high enough to charge the battery and run lights, ECUs and pumps. It matters because the battery alone cannot supply a long drive. Every petrol or diesel car uses this loop, and the same idea appears in agricultural pumpsets and small generators.

You need: battery voltage and capacity from Chapter 1.4 and Chapter 1.5, plus the idea that diodes turn AC into DC.

The regulated DC bus

The charging system is a closed loop. The engine belt turns the alternator. The alternator produces three-phase AC. A diode rectifier converts that AC into DC. The DC bus feeds the battery and every load. The regulator watches bus voltage and adjusts alternator field current. More field current means more generated voltage and current; less field current means less.

The target is a bus voltage of about 13.8 V to 14.4 V at normal temperature. This is above a rested 12 V battery (about 12.4 V to 12.7 V), so current flows into the battery. A fully charged battery needs only a small current. After a start, the battery accepts a large current for a short time. The regulator does not fix current; it fixes voltage, and current follows Ohm's law and the battery's state.

Temperature matters. A cold battery accepts charge at a higher voltage without gassing much, so some regulators raise the set point in cold weather. A hot battery needs a lower set point to avoid water loss. The nominal 14.2 V figure is a common room-temperature value, not a universal constant. The rotor and stator details are in Chapter 1.7.

Terminals, lamp and first field current

  • B+ is battery positive: thick cable from rectifier output to battery positive and main fuse box.
  • B− is battery negative: return through engine block, chassis and battery negative.
  • D+ is field or excitation: feeds the rotor field winding.
  • L is lamp: joins the charge warning lamp to the ignition switch.
  • Sense is a thin voltage-sense wire, often marked S or internal to B+.

At key-on, engine off, the ignition switch sends battery voltage to the lamp. Current goes from battery positive, through the ignition switch, through the lamp, into L and D+, through the field winding, and out through the regulator to ground. This small current creates a weak magnetic field. It is called initial excitation. The lamp glows because current flows through it.

When the engine starts, the alternator begins to generate. D+ voltage rises. With about 14 V on both sides of the lamp, the lamp goes out. The alternator now supplies its own field current through three small exciter diodes (the diode trio) that feed D+ from the stator phases, so the field no longer needs the lamp. The warning lamp is therefore both a check lamp and the path for pre-excitation. If the bulb is open, the alternator may not start charging, even though the belt is tight; many alternators have a resistor in parallel with the lamp so that a failed bulb, or an LED lamp that passes too little current, does not stop charging.

Other parts complete the circuit. A fuse protects the B+ cable and the sense wire. The ignition switch controls the lamp circuit. Grounds at the engine block, chassis and battery negative must be clean; a high-resistance ground makes the regulator see a low voltage and overcharge. The voltage sense wire may go to the battery positive or to a main junction. It carries almost no current; it only tells the regulator the true bus voltage.

Inside the alternator, the alternator circuitry has three groups of diodes. Six power diodes form the main bridge from the three stator phases to B+ and ground; some designs add two more on the star point of the stator to use its voltage at high speed. Three exciter diodes feed D+ and the regulator. In many alternators the power diodes are Zener diodes, which also clamp voltage surges such as the load dump. A suppression capacitor from B+ to ground keeps radio-frequency noise off the bus.

Current paths at low and high load

At low load, the battery is charged and few accessories are on. The regulator reduces field current. The alternator output is just enough to hold the bus at the set point and supply small loads. The battery current is near zero or a few amperes. The warning lamp stays off.

At high load, headlamps, wipers, blower and ECUs draw current. Bus voltage falls a little. The regulator increases field current. Alternator output rises. The battery may supply current for a short time if the alternator cannot keep up, especially at idle. Once engine speed rises, the alternator takes over and recharges the battery.

Current paths are simple. Supply path: alternator B+ → fusible link or fuse → battery positive → loads. Return path: load → chassis ground → engine block → B− → battery negative. The regulator senses bus voltage between B+ and ground. It changes field current in the rotor; it does not change engine speed. Raising engine speed only raises the alternator's available output, not the regulated voltage.

I<sub>charge</sub> ≈ (V<sub>reg</sub> − V<sub>oc</sub>) ÷ R<sub>internal</sub>

Icharge is charging current in A; Vreg is regulated bus voltage in V; Voc is battery open-circuit voltage in V; Rinternal is battery internal resistance in Ω.

Worked example

Question. A car alternator is regulated at 14.2 V. The battery open-circuit voltage is 12.4 V and its internal resistance is 0.02 Ω. Neglect wiring resistance. Find the initial charging current.

  1. Regulated bus voltage Vreg = 14.2 V.
  2. Battery open-circuit voltage Voc = 12.4 V.
  3. Driving voltage for charge = Vreg − Voc = 14.2 V − 12.4 V = 1.8 V.
  4. Battery internal resistance Rinternal = 0.02 Ω.
  5. Initial charging current I = 1.8 V ÷ 0.02 Ω = 90 A.
  6. As the battery charges, its rest voltage Voc rises, so the driving voltage Vreg − Voc and the current fall. The 90 A also assumes the alternator can supply it on top of the loads; at idle its own output curve may cap the current lower.

Answer. The initial charging current is about 90 A (if the alternator can deliver it), and it falls as the battery charges and its rest voltage rises.

Worked example 2

Question. An alternator regulates to 14.2 V measured at its own B+ terminal. The charging cable from B+ to the battery is 1.2 m of 10 mm² copper (ρ = 0.0172 Ω·mm²/m), and a corroded joint on it adds 0.004 Ω. The alternator delivers 80 A, of which the loads near the alternator take none. (a) Find the cable resistance and the voltage the battery receives. (b) Repeat with the corroded joint cleaned.   5. A sense wire carries almost no current, so it has almost no drop: it reports the true battery voltage. The regulator then raises B+ until the battery sees 14.2 V, which here means about 14.7 V at B+ with the bad joint.

Exam traps

  • The regulator controls field current, not engine speed. Revving the engine does not raise the regulated bus voltage above its set point.
  • A dead charge warning lamp can stop charging because it supplies initial field current on many machines.
  • Do not use open-circuit battery voltage as the charging voltage. A 12 V battery at rest is about 12.4 V; the bus runs near 14 V.
  • The B− path is not just the thin wire. Engine block and chassis grounds carry the return current; a bad ground causes wrong sensing and dim lamps.
  • Higher temperature usually calls for a lower charge voltage, not higher.

1.7 Alternator principle, construction and characteristic curves

An alternator is a three-phase AC generator bolted to the engine and driven by a belt. A field winding on the rotor creates a rotating magnetic field; this field induces AC in three stationary stator windings. A diode bridge rectifies that AC into the DC bus that charges the battery and runs the loads. The idea exists because a rotating winding cannot carry the full output current through sliding contacts, while a small field current can. Most petrol and diesel cars use this machine to generate electrical energy while the engine runs.

You need: the DC bus idea from Chapter 1.6, plus basic three-phase AC and diode rectification.

Principle and three-phase generation

The physical principle is Faraday's law. A changing magnetic flux through a coil induces a voltage in that coil. In an alternator the rotor carries a field winding. Direct current in this winding makes an electromagnet. The engine turns the rotor, so the magnetic flux sweeps past the three stator windings. Each stator winding sees a changing flux and produces an alternating voltage. The three windings are placed 120 electrical degrees apart, so their voltages form a balanced three-phase set. The stator is the stationary part. The rotor is the rotating part. The field current is small, so it can be fed through slip rings and brushes without heavy sparking. The output current stays in stationary stator windings and goes straight to the rectifier.

e = −N dΦ/dt

e is the instantaneous induced voltage in volts (V); N is the number of turns; Φ is the magnetic flux in webers (Wb); t is time in seconds (s). The minus sign is Lenz's law: the induced voltage drives a current whose own magnetic field opposes the change of flux that caused it, which is why the belt must do work to turn a loaded alternator.

The frequency depends on pole count and speed. One mechanical revolution produces p/2 electrical cycles, where p is the number of rotor poles. With speed N in revolutions per minute, the stator frequency is f = (p × N) ÷ 120. Here f is in hertz (Hz), p is the number of poles, and N is alternator speed in rpm. A 12-pole alternator at 6000 rpm gives 600 Hz, as the worked example shows.

Construction and rectifier bridge

The rotor is usually a claw-pole type. One field coil sits on the shaft. Two claw-shaped steel shells interlock around it and form many North and South poles. This shape gives a strong field with a simple, cheap rotor. Two slip rings and two brushes bring in the field current. The stator is a laminated iron core with three windings in slots. Laminated steel reduces eddy-current loss. A fan on the rotor and a vented housing remove heat. The pulley is sized to spin the alternator faster than the engine.

Alternator versions. The common car version is the compact claw-pole alternator with two internal fans and the regulator and rectifier built in. Liquid-cooled alternators sit in the engine coolant circuit and run quieter and suit hot, enclosed engine bays. Brushless (windingless-rotor) versions keep the field coil stationary and use only the claw poles on the rotor, so there are no brushes to wear; they suit commercial vehicles that run very long hours.

  • Rotor: field coil, claw poles, slip rings, brushes.
  • Stator: three-phase windings in a laminated core.
  • Rectifier: six power diodes in a three-phase bridge.
  • Mechanical: fan, housing, bearings, pulley.

Vdc ≈ 1.35 × VLL

Vdc is the ideal average DC output in volts (V); VLL is the line-to-line RMS AC voltage of the stator in volts (V).

The six diodes convert three-phase AC to DC. At any instant the diode pair with the highest and lowest phase voltages conducts. A real bridge drops about 1.4 V to 2 V because two diodes conduct in series. The rectified waveform has six pulses per stator cycle, so its ripple frequency is fr = 6f, where fr is ripple frequency in Hz and f is stator frequency in Hz. The charging circuit around the alternator is in Chapter 1.6.

Characteristic curves and vehicle operation

Manufacturers test an alternator at constant regulated voltage, warm, with full field. The main curve is output current against alternator speed. Below the zero-ampere speed n₀ (also called cut-in speed, often about 1000 rpm) the generated voltage is below the set point, so the alternator delivers nothing. Above it, current rises steeply and then flattens. Four speeds are marked on the curve: n₀; the alternator speed at engine idle, nL (often about 1800 rpm), where the alternator must already supply the continuous loads; the rated speed nN = 6000 rpm, where the rated current IN is quoted; and the maximum permitted speed. The rated current is the current the alternator delivers at 6000 rpm at the stated voltage and temperature.

N<sub>alt</sub> = N<sub>engine</sub> × (D<sub>engine</sub> ÷ D<sub>alt</sub>)

Nalt is alternator speed in rpm; Nengine is engine speed in rpm; Dengine is crank pulley diameter; Dalt is alternator pulley diameter, in the same unit.

Why does the curve flatten? The regulator is at full field, so it is not the regulator that stops the rise. The generated emf E grows in proportion to speed, but so does the stator reactance X = 2πfL, because the frequency f rises with speed. The current is roughly I ≈ (E − V) ÷ X; with E = kn and X = cn this gives I ≈ Imax(1 − n₀ ÷ n), which approaches Imax however fast the rotor turns. This self-limiting property protects the alternator from overload. The regulator acts only when the loads need less than the curve can give: it then cuts the field to hold the voltage at the set point, often about 14.2 V for a 12 V system. The pulley ratio is chosen so that engine idle puts the alternator well above n₀; a larger crank pulley or smaller alternator pulley raises Nalt and improves charging at idle. The battery and loads see a DC bus with a small 6f ripple. Losses and voltage regulation are covered in Chapter 1.8.

Worked example

Question. An alternator has 12 poles and runs at 6000 rpm. Find the frequency of the stator AC before rectification.

  1. List the data: p = 12 poles, N = 6000 rpm.
  2. Use f = (p × N) ÷ 120, because one mechanical revolution gives p/2 electrical cycles and 60 s = 1 min.
  3. Substitute: p × N = 12 × 6000 = 72,000 pole·rpm.
  4. Divide: 72,000 ÷ 120 = 600 s⁻¹.
  5. 600 s⁻¹ = 600 Hz. The period is 1 ÷ 600 = 0.00167 s = 1.67 ms.

Answer. The stator frequency before rectification is 600 Hz.

Worked example 2

Question. An alternator has a zero-ampere speed n₀ = 1000 rpm and a rated current of 120 A at 6000 rpm. Use the self-limiting model I = Imax(1 − n₀ ÷ n). Exam traps

  • The alternator generates AC; the diode bridge produces DC. Marks are lost by writing that the alternator generates DC directly.
  • Output current does not rise forever with speed, and the flat top is not the regulator's doing: the stator reactance grows with speed, so the current self-limits near Imax. The regulator cuts the field only when the loads need less.
  • Engine rpm is not alternator rpm. With a pulley ratio, Nalt = Nengine × Dengine ÷ Dalt; use the alternator value in f = pN/120.
  • Cut-in speed is not the same as rated speed. Cut-in is where charging starts; rated speed is where rated current is reached.
  • For a three-phase full-wave bridge, ripple frequency is 6f, not f. Do not use the single-phase 2f result.

1.8 Alternator power losses and voltage regulation

An alternator turns engine rotation into electrical power, but not all input becomes output. Copper, iron, diode, brush and mechanical losses turn some input into heat and noise. Efficiency is useful output power divided by input power. The voltage regulator holds the bus voltage steady by adjusting field current. Every vehicle charging system uses this balance: at cruising speed the regulator holds the bus near 14 V whatever the load, while at idle with headlights on the alternator may reach its limit and the bus sags towards battery voltage. This chapter connects loss accounting to regulator action.

You need: Chapter 1.7 for alternator construction and curves, plus basic power and efficiency.

Loss paths and power balance

An alternator takes mechanical power Pin from the engine belt. It delivers electrical output power Pout = V × I, where V is the regulated bus voltage in volts and I is the load current in amperes. The input splits into output and losses.

  • Stator copper loss: I²R heating in the stator windings. Field copper loss: If²Rf heating in the field winding, where If is field current in A and Rf is field resistance in Ω.
  • Iron loss: hysteresis and eddy-current loss in the stator core. The changing magnetic flux causes it. Laminated cores reduce eddy loss.
  • Diode loss: in the three-phase bridge, two diodes conduct at a time. Pdi ≈ 2 × VF × Idc, where VF is forward drop per diode in V and Idc is DC output current in A. Silicon power diodes have VF about 0.7 V to 1.0 V.
  • Brush and slip-ring loss: field current crosses the brushes. Pbrush ≈ If × Vbrush, where Vbrush is total brush contact drop in V.
  • Bearing and fan windage loss: friction in bearings and air movement by the fan. It rises with speed.

P_in = P_out + P_cu + P_fe + P_di + P_mech

P_in is mechanical input power in W; P_out is electrical output power in W; P_cu is total copper loss (stator plus field) in W; P_fe is iron loss in W; P_di is diode loss in W; P_mech is the brush-contact loss P_brush plus bearing and fan loss, in W.

η = (P_out ÷ P_in) × 100%

η is efficiency in percent; P_out is useful electrical output power in W; P_in is mechanical input power in W.

Use the bus voltage under load for Pout. Losses are input minus output. A loss budget shows where heat is produced and why the alternator needs cooling.

How voltage regulation works

The regulator holds the bus voltage near a setpoint, about 14 V for a 12 V system. It senses bus voltage, compares it with a reference, and drives the field winding. More field current gives more magnetic flux and higher generated voltage.

An electromechanical regulator uses a voltage coil and vibrating contacts. An electronic regulator uses a Zener reference, comparator, driver transistor and freewheeling diode. Most modern vehicles use electronic regulation.

D = t_on ÷ (t_on + t_off)

D is the field PWM duty cycle, no unit, from 0 to 1; t_on is switch-on time in s; t_off is switch-off time in s.

I_f,avg ≈ D × V_bus ÷ R_f

I_f,avg is average field current in A; D is duty cycle; V_bus is bus voltage in V; R_f is field resistance in Ω.

The regulator switches the field on and off quickly. This is field PWM. If voltage falls when lights switch on, the comparator increases D. Field inductance slows the response. Temperature compensation lowers the setpoint when the battery is hot, which reduces overcharging.

Overvoltage protection acts if the regulator or sensing wire fails. It can clamp the field drive off or protect sensitive loads.

Regulator versions. The oldest are contact (vibrating) regulators, in which a voltage coil opens and closes a contact in the field circuit. Hybrid regulators kept the contact as a sensor but switched the field with a transistor. Electronic regulators were first built from discrete transistors and are now monolithic integrated circuits fixed to the brush holder inside the alternator. Multifunction regulators add a load-response function, which ramps up the field slowly when a big load switches on so the engine idle speed does not dip, and an interface (often LIN) through which the engine ECU can set the voltage.

Reading a loss budget

  1. Load the alternator and record bus voltage V in V and load current I in A.
  2. Compute output power P_out = V × I in W.
  3. Estimate each loss: copper from winding resistance and current; iron from test data; diode loss as 2 × V_F × I_dc; brush and mechanical loss from test data.
  4. Add the losses and find input power: P_in = P_out + total loss.
  5. Compute efficiency η = (P_out ÷ P_in) × 100%.

In service, a hot rectifier points to diode loss. A hot stator points to copper or iron loss. Bearing noise points to mechanical loss. The loss budget tells whether to change the winding, the diode bridge or the cooling.

Worked example

Question. A 12 V alternator delivers 14.0 V at 70 A to the vehicle bus. The mechanical input power measured at the pulley is 1.30 kW. Find the electrical output power, the total loss, and the efficiency. State units.

  1. Given V = 14.0 V, I = 70 A, P_in = 1.30 kW = 1300 W.
  2. Output power: P_out = V × I = 14.0 V × 70 A = 980 W.
  3. Total loss: P_loss = P_in − P_out = 1300 W − 980 W = 320 W.
  4. Efficiency: η = (P_out ÷ P_in) × 100 = (980 ÷ 1300) × 100 = 75.3846...% ≈ 75.4%.

Answer. The output power is 980 W, the total loss is 320 W, and the efficiency is 75.4%.

  • Using battery open-circuit voltage instead of the regulated bus voltage under load for P_out.

  • Saying the regulator changes stator turns or pulley ratio. It changes field current, usually by PWM.
  • Mixing mechanical input power in hp with electrical watts. Convert 1 hp = 746 W before using the power balance.

1.9 Starting requirements of IC engines

A starter must turn a cold engine fast enough for fuel and compression to produce the first firings. It must supply cranking torque against compression, friction and oil drag, and hold a minimum cranking speed while the battery sags under hundreds of amperes. Petrol engines need less cranking effort than diesels. The same requirement appears in every car, bus and generator set: choose a starter and battery that crank the engine at the coldest expected temperature. This chapter sets out torque, speed, power and the stable cranking point.

You need: battery design and battery ratings for cranking current, and the four-stroke engine cycle.

Cranking torque, speed and power

Starting has its own history. Early engines were cranked by hand; the electric self-starter of 1912 replaced the crank with a battery-driven DC motor, and that idea, a motor that turns the flywheel through a small pinion for a few seconds, is still used. What changed is the demand: higher compression, diesels in small cars, start-stop driving with dozens of restarts per trip, and cold-climate starts.

Cranking is rotating the engine before it fires. The starter applies torque T (N·m) at the flywheel ring gear. Engine speed N is in revolutions per minute (rpm). Angular speed ω is in radians per second:

ω = 2πN ÷ 60

N is engine speed in rpm; ω is angular speed in rad/s; 2π rad is one revolution and 60 s is one minute.

Cranking power P (W) is the rate of doing cranking work:

P = Tω

T is cranking torque in N·m; ω is angular speed in rad/s; P is cranking power in W.

Power needs both torque and speed. A starter that gives 65 N·m at stall produces no cranking power because ω = 0. At 200 rpm and 45 N·m, ω = 20.94 rad/s and P = 45 × 20.94 = 942 W.

Minimum starting speed is the lowest cranking speed for reliable first firing. Petrol engines typically need about 60–100 rpm; diesels need about 100–200 rpm, because they must reach the self-ignition temperature by compression alone. Below this, gas leaks past the rings while the piston moves slowly, the compressed charge loses heat to the cold walls, and the mixture is poorly formed, so combustion may not start or sustain. Cranking torque must also exceed the peak compression torque of the next cylinder, which is larger than the average torque the curves in this chapter use.

Engine resistance and the stable cranking point

Engine resistance torque comes from compression, ring and bearing friction, valve-train friction, and pumping air through throttles and ports. It rises with speed, because the oil films in the bearings and on the cylinder walls shear faster (viscous friction torque grows with sliding speed) and the air is pushed through the ports faster, which needs a larger pressure difference. Starter torque falls with speed, as a series DC motor does. Plot both against speed. Their intersection is the stable cranking point. Below it, starter torque is larger, so speed rises. Above it, resistance is larger, so speed falls. The engine settles at the intersection.

At 200 rpm the starter curve in the figure gives T = 65 − 0.1 × 200 = 45 N·m. The resistance curve gives 10 + 0.1 × 200 = 30 N·m, so the engine still accelerates. The curves meet when 65 − 0.1N = 10 + 0.1N, giving N = 275 rpm and T = 37.5 N·m. Real curves are measured on a cold engine and change with temperature.

Design and temperature effects; battery sag

Compression ratio is the ratio of cylinder volume at bottom dead centre to volume at top dead centre. A diesel may use 16:1 to 22:1; a petrol engine may use 8:1 to 12:1. Higher compression raises peak pressure and cranking torque. Larger displacement and more cylinders raise total torque. Cold oil has higher viscosity, so friction rises and cranking speed falls. A cold battery also has higher internal resistance and less available capacity. A diesel therefore needs a larger battery and a stronger starter for the same swept volume.

Battery terminal voltage under cranking is:

V = E − IRint

V is terminal voltage in V; E is open-circuit emf in V; I is cranking current in A; Rint is battery internal resistance in Ω.

A starter may draw several hundred amperes. Even 0.01 Ω gives a 3 V drop at 300 A. The starter's torque at any speed depends on the voltage it receives: less voltage means less current at that speed, less torque, and a starter curve that sits lower, so the stable cranking point moves to a lower speed. A flat or cold battery lowers the voltage through its own emf and internal resistance (V = E − IRint). Corroded terminals, a loose ground strap or a thin cable add resistance outside the battery, so the starter receives less than the battery terminals show; measuring the drop along each cable while cranking tells the two apart.

Exam traps

  • High torque at zero speed is not cranking power: P = Tω = 0 at stall.

  • Battery voltage sag under cranking current is not the same as battery capacity in Ah; a battery can have enough Ah and still fail cold cranking.
  • The stable cranking point is the intersection of starter torque and engine resistance curves, not the starter's maximum torque.

Voltage drop = I × Rint = 300 × 0.015 = 4.5 V. Terminal voltage V = 12.6 − 4.5 = 8.1 V.

Analyze Why is a starter with very high stall torque but low speed not always better for cranking? 5 marks

Cranking power is P = Tω, so speed matters. A high stall torque at zero speed gives zero power. The starter must reach the engine's minimum starting speed at the stable cranking point.

1.10 Starter motor types, drives and control circuits

A starter cranks a cold engine in under two seconds. It needs maximum torque at zero speed, a drive that grips the flywheel only while cranking, and a switch that carries hundreds of amperes without burning the key. A small control current works a relay and solenoid; the solenoid meshes the pinion and closes the heavy contacts. The chapter covers motor types, drives, and control and power circuits. The torque and speed the engine demands come from Chapter 1.9.

You need: Chapter 1.9 for cranking torque and minimum starting speed, and Chapter 1.5 for cold cranking amps.

Types of starter motor

A starter is a DC motor built for one duty: maximum torque at zero speed, for a few seconds, a few times an hour.

In a series-wound motor the field winding is in series with the armature. At standstill back emf is zero, so current is limited only by small resistance. Current and torque are very large. As speed rises, back emf grows, current falls, and torque falls. That is the cranking curve an engine wants.

T = k φ I<sub>a</sub>      E<sub>b</sub> = k φ ω

T is torque in N·m; k is a constant fixed by the winding design; φ is the flux per pole in webers, set by the field current; Ia is armature current in A; Eb is back emf in V; ω is angular speed in rad/s.

A permanent-magnet starter uses ferrite magnets instead of a field winding. It has no field copper, fewer connections, less weight, and no field current. The magnets weaken if overheated or left stalled, so duty cycle matters.

A gear-reduction starter spins a small, fast armature through a planetary or spur gear set, usually 3:1 to 5:1, so the pinion turns slowly with multiplied torque. A lighter motor gives the same cranking torque as a bigger direct-drive one.

A coaxial starter puts the reduction gear on the armature axis, in line with the pinion. It is the most compact arrangement and suits tight engine bays.

Design variations follow the engine. Small petrol engines use permanent-magnet gear-reduction starters; large diesels in trucks use bigger series-wound starters, sometimes at 24 V, and some use sliding-gear starters that move the pinion into mesh in two stages, first slowly at low power and then at full power, so the large teeth mesh without damage. Start-stop cars use reinforced starters built for many more starts in their life.

Drives: engaging the flywheel

The drive pushes the pinion into mesh with the flywheel ring gear, passes cranking torque, and releases when the engine fires.

A Bendix inertia drive carries the pinion on a coarse helical screw thread on the armature shaft. When the motor spins, the pinion lags because of inertia and screws forward into mesh. When the engine fires and over-runs the starter, it drives the pinion back and it disengages. It is cheap and needs no solenoid for meshing, but teeth can clash if pinion tooth meets ring-gear tooth.

A pre-engaged drive uses the solenoid to swing a lever that pushes the pinion into mesh before the main contacts close. The motor gets current only after the teeth are in mesh, so there is no clash. Most cars use this.

An overrunning clutch (roller clutch) inside the pinion makes the drive one-way. The armature can turn the pinion, but the pinion cannot turn the armature, so a firing engine does not spin the starter to destruction. A pre-engaged drive always has one.

An armature shift drive pulls the whole armature axially with a solenoid so the pinion meshes; the same movement closes the contacts. It appears on older designs.

Control circuit and power circuit

Two circuits work together. The power circuit runs from battery positive, through a heavy cable, the solenoid contacts and the motor to earth, and carries 150 A to 400 A. The control circuit runs from the ignition switch through the relay coil to the solenoid coils. Its relay coil side carries 0.1 A to 0.3 A. The terminal 50 line that feeds the solenoid coils carries about 40 A while the plunger moves and about 10 A once the main contacts close. Both are small compared with the power circuit, but they are not the same current.

DIN terminal numbers name the nodes. Terminal 30 is battery positive, unswitched. Terminal 15 is ignition-switched positive. Terminal 50 is the starter control line. The solenoid has two coils: a pull-in coil (heavy wire, about 30 A) that also feeds the motor terminal so the armature turns slowly during meshing, and a hold-in coil (about 10 A) that keeps the plunger home after the contacts close.

  1. Key turns to START: the ignition switch feeds the starter relay coil.
  2. The relay coil draws 0.1 A to 0.3 A and closes its contact, feeding terminal 50 of the solenoid.
  3. Pull-in and hold-in coils energise together, about 40 A in total; the plunger pulls the lever and the pinion moves into mesh.
  4. Plunger travel closes the main contacts from terminal 30 to the motor terminal. The contacts short out the pull-in coil, so only the hold-in coil (about 10 A) keeps the plunger in.
  5. The motor cranks, the engine fires, the key is released, the solenoid drops out, and a spring returns the pinion.

A neutral or clutch safety switch sits in series in the terminal 50 line, so the motor cannot crank while a gear is selected. An immobiliser adds one more series contact in the same line.

Worked example

Question. A starter draws 250 A at 10.5 V for 2.0 s during a crank. Find (a) the charge taken from the battery in coulombs and in ampere-hours, (b) the electrical power at the starter terminals, and (c) the energy delivered to the starter.

  1. Data: I = 250 A, V = 10.5 V, t = 2.0 s.
  2. Q = I t = 250 A × 2.0 s = 500 C.
  3. 1 Ah = 3600 C, so Q = 500 ÷ 3600 = 0.139 Ah.
  4. Power delivered to the starter at its terminals: P = V I = 10.5 V × 250 A = 2625 W.
  5. Energy delivered to the starter: E = P t = 2625 W × 2.0 s = 5250 J. The battery supplies slightly more because of internal resistance and cable drop.

Answer. The crank takes 500 C (0.139 Ah) from the battery. The starter receives 2625 W and 5250 J at its terminals; the battery supplies slightly more because of internal and cable losses.

Worked example 2

Question. A gear-reduction starter has its armature running at 8000 rpm with a torque of 0.80 N·m while cranking. The planetary gear is 4.5:1, the pinion has 10 teeth and the flywheel ring gear 130 teeth, and the gears together are 90 % efficient. The motor draws 110 A at 10.5 V. Find (a) the overall gear ratio, (b) the cranking speed and the torque at the crankshaft, (c) the armature output power and the power at the crankshaft, and (d) the motor efficiency.

  1. Pinion-to-ring ratio = 130 ÷ 10 = 13. Overall ratio = 4.5 × 13 = 58.5.
  2. Cranking speed = 8000 ÷ 58.5 = 136.8 rpm.
  3. Crankshaft torque = armature torque × ratio × efficiency = 0.80 × 58.5 × 0.90 = 42.1 N·m.
  4. Armature output power: ω = 2π × 8000 ÷ 60 = 837.8 rad/s; P = 0.80 × 837.8 = 670 W.
  5. Crankshaft power: ω = 2π × 136.8 ÷ 60 = 14.32 rad/s; P = 42.1 × 14.32 = 603 W, which is 670 W × 0.90, as it must be.
  6. Electrical input = 10.5 V × 110 A = 1155 W. Motor efficiency = 670 ÷ 1155 × 100 = 58 %.

Answer. The overall ratio is 58.5, so the engine is cranked at 136.8 rpm with 42.1 N·m; the armature gives 670 W and the crankshaft receives 603 W; the motor is 58 % efficient. A small, fast armature thus gives a large cranking torque through the gears.

Exam traps

  • The ignition switch does not carry starter current. It works a relay or solenoid; a switch that carried 250 A would weld its contacts in weeks.
  • Pull-in and hold-in coils are not both active all the time. Once the main contacts close they short the pull-in coil; only the hold-in coil keeps the plunger in.
  • Terminal 30 is battery positive (unswitched) and terminal 50 is the control line. Swapping them sends cranking current through the key switch.
  • The pinion must disengage after the engine fires. Without a working overrunning clutch or return spring, the engine spins the starter and destroys it.

1.11 High-voltage circuits and power distribution

A vehicle has more than one voltage domain. The 12 V bus runs lamps, ECUs and relays from the battery and alternator. The ignition secondary makes kilovolts for the spark plug. Advanced electric vehicles add a traction bus near 400 V. Fuses, relays, contactors and isolation checks keep these domains apart. This chapter maps the distribution path and its safety devices. It keeps the 12 V backbone separate from ignition kilovolts and EV traction volts.

You need: Chapter 1.4 for the 12 V battery, Chapter 1.6 for the regulated supply, and basic electrical safety.

The 12 V backbone and its protection

The battery positive terminal feeds a main fuse or fusible link close to the battery. It protects the thick battery cable from a short circuit, the most dangerous fault in a car because that cable can carry hundreds of amperes. After the main fuse, the cable reaches a power distribution box (fuse and relay box), often one under the bonnet and one inside the cabin. Fuses protect each branch circuit; a fuse melts when the current stays above its rating long enough. Relays are electrically controlled switches: a small current in the coil closes heavy contacts for a large load, so the dashboard switch carries only the coil current. The ignition switch or a push-button feeds terminal 15 and controls many relay coils. Electronic control units (ECUs) sit on protected branches and need a stable supply. Wire sizes and voltage drops are chosen as in Chapter 1.2; a dirty ground adds resistance in the same way as a thin wire. Chassis ground uses the steel body as the return path; bond straps connect panels and engine. The 12 V bus is a tree of protected branches, with the main fuse at the root.

A fuse protects the wire, not the load. It is chosen above the normal load current, so it does not blow in service, and at or below the current the wire can carry continuously, so the wire never overheats. Blade fuses come in standard ratings such as 5, 7.5, 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 A, colour-coded by rating. Newer cars add smart power distribution: semiconductor high-side switches that measure their own current, switch off on overload and report the fault to an ECU.

Ignition secondary: a separate high-voltage circuit

The ignition secondary is a separate high-voltage circuit. The ignition coil has a primary winding and a secondary winding on one magnetic core. Current in the primary builds a magnetic field. When the ignition module interrupts that current, the field collapses and induces a high voltage in the secondary by mutual inductance. Distributing that voltage is the job of the high-voltage distribution: in older engines a distributor's rotor arm, turning at half crankshaft speed, passes the pulse across a small gap to each segment of the distributor cap in firing order, and high-tension (HT) leads carry it to the plugs. Distributorless systems use one coil for each pair of cylinders, and coil-on-plug (COP) systems put a small coil above each plug, so no HT lead is left. Suppression leads and resistor plugs add resistance in the secondary; they reduce radio-frequency interference by slowing the spark current pulse. The secondary voltage rises until the gap ionises at breakdown, then falls to the low arc voltage that sustains the arc. Spark generation and timing come with ignition systems. The secondary can shock; treat it as high voltage.

V<sub>s</sub> = V<sub>p</sub> × (N<sub>s</sub> / N<sub>p</sub>)

Vs is secondary peak voltage in volts; Vp is primary peak voltage in volts; Ns is secondary turns; Np is primary turns.

EV traction high voltage (advanced)

Only a few universities teach the EV traction part. A traction battery works at about 400 V class, not 12 V. Two heavy contactors connect the battery to the inverter, which is a DC-to-AC converter for the motor. Contactors are relays built for high current and high voltage. The service disconnect is a manual switch or plug that separates the battery for maintenance. Orange cable marks the high-voltage path. An isolation monitor measures resistance between the high-voltage conductors and chassis; low isolation means a fault. The EV still has a 12 V bus, fed by a DC-DC converter, for lamps and ECUs. Do not mix this domain with ignition secondary kilovolts or the 12 V bus.

  • Check isolation before closing any contactor.
  • Close the pre-charge contactor through a resistor.
  • Close the main contactor; then open the pre-charge path.
  • Open contactors on fault or key-off.

Worked example

Question. An ignition coil has a turns ratio of 100:1 (secondary turns to primary turns). The primary current is interrupted at a peak of 350 V. Find the ideal maximum secondary peak voltage. The spark plug gap breaks down at 8 kV. Find the reserve voltage (headroom) above breakdown.

  1. Primary peak voltage Vp = 350 V; turns ratio Ns/Np = 100.
  2. Ideal maximum secondary peak Vs = Vp × (Ns/Np) = 350 V × 100 = 35,000 V = 35 kV. This is the most the coil can produce.
  3. The secondary voltage rises until the plug gap ionises at breakdown, Vion = 8 kV. It then falls to the arc voltage, a few hundred volts, which sustains the arc. The arc current comes from energy stored in the coil's magnetic field.
  4. Reserve voltage (headroom) = Vs − Vion = 35 kV − 8 kV = 27 kV. This is spare capability to fire a wider, worn or fouled gap; it is not a voltage driving the arc.

Answer. The ideal maximum secondary voltage is 35 kV. The gap breaks down at about 8 kV, then the voltage falls to the arc voltage of a few hundred volts. The 27 kV difference is headroom that lets the coil fire a wider, worn or fouled gap; it does not appear across the arc.

Worked example 2

Question. A rear-window heater draws 15 A continuously at 12 V. Its feed is a 1.5 mm² wire that, in this harness, may carry at most 20 A continuously. Choose the fuse from the standard blade ratings 10, 15, 20, 25 and 30 A, allowing 25 % above the normal load current so the fuse does not blow in service. Then say what happens with a 30 A fuse if the heater element shorts to ground and 28 A flows.

  1. Lowest acceptable rating: 1.25 × 15 A = 18.75 A.
  2. Next standard rating above 18.75 A: 20 A.
  3. Check against the wire: 20 A is not more than the 20 A the wire may carry, so the fuse protects the wire.
  4. Choose a 20 A fuse. A 15 A fuse would sit at exactly the load current and blow after a while in service.
  5. With a 30 A fuse and a 28 A fault current, the fuse never blows, because 28 A is below its rating. The wire, rated 20 A, carries 28 A, overheats, and its insulation can melt or burn.

Answer. Fit a 20 A fuse (at least 18.75 A, at most the 20 A the wire can carry). A 30 A fuse would let a 28 A fault overheat the 1.5 mm² wire: the fuse rating must follow the wire, not the load alone.

Exam traps

  • Domain mix-up: ignition secondary is kilovolts; EV traction is hundreds of volts; the 12 V bus is neither.
  • Primary versus secondary: the coil primary is a low-voltage winding; the secondary is the high-voltage winding.
  • Fuse job: a fuse limits current to protect wiring; it does not set or limit spark voltage.
  • Voltage drop: a high-resistance wire or ground drops volts only when current flows; an open circuit can show full voltage with no current.
  • EV pre-charge: the pre-charge resistor is not the main contactor; it limits capacitor inrush before the main contactor closes.

Takeaways

  1. Vehicle electrics grew from a few lamps and an ignition coil into networked control because loads, emission laws and safety rules kept rising; 12 V and the alternator came first, ECUs and CAN later.
  2. The power supply is a generator, a store and grouped loads on one bus; the charge balance over a trip decides whether the battery stays charged.
  3. A wire is sized by A = Iρl ÷ ΔU, rounded up to a standard size and checked for temperature; a fuse is chosen to protect that wire.
  4. EMC has a source, a path and a sink: suppress at the source (diodes, capacitors, resistor plugs), block the path (short, twisted, screened wires, ground straps) and harden the sink.
  5. A 12 V battery is six lead-acid cells of about 2.1 V; discharge turns both plates to lead sulfate and uses acid, so specific gravity and rest voltage show the state of charge.
  6. Ah, reserve capacity and CCA answer different questions; load, conductance and open-circuit tests show the real condition, and a replacement must match the technology.
  7. The alternator makes three-phase AC rectified by six diodes; its current is zero below n₀ and limits itself at high speed, so idle speed and pulley ratio decide the charge balance in traffic.
  8. The regulator holds the bus voltage by switching the field; the diodes are usually the largest single loss.
  9. A starter must reach the minimum starting speed at the meeting point of its torque curve and the engine resistance; cold and voltage drop both pull that point down.
  10. A small key current works a relay and the solenoid, which mesh the pinion before closing the 250 A main contacts; the ignition secondary and EV traction voltage are separate high-voltage domains.

  8. Sketch both circuits with the four currents written on them: relay coil 0.2 A, solenoid 40 A then 10 A, motor 250 A. Check: the key switch never carries more than the relay current, so it does not burn.
Apply A fuel pump draws 8 A. It is 4.5 m from the relay and has its own return wire of the same length. The permitted voltage drop is 0.5 V and copper resistivity is 0.0172 Ω·mm²/m at 20 °C. (a) Size the wire. (b) Check it when the harness runs at 60 °C (resistivity +0.4 % per K). (c) Choose the fuse for a wire rated 20 A. (d) The car then stands for 21 days with a 25 mA standby current; find the charge lost from a 60 Ah battery. 13 marks

Points to cover. Three tests for a wire: voltage drop, temperature, strength; A = Iρl ÷ ΔU with l = 2 × 4.5 m because of the return wire; Hot resistivity and recheck; next size; Fuse between 1.25 × load and wire rating; Standby charge Q = I × t.

Model answer.

  1. A wire must pass three tests: the voltage drop at full load within the permitted value, the conductor temperature within the insulation limit, and mechanical strength. Here the voltage drop and the temperature effect decide.
  2. (a) The pump has its own return wire, so the current flows out and back: l = 2 × 4.5 = 9.0 m.
    A = I × ρ × l ÷ ΔU = 8 × 0.0172 × 9.0 ÷ 0.5 = 2.48 mm²
next standard size: 2.5 mm²
drop at 20 °C = 8 × 0.0172 × 9.0 ÷ 2.5 = 0.495 V   (just inside 0.5 V)
  3. (b) At 60 °C the resistivity is 40 K × 0.4 % = 16 % higher:
    ρ(60 °C) = 0.0172 × 1.16 = 0.0200 Ω·mm²/m
drop on 2.5 mm² = 8 × 0.0200 × 9.0 ÷ 2.5 = 0.575 V   (above 0.5 V: fails)
drop on 4 mm²   = 8 × 0.0200 × 9.0 ÷ 4   = 0.359 V   (passes)
  4. So a wire that passes at 20 °C fails in a hot engine bay; choose 4 mm² for the feed and return. A pump that receives 0.575 V less runs slower and delivers less fuel pressure, which shows up as hesitation under load.
  5. (c) Fuse: at least 25 % above the load so it does not blow in service, and not above the 20 A the wire can carry.
    lowest rating = 1.25 × 8 = 10 A
standard ratings: 5, 7.5, 10, 15, 20 A
choose 10 A (≤ 20 A wire rating)
  6. A 10 A fuse protects the 4 mm² wire with a wide margin; a 30 A fuse would let a short circuit overheat the wire before blowing, because the fuse must follow the wire, not only the load.
  7. (d) Standby discharge:
    t = 21 days × 24 h = 504 h
Q = 0.025 A × 504 h = 12.6 Ah
share of 60 Ah = 12.6 ÷ 60 = 21 %, so 79 % remains
  8. The battery still starts the car, but a few more weeks, or a cold morning, would not leave enough; long-parked vehicles need a maintenance charger or an isolator switch.
  9. Sketch the circuit: terminal 30 → fuse 10 A → relay contact 30-87 → 4 mm² feed 4.5 m → pump → 4 mm² return 4.5 m → ground 31. Check: the hot drop on the chosen wire, 0.36 V, is well inside the 0.5 V limit, and the fuse (10 A) sits between the load (8 A) and the wire rating (20 A).

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze A car cranks slowly on a cold morning. The battery rests at 12.3 V; the hydrometer reads 1.24, 1.24, 1.23, 1.12, 1.24 and 1.24 in the six cells; while cranking at 200 A the drop along the positive cable is 0.9 V. Using battery ratings, battery tests and starter circuit facts, locate the faults and justify each step. 13 marks

Points to cover. Observe symptoms: slow crank, dim lamps, solenoid click; Rest voltage and SG per cell; one weak cell; Load or conductance test against CCA; Voltage-drop test of cables and grounds; compare with expected resistance; Charging system check; decide what to replace.

Model answer.

  1. Observe first: slow cranking with lamps that dim heavily points to a weak battery or high resistance in the high-current path; a single click with no crank points to the solenoid or a very high resistance; normal lamps and a slow crank point to the starter or engine. Here the crank is slow and cold, so test the battery, then the cables, then the starter.
  2. Rest voltage: 12.3 V after rest. Using the chapter scale (12.6–12.8 V full, about 12.2 V half, about 12.0 V a quarter), the battery is a little above half charge. Specific gravity per cell:
    Cell123456
    SG1.241.241.231.121.241.24
    Cell V ≈ SG + 0.842.082.082.071.962.082.08
  3. Five cells agree within 0.01; cell 4 is 0.12 lower. Charging cannot cure a cell that stays far below the others, so cell 4 is faulty (sulfated, shorted or with shed material). Check against the rest voltage: 2.08 × 5 + 1.96 = 12.36 V, which matches the 12.3 V measured.
  4. Load or conductance test: a battery with one weak cell fails the load test (half the CCA for 15 s, at least 9.6 V) because that cell's voltage collapses under current. In the chapter example a healthy battery of CCA 450 A fell to 10.1 V (11.1 mΩ) and a weak one to 9.2 V (15.1 mΩ). Expect the second result here; the battery needs replacing.
  5. Voltage-drop test on the positive cable at 200 A: the measured 0.9 V gives the resistance; compare with a new 16 mm² cable 1.0 m long.
    measured R = 0.9 V ÷ 200 A = 0.0045 Ω = 4.5 mΩ
new cable R = 0.0172 × 1.0 ÷ 16 = 0.00108 Ω → drop = 200 × 0.00108 = 0.22 V
excess = 4.5 − 1.1 = 3.4 mΩ, about four times the cable itself
  6. The extra 3.4 mΩ is almost certainly a corroded terminal or a loose clamp: clean and tighten, then repeat. Test the ground side the same way (battery negative to engine block, engine to body); the drop there should also be only a few tenths of a volt.
  7. Effect on cranking: the starter sees the battery terminal voltage minus 0.9 V. With a weak cell and a corroded terminal both lowering the voltage, the starter torque curve drops and the stable cranking point moves to a lower speed, below the minimum starting speed on a cold morning.
  8. Charging system: after fixing, check the bus at fast idle (13.8–14.4 V). A battery left at half charge in winter suggests short trips or a weak charging system, which would bring the fault back.
  9. Decision: replace the battery (cell 4) with one of at least the original CCA and the same technology, clean and retighten the positive terminal, and confirm with a cranking voltage test. Check: each fault was found with a number (SG spread 0.12, 4.5 mΩ against 1.1 mΩ), not a guess.
Analyze An alternator delivers 70 A at 14.0 V with 1300 W input at the pulley. The field is 4.0 A in 3.0 Ω, each stator phase is 0.007 Ω carrying 0.816 × I_dc RMS, and each diode drops 0.9 V. Analyse the power losses, find the efficiency and explain how losses and the machine itself limit output at low engine speed. 13 marks

Points to cover. Output and total loss; efficiency; Field and stator copper loss; diode loss 2V_F I_dc; Remainder: iron, brush, bearing, fan; How each loss varies with load and speed; Low speed: reactance curve, cooling and diode heating.

Model answer.

  1. Output and efficiency:
    Pout = 14.0 × 70 = 980 W
Ploss = 1300 − 980 = 320 W
η = 980 ÷ 1300 × 100 = 75.4 %
  2. Individual losses:
    field copper   = If² Rf = 4.0² × 3.0 = 48 W
stator copper  = 3 × (0.816 × 70)² × 0.007 = 3 × 57.1² × 0.007 = 68.5 W
diodes         = 2 × 0.9 × 70 = 126 W
sum            = 242.5 W
remainder      = 320 − 242.5 = 77.5 W (iron, brush, bearing, fan)
  3. Loss budget:
    LossWShare of 320 WVaries with
    Diodes12639 %output current (about linear)
    Iron, brush, bearing, fan77.524 %speed (rises steeply)
    Stator copper68.521 %current squared
    Field copper4815 %field current squared
  4. The diodes are the largest single loss, because two always conduct and each drops about 0.9 V whatever the voltage; at a 14 V output, 1.8 V is about 11 % of the generated DC. This is why the rectifier sits on a heat sink in the air stream and why Zener or low-loss diodes matter.
  5. Copper losses rise with the square of current, so they dominate at full load; iron loss rises with frequency (speed), and fan and bearing losses rise steeply with speed, so efficiency falls again at very high speed even though output is near its limit.
  6. Low engine speed: the available output follows I ≈ I_max(1 − n₀ ÷ n). At idle the alternator is low on its curve (in the chapter example 72 A at 2000 rpm against 120 A at 6000 rpm), the regulator is already at full field (field copper loss at its maximum), and the fan, which turns with the rotor, moves less air while the diodes and windings still carry high current. Heat therefore limits how long the alternator can deliver its idle maximum.
  7. Effect on the vehicle: when the loads exceed the curve at idle the battery supplies the difference; raising idle speed or the pulley ratio moves the operating point up the curve, while reducing losses (better diodes, liquid cooling) lets the same machine deliver more at idle without overheating.
  8. Check: 48 + 68.5 + 126 + 77.5 = 320 W, and 980 + 320 = 1300 W, so the budget closes. The field duty cycle for 4.0 A is 4.0 × 3.0 ÷ 14.0 = 0.857, so the regulator still has a margin.
Evaluate Explain the structure of vehicle power distribution, the ignition high-voltage circuit and its distribution, and the EV traction domain. Justify fuses, relays and pre-charge with numbers: a 15 A heater on a 20 A wire, an ignition coil of ratio 100:1 interrupted at 350 V, and an inverter of 1 mF on a 400 V battery with a 50 Ω pre-charge resistor. 12 marks

Points to cover. 12 V tree: battery, main fuse, distribution box, fuses, relays, loads, ground; Fuse choice: above load, at or below wire; Relay: small control current switches large load; Ignition: coil primary/secondary, 35 kV ideal, breakdown 8 kV, distributor or COP, suppression; EV: 400 V battery, contactors, pre-charge (τ, peak current), isolation, DC-DC.

Model answer.

  1. 12 V distribution: battery positive → main fuse or fusible link near the battery (protects the heavy cable, the most dangerous short in the car) → power distribution boxes under the bonnet and in the cabin → branch fuses → relays or switches → loads → body ground → battery negative. ECUs sit on fused branches; smart distribution replaces some fuses and relays with semiconductor switches that measure current.
  2. Fuse for the heater: the fuse protects the wire.
    lowest rating = 1.25 × 15 A = 18.75 A → standard 20 A
20 A ≤ wire rating 20 A → protected
with a 30 A fuse, a 28 A fault never blows it and the wire overheats
  3. Relay: the dash switch carries only the relay coil current (about 0.1–0.2 A), while the relay contact carries the heater's 15 A through short, heavy wires; the switch stays small and cool and the heavy wire runs the shortest route.
  4. Ignition high-voltage circuit: interrupting the primary current makes the field collapse and induce a high voltage in the many-turn secondary.
    ideal peak Vs = Vp × Ns/Np = 350 × 100 = 35 kV
plug breakdown = 8 kV → voltage then falls to the arc voltage (a few hundred volts)
reserve (headroom) = 35 − 8 = 27 kV for worn or wide gaps
  5. Distribution of the high voltage: in older engines the distributor rotor, turning at half crankshaft speed, passes the pulse across a gap to each cap segment and HT lead in firing order; distributorless systems use one coil per pair of cylinders; coil-on-plug puts a coil on each plug. Resistor plugs and suppression leads slow the spark current pulse to cut radio interference.
  6. EV traction domain: a battery of about 400 V connects through contactors to the inverter, whose input capacitors are uncharged at start-up. Closing the main contactor directly would short them through a few tens of milliohms; a pre-charge resistor fills them first.
    without pre-charge, with 0.02 Ω in the loop: peak ≈ 400 ÷ 0.02 = 20,000 A
with 50 Ω: peak = 400 ÷ 50 = 8 A
time constant τ = RC = 50 × 0.001 = 0.05 s; 95 % charged after 3τ = 0.15 s
energy stored in the capacitors = ½CV² = 0.5 × 0.001 × 400² = 80 J
  7. Sequence: check isolation (isolation monitor), close the negative contactor and the pre-charge contactor, wait about 3τ until the capacitor voltage is near the battery voltage, close the main positive contactor, open the pre-charge path. Orange cables, a service disconnect and an interlock loop protect technicians; a DC-DC converter feeds the vehicle's own 12 V bus.
  8. Evaluation: the three domains differ by orders of magnitude (14 V, 400 V, 35 kV) and must never be mixed; fuses, relays and pre-charge each protect a specific weak point: the wire, the switch and the contactor. Check: the pre-charge peak of 8 A is 2500 times smaller than the 20,000 A that would weld the contacts.
Evaluate Choose a battery, a starter and the starter cable for a petrol engine that needs 45 N·m at 200 rpm on the coldest morning. Assume the starter and its gears are 50 % efficient, the starter needs at least 9.6 V at its terminals, the cable and ground total 2 mΩ, the cold battery emf is 12.4 V, and (as a rough model) a battery's internal resistance is about (12.4 − 7.2) ÷ CCA. Justify each choice. 15 marks

Points to cover. Cranking power from torque and speed; Electrical input and current at 9.6 V; Allowed battery internal resistance → minimum CCA; choose rating; Ah and RC for loads; Starter type (gear reduction) and ratio; Cable size for a 0.25 V drop on 1.5 m; Charging check and assumptions.

Model answer.

  1. Cranking power needed at the crankshaft:
    ω = 2π × 200 ÷ 60 = 20.94 rad/s
P = Tω = 45 × 20.94 = 942 W
  2. Electrical input and current at the starter:
    Pel = 942 ÷ 0.50 = 1885 W
I = 1885 ÷ 9.6 = 196 A
  3. Battery: at 196 A the cable and ground drop 196 × 0.002 = 0.39 V, so the battery terminals must hold at least 9.6 + 0.39 = 9.99 V.
    allowed Rint = (12.4 − 9.99) ÷ 196 = 0.0123 Ω = 12.3 mΩ
rough model Rint ≈ 5.2 ÷ CCA → CCA ≥ 5.2 ÷ 0.0123 = 424 A
choose a standard battery with CCA 450 A
  4. Capacity: 450 A CCA batteries for cars are commonly about 60 Ah with a reserve capacity near 100 min; that gives the parked-car margin (21 days at 25 mA uses only 12.6 Ah, 21 %) and keeps the lights and ignition running for over an hour and a half at 25 A if the alternator fails. One 2 s crank uses only 196 × 2 ÷ 3600 = 0.11 Ah, so starting is a current problem, not a capacity problem.
  5. Battery type: a maintenance-free (lead-calcium) flooded battery for a normal car; EFB or AGM if the car has start-stop, because it will be cycled far more often. For a cold region, the CCA, not the Ah, is the deciding number.
  6. Starter: a permanent-magnet gear-reduction starter gives the torque from a small, fast armature. With the chapter's gearing (planetary 4.5:1, pinion 10 and ring gear 130 teeth):
    overall ratio = 4.5 × 130 ÷ 10 = 58.5
armature speed for 200 rpm = 200 × 58.5 = 11,700 rpm
armature torque = 45 ÷ (58.5 × 0.9) = 0.85 N·m
  7. That armature speed is high; a slightly smaller ratio (for example a 3.5:1 planetary, overall 45.5, armature 9100 rpm, 1.10 N·m) suits a typical motor better. The pre-engaged drive with an overrunning clutch is chosen so the pinion meshes before full power and cannot be driven by the engine.
  8. Starter cable, 1.5 m, permitted drop 0.25 V:
    A = I ρ l ÷ ΔU = 196 × 0.0172 × 1.5 ÷ 0.25 = 20.2 mm² → choose 25 mm²
drop on 25 mm² = 196 × 0.0172 × 1.5 ÷ 25 = 0.20 V
  9. The chosen cable drops 0.20 V, inside the 0.39 V allowed for cable and ground together, leaving the rest for the ground strap and terminals, which must be clean.
  10. Charging check: the alternator must recover the start charge and the standby losses; with a 120 A alternator and an idle current near 72 A (as in the alternator chapter), the recharge after a start takes seconds. Assumptions: the torque figure is a mean value (peak compression torque is higher), the 50 % efficiency includes motor and gears, and the CCA model is rough, so a margin (450 A against 424 A) is kept.

Glossary

AGM battery
A valve-regulated lead-acid battery whose acid is held in glass mats; oxygen recombines inside, so it loses almost no water.
Alternator
A belt-driven three-phase generator with a rotating field and stationary stator, rectified to DC by diodes.
Charge balance
Charge put into the battery minus charge taken out over a trip.
Circuit diagram
A drawing that shows how a circuit works, with current paths drawn straight from supply to ground.
Claw-pole rotor
A rotor with one field coil enclosed by two interlocking claw pieces that form alternate north and south poles.
Conductance test
A quick test that injects a small AC signal to estimate available CCA without discharging the battery.
Contactor
A heavy relay built to switch high current at high voltage, as in an EV traction circuit.
Coupling path
The route by which interference reaches a victim: conducted, capacitive, inductive or radiated.
Cranking
Turning the engine at starting speed with the starter before combustion can sustain it.
Duty cycle
The fraction of each switching period that the regulator keeps the field switched on.
Exciter diodes
Three small diodes that feed D+ and the field from the stator once the alternator runs.
Field current
The DC through the rotor winding; more field current gives more output voltage.
Freewheeling diode
A diode across a coil that carries its current at switch-off and clamps the voltage spike.
Fuse
A calibrated metal link that melts when current stays above its rating, protecting the wire.
Gassing
Splitting of water into hydrogen and oxygen when a cell is pushed above about 2.3–2.4 V on charge.
Grid
The lead-alloy frame that holds the active material and carries current to the plate lug.
Hydrometer
A float-type tester that reads electrolyte specific gravity to show state of charge.
Ignition secondary
The high-voltage winding and circuit of the ignition coil that fires the spark plug.
Lead-acid cell
A rechargeable cell with a lead dioxide positive plate, a spongy lead negative plate and dilute sulfuric acid, giving about 2.1 V.
Load dump
A voltage surge on the bus when the battery is disconnected while the alternator is charging.
Load test
A test at half the CCA for 15 s; a good battery stays above about 9.6 V.
Pinion
The small gear on the starter that meshes with the engine's ring gear.
Relay
An electromagnetic switch that lets a small control current switch a heavy load current.
Separator
A thin porous sheet between plates that stops them touching while letting ions pass.
Slip ring
A rotating ring that, with a brush, feeds field current into the rotor.
Solenoid
An electromagnet with a plunger that moves the starter drive and closes the main contacts.
Starter motor
A short-duty DC motor that cranks the engine through a pinion on the flywheel ring gear.
Stator
The stationary laminated core with three windings in which the alternating emf is induced.
Sulfation
Hard lead sulfate that forms on plates left discharged and no longer converts back on charge.
Terminal 31
Ground: the return to battery negative through the body or a ground wire.
