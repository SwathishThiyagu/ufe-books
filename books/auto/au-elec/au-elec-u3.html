3.1 Automotive microelectronics: semiconductor components and microcontrollers

Every sensor in this unit ends at an electronic control unit (ECU), and every actuator starts at one. Inside the ECU sit semiconductor components (diodes, transistors, integrated circuits) and a microcontroller, a one-chip computer that reads the sensors, runs the control program and switches the outputs thousands of times a second. A car is a hard place for electronics, so these parts are built for heat, vibration and a noisy supply.

You need: Ohm's law, power P = V I = I²R, and the vehicle electrical system (12 V battery and alternator) from the batteries and charging topic.

Demands on automotive electronics and semiconductor basics

A vehicle ECU must work for 15 years or more in conditions an office computer never meets. Some syllabi list these as the demands on automotive electronics:

  • Temperature: −40 °C to +85 °C in the cabin and up to 125 °C or 150 °C on or near the engine. Automotive chips are qualified for these grades (the AEC-Q100 standard for integrated circuits).
  • Supply: a nominal 12 V system runs between about 9 V and 16 V, sags while cranking, and sees fast spikes from switched coils and a load dump (the alternator suddenly losing its battery connection while charging).
  • Vibration, humidity, EMC: ECUs are sealed, and must neither disturb other systems nor be upset by ignition noise.
  • Safety and cost: brake and steering ECUs check themselves, and every part is made in millions.

All these parts are built from semiconductors, mostly silicon. A semiconductor conducts better than an insulator and worse than a metal. Pure silicon has an energy gap of 1.12 eV, so few electrons are free at room temperature. Doping adds a tiny amount of another element: phosphorus gives spare electrons (n-type), boron gives holes, missing electrons that act as positive carriers (p-type). Where p-type meets n-type a pn junction forms. It conducts in one direction once the forward voltage passes about 0.6 to 0.7 V and blocks in the other. Every diode, transistor and chip in this unit is built from such junctions. A junction's forward voltage falls by about 2 mV per kelvin as it warms.

An ECU board carries passive parts (resistors, capacitors, inductors), which only store or dissipate energy, and active semiconductor parts, which switch or amplify. The common ones and their jobs:

A bipolar transistor needs a base current IB; the collector current is about β times larger (β, the current gain, is often 50 to 300; a saturated switch is designed for a forced gain of only 10 to 20). Fully on, it drops 0.2 to 0.3 V (VCE(sat)). A MOSFET needs almost no steady gate current: a gate voltage of 5 to 10 V turns it on, and it then behaves like a small resistor RDS(on), often a few milliohms. Its loss is P = I² RDS(on). That is why output drivers are now MOSFETs, often packaged as smart power switches that add current limiting, over-temperature shut-down and a fault flag the microcontroller can read.

Microcontrollers and data processing in the vehicle

A microcontroller (MCU) is a processor with its memory and input-output on one chip. Engine ECUs use 32-bit MCUs clocked at tens to a few hundred megahertz. Its parts:

  • CPU: executes the program, one or more instructions per clock cycle.
  • Flash memory: holds the program and the calibration maps; keeps them with the power off.
  • RAM: working values that change every cycle (speed, load, counters); lost at power-off.
  • EEPROM or data flash: learnt values and stored fault codes that must survive switch-off.
  • ADC: turns sensor voltages into numbers (10 or 12 bits); timers with capture inputs time crank-tooth edges, and compare outputs and PWM channels fire injectors, coils and valves at exact moments.
  • Communication: CAN and LIN controllers to talk to other ECUs; watchdog: a timer that resets the MCU if the program stops refreshing it.

Data processing in the ECU follows one loop: read the inputs, check that each is plausible, compute, drive the outputs, and log faults. Fast events such as a crank tooth arrive as interrupts that pause the main loop. Slow ones (coolant temperature) are read every 10 to 100 ms. Most control laws are not equations but maps: tables over speed and load, read with linear interpolation. If a 2000 rpm cell holds 20° of spark advance and the 3000 rpm cell holds 26°, the ECU uses 20 + (26 − 20) × (2500 − 2000) ÷ (3000 − 2000) = 23° at 2500 rpm.

Worked example

Question. An engine ECU has an 80 MHz microcontroller and a 60-2 crank wheel. (a) How much time and how many clock cycles pass between two tooth interrupts at 6000 rpm? (b) The tooth routine takes 1500 cycles. What share of the CPU does it use? (c) At what speed would the routine use half the CPU?

  1. Revolutions per second: 6000 ÷ 60 = 100 rev/s, so one revolution takes 10 ms.
  2. Tooth pitch period: 10 ms ÷ 60 pitches = 0.1667 ms = 166.7 µs.
  3. Clock cycles per tooth: 166.7 × 10⁻⁶ s × 80 × 10⁶ Hz = 13 333 cycles.
  4. Load of the tooth routine: 1500 ÷ 13 333 = 0.1125, about 11 % of the CPU.
  5. Half the CPU means 1500 cycles in a tooth period of 3000 cycles = 3000 ÷ 80 MHz = 37.5 µs. One revolution is then 60 × 37.5 µs = 2.25 ms, so n = 60 ÷ 0.00225 = 26 667 rpm.
  6. Check: no road engine reaches 26 667 rpm, so the MCU has time left for injection, ignition and diagnosis.

Answer. 166.7 µs and 13 333 cycles per tooth; the routine takes about 11 % of the CPU at 6000 rpm and would reach 50 % only at about 26 700 rpm.

Worked example 2

Question. A 55 W headlamp on a 13.5 V supply is switched (a) by a MOSFET with RDS(on) = 10 mΩ and (b) by a bipolar transistor with VCE(sat) = 0.3 V and a forced gain of 20 in saturation. Find the lamp current, the power lost in each switch, and the base current the bipolar transistor needs. Which can a microcontroller pin drive directly?

  1. Lamp current: I = P ÷ V = 55 ÷ 13.5 = 4.07 A.
  2. MOSFET loss: P = I² RDS(on) = 4.07² × 0.010 = 0.166 W.
  3. Bipolar loss: P = VCE(sat) × I = 0.3 × 4.07 = 1.22 W, more than seven times the MOSFET.
  4. Base current: IB = IC ÷ 20 = 4.07 ÷ 20 = 0.204 A.
  5. A microcontroller pin supplies only a few milliamps, so it cannot give 204 mA of base current. It can charge a logic-level MOSFET gate (nearly zero steady current), usually through a gate driver or inside a smart high-side switch.

Answer. I = 4.07 A; the MOSFET loses 0.166 W and the bipolar transistor 1.22 W and needs 0.204 A of base current. Only the MOSFET can be driven from the MCU (through a gate driver).

Exam traps

  • A pn junction does not conduct at any forward voltage: silicon needs about 0.6 to 0.7 V first. Writing "a diode drops 0 V" loses the mark in rectifier questions.
  • MOSFET loss is I² RDS(on), not V × I with the supply voltage; the supply voltage sits across the load, not the switch.
  • Flash and EEPROM both keep data at power-off: the program and maps live in flash, learnt values and fault codes in EEPROM.
  • A watchdog does not detect wrong sensor values; it detects a program that has stopped running and resets the MCU.

3.2 Sensor basics, classification and signal conditioning

A sensor turns a physical quantity — temperature, pressure, position, speed — into an electrical signal. That signal is often small, noisy and nonlinear, so signal conditioning amplifies, filters and shifts it before an ADC converts it to a number for the ECU. An error at the sensing element becomes a wrong fuel pulse or brake force. This chapter fixes the vocabulary, classifies sensors, and follows the signal to the ECU input.

You need: Ohm's law and the voltage divider, and a stable 5 V sensor supply; the microcontroller and its ADC are in automotive microelectronics.

Sensor, transducer, actuator, and the words used to judge them

A sensor converts a physical quantity into an electrical signal. The quantity it measures is the measured variable — coolant temperature, manifold pressure, wheel speed. The transducer is the element that does the conversion; most automotive writing uses both words for the same part. An actuator works the other way: an electrical command becomes motion or force (see solenoids, relays and stepper motors).

Specifications decide whether a sensor suits a job. Each word below has a precise meaning; marks are lost when units are dropped.

  • Range: the lowest and highest input the sensor is specified for, e.g. −40 °C to 150 °C.
  • Span: range maximum minus range minimum, e.g. 190 °C.
  • Sensitivity: output change per unit input change, e.g. 10 mV/°C. It always carries units.
  • Resolution: the smallest input change that gives a detectable output change.
  • Accuracy: how close the reading is to the true value, quoted as ±°C or as a percentage of full scale.
  • Linearity: how far the input–output graph bends away from a straight line, as a percentage of full scale.
  • Hysteresis: the output gap at the same input when the input arrives from below rather than from above.
  • Drift: slow change of output with time or ambient temperature while the input is held constant.
  • Response time: time for the output to reach a stated fraction (often 63% or 90%) of its final value after a step input.

Classification and the sensing elements inside

  • Active vs passive: a passive sensor generates its own signal (thermocouple, piezoelectric element, magnetic reluctance coil); an active sensor needs an excitation supply (thermistor, potentiometer, biased Hall device). Some instrumentation textbooks use the reverse convention — active for a self-generating element such as a thermocouple — so state which convention you are using in an answer.
  • Analog vs digital: analog sensors give a continuous voltage; digital ones give a pulse train whose frequency or count carries the value.
  • Contact vs non-contact: a coolant thermistor touches the fluid; a Hall or optical sensor reads a target across an air gap.
  • Absolute vs relative: an absolute sensor measures from true zero (manifold absolute pressure from vacuum); a relative sensor measures against a local reference such as ambient pressure.

Behind these classes sit a few physical effects. Resistive elements change resistance with position, temperature or strain (potentiometer, NTC thermistor, strain gauge). Capacitive elements change capacitance with diaphragm movement or humidity. Inductive and magnetic-reluctance elements produce a voltage from changing flux. Piezoelectric crystals produce charge under force, used for knock. Thermoelectric junctions (thermocouples) produce a small voltage proportional to temperature difference. Hall elements give a voltage proportional to magnetic field. MEMS devices etch a silicon diaphragm or proof mass so the sensing element and its conditioning circuit sit on one chip.

Signal conditioning: from millivolts to a number

Signal conditioning is everything between the sensing element and the ADC. It makes a weak, noisy, offset, nonlinear signal into a clean voltage in the ADC's input window.

  1. Excite and bridge: a Wheatstone bridge turns a small resistance change into a differential voltage that sits near zero when the input is zero.
  2. Amplify: an instrumentation amplifier multiplies the differential voltage by a fixed gain and converts it to a single-ended signal referred to ground.
  3. Filter: a low-pass filter removes ignition and switching noise; its corner is f = 1/(2πRC), with R in ohms and C in farads.
  4. Isolate: optocouplers or capacitive barriers separate high-voltage battery sensors from the low-voltage ECU.
  5. Linearise: a lookup table or polynomial maps the raw reading to engineering units, because thermistors and bridges are not straight lines.
  6. Cold-junction compensation: a thermocouple measures only a difference, so the ECU adds the measured temperature of the connector block.
  7. Sample and hold: a capacitor freezes the input while the ADC converts.
  8. ADC: an N-bit converter reports a count, count = round(V × (2N − 1) / Vref), where V is the input in volts and Vref is the reference in volts.

Vout = Vs × Rs / (Rs + Rf)

Vout is the junction voltage in volts; Vs is the supply voltage in volts; Rs is the sensing resistance in ohms, connected from Vout to ground; Rf is the fixed resistor in ohms, connected from +Vs to Vout.

Worked example

Question. A coolant temperature sensor uses a 1 kΩ fixed resistor Rf from +5 V to the output node, and an NTC thermistor Rs from the output node to ground. Rs is 2 kΩ at 25 °C and 0.5 kΩ at 80 °C. (a) Find the divider output at both temperatures. (b) Find the 10-bit ADC counts for a 0–5 V ADC. (c) State the ADC resolution in mV.

  1. Divider equation: Vout = Vs × Rs / (Rs + Rf), with Vs = 5 V and Rf = 1000 Ω. Rf connects +5 V to Vout; Rs connects Vout to ground.
  2. At 25 °C, Rs = 2000 Ω: Vout = 5 × 2000 / (2000 + 1000) = 3.333 V.
  3. At 80 °C, Rs = 500 Ω: Vout = 5 × 500 / (500 + 1000) = 1.667 V.
  4. Count for 10 bits: count = round(Vout × 1023 / 5).
  5. At 25 °C: count = round(3.333 × 1023 / 5) = 682.
  6. At 80 °C: count = round(1.667 × 1023 / 5) = 341.
  7. Resolution: one count = 5 / 1023 = 4.89 mV.
  8. Check the direction: resistance falls as temperature rises (NTC), so the output falls too. The count-to-°C map is nonlinear and is done by lookup table in the ECU.

Answer. The divider gives 3.333 V (682 counts) at 25 °C and 1.667 V (341 counts) at 80 °C, with 4.89 mV per ADC count.

Exam traps

  • The ADC count is not the physical value. 682 is a voltage code, not 25 °C; convert and linearise with the sensor curve before quoting a temperature.
  • NTC resistance falls with temperature, so the divider output falls with temperature. Many answers show the voltage rising and lose all the marks.
  • Sensitivity must carry units (mV/°C, mV/kPa).
  • Resolution is not accuracy. A 10-bit ADC resolves 4.89 mV, but the sensor may still be accurate only to ±2 °C; reference tolerance and self-heating add further error.
  • Self-heating: the excitation current warms the thermistor, so it reads hotter than the fluid. Keep the current small (a pull-up of a few kΩ).

Analyze A coolant thermistor reading climbs steadily for 20 minutes while the engine is off and the temperature is constant. Give two likely causes and one test for each. 5 marks

3.3 Air flow rate and manifold pressure (MAP) sensors

The engine needs a known mass of air for every stroke because fuel quantity is set from air charge. A mass air flow (MAF) sensor measures this mass directly; a vane meter measures air volume flow and needs a density correction. A manifold absolute pressure (MAP) sensor measures intake pressure, and the ECU computes mass from pressure, speed, displacement and temperature. These sensors sit in the intake path and feed the engine control unit (ECU). They are used in petrol and diesel fuel control, turbo boost control, exhaust gas recirculation and diagnostics. Get the air charge wrong and the mixture, torque and emissions all drift.

You need: sensor basics and signal conditioning (bridges and conditioning), the ideal gas law p = ρRT and engine displacement; how the ECU turns air mass into a fuel pulse is in the electronic fuel control topic of electronic engine control.

Manifold absolute pressure sensors

Manifold absolute pressure (MAP) is the absolute pressure inside the intake manifold after the throttle, measured in kPa. At idle the throttle is nearly closed, so MAP may be 20–40 kPa. At wide-open throttle in a naturally aspirated engine it rises toward barometric pressure. Barometric pressure is local atmospheric pressure, about 100 kPa at sea level and lower at altitude. With ignition on and engine off, MAP equals barometric pressure, which lets the ECU check the sensor and learn altitude.

A MAP sensor turns pressure into voltage. In a strain-gauge MAP sensor, a diaphragm bends under pressure. Foil or diffused strain gauges are bonded to it, and their resistance changes with strain. The gauges form a Wheatstone bridge. The bridge output is small, so signal conditioning amplifies and filters it.

Vo ≈ (Vex ÷ 4) × (ΔR ÷ R)

Vo is bridge output in V; Vex is excitation voltage in V; R is gauge resistance in Ω; ΔR is its change in Ω. This is the small-change quarter-bridge result.

A capacitive MAP sensor uses the diaphragm as one plate of a capacitor. Pressure moves the diaphragm, changes the gap and changes capacitance; an oscillator and detector convert this to voltage. A piezoresistive MAP sensor uses resistors diffused into a silicon diaphragm. Pressure bends the silicon and changes resistance. The bridge and signal conditioning sit inside the sensor. A typical transfer is 0.5 V at 20 kPa and 4.5 V at 100 kPa. The ECU uses a stored table for accuracy. Some syllabi call this a strain gauge pressure sensor.

Air flow meters

An air flow meter measures the air entering the engine. The oldest is the vane meter: a spring-loaded flap is pushed by air, and a potentiometer reports flap angle. It is simple but causes pressure loss and wears.

Hot-wire and hot-film meters keep a heated element at a fixed temperature above the incoming air. Moving air cools the element. The circuit increases current to hold the temperature, and that current measures mass flow. The element sits in a Wheatstone bridge. The bridge balances when the heated element resistance, and hence temperature, is fixed. After shutdown, a burn-off pulse heats the wire to around 1000 °C for about a second to burn off dirt. This keeps calibration stable. Hot-film sensors use a flat film and are more rugged; hot-wire sensors use a thin platinum wire and respond faster. Intake air temperature (IAT) is used to correct density and bridge drift.

A Karman vortex meter places a bluff body in the air stream. Vortices shed alternately downstream. The shedding frequency f is proportional to air velocity v: f = St × v ÷ d. Here f is frequency in Hz, St is the dimensionless Strouhal number (about 0.2), v is velocity in m/s, and d is bluff body width in m. A pressure or ultrasonic sensor counts vortices. Frequency gives velocity; density from pressure and temperature is needed for mass flow. These meters have no moving parts and low pressure loss.

Speed-density and mass-air methods

The speed-density method uses MAP, engine speed, displacement, volumetric efficiency and intake temperature. Volumetric efficiency ηv is the actual air volume trapped per intake stroke divided by the displaced volume, referenced to manifold conditions. It is dimensionless. With this reference it is typically 0.8–1.0 for naturally aspirated engines and stays near 1 for turbocharged engines. If VE is referenced to ambient pressure instead, a boosted engine can exceed 1.

ṁair = ηv × Pman × Vd × (N ÷ 2) ÷ (R × T)

ṁair is air mass flow in kg/s; Pman is MAP in Pa absolute; Vd is total engine displacement in m³; N is engine speed in rev/s; R is 287 J/(kg·K) for air; T is intake air temperature in K; ηv is volumetric efficiency. The factor N/2 appears because a four-stroke engine has one intake stroke per two revolutions.

The mass-air method uses a MAF sensor and measures mass flow directly. It needs no volumetric efficiency table, so it handles altitude, intake leaks and engine wear better. It costs more, and the hot element can be contaminated. The speed-density method is cheaper and uses a MAP sensor, but it depends on the VE table and correct IAT and barometric pressure. Many ECUs use MAF for steady operation and MAP for transient correction and diagnostics.

Worked example

Question. A 1.6 L four-stroke petrol engine runs at 3000 rpm. MAP is 60 kPa absolute and intake air temperature is 300 K. Volumetric efficiency is 0.90. Take R = 287 J/(kg·K). Estimate the air mass flow in kg/s and g/s.

  1. Convert displacement: Vd = 1.6 L = 0.0016 m³.
  2. Convert speed: N = 3000 ÷ 60 = 50 rev/s.
  3. For a four-stroke engine, the number of engine cycles per second = N ÷ 2 = 50 ÷ 2 = 25 s⁻¹.
  4. Convert pressure: Pman = 60 kPa = 60,000 Pa absolute.
  5. Air density at manifold conditions: ρ = Pman ÷ (R × T) = 60,000 ÷ (287 × 300) = 0.6969 kg/m³.
  6. Ideal air mass inducted per engine cycle (all cylinders together): ρ × Vd = 0.6969 × 0.0016 = 0.001115 kg.
  7. Actual air mass per engine cycle: ηv × 0.001115 = 0.90 × 0.001115 = 0.0010035 kg.
  8. Air mass flow: 0.0010035 × 25 = 0.02509 kg/s.
  9. Convert to g/s: 0.02509 × 1000 = 25.09 g/s, about 25.1 g/s.

Answer. The estimated air mass flow is 0.0251 kg/s, which is 25.1 g/s.

Worked example 2

Question. A Karman vortex air flow meter has a bluff body 20 mm wide in a 50 mm diameter intake duct. The ECU counts 200 vortices per second. Take the Strouhal number as 0.20, the intake air at 100 kPa and 300 K, and R = 287 J/(kg·K). Find the air velocity, the volume flow and the mass flow in g/s.

  1. Velocity from the shedding law f = St × v ÷ d: v = f × d ÷ St = 200 × 0.020 ÷ 0.20 = 20.0 m/s.
  2. Duct area: A = π d² ÷ 4 = π × 0.050² ÷ 4 = 0.001963 m².
  3. Volume flow: Q = v × A = 20.0 × 0.001963 = 0.03927 m³/s.
  4. Air density: ρ = p ÷ (R T) = 100 000 ÷ (287 × 300) = 1.161 kg/m³.
  5. Mass flow: ṁ = ρ Q = 1.161 × 0.03927 = 0.04561 kg/s = 45.6 g/s.
  6. Check: the vortex count gives volume flow only; the 1.161 kg/m³ came from the pressure and temperature sensors, so a wrong intake air temperature reading gives a wrong mass.

Answer. v = 20.0 m/s, Q = 0.0393 m³/s and ṁ = 45.6 g/s; the meter needs pressure and temperature to turn its volume reading into mass.

Exam traps

  • Using rpm directly in the speed-density formula. Convert to rev/s and remember the N/2 factor for a four-stroke engine.
  • Using gauge pressure instead of absolute pressure. MAP and Pman must be absolute, and in Pa inside the formula.
  • Treating MAF and MAP as interchangeable. MAF gives mass flow directly; MAP needs speed, displacement, volumetric efficiency and temperature.
  • Thinking Karman vortex frequency alone gives mass flow. Frequency gives velocity; density correction is needed for mass.
  • Reading burn-off current as a flow signal. Burn-off is a cleaning pulse after shutdown, not a measurement phase.

3.4 Crankshaft position and engine speed sensors

An engine must fire the spark and open each injector at a precise crank angle, not just at a precise average rate. The crankshaft position sensor (CKP) and the camshaft position sensor (CMP) report piston position and engine speed to the engine control unit (ECU). The ECU uses these pulses to synchronise ignition and fuel delivery, to detect misfire, and to run sequential injection. Every modern petrol engine uses at least a CKP. Some syllabi call the CKP the engine crankshaft angular position sensor, the engine angular speed sensor or the timing sensor.

You need: sensor basics and signal conditioning for threshold and filter circuits, and basic angular motion: angle in degrees, speed in rpm and frequency in hertz.

Position, speed and phase

The CKP faces a toothed wheel on the crankshaft pulley or the flywheel. Each tooth that passes the sensor produces one electrical pulse. The ECU reads angular position from the identity of the tooth and angular speed from the time between pulses. The same signal gives both quantities, so one sensor serves ignition timing, injector timing, idle control, the rev limiter and the tachometer.

The CMP reads a single tooth, window or magnetic target on the camshaft. In a four-stroke engine the camshaft turns at half crankshaft speed, so one cam pulse occurs per two crank revolutions. That pulse identifies cylinder 1 on its compression stroke. Sequential fuel injection and individual coil-on-plug ignition need this phase information; without it the ECU may fall back to wasted spark (spark on both strokes) and batch injection (injectors fired in groups).

Angular position is an angle, usually measured in degrees before top dead centre (BTDC). Angular speed is a rate, usually in revolutions per minute (rpm). Do not mix them. A CKP can report position to within one tooth pitch and speed to within a fraction of a percent after filtering.

n = 1 / (z × T) rpm = 60 / (z × T) Δθ = 360° / z

n is revolutions per second; rpm is revolutions per minute; z is the number of tooth pitches per revolution (60 for a 60-2 wheel, 36 for a 36-1 wheel); T is the time between successive normal tooth edges in seconds; Δθ is the crank angle per tooth pitch in degrees. For a 60-2 wheel Δθ = 6°.

Toothed wheels and missing-tooth synchronisation

A plain toothed wheel gives speed but not absolute position: the ECU cannot tell tooth 1 from tooth 20 after switch-on. Manufacturers cut one or more teeth out of the wheel to create a reference gap. Common wheels are 60-2 (58 teeth present, 2 missing), 36-1 (35 present, 1 missing) and 12+1. In a 12+1 wheel all teeth are present, and the reference is the single short interval produced by the extra tooth, so the ECU detects an unusually short period instead of an unusually long one. The reference feature appears once per revolution.

  1. Condition the sensor signal into clean edges (see sensor basics and signal conditioning).
  2. Measure the period T between consecutive edges with a timer.
  3. Compare each period with the previous one. A gap is longer than a normal tooth period by a known ratio: for 60-2 the gap is 3T.
  4. When the gap is detected, reset the tooth counter and assign the stored angle of the first tooth after the gap.
  5. Compute speed from T and update the ignition and injection schedules.

The missing-tooth gap for a 60-2 wheel is three tooth periods, not two. The last present tooth is followed by two empty slots and then the next present tooth, so the time from the last edge to the next edge is 3T. For a 36-1 wheel the gap is 2T. The ECU calibration stores the exact crank angle of the first tooth after the gap; that angle differs between engines.

Speed comes from the period, not from counting pulses in a fixed window. A period measurement updates once per tooth and reacts quickly during acceleration. The ECU filters very short periods to reject noise and uses a timeout to detect a stopped engine.

Sensor types and signal shape

A variable-reluctance (VR) sensor, also called an inductive sensor, has a permanent magnet and a coil. A tooth passing the pole piece changes the magnetic flux and induces an alternating voltage. The amplitude rises with engine speed and falls with a larger air gap (the clearance between sensor tip and tooth). At cranking speed the signal may be only a few hundred millivolts, so the conditioning circuit uses a zero-crossing detector with hysteresis (two threshold levels). A VR sensor has two wires and needs no supply.

A Hall sensor has a Hall element, a magnet and a rotating vane or toothed target. It switches its output between low and high, giving a digital square wave. The amplitude is set by the supply and load, not by speed, so the ECU sees a usable signal down to zero speed. Hall CKP sensors are common on engines that must start reliably in cold weather. They have three wires: supply, ground and signal.

An optical sensor uses a light-emitting diode (LED) and a phototransistor across a slotted disc. It gives clean digital pulses but is sensitive to dirt and oil, so it is more common in test rigs and some distributor-based systems. A magnetoresistive (MR) sensor uses a thin film whose resistance changes with magnetic field direction. It gives a digital output with a large air gap and good low-speed performance, and appears in some crank and cam sensors.

Signal shape matters to the ECU. A VR output rises and falls as the tooth approaches and leaves the pole piece. Its voltage is proportional to the rate of change of flux, so it crosses zero when the tooth is centred on the pole piece and the flux is maximum. The ECU detects that zero crossing as the timing reference. The time between successive zero crossings is still one tooth pitch period T, so the speed formulas do not change. Hall, optical and MR outputs are already logic levels; their reference is the chosen signal edge. The stored calibration offset therefore differs between sensor types, and a VR sensor and a Hall sensor on the same wheel need different offsets.

Worked example

Question. A 60-2 crank wheel turns at 3000 rpm. Find the time between successive normal tooth edges and the duration of the missing-tooth gap measured from the last present tooth to the first present tooth after the gap.

  1. Convert speed: 3000 rpm ÷ 60 = 50 rev/s.
  2. Time for one revolution: 1 ÷ 50 = 0.020 s = 20 ms.
  3. A 60-2 wheel has 60 tooth pitches per revolution. Normal tooth period T = 20 ms ÷ 60 = 0.333333 ms ≈ 0.333 ms.
  4. The gap spans three pitch periods: the last present tooth, two empty slots, then the next present tooth. Gap = 3 × 0.333333 ms = 1.000 ms.

Answer. The normal tooth period is 0.333 ms and the missing-tooth gap is 1.0 ms.

Exam traps

  • For a 60-2 wheel the missing-tooth gap is 3T, not 2T. Two teeth are missing, but the interval from the last present tooth to the next present tooth covers three pitch periods.
  • Use the pitch count z (60 for 60-2, 36 for 36-1) in speed = 1/(zT). Do not use the number of present teeth (58 or 35) when you measure T between normal teeth.
  • VR amplitude changes with speed; a clean signal at 3000 rpm does not prove the sensor will work at 150 rpm cranking. Hall output amplitude does not depend on speed.
  • Angular position is an angle in degrees; angular speed is a rate in rpm. A timing sensor gives phase information, not necessarily a second independent speed signal.
  • Do not assume the first tooth after the gap is at TDC. Its offset is calibration data and varies between engines.

3.5 Hall effect, magnetic reluctance and optical sensors

Some sensors read position or rotation without touching the part. The Hall sensor uses the Lorentz force on charge carriers in a thin semiconductor plate to make a small voltage proportional to magnetic field. A variable-reluctance sensor makes an AC pulse from a moving toothed wheel. An optical sensor breaks a light beam with a vane. These three give the ECU crank position, wheel speed and pedal position without wear, and each has a different output and a different tolerance to dirt.

You need: crankshaft position sensors (toothed wheels and pulse timing) and Faraday's law and the force on a moving charge from physics.

Hall effect and Hall switches

A thin semiconductor plate carries current I. A magnetic field B at right angles pushes the moving carriers sideways with force q v × B. Charge collects on one edge until the electric field balances this force. The steady voltage across the plate is the Hall voltage. It is small, often millivolts, so an amplifier and a Schmitt trigger follow it.

VH = KH I B, KH = 1/(n q t) and VH = RH I B / t, RH = 1/(n q)

VH is the Hall voltage in V; I is the current in A; B is the flux density in T; n is the carrier density in m⁻³; q is the carrier charge in C; t is the plate thickness in m; KH is the Hall sensitivity (current-related sensitivity) in V/(A·T) or m²/C; RH is the Hall coefficient in m³/C.

A Hall switch compares VH with two thresholds. The output changes at the operate point and returns at the release point, so a small field wobble does not chatter. A Hall latch keeps its last state until the field reverses. Hall sensors are used for cam and crank position, wheel speed, pedal position and gear selection. Most are digital switches; linear Hall versions give an analog voltage that is used for pedal and throttle position. They need a supply; many work down to zero speed. Magnetoresistive sensors use a thin film whose resistance changes with field angle, giving a compact angle signal for some pedal and motor-position applications.

Variable-reluctance sensors

A variable-reluctance (VR) sensor has a coil wound on a magnet, facing a toothed iron wheel. As a tooth moves near the coil, the air gap and the magnetic reluctance fall; flux rises. As the tooth leaves, flux falls. The changing flux induces a voltage in the coil by Faraday's law. The sensor is passive: it needs no supply. It is also called a magnetic reluctance or inductive sensor.

e = -N dΦ/dt and f = z n / 60

e is the induced voltage in V; N is the number of turns; Φ is the magnetic flux in Wb; t is time in s; f is the pulse frequency in Hz; z is the number of teeth; n is the shaft speed in rev/min.

The output is an AC voltage. Its amplitude and frequency both rise with speed. At low speed the amplitude may be too small for the ECU, and at zero speed there is no output. Signal conditioning uses a threshold and often a zero-crossing detector to make a clean pulse. A VR sensor is robust and simple, and is common on older crank and ABS wheel-speed sensing. The air gap must be small and uniform; run-out or metal debris changes the pulse.

Optical sensors and comparison

An optical sensor uses an LED and a phototransistor. In the slotted type, a vane on the moving part passes through the gap and blocks the light; the phototransistor switches. In the reflective type, light returns from a mark. The output is digital and works down to zero speed. Dirt, oil, water and strong ambient light can block or false-trigger the beam, so the gap is kept small and shielded. Some texts call these optoelectronic sensors.

Optical crankshaft position sensing uses a disc with slots fixed to the crank. The ECU counts slot edges for speed and uses one wider index slot for absolute position. A slotted coupler gives one pulse per slot; the index pulse marks top dead centre for cylinder 1. This is non-contact and has no wear, but it is less tolerant of oil mist than a Hall or VR sensor.

All three are non-contact, so there is no rubbing wear.

Worked example

Question. A Hall plate carries I = 10 mA in a flux density B = 0.1 T. The plate thickness is t = 0.5 mm and carrier density is n = 1×10²² m⁻³. Take q = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C. Find VH in volts and in millivolts.

  2. KH = 1/(n q t) = 1/(1×10²² × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 0.0005) = 1/0.8 = 1.25 m²/C.
  3. VH = KH I B = 1.25 × 0.010 × 0.1 = 0.00125 V.
  4. In millivolts: 0.00125 × 1000 = 1.25 mV.

Answer. The Hall voltage is 0.00125 V, or 1.25 mV; an amplifier raises this to a logic level.

Worked example 2

Question. An ABS wheel-speed sensor reads a tone ring of 48 teeth. The tyre rolling radius is 0.30 m and the car runs at 72 km/h. Find the wheel speed in rpm, the sensor pulse frequency and the time between pulses.

  1. Vehicle speed: v = 72 ÷ 3.6 = 20.0 m/s.

  5. Time between pulses: 1 ÷ 509.3 = 1.96 ms.
  6. Check at walking pace, 3.6 km/h: everything scales by 1/20, so f = 25.5 Hz. A VR sensor's voltage also falls by about 20 times, which is why ABS moved to active (Hall or magnetoresistive) wheel sensors that work down to standstill.

Exam traps

  • A VR sensor is not a Hall sensor: its output is an AC voltage whose size depends on speed, so it needs a threshold and often a zero-crossing detector. A Hall sensor needs a supply; a Hall switch gives a digital signal and a linear Hall sensor an analog one.
  • Hall voltage is proportional to B, but a Hall switch has hysteresis: the operate and release fields differ.
  • Convert mA to A and mm to m before using VH = I B/(n q t); a factor of 1000 is the common slip.
  • Optical sensors fail with dirt, oil and sunlight; a missing pulse may be a blocked beam, not a bad ECU.
  • A passive VR sensor cannot report zero speed; no rotation means no flux change and no output.

3.6 Throttle position and temperature sensors

The throttle position sensor tells the engine control unit (ECU) how far the driver has pressed the accelerator. Temperature sensors tell the ECU how hot the engine coolant, intake air, battery and cabin are. These signals set fuel, ignition, idle speed and climate comfort. A throttle position sensor is a position sensor with a safety check; a temperature sensor is usually a negative temperature coefficient (NTC) thermistor in a voltage divider. Both reach the ECU through an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) and a calibration table. This chapter shows the circuits, the non-linearity traps and the redundancy checks that keep the readings trustworthy.

You need: sensor basics and signal conditioning for voltage dividers and ADC counts, and Hall, reluctance and optical sensors for contactless position sensing.

Throttle position sensor (TPS)

The throttle plate controls air into a petrol engine. The throttle position sensor (TPS) converts plate angle into a voltage. The ECU uses it for idle control, acceleration enrichment, gear shift decisions and cruise control. A simple TPS is a potentiometer: a resistive track fed with a stable 5 V reference, and a wiper driven by the throttle shaft. The wiper voltage rises with opening. Many units have two tracks with opposite slopes; the ECU checks that their sum stays constant, so a broken wiper or short is detected.

VTPS = V₀ + k θ

VTPS is the wiper voltage in V; V₀ is the voltage at closed throttle in V; k is the slope in V per % throttle; θ is throttle opening in %.

A contactless TPS uses a Hall element or an inductive coil. A magnet turns with the throttle shaft and the output changes without rubbing. It lasts longer and has no wiper noise. Some systems call it a throttle angle sensor. The accelerator pedal has two independent position sensors, often with different slopes or a fixed ratio. The ECU compares them: if they disagree by more than a small window, it limits engine power and sets a fault code.

  1. Read both tracks through separate ADC channels.
  2. Check 0.5 V to 4.5 V range at ignition on.
  3. Check track sum or ratio at each angle.
  4. If plausible, use a calibrated table for throttle %.
  5. If not, use a limp-home value and store a fault code.

Older systems add a closed-throttle idle switch and a wide-open-throttle (WOT) switch. The idle switch confirms 0% for idle speed control; the WOT switch signals maximum load for enrichment. A switch is a yes/no contact, not a measured angle.

Coolant and air temperature sensors

Engine coolant temperature (ECT) and intake air temperature (IAT) sensors are negative temperature coefficient (NTC) thermistors. Their resistance falls as temperature rises. A typical circuit puts the thermistor to ground and a fixed pull-up resistor to a clean 5 V reference. The junction voltage goes to the ADC. With this arrangement the voltage falls as temperature rises. The same 5 V usually feeds the ADC reference, so a slow drift in it cancels (the reading is ratiometric); a bad ground or a sensor fed from a different supply does not cancel.

Vout = Vref × RNTC / (Rpull + RNTC)

Vout is the divider voltage in V; Vref is the sensor supply in V; Rpull is the fixed resistor in Ω; RNTC is the thermistor resistance in Ω at the measured temperature.

The NTC curve is not a straight line. A two-point linear conversion is wrong in the middle. The ECU stores a table of resistance or voltage against temperature, or evaluates the Steinhart-Hart equation. The table has more points near cold starts, where fuel correction matters. IAT corrects air density; ECT corrects fuel and ignition during warm-up and controls the radiator fan. Battery thermal sensors use the same thermistor idea to protect charging and traction batteries.

1/T = A + B ln R + C (ln R)³

T is absolute temperature in K; R is thermistor resistance in Ω; A, B and C are constants for that thermistor.

A thermistor is a resistor, not a thermocouple. It needs a divider and an ADC. The pull-up resistor value sets the voltage range. Choose it so the ADC uses most of its range over the expected temperature span. Check the connector and ground: a high-resistance ground or connector adds an offset, so compare the divider voltage with the table before replacing the sensor.

Cabin, humidity and air quality

Climate control needs more than one temperature. A cabin temperature sensor with a small fan samples the air near the dash. An outside temperature sensor gives ambient. A sun-load sensor on the dash measures solar heating. A humidity sensor, often capacitive, measures water vapour in the cabin air. An air-quality sensor detects reducing gases or particles and selects recirculation. The climate ECU combines these signals with the target temperature and blower status to drive air blend doors and the compressor. The flap and blend-door motors are covered in solenoids, relays and stepper motors.

The same thermal sensing appears in the battery pack. Several NTC thermistors report module temperature to the battery management system (BMS). The BMS compares them and reduces charging power if one is hot. Humidity sensing also protects electronics: condensation can short connectors. Air-quality signals are slow and noisy, so the ECU filters them and uses hysteresis, a small dead band, before changing recirculation.

  4. Full scale is 1023, not 1024, because a 10-bit ADC gives counts 0 to 1023.

Question. An NTC coolant sensor has R = 2.2 kΩ at 25 °C and B = 3500 K. It sits from the ECU input to ground with a 2.2 kΩ pull-up to 5 V, read by a 10-bit ADC. Using R = R25 exp[B (1/T − 1/298.15)], find the resistance, the divider voltage and the ADC count at 90 °C and at −10 °C.

Exam traps

  • Do not treat an NTC thermistor as linear. A straight line through two temperatures gives large errors in between.
  • The ADC count uses full scale 1023 for 10 bits, not 1024. A 5 V reference gives count = round(1023 × V / 5).
  • TPS redundancy is not two identical signals. The tracks often have opposite slopes or a fixed ratio; the ECU checks agreement, not equality.
  • A bad sensor ground or 5 V reference shifts TPS and temperature readings together. Check supply and ground before replacing the sensor.
  • Idle and WOT switches are not analog angle sensors. They only confirm endpoints and cannot replace the TPS curve.

3.7 Exhaust gas oxygen (lambda) sensors

The exhaust gas oxygen sensor, also called the lambda sensor, measures oxygen left in the exhaust. The ECU uses it to hold the air-fuel ratio near stoichiometric, where a three-way catalyst works best, or near a target lambda. Petrol stoichiometric AFR is 14.7:1 by mass. The sensor closes the fuel loop: air charge sensors set a base fuel mass, and the EGO trims it. Narrowband sensors switch sharply near λ = 1; wideband sensors give a linear reading. Used on petrol, gas and some diesel engines.

You need: sensor basics and signal conditioning for signal conditioning, air flow and MAP sensors for air charge, and throttle position and temperature sensors for temperature effects.

Lambda and closed-loop fuel trim

Lambda λ is the actual air-fuel ratio divided by the stoichiometric air-fuel ratio for the fuel. For petrol, stoichiometric AFR is 14.7 kg air per kg fuel, written 14.7:1. λ = AFRactual / AFRstoich. λ less than 1 is rich (excess fuel), λ equal to 1 is stoichiometric, λ greater than 1 is lean (excess air). The same λ can apply to petrol, CNG or ethanol if the correct stoichiometric AFR is used.

λ = AFRactual / AFRstoich

AFRactual is the actual air-fuel mass ratio; AFRstoich is the stoichiometric ratio, 14.7:1 for petrol; λ is dimensionless.

The ECU runs closed-loop fuel trim. Air charge sensors give the base fuel mass. The EGO signal tells the ECU whether the last combustion was rich or lean. Short-term fuel trim multiplies the base injection time. Long-term trim learns the slow offset. Around λ = 1 the narrowband output changes fast, so the ECU can hold the average close to stoichiometric.

The sensor does not measure AFR directly. It measures oxygen partial pressure in exhaust. Rich exhaust has little oxygen because fuel consumes it. Lean exhaust has excess oxygen. The zirconia cell turns that oxygen difference into a voltage.

Narrowband zirconia EGO sensor

The narrowband zirconia sensor is an oxygen concentration cell. A thimble of yttria-stabilised zirconia separates exhaust gas from reference air. Porous platinum electrodes cover both sides. Above about 300 °C the ceramic conducts oxygen ions. The voltage follows the Nernst equation.

E = (R T / (4 F)) ln(pO2,ref / pO2,exh)

E is the cell voltage in volts; R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) is the gas constant; T is absolute temperature in K; F = 96485 C/mol is the Faraday constant; pO2,ref is the oxygen partial pressure in reference air; pO2,exh is the oxygen partial pressure in exhaust.

In rich exhaust the oxygen partial pressure on the exhaust side is low, so the cell produces about 0.8–0.9 V. In lean exhaust the two oxygen pressures are closer, so the output falls to about 0.1 V. The transition is steep near λ = 1. The ECU sees a switch, not a proportional AFR. This is why the device is called a switching or narrowband sensor. Some syllabi call it lambda sensor, EGO or HEGO (heated exhaust gas oxygen).

A heater inside the sensor brings it to light-off quickly. The ECU controls the heater with pulse-width modulation through a power switch (see solenoids, relays and stepper motors). Thimble sensors use a cup-shaped ceramic. Planar sensors stack ceramic layers and electrodes on a flat strip; they heat faster and are smaller. A protective shield and a porous layer protect the electrodes from liquid water and deposits.

Desirable properties: fast light-off, high sensitivity near λ = 1, low hysteresis, resistance to lead and silicone poisoning, and stable heater control. Hysteresis means the rich-to-lean and lean-to-rich switching voltages differ; low hysteresis gives a cleaner switch. Poisoning reduces the active electrode area and slows response.

Wideband UEGO and titania sensors

The wideband universal exhaust gas oxygen (UEGO) sensor gives a linear λ output. It uses a pump cell and a sensing cell. The ECU pumps oxygen into or out of a small chamber to hold the sensing cell at about 450 mV, which corresponds to λ = 1. The pump current needed is proportional to the oxygen excess or deficit in the exhaust. A conditioning circuit converts that current into a linear voltage for the ECU.

Because the pump current is linear, the ECU can read λ over a wide range, roughly from λ = 0.7 up to free air (λ tending to infinity). Petrol direct-injection and diesel engines use wideband sensors for lean operation and for closed-loop control away from stoichiometric. A wideband sensor is not a narrowband sensor with a different connector; it has extra cells and a controller.

Titania (TiO2) sensors use a different principle. The electrical resistance of titania changes with oxygen partial pressure. They need a heater because resistance is temperature-sensitive. The ECU measures the resistance as a voltage divider. Titania sensors are less common than zirconia but appear in some systems. Their output also switches near λ = 1 in the simple form.

All EGO sensors sit before or after the catalyst. The upstream sensor controls fuel. The downstream sensor checks catalyst oxygen storage. Catalyst chemistry and emission norms belong to electronic engine control.

  • Lead and silicone poisoning reduce response. Do not confuse poisoning with a rich mixture or a faulty heater.
  • Wideband pump current is not the same as the Nernst voltage. The sensing cell is held near 450 mV; the pump current carries the λ information.

3.8 Knock, acceleration and torque sensors

Knock, acceleration, torque and angular-rate sensors turn mechanical vibration and motion into electrical signals. The engine ECU uses a knock sensor to hear detonation and retard spark before damage. Airbag and ESP systems use accelerometers and gyroscopes to detect a crash or a skid. Torque sensors measure shaft twist; LIDAR measures distance by light time of flight; flex-fuel sensors measure ethanol content. These sensors protect the engine and stabilise the vehicle, so they sit in both engine control and safety systems.

You need: sensor basics and signal conditioning; Hall, reluctance and optical sensors; basic mechanics (F = m a, strain).

Knock sensor and signal path

Knock is the self-ignition of the unburnt end gas ahead of the flame front, after the spark. The sharp pressure waves it sets up ring the engine block at a frequency set by the combustion chamber, usually in the 5–15 kHz band. A piezoelectric knock sensor bolts to the block. Its crystal makes charge when the block vibrates. Charge sensitivity d is often given in pC/N. A charge amplifier converts this charge to a voltage. A band-pass filter then keeps only the knock band. A rectifier and an integrator (a low-pass smoothing stage) turn the burst into one number per firing. The ECU opens a window from the crank-angle signal in the combustion period, roughly 10° to 60° after top dead centre, and compares that number with a threshold learnt from the quiet cylinders. Above threshold, it retards the spark of that cylinder by a few degrees and then creeps it back. The window stops the ECU reacting to valve or injector noise at other crank angles.

q = d F, V = q / Cf

q is charge (C); d is charge sensitivity (C/N); F is knock force (N); V is output voltage (V); Cf is feedback capacitance (F).

Acceleration and angular-rate sensors

A MEMS accelerometer has a proof mass on silicon springs. Comb fingers on the mass form capacitors with fixed fingers. Acceleration moves the mass, so one capacitance rises and the other falls. The circuit reads the differential capacitance, which is proportional to acceleration. A high-g accelerometer in the airbag system detects a crash pulse. A low-g lateral accelerometer in ESP measures cornering. Some syllabi call the lateral accelerometer and yaw-rate gyro the ESP sensors. A MEMS gyroscope vibrates a proof mass and uses the Coriolis force to measure angular rate. The output is rate in °/s, not angle. The ECU can integrate rate to get angle, but bias and noise make the angle drift. Vehicle stability algorithms combine rate, lateral acceleration, wheel speeds and steering angle. The control law belongs to the electronic stability program in vehicle motion control.

Torque, distance and fuel composition

Torque sensors measure twist in a shaft. A strain-gauge bridge bonded to the shaft changes resistance with shear strain. A magnetoelastic shaft changes magnetic permeability when twisted; a coil reads the change. A surface-acoustic-wave (SAW) torque sensor uses a wave delay that changes with strain. These sensors help transmission control and electric power steering. A strain-gauge torque sensor on a steering column helps electric power steering apply assist in proportion to driver torque. LIDAR measures distance by time of flight. A light pulse leaves the sensor, reflects from an object and returns. The round-trip distance is c t, so d = c t / 2. Here d is distance (m), c is speed of light (3×10⁸ m/s), and t is round-trip time (s). A flex-fuel sensor estimates ethanol in petrol. Ethanol has a higher dielectric constant than petrol, so a capacitive sensor changes with fuel composition. The ECU then adjusts fuel quantity and spark timing. How driver assistance uses LIDAR belongs to vehicle motion control and safety.

Worked example

Question. A piezoelectric knock sensor has charge sensitivity d = 2 pC/N. A knock force of 50 N acts on it. The charge amplifier has feedback capacitor Cf = 1000 pF. Find the charge produced and the output voltage.

  1. Given d = 2 pC/N = 2×10⁻¹² C/N.
  2. Given F = 50 N.
  3. q = d F = 2×10⁻¹² C/N × 50 N = 1×10⁻¹⁰ C = 100 pC.
  4. Given Cf = 1000 pF = 1000×10⁻¹² F = 1×10⁻⁹ F.
  5. V = q / Cf = 1×10⁻¹⁰ C / 1×10⁻⁹ F = 0.10 V.

Answer. The charge is 100 pC and the charge-amplifier output is 0.10 V.

Worked example 2

Question. A car at 50 km/h hits a barrier and the passenger cell stops in 80 ms. (a) Find the average deceleration in m/s² and in g. 3.9 Actuators: solenoids, relays and stepper motors

An actuator is the hand of the electronic control unit (ECU). The ECU computes a small electrical command; an actuator turns that command into force, motion or switching. Solenoids push or pull a plunger, relays and contactors switch heavy currents, and stepper motors move in precise angular steps. Drivers supply the current, diodes absorb inductive kickback, and PWM controls average current. You meet these devices in fuel injectors, exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) valves, variable valve timing, idle-air control and electric motor actuators. Some syllabi call them output devices.

You need: sensor basics and signal conditioning for signal levels and noise, basic transistors, diodes and magnetic circuits, and the ignition coil driver from the ignition systems topic.

Solenoids: current, force and the freewheeling diode

A solenoid is a coil with a movable iron plunger. Current in the coil creates a magnetic field; the field pulls the plunger into the coil. The pull force for an ideal magnetic circuit is

F ≈ μ0 N² I² A / (2 g²)

F is force in newtons (N); μ0 = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m; N is coil turns; I is coil current in A; A is pole face area in m²; g is air gap in m. The force rises with I² and falls quickly as g grows.

At switch-on the air gap is large, so the solenoid needs a pull-in current. After the plunger moves, a smaller hold current keeps it seated. The ECU uses pulse-width modulation (PWM):

Iavg ≈ D Vb / Rc

Iavg is average coil current in A; D is duty cycle from 0 to 1; Vb is battery voltage in V; Rc is coil resistance in Ω.

A low-side driver switches the coil's ground side with a MOSFET (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor); a high-side driver switches the battery side. Both need a freewheeling diode across the coil. When the MOSFET turns off, coil current cannot stop instantly. The diode gives a path, so the stored energy ½ L I² is dissipated safely; L is coil inductance in henries and I is coil current in A. Without it, a high voltage spike can destroy the driver. Fuel injectors, EGR valves and variable valve timing units use this pattern. Ignition coil drivers use the same low-side switch, but deliberately without a freewheeling diode, because the inductive kick is what fires the spark; the switch is protected by other means. Spark generation belongs to ignition systems. A vacuum-operated actuator uses a small solenoid valve to switch vacuum to a diaphragm; the diaphragm then moves a flap or EGR valve.

Relays, contactors and H-bridge motor drive

A relay is an electromagnetic switch. The coil is a solenoid; its plunger closes or opens contacts. A small ECU current controls a much larger load current. The coil still needs a flyback diode, because the driver transistor sees the same inductive kick. A contactor is a heavy-duty relay for high current, often used for battery isolation or a powerful motor. Relay contacts are slow and wear; use a semiconductor driver when fast PWM is needed.

An H-bridge drives a DC motor in both directions. Four switches sit around the motor: two high-side and two low-side. Closing one high-side and the opposite low-side makes current flow one way; the other diagonal reverses it. Never close both switches on the same leg: that is shoot-through, a short circuit from battery to ground. Dead time between changes prevents it. PWM on the high-side switch sets average motor voltage Vavg = D Vb, so speed changes with duty cycle. H-bridges operate throttle actuators, EGR motor valves and other bidirectional motor actuators. A relay can reverse a motor only with extra contacts; an H-bridge does it electronically and quietly.

Stepper motors: steps, modes and limits

A stepper motor moves in fixed angular steps. The stator has several phase windings; the rotor is a permanent magnet or a soft-iron toothed rotor. The driver energises phases in sequence. Each change moves the rotor to the next stable position.

β = 360° / S

β is the step angle in degrees and S is the number of steps per revolution.

In full-step mode, one or two phases are on at a time; a 1.8° motor has 200 steps per revolution. Half-step mode alternates one-phase and two-phase states, giving 0.9°. Microstep mode applies sine and cosine currents to two phases. The rotor then settles between full-step positions; a driver may use 1/8, 1/16 or finer microsteps. Microstepping gives smoother motion and less vibration, but it does not increase holding torque. Holding torque is the torque needed to move the rotor from its energised position.

Stepper motors are open-loop. The ECU sends pulses and assumes the rotor follows. If load torque is too high, or acceleration is too fast, the rotor slips and loses steps. There is no position feedback unless a sensor is added. Torque falls as speed rises because coil inductance limits how fast current can build; the driver needs a higher voltage to force current into the coil quickly. Pull-out torque is the maximum load torque at a given speed. Stepper actuators appear in idle-air control, headlamp levelling and some valve or flap positions.

Question. A stepper motor has a step angle of 1.8°. The ECU sends 400 steps in 0.8 s. Find the total angle turned, the number of revolutions and the average speed in rev/s and rpm.

  • Microstepping gives smoother motion, not more holding torque. The torque increment gained per microstep is smaller than for a full step; the motor's maximum holding torque stays the same.

3.10 Fluid-mechanical actuators

A fluid-mechanical actuator uses a fluid under pressure, oil (hydraulic) or air and vacuum (pneumatic), to make a force or a movement. The ECU does not push the load itself: it drives a small solenoid valve that lets pressure into a cylinder or onto a diaphragm, and the fluid does the heavy work. This is how a turbo wastegate opens, an automatic gearbox engages a clutch, an ABS unit lowers brake pressure and a cam phaser shifts valve timing. The idea in one line: force = pressure × area.

You need: solenoids, relays and stepper motors for the solenoid valve and its PWM drive, and pressure in pascals (1 bar = 100 kPa).

Pressure, area and the three numbers of a fluid actuator

Every fluid actuator turns fluid energy into mechanical work with the same three relations. A pressure difference Δp acting on a piston or diaphragm of area A gives a force; a flow Q filling a cylinder of area A gives a speed; and pressure times flow is the hydraulic power.

F = Δp × A, v = Q ÷ A, P = Δp × Q

F is force in N; Δp is the pressure difference across the piston in Pa; A is the effective area in m²; v is piston speed in m/s; Q is volume flow in m³/s; P is power in W.

The pressure source differs. Hydraulic actuators use oil from an engine-driven or electric pump at 2 to 20 bar (transmission, cam phaser) or much higher (brake systems, diesel common rail). Pneumatic actuators use intake vacuum, boost pressure or a small compressor. Oil hardly compresses, so a hydraulic actuator is stiff and holds position under load. Air compresses, so a pneumatic actuator is soft and is usually balanced against a spring: the position is where the pressure force equals the spring force.

Electrically controlled valves: switching and proportional

The interface between the ECU and the fluid is a valve worked by a solenoid. Some syllabi call these electrohydraulic or electropneumatic converters.

  • Switching (on-off) valve: open or shut. A 2/2 valve has two ports and two positions; a 3/2 valve connects the actuator either to pressure or to vent. ABS inlet and outlet valves are switching valves.
  • PWM-controlled valve: a switching valve pulsed at 20 to 300 Hz. The duty cycle sets the average opening, so the actuator sees a mean pressure between supply and vent. A wastegate or EGR vacuum modulator works this way.
  • Proportional valve: the solenoid current sets the spool position or the output pressure smoothly. The ECU controls the current (not just the duty) so that coil warming, which raises its resistance, does not change the pressure. Automatic gearbox pressure-control solenoids and cam-phaser oil-control valves are proportional.

Because the valve only meters a small flow, a 1 A solenoid can command kilonewtons of clamping force. The penalty is response time: the fluid must flow through restrictions before the pressure builds, so a fluid actuator takes tens of milliseconds where an electric motor actuator takes a few.

A diaphragm actuator has a rubber diaphragm in a pressed-steel can with a return spring. With boost on one side the rod starts to move when Δp × A reaches the spring preload, and then moves further as pressure rises, by (Δp × A − preload) ÷ spring rate. The ABS modulator works in three steps per wheel: hold (inlet closed), drop (outlet open, fluid to the pump) and rise (inlet open), cycling a few times a second.

Worked example

Question. A turbo wastegate actuator has a diaphragm of effective diameter 60 mm, a return spring preloaded to 90 N and a spring rate of 15 N/mm. Boost acts on the diaphragm and the other side is at atmosphere. (a) At what boost does the wastegate start to open? (b) How far has the rod moved at 60 kPa boost? (c) What boost gives the full 8 mm stroke?

  1. Effective area: A = π d² ÷ 4 = π × 0.060² ÷ 4 = 2.827 × 10⁻³ m².
  2. (a) Opening starts when Δp × A = preload: Δp = 90 ÷ 2.827 × 10⁻³ = 31 830 Pa ≈ 31.8 kPa (0.32 bar) boost.
  3. (b) At 60 kPa: F = 60 000 × 2.827 × 10⁻³ = 169.6 N. Spring travel beyond preload: x = (169.6 − 90) ÷ 15 = 5.31 mm.
  4. (c) Full stroke needs F = 90 + 15 × 8 = 210 N, so Δp = 210 ÷ 2.827 × 10⁻³ = 74 270 Pa ≈ 74.3 kPa.
  5. The ECU raises the boost above 31.8 kPa by venting the actuator through a PWM valve: the diaphragm then sees only part of the boost, so the wastegate stays shut longer.
  6. Check: at 40 kPa the force is 113.1 N and the travel (113.1 − 90) ÷ 15 = 1.54 mm, between the shut and the 60 kPa positions, as it should be.

Answer. The wastegate cracks open at 31.8 kPa boost, is 5.31 mm open at 60 kPa, and reaches its 8 mm stroke at 74.3 kPa.

Worked example 2

Question. An automatic-gearbox clutch has an annular piston of outer diameter 120 mm and inner diameter 80 mm. The pressure-control valve applies 10 bar. Find the clamp force. The piston must travel 2 mm to take up the clearance; if the valve delivers 0.5 L/s, how long does the fill take, and what hydraulic power flows during it at 10 bar?

  1. Annular area: A = π (D² − d²) ÷ 4 = π × (0.120² − 0.080²) ÷ 4 = π × 0.0080 ÷ 4 = 6.283 × 10⁻³ m².
  2. Pressure: 10 bar = 10 × 10⁵ Pa = 1.0 × 10⁶ Pa.
  3. Clamp force: F = Δp × A = 1.0 × 10⁶ × 6.283 × 10⁻³ = 6283 N, about 6.3 kN.
  4. Fill volume: V = A × stroke = 6.283 × 10⁻³ × 0.002 = 1.257 × 10⁻⁵ m³ = 12.6 cm³.
  5. Fill time: t = V ÷ Q = 1.257 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ 0.5 × 10⁻³ = 0.025 s = 25 ms.
  6. Hydraulic power: P = Δp × Q = 1.0 × 10⁶ × 0.5 × 10⁻³ = 500 W (the fill really runs at a lower pressure, so it takes less).
  7. Check: a 1 A solenoid commands 6.3 kN here because the valve only meters the flow; the pump supplies the energy.

Answer. Clamp force 6.28 kN; fill volume 12.6 cm³, filled in 25 ms; 500 W of hydraulic power at 10 bar and 0.5 L/s.

Exam traps

  • Use the pressure difference across the piston, and in pascals: 1 bar = 10⁵ Pa and 1 kPa = 1000 Pa. Boost read as 0.6 bar is 60 000 Pa, not 0.6 Pa.
  • For an annular (ring) piston the area is π(D² − d²) ÷ 4, not π(D − d)² ÷ 4.
  • A diaphragm actuator does not move until the pressure force exceeds the spring preload; below that it stays shut.
  • A PWM valve does not give a pressure proportional to duty in a straight line; the ECU uses a map or closes the loop with a pressure sensor.
  • Hydraulic and pneumatic are not interchangeable words: oil hardly compresses (stiff, holds position), air compresses (soft, needs a spring to set position).

3.11 Electrical machines in vehicles and duty-type ratings

A modern car carries from about twenty to over a hundred electric motors: the starter, wipers, window lifts, seat adjusters, blower and radiator fans, fuel and water pumps, and in hybrid and electric cars the traction motor. Most are direct-current (DC) machines; the alternator and the traction motors are three-phase machines. Each motor is sized for how it is used: a starter runs for seconds, a radiator fan for hours. That pattern of use is its duty type, and the rating on its nameplate is only valid for that duty.

You need: the force on a current in a magnetic field and Faraday's law from physics, the alternator and starter motor from the starting and charging topic, and solenoids, relays and stepper motors for the H-bridge.

Direct-current machines

A DC motor has a stationary field (permanent magnets or a field winding) and a rotating armature whose coils are fed through brushes and a commutator. The commutator reverses each coil's current as it passes the poles, so the torque always acts the same way. As the armature turns it also works as a generator and makes a back emf E that opposes the supply.

E = kΦ ω, V = E + I Ra, T = kΦ I

E is back emf in V; kΦ is the machine constant in V·s/rad (equal to N·m/A); ω is speed in rad/s; V is the supply voltage; I is armature current in A; Ra is armature resistance in Ω; T is torque in N·m.

Put together, ω = (V − I Ra) ÷ kΦ: a permanent-magnet motor runs fastest with no load, and its speed falls in a straight line as torque (and current) rise, down to zero at the stall current V ÷ Ra. Output power is T ω. The connection of the field gives four types:

  • Permanent magnet: wipers, window lifts, seats, blowers, fuel pumps; small, cheap and efficient. Speed is set by the supply voltage (PWM or series resistors).
  • Series wound: field in series with the armature, so the field grows with current and the starting torque is very high: the classic starter motor.
  • Shunt wound (field in parallel, near-constant speed) and compound (both windings, some heavy-vehicle starters).
  • Electronically commutated (brushless DC): the magnets turn and an electronic three-phase bridge, timed from Hall sensors, replaces the brushes; long life for fuel pumps, coolant pumps and fans.

Three-phase and single-phase AC machines

A three-phase machine has three stator windings 120° apart. Fed with three-phase current they make a magnetic field that rotates at the synchronous speed.

ns = 120 f ÷ p, s = (ns − n) ÷ ns

ns is synchronous speed in rpm; f is the supply frequency in Hz; p is the number of poles; s is the slip of an induction machine; n is the rotor speed in rpm.

  • Synchronous machine: the rotor carries magnets or a DC field and turns exactly at ns. The vehicle alternator is a claw-pole synchronous generator whose three-phase output a diode bridge rectifies; most hybrid and electric cars use a permanent-magnet synchronous motor (PMSM) for traction.
  • Induction (asynchronous) machine: the rotor has bars shorted by end rings; currents are induced only when it slips behind the field, so it runs a few percent below ns. Rugged and magnet-free.
  • Traction machines are fed from the battery through an inverter that makes three-phase current of variable frequency, so f, and with it ns, sets the speed.

Single-phase AC machines (universal motors, capacitor-start and shaded-pole induction motors) run from mains AC. They appear in vehicles only behind an inverter or in workshop equipment. A single-phase field pulsates rather than rotates, so these motors need a starting aid.

Duty-type ratings for electrical machines

A motor's limit is heat. Its windings warm while it runs and cool while it rests, so the same motor can deliver more power for a short run than for a continuous one. The duty type (IEC 60034-1 names ten, S1 to S10) says which pattern the rating assumes. For periodic duties the cyclic duration factor is the on-time as a share of the cycle (the cycle is 10 minutes unless stated).

CDF = ton ÷ (ton + toff) × 100 %

ton is the time under load and toff the rest time in one cycle, in the same units.

Worked example

Question. A 12 V permanent-magnet blower motor has armature resistance Ra = 0.2 Ω and machine constant kΦ = 0.02 V·s/rad. Ignore friction. Find (a) the no-load speed, (b) the speed, torque, output power and efficiency at 10 A, and (c) the stall current and stall torque.

  1. (a) No load, I ≈ 0: E = V = 12 V, so ω = E ÷ kΦ = 12 ÷ 0.02 = 600 rad/s = 600 × 60 ÷ 2π = 5730 rpm.
  2. (b) At 10 A: E = V − I Ra = 12 − 10 × 0.2 = 10 V, so ω = 10 ÷ 0.02 = 500 rad/s = 4775 rpm.
  3. Torque: T = kΦ I = 0.02 × 10 = 0.20 N·m.
  4. Output power: T ω = 0.20 × 500 = 100 W. Input power: V I = 12 × 10 = 120 W. Efficiency: 100 ÷ 120 = 83.3 % (the 20 W lost is I²Ra = 10² × 0.2).
  5. (c) Stall, ω = 0 so E = 0: I = V ÷ Ra = 12 ÷ 0.2 = 60 A; T = 0.02 × 60 = 1.20 N·m.
  6. Check: the speed falls in a straight line from 600 rad/s at 0 N·m to 0 at 1.20 N·m; at 0.20 N·m that line gives 600 × (1 − 0.20 ÷ 1.20) = 500 rad/s, as found.

Answer. No load 5730 rpm; at 10 A: 4775 rpm, 0.20 N·m, 100 W out, 83.3 % efficient; stall 60 A and 1.20 N·m.

Worked example 2

Question. In a test, a window-lift motor runs for 4 s and rests for 12 s, over and over. (a) Find the cyclic duration factor and name the duty type. (b) A traction PMSM has 8 poles and its inverter supplies 400 Hz. Find its speed. (c) An induction traction motor has 4 poles, is fed at 200 Hz and runs with 3 % slip. Find its speed.

  1. (a) CDF = 4 ÷ (4 + 12) × 100 % = 25 %. Identical on–off cycles with negligible starting heat: S3 25 %.
  2. (b) A synchronous motor turns at ns = 120 f ÷ p = 120 × 400 ÷ 8 = 6000 rpm.
  3. (c) ns = 120 × 200 ÷ 4 = 6000 rpm. Rotor speed n = ns (1 − s) = 6000 × (1 − 0.03) = 5820 rpm.
  4. Check: only the synchronous motor turns at the 6000 rpm field speed; the induction motor needs its 180 rpm of slip.

Answer. S3 with a CDF of 25 %; the PMSM turns at 6000 rpm and the induction motor at 5820 rpm.

Exam traps

  • A DC motor's current is not V ÷ Ra while it runs: the back emf subtracts, I = (V − E) ÷ Ra. V ÷ Ra is the stall (starting) current.
  • Use ω in rad/s in E = kΦω and P = Tω; rpm × 2π ÷ 60 converts. Mixing rpm into P = Tω gives an answer about ten times too large.
  • ns = 120 f ÷ p uses the number of poles, not pole pairs; with pole pairs it is 60 f ÷ (pairs).
  • A rating means nothing without its duty type: a 300 W S2 10 min motor is not a 300 W continuous motor.
  • The alternator is a three-phase synchronous machine; its DC output comes from the diode rectifier, not from a commutator.

3.12 Battery current, voltage and state-of-charge sensing

Battery current and voltage sensing measure the electrical flow into and out of a battery pack. They let the battery management system (BMS) estimate state of charge (SOC), state of health (SOH) and state of function (SOF). Electric vehicles, hybrid cars and stop-start systems use these estimates to protect cells, report range and control charging. Only a few universities teach this chapter. A shunt resistor or Hall sensor measures current; a resistor divider feeding an isolated amplifier measures voltage; software combines both with temperature.

You need: sensor basics and signal conditioning signal conditioning; throttle position and temperature sensors for thermistors; battery ratings and charging from the batteries and charging topic.

Current sensing with shunt and Hall sensors

Current is the rate of charge flow. One ampere is one coulomb per second. A battery pack can carry hundreds of amperes. A shunt resistor is a low-value resistor placed in series with the pack. The shunt voltage is Vshunt = I × Rshunt, where Vshunt is the voltage in volts, I is the current in amperes and Rshunt is the shunt resistance in ohms. A 0.1 mΩ shunt at 100 A gives 10 mV. The amplifier raises this small signal before the ADC. A Kelvin connection uses separate force and sense terminals so lead resistance does not add to the measured voltage. Shunt resistance changes with temperature, so the BMS compensates using a temperature sensor. A Hall current sensor measures the magnetic field produced by the current. It gives isolation and no insertion loss, which suits high-voltage packs. Its offset and low-current accuracy are weaker. The BMS must also record current direction: discharge is one sign, regenerative braking current is the opposite sign.

Voltage measurement and cell modules

Each cell or module needs a voltage reading. A resistor divider scales a pack voltage down to the ADC range: Vadc = Vpack × R2 / (R1 + R2), where Vadc is the ADC input voltage, Vpack is the pack voltage and R1, R2 are the divider resistors. The divider must have high resistance to avoid draining the pack, but high resistance makes it sensitive to ADC input current. An isolated amplifier or isolated ADC keeps the low-voltage electronics safe from the high-voltage pack. For an n-bit ADC with reference Vref, one count is Vref / (2n − 1), the same rule as in the other sensor chapters. A 12-bit ADC with a 5 V reference has a step of 5 ÷ 4095 = 1.22 mV. Battery modules often carry a small cell-voltage monitor that sends readings to the BMS. Temperature sensing uses thermistors, as in throttle position and temperature sensors. The BMS combines cell voltage, pack current and temperature before it estimates SOC, SOH and SOF.

SOC, SOH, SOF and coulomb counting

State of charge (SOC) is the remaining charge as a fraction of rated capacity. State of health (SOH) describes ageing, usually capacity fade and resistance rise. State of function (SOF) is the power the pack can deliver or accept now; it depends on SOC, temperature and SOH, because an aged cell has a higher resistance. Coulomb counting integrates current: SOC(t) = SOC(0) − (1/Cn) ∫ I dt, where SOC(t) is the present fraction, SOC(0) is the starting fraction, Cn is the rated capacity in ampere-hours, I is current in amperes with discharge positive, and t is time in hours. In discrete form, ΔSOC = −I × Δt / Cn, so a discharge (I positive) lowers SOC. The estimate drifts because a small current offset or gain error is integrated hour after hour; a stale Cn after capacity fade is a scale error on top of that. The BMS resets it with open-circuit voltage (OCV) after the pack has rested, when cell voltage reflects SOC. OCV is flat in the middle SOC range, so the reset is strongest near empty or full. Current, voltage and thermal sensing together support SOC, SOH and SOF.

Worked example

Question. A 50 Ah battery starts at SOC 80%. It discharges at 10 A for 30 min. Find the charge removed and the new SOC by coulomb counting.

  1. Time Δt = 30 min = 0.5 h.
  2. Charge removed ΔQ = I × Δt = 10 A × 0.5 h = 5 Ah.
  3. SOC drop = ΔQ / Cn = 5 Ah / 50 Ah = 0.10 = 10%.
  4. New SOC = 80% − 10% = 70%.

Answer. 5 Ah is removed and the new SOC is 70%.

Exam traps

  • Coulomb counting drifts because a current offset is integrated; a stale capacity value adds a scale error. Reset with OCV after rest and relearn Cn.
  • A shunt needs a Kelvin connection and temperature compensation, or lead and shunt resistance errors enter the reading.
  • Regenerative braking reverses current direction; a wrong sign makes SOC rise during discharge.
  • SOC, SOH and SOF are different: SOC is charge now, SOH is ageing, SOF is power capability.

Takeaways

  1. Every sensor and actuator meets a microcontroller: its ADC, capture timers and PWM outputs set how finely and how fast the ECU can measure and act.
  2. Automotive electronics must survive −40 °C to 125 °C and more, a sagging and spiky supply, vibration and interference; MOSFET drivers and smart switches are built for this.
  3. Signal conditioning (bridge, amplifier, filter, divider) and the ADC decide the resolution you can use; one 10-bit count on 5 V is 4.89 mV.
  4. Air charge is measured directly by a mass air flow meter or estimated from MAP by speed density; the choice changes the fuel calculation.
  5. Crankshaft sensing gives both angle and speed; the missing-tooth gap (3T on a 60-2 wheel) fixes the reference.
  6. Hall and optical sensors give a signal down to zero speed; a variable-reluctance sensor gives an AC voltage that grows with speed and vanishes at standstill.
  7. The oxygen sensor closes the fuel loop: it measures the result of combustion, and the ECU trims injection from it.
  8. Knock, acceleration and rate sensors work on vibration and motion and feed protection and stability functions, not fuel metering.
  9. Electromechanical actuators (solenoids, relays, steppers) are switched coils; fluid-mechanical actuators let a small valve command a large force, F = Δp × A.
  10. Electric motors are chosen by type (DC, brushless, three-phase) and by duty type; battery current and voltage sensing turn the battery into SOC, SOH and SOF.

Points to cover. Model answer.

  1. Demands. An ECU must work from −40 °C to 125 °C or more near the engine, on a 12 V supply that varies between about 9 V and 16 V, sags while cranking and carries spikes and load-dump transients; it must survive vibration, humidity and salt, meet electromagnetic compatibility (neither emit nor suffer interference), last 15 years or more, check itself where safety depends on it, and be cheap in millions.
  2. Semiconductor basics. Silicon is doped with phosphorus (n-type, free electrons) or boron (p-type, holes). Where they meet a pn junction forms: it conducts forward above about 0.6 to 0.7 V and blocks reverse current. Diodes, transistors and integrated circuits are built from junctions.
  3. Components and their ECU jobs:
    ComponentJob in the ECU
    Rectifier diodeReverse-polarity protection; alternator bridge
    Freewheeling diodePath for coil current at switch-off
    Zener / TVS diodeClamps spikes on supply and sensor lines
    Bipolar transistorSmall-signal stages; IC = β IB
    MOSFETInjector, lamp, valve and motor drivers; loss I² RDS(on)
    IGBTIgnition coil and high-voltage switching
    ICsMicrocontroller, voltage regulator, CAN transceiver, smart high-side switch
  4. Block diagram of the ECU and its microcontroller:
    sensors → input filter/protection → [ADC | capture timer]
                                   ↓
          [CPU] ↔ [flash: program, maps] [RAM] [EEPROM]
                                   ↓
          [PWM / compare outputs] → MOSFET drivers → injectors, coils, valves
          [CAN / LIN] ↔ other ECUs        [watchdog] resets the MCU if the program stalls
  5. Each block: the CPU executes the program; flash keeps the program and calibration maps; RAM holds working values; EEPROM keeps learnt values and fault codes after switch-off; the ADC converts sensor voltages (10 or 12 bits); capture timers time-stamp crank-tooth edges; compare and PWM outputs fire injectors and coils at set angles; CAN and LIN carry data to other ECUs; the watchdog resets a stalled program.
  6. Data processing: the program reads the inputs, checks each for plausibility (range, rate of change, agreement of redundant sensors), looks up maps over speed and load with linear interpolation, computes injection time and spark angle, drives the outputs and stores fault codes. Fast events (crank teeth) arrive as interrupts; slow ones (coolant) are read every 10 to 100 ms.
  7. Interpolation example: a map holds 20° at 2000 rpm and 26° at 3000 rpm, so at 2500 rpm the ECU uses 20 + 6 × 500 ÷ 1000 = 23°.
  8. Timing check: at 6000 rpm a 60-2 wheel gives a tooth every 1 ÷ (100 × 60) s = 166.7 µs, which is 13 333 clock cycles of an 80 MHz MCU; a 1500-cycle tooth routine uses 11 % of the CPU, leaving time for the other tasks.

Definition: physical quantity to electrical signal; Classification: position, speed, pressure, flow, temperature, gas concentration, torque, vibration; Active needs excitation; passive self-generating; Contact wears; non-contact (Hall, optical, inductive) does not; Conditioning: protection, excitation, bridge, amplify, filter (low-pass anti-alias), scale to ADC range; ADC resolution example: 10-bit on 5 V reference gives about 4.9 mV per step.

Model answer.

  1. Definition: a sensor converts a physical quantity (temperature, pressure, position, speed, gas concentration) into an electrical signal the ECU can read. The transducer is the element that does the conversion.
  2. Classification by measured quantity:
    QuantitySensor
    Position, anglePotentiometer, Hall, inductive
    SpeedVariable reluctance, Hall
    PressurePiezoresistive MEMS, capacitive
    Air flowHot wire or film, Karman vortex

Understand Describe the construction and working of a hot-wire mass air flow sensor and a piezoresistive MAP sensor. State the advantages and limits of each. 13 marks

Points to cover. Hot wire: heated element in intake, constant-temperature bridge, current is the flow signal; Hot film and compensation element for air temperature; Needs burn-off cycle after shut-down to clean the wire; MAP: silicon diaphragm, diffused piezoresistive gauges, Wheatstone bridge, absolute pressure; MAP used in speed-density fuel calculation with engine speed and intake air temperature; Limits: MAF sensitive to dirt and backflow pulsation; MAP needs a barometric reference for altitude.

Model answer.

  1. Hot-wire construction: a thin platinum wire (or a film on a ceramic chip in the hot-film type) sits in the intake air stream with a second, unheated element that measures air temperature. Both are arms of a Wheatstone bridge.
  2. Working: the bridge amplifier drives just enough current through the heated element to keep it a fixed temperature above the air. More air mass carries away more heat, so more current is needed; the bridge current (as a voltage across a measuring resistor) is the mass-flow signal. Because heat transfer depends on mass, not volume, no density correction is needed.
  3. Burn-off: after the engine stops, the ECU heats the wire to about 1000 °C for about a second to burn off deposits that would change its heat transfer and calibration.
  4. Advantages: direct mass flow, fast response, no moving parts, low pressure loss, automatic altitude compensation. Limits: dirt and oil on the element shift calibration; pulsating and reverse flow at low speed and wide throttle can be over-read unless the sensor detects flow direction; leaks after the meter are not measured.
  5. MAP construction: a silicon diaphragm with a sealed vacuum on one side and manifold pressure on the other; four piezoresistive resistors diffused into it form a Wheatstone bridge, and the amplifier and temperature compensation are on the same chip.
  6. Working: manifold pressure bends the diaphragm, the strained resistors change value, and the bridge gives a voltage proportional to absolute pressure, for example V = 0.05 p − 0.5 (0.5 V at 20 kPa to 4.5 V at 100 kPa); 60 kPa reads 2.5 V.
  7. Speed-density use, with the chapter's numbers (1.6 L, 3000 rpm, 60 kPa, 300 K, ηv = 0.90):
    ρ = p ÷ (R T) = 60 000 ÷ (287 × 300) = 0.6969 kg/m³
mass per cycle = ηv ρ Vd = 0.90 × 0.6969 × 0.0016 = 1.0035 × 10⁻³ kg
cycles per second = 3000 ÷ 60 ÷ 2 = 25
ṁ = 1.0035 × 10⁻³ × 25 = 0.0251 kg/s = 25.1 g/s
  8. MAP advantages: cheap, robust, quick to respond to throttle changes, also gives barometric pressure at key-on. Limits: depends on a stored volumetric-efficiency table and on correct intake temperature; it cannot see engine wear or cam changes, and needs the barometric reading for altitude.
  9. Comparison: MAF measures what enters; MAP infers it. Many ECUs use MAF for steady running and MAP for transients and diagnosis.
Understand Explain how the crankshaft angular position and engine speed are sensed, and how the ECU uses the reference mark for ignition and fuel timing. 13 marks

Points to cover. Target wheel on crank pulley or flywheel, tooth count sets angular resolution; Missing tooth or extra tooth gives a unique reference pulse at a known angle before top dead centre; Speed from pulse frequency, angle from pulse count since reference; Variable reluctance gives an AC signal; Hall gives a square wave; ECU calculates ignition advance and injection start angle from angle and speed; Also drives the tachometer and the stall detection.

Model answer.

  1. The target: a steel wheel on the crank pulley or flywheel with teeth on a fixed pitch, for example 60-2 (60 pitches of 6°, two teeth removed) or 36-1 (36 pitches of 10°, one removed). The tooth pitch sets the basic angular resolution; the ECU interpolates between teeth in time.
  2. The sensor: a variable-reluctance (inductive) sensor of magnet and coil gives an AC voltage whose amplitude rises with speed and whose zero crossing marks a tooth centre; a Hall or magnetoresistive sensor gives a square wave of fixed height that works down to zero speed.
  3. Speed: from the time T between normal tooth edges, n = 1 ÷ (z T) rev/s with z the number of pitches. At 3000 rpm on a 60-2 wheel T = 20 ms ÷ 60 = 0.333 ms.
  4. Reference mark: the missing teeth give one interval much longer than the rest, 3T on a 60-2 wheel (1.0 ms at 3000 rpm) and 2T on a 36-1 wheel. The ECU compares each interval with the previous one; a ratio near 3 (or 2) marks the gap. It then resets its tooth counter and gives the next tooth its stored angle, a calibrated number of degrees before top dead centre of cylinder 1.
  5. Phase: a crank wheel turns twice per four-stroke cycle, so the same gap appears at TDC compression and TDC exhaust. The camshaft sensor (one pulse per two crank turns) tells the ECU which, enabling sequential injection and coil-on-plug ignition.
  6. Ignition timing: the ECU looks up the advance (say 26° BTDC) from its speed-load map, counts teeth from the reference to the tooth just before that angle, and sets a timer for the remaining fraction of a pitch, using the latest tooth period to convert angle into time.
  7. Fuel timing: the start of injection is placed the same way at a crank angle before the intake valve opens; the pulse width comes from the air charge.
  8. Other uses: the tachometer, idle-speed control, the rev limiter, misfire detection from tooth-period changes, and stall detection when no tooth arrives within a time-out.
  9. Check of the numbers: on a 60-2 wheel at 3000 rpm, 57 normal intervals of 0.333 ms plus one gap of 1.0 ms make 19.0 + 1.0 = 20.0 ms, exactly one revolution.

Points to cover. Model answer.

  1. Throttle potentiometer: three wires (5 V reference, ground, wiper). The track lies across the reference; the wiper, turned by the throttle shaft, picks off a voltage proportional to angle: V = V0 + k θ.
  2. Numbers: 0.5 V closed and 4.5 V wide open give k = 4.0 ÷ 100 = 0.04 V per %. At 30 % the output is 0.5 + 1.2 = 1.70 V, count round(1.70 × 1023 ÷ 5) = 348.

    Vout = 5 × RNTC ÷ (Rpull + RNTC)

Understand Explain the working of the exhaust gas oxygen sensor and how it closes the fuel loop. Compare narrowband and wideband types. 13 marks

Points to cover. Zirconia electrolyte conducts oxygen ions above about 300 degrees Celsius; Nernst voltage switches sharply around lambda = 1; heater brings it up to temperature fast; Narrowband: switch output about 0.1 V lean to 0.9 V rich; Wideband: pumping cell with a reference, current proportional to lambda over a wide range; ECU short-term and long-term fuel trim from the sensor signal; Closed loop only after the sensor is hot; open loop at start and full load.

Model answer.

  1. Purpose: hold the mixture at λ = 1, where the three-way catalyst converts CO, HC and NOx together. λ = AFRactual ÷ AFRstoich, with 14.7 for petrol.
  2. Construction of the narrowband sensor: a thimble (or planar strip) of yttria-stabilised zirconia, porous platinum electrodes on both faces, exhaust outside, reference air inside, a protective slotted shield and an electric heater.
  3. Working: above about 300 °C zirconia conducts oxygen ions. The oxygen difference across it gives the Nernst voltage E = (RT ÷ 4F) ln(pO₂ ref ÷ pO₂ exh).

    lean, 1 % O₂: E = 0.02312 × ln(0.21 ÷ 0.01) = 0.070 V

Short-term trim ramps the injection time down while the sensor reads rich and up while it reads lean, so the mixture oscillates closely about λ = 1; long-term trim learns any steady offset. Example: actual AFR 15.4 gives λ = 15.4 ÷ 14.7 = 1.048, lean, about 0.1 V, so the ECU adds fuel.

Understand Describe a piezoelectric knock sensor and an angular-rate sensor, and state how the ECU uses each signal. 12 marks

Points to cover. Knock: detonation gives block vibration in a characteristic frequency band; Piezoelectric ceramic produces charge proportional to acceleration; charge amplifier converts it; ECU windows the signal after each spark and compares with a threshold; Action: retard spark on the knocking cylinder, then gradually restore advance; Angular rate: vibrating element or MEMS gyroscope giving yaw rate voltage; Used with wheel speed and steering angle for stability control.

Model answer.

  1. Knock: self-ignition of the unburnt end gas ahead of the flame front after the spark. The pressure waves ring the cylinder at a frequency set by the bore and gas temperature, typically 5 to 15 kHz, and repeated knock damages pistons and rings.
  2. Construction: a piezoelectric ceramic ring between a seismic mass and the base, bolted to the block with a set torque. Block vibration presses the mass on the ceramic, which produces charge q = d F.
  3. Signal chain with numbers:
    q = d F = 2 pC/N × 50 N = 100 pC
charge amplifier: V = q ÷ Cf = 100 pC ÷ 1000 pF = 0.10 V
→ band-pass 5–15 kHz → rectify and integrate → compare with threshold
  4. Crank-angle window: the ECU evaluates the integrated signal only inside a window in the combustion period (about 10° to 60° after TDC of each cylinder), so valve closing and injector noise at other angles are ignored. The threshold is learnt from the background noise of non-knocking cycles.
  5. Action: if a cylinder knocks, the ECU retards its spark by a few degrees at once, then creeps the advance back in small steps; with a turbo it may also lower boost. Running close to the knock limit gives the best efficiency.
  6. Angular-rate sensor: a MEMS structure kept vibrating; when the vehicle yaws, the Coriolis force deflects the vibrating mass at right angles in proportion to the turn rate, and capacitive electrodes read this as a voltage in °/s.
  7. Use: the stability program compares the measured yaw rate with the rate expected from steering angle and speed. If the car turns less than expected (understeer) or more (oversteer), it brakes single wheels and cuts torque to bring the yaw rate back.
  8. Accelerometers complete the set: a lateral accelerometer for the stability program and high-g accelerometers for airbags. In a 50 km/h barrier crash stopping in 80 ms the average deceleration is 13.89 ÷ 0.080 = 173.6 m/s² (17.7 g).
  9. Points of care: a gyro gives rate, not angle; integrating it drifts with bias, so the ECU re-zeros it at standstill.
  10. Checks on the knock sensor: it must be bolted at the specified torque, because the clamp force sets its sensitivity; tapping the block near it with the engine idling should show a burst on an oscilloscope, and an open circuit makes the ECU fall back to a safe, retarded spark map.

Points to cover. Solenoid: coil pulls plunger, force rises with current, flow area set by pulse width; Injector: peak-and-hold or saturated drive, flyback diode, pulse width sets fuel quantity; Relay: small coil current switches a large load current, coil driven by low-side switch; Stepper: two coils, sequence of steps moves the pintle, no feedback needed, position held by detent; PWM drive for proportional solenoid valves such as EGR and VVT; Failures: open coil, shorted coil, sticking plunger.

  1. Solenoid: a coil around a movable iron plunger. Current makes a field that pulls the plunger in; the force F ≈ μ₀N²I²A ÷ (2g²) rises with current squared and falls steeply as the gap g grows.
  2. Fuel-injector circuit (low side):
    +12 V ── injector coil ──┬── drain
                        │  MOSFET (gate ← ECU)
                       ground
(flyback path: diode or active clamp across the coil / switch)

S3 intermittent periodic with a cyclic duration factor: window lift, door lock. S4 and S5 add starting and braking heat; S6 to S10 are continuous running with load or speed changes, such as a traction motor over a drive cycle.
  8. Cyclic duration factor: CDF = ton ÷ (ton + toff). A window lift running 4 s and resting 12 s has CDF = 4 ÷ 16 = 25 %, duty S3 25 %.
  9. Consequence: an S2 30 min fan motor fitted where the fan runs for hours in traffic overheats; the duty must match the use.
Understand Describe current and voltage measurement in a battery management system and explain how state of charge, state of health and state of function are estimated. 12 marks

Points to cover. Current: shunt resistor with differential amplifier, or Hall or fluxgate clamp for isolation; Voltage: resistive divider into ADC, per cell and pack level, isolation barrier; Coulomb counting: charge = integral of current over time; SOC from coulomb counting with a model correction at rest, and open-circuit voltage lookup; SOH from capacity fade and internal resistance rise; SOF from available power at present SOC and temperature.

Model answer.

  1. Current measurement, shunt: a low resistance (for example 0.1 mΩ) in the pack line; V = I R gives 10 mV at 100 A. Kelvin (four-terminal) connections take the voltage from the resistor body so lead resistance does not add; an amplifier raises it for the ADC, and the shunt's temperature is measured to correct its resistance.
  2. Current measurement, Hall or fluxgate: a core around the conductor concentrates the field of the current onto a Hall element; it gives galvanic isolation and no insertion loss but has more offset and is less accurate at small currents. The sign must be kept: discharge one way, charging and regeneration the other.
  3. Voltage measurement: each cell or module voltage is read by a cell-monitor chip; the pack voltage is scaled by a high-value resistor divider Vadc = Vpack R2 ÷ (R1 + R2) and passed through an isolated amplifier or isolated ADC, so the low-voltage electronics are separated from the high-voltage pack. A 12-bit ADC on 5 V resolves 5 ÷ 4095 = 1.22 mV.
  4. Temperature: NTC thermistors on modules feed the BMS, which limits current when a cell is hot or cold.
  5. SOC by coulomb counting:
    SOC(t) = SOC(0) − (1 ÷ Cn) ∫ I dt     (I > 0 for discharge, t in hours)
example: 50 Ah at 80 %, 10 A for 0.5 h → 5 Ah removed → 80 % − 5 ÷ 50 = 70 %
  6. Correction: coulomb counting drifts because a current offset or gain error is integrated; after a rest the open-circuit voltage gives SOC from the OCV curve, best near full and empty because the curve is flat in the middle. Production BMSs blend both in a model-based filter.
  7. SOH: compares the present capacity (learnt between two OCV resets from the charge counted) and the internal resistance (ΔV ÷ ΔI at a current step) with the new values; for example 45 Ah of an original 50 Ah is 90 % capacity SOH.
  8. SOF: the power the pack can deliver or accept now, from SOC, temperature and SOH (resistance): a cold, aged or nearly empty pack gives less. The vehicle uses it to decide whether stop-start, boost or regeneration is allowed.
  9. Summary: current, voltage and temperature sensing are the inputs; SOC, SOH and SOF are estimates the BMS computes from them.

Apply A crankshaft target wheel has 36 teeth with one tooth missing and runs at 3000 rev/min. Calculate the pulses per revolution, the crank angle per tooth pitch, the tooth-pitch frequency, the time between normal pulses and the length of the gap. Explain how the missing tooth is detected as the reference. 10 marks

Points to cover. Pulses per revolution = 35; Crank angle per pitch = 360 ÷ 36 = 10°; Pitch frequency = 36 × 50 = 1800 Hz; average pulse rate 35 × 50 = 1750 per second; Normal period T = 1 ÷ 1800 = 0.556 ms; Gap = 2T = 1.111 ms; detected when an interval is about twice the one before; Reference angle stored as calibration before TDC.

Model answer.

  1. Data: 36 pitches per revolution, 35 teeth present, n = 3000 rpm = 50 rev/s, so one revolution lasts 1 ÷ 50 = 20 ms.
  2. Pulses per revolution: 35 (one per present tooth).
  3. Crank angle per tooth pitch: 360° ÷ 36 = 10°. Use the pitch count, not the 35 teeth present.
  4. Tooth-pitch frequency: 36 × 50 = 1800 Hz. The average pulse rate is 35 × 50 = 1750 pulses per second, but the spacing is not uniform, so timing uses the pitch.
  5. Time between normal pulses: T = 1 ÷ 1800 = 0.556 ms (= 20 ms ÷ 36).
  6. Gap: from the last tooth before the missing one to the first tooth after it is two pitches, 2T = 1.111 ms.
  7. Check that one revolution adds up:
    34 normal intervals × 0.5556 ms = 18.889 ms
 1 gap interval   × 1.1111 ms =  1.111 ms
 total                         = 20.000 ms = 1 ÷ 50 rev/s
  8. Detection: the ECU time-stamps each edge with a capture timer and divides the latest interval by the previous one. Normal intervals give a ratio near 1; the gap gives about 2 (a 60-2 wheel gives 3). A threshold such as 1.5, with a check that the next interval falls back to about 0.5 of the gap, marks the reference and rejects a single noisy edge.
  9. Use of the reference: at the first tooth after the gap the tooth counter is reset and given a calibrated angle (for example 90° before TDC of cylinder 1; the value differs between engines). Angles in between are found by counting teeth of 10° and interpolating in time with the latest T.
  10. During sharp acceleration the gap ratio drifts from exactly 2, which is why the threshold sits well between 1 and 2.

Apply An NTC coolant sensor forms a voltage divider with a 2.2 kΩ pull-up to a 5 V reference and feeds a 10-bit ADC. For a sensor resistance of 2.2 kΩ, find the divider voltage and the ADC count. The same sensor (B = 3500 K) reads 269 Ω at 90 °C: find the voltage and count there, and state the practical problems of this arrangement. 10 marks

Points to cover. Vout = 5 × Rt ÷ (Rt + Rpull) = 2.5 V; Count = round(2.5 × 1023 ÷ 5) = 512; one count 4.89 mV; At 90 °C: 0.545 V, count 111; Non-linear: lookup table; Low resolution when hot; self-heating; wire and ground resistance.

Model answer.

  1. Circuit: 5 V reference → 2.2 kΩ pull-up → ECU input node → NTC → sensor ground. The ADC reads the node voltage.
  2. At Rt = 2.2 kΩ (25 °C): V = 5 × 2200 ÷ (2200 + 2200) = 2.500 V.
  3. ADC count: round(2.500 × 1023 ÷ 5) = round(511.5) = 512; one count is 5 ÷ 1023 = 4.89 mV.
  4. At 90 °C, Rt = 269 Ω: V = 5 × 269 ÷ (269 + 2200) = 0.545 V; count = round(0.545 × 1023 ÷ 5) = 111.
  5. More points from R = 2200 exp[3500 (1/T − 1/298.15)]:
    TemperatureRNTCVoltageCount
    −10 °C10.48 kΩ4.133 V846
    25 °C2.20 kΩ2.500 V512
    90 °C269 Ω0.545 V111
  6. Problem 1, non-linearity: the voltage is not a straight line in temperature, so the ECU must use a table or the Steinhart–Hart equation; a straight line through two points is wrong in between.
  7. Problem 2, resolution when hot: near 90 °C the curve is flat; between 85 °C and 95 °C the voltage changes by only about 0.13 V (about 26 counts), so one count is worth about 0.4 °C. Many ECUs switch in a smaller second pull-up when warm to spread the hot range.
  8. Problem 3, self-heating: at 25 °C the thermistor carries 5 ÷ 4400 = 1.14 mA and dissipates 1.14 mA² × 2200 ≈ 2.8 mW, which can warm a small bead and make it read high; a larger pull-up lowers this.
  9. Problem 4, wiring: series resistance from a corroded connector or a poor sensor ground adds to the NTC. 100 Ω extra is small against 10 kΩ when cold but large against 269 Ω when hot, so the ECU reads too cold only when the engine is warm.
  10. The ADC reference and the pull-up share the 5 V supply, so slow drift of the 5 V cancels (ratiometric reading).
  11. Remedies: use a second, smaller pull-up (switched in by the ECU above about 50 °C) to spread the hot range, keep the pull-up large enough to limit self-heating, filter the input with an RC low-pass, and run a separate sensor ground wire back to the ECU.
Apply A 12 V permanent-magnet radiator-fan motor has Ra = 0.25 Ω and kΦ = 0.025 V·s/rad. Ignoring friction, find the no-load speed, the speed, torque, output power and efficiency at 8 A, and the stall current and torque. The fan then runs 5 min on and 5 min off in a 10 min cycle: find the cyclic duration factor and state which duty rating the motor needs, and whether an S2 10 min motor would do for traffic driving. 13 marks

Points to cover. No load: ω = 12 ÷ 0.025 = 480 rad/s (4584 rpm); At 8 A: E = 10 V, ω = 400 rad/s, T = 0.20 N·m, P = 80 W, η = 83.3 %; Stall: 48 A, 1.20 N·m; CDF = 50 %, S3 50 %; Traffic: continuous, needs S1.

Model answer.

  1. Equations: E = kΦω, V = E + I Ra, T = kΦ I, Pout = T ω.
  2. No load (I ≈ 0): E = 12 V, ω = 12 ÷ 0.025 = 480 rad/s = 480 × 60 ÷ 2π = 4584 rpm.
  3. At 8 A: E = 12 − 8 × 0.25 = 10 V; ω = 10 ÷ 0.025 = 400 rad/s = 3820 rpm.
  4. Torque: T = 0.025 × 8 = 0.20 N·m.
  5. Output power: 0.20 × 400 = 80 W; input power: 12 × 8 = 96 W; efficiency 80 ÷ 96 = 83.3 %; the 16 W difference is the copper loss 8² × 0.25.
  6. Stall (ω = 0, E = 0): I = 12 ÷ 0.25 = 48 A; T = 0.025 × 48 = 1.20 N·m.

Analyze State the Hall effect. Compare Hall-effect, variable reluctance and optical sensors for crankshaft position sensing in a hot, oily engine bay; analyse their failure modes and explain how the ECU can limp home when the signal is lost. 14 marks

Points to cover. Hall effect: VH = I B ÷ (n q t); VR: passive, amplitude grows with speed, none at zero; Hall: needs supply, works at zero speed, clean square wave; Optical: fine resolution but oil and dirt block the light; Failure modes and limp home; Failure modes: open coil, shorted turns, air-gap change, metal debris on the magnet, connector corrosion; Limp home: substitute cam sensor signal, or fixed timing and speed-density fuel, or default to a fixed advance.

Model answer.

  1. Hall effect: a current I through a thin semiconductor plate in a field B at right angles pushes the carriers sideways (force q v × B) until a transverse voltage balances it: VH = I B ÷ (n q t). With 10 mA, 0.1 T, n = 10²² m⁻³ and t = 0.5 mm: VH = 0.010 × 0.1 ÷ (10²² × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 0.0005) = 1.25 mV, so an amplifier and Schmitt trigger follow on the same chip.
  2. Comparison:
    FeatureVariable reluctanceHallOptical
    SupplyNone (passive)5 V or 12 VLED supply
    OutputAC, grows with speedSquare wave, fixed heightSquare wave
    At zero speedNo outputGives stateGives state
    HeatVery tolerantIC limit about 150 °CLED ages with heat
    Oil and dirtTolerant (metal debris on magnet is a risk)TolerantPoor: oil mist blocks light
    CostLowestLowHigher
  3. Analysis: in a hot, oily engine bay the optical sensor is the weak choice; VR is robust but weak at cranking speed (at 150 rpm a 60-2 wheel gives only 150 Hz and a small amplitude); Hall gives a clean edge from zero speed, which suits quick starts and stop-start engines. Most modern engines therefore use Hall or magnetoresistive crank sensors.
  4. Failure modes: VR: open coil, shorted turns, air gap too large, metal debris on the magnet, a cracked wheel. Hall: lost supply or ground, connector corrosion, IC over-temperature. Optical: dirty lens or disc, LED ageing. All: chafed wires picking up ignition noise, giving false teeth.
  5. Detection: the ECU checks that the tooth count between gaps is right (58 on a 60-2 wheel), that the period changes plausibly, and that crank and cam signals agree.
  6. Limp home: if the crank signal is lost but the cam signal is good, some ECUs run from the cam sensor with fixed, safe advance and less precise timing; if the cam signal is lost, the ECU keeps the crank signal and uses wasted spark and batch injection; with neither the engine stops. A fault code is stored and the warning lamp lit.
  7. Diagnosis: check supply and ground with a meter, then look at the signal on an oscilloscope while cranking: a VR trace should grow with speed and show the gap; a Hall trace should switch cleanly between its two levels.
  8. Judgement: for this engine bay, a Hall (or magnetoresistive) sensor with a VR or cam back-up strategy gives the best start reliability.
Evaluate Evaluate the choice between a narrowband and a wideband oxygen sensor for a petrol engine that must meet close-coupled catalyst light-off and lean-burn operation. Support your answer with the control strategy each sensor allows. 13 marks

Points to cover. Narrowband: cheap, sharp switch at lambda = 1, only useful for holding stoichiometric; Cannot measure individual cylinder or lean-burn deviation with accuracy; Wideband: pump cell, current proportional to lambda, range roughly lambda 0.7 to air; Enables catalyst heating strategy, individual cylinder trim, lean-burn and ethanol blend adaptation; Needs heater control and a reference air supply, higher cost; Evaluate: emissions benefit and fuel saving against cost and durability of the pump cell.

Model answer.

  1. Requirements: fast catalyst light-off after a cold start (the catalyst does nothing until hot) and lean-burn operation at part load for fuel economy. The question is which sensor lets the ECU control both.
  2. Narrowband: a single Nernst cell; about 0.1 V lean to 0.9 V rich, switching only at λ = 1. It is cheap and durable and is ideal for holding λ = 1 with a limit-cycle trim. It cannot say how lean a mixture is: at λ = 1.3 and at λ = 1.05 it reads the same 0.1 V.
  3. Wideband: a pump cell holds a Nernst cell at about 450 mV; the pump current is proportional to oxygen excess or deficit, giving λ from about 0.7 to free air. It needs its own controller and heater control and costs more.
  4. Control strategies each allows:
    StrategyNarrowbandWideband
    λ = 1 limit-cycle trimYesYes, with a steady λ target
    Lean burn at λ 1.3–1.5NoYes
    Catalyst heating (rich-lean split, retard)Only after light-offYes, λ known from early on
    Cylinder-to-cylinder trimNoPossible from the signal ripple
    Ethanol blend adaptationSlowFast
  5. Light-off: both need a heater; a planar wideband reaches operating temperature in seconds, so closed loop starts sooner and the ECU can hold a precise slightly lean mixture with retarded spark to heat the catalyst.
  6. Lean burn: only a wideband can hold λ = 1.4 accurately; a narrowband would see a constant "lean" and have no feedback. A NOx storage catalyst for lean burn also needs a downstream sensor to judge its purge.
  7. Numbers: at λ = 1.048 (AFR 15.4) a narrowband reads about 0.1 V, the same as at λ = 1.3; a wideband reports 1.048, so the ECU can correct a 4.8 % lean error at once.
  8. Cost and durability: a wideband costs more and its pump cell must be calibrated; the narrowband is simpler. Many engines use a wideband before the catalyst and a narrowband after it as the catalyst monitor.
  9. Evaluation: for close-coupled light-off plus lean burn the wideband upstream sensor is required; its higher cost is repaid by fuel saving and by meeting emission limits, while a cheap narrowband stays downstream for monitoring.

Glossary

Absolute pressure
Pressure measured with vacuum as the zero reference, so it never goes negative.
Actuator
A device that turns an electrical command from the ECU into mechanical movement or flow.
Back emf
The voltage a turning motor generates against its supply, E = kΦω.
BMS
Battery management system; the electronics that measures cell voltage, current and temperature and estimates SOC, SOH and SOF.
Cyclic duration factor
On-time as a percentage of the cycle in an intermittent duty, ton ÷ (ton + toff).
Detonation (knock)
Abnormal rapid burning of the end gas in the cylinder, producing a pressure spike and high-frequency block vibration.
Diaphragm actuator
A pneumatic actuator in which pressure on a diaphragm works against a return spring.
Duty type
The load-against-time pattern (S1 to S10) for which a motor rating is valid.
EGR valve
Exhaust gas recirculation valve; a solenoid or stepper or vacuum-operated valve that admits exhaust into the intake to lower combustion temperature and NOx.
Flyback diode
A diode across an inductive load that gives the collapsing coil current a path, protecting the driving switch.
Fuel trim
The percentage correction the ECU applies to injection quantity from oxygen sensor feedback; short term is fast, long term is learned.
Gyroscope
An angular-rate sensor, usually vibrating MEMS, whose output voltage is proportional to rotation rate about an axis.
Hysteresis
The difference in output when the same input is reached from increasing and from decreasing direction.
Injector
An electromagnetic valve that opens for a commanded time to spray a measured fuel quantity.
Lambda
The ratio of actual air-fuel ratio to the stoichiometric ratio; lambda = 1 means chemically correct.
LIDAR
Light detection and ranging; a pulsed laser and detector that measures distance to objects from time of flight, used in driver assistance.
Microcontroller
A processor with flash, RAM, EEPROM, ADC, timers and communication on one chip; the heart of every ECU.
MOSFET
A voltage-controlled transistor switch; when on it behaves as a small resistance RDS(on).
Proportional valve
A solenoid valve whose spool position or output pressure follows the coil current smoothly.
Relay
An electromagnetically operated switch that lets a small ECU current control a large load current.
Resolution
The smallest change in input that the sensor and ADC can distinguish, set by bit count and reference voltage.
Sensitivity
The change in output for a unit change in input, for example millivolts per kilopascal for a MAP sensor.
State of charge
Remaining charge in a battery as a percentage of its rated capacity.
State of function
The power the battery can actually deliver or accept now, at the present charge and temperature.
State of health
The present capacity and internal resistance of an aged battery compared with its new condition.
Synchronous speed
Speed of the rotating field of a three-phase machine, 120 f ÷ p in rpm.
Thermistor
A semiconductor resistor whose resistance changes strongly with temperature; NTC types fall in resistance as temperature rises.
Thermocouple
Two dissimilar metals joined at a hot junction, producing a small voltage proportional to the temperature difference between junctions.
Watchdog
A timer that resets the microcontroller if the program stops refreshing it.
Wheatstone bridge
Four resistances in a diamond, excited by a supply, giving a near-zero output at balance and a small differential voltage when one arm changes.
