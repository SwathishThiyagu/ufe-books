3.1 Sensor basics, classification and signal conditioning

A sensor turns a physical quantity — temperature, pressure, position, speed — into an electrical signal. That signal is often small, noisy and nonlinear, so signal conditioning amplifies, filters and shifts it before an ADC converts it to a number for the ECU. An error at the sensing element becomes a wrong fuel pulse or brake force. This chapter fixes the vocabulary, classifies sensors, and follows the signal to the ECU input.

You need Ohm's law, the voltage divider and a stable DC supply from Unit 1; the thermistor returns in 3.5.

Sensor, transducer, actuator, and the words used to judge them

A sensor converts a physical quantity into an electrical signal. The quantity it measures is the measured variable — coolant temperature, manifold pressure, wheel speed. The transducer is the element that does the conversion; most automotive writing uses both words for the same part. An actuator works the other way: an electrical command becomes motion or force, covered in 3.8.

Specifications decide whether a sensor suits a job. Each word below has a precise meaning; marks are lost when units are dropped.

  • Range: the lowest and highest input the sensor is specified for, e.g. −40 °C to 150 °C.
  • Span: range maximum minus range minimum, e.g. 190 °C.
  • Sensitivity: output change per unit input change, e.g. 10 mV/°C. It always carries units.
  • Resolution: the smallest input change that gives a detectable output change.
  • Accuracy: how close the reading is to the true value, quoted as ±°C or as a percentage of full scale.
  • Linearity: how far the input–output graph bends away from a straight line, as a percentage of full scale.
  • Hysteresis: the output gap at the same input when the input arrives from below rather than from above.
  • Drift: slow change of output with time or ambient temperature while the input is held constant.
  • Response time: time for the output to reach a stated fraction (often 63% or 90%) of its final value after a step input.

Classification and the sensing elements inside

  • Active vs passive: a passive sensor generates its own signal (thermocouple, piezoelectric element, magnetic reluctance coil); an active sensor needs an excitation supply (thermistor, potentiometer, biased Hall device). Some instrumentation textbooks use the reverse convention — active for a self-generating element such as a thermocouple — so state which convention you are using in an answer.
  • Analog vs digital: analog sensors give a continuous voltage; digital ones give a pulse train whose frequency or count carries the value.
  • Contact vs non-contact: a coolant thermistor touches the fluid; a Hall or optical sensor reads a target across an air gap.
  • Absolute vs relative: an absolute sensor measures from true zero (manifold absolute pressure from vacuum); a relative sensor measures against a local reference such as ambient pressure.

Behind these classes sit a few physical effects. Resistive elements change resistance with position, temperature or strain (potentiometer, NTC thermistor, strain gauge). Capacitive elements change capacitance with diaphragm movement or humidity. Inductive and magnetic-reluctance elements produce a voltage from changing flux. Piezoelectric crystals produce charge under force, used for knock. Thermoelectric junctions (thermocouples) produce a small voltage proportional to temperature difference. Hall elements give a voltage proportional to magnetic field. MEMS devices etch a silicon diaphragm or proof mass so the sensing element and its conditioning circuit sit on one chip.

Signal conditioning: from millivolts to a number

Signal conditioning is everything between the sensing element and the ADC. It makes a weak, noisy, offset, nonlinear signal into a clean voltage in the ADC's input window.

  1. Excite and bridge: a Wheatstone bridge turns a small resistance change into a differential voltage that sits near zero when the input is zero.
  2. Amplify: an instrumentation amplifier multiplies the differential voltage by a fixed gain and converts it to a single-ended signal referred to ground.
  3. Filter: a low-pass filter removes ignition and switching noise; its corner is f = 1/(2πRC), with R in ohms and C in farads.
  4. Isolate: optocouplers or capacitive barriers separate high-voltage battery sensors from the low-voltage ECU.
  5. Linearise: a lookup table or polynomial maps the raw reading to engineering units, because thermistors and bridges are not straight lines.
  6. Cold-junction compensation: a thermocouple measures only a difference, so the ECU adds the measured temperature of the connector block.
  7. Sample and hold: a capacitor freezes the voltage while the ADC converts, so a changing input does not smear the result.
  8. ADC: an N-bit converter reports a count, count = round(V × (2^N − 1) / V_ref), where V is the input in volts and V_ref is the reference in volts.

V_out = V_s × R_s / (R_s + R_f)

V_out is the junction voltage in volts; V_s is the supply voltage in volts; R_s is the sensing resistance in ohms, connected from V_out to ground; R_f is the fixed resistor in ohms, connected from +V_s to V_out.

Worked example

Question. A coolant temperature sensor uses a 1 kΩ fixed resistor R_f from +5 V to the output node, and an NTC thermistor R_s from the output node to ground. R_s is 2 kΩ at 25 °C and 0.5 kΩ at 80 °C. (a) Find the divider output at both temperatures. (b) Find the 10-bit ADC counts for a 0–5 V ADC. (c) State the ADC resolution in mV.

  1. Divider equation: V_out = V_s × R_s / (R_s + R_f), with V_s = 5 V and R_f = 1000 Ω. R_f connects +5 V to V_out; R_s connects V_out to ground.
  2. At 25 °C, R_s = 2000 Ω: V_out = 5 × 2000 / (2000 + 1000) = 3.333 V.
  3. At 80 °C, R_s = 500 Ω: V_out = 5 × 500 / (500 + 1000) = 1.667 V.
  4. Count for 10 bits: count = round(V_out × 1023 / 5).
  5. At 25 °C: count = round(3.333 × 1023 / 5) = 682.
  6. At 80 °C: count = round(1.667 × 1023 / 5) = 341.
  7. Resolution: one count = 5 / 1023 = 4.89 mV.
  8. Check the direction: resistance falls as temperature rises (NTC), so the output falls too. The count-to-°C map is nonlinear and is done by lookup table in the ECU.

Answer. The divider gives 3.333 V (682 counts) at 25 °C and 1.667 V (341 counts) at 80 °C, with 4.89 mV per ADC count.

Exam traps

  • The ADC count is not the physical value. 682 is a voltage code, not 25 °C; convert and linearise with the sensor curve before quoting a temperature.
  • NTC resistance falls with temperature, so the divider output falls with temperature. Many answers show the voltage rising and lose all the marks.
  • Sensitivity must carry units (mV/°C, mV/kPa). A bare number is treated as incomplete.
  • Resolution is not accuracy. A 10-bit ADC resolves 4.89 mV, but the sensor may still be accurate only to ±2 °C; reference tolerance and self-heating add further error.
  • Self-heating: the excitation current through a thermistor adds heat and shifts the reading, and a drifting 5 V reference scales every count you compute.

Understand Why is a Wheatstone bridge preferred to a single divider with a strain gauge? 3 marks

The bridge gives a differential output near zero at zero strain, so the amplifier sees only the small change; supply drift and temperature effects cancel because they affect both arms equally. A single divider has a large standing offset that must be amplified along with the signal.

Analyze A coolant thermistor reading climbs steadily for 20 minutes while the engine is off and the temperature is constant. Give two likely causes and one test for each. 5 marks

First, self-heating: the excitation current warms the element, so compare readings at normal excitation and at a reduced excitation current; self-heating shows as a lower reading at the higher current. Understand Why is a sample-and-hold circuit placed before the ADC? 3 marks

The ADC needs a steady input while it converts. The sample-and-hold capacitor freezes the voltage at the sampling instant, so a slowly changing or noisy signal does not change during conversion and corrupt the digital result.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Production sensors move to digital outputs such as SENT and PSI5, where conditioning, linearisation and self-diagnosis sit inside the sensor and the ECU receives engineering units. Sensor fusion combines several imperfect sensors — wheel speed, yaw rate and acceleration for stability control — instead of trusting one. Research is active on solid-state gas sensors that survive exhaust temperatures and self-calibrating sensors that correct drift in service. 3.2 Air flow rate and manifold pressure (MAP) sensors

The engine needs a known mass of air for every stroke because fuel quantity is set from air charge. A mass air flow (MAF) sensor measures this mass directly; a vane meter measures air volume flow and needs a density correction. A manifold absolute pressure (MAP) sensor measures intake pressure, and the ECU computes mass from pressure, speed, displacement and temperature. These sensors sit in the intake path and feed the engine control unit (ECU). They are used in petrol and diesel fuel control, turbo boost control, exhaust gas recirculation and diagnostics. Get the air charge wrong and the mixture, torque and emissions all drift.

You need 3.1 for signal conditioning, the gas law, and engine displacement; fuel pulse use is in Unit 4.

Manifold absolute pressure sensors

Manifold absolute pressure (MAP) is the absolute pressure inside the intake manifold after the throttle, measured in kPa. At idle the throttle is nearly closed, so MAP may be 20–40 kPa. At wide-open throttle in a naturally aspirated engine it rises toward barometric pressure. Barometric pressure is local atmospheric pressure, about 100 kPa at sea level and lower at altitude. With ignition on and engine off, MAP equals barometric pressure, which lets the ECU check the sensor and learn altitude.

A MAP sensor turns pressure into voltage. In a strain-gauge MAP sensor, a diaphragm bends under pressure. Foil or diffused strain gauges are bonded to it, and their resistance changes with strain. The gauges form a Wheatstone bridge. The bridge output is small, so signal conditioning amplifies and filters it.

V_o ≈ (V_ex ÷ 4) × (ΔR ÷ R)

V_o is bridge output in V; V_ex is excitation voltage in V; R is gauge resistance in Ω; ΔR is its change in Ω. This is the small-change quarter-bridge result.

A capacitive MAP sensor uses the diaphragm as one plate of a capacitor. Pressure moves the diaphragm, changes the gap and changes capacitance; an oscillator and detector convert this to voltage. A piezoresistive MAP sensor uses resistors diffused into a silicon diaphragm. Pressure bends the silicon and changes resistance. The bridge and signal conditioning sit inside the sensor. A typical transfer is 0.5 V at 20 kPa and 4.5 V at 100 kPa. The ECU uses a stored table for accuracy. Some syllabi call this a strain gauge pressure sensor.

Air flow meters

An air flow meter measures the air entering the engine. The oldest is the vane meter: a spring-loaded flap is pushed by air, and a potentiometer reports flap angle. It is simple but causes pressure loss and wears.

Hot-wire and hot-film meters keep a heated element at a fixed temperature above the incoming air. Moving air cools the element. The circuit increases current to hold the temperature, and that current measures mass flow. The element sits in a Wheatstone bridge. The bridge balances when the heated element resistance, and hence temperature, is fixed. After shutdown, a burn-off pulse heats the wire to around 1000 °C for about a second to burn off dirt. This keeps calibration stable. Hot-film sensors use a flat film and are more rugged; hot-wire sensors use a thin platinum wire and respond faster. Intake air temperature (IAT) is used to correct density and bridge drift.

A Karman vortex meter places a bluff body in the air stream. Vortices shed alternately downstream. The shedding frequency f is proportional to air velocity v: f = St × v ÷ d. Here f is frequency in Hz, St is the dimensionless Strouhal number (about 0.2), v is velocity in m/s, and d is bluff body width in m. A pressure or ultrasonic sensor counts vortices. Frequency gives velocity; density from pressure and temperature is needed for mass flow. These meters have no moving parts and low pressure loss.

Speed-density and mass-air methods

The speed-density method uses MAP, engine speed, displacement, volumetric efficiency and intake temperature. Volumetric efficiency η_v is the actual air volume trapped per intake stroke divided by the displaced volume, referenced to manifold conditions. It is dimensionless. With this reference it is typically 0.8–1.0 for naturally aspirated engines and stays near 1 for turbocharged engines. If VE is referenced to ambient pressure instead, a boosted engine can exceed 1.

m_dot_air = η_v × P_man × V_d × (N ÷ 2) ÷ (R × T)

m_dot_air is air mass flow in kg/s; P_man is MAP in Pa absolute; V_d is total engine displacement in m³; N is engine speed in rev/s; R is 287 J/(kg·K) for air; T is intake air temperature in K; η_v is volumetric efficiency. The factor N/2 appears because a four-stroke engine has one intake stroke per two revolutions.

The mass-air method uses a MAF sensor and measures mass flow directly. It needs no volumetric efficiency table, so it handles altitude, intake leaks and engine wear better. It costs more, and the hot element can be contaminated. The speed-density method is cheaper and uses a MAP sensor, but it depends on the VE table and correct IAT and barometric pressure. Many ECUs use MAF for steady operation and MAP for transient correction and diagnostics.

Worked example

Question. A 1.6 L four-stroke petrol engine runs at 3000 rpm. MAP is 60 kPa absolute and intake air temperature is 300 K. Volumetric efficiency is 0.90. Take R = 287 J/(kg·K). Estimate the air mass flow in kg/s and g/s.

  1. Convert displacement: V_d = 1.6 L = 0.0016 m³.
  2. Convert speed: N = 3000 ÷ 60 = 50 rev/s.
  3. For a four-stroke engine, the number of engine cycles per second = N ÷ 2 = 50 ÷ 2 = 25 s⁻¹.
  4. Convert pressure: P_man = 60 kPa = 60,000 Pa absolute.
  5. Air density at manifold conditions: ρ = P_man ÷ (R × T) = 60,000 ÷ (287 × 300) = 0.6969 kg/m³.
  6. Ideal air mass inducted per engine cycle (all cylinders together): ρ × V_d = 0.6969 × 0.0016 = 0.001115 kg.
  7. Actual air mass per engine cycle: η_v × 0.001115 = 0.90 × 0.001115 = 0.0010035 kg.
  8. Air mass flow: 0.0010035 × 25 = 0.02509 kg/s.
  9. Convert to g/s: 0.02509 × 1000 = 25.09 g/s, about 25.1 g/s.

Answer. The estimated air mass flow is 0.0251 kg/s, which is 25.1 g/s.

Exam traps

  • Using rpm directly in the speed-density formula. Convert to rev/s and remember the N/2 factor for a four-stroke engine.
  • Using gauge pressure instead of absolute pressure. MAP and P_man must be absolute, and in Pa inside the formula.
  • Treating MAF and MAP as interchangeable. MAF gives mass flow directly; MAP needs speed, displacement, volumetric efficiency and temperature.
  • Thinking Karman vortex frequency alone gives mass flow. Frequency gives velocity; density correction is needed for mass.
  • Reading burn-off current as a flow signal. Burn-off is a cleaning pulse after shutdown, not a measurement phase.

3.3 Crankshaft position and engine speed sensors

An engine must fire the spark and open each injector at a precise crank angle, not just at a precise average rate. The crankshaft position sensor (CKP) and the camshaft position sensor (CMP) report piston position and engine speed to the engine control unit (ECU). The ECU uses these pulses to synchronise ignition and fuel delivery, to detect misfire, and to run sequential injection. Every modern petrol engine uses at least a CKP. Some syllabi call the CKP the engine crankshaft angular position sensor, the engine angular speed sensor or the timing sensor.

You need: 3.1 for threshold and filter circuits, and basic angular motion: angle in degrees, speed in rpm and frequency in hertz.

Position, speed and phase

The CKP faces a toothed wheel on the crankshaft pulley or the flywheel. Each tooth that passes the sensor produces one electrical pulse. The ECU reads angular position from the identity of the tooth and angular speed from the time between pulses. The same signal gives both quantities, so one sensor serves ignition timing, injector timing, idle control, the rev limiter and the tachometer.

The CMP reads a single tooth, window or magnetic target on the camshaft. In a four-stroke engine the camshaft turns at half crankshaft speed, so one cam pulse occurs per two crank revolutions. That pulse identifies cylinder 1 on its compression stroke. Sequential fuel injection and individual coil-on-plug ignition need this phase information; without it the ECU may fall back to wasted spark (spark on both strokes) and batch injection (injectors fired in groups).

Angular position is an angle, usually measured in degrees before top dead centre (BTDC). Angular speed is a rate, usually in revolutions per minute (rpm). Do not mix them. A CKP can report position to within one tooth pitch and speed to within a fraction of a percent after filtering.

n = 1 / (z × T)    rpm = 60 / (z × T)    Δθ = 360° / z

n is revolutions per second; rpm is revolutions per minute; z is the number of tooth pitches per revolution (60 for a 60-2 wheel, 36 for a 36-1 wheel); T is the time between successive normal tooth edges in seconds; Δθ is the crank angle per tooth pitch in degrees. For a 60-2 wheel Δθ = 6°.

Toothed wheels and missing-tooth synchronisation

A plain toothed wheel gives speed but not absolute position: the ECU cannot tell tooth 1 from tooth 20 after switch-on. Manufacturers cut one or more teeth out of the wheel to create a reference gap. Common wheels are 60-2 (58 teeth present, 2 missing), 36-1 (35 present, 1 missing) and 12+1. In a 12+1 wheel all teeth are present, and the reference is the single short interval produced by the extra tooth, so the ECU detects an unusually short period instead of an unusually long one. The reference feature appears once per revolution.

  1. Condition the sensor signal into clean edges (see 3.1).
  2. Measure the period T between consecutive edges with a timer.
  3. Compare each period with the previous one. A gap is longer than a normal tooth period by a known ratio: for 60-2 the gap is 3T.
  4. When the gap is detected, reset the tooth counter and assign the stored angle of the first tooth after the gap.
  5. Compute speed from T and update the ignition and injection schedules.

The missing-tooth gap for a 60-2 wheel is three tooth periods, not two. The last present tooth is followed by two empty slots and then the next present tooth, so the time from the last edge to the next edge is 3T. For a 36-1 wheel the gap is 2T. The ECU calibration stores the exact crank angle of the first tooth after the gap; that angle differs between engines.

Speed comes from the period, not from counting pulses in a fixed window. A period measurement updates once per tooth and reacts quickly during acceleration. The ECU filters very short periods to reject noise and uses a timeout to detect a stopped engine.

Sensor types and signal shape

A variable-reluctance (VR) sensor, also called an inductive sensor, has a permanent magnet and a coil. A tooth passing the pole piece changes the magnetic flux and induces an alternating voltage. The amplitude rises with engine speed and falls with a larger air gap (the clearance between sensor tip and tooth). At cranking speed the signal may be only a few hundred millivolts, so the conditioning circuit uses a zero-crossing detector with hysteresis (two threshold levels). A VR sensor has two wires and needs no supply.

A Hall sensor has a Hall element, a magnet and a rotating vane or toothed target. It switches its output between low and high, giving a digital square wave. The amplitude is set by the supply and load, not by speed, so the ECU sees a usable signal down to zero speed. Hall CKP sensors are common on engines that must start reliably in cold weather. They have three wires: supply, ground and signal.

An optical sensor uses a light-emitting diode (LED) and a phototransistor across a slotted disc. It gives clean digital pulses but is sensitive to dirt and oil, so it is more common in test rigs and some distributor-based systems. A magnetoresistive (MR) sensor uses a thin film whose resistance changes with magnetic field direction. It gives a digital output with a large air gap and good low-speed performance, and appears in some crank and cam sensors.

Signal shape matters to the ECU. A VR output rises and falls as the tooth approaches and leaves the pole piece. Its voltage is proportional to the rate of change of flux, so it crosses zero when the tooth is centred on the pole piece and the flux is maximum. The ECU detects that zero crossing as the timing reference. The time between successive zero crossings is still one tooth pitch period T, so the speed formulas do not change. Hall, optical and MR outputs are already logic levels; their reference is the chosen signal edge. The stored calibration offset therefore differs between sensor types, and a VR sensor and a Hall sensor on the same wheel need different offsets.

Worked example

Question. A 60-2 crank wheel turns at 3000 rpm. Find the time between successive normal tooth edges and the duration of the missing-tooth gap measured from the last present tooth to the first present tooth after the gap.

  1. Convert speed: 3000 rpm ÷ 60 = 50 rev/s.
  2. Time for one revolution: 1 ÷ 50 = 0.020 s = 20 ms.
  3. A 60-2 wheel has 60 tooth pitches per revolution. Normal tooth period T = 20 ms ÷ 60 = 0.333333 ms ≈ 0.333 ms.
  4. The gap spans three pitch periods: the last present tooth, two empty slots, then the next present tooth. Gap = 3 × 0.333333 ms = 1.000 ms.

Answer. The normal tooth period is 0.333 ms and the missing-tooth gap is 1.0 ms.

Exam traps

  • For a 60-2 wheel the missing-tooth gap is 3T, not 2T. Two teeth are missing, but the interval from the last present tooth to the next present tooth covers three pitch periods.
  • Use the pitch count z (60 for 60-2, 36 for 36-1) in speed = 1/(zT). Do not use the number of present teeth (58 or 35) when you measure T between normal teeth.
  • VR amplitude changes with speed; a clean signal at 3000 rpm does not prove the sensor will work at 150 rpm cranking. Hall output amplitude does not depend on speed.
  • Angular position is an angle in degrees; angular speed is a rate in rpm. A timing sensor gives phase information, not necessarily a second independent speed signal.
  • Do not assume the first tooth after the gap is at TDC. Its offset is calibration data and varies between engines.

It converts a signal transition into a logic event at a chosen threshold, so the ECU can time the arrival of each tooth.

3.4 Hall effect, magnetic reluctance and optical sensors

Some sensors read position or rotation without touching the part. The Hall sensor uses the Lorentz force on charge carriers in a thin semiconductor plate to make a small voltage proportional to magnetic field. A variable-reluctance sensor makes an AC pulse from a moving toothed wheel. An optical sensor breaks a light beam with a vane. These three give the ECU crank position, wheel speed and pedal position without wear, and each has a different output and a different tolerance to dirt.

You need: 3.3 crank position and the magnetic field and induction basics from physics.

Hall effect and Hall switches

A thin semiconductor plate carries current I. A magnetic field B at right angles pushes the moving carriers sideways with force q v × B. Charge collects on one edge until the electric field balances this force. The steady voltage across the plate is the Hall voltage. It is small, often millivolts, so an amplifier and a Schmitt trigger follow it.

V_H = K_H I B,  K_H = 1/(n q t)    and    V_H = R_H I B / t,  R_H = 1/(n q)

V_H is the Hall voltage in V; I is the current in A; B is the flux density in T; n is the carrier density in m⁻³; q is the carrier charge in C; t is the plate thickness in m; K_H is the Hall sensitivity (current-related sensitivity) in V/(A·T) or m²/C; R_H is the Hall coefficient in m³/C.

A Hall switch compares V_H with two thresholds. The output changes at the operate point and returns at the release point, so a small field wobble does not chatter. A Hall latch keeps its last state until the field reverses. Hall sensors are used for cam and crank position, wheel speed, pedal position and gear selection. Most are digital switches; linear Hall versions give an analog voltage that is used for pedal and throttle position. They need a supply; many work down to zero speed. Magnetoresistive sensors use a thin film whose resistance changes with field angle, giving a compact angle signal for some pedal and motor-position applications.

Variable-reluctance sensors

A variable-reluctance (VR) sensor has a coil wound on a magnet, facing a toothed iron wheel. As a tooth moves near the coil, the air gap and the magnetic reluctance fall; flux rises. As the tooth leaves, flux falls. The changing flux induces a voltage in the coil by Faraday's law. The sensor is passive: it needs no supply. It is also called a magnetic reluctance or inductive sensor.

e = -N dΦ/dt and f = z n / 60

e is the induced voltage in V; N is the number of turns; Φ is the magnetic flux in Wb; t is time in s; f is the pulse frequency in Hz; z is the number of teeth; n is the shaft speed in rev/min.

The output is an AC voltage. Its amplitude and frequency both rise with speed. At low speed the amplitude may be too small for the ECU, and at zero speed there is no output. Signal conditioning uses a threshold and often a zero-crossing detector to make a clean pulse. A VR sensor is robust and simple, and is common on older crank and ABS wheel-speed sensing. The air gap must be small and uniform; run-out or metal debris changes the pulse.

Optical sensors and comparison

An optical sensor uses an LED and a phototransistor. In the slotted type, a vane on the moving part passes through the gap and blocks the light; the phototransistor switches. In the reflective type, light returns from a mark. The output is digital and works down to zero speed. Dirt, oil, water and strong ambient light can block or false-trigger the beam, so the gap is kept small and shielded. Some texts call these optoelectronic sensors.

Optical crankshaft position sensing uses a disc with slots fixed to the crank. The ECU counts slot edges for speed and uses one wider index slot for absolute position. A slotted coupler gives one pulse per slot; the index pulse marks top dead centre for cylinder 1. This is non-contact and has no wear, but it is less tolerant of oil mist than a Hall or VR sensor.

All three are non-contact, so there is no rubbing wear.

Worked example

Question. A Hall plate carries I = 10 mA in a flux density B = 0.1 T. The plate thickness is t = 0.5 mm and carrier density is n = 1×10²² m⁻³. Take q = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C. Find V_H in volts and in millivolts.

  1. Convert: I = 10 mA = 0.010 A; t = 0.5 mm = 0.0005 m.
  2. K_H = 1/(n q t) = 1/(1×10²² × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 0.0005) = 1/0.8 = 1.25 m²/C.
  3. V_H = K_H I B = 1.25 × 0.010 × 0.1 = 0.00125 V.
  4. In millivolts: 0.00125 × 1000 = 1.25 mV.

Answer. The Hall voltage is 0.00125 V, or 1.25 mV; an amplifier raises this to a logic level.

Exam traps

  • A VR sensor is not a Hall sensor: its output is an AC voltage that depends on speed, so it needs a threshold and often a zero-crossing detector. A Hall sensor needs a supply and gives a digital signal.
  • Hall voltage is proportional to B, but a Hall switch has hysteresis: the operate and release fields differ.
  • Convert mA to A and mm to m before using V_H = I B/(n q t); a factor of 1000 is the common slip.
  • Optical sensors fail with dirt, oil and sunlight; a missing pulse may be a blocked beam, not a bad ECU.
  • A passive VR sensor cannot report zero speed; no rotation means no flux change and no output.

3.5 Throttle position and temperature sensors

The throttle position sensor tells the engine control unit (ECU) how far the driver has pressed the accelerator. Temperature sensors tell the ECU how hot the engine coolant, intake air, battery and cabin are. These signals set fuel, ignition, idle speed and climate comfort. A throttle position sensor is a position sensor with a safety check; a temperature sensor is usually a negative temperature coefficient (NTC) thermistor in a voltage divider. Both reach the ECU through an analog-to-digital converter (ADC) and a calibration table. This chapter shows the circuits, the non-linearity traps and the redundancy checks that keep the readings trustworthy.

You need: 3.1 for signal conditioning, voltage dividers and ADC counts, and 3.4 for contactless position sensing.

Throttle position sensor (TPS)

The throttle plate controls air into a petrol engine. The throttle position sensor (TPS) converts plate angle into a voltage. The ECU uses it for idle control, acceleration enrichment, gear shift decisions and cruise control. A simple TPS is a potentiometer: a resistive track fed with a stable 5 V reference, and a wiper driven by the throttle shaft. The wiper voltage rises with opening. Many units have two tracks with opposite slopes; the ECU checks that their sum stays constant, so a broken wiper or short is detected.

V_TPS = V₀ + k θ

V_TPS is the wiper voltage in V; V₀ is the voltage at closed throttle in V; k is the slope in V per % throttle; θ is throttle opening in %.

A contactless TPS uses a Hall element or an inductive coil. A magnet turns with the throttle shaft and the output changes without rubbing. It lasts longer and has no wiper noise. Some systems call it a throttle angle sensor. The accelerator pedal has two independent position sensors, often with different slopes or a fixed ratio. The ECU compares them: if they disagree by more than a small window, it limits engine power and sets a fault code.

  1. Read both tracks through separate ADC channels.
  2. Check 0.5 V to 4.5 V range at ignition on.
  3. Check track sum or ratio at each angle.
  4. If plausible, use a calibrated table for throttle %.
  5. If not, use a limp-home value and store a fault code.

Older systems add a closed-throttle idle switch and a wide-open-throttle (WOT) switch. The idle switch confirms 0% for idle speed control; the WOT switch signals maximum load for enrichment. A switch is a yes/no contact, not a measured angle.

Coolant and air temperature sensors

Engine coolant temperature (ECT) and intake air temperature (IAT) sensors are negative temperature coefficient (NTC) thermistors. Their resistance falls as temperature rises. A typical circuit puts the thermistor to ground and a fixed pull-up resistor to a clean 5 V reference. The junction voltage goes to the ADC. With this arrangement the voltage falls as temperature rises. A small reference error moves every reading, so the ECU supply is regulated and checked.

V_out = V_ref × R_NTC / (R_pull + R_NTC)

V_out is the divider voltage in V; V_ref is the sensor supply in V; R_pull is the fixed resistor in Ω; R_NTC is the thermistor resistance in Ω at the measured temperature.

The NTC curve is not a straight line. A two-point linear conversion is wrong in the middle. The ECU stores a table of resistance or voltage against temperature, or evaluates the Steinhart-Hart equation. The table has more points near cold starts, where fuel correction matters. IAT corrects air density; ECT corrects fuel and ignition during warm-up and controls the radiator fan. Battery thermal sensors use the same thermistor idea to protect charging and traction batteries.

1/T = A + B ln R + C (ln R)³

T is absolute temperature in K; R is thermistor resistance in Ω; A, B and C are constants for that thermistor.

A thermistor is a resistor, not a thermocouple. It needs a divider and an ADC. The pull-up resistor value sets the voltage range. Choose it so the ADC uses most of its range over the expected temperature span. Check the connector and ground: a high-resistance ground or connector adds an offset, so compare the divider voltage with the table before replacing the sensor.

Cabin, humidity and air quality

Climate control needs more than one temperature. A cabin temperature sensor with a small fan samples the air near the dash. An outside temperature sensor gives ambient. A sun-load sensor on the dash measures solar heating. A humidity sensor, often capacitive, measures water vapour in the cabin air. An air-quality sensor detects reducing gases or particles and selects recirculation. The climate ECU combines these signals with the target temperature and blower status to drive air blend doors and the compressor. The actuators are covered in 3.8.

The same thermal sensing appears in the battery pack. Several NTC thermistors report module temperature to the battery management system (BMS). The BMS compares them and reduces charging power if one is hot. Humidity sensing also protects electronics: condensation can short connectors. Air-quality signals are slow and noisy, so the ECU filters them and uses hysteresis, a small dead band, before changing recirculation.

Worked example

Question. A throttle position sensor has a linear characteristic: 0.5 V at 0% throttle and 4.5 V at 100% throttle. The ECU uses a 10-bit ADC with 0 V → 0 and 5 V → 1023. Find (a) the TPS voltage at 30% throttle and (b) the ADC count for that voltage.

  1. Slope k = (4.5 V − 0.5 V) / (100% − 0%) = 4.0 V / 100% = 0.04 V per %.
  2. At 30%: V_TPS = 0.5 V + 0.04 V/% × 30% = 0.5 V + 1.20 V = 1.70 V.
  3. ADC count = round(1023 × V_TPS / 5 V) = round(1023 × 1.70 / 5) = round(347.82) = 348.
  4. Full scale is 1023, not 1024, because a 10-bit ADC gives counts 0 to 1023.

Answer. At 30% throttle the TPS voltage is 1.70 V and the 10-bit ADC count is 348.

Exam traps

  • Do not treat an NTC thermistor as linear. A straight line through two temperatures gives large errors in between.
  • The ADC count uses full scale 1023 for 10 bits, not 1024. A 5 V reference gives count = round(1023 × V / 5).
  • TPS redundancy is not two identical signals. The tracks often have opposite slopes or a fixed ratio; the ECU checks agreement, not equality.
  • A bad sensor ground or 5 V reference shifts TPS and temperature readings together. Check supply and ground before replacing the sensor.
  • Idle and WOT switches are not analog angle sensors. They only confirm endpoints and cannot replace the TPS curve.

3.6 Exhaust gas oxygen (lambda) sensors

The exhaust gas oxygen sensor, also called the lambda sensor, measures oxygen left in the exhaust. The ECU uses it to hold the air-fuel ratio near stoichiometric, where a three-way catalyst works best, or near a target lambda. Petrol stoichiometric AFR is 14.7:1 by mass. The sensor closes the fuel loop: air charge sensors set a base fuel mass, and the EGO trims it. Narrowband sensors switch sharply near λ = 1; wideband sensors give a linear reading. Used on petrol, gas and some diesel engines.

You need: 3.1 for signal conditioning, 3.2 for air charge, and 3.5 for temperature effects.

Lambda and closed-loop fuel trim

Lambda λ is the actual air-fuel ratio divided by the stoichiometric air-fuel ratio for the fuel. For petrol, stoichiometric AFR is 14.7 kg air per kg fuel, written 14.7:1. λ = AFRactual / AFRstoich. λ less than 1 is rich (excess fuel), λ equal to 1 is stoichiometric, λ greater than 1 is lean (excess air). The same λ can apply to petrol, CNG or ethanol if the correct stoichiometric AFR is used.

λ = AFR<sub>actual</sub> / AFR<sub>stoich</sub>

AFRactual is the actual air-fuel mass ratio; AFRstoich is the stoichiometric ratio, 14.7:1 for petrol; λ is dimensionless.

The ECU runs closed-loop fuel trim. Air charge sensors give the base fuel mass. The EGO signal tells the ECU whether the last combustion was rich or lean. Short-term fuel trim multiplies the base injection time. Long-term trim learns the slow offset. Around λ = 1 the narrowband output changes fast, so the ECU can hold the average close to stoichiometric.

The sensor does not measure AFR directly. It measures oxygen partial pressure in exhaust. Rich exhaust has little oxygen because fuel consumes it. Lean exhaust has excess oxygen. The zirconia cell turns that oxygen difference into a voltage.

Narrowband zirconia EGO sensor

The narrowband zirconia sensor is an oxygen concentration cell. A thimble of yttria-stabilised zirconia separates exhaust gas from reference air. Porous platinum electrodes cover both sides. Above about 300 °C the ceramic conducts oxygen ions. The voltage follows the Nernst equation.

E = (R T / (4 F)) ln(pO<sub>2,ref</sub> / pO<sub>2,exh</sub>)

E is the cell voltage in volts; R = 8.314 J/(mol·K) is the gas constant; T is absolute temperature in K; F = 96485 C/mol is the Faraday constant; pO2,ref is the oxygen partial pressure in reference air; pO2,exh is the oxygen partial pressure in exhaust.

In rich exhaust the oxygen partial pressure on the exhaust side is low, so the cell produces about 0.8–0.9 V. In lean exhaust the two oxygen pressures are closer, so the output falls to about 0.1 V. The transition is steep near λ = 1. The ECU sees a switch, not a proportional AFR. This is why the device is called a switching or narrowband sensor. Some syllabi call it lambda sensor, EGO or HEGO (heated exhaust gas oxygen).

A heater inside the sensor brings it to light-off quickly. The ECU controls the heater with pulse-width modulation, often using a power switch; see 3.8. Thimble sensors use a cup-shaped ceramic. Planar sensors stack ceramic layers and electrodes on a flat strip; they heat faster and are smaller. A protective shield and a porous layer protect the electrodes from liquid water and deposits.

Desirable properties: fast light-off, high sensitivity near λ = 1, low hysteresis, resistance to lead and silicone poisoning, and stable heater control. Hysteresis means the rich-to-lean and lean-to-rich switching voltages differ; low hysteresis gives a cleaner switch. Poisoning reduces the active electrode area and slows response.

Wideband UEGO and titania sensors

The wideband universal exhaust gas oxygen (UEGO) sensor gives a linear λ output. It uses a pump cell and a sensing cell. The ECU pumps oxygen into or out of a small chamber to hold the sensing cell at about 450 mV, which corresponds to λ = 1. The pump current needed is proportional to the oxygen excess or deficit in the exhaust. A conditioning circuit converts that current into a linear voltage for the ECU.

Because the pump current is linear, the ECU can read λ over a wide range, roughly from λ = 0.7 up to free air (λ tending to infinity). Petrol direct-injection and diesel engines use wideband sensors for lean operation and for closed-loop control away from stoichiometric. A wideband sensor is not a narrowband sensor with a different connector; it has extra cells and a controller.

Titania (TiO2) sensors use a different principle. The electrical resistance of titania changes with oxygen partial pressure. They need a heater because resistance is temperature-sensitive. The ECU measures the resistance as a voltage divider. Titania sensors are less common than zirconia but appear in some systems. Their output also switches near λ = 1 in the simple form.

All EGO sensors sit before or after the catalyst. The upstream sensor controls fuel. The downstream sensor checks catalyst oxygen storage. Catalyst chemistry and emission norms belong to Unit 4, Electronic Engine Control.

  • Lead and silicone poisoning reduce response. Do not confuse poisoning with a rich mixture or a faulty heater.
  • Wideband pump current is not the same as the Nernst voltage. The sensing cell is held near 450 mV; the pump current carries the λ information.

3.7 Knock, acceleration and torque sensors

Knock, acceleration, torque and angular-rate sensors turn mechanical vibration and motion into electrical signals. The engine ECU uses a knock sensor to hear detonation and retard spark before damage. Airbag and ESP systems use accelerometers and gyroscopes to detect a crash or a skid. Torque sensors measure shaft twist; LIDAR measures distance by light time of flight; flex-fuel sensors measure ethanol content. These sensors protect the engine and stabilise the vehicle, so they sit in both engine control and safety systems.

You need: 3.1 Sensor basics and signal conditioning; 3.4 Hall and optical position sensing; basic mechanics.

Knock sensor and signal path

Knock is uncontrolled detonation after the spark. It hits the engine block at a frequency set by the combustion chamber, usually in the 5–15 kHz band. A piezoelectric knock sensor bolts to the block. Its crystal makes charge when the block vibrates. Charge sensitivity d is often given in pC/N. A charge amplifier converts this charge to a voltage. A band-pass filter then keeps only the knock band. A rectifier makes an envelope. The ECU opens a window from the crank-angle signal around top dead centre and compares the envelope with a threshold. Above threshold, it retards spark angle. The window stops the ECU reacting to valve or injector noise at other crank angles.

q = d F, V = q / Cf

q is charge (C); d is charge sensitivity (C/N); F is knock force (N); V is output voltage (V); Cf is feedback capacitance (F).

Acceleration and angular-rate sensors

A MEMS accelerometer has a proof mass on silicon springs. Comb fingers on the mass form capacitors with fixed fingers. Acceleration moves the mass, so one capacitance rises and the other falls. The circuit reads the differential capacitance, which is proportional to acceleration. A high-g accelerometer in the airbag system detects a crash pulse. A low-g lateral accelerometer in ESP measures cornering. Some syllabi call the lateral accelerometer and yaw-rate gyro the ESP sensors. A MEMS gyroscope vibrates a proof mass and uses the Coriolis force to measure angular rate. The output is rate in °/s, not angle. The ECU can integrate rate to get angle, but bias and noise make the angle drift. Vehicle stability algorithms combine rate, lateral acceleration, wheel speeds and steering angle. See Unit 5, Electronic stability program (ESP), for the control law.

Torque, distance and fuel composition

Torque sensors measure twist in a shaft. A strain-gauge bridge bonded to the shaft changes resistance with shear strain. A magnetoelastic shaft changes magnetic permeability when twisted; a coil reads the change. A surface-acoustic-wave (SAW) torque sensor uses a wave delay that changes with strain. These sensors help transmission control and electric power steering. A strain-gauge torque sensor on a steering column helps electric power steering apply assist in proportion to driver torque. LIDAR measures distance by time of flight. A light pulse leaves the sensor, reflects from an object and returns. The round-trip distance is c t, so d = c t / 2. Here d is distance (m), c is speed of light (3×10⁸ m/s), and t is round-trip time (s). A flex-fuel sensor estimates ethanol in petrol. Ethanol has a higher dielectric constant than petrol, so a capacitive sensor changes with fuel composition. The ECU then adjusts fuel quantity and spark timing. Detailed ADAS and LIDAR use are in Unit 5.

Worked example

Question. A piezoelectric knock sensor has charge sensitivity d = 2 pC/N. A knock force of 50 N acts on it. The charge amplifier has feedback capacitor Cf = 1000 pF. Find the charge produced and the output voltage.

  1. Given d = 2 pC/N = 2×10⁻¹² C/N.
  2. Given F = 50 N.
  3. q = d F = 2×10⁻¹² C/N × 50 N = 1×10⁻¹⁰ C = 100 pC.
  4. Given Cf = 1000 pF = 1000×10⁻¹² F = 1×10⁻⁹ F.
  5. V = q / Cf = 1×10⁻¹⁰ C / 1×10⁻⁹ F = 0.10 V.

Answer. The charge is 100 pC and the charge-amplifier output is 0.10 V.

Exam traps

  • Do not amplify all engine noise: knock detection uses a band-pass filter around the knock frequency and a crank-angle window.
  • A gyroscope measures angular rate, not angle; integrating rate drifts because of bias and noise.
  • Piezo charge sensitivity is in pC/N, not V/g. A charge amplifier gain is set by Cf; cable capacitance matters little.
  • LIDAR time of flight is round trip: divide c t by 2. Mixing ns and s gives a factor of 10⁹ error.
  • A torque sensor measures shaft torque, not engine brake torque; mounting and shaft material affect sensitivity.

3.8 Actuators: solenoids, relays and stepper motors

An actuator is the hand of the electronic control unit (ECU). The ECU computes a small electrical command; an actuator turns that command into force, motion or switching. Solenoids push or pull a plunger, relays and contactors switch heavy currents, and stepper motors move in precise angular steps. Drivers supply the current, diodes absorb inductive kickback, and PWM controls average current. You meet these devices in fuel injectors, exhaust gas recirculation (EGR) valves, variable valve timing, idle-air control and electric motor actuators. Some syllabi call them output devices.

You need: 3.1 for signal levels and noise, basic transistors, diodes and magnetic circuits, and the ignition coil driver in Unit 2.

Solenoids: current, force and the freewheeling diode

A solenoid is a coil with a movable iron plunger. Current in the coil creates a magnetic field; the field pulls the plunger into the coil. The pull force for an ideal magnetic circuit is

F ≈ μ0 N² I² A / (2 g²)

F is force in newtons (N); μ0 = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m; N is coil turns; I is coil current in A; A is pole face area in m²; g is air gap in m. The force rises with I² and falls quickly as g grows.

At switch-on the air gap is large, so the solenoid needs a pull-in current. After the plunger moves, a smaller hold current keeps it seated. The ECU uses pulse-width modulation (PWM):

I<sub>avg</sub> ≈ D V<sub>b</sub> / R<sub>c</sub>

Iavg is average coil current in A; D is duty cycle from 0 to 1; Vb is battery voltage in V; Rc is coil resistance in Ω.

A low-side driver switches the coil's ground side with a MOSFET (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor); a high-side driver switches the battery side. Both need a freewheeling diode across the coil. When the MOSFET turns off, coil current cannot stop instantly. The diode gives a path, so the stored energy ½ L I² is dissipated safely; L is coil inductance in henries and I is coil current in A. Without it, a high voltage spike can destroy the driver. Fuel injectors, EGR valves and variable valve timing units use this pattern. Ignition coil drivers use the same low-side switch, but deliberately without a freewheeling diode, because the inductive kick is what fires the spark; the switch is protected by other means. Spark generation belongs to Unit 2. A vacuum-operated actuator uses a small solenoid valve to switch vacuum to a diaphragm; the diaphragm then moves a flap or EGR valve.

Relays, contactors and H-bridge motor drive

A relay is an electromagnetic switch. The coil is a solenoid; its plunger closes or opens contacts. A small ECU current controls a much larger load current. The coil still needs a flyback diode, because the driver transistor sees the same inductive kick. A contactor is a heavy-duty relay for high current, often used for battery isolation or a powerful motor. Relay contacts are slow and wear; use a semiconductor driver when fast PWM is needed.

An H-bridge drives a DC motor in both directions. Four switches sit around the motor: two high-side and two low-side. Closing one high-side and the opposite low-side makes current flow one way; the other diagonal reverses it. Never close both switches on the same leg: that is shoot-through, a short circuit from battery to ground. Dead time between changes prevents it. PWM on the high-side switch sets average motor voltage Vavg = D Vb, so speed changes with duty cycle. H-bridges operate throttle actuators, EGR motor valves and other bidirectional motor actuators. A relay can reverse a motor only with extra contacts; an H-bridge does it electronically and quietly.

Stepper motors: steps, modes and limits

A stepper motor moves in fixed angular steps. The stator has several phase windings; the rotor is a permanent magnet or a soft-iron toothed rotor. The driver energises phases in sequence. Each change moves the rotor to the next stable position.

β = 360° / S

β is the step angle in degrees and S is the number of steps per revolution.

In full-step mode, one or two phases are on at a time; a 1.8° motor has 200 steps per revolution. Half-step mode alternates one-phase and two-phase states, giving 0.9°. Microstep mode applies sine and cosine currents to two phases. The rotor then settles between full-step positions; a driver may use 1/8, 1/16 or finer microsteps. Microstepping gives smoother motion and less vibration, but it does not increase holding torque. Holding torque is the torque needed to move the rotor from its energised position.

Stepper motors are open-loop. The ECU sends pulses and assumes the rotor follows. If load torque is too high, or acceleration is too fast, the rotor slips and loses steps. There is no position feedback unless a sensor is added. Torque falls as speed rises because coil inductance limits how fast current can build; the driver needs a higher voltage to force current into the coil quickly. Pull-out torque is the maximum load torque at a given speed. Stepper actuators appear in idle-air control, headlamp levelling and some valve or flap positions. Only a few universities teach microstep current waveforms in detail.

Worked example

Question. A stepper motor has a step angle of 1.8°. The ECU sends 400 steps in 0.8 s. Find the total angle turned, the number of revolutions and the average speed in rev/s and rpm.

  1. Step angle β = 1.8°. Number of steps n = 400. Total angle θ = n × β = 400 × 1.8 = 720°.
  2. Revolutions = θ / 360 = 720 / 360 = 2 revolutions.
  3. Average speed in rev/s = revolutions / time = 2 / 0.8 = 2.5 rev/s.
  4. Average speed in rpm = 2.5 × 60 = 150 rpm.

Answer. The motor turns 720° (2 revolutions) at an average speed of 2.5 rev/s, which is 150 rpm.

Exam traps

  • A relay coil and a solenoid coil are inductors: removing current without a freewheeling diode causes a high voltage spike and can destroy the driver.
  • Pull-in current is larger than hold current. Do not use the pull-in current for continuous holding; the coil overheats.
  • A stepper motor is open-loop. It can lose steps under overload or fast acceleration; counting pulses does not guarantee position.
  • In an H-bridge, switching both transistors on one leg at the same time causes shoot-through. Dead time is required.
  • Microstepping gives smoother motion, not more holding torque. The torque increment gained per microstep is smaller than for a full step; the motor's maximum holding torque stays the same.

3.9 Battery current, voltage and state-of-charge sensing

Battery current and voltage sensing measure the electrical flow into and out of a battery pack. They let the battery management system (BMS) estimate state of charge (SOC), state of health (SOH) and state of function (SOF). Electric vehicles, hybrid cars and stop-start systems use these estimates to protect cells, report range and control charging. Only a few universities teach this chapter. A shunt resistor or Hall sensor measures current; an isolated divider measures voltage; software combines both with temperature.

You need: 3.1 signal conditioning; 3.5 thermistors; Unit 1 battery ratings and charging.

Current sensing with shunt and Hall sensors

Current is the rate of charge flow. One ampere is one coulomb per second. A battery pack can carry hundreds of amperes. A shunt resistor is a low-value resistor placed in series with the pack. The shunt voltage is V_shunt = I × R_shunt, where V_shunt is the voltage in volts, I is the current in amperes and R_shunt is the shunt resistance in ohms. A 0.1 mΩ shunt at 100 A gives 10 mV. The amplifier raises this small signal before the ADC. A Kelvin connection uses separate force and sense terminals so lead resistance does not add to the measured voltage. Shunt resistance changes with temperature, so the BMS compensates using a temperature sensor. A Hall current sensor measures the magnetic field produced by the current. It gives isolation and no insertion loss, which suits high-voltage packs. Its offset and low-current accuracy are weaker. The BMS must also record current direction: discharge is one sign, regenerative braking current is the opposite sign.

Voltage measurement and cell modules

Each cell or module needs a voltage reading. A resistor divider scales a pack voltage down to the ADC range: V_adc = V_pack × R2 / (R1 + R2), where V_adc is the ADC input voltage, V_pack is the pack voltage and R1, R2 are the divider resistors. The divider must have high resistance to avoid draining the pack, but high resistance makes it sensitive to ADC input current. An isolated amplifier or isolated ADC keeps the low-voltage electronics safe from the high-voltage pack. For an n-bit ADC with reference V_ref, one count is V_ref / 2^n. A 12-bit ADC with a 5 V reference has a step of about 1.22 mV. Battery modules often carry a small cell-voltage monitor that sends readings to the BMS. Temperature sensing uses thermistors, as in 3.5. The BMS combines cell voltage, pack current and temperature before it estimates SOC, SOH and SOF.

SOC, SOH, SOF and coulomb counting

State of charge (SOC) is the remaining charge as a fraction of rated capacity. State of health (SOH) describes ageing, usually capacity fade and resistance rise. State of function (SOF) is the power the pack can deliver now at its present SOC and temperature. Coulomb counting integrates current: SOC(t) = SOC(0) − (1/C_n) ∫ I dt, where SOC(t) is the present fraction, SOC(0) is the starting fraction, C_n is the rated capacity in ampere-hours, I is current in amperes with discharge positive, and t is time in hours. In discrete form, ΔSOC = I × Δt / C_n. The estimate drifts because current offset and capacity fade accumulate. The BMS resets it with open-circuit voltage (OCV) after the pack has rested, when cell voltage reflects SOC. OCV is flat in the middle SOC range, so the reset is strongest near empty or full. Current, voltage and thermal sensing together support SOC, SOH and SOF.

Worked example

Question. A 50 Ah battery starts at SOC 80%. It discharges at 10 A for 30 min. Find the charge removed and the new SOC by coulomb counting.

  1. Time Δt = 30 min = 0.5 h.
  2. Charge removed ΔQ = I × Δt = 10 A × 0.5 h = 5 Ah.
  3. SOC drop = ΔQ / C_n = 5 Ah / 50 Ah = 0.10 = 10%.
  4. New SOC = 80% − 10% = 70%.

Answer. 5 Ah is removed and the new SOC is 70%.

Exam traps

  • Coulomb counting drifts because current offset and capacity fade accumulate; reset with OCV after rest.
  • A shunt needs a Kelvin connection and temperature compensation, or lead and shunt resistance errors enter the reading.
  • Regenerative braking reverses current direction; a wrong sign makes SOC rise during discharge.
  • SOC, SOH and SOF are different: SOC is charge now, SOH is ageing, SOF is power capability.

Takeaways

  1. Every sensor chain ends at the ECU as a voltage or a pulse, so signal conditioning and the ADC set the resolution and accuracy you can actually use.
  2. Air charge is measured either directly by a mass air flow sensor or indirectly by a MAP sensor with speed density; the choice changes the fuel calculation.
  3. Crankshaft position sensing must give both angle and speed, and the missing-tooth reference is what fixes cylinder one to top dead centre.
  4. Hall, variable reluctance and optical sensors are all non-contact, so they avoid the wear of a rubbing contact and give a digital pulse.
  5. The oxygen sensor closes the fuel loop: it does not measure fuel, it measures the result and the ECU trims the injection quantity from it.
  6. Knock and angular-rate sensors feed protection and stability functions, not fuel metering, and they work on vibration rather than steady values.
  7. Actuators are the output side of the ECU, and almost all of them are driven by switched current: solenoid, relay, stepper or PWM motor drive.
  8. Battery current and voltage sensing turn a chemical store into numbers the BMS can act on, giving SOC, state of health and state of function.

Glossary

Absolute pressure
Pressure measured with vacuum as the zero reference, so it never goes negative.
Accuracy
How close a measured value is to the true value, usually quoted as a percentage of full scale or an absolute value.
Actuator
A device that turns an electrical command from the ECU into mechanical movement or flow.
BMS
Battery management system; the electronics that measures cell voltage, current and temperature and estimates SOC, SOH and SOF.
Detonation (knock)
Abnormal rapid burning of the end gas in the cylinder, producing a pressure spike and high-frequency block vibration.
EGR valve
Exhaust gas recirculation valve; a solenoid or stepper or vacuum-operated valve that admits exhaust into the intake to lower combustion temperature and NOx.
Filter
A circuit or routine that removes unwanted frequency content, such as a low-pass filter to reject ignition noise before the ADC.
Flyback diode
A diode across an inductive load that gives the collapsing coil current a path, protecting the driving switch.
Fuel trim
The percentage correction the ECU applies to injection quantity from oxygen sensor feedback; short term is fast, long term is learned.
Gauge pressure
Pressure measured relative to the surrounding atmosphere, so it can be positive or negative.
Gyroscope
An angular-rate sensor, usually vibrating MEMS, whose output voltage is proportional to rotation rate about an axis.
Hysteresis
The difference in output when the same input is reached from increasing and from decreasing direction.
Injector
An electromagnetic valve that opens for a commanded time to spray a measured fuel quantity.
Lambda
The ratio of actual air-fuel ratio to the stoichiometric ratio; lambda = 1 means chemically correct.
LIDAR
Light detection and ranging; a pulsed laser and detector that measures distance to objects from time of flight, used in driver assistance.
Linearity
How closely the sensor output follows a straight line against input over its working range.
Relay
An electromagnetically operated switch that lets a small ECU current control a large load current.
Resolution
The smallest change in input that the sensor and ADC can distinguish, set by bit count and reference voltage.
RTD
Resistance temperature detector, usually platinum, with a small and nearly linear positive resistance change with temperature.
Sensitivity
The change in output for a unit change in input, for example millivolts per kilopascal for a MAP sensor.
State of charge
Remaining charge in a battery as a percentage of its rated capacity.
State of function
The power the battery can actually deliver or accept now, at the present charge and temperature.
State of health
The present capacity and internal resistance of an aged battery compared with its new condition.
Thermistor
A semiconductor resistor whose resistance changes strongly with temperature; NTC types fall in resistance as temperature rises.
Thermocouple
Two dissimilar metals joined at a hot junction, producing a small voltage proportional to the temperature difference between junctions.
Wheatstone bridge
Four resistances in a diamond, excited by a supply, giving a near-zero output at balance and a small differential voltage when one arm changes.

  • Robert Bosch GmbH, Automotive Handbook: Reference sections on sensors, ignition and fuel injection systems.
  • Tom Denton, Automotive Electrical and Electronic Systems: Chapters on sensors, actuators and engine management.
  • Ronald K. Jurgen (editor), Automotive Electronics Handbook: Chapters on powertrain sensors and electronic control.
