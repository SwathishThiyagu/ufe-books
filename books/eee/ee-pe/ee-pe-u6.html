6.1 Uninterruptible power supplies (UPS)

A UPS keeps a load alive when the mains fails. Mains normally feeds a rectifier or charger, which holds a DC bus with a battery. An inverter turns that DC into AC for the load. In the on-line type the load always runs from the inverter, so a mains failure is invisible. UPS systems protect computers, hospital monitors, telecom towers, railway signalling and payment servers. This chapter compares off-line, line-interactive and on-line UPS topologies, explains transfer time, VA rating versus watts, output waveform quality, and battery sizing for a required backup time.

You need: Unit 3 for the charger stage and switched-mode power supplies, and Unit 4 for the full-bridge inverter and sinusoidal PWM; charger details are in 6.2.

Off-line, line-interactive and on-line UPS

An off-line UPS, also called standby, leaves the load on the mains in normal operation. The charger keeps the battery full and the inverter is off. When mains fails, a static transfer switch moves the load to the inverter. Transfer time is typically 4 to 10 ms. Some syllabi call this the standby type.

A line-interactive UPS adds a tap-changing transformer or buck-boost converter that corrects mains voltage while the load stays on the mains. The same inverter/charger charges the battery when mains is present. On mains failure it also switches the load to the inverter, with a transfer time of a few milliseconds, shorter than the off-line type because the inverter is already running and regulating. An on-line UPS, also called double conversion, rectifies the mains to a DC bus, holds a battery on that bus, and runs an inverter continuously into the load. The load never sees the mains directly. Mains failure changes the DC bus source from the charger to the battery, so there is no transfer time.

A static bypass is a fast switch, usually thyristors, from mains to load for inverter overload or fault. A maintenance bypass isolates the UPS for service. Ferro-resonant and rotary UPS exist for special cases; most data-centre UPS are on-line.

VA rating, waveform and battery sizing

UPS rating is in VA because inverter and transformer heating depends on current, and load current depends on apparent power S. Real power P = S × cos φ, where φ is the load power factor. A 1 kVA UPS with a 0.8 PF load can supply 800 W; with a 0.6 PF load it can supply only 600 W. Output THD = √(V₂² + V₃² + V₄² + …) ÷ V₁ × 100%, where V₁ is the fundamental RMS voltage and V₂, V₃, … are the harmonic RMS voltages. Below 3% is good for sensitive loads. Crest factor is peak value divided by RMS value. SMPS loads draw narrow pulses with high crest factor, so a UPS must supply that peak current without clipping.

S = P / cos φ; P_dc = P / η; E = V_bat × Ah × DoD; t = E / P_dc

S is apparent power in VA; P is real load power in W; cos φ is load power factor; P_dc is battery-side DC power in W; η is inverter efficiency; V_bat is nominal battery voltage in V; Ah is battery capacity in ampere-hours; DoD is depth of discharge as a fraction; E is usable energy in Wh; t is backup time in hours.

Battery sizing uses usable energy, not the printed Ah alone. The charger must also replace the energy taken during an outage. Ageing, low temperature and high discharge current reduce the delivered Ah, so field designs add margin. For grid-tied battery interfaces, see 6.6.

Power supplies and power conditioners

A linear supply steps down and rectifies the mains, then drops the excess voltage across a series transistor; it is clean but bulky and low efficiency. An SMPS chops at high frequency, so it is small and efficient, but it draws pulsed current with high crest factor and injects harmonics. That crest factor is what matters for UPS sizing: the UPS must supply the peak current of the SMPS load without clipping or tripping. A UPS is an AC power supply with stored energy. It also acts as a power conditioner, regulating voltage, filtering spikes and holding frequency. A power conditioner may only filter and regulate, with no battery. Choose UPS VA from load VA, not watts alone, and add margin for startup.

Worked example

Question. A 1 kVA on-line UPS feeds a 640 W load at 0.8 lagging power factor. The battery is 48 V, 100 Ah, the inverter efficiency is 90%, and the battery may be taken down to 80% of its capacity. Find the DC current drawn from the battery and the backup time.

  1. Load apparent power: S = P / cos φ = 640 / 0.8 = 800 VA. The 1 kVA UPS rating is greater than 800 VA, so the load fits.
  2. Inverter DC power: P_dc = P / η = 640 / 0.9 = 711.1 W.
  3. Battery DC current: I = P_dc / V_bat = 711.1 / 48 = 14.8 A.
  4. Usable battery energy: E = V_bat × Ah × DoD = 48 × 100 × 0.8 = 3840 Wh.
  5. Backup time: t = E / P_dc = 3840 / 711.1 = 5.4 h.

Answer. The battery supplies about 14.8 A at 48 V, and the usable energy gives about 5.4 hours of backup.

Exam traps

  • Multiplying VA by hours and calling it stored energy. Apparent power must pass through the load power factor and the inverter efficiency before it becomes battery watts.
  • Using battery Ah × voltage as backup time without depth of discharge and inverter efficiency. A 100 Ah battery is not 100 Ah of usable energy.
  • Choosing a UPS only by watts. The UPS VA rating must exceed load VA, because current heats the inverter and transformer.
  • Thinking every UPS has the same transfer time. Off-line and line-interactive types switch the load; on-line double conversion does not transfer the load on mains failure.
  • Ignoring crest factor. A low-THD UPS can still trip on an SMPS load that draws high peak current.

6.2 Battery chargers and emergency lighting

A battery charger is a controlled DC source that pushes current into a battery along a set profile. It does not simply connect a transformer and a diode; it regulates current, then voltage, and stops at the right point. The same switching devices and sensing circuits run emergency lamps, light dimmers and simple timers. You meet them in phone chargers, inverter packs, stairwell lights and theatre exit signs. This chapter shows the profiles, the regulator loops and the changeover that keeps a lamp alive when the mains fails.

You need Unit 2 for rectifiers and filters, Unit 3 for buck and flyback converters, and Unit 5 for phase-control switching; the charger–battery chain also appears in 6.1.

Charging methods and C-rate

A charger is a controlled DC source. It sets current and voltage along a charging profile. The profile depends on cell chemistry, temperature and duty. Phone chargers, inverter packs and forklift chargers use the same ideas at different power levels.

C-rate scales current to capacity. For a battery of capacity C Ah, nC means n × C amperes. A 100 Ah battery at 0.1C takes 10 A, not 100 A. Ideally 0.1C fills or empties it in about 10 h.

Charge efficiency η is Ah output divided by Ah input. Lead-acid is about 0.90. You push in more Ah than you took out. The charge factor is F = Ahin / Ahout = 1/η. To replace 50 Ah at η = 0.90, push in 50/0.90 = 55.6 Ah.

  • Trickle: a small current, often C/100, holds a full battery against self-discharge.
  • Constant current (CC): fixed current while voltage rises.
  • Constant voltage (CV): fixed voltage while current falls.
  • CC-CV: CC to a voltage limit, then CV until current tapers to a termination value.
  • Float versus boost: float is lower standby voltage; boost is higher cycle voltage. A 12 V lead-acid block often floats at 13.5–13.8 V and boosts at 14.4 V.
  • Pulse charging: current pulses with rest gaps; it reduces polarization but needs control.

Temperature changes the safe voltage. The charger senses battery temperature and reduces or stops charge when hot. Overcharge causes gassing, heat and short life. Termination can be a timer, a current threshold, a voltage drop (dV/dt) or a temperature limit.

Inside the charging regulator

Three power stages are common. A series pass regulator puts a transistor between rectified DC and the battery. It drops excess voltage, so it is simple and quiet but wastes (Vin − Vbatt) × I as heat. A buck converter chops and filters; it is efficient for step-down. A flyback gives isolation from mains.

Sensing closes the loops. A current-sense resistor Rs converts current to voltage: Vs = I × Rs. Rs is small, often milliohms, so it wastes little power. An amplifier raises Vs. A divider senses battery voltage: Vsense = Vbatt × R2/(R1 + R2).

P<sub>loss</sub> = (V<sub>in</sub> − V<sub>batt</sub>) × I

P_loss is heat in W; V_in is input DC in V; V_batt is battery voltage in V; I is charge current in A.

  1. Rectify and filter the mains; see Unit 2.
  2. Measure battery current and voltage.
  3. Compare both with references; the lower demand wins, so CC runs until the voltage limit, then CV.
  4. Drive the pass device with a linear signal or PWM duty cycle.
  5. Stop at termination; protect against reverse polarity, overcurrent and over-temperature.

A charge controller IC does these steps. A small emergency light may use a linear regulator with a timer. In a UPS, the charger holds a large battery on a DC bus.

Emergency lighting, dimmers and timers

An emergency lighting fitting has a lamp, a battery, a charger and a changeover circuit. A maintained fitting keeps its lamp on during normal mains and on battery. A non-maintained fitting keeps the lamp off until mains fails. Exit signs in a cinema are often maintained; a stairwell lamp may be non-maintained.

An inverter tube pack drives a fluorescent tube from a low-voltage battery. The charger is a rectifier plus regulator. With mains present, a relay or static switch keeps the inverter off and charges the battery. On failure, changeover starts the inverter. LED (light-emitting diode) emergency drivers use a constant-current supply; a battery and boost converter take over on mains failure. Three-hour duration is common for escape lighting.

Changeover can be a relay or a static switch. Transfer must be fast enough that the lamp does not extinguish. A test button breaks the mains input to simulate failure. A self-test checks battery voltage under load, not just open-circuit voltage. A weak battery can read 12 V with no load and collapse under a lamp. Over-discharge protection cuts the lamp before damage.

A phase-control dimmer switches the AC wave partway through each half-cycle. A TRIAC is a bidirectional thyristor switch. A DIAC is a bidirectional trigger diode. A DIAC and an RC network generate the gate pulse. The firing angle α is measured from the zero crossing. A later α gives a shorter conduction angle and a lower root-mean-square (RMS) voltage. This is the ordinary fan regulator and incandescent dimmer. Phase control creates harmonics and radio noise, so a filter is needed. The switching waveforms belong to Unit 5.

An electronic timer uses a 555 IC or a comparator. In monostable mode the output pulse is t = 1.1 R C. R is in ohms, C in farads, t in seconds. A zero-crossing detector synchronizes the timer to the mains. The timer drives a relay, a TRIAC or a solid-state relay. Zero-crossing switching reduces electromagnetic interference (EMI) because current starts at zero.

Worked example

Question. A 12 V, 100 Ah lead-acid battery is at 50% depth of discharge. It is charged at 0.1C in constant current up to 14.4 V, then held at 14.4 V until the current falls to 2 A. Charge efficiency is 90% and the taper takes 2 h. Find the constant-current time, the total charge time and the charger output power at the start of the constant-voltage stage.

  1. Capacity C = 100 Ah. 50% depth of discharge removes 0.50 × 100 = 50 Ah.
  2. 0.1C = 0.1 × 100 = 10 A.
  3. η = 0.90, so Ah to push in = 50 / 0.90 = 55.56 Ah ≈ 55.6 Ah.
  4. CC time = 55.56 / 10 = 5.56 h ≈ 5.6 h.
  5. Taper = 2 h, total = 5.56 + 2 = 7.56 h ≈ 7.6 h.
  6. At start of CV, I = 10 A, V = 14.4 V, P = 14.4 × 10 = 144 W.

Answer. Constant-current time 5.6 h, total charge time 7.6 h, and charger output power at the start of CV 144 W.

Exam traps

  • Reading 0.1C as 100 A for a 100 Ah battery. 0.1C is 10 A.
  • Forgetting charge efficiency. A 50 Ah discharge needs about 55.6 Ah of charge at 90% efficiency.
  • Mixing float and boost. Float is the lower standby voltage; boost is the higher cycle voltage.
  • Thinking the current is zero at the start of CV. It is still the CC value, then it tapers.
  • In a dimmer, thinking a later firing angle raises the RMS voltage. It lowers it.

6.3 Induction heating

Induction heating passes a high-frequency current through a coil and lets the metal part heat itself. Eddy currents and hysteresis inside the workpiece convert the coupled magnetic energy into heat, so the coil stays cooler than the charge. The method is clean, fast and local: it melts steel in a crucible, softens a billet for forging, hardens a gear surface, and boils a pan on an induction cooktop. Only a few universities teach its design as a full topic. This chapter shows the skin-depth choice, the transformer model and the inverter that drives the coil.

You need: inverters from Unit 4 and resonant switching from Unit 3, resonant converters.

Heat is made inside the metal

A work coil carries a high-frequency AC current and creates an alternating magnetic field. The metal to be heated, the workpiece, sits inside this field. The field induces eddy currents in the workpiece; its resistance turns those currents into heat. In magnetic steel, hysteresis also adds heat on every magnetisation cycle. The coil itself stays cooler because its resistance is small and it is water-cooled.

δ = √(ρ / (π f μ))

δ is skin depth in m; ρ is workpiece resistivity in Ω·m; f is coil current frequency in Hz; μ is workpiece permeability in H/m, equal to μ_r μ_0.

At 50 Hz, steel δ is centimetres. At 10 kHz it is a few millimetres. Choose f so δ is smaller than the billet radius; then current crowds near the surface and heats it fast. The coil and workpiece act as a transformer with a shorted single-turn secondary. The workpiece reflects a resistance R_ref into the coil; P ≈ I_coil² R_ref. Magnetising inductance makes the coil current lag, so power factor is low and a capacitor is added.

Resonant inverter and power control

A resonant inverter converts DC to the coil frequency. In a series load-resonant inverter, the coil and a capacitor form a series resonant tank; the current is near sinusoidal and the inverter switches near zero current. A parallel load-resonant inverter puts the capacitor across the coil and gives a near-sinusoidal coil voltage. See resonant converters for the switching ideas.

Power is set by frequency or DC link. Above resonance the tank is inductive; moving f closer to resonance raises current and power. Changing DC link voltage V_d changes coil voltage and power. Control must track load changes: cold steel, hot steel, and steel above the Curie point all have different μ and R_ref. Coupling gap and coil turns also change R_ref; poor coupling lowers efficiency.

Design steps for a billet or crucible

  1. Find metal power: P_metal = m c ΔT / t. Add melting or latent heat if the charge melts.
  2. Divide by overall efficiency η (often 0.5 to 0.7) to get inverter output P_inv = P_metal / η.
  3. Choose f from the skin-depth formula: make δ about one-third to one-half of the billet radius. Small parts need 10–100 kHz; large billets use 1–10 kHz.
  4. Choose coil turns N and voltage V_coil; coil current I_coil = P_inv / (V_coil × pf). Use the actual pf from the resonant tank, not 1.
  5. Water-cool the coil: copper losses I_coil² R_coil become heat; keep coolant flow enough to hold coil temperature.
  6. Check coupling, Curie point and duty cycle; tune the tank capacitor at the working frequency.

Worked example

Question. A steel billet of diameter 50 mm and length 100 mm is heated from 20 °C to 900 °C in 60 s. Density 7850 kg/m³, specific heat 500 J/(kg·K), overall efficiency 60%, coil voltage 400 V at 0.3 power factor. Find the inverter output power and the coil current.

  1. Volume V = π d² / 4 × L = π(0.050)² / 4 × 0.100 = 1.963 × 10⁻⁴ m³.
  2. Mass m = ρV = 7850 × 1.963 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.541 kg.
  3. Temperature rise ΔT = 900 − 20 = 880 K.
  4. Energy E = m c ΔT = 1.541 × 500 × 880 = 6.78191 × 10⁵ J.
  5. Metal power P_metal = E / t = 6.78191 × 10⁵ / 60 = 11,303 W.
  6. Inverter output P_inv = P_metal / η = 11,303 / 0.60 = 18,839 W ≈ 18.8 kW.
  7. Coil current I_coil = P_inv / (V_coil × pf) = 18,839 / (400 × 0.30) = 157 A.

Answer. The inverter must deliver about 18.8 kW (18,839 W), and the coil carries about 157 A.

Exam traps

  • Using the 50 Hz skin depth for steel; at 10 kHz the depth is a few millimetres, which is why the frequency is raised at all.
  • Thinking the coil makes most of the heat; the coil is water-cooled and the heat is in the workpiece.
  • Forgetting the reflected resistance and low power factor; coil current is not P/V, it is P/(V × pf).
  • Treating the Curie point as a fixed number; above it μ_r falls sharply and δ, R_ref and power all change.
  • Designing the tank at one load only; cold, hot and molten workpieces change the resonant frequency and coupling.

6.4 HVDC transmission

HVDC transmission sends bulk power as direct current between two converter stations. At one end a rectifier turns AC into DC, the line carries DC with only a small ripple, and at the other end an inverter turns it back into AC. With no charging current and no reactive voltage drop, DC wins on very long overhead routes, on undersea cables and between grids that are not synchronised. Power flow is set by converter control, not by the angle between two AC systems. Links run between regions and from offshore wind farms to shore.

You need: three-phase controlled rectifiers and the effect of source inductance (see Unit 2, Controlled and Uncontrolled Rectifiers), PWM bridges with IGBTs (see Unit 4, DC-AC Inverters), and device ratings from Unit 1.

Why DC instead of AC

An AC line has series inductance and shunt capacitance. On a long line the capacitance draws charging current, so part of the conductor current carries no useful power and the receiving-end voltage rises at light load. An AC submarine cable is worse: its capacitance is large and the whole length carries charging current, so AC sea crossings are limited to a few tens of kilometres.

A DC line carries only load current; the loss is I²R. For the same power DC needs two conductors instead of three, or one conductor with an earth return, so the cost per kilometre falls. Beyond a break-even distance, hundreds of kilometres for overhead lines, DC costs less overall even though the terminal stations cost more.

DC also separates the two grids. Rectifier and inverter need not stay in step, so a link joins systems of different frequency or systems not synchronised at all. Some back-to-back HVDC stations do this inside one country. Power flow is set by converter control, not by the angle between two voltage phasors, so it can be held at any value and reversed in a fraction of a second.

The line-commutated converter station

The classic station uses thyristors in a 12-pulse bridge (see Unit 2 for the three-phase controlled rectifier). A thyristor turns on by gate pulse but turns off only when its current falls to zero, so the AC line voltage itself must push current from one valve to the next. This is line commutation, and it means the station must sit on a strong AC network.

  • Converter transformer: star-star and star-delta secondaries, giving the 30° shift and the insulation against DC.
  • Two 6-pulse bridges 30° apart make one 12-pulse converter: the 5th and 7th AC-side harmonics cancel, so the lowest AC-side harmonic is the 11th and the DC ripple is at the 12th and 24th.
  • AC filters: tuned branches for the 11th, 13th and higher, plus a high-pass branch; they also supply part of the reactive power.
  • Smoothing reactor: a series inductor in the DC line that keeps the DC current continuous at light load and slows the rise of fault current.
  • Ground electrode or metallic return, and surge arresters across valves and transformers.

Reactive power: a line-commutated bridge absorbs reactive power at both ends. A working figure is Q ≈ 0.5 P_d, about half the DC power, at the rectifier and at the inverter. Filters and capacitors supply part of it and synchronous condensers the rest, so a weak AC system cannot feed a line-commutated station.

Commutation failure: the inverter valve that is turning off must be given time, the extinction angle γ, to regain its blocking ability. A dip in AC voltage or a jump in DC current steals that time, the current fails to transfer, and the bridge briefly short-circuits the DC terminal so that DC voltage collapses. The link recovers within a few cycles once AC voltage returns, so one event is not a permanent trip.

VSC-HVDC and the pole arrangements

A voltage-source converter station uses IGBT bridges with PWM (see Unit 4, DC-AC Inverters). An IGBT turns off on command, so the bridge does not need the AC line to commutate. It can therefore feed a passive network and black-start a dead grid after a blackout, work into a weak grid, and control active and reactive power independently. It reverses power by reversing the DC current while keeping the DC voltage polarity fixed, which suits extruded cable insulation. Losses are a little higher than for LCC and station ratings were traditionally smaller, but VSC dominates new cable links.

P_d = V_d × I_d

P_d is the total DC power in watts; V_d is the DC voltage in volts and I_d the line current in amperes. For a bipolar link V_d is the pole-to-pole voltage, twice the pole-to-ground value, and I_d is the current in one pole, so P_d = (2 × pole-to-ground voltage) × pole current. For a monopolar link V_d is the pole-to-ground voltage and I_d the conductor current, and the same product still holds.

Arrangements. Monopolar: one conductor, with return through earth or through a metallic return conductor. Bipolar: two poles, one at +V_d/2 and one at −V_d/2 to earth, each carrying half the power, and in balanced operation the earth current is nearly zero. If a pole trips, the healthy pole keeps running in monopolar mode with ground return, so half the power stays on. The ground electrode must have low resistance and is sited carefully for environmental reasons. A DC network with more than two stations is called MTDC.

Worked example

Question. A ±500 kV bipolar HVDC link transmits 2000 MW. Find (a) the DC current in each pole, (b) the power still delivered if one pole trips and the return is through ground, and (c) the reactive power each line-commutated station draws at 50% of DC power.

  1. The link is bipolar at ±500 kV, so the pole-to-pole DC voltage is V_d = 2 × 500 kV = 1000 kV = 1000 × 10³ V.
  2. Total DC power is P_d = 2000 MW = 2000 × 10⁶ W, and P_d = V_d × I_d.
  3. So I_d = 2000 × 10⁶ / (1000 × 10³) = 2000 A in each pole. Check: each pole carries 500 kV × 2000 A = 1000 MW, and two poles give 2000 MW.
  4. With one pole out, the remaining pole runs at 500 kV to ground and carries the same 2000 A: P = 500 × 10³ × 2000 = 1000 × 10⁶ W = 1000 MW.
  5. At 50% of DC power the DC power is only 1000 MW, so each station draws Q ≈ 0.5 × 1000 MW = 500 MVAr. At the full 2000 MW each station would draw about 1000 MVAr.

Answer. The pole current is 2000 A, the link still delivers 1000 MW in monopolar ground-return operation, and at 50% power each station draws about 500 MVAr (about 1000 MVAr each at full power).

Exam traps

  • Putting the pole-to-ground voltage into P_d = V_d I_d for a bipolar link. The formula uses the pole-to-pole voltage, twice the pole-to-ground value, with the current of one pole.
  • Assuming the inverter generates reactive power while the rectifier absorbs it. Both line-commutated stations absorb reactive power, roughly half the DC power each; the filters only supply part of it.
  • Treating a commutation failure as a permanent fault. It is a temporary short circuit across the DC terminal, and the link recovers within a few cycles after the AC voltage returns.
  • Swapping the reversal mechanisms: an LCC reverses DC voltage polarity at constant current direction, while a VSC reverses current direction at constant voltage polarity.
  • Believing an HVDC line has charging current or a reactive voltage drop. It has neither, and that is exactly why DC suits long overhead routes and cables.

6.5 Static VAR compensators and FACTS

A static VAR compensator (SVC) is a shunt power-electronics circuit that changes reactive power in milliseconds to hold a bus voltage steady. It exists because transmission lines and loads need reactive power, called vars: too little leaves voltage low and limits power transfer; too much raises voltage. Thyristors switch reactors and capacitors quickly, with no mechanical breaker. SVCs sit at weak 33 kV, 132 kV and 400 kV buses, at HVDC terminals and near arc furnaces. The wider family, FACTS (flexible AC transmission systems), adds series and combined converters that raise line capacity and steer power flow.

You need Unit 5 for thyristor AC controllers, Unit 4 for voltage-source converters and Unit 2 for converter reactive-power demand; HVDC transmission shows where that var demand appears.

Why reactive power sets voltage and transfer

Reactive power Q, measured in var, kvar or MVar, is the power that swings between a magnetic field or an electric field and the source. It does no useful work, but it sets voltage. An inductive load draws lagging current and absorbs Q, so the line drops more voltage. A capacitor draws leading current and supplies Q, which raises voltage.

P = (V_S V_R / X) sin δ

P is active power in watts (W); V_S and V_R are sending-end and receiving-end phase-to-phase voltages in volts (V); X is line series reactance in ohms (Ω); δ is the angle between the two voltages in radians. For small δ, P ≈ V_S V_R δ / X.

An SVC is a controlled shunt susceptance B_SVC. It injects reactive current I_Q = B_SVC V into the bus. B_SVC is in siemens (S); positive means leading current (capacitive) and negative means lagging current (inductive). The controller measures bus voltage and changes B_SVC so voltage returns to its set point. Response takes one or two cycles, far faster than a mechanical capacitor bank.

At a weak bus, this support raises V_R in the formula, so the same line carries more P.

TCR, TSC and the SVC characteristic

A thyristor-controlled reactor (TCR) is a fixed reactor L in series with two anti-parallel thyristors. The firing angle α is measured from the positive-going zero crossing of the bus voltage. At α = 90° the thyristors conduct for the whole half cycle; at α = 180° they do not conduct. The fundamental susceptance is:

B_L(α) = [2(π − α) + sin 2α] / (π X_L)

B_L(α) is the fundamental susceptance of the TCR in siemens (S); α is the firing angle in radians, between π/2 and π; X_L = ωL is the reactor reactance in ohms (Ω); ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s; L is inductance in henry (H).

With a fixed capacitor C in parallel, the SVC susceptance is B_SVC = B_C − B_L(α), where B_C = ωC. The TCR absorbs lagging vars and the capacitor supplies leading vars; the net is their difference. At α = 90° the TCR absorbs its maximum, so the SVC is at its most lagging setting. At α near 180° the TCR is off, so the capacitor makes the SVC leading.

  • TCR + fixed capacitor (TCR+FC): continuous control from leading to lagging within rating.
  • Thyristor-switched capacitor (TSC): capacitor connected in steps; leading vars only, no continuous control from one unit.
  • Thyristor-switched reactor (TSR): reactor connected in steps; lagging vars only.

The V-I characteristic shows the control range. Inside it, bus voltage stays nearly constant while reactive current changes. At the leading limit the SVC acts like a fixed capacitor, so its current falls when voltage falls. At the lagging limit it acts like a fixed reactor. The rating is the maximum continuous MVar at a stated bus voltage, with a short-time overload.

STATCOM, series devices and active filters

A STATCOM is a voltage-source converter (VSC) behind a coupling reactor. It controls the magnitude and phase of its AC output voltage. If the converter voltage is higher than the bus voltage, leading current flows and the STATCOM supplies vars; if lower, it absorbs vars. The converter can hold current up to its rating while the bus voltage sags, so its V-I curve is current-limited. An SVC of the same MVar rating gives less reactive current at low voltage because it behaves as a fixed susceptance. Unit 4 covers the VSC and its current control.

  • TCSC: a thyristor-controlled reactor in parallel with a series capacitor; it changes the line's effective series reactance, raising the transfer limit and damping oscillations.
  • SSSC: a series VSC injects a voltage in quadrature with line current; it behaves like a controllable series reactance.
  • UPFC: shunt and series converters share a DC link; the series converter controls line active and reactive power, while the shunt converter supplies active power and vars.

An active harmonic filter is a shunt VSC that measures the load current, extracts its harmonic components, and injects equal and opposite harmonic currents. The supply then sees a nearly sinusoidal current. The same converter can supply vars, so it also works as a STATCOM.

At an HVDC terminal, line-commutated converters absorb a large fraction of their active power as vars; see HVDC transmission. An SVC or STATCOM on the AC bus supplies those vars, holds the bus voltage and supports commutation after a fault.

Worked example

Question. A 33 kV, 50 Hz bus has a TCR with reactor reactance X_L = 10 Ω per phase. The firing angle is α = 120°. Using B_L(α) = [2(π − α) + sin 2α] / (π X_L), find the fundamental TCR current per phase and the reactive power absorbed. Repeat for α = 90°.

  1. Convert α = 120° to radians: α = 120 × π / 180 = 2.094 rad. Then π − α = 3.142 − 2.094 = 1.047 rad, and 2α = 4.189 rad.
  2. sin 2α = sin 240° = −0.866. The numerator is 2(1.047) + (−0.866) = 2.094 − 0.866 = 1.228.
  3. B_L(120°) = 1.228 / (π × 10) = 1.228 / 31.416 = 0.0391 S.
  4. The phase voltage is V_phase = 33 kV / √3 = 19.05 kV = 19052 V.
  5. The fundamental current is I = B_L V_phase = 0.0391 × 19052 = 745 A = 0.745 kA.
  6. The three-phase reactive power is Q = √3 V_LL I = 1.732 × 33 kV × 0.745 kA = 42.6 MVAr.
  7. For α = 90° = π/2 rad: π − α = 1.571 rad, 2(π − α) = 3.142 rad, and sin 2α = sin 180° = 0. So B_L(90°) = 3.142 / (π × 10) = 0.1 S.
  8. At α = 90°, I = 0.1 × 19052 = 1905 A = 1.905 kA, and Q = √3 × 33 × 1.905 = 108.9 MVAr.

Answer. At α = 120°, B_L = 0.0391 S, I = 0.745 kA and Q = 42.6 MVAr; at α = 90°, B_L = 0.1 S, I = 1.905 kA and Q = 108.9 MVAr.

Exam traps

  • Taking the TCR output as the SVC output. The TCR only absorbs vars; the fixed capacitor supplies leading vars; the net SVC output is the difference B_C − B_L(α).
  • Putting α in degrees inside the formula. Convert to radians first. Also keep the sign of sin 2α: at α = 120°, sin 240° = −0.866, not +0.866.
  • Mixing SVC and STATCOM V-I behaviour. An SVC at its limit looks like a fixed susceptance, so current falls with voltage. A STATCOM holds current up to its limit, so it gives more reactive current at low voltage.
  • Calling a TSC continuously variable. A single TSC switches a capacitor in steps; continuous control needs a TCR or several TSC steps.
  • Treating UPFC as a shunt-only SVC. A UPFC has a shunt converter and a series converter, and the series converter is what controls line power flow.

6.6 Renewable energy and battery storage interfacing

A grid-tied converter links DC sources such as solar panels and batteries to the AC grid. It boosts the panel voltage, holds a DC bus, and inverts DC into sinusoidal current matched to the grid in phase, frequency and amplitude. The inverter controller sets power factor, harmonics, and power direction. This chapter covers the PV curve and maximum power point, hysteresis current control, real and reactive power from angle and voltage, and battery storage with its management system and converter.

You need Unit 4 (grid-tied inverters and PWM), Unit 3 (boost and bidirectional converters) and Unit 2 (power-factor-corrected rectifiers); 6.1 shows the same DC bus with a battery.

The PV curve and the maximum power point

A PV cell is a current source in parallel with a diode; sunlight sets the current. Short circuit: V = 0, I = Isc. Open circuit: I = 0, V = Voc. Power P = V × I, where V is terminal voltage in volts, I current in amperes, P power in watts. P is zero at both ends and peaks once in the knee: the maximum power point (MPP).

Irradiance moves current almost in proportion. Cell temperature moves voltage the other way, so a hot panel has lower Voc. The MPP slides all day, so the converter chases it.

Perturb and observe (P&O): measure V and I, compute P, step the boost duty, measure again, compare. If power rose, step same way; if it fell, reverse. It hunts around the MPP by one step but needs no panel model.

Two arrangements: two-stage has a boost MPPT stage onto a DC bus plus a separate inverter; single-stage lets the inverter do MPPT and accept a wider input window. Indian rooftop systems are mostly two-stage.

Hysteresis current control

Hysteresis control makes inverter current follow a sine. Build reference iref(t) = Im sin(ωt) from grid phase and wanted power: Im is peak reference in amperes, ω = 2πf in rad/s, f grid frequency (50 Hz in India). Compare measured i with band of width h amperes: iref + h/2 and iref − h/2.

  1. i climbs above iref + h/2: switch the leg so inductor voltage reverses and i falls.
  2. i drops below iref − h/2: switch the other way and let i rise.
  3. Repeat. The current stays in the band: a sine with small triangular ripple.

f_s = (V_dc² − v_g²) / (2 h L V_dc)

f_s is switching frequency in Hz; V_dc the DC bus voltage in volts; v_g the instantaneous grid voltage in volts; h the hysteresis band in amperes; L the filter inductance in henries. A full bridge is assumed.

The switching frequency varies: highest near v_g = 0, lowest near the sine peak. Design the filter at the lowest frequency. Switching loss is worst at the highest frequency, near v_g = 0. Conduction loss and device current stress peak near the sine peak because current is largest there. Direction comes from the sign of iref. Take current positive from converter into grid: a reference in phase with v_g exports real power (inversion); a reference 180° out of phase draws real power (rectification at unity power factor). The sign of the reference sets direction. One circuit charges and discharges a battery. Merits: fast, robust, no carrier, small steady-state error. Faults: swinging frequency complicates the filter and audible noise; loss varies through the cycle.

Real and reactive power from angle and voltage

Model the inverter as sinusoidal source E behind coupling reactance X feeding stiff grid V. E and V are rms phase voltages in volts, X in ohms mostly filter inductor, δ the angle by which E leads V.

P = (E V / X) sin δ        Q = (E V cos δ − V²) / X

P is real power per phase in watts; Q reactive power per phase in var; E the inverter fundamental phase voltage in volts; V the grid phase voltage in volts; X coupling reactance in ohms; δ load angle in radians.

Signs: δ > 0 gives P > 0, power leaves inverter (battery discharges or PV exports). δ < 0 gives P < 0, power enters (battery charges). For small δ, cos δ ≈ 1 and Q ≈ (E − V)V / X, so amplitude difference sets Q: E above V supplies reactive power, E below V absorbs it.

This turns a dispatch order such as “export 3 kW at unity power factor” into two references, δ and E. An outer loop sets them; an inner current loop, often hysteresis, forces current. Unity-power-factor rectification means δ slightly negative and E trimmed until Q = 0, that is E cos δ = V; current sits in phase with grid voltage and only real power is drawn.

Battery storage: cells, BMS and the power conversion system

  • Battery pack: cells in series and parallel give DC voltage and Ah. State of charge (SoC) is percentage capacity left; depth of discharge (DoD) is fraction used. Lead-acid is kept near 50% DoD; lithium goes deeper. A lithium pack returns roughly 90% of energy put in.
  • BMS: measures every cell voltage plus pack temperature and current, bleeds charge between cells, estimates SoC, and opens contactor on over-voltage, over-current or over-temperature. It protects cells; it does not control power.
  • PCS: bidirectional converter that charges and discharges to the DC bus. Charging is rectification at unity power factor; discharging is inversion under the P and Q rules above.

Grid services: peak shaving under time-of-day tariffs (charge at night, discharge into evening peak), backup during a power cut, frequency response and voltage support. A grid-tied inverter must detect a dead grid within a few cycles and stop, or it energises a line a lineman believes dead. Passive methods watch voltage and frequency windows; active methods inject a small disturbance and check whether it grows. Anti-islanding limits are fixed by IEEE 1547 and IEC 62116.

Worked example

Question. A home uses 4 kWh per day. The site gets 4.5 peak sun hours a day, the wiring and dust loss factor is 0.75, the inverter efficiency is 0.9, and the panels are rated 335 Wp. The battery is 48 V lithium, sized for 2 days of autonomy at 60% depth of discharge and 90% round-trip efficiency. Find the array size in Wp, the number of panels, the daily DC energy taken from the bus, and the battery Ah rating.

  1. The inverter is not lossless, so the DC bus must supply 4000 / 0.9 = 4444.4 Wh per day.
  2. Array Wp = DC energy / (peak sun hours × loss factor) = 4444.4 / (4.5 × 0.75) = 4444.4 / 3.375 = 1316.9 Wp.
  3. Panels = 1316.9 / 335 = 3.93, so round up to 4 panels, giving 4 × 335 = 1340 Wp. Daily DC from the array = 1340 × 4.5 × 0.75 = 4522.5 Wh, enough for 4444.4 Wh.
  4. Energy to store = daily DC energy × days of autonomy / (DoD × round-trip efficiency) = 4444.4 × 2 / (0.6 × 0.9) = 16460.9 Wh.
  5. Battery capacity = 16460.9 / 48 = 342.9 Ah; choose at least 350 Ah, or 400 Ah for margin. A 300 Ah block is below the requirement.

Exam traps

  • Sizing the array straight from kWh per day, forgetting peak sun hours and the loss factor.
  • Treating the battery Ah rating as usable energy — DoD, round-trip efficiency and inverter efficiency all apply and increase the size.
  • In P = (E V / X) sin δ, mixing line-to-line volts with per-phase X, or rms with peak; the formula is per phase and in rms.
  • Expecting fixed switching frequency from a hysteresis controller; band, inductance and bus voltage set the frequency.
  • Assuming a grid-tied inverter can keep running during a blackout — anti-islanding forces it to trip, so backup needs a separate battery inverter or transfer scheme.

6.7 Industrial process control applications

A process control loop holds one physical variable — oven temperature, tank level, line pressure — at a set point the operator chooses. The loop measures the variable, compares it with the set point, and uses a power converter as the muscle that pushes the process back. Such loops run heat-treatment furnaces, boiler drums, water tanks and chemical reactors. A plating bath held at 60 °C all shift is one example.

You need: Unit 5 for phase-angle and integral-cycle AC controllers, and Unit 2 for controlled rectifiers feeding DC drives.

The loop: sensor, transmitter, controller, actuator

Only a few universities teach process control inside a power electronics course. Four parts form the loop, joined one way and closed by feedback.

  • Sensor: PT100 resistance thermometer, thermocouple, float switch or pressure transducer.
  • Transmitter: converts the reading to 4–20 mA. Current survives long cables, and 4 mA is a live zero, so 0 mA means a broken wire, not a cold oven.
  • Controller: compares the measurement with the set point and outputs 0–100 %.
  • Actuator: the power stage — thyristor power controller, solid-state relay, contactor, variable-frequency drive or valve.

On-off control switches fully inside a band and lets the temperature cycle. Proportional control makes the output proportional to the error; the proportional band is the error span that sweeps the output from 0 to 100 %. Integral action removes the steady error. Derivative action damps overshoot.

Switching a heater: three methods

A heater is the simplest load. P = V_rms²/R, so the controller only sets the RMS voltage across R.

On-off control uses a contactor: cheap, but cycles last minutes, temperature swings widely and contacts wear.

Phase-angle control fires the thyristor at angle α after each zero crossing. Output moves smoothly every half cycle, suiting fast loads such as infrared lamps, at the cost of harmonics and radio interference.

Integral-cycle (burst) control gates whole mains cycles at zero crossings over a fixed period, often 0.4 s, which is 20 cycles at 50 Hz. Current stays nearly sinusoidal, but the heater receives bursts of full power.

V_rms = Vs √(1 − α/π + sin(2α)/(2π))

V_rms is the RMS output voltage, Vs is the RMS supply voltage, α is the firing angle in radians with 0 ≤ α ≤ π, and the load is resistive.

Sizing the heater control and the rest of the plant

  1. From the rating, R = V²/P: 2 kW at 230 V gives R = 26.45 Ω and full-load current P/V = 8.70 A.
  2. Pick thyristors for at least twice that RMS current and for the peak voltage √2 × 230 = 325 V, plus margin.
  3. Add a fast fuse and a heatsink; one thyristor drops about 1 V, so it dissipates roughly 9 W at 8.70 A.
  4. Take the 4–20 mA measurement into the controller and use its 0–100 % output as the firing angle or duty cycle.

A PLC scans inputs, runs ladder logic and drives outputs; SCADA shows the same data to the operator as trends and alarms. Optocouplers or relays isolate the sensor circuit and the 4–20 mA loop from the power circuit, so a shorted heater cannot destroy the controller (gate drive: Unit 1).

A variable-frequency drive holds V/f constant so motor flux stays fixed as speed changes, using the inverter of Unit 4; a DC drive takes armature voltage from a controlled rectifier (Unit 2). Four-quadrant operation lets a drive motor or brake in either direction, so a crane can lower a load under control. Fans, pumps, conveyors and extruders are the usual plant loads.

Worked example

Question. A 2 kW, 230 V heater is fed from a 230 V, 50 Hz supply through a thyristor AC controller. The load is resistive. Find R, and the firing angle and RMS current at 50 % and at 25 % power.

  1. R = V²/P = 230²/2000 = 26.45 Ω. Full-load current = 2000/230 = 8.70 A.
  2. Power fraction p = (V_rms/Vs)², so V_rms = Vs√p.
  3. 50 %: V_rms = 230 × √0.5 = 162.63 V. The bracket equals 1 − 0.5 + 0 = 0.5 at α = π/2 = 90°.
  4. I_rms = 162.63 / 26.45 = 6.15 A.
  5. 25 %: V_rms = 230 × 0.5 = 115.0 V. Solving 1 − α/π + sin(2α)/(2π) = 0.25 gives α = 1.986 rad = 113.8°.
  6. I_rms = 115.0 / 26.45 = 4.35 A.

Answer. R = 26.45 Ω; at 50 % power α = 90° and I_rms = 6.15 A; at 25 % power α = 113.8° and I_rms = 4.35 A.

Exam traps

  • Controller output is not the firing angle: 50 % output is α = 90° on a phase-angle controller but a 50 % duty cycle on an integral-cycle one.
  • Heater power follows RMS current, P = I_rms²R, not average current.
  • 4 mA is zero, not 0 mA; a 0 mA reading is a broken loop.
  • Integral-cycle control cuts harmonics but still thermal-cycles the element if the period is long.

R = 230²/1000 = 52.9 Ω; V_rms = 230/√2 = 162.6 V; I_rms = 162.6/52.9 = 3.07 A.

Analyze An oven overshoots each time the set point steps up. Which controller action damps this, and what does it act on? 4 marks

Derivative action; it responds to the rate of change of the error, not to its size.

Analyze A crane lowers a load steadily. Which drive quadrants are in use, and why? 6 marks

The load overhauls the motor, so torque opposes rotation: braking operation in quadrant II or IV, with energy returned to the DC bus or dumped in a chopper resistor.

Takeaways

  1. A UPS is a charger, a battery and an inverter on a shared DC bus; the topology chosen decides efficiency, transfer time and cost.
  2. A battery charger is a controlled DC source following a fixed current-then-voltage profile, and the same thyristor switching runs dimmers, timers and emergency lamps.
  3. Induction heating puts the heat source inside the workpiece, so it is fast, clean and controllable by frequency and coil current.
  4. HVDC carries bulk power without line charging current, has no stability limit over distance and gives full, fast control of power flow.
  5. Thyristor-switched reactors and capacitors in SVC and FACTS devices hold voltage and push a line closer to its thermal limit without building new lines.
  6. A grid-tied converter with a fast current loop fixes power factor, limits harmonics and lets power flow in either direction between DC source and grid.
  7. Hysteresis current control is popular for grid interfaces because it is simple and robust, at the cost of variable switching frequency and wider ripple.
  8. Every process-control loop is sensor, transmitter, controller and actuator; the actuator here is a power converter whose duty or firing angle is set by the error.
  9. Small-signal sizing rules matter in practice: battery Ah for backup time, inverter kVA for surge load, PV kWp for daily energy, HVDC rating for bulk transfer.

Glossary

Active harmonic filter
A shunt converter that senses the load current, extracts its harmonic content and injects an equal and opposite current to clean the supply.
BESS
Battery energy storage system: a battery bank with a bidirectional converter that can both charge from and discharge into the grid.
Commutation
The process of transferring current from one device or phase to another; in line-commutated converters the supply voltage itself forces turn-off.
Converter
Any power electronic circuit that changes the form of electric power: AC to DC, DC to AC, DC to DC or AC to AC.
DIAC
Bidirectional trigger diode that breaks over at a fixed voltage and is used to fire a TRIAC in a simple RC phase-shift circuit.
Duty ratio
D = Ton ÷ T, the fraction of the switching period a device conducts; it sets the output of DC-DC converters and the fundamental of inverters.
FACTS
Flexible AC transmission system: power electronic controllers, shunt or series, that make an AC network controllable and raise its usable capacity.
Freewheeling diode
Diode placed across an inductive load to carry the stored current when the main switch turns off, protecting the switch and improving output.
IGBT
Insulated gate bipolar transistor; voltage-controlled switch combining MOS gate drive with low ON-state drop, widely used in inverters and drives.
Inverter
A converter that turns DC into AC, usually by switching devices in a bridge and shaping the output with PWM.
Islanding
Condition where a grid-connected inverter keeps feeding a local network after the grid has tripped; anti-islanding protection must detect and stop it.
PID controller
Control law with proportional, integral and derivative terms on the error; it holds the process variable at the set point despite disturbances.
Power factor
PF = P ÷ S, the ratio of real power to apparent power; it falls when current is out of phase with voltage or rich in harmonics.
PWM
Pulse-width modulation: switching on and off at a fixed frequency and varying the on-time to synthesise a wanted average voltage.
Reactive power
Q = V I sin φ, the power that oscillates between source and reactive element, in var or kvar; it loads the line without doing real work.
Rectifier
A converter that turns AC into DC; diode rectifiers are uncontrolled, thyristor rectifiers have a controllable output.
Ripple
The unwanted AC component left on a DC voltage or current after rectification, usually reduced by filters or higher switching frequency.
Snubber
Small RC or RCD network across a switching device that limits dV/dt and absorbs turn-off energy.
TCSC
Thyristor controlled series capacitor: a thyristor-controlled reactor in parallel with a series capacitor, giving a variable series reactance in the line.
Thyristor (SCR)
Four-layer latching switch that conducts only after a gate pulse and turns off only when its current falls below the holding value.
Total harmonic distortion
THD = √(Σ Vn²) ÷ V1 over n ≥ 2, the ratio of all harmonic content to the fundamental, in per cent.
Transmitter
Circuit that converts a sensor output into a standard signal, such as 4–20 mA or 1–5 V, for the controller.
TRIAC
Bidirectional thyristor that conducts in both half cycles, which makes it the standard switch in lamp dimmers and small AC controllers.
Zero-crossing switching
Turning a thyristor on only at the instant the supply voltage crosses zero, used for heaters to avoid the harmonics of phase control.
