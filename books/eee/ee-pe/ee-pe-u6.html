6.1 Uninterruptible power supplies (UPS)

A UPS keeps a load alive when the mains fails. Mains normally feeds a rectifier or charger, which holds a DC bus with a battery. An inverter turns that DC into AC for the load. In the on-line type the load always runs from the inverter, so a mains failure is invisible. UPS systems protect computers, hospital monitors, telecom towers, railway signalling and payment servers. This chapter compares off-line, line-interactive and on-line UPS topologies, explains transfer time, VA rating versus watts, output waveform quality, and battery sizing for a required backup time.

You need: switched-mode power supplies and the buck converter (DC-DC converters), and the full-bridge inverter with sinusoidal PWM (DC-AC inverters). The charger itself is in Battery chargers and emergency lighting.

Off-line, line-interactive and on-line UPS

An off-line UPS, also called standby, leaves the load on the mains in normal operation. The charger keeps the battery full and the inverter is off. When mains fails, a static transfer switch moves the load to the inverter. Transfer time is typically 4 to 10 ms, short enough for most computer power supplies to ride through. Some syllabi call this the standby type.

A line-interactive UPS adds a tap-changing transformer or buck-boost converter that corrects mains voltage while the load stays on the mains. The same inverter/charger charges the battery when mains is present. On mains failure it also switches the load to the inverter, with a transfer time of a few milliseconds, shorter than the off-line type because the inverter is already running and regulating. An on-line UPS, also called double conversion, rectifies the mains to a DC bus, holds a battery on that bus, and runs an inverter continuously into the load. The load never sees the mains directly. Mains failure changes the DC bus source from the charger to the battery, so there is no transfer time.

A static bypass is a fast switch, usually thyristors, from mains to load for inverter overload or fault. A maintenance bypass isolates the UPS for service. Ferro-resonant and rotary UPS exist for special cases; most data-centre UPS are on-line.

VA rating, waveform and battery sizing

UPS rating is in VA because inverter and transformer heating depends on current, and load current depends on apparent power S. Real power P = S × cos φ, where φ is the load power factor. A 1 kVA UPS with a 0.8 PF load can supply 800 W; with a 0.6 PF load it can supply only 600 W. Output THD = √(V₂² + V₃² + V₄² + …) ÷ V₁ × 100%, where V₁ is the fundamental RMS voltage and V₂, V₃, … are the harmonic RMS voltages. Below 3% is good for sensitive loads.

S = P / cos φ; Pdc = P / η; E = Vbat × Ah × DoD; t = E / Pdc

S is apparent power in VA; P is real load power in W; cos φ is load power factor; Pdc is battery-side DC power in W; η is inverter efficiency; Vbat is nominal battery voltage in V; Ah is battery capacity in ampere-hours; DoD is depth of discharge as a fraction; E is usable energy in Wh; t is backup time in hours.

Battery sizing uses usable energy, not the printed Ah alone. The charger must also replace the energy taken during an outage. Ageing, low temperature and high discharge current reduce the delivered Ah, so field designs add margin. Grid-scale battery interfaces are in Renewable energy and battery storage interfacing.

Power supplies and power conditioners

A linear supply steps down and rectifies the mains, then drops the excess voltage across a series transistor; it is clean but bulky and low efficiency. An SMPS chops at high frequency, so it is small and efficient, but it draws pulsed current with high crest factor and injects harmonics. Crest factor is the peak value divided by the RMS value: about 1.41 for a sine, often 2.5 to 3 for an SMPS. That crest factor is what matters for UPS sizing: the UPS must supply the peak current of the SMPS load without clipping or tripping. A UPS is an AC power supply with stored energy. It also acts as a power conditioner, regulating voltage, filtering spikes and holding frequency. A power conditioner may only filter and regulate, with no battery. Choose UPS VA from load VA, not watts alone, and add margin for startup.

Worked example

Question. A 1 kVA on-line UPS feeds a 640 W load at 0.8 lagging power factor. The battery is 48 V, 100 Ah, the inverter efficiency is 90%, and the battery may be taken down to 80% of its capacity. Find the DC current drawn from the battery and the backup time.

  1. Load apparent power: S = P / cos φ = 640 / 0.8 = 800 VA. The 1 kVA UPS rating is greater than 800 VA, so the load fits.
  2. Inverter DC power: Pdc = P / η = 640 / 0.9 = 711.1 W.
  3. Battery DC current: I = Pdc / Vbat = 711.1 / 48 = 14.8 A.
  4. Usable battery energy: E = Vbat × Ah × DoD = 48 × 100 × 0.8 = 3840 Wh.
  5. Backup time: t = E / Pdc = 3840 / 711.1 = 5.40 h, about 5 h 24 min. Check: 14.8 A × 5.40 h = 80 Ah, which is 80 % of the 100 Ah battery, as allowed.

Answer. The battery supplies about 14.8 A at 48 V, and the usable energy gives about 5.4 hours of backup.

Worked example 2

Question. A server room needs 30 minutes of backup for a 2 kVA load at 0.8 power factor. The on-line UPS has a 96 V battery string (eight 12 V blocks in series), the inverter efficiency is 92 %, the battery may be discharged to 80 %, and the designer adds 25 % for ageing. The maker's table says a block gives only 60 % of its 20-hour Ah rating when it is emptied in 30 minutes. Find the battery current and the Ah rating of each block.

  1. Real power: P = S × cos φ = 2000 × 0.8 = 1600 W.
  2. DC power from the battery: Pdc = P / η = 1600 / 0.92 = 1739.1 W. Battery current at 96 V: I = 1739.1 / 96 = 18.1 A.
  3. Energy for 30 minutes: 1739.1 W × 0.5 h = 869.6 Wh.
  4. Allow for depth of discharge: 869.6 / 0.8 = 1087.0 Wh. Add 25 % for ageing: 1087.0 × 1.25 = 1358.7 Wh.
  5. Ah at the 30-minute rate: 1358.7 Wh / 96 V = 14.15 Ah.
  6. Convert to the 20-hour rating printed on the block: 14.15 / 0.60 = 23.6 Ah. The next standard size is 26 Ah.

Answer. The battery supplies 18.1 A; use eight 12 V blocks of at least 23.6 Ah (20-hour rating), so 12 V, 26 Ah blocks.

Exam traps

  • Multiplying VA by hours and calling it stored energy. Apparent power must pass through the load power factor and the inverter efficiency before it becomes battery watts.
  • Using battery Ah × voltage as backup time without depth of discharge and inverter efficiency. A 100 Ah battery is not 100 Ah of usable energy.
  • Choosing a UPS only by watts. The UPS VA rating must exceed load VA, because current heats the inverter and transformer.
  • Thinking every UPS has the same transfer time. Off-line and line-interactive types switch the load; on-line double conversion does not transfer the load on mains failure.
  • Forgetting that a lead-acid block delivers fewer Ah at a high rate. A 30-minute UPS discharge gets only a part of the 20-hour Ah printed on the block, so use the maker's table.

6.2 Battery chargers and emergency lighting

A battery charger is a controlled DC source that pushes current into a battery along a set profile. It does not simply connect a transformer and a diode; it regulates current, then voltage, and stops at the right point. The same switching devices and sensing circuits run emergency lamps, light dimmers and simple timers. You meet them in phone chargers, inverter packs, stairwell lights and theatre exit signs. This chapter shows the profiles, the regulator loops and the changeover that keeps a lamp alive when the mains fails.

You need: diode rectifiers with capacitor filters, the buck and flyback converters (DC-DC converters), and phase control of AC voltage controllers. The charger-battery-inverter chain also appears in Uninterruptible power supplies (UPS).

Charging methods and C-rate

The charging profile depends on cell chemistry, temperature and duty.

C-rate scales current to capacity. For a battery of capacity C Ah, nC means n × C amperes. A 100 Ah battery at 0.1C takes 10 A, not 100 A. Ideally 0.1C fills or empties it in about 10 h.

Charge efficiency η is Ah output divided by Ah input. Lead-acid is about 0.90. You push in more Ah than you took out. The charge factor is F = Ahin / Ahout = 1/η. To replace 50 Ah at η = 0.90, push in 50/0.90 = 55.6 Ah.

  • Trickle: a small current, often C/100, holds a full battery against self-discharge.
  • Constant current (CC): fixed current while voltage rises.
  • Constant voltage (CV): fixed voltage while current falls.
  • CC-CV: CC to a voltage limit, then CV until current tapers to a termination value. Both stages put charge in: the CC stage returns most of it, the CV stage the rest.
  • Float versus boost: float is lower standby voltage; boost is higher cycle voltage. A 12 V lead-acid block often floats at 13.5–13.8 V and boosts at 14.4 V.
  • Pulse charging: current pulses with rest gaps; it reduces polarization but needs control.

Temperature changes the safe voltage. The charger senses battery temperature and reduces or stops charge when hot. Overcharge causes gassing, heat and short life. Termination can be a timer, a current threshold, a voltage drop (dV/dt) or a temperature limit.

Inside the charging regulator

Three power stages are common. A series pass regulator puts a transistor between rectified DC and the battery. It drops excess voltage, so it is simple and quiet but wastes (Vin − Vbatt) × I as heat. A buck converter chops and filters; it is efficient for step-down. A flyback gives isolation from mains.

Sensing closes the loops. A current-sense resistor Rs converts current to voltage: Vs = I × Rs. Rs is small, often milliohms, so it wastes little power. An amplifier raises Vs. A divider senses battery voltage: Vsense = Vbatt × R2/(R1 + R2).

Ploss = (Vin − Vbatt) × I

Ploss is heat in W; Vin is input DC in V; Vbatt is battery voltage in V; I is charge current in A.

  1. Rectify and filter the mains (a diode bridge and a capacitor).
  2. Measure battery current and voltage.
  3. Compare both with references; the lower demand wins, so CC runs until the voltage limit, then CV.
  4. Drive the pass device with a linear signal or PWM duty cycle.
  5. Stop at termination; protect against reverse polarity, overcurrent and over-temperature.

A charge controller IC does these steps. A small emergency light may use a linear regulator with a timer. In a UPS, the charger holds a large battery on a DC bus.

Emergency lighting, dimmers and timers

An emergency lighting fitting has a lamp, a battery, a charger and a changeover circuit. A maintained fitting keeps its lamp on during normal mains and on battery. A non-maintained fitting keeps the lamp off until mains fails. Exit signs in a cinema are often maintained; a stairwell lamp may be non-maintained.

An inverter tube pack drives a fluorescent tube from a low-voltage battery. The charger is a rectifier plus regulator. With mains present, a relay or static switch keeps the inverter off and charges the battery. On failure, changeover starts the inverter. LED (light-emitting diode) emergency drivers use a constant-current supply; a battery and boost converter take over on mains failure. Each fitting is rated for a duration that the local building code sets; 1 h and 3 h ratings are common.

Changeover can be a relay or a static switch. Transfer must be fast enough that the lamp does not extinguish. A test button breaks the mains input to simulate failure. A self-test checks battery voltage under load, not just open-circuit voltage. A weak battery can read 12 V with no load and collapse under a lamp. Over-discharge protection cuts the lamp before damage.

A phase-control dimmer switches the AC wave partway through each half-cycle. A TRIAC is a bidirectional thyristor switch. A DIAC is a bidirectional trigger diode. A DIAC and an RC network generate the gate pulse. The firing angle α is measured from the zero crossing. A later α gives a shorter conduction angle and a lower root-mean-square (RMS) voltage. This is the ordinary fan regulator and incandescent dimmer. Phase control creates harmonics and radio noise, so a filter is needed. AC voltage controllers treat the waveforms in full.

An electronic timer uses a 555 IC or a comparator. In monostable mode the output pulse is t = 1.1 R C. R is in ohms, C in farads, t in seconds. A zero-crossing detector synchronizes the timer to the mains. The timer drives a relay, a TRIAC or a solid-state relay. Zero-crossing switching reduces electromagnetic interference (EMI) because current starts at zero.

Worked example

Question. A 12 V, 100 Ah lead-acid battery is at 50 % depth of discharge. It is charged at 0.1C in constant current until it reaches 14.4 V, which happens when 80 % of the needed charge is back in. It is then held at 14.4 V while the current falls roughly linearly from the CC value to 2 A, where the charger stops. Charge efficiency is 90 %. Find the constant-current time, the constant-voltage time, the total time and the charger output power at the start and end of the constant-voltage stage.

  1. Charge removed: 50 % of 100 Ah = 0.50 × 100 = 50 Ah.
  2. Charge to push in: Ahin = 50 / 0.90 = 55.56 Ah (charge factor 1/0.90 = 1.11).
  3. CC current: 0.1C = 0.1 × 100 = 10 A.
  4. CC stage returns 80 %: 0.80 × 55.56 = 44.44 Ah, so tCC = 44.44 / 10 = 4.44 h.
  5. CV stage returns the rest: 55.56 − 44.44 = 11.11 Ah. With the current falling linearly from 10 A to 2 A, the average is (10 + 2) / 2 = 6 A, so tCV = 11.11 / 6 = 1.85 h.
  6. Total time = 4.44 + 1.85 = 6.30 h, about 6 h 18 min.
  7. Charger output power: at the start of CV, P = 14.4 × 10 = 144 W; at the end, P = 14.4 × 2 = 28.8 W.

Answer. CC for 4.44 h, CV for 1.85 h, 6.30 h in all; the charger gives 144 W at the start of the CV stage and 28.8 W at its end.

Worked example 2

Question. A fan-regulator style TRIAC dimmer feeds a 100 W, 230 V incandescent lamp from 230 V, 50 Hz mains. Treat the lamp as a fixed resistance. Find the lamp voltage and power at firing angles of 90° and 120°.

  1. Lamp resistance: R = V² / P = 230² / 100 = 529 Ω.
  2. For a TRIAC fired at α in both half cycles, Vrms = Vs √(1 − α/π + sin 2α / (2π)), with α in radians and Vs the supply RMS voltage.
  3. α = 90° = π/2: the bracket is 1 − 0.5 + sin 180° / (2π) = 0.5, so Vrms = 230 × √0.5 = 162.6 V and P = 162.6² / 529 = 50.0 W.
  4. α = 120° = 2.094 rad: the bracket is 1 − 0.667 + sin 240° / (2π) = 0.333 − 0.138 = 0.1955, so Vrms = 230 × √0.1955 = 101.7 V.
  5. P = 101.7² / 529 = 19.6 W. Check: P is also 100 W × 0.1955 = 19.6 W, because power goes as the square of the RMS voltage.

Answer. At α = 90° the lamp gets 162.6 V and 50 W; at α = 120° it gets 101.7 V and 19.6 W.

Exam traps

  • Reading 0.1C as 100 A for a 100 Ah battery. 0.1C is 10 A.
  • Forgetting charge efficiency. A 50 Ah discharge needs about 55.6 Ah of charge at 90% efficiency.
  • Adding a fixed taper time on top of a CC time that already returns all the charge. The CV stage also puts charge in, so the CC stage ends before the full Ah are back.
  • In a dimmer, thinking a later firing angle raises the RMS voltage. It lowers it.

6.3 Induction heating

Induction heating passes a high-frequency current through a coil and lets the metal part heat itself. Eddy currents and hysteresis inside the workpiece convert the coupled magnetic energy into heat, so the coil stays cooler than the charge. The method is clean, fast and local: it melts steel in a crucible, softens a billet for forging, hardens a gear surface, and boils a pan on an induction cooktop. Only a few universities teach its design as a full topic. This chapter shows the skin-depth choice, the transformer model and the inverter that drives the coil.

You need: the full-bridge inverter (DC-AC inverters) and resonant converters with soft switching (DC-DC converters).

Heat is made inside the metal

A work coil carries a high-frequency AC current and creates an alternating magnetic field. The metal to be heated, the workpiece, sits inside this field. The field induces eddy currents in the workpiece; its resistance turns those currents into heat. In magnetic steel, hysteresis also adds heat on every magnetisation cycle. The coil itself stays cooler because its resistance is small and it is water-cooled.

δ = √(ρ / (π f μ))

δ is skin depth in m; ρ is workpiece resistivity in Ω·m; f is coil current frequency in Hz; μ is workpiece permeability in H/m, equal to μr μ0.

Below the Curie point (about 770 °C) magnetic steel has μr of about 100 and ρ of about 2 × 10⁻⁷ Ω·m, so δ is only 3.2 mm at 50 Hz and 0.23 mm at 10 kHz. Above the Curie point μr falls to 1 and hot steel's ρ rises to about 1.2 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m, so δ grows to 78 mm at 50 Hz and 5.5 mm at 10 kHz. The hot stage is the hard one, so the frequency is chosen for hot steel. Choose f so δ is smaller than the billet radius; then current crowds near the surface and heats it fast. The coil and workpiece act as a transformer with a shorted single-turn secondary. The workpiece reflects a resistance Rref into the coil; P ≈ Icoil² Rref. Magnetising inductance makes the coil current lag, so power factor is low and a capacitor is added.

Resonant inverter and power control

A resonant inverter converts DC to the coil frequency. In a series load-resonant inverter, the coil and a capacitor form a series resonant tank; the current is near sinusoidal and the inverter switches near zero current. A parallel load-resonant inverter puts the capacitor across the coil and gives a near-sinusoidal coil voltage. The soft-switching ideas are those of resonant DC-DC converters.

Power is set by frequency or DC link. Above resonance the tank is inductive; moving f closer to resonance raises current and power. Changing DC link voltage Vd changes coil voltage and power. Control must track load changes: cold steel, hot steel, and steel above the Curie point all have different μ and Rref. Coupling gap and coil turns also change Rref; poor coupling lowers efficiency.

Design steps for a billet or crucible

  1. Find metal power: Pmetal = m c ΔT / t. Add melting or latent heat if the charge melts.
  2. Divide by overall efficiency η (often 0.5 to 0.7) to get inverter output Pinv = Pmetal / η.
  3. Choose f from the skin-depth formula for hot steel: make δ about one-third to one-half of the billet radius, so f = ρ / (π μ δ²). Small parts need 10–100 kHz; large billets use 1–10 kHz.
  4. Choose coil turns N and voltage Vcoil; coil current Icoil = Pinv / (Vcoil × pf). Use the actual pf from the resonant tank, not 1.
  5. Water-cool the coil: copper losses Icoil² Rcoil become heat; keep coolant flow enough to hold coil temperature.
  6. Check coupling, Curie point and duty cycle; tune the tank capacitor at the working frequency.

Worked example

Question. A steel billet of diameter 50 mm and length 100 mm is heated from 20 °C to 900 °C in 60 s. Density 7850 kg/m³, specific heat 500 J/(kg·K), overall efficiency 60 %, coil voltage 400 V at 0.3 power factor. Hot steel has ρ = 1.2 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m and μr = 1. Find the inverter output power, the coil current, a frequency that gives δ equal to half the radius, and the tank capacitor that brings the inverter to unity power factor.

  1. Volume V = π d² / 4 × L = π(0.050)² / 4 × 0.100 = 1.963 × 10⁻⁴ m³.
  2. Mass m = ρV = 7850 × 1.963 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.541 kg.
  3. Temperature rise ΔT = 900 − 20 = 880 K.
  4. Energy E = m c ΔT = 1.541 × 500 × 880 = 6.78191 × 10⁵ J.
  5. Metal power Pmetal = E / t = 6.78191 × 10⁵ / 60 = 11,303 W.
  6. Inverter output Pinv = Pmetal / η = 11,303 / 0.60 = 18,839 W ≈ 18.8 kW.
  7. Coil current Icoil = Pinv / (Vcoil × pf) = 18,839 / (400 × 0.30) = 157 A.
  8. Frequency: δ = r/2 = 12.5 mm. f = ρ / (π μ0 δ²) = 1.2 × 10⁻⁶ / (π × 4π × 10⁻⁷ × 0.0125²) = 1945 Hz; take f = 2 kHz (δ = 12.3 mm).
  9. Coil vars: S = 400 × 157 = 62.8 kVA and sin φ = √(1 − 0.3²) = 0.954, so Q = 62.8 × 0.954 = 59.9 kvar.
  10. Capacitor: Q = V² ω C, so C = 59.9 × 10³ / (400² × 2π × 2000) = 29.8 µF, about 30 µF.
  11. With the capacitor supplying the vars, the inverter carries only the real power: Iinv = 18,839 / 400 = 47.1 A instead of 157 A.

Answer. The inverter delivers about 18.8 kW, the coil carries 157 A, a 2 kHz supply gives δ ≈ 12.3 mm in hot steel, and a 30 µF tank capacitor cuts the inverter current to 47 A.

Exam traps

  • Using the skin depth of cold magnetic steel for the whole cycle. Above the Curie point δ becomes about 25 times larger at the same frequency, so the frequency is chosen for hot steel.
  • Thinking the coil makes most of the heat; the coil is water-cooled and the heat is in the workpiece.
  • Forgetting the reflected resistance and low power factor; coil current is not P/V, it is P/(V × pf).
  • Treating the Curie point as a fixed number; above it μr falls sharply and δ, Rref and power all change.
  • Designing the tank at one load only; cold, hot and molten workpieces change the resonant frequency and coupling.

6.4 HVDC transmission

HVDC transmission sends bulk power as direct current between two converter stations. At one end a rectifier turns AC into DC, the line carries DC with only a small ripple, and at the other end an inverter turns it back into AC. With no charging current and no reactive voltage drop, DC wins on very long overhead routes, on undersea cables and between grids that are not synchronised. Power flow is set by converter control, not by the angle between two AC systems. Links run between regions and from offshore wind farms to shore.

You need: three-phase controlled rectifiers, inversion mode and the effect of source inductance (controlled rectifiers), PWM bridges with IGBTs (DC-AC inverters), and thyristor and IGBT ratings (power semiconductor devices).

Why DC instead of AC

An AC line has series inductance and shunt capacitance. On a long line the capacitance draws charging current, so part of the conductor current carries no useful power and the receiving-end voltage rises at light load. An AC submarine cable is worse: its capacitance is large and the whole length carries charging current, so AC sea crossings are limited to a few tens of kilometres.

A DC line carries only load current; the loss is I²R. For the same power DC needs two conductors instead of three, or one conductor with an earth return, so the cost per kilometre falls. Beyond a break-even distance, hundreds of kilometres for overhead lines, DC costs less overall even though the terminal stations cost more.

DC also separates the two grids. Rectifier and inverter need not stay in step, so a link joins systems of different frequency or systems not synchronised at all. Some back-to-back HVDC stations do this inside one country. Power flow is set by converter control, not by the angle between two voltage phasors, so it can be held at any value and reversed in a fraction of a second.

The line-commutated converter station

The classic station uses thyristors in a 12-pulse bridge, two of the three-phase controlled rectifiers met earlier in the subject. A thyristor turns on by gate pulse but turns off only when its current falls to zero, so the AC line voltage itself must push current from one valve to the next. This is line commutation, and it means the station must sit on a strong AC network.

  • Converter transformer: star-star and star-delta secondaries, giving the 30° shift and the insulation against DC.
  • Two 6-pulse bridges 30° apart make one 12-pulse converter: the 5th and 7th AC-side harmonics cancel, so the lowest AC-side harmonic is the 11th and the DC ripple is at the 12th and 24th.
  • AC filters: tuned branches for the 11th, 13th and higher, plus a high-pass branch; they also supply part of the reactive power.
  • Smoothing reactor: a series inductor in the DC line that keeps the DC current continuous at light load and slows the rise of fault current.
  • Ground electrode or metallic return, and surge arresters across valves and transformers.

Reactive power: a line-commutated bridge absorbs reactive power at both ends. A working figure is Q ≈ 0.5 Pd, about half the DC power, at the rectifier and at the inverter. Filters and capacitors supply part of it and synchronous condensers the rest, so a weak AC system cannot feed a line-commutated station.

Commutation failure: the inverter valve that is turning off must be given time, the extinction angle γ, to regain its blocking ability. A dip in AC voltage or a jump in DC current steals that time, the current fails to transfer, and the bridge briefly short-circuits the DC terminal so that DC voltage collapses. The link recovers within a few cycles once AC voltage returns, so one event is not a permanent trip.

VSC-HVDC and the pole arrangements

A voltage-source converter station uses IGBT bridges with PWM, like the inverters of the DC-AC inverters topic. An IGBT turns off on command, so the bridge does not need the AC line to commutate. It can therefore feed a passive network and black-start a dead grid after a blackout, work into a weak grid, and control active and reactive power independently. It reverses power by reversing the DC current while keeping the DC voltage polarity fixed, which suits extruded cable insulation. Losses are a little higher than for LCC and station ratings were traditionally smaller, but VSC dominates new cable links.

Pd = Vd × Id

Pd is the total DC power in watts; Vd is the DC voltage in volts and Id the line current in amperes. For a bipolar link Vd is the pole-to-pole voltage, twice the pole-to-ground value, and Id is the current in one pole, so Pd = (2 × pole-to-ground voltage) × pole current. For a monopolar link Vd is the pole-to-ground voltage and Id the conductor current, and the same product still holds.

Arrangements. Monopolar: one conductor, with return through earth or through a metallic return conductor. Bipolar: two poles, one at +Vd/2 and one at −Vd/2 to earth, each carrying half the power, and in balanced operation the earth current is nearly zero. If a pole trips, the healthy pole keeps running in monopolar mode with ground return, so half the power stays on. The ground electrode must have low resistance and is sited carefully for environmental reasons. A DC network with more than two stations is called MTDC.

Worked example

Question. A ±500 kV bipolar HVDC link transmits 2000 MW. Find (a) the DC current in each pole, (b) the power still delivered if one pole trips and the return is through ground, and (c) the reactive power each line-commutated station draws at 50% of DC power.

  1. The link is bipolar at ±500 kV, so the pole-to-pole DC voltage is Vd = 2 × 500 kV = 1000 kV = 1000 × 10³ V.
  2. Total DC power is Pd = 2000 MW = 2000 × 10⁶ W, and Pd = Vd × Id.
  3. So Id = 2000 × 10⁶ / (1000 × 10³) = 2000 A in each pole. Check: each pole carries 500 kV × 2000 A = 1000 MW, and two poles give 2000 MW.
  4. With one pole out, the remaining pole runs at 500 kV to ground and carries the same 2000 A: P = 500 × 10³ × 2000 = 1000 × 10⁶ W = 1000 MW.
  5. At 50% of DC power the DC power is only 1000 MW, so each station draws Q ≈ 0.5 × 1000 MW = 500 MVAr. At the full 2000 MW each station would draw about 1000 MVAr.

Answer. The pole current is 2000 A, the link still delivers 1000 MW in monopolar ground-return operation, and at 50% power each station draws about 500 MVAr (about 1000 MVAr each at full power).

Exam traps

  • Putting the pole-to-ground voltage into Pd = Vd Id for a bipolar link. The formula uses the pole-to-pole voltage, twice the pole-to-ground value, with the current of one pole.
  • Assuming the inverter generates reactive power while the rectifier absorbs it. Both line-commutated stations absorb reactive power, roughly half the DC power each; the filters only supply part of it.
  • Treating a commutation failure as a permanent fault. It is a temporary short circuit across the DC terminal, and the link recovers within a few cycles after the AC voltage returns.
  • Swapping the reversal mechanisms: an LCC reverses DC voltage polarity at constant current direction, while a VSC reverses current direction at constant voltage polarity.
  • Believing an HVDC line has charging current or a reactive voltage drop. It has neither, and that is exactly why DC suits long overhead routes and cables.

6.5 Static VAR compensators and FACTS

A static VAR compensator (SVC) is a shunt power-electronics circuit that changes reactive power in milliseconds to hold a bus voltage steady. It exists because transmission lines and loads need reactive power, called vars: too little leaves voltage low and limits power transfer; too much raises voltage. SVCs sit at weak 33 kV, 132 kV and 400 kV buses, at HVDC terminals and near arc furnaces. The wider family, FACTS (flexible AC transmission systems), adds series and combined converters that raise line capacity and steer power flow.

You need: thyristor AC voltage controllers (AC-AC converters), the voltage-source inverter (DC-AC inverters) and the reactive power drawn by controlled rectifiers. HVDC transmission shows where that var demand appears.

Why reactive power sets voltage and transfer

Reactive power Q, measured in var, kvar or MVar, is the power that swings between a magnetic field or an electric field and the source. It does no useful work, but it sets voltage. An inductive load draws lagging current and absorbs Q, so the line drops more voltage. A capacitor draws leading current and supplies Q, which raises voltage.

P = (VS VR / X) sin δ

P is active power in watts (W); VS and VR are sending-end and receiving-end phase-to-phase voltages in volts (V); X is line series reactance in ohms (Ω); δ is the angle between the two voltages in radians.

An SVC is a controlled shunt susceptance BSVC. It injects reactive current IQ = BSVC V into the bus. BSVC is in siemens (S); positive means leading current (capacitive) and negative means lagging current (inductive). The controller measures bus voltage and changes BSVC so voltage returns to its set point. It responds in one or two cycles.

At a weak bus, this support raises VR in the formula, so the same line carries more P.

TCR, TSC and the SVC characteristic

A thyristor-controlled reactor (TCR) is a fixed reactor L in series with two anti-parallel thyristors. The firing angle α is measured from the positive-going zero crossing of the bus voltage. At α = 90° the thyristors conduct for the whole half cycle; at α = 180° they do not conduct. The fundamental susceptance is:

BL(α) = [2(π − α) + sin 2α] / (π XL)

BL(α) is the fundamental susceptance of the TCR in siemens (S); α is the firing angle in radians, between π/2 and π; XL = ωL is the reactor reactance in ohms (Ω); ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s; L is inductance in henry (H).

With a fixed capacitor C in parallel, the SVC susceptance is BSVC = BC − BL(α), where BC = ωC. The TCR absorbs lagging vars and the capacitor supplies leading vars; the net is their difference. At α = 90° the TCR absorbs its maximum, so the SVC is at its most lagging setting. At α near 180° the TCR is off, so the capacitor makes the SVC leading.

  • TCR + fixed capacitor (TCR+FC): continuous control from leading to lagging within rating.
  • Thyristor-switched capacitor (TSC): capacitor connected in steps; leading vars only, no continuous control from one unit.
  • Thyristor-switched reactor (TSR): reactor connected in steps; lagging vars only.

The V-I characteristic shows the control range. Inside it, bus voltage stays nearly constant while reactive current changes. At the leading limit the SVC acts like a fixed capacitor, so its current falls when voltage falls. At the lagging limit it acts like a fixed reactor.

STATCOM, series devices and active filters

A STATCOM (static synchronous compensator) is a voltage-source converter (VSC) behind a coupling reactor. It controls the magnitude and phase of its AC output voltage. If the converter voltage is higher than the bus voltage, the current it draws from the bus leads the bus voltage, so it looks like a capacitor and supplies vars; if lower, the current lags and it absorbs vars like a reactor. The converter can hold current up to its rating while the bus voltage sags, so its V-I curve is current-limited. An SVC of the same MVar rating gives less reactive current at low voltage because it behaves as a fixed susceptance. The VSC and its current control are the same as in a grid-tied inverter.

  • TCSC (thyristor-controlled series capacitor): a thyristor-controlled reactor in parallel with a series capacitor; it changes the line's effective series reactance, raising the transfer limit and damping oscillations.
  • SSSC (static synchronous series compensator): a series VSC injects a voltage in quadrature with line current; it behaves like a controllable series reactance.
  • UPFC (unified power flow controller): shunt and series converters share a DC link. The series converter injects a voltage of any phase and so controls line active and reactive power. The shunt converter supplies, through the DC link, the active power the series converter exchanges, and it can also give or take vars at its own bus.

An active harmonic filter is a shunt VSC that measures the load current, extracts its harmonic components, and injects equal and opposite harmonic currents. The supply then sees a nearly sinusoidal current. The same converter can supply vars, so it also works as a STATCOM.

At an HVDC terminal, an SVC or STATCOM supplies the converters' vars and supports commutation after a fault.

Worked example

Question. A 33 kV, 50 Hz bus has a star-connected TCR with reactor reactance XL = 10 Ω per phase. The firing angle is α = 120°. Using BL(α) = [2(π − α) + sin 2α] / (π XL), find the fundamental TCR current per phase and the fundamental reactive power absorbed. Repeat for α = 90°.

  1. α = 120° = 2.094 rad, so π − α = 1.047 rad and sin 2α = sin 240° = −0.866. Numerator = 2(1.047) − 0.866 = 1.228.
  2. BL(120°) = 1.228 / (π × 10) = 1.228 / 31.416 = 0.0391 S.
  3. Phase voltage: Vphase = 33 kV / √3 = 19,052 V.
  4. Fundamental current: I = BL Vphase = 0.0391 × 19,052 = 745 A.
  5. Fundamental reactive power: Q = √3 VLL I = 1.732 × 33 kV × 0.745 kA = 42.6 MVAr.
  6. α = 90°: π − α = 1.571 rad and sin 180° = 0, so BL(90°) = 3.142 / (π × 10) = 0.1 S = 1/XL.
  7. At α = 90°: I = 0.1 × 19,052 = 1905 A and Q = √3 × 33 × 1.905 = 108.9 MVAr.
  8. At 120° the TCR current is not a sine, so 42.6 MVAr is the fundamental value; at 90° the current is a full sine and the value is exact.

Answer. At α = 120°, BL = 0.0391 S, I = 0.745 kA and Q = 42.6 MVAr; at α = 90°, BL = 0.1 S, I = 1.905 kA and Q = 108.9 MVAr.

Worked example 2

Question. The TCR of worked example 1 (XL = 10 Ω per phase, 33 kV) is paired with a fixed capacitor bank of 60 MVAr. Find the range of the SVC output and the firing angle at which the output is zero.

  1. TCR at α = 90°: QL = 108.9 MVAr (worked example 1). Net output = 60 − 108.9 = −48.9 MVAr, that is 48.9 MVAr lagging.
  2. TCR at α = 180°: no conduction, QL = 0. Net output = 60 MVAr leading.
  3. Zero output needs QL = 60 MVAr, so BL(α) / BL(90°) = 60 / 108.9 = 0.551, that is [2(π − α) + sin 2α] / π = 0.551.
  4. Try α = 110°: (2.4435 − 0.6428) / π = 0.573, too high. Try α = 112°: (2.3736 − 0.6947) / π = 0.534, too low.
  5. Between them, α ≈ 111.1° gives 0.551. Check: QL = 108.9 × 0.551 = 60.0 MVAr.

Answer. The SVC can go from 48.9 MVAr lagging (α = 90°) to 60 MVAr leading (α = 180°), and its output is zero at α ≈ 111°.

Exam traps

  • Taking the TCR output as the SVC output. The TCR only absorbs vars; the fixed capacitor supplies leading vars; the net SVC output is the difference BC − BL(α).
  • Putting α in degrees inside the formula. Convert to radians first. Keep the sign: sin 240° = −0.866.
  • Mixing SVC and STATCOM V-I behaviour. An SVC at its limit looks like a fixed susceptance, so current falls with voltage. A STATCOM holds current up to its limit, so it gives more reactive current at low voltage.
  • Treating UPFC as a shunt-only SVC. A UPFC has a shunt converter and a series converter, and the series converter is what controls line power flow.

6.6 Renewable energy and battery storage interfacing

A grid-tied converter links DC sources such as solar panels and batteries to the AC grid. It boosts the panel voltage, holds a DC bus, and inverts DC into sinusoidal current matched to the grid in phase, frequency and amplitude. The inverter controller sets power factor, harmonics, and power direction. This chapter covers the PV curve and maximum power point, hysteresis current control, real and reactive power from angle and voltage, and battery storage with its management system and converter.

You need: grid-tied inverters and PWM (DC-AC inverters), boost and bidirectional converters (DC-DC converters) and PFC rectifiers. Uninterruptible power supplies (UPS) shows the same DC bus with a battery.

The PV curve and the maximum power point

A PV cell is a current source in parallel with a diode; sunlight sets the current. Short circuit: V = 0, I = Isc. Open circuit: I = 0, V = Voc. Power P = V × I, where V is terminal voltage in volts, I current in amperes, P power in watts. P is zero at both ends and peaks once in the knee: the maximum power point (MPP).

Irradiance moves current almost in proportion. Cell temperature moves voltage the other way, so a hot panel has lower Voc. The MPP slides all day, so the converter chases it.

Perturb and observe (P&O): measure V and I, compute P, step the boost duty, measure again, compare. If power rose, step same way; if it fell, reverse. It hunts around the MPP by one step but needs no panel model.

Two arrangements: two-stage has a boost MPPT stage onto a DC bus plus a separate inverter; single-stage lets the inverter do MPPT and accept a wider input window. Most rooftop string inverters are two-stage.

Hysteresis current control

Hysteresis control makes inverter current follow a sine. Build reference iref(t) = Im sin(ωt) from grid phase and wanted power: Im is peak reference in amperes, ω = 2πf in rad/s, f grid frequency (50 Hz in India). Compare measured i with band of width h amperes: iref + h/2 and iref − h/2.

  1. i climbs above iref + h/2: switch the leg so inductor voltage reverses and i falls.
  2. i drops below iref − h/2: switch the other way and let i rise.
  3. Repeat. The current stays in the band: a sine with small triangular ripple.

fs = (Vdc² − vg²) / (2 h L Vdc)

fs is switching frequency in Hz; Vdc the DC bus voltage in volts; vg the instantaneous grid voltage in volts; h the hysteresis band in amperes; L the filter inductance in henries. A full bridge is assumed.

The switching frequency varies: highest near vg = 0, lowest near the sine peak. Design the filter at the lowest frequency. Switching loss is worst at the highest frequency, near vg = 0. Conduction loss and device current stress peak near the sine peak because current is largest there. Direction comes from the sign of iref. Take current positive from converter into grid: a reference in phase with vg exports real power (inversion); a reference 180° out of phase draws real power (rectification at unity power factor). The sign of the reference sets direction. One circuit charges and discharges a battery. Merits: fast, robust, no carrier, small steady-state error. Faults: swinging frequency complicates the filter and audible noise; loss varies through the cycle.

Real and reactive power from angle and voltage

Model the inverter as sinusoidal source E behind coupling reactance X feeding stiff grid V. E and V are rms phase voltages in volts, X in ohms mostly filter inductor, δ the angle by which E leads V.

P = (E V / X) sin δ Q = (E V cos δ − V²) / X

P is real power per phase in watts; Q reactive power per phase in var; E the inverter fundamental phase voltage in volts; V the grid phase voltage in volts; X coupling reactance in ohms; δ load angle in radians.

Signs: δ > 0 gives P > 0, power leaves inverter (battery discharges or PV exports). δ < 0 gives P < 0, power enters (battery charges). For small δ, cos δ ≈ 1 and Q ≈ (E − V)V / X, so amplitude difference sets Q: E above V supplies reactive power, E below V absorbs it.

This turns a dispatch order such as “export 3 kW at unity power factor” into two references, δ and E. An outer loop sets them; an inner current loop, often hysteresis, forces current. Unity-power-factor rectification means δ slightly negative and E trimmed until Q = 0, that is E cos δ = V; current sits in phase with grid voltage and only real power is drawn.

Battery storage: cells, BMS and the power conversion system

  • Battery pack: cells in series and parallel give DC voltage and Ah. State of charge (SoC) is percentage capacity left; depth of discharge (DoD) is fraction used. Lead-acid is kept near 50% DoD; lithium goes deeper. A lithium pack returns roughly 90% of energy put in.
  • BMS: measures every cell voltage plus pack temperature and current, bleeds charge between cells, estimates SoC, and opens contactor on over-voltage, over-current or over-temperature. It protects cells; it does not control power.
  • PCS: bidirectional converter that charges and discharges to the DC bus. Charging is rectification at unity power factor; discharging is inversion under the P and Q rules above.

Grid services: peak shaving under time-of-day tariffs (charge at night, discharge into evening peak), backup during a power cut, frequency response and voltage support. A grid-tied inverter must detect a dead grid and stop within about two seconds, or it energises a line a lineman believes dead. Passive methods watch voltage and frequency windows; active methods inject a small disturbance and check whether it grows. IEEE 1547 sets the limits and IEC 62116 the test for anti-islanding.

Worked example

Question. A home uses 4 kWh per day. The site gets 4.5 peak sun hours a day, the wiring and dust loss factor is 0.75, the inverter efficiency is 0.9, and the panels are rated 335 Wp. The battery is 48 V lithium, sized for 2 days of autonomy at 60% depth of discharge and 90% round-trip efficiency. Find the array size in Wp, the number of panels, the daily DC energy taken from the bus, and the battery Ah rating.

  1. The inverter is not lossless, so the DC bus must supply 4000 / 0.9 = 4444.4 Wh per day.
  2. Array Wp = DC energy / (peak sun hours × loss factor) = 4444.4 / (4.5 × 0.75) = 4444.4 / 3.375 = 1316.9 Wp.
  3. Panels = 1316.9 / 335 = 3.93, so round up to 4 panels, giving 4 × 335 = 1340 Wp. Daily DC from the array = 1340 × 4.5 × 0.75 = 4522.5 Wh, enough for 4444.4 Wh.
  4. Energy to store = daily DC energy × days of autonomy / (DoD × round-trip efficiency) = 4444.4 × 2 / (0.6 × 0.9) = 16460.9 Wh.
  5. Battery capacity = 16460.9 / 48 = 342.9 Ah; choose at least 350 Ah, or 400 Ah for margin. A 300 Ah block is below the requirement.

Answer. The array is 1316.9 Wp, rounded to 4 panels of 335 Wp (1340 Wp), and the battery is 342.9 Ah at 48 V, so choose 350 Ah or 400 Ah. A 300 Ah block is too small.

Exam traps

  • Sizing the array straight from kWh per day, forgetting peak sun hours and the loss factor.
  • Treating the battery Ah rating as usable energy — DoD, round-trip efficiency and inverter efficiency all apply and increase the size.
  • In P = (E V / X) sin δ, mixing line-to-line volts with per-phase X, or rms with peak; the formula is per phase and in rms.
  • Expecting fixed switching frequency from a hysteresis controller; band, inductance and bus voltage set the frequency.
  • Assuming a grid-tied inverter can keep running during a blackout — anti-islanding forces it to trip, so backup needs a separate battery inverter or transfer scheme.

6.7 Industrial process control applications

A process control loop holds one physical variable — oven temperature, tank level, line pressure — at a set point the operator chooses. The loop measures the variable, compares it with the set point, and uses a power converter as the muscle that pushes the process back. Such loops run heat-treatment furnaces, boiler drums, water tanks and chemical reactors. A plating bath held at 60 °C all shift is one example.

You need: phase-angle and integral-cycle AC voltage controllers (AC-AC converters), and controlled rectifiers for DC drives.

The loop: sensor, transmitter, controller, actuator

Only a few universities teach process control inside a power electronics course. Four parts form the loop, joined one way and closed by feedback.

  • Sensor: PT100 resistance thermometer, thermocouple, float switch or pressure transducer.
  • Transmitter: converts the reading to 4–20 mA. Current survives long cables, and 4 mA is a live zero, so 0 mA means a broken wire, not a cold oven.
  • Controller: compares the measurement with the set point and outputs 0–100 %.
  • Actuator: the power stage — thyristor power controller, solid-state relay, contactor, variable-frequency drive or valve.

On-off control switches fully inside a band and lets the temperature cycle. Proportional control makes the output proportional to the error; the proportional band is the error span that sweeps the output from 0 to 100 %. Integral action removes the steady error. Derivative action damps overshoot.

Switching a heater: three methods

A heater is the simplest load. P = Vrms²/R, so the controller only sets the RMS voltage across R.

On-off control uses a contactor: cheap, but cycles last minutes, temperature swings widely and contacts wear.

Phase-angle control fires the thyristor at angle α after each zero crossing. Output moves smoothly every half cycle, suiting fast loads such as infrared lamps, at the cost of harmonics and radio interference.

Integral-cycle (burst) control gates whole mains cycles at zero crossings over a fixed period, often 0.4 s, which is 20 cycles at 50 Hz. Each burst is whole sine cycles, so there are no switching harmonics, but the on-off pattern draws current in bursts, which can flicker lights on a weak supply and makes the heater see bursts of full power.

Vrms = Vs √(1 − α/π + sin(2α)/(2π))

Vrms is the RMS output voltage, Vs is the RMS supply voltage, α is the firing angle in radians with 0 ≤ α ≤ π, and the load is resistive.

Liquid level control and pump drives

A tank level loop keeps water between two marks or at a set level. The simplest form is on-off: two probes or a float switch start the pump at the low mark and stop it at the high mark. The gap between the marks is the hysteresis band; it stops the pump from starting many times a minute. The pump is switched by a contactor or a solid-state relay, with a dry-run cut-out that stops it if the sump is empty.

For a steady level, a level transmitter (pressure or ultrasonic) sends 4–20 mA to a PID controller, and the controller sets the speed of the pump through a variable-frequency drive. A transmitter that spans 0 to 2 m and reads 12 mA shows (12 − 4) / 16 = 50 %, a level of 1.0 m.

Q ∝ N; H ∝ N²; P ∝ N³

Q is pump flow, H is the head, P is the shaft power and N is the pump speed (the affinity laws of a centrifugal pump).

Speed control saves energy because power falls as the cube of speed: at 80 % speed the pump gives 80 % of the flow for 0.8³ = 51 % of the power. A throttling valve gives the same flow cut but leaves the motor running near full power.

Sizing the heater control and the rest of the plant

  1. From the rating, R = V²/P: 2 kW at 230 V gives R = 26.45 Ω and full-load current P/V = 8.70 A.
  2. Each thyristor of the pair carries one half-cycle: its RMS current is 8.70 / √2 = 6.15 A and its average current √2 × 8.70 / π = 3.92 A. Rate the voltage at 2 to 2.5 times the peak √2 × 230 = 325 V, so 800 V devices are a common choice.
  3. Add a fast fuse and a heatsink. Each thyristor drops about 1 V, so it dissipates about 1 V × 3.92 A ≈ 4 W, and the pair about 8 W.
  4. Take the 4–20 mA measurement into the controller and use its 0–100 % output as the firing angle or duty cycle.

A PLC scans inputs, runs ladder logic and drives outputs; SCADA shows the same data to the operator as trends and alarms. Optocouplers or relays isolate the sensor circuit and the 4–20 mA loop from the power circuit, so a shorted heater cannot destroy the controller.

A variable-frequency drive holds V/f constant so motor flux stays fixed as speed changes, using a three-phase inverter; a DC drive takes its armature voltage from a controlled rectifier. Four-quadrant operation lets a drive run as a motor or as a brake in either direction of rotation, so a crane can lower a load under control. Fans, pumps, conveyors and extruders are the usual plant loads.

Worked example

Question. A 2 kW, 230 V heater is fed from a 230 V, 50 Hz supply through a thyristor AC controller. The load is resistive. Find R, and the firing angle and RMS current at 50 % and at 25 % power.

  1. R = V²/P = 230²/2000 = 26.45 Ω. Full-load current = 2000/230 = 8.70 A.
  2. Power fraction: p = P / Pfull = Vrms² / Vs², so Vrms = Vs √p, and the bracket in the Vrms formula must equal p: 1 − α/π + sin 2α / (2π) = p.
  3. 50 %: the bracket must be 0.5. At α = π/2 = 90°, 1 − 0.5 + sin 180° / (2π) = 0.5, so α = 90° and Vrms = 230 × √0.5 = 162.63 V.
  4. Irms = 162.63 / 26.45 = 6.15 A.
  5. 25 %: Vrms = 230 × 0.5 = 115.0 V. Solving 1 − α/π + sin(2α)/(2π) = 0.25 gives α = 1.986 rad = 113.8°.
  6. Irms = 115.0 / 26.45 = 4.35 A.

Answer. R = 26.45 Ω; at 50 % power α = 90° and Irms = 6.15 A; at 25 % power α = 113.8° and Irms = 4.35 A.

Exam traps

  • Controller output is not the firing angle: 50 % output is α = 90° on a phase-angle controller but a 50 % duty cycle on an integral-cycle one.
  • Heater power follows RMS current, P = Irms²R, not average current.
  • On a 4–20 mA loop, 4 mA is the 0 % reading of the scale; a 0 mA reading means a broken loop.
  • Integral-cycle control avoids switching harmonics, but its bursts still thermal-cycle the element if the period is long and can flicker lamps on the same supply.

Takeaways

  1. A UPS is a charger, a battery and an inverter on a shared DC bus; the topology chosen decides efficiency, transfer time and cost.
  2. A battery charger is a controlled DC source following a fixed current-then-voltage profile, and the same thyristor switching runs dimmers, timers and emergency lamps.
  3. Induction heating puts the heat source inside the workpiece, so it is fast, clean and controllable by frequency and coil current.
  4. HVDC carries bulk power without line charging current, has no stability limit over distance and gives full, fast control of power flow.
  5. Thyristor-switched reactors and capacitors in SVC and FACTS devices hold voltage and push a line closer to its thermal limit without building new lines.
  6. A grid-tied converter with a fast current loop fixes power factor, limits harmonics and lets power flow in either direction between DC source and grid.
  7. Hysteresis current control is popular for grid interfaces because it is simple and robust, at the cost of variable switching frequency and wider ripple.
  8. Every process-control loop is sensor, transmitter, controller and actuator; the actuator here is a power converter whose duty or firing angle is set by the error.
  9. Sizing rules matter in practice: battery Ah for backup time, charge time from the charge factor, PV Wp from daily energy and sun hours, SVC range from the TCR and capacitor, and HVDC pole current from Pd = Vd Id.
  10. Level and pump loops use the same muscle as heater loops; a variable-frequency drive saves energy because pump power falls as the cube of speed.

Understand Draw the block diagram of an on-line (double-conversion) UPS and explain the function of each block from mains input to load. Why does the load never see a mains failure? 13 marks

Model answer.

  1. Block diagram: mains → input filter and rectifier/charger → DC bus with the battery bank → inverter → output filter (and isolation transformer) → load. A static bypass switch joins the mains straight to the load, and a manual maintenance bypass runs round the whole UPS.
  2. Input filter and rectifier/charger: the filter keeps the UPS's switching noise out of the mains and stops mains spikes. The rectifier (thyristor, or an IGBT active front end with near-unity power factor) turns the AC into DC, holds the DC bus voltage and supplies the inverter. The charger part keeps the battery at its float voltage and recharges it after an outage at a limited current.
  3. Battery bank: lead-acid or lithium cells in series to the bus voltage (48 V in a small unit, a few hundred volts in a large one). It floats on the DC bus, so it is always connected and needs no switching. A low-voltage cut-off stops discharge at the allowed depth of discharge; a fuse or breaker protects the battery cables.
  4. Inverter: a full-bridge (or three-phase) IGBT inverter with sinusoidal PWM makes a 50 Hz sine. An LC output filter removes the switching ripple, so output THD is low (below about 3 % for linear loads), and the control holds voltage and frequency steady even when the input is poor. It must supply the peak current of SMPS loads, whose crest factor is 2.5 to 3.
  5. Static bypass: a thyristor switch that moves the load to the mains in a few milliseconds if the inverter is overloaded (for example by a motor starting) or develops a fault. The inverter stays synchronised to the mains so that this transfer does not jolt the load. Maintenance bypass: a manual switch path that isolates the whole UPS so it can be serviced while the load stays on the mains.
  6. Operation on mains: mains → rectifier → DC bus → inverter → load, with the charger topping up the battery. On mains failure: the rectifier stops, and the battery, already on the bus, feeds the inverter. The inverter never stops and its output never switches path, so the load sees no break: the transfer time is zero. On mains return: the rectifier takes over the bus again and recharges the battery.

Understand Explain the principle of induction heating. Why is the heat produced inside the workpiece rather than in the coil, and how is the frequency chosen? 12 marks

Points to cover. Alternating current in the work coil sets up an alternating field; Eddy currents and hysteresis heat the workpiece; Transformer model: coil primary, workpiece a shorted one-turn secondary, reflected resistance; Skin depth formula and the choice of frequency, with the Curie point; Resonant inverter and capacitor for the low power factor.

Model answer.

  1. Principle: a high-frequency current in a copper work coil sets up an alternating magnetic field. A metal workpiece inside the coil links this field, so by Faraday's law an EMF is induced in it. The EMF drives eddy currents round the workpiece, and the workpiece's own resistance turns them into I²R heat. In magnetic steel below the Curie point, hysteresis loss adds a little more heat each cycle.
  2. Transformer model: the coil is the primary of N turns; the workpiece is a single shorted turn as the secondary. Seen from the coil, the workpiece appears as a reflected resistance Rref, so the power into the metal is P ≈ Icoil² Rref. The large magnetising inductance makes the coil current lag, so the power factor is low, often 0.1 to 0.3.
  3. Why the heat is in the workpiece: the coil is thick copper of low resistance and is water-cooled, so its I²R loss is small and is carried away. The workpiece has higher resistivity and the eddy current is crowded into a thin surface layer, so its effective resistance is much higher. The same current therefore makes most of its heat in the metal.
  4. Skin depth: δ = √(ρ / (π f μ)). The current density falls to 1/e of its surface value at depth δ. Higher frequency gives a thinner heated layer: surface hardening uses high frequency, through-heating of a billet uses low frequency. A rule of thumb is δ of about one-third to one-half of the billet radius for through-heating.

Apply A 230 V, 50 Hz supply feeds a 100 W incandescent lamp through a TRIAC with phase-angle control. Explain the DIAC-TRIAC firing circuit and find the lamp voltage and power at firing angles of 60° and 135°. 12 marks

Points to cover. RC network charges each half cycle; DIAC breaks over and fires the TRIAC; Conduction from α to the end of each half cycle; Vrms = Vs √(1 − α/π + sin 2α / (2π)); Numbers at 60° and 135°; Snubber, dV/dt, radio-interference filter.

Model answer.

  1. Circuit: the TRIAC is in series with the lamp across the mains. A potentiometer R and capacitor C are connected across the TRIAC; the capacitor voltage lags the supply. A DIAC joins the capacitor to the TRIAC gate.
  2. Firing: in each half cycle C charges through R. Points to cover. On-off control with two probes or a float; hysteresis band; contactor or SSR; Continuous control: level transmitter, PID, VFD; Pump affinity laws Q ∝ N, H ∝ N², P ∝ N³; Numbers: 3.84 kW and 1.62 kW; Dry-run protection, soft start.

Model answer.

  1. On-off control: two probes (or a float switch) mark the low and high levels. When the water falls to the low probe, the controller starts the pump through a contactor or solid-state relay; when it reaches the high probe, the pump stops. The gap between the probes is the hysteresis band, which stops the pump from starting many times a minute.
  2. Band sizing: in a tank of 2 m² floor area with probes 0.4 m apart, the band holds 2 × 0.4 = 0.8 m³. If the pump adds a net 20 m³/h (0.333 m³ per minute), one fill takes 0.8 / 0.333 = 2.4 min, so the pump starts at most a few times an hour if the draw-off is slow. A wider band means fewer starts but a bigger swing in level.
  3. Continuous control: a level transmitter (pressure at the tank bottom or an ultrasonic sensor) sends 4–20 mA to a PID controller. The controller compares it with the set level and sets the speed reference of a variable-frequency drive. For example, a transmitter spanning 0–2 m that reads 12 mA shows (12 − 4) / 16 = 50 %, that is 1.0 m.
  4. The VFD: a diode rectifier, a DC link and a PWM inverter that keeps V/f constant, so the motor flux stays fixed while the speed changes. The pump then delivers just the flow needed to hold the level, instead of running flat out and stopping.
  5. Affinity laws of a centrifugal pump: flow Q ∝ N, head H ∝ N², power P ∝ N³.
  6. Power at reduced speed:
    80 % speed: P = 7.5 × 0.8³ = 7.5 × 0.512 = 3.84 kW  (80 % flow)
60 % speed: P = 7.5 × 0.6³ = 7.5 × 0.216 = 1.62 kW  (60 % flow)
  7. Why the VFD saves energy: throttling a valve also cuts the flow, but the motor still runs at full speed against a higher head and takes close to full power. With speed control the power falls as the cube of speed.
  8. Other gains of the VFD: soft start (no starting current of 6 to 8 times rated, no water hammer), dry-run protection from the drive's own current reading, and the PID loop can sit inside the drive.
  9. Limit: the head falls as N², so the pump must keep enough speed to lift water to the tank; below that speed the flow stops even though the motor still turns.
  10. Check: halving the speed gives 0.5³ = 12.5 % of full power, 0.94 kW, which lies below the 60 % value as it must.

Analyze Compare off-line, line-interactive and on-line UPS topologies for transfer time, efficiency, output quality and cost, and recommend one for a small office and one for a hospital operating theatre. 13 marks

Points to cover. Off-line: load on mains, 4–10 ms transfer, cheapest, least protection; Line-interactive: tap-changing or buck-boost regulation, shorter transfer; On-line: double conversion, zero transfer time, best output, lowest efficiency; Load power factor and crest factor; VA sizing; Justified choice for both cases.

Model answer.

  1. Off-line (standby): the load runs on the mains through a filter; the charger keeps the battery full and the inverter is off. On a failure, a transfer switch moves the load to the inverter in about 4 to 10 ms. It is cheap and efficient (the load sees the mains directly), but it does not correct sags, surges or frequency drift, and its inverter often makes a stepped wave.
  2. Line-interactive: the load still runs on the mains, but a tap-changing transformer or buck-boost stage corrects the voltage, and the bidirectional inverter/charger is always connected and synchronised. Transfer on failure takes a few milliseconds, and many sags are handled without using the battery. It is the usual choice for small loads.
  3. On-line (double conversion): rectifier → DC bus with the battery → inverter → load, all the time. On a failure only the source of the DC bus changes, so the transfer time is zero, and the output is a clean regulated sine whatever the input does. The cost is higher and the efficiency a few per cent lower, because the power goes through two converters.

Analyze Explain why a diode-bridge rectifier with a capacitor filter draws distorted current, and describe how unity-power-factor rectification and an active harmonic filter correct it. Find the power factor for a current THD of 100 % and of 5 %. 13 marks

Points to cover. Capacitor charges only near the voltage peak: narrow current pulses, high THD; PF = displacement factor × distortion factor = cos φ₁ / √(1 + THD²); Active front end: current reference in phase with the supply, hysteresis or PWM current control; Active harmonic filter injects the harmonic currents in antiphase; Numbers: 0.707 and 0.999.

Model answer.

  1. Cause: the capacitor holds the DC voltage near the peak of the supply. The diodes conduct only when the supply voltage rises above the capacitor voltage, a short time around each peak. The current is a train of narrow, tall pulses rich in 3rd, 5th, 7th … harmonics, with a high crest factor.
  2. Power factor: PF = P / S = (V I₁ cos φ₁) / (V I) = cos φ₁ × (I₁ / I), where I₁ is the fundamental current. With THD = Ih / I₁, the distortion factor is I₁ / I = 1 / √(1 + THD²).
  3. Numbers, with the fundamental in phase (cos φ₁ = 1):
    THD = 100 %:  PF = 1 / √(1 + 1.00²)   = 0.707
THD = 5 %:    PF = 1 / √(1 + 0.05²)   = 0.9988
  4. So a typical diode-capacitor front end wastes almost 30 % of the supply current rating on harmonics, even though its fundamental is in phase.
  5. Unity-power-factor rectification: replace the diode front end by an active front end: a boost PFC stage or an IGBT bridge with an inductor on the AC side. A phase-locked loop gives the supply angle; the DC-voltage controller sets the amplitude; the current reference is iref = Im sin ωt in phase with the supply. A hysteresis or PWM current controller forces the line current to follow it, so the current is sinusoidal and in phase, and power flows from AC to DC. The same circuit with iref reversed returns power to the supply.
  6. Active harmonic filter: when the load cannot be changed (many existing drives), a shunt voltage-source converter is placed beside it. It measures the load current, extracts the harmonic part (load current minus its fundamental), and injects the same harmonic currents with opposite sign. The supply then provides only the fundamental. It follows load changes and cannot resonate with the network, unlike a tuned passive filter, and it can supply vars like a STATCOM at the same time.
  7. Limits: the filter's converter must switch fast enough to track the highest harmonic to be cancelled, its DC bus must be above the supply peak, and its inductor must be small enough to let the current change quickly.
  8. Check: raising THD from 5 % to 100 % lowers the PF from 0.999 to 0.707, the factor √2 that the formula predicts for equal harmonic and fundamental current.

Evaluate Evaluate induction heating against resistance heating for a domestic cooking plate and for surface hardening of a steel gear tooth. 12 marks

Points to cover. Heat made inside the workpiece versus conducted in from a hot element; Efficiency, speed, control and safety on the cooktop; pan must be magnetic; Hardening needs a thin heated layer: skin depth sets frequency; Resonant inverter, power factor and cost; Conclusion for each application.

Model answer.

  1. Basic difference: a resistance heater makes heat in its own element, which then reaches the work by conduction, convection or radiation. Induction makes the heat inside the work itself, through eddy currents (and hysteresis in magnetic steel), so there is no hot element in between.
  2. Cooking plate, induction: the coil under the glass is fed at about 20 to 50 kHz by a resonant inverter. The pan base is the shorted secondary. For a magnetic steel pan at 25 kHz, δ = √(2 × 10⁻⁷ / (π × 25,000 × 100 × 4π × 10⁻⁷)) = 0.14 mm, so the heat is made right at the base. Heating is fast, the plate surface stays cooler, power responds at once to the control, and the stove stops heating when the pan is lifted.
  3. Cooking plate, resistance: a coil or radiant element heats a plate that heats the pan. It is cheap, works with any pan (aluminium, clay, copper), and needs no electronics, but it is slower, wastes heat from the hot plate, and stays hot after switch-off.
  4. Verdict for cooking: induction wins on speed, efficiency and safety, at a higher price and with the need for a magnetic pan; resistance is the low-cost choice that suits every vessel.
  5. Gear hardening, induction: only a thin surface layer must be heated above the hardening temperature and quenched, leaving a tough core. The layer depth is set by the frequency. A shaped coil heats one tooth or the whole gear in a few seconds, and the heating is repeatable for every part.
  6. Gear hardening, resistance: a furnace heats the whole gear through, so the core also hardens (it becomes brittle) unless the gear is carburised first, and a slow furnace cycle causes scaling and distortion.
  7. Supply points for induction: a resonant inverter at the right frequency, a capacitor to supply the coil's vars (the coil power factor is low), water cooling of the coil, and higher capital cost.
  8. Conclusion: for the cooktop, induction is better if magnetic pans are used and the cost is accepted. For surface hardening, induction is clearly better, because only induction can heat a thin, controlled layer quickly; resistance heating suits through-heating, not case hardening.
  9. Check: raising the frequency from 2 kHz to 135 kHz cuts δ in hot steel from 12.3 mm to 1.5 mm, a ratio of √(135 / 2) = 8.2, as δ ∝ 1/√f predicts.

Glossary

Active harmonic filter
A shunt converter that senses the load current, extracts its harmonic content and injects an equal and opposite current to clean the supply.
BESS
Battery energy storage system: a battery bank with a bidirectional converter that can both charge from and discharge into the grid.
C-rate
Charge or discharge current scaled to capacity: for a C Ah battery, nC means n × C amperes, so 0.1C is 10 A for 100 Ah.
Commutation
The process of transferring current from one device or phase to another; in line-commutated converters the supply voltage itself forces turn-off.
Converter
Any power electronic circuit that changes the form of electric power: AC to DC, DC to AC, DC to DC or AC to AC.
Crest factor
Peak value ÷ RMS value of a current or voltage; 1.41 for a sine, much higher for the pulsed current of an SMPS.
Curie point
The temperature, about 770 °C for steel, above which a magnetic material loses its magnetism; μr falls to about 1 and the skin depth grows.
Depth of discharge (DoD)
The fraction of a battery's capacity taken out; a deeper discharge gives more energy per cycle but shortens battery life.
DIAC
Bidirectional trigger diode that breaks over at a fixed voltage and is used to fire a TRIAC in a simple RC phase-shift circuit.
Duty ratio
D = Ton ÷ T, the fraction of the switching period a device conducts; it sets the output of DC-DC converters and the fundamental of inverters.
FACTS
Flexible AC transmission system: power electronic controllers, shunt or series, that make an AC network controllable and raise its usable capacity.
Freewheeling diode
Diode placed across an inductive load to carry the stored current when the main switch turns off, protecting the switch and improving output.
IGBT
Insulated gate bipolar transistor; voltage-controlled switch combining MOS gate drive with low ON-state drop, widely used in inverters and drives.
Inverter
A converter that turns DC into AC, usually by switching devices in a bridge and shaping the output with PWM.
Islanding
Condition where a grid-connected inverter keeps feeding a local network after the grid has tripped; anti-islanding protection must detect and stop it.
PID controller
Control law with proportional, integral and derivative terms on the error; it holds the process variable at the set point despite disturbances.
Power factor
PF = P ÷ S, the ratio of real power to apparent power; it falls when current is out of phase with voltage or rich in harmonics.
PWM
Pulse-width modulation: switching on and off at a fixed frequency and varying the on-time to synthesise a wanted average voltage.
Reactive power
Q = V I sin φ, the power that oscillates between source and reactive element, in var or kvar; it loads the line without doing real work.
Rectifier
A converter that turns AC into DC; diode rectifiers are uncontrolled, thyristor rectifiers have a controllable output.
Ripple
The unwanted AC component left on a DC voltage or current after rectification, usually reduced by filters or higher switching frequency.
Snubber
Small RC or RCD network across a switching device that limits dV/dt and absorbs turn-off energy.
TCSC
Thyristor controlled series capacitor: a thyristor-controlled reactor in parallel with a series capacitor, giving a variable series reactance in the line.
Thyristor (SCR)
Four-layer latching switch that conducts only after a gate pulse and turns off only when its current falls below the holding value.
Total harmonic distortion
THD = √(Σ Vn²) ÷ V1 over n ≥ 2, the ratio of all harmonic content to the fundamental, in per cent.
Transmitter
Circuit that converts a sensor output into a standard signal, such as 4–20 mA or 1–5 V, for the controller.
TRIAC
Bidirectional thyristor that conducts in both half cycles, which makes it the standard switch in lamp dimmers and small AC controllers.
Zero-crossing switching
Turning a thyristor on only at the instant the supply voltage crosses zero, used for heaters to avoid the harmonics of phase control.
