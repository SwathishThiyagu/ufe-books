About this unit

This unit covers circuits that change an AC supply into another AC supply directly, without a DC link. It starts with the single-phase AC voltage controller and its two control laws, phase control and on-off control. Next a TRIAC and a DIAC take the place of the SCR pair, with gate triggering in all four quadrants. Two phase-controlled groups then make a new output frequency in the cycloconverter, and the unit closes with the matrix converter, nine bidirectional switches that do the same job with no DC link at all.

5.1 AC voltage controllers: phase and on-off control

An AC voltage controller sets the RMS voltage of an AC load while the frequency stays at 50 Hz. It is a thyristor switch in series with the load: two SCRs back to back, or one TRIAC. Phase control delays the turn-on in every half cycle; on-off control passes some whole cycles and blocks the rest. Dimmers, fan regulators, heater controls and motor soft starters use it. Some syllabi call it an AC voltage regulator.

You need: the SCR, gate triggering and natural commutation from power semiconductor devices, and the firing angle of a controlled rectifier.

Single-phase configurations

The supply has RMS voltage Vs and peak Vm = √2 Vs. Each SCR turns off by itself when its current falls to zero, so the supply commutates it.

  • Half-wave (unidirectional): one SCR T1 with a diode in antiparallel. Only the positive half is controlled, so the output has a DC part. Small resistive loads only.
  • Full-wave (bidirectional): two SCRs T1 and T2 in antiparallel, or one TRIAC (see TRIAC-based phase controllers). Both halves are controlled alike, with no DC part. The standard circuit.
  • Other full-wave forms: two SCRs and two diodes with a common cathode (one gate circuit), or one SCR inside a diode bridge (cheapest drive, but three devices conduct at once).

Phase control with a resistive load

T1 is fired at angle α after the positive zero crossing and T2 at π + α, where θ = ωt is the supply angle. With a resistor the current is in phase with the voltage, so T1 conducts from α to π: the conduction angle is π − α.

Vₒ = Vₛ √[(π − α + ½ sin 2α)/π]

Vo is the RMS load voltage and α is in radians, 0 ≤ α ≤ π. It comes from Vo² = (1/π)∫Vm² sin²θ dθ from α to π.

The input power factor of a resistive load is (Vo²/R)/(VsVo/R) = Vo/Vs. It falls as α grows because the chopped current carries harmonics and its 50 Hz part lags the voltage: at α = 90° the fundamental lags by about 32° and the power factor is 0.707. The half-wave controller gives Vo = Vs√[(2π − α + ½ sin 2α)/(2π)]; at α = 90° on 230 V that is 199.2 V with a DC part of (Vm/2π)(cos α − 1) = −51.8 V.

Inductive (RL) load

With a series R-L load, Z = √(R² + ω²L²) and the load angle is φ = tan⁻¹(ωL/R). The current does not stop at the voltage zero: T1 conducts until its current reaches zero at the extinction angle β, beyond π, so the conduction angle is γ = β − α.

i(θ) = (Vₘ/Z)[sin(θ − φ) − sin(α − φ)·exp(−(θ − α)/tan φ)]

for α ≤ θ ≤ β. Find β by trial from sin(β − φ) = sin(α − φ)·exp[−(β − α)/tan φ], with the angle in the exponential in radians.

Vₒ = Vₛ √{[β − α + ½(sin 2α − sin 2β)]/π}
Iₒ = (Vₛ/Z) √{[γ − sin γ·cos(α + β + φ)/cos φ]/π}

the RMS load voltage and current, with angles in radians.

Control range. At α = φ the current is a full sine, so the useful range is φ ≤ α ≤ π. If α < φ and the gate pulses are short, T1 still conducts when T2 is fired at π + α; T2 is reverse biased and its pulse is lost. Only T1 conducts, and the current gets a DC part. A wide pulse lets T2 fire when the current of T1 ends, and the load then gets the full sine.

On-off (integral-cycle) control

On-off or burst control fires the SCRs at the voltage zero for n whole cycles and blocks them for m cycles, so the switching causes little interference. With duty ratio D = n/(n + m):

Vₒ = Vₛ √D, PF = √D (resistive load)

because P = D Vs²/R and Is = Vs√D/R.

The pattern repeats every n + m cycles, so the supply current carries subharmonics at 50/(n + m) Hz and its multiples. Lamps would flicker, so on-off control suits heaters and furnaces, whose thermal mass averages the bursts; phase control suits lamps, fans and soft starters.

Worked example

Question. A 230 V, 50 Hz supply feeds a 20 Ω resistor through a full-wave AC voltage controller. Find Vo, Io, the power and the power factor for (a) phase control at α = 90°, (b) on-off control with 5 cycles on and 5 off. For (b), give the lowest frequency in the supply current.

  1. (a) α = π/2, sin 2α = 0, so the bracket is (π − π/2)/π = 0.5. Vo = 230 × √0.5 = 162.6 V and Io = 162.6/20 = 8.13 A.
  2. P = 162.6²/20 = 1322.5 W. Power factor = 162.6/230 = 0.707.
  3. (b) D = 5/10 = 0.5, so Vo = 162.6 V, Io = 8.13 A, P = 1322.5 W and PF = √0.5 = 0.707.
  4. The pattern repeats every 10 cycles = 0.2 s, so the lowest frequency is 1/0.2 = 5 Hz.

Answer. Both give 162.6 V, 8.13 A, 1322.5 W and PF 0.707. Phase control adds 150 Hz, 250 Hz and higher harmonics; on-off control adds a 5 Hz subharmonic.

Exam traps

  • Putting α in degrees into the RMS formula: with α = 90 the bracket (π − 90)/π is negative. Write α = π/2.
  • Using the resistive formula for an RL load. It stops the voltage at 180° instead of β: at α = 60°, φ = 45° it gives 206.3 V instead of 217.1 V.
  • Firing below the load angle with short pulses and expecting control. The second SCR never fires and the current gets a DC part.
  • Taking D as the voltage ratio in on-off control. D = 0.5 gives Vo = 0.707 Vs, not 0.5 Vs.

5.2 TRIAC-based phase controllers

A TRIAC conducts in both directions, so one TRIAC replaces the antiparallel SCR pair of an AC voltage controller and needs only one gate circuit. Delaying its turn-on in each half cycle cuts away the start of every half wave, and the RMS voltage at the load falls smoothly. Lamp dimmers and fan regulators use it. The exam asks how the gate is fired with positive and negative pulses, and how the RC-DIAC network sets the firing angle.

You need: the full-wave AC voltage controller and its RMS formula from AC voltage controllers: phase and on-off control, and the TRIAC and DIAC devices from power semiconductor devices.

The TRIAC as an AC switch

A TRIAC has two main terminals, MT1 and MT2, and a gate G. MT1 is the reference: gate voltage and gate current are always measured from MT1. It behaves as two antiparallel SCRs sharing one gate.

  • Latching current IL: the current that must flow as the gate pulse ends for the TRIAC to stay on.
  • Holding current IH: the current below which a conducting TRIAC turns off. It is smaller than IL; the TRIAC turns off at every current zero.

A TRIAC must block the reverse voltage at once after turning off, whereas each SCR of a pair gets a half cycle to recover. With a fan the voltage jumps across the TRIAC at the current zero, and this commutating dv/dt can turn it straight back on, so fan regulators carry a snubber.

Triggering quadrants: positive and negative gate pulses

A triggering quadrant is one combination of MT2 polarity and gate-current polarity, both measured from MT1. A typical data sheet asks for about twice as much gate current in Q4 as in the other three, and three-quadrant TRIACs are not rated for Q4 at all.

QuadrantMT2GateGate current needed
Q1++lowest (most sensitive)
Q2+−low to medium
Q3−−low to medium
Q4−+highest (least sensitive)

A dimmer fires the TRIAC once with MT2 positive and once with MT2 negative, so the pulse polarity decides which two quadrants it uses.

  • Positive pulses only: Q1 in the positive half and Q4 in the negative half. The negative half may fire late or not at all, giving a DC component. Avoid it.
  • Negative pulses only: Q2 and Q3. The usual drive from a microcontroller: the gate is simply pulled below MT1.
  • Pulse polarity following MT2: Q1 and Q3. A DIAC does this by itself.

The RC-DIAC dimmer and its firing angle

The lamp is in series with the TRIAC. A resistor R (potentiometer plus fixed resistor) and a capacitor C sit across the TRIAC, and a DIAC joins C to the gate. While the TRIAC is off, the capacitor voltage vC is a sine that lags the supply by tan⁻¹(ωRC). When |vC| reaches the DIAC breakover voltage VBO, about 30 V, the DIAC snaps on and dumps the charge of C into the gate as one sharp pulse.

α ≈ tan⁻¹(ωRC) + sin⁻¹(VBO/VCm). The first term is the lag of vC; the second is how far vC must rise past its zero to reach VBO. Its peak is VCm = √2 VsXC/√(R² + XC²), with XC = 1/(ωC). This ignores the loading by the gate, which is fine for an estimate.

The DIAC is symmetrical, so its pulse has the polarity of vC, which follows MT2: Q1 and Q3. Raising R makes vC lag more and shrink, so α grows. One RC stage cannot delay much beyond 120° to 150°, so dimmers that must go fully dark add a second stage.

From a microcontroller, an optotriac gives isolated drive: a random-phase type for phase control, a zero-crossing type for on-off control of heaters. A series RC snubber across MT1 and MT2 limits dv/dt; 100 Ω with 0.1 µF is a common starting point.

Worked example

Question. A 230 V, 50 Hz supply feeds a 230 V, 100 W incandescent lamp through a TRIAC dimmer fired at α = 90° in both half cycles. Take the lamp resistance as its rated hot value. Find the lamp resistance, the RMS lamp voltage, the RMS current and the lamp power.

  1. Lamp resistance at rated conditions: R = V²/P = 230²/100 = 52 900/100 = 529 Ω.
  2. α = 90° = π/2 rad, so sin 2α = sin 180° = 0 and Vo = 230 × √[(π − π/2)/π] = 230 × √0.5 = 162.6 V.
  3. Io = Vo/R = 162.6/529 = 0.3074 A.
  4. P = Vo²/R = 162.6²/529 = 26 450/529 = 50.0 W. Check: Io²R = 0.3074² × 529 = 50.0 W.

Answer. R = 529 Ω, Vo = 162.6 V, Io = 0.307 A, P = 50.0 W, half the rated power. A real filament runs cooler, so it takes a little more than 50 W, and its light falls by much more than half.

Worked example 2

Question. The dimmer's trigger network has R = 100 kΩ and C = 0.1 µF across the TRIAC, and a DIAC with VBO = 30 V. Estimate the firing angle. What is it when the potentiometer is turned down so that R = 10 kΩ?

  1. XC = 1/(2π × 50 × 0.1 × 10⁻⁶) = 31 831 Ω = 31.83 kΩ.
  2. Lag of vC: tan⁻¹(R/XC) = tan⁻¹(100/31.83) = tan⁻¹(3.142) = 72.3°.
  3. Size of vC: 230 × 31.83/√(100² + 31.83²) = 230 × 31.83/104.9 = 69.8 V RMS, so its peak is 69.8 × √2 = 98.7 V.
  4. vC reaches 30 V at sin⁻¹(30/98.7) = 17.7° past its own zero, so α = 72.3° + 17.7° = 90.0°. This is the 90° of the first example.
  5. With R = 10 kΩ: lag = tan⁻¹(10/31.83) = 17.4°, vC = 230 × 31.83/33.36 = 219.4 V RMS (peak 310.3 V), and sin⁻¹(30/310.3) = 5.5°, so α = 17.4° + 5.5° = 23.0°.

Answer. α ≈ 90° at 100 kΩ and about 23° at 10 kΩ, where the lamp is nearly at full brightness (Vo = 228.5 V by the RMS formula).

Exam traps

  • Naming the quadrant from the gate polarity alone. A positive gate pulse is Q1 when MT2 is positive but Q4 when MT2 is negative.
  • Writing that a negative-only gate drive cannot fire the positive half cycle. It fires in Q2, which works; it is the positive-only drive that lands in the weak Q4.
  • Swapping latching and holding current. Latching current must flow as the gate pulse ends; holding current is the level at which a conducting TRIAC drops out, and IH < IL.
  • Curing false triggering with a weaker gate drive. The usual cause is a fast dv/dt across MT1 and MT2, and the cure is a snubber.
  • Assuming one RC stage gives the full 0° to 180° range. In the worked example even 220 kΩ only delays the pulse to about 122°.

5.3 Three-phase AC voltage controllers

A three-phase AC voltage controller sets the RMS voltage of a three-phase load by delaying thyristor firing, for heaters, furnaces and motor soft starters. The connection decides everything: with a neutral the phases are independent, but in a three-wire star the load currents must add to zero and the phases interact. The exam asks for the configurations and for the firing ranges and RMS output of the three-wire star.

You need: the single-phase controller from AC voltage controllers: phase and on-off control, and three-phase line and phase quantities.

Configurations

Each line or branch gets two antiparallel SCRs, or a TRIAC at low power. VL is the RMS line voltage and Vph = VL/√3 the RMS supply phase voltage.

  • Star load with neutral (four-wire): three independent single-phase controllers; α runs from 0° to 180° and the single-phase formula applies per phase. The neutral carries the triplen harmonics (3rd, 9th, …) of all three phases, which add.
  • Three-wire star, fully controlled: six SCRs in the lines, no neutral. The phases interact and the range for a resistive load is 0° to 150°. The usual circuit for soft starters.
  • Three-wire star, half-controlled: an SCR and a diode in antiparallel in each line. Only three gate circuits, but the halves differ, so even harmonics appear; the range is 0° to 210°. Small heaters only.
  • Delta load, controllers inside the delta: an SCR pair in series with each branch. Each branch is a single-phase controller on the line voltage (0° to 180°), and the SCRs carry only the branch current.
  • Delta load, controllers in the lines: behaves like the three-wire star, with the same 0° to 150° range.

Three-wire star with a resistive load

The SCRs are fired 60° apart in the order T1 (line a, positive), T2 (c, negative), T3 (b, positive), T4 (a, negative), T5 (c, positive), T6 (b, negative), with α measured from the zero crossing of phase a. Current needs two SCRs in different lines, so each SCR gets a second pulse 60° after its first (double pulsing); above α = 60° its partner would otherwise already be off.

Why zero at 150°? T1 conducts with T6 through vab or with T2 through vac. At α = 150° the pair T1, T6 is fired just as vab reaches zero, and the pair T1, T2 just as vac does, so no current can start.

Harmonics, the delta connection and uses

With equal firing and a balanced load the line currents contain harmonics of order 6k ± 1: 5th, 7th, 11th, 13th. Triplens are in phase in all three lines, and three-wire line currents must add to zero, so there are none. In the four-wire star the triplens add in the neutral, which carries a large current near α = 90°.

Inside a delta each branch current has its own 3rd harmonic, but the line current is the difference of two branch currents, so the triplens cancel in the lines and circulate round the delta.

A soft starter begins at a large α (low voltage) and reduces α so the motor voltage ramps up with the current held at a set limit. Unequal firing angles are avoided: they unbalance the load and add negative-sequence current that heats a motor.

Worked example

Question. A 415 V, 50 Hz, three-phase supply feeds a star-connected resistive load of 10 Ω per phase through a fully controlled three-wire controller. Find the RMS load phase voltage, the line current and the total power at (a) α = 60°, (b) α = 120°.

  1. Vph = 415/√3 = 239.6 V.
  2. (a) α = π/3, first range. Bracket = 1 − 0.5 + 3 × 0.866/(4π) = 0.5 + 0.2067 = 0.7067; √0.7067 = 0.8407.
  3. Vo = 239.6 × 0.8407 = 201.4 V. I = 201.4/10 = 20.14 A. P = 3 × 201.4²/10 = 12.17 kW.
  4. (b) α = 2π/3, third range. Inside: 5π/24 − α/4 = 0.6545 − 0.5236 = 0.1309; sin 240°/16 = −0.0541; √3 cos 240°/16 = −0.0541. Sum = 0.0227. Bracket = (6/π) × 0.0227 = 0.0433, and √0.0433 = 0.208.
  5. Vo = 239.6 × 0.208 = 49.8 V, I = 49.8/10 = 4.98 A, P = 3 × 49.8²/10 = 745 W.

Answer. (a) 201.4 V, 20.14 A, 12.17 kW. (b) 49.8 V, 4.98 A, 745 W. With a neutral, 120° would give 239.6 × 0.442 = 105.9 V by the single-phase formula.

Worked example 2

Question. Three 30 Ω resistors in delta are each fed through their own SCR pair inside the delta from a 415 V, 50 Hz supply. At α = 90°, find the branch voltage and current, the total power, and compare the line current with √3 times the branch current.

  1. Each branch is a single-phase controller on the 415 V line voltage. At α = 90° the bracket is 0.5, so Vbranch = 415 × √0.5 = 293.4 V.
  2. Ibranch = 293.4/30 = 9.78 A. P = 3 × 293.4²/30 = 8.61 kW.
  3. Sine currents would give a line current of √3 × 9.78 = 16.9 A. But the line current is the difference of two branch currents, whose equal 3rd harmonics cancel; subtracting the two chopped waveforms point by point and taking the RMS gives 14.9 A.

Answer. Vbranch = 293.4 V, Ibranch = 9.78 A, P = 8.61 kW, line current 14.9 A, which is 1.53 times the branch current, not 1.73. At α = 0° the currents are sines and the ratio is √3 (13.83 A and 23.96 A).

Exam traps

  • Applying the single-phase formula to one phase of a three-wire star. At α = 120° it gives 105.9 V on 415 V instead of the correct 49.8 V.
  • Writing 180° as the zero-output angle of a three-wire star. It is 150°; 180° holds for the four-wire star and for controllers inside a delta.
  • Forgetting double pulsing. With single short pulses the controller cannot work above α = 60°, because two SCRs in different lines must be on together.
  • Expecting triplen harmonics in the line currents of a balanced three-wire load. They circulate inside a delta or flow in a neutral, never in the three lines alone.

Analyze In a three-wire star controller one line is fired at α = 60° and another at 120°. What happens to the load? 4 marks

The phase voltages and currents become unequal and the floating star point moves. The line currents still add to zero, so they carry no zero-sequence part, but the unbalance adds negative-sequence current, which heats a motor, and lets 3rd-harmonic current into the lines.

5.4 PWM AC voltage controllers

A PWM AC voltage controller, or AC chopper, switches the load between the 50 Hz supply and a short circuit many thousand times a second. The fraction of each switching period spent on the supply, the duty ratio D, sets the output. Because every switching period passes a slice of the supply, the 50 Hz part of the output stays in phase with the supply and the unwanted frequencies sit near the switching frequency, where a small LC filter removes them.

You need: phase control and its harmonics from AC voltage controllers: phase and on-off control, and IGBT and MOSFET switching from power semiconductor devices.

Series and freewheeling switches

The power stage has two switches. S1, the series switch, joins the supply to the load; S2, the freewheeling switch, sits across the load. Both must be bidirectional, because voltage and current reverse every half cycle: each is two IGBTs joined emitter to emitter, each with its antiparallel diode. A TRIAC is far too slow for kilohertz switching.

  • S1 on, S2 off: the load sees the supply voltage vs.
  • S1 off, S2 on: the load is shorted, its voltage is zero, and an inductive load current keeps flowing through S2.
  • They must never be on together (a supply short) nor, with an inductive load, both fully off (an open load), so each changeover follows the sign of the supply voltage.

The switching frequency fsw is typically 5 kHz to 20 kHz for IGBTs. The load is connected for ton = DT of each period T and shorted for the rest.

Duty ratio, RMS value and harmonics

D = tₒₙ/T, Vₒ = Vₛ √D, V₁ = D·Vₛ

Vo is the RMS of the chopped load voltage and V1 the RMS of its 50 Hz part, with Vs the RMS supply voltage.

The squared output is vs² for a fraction D of the time, which gives Vs√D. The output is vs(t) times a switching function that is 1 for a fraction D of each period: its average D gives the 50 Hz part D·vs, and its terms at fsw, 2fsw, … give sidebands at m·fsw ± f. The lowest is fsw − f: 9950 Hz for a 10 kHz chopper on 50 Hz.

Vo exceeds V1 by the sideband content. A heater uses that power, but a motor would only be heated by it, so other loads get an output LC filter and then see close to D·vs.

Input current and power factor

With a resistor and no filter, the supply current is a pulse train of RMS Vs√D/R while the power is D·Vs²/R, so the power factor is √D. An LC input filter with its corner well between 50 Hz and fsw supplies the pulses from its capacitor, and the supply delivers only their 50 Hz average, D·vs/R, in phase with vs. The power factor then rises close to 1.

Question. A 230 V, 50 Hz supply feeds a 50 Ω resistor through a single-phase PWM AC chopper switching at 10 kHz with D = 0.6 and no output filter. Find ton, Vo, the power, V1 and its share of the power, the lowest sideband, and the supply power factor without and with an ideal input filter.

Exam traps

  • Calling D·Vs the RMS output. It is the 50 Hz part; the RMS of the chopped wave is Vs√D, which is larger: 178.2 V against 138.0 V at D = 0.6.

5.5 Cycloconverters: step-up and step-down

A cycloconverter turns AC of one frequency into AC of another frequency directly, with no DC link. It is two phase-controlled converters back to back, one for each direction of output current, building each output half cycle from pieces of the supply wave. Step-down works with natural commutation; step-up needs forced commutation. Large low-speed drives such as cement kilns, ore mills and ship propulsion use it.

You need: the single-phase and three-phase controlled rectifiers with their output formula (2Vm/π) cos α, and dual converters, from controlled and uncontrolled rectifiers.

Two groups and their configurations

The positive (P) group carries the load current one way and gives the positive output half cycles; the negative (N) group carries it the other way. In non-circulating mode only one group is fired at a time.

  • Single-phase to single-phase, midpoint: a centre-tapped transformer and four SCRs, two per group.
  • Single-phase to single-phase, bridge: two single-phase bridges in antiparallel, eight SCRs. Each group gives an average of (2Vm/π) cos α per half cycle, where Vm is the peak supply voltage.
  • Three-phase to single-phase: two three-phase bridges, twelve SCRs, each group giving (3VmL/π) cos α, with VmL the peak line voltage.
  • Three-phase to three-phase: three such converters, one per output phase: 18 SCRs with three-pulse groups or 36 with six-pulse bridges.

Step-down operation

Step-down means fo < fs, where fo is the output and fs the supply frequency. In the simplest case fo = fs/3: the P group is fired at the same α in three supply half cycles, giving three positive humps, then the N group gives three negative humps. One output cycle spans three supply cycles. With a resistive load each hump is a phase-controlled half sine, so the RMS output is the AC-controller value Vs√[(π − α + ½ sin 2α)/π].

A fixed α gives a nearly square output. For a sine, α is varied by cosine-wave crossing: a reference Vr sin ωot is compared with a cosine timing wave Vc cos ωt locked to the supply, and an SCR is fired where they cross, so cos α follows the reference:

α = cos⁻¹(r sin ωₒt), 0 ≤ r ≤ 1

r = Vr/Vc is the modulation ratio and ωo = 2πfo. With continuous current the local average output is (2Vm/π) cos α = (2Vm/π) r sin ωot, so the largest fundamental a single-phase group can give has peak 2Vm/π = 0.9 Vs, which is 146 V RMS from a 230 V supply.

The fewer supply pieces per output half cycle, the poorer the sine, so a common rule of thumb keeps fo ≤ fs/3, about 16.7 Hz from 50 Hz.

Step-up operation

Step-up means fo > fs: the output must reverse several times inside one supply half cycle. When an SCR must stop, the supply has not reversed across it, so natural commutation cannot turn it off. Step-up cycloconverters need forced commutation or turn-off devices such as GTOs or IGBTs, and are rarely built because an inverter does the job more simply.

Circulating current, power factor and uses

In non-circulating mode, the outgoing group is blocked when the load current reaches zero and the incoming group fired after a short dead time. It needs current-zero sensing and distorts the output at each changeover, but needs no reactor.

In circulating-current mode, both groups are fired all the time with αP + αN = 180°, so their average voltages match. Their instantaneous voltages differ, so a current circulates between them, limited by an intergroup reactor. Changeover is smooth, at the cost of the size and loss of the reactor.

The input current lags as in any phase-controlled rectifier, with displacement factor close to cos α for an inductive load. Near an output zero α is near 90°, so the input power factor is poor at low output voltage. Uses: kilns, mills, mine hoists and ship propulsion, where motors run at a few hertz with very large torque.

5.6 Matrix converters

A matrix converter is a direct AC-to-AC converter: nine bidirectional switches let each of three output lines connect to any of three input lines. Switching at several kilohertz, it builds an output of any frequency and adjustable voltage from pieces of the input voltages, with no DC-link capacitor, and power flows either way. Its limits are an output of at most 0.866 times the input and a careful switching sequence.

You need: bidirectional IGBT switches from PWM AC voltage controllers, and three-phase phase and line voltages.

The nine-switch array

The inputs are a, b and c, fed through a small LC filter; the outputs A, B and C go to the load. Switch SXy joins input y to output X, so SAa, SAb and SAc are the three switches that can feed output A. Each switch is two IGBTs joined emitter to emitter with antiparallel diodes: 18 IGBTs and 18 diodes in all.

Never close two switches feeding one output (SAa with SAb shorts a to b), and never open all three (the inductive load needs a path). So exactly one switch per output is closed, leaving 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 states:

  • 6 states connect each output to a different input (the outputs are a reordering of the inputs);
  • 18 states connect two outputs to the same input, giving one output line voltage of zero;
  • 3 zero states connect all three outputs to one input, so every output line voltage is zero.

Duty cycles and the 0.866 limit

In each switching period Ts, much shorter than the output period, output A spends fractions dAa, dAb and dAc of Ts on inputs a, b and c, with dAa + dAb + dAc = 1. Its average voltage over that period is:

vA = dAava + dAbvb + dAcvc (averaged over Ts), with va, vb, vc the input phase voltages at that instant; outputs B and C have the same form. The modulation (Venturini or space-vector) chooses the duty cycles so that the averages are sines of the wanted frequency and size.

Why 0.866? The output is a mix of the input voltages, so an output line voltage must fit inside the gap between the highest and lowest input voltage. With input phase peak Vim, that gap swings between √3 Vim and its narrowest value, 1.5 Vim, when one input is at its peak and the other two are at −0.5 Vim. A clean output must fit the narrowest gap: peak output line voltage ≤ 1.5 Vim, so the output phase peak ≤ 1.5/√3 Vim = 0.866 Vim. The voltage transfer ratio q = Vo/Vin is therefore at most √3/2 = 0.866. The simplest Venturini method reaches only 0.5; adding third harmonics to the input and output references raises it to 0.866.

The output frequency is set by the references, so it can be above or below the input frequency. The LC filter turns the chopped input current into a near-sine, which the modulation keeps in phase with the input voltage.

Commutation, regeneration and comparison

Moving output A from input a to b at once shorts a to b; a gap opens the load. Four-step commutation uses the sign of the load current. For current flowing from a into A, SAa conducts through its forward IGBT, Aa+.

  1. Turn off Aa−, the device of SAa not carrying current.
  2. Turn on Ab+, so SAb can carry current in the same direction only.
  3. Turn off Aa+; the load current moves to Ab+.
  4. Turn on Ab−, so SAb can again carry current both ways.

At no step is there a two-way path between a and b, and the load always has a path. With bidirectional switches and no diode rectifier, a braking lift or crane motor returns energy naturally, and with no DC-link capacitor the converter is compact. Against it: the 0.866 limit, 18 IGBTs instead of 12, complex control and no stored energy to ride through a supply dip. A back-to-back drive gives full voltage and simple control but needs a bulky capacitor and an active front end to regenerate.

Takeaways

  1. An AC voltage controller changes only the RMS value; the output stays at the supply frequency.
  2. With a resistor, Vo = Vs√[(π − α + ½ sin 2α)/π] and the power factor equals Vo/Vs, falling because of harmonics and a lagging fundamental.
  3. With an RL load the SCRs conduct to β beyond π, control starts only at α = φ, and short gate pulses below φ give a one-way current.
  4. On-off control gives Vs√D with clean current while on, but subharmonics at 50/(n + m) Hz; it suits heaters, not lamps.
  5. A TRIAC fires in all four quadrants, Q4 worst: use negative pulses (Q2 and Q3) or pulses that follow MT2 (Q1 and Q3), as a DIAC does.
  6. A three-wire star controller has no neutral, so the phases interact, double pulses are needed and the output reaches zero at 150°, not 180°.
  7. A PWM AC chopper moves the unwanted frequencies up to fsw ± 50 Hz; with an input filter its supply power factor is close to 1.
  8. A cycloconverter builds a lower frequency from pieces of the supply with natural commutation; it needs two groups and suits large, slow drives.
  9. A matrix converter changes voltage and frequency directly with nine bidirectional switches, regenerates naturally, and is limited to q ≤ 0.866.

Points to cover. Terminals MT1, MT2, G; gate measured from MT1; Quadrants by MT2 and gate polarity, sketch; Q1 most sensitive, Q2 and Q3 moderate, Q4 least; Positive only: Q1 + Q4; negative only: Q2 + Q3; DIAC: Q1 + Q3; Wrong choice: late or missed firing, unequal half cycles, DC component.

Model answer.

  1. The TRIAC. A TRIAC has main terminals MT1 and MT2 and a gate G. It behaves as two antiparallel SCRs sharing one gate, so it can be turned on whichever way MT2 is biased and conducts until its current falls below the holding current. MT1 is the reference: the polarity of MT2 and the gate current are both measured from MT1.
  2. The four quadrants are the four combinations of MT2 polarity and gate-current polarity:
  3. Sensitivity. Q1 needs the smallest gate current. Q2 and Q3 need somewhat more. Q4 (MT2 negative, gate positive) needs the most; a typical data sheet asks for about twice the Q1 value, and three-quadrant TRIACs are not rated for Q4 at all. The internal reason is that in Q4 the gate injects carriers into a region where they are less effective at starting regeneration.
  4. In a phase controller the TRIAC must fire twice per cycle: at α with MT2 positive and at π + α with MT2 negative. The polarity of the gate pulses therefore fixes which two quadrants are used.
  5. (a) Positive pulses only. Positive half cycle: Q1, the best quadrant. Negative half cycle: Q4, the worst. A drive sized for Q1 may fire late or not at all in Q4, so this scheme is avoided.
  6. (b) Negative pulses only. Positive half: Q2. Negative half: Q3. Both are moderately sensitive, and the two halves behave alike. A microcontroller or logic driver does this naturally by pulling the gate below MT1, so it is the usual choice with an optocoupler or a transistor driver.
  7. (c) DIAC trigger. The DIAC breaks over at about 30 V in either direction and passes the charge of the timing capacitor to the gate. The capacitor sits across MT2 and MT1, so its voltage, and hence the pulse, has the polarity of MT2: positive gate in the positive half (Q1), negative gate in the negative half (Q3). These are the two most sensitive quadrants.

    Gate drivePositive half (MT2 +)Negative half (MT2 −)Verdict

    negative pulses onlyQ2Q3logic and microcontroller drives
    pulse follows MT2 (DIAC)Q1Q3RC-DIAC dimmers

In the worst case the TRIAC misses the negative half altogether and the load sees half-wave DC.
  10. Closing check with numbers: with a 230 V lamp of 529 Ω fired at 90° in both halves, each half delivers 25.0 W of the 50.0 W total; if the negative half is missed, only 25.0 W reaches the lamp and the current has an average value, which a symmetrical drive (b) or (c) never gives.

Within one supply half cycle α changes little, so each piece is a phase-controlled section of the rectified supply.
  8. Sketch: draw the 50 Hz supply dashed over 80 ms. On it draw the output as pieces of the rectified supply (each starting at its α) for 0 to 40 ms, positive, and as pieces of the inverted rectified supply for 40 to 80 ms, negative. Through the pieces draw the 12.5 Hz sine of peak 169.7 V that their averages follow. The load current, smoothed by the inductance, is close to a 12.5 Hz sine lagging the voltage.
  9. Input power factor: the input current lags by roughly α, so near the output zeros (α near 90°) the displacement factor is poor; the average over the cycle is low unless r is near 1.
  10. Closing check: at the output peak, 207.1 × cos 35.0° = 207.1 × 0.820 = 169.8 V, the wanted peak within rounding.
Apply A three-phase to three-phase matrix converter uses nine bidirectional switches. Explain the switching constraints and the number of allowed states, state and justify the maximum voltage transfer ratio, find the highest output line voltage available from a 400 V supply, and find the duty cycles of output A that give an average of 150 V at an instant when input a is at its positive peak. 12 marks

Points to cover. SXy joins input y to output X; two IGBTs per switch, 18 in all; Never two switches on one output; never none; 27 states: 6 + 18 + 3; q ≤ √3/2 from the narrowest input gap 1.5 Vim; 346 V line; dAa = 0.640, dAb = dAc = 0.180.

Model answer.

  1. Structure. Inputs a, b, c come through an LC filter; outputs A, B, C go to the load. Switch SXy joins input y to output X. Each is bidirectional: two IGBTs joined emitter to emitter with antiparallel diodes, so there are 18 IGBTs.
  2. Constraint 1: never close two switches feeding the same output. SAa and SAb together connect input a to input b through output A: a line-to-line short circuit.
  3. Constraint 2: never open all three switches of an output. The load is inductive, so its current must always have a path; interrupting it gives a destructive voltage spike.
  4. States. Exactly one switch per output is closed, so each output has 3 choices and there are 3³ = 27 states: 6 in which the outputs take three different inputs, 18 in which two outputs share one input, and 3 zero states with all outputs on the same input.
  5. Maximum transfer ratio. Over a switching period an output voltage is a weighted mix of the three input voltages, so every output line voltage must fit inside the gap between the highest and lowest input voltage at that instant. With input phase peak Vim:
    gap = max(va, vb, vc) − min(va, vb, vc)
largest gap  = √3 Vim  (two inputs at ±0.866 Vim)
smallest gap = 1.5 Vim  (one input at Vim, two at −0.5 Vim)
output line peak √3 Vom ≤ 1.5 Vim  so  Vom ≤ 0.866 Vim
  6. So q = Vo/Vin ≤ √3/2 = 0.866. The basic Venturini method reaches only 0.5; injecting third harmonics into the input and output references reaches 0.866.
  7. Highest output from 400 V: output line voltage = 0.866 × 400 = 346 V RMS, or 200 V RMS per phase.
  8. Duty cycles at the instant: input phase peak = 400√2/√3 = 326.6 V, so va = 326.6 V and vb = vc = −163.3 V. Taking dAb = dAc:
    150 = dAa(326.6) + (1 − dAa)(−163.3)
150 + 163.3 = dAa(326.6 + 163.3)
dAa = 313.3/489.9 = 0.640,  dAb = dAc = 0.180
  9. With a 10 kHz switching frequency (Ts = 100 µs) these are on-times of 64.0 µs, 18.0 µs and 18.0 µs, adding to 100 µs so output A is never left open.
  10. Closing check: 0.640 × 326.6 + 0.360 × (−163.3) = 209.0 − 58.8 = 150.2 V, the wanted 150 V within rounding.

For α = 120°, find the RMS phase voltage, the phase current, and the total power. Repeat with the neutral removed and explain the difference. 10 marks

Points to cover. With neutral: single-phase formula per phase; Vph = 230.9 V; Vo = 102.1 V; 2.55 A; 782 W; Without neutral: 90° to 150° formula; Vo = 48.0 V; 1.20 A; 173 W; Reason: no return path, two lines must conduct, zero at 150°.

Model answer.

  1. Supply phase voltage: Vph = 400/√3 = 230.9 V. α = 120° = 2π/3 rad.
  2. With the neutral, each phase is an independent single-phase controller:

   = 230.9 √[(3.142 − 2.094 + ½ × (−0.866))/3.142]
   = 230.9 √(0.614/3.142) = 230.9 × 0.442 = 102.1 V
  3. Phase current = 102.1/40 = 2.55 A. Power per phase = 102.1²/40 = 260.7 W; total = 3 × 260.7 = 782 W. The neutral carries the triplen harmonics of the three phase currents.

Analyze Compare phase control, integral-cycle control and PWM AC chopping for a 1 kW lamp dimmer on a 230 V supply, on harmonics, power factor, flicker, noise and filter needs, with numbers at half power. Recommend one for a domestic dimmer and justify. 13 marks

Points to cover. Half power: α = 90°, D = 0.5, chopper D = 0.5; Phase control: PF 0.707, 3rd harmonic 54 %, smooth, cheap, RFI; On-off: PF 0.707, subharmonics, visible flicker; PWM: PF near 1 with filter, sidebands at kHz, more devices; Recommendation: TRIAC phase control with an EMI choke and snubber.

Model answer.

  1. Load: a 1 kW lamp at 230 V has R = 230²/1000 = 52.9 Ω (taken constant). Half power, 500 W, needs Vo = 230 × √0.5 = 162.6 V RMS in every scheme.
  2. Phase control at half power needs α = 90° (bracket 0.5). The current is a chopped sine of RMS 162.6/52.9 = 3.07 A. Its fundamental lags by 32.5° and its third harmonic is 54 % of the fundamental, so PF = 0.707. Switching at the peak of the voltage causes a fast current step, which radiates radio interference and can make the lamp filament buzz; an EMI choke and a snubber are needed. Control is smooth, every half cycle.
  3. Integral-cycle control at half power needs D = 0.5, for example 5 cycles on and 5 off. PF = √0.5 = 0.707 as well, there are no 150 Hz harmonics and hardly any interference, because switching is at the voltage zero. But the pattern repeats every 0.2 s, so the lamp blinks at 5 Hz, which the eye sees very clearly. Using 1 on and 1 off raises the blink to 25 Hz, still visible as flicker on a filament lamp.
  4. PWM chopper at half power: the RMS output is Vs√D, so D = 0.5 gives 162.6 V into the lamp. The unwanted frequencies sit at fsw ± 50 Hz, for example 9950 Hz at 10 kHz, so a small LC input filter makes the supply current nearly sinusoidal and in phase: PF close to 1 instead of 0.707. It needs four IGBTs with gate drives, a controller and the filter, and it has switching losses and some audible noise below 20 kHz.

Between the other two, the domestic dimmer is a small load (1 kW): its low power factor and third harmonic cost the user nothing on a household tariff and are within what the supply tolerates for one appliance, while price and size matter a great deal.

Analyze Distinguish the circulating-current and non-circulating-current modes of a cycloconverter. Discuss the effect of each on output waveform quality, the intergroup reactor, changeover time, efficiency and control. 12 marks

Points to cover. Circulating: both groups fired, αP + αN = 180°, intergroup reactor; Smooth changeover, continuous current, extra loss and size; Non-circulating: one group at a time, dead time at current zero; No reactor, better efficiency, distortion near changeover; Choice by output frequency and rating.

Model answer.

  1. Background. A cycloconverter has a P group for positive load current and an N group for negative current, connected in antiparallel. The question is what the idle group does while the other conducts.
  2. Circulating-current mode. Both groups are fired all the time, with αP + αN = 180°, so their average output voltages are equal: (2Vm/π) cos αP = −(2Vm/π) cos αN. Their instantaneous voltages are not equal, so a current circulates through the two groups. An intergroup reactor (often centre-tapped) between them absorbs the instantaneous voltage difference and limits this current.
  3. Effects of the circulating mode: the load current can reverse at any instant, because both groups are ready, so the changeover is instantaneous and the output follows the reference smoothly with no dead time. Both groups conduct continuously, so even light loads see continuous current and a predictable output voltage. Against this, the reactor is large and heavy, it and the thyristors carry the circulating current in addition to the load current, so losses rise and efficiency falls, and the input power factor gets worse.
  4. Non-circulating mode. Only the group that must carry the present load current is fired; the other gets no pulses. When the reference calls for a change of current direction, the controller removes the pulses of the outgoing group, waits until the load current has fallen to zero, adds a short dead time (a millisecond or so) so the outgoing thyristors regain their blocking ability, and then fires the incoming group.
  5. Effects of the non-circulating mode: no reactor is needed and no circulating current flows, so the equipment is smaller and more efficient. But during each dead time the load current is held at zero, which flattens the current waveform around its zero crossings and distorts the output; at light load the current becomes discontinuous and the output voltage no longer follows the cosine law. The control needs a reliable zero-current detector: firing the incoming group too early shorts the supply through the two groups.
  6. Comparison:
    FeatureCirculating currentNon-circulating
    Groups firedboth, αP + αN = 180°one at a time
    Intergroup reactorneedednot needed
    Changeoverinstantaneousafter current zero plus dead time
    Output near current zerosmoothdistorted
    Efficiency and sizelower, largerhigher, smaller
    Riskreactor sizingshort circuit if the zero is misjudged
  7. Choice. Circulating mode suits drives that reverse current often or need very smooth low-frequency torque, where the waveform matters more than the losses. Non-circulating mode is the common choice for large mill and kiln drives, where efficiency and size dominate and the dead time is small compared with the long output period: at 5 Hz the output period is 200 ms, so a 1 ms dead time hardly shows.
  8. Closing check: in both modes the average output of the working group is (2Vm/π) cos α, so on a 230 V supply neither can give more than 207.1 V peak of fundamental.
Evaluate A 5 Hz to 20 Hz, 400 V three-phase induction motor drive is to be fed from a 50 Hz, 400 V supply. Evaluate three options: a cycloconverter, a matrix converter, and a rectifier with a DC link and PWM inverter. Choose one for (a) a 5 MW ore mill and (b) a 20 kW conveyor that brakes often, and justify with device count, voltage, power factor, frequency range and regeneration. 15 marks

Points to cover. Cycloconverter: 36 SCRs, fo ≤ 16.7 Hz, poor input PF at low voltage, very high power; Matrix: 18 IGBTs, q ≤ 0.866 (346 V from 400 V), sinusoidal input, natural regeneration; DC link: 6 diodes + 6 IGBTs, any frequency, full voltage, capacitor, no regeneration without active front end; Mill: cycloconverter; conveyor: matrix or active front end; Scoring table.

Model answer.

  1. Requirement. Output 5 Hz to 20 Hz at up to 400 V line, from a 400 V, 50 Hz supply. At 20 Hz the motor needs only about 20/50 × 400 = 160 V for constant flux, so the voltage need is modest at the low end.
  2. Cycloconverter. A three-phase to three-phase cycloconverter with six-pulse bridge groups uses 36 thyristors (18 with three-pulse groups). Thyristors are cheap, rugged and available in very large ratings, and commutation is natural, so it suits multi-megawatt drives. Its limits: good waveforms only up to about fs/3 = 16.7 Hz, so the top of the 5 Hz to 20 Hz range is outside the comfortable zone; the input power factor is poor whenever the output voltage is low (α near 90°), as it is at 5 Hz; and the input current carries harmonics and interharmonics. It regenerates, because each group can invert.
  3. Matrix converter. Nine bidirectional switches, 18 IGBTs. Any output frequency, including 20 Hz, with a small LC input filter giving a near-sinusoidal input current at unity power factor, and natural regeneration. Its limit is q ≤ 0.866: from 400 V the most it gives is 0.866 × 400 = 346 V line, enough for 20 Hz (160 V) but not for a 400 V motor at 50 Hz. It has no DC-link capacitor, so it is compact, but it cannot ride through supply dips and needs four-step commutation.
  4. Rectifier, DC link and PWM inverter. Six diodes, a DC-link capacitor and six IGBTs. Any frequency from 0 to well above 50 Hz, full output voltage, mature control and the lowest cost at small and medium power. A diode rectifier cannot return braking energy, so a braking resistor (energy wasted) or an active front end (six more IGBTs, total 12) is needed. The capacitor is bulky and ages, and the diode rectifier draws a distorted input current.

  6. (a) 5 MW ore mill at 5 Hz to 20 Hz. The speed is low, the torque enormous and the power in megawatts, where thyristors are the cheapest and most robust switches. Most of the time the mill runs at its low design speed, well inside fs/3. A cycloconverter (in practice feeding a gearless synchronous motor) is the established choice; a reactive-power compensator usually corrects its poor input power factor. Choose the cycloconverter, accepting reduced quality near 20 Hz.
  7. (b) 20 kW conveyor that brakes often. The power is small, the full 5 Hz to 20 Hz range is needed, and braking energy should be returned rather than burnt. Both the matrix converter and a DC-link drive with an active front end meet this. The matrix converter is more compact and needs no electrolytic capacitor, and its 346 V limit is no handicap because the motor needs only 160 V at 20 Hz. The active-front-end drive is cheaper and more familiar to maintain. Choose the matrix converter if size and capacitor life matter, otherwise the active-front-end drive; the cycloconverter is not suitable at 20 kW.

Evaluate Derive the input power factor of a single-phase phase-controlled AC voltage controller with a resistive load as a function of α, and split it into displacement and distortion factors at α = 90°. Discuss how its harmonic currents affect the supply transformer and the neutral of a three-phase four-wire installation, with numbers for three 20 Ω loads on 230 V at α = 90°. 13 marks

Points to cover. PF = Vo/Vs = √[(π − α + ½ sin 2α)/π]; Fundamental lags: at 90°, DF = 0.844, distortion factor = 0.838; Odd harmonics, 3rd 54 % of fundamental at 90°; Neutral: triplens add, 11.5 A against 8.13 A phase current; Transformer: extra losses, derating; remedies.

Model answer.

  1. Power factor. For a resistor the supply current is the load current, of RMS Vo/R, and P = Vo²/R:
    PF = P/(VsIs) = (Vo²/R)/(Vs × Vo/R) = Vo/Vs
PF(α) = √[(π − α + ½ sin 2α)/π]
  2. Values: PF = 1 at α = 0°, 0.897 at 60°, 0.707 at 90°, 0.442 at 120° and 0 at 180°.
  3. Displacement factor. The current flows from α to π in each half cycle, later than the voltage, so its 50 Hz part lags. With Vm/R as the scale:
    a1 = (Vm/2πR)(cos 2α − 1),  b1 = (Vm/2πR)[2(π − α) + sin 2α]
at α = 90°: a1 = −(Vm/R)/π, b1 = (Vm/R)/2
lag φ1 = tan⁻¹(0.318/0.5) = 32.5°,  displacement factor = cos 32.5° = 0.844
  4. Distortion factor. The fundamental peak is (Vm/R)√(0.318² + 0.5²) = 0.593 Vm/R, RMS 0.419 Vm/R; the total RMS current is 0.5 Vm/R. Distortion factor = 0.419/0.5 = 0.838. Check: 0.844 × 0.838 = 0.707 = PF at 90°. So a phase controller lowers the power factor through both a lagging fundamental and harmonics; it is wrong to say its displacement factor is unity.
  5. Harmonics of one 20 Ω load on 230 V at α = 90° (Vm/R = 16.26 A); the waveform has half-wave symmetry, so only odd orders appear:
    OrderFrequencyRMS current% of fundamental
    150 Hz6.82 A100
    3150 Hz3.66 A54
    5250 Hz1.22 A18
    7350 Hz1.22 A18
    9450 Hz0.73 A11
    total8.13 A
  6. Neutral of a four-wire system. Put one such load on each phase of a 400/230 V four-wire supply. The fundamentals and the 5th and 7th harmonics are 120° apart in the three phases and cancel in the neutral, but the triplens (3rd, 9th, …) are in phase in all three and add. The 3rd alone gives 3 × 3.66 = 11.0 A; adding all triplens, the neutral carries 11.5 A RMS, more than the 8.13 A in each phase. A neutral sized like, or smaller than, the phase conductors can overheat although every phase is within its rating.
  7. Supply transformer. Harmonic currents raise the winding eddy-current and stray losses, which grow roughly with the square of frequency, so the transformer runs hotter than its kVA reading suggests and must be derated. In a delta-star distribution transformer the triplens circulate in the delta winding and add heating there. Harmonic currents through the source impedance also distort the voltage seen by other consumers.

Glossary

AC voltage controller
A thyristor switch in series with an AC load that varies the RMS load voltage at the supply frequency; also called an AC voltage regulator.
Bidirectional switch
A switch that blocks voltage and carries current in both directions, usually two IGBTs joined emitter to emitter with antiparallel diodes.
Commutating dv/dt
The rate of rise of voltage across a TRIAC just after its current reaches zero; too fast a rise turns it back on.
Conduction angle γ
The angle for which an SCR conducts, β − α; π − α for a resistive load.
Cosine-wave crossing
Firing control in which an SCR fires where a reference sine meets a cosine timing wave, so that cos α follows the reference.
Cycloconverter
A direct AC-AC frequency changer made of two phase-controlled converter groups in antiparallel, with no DC link.
DIAC
A two-terminal bidirectional trigger device that breaks over at about 30 V and dumps a capacitor charge into a gate.
Double pulsing
Giving each SCR of a three-phase controller a second gate pulse 60° after the first, so two SCRs in different lines conduct together.
Duty ratio D
In on-off control, n/(n + m); in a PWM AC chopper, ton/T.
Extinction angle β
The angle at which the load current returns to zero and the SCR turns off; π for a resistor, more than π for an RL load.
Firing angle α
The angle from the zero crossing of the supply voltage to the gate pulse.
Four-step commutation
The four-stage gate sequence, based on the sign of the load current, that moves a matrix-converter output from one input to another without a short or an open load.
Holding current
The current below which a conducting thyristor turns off; smaller than the latching current.
Intergroup reactor
An inductor between the two groups of a circulating-current cycloconverter that limits the circulating current.
Latching current
The main current that must flow as the gate pulse ends for a thyristor to stay on.
Load angle φ
tan⁻¹(ωL/R) for a series R-L load; the smallest useful firing angle.
Matrix converter
A direct AC-AC converter with nine bidirectional switches that join any input line to any output line.
On-off (integral-cycle) control
Passing n whole supply cycles and blocking m whole cycles, switching at the voltage zero.
Optotriac
An LED and a light-fired TRIAC in one package, used to drive a power TRIAC from isolated logic; random-phase or zero-crossing types.
Phase control
Delaying the turn-on of the thyristors by the firing angle α in every half cycle, so only part of each half sine reaches the load.
PWM AC chopper
An AC voltage controller that switches the load between the supply and a short at several kilohertz, with series and freewheeling bidirectional switches.
Snubber
A series RC network across a switch that limits dv/dt and prevents false triggering.
Soft starter
A three-phase AC voltage controller that raises the voltage of an induction motor gradually to limit the starting current.
Subharmonic
A frequency component below the supply frequency, such as the 50/(n + m) Hz of on-off control.
TRIAC
A bidirectional three-terminal thyristor (MT1, MT2, gate) that behaves like two antiparallel SCRs with one gate.
Triggering quadrant
One of the four combinations of MT2 polarity and gate-current polarity of a TRIAC, both measured from MT1.
Triplen harmonics
Harmonics of order 3, 9, 15, …; in a balanced three-phase system they are in phase in all three lines (zero sequence).
Voltage transfer ratio q
Output to input phase voltage ratio of a matrix converter; at most √3/2 = 0.866 in the linear range.
