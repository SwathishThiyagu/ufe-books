5.1 AC voltage controllers: phase and on-off control

An AC voltage controller controls the RMS voltage delivered to an AC load without changing the 50 Hz frequency. It is a thyristor switch in series with the load: two SCRs back to back, or one TRIAC. Phase control delays the firing angle in every half cycle. On-off control passes whole cycles and blocks whole cycles. Lamp dimmers, heater and furnace controls, fan regulators and induction-motor soft starters use this idea. The same circuit is also called an AC voltage regulator in some syllabi.

You need: the SCR and natural commutation (Unit 1), and the firing angle of a controlled rectifier (Unit 2).

Circuit and control methods

The controller is a switch in series with the load, fed from an AC supply of RMS value Vs. Two SCRs connected back to back conduct in opposite half cycles. One TRIAC does the same job; see 5.2 for its gate behaviour.

In phase control the gate pulse is delayed by the firing angle α after each supply-voltage zero crossing. α is measured in radians in the formulas below. The positive SCR conducts from α to the point where its current falls to zero; the negative SCR does the same in the next half cycle. For a resistive load, current falls to zero at the voltage zero, so each half cycle conducts from α to π.

V<sub>rms</sub> = V<sub>s</sub>√(1 − α/π + sin(2α)/(2π))

Vrms is the load RMS voltage; Vs is the supply RMS voltage; α is the firing angle in radians, 0 ≤ α ≤ π for a resistive load.

On-off control, also called integral-cycle or burst control, gates the switch for n whole cycles and blocks it for m whole cycles. The duty ratio is D = n/(n+m). Over a burst period the RMS voltage is Vrms = Vs√D.

Comparing the two methods

Phase control is smooth and fast. The load voltage is a chopped sine, so the supply current contains low-order harmonics. For a resistive load the input power factor equals Vrms/Vs. At α = 90° this ratio is 0.707; near α = 180° it is very small, because the supply still applies the full voltage Vs while the load receives almost none of it, so little useful power flows for the supply rating used.

On-off control keeps the current a full sine during the on cycles. Its unwanted frequencies sit at the burst rate and sidebands, not at the low odd harmonics of phase control. The penalty is flicker: lamps visibly blink and motors feel torque pulses when the burst period is long. Large n and m reduce this, but make the response slow.

Choose phase control for lamp dimming and fan speed, where smooth adjustment matters. Choose on-off control for heaters and furnaces, where thermal inertia hides the burst and high power factor during on cycles is welcome.

Resistive and inductive loads

With a purely resistive load, the conduction angle in each half cycle is π − α. At α = 0 the load gets the full sine. At α = π it gets zero. The simple RMS formula above covers this case.

With an RL load, current lags voltage and cannot stop at the voltage zero. The SCR stays on until the current reaches zero at the extinction angle β. The conduction angle is γ = β − α. During conduction the current is

i(θ) = (V<sub>m</sub>/Z)[sin(θ − φ) − sin(α − φ)e<sup>−(θ−α)/tan φ</sup>]

Vm is the peak supply voltage; Z = √(R2 + (ωL)2); φ = tan−1(ωL/R); θ is angular time; β is the first θ > α where i(θ) = 0.

The resistive RMS formula is not valid for RL loads; you must integrate the load voltage or current over the actual conduction interval. For α below φ, the current from the previous half cycle has not yet fallen to zero when the next SCR receives its gate pulse. That SCR is therefore not forward biased at that instant, a short pulse is lost, and conduction fails to start. Use a wide (continuous) gate pulse, or keep α ≥ φ; with a wide pulse the current becomes continuous and control is lost. Some syllabi call the whole circuit an AC voltage regulator.

Applications

  • Lamp dimming: phase control with a TRIAC gives smooth brightness; the filament hides some harmonic effect.
  • Heater and furnace: on-off control at zero crossing switches at near-zero current, so stress is low; thermal mass smooths the burst.
  • Fan regulator: a TRIAC phase controller is common; see 5.2.
  • Induction-motor soft starter: phase control raises RMS voltage gradually, limiting starting current and torque.

Worked example

Question. A 230 V, 50 Hz supply feeds a 20 Ω resistive load through a single-phase AC voltage controller. Find the RMS output voltage, RMS current and load power for (a) phase control at α = 90°, (b) on-off control with duty cycle D = 0.5.

  1. For a resistive load at α = 90°, α = π/2 rad. The bracket is 1 − (π/2)/π + sin(2×π/2)/(2π) = 1 − 0.5 + sin π/(2π) = 0.5 + 0 = 0.5.
  2. Vrms = 230 × √0.5 = 162.6 V.
  3. Irms = Vrms/R = 162.6/20 = 8.13 A.
  4. P = Vrms2/R = (2302 × 0.5)/20 = 1322.5 W = 1.32 kW.
  5. For on-off control, D = 0.5 gives Vrms = 230 × √0.5 = 162.6 V. The same 20 Ω load takes 8.13 A and 1.32 kW.

Answer. Both methods give 162.6 V RMS, 8.13 A and 1.32 kW; phase control adds low-order harmonics, on-off control adds burst-rate flicker.

Exam traps

  • Using degrees in a formula that needs radians. α = 90° must be π/2; the bracket is 0.5, so Vrms/Vs = 0.707, not 0.9.
  • Using the phase-control RMS formula for an RL load. It holds only for a resistive load; for RL integrate over the actual conduction angle.
  • Confusing on-off duty ratio D with the phase-control ratio Vrms/Vs. For on-off, Vrms/Vs = √D, so D = 0.5 gives 0.707, not 0.5.
  • Forgetting that α has a valid range. For a resistive load 0 ≤ α ≤ 180°; for an RL load, a short gate pulse below the load angle may not start conduction.
  • Treating the TRIAC as a controlled rectifier. In this circuit it conducts in both half cycles; the two-SCR pair is its equivalent.

5.2 TRIAC-based phase controllers

A TRIAC is one three-terminal device that conducts in both directions, so a single TRIAC replaces the pair of antiparallel SCRs in an AC voltage controller and still needs only one trigger network. Delaying its turn-on in each half cycle cuts a slice out of every half wave, and the RMS voltage reaching the load falls smoothly. This is the circuit inside a domestic lamp dimmer, a ceiling-fan regulator, a room heater and a soldering iron — the cheapest way to control AC power from a 230 V, 50 Hz wall socket.

You need: Unit 1 for the TRIAC and DIAC structure, latching and holding current, and Chapter 5.1 for the phase-control waveform and its RMS formula.

One device, two directions

A TRIAC is a three-terminal power switch that conducts in both directions. Its two main terminals are MT1 and MT2. MT1 is the reference terminal: the gate voltage and the gate current are always measured with respect to MT1, never with respect to MT2. Inside, the silicon behaves as two SCRs connected in antiparallel that share one gate region.

With MT2 positive with respect to MT1, one SCR path is forward biased and a gate pulse latches it on. In the next half cycle MT2 is negative, the other path is forward biased, and a second pulse latches that one. The load sees alternating current, but each internal path carries only its own half cycle.

  • Latching current I_L: the current that must be flowing when the gate pulse ends for the device to stay on.
  • Holding current I_H: the current below which a conducting device turns off. It is smaller than I_L and is reached near every zero crossing.
  • Gate pulse: a few microseconds is enough. After latching the gate has no control, so a continuous gate current only heats the driver.

The four triggering quadrants

A triggering quadrant is one combination of the MT2 polarity and the gate current polarity. Two signs give four cases, Q1 to Q4. The same gate pulse does not fire the device equally well in all four.

Triggering is easiest when the gate current flows in the same direction as the MT2 voltage, that is Q1 (MT2 positive, gate positive) and Q3 (MT2 negative, gate negative). The mixed quadrants need more gate current: Q2 is low sensitivity, and Q4 — MT2 negative with a positive gate — is the weakest of all. Some data sheets quote no Q4 gate current at all.

A single-polarity driver, fed from a capacitor or a pulse transformer, must therefore be checked for the quadrant it reaches in each half cycle. The firing angle is set by the trigger network, but the gate pulse polarity has to follow the mains, or the negative half cycle fires late or not at all.

Trigger circuits and protection

The common dimmer uses a resistor–capacitor network and a DIAC. A potentiometer in series with a fixed resistor, and a capacitor, form a phase-shifting network across MT2 and MT1. The capacitor voltage lags the supply. When it reaches the DIAC breakover voltage, about 30 V, the DIAC snaps on and dumps the capacitor charge into the gate as one sharp pulse.

The potentiometer sets the RC time constant, hence the delay, hence the firing angle α. At minimum resistance the capacitor charges early, α is small and the lamp is bright. At maximum resistance the delay is long, α approaches 180°, and the lamp is dim.

A UJT or a PUT trigger does the same job with a programmable threshold: the capacitor charges through a resistor and discharges through the device into the gate. Both allow a pulse transformer, which isolates the control circuit from the mains. An optotriac with an LED input gives opto-isolated drive straight from a microcontroller pin. A zero-crossing optotriac version holds off until the mains voltage passes near zero, producing the whole-cycle on-off control of Chapter 5.1 with very little switching noise.

A series RC snubber across MT1 and MT2 limits the rate of rise of voltage dv/dt at turn-off. Without it, the fast voltage step injects charge through the junction capacitance and can fire the TRIAC with no gate pulse: the lamp flickers, or the device fails. Typical values are 100 Ω with 100 nF, checked against the device data sheet.

Worked example

Question. A 230 V, 50 Hz supply feeds a 100 W incandescent lamp through a TRIAC phase controller. The DIAC trigger network is set so the TRIAC fires at α = 90° in every half cycle. Find the lamp resistance, the RMS voltage across the lamp, the RMS current and the power delivered.

  1. Rated lamp data: the lamp is specified for 230 V, 100 W, so at rated conditions R = V²/P = 230²/100 = 52900/100 = 529 Ω.
  2. Supply: V_s = 230 V RMS at 50 Hz, so the peak is 230√2 = 325.3 V and each half cycle lasts 10 ms.
  3. Firing angle: α = 90° = π/2 rad. The TRIAC conducts from α to 180° in each half cycle, so the conduction angle is also 90°.
  4. RMS load voltage from the AC controller formula V_rms = V_s √[(1/π)(π − α + sin2α/2)]. Here sin2α = sin180° = 0, so V_rms = 230 √[(π − π/2)/π] = 230 √0.5 = 230 × 0.707 = 162.61 V.
  5. RMS current: I_rms = V_rms/R = 162.61/529 = 0.3074 A.
  6. Power in the lamp: P = V_rms I_rms = V_rms²/R = 162.61²/529 = 26442/529 = 49.98 W, which is half of the 100 W rating, so the lamp runs at half brightness.
  7. Cross-check with the current form: P = I_rms²R = 0.3074² × 529 = 0.0945 × 529 = 49.98 W, the same value.

Answer. R = 529 Ω, V_rms = 162.61 V, I_rms = 0.3074 A and P = 49.98 W, that is half brightness at half power.

Exam traps

  • Naming the quadrant from the gate polarity alone. MT2 positive with a positive gate is Q1, but the same positive pulse with MT2 negative is Q4, the least sensitive quadrant.
  • Believing the gate must stay energised for the whole half cycle. After latching the gate does nothing; the device turns off only when the current falls below the holding current.
  • Swapping latching current and holding current. Latching current is needed at turn-on while the gate pulse is ending; holding current is the level at which a conducting device drops out.
  • Curing false triggering by raising the gate resistor. The usual cause is excessive dv/dt, and the cure is an RC snubber and a correct snubber value, not a weaker gate drive.
  • Using the controlled-rectifier formula V_rms = V_s cos α for a TRIAC dimmer. At α = 90° the dimmer gives 0.707 V_s, not zero.

The pulse starts the regenerative action inside the device and both internal transistors latch. Once the anode current exceeds the latching current the gate loses control, so the pulse can end. 5.3 Three-phase AC voltage controllers

A three-phase AC voltage controller sets the RMS voltage reaching a three-phase load by delaying thyristor firing in every cycle. It exists because three-phase heaters, soft starters and capacitor-bank switches need smooth, lossless voltage control without a transformer tap changer. The three phases interact: in a three-wire star or delta load there is no neutral wire, so the three load currents must add to zero. In a three-wire star, changing the firing angle of one phase changes the voltages of the other two. This chapter covers the three common connections and their firing range.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for the single-phase controller, Unit 1 for SCR pairs, and Unit 2 for three-phase source quantities.

Three connections and the neutral

A three-phase AC voltage controller is three thyristor pairs, one in each phase or branch. The pairs can be anti-parallel SCRs or, at low power, TRIACs (see Chapter 5.2). The connection decides whether the phases are independent.

Four-wire star: three separate single-phase controllers with the neutral brought out. Each phase sees its own pair and its own return through the neutral. Set α from 0° to 180° in each phase independently. The neutral carries the sum of the three phase currents, including zero-sequence current. Use this for unbalanced loads or when each phase must be trimmed.

Three-wire star: six thyristors feed a star load with no neutral wire. The three load currents must add to zero at every instant. A change in one firing angle shifts the star point and changes the voltages on the other two phases. For a balanced load, fire all six devices symmetrically with the same α.

Delta: six thyristors sit in the three branches of a delta load. Each branch voltage is a line-to-line voltage. The line current is the difference of two branch currents. This connection is used for high-current loads. Triplen currents can circulate inside the delta instead of entering the supply lines. Some syllabi call the whole family a three-phase AC voltage regulator.

Firing range for a resistive star load

Take a balanced star load of resistance R per phase and a supply with RMS line voltage V_L. For 0 ≤ α ≤ 60°, the RMS load phase voltage is:

V_o,ph = V_ph √[1 − (3α/2π) + (3 sin 2α)/(4π)]

V_o,ph is the RMS phase voltage across the load; V_ph is the RMS supply phase voltage V_L/√3; α is the firing angle in radians; this form holds for 0 ≤ α ≤ 60°.

At α = 0 the load phase voltage is the full supply phase voltage. As α rises to 60°, conduction stays continuous, with at least two devices conducting, but the RMS voltage falls. At α = 60° it is about 84% of full. Above 60°, gaps appear, the current becomes discontinuous, and the RMS falls faster. At α = 150° the output reaches zero. The usable range is therefore 0° to 150°, not 0° to 180°.

In the four-wire star each phase is independent and α can go to 180°. In the delta, each branch pair sees the line-to-line voltage and behaves as a single-phase controller: conduction stays continuous up to α = 90°, and the branch output falls to zero at α = 180°. The 60° and 150° landmarks belong to the three-wire star, not to the delta. Do not apply the single-phase RMS formula to one phase of a three-wire star. The floating star point makes the three phase voltages a set, not three separate outputs. An inductive load shifts the extinction angles; the resistive case is the exam standard.

Harmonics, firing symmetry and uses

Symmetrical firing uses the same α for all six devices. The three phase currents stay balanced. With symmetrical firing in a balanced three-phase load, the line currents contain harmonics of order 6k ± 1: 5th, 7th, 11th, 13th and so on. Triplen orders (3rd, 9th, 15th) are the zero-sequence family, discussed next. In a three-wire star or delta load, triplen harmonics (3rd, 9th, 15th) are absent from the line currents. A triplen harmonic is in phase in all three phases. Its sum is not zero, so it needs a neutral return. With no neutral, it cannot flow in the lines. In a four-wire star it returns in the neutral; in a delta it circulates in the delta.

Asymmetrical firing uses different α values in different phases or half-cycles. It can produce a DC component and triplen currents. Use it only where a neutral or delta path can absorb the zero-sequence current, or for a short start where the DC component is acceptable.

Uses: three-phase resistance heaters, motor soft starters, and static switches for capacitor banks. A soft starter starts with a large firing angle (near 180°) and reduces it slowly toward 0°, so the motor voltage ramps up from a small value without a large inrush current. A static switch turns a capacitor bank on and off without contact wear. Link to Chapter 5.4 for PWM control.

Worked example

Question. A 415 V, 50 Hz, three-phase supply feeds a star-connected resistive load of 10 Ω per phase through a three-phase AC voltage controller. (a) For α = 60°, find the RMS phase voltage, the line current and the total power. (b) At what firing angle does the output fall to zero?

  1. The supply phase voltage is V_ph = 415/√3 = 239.60 V.
  2. At α = 60° = π/3 rad, conduction is just continuous. Use the resistive star formula: V_o,ph = V_ph × √[1 − (3α/2π) + (3 sin 2α)/(4π)].
  3. Inside the square root: 1 − (3 × π/3)/(2π) + (3 sin 120°)/(4π) = 1 − 0.5 + 0.2067 = 0.7067.
  4. √0.7067 = 0.8407. So V_o,ph = 239.60 × 0.8407 = 201.43 V.
  5. In star, line current equals phase current: I = V_o,ph/R = 201.43/10 = 20.14 A.
  6. Power per phase = V_o,ph²/R = 201.43²/10 = 4057.4 W. Total power = 3 × 4057.4 = 12172 W = 12.17 kW.
  7. The output reaches zero at α = 150°.

Answer. V_o,ph = 201.43 V, I = 20.14 A, P = 12.17 kW; the output falls to zero at α = 150°.

Exam traps

  • Using the single-phase controller formula on each phase of a three-wire star. With no neutral the phase currents must sum to zero; the star point moves.
  • Calling α = 60° the full-voltage point. It is only the edge of continuous conduction; the RMS phase voltage is about 84% of the supply phase voltage.
  • Thinking the output falls to zero at α = 180° in a three-wire star. The zero point is α = 150° because a firing pulse at that instant comes after the relevant line-to-line voltage has already reversed, so no pair of devices can be turned on and no current can start.
  • Mixing line and phase quantities. In star, line current equals phase current, but line voltage is √3 times phase voltage. In delta, line current is not the branch current.
  • Expecting triplen harmonics in the line currents of a three-wire load. They need a neutral or a delta path.

5.4 PWM AC voltage controllers

A PWM AC chopper switches the load between the 50 Hz supply and a short, at a frequency of a few kilohertz. The fraction of each switching period spent on the supply, the duty ratio D, sets the RMS voltage the load receives. Unlike phase control, no part of the cycle is skipped: the load is fed in every switching period, so the supply current stays close to a sine and the input power factor stays high. A small filter removes the high-frequency ripple.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for the phase-control baseline, Unit 1 for IGBT and MOSFET switching and losses, and Unit 4 for the PWM idea.

Two bidirectional switches

The power stage has two switches. S1, the series switch, connects the supply to the load. S2, the freewheeling switch, lies across the load. Both must be bidirectional: two IGBTs or MOSFETs joined emitter to emitter, so that one device conducts while the other blocks, in each polarity. A TRIAC is bidirectional but far too slow to switch at kilohertz rates (see Chapter 5.2).

Why bidirectional? The load current is AC. In the positive half cycle it flows one way, in the negative half the other. Each switch must block voltage of either polarity and carry current in both directions, so one device with one antiparallel diode is not enough.

  1. S1 closed, S2 open: the load sees the supply voltage v_s.
  2. S1 opens, then S2 closes: the load voltage falls to nearly zero and the load current freewheels through S2.
  3. S2 opens, then S1 closes: the next on-time begins.
  4. The gate signals are complementary with a dead time between them, so S1 and S2 are never on together and the supply is never shorted.

The switching period T is typically 20 µs to 200 µs, that is f_s from 5 kHz to 50 kHz. During the on-time t_on the load is connected; for the rest of T it is shorted. A resistive load sees a rectangular chopped voltage. An inductive load current keeps flowing through S2 during the off-time, smoothed by its own inductance.

Duty ratio, RMS value and harmonics

D = t_on / T,   V_rms = V_s √D,   V_1 = D · V_s

D is the duty ratio, between 0 and 1; t_on is the on-time of the series switch in seconds; T is the switching period in seconds; V_s is the RMS supply voltage in volts; V_rms is the RMS load voltage in volts; V_1 is the RMS value of the supply-frequency component of the load voltage, in volts.

Where V_s√D comes from: the square of the load voltage equals v_s² during t_on and zero otherwise. Its average is D times the average of v_s², so V_rms² = D·V_s² and V_rms = V_s√D.

The chopped waveform is v_s(t) multiplied by a switching function that is 1 for a fraction D of the period and 0 for the rest. Multiplying by a square wave creates sidebands at m·f_s ± f, where m = 1, 2, 3, … and f is the supply frequency. The lowest harmonic sits at f_s − f, which for f_s = 10 kHz and f = 50 Hz is 9950 Hz. The supply-frequency term has value D·v_s, so V_1 = D·V_s.

The input current is the load current gated by the same switching function. For a resistive load the supply-frequency part of the input current is D·v_s/R, exactly in phase with v_s. The gating adds high-frequency ripple, which a small input filter removes. Displacement factor is close to 1, so power factor stays high even at half output; phase control cannot do this, because delaying conduction makes the current lag.

Switching frequency, filter and applications

Raising f_s shrinks the filter: inductor and capacitor values fall roughly as 1/f_s, so a 40 kHz chopper needs about one-eighth of the filter a 5 kHz one needs. It also pushes ripple above the audible range. The price is switching loss, which rises roughly in proportion to f_s (see Unit 1). Device loss, gate drive power and heat sink size set the upper limit; 5 kHz to 20 kHz covers most line-frequency designs.

The filter is a small LC low-pass at the input. Its corner frequency sits between 50 Hz and f_s, commonly a few hundred hertz to 2 kHz. It carries the full load current, so the inductor is the bulky part.

  • AC voltage stabilisers and dynamic voltage restorers: a series chopper corrects a feeder voltage sag in milliseconds.
  • Solid-state tap changers: they replace the mechanical tap changer on a distribution transformer.
  • Fast lamp dimming, for example in studios and theatres, without the flicker phase control gives at low settings.
  • Soft starting of induction motors: the voltage rises smoothly as D is increased.

Three-phase versions use three choppers, one per phase, driven from the same duty ratio. Each phase needs its own freewheeling path, and in a three-wire load the freewheeling current must return through the other phases, so the switching patterns are interleaved. Only a few universities teach the three-phase detail. Some syllabi call this whole family an AC chopper or a switched-mode AC controller.

Worked example

Question. A 230 V, 50 Hz supply feeds a 50 Ω resistive load through a single-phase PWM AC chopper with a freewheeling switch. The switching frequency is 10 kHz and the duty ratio is D = 0.6. Find the on-time, the RMS load voltage, the load power, the RMS value of the supply-frequency component of the load voltage, and the frequency of the lowest harmonic in the load voltage.

  1. Switching period T = 1/f_s = 1/10 000 Hz = 100 µs. On-time t_on = D·T = 0.6 × 100 µs = 60 µs.
  2. RMS load voltage: V_rms = V_s√D = 230 × √0.6 = 230 × 0.7746 = 178.2 V.
  3. Load power: P = V_rms²/R = (178.16 V)²/50 Ω = 31 740/50 = 634.8 W.
  4. Supply-frequency component: V_1 = D·V_s = 0.6 × 230 = 138.0 V. It is smaller than V_rms, as it must be.
  5. The lowest harmonic lies at f_s − f = 10 000 − 50 = 9950 Hz, harmonic order 199. The next ones sit at f_s + f = 10 050 Hz and around 2f_s.

Answer. t_on = 60 µs, V_rms = 178.2 V, P = 634.8 W, supply-frequency component 138.0 V, lowest harmonic at 9950 Hz, about 200 times the supply frequency.

Exam traps

  • V_o = D·V_s is the supply-frequency component, not the RMS value. The RMS value of the chopped waveform is V_s√D, which is always larger: 178.2 V against 138.0 V at D = 0.6.
  • With no dead time, S1 and S2 conduct together and the supply is shorted through two devices. The current spike destroys them.
  • One IGBT with an antiparallel diode is not a bidirectional switch; it blocks only one polarity. Two devices back to back are needed.
  • The freewheeling switch carries the full load current when the series switch is off. Ignoring 1–2 V of device drop is fine at 230 V, but not at 12 V.
  • The input filter corner must sit well below f_s. A corner too near the switching frequency leaves ripple on the supply current and spoils the power factor.

5.5 Cycloconverters: step-up and step-down

A cycloconverter changes an AC supply of one frequency into an AC output of another frequency without a DC link. It connects the load to the supply through two phase-controlled converter groups, one for each output half cycle. By slowly changing the firing angle of the conducting group, the average output follows a sine of the wanted frequency. This is used for low-speed, high-torque drives such as cement kilns, rolling mills and ship propulsion, where an ordinary inverter-fed motor would need a large gearbox.

You need the average output of a phase-controlled bridge (Unit 2) and firing-angle control (Chapter 5.1).

Two groups, one output cycle

The power circuit has two identical phase-controlled bridges connected in antiparallel between supply and load. Call them the P group and the N group. The P group passes current in one direction and gives a positive average voltage; the N group passes current in the opposite direction and gives a negative average voltage. In the common non-circulating mode, only one group conducts at a time.

For the positive output half, the P group is fired. For the negative half, the N group is fired. For a single-phase bridge group, V_d = (2 V_m/π) cos α, where V_m is the peak supply voltage. A three-phase bridge group gives V_d = (3 V_ml/π) cos α, where V_ml is the peak line-to-line supply voltage. Here α is measured from the supply zero crossing where V_d is largest. A large α gives small V_d; a small α gives large V_d.

A sine output comes from changing α during the output half cycle. The wanted output is v_o(t) = V_om sin(2π f_o t), where V_om is its peak and f_o is the output frequency. The control sets cos α proportional to sin(2π f_o t). The bridge then produces short pieces of supply-frequency voltage whose local average follows the sine. The load inductance removes most of the supply-frequency ripple.

The figure shows a 50 Hz supply and a 25 Hz output. The P group conducts for 20 ms, then the N group for 20 ms. One output cycle is 40 ms, so f_o = 25 Hz. The output is a staircase, not a pure sine.

This 1:2 ratio is drawn only to show group changeover; the output is coarse, at two supply half cycles per output half cycle, and is outside the f_o ≤ f_s/3 quality limit. For a smooth staircase use f_o = f_s/3, e.g. 16.7 Hz from 50 Hz.

Firing control and the step-down limit

The firing angle cannot jump instantly. A practical control uses cosine-wave crossing. A cosine wave at supply frequency is compared with a slowly varying reference proportional to the wanted output. The firing pulse is given when the reference crosses the cosine wave.

α = cos⁻¹(k sin ω_o t)

α is the firing angle in radians; for a single-phase group k = π V_om/(2 V_m) is the voltage ratio and k ≤ 1; ω_o = 2π f_o is the output angular frequency in rad/s; t is time in s.

This method keeps the average output proportional to the reference and reduces harmonic distortion. Two connection modes exist. In circulating-current mode, both groups receive firing pulses all the time. Their instantaneous voltages differ, so current circulates between them. An intergroup reactor limits this current. The reactor adds loss and cost, but the output current is continuous and the change from P to N is smooth. In non-circulating mode, only the group that must conduct is gated; the other is held blocked. The control waits until load current falls to zero before releasing the other group. A small dead time is needed. This mode is cheaper and more efficient but gives a rougher waveform at light load.

Step-down means f_o < f_s, where f_s is the supply frequency. The output is built from a limited number of supply half cycles. If f_o is too close to f_s, each output half cycle contains only one or two supply half cycles, and the staircase is a poor sine. A practical quality limit is f_o ≤ f_s/3. For a 50 Hz supply, the useful output goes up to about 16.7 Hz. Above this limit the output has large low-order harmonics and the motor torque pulsates.

For an RL load, current lags voltage. At large α and light inductance, current may become discontinuous. The average output then no longer follows the simple cosine formula, and the control must compensate. The input displacement factor is close to cos α, so low output voltage means poor input power factor.

Step-up operation, load effects, applications

Step-up means f_o > f_s. The output must change faster than the supply can naturally commutate the thyristors. Natural commutation fails because the supply voltage does not reverse at the needed time. Step-up cycloconverters therefore need forced commutation, or turn-off devices such as GTOs or IGBTs. The waveform is poor: the output contains subharmonics, that is, frequencies below f_o, and switching loss is higher. Step-up cycloconverters are rarely used in new designs.

Load inductance affects changeover between groups. In non-circulating mode, the control waits for zero current. If current is discontinuous, output voltage rises toward the peak supply voltage during the gap, so the waveform is distorted. In circulating-current mode, the intergroup reactor keeps current continuous, but it wastes power.

The input displacement factor varies through the output cycle. Near the output peak, α is small and the displacement factor is good. Near the zero crossing, α is near 90° and the displacement factor is poor. The supply current also contains harmonics at multiples of f_s and sidebands around them.

Cycloconverters suit low-speed, high-torque drives: cement kilns, rolling mills, ship propulsion and mine hoists. These loads run at a few hertz to about one third of supply frequency and need large torque at low speed. A cycloconverter can feed a synchronous or induction motor directly at that frequency, without a DC link. For higher speeds and ordinary pumps or fans, a DC-link inverter (Unit 4) is smaller and cheaper.

Exam traps

  • Do not draw a DC link or smoothing capacitor inside a cycloconverter. The two converter groups connect the supply straight to the load.
  • Do not claim that any output frequency can be produced with good quality. Step-down quality needs f_o ≤ f_s/3; above that the staircase is too coarse.

  • In non-circulating mode the outgoing group must be blocked until load current reaches zero. Ignoring this dead time gives a short circuit between groups.

5.6 Matrix converters

A matrix converter is a direct AC-to-AC converter. Nine bidirectional switches connect three input phases to three output phases. By closing the right switches for the right times, it makes an output of chosen voltage and frequency. There is no rectifier, no DC link and no large capacitor. The same switches carry current in both directions, so power can flow back to the supply. Compact motor drives, wind turbines and aircraft supplies use it where size and reliability matter.

You need: Unit 1 for bidirectional switches, Chapter 5.4 for PWM chopping, and Chapter 5.5 for building a new frequency by switching.

The 3×3 switch array

A matrix converter connects three input phases a, b, c to three output phases A, B, C through nine switches. The nine switches are numbered row by row. S1, S2, S3 connect inputs a, b, c to output A; S4, S5, S6 connect a, b, c to output B; S7, S8, S9 connect a, b, c to output C. Every switch is bidirectional: it carries current and blocks voltage in both directions. A common build is two IGBTs with series diodes, connected in antiparallel. The switch array replaces the rectifier, DC link and inverter of a back-to-back drive.

Two rules keep the circuit safe. First, never close two switches in the same output row. If S1 and S2 close together, input phases a and b meet at output A: a line-to-line short. Second, never leave an output row with all three switches open. An inductive load current must have a path; breaking it causes a voltage spike. Most modulation therefore keeps exactly one switch closed in each output row. In many states two or three outputs share one input phase. The zero states connect all three outputs to a single input. The mix of states chosen sets the input current waveform and its balance.

Each output row has three choices, so 3³ = 27 switch states are possible. The figure shows one state: S2, S6 and S7 closed, so A gets b, B gets c and C gets a. Over a switching period the controller moves through states so the average output follows the wanted sine.

Duty cycles and the 0.866 limit

Let Ts be the switching period, chosen much shorter than the output period. Let dAa, dAb, dAc be the fractions of Ts for which output A is connected to inputs a, b, c. For output A, dAa + dAb + dAc = 1. The average output phase voltage over one switching period is vA = dAava + dAbvb + dAcvc. Here va, vb, vc are the instantaneous input phase voltages. Similar equations hold for outputs B and C.

The scalar or Venturini method solves for duty cycles that make vA, vB, vC sinusoids at the wanted output frequency ωo. Space-vector modulation does the same with vectors and a switching table. In both, the output average is a weighted average of the three input voltages at that instant.

Let Vin be the input phase RMS voltage and Vo the output phase RMS voltage. The voltage transfer ratio is q = Vo / Vin. In the linear range q ≤ √3/2 ≈ 0.866. The limit comes from the envelope of the three input phase voltages. At any instant the output must lie between the largest and the smallest of va, vb, vc. That envelope has peak Vin,peak and its lowest peak is Vin,peak × cos 30° = (√3/2)Vin,peak, so the biggest clean sine output has peak (√3/2) times the input peak. Here Vin,peak = √2 Vin is the peak input phase voltage. A request for q = 1 forces duty cycles outside the valid range; the output clips and distorts.

The input current is chopped by the switches. A small LC input filter smooths it. The filter also removes the high-frequency components that would otherwise pollute the supply.

Commutation, regeneration and use

Bidirectional switches cannot change over instantly. If one switch opens before the next closes, the load current is interrupted. If both close, the supply is shorted. Four-step current-direction commutation solves this. The controller senses or estimates the current direction, then uses a short sequence of make-before-break steps that always leaves a safe path. The cost is extra switching logic.

Power can flow from supply to load or from load to supply. The switches are bidirectional and there is no diode rectifier to block reverse power. A motor can brake and return energy to the line. This natural regeneration is useful in lifts, cranes and wind turbines.

There is no DC-link capacitor. That removes a bulky, life-limited component and gives a compact converter. The price is a maximum voltage gain of 0.866, more complex control and an input filter that must be designed for the chopped current.

Compare a back-to-back inverter: rectifier, DC-link capacitor, inverter. It is simpler and can use standard motor control, but the capacitor is large and regeneration needs an active front end. A matrix converter is direct, bidirectional and compact. It suits integrated motor drives, wind power and aerospace supplies where weight and reliability matter.

Worked example

Question. A 415 V, 50 Hz three-phase supply feeds a star-connected load of R = 20 Ω per phase through a three-phase matrix converter. The voltage transfer ratio is q = 0.5 and the output frequency is 30 Hz. Find the output phase voltage, output current and total power. Check whether the design is valid.

  2. Output phase voltage: Vo = q × Vph,in = 0.5 × 239.6 = 119.8 V.
  3. Output current per phase: I = Vo / R = 119.8 / 20 = 5.99 A.
  4. Power per phase: Pph = Vo² / R = 119.8² / 20 = 717.6 W.

Exam traps

  • Do not close two switches in the same output row. That connects two input phases to one output and short-circuits the supply. If a diagram looks rotated, identify rows and columns first.
  • Do not expect q = 1. Linear modulation stops at 0.866; above that the output is not a clean sine.
  • No DC-link capacitor does not mean no stored energy. The input filter and the load inductance store energy, and the filter still needs design.
  • Never leave an output leg open with an inductive load; the current path must be continuous.
  • The voltage transfer ratio q is an output-to-input phase voltage ratio. It is not a transformer turns ratio and it does not set the output frequency; the switching does.

Frequency ratioThe ratio of output frequency to input frequency fo/fi; it is usually less than 1 (step-down), and stepped-up operation is possible only over a small range.5.5
Matrix converterNine bidirectional switches that connect any input phase to any output phase directly, giving amplitude and frequency control with no DC link.5.6
Bidirectional switchTwo IGBTs (or other devices) connected back to back with common emitter or common collector so that current can flow both ways when the switch is on.5.6
Duty-cycle constraintFor each output phase the three switch duty cycles must sum to one, and the output voltage must stay inside the input voltage envelope.5.6
Maximum voltage gain, matrix converterIn linear modulation the output voltage cannot exceed √3/2 ≈ 0.866 times the input voltage.5.6

Takeaways

  1. An AC voltage controller changes only the RMS value of a fixed-frequency supply; it never changes the frequency.
  2. Phase control is simple and cheap, but it chops the sine wave, so the load sees harmonics and the supply sees a poor power factor at large α.
  3. On-off control passes or blocks whole cycles, so the load current is always a clean sine when it flows, but the supply current has a low-frequency flicker content.
  4. A TRIAC replaces two SCRs, but the gate pulse polarity must match the MT2 polarity or the two half cycles fire unequally.
  5. A three-phase controller without a neutral cannot carry zero-sequence current, so its control range and waveforms differ from three independent single-phase controllers.
  6. A PWM AC chopper pushes the switching harmonics to kilohertz frequencies, where a small filter removes them, at the cost of more switching loss and an extra switch.
  7. A cycloconverter is a naturally commutated direct frequency changer, mostly used to step the frequency down for large, slow machines.
  8. A matrix converter is an all-silicon direct frequency changer with no DC link, but it needs bidirectional switches and its voltage gain is limited to about 0.866.

Exam question bank

Long answers here almost always demand a waveform sketch plus a derivation of the RMS output, and a comparison of phase control, on-off control, PWM chopping, cycloconversion and matrix conversion on power factor, harmonics and device count.

Remember and understand

Understand Explain the working of a single-phase AC voltage controller feeding a resistive load. Define the firing angle and the conduction angle, sketch the supply voltage, gate pulses and load voltage for α = 60°, and derive the expression for the RMS output voltage. 12 marks

Circuit: anti-parallel SCR pair (or TRIAC) in series with the load across the AC supply; T1 conducts from α to π, T2 from π + α to 2π, so positive and negative half cycles are chopped symmetrically; Definition of α and of conduction angle π − α for a resistive load; Three sketches: supply, gate pulses, output voltage with the shaded chopped region; Derivation: V_rms² = (1/π)∫ V_m² sin²θ dθ from α to π, giving V_rms = V_s√[(π − α)/π + sin(2α)/(2π)]; Limits: α = 0 gives V_rms = V_s, α = π gives zero, and the control is continuous.

Remember Describe integral-cycle (on-off) control of an AC load. Define the duty ratio k, give the RMS output formula, and state two advantages and two disadvantages compared with phase control. 10 marks

The thyristor pair is turned on at a zero crossing and kept on for n whole cycles out of every N; Duty ratio k = n/N, and the RMS output is V_s√k; Advantages: no chopped waveform, so the current during conduction is a pure sine with low harmonic content and near-unity displacement factor; Disadvantages: any value of k other than 0 or 1 injects sub-harmonic (low-frequency flicker) components; large N gives slow response; Application: heaters and ovens where thermal inertia smooths the flicker; not for lamps or fast servos.

Understand With a neat sketch of device structure and characteristics, explain the TRIAC and the four triggering quadrants. Why should the gate pulse polarity suit the MT2 polarity? 12 marks

Structure: two SCRs in anti-parallel on one chip, terminals MT1, MT2 and gate, MT1 as reference; V-I characteristic: conduction in both directions, with latching and holding current in each direction; Four quadrants defined by MT2 polarity and gate current polarity; Quadrants I and III (gate pulse of the same polarity as MT2) are the most sensitive; quadrant IV is the least sensitive; A pulse of the wrong polarity fires late or not at all, so the two half cycles differ: DC component, even harmonics, possible transformer saturation; Practical rule: use quadrant I/III triggering or a negative gate current train for all quadrants.

Understand Describe the different configurations of three-phase AC voltage controllers. Why does a three-wire star or delta load behave differently from three independent single-phase controllers? 12 marks

Configurations: three controllers in each line, star-connected load with neutral, star without neutral, and delta-connected load; Neutral-connected star: each phase is independent, and α has the full 0° to 180° range; Three-wire star or delta: no path for zero-sequence current, so the phase currents must sum to zero and the neutral point of the load shifts; Two devices conduct at a time, and the control range with a resistive load becomes 0° to 150°; Consequences: non-sinusoidal phase voltages, triplen harmonics in the line-to-neutral voltages but not in the line currents; Delta connection: triplen harmonic currents circulate inside the delta and do not reach the supply.

Apply

Apply A 230 V, 50 Hz supply feeds a 20 Ω resistor through an anti-parallel SCR pair. For α = 90°, calculate the RMS output voltage, the RMS current and the power delivered. Also state the power factor. 10 marks

Use V_rms = V_s√[(π − α)/π + sin(2α)/(2π)] with α = π/2; sin(2α) = sin(π) = 0, so V_rms = 230 × √0.5 = 162.6 V; I_rms = 162.6/20 = 8.13 A; P = V_rms²/R = 162.6²/20 ≈ 1322 W; Power factor = V_rms/V_s = 0.707 (resistive load, displacement factor unity but distortion present).

Apply A 230 V, 50 Hz heater of 20 Ω is fed through a pair of thyristors using integral-cycle control, 25 on-cycles out of every 100. Find the RMS voltage, the power and the input power factor. State the fundamental frequency of the supply current envelope. 10 marks

Duty ratio k = n/N = 25/100 = 0.25; V_rms = V_s√k = 230 × 0.5 = 115 V; P = V_rms²/R = 115²/20 ≈ 661 W; Power factor ≈ √k = 0.5; On-off pattern repeats every N = 100 cycles = 2 s, giving envelope components at 0.5 Hz and its multiples, not at 50 Hz.

Apply A PWM AC chopper feeds a resistive load from a 230 V, 50 Hz supply. Find the duty ratio needed for an output of 184 V RMS, and explain how the switching frequency is chosen with respect to the filter. 12 marks

V_rms = V_s√D, so √D = 184/230 = 0.8 and D = 0.64; The chopper connects the load to the supply for D·T_s and short-circuits the load for (1 − D)T_s; Switching sidebands appear around f_sw ± 2f_supply and their multiples, well above 50 Hz; A small low-pass LC filter at the load removes them; filter size falls as f_sw rises; Trade-off: higher f_sw reduces filter size but increases switching loss and EMI; The freewheel switch must be bidirectional (two devices back to back) to carry the load current in both half cycles.

Apply A single-phase step-down cycloconverter using two bridge converter groups is to produce 12.5 Hz from a 50 Hz supply feeding an RL load. Explain the firing-angle pattern of each group and sketch the output voltage. 12 marks

Frequency ratio f_o/f_i = 12.5/50 = 1/4, so one output cycle spans four supply cycles; Positive group is phase-controlled with α varying slowly to trace the positive half sine; the negative group traces the negative half; Firing angle is modulated as α(t) chosen so that the average output follows V_o sin(2πf_o t); Group changeover at the output current zero, or continuous gating with an intergroup reactor; Output waveform is a series of supply-frequency segments whose average is the low-frequency sine; the ripple is at f_i and its harmonics plus f_o sidebands; For an RL load the current is nearly sinusoidal at f_o because the load inductance filters the segments.

Apply A three-phase to three-phase matrix converter uses nine bidirectional switches. Explain the switching constraints, state the maximum voltage gain, and find the highest output line voltage available from a 400 V line supply. 12 marks

Nine switches, each made of two IGBTs back to back (18 devices), one for every input-phase/output-phase pair; Constraint 1: never short two input lines, so at most one switch per output phase is on; Constraint 2: the load must never be open-circuited (inductive load), so exactly one switch per output phase is on at all times; Duty cycles for the three switches feeding one output phase sum to one; Linear modulation limit gives a voltage transfer ratio of √3/2 ≈ 0.866; Highest output line voltage ≈ 0.866 × 400 ≈ 346 V.

Apply A 400 V, 50 Hz three-phase supply feeds a star-connected resistive load of 40 Ω per phase through three anti-parallel SCR pairs, with the neutral connected. For α = 120°, find the per-phase RMS voltage, the per-phase power and the total power. State what changes if the neutral is removed. 10 marks

With neutral, each phase is an independent single-phase controller; V_ph = 400/√3 = 231 V; V_ph,rms = 231√[(π − 2π/3)/π + sin(240°)/(2π)] = 231√(0.3333 − 0.1378) ≈ 102 V; Per-phase power = 102²/40 ≈ 260 W, total ≈ 780 W; Without neutral, zero-sequence current cannot flow, the neutral point of the load shifts, the control range becomes 0° to 150°, and the phase voltages are no longer equal at large α.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare phase control, integral-cycle control and PWM AC chopping for a 1 kW lamp dimmer on a 230 V supply, on harmonic content, power factor, flicker, acoustic noise and filter requirement. Recommend one for a domestic dimmer and justify. 13 marks

Phase control: continuous, cheapest, but high harmonic distortion and power factor falling with α; produces buzz in lamps and radio interference; Integral-cycle: clean current during conduction, unity displacement factor, but visible flicker unless the repetition rate is high; unsuitable for lighting; PWM chopper: output harmonics pushed to kilohertz; small filter needed; extra switch, switching loss and EMI; Comparison table of THD, PF, response speed, device count, filter size; For a domestic dimmer, phase control with a TRIAC wins on cost, size and continuity; PWM is used where low harmonic injection is mandated; Mention the practical add-on: an LC or EMI choke and snubber.

Analyze Distinguish circulating-current and non-circulating-current modes of a dual converter used as a cycloconverter. Discuss the effect of each on output waveform quality, intergroup reactor size and changeover time. 12 marks

Circulating mode: both groups gated continuously, firing angles set so their mean voltages are equal and opposite, current limited by an intergroup reactor; Advantages: changeover is instantaneous, no dead time, better low-frequency output, the load current is continuous; Disadvantages: reactor must carry the circulating current, poorer efficiency, larger equipment; Non-circulating mode: the idle group is blocked and fired only after the load current reaches zero and a small dead time passes; Advantages: no reactor, better efficiency; Disadvantages: dead time distorts the output at low output frequency, slower response, needs reliable current-zero detection; Conclusion: circulating mode suits low output frequencies; non-circulating suits moderate frequencies and higher efficiency.

Evaluate A 5 Hz to 20 Hz, three-phase induction motor drive is to be fed from a 50 Hz, 400 V supply. Evaluate three options: a cycloconverter, a matrix converter, and a rectifier plus PWM inverter. Choose one and justify with device count, efficiency and control range. 15 marks

Cycloconverter: naturally commutated, rugged, handles large power, no DC link; but many thyristors, poor input power factor at low output voltage, output frequency limited to about f_i/3; Matrix converter: no DC link, bidirectional power flow, sinusoidal input current, 18 IGBTs for a 3×3 array, gain limited to 0.866 and complex commutation; Rectifier–inverter: mature, wide frequency range, cheap, but has a DC-link capacitor (bulk, finite life) and only unidirectional power flow unless made active-front-end; For 5 to 20 Hz the cycloconverter's f_o ≤ f_i/3 limit is met, so it is technically feasible; for a small drive the rectifier–inverter is cheaper and smaller; Recommendation by size: cycloconverter for multi-megawatt slow mills and cement kilns; rectifier–inverter for general industrial drives; matrix converter where regeneration and DC-link-free design are needed; A scoring table on cost, efficiency, input current quality and reliability.

Evaluate Derive the input power factor of a single-phase phase-controlled AC controller with a resistive load as a function of α. Discuss how the harmonic content of the supply current affects the supply transformer and the neutral conductor in a three-phase installation. 13 marks

PF = V_rms/V_s = √[(π − α)/π + sin(2α)/(2π)] for a resistive load; Displacement factor is unity; the whole reduction comes from distortion, so PF = 1/√(1 + THD²); Current waveform is a chopped sine; Fourier analysis gives odd harmonics only, of significant amplitude; Effect on transformer: extra eddy-current and stray losses, derating, possible overheating of the neutral in three-phase four-wire systems carrying triplen harmonics; Triplen harmonics add in the neutral instead of cancelling, so the neutral current can exceed the phase current; Remedies: passive filter tuned near the 3rd and 5th harmonic, PWM chopper operation, or line-side active filter.

Glossary

AC voltage controller
A circuit that varies the RMS voltage applied to a load from a fixed-frequency AC supply, without changing the frequency.
Bidirectional switch
Two unidirectional devices connected back to back so that the combination blocks and conducts in both directions.
Circulating current
Current that flows between two converter groups of a dual converter when both are gated at once, limited by an intergroup reactor.
Conduction angle
The angular interval during which the device conducts, equal to β − α.
Cycloconverter
A direct frequency changer that builds a low-frequency output from segments of the input voltage by phase control.
Duty ratio D
The fraction of one switching period for which the load is connected to the supply in a PWM chopper; V_rms = V_s√D.
Duty ratio k
In on-off control, the ratio n/N of conducting cycles to total cycles; the RMS output becomes V_s√k.
Extinction angle β
The angle at which the load current falls to zero and the device turns off; for a resistive load β = π.
Firing angle α
The electrical angle, measured from the start of the half cycle, at which the thyristor is triggered.
Integral-cycle control
On-off control in which the load is connected for whole supply cycles and disconnected for whole supply cycles.
Intergroup reactor
An inductor placed between the two converter groups of a circulating-current dual converter to limit the current that flows between them.
Low-pass filter
An LC network at the load that passes the 50 Hz component and blocks the switching-frequency sidebands.
Matrix converter
An array of nine bidirectional switches giving direct AC-to-AC conversion with independent control of output amplitude and frequency.
MT1 and MT2
The two main terminals of a TRIAC; MT1 is the reference terminal for the gate circuit.
Phase control
Delaying the turn-on of the thyristor within each half cycle so that only a part of the sine wave reaches the load.
PWM AC chopper
A converter that switches the load rapidly between the supply and a short circuit to control the RMS load voltage.
Static AC switch
An anti-parallel thyristor pair, or a TRIAC, used to connect and disconnect an AC load without moving contacts.
Switching frequency
The rate at which a chopper switch operates, chosen far above the supply frequency to place harmonics where they are easy to filter.
Symmetrical firing
Equal firing delay in both half cycles, so the output contains no DC component and only odd harmonics.
Three-phase AC controller
Three single-phase controllers arranged in the three lines of a three-phase supply, with the load in star or delta.
TRIAC
A bidirectional three-terminal thyristor that can conduct current in either direction from a single gate pulse.
Trigger quadrant
One of four operating conditions of a TRIAC, defined by the polarity of MT2 and of the gate current.
Voltage transfer ratio
The ratio of output voltage amplitude to input voltage amplitude; in a matrix converter it is limited to about 0.866 in linear modulation.
Zero-sequence current
A current component identical in all three phases that can flow only when a neutral or delta path exists.
