4.1 Single-phase half-bridge and full-bridge inverters

An inverter turns DC into AC by switching the load between two voltage rails. In single-phase square-wave operation the output is not a sine: it is a rectangular wave whose height is fixed by the DC bus. The half-bridge uses two switches and a split capacitor bus; the full bridge uses four switches and gives twice the voltage. These circuits appear in small UPS units, solar microinverters and variable-speed drives, and they are the base on which PWM is built.

You need: Unit 1 for the IGBT, its anti-parallel diode and switching times, and Unit 3 for the idea of a switched-mode source.

Half-bridge: split bus, half voltage

The half-bridge has two switches, S1 on top and S2 below, in series across the DC bus Vdc. Two equal capacitors C1 and C2 also sit across the bus, and their midpoint is the circuit reference. The top rail is then +Vdc/2 and the bottom rail is −Vdc/2.

S1 on connects the load to +Vdc/2; S2 on connects it to −Vdc/2. Each switch conducts for 180° of the output cycle. The load voltage is a square wave of amplitude Vdc/2, so its rms value is Vdc/2. The fundamental peak is 2Vdc/π.

v<sub>o</sub> = ±V<sub>dc</sub>/2,  V<sub>rms</sub> = V<sub>dc</sub>/2,  V<sub>1,peak</sub> = 2V<sub>dc</sub>/π

vo is the load voltage; Vdc is the DC bus; Vrms is the root-mean-square output; V1,peak is the peak of the fundamental.

C1 and C2 must hold the midpoint steady while carrying the load's alternating current. Before S2 turns on, S1 must be off for a short dead time, and the reverse for the other half cycle. If both conduct together, the bus is shorted. Some syllabi call this a split-supply inverter.

Full bridge: four switches, full voltage

The full bridge, or H-bridge, has four switches in two legs. Leg A is S1 above S2; leg B is S3 above S4. The load sits between the midpoints. S1 and S4 conduct together for the positive half cycle; S2 and S3 conduct together for the negative half cycle. Each device conducts for 180°. The load voltage is +Vdc for one half cycle and −Vdc for the other, so its rms value is Vdc.

v<sub>o</sub>(t) = (4V<sub>dc</sub>/π)[sin ωt + (1/3)sin 3ωt + (1/5)sin 5ωt + …]

ω = 2πf; f is the output frequency in hertz; n = 1, 3, 5, … is the harmonic order. The peak of the fundamental is 4Vdc/π. The nth harmonic peak is 4Vdc/(nπ), which is 1/n of the fundamental.

Even harmonics and DC are zero because the wave has half-wave symmetry. With a pure resistor, the current is the same square wave as the voltage. With an R-L load, the current is smoother and lags; when a switch turns off, the load current continues through the anti-parallel diode of the other switch and returns energy to the bus. The output voltage remains a square wave, but the current is not.

Harmonics, loads and dead time

The square wave's rms value is Vdc for the full bridge and Vdc/2 for the half-bridge. The nth harmonic rms is 4Vdc/(nπ√2) for the full bridge, with n odd. The fundamental carries most of the power; the 3rd, 5th and 7th cause extra heating and torque ripple.

V<sub>n,rms</sub> = 4V<sub>dc</sub>/(nπ√2),  n = 1, 3, 5, …

Vn,rms is the rms of the nth harmonic of a full-bridge square wave.

Dead time is the short interval when both switches of one leg are off. It prevents shoot-through, a direct short of the DC bus. Dead time slightly lowers the fundamental and adds low-order distortion, so it is kept small. To control the fundamental without changing Vdc, see Chapter 4.3.

Worked example

Question. A single-phase full bridge runs from Vdc = 300 V and feeds a 20 Ω resistor at 50 Hz in square-wave mode. Find (a) the rms output voltage, (b) the output power, (c) the rms of the fundamental and its power, (d) the rms of the third harmonic.

  1. Full-bridge square wave: amplitude A = Vdc = 300 V. The wave is at +300 V or −300 V all the time; f = 50 Hz sets the fundamental frequency only.
  2. Vrms = A = 300 V.
  3. P = Vrms²/R = 300²/20 = 4500 W.
  4. V1,peak = 4Vdc/π = 4×300/3.1416 = 381.97 V.
  5. V1,rms = 381.97/√2 = 270.09 V. P1 = 270.09²/20 = 3647.56 W.
  6. V3,peak = 381.97/3 = 127.32 V. V3,rms = 127.32/√2 = 90.03 V.

Answer. The full bridge gives 300 V rms, 4500 W total, fundamental 270.09 V rms (3647.56 W) and third harmonic 90.03 V rms.

Exam traps

  • Half-bridge amplitude is Vdc/2, not Vdc; its rms is Vdc/2, while the full bridge gives Vdc.
  • If both switches in one leg are on together, the DC bus is shorted: this is shoot-through. Dead time prevents it.
  • The fundamental peak is 4Vdc/π for a full bridge, not Vdc. The nth harmonic is 1/n of that, not 1/n².
  • With an R-L load the output voltage is still a square wave; the current is smoother. Do not draw the current as a square wave.
  • 180° conduction means each device is on for half the cycle. 120° conduction belongs to the three-phase inverter in Chapter 4.2.

4.2 Three-phase VSI: 180 and 120 degree conduction

A three-phase voltage-source inverter (VSI) has three legs and six switches across a DC bus. Each leg connects its output to either the positive or the negative rail. In 180° conduction every switch stays on for 180°, so three switches — one in each leg — are on at every instant. The line-to-line voltage becomes a six-step wave. In 120° conduction each switch stays on for 120°, only two switches are on, and each leg has two 60° dead intervals, 120° in total. Motor drives, UPS units and solar inverters use this bridge.

You need Chapter 4.1 for the single-phase bridge and its square wave.

Three legs and the 180° switch table

Label the six switches S1 to S6. Leg A uses S1 (top) and S4 (bottom); leg B uses S3 (top) and S6 (bottom); leg C uses S5 (top) and S2 (bottom). Vdc is the DC bus voltage in volts. In 180° mode each gate pulse is 180° wide and the pulses are spaced by 60°. The two switches in one leg are complementary: S1 on means S4 off, so the leg never connects both rails together.

At any instant exactly three switches are on, one per leg. From 0° to 60°, S1, S5 and S6 conduct. Phases A and C are tied to the positive rail through S1 and S5, and phase B is tied to the negative rail through S6, so current flows from the positive rail through A and C and returns through B. The table lists the active switches for each 60° interval. The pattern repeats every 360°. The output period is 1/f, where f is the output frequency in hertz.

60°–120°S1, S2, S6
120°–180°S1, S2, S3
180°–240°S4, S2, S3

Line and phase voltages

Take the midpoint of the DC bus as the reference. A phase terminal sits at +Vdc/2 when its top switch is on and at −Vdc/2 when its bottom switch is on. The phase-to-neutral voltage is not a two-level square wave: it steps among +Vdc/3, +2Vdc/3, −Vdc/3 and −2Vdc/3. The line-to-line voltage has only three levels: +Vdc, 0 and −Vdc. It changes every 60°, which gives the name six-step inverter. The same bridge with 180° pulses is also called a square-wave VSI.

V<sub>LL(rms)</sub> = √(2/3) V<sub>dc</sub> ≈ 0.816 V<sub>dc</sub>; V<sub>ph(rms)</sub> = V<sub>LL(rms)</sub>/√3 ≈ 0.471 V<sub>dc</sub>; V<sub>LL1(rms)</sub> = (√6/π) V<sub>dc</sub> ≈ 0.780 V<sub>dc</sub>; V<sub>ph1(rms)</sub> = (√2/π) V<sub>dc</sub> ≈ 0.450 V<sub>dc</sub>

Vdc is the DC bus voltage in volts; VLL(rms) is the total rms line-to-line voltage; Vph(rms) is the total rms phase-to-neutral voltage; VLL1 and Vph1 are fundamental rms values.

The total rms is larger than the fundamental because the six-step wave contains 5th, 7th, 11th, 13th and higher harmonics. The fundamental produces positive torque in a motor; the harmonics heat the motor and cause torque ripple. Voltage control methods change the fundamental without changing Vdc; see Chapter 4.3.

120° conduction mode

In 120° mode each gate pulse is only 120° wide. At any instant two switches conduct, not three. Each device conducts for 120°, which is one third of the cycle. Each leg has two 60° intervals — 120° in total per cycle — in which both of its switches are off. For example, the top device conducts from 0° to 120° and the bottom device from 180° to 300°, so that leg is off from 120° to 180° and again from 300° to 360°. Because the gate pattern itself leaves two 60° dead intervals in every leg, an overlap fault is much less likely than in 180° mode.

The penalty is a more distorted output: each device conducts for only one third of the cycle, the line-to-line voltage steps through +Vdc, +Vdc/2, −Vdc/2 and −Vdc, and the fundamental is lower than in 180° mode (about 0.39 Vdc phase rms, 0.68 Vdc line rms). With an inductive load the phase current freewheels through the freewheeling diode of that leg, which clamps the terminal to the positive or the negative rail depending on the current direction; no switch of that leg is on. With a purely resistive load the current is simply zero and the terminal floats to the neutral potential. The bridge is still a square-wave VSI, but it is less common because switch utilisation is poorer.

The name conduction angle means the angle for which one switch is on in one cycle. It is not the firing delay angle of a thyristor rectifier. A 180° switch conducts half the cycle; a 120° switch conducts one third.

  • Reading 180° mode as 'both switches of a leg on together' — it means each device conducts 180°, and the two devices of a leg stay complementary.

4.3 Voltage control: single and multiple pulse modulation

The DC bus sets the square-wave inverter output, so the fundamental peak is fixed at 4Vdc/π, that is 1.273Vdc. To change the output voltage without changing Vdc, cut zero-voltage notches into each half cycle. Single-pulse modulation gives one pulse per half cycle; with d as half the pulse width, the pulse is 2d wide and the zero-voltage notches, each π−2d wide, sit on either side of every zero crossing. Multiple-pulse modulation gives p equal pulses per half cycle, each of width 2d. Both keep the switches hard on or hard off, so efficiency stays high. They appear in older variable-voltage drives, induction heating, UPS units and cheap fan or pump inverters, before sinusoidal PWM took over.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for the square wave and its 4Vdc/π fundamental.

The fixed square wave and the notch

A full-bridge inverter switches the load between +Vdc and −Vdc. With no control, the output is a square wave. Its fundamental peak is fixed at 4Vdc/π, which is 1.273Vdc. If Vdc comes from a battery or a rectifier that you do not want to vary, you cannot change the AC voltage by changing the bus.

The fix is to insert zero-voltage intervals, called notches, into each half cycle. Keep the switches either fully on or fully off. In the positive half cycle, the output is +Vdc for a pulse of width 2d centred at 90°, and zero for the rest. The negative half cycle mirrors it. The result is a quasi-square wave: a square wave with a bite taken out. One pulse per half cycle is single-pulse modulation, also called notch modulation. Several equal pulses per half cycle give multiple-pulse modulation, also called uniform PWM. The DC bus stays fixed; the pulse width sets the output voltage.

Single-pulse modulation

Let d be half the pulse width, measured in radians. The pulse width is 2d. The fundamental peak is given by the formula below. If your d is in degrees, use sin(d×π/180) inside the sine.

V1p = (4Vdc/π) sin d

V1p is the fundamental peak voltage in V; Vdc is the DC bus voltage in V; d is half the pulse width in radians inside the sine.

The rms output over a half cycle is set by the total on-time. With d in radians, use the next formula. With d in degrees, the same result is Vdc√(d/90). At d = 90°, the pulse fills the half cycle, so V1p = 4Vdc/π and Vrms = Vdc. At d = 0, the output is zero.

Vrms = Vdc√(2d/π)

Vrms is the rms output voltage in V; Vdc is the DC bus voltage in V; d is half the pulse width in radians.

The control is not linear. Near d = 90°, sin d changes slowly, so a large change in d gives a small change in fundamental. Near d = 0, sin d ≈ d, so the fundamental is almost proportional to d. The nth harmonic amplitude for odd n is (4Vdc/(nπ)) sin(n d). The 3rd harmonic is strong for most d, which is why single-pulse output needs a filter.

Multiple-pulse modulation and the trade-off

Split each half cycle into p equal pulses, each of width 2d and equally spaced. The total on-time in a half cycle is 2pd radians. The rms output is given by the formula below. If each pulse is w degrees wide, the on fraction is p w /180, so Vrms = Vdc√(p w/180). The formula uses p pulses per half cycle; a full cycle has 2p pulses.

Vrms = Vdc√(2pd/π)

Vrms is the rms output voltage in V; Vdc is the DC bus voltage in V; p is the number of equal pulses per half cycle; d is half of one pulse width in radians.

The lowest strong harmonic moves up to about order 2p−1. For p = 2, the first strong group is near the 3rd; for p = 4, near the 7th; for p = 6, near the 11th. Higher p means the filter can be smaller, because the ripple is at a higher frequency. The price is switching loss. Each pulse has two edges, so p pulses per half cycle give 2p pulses and 4p switching transitions per cycle. More transitions mean more switching loss in the devices and more gate-drive power.

Choose p from the allowed harmonic spectrum and the switching-loss budget. Audio noise also matters: a low-order harmonic can make a motor or inductor sing. For the same total on-time, the rms stays the same when p changes, but the harmonic positions change. That is the trade-off: p buys harmonic quality with switching loss.

Worked example

Question. A full-bridge inverter has Vdc = 200 V. (a) It uses single-pulse modulation with d = 30°. Find the fundamental peak and the rms output. (b) It is changed to 4 equal pulses per half cycle, each 10° wide. Find the new rms output and the approximate order of the first strong harmonic.

  1. For (a), convert d: 30° = π/6 rad = 0.5236 rad.
  2. 4Vdc/π = 4×200/π = 254.65 V.
  3. sin 30° = 0.5, so V1p = 254.65×0.5 = 127.3 V.
  4. On fraction = 2d/π = 2×(π/6)/π = 1/3 = 0.3333.
  5. Vrms = 200×√(1/3) = 200×0.5774 = 115.5 V.
  6. For (b), total on-time per half cycle = 4×10° = 40°.
  7. On fraction = 40/180 = 0.2222.
  8. Vrms = 200×√0.2222 = 200×0.4714 = 94.3 V.
  9. First strong harmonic order ≈ 2p−1 = 2×4−1 = 7.

Answer. The single-pulse case gives a fundamental peak of 127.3 V and rms of 115.5 V; with four pulses the rms is 94.3 V and the first strong harmonic is near the 7th order.

Exam traps

  • Putting degrees into sin without converting: sin 30 is not 0.5 on a calculator set to radians. Convert d to radians or use sin(d×π/180).
  • Assuming the fundamental falls linearly with pulse width: it follows sin d, so halving d near 90° does not halve the fundamental.
  • Calling p the pulses per cycle: the formulas use p pulses per half cycle, so a full cycle has 2p pulses and 4p switching transitions, not p pulses and 2p transitions.
  • Confusing rms with fundamental peak: rms is set by total on-time, not by pulse position; the fundamental also depends on where the pulses sit.
  • Forgetting the first strong harmonic order: for multiple-pulse modulation it is about 2p−1, not 2p+1; single-pulse modulation still has a strong 3rd harmonic.

4.4 Sinusoidal PWM: unipolar and bipolar

Sinusoidal PWM compares a low-frequency sine reference with a high-frequency triangular carrier. The comparator output drives the bridge, so the switch duty ratio changes sinusoidally across the output cycle. The fundamental then follows the reference, while the unwanted harmonics move up to the carrier frequency and its sidebands. This is the standard way to make a fixed DC bus behave like a variable-voltage, variable-frequency AC source. It is used in motor drives, UPS units, solar inverters and small domestic inverters.

You need: Chapter 4.1 and Chapter 4.3 for H-bridge operation and notch-based voltage control; Unit 1 for switching loss that sets the carrier frequency.

The sine-triangle comparator

An inverter bridge can apply only fixed DC rails or zero. To make a sine wave, the switches are turned on and off many times in every output cycle. Let vref = Vm sin(2π fm t) be the sine reference. Vm is its peak in V and fm is its frequency in Hz. A triangular carrier of peak Vc and frequency fc is compared with this reference. When vref is greater than the carrier, the comparator output is high. The duty ratio d therefore changes across the fm cycle, and the average bridge voltage follows a sine.

m<sub>a</sub> = V<sub>m</sub> / V<sub>c</sub>,  m<sub>f</sub> = f<sub>c</sub> / f<sub>m</sub>

ma is the modulation index; Vm is the sine reference peak in V; Vc is the triangular carrier peak in V; mf is the frequency ratio; fc is the carrier frequency in Hz; fm is the reference frequency in Hz.

For a full bridge, the average output over one carrier period is Vdc(2d − 1). With d = 0.5 + 0.5 ma sin θ, this average equals maVdc sin θ. The fundamental peak is maVdc and the fundamental rms is maVdc/√2. The linear range is ma ≤ 1. Above 1 the reference cuts the carrier peak, so the comparator saturates and low-order harmonics appear.

  1. Choose fm from the load, for example 50 Hz for a motor.
  2. Choose fc from switching loss and filter size; a higher fc gives smaller filters but more loss.
  3. Set ma for the required fundamental voltage.
  4. Compare the sine and triangle and send the pulses to the gate drive.

Bipolar sinusoidal PWM

Bipolar PWM uses one sine reference and one triangular carrier. The two legs of the full bridge switch as diagonal pairs. When the comparator is high, the output is +Vdc; when it is low, the output is −Vdc. There is no zero state. The output is a two-level PWM wave whose average follows the sine reference. The fundamental peak is maVdc in the linear range.

The unwanted harmonics are grouped around the carrier frequency and its sidebands. The first cluster is near mffm = fc, with sidebands at fc ± 2fm, fc ± 4fm and so on. A low-pass filter after the bridge removes this cluster. If ma is pushed above 1, the output becomes overmodulated. The fundamental then rises slowly towards the square-wave value 4Vdc/π, but low-order harmonics grow quickly. Most designs stay at or below ma = 1 unless extra voltage is essential.

Unipolar PWM and third-harmonic injection

Unipolar PWM uses two sine references 180° apart, or one reference compared with two carriers. Each leg is switched independently. The bridge output can then be +Vdc, 0 or −Vdc. When both upper switches are on, the load sees 0 V; when both lower switches are on, it also sees 0 V. The differential output still has the wanted sine fundamental, but the ripple pulses come twice per carrier period. The first harmonic cluster moves from near fc to near 2fc. At the same carrier frequency, the output filter can be smaller because the ripple is at a higher frequency.

Third-harmonic injection adds a common third-harmonic component to the three-phase references. A common choice is one-sixth of the fundamental amplitude. The phase reference peak falls, so the sine reference can be made larger before it hits the carrier. In a three-phase inverter this allows ma up to 2/√3 ≈ 1.15 before clipping. The line-to-line fundamental rises by about 15%, while the third harmonic cancels in the line-to-line voltage. Beyond 1.15, overmodulation begins and low-order harmonics return.

Worked example

Question. A single-phase full bridge has Vdc = 400 V. It uses sinusoidal PWM with fm = 50 Hz, ma = 0.8 and mf = 21. (a) Find the fundamental peak and rms output voltage. (b) Find the carrier frequency and the first harmonic cluster for bipolar PWM. (c) State where the first cluster moves with unipolar PWM.

  1. Fundamental peak V1p = maVdc = 0.8 × 400 = 320 V.
  2. Fundamental rms V1rms = 320 / √2 = 226.3 V.
  3. Carrier frequency fc = mffm = 21 × 50 = 1050 Hz = 1.05 kHz.
  4. Bipolar PWM puts the first cluster near fc = 1.05 kHz.
  5. Unipolar PWM cancels the odd carrier ripple, so the first cluster moves near 2fc = 2.10 kHz.

Answer. The fundamental is 320 V peak, 226.3 V rms; fc is 1050 Hz; unipolar PWM moves the first cluster near 2100 Hz.

Exam traps

  • Swapping ma and mf. ma sets voltage; mf sets the harmonic position.
  • Using the peak fundamental maVdc as an rms value. Divide by √2 for a sine rms.
  • Assuming unipolar and bipolar PWM have the same spectrum. Unipolar pushes the first cluster to about 2fc.
  • Believing ma can rise far above 1 without distortion. Above 1 the comparator saturates and low-order harmonics appear.
  • Forgetting that third-harmonic injection is a three-phase common-mode trick. It helps line-to-line voltage, not every single-phase load.

4.5 Space vector modulation

Space vector modulation (SVPWM) turns the three inverter legs into one rotating voltage vector. The six switches give eight states: six active vectors 60° apart and two zero vectors at the origin. In every switching period SVPWM averages the two nearest active vectors with a zero state to make the wanted vector. It exists because it uses the DC bus better than sine-triangle PWM. You meet it in three-phase motor drives, grid-tied inverters and UPS units.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for the six switch states and Chapter 4.4 for the PWM frame of mind.

Eight states, one rotating vector

A three-phase bridge has six switches. Each leg is either top-on or bottom-on. Write Sa = 1 when the top switch of phase a is on. The state (Sa,Sb,Sc) has eight values: 000, 100, 110, 010, 011, 001, 101, 111. States 000 and 111 put all three output terminals on the same rail, so the load sees zero voltage; call them zero vectors V0 and V7. The other six are active vectors V1 to V6.

For a balanced star load, state 100 gives phase voltages (2Vdc/3, −Vdc/3, −Vdc/3). Here Vdc is the DC bus voltage. Draw these three voltages as one vector in the αβ plane. Every active vector has length 2Vdc/3, not Vdc/2. The six active vectors are 60° apart and form a hexagon; the zero vectors sit at the centre. The space between V1 and V2 is sector 1, and so on up to sector 6.

A wanted three-phase set becomes one reference vector Vref of length Vm rotating at the output frequency. Vm is the peak phase voltage you want. In sector 1, Vref lies between V1 and V2, as the figure shows. SVPWM does not produce Vref instantly. It produces it on average over a switching period Ts.

Dwell times and the linear limit

Vref Ts = V1 T1 + V2 T2 + V0 T0

Vref is the wanted vector; V1 and V2 are the two active vectors of the sector; V0 is a zero vector; T1, T2 and T0 are their times in one period Ts.

For a reference at angle θ from the start of its sector, use V1 for T1, the next active vector V2 for T2, and a zero vector for T0.

T1 = ma Ts sin(60° − θ),  T2 = ma Ts sin θ,  T0 = Ts − T1 − T2

ma = √3 Vm / Vdc is the SVPWM modulation index; Ts is the switching period in seconds; θ is the angle inside the sector, from 0 to 60°; T1, T2 and T0 are times in seconds.

Find the sector by measuring the reference angle α from V1. Sector number n = floor(α/60°) + 1. Local angle θ = α − (n−1)×60°. The two active vectors are the ones bounding that sector.

The linear limit is T1 + T2 ≤ Ts. Since T1 + T2 = ma Ts cos(30° − θ), the worst point is θ = 30°, where the sum is ma Ts. So ma ≤ 1, which means Vm ≤ Vdc/√3. For Vdc = 600 V this is 346.4 V peak phase. Sine-triangle PWM stops at Vdc/2 = 300 V, so SVPWM gives 15.5% more fundamental before overmodulation.

Seven-segment switching and where it is used

T0 must not be one frozen block with all switches held. Split it between the two zero states. A common seven-segment order for sector 1 over one Ts is: V0 for T0/4, V1 for T1/2, V2 for T2/2, V7 for T0/2, V2 for T2/2, V1 for T1/2, V0 for T0/4. In bits: 000 → 100 → 110 → 111 → 110 → 100 → 000. Each change flips only one leg. This lowers switching loss and makes the current ripple symmetric about the centre of the period.

The full name is space vector pulse width modulation, SVPWM. It is a carrier-based PWM in disguise: it matches sine-triangle PWM with a common-mode third-harmonic component added to the references. That common-mode part cancels in line-to-line voltages, so the load gets a larger fundamental from the same bus.

Use it in three-phase motor drives for pumps, fans and electric vehicles, in grid-tied inverters (see Chapter 4.9) and in UPS units. At the same switching frequency it gives a higher usable bus voltage and lower current ripple than plain sine-triangle PWM. Digital implementation is simple: find the sector, compute T1, T2 and T0, then load three compare registers.

Worked example

Question. A three-phase VSI has Vdc = 600 V, output frequency 50 Hz and switching frequency 6 kHz, so Ts = 166.7 μs. The required fundamental phase-voltage peak is Vm = 300 V. At one instant the reference vector is at θ = 20° inside its sector. Find ma, T1, T2, T0 and the two zero-state times. Check that the reference is inside the linear range.

  1. Ts = 1/fsw = 1/6000 s = 166.7 μs.
  2. ma = √3 Vm / Vdc = √3 × 300 / 600 = 0.866.
  3. Linear limit: Vdc/√3 = 600/1.732 = 346.4 V peak. Vm = 300 V < 346.4 V, so the reference is in the linear range.
  4. T1 = ma Ts sin(60° − θ) = 0.866 × 166.7 μs × sin 40° = 0.866 × 166.7 × 0.6428 = 92.8 μs.
  5. T2 = ma Ts sin θ = 0.866 × 166.7 × sin 20° = 0.866 × 166.7 × 0.3420 = 49.4 μs.
  6. T0 = Ts − T1 − T2 = 166.7 − 92.8 − 49.4 = 24.5 μs.
  7. Split T0 equally between the zero states: 12.3 μs total of V0 and 12.3 μs total of V7. In the seven-segment order V0 is used as 6.1 μs at the start and 6.1 μs at the end; V7 is used as one 12.3 μs block in the middle.

Answer. ma = 0.866; T1 = 92.8 μs; T2 = 49.4 μs; T0 = 24.5 μs, split as 12.3 μs of V0 and 12.3 μs of V7, with each V0 quarter equal to 6.1 μs.

Exam traps

  • Using Vdc/2 as the length of an active vector; it is 2Vdc/3.
  • Thinking T0 is 'all switches off' or a single state; T0 must be spent as V0 and V7.
  • Measuring θ from the α-axis in the T1 and T2 formulas instead of from the start of the sector.
  • Confusing ma with the sine-triangle modulation index; here ma = √3 Vm/Vdc, and ma = 1 already means Vm = Vdc/√3.
  • Calling Vdc/2 the linear limit; Vdc/2 belongs to sine-triangle PWM, while SVPWM reaches Vdc/√3.

4.6 Harmonic elimination techniques

Harmonic elimination chooses inverter switching instants so named low-order harmonics are exactly zero. Notches are placed at angles that make the Fourier coefficients of the 3rd, 5th or other chosen orders vanish. This is selective harmonic elimination (SHE). It suits a fixed DC bus feeding a motor or transformer without a heavy low-order filter. Only a few universities teach it. The angles are solved offline and stored.

You need: Chapter 4.3 for notches and the sin δ result, and Chapter 4.4 for switching waveforms.

The coefficient you set to zero

Take a full-bridge output whose quarter-wave pattern is zero at the start of each quarter, steps to +Vdc at α1, back to zero at α2, to +Vdc at α3, and so on. The negative half is its mirror image. Call the N switching instants per quarter α1 to αN; each α is an angle after the quarter starts. Quarter-wave symmetry makes all even harmonics zero.

b_n = (4Vdc/(nπ)) · Σ(k=1..N) (−1)^(k−1) cos(n·α_k)

b_n is peak volts of harmonic n; Vdc is DC bus volts; n is odd harmonic order; N is switching angles per quarter; α_k is k-th switching angle; the sign alternates with each switching angle.

For N=1, b_n = (4Vdc/(nπ)) cos(nα1). N angles give N equations. One sets the wanted fundamental b1. The other N−1 set chosen odd harmonics to zero: b_n = 0. So N angles delete N−1 harmonics, not N. The equations are nonlinear; solve them offline numerically. Store the angles for each modulation index in a table. The inverter reads the table and switches.

Three-phase triplens and the filter contrast

In a three-phase inverter the three legs are 120° apart. A triplen harmonic (order 3, 9, 15, ...) is in phase in all legs. It appears in phase-to-neutral voltage but cancels in line-to-line voltage. In an isolated star load it cannot drive current. For a delta load it drives circulating current. A three-phase SHE table often ignores triplens and deletes 5, 7, 11, ... instead. Check which voltage you mean.

A filter attenuates a harmonic after the inverter generates it; it carries reactive current and adds loss and size. SHE stops the chosen harmonic from appearing. The cost is fixed angles: a new DC bus or output needs a new table. PWM from Chapter 4.4 pushes harmonics to the carrier; SHE removes named low orders with few switchings.

Worked example

Question. A full bridge has Vdc = 220 V, one switching angle per quarter at α1 = 30°, and a quarter-wave output that is zero at the start and steps to +Vdc at α1. Use b_n = (4Vdc/(nπ)) cos(nα1) for odd n. Find peak b1, b3 and b5.

  1. Square-wave peak factor: 4Vdc/π = 280.1 V.
  2. b1 = 280.1 × cos 30° = 280.1 × 0.8660 = 242.6 V peak.
  3. b3 = (280.1/3) × cos 90° = 93.37 × 0 = 0 V.
  4. b5 = (280.1/5) × cos 150° = 56.02 × (−0.8660) = −48.5 V peak; magnitude 48.5 V.
  5. A second angle α2 gives a second equation, so it can also force b5 = 0.

Answer. b1 = 242.6 V peak, b3 = 0 V, b5 = −48.5 V peak; a second angle can delete one more odd harmonic.

Exam traps

  • Do not say N angles remove N harmonics. N angles give N equations: one fundamental target plus N−1 zero conditions.
  • Do not spend an angle on an even harmonic. Quarter-wave and half-wave symmetry already make all even harmonics zero.
  • Use peak values. 4Vdc/π is the peak of the fundamental square wave, not its RMS. RMS is peak/√2.
  • In three-phase, triplen harmonics cancel in line-to-line voltage, not in phase-to-neutral voltage. State which voltage you mean.
  • The angle table is not universal. It depends on Vdc, the target fundamental and the chosen harmonic set.

4.7 Current source inverters

A voltage source inverter holds the DC bus voltage steady with a capacitor and lets the load decide the current. A current source inverter does the opposite. A large series choke Ld holds the DC link current Id almost constant, and the bridge steers that current through two load lines at a time. The output current becomes a quasi-square of amplitude Id, and the load voltage is whatever the load needs. CSIs drive induction heaters, megawatt synchronous motor drives and long-cable installations. Some syllabi call this the current-fed inverter.

You need: Chapter 4.1 and Chapter 4.2 for bridge states and 120° conduction, and see Unit 1 for reverse blocking of power devices.

Hold the current, not the voltage

In a voltage source inverter (VSI) a large capacitor sits across the DC bus. The bus voltage is stiff. When a leg switches, the load current changes only as fast as the load allows, and the bridge output is a voltage square wave.

A current source inverter (CSI) swaps the roles. A large inductor Ld in series with the DC source makes the link current stiff. The bridge connects that current to two of the three load lines at a time. The output current is a quasi-square of amplitude Id, and the load voltage is whatever the load impedance demands, v = L di/dt for an inductive load.

Ld must be large enough that Id stays nearly flat over one switching period. The link stores energy ½LdId². That energy needs a path at every instant. If a load line opens, a fuse blows or a cable falls out, the current has nowhere to go, di/dt becomes enormous and the induced voltage destroys the devices. A CSI therefore needs an overlap period between outgoing and incoming devices, or a freewheel path, or a clamp circuit.

The two inverters are duals.

Reverse blocking is the hard requirement. A plain IGBT blocks only a few tens of volts in reverse, so each IGBT gets a series diode. An SCR blocks reverse voltage by itself, but needs commutation, which is why SCR bridges in CSIs rely on capacitors or on the load's own EMF.

Three-phase CSI: a quasi-square current

The bridge has six devices, numbered 1 to 6. With 120° conduction each device is on for 120°, so at any instant exactly two devices conduct: one from the upper group 1, 3, 5 and one from the lower group 2, 4, 6. Two load lines carry Id; the third carries nothing.

  1. 0° to 60°: devices 6 and 1 conduct; current enters line A and returns through line B.
  2. 60° to 120°: devices 1 and 2; line A in, line C out.
  3. 120° to 180°: devices 2 and 3; line B in, line C out.
  4. 180° to 240°: devices 3 and 4; line B in, line A out.
  5. 240° to 300°: devices 4 and 5; line C in, line A out.
  6. 300° to 360°: devices 5 and 6; line C in, line B out.

Each line current is therefore +Id for 120°, zero for 60°, −Id for 120°, zero for 60°.

I₁ = (√6/π) Id ≈ 0.78 Id      I(rms) = √(2/3) Id ≈ 0.816 Id

I₁ is the rms value of the fundamental line current; I(rms) is the rms value of the whole quasi-square line current; Id is the DC link current in amperes.

The waveform has no triplen harmonics because the load is three-wire. The 5th harmonic is 20% of the fundamental, the 7th is 14.3%, the 11th is 9.1% and the 13th is 7.7%, which gives a current total harmonic distortion near 31%.

The load voltage is not square. With a motor load, leakage inductance and any parallel capacitor smooth the current pulses into a nearly sinusoidal voltage. In a star load the line current equals the phase current; in delta the phase current is the line current divided by √3.

Where CSIs are used, and how they fail

Medium-voltage, high-power drives are the main home. A load-commutated inverter (LCI) uses SCRs and takes its commutation voltage from the motor's own back EMF. Such drives run synchronous motors of several megawatts in pumps, fans and compressors.

Induction heating is a natural fit. The work coil and a parallel capacitor form a tank circuit that wants a sinusoidal voltage. Feeding it from a current source lets the capacitor absorb the current harmonics while the tank rings at resonance (see Unit 6, Induction heating).

Long cables favour the CSI too. A VSI sending fast voltage edges down a long feeder suffers charging current and reflected waves. A CSI at the sending end, with a capacitor at the load end, behaves better.

Four weak points to remember. An open load is destructive. An LCI can not start at standstill, because a stopped motor has no EMF to turn the SCRs off. Series diodes add conduction loss when IGBTs are used. On the plus side, regeneration is easy: reverse the DC link voltage with a dual converter at the supply side while Id keeps its direction.

Worked example

Question. A three-phase current source inverter has a DC link current Id = 50 A and uses 120° conduction. It feeds a star-connected resistive load of 2 Ω per phase. Find (a) the rms fundamental line current, (b) the total rms line current, (c) the fundamental power delivered to the load, and (d) the rms harmonic current and the current THD.

  2. Step 2. The total rms of the same wave is I = Id√(2/3) = 50×0.8165 = 40.82 A.
  3. Step 3. The load is star, so line current equals phase current. The fundamental phase voltage is V₁ = I₁R = 38.98×2 = 77.97 V.
  4. Step 4. Fundamental power P₁ = 3I₁²R = 3×(38.98)²×2 = 3×1519.8×2 = 9118.9 W, about 9.12 kW.
  5. Step 5. Harmonic current I_h = √(I² − I₁²) = √(40.82² − 38.98²) = √(1666.3 − 1519.8) = √146.5 = 12.12 A.

  • Forgetting the series diode. A plain IGBT cannot block reverse voltage, and the load voltage does reverse in a CSI, so every IGBT needs one.
  • Expecting a square output voltage. The CSI fixes the current; the voltage follows the load impedance, and with a motor plus capacitor it comes out close to a sine.
  • Ignoring the open-circuit danger. Breaking the load current with a large choke produces a destructive voltage spike; a real CSI needs overlap, a freewheel path or a clamp.
  • Confusing a CSI with the current-controlled VSI of Chapter 4.9. A grid-tied inverter controls its current by PWM, but its DC link is still a capacitor, so it is a VSI.

4.8 Multilevel inverters

A multilevel inverter makes AC not by jumping between two rails but by stacking many small DC steps. If the DC bus is Vdc and the output has m levels, each step is Vdc/(m−1). The waveform then looks closer to a sine, the harmonic content and dv/dt fall, and each device blocks only a fraction of the bus. Electric drives, solar farms, UPS units and HVDC links use this idea. The three common families, their level counts, and their costs in devices, capacitors and DC supplies are below.

You need: the six-step bridge in Chapter 4.2 and carrier PWM in Chapter 4.4.

Why many levels: small steps, small device voltages

A two-level inverter connects the load to either the positive or negative DC rail. The output moves in one large jump. A multilevel inverter adds intermediate rails or cells, so the same DC bus is divided into smaller steps. The load voltage climbs or falls one step at a time. The waveform is a staircase, not a square wave.

ΔV = Vdc/(m−1),   phase levels = m,   line-to-line levels = 2m−1

ΔV is the voltage step in volts; Vdc is the total DC bus in volts; m is the number of phase-voltage levels, with m ≥ 2.

With m = 3 and Vdc = 600 V, ΔV = 300 V. The phase voltage can be +300 V, 0 or −300 V. Each device in a clamped leg blocks only one step, Vdc/(m−1), not the whole bus. That is why a 600 V bus can use 300 V devices in a three-level leg.

Phase voltage has m levels. Line-to-line voltage is the difference between two phase voltages. If the phase steps are spaced by ΔV, the difference can take values from +(m−1)ΔV down to −(m−1)ΔV in steps of ΔV. The count is 2m−1 levels. For m = 3, the line voltage has five levels: +600 V, +300 V, 0, −300 V and −600 V. Do not use m for the line-to-line count.

Three families: diode-clamped, flying capacitor, cascaded

A diode-clamped leg (the three-level version is called neutral-point-clamped, NPC) uses one DC bus split by m−1 capacitors. Diodes clamp each switch node to a capacitor tap. Per leg it needs 2(m−1) active switches and (m−1)(m−2) clamping diodes. A three-level leg has four switches and two clamping diodes. It is common in medium-voltage drives because it uses one DC bus.

A flying-capacitor leg uses floating capacitors to clamp the switch nodes. It has no clamping diodes. The capacitor voltages must be precharged and balanced. Balance is harder when the load current is small or the modulation index is low.

A cascaded H-bridge uses s separate H-bridge cells per phase. Each cell has its own isolated DC source, such as a transformer winding, battery pack or PV string. A cell gives +Vcell, 0 or −Vcell. The series connection gives 2s+1 phase levels. The peak phase voltage is sVcell. For s = 3 cells of 100 V, the phase has 7 levels and a 300 V peak.

Modulation and trade-offs

Level-shifted carrier PWM compares one sine reference with m−1 triangular carriers stacked across the range. The carriers can be in phase or shifted; the comparison decides which level is switched. Nearest level control simply rounds the reference to the nearest available level. It switches slowly and suits large m.

More levels reduce the step size. Lower step size means lower dv/dt, lower harmonic distortion and less stress on motor insulation. Each device blocks less voltage. The cost is more devices, gate drivers, capacitors, voltage sensors and control code. Capacitor voltage balance becomes a control problem. NPC has neutral-point drift. Flying-capacitor balancing is harder. Cascaded H-bridge needs isolated DC sources, which means a multi-winding transformer or separate packs.

Use multilevel inverters where the DC bus is high and waveform quality matters: medium-voltage motor drives, solar farms, UPS, HVDC and static compensators. For low-voltage single-phase work, a two-level bridge is usually cheaper. For grid current control, see Chapter 4.9.

Worked example

Question. A three-level neutral-point-clamped inverter runs from a total DC bus Vdc = 600 V. (a) Find the voltage blocked by each active device. (b) List the phase-voltage levels. (c) List the line-to-line voltage levels. (d) A cascaded H-bridge uses 3 cells per phase, each cell with a 100 V isolated DC source. Find the number of phase levels and the peak phase voltage.

  1. The number of levels is m = 3. The step size is ΔV = Vdc/(m−1) = 600 V / 2 = 300 V.
  2. Each active device blocks one step, so the device voltage is 300 V.
  3. The phase voltage levels are +300 V, 0 and −300 V.
  4. The line-to-line voltage has 2m−1 = 2×3−1 = 5 levels. They are +600 V, +300 V, 0, −300 V and −600 V.
  5. For the cascaded converter, s = 3 cells per phase. The phase level count is 2s+1 = 2×3+1 = 7 levels.
  6. The peak phase voltage is sVcell = 3×100 V = 300 V.

Answer. Each device blocks 300 V; the phase levels are +300 V, 0 and −300 V; the line-to-line voltage has 5 levels: +600 V, +300 V, 0, −300 V and −600 V; the cascaded converter has 7 phase levels and a 300 V peak phase voltage.

Exam traps

  • Do not use m for line-to-line levels. A phase with m levels gives 2m−1 line-to-line levels.
  • Do not say each device blocks Vdc. In an m-level clamped leg the ideal block is Vdc/(m−1).
  • Cascaded H-bridge cells need isolated DC sources. They cannot share one common DC bus without extra isolation.
  • NPC has m−1 DC-bus capacitors for the whole inverter, shared by all legs, and (m−1)(m−2) clamping diodes per leg. For three-level, that is two DC-bus capacitors in total and two clamping diodes per leg.
  • More levels does not remove capacitor-balance control. NPC neutral-point drift and flying-capacitor balance are real control problems.

4.9 Grid-tied inverters and current control

On the grid, the inverter does not set the voltage. The grid sets it, and the inverter injects a controlled sine current. A phase-locked loop (PLL) finds the grid angle θ; a slower power loop sets the current amplitude; a fast current loop switches the bridge so the measured current follows the reference. Rooftop solar, battery storage and wind converters use this scheme. It also sets power factor, limits harmonics, and detects an island. This chapter covers the loops, the controllers and the filter.

You need: Chapters 4.4 and 4.5 for the modulator; Unit 6 for PV and storage context.

The grid sets voltage, the inverter sets current

A utility grid is a stiff voltage source. Its voltage amplitude and frequency stay nearly fixed while loads change. If a grid-tied inverter also tried to set voltage, two voltage sources joined by a cable would fight; the current would be set only by the small difference between them. Instead the inverter works as a current source. It measures grid voltage, locks to its angle, and injects a current of the size and phase it wants. The grid absorbs that current.

The PLL gives θ, the grid angle in radians. A DC-link or maximum-power-point-tracking (MPPT) loop compares available DC power with delivered AC power and sets I*, the peak current reference in amperes. A nonzero φ* commands reactive power.

i*(t) = I* sin(θ + φ*)

i* is the current reference in A; I* is its peak in A; θ is the grid angle in rad from the PLL; φ* is the commanded phase shift in rad (0 for unity power factor).

Single-phase real power is P = V_rms I_rms cos φ, where V_rms and I_rms are rms grid voltage and current in volts and amperes, and φ is the phase angle between them. In three-phase, P = √3 V_LL I_rms cos φ, with V_LL the line-to-line rms voltage in volts.

  1. Measure grid voltage; the PLL gives θ and frequency.
  2. The slower power loop sets I* from the DC-link error or MPPT.
  3. Form i* from θ, I* and the commanded φ*.
  4. The current controller compares measured i with i* and drives the bridge through PWM.
  5. The LCL filter attenuates switching harmonics before the current enters the grid.

Three current controllers and the LCL filter

The current loop must be faster than the power loop. A power loop at 10–20 Hz is enough; a current loop often runs at 1–2 kHz.

Hysteresis control compares measured current i with a band of width h around i*. When i reaches i* + h/2, the bridge switches one way; at i* − h/2, it switches back. It is simple and robust, but switching frequency varies with current slope and grid voltage, so the filter is harder to design.

In the synchronous dq frame, the PLL angle rotates the measured currents into DC quantities i_d and i_q. PI controllers drive their errors to zero; the integrator gives zero steady-state error at the fundamental. The axes are coupled by ωL, where ω = 2πf is grid angular frequency in rad/s and L is the filter inductance in henry. Decoupling adds ±ωL i_q and ∓ωL i_d so one axis does not disturb the other. The bridge then switches at a fixed PWM frequency.

A proportional-resonant (PR) controller works in the stationary frame. G_PR(s) = K_p + K_r s/(s² + ω₀²), where s is the Laplace variable, K_p is proportional gain, K_r is resonant gain, and ω₀ = 2πf₀ is the fundamental angular frequency in rad/s. It gives large gain at f₀ and no transform is needed.

An LCL filter has inverter-side inductance L1, grid-side inductance L2 and shunt capacitance C. It attenuates switching harmonics faster than a single L. Its resonance f_res = (1/2π)√((L1+L2)/(L1L2C)) must be damped, by a resistor or active damping, or the loop can oscillate. Here f_res is in hertz, L1 and L2 in henry, C in farad.

Islanding, power factor and off-grid contrast

Anti-islanding protection stops the inverter when the grid disconnects. Otherwise it could energise a local line and endanger a lineman. Passive methods watch voltage and frequency; active methods inject a small disturbance and check the response. Standard tests require a trip within a set time.

Non-unity power factor is commanded by φ*. A phase shift makes the inverter deliver or absorb reactive power within the grid code. The current amplitude then follows the power demand and the reactive limit.

An off-grid inverter has no stiff grid. It must set voltage amplitude and frequency itself, so it uses a voltage loop, often with droop. It has no PLL to a live grid. A grid-tied unit sets current. Some inverters switch between both modes when the grid fails.

Worked example

Question. A rooftop solar inverter delivers P = 5 kW to a 230 V, 50 Hz single-phase grid at unity power factor. Find the rms and peak grid current reference. The grid voltage then rises to 240 V while the DC power stays 5 kW; find the new rms current. Explain why the amplitude must come from the power loop.

  1. At unity power factor cos φ = 1, so I_rms = P/V_rms = 5000 W ÷ 230 V = 21.739 A rms, which rounds to 21.7 A rms.
  2. The peak reference is I* = √2 × I_rms = 1.4142 × 21.739 A = 30.744 A, which rounds to 30.7 A peak.
  3. At V_rms = 240 V, I_rms = 5000 W ÷ 240 V = 20.833 A rms, which rounds to 20.8 A rms.
  4. A fixed 21.739 A reference would give 240 V × 21.739 A = 5217 W at 240 V. The power loop must reduce I* to hold 5 kW.
  5. The PLL keeps θ at the grid angle; with i* = I* sin θ, the current stays in phase with voltage at unity power factor.

Answer. The reference is 21.7 A rms (30.7 A peak) at 230 V and 20.8 A rms at 240 V; the power loop must set the amplitude.

Exam traps

  • Commanding a voltage amplitude on a grid-tied inverter, or omitting the PLL, makes the current land at the wrong phase and the inverter push reactive power.
  • Mixing rms and peak current references: the sine table and comparator use the peak I*, while cable and meter ratings use rms.
  • Using the three-phase formula with phase voltage instead of line-to-line voltage, or forgetting √3, gives a current larger by √3.
  • Treating an LCL filter as a plain L and ignoring its resonance; without damping the current loop can oscillate near f_res.
  • Assuming anti-islanding means the inverter keeps running in an island; it must trip, while an off-grid inverter is designed to run without the grid.

Takeaways

  1. A VSI switches the load between DC rails, so its unfiltered output is a rectangular wave whose amplitude and harmonic content are fixed by the bus and switching pattern.
  2. Three-phase VSI operation is described by 60°-spaced states; 180° and 120° conduction differ in how many devices conduct and whether phase voltage has zero intervals.
  3. Notch-based voltage control lowers the fundamental by removing voltage-time area while keeping the DC bus unchanged.
  4. SPWM moves harmonics to the carrier frequency and makes the fundamental proportional to the reference amplitude.
  5. SVM uses the same bridge states as SPWM but chooses dwell times geometrically, giving better DC-bus utilization and lower harmonic current.
  6. Harmonic elimination places switching angles to null chosen low-order harmonics, at the cost of solving nonlinear angle equations offline.
  7. A CSI needs a stiff DC current and uses load or forced commutation; it is suited to high-power rugged loads but cannot freewheel through antiparallel diodes.
  8. Multilevel inverters reduce voltage steps, device voltage stress, and harmonic content by adding levels.
  9. A grid-tied inverter controls current, not voltage: the PLL supplies the grid angle and the current loop tracks a sine reference.

Glossary

Anti-islanding
Protection that stops a grid-tied inverter from energizing a disconnected grid section.
Bipolar PWM
A PWM method where the load voltage switches between +Vdc and −Vdc.
Carrier frequency
The frequency of the triangular wave in PWM, which sets the switching frequency.

The electrical angle interval during which a switch is kept on.
Current source inverter
An inverter whose DC link is held at a stiff current, usually by an inductor, so the output current is set by switching.

An inductor in the DC link of a current source inverter that keeps the DC current nearly constant.

The fraction of a switching period for which a switch is on.

An antiparallel diode that carries inductive load current when its switch turns off.
Full-bridge inverter
A two-leg inverter with four switches that applies positive, negative, or zero voltage to the load.
Grid-tied inverter
An inverter that injects power into an AC grid and stays synchronized with the grid voltage.
Half-bridge inverter
A single-leg inverter with two switches and a split DC bus that produces two output levels.
Harmonic elimination
A switching-angle design method that makes selected Fourier coefficients zero.
Inverter
A power electronic converter that changes DC into AC by controlled switching.
Line-to-line voltage
The difference between two pole voltages in a three-phase inverter.
Modulation index
In sine-triangle PWM, the ratio of sine reference peak to triangular carrier peak; it sets the fundamental output in the linear range.
Multilevel inverter
An inverter that produces more than two voltage levels so the output approaches a sine in small steps.
