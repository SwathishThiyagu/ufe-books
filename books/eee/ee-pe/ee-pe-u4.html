4.1 Single-phase half-bridge and full-bridge inverters

An inverter turns DC into AC by switching a load between DC rails. In square-wave operation each switch is on for half the output period, so the load sees a rectangular voltage whose height is fixed by the DC bus and whose frequency is set by the switching rate. The half-bridge uses two switches and a split capacitor bus; the full bridge (H-bridge) uses four switches and gives twice the voltage. Every PWM method later in this unit runs on the same bridge.

You need: the IGBT and its anti-parallel diode (see Power BJT, MOSFET and IGBT) and the Fourier series of a square wave.

Half-bridge: split bus, half voltage

The half-bridge has two switches, S1 on top and S2 below, in series across the DC bus Vdc. Two equal capacitors C1 and C2 are also in series across the bus, so their junction O sits at half the bus voltage. The load is connected between the switch midpoint A and the capacitor midpoint O. Taking O as the reference, the top rail is at +Vdc/2 and the bottom rail at −Vdc/2.

With S1 on, vAO = +Vdc/2; with S2 on, vAO = −Vdc/2. Each switch is gated for 180° of the output cycle, so the load voltage is a square wave of height Vdc/2. Its rms is also Vdc/2, since v² never changes.

vo = Σ (2Vdc/nπ) sin nωt, n = 1, 3, 5, … ω = 2πf is the output angular frequency and n the harmonic order. The fundamental peak is 2Vdc/π = 0.637Vdc (rms 0.45Vdc).

The capacitors must be large enough that O does not move while they carry the load current. Each switch still blocks the whole bus Vdc when off. Some syllabi call this the split-supply inverter.

Full bridge: four switches, twice the voltage

The full bridge has two legs: S1 over S2 (leg A) and S3 over S4 (leg B), with the load between the midpoints A and B. The diagonal pairs are gated in turn, and the two switches of one leg are never on together.

vo = Σ (4Vdc/nπ) sin nωt, n = 1, 3, 5, … The fundamental peak is 4Vdc/π = 1.273Vdc (rms 0.9Vdc); the nth harmonic is 1/n of it.

Even harmonics and DC are absent because the wave has half-wave symmetry, v(t + T/2) = −v(t). The rms of the whole wave is Vdc, so the total harmonic distortion is THD = √(Vrms² − V1²)/V1 = √(1 − 0.9²)/0.9 = 48.3 %, whatever Vdc is. The full bridge gives twice the half-bridge voltage from the same bus and switch rating.

R-L load and the feedback diodes

With an R-L load the current cannot reverse at the instant the voltage does. In steady state it rises exponentially towards +Vdc/R in the positive half cycle and falls towards −Vdc/R in the negative half, with time constant τ = L/R, swinging between −Imax and +Imax.

Imax = (Vdc/R)·tanh(T/4τ) T is the output period in seconds and τ = L/R in seconds.

Follow one half cycle. At t = 0 the gates move from S2, S3 to S1, S4, but the current is still −Imax. S1 and S4 cannot carry negative current, so it flows through their anti-parallel diodes D1 and D4 into the positive rail, returning the inductor's energy to the source. Only when the current passes zero, at tx = τ ln(1 + tanh(T/4τ)), do S1 and S4 take over. These are the feedback diodes.

Harmonic by harmonic, In = Vn/|Zn| with |Zn| = √(R² + (nωL)²). |Zn| grows with n, so the current is much closer to a sine than the voltage, and Irms = √(I1² + I3² + I5² + …).

Dead time and device ratings

If both switches of one leg are on together the bus is shorted: this is shoot-through. The gate pattern turns the outgoing switch off and waits a dead time of 1 to 3 μs before turning the incoming one on; a diode carries the load current in the gap. Dead time slightly lowers the fundamental and adds low-order distortion.

  • Peak blocking voltage of each switch: Vdc, in both bridges.
  • Peak switch current: the peak load current (Vdc/R for a resistor, Imax for R-L); with a resistor the average is half of it.
  • To change the output voltage without changing Vdc, the switching pattern must change: see single and multiple pulse modulation.

Worked example

Question. A single-phase full bridge runs from Vdc = 300 V and feeds a 20 Ω resistor at 50 Hz in square-wave mode. Find (a) the rms output, (b) the power, (c) the rms fundamental and its power, (d) the rms third harmonic, (e) the THD and (f) the peak and average switch current.

  1. (a) Vrms = Vdc = 300 V, because v² = 300² at every instant.
  2. (b) P = Vrms²/R = 300²/20 = 4500 W.
  3. (c) V1,peak = 4Vdc/π = 4 × 300/π = 381.97 V, so V1 = 381.97/√2 = 270.09 V. P1 = 270.09²/20 = 3647.6 W (81 % of the total).
  4. (d) V3 = V1/3 = 270.09/3 = 90.03 V rms.
  5. (e) THD = √(300² − 270.09²)/270.09 = 130.57/270.09 = 48.3 %.
  6. (f) Peak current = Vdc/R = 300/20 = 15 A. Each switch carries it for half the period, so its average is 7.5 A.

Answer. Vrms = 300 V, P = 4500 W, V1 = 270.09 V carrying 3647.6 W, V3 = 90.03 V, THD = 48.3 %, switch current 15 A peak and 7.5 A average.

Exam traps

  • The half-bridge output is Vdc/2 high (rms Vdc/2); the full bridge gives Vdc. Each switch blocks the full Vdc in both.
  • The fundamental peak is 4Vdc/π (full bridge) or 2Vdc/π (half-bridge), not Vdc; its rms is 0.9Vdc or 0.45Vdc. The nth harmonic is 1/n of the fundamental, not 1/n².
  • With an R-L load the voltage is still a square wave but the current is exponential, and the diodes, not the switches, conduct just after each switching.
  • 120° conduction belongs to the three-phase bridge; a single-phase square-wave bridge gates each switch for 180°.

At each switching the inductor current still flows the old way; the incoming switches cannot carry it, so the diodes return it to the bus until it passes zero. Without them the L di/dt spike would destroy the switches.

Both switches of one leg conduct and short the bus. A dead time is inserted between turning one off and the other on.

4.2 Three-phase VSI: 180 and 120 degree conduction

A three-phase voltage source inverter (VSI) has three legs, six switches and one DC bus. Each leg ties its output terminal to the positive or the negative rail. In 180° conduction every switch is gated for 180°, so three switches, one per leg, are on at every instant and the line voltage is a six-step wave. In 120° conduction each switch is gated for 120°, only two are on, and each phase has two 60° gaps. Motor drives, UPS units and solar inverters are built on this bridge.

You need: the single-phase bridge and star-delta circuit relations.

Three legs and the 180° switch table

Number the switches in the order they are gated: leg A has S1 (top) and S4 (bottom), leg B has S3 and S6, leg C has S5 and S2. In 180° mode each gate pulse is 180° wide and a new switch is gated every 60°, in the order S1, S2, S3, S4, S5, S6. The two switches of one leg are complementary, so a terminal is always tied to one rail.

In each 60° interval, two terminals share one rail and the third is on the other. For a balanced star load of R per phase, the two parallel phases (R/2) are in series with the single one, so the bus current is Vdc/(1.5R). The single phase takes 2Vdc/3 and each of the parallel pair Vdc/3.

Line and phase voltages in 180° mode

The pole voltage vAO, measured from the bus midpoint O, is a ±Vdc/2 square wave. The line voltage vAB = vAO − vBO is a quasi-square wave: +Vdc for 120°, zero for 60°, −Vdc for 120°, zero for 60°. The phase voltage vAN steps through Vdc/3, 2Vdc/3, Vdc/3, −Vdc/3, −2Vdc/3, −Vdc/3: the six-step wave.

vAB = Σ (4Vdc/nπ) cos(nπ/6) sin n(ωt + π/6) The sum runs over n = 1, 5, 7, 11, 13, …: cos(nπ/6) is zero for n = 3, 9, 15, so no triplen harmonic appears in the line or phase voltage, and the nth harmonic is 1/n of the fundamental.

The total rms exceeds the fundamental because of the 5th, 7th, 11th and 13th harmonics. They heat a motor and cause torque ripple; the fundamental does the useful work. Some syllabi call this bridge the six-step or square-wave VSI.

120° conduction mode

In 120° mode each gate pulse is 120° wide, still one new switch every 60°. At any instant only two switches conduct, one in the upper group and one in the lower: S6, S1 from 0° to 60°, S1, S2 from 60° to 120°, and so on. Each leg is idle for two 60° gaps per cycle, one after its top switch and one after its bottom switch. In a gap that terminal floats.

With a resistive star load the two connected phases each take Vdc/2 and the floating phase takes zero. The phase voltage is +Vdc/2 for 120°, zero for 60°, −Vdc/2 for 120°, zero for 60°. The line voltage has no zero interval: it steps through Vdc, Vdc/2, −Vdc/2, −Vdc, −Vdc/2 and Vdc/2.

Question. A three-phase bridge in 180° mode has Vdc = 400 V and feeds a star-connected resistive load of 10 Ω per phase. Find (a) the rms line and phase voltages, (b) the rms phase current and the load power, (c) the rms fundamental phase voltage, and (d) the peak and rms current of each switch.

  2. (b) IP = VP/R = 188.6/10 = 18.86 A. P = 3 IP² R = 3 × 18.86² × 10 = 10667 W.

Exam traps

  • 180° mode does not mean both switches of a leg are on: each switch is gated for 180° and the two switches of a leg are complementary.
  • Keep line and phase values apart: in 180° mode the line rms is 0.816Vdc and the phase rms 0.471Vdc; in 120° mode 0.707Vdc and 0.408Vdc.
  • The six-step wave is the 180° phase voltage; the 180° line voltage is a 120° quasi-square. Many students swap the two sketches.
  • 120° mode has two switches on at a time and two 60° gaps per leg per cycle, not one.
  • No triplen harmonics appear in a three-phase VSI line voltage; the lowest harmonics are the 5th and 7th.

4.3 Voltage control: single and multiple pulse modulation

A square-wave inverter has a fixed output: the full-bridge fundamental is always 4Vdc/π peak. To vary it without changing the bus, the bridge leaves the load at zero volts for part of each half cycle. In single-pulse modulation each half cycle holds one pulse of adjustable width; in multiple-pulse modulation it holds p equal, equally spaced pulses. Both control the voltage with switches that are fully on or fully off, so the efficiency stays high. They are the first step from square-wave inverters to PWM.

You need: the full-bridge square wave and its Fourier series from single-phase bridges.

Why the square wave needs control

An AC motor needs its voltage to rise with frequency, a UPS must hold its output while the battery runs down, and a heater needs adjustable power. All three need a variable fundamental from a fixed Vdc. The full bridge can apply +Vdc, −Vdc or zero (S1 and S3 on, or S2 and S4 on), so the trick is to insert zero intervals. This is called pulse-width modulation (PWM) in general; with one or a few pulses per half cycle the frequency of switching stays low.

Single-pulse modulation

Let the positive half cycle be +Vdc for a pulse of width 2d centred on 90°, and zero for the rest; the negative half cycle mirrors it. The zero interval of width π − 2d therefore straddles each zero crossing of the output. The gate pulses come from comparing a rectangular reference of amplitude Ar with a triangular carrier of amplitude Ac at twice the output frequency; the modulation index M = Ar/Ac sets 2d = M × 180°.

Vrms = Vdc√(2d/π)

Vrms is the rms output in volts and d is half the pulse width in radians (2d/π = 2d°/180°).

bn = (4Vdc/nπ) sin(nd), n = 1, 3, 5, … bn is the peak of the nth harmonic in volts. At 2d = 180° this is the square wave; at 2d = 0 the output is zero.

The fundamental follows sin d, not d: near full width a large change of width changes the fundamental very little. A harmonic vanishes when nd = π, so a pulse 120° wide (d = 60°) removes the 3rd harmonic. At low outputs, though, the 3rd, 5th and 7th are large compared with the fundamental, so single-pulse control gives a poor waveform at low voltage.

Multiple-pulse modulation

Raise the carrier frequency so that it gives p equal pulses per half cycle, each 2d wide and centred at γk = (k − ½)180°/p. The carrier runs at fc = 2pf, and each pulse width is 2d = M × 180°/p. Some syllabi call this symmetric or uniform PWM.

Vrms = Vdc√(2pd/π)

With d in degrees the on fraction is 2pd/180.

bn = (4Vdc/nπ) sin(nd) Σ sin(nγk) The sum runs over the p pulse centres γk of one half cycle.

For the same total on-time the rms is the same as with one pulse, but the spectrum changes: the strongest harmonics move to orders 2p − 1 and 2p + 1, near the carrier, where a smaller filter removes them. The lower harmonics are smaller than in single-pulse control but do not vanish. The price is switching loss: p pulses per half cycle mean 2p pulses and 4p switching edges per cycle.

Exam traps

  • Set the calculator to degrees for sin 30°, or convert d to radians first; in Vrms = Vdc√(2d/π) d must be in radians.
  • d is half the pulse width. A "60° pulse" means d = 30°, so the fundamental is (4Vdc/π) sin 30°, not sin 60°.
  • The fundamental falls as sin d, not in proportion to the width; near full width it hardly changes.
  • p counts pulses per half cycle. A full cycle has 2p pulses and 4p switching edges.
  • Multiple pulses do not change the rms for the same total on-time; they move the strongest harmonics to 2p ± 1.

4.4 Sinusoidal PWM: unipolar and bipolar

Sinusoidal PWM (SPWM) compares a sine reference with a fast triangular carrier and switches the bridge at every crossing. The duty ratio then varies sinusoidally over the output cycle, so the average output follows the reference. The fundamental is set by the reference amplitude and its frequency by the reference frequency, while the unwanted harmonics move up to the carrier frequency, where a small filter or the load inductance removes them. Bipolar SPWM gives a two-level output; unipolar SPWM gives a three-level output with half the ripple.

You need: the full bridge and the idea of a variable pulse width from single and multiple pulse modulation.

The sine-triangle comparator

Let the reference be vr = Vr sin 2πfm t, with peak Vr and frequency fm (the output frequency), and let the triangular carrier have peak Vc and frequency fc. Each crossing of the two is a switching instant. Two ratios describe the modulator.

ma = Vr/Vc, mf = fc/fm ma is the amplitude modulation index and mf the frequency modulation ratio.

In one carrier period the leg is on top for a fraction d = ½(1 + ma sin θ) of the time, so the average leg voltage, measured from the bus midpoint, is (Vdc/2) ma sin θ. Its fundamental is a sine of peak ma Vdc/2 per leg. This holds while ma ≤ 1, the linear range. Above 1 the reference passes the carrier peak, some carrier periods have no crossing, and low-order harmonics return; this is overmodulation, and as ma grows the output tends to the square wave, 4Vdc/π for the full bridge.

Bipolar SPWM

Bipolar SPWM uses one reference for the whole bridge. When vr > carrier, S1 and S4 are on and vo = +Vdc; otherwise S2 and S3 are on and vo = −Vdc. The output has two levels and no zero state. Its fundamental peak is ma Vdc.

The harmonics sit at mf and its sidebands mf ± 2, mf ± 4, then around 2mf (2mf ± 1, 2mf ± 3), and so on. Choose mf odd, so that the wave has half-wave symmetry and no even harmonics. For mf of about 9 or more the harmonic amplitudes depend on ma but hardly on mf: at ma = 0.8 the mf harmonic is 0.818Vdc peak and each mf ± 2 sideband 0.220Vdc.

Unipolar SPWM

Unipolar SPWM switches the two legs separately: leg A compares +vr with the carrier and leg B compares −vr with the same carrier. When both top or both bottom switches are on, the load sees zero, so vo takes +Vdc, 0 or −Vdc. The fundamental is still ma Vdc, but the output pulses come twice per carrier period and the harmonics at mf and its sidebands cancel between the legs.

The first harmonic group is at 2mf ± 1: at ma = 0.8 each is 0.314Vdc peak. The ripple is at twice the frequency and each step is only Vdc, so the filter can be smaller, and the load sees half the voltage jumps. Each switch still switches at fc.

Three-phase SPWM and third-harmonic injection

A three-phase bridge uses three references 120° apart and one carrier. Each pole voltage has a fundamental peak ma Vdc/2, so the line voltage fundamental is √3 times that. Choose mf odd and a multiple of 3: the carrier harmonic mf is then the same in all three poles and cancels in the line voltage, leaving mf ± 2 as the first group.

VL1 = 0.612 ma Vdc VL1 is the rms fundamental line voltage, (√3/2√2) ma Vdc, valid for ma ≤ 1.

Adding a third harmonic of one sixth of the fundamental to all three references flattens their tops, so ma can rise to 2/√3 = 1.155 before overmodulation. The added harmonic is the same in every phase, so it cancels in the line voltage. The usable line voltage rises by 15.5 %, to 0.707Vdc rms, the same limit as space vector modulation.

Worked example

Question. A single-phase full bridge has Vdc = 400 V and uses SPWM with fm = 50 Hz, ma = 0.8 and mf = 21. Find (a) the fundamental peak and rms, (b) the carrier frequency, (c) the frequencies and sizes of the first harmonic group for bipolar SPWM, and (d) the same for unipolar SPWM.

  5. A filter that passes 50 Hz must attenuate 950 Hz for bipolar but only 2050 Hz for unipolar: more than twice as far from the fundamental.

4.5 Space vector modulation

Space vector modulation (SVM or SVPWM) treats the three legs of the bridge as one unit. The eight switch states become eight voltage vectors: six active vectors 60° apart and two zero vectors. The wanted three-phase output is one vector rotating at the output frequency, and in every switching period the modulator makes it on average from the two nearest active vectors and the zero vectors. SVM uses the bus 15.5 % better than plain sine-triangle PWM and is easy to code, so most three-phase drives and grid inverters use it.

You need: the six-switch bridge from 180° and 120° conduction and the linear limit of sinusoidal PWM.

Eight states and the space vector

Write Sa = 1 when the top switch of leg a is on and 0 when the bottom one is. The state (Sa Sb Sc) takes eight values. In 000 and 111 all three terminals are on one rail, so the load voltage is zero: these are the zero vectors V0 and V7. The other six, 100, 110, 010, 011, 001 and 101, are the active vectors V1 to V6.

Three phase voltages that add to zero can be drawn as one vector in a plane with axes α (along phase a) and β (90° ahead). The Clarke transform with the 2/3 scale keeps the vector length equal to the phase peak:

vα = (2/3)(va − vb/2 − vc/2) and vβ = (vb − vc)/√3. State 100 gives star-load phase voltages 2Vdc/3, −Vdc/3, −Vdc/3, so vα = 2Vdc/3 and vβ = 0.

So every active vector is 2Vdc/3 long. The six tips form a hexagon, the zero vectors sit at its centre, and the six triangles between neighbouring vectors are sectors 1 to 6. A balanced sine output of phase peak Vm is a reference vector Vref of length Vm turning at ω = 2πf.

Dwell times

In each switching period Ts the modulator applies the two active vectors that bound the sector, here called Vx (the one at the start of the sector) and Vy (the next one), for times Tx and Ty, and the zero vectors for the rest, T0. The volt-seconds must match:

Vref Ts = Vx Tx + Vy Ty with T0 = Ts − Tx − Ty spent on V0 and V7, which add nothing.

Tx = m Ts sin(60° − θ), Ty = m Ts sin θ θ is the reference angle measured from Vx (0° to 60°), m = √3 Vm/Vdc is the modulation index and Ts the switching period.

Switching sequence and leg times

The zero time is split equally between V0 and V7, and the period is arranged so that only one leg changes at each step. In sector 1 the seven-segment sequence is 000 → 100 → 110 → 111 → 110 → 100 → 000, spending T0/4, Tx/2, Ty/2, T0/2, Ty/2, Tx/2, T0/4. Each leg switches on once and off once per period, and the pattern is symmetric about the centre, which lowers current ripple.

Each leg is on for the sum of the segments in which its bit is 1. In sector 1 leg a is on for Tx + Ty + T0/2, leg b for Ty + T0/2 and leg c for T0/2. These three times go straight into the compare registers of a microcontroller timer. The result equals sine-triangle PWM with a common-mode term −(max + min)/2 added to the references; it cancels in the line voltages.

4.6 Harmonic elimination techniques

Harmonic elimination chooses the switching angles of an inverter so that chosen low-order harmonics are exactly zero. Each harmonic is a Fourier coefficient, a sum of cosines of the switching angles, and setting it to zero gives one equation per harmonic. With N angles per quarter cycle one equation fixes the fundamental and the other N − 1 remove N − 1 harmonics. This is selective harmonic elimination (SHE). Pulse-width choice, notching, phase-shifted transformers and stepped waves are the classical ways to do it.

You need: the Fourier coefficients of a pulse from single and multiple pulse modulation.

A harmonic is an equation in the switching angles

Make the output quarter-wave symmetric: the second quarter mirrors the first and the negative half mirrors the positive half. Then all even harmonics and all cosine terms vanish, and only the odd sine coefficients bn remain. They depend only on the switching angles α1 < α2 < … < αN in the first quarter (0° to 90°).

bn = (4Vdc/nπ) Σ (−1)k+1 cos nαk This is for a three-level (unipolar) output that is zero from 0 to α1, +Vdc from α1 to α2, zero again from α2 to α3, and so on; n is odd.

bn = (4Vdc/nπ)[1 + 2Σ (−1)^k cos nαk] This is for a two-level (bipolar) output that starts at +Vdc and changes sign at each αk.

N angles give N equations: b1 equal to the wanted fundamental and bn = 0 for N − 1 chosen harmonics. The equations are nonlinear, so they are solved offline (by Newton's method, for example) for each output level, and the angles are stored in a table that the controller reads.

Four classical methods

  • Pulse width. A single pulse 2d wide has bn ∝ sin nd, which is zero when nd is a multiple of 180°: a 120° pulse removes the 3rd, and a 72° or 144° pulse the 5th. This is the one-angle case, α1 = 90° − d.
  • Notching. Extra notches in each quarter of a square wave give extra angles: two notches can remove the 3rd and 5th while the fundamental stays near the square-wave value.
  • Phase-shifted transformers. Two square-wave bridges displaced by φ and added in series transformer windings give bn ∝ cos(nφ/2): φ = 60° removes the 3rd and φ = 36° the 5th. In three-phase, two six-step bridges feeding star and delta windings (30° apart) cancel the 5th and 7th, leaving the 11th and 13th: a twelve-step wave.
  • Stepped waves. Several bridges or cells with their own sources add up to a staircase; choosing each step angle removes harmonics, as in multilevel inverters.

Removing low harmonics does not destroy their energy. It moves to the next orders, which a small filter or the load inductance handles more cheaply because their impedance is n times larger.

Three-phase choices and limits

In a three-phase three-wire system the triplen harmonics (3rd, 9th, 15th) are the same in all three phases and cancel in the line voltage, so no angle is spent on them. A three-phase SHE table removes the 5th, 7th, 11th and 13th instead. Compared with an output filter, SHE removes the harmonic at the source with a low switching frequency, which suits high-power drives. Its costs: the angles are fixed for each operating point, a change of output needs a jump to another row of the table, and closely spaced angles need a minimum pulse width the devices can make.

4.7 Current source inverters

A voltage source inverter holds its DC voltage steady with a capacitor and lets the load decide the current. A current source inverter (CSI) does the opposite: a large series inductor Ld holds the DC link current Id almost constant, and the bridge steers that current into the load lines. The output current is a rectangular wave of height Id and the load voltage is whatever the load needs. CSIs survive load short circuits well and regenerate easily, so they are used in megawatt motor drives and induction heating. Some syllabi call it the current-fed inverter.

You need: thyristor commutation (see Commutation techniques: natural and forced) and the three-phase bridge of 180° and 120° conduction.

Hold the current, not the voltage

A CSI is fed from a controlled rectifier or a chopper through Ld. The inductor makes the link current stiff: over one output cycle Id hardly changes. The bridge can only choose which load lines carry it, so the switches must never open the current path. If every path opened, Ld di/dt would destroy the devices, so the incoming device is turned on before the outgoing one is turned off (an overlap), the opposite of the dead time in a VSI.

Single-phase capacitor-commutated CSI

Four thyristors T1 to T4 form a bridge fed with Id, and a capacitor C is connected across the load. Firing T1 and T2 sends +Id into the load-and-capacitor pair; firing T3 and T4 sends −Id. The load current is a square wave of ±Id split between R and C.

Commutation is done by the capacitor. While T1, T2 conduct, C charges with the load side positive at the terminal T1 feeds. When T3 and T4 are fired, that capacitor voltage appears in reverse across T1 and T2, their current falls to zero at once (Id moves to T3, T4) and they stay reverse-biased while C discharges through the load and recharges the other way. With a resistive load the capacitor voltage at each switching is V0 = Id R tanh(T/4RC), where T is the output period.

tc = RC ln(1 + tanh(T/4RC))

tc is the circuit turn-off time in seconds; it must exceed the thyristor turn-off time tq. When the half period is long compared with RC, tc = RC ln 2 = 0.693RC.

The three-phase form of this circuit, with commutating capacitors and series diodes that isolate them from the load, is the auto-sequential commutated inverter (ASCI).

Three-phase CSI with 120° conduction

Six devices conduct for 120° each, two at a time, one from the upper group and one from the lower. From 0° to 60° devices 6 and 1 conduct, so Id enters line A and returns through B; then 1 and 2 (A to C), 2 and 3 (B to C), 3 and 4 (B to A), 4 and 5 (C to A), 5 and 6 (C to B). Each line current is +Id for 120°, zero for 60°, −Id for 120°, zero for 60°: the same shape as the 180° VSI line voltage.

I1 = (√6/π)Id = 0.780Id, Irms = 0.816Id I1 is the rms fundamental line current and Irms the rms of the whole wave.

The 120° shape has no triplen harmonics (cos 3 × 30° = 0); the 5th is 20 % of the fundamental, the 7th 14.3 %, the 11th 9.1 % and the 13th 7.7 %, a THD of 31.1 %. With a motor, capacitors at its terminals take the harmonic currents, so the motor voltage comes out nearly sinusoidal.

A load-commutated inverter (LCI) uses the back EMF of an over-excited synchronous motor, which runs at a leading power factor, to turn its thyristors off, and drives motors of several megawatts in pumps, fans and compressors. At standstill there is no EMF, so the LCI starts by pulsing the link current to zero at each commutation until the motor has speed.

Exam traps

  • Id is the height of the current wave, not its rms: the fundamental rms is 0.78Id and the total rms 0.816Id.
  • A CSI needs overlap between incoming and outgoing devices, not dead time; an open circuit, not a short circuit, is the dangerous fault.
  • The diode with each CSI switch is in series, for reverse blocking; the VSI diode is anti-parallel, for feedback.
  • In the capacitor-commutated CSI, the turn-off time is RC ln 2 only when the half period is long compared with RC.
  • A grid-tied inverter controls its current but has a capacitor DC link, so it is a VSI, not a CSI.

4.8 Multilevel inverters

A multilevel inverter builds its output from several small DC steps instead of jumping between two rails. With m levels in the phase voltage, each step is Vdc/(m − 1), the output is a staircase close to a sine, and each device blocks only one step. Lower dv/dt, lower harmonics and lower device voltage are why medium-voltage drives, large solar inverters, STATCOMs and HVDC converters use it. The three classic families are diode-clamped, flying-capacitor and cascaded H-bridge inverters.

You need: the half-bridge leg from single-phase bridges and the stepped-wave idea from harmonic elimination.

Levels, steps and device voltage

A two-level leg gives a pole voltage of +Vdc/2 or −Vdc/2. Split the bus into m − 1 equal parts and the leg can stop at m levels, from +Vdc/2 to −Vdc/2 in steps of Vdc/(m − 1). The line voltage is the difference of two pole voltages, so it has 2m − 1 levels.

ΔV = Vdc/(m − 1), line levels = 2m − 1 ΔV is the step in volts, Vdc the total bus voltage and m the number of pole-voltage levels.

Each switch in a clamped leg blocks only ΔV, so on a 3.3 kV bus each switch of a three-level leg blocks only 1.65 kV. A staircase with more steps has a lower THD and smaller voltage jumps, which spares motor insulation and long cables.

The three families

Diode-clamped (neutral-point-clamped, NPC). One bus is split by m − 1 capacitors, and diodes tie the inner switch nodes to the capacitor taps. In the three-level leg, S1 and S2 on give +Vdc/2, S2 and S3 on give 0 (through a clamping diode to the midpoint), and S3 and S4 on give −Vdc/2. S1 and S3 are complementary, as are S2 and S4.

Modulation and trade-offs

Level-shifted carrier PWM compares one sine with m − 1 stacked triangles; phase-shifted carriers are used for flying-capacitor and CHB cells so each cell switches equally. At low switching frequency, a staircase whose step angles are chosen by harmonic elimination removes s − 1 harmonics with s cells; nearest-level control rounds the reference to the nearest step and suits converters with many cells.

More levels cost more devices, gate drivers, capacitors and sensors. NPC needs control of the midpoint voltage, flying capacitors need voltage balancing, and CHB needs isolated sources, usually a phase-shifting transformer, whose shifted windings also cancel input-current harmonics. For a low-voltage single-phase inverter a two-level bridge is cheaper.

4.9 Grid-tied inverters and current control

On the grid the inverter does not set the voltage: the grid does. A grid-tied inverter instead injects a controlled sine current in step with the grid voltage. A phase-locked loop (PLL) finds the grid angle, an outer loop sets how much current is needed, and a fast inner current loop switches the bridge so that the measured current follows the reference. Rooftop solar, battery storage and wind converters work this way, and the same loops set the power factor, keep the harmonics low and disconnect the inverter if the grid fails.

You need: sinusoidal PWM and phasors with real and reactive power.

The grid sets the voltage, the inverter sets the current

The grid is a stiff voltage source: its amplitude and frequency barely move when one inverter connects. Two voltage sources joined by a small inductance would exchange a current set only by their small difference, which is hard to control. So the inverter behaves as a controlled current source behind its filter inductance.

i* = I* sin(θ + φ*)

i* is the current reference in amperes, I* its peak, θ the grid angle from the PLL and φ* the commanded phase shift (0 for unity power factor).

Single-phase power is P = V I cos φ and Q = V I sin φ, with V and I rms values and φ the angle between them; in three-phase, P = √3 VL I cos φ with VL the line voltage. At fixed I*, changing φ* trades real power for reactive power, so the outer loop must set I* and φ* together.

  1. Measure the grid voltage; the PLL gives θ and the frequency.
  2. The DC-link voltage loop compares the link voltage with its set value: if power arrives faster than it leaves, the voltage rises and I* is raised. (A solar maximum power point tracker sets the link voltage set-point; it does not set I* directly.)
  3. Form i* from θ, I* and φ*.
  4. The current controller compares the measured i with i* and drives the bridge.
  5. An L or LCL filter removes the switching ripple before the grid.

Current controllers and the LCL filter

The current loop must be much faster than the DC-link loop: a link loop of 10 to 20 Hz bandwidth, a current loop of 1 to 2 kHz bandwidth, with the bridge switching and sampling at 10 to 20 kHz.

Hysteresis control switches the bridge whenever the current leaves a band of width h around i*. It is simple and very fast, but its switching frequency changes with the grid voltage and the inductance, which makes the filter hard to design. In the synchronous (dq) frame, the PLL angle turns the three-phase currents into two DC quantities, id (active) and iq (reactive); PI controllers then give zero steady-state error, with ωL cross-coupling terms cancelled. A proportional-resonant (PR) controller, Kp + Kr s/(s² + ω0²), does the same in the stationary frame by having very high gain at the grid frequency ω0.

fres = (1/2π)√((L1 + L2)/(L1 L2 C)) L1 is the inverter-side inductance, L2 the grid-side inductance and C the filter capacitance; fres in hertz.

An LCL filter attenuates the switching ripple at 60 dB per decade, far better than a single L, with less inductance. Its resonance must be damped, by a resistor in series with C or by active damping in the controller, and is usually placed between ten times the grid frequency and half the switching frequency.

Islanding, power factor and off-grid operation

If the grid is disconnected for repair, an inverter that kept feeding local loads would energise the line and endanger the line crew. Anti-islanding protection trips it: passive methods watch for voltage or frequency moving out of limits, and active methods inject a small disturbance and watch the response. Grid codes set the time within which the inverter must stop.

Takeaways

  1. A square-wave bridge fixes the output by the bus: 0.9Vdc fundamental rms for the full bridge, with odd harmonics of 1/n.
  2. With an R-L load the voltage stays square, the current is exponential, and the feedback diodes conduct after every switching.
  3. In 180° mode three switches conduct and the phase voltage is a six-step wave; in 120° mode two conduct and the output is lower.
  4. Single and multiple pulse modulation vary the fundamental by pulse width; more pulses move the harmonics up to 2p ± 1.
  5. Sinusoidal PWM makes the fundamental ma Vdc and pushes the harmonics to the carrier; unipolar SPWM doubles their frequency.
  6. Space vector modulation averages two active vectors and the zero vectors each period and reaches 15.5 % more voltage than SPWM.
  7. Harmonic elimination solves for switching angles that zero chosen harmonics; N angles remove N − 1 of them.
  8. A CSI holds the link current, needs reverse-blocking switches and overlap, survives shorts and regenerates easily.
  9. Multilevel inverters divide the bus into steps, lowering device voltage, dv/dt and THD at the cost of more parts and balancing.
  10. A grid-tied inverter is a controlled current source: the PLL gives the angle, the outer loop the amplitude, the inner loop the waveform.

Evaluation: SHE gives a clean low-order spectrum at a low switching frequency, ideal for large drives, but the angles must be stored for every output level and a change of output means a jump in the table.

Points to cover. Grid is a voltage source; inverter is a current source; PLL gives θ; reference i* = I* sin(θ + φ*); Inner current loop with PWM; outer DC-link loop sets I*; Numbers for 5 kW; 5° error: P and Q, and correction by the loops.

Model answer.

  1. Principle: the grid fixes the voltage, so the inverter controls the current it injects. The voltage it must make is the grid voltage plus the drop across its filter, and this is produced automatically by the current loop.
  2. PLL: measures the grid voltage and produces its angle θ and frequency; in a single-phase unit it compares the grid voltage with an internal sine and adjusts the internal frequency until the error is zero.
  3. Current reference: i* = I* sin(θ + φ*), with φ* = 0 for unity power factor. The inner controller (PI in dq, PR in the stationary frame, or hysteresis) compares the measured current with i* and sets the PWM duty of the bridge each switching period; its bandwidth is about 1–2 kHz.
  4. DC-link loop: if the PV array delivers more power than the inverter injects, the DC-link capacitor charges and its voltage rises. The slow outer loop (10–20 Hz) raises I* until the link voltage returns to its set point, so power in equals power out. The MPPT sets that set point.
  5. Numbers: I = P/V = 5000/230 = 21.74 A rms, I* = 30.74 A peak.
  6. PLL error of 5°: the current leads (or lags) the voltage by 5°.
    P = V I cos 5° = 230 × 21.74 × 0.9962 = 4981 W
Q = V I sin 5° = 230 × 21.74 × 0.0872 = 436 var
  7. Effect: the real power falls by 19 W, so the DC-link voltage rises and the outer loop raises I* by 1/cos 5° = 1.0038, to 21.82 A; the power is restored but the inverter now also exchanges about 437 var and runs at power factor 0.996. A large or changing error (weak grid, distorted voltage) can make the current loop interact with the grid impedance and become unstable.
  8. Choice of PLL: a single-phase PLL builds a second, 90° shifted signal (a second-order generalised integrator or a transport delay) so that it can work like a three-phase dq PLL; its bandwidth is kept to a few tens of hertz so that grid harmonics do not disturb θ.
  9. Harmonic limits: the current loop must also keep low-order current harmonics within the grid code, typically a few per cent THD; the PR controller can be given extra resonant terms at the 3rd, 5th and 7th for this.
  10. Evaluation: the PLL sets the quality of the power factor, the current loop sets the waveform quality and harmonics, and the DC-link loop sets the power balance. A good design keeps the loops about a decade apart in bandwidth so that each sees the faster one as ideal.
Evaluate Evaluate a three-level neutral-point-clamped inverter against a two-level VSI for a 3.3 kV medium-voltage motor drive. Cover device voltage, harmonic content, dv/dt, component count and control complexity. 15 marks

Points to cover. DC bus from a 3.3 kV rectifier; Device blocking Vdc against Vdc/2; Line voltage levels 3 against 5; dv/dt halves; Component counts; Midpoint balancing; verdict.

Model answer.

  1. Bus: a six-pulse rectifier on 3.3 kV gives about 1.35 × 3300 = 4455 V DC.
  2. Device voltage: in a two-level leg each switch blocks the whole bus, 4455 V, which needs the highest-voltage (and slowest) devices or devices in series. In a three-level NPC leg each switch blocks Vdc/2 = 2228 V, within the range of standard high-voltage IGBTs.
  3. Waveform: the two-level pole voltage has 2 levels and the line voltage 3; the NPC pole has 3 levels and the line 5. Each step is 2228 V instead of 4455 V, so dv/dt is halved for the same switching speed, which protects motor insulation and bearings and reduces reflected-wave overvoltage on long cables.
  4. Harmonics: for the same switching frequency the three-level line voltage has roughly half the ripple, so the motor current THD and the torque ripple are lower, or the switching frequency can be lowered for the same THD.
  5. Components per inverter:
    Two-levelThree-level NPC
    Switches612
    Clamping diodes06
    Bus capacitors1 bank2 (split bus)
    Gate drivers612
    Device blocking voltage4455 V2228 V
  6. Control: the NPC needs a midpoint-voltage balancing loop (by choosing between redundant small vectors) and more complex modulation; the two-level bridge needs only standard SPWM or SVM.
  7. Losses: the NPC switches half the voltage, so switching loss per device falls, but current passes through two devices in series, so conduction loss rises; the inner switches carry more loss than the outer ones.
  8. Effect on the motor: the five-level line voltage of the NPC follows the sine more closely; with the same 1 kHz switching the current ripple and the torque ripple are about halved, and the motor can often be used without the sine filter a two-level drive would need on a long cable.
  9. Reliability: more parts mean more possible failures, but each device is less stressed; NPC drives can also run on with reduced output after some device failures, using the remaining redundant states.
  10. Verdict: at 3.3 kV the NPC is preferred: it uses standard devices without series connection, halves dv/dt and improves the waveform, for twice the switches and a balancing loop. Below about 690 V the two-level bridge is cheaper and is the normal choice.
Evaluate Choose between a voltage source inverter and a current source inverter for a 5 MW pump drive that must survive output short circuits and return braking energy to the supply. Justify the choice. 13 marks

Points to cover. Link element and what it holds; Fault behaviour: short against open circuit; Regeneration path; Commutation: LCI with a synchronous motor; Verdict with its costs.

Model answer.

  1. A VSI holds the DC voltage with a capacitor; its output voltage is set by the switches and the load sets the current. A CSI holds the DC current with an inductor; its output current is set by the switches and the load sets the voltage.
  2. Short circuit: in a VSI the bus capacitor discharges straight into a short, so the devices must be turned off within microseconds. In a CSI the link inductor limits di/dt and the rectifier controls Id, so a short only stops the power flow. A CSI tolerates it; its dangerous fault is an open circuit.
  3. Regeneration: a CSI returns energy by reversing the link voltage with a phase-controlled rectifier (firing angle above 90°) while Id keeps its direction; no extra converter is needed. A diode-fed VSI can not return energy and needs an active front end or a braking resistor.
  4. Commutation: at 5 MW, a synchronous motor run over-excited supplies the leading current that turns the thyristors off (a load-commutated inverter), so cheap, rugged thyristors can be used. Starting needs pulsed link current until the motor has about a tenth of its speed.
  5. Comparison for this duty:

  6. Costs of the CSI: the large choke, a lagging input power factor at low speed, six-step torque ripple that needs attention for torsional resonance, and slower dynamic response.
  7. Harmonics to the motor: the six-step current of an LCI has a 5th of 20 % and a 7th of 14 %, which produce sixth-harmonic torque pulsations; for a pump with a stiff shaft this is acceptable, and twelve-pulse arrangements reduce it further.
  8. Verdict: for a 5 MW pump, where dynamics are slow and ruggedness and regeneration matter most, the CSI (as an LCI with a synchronous motor) is the better choice; for a fast, compact drive a VSI with an active front end would be chosen instead.

Glossary

Amplitude modulation index (ma)
Reference peak divided by carrier peak in sine-triangle PWM.
Anti-islanding
Protection that stops a grid-tied inverter when the grid is lost.
Auto-sequential commutated inverter (ASCI)
A three-phase thyristor CSI with commutating capacitors and isolating diodes.
Bipolar PWM
PWM in which the bridge output takes only +Vdc and −Vdc.
Cascaded H-bridge
Series full-bridge cells with isolated sources forming a multilevel phase.
Conduction angle
The angle in each cycle for which a switch is gated on.
Current source inverter (CSI)
An inverter with a stiff DC current held by a series inductor.
Dead time
A short delay between turning one switch of a leg off and the other on, to avoid shoot-through.
Distortion factor (DF)
rms of the harmonics each weighted by 1/n², divided by the fundamental.
Dwell time
The time for which a switch state is applied in one switching period.
Feedback diode
A diode across each switch that carries reactive load current back to the DC bus.
Frequency modulation ratio (mf)
Carrier frequency divided by output frequency.
Full bridge (H-bridge)
Two switching legs with the load between their midpoints; output ±Vdc or 0.
Half-bridge
One switching leg with a split capacitor bus; output ±Vdc/2.
Inverter
A converter that changes DC into AC by switching.
LCL filter
An inverter output filter of two inductors and a shunt capacitor.
Load-commutated inverter (LCI)
A thyristor CSI whose devices are turned off by the back EMF of a leading power factor load.
Neutral-point-clamped (NPC) inverter
The three-level diode-clamped inverter.
Overmodulation
Operation with ma above 1, where low-order harmonics return.
Phase-locked loop (PLL)
A loop that tracks the grid angle and frequency.
Pole voltage
The voltage of a leg midpoint measured from the bus midpoint.
Selective harmonic elimination
Choosing switching angles so that chosen harmonics are zero.
Shoot-through
Both switches of one leg on together, shorting the bus.
Six-step inverter
A three-phase VSI in 180° mode; its phase voltage has six steps per cycle.
Space vector
The single rotating vector that represents three balanced phase quantities.
Third-harmonic injection
Adding a common third harmonic to three-phase references to raise the linear limit to ma = 1.155.
Total harmonic distortion (THD)
rms of all harmonics divided by the rms fundamental.
Unipolar PWM
PWM in which the bridge output takes +Vdc, 0 and −Vdc.
Voltage source inverter (VSI)
An inverter with a stiff DC voltage held by a capacitor.
Zero vector
A state (000 or 111) that puts all terminals on one rail.
