2.1 Diode rectifiers: half-wave, full-wave and bridge

A diode rectifier turns AC into pulsating DC using only the one-way conduction of a diode, so its average output is fixed by the supply peak and the circuit shape. Half-wave, centre-tap and bridge circuits are the three standard shapes, found at the front of almost every mains-powered supply. This chapter derives their average and RMS output, ripple factor, peak inverse voltage (PIV) and transformer use, and shows what an inductive load does.

You need: the power diode and its static characteristic (a conducting silicon diode drops about 0.7 V; a reverse-biased one blocks up to its rated reverse voltage), from the power semiconductor devices topic.

Half-wave rectifier with R and RL load

One diode connects the AC source vs = Vm sin θ to the load, where Vm is the peak supply voltage in volts and θ = ωt is the angle in radians. In the positive half-cycle the diode conducts and the load sees vs. In the negative half-cycle the diode blocks and the load voltage is zero. The output is one half-sine pulse per supply cycle, so the averages are taken over the full period 2π.

Vdc = (1/2π) ∫₀^π Vm sin θ dθ = Vm/π = 0.318 Vm

Vdc is the average (DC) output voltage in V.

Vrms = √[(1/2π) ∫₀^π Vm² sin²θ dθ] = Vm/2 = 0.5 Vm

Vrms is the RMS output voltage in V.

FF = Vrms/Vdc = π/2 = 1.57;   RF = √(FF² − 1) = 1.21

FF is the form factor and RF the ripple factor, both plain numbers. The diode blocks the full negative peak, so PIV = Vm. The ripple repeats once per cycle, at the supply frequency f.

With an RL load the inductor keeps current flowing after θ = π, so the diode stays on and the load follows vs into its negative half until the current reaches zero at the extinction angle β (between π and 2π). The negative part lowers the average: Vdc = (Vm/2π)(1 − cos β). A freewheeling diode across the load takes over the current at π, clamps the load voltage at zero and restores Vdc = Vm/π.

The transformer utilisation factor TUF = Pdc / (secondary VA rating) is only 0.287 here: the secondary works half the time and carries a DC component that pushes the core towards saturation.

Centre-tap full-wave rectifier

A centre-tapped transformer has two equal halves, each giving Vm peak measured from the centre tap. Diode D1 conducts on the positive half-cycle and D2 on the negative one, and both push current through the load in the same direction. The output has two pulses per supply cycle, so the average is taken over π.

Vdc = (1/π) ∫₀^π Vm sin θ dθ = 2Vm/π = 0.637 Vm

Vm is the peak of each half of the secondary in V.

Vrms = Vm/√2 = 0.707 Vm;   FF = 1.11;   RF = 0.482

The ripple is at 2f, so 100 Hz on a 50 Hz supply.

When D1 conducts, the load's positive end sits at +Vm above the centre tap while the anode of D2 is at the far end of the secondary, −Vm. So D2 blocks 2Vm: the PIV is the voltage across the whole secondary. Some syllabi call this a two-pulse (p = 2) rectifier, p being the output pulses per supply cycle. Its TUF is 0.57 for the secondary and 0.81 for the primary, about 0.69 on average.

Bridge rectifier and comparison

Four diodes in a bridge give full-wave output without a centre tap. The AC supply connects to the two nodes where D1 meets D4 and D3 meets D2; the load connects across the other two nodes, which become DC+ and DC−. D1 and D2 conduct together in one half-cycle, D3 and D4 in the other, and the load current keeps the same direction.

Vdc = 2Vm/π;   Vrms = Vm/√2;   RF = 0.482;   PIV = Vm;   TUF ≈ 0.81

Vm is the peak supply voltage in V.

Each off diode is connected straight across the supply through a conducting diode, so it blocks the full Vm on its own; the two off diodes do not share the voltage. The price is two diode drops in the current path, about 1.4 V for silicon, which matters only at low output voltages. Replacing the diodes by thyristors gives phase control: see single-phase controlled rectifiers.

Worked example

Question. A single-phase bridge rectifier feeds a 100 Ω resistive load from a 230 V rms, 50 Hz supply. Take the diodes as ideal. Find Vm, Vdc, Idc, Vrms, Irms, the form factor, the ripple factor, the PIV of each diode and the output ripple frequency.

  1. Vm = √2 × 230 = 325.27 V.
  2. Vdc = 2Vm/π = 2 × 325.27 / 3.1416 = 207.07 V.
  3. Idc = Vdc/R = 207.07 / 100 = 2.07 A.
  4. Vrms = Vm/√2 = 325.27 / 1.4142 = 230.0 V. Irms = 230.0 / 100 = 2.30 A.
  5. FF = Vrms/Vdc = 230.0 / 207.07 = 1.111. RF = √(1.111² − 1) = √0.2337 = 0.483 (0.482 when FF is rounded to 1.11 first).
  6. PIV = Vm = 325.27 V.
  7. Ripple frequency = 2f = 2 × 50 = 100 Hz.

Answer. Vm = 325.27 V, Vdc = 207.07 V, Idc = 2.07 A, Vrms = 230.0 V, Irms = 2.30 A, FF = 1.111, RF = 0.483, PIV = 325.27 V and 100 Hz ripple.

Worked example 2

Question. A half-wave diode rectifier on 230 V, 50 Hz feeds R = 10 Ω in series with L = 31.8 mH (ωL = 10 Ω). The current dies out at β = 225.8°. Find Vdc without and with a freewheeling diode.

  1. Vm/2π = 325.27 / 6.2832 = 51.77 V.
  2. Without a freewheeling diode the output follows vs from 0 to β: Vdc = (Vm/2π)(1 − cos β) = 51.77 × (1 − cos 225.8°) = 51.77 × (1 + 0.6971) = 87.86 V.
  3. With a freewheeling diode the output is clamped at zero after π, leaving the plain half-sine: Vdc = Vm/π = 103.54 V, the same as for a pure 10 Ω load.
  4. Average load current with the freewheeling diode: Idc = Vdc/R = 103.54 / 10 = 10.35 A (the inductor has no average voltage, so only R sets Idc).

Answer. 87.86 V without the freewheeling diode, 103.54 V with it.

Exam traps

  • Averaging the half-wave output over π instead of 2π. That gives 2Vm/π, the full-wave value; the half-wave average is Vm/π = 0.318 Vm.
  • Quoting the full-wave ripple factor 0.482 for a half-wave circuit, whose ripple factor is 1.21.
  • Giving the centre-tap PIV as Vm. It is 2Vm, the voltage across the whole secondary; the bridge diode sees Vm.
  • Using 230 V as Vm. For a 230 V rms supply, Vm = 230√2 = 325.3 V.
  • Calling TUF an efficiency. It compares Pdc with the transformer's VA rating.

2.2 Capacitor and LC filters for rectifiers

A rectifier alone gives pulsating DC. A capacitor across the load stores charge near each peak and feeds the load in the gaps; a series inductor slows every change of current. Only a few syllabi list this topic, but almost every mains-powered supply starts with one of these filters.

You need: the full-wave bridge and its ripple at 2f from diode rectifiers.

Shunt capacitor filter

Connect a capacitor C across the load. While the rectified voltage climbs to Vm the diodes conduct and charge C to Vm. Once the rectified voltage falls below the capacitor voltage, every diode is reverse biased and C alone feeds the load. The output becomes a small sawtooth just below Vm.

Vr = Idc/(2fC) (full wave);   Vr = Idc/(fC) (half wave)

Vr is the peak-to-peak ripple in V, Idc the load current in A, f the supply frequency in Hz, C in F; it assumes C discharges almost linearly for the whole gap.

Vdc ≈ Vm − Vr/2;   RF = Vr/(2√3 Vdc) = 1/(4√3 f C R) (full wave)

R is the load resistance in Ω. The sawtooth's RMS value is Vr/(2√3).

Ripple grows with load current, so a capacitor filter suits light loads. The diodes conduct only for a few tens of degrees, so the charge arrives in tall narrow pulses that distort the supply current (see PFC rectifiers). The PIV is unchanged in full-wave circuits but rises to 2Vm in a half-wave circuit: the diode blocks the capacitor's +Vm plus the supply's −Vm.

Series inductor and L-C filters

An inductor L in series with the load presents a reactance 2πf_r L to the ripple, where f_r is the ripple frequency (2f for full wave), so doubling L halves the ripple current. It works best at heavy load, where the current never stops.

The L-C (choke-input) filter puts L in series and C across the load: L blocks the ripple current and C bypasses what is left. With a full-wave input the output stays at Vdc = 2Vm/π, not Vm, and

RF = √2 / (12 ω² L C)

ω = 2πf is the supply angular frequency in rad/s, L in H and C in F; valid when the choke current is continuous.

The choke current stays continuous only while L is above the critical inductance Lc = R/(3ω). For R = 100 Ω on 50 Hz, Lc = 0.106 H; with L = 0.2 H and C = 1000 µF, ω²LC = 19.74 and RF = 1.4142/(12 × 19.74) = 0.6 %. At light load Lc rises above L and the output climbs towards Vm; a bleeder resistor prevents this and discharges C after switch-off.

Worked example

Question. A bridge rectifier on 230 V, 50 Hz feeds a 2 A load through a 1000 µF capacitor. Estimate the ripple, Vdc, the ripple factor and the load resistance.

  1. Vm = 230 × √2 = 325.27 V.
  2. Vr = Idc/(2fC) = 2 / (2 × 50 × 1000 × 10⁻⁶) = 20.0 V peak to peak.
  3. Vdc ≈ 325.27 − 10.0 = 315.27 V, so R = 315.27/2 = 157.6 Ω.
  4. RF = Vr/(2√3 Vdc) = 20.0/(3.464 × 315.27) = 0.018, against 0.482 with no filter.
  5. Check: RC = 0.158 s, sixteen ripple periods. A step-by-step simulation gives 17.9 V because C recharges about 19° before each peak, so the formula errs on the safe side.

Answer. Vr ≈ 20 V, Vdc ≈ 315.3 V, RF ≈ 0.018, R ≈ 157.6 Ω.

Exam traps

  • Using 2f in the half-wave ripple formula. Half wave uses f; full wave uses 2f.
  • Saying the capacitor never changes the PIV. A half-wave circuit's PIV doubles to 2Vm.
  • Giving Vdc = 2Vm/π for a capacitor filter. That belongs to the choke-input filter; with C the output sits near Vm.
  • Treating a bigger capacitor as free improvement: it raises the peak diode current.

2.3 Voltage doubler and three-phase diode bridge rectifiers

Two more diode circuits complete the uncontrolled family. A voltage doubler uses two diodes and two capacitors to give about twice the supply peak with no step-up transformer, as in small high-voltage supplies and 115/230 V selectable supplies. A three-phase diode bridge takes all three phases and gives a stiff DC with only 4 % ripple; it is the front end of almost every industrial drive, welder and large battery charger.

You need: the capacitor filter and its ripple Idc/(fC) from capacitor and LC filters, and the bridge PIV argument from diode rectifiers.

Voltage doublers

The full-wave (bridge) doubler puts two equal capacitors C1 and C2 in series across the load, with their midpoint joined to one supply terminal. On the positive half-cycle D1 charges C1 to Vm; on the negative half-cycle D2 charges C2 to Vm. The load sees the two in series:

Vo(no load) = 2Vm;   ΔVpp ≈ Idc/(fC);   Vdc ≈ 2Vm − Idc/(fC)

Vm is the peak supply voltage in V, Idc the load current in A, f the supply frequency in Hz and C the value of each capacitor in F. Each capacitor is recharged once per cycle but the output is topped up twice, so the ripple is at 2f.

Why Idc/(fC)? Each capacitor carries the full load current for a whole cycle T = 1/f before it is recharged, so each loses Idc·T/C. The output dips by that amount in the half-cycle when neither is charging, and on average sits one full ΔV below 2Vm. Each diode blocks 2Vm; each capacitor needs a Vm rating.

The half-wave doubler (a clamp followed by a peak rectifier) puts C1 in series with the supply. On the negative half-cycle D1 charges C1 to Vm; on the positive half-cycle the supply and C1 add, so D2 charges C2 to 2Vm. One output terminal is common with the supply, but C2 is topped up only once per cycle, so the ripple is at f and the output falls faster with load. C1 needs a Vm rating, C2 and both diodes 2Vm. Repeated stages make a voltage multiplier for kilovolt supplies at microamps.

Three-phase diode bridge

Six diodes, two per phase: D1, D3, D5 have their cathodes joined to DC+, and D4, D6, D2 their anodes joined to DC−. At every instant the upper diode on the most positive phase and the lower diode on the most negative phase conduct, so the load sees the largest line-to-line voltage. The pair changes every 60° in the order D1D2, D2D3, D3D4, D4D5, D5D6, D6D1, and each diode conducts for 120°.

Vdc = (3/π) VLm = (3√2/π) VL = 1.35 VL

VL is the line-to-line RMS voltage and VLm = √2 VL its peak, in V. The average is taken over one 60° cap of a line voltage: (3/π)∫ VLm sin θ dθ from 60° to 120°.

The output swings between VLm (the top of each cap) and VLm cos 30° = 0.866 VLm, six times per cycle. Its RMS is 0.9558 VLm, so FF = 1.0009 and RF = 0.042, that is 4.2 %, against 48 % for a single-phase bridge. The lowest ripple frequency is 6f = 300 Hz on a 50 Hz supply. Each diode blocks the line peak, PIV = VLm, and the TUF is 0.955.

With a large DC inductor the load current is a flat Id. Each line then carries a quasi-square current: +Id for 120°, zero for 60°, −Id for 120°, zero for 60°. Its RMS is √(2/3) Id = 0.816 Id and its fundamental RMS is (√6/π) Id = 0.780 Id, so the distortion factor is 3/π = 0.955 and the harmonics present are 5, 7, 11, 13 and so on (more in performance).

The three-phase half-wave (three-pulse) diode circuit has one diode per phase and returns the load to the neutral: Vdc = (3√3/2π) Vm = 1.17 Vph (Vm the phase peak, Vph the phase RMS), with ripple at 3f and DC in the transformer windings.

Worked example

Question. A full-wave voltage doubler runs from a 12 V rms, 50 Hz transformer secondary with two 1000 µF capacitors and supplies 50 mA. Take ideal diodes. Find the no-load output, the ripple and the loaded average output, and the diode PIV.

  1. Vm = √2 × 12 = 16.97 V, so the no-load output is 2Vm = 33.94 V.
  2. Each capacitor loses Idc/(fC) = 0.05 / (50 × 1000 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.00 V per cycle, so the output ripple is about 1.0 V peak to peak, at 2f = 100 Hz.
  3. Vdc ≈ 2Vm − Idc/(fC) = 33.94 − 1.00 = 32.94 V.
  4. A step-by-step simulation of the circuit gives 33.0 V average and 0.9 V ripple: the estimate is good.
  5. PIV of each diode = 2Vm = 33.94 V; each capacitor sees Vm = 16.97 V.

Answer. 33.94 V at no load; with 50 mA about 32.9 V with 1.0 V of 100 Hz ripple; PIV 33.9 V.

Question. A three-phase diode bridge is fed from a 415 V, 50 Hz line and supplies R = 20 Ω through a large smoothing inductor. Find Vdc, Id, the diode average and RMS currents, the PIV, the line RMS current and the input power factor.

Exam traps

  • Putting the half-wave doubler's ripple at 2f. Its output capacitor is topped up once per cycle, so its ripple is at f; only the full-wave doubler gives 2f.
  • Using the phase voltage in Vdc = 1.35 VL. The 1.35 form needs line RMS; with phase RMS it is 2.34 Vph.
  • Saying each diode of a three-phase bridge conducts for 60°. Each conducts 120°; the conducting pair changes every 60°.
  • Taking the three-phase bridge PIV as the phase peak. Each diode blocks the line-to-line peak, √2 VL.

2.4 Single-phase half and fully controlled rectifiers

Replace the diodes of a rectifier with thyristors and every turn-on can be delayed by a firing angle α. The average output then falls as α grows, and with a load that keeps current flowing it can even turn negative. Battery chargers, DC motor drives and electrolysis supplies work this way. This chapter covers the one-pulse (half-wave), half-controlled and fully controlled two-pulse circuits with R, RL and RLE loads, and when the current is continuous.

You need: thyristor turn-on by a gate pulse and turn-off at current zero, from the power semiconductor devices topic, and the diode circuits of diode rectifiers.

Firing angle and the half-wave controlled rectifier

A thyristor waits for a gate pulse. The firing angle α is the delay, in electrical degrees, from the instant a diode in the same place would start conducting (the natural firing point) to the gate pulse. In single-phase circuits fed by vs = Vm sin ωt that point is the supply zero crossing: ωt = 0 for one device and π for the other.

Half-wave (one-pulse) circuit, R load. One thyristor conducts from α to π and turns off when its current falls to zero at π:

Vdc = (Vm/2π)(1 + cos α);   Vrms = (Vm/2)√[(π − α + ½ sin 2α)/π]

Vdc is the average and Vrms the RMS load voltage in V; Vm is the peak supply voltage in V; α is the firing angle.

RL load. The inductor carries current past π to the extinction angle β while the supply is negative, so Vdc = (Vm/2π)(cos α − cos β), less than the R-load value. The larger ωL/R, the later β and the nearer Vdc comes to zero; a freewheeling diode restores (Vm/2π)(1 + cos α).

RLE load (battery charging). With a back EMF E the thyristor is forward biased only while vs > E, that is between θ1 = sin⁻¹(E/Vm) and π − θ1. A gate pulse earlier than θ1 does nothing, so the firing range shrinks to θ1 < α < π − θ1.

Fully controlled bridge: two pulses, two quadrants

Four thyristors in a bridge: T1 and T2 form one diagonal and are fired at α; T3 and T4 form the other and are fired at π + α. The output has two pulses per cycle, so its ripple is at 2f. Each thyristor blocks Vm in both directions.

Vdc = (2Vm/π) cos α   (continuous current);   Vdc = (Vm/π)(1 + cos α)   (R load)

On a 230 V supply 2Vm/π = 207.07 V, the most the bridge can give.

With an R load the current stops at π, so the output is zero from 0 to α in each half-cycle. With a large inductance the current is continuous: T1 and T2 carry it past π until T3 and T4 are fired at π + α, so the output follows vs into its negative part. With RLE the averages obey Vdc = E + Idc R.

The current is continuous when the load inductance can carry it from one firing to the next. A large ωL/R helps; a large back EMF works against it, because E opposes the driving voltage and the current dies out sooner. When the current is discontinuous the output stops following the negative part of vs and rests at E (at zero for an RL load), so the actual Vdc is higher than (2Vm/π) cos α: the continuous-current formula under-predicts it.

Above α = 90° the formula gives a negative Vdc while the current keeps its direction, so power flows from the DC side to the AC supply. That needs a DC source in the load (a machine running as a generator, a reversed battery); an RL load alone cannot do it, since its average voltage is Idc R > 0. See inversion mode.

Half-controlled bridge and the freewheeling diode

The half-controlled (semi) bridge replaces two thyristors with diodes. When vs reverses, the load current freewheels through a thyristor and a diode in the same leg, so the output is clamped at zero. For R and RL loads Vdc = (Vm/π)(1 + cos α), never negative: one quadrant only. It needs two gate drives instead of four, and draws supply current only from α to π in each half-cycle, which raises the input displacement factor from cos α to cos(α/2).

A freewheeling diode across the load of a fully controlled bridge does the same job: the output is clamped at zero, Vdc = (Vm/π)(1 + cos α), and inversion is lost.

2.5 Three-phase controlled rectifiers

A three-phase controlled rectifier replaces the diodes of a three-phase rectifier with thyristors and delays every gate pulse by the firing angle α. With three supplies 120° apart the output has three or six pulses per cycle instead of two, so the ripple is smaller and higher in frequency and the load is shared by all three lines. These converters, the three-pulse and six-pulse circuits, feed large DC motor drives, electrolysers, battery charging stations and the terminals of HVDC links.

You need: the three-phase diode bridge and its conduction pairs from three-phase diode bridge rectifiers, and the firing angle and continuous current from single-phase controlled rectifiers.

Pulse number and the firing reference

Let the phase voltages be va = Vm sin θ, vb = Vm sin(θ − 120°) and vc = Vm sin(θ − 240°), where Vm is the phase peak in volts, Vph = Vm/√2 the phase RMS and VL = √3 Vph the line RMS. The pulse number p is the number of output pulses per supply cycle; the lowest ripple frequency is p f. The single-phase bridge has p = 2, the three-phase half-wave circuit p = 3 and the three-phase bridge p = 6.

The natural firing point is no longer the zero crossing. A thyristor on phase a can conduct only once va is the most positive phase, which with these definitions first happens at θ = 30°, where va crosses vc. So α is measured from θ = 30° for T1: at α = 0 the converter behaves exactly like the diode rectifier. Measuring α from the zero crossing of va is the classic error.

Three-pulse (half-wave) circuit

Three thyristors, one per phase, with their cathodes joined; the load returns to the star point of the transformer. Each thyristor conducts for 120° when the current is continuous, and is turned off when the next phase's thyristor is fired, because that phase is then more positive.

Vdc = (3√3 Vm / 2π) cos α = 1.17 Vph cos α

valid for continuous current. With an R load the current is continuous only up to α = 30°; above it Vdc = (3Vm/2π)[1 + cos(α + 30°)], zero at α = 150°.

The neutral carries the DC load current, so the transformer core sees a DC flux; a zig-zag secondary avoids it. The circuit is cheap but is mainly a teaching step towards the bridge.

Six-pulse fully controlled bridge

Six thyristors: T1, T3, T5 on phases a, b, c form the upper (common-cathode) group, T4, T6, T2 the lower (common-anode) group. The numbering follows the firing order: a new thyristor is fired every 60°, in the order T1, T2, T3, T4, T5, T6, and the conducting pairs are T6T1, T1T2, T2T3, T3T4, T4T5, T5T6. Each thyristor conducts for 120°, and every pair puts one line voltage across the load, so a thyristor needs double gate pulses (or one 120° pulse) to start the bridge.

Vdc = (3√3 Vm / π) cos α = (3√2 VL / π) cos α = 1.35 VL cos α = 2.34 Vph cos α

valid for continuous current; Vm is the phase peak, VL the line RMS and Vph the phase RMS, in V. The 1.35 already contains √2 and √3. The output repeats every 60°, so the average is (3/π) ∫ √3 Vm sin(θ + 30°) dθ over one 60° window starting at 30° + α.

Vdc is 1.35 VL at α = 0, zero at 90° and negative beyond, which needs a DC source to keep the current going, exactly as in the single-phase bridge. With an R load the current is continuous up to α = 60°. Above that a line voltage reaches zero before the next thyristor is fired, the output rests at zero, and Vdc = (3√2 VL/π)[1 + cos(α + 60°)], falling to zero at 120°. This is higher than the cosine formula, which under-predicts the output whenever the current is discontinuous.

The half-controlled three-phase bridge uses three thyristors and three diodes: Vdc = (3√2 VL/2π)(1 + cos α), never negative, with freewheeling built in. With continuous current each line carries 120° blocks of ±Id, the same quasi-square wave as the diode bridge but shifted by α, so the input displacement factor is cos α (see performance).

2.6 Performance: displacement factor, ripple and harmonics

A rectifier is judged from both sides. On the DC side the question is how much ripple rides on the average; on the AC side it is how much of the input current is useful in-phase fundamental, which sets the power factor and the harmonic pollution. This chapter defines the standard performance parameters, works them out for the usual converters, and lists the ways the power factor of a phase-controlled converter is improved.

You need: the output waveforms and supply currents of single-phase and three-phase controlled rectifiers, and Fourier series of a square wave.

Output side: form factor, ripple factor and TUF

Take an output voltage vo(t) of period T. Its average Vdc carries the useful DC power; its RMS Vrms sets the heating. The ripple is what is left after the average is removed, and the RMS values add in quadrature: Vrms² = Vdc² + Vac², where Vac is the RMS of the ripple.

FF = Vrms/Vdc;   RF = Vac/Vdc = √(FF² − 1);   CF = Vpeak/Vrms;   TUF = Pdc/(Vs Is)

FF is the form factor, RF the ripple factor, CF the crest factor with Vpeak the highest instantaneous value, and TUF the transformer utilisation factor with Vs, Is the RMS voltage and current of the transformer secondary. The rectification efficiency Pdc/Pac = (Vdc/Vrms)² for a resistive load.

Typical values with a resistive load and no firing delay: half-wave RF = 1.21, single-phase bridge RF = 0.482, three-phase bridge RF = 0.042. The ripple frequency is p f, where p is the pulse number.

Input side: displacement, distortion and power factor

The supply voltage is close to a sine, but the input current is not. Write its total RMS as Is and the RMS of its fundamental as I1. Only the fundamental carries average power with a sinusoidal voltage, so

PF = P/S = (Vs I1 cos φ1)/(Vs Is) = (I1/Is) × cos φ1 = g × DF

φ1 is the angle by which the fundamental current lags the voltage; DF = cos φ1 is the displacement factor; g = I1/Is is the distortion factor (some books call it the current distortion factor, a few use DF for it, so read the definition).

HF = THD = √(Is² − I1²)/I1 = √(1/g² − 1)

HF is the harmonic factor, the RMS of all the harmonics divided by the fundamental; most syllabi call it the total harmonic distortion.

Single-phase fully controlled bridge, continuous current. The supply current is a square wave of height Id, shifted by α. Its fundamental peak is 4Id/π, so I1 = (2√2/π) Id = 0.900 Id while Is = Id. Hence g = 0.900, THD = 48.3 % and DF = cos α, giving PF = 0.900 cos α. The harmonics are odd only, with In = I1/n: the 3rd is 33 %, the 5th 20 %, the 7th 14 %.

Three-phase bridge. Each line carries 120° blocks of ±Id: Is = 0.816 Id, I1 = 0.780 Id, so g = 3/π = 0.955, THD = 31.1 %, DF = cos α and PF = 0.955 cos α. A p-pulse converter has only the characteristic harmonics h = kp ± 1 (k = 1, 2, 3 …): 5, 7, 11, 13 … for p = 6. The DC output ripple sits at multiples of p f.

Improving (mitigating) the power factor

The displacement factor cos α falls as the output is turned down, and the converter draws reactive power Q1 = Vs I1 sin α even when it delivers little DC power. The usual remedies are:

  • Freewheeling diode or half-controlled bridge: the supply current flows only from α to π, so DF = cos(α/2) instead of cos α.
  • Extinction angle control: self-commutated switches turn on at the zero crossing and off before π, so the fundamental leads.
  • Symmetrical angle control: the switches conduct for a window centred on the voltage peak, so φ1 = 0 and DF = 1 at every output.
  • PWM control: many sine-weighted pulses per half-cycle push the harmonics up to frequencies a small filter removes.
  • Sequence control: two series bridges, one held at full output while the other is phase controlled, so only half of the voltage is ever delayed.
  • Shunt capacitors and harmonic filters on the AC side, or a PFC rectifier.

2.7 Effect of source inductance

A real supply has inductance: transformer leakage, cables, the alternator's own reactance. When a thyristor is fired, the current cannot jump at once from the outgoing device to the incoming one, so both conduct together for an overlap angle μ. During the overlap the output loses a slice of voltage, so the DC voltage falls as the load current rises, the supply waveform is notched, and the converter's inverter range shrinks. Every line-commutated converter has this effect, and examiners ask for its derivation almost every year.

You need: the continuous-current waveforms of single-phase and three-phase controlled rectifiers.

Why the current overlaps

Call the source inductance Ls, in henries. An inductor obeys v = Ls di/dt, so its current can change only at a finite rate. Take a single-phase bridge with a level load current Id (a large DC inductor), where Ls is the total inductance in the AC loop (leakage of the transformer referred to the secondary).

  1. Before ωt = α, T3 and T4 carry Id and the supply current is −Id.
  2. At α, T1 and T2 are fired. The supply current must swing from −Id to +Id, but Ls holds it back.
  3. For a while all four thyristors conduct. The bridge output is short-circuited, so vo = 0, and the whole supply voltage appears across Ls: vs = Ls dis/dt.
  4. When is reaches +Id, T3 and T4 turn off at ωt = α + μ and vo follows vs again.

Integrating vs = Ls dis/dt over the overlap gives Vm[cos α − cos(α + μ)] = 2ωLs Id. In a three-phase bridge only two phases are shorted during each commutation, so the output sits halfway between the two phase voltages instead of at zero, but the idea is the same. Some syllabi call ωLs the commutating reactance.

Overlap angle and voltage drop

single-phase bridge:   cos α − cos(α + μ) = 2ωLs Id / Vm;   ΔVd = 2ωLs Id / π

ω = 2πf in rad/s; Ls is the total AC-side inductance in H; Id is the DC current in A; Vm is the supply peak in V; ΔVd is the average DC voltage lost, in V.

three-phase bridge:   cos α − cos(α + μ) = 2ωLs Id / (√2 VL);   ΔVd = 3ωLs Id / π

Ls is the inductance per phase in H and VL the line RMS voltage in V. Six commutations per cycle instead of two give the factor 3 instead of 2.

Vdc = Vd0 cos α − ΔVd = (Vd0/2)[cos α + cos(α + μ)]

Vd0 is the no-load output at α = 0: 2Vm/π (single-phase) or 3√2 VL/π (three-phase).

ΔVd/Id = 2ωLs/π or 3ωLs/π behaves like a resistance, the equivalent commutating resistance Rc. It drops voltage but dissipates nothing: the converter acts as an ideal source Vd0 cos α in series with Rc. The overlap grows with Id and Ls, and for a given current it is smallest near α = 90°, where the commutating voltage is largest. A DC smoothing inductor does not cause any of this; it only keeps Id level.

Notches, regulation and the inverter limit

During each overlap the supply terminals are shorted through the thyristors, so the voltage seen by other loads has notches, p of them per cycle. They disturb electronic loads, and a nearby converter that fires on a notch can misfire; large drives are therefore fed from their own transformer or through line reactors. The output falls with load (poor regulation), and the top of the range is lost: even at α = 0 the converter gives Vd0 − ΔVd.

In inverter mode the overlap eats into the time the outgoing thyristor needs to recover, which sets the largest usable firing angle. That limit is worked out in inversion mode and the inverter angle limit.

  1. ω = 2π × 50 = 314.16 rad/s; ωLs = 314.16 × 0.001 = 0.3142 Ω.

2.8 Inversion mode and the inverter angle limit

A fully controlled converter fired beyond 90° gives a negative average voltage while its current keeps the same direction, so power flows from the DC side back into the AC supply: the converter inverts. This is how a DC drive brakes regeneratively, how an HVDC receiving station feeds power into the grid, and how a line-commutated converter returns the energy of a battery or a superconducting magnet. Inversion has a hard upper limit on α, set by the overlap and by the time a thyristor needs to recover; firing later than that causes commutation failure.

You need: continuous current and Vdc = Vd0 cos α from single-phase controlled rectifiers, and the overlap angle from effect of source inductance.

Conditions for inversion

A thyristor bridge cannot reverse its current, so the only way to reverse power P = Vdc Id is to reverse Vdc. Three things are needed:

  1. A fully controlled converter: a half-controlled bridge or a freewheeling diode clamps the output at zero and cannot invert.
  2. A DC source in the load whose polarity drives current into the converter's positive terminal: a DC machine running as a generator (its field or armature reversed), a battery connected the other way, or the DC line of an HVDC link.
  3. α between 90° and the limit α_max, with the current continuous, so that Vdc = Vd0 cos α is negative.

In steady state the averages obey Vdc = E + Id R, with E now negative and |E| > |Vdc|, so Id = (Vdc − E)/R > 0. The DC source supplies |E| Id, the resistance takes Id²R and the rest, |Vdc| Id, goes into the AC supply. In the V–I plane the converter has moved from quadrant I (rectifier) to quadrant IV (inverter).

Extinction angle and turn-off time

When the incoming thyristor is fired at α, the outgoing one must hand over its current and then block. After the overlap ends at α + μ, the outgoing thyristor sees a reverse voltage only until 180°, where the two commutating voltages cross; after that its anode is positive again. That reverse-biased interval is the extinction angle (margin angle):

γ = 180° − (α + μ)   and it must satisfy   γ ≥ γ0 > ω tq

γ is the extinction angle in degrees; tq is the thyristor's circuit turn-off time in s; ω = 2πf in rad/s. On 50 Hz, tq = 100 µs is ωtq = 1.8°; γ0 is chosen larger than ωtq to leave a safety margin.

If γ is too short, the outgoing thyristor has not recovered when its voltage turns positive, so it conducts again. Current returns to it, the incoming thyristor turns off, and the DC source drives current straight through two thyristors of the same leg: the DC side is short-circuited and the current climbs. This commutation failure (an inverter fault) is why the firing angle in inversion is never taken to 180°.

The inverter angle limit

Without overlap the limit is simply α_max = 180° − γ0. With source inductance the overlap also has to fit inside the half-cycle, and it grows with current. Put α + μ = 180° − γ0 into the overlap equation of the three-phase bridge, cos α − cos(α + μ) = 2ωLs Id/(√2 VL):

cos α_max = 2ωLs Id/(√2 VL) − cos γ0;   Vdc = (Vd0/2)(cos α − cos γ) = −(Vd0 cos γ − ΔVd)

Vd0 = 3√2 VL/π and ΔVd = 3ωLs Id/π. For a single-phase bridge use 2ωLs Id/Vm on the right of the first equation.

So the heavier the current, the larger μ and the smaller α_max, and the less inverse voltage the converter can offer. A controller that holds α fixed would fail as soon as the current rose; HVDC inverters and regenerative drives therefore control γ directly (constant extinction angle control), computing the firing instant from the measured current.

2.9 Dual converters

A dual converter is two fully controlled bridges connected in inverse parallel to the same DC load. Converter P can pass current in one direction and converter N in the other, and each can make its voltage positive or negative with its firing angle, so the load can have either polarity of voltage and current: all four quadrants. Reversing DC drives for rolling mills, mine winders, lifts and crane hoists use it to drive, brake and reverse without switching contactors.

You need: fully controlled bridges with continuous current and inversion mode.

Two bridges in inverse parallel

Converter P drives current +Id into the load; converter N, connected the other way round, drives −Id. With continuous current each gives an average voltage set by its own firing angle: for single-phase bridges V1 = (2Vm/π) cos α1 for P and, measured with the same load polarity, V2 = −(2Vm/π) cos α2 for N. For both to agree on the load voltage, V1 = V2, which needs

cos α1 = −cos α2 that is α1 + α2 = 180°

α1 and α2 are the firing angles of P and N in degrees. When one bridge rectifies (α < 90°) the other is ready to invert (α > 90°).

The four quadrants are then: P rectifying (+V, +I, forward motoring); N inverting (+V, −I, forward braking, power returned); N rectifying (−V, −I, reverse motoring); P inverting (−V, +I, reverse braking). Some syllabi call the circuit a four-quadrant converter.

Non-circulating and circulating-current modes

Equal average voltages are not equal instantaneous voltages: each bridge's output has a different ripple shape. If both bridges were gated with nothing between them, this ripple difference would drive a large current around the loop P–N, limited only by the source inductance.

  • Non-circulating mode: only one bridge receives gate pulses at a time. To reverse the current the controller removes the pulses of the conducting bridge, waits until its current has reached zero and its thyristors have recovered (a dead time of a few milliseconds), then releases the other bridge. No reactor is needed, but there is a short gap in torque.
  • Circulating-current mode: both bridges are gated all the time, with α1 + α2 = 180°, and an intergroup reactor between them limits the circulating current to a small fraction of the rated current. Each bridge carries its share of the circulating current plus the load current, so the current can change direction smoothly and quickly; the price is the reactor, larger thyristor ratings and extra losses.

In both modes the firing logic must never let both bridges conduct the load current with no reactor in the loop, which would short-circuit the supply through two thyristors.

Where dual converters are used

A reversing rolling mill drives the metal one way, brakes, and drives it back, many times a minute. A crane hoist needs torque in the lifting direction both to lift a load and to lower a heavy one slowly (lowering it regeneratively, quadrant IV), and reverse torque only to drive a light hook down. Mine winders and lifts brake regeneratively on every trip. A single bridge with a reversing contactor is cheaper and is used where reversals are rare. Three-phase dual converters are the usual choice above a few kilowatts, and the same back-to-back bridges also make the two halves of a cycloconverter, met in the AC-AC converters topic.

2.10 Power factor correction (PFC) rectifiers

A diode bridge with a large capacitor draws its current in short spikes near the mains peak, so its power factor is poor even though those spikes sit in phase with the voltage. A power factor correction (PFC) rectifier adds a fast switching stage, usually a boost converter, that forces the input current to follow the sine of the mains voltage, so the supply sees a plain resistor. Harmonic limits make PFC compulsory in computer and TV supplies above about 75 W, in lighting above about 25 W, and in every EV charger and server supply.

You need: the capacitor filter from capacitor and LC filters, power factor and THD from performance, and the boost converter from the DC-DC converters topic.

Why a capacitor-input rectifier has a poor power factor

The capacitor charges to the mains peak, and the diodes conduct only while the mains voltage is above the capacitor voltage: in the filter example of this unit, for about 19° before each peak. The whole charge for the load arrives in that window, so the current is a tall narrow pulse. Its fundamental is almost in phase with the voltage (displacement factor close to 1), but its RMS value is large for the power delivered: the distortion factor g = I1/Is is low, the THD is high (often above 100 %), and the power factor is typically only 0.5 to 0.7.

A capacitor never corrects this; it causes it. Passive PFC puts a 50 Hz inductor in series, which widens the pulses and lowers the THD, but the inductor is large and heavy and cannot meet tight limits at higher power.

Active boost PFC: the current follows the voltage

The diode bridge (with only a small filter capacitor) gives a rectified sine |vs|. A boost cell follows it: inductor L, switch S (a MOSFET) and diode D feeding a bus capacitor at Vo, typically 400 V. S switches at tens of kilohertz, so within one switching period the input looks like a slowly changing DC voltage.

The control has two loops. An outer, slow voltage loop compares the bus with its 400 V reference and sets a gain k. A multiplier forms the current reference iref = k |vs|, a scaled copy of the rectified mains. An inner, fast current loop switches S so that the average inductor current follows iref. The input current is then in phase with and proportional to the voltage, so the mains sees an emulated resistance

Re = Vs²/P;   D(t) = 1 − |vs(t)|/Vo;   ΔiL = |vs| D/(L fs)

Re is the emulated input resistance in Ω, Vs the RMS mains voltage in V and P the input power in W; D is the switch duty ratio; ΔiL the peak-to-peak inductor ripple in A, L in H and fs the switching frequency in Hz.

A boost converter can only raise voltage, so Vo must stay above the mains peak (325 V for 230 V mains); near each zero crossing D approaches 1. The voltage loop must be slow, about 10 to 20 Hz, because single-phase power pulses at 100 Hz and the bus carries a 100 Hz ripple ΔVo = P/(2πf C Vo); a fast loop would copy that ripple into iref and distort the current. In continuous conduction mode a well-designed stage reaches PF above 0.99 with THD of a few per cent.

Practical versions and limits

Interleaved PFC runs two boost cells 180° apart, so their ripple currents partly cancel and each inductor is smaller. Bridgeless and totem-pole PFC remove the diode bridge, saving a diode drop in the current path; totem-pole stages use GaN or SiC switches and are the usual choice in high-efficiency server supplies. For three-phase loads the same idea gives the Vienna rectifier and the PWM active front end.

The PFC stage shapes the current and regulates the bus; a second DC-DC stage normally provides isolation and the low output voltage. Power factor is not efficiency: a supply can have PF 0.99 and still waste heat. IEC 61000-3-2 limits the harmonic currents of equipment up to 16 A per phase; IEEE 519 sets limits where a plant connects to the grid.

Near the top of each half-cycle the rectified mains exceeds the bus, the bridge and boost diode conduct straight into the capacitor, and the switch loses control of the current: an uncontrolled charging spike appears and the current no longer follows the sine.

Takeaways

  1. A diode rectifier's average output is fixed by the supply peak and the circuit: Vm/π, 2Vm/π or 1.35 VL.
  2. A capacitor filter lifts the output towards Vm with ripple Idc/(2fC), at the cost of narrow, peaky diode currents.
  3. Doublers give 2Vm without a transformer; the three-phase bridge gives a stiff DC with 4 % ripple at 6f.
  4. Phase control sets Vdc = Vd0 cos α for continuous current; discontinuous current always gives more than this.
  5. In three-phase circuits α is counted from the crossing of two phase voltages, 30° after the phase zero crossing.
  6. Power factor is distortion factor times displacement factor; cos α alone is never the whole answer.
  7. Source inductance makes the devices overlap: the output drops by 2ωLs Id/π or 3ωLs Id/π, and μ grows with current.
  8. Inversion needs α above 90°, a DC source and continuous current; α + μ must stay below 180° − γ0.
  9. A dual converter gives all four quadrants, with a dead time or an intergroup reactor to stop a short circuit between the bridges.
  10. A PFC rectifier makes the input current follow the voltage, so the mains sees a resistor and the power factor is near 1.

Understand Explain the single-phase dual converter. Derive the firing-angle condition, describe the circulating-current and non-circulating modes, and show the four quadrants of operation. 13 marks

Points to cover. Two fully controlled bridges in inverse parallel; V1 = (2Vm/π) cos α1, V2 = −(2Vm/π) cos α2; α1 + α2 = 180°; Non-circulating: one bridge gated, dead time; Circulating: intergroup reactor, smooth reversal; Quadrants I to IV.

Model answer.

  1. Circuit. Converter P and converter N, each a single-phase fully controlled bridge, are connected to the same DC load in inverse parallel: P can push current +Id into the load, N can push −Id. Each needs continuous current for its cosine law.
  2. Firing condition. Measured with the same load polarity:
    V1 = (2Vm/π) cos α1          (converter P)
V2 = −(2Vm/π) cos α2         (converter N, connected the other way)
V1 = V2  →  cos α1 = −cos α2  →  α1 + α2 = 180°
  3. Example. On 230 V with α1 = 30°: V1 = 207.07 × 0.866 = 179.33 V, so α2 = 150° and N's own output is 207.07 × cos 150° = −179.33 V, which is +179.33 V at the load.
  4. Non-circulating mode. Only one bridge receives gate pulses. To reverse, the controller removes P's pulses, waits until the load current is zero and P's thyristors have recovered (a dead time of a few milliseconds), then fires N. No reactor is needed; torque is lost during the dead time.
  5. Circulating-current mode. Both bridges are gated with α1 + α2 = 180°. Their averages are equal but their instantaneous voltages differ (different ripple shapes), and this difference drives a current round the P–N loop. An intergroup reactor, split as Lr/2 in each path, limits it. Current can then reverse smoothly with no dead time; the cost is the reactor, extra losses and larger thyristor ratings.

Model answer.

Glossary

Average (DC) voltage
Mean of the output voltage over one period.
Capacitor-input filter
Shunt capacitor that holds the output near the peak voltage.
Commutating resistance
ΔVd/Id: models the overlap drop; dissipates no power.
Commutation failure
Outgoing thyristor conducts again in inversion, shorting the DC source through one leg.
Continuous conduction
Load current never falls to zero between firings.
Critical inductance
Least choke inductance, R/(3ω) for full wave, that keeps an L-C filter's current continuous.
Displacement factor
Cosine of the angle between the fundamental input current and the voltage.
Distortion factor
Fundamental RMS current divided by total RMS current.
Dual converter
Two fully controlled bridges in inverse parallel for four-quadrant operation.
Emulated resistance
Vs²/P: the resistance a PFC rectifier presents to the mains.
Extinction angle (RL circuits)
Angle β at which the load current of a half-wave RL circuit falls to zero.
Firing angle
Delay from the natural firing point to the gate pulse.
Form factor
RMS value divided by average value.
Freewheeling diode
Diode across an inductive load that carries its current when the supply reverses, clamping the output at zero.
Fully controlled bridge
Bridge of thyristors only; can invert with a DC source in the load.
Half-controlled bridge
Bridge of thyristors and diodes; output never negative.
Harmonic factor
RMS of all harmonics divided by the fundamental; the THD.
Intergroup reactor
Inductor between the bridges of a dual converter that limits circulating current.
Margin (extinction) angle γ
Reverse-bias time of the outgoing thyristor in inversion, 180° − (α + μ).
Natural firing point
Instant at which a diode in the same position would start conducting.
Overlap angle
Angle during which the outgoing and incoming devices conduct together because of source inductance.
Peak inverse voltage
Largest reverse voltage across an off diode or thyristor.
Power factor correction
Shaping the input current into a sine in phase with the voltage.
Pulse number
Number of output pulses per supply cycle; the lowest ripple is at p f.
Rectifier
Circuit that converts AC to DC.
Ripple factor
RMS of the AC ripple divided by the average output.
Transformer utilisation factor
DC output power divided by the transformer's VA rating.
Voltage doubler
Two diodes and two capacitors that give about twice the supply peak.
