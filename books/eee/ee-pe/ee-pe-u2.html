2.1 Diode rectifiers: half-wave, full-wave and bridge

A diode rectifier turns AC into pulsating DC using only the one-way conduction of a diode. The average output is fixed by the supply peak and the circuit shape; no gate signal can change it. Half-wave, centre-tap full-wave and bridge circuits are the three standard shapes. They appear in low-cost chargers, soldering irons, DC motor field supplies and as the front end of many power supplies before a filter. This chapter derives their average and RMS output, ripple, peak inverse voltage and diode count.

You need: the diode V-I characteristic and static resistance from Unit 1, especially that a conducting diode drops about 0.7 V and a reverse-biased one blocks until its peak inverse voltage.

Half-wave rectifier with R and RL load

One diode connects the AC source to the load. During the positive half-cycle the diode conducts and the load sees v = Vm sin θ. During the negative half-cycle the diode blocks and the load voltage is zero. The output is a train of positive half-sine pulses, one per supply cycle.

Vdc = (1/π) ∫₀^π Vm sinθ dθ = Vm/π = 0.318 Vm

Vdc is the average output voltage in V; Vm is the peak supply voltage in V.

Vrms = √((1/π) ∫₀^π Vm² sin²θ dθ) = Vm/2 = 0.5 Vm

Vrms is the RMS output voltage in V; θ is the angle in radians.

RF = √((Vrms/Vdc)² − 1) = 1.21

RF is the ripple factor, dimensionless. PIV is the largest reverse voltage across the diode, PIV = Vm. Output ripple is at the supply frequency f: 50 Hz supply gives 50 Hz ripple.

With a purely resistive load the current copies the voltage and falls to zero at θ = π. With an RL load and no freewheeling diode, the inductor keeps the diode on after π; the output voltage follows the supply negative until the current decays. A freewheeling diode across the load gives the inductor current a path at θ = π, so the output voltage clips at zero and the load current stays positive. That clipped waveform has a higher average than the raw RL case. Transformer utilisation factor TUF = Pdc / transformer VA rating is poor, about 0.287, because the transformer carries DC magnetising current and is idle for half the cycle.

Centre-tap full-wave rectifier

A centre-tapped transformer and two diodes make a full-wave rectifier. The secondary has two equal halves, each giving Vm peak with respect to the centre tap. On the positive half-cycle diode D1 conducts; on the negative half-cycle D2 conducts. Each diode sends current through the load in the same direction, so the output has two pulses per supply cycle.

Vdc = (1/π) ∫₀^π Vm sinθ dθ = 2Vm/π = 0.637 Vm

Vdc is the average output voltage in V; Vm is the peak of each half-secondary in V.

Vrms = √((1/π) ∫₀^π Vm² sin²θ dθ) = Vm/√2 = 0.707 Vm

Vrms is the RMS output voltage in V.

FF = Vrms/Vdc = 1.11;   RF = √(FF² − 1) = 0.482

FF is the form factor; RF is the ripple factor. PIV of each diode is 2Vm: when D1 conducts, D2 is reverse-biased by the full secondary voltage, that is 2Vm measured from one end of the secondary to the other. Output ripple is at 2f.

Some syllabi call this a two-pulse rectifier and write p = 2, where p is the number of output pulses per supply cycle. TUF is about 0.693. The circuit needs a special centre-tapped transformer, and each diode must withstand twice the peak that a bridge diode sees for the same output.

Bridge rectifier and comparison

Four diodes in a bridge give full-wave output without a centre tap. D1 and D3 conduct on one half-cycle; D2 and D4 conduct on the other. The load current direction is the same in both halves. The AC supply connects to two opposite nodes of the bridge (the junctions of D1-D2 and D3-D4), and the load connects across the other two opposite nodes, which become the DC positive and DC negative terminals.

Vdc = 2Vm/π;   Vrms = Vm/√2;   PIV = Vm;   RF = 0.482;   TUF ≈ 0.812

Vm is the peak supply voltage in V; PIV is the reverse voltage per diode in V. PIV of each bridge diode is Vm. When D1 and D3 conduct, D2 and D4 are each reverse-biased directly across the supply (through a conducting diode), so each blocks the full Vm on its own. The bridge PIV is Vm because each diode sits across the supply, not because the diodes share voltage.

A few syllabi call this a full-wave bridge to separate it from the centre-tap type. The bridge is the common choice: no centre tap, smaller transformer, better TUF, but two diode drops in the conduction path, about 1.4 V for silicon. A half-wave circuit uses one diode and has RF 1.21; a full-wave circuit uses two or four diodes and has RF 0.482. Firing-angle control replaces these diodes with thyristors in 2.3.

Question. A single-phase bridge rectifier feeds a 100 Ω resistive load from a 230 V rms, 50 Hz supply. The diodes are ideal.   2. Vdc = 2Vm/π = 2 × 325.27 / 3.1416 = 207.07 V.

  4. Vrms = Vm/√2 = 325.27 / 1.414 = 230.0 V.
  5. Irms = Vrms / R = 230.0 / 100 = 2.30 A.

  8. Output ripple frequency = 2f = 2 × 50 = 100 Hz.

  • Quoting the full-wave ripple factor 0.482 for a half-wave circuit, whose ripple factor is 1.21.
  • Giving the centre-tap PIV as Vm instead of 2Vm.
  • Using 230 V as Vm. For a 230 V rms supply, Vm = 230√2 = 325.3 V.

2.2 Capacitor and LC filters for rectifiers

A rectifier gives pulsating DC: the voltage swings from zero up to the peak and back, at twice the supply frequency in a full-wave circuit. A capacitor across the load stores charge at the peak and returns it in the gaps. An inductor in series blocks fast current changes. Together they make a nearly steady DC. Phone chargers and inverter DC links use this.

You need 2.1 for the unsmoothed output waveform.

Shunt capacitor filter

Only a few universities teach this chapter; most stop at the raw rectified waveform.

Connect a capacitor C across the load. While the rectified voltage climbs to its peak Vm, the diodes conduct and charge C almost to Vm. When the rectified voltage falls below the capacitor voltage, every diode is reverse biased and C alone feeds the load, discharging through R with time constant RC. The output becomes a small sawtooth sitting near Vm.

Vr = Idc / (2fC)  (full wave)      Vr = Idc / (fC)  (half wave)

Vr is the peak-to-peak ripple in V, Idc the load DC current in A, f the supply frequency in Hz, C the capacitance in F. Assumes RC much greater than 1/f, small ripple and ideal diodes.

Vdc ≈ Vm − Vr / 2

Vm is the peak of the rectified waveform in V. The average sits midway between the peak and the trough.

Ripple grows with load current, so a capacitor filter suits light loads: holding the same ripple at 10 A needs ten times the capacitance of 1 A. The diode conducts only near the peak, so the same charge arrives in short tall pulses and the repetitive peak diode current can be several times Idc. In a full-wave circuit the peak inverse voltage is unchanged by the capacitor (Vm per diode in a bridge, 2Vm in a centre-tap circuit). In a half-wave circuit the capacitor raises it from Vm to 2Vm, because the diode must block the capacitor voltage plus the reverse supply peak.

Series L and LC filters

An inductor in series with the load opposes change of current. It stores energy when the rectified voltage is above the load voltage and gives it back in the dips. The ripple current falls roughly in proportion to 1/(2πf_r L), where f_r is the ripple frequency (2f for full wave, f for half wave) and L is in henries. Doubling the reactance — double L, or double f_r — halves the ripple current. A series inductor works better at heavy load, where the current stays continuous.

The L-C filter puts L in series and C across the load. L blocks the ripple current, C absorbs what is left. The output ripple falls to roughly 1/((2πf_r)²LC) of the ripple entering the filter; for example, LC = 0.01 s² at f_r = 100 Hz gives about 1/4 of the input ripple.

A bleeder resistor across C draws a small fixed current. It keeps the inductor working at light load and discharges C after switch-off.

Worked example

Question. A bridge rectifier works from a 230 V, 50 Hz supply and feeds a load drawing Idc = 2 A through a 1000 µF filter capacitor. Estimate the ripple voltage and the DC output voltage.

  1. Peak of the rectified waveform: Vm = 230 × √2 = 325.27 V.
  2. Ripple, full wave: Vr = Idc/(2fC) = 2 / (2 × 50 × 1000 × 10⁻⁶) = 20 V.
  3. The average sits half a ripple below the peak: Vdc ≈ Vm − Vr/2 = 325.27 − 10 = 315.27 V.
  4. Check: R = Vdc/Idc = 157.6 Ω, so RC = 0.158 s, about eight cycles of 20 ms. The linear-discharge approximation is fair.

Answer. Ripple Vr ≈ 20 V peak-to-peak and DC output Vdc ≈ 315.3 V.

Exam traps

  • Using 2f in the ripple formula for a half-wave rectifier. Half wave uses f, full wave uses 2f.
  • Expecting the capacitor to lower the peak inverse voltage. For full-wave circuits the PIV stays the same (Vm bridge, 2Vm centre-tap). For a half-wave circuit the capacitor doubles it, from Vm to 2Vm.
  • Keeping the unsmoothed average (0.637 Vm for full wave) as Vdc. With a capacitor, Vdc climbs towards Vm.
  • Treating a bigger capacitor as free improvement. It narrows the conduction angle and raises the repetitive peak diode current and supply current distortion.
  • Swapping the load preferences: the C filter suits light loads, the series L filter suits heavy loads.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. In a real DC link the capacitor is chosen by its ripple current rating, not only by capacitance, because the narrow pulses heat it. Electrolytic banks age, so a dried capacitor raising ripple until a downstream converter trips is a common field failure. Modern designs shrink the bank with active ripple compensation or a PFC front end (see 2.8).

2.3 Single-phase half and fully controlled rectifiers

A diode rectifier gives one fixed DC voltage. Replace the diodes with thyristors and you can delay every turn-on by a firing angle α. The average output then follows a cosine of α: full positive at α = 0, zero at 90°, and negative beyond 90° if the current keeps flowing. Battery chargers, DC motor drives and electrolysis supplies work this way: a gate pulse worth milliwatts controls kilowatts of DC. This chapter covers the single-phase half-wave, half-controlled and fully controlled circuits with R and RL loads, and the difference between continuous and discontinuous current.

You need: SCR turn-on and natural commutation (Unit 1, Power Semiconductor Devices), and the diode circuits of 2.1, which are this chapter at α = 0.

The firing angle sets the start of conduction

A diode conducts the instant the supply forward-biases it. A thyristor waits for a gate pulse at ωt = α. The firing angle α, in degrees, is measured from the natural firing point: the instant the same device would start conducting if it were a diode. Never measure it from the supply zero crossing. Natural commutation is the later instant when the device current reaches zero and it turns off; in the bridge circuits here the two instants coincide.

Half-wave controlled rectifier: one SCR fed by vs = Vm sin ωt, where Vm is the peak supply voltage in volts. The SCR conducts from α to π, then blocks, so:

Vdc = (Vm/2π)(1 + cos α)

Vdc is the average load voltage in volts; Vm is the peak supply voltage in volts; α is the firing angle in degrees.

At α = 0 this is Vm/π, the diode half-wave value. With a pure resistance the current is discontinuous at every α: it reaches zero at π and stays there until the next pulse.

Add inductance and the current cannot stop at π. Energy stored in L drives it on to ωt = β, the extinction angle, where the current reaches zero. Between π and β the supply is already negative while the current is still positive, so the output has a negative part and Vdc falls below the resistance value:

Vdc = (Vm/2π)(cos α − cos β)

β is the extinction angle in degrees, between π and π + α. As β grows over that range, Vdc falls from the resistance value (Vm/2π)(1 + cos α) to (Vm/π)cos α.

With enough inductance the current never stops; conduction is then continuous and Vdc = (Vm/π)cos α, which is negative for α > 90°. Half-wave circuits push DC through the transformer and appear only in small, cheap supplies.

With a back EMF E in the load, conduction can start only once vs exceeds E, so the SCR fires at ωt = sin⁻¹(E/Vm) + α.

Fully controlled bridge: four thyristors, two pulses

Four SCRs in a bridge. T1 and T2 form one diagonal pair and are fired at α; T3 and T4 at α + 180°. Each pair conducts for half a cycle, so the output has two pulses per supply cycle and its ripple sits at 2f, that is 100 Hz on a 50 Hz supply. Each device blocks Vm in reverse.

Vdc = (2Vm/π)cos α (continuous current)

2Vm/π is the highest average available, 0.6366 Vm; on a 230 V supply Vm = 325.3 V, so the ceiling is 207.1 V. The range runs from +207 V through zero at α = 90° down to −207 V.

With a pure resistance the current stops at π, so only the positive part of each pulse survives: Vdc = (Vm/π)(1 + cos α), always positive and zero only at α = 180°. Conduction is continuous when the load inductance carries current past the next firing instant; otherwise it is discontinuous, conduction ends at an extinction angle before the next firing instant, and the actual Vdc is higher than the continuous-current formula predicts (for example (Vm/π)(1 + cos α) against (2Vm/π)cos α).

Above α = 90° the cosine formula gives a negative Vdc. That is real only if current can keep flowing the same way: an RL load, or an RLE load whose back EMF is more negative than the average output (its magnitude is larger), so the load itself drives the current round in the same direction. Power then flows from the DC side into the AC supply. This is inverter mode, the two-quadrant operation of 2.7. A pure resistor cannot do it, because a thyristor conducts one way only.

Half-controlled bridge and the freewheeling diode

The half-controlled (semi) bridge replaces two SCRs with diodes. When the supply voltage tries to reverse across the load, the diodes conduct and hold the output at zero, so Vdc never goes negative. For both R and RL loads Vdc = (Vm/π)(1 + cos α). The gate drive is simpler and the input power factor at a given α is better, but inversion is impossible: one quadrant only.

A freewheeling diode across the load does the same job inside a fully controlled bridge. Connect its cathode to the positive rail. The moment the bridge output tries to go negative, the diode conducts and the load current freewheels through it; the load sees zero instead of a negative voltage, so Vdc = (Vm/π)(1 + cos α). The energy in L is not returned to the supply, which raises the displacement factor (see 2.5), but the supply current now flows in short blocks.

Exam traps

  • Using Vdc = (2Vm/π)cos α on a pure resistance load. That formula needs continuous current; with R the correct one is (Vm/π)(1 + cos α).
  • Measuring α from the supply zero crossing when the load has a back EMF E. The SCR can start only once vs exceeds E, so the reference has already moved by sin⁻¹(E/Vm) and α adds to it.
  • Reporting a negative Vdc for a half-controlled bridge. Its diodes clamp the output at zero; only the fully controlled bridge inverts.
  • Forgetting that inversion needs a current path. With a pure resistor the current cannot reverse and Vdc stays at or above zero, whatever the formula suggests.
  • Losing the factor of two between the half-wave value (Vm/2π)(1 + cos α) and the bridge value (Vm/π)(1 + cos α).

2.4 Three-phase controlled rectifiers

A three-phase controlled rectifier takes three AC supplies spaced 120° apart and replaces diodes with thyristors. The phases share the load, so the DC output has less ripple and the supply currents are more balanced than in a single-phase circuit. Delaying each gate pulse by the firing angle α sets the average DC voltage. The output ripple repeats p times per supply cycle, where p is the pulse number. Such converters feed DC motor drives, battery chargers, electrolysers and high-voltage DC terminals.

You need: Chapter 2.3 for firing-angle control, and three-phase phasors for line and phase values.

Pulse number and three-phase supply

Let the phase voltages be va = Vm sin θ, vb = Vm sin(θ − 120°) and vc = Vm sin(θ + 120°). Vm is the phase peak in volts. If Vph is the phase RMS voltage, Vm = √2 Vph. The line RMS voltage is VL = √3 Vph.

A converter with p output pulses per supply cycle has its lowest ripple at p f, where f is the supply frequency in Hz. A single-phase full-wave rectifier has p = 2. The three-pulse half-wave circuit has p = 3. The six-pulse bridge has p = 6. Higher p gives smaller ripple and a smaller filter for the same load.

p = pulses per supply cycle; f<sub>ripple</sub> = p f

p is 3 for the three-pulse circuit and 6 for the six-pulse bridge; f is the supply frequency in Hz; fripple is the lowest ripple frequency in Hz.

The three-pulse, or M3, circuit is a half-wave connection: one thyristor per phase, all cathodes joined, and the load returned to the neutral. Each device conducts for 120°. The six-pulse, or B6, bridge uses six thyristors, two per phase leg, and needs no neutral. Each device conducts for 120°, and a new conducting pair forms every 60°. That gives p = 6 and ripple at 6f.

Six-pulse bridge with continuous current

Assume the DC current is continuous: the load inductor is large enough that id never falls to zero. At any instant one upper and one lower thyristor conduct.

The firing angle α is measured from the natural commutation instant of each device: the angle where that device's anode voltage first becomes more positive than the cathode it works against, so the device becomes forward biased. With va = Vm sin θ, va equals vc at θ = 30°, so T1 is forward biased from θ = 30° onward; at α = 0 it conducts from 30° to 150°, with T6 already on. Firing T1 at θ = 30° + α starts conduction with T6. T2 joins 60° later, and so on. Each thyristor stays on for 120°. The bridge needs no neutral because the return path is through the conducting thyristor in the other leg, not through a neutral wire.

V<sub>dc</sub> = (3√3 V<sub>m</sub>/π) cos α = 1.35 V<sub>L</sub> cos α = 2.34 V<sub>ph</sub> cos α

Vdc is the average DC voltage in V for continuous current; Vm is phase peak in V; VL is line RMS in V; Vph is phase RMS in V; α is the firing angle in degrees, counted from the natural commutation instant of the device (θ = 30° for T1 with va = Vm sin θ), not from the zero crossing of va. The 1.35 already contains √3 and √2.

At α = 0°, Vdc ≈ 1.35 VL. At α = 90°, Vdc = 0. For α above 90°, Vdc is negative if current stays continuous and the load can supply energy; the bridge then works as an inverter. With a plain R load, current may become discontinuous at large α, and the formula overstates Vdc. A large RL load keeps current continuous. With an RLE load, current starts when the conducting pair's line-to-line supply voltage exceeds E (ignoring device drops); the averages then obey Vdc = E + IdR. See Chapter 2.6 for overlap.

Three-pulse circuit and comparison

The M3 circuit has three thyristors, one per phase, with cathodes common and the load between the common cathode and neutral. Each thyristor conducts for 120°. Current returns through the neutral, so the supply transformer secondary needs a neutral. Its average voltage for continuous current is Vdc = 1.17 Vph cos α, where Vph is phase RMS in V.

A transformer may be delta-star or star-star. It isolates the converter, sets VL, and for M3 provides the neutral. The B6 bridge is standard for industrial DC drives because its 6f ripple is easier to filter and its supply current has less low-order harmonic content than the M3 circuit. The M3 circuit suits low-cost supplies where a neutral is available. Harmonic and power-factor measures are in Chapter 2.5.

2.5 Performance: displacement factor, ripple and harmonics

A rectifier is judged by two ratios: how much ripple rides on its average DC, and how much of its AC input current is useful fundamental. The first decides filter size and load heating. The second decides power factor and harmonic pollution. A 50 Hz bridge with a capacitor can draw current in narrow spikes, so its power factor looks poor even when voltage and current cross zero together. This chapter defines the standard numbers — ripple factor, form factor, distortion factor, THD and displacement factor — and shows how they combine into power factor.

You need: 2.1 for the output waveforms, 2.3 and 2.4 for controlled shapes, and Fourier series basics.

Output quality: average, RMS and ripple

Take the output voltage v_o(t) repeating every T seconds. Its average is the DC value. Its RMS is the heating-equivalent value. The ripple is the AC part left after the average.

V_dc = (1/T)∫₀ᵀ v_o(t) dt,   V_rms = √[(1/T)∫₀ᵀ v_o²(t) dt]

v_o(t) is the output voltage in volts, T is one period in seconds, V_dc is the average output in volts, V_rms is the RMS output in volts.

Ripple factor compares the RMS of the AC ripple to the DC average. The RMS values add in quadrature: V_rms² = V_dc² + V_r,rms².

RF = V_r,rms / V_dc = √[(V_rms/V_dc)² − 1]

RF is the ripple factor (dimensionless), V_r,rms is the RMS of the AC ripple in volts, V_dc is the average output in volts, V_rms is the total RMS output in volts.

Form factor FF = V_rms/V_dc = √(1 + RF²). Crest factor CF = V_peak/V_rms. Transformer utilisation factor TUF = P_dc/(V_s,rms I_s,rms), where P_dc is DC power delivered, V_s,rms and I_s,rms are the transformer secondary RMS voltage and current.

Typical values for a resistive load: half-wave RF = 1.21, FF = 1.57; full-wave RF = 0.482, FF = 1.11; three-phase six-pulse RF ≈ 0.042, that is 4.2%. Ripple frequency is f_r = p f_s, where p is the pulse number and f_s is supply frequency. On a 50 Hz supply, a single-phase full bridge has p = 2 and f_r = 100 Hz; a three-phase six-pulse bridge has p = 6 and f_r = 300 Hz.

Input quality: displacement and distortion

The supply voltage is nearly sinusoidal, but the input current i_s(t) is not. Write its fundamental RMS as I₁ and its total RMS as I_s,rms. Only the fundamental carries real power with a sine voltage.

PF = P/S = (I₁/I_s,rms) cos φ₁ = DF × cos φ₁

P is real power in watts, S is apparent power in volt-amperes, I₁ is fundamental RMS current in amperes, I_s,rms is total RMS current in amperes, φ₁ is the phase angle between fundamental current and voltage, DF = I₁/I_s,rms is the distortion factor.

The displacement factor is cos φ₁. The distortion factor is DF = I₁/I_s,rms. Therefore PF = DF × displacement factor. A rectifier with perfect phase alignment can still have PF below 1 because of distortion.

THD = √[(1/DF)² − 1] = √[(I_s,rms/I₁)² − 1]

THD is total harmonic distortion (dimensionless, often given in %), DF is distortion factor, I₁ and I_s,rms are in amperes.

Some syllabi call THD the harmonic factor; a few write distortion index. Invert the formula when you know THD: DF = 1/√(1 + THD²).

For a single-phase fully controlled bridge in continuous conduction, i_s is a square wave of amplitude I_d delayed by α. Its fundamental peak is 4I_d/π, so I₁ = 2√2 I_d/π = 0.900 I_d. Total RMS is I_d. Thus DF = 2√2/π ≈ 0.900. The fundamental shifts by α, so displacement factor = cos α. PF = (2√2/π) cos α ≈ 0.900 cos α. At α = 0°, PF = 0.900; at α = 90°, PF = 0.

Harmonic orders and the 1/h rule

A p-pulse converter has p output pulses per supply cycle. Its characteristic input harmonics are h = kp ± 1, with k = 1, 2, 3, … . A single-phase bridge has p = 2, so h = 1, 3, 5, 7, … — odd only. A three-phase six-pulse bridge has p = 6, so h = 5, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, … .

A symmetric square current has harmonic amplitude I_h = I₁/h. The third harmonic is 1/3 = 33% of the fundamental, the fifth is 20%, the seventh is 14%, the ninth is 11%. The figure lists these values.

Summing the squares gives THD = √(1/3² + 1/5² + 1/7² + …) = √(π²/8 − 1) ≈ 0.48, about 48% for an ideal square wave. A real bridge with overlap has smaller high-frequency harmonics; see 2.6. Ripple on the DC side repeats p times per cycle, so its lowest harmonic is p f_s. The same p sets both the output ripple frequency and the input harmonic family.

Question. A single-phase fully controlled bridge supplies a steady load current of 10 A, so the AC input current is a symmetric square wave of peak 10 A. The firing delay is α = 0°. Find the fundamental peak, fundamental RMS, distortion factor, THD and power factor.

  • Ripple frequency is p f_s, not f_s. For a single-phase full bridge p = 2, so 100 Hz on a 50 Hz supply.

2.6 Effect of source inductance

A real supply has inductance: transformer leakage, cable, alternator reactance. So when a thyristor is fired, the current cannot jump from the outgoing device to the incoming one. Both conduct for a short overlap angle µ, and during that time two supply lines are shorted together through the thyristors. Every commutation therefore loses a small slice of DC voltage, so the output falls as load current rises. The effect appears in every rectifier, and it is what limits how hard a converter can be driven in inverter mode.

You need: firing-angle operation from 2.3 and 2.4, and the inductor law v = L di/dt.

Where the overlap comes from

Every real supply has inductance. Call it Ls: the source inductance per line, in henries (H). Most of it is transformer leakage reactance; the rest is alternator reactance and wiring. An inductor obeys v = L di/dt, so its current cannot change instantly — a finite voltage moves the current at a finite rate.

  1. Before commutation, one thyristor pair carries the full load current Idc.
  2. A new thyristor is fired.
  3. The outgoing thyristor does not stop at once, because its line current is held by Ls. Both devices conduct for a short time.
  4. Two supply lines are now joined through two thyristors: a two-phase short circuit, limited only by Ls and the device drops.
  5. Current in the incoming line rises from 0 to Idc while the outgoing current falls to 0; then the outgoing device turns off and the overlap ends.

That interval is the overlap, and its length in electrical degrees is the overlap angle µ. In a three-phase bridge the DC output during overlap sits halfway between the two commutating phase voltages, so a slice of voltage is missing from every commutation. In a single-phase bridge all four thyristors conduct and the DC terminals are shorted for the overlap, so the output is zero there.

Overlap angle and the voltage lost

The lost area is easy to compute, because the current swings over a known range in a time fixed by the supply voltage. The average loss turns out to be a constant times the current, so it behaves like a resistor.

ΔVd = 2ωLs Idc/π (single-phase bridge)   ΔVd = 3ωLs Idc/π (three-phase bridge)

ω = 2πf is the angular frequency in rad/s (f in Hz); Ls is the source inductance per line in H; Idc is the steady load current in A; ΔVd is the average voltage lost from the DC output, in V.

cos α − cos(α + µ) = 2ωLs Idc/(√2 V) = 2ΔVd/Vd0

α is the firing angle; µ is the overlap angle, in the same unit as α; V is the rms supply voltage, line-to-line for a three-phase bridge and plain rms for a single-phase one; Vd0 is the ideal no-load average voltage.

  • µ grows with Idc and with Ls: more current or more reactance takes longer to transfer.
  • For the same current, µ is smallest when α is near 90°, because the voltage between the two commutating phases is then at its peak.
  • The ratio ΔVd/Idc is the commutating resistance Rc, equal to 2ωLs/π or 3ωLs/π. Some syllabi call Ls the commutating reactance and quote the drop as a per-unit reactance, which is the same number scaled by a base impedance.

Because ΔVd is subtracted after the cosine term, the converter behaves like an ideal rectifier in series with Rc. A smoothing choke on the DC side does not create this drop; it only keeps Idc flat so the formulas stay valid.

Notches, reduced range and the inverter limit

Because the supply is shorted during overlap, the terminal voltage collapses briefly at every commutation. On an oscilloscope the AC bus shows flat-topped notches, p of them per supply cycle for a p-pulse converter. Notches travel along the bus, disturb other loads and can cause misfiring in a neighbouring converter. Large drives are therefore fed from a dedicated transformer or fitted with a line reactor. Supply current harmonics are the subject of 2.5.

The DC side now obeys Vd = Vd0 cos α − ΔVd. The drop is present even at α = 0, so the top of the control range is lost: the converter can no longer reach Vd0, and the firing range that gives useful output is shorter. The loss is proportional to current, so regulation is poor at heavy load.

In inverter mode the overlap becomes a hard limit. The outgoing thyristor must recover its blocking ability before the voltage across it turns positive again. Recovery takes the turn-off time tq, which corresponds to a margin angle δ = ω tq, usually a few degrees.

α + µ ≤ 180° − δ, with δ = ω tq

α is the firing angle; µ is the overlap angle; δ is the margin angle, in degrees; tq is the thyristor turn-off time in s; ω = 2πf in rad/s.

Rearranged, α_max = 180° − δ − µ. A converter running at a large current has a large µ and must use a smaller α. Push α higher and commutation failure follows: the current fails to transfer and the bridge shorts the supply.

Worked example

Question. A three-phase fully controlled bridge runs from a 400 V, 50 Hz line with a source inductance of 1 mH per phase. The load current is a steady 20 A. Find the commutating reactance ωLs, the average voltage drop ΔVd, the DC output at α = 0, and the overlap angle at that point. Take Vd0 ≈ 540 V.

  1. ω = 2πf = 2π × 50 = 314.16 rad/s.
  2. ωLs = 314.16 × 1 × 10⁻³ = 0.3142 Ω. This is the commutating reactance.
  3. Three-phase bridge: ΔVd = 3ωLs Idc/π = 3 × 0.3142 × 20/π = 18.85/π = 6.0 V.
  4. Check Vd0 = 3√2 × 400/π = 540 V to three figures. Then Vd = 540 − 6.0 = 534.0 V.
  5. cos α − cos(α + µ) = 2ΔVd/Vd0 = 12.0/540 = 0.0222. At α = 0 this gives cos µ = 1 − 0.0222 = 0.9778, so µ = 12.1°.

Answer. ΔVd = 6.0 V, so the output at α = 0 is 534.0 V instead of 540 V, with an overlap angle of 12.1°.

Exam traps

  • A smoothing choke in the load does not cause overlap. It flattens Idc and so makes the overlap steady; the overlap itself comes only from inductance on the AC side.
  • The drop is not zero at α = 0. ΔVd is subtracted after the cosine term, so even full firing loses 6.0 V in the worked example.
  • Factor slip: the single-phase bridge loses 2ωLs Idc/π, the three-phase bridge 3ωLs Idc/π. Using 3 in the single-phase case is a standard error.
  • In the overlap formula the voltage is rms, and for a three-phase bridge it is the line voltage. Substituting a peak value or a phase voltage gives a wrong µ.
  • Writing the inverter condition as α + µ ≤ 180° ignores the turn-off time. The true limit is α + µ ≤ 180° − δ, and it bites earlier.

2.7 Dual converters

A dual converter is two fully controlled rectifier bridges connected back to back to the same load. One bridge, converter P, can pass current in the forward direction; the other, converter N, can pass current in the reverse direction. By setting the firing angles correctly, the load can get positive or negative voltage and current, so the drive works in all four quadrants. This is used where a DC motor must reverse quickly, such as a rolling mill or a crane. The two bridges must either take turns or share through a reactor that limits circulating current.

You need: Chapter 2.3 for the fully controlled bridge and Unit 1 for commutation.

Two bridges in opposition

A dual converter is built from two fully controlled rectifier bridges joined at their DC terminals. Converter P is the positive-current bridge. Converter N is the negative-current bridge. Each bridge can set its own average DC voltage by its firing delay. For a single-phase bridge with continuous current, the average is V1 = (2Vm/π) cos α1. Vm is the peak supply voltage in volts; α1 is the firing delay of converter P in degrees; V1 is the average DC voltage of converter P in volts. Converter N uses α2 = 180° − α1, so V2 = (2Vm/π) cos α2 = −V1. This is the ideal balance. It makes the two open-circuit averages equal in size and opposite in sign, so no steady DC voltage is forced across the reactor that links them. The load can receive positive or negative voltage, and current can flow from P or from N. That gives four-quadrant operation: voltage and current can each be positive or negative.

Non-circulating and circulating modes

Non-circulating mode lets only one bridge conduct at a time. While converter P supplies the load, converter N gets no gate pulses. At reversal, P is blocked, both bridges wait for a short dead time, then N is released with α2 = 180° − α1. The dead time lets the thyristors of P turn off and recover before N fires. The cost is a short interval with no torque.

Circulating-current mode keeps both bridges enabled. Their average voltages are set to match, but their instantaneous voltages are not equal because each bridge has ripple. That ripple drives a current around the loop formed by P and N. An intergroup reactor, an inductor placed between the two bridges, limits this circulating current. The reactor carries the ripple current without saturating and keeps the loop current within the thyristor rating. The load current is the difference between the two bridge currents.

A real dual converter must either block the circulating path or limit it. If both bridges conduct with no reactor, the supply sees a low-impedance thyristor path and a large current can flow even when the average voltages cancel. Non-circulating mode blocks by gating; circulating mode limits by inductance.

Where the dual converter is used

Reversible rolling-mill drives need forward torque to bite into the metal and reverse torque to brake or back the rolls out. A crane hoist needs torque in the lifting direction to lift a load and to hold it, and torque in the opposite direction to lower a light load under power; an overhauling load is lowered regeneratively with the lifting-direction current. Either way the drive must supply both current directions. In these drives the motor may have to produce torque in either direction while the speed stays positive, or brake while speed is negative. A single bridge cannot reverse current. A dual converter does it without reversing contactors, because converter P and converter N give the two current directions. Some syllabi call this a four-quadrant converter. The price is extra thyristors, a reactor or dead-time control, and more complex firing logic. Small drives often use a single bridge with a reversing contactor instead; dual converters appear where fast, frequent reversal is worth the cost. The same arrangement is used in reversible DC test benches and some high-power supplies.

  5. Load current: I = V1 / R = 179.3 V / 5 Ω = 35.9 A.
  6. Complementary angle: α2 = 180° − 30° = 150°.
  7. Converter N average: V2 = 207.1 V × cos 150° = 207.1 V × (−0.8660) = −179.3 V.

Answer. V1 = 179.3 V, I = 35.9 A, α2 = 150°, and V2 = −179.3 V.

Exam traps

  • Do not set α2 = α1. The complementary bridge needs α2 = 180° − α1; equal angles do not make the two average voltages cancel.
  • Do not say both bridges conduct in non-circulating mode. Only the selected bridge gets gate pulses; the other is held off even though its thyristors may see supply voltage.
  • Matching average voltages does not remove circulating current in circulating-current mode. Ripple makes the instantaneous voltages differ, so the intergroup reactor is still needed.
  • Watch the sign of V2. For α1 < 90°, α2 = 180° − α1 > 90°, so cos α2 is negative and V2 is negative.
  • Use degrees consistently. A calculator left in radian mode gives a wrong cosine for 30°.

The instantaneous bridge voltages have ripple, so a ripple voltage drives current around the P-N loop. The intergroup reactor's inductance limits the current.

2.8 Power factor correction (PFC) rectifiers

A diode bridge with a large capacitor draws current in short spikes near the mains peak. The current is not sine-shaped, so the rectifier has a poor power factor even when the current is almost in phase with the voltage. A power factor correction (PFC) rectifier adds a switching stage that forces the input current to follow the sine voltage, so the supply sees a resistor. Laptop and desktop supplies, LED drivers above roughly 25 W, and EV chargers and fast chargers above roughly 75 W use PFC to meet harmonic limits and to use the grid better.

You need: power factor, displacement factor and THD from 2.5, and the boost converter idea from Unit 3.

Why a capacitor-input rectifier has poor power factor

A diode bridge with a capacitor is the cheapest DC source. The capacitor charges to the mains peak. Diodes conduct only while the instantaneous mains voltage is above the capacitor voltage. Current flows in narrow pulses at the peak, not over the whole cycle. The RMS current is high for the power delivered, and the pulses contain large harmonics.

Power factor is real power divided by apparent power: PF = P/(Vrms Irms). P is average power in W, Vrms and Irms are RMS voltage and current. For a sine voltage, PF = displacement factor × distortion factor. Displacement factor is cos φ1, where φ1 is the phase of the current fundamental. Distortion factor is I1,rms/Irms, where I1,rms is fundamental RMS current.

The current pulses sit at the voltage peak, so φ1 is near zero and displacement factor is near one. Distortion factor is low. Total harmonic distortion (THD) is the RMS harmonic current divided by the fundamental. High THD gives low distortion factor and poor PF. The capacitor does not correct PF; it makes the current peakier.

Passive PFC adds a series inductor. The inductor slows current rise and spreads each pulse over a wider angle. THD falls moderately and PF improves. The inductor carries 50 Hz current, so it is large, heavy and costly. It cannot meet tight harmonic limits at high power.

Active boost PFC: current follows the voltage

An active PFC rectifier places a boost cell after the diode bridge. The bridge gives a rectified sine, |vin|. The boost cell has inductor L, switch S and diode D. When S is on, L stores energy. When S is off, L current flows through D to the 400 V bus. The switch works at high frequency, far above 50 Hz.

The inner current loop measures inductor current and compares it with a reference. The reference comes from a multiplier. One input is a scaled copy of |vin|. The other is the output of a slow error amplifier comparing the bus voltage with 400 V. The result is iref = k |vin|, where k is set by the voltage loop. The current loop switches S so average input current equals iref. Thus iin is proportional to vin, and the mains sees an effective resistance Re = Vrms2/P, where Re is the effective input resistance in Ω, Vrms is the RMS mains voltage in V and P is the input real power in W.

The boost duty is D = 1 − vin/Vdc, where vin is instantaneous rectified voltage and Vdc is bus voltage. At the 230 V mains peak, vin = 325.3 V. With Vdc = 400 V, D = 0.19. Near zero crossings D rises towards 1. A boost converter only raises voltage, so the bus must be above the mains peak; 400 V gives margin.

The voltage loop must be slow. Single-phase power pulsates at twice mains frequency, so the bus has 100 Hz ripple. A fast loop would put that ripple into the current reference and distort the input current. Loop bandwidth is kept below 100 Hz, often 10–20 Hz. Continuous conduction mode (CCM) is used at higher power; discontinuous conduction mode (DCM) appears at light load. Both can give PF near 0.99 and THD below 5%.

Practical versions and limits

A bridgeless PFC removes the diode bridge. Current uses two switches, saving one diode drop and raising efficiency. Current sensing and EMI are harder because the mains is not tied to the bus. An interleaved PFC runs two boost cells 180° apart. Input ripple partly cancels, inductors shrink, and heat spreads. Server supplies and EV chargers use these versions.

The 400 V bus is not a battery. The bus capacitor is sized for 100 Hz ripple and hold-up time, not long storage. The PFC stage sets input current shape; a second DC-DC converter usually gives isolation and a regulated low voltage. Harmonic standards limit grid current. IEC 61000-3-2 covers many low-power types of equipment (rated input current up to 16 A per phase); IEEE 519 covers industrial installations. PFC is not efficiency: a supply can have PF 0.99 and still waste heat. At light load, some controllers enter burst mode to keep efficiency high.

Worked example

Question. A 500 W PFC rectifier runs from a 230 V, 50 Hz mains with power factor 0.99 and a 400 V output bus. Assume ideal devices and sinusoidal input current. Find the input RMS current, the peak input current, the peak mains voltage, and the boost switch duty at the mains peak.

  1. Real power P = 500 W. PF = P/(Vrms Irms), so Irms = P/(PF × Vrms) = 500/(0.99 × 230) = 2.196 A ≈ 2.2 A.
  2. For a sine current, peak current Ipk = √2 × Irms = √2 × 2.196 = 3.105 A ≈ 3.1 A.
  3. Peak mains voltage Vpk = √2 × 230 = 325.269 V ≈ 325.3 V.
  4. Boost duty at the peak: D = 1 − Vpk/Vdc = 1 − 325.269/400 = 0.1868 ≈ 0.19. Duty rises towards 1 as vin falls towards zero.

Answer. The input RMS current is 2.2 A, the peak input current is 3.1 A, the peak mains voltage is 325.3 V, and the duty at the mains peak is 0.19.

Exam traps

  • A capacitor filter improves power factor. It does the opposite: it narrows diode conduction and raises THD.
  • PF = cos φ is enough for any rectifier. It is true only for sine current; with harmonics PF = displacement factor × distortion factor.
  • PFC and efficiency are the same. PF = P/S; efficiency = Pout/Pin. A supply can have PF 0.99 and low efficiency.
  • The PFC voltage loop should be fast to hold 400 V. A fast loop copies 100 Hz bus ripple into the current reference and distorts input current.
  • A 400 V bus is arbitrary. The boost can only step up, so the bus must exceed the 325.3 V peak of a 230 V mains.

Check yourself

Remember Define THD for an input current with fundamental RMS I1 and total RMS I. 2 marks

THD = √(I² − I1²)/I1, the RMS harmonic current divided by the fundamental.

Understand Why does a capacitor-input rectifier have near-unity displacement factor but poor power factor? 3 marks

Its current pulses are at the voltage peak, so φ1 ≈ 0. The pulse shape has large harmonics, so distortion factor is low and THD is high.

Irms = 200/(0.95 × 230) = 0.915 A ≈ 0.92 A.

D = 1 − 200/400 = 0.5.

Analyze Why is the PFC voltage loop kept slow? 4 marks

The bus has 100 Hz ripple from single-phase power. A slow loop keeps that ripple out of the current reference, so the input current stays sine-shaped.

Analyze Why must the bus be above 325.3 V for a 230 V mains? 3 marks

The boost cell only steps up. If the bus voltage were below the mains peak (325.3 V), then near the top of each half-cycle the rectified input would exceed the bus, the bridge diodes would conduct straight into the bus, and the switch would lose control of the inductor current. 400 V keeps the bus above the peak with margin.

Bridgeless and interleaved PFC are common in server and EV chargers. Search for totem-pole PFC, average current mode control and IEC 61000-3-2.

Takeaways

  1. A diode rectifier has a fixed average voltage set by supply peak and topology; no gate control can change it.
  2. A capacitor filter raises no-load voltage and narrows diode current pulses; an LC filter smooths current but needs proper inductor design.
  3. Phase control by α moves the average output continuously and can make it negative when the load can supply energy.
  4. A p-pulse converter has output ripple at p times supply frequency; six-pulse three-phase is the common industrial case.
  5. Power factor is not just cos α: input current harmonics also reduce PF.
  6. Source inductance creates overlap, lowers average output with load, and limits the maximum inverter angle.
  7. A dual converter gives reversible voltage and current; circulating-current mode is fast but needs a reactor.
  8. A PFC rectifier makes input current follow the sine voltage, giving near-unity power factor and low THD.

Glossary

Average DC voltage
Mean of output voltage over one period.
Boost PFC
Diode bridge plus boost converter that regulates DC output and shapes input current.
Capacitor filter
Shunt capacitor that stores charge and reduces output ripple.
Circulating current
Current that flows between the two bridges of a dual converter when both are enabled.
Commutation
Transfer of load current from one device to the next.
Continuous conduction
Load current remains above zero throughout the cycle.
Critical inductance
Minimum choke inductance that keeps an LC filter in continuous current at a given load.
Discontinuous conduction
Load current reaches zero before the next firing, so output voltage depends on load.
Displacement factor
Cosine of the phase angle between fundamental input current and supply voltage.
Dual converter
Two anti-parallel controlled rectifiers that allow reversible voltage and current.
Firing angle
Delay angle α measured from the natural commutation point.
Form factor
Rms value divided by average value.
Freewheeling diode
Diode that carries inductive load current when the main switch turns off.
Fully controlled rectifier
Converter with all thyristors; can invert with an active load.
Half-controlled rectifier
Converter with both diodes and thyristors; output voltage has one polarity.
LC filter
Series inductor plus shunt capacitor that blocks ripple.
Overlap angle
Angle during which two devices conduct together because of source inductance.
Peak inverse voltage
Maximum reverse voltage across an off diode or thyristor.
Phase control
Delaying thyristor turn-on within each supply half-cycle to control average voltage.
Power factor
Real power divided by apparent power.
Power factor correction
Input shaping that makes line current sinusoidal and in phase with voltage.
Pulse number
Number of output pulses per supply cycle.
Rectifier
Circuit that converts AC to DC.
Ripple factor
Rms of AC ripple divided by average DC.
Thyristor
Four-layer controlled switch that latches on with a gate pulse and turns off when current falls.
Total harmonic distortion
Ratio of harmonic rms current to fundamental rms current.
