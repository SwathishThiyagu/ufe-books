3.1 Chopper principle and control strategies

A chopper is a switch between a DC source and load, opening and closing thousands of times a second. Closed, the load sees the source voltage; open, almost zero. The average load voltage equals source voltage times the fraction of each period the switch is closed. This idea runs DC motor drives, chargers, LED drivers and solar trackers. Names: DC chopper, buck chopper, DC-DC converter.

You need Unit 1 for the power switch, diode, on-state drops and gate drive, and Unit 2 for average voltage from a switched source.

The chopper and its duty ratio

A basic chopper has a DC source Vs, a controlled switch (MOSFET or IGBT with gate driver), a freewheeling diode Df, and the load. The switch is fully on (almost zero voltage) or fully off (zero current), so it wastes little power as heat.

Switch on: load tied to source, vo(t) = Vs. Switch off: inductive load current cannot stop instantly, so it turns through Df and load voltage falls to about −Vd, close to zero. Df is also called flywheel or catch diode.

The current formula assumes an R-L load with an inductor large enough to keep current continuous, or a chopper feeding an L-C filter.

T = T_on + T_off = 1/f_s ,   D = T_on / T ,   V_o = D·V_s ,   I_o = V_o / R = D·V_s / R

T is switching period in seconds; f_s is switching frequency in hertz; T_on and T_off are on and off times in seconds; D is duty ratio, 0 to 1; V_s is source voltage in volts; V_o is average load voltage in volts; R is load resistance in ohms; I_o is average load current in amperes, for continuous inductor current.

Vo = D·Vs is an average. The load voltage is a pulse train between Vs and about 0; a moving-coil DC voltmeter shows only the mean. At D = 0 load gets nothing; at D = 1 full source. With an inductor keeping current continuous, Po = Vo·Io = D²·Vs²/R watts. For a purely resistive load the current pulses with voltage, so average power is D·Vs²/R, not Vo·Io.

Three control strategies

Open loop, output obeys D·Vs and nothing corrects it; input sag or load change shifts Vo. Closed loop measures Vo, compares with reference Vref, amplifies error, and lets error set D. Three strategies set D.

  • Constant-frequency pulse-width (time-ratio) control. An oscillator fixes T and only T_on moves. A comparator compares the error signal with a sawtooth of period T; where they cross, the switch turns off, so a larger error gives a wider pulse. Ripple frequency is fixed, filter design is easy, switching loss is predictable. Most controller ICs do this.
  • Variable-frequency control. T_on is fixed and f_s changes. Pulse energy per cycle is constant and switch conduction time is known, but the filter must suit the lowest frequency used, and noise spreads across a band that is harder to filter and clear for EMI.
  • Current-limit control. The switch turns off when inductor or load current reaches upper limit Imax and back on at lower limit Imin. Output follows the current band; a short circuit is limited cycle by cycle, so chargers and motor drives use it. Frequency varies with load and source voltage; ripple current is fixed by the chosen band Imax − Imin.

Families and conduction modes

Choppers are named by what they do to voltage.

  • Step-down (buck) chopper: Vo < Vs. The switch-diode pair sits before an L-C filter and the inductor averages the pulse down; see 3.3.
  • Step-up (boost) chopper: Vo > Vs. The inductor charges from source with switch on, then sits in series with source through a diode; see 3.4.
  • Step-down/step-up (buck-boost) chopper: output above or below input, reversed polarity; see 3.5.
  • Cuk chopper: same gain, a capacitor in the middle, smooth current at both ports; also in 3.5.

Two conduction modes describe inductor current. In continuous conduction mode (CCM) that current never reaches zero inside a period. In discontinuous conduction mode (DCM) current falls to zero before period ends and rests until next pulse, so gain depends on load and D.

In DC motor drives choppers are numbered by quadrants — one-, two-, four-quadrant — because a motor must send braking power back to source. Gains for each family are derived in next chapters.

Worked example

Question. A chopper feeds an R-L load (R = 10 Ω, inductor large enough for continuous current) from 200 V DC. f_s = 1 kHz, T_on = 0.4 ms. (a) Find D, V_o, I_o and P_o. (b) At 2 kHz with same T_on, find D, V_o and P_o.

  1. (a) T = 1/(1×10³ Hz) = 1 ms.
  2. D = 0.4 ms / 1 ms = 0.4.
  3. V_o = D·V_s = 0.4 × 200 V = 80 V.
  4. Continuous current: I_o = V_o/R = 80 V / 10 Ω = 8 A.
  5. P_o = V_o·I_o = 80 V × 8 A = 640 W.
  6. (b) T = 1/(2×10³ Hz) = 0.5 ms.
  7. D = 0.4 ms / 0.5 ms = 0.8.
  8. V_o = 0.8 × 200 V = 160 V; I_o = 160 V / 10 Ω = 16 A; P_o = 160 V × 16 A = 2560 W.
  9. Both V_o and I_o doubled, so power rose four times.

Answer. At 1 kHz: D = 0.4, V_o = 80 V, I_o = 8 A, P_o = 640 W. At 2 kHz: D = 0.8, V_o = 160 V, P_o = 2560 W. This assumes continuous current; for a pure resistor use P_o = D·V_s²/R.

Exam traps

  • D = T_on/T, never T_off/T. 0.4 ms on in a 1 ms period is D = 0.4, not 0.6.
  • V_o = D·V_s is average. A true-RMS meter reads V_s·√D, a different number.
  • Same on-time at double frequency doubles D; power rises four times because V_o and I_o both double.
  • The freewheeling diode holds load voltage near zero in off-time and carries inductive current. Without it the switch sees a large turn-off spike.
  • A chopper works on DC. AC voltage controllers and cycloconverters work on AC, see Unit 5.

3.2 Linear regulators and switched-mode power supplies

A regulated DC supply must hold its output steady when the input and the load change. A linear regulator does this by placing a transistor in series and burning the extra voltage as heat. A switched-mode power supply instead chops the input at high frequency, stores energy in an inductor or transformer, and filters the result. The first is simple and quiet but wasteful; the second reaches 70–95 % efficiency and is used in phone chargers, laptop adapters and LED drivers. This chapter compares both and shows where the heat goes.

You need the MOSFET switch and heat-sink ideas from Unit 1, the rectifier and capacitor filter from Unit 2, and the chopper idea from Chapter 3.1.

Linear regulator: a controlled voltage drop

A linear regulator keeps Vo fixed by using a series pass transistor as a variable resistor. The transistor, often a BJT Darlington or a MOSFET, stays in its active (linear) region, never fully on or off. An error amplifier compares a fraction βVo of the output (β is the divider ratio, below 1) with a zener or bandgap reference Vref. It drives the pass device so βVo ≈ Vref. If Vo falls, the error amplifier turns the pass device on harder and reduces its voltage drop.

The input must stay above Vo by the dropout voltage, the minimum across the pass device that still allows control. Below dropout the regulator shuts down or loses regulation. A low-dropout (LDO) type uses a pass device with a small dropout, often under 0.5 V. A shunt regulator places the control device across the load instead; it is simple but wastes more current.

η = (V<sub>o</sub>·I<sub>o</sub>) / [V<sub>in</sub>·(I<sub>o</sub> + I<sub>q</sub>)] ≈ V<sub>o</sub> / V<sub>in</sub> ; P<sub>d</sub> = (V<sub>in</sub> − V<sub>o</sub>) · I<sub>o</sub>

η is efficiency (fraction); Vo is output voltage (V); Vin is input voltage (V); Io is load current (A); Iq is quiescent current (A); Pd is heat in the pass device (W). The approximation holds when Iq is much smaller than Io.

The dissipation Pd sets the heat-sink size using the thermal design of Unit 1. A 5 V, 1 A load from 12 V makes 7 W of heat, so a small plastic package overheats without a heatsink. Linear regulators suit low-current, low-noise analogue circuits and post-regulation after a switcher.

Switched-mode supply: chop, store, filter

A switched-mode power supply (SMPS) takes AC mains through a rectifier and capacitor filter to a rough DC bus. A high-frequency switch then chops this DC into pulses. A transformer gives isolation and a voltage step; in non-isolated types an inductor stores energy. An output rectifier and L-C filter smooth the pulses. A PWM controller changes the duty ratio D, the on-time fraction, to hold Vo fixed.

  1. Rectify and filter the mains to a DC bus.
  2. Chop the DC bus with a high-frequency switch.
  3. Transfer energy through a transformer or inductor.
  4. Rectify and L-C filter the pulses.
  5. Feed back Vo through an optocoupler or auxiliary winding to the PWM controller.

Loss is mostly conduction loss I²R in the switch, winding and diode, plus switching loss from voltage-current overlap at every transition. Good designs reach η = 70–95 %. Higher switching frequency fs shrinks the transformer and inductor because the volt-second (flux) swing per cycle is smaller, so fewer turns and a smaller core are needed; it raises switching loss and EMI. Phone chargers, laptop bricks and LED drivers use fs from tens of kHz to a few hundred kHz.

η = P<sub>o</sub> / P<sub>in</sub> ; P<sub>loss</sub> = P<sub>in</sub> − P<sub>o</sub>

Po is output power (W); Pin is input power (W); Ploss is loss as heat (W); η is efficiency (fraction).

The price is output ripple, electromagnetic interference (EMI), and a control loop that must stay stable. Layout, snubbers and input filters become part of the design. See Chapter 3.3 for the buck cell and Chapter 3.6 for isolated types.

Worked example

Question. A 5 V linear regulator takes 1 A from a 12 V input. (a) Find the pass-transistor dissipation and the efficiency. (b) A switched-mode supply of 85 % efficiency does the same job; find its input power, its loss, and the ratio of the linear loss to the SMPS loss.

  1. Voltage across the pass device = Vin − Vo = 12 V − 5 V = 7 V.
  2. Pd = 7 V × 1 A = 7 W.
  3. ηlin = Vo / Vin = 5 / 12 = 0.4167 = 41.7 %.
  4. Po = 5 V × 1 A = 5 W.
  5. Pin = Po / 0.85 = 5 W / 0.85 = 5.88 W.
  6. Ploss = Pin − Po = 5.88 W − 5 W = 0.88 W (0.882 W before rounding).
  7. Ratio = 7 W / 0.8824 W = 7.93 (about eight times); the division uses the unrounded loss.

Answer. The linear regulator heats the pass device by 7 W at 41.7 % efficiency; the SMPS draws 5.88 W and loses 0.88 W, so the linear regulator's loss is 7.93 times the SMPS loss.

Exam traps

  • Linear-regulator loss is (Vin − Vo)·Io, not Vin·Io. Using Vin·Io counts the useful output power as heat.
  • SMPS efficiency is Po/Pin. Vo/Vin is meaningless for a switcher, which can step up or step down.
  • Dropout is a voltage difference, not a current. Below Vo + dropout the regulator cannot hold regulation.
  • A higher switching frequency shrinks the magnetics but does not automatically raise efficiency; switching loss and EMI grow.

3.3 Buck converter

A buck converter steps a DC voltage down. A switch chops the input. An inductor and capacitor average the pulses, so the output is smooth and lower than the input. The diode gives inductor current a path when the switch opens. Gain comes from the duty ratio D, not a lossy resistor. Buck converters sit inside phone chargers, laptop adapters, LED drivers and processor regulators, turning 12 V or 24 V into 5 V, 3.3 V or 1.2 V efficiently.

You need Chapter 3.1 for duty ratio and continuous conduction, Chapter 3.2 for the filter in a switched-mode supply, and Unit 2 for capacitor filtering.

The circuit and the two states

The buck converter has source Vs, switch S, freewheeling diode Df, inductor L, capacitor C and load resistor R. S and Df sit before the L-C filter. Output Vo is across C and R.

When S is ON for Ton, the input connects to L and the diode is reverse biased. Inductor voltage is vL = Vs − Vo. Inductor current iL rises. L stores energy and feeds C and R. When S is OFF for Toff, iL cannot stop instantly. The diode turns on and gives iL a path. L is now between ground and the output, so vL = −Vo. Current falls and L supplies the load.

vL = L · diL/dt

vL is inductor voltage in volts; L is inductance in henries; iL is inductor current in amperes; t is time in seconds.

The inductor integrates this voltage. In steady state the average voltage across L over one period T is zero; otherwise current would drift each cycle. C holds Vo nearly constant because C is large and fs is high. With Vs = 50 V and Vo = 20 V, current ramps up for 4 µs and down for 6 µs, as the figure shows.

Volt-second balance and the gain

Apply volt-second balance. During ON, volt-seconds are (Vs − Vo)Ton = (Vs − Vo)DT. During OFF they are (−Vo)(1−D)T. Their sum is zero.

(V<sub>s</sub> − V<sub>o</sub>)·D·T − V<sub>o</sub>·(1−D)·T = 0

Vs is input voltage in volts; Vo is average output voltage in volts; D = Ton/T is duty ratio, unitless, 0 ≤ D ≤ 1; T is switching period in seconds; Ton is on-time in seconds.

Vo = D·Vs

Vo is average output voltage in volts; Vs is input voltage in volts; D is duty ratio, unitless.

Divide by T: D(Vs − Vo) = (1−D)Vo, so Vo = D Vs. Since D ≤ 1, Vo ≤ Vs. Buck only steps down. Duty ratio sets output; the switch does not burn excess as heat like a linear regulator.

A real diode has forward drop VD. During OFF, vL = −(Vo + VD). Balance gives D(Vs − Vo) = (1−D)(Vo + VD), so Vo ≈ D Vs − (1−D)VD. The drop lowers output, worst at low Vo. A synchronous buck replaces the diode with a MOSFET. Its low on-resistance gives less drop, common for low-voltage high-current rails.

At light load the inductor current can reach zero before the period ends. This is discontinuous conduction mode (DCM). Then Vo rises above D Vs toward Vs. The gain Vo = D Vs holds only in continuous conduction mode (CCM).

Ripple, peak current and design limits

During ON, diL/dt = (Vs − Vo)/L. Over Ton = DT the current rises by ΔIL = (Vs − Vo)DT/L. Put Vo = D Vs: Vs − Vo = Vs(1−D), and Vs(1−D)D = Vo(1−D). So ΔIL = Vo(1−D)/(fs L), with T = 1/fs.

ΔI<sub>L</sub> = (V<sub>s</sub> − V<sub>o</sub>)·D·T/L = V<sub>o</sub>(1−D)/(f<sub>s</sub>·L)

ΔIL is peak-to-peak inductor current ripple in amperes; fs is switching frequency in hertz; L is inductance in henries; T is period in seconds.

Average inductor current equals load current Io. Peak and minimum are imax = Io + ΔIL/2 and imin = Io − ΔIL/2. For CCM, imin > 0. Set imin = 0 for the boundary: Io = ΔIL/2 = Vo(1−D)/(2 fs L). Rearrange Lmin = (1−D)Vo/(2 fs Io). If L is below Lmin at that load, the converter enters DCM.

C filters the triangular inductor current. Output ripple is ΔVo = ΔIL/(8 fs C). This assumes an ideal capacitor and CCM. Real electrolytic capacitors have ESR, adding a step ΔIL × ESR. Ceramic capacitors have low ESR for tight outputs.

Design steps: choose D = Vo/Vs; choose fs high enough to shrink L and C; choose L above Lmin for the lowest load that must stay in CCM; choose C for the allowed ΔVo; check capacitor ESR and diode or MOSFET drop. Larger L lowers ripple and peak current but slows transient response.

Worked example

Question. A buck converter has Vs = 50 V, D = 0.4, fs = 100 kHz, L = 100 µH, C = 220 µF and a 5 Ω load. Find Vo, the inductor ripple, the peak and minimum inductor current, the output ripple, and the inductance at the CCM boundary.

  1. Output voltage: Vo = D·Vs = 0.4 × 50 V = 20 V.
  2. Load current: Io = Vo/R = 20 V / 5 Ω = 4 A.
  3. Period: T = 1/fs = 1/(100 kHz) = 10 µs. On-time: Ton = D·T = 0.4 × 10 µs = 4 µs.
  4. Inductor ripple: ΔIL = (Vs − Vo)·Ton/L = (50 − 20) V × 4 µs / 100 µH = 1.2 A.
  5. Peak current: imax = Io + ΔIL/2 = 4 A + 0.6 A = 4.6 A. Minimum: imin = 4 A − 0.6 A = 3.4 A. Since imin > 0, the converter is in CCM.
  6. Output ripple: ΔVo = ΔIL/(8 fs C) = 1.2 A / (8 × 100 kHz × 220 µF) = 6.8 mV.
  7. CCM boundary: Lmin = (1−D)Vo/(2 fs Io) = (0.6 × 20 V)/(2 × 100 kHz × 4 A) = 15 µH. The 100 µH inductor is safely above this.

Answer. Vo = 20 V, Io = 4 A, ΔIL = 1.2 A, iL swings between 3.4 A and 4.6 A, ΔVo = 6.8 mV, and Lmin = 15 µH, so the 100 µH inductor keeps the converter in CCM.

Exam traps

  • Vo = D·Vs is a CCM result. In DCM the output floats upward toward Vs as load lightens; do not use the gain formula at no load.
  • During on-time the inductor voltage is Vs − Vo, not Vs. Forgetting the output term overestimates gain and ripple.
  • Duty ratio is unitless. Ton and T must use the same unit: 4 µs / 10 µs, not 4 µs / 100 kHz.
  • Average inductor current is Io; peak is Io + ΔIL/2. Peak switch current equals peak inductor current.

3.4 Boost converter

A boost converter is a DC-DC circuit that makes a higher DC output from a lower DC input. It stores energy in an inductor from the source while a switch is on, then opens the switch so the inductor drives current through a diode into the output, in series with the source. The output is always above the input: Vo = Vs/(1 − D). Boost cells appear in battery-to-rail boosters, power-factor-correction front ends, and step-up chopper drives.

You need: Chapter 3.3 for volt-second balance and ripple formulas, and Unit 1 for switch voltage and current ratings.

Circuit and the two states

Draw the boost cell. The source Vs feeds inductor L. The other end of L joins the switch S and the anode of diode D1. S goes from that node to the return rail. D1 goes to output capacitor C and load R. The load sits across C. The switch is a power MOSFET or IGBT with a gate drive; the diode is a fast power diode.

State ON, switch closed. D1 is reverse biased. L sees Vs, so the inductor voltage vL = Vs. The inductor current iL rises linearly. The output capacitor feeds the load alone.

State OFF, switch open. L forces current through D1 into the output. L is now in series with the source, so vL = Vs − Vo. This is negative because Vo > Vs. The inductor current falls linearly.

Let T be the switching period in seconds and D = Ton/T the duty ratio, where Ton is the on-time and Toff = T − Ton is the off-time. Volt-second balance over one period gives the gain:

V<sub>o</sub> = V<sub>s</sub>/(1 − D),   D = 1 − V<sub>s</sub>/V<sub>o</sub>

Vo is output voltage in V; Vs is source voltage in V; D is duty ratio, between 0 and 1.

The gain is at least one. It grows without limit as D approaches one. In practice keep D below about 0.9. Above that, winding resistance, switch drop and diode drop take over, and the useful gain stops rising. The switch carries the full inductor current, not the load current.

Waveforms, ripple and CCM limit

During ON, iL rises by ΔIL = Vs·D·T/L. During OFF, it falls by (Vo − Vs)(1 − D)T/L. The two changes are equal because the average vL over a period is zero.

ΔI<sub>L</sub> = V<sub>s</sub>·D/(f<sub>s</sub>·L)

ΔIL is peak-to-peak inductor ripple current in A; fs = 1/T is switching frequency in Hz; L is inductance in H.

IL = Io/(1 − D)

IL is average inductor current in A; Io = Vo/R is load current in A; R is load resistance in Ω.

The switch current is iL during Ton and zero during Toff. The diode current is zero during Ton and iL during Toff. So the input current is smooth, because L is in series with the source. The output current is pulsed, because the diode feeds the output only in the off-time. C supplies the load during the on-time.

The inductor current stays continuous if its minimum is above zero. This is continuous conduction mode (CCM). The boundary inductance is:

L<sub>min</sub> = V<sub>s</sub>·D·T/(2I<sub>L</sub>)

Lmin is the CCM boundary inductance in H; T is switching period in s; IL is average inductor current in A.

For L below Lmin the converter enters discontinuous conduction mode (DCM) and the gain formula changes. The output ripple from C alone is:

ΔV<sub>o</sub> = I<sub>o</sub>·D/(f<sub>s</sub>·C)

ΔVo is output ripple voltage in V; C is output capacitance in F.

This comes from C feeding the load during D·T. Capacitor equivalent series resistance (ESR) adds a step set by the change in capacitor current, so the measured ripple is larger.

Why the loop is slow and where it is used

The control-to-output response has a right-half-plane (RHP) zero. When D rises, the off-time shrinks at once, so the diode delivers current to the output for a shorter part of the cycle. The output voltage dips before the inductor current can build up. A feedback loop must therefore be slower than in a buck converter. Push the crossover too high and the loop can become unstable.

The boost is also called a step-up chopper. It is the cell behind battery-to-rail boosters, where a low battery voltage must feed a higher DC bus. It is also the boost stage in power-factor-correction (PFC) front ends, where the input current is shaped to follow the line voltage. See PFC rectifiers and battery and solar interfaces. For reversed polarity and step-down/step-up action, see Chapter 3.5.

The inductor is in series with the source, so the input current has low ripple. That is why the boost is preferred at the input of a PFC stage. The output current is pulsed, so the output capacitor must absorb the diode current pulses. The switch must be rated for Vo plus margin, and it carries IL. The diode must be fast, because it turns off while the switch turns on. At high gain the switch on-time is long and the diode off-time is short, so the diode current is a narrow pulse with high peak.

Worked example

Question. A boost converter runs from a 24 V source with duty ratio D = 0.6, switching frequency fs = 50 kHz, L = 300 µH, C = 470 µF, and a 100 Ω load. Find Vo, Io, the average and peak-to-peak ripple inductor current, the minimum and maximum inductor current, and the output ripple.

  1. T = 1/fs = 1/(50 kHz) = 20.0 µs.
  2. Vo = Vs/(1 − D) = 24 V/(1 − 0.6) = 60.0 V.

Exam traps

  • Writing Vo = Vs·D or Vo = Vs/(1 + D). The boost gain is Vs/(1 − D), so D = 1 − Vs/Vo.
  • Forgetting the (1 − D) in the current transfer. The average inductor current is IL = Io/(1 − D), and the switch carries IL, not Io.
  • Saying the diode conducts during the on-time. It is reverse biased then; it conducts only during the off-time.
  • Using the buck ripple formula. In a boost the inductor sees Vs during the on-time, so ΔIL = Vs·D·T/L.
  • Ignoring the output capacitor duty. C alone feeds the load during the on-time, giving ΔVo = Io·D/(fs·C); ESR makes the measured ripple larger.

3.5 Buck-boost and Cuk converters

A buck converter can only step down and a boost can only step up. A buck-boost converter gets both from one inductor: the switch charges it from the source, then the switch opens and the inductor pushes that energy into the output. The output comes out inverted, and the same circuit works below or above the input voltage. The Cuk converter reaches the same gain with a capacitor carrying the energy in the middle, so the currents at both ports stay smooth. Both appear in battery equipment, LED drivers and negative-rail supplies.

You need: the on/off state method and volt-second balance from Chapter 3.3 and Chapter 3.4.

The buck-boost circuit and its two states

Four parts sit around node A. The source Vs (volts) feeds switch S, which connects it to node A. Inductor L (henries) runs from node A to the return rail. Diode D runs from the output node to node A, with its cathode at A. Capacitor C and load R sit between the output node and the return rail. This diode orientation makes the output negative.

Switch ON, for D·T seconds of each period T = 1/f. Here D is the duty ratio (0 ≤ D ≤ 1), T is the switching period in seconds and f = 1/T is the switching frequency in hertz. With the switch on, node A sits at Vs, the diode cathode is the highest voltage in the circuit, so the diode blocks. The inductor sees vL = Vs and its current rises in a straight line. The capacitor alone feeds the load.

Switch OFF, for the rest of the period. The inductor current cannot stop. It keeps flowing the same way as before, from node A down through L to the return rail, so node A falls until the diode conducts and supplies that current from the output node. The diode then holds node A at Vo, which is negative, so vL = Vo and the inductor current falls. The load current now comes from the return rail through R into the output node, then through the diode and the inductor, so the stored energy reaches the load.

D·V_s + (1 − D)·V_o = 0   →   V_o = −V_s·D/(1 − D)

Vs is the input voltage in volts; Vo is the output voltage in volts, negative with respect to the return rail; D is the duty ratio; T is the period in seconds.

The magnitude ratio |Vo|/Vs = D/(1 − D) equals 1 at D = 0.5. Below 0.5 the converter steps down, above 0.5 it steps up. Both jobs from one inductor give it the name step-down/step-up converter, or inverting regulator.

Stress, currents and what it costs

Blocking voltage. With the switch off, its two terminals sit at Vs and Vo, so it must block Vs + |Vo| = Vs/(1 − D). The diode blocks the same. At 12 V input and D = 0.6 that is 30 V, not 12 V, and a 25 V MOSFET would fail.

Inductor current. The diode carries the load current only during the off-time, so the inductor average is IL = Io/(1 − D). The ripple is ΔIL = Vs·D/(L·f). Continuous conduction needs IL ≥ ΔIL/2, which gives L ≥ (1 − D)²·R/(2f).

Pulsed input current. The source supplies current only while the switch is on: a rectangle of height IL and width D·T. Its average is Is = D·IL = |Vo|·Io/Vs, which is just power balance. A practical buck-boost always has an input capacitor across the source. This capacitor absorbs the difference between the pulsed switch current and the smooth source current, so it carries a large ripple current and needs a low ESR.

Rough output. The diode feeds the output in pulses too, so the output capacitor also carries a large ripple current. That is the practical weakness of the buck-boost: two noisy ports, one small inductor.

The Cuk converter

Cuk (say "chook") rearranges the same parts. Inductor L1 runs from Vs to node A, switch S from A to the return rail, capacitor C1 from A to node B, inductor L2 from B to the output node, diode D from B to the return rail with its anode at B and its cathode at the return rail, and C2 across the load. The output is inverted again.

ON: node A is at zero, so B sits at −VC1 and the diode blocks. C1 discharges through L2 into the load while L1 charges from Vs. OFF: the diode conducts and clamps B near zero, one diode drop above the return rail (B swings between −VC1 and 0). The current of L1 flows through C1 into B, where it splits: part goes through the diode to the return rail and the rest through L2 to the load, charging C1 on the way.

D·V_s + (1 − D)(V_s − V_C1) = 0   →   V_C1 = V_s/(1 − D) = V_s + |V_o|

VC1 is the average voltage across the coupling capacitor in volts; the other symbols are as before. Balancing L2 the same way gives Vo = −Vs·D/(1 − D), the buck-boost gain.

The gain is identical, the currents are not. L1 carries the source current and L2 the load current, both continuous, so ripple at each port is small and filtering is easy. The price is two inductors, plus C1 carrying the full input current as a large alternating current, so it is a film or ceramic part, not a small electrolytic.

SEPIC and Zeta move the same parts to get a positive output; their isolated forms appear in Chapter 3.6.

3.6 Isolated converters: flyback and forward

An isolated DC-DC converter puts a transformer between the switch and the load, so the input and output have no direct electrical path. The transformer carries energy by magnetic field, gives safety, allows a large voltage ratio, and can feed several outputs. The flyback stores energy in the transformer during the on-time and delivers it during the off-time. The forward delivers energy during the on-time and resets the core every cycle. Both appear in chargers, standby supplies and industrial DC rails.

You need: buck converter and buck-boost for duty ratio and output stages, and Unit 1 for device ratings and snubbers.

Why isolation, and the flyback idea

An isolated converter puts a transformer between the switch and the load. There is no direct DC path; energy crosses by magnetic field. Isolation is wanted for safety, for a step-up or step-down too large for one duty ratio, and for several outputs from one primary. The flyback is the simplest isolated converter. Its transformer is really a coupled inductor with an air gap, a small non-magnetic gap in the core. The primary inductance stores energy.

With the switch ON, the input voltage appears across the primary, primary current ramps, and the secondary diode is reverse biased. The core stores energy in its gap. With the switch OFF, the primary voltage reverses, the secondary diode conducts, and the stored energy charges the output capacitor. CCM means the magnetic current never falls to zero in a cycle. In CCM the gain is:

Vo = Vs·D / ((1 − D)·n)

Vo is the output DC voltage; Vs is the input DC voltage; D is the duty ratio, the fraction of the switching period the switch is on; n = N1/N2 is the primary-to-secondary turns ratio.

The switch in a flyback holds the input voltage plus the reflected output voltage: stress = Vs + n·Vo, plus a spike from leakage inductance. Leakage inductance is primary flux that does not link the secondary. An RCD clamp, a resistor-capacitor-diode network, absorbs that spike. Without it the switch may break at turn-off.

Forward converter: energy passes in ON

A forward converter passes energy while the switch is ON. The transformer is a true transformer, not the main energy store. When the switch is ON, primary current flows, the secondary voltage forward-biases the rectifier, and current ramps in output inductor Lo. When the switch is OFF, a freewheeling diode keeps Lo current flowing. The output stage is a buck converter after the transformer, so it is smooth and suits medium power.

V<sub>o</sub> = V<sub>s</sub>·D·(N<sub>2</sub>/N<sub>1</sub>)

Vo is the output DC voltage; Vs is the input DC voltage; D is the duty ratio; N2/N1 is the secondary-to-primary turns ratio.

The core must be reset every cycle. During ON, magnetizing flux rises. During OFF, it must fall back to the same value. A third reset winding does this. If the reset winding has the same turns as the primary, it applies Vs in the off-time and takes D·Ts to reset. That time must fit inside (1 − D)·Ts, so D must stay below 0.5. If reset is incomplete, flux walks up cycle by cycle, the core saturates, and the switch current spikes. An active clamp can reset the core and allow a wider duty range; only a few universities teach it.

Choosing between them

The choice is mostly power level and cost. Flyback is simple and cheap: phone chargers, standby supplies, auxiliary bias rails. It has high peak currents and more output ripple. Forward is for medium power: telecom and industrial DC rails, where a smoother output is worth the extra transformer, reset winding and output inductor. Both need a clamp or snubber for the leakage spike, and both trade a duty limit and isolation against a non-isolated buck.

3.7 Resonant converters and soft switching

Small transformers and inductors need a high switching frequency, but every hard-switched transition burns energy while voltage and current overlap. Resonant converters place an inductor and capacitor around the switch and time the gate to the tank's zero crossings, so the device turns on at zero voltage or turns off at zero current. The loss per transition falls, the heatsink shrinks, and frequencies of hundreds of kilohertz become practical. You meet the idea in phone and laptop adapters, TV supplies and server power shelves.

You need: switching loss, output capacitance and heat-sink ideas from Unit 1, and the PWM cells of Chapters 3.3 to 3.5.

Hard switching sets a frequency ceiling

In a PWM converter the switch is told to change state while it holds the full load voltage and carries the full load current. For the short overlap time the device is neither off nor on: voltage falls while current rises, or current falls while voltage rises. That overlap is the hard-switching loss.

P_sw = ½ · V · I · (t_r + t_f) · f_s

V is the off-state voltage in volts (V); I is the on-state current in amperes (A); t_r is the turn-on overlap time in seconds (s); t_f is the turn-off overlap time in seconds (s); f_s is the switching frequency in hertz (Hz). The ½ assumes a triangular voltage-current overlap.

Two more terms appear every cycle. A diode that turns off must sweep out its stored charge Q_rr, costing about P_rr = Q_rr · V · f_s, where Q_rr is the reverse-recovery charge in coulombs (C). The device output capacitance C_oss stores ½·C_oss·V² and dumps it each cycle, costing P_C = ½ · C_oss · V² · f_s, where C_oss is in farads (F).

Conduction loss is separate: P_cond = I_rms² · R_on, where I_rms is the root-mean-square device current in amperes (A) and R_on is the on-resistance in ohms (Ω). The total is P_total = P_cond + P_sw + P_rr + P_C. At low f_s the conduction term dominates and is roughly constant. Above some frequency the switching terms rise in proportion to f_s, so P_total has a minimum at a best frequency. Plain PWM pushed past that minimum gets hot faster than the magnetics shrink.

Soft switching: ZVS and ZCS

Soft switching moves the device transition to a zero. Zero-voltage switching (ZVS) turns the device on only after its voltage has fallen to zero. Zero-current switching (ZCS) turns the device off only after its current has fallen to zero. In both cases the voltage-current overlap is removed, so the ½·V·I·t term for that transition goes to zero.

f_0 = 1 / (2π · √(L_r · C_r))

f_0 is the natural resonant frequency in hertz (Hz); L_r is the resonant inductance in henries (H); C_r is the resonant capacitance in farads (F). The tank exchanges energy at this frequency, and the switch is timed to its zero crossings.

For ZVS, a small resonant current flows before the gate pulse. It charges and discharges the device capacitance until the switch voltage reaches zero. Then the antiparallel body diode conducts and clamps the voltage at about zero. The gate pulse arrives while the diode is conducting, so the channel turns on with V ≈ 0. The body diode carries the current for that short wait.

For ZCS, the resonant inductor holds the current down. A resonant pulse brings the switch current to zero before the gate is removed. The device turns off with I = 0, and the voltage rises only afterwards. The price is a higher circulating current and a tank that must be tuned to the load.

Resonant families and frequency control

A resonant converter replaces the PWM cell with a tank plus a switch. Four families cover most hardware. A series load-resonant converter puts the L-C tank in series with the load; the tank current is sinusoidal. A parallel load-resonant converter places the tank across the load; it can hold output at light load but carries more circulating current. A resonant-switch or quasi-resonant converter puts a small L_r and C_r around the switch, so the switch sees a zero crossing every cycle while the rest of the circuit still looks like a PWM cell. An LLC converter uses two inductors and one capacitor; it is common in laptop, phone and TV adapters because it gives ZVS on the primary switches.

The control variable changes. A buck sets V_o = D·V_s with duty ratio D; a resonant converter sets its gain by the ratio f_s/f_0. Moving f_s away from f_0 lowers the gain. The gain curve also depends on the tank quality factor Q, so at light load the usable range shrinks. This makes the regulation range narrower than a buck's: an LLC may hold output over a 2:1 input range, not from 0 to V_s.

Only a few universities derive the full gain curves. For most exams, know the loss comparison and the ZVS/ZCS definitions. If a paper asks for the gain, it usually gives the curve or asks for f_0 and the operating region.

Worked example

Question. A MOSFET in a hard-switched converter blocks 200 V and carries 5 A. It switches at 100 kHz. The voltage-current overlap times are t_r = 100 ns at turn-on and t_f = 100 ns at turn-off, and the overlap is triangular. The on-resistance is 0.1 Ω. Take 5 A as the rms device current for conduction. (a) Find the switching loss. (b) Find the conduction loss. (c) The circuit is changed to ZVS, which removes the turn-on overlap but leaves the turn-off overlap. Find the new total loss and compare with the hard-switched total.

  1. List the data: V = 200 V, I = 5 A, f_s = 100 kHz = 1×10⁵ Hz, t_r = 100 ns = 1×10⁻⁷ s, t_f = 100 ns = 1×10⁻⁷ s, R_on = 0.1 Ω.
  2. Switching loss: P_sw = ½ × V × I × (t_r + t_f) × f_s. First V × I = 200 × 5 = 1000 W. Then t_r + t_f = 200 ns = 2×10⁻⁷ s. So P_sw = ½ × 1000 × 2×10⁻⁷ × 1×10⁵ = 10 W.
  3. Conduction loss: P_cond = I² × R_on = 5² × 0.1 = 25 × 0.1 = 2.5 W.
  4. Hard-switched total: P_total = P_sw + P_cond = 10 + 2.5 = 12.5 W.
  5. With ZVS the turn-on overlap t_r is removed. Only turn-off overlap remains: P_off = ½ × V × I × t_f × f_s = ½ × 200 × 5 × 1×10⁻⁷ × 1×10⁵ = 5 W.
  6. New total: P_ZVS = P_off + P_cond = 5 + 2.5 = 7.5 W. The saving is 5 W, or 40 % of the hard-switched loss.

Answer. P_sw = 10 W, P_cond = 2.5 W, and after ZVS the total falls from 12.5 W to 7.5 W.

Exam traps

  • Using V·I·t·f_s without the ½ doubles the switching loss. The ½ comes from the triangular overlap, not from a duty ratio.
  • ZVS removes the turn-on overlap, not the conduction loss. The MOSFET still carries current, so I²R_on stays.
  • ZCS is a turn-off condition. It does not make the switch carry zero current during the on-time, and it does not clamp the off-state voltage to zero.
  • The tank frequency is 1/(2π√(L_r C_r)), not 1/√(L_r C_r). Missing 2π gives an answer in rad/s.
  • A resonant converter's gain is not D·V_s. Do not use the buck formula when the control variable is switching frequency.

Analyze In the figure the gate pulse arrives at 2.5 µs, but the switch voltage has already reached 0 V at 2 µs. What makes the voltage zero, and why is this ZVS? 4 marks

Analyze Why is the regulation range of an LLC converter narrower than that of a buck converter? 4 marks

The LLC gain is set by f_s/f_0 and the tank quality factor Q, not by duty ratio. Moving f_s far from resonance lowers the gain and limits power. At light load Q is small and the gain curve flattens. A buck can use any duty from 0 to 1, but an LLC needs a frequency window and a tank designed for the load.

Understand What is the role of the body diode in ZVS? 3 marks

Before the gate pulse, the resonant current charges and discharges C_oss. When the voltage reaches zero, the body diode conducts and clamps it. The gate pulse can then arrive while the diode holds V near zero, so the channel turns on at zero voltage.

3.8 Design of inductors, transformers and capacitors

Design converts the ripple formulas into hardware: core, turns, air gap, wire and capacitor. The converter chapter gives L and C; design fixes peak current, peak flux, current density and temperature rise so nothing saturates or burns. Every phone charger is this step. Done right, the converter meets ripple at 90 % efficiency; done wrong, the inductor saturates on the first overload.

You need: Chapter 3.3 and Chapter 3.4 for L, C and ripple current, Chapter 3.7 for skin effect and high-frequency loss, and Unit 1, Switching losses, thermal design and heat sinks, for thermal resistance.

Inductor: peak current, turns and air gap

The inductor starts from L, the average current Iavg and the peak-to-peak ripple ΔIL. The core must carry the peak, not the average. Sizing on the average lets the core saturate every switching cycle.

I<sub>pk</sub> = I<sub>avg</sub> + ΔI<sub>L</sub>/2

Ipk is the peak winding current in A; Iavg the average current in A; ΔIL the peak-to-peak ripple in A.

N = L · I<sub>pk</sub> / (B<sub>max</sub> · A<sub>e</sub>)

N is the number of turns; L the inductance in H; Ipk the peak current in A; Bmax the allowed peak flux density in T (about 0.3 T for ferrite); Ae the effective core area in m² from the datasheet.

Round N up to a whole number, then recompute B = L·Ipk/(N·Ae) to confirm B stays below Bmax. Fewer turns means more flux.

ℓ<sub>g</sub> = µ<sub>0</sub> · N² · A<sub>e</sub> / L

ℓg is the total air-gap length in m (valid when the gap dominates core reluctance); µ0 = 4π × 10−7 H/m; N the turns; Ae in m²; L in H.

Most energy sits in the air gap, so the gap sets both L and the saturation current. An ungapped ferrite core cannot give both the required L and a useful saturation current: turns needed for L make Isat small; cutting turns to raise Isat drops L.

Wire comes from a current density J of 2 to 4 A/mm²: copper area Acu = Irms/J, where Irms is the RMS winding current. At 100 kHz the skin depth in copper is about 0.21 mm, so use strands under about 0.4 mm, or litz wire (Chapter 3.7). Thicker strands waste copper: current crowds to the surface and AC resistance climbs.

Transformer: turns ratio and window area

The turns ratio comes first, from the gain formula. A forward converter gives Vo = D · Vs · Ns/Np, so the duty cycle at nominal input fixes the ratio. In a flyback the ratio sets the reflected voltage and the leakage-inductance spike.

The window must hold both windings. With fill factor ku about 0.4, NpAp + NsAs ≤ kuAw, where Ap and As are bare copper areas and Aw the window area from the datasheet. If it does not fit, take a bigger core, not thinner wire that overheats.

Core loss grows with frequency and flux swing ΔB. About 0.3 T is the ferrite saturation ceiling. At 100 kHz the loss curve sets the working flux: often a peak of 0.10 to 0.15 T, not saturation.

A flyback transformer is a gapped inductor that also isolates, so the gap rule applies. A forward transformer must reset its core every cycle.

Capacitor: ESR before capacitance

Capacitance comes from the converter ripple formula, but output ripple has two parts: charge on C and drop across equivalent series resistance (ESR).

ΔV<sub>o</sub> ≈ ΔI<sub>L</sub> · ( ESR + 1/(8 f<sub>s</sub> C) )

ΔVo is output ripple in V; ΔIL inductor ripple in A; ESR in Ω; fs switching frequency in Hz; C in F. The 8 holds for a buck converter in continuous conduction.

At 100 kHz with an electrolytic, ESR usually dominates: a 100 µF part with 100 mΩ ESR gives more ripple than the capacitance term. Choose C for the capacitance term, then check ESR ≤ (ΔVo − capacitive part)/ΔIL.

Two more checks. RMS ripple current through the capacitor is about ΔIL/√12 and must stay under the datasheet rating; electrolytic life halves for every 10 °C rise. Ceramic parts have low ESR and suit high frequency, but capacitance falls under DC bias, so read the value at working voltage.

Losses and temperature rise

Add copper loss Irms²Rac (Rac is above Rdc because of skin and proximity effect) to core loss from the curve. Multiply total by thermal resistance Rth from the datasheet: ΔT = Ptotal · Rth. Keep the winding below its insulation class limit with margin for a hot Indian summer inside a sealed box.

Worked example

Question. Design the inductor for the buck converter of Chapter 3.3: Vs = 50 V, Vo = 20 V, Io = 4 A, fs = 100 kHz, L = 100 µH, ripple ΔIL = 1.2 A. The core has Ae = 1.0 cm² = 1.0 × 10−4 m² and Bmax = 0.3 T. (a) Peak current. (b) Turns and resulting flux. (c) Air gap. (d) Copper area and wire diameter for J = 4 A/mm².

  1. Peak current: Ipk = Io + ΔIL/2 = 4 + 0.6 = 4.6 A. The core must carry 4.6 A, not 4 A.
  2. Turns before rounding: N = L·Ipk/(Bmax·Ae) = (100 × 10−6 × 4.6)/(0.3 × 1.0 × 10−4) = 4.6 × 10−4 / 3.0 × 10−5 = 15.33.
  3. Round up to 16 turns. Check: B = 4.6 × 10−4/(16 × 1.0 × 10−4) = 0.2875 ≈ 0.29 T, below 0.3 T.
  4. Gap: ℓg = µ0N²Ae/L = (4π × 10−7 × 256 × 1.0 × 10−4)/(100 × 10−6) = 3.22 × 10−4 m = 0.32 mm.
  5. Copper area: Acu = Io/J = 4 A / 4 A/mm² = 1.0 mm².
  6. Single-wire diameter: d = √(4A/π) = √(4 × 1.0/π) = 1.13 ≈ 1.1 mm, above twice the 0.21 mm skin depth. Use about eight strands of 0.40 mm (8 × π/4 × 0.40² = 1.0 mm²), or litz wire of the same copper area.

Exam traps

  • Sizing the core on average current instead of peak. The core saturates at the top of every ripple, inductance collapses and switch current spikes.
  • Choosing C from ΔIL/(8fsC) alone. Ripple is usually set by ESR × ΔIL, so the part meets the value on paper and fails on the bench.
  • Forgetting that the air gap stores energy. An ungapped ferrite core has high inductance but saturates at small current, so the design works at no load and fails at full load.
  • Using DC resistance for loss at 100 kHz. Skin and proximity effect can make Rac several times Rdc, so the winding runs hotter than calculated.
  • Mixing units: Ae in cm² (1 cm² = 1 × 10−4 m²) and L in µH. Convert both to m² and H before dividing.

Apply Find the skin depth in copper at 100 kHz and state the strand thickness you would use. 4 marks

Understand Why does the window area limit a transformer design? 3 marks

Copper for both windings must fit in the window with a fill factor of about 0.4, so NpAp + NsAs ≤ kuAw. If it does not fit, a larger core is needed, otherwise the wire is too thin and the winding overheats.

Analyze A flyback transformer is built on an ungapped ferrite core. Explain what fails. 4 marks

Without a gap the core reluctance Rc is small, so saturation current Isat = Bmax·Ae·Rc/N is only a fraction of an amp. Primary current reaches it before the end of the on-time, inductance collapses, and current then rises limited only by leakage inductance, giving a large spike and switch failure.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Industry uses the area-product method to pick a core in one step: compare Ae·Aw with the value the design needs. Planar magnetics, with windings etched on a PCB, and litz wire are standard above 100 kHz. Wide band-gap devices push switching to hundreds of kilohertz, which shrinks L and C but makes core loss and AC resistance the limiting factors.

Takeaways

  1. A chopper sets average DC output by duty cycle, not by dissipating excess voltage as heat.
  2. A linear regulator is simple and quiet but wastes (1 − efficiency) of input power as heat; an SMPS chops and filters for 70–95 % efficiency with ripple and EMI penalties.
  3. The buck converter gives V_o = D·V_s in CCM, and its inductor ripple sets the inductance and output-capacitor requirement.
  4. The boost converter gives V_o = V_s/(1 − D) in CCM, so the output is always higher than the input and D must stay away from 1.
  5. The buck-boost converter inverts the output and gives V_o = −V_s·D/(1 − D); the Cuk converter gives the same gain magnitude with smoother port currents.
  6. A flyback stores energy in the transformer during on-time and delivers it during off-time; a forward converter delivers during on-time and must reset the core.
  7. High switching frequency shrinks magnetics but raises switching loss; resonant soft switching reduces loss by forcing zero voltage or zero current before a transition.
  8. Inductor and transformer design keeps peak flux density, current density and temperature inside limits; capacitor ESR often sets the real output ripple.

Glossary

Air gap
A non-magnetic gap in a core that increases energy storage and prevents saturation.
Boost converter
A step-up DC-DC converter whose ideal CCM gain is V_o = V_s/(1 − D).
Buck converter
A step-down DC-DC converter whose ideal CCM gain is V_o = D·V_s.
Buck-boost converter
An inverting converter whose ideal CCM gain is V_o = −V_s·D/(1 − D).
Capacitor ESR
Equivalent series resistance; it makes ripple current produce ripple voltage and internal heating.
Chopper
A power electronic switch or circuit that repeatedly connects and disconnects a DC source to a load to control average voltage or current.
Continuous conduction mode
CCM: inductor current never falls to zero during a switching period.
Cuk converter
A converter with capacitor energy transfer, gain magnitude D/(1 − D), inverted output and low port current ripple.
Current-limit control
A chopper control method that switches off at a set maximum current and switches on again when current falls to a lower limit.
Discontinuous conduction mode
DCM: inductor current stays at zero for part of each switching period.
Dropout voltage
The minimum input-to-output voltage difference a linear regulator needs to keep regulating.
Duty cycle
The ratio D = T_on/T of switch on-time to switching period, ranging from 0 to 1.
EMI
Electromagnetic interference, unwanted high-frequency noise that switching converters can conduct or radiate.
Flyback converter
An isolated converter that stores energy in transformer magnetizing inductance during on-time and delivers it during off-time.
Forward converter
An isolated converter that transfers energy through the transformer during on-time and must reset the core each cycle.
Linear regulator
A regulator that uses a pass transistor in its linear region to drop excess input voltage as heat.
Pass transistor
The series transistor in a linear regulator that carries load current and dissipates the unused voltage.
Reset winding
An extra transformer winding that returns magnetizing energy to the source and resets core flux in a forward converter.
Ripple
The small periodic variation on a DC output caused by switching and finite energy storage.
Snubber
A small R-C or R-D network across a switch or diode that limits voltage or current spikes and shapes switching trajectories.
Switched-mode power supply
A power supply that chops the input and uses inductors, capacitors and a controller to regulate output with high efficiency.
Switching frequency
f_s = 1/T, the number of on-off cycles per second, in hertz.
Variable-frequency control
A chopper control method that changes f_s while keeping on-time or off-time fixed, so D and average voltage change.
Zero-current switching
ZCS: switching a device when its current is near zero.
Zero-voltage switching
ZVS: switching a device when its voltage is near zero.
