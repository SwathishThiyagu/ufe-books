1.1 Power diodes; static and switching characteristics

A power diode is a two-terminal semiconductor switch. In forward bias it conducts with a small voltage drop; in reverse bias it blocks with tiny leakage. Its static V–I curve gives the on-state drop and reverse current. Its switching curve shows charge stored in the junction during turn-off. This chapter defines static and dynamic characteristics, reverse recovery time, peak reverse current and charge. It compares the real diode with an ideal switch. Power diodes appear in rectifiers, choppers, inverters and AC controllers.

You need: the basic PN junction diode from semiconductor devices, including forward bias, reverse bias and the depletion region.

Structure and static V–I curve

A power diode has three layers: a heavily doped p+ anode, a lightly doped n− drift region, and a heavily doped n+ cathode. The drift region is wide because it must support the reverse voltage. The anode is terminal A and the cathode is terminal K. The diode current is iD and the diode voltage is vD, positive from A to K.

In forward bias, vD must exceed a cut-in voltage VF before current rises sharply. For a silicon power diode, VF is roughly 0.8 V to 1.2 V, depending on current. Above cut-in, a useful model is vD = VF + iD rd, where rd is the slope resistance. In reverse bias, only a small reverse saturation current IS flows until avalanche breakdown at VBR.

An ideal switch has zero on-state voltage, infinite off-state resistance, zero switching time and no loss. A real diode fails all four: it has VF, finite rd, leakage IS, breakdown VBR, and stored charge that delays turn-off. The static curve is measured slowly, so it shows only conduction and blocking, not switching.

v<sub>D</sub> = V<sub>F</sub> + i<sub>D</sub> r<sub>d</sub>

vD is forward diode voltage in volts (V); VF is cut-in voltage in volts (V); iD is forward current in amperes (A); rd is slope resistance in ohms (Ω).

Dynamic turn-on and turn-off

Turn-on has a forward recovery time tfr. It is the delay from applying forward voltage until the diode reaches a specified forward drop. It is short in most power diodes. During this time, charge builds in the drift region and the voltage briefly overshoots.

Turn-off is slower because stored charge must be removed. When the external circuit reverses the current, the diode still conducts for a short time. The current crosses zero, reaches a peak reverse value IRRM, then returns to near zero after reverse recovery time trr. The area under the reverse current is the reverse recovery charge Qrr. A triangular approximation gives Qrr = ½ IRRM trr.

Split trr into ta, from zero crossing to peak reverse current, and tb, from peak back to zero. The softness factor is S = tb / ta. A large S means gentle recovery and lower current slope. The average reverse-recovery loss is Prr = Qrr Vs fs, where Vs is the blocking voltage and fs is the switching frequency.

Q<sub>rr</sub> = ½ I<sub>RRM</sub> t<sub>rr</sub>

Qrr is reverse recovery charge in coulombs (C); IRRM is peak reverse recovery current in amperes (A); trr is reverse recovery time in seconds (s).

P<sub>rr</sub> = Q<sub>rr</sub> V<sub>s</sub> f<sub>s</sub>

Prr is average reverse-recovery power loss in watts (W); Qrr is charge in coulombs (C); Vs is blocking voltage in volts (V); fs is switching frequency in hertz (Hz).

Diode types and where they sit

Power diodes are named by their recovery and conduction behaviour: standard recovery, fast-recovery and Schottky. Standard diodes are used at line frequency. Fast-recovery diodes are used when switching is fast. Schottky diodes have a low forward drop and very short recovery, but lower voltage ratings.

A one-line map of converters: a rectifier changes AC to DC, a chopper changes DC to DC, an inverter changes DC to AC, and an AC controller changes AC to AC. The circuits belong to later units. See Unit 2, Controlled and Uncontrolled Rectifiers for diode rectifiers, and Unit 3, DC-DC Converters for choppers. See Unit 4, DC-AC Inverters and Unit 5, AC-AC Converters for the other two.

Worked example

Question. A diode carries 10 A. Its reverse recovery time is 2 μs and its peak reverse recovery current is 8 A. Treat the recovery charge as a triangle and find Qrr. If the diode blocks 300 V and switches at 20 kHz, find the average reverse-recovery power loss using Prr = Qrr Vs fs.

  1. Use the triangular charge model: Qrr = ½ × IRRM × trr.
  2. Put IRRM = 8 A and trr = 2 μs = 2 × 10−6 s.
  3. Qrr = 0.5 × 8 A × 2 × 10−6 s = 8 × 10−6 C = 8 μC.
  4. Use Vs = 300 V and fs = 20 kHz = 20 × 103 Hz.
  5. Prr = 8 × 10−6 C × 300 V × 20 × 103 Hz = 48 W.

Answer. Qrr = 8 μC and Prr = 48 W.

Exam traps

  • Do not add trr to turn-on time. Reverse recovery is a turn-off effect.
  • Qrr is charge in coulombs or microcoulombs, not current. IRRM is the current peak.
  • The softness factor is S = tb / ta, not the total recovery time.
  • Cut-in voltage VF is not the full on-state voltage at high current; include the iD rd term.

1.2 Thyristor (SCR): structure, V-I characteristics, two-transistor model

An SCR, also called a thyristor or silicon controlled rectifier, is a four-layer P-N-P-N switch. With the anode negative it blocks in reverse whatever the gate does. With the anode positive it blocks until a small gate pulse starts conduction; then it latches on and the gate loses control. The latch works because two internal transistors feed each other. SCRs are used where a circuit must switch large AC or DC power slowly and reliably: controlled rectifiers, AC voltage controllers, battery chargers and HVDC links. This chapter explains the layers, the V-I curve and the two-transistor model that predicts turn-on.

You need: power diode blocking and conduction from Chapter 1.1, and the transistor current gain α.

Four layers, three junctions

An SCR is a single silicon crystal with four layers: P1, N1, P2 and N2. The outer P1 layer is the anode A. The outer N2 layer is the cathode K. The gate G is a metal contact on the inner P2 layer. Three PN junctions form between the layers: J1 at P1-N1, J2 at N1-P2, and J3 at P2-N2. Four layers give three junctions, but only three terminals come out: anode, cathode and gate.

With anode positive and cathode negative, J1 and J3 are forward biased, but J2 is reverse biased. J2 blocks current, so the SCR stays off. This is forward blocking. With anode negative, J1 and J3 are reverse biased and the SCR blocks in reverse. The gate contact injects a small current into P2. That current is the trigger; it does not carry the main load current.

V-I characteristics and the three regions

Plot anode-to-cathode voltage V_AK on the horizontal axis and anode current I_A on the vertical axis. In forward blocking, I_A is a small leakage current and the curve is nearly flat until V_BO, the forward breakover voltage. At V_BO, J2 avalanches and the SCR turns on even without gate current. A gate pulse turns it on earlier, at a lower V_AK.

After turn-on, the SCR enters forward conduction. The curve rises steeply at a low voltage drop, typically about 1 V to 2 V. The external circuit, not the SCR, sets the current. The gate can be removed and the SCR stays on because the two internal transistors now sustain each other.

Two current thresholds describe this latch. Latching current I_L is the minimum anode current just after turn-on that keeps the SCR on when the gate pulse ends. Holding current I_H is the minimum anode current that keeps an already conducting SCR on. I_L is larger than I_H. In reverse blocking, the curve shows only leakage until reverse breakdown, which is avoided.

Two-transistor model and the turn-on condition

Split the four layers into two transistors. T1 is PNP: emitter P1 (anode), base N1, collector P2. T2 is NPN: collector N1, base P2 (gate), emitter N2 (cathode). T1 collector connects to T2 base. T2 collector connects to T1 base. This closed loop gives regeneration.

Let α₁ = I_C1/I_A be the common-base current gain of T1, and α₂ = I_C2/I_K be that of T2. I_A is anode current; I_K is cathode current; I_G is gate current. KCL gives I_K = I_A + I_G. T1 collector current α₁I_A + I_CBO1 feeds T2 base. T2 collector current α₂I_K + I_CBO2 feeds T1 base. I_CBO1 and I_CBO2 are small leakage currents.

I_A = (α₂ I_G + I_CBO1 + I_CBO2) / [1 − (α₁ + α₂)]

I_A is anode current in A; I_G is gate current in A; α₁ and α₂ are the common-base current gains of T1 and T2; I_CBO1 and I_CBO2 are the collector-base leakage currents in A.

When α₁+α₂ is well below 1, the denominator is positive and large. A small gate current produces only a small anode current. When α₁+α₂ approaches 1, the denominator approaches zero. The loop gain reaches one, each transistor drives the other harder, and anode current rises until the external circuit limits it. The SCR latches. A gate pulse only starts this process. After latch, gate current is not needed and cannot stop conduction.

Worked example

Question. In the two-transistor model of an SCR, α₁ = 0.6 and α₂ = 0.3. Find α₁ + α₂. Can the SCR stay on after the gate pulse? Repeat if α₂ rises to 0.45.

  1. First case: α₁ + α₂ = 0.6 + 0.3 = 0.90. The loop gain is less than 1, so the regenerative action dies out. The SCR cannot stay on after the gate pulse; it returns to forward blocking.
  2. Second case: replace α₂ by 0.45. α₁ + α₂ = 0.6 + 0.45 = 1.05. The loop gain reaches and slightly exceeds 1, so the two-transistor loop sustains conduction. The SCR stays on after the gate pulse, with current limited by the external circuit.

Answer. The sums are 0.90 (no latch) and 1.05 (latches); the SCR stays on only in the second case.

Exam traps

  • Gate current only starts conduction; it cannot turn off an SCR. Removing the gate pulse does not stop an on SCR; turn-off is commutation, covered later.
  • Latching current I_L is larger than holding current I_H. They are anode current thresholds at turn-on and turn-off, not gate ratings.
  • Do not confuse forward breakover voltage V_BO with reverse breakdown voltage. V_BO turns the SCR on without a gate; reverse breakdown damages the device.
  • In the two-transistor model, α₁+α₂=1 is the ideal turn-on boundary. Gate current starts the loop, but after latching the gate loses control.

1.3 Thyristor turn-on and gate triggering circuits

A thyristor is a latch: once anode current exceeds the latching value, the gate loses control. Turn-on needs a small local trigger to start that latch. Gate triggering is the usual method because it is fast and controllable. A gate circuit must deliver a steep pulse with enough voltage and current at the gate-cathode terminals, then remove the pulse after latching. The same idea appears in a dimmer, a battery charger and an HVDC valve.

You need: the PNPN structure, the two-transistor model and latching current from Chapter 1.2.

Five ways a thyristor turns on

An SCR in forward blocking has two stable states: off and on. Five physical actions can move it to on.

  • Gate: a positive current into the gate-cathode junction injects carriers. The two internal transistors regenerate and the SCR latches. This is the normal controlled method.
  • Thermal: high junction temperature raises leakage current. At a critical temperature the leakage alone can trigger the device. This is unwanted turn-on; heat sinks and thermal design prevent it.
  • Light: photons absorbed near a junction create electron-hole pairs. A light-triggered thyristor has a small optical gate; it is used in high-voltage HVDC valves because it isolates control from the high-voltage side.
  • Forward-voltage breakover: if anode voltage reaches the forward breakover voltage VBO, avalanche multiplication triggers the SCR. This stresses the device and is not a normal control method.
  • dv/dt: a fast rise of anode voltage charges the junction capacitance. The displacement current C dv/dt acts like gate current and can false-trigger the SCR. See Chapter 1.7 for snubbers.

Gate turn-on is preferred because the designer chooses the instant; the other four are either slow, destructive or accidental.

Gate-cathode path and pulse shape

The gate-cathode path is a PN junction, so its V-I curve is like a diode: current rises sharply after a knee. Two datasheet numbers define triggering. VGT is the gate-cathode voltage that turns on every device of that type at a stated temperature and anode voltage. IGT is the corresponding gate current. They are minimum values for reliable turn-on, not maximum ratings.

The series resistor sets the gate current. If a trigger source Vs drives the gate through Rs, then Rs = (Vs − VGT) / IGT. The resistor must carry the pulse current; its peak power is IGT² Rs and its average power is lower by the duty ratio.

Pulse shape matters. A gate pulse should rise quickly, often below 1 µs, and stay on long enough for anode current to exceed the latching current IL. Typical pulse widths are 10 µs to 50 µs. A long DC gate current wastes power and can heat the gate. The pulse is removed after latching; holding current is the lower current at which an already-on SCR turns off.

Trigger circuits: R, RC, UJT and pulse transformer

Four gate trigger circuits appear in exams.

  • R triggering: a resistor from a DC supply to the gate. It is simple and cheap. It applies continuous gate current, wastes power and gives no isolation. Use it only in low-cost small SCR circuits.
  • RC triggering: a capacitor charges through a resistor; when the control switch closes, the capacitor discharges into the gate through a resistor. The capacitor gives a short pulse and can also shift phase in AC controllers.
  • UJT relaxation oscillator: a UJT has an emitter and two bases. With base supply VBB, the emitter peak voltage is VP = η VBB + VD, where η is the intrinsic standoff ratio and VD is a diode drop. The capacitor charges through R until VP; the UJT fires, discharges the capacitor, and a voltage pulse across base-1 resistor R1 triggers the SCR. Some syllabi call this a relaxation oscillator.
  • Pulse transformer: a small transformer couples the control pulse to the gate. It isolates the control circuit from the power circuit and can invert polarity. The core cannot pass a long DC pulse, so the pulse width is limited; use a short pulse or a pulse train.

Worked example

Question. A 12 V trigger source drives an SCR gate. The datasheet gives V_GT = 1.5 V and I_GT = 100 mA. Find the series gate resistance R_s and the peak power lost in it when the gate pulse is on.

  1. Convert the gate current: I_GT = 100 mA = 0.100 A.
  2. Voltage across the series resistor: V_s − V_GT = 12 V − 1.5 V = 10.5 V.
  3. Use R_s = (V_s − V_GT) / I_GT = 10.5 V / 0.100 A = 105 Ω.
  4. Peak pulse power in R_s: P = I_GT² R_s = (0.100 A)² × 105 Ω = 1.05 W.

Answer. The series gate resistance is 105 Ω and its peak pulse power is 1.05 W.

Exam traps

  • V_GT and I_GT are minimum trigger values, not absolute maxima. Driving exactly at these values may fail at low temperature; use a margin.
  • The gate resistor sees peak pulse power I²R, not average DC power. A 1.05 W pulse in a 0.25 W resistor can still fail if the pulse is long or repeated fast.
  • Latching current and holding current are different. Latching is the anode current needed to keep the SCR on when the gate is removed; holding is the lower current at which an on SCR turns off.
  • dv/dt turn-on is not caused by gate current. It is junction-capacitance displacement current; a snubber or softer anode voltage slows dv/dt.
  • A pulse transformer blocks DC. A single long gate pulse saturates it; use a short pulse or a pulse train.

1.4 Commutation techniques: natural and forced

Commutation is the act of turning off a conducting SCR. The anode current must fall below the holding current, and the device must stay reverse-biased long enough to regain forward blocking. In AC circuits the supply voltage does this at every zero crossing; that is natural commutation. In DC circuits an external circuit must force the current down; that is forced commutation. These ideas decide how rectifiers, choppers and inverters release a conducting thyristor, so every converter designer needs them.

You need: Chapter 1.2 for holding current and latching, and Chapter 1.3 for turn-on and gate pulses.

Why an SCR needs commutation

An SCR latches on when gate current starts regeneration. Once anode current exceeds the latching current, the gate loses control. The SCR stays on while anode current is above the holding current IH. It turns off only when anode current falls below IH and the anode-cathode voltage stays negative for a minimum time. That time lets stored charge in the four layers clear. The process is commutation.

  • Natural commutation happens when the source is AC. At the end of each half cycle the supply current passes through zero, the SCR reverse-biases, and it blocks again. Some syllabi call this line commutation. Class F is the line-commutated case.
  • Forced commutation happens when the source is DC. The supply current does not fall by itself, so a capacitor, an inductor or a separate pulse must push the SCR current to zero and apply reverse voltage. Classes A to E are forced methods.

Circuit turn-off time and device turn-off time

The circuit gives a reverse-bias window tc. The device needs a minimum tq from its datasheet. Successful commutation requires tc > tq. If tc is shorter, the SCR has not recovered and may turn on again when forward voltage returns. Bringing current to zero is only the start; the SCR must stay reverse-biased for longer than tq.

tc = C × V / I

tc is the circuit turn-off time in seconds; C is the commutating capacitance in farads; V is the capacitor voltage at the start of commutation in volts; I is the load current in amperes.

On a waveform, anode current falls to zero and anode voltage goes negative for tc. The negative part must extend past tq. tc is set by the circuit; tq is set by the device and its junction temperature. A larger capacitor or a higher capacitor voltage lengthens tc, but it also stresses the SCR.

Forced commutation classes

Forced commutation circuits are named by how they create the reverse-bias window. Class A self-commutation uses a series resonant load. The load current is a damped sine and crosses zero on its own. Class B resonant pulse uses an LC circuit that sends a reverse pulse through the SCR. Class C complementary uses two SCRs and a capacitor; turning on one SCR reverse-biases the other. Class D auxiliary uses a second SCR to connect a charged capacitor across the main SCR. Class E external pulse uses a separate voltage or current pulse. Class F line is natural commutation from an AC supply.

Worked example

Question. A capacitor commutation circuit has C = 1 μF charged to 200 V. The load current is 5 A. The SCR datasheet gives tq = 30 μs. Find the circuit turn-off time tc = C × V / I. Is commutation successful?

  1. Convert capacitance: C = 1 μF = 1 × 10−6 F.
  2. Write the given values: V = 200 V and I = 5 A.
  3. Substitute in tc = C × V / I = (1 × 10−6 F × 200 V) / 5 A.
  4. Compute the numerator: 1 × 10−6 × 200 = 200 × 10−6 = 2 × 10−4 C.
  5. Divide by 5 A: tc = 2 × 10−4 / 5 = 4 × 10−5 s = 40 μs.
  6. Compare with tq: 40 μs > 30 μs, so the reverse-bias window is long enough.

Answer. tc = 40 μs; since 40 μs > 30 μs, commutation is successful.

Exam traps

  • Natural commutation cannot turn off a DC-fed SCR; the DC current never crosses zero by itself.
  • Bringing current to zero is not enough. The SCR must stay reverse-biased for longer than tq.
  • Do not confuse tc (circuit turn-off time) with tq (device turn-off time). tc comes from C, V and I; tq comes from the datasheet.
  • Class B is resonant pulse, not load commutation; class A is load or self-commutation.
  • In a capacitor commutation circuit, use the capacitor voltage after charging, not the supply voltage, in tc = C × V / I.

1.5 TRIAC, DIAC and GTO

A TRIAC and a DIAC together make a simple bidirectional AC switch. The TRIAC carries current in both half-cycles; the DIAC gives a clean trigger pulse at a set voltage. They appear in lamp dimmers, table-fan regulators and small AC motor controls. A GTO is different: it is a thyristor that a negative gate pulse can turn off, so it suits DC and high-power switching where an SCR would need extra commutation. The structures, V-I curves, triggering and limits follow.

You need: Chapter 1.2 for SCR latching and Chapter 1.3 for gate triggering.

TRIAC: two SCRs in antiparallel

A TRIAC is a three-terminal AC switch. Its terminals are main terminal 1 (MT1), main terminal 2 (MT2) and gate (G). It works like two SCRs connected in antiparallel between MT2 and MT1. Some syllabi call it a bidirectional triode thyristor.

In the positive half-cycle, MT2 is positive with respect to MT1. The upper SCR can turn on and current flows from MT2 to MT1. In the negative half-cycle, MT1 is positive, so the lower SCR turns on and current flows from MT1 to MT2. The gate near MT1 supplies trigger current to whichever SCR is forward biased.

The TRIAC latches like an SCR. After turn-on it stays on until the current falls below the holding current, which happens near the zero crossing in a 50 Hz supply. Its V-I curve therefore has forward and reverse blocking regions and conduction regions in both directions. One TRIAC replaces two SCRs in a lamp dimmer or a table-fan regulator.

Triggering quadrants and the DIAC

Four triggering quadrants exist. A quadrant is set by two signs: the polarity of MT2 with respect to MT1, and the polarity of the gate with respect to MT1. In quadrant I, MT2 is positive and the gate is positive. In quadrant II, MT2 is positive and the gate is negative. In quadrant III, MT2 is negative and the gate is negative. In quadrant IV, MT2 is negative and the gate is positive.

A TRIAC is not equally sensitive in all four quadrants. Datasheets give the gate current needed in each quadrant; many designs use quadrants I and III, but the correct choice comes from the datasheet. A gate pulse with the wrong polarity may need more current or may fail to trigger.

A DIAC is a two-terminal bidirectional trigger diode. It has no gate. It blocks current in both directions until the voltage across it reaches the breakover voltage VBO, often about 30 V. Then it conducts and its voltage drops, so it delivers a sharp current pulse. In a simple lamp dimmer, an RC network charges until the DIAC reaches VBO, the DIAC fires, and the pulse triggers the TRIAC. The resistor sets the delay and so sets the firing angle.

The DIAC V-I curve is symmetric: it blocks up to +VBO and −VBO, then enters a negative-resistance conduction region. Gate drive details for TRIACs are in Chapter 1.7.

GTO: gate turn-off thyristor

A GTO (gate turn-off thyristor) is a four-layer PNPN device like an SCR, but its gate and cathode are interdigitated. The gate therefore touches the whole cathode area and can pull stored charge out of the device. It turns on with a positive gate pulse, like an SCR. It turns off with a negative gate pulse, which an SCR cannot do.

The turn-off gain compares anode current to peak negative gate current.

β<sub>off</sub> = I<sub>A</sub> / I<sub>G(off)</sub>

βoff is the turn-off gain (no unit); IA is the anode current just before turn-off in amperes; IG(off) is the peak negative gate current in amperes.

A GTO has a low βoff, often only a few. A 100 A anode current with βoff = 5 needs a 20 A negative gate pulse. That large pulse is the price of gate turn-off.

The GTO V-I curve is similar to the SCR curve in forward blocking and forward conduction. Many GTOs are asymmetric and have limited reverse blocking, so check the datasheet. GTOs were used in traction, large DC drives, HVDC and pulsed power. IGBTs and IGCTs have replaced them in many new designs. Gate drive circuits are covered in Chapter 1.7.

Worked example

Question. A TRIAC phase controller runs from a 230 V rms, 50 Hz supply with a 100 Ω resistive load. The firing angle is α = 90°. Find the rms load voltage and the load power. Use Vrms = Vs sqrt(1 − α/π + sin(2α)/(2π)).

  1. Write the data: Vs = 230 V rms, f = 50 Hz, R = 100 Ω, and α = 90° = π/2 rad.
  2. Compute α/π = (π/2)/π = 0.5. Also 2α = π, so sin(2α) = sin(π) = 0, and sin(2α)/(2π) = 0.
  3. Vrms = 230 × sqrt(1 − 0.5 + 0) = 230 × sqrt(0.5) = 162.6 V.
  4. P = Vrms² / R = (162.6)² / 100 = 264.5 W.

Answer. The rms load voltage is 162.6 V and the load power is 264.5 W.

Exam traps

  • Do not call MT1 and MT2 anode and cathode. Their roles swap every half-cycle; only the gate stays the control terminal.
  • A TRIAC is not equally sensitive in all four quadrants. A gate pulse with the wrong polarity may need more current or may fail to trigger; use the datasheet values.
  • A DIAC does not set the load power by itself. It only fires at VBO; the RC time constant sets the firing angle.
  • GTO turn-off is not a small gate signal. The turn-off gain is low, so the negative gate pulse can be a large fraction of the anode current.
  • With an inductive load, a TRIAC can fail to commutate at zero current because the voltage rises fast and retriggers it. A snubber or a different device may be needed.

1.6 Power BJT, MOSFET and IGBT

A power BJT, a power MOSFET and an IGBT are three-terminal controlled switches. Each has a control terminal that decides whether the main path conducts. The BJT needs base current, the MOSFET needs gate voltage, and the IGBT needs gate voltage but conducts like a BJT. They exist because no single device is best at every voltage and current. You meet them in switch-mode power supplies, inverters, motor drives and electric-vehicle chargers. Choosing between them is mostly a question of conduction loss at your operating current.

You need: the diode static curve in Chapter 1.1, and the basic BJT and MOSFET from an earlier electronics course.

Power BJT: current-controlled switch

A power BJT is an NPN transistor built vertically: a heavily doped n+ emitter, a thin p base, a lightly doped n− drift region, and an n+ collector substrate. The drift region blocks voltage when the device is off. The base current controls the collector current. In the active region the collector current is set by the base current and the current gain.

I_C = β I_B

I_C is the collector current in amperes, I_B is the base current in amperes, and β is the common-emitter current gain, dimensionless. Power BJTs have small β, often 5 to 20.

As a switch the BJT is either cut-off or in saturation. In cut-off the base is open and no current flows. In hard saturation both junctions are forward biased and the collector-emitter voltage falls to V_CE(sat), typically 1 V to 2 V. Before hard saturation the drift region is only partly filled with carriers; this quasi-saturation state has a higher V_CE and extra loss. A BJT needs continuous base current while on, so the driver burns power.

The weak point is the safe operating area. The base-emitter voltage falls as temperature rises, so a hotter spot in the die takes more current. That local current crowding can create a hot spot and secondary breakdown, which destroys the device even inside the rated power. Datasheets give a forward-bias SOA and a switching SOA. Keep the load line inside them.

Power MOSFET: voltage-controlled resistance

A power MOSFET is a vertical structure with an n+ source, a p body, an n− drift region and an n+ drain. A thin oxide isolates the gate. Gate voltage, not gate current, controls the drain current: the gate charges a capacitance. Once charged, the gate draws almost no steady current.

P_cond = I_D² R_DS(on)

P_cond is conduction loss in watts, I_D is drain current in amperes, and R_DS(on) is the on-state resistance in ohms at a stated gate voltage.

The on state is resistive. R_DS(on) has a positive temperature coefficient, so a hotter part of the die carries less current. This makes paralleling easy and removes secondary breakdown. The price is that conduction loss rises as I², so at high current the resistance hurts.

Every power MOSFET contains a body diode from the p body to the n drift region, antiparallel to the channel. In a bridge it conducts during dead time. It is a slow diode, so check its reverse recovery before relying on it.

Dynamic behaviour is set by gate charge Q_g and the Miller plateau. When the gate voltage reaches the threshold, the drain voltage starts to fall and the gate-drain capacitance holds the gate voltage almost flat. The driver must push charge through this plateau before the device is fully on. A larger plateau means slower switching and more switching loss.

IGBT and device selection

An IGBT, insulated-gate bipolar transistor, combines a MOS gate with a bipolar output. The gate sits over a p body as in a MOSFET. A p+ layer is added at the collector, so the drift region fills with carriers: conductivity modulation. The output behaves like a BJT, with a collector-emitter saturation voltage V_CE(sat).

The common equivalent circuit is a MOSFET driving the base of a PNP transistor. Gate voltage turns the MOSFET on, which supplies base current to the PNP, and the main current flows from collector to emitter. The gate still needs only a short charge pulse.

Turn-off has a tail current. Stored charge in the drift region must be removed, so the collector current falls slowly at the end. This tail adds turn-off loss and limits the switching frequency. Many IGBTs block little or no reverse voltage; the datasheet may show a small reverse rating, and an external diode handles reverse current. Some modules include a freewheeling diode.

Selection follows the loss curves. MOSFET loss is I²R, a curve through zero. IGBT loss is V_CE(sat) × I plus a small resistive part, a line with an offset. At low current the MOSFET wins; at high current the IGBT wins. The crossover is where I² R_DS(on) = V_CE(sat) I, so I_cross = V_CE(sat) / R_DS(on).

Worked example

Question. A power MOSFET has R_DS(on) = 0.1 Ω. An IGBT in the same circuit has V_CE(sat) = 2 V. Both carry a current I. (a) Find the conduction loss of each at 5 A and at 50 A. (b) Find the crossover current where the losses are equal. (c) State which device is better at each current.

  1. MOSFET conduction loss: P_M = I² R_DS(on). At 5 A, P_M = 5² × 0.1 = 25 × 0.1 = 2.5 W.
  2. IGBT conduction loss: P_I = V_CE(sat) × I. At 5 A, P_I = 2 × 5 = 10 W. The MOSFET is better at 5 A.
  3. At 50 A, P_M = 50² × 0.1 = 2500 × 0.1 = 250 W.
  4. At 50 A, P_I = 2 × 50 = 100 W. The IGBT is better at 50 A.
  5. Set the losses equal: I² R_DS(on) = V_CE(sat) I. Cancel one I: I = V_CE(sat) / R_DS(on) = 2 / 0.1 = 20 A.

Answer. At 5 A the MOSFET dissipates 2.5 W against 10 W for the IGBT; at 50 A the MOSFET dissipates 250 W against 100 W; the crossover is 20 A, so the MOSFET is better below 20 A and the IGBT above.

Exam traps

  • MOSFET conduction loss rises as I², not linearly. At 5 A a 0.1 Ω device loses 2.5 W, but at 50 A it loses 250 W. Do not use one loss figure for all currents.
  • The IGBT has a voltage offset V_CE(sat); it does not become a short circuit at low current. Its loss is roughly V_CE(sat) × I, not I²R.
  • Do not ignore the MOSFET body diode. In a half bridge it carries freewheeling current during dead time and its reverse recovery can cause voltage spikes.
  • Do not ignore the IGBT tail current when estimating turn-off loss; it adds a slow current fall that overlaps with rising voltage.
  • Quasi-saturation is not hard saturation. In quasi-saturation V_CE is higher, so the BJT dissipates more than the datasheet V_CE(sat) value suggests.

1.7 Ratings, snubber circuits and gate drive circuits

A power device dies when one of its limits is crossed, even for a microsecond. Ratings are those limits, printed on the datasheet as absolute maximum values. Protection keeps the device inside them: a snubber is a small R-C or L network beside the switch that slows the voltage and current edges, and a gate drive delivers a clean, isolated pulse to the gate. Phone chargers, inverters and 400 V traction drives use both.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for diode reverse recovery, Chapter 1.2 for SCR voltage and current ratings, and Chapter 1.6 for MOSFET and IGBT gate behaviour.

Ratings and derating

Absolute maximum ratings are values beyond which the device is damaged, not values at which it works well. A 600 V MOSFET run at 600 V fails; at 400 V it survives the overshoot every switching circuit produces.

Voltage ratings come in pairs. VDRM is the repetitive peak off-state voltage an SCR blocks every cycle; VDSM is the higher transient value it may survive once. For a MOSFET, VDSS is the drain-source breakdown; for a diode, VRRM is the reverse value. A non-repetitive rating is always higher than the matching repetitive one.

Current ratings are average on-state current IT(AV) or IF(AV) at a stated case temperature, RMS current, repetitive peak current, and surge current ITSM — the single 50 Hz half-cycle current the device survives in a fault. The surge value falls as the pulse gets longer.

Two dynamic ratings decide protection. di/dt is the highest rate at which anode or drain current may rise at turn-on; in an SCR a fast rise turns on only a small patch near the gate and burns a hot spot. dv/dt is the highest rate at which off-state voltage may rise; it drives current through junction capacitance and can turn an SCR or MOSFET on by itself. Gate charge Qg is the charge the driver must supply on every pulse.

Derating means running below the ratings. A common rule keeps steady voltage under 60–70 % of the blocking rating, current under 60–75 % of the rated value, and junction temperature at least 20 °C below Tj(max). Derating pays for overshoot, hot ambient and a cheap heat sink. Junction temperature is worked out in Chapter 1.8.

Devices in series share voltage only if their leakage and switching match; a resistor across each shares the DC voltage and an R-C shares the transient. Parallel MOSFETs share current well because on-resistance rises with temperature, pushing current away from the hotter device. BJTs and SCRs share poorly, because their on-state drop falls with temperature; derate them heavily.

Snubber circuits

A snubber is a small network mounted close to the switch. It absorbs switching energy, slows one edge, and moves the loss into a resistor. It never removes loss.

The turn-off snubber is Rs and Cs in series across the switch; some syllabi call it the R-C snubber. At turn-off the load current diverts into Cs, so the switch voltage rises at dv/dt = I/Cs. A larger Cs gives a slower rise and less stress. The capacitor ends charged to the off-state voltage V. At the next turn-on it discharges through Rs and the switch, and Rs limits the peak discharge current to about V/Rs.

E = ½ C<sub>s</sub> V²   and   P<sub>Rs</sub> = ½ C<sub>s</sub> V² f<sub>s</sub>

E is the energy stored in the snubber capacitor in joules; Cs is its capacitance in farads; V is the off-state voltage across the switch in volts; fs is the switching frequency in hertz; PRs is the average power dissipated in Rs in watts when the capacitor is fully discharged once per cycle.

The resistor must be rated for this continuous pulse power, not its average DC value. Wirewound or thick-film types are used. Place Rs and Cs with short leads at the switch terminals so the snubber loop has small area and low inductance.

The turn-on snubber is a series inductor Ls in the main current path. At turn-on it slows the current rise to di/dt ≈ V/Ls. At turn-off the inductor current cannot stop instantly, so a freewheeling diode gives it a path; without that diode the switch sees a destructive voltage spike.

Gate drive circuits

A gate driver turns a weak logic pulse into the current and voltage the gate needs. It must deliver the gate charge Qg quickly, give a clean edge, keep the power-side voltage away from the control side, and hold the gate firmly off when the switch must stay off.

Isolation is the first design choice. An optocoupler passes the pulse as light and gives several kilovolts of isolation, but it is slower and needs its own isolated supply. A pulse transformer is fast and cheap but cannot pass a DC level, so it suits narrow pulses. Isolated driver ICs with capacitive or magnetic barriers are common today. The gate return wire is separate from the power return, twisted with the gate wire, to keep the loop small.

I<sub>g,peak</sub> ≈ (V<sub>drive</sub> − V<sub>GE</sub>) / (R<sub>g</sub> + R<sub>driver</sub>)

Ig,peak is the peak gate current in amperes; Vdrive is the driver supply voltage in volts; VGE is the gate-emitter voltage at the start of the pulse in volts; Rg is the external gate resistor in ohms; Rdriver is the driver's internal resistance in ohms.

The gate resistor Rg sets the switching speed. A small Rg switches fast with low switching loss but produces high dv/dt, ringing and EMI. A large Rg is quiet but slow and hot. Pick Rg from the datasheet gate-charge curve, then check the waveform on the bench.

An IGBT in a bridge needs a negative off bias, typically −5 V to −8 V. When the opposite switch turns on, its rising collector voltage pushes Miller current through the gate-collector capacitance of the off device; that current raises the gate voltage, and without negative bias the IGBT can turn on and short the DC link. A negative bias plus a low-impedance turn-off path or a Miller clamp prevents this.

  1. With the DC link off, apply a pulse train and check the gate-emitter voltage: negative when off, +15 V when on.
  2. Measure the rise time and the peak gate current across Rg; compare with Qg from the datasheet.
  3. Run into a resistive load and check that turn-off overshoot stays under the derated voltage rating.
  4. Repeat at full load and full temperature; look for gate ringing and false turn-on at the Miller plateau.

Worked example

Question. A turn-off snubber has Cs = 10 nF across a switch that blocks 400 V. The switching frequency is 20 kHz. Find the energy stored in Cs at turn-off and the average power dissipated in Rs if all that energy is lost once per cycle.

  1. Write the data with units: Cs = 10 nF = 10 × 10⁻⁹ F, V = 400 V, fs = 20 kHz = 20 × 10³ Hz.
  2. Energy stored: E = ½ Cs V², with V² = 400² = 1.6 × 10⁵ V².
  3. E = 0.5 × 10 × 10⁻⁹ F × 1.6 × 10⁵ V² = 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ J = 0.8 mJ.
  4. That energy is dumped into Rs at the next turn-on, once per cycle: PRs = E × fs = 0.8 × 10⁻³ J × 20 × 10³ Hz = 16 W.
  5. Rs must also limit the peak discharge current to V/Rs, and it needs a 16 W continuous pulse rating, not a 2 W carbon resistor.

Answer. The snubber capacitor stores 0.8 mJ at turn-off, and Rs must dissipate about 16 W.

Exam traps

  • A snubber does not save energy. It moves loss from the switch to the snubber resistor; total heat in the enclosure stays about the same.
  • The R-C snubber is useless without Rs. With Cs alone the capacitor discharges through the switch at turn-on, limited only by stray resistance.
  • di/dt and dv/dt are easy to swap in the exam. di/dt is a turn-on limit on current rise; dv/dt is an off-state limit on voltage rise.
  • The snubber resistor is rated for pulse power at fs, not DC power. A 16 W average pulse load destroys a 5 W resistor.
  • Rg is not a free choice. Too small gives ringing, EMI and false turn-on; too large gives low noise, high switching loss and a hot device.

1.8 Switching losses, thermal design and heat sinks

Every power switch loses energy twice: once while it turns on and once while it turns off. During those short transitions, voltage across the device and current through it are both nonzero, so they multiply into heat. Switching loss is that heat per second. Thermal design carries it from the tiny junction to the air without letting the junction exceed its limit. This matters in switch-mode power supplies, motor drives, inverters and chargers, including the charger brick for a phone or an electric scooter.

You need: Chapter 1.6 for device conduction models and Chapter 1.7 for snubber energy and ratings.

Switching loss comes from overlap

An ideal switch drops zero volts when on and carries zero current when off. A real switch spends a short time between these states. During turn-on, current rises while voltage is still high. During turn-off, voltage rises while current still flows. The product v × i is power, so each transition gives a pulse of heat.

Use the triangular approximation for one transition: E = 0.5 × V × I × t. E is energy in joules, V is blocking voltage in volts, I is load current in amperes, and t is transition time in seconds. Turn-on energy is E_on = 0.5 × V × I × t_on. Turn-off energy is E_off = 0.5 × V × I × t_off.

E_on = 0.5 × V × I × t_on,   E_off = 0.5 × V × I × t_off

E_on and E_off are transition energies in J; V is blocking voltage in V; I is load current in A; t_on and t_off are transition times in s.

Average switching power multiplies the energy of all transitions in one cycle by the number of cycles per second.

P_sw = (E_on + E_off) × f_s = 0.5 × V × I × (t_on + t_off) × f_s

P_sw is average switching power in W; f_s is switching frequency in Hz; E_on and E_off are transition energies in J.

Use the total transition time inside the bracket. Do not use t_on alone when t_off is nonzero. A 10 kHz switch has 10,000 turn-on events and 10,000 turn-off events each second. Shorter edges (smaller t_on, t_off) reduce P_sw, but faster edges increase dv/dt and di/dt; snubbers and layout then matter, as in Chapter 1.7. Switching loss rises linearly with f_s. At high frequency it can exceed conduction loss.

Add conduction loss to get total heat

While the device is fully on, it still has a voltage drop. For a diode, thyristor, BJT or IGBT, P_cond = V_on × I × D. V_on is the on-state drop in volts, I is the average current while the device conducts (the on-interval) in amperes, and D is duty ratio, the fraction of the period the device conducts. If the current you have is already the average over the whole period, do not multiply by D again.

For a MOSFET, use its channel resistance: P_cond = I_rms² × R_on × D. I_rms is the rms current during the on-interval in amperes and R_on is on-resistance in ohms. If the rms you have is taken over the whole period, D must not be multiplied in again. In a switch-mode converter, D is set by the conversion ratio; see Unit 3, DC-DC converters.

P_total = P_cond + P_sw

P_total is total device loss in W; P_cond is conduction loss in W; P_sw is switching loss in W.

P_total is the heat that the thermal path must remove. Gate-drive loss is small and is not repeated here. For a first design, use the worst-case load current and the highest ambient temperature expected. Use the same units before adding: watts with watts, amperes with amperes.

Thermal path: junction to ambient

Heat is generated at the semiconductor junction. It travels through the device case, through the mounting interface, into the heat sink, and then to the surrounding air. Each step has a thermal resistance Rθ, measured in °C/W. A thermal resistance of 1 °C/W means 1 W of heat raises that layer's temperature by 1 °C above the next layer.

The three series resistances are Rθ_JC from junction to case, Rθ_CS from case to heat sink, and Rθ_SA from heat sink to ambient. Some texts write Rθ_sa or R_th(h-a) for the same heat-sink-to-ambient resistance. Thermal resistances add like series resistors. The steady-state junction temperature follows from that sum.

T_j = T_a + P_total × (Rθ_JC + Rθ_CS + Rθ_SA)

T_j is junction temperature in °C; T_a is ambient temperature in °C; P_total is total loss in W; Rθ_JC, Rθ_CS and Rθ_SA are thermal resistances in °C/W.

To choose a heat sink, rearrange the formula: Rθ_SA ≤ (T_j,max − T_a)/P_total − Rθ_JC − Rθ_CS. T_j,max is the datasheet limit, often 125 °C or 150 °C. A lower Rθ_SA means a larger heat sink, more fins, or more airflow. The case-to-heat-sink value depends on the thermal pad and mounting pressure.

For a short pulse, the junction does not heat instantly. Datasheets give transient thermal impedance Zθ_JC(t), which is lower than Rθ_JC for times shorter than the thermal time constant. Use Zθ_JC for single pulses and for repeated pulses with low duty ratio. For steady-state design, use the sum of the Rθ values.

Worked example

Question. A MOSFET switches V = 200 V and I = 10 A. Turn-on time is 1 μs and turn-off time is 2 μs. Switching frequency is 10 kHz. Ambient is 40 °C, junction-to-case Rθ_JC = 1.0 °C/W, case-to-heat-sink Rθ_CS = 0.5 °C/W. The junction limit is 125 °C. Find the switching power loss and the maximum heat-sink thermal resistance Rθ_SA allowed. Ignore conduction loss for this calculation.

  1. Total transition time: t_on + t_off = 1 μs + 2 μs = 3 μs = 3 × 10⁻⁶ s.
  2. Switching energy per cycle: E_cycle = 0.5 × 200 V × 10 A × 3 × 10⁻⁶ s = 0.003 J.
  3. Switching power: P_sw = E_cycle × f_s = 0.003 J × 10,000 Hz = 30 W.
  4. Allowed total thermal resistance: Rθ_total = (T_j,max − T_a)/P_sw = (125 − 40)/30 = 85/30 = 2.833 °C/W.
  5. Already have Rθ_JC + Rθ_CS = 1.0 + 0.5 = 1.5 °C/W.
  6. Rθ_SA ≤ 2.833 − 1.5 = 1.33 °C/W. Choose a heat sink rated at or below 1.33 °C/W.

Answer. P_sw = 30 W and the heat sink must have Rθ_SA ≤ 1.33 °C/W.

Exam traps

  • Adding only t_on and forgetting t_off. Add t_on + t_off in the total transition time. Leaving out t_off always underestimates P_sw, and it halves the answer when t_on = t_off.
  • Putting f_s in kHz. 10 kHz is 10,000 Hz; a factor of 1000 error changes P_sw by 1000.
  • Adding thermal resistances in parallel. Rθ_JC, Rθ_CS and Rθ_SA are in series, so they add.
  • Thinking a lower Rθ_SA means a smaller heat sink. Lower resistance needs more metal surface, more fins, or more airflow.
  • Using Rθ_JC for a short pulse. Use Zθ_JC(t) from the transient curve for pulse widths well below the thermal time constant.

1.9 Wide band-gap devices: SiC and GaN

A wide band-gap device is a power switch made from a semiconductor with a large energy gap between its valence and conduction bands. Silicon carbide (SiC) and gallium nitride (GaN) are the two common ones. The wide gap gives a high critical electric field, so a thin drift layer can block high voltage. That means lower on-resistance and faster switching than silicon at the same rating. You meet these devices in electric-vehicle chargers, solar inverters and server power supplies.

You need: Chapter 1.6 for MOSFET behaviour and Chapter 1.7 for gate drive.

Bandgap sets the critical field

The bandgap E_g is the energy needed to lift an electron from the valence band to the conduction band. It is measured in electron volts (eV). One eV is the energy an electron gains when it crosses a potential of 1 volt. Silicon has E_g = 1.1 eV. SiC has 3.2 eV and GaN has 3.4 eV. A wider gap means the material can withstand a stronger electric field before avalanche breakdown. That limit is the critical electric field E_c, in megavolts per centimetre (MV/cm). Silicon breaks down near 0.3 MV/cm. SiC reaches about 2.2 MV/cm and GaN about 3.0 MV/cm.

t_drift ≈ 2 V_B / E_c

t_drift is drift layer thickness in cm; V_B is blocking voltage in V; E_c is critical field in V/cm. A higher E_c gives a thinner drift layer for the same V_B.

A thinner drift layer has less resistance when the device is on. Heat also leaves SiC easily because its thermal conductivity is about two to three times that of silicon. GaN conducts heat less well, so it is often grown on a SiC or silicon substrate. The figure plots E_c against E_g for the three materials.

SiC MOSFET and GaN HEMT

The SiC MOSFET looks like a silicon power MOSFET but uses a vertical structure. Current flows from drain at the bottom to source at the top through a drift region. It is normally off: zero gate voltage blocks. Turn-on gate voltage is often +15 V to +18 V; a negative bias of a few volts holds it off safely. The gate oxide is thin, so the gate voltage must never exceed the datasheet absolute maximum. A SiC MOSFET has a body diode. That diode can conduct in the reverse direction, but it has reverse recovery charge Q_rr.

The GaN HEMT (high electron mobility transistor) is lateral: source, gate and drain sit on the top surface. A thin AlGaN layer on GaN creates a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG) — a sheet of mobile electrons with high mobility. Most power GaN HEMTs are enhancement-mode, normally off. The gate window is narrow, often 0 to 6 V, and a spike above the limit destroys the gate. GaN has no p-n body diode. When the source-to-drain voltage reverses, the channel conducts in reverse with a higher drop than a diode. Do not assume GaN has a simple body diode.

  • Read these datasheet lines: V_GS absolute max; R_DS(on) at 25 °C and 125 °C; Q_g; C_oss; R_thJC.

Switching, losses and comparison

Both SiC and GaN are unipolar: only majority carriers conduct. There is no minority-carrier storage, so turn-off is fast and Q_g is small. Gate drive loss is P_gate = Q_g × V_GS × f_sw, where Q_g is gate charge in coulombs, V_GS gate voltage in volts and f_sw switching frequency in hertz. A small Q_g lets you switch at 100 kHz or more with little drive loss. The penalty is a fast dv/dt — rate of change of voltage, often tens of volts per nanosecond. Fast dv/dt charges parasitic capacitances and causes ringing, EMI and false turn-on. Layout and gate resistors matter.

Worked example

Question. A SiC MOSFET has R_DS(on) = 20 mΩ. A silicon MOSFET of the same voltage rating has R_DS(on) = 100 mΩ. Both carry a drain current of 20 A. (a) Find the conduction loss in each. (b) Find the ratio P_Si / P_SiC. (c) State one reason to choose SiC.

  1. Conduction loss is P = I² R_DS(on).
  2. For SiC: P_SiC = (20 A)² × 0.020 Ω = 400 × 0.020 = 8 W.
  3. For Si: P_Si = (20 A)² × 0.100 Ω = 400 × 0.100 = 40 W.
  4. Ratio: P_Si / P_SiC = 40 W / 8 W = 5, so P_Si : P_SiC = 5 : 1.
  5. Reason: SiC has lower conduction loss and can switch at higher frequency because it is unipolar with low Q_g.

Answer. P_SiC = 8 W, P_Si = 40 W, ratio P_Si/P_SiC = 5; SiC gives lower conduction loss and faster switching.

Exam traps

  • Wide band-gap devices are not drop-in replacements. A SiC MOSFET may need +15 V and negative off-bias; a GaN HEMT may accept only 6 V. Check the datasheet absolute maximum before power-up.
  • Do not assume GaN has a simple body diode. Reverse conduction is through the channel, with a higher drop and no Q_rr in the silicon sense.
  • Fast dv/dt can destroy a device even when average current and voltage look safe. Probe gate-source ringing; add a gate resistor and tighten the layout.
  • R_DS(on) rises with temperature. Use the hot value, not the 25 °C value, for loss calculations.
  • Reverse recovery of a SiC MOSFET body diode still exists. It is smaller than a silicon PiN diode but not zero; it can cause losses and ringing.

Takeaways

  1. A power diode is a switch with a fixed forward drop and a turn-off delay set by reverse recovery, so it matters most at high frequency.
  2. An SCR is a four-layer latch: gate current only starts the regeneration, after which the anode current itself keeps the device on.
  3. A gate trigger circuit must give a fast-rising pulse of adequate current and voltage, not just a slow DC level, otherwise turn-on is local and lossy.
  4. Commutation is the circuit's job, not the device's: you must drop current below I_H and then hold reverse voltage for longer than t_q.
  5. TRIAC and DIAC make a cheap, phase-controlled AC switch, while GTO gives a thyristor-like device that can be turned off from the gate.
  6. Power BJT is current driven, MOSFET is voltage driven with a gate charge, and IGBT puts a MOSFET gate on a BJT output for high-voltage work.
  7. Ratings mean nothing without protection: snubbers limit dv/dt and di/dt, and gate drives must deliver clean, fast pulses with isolated returns.
  8. Switching loss and thermal resistance together decide the heat sink and the maximum switching frequency a design can survive.
  9. SiC and GaN switch faster and block more volts per unit area than silicon, but demand tighter gate drive, careful layout and attention to EMI.

Glossary

Anode, cathode, gate
The three terminals of an SCR: anode and cathode carry the main current, gate receives the control pulse.
Class A commutation
Load or self-commutation in which a resonant load current itself creates the zero crossing.
Commutation circuit
Network of L, C and auxiliary switches that forces the anode current of an SCR to zero in a DC circuit.
Critical electric field
Field strength at which avalanche breakdown begins; it is roughly ten times higher in SiC than in silicon.
di/dt rating
Maximum rate of rise of on-state current allowed at turn-on, set by how fast the conduction area can spread.
dv/dt rating
Maximum rate of rise of off-state voltage a device can withstand without turning on by itself.
Gate drive
Circuit that converts a control signal into the current or voltage pulse a device gate needs, usually with isolation.
GTO latching
The anode current above which a GTO can no longer be turned off by its gate.
Heat sink
Metal structure that carries heat from the device case to the surrounding air, characterised by its thermal resistance in °C/W.
Junction temperature
Temperature of the semiconductor chip itself; it must stay below the rated maximum, typically 125 °C to 175 °C.
Miller plateau
Flat part of the gate voltage waveform during turn-on, where gate charge is used to swing the drain voltage instead of raising V_gs.
Phase control
AC power control method in which the firing angle of a thyristor or TRIAC sets the portion of each half cycle delivered to the load.
Power diode
Two-terminal P-N junction device built with a thick, lightly doped drift layer so it can block high reverse voltage and carry large forward current.
Power electronics
Branch of electronics that converts and controls electric power using semiconductor switches operating as ON/OFF devices.
Reverse recovery
Delay in turning off a diode because stored minority carriers must be removed before it blocks reverse voltage.
Safe operating area (SOA)
Graph of permitted device current against voltage for a stated pulse width, inside which the device will not be damaged.
Schottky diode
Metal–semiconductor diode with no minority carriers, very fast switching and a low forward drop, but limited reverse voltage.
Snap-off
Abrupt end of reverse recovery, which produces a sharp current fall and a large voltage spike; a soft recovery avoids it.
Thyristor (SCR)
Four-layer P-N-P-N device that turns on when a gate pulse starts internal regeneration and then stays on until anode current falls below holding current.
TRIAC quadrants
Four combinations of MT2 and gate polarity in which a TRIAC can be triggered; sensitivity is poorest in the third quadrant.
Turn-off time t_q
Minimum time the SCR needs, after current zero, with reverse voltage applied, before it can block forward voltage again.
Wide band-gap semiconductor
Material such as silicon carbide or gallium nitride with a band gap well above silicon's 1.1 eV, giving a higher breakdown field.
α (current gain)
Fraction of emitter current that reaches the collector in a transistor; in the two-transistor model, regeneration needs α₁ + α₂ ≥ 1.
