1.1 Power diodes; static and switching characteristics

A power diode is the simplest power switch: it conducts when its anode is positive and blocks when it is negative, with no control terminal. It is not ideal: on, it drops about a volt; off, it leaks a little; and at turn-off it conducts backwards for a moment while its stored charge is removed. That reverse recovery decides which diode can work at which switching frequency. Power diodes sit in every rectifier and in the freewheeling paths of choppers and inverters.

You need: the PN junction diode (forward bias, reverse bias, depletion region).

Structure and the static V–I curve

A power diode has three layers: a heavily doped p+ anode (A), a lightly doped n− drift region and a heavily doped n+ cathode (K). The drift region is wide and lightly doped because it holds the reverse voltage. The current is iD and vD is measured from A to K.

Forward bias: almost no current flows until vD passes the threshold (cut-in) voltage VTO, about 0.7 V to 0.8 V for silicon. Above it the curve is nearly a straight line of slope 1/rd, because holes and electrons flood the drift region and lower its resistance (conductivity modulation). At rated current the drop is usually 1 V to 1.5 V.

vD = VTO + iD rd

where vD is the on-state voltage (V), VTO the threshold voltage (V), iD the forward current (A) and rd the slope resistance (Ω). The average conduction loss is then Pcond = VTO Iavg + rd Irms², with Iavg and Irms the average and rms diode currents.

Reverse bias: only a small leakage current IR flows until the breakdown voltage VBR. The datasheet's VRRM, the repetitive peak reverse voltage, lies below VBR. An ideal switch would have zero drop, zero leakage and zero switching time; the real diode has VTO and rd, IR, and a turn-off delay from stored charge.

Switching: forward and reverse recovery

Turn-on: for a short forward recovery time tfr the drift region is not yet flooded with carriers, so the voltage overshoots its steady value.

Turn-off: the circuit forces the current down at a rate di/dt set by the supply voltage and circuit inductance. The current passes zero, but stored charge keeps the junction conducting, so the current goes negative. After ta it reaches the peak reverse recovery current IRRM; then the junction blocks and the current returns to zero in tb. The reverse recovery time is trr = ta + tb, and the area of the negative pulse is the reverse recovery charge Qrr.

Qrr ≈ ½ IRRM trr

the triangle approximation, with Qrr in coulombs, IRRM in amperes and trr in seconds. Because the current falls at the circuit's di/dt until the peak, IRRM ≈ ta × di/dt.

The softness factor is S = tb / ta. A snappy diode (S well below 1) stops its reverse current abruptly, and the circuit inductance turns that into a voltage spike and ringing. Recovery also costs energy: the recovery current flows while the switch turning on still holds most of the supply voltage Vs, so the loss is about Prr ≈ Qrr Vs fs at switching frequency fs, and most of it heats the switch.

Worked example

Question. A diode carrying 10 A is turned off with di/dt = 8 A/µs; it has IRRM = 8 A and trr = 2 µs. Find ta, tb, the softness factor S and Qrr. If the diode blocks Vs = 300 V and switches at fs = 20 kHz, estimate the recovery loss Prr.

  1. Time to reach the reverse peak: ta = IRRM / (di/dt) = 8 A ÷ 8 A/µs = 1 µs.
  2. Recovery tail: tb = trr − ta = 2 µs − 1 µs = 1 µs, so S = tb / ta = 1 ÷ 1 = 1 (a soft diode).
  3. Charge: Qrr = ½ × IRRM × trr = 0.5 × 8 A × 2 × 10−6 s = 8 × 10−6 C = 8 µC.
  4. Loss: Prr ≈ Qrr Vs fs = 8 × 10−6 C × 300 V × 20 × 103 Hz = 48 W.

Answer. ta = 1 µs, tb = 1 µs, S = 1, Qrr = 8 µC and Prr ≈ 48 W.

Worked example 2

Question. The same diode conducts its 10 A for half of each cycle (duty ratio 0.5). It has VTO = 0.85 V and rd = 12 mΩ. Find its conduction loss and compare it with the 48 W of recovery loss. What changes if a fast-recovery diode with IRRM = 2 A and trr = 0.2 µs is used?

  1. On-state drop at 10 A: vD = 0.85 + 10 × 0.012 = 0.97 V.
  2. Average and rms currents: Iavg = 10 × 0.5 = 5 A; Irms² = 10² × 0.5 = 50 A².
  3. Pcond = VTO Iavg + rd Irms² = 0.85 × 5 + 0.012 × 50 = 4.25 + 0.60 = 4.85 W (check: 0.97 V × 10 A × 0.5 = 4.85 W).
  4. Fast-recovery diode: Qrr = 0.5 × 2 A × 0.2 µs = 0.2 µC, so Prr = 0.2 × 10−6 × 300 × 20 000 = 1.2 W.

Answer. Pcond = 4.85 W against Prr = 48 W; the fast-recovery diode cuts the recovery loss to 1.2 W.

Exam traps

  • Reverse recovery is a turn-off effect. Do not add trr to the turn-on time.
  • Qrr is a charge (µC), IRRM is a current (A). Qrr = ½ IRRM trr, not IRRM trr: forgetting the ½ doubles the answer (16 µC instead of 8 µC).
  • VTO is the threshold, not the drop at rated current. Using 0.85 V instead of 0.97 V at 10 A under-reads the conduction loss.

1.2 Thyristor (SCR): structure, V-I characteristics, two-transistor model

A thyristor, or silicon controlled rectifier (SCR), is a four-layer p-n-p-n switch with anode, cathode and gate. With the anode negative it blocks whatever the gate does. With the anode positive it blocks until a small gate current turns it on; then it latches and needs no gate current. It turns off only when the circuit brings its current to zero. Two internal transistors that drive each other explain the latch. SCRs switch large power in controlled rectifiers, AC voltage controllers, battery chargers and HVDC links.

You need: the bipolar transistor and its current gain, and the power diode of the first chapter.

Four layers, three junctions

An SCR is one silicon wafer with four layers P1, N1, P2 and N2. P1 is the anode A, N2 the cathode K, and the gate G is a contact on P2 near the cathode. The layers form three junctions: J1 (P1-N1), J2 (N1-P2) and J3 (P2-N2). N1 is the wide, lightly doped layer that holds the blocking voltage.

Anode positive, no gate current: J1 and J3 are forward biased but J2 is reverse biased, so only a small leakage current flows. This is the forward blocking state. Anode negative: J1 and J3 are reverse biased and the device is in reverse blocking. A gate pulse cannot turn it on in this state, and a gate current applied with the anode negative only increases the reverse leakage and heats the device, so the gate is not driven then.

V–I characteristic

Plot the anode current IA against the anode-cathode voltage VAK. The characteristic has three regions.

  • Reverse blocking (VAK negative): a small leakage current until the reverse breakdown voltage VBR, which damages the device.
  • Forward blocking (VAK positive, device off): a small leakage current until the forward breakover voltage VBO. With IG = 0, at VBO junction J2 breaks down and the device switches on by itself. A larger gate current lowers the breakover voltage.
  • Forward conduction (on): a steep line at a low drop of about 1 V to 2 V. The load and the supply, not the SCR, set the current.

Two anode currents describe the latch. The latching current IL is the minimum anode current that must be reached while the gate pulse is still present, so that the SCR stays on when the pulse ends. The holding current IH is the minimum anode current that keeps an SCR that is already on in conduction; below IH it turns off. IL is larger than IH, often two to three times. Both are anode currents, not gate ratings.

Two-transistor model

Cut the four layers diagonally into two transistors that share the middle layers. T1 is p-n-p (P1-N1-P2): emitter at the anode. T2 is n-p-n (N1-P2-N2): emitter at the cathode, base at the gate. The collector of T1 feeds the base of T2, and the collector of T2 feeds the base of T1. Each transistor supplies the other's base current; this loop is called regeneration.

Let α₁ and α₂ be the common-base current gains of T1 and T2, and ICBO1, ICBO2 their leakage currents. T1 collector current is α₁IA + ICBO1 and T2 collector current is α₂IK + ICBO2. Their sum is the anode current, and IK = IA + IG. Solving gives the anode current:

IA = (α₂ IG + ICBO1 + ICBO2) / (1 − (α₁ + α₂))

where IA is the anode current, IG the gate current, α₁ and α₂ the common-base gains and ICBO1, ICBO2 the leakage currents, all currents in amperes.

In silicon transistors α rises with current. At low current α₁ + α₂ is well below 1 and IA is small: the device blocks. Gate current raises the current, both α values rise, the denominator shrinks, and when α₁ + α₂ reaches 1 the loop sustains itself and only the external circuit limits IA. The SCR has latched; removing the gate current changes nothing.

Worked example

Question. In the two-transistor model of an SCR, IG = 20 mA and ICBO1 + ICBO2 = 10 µA. (a) At low current α₁ = 0.40 and α₂ = 0.45; find IA. (b) As the current grows the gains become α₁ = 0.48 and α₂ = 0.50; find IA again. (c) What happens when α₁ + α₂ reaches 1 in a circuit of 200 V and 20 Ω?

  1. (a) Denominator: 1 − (0.40 + 0.45) = 0.15. Numerator: 0.45 × 0.020 + 0.000 010 = 0.009 01 A.
  2. IA = 0.009 01 ÷ 0.15 = 0.0601 A = 60.1 mA.
  3. (b) Denominator: 1 − (0.48 + 0.50) = 0.02. Numerator: 0.50 × 0.020 + 0.000 010 = 0.010 01 A.
  4. IA = 0.010 01 ÷ 0.02 = 0.50 A: the same gate current now gives eight times as much anode current.
  5. (c) At α₁ + α₂ = 1 the denominator is zero: the device latches and the circuit sets the current, IA ≈ 200 V ÷ 20 Ω = 10 A.

Answer. IA = 60.1 mA, then 0.50 A; at α₁ + α₂ = 1 the SCR latches and carries about 10 A set by the load.

Worked example 2

Question. An SCR with latching current IL = 50 mA feeds a load of R = 20 Ω and L = 0.5 H from a 100 V DC supply. Find the minimum width of the gate pulse.

  1. After turn-on the current rises as i(t) = (V/R)(1 − e−Rt/L), with V/R = 100 ÷ 20 = 5 A and L/R = 0.5 ÷ 20 = 0.025 s.

Answer. The gate pulse must last at least about 251 µs; with a 10 µs pulse this SCR would turn off again when the pulse ends.

Exam traps

  • The gate cannot turn an SCR off. Removing the gate pulse after latching does nothing; turn-off needs the anode current below IH and reverse bias for long enough (commutation).
  • IL is larger than IH, not the other way round. Both are anode currents.
  • VBO (forward breakover, the device turns on) is not VBR (reverse breakdown, the device is damaged).
  • Drawing the SCR on in the third quadrant: the conduction branch is only in the first quadrant (VAK and IA positive).

1.3 Thyristor turn-on and gate triggering circuits

An SCR in forward blocking turns on when something starts its internal regeneration. Gate current is the method designers use, because it fixes the instant of turn-on; heat, excessive voltage and a fast voltage rise do it by accident, and light is used on purpose in HVDC valves. A trigger circuit must give a fast pulse with enough current, lasting until the anode current passes the latching current, within the gate's power limits. The R, RC and UJT circuits and the pulse transformer are the standard exam circuits.

You need: latching current and the two-transistor model of the SCR chapter.

Five ways to turn on an SCR

  • Gate triggering: a positive gate-cathode current injects carriers into P2, regeneration starts and the SCR latches. This is the normal, controlled method.
  • Forward-voltage triggering: raising VAK to VBO breaks down J2; it can damage the device, so it is not used for control.
  • dv/dt triggering: a fast rise of anode voltage drives a charging current Cj dv/dt through the capacitance of J2, which acts like gate current. Snubbers prevent it.
  • Temperature triggering: leakage rises steeply with junction temperature until it alone starts regeneration. Good heat sinking prevents it.
  • Light triggering: light on the gate region creates carriers. The light-activated SCR (LASCR) is fired through optical fibre in HVDC valves, isolating the control.

Gate ratings and the gate pulse

The gate-cathode path is a p-n junction, so its V–I curve is like a diode's and it varies from one device to the next. The datasheet gives VGT and IGT, the gate voltage and current that are sure to trigger every device of that type at a stated temperature; the drive must supply at least these, with margin for cold days. It also gives maximum limits: peak gate voltage and current, and peak and average gate power PGM and PG(AV). The operating point must lie between the ‘sure trigger’ values and these limits.

Rs = (Vs − VGT) / IGT

where Rs is the series gate resistor (Ω), Vs the trigger source voltage (V), VGT the gate trigger voltage (V) and IGT the gate trigger current (A). The resistor and the gate see the peak power only while the pulse is on; the average power is the peak power times the pulse duty ratio.

A good gate pulse rises in well under a microsecond with an initial current several times IGT (hard triggering), so the whole cathode turns on quickly. It must last until the anode current passes IL, which with an inductive load can take hundreds of microseconds. Firing circuits often send a burst of short pulses (a pulse train): it keeps a pulse transformer out of saturation, cuts gate power, and supplies a pulse whenever the SCR becomes forward-biased.

Trigger circuits: R, RC, UJT and pulse transformer

  • R triggering: on an AC supply, a resistor (with a diode) feeds gate current from the anode voltage itself. The SCR fires when the gate current reaches IGT, which can happen only while the supply is rising, so the firing angle can be varied from about 0° to 90° only.
  • RC triggering: a capacitor in the gate network charges through a variable resistor. The capacitor voltage lags the supply, so the gate threshold is reached later in the cycle; the firing angle can be varied from about 0° to nearly 180°.
  • UJT relaxation oscillator: a unijunction transistor has an emitter E and two bases B1 and B2. A capacitor C charges through R from VBB. When the emitter voltage reaches the peak point VP = η VBB + VD (η is the intrinsic stand-off ratio, typically 0.5 to 0.8, and VD ≈ 0.7 V), the UJT fires and discharges C through R1, giving a sharp pulse across R1 that triggers the SCR. The period is T ≈ R C ln(1/(1 − η)).
  • Pulse transformer: couples the trigger pulse to the gate while isolating the control circuit from the power circuit, and can drive two SCRs from one source. Its core cannot pass a long pulse without saturating, so it carries short pulses or a pulse train.

R must lie between two limits for the UJT to oscillate: below (VBB − VP)/IP so that it can supply the peak-point current IP, and above (VBB − VV)/IV so that the UJT can turn off at the valley point (VV, IV).

Worked example

Question. A 12 V trigger source drives an SCR gate. The datasheet gives VGT = 1.5 V and IGT = 100 mA. Find the series resistor Rs and the peak power in it. If the gate pulse lasts 50 µs and repeats at 1 kHz, find the average power in Rs.

  1. Voltage across Rs: 12 − 1.5 = 10.5 V.
  2. Rs = 10.5 V ÷ 0.100 A = 105 Ω.
  3. Peak power: IGT² Rs = 0.100² × 105 = 1.05 W.
  4. Duty ratio of the pulse: 50 µs × 1000 Hz = 0.05.
  5. Average power: 1.05 W × 0.05 = 0.0525 W = 52.5 mW, so a 0.25 W resistor is enough for this pulse train.

Answer. Rs = 105 Ω, peak power 1.05 W, average power 52.5 mW.

Exam traps

  • VGT and IGT are the values the drive must at least supply, not maximum ratings. The maximum limits are PGM, PG(AV) and the peak gate voltage and current.
  • R triggering cannot fire beyond 90°; RC triggering is needed for nearly 0° to 180°.
  • In T = R C ln(1/(1 − η)), using log base 10 gives 0.432 ms instead of 0.994 ms; the formula needs the natural log.
  • A pulse transformer cannot pass a long pulse: its core saturates. Use a short pulse or a pulse train.

1.4 Commutation techniques: natural and forced

Commutation is turning off a conducting SCR. Because the gate cannot do it, the circuit must bring the anode current below the holding current and then keep the SCR reverse-biased long enough for it to recover its blocking ability. In AC circuits the supply does this by itself every half-cycle (natural commutation). In DC circuits the current never falls by itself, so a capacitor, a resonant circuit or another switch must force it to zero (forced commutation). The one design rule behind all methods is that the circuit turn-off time must exceed the device turn-off time.

You need: holding current and the two-transistor model from the SCR chapter.

Natural and forced commutation

An SCR is latched while its anode current stays above the holding current IH: the two internal transistors keep each other on. To turn it off, the anode current must fall below IH, and the stored charge in the layers must then disappear before forward voltage returns.

  • Natural (line) commutation: with an AC supply the anode voltage reverses every half-cycle, the current falls to zero and the SCR is reverse-biased for the rest of that half-cycle. No extra parts are needed. It is used in controlled rectifiers, AC voltage controllers and cycloconverters. It is also called class F commutation.
  • Forced commutation: with a DC supply the current does not fall, so an external circuit, usually a charged capacitor, is switched to reverse-bias the SCR and divert its current. It is used in DC choppers and in SCR inverters. Classes A to E are forced methods.

Circuit turn-off time and device turn-off time

The device turn-off time tq (from the datasheet) is the time from current zero until the SCR can block forward voltage again; it is about 10 µs to 20 µs for fast (inverter-grade) SCRs and 50 µs to a few hundred microseconds for converter-grade ones, and it grows with junction temperature. The circuit turn-off time tc is the time for which the circuit actually holds the SCR reverse-biased after its current reaches zero. Commutation succeeds only if tc > tq, usually with a margin of 1.2 to 1.5. If tc is too short, the SCR turns on again as soon as forward voltage returns.

tc = C V / I

for a capacitor of C farads, charged to V volts, placed across the SCR to commutate a load current of I amperes that stays constant during commutation (true for an inductive load). The capacitor voltage, which is the reverse voltage on the SCR, falls linearly from −V to zero in tc seconds. Turned round, it gives the design rule C ≥ I tc / V.

Worked example

Question. An impulse-commutated (class D) chopper has a commutating capacitor C = 1 µF charged to 200 V. The load current, constant during commutation, is 5 A. The SCR has tq = 30 µs. Find tc and say whether commutation succeeds.

  1. Rate at which the load current discharges the capacitor: dv/dt = I / C = 5 A ÷ 1 × 10−6 F = 5 V/µs.
  2. The SCR voltage rises from −200 V to 0 at 5 V/µs: tc = C V / I = 1 × 10−6 × 200 ÷ 5 = 4 × 10−5 s = 40 µs.
  3. Compare: tc = 40 µs > tq = 30 µs, a margin of 40 ÷ 30 = 1.33.

Answer. tc = 40 µs, which exceeds tq = 30 µs, so the SCR turns off.

Worked example 2

Question. Design the commutating capacitor for a class D chopper on V = 220 V with a load current of 50 A. The SCR has tq = 30 µs; use a safety factor of 1.5.

  1. Required circuit turn-off time: tc = 1.5 × 30 µs = 45 µs.
  2. C ≥ I tc / V = 50 × 45 × 10−6 ÷ 220 = 10.2 × 10−6 F = 10.2 µF.
  3. Choose the next standard value, 12 µF, rated above 220 V.

Exam traps

  • Current zero is not turn-off. The SCR must also stay reverse-biased for longer than tq.
  • tc belongs to the circuit, tq to the device. A bigger capacitor lengthens tc; nothing in the circuit shortens tq, except keeping the junction cool.
  • Using tc = C V / I for class C with resistive loads: there tc = 0.693 R1 C. For R1 = 50 Ω and C = 1 µF that is 34.7 µs.
  • Class A is self or load commutation and class B is resonant pulse; do not swap them.
  • Natural commutation cannot turn off an SCR fed from DC: the DC current never reaches zero by itself.

1.5 TRIAC, DIAC and GTO

Three members of the thyristor family fill gaps the SCR leaves. A TRIAC conducts in both directions and is triggered by one gate, so a single device switches AC. A DIAC is a two-terminal trigger device that breaks over at about 30 V in either direction; it gives the TRIAC a clean, symmetric gate pulse but carries no load current. A GTO (gate turn-off thyristor) is turned off by a large negative gate current, so it needs no commutation circuit. TRIACs with DIACs sit in lamp dimmers and fan regulators; GTOs drove traction before IGBTs.

You need: the SCR structure, latching and holding current, and gate triggering.

TRIAC

A TRIAC (triode AC switch) behaves like two SCRs in inverse parallel on one chip with a common gate. Its terminals are main terminal 1 (MT1), main terminal 2 (MT2) and the gate G, which sits next to MT1; all voltages are measured from MT1. When MT2 is positive, current flows MT2 → MT1; when MT2 is negative, it flows MT1 → MT2. The V–I characteristic therefore has identical blocking and conduction branches in the first and third quadrants, with breakover at ±VBO.

It is triggered in four modes, named by the sign of MT2 and of the gate, both measured from MT1: I+ (MT2 +, G +), I− (MT2 +, G −), III− (MT2 −, G −) and III+ (MT2 −, G +). Sensitivity differs: I+ is the most sensitive, I− and III− need somewhat more gate current, and III+ is the least sensitive, so most circuits use I+ and III−, or a negative gate pulse in both halves. Like an SCR, a TRIAC turns off when its current falls below the holding current near each current zero.

With an inductive load the current lags the voltage, so when the TRIAC current reaches zero the supply voltage is already large and reappears across it very fast. If this commutating dv/dt exceeds the TRIAC's rating it turns on again and the load stays on. An RC snubber across the TRIAC slows the voltage rise. Most TRIACs are made for currents up to a few tens of amperes at 50 Hz.

DIAC and the dimmer circuit

A DIAC is a two-terminal, bidirectional trigger diode with no gate. It blocks in both directions until the voltage across it reaches its breakover voltage VBO, typically about 30 V; its voltage then falls by several volts (a negative-resistance region) and it passes a sharp current pulse. Its V–I curve is symmetric about the origin.

In a lamp dimmer a variable resistor R and a capacitor C form a phase-shifting network across the supply. Each half-cycle the capacitor charges until its voltage reaches VBO; the DIAC fires and dumps the capacitor charge into the TRIAC gate, and the TRIAC turns on for the rest of the half-cycle. A larger R gives a later firing angle α and a dimmer lamp. Because the DIAC is symmetric, the firing angle is the same in both half-cycles, so the load sees no DC component. The control of the load voltage itself is treated under AC voltage controllers.

GTO: gate turn-off thyristor

A GTO has the same p-n-p-n layers as an SCR, but the cathode is split into many narrow fingers surrounded by gate metal (interdigitated), and the p-n-p part has a lower gain. A positive gate pulse turns it on like an SCR. A large negative gate current pulls holes out of the P2 layer under every cathode finger, the n-p-n transistor leaves saturation, and the device turns off without any commutation circuit.

βoff = IA / IG(off)

where βoff is the turn-off gain (no unit), IA the anode current to be turned off and IG(off) the peak negative gate current, both in amperes. βoff is only about 3 to 5.

The low turn-off gain is the GTO's price: a 600 A GTO needs a negative gate pulse of 120 A to 200 A, from a low-impedance drive. Its on-state drop is somewhat higher than an SCR's, many GTOs block little reverse voltage, and they need a turn-off snubber to limit dv/dt. GTOs were used in traction and large drives; IGBTs and IGCTs have replaced them in new designs.

  • A TRIAC on an inductive load can fail to turn off because of the commutating dv/dt; the fix is a snubber, not a bigger gate pulse.

1.6 Power BJT, MOSFET and IGBT

A power BJT, a power MOSFET and an IGBT are switches that the control terminal turns both on and off. The BJT is driven by base current, the MOSFET by gate voltage, and the IGBT has a MOSFET gate with a bipolar output. They differ in drop, speed and voltage range, so the converter's voltage, current and frequency decide the choice. The MOSFET's switching intervals underlie every switching-loss and gate-drive calculation. They fill power supplies, inverters, motor drives and chargers.

You need: the transistor regions (cut-off, active, saturation) and the MOSFET threshold voltage.

Power BJT

A power BJT is a vertical n+ emitter, p base, n− drift, n+ collector structure. Off, its base-emitter junction is reverse-biased; on, enough base current saturates it, with VCE(sat) about 1 V to 2 V. Its gain β = IC / IB is only 5 to 20, so a 50 A switch needs several amperes of base current all the time. Between active and hard saturation lies quasi-saturation, with a higher VCE.

Its weak point is second breakdown: current crowds into a hot spot, which takes still more current and fails, even below rated power, so the safe operating area (SOA) bounds the allowed VCE–IC path. Slow removal of stored charge limits it to about 20 kHz, and it is now rarely chosen.

Power MOSFET and its dynamic behaviour

A power MOSFET is a vertical n+ source, p body, n− drift, n+ drain structure with an insulated gate. A gate-source voltage above the threshold VGS(th) inverts the p body and the channel conducts. Only majority carriers flow, so it switches in tens of nanoseconds. On, it is a resistance RDS(on), so Pcond = ID,rms² RDS(on). RDS(on) rises with temperature, which makes paralleling easy and removes second breakdown, and rises steeply with voltage rating, so silicon MOSFETs suit a few hundred volts (superjunction designs reach 600 V to 900 V). A body diode conducts reverse current.

The gate sees Cgs, Cgd (the Miller capacitance) and Cds; datasheets give Ciss = Cgs + Cgd, Coss = Cds + Cgd, Crss = Cgd and a gate charge curve. With a clamped inductive load, turn-on has four intervals:

  1. Turn-on delay td(on): vGS rises from 0 to VGS(th).
  2. Current rise tri: vGS rises to the plateau Vpl; iD rises to the load current while vDS stays at the supply voltage (the freewheeling diode still conducts).
  3. Voltage fall tfv (Miller plateau): vGS stays flat at Vpl while the gate current discharges Cgd; vDS falls to nearly zero. The charge needed is Qgd, so tfv = Qgd / Ig.
  4. vGS rises to the drive voltage and RDS(on) reaches its final low value. Turn-off runs the same intervals backwards.

Switching loss happens in the current-rise and voltage-fall intervals; a stronger gate current shortens them.

IGBT and choosing a device

An IGBT is a MOSFET structure with an extra p+ layer at the collector. When it conducts, this layer injects holes into the drift region (conductivity modulation), so the drop is VCE(sat) ≈ 1.5 V to 2.5 V almost independent of voltage rating. Its equivalent circuit is a MOSFET supplying the base current of a p-n-p transistor. The gate voltage (about +15 V) is held on while it conducts, but charge flows only at the edges. At turn-off the stored holes give a tail current that adds loss and limits switching to about 20 kHz to 50 kHz. The layers also form a parasitic thyristor that excess current can latch on (latch-up). Most IGBTs come with an inverse diode.

1.7 Ratings, snubber circuits and gate drive circuits

A power device fails as soon as one of its limits is crossed, even for microseconds. Datasheet ratings are those limits; protection keeps the device inside them. A snubber beside the switch limits the rise of voltage or current, a fuse clears faults before the surge limit is passed, and the gate drive gives clean, isolated, firm gate pulses.

You need: the turn-on and turn-off behaviour of the SCR, MOSFET and IGBT chapters.

Series devices need a resistor across each for steady voltage sharing and an R-C across each for sharing during switching; parallel SCRs need matched devices or small series inductors, while MOSFETs share naturally.

Snubber circuits

A turn-off (R-C) snubber is a capacitor Cs across the switch with a resistor Rs in series. At turn-off the load current I flows into Cs, so the switch voltage rises at dv/dt = I / Cs and the switch loss falls. At the next turn-on Cs discharges through Rs, which limits the peak to V / Rs; the discharge, about 3 Rs Cs, must finish within the shortest on-time. A diode across Rs (polarised snubber) lets Cs charge directly.

Cs = I / (dv/dt), PRs = ½ Cs V² fs

where Cs is in farads, I the current at turn-off (A), dv/dt the allowed rate of rise (V/s), V the off-state voltage (V), fs the switching frequency (Hz) and PRs the average power in Rs (W). The snubber moves loss from the switch to Rs; it does not remove it.

A turn-on snubber is a small inductor Ls in series with the switch. It limits the current rise to di/dt ≈ V / Ls, so Ls ≥ V / (di/dt rating). At turn-off its stored energy needs a path, a diode and resistor across Ls; without it the inductor produces a voltage spike across the switch. Thyristors usually need both: Ls for di/dt and R-C for dv/dt.

Gate drive circuits

A gate driver turns a logic signal into the gate current the device needs, isolates control from power, and holds the gate firmly off. The high-side switch of a bridge has its source or emitter moving with the output, so its drive needs isolation: an optocoupler (passes a DC level, needs an isolated supply), a pulse transformer (fast, but no long pulses) or an isolated driver IC. A bootstrap capacitor, charged while the low-side switch is on, is the cheap high-side supply.

Ig,peak ≈ (Von − Voff) / (Rg + Rdrv)

where Von and Voff are the driver's on and off voltages (V), Rg the external gate resistor and Rdrv the driver's output resistance (Ω). The driver must supply Qg in a few tens of nanoseconds, so peak currents of 1 A to several amperes are normal.

Rg sets the speed: small Rg gives fast edges and low loss but high dv/dt and ringing; large Rg is quiet but slow and lossy. When the other switch of a bridge turns on, Miller current Cgd dv/dt flows into the off device's gate; a negative off-voltage (−5 V to −8 V for IGBTs) or a Miller clamp keeps it below threshold.

1.8 Switching losses, thermal design and heat sinks

Every power switch loses energy in two ways: its drop while it conducts (conduction loss) and a burst of heat at each turn-on and turn-off while voltage and current overlap (switching loss), which grows with frequency. All the heat flows from the junction through the case and heat sink to the air, each step with a thermal resistance. Thermal design picks the heat sink that keeps the junction below its limit on the hottest day.

You need: the turn-on and turn-off intervals of the MOSFET and IGBT chapter.

Switching loss from voltage-current overlap

During a transition the switch has both a large voltage and a large current, and their product is power. The energy of one transition depends on how v and i change. With a clamped inductive load (the usual case in choppers and inverters, with a freewheeling diode), the current changes first at full voltage, then the voltage changes at full current, so each transition is a triangle of power with peak V I. With a resistive load, voltage and current change together, and the energy is a third as large.

Esw = ½ V I tsw (inductive), Esw = V I tsw / 6 (resistive)

where Esw is the energy of one transition (J), V the blocking voltage (V), I the on-state current (A) and tsw the transition time (s).

Psw = ½ V I (ton + toff) fs

the average switching loss with an inductive load, in watts, where ton and toff are the turn-on and turn-off transition times (s) and fs the switching frequency (Hz). Datasheets often give Eon and Eoff directly at a test voltage and current; then Psw = (Eon + Eoff) fs.

Shorter transitions cut the loss; at fixed transition times, Psw is proportional to fs.

Conduction loss and total loss

While on, a diode, thyristor, BJT or IGBT drops a roughly constant voltage, so Pcond = Von I D, where I is the current while conducting and D the fraction of the period it conducts. A MOSFET is a resistance, so Pcond = I² RDS(on) D. If the current given is already the average or rms over the whole period, D is already included and must not be applied again. The total device loss is P = Pcond + Psw; use the worst-case current and the hot value of RDS(on) or Von.

P = Pcond + Psw

the total heat the cooling path must remove, in watts.

Thermal path and heat sink

Heat flows from the junction to the case, across the mounting interface to the heat sink, and from the sink to the ambient air. Each step has a thermal resistance in °C/W: RθJC (junction to case, from the datasheet), RθCS (case to sink, set by the pad or grease and the mounting pressure) and RθSA (sink to ambient, from the heat-sink maker). They are in series, like resistors carrying the heat flow P, and temperature plays the role of voltage.

Tj = Ta + P (RθJC + RθCS + RθSA)

where Tj is the junction temperature and Ta the ambient temperature (°C), P the total loss (W) and each Rθ in °C/W.

To choose a heat sink, rearrange: RθSA ≤ (Tj,max − Ta)/P − RθJC − RθCS. A lower RθSA means a bigger sink, more fins or forced air. For short pulses the junction does not reach the steady value, because the die and case store heat; datasheets give the transient thermal impedance ZθJC(t), which is smaller than RθJC for short pulses.

Worked example

Question. An IGBT in a chopper switches 10 A off and on against 200 V at 10 kHz, with an inductive load. Its turn-on time is 1 µs and turn-off time 2 µs. It conducts for half of each period with VCE(sat) = 2 V. RθJC = 1.0 °C/W, RθCS = 0.5 °C/W, Ta = 40 °C and Tj,max = 125 °C. Find the total loss and the largest heat-sink resistance allowed. A 0.6 °C/W sink is chosen; find Tj and the case and sink temperatures.

  1. Switching energy per cycle: ½ × 200 × 10 × (1 + 2) × 10−6 = 3 × 10−3 J; Psw = 3 mJ × 10 000 Hz = 30 W.
  2. Conduction loss: Pcond = 2 V × 10 A × 0.5 = 10 W. Total P = 30 + 10 = 40 W.
  3. Allowed total resistance: (125 − 40) ÷ 40 = 2.125 °C/W, so RθSA ≤ 2.125 − 1.0 − 0.5 = 0.625 °C/W.
  4. With the 0.6 °C/W sink: Tj = 40 + 40 × (1.0 + 0.5 + 0.6) = 40 + 84 = 124 °C.
  5. Down the path: Tcase = 124 − 40 × 1.0 = 84 °C; Tsink = 84 − 40 × 0.5 = 64 °C; and 64 − 40 × 0.6 = 40 °C, the ambient.

Answer. P = 40 W (30 W switching, 10 W conduction); RθSA ≤ 0.625 °C/W; with 0.6 °C/W, Tj = 124 °C, Tcase = 84 °C, Tsink = 64 °C.

1.9 Wide band-gap devices: SiC and GaN

Silicon carbide (SiC) and gallium nitride (GaN) have a band gap about three times that of silicon. A wider gap means a much higher critical electric field, so a thin, heavily doped drift layer can block the same voltage. That gives far lower on-resistance at a given rating, fast unipolar switching at high voltage, and operation at higher temperature. SiC MOSFETs now replace IGBTs in 800 V electric-vehicle inverters and solar inverters; GaN transistors fill laptop and phone fast chargers and server supplies.

You need: the power MOSFET, its RDS(on) and gate charge, from the BJT, MOSFET and IGBT chapter.

Why a wide band gap helps

The band gap Eg is the energy needed to free an electron from the valence band into the conduction band, in electron-volts (eV). Silicon has about 1.1 eV, 4H-SiC about 3.3 eV and GaN about 3.4 eV. A wider gap makes avalanche breakdown need a much stronger field: the critical field Ec is about 0.3 MV/cm in silicon, about 2.2 MV/cm in SiC and about 3 MV/cm in GaN (1 MV/cm = 106 V/cm).

Wdrift ≈ 2 VB / Ec

where Wdrift is the drift-layer thickness (cm), VB the blocking voltage (V) and Ec the critical field in V/cm, for a triangular field profile.

A thinner drift layer that can also be doped more heavily has much lower resistance. The ideal specific on-resistance of the drift region is 4 VB² / (ε μ Ec³), so the material quality is ranked by ε μ Ec³, the Baliga figure of merit (ε is permittivity, μ electron mobility). Because Ec is cubed, SiC and GaN beat silicon by hundreds of times. SiC also conducts heat two to three times better than silicon, so heat leaves the die easily; GaN is closer to silicon.

SiC MOSFET and GaN HEMT

A SiC MOSFET is a vertical device like a silicon power MOSFET, with the drain at the bottom. It is normally off, made from 650 V to 3.3 kV, and usually driven at +15 V to +20 V on and 0 V to −5 V off; the gate oxide does not tolerate overshoot beyond the datasheet limits. Its body diode works but has a forward drop of about 3 V and a small Qrr.

A GaN HEMT (high electron mobility transistor) is lateral: source, gate and drain are on the top surface. An AlGaN layer on GaN forms a two-dimensional electron gas (2DEG), a thin sheet of very mobile electrons that is the channel. Power GaN parts are mostly enhancement-mode (normally off), rated up to about 650 V. The gate tolerates only about +6 V to +7 V, so drives are tight. GaN has no p-n body diode: in reverse the channel itself conducts, with a drop of a few volts, and there is no reverse recovery charge.

  • Datasheet lines to read: maximum VGS; RDS(on) at 25 °C and at high temperature; Qg and Qrr; Coss; RθJC.

Switching, losses and where each is used

Both are unipolar devices, so there is no tail current, and their small capacitances and gate charge allow switching at 100 kHz to several megahertz. Gate drive loss is Pgate = Qg Vdrive fs. The price is very fast edges, tens of volts per nanosecond, which push current through every parasitic capacitance and make ringing, interference and false turn-on likely unless the layout is tight.

Takeaways

  1. A power diode is a switch with a fixed forward drop and a turn-off delay set by reverse recovery, so it matters most at high frequency.
  2. An SCR is a four-layer latch: gate current only starts the regeneration, after which the anode current itself keeps the device on.
  3. A gate trigger circuit must give a fast-rising pulse with enough current, lasting until the anode current passes the latching current, and inside the gate power limits.
  4. Commutation is the circuit's job, not the device's: you must drop current below IH and then hold reverse voltage for longer than tq.
  5. TRIAC and DIAC make a cheap, phase-controlled AC switch, while GTO gives a thyristor-like device that can be turned off from the gate.
  6. Power BJT is current driven, MOSFET is voltage driven with a gate charge, and IGBT puts a MOSFET gate on a BJT output for high-voltage work.
  7. Ratings mean nothing without protection: snubbers limit dv/dt and di/dt, and gate drives must deliver clean, fast pulses with isolated returns.
  8. Switching loss and thermal resistance together decide the heat sink and the maximum switching frequency a design can survive.
  9. SiC and GaN block the same voltage with a much thinner drift layer than silicon, so they conduct and switch with far less loss, but they demand tighter gate drive, careful layout and attention to interference.

Then run the circuit and record each SCR's voltage with differential probes through turn-on and turn-off: the transient peaks should stay below the 1200 V rating, and the two waveforms should cross over within microseconds.
  8. Check: with R = 40 kΩ, a 10 mA leakage difference shifts the sharing by 0.010 × 40 000 ÷ 2 = 200 V from the 1000 V midpoint, so the worse device sees 1200 V, exactly its rating; a slightly smaller R gives margin.
Evaluate A converter fails in the field with short-circuited switches and signs of excessive junction temperature. Evaluate the roles of snubber design, gate drive and heat-sink selection in preventing such failures, using the device of the switching-loss worked example (40 W total loss, RθJC = 1.0 °C/W, RθCS = 0.5 °C/W, heat sink 0.6 °C/W, designed for 40 °C ambient, Tj,max = 125 °C). 15 marks

Points to cover. Thermal check at design and at hot ambient or fan failure; Snubber: dv/dt false turn-on, overshoot, switching loss; Gate drive: Miller turn-on, negative bias, Rg, desaturation protection; Combined failure chain and an evaluation of the fixes.

Model answer.

  1. How the failures connect. A switch that runs hot has a higher leakage and longer turn-off, more switching loss, and a lower safe current; a switch that turns on falsely shorts the DC link (shoot-through) and fails as a short circuit. So heat, snubber and gate drive faults reinforce each other.
  2. Thermal check of the design:
    ConditionRθSATj = Ta + 40 W × ΣRθ
    Design, 40 °C ambient0.6 °C/W40 + 40 × 2.1 = 124 °C
    Cabinet at 50 °C0.6 °C/W50 + 40 × 2.1 = 134 °C
    Fan failed, sink 1.2 °C/W1.2 °C/W40 + 40 × 2.7 = 148 °C
  3. Evaluation of the heat sink. The design leaves only 1 °C of margin at 40 °C, so a hot cabinet or a dusty, slow fan pushes the junction past 125 °C. A sound design allows for the worst ambient and a derated fan: for 50 °C and Tj ≤ 115 °C, ΣRθ ≤ 65 ÷ 40 = 1.625 °C/W, so RθSA ≤ 0.125 °C/W, which needs forced air or a lower-loss device. Heat-sink selection is the first line of defence.
  4. Snubber. Without an adequate snubber, fast dv/dt at turn-off drives C dv/dt current into the device and the overshoot from stray inductance (v = L di/dt) can exceed the voltage rating; in thyristor circuits dv/dt causes false turn-on. A snubber with Cs = I/(dv/dt) limits both and moves part of the turn-off loss into Rs; but an oversized Cs raises the Rs loss (½ Cs V² fs) and the turn-on current, so it must be sized, not just added.
  5. Gate drive. If the off-state gate is held at 0 V through a weak path, the Miller current Cgc dv/dt from the other switch's turn-on can lift it past threshold and both switches of a leg conduct. A −5 V to −8 V off bias, a low-impedance turn-off path or a Miller clamp prevents this; a dead time between the two gate signals is also needed. Too large an Rg raises switching loss and temperature; too small an Rg raises dv/dt and ringing. A driver with desaturation detection turns the device off softly within microseconds of a short circuit, before it fails.
  6. Combined chain in this failure. The likely sequence: the junction runs near or above 125 °C on a hot day, switching loss and leakage rise, a dv/dt transient or Miller current turns on the off device, the leg shoots through, and with no fast short-circuit protection both switches fail short.
  7. Recommended fixes, in order of value: (1) re-rate the heat sink for the worst ambient with margin (about 15 °C below Tj,max); (2) negative gate bias, dead time and desaturation protection in the driver; (3) a snubber sized from I/(dv/dt), with a non-inductive resistor rated for ½ Cs V² fs; (4) a temperature sensor on the heat sink that derates the current.
  8. Check: the design point satisfies Tj = 40 + 40 × (1.0 + 0.5 + 0.6) = 124 °C ≤ 125 °C, so the original design was legal but had no margin; the field failure is consistent with operation above the design ambient.

Glossary

Anode, cathode, gate
The three terminals of an SCR: anode and cathode carry the main current, gate receives the control pulse.
Class A commutation
Load or self-commutation in which a resonant load current itself creates the zero crossing.
Commutation circuit
Network of L, C and auxiliary switches that forces the anode current of an SCR to zero in a DC circuit.
Critical electric field
Field strength at which avalanche breakdown begins; about 0.3 MV/cm in silicon and about seven to ten times higher in SiC and GaN.
di/dt rating
Maximum rate of rise of on-state current allowed at turn-on, set by how fast the conduction area can spread.
dv/dt rating
Maximum rate of rise of off-state voltage a device can withstand without turning on by itself.
Gate drive
Circuit that converts a control signal into the current or voltage pulse a device gate needs, usually with isolation.
Heat sink
Metal structure that carries heat from the device case to the surrounding air, characterised by its thermal resistance in °C/W.
Junction temperature
Temperature of the semiconductor chip itself; it must stay below the rated maximum, typically 125 °C to 175 °C.
Maximum controllable anode current (GTO)
The largest anode current a GTO can turn off with its gate; above it, gate turn-off fails.
Miller plateau
Flat part of the gate voltage during switching, while the gate charge swings the drain voltage through Cgd instead of raising vGS.
Phase control
AC power control method in which the firing angle of a thyristor or TRIAC sets the portion of each half cycle delivered to the load.
Power diode
Two-terminal P-N junction device built with a thick, lightly doped drift layer so it can block high reverse voltage and carry large forward current.
Power electronics
Branch of electronics that converts and controls electric power using semiconductor switches operating as ON/OFF devices.
Reverse recovery
Delay in turning off a diode because stored minority carriers must be removed before it blocks reverse voltage.
Safe operating area (SOA)
Graph of permitted device current against voltage for a stated pulse width, inside which the device will not be damaged.
Schottky diode
Metal–semiconductor diode with no minority carriers, very fast switching and a low forward drop, but limited reverse voltage.
Snap-off
Abrupt end of reverse recovery, which produces a sharp current fall and a large voltage spike; a soft recovery avoids it.
Thyristor (SCR)
Four-layer P-N-P-N device that turns on when a gate pulse starts internal regeneration and then stays on until anode current falls below holding current.
TRIAC quadrants
The four combinations of MT2 and gate polarity (measured from MT1) in which a TRIAC can be triggered: I+, I−, III− and III+; sensitivity is poorest in III+.
Turn-off time tq
Minimum time the SCR needs, after current zero, with reverse voltage applied, before it can block forward voltage again.
Wide band-gap semiconductor
Material such as silicon carbide or gallium nitride with a band gap well above silicon's 1.1 eV, giving a higher breakdown field.
α (current gain)
Fraction of emitter current that reaches the collector in a transistor; in the two-transistor model, regeneration needs α₁ + α₂ ≥ 1.
