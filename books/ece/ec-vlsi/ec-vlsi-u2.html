2.1 Oxidation, diffusion and ion implantation

Silicon is only useful once you can put dopants in chosen places. Three unit processes do that work. Oxidation grows a silicon dioxide layer that insulates, masks and protects. Diffusion moves dopant atoms into the crystal using heat alone. Ion implantation fires dopants in with a set energy and a set dose, so the depth is chosen in keV rather than guessed from a furnace schedule. Every CMOS flow uses all three: the gate oxide, the wells and the shallow source-drain extensions all come from this chapter's steps.

You need: donors, acceptors, carrier concentration and the MOS structure from Unit 1; the mask window itself is opened in 2.2.

Thermal oxidation: growing the oxide

Silicon oxidises easily, and the oxide it forms is a dense, stable insulator that sticks to the silicon beneath it. That is why silicon won: one material supplies both the semiconductor and its own insulator. SiO2 does three jobs on a chip — gate insulator, field insulator between devices, and a mask that dopants cannot pass through.

Thermal oxidation grows this layer in a furnace at 900–1100 °C. Dry oxidation uses oxygen: Si + O2 → SiO2. Wet oxidation uses steam: Si + 2H2O → SiO2 + 2H2. Wet growth is many times faster, so it makes thick field oxide. Dry growth is slow and gives a denser film with fewer defects, so it makes the thin gate oxide, where a few nanometres decide the threshold voltage (see Unit 1, Threshold voltage and body effect).

xox² + A·xox = B·(t + τ)

x_ox is the oxide thickness in μm; t is the oxidation time in hours; A (μm) and B (μm²/h) depend on temperature and on whether the gas is O2 or H2O; τ is a small time offset covering oxide already present.

  • Thin oxide, reaction-limited: x_ox ≈ (B/A)(t + τ). The surface reaction is the bottleneck, so growth is linear in time.
  • Thick oxide, diffusion-limited: x_ox ≈ √(B·t). The oxidant must now diffuse through oxide already grown, so growth slows to a square root.
  • Growing 1 μm of oxide consumes about 0.44 μm of silicon, so the oxide surface sits above the original wafer surface.
  • Field oxide is thick and wet-grown; gate oxide is thin and dry-grown, often only 2–10 nm.

Diffusion: moving dopants with heat

A dopant atom inside silicon hops from lattice site to site at random, but a concentration gradient makes the hops carry more atoms downhill than uphill. Fick's first law states the flux: J = −D(∂C/∂x). J is the flux in atoms cm⁻² s⁻¹, D the diffusion coefficient in cm²/s, C the concentration in cm⁻³ and x the depth; the minus sign says flow goes from high concentration to low.

Conservation of atoms gives Fick's second law, ∂C/∂t = D(∂²C/∂x²).

D = D0·exp(−Ea/kT)

D is the diffusion coefficient in cm²/s; D0 is a material constant; Ea is the activation energy, about 3–4 eV for common dopants in silicon; k = 8.62×10⁻⁵ eV/K is Boltzmann's constant; T is the absolute temperature in kelvin. Near 1000 °C, D roughly doubles for every 30 °C rise, so furnace temperature sets junction depth.

A doping step has two parts. Predeposition holds the wafer at a lower temperature in a gas carrying the dopant; the surface concentration saturates at the solid solubility and a fixed dose enters. Drive-in switches the gas off and raises the temperature; dopants spread deeper and the surface concentration falls. Most of the dose ends up within one diffusion length of the surface, L = 2√(Dt), with L in cm, D in cm²/s and t in s.

C(x, t) = (Q / √(πDt))·exp(−x²/4Dt)

C is the concentration in cm⁻³ at depth x and time t; Q is the dose in cm⁻²; D is the diffusion coefficient; t is the time. At x = L = 2√(Dt) the concentration has fallen to 1/e of its surface value.

Diffusion is isotropic: dopant spreads sideways under a mask edge by the same distance it spreads down. Layout must reserve room for that lateral spread; the design rules in 2.6 encode it.

Ion implantation: placing dopants by energy

Diffusion cannot make a doping that is shallow, heavy and precisely placed. Ion implantation can. Dopant atoms are ionised, accelerated through 30–200 keV and fired at the wafer. They enter the crystal, lose energy by nuclear collisions and by electronic drag, and stop at a depth set by the energy.

Two numbers describe the step. The dose φ is the number of ions per unit area, in cm⁻², typically 10¹¹ to 10¹⁶ cm⁻². The energy is in keV; more energy means a deeper stop. The average stopping depth is the projected range Rp, and the spread around it is the straggle ΔRp.

C(x) = (φ / (√(2π)·ΔR<sub>p</sub>))·exp(−(x − R<sub>p</sub>)² / (2ΔR<sub>p</sub>²))

C(x) is the implanted concentration in cm⁻³ at depth x; φ is the dose in cm⁻²; Rp is the projected range in cm; ΔRp is the straggle in cm. The peak value, at x = Rp, is about 0.4φ/ΔRp.

If the beam lines up with a crystal channel, ions slip between rows of atoms and travel far deeper, leaving a long tail. The fix is to tilt the wafer about 7° from the beam and rotate it, so no channel is open. Masking works the other way: an ion stops inside resist or oxide when Rp is less than the mask thickness. The same oxide window that defines a pattern therefore defines the doping, so the implant is self-aligned and needs no extra mask.

The collisions knock silicon atoms off their sites, leaving a damaged, partly amorphous layer. A short anneal at 900–1000 °C repairs the lattice and moves dopants onto substitutional sites, which activates them. Anneal too long or too hot and the profile you placed diffuses away. That balance is the process engineer's daily trade.

Worked example

Question. At 1000 °C the diffusion coefficient of phosphorus in silicon is D = 3×10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s. A drive-in lasts t = 1 hour. (a) Find the diffusion length L = 2√(Dt). (b) Is the resulting junction shallower or deeper than 0.5 μm? (c) How long would a diffusion length of 0.5 μm take?

  1. Convert the time to the units D is given in: t = 1 hour = 3600 s.
  2. Form the product: D·t = 3×10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s × 3600 s = 1.08×10⁻¹⁰ cm².
  3. Take the square root: √(D·t) = √(1.08×10⁻¹⁰) cm = 1.04×10⁻⁵ cm.
  4. Multiply by 2: L = 2 × 1.04×10⁻⁵ cm = 2.08×10⁻⁵ cm = 0.21 μm.
  5. Compare with 0.5 μm: 0.21 μm is smaller, so the junction is shallower.
  6. For L = 0.5 μm = 0.5×10⁻⁴ cm, first halve it: √(D·t) = 0.25×10⁻⁴ cm.
  7. Square: D·t = 6.25×10⁻¹⁰ cm².
  8. Divide by D: t = 6.25×10⁻¹⁰ / 3×10⁻¹⁴ s = 2.08×10⁴ s.
  9. Convert to hours: t = 2.08×10⁴ / 3600 h = 5.8 hours.

Exam traps

  • Dose is per area (cm⁻²); concentration is per volume (cm⁻³). A 10¹⁵ cm⁻² implant spread over 0.1 μm gives about 10²⁰ cm⁻³, not 10¹⁵ cm⁻³.
  • Diffusion is isotropic and implantation is directional. Only the implant keeps a junction shallow without sideways spread, which is why source-drain extensions use it.
  • Wet oxide is fast and thick, dry oxide is slow and dense. Gate oxide is dry, field oxide is wet — not the other way round.
  • Deal-Grove: linear for thin oxide (reaction-limited), parabolic for thick (diffusion-limited). Examiners deliberately swap the two labels.
  • The anneal that repairs implant damage also diffuses the profile. It must be the minimum that activates the dopants — usually seconds at 900–1000 °C, not an hour.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Modern fabs run these steps for seconds, not hours: rapid thermal oxidation, spike anneals and millisecond laser anneals exist because an hour at 1000 °C moves a 20 nm junction far too much. Thermal SiO₂ has been partly replaced as the gate insulator by plasma-nitrided oxide and by high-k dielectrics such as HfO₂, because a 1 nm oxide leaks by tunnelling. Implants now use molecular clusters and cryogenic wafer temperatures to keep damage shallow and activation high. To go further, search for rapid thermal processing, high-k metal gate, plasma doping and cryogenic implantation.

2.2 Lithography, etching and deposition

Every CMOS layer starts as a pattern in a polymer film called resist. Lithography prints that pattern: clean, coat, bake, align a mask, expose, develop. The resist then protects some areas while the layer below is cut away by etching or built up by deposition. Strip the resist and the wafer is ready for the next mask. The same loop runs for every layer, so its resolution sets the smallest transistor and wire. Fabs use it for all CMOS and BiCMOS products.

You need: Chapter 2.1, where thermal oxide is grown as the layer this resist patterns.

Lithography: the print loop

Lithography transfers a pattern from a mask to resist. The wafer is cleaned, coated with a light-sensitive polymer called resist, and soft baked to remove solvent. The mask (a 1× pattern) or reticle (usually 4× or 5×, reduced by the lens) is aligned to the previous layer. Light exposes the resist through the clear windows. Developer dissolves the exposed resist in a positive resist; in a negative resist the exposed part cross-links and stays, and the unexposed part dissolves. After etch or deposition, a strip removes all resist.

  1. Clean and dehydrate the wafer so resist sticks.
  2. Spin-coat resist to a uniform thickness, often below 1 μm.
  3. Soft bake to drive off solvent.
  4. Align the mask or reticle to the existing pattern.
  5. Expose with UV light through the mask.
  6. Develop: positive resist removes exposed areas; negative resist keeps them.
  7. Etch or deposit, then strip the resist.

Older printers place the mask in contact with the resist (contact printing) or close to it (proximity printing). Both damage or blur the mask. Projection printing uses a lens to image the reticle, so the mask never touches the wafer. Resolution is R = k·λ/NA, where R is the smallest printable half-pitch in nm, λ is the exposure wavelength in nm, NA is the numerical aperture of the lens, and k is a process factor (about 0.5 to 0.8 for production). Depth of focus is DOF = λ/(2NA²), the vertical distance over which the image stays sharp. A shorter λ or larger NA lowers R but also lowers DOF.

R = k·λ / NA DOF = λ / (2NA²)

R is the smallest printable feature in nm; λ is the exposure wavelength in nm; NA is the lens numerical aperture; k is the process factor; DOF is the depth of focus in nm.

Etching: cut the layer below

Etching removes material through the resist window. Wet etching uses a liquid acid or base. It is isotropic: it etches sideways at the same rate as down, so the hole undercuts the resist and ends wider than the window. Dry etching uses a plasma of ions and radicals. Reactive ion etching (RIE) combines chemical attack with ion bombardment that clears the floor of the hole, so it is anisotropic: the sidewalls stay nearly vertical. Selectivity is the etch rate of the target layer divided by the etch rate of resist or the layer below; high selectivity means the mask and underlayer survive. Endpoint detection watches the plasma emission or a reflected laser signal and stops when the target layer clears. A short over-etch, often 10-30% of the main time, removes residue.

Deposition and planarisation

Deposition adds a thin film. LPCVD (low-pressure chemical vapour deposition) runs at high temperature and gives conformal films, but the wafer sees the furnace temperature. PECVD (plasma-enhanced CVD) uses a plasma to allow lower temperatures, useful after aluminium. Sputtering strikes a target with ions and throws atoms onto the wafer; it deposits metals such as aluminium. Evaporation heats a source in vacuum; it is simple but has poor step coverage. Epitaxy grows a single-crystal silicon layer on the wafer, lattice-matched to the substrate. In BiCMOS it forms the lightly doped collector (see Chapter 2.5).

  • LPCVD: high temperature; conformal poly-Si and nitride.
  • PECVD: plasma allows lower temperature; oxide and nitride over metal.
  • Sputtering: ion bombardment of a target; aluminium and barrier metals.
  • Evaporation: heated source in vacuum; simple laboratory metal films.
  • Epitaxy: single-crystal Si on Si; lightly doped BiCMOS collector.

After deposition, the surface is often not flat. CMP (chemical mechanical planarisation) polishes the wafer with a slurry and a pad until the step height falls, so the next lithography step can focus. Planarisation matters because DOF is only a few hundred nm for advanced lenses.

Exam traps

  • Wet etching undercuts the mask: the hole is wider than the window at the surface. A square vertical profile is wrong for wet etch.
  • Positive resist: the exposed part dissolves. Negative resist: the exposed part stays. Swapping them reverses the whole pattern.

2.3 NMOS and CMOS fabrication process flow

Fabrication is a sequence of masks. Each mask lets one layer be grown, deposited, implanted or cut in a fixed order. Temperature decides what can come after what, and alignment decides how the layers land on each other. Follow an nMOS transistor first, then the extra masks that put an nMOS and a pMOS on the same die. The order explains why source and drain are self-aligned to the gate, and why contacts and metal come last.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for oxidation, diffusion and ion implantation, and Chapter 2.2 for lithography, etching and deposition; Unit 1 for what a transistor needs.

The nMOS-only flow

Begin with a p-type silicon wafer. The active mask defines the islands where transistors will sit. Outside each island the field oxide, a thick oxide, isolates neighbours.

  1. Form field oxide outside the active islands. LOCOS, local oxidation of silicon, grows thick oxide locally but spreads sideways as a bird's beak. STI, shallow trench isolation, etches a trench and fills it with oxide.
  2. Grow the gate oxide, a thin SiO2 layer, then deposit polycrystalline silicon (poly).
  3. Use the poly mask and etch to leave the gate. Dope the poly so it conducts.
  4. Implant a light n− dose for the lightly doped drain, LDD. The poly gate and field oxide block the implant elsewhere.
  5. Deposit an oxide spacer and etch it back. The spacer sets the offset for the heavy n+ implant.
  6. Implant n+ source and drain, then anneal to activate dopants. The poly gate is the mask, so source and drain align to it.
  7. Open contacts, deposit and pattern metal, passivate, and open the pads.

The self-aligned step is the key. If source and drain were implanted through a separate window, mask misalignment would leave a gap or overlap. With the gate as the mask, the only offset comes from the spacer, set by deposition and etch, not by lithography.

The CMOS flow: wells and two S/D masks

CMOS, complementary MOS, needs an nMOS and a pMOS. In a simple n-well flow on a p-type wafer, the pMOS sits inside an n-well. The n-well mask comes before the active mask: implant phosphorus and drive it in. The nMOS stays in the p-substrate. Well variants are in Chapter 2.4.

After wells, the flow repeats the nMOS steps: active, gate oxide, poly, poly mask. Then it splits. The n+ source/drain mask covers the pMOS area with resist and opens the nMOS area. An arsenic or phosphorus implant makes n+ source and drain. The p+ source/drain mask covers the nMOS and opens the pMOS. A boron implant makes p+ source and drain. Two masks exist because one implant must not enter the other transistor: n+ in a p+ region would compensate the doping.

Then come contact, metal 1, via (a hole between metal layers), metal 2, and pad. Each is one lithography step with its own mask. The figure lists the mask order. The back end starts at contact: wiring, not transistor formation.

Why the order is fixed

The order follows temperature and alignment. Well implants and isolation see high-temperature drives, so they come before the gate oxide. The gate oxide must stay clean and thin; later high-temperature steps are limited. Poly is deposited after gate oxide, and the source/drain implant comes after poly because the poly is the mask. The activation anneal follows the implants. Contacts and metal come after that anneal because metal wiring cannot take the same heat.

The line splits into front-end-of-line (FEOL), where transistors are made, and back-end-of-line (BEOL), where contacts, vias and wires are made. Contact is the first back-end step. Passivation protects the chip; the pad mask opens windows over the pads so a bond wire or probe can touch metal.

MOS memory processes reuse this flow and add layers. DRAM adds a stacked capacitor; flash adds a floating gate. The cells and their operation are in Unit 4, Memory design. Here, only note that memory adds masks; it does not change the logic of the mask sequence.

Worked example

Question. A CMOS process uses these masks, one lithography step each: n-well, active, poly, n+ source/drain, p+ source/drain, contact, metal 1, via, metal 2, pad. (a) How many lithography steps? (b) A die is 0.5 cm² and the line has D = 1 defect/cm²; use Y = e^(−A·D) for yield. (c) How many steps if metal 2 is dropped?

  1. Count the masks: n-well, active, poly, n+ S/D, p+ S/D, contact, metal 1, via, metal 2, pad. That is 10 masks, and one lithography step each gives 10 steps.
  2. For yield, A = 0.5 cm² and D = 1 defect/cm². A·D = 0.5 × 1 = 0.5. Y = e^(−0.5) = 0.6065. As a percentage, 60.7%, about 61%.
  3. Drop metal 2. The via connects metal 1 to metal 2, so the via mask also goes. Steps = 10 − 2 = 8.

Answer. (a) 10 lithography steps; (b) Y = 0.607, about 61%; (c) 8 steps.

Exam traps

  • Saying source and drain are diffused through a window. They are implanted with the poly gate acting as the mask; that self-alignment keeps gate and source/drain from drifting apart.
  • Mixing up mask count with layer count. A mask is one lithography step; one layer may use more than one mask, and one mask may open more than one hole.
  • Dropping metal 2 but keeping the via mask. The via connects metal 1 to metal 2, so both go together.
  • Merging the n+ and p+ source/drain masks. The same implant would counter-dope one transistor.
  • Putting contact before the source/drain anneal. Contact holes come after high-temperature steps.

2.4 P-well, N-well and twin-tub processes

Every CMOS chip needs both an NMOS and a PMOS. An NMOS needs a p-type body; a PMOS needs an n-type body. One silicon substrate can supply only one doping, so the other device must sit in a locally doped well. The p-well, n-well and twin-tub processes are three answers to the same question: which device goes inside a well, and how freely can each body be doped? The choice fixes mask count, body resistance, threshold voltages and latch-up behaviour, and it appears in every CMOS process flow.

You need: Chapter 2.3 and Unit 1 for threshold voltage and the body effect.

Why one device must sit in a well

An NMOS conducts when electrons are pulled through a p-type body toward an n+ drain. A PMOS conducts when holes are pulled through an n-type body toward a p+ drain. The body is the terminal that sets the channel type and the threshold voltage. A plain silicon wafer is doped either p-type or n-type throughout; it cannot be both at the surface. The well solves this. A well is a deep doped region formed early in the flow, before the gate stack, by ion implantation and a high-temperature drive. The device inside the well uses the well as its body. The device outside uses the substrate as its body.

The well-substrate junction must stay reverse biased. An n-well sits in a p-substrate and is tied to the most positive supply. A p-well sits in an n-substrate and is tied to the most negative supply. The well is deep, not a thin sheet, so its doping and size affect junction capacitance and the parasitic devices discussed in Chapter 2.8.

p-well, n-well and twin-tub

In a p-well process the wafer starts n-type. A p-well holds the NMOS; the PMOS sits in the n-substrate. The n-substrate is tied to VDD. In an n-well process the wafer starts p-type. An n-well holds the PMOS; the NMOS sits in the p-substrate, which is tied to VSS. This is the common choice. The p-substrate tie is a low-resistance path, and the NMOS keeps the substrate as its body. In a twin-tub process the starting wafer is usually p-type, but both an n-well and a p-well are implanted and driven separately. The PMOS body is the n-well; the NMOS body is the p-well. Each well has its own doping dose, so both threshold voltages and both body-effect coefficients can be set. The price is one extra well mask and a longer thermal budget.

Trade-offs and newer options

Most digital CMOS uses n-well because the p-substrate is thick, so the path from the substrate to the VSS pad has low resistance, which shunts the parasitic path that causes latch-up. The NMOS also stays in the better substrate, so its body effect is predictable. The PMOS in the n-well can be doped to raise or lower its threshold. Twin-tub costs one mask but gives independent control; it appears in low-voltage, analog and mixed-signal processes where matching and body effect matter. A triple-well process adds a deep n-well under a p-well. That isolates the p-well from the p-substrate, so a block of NMOS can have its own body bias, or a noisy digital block can be separated from analog devices. Silicon-on-insulator goes further: a buried oxide layer isolates the transistor body from the wafer, removing the well-substrate junction. SOI gives lower capacitance and no latch-up path, but the wafer is more expensive; only a few universities teach it in detail. The parasitic bipolar path itself is left to Chapter 2.8.

Worked example

Question. In an n-well process μn = 500 cm²/V·s and μp = 200 cm²/V·s. (a) For a symmetric inverter, find Wp/Wn. (b) With L = 0.5 μm and Wn = 2 μm, find Wp. (c) Which transistor sits inside the well, and which carrier sets the speed?

  1. A symmetric inverter has equal pull-up and pull-down current at the same supply. Set μn(W/L)n = μp(W/L)p.
  2. The channel length L is the same for both devices, so it cancels. Wp/Wn = μn/μp = 500/200 = 2.5.
  3. Use Wp = 2.5 × Wn = 2.5 × 2 μm = 5 μm.
  4. The given L = 0.5 μm is not needed for the ratio or for Wp because it cancels in the current balance.
  5. The PMOS sits in the n-well. Electrons in the NMOS have higher mobility than holes in the PMOS, so the NMOS sets the speed and the PMOS must be wider.

Answer. Wp/Wn = 2.5, Wp = 5 μm; the PMOS sits in the n-well and electrons in the NMOS set the speed.

Exam traps

  • Saying the n-well process puts the NMOS in a well. It does not: the NMOS stays in the p-substrate and only the PMOS is in the n-well.
  • Swapping the bodies: in a p-well process the NMOS is in the p-well and the PMOS is in the n-substrate.
  • Thinking twin-tub is just two names for one well. It uses two independently doped wells and one extra well mask, so both Vt and body effect can be set.
  • Using the mobility ratio upside down. Holes are slower, so Wp/Wn = μn/μp is greater than one.
  • Believing the well is a thin surface layer. It is a deep region whose doping and depth affect parasitic capacitance and latch-up.

2.5 BiCMOS technology

One chip, two transistors. CMOS gives high input impedance, low static power and dense logic. A bipolar transistor gives large transconductance and current drive. BiCMOS runs the CMOS flow, then adds one vertical NPN using three or four extra masks. One die senses with CMOS and drives with bipolar. Uses: radio front ends, high-speed links, mixed-signal chips and power ICs.

You need Chapter 2.3 for the mask sequence, Chapter 2.4 for wells, Chapter 2.1 for doping, and Unit 1 for MOSFET transconductance.

The vertical NPN

An NPN has an n+ emitter, a p base and an n collector. In normal operation the emitter-base junction is forward biased and the base-collector junction is reverse biased, so electrons cross the thin base into the collector. Current gain is β = Ic / Ib, where Ic is collector current and Ib is base current. In an IC, β is typically 50 to 200.

The device is built vertically: a small n+ emitter window at the surface, a thin p base under it, and the n collector below. Current flows down into the wafer, so the device packs into a small area and the base can be very thin.

The collector must be lightly doped. It must hold the reverse bias of the base-collector junction without breaking down, and heavy doping would lower breakdown voltage and raise junction capacitance. Light doping means high resistivity, so the collector resistance Rc is large: current travels a long, narrow n path to the collector contact, wasting voltage headroom and slowing the device.

Three steps fix this. A buried layer mask defines an n+ region in the p-substrate, implanted with arsenic or antimony at a heavy dose. A thin n-type epitaxial layer is grown over it and becomes the collector. A deep n+ sinker implant runs from the surface down to touch the buried layer, so collector current flows down a short, heavily doped pipe to the contact.

Epitaxial thickness is a trade-off. Thin epi gives low Rc, so speed, but raises base-collector capacitance and lowers breakdown voltage. Thick, lightly doped epi holds high voltage and gives low capacitance, but Rc is large. Neighbouring devices are separated by junction isolation: p-type regions surround each n island, so every pair of neighbours is held apart by a reverse-biased p-n junction.

Adding the NPN to the CMOS flow

The CMOS part is unchanged. Run the twin-tub flow of Chapter 2.4, but put the collector steps in front of it.

  1. Start on a p-substrate. Grow pad oxide, expose the buried layer mask, implant n+ arsenic or antimony, strip the resist.
  2. Grow thin, lightly doped n-type epitaxial silicon. This is the collector.
  3. Sinker implant immediately after the epitaxy, before or with the n-well/p-well implants. The well drive-in also diffuses the sinker down to the buried layer.
  4. Gate oxidation, poly deposition, gate mask and etch. Then n+ and p+ source/drain masks and the anneal.
  5. Base mask: shallow p implant for the NPN base.
  6. Emitter mask: shallow n+ arsenic implant for the emitter.
  7. Contacts, metal, passivation.

The order is fixed by the thermal budget. The buried layer goes first because the epitaxial growth and the well drive-in that follow are hot steps, and it must be diffused deep by them. The base is only a few tenths of a micrometre deep, so it comes after the hot steps, or is finished with a short low-temperature anneal. Extra heat spreads the base, and a wider base lowers both β and speed.

What it buys and what it costs

Transconductance is the payoff. For a bipolar transistor gm = Ic / VT, where VT is the thermal voltage, about 26 mV at room temperature. For a MOSFET in saturation gm = 2 Ic / Vov, where Vov = VGS − VTH is the overdrive. At the same current the bipolar wins because VT is only 26 mV while a usable overdrive is a few hundred millivolts.

gm = Ic / VT   (bipolar)        gm = 2 Ic / Vov   (MOSFET, saturation)

gm is transconductance in A/V; Ic is collector or drain current in A; VT is the thermal voltage, about 26 mV at 300 K; Vov = VGS − VTH is the overdrive in V; VGS is gate-source voltage; VTH is MOSFET threshold voltage. VT and VTH are different quantities.

The second payoff is a nearly constant base-emitter voltage. VBE sits near 0.7 V over a wide current range, so a bipolar pair makes a stable reference, a current mirror and a sharp comparator. CMOS brings a gate that draws no DC input current, dense logic and near-zero static power, because it burns power only while switching.

The cost is process. BiCMOS needs three or four masks beyond CMOS: buried layer, sinker, base and emitter. Each adds lithography, etch and implant, so cycle time grows and yield falls, and every step after the base must stay cool. The extra n+ regions also join the parasitic p-n-p-n path, so latch-up care in layout matters more (see Chapter 2.8).

Against pure bipolar, BiCMOS trades the last bit of speed for density and power: bipolar-only is fastest, but every gate draws standing current and takes far more area. Uses follow that split: radio front ends with an NPN low-noise amplifier beside CMOS control, high-speed output drivers and serial links, mixed-signal chips with bipolar analog and CMOS digital, and power ICs whose bandgap reference is bipolar.

Worked example

Question. An NPN in a BiCMOS process is biased at Ic = 2 mA with β = 100, VT = 26 mV. (a) Find Ib and gm = Ic / VT. (b) An nMOS carries the same 2 mA with Vov = 0.5 V; find gm = 2 Ic / Vov and the ratio. (4 marks)

  1. Ib = Ic / β = 2 mA / 100 = 0.02 mA = 20 μA.
  2. gm = Ic / VT = 2 × 10⁻³ A / 26 × 10⁻³ V = 7.69 × 10⁻² A/V = 76.9 mA/V.
  3. gm = 2 Ic / Vov = 4 mA / 0.5 V = 8 mA/V.
  4. Ratio: 76.9 ÷ 8 = 9.6, so the bipolar has roughly ten times the transconductance at the same current.

Answer. Ib = 20 μA, gm(NPN) = 76.9 mA/V, gm(MOS) = 8 mA/V; the bipolar has about 9.6 times the transconductance at 2 mA.

Worked example 2

Question. At what overdrive Vov would the nMOS and the NPN have equal transconductance at equal current?

  1. Set Ic / VT = 2 Ic / Vov. The current cancels.
  2. So Vov = 2 VT = 2 × 26 mV = 52 mV.
  3. Below 52 mV the MOSFET wins; above it the bipolar does. Real overdrives are 200–500 mV, so the bipolar advantage is large.

Answer. Equal at Vov = 52 mV; the ratio is Vov / (2VT), independent of current.

Exam traps

  • Treating BiCMOS as plain CMOS plus one transistor. The buried layer, epitaxy and sinker are exactly what make the NPN fast, and what cost masks, yield and thermal budget.
  • Swapping buried layer and sinker. Both are n+; the buried layer is implanted before the epitaxy and lies under the device, the sinker is a deep implant from the surface down to it.
  • Assuming a higher β is always better. A thinner base raises β but lowers breakdown; lighter base doping raises β but raises base resistance. β is a design trade.
  • Getting the thermal order wrong. The buried layer goes first because epitaxy and well drive-in are hot; the shallow base goes late so it does not spread.
  • Using gm = Ic / VT for a MOSFET. In saturation it is gm = 2 Ic / Vov; the two differ by about Vov / (2 VT).

2.6 Layout design rules

A layout is only useful if the fab can actually print it. Design rules are the minimum widths, spacings, overlaps and enclosures that a drawn shape must obey so that every mask prints, aligns and etches without breaks or shorts. Foundries publish these rules as a contract; designers draw to them, and a computer program checks every shape before the file is sent out. The same rules shape every standard cell, every SRAM bitcell and every pad ring in a chip.

You need: Chapter 2.3 for which layers exist, and Chapter 2.2 for why alignment errors happen.

Why a drawn shape needs a rule

A layout is a stack of rectangles, one set per layer. Each layer becomes a mask, and the fab prints that mask onto the wafer. Three things go wrong during printing.

  • Mask misalignment: the next mask lands a little off the one below. A contact drawn exactly on the metal edge may end up half outside it.
  • Etch bias: the etch cuts sideways as well as down, so each edge of a shape can move outward or inward by a small amount; a line or gap can print narrower or wider than drawn.
  • Finite resolution: the printer cannot form features below some size. Corners round off and very thin lines simply disappear.

A rule is a margin bought against these errors. Two shapes on the same mask move together, so mask misalignment does not change their spacing. The spacing rule guards against variation in printed size: if random variation lets each edge of a shape move outward by as much as δ (resist and etch bias, or lithographic variation), a gap shrinks by 2δ, so minimum spacing must exceed 2δ. Misalignment Δ enters only the rules between different layers, which is why overlap and enclosure must exceed Δ. Rules are therefore not laws of physics; they are a contract between the designer and the foundry.

A layout that breaks a rule is rejected before fabrication. A layout that obeys every rule still has to work electrically, which is a separate question.

λ, the scalable unit

Writing every rule in micrometres makes the rule set useless the moment the process shrinks. Instead, most teaching rule sets use one length unit, λ (lambda), chosen by the foundry. All rules are then whole multiples of λ.

L(μm) = n × λ(μm)

L is the drawn length in micrometres, n is the rule expressed in λ (a pure number), and λ is the unit length in micrometres, often half the smallest drawn feature.

A layout drawn in λ can be moved to a new process by changing one number. Some syllabi call these scalable design rules. Production processes instead publish micron rules, absolute values in μm or nm, which are tighter and layer-specific.

λ is not the wavelength of the lithography light. The wavelength is a property of the printer and is written in nm; λ here is only a drawing unit. Mixing the two is the commonest slip in an exam answer.

The rule families

Every rule set, however large, is built from a few families.

  • Minimum width: the narrowest rectangle allowed on a layer. A thinner line may break during etch.
  • Minimum spacing: the smallest gap allowed between two shapes on the same layer. A smaller gap may short.
  • Minimum overlap: two layers that must connect need at least this much shared area, so a misalignment still leaves contact.
  • Enclosure: one layer must surround another on all sides, for example metal around a contact.
  • Extension: one layer must stick out past another by a set amount, for example poly past the diffusion at the gate edge.
  • Separation between unrelated layers: a minimum distance between layers that never connect, to limit coupling and etch interference.

Minimum pitch is not a separate rule; it follows from the other two. For wires on one layer, pitch equals minimum width plus minimum spacing, measured centre to centre.

Checking the rules, and wire resistance

A design rule checker (DRC) reads the layout file, rebuilds each layer from the drawn shapes, and tests every rule on every pair of shapes. Real decks run to hundreds or thousands of rules. One violation blocks the design. DRC is only one check; a layout-versus-schematic (LVS) check confirms the drawn circuit matches the intended netlist, and parasitic extraction follows.

Rules also decide how much resistance a wire adds. A wire of uniform width is counted in squares, and each square contributes the same resistance however large the square is.

R = R_s × L / W

R is the wire resistance in Ω, R_s is the sheet resistance of the layer in Ω per square, L is the length along the current in μm, and W is the width in μm. The ratio L/W is the number of squares.

Because a minimum-width wire is the narrowest allowed, it is also the most resistive per unit length. Making a wire wider reduces R in proportion to W.

Worked example

Question. A process uses λ = 0.5 μm with these rules: metal 1 minimum width 3λ, metal 1 minimum spacing 3λ, poly minimum width 2λ, contact 2λ × 2λ, and metal enclosure of a contact 1λ on every side. (a) Find the minimum metal width and the minimum metal pitch in μm. (b) Find the minimum poly width in μm. (c) A transistor is drawn with W = 8λ and L = 2λ. Find its gate area and its gate capacitance, given C_ox = 2.3 fF/μm².

  1. Convert the unit: λ = 0.5 μm, so one λ is 0.5 μm.
  2. (a) Metal width = 3λ = 3 × 0.5 μm = 1.5 μm.
  3. Pitch = width + spacing = 3λ + 3λ = 6λ = 6 × 0.5 μm = 3.0 μm, measured centre to centre.
  4. (b) Poly width = 2λ = 2 × 0.5 μm = 1.0 μm.
  5. (c) Channel width W = 8λ = 8 × 0.5 μm = 4 μm. Channel length L = 2λ = 2 × 0.5 μm = 1 μm.
  6. Gate area = W × L = 4 μm × 1 μm = 4 μm².
  7. Gate capacitance = C_ox × area = 2.3 fF/μm² × 4 μm² = 9.2 fF.
  8. Check on the enclosure rule: the metal around a 2λ contact must be 4λ × 4λ, that is 2.0 μm × 2.0 μm.

Exam traps

  • Using λ for both the layout unit and the lithography wavelength. In a layout question λ is the unit; the printer wavelength is a separate number in nm.
  • Confusing minimum width with minimum spacing. Width is one shape; spacing is the gap between two shapes.
  • Swapping enclosure and extension. Enclosure surrounds a shape on all sides; extension runs past an edge on one side only.
  • Computing pitch as width plus twice the spacing, or as width alone. Pitch is one width plus one spacing.
  • Mixing units halfway: a length in λ multiplied by λ in nm instead of μm, giving an answer off by 1000.

2.7 Stick diagrams

A stick diagram is a topology sketch of a CMOS gate. It uses one line per layer — diffusion, polysilicon, metal — and a dot for each contact. It has no dimensions. It keeps only order and crossings. The sketch exists because a layout's electrical behaviour depends first on which pieces touch and which cross, not on exact widths. Designers use it to plan a standard cell, to check a transistor network against a drawn layout, and to find a clean gate ordering before drawing real shapes.

You need: Chapter 2.6 for real widths and spacing, and Unit 3 for reading a CMOS gate as series and parallel networks.

Lines, crossings and rails

A stick diagram is a map, not a mask. Each straight line is a whole layer. A diffusion line is the source-drain path. A poly line is a gate. A metal line is wiring. A dot is a contact between two layers. The top metal rail is VDD and the bottom metal rail is VSS: every pMOS source that must go high contacts VDD, every nMOS source that must go low contacts VSS.

A poly line crossing diffusion always forms a transistor; poly cannot cross diffusion as plain wiring. The poly is the gate and the diffusion below it is the channel. Metal crossing poly or diffusion with no contact dot is only a crossing, not a connection. If a poly column crosses diffusion where you wanted no transistor, you have drawn an unwanted device: route the poly around, break the diffusion, or change the column order.

  • Convention: diffusion green, poly red, metal blue, contact a black dot.
  • No dimensions in the sketch; widths and spacings come from Chapter 2.6.
  • Only order and crossings matter: which diffusion segment two transistors share, and which poly column comes first.

Shared source-drain regions show up in the sketch. Two series transistors can share one diffusion segment; that segment is a node, not a metal contact, unless the circuit needs it. Contacts drop from a rail to diffusion only at the correct source.

Euler path sets the column order

Write the gate as two networks. The nMOS pull-down conducts when the output must be 0; the pMOS pull-up conducts when the output must be 1. Series transistors are an AND condition, parallel transistors an OR condition. For y = ¬(a·b + c), the nMOS network has a and b in series and c in parallel with that pair. The pMOS network is the dual: c from y to an internal node n2, then a and b in parallel from n2 to VDD.

One continuous diffusion strip per side is best: it removes extra contacts and breaks. To draw one strip, walk through every transistor edge exactly once. That walk is an Euler path. The graph's vertices are the network's nodes; its edges are the transistors. The edge order in the path is the poly column order along the strip.

An Euler path exists only between the two vertices of odd degree. If every vertex has even degree, the Euler trail is a closed circuit: the strip returns to its starting node, so both ends of the strip contact the same rail or node. Use the same edge order on both sides; if that is impossible, add a diffusion break. Then place poly columns left to right in that order.

  1. Draw the nMOS graph and the pMOS graph; mark VDD, VSS, y and internal nodes.
  2. Find the odd-degree vertices on each side; the Euler path must run between them.
  3. Walk the nMOS graph from one odd vertex to the other; if all degrees are even, start and end at the same vertex.
  4. Do the same on the pMOS graph, keeping the same edge order if possible.
  5. Place poly columns left to right in that order.

For y = ¬(a·b + c) the two sides share the order c, a, b. The n-graph (VSS, y, n1) has all degrees even, so its trail is a closed circuit: VSS–c–y–a–n1–b–VSS. Both ends of the n-strip contact the VSS rail, and the y segment carries the output contact. The p-graph has odd vertices y and n2, so its trail runs y–c–n2–a–VDD–b–n2: the VDD segment sits in the middle with a rail contact, and the two n2 ends are joined by a metal line.

From sticks to real shapes

The stick diagram fixes topology; Chapter 2.6 fixes dimensions. Transfer the column order first, then draw the diffusion strips, then the poly columns, then the contacts and rails.

  • Each layer line becomes a drawn shape of the width the design rules give, and each gap becomes a spacing from the design rules, all in λ.
  • Put contacts at VDD, VSS, y and any internal node that needs metal.
  • Check that every poly-diffusion crossing is a wanted transistor; count them.
  • Do not add a crossing to make the drawing neat.

For y = ¬(a·b + c) the finished static CMOS gate has 3 nMOS plus 3 pMOS transistors, so the mask layout must show 6 wanted poly-diffusion crossings. The stick diagram shows 3 poly columns because each input column crosses both diffusion strips. One missing crossing means one missing device; one extra crossing means a short or an unwanted channel.

Worked example

Question. For the CMOS gate y = ¬(a·b + c): (a) write the nMOS and pMOS networks, (b) find an Euler path on each side, (c) state how many diffusion strips and poly columns the stick diagram needs.

  1. Output is 0 when a·b + c = 1, so the nMOS pull-down must conduct when a = 1 and b = 1, or when c = 1.
  2. nMOS network: a and b in series, c in parallel with that pair. Three nMOS transistors.
  3. pMOS dual: output is 1 when (a = 0 or b = 0) and c = 0. Draw c from y to n2, and a and b in parallel from n2 to VDD. Three pMOS transistors.
  4. n-graph vertices VSS, y, n1: edges c: VSS–y, a: y–n1, b: n1–VSS. Every degree is 2, so the trail is a closed circuit VSS–c–y–a–n1–b–VSS. Both strip ends contact VSS; the y segment takes the output contact.
  5. p-graph vertices y, n2, VDD: edges c: y–n2, a: n2–VDD, b: n2–VDD. The odd vertices are y (degree 1) and n2 (degree 3), so the trail runs y–c–n2–a–VDD–b–n2. The middle VDD segment takes a rail contact; the two n2 ends are joined by metal.
  6. Both sides use edge order c, a, b: three poly columns, one n-diffusion strip and one p-diffusion strip.

Answer. The stick diagram needs 1 n-diffusion strip, 1 p-diffusion strip and 3 poly columns, with column order c, a, b.

Exam traps

  • A poly-diffusion crossing is always a transistor. Never route poly across diffusion as plain wiring.
  • Series and parallel swap between nMOS and pMOS. Write the dual; do not copy the nMOS network to the pMOS side.
  • An Euler trail runs between the two odd-degree vertices. When every degree is even the trail is closed, so both strip ends meet the same rail or node.
  • The same input order must be used on both sides; reversing one side breaks the shared poly columns.
  • Stick diagrams carry no dimensions, and a contact is not a transistor. Missing contacts leave a source floating; extra contacts short two nodes.

2.8 Latch-up in CMOS and its prevention

In CMOS, the source diffusions, the well and the substrate form a hidden four-layer p-n-p-n switch. A current spike can forward-bias one of its junctions. If the parasitic npn and pnp transistors have enough gain and the well and substrate resistances are large enough, they feed each other and the supply rails short together. This is latch-up, and it can destroy or reset a chip. Layout prevents it with frequent substrate and well taps, guard rings, and process choices that lower the parasitic resistances. The current-injection test measures the margin.

You need Chapter 2.4 on well structure and Chapter 2.6 on layout design rules, because taps and guard rings are spacing decisions as much as circuit decisions.

The hidden p-n-p-n switch

In an n-well CMOS process, every PMOS sits inside an n-well and every NMOS sits in the p-substrate. The PMOS source is a p+ diffusion tied to VDD. The NMOS source is an n+ diffusion tied to VSS. Between them lie the n-well and the p-substrate. These four regions form a p+ / n-well / p-substrate / n+ stack, which is a p-n-p-n structure.

Two parasitic bipolar transistors appear inside that stack. The pnp transistor has its emitter at the p+ PMOS source (VDD), its base in the n-well, and its collector in the p-substrate. The npn transistor has its emitter at the n+ NMOS source (VSS), its base in the p-substrate, and its collector in the n-well. Neither transistor is drawn or intended, but both are physically present.

The collector of each transistor drives the base of the other. The npn collector is the n-well, which is the pnp base. The pnp collector is the p-substrate, which is the npn base. The n-well has resistance R_well from the base region to the well tap. The p-substrate has resistance R_sub from the base region to the substrate tap. Together the two transistors and these two resistors close a positive-feedback loop, as the figure shows.

Triggering and the regenerative condition

Latch-up needs a trigger. A current spike on an input or output pad can inject carriers into the substrate. An input undershoot below VSS can forward-bias an n+ diffusion. A fast supply ramp or radiation can create electron-hole pairs. Any of these can make a base-emitter junction conduct. A 2 mA spike through 500 Ω, for example, gives 1.0 V, enough to turn on a silicon junction.

V_sub = I_sub × R_sub,   V_well = I_C,npn × R_well,   β_npn × β_pnp > 1

V_sub is how far the substrate rises above VSS and V_well is how far the well falls below VDD (V). I_sub is the injected substrate current (A). I_C,npn is the npn collector current flowing from the well tap through R_well (A). R_sub and R_well are the substrate and well resistances (Ω). β_npn and β_pnp are the common-emitter current gains of the parasitic transistors. V_sub raises the substrate above VSS and V_well lowers the well below VDD; either junction reaching about 0.7 V forward-biases its emitter and turns that transistor on. The loop is regenerative when both emitters are forward biased and the gain product exceeds one.

Both emitters must be forward biased for the loop to close. Once they are on, the npn collector current supplies the pnp base, and the pnp collector current supplies the npn base. If β_npn × β_pnp > 1, each pass around the loop adds current. The current rises until the supply limits it. The path then stays on after the trigger is gone, and only removing power resets it. If the gain product is below one, the disturbance dies out.

Prevention: taps, guard rings and process

Layout is the first defence. Place p+ substrate taps tied to VSS close to every NMOS, and n+ well taps tied to VDD close to every PMOS. Tap spacing matters more than tap presence, because R_sub and R_well are the resistances from the injection point to the nearest tap. A tap that is far away does little for a device that injects current locally. Put taps on both sides of a wide transistor. Surround sensitive NMOS devices with a p+ guard ring tied to VSS, and sensitive PMOS devices with an n+ guard ring tied to VDD. The ring lowers local resistance and diverts carriers before they reach a parasitic base.

Process choices lower the resistances further. A low-resistance p+ substrate with a thin p-epitaxial layer shunts substrate current. Retrograde wells place their doping peak below the surface instead of at it. This lowers the well sheet resistance and keeps the well-substrate junction shallow, both of which weaken the parasitic pnp. Shallow trench isolation removes the deep sidewall paths of older LOCOS isolation. Silicide on diffusions and taps reduces sheet resistance. A standard current-injection latch-up test injects current into a pin, removes it, and checks whether the supply current stays high; a sustained high current means the chip has latched.

Worked example

Question. An n-well CMOS inverter has R_sub = 200 Ω and R_well = 1 kΩ, with β_npn = 50 and β_pnp = 20. A spike injects 2 mA into the substrate. (a) Find the substrate drop; the npn emitter needs 0.7 V to turn on. Does it turn on? (b) Repeat for R_sub = 500 Ω. (c) Given that both emitters are forward biased, is the loop regenerative?

  1. Convert the injected current: I_inj = 2 mA = 0.002 A.
  2. For R_sub = 200 Ω, V_sub = I_inj × R_sub = 0.002 A × 200 Ω = 0.4 V. Since 0.4 V < 0.7 V, the npn does not turn on.
  3. For R_sub = 500 Ω, V_sub = I_inj × R_sub = 0.002 A × 500 Ω = 1.0 V. Since 1.0 V > 0.7 V, the npn turns on.
  4. The loop gain is β_npn × β_pnp = 50 × 20 = 1000. Since 1000 > 1, once both emitters are forward biased each pass around the loop adds current, so the loop is regenerative.

Answer. With R_sub = 200 Ω the substrate drop is 0.4 V, below 0.7 V, so the npn stays off and there is no latch-up; with R_sub = 500 Ω the drop is 1.0 V and the npn turns on. Given that both emitters are forward biased, yes, the loop is regenerative, because β_npn × β_pnp = 1000 > 1 and each pass around the loop adds current.

Exam traps

  • Adding a tap anywhere is not enough: what matters is the resistance from the injection point to the nearest tap. A distant tap leaves a large R_sub or R_well.
  • Latch-up is not the same as ESD damage. ESD is a fast overvoltage event; latch-up is a sustained low-resistance path that remains after the trigger.
  • The loop condition is the product β_npn × β_pnp > 1, not the sum. Two modest gains can still latch if both are above about 1.
  • A tap does not replace a guard ring. The ring lowers local resistance and diverts carriers, while the tap sets the reference potential.
  • In the current-injection test, the chip has latched only if the supply current stays high after the injected current is removed; a temporary rise is not latch-up.
