2.1 Oxidation, diffusion and ion implantation

Three unit processes put material and dopant where a transistor needs them. Oxidation grows silicon dioxide that insulates, masks and protects. Diffusion moves dopant atoms into the crystal with heat. Ion implantation fires dopants in at a set energy and dose, so the depth is chosen in keV. The gate oxide, the wells and the source-drain junctions of every CMOS flow come from these steps.

You need: donors, acceptors and the MOS structure. The mask window is opened in the chapter on lithography, etching and deposition.

Thermal oxidation: growing the oxide

Silicon oxidises into a dense, stable insulator that sticks to the silicon beneath it, so one material gives both the semiconductor and its insulator. SiO2 serves as the gate insulator, as field insulator between devices, and as a mask that dopants cannot pass.

Thermal oxidation runs in a furnace at 900–1100 °C. Dry oxidation uses oxygen: Si + O2 → SiO2. Wet oxidation uses steam: Si + 2H2O → SiO2 + 2H2. Wet growth is many times faster and makes the thick field oxide. Dry growth is slow and dense and makes the thin gate oxide, where a few nanometres set the threshold voltage (see Threshold voltage and body effect).

xox² + A·xox = B·(t + τ)

xox is the oxide thickness in μm; t is the time in hours; A (μm) and B (μm²/h) depend on temperature and on the gas; τ allows for oxide already present. This is the Deal-Grove model.

  • Thin oxide: xox ≈ (B/A)(t + τ). The surface reaction limits growth, so it is linear in time.
  • Thick oxide: xox ≈ √(B·t). The oxidant must diffuse through the oxide already grown, so growth slows to a square root.
  • Growing 1 μm of oxide uses about 0.44 μm of silicon; the rest of the film rises above the old surface.
  • Gate oxide is dry, often 2–10 nm; field oxide is wet, 0.2–1 μm.

Diffusion: moving dopants with heat

A dopant atom hops at random between lattice sites, but a concentration gradient makes more hops go downhill. Fick's first law gives the flux: J = −D(∂C/∂x), with J in atoms cm⁻² s⁻¹, D the diffusion coefficient in cm²/s, C the concentration in cm⁻³ and x the depth. Conservation of atoms gives Fick's second law, ∂C/∂t = D(∂²C/∂x²).

D = D0·exp(−Ea/kT)

D0 is the pre-exponential factor, in cm²/s like D; Ea is the activation energy, about 3–4 eV for common dopants; k = 8.62×10⁻⁵ eV/K; T is in kelvin. Near 1000 °C, D roughly doubles for every 30 °C, so furnace temperature sets junction depth.

A doping step has two parts. Predeposition holds the wafer in a dopant gas: the surface sits at the solid solubility and a complementary error function (erfc) profile with a fixed dose Q forms. Drive-in removes the source and heats the wafer further: the dose spreads deeper as a Gaussian and the surface concentration falls.

C(x, t) = (Q / √(πDt))·exp(−x²/4Dt)

C is the concentration in cm⁻³ at depth x; Q is the dose in cm⁻². The surface value is Cs = Q/√(πDt); at one diffusion length, x = L = 2√(Dt), the concentration has fallen to Cs/e.

xj = 2√(Dt) · √ln(Cs/NB)

xj is the junction depth, where the dopant equals the wafer's background doping NB. It is usually two to three diffusion lengths, so L and xj are different numbers.

Diffusion is isotropic: dopant spreads sideways under a mask edge nearly as far as it goes down, about 0.8 xj. Layout must leave room for this, and the layout design rules encode it.

Ion implantation: placing dopants by energy

Diffusion cannot make a doping that is shallow, heavy and precisely placed; implantation can. Dopant ions are accelerated through tens to hundreds of keV and fired at the wafer, where nuclear collisions and electronic drag stop them at a depth set by the energy.

The dose φ is the number of ions per area, typically 10¹¹ to 10¹⁶ cm⁻², counted from the beam current. The average stopping depth is the projected range Rp, and the spread around it is the straggle ΔRp.

C(x) = (φ / (√(2π)·ΔRp))·exp(−(x − Rp)² / (2ΔRp²))

C(x) is in cm⁻³ at depth x; φ is in cm⁻²; Rp and ΔRp are in cm. The peak, at x = Rp, is about 0.4φ/ΔRp.

If the beam lines up with a crystal channel, ions travel far deeper and leave a tail, so the wafer is tilted about 7° and rotated. A mask must be about Rp + 4ΔRp thick to block the implant; one just thicker than Rp lets almost half the dose through. When the poly gate is the mask, source and drain line up with its edge without a separate alignment: a self-aligned implant.

The collisions leave a damaged, partly amorphous layer. A short anneal near 900–1000 °C repairs the lattice and moves dopants onto lattice sites, which activates them; too long or too hot an anneal diffuses the profile away.

Worked example

Question. A predeposition puts a phosphorus dose Q = 1×10¹⁴ cm⁻² into a p-type wafer doped NB = 1×10¹⁶ cm⁻³. It is then driven in at 1000 °C, where D = 3×10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s, for t = 1 hour. (a) Find the diffusion length L = 2√(Dt). (b) Find the surface concentration after the drive-in. (c) Find the junction depth xj. (d) Is the junction deeper or shallower than 0.5 μm, and why is xj not equal to L?

  1. Time in seconds: t = 1 h = 3600 s. Then D·t = 3×10⁻¹⁴ cm²/s × 3600 s = 1.08×10⁻¹⁰ cm².
  2. (a) √(D·t) = 1.04×10⁻⁵ cm, so L = 2 × 1.04×10⁻⁵ cm = 2.08×10⁻⁵ cm = 0.208 μm.
  3. (b) Cs = Q/√(πDt) = 1×10¹⁴ / √(π × 1.08×10⁻¹⁰) = 1×10¹⁴ / 1.84×10⁻⁵ = 5.43×10¹⁸ cm⁻³.
  4. (c) The junction is where C(xj) = NB. Cs/NB = 5.43×10¹⁸ / 1×10¹⁶ = 543; ln 543 = 6.30; √6.30 = 2.51.
  5. xj = L × 2.51 = 0.208 μm × 2.51 = 0.522 μm.
  6. (d) 0.522 μm is just deeper than 0.5 μm. At x = L the dopant is still Cs/e = 2.0×10¹⁸ cm⁻³, 200 times the background; the type changes only at 2.51 L.
  7. Check: (xj/L)² = (0.522/0.208)² = 6.30 = ln 543, so C(xj) = Cs/543 = 1.0×10¹⁶ cm⁻³.

Answer. L = 0.21 μm, Cs = 5.4×10¹⁸ cm⁻³ and xj = 0.52 μm, just deeper than 0.5 μm. The junction lies 2.5 diffusion lengths down because the dopant must fall all the way to the background doping, not just to 1/e of its surface value.

Worked example 2

Question. Boron is implanted into an n-well doped ND = 1×10¹⁷ cm⁻³ with dose φ = 1×10¹⁵ cm⁻², Rp = 0.10 μm and ΔRp = 0.03 μm. (a) Find the peak concentration. (b) Find the depth of the p-n junction under the implant. (c) Taking the range in oxide as close to the range in silicon, how thick must an oxide mask be to block the implant elsewhere?

  1. (a) ΔRp = 0.03 μm = 3×10⁻⁶ cm. Cp = φ/(√(2π)·ΔRp) = 1×10¹⁵ / (2.507 × 3×10⁻⁶) = 1.33×10²⁰ cm⁻³.
  2. (b) Below the peak the boron falls to the well doping where Cp·exp(−(x − Rp)²/2ΔRp²) = ND.
  3. So x − Rp = ΔRp·√(2 ln(Cp/ND)) = 0.03 × √(2 ln 1330) = 0.03 × √14.4 = 0.03 × 3.79 = 0.114 μm.
  4. xj = 0.10 + 0.114 = 0.214 μm. The surface value, 5.1×10¹⁷ cm⁻³, is also above ND, so the layer is p-type from the surface down.
  5. (c) Mask thickness ≥ Rp + 4ΔRp = 0.10 + 4 × 0.03 = 0.22 μm of oxide.

Answer. Peak 1.33×10²⁰ cm⁻³ at 0.10 μm; junction at 0.214 μm; an oxide mask about 0.22 μm thick blocks the implant.

Exam traps

  • Dose is per area (cm⁻²); concentration is per volume (cm⁻³). A 10¹⁵ cm⁻² implant spread over 0.1 μm gives about 10²⁰ cm⁻³, not 10¹⁵ cm⁻³.
  • L = 2√(Dt) is not the junction depth. The junction is where the dopant falls to the background doping, xj = L·√ln(Cs/NB), usually two to three times L.
  • Wet oxide is fast and thick, dry oxide is slow and dense. Gate oxide is dry, field oxide is wet — not the other way round.
  • Deal-Grove: linear for thin oxide (reaction-limited), parabolic for thick (diffusion-limited). Examiners deliberately swap the two labels.

Understand Why does oxide growth slow down as the film gets thicker? 3 marks

The oxidant must reach the silicon by diffusing through the oxide already grown. In the thick-film limit that diffusion, not the surface reaction, limits the rate, so xox grows as √(B·t) instead of linearly.

Apply Dry oxidation at 1000 °C has A = 0.165 μm and B = 0.0117 μm²/h. Ignoring τ, how long does a 10 nm gate oxide take, and how far off is the linear limit? 4 marks

Exact: t = (0.0001 + 0.00165)/0.0117 = 0.150 h ≈ 9.0 min. Linear: B/A = 0.0709 μm/h, t = 0.01/0.0709 = 0.141 h ≈ 8.5 min, 6 % short, so the linear limit is close enough.

Analyze A wet field oxidation gives 0.2 μm; 0.5 μm is wanted. In the parabolic regime (x² = B·t), by what factor must the total time grow, and how much extra time is that? 4 marks

t grows as x², so the total time must be (0.5/0.2)² = 6.25 times the first run. The extra time is 5.25 times the first run.

Analyze Why is the wafer tilted about 7° during implantation? 4 marks

To prevent channeling. If the beam is parallel to a major crystal direction, ions travel between atom rows and stop far deeper than Rp, leaving an unwanted tail; the tilt plus a rotation closes every channel.

Remember What is the difference between predeposition and drive-in? 2 marks

Predeposition keeps a dopant source on the surface at a lower temperature, so a fixed dose enters at the solubility limit. Drive-in removes the source and raises the temperature, so the existing dopants spread deeper and the surface concentration drops.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Modern fabs run these steps for seconds: rapid thermal oxidation, spike anneals and millisecond laser anneals keep a 20 nm junction where it was put. A 1 nm oxide leaks by tunnelling, so the gate insulator is now nitrided oxide or high-k HfO2. Search for rapid thermal processing, high-k metal gate and plasma doping.

2.2 Lithography, etching and deposition

Every CMOS layer starts as a pattern in a polymer film called resist. Lithography prints that pattern: clean, coat, bake, align a mask, expose, develop. The resist then protects some areas while the layer below is cut away by etching or built up by deposition. Strip the resist and the wafer is ready for the next mask. The same loop runs for every layer, so its resolution sets the smallest transistor and wire. Fabs use it for all CMOS and BiCMOS products.

You need: the chapter on oxidation, diffusion and ion implantation, where thermal oxide is grown as the layer this resist patterns.

Lithography: the print loop

Lithography transfers a pattern from a mask to resist. The wafer is cleaned, coated with a light-sensitive polymer called resist, and soft baked to remove solvent. The mask (a 1× pattern) or reticle (usually 4× or 5×, reduced by the lens) is aligned to the previous layer. Light exposes the resist through the clear windows. Developer dissolves the exposed resist in a positive resist; in a negative resist the exposed part cross-links and stays, and the unexposed part dissolves. After etch or deposition, a strip removes all resist.

  1. Clean and dehydrate the wafer so resist sticks.
  2. Spin-coat resist to a uniform thickness, often below 1 μm.
  3. Soft bake to drive off solvent.
  4. Align the mask or reticle to the existing pattern.
  5. Expose with UV light through the mask.
  6. Develop: positive resist removes exposed areas; negative resist keeps them.
  7. Etch or deposit, then strip the resist.

Older printers place the mask in contact with the resist (contact printing) or close to it (proximity printing). Contact printing resolves well, but mask and resist touch, so the mask picks up damage and particles. Proximity printing leaves a gap of a few to tens of micrometres, which saves the mask but blurs the image by diffraction. Projection printing uses a lens to image the reticle, so the mask never touches the wafer. Resolution is R = k·λ/NA, where R is the smallest printable feature, the half-pitch of a dense line-and-space pattern, in nm, λ is the exposure wavelength in nm, NA is the numerical aperture of the lens, and k is a process factor (about 0.5 to 0.8 for simple processes; immersion lenses and resolution-enhancement tricks bring it down to about 0.3, and 0.25 is the limit for one exposure). Depth of focus is DOF = λ/(2NA²), the vertical distance over which the image stays sharp. A shorter λ or larger NA lowers R but also lowers DOF.

R = k·λ / NA DOF = λ / (2NA²)

R is the smallest printable feature (half-pitch) in nm; λ is the exposure wavelength in nm; NA is the lens numerical aperture; k is the process factor; DOF is the depth of focus in nm.

Etching: cut the layer below

Etching removes material through the resist window. Wet etching uses a liquid acid or base. It is isotropic: it etches sideways at the same rate as down, so the hole undercuts the resist and ends wider than the window. Dry etching uses a plasma of ions and radicals. Reactive ion etching (RIE) combines chemical attack with ion bombardment that clears the floor of the hole, so it is anisotropic: the sidewalls stay nearly vertical. Selectivity is the etch rate of the target layer divided by the etch rate of resist or the layer below; high selectivity means the mask and underlayer survive. Endpoint detection watches the plasma emission or a reflected laser signal and stops when the target layer clears. A short over-etch, often 10-30% of the main time, removes residue.

Deposition and planarisation

Deposition adds a thin film. LPCVD (low-pressure chemical vapour deposition) runs at high temperature and gives conformal films, but the wafer sees the furnace temperature. PECVD (plasma-enhanced CVD) uses a plasma to allow lower temperatures, useful after aluminium. Sputtering strikes a target with ions and throws atoms onto the wafer; it deposits metals such as aluminium. Evaporation heats a source in vacuum; it is simple but has poor step coverage. Epitaxy grows a single-crystal silicon layer on the wafer, lattice-matched to the substrate. In BiCMOS it forms the lightly doped collector (see the chapter on BiCMOS technology).

  • LPCVD: high temperature; conformal poly-Si and nitride.
  • PECVD: plasma allows lower temperature; oxide and nitride over metal.
  • Sputtering: ion bombardment of a target; aluminium and barrier metals.
  • Evaporation: heated source in vacuum; simple laboratory metal films.
  • Epitaxy: single-crystal Si on Si; lightly doped BiCMOS collector.

After deposition, the surface is often not flat. CMP (chemical mechanical planarisation) polishes the wafer with a slurry and a pad until the step height falls, so the next lithography step can focus. Planarisation matters because DOF is only a few hundred nm for advanced lenses.

Worked example 2

Question. A 0.5 μm oxide is etched through a 2.0 μm wide resist window. The oxide etches at 100 nm/min downward, the selectivity of oxide to resist is 5 : 1, and a 20 % over-etch is used. (a) Find the etch time. (b) Find the width of the hole at the top of the oxide for a wet etch and for RIE with anisotropy A = 0.9. (c) How much resist is lost?

  1. (a) Main etch: 500 nm ÷ 100 nm/min = 5.0 min. With 20 % over-etch: 5.0 × 1.2 = 6.0 min.
  2. (b) Wet etch is isotropic, so the sideways rate is also 100 nm/min. Undercut per side = 100 × 6.0 = 600 nm = 0.6 μm.
  3. Hole width at the top = 2.0 + 2 × 0.6 = 3.2 μm.
  4. RIE: A = 1 − lateral/vertical = 0.9, so the lateral rate is 0.1 × 100 = 10 nm/min. Undercut per side = 10 × 6.0 = 60 nm.
  5. Hole width at the top = 2.0 + 2 × 0.06 = 2.12 μm.
  6. (c) Resist etch rate = 100 ÷ 5 = 20 nm/min, so the resist loses 20 × 6.0 = 120 nm.

Answer. Etch time 6.0 min; the hole is 3.2 μm wide after a wet etch but 2.12 μm after RIE; 120 nm of resist is lost.

Exam traps

  • Wet etching undercuts the mask: the hole is wider than the window at the surface. A square vertical profile is wrong for wet etch.
  • Positive resist: the exposed part dissolves. Negative resist: the exposed part stays. Swapping them reverses the whole pattern.
  • A mask is 1×; a reticle is usually 4× or 5× and the projection lens reduces it. Do not use reticle dimensions directly as wafer dimensions.
  • R = λ/NA is incomplete; the process factor k (from about 0.8 down to 0.25) must be included. DOF is in nm, not μm, unless converted.
  • Over-etch is intentional, not a defect; endpoint detection is a signal, not a fixed time.

2.3 NMOS and CMOS fabrication process flow

Fabrication is a sequence of masks. Each mask lets one layer be grown, deposited, implanted or cut in a fixed order. Temperature decides what can come after what, and alignment decides how the layers land on each other. Follow an nMOS transistor first, then the extra masks that put an nMOS and a pMOS on the same die. The order explains why source and drain are self-aligned to the gate, and why contacts and metal come last.

You need: the chapter on oxidation, diffusion and ion implantation for oxidation, diffusion and ion implantation, and the chapter on lithography, etching and deposition for lithography, etching and deposition; the MOS structure and its operating regions for what a transistor needs.

The nMOS-only flow

Begin with a p-type silicon wafer. The active mask defines the islands where transistors will sit. Outside each island the field oxide, a thick oxide, isolates neighbours.

  1. Form field oxide outside the active islands. LOCOS, local oxidation of silicon, grows thick oxide locally but spreads sideways as a bird's beak. STI, shallow trench isolation, etches a trench and fills it with oxide.
  2. Grow the gate oxide, a thin SiO2 layer, then deposit polycrystalline silicon (poly).
  3. Use the poly mask and etch to leave the gate. Dope the poly so it conducts.
  4. Implant a light n− dose for the lightly doped drain, LDD. The poly gate and field oxide block the implant elsewhere.
  5. Deposit an oxide spacer and etch it back. The spacer sets the offset for the heavy n+ implant.
  6. Implant n+ source and drain, then anneal to activate dopants. The poly gate with its spacers is the mask, so source and drain line up with the gate without a separate alignment.
  7. Open contacts, deposit and pattern metal, passivate, and open the pads.

The self-aligned step is the key. If source and drain were implanted through a separate window, mask misalignment would leave a gap or overlap. With the gate as the mask, the only offset comes from the spacer, set by deposition and etch, not by lithography.

The CMOS flow: wells and two S/D masks

CMOS, complementary MOS, needs an nMOS and a pMOS. In a simple n-well flow on a p-type wafer, the pMOS sits inside an n-well. The n-well mask comes before the active mask: implant phosphorus and drive it in. The nMOS stays in the p-substrate. Well variants are in the chapter on p-well, n-well and twin-tub processes.

After wells, the flow repeats the nMOS steps: active, gate oxide, poly, poly mask. Then it splits. The n+ source/drain mask covers the pMOS area with resist and opens the nMOS area. An arsenic or phosphorus implant makes n+ source and drain. The p+ source/drain mask covers the nMOS and opens the pMOS. A boron implant makes p+ source and drain. Two masks exist because one implant must not enter the other transistor: n+ in a p+ region would compensate the doping.

Then come contact, metal 1, via (a hole etched in the oxide between two metal layers and filled with metal), metal 2, and pad. Each is one lithography step with its own mask. The figure lists all ten masks in order. The back end starts at contact: wiring, not transistor formation.

Why the order is fixed

The order follows temperature and alignment. Well implants and isolation see high-temperature drives, so they come before the gate oxide. The gate oxide must stay clean and thin; later high-temperature steps are limited. Poly is deposited after gate oxide, and the source/drain implant comes after poly because the poly is the mask. The activation anneal follows the implants. Contacts and metal come after that anneal because metal wiring cannot take the same heat.

The line splits into front-end-of-line (FEOL), where transistors are made, and back-end-of-line (BEOL), where contacts, vias and wires are made. Contact is the first back-end step. Passivation protects the chip; the pad mask opens windows over the pads so a bond wire or probe can touch metal.

MOS memory processes reuse this flow and add layers. DRAM adds a stacked capacitor; flash adds a floating gate. The cells and their operation are in Memory design: SRAM, DRAM and ROM. Here, only note that memory adds masks; it does not change the logic of the mask sequence.

From wafer to packaged chip

The mask flow is the middle of a longer chain, and some syllabi ask for the whole wafer-to-chip flow. Each stage hands the next a product it can test.

  1. Crystal growth: a single-crystal silicon ingot is pulled from molten silicon (the Czochralski method), doped p- or n-type.
  2. Wafer preparation: the ingot is sliced into wafers, today 300 mm across and under 1 mm thick, then lapped, etched and polished to a mirror finish.
  3. Front end: wells, isolation, gate and source/drain, as above.
  4. Back end: contacts, vias and metal layers, then passivation and pad openings.
  5. Wafer probe: every die is tested on the wafer and failing dies are marked.
  6. Dicing: the wafer is sawn into separate dies.
  7. Packaging: good dies are attached to a package, joined by bond wires or solder bumps, and sealed.
  8. Final test: packaged chips are tested again, sorted by speed, and shipped.

N ≈ π(d/2)²/A − πd/√(2A) Y = e−A·D

N is the number of whole dies on a wafer of diameter d (cm) for die area A (cm²); the second term removes the part dies at the edge. Y is the fraction of dies with no killer defect when defects of density D (per cm²) fall at random.

Worked example

Question. A CMOS process uses these masks, one lithography step each: n-well, active, poly, n+ source/drain, p+ source/drain, contact, metal 1, via, metal 2, pad. (a) How many lithography steps? (b) A die is 0.5 cm² and the line has D = 1 defect/cm²; use Y = e−A·D for yield. (c) How many steps if metal 2 is dropped? (d) How many good dies does a 300 mm wafer give? Use N ≈ π(d/2)²/A − πd/√(2A).

  1. Count the masks: n-well, active, poly, n+ S/D, p+ S/D, contact, metal 1, via, metal 2, pad. That is 10 masks, and one lithography step each gives 10 steps.
  2. For yield, A = 0.5 cm² and D = 1 defect/cm². A·D = 0.5 × 1 = 0.5. Y = e−0.5 = 0.6065. As a percentage, 60.7%, about 61%.
  3. Drop metal 2. The via connects metal 1 to metal 2, so the via mask also goes. Steps = 10 − 2 = 8.
  4. (d) d = 30 cm: π × 15²/0.5 = 1413.7 and π × 30/√(2 × 0.5) = 94.2, so N = 1413.7 − 94.2 = 1319 whole dies.
  5. Good dies = N × Y = 1319 × 0.6065 = 800.

Exam traps

2.4 P-well, N-well and twin-tub processes

Every CMOS chip needs both an NMOS and a PMOS. An NMOS needs a p-type body; a PMOS needs an n-type body. One silicon substrate can supply only one doping, so the other device must sit in a locally doped well. The p-well, n-well and twin-tub processes are three answers to the same question: which device goes inside a well, and how freely can each body be doped? The choice fixes mask count, body resistance, threshold voltages and latch-up behaviour, and it appears in every CMOS process flow.

You need: the chapter on nMOS and CMOS fabrication process flow and the topic Threshold voltage and body effect.

Why one device must sit in a well

An NMOS conducts when electrons are pulled through a p-type body toward an n+ drain. A PMOS conducts when holes are pulled through an n-type body toward a p+ drain. The body is the terminal that sets the channel type and the threshold voltage. A plain silicon wafer is doped either p-type or n-type throughout; it cannot be both at the surface. The well solves this. A well is a deep doped region formed early in the flow, before the gate stack, by ion implantation and a high-temperature drive. The device inside the well uses the well as its body. The device outside uses the substrate as its body.

The well-substrate junction must stay reverse biased. An n-well sits in a p-substrate and is tied to the most positive supply. A p-well sits in an n-substrate and is tied to the most negative supply. The well is deep, not a thin sheet, so its doping and size affect junction capacitance and the parasitic devices discussed in the chapter on latch-up in CMOS and its prevention.

p-well, n-well and twin-tub

In a p-well process the wafer starts n-type. A p-well holds the NMOS; the PMOS sits in the n-substrate. The n-substrate is tied to VDD. In an n-well process the wafer starts p-type. An n-well holds the PMOS; the NMOS sits in the p-substrate, which is tied to VSS. This is the common choice. The NMOS keeps the lightly doped substrate as its body. In a twin-tub process the starting wafer is usually p-type, but both an n-well and a p-well are implanted and driven separately. The PMOS body is the n-well; the NMOS body is the p-well. Each well has its own doping dose, so both threshold voltages and both body-effect coefficients can be set. The price is one extra well mask and a longer thermal budget.

Trade-offs and newer options

Most digital CMOS uses n-well. The NMOS, which carries most of the logic current, sits in the lightly doped substrate, where its electron mobility is high and its junction capacitance and body effect are low; the well dose is then chosen to suit the PMOS. A p-type wafer at VSS suits the whole die, and the flow grew straight out of the older nMOS process. For latch-up, the wafer is often a thin, lightly doped p-epitaxial layer on a heavily doped p+ substrate, whose low resistance shunts the parasitic path. Twin-tub costs one mask but gives independent control; it appears in low-voltage, analog and mixed-signal processes where matching and body effect matter. A triple-well process adds a deep n-well under a p-well. That isolates the p-well from the p-substrate, so a block of NMOS can have its own body bias, or a noisy digital block can be separated from analog devices. Silicon-on-insulator goes further: a buried oxide layer isolates the transistor body from the wafer, removing the well-substrate junction. SOI gives lower capacitance and no latch-up path, but the wafer is more expensive; only a few universities teach it in detail. The parasitic bipolar path itself is left to the chapter on latch-up in CMOS and its prevention.

2.5 BiCMOS technology

One chip, two transistors. CMOS gives high input impedance, low static power and dense logic. A bipolar transistor gives large transconductance and current drive. BiCMOS adds one vertical NPN to the CMOS flow with three or four extra masks, some before the wells and some after the gates. One die senses with CMOS and drives with bipolar. Uses: radio front ends, high-speed links, mixed-signal chips and power ICs.

You need the chapter on nMOS and CMOS fabrication process flow for the mask sequence, the chapter on p-well, n-well and twin-tub processes for wells, the chapter on oxidation, diffusion and ion implantation for doping, and MOSFET I-V characteristics and device equations for MOSFET transconductance.

The vertical NPN

An NPN has an n+ emitter, a p base and an n collector. In normal operation the emitter-base junction is forward biased and the base-collector junction is reverse biased, so electrons cross the thin base into the collector. Current gain is β = IC / IB, where IC is collector current and IB is base current. In an IC, β is typically 50 to 200.

The device is built vertically: a small n+ emitter window at the surface, a thin p base under it, and the n collector below. Current flows down into the wafer, so the device packs into a small area and the base can be very thin.

The collector must be lightly doped. It must hold the reverse bias of the base-collector junction without breaking down, and heavy doping would lower breakdown voltage and raise junction capacitance. Light doping means high resistivity, so the collector resistance RC is large: current travels a long, narrow n path to the collector contact, wasting voltage headroom and slowing the device.

Three steps fix this. A buried layer mask defines an n+ region in the p-substrate, implanted with arsenic or antimony at a heavy dose. A thin n-type epitaxial layer is grown over it and becomes the collector. A deep n+ sinker implant runs from the surface down to touch the buried layer, so collector current flows down a short, heavily doped pipe to the contact.

Epitaxial thickness is a trade-off. Thin epi gives low RC, so speed, but raises base-collector capacitance and lowers breakdown voltage. Thick, lightly doped epi holds high voltage and gives low capacitance, but RC is large. Neighbouring devices are separated by junction isolation: p-type regions surround each n island, so every pair of neighbours is held apart by a reverse-biased p-n junction.

Adding the NPN to the CMOS flow

The CMOS part is unchanged. Run the twin-tub flow (see p-well, n-well and twin-tub processes), but put the collector steps in front of it.

  1. Start on a p-substrate. Grow pad oxide, expose the buried layer mask, implant n+ arsenic or antimony, strip the resist.
  2. Grow thin, lightly doped n-type epitaxial silicon. This is the collector.
  3. Sinker implant immediately after the epitaxy, before or with the n-well/p-well implants. The well drive-in also diffuses the sinker down to the buried layer.
  4. Gate oxidation, poly deposition, gate mask and etch. Then n+ and p+ source/drain masks and the anneal.
  5. Base mask: shallow p implant for the NPN base.
  6. Emitter mask: shallow n+ arsenic implant for the emitter.
  7. Contacts, metal, passivation.

The order is fixed by the thermal budget. The buried layer goes first because the epitaxial growth and the well drive-in that follow are hot steps, and it must be diffused deep by them. The base is only a few tenths of a micrometre deep, so it comes after the hot steps, or is finished with a short low-temperature anneal. Extra heat spreads the base, and a wider base lowers both β and speed.

What it buys and what it costs

Transconductance is the payoff. For a bipolar transistor gm = IC / VT, where VT is the thermal voltage, about 26 mV at room temperature. For a MOSFET in saturation gm = 2 IC / Vov, where Vov = VGS − VTH is the overdrive. At the same current the bipolar wins because VT is only 26 mV while a usable overdrive is a few hundred millivolts.

gm = IC / VT (bipolar) gm = 2 IC / Vov (MOSFET, saturation)

gm is transconductance in A/V; IC is collector or drain current in A; VT is the thermal voltage, about 26 mV at 300 K; Vov = VGS − VTH is the overdrive in V; VGS is gate-source voltage; VTH is MOSFET threshold voltage. VT and VTH are different quantities.

The second payoff is a nearly constant base-emitter voltage. VBE sits near 0.7 V over a wide current range, so a bipolar pair makes a stable reference, a current mirror and a sharp comparator. CMOS brings a gate that draws no DC input current, dense logic and near-zero static power, because it burns power only while switching.

The cost is process. BiCMOS needs three or four masks beyond CMOS: buried layer, sinker, base and emitter. Each adds lithography, etch and implant, so cycle time grows and yield falls, and every step after the base must stay cool. The extra n+ regions also join the parasitic p-n-p-n path, so latch-up care in layout matters more (see the chapter on latch-up in CMOS and its prevention).

Against pure bipolar, BiCMOS gives up a little speed for density and low power. Typical uses: radio front ends with an NPN low-noise amplifier, high-speed drivers and serial links, mixed-signal chips, and power ICs with a bipolar bandgap reference.

  • Treating BiCMOS as plain CMOS plus one transistor. The buried layer, epitaxy and sinker are exactly what make the NPN fast, and what cost masks, yield and thermal budget.

2.6 Layout design rules

A layout is only useful if the fab can actually print it. Design rules are the minimum widths, spacings, overlaps and enclosures that a drawn shape must obey so that every mask prints, aligns and etches without breaks or shorts. Foundries publish these rules as a contract; designers draw to them, and a computer program checks every shape before the file is sent out. The same rules shape every standard cell, every SRAM bitcell and every pad ring in a chip.

You need: the chapter on nMOS and CMOS fabrication process flow for which layers exist, and the chapter on lithography, etching and deposition for why alignment errors happen.

Why a drawn shape needs a rule

A layout is a stack of rectangles, one set per layer. Each layer becomes a mask, and the fab prints that mask onto the wafer. Three things go wrong during printing.

  • Mask misalignment: the next mask lands a little off the one below. A contact drawn exactly on the metal edge may end up half outside it.
  • Etch bias: the etch cuts sideways as well as down, so each edge of a shape can move outward or inward by a small amount; a line or gap can print narrower or wider than drawn.
  • Finite resolution: the printer cannot form features below some size. Corners round off and very thin lines simply disappear.

A rule is a margin bought against these errors. Two shapes on the same mask move together, so mask misalignment does not change their spacing. The spacing rule guards against variation in printed size: if random variation lets each edge of a shape move outward by as much as δ (resist and etch bias, or lithographic variation), a gap shrinks by 2δ, so minimum spacing must exceed 2δ. Misalignment Δ enters only the rules between different layers, which is why overlap and enclosure must exceed Δ. Rules are therefore not laws of physics; they are a contract between the designer and the foundry.

A layout that breaks a rule is rejected before fabrication. A layout that obeys every rule still has to work electrically, which is a separate question.

λ, the scalable unit

Writing every rule in micrometres makes the rule set useless the moment the process shrinks. Instead, most teaching rule sets use one length unit, λ (lambda), chosen by the foundry. All rules are then whole multiples of λ.

L(μm) = n × λ(μm)

L is the drawn length in micrometres, n is the rule expressed in λ (a pure number), and λ is the unit length in micrometres, often half the smallest drawn feature.

A layout drawn in λ can be moved to a new process by changing one number. Some syllabi call these scalable design rules. Production processes instead publish micron rules, absolute values in μm or nm, which are tighter and layer-specific.

λ is not the wavelength of the lithography light. The wavelength is a property of the printer and is written in nm; λ here is only a drawing unit. Mixing the two is the commonest slip in an exam answer.

The rule families

Every rule set, however large, is built from a few families.

  • Minimum width: the narrowest rectangle allowed on a layer. A thinner line may break during etch.
  • Minimum spacing: the smallest gap allowed between two shapes on the same layer. A smaller gap may short.
  • Minimum overlap: two layers that must connect need at least this much shared area, so a misalignment still leaves contact.
  • Enclosure: one layer must surround another on all sides, for example metal around a contact.
  • Extension: one layer must stick out past another by a set amount, for example poly past the diffusion at the gate edge.
  • Separation between unrelated layers: a minimum distance between layers that never connect, to limit coupling and etch interference.

Minimum pitch is not a separate rule; it follows from the other two. For wires on one layer, pitch equals minimum width plus minimum spacing, measured centre to centre.

Checking the rules, and wire resistance

A design rule checker (DRC) reads the layout file, rebuilds each layer from the drawn shapes, and tests every rule on every pair of shapes. Real decks run to hundreds or thousands of rules. One violation blocks the design. DRC is only one check; a layout-versus-schematic (LVS) check confirms the drawn circuit matches the intended netlist, and parasitic extraction follows.

Rules also decide how much resistance a wire adds. A wire of uniform width is counted in squares, and each square contributes the same resistance however large the square is.

R = Rs × L / W

R is the wire resistance in Ω, Rs is the sheet resistance of the layer in Ω per square, L is the length along the current in μm, and W is the width in μm. The ratio L/W is the number of squares.

Because a minimum-width wire is the narrowest allowed, it is also the most resistive per unit length. Making a wire wider reduces R in proportion to W.

Worked example

Question. A process uses λ = 0.5 μm with these rules: metal 1 minimum width 3λ, metal 1 minimum spacing 3λ, poly minimum width 2λ, contact 2λ × 2λ, and metal enclosure of a contact 1λ on every side. 2.7 Stick diagrams

A stick diagram is a topology sketch of a CMOS gate. It uses one line per layer — diffusion, polysilicon, metal — and a dot for each contact. It has no dimensions. It keeps only order and crossings. The sketch exists because a layout's electrical behaviour depends first on which pieces touch and which cross, not on exact widths. Designers use it to plan a standard cell, to check a transistor network against a drawn layout, and to find a clean gate ordering before drawing real shapes.

You need: the chapter on layout design rules for real widths and spacing, and static CMOS and ratioed logic for reading a CMOS gate as series and parallel networks.

Lines, crossings and rails

A stick diagram is a map, not a mask. Each straight line is a whole layer. A diffusion line is the source-drain path. A poly line is a gate. A metal line is wiring. A dot is a contact between two layers. The top metal rail is VDD and the bottom metal rail is VSS: every pMOS source that must go high contacts VDD, every nMOS source that must go low contacts VSS.

A poly line crossing diffusion always forms a transistor; poly cannot cross diffusion as plain wiring. The poly is the gate and the diffusion below it is the channel. Metal crossing poly or diffusion with no contact dot is only a crossing, not a connection. If a poly column crosses diffusion where you wanted no transistor, you have drawn an unwanted device: route the poly around, break the diffusion, or change the column order.

  • Convention: diffusion green, poly red, metal blue, contact a black dot.
  • No dimensions in the sketch; widths and spacings come from the chapter on layout design rules.
  • Only order and crossings matter: which diffusion segment two transistors share, and which poly column comes first.

Shared source-drain regions show up in the sketch. Two series transistors can share one diffusion segment; that segment is a node, not a metal contact, unless the circuit needs it. Contacts drop from a rail to diffusion only at the correct source.

Euler path sets the column order

Write the gate as two networks. The nMOS pull-down conducts when the output must be 0; the pMOS pull-up conducts when the output must be 1. Series transistors are an AND condition, parallel transistors an OR condition. For y = ¬(a·b + c), the nMOS network has a and b in series and c in parallel with that pair. The pMOS network is the dual: c from y to an internal node n2, then a and b in parallel from n2 to VDD.

One continuous diffusion strip per side is best: it removes extra contacts and breaks. To draw one strip, walk through every transistor edge exactly once. That walk is an Euler path. The graph's vertices are the network's nodes; its edges are the transistors. The edge order in the path is the poly column order along the strip.

An Euler path exists only between the two vertices of odd degree. If every vertex has even degree, the Euler trail is a closed circuit: the strip returns to its starting node, so both ends of the strip contact the same rail or node. Use the same edge order on both sides; if that is impossible, add a diffusion break. Then place poly columns left to right in that order.

For y = ¬(a·b + c) the two sides share the order c, a, b. The n-graph (VSS, y, n1) has all degrees even, so its trail is a closed circuit: VSS–c–y–a–n1–b–VSS. Both ends of the n-strip contact the VSS rail, and the y segment carries the output contact. The p-graph has odd vertices y and n2, so its trail runs y–c–n2–a–VDD–b–n2: the VDD segment sits in the middle with a rail contact, and the two n2 ends are joined by a metal line.

From sticks to real shapes

The stick diagram fixes topology; the chapter on layout design rules fixes dimensions. Transfer the column order first, then draw the diffusion strips, then the poly columns, then the contacts and rails.

  • Each layer line becomes a drawn shape of the width the design rules give, and each gap becomes a spacing from the design rules, all in λ.
  • Put contacts at VDD, VSS, y and any internal node that needs metal.
  • Check that every poly-diffusion crossing is a wanted transistor; count them.
  • Do not add a crossing to make the drawing neat.

For y = ¬(a·b + c) the finished static CMOS gate has 3 nMOS plus 3 pMOS transistors, so the mask layout must show 6 wanted poly-diffusion crossings. The stick diagram shows 3 poly columns because each input column crosses both diffusion strips. One missing crossing means one missing device; one extra crossing means a short or an unwanted channel.

Worked example

Question. For the CMOS gate y = ¬(a·b + c): (a) write the nMOS and pMOS networks, (b) find an Euler path on each side, (c) state how many diffusion strips and poly columns the stick diagram needs.

  2. nMOS network: a and b in series, c in parallel with that pair. Three nMOS transistors.
  3. pMOS dual: output is 1 when (a = 0 or b = 0) and c = 0. Draw c from y to n2, and a and b in parallel from n2 to VDD. Three pMOS transistors.

Exam traps

  • A poly-diffusion crossing is always a transistor. Never route poly across diffusion as plain wiring.
  • Series and parallel swap between nMOS and pMOS. Write the dual; do not copy the nMOS network to the pMOS side.
  • An Euler trail runs between the two odd-degree vertices. When every degree is even the trail is closed, so both strip ends meet the same rail or node.
  • The same input order must be used on both sides; reversing one side breaks the shared poly columns.
  • Stick diagrams carry no dimensions, and a contact is not a transistor. Missing contacts leave a source floating; extra contacts short two nodes.

2.8 Latch-up in CMOS and its prevention

In CMOS, the source diffusions, the well and the substrate form a hidden four-layer p-n-p-n switch. A current spike can forward-bias one of its junctions. If the parasitic npn and pnp transistors have enough gain and the well and substrate resistances are large enough, they feed each other and the supply rails short together. This is latch-up, and it can destroy or reset a chip. Layout prevents it with frequent substrate and well taps, guard rings, and process choices that lower the parasitic resistances. The current-injection test measures the margin.

You need the chapter on p-well, n-well and twin-tub processes on well structure and the chapter on layout design rules on layout design rules, because taps and guard rings are spacing decisions as much as circuit decisions.

The hidden p-n-p-n switch

In an n-well CMOS process, every PMOS sits inside an n-well and every NMOS sits in the p-substrate. The PMOS source is a p+ diffusion tied to VDD. The NMOS source is an n+ diffusion tied to VSS. Between them lie the n-well and the p-substrate. These four regions form a p+ / n-well / p-substrate / n+ stack, which is a p-n-p-n structure.

Two parasitic bipolar transistors appear inside that stack. The pnp transistor has its emitter at the p+ PMOS source (VDD), its base in the n-well, and its collector in the p-substrate. The npn transistor has its emitter at the n+ NMOS source (VSS), its base in the p-substrate, and its collector in the n-well. Neither transistor is drawn or intended, but both are physically present.

The collector of each transistor drives the base of the other. The npn collector is the n-well, which is the pnp base. The pnp collector is the p-substrate, which is the npn base. The n-well has resistance Rwell from the base region to the well tap. The p-substrate has resistance Rsub from the base region to the substrate tap. Together the two transistors and these two resistors close a positive-feedback loop, as the figure shows.

Triggering and the regenerative condition

Latch-up needs a trigger. A current spike on an input or output pad can inject carriers into the substrate. An input undershoot below VSS can forward-bias an n+ diffusion. A fast supply ramp or radiation can create electron-hole pairs. Any of these can make a base-emitter junction conduct. A 2 mA spike through 500 Ω, for example, gives 1.0 V, enough to turn on a silicon junction.

Vsub = Isub × Rsub, Vwell = IC,npn × Rwell, βnpn × βpnp > 1

Vsub is how far the substrate rises above VSS and Vwell is how far the well falls below VDD (V). Isub is the injected substrate current (A). IC,npn is the npn collector current flowing from the well tap through Rwell (A). Rsub and Rwell are the substrate and well resistances (Ω). βnpn and βpnp are the common-emitter current gains of the parasitic transistors. Vsub raises the substrate above VSS and Vwell lowers the well below VDD; either junction reaching about 0.7 V forward-biases its emitter and turns that transistor on. The loop is regenerative when both emitters are forward biased and the gain product exceeds one.

Both emitters must be forward biased for the loop to close. Once they are on, the npn collector current supplies the pnp base, and the pnp collector current supplies the npn base. If βnpn × βpnp > 1, each pass around the loop adds current. The current rises until the supply limits it. The path then stays on after the trigger is gone, and only removing power resets it. If the gain product is below one, the disturbance dies out.

Prevention: taps, guard rings and process

Layout is the first defence. Place p+ substrate taps tied to VSS close to every NMOS, and n+ well taps tied to VDD close to every PMOS. Tap spacing matters more than tap presence, because Rsub and Rwell are the resistances from the injection point to the nearest tap. A tap that is far away does little for a device that injects current locally. Put taps on both sides of a wide transistor. Surround sensitive NMOS devices with a p+ guard ring tied to VSS, and sensitive PMOS devices with an n+ guard ring tied to VDD. The ring lowers local resistance and diverts carriers before they reach a parasitic base.

Process choices lower the resistances further. A low-resistance p+ substrate with a thin p-epitaxial layer shunts substrate current. A retrograde well is made by a high-energy implant whose doping peak lies deep, near the bottom of the well, while the surface stays lightly doped. The heavy buried layer lowers the well resistance Rwell and raises the doping of the vertical pnp base, which cuts βpnp. Shallow trench isolation removes the deep sidewall paths of older LOCOS isolation. Silicide on diffusions and taps reduces sheet resistance. A standard current-injection latch-up test injects current into a pin, removes it, and checks whether the supply current stays high; a sustained high current means the chip has latched.

Worked example

Question. An n-well CMOS inverter has Rsub = 200 Ω and Rwell = 1 kΩ, with βnpn = 50 and βpnp = 20. A spike injects 2 mA into the substrate. (a) Find the substrate drop; the npn emitter needs 0.7 V to turn on. Does it turn on? (b) Repeat for Rsub = 500 Ω. (c) Given that both emitters are forward biased, is the loop regenerative?

  1. Convert the injected current: Iinj = 2 mA = 0.002 A.
  2. For Rsub = 200 Ω, Vsub = Iinj × Rsub = 0.002 A × 200 Ω = 0.4 V. Since 0.4 V < 0.7 V, the npn does not turn on.
  3. For Rsub = 500 Ω, Vsub = Iinj × Rsub = 0.002 A × 500 Ω = 1.0 V. Since 1.0 V > 0.7 V, the npn turns on.
  4. The loop gain is βnpn × βpnp = 50 × 20 = 1000. Since 1000 > 1, once both emitters are forward biased each pass around the loop adds current, so the loop is regenerative.

Answer. With Rsub = 200 Ω the substrate drop is 0.4 V, below 0.7 V, so the npn stays off and there is no latch-up; with Rsub = 500 Ω the drop is 1.0 V and the npn turns on. Given that both emitters are forward biased, yes, the loop is regenerative, because βnpn × βpnp = 1000 > 1 and each pass around the loop adds current.

Worked example 2

Question. An n-well is 20 μm wide with sheet resistance 1 kΩ per square. A pad can inject up to 0.2 mA into the well, and the well drop must stay below 0.3 V so the pnp emitter-base junction stays off with margin. How far from the injection point may the nearest well tap be?

  1. Largest allowed well resistance: Rwell = V/I = 0.3 V ÷ 0.2×10⁻³ A = 1500 Ω.
  2. Squares allowed: Rwell/Rs = 1500 ÷ 1000 = 1.5 squares.
  3. One square of a 20 μm wide well is 20 μm long, so the path may be 1.5 × 20 μm = 30 μm long.
  4. Check: R = 1000 × 30/20 = 1500 Ω, and 0.2 mA × 1500 Ω = 0.30 V.

Answer. The nearest n+ well tap must be within 30 μm of the injection point; taps every 30 μm or closer along the well meet the limit.

Exam traps

  • Adding a tap anywhere is not enough: what matters is the resistance from the injection point to the nearest tap. A distant tap leaves a large Rsub or Rwell.
  • Latch-up is not the same as ESD damage. ESD is a fast overvoltage event; latch-up is a sustained low-resistance path that remains after the trigger.
  • The loop condition is the product βnpn × βpnp > 1, not the sum. One weak device does not make the loop safe: βnpn = 5 with βpnp = 0.5 still gives 2.5 > 1.
  • A tap does not replace a guard ring. The ring lowers local resistance and diverts carriers, while the tap sets the reference potential.
  • In the current-injection test, the chip has latched only if the supply current stays high after the injected current is removed; a temporary rise is not latch-up.

Example: a 20 μm wide well of 1 kΩ per square, 0.2 mA injected and a 0.3 V limit allow 1500 Ω, which is 1.5 squares, so a tap within 30 μm.
  8. Layout prevention 2, guard rings: a p+ ring tied to VSS round the NMOS and an n+ ring tied to VDD round the PMOS collect the injected carriers before they reach a parasitic base, and lower the local resistance.
  9. Layout prevention 3, spacing: keeping NMOS n+ diffusions farther from the n-well edge widens the base of the lateral npn and lowers its β, so I/O cells, which see the outside world, use much larger n-well to n+ spacings.
  10. Layout prevention 4, place I/O and drivers away from the core logic, with their own guard rings and supply pads, so injected current is collected at the edge of the die.
  11. Process help: a thin p-epi layer on a p+ substrate makes Rsub small; a retrograde well puts heavy doping at the well bottom, lowering Rwell and βpnp; trench isolation lengthens the lateral path; SOI removes the path altogether.
  12. Holding condition: once latched, the SCR stays on as long as the supply can provide more than the holding current through a voltage above the holding voltage. If the holding voltage is above the supply, the path cannot stay latched, which is one way I/O cells are made safe.
  13. Current-injection test: current is forced into (and out of) each pin in turn, for example ±100 mA, with the supply at its maximum. The injection is then removed and the supply current is measured; if it stays high, the chip has latched. The test is run hot, since β rises and the junction turn-on voltage falls with temperature.
  14. Where it bites: I/O pads, which see undershoot, overshoot and electrostatic events, and any place where large drivers sit next to logic. A layout puts guard rings round every I/O transistor and keeps the core well away from the pad ring.
  15. Check: halving Rsub from 500 Ω to 250 Ω gives 2 mA × 250 Ω = 0.5 V, below 0.7 V, so taps placed twice as close turn the latching case into a safe one.
Evaluate Evaluate why a process flow fixes mask order by temperature and alignment. Give examples of steps that must come before others. 13 marks

Points to cover. High-temperature steps come before low-temperature metal; Each mask aligns to an earlier reference layer; Gate oxide must form after well drive-in; Contacts open after transistor and dielectric formation; Metal must come after contacts and vias.

Model answer.

  1. The order of a CMOS flow is fixed by two things: temperature (each step must survive every later step) and alignment (each mask must line up with a pattern that already exists).
  2. Thermal budget: dopant profiles keep moving at every hot step, and metal cannot take high temperatures (aluminium melts at 660 °C and reacts with silicon well below that). So the hottest, longest steps go first and the coolest last.
  3. Typical steps in order, with their temperatures:
    StepTypical temperatureWhy it sits here
    Well implant and drive-in1000–1150 °C, hoursdeepest diffusion; must not disturb anything made later
    Field oxide (LOCOS) or STI fillabout 1000 °Cisolation must exist before devices are placed
    Gate oxide800–1000 °C, dryafter the long hot steps, so it stays thin and clean
    Poly deposition (LPCVD)about 620 °Cneeds the gate oxide under it
    Source/drain implants and annealroom temperature, then about 1000 °C for secondsneed the poly gate as the mask
    Contacts and metalabout 400–450 °C sintermetal comes after every high-temperature step
  4. Examples of "must come before": the well before the gate oxide (the well drive-in would thicken and contaminate an oxide already grown); the gate oxide before poly (poly is deposited on it); poly before the source/drain implant (the gate is the implant mask, which makes source and drain self-aligned); the activation anneal before contacts (metal cannot take it); contacts before metal 1, and vias before metal 2 (metal fills the holes).
  5. Alignment: the first mask (well or active) sets alignment marks; every later mask is aligned to a layer already on the wafer, usually active or poly, within a tolerance of about one λ. A layer cannot be aligned to one that does not exist yet, so the layers that others land on (active, poly) come early.
  6. Self-alignment removes one alignment from the critical path: because the poly gate blocks the source/drain implant, the source and drain edges are fixed by the gate itself, not by a separate mask, and the n-select and p-select masks can be drawn with loose margins.
  7. BiCMOS shows the same logic: the buried layer goes before the epitaxy and well drive-in because it must be driven deep, and the shallow base goes after them so it does not spread.
  8. Evaluation: a different order would either destroy a finished structure (a shallow junction spread by a later drive-in, aluminium melted by an anneal) or leave a mask with nothing to align to. Process engineers can shorten steps, for example by using rapid thermal anneals, but they cannot swap them.
  9. Isolation order: in a LOCOS flow the field oxide is grown before the gate oxide because it is the longest oxidation; in an STI flow the trench is etched, filled and polished before the wells or right after them, because CMP needs a flat surface with no devices on it.
  10. Spacer and LDD order: the light LDD implant uses the bare gate as its mask; the oxide spacer is then formed and the heavy source/drain implant uses gate plus spacer. Swapping them would put the heavy implant right at the gate edge and lose the low-field drain.
  11. Silicide: where it is used, titanium, cobalt or nickel is reacted with the exposed silicon and poly after the source/drain anneal and before the first dielectric, at a few hundred degrees; earlier would be too hot for it, later the silicon would be covered.
  12. Back-end alignment: each metal layer is aligned to the contacts or vias below it, and each via layer to the metal below, so the back end is a chain in which every mask lands on the one just made.
  13. Check: the ten masks of a two-metal flow (n-well, active, poly, n+, p+, contact, metal 1, via, metal 2, pad) run from the hottest steps to the coolest, in exactly this logic.
Analyze Compare ion implantation with thermal diffusion for source and drain doping. Discuss dose control, lateral spread, damage and annealing. 12 marks

Points to cover. Implantation gives precise dose and energy; Diffusion gives deeper, more lateral spread; Implant damages crystal lattice and needs anneal; Diffusion needs high thermal budget; Advanced CMOS relies on implantation plus rapid anneal.

Model answer.

  1. Source and drain must be shallow, heavily doped and placed exactly at the gate edge. Both thermal diffusion and ion implantation can dope them; modern CMOS uses implantation, and the comparison shows why.
  2. Dose control. Implantation counts the ions directly: the dose is the integrated beam current divided by the charge per ion and the wafer area, set to about one per cent anywhere from 10¹¹ to 10¹⁶ cm⁻². Diffusion from a gas source fixes the surface at the solid solubility, so the dose depends on temperature and time and is hard to set at low values.
    implant: φ = (I × t)/(q × area)
diffusion: Q set by solubility, temperature and time
  3. Depth control. The implant depth is set by the energy through Rp and ΔRp; diffusion depth is set by D·t, and D changes by a factor of two for every 30 °C near 1000 °C.
  4. Lateral spread. Diffusion is isotropic: the dopant moves sideways under the mask edge nearly as far as it goes down (about 0.8 xj), which would push source and drain under the gate and shorten the channel. Implantation is directional: the lateral straggle is of the order of ΔRp, and the only extra spread comes from the anneal.
  5. Numbers from the chapter examples:
    Diffusion (1 h, 1000 °C)Implantation (boron)
    Dose10¹⁴ cm⁻², from predeposition10¹⁵ cm⁻², counted
    Peak or surface5.43×10¹⁸ cm⁻³ at the surface1.33×10²⁰ cm⁻³ at 0.10 μm
    Junction depth0.522 μm0.214 μm
    Sideways spreadabout 0.4 μmabout 0.03 μm before anneal
    Maskthick oxide (resist cannot take the heat)resist, oxide or the poly gate (0.22 μm of oxide is enough)
  6. Damage and annealing. Implanted ions knock silicon atoms off their sites and can leave an amorphous layer; until annealed, many dopants are not on lattice sites and do not conduct. An anneal (rapid thermal, seconds near 1000 °C) repairs the lattice and activates the dopant. Diffusion causes no such damage, since the atoms arrive by heat.
  7. Thermal budget. Diffusion needs long hot steps that move every earlier profile; implantation is done at room temperature and needs only the short anneal, which keeps the wells and threshold implants where they were placed.
  8. Masking and alignment. A resist mask can block an implant, so the n+ and p+ implants are separated by the select masks, and the poly gate itself blocks the implant under it: the source and drain are self-aligned to the gate. Channelling is avoided by tilting the wafer about 7°.
  9. Lightly doped drain: a modern source/drain is made in two implants. A light n− implant uses the bare poly gate as its mask; an oxide spacer is formed; then the heavy n+ implant uses gate plus spacer. The light region under the spacer lowers the peak electric field at the drain and the hot-carrier damage it causes. Only implantation can make this pair, because each dose and depth must be set separately.
  10. Comparison at a glance: diffusion gives a deep, graded junction with a large sideways spread and no damage; implantation gives a set dose, a shallow junction and a sharp edge, at the cost of damage that must be annealed.
  11. Conclusion: implantation gives a set dose, a shallow junction and little sideways spread with a small thermal budget, so it is used for source/drain, LDD, threshold-adjust and well implants; diffusion survives as the drive-in and anneal that follow. Check: the implanted junction (0.214 μm) is less than half the diffused one (0.522 μm) despite ten times the dose.
Analyze A twin-tub CMOS process is changed to an n-well process. Analyze which transistor body doping and well spacing rules change, and one effect on latch-up. 13 marks

Points to cover. NMOS body becomes p-substrate instead of p-well; PMOS still sits in n-well; Well-to-well spacing rules simplify or disappear; Substrate tap and well tap placement affect parasitic resistance; Latch-up immunity may change with body doping and spacing.

Model answer.

  1. In the twin-tub process the NMOS sits in a p-well and the PMOS in an n-well, each doped separately. Changing to an n-well process removes the p-well: the NMOS is built directly in the lightly doped p-substrate, and the PMOS stays in the n-well.
  2. NMOS body doping falls from the p-well value to the substrate value. With Cox = 3.45×10⁻⁷ F/cm²:
    NMOS bodyNA (cm⁻³)γ (V½)ΔVT at VSB = 1 V
    p-well (twin-tub)10¹⁷0.530.23 V
    p-substrate (n-well)10¹⁵0.0530.026 V
  3. Effects on the NMOS: the body effect falls about nine-fold; the threshold voltage falls too, so a threshold-adjust implant (boron) is usually added to set it; the source/drain junction capacitance falls because the depletion region in the lighter body is wider; the electron mobility rises slightly with less impurity scattering.
  4. PMOS: unchanged; it stays in the n-well, with the same well doping, taps and threshold.
  5. Masks: one fewer well mask, and a shorter thermal budget without the p-well drive-in.
  6. Design rules that change: the rules that involve the p-well disappear (p-well width and spacing, p-well enclosure of n+ active, and the p-well to n-well spacing or overlap at the well boundary). The n-well rules stay: n-well minimum width, n-well enclosure of p+ active, and n-well to n+ active spacing, which also sets how close an NMOS may sit to the well.
  7. Effect on latch-up: the npn base is now the lightly doped substrate, whose resistance is higher than that of a p-well, and the npn β can rise. For the same 2 mA injection:
    RsubDropnpn
    200 Ω0.4 Voff
    500 Ω1.0 Von
  8. So a substrate that is 2.5 times more resistive turns a safe layout into one that can latch. The n-well process must therefore keep Rsub small: closer p+ substrate taps, guard rings round the NMOS, or a p-epi wafer on a heavily doped p+ substrate.
  9. Threshold: φF falls from 0.417 V to 0.298 V and the depletion charge under the gate falls ten-fold, so the NMOS threshold drops by a few tenths of a volt. A boron threshold-adjust implant, masked to the NMOS areas, restores it; this mask is often already in the flow.
  10. Junction capacitance: for an n+-p junction the capacitance per area varies as √NA, so moving the NMOS from a 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ p-well to a 10¹⁵ cm⁻³ substrate cuts its drain capacitance per area by about √100 = 10. The gates it drives switch faster.
  11. Body bias: in the twin-tub process the p-well could, in a triple-well variant, be biased on its own; in the n-well process every NMOS shares the one substrate, so its body is always at VSS.
  12. Layout area: without the p-well boundary, the NMOS can sit closer to the n-well edge (only the n-well to n+ spacing remains), so a standard cell may become slightly shorter.
  13. Conclusion: the change saves a mask and gives a faster NMOS with less body effect, at the cost of losing independent control of the NMOS threshold and of needing more care with substrate taps for latch-up. Check: γ scales as √NA, so a 100-fold drop in doping gives a ten-fold drop in γ (0.53 to 0.053 V½).
Evaluate Evaluate stick diagrams as a first layout step. What information they preserve and what they omit, and how design rules enter later. 10 marks

Points to cover. Preserve layer topology, transistor order and connections; Omit dimensions, exact widths and spacing; Help compare alternative gate layouts quickly; Design rules fix final widths, spacings and overlaps; Stick diagram becomes a mask layout after rule-driven sizing.

Model answer.

  1. A stick diagram is the first layout step: it shows each layer of a cell as a line (diffusion, poly, metal), contacts as dots, the VDD and VSS rails, and nothing else.
  2. What it preserves: the topology. It shows which transistors exist (every poly-diffusion crossing), which diffusion segments two transistors share, the order of the poly columns along each strip, where contacts go and how nodes are wired in metal. These decide the electrical behaviour, the number of contacts and the number of diffusion breaks.
  3. What it omits: every dimension. Widths, lengths, spacings and enclosures are not shown, nor transistor sizing (W/L), nor the exact positions of wires. A stick diagram cannot be checked by DRC and does not show area or parasitic capacitance.
  4. Why it is a good first step: it is quick to draw and change. For y = ¬(a·b + c) the Euler path shows that the order c, a, b gives one unbroken strip on each side; trying another order on paper costs seconds, against many minutes of redrawing a full layout. It also lets the designer check the transistor count (6 crossings for this gate) against the schematic before any geometry exists.
  5. How design rules enter later: each line becomes a shape of rule width and each gap a rule spacing. For a two-input NAND with λ rules (contact 2λ, 1λ of diffusion round a contact, contact to gate 2λ, poly 2λ, 3λ between gates sharing an uncontacted node):
    StripSum along the stripLength
    p (three contacts)1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 120λ
    n (two contacts)1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 117λ
  6. The cell is about 20λ, 10 μm for λ = 0.5 μm. The stick diagram decided the order and the contacts; the rules decided the size.
  7. Limits: at advanced nodes, layouts sit on fixed grids with restricted rules, multiple metal layers and fins, so the stick diagram is mainly a teaching and planning tool, but the Euler-path ordering it captures still drives standard-cell layout.
  8. Using a stick diagram in practice: draw the networks, find a common Euler path, sketch the strips, poly columns, contacts and rails, then count crossings and contacts against the schematic. Only when the sketch is right are the shapes drawn to the rules, so DRC and LVS find fewer errors later.
  9. Verdict: a stick diagram is the right first step because it fixes the decisions that are hard to change later (topology, order, sharing) and leaves the ones the rules make for you (dimensions). Check: the NAND's 4 crossings and 5 contacts in the stick match the 4 transistors and the 5 diffusion nodes that need metal in the final layout.

Glossary

Body tap
Contact that ties a well or substrate to a supply and lowers parasitic resistance.
Buried layer
Heavily doped n+ region under a BiCMOS NPN that carries collector current to the sinker with little resistance.
Chemical vapour deposition
Film growth by chemical reaction of gases on the wafer surface.
Contact
Opening that connects metal to silicon or polysilicon.
Design rule check
Software check that a layout meets width, spacing and overlap rules.
Dopant
Impurity atom added to silicon to make it n-type or p-type.
Drive-in diffusion
High-temperature step that moves dopant atoms deeper into silicon.
Epitaxy
Growth of a crystalline silicon layer on a crystalline silicon substrate.
Euler path
A walk through every edge of a graph exactly once; in layout it gives the poly column order for one unbroken diffusion strip.
Field oxide
Thick oxide that isolates neighbouring active devices.
Gate oxide
Thin high-quality oxide between the gate and the channel.
Guard ring
Heavily doped ring tied to a supply round a group of transistors; it collects injected carriers and lowers local resistance against latch-up.
Ion implantation
Process that shoots energetic dopant ions into silicon to set dose and depth.
Junction depth
Depth at which the added dopant equals the background doping, so the type changes.
Lambda-based rules
Layout rules written as multiples of a scalable unit λ.
Latch-up
Parasitic thyristor action that can lock a CMOS circuit into a high-current state.
Mask aligner
Tool that aligns a photomask to the wafer and exposes the resist.
n-well
n-doped region built inside a p-substrate to hold PMOS transistors.
p-well
p-doped region built inside an n-substrate to hold NMOS transistors.
Photoresist
Light-sensitive polymer used as a temporary mask for etching or implantation.
Plasma etching
Dry etch using reactive ions and radicals to remove material, often anisotropically.
Projected range
Average depth Rp at which implanted ions stop; the spread around it is the straggle ΔRp.
Self-aligned gate
Source and drain implanted with the poly gate as the mask, so their edges line up with the gate without a separate alignment.
Silicide
Low-resistance metal-silicon compound formed on poly or diffusion.
Sputtering
Physical vapour deposition method that knocks target atoms onto the wafer by ion bombardment.
Stick diagram
Topological layer sketch without dimensions, used before exact layout.
Thermal oxidation
High-temperature growth of silicon dioxide on silicon using oxygen or water vapour.
Twin-tub
CMOS process that forms both an n-well and a p-well.
Via
Opening that connects one metal layer to another metal layer.
