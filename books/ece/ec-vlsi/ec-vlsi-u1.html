1.1 MOS structure; nMOS and pMOS operating regions

A MOSFET is a capacitor taught to conduct. A gate on a thin oxide above doped silicon first pushes the majority carriers away from the surface, then pulls minority carriers into a thin channel. A source and a drain let current flow along that channel. Whether the channel is absent, continuous or pinched off gives the three regions: cutoff, linear and saturation. Every current, delay and power formula in VLSI starts by naming the region.

The MOS capacitor: accumulation, depletion and inversion

Take a p-type body with acceptor doping NA (cm⁻³), so holes are the majority carriers. Silicon dioxide of thickness tox, a few nanometres, covers it, and a metal or polysilicon gate sits on top. This is a parallel-plate capacitor with Cox = εox/tox per unit area, where εox ≈ 3.45 × 10⁻¹¹ F/m.

The flat-band voltage VFB is the gate-to-body voltage at which the surface holds no net charge. Three states follow as the gate voltage VG rises.

  • Accumulation (VG < VFB). The negative gate charge attracts holes, which pile up at the surface.
  • Depletion (VG a little above VFB). The positive gate repels holes, leaving a depletion region of width Wd that holds only fixed acceptor ions. The band bending at the surface, φs, grows with VG.
  • Inversion (φs ≥ 2φF). Electrons, the minority carriers, are drawn to the surface. At φs = 2φF the surface electron density equals the bulk hole density: the surface has turned n-type, and this layer is the channel. Here φF = (kT/q) ln(NA/ni) is the bulk Fermi potential and ni ≈ 1.5 × 10¹⁰ cm⁻³.

The capacitance-voltage (C-V) curve shows the same states. In accumulation C = Cox. In depletion a depletion capacitance Cdep adds in series, so C = CoxCdep/(Cox + Cdep) dips to a minimum near threshold. In inversion C returns to Cox at low frequency; at high frequency the electrons cannot follow and C stays low. The gate voltage where strong inversion begins is the threshold voltage VT (see Threshold voltage and body effect).

From capacitor to transistor: the channel and pinch-off

Add n⁺ source (S) and drain (D) regions on either side of the gate (G); with the body (B) the device has four terminals. In an nMOS the source is the lower-voltage terminal, usually tied to the body at ground.

With VDS = 0 and VGS > VT, a uniform channel joins source and drain. The overdrive Vov = VGS − VT sets its charge per unit area, QI ≈ Cox·Vov.

Now raise VDS. The channel potential V(x) rises from 0 at the source to VDS at the drain, so the local charge Cox[VGS − V(x) − VT] is largest at the source and smallest at the drain. When VDS = VGS − VT the drain-end charge falls to zero: the channel is pinched off. Extra drain voltage drops across a short depleted gap; electrons are swept across it, so the current stays nearly constant.

The three regions of nMOS and pMOS

Cutoff: no channel, only leakage (see Leakage and subthreshold conduction). Linear (triode or ohmic): a continuous channel, a gate-controlled resistor. Saturation: pinched off at the drain, a current source set by VGS.

A pMOS is the mirror image: n-type body (an n-well in CMOS), p⁺ source and drain, a hole channel. Its source is the higher-voltage terminal, so VGS, VDS and VTp are negative. Use VSG = VS − VG and VSD = VS − VD, positive when it conducts, and compare them with |VTp|.

RegionnMOS testpMOS test
CutoffVGS < VTnVSG < |VTp|
LinearVGS ≥ VTn and VDS < VGS − VTnVSG ≥ |VTp| and VSD < VSG − |VTp|
SaturationVGS ≥ VTn and VDS ≥ VGS − VTnVSG ≥ |VTp| and VSD ≥ VSG − |VTp|

Some syllabi speak of characteristics "under static and dynamic conditions": static is the DC behaviour in these regions (I-V characteristics); dynamic is the charging of the device capacitances (capacitances and parasitic resistances).

Switches, NMOS logic and CMOS

In logic a transistor is a switch. An nMOS turns on with a high gate; it passes a strong 0 but only a weak 1 (VDD − VTn), because it turns off once VGS falls to VTn. A pMOS turns on with a low gate and passes a strong 1 but a weak 0.

NMOS logic pulls the output down with nMOS transistors and up with a load (a resistor or depletion-mode nMOS); while the output is low, current flows through the load all the time. CMOS logic pairs each pull-down nMOS network with a complementary pull-up pMOS network on the same inputs, so in any steady state one network is off and almost no static current flows. Load types and inverter curves belong to the topics NMOS inverters and CMOS inverter DC characteristics.

Worked example

Question. A CMOS inverter runs from VDD = 1.8 V with VTn = 0.5 V and VTp = −0.5 V. At one instant Vin = 0.8 V and Vout = 1.5 V. The nMOS source is at ground and the pMOS source at VDD. Name the region of each transistor.

  1. nMOS: VGS = 0.8 V ≥ 0.5 V, so a channel exists; overdrive = 0.8 − 0.5 = 0.3 V.
  2. VDS = 1.5 V ≥ 0.3 V, so the drain end is pinched off: saturation.
  3. pMOS: VSG = 1.8 − 0.8 = 1.0 V ≥ 0.5 V, so a hole channel exists; overdrive = 0.5 V.
  4. VSD = 1.8 − 1.5 = 0.3 V < 0.5 V, so the channel is continuous: linear.

Answer. nMOS saturated, pMOS linear.

Worked example 2

Question. An nMOS has VT = 0.5 V and Cox = 8.6 fF/µm², with VGS = 1.5 V and VDS = 0.4 V. Find the channel charge per µm² at the source and drain ends, the number of electrons per µm² at the source end, and the VDS that pinches the channel off.

  1. Source end: local overdrive = 1.5 − 0 − 0.5 = 1.0 V; charge = 8.6 fF/µm² × 1.0 V = 8.6 fC/µm².
  2. Drain end: local overdrive = 1.5 − 0.4 − 0.5 = 0.6 V; charge = 8.6 × 0.6 = 5.16 fC/µm², 40 % less.
  3. Electrons at the source end: 8.6 × 10⁻¹⁵ C ÷ 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C = 5.4 × 10⁴ per µm².
  4. Pinch-off when the drain-end overdrive is zero: VDS = VGS − VT = 1.0 V.

Answer. 8.6 fC/µm² at the source, 5.16 fC/µm² at the drain, about 54 000 electrons per µm² at the source, pinch-off at VDS = 1.0 V.

Exam traps

  • For a p-type body, accumulation is below flat band (VG < VFB); above VFB comes depletion, then inversion.
  • Compare the full VDS with VGS − VT. Do not subtract the overdrive from VDS first.
  • For a pMOS compare VSG with |VTp| and VSD with VSG − |VTp|; mixing a negative VGS with a positive threshold gives the wrong region.
  • Pinch-off does not stop the current: only the drain end of the channel vanishes.
  • An nMOS passes a weak 1 (VDD − VTn), a pMOS a weak 0.

1.2 Threshold voltage and body effect

The threshold voltage VT is the gate-to-source voltage at which an nMOS surface just reaches strong inversion. It is a voltage budget: part of the gate voltage cancels the work-function difference and oxide charge, part bends the bands by 2φF, and part holds up the depletion charge. Only gate voltage above VT makes channel charge. When the source sits above the body, the depletion region holds more charge and VT rises: the body effect. Designers meet it in every stacked transistor and every pass gate.

You need: the MOS capacitor and inversion at φs = 2φF, from MOS structure; nMOS and pMOS operating regions.

Threshold voltage from its parts

Take an nMOS on a p body with the source tied to the body. Three things must be paid for before inversion charge appears.

  1. Flat band. The gate and body materials have different work functions, and the oxide traps some fixed positive charge Qox (C/m²). The gate voltage that makes the bands flat is VFB = φMS − Qox/Cox, where φMS is the gate-to-body work-function difference in volts.
  2. Band bending. The surface potential must rise by 2φF, where φF = (kT/q) ln(NA/ni). At 300 K, kT/q ≈ 0.026 V.
  3. Depletion charge. At φs = 2φF the depletion region holds charge Qdep = √(2qεsiNA·2φF) per unit area. The gate needs Qdep/Cox volts across the oxide to hold it.

VT0 = VFB + 2φF + √(2qεsiNA·2φF)/Cox

Here VT0 is the threshold with VSB = 0; εsi ≈ 1.04 × 10⁻¹⁰ F/m is the permittivity of silicon; Cox = εox/tox; NA is in m⁻³ when the other quantities are in SI units.

With an n⁺ polysilicon gate, φMS ≈ −(0.56 + φF) V for a p body, so VT0 often comes out close to zero. A shallow threshold-adjust implant of acceptors, dose DI ions per m², adds a sheet of charge at the surface and raises the threshold by ΔVT = qDI/Cox. This is how a process sets VT to the value in its design kit.

The body effect

Now let the source rise above the body by VSB = VS − VB > 0. The source-body junction is reverse biased, so the surface must reach φs = 2φF + VSB before electrons from the source can collect there. The depletion region is wider and holds more charge, so the gate must supply more voltage.

VT = VT0 + γ[√(2φF + VSB) − √(2φF)], with γ = √(2qεsiNA)/Cox

γ is the body-effect coefficient in √V (typically 0.2 to 0.5 √V); heavier doping or a thicker oxide makes it larger. The rise is fastest at small VSB and grows only as a square root later. When source and body are tied, VSB = 0 and the effect vanishes.

In a two-input NAND, the lower nMOS has its source at ground, but the upper nMOS has its source at the node between them. When that node rises, the upper transistor has VSB > 0, a higher VT and less current. An nMOS pass gate is hit the same way: as its output (its source) rises, VT rises too, so the weak 1 it passes is lower than VDD − VT0.

pMOS, temperature and SPICE parameters

For a pMOS in an n-well the same terms appear with opposite signs: VTp is negative, the body effect makes |VTp| larger when the well sits above the source (VBS > 0), and the formula uses |φF|, the donor doping ND and VBS in place of VSB.

Temperature lowers |VT| by roughly 1 to 2 mV per kelvin, because φF shrinks as ni grows. Mobility also falls with temperature, so at high gate voltage the current drops when hot, while leakage near threshold rises. Channel length and drain voltage shift VT too; those short-channel changes are in MOSFET scaling and short-channel effects.

In the SPICE Level 1 model the threshold is carried by VTO (VT0), GAMMA (γ), PHI (2φF) and TOX (tox).

  • Use VSB = VS − VB under the root, positive for reverse bias in an nMOS. Putting in VBS = −1 V gives √(2φF − 1), which is not even real when 2φF < 1 V.
  • The formula has 2φF, not φF, in both the bending term and the square roots.

1.3 MOSFET I-V characteristics and device equations

Above threshold, the gate sets how much charge sits in the channel and the drain voltage drags that charge along. Writing this down gives the two square-law equations, one for the linear region and one for saturation. They give the drain current from the overdrive, the oxide capacitance, the mobility and the width-to-length ratio W/L. Designers use them to size transistors, find on-resistance and transconductance, and estimate delay before running a simulator.

You need: the region tests from MOS structure; nMOS and pMOS operating regions and VT from Threshold voltage and body effect.

Channel charge and the drift integral

Put the source at 0 V and let x run from 0 at the source to L at the drain. The channel potential V(x) rises from 0 to VDS. Above threshold, the inversion charge per unit area is QI(x) = Cox[VGS − VT − V(x)]. Electrons drift with velocity v = µn·dV/dx, where µn is the electron mobility. The current is width × charge × velocity:

ID = W·µnCox[VGS − VT − V(x)]·dV/dx

ID is the same at every x, so multiply by dx and integrate from source (V = 0) to drain (V = VDS). The left side gives ID·L; the right side gives WµnCox[(VGS − VT)VDS − VDS²/2]. This is the gradual-channel approximation: the field along the channel is assumed much weaker than the field across the oxide.

The linear and saturation equations

Write kn′ = µnCox, the process transconductance parameter (A/V²), and Vov = VGS − VT.

Linear (VDS < Vov): ID = kn′(W/L)[Vov·VDS − VDS²/2]

Saturation (VDS ≥ Vov): ID = ½kn′(W/L)Vov²

The saturation formula is the linear one at VDS = Vov, where the bracket becomes Vov²/2. Beyond it the channel is pinched off and the ideal current stays flat. The boundary between the regions is the curve ID = ½kn′(W/L)VDS², a parabola through the knee of every curve.

Two graphs show the equations. The output characteristics plot ID against VDS for several VGS: each curve rises, bends at VDS = Vov and goes flat, and the flat levels grow as Vov². The transfer characteristic plots ID against VGS at fixed VDS in saturation: zero up to VT, then a parabola. Some syllabi call these the square-law, Shichman-Hodges or SPICE Level 1 equations.

A pMOS obeys the same equations with VSG, VSD and |VTp| in place of VGS, VDS and VT, and kp′ = µpCox. Hole mobility is roughly a third to a half of electron mobility, so a pMOS is made two to three times wider to match an nMOS.

Transconductance, on-resistance and design equations

Transconductance gm = ∂ID/∂VGS in saturation: gm = kn′(W/L)Vov = 2ID/Vov, in A/V (siemens). It measures how strongly the gate controls the current, which sets the gain of an amplifier.

On-resistance. Near VDS = 0 the VDS² term is negligible, so ID ≈ kn′(W/L)Vov·VDS and the transistor is a resistor Ron = 1/[kn′(W/L)Vov]. This is the resistance of a closed switch; doubling W halves it, which is why wider drivers are faster.

Design equation. For a required saturation current, W/L = 2ID/(kn′Vov²). To match a pMOS to an nMOS, make (W/L)p = (W/L)n × kn′/kp′. In SPICE Level 1 these numbers appear as KP (kn′), VTO, GAMMA, PHI and LAMBDA; the last is the slope correction in Non-ideal effects.

Worked example

Question. An nMOS has kn′ = 100 µA/V², W/L = 20 and VT = 0.5 V, with VGS = 1.5 V. Find ID and gm at VDS = 2 V, ID at VDS = 0.5 V, and Ron near VDS = 0.

  1. kn′(W/L) = 100 × 20 = 2000 µA/V²; Vov = 1.5 − 0.5 = 1.0 V.
  2. VDS = 2 V ≥ 1.0 V: saturation. ID = ½ × 2000 × 1.0² = 1000 µA = 1 mA.
  3. gm = 2000 µA/V² × 1.0 V = 2 mA/V (check: 2ID/Vov = 2 × 1 mA / 1 V).
  4. VDS = 0.5 V < 1.0 V: linear. ID = 2000 × (1.0 × 0.5 − 0.5²/2) = 2000 × 0.375 = 750 µA.
  5. Ron = 1/(2000 × 10⁻⁶ × 1.0) = 500 Ω.

Answer. ID = 1 mA and gm = 2 mA/V in saturation; 0.75 mA at VDS = 0.5 V; Ron = 500 Ω.

Worked example 2

Question. In a process with kn′ = 100 µA/V² and kp′ = 40 µA/V², an nMOS of W/L = 2 is used with VDD = 1.8 V and VTn = |VTp| = 0.5 V. Size the pMOS for equal saturation current at full gate drive, and find that current.

  1. Equal current needs kp′(W/L)p = kn′(W/L)n, so (W/L)p = 2 × 100/40 = 5.

  3. ID = ½ × 100 µA/V² × 2 × 1.3² = 100 × 1.69 = 169 µA; pMOS: ½ × 40 × 5 × 1.69 = 169 µA.

Exam traps

  • In saturation the current depends on Vov, not VDS; putting VDS into the square law makes the current keep rising.
  • The ½ belongs to the saturation formula; dropping it doubles every answer.
  • Write W/L, not L/W, when rearranging for size.
  • For a pMOS use VSG, VSD and |VTp|, all positive, with kp′.
  • Ron = 1/[kn′(W/L)Vov] holds only near VDS = 0, not across the whole linear region.

1.4 Non-ideal effects: velocity saturation, channel length modulation

The square law assumes long channels, constant mobility and weak fields. Real transistors break all three. The vertical gate field drags carriers against the rough oxide interface, so mobility falls as the gate voltage rises: mobility degradation. The lateral drain field cannot speed carriers up beyond about 10⁷ cm/s: velocity saturation. And past pinch-off the channel gets shorter as VDS rises: channel length modulation. Together they explain why short transistors give less current than the square law predicts and why their output curves slope upward.

You need: the square-law equations from MOSFET I-V characteristics and device equations.

Mobility degradation: the vertical field

A higher VGS adds channel charge, but it also presses the electrons harder against the silicon-oxide interface, where they scatter more often. Each carrier then moves more slowly. A common fit is:

µeff = µ0 / (1 + θ·Vov), so ID = ½kn′(W/L)Vov² / (1 + θ·Vov) in saturation

µ0 is the low-field mobility, kn′ = µ0Cox, and θ (in V⁻¹) is a fitted process constant, larger for thinner oxides. Doubling the overdrive now gives less than four times the current, and the transfer curve bends over. Mobility also falls as temperature rises (more lattice vibration), so the on-current of a hot chip is lower.

Velocity saturation: the lateral field

At low field the drift velocity is µE. At high field the carriers lose their extra energy to the lattice and the velocity levels off at vsat ≈ 10⁷ cm/s for electrons in silicon (somewhat lower for holes). A simple fit is:

v = µE / (1 + E/Ec), with critical field Ec = vsat/µ

The channel field is roughly VDS/L, so the shorter the channel, the sooner the carriers saturate. Putting this velocity into the drift integral divides the linear-region current by (1 + VDS/(EcL)). The current stops rising, and the device saturates, at

VDSsat = EcL[√(1 + 2Vov/(EcL)) − 1] and IDsat = W·Cox·vsat(Vov − VDSsat)

Check the limits. For a long channel (EcL ≫ Vov), VDSsat → Vov and IDsat → ½kn′(W/L)Vov², the square law. For a very short channel (EcL ≪ Vov), VDSsat is small and IDsat → W·Cox·vsat·Vov: the current is linear in overdrive and no longer depends on L. Many books fit measured devices with the alpha-power law ID ∝ Vovα, with α between 1 (fully velocity-saturated) and 2 (square law). Textbooks differ in how they define Ec (some use 2vsat/µ), so always state the model you use.

Channel length modulation: the moving pinch-off point

Past pinch-off, the extra voltage VDS − VDSsat drops across a depleted gap at the drain. As VDS rises the gap widens by ΔL and the conducting channel shortens to L − ΔL. A shorter channel carries more current, so the saturated current creeps up:

ID = ½kn′(W/L)Vov²(1 + λVDS) and ro = 1/(λID)

λ (V⁻¹) is a fitting parameter that grows roughly as 1/L; ro is the small-signal output resistance, the inverse of the slope of the output curve. A larger λ lowers ro and the intrinsic gain gm·ro, which is why short-channel amplifiers are harder to design. The factor applies only in saturation; in the linear region there is no pinch-off point to move.

Worked example

Question. An nMOS has W = 1 µm, L = 0.25 µm, Cox = 8.6 fF/µm², µn = 500 cm²/V·s, vsat = 10⁷ cm/s and λ = 0.1 V⁻¹, biased at Vov = 2 V. Find (a) the square-law saturation current, (b) Ec, EcL and VDSsat, (c) the velocity-saturated current and the ratio of the two, (d) ro at that current.

1.5 MOSFET capacitances and parasitic resistances

Every pair of MOSFET terminals forms a small capacitor, and every time a gate switches, the driver must charge or discharge them through resistance. The gate oxide gives the largest capacitance; overlap and junction capacitances add to it, and how the gate capacitance splits between source, drain and body depends on the region. Series resistances of the source, drain, contacts and gate slow the charging further. Together these parasitics decide the dynamic behaviour: switching delay, dynamic power and how many gates one output can drive.

You need: the regions from MOS structure; nMOS and pMOS operating regions.

Oxide and overlap capacitance

Gate and channel form a parallel-plate capacitor through the oxide: Cox = εox/tox per unit area, and the gate area gives Cox·W·L. A handy unit is fF/µm²: with W and L in µm the product is in fF (1 F/m² = 1000 fF/µm²).

The source and drain diffusions spread a distance Lov under the gate edges, so the gate also overlaps them. Each overlap gives

Cov = Cox·W·Lov, stored in SPICE as CGSO and CGDO per unit width (Cov = CGSO·W)

Overlap capacitance is there in every region. Fringing field from the gate edge to the diffusions is usually folded into the same parameters. Because Lov does not shrink as fast as L, the overlap share of the gate capacitance grows as transistors get shorter.

Gate capacitance in each region

The intrinsic part, Cox·W·L, connects the gate to whichever conductor sits under it. A simple model (often called the Meyer model) splits it as follows:

RegionCgsCgdCgb
CutoffCovCovup to CoxWL
Linear½CoxWL + Cov½CoxWL + Cov0
Saturation⅔CoxWL + CovCov0

In cutoff there is no channel, so the gate sees the body through the oxide. In depletion the depletion capacitance is in series, so Cgb is less than CoxWL; it approaches CoxWL only in accumulation. In the linear region the channel connects to both ends and the charge splits about equally. In saturation the channel is pinched off at the drain, so the drain loses its share and the source takes about two-thirds.

Cgd matters more than its size suggests: in an inverter the drain swings opposite to the gate, so Cgd is charged by both swings (the Miller effect) and counts about twice in delay estimates.

Junction capacitance and parasitic resistance

Source and drain form reverse-biased pn junctions with the body, giving Csb and Cdb. Each has a bottom part (per unit area) and a sidewall part (per unit perimeter), and both shrink as the reverse voltage VR grows:

Cj = Cj0 / (1 + VR/φB)m

Cj0 is the zero-bias capacitance, φB the built-in potential (about 0.7 to 0.9 V) and m the grading coefficient (about 0.5 for the bottom, 0.33 for the sidewall). In SPICE these are CJ, CJSW, PB, MJ and MJSW; the drawn area and perimeter of each diffusion are given as AD, PD, AS and PS.

Resistance appears in series with the channel. Source and drain diffusions have a sheet resistance Rsh (Ω per square), so a diffusion of length l and width W adds Rsh·l/W. Each contact adds a contact resistance Rc. The lightly doped drain extensions of short devices add more. The polysilicon gate is a distributed resistor along its width; a very wide gate behaves as an RC ladder, so layouts split wide transistors into several parallel fingers. Silicide on diffusions and gates cuts these resistances by about ten times.

1.6 MOSFET scaling and short-channel effects

Scaling builds the same circuit with smaller devices. Shrink the channel length, the oxide thickness and the supply voltage together by a factor S and the electric field inside the transistor does not change, so the device still works. Shrink only the lengths and the gate slowly loses control of the channel. Below about a quarter micrometre that loss shows up as a family of short-channel effects: threshold roll-off, drain-induced barrier lowering, punch-through, hot carriers and slow reliability drift. Every node you name — 28 nm, 7 nm — is described by these numbers.

You need: 1.3 for the drain current equations and 1.4 for velocity saturation and channel length modulation.

Constant-field scaling by a factor S

Pick a factor S > 1. Divide the channel length L, the width W, the oxide thickness tox and every voltage (VDD, VT) by S. Multiply the channel doping NA by S.

Why together? Field is voltage divided by distance. Both fall by S, so the field pattern inside the device is unchanged. Some syllabi call this constant-field scaling, or Dennard scaling after the 1974 paper that proposed it.

Now follow the consequences. Oxide capacitance per unit area is Cox = εox/tox, so Cox multiplies by S. The parameter κ = µnCox(W/L) also multiplies by S, because W/L is a ratio and does not change.

  • Drain current ID ∝ κ(VGS − VT)² → S × (1/S)² = 1/S.
  • Gate capacitance C = CoxWL → S × (1/S) × (1/S) = 1/S.
  • Delay τ = CVDD/ID → (1/S)(1/S)/(1/S) = 1/S, so the clock can rise by S.
  • Area per gate = WL → 1/S², so power per gate divided by area stays at 1.
P = C·V<sub>DD</sub>²·f  →  (1/S)(1/S²)(S) = 1/S²

P is the dynamic power of one gate in watts, C is its capacitance in farads, VDD is the supply in volts and f is the clock frequency in hertz. Here f scales as S because delay scales as 1/S.

That last line is the prize: the same watts per square millimetre, S² times more gates on the chip.

Constant-voltage scaling and the power wall

Hold VDD fixed instead and let only the lengths shrink. Field now rises by S, carriers hit velocity saturation, and the current grows only as S. Delay = CV/ID → (1/S)(1)/(S) = 1/S². Power per gate = C·VDD²·f → (1/S)(1)(S²) = S, so each gate burns S times more, not less.

Power density = S ÷ (1/S²) = S³. With S = 2 that is eight times the heat per square millimetre for the same circuit. This is why VDD fell from 5 V in the early 1990s to about 1 V, and why the fall stopped around 2005.

Once VDD was pinned near a volt, constant-field scaling ended. Clock frequency stalled at a few gigahertz and processors went multicore instead of faster.

Short-channel effects: roll-off, DIBL, punch-through, tunnelling

Below L ≈ 0.25 µm the drain is close enough that its field reaches the source end of the channel. The gate no longer owns the channel charge alone.

  • Threshold roll-off: the depletion charge under the gate is shared with the source and drain junctions. The gate has less bulk charge to support before inversion, so VT falls as L falls. A VT versus L plot drops sharply at short L.
  • DIBL (drain-induced barrier lowering): raising VDS lowers the potential barrier at the source end, so VT falls as VDS rises. A DIBL of 100 mV/V means VT is 100 mV lower at VDS = 1.1 V than at VDS = 0.1 V. The output curve gains slope in saturation, like channel length modulation but from a different cause, and off-state leakage rises.
  • Punch-through: at high VDS the drain and source depletion regions meet below the channel. The gate loses control completely and a large current flows whatever VGS is. Fixes are heavier doping, halo (pocket) implants near source and drain, and a thinner oxide.
  • GIDL (gate-induced drain leakage): in the off state (VGS = 0 or negative for nMOS) the bands bend sharply in the gate-drain overlap and electrons tunnel out of the valence band, adding drain leakage.
  • Gate tunnel current: below tox ≈ 2 nm electrons tunnel straight through the oxide. It grows exponentially as the oxide thins, and it is one reason oxide scaling stopped. See 1.7.

Hot carriers, LDD, NBTI and oxide breakdown

Hot carriers: the lateral field peaks near the drain. Electrons there gain enough energy to cross into the oxide or to break Si-H bonds at the interface, creating traps. VT shifts and gm falls over months of use. A lightly doped drain (LDD) places an n⁻ region between channel and n⁺ drain, so the voltage drops over a longer distance and the peak field falls. The cost is extra series resistance and less drive current.

NBTI (negative bias temperature instability): a pMOS held at a negative gate voltage and high temperature slowly builds interface traps. Its |VT| rises by tens of millivolts over years, so the gate gets slower. It partly recovers when the stress is removed. The nMOS counterpart is PBTI.

TDDB (time-dependent dielectric breakdown): a thin oxide carries stress until a percolation path forms and it shorts. It is random, so it sets a lifetime specification rather than a design rule. Taken together, these effects are why the planar transistor ran out of gate control below about 28 nm, and why the gate moved onto a fin in 1.8.

Worked example

Question. A gate in a 0.25 µm process drives a load of C = 10 fF at VDD = 2.5 V with a 100 MHz clock. The process is scaled to 0.125 µm using constant-field rules. Find the dynamic power before and after scaling, and the ratio between them.

  1. Scale factor S = 0.25 µm ÷ 0.125 µm = 2.
  2. Before scaling: P = C·VDD²·f = (10 × 10⁻¹⁵ F)(2.5 V)²(100 × 10⁶ Hz) = 10⁻¹⁴ × 6.25 × 10⁸ = 6.25 × 10⁻⁶ W.
  3. New capacitance C′ = C/S = 10 fF ÷ 2 = 5 fF.
  4. New supply VDD′ = VDD/S = 2.5 V ÷ 2 = 1.25 V.
  5. Delay halves, so the clock doubles: f′ = 200 MHz.
  6. After scaling: P′ = (5 × 10⁻¹⁵)(1.25)²(200 × 10⁶) = 5 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 1.5625 × 2 × 10⁸ = 1.5625 × 10⁻⁶ W.
  7. Ratio P/P′ = 6.25 ÷ 1.5625 = 4 = S². Area also fell by 4, so power per unit area is unchanged.

Exam traps

  • Power density: only constant-field scaling keeps it at 1. If VDD is held fixed you get S³, not S or S². This is the examiner's favourite version of the question.
  • Delay: constant-field delay scales as 1/S. The 1/S² answer belongs to constant-voltage scaling. Reading off the wrong table costs the whole mark.
  • DIBL versus punch-through: DIBL is a drain-field shift of VT while the gate still controls the channel; punch-through is the source and drain depletion regions meeting, after which the gate controls nothing.
  • Roll-off direction: VT falls as L falls. The body effect in 1.2 raises VT when VSB rises — a different knob, easy to mix up.
  • NBTI shifts the pMOS threshold under negative gate bias at temperature; PBTI is the nMOS counterpart. Also do not quote a fixed millivolt number — the shift depends on stress time and temperature.

1.7 Leakage and subthreshold conduction

A MOSFET that is off still passes a small current. Below threshold the surface is weakly inverted, not empty, so drain current falls exponentially with gate voltage instead of dropping to zero. This subthreshold current, plus gate-oxide tunnelling and junction leakage, sets much of a chip's standby power. Designers quote the off current Ioff per micrometre of width and use the subthreshold swing to compare processes. It decides how low the supply voltage can go before standby power becomes unacceptable.

You need Chapter 1.2 for threshold voltage and its temperature trend, and Chapter 1.3 for the above-threshold current this curve joins.

Weak inversion below threshold

When VGS is below Vth, the gate has not formed a strong inversion layer. A small inversion charge still exists. The channel is weakly inverted, and drain current is limited by diffusion of carriers over the source barrier rather than by drift along the channel. That is why current depends exponentially on gate voltage.

I<sub>D</sub> ≈ µ C<sub>ox</sub> (W/L)(n − 1) V<sub>T</sub><sup>2</sup> e<sup>(V<sub>GS</sub> − V<sub>th</sub>)/(nV<sub>T</sub>)</sup> (1 − e<sup>−V<sub>DS</sub>/V<sub>T</sub></sup>)

ID is drain current in A; µ is carrier mobility in m²/(V·s); Cox is gate oxide capacitance per unit area in F/m²; W and L are channel width and length in m; n = 1 + Cd/Cox is the body factor, with Cd the depletion capacitance per unit area; VT = kT/q is the thermal voltage, about 26 mV at 300 K; Vth is threshold voltage; VGS and VDS are gate-source and drain-source voltages. The exponent uses the thermal VT, never Vth. n is between 1.0 and 1.5 for a bulk device.

For VDS greater than about 3VT, the last bracket is close to 1. The curve is then a straight line on a log-current against VGS graph. The exponential law holds for VGS a few tenths of a volt below Vth. Above Vth the square law of Chapter 1.3 takes over. In the narrow moderate-inversion band around Vth neither formula is exact, so the real curve bends smoothly from exponential to square law instead of meeting at a point. A lower Vth shifts the line left and raises current at every gate voltage below threshold.

Subthreshold swing and off current

The inverse of that line's slope, expressed as a gate voltage, is the subthreshold swing S. It is the gate voltage needed to change ID by one decade, a factor of 10:

S = 2.3 n V<sub>T</sub> = 2.3 V<sub>T</sub>(1 + C<sub>d</sub>/C<sub>ox</sub>)

S is in mV/decade; 2.3 = ln 10; the other symbols are as above. Typical values are 70 to 100 mV/decade. The best possible bulk MOSFET at 300 K has n = 1 and S = 2.3 × 26 mV ≈ 60 mV/decade. Smaller S means a sharper switch.

Off current Ioff is quoted at VGS = 0 and VDS = VDD, normally in nA/µm of transistor width. VDD is the supply voltage. A wider transistor leaks proportionally more, so a per-µm number compares processes fairly. Shorter L shifts Vth through short-channel effects (Chapter 1.6) and narrower W shifts it through the narrow-width effect; both change Ioff. Temperature matters: Ioff roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise. A 25 °C value can be 64 times larger at 85 °C.

Other leakage paths

Subthreshold conduction is not the only leak. Direct gate-oxide tunnelling flows from gate to channel when the oxide is thin. It becomes serious below about 2 nm of silicon dioxide, because electrons tunnel through the barrier. High-k dielectrics reduce this for a given capacitance.

Gate-induced drain leakage (GIDL) appears at low VGS and high VDS. The gate overlaps the drain; the field there bends the drain band so much that electrons tunnel from valence to conduction band. Tunnelled electrons are collected by the drain and the holes go to the body, so the conventional GIDL current flows from the drain to the body. Either way, the drain terminal carries this extra current even when the channel is off.

A reverse-biased source-body or drain-body junction also leaks. Thermal generation in the depletion region adds a small current. In modern processes these paths together can equal or exceed subthreshold current. Each has a different bias dependence, so one Ioff number hides which path dominates. See Unit 3 for the power budget and Unit 6 for leakage test structures.

Worked example

Question. A process has subthreshold swing S = 80 mV/decade and Ioff = 10 nA/µm at Vth = 0.3 V. A designer raises Vth by 100 mV. (a) Find the leakage reduction factor. (b) Find the new Ioff. (c) A chip has 10 mm of total transistor width. Find its new total leakage and compare with the original. (d) Find n. Take VT = 26 mV.

  1. 1. S = 80 mV/decade means every 80 mV rise in Vth cuts Ioff by 10×. For ΔVth = 100 mV, factor = 10100/80 = 101.25 = 17.78.
  2. 2. New Ioff = 10 nA/µm ÷ 17.78 = 0.562 nA/µm ≈ 0.56 nA/µm.
  3. 3. Total width W = 10 mm = 10 000 µm. New total = 0.562 nA/µm × 10 000 µm = 5 623 nA = 5.62 µA. Original = 10 nA/µm × 10 000 µm = 100 000 nA = 100 µA.
  4. 4. From S = 2.3 n VT, n = S/(2.3VT) = 0.080 V/(2.3×0.026 V) = 1.34.

Answer. The factor is 17.78, Ioff falls to 0.56 nA/µm, the chip leaks 5.62 µA instead of 100 µA, and n = 1.34.

Exam traps

  • Using VT for both threshold and thermal voltage. The exponent uses VT = kT/q ≈ 26 mV; the threshold is Vth (or VT0).
  • Claiming a bulk MOSFET can have S below 60 mV/decade at room temperature. S = 2.3nVT with n ≥ 1 sets a floor near 60 mV/decade.
  • Treating Ioff as a fixed current. It is quoted per µm of width, so total leakage changes with total W and with L through Vth.
  • Saying the transistor carries no current at VGS = 0. Ioff is defined at exactly that bias, with VDS = VDD.
  • Forgetting the direction of temperature dependence. Leakage rises with temperature, roughly doubling every 10 °C, not falling.

1.8 FinFET technologies

A planar gate controls the channel from above only. Once the channel is short, that grip is too weak, and the usual fixes — thinner oxide, heavier doping — raise leakage faster than they help. A FinFET lifts the channel into a thin vertical silicon fin and wraps the gate over the top and both sides. Control returns without an impossibly thin oxide. FinFETs became the standard transistor of advanced logic from the 22 nm node.

You need: 1.6 for short-channel effects and 1.7 for the leakage they cause.

Why one-sided control fails

In a planar MOSFET the gate sits on top of a flat channel and controls it from one side. The drain also pulls on the channel, and its share grows as L falls (see 1.6). The standard fixes make things worse. A thinner oxide leaks by tunnelling. Heavier body doping raises junction leakage and junction capacitance and cuts mobility. It does shrink the depletion widths, so it helps contain the drain field, but it stops working once the body must be thin.

The real fix is a thinner body. If the silicon under the gate is thinner than the depletion width it would otherwise need, it is fully depleted even at zero gate bias. Almost no mobile charge is left for the drain to steal, and the gate field reaches the whole body. Control comes from geometry instead of a thinner oxide (1.7).

The fin and its effective width

The fin is a narrow ridge of silicon etched from the wafer. Its width is Wfin (nm) and its height is Hfin (nm). Gate oxide and gate metal cover the top and both sides — a tri-gate. A double-gate version covers only the two sides.

Weff = 2Hfin + Wfin

Weff is the effective channel width of one fin in nm; Hfin is the fin height in nm, counted twice for the two sidewalls; Wfin is the fin width in nm, the top surface. A pure double gate gives Weff = 2Hfin.

Because the body is thin, the channel is undoped or lightly doped. Threshold voltage is set by the gate work function — the energy needed to pull an electron out of the gate metal, in eV — and by Wfin, not by body doping. There is little body effect and less impurity scattering. Trade-offs: Wfin must stay well below the gate length to remain fully depleted, Hfin sets current per fin, and tall thin fins are hard to pattern and can bend.

Width quantisation and gate-all-around

A planar designer draws any W. A FinFET designer chooses a whole number of fins in parallel, so drive strength snaps to fin counts and cells are drawn on a fin pitch — the centre-to-centre distance between neighbouring fins, in nm. Threshold is tuned by work function, drive by fin count. Gate-all-around surrounds a stacked horizontal nanosheet on all four sides, so a wider body gives the same control and more current per unit area.

Worked example

Question. A FinFET has Wfin = 10 nm and Hfin = 30 nm. (a) Find Weff per fin. (b) How many fins give 700 nm of drive width? (c) At 1 mA per µm, find the current per fin and the cell total.

  1. Weff = 2 × 30 nm + 10 nm = 70 nm.
  2. N = 700 nm ÷ 70 nm = 10 fins, exact.
  3. 70 nm = 0.07 µm, so current per fin = 0.07 × 1 mA/µm = 70 µA.
  4. Cell total = 10 × 70 µA = 0.7 mA.

Answer. Weff = 70 nm, 10 fins, 70 µA per fin, 0.7 mA total.

Exam traps

  • Using Wfin alone as the effective width. With Wfin = 10 nm and Hfin = 30 nm that under-counts drive seven times.
  • Forgetting width quantisation. A request for 720 nm needs 11 fins (770 nm), not 10.3 fins.
  • Saying the FinFET removes short-channel effects. It reduces them; drain-induced barrier lowering still exists.
  • Mixing formulas: a double-gate fin gives Weff = 2Hfin, a tri-gate fin gives 2Hfin + Wfin.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Production logic at the smallest nodes has moved to gate-all-around nanosheets, with the gate surrounding stacked horizontal channels. Takeaways

  1. A MOS capacitor becomes a transistor once the gate voltage inverts the surface and the source and drain contact that inversion layer.
  2. Accumulation, depletion and inversion are three states of surface charge; cutoff, linear and saturation are three shapes of the inversion channel.
  3. Threshold voltage is a voltage budget: flat-band, band bending and depletion charge are paid first, and only what remains becomes inversion charge.
  4. The square-law equations are a long-channel, low-field model: current depends on overdrive voltage, W/L and Cox, and turns quadratic in saturation.
  5. Velocity saturation, mobility degradation and channel length modulation are the three corrections that turn the square law into a usable real-device model.
  6. Every terminal pair of a MOSFET is a capacitor; the driver charges and discharges these capacitances, and series resistances slow the charging.
  7. Below threshold the device still conducts exponentially, and that leakage plus gate and junction leakage is now a large share of chip power.
  8. When scaling made planar gates lose control of the channel, the FinFET recovered control by wrapping the gate around a thin fin.

Glossary

Accumulation
The surface condition where the gate attracts majority carriers, making the surface richer in the bulk carrier type than the bulk itself.
Aspect ratio
The ratio W/L of channel width to channel length, which multiplies the current a transistor can carry.
Body bias
The voltage applied between body and source, used to raise or lower threshold voltage through the body effect.
Body effect
The increase in threshold voltage caused by a reverse bias between source and body, which adds depletion charge under the gate.
Channel length modulation
The shortening of the effective channel as VDS grows, which raises the saturation current slightly instead of leaving it flat.
Depletion region
The layer under the gate that holds no free carriers, only fixed ionised dopant atoms, when the gate pushes majority carriers away.
DIBL
Drain-induced barrier lowering: the fall of threshold voltage with rising drain voltage in a short-channel device.
FinFET
A transistor built on a thin silicon fin with a gate wrapped around two or three of its sides, giving better electrostatic control.
Flat-band voltage
The gate voltage at which the semiconductor bands are flat, equal to the work-function difference minus the oxide charge effect.
GIDL
Gate-induced drain leakage: band-to-band tunnelling current in the drain overlap region when the gate is low and the drain is high.
Hot carrier
A carrier that gains high energy from the lateral field and can be injected into the oxide, damaging the device over time.
Inversion layer
A thin surface layer of minority carriers, created by a strong gate voltage, that connects source and drain and carries the transistor current.
LDD
Lightly doped drain: a lightly doped extension between channel and drain that spreads the field and reduces hot-carrier damage.
Mobility degradation
The fall of carrier mobility with the vertical gate field, caused by increased surface scattering.
Output resistance
The small-signal resistance looking into the drain, r0 = 1/(λID), created by channel length modulation.
Overdrive voltage
The gate voltage above threshold, VGS − VTH, that determines how much inversion charge the channel holds.
Oxide capacitance
Cox = εox/tox, the capacitance per unit area of the gate oxide that couples the gate to the channel.
Pinch-off
The drain-end condition where VDS equals the overdrive and the inversion charge density becomes zero.
Pinch-off region
Another name for the saturation region, where the channel is pinched off near the drain and the current is almost flat.
Subthreshold conduction
The exponential drain current that flows even when VGS is below threshold, because carriers still surmount the source barrier.
Subthreshold swing
The change in gate voltage needed to change subthreshold current by one decade, quoted in millivolts per decade.
Threshold voltage
The gate-to-source voltage at which strong inversion starts and a conducting channel just forms.
Transconductance
The small-signal change in drain current for a change in gate voltage, gm = ∂ID/∂VGS, set by μnCox(W/L)Vov in saturation.
Triode region
The same as the linear region: the channel is continuous and the transistor acts as a resistor set by the gate voltage.
Velocity saturation
The limiting drift velocity of carriers at high lateral field, which makes drain current grow only linearly with overdrive.
