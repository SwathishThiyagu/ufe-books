1.1 MOS structure; nMOS and pMOS operating regions

A MOSFET is a capacitor made to conduct. A gate plate sits above doped silicon with a thin oxide between. Gate voltage first pushes majority carriers away, then pulls minority carriers to the surface, forming a thin channel. Source and drain turn the capacitor into a switch. Cutoff, linear and saturation describe the channel. Naming the region from terminal voltages is the first VLSI design skill.

You need: School-level electrostatics (charge, field, capacitance) and semiconductor devices — doping, the pn junction and the depletion region.

The MOS capacitor: accumulation, depletion, inversion

A p-type body has acceptor doping NA (cm−3), so holes are majority carriers. A silicon dioxide layer of thickness tox, a few nanometres, covers the surface. A polysilicon or metal gate on the oxide forms a parallel-plate capacitor with the body.

VFB is the gate-to-body voltage with flat bands and no surface charge. Measure gate voltage VG from there.

Below VFB, the negative gate attracts holes into accumulation. Above VFB, the positive gate repels holes; a depletion region of ionised acceptors widens to width Wd. The surface potential φs (V) is the band bending and grows with VG.

Electrons, minority carriers in p-type silicon, are attracted to the positive gate. When φs reaches 2φF, surface electron concentration equals bulk hole concentration: the surface has inverted to n-type. This thin n-type sheet is the channel; the gate voltage where it first appears is VT.

φ<sub>F</sub> = (kT/q) ln(N<sub>A</sub>/n<sub>i</sub>)

φF is bulk Fermi potential (V); k = 1.38×10−23 J/K is Boltzmann's constant; T is temperature (K, 300 K at room); q = 1.6×10−19 C is electron charge; NA is acceptor doping (cm−3); ni ≈ 1010 cm−3 for silicon. Inversion starts at surface potential 2φF.

From capacitor to transistor: the channel appears

Add two n+ source and drain regions in the p body on either side of the gate. Terminals: gate (G), source (S), drain (D), body (B). Carriers enter at the source; source and body are usually tied.

With VGS > 0 and VDS = 0, the inverted sheet joins the n+ regions once VGS > VT. The excess VOV = VGS − VT buys inversion charge.

With VDS > 0, channel potential rises from 0 at source to VDS at drain. Gate-to-channel voltage is VGS at source and VGS − VDS at drain. Inversion charge follows local gate-to-channel voltage minus VT, so the channel is thick at source and thin at drain.

Increasing VDS thins the drain end. It vanishes at VGS − VDS = VT, that is VDS = VGS − VT: pinch-off. Beyond it, extra voltage drops across a short depleted region near the drain and current stays almost flat; the gate feels only overdrive.

The three regions, nMOS and pMOS

Cutoff: no channel, only leakage (see 1.7). Linear: continuous channel; gate-controlled resistor (also called triode or ohmic in some syllabi). Saturation: channel pinched off at drain; current source set by VGS.

A pMOS flips signs: n-type body, p+ source and drain, holes form the channel. Source is the higher potential, so VGS, VDS and VT are negative. Habit: compare magnitudes |VGS| with |VT|, and |VDS| with |VGS| − |VT|.

Exam traps

  • Swapping nMOS and pMOS tests. A pMOS conducts when VGS is more negative than VT; compare magnitudes.
  • Using excess above overdrive in the region test. The VDS in the test is the full drain-to-source voltage, not VDS − (VGS − VT).
  • Forgetting that VDS on a pMOS is negative when the source is the higher terminal.
  • Believing the channel vanishes at pinch-off. Only the drain end pinches; current continues, set by overdrive.
  • Treating VT as turn-on for useful current. VT is where the channel first forms; real current needs VGS comfortably above it.

1.2 Threshold voltage and body effect

Threshold voltage VT is the gate-source voltage at which an nMOS surface flips into inversion. Part of that voltage bends the bands and empties the body; only the rest creates inversion charge. This chapter splits VT into its physical pieces and adds the body effect: when the source sits above the body, the depletion region holds more charge and VT rises. Circuit designers use this to size stacked transistors, estimate pass-transistor levels, and set SPICE models.

You need: accumulation, depletion and inversion from 1.1.

Threshold voltage from three parts

For an nMOS with a p-type body, start with zero gate voltage. The gate work function and the body differ, so a flat-band voltage VFB is needed just to make the bands flat. Fixed oxide charge Qox shifts it too: VFB = φMS − Qox/Cox. φMS is the gate-to-body work function difference in volts; Qox is fixed oxide charge per unit area in C/m²; Cox is oxide capacitance per unit area in F/m².

As gate voltage rises, holes are pushed away and a depletion region forms. At inversion onset the surface potential is 2φF. φF is the bulk Fermi potential: φF = (kT/q) ln(NA/ni). kT/q is thermal voltage, about 26 mV at 300 K; NA is acceptor doping in m⁻³; ni is intrinsic concentration in m⁻³. For NA = 10¹⁷ cm⁻³, φF ≈ 0.41 V and 2φF ≈ 0.82 V.

VT0 = VFB + 2φF + √(2q εsi NA · 2φF) / Cox

VT0 is threshold voltage with source tied to body, in V; VFB is flat-band voltage in V; φF is bulk Fermi potential in V; q = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C is electron charge; εsi is silicon permittivity, about 1.04×10⁻¹⁰ F/m; NA is acceptor doping in m⁻³; Cox = εox/tox is oxide capacitance per unit area in F/m²; εox is oxide permittivity, about 3.45×10⁻¹¹ F/m; tox is oxide thickness in m.

The square-root term is depletion charge per unit area divided by Cox. It is the gate voltage spent on depletion charge before inversion charge appears. At threshold the inversion charge is still zero, so the gate supports band bending, depletion charge and flat-band shift only.

Body effect moves the threshold

Tie the source to the body and VSB = 0. Raise the source above the body and the source-body junction is reverse biased. VSB is source voltage minus body voltage in volts. The depletion region widens, exposing more ionized acceptors, so depletion charge Qdep increases. The gate must supply more voltage before the surface reaches inversion. That is the body effect.

VT = VT0 + γ(√(2φF + VSB) − √(2φF))

VT is threshold voltage at a given source-to-body voltage, in V; VT0 is threshold voltage with VSB = 0, in V; γ is body-effect coefficient in √V; φF is bulk Fermi potential in V; VSB is source-to-body voltage in V, positive for reverse bias in an nMOS.

γ = √(2q εsi NA) / Cox

γ is body-effect coefficient in √V; q = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C; εsi is silicon permittivity in F/m; NA is acceptor doping in m⁻³; Cox is oxide capacitance per unit area in F/m². Typical γ is 0.1 to 0.5 √V.

For small VSB the rise is almost linear; for large VSB it grows slowly as √VSB. The formula assumes reverse bias, strong inversion and uniform body doping. It is an nMOS formula; a pMOS needs reversed signs. Body effect is zero when source and body are tied together. It matters for stacked and pass transistors, whose source can float above the body.

Temperature also shifts VT. As temperature rises, φF and depletion charge change, and VT falls by roughly 1 to 2 mV per °C. In SPICE Level 1 the numbers are carried by VTO, GAMMA, PHI and TOX.

Reading the curve and the numbers

The figure after Section 1 plots VT against VSB with VT0 = 0.45 V and 2φF = 0.82 V. At VSB = 1 V, γ = 0.21 √V gives VT ≈ 0.544 V; γ = 0.42 √V gives VT ≈ 0.636 V. At VSB = 3 V the same curves reach about 0.670 V and 0.891 V. The stronger coefficient does not change VT at VSB = 0; it changes how fast VT climbs.

  • Use VSB, not VBS, in the square root.
  • Keep NA in m⁻³ when you use q and εsi in SI units.
  • γ has units √V, so γ times a square-root voltage gives volts.

Most courses expect you to use the result and explain the mechanism. The phrase threshold voltage effects means body effect, temperature and short-channel changes. Short-channel changes belong to 1.6; leakage cost of a low VT belongs to 1.7.

Worked example

Question. An nMOS has NA = 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ and tox = 4 nm. Use εsi = 1.04×10⁻¹⁰ F/m, εox = 3.45×10⁻¹¹ F/m, q = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, ni = 1.5×10¹⁰ cm⁻³, and kT/q = 0.026 V. (a) Find Cox, φF and γ. (b) Take VT0 = 0.45 V and find VT at VSB = 1 V.

  1. Convert doping: NA = 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ × 10⁶ cm³/m³ = 10²³ m⁻³.
  2. Oxide capacitance: Cox = εox/tox = 3.45×10⁻¹¹ F/m ÷ 4×10⁻⁹ m = 8.625×10⁻³ F/m².
  3. Fermi potential: φF = 0.026 V × ln(10¹⁷/1.5×10¹⁰) = 0.026 × 15.713 = 0.4085 V.
  4. Band-bending term: 2φF = 0.8171 V.
  5. Body-effect coefficient: γ = √(2 × 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ × 1.04×10⁻¹⁰ × 10²³) / 8.625×10⁻³ = √(3.328×10⁻⁶) / 8.625×10⁻³ = 1.824×10⁻³ / 8.625×10⁻³ = 0.2115 √V.
  6. Threshold shift: ΔVT = γ(√(2φF + VSB) − √(2φF)) = 0.2115(√1.8171 − √0.8171) = 0.2115(1.3480 − 0.9039) = 0.0939 V.
  7. Threshold: VT = VT0 + ΔVT = 0.45 V + 0.0939 V = 0.5439 V.

Answer. VT ≈ 0.544 V at VSB = 1 V; γ ≈ 0.2115 √V and Cox ≈ 8.625×10⁻³ F/m².

Exam traps

  • VBS versus VSB: the body-effect formula needs VSB = VS − VB. Using VBS flips the sign and can make the threshold appear to fall under reverse bias.
  • γ is not dimensionless. It has units √V; a γ quoted in volts or V⁻¹ is wrong.
  • The formula uses 2φF, not φF. Using φF alone halves the band-bending term.
  • For SI calculation, convert NA from cm⁻³ to m⁻³. Mixing cm⁻³ with F/m and q gives an answer off by 10³ or worse.
  • Body effect is zero only when source and body are at the same potential. In a stack, the upper transistor can have VSB > 0 even though the lower one has VSB = 0.

1.3 MOSFET I-V characteristics and device equations

A MOSFET does not obey Ohm's law. Above threshold the gate sets the channel charge and the drain voltage pulls that charge along. Two square-law formulas follow — one for the linear region, one for saturation — giving drain current from the gate overdrive and the W/L ratio. Designers use them to size transistors, estimate gain and find on-resistance before running a simulator.

You need: 1.1 for the three regions and 1.2 for VT.

Channel charge and the drift integral

Take an nMOS with the source at 0 V. Let x run from 0 at the source to L at the drain, where L is the channel length in metres. Let V(x) be the channel potential, so V(0) = 0 and V(L) = VDS. The oxide capacitance per unit area is Cox = εox/tox in F/m², with εox the oxide permittivity and tox the oxide thickness. Above threshold the inversion charge per unit area at x is Q(x) = Cox[VGS − VT − V(x)] in C/m². VGS is gate-to-source voltage, VT is threshold voltage, and VGS − VT is the overdrive Vov.

An electron drifts with velocity v = µnE, where µn is electron mobility in m²/(V·s) and E = dV/dx is the lateral field. Current is charge per unit length times velocity: ID = WQ(x)v, where W is channel width in metres. Substituting, ID = WµnCox[VGS − VT − V(x)]dV/dx. ID is the same at every x, since the channel stores no net charge in steady state.

Separate and integrate from source to drain. The left side gives IDL; the right side gives WµnCox[(VGS − VT)VDS − VDS²/2]. Divide by L and write kn′ = µnCox.

I<sub>D</sub> = k<sub>n</sub>′(W/L)[(V<sub>GS</sub> − V<sub>T</sub>)V<sub>DS</sub> − V<sub>DS</sub>²/2]

valid for VDS ≤ VGS − VT (that is, VDS ≤ Vov); kn′ = µnCox in A/V²; W/L is the width-to-length ratio; VGS, VDS and VT are in volts; ID is in amperes.

Saturation and the square law

At VDS = Vov the charge at the drain end falls to zero, Q(L) = 0, and the channel is pinched off. Beyond that point the extra drain voltage drops across a short depleted region near the drain, not across the conducting channel, so the current stays almost constant. Put VDS = Vov in the linear formula: the bracket becomes Vov² − Vov²/2 = Vov²/2.

I<sub>D</sub> = ½ k<sub>n</sub>′(W/L)(V<sub>GS</sub> − V<sub>T</sub>)²

valid for VDS ≥ VGS − VT; the factor ½ belongs to saturation; kn′ = µnCox in A/V²; W/L is dimensionless; ID is in amperes.

The transfer characteristic plots ID against VGS at fixed VDS: zero for VGS ≤ VT, then rising as (VGS − VT)². The output family plots ID against VDS for several VGS; each curve rises almost linearly, bends near VDS = Vov and flattens. The figure uses kn′ = 1 mA/V², W/L = 1 and VT = 0.5 V. At VGS = 2.0 V the overdrive is 1.5 V, so the flat current is ½ × 1 mA/V² × 1 × (1.5 V)² = 1.125 mA. At VGS = 1.5 V it is 0.5 mA; at VGS = 1.0 V it is 0.125 mA.

Some syllabi call these the square-law equations, the Shichman-Hodges model or SPICE Level-1. The pMOS has the same shape with all signs flipped: use VSG = VS − VG, VSD = VS − VD and |VT|. Saturation: ID = ½ kp′(W/L)(VSG − |VT|)². Linear: ID = kp′(W/L)[(VSG − |VT|)VSD − VSD²/2], with kp′ = µpCox. In many processes µp is about 0.25 to 0.5 times µn, so a pMOS of the same W/L is weaker and is made wider.

Transconductance, on-resistance and use

The transconductance gm is the change in ID for a small change in VGS at fixed VDS. Differentiating the square law: gm = kn′(W/L)(VGS − VT), which is also gm = 2ID/(VGS − VT). Units are A/V, also called siemens (S). A larger overdrive or larger W/L gives more gain per volt.

Near VDS = 0 the transistor acts as a resistor. Differentiating the linear formula at VDS = 0 gives the slope kn′(W/L)(VGS − VT); the on-resistance is its inverse, Ron ≈ 1/[kn′(W/L)(VGS − VT)]. This is the resistance of a closed switch. It falls as the overdrive or W/L rises, which is why a gate driven at a higher supply makes a faster switch.

Rearranged for design, saturation gives W/L = 2ID/[kn′(VGS − VT)²]. In a current mirror the ratio of W/L sets the ratio of currents. In a digital gate the on-resistance and the load capacitance set the delay; see 1.5. Channel-length modulation and velocity saturation are ignored here; their corrections are in 1.4.

Worked example

Question. An nMOS has kn′ = 100 µA/V², W/L = 20, VT = 0.5 V, VGS = 1.5 V and VDS = 2 V. Find the drain current and transconductance. Then find the drain current with the same gate voltage when VDS = 0.5 V.

  1. Overdrive: Vov = VGS − VT = 1.5 V − 0.5 V = 1.0 V.
  2. VDS = 2 V ≥ Vov = 1.0 V, so the device is in saturation.
  3. ID = ½ kn′(W/L)Vov² = 0.5 × 100 µA/V² × 20 × (1.0 V)² = 1000 µA = 1 mA.
  4. gm = kn′(W/L)Vov = 100 µA/V² × 20 × 1.0 V = 2000 µA/V = 2 mA/V.
  5. At VDS = 0.5 V < 1.0 V the device is in the linear region.
  6. ID = kn′(W/L)[VovVDS − VDS²/2] = 100 µA/V² × 20 × [(1.0 V)(0.5 V) − (0.5 V)²/2] = 2000 µA/V² × 0.375 V² = 750 µA = 0.75 mA.

Answer. The device carries ID = 1 mA in saturation, its transconductance is gm = 2 mA/V, and at VDS = 0.5 V in the linear region it carries 0.75 mA.

Exam traps

  • Putting VDS into the saturation formula in place of the overdrive (VGS − VT), which makes the current keep growing after saturation.
  • Dropping the factor ½ in the saturation formula, which doubles every saturation answer.
  • Writing L/W where the formula needs W/L, which is easy to do when rearranging for a given current.
  • Treating a pMOS as an nMOS with a positive VT; flip the signs and use VSG, VSD and |VT|.

1.4 Non-ideal effects: velocity saturation, channel length modulation

The square law is ideal. Mobility degradation comes from the vertical gate field: it drags carriers against a rough interface. Velocity saturation comes from the horizontal drain field: it caps carrier speed. Channel length modulation comes from the pinch-off point sliding toward the source as VDS rises. These explain why a short transistor gives less current than predicted, why gm stops growing, and why the output curve has a slope.

You need the square-law equations, the saturation condition and the symbols kn′, Cox and W/L from 1.3, and the threshold voltage VT from 1.2.

Mobility degradation: the vertical field spoils µ

The square law assumes constant µ0. The vertical gate field presses carriers into a thin sheet against the rough silicon–oxide interface. Each collision there scatters a carrier. More gate voltage means more charge but more scattering, so each carrier moves more slowly.

µeff = µ0 / (1 + θ(VGS − VT))

µeff is the effective mobility in cm²/V·s; µ0 is the low-field mobility in cm²/V·s; θ is the mobility degradation coefficient in 1/V, a process constant typically between 0.1 V⁻¹ and 1 V⁻¹ for thin oxides; VGS is gate–source voltage and VT is threshold voltage, both in volts.

ID = ½ kn′(W/L)(VGS − VT)² / (1 + θ(VGS − VT))

ID is the drain current in A; kn′ = µ0Cox is the process transconductance parameter in A/V²; Cox is the oxide capacitance per unit area in F/m²; W/L is the width-to-length ratio, both in the same length unit.

The denominator grows with overdrive, so the transfer curve bends over. Going from 1 V to 2 V of overdrive gives less than four times the current. The transconductance gm = ∂ID/∂VGS flattens too. Long-channel analog stages lose gain at high gate bias for this reason.

Velocity saturation: the horizontal field caps vd

Carriers do not speed up without limit. Above a critical field, energy goes into optical phonons instead of velocity, and drift velocity levels off. A compact fit is:

vd = µE / (1 + E/Ec)

vd is drift velocity in cm/s; µ is mobility in cm²/V·s; E is lateral field in V/cm; Ec is critical field in V/cm. At E = Ec, velocity is half its ceiling; for E much larger than Ec, vd approaches vsat.

vsat is about 10⁷ cm/s for electrons in silicon and lower for holes. By definition Ec = vsat/µ. The channel field is roughly VDS/L. Take L = 0.25 µm and VDS = 2.5 V: E = 10⁵ V/cm. With µn = 500 cm²/V·s, Ec = 10⁷/500 = 2 × 10⁴ V/cm = 2 × 10⁶ V/m, far into saturation. Saturation arrives earlier than the square law says:

VDSsat = (VGS − VT)EcL / ((VGS − VT) + EcL)

VDSsat is the drain–source voltage where saturation begins, in V; EcL is critical field times channel length, in V. For long L, EcL is much larger than overdrive and VDSsat ≈ VGS − VT. For short L it falls to about EcL, a few tenths of a volt.

ID ≈ vsatCoxW(VGS − VT − VDSsat)

vsat is saturation velocity in cm/s; Cox is oxide capacitance per unit area in F/cm²; W is channel width in cm; the bracket is a voltage in V. When VDSsat is small this is close to vsatCoxW(VGS − VT): linear in overdrive, not quadratic. Then gm = vsatCoxW is constant, independent of VGS. This is the biggest reason the square law over-predicts short-channel current.

Channel length modulation: the channel shortens

Past pinch-off the square law freezes current. In a real device, pinch-off sits where channel potential equals VGS − VT; the surplus VDS − VDSsat drops across a short depletion region to the drain. Raising VDS widens this region and pushes pinch-off toward the source, so the conducting channel is only L − ΔL long. A shorter channel carries more current, so ID creeps up with VDS.

ID = ½ kn′(W/L)(VGS − VT)² (1 + λVDS)

λ is the channel length modulation parameter in 1/V; VDS is drain–source voltage in V. λ is a fitting parameter, not a material constant, and grows roughly as 1/L: negligible for a long channel, about 0.1 V⁻¹ for a quarter-micrometre device.

ro = 1/(λID)

ro is the small-signal output resistance in Ω; ID is the saturation current in A. A larger λ means a lower ro and lower intrinsic gain gm·ro, so short-channel amplifiers are harder to design.

The (1 + λVDS) factor belongs only in saturation. In the linear region the channel is continuous and there is no pinch-off point to move.

Worked example

Question. An nMOS transistor has W = 1 µm, L = 0.25 µm, Cox = 8.6 fF/µm², µn = 500 cm²/V·s and vsat = 10⁷ cm/s. It is biased at VGS − VT = 2 V, and λ = 0.1 V⁻¹. (a) Find the square-law saturation current. (b) Find Ec, EcL and VDSsat. (c) Find the velocity-saturated current and the ratio of the two currents. (d) Find ro at the velocity-saturated current.

  1. Convert to SI: µn = 500 cm²/V·s = 0.05 m²/V·s; Cox = 8.6 fF/µm² = 8.6 × 10⁻³ F/m²; W = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m; L = 0.25 × 10⁻⁶ m; vsat = 10⁷ cm/s = 1 × 10⁵ m/s.
  2. Square law: ID = ½µnCox(W/L)(VGS − VT)² = 0.5 × 0.05 × 8.6 × 10⁻³ × 4 × 4 = 3.44 × 10⁻³ A = 3.44 mA.
  3. Ec = vsat/µn = 1 × 10⁵/0.05 = 2 × 10⁶ V/m; EcL = 2 × 10⁶ × 0.25 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.5 V.
  4. VDSsat = (2 × 0.5)/(2 + 0.5) = 0.4 V, far below 2 V.
  5. ID = vsatCoxW(VGS − VT − VDSsat) = 1 × 10⁵ × 8.6 × 10⁻³ × 1 × 10⁻⁶ × 1.6 = 1.376 × 10⁻³ A ≈ 1.38 mA.
  6. Ratio: 3.44/1.376 = 2.5, so the square law over-predicts by about 2.5×.
  7. ro = 1/(λID) = 1/(0.1 × 1.376 × 10⁻³) = 7267 Ω ≈ 7.3 kΩ.

Answer. The device carries about 1.38 mA, 2.5 times less than the 3.44 mA the square law predicts, and its output resistance is 7.3 kΩ.

Exam traps

  • Applying (1 + λVDS) in the linear region. The factor exists only after pinch-off; in triode the channel is continuous.
  • Treating mobility degradation and velocity saturation as one effect. One comes from the vertical gate field, the other from the horizontal drain field; only velocity saturation makes the saturation current linear in overdrive.
  • Using VDSsat = VGS − VT for a short channel. Use VDSsat = (VGS − VT)EcL/((VGS − VT) + EcL); the square-law value can be several times too large.
  • Unit slips between cm and m. µ in cm²/V·s becomes m²/V·s by multiplying by 10⁻⁴; Cox in fF/µm² becomes F/m² by multiplying by 10⁻³; vsat in cm/s becomes m/s by multiplying by 10⁻². Convert everything to one set before substituting.
  • Believing λ is a measured material constant. It is a curve-fitting parameter that grows roughly as 1/L; ro = 1/(λID) is only a first-order estimate.

1.5 MOSFET capacitances and parasitic resistances

Every MOSFET terminal pair is a small capacitor, and every cycle the driving gate charges and discharges these capacitors through series resistances. The gate oxide gives the largest part, but overlap and junction capacitances add to it, and their split among source, drain, gate and bulk changes with the operating region. These parasitics set switching delay, dynamic power and fan-out. Cell libraries and SPICE models carry them as Cox, overlap and junction parameters. The same numbers explain why a wide driver is fast and why a minimum-size transistor still has input capacitance.

You need: 1.1 for the channel and 1.2 for the region boundary at V_T.

Oxide capacitance and overlap

The gate and the channel form a parallel-plate capacitor. The insulator is the gate oxide. Its capacitance per unit area is:

Cox = εox / tox

Cox is oxide capacitance per unit area in F/m²; εox is the permittivity of silicon dioxide, about 3.45×10⁻¹¹ F/m; tox is oxide thickness in m.

For a transistor of width W and length L, the gate area capacitance is CoxWL. Define W as the distance across the channel, perpendicular to current, and L as the channel length from source to drain. Use consistent units: Cox in fF/µm² with W and L in µm gives capacitance in fF.

Real source and drain diffusions spread a little under the gate. This lateral diffusion creates overlap capacitance. SPICE stores it per unit width:

Cgs,ov = CGSO · W,   Cgd,ov = CGDO · W,   Cgb,ov = CGBO · W

CGSO, CGDO and CGBO are overlap capacitances per unit width in F/m; W is width in m. Write Cov for the overlap capacitance per side; Cov = CGSO·W = CGDO·W when the two sides match.

CGSO and CGDO grow when the lateral diffusion is large relative to L. In a short-channel device the overlap part can exceed the ideal area part. Fringing fields from the gate to source and drain are often folded into these same parameters by the model.

The Meyer model: splitting the gate capacitance

The gate capacitance is not always connected the same way. The Meyer model splits the gate charge among source, drain and bulk according to the region. The inversion channel is the moving plate. The figure above lists the split for the worked device.

In cutoff there is no inversion channel. The gate sees only the overlap capacitances to source and drain, and the area capacitance goes to bulk through the depleted silicon: Cgs = Cgd = Cov and Cgb ≈ CoxWL. In the linear region the channel is a resistor connecting source and drain. The gate capacitance divides equally: Cgs = Cgd ≈ ½CoxWL + Cov and Cgb = 0. In saturation the channel is pinched off near the drain. Most of the gate charge is reached from the source: Cgs ≈ ⅔CoxWL + Cov, Cgd ≈ Cov and Cgb = 0.

The fractions ½ and ⅔ are approximations. They come from integrating the channel charge along the channel. Use them for hand estimates. A simulator uses a smoother model near the boundaries, but the three cases give the right intuition.

The sum changes with region. In saturation the gate capacitance seen by the driver is Cgs + Cgd, not CoxWL. A transistor driven into saturation presents less capacitance than its area suggests, while the same transistor in cutoff presents almost none to the source but the full area to the bulk.

Junction capacitances and series resistances

Source and drain diffusions form pn junctions with the bulk. Each junction has two parts: a bottom area and a sidewall perimeter. SPICE uses:

Cj = Cj0 / (1 + V/VB)^m

Cj0 is zero-bias capacitance per unit area in F/m²; V is reverse bias across the junction in V, positive for reverse bias; VB is built-in potential in V; m is the grading coefficient, about 0.3 to 0.5.

The area part is CJ × area. The sidewall part is CJSW × perimeter. CJ and CJSW are per unit area and per unit length. Both are voltage dependent. As V rises, Cj falls. For a source at the same potential as the bulk, V is small and the capacitance is near Cj0. For a drain that swings, the capacitance changes during the cycle.

Series resistance comes from three places. The source and drain diffusion have sheet resistance, so a long diffusion acts as a resistor. The contacts add fixed contact resistance. The polysilicon gate is a distributed resistor: the gate contact charges the channel through a resistive strip. For a wide device, model the gate as an RC ladder, not one resistor. These resistances slow charging even when the capacitance is small. In a short-channel device, source/drain resistance can be a significant fraction of the channel resistance.

Use the parasitic values from the technology file. Never guess them from W and L alone when a layout is available.

Worked example

Question. An nMOS has W = 1 µm, L = 0.25 µm, tox = 4 nm and an overlap of Lov = 0.02 µm at each side. Take εox = 3.45×10⁻¹¹ F/m. Find Cox in fF/µm², the gate area capacitance, the overlap capacitance per side, Cgs and Cgd in saturation, the total gate capacitance in saturation, and Cgs in cutoff.

  1. Step 1: Cox = εox / tox = 3.45×10⁻¹¹ / 4×10⁻⁹ = 8.625×10⁻³ F/m². Since 1 F/m² = 1000 fF/µm², Cox = 8.625 fF/µm² ≈ 8.63 fF/µm².
  2. Step 2: Gate area capacitance = CoxWL = 8.625 × 1 × 0.25 = 2.156 fF ≈ 2.16 fF.
  3. Step 3: Overlap per side = Cov = Cox W Lov = 8.625 × 1 × 0.02 = 0.1725 fF ≈ 0.17 fF.
  4. Step 4: In saturation, Cgs = ⅔ × area + Cov = 1.4375 + 0.1725 = 1.610 fF ≈ 1.61 fF. Cgd = Cov = 0.17 fF.
  5. Step 5: Total gate capacitance in saturation = Cgs + Cgd = 1.610 + 0.1725 = 1.7825 fF ≈ 1.78 fF.
  6. Step 6: In cutoff, the channel is absent, so Cgs = Cgd = Cov = 0.17 fF. The area capacitance appears from gate to bulk: Cgb ≈ 2.16 fF.

Answer. Cox ≈ 8.63 fF/µm², gate area ≈ 2.16 fF, overlap ≈ 0.17 fF per side, saturation Cgs ≈ 1.61 fF and Cgd ≈ 0.17 fF, total gate capacitance ≈ 1.78 fF, and cutoff Cgs ≈ 0.17 fF.

Exam traps

  • Using CoxWL as the gate capacitance in every region. In saturation Cgs is about ⅔CoxWL plus overlap, and Cgd is only overlap.
  • Ignoring overlap capacitance. For short L, CGSO·W and CGDO·W can dominate the gate capacitance.
  • Writing the junction capacitance as a fixed number. It falls with reverse voltage as Cj0/(1 + V/VB)^m.
  • Mixing units: Cox in F/m² with W and L in µm gives the wrong scale. Convert Cox to fF/µm² or use metres throughout.
  • Treating gate resistance as one lump. A wide gate is an RC ladder; one resistor underestimates the charging delay.

1.6 MOSFET scaling and short-channel effects

Scaling builds the same circuit with smaller devices. Shrink the channel length, the oxide thickness and the supply voltage together by a factor S and the electric field inside the transistor does not change, so the device still works. Shrink only the lengths and the gate slowly loses control of the channel. Below about a quarter micrometre that loss shows up as a family of short-channel effects: threshold roll-off, drain-induced barrier lowering, punch-through, hot carriers and slow reliability drift. Every node you name — 28 nm, 7 nm — is described by these numbers.

You need: 1.3 for the drain current equations and 1.4 for velocity saturation and channel length modulation.

Constant-field scaling by a factor S

Pick a factor S > 1. Divide the channel length L, the width W, the oxide thickness tox and every voltage (VDD, VT) by S. Multiply the channel doping NA by S.

Why together? Field is voltage divided by distance. Both fall by S, so the field pattern inside the device is unchanged. Some syllabi call this constant-field scaling, or Dennard scaling after the 1974 paper that proposed it.

Now follow the consequences. Oxide capacitance per unit area is Cox = εox/tox, so Cox multiplies by S. The parameter κ = µnCox(W/L) also multiplies by S, because W/L is a ratio and does not change.

  • Drain current ID ∝ κ(VGS − VT)² → S × (1/S)² = 1/S.
  • Gate capacitance C = CoxWL → S × (1/S) × (1/S) = 1/S.
  • Delay τ = CVDD/ID → (1/S)(1/S)/(1/S) = 1/S, so the clock can rise by S.
  • Area per gate = WL → 1/S², so power per gate divided by area stays at 1.
P = C·V<sub>DD</sub>²·f  →  (1/S)(1/S²)(S) = 1/S²

P is the dynamic power of one gate in watts, C is its capacitance in farads, VDD is the supply in volts and f is the clock frequency in hertz. Here f scales as S because delay scales as 1/S.

That last line is the prize: the same watts per square millimetre, S² times more gates on the chip.

Constant-voltage scaling and the power wall

Hold VDD fixed instead and let only the lengths shrink. Field now rises by S, carriers hit velocity saturation, and the current grows only as S. Delay = CV/ID → (1/S)(1)/(S) = 1/S². Power per gate = C·VDD²·f → (1/S)(1)(S²) = S, so each gate burns S times more, not less.

Power density = S ÷ (1/S²) = S³. With S = 2 that is eight times the heat per square millimetre for the same circuit. This is why VDD fell from 5 V in the early 1990s to about 1 V, and why the fall stopped around 2005.

Once VDD was pinned near a volt, constant-field scaling ended. Clock frequency stalled at a few gigahertz and processors went multicore instead of faster.

Short-channel effects: roll-off, DIBL, punch-through, tunnelling

Below L ≈ 0.25 µm the drain is close enough that its field reaches the source end of the channel. The gate no longer owns the channel charge alone.

  • Threshold roll-off: the depletion charge under the gate is shared with the source and drain junctions. The gate has less bulk charge to support before inversion, so VT falls as L falls. A VT versus L plot drops sharply at short L.
  • DIBL (drain-induced barrier lowering): raising VDS lowers the potential barrier at the source end, so VT falls as VDS rises. A DIBL of 100 mV/V means VT is 100 mV lower at VDS = 1.1 V than at VDS = 0.1 V. The output curve gains slope in saturation, like channel length modulation but from a different cause, and off-state leakage rises.
  • Punch-through: at high VDS the drain and source depletion regions meet below the channel. The gate loses control completely and a large current flows whatever VGS is. Fixes are heavier doping, halo (pocket) implants near source and drain, and a thinner oxide.
  • GIDL (gate-induced drain leakage): in the off state (VGS = 0 or negative for nMOS) the bands bend sharply in the gate-drain overlap and electrons tunnel out of the valence band, adding drain leakage.
  • Gate tunnel current: below tox ≈ 2 nm electrons tunnel straight through the oxide. It grows exponentially as the oxide thins, and it is one reason oxide scaling stopped. See 1.7.

Hot carriers, LDD, NBTI and oxide breakdown

Hot carriers: the lateral field peaks near the drain. Electrons there gain enough energy to cross into the oxide or to break Si-H bonds at the interface, creating traps. VT shifts and gm falls over months of use. A lightly doped drain (LDD) places an n⁻ region between channel and n⁺ drain, so the voltage drops over a longer distance and the peak field falls. The cost is extra series resistance and less drive current.

NBTI (negative bias temperature instability): a pMOS held at a negative gate voltage and high temperature slowly builds interface traps. Its |VT| rises by tens of millivolts over years, so the gate gets slower. It partly recovers when the stress is removed. The nMOS counterpart is PBTI.

TDDB (time-dependent dielectric breakdown): a thin oxide carries stress until a percolation path forms and it shorts. It is random, so it sets a lifetime specification rather than a design rule. Taken together, these effects are why the planar transistor ran out of gate control below about 28 nm, and why the gate moved onto a fin in 1.8.

Worked example

Question. A gate in a 0.25 µm process drives a load of C = 10 fF at VDD = 2.5 V with a 100 MHz clock. The process is scaled to 0.125 µm using constant-field rules. Find the dynamic power before and after scaling, and the ratio between them.

  1. Scale factor S = 0.25 µm ÷ 0.125 µm = 2.
  2. Before scaling: P = C·VDD²·f = (10 × 10⁻¹⁵ F)(2.5 V)²(100 × 10⁶ Hz) = 10⁻¹⁴ × 6.25 × 10⁸ = 6.25 × 10⁻⁶ W.
  3. New capacitance C′ = C/S = 10 fF ÷ 2 = 5 fF.
  4. New supply VDD′ = VDD/S = 2.5 V ÷ 2 = 1.25 V.
  5. Delay halves, so the clock doubles: f′ = 200 MHz.
  6. After scaling: P′ = (5 × 10⁻¹⁵)(1.25)²(200 × 10⁶) = 5 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 1.5625 × 2 × 10⁸ = 1.5625 × 10⁻⁶ W.
  7. Ratio P/P′ = 6.25 ÷ 1.5625 = 4 = S². Area also fell by 4, so power per unit area is unchanged.

Answer. 6.25 µW before scaling and 1.5625 µW after; power per gate falls by S² = 4 and power density stays the same.

Exam traps

  • Power density: only constant-field scaling keeps it at 1. If VDD is held fixed you get S³, not S or S². This is the examiner's favourite version of the question.
  • Delay: constant-field delay scales as 1/S. The 1/S² answer belongs to constant-voltage scaling. Reading off the wrong table costs the whole mark.
  • DIBL versus punch-through: DIBL is a drain-field shift of VT while the gate still controls the channel; punch-through is the source and drain depletion regions meeting, after which the gate controls nothing.
  • Roll-off direction: VT falls as L falls. The body effect in 1.2 raises VT when VSB rises — a different knob, easy to mix up.
  • NBTI shifts the pMOS threshold under negative gate bias at temperature; PBTI is the nMOS counterpart. Also do not quote a fixed millivolt number — the shift depends on stress time and temperature.

1.7 Leakage and subthreshold conduction

A MOSFET that is off still passes a small current. Below threshold the surface is weakly inverted, not empty, so drain current falls exponentially with gate voltage instead of dropping to zero. This subthreshold current, plus gate-oxide tunnelling and junction leakage, sets much of a chip's standby power. Designers quote the off current Ioff per micrometre of width and use the subthreshold swing to compare processes. It decides how low the supply voltage can go before standby power becomes unacceptable.

You need Chapter 1.2 for threshold voltage and its temperature trend, and Chapter 1.3 for the above-threshold current this curve joins.

Weak inversion below threshold

When VGS is below Vth, the gate has not formed a strong inversion layer. A small inversion charge still exists. The channel is weakly inverted, and drain current is limited by diffusion of carriers over the source barrier rather than by drift along the channel. That is why current depends exponentially on gate voltage.

I<sub>D</sub> ≈ µ C<sub>ox</sub> (W/L)(n − 1) V<sub>T</sub><sup>2</sup> e<sup>(V<sub>GS</sub> − V<sub>th</sub>)/(nV<sub>T</sub>)</sup> (1 − e<sup>−V<sub>DS</sub>/V<sub>T</sub></sup>)

ID is drain current in A; µ is carrier mobility in m²/(V·s); Cox is gate oxide capacitance per unit area in F/m²; W and L are channel width and length in m; n = 1 + Cd/Cox is the body factor, with Cd the depletion capacitance per unit area; VT = kT/q is the thermal voltage, about 26 mV at 300 K; Vth is threshold voltage; VGS and VDS are gate-source and drain-source voltages. The exponent uses the thermal VT, never Vth. n is between 1.0 and 1.5 for a bulk device.

For VDS greater than about 3VT, the last bracket is close to 1. The curve is then a straight line on a log-current against VGS graph. The exponential law holds for VGS a few tenths of a volt below Vth. Above Vth the square law of Chapter 1.3 takes over. In the narrow moderate-inversion band around Vth neither formula is exact, so the real curve bends smoothly from exponential to square law instead of meeting at a point. A lower Vth shifts the line left and raises current at every gate voltage below threshold.

Subthreshold swing and off current

The inverse of that line's slope, expressed as a gate voltage, is the subthreshold swing S. It is the gate voltage needed to change ID by one decade, a factor of 10:

S = 2.3 n V<sub>T</sub> = 2.3 V<sub>T</sub>(1 + C<sub>d</sub>/C<sub>ox</sub>)

S is in mV/decade; 2.3 = ln 10; the other symbols are as above. Typical values are 70 to 100 mV/decade. The best possible bulk MOSFET at 300 K has n = 1 and S = 2.3 × 26 mV ≈ 60 mV/decade. Smaller S means a sharper switch.

Off current Ioff is quoted at VGS = 0 and VDS = VDD, normally in nA/µm of transistor width. VDD is the supply voltage. A wider transistor leaks proportionally more, so a per-µm number compares processes fairly. Shorter L shifts Vth through short-channel effects (Chapter 1.6) and narrower W shifts it through the narrow-width effect; both change Ioff. Temperature matters: Ioff roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise. A 25 °C value can be 64 times larger at 85 °C.

Other leakage paths

Subthreshold conduction is not the only leak. Direct gate-oxide tunnelling flows from gate to channel when the oxide is thin. It becomes serious below about 2 nm of silicon dioxide, because electrons tunnel through the barrier. High-k dielectrics reduce this for a given capacitance.

Gate-induced drain leakage (GIDL) appears at low VGS and high VDS. The gate overlaps the drain; the field there bends the drain band so much that electrons tunnel from valence to conduction band. Tunnelled electrons are collected by the drain and the holes go to the body, so the conventional GIDL current flows from the drain to the body. Either way, the drain terminal carries this extra current even when the channel is off.

A reverse-biased source-body or drain-body junction also leaks. Thermal generation in the depletion region adds a small current. In modern processes these paths together can equal or exceed subthreshold current. Each has a different bias dependence, so one Ioff number hides which path dominates. See Unit 3 for the power budget and Unit 6 for leakage test structures.

Worked example

Question. A process has subthreshold swing S = 80 mV/decade and Ioff = 10 nA/µm at Vth = 0.3 V. A designer raises Vth by 100 mV. (a) Find the leakage reduction factor. (b) Find the new Ioff. (c) A chip has 10 mm of total transistor width. Find its new total leakage and compare with the original. (d) Find n. Take VT = 26 mV.

  1. 1. S = 80 mV/decade means every 80 mV rise in Vth cuts Ioff by 10×. For ΔVth = 100 mV, factor = 10100/80 = 101.25 = 17.78.
  2. 2. New Ioff = 10 nA/µm ÷ 17.78 = 0.562 nA/µm ≈ 0.56 nA/µm.
  3. 3. Total width W = 10 mm = 10 000 µm. New total = 0.562 nA/µm × 10 000 µm = 5 623 nA = 5.62 µA. Original = 10 nA/µm × 10 000 µm = 100 000 nA = 100 µA.
  4. 4. From S = 2.3 n VT, n = S/(2.3VT) = 0.080 V/(2.3×0.026 V) = 1.34.

Answer. The factor is 17.78, Ioff falls to 0.56 nA/µm, the chip leaks 5.62 µA instead of 100 µA, and n = 1.34.

Exam traps

  • Using VT for both threshold and thermal voltage. The exponent uses VT = kT/q ≈ 26 mV; the threshold is Vth (or VT0).
  • Claiming a bulk MOSFET can have S below 60 mV/decade at room temperature. S = 2.3nVT with n ≥ 1 sets a floor near 60 mV/decade.
  • Treating Ioff as a fixed current. It is quoted per µm of width, so total leakage changes with total W and with L through Vth.
  • Saying the transistor carries no current at VGS = 0. Ioff is defined at exactly that bias, with VDS = VDD.
  • Forgetting the direction of temperature dependence. Leakage rises with temperature, roughly doubling every 10 °C, not falling.

1.8 FinFET technologies

A planar gate controls the channel from above only. Once the channel is short, that grip is too weak, and the usual fixes — thinner oxide, heavier doping — raise leakage faster than they help. A FinFET lifts the channel into a thin vertical silicon fin and wraps the gate over the top and both sides. Control returns without an impossibly thin oxide. FinFETs became the standard transistor of advanced logic from the 22 nm node.

You need: 1.6 for short-channel effects and 1.7 for the leakage they cause.

Why one-sided control fails

In a planar MOSFET the gate sits on top of a flat channel and controls it from one side. The drain also pulls on the channel, and its share grows as L falls (see 1.6). The standard fixes make things worse. A thinner oxide leaks by tunnelling. Heavier body doping raises junction leakage and junction capacitance and cuts mobility. It does shrink the depletion widths, so it helps contain the drain field, but it stops working once the body must be thin.

The real fix is a thinner body. If the silicon under the gate is thinner than the depletion width it would otherwise need, it is fully depleted even at zero gate bias. Almost no mobile charge is left for the drain to steal, and the gate field reaches the whole body. Control comes from geometry instead of a thinner oxide (1.7).

The fin and its effective width

The fin is a narrow ridge of silicon etched from the wafer. Its width is Wfin (nm) and its height is Hfin (nm). Gate oxide and gate metal cover the top and both sides — a tri-gate. A double-gate version covers only the two sides.

Weff = 2Hfin + Wfin

Weff is the effective channel width of one fin in nm; Hfin is the fin height in nm, counted twice for the two sidewalls; Wfin is the fin width in nm, the top surface. A pure double gate gives Weff = 2Hfin.

Because the body is thin, the channel is undoped or lightly doped. Threshold voltage is set by the gate work function — the energy needed to pull an electron out of the gate metal, in eV — and by Wfin, not by body doping. There is little body effect and less impurity scattering. Trade-offs: Wfin must stay well below the gate length to remain fully depleted, Hfin sets current per fin, and tall thin fins are hard to pattern and can bend.

Width quantisation and gate-all-around

A planar designer draws any W. A FinFET designer chooses a whole number of fins in parallel, so drive strength snaps to fin counts and cells are drawn on a fin pitch — the centre-to-centre distance between neighbouring fins, in nm. Threshold is tuned by work function, drive by fin count. Gate-all-around surrounds a stacked horizontal nanosheet on all four sides, so a wider body gives the same control and more current per unit area.

Worked example

Question. A FinFET has Wfin = 10 nm and Hfin = 30 nm. (a) Find Weff per fin. (b) How many fins give 700 nm of drive width? (c) At 1 mA per µm, find the current per fin and the cell total.

  1. Weff = 2 × 30 nm + 10 nm = 70 nm.
  2. N = 700 nm ÷ 70 nm = 10 fins, exact.
  3. 70 nm = 0.07 µm, so current per fin = 0.07 × 1 mA/µm = 70 µA.
  4. Cell total = 10 × 70 µA = 0.7 mA.

Answer. Weff = 70 nm, 10 fins, 70 µA per fin, 0.7 mA total.

Exam traps

  • Using Wfin alone as the effective width. With Wfin = 10 nm and Hfin = 30 nm that under-counts drive seven times.
  • Forgetting width quantisation. A request for 720 nm needs 11 fins (770 nm), not 10.3 fins.
  • Saying the FinFET removes short-channel effects. It reduces them; drain-induced barrier lowering still exists.
  • Mixing formulas: a double-gate fin gives Weff = 2Hfin, a tri-gate fin gives 2Hfin + Wfin.

Takeaways

  1. A MOS capacitor becomes a transistor once the gate voltage inverts the surface and the source and drain contact that inversion layer.
  2. Accumulation, depletion and inversion are three states of surface charge; cutoff, linear and saturation are three shapes of the inversion channel.
  3. Threshold voltage is a voltage budget: flat-band, band bending and depletion charge are paid first, and only what remains becomes inversion charge.
  4. The square-law equations are a long-channel, low-field model: current depends on overdrive voltage, W/L and Cox, and turns quadratic in saturation.
  5. Velocity saturation, mobility degradation and channel length modulation are the three corrections that turn the square law into a usable real-device model.
  6. Every terminal pair of a MOSFET is a capacitor; the driver charges and discharges these capacitances, and series resistances slow the charging.
  7. Below threshold the device still conducts exponentially, and that leakage plus gate and junction leakage is now a large share of chip power.
  8. When scaling made planar gates lose control of the channel, the FinFET recovered control by wrapping the gate around a thin fin.

Glossary

Accumulation
The surface condition where the gate attracts majority carriers, making the surface richer in the bulk carrier type than the bulk itself.
Aspect ratio
The ratio W/L of channel width to channel length, which multiplies the current a transistor can carry.
Body bias
The voltage applied between body and source, used to raise or lower threshold voltage through the body effect.
Body effect
The increase in threshold voltage caused by a reverse bias between source and body, which adds depletion charge under the gate.
Channel length modulation
The shortening of the effective channel as VDS grows, which raises the saturation current slightly instead of leaving it flat.
Depletion region
The layer under the gate that holds no free carriers, only fixed ionised dopant atoms, when the gate pushes majority carriers away.
DIBL
Drain-induced barrier lowering: the fall of threshold voltage with rising drain voltage in a short-channel device.
FinFET
A transistor built on a thin silicon fin with a gate wrapped around two or three of its sides, giving better electrostatic control.
Flat-band voltage
The gate voltage at which the semiconductor bands are flat, equal to the work-function difference minus the oxide charge effect.
GIDL
Gate-induced drain leakage: band-to-band tunnelling current in the drain overlap region when the gate is low and the drain is high.
Hot carrier
A carrier that gains high energy from the lateral field and can be injected into the oxide, damaging the device over time.
Inversion layer
A thin surface layer of minority carriers, created by a strong gate voltage, that connects source and drain and carries the transistor current.
LDD
Lightly doped drain: a lightly doped extension between channel and drain that spreads the field and reduces hot-carrier damage.
Mobility degradation
The fall of carrier mobility with the vertical gate field, caused by increased surface scattering.
Output resistance
The small-signal resistance looking into the drain, r0 = 1/(λID), created by channel length modulation.
Overdrive voltage
The gate voltage above threshold, VGS − VTH, that determines how much inversion charge the channel holds.
Oxide capacitance
Cox = εox/tox, the capacitance per unit area of the gate oxide that couples the gate to the channel.
Pinch-off
The drain-end condition where VDS equals the overdrive and the inversion charge density becomes zero.
Pinch-off region
Another name for the saturation region, where the channel is pinched off near the drain and the current is almost flat.
Subthreshold conduction
The exponential drain current that flows even when VGS is below threshold, because carriers still surmount the source barrier.
Subthreshold swing
The change in gate voltage needed to change subthreshold current by one decade, quoted in millivolts per decade.
Threshold voltage
The gate-to-source voltage at which strong inversion starts and a conducting channel just forms.
Transconductance
The small-signal change in drain current for a change in gate voltage, gm = ∂ID/∂VGS, set by μnCox(W/L)Vov in saturation.
Triode region
The same as the linear region: the channel is continuous and the transistor acts as a resistor set by the gate voltage.
Velocity saturation
The limiting drift velocity of carriers at high lateral field, which makes drain current grow only linearly with overdrive.
