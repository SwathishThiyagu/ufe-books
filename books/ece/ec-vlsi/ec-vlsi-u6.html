6.1 Need for testing and manufacturing test principles

Every die that leaves the fab looks the same from outside, yet some work and some do not. Test applies known inputs to each chip, reads its outputs and compares them with what a good chip gives, so bad parts are thrown out before a customer pays for them. Test costs a machine, a program and seconds per chip, so it must be planned. This chapter follows a chip from wafer to customer, names the steps where it is tested, and works out how yield and fault coverage decide how many bad chips still escape.

You need: the picture of a processed wafer full of identical die, from CMOS fabrication.

From wafer to chip, and where test sits

A chip is made in two halves. The front end builds transistors and wires on a silicon wafer: oxidation, lithography, etching, ion implantation and deposition, repeated layer by layer, then the metal interconnect. The back end turns tested die into parts.

  1. Wafer fabrication: hundreds of identical die are built on one wafer.
  2. Wafer probe (wafer sort): a probe card touches the bond pads of each die; failing die are marked on a wafer map.
  3. Dicing and assembly: the wafer is sawn, good die are attached to a package and wire-bonded or flip-chip bonded, then sealed.
  4. Burn-in (for parts that need it): hours of powered running at high temperature and voltage.
  5. Final test: the packaged part runs the full test program, often at hot and cold temperature, and is sorted into bins.
  6. Board and system test: after the part is soldered into a product (see 6.8).

Test is planned long before the wafer exists: in the design flow, test hardware (scan, BIST, boundary scan) is added after synthesis. Verification asks whether the design is right; test asks whether this copy was built right. A design can pass every simulation and still fail on the tester, because the fab made what the netlist never described: a short, an open, a missing contact. On a complex SoC both jobs are hard: too many states to simulate, too few pins to reach the inside, and cores that arrive with their own tests.

The test floor

ATE, automatic test equipment, is the tester: pin electronics that drive and sense every chip pin, a precise timing generator, programmable power supplies and a computer that runs the test program.

  • DUT: the device under test, the chip being measured now.
  • Probe card: fine needles that land on the bond pads, so die are tested before packaging.
  • Device interface board (DIB): routes the tester channels to the socket or the probe card.
  • Handler: picks packaged chips from a tray, presses each into a socket at a set temperature, and drops it into a pass or fail bin.

A tester has more channels than one chip needs, so one program can drive s die at once (multi-site test). The cost charged to each die is

cost per die = (R × t) ÷ s

where R is the running cost of the tester in rupees per second (machine, power, floor space, operator), t is the test time of one die in seconds and s is the number of sites tested in parallel.

Outside the formula sit the one-time cost of writing the test program and overkill, the loss when a good die fails a marginal measurement.

Yield, fault coverage and defect level

Yield Y is the fraction of die that are free of killer defects and meet the specification. It falls as die area grows, because a random defect anywhere on a larger die kills it. A simple model with defects scattered at random is Y = e−A·D, where A is the die area and D the number of killer defects per unit area.

Fault coverage T is the fraction of the modelled faults that the test vectors detect. The faults come from a fault model (6.2) and T is measured by fault simulation (6.3). No real test program reaches T = 1.

DL = 1 − Y(1 − T)

where DL is the defect level, the fraction of shipped parts that are bad; Y is the yield and T the fault coverage, both fractions between 0 and 1.

DPM = DL × 10⁶

where DPM is defective parts per million shipped.

The formula (the Williams–Brown model) assumes defects are independent and that the test catches a fraction T of them, so (1 − T) escapes. Real defects cluster, so DL is an estimate, but as T rises towards 1 it falls steeply.

Quality, reliability and the four test stages

Quality describes parts at shipping: how many are bad, in DPM. Reliability describes parts after shipping: how long they keep working, in FIT, failures per 10⁹ device-hours.

The failure rate follows the bathtub curve: high and falling in the first weeks (infant mortality: weak oxide, bad vias), low in useful life, rising at wear-out (electromigration, oxide breakdown). Burn-in makes the infant failures happen in the factory.

A chip meets four kinds of test: silicon debug of first silicon on a bench; characterisation across voltage and temperature corners for the datasheet; production test of every chip on the ATE; and the customer's incoming inspection of a sample.

Worked example

Question. A wafer lot has yield Y = 0.90. The production test program has fault coverage T = 0.95. (a) Find the defect level in DPM. (b) Repeat for a program with T = 0.99. (c) By what factor does the number of shipped bad parts fall?

  1. Formula: DL = 1 − Y(1 − T). For (a), Y = 0.90 and 1 − T = 0.05.
  2. Take logs to evaluate the power: ln 0.90 = −0.10536, and 0.05 × (−0.10536) = −0.005268.
  3. Y0.05 = e−0.005268 = 0.994746, so DL = 1 − 0.994746 = 0.005254 and DPM = 0.005254 × 10⁶ = 5254.
  4. For (b), 1 − T = 0.01: 0.01 × (−0.10536) = −0.0010536, e−0.0010536 = 0.998947, DL = 0.001053, DPM = 1053.
  5. For (c), 5254 ÷ 1053 = 4.99.
  6. Check: with T = 1 the exponent is 0, Y⁰ = 1 and DL = 0, so the numbers fall the right way as T rises.

Answer. About 5254 DPM at T = 0.95 and about 1053 DPM at T = 0.99, a fall by a factor of about 5 for 4 more points of coverage.

Exam traps

  • Assuming 100 % fault coverage means zero escapes. T counts only the faults the model lists; a defect the model does not describe can still escape.
  • Swapping yield and fault coverage. Yield is a property of the fab (good die out of all die); fault coverage is a property of the test program (faults detected out of faults listed).
  • Mixing up the chain: a defect is physical, a fault is its logical model, an error is a wrong node value, a failure is lost function.
  • A unit slip in DPM: DL = 0.005 is 5000 DPM. Multiply by 10⁶, not 10⁴.
  • Saying verification proves the chip works. Simulation checks the design; only the tester checks each manufactured copy.

6.2 Fault models: stuck-at and delay faults

A physical defect can be a short, an open or a missing implant, in endless shapes and sizes, so no test program can target defects one by one. A fault model replaces them with a short logical list: a line stuck at 0 or 1, two lines bridged, a transistor that never conducts, a path that is too slow. The single stuck-at model is the workhorse of digital test; delay faults catch chips that compute the right value too late. Fault simulation and ATPG in 6.3 work on this list.

You need: logic gates and truth tables, and gate-level modelling from the Verilog chapters.

From physical defect to logical fault

A defect is physical: a metal short, an open contact, a missing implant, a pinhole in thin oxide. A fault is the logical model of that defect, for example 'line A is stuck at 0'. An error is a wrong value on some line inside the circuit, and a failure is lost function: a wrong value reaches an output pin and the chip misses its specification. A short from one input of a NAND gate to ground behaves like that input stuck at 0.

A defect has a size, a resistance and a place, and its effect changes with all three. A fault model fixes the effect, not the cause. Because the list is finite, a program can enumerate it, generate vectors for it and report fault coverage.

Stuck-at faults, equivalence and collapsing

A line is a primary input, a primary output, a gate input or a gate output; each branch of a fanout is a separate line. A line stuck-at-0 (SA0, written A/0) always reads 0; stuck-at-1 (SA1, A/1) always reads 1. Single stuck-at means only one line is faulty at a time.

N = 2 × L

where N is the number of single stuck-at faults and L the number of lines; the 2 counts SA0 and SA1.

Two faults are equivalent if no input vector can tell their faulty circuits apart. On a two-input NAND, A/0, B/0 and Z/1 all force the output to 1 for every input, so they form one class. A/1 and B/1 are not equivalent: A/1 shows only when B = 1, and B/1 only when A = 1. Equivalence collapsing keeps one fault per class; a k-input NAND, NOR, AND or OR gate goes from 2(k + 1) faults to k + 2.

Fault f dominates fault g if every test for g also detects f. Then f can be dropped too, because the test for g catches it. On the NAND, the only test for A/1 is 01, and 01 also detects Z/0, so Z/0 dominates A/1 and is dropped.

For a whole circuit the checkpoint theorem says a test set that detects every stuck-at fault on the primary inputs and on the fanout branches detects every single stuck-at fault in the circuit. So the fault list can start from the checkpoints only. A fault that no vector can detect is called redundant; only ATPG can prove it, and coverage reports count it separately (6.3).

Transistor, bridging and delay faults

A transistor stuck-open never conducts; a transistor stuck-short always conducts. In static CMOS a stuck-open pMOS cuts the output off from VDD, and a stuck-open nMOS cuts it off from ground, for some input combinations. The output then floats and keeps its old value on its capacitance, so the gate behaves like a memory. A test needs two vectors: the first sets the output to a known value (say 0), the second asks the broken transistor to drive it to 1. If the output stays 0, the fault is caught. A stuck-short makes pull-up and pull-down fight; it is best caught by the large quiescent supply current it causes (IDDQ test).

A bridging fault shorts two lines. When one driver pulls 0 and the other pulls 1, the result depends on drive strength; simple models are wired-AND (0 wins) and wired-OR (1 wins). A resistive bridge may give the right logic value but slowly.

A transition delay fault makes one line slow-to-rise or slow-to-fall. A path delay fault makes a whole input-to-output path too slow, even when each gate on it is only a little slow. Both need a two-vector test applied at the rated clock: the first vector V1 sets the line to its starting value, the launch edge applies V2 and creates the transition, and the capture edge one clock period later stores the result. A slow path still holds the old value at capture.

Worked example

Question. A two-input NAND gate has inputs A, B and output Z. (a) List every single stuck-at fault. (b) Collapse the faults by equivalence, then by dominance. (c) Give the smallest test set that detects all six faults. Write each vector as AB.

  1. Lines: A, B and Z, so N = 2 × 3 = 6 faults: A/0, A/1, B/0, B/1, Z/0, Z/1.
  2. Good outputs for AB = 00, 01, 10, 11 are 1, 1, 1, 0.
  3. A/0 makes Z = 1 for every input; it differs from the good gate only at 11. B/0 and Z/1 give the same faulty table. Class {A/0, B/0, Z/1}, detected only by 11.
  4. A/1 gives Z = NOT B: it differs only at 01 (good 1, faulty 0). B/1 differs only at 10. Z/0 differs at 00, 01 and 10. So there are 4 equivalence classes: {A/0, B/0, Z/1}, {A/1}, {B/1}, {Z/0}.
  5. Dominance: the test for A/1 (01) also detects Z/0, so Z/0 dominates A/1 and is dropped. The collapsed list is A/0, A/1, B/1: 3 faults, which is k + 1 for k = 2 after dominance.
  6. Each of the 3 faults has exactly one test (11, 01 and 10), so no smaller set exists. Check that {01, 10, 11} also catches Z/0: at 01 the good Z is 1 and the faulty Z is 0, so yes.

Answer. Six faults collapse to four equivalence classes and to three faults after dominance; the smallest complete test set is {01, 10, 11}, three vectors, and 00 is not needed.

Worked example 2

Question. Line A of a two-input AND gate Z = A·B is slow-to-rise. The path from A to the capturing flip-flop normally takes 1.2 ns, and the defect adds 1.0 ns. The clock period is 2.0 ns. Give the two-vector test, and say what the flip-flop captures at speed and on a slow tester with a 10 ns period.

  1. To see a rising A, V1 must hold A = 0 and V2 must have A = 1. B must be 1 in V2 so that Z follows A. So V1 = (A, B) = (0, 1) and V2 = (1, 1).
  2. Good circuit: Z goes 0 → 1 and settles 1.2 ns after the launch edge, before the capture edge at 2.0 ns. Captured Z = 1.
  3. Faulty circuit: Z settles after 1.2 + 1.0 = 2.2 ns, which is 0.2 ns after the capture edge. Captured Z = 0, the old value, so the fault is detected.
  4. Slow tester: capture comes 10 ns after launch; even the faulty Z has settled by 2.2 ns, so it captures 1 and the chip wrongly passes.

Answer. The test is V1 = 01, V2 = 11 at the rated 2.0 ns clock; the faulty chip captures 0 instead of 1. At a 10 ns period the same vectors miss the fault, which is why delay tests run at speed.

Exam traps

  • On a two-input NAND, Z/1 is equivalent to A/0 and B/0; it is not a class of its own.
  • A/1 and B/1 are not equivalent on a NAND: each shows only when the other input is 1.
  • Counting lines without the fanout branches. Each branch is a line with its own two faults.
  • A stuck-open can pass a single vector, because the floating output keeps a value that may happen to be right. It needs a two-vector sequence.
  • Applying a delay test slowly. Launch and capture must be one rated clock period apart, or a slow path looks good.

6.3 Fault simulation and test pattern generation

Fault simulation asks how many listed faults a set of vectors catches. ATPG, automatic test pattern generation, asks the reverse: for one fault not yet caught, which input vector shows it at an output? The two programs work in a loop: fault simulation measures coverage, ATPG adds vectors for the faults still missed, and the loop stops at the coverage target. The compact vector set that comes out is applied to every chip that is made.

You need: the stuck-at fault list and collapsing from 6.2, and gate-level simulation from the Verilog chapters.

Fault simulation measures detection

Fault simulation applies each vector to the good circuit and to every faulty circuit in the collapsed fault list. A fault is detected when at least one primary output differs between the good and the faulty circuit. A detected fault is dropped from the list, so later vectors simulate fewer faulty circuits (fault dropping).

A large chip has millions of faults, so speed matters. Serial simulation runs one faulty circuit at a time. Parallel simulation packs circuits into the bits of a machine word: a 32-bit word carries the good circuit in one bit and 31 faulty circuits in the others, and one bitwise AND evaluates a gate for all of them. Deductive simulation carries, on each line, the list of faults that would flip it. Concurrent simulation re-evaluates a faulty circuit only where it differs from the good one.

FC = (detected ÷ total faults) × 100 %

where FC is the fault coverage, 'detected' is the number of faults the vectors detect and 'total faults' is the size of the collapsed fault list.

Tools also report fault efficiency (some call it ATPG efficiency or test coverage): detected faults divided by the faults left after removing those ATPG proved redundant. It shows how well ATPG did on the faults that can be tested; fault coverage shows how well the chip is tested.

ATPG methods, from exhaustive to algorithmic

Exhaustive test applies all 2ⁿ vectors of an n-input circuit: complete, but impossible beyond small n. Pseudo-exhaustive test splits the circuit into small cones and exhausts each. Random test applies pseudo-random vectors and keeps those that detect new faults; weighted random biases the 0/1 probability of each input. Algorithmic ATPG works backwards from one target fault.

In the D-algorithm, D means 1 in the good circuit and 0 in the faulty circuit; D̄ means 0 in the good and 1 in the faulty circuit. The D-frontier is the set of gates with D or D̄ on an input and an output still unknown; the J-frontier is the set of gates whose output value is set but whose inputs are not yet justified.

  1. Activate the fault: give the faulty line the opposite of its stuck value, creating D or D̄.
  2. Propagate: pick a gate from the D-frontier and set its other inputs to non-controlling values (1 for AND/NAND, 0 for OR/NOR) so the difference passes through. Repeat until D or D̄ reaches a primary output.
  3. Justify: work back from every value set so far to primary inputs, emptying the J-frontier.
  4. Backtrack on a conflict: undo the last choice and try another. If every choice fails, the fault is redundant.

PODEM assigns values only at primary inputs, chosen by tracing back from the current objective, so its search space is the 2ⁿ input combinations rather than all internal lines and it usually backtracks less. FAN adds smarter backtracing through fanout-free regions and several objectives at once.

Redundant faults, compaction and the ATPG loop

A fault with no test is undetectable. In combinational logic an undetectable stuck-at fault is called redundant, because the line could be tied to that constant without changing the function. It comes from redundant logic, such as a consensus term added to remove a hazard, or from reconvergent fanout whose two paths cancel the difference. ATPG proves redundancy by exhausting the search; the designer may then remove the redundant logic, which also removes the fault.

Test compaction removes vectors that detect nothing new and merges vectors whose specified bits do not clash. Vector ordering puts high-yield vectors first so a bad chip fails, and leaves the tester, early. Neither changes the coverage of the final set.

The loop in the figure is iterative: simulate, check coverage, run ATPG on the undetected faults, add the new vectors, simulate again. It stops at the coverage target or when only redundant and aborted faults remain.

Worked example

Question. The circuit is Z = A·(B + C), built as G1 = NAND(A, B), G2 = NAND(A, C), Z = NAND(G1, G2); input A fans out to both G1 and G2. Use the D-algorithm to find a test for A stuck-at-0. Show activation, the D-frontier at each step, propagation and the final vector, and give the good and faulty values of Z.

  1. Activate: A/0 needs A = 1 in the good circuit; the faulty circuit has A = 0. A carries D, and so do both fanout branches.
  2. D-frontier = {G1, G2}: both gates have D on an input and an unknown output.
  3. Propagate through G1: set B = 1 (non-controlling for NAND). Good G1 = NAND(1, 1) = 0, faulty G1 = NAND(0, 1) = 1, so G1 = D̄.
  4. Set C = 0. Then G2 = 1 in both circuits, because a 0 input forces a NAND to 1. G2 leaves the D-frontier and becomes a steady 1 into the last gate.
  5. D-frontier = {Z gate}. Z = NAND(D̄, 1): good NAND(0, 1) = 1, faulty NAND(1, 1) = 0, so Z = D and the difference has reached the output.
  6. Justify: B = 1 and C = 0 are primary inputs, so the J-frontier is empty and no backtrack is needed. Vector ABC = 110.

Answer. ABC = 110 detects A stuck-at-0: Z = 1 in the good circuit and Z = 0 in the faulty circuit.

Exam traps

  • Reading D as 'fault present here'. D means good 1 and faulty 0; D̄ means good 0 and faulty 1.
  • Calling a vector a test because it activates the fault. It is a test only if a primary output differs.
  • Using the wrong denominator. Fault coverage divides by all faults in the list; fault efficiency leaves out the faults proved redundant.
  • Confusing the D-frontier (where the difference can move forward) with the J-frontier (values still to be justified backwards).
  • Thinking compaction or ordering raises coverage. They cut test time and tester memory; coverage stays the same.

6.4 Design for testability and scan design

Scan design makes a sequential chip testable by turning its flip-flops into one or more long shift registers during test. In normal mode each flip-flop takes its functional input. In scan mode the same flip-flops are chained, so the tester can shift any pattern into every state bit, run one functional clock, and shift the captured result out. The hard sequential problem becomes an easy combinational one. Full scan is standard in digital ASICs and SoCs; some syllabi call it scan-path design or scan-based DFT.

You need: the D flip-flop and the 2:1 multiplexer, from the chapter on latches and registers, and combinational ATPG from 6.3.

Why sequential circuits are hard, and ad hoc DFT

A combinational circuit is easy to test: apply a vector at the pins, wait, compare the outputs. A sequential circuit hides its state. To set one internal flip-flop to 1, the tester may need a long sequence of clocked input vectors, and the effect of a fault must then travel through more state to reach a pin. Controllability is how easily a node can be set to 0 or 1 from the primary inputs; observability is how easily its value can be seen at a primary output. Both get worse as the state grows, and ATPG time explodes.

Design for testability (DFT) means adding hardware or following rules so the chip is cheap to test. Ad hoc DFT is a set of good habits, applied case by case:

  • add test points: a control point (a gate or multiplexer that forces a node) and an observation point (a node brought to a pin or a register);
  • partition a large block so each part can be tested on its own;
  • give every flip-flop a reset, so the state starts known;
  • avoid asynchronous logic and combinational feedback loops, or break them in test mode;
  • split long counters, and let the tester bypass on-chip oscillators and clock dividers.

These help, but they give no systematic method. Structured DFT, scan and BIST, changes the design so every state bit can be loaded and read.

Muxed-D scan cell, full scan and partial scan

The standard muxed-D scan cell puts a 2:1 multiplexer in front of a D flip-flop. Its inputs are the functional data D and the scan input SI; the select is scan enable SE. With SE = 0 the cell is a normal flip-flop; with SE = 1 it takes SI. Joining Q of one cell to SI of the next makes a shift register, the scan chain, from the chip's scan-in pin to its scan-out pin.

Full scan makes every flip-flop a scan cell. ATPG then treats each flip-flop output as a pseudo-primary input and each flip-flop input as a pseudo-primary output, and sees only the combinational logic between them. Partial scan converts only some flip-flops, usually those that break feedback loops; it saves area and delay but leaves a small sequential circuit for ATPG.

The costs: a multiplexer and extra wiring per flip-flop (a few per cent of logic area), one multiplexer delay on every functional D path, an SE net that reaches every cell, and two or more extra pins. During shift, if clock skew between two neighbouring cells is large, the later cell can catch the new value its neighbour has just launched instead of the old one, a hold-time race; a lock-up latch between cells on different clocks prevents it. LSSD, level-sensitive scan design, reaches the same goal with latches and two non-overlapping clocks instead of a multiplexer.

The scan test procedure and its cost

  1. Shift in: SE = 1; the pattern is clocked into the chain one bit per clock. The bit meant for the last cell goes in first.
  2. Capture: SE = 0; one functional clock loads the combinational logic's response into the flip-flops. Primary outputs are compared at the same time.
  3. Shift out: SE = 1; the captured bits leave through scan-out and the tester compares each with its expected value. The next pattern shifts in during the same clocks.

Because shift-out of one pattern overlaps shift-in of the next, P patterns on a chain of N cells take

cycles = (N + 1) × P + N

where N is the length of the longest chain, P the number of patterns, the 1 is the capture clock, and the last N is the final shift-out. Test time = cycles × scan clock period.

Splitting the flip-flops into K balanced chains cuts N to about N ÷ K, so test time falls almost K times, but each chain needs its own scan-in and scan-out pin. Scan makes ATPG easy and can reduce the number of patterns, yet every pattern still costs N shift clocks.

Worked example

Question. A chip has three scan cells chained SI → FF1 → FF2 → FF3 → SO. The logic feeding them is D1 = Q2·Q3, D2 = Q1 ⊕ Q3 and D3 = NOT Q1. Load the pattern Q1Q2Q3 = 011 to test the AND output D1 stuck-at-0, capture, and shift out. Give the bits on SO for the good and the faulty chip.

  4. Count: 3 shift-in + 1 capture + 3 shift-out = 7 clocks = (N + 1)P + N with N = 3, P = 1.

  • Saying scan has no delay cost. The multiplexer sits on every functional D path.
  • Calling LSSD a muxed-D design. LSSD uses latches with two non-overlapping clocks and no multiplexer.

6.5 Boundary scan (JTAG)

Boundary scan puts a small test cell at every digital signal pin of a chip and a serial controller inside it, so a whole board of chips can be tested through four wires: TCK, TMS, TDI and TDO. The standard is IEEE 1149.1, usually called JTAG. It checks the wiring between chips after soldering, not the logic inside them, and the same port is used to program flash and FPGAs on the board and to reach a chip's internal test hardware.

You need: shift registers and scan cells from 6.4, and the board-test step from 6.1.

The test access port and the boundary cells

JTAG adds a test access port (TAP) to the chip. Four pins are required: TCK, the test clock; TMS, test mode select, which steers the TAP controller; TDI, serial test data in; and TDO, serial test data out. TMS and TDI are sampled on the rising edge of TCK, and TDO changes on the falling edge, so the next device in a chain samples a steady bit. A fifth pin, TRST, an asynchronous reset, is optional.

A boundary-scan cell sits between the core logic and each digital signal pad (power, ground and analog pins have none). In normal mode the cell is transparent. In test mode an output cell can drive its pad from a stored bit, and an input cell can capture the value arriving at its pad. All the cells join into one shift register, the boundary-scan register (BSR).

Why at the pin? Inside the chip, scan and BIST do the testing. The copper trace between two chips on a board has no access point once fine-pitch and ball-grid-array parts are soldered, and a bed-of-nails fixture cannot reach it. Boundary scan moves the probe to the pin: the tester shifts a pattern into the output cells, the chips drive the traces, and the input cells capture what arrives. A wrong bit shows an open trace, a short to a neighbour or a missing part.

TAP controller, registers and instructions

The TAP controller is a 16-state machine that moves on each rising edge of TCK according to TMS. Besides Test-Logic-Reset and Run-Test/Idle it has two matching columns of seven states, one for a data register (DR) and one for the instruction register (IR): Select, Capture, Shift, Exit1, Pause, Exit2 and Update. Capture loads the register in parallel, Shift moves it one bit per TCK from TDI to TDO, and Update copies the shifted bits to the outputs. Holding TMS = 1 for five TCK edges reaches Test-Logic-Reset from any state.

Every compliant chip has the instruction register (IR, at least 2 bits), the 1-bit bypass register and the BSR; most also have a 32-bit IDCODE register that returns the maker and part number. The instruction in the IR decides which data register sits between TDI and TDO.

Daisy chain on a board

On a board the chips form a daisy chain: the board's TDI goes to the first chip's TDI, each TDO feeds the next TDI, and the last TDO is the board's TDO. TCK and TMS go to every chip in parallel, so all TAP controllers step through the same states together and the board behaves as one long shift register.

L = L₁ + L₂ + … + Lₙ

where L is the number of bits shifted in one pass of Shift-IR or Shift-DR and Lᵢ is the length of the register selected in chip i. The bits for the chip nearest TDO are shifted in first.

In an interconnect test the tester loads EXTEST into every chip, preloads drive values, then for each pattern captures the received bits and shifts them out while the next pattern shifts in. A short changes the captured bit of both nets; an open leaves the receiving pin floating, so its bit is unreliable. In-system programming uses the same port: the FPGA or flash is put in programming mode and its configuration is shifted through TDI and TDO.

Worked example

Question. A board carries three JTAG chips in a daisy chain. Their instruction registers are 4, 4 and 5 bits; their boundary-scan registers are 20, 20 and 30 cells. Starting in Test-Logic-Reset, the tester loads EXTEST into all three chips and then shifts one complete boundary-scan pattern. (a) How many bits pass in Shift-IR and in Shift-DR? (b) Write the TMS sequence. (c) How many TCK cycles does the whole job take?

  1. (a) Shift-IR: 4 + 4 + 5 = 13 bits. Shift-DR with EXTEST selects the BSRs: 20 + 20 + 30 = 70 bits. (With BYPASS in all three it would be 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 bits.)
  2. (b) To reach Shift-IR: TMS = 0 (Run-Test/Idle), 1 (Select-DR), 1 (Select-IR), 0 (Capture-IR), 0 (Shift-IR): 5 edges.
  3. Shift 13 instruction bits: TMS = 0 for 12 edges and 1 on the 13th, which shifts the last bit and moves to Exit1-IR. Then TMS = 1 (Update-IR): EXTEST becomes active.
  4. From Update-IR: TMS = 1 (Select-DR), 0 (Capture-DR, the input cells capture the pins), 0 (Shift-DR): 3 edges.
  5. Shift 70 data bits: TMS = 0 for 69 edges and 1 on the 70th (to Exit1-DR). Then TMS = 1 (Update-DR, the output cells drive the new pattern) and 0 (Run-Test/Idle).
  6. (c) Count: 5 + 13 + 1 + 3 + 70 + 2 = 94 TCK cycles.

Answer. 13 instruction bits and 70 data bits; TMS = 0 1 1 0 0, then twelve 0s and a 1, then 1 1 0 0, then sixty-nine 0s and a 1, then 1 0; 94 TCK cycles in all, or 9.4 µs at a 10 MHz TCK.

Exam traps

  • Thinking boundary scan tests the logic inside the chip. It tests pins and board wiring; the core needs scan or BIST.
  • Using one chip's IR length for the board. In a daisy chain the instruction lengths add, and so do the selected data-register lengths.
  • Forgetting that the last shift happens on the edge that leaves Shift-DR: n bits need n edges in the Shift state, the last with TMS = 1.
  • Saying SAMPLE/PRELOAD drives the board. It only captures pin values and preloads the cells; EXTEST drives.
  • Treating TMS as a data pin. TDI and TDO carry data, TMS steers the state machine, TCK clocks both.

6.6 Built-in self-test (BIST)

An external tester drives every input and reads every output, and it costs money for every second it runs. Built-in self-test (BIST) moves the pattern generator and the response checker onto the chip. An on-chip LFSR makes pseudo-random vectors, a MISR squeezes the responses into a short signature, and one comparison with a stored good signature gives a single pass or fail bit. The chip can then test itself at power-on, after packaging or in the field, with almost no tester.

You need: shift registers and XOR gates, and scan chains from 6.4.

The BIST loop and its parts

BIST wraps three blocks around the circuit under test (CUT). The test pattern generator (TPG) makes the input vectors. The output response analyser (ORA) compresses the CUT outputs into a short signature. The BIST controller runs the sequence: reset, load the seed, apply the vectors, stop, compare the signature with the good one, set pass or fail.

  • TPG: an LFSR, a binary counter, or a small ROM holding ATPG vectors.
  • CUT: combinational logic, logic reached through scan chains, or a memory array.
  • ORA: a MISR, or a simple ones-counter or transition counter; a pass/fail flip-flop holds the result for the tester.

Vectors reach the CUT in two ways. In test-per-clock BIST the TPG drives the CUT inputs directly, one new vector every clock. In test-per-scan BIST the TPG drives the scan-in of each chain and the scan-outs feed the ORA, so each vector needs L shift clocks and one capture clock:

T = N × (L + 1)

where T is the BIST time in clocks, N the number of vectors, L the length of the longest scan chain, and the 1 the capture clock.

The LFSR as pattern generator

A linear feedback shift register (LFSR) is a shift register whose input is the XOR of a few chosen stages, the taps. With taps taken from a primitive polynomial, an n-stage LFSR steps through every non-zero n-bit word once before repeating; it is then maximal-length.

period = 2ⁿ − 1

where n is the number of stages. The one word left out is all zeros.

The all-zero word is a lock-up state: XOR of zeros is zero, so the register would stay there for ever. The seed, the starting word, must be non-zero; different seeds start the same cycle at different points. In the external-XOR (Fibonacci) form the XOR gates combine the taps and feed the first stage; in the internal-XOR (Galois) form one XOR sits between stages, so the feedback passes through only one gate and the register runs faster.

Some faults need a vector a plain LFSR rarely makes; they are called random-pattern-resistant. A 10-input AND gate output stuck-at-0 needs all ten inputs at 1, a chance of 1 in 1024 per vector. A weighted generator ORs two LFSR bits to make a 1 three times in four, or ANDs them to make a 0 three times in four; reseeding loads seeds that expand into chosen ATPG vectors; a test point can also be added to the logic.

Signature analysis and BILBO

A multiple-input signature register (MISR) is an LFSR with one extra XOR in front of each stage, where one CUT output enters each clock. After the last vector the register holds an n-bit signature. It is compared with the golden signature, found by simulating the fault-free design with the same seed and the same vectors.

P(aliasing) ≈ 2⁻ⁿ

where P(aliasing) is the chance that a faulty CUT still ends with the golden signature and n is the MISR width. A 16-bit MISR gives about 1 in 65,536.

BIST comes in flavours by its TPG: exhaustive (a counter applies all 2ᵐ vectors to an m-input block), pseudo-exhaustive (each output cone gets all combinations of its own inputs), pseudo-random (the LFSR stream) and deterministic (stored or reseeded ATPG vectors).

A BILBO, built-in logic block observer, is one register with four modes set by two control bits B1 B2: 11 normal register, 00 scan shift register, 10 LFSR or MISR, 01 reset. Between two blocks, the same BILBO is the ORA for the block before it and the TPG for the block after it. The price of BIST is area for the generator, analyser and controller, a little delay on functional paths, and a golden signature that must be re-simulated whenever the netlist changes.

Question. A 4-bit LFSR shifts left every clock. Its feedback f = b3 ⊕ b0 enters at b0, where b3 is the leftmost bit. Seed it with 0001, list the states until it repeats, and give the period.

Worked example 2

Question. A 3-bit MISR with state b2b1b0 updates as b2′ = b1 ⊕ x2, b1′ = b0 ⊕ x1, b0′ = b2 ⊕ b0 ⊕ x0, where x2x1x0 is the CUT output that clock. It starts at 000.   • Expecting a MISR to say which output was wrong. It only compresses; diagnosis needs the raw responses.
  • Saying BIST makes ATPG unnecessary. Random-pattern-resistant faults still need weighted, reseeded or ATPG vectors, checked by fault simulation.

6.7 Memory testing and memory fault models

Memory arrays fail in ways random logic does not. A write to one cell can disturb its neighbour, a cell can refuse to change, and the address decoder can select no cell, two cells or the wrong cell. Vectors built for stuck-at faults in logic miss these, so memories are tested with ordered read and write sequences called march tests. The same tests run at wafer sort, at final test and inside the chip through memory BIST.

You need: the SRAM cell, the row and column decoders and the sense amplifier, from the memory chapters, and stuck-at faults from 6.2.

Fault models the test must excite

For test, a memory is five blocks: the address decoder, the cell array, the sense amplifiers, the write drivers and the read/write control. The cells sit in a dense grid, so one cell can disturb a neighbour; the decoder is a tree of gates, so a bad address line can open no word, two words or the wrong word. In this chapter the memory is bit-oriented: n is the number of addresses, each holding one bit.

Coupling (CF)A write to an aggressor cell changes a victim cell: an inversion CF flips it, an idempotent CF forces it to a value; a state CF needs the aggressor only to hold a value.
Address decoder (AF)An address reaches no cell, several cells, or the wrong cell.
Neighbourhood pattern (NPSF)A cell's value depends on the pattern held in the cells around it.
Data retention (DRF)A cell loses its data after a pause, or without refresh in DRAM.

A coupling fault always has an aggressor and a victim. To excite it, the test must first give the victim a known value, then write the aggressor with the right transition, then read the victim. Because the aggressor can sit above or below the victim in the address space, the test needs passes in both address orders.

March tests and their length

A march test is a list of march elements. Each element visits every address in one order and does the same operations at each. ⇑ means ascending address order, ⇓ descending, and ⇕ either. w0 and w1 write 0 and 1; r0 and r1 read and expect 0 and 1, and a mismatch is a fail.

MATS+ costs only 5n but misses many coupling faults. Walking and GALPAT grow with n², so a 1 Mbit array would need trillions of operations; they are used on small arrays or for diagnosis.

Memory BIST and the logic around the array

Memory BIST puts the march engine on the chip. Its controller holds:

  • a sequencer that steps through the march elements and their operations;
  • an address counter that counts up or down;
  • a data generator that supplies the write data and the expected read data (all 0s, all 1s, or a background such as a checkerboard for word-wide memories);
  • a comparator that checks every read and sets a fail flag, and can record the failing address;
  • a multiplexer at the memory ports that switches the array between functional logic and BIST.

One controller can serve many memories, testing them in parallel to save time or one after another to limit power; that choice is the BIST controller configuration. The logic around the memory (port multiplexers, byte enables, ECC, address and data wrap logic) also needs testing: through scan, or by running BIST through it. Algorithmic optimisation picks the shortest march that still covers the fault list the product needs, and large memories add spare rows and columns that a repair step, built-in self-repair, switches in after BIST reports the failures.

Question. A 1024-address bit-oriented SRAM is tested with March C−. Count the operations per address and the total accesses. At 5 ns per access, find the test time. Compare it with GALPAT at about 4n² accesses.

Exam traps

  • Running the middle of March C− with alternating directions (⇑, ⇓, ⇑, ⇓). The standard order is two ascending elements, then two descending. 6.8 Chip-level and system-level test techniques

A chip is tested many times: on the wafer, after packaging, after it is soldered to a board, and inside the finished product. Each level uses a different fixture, catches different faults and costs more per failure than the one before. Chip-level and system-level test plans what to test where, and how to reach the cores buried inside a system-on-chip (SoC) through a small number of pins.

You need: test cost and the test steps from 6.1, scan from 6.4 and boundary scan from 6.5.

Four levels, four questions

  • Wafer sort. A probe card touches every die. DC checks, scan and memory tests run at modest speed; bad die are mapped and never packaged.
  • Package (final) test. The packaged part runs the full test at speed, hot and cold. It catches bonding and assembly faults and speed failures the probe could not measure.
  • Board test. After soldering: solder bridges, open tracks, missing or rotated parts. In-circuit test and boundary scan do this work.
  • System test. The finished product boots and runs its software and interfaces. It tests the whole product, so it is the last place an escape can be caught.

The cost of finding a fault rises at each level; a common rule of thumb is about ten times per level. A fault found at wafer sort costs one die. Found at system test, it costs a die, a package, a board, other parts and hours of debug.

C = (N × cw + Np × cp) ÷ Ng

where C is the wafer-sort plus packaging cost per good die shipped (₹), N the die on the wafer, cw the wafer-sort cost per die, Np the die that reach packaging, cp the packaging cost per die and Ng the good die shipped.

  • Known-good die (KGD). A die sold bare for a multi-chip module or a 3D stack must be proved good before bonding, because one bad die scraps the whole module.
  • Burn-in. Hours at raised voltage and temperature turn weak parts into dead ones before shipping.
  • Multi-site test. Testing many die at once lowers cw but needs more tester channels.

Testing a core-based SoC

An SoC is assembled from cores: processor, memory controller, USB, DSP, analog blocks, often from different vendors. The issues in testing such a chip are set by its size and its pins. Verification has billions of states to cover, so the design is checked block by block and then as a system; test must be planned at RTL, because adding it after layout costs area and timing. Each core arrives with its own test set and cannot be reached from the pins directly; the chip has far fewer pins than all its cores' test ports added up; and testing every core at once may draw more power than the package can take.

  • Core wrapper. A ring of cells around a core. In test mode it isolates the core, applies its vectors and captures its responses. IEEE 1500 standardises the wrapper and its serial control.
  • Test access mechanism (TAM). On-chip wires that carry test data from chip pins to the wrappers. A TAM of width w moves w bits per clock.
  • Test scheduling. Decides which cores are tested together, within the TAM width and the power limit.
  • Control. One IEEE 1149.1 TAP (6.5) usually sets the modes of all the wrappers.

T = (ℓ + 1) × P + ℓ

where T is a core's test time in clocks, P its number of patterns and ℓ the length of its longest wrapper scan chain. Giving a core more TAM wires shortens ℓ only if its scan cells can be split into more chains.

Board test, system test and test coding

In-circuit test (ICT) presses a bed of nails onto the board's nets and measures each part: fast, but the fixture is costly and fits one board. A flying probe moves a few needles over the board: no fixture, slower, good for prototypes. Boundary scan replaces most nails on dense boards, and its port also loads chip tests and programs flash.

Test coding, as some syllabi call it, is how a test is written down for the tester and kept small. The test program is a file of vectors plus timing (when each input changes and when each output is strobed), written in a tester language or a standard format such as STIL (IEEE 1450), so a pattern made by an ATPG tool runs on any tester. The scan data is then coded to fit the tester memory. Most bits of an ATPG vector are don't-cares, so they can be filled to make long runs of 0s and the runs sent as short codes, or an on-chip decompressor can expand a few tester channels into hundreds of short internal chains, with a compactor squeezing the outputs back.

Worked example

Question. A wafer holds 1000 die, of which 200 are defective. Wafer sort costs ₹6 per die and catches 90 % of the bad die. Packaging costs ₹80 per die. Final test removes any bad die left, so 800 good die ship in both plans. Find the wafer-sort plus packaging cost per good die (a) without wafer sort and (b) with it.

  1. (a) All 1000 die are packaged: 1000 × ₹80 = ₹80,000. Per good die: 80,000 ÷ 800 = ₹100.00.
  2. (b) Wafer sort: 1000 × ₹6 = ₹6,000. Bad die caught: 0.90 × 200 = 180, so 1000 − 180 = 820 die are packaged.
  3. Packaging: 820 × ₹80 = ₹65,600. Total = ₹6,000 + ₹65,600 = ₹71,600. Per good die: 71,600 ÷ 800 = ₹89.50.
  4. Saving = ₹100.00 − ₹89.50 = ₹10.50 per good die, 10.5 %. Check: the 180 die not packaged save 180 × ₹80 = ₹14,400, the sort costs ₹6,000, and the difference ₹8,400 ÷ 800 = ₹10.50.

Answer. ₹100.00 per good die without wafer sort and ₹89.50 with it, a saving of ₹10.50 (10.5 %).

Question. An SoC has a TAM 8 wires wide. Core A has 4 internal scan chains of 200 cells and 500 patterns; core B has 4 chains of 150 cells and 800 patterns.   5. Saving = 221,650 − 120,950 = 100,700 clocks, 45.4 %. If the cores could use the full width, the serial plan would be as fast; sharing pays here because each core uses only half the TAM.

Exam traps

  • Dividing by all die instead of good die shipped. Cost per good die uses the good parts that leave the line.
  • Thinking wafer sort replaces final test. Bonding faults and speed failures appear only after assembly.
  • Mixing up scan (chip-internal DFT) and boundary scan (pins and board wiring). They use different cells and catch different faults.
  • Assuming a wider TAM always speeds a core. A core with fixed internal chains cannot use more wires than it has chains.
  • Testing every core at once without checking power. Scan switching in all cores together can exceed the package's power limit.

6.9 Analog and mixed-signal testing

Amplifiers, filters, data converters and phase-locked loops have no truth table and no clean stuck-at model. Their outputs are continuous, so an analog block is judged by measuring its specifications and checking that each lies inside a tolerance window. Hard faults are opens and shorts; soft faults are drifts that pass a quick check but break a specification. Quick structural checks such as IDDQ, oscillation test and loopback sit beside converter measurements such as DNL, INL, SNR and ENOB. Every chip with an ADC, a DAC or a radio needs them.

You need: test cost from 6.1, the test levels from 6.8, and ADC and DAC basics from an analog electronics course.

Why digital test methods stop at the analog boundary

A digital line has two values and a stuck-at model, so ATPG can aim at one fault (6.3). An analog node can sit at any voltage, and its correct value is a range: gain 10 ± 0.2, offset below 5 mV, noise below 1 mV rms. Measurement noise and tester error use up part of that window. Few internal nodes can be brought out, because a probe adds capacitance and changes the circuit, and analog tester channels are expensive, so on a mixed-signal SoC the analog part can cost more test time than all the logic.

A hard (catastrophic) fault is an open, a short or a dead component. A soft (parametric) fault is a drift: a resistor 20 % off, a transistor with low gain, a leaky capacitor. The block may still work in a quick check but miss its specification, which is why analog test measures specifications, not only connections.

Quick structural checks find hard faults cheaply. IDDQ measures the quiescent supply current after the block settles; a short or bridge raises it. Oscillation test reconfigures a filter or amplifier into an oscillator; its frequency depends on the component values, so a shifted frequency points to a fault. Loopback sends a digital pattern through a DAC, the analog path and an ADC, and compares the returned codes with a tolerance.

Converter specifications and how they are measured

For an N-bit ADC with full-scale range FSR, 1 LSB = FSR ÷ 2N. Offset error is the shift of the whole transfer curve, in LSB; gain error is the error in its slope after offset is removed. Both are taken out before the linearity errors are found.

DNLk = Wk ÷ (1 LSB) − 1

where DNLk is the differential nonlinearity of code k and Wk the measured width of that code in volts. DNLk = −1 LSB means Wk = 0: a missing code.

INLk = DNL1 + DNL2 + … + DNLk

where INLk is the integral nonlinearity at the upper edge of code k, its distance from the ideal straight line in LSB; the INL quoted on a data sheet is the largest magnitude.

SNR is signal power over noise power, with the harmonics left out; SINAD is signal power over noise plus distortion. An ideal N-bit converter with a full-scale sine has SNR = 6.02N + 1.76 dB, and the effective number of bits is ENOB = (SINAD − 1.76) ÷ 6.02.

The servo method finds each code edge in turn with a feedback loop: accurate but slow. The histogram method applies a slow linear ramp (or a sine), counts how many samples land in each code, and turns counts into widths, so DNL and INL come out of simple counters. The FFT method applies a pure sine and transforms the output codes, giving SNR, THD, SINAD and ENOB. Histogram and FFT tests are the production standard.

Design for testability for analog blocks

Analog DFT adds access and control. Test points bring internal nodes out through unity-gain buffers so the probe does not load them. Bypass switches isolate a block, so the ADC can be tested without the filter in front of it. Loopback is the workhorse: digital pattern, on-chip DAC, analog path, on-chip ADC, digital compare. It catches opens, shorts, gross gain loss and filter shifts, but not every fault: a DAC gain 2 % high and an ADC gain 2 % low cancel in the returned code.

On-chip measurement puts a peak detector, an RMS detector or a small ADC beside the block, so the tester reads a number: this is analog BIST. IEEE 1149.4 extends boundary scan to analog pins: an analog boundary module switches each pin onto a two-wire internal analog test bus, and an external instrument connected to that bus forces a current or measures a voltage. It is mainly used for testing analog board wiring and discrete parts.

Worked example

Question. An 8-bit ADC has a full-scale range of 2.56 V. A ramp histogram gives code widths W64 = 14 mV and W65 = 6 mV, and INL63 = 0 LSB. (a) Find 1 LSB. (b) Find DNL at codes 64 and 65.   1. (a) 2⁸ = 256 codes, so 1 LSB = 2.56 V ÷ 256 = 0.010 V = 10 mV.
  2. (b) DNL64 = 14 ÷ 10 − 1 = +0.4 LSB. DNL65 = 6 ÷ 10 − 1 = −0.4 LSB.
  3. (c) INL64 = INL63 + DNL64 = 0 + 0.4 = +0.4 LSB. INL65 = 0.4 + (−0.4) = 0.0 LSB.
  4. (d) DNL = 0 ÷ 10 − 1 = −1 LSB: the code never appears, a missing code. At that code INL drops by 1 LSB from the previous code's value; it looks near zero only if the previous INL was about +1 LSB.

Exam traps

  • Treating DNL and INL as the same. DNL is the error of one code width; INL is the running sum of DNL, the distance from the ideal line.
  • Writing a missing code as INL = −1 LSB. A missing code has DNL = −1 LSB; INL at that code falls by 1 LSB from its previous value.
  • Finding INL before removing offset and gain error. A tilted transfer curve is gain error, not a large INL.
  • Dividing by 2N − 1 for 1 LSB when the question defines FSR over 2N codes. Use FSR ÷ 2N unless told otherwise.
  • Calling SINAD 'SNR'. SNR leaves the harmonics out; SINAD includes them, and ENOB is computed from SINAD.

Takeaways

  1. Every chip is tested because the fab makes defects; yield and fault coverage together set how many bad chips still ship, DL = 1 − Y(1 − T).
  2. Fault models turn countless physical defects into a short logical list: stuck-at for static logic, transition and path delay for timing.
  3. Collapsing (equivalence, dominance, checkpoints) shrinks the list before ATPG; fault simulation with dropping measures coverage vector by vector.
  4. The D-algorithm activates a fault, propagates D or D̄ to an output, and justifies every value back to the primary inputs.
  5. Full scan turns a sequential chip into combinational logic for ATPG, at the cost of a multiplexer per flip-flop and (N + 1)P + N shift clocks.
  6. Boundary scan tests board wiring through four pins; in a daisy chain the register lengths add, and TMS walks every chip through the same 16 states.
  7. BIST puts an LFSR and a MISR on the chip; maximal-length LFSRs give 2ⁿ − 1 vectors and an n-bit MISR aliases with probability about 2⁻ⁿ.
  8. Memories need march tests; March C− (10n) catches stuck-at, transition, address and unlinked coupling faults, and memory BIST runs it on chip.
  9. Test is spread over wafer, package, board and system; SoC cores are reached through wrappers and a TAM, scheduled within pins and power.
  10. Analog blocks are tested against specifications: DNL, INL, offset, gain, SNR, SINAD and ENOB, with structural checks such as IDDQ and loopback.

Exam question bank

Long answers usually join two or three chapters: a fault model with ATPG, scan with boundary scan, or BIST with memory test. Most syllabi ask for the scan cell and test procedure, the TAP state diagram, the LFSR and MISR, and a march test written out element by element; show a numerical trace wherever the question gives numbers.

Remember and understand

Understand Explain why every fabricated chip is tested, and how yield and fault coverage decide the quality the customer receives. Use a yield of 0.80 to show the effect of coverage. 8 marks

Points to cover. Defects come from the fab; verification cannot find them; Where test sits from wafer to customer; Yield and its link to die area; Fault coverage from fault simulation; Defect level DL = 1 − Y^(1 − T) with numbers; Cost: tester time, multi-site, escapes.

Model answer.

  1. Why test at all. Fabrication adds random defects: dust that breaks a wire, a missing contact, a pinhole in the gate oxide. Verification only proves the design is right; it cannot see whether one particular copy was built right. So every die is driven with known inputs and its outputs are compared with the expected ones.
  2. Where test happens. Wafer probe removes bad die before packaging money is spent; final test removes parts damaged in assembly or too slow at temperature; burn-in removes weak parts; board and system tests catch what is left. Each later step costs more per failure.
  3. Yield. Yield Y is the fraction of die that are good. Random defects make it fall with die area A and defect density D, roughly Y = e−A·D; a 1 cm² die at 0.2 killer defects per cm² gives Y = e−0.2 = 0.82.
  4. Fault coverage. Fault coverage T is the fraction of modelled faults (for example single stuck-at faults) that the test vectors detect, measured by fault simulation. It describes the test program, not the fab.
  5. Defect level. The fraction of shipped parts that are bad is DL = 1 − Y(1 − T). With Y = 0.80:
    Fault coverage T1 − TY(1 − T)DLDPM
    0.950.050.9889050.01109511,095
    0.990.010.9977710.0022292,229
    1.000100
  6. Reading the table. Four more points of coverage cut the escapes about five times, from 11,095 to 2,229 parts per million. Because coverage sits in the exponent, the last few per cent matter most, and that is where test engineering effort goes.
  7. Cost. Test is paid per second of tester time: cost per die = (R × t) ÷ s for a tester rate R, test time t and s sites tested together. Higher coverage means more vectors and longer t, so the team balances test cost against the cost of escapes: field returns, warranty and lost customers.
  8. Check. At T = 1 the exponent is zero and DL = 0; at Y = 1 there is nothing to escape and DL = 0. Both limits agree with the formula, and the numbers fall as T rises.
Remember Define defect, fault, error and failure with an example of each. Explain why fault models are needed, using the single stuck-at faults of a two-input NAND gate. 8 marks

Points to cover. Four definitions with examples; Why defects cannot be targeted directly; Single stuck-at model and fault count 2L; NAND: 6 faults, 4 classes, 3 tests; Other models: bridging, stuck-open, delay.

Model answer.

  1. Defect. A physical flaw in the manufactured chip. Example: a dust particle breaks a metal wire.
  2. Fault. The logical model of a defect, used by test software. Example: the broken wire behaves as a line stuck at 0.
  3. Error. A wrong value on a line at some moment, caused by the fault when the circuit is exercised. Example: an adder output is 0110 instead of 0111.
  4. Failure. Lost function: the error reaches an output and the chip misses its specification. Example: a phone screen stays black.
  5. Why models are needed. Defects come in endless sizes, shapes and places, so no program can list them. A fault model maps them onto a finite logical list that a program can enumerate, generate tests for and count; fault coverage is defined on that list.
  6. Single stuck-at on a NAND. The lines A, B and Z give 2 × 3 = 6 faults. The faulty outputs for AB = 00, 01, 10, 11 (good: 1 1 1 0) are:
    Fault00011011Detected by
    A/0, B/0, Z/1111111
    A/1101001
    B/1110010
    Z/0000000, 01, 10
  7. Collapsing. A/0, B/0 and Z/1 give identical tables, so they are equivalent: 4 classes remain. Z/0 is detected by every test of A/1, so it dominates A/1 and can be dropped: 3 faults remain, and {01, 10, 11} is a complete test set of three vectors.
  8. Other models. Bridging faults (two lines shorted, wired-AND or wired-OR), transistor stuck-open (needs a two-vector test), and transition or path delay faults (need launch and capture at speed) cover defects the stuck-at model misses.
  9. Check. The test set covers every row of the table: 11 catches the first class, 01 catches A/1 and Z/0, 10 catches B/1 and Z/0.
Understand Explain scan design with a neat diagram of a scan chain. Define full scan, partial scan, controllability and observability, and give the test procedure and its cost. 10 marks

Points to cover. Why sequential circuits are hard: controllability and observability; Muxed-D scan cell, SE and SI; Scan chain and the three phases; Full versus partial scan; Cost: area, delay, pins, test time (N + 1)P + N.

Model answer.

  1. The problem. A sequential circuit hides its state. Controllability is how easily a node can be set to 0 or 1 from the primary inputs; observability is how easily its value can be seen at a primary output. Deep state makes both poor, so a fault may need a long input sequence to excite and another to observe, and ATPG time explodes.
  2. The scan cell. A 2:1 multiplexer in front of each D flip-flop chooses the functional input D when scan enable SE = 0, and the scan input SI when SE = 1.
  3. The chain. Joining Q of each cell to SI of the next forms a shift register from the scan-in pin to the scan-out pin. The example chain below has three cells and a small logic block.
  4. Procedure. (1) Shift in: SE = 1, the pattern enters one bit per clock, the bit for the last cell first. (2) Capture: SE = 0 for one clock; every flip-flop loads the logic's response. (3) Shift out: SE = 1, the response leaves through scan-out while the next pattern enters.
  5. Trace. In the figure, loading 011 (SI = 1, 1, 0) and capturing gives 111 in a good chip; with D1 stuck-at-0 the chip captures 011, and the third bit out is 0 instead of 1.
  6. Full scan makes every flip-flop a scan cell, so ATPG sees only combinational logic between pseudo-primary inputs and outputs: high coverage, simple ATPG. Partial scan converts only the flip-flops that break feedback loops: less area and delay, but ATPG must handle the remaining sequential logic.
  7. Cost. One multiplexer per flip-flop (area), one multiplexer delay on each functional path, the SE net and two or more pins, and test time. With overlapped shifting, P patterns on a chain of N cells take (N + 1) × P + N clocks; 1000 patterns on one 200-cell chain take 201,200 clocks, on four 50-cell chains 51,050.
  8. Check. The three-cell trace uses (3 + 1) × 1 + 3 = 7 clocks: three to shift in, one to capture, three to shift out, matching the formula.
Understand Describe boundary scan with the architecture of an IEEE 1149.1 device and the TAP controller state diagram. Explain how the instructions select the registers. 10 marks

Points to cover. TAP pins: TCK, TMS, TDI, TDO, optional TRST; Boundary cells at each digital pin, BSR; IR, bypass, BSR, IDCODE; 16-state TAP controller; BYPASS, SAMPLE/PRELOAD, EXTEST, IDCODE; Daisy chain on a board.

Model answer.

  1. Aim. Boundary scan tests the wiring between chips on a board, where fine-pitch and ball-grid-array parts leave no probe points, through four pins on each chip.
  2. Pins. TCK clocks the logic; TMS steers the TAP controller; TDI and TDO carry serial data in and out. TMS and TDI are sampled on the rising edge of TCK and TDO changes on the falling edge. TRST is an optional asynchronous reset.
  3. Boundary cells. A cell sits between the core and every digital signal pad. In normal mode it passes the signal; in test mode an output cell can drive the pad from a stored bit and an input cell can capture the pad value. All cells form the boundary-scan register (BSR).
  4. Registers. The instruction register (IR, at least 2 bits) holds the current instruction. The data registers are the 1-bit bypass register, the BSR and, on most chips, the 32-bit IDCODE register. The instruction decides which data register is connected between TDI and TDO.
  5. TAP controller. A 16-state machine moved by TMS on each rising TCK edge. Five TCK edges with TMS = 1 reach Test-Logic-Reset from any state. Each of the DR and IR columns has Select, Capture (parallel load), Shift (one bit per TCK), Exit1, Pause, Exit2 and Update (copy to the outputs).
  6. Instructions.
    InstructionRegister selectedUse
    BYPASS (all 1s, required)Bypass, 1 bitSkip a chip in the chain
    SAMPLE/PRELOAD (required)BSRRead pins in normal operation; load safe drive values
    EXTEST (required)BSRDrive and capture pins: interconnect test
    IDCODE (optional)IDCODERead the chip identity
  7. Board use. Chips are daisy-chained TDI to TDO with TCK and TMS shared, so the lengths add. With IRs of 4, 4 and 5 bits and BSRs of 20, 20 and 30 cells, loading EXTEST shifts 13 bits and one pattern shifts 70 bits; from Test-Logic-Reset the whole job takes 5 + 13 + 1 + 3 + 70 + 2 = 94 TCK cycles.
  8. Check. The sum of shift bits (13 + 70 = 83) plus the 11 state-change edges gives the same 94 cycles.
Understand Explain the BIST architecture with a pattern generator and a response analyser. Work out the states of a 4-bit LFSR and the aliasing probability of a 16-bit MISR. 10 marks

Points to cover. Why BIST: tester cost, at-speed and field test; TPG, CUT, ORA, controller; LFSR, maximal length 2ⁿ − 1, non-zero seed; MISR signature, golden value, aliasing 2⁻ⁿ; Test-per-clock versus test-per-scan; Costs and limits.

Model answer.

  1. Why BIST. An external tester is expensive per second and cannot always apply vectors at the chip's full speed. BIST puts the pattern generator and the response checker on the chip, so the chip can test itself at power-on, after packaging and in the field.
  2. Architecture. The test pattern generator (TPG) drives the circuit under test (CUT); the output response analyser (ORA) compresses the outputs into a signature; the controller resets, seeds, counts the vectors and compares the signature with the stored golden value.
  3. LFSR. A shift register whose input is the XOR of chosen taps. With taps from a primitive polynomial an n-stage LFSR visits all 2ⁿ − 1 non-zero states. The seed must be non-zero, because all zeros feeds back zero for ever.
  4. Worked states. 4 bits, shift left, f = b3 ⊕ b0 into b0, seed 0001:
    ClockStatefClockStatef
    000011810110
    100111901100
    2011111011001
    3111101110010
    4111011200100
    5110101301000
    6101011410001
    701011150001–
  5. The seed returns after 15 clocks = 2⁴ − 1, so the LFSR is maximal-length.
  6. MISR. An LFSR with an XOR at each stage input where one CUT output enters each clock. After N vectors it holds an n-bit signature. A faulty circuit can end on the golden signature (aliasing) with probability about 2⁻ⁿ: for 16 bits, 1 ÷ 65,536 = 1.53 × 10⁻⁵.
  7. Delivery. Test-per-clock applies one vector per clock to the CUT inputs; test-per-scan shifts each vector into scan chains, N × (L + 1) clocks for N vectors and chains of L cells.
  8. Costs and limits. Area for the LFSR, MISR and controller; a little delay where multiplexers enter functional paths; random-pattern-resistant faults that need weighting, reseeding or test points; and a golden signature that must be re-simulated whenever the design changes. A pass means the signature matched, not that the chip is perfect.
  9. Check. All 15 listed states are different and none is 0000, as a maximal-length 4-bit LFSR requires.

Apply The circuit has G1 = AND(A, B), G2 = OR(G1, C) and Z = AND(G2, D). Use the D-algorithm to find a test for G1 stuck-at-0. Show the D-frontier and J-frontier at each step, and verify the test. 10 marks

Points to cover. Activate G1/0: G1 = 1, so A = B = 1; Propagate through OR with C = 0; Propagate through AND with D = 1; Frontiers at each step; Vector ABCD = 1101; good Z = 1, faulty Z = 0.

Model answer.

  1. Notation. D means good 1 and faulty 0; D̄ means good 0 and faulty 1. Non-controlling values: 1 for AND, 0 for OR.
  2. Activate. The fault is G1 stuck-at-0, so the good circuit must have G1 = 1: G1 = D. An AND output of 1 needs A = 1 and B = 1, which go on the J-frontier.

  4. Propagate through Z. Z = AND(G2, D) is now on the D-frontier. Set D = 1: Z = AND(D, 1) = D, so the difference reaches the output.
  5. Justify. A = 1, B = 1, C = 0 and D = 1 are all primary inputs, so the J-frontier empties without conflict and no backtrack is needed.

Apply Explain how a march test is applied to a memory. Run March C− on a 4-address bit-oriented memory in which a 0 → 1 write to cell 0 inverts cell 3, and show where it fails. Why does address order matter? 10 marks

Points to cover. Notation ⇑ ⇓ ⇕, w0 w1 r0 r1; March C− elements and length 10n; Trace on 4 cells with the coupling fault; Address order and aggressor position; Other faults caught.

Model answer.

  1. Notation. A march element visits every address in one order (⇑ ascending, ⇓ descending, ⇕ either) and does the same operations at each. w0/w1 write 0/1; r0/r1 read and expect 0/1, and a mismatch is a fail.
  2. March C−. ⇕(w0); ⇑(r0, w1); ⇑(r1, w0); ⇓(r0, w1); ⇓(r1, w0); ⇕(r0). Operations per address: 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 10, so the length is 10n; for n = 4 that is 40 operations.
  3. Trace with the coupling fault (aggressor cell 0, victim cell 3, rising write of the aggressor inverts the victim):
    ElementAddressOperationCells 0 1 2 3 afterResult
    ⇕(w0)0 to 3w00 0 0 0ok
    ⇑(r0, w1)0r0, w1 (0 → 1 inverts cell 3)1 0 0 1ok
    1r0, w11 1 0 1ok
    2r0, w11 1 1 1ok
    3r01 1 1 1fail: reads 1
  4. Where it fails. The read of cell 3 in the second element expects 0 but finds 1. The fault was excited at address 0 and observed three addresses later in the same ascending pass.
  5. Why order matters. Here the aggressor is below the victim, so an ascending pass reaches the victim after the aggressor has been written. When the aggressor is above the victim (say cell 2 disturbs cell 0), the ascending pass has already read the victim; the fault is then caught by the next element's read, ⇑(r1, w0), or by a descending element. Running both orders with both transitions covers every aggressor-victim pair. On a 4-cell memory, March C− catches all 72 single coupling faults, while MATS+ (5n) misses 40.
  6. Other faults. A cell that cannot rise (up-transition fault) fails the r1 of ⇑(r1, w0); a stuck-at cell fails one of the reads; an address that writes the wrong cell fails a later read of that cell.
  7. Check. Total operations for the whole test on 4 cells: 10 × 4 = 40, of which the failing read is the 12th: 4 writes in the first element, then r0 and w1 at addresses 0, 1 and 2 (6 operations), then r0 at address 3.

Analyze Compare stuck-at testing with delay testing. Why must delay faults be tested at speed? Use a two-input AND gate whose input A is slow to rise, with a 1.2 ns path, a 1.0 ns defect and a 2.0 ns clock. 12 marks

Points to cover. Static versus timing faults; Transition and path delay models; Two-vector test, launch and capture; Numerical case at 2.0 ns and 10 ns; Launch-on-shift and launch-on-capture in scan; Cost and coverage trade-offs.

Model answer.

  1. Stuck-at faults are static: a line holds 0 or 1 whatever happens. One vector that activates the fault and propagates it to an output is enough, and the speed of the test does not matter.
  2. Delay faults are about time: the logic value is right but arrives late. A transition fault makes one line slow to rise or fall; a path delay fault makes a whole path too slow even when every gate is only a little slow. Resistive opens, resistive bridges and weak transistors cause them, and they grow in importance as clock periods shrink.
  3. Two-vector test. V1 sets the starting value, the launch edge applies V2 and creates the transition, and the capture edge one clock period later stores the result. If the path is slow, the old value is captured.
  4. Numerical case. Z = A·B, A slow to rise. V1 = (A, B) = (0, 1), V2 = (1, 1).
    CaseArrival at flip-flopCapture after launchCaptured ZResult
    Good chip, at speed1.2 ns2.0 ns1pass
    Faulty chip, at speed1.2 + 1.0 = 2.2 ns2.0 ns0fail: detected
    Faulty chip, slow tester2.2 ns10 ns1pass: escape
  5. Why at speed. The slow tester waits 10 ns, longer than the faulty 2.2 ns, so the delay is invisible. Only a capture one rated period after launch exposes it. The slack of this path is 2.0 − 1.2 = 0.8 ns, so defects smaller than 0.8 ns escape even at speed; those small delays are caught only through longer paths.
  6. In scan designs the pair is made in two ways. Launch-on-shift: the last shift clock creates V2, then capture follows at speed; easy ATPG but SE must switch fast. Launch-on-capture: V1 is shifted in, a first functional clock launches V2 from the logic itself, a second captures; SE can be slow, but ATPG is harder.
  7. Comparison. Stuck-at test: one vector per fault, small pattern count, no timing needs, high coverage of hard defects. Delay test: two vectors per fault, more patterns, an accurate at-speed clock (often from the on-chip PLL), and path selection for path delay faults. Both are needed: stuck-at for opens and shorts, delay for chips that are logically right but too slow.
  8. Check. The faulty arrival 2.2 ns exceeds the 2.0 ns capture time by 0.2 ns, so the at-speed test captures the old 0; at 10 ns it does not.
Evaluate Evaluate full scan, partial scan and logic BIST for a large SoC built from reused IP cores. Recommend a test strategy. 14 marks

Points to cover. Requirements of an SoC with cores; Full scan: coverage, ATPG, cost, test time; Partial scan: savings and penalties; Logic BIST: field test, aliasing, X-sources, test points; Core access: wrappers, TAM, JTAG; Recommendation with reasons and numbers.

Model answer.

  1. Requirements. High stuck-at and transition coverage for low DPM; test time that fits the tester budget; few pins; cores from vendors that arrive with their own tests; and, for automotive or safety parts, self-test in the field.
  2. Full scan. Every flip-flop is a scan cell, so combinational ATPG reaches very high coverage with modest effort, and failing patterns can be diagnosed. Costs: a multiplexer per flip-flop and a delay on functional paths, SE routing, and long shift times. Example: 10,000 patterns through chains of 1,000 cells at 100 MHz take (1,001 × 10,000 + 1,000) × 10 ns = 100.1 ms per chip. Compression cuts this many times.
  3. Partial scan. Only chosen flip-flops are scanned, usually to break feedback loops. It saves some area and delay on critical paths, but ATPG must handle the remaining sequential logic, run time rises and coverage usually falls. For a large SoC the savings are small compared with the coverage lost, so it suits only timing-critical blocks.
  4. Logic BIST. An LFSR feeds the scan chains and a MISR collects their outputs. It needs no tester memory, can run at speed and in the field, and suits reused cores. Limits: random-pattern-resistant faults need test points or reseeding; any unknown (X) value corrupts the MISR, so X-sources must be blocked; aliasing about 2⁻ⁿ; and area for the generator, analyser and controller. Because BIST is not limited by tester pins, it can use many short chains: 100,000 random patterns through 100-cell chains take (101 × 100,000 + 100) × 10 ns ≈ 0.101 s.
  5. Comparison.
    CriterionFull scanPartial scanLogic BIST
    CoverageHighest, deterministicLowerHigh after test points
    ATPG effortLowHighFault simulation only
    Tester memoryHigh (compression helps)MediumVery low
    Area and delayMux per flip-flopLessScan plus BIST logic

    DiagnosisGoodFairPoor (signature only)
  6. Core access. Whatever the logic test, each core needs an IEEE 1500 wrapper, a TAM sized to its scan chains, and a schedule that fits the power limit. A JTAG port sets all modes and tests the board wiring.
  7. Recommendation. Use full scan with compression on all digital logic for production test, for the highest coverage and good diagnosis. Add logic BIST on the same chains for at-speed and in-field self-test where the product needs it, with test points for resistant faults. Use memory BIST (March C− or stronger) for every embedded memory. Keep partial scan only where a multiplexer cannot fit a critical path. Wrap every core and schedule cores in parallel where their chain counts leave TAM wires idle.
  8. Check. The strategy meets each requirement: coverage and diagnosis from scan, field test from BIST, pins from compression and JTAG, core reuse from wrappers.

  3. What DNL tells. The worst DNL is ±0.5 LSB, at codes 4 and 5. No code has DNL = −1, so there are no missing codes; with |DNL| below 1 LSB the converter is monotonic.
  4. What INL tells. The worst INL is +0.5 LSB, at the edge after code 4: the transfer curve bends half an LSB away from the ideal line there. INL limits accuracy for DC measurements.
  5. Offset and gain. Offset error is the shift of the whole curve and gain error the slope error; both are removed before DNL and INL, which is why the six widths add to 600 mV = 6 LSB exactly and INL ends at 0.
  6. Dynamic tests. SNR leaves harmonics out; SINAD includes noise and distortion. An ideal 3-bit converter has SNR = 6.02 × 3 + 1.76 = 19.82 dB. ENOB = (18.5 − 1.76) ÷ 6.02 = 2.78 bits, so noise and distortion cost about 0.2 bit.
  7. Setting limits. Limits come from the data sheet, for example |DNL| ≤ 0.75 LSB, |INL| ≤ 1 LSB, ENOB ≥ 2.7 bits. The tester's own uncertainty is then subtracted as a guard band: with ±0.1 LSB uncertainty the test limits become 0.65 and 0.9 LSB, so a part that passes the test is sure to meet the specification. Wider guard bands mean fewer escapes but more good parts thrown away (yield loss).
  8. Verdict. Worst DNL 0.5 ≤ 0.65, worst INL 0.5 ≤ 0.9 and ENOB 2.78 ≥ 2.7: the part passes.

Evaluate Discuss the test flow of a chip from wafer to system. Include known-good die, burn-in, in-circuit test, boundary scan, SoC test access and test compression, and evaluate where each catches faults most cheaply. 14 marks

Points to cover. Wafer sort, package test, board test, system test; Cost per fault rises by level; Wafer-sort economics with numbers; KGD and burn-in; ICT, flying probe, boundary scan; SoC: wrappers, TAM, scheduling; Compression and test coding.

Model answer.

  1. Wafer sort. A probe card contacts every die: DC checks, scan and memory BIST at modest speed. Bad die are inked or mapped and never packaged.
  2. Package (final) test. The packaged part runs the full program at speed, hot and cold, catching bonding and assembly faults and speed failures that the probe cannot measure.
  3. Burn-in. Hours at raised voltage and temperature turn weak parts (thin oxide, marginal vias) into dead ones, so infant failures happen in the factory; a retest removes them.
  4. Board test. In-circuit test presses a bed of nails onto every net and measures parts; a flying probe does it without a fixture, slowly. On dense boards with ball-grid arrays, boundary scan (EXTEST) checks the nets through four pins.
  5. System test. The product boots and runs its software. It is the only level that tests the whole product, and the most expensive place to find and debug a fault.
  6. Why earlier is cheaper. Taking 1000 die, 200 bad, wafer sort at ₹6 per die catching 90 % of the bad ones, and packaging at ₹80:
    PlanWafer sortPackagedPackaging costPer good die
    No wafer sort₹01000₹80,000₹100.00
    Wafer sort₹6,000820₹65,600₹89.50
  7. Known-good die. For multi-chip modules and 3D stacks, die are sold bare; one bad die scraps the module, so wafer-level test must be as thorough as final test, often at speed and with burn-in at wafer level.
  8. SoC access. Cores are wrapped (IEEE 1500) and fed through a TAM; scheduling packs cores by chain count and power. Two cores with 4 chains each on an 8-wire TAM finish in 120,950 clocks side by side instead of 221,650 one after the other.
  9. Compression and test coding. Patterns are written for the tester with timing in a standard format such as STIL, and compression expands a few tester channels into hundreds of short chains, cutting tester time and memory many times.
  10. Evaluation. Logic and memory faults are cheapest to catch at wafer sort with scan and BIST; assembly and speed faults at final test; weak parts through burn-in; solder and wiring faults at board test with boundary scan; interaction and software faults only at system test. A good flow puts each fault class at the earliest level that can see it.
  11. Check. The per-good-die costs agree with the totals: 80,000 ÷ 800 = 100.00 and 71,600 ÷ 800 = 89.50.

Glossary

Aliasing
A faulty circuit ending with the golden signature, so BIST passes it; about 2⁻ⁿ for an n-bit MISR.
ATE
Automatic test equipment: the tester that drives and senses the chip pins.
ATPG
Automatic test pattern generation: finding input vectors that detect chosen faults.
BILBO
Built-in logic block observer: a register that can act as a normal register, scan register, LFSR or MISR.
BIST
Built-in self-test: on-chip pattern generation and response checking.
Boundary scan
Test cells at the pins of a chip, chained so board wiring can be tested through a serial port (IEEE 1149.1).
Burn-in
Running parts for hours at raised voltage and temperature to screen early-life failures.
Controllability
How easily a node can be set to 0 or 1 from the primary inputs.
D-frontier
Gates with D or D̄ on an input and an unknown output, where the fault effect can move forward.
Defect level
Fraction of shipped parts that are bad, often given in DPM.
DNL
Differential nonlinearity: the error of one ADC code width, in LSB.
Fault coverage
Detected faults divided by all faults in the fault list, as a percentage.
Fault efficiency
Detected faults divided by the faults not proved redundant.
IDDQ
Quiescent supply current; a high value reveals shorts and bridges.
INL
Integral nonlinearity: the running sum of DNL, the distance of a code edge from the ideal line.
Known-good die
A bare die proved good before it is bonded into a module or stack.
LFSR
Linear feedback shift register; used as a pseudo-random pattern generator.
March test
A memory test made of elements that visit every address in a set order with fixed reads and writes.
MISR
Multiple-input signature register: an LFSR that compresses many outputs into one signature.
Observability
How easily the value of a node can be seen at a primary output.
Redundant fault
A fault no input vector can detect, because the logic it sits in is redundant.
Scan chain
Scan flip-flops joined into a shift register from scan-in to scan-out.
Stuck-at fault
A line that always reads 0 (SA0) or always reads 1 (SA1).
TAM
Test access mechanism: on-chip wires that carry test data to core wrappers.
TAP
Test access port: TCK, TMS, TDI, TDO (and optional TRST) with its 16-state controller.
Transition delay fault
A line that is slow to rise or slow to fall.
Yield
The fraction of die on a wafer that are good.
