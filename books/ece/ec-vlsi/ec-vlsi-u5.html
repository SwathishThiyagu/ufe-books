5.1 VLSI design flow and methodology

A VLSI design flow is the ordered set of stages that turns a written specification into a tested silicon chip. A chip with millions of gates cannot be designed in one jump, so each stage solves a smaller, checkable problem. Front-end work (specification, architecture, RTL, verification, synthesis) and back-end work (DFT insertion, floorplan, placement, clock tree, routing, signoff) end at tapeout, when the final layout goes to the foundry; fabrication, wafer test, packaging and final test follow. Phone SoCs, set-top box chips and payment-terminal chips are all made this way.

You need: basic digital logic (gates, flip-flops, Boolean algebra, binary numbers); the rest of this chapter starts from zero.

Front-end and back-end stages

Each stage takes one description of the chip, produces a more concrete one and hands a checked file to the next.

  1. Specification: function, clock frequency in MHz or GHz, power in W, cost, package, interfaces.
  2. Architecture: partition into blocks, count clock domains, fix bus widths, memory sizes and I/O pins.
  3. RTL: write the design as register-transfer-level Verilog modules (Verilog HDL, modelling styles).
  4. Verification: simulate against a test bench (test benches) and add formal proofs.
  5. Synthesis: map RTL to a gate netlist of standard cells under a clock-period constraint.
  6. DFT insertion: add scan chains and self-test logic while the design is still a netlist.
  7. Physical design: floorplan, placement, clock-tree synthesis, routing (floorplanning, placement and routing).
  8. Signoff: design-rule check (DRC), layout versus schematic (LVS), static timing, power and IR drop.
  9. Tapeout: the final layout database (GDSII or OASIS) is handed to the foundry for mask making. Tapeout is only this handoff.

Some syllabi call this the ASIC design flow. The order is top-down (specification to blocks, blocks to RTL, RTL to gates), but real projects also reuse verified blocks bottom-up. Verification is not one box: RTL simulation starts it; after synthesis, equivalence checking proves the netlist does what the RTL does; after routing, static timing analysis and gate-level simulation check the real wires.

From wafer to chip

After tapeout the foundry makes one photomask per layer and builds hundreds of identical dies on a silicon wafer, today usually 300 mm across. The process steps themselves are covered under CMOS fabrication and layout; the chip goes through them in this order.

  1. Front-end-of-line: wells, isolation, gate oxide, gates and source-drain implants build the transistors.
  2. Back-end-of-line: contacts, then layers of metal wires and vias in insulating oxide, connect them.
  3. Wafer probe (wafer sort): a probe card touches each die's pads for a short test; failing dies are marked on a wafer map.
  4. Dicing and packaging: the wafer is sawn into dies; good dies are bonded and sealed in packages.
  5. Final test: each packaged chip is tested again on automatic test equipment (ATE), then binned and shipped.

Yield = good dies ÷ total dies. It is never 100 %, and a bad chip found in a customer's phone costs far more than one found on the tester.

Block-level choices and SoC verification issues

At block level the flow fixes the details that sink projects late: the clock plan (PLL, clock domains, resets), the I/O ring (pads, electrostatic discharge protection), the device modes (functional, scan, self-test, sleep), the memories (compiled SRAM macros), the analog blocks (PLL, ADC, regulator) and power management (power gating, isolation cells, retention flip-flops). A wrong clock decision found at signoff costs weeks; found in the specification it costs an hour. Which style builds the gates is the subject of the next chapter.

A complex chip or SoC is hard to verify and test because: (1) its state space is too large to simulate fully, so teams measure coverage and add formal proofs and emulation; (2) embedded IP cores sit deep inside, so their pins are not chip pins and tests must reach them through a wrapper (IP cores); (3) several clock and power domains add crossing and power-up bugs; (4) analog blocks need their own tests; (5) firmware must be verified with the hardware.

The test handoff

The design team hands the test team three things: the DUT (device under test, as a netlist), the inserted DFT hardware, and the pattern set. ATPG (automatic test pattern generation) makes the patterns from the netlist and a fault model; fault simulation grades them as fault coverage in percent. BIST (built-in self-test) puts a pattern generator and checker on the chip itself, commonly for memories.

Fault modelWhat it models
Stuck-at (SAF)A line fixed at 0 or 1

In scan design each flip-flop gets a multiplexer, and in test mode the flip-flops join into a shift register (a scan chain): a pattern is shifted in, captured in one clock and shifted out. Boundary scan (IEEE 1149.1, JTAG) puts a scan cell at every I/O pin behind the test access port TDI, TDO, TCK and TMS (TRST optional). Turning patterns into a tester program with timing, levels and compare strobes is the test-coding step; STIL (IEEE 1450) is a common format for it.

test time = (N × (L + 1) + L) × T<sub>clk</sub>,   cost = test time × tester rate

N is the number of patterns, L the longest chain in flip-flops, Tclk the scan clock period and the tester rate in rupees per second. Shifting in the next pattern overlaps shifting out the last, so each pattern costs L + 1 cycles, plus one final unload of L. Fault models, ATPG, scan and BIST are taught in full under VLSI testing and design for testability.

Worked example

Question. An SoC has 24 lakh standard-cell gates. Synthesis runs at 1,20,000 gates per hour, placement and routing together run at 60,000 gates per hour, and verification needs 3 hours per 10,000 gates. Find the synthesis time, the physical-design time, the verification time, the total flow time, and the verification share of that total.

  1. Synthesis time = 24,00,000 gates ÷ 1,20,000 gates/h = 20 h.
  2. Physical-design time = 24,00,000 gates ÷ 60,000 gates/h = 40 h.
  3. Verification blocks = 24,00,000 ÷ 10,000 = 240 blocks of 10,000 gates.
  4. Verification time = 240 × 3 h = 720 h.
  5. Total time = 20 h + 40 h + 720 h = 780 h.
  6. Verification share = 720 ÷ 780 = 0.923, or 92.3 %.

Answer. Synthesis 20 h, physical design 40 h, verification 720 h, total 780 h; verification is 92.3 % of the flow.

Worked example 2

Question. A chip has 5,000 scan flip-flops. ATPG gives 1,000 patterns, the scan clock is 50 MHz and the test house charges ₹3 per second of tester time. Find the test time and test cost per chip (a) with one scan chain of 5,000 flip-flops and (b) with ten chains of 500. (c) What does the change save on 10 lakh chips?

  1. Scan clock period: Tclk = 1 ÷ 50 MHz = 20 ns.
  2. (a) One chain, L = 5,000: cycles = 1,000 × 5,001 + 5,000 = 50,06,000; time = 50,06,000 × 20 ns = 100.12 ms; cost = 0.10012 s × ₹3/s = ₹0.300.
  3. (b) Ten chains shift in parallel, so L = 500: cycles = 1,000 × 501 + 500 = 5,01,500; time = 10.03 ms; cost = 0.01003 s × ₹3/s = ₹0.0301.
  4. (c) On 10 lakh chips: (a) costs ₹3,00,360 and (b) costs ₹30,090, so ten chains save ₹2,70,270 of tester time.
  5. Check: L falls 10 times and the time falls from 100.12 ms to 10.03 ms, almost 10 times (the capture cycles do not shrink).

Answer. One chain: 100.12 ms, ₹0.300 per chip. Ten chains: 10.03 ms, ₹0.0301 per chip, saving about ₹2.7 lakh on 10 lakh chips at the cost of 20 scan pins.

Exam traps

  • Do not draw the flow as one line with test at the end: verification runs beside it from RTL to signoff, and DFT insertion comes before physical design.
  • The front end ends at the gate netlist (synthesis), not at RTL.
  • Tapeout is only the handoff of the final layout for mask making; fabrication, probe, packaging and final test come after it.
  • DFT is not testing: DFT adds hardware during design; the tester applies ATPG patterns after fabrication.
  • High stuck-at coverage does not mean zero escapes: delay defects, bridging and Iddq need their own models.

5.2 Introduction to ASICs: full-custom, standard-cell and gate-array

An ASIC (application-specific integrated circuit) is a chip made for one product or one job, such as the controller in a smart electricity meter, rather than a standard part like a memory or a microcontroller sold to everyone. ASICs are grouped by how much of the chip is made for that one design. In a full-custom ASIC every transistor is drawn for it. In a standard-cell ASIC the gates come from a pre-designed library, but every mask is still made for it. In a gate-array ASIC the transistors are already built on a stocked base wafer and only the metal wiring is custom. Programmable devices (PLDs and FPGAs) need no custom mask at all. The choice trades design cost and time against the cost, speed, power and size of each chip.

You need: the stages of the VLSI design flow, especially synthesis, physical design and tapeout.

What makes a chip an ASIC

A standard part is designed once and sold to many customers: a 74-series gate, a DRAM, a general microcontroller. An ASIC is designed for one customer's product. In between sits the ASSP (application-specific standard product): a chip for one kind of job, such as a USB controller, sold to many makers.

ASICs are classified by what is made only for this design. The less that is custom, the lower the one-time cost and the faster the turnaround, but the less efficient the chip.

TypePre-made before the designMade for this designTurnaroundTypical use
Full-customOnly the processEvery transistor and every maskLongestMemory cells, analog, fast datapaths
Standard-cell (cell-based)A library of characterised cellsEvery mask; cells placed and routed by toolsMonthsMost digital ASICs and SoCs

Some syllabi group standard-cell and gate-array ASICs together as semicustom ASICs. The non-recurring engineering cost (NRE) is the one-time cost of design, tools, masks and test programs; the unit cost is what each further chip costs.

Full-custom and standard-cell design

In full-custom design the engineer sizes and draws every transistor in a layout editor, then iterates DRC, LVS and extraction by hand. The result has the best speed, power and area, at the highest design effort. Today it is used for blocks that repeat millions of times or that no tool can build well: SRAM bit cells, standard cells themselves, PLLs, ADCs and high-speed I/O.

In standard-cell design the gates come from a library: inverters, NAND, NOR, AND-OR-invert, multiplexers, flip-flops and adder cells, each in several drive strengths. Every cell in a library has the same height and a width that depends on its function. A VDD rail runs along the top edge and a VSS rail along the bottom, so cells placed side by side in a row join their rails, and alternate rows are flipped to share a rail. The library is characterised: for each cell, files give its delay, power and area at several voltage and temperature corners, so synthesis and timing tools can use it.

Synthesis picks cells, placement puts them in rows and routing joins them in metal above the cells. A standard-cell chip still needs a full mask set, but it can hold hard macros (SRAMs, PLLs) drawn full-custom. Most digital ASICs are built this way.

Gate arrays

A masked gate array (MGA) starts from a base wafer that the foundry has already processed up to the transistors. The base is a regular array of base cells, each a few nMOS and pMOS transistors. A design defines only the interconnect: metal on the first layer turns each base cell into a gate (a NAND, an inverter), and higher metal layers wire the gates together. Because the base wafers are stocked, only the few metal and via masks are made for the design.

  • Channelled gate array: rows of base cells with fixed routing channels between the rows.
  • Channelless gate array (sea of gates): no reserved channels; wires run over unused transistors, so density is higher.
  • Structured gate array (structured ASIC): the base also holds fixed blocks such as RAM, so memory-heavy designs fit.

Not every transistor of a base can be used: routing blocks some of them, so a design can fill only part of the gates, and the usable fraction depends on the array and the design. Transistor sizes are fixed by the base, so a gate array is slower and less dense than a standard-cell chip of the same function.

Choosing a style: cost against volume

total cost = NRE + N × unit cost,   N<sub>break-even</sub> = (NRE<sub>2</sub> − NRE<sub>1</sub>) ÷ (c<sub>1</sub> − c<sub>2</sub>)

N is the number of chips and c the cost of each chip. Option 2 has the larger NRE and the smaller c; above the break-even volume it is cheaper in total.

Cost is not the only test. A standard-cell chip takes months from netlist to silicon and a bug after tapeout needs new masks (a respin), while an FPGA bug is fixed by loading a new configuration. So a product with an early deadline or an unfinished standard may start on an FPGA and move to an ASIC once the volume is known.

Worked example

Question. A smart-meter controller can be built four ways. FPGA: NRE ₹0, ₹1,800 per chip. Gate array: NRE ₹40 lakh, ₹500 per chip. Standard cell: NRE ₹2 crore, ₹250 per chip. Full custom: NRE ₹8 crore, ₹200 per chip. (a) Find the total cost of each for 50,000 meters. (b) Find the break-even volumes between neighbouring options. (c) Which is cheapest for 2,000, 50,000 and 20 lakh meters?

  1. (a) FPGA: 0 + 50,000 × ₹1,800 = ₹9.00 crore.
  2. Gate array: ₹40 lakh + 50,000 × ₹500 = ₹40 lakh + ₹250 lakh = ₹2.90 crore.
  3. Standard cell: ₹200 lakh + 50,000 × ₹250 = ₹200 lakh + ₹125 lakh = ₹3.25 crore.
  4. Full custom: ₹800 lakh + 50,000 × ₹200 = ₹800 lakh + ₹100 lakh = ₹9.00 crore.
  5. (b) FPGA to gate array: ₹40,00,000 ÷ (1,800 − 500) = 3,077 chips.
  6. Gate array to standard cell: (₹2,00,00,000 − ₹40,00,000) ÷ (500 − 250) = ₹1,60,00,000 ÷ 250 = 64,000 chips.
  7. Standard cell to full custom: (₹8 crore − ₹2 crore) ÷ (250 − 200) = ₹6,00,00,000 ÷ 50 = 12 lakh chips.
  8. (c) 2,000 is below 3,077, so the FPGA is cheapest: ₹36 lakh against ₹50 lakh for the gate array.
  9. 50,000 lies between 3,077 and 64,000, so the gate array is cheapest (₹2.90 crore), as part (a) showed.
  10. 20 lakh is above 12 lakh: full custom costs ₹8 crore + ₹40 crore = ₹48 crore against ₹2 crore + ₹50 crore = ₹52 crore for standard cell.

Answer. (a) ₹9.00 crore, ₹2.90 crore, ₹3.25 crore and ₹9.00 crore. (b) 3,077, 64,000 and 12 lakh chips. (c) FPGA for 2,000, gate array for 50,000 and full custom for 20 lakh, if the full-custom schedule (often a year or more longer) is acceptable; otherwise standard cell.

Exam traps

  • An ASIC is not the same as full-custom. Most ASICs are standard-cell designs; full-custom is one style among several.
  • A masked gate array is not an FPGA. The gate array is wired by metal masks at the foundry and cannot change afterwards; the FPGA is configured by the user.
  • Standard cells share one height, not one width. The width changes with the function and the drive strength.
  • Break-even volume is the difference in NRE divided by the difference in unit cost. NRE divided by a single unit cost means nothing.
  • The lowest total cost is not the whole answer. Time to market and the cost of a respin can rule out the cheapest style.

5.3 Verilog HDL: modules, ports, data types, operators

Verilog HDL is a hardware description language. A module is the basic design block. Its port list gives the inputs and outputs; wire and reg declare connections and stored values; operators build expressions from those values. Every bit carries one of four states: 0, 1, x or z. You write this text, compile it, and simulate it before synthesis turns it into gates or FPGA logic. The same rules cover small blocks and large chips, so exam questions test reading and writing exact expressions.

You need: the VLSI design flow for where RTL sits in the flow.

Lexical rules and module ports

Verilog source is plain text. White space separates tokens; // comments to end of line and /* */ comments across lines are ignored. Identifiers start with a letter or underscore, then letters, digits, underscore or $. Identifiers are case-sensitive; keywords are lowercase. System tasks start with $, for example $display.

Number literals use size'radix value. 4'b1010 is 4 bits binary; 8'hFF is 8 bits hex; 32'd10 is 32 bits decimal. An unsized decimal like 10 is 32 bits. Strings sit in double quotes.

A module is the design block: module m (input [3:0] a, output [3:0] y); ... endmodule. The port list gives direction input, output or inout, optional type wire or reg, width such as [3:0], and name. An instance connects ports: m u1 (.a(a), .y(y));. Named connections are safer than positional order. Ports are the module's pins.

Nets, variables, vectors, memories

A net is a connection, not storage. wire and tri are nets. A continuous assign or a gate output drives a net. A variable holds a value between procedural assignments. reg is that variable in classic Verilog; some syllabi call reg a variable. It is assigned inside always or initial. parameter names a constant.

Vectors are ordered bits. wire [3:0] v; has v[3] as MSB and v[0] as LSB. You may select one bit v[2] or a part v[3:1]. Arrays and memories add an index after the name: reg [7:0] mem [0:15]; is 16 words of 8 bits. mem[3] is a word; mem[3][5] is one bit. wire [3:0] bus [0:7]; is an array of 8 four-bit nets.

Four-state values: 0, 1, x is unknown, z is high impedance (undriven). A reg starts as x; a wire with no driver is z. Simulation uses x for uninitialized or conflicting drivers. Synthesis must not rely on x or z as data.

Operators, precedence, expression rules

Arithmetic: + - * / %. Integer division truncates toward zero; % takes the sign of the dividend. Bitwise: & | ^ ~ work bit by bit. Reduction: & | ^ ~& ~| ~^ applied to a single vector return one bit. For example, &4'b1111 is 1. Logical: && || ! return 1-bit 0 or 1; any nonzero operand is true. Relational <, <=, >, >= and equality ==, != return 1-bit 0 or 1 (x if an operand bit is x or z). Case equality === and !== also match x and z.

Shift: << is logical left shift, >> is logical right shift, and >>> is arithmetic right shift (sign-extending) for signed values. Example: 4'b0011 << 1 = 4'b0110; 4'b1000 >>> 1 = 4'b1100 for a signed operand. Conditional: cond ? a : b chooses a when cond is true. Concatenation {a,b} joins bits; replication {4{a}} repeats a. Precedence, high to low: unary operators and reductions, the power operator **, then * / %, + -, shifts, relational, equality, bitwise &, ^, |, logical &&, ||, then ?: lowest.

Expression rules: an unsized decimal is 32 bits; the result width is set by the widest operand and by the target of the assignment, so a carry out of a 4-bit sum is kept only when the target has 5 bits. Signedness matters. x propagates through most operations. To compile and simulate at command level: iverilog -o sim design.v tb.v, then vvp sim. Commercial tools use vlog and vsim. A test bench supplies inputs; see test benches and simulation.

Worked example

Question. With 4-bit unsigned values a=4, b=3, c=2, d=1, evaluate y = a + b * c > d ? (a << 1) : ~b. State y in decimal and 4-bit binary.

  1. Precedence: * before +, + before >, > before ?:. So b*c = 3*2 = 6.
  2. a + 6 = 4 + 6 = 10.
  3. 10 > 1 is true, so the condition value is 1.
  4. True branch: a << 1 = 4 << 1 = 8.
  5. False branch: ~b = ~4'b0011 = 4'b1100 = 12. It is not selected.
  6. y = 8. In four bits, 8 is written 1000.

Answer. y = 8 decimal; the 4-bit binary pattern is 1000.

Worked example 2

Question. Let p = 4'b1011 and q = 4'b0101, both unsigned. Find (a) {p[1:0], q}; (b) {{2{p[3]}}, p}; (c) p >> 2, p >>> 2 and $signed(p) >>> 2; (d) &p, |q and ^q; (e) p == 4'b1x11 and p === 4'b1011; (f) p + q assigned to a 4-bit and to a 5-bit target.

  1. (a) p[1:0] = 11. Joined in front of q = 0101 it gives 6'b110101 = 32 + 16 + 4 + 1 = 53.
  2. (b) p[3] = 1, so {2{p[3]}} = 11. With p after it: 6'b111011 = 59. As a signed number this is p sign-extended to 6 bits (−5).
  3. (c) p >> 2 = 0010 (zeros enter from the left). p is unsigned, so p >>> 2 is also 0010. $signed(p) >>> 2 copies the sign bit: 1110, that is −5 shifted to −2.
  4. (d) &p = 1 & 0 & 1 & 1 = 0. |q = 1 because q has a 1. ^q = 0 ^ 1 ^ 0 ^ 1 = 0 (an even number of 1s).
  5. (e) p == 4'b1x11 is x: bit 2 compares 0 with x, so the answer is unknown. p === 4'b1011 is 1: case equality compares x and z as values too.
  6. (f) p + q = 11 + 5 = 16 = 5'b10000. A 4-bit target keeps only 0000 and loses the carry; a 5-bit target keeps 10000.

Answer. (a) 110101 = 53; (b) 111011 = 59; (c) 0010, 0010, 1110; (d) 0, 1, 0; (e) x and 1; (f) 0000 in 4 bits, 10000 in 5 bits. All six results were printed by a simulator run.

Exam traps

  • Bitwise ~ is not logical !. ~4'b0011 is 1100 = 12; !4'b0011 is 0 because the operand is nonzero (true).
  • Reduction & returns one bit; bitwise & returns a vector. &4'b1111 is 1, while 4'b1111 & 4'b1010 is 1010.
  • An unsized decimal literal like 10 is 32 bits. Mixing it with a 4-bit vector can widen the result; write 4'd10 when 4 bits are intended.
  • Conditional ?: has very low precedence. a + b > c ? x : y groups as (a + b > c) ? x : y, not as a + (b > c ? x : y).
  • A wire cannot store a value assigned inside an always block. Use a reg variable for procedural storage, and a wire for continuous drive.

5.4 Gate-level, dataflow and behavioural modelling

A digital circuit can be written in Verilog at three levels. Gate-level modelling instantiates primitive gates and switches. Dataflow modelling writes continuous Boolean equations with assign. Behavioural modelling writes algorithms inside initial and always blocks. All three can describe the same multiplexer, counter or FSM. The level you choose controls how close the code sits to the netlist, how easy it is to read, and what a synthesis tool can accept. Designers use gate level for netlists, dataflow for datapaths, and behavioural for control.

You need: Verilog HDL basics for modules, ports, data types and operators, and the switch view of the transistor from MOS transistor theory.

Three levels, one circuit

Gate-level modelling is structural. You place gates by name: and, or, not, nand, nor, xor, xnor. Each gate instance has output first, then inputs. A 2-input AND is and g1(y, a, b);. Output y is a wire. Gate primitives have no memory. A user-defined primitive, or UDP, adds a truth-table gate when the built-in set is not enough. A UDP declares a table of input combinations and output values. Switch-level primitives such as nmos, pmos and cmos model transistors as switches. They are used to model MOS circuits such as the CMOS inverter below, and for cell-level simulation, not for ordinary synthesis. How the transistors themselves behave is covered under MOS transistor theory.

Verilog program.

module inv_sw (input a, output y);
  supply1 vdd;  supply0 gnd;
  pmos p1 (y, vdd, a);   // on when a = 0
  nmos n1 (y, gnd, a);   // on when a = 1
endmodule

Output

a=0 y=1
a=1 y=0

Dataflow modelling is equation-based. A continuous assignment assign y = a & b; drives y whenever a or b changes. The right side is an expression, not a sequence of statements. The conditional operator ?: builds a mux. Dataflow suits adders, comparators and multiplexers because the equations match the datapath.

Behavioural modelling is algorithmic. An initial block runs once at time zero. An always block repeats forever. The sensitivity list says when it runs: always @(*) for combinational logic, always @(posedge clk) for a clocked register. Inside you write if, case, for and while. Synthesis turns these into gates, but only a subset is synthesizable.

Dataflow: equations as hardware

A continuous assignment targets a wire. wire [3:0] y; assign y = sel ? b : a; is a 4-bit 2-to-1 mux. The expression uses bitwise operators &, |, ^, ~, logical operators &&, ||, !, reduction operators such as &a, and concatenation {a,b}. The conditional operator cond ? x : y returns x when cond is 1 and y when cond is 0. For sel=1, the mux gives b; for sel=0, it gives a.

Y = sel ? B : A

Y is the 4-bit output; sel is the 1-bit select; A and B are 4-bit inputs. sel=1 selects B, and sel=0 selects A.

Continuous assignments run in parallel. Order in the file does not matter. A change on any right-side signal schedules a new evaluation. This is why dataflow code reads like hardware. Use one assign per output or per equation. Do not assign the same wire from two different assign statements; that creates multiple drivers and X in simulation.

The synthesizable subset allows assign with operators, conditional expressions, concatenation and bit selects. It does not allow delays such as assign #5 y = a; in synthesis, real arithmetic, or assignments to reg. A dataflow mux maps to gates or to a mux cell. The worked example uses the same equation.

Behavioural: processes and always blocks

Behavioural code runs as a process. initial executes once; it is common in test benches and FPGA initialisation, but not in ASIC synthesis. always repeats. A combinational block uses always @(*). A clocked block uses always @(posedge clk). The star means every signal read in the block is in the sensitivity list. An incomplete list can simulate but synthesis assumes all reads, so hardware and simulation drift apart.

Use blocking = in combinational always blocks: statements execute in order, like gates settling. Use nonblocking <= in sequential always blocks: all right sides are sampled at the clock edge, then left sides update together. Mixing them in one clocked block creates races and can infer different registers.

if and case are synthesizable. A case must cover all input patterns or have a default. A for loop with a constant bound is unrolled into parallel hardware. A while loop with a nonconstant bound is not synthesizable. A combinational always block must assign every output on every path, or synthesis infers a latch.

An FSM uses two common styles. The two-block style has one clocked always block for the state register and one combinational always block for next state and outputs. The one-block style puts state register, next-state and output logic in a single clocked always block. Two-block code separates sequential and combinational logic clearly. One-block code is shorter but easier to make a mistake with blocking and nonblocking.

Choosing the level

Use gate level for a netlist from synthesis or a cell library. Use dataflow for an equation: adder, comparator, mux, bitwise unit. Use behavioural for control: FSM, handshake, counter, register file. A D flip-flop is a clocked always block; at gate level it is a library cell. Most RTL mixes dataflow and behavioural code in one module.

Question. A 4-bit 2-to-1 multiplexer selects A when sel = 0 and B when sel = 1. Write it at gate level, in dataflow and in behavioural style, and find Y for A = 4'b1010, B = 4'b0110 with sel = 1 and with sel = 0.

module mux2_gate (input a, input b, input sel, output y);
  wire nsel, t0, t1;
  not g0 (nsel, sel);
  and g1 (t0, a, nsel);
  and g2 (t1, b, sel);
  or  g3 (y, t0, t1);
endmodule

  mux2_gate m1 (A[1], B[1], sel, Y[1]);
  mux2_gate m2 (A[2], B[2], sel, Y[2]);
  mux2_gate m3 (A[3], B[3], sel, Y[3]);
endmodule
// dataflow
module mux4b_df (input [3:0] A, B, input sel, output [3:0] Y);
  assign Y = sel ? B : A;
endmodule
// behavioural
module mux4b_beh (input [3:0] A, B, input sel, output reg [3:0] Y);
  always @(*)
    if (sel) Y = B;
    else     Y = A;
endmodule

  3. Dataflow, sel = 1: the condition is true, so the ?: expression returns B. Y = 0110.
  4. Behavioural, sel = 1: the if branch runs, Y = B = 0110.
  5. sel = 0: nsel = 1, so every bit gives y = a, and the dataflow and behavioural forms take A. All three give Y = 1010.
  6. Check: 0110 is 0×8 + 1×4 + 1×2 + 0×1 = 6, the value of B, and 1010 is 10, the value of A.

  • Using an initial block for ASIC synthesis. Initial blocks are for test benches and for some FPGA power-up states.

5.5 Test benches and simulation

A test bench is a Verilog module that drives a design under test (DUT) with inputs and checks its outputs in simulation. It exists because a design can compile and still be wrong. The bench supplies clock, reset and data vectors, waits for the DUT to settle, compares each output with a golden value, and reports pass or fail. It also writes a VCD waveform file for inspection. Engineers use test benches before synthesis, during FPGA bring-up, and whenever a block changes and must be rechecked.

You need: Verilog HDL basics for modules and ports, and modelling styles for initial and always blocks.

Test bench parts and stimulus

A test bench has no ports. It declares reg signals for inputs it drives, wire signals for outputs it observes, and instantiates the DUT by module name. The connection can be by name, as in mux2 uut(.a(a), .b(b), .s(s), .y(y)); here uut is the instance name and y is the observed output. A clock needs three parts. First a timescale directive, `timescale 1ns/1ps, so that #1 means 1 ns. Second, a clock register that starts at 0: reg clk = 0;, or an initial block that sets clk = 0;. Third, the free-running always block: always #5 clk = ~clk;. With a 1 ns/1 ps timescale, #5 is 5 ns, so the half period is 5 ns and the full period is 10 ns. Without the zero start the register holds x, ~x is still x, and the clock line never toggles, so every clocked block in the bench stalls. Reset is driven in an initial block: set rst=1, wait #20, then rst=0. Stimulus is also an initial block. Assign inputs, wait a delay, read outputs, and repeat. A task groups one vector: drive a, b and s, wait #10, then compare y. $display prints one event; $monitor prints again whenever any listed signal changes. $dumpfile("dump.vcd") names the value change dump (VCD) file and $dumpvars(0, tb) records all signals below the test bench. Some syllabi call this verification by simulation; it uses vectors, not formal proof.

Simulation cycle and races

At each simulation time, the simulator runs active events, then inactive events, then nonblocking updates. A blocking assignment (=) changes the variable at once. A nonblocking assignment (<=) computes the right side now and updates at the end of the time step, in the nonblocking region. Clocked DUTs should use <= so all flip-flops sample the old values together. The test bench must respect that order. If the bench drives a clocked DUT with blocking assignments at posedge clk, the DUT may see the new value in the same time step and the result depends on event order. This is a race. If the bench samples y with $display at the same posedge, it may print the old y before the nonblocking update. Fix: drive inputs away from the active edge, use nonblocking in sequential logic, and check outputs after a small delay such as #1, or on the opposite edge. A combinational DUT also needs time to settle; a zero-delay compare after changing inputs can catch y while it is still x.

Self-checking, vectors and VCD

A self-checking test bench holds the expected value for each vector and compares it with the DUT output. It keeps an error count, prints FAIL with the time and vector when y differs, and prints PASS at the end only if errors=0. Golden vectors come from a truth table, a reference model, or a known-good simulation. For long tests, put vectors in a file and load them with $readmemh("vectors.hex", mem); then loop over the array, drive inputs, wait, and compare. This separates data from code and lets you reuse the same bench. For clocked logic, sample a short delay after the active edge, for example #1 y_sample = y; after posedge clk, so that the flip-flop's clock-to-Q delay has passed. Setup and hold times are constraints on the DUT inputs relative to that edge; they are not a delay after which the bench samples. $dumpfile and $dumpvars write a VCD file that a waveform viewer can open. The VCD is for debug, not for pass/fail. Production test of the finished chip is a separate job, covered under VLSI testing and design for testability.

Compile and run

Simulation is a two-step job: compile the DUT and test bench together, then run the top test-bench module. With Icarus Verilog, a common command is iverilog -o tb.out tb.v mux2.v; the first file can be the bench and the second the DUT. Run it with vvp tb.out. The $display and $monitor lines appear on the terminal. The $dumpfile and $dumpvars calls create dump.vcd; open it with gtkwave dump.vcd. Other simulators use different commands, such as vlog and vsim, but the idea is the same. Always end the stimulus with $finish so the simulator stops. If the transcript shows PASS and no FAIL lines, the vectors passed. Read the first FAIL line, note the time, inputs and expected value, then open the VCD near that time. That is the normal debug loop.

5.6 Programmable logic devices and FPGA architecture

A programmable logic device (PLD) is an IC whose logic function you set after manufacture. Instead of drawing new masks, you store connections or truth tables in programmable cells. This makes PLDs useful for prototypes, low-volume products, education and glue logic. FPGA (field-programmable gate array) is the largest PLD family: it builds arbitrary logic from lookup tables, flip-flops, routing and I/O blocks. This chapter explains the PLD families, the blocks inside an FPGA, and how SRAM, flash and antifuse programming differ at block level.

You need: modelling styles for the synthesizable Verilog subset, and Boolean functions in sum-of-products form from digital electronics.

PLD families and programmable cells

Programmable logic devices (PLDs) let you set a logic function after the chip is made. A masked gate array is fixed once its metal masks are made; a PLD stores its connections in programmable elements. In a programmable logic array (PLA), both the AND plane and the OR plane are programmable, so each output is an OR of selected product terms. A product term is one AND of input literals, where a literal is a variable or its complement. A programmable array logic (PAL) keeps the AND plane programmable and fixes the OR plane; it is simpler and faster. Programmable inversion lets an input or output be used in true or complemented form. Expander logic feeds extra product terms into a macrocell without using output pins. A macrocell is the programmable logic cell of a complex PLD (CPLD); it contains product-term logic, a register and polarity control. A CPLD joins several PAL-like blocks with a central interconnect. A mask-programmable gate array (MPGA) is programmed by custom wiring masks, so its function is fixed at fabrication. An FPGA is field-programmable: the user loads a configuration after packaging.

FPGA logic, I/O and routing

An FPGA logic cell is built around a lookup table (LUT) and a flip-flop. An n-input LUT stores 2ⁿ truth-table bits; a multiplexer chosen by the n inputs selects one bit. A 4-input LUT has 16 bits and can implement any 4-variable Boolean function. The flip-flop after the LUT stores the result for sequential logic; a bypass multiplexer selects combinational or registered output. A logic block packs several LUTs, flip-flops and carry logic. In Xilinx (now AMD) FPGAs it is the configurable logic block (CLB); Intel FPGAs call it a logic array block (LAB). Not every FPGA uses LUTs: the Actel (now Microchip) antifuse families built their logic module from multiplexers; the ACT 1 module is three 2:1 multiplexers and an OR gate, and a function is made by tying its inputs to signals, 0 or 1. Input/output blocks (IOBs) connect pads to the array with registers and drive strength. Routing runs through horizontal and vertical channels; a switch box at each crossing connects wire segments. Routing delay often dominates. Block RAM (BRAM) gives on-chip memory. DSP slices give fast multiply-accumulate. Clock management blocks such as phase-locked loops (PLLs) clean and multiply clocks. All these choices are set by configuration memory.

NLUT = 2n

NLUT is the number of configuration bits in an n-input LUT; n is the number of LUT inputs.

Configuration memory and mapping

FPGA programming is stored in configuration memory. SRAM-based FPGAs use volatile SRAM cells that drive pass transistors. They lose configuration at power-off, so a bitstream must be reloaded from flash or a host. Their advantage is unlimited reprogramming and standard CMOS fabrication. Flash-based FPGAs store charge on a floating gate; they keep configuration without power and can be reprogrammed, but need special process steps. Antifuse FPGAs blow a one-time link; they are non-volatile and hard to reverse, used where radiation or security matters. Older PLDs and CPLDs used EPROM or EEPROM cells, which are erased by UV light or electrically. Some syllabi call this choice the switching (or programming) technology. At block level, an SRAM cell, a floating-gate transistor or a blown antifuse controls a switch. Synthesis maps Verilog to LUTs; packing groups them; placement and routing choose sites and wires; bitstream generation writes the memory. See floorplanning, placement and routing for ASIC placement and routing, and IP cores and soft-core processors for reusing soft cores.

Implementing logic with ROM, PAL and PLA

A ROM implements logic by storing the truth table: n inputs address 2n words, and each output is one bit of the word, so the size is 2n × m bits for m outputs. It needs no minimisation, but its size doubles with every input. A PLA needs the functions in sum-of-products form; its size is set by the number of distinct product terms, and outputs share any term they have in common. A PAL also needs sum-of-products form, but each output has its own fixed set of AND gates, so a shared term is built twice.

A PLA is specified by a programming table: one row per product term, a 1, 0 or – for each input (true, complemented or not used), and a 1 under each output whose OR gate uses that term. The second worked example fills one in.

  2. For A = 1, B = 1, C = 0, D = 1 the inputs form the address ABCD = 1101 = 13. A·B = 1 and C·D = 0, so f = 1 OR 0 = 1, the bit stored at address 13.
  3. Useful fraction after packing = 1 − 0.15 = 0.85. Required LUTs = 3,000 ÷ 0.85 = 3529.4. Round up because LUT count is an integer: 3530 LUTs.

Answer. The LUT needs 16 configuration bits, f = 1 for A=1, B=1, C=0, D=1, and 3530 LUTs are needed after 15% packing loss.

Worked example 2

Question. Implement F1 = AB + AC′ and F2 = AB + BC with (a) a ROM, (b) a PLA and (c) a PAL. Give the ROM size and truth table, the PLA programming table, and the number of AND gates (product terms) each programmable device needs.

  1. (a) Three inputs address 2³ = 8 words; two outputs give 2 bits per word. ROM size = 8 × 2 = 16 bits.
  2. Truth table, ABC → F1 F2: 000 → 00, 001 → 00, 010 → 00, 011 → 01 (BC = 1), 100 → 10 (AC′ = 1), 101 → 00, 110 → 11 (AB = 1), 111 → 11 (AB = 1).
  3. So F1 = Σm(4, 6, 7) and F2 = Σm(3, 6, 7); the ROM stores these eight words.
  4. (b) The distinct product terms are P1 = AB, P2 = AC′ and P3 = BC. F1 = P1 + P2 and F2 = P1 + P3 share P1, so the PLA needs only 3 AND gates.
  5. PLA table (inputs A B C, outputs F1 F2): P1 = AB → 1 1 –, 1 1. P2 = AC′ → 1 – 0, 1 0. P3 = BC → – 1 1, 0 1.
  6. (c) In a PAL each output has its own AND gates, so AB is built twice: F1 uses AB and AC′, F2 uses AB and BC, 4 AND gates in all, and the PAL must offer at least 2 terms per output.
  7. Check one row: ABC = 110 gives AB = 1, so F1 = F2 = 1 in every form, matching word 6 of the ROM.

Answer. ROM: 8 × 2 = 16 bits, F1 = Σm(4, 6, 7), F2 = Σm(3, 6, 7). PLA: 3 product terms (AB shared). PAL: 4 product terms, 2 per output.

Exam traps

  • An n-input LUT is not an n-input gate; it stores 2ⁿ truth-table bits and behaves like a small memory.
  • An n-input LUT needs 2ⁿ configuration bits, not n bits; a 4-input LUT needs 16 bits.
  • In a PAL the OR plane is fixed, so each output gets a fixed number of product terms — count them per output, not as a shared pool. In a PLA the product terms are shared, and each output ORs a programmable subset of them.
  • SRAM FPGA configuration memory is volatile like user SRAM, but it holds the logic configuration, not program data.
  • Routing and switch-box delay can dominate, so equal LUT counts do not guarantee equal speed.

5.7 Floorplanning, placement and routing

Floorplanning, placement and routing turn a gate netlist (a list of gates and their connections) into physical shapes on the die (the silicon rectangle). Floorplanning sets the core, I/O pins, large blocks and power grid. Placement fixes each standard cell. Clock-tree synthesis adds buffers so the clock reaches registers with small skew. Routing draws wires and vias. Signoff checks design rules, layout versus schematic, timing and power. The result is GDSII (the final layout database) sent to the foundry. Almost every digital standard-cell chip goes through this back-end flow; hand-drawn analog and full-custom blocks skip automatic placement but still end in GDSII and signoff.

You need: the VLSI design flow for where physical design sits, the layout design rules from CMOS fabrication and layout, and wire delay from the Elmore delay model.

Floorplanning: core, blocks and power

Floorplanning fixes the physical frame before any cell moves. The core is the logic area inside the I/O ring. The floorplan chooses core width W, core height H, aspect ratio H ÷ W, I/O pin positions, hard macro positions and the power grid. A hard macro is a fixed layout block such as an SRAM or PLL. A soft block can change shape. Pins sit on the core edge or die edge, near the signals they serve. The power grid uses wide VDD and VSS straps to limit IR drop, the voltage lost in the resistive supply network.

Utilization measures how much core area is occupied. Very high utilization saves die area but can block routing. Whitespace is not waste: routing wires, clock buffers and decoupling cells (local charge stores) need it. Aspect ratio near 1 gives shorter average wires; a tall thin core may suit a memory-heavy block.

U = A<sub>used</sub> ÷ A<sub>core</sub>,  A<sub>core</sub> = W × H

U is utilization; Aused is area of blocks plus standard cells (fixed-height gate layouts); Acore is core area in mm²; W and H are core width and height in mm.

Placement and clock-tree synthesis

Placement assigns coordinates to every standard cell inside the core rows. Global placement spreads cells to reduce wirelength and congestion while ignoring exact row sites. Detailed placement then legalizes cells onto legal non-overlapping sites. A common wirelength estimate is half-perimeter wirelength: HPWL = Σnets [(max x − min x) + (max y − min y)]. HPWL is the sum over nets of half the bounding-box perimeter; x and y are pin coordinates. Congestion appears when demanded routing tracks exceed available tracks in a region. Timing-driven placement gives extra weight to nets on critical paths (slowest timing paths), using the wire-delay estimates taught under interconnect parameters and the Elmore delay model.

Clock-tree synthesis (CTS) runs after placement. The clock net drives every register, so it is a large load. CTS inserts buffers or inverters in a tree to balance this load. Skew is the difference in clock arrival time between registers. Insertion delay is the time from clock source to a register. Small skew and short insertion delay are the goals; useful skew can deliberately delay some registers to help timing. CTS must precede routing because clock buffers become real cells that routing must connect.

Routing and signoff

Routing turns connections into metal wires. Global routing assigns each net to a sequence of routing regions (global cells or channels) and estimates congestion; it does not pick tracks. Detailed routing chooses exact tracks, wire widths, spacing and vias. A via is a metal-to-metal connection between layers; each via adds resistance (its capacitance is small next to the wire it joins) and is a possible failure point, so routers use as few as they can. Maze routing searches a grid for a shortest legal path around obstacles. Channel routing orders horizontal and vertical segments inside a channel, often with a left-edge rule. The layout design rules (see CMOS fabrication and layout) set minimum width, spacing and enclosure.

Crosstalk is unwanted coupling between nearby wires: an aggressor switches and injects a glitch or delay into a victim. Shielding, wider spacing and layer choice reduce it. Signoff checks the finished layout. DRC (design rule check) checks geometry. LVS (layout versus schematic) checks that the layout netlist matches the source netlist. Static timing analysis checks setup and hold, and power/IR-drop checks check supply integrity. The final database is GDSII, sent to the foundry.

Worked example

Question. A core is 3.0 mm × 3.0 mm. Four hard blocks have areas 2.0, 1.5, 1.0 and 0.5 mm². Standard cells occupy 1.8 mm². Find block area, used area, utilization, whitespace, and whether 20% extra area for routing fits.

  1. Core area = 3.0 × 3.0 = 9.0 mm².
  2. Block area = 2.0 + 1.5 + 1.0 + 0.5 = 5.0 mm².
  3. Used area = 5.0 + 1.8 = 6.8 mm².
  4. Utilization = 6.8 ÷ 9.0 = 0.7556 = 75.6%.
  5. Whitespace = 9.0 − 6.8 = 2.2 mm².
  6. Extra area at 20% = 0.20 × 6.8 = 1.36 mm². Total = 6.8 + 1.36 = 8.16 mm².
  7. Spare area = 9.0 − 8.16 = 0.84 mm². Since 8.16 ≤ 9.0, the extra area fits.

Answer. Block area is 5.0 mm², used area is 6.8 mm², utilization is 75.6%, whitespace is 2.2 mm², and with 20% extra area the total is 8.16 mm², so it fits with 0.84 mm² spare.

Worked example 2

Question. Four cells sit in four slots of a placement grid: A at (1, 1), B at (5, 1), C at (1, 4) and D at (5, 4), in grid units of 10 µm. Three nets join them: n1 = {A, D}, n2 = {B, C}, n3 = {A, B, C}. Find the total HPWL, then the total after swapping A and B, and say whether a placer should keep the swap.

  1. HPWL of a net = (max x − min x) + (max y − min y) over its pins.
  2. Before: n1, A(1, 1) to D(5, 4): 4 + 3 = 7. n2, B(5, 1) to C(1, 4): 4 + 3 = 7. n3, A, B, C: x from 1 to 5 and y from 1 to 4, 4 + 3 = 7. Total = 21 units = 210 µm.
  3. Swap A and B: now A is at (5, 1) and B at (1, 1).
  4. After: n1, A(5, 1) to D(5, 4): 0 + 3 = 3. n2, B(1, 1) to C(1, 4): 0 + 3 = 3. n3 still spans x 1 to 5 and y 1 to 4: 7. Total = 13 units = 130 µm.
  5. Change = 21 − 13 = 8 units, a 38 % cut (8 ÷ 21 = 0.381), so the placer keeps the swap; an iterative placer tries many such swaps and keeps the ones that cut the cost.

Answer. HPWL falls from 21 units (210 µm) to 13 units (130 µm) after the swap, a 38 % reduction, so the swap is kept.

Exam traps

  • High utilization is not automatically good: above a point it causes routing congestion, detours and timing loss.
  • Placement is not routing: short placement wirelength can still route badly, and legal placement does not prove timing.
  • Global routing is not detailed routing: global assigns regions and estimates congestion; detailed picks exact tracks and vias.
  • DRC clean is not signoff clean: DRC checks geometry only; LVS, timing, power and IR drop are separate checks.
  • Clock skew is not always driven to zero: useful skew can improve timing, and insertion delay matters too.

5.8 IP cores and soft-core processors

An IP core is a pre-designed, pre-verified circuit block that a team reuses inside a larger chip. Instead of designing every function from scratch, a system-on-chip (SoC) team buys or licenses processors, memories, buses and peripherals, then integrates them. IP cores exist because verification and timing closure cost more than drawing logic. They appear in phones, cars, set-top boxes and FPGA boards. The same idea covers a hard memory macro, a firm DSP block and a soft processor written in Verilog.

You need: the VLSI design flow for the design flow and programmable logic and FPGAs for FPGA resources.

IP core classes

IP cores are classified by what the supplier delivers and how much the buyer can change.

Hard IP arrives as a mask-level layout, often GDSII. Its transistors, wires and pins are fixed for one fabrication process. Hard IP gives predictable speed, power and area, but it cannot be moved to another process without redesign. Memories, PLLs and high-speed SerDes are common examples. The supplier has already verified the layout, so the buyer's risk is lower.

Firm IP arrives as a gate-level netlist or a characterized RTL structure for a selected process. Placement may have some freedom, but the structure is largely fixed. Firm IP sits between hard and soft IP in flexibility and predictability.

Soft IP arrives as synthesizable register-transfer level (RTL) code, usually Verilog or VHDL. The buyer synthesizes it with its own library, clock and constraints. Soft IP is portable and configurable, but the buyer owns timing closure, power and physical risk. A soft IP block can be optimised for area or speed, but poor constraints can break it.

The license matters. An IP license states how many times the block may be used, whether it may be modified, and whether it may be redistributed. A license is not ownership of the source.

Integrating an IP core

Integration starts with a wrapper. A wrapper is RTL placed around an IP core. It converts the core's native signals to the SoC bus, adds register slices, and isolates the core from the rest of the chip. The wrapper also holds the core's configuration registers.

The core connects to an on-chip bus. Common examples are Arm AMBA AXI and AHB. AXI uses separate address and data channels with handshake signals; AHB is a shared bus with an arbiter. The cycle-level protocol is beyond most syllabi; for integration, know that the bus defines how the core is addressed and how transfers complete.

Every core with registers or memory that software reads or writes needs an address range in the memory map; a PLL or a purely streaming datapath may have none. The map lists base address, size and access type for each range, and firmware uses these addresses. A range should start on a multiple of its size, and two ranges must never overlap: an overlap corrupts data with no error. The second worked example checks a map.

Clock and reset are next. State the clock frequency, source and reset polarity. Check whether reset is synchronous or asynchronous. If a core runs on a different clock from the bus, add clock-domain crossing (CDC) logic: a synchronizer for single-bit control, or an asynchronous FIFO for data. Never pass multi-bit data through a simple two-flop synchronizer.

Power domains add isolation cells, level shifters and retention. A core that can be powered off must not drive live signals. Its outputs are clamped by isolation cells.

Verify at block level before integration. Run the IP testbench, check bus protocol assertions, then run SoC-level tests. Check the license terms for simulation models and synthesis. An embedded core is also hard to test, because its pins are buried. The IEEE 1500 standard puts a test wrapper of boundary cells round the core, and a test access mechanism (TAM) carries the core's patterns from the chip pins to the wrapper.

Soft-core processors

A soft-core processor is a CPU delivered as synthesizable RTL. It is not software. Synthesis maps its registers, adders and control logic to FPGA lookup tables, flip-flops, block RAM and DSP slices, or to standard cells in an ASIC. The result is real hardware that consumes area and power.

Soft cores are configurable. A team may choose cache size, bus width, debug support or custom instructions. They are useful in FPGAs because the CPU can be added after the board is built. In an ASIC, a soft core competes with a hard processor core. Common examples are MicroBlaze on AMD (Xilinx) FPGAs, Nios V, a RISC-V core, on Intel FPGAs, and open RISC-V cores such as PicoRV32.

A hard processor core is fixed silicon, placed and routed by the vendor for one process. It usually gives higher clock speed, lower power and smaller area than a soft core, but it cannot be changed. A soft core gives flexibility and process portability, but the buyer pays in LUTs, timing effort and power.

On-chip buses connect both kinds of core. AMBA AXI and AHB are interface examples, not processor architecture. Processor pipeline design belongs to computer architecture courses.

When you count soft cores in an FPGA, count every resource. A core may fit by lookup tables but fail on DSP slices or block RAM. The worked example shows this.

Worked example

Question. An FPGA has 12,000 LUTs, 20 DSP slices and 30 BRAMs. A soft-core processor needs 1,800 LUTs, 4 DSP slices and 6 BRAMs. How many cores fit, and which resource limits the count?

  1. LUT limit: 12000 ÷ 1800 = 6.67 cores, so only 6 complete cores fit by LUT count.
  2. DSP limit: 20 ÷ 4 = 5 cores.
  3. BRAM limit: 30 ÷ 6 = 5 cores.
  4. Take the smallest limit: minimum of 6, 5 and 5 is 5 cores.
  5. DSP slices and BRAMs give the same limit, so both limit the count.

Answer. 5 soft cores fit, limited equally by DSP slices and BRAMs.

Worked example 2

Question. An FPGA SoC has a soft CPU and five slaves on its bus: a 16 KB boot ROM at 0x0000_0000, a 64 KB SRAM at 0x2000_0000, and a 4 KB register block each for a UART, a timer and GPIO, placed one after another from 0x4000_0000. A 32 KB DSP accelerator is proposed at 0x2000_8000. Write the end address of every range, find the clash, and move the DSP block to a legal base in 0x5000_0000 onwards.

  1. End address = base + size − 1. In hex, 16 KB = 0x4000, 64 KB = 0x10000, 4 KB = 0x1000 and 32 KB = 0x8000.
  2. Boot ROM: 0x0000_0000 + 0x4000 − 1 = 0x0000_3FFF.
  3. SRAM: 0x2000_0000 + 0x10000 − 1 = 0x2000_FFFF.
  4. UART 0x4000_0000 to 0x4000_0FFF; timer 0x4000_1000 to 0x4000_1FFF; GPIO 0x4000_2000 to 0x4000_2FFF.
  5. Proposed DSP: 0x2000_8000 + 0x8000 − 1 = 0x2000_FFFF. Its whole range lies inside the SRAM range (0x2000_0000 to 0x2000_FFFF): the top 32 KB of SRAM would be hidden or corrupted.
  6. Move it to 0x5000_0000. That base is a multiple of 0x8000 (32 KB), so the range 0x5000_0000 to 0x5000_7FFF is aligned and overlaps nothing.

Answer. ROM 0x0000_0000–0x0000_3FFF, SRAM 0x2000_0000–0x2000_FFFF, UART 0x4000_0000–0x4000_0FFF, timer 0x4000_1000–0x4000_1FFF, GPIO 0x4000_2000–0x4000_2FFF. The DSP at 0x2000_8000 overlaps the top half of the SRAM; at 0x5000_0000 it runs to 0x5000_7FFF with no clash.

Exam traps

  • A soft core is not software. It is synthesizable hardware RTL and still consumes LUTs, memories, DSP slices and power.
  • Hard IP is fixed to one fabrication process. You cannot copy GDSII to another foundry node without redesign.
  • Soft IP portability does not remove integration work: bus adaptation, clock-domain crossing, reset and timing closure remain.
  • Counting cores by lookup tables alone is wrong. Check every resource: DSP slices, block RAM, I/O pins and power.
  • A license is not ownership. Check modification, reuse and redistribution rights before shipping.

Takeaways

  1. The ASIC design flow runs specification, architecture, RTL, verification, synthesis, DFT insertion, physical design and signoff to tapeout; fabrication, wafer probe, packaging and final test follow.
  2. Test is designed in: scan chains and BIST are added at the netlist stage, ATPG patterns come from the netlist, and splitting one 5,000-flop chain into ten cuts scan test time about ten times.
  3. ASICs differ in what is made for one design: every mask (full-custom, standard-cell), only metal masks (gate array) or nothing (PLD, FPGA); total cost = NRE + N × unit cost decides by volume.
  4. A Verilog module with its ports is the unit of design; nets are driven continuously, variables hold values between procedural assignments, and widths and x values change what an expression gives.
  5. Gate-level, dataflow and behavioural code can describe the same circuit; only the synthesizable subset becomes hardware, and a combinational always block must assign every output on every path.
  6. A test bench has no ports: it drives the DUT, waits, compares with expected values and reports PASS or FAIL, so errors are caught in simulation instead of in silicon.
  7. An FPGA is an array of LUTs, flip-flops, routing and I/O blocks set by configuration memory; ROMs, PALs and PLAs implement the same logic with fixed or programmable AND and OR planes.
  8. Floorplanning, placement and routing decide wire length, congestion, timing and power; utilization and HPWL put numbers on a floorplan and a placement.
  9. IP cores (soft, firm or hard) and soft-core processors let an SoC reuse verified blocks, with wrappers, an aligned memory map without overlaps, clock-domain crossing and a test wrapper for buried cores.

Understand Describe the architecture of an FPGA: logic cells, LUTs, flip-flops, routing and I/O blocks. How is the function configured? 12 marks

Points to cover. Model answer.

  1. An FPGA (field-programmable gate array) is a chip of identical logic blocks in a grid, wire segments between them, and I/O blocks round the edge. Every choice (the logic each block does, which wires join, how each pin behaves) is held in configuration memory that the user loads.
  2. Logic block: a configurable logic block (CLB, the Xilinx name; LAB in Intel parts) holds several logic cells. Each cell is a lookup table (LUT), a flip-flop, a bypass multiplexer that picks the LUT output or the registered output, and fast carry logic for adders.
  3. LUT: an n-input LUT is a 2ⁿ × 1 memory whose n inputs are the address. It stores the truth table, so it implements any function of n inputs. A 4-input LUT holds 2⁴ = 16 bits; a 6-input LUT holds 64.
  4. Example: f = A·B + C·D in a 4-input LUT. The 16 stored bits, address ABCD from 0000 to 1111:
    Address (decimal)0–234–678–101112–15
    f0101011
  5. For A = 1, B = 1, C = 0, D = 1 the address is 1101 = 13, and the bit stored there is 1, as A·B = 1 requires.
  6. Routing: horizontal and vertical channels of wire segments of several lengths. A connection box joins a block's pins to the channel wires; a switch box at each crossing joins segments. Each switch is a pass transistor or multiplexer controlled by one configuration bit. Routing often takes most of the delay and area of an FPGA.
  7. I/O blocks (IOBs) sit at the pads: input and output buffers with selectable voltage standards, drive strength and slew rate, plus input and output registers.
  8. Hard blocks: block RAM, DSP slices (multiply-accumulate), clock managers (PLLs), and in large parts transceivers and processor cores.
  9. Configuration: synthesis maps the HDL to LUTs and flip-flops, packing groups them into blocks, placement and routing choose sites and switches, and bitstream generation writes every configuration bit. In an SRAM FPGA the bitstream is loaded at each power-up from flash or a host, because SRAM is volatile; flash FPGAs keep it without power, and antifuse FPGAs are programmed once.
  10. Trade-off against an ASIC: no NRE and no mask, a new bitstream fixes a bug, and a board can be ready in hours. The price is a higher unit cost, a lower clock rate and more power and area for the same function, because programmable switches and LUTs replace fixed wires and gates.
  11. Closing check with numbers: 3,000 such functions at 15 % packing loss need 3,000 ÷ 0.85 = 3,529.4, so 3,530 LUTs, and 3,530 × 16 = 56,480 configuration bits for the LUTs alone, before any routing bits.
Understand What is design for testability? Explain scan insertion, ATPG and BIST, and where the DUT and tester fit in the flow. 12 marks

Points to cover. Why testing matters: stuck-at and other faults, defective parts must be screened; Scan: flip-flops replaced by scan flip-flops chained into shift registers, giving controllability and observability; ATPG: excite a fault, propagate to an output, generate compact pattern sets; BIST: on-chip LFSR pattern generator plus signature analyser (MISR); Flow position: DFT insertion after synthesis, patterns after netlist, DUT tested on ATE with test program; Metrics: fault coverage, pattern count, test time and test cost.

Model answer.

  1. Design for testability (DFT) is hardware added during design so that a tester can control and observe the inside of a chip. Manufacturing defects (shorts, opens, bad vias) are certain on some dies, and a tester sees only the pins, so without DFT most internal nodes cannot be tested.
  2. Faults are modelled so that tests can be computed. The stuck-at model treats a line as fixed at 0 or 1; transition and path delay models treat it as too slow; bridging models treat two lines as shorted.
  3. A test must excite the fault and make it visible. For a 2-input AND gate y = a·b:
    FaultTest (a, b)Good yFaulty y
    y stuck-at-0(1, 1)10
    a stuck-at-1(0, 1)01
    b stuck-at-1(1, 0)01
    a stuck-at-0(1, 1)10
  4. Scan insertion: after synthesis each flip-flop is replaced by a scan flip-flop, a flip-flop with a multiplexer in front. When scan enable is 1 the flip-flops join into a shift register (a scan chain) from a scan-in pin to a scan-out pin. A test shifts a pattern in, pulses one functional clock to capture the logic's response, and shifts the result out while the next pattern shifts in. Every flip-flop becomes directly controllable and observable, so a sequential circuit is tested like a combinational one.
  5. ATPG (automatic test pattern generation) works on the scan netlist and the fault list. For each fault it finds inputs that excite it and a path that carries the error to a scan flip-flop or an output (algorithms such as the D-algorithm and PODEM). Fault simulation then marks every other fault each pattern also detects, so the set stays small. The result is fault coverage = detected faults ÷ total faults.
  6. BIST (built-in self-test) puts the tester on the chip: a linear-feedback shift register (LFSR) makes pseudo-random patterns and a multiple-input signature register (MISR) compresses the responses into a signature compared with the expected one. Example: a 3-bit LFSR that shifts left and feeds Q2 ⊕ Q1 into Q0, seeded 001, runs 001, 010, 101, 011, 111, 110, 100 and back to 001, all 7 nonzero states. BIST is standard for memories and runs at full speed, at the cost of area.
  7. Boundary scan (IEEE 1149.1, JTAG) adds a scan cell at every pin behind the TDI, TDO, TCK and TMS test access port, so the board connections between chips can be tested.
  8. Where they sit in the flow: DFT insertion comes after synthesis and before physical design, so scan chains are placed, routed and timed; ATPG runs on the final netlist; the patterns are written into a tester program. After fabrication each die is the DUT (device under test): it is tested at wafer probe and again after packaging on automatic test equipment (ATE).
  9. Closing check with numbers: 1,000 patterns on one chain of 5,000 flip-flops at 50 MHz take (1,000 × 5,001 + 5,000) × 20 ns = 100.12 ms per chip, ₹0.30 at ₹3 per tester-second; ten chains of 500 take 10.03 ms and ₹0.03. Test cost is a design decision.

Evaluate A start-up in Chennai must ship 5,000 units of a signal-processing board in six months. Compare an ASIC and an FPGA implementation on NRE, unit cost, performance, power and time to market, and recommend one. 15 marks

Points to cover. ASIC: high fixed NRE (masks, EDA, IP, packaging), low unit cost at volume, best speed and power; FPGA: near-zero NRE, higher unit cost, lower clock speed, more power, but reprogrammable in the field; Break-even volume analysis: difference in NRE divided by difference in unit cost; Schedule: an ASIC adds physical design, signoff and fabrication months; an FPGA can be re-programmed after a bug; Risk: respin cost of an ASIC versus bitstream update of an FPGA; Justified recommendation for 5,000 units and six months.

Model answer.

  1. Use the cost figures of the ASIC chapter's worked example for the main chip: FPGA ₹1,800 per chip with no NRE; gate array ₹40 lakh NRE and ₹500 per chip; standard-cell ASIC ₹2 crore NRE and ₹250 per chip.
  2. Total chip cost for 5,000 boards (total = NRE + N × unit cost):
    OptionNRE5,000 × unit costTotal
    FPGA₹0₹90 lakh₹90 lakh
    Gate array₹40 lakh₹25 lakh₹65 lakh
    Standard-cell ASIC₹200 lakh₹12.5 lakh₹212.5 lakh
  3. Break-even volumes against the FPGA:
    gate array:    ₹40,00,000 ÷ (1,800 − 500) = 3,077 boards
standard cell: ₹2,00,00,000 ÷ (1,800 − 250) = 12,903 boards
  4. NRE: the FPGA needs none; an ASIC pays for masks, tools, IP licences, test programs and packaging up front. At 5,000 units the standard-cell ASIC is the most expensive option by far, because 5,000 is well below its break-even of 12,903.
  5. Unit cost: the ASIC wins per chip (₹250 against ₹1,800), but that saving only pays back its NRE above the break-even volume.
  6. Performance and power: an ASIC built from fixed gates and wires runs at a higher clock and uses less power and area for the same function; an FPGA pays for its programmable LUTs and switches. For a signal-processing board, check that the FPGA's DSP slices meet the sample rate; if they do, performance does not decide the case.
  7. Time to market: an FPGA design can be on a board in weeks. A standard-cell ASIC needs synthesis, DFT, physical design and signoff, then several weeks of fabrication, packaging and test; even a gate array, with only metal masks, needs weeks of foundry time. Six months leaves no room for a respin.
  8. Risk: an ASIC bug found after tapeout needs new masks, months and a second NRE. An FPGA bug is fixed with a new bitstream, even on boards already shipped.
  9. Evaluation: the gate array is ₹25 lakh cheaper than the FPGA on paper, but that saving is lost with one respin (another ₹40 lakh) and its schedule leaves almost no margin in six months. The standard-cell ASIC is ruled out by both cost and time.
  10. Recommendation: build the board on an FPGA. Ship on time, fix bugs in the field, and revisit a gate array or ASIC only if orders rise past the break-even volumes (about 3,100 for a gate array, 12,900 for a standard cell) and the design is stable.
  11. Closing check: 5,000 × ₹1,800 = ₹90,00,000; ₹40,00,000 + 5,000 × ₹500 = ₹65,00,000; ₹2,00,00,000 + 5,000 × ₹250 = ₹2,12,50,000.
Analyze Discuss test cost metrics and test economics. Analyse the trade-off between external ATE testing, scan-based ATPG and BIST for a large logic chip. 14 marks

Points to cover. Cost drivers: tester time per device, pattern count, tester pin count and capital cost, yield learning; Test time equals pattern count times shift and capture cycles; more coverage usually means more patterns; ATE with scan ATPG: high coverage, needs a tester, more pins and memory for patterns; BIST: on-chip generation, runs at speed, but area overhead and limited diagnosis; Fault coverage versus defect level; escape cost and customer returns; Balanced scheme: BIST for memories and embedded blocks, scan ATPG for random logic.

Model answer.

  1. Test cost per chip = test time × tester rate, plus the share of DFT area on every die, plus the cost of the chips that escape. Test economics balances these three.
  2. Metrics: fault coverage (detected ÷ modelled faults), pattern count, test time per chip, test pins, tester memory for the patterns, DFT area overhead, and the defect level (bad chips shipped, in parts per million).
  3. Test time comes from scan shifting. For 1,000 patterns at a 50 MHz scan clock (20 ns) and a ₹3 per second tester:
    Scan chainsLongest chain LCycles N(L + 1) + LTime per chipCost per chip
    15,00050,06,000100.12 ms₹0.300
    105005,01,50010.03 ms₹0.0301
  4. On 10 lakh chips that is ₹3,00,360 against ₹30,090. Ten chains need 20 scan pins, so pins are traded for time; on-chip test compression gets the same cut with fewer pins.
  5. Escapes: with yield Y and fault coverage T, the defect level is DL = 1 − Y(1 − T) (the Williams-Brown estimate). For Y = 90 %:
    T = 99 %:   DL = 1 − 0.90.01 = 0.00105  → about 1,050 ppm
T = 99.9 %: DL = 1 − 0.90.001 = 0.000105 → about 105 ppm
  6. So the last per cent of coverage cuts escapes ten times. For a car or a phone maker that returns a bad chip at many times its price, the extra patterns pay for themselves.
  7. External ATE with functional patterns: no DFT area, but long hand-written test programs, low coverage of deep logic and an expensive tester that must run at full chip speed. It does not scale to large logic chips.
  8. Scan-based ATPG on ATE: scan flip-flops cost a few per cent of area and some speed (the scan multiplexer), but ATPG reaches very high stuck-at and transition coverage automatically and pinpoints the failing flip-flop for diagnosis. The cost is tester time and pattern memory, as the table shows.
  9. BIST: an LFSR and a MISR on the chip test at full speed with almost no tester time and only a few pins, and the same test can run in the field. But pseudo-random patterns miss hard-to-reach faults, the signature hides which bit failed, and the logic adds area. It suits regular blocks such as memories, where March tests cover the usual memory fault models.
  10. Analysis: for random logic scan ATPG gives the best coverage per rupee; for memories BIST wins; boundary scan covers the board connections; functional tests remain only for speed binning and analog checks.
  11. Recommended scheme: scan with compression and ATPG for the logic, memory BIST for every SRAM, boundary scan at the pins, and a coverage target set from the defect level the customer accepts.
  12. Closing check: 0.10012 s × ₹3 = ₹0.300 and 0.90.01 = e0.01 × ln 0.9 = e−0.00105 = 0.99895, so DL = 0.00105.
Evaluate Compare full-custom and semi-custom design styles. When is each justified, and how does the choice change the design flow? 13 marks

Points to cover. Full-custom: every transistor drawn by hand, best area, speed and power, longest schedule and highest effort; Semi-custom: standard cells or gate arrays, library-based synthesis and place-and-route; Flow differences: full-custom needs schematic entry, layout editing, extraction and intensive DRC/LVS iteration; Semi-custom relies on characterised libraries, timing-driven synthesis and signoff with standard reports; Suitable cases: full-custom for high-volume processors, memories and analog; semi-custom for most digital logic; Cost, risk and reuse argument in the conclusion.

Model answer.

  1. Full-custom: the designer sizes and draws every transistor and wire. Semi-custom: the designer works with pre-designed pieces, either library standard cells placed by tools (every mask still custom) or a masked gate array whose base transistors are already made (only metal masks custom).
  2. Comparison:
    PointFull-customStandard cellGate array
    What is customEvery transistor and maskEvery mask; cells from a libraryContact, via and metal masks only
    Speed, power, areaBestGoodLowest of the three (fixed transistor sizes, unused gates)
    Design effort and timeHighest, longestMedium; tools do placement and routingLowest; quick turnaround
    NRE in the worked example₹8 crore₹2 crore₹40 lakh
    Unit cost in the worked example₹200₹250₹500
  3. How the flow changes in full-custom: schematic entry and transistor-level simulation (SPICE) replace synthesis; the layout is drawn in a layout editor, then extracted, and DRC, LVS and post-layout simulation are repeated by hand until clean. Timing comes from circuit simulation, not from a cell library.
  4. How the flow changes in semi-custom: RTL is synthesised onto a characterised library; placement, clock-tree synthesis and routing are automatic; static timing analysis uses the library's delay tables; signoff runs standard DRC, LVS and timing reports. A gate array shortens the back end further because only the wiring is made.
  5. Cost against volume with the worked-example figures:
    gate array ↔ standard cell:   (₹2 crore − ₹40 lakh) ÷ (500 − 250) = 64,000 chips
standard cell ↔ full custom:  (₹8 crore − ₹2 crore) ÷ (250 − 200) = 12 lakh chips
  6. When full-custom is justified: very high volume (above about 12 lakh chips here), a block repeated millions of times (an SRAM bit cell, a standard cell itself), analog and RF circuits, and the critical datapaths of high-speed processors where a few per cent of speed or power sells the product.
  7. When semi-custom is justified: almost all other digital logic. Standard cells give near-custom quality with a fraction of the effort and are the default for SoCs; gate arrays fit lower volumes, from about 3,100 chips (against an FPGA) to 64,000 (against standard cells) in the example, or when the design must reach silicon fast.
  8. Risk and reuse: full-custom layouts must be redrawn for each process and each bug costs more effort; standard-cell RTL moves to a new process by re-synthesis, and libraries and IP are reused across projects.
  9. Evaluation: the two styles are combined in practice. A modern SoC is a standard-cell chip that contains full-custom macros (SRAMs, PLLs, I/O cells), so each style is used where it pays.
  10. Closing check: at 20 lakh chips, full custom costs ₹8 crore + 20 lakh × ₹200 = ₹48 crore and standard cell ₹2 crore + 20 lakh × ₹250 = ₹52 crore, so full custom wins only well above its 12-lakh break-even, and only if its longer schedule is acceptable.
Analyze Analyse how floorplanning decisions propagate through placement and routing. Show what fails at signoff when a floorplan is poor. 13 marks

Points to cover. Macro positions and aspect ratio fix the distance between communicating blocks and the routing channels; Pin assignment sets where signals enter; the wrong side forces long detours; Utilisation too high leaves no space for the router: congestion and detours; Congestion raises wire length, which raises delay, dynamic power and crosstalk; Clock tree and power grid need space in the floorplan; ignoring them causes IR drop and skew failures; Feedback loop: congestion or timing violations force re-floorplanning.

Model answer.

  1. The floorplan fixes the frame that placement and routing must work inside: core size, aspect ratio, macro positions, pin positions and the power grid. Later stages can move cells within that frame but cannot repair the frame itself.
  2. Start from the floorplan of the floorplanning chapter: a 3.0 mm × 3.0 mm core with hard blocks of 2.0, 1.5, 1.0 and 0.5 mm² and 1.8 mm² of standard cells:
    Acore = 3.0 × 3.0 = 9.0 mm²
Aused = 5.0 + 1.8 = 6.8 mm²     U = 6.8 ÷ 9.0 = 75.6 %
whitespace = 2.2 mm²; with 20 % routing margin 8.16 mm² ≤ 9.0 mm², 0.84 mm² spare
  3. Now squeeze the core to 2.7 mm × 2.7 mm to save area and follow the effects:
    Acore = 7.29 mm²     U = 6.8 ÷ 7.29 = 93.3 %
needed with 20 % margin: 8.16 mm² > 7.29 mm², short by 0.87 mm²
  4. Placement: at 93 % there are no free sites near the macros, so cells that talk to each other are spread apart and the HPWL rises; buffers needed for long nets have nowhere to go.
  5. Routing: demand for tracks exceeds supply in the channels between the macros. The router detours nets, adds vias and leaves some nets unrouted or with shorts, which shows up as DRC errors.
  6. Timing: detoured wires are longer, so their resistance and capacitance rise and the critical paths miss the clock period (setup violations); hold fixing needs buffers that cannot be placed.
  7. Power and noise: longer wires mean more switched capacitance and dynamic power; wires packed side by side couple more, so crosstalk glitches and delay changes appear.
  8. Macro and pin decisions propagate the same way. A memory placed in the far corner from the processor that reads it makes every read path cross the die. Pins on the wrong side force every net to cross the core. A macro with its pins facing the die edge leaves no room to reach them.
  9. Clock and power: a floorplan with no space for clock buffers gives large skew; straps that are too narrow, or blocks placed far from the supply pads, give IR drop that slows every gate in that region and can fail the IR-drop signoff check.
  10. What fails at signoff, and the floorplan cause:
    Signoff check that failsFloorplan cause
    DRC: shorts, spacing, opens from unrouted netsUtilization too high; narrow channels between macros
    Setup timingLong detours; communicating blocks placed far apart
    Hold timing and clock skewNo room for clock and hold buffers
    IR drop and electromigrationPower grid too thin; blocks far from the supply pads
    Crosstalk (signal integrity)Congested regions with parallel wires
  11. Feedback: these failures cannot be fixed by the router alone, so the team returns to the floorplan, moves macros, adds channels or enlarges the core, and runs placement and routing again. Each loop costs days, which is why floorplanning is checked early with congestion and IR-drop estimates.
  12. Closing check: the 3.0 mm core keeps 0.84 mm² spare after the 20 % routing margin; the 2.7 mm core is 0.87 mm² short. Saving 1.71 mm² of area (9.0 − 7.29) costs a failed signoff, so the larger core is the right floorplan.

Glossary

ASIC
A chip manufactured for one fixed function, customised through masks at fabrication.
ATPG
Automatic test pattern generation: software that computes input patterns to detect modelled faults.
BIST
Built-in self-test: on-chip circuitry that generates test patterns and compresses the responses.
CLB
Configurable logic block: an FPGA tile holding lookup tables, flip-flops and carry logic.
Clock-domain crossing
A connection between logic on two unrelated clocks, needing synchronisers or asynchronous FIFOs.
CPLD
Complex programmable logic device: a small programmable part with macrocells and a central switch matrix.
DFT
Design for testability: on-chip structures added to make internal nodes controllable and observable from the pins.
DUT
Design Under Test: the instantiated module that a test bench exercises.
Fault model
An abstract representation of a physical defect, such as a line stuck-at 0, used for test generation.
Floorplanning
Assigning die area, macro positions, pin locations and power structure before placement.
FPGA
Field-programmable gate array: a chip whose logic cells and wiring are configured by a bitstream after manufacture.
Full-custom design
A style where the designer draws and optimises every transistor and its layout by hand.
Gate array
An ASIC built on a pre-made base wafer of transistors, customised only by its metal and via masks.
Hard IP
IP delivered as a fixed physical layout for one process, with guaranteed timing and area.
IP core
A pre-designed, verified functional block that is licensed and reused in a larger chip.
LUT
Lookup table: a small memory in an FPGA that holds the truth table of a combinational function.
Netlist
A list of cells and the nets that connect their pins, the usual output of synthesis.
NRE
Non-recurring engineering cost: the one-time cost of design, tools, masks and test programs.
Placement
Choosing the exact die coordinate of each cell with the aim of short wires and low congestion.
Routing
Creating the metal wires and vias that realise the nets between placed cells.
RTL
Register-transfer level: a description of sequential logic as registers and the combinational logic between them.
Scan chain
A series of scan flip-flops linked into a shift register so test data can be loaded and unloaded serially.
Semi-custom design
A style that uses pre-characterised cells or a pre-built array and customises mainly the interconnection.
Signoff
The final set of checks (timing, power, physical verification) that must pass before tapeout.
Soft IP
IP delivered as synthesizable HDL source, mapped to the user's technology at build time.
Soft-core processor
A processor supplied as HDL and implemented in the programmable or standard-cell fabric.
Standard cell
A pre-designed, characterised library gate of fixed height and variable width, placed in rows by tools.
Tapeout
The handoff of the final layout database (GDSII) to the foundry for mask making.
Test bench
A Verilog module, with no ports, that generates stimulus and checks the outputs of a design instance.
Wrapper
Logic placed around a reused block to adapt its signal names, bus protocol, clocks and resets to the surrounding system.
