3.1 CMOS inverter DC characteristics and noise margins

The CMOS inverter is one nMOS pull-down and one pMOS pull-up with their gates tied together. Its voltage transfer characteristic (VTC) plots the output Vout against the input Vin at a fixed supply VDD. From this one curve come the switching threshold VM, the output levels VOH and VOL, the input limits VIL and VIH, and the two noise margins. They tell a designer how much noise an input can take, so the VTC is the first check on every standard cell.

You need: the square-law drain current of the MOSFET in its cut-off, linear and saturation regions (see MOSFET I-V characteristics and device equations).

The five regions of the VTC

The nMOS source is at ground, so its VGS = Vin; the pMOS source is at VDD, so its VSG = VDD − Vin. No current leaves the output, so the two drain currents are equal, and each region of the curve is the pair of transistor states that satisfies this.

Saturation: ID = (k/2)(VGS − Vt)². Linear: ID = k[(VGS − Vt)VDS − VDS²/2]. Here k = µCox(W/L) is the device gain in A/V² (some books write β).

At VM both devices are saturated. Setting kn(VM − Vtn)² = kp(VDD − VM − |Vtp|)² and taking square roots gives the design equation:

VM = (VDD + rVtn − |Vtp|)/(1 + r) Here r = √(kn/kp) is the strength ratio. A stronger nMOS (r > 1) pulls VM below VDD/2; a stronger pMOS pushes it up.

Noise margins

VOH and VOL are the flat output levels: VDD and 0 V for a static CMOS inverter, because one transistor is always off. VIL is the largest input that still counts as a 0 and VIH the smallest that still counts as a 1. Both are taken where the slope dVout/dVin = −1; between them the gate amplifies noise instead of rejecting it.

NMH = VOH − VIH, NML = VIL − VOL Here nMH and NML are the high and low noise margins in volts: how far a valid output can be pushed by noise before the next gate misreads it.

For a matched inverter (kn = kp and Vtn = |Vtp| = Vt) the slope condition can be solved in closed form. In region B the nMOS is saturated and the pMOS linear; differentiating the current equality and putting the slope at −1 gives:

VIL = (3VDD + 2Vt)/8, VIH = (5VDD − 2Vt)/8 Here both margins then equal (3VDD + 2Vt)/8, and VM = VDD/2.

Electrons are about two to three times more mobile than holes, so equal widths do not give a matched pair. To centre VM, the pMOS is made wider by the mobility ratio: Wp/Wn = µn/µp. When VM moves away from VDD/2, one margin grows and the other shrinks, and the smaller one sets the noise budget.

Using the curve in design

In region C the gain is much larger than 1, so a slightly bad input comes out as a clean rail. This regeneration is why long chains of CMOS gates do not lose their levels.

Some syllabi call the VTC the DC transfer characteristic; the two VTCs of an SRAM cell drawn on one graph form the butterfly curve. The inverter's widths also set its load and delay (see switching characteristics).

Worked example

Question. A matched CMOS inverter has VDD = 3.3 V and Vtn = |Vtp| = 0.6 V, with kn = kp. Find VM, VIL, VIH and both noise margins, and check the transistor regions at Vin = VM.

  1. Matched means r = 1, so VM = (3.3 + 0.6 − 0.6)/2 = 1.65 V.
  2. At Vin = Vout = 1.65 V the nMOS has VGS − Vt = 1.05 V and VDS = 1.65 V > 1.05 V, so it is saturated. The pMOS has VSG − |Vtp| = 1.65 − 0.6 = 1.05 V and VSD = 1.65 V > 1.05 V, so it is saturated too.
  3. VIL = (3 × 3.3 + 2 × 0.6)/8 = (9.9 + 1.2)/8 = 1.3875 V ≈ 1.39 V.
  4. VIH = (5 × 3.3 − 2 × 0.6)/8 = (16.5 − 1.2)/8 = 1.9125 V ≈ 1.91 V.
  5. VOH = 3.3 V and VOL = 0 V, so NML = 1.39 − 0 = 1.39 V and NMH = 3.3 − 1.91 = 1.39 V.
  6. Check on the computed curve (Figure 3.1.1): the slope is −1 at both points, where the output is 3.04 V and 0.26 V.

Answer. VM = 1.65 V with both devices saturated; VIL ≈ 1.39 V, VIH ≈ 1.91 V; NML = NMH ≈ 1.39 V.

Worked example 2

Question. The same process has µn = 2.5µp. A designer draws the pMOS with the same W/L as the nMOS. Find VM at VDD = 3.3 V, Vtn = |Vtp| = 0.6 V. What Wp/Wn would bring VM back to 1.65 V?

  1. Equal sizes give kn/kp = µn/µp = 2.5, so r = √2.5 = 1.581.
  2. VM = (3.3 + 1.581 × 0.6 − 0.6)/(1 + 1.581) = (3.3 + 0.949 − 0.6)/2.581 = 3.649/2.581 = 1.414 V.
  3. VM = 1.65 V needs r = 1, that is kp = kn, so µpWp = µnWn and Wp/Wn = 2.5.

Answer. VM ≈ 1.41 V, 0.24 V below the centre, so NML shrinks; a pMOS 2.5 times wider restores VM = 1.65 V.

Exam traps

  • Writing VM = VDD/2 for every inverter. It holds only when kn = kp and Vtn = |Vtp|; with equal widths the stronger nMOS pulls VM down.
  • Writing the pMOS overdrive as Vin − |Vtp|. Its source sits at VDD, so the overdrive is VDD − Vin − |Vtp|.
  • Taking VIL and VIH as the thresholds Vtn and VDD − |Vtp|. They are the points where the slope is −1, inside regions B and D.
  • Swapping the margin formulas. NMH = VOH − VIH and NML = VIL − VOL; logic swing VOH − VOL is a different, larger number.

3.2 NMOS inverters: resistive, enhancement, depletion, pseudo-nMOS loads

An NMOS inverter is one nMOS driver that pulls the output down plus a load that pulls it up. The driver alone cannot make a logic 1, so the load sets the high level, and because the load is still on when the driver pulls down, the low level is a tug-of-war between the two. Four loads are standard: a resistor, an enhancement nMOS with its gate at VDD, a depletion nMOS with its gate tied to its source, and a pMOS with its gate at ground (pseudo-nMOS). Each gives a different VOH, VOL and static current; the idea survives today as ratioed logic.

You need: the five-region analysis of the CMOS inverter and the body effect on threshold voltage (see Threshold voltage and body effect).

The load line and the resistive load

With a resistor RL from VDD to the output, the load current (VDD − Vout)/RL is a straight load line on the driver's I-V curves, and each input picks the crossing. With Vin = 0 the driver is off and VOH = VDD. With Vin = VDD, VDS = VOL is far below VGS − Vtn, so the driver is linear:

(VDD − VOL)/RL = kn[(VDD − Vtn)VOL − VOL²/2] Here kn = µnCox(W/L) is the driver gain in A/V². The left side is the resistor current and the right side the linear-region driver current.

A small low level needs a large RL, and a 100 kΩ resistor is far larger than a transistor, so it was replaced by a transistor load.

Enhancement and depletion loads

Enhancement load. An nMOS with drain and gate at VDD and source at the output. Its VGS = VDS = VDD − Vout, so it is always saturated while on. It stops conducting when VGS falls to its threshold, so VOH = VDD − Vt,load. The load's source is the output, so VSB = Vout and the body effect raises Vt,load and lowers VOH further. At Vin = VDD:

(kL/2)(VDD − VOL − Vt)² = kD[(VDD − Vt)VOL − VOL²/2] Here kL and kD are the load and driver gains. VOL depends on the ratio kD/kL, so the driver must be several times stronger.

Depletion load. An nMOS with an extra channel implant has a negative threshold −Vtd, so with its gate tied to its source (VGS = 0) it is always on. With the driver off, the only consistent state is zero load current, which needs VDS = 0: VOH = VDD, a full high level. While saturated it pulls up with a nearly constant current IDSS = (kL/2)Vtd². The body effect makes Vtd smaller as the output rises, so this current falls somewhat near the top of the swing. The constant-current pull-up charges the load faster than an enhancement load; the price is one more process step.

Pseudo-nMOS and the four compared

Pseudo-nMOS. A pMOS load with source at VDD, drain at the output and gate at ground is always on, so VOH = VDD. With the driver on, the pMOS is saturated as long as VOL ≤ |Vtp|, and then (kp/2)(VDD − |Vtp|)² = kn[(VDD − Vtn)VOL − VOL²/2]. It needs no extra process step in a CMOS process, which is why it survives; it is worked with numbers in the chapter on ratioed logic.

Worked example

Question. A resistive-load NMOS inverter has VDD = 5 V, RL = 100 kΩ, driver kn = 20 µA/V² and Vtn = 0.8 V. The input is at 5 V. Find VOL and the static power.

  1. The driver is on (VGS = 5 V > 0.8 V). If it were saturated it would carry (20/2) × (4.2)² = 176.4 µA, which would drop 17.6 V across RL, more than VDD. So it is linear.
  2. Equate (5 − VOL)/100 kΩ = 20 µA/V² × [4.2VOL − VOL²/2]. Multiply by 100 kΩ: 5 − VOL = 8.4VOL − VOL², so VOL² − 9.4VOL + 5 = 0.
  3. VOL = (9.4 − √(88.36 − 20))/2 = (9.4 − 8.268)/2 = 0.566 V. The other root, 8.83 V, is above VDD and is rejected.
  4. Check: 0.566 V < 4.2 V, so the linear assumption holds.
  5. Static current I = (5 − 0.566)/100 kΩ = 44.3 µA; static power = 5 V × 44.3 µA = 0.22 mW.

Answer. VOL ≈ 0.57 V; the gate draws 44.3 µA, 0.22 mW, all the time its output is low.

Worked example 2

Question. An enhancement-load inverter has VDD = 5 V, Vt = 0.8 V for both transistors (ignore body effect), kL = 20 µA/V² and kD = 180 µA/V² (ratio 9). Find VOH, VOL and the static current at the low output.

  1. VOH = VDD − Vt = 5 − 0.8 = 4.2 V.

  4. VOL = (84 − √(7056 − 705.6))/20 = (84 − 79.69)/20 = 0.216 V.
  5. Current = 10 µA/V² × (4.2 − 0.216)² = 10 × 15.88 = 159 µA, so 0.79 mW at 5 V.

Exam traps

  • Using the saturation equation for the driver at the low output. VDS = VOL is far below VGS − Vtn, so the driver is linear.
  • Giving VOH = VDD for the enhancement load. It stops one threshold short, and the body effect makes that threshold larger than Vt0.
  • Calling a depletion load off because VGS = 0. Its threshold is negative, so it conducts until VDS = 0.
  • Taking the supply side as the source of an nMOS load. Its source is the output, so its VGS and VSB follow the output.

3.3 Switching characteristics and propagation delay

A gate cannot switch instantly: its output node carries a load capacitance CL, and the transistors can supply only a limited current. The propagation delay is the time from the input crossing its 50% level to the output crossing its 50% level. Every clock period, setup check and timing report is built from these delays, so this chapter defines tPHL, tPLH, tp, rise time and fall time, gives two hand estimates that agree, and lists what makes up CL.

You need: the saturation current of a MOSFET (see MOSFET I-V characteristics) and the CMOS inverter.

The delay numbers

  • tPHL, output high-to-low: from the input crossing VDD/2 rising to the output crossing VDD/2 falling. The nMOS pull-down sets it.
  • tPLH, output low-to-high: from the input crossing VDD/2 falling to the output crossing VDD/2 rising. The pMOS pull-up sets it.
  • tp = (tPHL + tPLH)/2, the single number used for a gate in a path.
  • tf and tr, fall and rise times: the output alone moving between 90% and 10% of VDD (some books use 80% and 20%).

Delay and edge time answer different questions. Delay says when the edge arrives; rise or fall time says how sharp it is. A slow edge at the input of the next gate makes that gate slower and wastes short-circuit power, so timing tools track both.

A ring oscillator, an odd number N of inverters in a loop, makes these numbers measurable on silicon. Each edge travels round the ring twice per period, so its frequency is f = 1/(2Ntp).

Two hand estimates

Average current. For tPHL the output must lose the charge CLVDD/2 to reach the 50% point. With an input step, the nMOS is saturated while Vout > VDD − Vtn and linear below that, where its current is a little smaller. Using the saturation current for the whole half swing gives:

tPHL ≈ CLVDD/(2IDsat), IDsat = (kn/2)(VDD − Vtn)² Here cL is in farads, VDD in volts and IDsat in amperes, giving seconds. tPLH uses the pMOS current (kp/2)(VDD − |Vtp|)² the same way.

RC model. Replace the on transistor by a resistance Req. A step into an RC circuit reaches 50% after ln 2 × ReqCL, so tp = 0.69ReqCL, and its 10% to 90% time is ln 9 × ReqCL = 2.2ReqCL. A fair Req is the average of the transistor's V/I over the half swing: about (3/4)VDD/IDsat. With that Req the RC delay is 0.69 × 0.75 = 0.52 CLVDD/IDsat, within 4% of the average-current estimate. Neither is exact; simulation is the reference, and the computed waveform in Figure 3.3.1 shows how close both come.

What makes up the load

CL is the sum of the driver's own drain diffusion capacitances (nMOS and pMOS), the gate-drain overlaps, the wire, and the gate capacitance of every fan-out input, Cin ≈ Cox(WnLn + WpLp). Doubling the fan-out roughly doubles CL and so the delay.

The gate-drain capacitance Cgd joins input and output, which swing in opposite directions. During a transition the voltage across it changes by about twice the swing, so it counts roughly twice (the Miller effect). Long wires add capacitance and resistance; their models are in interconnect parameters.

Sizing trades these against each other: a wider driver gives more current but loads the stage before it. The chapter on logical effort finds the best balance.

Worked example

Question. A CMOS inverter drives CL = 50 fF at VDD = 1.8 V. The nMOS has kn = 200 µA/V², Vtn = 0.5 V; the pMOS has kp = 80 µA/V², |Vtp| = 0.5 V. Estimate tPHL, tPLH and tp. What kp would make the edges equal?

  1. nMOS: IDsat = (200/2)(1.8 − 0.5)² = 100 × 1.69 = 169 µA.
  2. Charge to remove: CLVDD/2 = 50 fF × 0.9 V = 45 fC. tPHL = 45 fC / 169 µA = 0.266 ns.
  3. pMOS: I = (80/2)(1.3)² = 67.6 µA, so tPLH = 45 fC / 67.6 µA = 0.666 ns.
  4. tp = (0.266 + 0.666)/2 = 0.466 ns.
  5. Equal edges need equal currents, kp = kn = 200 µA/V², a pMOS 200/80 = 2.5 times wider.
  6. Check with the step response of the square-law nMOS, integrated numerically: the output leaves saturation at 1.3 V after 0.148 ns and crosses 0.9 V at 0.270 ns, 1.5% above the estimate.

Answer. tPHL ≈ 0.27 ns, tPLH ≈ 0.67 ns, tp ≈ 0.47 ns; widening the pMOS 2.5 times balances them at about 0.27 ns each.

Question. For the nMOS of the first example find Req, the RC estimate of tPHL and the RC estimate of the fall time.

  2. tPHL ≈ 0.69 × 8.0 kΩ × 50 fF = 0.276 ns, 4% above the average-current value of 0.266 ns.

Exam traps

  • Swapping tPHL and tPLH. tPHL is the output falling, set by the nMOS and caused by a rising input.
  • Dropping the 2 in CLVDD/(2IDsat). Delay ends at the 50% point, so only half the swing is moved.
  • Squaring VDD instead of the overdrive VDD − Vtn in the saturation current.
  • Calling rise time a delay. Rise time is measured on the output alone; delay is from input to output.
  • Unit slips: fF × V = fC and fC ÷ µA = ns. Keep fF with µA and read nanoseconds.

3.4 Delay estimation, logical effort and transistor sizing

Logical effort is a pencil-and-paper method for finding the delay of a logic path and the transistor sizes that make it fastest. It splits each gate's delay into a part that depends on the gate's topology (its logical effort g and parasitic delay p) and a part that depends on its load (its electrical effort h). For a path with a fixed number of stages, delay is least when every stage bears the same effort, and that rule gives every gate's size in a few lines. Cell designers and synthesis tools use the same idea to choose drive strengths.

You need: propagation delay and how input capacitance grows with transistor width.

The delay of one gate

Measure delay in units of τ, the delay of an ideal inverter driving an identical inverter with no parasitic capacitance (about 3RC for a unit transistor of resistance R and gate capacitance C). Then the delay of a gate is:

d = gh + p = f + p

h = Cout/Cin is the electrical effort (often called fan-out), g the logical effort, f = gh the stage effort and p the parasitic delay, all in units of τ or dimensionless.

Logical effort g is how much more input capacitance a gate needs than an inverter to deliver the same output current. Take a unit inverter with nMOS width 1 and pMOS width 2 (holes are about half as mobile), so its input capacitance is 3 units. A NAND2 has two nMOS in series, so each must be width 2 to match the inverter's pull-down, and its parallel pMOS stay at width 2: each input sees 2 + 2 = 4 units, so g = 4/3. A NOR2 has its two pMOS in series, each width 4, and nMOS of width 1: g = (4 + 1)/3 = 5/3.

Path effort and the best stage effort

For a path of N stages, define the path logical effort G = g1g2…gN and the path electrical effort H = Cload/Cin,path. Where a node also drives gates off the path, its branching effort is b = (Con + Coff)/Con, and B is the product of all b. The path effort is F = GBH.

The product of the stage efforts is fixed at F, so their sum is least when all are equal (the arithmetic mean is never below the geometric mean). That gives the best stage effort f̂ = F1/N and the least path delay:

D = N F1/N + P, P = p1 + … + pN Here d is the path delay in τ. P is added once, not once per stage on top of it.

The number of stages can be chosen too. D is smallest when the stage effort is about 4, so the best N is close to log4 F. Adding inverters to a path that has too few stages makes it faster, even though each inverter adds its own delay.

Sizing the gates

  1. Compute G, B, H and F = GBH; pick N (about log4 F) and find f̂ = F1/N.
  2. Start at the load and work back: each stage's input capacitance is Cin = g × Cout/f̂, where Cout is what that stage drives.
  3. Turn capacitance into widths: a gate whose input is k times its unit version gets every transistor k times wider.

This is often called path delay optimisation or the method of logical effort. It gives first-pass sizes; a timing tool then refines them with real wire loads.

Worked example

Question. A path is inverter → NAND2 → inverter. Its input capacitance is C (a unit inverter) and it drives 64C. There is no branching. Find F, f̂, D and the input capacitance of each stage, and the transistor widths of the NAND2.

  1. N = 3. G = 1 × 4/3 × 1 = 4/3. H = 64C/C = 64. B = 1.

Answer. D ≈ 17.2τ with input capacitances 1C, 4.40C and 14.54C (NAND2 transistors about 6.6 units wide, last inverter 14.5/29.1).

Exam traps

  • Writing f̂ = F/N. The best stage effort is the Nth root, F1/N.
  • Forgetting branching effort where a node also drives gates off the path; F comes out too small and the path looks faster than it is.
  • Inverting H. H = Cload/Cin,path, the output load over the path input.
  • Adding parasitic delay twice: D = NF1/N + P, with P counted once.
  • Giving every gate the same width. Equal effort means equal f, so the gates grow towards the load.

3.5 Interconnect parameters and electrical wire models

On a modern chip the metal wires between gates are often slower than the gates themselves. A wire has resistance along its length, capacitance to the layers below and to its neighbours, and inductance when the current loop is large. These are the interconnect parameters. An electrical wire model turns them into a circuit you can analyse: a lumped capacitor, a lumped RC, a distributed RC line or a transmission line, chosen by how long the wire is and how fast its edges are. This chapter shows how to compute R and C from the geometry and when each model is good enough.

You need: the RC delay estimate in switching characteristics; the metal layers come from Lithography, etching and deposition.

Resistance: sheet resistance and squares

A wire of length L, width W and thickness t, made of a metal of resistivity ρ, has R = ρL/(Wt). Every metal layer has a fixed thickness, so designers fold ρ and t into one number, the sheet resistance R□ = ρ/t in ohms per square. Then R = R□ × (L/W): count how many W × W squares fit along the wire and multiply.

R = R□ × L/W, R□ = ρ/t

R□ is in Ω/□; L/W is the number of squares. A square of any size has the same resistance.

Copper replaced aluminium in the late 1990s because its bulk resistivity is about 1.7 µΩ·cm against about 2.7 µΩ·cm. In very narrow lines electrons scatter off the surfaces and grain boundaries, so the effective resistivity is higher than the bulk value. Polysilicon and diffusion have sheet resistances hundreds of times larger than metal, so they are used only for short runs.

Capacitance: plate, fringe and coupling

A wire over a ground layer is a parallel-plate capacitor: Cpp = εWL/h, where h is the dielectric thickness and ε = εrε0 (εr = 3.9 for silicon dioxide, ε0 = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m). In a nanometre process W is about as small as h, so field lines also leave the sides of the wire. This fringing capacitance can be larger than the plate term.

Neighbouring wires on the same layer add coupling capacitance Cc. If a neighbour switches the opposite way, the voltage across Cc changes by twice the swing and it acts like 2Cc (the Miller factor); switching the same way it acts like zero. Coupling also injects crosstalk noise into a quiet wire. A wire's total capacitance is roughly 0.2 fF per micrometre of length, nearly independent of its width.

Cw = Cpp + Cfringe + Cc Here each term is in farads and scales with the wire length L.

Inductance. A wire and its return path form a loop with inductance. It matters only when the wire's resistance is low (wide clock and power wires) and the edges are fast compared with the time of flight, about when the rise time is under 2.5 times the flight time. Signals in silicon dioxide travel at c/√3.9 ≈ 1.5 × 10⁸ m/s, so 5 mm takes about 33 ps. Ordinary signal wires have enough resistance to damp the ringing, so RC models are used for them.

In the distributed RC line every small piece of resistance r dx drives all the capacitance after it, and the voltage obeys a diffusion equation. Its 50% step delay is 0.38RC instead of the lumped 0.69RC, where R = rL and C = cL. Both grow with L², so doubling a wire's length quadruples its own delay. Long wires are therefore broken by repeaters (buffers), which make the delay grow only linearly with length.

A π model splits the line into Cw/2, Rw, Cw/2; a few π sections in series match the distributed line well and are what extraction tools hand to the simulator.

Question. A copper wire on an intermediate metal layer is L = 1 mm long, W = 0.2 µm wide and t = 0.4 µm thick, with an effective resistivity of 2.2 µΩ·cm. 3.6 Elmore delay model

Real loads are not one resistor and one capacitor. A gate drives a tree of wires and transistor stacks, each piece with its own resistance and capacitance. The Elmore delay model gives one time constant for any node of such an RC tree by a simple rule: for every capacitor, multiply it by the resistance its charging current shares with the path to the node, and add. Some syllabi call this Elmore's constant. It is quick enough to use by hand on a series transistor stack or a branching wire, and timing tools use it as their first estimate of wire delay.

You need: the RC delay idea in switching characteristics and the wire models in interconnect parameters.

The rule

An RC tree has one input (the driver) and resistors that branch out without loops, with a capacitor from every node to ground. Let Rik be the resistance of the part of the path from the input to node i that is shared with the path from the input to node k. The Elmore delay at node i is:

τDi = Σk CkRik Here the sum runs over every capacitor Ck in the tree, including those on side branches. A side-branch capacitor sees only the resistance up to where its branch leaves the path.

Mathematically τDi is the first moment of the node's impulse response, the average arrival time of its edge. For a single RC it equals RC exactly, and the 50% delay is 0.69RC. For a tree, 0.69τD is the usual estimate of the 50% delay. (Some textbooks fold the 0.69 into an effective resistance and quote the Elmore delay itself as the delay; either way, keep one convention through a problem.)

  1. Draw the tree with every resistor and node capacitor.
  2. Mark the path from the driver to the node you care about.
  3. For each capacitor, find where its own path leaves the marked path and add the resistance up to that point.
  4. Multiply, add the products for τD, and take 0.69τD as the delay.

Ladders and distributed wires

For an RC ladder (a chain) the rule becomes: each capacitor is multiplied by all the resistance between it and the driver. With N equal sections of R/N and C/N:

τD = RC(N + 1)/(2N)

R and C are the totals of the line. For N = 1 this is RC; as N grows it falls towards RC/2.

So the Elmore delay of a distributed wire is RC/2, against the exact 50% delay of 0.38RC. The rule overestimates a little, and it is never optimistic at the far end of a tree, which is why it is safe for a first pass. A driver of resistance Rd feeding a wire Rw, Cw and a load CL gives τD = Rd(Cw + CL) + Rw(Cw/2 + CL).

Transistor stacks

A conducting transistor is a resistor, and every internal node of a stack has diffusion capacitance. In a three-input NAND the output falls through three series nMOS. Number the nodes from the output down: the output node Cout sees all three resistances, the node below it two, the one below that one. The output capacitance therefore dominates, but internal nodes add delay too.

This gives two design rules. Place the input that arrives last on the transistor nearest the output: by then the lower internal nodes are already discharged, so the late edge has less charge to move. And keep stacks short: τD of an n-high stack grows roughly as n², one reason gates with more than four series transistors are split.

Worked example

Question. A driver of 1 kΩ feeds node 1 (20 fF). From node 1, one branch of 2 kΩ goes to node 2 (30 fF) and continues through 1 kΩ to node 4 (40 fF); another branch of 3 kΩ goes to node 3 (10 fF). Find the Elmore delay and the estimated 50% delay at node 4 and at node 3.

  1. Path to node 4: 1 kΩ, 2 kΩ, 1 kΩ. Shared resistances: C1 sees 1 kΩ; C2 sees 1 + 2 = 3 kΩ; C3 leaves at node 1, so 1 kΩ; C4 sees 4 kΩ.
  2. τD4 = 20 fF × 1 kΩ + 30 fF × 3 kΩ + 10 fF × 1 kΩ + 40 fF × 4 kΩ = 20 + 90 + 10 + 160 = 280 ps.
  3. Path to node 3: 1 kΩ, 3 kΩ. C1: 1 kΩ; C2 and C4 leave at node 1: 1 kΩ each; C3: 4 kΩ.
  4. τD3 = 20 × 1 + 30 × 1 + 10 × 4 + 40 × 1 = 20 + 30 + 40 + 40 = 130 ps (fF × kΩ = ps).
  5. 50% delays: 0.69 × 280 = 193 ps at node 4 and 0.69 × 130 = 90 ps at node 3.

Answer. τD4 = 280 ps (about 193 ps to 50%) and τD3 = 130 ps (about 90 ps).

Worked example 2

Question. A NAND3 pull-down has three nMOS of 2 kΩ each when on. The output node has 30 fF; the two internal nodes have 5 fF each. All internal nodes start charged. Find the Elmore delay of the falling output. How much of it is due to the internal nodes?

  1. Name the nodes from the bottom: x1 (above the lowest transistor), x2, then the output.
  2. x1 discharges through 2 kΩ, x2 through 4 kΩ, the output through 6 kΩ.
  3. τD = 5 fF × 2 kΩ + 5 fF × 4 kΩ + 30 fF × 6 kΩ = 10 + 20 + 180 = 210 ps.
  4. 50% delay ≈ 0.69 × 210 = 145 ps.

  • Quoting τD as the 50% delay in one step and 0.69τD in the next. State the convention once.
  • Using RC for a distributed wire. The Elmore delay of a uniform line is RC/2.
  • Putting the late-arriving input at the bottom of a stack. It belongs nearest the output.

3.7 Static and dynamic power dissipation

Every CMOS chip draws current from its supply for two reasons. When nodes switch, the supply charges their capacitance and that energy ends up as heat: this is dynamic power, and it grows with the clock frequency. Even when nothing switches, transistors that are meant to be off still leak: this is static power. Dynamic power sets how hot a busy processor runs; static power sets how fast an idle phone's battery drains. Both are estimated before layout, because they decide the supply voltage, the clock and the package.

You need: the CMOS inverter and subthreshold conduction (see Leakage and subthreshold conduction).

Dynamic power: switching and short-circuit

When the output of a gate rises, the pMOS draws charge CLVDD from the supply, which delivers energy CLVDD². Half, ½CLVDD², is stored on CL; the other half is lost as heat in the pMOS. When the output falls, the nMOS turns the stored half into heat. So each 0 → 1 transition costs CLVDD² from the supply, whatever the transistor sizes.

Psw = αCLVDD²f Here α is the activity factor: the probability that the node makes a 0 → 1 transition in a clock cycle. f is the clock frequency in Hz. A clock line has α = 1; a signal that toggles every cycle has α = 0.5; random data has α = 0.25.

Short-circuit power. While the input ramps through the band Vtn < Vin < VDD − |Vtp|, both transistors conduct for a moment and current flows straight from VDD to ground. For a symmetric inverter with an input ramp of rise and fall time tr:

Psc ≈ (k/12)(VDD − 2Vt)³trf Here k is the transistor gain in A/V² and Vt the threshold. It is zero if VDD < 2Vt and small when edges are sharp; it is usually kept under about a tenth of Psw by keeping input and output edges similar.

Glitches. When inputs of a gate arrive at different times, its output can switch and switch back in one cycle. Each glitch costs energy like a real transition, so α must count glitches too.

Static power: leakage

Static power is drawn with all inputs steady. In static CMOS there is no designed path from VDD to ground, so only leakage flows:

  • Subthreshold leakage: an off transistor (VGS < Vt) still conducts a little, and the current rises tenfold for every drop of S in Vt. S, the subthreshold swing, is about 60 mV per decade at room temperature in the ideal case and 80 to 100 mV in real devices. It grows quickly with temperature.
  • Gate leakage: carriers tunnel through a very thin gate oxide; high-k gate dielectrics were introduced to cut it.
  • Junction leakage: reverse-biased drain and source junctions, including band-to-band tunnelling in heavily doped devices.

Pstatic = VDDIleak Here ileak is the total leakage current of the chip in amperes. Some syllabi call this standby or leakage power.

Ratioed circuits such as NMOS and pseudo-nMOS inverters add a third, much larger static term: the load current that flows whenever their output is low.

Total power and the crossover

P = αCLVDD²f + Psc + VDDIleak Here the first two terms scale with f; the last does not.

At high clock rates the switching term dominates; at low rates, or with the clock stopped, leakage is all that is left. The frequency where the two are equal, f = Ileak/(αCLVDD), tells you which to attack. The supply appears squared in the switching term and also scales leakage, so it is the strongest single knob; the methods that use it are in low-power design.

A power figure is only as good as its α and C. α comes from simulating real workloads; C comes from gate, diffusion and wire capacitance, the same load used for delay.

Worked example

Question. A CMOS block has VDD = 1.8 V, total switched capacitance CL = 50 pF, f = 100 MHz and α = 0.1. Its leakage current is 10 µA. Find the switching power, the leakage power, the total, and the frequency at which the two are equal. Neglect short-circuit power.

  1. VDD² = 3.24 V², so CLVDD² = 50 × 10⁻¹² × 3.24 = 1.62 × 10⁻¹⁰ J.
  2. Psw = 0.1 × 1.62 × 10⁻¹⁰ × 10⁸ = 1.62 × 10⁻³ W = 1.62 mW.
  3. Pleak = 1.8 V × 10 µA = 18 µW = 0.018 mW.
  4. Total = 1.62 + 0.018 = 1.638 mW.
  5. Crossover: f = Ileak/(αCLVDD) = 10 × 10⁻⁶/(0.1 × 50 × 10⁻¹² × 1.8) = 1.11 MHz. Below about 1.1 MHz leakage is the larger part (Figure 3.7.1).

Answer. Psw = 1.62 mW, Pleak = 18 µW, total 1.638 mW; the two are equal at about 1.11 MHz.

3.8 Low-power design and power management

Low-power design keeps a chip inside its battery and heat budget by attacking each term of the power equation. Dynamic power α CVDD²f falls if fewer nodes switch, if less capacitance is switched, or above all if the supply is lowered; leakage falls if idle blocks are switched off or slower, low-leakage transistors are used where speed is not needed. A power manager applies these methods block by block while the chip runs. This chapter covers the design principles, clock gating, power gating, multi-threshold cells and dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS), with what each one costs.

You need: the power equation in static and dynamic power dissipation and how delay depends on VDD in switching characteristics.

Lowering VDD makes gates slower, so it must be paid for in speed. Parallelism pays it in area: two copies of a block, each at half the clock, deliver the same throughput, and the extra time per operation lets VDD fall. Pipelining does the same by shortening the logic between registers. The energy per operation then drops roughly as VDD², with some overhead for the extra multiplexers or registers.

Clock gating, power gating and multi-threshold cells

Clock gating stops the clock to registers that have no new data. An integrated clock-gating cell holds the enable in a latch while the clock is high, so the gated clock cannot glitch. The clock tree and registers take a large share of a chip's dynamic power, and gating them is cheap, but it leaves leakage untouched because the supply stays on.

Power gating puts a large switch between a block and a rail: a pMOS header to VDD or an nMOS footer to ground, usually a high-Vt device that leaks little itself. When the block is idle the switch opens and its leakage nearly vanishes. The costs: the switch's resistance slows the block a little; the block forgets its state unless retention flip-flops keep it; its outputs need isolation cells so they do not float into neighbours; and waking up takes time and energy to recharge the block's rail. Power gating saves energy only if the idle time exceeds the break-even time, wake energy divided by leakage power.

Multi-threshold (multi-Vt) cells. A library offers each cell with low, standard and high Vt. Low-Vt cells are fast but leak; high-Vt cells are slow but leak far less. Synthesis puts low-Vt cells only on the critical paths and high-Vt cells everywhere else, often cutting leakage several times at no cost in clock speed.

DVFS and the power manager

DVFS picks the lowest voltage and frequency pair that meets the current workload, from a table checked at every process corner. With α and C fixed, moving from (V1, f1) to (V2, f2) scales dynamic power by (V2/V1)² × (f2/f1), and energy per operation by (V2/V1)².

Order matters. Going down, lower the frequency first and then the voltage; going up, raise the voltage first and then the frequency. Otherwise the gates are briefly too slow for the clock and timing fails. Regulators and PLLs need microseconds to settle, so the operating system's governor changes points with some hysteresis.

A power manager ties these together: the operating system or a hardware monitor reports the workload, and the manager sets the DVFS point, gates clocks and switches power domains. Sleep states form a ladder: clock-gated (wakes in a cycle, small saving), retention (registers kept at low voltage), and fully power-gated (largest saving, slowest wake-up).

Worked example

Question. A block runs at 1.2 V and 1 GHz. DVFS moves it to 1.0 V and 0.8 GHz with the same α and C. Find the ratio of the new dynamic power to the old, the saving, and the change in energy per operation.

  1. P2/P1 = (1.0/1.2)² × (0.8/1.0). α and C cancel.
  2. (1.0/1.2)² = 0.8333² = 0.6944; frequency ratio = 0.8.
  3. P2/P1 = 0.6944 × 0.8 = 0.5556, a saving of 44.4%.
  4. Energy per operation depends only on V²: ratio 0.6944, a saving of 30.6%. The rest of the power saving comes from doing fewer operations per second.

Answer. New power is 0.556 of the old (44.4% saved); energy per operation falls by 30.6%.

Worked example 2

Question. A block leaks 0.5 mW. Power-gating it costs 1.5 nJ for each sleep and wake-up (recharging its rail and saving state). Find the break-even idle time. Is it worth gating for idle periods of 2 µs and of 10 µs?

  1. Break-even time = wake energy ÷ leakage power = 1.5 nJ ÷ 0.5 mW = 3 µs.
  2. Idle 2 µs: leakage saved = 0.5 mW × 2 µs = 1.0 nJ, less than the 1.5 nJ cost, so gating loses 0.5 nJ. Clock gating alone is better here.
  3. Idle 10 µs: saved = 5.0 nJ, net gain 5.0 − 1.5 = 3.5 nJ, 70% of the leakage energy.

Answer. Break-even is 3 µs: gating loses 0.5 nJ for a 2 µs gap and gains 3.5 nJ for a 10 µs gap.

Exam traps

  • Calling power gating a dynamic-power method. It cuts leakage while a block is idle; an idle block is not switching anyway.
  • Saying clock gating stops leakage. The supply is still on.
  • Lowering V without lowering f. Gates become slower, so the clock must drop first.
  • Assuming high-Vt cells are free. They are slower, so they go only off the critical path.
  • Power-gating for short idle gaps. Below the break-even time it wastes energy.

3.9 Static CMOS and ratioed logic

Static CMOS builds every gate from two complementary networks: an nMOS pull-down network (PDN) that connects the output to ground and a pMOS pull-up network (PUN) that connects it to VDD. For every input combination exactly one of them conducts, so the output always sits at a rail and no steady current flows. Ratioed logic keeps the pull-down network but replaces the pull-up with one always-on load. It saves transistors, but its low output sits above 0 V and it draws current whenever the output is low. Static CMOS is the default style of standard-cell libraries.

You need: the CMOS inverter, the pseudo-nMOS load and logical effort.

Designing a static CMOS gate

  1. Write the function in inverted form, Y = NOT(F). Static CMOS gates are inverting.
  2. Build the PDN from F: an AND becomes nMOS in series, an OR becomes nMOS in parallel.
  3. Build the PUN as the dual: every series connection in the PDN becomes parallel in the PUN, and every parallel one becomes series. The pMOS take the same inputs.
  4. Size for the same drive as a unit inverter (nMOS 1, pMOS 2): a device in a stack of k gets k times the unit width.
  5. Check each input's logical effort, and draw the stick diagram with an Euler path so one diffusion strip serves each network (see Stick diagrams).

A simple N-input gate uses 2N transistors. Because one network is always off in steady state, VOH = VDD, VOL = 0 V, static current is only leakage, and the noise margins are those of the inverter. The costs are the transistor count and the input capacitance of the series pMOS, which grow quickly for wide NOR gates; this is why gates are kept to about four inputs and larger functions are built as trees.

Some syllabi call this style complementary CMOS. It gives compound gates such as AND-OR-INVERT (AOI) and OR-AND-INVERT (OAI) in one stage.

Ratioed logic: an always-on load

In pseudo-nMOS the PUN is a single pMOS with its gate at ground. An N-input gate needs N + 1 transistors. When the PDN is off the load pulls the output to VDD. When the PDN is on, the load and the PDN both conduct, so a static current flows and the output settles at a VOL above 0 V set by the ratio of their strengths. For a NOR, the worst case is a single conducting nMOS, so each nMOS is sized to hold VOL on its own.

The load current is (kp/2)(VDD − |Vtp|)² while the pMOS stays saturated, which needs VOL ≤ |Vtp|. Making kp small lowers VOL and the static current but slows the rising edge, so a pseudo-nMOS gate is fast falling and slow rising. Its logical effort for a falling output is low (an input drives only one nMOS), which is why wide pseudo-nMOS NOR gates appear in PLA planes, ROM address decoders and comparators.

3.10 Pass transistor logic and transmission gates

A pass transistor uses a MOSFET as a switch: its gate is the control and data flows between its source and drain. An nMOS passes a clean 0 but stops a 1 one threshold below its gate; a pMOS passes a clean 1 but stops a 0 one threshold above ground. A transmission gate (TG) puts an nMOS and a pMOS in parallel with complementary controls, so together they pass both rails. Pass-transistor logic builds multiplexers, XOR gates, latches and adder cells with fewer transistors than static CMOS, at the price of weak levels, charge sharing and the need to restore signals.

You need: the body effect on threshold voltage (see Threshold voltage and body effect) and static CMOS gates.

The nMOS switch and its threshold drop

Put an nMOS between input A and output Y with its gate at control C = VDD. Whichever terminal is at the lower voltage acts as the source.

  • Passing a 0. A = 0 V, Y starts high. A is the source, so VGS = VDD − 0 = VDD for the whole discharge. Y falls all the way to 0 V: a strong 0.
  • Passing a 1. A = VDD, Y starts at 0 V. Now Y is the source, so VGS = VDD − VY falls as Y rises. The transistor cuts off when VGS reaches Vtn, so Y stops at VDD − Vtn: a weak 1.

The source of this nMOS is Y, which is above the grounded substrate, so VSB = VY and the body effect raises the threshold just when it matters:

Vtn = Vt0 + γ(√(2φF + VSB) − √(2φF)) Here vt0 is the zero-bias threshold, γ the body-effect coefficient in V1/2 and 2φF the surface potential term in volts.

Because VY appears on both sides, the final level is found by iteration (worked example 1). A weak 1 is a problem: an inverter driven by it has its pMOS still slightly on, so it draws static current and switches slowly. If a later pass transistor's gate is itself driven by a weak level, the drops add up.

The pMOS switch and the transmission gate

A pMOS switch is the mirror image: with its gate at 0 V it passes a strong 1 but its output can fall only to |Vtp| above ground, a weak 0.

A transmission gate puts both in parallel: the nMOS gate gets C and the pMOS gate gets C̄, the complement. With C = 1 both can conduct and Y follows A over the full range; with C = 0 both are off and Y is high-impedance (floating). Near 0 V the nMOS carries most of the current and near VDD the pMOS does, so the TG's on-resistance stays nearly constant across the swing. The cost is two transistors per switch and a C̄ wire.

Pass-transistor logic in use

2:1 multiplexer. Two TGs share output Y: one passes A when S = 1, the other passes B when S = 0. One of the two is always on, so Y is always driven. It takes 4 transistors plus an inverter for S̄, against 12 for a static CMOS version (an AOI22 gate, an output inverter and a select inverter).

XOR. A TG passes B̄ or B depending on A, giving A ⊕ B with 6 to 8 transistors; full adders built this way are smaller than static ones. Latches use a TG as the input switch and another to close the feedback loop (see Static and dynamic latches and registers).

Complementary pass-transistor logic (CPL) uses nMOS-only networks with both true and complement signals, then restores each output with an inverter; some syllabi list it with PTL. The rules for every pass network: never drive a gate input straight from a long chain of switches, add a restoring inverter after about three or four switches, because delay grows roughly with the square of the chain length (it is an RC ladder, see Elmore delay); and watch for charge sharing when a floating node is connected to another.

3.11 Dynamic, domino and cascode voltage switch logic

A dynamic gate stores its output on a capacitor instead of holding it with a pull-up network. A clock φ first precharges the dynamic node to VDD, then lets an nMOS network discharge it if the inputs say so. An N-input gate needs only N + 2 transistors, and its inputs drive only nMOS, so it is small and fast. The price is a clock on every gate, charge sharing, leakage and the rule that inputs may only rise during evaluation. Domino logic adds an inverter so dynamic gates can be chained, and cascode voltage switch logic (CVSL) gives true and complement outputs from one gate.

You need: static CMOS and ratioed logic and charge conservation on capacitors.

Precharge and evaluate

The dynamic node X connects to VDD through a pMOS precharge transistor and to ground through the nMOS pull-down network (PDN) in series with an nMOS evaluate (foot) transistor. Both clock transistors take φ.

  • Precharge (φ = 0): the pMOS is on and the foot is off, so X charges to VDD whatever the inputs.
  • Evaluate (φ = 1): the pMOS is off and the foot is on. If the PDN conducts, X discharges to 0 V; if not, X stays high, held only by its capacitance CX.

X computes NOT(F) of the PDN function, like a static gate, with full rails and no static current. Once X has fallen it cannot rise again until the next precharge, so an input glitch during evaluation can discharge X wrongly and the error is permanent. Inputs must therefore be stable, or only rise, during evaluation. Because the node floats when the PDN is off, leakage slowly discharges it, which sets a lowest clock frequency.

Some syllabi list clock feedthrough too: the rising clock couples through the gate-drain capacitance of the precharge pMOS and can push X briefly above VDD.

Charge sharing and the keeper

In a stacked PDN, an internal node may be at 0 V from an earlier cycle. If during evaluation the top transistor turns on but the path to ground stays open, X shares its charge with that internal node and drops, even though the output should stay high:

VX = (CXVDD + CintVint)/(CX + Cint) Here cint and Vint are the internal node's capacitance and starting voltage. Total charge is conserved.

If VX falls below the switching threshold of the gate that reads it, the logic fails. Cures: precharge the internal nodes too, keep Cint small, and add a keeper, a weak pMOS from VDD to X whose gate is driven by the output inverter. While X is high the output is low, so the keeper is on and replaces charge lost to leakage and sharing. It must be weak enough that the PDN can still pull X down, which makes the gate slightly ratioed.

Domino, np-CMOS and CVSL

A dynamic node is high during precharge. If it drove the nMOS of a second dynamic gate directly, that gate would start evaluating with a false 1 and could discharge wrongly before the first gate settles. Domino logic puts a static inverter after every dynamic node. The domino output is 0 during precharge and can only rise during evaluation, so each stage triggers the next like a row of falling dominoes. A domino gate is non-inverting (AND, OR), so inverting functions need dual-rail signals or are done in static logic at the ends.

np-CMOS (NORA) alternates nMOS-network stages with pMOS-network stages clocked by the inverted clock, so no inverter is needed between them. TSPC (true single-phase clock) logic builds latches and dynamic gates from one clock phase. Only a few syllabi teach these two in detail.

CVSL (also DCVSL) has two nMOS networks, one for F and one for F̄, each pulling down one of two outputs, and two cross-coupled pMOS loads. When one side pulls its output low, it turns on the opposite pMOS, which pulls the other output fully high and turns its own load off. Both outputs swing rail to rail and there is no static current, but during the transition the nMOS must overpower the pMOS, so the sizes are ratioed. CVSL needs complementary inputs and about twice the pull-down transistors; it is used for complex functions such as XOR trees in adders.

Worked example

Question. A domino AND gate has its dynamic node CX = 50 fF precharged to 1.8 V. An internal node of 10 fF in the stack is at 0 V. During evaluation the top nMOS turns on but the bottom one stays off. Find VX. If the output inverter switches at 1.5 V, what is the largest internal capacitance that is safe?

  1. Charge before: Q = 50 fF × 1.8 V + 10 fF × 0 V = 90 fC.
  2. Capacitance after: 50 + 10 = 60 fF.
  3. VX = 90 fC / 60 fF = 1.50 V: the drop of 0.30 V brings X right to the inverter's switching point.
  4. Safe limit: VX ≥ 1.5 V needs 50 × 1.8/(50 + Cint) ≥ 1.5, so 50 + Cint ≤ 60 and Cint ≤ 10 fF.
  5. So 10 fF is the edge, with no margin; a keeper or precharging the internal node is needed.

Answer. VX = 1.50 V; any internal capacitance above 10 fF (one fifth of CX) makes the gate fail without a keeper.

Worked example 2

Question. The same dynamic node (50 fF) leaks 10 nA when the PDN is off and there is no keeper. The output stays valid while X loses at most 0.3 V. The node must hold for half a clock period. Find the lowest safe clock frequency.

  1. Hold time = C × ΔV / I = 50 fF × 0.3 V / 10 nA = 15 fC / 10 nA = 1.5 µs.
  2. Half the period must be no longer than 1.5 µs, so T ≤ 3 µs.
  3. f ≥ 1/3 µs = 333 kHz.

Answer. The clock must not fall below about 333 kHz; for slower or stopped clocks a keeper is required.

Exam traps

  • Cascading plain dynamic gates. The precharged high output falsely turns on the next gate; use domino or np-CMOS.
  • Ignoring charge sharing: X can drop although the pull-down path is open.
  • Making the keeper strong. It then fights the PDN, slowing or stopping evaluation.
  • Letting inputs fall during evaluation. A discharged node cannot recover until the next precharge.
  • Calling CVSL ratioless. The nMOS network must overpower the cross-coupled pMOS while switching.

Takeaways

  1. The VTC gives VM, VIL, VIH and both noise margins; a matched inverter (kn = kp) has VM = VDD/2, which needs a pMOS about µn/µp times wider.
  2. NMOS inverters need a load; every load draws static current when the output is low, and only the enhancement load loses a threshold at the top.
  3. Delay is charge over current: tPHL ≈ CLVDD/(2IDsat), and the RC model with Req ≈ 0.75VDD/IDsat agrees within a few per cent.
  4. Logical effort sizes a path for least delay: equal stage effort F1/N, about 4 per stage.
  5. Wire resistance is R□L/W, wire capacitance about 0.2 fF/µm, and a distributed wire's own delay is 0.38RC, growing as length squared.
  6. The Elmore delay Σ CkRik handles RC trees and transistor stacks by hand; put the latest input nearest the output.
  7. Power is αCVDD²f plus short-circuit plus VDDIleak; leakage wins below the crossover frequency.
  8. Low-power design attacks each term: lower VDD (DVFS, parallelism), lower α (clock gating), lower leakage (power gating beyond break-even, high-Vt cells).
  9. Static CMOS is robust and default; ratioed, pass-transistor and dynamic styles save transistors or time but pay with static current, weak levels, or clocking and charge sharing.

Glossary

Activity factor
Probability that a node makes a 0 → 1 transition in a clock cycle.
Body effect
Rise in threshold voltage when the source is above the substrate.
Branching effort
b = (Con + Coff)/Con at a node that also drives gates off the path.
Charge sharing
Loss of voltage on a dynamic node when it connects to an internal node at a lower voltage.
Clock gating
Stopping the clock to idle registers to save dynamic power.
Coupling capacitance
Capacitance between neighbouring wires; acts doubled when they switch in opposite directions.
CVSL
Cascode voltage switch logic: two complementary nMOS networks with cross-coupled pMOS loads.
Depletion load
nMOS load with a negative threshold and VGS = 0, always on.
Domino logic
Dynamic gates each followed by a static inverter, so they can be cascaded.
DVFS
Dynamic voltage and frequency scaling.
Elmore delay
Sum over all capacitors of C times the resistance shared with the path to the node.
Fan-out of 4 (FO4)
Delay of an inverter driving four copies of itself, about 5τ; a process speed unit.
Fringing capacitance
Capacitance from the sides and edges of a wire to the layers around it.
Keeper
Weak pMOS that holds a dynamic node high against leakage and charge sharing.
Logical effort
Input capacitance of a gate relative to an inverter that gives the same output current.
Noise margin
NMH = VOH − VIH, NML = VIL − VOL.
Parasitic delay
Delay of a gate driving no load, from its own diffusion capacitance.
Power gating
Cutting the supply to an idle block with a header or footer switch.
Propagation delay
Time from the input to the output crossing 50% of VDD.
Pseudo-nMOS
Ratioed logic with an always-on pMOS load and an nMOS pull-down network.
Ratioed logic
Logic whose low level depends on the ratio of pull-down to pull-up strength.
Repeater
Buffer inserted along a long wire so delay grows linearly with length.
Sheet resistance
Resistance of one square of a layer, ρ/t, in Ω/□.
Short-circuit power
Power from current flowing straight through both transistors during a switching input.
Subthreshold leakage
Current of an off transistor below threshold; rises tenfold per S of lower Vt.
Switching threshold
Input voltage VM at which Vout = Vin.
Transmission gate
nMOS and pMOS in parallel with complementary controls, passing both rails.
