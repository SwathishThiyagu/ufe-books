3.1 CMOS inverter DC characteristics and noise margins

The CMOS inverter is one nMOS pull-down and one pMOS pull-up. Its voltage transfer characteristic (VTC) plots Vout against Vin at a fixed supply VDD. The curve sets the switching threshold VM, the output high and low levels VOH and VOL, and the noise margins NMH and NML. These numbers tell a designer whether a gate will restore a weak logic level and how much noise an input can take. The same curve is the first check on every standard cell, memory bit cell and level shifter.

You need: MOS operating regions and drain-current equations from Unit 1, and the CMOS cross-section from Unit 2; the load comparison appears in Chapter 3.2.

The five regions of the VTC

The CMOS inverter has an nMOS pull-down and a pMOS pull-up. The voltage transfer characteristic (VTC) plots Vout against Vin at a fixed supply VDD. VOH is the high output, VOL the low output, and VM the input where Vout = Vin. Vtn and |Vtp| are the nMOS and pMOS threshold magnitudes.

The five regions follow from the transistor states. (1) Vin < Vtn: nMOS off, pMOS linear, Vout = VDD. (2) Vtn ≤ Vin < VM: nMOS saturation, pMOS linear; Vout falls. (3) Vin = VM: both transistors in saturation. (4) VM < Vin ≤ VDD − |Vtp|: nMOS linear, pMOS saturation; Vout approaches 0 V. (5) Vin > VDD − |Vtp|: pMOS off, nMOS linear, Vout = 0 V.

Each region comes from Kirchhoff's current law at the output: the nMOS drain current equals the pMOS drain current. ID is drain current, VGS is gate-source voltage and VDS is drain-source voltage. In saturation ID = (β/2)(VGS − Vt)²; in linear ID = β[(VGS − Vt)VDS − VDS²/2].

β<sub>n</sub> = μ<sub>n</sub>C<sub>ox</sub>(W/L)<sub>n</sub>, β<sub>p</sub> = μ<sub>p</sub>C<sub>ox</sub>(W/L)<sub>p</sub>

β is the transconductance parameter in A/V²; μ is carrier mobility; Cox is oxide capacitance per area; W/L is the width-to-length ratio.

At VM set βn(VM − Vtn)² = βp(VDD − VM − |Vtp|)². Taking positive square roots gives the design equation.

V<sub>M</sub> = (V<sub>DD</sub> + rV<sub>tn</sub> − |V<sub>tp</sub>|)/(1 + r), r = √(β<sub>n</sub>/β<sub>p</sub>)

VM is the switching threshold in volts; r is the strength ratio, dimensionless; VDD, Vtn and |Vtp| are in volts.

Noise margins and the matched case

VIL is the largest input still treated as low; VIH is the smallest input still treated as high. They are the points where the VTC slope dVout/dVin = −1. The noise margins are the gaps between the guaranteed output levels and these input limits.

NM<sub>H</sub> = V<sub>OH</sub> − V<sub>IH</sub>, NM<sub>L</sub> = V<sub>IL</sub> − V<sub>OL</sub>

NMH is the high noise margin and NML is the low noise margin, both in volts; VOH and VOL are the output high and low levels.

A matched inverter has βn = βp and Vtn = |Vtp|. Then r = 1 and VM = VDD/2. For VDD = 5 V, Vtn = |Vtp| = 0.6 V, VM = 2.5 V. The figure uses these values.

Read VOH and VOL from the flat parts: VOH = 5 V and VOL = 0 V in the ideal curve. A larger βn/βp makes r larger and pulls VM down; a stronger pMOS pulls it up.

With VM away from VDD/2, one noise margin shrinks before the other. That is why symmetric sizing is common in gates that must accept signals from both directions.

Design use: ratio and levels

The pMOS overdrive is VDD − Vin − |Vtp|, because the pMOS source is at VDD. The nMOS overdrive is Vin − Vtn. Mixing these expressions is a common sign error.

Use the VTC to check a chain: each gate must drive the next gate so that its output high is at least VIH and its output low is at most VIL, both by the required margin. After DC design, the same widths set load capacitance and delay; see Chapter 3.3. Power follows in Chapter 3.5.

Some syllabi call this the DC transfer characteristic; for a memory cell drawn in both directions it is the butterfly curve. The static CMOS gate in Chapter 3.7 repeats this inverter as its output stage.

For the figure, VDD = 5 V, Vtn = 0.6 V, |Vtp| = 0.6 V and βn = βp. Then VM = (5 + 0.6 − 0.6)/2 = 2.5 V. The plotted crossing at (2.5 V, 2.5 V) is VM.

Worked example

Question. A matched CMOS inverter has VDD = 3.3 V, Vtn = 0.6 V, |Vtp| = 0.6 V and βn = βp. Find VM, and state the region of both transistors at Vin = VM. Also find each overdrive voltage.

  1. Matched means βn/βp = 1, so r = 1.
  2. At Vin = VM, both transistors are in saturation. Equate saturation currents: βn(VM − 0.6)² = βp(3.3 − VM − 0.6)².
  3. Equal β gives VM − 0.6 = 3.3 − VM − 0.6 = 2.7 − VM. Thus 2VM = 3.3 V, so VM = 1.65 V.
  4. At VM, Vout = VM = 1.65 V. nMOS: VGS = 1.65 V, VDS = 1.65 V, overdrive = 1.65 − 0.6 = 1.05 V. VDS > overdrive, so saturation.
  5. pMOS: VSG = 3.3 − 1.65 = 1.65 V, |VSD| = 3.3 − 1.65 = 1.65 V, overdrive = 1.65 − 0.6 = 1.05 V. |VSD| > overdrive, so saturation.

Answer. VM = 1.65 V; both transistors are in saturation with overdrive 1.05 V each.

Exam traps

  • Do not assume VM = VDD/2 unless βn = βp and Vtn = |Vtp|; a stronger nMOS lowers VM.
  • The pMOS overdrive is VDD − Vin − |Vtp|, not Vin − |Vtp|; the pMOS source sits at VDD.
  • VIL and VIH are not the transistor threshold voltages; they are the input points where |dVout/dVin| = 1.
  • Noise margin is not the same as logic swing; swing is VOH − VOL, while NMH = VOH − VIH and NML = VIL − VOL subtract the undefined input bands.

3.2 NMOS inverters: resistive, enhancement, depletion, pseudo-nMOS loads

An NMOS inverter is one nMOS driver pulling the output down plus a load pulling it up. The driver alone cannot make a logic high, so the load sets output levels. Four loads are common: resistor, enhancement nMOS with gate at VDD, depletion nMOS with gate at its source, and pMOS with gate at ground (pseudo-nMOS). Each gives different VOL, VOH and static current. Old NMOS chips used these; today they appear as ratioed logic and weak pull-ups inside CMOS blocks.

You need the nMOS linear and saturation current equations from Unit 1, and the voltage transfer curve and noise-margin definitions from 3.1.

The load line and the resistive load

The driver is a single nMOS: drain at the output, source at ground, gate at the input. It can only pull down. The load pulls up.

With a resistor RL from VDD to the output, the load current is I = (VDD − Vout)/RL. On the driver's I-V curves this is a straight line through (Vout = VDD, I = 0) with slope −1/RL. Each input voltage selects one driver curve; where that curve crosses the load line is the operating point.

Vin = 0: the driver is off, no current flows, so VOH = VDD.

Vin = VDD: the driver sinks current while holding the output low. The crossing lies in the linear region, because VOL is small compared with VGS − Vtn. Equate the two currents:

(V<sub>DD</sub> − V<sub>OL</sub>)/R<sub>L</sub> = k<sub>n</sub>[(V<sub>DD</sub> − V<sub>tn</sub>)V<sub>OL</sub> − V<sub>OL</sub>²/2]

VDD is the supply in volts, RL the load resistor in ohms, kn = µnCox(W/L) the driver gain in A/V², Vtn the driver threshold in volts, VOL the output low voltage. The left side is the resistor current, the right side the linear-region driver current.

A small VOL needs a large RL and a wide driver. A 100 kΩ resistor is far larger than a transistor and draws current whenever the output is low: at 5 V, tens of microamperes per gate, so ten thousand gates burn watts. The resistor was replaced by a transistor, small and ratioed by width.

Enhancement and depletion NMOS loads

Enhancement load. An nMOS with drain at VDD, source at the output and gate at VDD. Then VGS = VDD − Vout, so it conducts strongly only while the output is well below VDD.

With Vin = 0 the driver is off, so no current flows; zero load current needs VGS = Vtn, giving VOH = VDD − Vtn. Body effect raises that threshold, so VOH drops further. With Vin = VDD the load is saturated and the driver linear; equating the currents gives VOL, a few tenths of a volt.

Depletion load. An nMOS with a negative threshold (an extra channel implant) has drain at VDD, source at the output and gate at the source. Then VGS = 0 always and the device is on. While saturated it is a constant current source of value IDSS.

I<sub>DSS</sub> = (k<sub>nL</sub>/2)V<sub>td</sub>² = k<sub>n</sub>[(V<sub>DD</sub> − V<sub>tn</sub>)V<sub>OL</sub> − V<sub>OL</sub>²/2]

knL is the load gain in A/V², Vtd the magnitude of the depletion threshold in volts (the threshold is −Vtd), kn and Vtn belong to the driver, VOL is the output low voltage.

Now VOH = VDD. At Vout = VDD the load has VDS = 0 and carries no current, and the driver is off, so zero current satisfies both devices. As the output falls the load becomes a constant-current pull-up, so a modest driver already gives VOL below 0.1 V. The price is a process step and a load that is on whenever the output is low.

Pseudo-nMOS and the four compared

Pseudo-nMOS. A pMOS load: source at VDD, drain at the output, gate at ground. Then VGS = −VDD at all times, so it is a permanent current source and VOH = VDD. While the pMOS is saturated:

(k<sub>p</sub>/2)(V<sub>DD</sub> − |V<sub>tp</sub>|)² = k<sub>n</sub>[(V<sub>DD</sub> − V<sub>tn</sub>)V<sub>OL</sub> − V<sub>OL</sub>²/2]

kp is the pMOS gain in A/V², Vtp its threshold (negative, so |Vtp| is its magnitude in volts), and the right side is the driver in the linear region.

VOL depends on the ratio kp/kn, not on absolute widths, so this is ratioed logic (3.7). A pMOS several times weaker than the driver keeps VOL a few tenths of a volt. Static current flows whenever the driver is on.

Worked example

Question. A resistive-load NMOS inverter has VDD = 5 V, RL = 100 kΩ, driver kn = 20 µA/V² and Vtn = 0.8 V. The input is at 5 V. Find VOL and the static supply current.

  1. Input high: VGS = 5 V > 0.8 V, so the driver is on.
  2. Try saturation: ID = 10 µA/V² × (4.2 V)² = 176.4 µA, which would drop 17.64 V across RL, more than VDD. So the driver is linear.
  3. Driver: ID = 20 µA/V² × [4.2VOL − VOL²/2]. Resistor: ID = (5 − VOL)/100 kΩ.
  4. Equate and multiply by 100 kΩ: 5 − VOL = 8.4VOL − VOL², so VOL² − 9.4VOL + 5 = 0.
  5. Solve: VOL = (9.4 − √(88.36 − 20))/2 = (9.4 − 8.268)/2 = 0.566 V. The root 8.83 V lies above VDD and is rejected.
  6. Check: 0.566 V < 4.2 V, so the linear assumption holds.
  7. Static current: I = (5 − 0.566)/100 kΩ = 44.3 µA, so static power = 5 V × 44.3 µA = 0.22 mW.

Answer. VOL ≈ 0.57 V and the inverter draws 44.3 µA (0.22 mW) from the 5 V supply.

Exam traps

  • Using the saturation equation for the driver at the output low point. There VDS = VOL is much smaller than VGS − Vtn, so the driver is linear.
  • Forgetting the enhancement load loses a threshold: VOH = VDD − Vtn, and body effect lowers it further.
  • Thinking a depletion load is off because VGS = 0. It carries IDSS and only stops at VDS = 0, when the output is at VDD.
  • Swapping source and drain of the load. In enhancement and depletion loads the source is the output node, so the load's VGS and VSB follow the output.

Analyze Why is pseudo-nMOS called ratioed logic, and what happens to VOL if the pMOS is made twice as strong? 4 marks

VOL comes from balancing load current against driver current, so it depends on kp/kn, not on absolute sizes; scaling both widths changes nothing. Doubling kp doubles the pull-up current, so the driver must sink more and the output settles at a higher VOL, with double the static current.

Analyze How does the voltage transfer curve of a pseudo-nMOS inverter differ from that of a CMOS inverter, and what does that do to the two noise margins? 4 marks

The weak pull-up makes the curve asymmetric, with the switching threshold well below VDD/2, and both VIL and VIH move to low input values. With VOH = VDD the high noise margin VOH − VIH is large, but the low noise margin VIL − VOL is small, so the gate is weak against a high input on the driven node.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Pseudo-nMOS survives as the weak keeper that holds a dynamic node high, and as the single load shared by a large NOR plane in PLAs and memory arrays, where saving transistors matters more than static current. Depletion-mode devices appear in compound-semiconductor logic such as GaAs DCFL and in analog bias circuits. Designers now combine a weak always-on pMOS with a feedback inverter to build a keeper that fights charge sharing and leakage in domino gates.

3.3 Switching characteristics and propagation delay

An inverter cannot switch instantly: the output node has capacitance and the transistors supply only limited current. Delay is the time the output takes to reach the 50% point after the input crosses it. Clock frequency, setup slack and hold slack are all built from these delays. This chapter defines tPHL, tPLH, tp, tr and tf, gives two hand estimates, and lists what loads the node.

You need Chapter 3.1 for the inverter's voltage transfer curve, and Unit 1 for MOSFET capacitances.

Four numbers and one average

Every delay is measured between 50% crossings. VDD is the supply voltage, so the 50% point is VDD/2.

  • tPHL — output high-to-low: input crosses VDD/2 rising, output crosses VDD/2 falling. The nMOS pull-down sets it.
  • tPLH — output low-to-high: input crosses VDD/2 falling, output crosses it rising. The pMOS pull-up sets it.
  • tp — the average of the two.
  • tr — rise time, output 10% to 90% of VDD (some books use 20% to 80%).
  • tf — fall time, output 90% to 10% of VDD.
t<sub>p</sub> = (t<sub>PHL</sub> + t<sub>PLH</sub>)/2

tPHL, tPLH and tp are in seconds (usually ns or ps).

Rise time and delay answer different questions. tr describes the shape of one edge; tp describes when that edge arrives. A node with a weak resistive pull-up crosses 50% quickly and then crawls up to VDD, so its tr can be several times its tp.

A ring oscillator of N inverters has period 2·N·tp, so its frequency is 1/(2·N·tp). Measuring that frequency on silicon measures tp.

Estimating delay by average current

Count the charge. For tPHL the output falls from VDD to VDD/2, so the charge removed is CL·VDD/2. The nMOS is saturated only until the output reaches VDD − Vtn; below that it enters the linear region and its current drops. Using the saturation current for the whole half swing gives an optimistic estimate, so the real delay is a little longer.

I<sub>Dsat</sub> = (k<sub>n</sub>/2)(V<sub>DD</sub> − V<sub>tn</sub>)²

kn = µnCox(W/L) is the nMOS gain in A/V²; Vtn is the nMOS threshold in V; VDD is the supply in V; IDsat is in A. Channel-length modulation is ignored.

t<sub>PHL</sub> ≈ C<sub>L</sub>·V<sub>DD</sub>/(2·I<sub>Dsat</sub>)

CL is the total load capacitance in farads, VDD in volts, IDsat in amperes; tPHL comes out in seconds.

  1. Find kn and Vtn from the process data and the drawn W/L.
  2. Compute IDsat from the overdrive VDD − Vtn.
  3. Add up CL: drain diffusions, gate-drain overlaps, wire, and the input capacitance of every fanout gate.
  4. Apply the formula. Repeat with the pMOS current for tPLH, then average.

The factor 2 matters: the delay stops at the 50% point, so only half the swing is charged. A full swing to 0 V would take about twice as long, and in practice longer, because the nMOS leaves saturation.

The RC method treats the ON transistor as a resistance Req. The output follows VDD(1 − e−t/τ) with τ = ReqCL, and reaches x = 0.5 at t = τ·ln2.

t<sub>p</sub> ≈ 0.69·R<sub>eq</sub>·C<sub>L</sub>

Req is the equivalent ON resistance in Ω (roughly VDD/(2·IDsat)); 0.69 = ln 2; tp is in seconds.

The two estimates differ by roughly 30%, because one averages current and the other fixes one equivalent resistance. Both are hand estimates; simulation is the reference.

Which capacitances load the node

CL is rarely one device. It is the sum of four things: drain diffusion capacitance of the nMOS and pMOS, gate-drain overlap capacitance, wire capacitance, and the input capacitance of each fanout gate.

One gate's input capacitance is its gate oxide capacitance plus overlap, for both transistors: Cin ≈ Cox(WnLn + WpLp) + overlaps. Doubling the fanout roughly doubles CL and so doubles the delay, if the driver current is fixed.

The gate-drain capacitance Cgd bridges input and output, so it is not a simple load. When the output swings A times as far as the input, the input must supply charge for Cgd(1 + A). This is the Miller effect.

C<sub>in,eff</sub> = C<sub>g</sub> + C<sub>gd</sub>(1 + A)

Cg is the gate capacitance to the channel, Cgd is the gate-drain overlap, and A = |dVout/dVin| in the transition region, typically 5 to 20 for a sharp inverter.

So a switching inverter loads its driver more heavily than a static capacitance measurement suggests. Separately, Cgd feeds a short spike to the output before the real transition, which appears as a glitch on a dynamic node. No output node is left floating: leakage and charge sharing would make its level undefined.

This sets up the sizing trade-off. A wider driver passes more current but presents more input capacitance to the stage before it; see Chapter 3.4. Delay also trades against the dynamic energy of Chapter 3.5: faster edges mean more short-circuit current.

Question. A CMOS inverter drives CL = 50 fF. The nMOS has kn = 200 µA/V² and Vtn = 0.5 V; the supply is VDD = 1.8 V. Estimate tPHL in nanoseconds.

  4. tPHL = Q/IDsat = 4.5×10−14/1.69×10−4 = 2.66×10−10 s = 266 ps.

Exam traps

  • Swapping tPHL and tPLH. tPHL is the output falling, driven by the nMOS and triggered by a rising input. Examiners check this first.
  • Dropping the factor 2. Delay ends at the 50% point, so only half the swing is charged; CLVDD/IDsat doubles the answer.
  • Using VDD instead of the overdrive VDD − Vtn inside the square. Current depends on how far above threshold the gate is driven.
  • Confusing rise time with delay. tr is 10% to 90% on the output alone; tp is measured between input and output 50% points.
  • Mixing units. fF × V = fC and µA × ns = fC, so keep fF with µA and read the answer in ns. A 1000× slip is the commonest error here.

3.4 Delay estimation, logical effort and transistor sizing

Logical effort is a hand calculation that predicts the delay of a logic path before you size transistors. It separates a gate's topology cost (logical effort g and parasitic delay p) from its load (electrical effort h). Multiplying stage efforts gives a path effort F. For a fixed load and a fixed number of stages, delay is smallest when every stage carries the same effort. The result tells you how wide to make each transistor. Cell designers, synthesis tools and students checking a timing budget use it when a quick estimate is more useful than a full SPICE run.

You need Chapter 3.3 for propagation delay and load capacitance.

The delay model for one gate

A logic gate drives capacitance through transistors that have finite on-resistance. Chapter 3.3 called the result propagation delay. Logical effort writes that delay in units of τ. A reference inverter with no external load has delay τ. Driving an identical inverter, it has delay 2τ in this model. The model has two parts: a load-dependent part and a fixed part.

The electrical effort h = Cout/Cin. Cout is the total capacitance at the gate output, in farads. Cin is the capacitance looking into the gate input, in farads. h is dimensionless and is also called fanout. The logical effort g is dimensionless. It compares the input capacitance of the gate with an inverter that can deliver the same output current. A gate with large g needs more input capacitance for the same drive. The parasitic delay p is the delay with no external load, in τ units. It comes from the gate's own diffusion capacitance.

d = (g h + p) τ = (f + p) τ

d is the stage delay in seconds; τ is the reference inverter delay in seconds; g is logical effort (dimensionless); h = Cout/Cin is electrical effort (dimensionless); p is parasitic delay in τ units; f = g h is stage effort (dimensionless).

Equal stage effort and transistor sizing

Consider a path of N gates from an input capacitance Cin,path to an output load Cout,path. The path logical effort G = Π gi. The path electrical effort H = Cout,path/Cin,path. If a node drives extra gates off the path, include branch effort b = (Con + Coff)/Con, where Con is capacitance on the path and Coff is capacitance off the path. The path branch effort B = Π bi. The path effort F = G B H. With no branches B=1.

The path delay in τ units is D = Σ fi + Σ pi. For fixed F and N, the arithmetic mean–geometric mean inequality says Σ fi is smallest when all fi equal. Set each stage effort to f = F1/N. Then D = N f + P, where P = Σ pi.

F = G B H, f = F1/N, D = N f + P

F is path effort; G = Π gi; B = Π bi; H = Cout,path/Cin,path; N is number of stages; f is equal stage effort; P = Σ pi; D is path delay in τ units.

  1. List the gates in order and the load capacitance at each node.
  2. Compute G = product of g values; H = Cout,path/Cin,path; B = product of b values.
  3. Compute F = G B H; N = number of stages; f = F1/N.
  4. Work backward from the load. For stage i, Cin,i = gi Cout,i/f, where Cout,i is the total capacitance driven by that stage.
  5. Set transistor widths in proportion to Cin,i. For equal channel length, W scales with capacitance.

This is path delay optimisation; some syllabi call it the method of logical effort. It gives first-pass sizes. A static timing tool refines them.

Worked example

Question. A path has three stages: INV → NAND2 → INV. Its final load is 64 times the input capacitance C of a minimum inverter. Ignore branches and use the standard values in this chapter.   • Mixing up g and p: g multiplies fanout, while p is a constant intercept. A NAND2 has larger p (2τ) than an inverter (1τ).

3.5 Static and dynamic power dissipation

Every CMOS gate draws current from the supply. Part of that current charges and discharges capacitances when the output changes; part leaks through transistors that are meant to be off. The first is dynamic power, the second is static power. Dynamic power sets the battery life of a phone and the heat of a server. Static power decides whether a chip can sit idle without draining its battery. Both are estimated early, before layout, so that supply voltage, clock frequency and transistor sizes can be chosen.

You need: Chapter 3.3 for load capacitance, and the leakage discussion in Unit 1.

Dynamic power: switching and short-circuit

A CMOS gate drives a load capacitance CL. When the output goes from 0 to 1, the pMOS connects the output to VDD and supplies charge Q = CL VDD. The supply delivers energy E = Q VDD = CL VDD². Half of this energy is stored in CL; half is dissipated in the pMOS. When the output goes from 1 to 0, the stored half is dissipated in the nMOS. One full cycle therefore takes CL VDD² from the supply.

P<sub>dyn</sub> = P<sub>sw</sub> + P<sub>sc</sub> = α C<sub>L</sub> V<sub>DD</sub>² f + P<sub>sc</sub>

Pdyn is total dynamic power (W); Psw is switching power (W); Psc is short-circuit power (W); α is activity factor (dimensionless, 0→1 transitions per clock cycle); CL is load capacitance (F); VDD is supply voltage (V); f is clock frequency (Hz).

The activity factor α counts 0→1 transitions, not all transitions. A clock has α = 1. Data that toggles every cycle has α = 0.5. Random data with equal 0 and 1 has α = 0.25.

Short-circuit power comes from the brief time in a switching input when both nMOS and pMOS are on. A simple symmetric-inverter model gives the next formula.

P<sub>sc</sub> ≈ (β/12)(V<sub>DD</sub> − 2V<sub>t</sub>)³ τ f

β = μ Cox W/L is transistor gain (A/V²); Vt is threshold voltage magnitude (V); τ is input rise or fall time (s); f is clock frequency (Hz).

Fast edges make τ small, so Psc falls. If VDD ≤ 2Vt, the simple model gives no short-circuit path. Psc is still dynamic: it happens only while the input changes.

Static power: leakage paths

Static power is drawn when inputs do not change. In static CMOS an output is connected to one rail, so there is no deliberate path from VDD to ground. Current still leaks through off transistors and reverse-biased junctions.

  • Subthreshold leakage: drain-to-source current when VGS is below Vt. It rises exponentially as Vt falls and increases strongly with temperature.
  • Gate-oxide leakage: carriers tunnel through the thin gate oxide. It grows as the oxide becomes thinner.
  • Junction leakage: reverse-biased source and drain junctions leak. In scaled devices this includes band-to-band tunneling.
P<sub>static</sub> = V<sub>DD</sub> I<sub>leak</sub> = V<sub>DD</sub>(I<sub>sub</sub> + I<sub>gate</sub> + I<sub>junction</sub>)

Pstatic is static power (W); VDD is supply voltage (V); Ileak is total leakage current (A); Isub, Igate and Ijunction are the three leakage components (A).

Static power does not contain f. At low clock frequency it can be larger than dynamic power. Some syllabi call this standby power or leakage power.

Total power and the activity factor

Total power adds the dynamic and static parts.

P<sub>total</sub> = α C<sub>L</sub> V<sub>DD</sub>² f + P<sub>sc</sub> + V<sub>DD</sub> I<sub>leak</sub>

Ptotal is total power (W); the first term is switching power; Psc is short-circuit power (W); VDD Ileak is static power (W).

The activity factor is the only term that depends on what the circuit computes. A datapath that switches every cycle has a higher α than a control block that wakes occasionally. Dynamic logic in Chapter 3.9 can switch more nodes per cycle, so its α is higher for the same function.

At high f, α CL VDD² f usually dominates. At low f, VDD Ileak remains. Lowering VDD helps the switching term as VDD² and also lowers static power for a given leakage current. Methods that trade speed, voltage or idle blocks for power are in Chapter 3.6.

A power estimate is only as good as its α and CL. α comes from simulation or from signal statistics. CL comes from gate capacitance, wiring capacitance and fanout; Chapter 3.3 gives the load model.

Worked example

Question. A CMOS chip has VDD = 1.8 V, load capacitance CL = 50 pF, clock frequency f = 100 MHz and activity factor α = 0.1. Leakage current is 10 µA. Find the switching power, the leakage power and the total power. Neglect short-circuit power.

  1. VDD² = (1.8 V)² = 3.24 V².
  2. CL VDD² = 50 pF × 3.24 V² = 50×10⁻¹² F × 3.24 V² = 1.62×10⁻¹⁰ J.
  3. Psw = α CL VDD² f = 0.1 × 1.62×10⁻¹⁰ J × 100×10⁶ s⁻¹ = 1.62×10⁻³ W = 1.62 mW.
  4. Pleak = VDD Ileak = 1.8 V × 10×10⁻⁶ A = 18×10⁻⁶ W = 18 µW = 0.018 mW.
  5. Ptotal = 1.62 mW + 0.018 mW = 1.638 mW.

Answer. The switching power is 1.62 mW, the leakage power is 18 µW, and the total power is 1.638 mW.

Exam traps

  • Adding CL VDD² f and VDD Ileak with mixed units; convert µA and mW to watts or milliwatts first.
  • Putting f into the leakage term; static power is VDD Ileak and has no clock frequency.
  • Calling short-circuit power static because both transistors are on; it flows only during switching, so it is dynamic.
  • Assuming leakage is zero at low frequency; at low f leakage can dominate total power.

3.6 Low-power design and power management

Low-power design keeps a CMOS chip inside its power and thermal budget. Dynamic energy falls if you switch fewer nodes, use smaller capacitance, or lower the supply; leakage falls if you cut power to idle blocks or use high-threshold transistors. A power manager applies these methods block by block. Phones, laptops and battery sensors use it; even a mains-powered router uses it to avoid a fan. This chapter covers clock gating, power gating, DVFS, multi-threshold cells and sleep modes, and their costs.

You need: Chapter 3.5 for the dynamic and static power components.

Where the power goes, and clock gating

Dynamic power comes from charging and discharging node capacitance, plus a short-circuit part. Short-circuit power flows for a brief moment during a transition, when both the nMOS and the pMOS of a gate conduct and current runs straight from VDD to ground. The switching part dominates, and clock gating, power gating and DVFS all attack it. The supply voltage is the strongest knob because it appears squared.

P<sub>dyn</sub> = α C V<sub>DD</sub><sup>2</sup> f

α is the activity factor, the fraction of cycles a node switches (0 to 1); C is the switched capacitance in farads; VDD is the supply voltage in volts; f is the clock frequency in hertz.

Energy per clock cycle is E = α C VDD2. One single 0-to-1 switching event on that capacitance draws C VDD2. Halving VDD cuts dynamic energy per cycle to one quarter at the same activity and capacitance.

Clock gating removes edges from idle registers. An enable signal is ANDed with the clock, so a block receives no clock edges when it has no new data. The clock tree and the flip-flops then stop switching. Integrated clock-gating cells include a latch so the enable cannot change while the clock is high, which avoids a narrow glitch pulse.

  • Saves dynamic power in the clock tree and registers.
  • Does not cut leakage, because the supply stays on.
  • Costs an enable signal and a gate for each gated block.

Power gating, multi-Vt cells and sleep modes

Leakage flows through off transistors. It rises with temperature and is a large share of total power in nanometre processes. Power gating places a switch between a block and a supply rail: a header pMOS to VDD or a footer nMOS to ground.

In normal mode the switch is on and the block works. In sleep mode the switch is off and the block sees no supply. The main saving is leakage while the block is off. Dynamic power in that block is already zero because it is not switching.

Costs are real. The switch adds area and resistance, so it must be sized for the block current. The block loses its state unless retention flip-flops keep it. Turning on causes a rush current that can disturb the grid, and the block cannot work until the supply settles: that is wake-up latency.

Multi-threshold cells give a second knob. A high-Vt transistor has a larger threshold, so it leaks less but switches slower. A low-Vt transistor is fast but leaky. Synthesis uses low-Vt cells on critical paths and high-Vt cells elsewhere.

Sleep modes form a ladder: clock gating (fast, small saving), state retention (keeps registers), and full power gating (largest saving, longest wake-up).

DVFS and the power manager

DVFS means dynamic voltage and frequency scaling. The power manager chooses a voltage-frequency pair from a table. A lower supply saves energy, but transistors get slower, so the clock must also fall. The pair must meet timing at every corner.

For a workload change from (V1, f1) to (V2, f2), the dynamic power ratio is (V2/V1)2 × (f2/f1) when capacitance and activity stay fixed.

The manager gets workload hints from the operating system, from performance counters, or from a hardware queue. It must respect regulator and PLL delays. Changing the pair too often spends more energy than it saves, so governors use hysteresis and minimum residency times.

Question. A block runs at VDD = 1.2 V and f = 1 GHz. DVFS moves it to VDD = 1.0 V and f = 0.8 GHz. The activity factor and capacitance stay the same. Find the ratio of new dynamic power to old dynamic power and the percentage saving.

3.7 Static CMOS and ratioed logic

Static CMOS builds every gate from two complementary networks: a pMOS pull-up that connects the output to VDD and an nMOS pull-down that connects it to ground. In steady state one network is off, so the output reaches a rail and no direct path carries current. Ratioed logic removes part of that complement: an always-on load pulls up while an nMOS network pulls down. It saves transistors, but the low output sits above 0 V and the load draws static current whenever the output is low. These styles appear in dense gates, SRAM-like cells and older NMOS designs.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for the inverter voltage transfer curve and Chapter 3.2 for pseudo-nMOS loads.

Static CMOS: two complementary networks

Static CMOS builds a gate from two complementary networks. The pMOS pull-up network connects the output to VDD when it is on. The nMOS pull-down network connects the output to ground when it is on. For every input combination, exactly one network conducts. In an inverter, a high input turns the nMOS on and the output is 0 V; a low input turns the pMOS on and the output is VDD. In a multi-input gate the pull-down conducts only when its own combination of inputs is satisfied, and the pull-up is then off. Some syllabi call this complementary CMOS.

Drawing rule: the pull-down network uses nMOS transistors and implements the logic function directly. The pull-up network uses pMOS transistors and is the dual: series branches become parallel, and parallel branches become series. An inverter needs 1 pMOS and 1 nMOS. A two-input NAND or NOR needs 4 transistors. A simple N-input gate needs 2N transistors.

Because one network is always off in steady state, there is no direct path from VDD to ground. The output reaches a rail: VOH = VDD and VOL = 0 V. Full swing gives large noise margins, as Chapter 3.1 explains. Static current is only leakage. The cost is transistor count and input capacitance: 2N transistors for N inputs, and the pMOS network is often widened to match nMOS drive.

Ratioed logic: an always-on load

Ratioed logic replaces the complementary pull-up with an always-on load. In pseudo-nMOS, the load is a pMOS transistor with its gate tied to 0 V. The pMOS is always on. The nMOS pull-down network still implements the logic. In a saturated nMOS load, the load is an nMOS transistor with its gate tied to VDD. Both are ratioed: the output low voltage depends on the ratio of the pull-down strength to the load strength.

When the output is high, the nMOS network is off. In pseudo-nMOS the pMOS load pulls the output all the way to VDD. In a saturated nMOS load the load turns off once its gate-source voltage falls to Vtn, where Vtn is the nMOS threshold voltage, so the output high is VOH = VDD − Vtn. The swing is degraded at both ends. In either case no current flows except leakage. When the output is low, the nMOS network is on. The load and the driver both conduct, so a static current flows from VDD to ground. The output does not reach 0 V. The load and driver form a voltage divider, so VOL > 0 V.

I<sub>load</sub> = (k<sub>p</sub>/2)(V<sub>DD</sub> − |V<sub>tp</sub>|)²

Iload is the pMOS saturation current in µA; kp = µpCox(W/L)p in µA/V²; VDD is the supply in V; |Vtp| is the pMOS threshold magnitude in V. This holds when the pMOS is in saturation. The pMOS is in saturation when |VDS| ≥ |VGS| − |Vtp|, that is VDD − VOL ≥ VDD − |Vtp|, which needs VOL ≤ |Vtp|. The static power is Pstatic = VDD × Iload when the output is low. Pseudo-nMOS uses N+1 transistors for an N-input gate, not 2N. The saving in area is the reason it exists.

Question. A pseudo-nMOS inverter uses VDD = 2.5 V, kn = 100 µA/V², Vtn = 0.5 V, kp = 20 µA/V² and |Vtp| = 0.5 V. The pMOS gate is tied to 0 V and the nMOS input is at VDD. (a) Find the load current. (b) Find VOL and state whether the pMOS load is in saturation.

Exam traps

  • Never say a ratioed gate has zero static power. The load conducts whenever the output is low, so Pstatic = VDD × Iload.
  • Do not use the driver saturation equation when VOL is small. The driver is in the linear region because VDS = VOL is below VGS − Vtn.

  • Static CMOS has almost no static current in steady state, but it has short-circuit current during switching. That is dynamic power, not the always-on load current of ratioed logic.

3.8 Pass transistor logic and transmission gates

A pass transistor connects an input to an output through a switch, so data flows without a logic gate. An nMOS alone cannot pass a full logic 1: it stops one threshold voltage below its gate. A pMOS alone cannot pass a full logic 0. A transmission gate puts an nMOS and a pMOS in parallel, with complementary controls, and passes both rails. These circuits are used in multiplexers, XOR gates, flip-flops and adder cells, where saving transistors matters more than restoring every node.

You need: threshold voltage and body effect from Unit 1, and static CMOS from Chapter 3.7.

nMOS pass transistor and threshold drop

An nMOS pass transistor is one nMOS switch placed between a data input A and an output Y. The gate is the control C. When C is high, the channel can connect A to Y. The switch is not a gate: it does not invert or restore the signal; it only passes it.

Passing a low input works well. With A = 0 V and C = VDD, the input side A is the source because it is at the lower potential. Then VGS = VG − VA = VDD − 0 V = VDD, independent of Y. The nMOS stays on for the whole discharge, so Y reaches 0 V. Use VGS = C − Y only for the high-level case, where the output Y is the source.

Passing a high input is different. Start with Y at 0 V and A = VDD. The nMOS turns on and charges Y. As Y rises, VGS falls because VGS = C − Y; here Y is the source. The transistor turns off when VGS = Vtn, where Vtn is the nMOS threshold voltage in V. At that point Y stops at VDD − Vtn. The highest output is min(Vin, VG − Vtn).

V<sub>out(max)</sub> = min(V<sub>in</sub>, V<sub>G</sub> − V<sub>tn</sub>)

Vout(max) is the highest output voltage in V; Vin is the input high level in V; VG is the gate voltage in V; Vtn is the nMOS threshold in V.

When Y is above ground, the source-to-body voltage VSB is not zero. The threshold rises with body effect:

V<sub>t</sub> = V<sub>t0</sub> + γ(√(2φ<sub>F</sub> + V<sub>SB</sub>) − √(2φ<sub>F</sub>))

Vt is the body-biased threshold in V; Vt0 is the zero-bias threshold in V; γ is the body-effect coefficient in V1/2; 2φF is the surface potential parameter in V; VSB is the source-to-body voltage in V.

A larger Vtn means a larger drop. The body of the nMOS is tied to ground in a standard n-well process, so this effect is always present when the source node is high.

pMOS pass transistor and transmission gate

A pMOS pass transistor is the dual. It passes a high input well because its source can sit at the high rail and its gate at 0 V keeps VGS negative. It passes a low input poorly: the output falls only to |Vtp| above ground, then the pMOS turns off. So one transistor alone always loses one threshold on one rail.

A transmission gate (TG) fixes this by placing an nMOS and a pMOS in parallel. The nMOS gate is driven by C, the pMOS gate by C bar, the complement of C. When C = 1, both are on and Y = A. When C = 0, both are off and Y is high impedance (high-Z). The pMOS carries the high part of the swing; the nMOS carries the low part. Together they pass the full rail.

The symbol is a switch with two control inputs, one drawn with a bubble for the pMOS. Some syllabi call a network of these switches pass transistor logic (PTL). When complementary data and control signals are used in several branches, some syllabi call it complementary pass-transistor logic. The idea is the same: data travels through switches, not through a complementary CMOS gate.

Using transmission gates and PTL

Transmission gates build useful functions with few transistors. Two TGs form a 2:1 multiplexer: one TG connects A when C = 1, the other connects B when C = 0. An XOR gate uses two TGs and two inverters, or a small TG network, and is common in adder cells. A D flip-flop can use TGs as the input and feedback switches.

PTL saves transistors but has costs. A passed high through an nMOS alone stops at VDD − Vtn, and a passed low through a pMOS alone stops at |Vtp| above ground, not at 0 V. So outputs may be weak. Weak nodes are slower and more sensitive to noise. Each stage whose gate is held at VDD stops at VDD − Vtn. Drops pile up only when a later gate or control is itself a passed level, as in some PTL networks. Delay grows with chain length in both cases. If the output drives a gate, a restoring inverter or a full CMOS stage is added at the end. Charge sharing is another issue: when switches change, charge on an internal node can redistribute and disturb the output. Floating nodes must not drive control inputs.

Use PTL where the function is switch-like: multiplexers, XOR/XNOR, adders and flip-flops. Use static CMOS from Chapter 3.7 when full rail restoration and robust noise margins are required. Dynamic logic in Chapter 3.9 also uses clocked switches, but it precharges first.

Worked example

Question. An nMOS pass transistor has Vin = 3.3 V, gate voltage VG = 3.3 V and threshold Vtn = 0.6 V. (a) Find the maximum output voltage. (b) A transmission gate adds a pMOS in parallel with its gate at 0 V. What maximum output does the transmission gate pass?

  1. For the nMOS passing a high level, the source is the output Y. VGS = VG − Y.
  2. The nMOS turns off at VGS = Vtn. Set VG − Y = Vtn.
  3. Solve: Y = VG − Vtn = 3.3 V − 0.6 V = 2.7 V.
  4. The transmission gate has a pMOS in parallel. Its gate is at 0 V, its source is at 3.3 V, so VGS = −3.3 V and the pMOS is on.
  5. The pMOS passes the full high rail, so the TG maximum output is 3.3 V.

Answer. The nMOS alone passes a maximum of 2.7 V; the transmission gate passes 3.3 V.

Exam traps

  • Do not use Vout = Vin for an nMOS passing a high level. The output stops at VG − Vtn, and body effect makes Vtn larger.
  • Do not say a pMOS passes a low level perfectly. A pMOS alone stops at |Vtp| above ground.
  • Do not confuse a transmission gate with a single pass transistor. The TG has both nMOS and pMOS in parallel and needs C and C bar.
  • Do not ignore high-Z. When C = 0, a TG output is floating; a following gate must not be left without a defined level.
  • Do not forget that the pMOS gate is controlled by C bar, not C. Reversing the controls turns both transistors off or makes the switch behave wrongly.

3.9 Dynamic, domino and cascode voltage switch logic

Static CMOS waits for inputs; dynamic logic uses a clock to precharge a node high, then evaluate a pull-down network. This saves transistors and can be faster, but the floating dynamic node brings charge sharing and leakage problems. Domino logic adds an inverter so gates can cascade, and cascode voltage switch logic (CVSL) uses a cross-coupled pMOS pair for differential outputs. These styles appear in high-speed datapaths and adders. The cost is clock power, careful timing and extra design rules.

You need: Chapter 3.3 for capacitance and Chapter 3.8 for pass transistors.

Dynamic gate: precharge, evaluate, hold

A dynamic gate has a clock φ (phi). When φ = 0, a pMOS precharge transistor connects the dynamic node to VDD. The node voltage Vdyn becomes VDD. The evaluate nMOS is off, so the pull-down network cannot discharge.

When φ = 1, precharge turns off and evaluate turns on. If the NMOS pull-down network conducts, it discharges Cdyn to 0 V. If the network is off, the node stays high only because Cdyn stores charge. It is dynamic: the value is held by capacitance, not by a DC path.

  1. Set φ = 0: precharge Cdyn to VDD; the dynamic node is high.
  2. Set φ = 1: evaluate the NMOS pull-down network.
  3. Inputs high enough: Cdyn discharges to 0 V.
  4. Inputs low: Cdyn stays high; leakage slowly removes charge.

A dynamic NAND with n inputs uses n series nMOS devices, one evaluate nMOS and one precharge pMOS. Static CMOS needs 2n transistors for the same NAND. The saving is large for wide gates. The clock line drives many transistors, so its power and skew matter.

Charge sharing, keepers and leakage

Charge sharing happens when an internal node with capacitance Cint connects to Cdyn during evaluation. The charge on Cdyn is shared, so Vdyn falls even if the pull-down path is not complete to ground. If the drop crosses the inverter threshold, the output flips wrongly.

V<sub>dyn</sub> = (C<sub>dyn</sub>V<sub>pre</sub> + C<sub>int</sub>V<sub>int</sub>) ÷ (C<sub>dyn</sub> + C<sub>int</sub>)

Cdyn is the dynamic node capacitance in farads (F); Cint is the internal node capacitance in F; Vpre is the precharged voltage in volts (V); Vint is the internal node voltage before connection in V; Vdyn is the final shared voltage in V.

A keeper transistor is a weak pMOS from VDD to the dynamic node. Its gate is driven by the output of the inverter, that is, by the gate output node. When the node should stay high, the keeper supplies leakage current and fights charge sharing. The keeper must be weak enough that the NMOS pull-down can still discharge the node during evaluation.

Charge sharing is reduced by precharging internal nodes, by adding dummy capacitance, or by keeping Cint small. Leakage sets a minimum clock frequency: if the clock runs too slowly, the dynamic node may droop before evaluation. Dynamic gates therefore suit continuous high-speed operation, not long pauses.

Domino logic and the non-inverting rule

A dynamic gate cannot drive another dynamic gate directly in a simple cascade. During precharge, every dynamic output goes high. The next gate would see a high input and may start evaluating with the wrong value. Domino logic adds an inverter after the dynamic node.

The inverter output is low during precharge. During evaluation it can only rise from 0 to 1, never fall. A chain of domino gates therefore evaluates in order, like falling dominoes. The overall gate is non-inverting: the pull-down condition is inverted at the dynamic node and inverted again at the output.

Because the output makes only low-to-high transitions in evaluation, a domino gate can feed another domino gate safely. Inverting logic inside a domino stage needs differential or special techniques. Domino is common in high-speed adders and critical datapaths, but clock power and charge sharing remain.

Domino logic is grouped with other clocked logic families such as clocked CMOS (C²MOS), NORA and TSPC, but it is not the same circuit. NORA and TSPC are clocked dynamic families: NORA uses alternating clock phases, and TSPC uses a true single-phase clock. Only a few universities teach NORA and TSPC in detail; know their names and purpose.

Cascode voltage switch logic (CVSL)

CVSL is a differential logic family. It has two NMOS pull-down networks: one for the true function and one for its complement. Two cross-coupled pMOS transistors act as the load. The pMOS gate of each side connects to the other output node.

During evaluation, one NMOS network conducts and pulls its output low. The cross-coupled pMOS whose gate is low turns on and holds the other output high. The circuit therefore produces both the true and complement outputs. The NMOS network must be strong enough to overpower the pMOS keeper, so CVSL is ratioed during switching.

CVSL can implement complex functions in one gate because it uses both true and complement inputs. The cost is more wiring, more transistors and careful sizing. It is used in complex high-speed logic where differential signals are available.

  • Static CMOS: complementary networks, no clock, robust, 2n transistors for an n-input gate.
  • Dynamic: clocked precharge and evaluate; for an n-input NAND, n pull-down nMOS plus one evaluate and one precharge transistor (n+2), before adding a keeper or output inverter.
  • Domino: dynamic plus inverter, cascadable, non-inverting.
  • CVSL: differential, cross-coupled pMOS load, ratioed during switching.

Worked example

Question. A dynamic node Cdyn = 50 fF is precharged to Vpre = 1.8 V. An internal node Cint = 10 fF starts at Vint = 0 V and connects to the dynamic node during evaluation. No keeper is present. Find the final dynamic node voltage.

  1. Before connection, charge on the dynamic node is Qdyn = CdynVpre = 50 fF × 1.8 V = 90 fC.
  2. Charge on the internal node is Qint = CintVint = 10 fF × 0 V = 0 fC.
  3. After connection, total capacitance is Ctotal = Cdyn + Cint = 50 fF + 10 fF = 60 fF.
  4. Total charge is conserved: Qtotal = Qdyn + Qint = 90 fC + 0 fC = 90 fC.
  5. Final voltage is Vdyn = Qtotal / Ctotal = 90 fC / 60 fF = 1.5 V.

Answer. The final dynamic node voltage is 1.5 V.

Exam traps

  • Cascading dynamic gates without domino inverters; the precharged high output can make the next gate evaluate the wrong value.
  • Ignoring charge sharing: an internal node at 0 V can pull the dynamic node down even when the pull-down path is off, flipping the output.
  • Making the keeper too strong: it holds the node high but the NMOS pull-down cannot discharge it, so evaluation fails or slows.
  • Mixing dynamic and static inputs at different times; inputs must be stable before evaluate and must not glitch.
  • Calling CVSL non-ratioed: the NMOS network must overpower the cross-coupled pMOS during switching.

Takeaways

  1. The CMOS inverter voltage transfer curve has five regions, and its switching threshold is the input where output equals input.
  2. Noise margins come from VIL, VIH, VOL and VOH; a symmetric inverter gives the widest margins.
  3. An NMOS inverter needs a load, and the resistive, enhancement, depletion and pseudo-nMOS choices trade output levels, static current and speed.
  4. Propagation delay is set by charging and discharging load capacitance through the finite drive current of the transistors.
  5. Logical effort estimates multistage path delay and shows how to size each stage for minimum delay.
  6. CMOS power is dynamic, short-circuit and leakage power; dynamic power dominates when switching and leakage dominates when idle.
  7. Low-power design lowers activity, capacitance and supply voltage, and cuts leakage with power gating or high-Vt devices.
  8. Static CMOS is robust; ratioed, pass-transistor and dynamic styles save area or transistors but add static current, voltage loss, clocking or charge-sharing problems.

Glossary

CMOS inverter
A complementary pair of one pMOS and one nMOS transistor that drives the output to VDD or ground.
CVSL
Cascode voltage switch logic, a differential logic family with a cross-coupled pMOS pull-up latch.
Depletion load
An nMOS load transistor that conducts even when its gate-source voltage is zero.
Domino logic
Dynamic logic with an inverter after each gate so cascaded stages can evaluate without false discharge.
Dynamic logic
Clocked logic that precharges an output node and then evaluates a pull-down condition.
Dynamic power
Power used to charge and discharge capacitance, equal to α C VDD2 f.
Electrical effort
The ratio of output capacitance to input capacitance for a logic stage, also called fanout.
Enhancement load
An nMOS load transistor that needs a gate voltage above its threshold to conduct.
Gate-oxide leakage
Tunnelling current through a thin gate oxide from gate to channel.
Junction leakage
Reverse-bias current through drain and source p-n junctions.
Load capacitance
The total capacitance an inverter or gate must charge and discharge, including gate, diffusion and interconnect capacitance.
Logical effort
A number that compares the input capacitance of a gate with that of an inverter for the same output current.
Noise margin
The voltage disturbance a logic gate can tolerate at its input without changing the interpreted output.
Parasitic delay
The delay of a gate caused by its own internal capacitance when it drives no external load.
Pass transistor
A MOS transistor used as a switch to pass a signal from input to output.
Path effort
The product of logical effort, electrical effort and branching effort along a logic path.
Power gating
A low-power method that disconnects idle blocks from the supply using high-threshold switch transistors.
Propagation delay
The time from a 50% input transition to the 50% output transition, averaged for high-to-low and low-to-high.
Pseudo-nMOS
A ratioed CMOS style with a pMOS pull-up that is always on and an nMOS pull-down network.
Ratioed logic
Logic whose output low level depends on the relative strength of a pull-up load and a pull-down network.
Resistive load
An inverter load made from a resistor connected between the supply and the output.
Short-circuit power
Power dissipated when both nMOS and pMOS conduct during an input transition and current flows from supply to ground.
Static CMOS
A complementary logic style with pMOS pull-up and nMOS pull-down networks that gives full rail-to-rail outputs.
Subthreshold leakage
Current that flows between drain and source when the gate-source voltage is below the threshold voltage.
Switching threshold
The input voltage at which the inverter output equals the input, often written VM.
Transmission gate
An nMOS and a pMOS in parallel that together pass both a strong 0 and a strong 1.
