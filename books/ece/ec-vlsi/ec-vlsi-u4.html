4.1 Adders: ripple carry and carry look-ahead

A full adder adds three bits and gives a sum and a carry. A wide adder chains full adders and passes carries along. The real design question is how fast the carry reaches the last bit. Ripple carry hands it stage by stage. Carry look-ahead computes every carry from the a and b bits. This sets the speed of ALUs, address incrementers and loop counters.

You need the static CMOS gates of Unit 3 (AND, OR, XOR) and the half-adder and full-adder truth tables.

Half adder, full adder and the standard cell

A half adder adds two bits, a and b. The symbol ⊕ means exclusive OR: the result is 1 when exactly one input is 1.

S = a ⊕ b, C = a · b

a and b are the input bits; S is the sum bit; C is the carry bit.

A full adder takes three inputs: a, b and the carry-in c from the right column.

S = a ⊕ b ⊕ c, C = a · b + c · (a ⊕ b)

c is the carry into this column; S is the sum bit; C is the carry out.

The term a·b says a carry leaves when both inputs are 1. The term c·(a⊕b) says a carry also leaves when exactly one input is 1 and a carry came in.

In static CMOS a full adder is one complex gate, not five separate gates. The usual cell uses 28 transistors: a majority network for carry, an XOR network for sum. The carry-out is ready before the sum, because the sum waits for the carry. Some syllabi call it the mirror adder.

Ripple carry adder

An n-bit ripple carry adder puts n full adders in a row. Bit i of a and bit i of b enter adder i, and the carry-out of adder i becomes the carry-in of adder i+1. Bit 0 is the least significant column. c0 is the carry into it, and c0 = 0 for plain addition.

Each full adder needs two gate delays to turn its carry-in into a carry-out: one AND level, then one OR level. The worst case is a carry born at bit 0 and propagated through every stage. That happens when g₀ = 1 and every later pᵢ = 1, for example a = 1111 and b = 0001.

t_carry = 2n gate delays,   t_sum = 2n + 1 gate delays

n is the number of bits; a gate delay is the delay of one simple CMOS gate, a few tens of picoseconds in a modern process.

For n = 32 the carry reaches the top after 64 gate delays and the final sum after 65. At 40 ps per gate delay that is 2.6 ns, longer than a 1 GHz clock period. The layout is regular, so ripple adders stay in slow timers, peripheral registers and address increments. Faster answers are in Chapter 4.2.

Subtraction reuses the same hardware. a − b equals a + b̄ + 1, where b̄ is b with every bit inverted. XOR each bit of b with a subtract control line, and drive c0 from the same line.

Carry look-ahead adder

Carry look-ahead stops waiting for the carry to walk. Each column computes two signals from its own aᵢ and bᵢ only. Generate gᵢ = aᵢ·bᵢ is 1 when the column creates a carry. Propagate pᵢ = aᵢ⊕bᵢ is 1 when the column passes an incoming carry on.

Careful: some texts define pᵢ = aᵢ + bᵢ. That also works in the carry recurrence below, but not in the sum equation, where the XOR version is needed. Fix your choice on paper before writing any formula.

cᵢ₊₁ = gᵢ + pᵢ · cᵢ

cᵢ is the carry entering column i; gᵢ and pᵢ are that column's generate and propagate; cᵢ₊₁ is the carry leaving it.

Substituting the recurrence into itself removes every carry from the right-hand side. For four bits:

c₁ = g₀ + p₀c₀
c₂ = g₁ + p₁g₀ + p₁p₀c₀
c₃ = g₂ + p₂g₁ + p₂p₁g₀ + p₂p₁p₀c₀
c₄ = g₃ + p₃g₂ + p₃p₂g₁ + p₃p₂p₁g₀ + p₃p₂p₁p₀c₀

each cᵢ now depends only on the g and p of the columns below it and on c₀; no carry is computed from another carry in sequence.

Every carry is now a two-level AND-OR function of g, p and c0, so one block — the look-ahead carry unit, also called LCU — builds all of them at the same time. A 4-bit look-ahead adder gives all carries in about three gate delays (one for g and p, two for the widest AND-OR), against eight for ripple. Each sum then costs one more XOR: sᵢ = pᵢ ⊕ cᵢ.

For wider adders the same trick is applied to blocks. A 4-bit block has a group generate G = g₃ + p₃g₂ + p₃p₂g₁ + p₃p₂p₁g₀ and a group propagate P = p₃p₂p₁p₀. Blocks are chained with c_next = G + P·c_in, giving a tree of look-ahead units whose delay grows with log n instead of n. A single-level look-ahead for n bits would need AND terms of n+1 inputs, so practical designs stop at 4 or 8 bits per block and add group logic above.

Question. A 4-bit carry look-ahead adder has a = 1011 and b = 0110, with c0 = 0. Using g_i = a_i·b_i and p_i = a_i⊕b_i, find every g, every p, every carry, every sum bit, and the final result.

  1. Bits: a₃a₂a₁a₀ = 1011, so a₀ = 1, a₁ = 1, a₂ = 0, a₃ = 1. b₃b₂b₁b₀ = 0110, so b₀ = 0, b₁ = 1, b₂ = 1, b₃ = 0.

Worked example 2

Question. A 32-bit ripple carry adder uses full adder cells in which the carry takes 2 gate delays per stage and the sum takes one more delay. Each gate delay is 40 ps. Find the worst-case delay to the final sum bit.

  1. Carry chain: n = 32 stages, so the carry out of the last stage is ready after 2 × 32 = 64 gate delays.
  2. Last sum bit: one extra XOR after its carry arrives, giving 64 + 1 = 65 gate delays.
  3. Time: 65 × 40 ps = 2600 ps = 2.6 ns.

Answer. The worst-case delay to the final sum bit is 65 gate delays, that is 2.6 ns.

Exam traps

  • Mixing the two definitions of p. With pᵢ = aᵢ + bᵢ the carry recurrence still holds, but sᵢ = pᵢ ⊕ cᵢ is wrong; the sum must be written aᵢ ⊕ bᵢ ⊕ cᵢ.
  • Forgetting the last XOR delay: the carry arrives at 2n gate delays, the final sum one delay later at 2n + 1.
  • Index slip: the recurrence is cᵢ₊₁ = gᵢ + pᵢcᵢ, driven by column i, not gᵢ₊₁.
  • Building a single-level look-ahead for 32 bits. The AND terms grow to 33 inputs and the gate becomes huge and slow; use 4-bit blocks with group generate and group propagate.

4.2 Carry select, carry save and carry skip adders

A wide adder is slow because the carry must reach the last bit. Three architectures attack that chain without changing the final arithmetic. Carry select computes each block twice, with carry in 0 and 1, then selects. Carry skip passes the carry over a block whose bits all propagate. Carry save never propagates the carry inside a row: it keeps a sum vector and a carry vector for a later adder. The names CSL, CSK and CSA appear in datapath and multiplier design.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for the full adder and for generate g and propagate p.

Carry select: compute both answers

Carry select splits an n-bit adder into blocks of k bits. Each block contains two ripple adders. One assumes its carry in is 0. The other assumes 1. Both run at the same time.

For each sum bit in the block, a multiplexer picks the output of the correct adder. The select signal is the real carry coming from the lower block. The carry out of the block is also selected. No sum bit waits for the lower block; only the final selection waits.

Take a = 1011 (11) and b = 0110 (6), split into two-bit blocks. Low block (bits 1:0) adds 11 and 10 with carry in 0: sum 01, carry out 1. High block (bits 3:2) adds 10 and 01. With carry in 0 it gives sum 11, carry out 0. With carry in 1 it gives sum 00, carry out 1. The real carry is 1, so the multiplexers select the carry-in-1 result. The sum is 1 00 01 = 10001 (17).

Area is the cost. A plain two-bit block needs two full adders. A carry-select block needs four full adders plus three 2:1 multiplexers: two for the sum bits and one for the carry out. A square-root layout duplicates only high blocks in a tree. The first block is a single ripple adder. Later blocks get more copies with fixed carry assumptions. Delay falls to about one block ripple plus a multiplexer tree, while area stays proportional to n — roughly double that of a plain ripple adder when each block holds two copies. The name square-root adder comes from the √n choice of block size; carry select and carry skip are different architectures that both end up with square-root-style delay.

Carry skip: bypass a propagating block

Carry skip, also called carry bypass, uses the propagate signal p_i = a_i XOR b_i from Chapter 4.1. A bit propagates a carry when p_i = 1: a carry in produces a carry out. For a block from bit m to bit n, define block propagate P = p_m · p_{m+1} · ... · p_n. The dot means AND.

If P = 1, every bit in the block propagates. Any carry entering the block will leave it unchanged. A multiplexer then sends the incoming carry straight to the next block, bypassing the ripple chain. If P = 0, at least one bit stops or generates a carry, so the block uses its normal ripple carry out.

The block size k controls delay. Small blocks skip often but add many multiplexers. Large blocks ripple too far inside each block. A common estimate is T_skip ≈ (k + n/k) t_gate, where n is the adder width in bits, k is block width in bits, and t_gate is one gate delay. This is smallest near k = √n. The name square-root adder comes from this √n choice of block size; carry skip and carry select are different architectures, and both can have square-root-style delay. For a 32-bit adder the useful block size is near 5 or 6 bits. The carry skip adder is cheap: one AND chain and one multiplexer per block.

Carry save: keep the carry for later

A carry save adder does not try to finish the sum. It takes three numbers and reduces them to two. Each column uses one full adder as a 3:2 compressor. The full adder adds three input bits at the same position and produces a sum bit s_i and a carry bit c_i. The carry bit belongs one place to the left.

For inputs a, b and d, the row produces S = s_{n-1}...s_0 and raw carry bits c_{n-1}...c_0. The arithmetic identity is a + b + d = S + 2C, where C is the raw carry word c_{n-1}...c_0. In hardware the shifted carry vector is C' = 2C, so a + b + d = S + C'. A final carry propagate adder, such as the ripple or look-ahead adder of Chapter 4.1, adds S and C'. That final adder is the only place where a carry ripples.

The delay of the compressor row is one full adder delay. There is no carry chain across columns. The price is the final adder. In a multiplier, many partial-product rows are reduced by a tree of these 3:2 compressors. Wallace and Booth multipliers use exactly this block; see Chapter 4.4. The same row appears in a carry save adder used for multioperand addition.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting to shift the carry vector left by one bit before the final add. The raw carry word 0111 is 7, but the shifted vector 1110 is 14.
  • Expecting a carry save adder to produce a finished sum on its own. It only reduces three inputs to two vectors; a final carry propagate adder is still needed.
  • Using OR instead of AND for block propagate P in a carry skip adder. A carry crosses the whole block only when every p_i is 1.
  • In carry select, the multiplexer select is the real carry from the lower block, not the block's own carry out. Both candidate carries are already computed.

4.3 Array multipliers: Braun and Baugh-Wooley

An array multiplier makes every partial product at the same time. Each bit of the multiplier opens a row of AND gates, and each row is the multiplicand shifted by that bit's place. Full adders then combine the rows at fixed columns, with carries moving diagonally down the array. The answer appears after one long ripple through the diagonals. Braun does this for unsigned numbers. Baugh-Wooley does it for two's complement by inverting the mixed sign partial products and adding two fixed 1 bits. Small embedded multipliers and many hardware interview questions still use these arrays.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for the full adder, and Chapter 4.2 for why saving carries helps when many rows must be added.

Rows of AND gates, added by place value

Write the multiplicand as A = an−1...a0 and the multiplier as B = bn−1...b0. Each bit ai and bj is 0 or 1, and n is the word length. Unsigned multiplication is shift and add. Multiplying by bj2j either adds A<<j or adds zero. The array does all these additions together.

P = Σ<sub>j=0</sub><sup>n−1</sup> (Σ<sub>i=0</sub><sup>n−1</sup> a<sub>i</sub>b<sub>j</sub> 2<sup>i</sup>) 2<sup>j</sup> = Σ<sub>i=0</sub><sup>n−1</sup>Σ<sub>j=0</sub><sup>n−1</sup> a<sub>i</sub>b<sub>j</sub> 2<sup>i+j</sup>

P is the 2n-bit product; ai is bit i of A; bj is bit j of B; ppij = aibj is one partial product bit; 2i+j fixes its column.

A 4×4 array needs 16 AND gates to form pp00 to pp33. It needs 12 full adders to reduce the four rows: n(n−1)=4×3=12. The first row is not added to anything, so it enters the array directly. Carries do not run along a row; they run diagonally into the next row at the next column. The last carry reaches the final product bit.

Braun multiplier

The Braun multiplier is the plain unsigned array just described. Row j is A AND bj, shifted left by j. Row 0 has no shift. Row 1 is shifted one place, row 2 two places, and so on. Each column adds the bits that land in it plus the carry from the column above. The layout is rectangular and regular, so it maps cleanly to layout and to a cell library.

  • Gate count: n2 AND gates and n(n−1) full adders for an n×n array. A 4×4 Braun array has 16 AND gates and 12 full adders.
  • Critical path: the slowest signal enters near the top and travels down the diagonal carry chain, then across the last row. It grows with n, not with n2.
  • No correction terms: every partial product is used exactly as generated. Braun is unsigned only.

A simple delay model is tp ≈ (n−1)tc + tlast, where tc is the carry delay of one full adder and tlast is the final carry ripple to the top product bit. This is only a first estimate; wire delay and gate sizing matter in real layout (see Unit 3, delay estimation). Some syllabi ask only for the block diagram and the partial product array, not this estimate.

Baugh-Wooley for two's complement

Signed numbers use two's complement. For an n-bit number, A = −an−12n−1 + Σi=0n−2 ai2i. The top bit has weight −2n−1, not +2n−1. If you sign-extend both inputs to 2n bits and use a Braun array, the array becomes larger and the sign bits create long rows of copies. Baugh-Wooley keeps the n×n array and corrects the sign inside it.

Generate the low bits normally: aibj for i,j ≤ n−2. For a partial product with exactly one sign bit, use the inverted bit: NOT(an−1bj) for j ≤ n−2, and NOT(aibn−1) for i ≤ n−2. Keep the corner an−1bn−1 unchanged. Then add two 1 bits at fixed positions: bit n and bit 2n−1. In modulo 22n arithmetic, these two 1s are the correction constant 2n + 22n−1.

P<sub>BW</sub> = Σ<sub>i=0</sub><sup>n−2</sup>Σ<sub>j=0</sub><sup>n−2</sup> a<sub>i</sub>b<sub>j</sub>2<sup>i+j</sup> + Σ<sub>j=0</sub><sup>n−2</sup> NOT(a<sub>n−1</sub>b<sub>j</sub>)2<sup>n−1+j</sup> + Σ<sub>i=0</sub><sup>n−2</sup> NOT(a<sub>i</sub>b<sub>n−1</sub>)2<sup>n−1+i</sup> + a<sub>n−1</sub>b<sub>n−1</sub>2<sup>2n−2</sup> + 2<sup>2n−1</sup> + 2<sup>n</sup>

PBW is the signed product modulo 22n; NOT(x) is 1−x; the two added powers are the two 1 bits; all powers of 2 are bit positions in the final product.

Baugh-Wooley needs n2 AND gates, 2(n−1) inverters for the mixed sign bits, two constant 1 cells, and about n(n−1) full adders. It avoids a separate sign-fix adder and avoids sign extension rows. The array topology and diagonal carry path stay the same as Braun, so the delay is still dominated by the diagonal carry. The saving is area and regularity, not a shorter critical path.

Worked example

Question. Multiply a = 1101₂ (13) by b = 1011₂ (11) on a 4×4 Braun array. Show every partial product row, its place value, the sum, and the final 8-bit product.

  1. Read the multiplier bits from LSB: b0=1, b1=1, b2=0, b3=1.
  2. Row for b0=1: A×1 = 1101₂ = 13. Place value 2^0=1, so contribution 13×1=13.
  3. Row for b1=1: A×2 = 1101₂<<1 = 11010₂ = 26. Contribution 26.
  4. Row for b2=0: A×0 = 0000₂ = 0. Contribution 0.
  5. Row for b3=1: A×8 = 1101₂<<3 = 1101000₂ = 104. Contribution 104.
  6. Add: 13 + 26 + 0 + 104 = 143.
  7. 143 in 8 bits: 143 = 128+8+4+2+1 = 10001111₂.
  8. Check: 13×11=143.

Answer. The product is 10001111₂ = 143.

Exam traps

  • In Braun, adding the partial products without their place-value shifts. Row 1 is shifted one place, not added as the same weight as row 0.
  • In Baugh-Wooley, dropping the two correction 1 bits, or inverting the corner an−1bn−1. The corner has a positive weight and stays normal.
  • Inverting every sign-bit row completely. Only partial products with exactly one sign bit are inverted; the corner is the exception.
  • Mixing the counts: an n×n array has n2 AND gates and n(n−1) full adders, not n2 full adders.
  • Assuming array delay is n2 gate delays. The critical path is the diagonal carry plus the final ripple, roughly linear in n.

4.4 Wallace tree and Booth multipliers

A multiplier is slow for two reasons: too many partial-product rows to add, or a long carry chain through them. A Wallace tree reduces the rows with parallel 3:2 counters, so the carry chain almost disappears until the last step. Booth recoding changes the multiplier into signed digits, so there are fewer rows to reduce. High-speed CPU and DSP multipliers use both ideas together.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for the 3:2 compressor and Chapter 4.3 for partial-product rows.

Wallace tree: reduce rows in parallel

Start with N partial-product rows from Chapter 4.3. A carry save adder (CSA) takes three bits of the same weight. It outputs a sum bit of weight 1 and a carry bit of weight 2. This is a 3:2 counter. A full adder is one column of a CSA. The CSA does not propagate the carry across columns, so it has no long carry chain.

At each level, split the rows into disjoint groups of three. Each group of three rows becomes two rows. Any unpaired rows pass down unchanged. All groups at a level work in parallel. For N = 8 the row count falls 8 → 6 → 4 → 3 → 2, as the figure shows. For N = 8 this is four reduction levels.

D ≈ ⌈log3/2(N/2)⌉

D is the number of reduction levels, N is the starting row count, and ⌈ ⌉ means round up. For N = 8, D = 4.

After the last level two rows remain. A final carry propagate adder (CPA) adds them and gives the product. The CPA is not a reduction level; it is the one place where carries travel. Wiring is irregular because bit columns of different weights must be aligned. The Dadda tree is a variant that uses counters as late as possible.

Booth radix-2: recode the multiplier

Booth radix-2 recodes the multiplier Q into signed digits. Let M be the multiplicand and Q an n-bit two's complement multiplier. Bit i of Q is qi. Add one extra bit q−1 = 0 below the LSB. For each i, read the pair (qi, qi−1) and choose a digit di from the table.

(qi, qi−1)Digit diAdded value
0000
01+1+M
10−1−M
1100

Then Q = Σ di 2i. In hardware all digits are formed at once, so each digit makes a partial product di M. Negative digits use the two's complement of M. In the step-by-step view, add di M to an accumulator and then do an arithmetic right shift. Arithmetic right shift copies the sign bit. A logical shift would break negative products. The extra bit q−1 = 0 starts the first pair.

Modified Booth radix-4 and why it helps

Modified Booth radix-4 reads overlapping triplets and produces digits from −2 to +2. It halves the number of partial products compared with radix-2. It needs M and 2M, where 2M is one left shift. The 3M multiple is not needed for radix-4; radix-8 Booth needs 3M. Booth's gains are fewer rows for the adder tree and no separate sign correction. The recoding already handles the two's complement sign.

A radix-4 digit covers two multiplier bits, so an n-bit multiplier gives about n/2 digits. Fewer digits mean fewer rows for a Wallace tree. The cost is more selection logic: the multiplier must choose 0, ±M, or ±2M. That selection is simple in CMOS because 2M is wiring and negation is a complement plus one. For wide multipliers this trade is usually good. For an 8-bit multiplier, radix-4 gives four digits instead of eight. Radix-4 is the common compromise in datapath libraries.

Worked example

Question. Recode Q = 1101 with q−1 = 0 for a Booth multiplier with M = 0110 (+6). Find the recoded digits, the value of Q, and the 8-bit product.

  1. M = 0110₂ = +6. Q = 1101₂. q−1 = 0. Bit order is q3 q2 q1 q0 = 1 1 0 1, so q0 = 1, q1 = 0, q2 = 1, q3 = 1.
  2. i = 0: pair (q0, q−1) = (1, 0) gives digit d0 = −1. Partial product is −M = −6 at weight 1.
  3. i = 1: pair (q1, q0) = (0, 1) gives d1 = +1. Partial product is +M = +6 at weight 2.
  4. i = 2: pair (q2, q1) = (1, 0) gives d2 = −1. Partial product is −M = −6 at weight 4.
  5. i = 3: pair (q3, q2) = (1, 1) gives d3 = 0. Partial product is 0 at weight 8.
  6. Recoded value = d0·1 + d1·2 + d2·4 + d3·8 = −1 + 2 − 4 + 0 = −3. Check: Q = 1101₂ as a 4-bit two's complement number is −3.
  7. Product = M × Q = 6 × (−3) = −18. In 8 bits, −18 = 256 − 18 = 238 = 11101110₂.

Answer. The digits are −1, +1, −1, 0 from LSB to MSB. Q = −3 and the product is −18, which is 11101110 in 8 bits.

  • Wallace: counting the final carry propagate adder as one more reduction level. It only adds the last two rows.
  • Booth table: swapping the pair order. (q_i, q_i−1) = 10 gives −M, not +M.
  • Radix-4: saying modified Booth needs 3M. Radix-4 digits are −2 to +2, so M and 2M are enough; 3M belongs to radix-8.

4.5 Decoders, multiplexers and comparators

A decoder takes an n-bit code and activates exactly one of 2ⁿ output lines. A multiplexer does the opposite job: it selects one of many data inputs and sends it to a single output. A comparator takes two words and decides whether they are equal, or which is larger. These three blocks route and judge data inside adders, memories and processors. A decoder drives a memory wordline, a multiplexer chooses between results, and a comparator makes a branch decision.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for XOR and equality, and Unit 3 for pass transistors and transmission gates.

Decoder: a code in, one line out

An n-to-2ⁿ decoder has n input bits and 2ⁿ output lines. For each input code exactly one output is active. A 3-to-8 decoder takes inputs A, B, C, where A is the most significant bit. Input ABC = 101 activates output Y5, because binary 101 equals decimal 5.

Inside is an AND plane. Each input bit drives an inverter, so both true and complement are available. Output Yi is an n-input AND of the chosen literals. For the 3-to-8 decoder, Y5 = A·B′·C. The decoder is a minterm generator: output i is minterm mi. Active-high means the selected line is 1. Active-low means it is 0; a NAND plane or an output inverter gives that.

Y<sub>i</sub> = 1 ⇔ (b<sub>n−1</sub> … b<sub>0</sub>) = i

Yi is output line i; bn−1…b0 is the input code; i is the line number from 0 to 2ⁿ−1.

An enable input turns the whole decoder on or off. Larger decoders use enables to combine smaller ones. A 3-to-8 decoder can be two 2-to-4 decoders: A enables the upper half when A=1 and the lower half when A=0; B and C select the line inside each half. In a memory, a row decoder drives one wordline high for the addressed row (Chapter 4.7). The delay is about one inverter delay plus one n-input AND delay.

Multiplexer: many lines in, one out

A 2:1 multiplexer has data inputs D0 and D1, select S, and output Y. Y equals D0 when S=0 and D1 when S=1, so Y = S′·D0 + S·D1. A pass-transistor pair can build it with two transistors. A transmission gate uses an nMOS and a pMOS in parallel, so both 0 and 1 pass without a threshold drop (Unit 3).

A mux tree builds wider multiplexers. A 4:1 mux uses three 2:1 muxes: the first stage selects D0/D1 and D2/D3 with S0, the second stage selects between those two results with S1. An 8:1 mux uses seven 2:1 muxes and three select levels. For an N:1 mux tree the number of select levels is ceil(log₂N), so tpd ≈ ceil(log₂N) × tmux.

Shannon expansion makes the mux a logic element. Any function f(x1,…,xn) = x1·f(1,…) + x1′·f(0,…). Use the select lines as the variables and tie each data input to 0 or 1, or to a remaining function. An 8:1 mux with selects A,B,C and data inputs equal to the truth table implements any f(A,B,C). A tristate buffer adds a high-impedance state, so many drivers can share a bus; enable exactly one driver at a time.

Comparator: two words, three decisions

A 1-bit equality gate is an XNOR: E = A·B + A′·B′. It gives 1 when the two bits are equal. For n bits, E = En−1·…·E0. This is an AND tree. A linear AND chain has delay tXNOR + (n−1)·tAND; a balanced tree has delay tXNOR + ceil(log₂n)·tAND.

The magnitude cell compares one bit pair and carries the decision. It has inputs Ai, Bi and incoming Gin, Lin, Ein. G means A>B so far, L means A<B so far, E means equal so far. Outputs are: Gout = Gin + Ein·Ai·Bi′ ; Lout = Lin + Ein·Ai′·Bi ; Eout = Ein·(Ai XNOR Bi). Where Gout, Lout, Eout are the updated flags; + is OR; · is AND; ′ is NOT.

Start at the MSB with Gin=0, Lin=0, Ein=1. Feed each cell's outputs to the next less significant cell. The first differing bit decides, and later bits cannot change it. After the last cell, Gout=1 means A>B, Lout=1 means A<B, and Eout=1 means A=B. The chain has n cells, so delay is about n × tcell. Reading from the LSB end is wrong because a later MSB difference must override the earlier answer.

Worked example

Question. A logic function is f(A,B,C)=Σm(1,2,5,7). (a) Implement it with a 3-to-8 decoder and one OR gate. (b) Compare A=1011₂ (11) with B=1010₂ (10) using the magnitude-cell chain from the MSB. Give the final G, L, E flags.

  2. Tie Y1, Y2, Y5, Y7 to a 4-input OR gate. Output f=Y1+Y2+Y5+Y7. f=1 for ABC=001, 010, 101, 111 and 0 otherwise.
  3. Compare bit 3 (MSB): A3=1, B3=1. Equal, so E=1, G=0, L=0.
  4. Bit 2: A2=0, B2=0. Equal remains E=1.
  5. Bit 1: A1=1, B1=1. Equal remains E=1.
  6. Bit 0: A0=1, B0=0. Here Ai=1 and Bi=0, so the cell sets Gout=1, Lout=0, Eout=0.

Answer. f = Σm(1,2,5,7) using Y1+Y2+Y5+Y7; for 1011 vs 1010, the final flags are G=1, L=0, E=0, so A>B.

  • Building a large decoder from smaller ones without using the enable as the extra address bit; the MSB must enable the correct half.
  • Letting a comparator decide from the LSB end. The first differing MSB decides, and later bits cannot override it.
  • Forgetting that a tristate bus needs exactly one enabled driver: two drivers cause contention, none causes a floating bus.
  • Confusing a multiplexer's select lines with its data inputs in Shannon expansion, or tying a data input to a variable that is also a select.

4.6 Static and dynamic latches and registers

A latch stores one bit in a feedback loop. The loop is closed for part of the clock, so the output either follows the input or holds the last value. Two latches in series, driven by opposite clock phases, form a master-slave flip-flop: the register output changes only at a clock edge. Designers use latches inside flip-flops and SRAM cells, and use registers for pipeline state, counters and control. Correct operation depends on setup and hold checks, and on whether the bit is held by a static loop or by dynamic charge.

You need: Unit 3 for the transmission gate and for the delay of the inverter pair inside the storage loop.

SR latch, gated D latch and transparency

A latch is a bistable circuit: it has two stable states, one for logic 0 and one for logic 1. In a cross-coupled NOR latch, output Q drives one input of the other NOR gate, and Qbar drives the other. Set S=1, R=0 forces Q=1. Set R=1, S=0 forces Q=0. With S=R=0 the loop holds its state. S=R=1 is forbidden: both outputs go to 0, and the final state after release is a race.

A cross-coupled NAND latch works the same way with active-low set and reset. The feedback loop continuously restores the stored level, so this is static storage.

A gated D latch removes the forbidden input. It has data D and enable EN. When EN=1, Q follows D and the latch is transparent. When EN=0, the loop closes and Q holds the last value. A transmission-gate latch uses two TG switches controlled by EN and its complement. One switch admits D; the other closes the inverter-pair feedback. The D latch is level-sensitive, not edge-triggered. It is small, fast and common inside flip-flops and as the storage element of static cells. The 6-transistor SRAM cell achieves the same static storage with cross-coupled inverters and access transistors, not with a gated D latch.

Master-slave register and edge triggering

A master-slave D flip-flop uses two D latches in series. The first is the master, the second the slave. Section 1 defined the D latch: it is transparent when its EN input is 1. Drive the master's EN with CLKbar and the slave's EN with CLK, so their transparent phases do not overlap. While CLK=0, the master is transparent and follows D; the slave is opaque and holds the old output. At the rising edge, the master becomes opaque and stores the value at D. The slave becomes transparent and copies the master. The output changes once, at the edge. After that, the master is fixed, so the slave has nothing new to pass. This is why two latches make a rising-edge-triggered register.

Edge triggering matters in synchronous systems. A transparent latch can pass a glitch or a late arrival while enabled; a flip-flop samples only around the edge. A register is n flip-flops in parallel, sharing one clock. Each bit has the same control. An enable input adds a multiplexer or clock gating so Q keeps its value. An asynchronous reset forces Q=0. A shift register connects Q of stage i to D of stage i+1.

Setup and hold timing checks

Timing defines whether a register-to-register path works. Let the launch flip-flop send data and the capture flip-flop receive it. t_cq is the clock-to-Q delay: the maximum time from the clock edge to a valid Q. t_comb is the combinational logic delay between them. t_su is the setup time: data must be stable this long before the capture edge. t_h is the hold time: data must stay stable this long after the edge. The aperture is the small window around the edge, roughly t_su plus t_h, during which data must not change.

T ≥ t_cq + t_comb,max + t_su

T is the clock period in ns; t_cq is clock-to-Q delay; t_comb,max is the longest combinational delay; t_su is setup time.

Setup is checked on the longest path. Setup slack = T − t_cq − t_comb,max − t_su, and it must be ≥0. Hold is checked on the shortest path.

t_cq + t_comb,min ≥ t_h

t_comb,min is the shortest combinational delay between the same registers; t_h is hold time.

Hold slack = t_cq + t_comb,min − t_h, and it must be ≥0. The hold check does not contain T; slowing the clock cannot fix a hold failure. The shortest clock period is T_min = t_cq + t_comb,max + t_su, so f_max = 1 ÷ T_min.

Clock skew is the difference in clock arrival time between the two flip-flops. Define t_skew = capture clock arrival time − launch clock arrival time. Positive t_skew means the capture edge arrives later, which relaxes setup and tightens hold: setup slack = T − t_cq − t_comb,max − t_su + t_skew, and hold slack = t_cq + t_comb,min − t_h − t_skew.

Static versus dynamic storage

Static storage uses the feedback loop. The loop supplies charge lost by leakage, so the bit survives with the clock stopped or running arbitrarily slowly. Dynamic storage uses charge on a transistor gate capacitance or a wire capacitance. A transmission gate writes the charge; when the gate turns off, no loop restores it. Leakage removes the charge and the node voltage droops. The latch must be refreshed before the droop exceeds the allowed ΔV.

f_min ≈ I_leak ÷ (C × ΔV)

I_leak is leakage current in A; C is storage capacitance in F; ΔV is allowed droop in V; f_min is the minimum refresh frequency in Hz.

A dynamic latch cannot run at DC. A static latch can. Two-phase non-overlapping clocking uses Φ1 and Φ2. The two phases are never high together. This prevents two latches from being transparent at the same time and racing. Only a few universities teach the detail; the rule is enough for most exams.

Worked example

Question. A register-to-register path has clock-to-Q delay t_cq = 0.3 ns, combinational delay t_comb = 1.2 ns, setup time t_su = 0.15 ns, hold time t_h = 0.10 ns and clock period T = 2 ns. Use the same t_comb for the hold check. (a) Find the setup slack and state whether setup passes. (b) Find the hold margin and state whether hold passes. (c) Find the shortest clock period and the maximum clock frequency.

  1. Setup slack = T − t_cq − t_comb − t_su = 2 − 0.3 − 1.2 − 0.15 = 0.35 ns. This is ≥ 0, so setup passes.
  2. Data arrival for hold = t_cq + t_comb = 0.3 + 1.2 = 1.5 ns. Hold margin = 1.5 − t_h = 1.5 − 0.10 = 1.4 ns. This is ≥ 0, so hold passes.
  3. Shortest clock period T_min = t_cq + t_comb + t_su = 0.3 + 1.2 + 0.15 = 1.65 ns.
  4. Maximum frequency f_max = 1 ÷ T_min = 1 ÷ (1.65 × 10^−9 s) = 606.06 × 10^6 Hz = 606.06 MHz.

Answer. Setup slack is 0.35 ns, hold margin is 1.4 ns, the shortest clock period is 1.65 ns, and the maximum clock frequency is 606.06 MHz.

Exam traps

  • A single gated D latch is level-sensitive, not edge-triggered. Only the master-slave pair or another edge circuit is a flip-flop.
  • Setup uses the longest path and contains T; hold uses the shortest path and has no T. Slowing the clock fixes setup, not hold.
  • Swapping the clock phases in a master-slave latch changes the active edge and can create a race if the two phases overlap.
  • A dynamic latch has a minimum clock frequency; a static latch works down to DC. Do not give a dynamic latch a maximum-frequency-only answer.
  • Mixing t_su and t_h signs in slack formulas is the most common numeric slip. Write data-arrival minus required-arrival for each check.

4.7 Memory design: SRAM, DRAM and ROM

A memory chip is a grid of identical cells. The row decoder drives one wordline high; that row's cells place their bits on vertical bitlines; sense amplifiers decide each bit as 0 or 1. This one pattern covers SRAM, DRAM and ROM, though the cell and the read mechanism differ. It exists because a processor needs many words close at hand at nanosecond speed, and a regular grid is the cheapest way to store millions of bits. Every register file, cache, main memory and instruction ROM in a phone or a laptop uses it.

You need: Chapter 4.5 for decoders and multiplexers, and Chapter 4.6 for the storage loop and timing rules.

Array organisation, address and timing

An array has R = 2r rows. Each row has C cell columns. The address splits into r row bits and c column bits, so it has r + c bits in all. The row decoder is an r-to-2r decoder (Chapter 4.5). It drives one wordline (WL) high. Every cell on that row connects to its vertical bitline (BL). The c-bit column address selects one of 2c groups of Cout = C / 2c bitline pairs and sends that group to the sense amplifiers. The memory therefore holds 2r+c words of Cout bits each. When Cout = C, c = 0, there is no column multiplexer, and the array is R words of C bits each. A sense amplifier is a small cross-coupled amplifier that turns a small bitline swing into a full logic level. A write driver forces the bitline pair to the input data during a write. A 4×4 array with c = 0 has four words of four bits.

Access time tAA is address-to-data delay. Cycle time tCYC is the shortest gap between the starts of two accesses. tCYC ≥ tAA because the bitlines must recover and the old wordline must fall. A processor may request a word every cycle, so the array must meet tCYC. Mixing these two is a common error.

R = 2<sup>r</sup>, C = 2<sup>c</sup> × C<sub>out</sub>, words = 2<sup>r+c</sup>

R is the number of rows or wordlines; r is the number of row-address bits; c is the number of column-address bits; 2c is the column multiplexer factor; C is the number of cell columns in one row; Cout = C / 2c is the number of bitline pairs sent to the sense amplifiers and also the bits per word. The total number of address bits is r + c. When Cout = C, c = 0 and the array is R words of C bits each.

SRAM and DRAM cells

6T SRAM. Six transistors hold one bit. Two cross-coupled inverters (four transistors) form a loop; two access transistors connect the loop to BL and BLB, the true and complement bitlines. Read: precharge both bitlines to about VDD/2, then drive WL high. The cell pulls one bitline slightly down. The sense amplifier resolves it. Write: the write driver drives BL and BLB strongly opposite; with WL high it overpowers the loop and flips it. When WL falls, the loop keeps the new bit. Read disturb: during a read the low internal node rises because the access transistor and pull-down transistor act as a divider. If it crosses the trip point, the cell flips. The cell ratio is pull-down strength divided by access strength. Make the pull-down transistor stronger than the access transistor. Keep the cell ratio (pull-down W/L divided by access W/L) usually between about 1.2 and 2, and the read stays safe. SRAM is fast and needs no refresh, but six transistors per bit make it less dense and it leaks.

1T1C DRAM. One access transistor and one storage capacitor CS hold one bit as charge. Logic 1 is VDD on CS; logic 0 is 0 V. Read: precharge BL to VDD/2, drive WL high, and let charge share between CS and bitline capacitance CBL. The bitline moves by ΔV = (Vcell − VDD/2) × CS/(CS + CBL), where Vcell is the stored cell voltage before the read. CS/(CS + CBL) is around 0.1, so ΔV is only tens of millivolts, typically about 50–150 mV. The sense amplifier resolves it. The read is destructive: charge sharing removes charge, so the value must be written back (restore). Leakage drains CS, so every row must be refreshed within tREF, typically 64 ms. DRAM is dense and cheap per bit, but volatile and slower.

ROM and comparison

Mask ROM stores one bit at each row-column crossing by the presence or absence of a transistor: one transistor per bit. The row decoder drives a wordline; a programmed cell pulls its bitline low, a missing cell leaves it high. Mask ROM is programmed at fabrication, cheap in volume and non-volatile. PROM is one-time programmable by blowing fuses or antifuses. EPROM is erased by ultraviolet light through a quartz window. EEPROM is erased electrically, byte by byte. Flash is EEPROM-like but erased in blocks; it is the common code and SSD memory.

Worked example

Question. A 256 Kbit SRAM is built as 1024 words × 256 bits. (a) How many address bits are needed? (b) How many cells are there? (c) A DRAM has 4096 rows and must refresh every row within 64 ms. Find the refresh rate in rows per millisecond and the interval between row refreshes.

  1. The word count is 1024 = 210, so the address needs 10 bits.
  2. Cells = 1024 × 256 = 262144 cells, which is 218 or 256 Kbit.
  3. Refresh rate = 4096 rows ÷ 64 ms = 64 rows per millisecond.
  4. Interval between rows = 64 ms ÷ 4096 = 0.015625 ms = 15.625 µs.

Answer. The SRAM needs 10 address bits and has 262144 cells; the DRAM refreshes 64 rows per millisecond, one row every 15.625 µs.

Exam traps

  • Access time is address-to-data delay; cycle time is the minimum gap between two accesses. Cycle time is never smaller than access time.
  • A DRAM read shares charge with the bitline and destroys the stored value; it must be restored before the next access. SRAM read does not destroy the bit, but a poor cell ratio can disturb it.
  • Refresh interval tREF is the maximum time for all rows, not for one row. For 4096 rows and 64 ms, each row is refreshed every 15.6 µs while the whole array is refreshed every 64 ms.
  • ROM is non-volatile and needs no refresh; DRAM is volatile and needs refresh; SRAM is volatile but needs no refresh.
  • A stronger access transistor helps write but worsens read disturb; a stronger pull-down helps read but makes write harder. Cell design is this trade-off.

Takeaways

  1. A wide adder is a carry problem, not an addition problem: every adder family is a different answer to how soon the last stage learns its carry.
  2. A carry look-ahead adder turns the ripple into a tree of generate and propagate terms, trading silicon area and heavy fan-in for logarithmic delay.
  3. Carry select, carry skip and carry save each dodge the carry chain in a different way: predict both answers, bypass a propagating block, or postpone the carry.
  4. An array multiplier is regular and easy to lay out, but its delay includes one long diagonal carry ripple from the top-right to the bottom-left corner.
  5. The Wallace tree removes that ripple by summing columns as a tree of 3:2 counters, cutting the delay to roughly log of the number of partial products.
  6. Booth recoding attacks the number of rows instead of the number of adders, halving the partial products for radix-4 and handling signed numbers naturally.
  7. Decoders, multiplexers and comparators are the glue of a datapath: a decoder makes minterms, a multiplexer selects a result, and a comparator makes the branch decision.
  8. A latch is level-sensitive and a register is edge-triggered; two latches in series with opposite clocks give you the edge that makes synchronous design predictable.
  9. An SRAM bit is a feedback loop kept alive by power, while a DRAM bit is charge on a capacitor that leaks away and must be refreshed.
  10. Memory speed comes from parallelism: the row decoder activates a whole word at once, and the sense amplifiers decide many bits at the same time.

Glossary

Array multiplier
A multiplier built as a regular two-dimensional array of AND gates and adders.
Baugh-Wooley
A two's complement array multiplication scheme using bit inversion and correction constants to keep the array regular.
Bitline
The column wire that carries the stored bit from the selected cell to the sense amplifier.
Block carry
A carry computed for a group of bits using that group's generate and propagate terms.
Booth encoding
Recoding the multiplier bits into signed digits so that fewer partial products are needed.
Carry look-ahead
Computing carries in parallel from generate and propagate signals instead of waiting for them to ripple.
Carry save adder
A structured full-adder array that reduces three numbers to two without resolving carries.
Carry select adder
An adder that precomputes sums for both possible carry inputs and selects one with a multiplexer.
Carry skip adder
An adder block that bypasses the carry straight to the next block when every bit only propagates.
Comparator
A circuit that reports whether two binary words are equal, or which one is larger.
Dadda multiplier
A multiplier that uses the minimum number of counters at each stage, at the cost of irregular wiring.
Decoder
A circuit that asserts exactly one of 2ⁿ outputs for each n-bit input code.
Flip-flop
An edge-triggered storage element that samples data only at a clock transition.
Half adder
A two-input circuit producing a sum bit a ⊕ b and a carry bit ab.
Latch
A level-sensitive storage element that is transparent for half the clock period.
Manchester carry chain
A dynamic chain of pass transistors that propagates or kills a carry along a block.
Metastability
The unresolved state a storage element may enter when its input changes inside the setup-hold window.
Multiplexer
A selector that connects one of several inputs to a single output under select control.
Partial product
The shifted multiplicand row produced by one multiplier bit before the rows are summed.
Refresh
Periodic rewriting of DRAM cells to replace charge lost by leakage.
Ripple carry
Passing the carry out of one adder stage into the next, so carries travel through the whole word.
Sense amplifier
A small cross-coupled amplifier that resolves the weak bitline signal into a full logic level.
Wallace tree
A tree of 3:2 counters that reduces partial product rows to two in logarithmic depth.
Wordline
The row wire a decoder drives to connect a whole row of memory cells to their bitlines.
