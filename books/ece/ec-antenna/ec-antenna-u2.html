2.1 Radiation mechanism, potentials and Hertzian dipole

A charge moving at constant speed drags its field along. A charge that accelerates launches a new field that keeps travelling after the charge has gone. That outgoing part falls as 1/r and is radiation. The simplest radiator is the Hertzian dipole: a short straight current element much shorter than a wavelength. Its fields contain 1/r³, 1/r² and 1/r terms; only the 1/r part survives far away. This chapter shows how retarded potentials turn a current into that far field, and why every wire antenna and every phone antenna is built from this idea.

You need: Maxwell's equations, the wave equation and the idea of a retarded solution from Unit 1.

Why a steady current does not radiate

A straight wire carrying a steady current has a magnetic field around it. That field is tied to the wire. While the current stays steady, only the static field tied to the wire exists. Switch it off slowly and that field simply collapses; switch it off abruptly and the deceleration itself radiates a transient. Static thinking fails because it assumes the field at distance r depends on the charge and current at this instant. In reality, news travels at c.

Potentials carry the news. The magnetic vector potential A at point r and time t is built from the current density J at every source point r′, evaluated at the retarded time t − R/c. R = |r − r′| is the source-to-point distance and c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. A = (μ₀/4π) ∫ J(r′, t − R/c) / R dV′. μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m is the permeability of free space. The electric vector potential F comes from a magnetic current density M in the same way, with ε₀ in place of μ₀: F = (ε₀/4π) ∫ M(r′, t − R/c) / R dV′. ε₀ = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m.

The field at r shows what the current was doing R/c seconds ago. A steady current has the same value at every past time, so the delayed value equals the present value; nothing new arrives. An accelerating current changes with time, so the delayed value differs and a travelling field is left behind. Some syllabi call this potential theory. A few universities also state the general Helmholtz integral for an arbitrary current distribution and the F potential; the integral sums these retarded contributions over the whole source, and you will not need to evaluate it for the dipole.

The infinitesimal dipole and its three field terms

Take a straight current element of length l much smaller than λ, centred at the origin along the z-axis. The current is uniform: i(t) = I₀ cos(ωt), with I₀ the peak current in amperes and ω = 2πf rad/s. In phasor form I = I₀. The phase constant is β = 2π/λ rad/m; λ = c/f is the wavelength in metres. The intrinsic impedance of free space is η = 120π ≈ 377 Ω.

The exact fields have three distance terms. For the electric field in the θ direction, Eθ = j η β I₀ l sinθ e^(−jβr) / (4πr) × [1 + 1/(jβr) − 1/(βr)²]. The bracket gives terms falling as 1/r, 1/r² and 1/r³. The 1/r term is the radiation field. The 1/r² and 1/r³ terms are near field; they store energy and die quickly. Only the 1/r term carries power away.

In the far field keep only 1/r: Eθ = j η β I₀ l sinθ e^(−jβr) / (4πr). The magnetic field is Hφ = Eθ/η. Here θ is the angle from the wire axis, r is distance in metres, I₀ is peak amperes, l is metres, β is rad/m, η is ohms, and j = √(−1) means Eθ leads the current by 90°. The pattern is sinθ: maximum broadside at θ = 90°, zero along the wire axis at θ = 0° and 180°. The element is omnidirectional in azimuth around the wire, but directional in the plane containing the wire. An isotropic antenna is only a mathematical reference with equal radiation in all directions; a directional antenna concentrates more power into a narrow beam. Radiation resistance is in Chapter 2.3; the near-field boundary is in Chapter 2.4.

Where the 1/r idea is used

The Hertzian dipole is the building block. A long wire, a half-wave dipole, a monopole over ground and a phone antenna are collections of short current elements. Their far fields are sums of this 1/r term, with the phase e^(−jβr) accounting for the different distances. The same reasoning explains why a phone antenna must be a fair fraction of a wavelength. At 900 MHz, λ = 33 cm; a 1 cm stub is only 0.03λ and radiates weakly. At 2.4 GHz, λ = 12.5 cm, and a quarter wave is about 3 cm, which fits inside a handset. The 1/r spreading also sets the link budget: double the distance, and the far field halves. The loop version is in Chapter 2.6.

Worked example

Question. A Hertzian dipole of length l = 1 cm carries a peak current I₀ = 1 A at f = 1 GHz. Find l/λ, the radiation resistance R_r, the radiated power P_rad, and the far-field magnitude |E_θ| at r = 100 m broadside. Check the result with power density.

  1. λ = c/f = (3×10⁸)/(1×10⁹) = 0.3 m.
  2. l/λ = 0.01/0.3 = 0.0333.
  3. For the Hertzian dipole with uniform current, R_r = 80π²(l/λ)² (a short dipole with triangular current gives 20π²(l/λ)²) = 80 × 9.8696 × (0.03333)² = 0.877 Ω.
  4. P_rad = ½ I₀² R_r = 0.5 × 1² × 0.877 = 0.439 W.
  5. β = 2π/λ = 2π/0.3 = 20.94 rad/m. Broadside θ = 90°, sinθ = 1. |Eθ| = ηβI₀l/(4πr) = 377 × 20.94 × 1 × 0.01 / (4π × 100) = 0.0628 V/m.
  6. S = |Eθ|²/(2η) = (0.0628)²/(2 × 377) = 5.24 × 10⁻⁶ W/m² = 5.24 µW/m². With D = 1.5, P_radD/(4πr²) = 0.439 × 1.5/(4π × 100²) = the same.

Answer. l/λ = 0.0333; R_r = 0.877 Ω; P_rad = 0.439 W; |E_θ| = 0.0628 V/m at 100 m broadside; S = 5.24 µW/m².

Exam traps

  • Keeping 1/r² or 1/r³ in the far field. The far field is only the 1/r term; the others are near field.
  • Forgetting sinθ. The field is zero on the wire axis, maximum broadside. A sketch with maximum along the wire is wrong.
  • Using l in centimetres with λ in metres. Convert both to metres before l/λ.
  • Mixing peak and rms current. The formula uses I₀ peak; ½I₀²R_r already gives the time average.
  • Calling Eθ/η exact everywhere. Hφ = Eθ/η is the far-field relation; in the near field the bracket differs.

2.2 Antenna parameters: pattern, directivity, gain, beamwidth

An antenna pattern is a shape: field or power plotted against angle. A link budget cannot use a whole shape, so we reduce it to a few numbers. Directivity compares the peak with an isotropic radiator; gain adds the antenna's losses; beamwidth gives the width of the main beam. This chapter defines radiation intensity, beam solid angle, directivity, gain, half-power beamwidth and first-null beamwidth, and shows how they are quoted in dBi and used in a datasheet or a radio link.

You need the far-field expression from Chapter 2.1; VSWR is covered in Unit 1.

Pattern: field, power and lobes

An antenna does not radiate equally in all directions. Plot the far-field electric field strength at a fixed distance r against θ, the angle from the antenna axis, and φ, the angle around the axis. That plot is the radiation pattern. Plot |E| for a field pattern; plot |E|² for a power pattern. Datasheets usually use decibels: power in dB = 10 log₁₀(power ratio). The main lobe is the direction of maximum radiation. Side lobes are smaller lobes near the main lobe. The back lobe points opposite the main lobe. A null is a direction where the pattern falls to zero.

U(θ, φ) = r² S(θ, φ)

U is radiation intensity in W/sr; S is power density in W/m²; r is distance in m; θ and φ are angles in radians.

Radiation intensity removes spreading. Far from the antenna the power density S falls as 1/r², so r²S depends only on angle. Normalise it to compare shapes: Pn(θ, φ) = U(θ, φ) / Umax, where Umax is the peak radiation intensity.

Beam solid angle and directivity

The beam solid angle ΩA is the solid angle through which all the radiated power would pass if the pattern were a flat-topped beam of height 1. Integrate the normalised pattern over the whole sphere: ΩA = ∬ Pn(θ, φ) dΩ. The solid-angle element is dΩ = sinθ dθ dφ, and ΩA is in steradians (sr). Directivity compares the peak radiation intensity with the average over the sphere: D = 4π / ΩA. The whole sphere is 4π sr. An isotropic radiator spreads equally, so Pn = 1, ΩA = 4π sr and D = 1. A directional antenna concentrates power, so ΩA is smaller and D is larger. D has no units. In dBi: D(dBi) = 10 log₁₀ D, meaning decibels relative to an isotropic radiator.

Gain, beamwidth and datasheet numbers

Gain includes losses: G = ηrad D. Here ηrad is radiation efficiency, from 0 to 1; 0.9 means 90% of input power radiates and the rest heats the conductor or dielectric. A lossless antenna has G = D. Directivity describes only the pattern; gain is the number a link budget uses. Both are quoted in dBi: value in dBi = 10 log₁₀(value). The half-power beamwidth HPBW is the angle between the two directions where the power pattern is half its peak, that is −3 dB. First-null beamwidth FNBW is the angle between the first nulls on either side. For one pencil beam, D ≈ 41253 / (θE θH), with θE and θH in degrees; 41253 is 4π converted to square degrees. This estimate fails for wide or multi-lobe patterns. Antennas are classed as isotropic (ideal, D = 1), omnidirectional (equal in one plane, doughnut pattern), or directional (main beam, higher D). A datasheet may give return loss = −20 log₁₀|Γ|, where Γ is the voltage reflection coefficient at the antenna input; larger return loss means less reflected power. VSWR is the companion measurement, in Unit 1.

Worked example

Question. An antenna has field pattern F(θ) = sin θ and no φ dependence. Find its HPBW, beam solid angle, directivity and gain. Take radiation efficiency ηrad = 0.9.

  1. The power pattern is F² = sin²θ. Half power means sin²θ = 0.5.
  2. sin θ = √0.5 = 0.7071, so θ = 45° and θ = 135° in 0° to 180°. HPBW = 135° − 45° = 90°.
  3. ΩA = ∬ sin²θ dΩ = ∫₀²π ∫₀π sin²θ sinθ dθ dφ = 2π ∫₀π sin³θ dθ.
  4. ∫₀π sin³θ dθ = 4/3. ΩA = 2π(4/3) = 8π/3 = 8.3776 sr.
  5. D = 4π / ΩA = 4π / (8π/3) = 3/2 = 1.5. D = 10 log₁₀(1.5) = 1.76 dBi.
  6. G = ηrad D = 0.9 × 1.5 = 1.35. G = 10 log₁₀(1.35) = 1.30 dBi.

Answer. HPBW = 90°, ΩA = 8.38 sr, D = 1.5 = 1.76 dBi, G = 1.35 = 1.30 dBi.

Exam traps

  • Half-power means the power is half, so square the field. Setting the field amplitude to 0.5 is not the −3 dB point.
  • The quick estimate D ≈ 41253/(θ_E θ_H) assumes one pencil beam and both angles in degrees. It is wrong for a wide beam, a multi-lobe pattern, or angles entered in radians.
  • Directivity is pattern only; gain includes η_rad. If a question says lossless, G = D; otherwise G < D.
  • dBi is relative to an isotropic radiator (D = 1). Do not read it as a plain power ratio: 3 dBi is about 2, not 3.
  • Return loss is positive for a good match and is not the same as the reflection coefficient; RL = −20 log₁₀|Γ|. VSWR belongs to Unit 1.

2.3 Radiation resistance, efficiency, effective aperture and polarization

Four quantities decide antenna performance. Radiation resistance turns radiated power into an equivalent resistor. Efficiency says how much input power escapes instead of heating metal. Effective aperture says how much of an incoming wave is captured. Polarization decides whether the wave can be received at all. Together they set the power budget of every radio link, from FM to satellite.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the radiated field of a current element, and Chapter 2.2 for directivity and gain.

Radiation resistance: the resistor that is not there

Drive a dipole and it draws current as if a resistor sat at its terminals. That resistor does not exist: the power has left as a wave. It is the radiation resistance Rr, defined so the radiated power comes out right:

Prad = ½ |I|² Rr

Prad is total radiated power in W; I is peak current in A at a stated reference point; Rr is in Ω. With rms current the ½ disappears.

I must be quoted at a stated point: terminals or current maximum. A folded dipole shows four times the input resistance of a plain dipole for the same radiated power, because its feed current splits between two paths.

Two short-dipole conventions. An infinitesimal dipole with the same current all along has Rr = 80π²(l/λ)², referred to terminal current. A real short dipole has triangular current, zero at the tips; referred to the current maximum, Rr = 20π²(l/λ)², one quarter as large. l is dipole length in m; λ is free-space wavelength in m.

A half-wave dipole has 73 Ω at its current maximum. A quarter-wave monopole over a ground plane has 36.5 Ω, half, because it fills half the sphere. On the triangular convention the short dipole falls fast: about 2 Ω at l = λ/10, and 0.08 Ω at λ/50.

Loss resistance and radiation efficiency

Metal resistance and insulator leakage collect into one loss resistance RL, so input resistance is RA = Rr + RL. Only Rr radiates; RL heats.

η<sub>rad</sub> = R<sub>r</sub> / (R<sub>r</sub> + R<sub>L</sub>)

ηrad is radiation efficiency, a plain ratio; Rr and RL are in Ω. A value of 0.8 means 80 per cent of accepted power leaves as radiation.

Gain and directivity are linked by G = ηrad D, with D and G plain ratios; convert to dB afterwards. Loss does not change the pattern, so D is untouched while G drops.

Small antennas are hard because Rr is small. Two ohms in series with 0.5 Ω of matching coil throws away 20 per cent. At l = λ/50 the same 0.5 Ω leaves ηrad = 0.08/(0.08+0.5) ≈ 0.14, so most input power heats the metal. A half-wave dipole at 73 Ω sits far above typical loss and is naturally efficient.

Effective aperture

On receive, an antenna behaves like a catching area. If the incoming wave carries power density S in W/m² and the antenna delivers Pr watts to a matched load, effective aperture is Ae = Pr/S, in m².

A<sub>e</sub> = D λ² / (4π)   or   A<sub>e</sub> = G λ² / (4π) with loss

Ae is effective aperture in m²; D is directivity and G is gain, both plain ratios; λ is free-space wavelength in m. The relation holds for any antenna shape.

Ae is not physical area. A half-wave dipole at 300 MHz has D = 1.64 and Ae = 1.64 × 1/(12.57) ≈ 0.13 m², though it is a thin half-metre wire. Only for D = 1 does Ae equal λ²/4π.

For a real opening — horn mouth, dish — aperture efficiency eap = Ae/Aphys. It stays below 1 because the field across the opening is not uniform and some power spills past the rim; 0.5 to 0.7 is usual for a dish.

Polarization and the polarization loss factor

Polarization is the path traced by the electric field tip as the wave passes, looking along travel. Linear: a fixed straight line. Circular: a circle, traced once per period. Elliptical: an ellipse, the general case. Axial ratio AR is major axis divided by minor axis: 1 for circular, infinite for linear.

Build circular polarization from two crossed dipoles fed equal currents with a 90° phase difference. The linear fields add in quadrature and the resultant rotates. Reverse the 90° and the sense flips between right-hand and left-hand circular; wrong sense on a satellite link throws the signal away.

When wave and antenna differ, only the component along the antenna is picked up. Unit vectors in the plane perpendicular to propagation: ρ̂w for the wave, ρ̂a for the antenna. The polarization loss factor multiplies received power:

PLF = |ρ̂w · ρ̂a|²

ρ̂w and ρ̂a are unit vectors for wave and antenna polarization; PLF is a plain ratio between 0 and 1.

  • Matched polarizations: PLF = 1, that is 0 dB.
  • Linear antenna against a circular wave: PLF = 0.5, a 3 dB loss.
  • Crossed linear, vertical against horizontal: PLF = 0, a complete null.
  • Circular against circular of opposite sense: PLF = 0.

Worked example

Question. A short dipole has length l = λ/20 and a loss resistance RL = 0.5 Ω. Find (a) its radiation resistance on the triangular-current convention, (b) its radiation efficiency, and (c) its effective aperture at 300 MHz, taking D = 1.5.

  1. Electrical length: l/λ = 1/20 = 0.05, so (l/λ)² = 0.0025.
  2. Rr = 20π²(l/λ)² = 20 × 9.8696 × 0.0025 = 0.4935 Ω ≈ 0.49 Ω.
  3. Total resistance: RA = Rr + RL = 0.4935 + 0.5 = 0.9935 Ω ≈ 0.99 Ω.
  4. Efficiency: ηrad = 0.4935/0.9935 = 0.497 ≈ 0.50, that is 50 per cent.
  5. Wavelength: λ = c/f = (3 × 10⁸ m/s)/(300 × 10⁶ Hz) = 1 m.
  6. Effective aperture: Ae = Dλ²/(4π) = 1.5 × 1/12.566 = 0.119 m².
  7. With loss charged to the aperture: Ae = ηradDλ²/(4π) = 0.497 × 0.119 = 0.059 m².

Answer. Rr ≈ 0.49 Ω, ηrad ≈ 0.50 (50 per cent), and Ae ≈ 0.119 m² from D = 1.5, falling to 0.059 m² once the loss is included.

Exam traps

  • Quoting Rr without saying which current it refers to. A folded dipole has four times the input resistance of a plain dipole for the same radiated power; the number is meaningless without a reference point.
  • Using Ae = λ²/4π for every antenna. That value belongs to D = 1 alone; multiply by D or G for anything else.
  • Mixing peak and rms current in Prad = ½|I|²Rr. This halves or doubles computed Rr; the efficiency error depends on RL.
  • Measuring radiation resistance with a multimeter. A DC ohmmeter sees only RL; Rr exists only at the operating frequency and only as radiated power.
  • Forgetting the polarization loss factor, or calling axial ratio 1 a linear wave. AR = 1 is circular, AR → ∞ is linear, and linear against circular quietly costs 3 dB.

2.4 Near-field and far-field regions

The formulas of this unit — pattern, directivity, gain — hold only when you stand far enough from the antenna. Close in, the antenna stores energy in fields that never leave. The distance at which the travelling 1/r field takes over is set by the antenna's largest dimension D and by the wavelength λ. Every pattern measurement, every link budget and every handset exposure test depends on knowing which region you are standing in.

You need the three field terms of Chapter 2.1: the 1/r, 1/r² and 1/r³ parts of the field of an accelerating charge.

Three regions, three rules

Chapter 2.1 showed that the field of a small current element splits into three parts, falling as 1/r, 1/r² and 1/r³. Here r is the distance from the antenna in metres. Close to the antenna the fast-falling terms dominate. Far away only the 1/r term survives. The space around an antenna is therefore cut into three regions, sorted by which term wins.

r < 0.62 √(D³ / λ)

r is the distance from the antenna in metres; D is the largest physical dimension of the antenna in metres (for a horn, its largest mouth dimension or diagonal), not the directivity; λ is the free-space wavelength in metres. This is the outer edge of the reactive near field.

Inside that boundary the antenna behaves like a capacitor or an inductor: energy sloshes back and forth between the antenna and the space around it and none of it escapes. The ratio E/H is not 377 Ω here; it is reactive and can be several hundred ohms for a short dipole. Near-field probes and contactless cards work in this region.

RegionTestWhat you get
Reactive near fieldr < 0.62√(D³/λ)Stored energy, E/H not 377 Ω
Radiating near field (Fresnel)0.62√(D³/λ) < r < 2D²/λPattern still changing with r
Far field (Fraunhofer)r > 2D²/λ, r ≫ D, r ≫ λPattern fixed, E/H = η = 377 Ω

In the far field the antenna looks like a point source. The pattern no longer depends on r, and E/H settles to the free-space wave impedance η = 377 Ω, so the field is locally a plane wave. Every formula in this unit assumes you are here.

Why 2D²/λ

The 2D²/λ rule is a geometry result, not a guess. It is the distance at which the wavefront leaving a flat aperture of size D is flat enough in phase to be treated as a plane wave. The allowed error is a phase of π/8, that is 22.5°, across the aperture.

  1. Take an aperture whose largest dimension is D metres, and two points on it: the centre and the edge, D/2 apart.
  2. For an observer on the axis at distance r, the edge is farther away by Δ = √(r² + (D/2)²) − r ≈ D²/(8r) metres, for r much larger than D.
  3. A path difference Δ shifts the phase by 2πΔ/λ radians.
  4. Allow a phase error of π/8: then 2πΔ/λ ≤ π/8, so Δ ≤ λ/16.
  5. Put D²/(8r) ≤ λ/16 and solve: 2D² ≤ λr, so r ≥ 2D²/λ.

The constant 2 comes straight from that π/8 tolerance. A tighter tolerance, say π/16, would push the boundary farther out; 22.5° is the usual compromise, because the pattern is then close to the true one and measurement ranges stay affordable. The conditions r ≫ D and r ≫ λ come free when D is much larger than λ, since 2D²/λ is then already many times D.

Small antennas and where the boundary matters

An electrically small antenna has D much smaller than λ. Then 2D²/λ is smaller than λ and the rule is meaningless: it would put the far field inside the antenna's own stored-energy zone. The reactive near field of a small antenna reaches out to about λ/2π ≈ 0.16λ. A practical floor is ten times that, r ≥ 1.6λ, so the far field starts where both tests are satisfied.

r_ff > max(2D²/λ, 1.6λ)

r_ff is the distance from the antenna in metres at which the far field begins; D is the largest physical dimension in metres; λ is the wavelength in metres.

  • Pattern measurement: the test antenna must sit beyond 2D²/λ, or the measured shape belongs to the near field and not to the antenna (Unit 3, antenna measurements).
  • Near-field probes: an EMI scan or a field probe is deliberately held inside the reactive region, where the stored field is strong and local.
  • NFC and RFID at 13.56 MHz: λ is 22.1 m and a coil is roughly 0.1 m across, so a tap of a few centimetres happens deep inside the reactive near field, through magnetic coupling (see Chapter 2.6).
  • Handset exposure: a head held against a phone sits in the reactive near field, so the local field is not a plane wave and the far-field gain cannot predict it.

Worked example

Question. A horn antenna has largest dimension D = 1 m. Find the outer edge of its reactive near field and the distance at which its far field begins, first at 300 MHz and then at 100 MHz. Take c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s.

  1. At 300 MHz: λ = c/f = (3 × 10⁸)/(300 × 10⁶) = 1 m.
  2. Reactive edge = 0.62√(D³/λ) = 0.62√(1³/1) = 0.62 m.
  3. Far field needs 2D²/λ = 2(1)²/1 = 2.0 m, and also 1.6λ = 1.6 m. The larger is 2.0 m.
  4. At 100 MHz: λ = (3 × 10⁸)/(100 × 10⁶) = 3 m.
  5. Reactive edge = 0.62√(1³/3) = 0.62 × 0.577 = 0.358 m ≈ 0.36 m.
  6. Far field needs 2D²/λ = 2/3 = 0.67 m, and also 1.6λ = 4.8 m. The wavelength rule now wins, so the far field starts at 4.8 m.

Answer. At 300 MHz the reactive near field ends at 0.62 m and the far field starts at 2.0 m; at 100 MHz they are 0.36 m and 4.8 m.

Exam traps

  • Applying 2D²/λ to an electrically small antenna, where the answer comes out shorter than a wavelength. The λ criteria (r ≥ 1.6λ) then decide the boundary.
  • Reading D as directivity. In 0.62√(D³/λ) and 2D²/λ, D is the largest physical dimension in metres.
  • Thinking the far field starts where the signal is strong enough. The boundary is about phase flatness across the aperture and the pattern becoming independent of r, not about amplitude.
  • Assuming E/H = 377 Ω everywhere. In the reactive near field the ratio is reactive and can be far above 377 Ω.
  • Measuring a pattern at a distance shorter than 2D²/λ and reporting it as the antenna's pattern; in the Fresnel region the shape still changes with r.

2.5 Half-wave dipole, monopole and folded dipole

A half-wave dipole is two straight wires, each a quarter wavelength long, fed at the gap between them. Near half a wavelength the wire resonates: the current stands as a cosine, zero at the tips and largest at the feed, and the input impedance is close to a pure 73 Ω. That one number makes the dipole the reference antenna of radio. The monopole is half of it on a ground plane; the folded dipole is two joined at the ends. Between them they cover most FM, TV, cellular and Wi-Fi hardware.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the field of a current element, Chapter 2.2 for pattern and directivity, and Chapter 2.3 for radiation resistance.

Current on a half-wave dipole

A half-wave dipole is a straight wire of total length l = λ/2, broken by a small feed gap at its centre. λ is the free-space wavelength in metres. Drive the gap and the current settles into a standing wave. The tips are open circuits, so no current flows there. Current must be zero at both tips and largest at the feed.

I(z) = I₀ cos(βz)

I(z) is the current in amperes at distance z metres from the centre; I₀ is the current maximum at the feed; β = 2π/λ is the phase constant in radians per metre; z runs from −λ/4 to +λ/4.

At the tips βz = ±π/2 and cos = 0, as required. At the centre cos 0 = 1. The graph is a cosine half-arch, not a straight line. That difference from the short dipole of Chapter 2.1 changes the radiation resistance and adds a reactance.

Feed an exactly half-wave thin dipole in free space and the input impedance is Z_in = 73 + j42.5 Ω. The real part is radiation resistance. The +j42.5 Ω is inductive: energy stored in the near field that has not left. So a practical dipole is cut to about 0.47λ, not 0.50λ. The shorter wire adds capacitance, which cancels the +j42.5 Ω, and the input becomes nearly a pure 73 Ω — a good match to 75 Ω coax. Thicker wire widens the match but needs a slightly different shortening.

Pattern, directivity and radiation resistance

In the far field the half-wave dipole radiates a doughnut: maximum broadside, nulls along the wire axis.

F(θ) = cos((π/2) cos θ) / sin θ

F is the normalised field strength (0 to 1); θ is the angle in radians measured from the wire axis. F = 0 at θ = 0° and 180°, F = 1 at θ = 90°.

The half-power beamwidth is 78°. Directivity D = 1.64, that is 2.15 dBi. Radiation resistance R_r = 73 Ω, referred to the current maximum I₀, so radiated power is P_rad = ½ I₀² R_r watts. Only the 73 Ω radiates; the j42.5 Ω does not. Carry the gain 1.64 (2.15 dBi), and the 78° beamwidth if you are checking coverage, into any link budget (Chapter 2.8); the 73 Ω is what you match the feed line to.

Quarter-wave monopole over a ground plane

Cut the dipole in half at its centre and stand the quarter-wave wire upright on a large, flat, conducting ground plane. This is the quarter-wave monopole: the whip on a car roof, the mast of a broadcast station, the stub on a router. If the plane is infinite and perfectly conducting, image theory replaces it with a mirror image below the plane. Wire plus image is exactly the half-wave dipole.

Above the plane the fields equal the upper half of the dipole's fields; below there is none. Two consequences follow. For the same current only half the power is radiated, so Z_in = ½ × 73 ≈ 36.5 Ω, close to 50 Ω coax. And the same power is squeezed into a half space, so directivity doubles: D = 2 × 1.64 = 3.28, that is 5.15 dBi.

The pattern above the plane is the top half of the dipole doughnut. A real ground plane is finite and lossy, so 36.5 Ω and 5.15 dBi are ideals; radials, a car roof or a metal sheet approximate the plane. On a handset the circuit board is the counterpoise, which is why touching a phone detunes its antenna.

Folded dipole and the balun

Take two half-wave wires, hold them parallel a small distance apart (much less than λ), join them at both ends, and feed the gap at the centre of one wire. This is the folded dipole. The two wires are joined at both ends, so the feed current passes through both arms in series and both carry the same current I_in. The two arms radiate in phase, so their fields add: the field doubles at the same current, and power, which goes as field squared, quadruples.

So R_in = 4 R_r ≈ 4 × 73 = 292 Ω, usually quoted as about 300 Ω. That is the impedance of 300 Ω twin-lead ribbon, the flat two-wire cable of older FM and TV sets, so the antenna feeds the cable directly with no transformer. Two wires in parallel also lower the Q, widening the bandwidth compared with one thin dipole.

A dipole is balanced: its two halves are symmetric about earth. Coax is unbalanced: its shield sits at earth. Join them directly and current creeps along the outside of the shield, distorting the pattern. A balun is the transition from a balanced dipole to an unbalanced coaxial feed — a small transformer, a λ/4 sleeve, or a half-wave loop of coax.

2.6 Small loop antennas

A loop much smaller than a wavelength carries nearly the same current all the way round, so it behaves as a magnetic dipole: the same doughnut pattern as a short dipole, with E and H swapped. Its radiation resistance falls as the fourth power of size, so on medium wave a small loop radiates almost nothing and copper loss wins. Yet the same loop is the ferrite rod inside every AM radio and the sharp-null loop a direction finder turns to take a bearing.

You need: the Hertzian dipole and its sin θ pattern from Chapter 2.1, and radiation resistance and efficiency from Chapter 2.3.

What makes a loop small

A loop antenna is a conductor bent into a closed shape, fed so current runs round it. Its size is the circumference C = 2πa, where a is the loop radius in metres. The loop is small when C ≤ λ/10, with λ the wavelength in metres.

The limit comes from phase. A wave travelling round the wire makes the current at the far side lag the current at the near side. When C is under λ/10 the lag stays under 36°, so the current can be treated as one value I everywhere. A bigger loop carries a travelling wave and needs a different analysis.

With uniform current the loop reduces to one number, its magnetic moment m.

m = N I A

m is the magnetic moment in A·m²; N is the number of turns; I is the current in amperes, the same in every turn; A is the area enclosed by one turn in m². For a circular loop A = πa². The vector m points along the loop axis: curl the fingers of the right hand along the current and the thumb gives the direction.

The small loop is the twin of the short dipole. The dipole is an electric dipole — charge q pushed a distance d apart, moment qd. The loop is a magnetic dipole of moment m. Both have the same pattern and directivity, but E and H swap roles. N turns multiply m by N, because each turn carries the same current and adds its own area.

Far field and radiation resistance

In the far field the small loop radiates only E_φ and H_θ; the short dipole radiates E_θ and H_φ. Swap one for the other and the dipole formulas carry over.

E_φ = 120π² (N I A) sin θ / (λ² r)

E_φ is the far electric field in V/m; θ is the angle measured from the loop axis; r is the distance in metres; 120π Ω is the impedance of free space. The magnetic field is H_θ = E_φ / 120π, in A/m. Both fall as 1/r, and the pattern is sin θ.

The pattern peaks in the plane of the loop and is zero along the axis, so the directivity is 1.5, that is 1.76 dBi, as for the short dipole. Pushing the power back through the field gives:

R_r = 320π⁴ (N A / λ²)²

R_r is the radiation resistance in ohms; N is the number of turns; A is the loop area in m²; λ is the wavelength in metres. The constant 320π⁴ is about 31 171.

For a single-turn circular loop, putting A = C²/(4π) turns this into a formula in the circumference alone:

R_r = 20π² (C/λ)⁴

C is the circumference in metres and λ the wavelength in metres. Both forms agree; use whichever data you are given.

The fourth power is the whole story. A single turn of area 1 m² at λ = 30 m has R_r = 31 171 × (1/900)² = 0.038 Ω. Halve every dimension and the area drops four times while R_r drops sixteen times. Wire loss of a few tenths of an ohm then swamps radiation, and efficiency falls to a few per cent.

Ferrite rods and direction finding

Medium-wave broadcast runs from about 530 kHz to 1.6 MHz, so λ lies between roughly 570 m and 190 m. Even a λ/10 loop would be several metres to nearly twenty metres across — impossible inside a radio set. The practical answer is a coil wound on a ferrite rod.

The rod concentrates flux, so the coil behaves as if its area were A_eff = μ_eff A, where μ_eff is the effective relative permeability of the rod. It is smaller than the material's own μ_r because the rod is short, but still tens to a few hundred. Since R_r goes as A², the rod raises the radiation resistance by μ_eff², while the open-circuit received voltage rises by only μ_eff, in proportion to the effective area A_eff = μ_eff A. The null lies along the rod's axis, which is why an AM radio is turned to put the rod broadside to the station.

The other everyday use is direction finding. The loop null is a true zero, and near it the pattern rises as sin θ ≈ θ, so a small rotation gives a large change in signal — a bearing to a degree or two. The deeper the null, the better the bearing; that sharpness, not gain, is the loop's real gift.

The pattern has two nulls 180° apart, so one loop cannot tell a station from its mirror image. A short vertical sense antenna, or a second loop at right angles, breaks the ambiguity. The loop impedance is small and inductive and is tuned by a capacitor; receive behaviour belongs to Chapter 2.7.

2.7 Receiving antenna, reciprocity and effective area

A receiving antenna is the same metal as a transmitting antenna, used backwards. An incoming wave induces an open-circuit voltage, and a Thevenin source with the antenna impedance drives the receiver load. Reciprocity says the pattern, impedance and gain are the same in both roles, so one measurement serves both. Effective length and effective aperture turn a field strength or a power density into a received voltage or power. Link budgets, radar and RFID all use these ideas.

You need: Chapter 2.3 for effective aperture and polarization, and Chapter 2.2 for directivity.

The receiving antenna as a Thevenin source

An incoming wave pushes charge along the metal. Model the antenna as a Thevenin source. E is the electric field strength in volts per metre (V/m). leff is the effective length in metres (m). ψ is the angle between E and the antenna. The source sits in series with the antenna impedance Za = Rr + RL + jXa. Rr is the radiation resistance; it represents power re-radiated. RL is the loss resistance; it represents heat. Xa is the antenna reactance in ohms (Ω).

Voc = E leff cos ψ

Voc is the open-circuit voltage in volts (V); E is the electric field strength in V/m; leff is the effective length in m; ψ is the angle between E and the antenna.

Connect a load ZL = Rload + jXload. Rload is the load resistance in Ω. Xload is the load reactance in Ω. I is the current in amperes (A), I = Voc/(Za + ZL). The load power is Pload = ½|I|²Rload. Maximum power transfer needs a conjugate match, ZL = Za*, so Rload = Rr + RL and Xload = −Xa. Under this match the load gets the available power, the maximum power the source can deliver. For a lossless antenna an equal amount is scattered by Rr. Half the captured power reaches the load, half is re-radiated; with loss, RL takes heat too.

Effective length and effective aperture

Effective length converts field to open-circuit volts. Assume the current distribution is known. For a short dipole of physical length l much less than λ, with current falling linearly from the feed to zero at each end, leff = l/2. For an ideal Hertzian element of length l with uniform current, leff = l. For a half-wave dipole leff = λ/π ≈ 0.318λ. At 100 MHz λ = 3 m, so leff ≈ 0.955 m. Effective aperture converts power density to received power. For a polarization-matched, conjugate-matched lossless antenna, Pr = S Ae PLF. S is power density in W/m². Ae is effective aperture in m². PLF is the polarization loss factor from Chapter 2.3; it is 1 for matched polarization and 0 for cross-polarized. Ae = D λ²/(4π). D is directivity (dimensionless), λ is wavelength in m. A short dipole has D = 1.5. Ae is not the physical area of the wire. It can be much larger than the metal. At 100 MHz a short dipole has Ae = 1.5 × 3²/(4π) = 1.07 m², even though its metal area is tiny. Received power falls with λ² for fixed D, so small antennas capture little at low frequency. Use Pr in W; convert to dBm with 10 log₁₀(Pr/1 mW).

Reciprocity

Reciprocity theorem: the transmit and receive patterns of an antenna are identical, and its impedance is the same in both roles. A two-way test makes this concrete. Use two identical antennas A and B, or at least antennas with equal radiation resistance, and terminate the receiver with the same load impedance in both measurements. Drive antenna A with current I and measure power P₁ at antenna B. Then drive B with the same current and measure at A. Under these conditions the received power is P₁ again. For identical antennas, the field at distance r is the same in both directions for the same current. Therefore one set of pattern or gain measurements describes both transmitting and receiving. This is why the Friis equation in Chapter 2.8 uses one gain for each end, and why a radar can use the same antenna to transmit and receive. Reciprocity also lets you find an unknown receiving antenna's gain by measuring it as a transmitter in a known setup.

Worked example

Question. A short dipole of directivity 1.5 receives a wave at 100 MHz with power density S = 10 µW/m², polarization matched. Find its effective aperture and the power delivered to a matched load, in watts and dBm.

  1. λ = c/f = (3 × 10⁸ m/s)/(100 × 10⁶ Hz) = 3 m.
  2. A_e = D λ²/(4π) = 1.5 × (3 m)²/(4π) = 1.5 × 9/12.566 = 1.074 m².
  3. S = 10 µW/m² = 10 × 10⁻⁶ W/m². PLF = 1. P_r = S A_e PLF = 10 × 10⁻⁶ × 1.074 × 1 = 1.074 × 10⁻⁵ W.
  4. P_dBm = 10 log₁₀(P_r/1 mW) = 10 log₁₀(1.074 × 10⁻⁵ / 10⁻³) = 10 log₁₀(0.01074) = −19.69 dBm.

Answer. A_e ≈ 1.074 m²; P_r ≈ 1.074 × 10⁻⁵ W = 10.74 µW ≈ −19.69 dBm.

Exam traps

  • A_e is not physical area. A thin wire can have A_e = 1.07 m² at 100 MHz while its metal area is tiny.
  • Conjugate match is Z_L = Z_a*, not Z_L = Z_a. Equal complex impedances do not give maximum power unless the reactances are already zero.
  • Under a conjugate match the load does not receive all intercepted power. For a lossless antenna half is scattered by R_r; loss resistance R_L adds heat.
  • Do not use physical length for a half-wave dipole's induced volts. l_eff = λ/π, not λ/2.
  • Forgetting PLF is a common slip. A cross-polarized wave gives zero received power even when A_e is large.

2.8 Friis transmission equation and link budget

The Friis transmission equation tells how much of a transmitter’s power reaches a receiver at distance d. It multiplies transmit power by both antenna gains and divides by free-space spreading loss. In decibels the same result becomes a sum of gains and losses, called a link budget. Every Wi-Fi router, mobile tower, satellite dish and radar is checked this way before it is built or installed.

You need: gain and directivity, effective aperture and the receiving antenna.

Derivation and symbols

Put an isotropic source at the transmitter. At distance d its power spreads over a sphere of area 4πd². The power density there is S = P_t G_t/(4πd²). Here P_t is transmit power in W, G_t is transmit gain as a ratio, d is separation in m, and S is in W/m².

The receiving antenna captures power through its effective aperture, A_e = G_r λ²/(4π). Here G_r is receive gain as a ratio and λ is wavelength in m. Received power is P_r = S A_e.

P_r = P_t G_t G_r (λ / (4πd))²

P_r is received power in W; P_t is transmit power in W; G_t and G_r are gain ratios, not dB; λ is wavelength in m; d is distance in m.

FSPL = (4πd / λ)²

FSPL is free-space path loss, a ratio. It is spreading loss, not absorption by air.

FSPL(dB) = 20 log₁₀(4πd / λ) = 32.44 + 20 log₁₀(f_MHz) + 20 log₁₀(d_km)

f_MHz is frequency in MHz; d_km is distance in km; 32.44 is the constant for these units. With metres and Hz, use the first form.

Link budget in decibels

Convert every power to dBm: P(dBm) = 10 log₁₀(P / 1 mW). Convert every gain to dBi: G(dBi) = 10 log₁₀(G). In dB the Friis product becomes a sum of gains minus losses.

P_r(dBm) = P_t(dBm) + G_t(dBi) + G_r(dBi) − FSPL(dB) − L_misc(dB)

L_misc is extra loss in dB from cable, connectors, mismatch and radome. Each positive dB loss is subtracted.

EIRP is the effective isotropic radiated power. In ratio form EIRP = P_t G_t; in dB EIRP(dBm) = P_t(dBm) + G_t(dBi). Regulators set band-wise EIRP limits, so a high-gain antenna must often use lower transmit power.

Link margin compares received power with receiver sensitivity: M = P_r(dBm) − P_sens(dBm). P_sens is the weakest signal the receiver needs, in dBm. Positive M means a working link; zero is exactly at threshold; negative M fails. Designers keep extra fade margin because real links change.

Assumptions and use

Friis holds only in the far field, roughly d ≥ 2D²/λ for an antenna of largest dimension D, and d much larger than λ. Both antennas must be aligned and co-polarized. The path must be clear, with no ground reflection, no multipath and no atmospheric or rain loss. Those effects belong to propagation models in Unit 3.

  • Use the same length unit for d and λ; mixing km with m gives nonsense.
  • Use ratio gains in Friis, or dB gains in the dB budget, never both in one line.
  • FSPL is only spreading loss; cable, connector, mismatch and polarization add more loss.
  • At fixed d, doubling frequency adds 20 log₁₀ 2 = 6.02 dB to FSPL, as the figure shows.

Worked example

Question. A 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi link uses P_t = 100 mW, G_t = G_r = 1 (0 dBi), distance d = 100 m, cable and connector loss L_misc = 2 dB, and receiver sensitivity −90 dBm. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s. Find λ, the free-space path loss, the received power and the link margin.

  1. Wavelength: λ = c / f = 3.00 × 10⁸ / 2.4 × 10⁹ = 0.125 m.
  2. Transmit power in dBm: P_t = 10 log₁₀(100) = 20.0 dBm.
  3. FSPL = 20 log₁₀(4πd / λ) = 20 log₁₀(4π × 100 / 0.125) = 20 log₁₀(10053.1) = 80.0 dB.
  4. Received power: P_r = 20.0 + 0 + 0 − 80.0 − 2.0 = −62.0 dBm.
  5. Link margin: M = P_r − P_sens = −62.0 − (−90) = 28.0 dB.

Answer. λ = 0.125 m, FSPL = 80.0 dB, P_r = −62.0 dBm and link margin = 28.0 dB, so the link works.

Exam traps

  • Dropping one of the two antenna gains; Friis has both G_t and G_r, and both must be in the product.
  • Mixing units: d and λ must use the same length unit, so d in km with λ in m gives a wrong answer.
  • Putting dB gains into the ratio form of Friis; convert 10 dB to 10 as a ratio, or use the dB budget.
  • Treating FSPL as the only loss and forgetting cable, connector, mismatch and polarization loss.
  • Getting the link-margin sign wrong: M = P_r − P_sens; positive works, negative fails.

EIRP is P_t G_t, the power an isotropic antenna would need to give the same peak power density. In dB, EIRP(dBm) = P_t(dBm) + G_t(dBi).

Understand At fixed distance, why does doubling the frequency add about 6 dB to FSPL? 3 marks

FSPL = (4πd/λ)² and λ = c/f, so FSPL is proportional to f². Doubling f multiplies FSPL by 4, which is 10 log₁₀ 4 = 6.02 dB.

Understand Why must a practical link have positive link margin rather than exactly zero? 3 marks

Zero margin means received power equals sensitivity, so any extra cable loss, fading or misalignment breaks the link. Positive margin reserves power for these changes.

Apply A 5 GHz link has P_t = 20 dBm, G_t = G_r = 6 dBi, d = 50 m, L_misc = 1 dB. Find FSPL and P_r. Take λ = 0.06 m. 4 marks

FSPL = 20 log₁₀(4π × 50 / 0.06) = 80.4 dB. P_r = 20 + 6 + 6 − 80.4 − 1 = −49.4 dBm.

Apply If distance d doubles and nothing else changes, how much does received power in dB change? 4 marks

FSPL is proportional to d², so doubling d adds 20 log₁₀ 2 = 6.02 dB loss. Received power falls by 6.02 dB.

Analyze A link has 3 dB margin. An extra 4 dB cable loss is added. What happens, and what is one fix? 5 marks

New margin = 3 − 4 = −1 dB, so the link fails at threshold. Fixes include reducing cable loss, increasing antenna gain, reducing distance, or increasing transmit power within the EIRP limit.

Takeaways

  1. Only an accelerating charge produces the 1/r field that survives as radiation; a charge at constant velocity carries only its near field with it.
  2. The Hertzian dipole gives the sin θ doughnut pattern and shows that field strength falls as 1/r while power density falls as 1/r².
  3. Directivity, gain and beamwidth turn a two-dimensional pattern into three numbers a link budget can multiply.
  4. Radiation resistance converts radiated power into a resistor, so a low R_r compared with loss resistance means poor efficiency.
  5. Effective aperture links a receiving antenna to the incident power density, and A_e = λ²D/4π ties it back to directivity.
  6. All the far-field formulas assume r > 2D²/λ; closer in, the reactive and radiating near fields break the simple rules.
  7. The half-wave dipole is resonant and near 73 Ω, the quarter-wave monopole is its half with half the resistance, and folding adds about 4×.
  8. A small loop radiates like a magnetic dipole, but its radiation resistance is so small that copper loss usually dominates.
  9. Reciprocity lets you measure an antenna in transmit and use the results for receive, which is how almost every antenna range works.
  10. The Friis equation in decibels is a plain sum, which is why every microwave link is planned as a link budget with a margin.

Glossary

Antenna
A structure that converts a guided wave on a transmission line into a wave in free space, or the reverse.
Aperture efficiency
The ratio of a horn or dish's effective area to its physical area, typically 0.5 to 0.7.
Axial ratio
Ratio of the major to minor axis of the polarisation ellipse; 1 (0 dB) means circular polarisation.
Balun
A device that connects a balanced antenna such as a dipole to an unbalanced coaxial line.
Beam solid angle Ω_A
The solid angle through which all the radiated power would flow at peak intensity; Ω_A = 4π/D.
Fade margin
The extra decibels a link budget keeps above the sensitivity threshold to survive fading and rain.
Fraunhofer region
The far field beyond 2D²/λ, where the wave is locally planar and pattern is distance independent.
Free-space path loss
The spreading loss (4πd/λ)² in the Friis equation, expressed in dB as 20log₁₀d + 20log₁₀f + 32.44 with d in km and f in MHz.
Fresnel region
The radiating near field between the reactive region and 2D²/λ, where the pattern changes with distance.
Front-to-back ratio
The ratio of maximum forward radiation to radiation in the exactly backward direction.
Image theory
Replacing an infinite ground plane by a mirror image of the source, used to analyse monopoles over a ground plane.
Input impedance
Z_in = R_r + R_L + jX_in, the impedance the feed line sees at the antenna terminals.
Isotropic radiator
A hypothetical antenna radiating equally in all directions; its directivity is exactly 1 (0 dBi).
Main lobe
The lobe of a pattern containing the direction of maximum radiation.
Polarisation mismatch loss
The loss from misalignment between the wave's polarisation and the antenna's, given by |ê_wave · ê_ant|².
Power density S
Power crossing unit area of a sphere around the antenna, S = E²/(2η₀), in W/m².
Radiation efficiency
The ratio of radiated power to input power, e_cd = R_r/(R_r + R_L).
Radiation intensity U
Power radiated per unit solid angle, U = r²S, measured in W/sr.
Reactive near field
The region within about 0.62√(D³/λ) where stored reactive energy dominates.
Receiver sensitivity
The smallest received power, usually in dBm, at which a receiver still meets its specified error rate.
Return loss
−20log₁₀|Γ| in dB, the fraction of incident power reflected back at the antenna terminals.
Side lobe
Any lobe other than the main lobe; it wastes power and causes interference.
VSWR
Voltage standing wave ratio on the feed line, (1+|Γ|)/(1−|Γ|); 1 means a perfect match.
