2.1 Radiation mechanism, potentials and Hertzian dipole

A wire radiates when the charges in it accelerate. A current that changes in time sends out a field that keeps travelling at the speed of light, falls as 1/r and carries power to any distance: that is radiation. The simplest radiator is the Hertzian dipole, a current element much shorter than a wavelength with the same current all along it. Its fields follow from the retarded vector potential, and every wire antenna is a sum of such elements.

You need: Maxwell’s equations in phasor form and the plane wave in free space, with intrinsic impedance η = 120π ≈ 377 Ω and phase constant β = 2π/λ.

How a current radiates

A charge moving at steady speed carries its field lines with it and nothing leaves. When it speeds up or slows down, the distant parts of its field cannot learn of the change at once, because news travels at c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. A kink forms between the old field and the new one and runs outward as a wave: that detached field is radiation.

In a wire of length l, l dI/dt = q dv/dt: a changing current is accelerating charge. A steady (DC) current does not radiate. Switching it off abruptly is a sudden deceleration and launches a short pulse, as in a spark-gap transmitter; a sinusoidal current accelerates its charges all the time, so it radiates all the time.

On a two-wire line the currents in the two close wires are opposite, so their fields cancel at a distance. Bend the last quarter wavelength of each wire outwards until they are in line: the two currents now point the same way, their fields add, and the open line has become a half-wave dipole.

Retarded potentials

The magnetic vector potential A at a point P sums every bit of current in the antenna, each taken as it was R/c seconds earlier, R being its distance from P; hence “retarded”. In phasor form the delay is the factor e−jβR.

A = (μ₀/4π) ∫ J e−jβR / R dV′. Here J is the current density in A/m² at the source point, dV′ the source volume element, R the source-to-point distance in m and μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m

Once A is known, the fields follow: H = (1/μ₀) ∇ × A, and away from the source E comes from Maxwell’s equation E = (1/jωε₀) ∇ × H, with ε₀ = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² F/m and ω = 2πf. Some syllabi also use the electric vector potential F = (ε₀/4π) ∫ M e−jβR/R dV′, built from a magnetic current density M, for slots and apertures. The route is always current → A → H → E.

Fields of the Hertzian dipole

Put an element of length l ≪ λ at the origin along z, carrying a uniform peak current I₀. In spherical coordinates r is the distance, θ the angle from the wire and φ the angle round it.

  1. Since l ≪ r, every part of the element is at R ≈ r, so the integral collapses to Az = μ₀ I₀ l e−jβr / (4πr).
  2. Resolve Az into spherical parts: Ar = Az cos θ and Aθ = −Az sin θ.
  3. The curl gives one magnetic component: Hφ = j β I₀ l sin θ e−jβr / (4πr) × [1 + 1/(jβr)].
  4. Then E = (1/jωε₀) ∇ × H gives two electric components: Er = η I₀ l cos θ e−jβr / (2πr²) × [1 + 1/(jβr)] and Eθ = j η β I₀ l sin θ e−jβr / (4πr) × [1 + 1/(jβr) − 1/(βr)²].

The brackets hold terms in 1/r, 1/r² and 1/r³. The 1/r³ (electrostatic) and 1/r² (induction) terms store energy; the 1/r term is the radiation field. All three are equal at βr = 1, that is r = λ/2π.

Far away (βr ≫ 1) only the 1/r terms are left, Er vanishes and the far field is Eθ = j η β I₀ l sin θ e−jβr / (4πr), with Hφ = Eθ/η. Using ηβ/4π = 30β = 60π/λ, the magnitude is |Eθ| = 60π I₀ l sin θ / (λ r). E ⊥ H ⊥ r with E/H = η: locally a plane wave. The pattern is sin θ: zero along the wire, largest broadside, the same at every φ.

Radiated power and radiation resistance

The average power density is S = |Eθ|²/(2η) W/m². Integrating it over a sphere of radius r, using the integral of sin³θ from 0 to π, which is 4/3, gives

Prad = 40π² (I₀ l/λ)² W. The r cancels, as it must: the same power crosses every sphere

Defining the radiation resistance Rr by Prad = ½ I₀² Rr gives Rr = 80π² (l/λ)² Ω. This holds for uniform current; a real short dipole, whose current falls linearly to zero at the tips, has one quarter of it, 20π²(l/λ)². The sin²θ power pattern has directivity 1.5.

Worked example

Question. A Hertzian dipole of length l = 1 cm carries a uniform peak current I₀ = 1 A at f = 1 GHz. Find (a) l/λ, (b) Rr and Prad, (c) |Eθ| and |Hφ| at r = 100 m broadside and at θ = 30°, and (d) check the power density against Prad with D = 1.5.

  1. λ = c/f = 3 × 10⁸ / 10⁹ = 0.3 m, so l/λ = 0.01/0.3 = 0.0333: well below 0.1, so the Hertzian model holds.
  2. Rr = 80π²(l/λ)² = 80 × 9.870 × 0.001111 = 0.877 Ω.
  3. Prad = ½ I₀² Rr = 0.5 × 1² × 0.877 = 0.439 W.
  4. Broadside, with βr = 2094 ≫ 1 so only the 1/r term matters: |Eθ| = 60π I₀ l / (λ r) = 60π × 1 × 0.01 / (0.3 × 100) = 0.0628 V/m = 62.8 mV/m.
  5. |Hφ| = |Eθ|/η = 0.0628/377 = 1.67 × 10⁻⁴ A/m = 0.167 mA/m.
  6. At θ = 30°: |Eθ| = 62.8 × sin 30° = 31.4 mV/m, half the broadside value.
  7. Power density broadside: S = |Eθ|²/(2η) = (0.0628)²/(2 × 377) = 5.24 × 10⁻⁶ W/m².
  8. Check: S = D Prad/(4πr²) = 1.5 × 0.439 / (4π × 100²) = 5.24 × 10⁻⁶ W/m². The two routes agree.

Answer. l/λ = 0.0333; Rr = 0.877 Ω; Prad = 0.439 W; |Eθ| = 62.8 mV/m broadside and 31.4 mV/m at 30°; |Hφ| = 0.167 mA/m; S = 5.24 µW/m².

Worked example 2

Question. For the same dipole (λ = 0.3 m), compare the sizes of the three terms 1, 1/(βr) and 1/(βr)² in the Eθ bracket at r = λ/2π, r = λ and r = 10λ. Where may the 1/r² and 1/r³ terms be dropped?

  1. r = λ/2π = 0.0477 m: βr = 1, so the three terms are 1, 1 and 1; the far-field formula is badly wrong here.
  2. r = λ = 0.3 m: βr = 6.28, so 1/(βr) = 0.159 and 1/(βr)² = 0.0253; the induction term is still 16 per cent.
  3. r = 10λ = 3 m: βr = 62.8, so 1/(βr) = 0.0159 and 1/(βr)² = 0.000253; below 2 per cent, so they can be dropped.

Answer. The three terms are equal at r = λ/2π = 4.8 cm; at r = λ the 1/r² term is still 16 %; by r = 10λ = 3 m it is 1.6 % and the far-field formula may be used.

Exam traps

  • Using Rr = 80π²(l/λ)² for a short dipole with triangular current. That formula is for uniform current (the Hertzian dipole); a real short dipole gives 20π²(l/λ)², four times less.
  • Keeping 1/r² or 1/r³ terms in a far-field answer, or dropping them close in. The far field is the 1/r term only, and only once βr ≫ 1.
  • Drawing the pattern with its maximum along the wire. The sin θ factor makes the axis a null and broadside the maximum.
  • Using P = I₀² Rr with a peak current. With peak I₀ the power is ½ I₀² Rr; the ½ goes only if I is an rms value.

2.2 Antenna parameters: pattern, directivity, gain, beamwidth

A radiation pattern is a shape: field or power plotted against direction. A link budget cannot use a whole shape, so the shape is reduced to a few numbers. Directivity says how strongly the antenna favours its best direction compared with an isotropic radiator. Gain is directivity reduced by the antenna’s own losses. Beamwidth says how wide the main beam is. This chapter defines radiation intensity, beam solid angle, directivity, gain, half-power and first-null beamwidths, and shows how they are quoted in dBi on a datasheet.

You need: the far field of the Hertzian dipole and its sin θ pattern (Hertzian dipole).

The radiation pattern and its lobes

Measure the far field on a large sphere round the antenna and plot it against the angles θ (from the antenna axis) and φ (round the axis). A field pattern plots |E| normalised to its peak, a power pattern plots |E|², and a dB pattern plots the power pattern in decibels (peak 0 dB, half power −3 dB). Two cuts through the peak are used: the E-plane, containing the electric field, and the H-plane, containing the magnetic field. For a vertical dipole these are any vertical plane and the horizontal plane.

The pattern breaks into lobes separated by nulls, directions where the field falls to zero. The main lobe is the lobe that contains the direction of maximum radiation. Every other lobe is a minor lobe; those beside the main lobe are side lobes, and the one pointing opposite the main lobe is the back lobe. The side-lobe level is the peak of the largest side lobe in dB below the main peak, and the front-to-back ratio compares the main peak with the back lobe.

An isotropic radiator (equal in all directions) is only a reference; an omnidirectional antenna is uniform in one plane, like a dipole; a directional antenna, like a dish, puts most of its power into one main lobe.

Radiation intensity, beam solid angle and directivity

In the far field the power density S (W/m²) falls as 1/r². Multiplying by r² removes the spreading and leaves a quantity that depends only on direction, the radiation intensity U = r² S, in watts per steradian (W/sr). The total radiated power is its integral over the sphere, Prad = ∯ U dΩ, where dΩ = sin θ dθ dφ is the element of solid angle and the whole sphere is 4π sr.

D = 4π Umax / Prad = 4π / ΩA. D is the directivity, a plain ratio; Umax is the peak intensity; ΩA is the beam solid angle in sr

The beam solid angle ΩA = ∯ Pn dΩ, with Pn = U/Umax the normalised power pattern, is the solid angle the power would fill if it flowed at peak intensity everywhere inside it and nowhere else. An isotropic source has Pn = 1 everywhere, ΩA = 4π and D = 1. Squeeze the beam and D grows. In decibels, D(dBi) = 10 log₁₀ D, “i” for isotropic.

Gain, beamwidth and datasheet numbers

G = ηrad D. G is the gain; ηrad is the radiation efficiency, the fraction of the power accepted at the terminals that is radiated (0 to 1)

Losses heat the metal and dielectric without changing the pattern, so they lower G and leave D alone: G = D only for a lossless antenna. Gain is the number a link budget uses, quoted in dBi, or in dBd relative to a half-wave dipole (dBd = dBi − 2.15).

The half-power beamwidth (HPBW) is the angle between the two directions in the main lobe where the power pattern is half its peak (−3 dB), which is where the field pattern is 1/√2 = 0.707 of its peak. The first-null beamwidth (FNBW) is the angle between the first nulls either side of the main lobe; it is always wider than the HPBW.

For a single narrow pencil beam a quick estimate links beamwidth to directivity: D ≈ 41 253 / (θE θH), where θE and θH are the half-power beamwidths in the E-plane and the H-plane, in degrees, and 41 253 is the number of square degrees in a sphere (4π × (180/π)²). It treats the beam as flat-topped and ignores side lobes, so it is rough for broad beams and wrong for more than one major lobe.

Datasheets also give the input match as return loss, −20 log₁₀|Γ| dB, or as VSWR (see reflection coefficient, standing waves and VSWR).

Worked example

Question. An antenna has the field pattern F(θ) = sin θ with no φ dependence (the Hertzian dipole). Find its HPBW, beam solid angle and directivity, and its gain in dBi if ηrad = 0.9.

  1. Power pattern: Pn = F² = sin²θ. Half power where sin²θ = 0.5, so sin θ = 0.7071 and θ = 45° or 135°.
  2. HPBW = 135° − 45° = 90°.
  3. ΩA = ∫∫ sin²θ sin θ dθ dφ = 2π × (integral of sin³θ from 0 to π) = 2π × 4/3 = 8.378 sr.
  4. D = 4π/ΩA = 12.566/8.378 = 1.5, that is 10 log₁₀ 1.5 = 1.76 dBi.
  5. G = ηrad D = 0.9 × 1.5 = 1.35, that is 10 log₁₀ 1.35 = 1.30 dBi.

Answer. HPBW = 90°, ΩA = 8.38 sr, D = 1.5 (1.76 dBi), G = 1.35 (1.30 dBi).

Worked example 2

Question. A patch-like antenna radiates only into the upper half space with radiation intensity U = U₀ cos²θ for 0 ≤ θ ≤ 90° and U = 0 below. Find (a) its exact directivity, (b) its HPBW, (c) the estimate 41 253/(θE θH) and its error in dB, and (d) its gain in dBi if ηrad = 0.8.

  1. Prad = ∫ from 0 to 2π ∫ from 0 to π/2 of U₀ cos²θ sin θ dθ dφ = 2π U₀ × 1/3 = 2.094 U₀.
  2. D = 4π Umax/Prad = 4π U₀ / (2.094 U₀) = 6.00, that is 7.78 dBi.
  3. Half power: cos²θ = 0.5 gives θ = 45° either side of the axis, so HPBW = 90° in every plane through the axis: θE = θH = 90°.
  4. Estimate: D ≈ 41 253/(90 × 90) = 5.09, that is 7.07 dBi.
  5. The estimate is low by 7.78 − 7.07 = 0.71 dB, because this beam is broad and not flat-topped.
  6. Gain: G = 0.8 × 6.00 = 4.80, that is 6.81 dBi.

Answer. D = 6.00 (7.78 dBi); HPBW = 90°; the beamwidth estimate gives 5.09 (7.07 dBi), 0.71 dB low; G = 4.80 (6.81 dBi).

Exam traps

  • Taking half power where the field is 0.5. Half power is where the field is 0.707 (power 0.5); field 0.5 is the −6 dB point.
  • Using D ≈ 41 253/(θE θH) with angles in radians, or for a broad or multi-lobe beam. It needs degrees and one pencil beam, and even then it is an estimate.
  • Writing G > D. Gain is directivity times an efficiency of at most 1, so G ≤ D; “lossless” in a question means G = D.
  • Adding dBi values to plain ratios. Convert first: 3 dBi is a ratio of 2, not 3, and G = ηrad D is done in ratios, then converted.
  • Calling the back lobe “all the minor lobes”. Minor lobes are every lobe except the main one; the back lobe is only the one opposite the main beam.

2.3 Radiation resistance, efficiency, effective aperture and polarization

Four quantities decide how well an antenna turns power into a wave and back. Radiation resistance turns radiated power into an equivalent resistor; radiation efficiency says what fraction of the input escapes instead of heating the metal; effective aperture says how much of an incoming wave is captured; polarization says whether wave and antenna agree on the direction of the electric field.

You need: directivity and gain from antenna parameters, and Prad of the Hertzian dipole.

Radiation resistance

A driven antenna draws current as if a resistor sat at its terminals, though the power has really left as a wave. The radiation resistance Rr is that resistor:

Prad = ½ |I|² Rr. Prad in W; I is the peak current in A at a stated point (the terminals or the current maximum); Rr in Ω. With an rms current the ½ goes

The reference point matters because the current varies along an antenna. A half-wave dipole has 73 Ω at its feed, which is its current maximum; a quarter-wave monopole has 36.5 Ω.

Short dipoles have two conventions. With the same current all along (the Hertzian dipole), Rr = 80π²(l/λ)². A real centre-fed short dipole has a current falling linearly to zero at the tips; its average current is half the feed current, its field half as strong, so referred to the feed Rr = 20π²(l/λ)². On this triangular convention Rr is 1.97 Ω at l = λ/10, 0.49 Ω at λ/20 and only 0.079 Ω at λ/50. The square law holds only for short dipoles; by l = λ/2 the current is a cosine and Rr is 73 Ω.

Loss resistance and radiation efficiency

Metal resistance (raised by the skin effect as √f), dielectric loss and any loading coil are lumped into a loss resistance RL in series with Rr, so ZA = Rr + RL + jXA. The same current flows through both, so power divides in proportion to them.

ηrad = Rr / (Rr + RL). ηrad is the radiation efficiency, a ratio from 0 to 1

Gain follows as G = ηrad D. Small antennas are hard to make efficient because Rr is small. With RL = 0.5 Ω, a λ/10 dipole (1.97 Ω) has ηrad = 0.80, a λ/20 dipole 0.50, and a λ/50 dipole only 0.079/0.579 = 0.14: 86 per cent of its input power heats the wire and coil. A half-wave dipole at 73 Ω sits far above any normal loss and is naturally efficient.

A DC ohmmeter cannot measure Rr; it exists only at the working frequency, as power that leaves.

Effective aperture

On receive, an antenna acts as a catching area. If a wave of power density S (W/m²) makes the antenna, matched in polarization and impedance, deliver Pr watts, the effective aperture is Ae = Pr/S in m². It is tied to directivity:

Aem = D λ² / 4π. Aem is the maximum effective aperture of a lossless antenna in m²; with loss, Ae = ηrad Aem = G λ²/4π

Ae is not the metal’s area. A half-wave dipole at 300 MHz (λ = 1 m) is a thin wire 0.5 m long, yet Ae = 1.64 × 1²/4π = 0.131 m². The relation is derived in the chapter on receiving antennas. For horns and dishes the aperture efficiency eap = Ae/Aphys stays below 1 because the field across the mouth is not uniform and some power spills past the rim; dishes reach 0.5 to 0.7, giving the dish gain G = eap(πd/λ)² for diameter d.

Polarization and the polarization loss factor

The polarization of a wave is the path traced by the tip of its electric field at a fixed point, seen as the wave approaches. Linear: a straight line. Circular: a circle, when two equal perpendicular components are 90° apart in time; the sense of rotation gives right-hand or left-hand circular. Elliptical: the general case. The axial ratio AR, major axis over minor axis of the ellipse, is 1 for circular and infinite for linear. An antenna’s polarization is that of the wave it radiates.

Two crossed dipoles fed with equal currents 90° apart radiate circular polarization along their common broadside; fed in phase they radiate linear polarization at 45°. A receiving antenna picks up only the part of the field that matches its own polarization:

PLF = |ρ̂w · ρ̂a|² = cos²ψp. ρ̂w and ρ̂a are the unit polarization vectors of the wave and of the antenna; ψp is the angle between two linear polarizations; PLF is a ratio from 0 to 1 that multiplies the received power

Worked example

Question. A centre-fed short dipole is λ/20 long and has a loss resistance RL = 0.5 Ω. Find (a) its radiation resistance with the triangular current, (b) its radiation efficiency and gain in dBi (D = 1.5), and (c) its effective aperture at 300 MHz with and without the loss.

  1. l/λ = 1/20 = 0.05, so (l/λ)² = 0.0025.
  2. Rr = 20π²(l/λ)² = 20 × 9.870 × 0.0025 = 0.493 Ω.
  3. Rr + RL = 0.493 + 0.5 = 0.993 Ω, so ηrad = 0.493/0.993 = 0.497, about 50 per cent.
  4. G = ηrad D = 0.497 × 1.5 = 0.745, that is 10 log₁₀ 0.745 = −1.28 dBi: the loss more than cancels the 1.76 dBi of directivity.
  5. λ = c/f = 3 × 10⁸/(300 × 10⁶) = 1 m (so the dipole is 5 cm long).
  6. Lossless: Aem = Dλ²/4π = 1.5 × 1/12.566 = 0.119 m².
  7. With loss: Ae = ηrad Aem = 0.497 × 0.119 = 0.0593 m².

Answer. Rr = 0.493 Ω; ηrad = 0.497; G = 0.745 (−1.28 dBi); Aem = 0.119 m², falling to 0.0593 m² with the loss.

Worked example 2

Question. A wave of power density S = 1 mW/m² reaches an antenna whose effective aperture is 0.02 m². Find the received power when (a) both are vertically polarized, (b) the antenna is tilted 30° from the wave’s linear polarization, (c) the wave is circular and the antenna linear, (d) the antenna is horizontal.

  1. Matched power: S Ae = 10⁻³ × 0.02 = 2 × 10⁻⁵ W = 20 µW.
  2. (a) PLF = 1: Pr = 20 µW.
  3. (b) PLF = cos²30° = 0.75: Pr = 15 µW, a loss of 10 log₁₀(1/0.75) = 1.25 dB.
  4. (c) A circular wave has equal components along any two perpendicular lines; the linear antenna takes one of them, PLF = 0.5: Pr = 10 µW, a 3.01 dB loss.
  5. (d) Vertical wave, horizontal antenna: PLF = cos²90° = 0, so Pr = 0 in theory; in practice a cross-polar null is 20 to 30 dB down, not zero.

Answer. 20 µW, 15 µW, 10 µW and (ideally) 0.

Exam traps

  • Quoting Rr without the reference point or the current convention. 80π²(l/λ)² is uniform current; 20π²(l/λ)² is a centre-fed short dipole referred to its feed current.
  • Using Ae = λ²/4π for every antenna. That is the isotropic value (D = 1); multiply by D, or by G if loss is counted.
  • Mixing peak and rms current in Prad = ½|I|²Rr. This doubles or halves Rr; the resulting efficiency error depends on RL, so it is not a simple factor of two.
  • Assuming a resonant antenna is efficient. Resonance only cancels the reactance; efficiency is set by Rr against RL.
  • Calling AR = 1 linear. AR = 1 is circular; a linear wave has AR → ∞, and a linear antenna receiving a circular wave loses 3 dB.

2.4 Near-field and far-field regions

The pattern, directivity and gain of an antenna hold only far enough away from it. Close in, fields that store energy and hand it back each cycle are stronger than the travelling wave, and the shape of the field still changes with distance. Two distances, set by the antenna’s largest dimension D and the wavelength λ, split the space into the reactive near field, the radiating near field and the far field. Every pattern measurement, link budget and exposure test depends on knowing which region you are in.

You need: the 1/r, 1/r² and 1/r³ terms of the Hertzian dipole.

Three regions

The Hertzian dipole showed that the field of a current has terms in 1/r, 1/r² and 1/r³, equal at r = λ/2π. Close to the antenna the fast-falling terms win; far away only the 1/r term is left. For an antenna whose largest dimension D is comparable with or larger than λ, the regions are bounded by two standard distances.

RegionDistance from the antennaWhat happens there
Reactive near fieldr < 0.62 √(D³/λ)Stored electric and magnetic energy dominates; E and H are not in phase and E/H is not 377 Ω
Radiating near field (Fresnel)0.62 √(D³/λ) < r < 2D²/λRadiation dominates, but the field shape still changes with distance
Far field (Fraunhofer)r > 2D²/λ, with r ≫ D and r ≫ λPattern fixed; E ⊥ H ⊥ r and E/H = η = 377 Ω; power density falls as 1/r²

Here D is the largest physical dimension of the antenna in metres (the diameter of a dish, the diagonal of a horn mouth), not the directivity, and λ is the free-space wavelength in metres. The boundaries are not walls: the field changes smoothly, and these distances mark where one description becomes good enough.

In the reactive region energy surges out and back each cycle. A small part of the power still leaves as radiation, but it is swamped by the stored fields, and the ratio of E to H is far from 377 Ω. For a Hertzian dipole at βr = 0.1 (r = λ/20π) the wave impedance |Eθ/Hφ| works out to about 3700 Ω: an electric dipole is a high-impedance source close in. A small loop is the opposite, a low-impedance, mostly magnetic source.

Where 2D²/λ comes from

The far-field boundary is a phase rule. Look at an aperture of size D from a point on its axis at distance r. Rays from the centre and from the edge, D/2 off axis, travel different distances, so they arrive with different phases. The pattern settles to its final shape once that phase error is small.

  1. Path from the edge: √(r² + (D/2)²) ≈ r + D²/(8r) for r ≫ D, so the extra path is Δ = D²/(8r).
  2. Phase error: 2πΔ/λ = πD²/(4λr) radians.
  3. Allow at most π/8 (22.5°): πD²/(4λr) ≤ π/8, so r ≥ 2D²/λ.

The 2 comes straight from the π/8 tolerance. A stricter tolerance would push the boundary further out, and a looser one would bring it in; π/8 is the usual compromise between pattern accuracy and the length of a test range. The reactive boundary 0.62√(D³/λ) comes from the same expansion taken one term further.

Small antennas and practical rules

For an electrically small antenna (D ≪ λ) the formula 2D²/λ comes out smaller than the antenna’s own near field, so it cannot be the boundary. Then the wavelength decides. The reactive region ends near r = λ/2π, and the 1/r² term falls to a tenth of the 1/r term at βr = 10, that is r = 10λ/2π ≈ 1.6λ. This book uses that working rule: the far field starts at r = max(2D²/λ, 1.6λ), always with r ≫ D.

  • Pattern measurement: a test range must be longer than 2D²/λ, or the measured pattern has filled-in nulls and a broadened beam (see antenna measurements).
  • Near-field scanning: probes are moved deliberately through the radiating near field and the far-field pattern is computed from the measured phases, which saves building a long range.
  • NFC and RFID at 13.56 MHz: λ = 22.1 m and λ/2π = 3.5 m, so a card tapped at 4 cm is deep in the reactive near field and couples through the magnetic field of the two coils (see small loop antennas).
  • Exposure: a phone held to the head is in the reactive near field of its antenna, so exposure is checked by measuring SAR, not from the far-field gain.

Worked example

Question. A DTH satellite-TV dish is 0.6 m across and receives at 12 GHz. Find the outer edge of its reactive near field and the start of its far field. A technician wants to check its pattern from a terrace 20 m away. Is that far enough?

  1. λ = c/f = 3 × 10⁸ / (12 × 10⁹) = 0.025 m. D = 0.6 m is 24λ, so the large-antenna rules apply.
  2. Reactive edge: 0.62 √(D³/λ) = 0.62 √(0.216/0.025) = 0.62 × 2.939 = 1.82 m.
  3. Far field: 2D²/λ = 2 × 0.36/0.025 = 28.8 m; 1.6λ = 0.04 m is far smaller, so 28.8 m decides.
  4. At 20 m the dish is in the radiating near field (1.82 m < 20 m < 28.8 m): the measured beam would be wider and the nulls shallower than the true far-field pattern.

Answer. Reactive near field to 1.82 m; far field from 28.8 m; 20 m is not enough.

Exam traps

  • Applying 2D²/λ to an electrically small antenna. When it gives less than a wavelength, the wavelength rule (about 1.6λ here) decides.
  • Thinking that a higher frequency shortens the far-field distance. For a fixed D, 2D²/λ grows as f grows: a dish needs a longer range at a higher frequency.
  • Taking E/H = 377 Ω everywhere. In the reactive region of a short dipole it can be thousands of ohms.
  • Thinking the far field is where the signal is strong. The boundary is about phase flatness and a fixed pattern, not about signal level.

2.5 Half-wave dipole, monopole and folded dipole

A half-wave dipole is a straight wire half a wavelength long, fed at its centre. At that length the wire resonates: the current stands as a cosine, largest at the feed and zero at the tips, and the input resistance is about 73 Ω. It is the reference antenna of radio. The quarter-wave monopole is half of it on a ground plane, and the folded dipole is two of them joined at the ends.

You need: radiation resistance and directivity (radiation resistance and efficiency) and the Hertzian dipole field.

Current on a half-wave dipole

The tips of the wire are open circuits, so the current must be zero there. Fed at the centre, a thin wire of total length l = λ/2 carries a standing wave of current

I(z) = I₀ cos(βz), −λ/4 ≤ z ≤ λ/4. I₀ is the peak current at the centre in A; z is the distance from the centre in m; β = 2π/λ

The current is a cosine half-arch, fuller than the triangular current of a short dipole, so the half-wave dipole radiates far more for the same feed current.

An exactly half-wave thin dipole has input impedance Zin = 73 + j42.5 Ω. The 73 Ω is radiation resistance; the +j42.5 Ω is inductive reactance from stored near-field energy. Shortening the wire slightly makes it capacitive, and at about 0.47λ to 0.48λ (thicker wire needs more shortening) the reactance cancels and the input is close to a pure resistance near 70 Ω: a good match to 75 Ω coaxial cable.

Far field, directivity and radiation resistance

Adding the fields of Hertzian elements carrying I(z), each with its own phase, gives the far field

Eθ = j 60 I₀ e−jβr cos((π/2) cos θ) / (r sin θ). Eθ in V/m, I₀ in A, r in m; Hφ = Eθ/η

Broadside the magnitude is simply |E| = 60 I₀/r. The pattern is a doughnut slightly slimmer than the short dipole’s.

Quarter-wave monopole over a ground plane

Stand one half of the dipole, λ/4 long, on a large conducting sheet and feed it against the sheet. By image theory a perfect, infinite ground plane acts as a mirror: wire plus image is the half-wave dipole. Above the plane the fields are the dipole’s; below it there is none.

  • Impedance: the same current radiates only the upper half of the dipole’s power, so Zin = ½(73 + j42.5) = 36.5 + j21.25 Ω, close to 50 Ω coaxial cable once trimmed.
  • Directivity: that half power fills only half the space, so D = 2 × 1.64 = 3.28, that is 5.16 dBi. The pattern is the top half of the doughnut.
  • Size: λ/4 is half the dipole’s length: 0.75 m at 100 MHz, 3.1 cm at 2.4 GHz.

Real ground planes are finite (mast radials, a car roof, a phone’s circuit board), so the impedance and gain move away from these ideals.

Folded dipole and balun

Bend a wire one wavelength long into a narrow loop λ/2 long, its two parallel sides a small distance s ≪ λ apart and joined at both ends, and feed one side at its centre. This is the folded dipole. On the antenna mode both sides carry equal currents in the same direction, so for the same feed current I the radiating current is 2I and the field is doubled. Power goes as field squared, so the same feed current delivers four times the power: Rin = 4 × 73 = 292 Ω, usually called 300 Ω. With N equal parallel wires the step-up is N².

That matches 300 Ω twin-lead, which is why TV Yagis use a folded driven element; the fatter effective conductor also widens the bandwidth.

A dipole is balanced: its two arms are symmetric about ground. Coaxial cable is unbalanced: its outer conductor is at ground. Connected directly, current flows on the outside of the shield and skews the pattern. A balun (balanced to unbalanced) sits between them: a transformer, a λ/4 sleeve, or a λ/2 loop of coax, which also steps impedance 4 : 1.

2.6 Small loop antennas

A loop much smaller than a wavelength carries nearly the same current all the way round, so it behaves as a magnetic dipole: the short dipole’s doughnut pattern with E and H exchanged. Its radiation resistance grows as the fourth power of its size, so it radiates poorly; as a receiver that hardly matters, which is why a coil on a ferrite rod sits in every AM radio.

You need: the Hertzian dipole field and radiation efficiency.

When a loop is small

A loop antenna is a conductor bent into a closed shape (circle or square) with a feed gap. For a circle of radius a the circumference is C = 2πa. The loop is small when C ≤ λ/10. The phase then changes by at most 36° round the loop, so the current is one value I everywhere.

A uniform current loop is a magnetic dipole with moment

m = N I A. m in A·m²; N is the number of turns; I the current in A; A the area of one turn in m² (πa² for a circle). m points along the loop axis by the right-hand rule

The loop is the twin of the short electric dipole: both have the sin θ pattern and D = 1.5, but the loop radiates Eφ and Hθ where the dipole radiates Eθ and Hφ.

Far field and radiation resistance

Eφ = 120π² N I A sin θ / (λ² r). Eφ in V/m; θ is measured from the loop axis; r in m; Hθ = −Eφ/η

The pattern is largest in the plane of the loop and zero along its axis; E lies in the loop’s plane. Integrating |Eφ|²/(2η) over a sphere and setting it equal to ½ I² Rr gives

Rr = 320π⁴ (N A / λ²)² ≈ 31 171 (N A / λ²)² Ω. A in m², λ in m

For a single circular turn A = C²/4π, which turns this into Rr = 20π² (C/λ)⁴. The fourth power is the whole story. A single turn of 1 m² at λ = 30 m (10 MHz) has Rr = 31 171 × (1/900)² = 0.038 Ω. Halve its radius and the area falls four times while Rr falls sixteen times. Rr grows as N² while loss grows roughly as N, so turns help only slowly.

Its input impedance is a small resistance plus a large inductive reactance, tuned out by a capacitor; the high Q makes a small loop narrow-band.

Ferrite rods and direction finding

Medium-wave broadcasting runs from about 530 kHz to 1.6 MHz, where λ is 566 m to 188 m. A loop with C = λ/10 would be 6 m to 18 m across, so a coil is wound on a ferrite rod instead. The rod gathers flux, so the coil acts as if its area were μeff A; the effective permeability μeff of a short rod is well below the ferrite’s μr but still tens to a few hundred.

On receive, the induced voltage is V = E leff with an effective length leff = 2π N μeff A / λ, so the rod raises the received voltage by μeff and the radiation resistance by μeff². The rod’s null lies along its axis, which is why a portable radio is turned until the rod lies broadside to the station.

A loop null is a true zero with the pattern rising as sin θ ≈ θ beside it, so turning a loop for the null gives a bearing to a degree or two. The two nulls are 180° apart; adding a short vertical sense antenna gives a heart-shaped pattern with one null and removes the doubt.

2.7 Receiving antenna, reciprocity and effective area

A receiving antenna is a transmitting antenna used the other way round: the incoming wave induces a voltage, and the antenna drives the receiver like a source in series with its own impedance. Reciprocity makes the pattern, impedance and gain the same in both roles. Effective length turns a field strength into volts, and effective area turns a power density into watts.

You need: effective aperture and the polarization loss factor (radiation resistance, efficiency and aperture).

The receiving antenna as a Thevenin source

A wave of field strength E (V/m) arriving at an antenna pushes charge along the metal. At the open terminals this appears as a voltage

Voc = E leff cos ψ. Voc in V; leff is the effective length in m; ψ is the angle between the field and the antenna’s own polarization direction

Seen from the receiver, the antenna is a source Voc in series with its impedance ZA = Rr + RL + jXA, the same impedance it shows as a transmitter. A load ZL takes the current I = Voc/(ZA + ZL) and the power ½|I|² Rload. The most power flows with a conjugate match, ZL = ZA* (Rload = Rr + RL, Xload = −XA), and then

PL = |Voc|² / (8(Rr + RL)). the available power in W, with Voc a peak value

Under that match, for a lossless antenna, an equal power is re-radiated (scattered) through Rr: half of what the antenna collects reaches the load. With loss, RL takes its share as heat. Any mismatch costs a further factor 1 − |Γ|².

Effective length and effective area

Effective length is the length of a uniform current element that would give the same Voc. It depends on the current distribution: leff = l for the Hertzian dipole (uniform current), leff = l/2 for a centre-fed short dipole (triangular current), and leff = λ/π ≈ 0.318λ for a half-wave dipole, not its physical λ/2.

The link between effective area and directivity can be checked on the Hertzian dipole, using only results of this unit:

  1. Matched, broadside: Voc = E l, so PL = E² l² / (8Rr) = E² l² / (8 × 80π² l²/λ²) = E² λ² / (640π²).
  2. Power density of the wave: S = E²/(2η) = E²/(240π).
  3. Aem = PL/S = 240π λ² / (640π²) = 3λ²/(8π) = 1.5 × λ²/(4π).

Since D = 1.5 for this dipole, Aem = D λ²/4π. Reciprocity makes this ratio Aem/D = λ²/4π the same for every antenna, so it holds in general. The capture area of a thin wire is far larger than its metal: a short dipole at 100 MHz has Aem = 1.5 × 9/4π = 1.07 m². With polarization mismatch the received power is Pr = S Ae × PLF.

Reciprocity

Take two antennas, 1 and 2, in any linear medium. Drive antenna 1 with current I₁ and measure the open-circuit voltage V₂ at antenna 2; then drive antenna 2 with the same current and measure V₁ at antenna 1. The reciprocity theorem says the two transfer impedances are equal: Z₂₁ = V₂/I₁ = V₁/I₂ = Z₁₂, even if the antennas are quite different. A network with this property is reciprocal, like any circuit of resistors, inductors and capacitors.

  • Pattern: moving antenna 2 round antenna 1 maps the same pattern whether 1 transmits or receives.
  • Impedance: the self-impedance Z₁₁ seen at the terminals is the same in both roles, so a match found on transmit holds on receive.
  • Gain and effective area: they are tied by Ae = Gλ²/4π, so a gain measured on transmit gives the receiving area.

Received powers are not automatically equal in the two directions. They are equal when the two set-ups are identical (same source and load impedances, same current), which is how an antenna range uses one measurement for both roles. Reciprocity fails with ferrites in a magnetic field or with the ionosphere in the Earth’s field, which are non-reciprocal media; circulators and isolators use this on purpose.

Worked example

Question. A short dipole (D = 1.5) receives a 100 MHz wave of power density S = 10 µW/m², polarization matched. Find its maximum effective aperture and the power delivered to a conjugate-matched load, in watts and dBm.

  2. Aem = Dλ²/4π = 1.5 × 9/12.566 = 1.074 m².

  4. In dBm: 10 log₁₀(1.074 × 10⁻⁵ / 10⁻³) = 10 log₁₀(0.01074) = −19.7 dBm.

Answer. Aem = 1.07 m²; Pr = 10.7 µW = −19.7 dBm.

Question. A resonant half-wave dipole (ZA = 73 Ω, D = 1.64) receives a 100 MHz wave of field strength 1 mV/m (peak), polarization matched. Find (a) Voc, (b) the power into a matched 73 Ω load, (c) the same power from S and Aem, and (d) the power into a 50 Ω receiver.

Exam traps

  • Using the physical λ/2 for a half-wave dipole. Its effective length is λ/π.
  • Writing the match as ZL = ZA. Maximum power needs the conjugate, ZL = ZA*.
  • Thinking the matched load gets all the captured power. For a lossless antenna half is re-radiated through Rr.
  • Saying reciprocity makes the received powers equal in both directions for any two antennas. It makes the transfer impedances equal; equal powers need identical source and load set-ups.

2.8 Friis transmission equation and link budget

The Friis transmission equation gives the power a receiving antenna collects from a transmitter at distance d in free space. It multiplies the transmit power by both antenna gains and divides by the spreading of the wave. Written in decibels it becomes a plain sum of gains and losses, the link budget, which is checked against the receiver’s sensitivity to find the margin. Wi-Fi, point-to-point microwave and satellite links are all planned this way; radar uses the same idea twice, out to the target and back.

You need: gain (antenna parameters), effective aperture (receiving antenna) and the far-field distance (near and far field).

Derivation

  1. An isotropic source of power Pt spreads it over a sphere of area 4πd², giving S = Pt/(4πd²) at distance d.
  2. A transmitting antenna of gain Gt pointed at the receiver raises that density by Gt: S = Pt Gt/(4πd²). The product PtGt is the EIRP.
  3. The receiving antenna collects S times its effective area Aer = Gr λ²/4π: Pr = S Aer.

Pr = Pt Gt Gr (λ / 4πd)². Pr, Pt in W; Gt, Gr as ratios, not dB; λ and d in the same unit

Writing both gains as apertures gives the aperture form Pr = Pt Aet Aer / (λ² d²). The two forms tell different stories. For fixed gains, received power falls as λ² gets smaller, that is with frequency squared; for fixed apertures (dishes of fixed size) it rises with frequency squared, because each dish’s gain grows faster than the spreading loss.

The factor (4πd/λ)² is the free-space path loss (FSPL). It is not absorption by the air: it is the share of the spreading wave that an isotropic receiving antenna happens to catch.

FSPL(dB) = 32.44 + 20 log₁₀ fMHz + 20 log₁₀ dkm. f in MHz and d in km; the constant is 20 log₁₀(4π × 10⁹/c)

The link budget in decibels

Convert powers to dBm, P(dBm) = 10 log₁₀(P/1 mW), and gains to dBi. The product becomes a sum:

Pr = Pt + Gt + Gr − FSPL − Lt − Lr − Lmisc. all in dBm or dB; Lt and Lr are the feeder (cable and connector) losses at each end; Lmisc covers polarization, pointing and radome losses

In dB form, EIRP(dBm) = Pt + Gt − Lt. Regulators limit EIRP in each band, so fitting a higher-gain antenna can force a lower transmit power.

The receiver’s sensitivity Psens is the weakest signal it can use at its specified error rate. The link margin M = Pr − Psens must be positive; designers keep a fade margin of 10 to 30 dB or more for rain, multipath and misalignment, depending on how reliable the link must be. Solving the budget for the largest FSPL that still leaves the required margin gives the maximum range.

When Friis holds

  • Far field at both ends: d > 2D²/λ for the larger antenna, and d ≫ λ.
  • Free space: a clear line of sight, no ground reflection, no multipath, no rain or gas absorption. Real paths add these (see ground, sky and space wave propagation).
  • Matched antennas: both pointed at each other, co-polarized and impedance matched; otherwise multiply by the PLF and by 1 − |Γ|² at each end.
  • Consistent units: d and λ in the same unit; gains as ratios in the product form and in dB only in the dB form.

FSPL grows by 20 log₁₀ 2 = 6.02 dB for every doubling of distance, and by the same 6.02 dB for every doubling of frequency with fixed gains.

Worked example

Question. A 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi link has Pt = 100 mW, 0 dBi antennas at both ends, d = 100 m, 2 dB of cable and connector loss in all, and a receiver sensitivity of −90 dBm. Find λ, FSPL, the received power and the link margin.

  1. λ = c/f = 3 × 10⁸ / (2.4 × 10⁹) = 0.125 m.
  2. Pt = 10 log₁₀(100 mW/1 mW) = 20.0 dBm.
  3. FSPL = 20 log₁₀(4πd/λ) = 20 log₁₀(4π × 100/0.125) = 20 log₁₀(10 053) = 80.05 dB. Check with the MHz–km form: 32.44 + 67.60 + (−20.00) = 80.04 dB.
  4. Pr = 20.0 + 0 + 0 − 80.05 − 2.0 = −62.05 dBm.
  5. M = −62.05 − (−90) = 27.95 dB, about 28 dB.

Answer. λ = 0.125 m; FSPL = 80.05 dB; Pr = −62.0 dBm; margin 28.0 dB, so the link works with room for walls and fading.

Worked example 2

Question. A 6 GHz point-to-point microwave hop is 40 km long. Each end has a 1.2 m dish with aperture efficiency 0.55 and 2 dB of feeder loss; the transmitter gives 1 W and the receiver needs −75 dBm. Find the dish gain, the FSPL, the received power and the margin, and check that Friis applies.

  1. λ = 3 × 10⁸ / (6 × 10⁹) = 0.05 m.
  2. Dish gain: G = eap(πD/λ)² = 0.55 × (π × 1.2/0.05)² = 0.55 × 5685 = 3127, that is 34.95 dBi.
  3. FSPL = 32.44 + 20 log₁₀ 6000 + 20 log₁₀ 40 = 32.44 + 75.56 + 32.04 = 140.04 dB.
  4. Pt = 1 W = 30 dBm; EIRP = 30 − 2 + 34.95 = 62.95 dBm.
  5. Pr = 62.95 − 140.04 + 34.95 − 2 = −44.14 dBm.
  6. M = −44.14 − (−75) = 30.86 dB, about 31 dB of fade margin.
  7. Far field: 2D²/λ = 2 × 1.44/0.05 = 57.6 m, far less than 40 km, so the far-field formula is valid.

Exam traps

  • Dropping one of the two gains. Friis has both Gt and Gr.
  • Putting dB values into the ratio form, or ratios into the dB form. A 20 dBi antenna enters the product form as 100 and the dB form as 20.
  • Using d in km with λ in m in 20 log₁₀(4πd/λ). Only the 32.44 form takes MHz and km.
  • Treating FSPL as the only loss. Feeder, mismatch, polarization and pointing losses are added on top.
  • Getting the margin backwards. M = Pr − Psens; with Psens = −90 dBm and Pr = −62 dBm the margin is +28 dB.

Takeaways

  1. Only accelerating charge, that is a time-varying current, radiates; the radiated field falls as 1/r, and its power density as 1/r².
  2. The Hertzian dipole’s fields come from the retarded vector potential and have 1/r, 1/r² and 1/r³ terms that are equal at r = λ/2π; far away only the 1/r term with its sin θ pattern is left.
  3. Directivity, gain and beamwidth reduce a pattern to numbers: D = 4π/ΩA, G = ηradD, and the HPBW sits at the −3 dB (field 0.707) points.
  4. Radiation resistance must be quoted with its current convention: 80π²(l/λ)² for uniform current, 20π²(l/λ)² for a real short dipole, 73 Ω for a half-wave dipole.
  5. Small antennas have tiny Rr, so loss resistance takes most of the power; a λ/20 dipole with 0.5 Ω of loss radiates only half its input.
  6. Every antenna has Aem = Dλ²/4π, so a thin wire can capture power from an area much larger than its metal.
  7. Far-field formulas need r > 2D²/λ for large antennas and a few wavelengths for small ones; a 0.6 m dish at 12 GHz needs 28.8 m.
  8. The half-wave dipole (73 Ω, 2.15 dBi), the monopole (36.5 Ω, 5.16 dBi) and the folded dipole (292 Ω) are the workhorse wire antennas; a small loop is a magnetic dipole with Rr ∝ (size)⁴.
  9. Reciprocity (Z₁₂ = Z₂₁) lets an antenna measured on transmit be used on receive with the same pattern, impedance and gain.
  10. In decibels the Friis equation is a sum; the link margin Pr − Psens must stay positive with room for fading.

Remember and understand

Understand Explain the radiation mechanism of an antenna. Why does a charge moving at constant velocity not radiate while an accelerating charge does? How does a two-wire line become a radiator? 10 marks

Points to cover. Static charge and steady current: fields tied to the source, nothing leaves; Constant velocity: field lines move with the charge, no kink; Acceleration: a kink forms and travels outward at c as the radiation field; l dI/dt = q dv/dt: a time-varying current is accelerating charge; Two-wire line: opposite currents cancel; flared into a dipole they add and radiate; Radiated field falls as 1/r and power density as 1/r².

Model answer.

  1. Radiation is a field that leaves its source and keeps travelling, carrying power to any distance. Its electric field falls as 1/r, so its power density falls as 1/r², and the power crossing any sphere round the source is the same: it never returns.
  2. A charge at rest has radial field lines fixed to it; a steady current has a magnetic field fixed to the wire. Neither changes with time, so nothing travels away. A charge moving at constant velocity drags its field pattern with it unchanged; at every point the field simply follows the charge, again with no outward wave.
  3. Now let the charge accelerate for a short time. Information about the change spreads at c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. Beyond a radius ct the field lines still point to where the charge would have been; inside it they point to its new position. Between the two a kink forms. The kink is a transverse field that moves outward at c and is no longer attached to the charge: that is radiation. The faster the acceleration, the sharper the kink and the stronger the radiated field.
  4. In a wire of length l carrying charge q, a change of current is a change of velocity of the charge: l dI/dt = q dv/dt. So a time-varying current radiates, and a sinusoidal current, which accelerates its charges all the time, radiates all the time. A DC current does not radiate; switching it off suddenly radiates a short pulse.
  5. A two-wire transmission line carries equal and opposite currents on two wires a small distance apart. Their fields cancel almost completely at a distance, so the line guides power without radiating. Bend the last λ/4 of each wire outward until the two are in line. The currents on the two arms now flow in the same direction, so their fields add instead of cancelling, and the open end of the line has become a half-wave dipole that radiates strongly. An antenna is therefore a transition between a guided wave and a free-space wave.
  6. The field of a small current element shows both parts of the field at once:
    Eθ ∝ [1/r (radiation) + 1/r² (induction) + 1/r³ (electrostatic)]
terms equal at r = λ/2π; only the 1/r term reaches far away
  7. The usual picture of a dipole shows it clearly. In the first quarter cycle charges separate to the two arms and field lines stretch from one arm to the other. In the next quarter cycle the charges return and the ends of each field line move back towards the feed; the outer part of the line cannot follow at once, so the line closes on itself into a loop and breaks away. A new set of loops with the opposite direction forms in the next half cycle and pushes the first outward. The detached loops, spaced λ/2, travel outward at c: the radiated wave.
  8. Radiation therefore needs (a) a time-varying current, (b) a structure whose currents do not cancel at a distance, and (c) a size that is not negligible against λ, since the radiated power grows as (l/λ)² for a short element.
  9. Check with numbers: a Hertzian element 1 cm long carrying 1 A at 1 GHz (l/λ = 0.0333) radiates Prad = 40π²(I₀ l/λ)² = 0.439 W, while the same 1 A as a steady current radiates nothing. At 100 m its field is 62.8 mV/m and at 1 km 6.28 mV/m, a tenth: the 1/r law of radiation.
Understand Define radiation pattern, radiation intensity, beam solid angle, directivity, gain and half-power beamwidth. How is gain related to directivity? Find D, G and HPBW for a field pattern sin θ with 90 % efficiency. 10 marks

Points to cover. Pattern: field or power against angle; E-plane and H-plane cuts; lobes and nulls; U = r²S in W/sr; ΩA = ∯ Pn dΩ; D = 4πUmax/Prad = 4π/ΩA; isotropic D = 1; G = ηradD; dBi; HPBW between −3 dB points; FNBW between first nulls; sin θ pattern: HPBW 90°, D 1.5, G 1.35.

Model answer.

  1. Radiation pattern: the far-field strength plotted against direction (θ, φ) at a fixed large distance, normalised to its peak. A field pattern plots |E|, a power pattern |E|², a dB pattern 10 log₁₀ of the power pattern. Two cuts are drawn: the E-plane (containing E and the peak direction) and the H-plane (containing H). The pattern has a main lobe, minor lobes (side and back lobes) and nulls.
  2. Radiation intensity U = r²S, the power per unit solid angle in W/sr. Since S falls as 1/r² in the far field, U depends only on direction. The total power is Prad = ∯ U dΩ, with dΩ = sin θ dθ dφ.
  3. Beam solid angle ΩA = ∯ Pn dΩ, with Pn = U/Umax: the solid angle through which all the power would flow if the intensity were Umax inside it and zero outside.
  4. Directivity D = 4πUmax/Prad = 4π/ΩA: the peak intensity over the average intensity. An isotropic source has D = 1 (0 dBi).
  5. Gain G = ηradD, where ηrad = Prad/Pin is the radiation efficiency. Losses lower the radiated power but not the pattern shape, so G ≤ D and G = D only for a lossless antenna. Both are quoted in dBi.
  6. Half-power beamwidth: the angle between the two directions of the main lobe where the power is half the peak (−3 dB), that is the field is 0.707 of the peak. First-null beamwidth: the angle between the first nulls either side; it is wider.

Model answer.

  1. Radiation resistance Rr: the fictitious resistance that would absorb the radiated power when the antenna current flows through it, Prad = ½|I|²Rr with I the peak current at a stated point (feed or current maximum). It is not a physical resistor and cannot be measured with an ohmmeter; it exists only at the working frequency.
  2. The value depends on the current convention. Uniform current (Hertzian dipole): Rr = 80π²(l/λ)². A real centre-fed short dipole has current falling linearly to zero at the tips, half the average current and so a quarter of the power for the same feed current: Rr = 20π²(l/λ)². Half-wave dipole: 73 Ω; quarter-wave monopole: 36.5 Ω.
  3. Loss resistance RL: the series resistance that accounts for power turned into heat in the conductors (raised by the skin effect, rising as √f), in the dielectric and in any loading coil or matching network.
  4. Input impedance ZA = Rr + RL + jXA: what the feed line sees at the terminals. The reactance XA comes from energy stored in the near field. At resonance XA = 0; resonance makes matching easy but says nothing about efficiency.

Analyze Derive the fields of a Hertzian dipole from the retarded vector potential, identify the 1/r, 1/r² and 1/r³ terms, and hence find its radiated power, radiation resistance and directivity. 14 marks

Points to cover. Az = μ₀I₀l e−jβr/(4πr); spherical components; H = ∇ × A/μ₀ → Hφ; E = ∇ × H/(jωε₀) → Er, Eθ; Near terms store energy; far field Eθ = jηβI₀l sin θ e−jβr/(4πr); Prad = 40π²(I₀l/λ)², Rr = 80π²(l/λ)², D = 1.5.

Model answer.

  1. Set-up: a straight element of length l ≪ λ along the z-axis at the origin, carrying a uniform phasor current I₀ (peak). Observation point at (r, θ, φ). Free space: μ₀, ε₀, η = √(μ₀/ε₀) = 120π Ω, β = ω√(μ₀ε₀) = 2π/λ.
  2. Vector potential. Since l ≪ r, every part of the element is at distance R ≈ r:
    A = (μ₀/4π) ∫ I e−jβR/R dl  →  Az = μ₀ I₀ l e−jβr/(4πr)
Ar = Az cos θ,   Aθ = −Az sin θ,   Aφ = 0
  3. Magnetic field. Only the φ component of the curl survives:
    Hφ = (1/μ₀r)[∂(rAθ)/∂r − ∂Ar/∂θ]
    = j β I₀ l sin θ e−jβr/(4πr) × [1 + 1/(jβr)]
Hr = Hθ = 0
  4. Electric field from Maxwell’s equation E = ∇ × H/(jωε₀):
    Er = η I₀ l cos θ e−jβr/(2πr²) × [1 + 1/(jβr)]
Eθ = j η β I₀ l sin θ e−jβr/(4πr) × [1 + 1/(jβr) − 1/(βr)²]
Eφ = 0
  5. The terms: in Eθ the 1/r³ term (with 1/(βr)²) is the field of a static electric dipole of moment q l, the 1/r² term is the induction field (Biot–Savart for Hφ), and the 1/r term is the radiation field. The near terms are 90° out of phase with the matching magnetic terms, so they hold stored energy that flows back each cycle; only the 1/r terms of Eθ and Hφ are in phase and carry net power. All terms are equal in size at βr = 1, r = λ/2π. Er has no 1/r term, so it vanishes far away.
  6. Far field (βr ≫ 1):
    Eθ = j η β I₀ l sin θ e−jβr/(4πr),   Hφ = Eθ/η
|Eθ| = 60π I₀ l sin θ/(λr)
  7. Radiated power. Power density S = |Eθ|²/(2η); integrate over a sphere:
    Prad = ∫∫ S r² sin θ dθ dφ = (η β² I₀² l²/32π²) × 2π × (4/3)
      = η π (I₀ l/λ)²/3 = 40π² (I₀ l/λ)²   (η = 120π)
  8. Radiation resistance: ½ I₀² Rr = Prad gives Rr = 80π²(l/λ)² Ω. Directivity: U = r²S ∝ sin²θ, Umax at θ = 90°, so D = 4πUmax/Prad = 4π/(8π/3) = 1.5 (1.76 dBi). HPBW = 90°.
  9. Wave impedance of the near field: dividing Eθ by Hφ gives Z = η[1 + 1/(jβr) − 1/(βr)²]/[1 + 1/(jβr)]. Its magnitude is about 3730 Ω at βr = 0.1, 267 Ω at βr = 1 and 373 Ω at βr = 10, approaching η = 377 Ω only far away: close to an electric dipole the field is mostly electric.
  10. Power in the near terms: the complex Poynting vector ½ E × H* of the near terms is imaginary, so their time-average power is zero; it is reactive power that sets the large capacitive reactance of a short dipole. The real part of the power comes entirely from the 1/r terms, which is why the integral over a sphere of any radius gives the same Prad.
  11. Half-power beamwidth check: sin²θ = 0.5 at 45° and 135°, so HPBW = 90°, and the doughnut is uniform in φ.
  12. The curl worked out for Hφ:
    rAθ = −μ₀I₀l sin θ e−jβr/(4π)    →  ∂(rAθ)/∂r = jβ μ₀I₀l sin θ e−jβr/(4π)
∂Ar/∂θ = −μ₀I₀l sin θ e−jβr/(4πr)
Hφ = (1/μ₀r)[jβ μ₀I₀l sin θ/(4π) + μ₀I₀l sin θ/(4πr)] e−jβr
    = jβI₀l sin θ e−jβr/(4πr) × [1 + 1/(jβr)]
  13. Duality: a small loop of moment m = NIA gives the same fields with E and H exchanged (Eφ, Hr, Hθ); its far field is Eφ = 120π²NIA sin θ/(λ²r), so the derivation above also yields the loop by replacing I₀l with jβNIA.
  14. Check with numbers: l = 1 cm, I₀ = 1 A, f = 1 GHz (λ = 0.3 m): Rr = 0.877 Ω, Prad = 0.439 W; at 100 m broadside |Eθ| = 60π × 0.01/(0.3 × 100) = 62.8 mV/m, and S = (0.0628)²/754 = 5.24 µW/m² equals 1.5 × 0.439/(4π × 10⁴) = 5.24 µW/m²: the field, the power and the directivity are consistent.

Evaluate State and explain the reciprocity theorem for antennas, and show that the maximum effective aperture of any antenna is Dλ²/4π, working it out for the Hertzian dipole. Use them to justify measuring an antenna’s gain on transmit and using it on receive. 12 marks

Points to cover. Z₁₂ = Z₂₁ for two antennas in a linear, isotropic medium; Consequences: same pattern, impedance, gain in both roles; Hertzian dipole: PL = E²λ²/(640π²), S = E²/(240π), Aem = 3λ²/8π = 1.5λ²/4π; Aem/D = λ²/4π for all antennas; half-wave dipole 0.13λ²; Range measurement; half-wave dipole numbers at 100 MHz.

Model answer.

  1. Reciprocity theorem: if a current I₁ at the terminals of antenna 1 produces an open-circuit voltage V₂ at antenna 2, then the same current at antenna 2 produces the same open-circuit voltage at antenna 1. In network terms the two antennas form a two-port with Z₁₂ = Z₂₁. It holds in any linear, passive, isotropic medium (air, ordinary dielectrics and metals), whatever the two antennas are.
  2. Consequences: (1) the receiving pattern of an antenna equals its transmitting pattern, because moving antenna 2 round antenna 1 maps the same transfer impedance either way; (2) the self-impedance Z₁₁ is the same in both roles, so a match made on transmit holds on receive; (3) the gain on transmit and the effective aperture on receive are tied by one universal constant, shown below.
  3. Effective aperture of the Hertzian dipole (uniform current, l ≪ λ), wave arriving broadside and matched:
    Voc = E l                         (leff = l)
PL = Voc²/(8Rr) = E² l²/(8 × 80π² l²/λ²) = E² λ²/(640π²)
S = E²/(2η) = E²/(240π)
Aem = PL/S = 240π λ²/(640π²) = 3λ²/(8π) = 1.5 × λ²/(4π)
  4. Since D = 1.5 for this dipole, Aem = Dλ²/4π. By reciprocity, if two antennas face each other, the power transfer is the same in both directions for identical set-ups, which forces Aem/D to be the same for both antennas. The dipole fixes the constant, so Aem/D = λ²/4π for every antenna, and with loss Ae = Gλ²/4π. For a half-wave dipole Aem = 1.64λ²/4π = 0.13λ²; for an isotropic antenna λ²/4π = 0.0796λ².
  5. Justification of the measurement practice: an antenna range drives the antenna under test as a transmitter and measures its pattern and gain (for example by comparison with a standard horn). Reciprocity guarantees that the same pattern applies on receive, and Ae = Gλ²/4π converts the measured gain into the receiving aperture. The same reasoning lets one dish serve both directions of a satellite or radar link.
  6. Numerical check, half-wave dipole at 100 MHz (λ = 3 m) in a 1 mV/m field:
    RouteWorkingReceived power
    CircuitVoc = E λ/π = 0.955 mV; Voc²/(8 × 73)1.56 nW
    ApertureS = 10⁻⁶/754 = 1.326 nW/m²; Aem = 1.64 × 9/4π = 1.175 m²1.56 nW
  7. Equivalent circuit on receive: the antenna is a source Voc in series with ZA. With a conjugate-matched load the load takes |Voc|²/8RA, and for a lossless antenna the same power is re-radiated through Rr; Aem is defined with this matched load.
  8. Using reciprocity in a gain measurement (two identical antennas): measure Pr/Pt at a known distance and solve Friis for G. Two identical horns at 10 GHz (λ = 0.03 m), 3 m apart:
    measured Pr/Pt = 6.33 × 10⁻³  (−22.0 dB)
G = (4πd/λ) √(Pr/Pt) = (4π × 3/0.03) × 0.0796 = 100  (20 dBi)
  9. The method works only because the two antennas have the same gain in both roles; the horns are about 12 cm across, so 2D²/λ ≈ 1 m and 3 m is in the far field.
  10. Why the constant is universal: place antenna 1 (gain D₁, area A₁) and antenna 2 (D₂, A₂) facing each other. Power sent from 1 to 2 is P D₁ A₂/(4πd²); from 2 to 1 it is P D₂ A₁/(4πd²). Reciprocity makes them equal for identical set-ups, so A₁/D₁ = A₂/D₂: the ratio of aperture to directivity is the same for every antenna.
  11. In general the receiving area includes every loss: Ae = ηrad(1 − |Γ|²) × PLF × Dλ²/4π. The ideal Aem = Dλ²/4π assumes a lossless antenna, a conjugate-matched load and a polarization-matched wave.
  12. Limits: reciprocity fails in non-reciprocal media (magnetized ferrite, the ionosphere in the Earth’s field), and equal received powers need equal source and load conditions; the transfer impedances are always equal, the powers only when the set-ups match.
Analyze Explain the reactive near-field, radiating near-field and far-field regions with a labelled sketch, derive the 2D²/λ boundary, and describe what goes wrong if a pattern is measured too close. Illustrate with a 0.6 m dish at 12 GHz. 12 marks

Points to cover. Field terms 1/r³, 1/r², 1/r; stored versus radiated energy; Reactive edge 0.62√(D³/λ); Fresnel; Fraunhofer beyond 2D²/λ; Derivation: path difference D²/8r ≤ λ/16 (π/8); Small antennas: wavelength rule; Too-close measurement: broadened beam, filled nulls, low gain; DTH dish 1.82 m and 28.8 m.

Model answer.

  1. Around any antenna the field contains terms falling as 1/r³ and 1/r² (stored, reactive energy that flows out and back each cycle) and a term falling as 1/r (radiation). Their balance divides space into three regions.
  2. Sketch (antenna at the left, distance increasing to the right):
  3. Reactive near field, r < 0.62√(D³/λ): stored energy dominates, E and H are not in phase, and the wave impedance is far from 377 Ω (thousands of ohms close to a short dipole). Radiating near field (Fresnel), up to 2D²/λ: radiation dominates but the angular shape of the field still depends on r. Far field (Fraunhofer), r > 2D²/λ with r ≫ D and r ≫ λ: the pattern no longer changes with distance, E ⊥ H ⊥ r, E/H = 377 Ω and S ∝ 1/r².
  4. Derivation of 2D²/λ. For a point on the axis at distance r from an aperture of size D, the extra path from the rim is
    Δ = √(r² + (D/2)²) − r ≈ D²/(8r)        (r ≫ D)
phase error = 2πΔ/λ ≤ π/8   →   D²/(8r) ≤ λ/16   →   r ≥ 2D²/λ
  5. For an electrically small antenna 2D²/λ is shorter than a wavelength and meaningless; then the wavelength rules: the reactive zone ends near λ/2π and the induction term falls to a tenth of the radiation term by about 1.6λ.
  6. Worked illustration, DTH dish D = 0.6 m at 12 GHz (λ = 0.025 m):
    BoundaryWorkingDistance
    Reactive edge0.62√(0.216/0.025) = 0.62 × 2.9391.82 m
    Far field2 × 0.36/0.02528.8 m

The partial cancellation broadens the measured main beam and lowers its peak, fills in the nulls, and raises and smears the side lobes, so the gain is underestimated and the side-lobe level looks worse than it is. Inside the reactive region the probe also couples to the stored field and loads the antenna, changing its input impedance.
  8. Wave impedance as a marker of the regions (Hertzian dipole): |Eθ/Hφ| ≈ 3730 Ω at βr = 0.1, 267 Ω at βr = 1 and 373 Ω at βr = 10, settling to 377 Ω only well into the far field.
  9. Remedies when the far-field distance is too long to build: a compact range, where a large reflector turns the spherical wave of a feed into a plane wave over a few metres, or a near-field scanner, which measures amplitude and phase on a surface in the radiating near field and computes the far-field pattern by a Fourier transform. Both are standard for dishes and arrays whose 2D²/λ runs to hundreds of metres.
  10. Pattern in the Fresnel region of a uniformly lit aperture: the phase error grows towards the rim, so the edge contributions add slightly out of step. The main beam “defocuses”: for a uniformly lit circular aperture the on-axis gain falls by about 0.06 dB at 2D²/λ and about 0.9 dB at a quarter of that distance, and the first nulls fill in.
  11. Small antennas follow the wavelength instead. For a 15 cm whip at 100 MHz (λ = 3 m), 2D²/λ = 0.015 m is meaningless; the reactive zone extends to about λ/2π = 0.48 m and the far field starts at about 1.6λ = 4.8 m. A phone at the ear is inside the reactive zone of its antenna, which is why its exposure is assessed by measured SAR, not from far-field gain.
  12. Check: at exactly 28.8 m the path difference is 0.36/230.4 = 1.5625 mm = λ/16, the π/8 limit.

Glossary

Antenna
A structure that turns a guided wave on a line into a free-space wave, or the reverse.
Aperture efficiency
The effective area of a horn or dish divided by its physical area, typically 0.5 to 0.7 for a dish.
Axial ratio
Major over minor axis of the polarization ellipse: 1 for circular, infinite for linear.
Balun
A device that joins a balanced antenna such as a dipole to an unbalanced line such as coaxial cable.
Beam solid angle
ΩA = ∯ Pn dΩ, the solid angle the power would fill at peak intensity; D = 4π/ΩA.
dBi and dBd
Gain in decibels relative to an isotropic radiator and to a half-wave dipole; dBd = dBi − 2.15.
Effective length
The length leff for which Voc = E leff; λ/π for a half-wave dipole.
EIRP
Effective isotropic radiated power, PtGt (less feeder loss), the transmitter’s strength in its best direction.
Fade margin
The decibels a link budget keeps above the sensitivity to survive fading, rain and misalignment.
Fraunhofer region
The far field, beyond 2D²/λ, where the pattern is fixed and the wave is locally plane.
Free-space path loss
(4πd/λ)², the spreading term of the Friis equation; 32.44 + 20 log₁₀ fMHz + 20 log₁₀ dkm in dB.
Fresnel region
The radiating near field, between the reactive region and 2D²/λ, where the pattern still changes with distance.
Front-to-back ratio
The main-lobe peak divided by the radiation in the opposite direction, usually in dB.
Hertzian dipole
A current element much shorter than a wavelength with the same current along its length.
Image theory
Replacing a perfect ground plane by a mirror image of the source, used to analyse the monopole.
Isotropic radiator
An imaginary antenna radiating equally in all directions; directivity 1 (0 dBi).
Loss resistance
The series resistance RL that accounts for heat in the conductors, dielectric and coils of an antenna.
Magnetic dipole moment
m = NIA for an N-turn loop of area A carrying current I, directed along the loop axis.
Main lobe
The lobe of a pattern that contains the direction of maximum radiation.
Minor lobe
Any lobe other than the main lobe; side lobes lie beside it and the back lobe opposite it.
Polarization loss factor
The fraction of power a receiving antenna takes from a wave of another polarization, |ρ̂w · ρ̂a|².
Power density
Average power crossing unit area, S = |E|²/(2η) in the far field, in W/m².
Radiation efficiency
ηrad = Rr/(Rr + RL), the share of the accepted power that is radiated.
Radiation intensity
Power radiated per unit solid angle, U = r²S, in W/sr.
Reactive near field
The region within about 0.62√(D³/λ) of a large antenna (λ/2π of a small one) where stored energy dominates.
Receiver sensitivity
The weakest signal, in dBm, at which a receiver still meets its specified error rate.
Retarded potential
A potential built from each source current as it was R/c seconds earlier; in phasor form, the factor e−jβR.
Return loss
−20 log₁₀|Γ| in dB: how far the reflected power is below the incident power at the antenna terminals; larger is better.
VSWR
(1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|), the voltage standing wave ratio on the feed line; 1 for a perfect match.
