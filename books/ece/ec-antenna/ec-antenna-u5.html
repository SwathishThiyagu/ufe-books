5.1 RF passive components: inductors, capacitors, varactors

An RF passive part stores or converts energy without gain: an inductor stores magnetic energy, a capacitor stores electric energy, and a varactor is a capacitor tuned by voltage. At radio frequency the same part also has resistance, stray capacitance and stray inductance. These parasitics set loss, matching and tuning range. Every receiver front end, filter and voltage-controlled oscillator uses them. The quality factor Q is 2π times stored energy divided by energy lost per cycle; high Q means low loss and narrow resonance.

You need: Unit 1 for microstrip and transmission-line parasitics, and Unit 4 for microwave integrated circuits and MMIC passives.

On-chip inductors

An on-chip inductor is usually a spiral of metal. The spiral creates magnetic flux and gives inductance L in henry. The same metal has series resistance Rs in ohm. The turns also have capacitance to each other and to the substrate; call it shunt capacitance Cp in farad. At low frequency the part looks like L in series with Rs. At higher frequency Cp takes current, so the impedance is no longer a pure inductor.

fSR = 1 / (2π√(L Cp))

fSR is self-resonant frequency in Hz; L is inductance in H; Cp is shunt capacitance in F.

Below fSR the part is inductive; above it the part is capacitive. The quality factor at a frequency where Cp is small is:

Q = ωL / Rs

ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s; L is inductance in H; Rs is series resistance in Ω.

Skin effect pushes current to the metal surface as f rises, so Rs grows roughly with √f. Eddy currents in the substrate add more loss. A typical on-chip spiral has Q in the single digits to low tens and takes much chip area. The design trade is simple: wider metal lowers Rs but raises Cp and lowers fSR.

MIM and MOS capacitors

An RF capacitor is two conductors separated by an insulator. A MIM capacitor uses two metal layers with a thin insulator between them. A MOS capacitor uses the gate oxide of a transistor. MIM capacitors are linear and have low series resistance, so they suit filters and matching networks. MOS capacitors are free in a CMOS process but their capacitance changes with gate voltage.

Both have series resistance Rs in the plates and vias. They also have series inductance Ls from the connecting metal. At low frequency the quality factor is:

Q = 1 / (ω C Rs)

C is capacitance in F; Rs is series resistance in Ω; ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s.

Series Ls causes a self-resonance, just as shunt Cp does for an inductor. Above that frequency the capacitor looks inductive. A MIM capacitor usually has higher Q and tighter tolerance; a MOS capacitor saves area and can be tuned. Layout decides which parasitics dominate: short, wide connections lower Rs and Ls but add capacitance to nearby metal. MMIC passives are covered in Unit 4.

Varactors

A varactor is a voltage-variable capacitor. Some syllabi call it a voltage-variable capacitor. A diode varactor is a reverse-biased p-n junction. Reverse bias widens the depletion region, so capacitance falls. A MOS varactor uses the gate capacitance of a MOS device, which also changes with gate voltage. Write the capacitance as C(V). The tuning range is Cmax / Cmin, the ratio at the two bias limits. A large ratio gives a wide tuning band; a small ratio gives fine control.

Q = 1 / (ω C(V) Rs)

C(V) is the voltage-dependent capacitance in F; Rs is series resistance in Ω; ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s.

Rs is the series resistance of the diode or the gate and channel. Q falls when C is large and when Rs is large. In a voltage-controlled oscillator, the varactor is part of the resonant tank. Any noise on the control voltage changes C(V), which changes frequency; this becomes phase noise. The bias line therefore needs filtering and a clean reference. Diode varactors often give a wide range; MOS varactors suit CMOS integration. The tuning range, Q and control-line noise trade against each other. See oscillators and phase noise.

Worked example

Question. A 2 nH on-chip spiral inductor at 2 GHz has series resistance 4 Ω and shunt capacitance 1 pF. Find its quality factor Q, its equivalent parallel resistance R_p using R_p ≈ Q² R_s, and its self-resonant frequency f_SR. State whether 2 GHz is below self-resonance.

  1. ω = 2πf = 2π × 2×10⁹ = 1.257×10¹⁰ rad/s.
  2. Q = ωL/R_s = (1.257×10¹⁰ × 2×10⁻⁹)/4 = 25.13/4 = 6.283.
  3. R_p ≈ Q² R_s = (6.283)² × 4 = 39.48 × 4 = 157.9 Ω.
  4. L C_p = 2×10⁻⁹ × 1×10⁻¹² = 2×10⁻²¹ H·F. √(2×10⁻²¹) = 4.472×10⁻¹¹ s. 2π × 4.472×10⁻¹¹ = 2.810×10⁻¹⁰ s. f_SR = 1 / (2.810×10⁻¹⁰) = 3.56×10⁹ Hz = 3.56 GHz.
  5. 2×10⁹ Hz < 3.56×10⁹ Hz, so 2 GHz is below self-resonance.

Answer. Q = 6.28, R_p ≈ 157.9 Ω, and f_SR ≈ 3.56 GHz; 2 GHz is below self-resonance.

Exam traps

  • Using C = 1/(2πfX) for a real capacitor above self-resonance; the part may be inductive.
  • Quoting one Q over a wide band; Q depends on frequency through R_s, skin effect and C(V).
  • Swapping the Q formulas: inductor Q = ωL/R_s, capacitor Q = 1/(ω C R_s).
  • Ignoring varactor bias-line noise; a clean DC control is part of the RF design.
  • Confusing tuning range C_max/C_min with Q; a wide range can still have low Q.

5.2 Noise figure, cascaded noise and sensitivity

Every receiver adds its own noise. A weak signal enters with a signal-to-noise ratio and leaves with a worse one. Noise figure says how much worse. The cascade formula shows that the first amplifier decides almost everything, so a receiver is built front to back around it. Sensitivity is the smallest input signal that still leaves the demodulator the signal-to-noise ratio it needs. Every handset, Wi-Fi router and satellite receiver is specified with these numbers, and they close the link budget from Unit 2.

You need: dB, dBm and the Friis link budget from Unit 2, plus the thermal noise power kTB; the loss of real passive parts is in 5.1.

Noise factor, noise figure and noise temperature

A resistor at temperature T delivers available noise power kTB over a bandwidth B. Here k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann's constant and T is in kelvin. At the standard reference temperature T₀ = 290 K the noise density kT₀ is 4.0×10⁻²¹ W/Hz, which is −174 dBm in 1 Hz.

A two-port block — amplifier, mixer or filter — receives a signal with some signal-to-noise ratio and delivers it with a lower one, because the block adds noise of its own. The noise factor compares the two ratios.

F = SNR<sub>in</sub> / SNR<sub>out</sub> = (S<sub>in</sub>/N<sub>in</sub>) ÷ (S<sub>out</sub>/N<sub>out</sub>)

SNRin and SNRout are input and output signal-to-noise ratios as power ratios, measured in the same bandwidth with the source at T₀ = 290 K; F is the noise factor, a plain ratio, ≥ 1 for any real block.

Noise figure is the same quantity in decibels. A block with F = 2 has NF = 3.01 dB. Because F ≥ 1, NF is never negative for a block at room temperature.

NF = 10 log₁₀ F    and    F = 10<sup>NF/10</sup>

NF is the noise figure in dB; F is the linear noise factor.

Noise temperature says the same thing in kelvin. An amplifier with F = 1.585 has Te = (1.585 − 1) × 290 = 170 K. Sky noise and antenna noise are usually quoted this way.

Te = (F − 1) T₀ and F = 1 + Te/T₀

Te is the equivalent noise temperature in kelvin; T₀ = 290 K is the reference; F is the linear noise factor.

  • dB is 10 log₁₀ of a power ratio; for voltages across equal resistance, 20 log₁₀ gives the same dB.
  • dBm is power relative to 1 mW: P(dBm) = 10 log₁₀(P / 1 mW); 0 dBm = 1 mW and −174 dBm = 4×10⁻²¹ W.
  • Linear power gain from dB: G = 10^(GdB/10); a 15 dB amplifier has G = 31.62.

Cascaded noise: the Friis formula

F = F₁ + (F₂ − 1)/G₁ + (F₃ − 1)/(G₁G₂) + …

F is the total noise factor of the chain, linear; F₁, F₂, F₃ are the noise factors of the stages, linear; G₁ and G₂ are the available power gains of stages 1 and 2 as linear ratios, never in dB.

Read the formula as a noise budget referred to the input. Stage 1 contributes all of its own noise. Stage 2's noise is divided by G₁, because stage 1 amplifies signal and noise together. Stage 3 is divided by G₁G₂ and usually vanishes.

  1. Convert every stage's noise figure to a linear factor: F = 10^(NF/10).
  2. Convert every stage's gain to a linear power ratio: G = 10^(GdB/10).
  3. Substitute the linear numbers into the Friis formula.
  4. Convert the result back with NF = 10 log₁₀ F.

The consequence is a design rule. A first amplifier with 20 dB gain (G₁ = 100) makes every later stage 100 times less important, so the low noise amplifier goes first (see 5.6). A passive part has loss L at T₀, so F = L and G = 1/L: its noise figure equals its loss in dB.

For a lossy filter placed first, followed by an amplifier, the cascade reduces neatly to NF = (loss in dB) + NF of the amplifier. The same filter after 20 dB of gain barely changes the total.

Sensitivity and selectivity

Sensitivity is the weakest input the receiver can use. Start from the thermal noise in the channel, add the receiver's own noise, then the margin the demodulator needs.

P<sub>min</sub> = −174 dBm/Hz + 10 log₁₀ B + NF + SNR<sub>min</sub>

Pmin is the minimum usable input power in dBm; B is the channel bandwidth in Hz; NF is the receiver noise figure in dB; SNRmin is the signal-to-noise ratio the demodulator requires, in dB.

  1. −174 dBm is kT₀ in 1 Hz, the noise floor at 290 K.
  2. Adding 10 log₁₀ B widens that floor to the channel.
  3. Adding NF is the noise the receiver itself contributes.
  4. Adding SNRmin gives the input power that leaves enough margin at the detector.

Each factor of 10 in bandwidth costs 10 dB, so a 10 MHz channel sits 70 dB above a 1 Hz one. Halving the bandwidth buys 3 dB of sensitivity, which is why narrowband links reach further. Handset sensitivity is written into cellular standards, and GNSS signals arrive below −130 dBm.

  • Adjacent-channel rejection (ACR) is the attenuation in dB that the receiver gives to a signal in the next channel; the IF filter sets it.
  • Alternate-channel rejection (AACR) is the same figure for the channel two channels away, again a filter selectivity number.

Worked example

Question. An LNA has G₁ = 15 dB and F₁ = 2 dB. A mixer has G₂ = 10 dB and F₂ = 12 dB. An IF amplifier has G₃ = 20 dB and F₃ = 10 dB. Find the cascaded noise figure of the chain, and its sensitivity for a channel bandwidth B = 10 MHz when the demodulator needs SNR = 10 dB.

  1. Linear noise factors: F₁ = 10^(2/10) = 1.585, F₂ = 10^(12/10) = 15.85, F₃ = 10^(10/10) = 10.
  2. Linear gains: G₁ = 10^(15/10) = 31.62, G₂ = 10^(10/10) = 10; G₃ is not needed, it is the last stage.
  3. First term: F₁ = 1.585.
  4. Second term: (F₂ − 1)/G₁ = 14.85/31.62 = 0.4696.

  6. F = 1.585 + 0.4696 + 0.0285 = 2.083, so NF = 10 log₁₀(2.083) = 3.19 dB.
  7. Channel noise: 10 log₁₀(10 × 10⁶) = 70 dB, so N = −174 + 70 + 3.19 = −100.81 dBm.
  8. Add the required SNR: P_min = −100.81 + 10 = −90.81 dBm, about 0.83 pW.

Exam traps

  • Adding noise figures directly in dB. 2 dB followed by 12 dB is not 14 dB; convert to linear factors, use Friis, convert back.
  • Putting dB gains into the Friis denominator. That G₁ must be 31.62, not 15.
  • Confusing noise factor with noise figure. F = 2.083 is a ratio; NF = 3.19 dB is the same number in decibels.

5.3 Nonlinearity: gain compression, intermodulation, IIP3, dynamic range

An RF amplifier is linear only for small signals. Push the input harder and the output compresses; apply two close tones and the amplifier creates new tones that were not at the input. Those effects set the strongest signal a receiver can handle and the weakest that survives beside it. Gain compression, intermodulation, intercept points and dynamic range turn “linear enough” into numbers on a datasheet and a specification for a phone, a base station or a radar front end.

You need: 5.2 for noise floor and sensitivity, and dB and dBm from Unit 2.

Gain compression and the 1 dB point

An amplifier's transfer curve is not a straight line. A simple model is y = a1x + a2x² + a3x³ + …, where x is the input voltage, y is the output voltage, and a1, a2 and a3 are constants. The a1 term is the wanted linear gain. Feed a sine wave of amplitude A volts and the small-signal gain becomes a1 + (3/4)a3A². Gain falls as A rises only when a3 < 0; that is the compression case used here. A positive a3 gives gain expansion at low drive. The a2 term makes second-order products and a DC shift. Amplitude-to-amplitude (AM-AM) conversion is this change of output amplitude with input amplitude, with no change of frequency.

At low power the output rises 1 dB for every 1 dB of input; on a dB plot the slope is 1. At high power the measured curve bends below that straight line. The 1 dB compression point is the input power where the measured output is 1 dB below the straight-line output. Call that input P1dB,in, in dBm. Call the measured output at that same input P1dB,out, also in dBm; this is the number datasheets quote. Because the measured output is 1 dB below the straight line, P1dB,out = P1dB,in + G − 1 dB, which gives P1dB,in = P1dB,out + 1 dB − G, where G is the small-signal gain in dB. The straight-line output at P1dB,in is P1dB,in + G, which is P1dB,out + 1 dB. Some syllabi call the compressed output P1dBc; here the 1 dB drop is stated separately to avoid confusion.

P1dB,in = P1dB,out + 1 dB − G

P1dB,in is the input 1 dB compression point in dBm; P1dB,out is the measured output at that input in dBm, already 1 dB below the straight line; G is the small-signal gain in dB. The straight-line output at P1dB,in is P1dB,out + 1 dB.

Back-off is how far a signal sits below P1dB,in. A large off-channel blocker can push an amplifier into compression and reduce gain for a weak wanted signal; that is blocking. The figure uses G = 20 dB and a measured P1dB,out = 19 dBm, so P1dB,in = 0 dBm and the straight line at that input gives 20 dBm.

Two tones, IM3 and intercept points

Two-tone test: apply two equal sine waves at f1 and f2, each with input power Pin in dBm. A nonlinear amplifier produces mixing products. The dangerous ones are third-order intermodulation (IM3) products at 2f1 − f2 and 2f2 − f1. They sit close to the wanted tones, so a filter that passes f1 and f2 also passes them.

On a dB plot the wanted output per tone has slope 1: it rises 1 dB per dB of Pin. The IM3 output has slope 3: it rises 3 dB per dB. The two straight lines meet at the third-order intercept. The input intercept IIP3 is the Pin at that meeting in dBm. The output intercept is OIP3 = IIP3 + G, also in dBm.

OIM3 = 3(Pin + G) − 2 OIP3 = 3 Pin − 2 IIP3 + G

OIM3 is the third-order intermodulation output power per product in dBm; Pin is each tone's input power in dBm; G is gain in dB; IIP3 and OIP3 are the input and output intercept points in dBm.

The intercept is extrapolated: the amplifier compresses before the lines cross. IIP3 usually lies about 10 dB above P1dB,in. IM3 relative to the wanted carrier is OIM3 − (Pin + G), quoted in dBc. A more negative IM3 level in dBc means cleaner performance; −40 dBc is 10 dB cleaner than −30 dBc.

Dynamic range, blocking and spectral mask

Dynamic range is the span between the weakest and strongest usable signal. For a two-tone test, the lower end is the input-referred noise floor N in dBm. The upper end is where the IM3 output equals the noise floor referred to the output, N + G. Write the output IM3 as OIM3 = 3Pin − 2IIP3 + G and set it equal to N + G: 3Pin − 2IIP3 + G = N + G. The gain cancels, so 3Pin − 2IIP3 = N, which gives Pin = (2IIP3 + N)/3. The spurious-free dynamic range (SFDR) is Pin − N, in dB.

SFDR = (2/3)(IIP3 − N)

SFDR is spurious-free dynamic range in dB; IIP3 is input third-order intercept in dBm; N is the input-referred noise floor in dBm.

Blocking dynamic range uses the 1 dB compression point instead: BDR = P1dB,in − N. A strong off-channel signal compresses the amplifier and desensitizes the receiver. dBc is power relative to the carrier; dBm is absolute power. A spectral mask is the maximum allowed transmitter power versus frequency offset. Compression and IM3 spread energy outside the wanted channel and can break the mask.

Receiver selectivity and transmitter linearity are set together with the architecture. See 5.4 for where filtering and gain happen, and 5.5 for the transmit mask. Mixer-specific spurs belong to 5.7; power-amplifier linearization belongs to 5.8.

Worked example

Question. An amplifier has small-signal gain G = 20 dB, OIP3 = 30 dBm and a measured output 1 dB compression point P1dB,out = 19 dBm. Two input tones are each −10 dBm. Find IIP3, the ideal output per tone, the IM3 output level in dBm and dBc, and P1dB,in. State the straight-line output at P1dB,in.

  1. IIP3 = OIP3 − G = 30 dBm − 20 dB = 10 dBm.
  2. Ideal output per tone = Pin + G = −10 dBm + 20 dB = 10 dBm.
  3. OIM3 = 3(Pin + G) − 2 OIP3 = 3 × 10 dBm − 2 × 30 dBm = 30 − 60 = −30 dBm.
  4. IM3 relative to carrier = OIM3 − ideal output per tone = −30 dBm − 10 dBm = −40 dBc.
  5. P1dB,in = P1dB,out + 1 dB − G = 19 dBm + 1 dB − 20 dB = 0 dBm.
  6. Straight-line output at P1dB,in = P1dB,in + G = 0 dBm + 20 dB = 20 dBm; the measured output there is P1dB,out = 19 dBm, the 1 dB drop.

Answer. IIP3 = 10 dBm, ideal output per tone = 10 dBm, IM3 output = −30 dBm (−40 dBc), P1dB,in = 0 dBm, and the straight-line output at compression is 20 dBm while the measured output is 19 dBm.

Exam traps

  • IIP3 is not a safe maximum input. It is an extrapolated intercept, usually about 10 dB above P1dB,in; driving to IIP3 is already deep compression.
  • Do not mix input and output intercepts. OIP3 = IIP3 + G. Using OIP3 where IIP3 belongs shifts the IM3 result by 2G.
  • The 3:1 slope applies to IM3 power versus each tone's input power in dB; the fundamental has 1:1 slope. Compare both against the same per-tone power.
  • P1dB,out is the measured output at compression, not the straight-line value. The straight-line output at P1dB,in is 1 dB above P1dB,out, and P1dB,in = P1dB,out + 1 dB − G. Watch that 1 dB.
  • dBc is relative to the carrier, dBm is absolute. SFDR needs an input-referred noise floor with IIP3, or an output noise floor with OIP3 used consistently.

Blocking is a strong off-channel signal that drives an amplifier into compression and reduces gain for the weak wanted signal. The wanted signal is not at the blocker frequency, but the gain and noise change.

Apply An amplifier has OIP3 = 35 dBm and G = 25 dB. Two tones are each −15 dBm. Find IIP3, the ideal output per tone, and the IM3 output. 4 marks

IIP3 = OIP3 − G = 35 − 25 = 10 dBm. Output per tone = −15 + 25 = 10 dBm. OIM3 = 3(10) − 2(35) = 30 − 70 = −40 dBm.

Analyze Why is SFDR not simply P1dB,in − noise floor? 4 marks

P1dB,in describes single-tone compression. SFDR describes the two-tone case where IM3 falls to the noise floor. IM3 grows three times faster than the fundamental, so the IM3 limit is reached before compression; SFDR is smaller and uses IIP3, not P1dB,in.

5.4 Receiver architectures: superheterodyne, direct conversion, image reject

A receiver must pick one weak signal, amplify it, and hand a clean baseband copy to the demodulator. It cannot filter sharply at 900 MHz or 2.4 GHz, so it changes frequency to where good filters live. The superheterodyne receiver does this with a mixer and an intermediate frequency (IF). Direct conversion goes straight to zero frequency. Image-reject mixers cancel the unwanted mirror channel without a sharp RF filter. Every phone, Wi-Fi card and TV tuner uses one of these plans.

You need: 5.2 for noise figure and sensitivity, 5.3 for blockers and dynamic range, and 5.7 for mixer operation.

Superheterodyne: translate first, filter later

An antenna delivers many signals at once. The RF filter is a band-pass filter at the radio frequency (RF); it passes the wanted band and attenuates the image. The low-noise amplifier (LNA) boosts the signal with little added noise. The mixer multiplies the RF signal by the local oscillator (LO) and produces sum and difference frequencies. The difference is the intermediate frequency (IF). The IF filter selects one channel because a sharp filter at 45 MHz or 10.7 MHz is cheaper and more stable than a sharp filter at 900 MHz. The IF amplifier raises the level and the demodulator recovers the message.

f<sub>IF</sub> = |f<sub>RF</sub> − f<sub>LO</sub>|

fRF is the wanted radio frequency, fLO is the local oscillator frequency, and fIF is the intermediate frequency; all in Hz.

The mixer also responds to a second frequency, the image, that gives the same IF. For a high-side LO, fLO > fRF and fLO = fRF + fIF. The image is fimage = fLO + fIF = fRF + 2fIF. For a low-side LO, fLO < fRF and fimage = fRF − 2fIF. The image sits 2fIF away from the wanted signal.

f<sub>image</sub> = f<sub>RF</sub> + 2f<sub>IF</sub> (high-side LO); f<sub>image</sub> = f<sub>RF</sub> − 2f<sub>IF</sub> (low-side LO)

fimage is the image frequency; the sign follows the LO side.

The RF filter must reject the image before the mixer. If it does not, the image overlaps the wanted channel at IF and cannot be removed later.

Direct conversion (zero-IF)

A direct-conversion receiver sets fLO = fRF, so fIF = 0. The mixer output is baseband. A low-pass filter selects the channel and the analog-to-digital converter (ADC) digitizes it. Some syllabi call this zero-IF. There is no image at a non-zero IF, because the wanted signal is at DC.

Four problems dominate. First, LO leakage radiates from the mixer or antenna and mixes with itself, producing a DC offset. Second, second-order distortion rectifies strong blockers to DC, adding another offset. Third, flicker noise (1/f noise) is large near DC and raises the noise floor for low baseband frequencies. Fourth, the I and Q paths have gain and phase mismatch, which leaves a residual image and increases error vector magnitude (EVM). DC offset can saturate the baseband amplifier, so zero-IF radios use calibration, AC coupling or offset cancellation.

Image-reject mixing: Hartley and Weaver

An image-reject mixer cancels the image by using two mixing paths instead of a sharp RF filter. In the Hartley architecture, the RF input splits into two mixers. The LO drives one mixer at 0° and the other at 90°. Each mixer output passes through a low-pass filter. One path then gets a 90° phase shift, and the two paths are summed. The wanted signal adds in phase; the image adds in antiphase and cancels.

The Weaver architecture uses two quadrature mixing stages. The first stage translates to a low IF. The second stage translates to baseband with another pair of quadrature mixers and low-pass filters. Weaver avoids the wideband 90° phase shifter that Hartley needs, but it needs two well-matched LO paths. Only a few universities teach Weaver detail.

The image-reject ratio (IRR) is the ratio of wanted-signal power to image-signal power at the output. A perfect Hartley or Weaver mixer has infinite IRR. Real gain mismatch and phase mismatch leave a residual image. IRR of 30 dB to 40 dB is common; digital calibration can improve it. The mixer core, conversion gain and spurs belong to 5.7.

Worked example

Question. A superheterodyne receiver receives a wanted signal at 900 MHz with an IF of 45 MHz and a high-side LO. (a) Find the LO frequency. (b) Find the image frequency. (c) A −20 dBm image blocker meets an RF filter that gives 30 dB rejection at the image, while the wanted signal is −80 dBm. Find the blocker power after the filter and compare it with the wanted signal.

  1. High-side LO means fLO is above fRF: fLO = fRF + fIF = 900 MHz + 45 MHz = 945 MHz.
  2. The image is on the other side of the LO: fimage = fLO + fIF = 945 MHz + 45 MHz = 990 MHz. Equivalently fimage = fRF + 2fIF = 900 + 90 = 990 MHz.
  3. RF filter rejection subtracts in dB: Pblocker,after = −20 dBm − 30 dB = −50 dBm.
  4. Wanted signal is −80 dBm. The blocker is −50 − (−80) = 30 dB stronger than the wanted signal after the filter.

Answer. LO = 945 MHz, image = 990 MHz, blocker after the RF filter = −50 dBm, still 30 dB above the −80 dBm wanted signal.

Exam traps

  • Using fLO − fIF for the image when the LO is high side. For a high-side LO the image is at fLO + fIF, which is fRF + 2fIF. A low-side LO puts it at fRF − 2fIF.
  • Saying the image spacing is fIF. The spacing is 2fIF.
  • Confusing DC offset with image in a direct-conversion receiver. The DC offset comes from LO self-mixing and second-order distortion, not from a non-zero IF image.
  • Assuming Hartley or Weaver cancels the image perfectly. Gain and phase mismatch leave a residual image, so IRR is finite.
  • Placing the IF filter before the mixer. A fixed IF filter cannot select a channel until after frequency translation.

5.5 Transmitter architectures and transceivers

A transmitter turns digital baseband samples into an RF signal at the antenna with enough power, in the correct band, and without spreading into neighbouring channels. It mirrors the receiver: filters, mixers and amplifiers return, but power and spectral mask now dominate. The same local oscillator and duplexer are often shared with the receiver, so transmit leakage and loss affect both sides. Direct upconversion is common in phones and Wi-Fi. Two-step upconversion survives where sharper filtering of spurs is needed. This chapter builds the chain and its numbers.

You need: receiver chain (5.4), mixer up/down conversion (5.7), and antenna gain/EIRP (Unit 2).

Direct upconversion chain

Digital baseband produces two streams, I and Q. The DAC converts each stream to an analog voltage. A reconstruction filter after the DAC removes sampling images. The I/Q modulator multiplies I by cos(2πf_LO t) and Q by sin(2πf_LO t), then adds them. Here f_LO is the local-oscillator frequency and t is time. With ideal gain and 90° phase, the two paths cancel the unwanted sideband and place the signal at f_LO. The driver amplifier raises the level to the PA input. The PA sets output power. A harmonic filter after the PA suppresses 2f_LO, 3f_LO and higher components. A duplexer, or a TDD switch, connects the PA to the antenna while protecting the receiver. The antenna radiates the RF wave.

Two-step upconversion uses two mixers: baseband to IF with f_IF, an IF filter, then IF to RF with f_LO2. It costs more parts but gives sharper filtering of spurs and images. Direct upconversion avoids the IF filter and is smaller; it needs good I/Q matching.

EIRP, spectral mask and ACLR

The transmitter must radiate inside its assigned channel and stay quiet outside. A spectral mask is a regulatory limit on power versus frequency offset from the carrier. It is an absolute curve, not a ratio. ACLR stands for adjacent-channel leakage ratio. Some syllabi call the same quantity ACPR, adjacent-channel power ratio. ACLR is the ratio of power in the wanted channel to power in the adjacent channel, in dBc. A good ACLR is a large number, for example 45 dBc, meaning the adjacent channel is 45 dB below the wanted one. Nonlinearity in the PA broadens the spectrum and raises adjacent power. I/Q gain or phase error also creates an image and worsens the mask.

EIRP_dBm = P_PA,dBm − L_feed,dB + G_ant,dBi

EIRP is equivalent isotropic radiated power; P_PA is the power at the output of the power amplifier, before the harmonic filter and duplexer; L_feed is the loss of the harmonic filter, duplexer or switch and any cable between the PA and the antenna; G_ant is the antenna gain in dBi relative to an isotropic radiator. Count L_feed once, on the transmit path. If you already know the power at the antenna port, write EIRP_dBm = P_ant,dBm + G_ant,dBi and add no loss term.

Shared hardware: LO, duplexer and TDD switch

A transceiver shares hardware. One synthesizer can feed the RX and TX mixers. Sharing saves area and power, but the TX LO can leak into the RX mixer, and a strong TX signal can couple into the LNA. The duplexer provides isolation, yet its loss appears in both directions. On transmit, duplexer loss reduces EIRP by the same number of dB. On receive, the same loss adds directly to the receiver noise figure and reduces sensitivity. Do not count it once for both. In FDD, the duplexer has a transmit band filter and a receive band filter; its loss is typically 1–3 dB in a handset. In TDD, a switch replaces the duplexer. The switch has insertion loss and finite isolation; it must disconnect the PA before the LNA is connected. A guard period covers switching time. Poor isolation lets the PA burn the LNA or desensitize it. The shared LO also needs good phase noise, because phase noise on TX spreads the spectrum and on RX transfers to the downconverted signal.

Worked example

Question. A direct-conversion transmitter delivers 0 dBm at the I/Q modulator output. The PA has 25 dB gain and an output 1-dB compression point P1dB,out = 26 dBm. The harmonic filter and duplexer together have 2 dB loss. The antenna gain is 3 dBi. Find the PA output, the power at the antenna port, the EIRP and the PA back-off from P1dB,out.

  1. Convert the modulator output to dBm: P_mod = 0 dBm.
  2. Add PA gain: P_PA = P_mod + G_PA = 0 dBm + 25 dB = 25 dBm. This is the PA output.
  3. Subtract filter and duplexer loss: P_ant = P_PA − L_feed = 25 dBm − 2 dB = 23 dBm at the antenna port.
  4. Add antenna gain: EIRP = P_ant + G_ant = 23 dBm + 3 dBi = 26 dBm. The same result comes from P_PA − L_feed + G_ant.
  5. Compare with P1dB,out: back-off = P1dB,out − P_PA = 26 dBm − 25 dBm = 1 dB. The PA is 1 dB below compression, so compression is small but not zero.

Answer. EIRP = 26 dBm, with PA output 25 dBm, antenna-port power 23 dBm and 1 dB back-off from P1dB,out.

Exam traps

  • Do not multiply power in dBm by a gain in dB. Gains in dB add to power in dBm: 0 dBm + 25 dB = 25 dBm. Writing 0 dBm × 25 dB is meaningless; convert to mW first if you must multiply (1 mW × 316 = 316 mW = 25 dBm).
  • Duplexer loss is a loss on the TX path and again on the RX path. Count each path separately; never subtract it twice on one path.
  • EIRP uses antenna gain but not receiver gain. A 3 dBi antenna adds 3 dB to EIRP; an LNA gain belongs to the receive chain.
  • ACLR is a ratio in dBc; a larger dBc number means less adjacent leakage. A spectral mask is an absolute limit in dBm or dBm/Hz at a frequency offset.
  • P1dB,out is an output power, not a gain. Back-off is P1dB,out minus the actual PA output in dBm; the result is in dB.

5.6 Low noise amplifiers

A low noise amplifier (LNA) is the first active block after the antenna in most receivers. It must add as little noise as possible, provide enough gain to suppress later stages, and remain linear and stable. Its input is a compromise: minimum noise figure needs a noise match, while minimum reflection needs a power match. The LNA sets the receiver's sensitivity and much of its dynamic range. You meet it in phones, Wi-Fi cards, GPS receivers and base stations; a weak signal from a distant tower must survive this first transistor.

You need: 5.2 noise figure, 5.3 nonlinearity, and Unit 1 matching.

Noise match is not power match

An LNA sits directly after the antenna or band filter. Its noise figure NF adds directly to the receiver noise figure because the cascade formula is dominated by the first block with gain (see 5.2). A 1 dB change at the LNA can move the whole receiver sensitivity by about 1 dB. Its gain also reduces the noise contribution of the mixer that follows; mixer noise belongs to 5.7.

Noise matching is different from power matching. The source reflection coefficient Γs that gives the minimum noise factor Fmin is called Γopt. F rises as Γs moves away from Γopt; the rate depends on the device's equivalent noise resistance Rn.

F = Fmin + (4Rn/Z0) · |Γs − Γopt|² / ((1 − |Γs|²)|1 + Γopt|²); NF(dB) = 10 log₁₀ F

F is the linear noise factor (a power ratio, not dB); Fmin is the minimum linear noise factor; Rn is equivalent noise resistance in ohms; Z0 is reference impedance, usually 50 Ω; Γs is source reflection coefficient; Γopt is source reflection for Fmin.

Power match sets Γs = Γin* for maximum power transfer. In a common-source MOSFET the two Γ values differ. A network that minimizes NF can give input return loss of only 6–10 dB. Designers trade NF against VSWR, and some add feedback to bring noise and power matches closer.

Source degeneration gives a real input resistance

The common narrowband LNA uses a common-source MOSFET with a source inductor Ls and a gate inductor Lg. Its input impedance is:

Zin ≈ jω(Lg + Ls) + 1/(jωCgs) + (gm/Cgs)Ls

gm is the transistor transconductance in siemens; Cgs is the gate-source capacitance in farads; Lg and Ls are inductances in henries; ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s.

At resonance ω0 = 1/√((Lg + Ls)Cgs), the imaginary part cancels and the real part is Rin = (gm/Cgs)Ls = ωT Ls, where ωT = gm/Cgs is the angular unity-current-gain (transit) frequency in rad/s; the datasheet value is fT = ωT/2π = gm/(2πCgs) in Hz. Choose Ls so that Rin = ωT Ls = 50 Ω, then choose Lg so that ω0 = 1/√((Lg + Ls)Cgs) equals the channel frequency. No physical resistor is added, so the input resistance is quiet.

A cascode adds a common-gate transistor above the common-source device. It reduces Miller feedback from output to input, raises output resistance, and improves reverse isolation and stability. The cascode transistor carries the same current, and its own noise, referred to the input, is divided by the voltage gain of the common-source device, so it adds little to the overall noise figure. The cost is extra voltage headroom: two drain-source drops must fit under the supply. The output match is a power match to 50 Ω, often an LC network or source follower; it sets gain and return loss, not NF.

Gain, linearity, stability and bias

LNA gain G in dB converts input-referred to output-referred values: Pout,1dB = Pin,1dB + G and OIP3 = IIP3 + G. Raising gain by increasing gm or load resistance lowers Pin,1dB and IIP3, because the output still saturates at roughly the same voltage. For a receiver, use enough gain to overcome mixer noise, but not so much that strong nearby signals compress the LNA.

Check stability with S-parameters: the Rollett factor k > 1 and |Δ| < 1 give unconditional stability. Cascode reverse isolation helps. Bias the MOSFET at the current density for NFmin, often moderate inversion. A BJT needs its optimum collector current; a HEMT needs a low-noise gate bias.

Question. An LNA has gain 12 dB, NF = 1.5 dB, bandwidth 10 MHz, input P1dB = −10 dBm and IIP3 = 0 dBm. Find output P1dB, OIP3 and sensitivity for SNRmin = 10 dB.

  1. Gain G = 12 dB.
  2. Output P1dB = input P1dB + G = −10 dBm + 12 dB = 2 dBm.

5.7 Mixers: up-conversion and down-conversion

A mixer is a three-port circuit that multiplies two signals. The radio-frequency (RF) port carries the signal, the local-oscillator (LO) port carries a strong sine wave, and the intermediate-frequency (IF) port carries the shifted result. Multiplication creates sum and difference frequencies, so a mixer translates a signal from one band to another. Receivers use down-conversion to a fixed IF; transmitters use up-conversion to the radio band. Its gain, noise, isolation, linearity and spurious products decide whether the translation is clean.

You need: receiver architecture from 5.4, and IIP3 and compression from 5.3.

Three ports and frequency translation

A mixer is a multiplier. Let f_RF be the radio frequency at the RF port, f_LO the local-oscillator frequency, and f_IF the intermediate frequency at the IF port. The product contains f_LO + f_RF and |f_LO − f_RF|. A filter after the mixer keeps the wanted term. Some syllabi call a mixer a frequency translator because it moves a signal without changing its modulation.

f_IF = |f_RF − f_LO| and f_RF = f_LO ± f_IF

f_IF is the selected difference in Hz for down-conversion; f_RF is the radio frequency; f_LO is the local-oscillator frequency; ± means the sum or difference kept by the output filter. For up-conversion, the IF input and LO produce f_LO ± f_IF.

  1. Down-conversion: multiply RF and LO, then filter the difference f_IF = |f_RF − f_LO| for a fixed IF.
  2. Up-conversion: multiply IF and LO, then filter the sum f_RF = f_LO + f_IF, or the difference if the design uses it.
  3. Check the image: the other input frequency that gives the same f_IF must be rejected before the mixer or by an image-reject mixer.

Conversion gain, noise and large-signal limits

Conversion gain is the IF power divided by the RF power: G_c = P_IF/P_RF. In decibels, G_c,dB = P_IF,dBm − P_RF,dBm. A negative value is conversion loss. Passive diode mixers have loss, need a strong LO, and are linear. Active transistor or Gilbert-cell mixers give gain, need less LO drive, but add noise and have finite output compression.

G_c = P_IF / P_RF;  G_c,dB = P_IF,dBm − P_RF,dBm

G_c is conversion gain as a ratio; P_IF is IF power in W; P_RF is RF power in W; dBm is power relative to 1 mW; dB is a ratio.

Noise figure is specified as single-sideband (SSB) or double-sideband (DSB). DSB counts noise from both sidebands; SSB counts one sideband. With no image rejection, the SSB value is often about 3 dB higher than DSB. Use the number that matches the image filtering in your receiver.

Isolation is leakage between ports: LO-to-RF, LO-to-IF and RF-to-IF. Poor isolation lets the LO appear at the RF input or at the IF output. The 1 dB compression point and IIP3 set the largest signal; they follow the same definitions as 5.3. LO drive must be high enough for the mixer to switch, but not so high that it damages the device.

High-side and low-side LO; image and leakage

The LO can sit above or below the RF. For down-conversion with a high-side LO, f_LO = f_RF + f_IF. With a low-side LO, f_LO = f_RF − f_IF. High-side LO puts the image above the LO; low-side LO puts it below. The image frequency is f_img = f_LO + f_IF for the high-side case and f_img = f_LO − f_IF for the low-side case.

The RF filter before the mixer must pass f_RF and reject f_img. If it does not, the image also converts to the same IF and adds interference. After multiplication, spurious products satisfy m f_LO ± n f_RF = f_IF for integers m and n; keep them out with filters and linear mixer design. LO leakage and RF feedthrough are separate from the image: they are direct leaks, not translated signals.

For up-conversion, the IF and LO create f_LO + f_IF and f_LO − f_IF. The output filter keeps one and rejects the other, called the unwanted sideband. A single-sideband up-converter suppresses one sideband inside the mixer; a double-sideband up-converter needs an output filter.

Worked example

Question. A down-converter receives RF = 2.45 GHz and uses IF = 100 MHz. It uses a high-side LO. (a) Find the LO and image frequencies. (b) The conversion gain is −6 dB. Find the IF output for an RF input of −20 dBm.

  1. Convert IF to the same unit: 100 MHz = 0.100 GHz.
  2. High-side LO: f_LO = f_RF + f_IF = 2.45 + 0.100 = 2.55 GHz.
  3. Image for high-side LO: f_img = f_LO + f_IF = 2.55 + 0.100 = 2.65 GHz.
  4. Conversion gain is −6 dB, so IF output = RF input + gain = −20 dBm + (−6 dB) = −26 dBm.

Answer. LO = 2.55 GHz, image = 2.65 GHz, and IF output = −26 dBm.

Exam traps

  • Using f_LO = f_RF − f_IF for a high-side LO, which gives the wrong LO and image.
  • Forgetting that the mixer makes both sum and difference; the unwanted product or image must be filtered.
  • Writing conversion loss as a positive gain, or adding dB to dBm and changing the unit.
  • Confusing isolation with image rejection; LO leakage at f_LO is not the image at f_img.
  • Using DSB noise figure when the receiver filters the image and needs SSB noise figure.

5.8 RF power amplifiers: classes, efficiency, linearization

An RF power amplifier is the last active block before the antenna. It must deliver watts, not microwatts, while staying linear enough for the modulation. The design problem is a trade: the most linear classes waste DC power as heat, and the most efficient classes distort the signal and splatter energy into neighbouring channels. Class choice, load matching, back-off and linearization set output power, battery life and spectral mask. Handsets, Wi-Fi routers, cell towers and radar transmitters all depend on this trade.

You need: 5.3 for compression and IM3, and 5.5 for the transmitter chain.

Conduction angle, classes and efficiency

The transistor is biased at a DC operating point. The conduction angle is the part of one RF cycle, measured in degrees, during which drain current flows. It sets the class. Class A conducts for 360°: the device is always on, quiescent current is large, and the waveform stays close to a scaled copy of the input. Class B conducts for 180°; two devices in push-pull cover the full cycle. Class AB sits between 180° and 360°. Class C conducts for less than 180°, so it is nonlinear but efficient. Classes D, E and F switch the transistor hard on and off; output filtering removes harmonics.

η<sub>D</sub> = (P<sub>RF,out</sub> / P<sub>DC</sub>) × 100%

ηD is drain efficiency in percent; PRF,out is RF output power in W; PDC is DC supply power in W.

PAE = ((P<sub>RF,out</sub> − P<sub>RF,in</sub>) / P<sub>DC</sub>) × 100%

PAE is power-added efficiency in percent; PRF,in is RF input drive power in W. PAE subtracts drive power and is always lower than drain efficiency.

Load line, matching and back-off

At maximum output the drain current swings from near zero to Imax, and in a choke-fed Class A stage the drain voltage swings from the knee voltage Vknee up to about 2VDD − Vknee. The load line is the straight line between these limits on the output I–V plane. Its slope is 1/Ropt, where Ropt is the optimum load resistance. For Class A, Ropt ≈ 2(VDD − Vknee) / Imax. The quiescent current is about IDC = Imax/2, so the same result reads Ropt ≈ (VDD − Vknee) / IDC. The matching network transforms the 50 Ω antenna or filter load to Ropt. Too low Ropt wastes current; too high Ropt clips the drain voltage and compresses the gain.

BO = 10 log<sub>10</sub>(P<sub>sat</sub> / P<sub>avg</sub>) dB

BO is back-off in dB; Psat is saturated output power in W; Pavg is average output power in W.

Back-off is how far average output sits below saturated output. A modulated signal with high peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR) must back off so peaks are not clipped. The two classes fall at different rates. Class A DC current stays fixed, so its drain efficiency follows the power ratio 10−BO/10. Class B DC current falls with signal amplitude, so its drain efficiency follows 10−BO/20. At 6 dB back-off, Class A drops from 50% to about 12.5%, while Class B drops from 78.5% only to about 39.3%. The average efficiency over a burst is therefore much lower than the peak data-sheet number.

Memory effects are slow changes in the PA response from thermal drift, bias-circuit time constants and transistor trapping. Distortion at one instant depends on earlier envelope values. Memory makes the inverse nonlinearity frequency-dependent, so a static predistorter cannot cancel it.

Linearization: predistortion, Doherty, envelope tracking

Modulated signals such as OFDM have high PAPR. A nonlinear PA creates spectral regrowth: energy appears in adjacent channels, raising adjacent-channel leakage ratio (ACLR) and breaking the spectral mask. Linearization reduces this without backing off too far.

Digital predistortion (DPD) is a baseband block before the up-converter. It computes a nonlinear function with memory that approximates the inverse of the PA. A directional coupler samples the PA output; a feedback receiver down-converts it; an adaptation engine compares it with the wanted signal and updates the DPD coefficients. The PA then looks linear up to a higher power and can run closer to saturation.

Doherty: two devices, carrier and peaking. A quarter-wave impedance inverter combines them. At low power the peaking device is off and the carrier sees a higher load, so it reaches voltage swing early and stays efficient. At peaks the peaking device turns on and shares current. Doherty gives high efficiency at 6–10 dB back-off and is common in base stations.

Envelope tracking (ET): a supply modulator makes VDD follow the RF envelope. The supply tracks the RF envelope plus a small headroom above Vknee, so VDD(t) − Vout(t) stays small and dissipation falls. ET suits handsets where battery life matters. Doherty and ET can be combined. All three methods trade complexity and cost for average efficiency.

Worked example

Question. A Class AB power amplifier has VDD = 3.3 V, average DC current IDC = 300 mA, RF output power PRF,out = 250 mW and RF input power PRF,in = 50 mW. Find the DC power, the drain efficiency and the power-added efficiency.

  1. Convert current to amperes: IDC = 300 mA = 0.300 A.
  2. DC power: PDC = VDD × IDC = 3.3 V × 0.300 A = 0.99 W.
  3. Drain efficiency: ηD = PRF,out / PDC = 0.250 W / 0.99 W = 0.2525 = 25.3%.
  4. RF input power in watts: PRF,in = 50 mW = 0.050 W.
  5. PAE = (PRF,out − PRF,in) / PDC = (0.250 W − 0.050 W) / 0.99 W = 0.2020 = 20.2%.

Answer. DC power is 0.99 W, drain efficiency is 25.3%, and PAE is 20.2%.

Exam traps

  • Do not quote peak drain efficiency for a modulated signal. At 8–10 dB back-off the average efficiency can be a small fraction of the peak; exam answers must use average power.
  • Drain efficiency and PAE are not the same. PAE subtracts RF input power; ηD does not. PAE is always lower, and the drive power can be a large part of a small PA's budget.
  • Class C maximum efficiency approaches 100% only as output power approaches zero. A real Class C stage at useful output power has lower efficiency and is strongly nonlinear.
  • Doherty is an analog load-modulation architecture, not a digital algorithm. DPD is the digital correction block. Mixing the two names loses marks.
  • Back-off in dB is a power ratio: 6 dB means Pavg is one-quarter of Psat, not one-sixth.

5.9 Oscillators, VCO, phase noise and frequency synthesizers

An oscillator makes a periodic RF signal without an input; a VCO lets a voltage tune its frequency. Phase noise measures how much of that signal's power spreads into nearby offsets, in dBc/Hz. A frequency synthesizer locks a VCO to a stable reference using a PLL, so the output has the reference's long-term accuracy and the VCO's fast tuning. These blocks supply the local oscillator for mixers in receivers and transmitters; see 5.7.

You need: the LO port of a mixer in 5.7 and noise density in 5.2.

Feedback, Barkhausen and negative resistance

An oscillator is an amplifier that feeds part of its output back to its input with the right phase. The loop starts from thermal noise. At one frequency the phase shift around the loop is 0° (or a whole number of 360° turns) and the loop gain magnitude is at least one. The signal grows; then the amplifier saturates and the gain falls to exactly one. This is the Barkhausen condition.

Aβ = 1 at ∠0°

A is the amplifier voltage gain; β is the feedback fraction; Aβ is the loop gain.

The same idea appears as a negative-resistance one-port. An LC tank has loss; an active device supplies a negative resistance that cancels it. The tank sets frequency and Q. A ring oscillator uses an odd number of inverting delay stages; each stage adds delay, and the loop oscillates when the total phase is 180° plus inversion. Ring VCOs are small in CMOS but have low Q, so their phase noise is worse. To analyse a feedback oscillator: break the loop, calculate Aβ(jω), find the frequency where phase is 0°, check |Aβ| ≥ 1 there, then confirm the steady-state amplitude by saturation or a limiting circuit.

VCO tuning, KVCO and phase noise

A voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) changes frequency with a tuning voltage: f_out = f_0 + K_VCO V_tune. f_0 is the centre frequency in Hz, K_VCO is the tuning sensitivity in Hz/V, and V_tune is the control voltage. The tuning range is f_min to f_max. K_VCO is not perfectly constant because a varactor is nonlinear; see 5.1. A large K_VCO gives wide tuning but also turns small control noise into frequency modulation.

Phase noise is the single-sideband noise power in a 1 Hz bandwidth at offset Δf from the carrier, divided by the carrier power. It is written L(Δf) and measured in dBc/Hz: dB relative to the carrier, per hertz. The Leeson model gives the shape.

L(Δf) = 10 log₁₀[(2FkT/P_s)(1 + f_0²/(4Q²Δf²))(1 + f_c/Δf)]

F is the device noise factor; k is Boltzmann's constant (1.38×10⁻²³ J/K); T is absolute temperature in K; P_s is the oscillator signal power in W; Q is the loaded tank quality factor; f_c is the flicker corner frequency; Δf is the offset from the carrier in Hz.

Far from the carrier the term in f_0²/Δf² gives a 1/f² slope: 20 dB per decade. Close in, flicker adds a 1/f³ slope: 30 dB per decade. Higher Q and higher P_s lower the noise. The figure plots example densities at 1 kHz to 1 MHz offsets.

PLL frequency synthesizer

A phase-locked loop (PLL) makes a stable, tuneable output from a clean reference. A phase-frequency detector (PFD) compares the reference frequency f_ref with the divided VCO frequency f_vco/N. A charge pump converts the phase error into current pulses. The loop filter turns that current into the VCO control voltage and sets the loop bandwidth. The divider sets N.

In lock, the feedback forces f_vco/N = f_ref, so f_out = N f_ref. An integer-N synthesizer uses a whole-number N; its channel spacing equals f_ref. A fractional-N synthesizer uses N = N_int + K/M, where K/M is a fraction set by a modulus; this gives finer spacing without a very low reference. Only a few universities teach fractional-N detail; the cost is fractional spurs, which need a DAC or dithering to reduce.

The loop bandwidth f_L decides which source dominates. Inside f_L, the PLL copies the reference noise multiplied by N and suppresses VCO noise. Outside f_L, the output follows the free-running VCO. A wide loop gives fast lock but passes more reference noise; a narrow loop gives clean close-in output but slow lock. Lock time is roughly a few cycles of 1/f_L. Spurs appear at f_ref, at fractional frequencies, and at multiples.

Worked example

Question. A 2 GHz VCO has phase noise −100 dBc/Hz at 1 MHz offset. A PLL uses a 10 MHz reference and N = 200. The reference phase noise is −150 dBc/Hz at 1 MHz offset. (a) Estimate the multiplied reference phase noise at 1 MHz offset. (b) Compare it with the free-running VCO. (c) If the PLL loop bandwidth is 100 kHz, which source sets the output phase noise at 1 MHz?

  1. 1. Find N: N = f_vco / f_ref = 2×10⁹ Hz / 10×10⁶ Hz = 200.
  2. 2. Frequency multiplication by N raises phase noise by 20 log₁₀ N = 20 log₁₀ 200 = 46.02 dB.
  3. 3. Multiplied reference phase noise = −150 + 46.02 = −103.98 dBc/Hz at 1 MHz offset.
  4. 4. Compare: VCO is −100 dBc/Hz. Difference = −100 − (−103.98) = 3.98 dB; the reference path is 3.98 dB lower (better).
  5. 5. Loop bandwidth = 100 kHz. The 1 MHz offset is ten times outside the loop, so the PLL output there is set mainly by the VCO, not by the reference path.

Answer. The multiplied reference is about −103.98 dBc/Hz at 1 MHz offset, 3.98 dB below the free-running VCO; with a 100 kHz loop bandwidth the VCO dominates at 1 MHz because that offset is outside the loop.

Exam traps

  • dBc/Hz is a density at one offset, not total integrated phase noise. Integrating over the loop bandwidth is a separate step.
  • Multiplication adds 20 log₁₀N to phase noise, not 10 log₁₀N. The 20 comes from voltage; 10 would be a power ratio without squaring.
  • A PLL does not remove reference noise. Inside the loop bandwidth the reference noise appears multiplied by N; outside, the VCO dominates.
  • K_VCO is in Hz/V or MHz/V; control voltage noise in V becomes frequency deviation in Hz. Keep units consistent.
  • Integer-N channel spacing is f_ref; fractional-N can have finer spacing but produces fractional spurs. Do not call all PLLs integer-N.
