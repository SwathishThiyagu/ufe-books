5.1 RF passive components: inductors, capacitors, varactors

An RF passive part stores energy without adding gain: an inductor stores magnetic energy, a capacitor stores electric energy, and a varactor is a capacitor whose value is set by a DC voltage. At radio frequency each part also carries resistance and stray capacitance or inductance, which set its loss, its highest working frequency and its tuning range. Matching networks, filters and voltage-controlled oscillators (VCOs) are built from these parts, so their quality factor Q and self-resonance limit the rest of the unit.

You need: reactance XL = ωL and XC = 1/(ωC), series and parallel resonance, and the idea of a matching network from transmission lines and impedance matching.

Quality factor and the on-chip inductor

The quality factor of a reactive part is Q = 2π × (peak energy stored) ÷ (energy lost per cycle). For a part modelled as a reactance X in series with a resistance R, this reduces to Q = |X|/R. High Q means low loss, a sharp resonance and, in an oscillator, low phase noise.

An on-chip inductor is a flat spiral of metal. The spiral gives inductance L in henry. The metal has series resistance Rs in ohm. The turns couple to each other and to the substrate through a shunt capacitance Cp in farad. At low frequency the part is simply L in series with Rs, and its Q rises in proportion to frequency:

Q = ωL / Rs

ω = 2πf is the angular frequency in rad/s; L is in H; Rs is in Ω. This is the series-model Q, true only well below self-resonance.

As frequency rises, Cp carries more current. At the self-resonant frequency fSR the inductive and capacitive currents cancel; above it the part behaves as a capacitor.

fSR ≈ 1 / (2π√(L Cp))

fSR is in Hz. The exact value is a little lower because Rs damps the resonance.

Including Cp, the inductor's useful Q is Qeff ≈ (ωL/Rs)[1 − Rs²Cp/L − (f/fSR)²]. The bracket falls to zero at self-resonance, so Qeff rises, peaks and then collapses. Skin effect (Rs grows roughly as √f) and substrate eddy currents add loss, so real spirals reach Q of only about 5 to 20. Wider metal lowers Rs but raises Cp; a rule of thumb is to stay below about half of fSR.

MIM and MOS capacitors

A metal-insulator-metal (MIM) capacitor uses two metal layers with a thin insulator between them. It is linear, has a tight tolerance and low series resistance, so it suits filters and matching networks. A MOS capacitor uses a transistor's gate oxide: it is dense and free in a CMOS process, but its value changes with the voltage across it and its channel adds resistance.

Q = 1 / (ω C Rs)

C is the capacitance in F; Rs is the series resistance of the plates, vias and (for a MOS part) the channel, in Ω; ω = 2πf in rad/s.

Capacitor Q falls as frequency rises, the opposite of the inductor. The leads add a series inductance Ls that resonates with C; above that self-resonance the capacitor looks inductive. Microwave integrated circuits (MMICs) use the same parts.

Varactors: the voltage-tuned capacitor

A varactor is a capacitor whose value is set by a DC control voltage. A diode varactor is a reverse-biased p-n junction: more reverse bias widens the depletion region, so the capacitance falls. (A MOS varactor, used in CMOS VCOs, tunes its gate capacitance between depletion and accumulation.)

C(VR) = Cj0 / (1 + VR/φ)γ

Cj0 is the zero-bias capacitance in F; VR is the reverse bias in V; φ is the built-in potential, about 0.7 V for silicon; γ is the grading exponent: 1/2 for an abrupt junction, 1/3 for a linearly graded one, and up to about 2 for a hyperabrupt tuning diode.

The capacitance ratio Cmax/Cmin is taken between the two ends of the bias range: Cmax at the smallest reverse bias, Cmin at the largest. In an LC tank f = 1/(2π√(LC)), so the frequency tuning range is only the square root of the capacitance ratio. A small ratio gives a narrow band and a small VCO gain KVCO (Hz/V), so control-line noise moves the frequency less.

A varactor's Q = 1/(ωC(V)Rs) is lowest where C is largest. Noise on the control voltage moves C(V) and the frequency, which becomes phase noise; see oscillators and phase noise.

Worked example

Question. A 2 nH on-chip spiral inductor has series resistance Rs = 4 Ω and shunt capacitance Cp = 1 pF. At 2 GHz find (a) the series-model Q and the equivalent parallel resistance Rp ≈ Q²Rs, (b) the self-resonant frequency, and (c) the effective Q once Cp is included. Is 2 GHz a sensible operating frequency?

  1. ω = 2πf = 2π × 2 × 10⁹ = 1.257 × 10¹⁰ rad/s.
  2. XL = ωL = 1.257 × 10¹⁰ × 2 × 10⁻⁹ = 25.13 Ω, so Q = XL/Rs = 25.13/4 = 6.28.
  3. Rp ≈ Q²Rs = 6.28² × 4 = 39.48 × 4 = 157.9 Ω. This is the loss resistance the inductor puts across a tank at 2 GHz.
  4. LCp = 2 × 10⁻⁹ × 1 × 10⁻¹² = 2 × 10⁻²¹; √(LCp) = 4.472 × 10⁻¹¹ s; fSR = 1/(2π × 4.472 × 10⁻¹¹) = 3.56 GHz.
  5. f/fSR = 2/3.56 = 0.562, so (f/fSR)² = 0.316. Rs²Cp/L = 16 × 10⁻¹²/2 × 10⁻⁹ = 0.008.
  6. Qeff = 6.28 × (1 − 0.008 − 0.316) = 6.28 × 0.676 = 4.25. The impedance of the full model at 2 GHz is 8.50 + j36.1 Ω, and 36.1/8.50 = 4.25 confirms it; the apparent inductance is 36.1/ω = 2.87 nH, not 2 nH.
  7. 2 GHz is 0.56 of fSR, just above the half-fSR rule and near the Qeff peak (4.25 at about 2.05 GHz). It works, but the tank must be designed with 2.87 nH and Q = 4.25.

Answer. Series-model Q = 6.28 and Rp ≈ 158 Ω; fSR = 3.56 GHz; with Cp included Qeff = 4.25 and the inductance looks like 2.87 nH at 2 GHz.

Question. An abrupt-junction varactor (γ = 0.5, φ = 0.7 V) has Cj0 = 2 pF and series resistance 2 Ω. It tunes a tank with L = 5 nH as the reverse bias goes from 1 V to 8 V. Find Cmax, Cmin, the capacitance ratio, the frequency range and the varactor Q at each end.

Exam traps

  • Using Q = ωL/Rs close to self-resonance. In the worked example it gives 6.28 at 2 GHz while the real value is 4.25; near fSR the true Q falls towards zero.
  • Swapping the formulas: inductor Q = ωL/Rs rises with frequency; capacitor Q = 1/(ωCRs) falls with frequency.
  • Taking the frequency tuning range equal to the capacitance ratio. A C ratio of 2.26 tunes an LC tank by only √2.26 = 1.50.
  • Putting Cmax at the largest reverse bias. A reverse-biased junction has its largest capacitance at the smallest bias, because the depletion layer is thinnest.
  • Calculating a capacitor's reactance as 1/(2πfC) above its series self-resonance, where the part is inductive.

5.2 Noise figure, cascaded noise and sensitivity

Every receiver adds its own noise, so a weak signal enters with one signal-to-noise ratio and leaves with a worse one. Noise figure says how much worse, the Friis cascade formula shows that the first stage with gain decides most of it, and sensitivity turns the result into the weakest signal the receiver can use. Phones, Wi-Fi routers and satellite receivers are all specified with these three numbers, and they close the link budget that starts with the Friis transmission equation.

You need: decibels (dB and dBm) and the link budget from antenna fundamentals.

Noise factor, noise figure and noise temperature

A resistor at temperature T delivers available noise power kTB into a matched load over a bandwidth B. Here k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann's constant and T is in kelvin. At the standard reference temperature T₀ = 290 K, kT₀ = 4.00 × 10⁻²¹ W/Hz, which is −174 dBm in 1 Hz.

A two-port block (amplifier, mixer or filter) adds noise of its own, so its output signal-to-noise ratio is lower than its input ratio. The noise factor compares the two:

F = SNRin / SNRout

both ratios are power ratios in the same bandwidth, with the source at T₀ = 290 K. F is a plain number and F ≥ 1 for any real block.

Noise figure is the same quantity in decibels, NF = 10 log₁₀ F, and back again F = 10NF/10. A block with F = 2 has NF = 3.01 dB. Noise temperature says the same thing in kelvin: Te = (F − 1)T₀, so an amplifier with NF = 2 dB (F = 1.585) has Te = 0.585 × 290 = 170 K. Satellite receivers are quoted in kelvin because Te adds directly to the antenna's noise temperature TA.

A passive lossy part at T₀ (a cable, filter or switch) with loss L as a power ratio has G = 1/L and F = L. Its noise figure equals its loss in dB, and its noise temperature is (L − 1)T₀.

Cascaded noise: the Friis formula

F = F₁ + (F₂ − 1)/G₁ + (F₃ − 1)/(G₁G₂) + …

F is the noise factor of the whole chain; F₁, F₂, F₃ are the stage noise factors and G₁, G₂ the available power gains, all linear ratios, never dB.

Read it as noise referred to the input. Stage 1 counts in full. Stage 2's extra noise (F₂ − 1) is divided by G₁, because stage 1 has already amplified the signal by G₁ before stage 2 adds anything. Stage 3 is divided by G₁G₂ and is usually tiny. The same formula in kelvin is Te = Te1 + Te2/G₁ + Te3/(G₁G₂) + …

  1. Convert every noise figure to a linear factor, F = 10NF/10.
  2. Convert every gain to a linear power ratio, G = 10G(dB)/10; a loss L dB gives G = 10−L/10.
  3. Put the linear numbers into the Friis formula.
  4. Convert back with NF = 10 log₁₀ F.

The design rule follows at once: put a low-noise amplifier with real gain first. A lossy part in front of the LNA adds its whole loss to the system noise figure, because the LNA's noise is then divided by G = 1/L, which is less than one. The same part after 20 dB of gain barely shows.

Sensitivity and the receiver noise floor

Sensitivity is the weakest input that still gives the demodulator the signal-to-noise ratio it needs. Start from the thermal noise in the channel, add the receiver's own noise, then add the required SNR:

Pmin = −174 + 10 log₁₀B + NF + SNRmin

Pmin is in dBm; −174 dBm/Hz is kT₀; B is the noise bandwidth of the channel in Hz; NF is the receiver noise figure in dB; SNRmin is the demodulator's requirement in dB. The first three terms are the input-referred noise floor.

Every factor of 10 in bandwidth costs 10 dB: a 10 MHz channel's floor is −104 dBm before NF is added. With SNRmin = 0 dB the formula gives the minimum detectable signal (MDS).

When the antenna sees a sky or ground cooler than 290 K, work in kelvin instead: the system noise temperature is Tsys = TA + Te, the noise floor is kTsysB, and the sensitivity is that floor plus SNRmin. Sensitivity sets the weak end of the receiver's range; the strong end is set by compression and intermodulation (see nonlinearity).

5.3 Nonlinearity: gain compression, intermodulation, IIP3, dynamic range

An RF amplifier is linear only for small signals. Drive it harder and the output compresses; feed it two close tones and it creates new tones that were never at its input, some of them right inside the wanted band. The 1 dB compression point, the third-order intercept (IIP3) and the spurious-free dynamic range turn 'linear enough' into numbers on a datasheet. Noise sets the weakest signal a receiver can use; these numbers set the strongest signal it can survive beside it.

You need: dB and dBm, and the receiver noise floor from noise figure and sensitivity.

Gain compression and the 1 dB point

Model a memoryless amplifier as y = a₁x + a₂x² + a₃x³, where x is the input voltage, y the output voltage, a₁ the small-signal gain and a₂, a₃ constants. For an input A cos ωt the output at ω has amplitude a₁A + (3/4)a₃A³. When a₃ is negative (the usual case: the device runs out of voltage or current) the gain a₁ + (3/4)a₃A² falls as A rises. That is gain compression. A positive a₃ would give gain expansion instead. The a₂ term makes second harmonics and a DC shift.

On a dB plot of output power against input power, the small-signal line has slope 1: 1 dB more input gives 1 dB more output. The real curve bends below it. The input 1 dB compression point P1dB,in is the input power at which the measured output is 1 dB below the straight line. The output compression point P1dB,out is the measured output at that input, the number datasheets quote. So

P1dB,out = P1dB,in + G − 1 dB

all powers in dBm; G is the small-signal gain in dB. The straight line at P1dB,in reads P1dB,out + 1 dB.

Setting the gain drop to 1 dB in the cubic model gives A²1dB = 0.145|a₁/a₃|. A strong off-channel signal near P1dB,in lowers the gain for the weak wanted signal too: blocking, or desensitisation.

Two tones, IM3 and the intercept point

In a two-tone test two equal tones at f₁ and f₂, each of power Pin, enter the amplifier. The cubic term creates third-order intermodulation (IM3) products at 2f₁ − f₂ and 2f₂ − f₁. With tones at 900.0 and 900.2 MHz the IM3 products land at 899.8 and 900.4 MHz, inside any filter that passes the tones. Their amplitude is (3/4)|a₃|A³, so the IM3 output rises 3 dB for every 1 dB of input, while the wanted output rises 1 dB per dB.

Extend both straight lines: they cross at the third-order intercept point. Its input value is IIP3 and its output value is OIP3 = IIP3 + G. The crossing is extrapolated; the real amplifier compresses well before it. In the cubic model A²IIP3 = (4/3)|a₁/a₃|, so IIP3 is 10 log₁₀(1.333/0.145) = 9.6 dB above P1dB,in. Real amplifiers are not pure cubics, so measured gaps of 9 to 12 dB are common.

OIM3 = 3Pout − 2·OIP3

OIM3 is the output power of each IM3 product in dBm; Pout = Pin + G is the output per tone in dBm. Input-referred, IM3 = 3Pin − 2·IIP3.

A measurement gives IIP3 directly. If ΔP is the gap in dB between the output per tone and the output IM3 product, then ΔP = 2(IIP3 − Pin), so IIP3 = Pin + ΔP/2. IM3 is often quoted relative to the tone in dBc; −40 dBc is 10 dB cleaner than −30 dBc.

In a chain, input intercepts combine as powers in milliwatts: 1/IIP3 = 1/IIP3₁ + G₁/IIP3₂ + G₁G₂/IIP3₃ + …, with gains as linear ratios. This is the mirror image of Friis: later stages see the signal after gain, so the last stages usually limit linearity, while the first stage limits noise.

Dynamic range

The spurious-free dynamic range (SFDR) runs from the input-referred noise floor N up to the per-tone input at which the input-referred IM3 just equals N. Setting 3Pin − 2·IIP3 = N gives Pin = (2·IIP3 + N)/3, and SFDR = Pin − N:

SFDR = (2/3)(IIP3 − N)

SFDR in dB; IIP3 and N in dBm, both referred to the input; N = −174 + 10 log₁₀B + NF.

At the output, G appears on both sides of 3Pout − 2·OIP3 = N + G and cancels. Blocking dynamic range uses compression instead: BDR = P1dB,in − N. Compression and IM3 also spread a transmitter's energy outside its channel, which is how the same numbers reappear in the spectral mask of transmitter architectures.

Worked example

Question. A two-tone test applies two tones of −10 dBm each to an amplifier. The output shows 10 dBm per tone and IM3 products at −30 dBm. A single-tone sweep gives P1dB,out = 19 dBm. Find G, the IM3 level in dBc, IIP3, OIP3, P1dB,in, and how far the tones sit below compression.

  1. G = 10 − (−10) = 20 dB.
  2. Gap between tone and IM3 at the output: ΔP = 10 − (−30) = 40 dB, so IM3 = −40 dBc.
  3. IIP3 = Pin + ΔP/2 = −10 + 20 = 10 dBm; OIP3 = IIP3 + G = 30 dBm.
  4. Check with the output form: OIM3 = 3Pout − 2·OIP3 = 3 × 10 − 2 × 30 = −30 dBm, as measured.
  5. P1dB,in = P1dB,out + 1 − G = 19 + 1 − 20 = 0 dBm; the straight line there reads 20 dBm, 1 dB above the measured 19 dBm.
  6. The tones sit 10 dB below P1dB,in, and IIP3 is 10 dB above P1dB,in, close to the 9.6 dB of a pure cubic.

Answer. G = 20 dB, IM3 = −40 dBc, IIP3 = 10 dBm, OIP3 = 30 dBm, P1dB,in = 0 dBm, and the tones are backed off 10 dB from compression.

5.4 Receiver architectures: superheterodyne, direct conversion, image reject

A receiver must pick one weak channel out of everything the antenna delivers. A sharp filter that can also be tuned from channel to channel is very hard to build at 900 MHz or 2.4 GHz, so most receivers move the signal to where a fixed sharp filter can select it. The superheterodyne does this with a mixer and an intermediate frequency (IF), direct conversion goes straight to zero frequency, and image-reject receivers cancel the unwanted mirror channel by phase instead of by filtering. Every phone, Wi-Fi card and TV tuner uses one of these plans.

You need: noise figure and IIP3; the mixer itself is covered in mixers.

Superheterodyne: translate first, filter later

The chain is antenna, RF band-pass filter (preselector), low-noise amplifier (LNA), image filter, mixer with a local oscillator (LO), IF filter, IF amplifier and demodulator. The mixer's difference frequency is the IF. Changing the LO tunes the receiver while the IF filter stays fixed, so it can be a sharp SAW, crystal or ceramic filter.

fIF = |fRF − fLO|

fRF is the wanted radio frequency, fLO the local-oscillator frequency and fIF the intermediate frequency, all in Hz.

A second input frequency, the image, gives the same IF from the other side of the LO. With a high-side LO (fLO = fRF + fIF) the image is fsi = fRF + 2fIF. With a low-side LO (fLO = fRF − fIF, which needs fRF > fIF) it is fRF − 2fIF. In both cases fsi = 2fLO − fRF, and the image is 2fIF away from the wanted signal. Once converted, the image sits on the wanted channel at the IF, so the filters ahead of the mixer must remove it.

A single tuned circuit of loaded quality factor Q, tuned to fs, attenuates the image by the image rejection ratio

α = √(1 + Q²ρ²),  ρ = fsi/fs − fs/fsi

α is a voltage ratio (20 log₁₀α in dB); fs is the signal frequency and fsi the image frequency. Stages in cascade multiply their α values, so their dB values add.

This is the IF trade-off: a high IF puts the image far away but makes a sharp channel filter hard; a low IF does the reverse. Double-conversion receivers use a high first IF for image rejection and a low second IF for selectivity.

Direct conversion (zero-IF)

A direct-conversion receiver sets fLO = fRF, so the IF is zero. Two mixers driven by LO signals 90° apart give the in-phase (I) and quadrature (Q) baseband outputs; low-pass filters select the channel and analog-to-digital converters (ADCs) digitise it. Some syllabi call it a homodyne receiver. With fIF = 0 the image of each frequency is its own mirror about the carrier, inside the wanted channel, so no filter can remove it. The I/Q pair separates the two halves of the spectrum instead, and any gain or phase mismatch between I and Q leaves a residual image that raises the error vector magnitude (EVM).

With no image or IF filter it suits a single-chip radio. The price is four problems near DC: LO leakage that reflects back and self-mixes into a DC offset; second-order distortion (IIP2) that turns a strong amplitude-varying blocker into baseband; flicker (1/f) noise; and I/Q mismatch. Calibration, offset cancellation and AC coupling handle the first three. A low-IF receiver puts the channel at a few hundred kilohertz to escape DC while keeping most of the integration.

Image-reject receivers: Hartley and Weaver

In the Hartley architecture the RF signal drives two mixers with LO signals at cos ωLOt and sin ωLOt. Each output is low-pass filtered, one path is shifted by 90°, and the two paths are added. The shift is chosen so the wanted signal adds and the image subtracts; with the opposite sign of shift (or a subtraction in place of the addition) the image adds and the wanted signal cancels, so the sign of the 90° shift and of the combiner must be stated together. The Weaver architecture replaces the 90° shifter with a second pair of quadrature mixers: wider band, but a second LO and a secondary image.

The image-reject ratio (IRR) is the conversion gain for the wanted signal divided by the conversion gain for the image. With a relative gain error ε and a phase error θ between the two paths,

IRR ≈ 4 / (ε² + θ²)

θ in radians; ε as a fraction (1 % is 0.01, about 0.09 dB); IRR as a power ratio, 10 log₁₀ for dB. Valid when ε and θ are small.

Matching of 1 % and 1° gives about 40 dB, typical of an uncalibrated chip; calibration reaches 50 to 60 dB. The mixer core, conversion gain and spurious responses are in mixers.

Worked example

5.5 Transmitter architectures and transceivers

A transmitter turns digital baseband samples into an RF signal at the antenna with the right power, in the right channel, and without spilling into the neighbouring channels. It mirrors the receiver, but its limits are output power, efficiency and the spectral mask rather than noise. In a transceiver both halves share an antenna and often a synthesizer, so the strong transmit signal must be kept out of the sensitive receiver.

You need: compression and IM3 and the receiver plans of receiver architectures.

Direct and two-step up-conversion

Digital baseband produces two sample streams, I and Q. A digital-to-analog converter (DAC) turns each into a voltage, and a reconstruction low-pass filter removes the DAC's sampling images. The I/Q (quadrature) modulator forms I(t) cos ωLOt − Q(t) sin ωLOt, which places the complex baseband signal at fLO with one sideband only. A driver amplifier lifts the level, the power amplifier (PA) sets the output power, a harmonic low-pass filter removes 2fLO and 3fLO, and a duplexer or a transmit/receive switch connects the antenna.

Direct up-conversion has two weaknesses: LO feedthrough leaves a spur at fLO in mid-channel, and the large PA output at the VCO's own frequency can couple back and pull the VCO (LO pulling). Running the VCO at 2fLO and dividing by 2 cures pulling.

Two-step up-conversion first mixes baseband to a fixed IF, filters it, then mixes to RF with a second LO. The second mixer creates fLO2 + fIF and fLO2 − fIF; an RF band-pass filter keeps one and removes the other, called the unwanted sideband. With fIF = 400 MHz and fLO2 = 2.0 GHz, the wanted output is 2.4 GHz and the unwanted sideband 1.6 GHz, far enough apart to filter easily. The cost is a second synthesizer, an IF filter and more power.

Output power, EIRP, ACLR and the spectral mask

EIRP = PPA − Lfeed + Gant

EIRP (effective isotropic radiated power) in dBm; PPA is the PA output in dBm; Lfeed is the total loss in dB of the harmonic filter, duplexer or switch and cable between the PA and the antenna; Gant is the antenna gain in dBi. If the power at the antenna port Pant is already known, EIRP = Pant + Gant, with no loss term.

A spectral mask is a regulatory or standard limit on transmitted power against frequency offset from the carrier, given as absolute power in a measurement bandwidth. The adjacent-channel leakage ratio (ACLR) is the power in the wanted channel divided by the power that leaks into the adjacent channel, in dB; some syllabi call it the adjacent-channel power ratio (ACPR). A larger ACLR is better: 45 dB means the adjacent channel receives 45 dB less power than the wanted one. PA compression and IM3 widen the spectrum (spectral regrowth) and lower ACLR, and I/Q gain and phase error add an image sideband.

Modulated signals have a peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR), so the average output must be backed off from compression by about the PAPR: an 8 dB PAPR signal at 25 dBm average has 33 dBm peaks. The PA is chosen so these peaks stay near or below P1dB,out, or linearised as in RF power amplifiers.

Transceivers: duplexer, TDD switch and shared LO

In frequency-division duplex (FDD) the transmitter and receiver work at the same time on two frequencies, joined to one antenna by a duplexer: two band-pass filters, one for each band. Its insertion loss (typically 1 to 3 dB in a handset) appears twice: it cuts the EIRP on transmit, and on receive it adds directly to the noise figure because it sits before the LNA. Its isolation keeps two things out of the receiver: the transmit carrier itself, which can compress the LNA, and the PA's noise in the receive band, which adds to the receiver's own noise floor.

In time-division duplex (TDD) the two directions take turns on one frequency, so a switch replaces the duplexer. The switch has lower loss but finite isolation, and the PA must be off before the LNA is connected; a guard period covers the switching time. One synthesizer can feed both mixers in TDD; in FDD the transmit and receive LOs differ by the duplex spacing, so two synthesizers or an offset scheme are used. The LO's phase noise matters on both sides: on transmit it spreads into the adjacent channel, and on receive a strong blocker mixes with it (reciprocal mixing).

5.6 Low noise amplifiers

The low noise amplifier (LNA) is the first active block after the antenna and its band filter. It must add as little noise as possible, give enough gain to hide the noise of the mixer behind it, stay linear when strong signals arrive, and be stable with any antenna. Its input is a compromise: the source impedance that gives the lowest noise is usually not the one that gives a perfect power match. Every phone, GPS receiver and base station starts with one.

You need: the Friis formula and sensitivity from noise figure, reflection coefficient Γ and the Smith chart from transmission lines, and S-parameters from microwave components.

Noise match is not power match

The LNA's noise figure adds almost directly to the receiver's, because the first stage dominates the Friis sum, so 1 dB saved here is about 1 dB of sensitivity. Every transistor has four noise parameters: the minimum noise factor Fmin, the source reflection coefficient Γopt at which Fmin is reached, and the equivalent noise resistance Rn, which says how fast F rises as the source reflection Γs moves away from Γopt:

F = Fmin + (4Rn/Z₀)·|Γs − Γopt|² / [(1 − |Γs|²)·|1 + Γopt|²]

F and Fmin are linear noise factors (convert to dB only at the end, NF = 10 log₁₀F); Rn in Ω; Z₀ is the reference impedance, usually 50 Ω; Γs and Γopt are complex reflection coefficients.

On a Smith chart points of equal F form circles around Γopt. Maximum power transfer needs Γs = Γin*, generally elsewhere, so a pure noise match can leave a return loss of only 6 to 12 dB. Designers pick a Γs between the two, or use inductive degeneration, which moves the power-match point close to Γopt.

Inductively degenerated common-source LNA

The standard narrowband CMOS LNA is a common-source MOSFET with an inductor Ls in its source and an inductor Lg in series with its gate. Its input impedance is

Zin = jω(Lg + Ls) + 1/(jωCgs) + ωTLs

gm is the transconductance in S; Cgs the gate-source capacitance in F; ωT = gm/Cgs is the transit frequency in rad/s (the datasheet value fT = ωT/2π is in Hz).

The real part ωTLs is made by feedback through Ls, not by a resistor, so it adds no thermal noise of its own. The design takes two steps: choose Ls = 50/ωT so that Re Zin = 50 Ω, then choose Lg so that Lg + Ls resonates with Cgs at the operating frequency ω₀, that is (Lg + Ls) = 1/(ω₀²Cgs). At resonance the gate voltage is Qin times the source voltage, with Qin = 1/(2Rsω₀Cgs) for a source Rs, so the stage's effective transconductance is gmQin = ωT/(2ω₀Rs).

A cascode transistor stacked on top (common-gate) cuts the Miller feedback from drain to gate, so the input match does not depend on the load and reverse isolation is high. Its own noise, referred to the input, is divided by the gain of the first device, so it adds little. The cost is headroom: two drain-source voltages must fit under the supply. A tuned LC load or a matching network sets the output match and the gain.

Gain, linearity, stability and devices

An LNA needs just enough gain, typically 12 to 20 dB: enough to make the mixer's term (F₂ − 1)/G₁ small, not so much that the stages after it compress first; cascaded IIP3 falls as G₁ rises. Output values follow from input values by adding G: P1dB,out = P1dB,in + G − 1 and OIP3 = IIP3 + G.

The LNA must not oscillate with any passive source or load. With S-parameters, Δ = S₁₁S₂₂ − S₁₂S₂₁ and the Rollett factor

K = (1 − |S₁₁|² − |S₂₂|² + |Δ|²) / (2|S₁₂S₂₁|)

K > 1 together with |Δ| < 1 means unconditionally stable at that frequency; check it at every frequency where the device has gain.

5.7 Mixers: up-conversion and down-conversion

A mixer is a three-port circuit that multiplies two signals. The radio-frequency (RF) port carries the signal, the local-oscillator (LO) port carries a strong sine or square wave, and the intermediate-frequency (IF) port carries the product. Multiplication creates sum and difference frequencies, so a mixer moves a signal from one band to another without changing its modulation: down to an IF or baseband in a receiver, up to the radio band in a transmitter. Its conversion gain, noise figure, port isolation, linearity and spurious responses decide how clean that translation is.

You need: the image frequency from receiver architectures and IIP3 from nonlinearity.

Frequency translation

Multiplying A cos ωRFt by cos ωLOt gives (A/2)[cos(ωLO − ωRF)t + cos(ωLO + ωRF)t]: half the amplitude at the difference and half at the sum. A filter after the mixer keeps the one that is wanted.

  1. Down-conversion (receiver): the RF and LO inputs give fIF = |fRF − fLO|; a filter keeps the difference.
  2. Up-conversion (transmitter): the IF and LO inputs give fLO + fIF and fLO − fIF; a filter keeps one, the other is the unwanted sideband.
  3. Either way the image, the second input that gives the same output (2fLO − fRF), must be removed before the mixer or cancelled by an image-reject mixer.

Real mixers switch rather than multiply: the LO turns diodes or transistors fully on and off, which is the same as multiplying by a square wave. A square wave contains the odd harmonics 3fLO, 5fLO and so on, and any nonlinearity adds powers of the RF signal. So the output contains every product |m·fLO ± n·fRF| for whole numbers m and n. A spur, or spurious response, is any input frequency other than the wanted one for which some such product falls at fIF. The half-IF spur, an input at fRF + fIF/2 when the LO is high-side, is the most troublesome because it lies so close to the wanted signal.

Conversion gain and the mixer families

Gc = PIF / PRF

conversion gain as a power ratio; in dB, Gc = PIF(dBm) − PRF(dBm). A negative value is conversion loss.

A switch driven by a square LO multiplies the signal by a waveform whose fundamental has amplitude 4/π times that of the square wave's ±1 swing; keeping one of the two output terms leaves a voltage conversion gain of 2/π, that is −3.9 dB, for an ideal double-balanced switching mixer. Real passive mixers (a diode ring or a CMOS switch quad) show 5 to 7 dB of loss. They need a strong LO, typically +7 dBm or more for a diode ring, draw no DC current and have a high IIP3, though they still compress when the RF level nears the LO drive. Their SSB noise figure is close to their loss.

An active mixer adds gain. In the Gilbert cell a differential pair converts the RF voltage into a current (gm), two cross-coupled pairs switched by the LO commutate that current, and load resistors RL turn it back into voltage. Its voltage conversion gain is (2/π)gmRL. A single-balanced mixer has one switching pair and lets the LO through to the output; the double-balanced Gilbert cell cancels both the LO and the RF at the IF port, which is why it is the standard choice on chip. Active mixers need less LO power but add more noise and have a lower IIP3.

Noise, isolation and LO leakage

A mixer converts noise from both the wanted band and the image band to the IF. Its double-sideband (DSB) noise figure assumes the signal is present in both bands, as in a zero-IF receiver; the single-sideband (SSB) figure assumes the signal is in one band only, as in a superheterodyne. Without image filtering, the SSB value is about 3 dB higher than the DSB value.

Isolation is the loss between two ports: LO-to-RF, LO-to-IF and RF-to-IF, in dB. LO-to-RF leakage sends the LO back out to the LNA and antenna; in a zero-IF receiver it self-mixes to a DC offset. LO-to-IF leakage puts a large tone at the IF output that can overload the next stage. These leaks are direct feedthrough at the original frequencies; they are not translated signals and not the image. Balanced circuits and careful layout give 30 to 50 dB of isolation.

5.8 RF power amplifiers: classes, efficiency, linearization

An RF power amplifier (PA) is the last active block before the antenna. It must deliver hundreds of milliwatts to many watts while staying linear enough for the modulation. The classes that are most linear waste most of their DC power as heat, and the classes that are most efficient distort the signal and splatter energy into neighbouring channels. Class choice, the load line, back-off and linearisation together set the output power, the battery life and whether the spectral mask is met in handsets, Wi-Fi routers, base stations and radar transmitters.

You need: compression and IM3, ACLR and PAPR from transmitter architectures, and L-section matching from transmission lines.

Conduction angle, classes and efficiency

The bias point fixes the conduction angle, the part of each RF cycle (in degrees) during which the transistor carries current. Class A conducts for 360° (always on, most linear), Class B for 180° (two devices in push-pull cover the cycle), Class AB between the two (the usual linear handset PA), and Class C for less than 180° (efficient, but the output amplitude no longer follows the input). Classes D, E and F switch the transistor and shape its voltage and current so they do not overlap.

ηD = Pout / PDC

drain (or collector) efficiency; Pout is the RF output power and PDC = VDDIDC the supply power, both in W.

PAE = (Pout − Pin) / PDC

power-added efficiency; Pin is the RF drive power in W. PAE is always below ηD, and much below it when the PA gain is low.

For ideal reduced-conduction stages driven to full swing, the maximum efficiency for a conduction angle α (radians) is η = (α − sin α)/[4(sin(α/2) − (α/2)cos(α/2))]. It gives 50 % at 360°, 60.2 % at 270°, 78.5 % (π/4) at 180°, 89.7 % at 120° and 94.0 % at 90°. The output power for the same peak current falls below 180°: by 1.1 dB at 120° and 2.1 dB at 90°.

Load line, matching and back-off

A choke-fed Class A stage biased at IDC = Imax/2 swings its drain current from 0 to Imax and its drain voltage from the knee voltage Vknee to 2VDD − Vknee. The load that allows both full swings at once is

Ropt = 2(VDD − Vknee) / Imax

Ropt in Ω; voltages in V; Imax the peak current in A. The output power is Pout = (VDD − Vknee)Imax/4.

This load-line match, not a conjugate match, gives maximum swing rather than maximum gain. Ropt is usually a few ohms, so a network transforms the 50 Ω load down to it.

Back-off is how far the average output sits below the maximum, BO = 10 log₁₀(Pmax/Pavg). The classes lose efficiency at different rates. A Class A stage draws the same DC current at any output, so η falls with the power ratio: η = 50 % × 10−BO/10. An ideal Class B stage's DC current falls with the signal amplitude, so η = 78.5 % × 10−BO/20. At 6 dB back-off Class A falls to 12.6 % and Class B to 39.4 %. Signals with 8 to 10 dB of PAPR therefore make a plain PA run at a small fraction of its datasheet efficiency.

Linearisation and efficiency enhancement

A nonlinear PA spreads a modulated signal's spectrum (spectral regrowth), raising the adjacent-channel power. Four standard remedies trade complexity for efficiency.

  • Back-off: run the PA far below compression. Simple and linear, but efficiency falls as in the paragraph above.
  • Digital predistortion (DPD): a baseband block applies the inverse of the PA's nonlinearity before the DAC. A coupler and a feedback receiver sample the PA output so the DPD coefficients can adapt. Memory effects (thermal and bias time constants) make the needed inverse depend on past samples, so DPD uses memory models.
  • Feedforward: a main PA's output is compared with the clean input, the error is amplified by a separate error amplifier and subtracted at the output through a coupler. It is wideband and very linear, but the error amplifier and couplers waste power, so it is now used mostly where linearity matters more than efficiency.
  • Doherty and envelope tracking (ET): a Doherty PA has a carrier amplifier and a peaking amplifier joined by a quarter-wave impedance inverter; at low power the peaking amplifier is off and the carrier sees a doubled load, so it reaches full voltage swing (and peak efficiency) 6 dB below maximum. ET makes the supply VDD(t) follow the signal envelope, so the voltage across the transistor stays small and little power is lost as heat.

Base stations commonly pair a Doherty PA with DPD; handsets pair ET with DPD.

5.9 Oscillators, VCO, phase noise and frequency synthesizers

An oscillator makes a steady RF sine wave with no input; a voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) lets a DC voltage set its frequency. No oscillator is perfect: its phase wanders, which spreads some of its power into nearby frequencies, measured as phase noise in dBc/Hz. A frequency synthesizer locks a VCO to a clean crystal reference with a phase-locked loop (PLL), so the output has the crystal's accuracy and stability at any channel the divider asks for. These blocks supply the LO behind every mixer in a radio.

You need: LC resonance and varactors from RF passive components, and the mixer of mixers.

Feedback, Barkhausen and negative resistance

An oscillator is an amplifier of gain A with a frequency-selective feedback network β. At power-up, noise at the frequency where the loop phase is 0° (or n × 360°) and the loop gain exceeds one grows. As the amplitude rises the amplifier saturates, its gain falls, and the amplitude settles where the loop gain is exactly one. That steady state is the Barkhausen condition:

|Aβ| = 1 and ∠Aβ = 0° (or n × 360°)

A is the amplifier gain and β the feedback fraction, both complex; to start reliably, |Aβ| is designed at 2 to 3 at power-up.

At RF the same idea is written as a negative resistance. An LC tank with loss resistance Rp rings down; an active circuit (a cross-coupled transistor pair, or a Colpitts transistor) presents −R, and when |−R| ≤ Rp the losses are cancelled and the tank oscillates at f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)). The tank's Q sets how pure the tone is. A ring oscillator instead chains an odd number N of inverting stages: the inversions give 180°, and the stage delays supply the other 180°, so the loop's total is 360° at f = 1/(2Ntd), where td is one stage's delay. Rings are small and tune widely, but with no resonator their phase noise is far worse.

VCO tuning and phase noise

A VCO's output frequency is fout = f₀ + KVCOVtune, where KVCO is the tuning gain in Hz/V. In an LC VCO the tuning element is a varactor or a switched-capacitor bank. Because C(V) is not linear, KVCO changes across the band. A large KVCO tunes widely but turns control-line noise into frequency noise.

Phase noise L(Δf) is the noise power in a 1 Hz bandwidth at an offset Δf from the carrier, divided by the carrier power, in dBc/Hz. Leeson's model gives its shape for an LC oscillator:

L(Δf) = 10 log₁₀[(2FkT/Ps)·(1 + (f₀/(2QΔf))²)·(1 + fc/Δf)]

F is the active device's noise factor; k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K; T in K; Ps is the signal power in the tank in W; f₀ the carrier frequency; Q the loaded tank Q; fc the flicker corner; Δf the offset, all in Hz. Some textbooks write FkT/(2Ps) as the first factor, which is 6 dB lower; use your prescribed book's form.

Far from the carrier (Δf above f₀/2Q) the noise is flat at 2FkT/Ps; below f₀/2Q it rises 20 dB per decade towards the carrier (1/f²), and below the flicker corner fc 30 dB per decade (1/f³). Doubling Q lowers the 1/f² region by 6 dB; doubling the signal power lowers everything by 3 dB.

The PLL frequency synthesizer

A phase-frequency detector (PFD) compares the reference fref with the VCO frequency divided by N. A charge pump turns the phase error into current pulses, a loop filter turns those into the VCO control voltage, and the loop settles when fVCO/N = fref, so fout = N·fref. In an integer-N synthesizer N is a whole number and the channel spacing equals fref: channels at 2402 to 2480 MHz in 1 MHz steps need fref = 1 MHz and N = 2402 to 2480. A fractional-N synthesizer makes the average N = Nint + K/M by switching the divider between Nint and Nint + 1, so a high reference can still give fine steps; its price is fractional spurs, reduced by a sigma-delta modulator that randomises the switching.

Inside the loop bandwidth fL the output follows the reference, raised by 20 log₁₀N (phase is multiplied by N with frequency, and noise power goes as phase squared). Outside fL the output follows the free VCO. The lowest total comes from setting fL where the two curves cross. A wider loop settles faster (in a few times 1/fL) but passes more reference noise and spurs.

Worked example

Exam traps

  • Treating dBc/Hz as total noise. It is a density at one offset; total phase noise (or jitter) needs an integral over the offsets.
  • Adding 10 log₁₀N for frequency multiplication. Phase deviation is multiplied by N and noise power goes as its square, so the rise is 20 log₁₀N: 46 dB for N = 200.
  • Saying a PLL removes the VCO's noise at every offset. It suppresses VCO noise only inside the loop bandwidth; far out, the output is the free VCO.
  • Stating the Barkhausen condition as gain = 1 only. The loop phase must also be 0° (or n × 360°).
  • Using fref as the channel spacing of a fractional-N synthesizer. Its spacing is fref/M, much finer than fref.

Takeaways

  1. A spiral inductor's real Q falls to zero at self-resonance: the 2 nH, 1 pF example has Q = 4.25 at 2 GHz, not the 6.28 of ωL/Rs.
  2. A varactor's capacitance ratio tunes a tank by only its square root: 2.26 in capacitance gives 1.50 in frequency.
  3. Noise figures cascade by Friis in linear units; the first stage with gain sets most of the total, and any loss in front of it adds dB for dB.
  4. Sensitivity is −174 dBm/Hz + 10 log₁₀B + NF + SNRmin; every factor of 10 in bandwidth costs 10 dB.
  5. Intercepts cascade the other way: later stages, seen after gain, usually limit IIP3, so LNA gain trades noise against linearity.
  6. The image sits 2fIF from the signal and must be removed before the mixer, by filtering (α = √(1 + Q²ρ²)) or by I/Q cancellation (IRR ≈ 4/(ε² + θ²)).
  7. In a transceiver the duplexer's loss counts on both paths, and its isolation must keep the transmit carrier and transmit noise out of the receiver.
  8. An inductively degenerated LNA gets its 50 Ω from ωTLs with no resistor; the noise match and power match are close but not equal.
  9. PA efficiency falls with back-off, Class A as the power ratio and Class B as the amplitude ratio; Doherty, envelope tracking and DPD win it back.
  10. A PLL output follows the reference raised by 20 log₁₀N inside the loop and the free VCO outside it; the best loop bandwidth is where the two cross.

Understand State the Barkhausen criterion. Explain how a VCO tunes and what phase noise means in dBc/Hz. A receiver's 2 GHz LO has −125.6 dBc/Hz at 1 MHz offset; a −30 dBm blocker sits 1 MHz from a −90 dBm wanted signal in a 200 kHz channel. Find the reciprocal-mixing noise and the resulting SNR. 8 marks

Points to cover. |Aβ| = 1, ∠Aβ = 0° (start-up above 1); varactor tank, KVCO in Hz/V; L(Δf): SSB noise in 1 Hz at Δf ÷ carrier; reciprocal mixing: Pb + L + 10 log₁₀B = −102.6 dBm; SNR = 12.6 dB.

Model answer.

  1. Barkhausen: an amplifier A with feedback β oscillates steadily at the frequency where the loop gain Aβ has magnitude exactly 1 and phase 0° (or a whole number of 360°). At start-up |Aβ| is made 2 to 3 so noise grows; saturation then brings it down to 1.
  2. VCO tuning: an LC tank sets f = 1/(2π√(LC)). A varactor in the tank changes C with the control voltage, so fout = f₀ + KVCOVtune, where KVCO (Hz/V) is the slope. A wide range needs a large KVCO, which also turns control-line noise into frequency noise.
  3. Phase noise: random phase wander spreads part of the carrier's power to nearby frequencies. L(Δf) is the single-sideband noise power in a 1 Hz band at offset Δf, divided by the carrier power, in dBc/Hz. −125.6 dBc/Hz at 1 MHz means that 1 MHz from the carrier, each hertz holds 125.6 dB less power than the carrier.
  4. Reciprocal mixing: in the mixer the LO's noise skirt multiplies every input. The strong blocker, mixed with the LO's noise 1 MHz from the LO, lands exactly on the IF of the wanted signal, as if the blocker carried that skirt.
  5. Noise added in the 200 kHz channel: Pb + L(1 MHz) + 10 log₁₀B = −30 − 125.6 + 53.0 = −102.6 dBm.
  6. SNR = −90 − (−102.6) = 12.6 dB, set by the LO, not by the receiver's own noise. For comparison, the thermal floor of a 3 dB-NF receiver in 200 kHz is −174 + 53.0 + 3 = −118.0 dBm, 15.4 dB lower.
  7. To make reciprocal mixing as small as the thermal floor, L(1 MHz) would need to be −118.0 + 30 − 53.0 = −141 dBc/Hz.
  8. Ways to improve it: a VCO with a higher-Q tank or more tank power (Leeson's model gives 6 dB per doubling of Q in the 1/f² region and 3 dB per doubling of power); a sharper RF or IF filter that attenuates the blocker before the mixer; or a frequency plan that keeps the strongest expected blockers further from the channel.

  9. The required phase noise also fixes the VCO design: with Leeson's model, L(1 MHz) ≤ −123.0 dBc/Hz leaves only 2.6 dB of slack, so the tank Q (8) and power (1 mW) cannot be relaxed, and the loop bandwidth must stay well below 1 MHz so that offset remains in the VCO-dominated region.
  10. Closing check: −30 − 125.6 + 53.0 = −102.6 dBm and −90 − (−102.6) = 12.6 dB ≥ 10 dB; −30 − 109.0 + 53.0 = −86.0 dBm, which exceeds −90 dBm.

Glossary

ACLR
Adjacent-channel leakage ratio: wanted-channel power ÷ adjacent-channel power, in dB.
Back-off
How far, in dB, a PA's average output sits below its maximum or compressed output.
Conversion gain
A mixer's output power at the wanted frequency ÷ its input signal power.
dBm
Power in dB relative to 1 mW: P(dBm) = 10 log₁₀(P/1 mW); 0 dBm = 1 mW.
Direct conversion
A zero-IF receiver that mixes straight to baseband I and Q.
Doherty amplifier
A carrier and a peaking amplifier joined by a quarter-wave inverter to keep efficiency high at back-off.
Duplexer
A pair of band filters that lets an FDD transmitter and receiver share one antenna.
EIRP
Effective isotropic radiated power: transmit power at the antenna plus antenna gain in dBi.
Gain compression
The fall of gain at high input; P1dB is where it reaches 1 dB.
IIP3
Input third-order intercept: the extrapolated input where the fundamental and IM3 lines meet.
Image frequency
The unwanted input that mixes to the same IF as the wanted signal: fsi = 2fLO − fRF.
Image-reject ratio
Conversion gain for the wanted signal ÷ conversion gain for the image, in dB.
Inductive degeneration
A source inductor that gives an LNA a noiseless real input resistance ωTLs.
Intermodulation
New tones made when two or more tones pass through a nonlinear stage, such as 2f₁ − f₂.
LNA
Low-noise amplifier: the first gain stage, which sets the receiver noise figure.
Noise factor
F = SNRin/SNRout with the source at 290 K; a linear ratio.
Noise figure
NF = 10 log₁₀F, the SNR degradation of a stage in dB.
Noise temperature
Te = (F − 1) × 290 K, the input noise temperature that accounts for a stage's own noise.
PAE
Power-added efficiency: (Pout − Pin)/PDC.
Phase noise
L(Δf): single-sideband noise in 1 Hz at offset Δf ÷ carrier power, in dBc/Hz.
PLL
Phase-locked loop: locks a VCO to N times a reference frequency.
Quality factor (Q)
2π × energy stored ÷ energy lost per cycle; for a series model |X|/R.
Reciprocal mixing
A strong blocker mixing with the LO's phase-noise skirt onto the wanted signal's IF.
Self-resonant frequency
The frequency where a part's parasitic reactance cancels its main reactance; above it an inductor looks capacitive.
Sensitivity
The weakest input that gives the demodulator its required SNR.
SFDR
Spurious-free dynamic range: from the noise floor to the input at which IM3 reaches the noise floor.
Spurious response
An input frequency other than the wanted one that some product m·fLO ± n·fRF moves to the IF.
Superheterodyne
A receiver that converts the RF signal to a fixed IF for filtering and gain before demodulation.
Varactor
A reverse-biased junction or MOS capacitor whose capacitance is set by a DC voltage.
