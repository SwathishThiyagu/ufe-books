1.1 Line parameters, equations and characteristic impedance

A transmission line is a distributed circuit: every metre adds series resistance and inductance and shunt conductance and capacitance, the four primary constants. Put them into the telegrapher's equations and the solution is two waves travelling in opposite directions, with one characteristic impedance Z0 and one propagation constant γ. From γ come attenuation, phase constant, wavelength and velocity. Every RF design, from a 50 Ω cable to a printed trace, starts here.

You need: Kirchhoff's voltage and current laws, phasor notation with angular frequency ω = 2πf, complex numbers in rectangular and polar form, and the physical idea that a wire stores magnetic energy while two nearby conductors store electric energy.

The four primary constants

A transmission line always has a go and a return conductor: the inner and outer of a coaxial cable, a microstrip trace over a ground plane, or a twisted pair.

At radio and microwave frequency the line is long compared with the wavelength, so voltage and current change from centimetre to centimetre. We therefore describe a short slice of length Δz and let Δz shrink to zero.

RΩ/mresistance of the metal
LH/mmagnetic field around conductors
GS/mleakage through the dielectric
CF/melectric field between conductors

Some syllabi call R, L, G and C the primary line constants. All four are per metre: a 20 m cable has total series inductance 20L, not L.

Telegrapher's equations and the general solution

Take a slice Δz, apply Kirchhoff's voltage law round the series path and Kirchhoff's current law at the shunt node, divide by Δz and let Δz → 0. In phasor form:

dV/dz = −(R + jωL) I        dI/dz = −(G + jωC) V

V and I are phasor voltage and current at distance z from the sending end, in V and A; R, L, G, C are per-metre constants; ω = 2πf rad/s; j is the imaginary unit.

Differentiate the first equation and substitute the second. The voltage obeys d²V/dz² = γ²V, with

γ = α + jβ = √((R + jωL)(G + jωC))

γ is the propagation constant; α is the attenuation constant in nepers per metre (Np/m); β is the phase constant in radians per metre (rad/m).

V(z) = V⁺e−γz + V⁻eγz

V⁺ is the forward wave amplitude at z = 0, V⁻ is the backward wave amplitude, both in V.

The current that goes with it is I(z) = (V⁺/Z0)e−γz − (V⁻/Z0)eγz, with

Z0 = √((R + jωL)/(G + jωC))

Z0 is the characteristic impedance in ohms; it is the ratio V⁺/I⁺ of a single forward wave.

Most real lines are low-loss: R ≪ ωL and G ≪ ωC. Then Z0 ≈ √(L/C), β ≈ ω√(LC) and α ≈ R/(2Z0) + GZ0/2 Np/m. The first term is conductor loss and the second is dielectric loss. One neper is 8.686 dB, so α in dB/m is 8.686α.

The minus sign on the backward current is the step most often lost in exams: the backward wave carries its current the other way. γ and Z0 are called secondary constants because they follow from R, L, G, C rather than being measured separately.

Lossless line, distortion and loading

Set R = G = 0. Then the constants become simple:

Z0 = √(L/C)    β = ω√(LC)    vp = 1/√(LC)    λ = 2π/β = vp/f

Z0 in Ω is now purely real; β in rad/m; vp is the phase velocity in m/s; λ in m; f in Hz.

With R and G present, Z0 is complex and α > 0. A pulse contains many frequencies; if α and β/ω change with frequency, the components are attenuated unequally and travel at different speeds, so the pulse arrives smeared. The line is distortionless when R/L = G/C. Then Z0 = √(L/C) is real, β = ω√(LC), and α = √(RG) is flat in frequency. Telephone cables have R/L far above G/C, so loading coils add series inductance to raise L.

An infinite line has no backward wave, so its input impedance is exactly Z0. A finite line ended in Z0 behaves the same; any other load creates a reflected wave, which is the subject of 1.2, and the impedance seen at the input is covered in 1.3.

In this book every phasor amplitude is a peak value, so the forward-wave power is P⁺ = |V⁺|²/(2Z0) W for real Z0 (with an rms value it would be |V⁺|²/Z0). On a matched line of length l the voltage falls by e−αl, so the power that arrives is a fraction e−2αl of the power sent: the transmission efficiency of a matched line is η = e−2αl.

Worked example

Question. A lossless coaxial line has L = 0.25 μH/m and C = 100 pF/m. (a) Find Z0. (b) Find the phase velocity. (c) Find the wavelength at 100 MHz. (d) How long does a signal take to travel 3 m of this line?

  1. Convert to SI: L = 0.25 μH/m = 2.5×10⁻⁷ H/m; C = 100 pF/m = 1.0×10⁻¹⁰ F/m.
  2. (a) Lossless, so Z0 = √(L/C) = √(2.5×10⁻⁷ / 1.0×10⁻¹⁰) = √2500 = 50 Ω.
  3. (b) vp = 1/√(LC) = 1/√(2.5×10⁻⁷ × 1.0×10⁻¹⁰) = 1/√(2.5×10⁻¹⁷) = 1/(5×10⁻⁹) = 2×10⁸ m/s, about 0.667c.
  4. (c) λ = vp/f = 2×10⁸ / 1×10⁸ = 2 m. Check: β = 2π/λ = π rad/m, and ω = 2π×10⁸ rad/s, so ω/β = 2×10⁸ m/s.
  5. (d) t = d/vp = 3 / 2×10⁸ = 1.5×10⁻⁸ s = 15 ns.

Answer. Z0 = 50 Ω, vp = 2×10⁸ m/s, λ = 2 m at 100 MHz, and a 3 m delay of 15 ns.

Worked example 2

Question. A coaxial line has R = 0.1 Ω/m, L = 0.25 µH/m, G = 10 µS/m and C = 100 pF/m. At 10 MHz find (a) Z0, (b) α and β, (c) the loss of 100 m in dB, (d) the L that would make the line distortionless.

  1. ω = 2π × 10⁷ = 6.283×10⁷ rad/s. R + jωL = 0.1 + j15.708 = 15.708∠89.64° Ω/m. G + jωC = 10⁻⁵ + j6.283×10⁻³ = 6.283×10⁻³∠89.91° S/m.
  2. (a) Z0 = √(15.708/6.283×10⁻³ ∠(89.64° − 89.91°)) = √(2500∠−0.27°) = 50.0∠−0.14° Ω, which is 50.00 − j0.12 Ω: almost real.
  3. (b) γ = √(15.708 × 6.283×10⁻³ ∠(89.64° + 89.91°)) = √(0.09870∠179.54°) = 0.3142∠89.77° m⁻¹ = 0.00125 + j0.3142 m⁻¹. So α = 0.00125 Np/m and β = 0.3142 rad/m, giving λ = 2π/β = 20.0 m and vp = ω/β = 2.0×10⁸ m/s.
  4. Low-loss check: α ≈ R/(2Z0) + GZ0/2 = 0.1/100 + (10⁻⁵ × 50)/2 = 0.00100 + 0.00025 = 0.00125 Np/m. Conductor loss is four-fifths of it.
  5. (c) 8.686 × 0.00125 = 0.01086 dB/m, so 100 m loses 1.09 dB (matched-line efficiency e−0.25 = 0.779).
  6. (d) R/L = 4×10⁵ s⁻¹ but G/C = 1×10⁵ s⁻¹, so the line distorts. R/L = G/C needs L = RC/G = (0.1 × 10⁻¹⁰)/10⁻⁵ = 1.0 µH/m; then α = √(RG) = 0.0010 Np/m and Z0 = √(L/C) = 100 Ω.

Answer. Z0 = 50.0∠−0.14° Ω; α = 0.00125 Np/m, β = 0.3142 rad/m; 1.09 dB per 100 m; L = 1.0 µH/m makes it distortionless.

Exam traps

  • Per metre versus total: putting the total L of a 20 m cable into √(L/C) with the per-metre C gives an answer √20 too large. Convert both to per-metre values first.
  • Using free-space c for vp. On a line vp = 1/√(LC) is fixed by the dielectric and is normally below c.
  • Writing Z0 = √(L/C) when R and G are not zero. That formula is the lossless line only; the general Z0 is complex.
  • Dropping the minus sign in the backward current, I(z) = (V⁺/Z0)e−γz − (V⁻/Z0)eγz. With a plus sign the backward wave would carry its current the wrong way, and every Γ and Zin built on it comes out wrong.
  • Thinking Z0 can be measured with an ohmmeter on an open cable. The meter reads the DC open-circuit resistance, which is nearly infinite, not Z0.

Analyze A cable has R/L greater than G/C. Are the high-frequency components of a pulse attenuated more or less than the low ones, and how do loading coils help? 5 marks

α is not flat: it rises from √(RG) at low frequency towards R/(2Z0) + GZ0/2 at high frequency, and β/ω is not constant, so the high-frequency parts of a pulse are attenuated more and arrive at a different time; the edges round off. This happens whenever R/L ≠ G/C. Loading coils raise L, bringing R/L down to G/C, so α and vp stop depending on frequency.

1.2 Reflection coefficient, standing waves and VSWR

A transmission line carries a forward wave. When the load impedance is not equal to the line's characteristic impedance, part of the wave reflects back. The forward and reflected waves add to make a standing wave. The reflection coefficient measures the reflected wave, and VSWR measures how strong the standing wave is. These numbers tell you how much power reaches the load and how badly a link or antenna is matched.

You need: characteristic impedance Z0 and propagation constant β from Chapter 1.1.

Reflection coefficient at the load

A load ends a line of characteristic impedance Z0. If ZL = Z0, all incident power is absorbed and no wave returns. If ZL differs, the boundary cannot support the same voltage-to-current ratio as the line, so a reflected wave must exist. The reflection coefficient ΓL is the complex ratio of reflected voltage wave to incident voltage wave at the load.

ΓL = (ZL − Z0)/(ZL + Z0)

ZL is load impedance in Ω; Z0 is line characteristic impedance in Ω; ΓL is dimensionless and complex.

For a passive load, |ΓL| lies between 0 and 1. |ΓL| = 0 means a matched load. |ΓL| = 1 means total reflection, as with an open or short circuit. Some books call |Γ| the reflection factor. The phase of ΓL records whether the reflected voltage is in phase or out of phase with the incident voltage.

Move a distance d from the load toward the generator. On a lossless line the magnitude does not change, but the phase rotates. The factor 2 appears because the wave travels d forward and d back, a round trip of 2d.

Γ(d) = ΓL e−j2βd

Γ(d) is reflection coefficient at distance d from the load; ΓL is its value at the load; β is phase constant in rad/m; d is distance toward the generator in m.

Standing waves and VSWR

The total voltage is the sum of forward and reflected waves. With d measured from the load towards the generator, the forward wave grows in phase as d grows, so on a lossless line with real Z0, V(d) = V+ ejβd[1 + Γ(d)] and I(d) = (V+/Z0)ejβd[1 − Γ(d)]. V+ is the forward voltage amplitude (peak) at the load, in V. The bracketed terms add or cancel as the phase of Γ(d) changes, so |V| rises and falls along the line.

|V(d)| = |V+| √(1 + |Γ|² + 2|Γ| cos(θ − 2βd))

|V(d)| is voltage magnitude at distance d in V; |V+| is forward voltage amplitude in V; |Γ| is magnitude of load reflection coefficient; θ is phase angle of ΓL in rad; β is phase constant in rad/m; d is distance from load in m.

The largest voltage is Vmax = |V+|(1 + |Γ|). The smallest is Vmin = |V+|(1 − |Γ|). The voltage standing wave ratio is VSWR = Vmax/Vmin = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|). VSWR is real and at least 1. A matched line has VSWR = 1. A short or open line has VSWR = ∞. Invert the formula when you know VSWR: |Γ| = (VSWR − 1)/(VSWR + 1).

Voltage maxima and current minima occur together. Voltage minima and current maxima occur together. Adjacent maximum and minimum are λ/4 apart; the pattern repeats every λ/2. On a lossless line VSWR does not change with d. The figure draws the worked example below: |Γ| = 0.447, so Vmax/Vmin = 1.447/0.553 = 2.62.

Power, return loss and efficiency

For a real Z0 and a peak amplitude |V+|, incident power is P+ = |V+|²/(2Z0). Reflected power is P− = |Γ|² P+. Power delivered to the load is PL = P+ − P− = P+(1 − |Γ|²). The fraction |Γ|² is reflected; 1 − |Γ|² reaches the load. A load with |Γ| = 0.447 reflects 0.20 of the power and delivers 0.80.

RL = −20 log10 |Γ| dB

RL is return loss in dB; |Γ| is magnitude of reflection coefficient at the reference plane. It is positive for |Γ| < 1. Do not call it reflection loss: many texts use that name for the mismatch loss below.

Mismatch loss is different: ML = −10 log10(1 − |Γ|²) dB. It is the extra loss caused by mismatch when the source is matched. Line efficiency for the mismatch alone is η = PL/P+ = 1 − |Γ|². This is not the same as conductor and dielectric efficiency. If |Γ| = 0, return loss is infinite dB and all power is delivered. If |Γ| = 1, return loss is 0 dB and no net power reaches the load.

Worked example

Question. A 50 Ω lossless line is terminated by ZL = 100 + j50 Ω. Find ΓL, |Γ|, the VSWR, the return loss and the mismatch loss in dB, the fractions of incident power reflected and delivered, and where the first voltage maximum and minimum sit.

  1. Z0 = 50 Ω and ZL = 100 + j50 Ω. Use ΓL = (ZL − Z0)/(ZL + Z0).
  2. ZL − Z0 = 50 + j50 Ω. ZL + Z0 = 150 + j50 Ω. So ΓL = (50 + j50)/(150 + j50).
  3. Multiply numerator and denominator by 150 − j50 Ω. Numerator = (50 + j50)(150 − j50) = 10000 + j5000 Ω². Denominator = 150² + 50² = 25000 Ω². ΓL = 0.4 + j0.2.
  4. |Γ| = √(0.4² + 0.2²) = √0.20 = 0.447.
  5. VSWR = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|) = 1.447/0.553 = 2.62.
  6. Return loss = −20 log10(0.447) = 6.99 dB.
  7. Reflected power fraction = |Γ|² = 0.447² = 0.20. Delivered fraction = 1 − 0.20 = 0.80.
  8. Mismatch loss = −10 log10(1 − |Γ|²) = −10 log10(0.80) = 0.97 dB.
  9. Phase of ΓL: θ = tan⁻¹(0.2/0.4) = 26.57°. A voltage maximum sits where θ − 2βd = 0, so 2βd = 26.57° and d = (26.57°/720°)λ = 0.037λ from the load. Vmax = 1.447|V+|.
  10. The first minimum is λ/4 further on, at d = 0.037λ + 0.25λ = 0.287λ, with Vmin = 0.553|V+|. Check: 1.447/0.553 = 2.62 = VSWR.

Answer. ΓL = 0.4 + j0.2 = 0.447∠26.57°, VSWR = 2.62, return loss = 6.99 dB, mismatch loss = 0.97 dB; 20 % of the power is reflected and 80 % delivered; first Vmax at 0.037λ and first Vmin at 0.287λ from the load.

Exam traps

  • Treating Γ as a real number: Γ is complex. Its magnitude sets VSWR and reflected power, but its phase sets where the voltage maxima and minima sit.
  • Writing VSWR = (1 − |Γ|)/(1 + |Γ|): the correct form is VSWR = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|), so VSWR is at least 1 and becomes infinite at total reflection.
  • Calling return loss negative: return loss = −20 log10|Γ| dB is already positive for a passive load; 6.99 dB is correct, −6.99 dB is not.
  • Confusing return loss with mismatch loss: return loss uses |Γ| and 20 log, while mismatch loss uses 1 − |Γ|² and 10 log.
  • Thinking VSWR changes along a lossless line: only the phase of Γ rotates; |Γ| and VSWR stay the same.

1.3 Input impedance of open, short and loaded lines

A generator at the sending end does not see the load directly. It sees the input impedance of the line. That impedance depends on the load ZL, the line characteristic impedance Z0, and the electrical length βd. The formula comes from the sum of forward and reflected waves. An open or short line therefore behaves as a capacitor or inductor, and a quarter-wave line turns a load upside down. This idea is used in antenna feeds, impedance matching, bias tees, filters, and printed RF circuits.

You need Chapter 1.1 for Z0 and β, and Chapter 1.2 for reflection and standing waves.

Impedance seen at the input

Let d be the line length in metres and λ the wavelength on the line in metres. The electrical length is βd = 2πd/λ radians, where β = 2π/λ is the phase constant in radians per metre. For a lossless line with load ZL, the input impedance is:

Zin = Z0 (ZL + jZ0 tan βd) / (Z0 + jZL tan βd)

Zin is the sending-end impedance in ohms; Z0 is the characteristic impedance in ohms; ZL is the load impedance in ohms; βd is in radians; tan is the tangent in radians.

At d=0, βd=0 and Zin=ZL. If ZL=Z0, Zin=Z0 for every d. A matched line gives no reflected wave, so its length does not change the input impedance. The formula uses complex numbers; keep the real part as resistance and the imaginary part as reactance.

  1. Find λ on the line, then βd = 2πd/λ in radians.
  2. Evaluate tan βd. Keep it as a number, not degrees.
  3. Put the complex ZL and Z0 into the formula.
  4. Separate the result into real and imaginary parts in ohms.

Open and short lines

For a short circuit, ZL=0. The formula reduces to Zin,short = jZ0 tan βd. For small βd, tan βd ≈ βd, so Zin,short ≈ jZ0βd. A short line shorter than λ/4 looks inductive.

For an open circuit, let ZL→∞. Divide top and bottom by ZL and take the limit: Zin,open = Z0/(j tan βd) = −jZ0 cot βd, where cot βd = 1/tan βd. For small βd, Zin,open ≈ −jZ0/(βd). An open line shorter than λ/4 looks capacitive.

At βd=π/2, d=λ/4, tan βd tends to infinity. A shorted λ/4 line presents an open circuit; an open λ/4 line presents a short circuit. Ideal open and short lines store energy and dissipate none. Their reactance changes with frequency, so a stub gives the designed reactance only near its design frequency. Stub behaviour is narrowband and must be checked across the band of interest. The sign tells the reactance: positive is inductive, negative is capacitive.

Quarter-wave inversion and half-wave repeat

At d=λ/4, βd=π/2 and tan βd→∞. The formula limit is Zin = Z0²/ZL. For a real load RL, Zin = Z0²/RL. A 50 Ω line with a 100 Ω load shows 25 Ω at its input. This is impedance inversion. It becomes matching only when the transformed value equals the source impedance; design is in Chapter 1.4.

At d=λ/2, βd=π and tan βd=0, so Zin=ZL. A half-wave line repeats the load impedance. Every whole multiple of λ/2 also repeats it. Electrical length, not physical length, decides these special cases. Physical length is λ/4 or λ/2 at the operating frequency; at another frequency the same piece is a different electrical length.

The wavelength on the line is λ = v/f, where v is the wave velocity in metres per second and f is frequency in hertz. A line with a dielectric has v less than the free-space value, so its λ is shorter and the same physical section is electrically longer.

Loss, power and transfer impedance

A real line has attenuation α in nepers per metre. Forward voltage falls by e−αd. On the round trip the voltage falls by e−2αd and the power falls by e−4αd. For a lossy line the input impedance is Zin = Z0(ZL + Z0 tanh γd)/(Z0 + ZL tanh γd), with γ = α + jβ; when α = 0, tanh jβd = j tan βd and the lossless formula returns. Zin moves toward Z0 as d grows because the reflected wave weakens. For a matched line, the power reaching the load is PL = Pin e−2αd one way, with efficiency η = PL/Pin = e−2αd.

Input impedance is V(0)/I(0) at the sending end. A transfer impedance relates a voltage at one port to a current at another, such as V(d)/I(0) for a stated termination. It is not the same as input impedance. Two-port S-parameters, which describe both, come with microwave components and measurements.

Attenuation is often quoted in dB per metre. One neper is 8.686 dB. Use α in nepers per metre in e−αd; use the dB value only after converting. On a long cable, the simple λ/2 repeat fails because the returning wave is too weak. The line then looks closer to a matched load.

Worked example

Question. A 50 Ω lossless line is terminated by ZL = 100 + j50 Ω, the load of the reflection chapter. Its length is d = λ/8. Find the input impedance and say whether it is inductive or capacitive.

  1. βd = 2πd/λ = 2π(λ/8)/λ = π/4 rad = 45°.
  2. tan βd = tan(π/4) = 1.
  3. Numerator inside brackets: ZL + jZ0 tan βd = 100 + j50 + j50 = 100 + j100 Ω.
  4. Denominator inside brackets: Z0 + jZL tan βd = 50 + j(100 + j50) = 50 + j100 − 50 = j100 Ω.
  5. Divide: (100 + j100)/(j100) = 1 − j. Multiply by Z0=50: Zin = 50(1 − j) = 50 − j50 Ω.
  6. The imaginary part is negative, so the input looks capacitive.

Answer. Zin = 50 − j50 Ω, a capacitive input impedance.

Exam traps

  • Calculator mode in tan. Convert βd = 2πd/λ to radians, keep the calculator in radian mode, and then evaluate tan βd. βd = π/4 rad is the same angle as 45°, and tan is 1. Entering 0.785 with the calculator in degree mode wrongly gives about 0.0137.
  • Swapping λ/2 and λ/4 behaviour. A λ/2 line repeats ZL; a λ/4 line inverts it as Z0²/ZL.
  • Treating an open circuit as Zin=∞ for every length. Only a zero-length open line gives infinity; a λ/4 open line gives a short at the input.
  • Using the lossless formula on a long lossy cable: the lossless formula repeats every λ/2 forever, but a real line drifts towards Z0; use tanh γd.
  • Mixing input and transfer impedance. Zin is V(0)/I(0); a transfer impedance uses a voltage at one port and a current at another.

1.4 Quarter-wave transformer and stub matching

A mismatched load reflects power. A quarter-wave transformer inserts a λ/4 section whose characteristic impedance is the geometric mean of line and load, so a real load looks like Z0 at the design frequency. A stub match instead adds a short open or shorted line at a chosen distance from the load, which cancels the remaining susceptance. These are the two hand designs for matching, used on RF boards, antenna feeds and test benches.

You need: 1.1 for Z0 and wavelength, 1.2 for reflection, and 1.3 for input impedance of loaded and open/short lines.

Quarter-wave transformer

Take a line of characteristic impedance Z0 feeding a real load ZL. Insert a section of length l and characteristic impedance Z0′ between them. From the input-impedance formula of 1.3, at l = λ/4 (βl = π/2) the section shows Zin = Z0′²/ZL.

Set Zin = Z0: Z0′²/ZL = Z0, so Z0′ = √(Z0 ZL). Z0′ is the geometric mean of source and load. For 50 Ω and 100 Ω, Z0′ = 70.71 Ω. The section is exactly λ/4 at the design frequency f0, with λ = vp/f0; vp is the phase velocity in the section, not c unless the dielectric is air.

Z0′ = √(Z0 ZL), l = λ/4 = vp/(4 f0)

Z0 is the source-line impedance in Ω; ZL is the real load resistance in Ω; Z0′ is the quarter-wave section impedance in Ω; vp is the phase velocity in m/s; f0 is the design frequency in Hz; l is the section length in m.

The match is exact at f0, and also at 3f0, 5f0, … for the ideal lossless section, because βl = π/2, 3π/2, 5π/2, … there. Between those points the electrical length is not 90° and Zin moves away from Z0, so the useful band around f0 is limited, and it shrinks as ZL/Z0 moves away from 1. It is used at antenna feeds and in single-frequency RF paths. Some syllabi call it a quarter-wave matching section.

Stub matching

A stub is a short piece of line connected in shunt (across the line) or in series. It adds pure susceptance or reactance. The design has two unknowns: the distance d from the load to the stub, and the stub length ls.

For a shunt stub, work in admittance. Move along the line from the load until the input admittance has real part equal to Y0 = 1/Z0. At that plane write Y(d) = Y0 + jB(d). A stub of input admittance Ys = jBs is connected in parallel, so the total admittance is Y0 + j[B(d) + Bs]. Choose Bs = −B(d). The line then sees admittance Y0, corresponding to impedance Z0.

A shorted stub of characteristic admittance Y0 has Ys = −jY0 cot βls; an open stub has Ys = jY0 tan βls. Set either equal to −jB(d) and solve for ls. A series stub is the dual: move until Z(d) = Z0 + jX(d) and cancel X(d) with a series reactance.

For a load ZL = RL + jXL the stub position has a closed form. Write t = tan βd; setting Re Y(d) = Y0 gives a quadratic in t with the two roots

t = [XL ± √(RL((Z0 − RL)² + XL²)/Z0)] / (RL − Z0)

d = (λ/2π) tan⁻¹ t when t ≥ 0 and d = (λ/2π)(π + tan⁻¹ t) when t < 0; RL, XL and Z0 in Ω. Put each d into the input-impedance formula, invert to get Y(d) = Y0 + jB, and size the stub to supply −jB.

  1. Normalize the load admittance to Y0.
  2. Find the distance d (from the load toward the generator) where Re[Y(d)] = Y0. There are generally two possible stub positions within λ/2; choose the practical one.
  3. Compute the susceptance B(d) at that plane.
  4. Choose open or short stub and solve for length ls so that Bs = −B(d).
  5. Check that the input admittance is Y0 + j0.

Measure d from the load, and ls from the junction to the open or short end. In microstrip an open stub is a printed line with no via; a short stub needs a via to ground.

Bandwidth, tolerance and design choices

Matching is specified over a band, not at one point. A common criterion is |Γ| ≤ Γm = 0.2, which gives VSWR ≤ 1.5. For one quarter-wave section the fractional bandwidth is Δf/f0 = 2 − (4/π) cos⁻¹[(Γm/√(1 − Γm²)) · 2√(Z0ZL)/|ZL − Z0|]. For ZL/Z0 = 2 this gives 0.784, a band from 0.61f0 to 1.39f0; for ZL/Z0 = 4 only 0.351. Larger impedance ratios or tighter VSWR reduce the bandwidth.

Of the two stub positions, the one nearer the load usually gives the wider band, because less line sits between the load and the stub. In practice CAD tools sweep d and ls to minimise the largest |Γ| over the band.

Keep characteristic impedances realisable (about 20 Ω to 120 Ω in microstrip), keep stubs short to limit loss and radiation, and choose open or short stubs from fabrication: an open stub needs no via. Multi-section transformers and tapered lines trade length for bandwidth. The graphical solution is in 1.5; printed realisation is in 1.6.

1.5 Smith chart and its applications

The Smith chart is graph paper for the reflection coefficient Γ = (Z − Z0)/(Z + Z0). It maps every normalized impedance z = Z/Z0 to a point inside a unit circle. A length of line becomes a circular arc, and VSWR (voltage standing wave ratio, the ratio of maximum to minimum voltage on the line) becomes a circle. Matching becomes an intersection. Engineers use it to read input impedance, VSWR, stub position and stub length without solving complex equations. It stays useful in RF labs and in CAD tools that draw the same chart on screen.

You need: reflection coefficient and VSWR from 1.2, input impedance from 1.3, and stub matching from 1.4.

The map from impedance to reflection coefficient

Normalize the load first. z = Z/Z0 = r + jx. Z is the load impedance in ohms, Z0 is the real characteristic impedance in ohms, r is normalized resistance, x is normalized reactance. Both r and x are pure numbers. A 100 + j50 Ω load on a 50 Ω line gives z = 2 + j1.

The reflection coefficient is Γ = (Z − Z0)/(Z + Z0) = (z − 1)/(z + 1). Γ is complex: Γ = Γr + jΓi. The inverse is z = (1 + Γ)/(1 − Γ). For a passive load |Γ| ≤ 1, so every point lies inside or on the unit circle.

Special points fix the chart. Short circuit z = 0 gives Γ = −1, the left edge. Open circuit z → ∞ gives Γ = +1, the right edge. Match z = 1 gives Γ = 0, the centre. Pure reactance r = 0 lies on the outer circle.

Constant r circle: centre r/(1+r), radius 1/(1+r). Constant x circle: centre 1 + j/x, radius 1/|x|.

r and x are normalized resistance and reactance; centres and radii are in the Γ plane.

Constant-r circles touch the right point Γ = +1. Constant-x arcs also pass through that point. The chart prints both families. Read r and x where the two curves meet.

VSWR, rotation and admittance

Every point at the same radius has the same |Γ|. Therefore it has the same VSWR: VSWR = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|). A VSWR circle is centred at the match Γ = 0. Moving along a lossless line does not change |Γ|; the point only slides on this circle. Where the circle cuts the positive real axis, the r value printed there equals the VSWR.

Γ(d) = ΓL e−j2βd

ΓL is the load reflection coefficient; d is the distance from the load toward the generator in metres; β = 2π/λ is the phase constant in rad/m; λ is the guide wavelength in metres.

The exponent changes phase by 2βd. The factor 2 appears because the wave travels d forward and d back. A full 360° turn needs 2βd = 2π, so d = λ/2. A λ/4 length turns the point by 180°. On a standard chart, moving toward the generator is clockwise.

Admittance uses the same chart. y = Y/Y0 = 1/z = g + jb. Here Y is admittance in siemens, Y0 = 1/Z0, g is normalized conductance, b is normalized susceptance. The admittance point is the impedance point rotated by 180°, because the admittance reflection coefficient is −Γ. Many charts also print admittance scales; do not mix the two readings.

Shunt stubs are easier on the admittance side. A shunt stub adds susceptance in parallel, so you need a point where the line admittance has g = 1. The stub cancels the remaining ±jb.

Using the chart to design a match

  1. Normalize the load: zL = ZL/Z0.
  2. Plot Γ = (zL − 1)/(zL + 1), or locate zL directly on the chart.
  3. Draw the VSWR circle through the point, centred at the match.
  4. For a shunt stub, use admittance scales and move clockwise toward the generator until the point meets the g = 1 circle. Read d/λ on the outer wavelength scale; d is the stub position from the load.
  5. At that point read susceptance b. The stub must supply −b. Read the short or open stub length from the chart or from the stub formula.

For a series stub, stay on impedance scales, move to the r = 1 circle and cancel x. Accuracy is limited by the printed grid. Use a sharp pencil, a compass and a large chart. Check the answer: d must lie between 0 and λ/2. For a short-circuited series stub of length l, the reactance is X = Z0·tan(βl), with X in ohms, l in metres and β = 2π/λ; the stub is shorter than λ/4 when it supplies positive (inductive) reactance. For a short-circuited shunt stub, the susceptance is B = −Y0·cot(βl), with B in siemens and Y0 = 1/Z0; the stub is shorter than λ/4 when it supplies negative (inductive) susceptance, and lies between λ/4 and λ/2 when it supplies positive (capacitive) susceptance.

1.6 Microstrip, stripline and printed lines

Printed transmission lines are copper strips made on a dielectric board. A microstrip has a top strip and a bottom ground plane; a stripline hides the strip between two grounds; a coplanar line puts the strip and side grounds on one face. These lines guide microwave fields in a flat, cheap, repeatable layer instead of a metal pipe. Their effective permittivity εeff sets characteristic impedance Z0, guided wavelength λg and phase velocity, so the same board can hold matching sections, filters and feeds for MIC and MMIC circuits.

You need Chapter 1.1 for Z0 and wavelength, and Chapter 1.3 for line impedance and electrical length.

Three printed line shapes

A microstrip is a strip conductor of width W on a dielectric substrate of thickness h, with a continuous ground plane on the other side. The dielectric has relative permittivity εr (dimensionless). Fields travel in the substrate and in the air above the strip. Because the two media differ, the wave is quasi-TEM: it looks like TEM at low frequency, but a small longitudinal field remains.

A stripline is a strip buried in a homogeneous dielectric between two ground planes. All fields stay in one dielectric, so it supports pure TEM and εeff = εr. A coplanar waveguide (CPW) places a centre strip and two side grounds on the same top surface; the fields cross the narrow gaps. CPW needs no backside ground for the microwave path, which helps in monolithic circuits. Some syllabi call these printed transmission lines.

Field confinement improves as frequency rises. At low frequency the fields spread several substrate thicknesses into air; at high frequency they crowd under the strip. εeff is the weighted permittivity seen by the wave. It lies between 1 (air) and εr: 1 ≤ εeff ≤ εr. It sets phase velocity vp = c/√εeff and guided wavelength λg = λ0/√εeff, where c is the speed of light in vacuum and λ0 the free-space wavelength.

Effective permittivity and microstrip design

For W/h ≥ 1, a standard approximate design pair is:

εeff = (εr + 1)/2 + (εr − 1)/(2√(1 + 12h/W))

εr is the substrate relative permittivity (dimensionless); h is substrate thickness in metres; W is strip width in metres; εeff is the effective relative permittivity (dimensionless).

Z0 = 120π / (√εeff × (W/h + 1.393 + 0.667 ln(W/h + 1.444))) Ω

Z0 is characteristic impedance in ohms; 120π Ω ≈ 377 Ω; ln is the natural logarithm; W/h is dimensionless. Use W and h in the same unit.

The design steps are simple.

  1. Choose εr and h from the substrate data sheet, and choose the target Z0, often 50 Ω.
  2. Guess W/h, then compute εeff from the first formula.
  3. Compute Z0 from the second formula. If Z0 is too high, widen the strip; if too low, narrow it (a wider strip has more capacitance per metre). Repeat until Z0 is close to the target.
  4. Check that W is manufacturable and that loss and dispersion are acceptable.

Guessing can be skipped. For a target Z0 the standard synthesis formula (for W/h < 2) is

W/h = 8 exp(A)/(exp(2A) − 2)

with A = (Z0/60)√((εr + 1)/2) + ((εr − 1)/(εr + 1))(0.23 + 0.11/εr). For 50 Ω on εr = 4.4, A = 1.530 and W/h = 1.91.

For a narrow strip (W/h ≤ 1) use Z0 = (60/√εeff) ln(8h/W + W/(4h)) Ω, with 0.04(1 − W/h)² added inside the bracket of the εeff formula; do not use the wide-strip formula outside its range. In stripline, εeff = εr and a wider strip lowers Z0. In CPW, Z0 falls as the gap narrows or W rises.

Loss, dispersion and use in MIC and MMIC

Three loss mechanisms matter. Conductor loss comes from the finite conductivity of copper. At high frequency current flows in a thin skin depth δs = √(2/(ω μ σ)), where ω = 2πf is angular frequency, μ is permeability and σ is conductivity. Resistance per metre rises roughly as √f. Dielectric loss comes from the substrate loss tangent tan δ (δ here is the loss angle, not the skin depth); it rises with frequency and with field in the substrate. Radiation loss comes from the open top of microstrip and from bends and discontinuities; it grows at high frequency and on thick, low-εr substrates.

Dispersion means εeff, Z0 and vp change with frequency. Microstrip is not pure TEM, so short pulses and wideband signals distort; the effect grows as h becomes a noticeable fraction of λg. Skin effect and surface roughness add conductor loss. A good design keeps h small, uses a low-loss substrate, and avoids sharp discontinuities.

Printed lines are the wiring of microwave integrated circuits (MICs) and monolithic microwave integrated circuits (MMICs). A MIC puts separate chips and printed passives on a substrate; an MMIC puts transistors and lines on one semiconductor die. Microstrip and CPW make matching sections, filters, couplers and bias lines. Stripline gives good shielding when both sides can be grounded. MMIC technology belongs with microwave devices, and patch antennas are printed on the same kinds of substrate.

Exam traps

  • Using εr instead of εeff in λg and vp; the wave sees air plus dielectric, so it travels faster than a wave in bulk dielectric.
  • Calling microstrip pure TEM. It is quasi-TEM and has dispersion; stripline in a homogeneous dielectric is the pure-TEM printed line.
  • Mixing up microstrip and stripline: microstrip has air above the strip; stripline has the strip buried between two ground planes.
  • Putting W and h into a ratio formula with different units, or treating W/h as if it had units. Only the dimensionless ratio enters.
  • Ignoring skin effect, dielectric loss and radiation loss at high frequency; conductor loss rises roughly as √f and radiation grows on thick, low-εr substrates.

1.7 Rectangular and circular waveguides: TE and TM modes

A hollow metal pipe a few centimetres across carries microwave power with very little loss. It cannot carry a plain wave like a coaxial cable: the field must fit the metal walls, so only certain patterns — modes — survive, and each mode has a cutoff frequency below which it dies away. The pipe dimensions and two integers called mode indices set that cutoff, the guide wavelength and the wave velocities. Waveguides feed horn antennas and form the bench hardware of every microwave laboratory.

You need: the propagation constant, phase velocity and wavelength of 1.1, plus basic electromagnetics (Maxwell's equations and the boundary condition on a conductor).

Why a hollow pipe has modes

Inside a hollow metal guide a wave cannot have any pattern it likes: the tangential electric field must vanish on a perfect conductor, so the field across the guide is pinned at the walls and settles into a standing-wave pattern, like a rope fixed at both ends.

Only patterns that fit exactly survive. Two integers label each one: m counts half-cycles of field variation along the broad wall a (metres), n along the narrow wall b (metres). That gives two families:

  • TE modes (transverse electric): Ez = 0, Hz ≠ 0 — no electric field along the guide axis.
  • TM modes (transverse magnetic): Hz = 0, Ez ≠ 0 — no magnetic field along the axis.
  • TEM is impossible: it needs two separate conductors, as in a coaxial cable, not one hollow pipe.

Every mode has a cutoff frequency fc. Below fc the fields decay exponentially along the guide and carry no real power: the mode is evanescent. Above fc it propagates. So a waveguide is a high-pass device for each mode, with no upper limit.

For TE10 the electric field runs across the guide parallel to the narrow wall, strongest at the centre of the broad wall and zero at its side walls. Wall current density is Js = n̂ × H, so current lines cross the magnetic field lines at right angles. At the centre of the broad wall the TE10 current runs only along the guide, so a slot cut lengthwise there cuts no current: that is why slotted measuring lines work.

Rectangular guide: cutoff, guide wavelength, velocities

For a guide of broad wall a and narrow wall b filled with a dielectric of relative permittivity εr, the cutoff frequency of the TEmn or TMmn mode is:

fc = (c / (2√εr)) · √((m/a)² + (n/b)²)

c = 3×10⁸ m/s; a, b in metres; m, n the mode indices; εr = 1 for air filling. TM modes need m ≥ 1 and n ≥ 1.

The cutoff wavelength follows directly, and the wavelength actually measured inside the guide is longer than in free space:

λc = 2 / √((m/a)² + (n/b)²)      λg = λ0 / √(1 − (fc/f)²)

λc, λg, λ0 in metres; λ0 = c/(f√εr) at operating frequency f in hertz.

Two velocities describe the wave. Phase velocity vp is the speed of a point of constant phase; group velocity vg is the speed of the energy and of the modulation:

vp = c / (√εr · √(1 − (fc/f)²))      vg = (c/√εr) · √(1 − (fc/f)²)      vp·vg = c²/εr

vp, vg in m/s. Near cutoff vp is far above c and vg near zero; far above cutoff both approach c/√εr.

ηTE = η / √(1 − (fc/f)²)      ηTM = η · √(1 − (fc/f)²)

η = 377/√εr Ω is the filling's intrinsic impedance; ηTE is above η, ηTM below.

The lowest cutoff belongs to TE10: fc10 = c/(2a√εr). It is the dominant mode. Next come TE20 at c/(a√εr) and TE01 at c/(2b√εr); choosing b < a/2 keeps TE01 above TE20 and gives a single-mode band from fc10 to 2fc10. TE11 and TM11 share a higher cutoff, (c/(2√εr))·√(1/a² + 1/b²); TM modes need m ≥ 1 and n ≥ 1. Above the single-mode band several modes travel at different velocities and the signal smears.

Circular waveguide

A circular guide of radius a (metres) uses cylindrical field patterns and Bessel functions in place of sines. Its cutoff is set by a Bessel root:

fc = p · c / (2π a √εr)

p is the Bessel root for the chosen TEnm or TMnm mode, from the table; a in metres; c = 3×10⁸ m/s.

ModeBessel root pNote
TE111.841dominant mode
TM012.405first TM mode
TE213.054next TE mode
TE013.832low loss at high f
TM113.832same cutoff as TE01

TE11 is dominant, but two polarisations share its cutoff, so a slight bend or ovality makes the pattern rotate. Circular guide is used where the joint must turn, as in rotating radar joints and dish feeds, and its TE01 mode has wall loss that falls as frequency rises, which suits long runs. Rectangular guide stays the bench default: clean mode spacing and polarisation fixed by the walls.

Worked example

Question. An air-filled rectangular waveguide has a = 2.286 cm and b = 1.016 cm. (a) Find the cutoff frequencies of TE10, TE20 and TE01 and the single-mode band. (b) At 10 GHz find λg, vp, vg and the wave impedance of TE10. Take c = 3×10⁸ m/s and η = 377 Ω.

  1. a = 0.02286 m, b = 0.01016 m; air filling, so εr = 1.
  2. fc10 = c/(2a) = 3×10⁸/(2 × 0.02286) = 6.56×10⁹ Hz = 6.56 GHz.
  3. fc20 = 2fc10 = 13.12 GHz.
  4. fc01 = c/(2b) = 3×10⁸/(2 × 0.01016) = 14.76 GHz.
  5. Order 6.56 < 13.12 < 14.76 GHz, so the single-mode band is 6.56 to 13.12 GHz.
  6. λ0 = c/f = 3×10⁸/(10×10⁹) = 0.03 m = 3 cm.
  7. fc10/f = 0.656, so 1 − 0.656² = 0.5694 and √0.5694 = 0.7546.

  10. 10 GHz lies in the single-mode band, so only TE10 propagates.

Answer. fc10 = 6.56 GHz, fc20 = 13.12 GHz, fc01 = 14.76 GHz, single-mode band 6.56 to 13.12 GHz; at 10 GHz λg = 3.98 cm, vp = 3.98×10⁸ m/s, vg = 2.26×10⁸ m/s and ZTE = 500 Ω.

Exam traps

  • Using λ0 where λg is needed. Phase shift along a guide is 2π per λg, not per λ0, so a guide section is longer than the free-space path for the same shift.
  • Saying a mode 'does not exist' below cutoff. It is evanescent: the field decays as e−αz and carries no real power.
  • Writing a TM mode with m = 0 or n = 0. Ez ∝ sin(mπx/a)·sin(nπy/b) vanishes if either index is zero, so TM11 is the lowest TM mode.
  • Swapping a and b. The broad wall a sets fc10; using b is wrong by more than a factor of two for standard X-band guide.
  • Forgetting √εr. A filling of εr = 4 halves every fc and changes λ0 to c/(f√εr).

1.8 Microwave resonators and Q factor

A microwave resonator stores electric and magnetic energy and returns it at one frequency, f0. Energy sloshes between a capacitor and an inductor, or in the fields of a cavity or line section. Q, the quality factor, compares energy stored with energy lost per radian. High Q gives a narrow response; low Q gives a broad one. Resonators sit inside filters, oscillators, duplexers and frequency meters — every block that picks one frequency and rejects the rest.

You need: 1.1 for distributed L and C, 1.3 for the input impedance of shorted and open lines, and 1.7 for waveguide modes.

Series and parallel resonance

A series RLC circuit has impedance Z = R + j(ωL − 1/(ωC)), where R is resistance in ohms, L inductance in henries, C capacitance in farads and ω = 2πf the angular frequency in rad/s. At one frequency the reactances cancel: ω0L = 1/(ω0C). The impedance drops to R and current peaks. That is resonance.

f0 = 1/(2π√(LC))

f0 is the resonant frequency in hertz, L is inductance in henries, C is capacitance in farads.

A parallel RLC circuit is the dual. At the same f0 its admittance is minimum, so impedance and voltage peak. Both circuits are resonators: they store energy and give it back.

The sharpness of that peak is Q, the quality factor: Q = ω0 × (energy stored) ÷ (power lost). A lossless circuit has infinite Q; a lossy one has a rounded peak. Q also equals f0 divided by the 3 dB bandwidth.

Q = ω0 W / Ploss

ω0 = 2πf0 in rad/s, W is the energy stored in joules, Ploss is the power lost in watts.

For a series circuit Q = ω0L/R = (1/R)√(L/C); for a parallel circuit Q = Rp/(ω0L) = ω0RpC, with Rp the shunt resistance in ohms. A large series R spoils Q; a small shunt Rp spoils it. Every resonator is an L–C pair plus one loss resistance.

Unloaded, loaded and external Q

A real resonator is coupled to a source and a load, so three Q values describe it. Q0, the unloaded Q, counts only internal loss (metal, dielectric, radiation). Qe, the external Q, counts only the power leaving through the coupling. QL, the loaded Q, counts both; they combine like parallel resistors.

1/QL = 1/Q0 + 1/Qe

QL, Q0 and Qe are dimensionless quality factors: loaded, unloaded and external.

QL = f0/BW

f0 is the resonant frequency in hertz and BW is the 3 dB (half-power) bandwidth in hertz — the width over which the response stays above 1/√2 = 0.707 of its peak.

A QL of 100 at 2 GHz means a 20 MHz bandwidth. After the drive is removed the field amplitude decays as exp(−t/τ) with τ = 2QL/ω0 (the stored energy decays twice as fast). The coupling coefficient g = Q0/Qe says how hard the resonator is coupled: g < 1 is undercoupled, g = 1 critically coupled (QL = Q0/2, all power absorbed at f0), and g > 1 overcoupled.

Resonators at microwave frequencies

Below about 1 GHz a resonator is a coil and a capacitor. At 10 GHz the needed L and C are so small that the coil is a fraction of a millimetre and its resistance destroys Q. Microwave design therefore uses distributed resonators, where a line or metal box stores the energy.

λ/2 resonator. A line of length l = λ/2 shorted at its far end shows Zin = 0 at its input at f0: a series resonance. The same length open at the far end shows Zin = ∞: a parallel resonance. Resonance repeats at every multiple of f0.

λ/4 resonator. A shorted stub of length λ/4 shows infinite input impedance at its open end — it behaves as a parallel resonant circuit. A physical short becomes an electrical open. An open λ/4 stub is the dual and shows a series resonance.

fmnp = (c/2)√((m/a)² + (n/b)² + (p/d)²)

c is the speed of light in the filling medium in m/s, a, b, d are the cavity width, height and length in metres, and m, n, p are the mode indices (TEmnp or TMmnp).

A closed metal box is a waveguide with walls at both ends, so only certain field patterns fit: it is a rectangular cavity resonator with these frequencies. Its Q of several thousand comes from the large volume-to-surface ratio, and it falls as the surface resistance Rs = √(πfμ/σ) grows, where f is frequency in hertz, μ the permeability of the metal in H/m and σ its conductivity in S/m. For the TE10p mode of a cavity of width a, height b and length d, Q0 = (kad)³bη / (2π²Rs) × 1/(2p²a³b + 2bd³ + p²a³d + ad³), with k = 2πf0/c and η = 377 Ω.

  • Dielectric resonator: a ceramic puck with εr of a few tens and Q set mainly by its loss tangent, Qd ≈ 1/tan δ, often several thousand. Small enough to sit on a printed board.
  • Microstrip resonator: a printed λ/2 open line. Q is modest, tens to a few hundred, because the fields touch lossy dielectric and thin metal.

Worked example

Question. A bandpass resonator is centred at f0 = 2 GHz. Its 3 dB bandwidth measured on a network analyser is 20 MHz. (a) Find the loaded Q. (b) The unloaded Q of the resonator alone is 500. Find the external Q and comment on the coupling.

  1. QL = f0/BW = (2 × 10⁹ Hz) ÷ (20 × 10⁶ Hz) = 100.
  2. Use 1/QL = 1/Q0 + 1/Qe, so 1/Qe = 1/QL − 1/Q0.
  3. 1/Qe = 1/100 − 1/500 = 0.010 − 0.002 = 0.008.
  4. Qe = 1 ÷ 0.008 = 125.
  5. Check the fractional bandwidth: BW/f0 = 20 MHz ÷ 2000 MHz = 1%, and 1/QL = 0.01, so the two agree.
  6. The coupling coefficient is g = Q0/Qe = 500/125 = 4, so the resonator is overcoupled: external loss dominates and QL (100) is well below Q0. Loosening the coupling raises Qe; at g = 1 (Qe = 500) QL would be 250 and the bandwidth 8 MHz.

Answer. QL = 100 and Qe = 125; g = 4, so the resonator is overcoupled.

Takeaways

  1. A transmission line is described completely by four per-metre quantities, and from them Z₀, α, β and vp follow.
  2. When R/L equals G/C the line is distortionless, so attenuation is flat and a pulse keeps its shape.
  3. Any load mismatch sends back a wave; Γ measures it, S and return loss report it, and both are read directly from |Γ|.
  4. Input impedance depends on load and electrical length, so a short looks like an inductor, an open like a capacitor, and a λ/2 section repeats the load.
  5. A quarter-wave transformer matches a real load only near one frequency, while a stub cancels reactance at a chosen point on the line.
  6. The Smith chart is just the Γ plane with impedance circles drawn on it, so every matching step is a rotation and a circle crossing.
  7. Printed lines such as microstrip and stripline trade compactness and easy fabrication against loss and radiation, with ε_eff setting Z₀ and λ.
  8. A hollow waveguide passes a mode only above its cutoff, and the mode indices plus the metal dimensions fix fc, λg and vp.
  9. A resonator's Q ties stored energy to loss; higher Q means a narrower bandwidth and a longer ring-down time.
  10. Matching buys power transfer and low reflection, but every matching network has a finite bandwidth that must be checked.

Long answers usually combine a derivation of the line equations with a numerical impedance, matching or waveguide calculation, and ask you to justify the choice between coax, printed line and waveguide for a given band. Every model answer below ends with a numerical check.

Understand Define the distributed parameters of a transmission line and derive the telegrapher's equations from a short element of length Δz. Solve them for a sinusoidal source to obtain Z₀ and γ. 13 marks

Points to cover. Draw the element with series RΔz, LΔz and shunt GΔz, CΔz; Apply KVL round the series path and KCL at the shunt node, divide by Δz, let Δz → 0; Write the two first-order equations; differentiate to get second-order wave equations; Assume V = V₀e−γz and state γ = α + jβ = √((R + jωL)(G + jωC)); State Z₀ = √((R + jωL)/(G + jωC)) and the lossless limits √(L/C) and β = ω√(LC); Give units: R, L, G, C per metre; α in Np/m, β in rad/m, Z₀ in ohms.

Model answer.

  1. Distributed parameters. A line is two conductors whose length is comparable with the wavelength, so its resistance, inductance, leakage and capacitance are spread along it. Per metre they are R (Ω/m, metal resistance), L (H/m, magnetic field around the conductors), G (S/m, leakage through the dielectric) and C (F/m, electric field between the conductors).
  2. Model a slice of length Δz as series RΔz and LΔz followed by shunt GΔz and CΔz, with voltage v(z, t) and current i(z, t) at its input and v(z + Δz, t), i(z + Δz, t) at its output.
    i(z) → ──RΔz──LΔz──┬───── → i(z + Δz)
  v(z)              GΔz  CΔz      v(z + Δz)
        ────────────┴─────
  3. Kirchhoff's voltage law round the series path and Kirchhoff's current law at the shunt node give
    v(z, t) − RΔz i(z, t) − LΔz ∂i/∂t − v(z + Δz, t) = 0
i(z, t) − GΔz v(z + Δz, t) − CΔz ∂v/∂t − i(z + Δz, t) = 0
  4. Divide by Δz and let Δz → 0. These are the telegrapher's equations; in phasor form (∂/∂t → jω) they become ordinary equations in z:
    ∂v/∂z = −R i − L ∂i/∂t        ∂i/∂z = −G v − C ∂v/∂t
dV/dz = −(R + jωL) I          dI/dz = −(G + jωC) V
  5. Differentiate the first phasor equation and substitute the second:
    d²V/dz² = −(R + jωL) dI/dz = (R + jωL)(G + jωC) V = γ² V
γ = α + jβ = √((R + jωL)(G + jωC))
  6. The general solution is a forward and a backward wave. Putting it into dV/dz = −(R + jωL)I gives the current, and the ratio of voltage to current of the forward wave is Z0:
    V(z) = V⁺e−γz + V⁻eγz
I(z) = (V⁺e−γz − V⁻eγz) / Z0
Z0 = (R + jωL)/γ = √((R + jωL)/(G + jωC))
  7. Units and meaning: α in Np/m sets how fast the wave shrinks (8.686 dB per neper), β in rad/m sets the wavelength λ = 2π/β and the phase velocity vp = ω/β, and Z0 is in ohms. The minus sign on the backward current means that wave carries current the other way.
  8. Lossless limits: with R = G = 0, Z0 = √(L/C) is real, α = 0, β = ω√(LC) and vp = 1/√(LC). For a low-loss line α ≈ R/(2Z0) + GZ0/2.

Understand What is waveform distortion on a line? Derive the condition for a distortionless line and explain how loading coils approximate it. 10 marks

Points to cover. Distortion = different frequencies attenuated differently and travelling at different speeds, so a pulse spreads; α = Re(γ) depends on ω in general; β/ω also varies, giving dispersion; Distortionless condition R/L = G/C; Then α = √(RG) independent of ω and vp = 1/√(LC) independent of ω; Loading adds series inductance L to bring R/L down towards G/C; Remark that this works for audio pairs, not for microwave lines.

Model answer.

  1. Distortion. A pulse is a sum of sinusoids. It keeps its shape only if every component is attenuated by the same factor and delayed by the same time. On a general line α = Re γ depends on ω (amplitude distortion) and β is not proportional to ω, so vp = ω/β changes with frequency (phase or delay distortion). The pulse arrives smeared.
  2. Factor L and C out of γ²:
    γ² = (R + jωL)(G + jωC) = LC (R/L + jω)(G/C + jω)
  3. If R/L = G/C = k, the two brackets are equal and the square root is exact:
    γ = √(LC) (k + jω)
α = k√(LC) = R√(C/L) = √(RG)      (independent of ω)
β = ω√(LC)                          (proportional to ω)
vp = ω/β = 1/√(LC)                  (independent of ω)
  4. The characteristic impedance is real and constant too:
    Z0 = √((R + jωL)/(G + jωC)) = √(L(k + jω) / (C(k + jω))) = √(L/C)
  5. So the distortionless condition is R/L = G/C (equivalently RC = LG). Every frequency is attenuated by e−αl with the same α and arrives after the same delay l√(LC); the pulse shrinks but keeps its shape.
  6. Why loading coils. In a real telephone pair R/L is much larger than G/C (copper resistance is large, leakage is tiny). Lowering R is costly, and raising G adds loss, so instead L is raised: loading coils are series inductors inserted at regular intervals, which raise the average L per metre and bring R/L down towards G/C.
  7. Numerical check with the line R = 0.1 Ω/m, L = 0.25 µH/m, G = 10 µS/m, C = 100 pF/m:
    QuantityAs builtLoaded to L = RC/G
    R/L (s⁻¹)0.1/0.25×10⁻⁶ = 4×10⁵0.1/1.0×10⁻⁶ = 1×10⁵
    G/C (s⁻¹)1×10⁵1×10⁵ (equal: distortionless)
    α (Np/m)0.00125 at 10 MHz, √(RG) = 0.0010 at low f√(RG) = 0.0010 at every f
    Z050 Ω√(10⁻⁶/10⁻¹⁰) = 100 Ω
  8. Remark: loading works for audio-band pairs, where lumped coils every kilometre or so look continuous to the signal. It is not used on microwave lines, where the coils would themselves be electrically long; there, low-loss dielectrics and wide conductors keep α small instead.
  9. Check: with L = 1.0 µH/m, R√(C/L) = 0.1 × √(10⁻¹⁰/10⁻⁶) = 0.1 × 0.01 = 0.0010 Np/m and G√(L/C) = 10⁻⁵ × 100 = 0.0010 Np/m. The two forms of α agree, as they must when R/L = G/C.
Understand Define reflection coefficient, VSWR and return loss for a line terminated in ZL. Derive the relations between them and state the values for open, short and matched loads. 10 marks

Points to cover. Γ = (ZL − Z₀)/(ZL + Z₀), generally complex; |Γ| ≤ 1; Γ = +1 open, −1 short, 0 matched; With d from the load, V(d) = V⁺(ejβd + ΓL e−jβd); adjacent maxima λ/2 apart, a maximum and the next minimum λ/4 apart; S = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|) and |Γ| = (S − 1)/(S + 1); Return loss = −20 log|Γ| dB; matched gives infinite return loss; Power delivered to load = incident power × (1 − |Γ|²).

Model answer.

  1. Reflection coefficient. At the load the voltage and current are the sum of an incident wave V⁺ and a reflected wave V⁻. The load forces V/I = ZL, while each wave alone has V/I = ±Z0:
    V(0) = V⁺ + V⁻          I(0) = (V⁺ − V⁻)/Z0
ZL = Z0 (V⁺ + V⁻)/(V⁺ − V⁻)
ΓL = V⁻/V⁺ = (ZL − Z0)/(ZL + Z0)
  2. ΓL is complex in general. For a passive load |ΓL| ≤ 1. At a distance d from the load towards the generator, Γ(d) = ΓL e−j2βd: on a lossless line only its phase turns.
  3. Standing wave and VSWR. With d measured from the load,
    V(d) = V⁺(ejβd + ΓL e−jβd)
|V(d)| = |V⁺| √(1 + |Γ|² + 2|Γ| cos(θ − 2βd))
  4. The maximum |V⁺|(1 + |Γ|) occurs where the two waves are in phase and the minimum |V⁺|(1 − |Γ|) where they are opposite. Adjacent maxima are λ/2 apart; a maximum and the next minimum are λ/4 apart. So VSWR S = Vmax/Vmin = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|), and inverting, |Γ| = (S − 1)/(S + 1).

    pure reactance jX|Γ| = 1∞0 dB

Understand Explain TE and TM modes in a rectangular waveguide. Derive the cutoff frequency of the TE₁₀ mode and explain why it is called the dominant mode. 13 marks

Points to cover. TE: Ez = 0, Hz ≠ 0; TM: Hz = 0, Ez ≠ 0; TEM cannot exist in a single-conductor hollow guide; Boundary conditions give kc² = (mπ/a)² + (nπ/b)²; fc = (c/2)√((m/a)² + (n/b)²); TE₁₀ has the lowest fc = c/2a for a > b; Below fc, γ is real, the wave decays as e−αz, no real power flows; Sketch the field pattern and the surface current on the broad wall.

Model answer.

  1. Modes. Inside a hollow guide of broad wall a and narrow wall b the fields must satisfy Maxwell's equations and make the tangential E zero on the walls. Only discrete patterns fit. TE (transverse electric) modes have Ez = 0 and Hz ≠ 0; TM (transverse magnetic) modes have Hz = 0 and Ez ≠ 0. TEM cannot exist, because it needs two separate conductors at different potentials.
  2. For TE modes solve the wave equation for Hz with fields varying as e−γz:
    ∂²Hz/∂x² + ∂²Hz/∂y² + kc² Hz = 0,     kc² = γ² + k²,   k = ω√(με)
Hz = H0 cos(mπx/a) cos(nπy/b) e−γz
  3. The cosines are forced by the walls: Ey ∝ ∂Hz/∂x must vanish at x = 0 and x = a, and Ex ∝ ∂Hz/∂y at y = 0 and y = b. So kx = mπ/a and ky = nπ/b, with m, n = 0, 1, 2, … but not both zero.
  4. The propagation constant then follows:
    kc² = (mπ/a)² + (nπ/b)²
γ = √(kc² − k²)
  5. If k < kc, γ is real: the field decays as e−αz and no real power flows (evanescent). If k > kc, γ = jβ is imaginary and the mode propagates. The boundary k = kc is cutoff:
  6. 2πfc √(με) = √((mπ/a)² + (nπ/b)²)
fc = (1/(2√(με))) √((m/a)² + (n/b)²) = (c/2) √((m/a)² + (n/b)²) in air
  7. TE10: m = 1, n = 0 gives fc10 = c/(2a). Its only field components are Ey, Hx and Hz, with Ey = E0 sin(πx/a): zero at the side walls and largest at the centre, the same at every height y.
  8. Why dominant. With a > b, every other mode has a larger kc: TE20 has 2π/a, TE01 has π/b > π/a, and TM modes need m, n ≥ 1, so TM11 has √((π/a)² + (π/b)²). TE10 therefore has the lowest cutoff and is the only mode between fc10 and the next cutoff, so a guide used in that band carries one known pattern with one λg and one wave impedance.
  9. Surface current. Js = n̂ × H. On the broad walls the current has a transverse part that is zero at the centre line x = a/2, so a narrow longitudinal slot there cuts no current and does not radiate; that is how slotted measuring lines work.
  10. Numbers for the standard X-band guide, a = 2.286 cm, b = 1.016 cm:
    ModeWorkingfc
    TE103×10⁸ / (2 × 0.02286)6.56 GHz
    TE203×10⁸ / 0.0228613.12 GHz
    TE013×10⁸ / (2 × 0.01016)14.76 GHz

  11. Check: the single-mode band is 6.56 to 13.12 GHz, a 2 : 1 range because b < a/2 keeps TE01 above TE20; at 10 GHz λg = 3 cm/√(1 − 0.656²) = 3.98 cm.

  1. The three lines differ in where their field sits, and every other difference follows from that.

  1. The R, L, G, C model treats the four constants as fixed numbers. At high frequency each of them changes, and new effects appear that the model does not contain.

Glossary

Attenuation constant
α, the real part of γ, in nepers per metre; it sets how fast the wave shrinks.
Characteristic impedance
The ratio of forward voltage to forward current on a line, Z₀ = √((R + jωL)/(G + jωC)).
Coplanar waveguide
A centre strip with ground strips on the same face of the substrate.
Critical coupling
Coupling at which the external Q equals the unloaded Q, so the loaded Q is half the unloaded Q and all power is absorbed at resonance.
Cutoff frequency
The frequency below which a waveguide mode cannot propagate.
Distortionless line
A line with R/L = G/C, so all frequencies are attenuated equally and travel at the same speed.
Distributed parameters
Per-metre values R, L, G and C that describe a line whose length is comparable with a wavelength.
Dominant mode
The mode with the lowest cutoff frequency in a waveguide; TE₁₀ in a rectangular guide and TE₁₁ in a circular one.
Effective permittivity
The permittivity a printed line behaves as if it had when its field is partly in air.
Electrical length
Line length expressed as βl in radians or as a fraction of a wavelength.
Group velocity
The speed at which the envelope of a modulated signal travels, vg = dω/dβ.
Guide wavelength
The distance between phase fronts inside a waveguide, λg = λ₀/√(1 − (fc/f)²).
Input impedance
The impedance seen looking into a line at a given distance from the load.
Microstrip
A printed strip on one side of a dielectric slab with a ground plane on the other.
Mismatch loss
The loss of delivered power caused by reflection, −10 log₁₀(1 − |Γ|²) dB.
Phase constant
β, the imaginary part of γ, in radians per metre; it sets wavelength along the line.
Phase velocity
The speed at which one frequency's phase travels, vp = ω/β.
Propagation constant
γ = α + jβ, the complex number giving attenuation and phase change per metre.
Quality factor
Q = ω₀ × stored energy ÷ lost power, which fixes a resonator's bandwidth.
Quarter-wave transformer
A λ/4 line of impedance √(Z₀ZL) that matches a real load to a line at one frequency.
Reflection coefficient
Γ = (ZL − Z₀)/(ZL + Z₀), the complex ratio of reflected to incident voltage at the load.
Return loss
−20 log₁₀|Γ| in dB; the larger the value, the better the match.
Smith chart
A polar chart of reflection coefficient on which normalized impedance and admittance are plotted.
Standing wave
A fixed pattern of voltage maxima and minima formed by forward and reflected waves.
Stripline
A printed strip buried between two ground planes in a dielectric.
Stub
A short length of line, open or shorted, placed in shunt or series to cancel reactance.
Telegrapher's equations
The coupled first-order equations for voltage and current along a line.
Transmission line
A pair of conductors, or a conductor over a ground plane, that guides a wave from source to load.
VSWR
Voltage standing wave ratio, S = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|), between 1 and infinity.
