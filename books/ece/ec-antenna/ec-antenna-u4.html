4.1 S-parameters and the scattering matrix

At microwave frequencies a component is not measured with a multimeter. Power enters and leaves through ports, and a port is a transmission-line cross-section with a fixed reference impedance Z₀, usually 50 Ω. The scattering matrix collects the ratios of outgoing to incoming waves. It describes amplifiers, filters, couplers and antennas near 1–30 GHz, where open and short circuits are hard to apply. Engineers read S-parameters from a vector network analyser and use them in design.

You need: travelling waves, reflection coefficient and VSWR from reflection coefficient, standing waves and VSWR.

Waves in and out of a port

At low frequency we use voltage V and current I. Above about 1 GHz the connecting lines behave as transmission lines, so a wave picture is easier. At port i, define aᵢ as the wave travelling into the component and bᵢ as the wave travelling out. Both are normalised so |aᵢ|² and |bᵢ|² are powers in watts.

aᵢ = (Vᵢ + Z₀Iᵢ) / (2√Z₀),   bᵢ = (Vᵢ − Z₀Iᵢ) / (2√Z₀)

Vᵢ and Iᵢ are the total voltage and current at port i; Z₀ is the real reference impedance, usually 50 Ω; aᵢ is the incident wave and bᵢ is the outgoing wave.

For N ports the outgoing waves are linear combinations of the incoming waves: b = S a. The element Sᵢⱼ = bᵢ / aⱼ with every port except j terminated in a matched load, so aₖ = 0 for k ≠ j. This ratio is a complex number: its magnitude gives amplitude and its angle gives phase. The array S is the scattering matrix; some syllabi call it the scattering matrix.

The definition exists because a matched load is easy to build and repeat at microwave frequencies; a perfect open or short is not. S-parameters also connect directly to power, so return loss, gain and isolation follow from |Sᵢⱼ|.

The four S-parameters of a two-port

For a two-port, match port 2 (a₂ = 0) and drive port 1. Then b₁ = S₁₁a₁ and b₂ = S₂₁a₁. So S₁₁ is the input reflection coefficient and S₂₁ is the forward voltage gain. Match port 1 (a₁ = 0) and drive port 2: b₁ = S₁₂a₂ and b₂ = S₂₂a₂. S₂₂ is the output reflection coefficient; S₁₂ is the reverse voltage gain.

  • S₁₁ = input reflection coefficient with port 2 matched.
  • S₂₁ = forward voltage gain with port 2 matched.
  • S₁₂ = reverse voltage gain with port 1 matched.
  • S₂₂ = output reflection coefficient with port 1 matched.

The signal-flow graph shows the same four paths. A wave a₁ can reflect at port 1 through S₁₁ or cross to b₂ through S₂₁. A wave a₂ can cross to b₁ through S₁₂ or reflect at port 2 through S₂₂.

Rules that catch errors

Reciprocity: a passive component with no ferrite or active material has S = Sᵀ, so S₁₂ = S₂₁. Losslessness: a lossless component obeys S†S = I, where S† is the conjugate transpose and I is the identity matrix. This means the total power leaving equals the total power entering. Matched: all diagonal terms Sᵢᵢ = 0.

The reference plane matters. If you move a port outward by a line length l, the phase of every S-parameter touching that port changes by e^{−jβl}, where β = 2π/λ is the phase constant and λ is the guided wavelength. S-parameters also depend on frequency and on the reference impedance Z₀. A 50 Ω S-matrix is not valid for a 75 Ω system without renormalisation. Report the frequency and Z₀ with every S-parameter.

For a matched lossless reciprocal two-port, |S₂₁| = 1 and S₁₁ = S₂₂ = 0; a real filter is close to this only in its passband.

Worked example

Question. A 50 Ω two-port at 10 GHz has S₁₁ = 0.2∠0°, S₂₁ = 0.9∠−30°, S₁₂ = 0, S₂₂ = 0.1∠0°. Find (a) return loss at port 1, (b) insertion loss, (c) input VSWR when port 2 is matched, and (d) reverse isolation.

  1. Port 2 is matched, so a₂ = 0. The input reflection coefficient is Γin = S₁₁ = 0.2∠0°, hence |S₁₁| = 0.2.
  2. Return loss: RL = −20 log₁₀(0.2) = −20 × (−0.69897) = 13.9794 dB ≈ 13.98 dB.
  3. Forward magnitude |S₂₁| = 0.9. Insertion loss: IL = −20 log₁₀(0.9) = −20 × (−0.04576) = 0.9151 dB ≈ 0.92 dB. The phase −30° does not change loss.
  4. VSWR = (1 + |S₁₁|)/(1 − |S₁₁|) = (1 + 0.2)/(1 − 0.2) = 1.2/0.8 = 1.5.
  5. S₁₂ = 0, so reverse isolation = −20 log₁₀(0) → ∞ dB. This is an ideal unilateral device: no signal travels from port 2 to port 1.

Answer. Return loss = 13.98 dB, insertion loss = 0.92 dB, input VSWR = 1.5, and reverse isolation = ∞ dB (ideal).

Exam traps

  • Using |S₂₁| directly as dB gain. Convert with 20 log₁₀|S₂₁|; a magnitude of 0.9 is not 0.9 dB.
  • Forgetting that all other ports must be matched when measuring Sᵢⱼ. If port 2 is not matched, b₁/a₁ is not S₁₁; it includes the load reflection.
  • Ignoring the reference impedance Z₀ and the reference plane. A 50 Ω S-matrix does not apply directly to a 75 Ω system, and added line length shifts phase.
  • Confusing S₁₂ with S₂₁. S₂₁ is forward transmission; S₁₂ is reverse transmission. A value S₁₂ = 0 means no reverse path, not no forward path.
  • Treating |S₁₁| as VSWR. |S₁₁| is the reflection coefficient magnitude; VSWR = (1 + |S₁₁|)/(1 − |S₁₁|).

4.2 Waveguide tees: E-plane, H-plane and magic tee

A waveguide tee is a three-port junction: one rectangular guide joined to the side of another. It splits power into two paths or combines two into one. The wall you cut decides E-plane or H-plane. A magic tee joins both on one junction: a four-port hybrid with equal split, fixed phase, and two isolated arms. Tees feed antennas, balanced mixers and radar comparators.

You need: the TE₁₀ mode and its field directions (Unit 1), and the scattering matrix of 4.1.

Tees and the branch plane

A tee is a third waveguide joined to the side of a through guide. The through ends are ports 1 and 2; the side guide is port 3. Power into port 3 divides between 1 and 2, and power into 1 and 2 combines into 3. The junction is reciprocal.

In TE₁₀ the electric field E runs across the narrow dimension b. The broad wall is the face of width a.

E-plane tee (series tee): the branch joins the broad wall, so the branch axis is parallel to E and E lies in the plane of the junction. The branch drives the two collinear guides in opposite senses, so ports 1 and 2 are 180° out of phase. The junction adds impedances in series.

H-plane tee (shunt tee): the branch joins the narrow side wall, so the branch axis is perpendicular to E and parallel to H. Ports 1 and 2 come out in phase. The junction behaves like admittances in parallel.

Two facts about the ideal tee: it is lossless and reciprocal, so S = Sᵀ and S†S = I; and a lossless reciprocal three-port cannot be matched at all three ports. Proof: set S11 = S22 = S33 = 0. The diagonal conditions of S†S = I are |S12|²+|S13|²=1, |S12|²+|S23|²=1, |S13|²+|S23|²=1. Adding and halving gives |S12|²+|S13|²+|S23|²=3/2, so each squared magnitude is 1/2, giving |S12|=|S13|=|S23|=1/√2. Rows 1 and 2 orthogonal give S11S21* + S12S22* + S13S23* = 0; with S11=S22=0 this is S13S23*=0. A product of two numbers each of magnitude 1/√2 cannot be zero. Contradiction. So every real tee reflects at some port.

  • Port 3 is the branch; do not renumber it as port 1 in an answer.
  • Split is nominally 3 dB (half the power) to each collinear arm.

Magic tee and its S-matrix

A magic tee is an E-plane tee and an H-plane tee built on one junction. Ports 1 and 2 are collinear, port 3 is the H-arm (narrow-wall branch), port 4 is the E-arm (broad-wall branch).

Ideal behaviour: power into the H-arm splits equally and in phase between ports 1 and 2, with none reaching the E-arm. Power into the E-arm splits equally with 180° between ports 1 and 2, with none reaching the H-arm. All four ports are matched, and ports 1 and 2 are isolated.

Phase reason: the E-arm feeds the collinear guides as opposite paths along the field direction, so contributions subtract at the junction. The H-arm feeds them as parallel paths of the same sign, so they add. Fed at both collinear ports, the tee adds in the H-arm and subtracts in the E-arm.

S = (1/√2) × [0 0 1 1; 0 0 1 −1; 1 1 0 0; 1 −1 0 0]

Rows and columns are ports 1, 2, 3 (H-arm), 4 (E-arm). S13 = S23 = 1/√2 gives the equal in-phase H split. S14 = −S24 = 1/√2 gives the equal 180° E split. The zeros are the four matched ports (S11 = S22 = S33 = S44 = 0) and the two isolated pairs (S12 = S21 = 0 between collinear arms, S34 = S43 = 0 between E and H arms).

  1. Matched junction: set S11 = S22 = S33 = S44 = 0.
  2. Isolated pairs: set S12 = S21 = 0 and S34 = S43 = 0.
  3. Reciprocity (S = Sᵀ): S13 = S31, S14 = S41, S23 = S32, S24 = S42.
  4. Equal in-phase H split: S13 = S23 = 1/√2.
  5. Equal opposite E split: S14 = −S24 = 1/√2.
  6. Check losslessness: row 1 · row 2 = 1/2 − 1/2 = 0, row 1 · row 3 = 0, and each row has magnitude 1. So S†S = I.

Hardware around the junction

  • A mitred E-plane or H-plane bend carries a collinear arm of a tee to its load or antenna; the mitre lowers reflection.
  • A 90° twist is used on an arm when the cross-section of the two guides being joined must be rotated.

Worked example

Question. An ideal magic tee has all four ports matched. A source delivers 10 mW into the H-arm (port 3), and the E-arm and both collinear arms end in matched loads. Find the power leaving each of the other three ports and state the phase relation between ports 1 and 2.

  1. Lossless tee: all 10 mW entering port 3 leaves through ports 1, 2 and 4.
  2. Isolation between E and H arms: S43 = 0, so power at port 4 = |S43|² × 10 mW = 0 mW.
  3. H-arm splits equally in phase: |S13| = |S23| = 1/√2 = 0.707.
  4. Power at port 1 = |S13|² × 10 mW = 0.5 × 10 mW = 5 mW.
  5. Power at port 2 = |S23|² × 10 mW = 0.5 × 10 mW = 5 mW.
  6. Power balance: 5 mW + 5 mW + 0 mW = 10 mW, equal to the input.
  7. S13 and S23 are both +1/√2, so the two output waves are in phase (0° difference).

Exam traps

  • Swapping the arms: the H-arm splits in phase and the E-arm splits 180° out.
  • Treating the tee as a matched three-port. A lossless reciprocal three-port cannot be matched at all ports, so a lossless tee must reflect somewhere.
  • Forgetting isolation: S34 = S43 = 0 between the E and H arms and S12 = S21 = 0 between the collinear arms. Both are needed for the ideal matrix.
  • Feeding equal in-phase sources into ports 1 and 2: they add in the H-arm and cancel in the E-arm.
  • Renumbering ports: keep 1 and 2 collinear, 3 the H-arm, 4 the E-arm, or the signs in the matrix will not match.

4.3 Directional couplers and hybrid rings

A directional coupler is a four-port microwave component that samples a small, known fraction of the wave travelling from port 1 to port 2 and sends it to a coupled port 3. The fourth port, port 4, should receive almost nothing; how little it receives is set by the isolation I, which equals the coupling plus the directivity, I = C + D. A hybrid ring is a four-port ring that splits power equally between two outputs and fixes a 180° phase relation, so it can add or subtract two signals. Both are built from waveguides, microstrip or stripline and are used in power monitoring, balanced mixers, reflectometers and antenna feeds.

You need S-parameters from 4.1 and tee junctions from 4.2.

Ports, coupling, isolation and directivity

Number the ports in the usual way: port 1 is the input, port 2 is the through port, port 3 is the coupled port and port 4 is the isolated port. With all ports matched, S₃₁ is the outgoing wave at port 3 divided by the wave incident at port 1. Its magnitude is small, so the coupling is written as a positive number of decibels.

C = −20 log₁₀ |S₃₁| dB,   I = −20 log₁₀ |S₄₁| dB,   D = I − C = −20 log₁₀ (|S₄₁|/|S₃₁|) dB

S₃₁ and S₄₁ are voltage-wave ratios from the scattering matrix; C is coupling in dB; I is isolation in dB; D is directivity in dB.

A 10 dB coupler has |S₃₁| = 0.316, so 10% of the input power reaches port 3. A 20 dB coupler sends 1%. The through port gets most of the remaining power. The isolated port is not perfect: leakage through it is set by I. Directivity compares the wanted coupled wave with that leakage, so a large D means a clean coupler. Practical waveguide couplers give D of about 20 dB to 40 dB. The coupled wave also has a phase relative to the input; C gives only magnitude. The phase is set by the coupler design and must be checked separately. For a matched, reciprocal coupler the S-matrix is four by four: the diagonal terms are zero in the ideal case, S₂₁ is the through term, S₃₁ is the coupled term and S₄₁ is the isolated term.

Two-hole coupler and the hybrid ring

A two-hole directional coupler uses two small holes, spaced a quarter of a guide wavelength λg apart, in the wall between two waveguides. Power leaks through both holes. In the forward direction the two leaked waves travel equal extra paths and add. In the reverse direction they differ by λg/2 and cancel. This is why a two-hole coupler has directivity: it favours one direction. The full field theory of the holes is not needed for exams; remember the quarter-wave spacing and the add/cancel picture.

A hybrid ring, also called a rat-race, is a closed ring of circumference 3λg/2 with four ports. Number them in order around the ring as 1, 2, 3, 4. Three arcs are λg/4 (between 1-2, 2-3 and 3-4) and one arc is 3λg/4 (between 4 and 1). The 3λg/4 arm is λg/4 longer than the other three, so it adds 180° relative to them. Port 1 is the sum port and port 3 is the difference port. Feed the sum port (port 1): ports 2 and 4 get equal power in phase, and port 3 is isolated. Feed the difference port (port 3): ports 2 and 4 get equal power with 180° between them, and port 1 is isolated. This sum-and-difference action is used in balanced mixers, push-pull amplifiers and monopulse antenna comparators. A branch-line hybrid is the 90° relative: it uses λg/4 arms and gives a 90° phase difference between outputs. A magic tee from 4.2 is another 180° hybrid, but in waveguide form.

Worked example

Question. A directional coupler is fed with 20 dBm at port 1. Its coupling is 10 dB and its directivity is 20 dB. The other ports are matched. Find the power at the coupled port and at the isolated port, in dBm and mW.

  1. Coupling gives P₃ = P₁ − C = 20 dBm − 10 dB = 10 dBm.
  2. Convert the coupled power: P₃ = 10^(10/10) mW = 10 mW.
  3. Isolation is I = C + D = 10 dB + 20 dB = 30 dB.
  4. The isolated-port power is P₄ = P₁ − I = 20 dBm − 30 dB = −10 dBm.
  5. Convert the isolated power: P₄ = 10^(−10/10) mW = 0.1 mW.

Answer. The coupled port delivers 10 dBm (10 mW), and the isolated port delivers −10 dBm (0.1 mW).

Exam traps

  • Coupling is not directivity. Coupling compares port 3 with port 1; directivity compares port 4 leakage with port 3.
  • Use 10 log for power ratios and 20 log only for voltage-wave ratios such as |S|. Mixing them shifts every answer by a factor of 2.
  • The isolated-port level uses I = C + D, not D alone. Subtracting D from the input is a common error.
  • Do not assume port 4 is zero. Real isolation is finite; a 20 dB directivity coupler still leaks.
  • C and I give magnitudes only. The coupled port has a designed phase; skip it and a phase question loses marks.

4.4 Power dividers and attenuators

A power divider takes one microwave input and gives two or more outputs at a set power ratio. An attenuator lowers power by a known number of decibels. Both parts let a designer set signal level without guessing: the divider sets how power is shared, the attenuator sets how much reaches the next stage. They appear in antenna feeds, receiver front ends, test benches and phased arrays. The key ideas are matched ports, isolation between outputs, and adding decibels through a chain. Splits and losses are ratios of power, so count them in decibels of power, not in decibels of voltage.

You need: S-parameters for port waves and waveguide tees for the basic tee split.

T-junction and resistive dividers

A T-junction is a three-port made by joining three lines or waveguides. The input power P1 splits between two arms. An ideal tee is lossless: output powers add to input power when reflections are absent. A simple tee is cheap and small, so it is used for rough splits and monitors.

A lossless reciprocal three-port cannot be matched at all three ports at once. A tee therefore reflects some power when its outputs are matched. It is not the best choice when a clean 50 Ω match matters.

A resistive divider uses three resistors in a star or delta. It can be matched at every port, but resistors turn part of the input into heat. For an equal two-way matched resistive divider, each output receives one quarter of input power: half is split and half is dissipated. That is 6.02 dB loss to each output.

Wilkinson divider

The Wilkinson divider is a three-port that uses two quarter-wave branches of impedance √2 Z0 for an equal split between Z0 ports. Z0 is the system impedance, often 50 Ω. A resistor of 2Z0 connects the two outputs.

At the design frequency, the quarter-wave lines and resistor make all three ports matched. The two outputs are isolated: a signal entering output 2 does not reach output 3. In ideal matched use the resistor carries no current, so the divider is lossless and each output gets half power, −3.01 dB.

|S<sub>21</sub>|<sup>2</sup> = |S<sub>31</sub>|<sup>2</sup> = 1/2,  S<sub>23</sub> = 0

S21 and S31 are voltage-wave transmission coefficients from input to outputs; S23 is output-to-output isolation. |S|2 is a power ratio.

Unequal split uses different branch impedances and a different resistor value. The bigger output receives more power. Bandwidth is limited because the branches are quarter-wave only near the design frequency. If an output is mismatched, reflected power is absorbed in the isolation resistor, so the divider is no longer lossless.

Attenuators: fixed, step and variable

An attenuator reduces power by a fixed or adjustable number of decibels. A fixed pad is a resistor network, often T or π, chosen to present Z0 at both ports. Common values are 3, 6, 10 and 20 dB. The pad absorbs the removed power as heat.

A step attenuator switches fixed pads in or out, so the loss changes in known steps, such as 1 dB. A variable attenuator changes continuously, using a potentiometer or a biased PIN diode. Pads match both ports, so they protect a source from a bad load and protect a load from a strong source.

A = 10 log<sub>10</sub>(P<sub>in</sub>/P<sub>out</sub>),  P<sub>out</sub> = P<sub>in</sub> 10<sup>−A/10</sup>

A is attenuation in dB; Pin and Pout are powers in W. Cascaded pads add: Atotal = A1 + A2. A level in dBm falls by A dB.

Power rating matters. A pad must dissipate Pin − Pout. A level of 20 dBm means 20 dB above 1 mW, that is 100 mW. A 6 dB pad leaves about 25.1 mW, so the pad heats by about 74.9 mW. Choose a rating above the input power, not just above the output power.

Worked example

Question. A 20 dBm source feeds a 6 dB fixed attenuator, then an ideal equal two-way Wilkinson divider with matched loads. Find the power at each output in dBm and in mW. Use a 3 dB split for the rounded answer and give the exact value too.

  1. dBm means decibels above 1 mW. Convert input: Pin = 1020/10 mW = 100 mW.
  2. After the attenuator: A = 6 dB. Pafter = 100 × 10−6/10 mW = 25.12 mW. Level = 20 − 6 = 14 dBm.
  3. Exact half-power split: each output = 25.12/2 = 12.56 mW. Level = 10 log10(12.56/1 mW) = 10.99 dBm.
  4. Rounded split loss: 14 − 3 = 11 dBm. Exact split loss is 3.01 dB, so 10.99 dBm is the precise value.

Answer. Each output is about 11 dBm (exact 10.99 dBm), or about 12.56 mW.

Exam traps

  • A 3 dB split is half power, not half voltage. Voltage falls by 1/√2; power falls by 1/2.
  • A Wilkinson is lossless only when all ports are matched. With a mismatched output, the isolation resistor turns reflected power into heat.
  • A matched pad is not a bare series resistor. It is designed to look like Z0 at both ports; that match is its useful feature.
  • Do not add dBm values as if they were dB. 20 dBm minus 6 dB is 14 dBm; 20 dBm plus 20 dBm means 200 mW, not 40 dBm.
  • An equal matched resistive divider has about 6 dB loss to each output, not 3 dB, because half the power is dissipated in its resistors.

4.5 Ferrite devices: isolators, circulators and phase shifters

Under a steady DC magnetic field, a ferrite in a waveguide treats the two travel directions differently. A wave going one way is rotated, passed or delayed differently from a wave going back. Three devices use this: the isolator (passes one way, absorbs the other), the circulator (hands power round ports in fixed rotation), and the nonreciprocal phase shifter. They sit at radar front ends, transmitters and phased arrays.

You need 4.1 S-parameters for port waves and the scattering matrix, and Unit 1 for waveguide propagation and phase constant β.

Ferrite under DC bias: rotation that does not undo itself

A ferrite is an insulating ceramic of iron oxide with other metal oxides. It has low microwave loss. In a waveguide it is a rod, disc or toroid placed where the wave's magnetic field is strongest.

With no magnet a ferrite is just a dielectric. A steady DC magnetic field along the rod makes electron spins precess. The permeability becomes a tensor, and its value depends on whether the wave's magnetic field rotates clockwise or anticlockwise about the bias.

A linearly polarised wave is two counter-rotating circular waves. In biased ferrite they travel at different speeds, so the plane of polarisation turns on exit. This is Faraday rotation.

θ = V B L

θ is rotation angle in radians, V is the Verdet constant of the material in rad/(T·m), B is DC flux density in tesla, and L is ferrite length in metres.

The bias sets the rotation sense, not the travel direction. A wave 1→2 turns by +θ; reflected 2→1 it turns by +θ again, giving 2θ, not zero. That is nonreciprocity. A reciprocal part such as plain waveguide undoes its effect on return.

The Y-junction circulator and its S-matrix

A circulator sends power round its ports in fixed rotation. The three-port form is a Y junction: three waveguides at 120°, or three striplines on ferrite, with a ferrite post at the centre and a small permanent magnet across it.

In the junction the biased ferrite supports two counter-rotating modes with different phase constants. Their sum bulges towards one arm. Power in at port 1 goes to port 2, port 2 to port 3, port 3 to port 1. Flip the magnet and the rotation reverses.

[S] = e<sup>−jφ</sup> × [[0, 0, 1], [1, 0, 0], [0, 1, 0]]

row index is output port, column index is input port, so S₂₁ = 1 means a wave in at port 1 comes out at port 2; S₃₂ = 1 and S₁₃ = 1 carry the rotation 2→3→1. The zeros are isolated paths. φ is insertion phase. The ideal matrix is matched and lossless but not symmetric, because S₂₁ = 1 while S₁₂ = 0.

A good waveguide circulator has insertion loss under 1 dB and isolation 20 dB or more over one waveguide band.

A four-port circulator circulates 1→2→3→4→1. It is used as a radar duplexer sharing one antenna between transmitter and receiver (Unit 6), and to separate incident and reflected waves at a load.

Isolator: a circulator with a matched load

Terminate port 3 of a three-port circulator in a matched load: that is an isolator, a two-port passing power one way and absorbing it the other way.

Forward, port 1 → port 2, the loss is insertion loss IL, so P₂ = P₁ − IL. Reverse, power enters port 2 and is circulated to port 3, where the matched load absorbs it; only the circulator's leakage reaches port 1. That reverse transmission from port 2 to port 1 is the isolation I, and it equals the 1→3 leakage of the same circulator. With IL = 0.5 dB, I = 25 dB and input 10 dBm, port 2 carries 9.5 dBm and port 3 sees −15 dBm.

The load must stay matched. A reflection from port 3 comes back in at port 3 and circulates 3→1, straight into the port supposed to be isolated, so a poor load destroys isolation.

Isolators sit between transmitter and antenna, so a mismatch or arc reflects nothing into the source, and before low-noise receivers or parametric amplifiers that must not see a transmitter pulse.

Phase shifters, reciprocal and nonreciprocal

A phase shifter adds a known phase φ. Reciprocal designs give the same φ both ways: a dielectric slab slid across the guide, switched line lengths using PIN diodes, or a loaded line.

φ = (β − β₀) L

φ is phase shift in radians, β is phase constant of the biased ferrite section in rad/m, β₀ is phase constant of the empty guide in rad/m, and L is ferrite length in metres.

A DC coil sets the bias, the bias sets β, so coil current sets phase shift: beam steering with no moving parts (Unit 3). Because bias acts differently on the two directions, forward and reverse shifts differ. In a reflection-type phase shifter, port 2 of a circulator is closed by a short-circuited phase shifter: forward φ_f, reflection adds 180°, return reverse φ_r, leave port 3 with φ_f + 180° + φ_r.

A gyrator is the abstract two-port version with 180° differential phase shift between directions; the ferrite circulator is its microwave form.

Exam traps

  • Drawing circulation arrows the wrong way. Read the arrow on the device, or remember that reversing the DC magnet reverses 1→2→3 into 1→3→2.
  • Treating an isolator as reciprocal. Its scattering matrix is not symmetric: S₂₁ ≈ 1, S₁₂ ≈ 0. Never assume its loss works both ways.
  • Leaving unused circulator ports open. An open port reflects, the reflection circulates into the isolated port, and measured isolation collapses.

4.6 Microwave tubes: klystron, reflex klystron, magnetron, TWT

At a few gigahertz, high power still comes from electrons flying through vacuum, not from a transistor. A microwave tube takes a DC beam from an electron gun and makes it hand energy to an RF field. Klystrons amplify, reflex klystrons oscillate, magnetrons deliver pulsed megawatts, and travelling wave tubes give wideband gain. You find them in radar transmitters, satellite ground stations, microwave ovens and older laboratory benches. This chapter shows the mechanism of each tube, the mode it runs in, and how gain, bandwidth and efficiency differ between them.

You need: waveguide modes and cavity resonators from Unit 1, and the port picture of a microwave component from 4.1 S-parameters.

Two-cavity klystron: velocity modulation and bunching

A two-cavity klystron is an amplifier. An electron gun makes a beam with DC voltage V₀ and current I₀. The beam crosses the gap of the input cavity, where a small RF voltage adds energy to some electrons and removes it from others. Electrons that cross the gap while the RF field accelerates them leave just after the reference electron and travel faster, so they catch up with the slower electrons that crossed just before them. A quarter cycle later the beam is a train of dense bunches, one per RF cycle. The output cavity converts part of their kinetic energy into output power. A collector absorbs the spent beam.

v(t) = v₀ [1 + (V₁ / 2V₀) sin ωt],   v₀ = √(2eV₀ / m)

V₀ is the beam voltage in volts, v₀ the entry speed in m/s, V₁ the RF gap voltage, ω = 2πf the angular frequency, e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and m = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

X = (θ / 2)(V₁ / V₀), θ = ωL / v₀

X is the bunching parameter (dimensionless), θ the drift transit angle in radians and L the drift length in metres. Bunching is strongest near X = 1.84.

  • Gain of a multicavity klystron is about 30 dB to 60 dB.
  • Bandwidth is narrow, roughly 1 % to 5 % of the centre frequency, set by the cavity Q.
  • Efficiency is 15 % to 40 % for two cavities and up to about 60 % with more cavities and a depressed collector.
  • Power runs from watts to megawatts CW at UHF and microwave bands.

Reflex klystron: one cavity, repeller, transit-time modes

A reflex klystron is an oscillator built from one cavity. A repeller electrode behind the cavity is held negative, so it slows the electrons, stops them and sends them back through the same gap. Electrons that return while the gap field opposes them give energy to the cavity. That is feedback without a second cavity.

t = (n + 3/4) / f, n = 0, 1, 2, …

t is the round-trip transit time in the repeller space in seconds, f the oscillation frequency in hertz and n the mode number. Each n is a separate mode with its own power and frequency.

Changing the repeller voltage changes the electron speed and so the transit time t. That shifts the frequency, which is electronic tuning. A tuning screw changes the cavity size, which is mechanical tuning. Output is a few milliwatts to a few watts, efficiency is 1 % to 20 %, and the tube served as a local oscillator in radar receivers and as a bench source.

Magnetron: crossed fields and the π-mode

A magnetron has a central cylindrical cathode and an anode block hollowed into N resonant cavities. A DC voltage puts a radial electric field between cathode and anode. A permanent magnet puts a steady axial magnetic field along the same axis. The two fields are crossed, so electrons do not travel straight; they curve, and near the correct field they form a rotating spoke that passes energy into the cavities.

f_c = eB / (2πm) ≈ 28 GHz × B

f_c is the cyclotron frequency in gigahertz, B the magnetic flux density in tesla, e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and m = 9.1 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

In the π-mode, neighbouring cavities oscillate 180° out of phase. This mode gives the best efficiency and is fixed by strapping the cavities together. The oscillation frequency comes from the cavity dimensions, near 3 GHz for S-band radar and 2.45 GHz for a microwave oven. Pulsed output reaches megawatts, efficiency is 40 % to 70 %, and bandwidth is narrow because the cavities resonate.

Travelling wave tube: helix and broadband gain

A travelling wave tube (TWT) has no resonant cavity in the interaction region. A helix wound inside the vacuum tube slows the RF wave so that its axial speed matches the electron beam. The beam speed is set slightly above the wave speed, so electrons give energy to the wave continuously along the whole length instead of at one gap.

v_p ≈ c · p / (2πa)

v_p is the axial phase velocity of the wave on the helix in m/s, c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s, p the helix pitch (turn spacing) in metres and a the helix radius in metres.

Because the helix is not resonant, one tube covers more than an octave. Gain is 20 dB to 40 dB, efficiency 10 % to 20 %, and CW output goes from a few watts to kilowatts. The price of bandwidth is low efficiency and a long, delicate structure.

Question. A two-cavity klystron has a beam voltage of 2 kV, a beam current of 50 mA and an RF output power of 40 W. Find the DC input power and the efficiency.

  1. Convert to base units: V₀ = 2 kV = 2000 V, I₀ = 50 mA = 0.050 A, P_rf = 40 W.
  2. DC input power P_dc = V₀ × I₀ = 2000 V × 0.050 A = 100 W.
  3. Efficiency η = (P_rf / P_dc) × 100 % = (40 W / 100 W) × 100 % = 40 %.
  4. Check the units: watts divided by watts is dimensionless, so the answer is a plain percentage.

Answer. The DC input power is 100 W and the efficiency is 40 %.

Exam traps

  • A klystron and a reflex klystron are different devices: the first is a two-cavity or multicavity amplifier, the second a single-cavity oscillator. A question about the drift tube is not about the reflex tube.
  • π-mode means neighbouring cavities differ by 180° in phase, not that the tube runs at π radians per second. Other modes exist and give lower efficiency at the wrong frequency.
  • Efficiency uses the DC input V₀I₀. kV × mA already gives watts, so converting both or neither is safe. Converting only one (kV with A, or V with mA) gives an answer that is wrong by 10³.
  • The collector heat is loss, not output power. Never add it to P_rf when computing efficiency.

4.7 Solid-state sources: Gunn, IMPATT, TRAPATT, tunnel diodes

A solid-state microwave source makes RF power from a DC supply inside a semiconductor, without a heated cathode or vacuum. It relies on negative resistance: over part of its current-voltage curve, more voltage gives less current, so a suitable cavity can build up an oscillation. Gunn diodes use bulk transferred-electron effects, IMPATT and TRAPATT diodes use avalanche and transit time, and tunnel diodes use quantum tunnelling. These sources are small, low-voltage and rugged; they appear in radar front ends, point-to-point links, sensors and test oscillators.

You need: microwave resonators and Q factor from Unit 1, and the wave picture of 4.1 S-parameters.

Gunn diode: transferred electrons and negative resistance

A Gunn diode is a bulk semiconductor device, not a p–n junction. It is a thin n-type GaAs slab with ohmic contacts at both ends. A DC bias sets an electric field across the slab.

At low field, electrons sit in the high-mobility central valley and drift normally. Above a threshold field of about 3.2 kV/cm in GaAs, many electrons gain enough energy to move to a low-mobility satellite valley. The average drift velocity then falls as the field rises. This falling current with rising voltage is negative differential resistance (NDR).

With NDR, a small RF voltage in a cavity can grow instead of decay. Charge domains form near the cathode and travel to the anode. The domain transit sets a natural frequency: f ≈ v_d / L. Here f is frequency in hertz, v_d is domain drift velocity in metres per second, and L is the active length in metres. A cavity selects the oscillation frequency; the diode supplies the negative resistance. Output is taken from the cavity.

f ≈ v_d / L

f is oscillation frequency in hertz; v_d is domain drift velocity in metres per second; L is the active semiconductor length in metres.

The supply is low voltage, often a few volts to tens of volts. The diode is small and rugged. The cavity is not optional: without a resonator the frequency is poorly defined and the output is weak.

IMPATT and TRAPATT: avalanche and transit time

An IMPATT diode uses a reverse-biased junction near breakdown. Its name means impact ionisation avalanche transit time. The structure is often a p+–n–n+ or a Read diode: an avalanche region followed by a drift region.

The RF cycle works in two parts. In the avalanche part, a rising reverse voltage causes impact ionisation and a burst of carriers. These carriers are injected into the drift region. They cross it in about half an RF period. The delayed current gives negative resistance at the terminals. The frequency is roughly f ≈ v_s / (2L_d). Here v_s is the saturated carrier velocity in metres per second and L_d is the drift length in metres.

IMPATT diodes give useful power at microwave and millimetre-wave frequencies. Efficiency is usually a few per cent to about fifteen per cent, because avalanche and drift losses are significant. They are used in radar transmitters and high-frequency oscillators where a compact solid-state source is needed.

TRAPATT means trapped plasma avalanche triggered transit. It is a mode of a similar diode, not a separate material. A large-signal pulse drives the diode into a plasma-filled state; the plasma is trapped, then extracted. The waveform is rich in harmonics, so the cavity may operate at a lower frequency. TRAPATT gives high efficiency, often tens of per cent, at lower microwave frequencies. It is less common than IMPATT.

BARITT is a short comparison. A BARITT diode injects carriers by punch-through rather than avalanche. It has lower noise but lower power and efficiency; it appears in some low-noise oscillator designs.

Tunnel diode and source choice

A tunnel diode is a heavily doped p–n junction with a very thin depletion layer. Electrons tunnel through the barrier instead of climbing over it. Its current–voltage curve rises to a peak current I_p, falls to a valley current I_v, then rises again. Between the peak and the valley the slope is negative, so the diode has negative resistance.

Bias the diode in this negative-resistance region and add a cavity. The combination oscillates at microwave frequencies. The tunnelling is fast, so the diode can work at high frequency. The current and voltage are small, so output power is low, often microwatts to a few milliwatts. Tunnel diodes are used in low-power oscillators, detectors and low-noise amplifiers, not in high-power transmitters.

Source choice follows the job. Gunn diodes are simple fixed-frequency or varactor-tuned oscillators with moderate power. IMPATT diodes give more power at higher frequency but have higher noise. TRAPATT diodes give high efficiency at lower frequency. Tunnel diodes give low power and very low noise.

  • Gunn: bulk NDR, cavity-controlled, moderate power.
  • IMPATT: avalanche plus drift, higher power, higher noise.
  • TRAPATT: trapped plasma, high efficiency, lower frequency.
  • Tunnel: quantum tunnelling, low power, fast.

Worked example

Question. A Gunn-diode oscillator works at 10 GHz. The DC supply is 8 V and the diode current is 0.5 A. The measured RF output is 1 W. Find the DC input power and the efficiency.

  1. DC input power is the product of supply voltage and current: P_DC = V × I.
  2. Substitute: P_DC = 8 V × 0.5 A = 4 W.
  3. Efficiency is RF output divided by DC input: η = P_RF / P_DC.
  4. Substitute: η = 1 W / 4 W = 0.25.
  5. As a percentage: η = 0.25 × 100% = 25%.

Answer. The DC input power is 4 W and the efficiency is 25%.

  • Calling a Gunn diode a p–n junction. It is a bulk n-type slab; the negative resistance comes from transferred electrons, not from a junction.
  • Mixing IMPATT and Gunn mechanisms. IMPATT uses avalanche multiplication plus transit time; Gunn uses a field-induced valley transfer.
  • Expecting TRAPATT to be the high-frequency choice. It is a high-efficiency, lower-frequency mode; IMPATT usually covers higher frequencies.
  • Using a tunnel diode for high power. It is a low-power, fast device; bias must sit between peak and valley current.
  • Sliding on efficiency. η = P_RF/P_DC, and P_DC = V×I; multiply by 100 only to report per cent.

4.8 Microwave measurements: VSWR, power, frequency, network analyzer

At microwave frequencies you cannot clip a multimeter across a wave. Wavelengths are millimetres, so voltage and current vary along the line. Engineers measure indirect quantities: standing-wave ratio from a slotted line, power with thermal sensors, frequency with a cavity wavemeter, and S-parameters with a vector network analyser. These measurements check filters, couplers, amplifiers and antennas before a system is deployed. A wrong calibration can make a bad component look good. VSWR, power, frequency and S-parameters are the basic bench checks in any microwave lab.

You need Unit 1 on standing waves, reflection coefficient and VSWR, and 4.1 S-parameters and the scattering matrix.

VSWR and guide wavelength from a slotted line

A slotted line is a section of coaxial line or waveguide with a narrow longitudinal slot. A small probe enters the slot and samples the electric field at one point. You move the probe along the line and watch a detector. The probe draws very little power; it only samples the field.

When the load is not matched, incident and reflected waves add and subtract. The envelope has maxima and minima. Define Vmax as the maximum probe voltage and Vmin as the minimum probe voltage, both in volts.

|Γ| = (V<sub>max</sub> − V<sub>min</sub>) / (V<sub>max</sub> + V<sub>min</sub>)

|Γ| is the magnitude of the load reflection coefficient, a number from 0 to 1; Vmax and Vmin are the maximum and minimum RF voltages along the line, in volts.

VSWR = V<sub>max</sub> / V<sub>min</sub> = (1 + |Γ|) / (1 − |Γ|)

VSWR is the voltage standing-wave ratio, a number ≥ 1; it has no unit.

A square-law detector gives a DC output proportional to RF power, so meter reading M follows M ∝ V². If the meter is calibrated in power, take square roots before using the voltage formulas: VSWR = √(Mmax / Mmin). If the meter is calibrated in voltage, use the readings directly.

The guide wavelength λg is twice the distance between adjacent minima. Successive minima are half a guide wavelength apart, not one full guide wavelength.

λg = 2d

λg is the guide wavelength in metres; d is the distance between two adjacent minima in metres.

  1. Connect the source, slotted line and unknown load; keep the source level fixed.
  2. Move the probe and record Vmax and Vmin.
  3. Compute |Γ| and VSWR from the two voltages.
  4. Measure d between two adjacent minima.
  5. Compute λg = 2d.

Power and frequency measurement

Power is the basic microwave quantity. A power sensor absorbs the RF signal and converts it to heat or to a DC voltage. Three common sensors are bolometer, thermistor and thermocouple.

  • Bolometer: a temperature-sensitive resistor in a bridge. RF power heats it; the bridge restores balance. The DC substitute power equals the RF power.
  • Thermistor: a semiconductor bead whose resistance falls with temperature. It uses the same DC-substitution idea.
  • Thermocouple: RF heats a hot junction; a DC voltage appears at the cold junction, proportional to absorbed power.
  • Directional coupler: samples a known small fraction of the main-line power. A 20 dB coupler gives one hundredth of the main power at the coupled port.

A cavity wavemeter measures frequency. It is a tunable high-Q cavity coupled to the line. Tune the cavity until it absorbs power and the meter dips; the calibrated dial then reads frequency. For lower microwave bands, an electronic counter counts cycles directly, and a prescaler extends its range.

Vector network analyser and SOLT calibration

A vector network analyser sends a known incident wave into a port and measures the reflected and transmitted waves. It reports complex S-parameters: magnitude and phase. For a two-port, S11 is input reflection, S21 is forward transmission, S12 is reverse transmission, and S22 is output reflection. This is the practical measurement of the matrix in 4.1.

Raw VNA data has systematic errors: directivity, source match, load match and frequency tracking. Calibration measures known standards at the reference plane and computes correction. SOLT uses Short, Open, Load and Thru. Short gives Γ = −1; Open gives Γ = +1; Load gives Γ = 0; Thru connects the two ports.

After calibration, the VNA applies error correction to every sweep. Save the calibration and check it with a known air line or attenuator. Uncalibrated data is not final; a poor calibration can hide a bad connector.

S<sub>21,dB</sub> = 20 log<sub>10</sub> |S<sub>21</sub>|

|S21| is the forward voltage gain magnitude, a ratio; S21,dB is the same gain in decibels.

4.9 Microwave integrated circuits and MMIC

A microwave integrated circuit (MIC) puts transmission lines, resistors, capacitors, inductors and transistors on one flat substrate, so a whole front end becomes a single printed board or a single chip. The hybrid version mounts ready-made transistors on a ceramic board. The monolithic version, the MMIC, builds every part inside one semiconductor wafer. Phones, radar modules, satellite receivers and 5G base stations use them because a compact, repeatable circuit with very short connections still works well above 1 GHz. This chapter covers the substrates, the on-chip components, the trade-offs and the packaging.

You need: microstrip and stripline from Unit 1, the wave view of a two-port from 4.1 S-parameters, and gain compression from the amplifier section of Unit 5.

Hybrid MIC: printed passives, mounted transistors

A microwave integrated circuit (MIC) is a complete microwave circuit built as a flat layered structure on one substrate. Every connection is a planar transmission line: microstrip, coplanar waveguide (CPW) or stripline. No long wires exist, so the circuit still behaves at frequencies where a wire would act as an inductor or an antenna.

In a hybrid MIC the substrate carries only the passive pattern: lines, couplers, filters and matching stubs. Transistors, diodes and chip capacitors are made separately and attached to that pattern by epoxy or solder, then joined with short bond wires. Hybrids are easy to modify and handle power well, but each bond wire adds roughly 1 nH per millimetre of inductance, which hurts at high frequency.

  • Alumina (Al₂O₃): εr ≈ 9.8, hard, cheap, good heat conduction; the workhorse for hybrid MICs. Common thicknesses are 0.25 mm and 0.635 mm.
  • Fused silica and quartz: εr ≈ 3.8, very low loss, used above about 20 GHz.
  • Semi-insulating GaAs: εr ≈ 12.9; used when the board must also carry monolithic devices.
  • LTCC (low-temperature co-fired ceramic): many dielectric layers, so 3-D passives and buried striplines fit inside one package.

Line geometry sets the impedance. For microstrip, the strip width w over substrate height h and permittivity εr fixes Z₀, and the guided wavelength is λg = λ₀/√εeff, where εeff lies between 1 and εr because part of the field travels in air. A microstrip patch antenna is just one more printed shape on the same substrate (see Unit 3). Thick-film paste printing is cheap but coarse; thin-film sputtering with photolithography gives fine lines and stable resistors.

MMIC: the whole circuit inside one wafer

A monolithic microwave integrated circuit (MMIC) forms every component — transistor, resistor, capacitor, inductor and line — in or on one semiconductor wafer. Nothing is bonded on. One process run yields thousands of identical circuits, and there are no bond-wire parasitics anywhere inside the chip.

  • Transistor: MESFET or HEMT on GaAs; HBT or RF-CMOS on silicon. Gain falls roughly 6 dB per octave, so a device with usable gain at 2 GHz has about 20 dB less gain at 20 GHz (about 3.3 octaves higher).
  • Resistor: a thin film (NiCr or TaN) or the active layer itself, set by its sheet resistance in Ω per square; value = sheet resistance × (length/width).
  • Capacitor: metal–insulator–metal (MIM) sandwich, C = εA/d with Si₃N₄ of thickness d ≈ 0.2 µm. Values are small, a few pF.
  • Inductor: a flat metal spiral; the best on-chip Q is about 10–20 because of series metal resistance and loss leaking into the substrate.
  • Airbridge: a thick plated metal strap jumping over the surface. It crosses other lines with little capacitance and carries current with low resistance.
  1. Grow or implant the active layer on the semi-insulating wafer.
  2. Isolate each device region by mesa etch or ion implantation.
  3. Form ohmic contacts (AuGe/Ni/Au) and anneal; deposit the gate metal.
  4. Deposit the dielectric, open contact windows, and build the MIM capacitors and thin-film resistors.
  5. Sputter metal 1 for interconnects and spirals; plate the airbridge as metal 2, landing on metal 1.
  6. Thin the wafer to about 100 µm, etch backside via holes down to ground, then dice and test.

GaAs is semi-insulating, with resistivity above 10⁷ Ω·cm, which is why a GaAs MMIC can carry low-loss lines straight on its own surface. InP serves circuits above 100 GHz. Silicon is the cheap high-volume choice, carrying RF-CMOS and SiGe HBT circuits such as the switch and front-end blocks inside a phone.

Trade-offs, packaging and measurement

  • MMIC advantages: very small, repeatable from die to die, broadband because there are no bond wires, and cheap per piece in volume.
  • MMIC limits: low output power (a few watts at most, less on GaAs), a very expensive mask set and wafer run, no tuning after fabrication, low-Q passives, and yield loss across a large chip.
  • Hybrid advantages: tuneable by trimming, higher power, cheap tooling, quick to prototype.

Packaging is part of the design. The die is soldered or glued into a metal cavity, and connections leave by bond wires, by flip-chip bumps, or by a CPW launch into a coaxial connector. Wire bonds add series inductance, and a cavity that is a half-wave long at the operating frequency will resonate and spoil the match, so the lid height is chosen with care.

Passive measurement settles the process before any circuit is committed. A microstrip ring or half-wave resonator made on the same wafer gives the effective permittivity from its resonant frequency and the substrate loss from its Q, so εeff and loss tangent are known. A network analyser, as in 4.8, then measures S₂₁ and S₁₁ of the finished chip. Filters, couplers, dividers, low-noise amplifiers and power amplifiers built on these substrates are the subject of Unit 5.

Worked example

Question. A MMIC power amplifier for a 5 GHz link has small-signal gain 10 dB and an output 1 dB compression point of 18 dBm. The input power is 8 dBm. Find (a) the output power if the amplifier were perfectly linear, (b) the actual output power, and (c) the compression in dB.

  1. Linear case: P_out = P_in + G = 8 dBm + 10 dB = 18 dBm. Adding a dB to a dBm gives a dBm.
  2. The output 1 dB compression point is P1dB(out) = 18 dBm: the output at which the real curve sits 1 dB below the straight-line extension.
  3. The linear value equals P1dB(out), so the device is exactly at the 1 dB compression point. The actual output is 1 dB below the linear value: 18 dBm − 1 dB = 17 dBm.
  4. Compression = linear output − actual output = 18 dBm − 17 dBm = 1 dB. Subtracting dBm from dBm gives plain dB.
  5. Gain under this drive = 17 dBm − 8 dBm = 9 dB, i.e. 1 dB below the small-signal gain of 10 dB.

Answer. Linear output 18 dBm, actual output 17 dBm, so the amplifier is compressed by 1 dB.

Exam traps

  • Calling every flat microwave board an MMIC. The test is whether the transistor is made inside the wafer or bonded onto it.
  • Treating on-chip spiral inductors as ideal. Their Q is only about 10–20, so a matching network built from them always loses some signal.
  • Using εr where εeff belongs. The microstrip guided wavelength uses εeff, which is smaller than εr because part of the field is in air.
  • Forgetting that bond wires and via holes are inductors, roughly 1 nH per millimetre of wire, which shifts the match at high frequency.
  • Mixing dB and dBm: dBm + dB = dBm, but dBm − dBm = dB. The compression figure is a plain dB number.

A ring-shaped four-port hybrid that gives equal power split with a fixed 0° or 180° phase difference between outputs.
IMPATT diode
An avalanche transit-time diode that uses avalanche multiplication and transit delay to produce negative resistance at microwave frequencies.
Isolation
Isolation I = 10 log10(P1/P4) dB, the ratio of input power to power at the isolated port; I = C + D.
Isolator
A ferrite two-port that transmits in one direction and absorbs power in the reverse direction.
Klystron
A linear-beam vacuum tube that uses cavity velocity modulation and drift-space bunching to amplify microwaves.
Lossless network
A network with no resistive loss; its scattering matrix is unitary, [S]†[S] = [I].
Magic tee
A four-port hybrid made from E-plane and H-plane tees, with matched collinear arms and isolated E and H arms.
Magnetron
A crossed-field vacuum tube with resonant cavities, used as a high-power microwave oscillator.
Matched load
A load whose impedance equals the line reference impedance, so no wave reflects from it.
MMIC
A monolithic microwave integrated circuit in which passive and active devices are fabricated on one semiconductor chip.
Network analyser
A calibrated instrument that measures amplitude and phase of S-parameters of a device over frequency.
Port
A terminal pair or waveguide opening where power enters or leaves a microwave network.
Power divider
A passive network that divides input power between two or more outputs, often with matching and isolation.
Reciprocal network
A network whose scattering matrix is symmetric, Sij = Sji, when all ports use the same impedance.
S-parameter
A ratio of an outgoing wave at one port to an incident wave at another port when all other ports are matched.
Traveling-wave tube
A wideband vacuum-tube amplifier in which a slow-wave helix keeps an RF wave in step with an electron beam.
VSWR
Voltage standing wave ratio = (1 + |Γ|)/(1 − |Γ|), a measure of mismatch on a transmission line.
