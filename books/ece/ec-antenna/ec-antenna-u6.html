6.1 Radar principle, block diagram and frequency bands

A radar sends out a radio pulse and listens for the echo. The round-trip delay gives the target range, and the pointing angle of the antenna gives its direction. Everything else — duplexers, receivers, processors, displays — exists to make these two measurements reliably in noise and clutter. The same idea runs air traffic control at an airport, weather radar over the Bay of Bengal, a police speed gun on the highway, and the altimeter in an aircraft. This chapter builds the block diagram and then chooses a frequency band for the job.

You need Unit 2, Antenna Fundamentals and Radiation: antenna gain, beamwidth and the 1/R² fall of received power.

Delay gives range, pointing gives direction

Radar is short for radio detection and ranging. A transmitter sends a short burst of radio energy, the antenna focuses it into a beam, and the target returns a small echo. The receiver records the echo's arrival time and strength.

R = c t / 2

R is the target range in metres; c is the speed of light, 3 × 10⁸ m/s; t is the round-trip echo delay in seconds.

The factor 2 matters: the wave travels to the target and back, so only half the delay belongs to the outward trip.

Direction is the antenna pointing angle. A narrow beam gives a precise bearing; the beamwidth sets the angular accuracy. A moving target shifts the echo frequency by the Doppler shift f_d = 2 v_r / λ for a monostatic radar, where v_r is the radial velocity in m/s and λ is the wavelength in m. Speed measurement is covered in 6.5.

Monostatic radar uses one antenna for both transmit and receive. Bistatic radar uses a separate transmitter and receiver site.

Range resolution: a pulse of duration τ occupies c τ metres in space. Two targets are resolved only if their echoes do not overlap.

ΔR = c τ / 2

ΔR is the smallest resolvable range difference in metres; τ is the pulse width in seconds; c is 3 × 10⁸ m/s.

The pulse radar block diagram

The figure shows the signal path. The synchronizer is the master clock, and each block has one job.

  • Synchronizer: master timer. It sets the pulse repetition frequency (PRF), triggers the modulator, and gives the zero-time reference for range.
  • Modulator: generates the high-voltage pulse that switches the transmitter on and off, and sets the pulse width τ and the pulse shape. In amplifier-based radars a low-level waveform generator sets the pulse, and the transmitter amplifies it.
  • Transmitter: raises the pulse to high power, from kilowatts to megawatts. It uses a magnetron, klystron or TWT (Unit 4).
  • Duplexer: uses TR and ATR tubes or a circulator to connect the antenna to the transmitter on transmit and to the receiver on receive. It blocks the high-power pulse from the receiver.
  • Antenna: high-gain reflector or array. It is scanned mechanically or electronically.
  • Receiver: superheterodyne chain with LNA, mixer and IF amplifier. It must detect a weak echo in noise (Unit 5).
  • Signal processor: matched filter, MTI, pulse Doppler and CFAR. It separates target from clutter and noise.
  • Display: a PPI screen shows range and bearing to the operator.

Choose a band by the job, not by a ranking. Lower bands have lower atmospheric and rain attenuation, so they reach farther. For the same physical antenna size, gain falls as wavelength grows (Unit 2), so a low-band radar needs a large antenna for a narrow beam. Lower bands suffer less rain and ground clutter, because both rain backscatter and terrain reflectivity grow with frequency. Their clutter penalty comes from the wider beam, which illuminates a larger patch of ground, and from anomalous propagation and ducting at long range. Higher bands give high gain and a narrow beam from a small antenna, and short pulses give fine resolution, but rain attenuation rises and high solid-state power is harder.

Typical assignments: air traffic control uses L and S; weather radar uses S, C and X; police speed guns use X, Ku or Ka; radio altimeters use Ku or Ka; proximity fuses and missile seekers use X, Ka or mm-wave; ground-penetrating radar uses VHF or UHF to see through soil. Atmospheric refraction, ducting and rain backscatter are propagation effects, named here only; Unit 3 covers the basics.

Worked example

Question. A pulse radar transmits a pulse of width 1 μs. The echo from a target arrives 124 μs after the transmitted pulse leaves the antenna. (a) Find the target range. (b) Find the pulse length in space. (c) Find the range resolution.

  1. Write the data: c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s, t = 124 μs = 124 × 10⁻⁶ s, τ = 1 μs = 1 × 10⁻⁶ s.
  2. Range: R = c t / 2 = (3 × 10⁸ × 124 × 10⁻⁶) / 2.
  3. The numerator is 3 × 10⁸ × 124 × 10⁻⁶ = 3.72 × 10⁴ m = 37 200 m.
  4. R = 37 200 / 2 = 18 600 m = 18.6 km.
  5. Pulse length in space: c τ = 3 × 10⁸ × 1 × 10⁻⁶ = 300 m.
  6. Range resolution: ΔR = c τ / 2 = 300 / 2 = 150 m.

Answer. The target is at 18.6 km, the pulse occupies 300 m in space, and the range resolution is 150 m.

Exam traps

  • Using R = c t instead of R = c t / 2. The wave travels to the target and back, so only half the delay belongs to the outward trip.
  • Confusing pulse length in space c τ with range resolution c τ / 2. The pulse is twice as long as the closest resolvable spacing.
  • Treating radar band letters as exact, fixed frequency limits. They are conventional labels with overlaps and regional differences.
  • Assuming a higher band is always better. Rain attenuation, water vapour absorption and lower solid-state power can make a high band worse for long range.
  • Forgetting that the duplexer protects the receiver; it does not amplify the echo or improve sensitivity.

6.2 Radar range equation and range performance

The echo from a target is unimaginably weak. A radar may send hundreds of kilowatts, yet the power that returns is often picowatts. The radar range equation explains why. It follows power from the transmitter, through the spreading beam, off the target, back through space and into the antenna. The result links transmit power, antenna gain, wavelength, target cross section and receiver sensitivity to one useful number: the greatest range at which the target can be detected. Air-traffic control, weather radar and missile tracking all use this budget.

You need: Chapter 6.1 for the radar block diagram, and Unit 2 for gain, effective aperture and the Friis spreading-loss idea.

Build the echo-power equation

Take a monostatic radar: one antenna transmits and receives. The transmitter delivers Pt watts to an antenna of gain G. G is a ratio, not dB. On the beam axis at range R metres, the power density is S = PtG/(4πR²), in W/m². The 4πR² is the area of a sphere of radius R; the gain multiplies the density in the beam direction.

The target intercepts power equal to S times its radar cross section σ, in square metres. Think of σ as an equivalent capture area, not the physical size; Chapter 6.3 explains it. If the target scatters this power equally in all directions, the density back at the radar is PtGσ/((4π)²R⁴). Two spreads by R² give the R⁴.

The receiving antenna does not collect all that density. It has an effective aperture Ae in m², so the received power is Pr = SreturnAe. Use the antenna relation G = 4πAe/λ², with λ the wavelength in metres. Substitute Ae = Gλ²/(4π):

Pr = PtG²λ²σ / ((4π)³R⁴)

Pr is received echo power in W; Pt is transmitted power in W; G is antenna gain as a ratio; λ is wavelength in m; σ is radar cross section in m²; R is target range in m.

The same result in terms of aperture is Pr = PtAe²σ/(4πλ²R⁴). This form is handy when the antenna is described by its physical area. Notice G appears squared: one G for the transmit beam, one G for the receive capture. That square is the most common slip in exams.

The weakest echo the receiver can see

Set Pr equal to the minimum detectable signal Smin. The receiver cannot declare a target from an echo weaker than its own noise. For a receiver with noise figure F as a ratio, noise bandwidth B in Hz, and reference temperature T0 = 290 K, the noise power is

Smin = kT0FB

k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann's constant; T0 = 290 K; F is noise figure as a ratio; B is noise bandwidth in Hz; Smin is in W.

Losses reduce the echo before it reaches the detector. Cable loss, duplexer loss, radome loss and signal-processing loss are collected into a system loss factor L, a ratio greater than 1. Put L in the denominator: the effective received power is Pr/L.

A single pulse may be too weak, so the radar integrates n pulses. Coherent integration adds the echoes with the right phase; the signal-to-noise power ratio rises as n. Noncoherent integration, such as a square-law detector, gives less: roughly √n at low signal-to-noise ratio and closer to n at high signal-to-noise ratio, depending on the detector. Use n as the optimistic case and √n as the cautious case.

Maximum range and the fourth-power law

Set Pr/L = Smin and solve for R. The result is the radar range equation in its maximum-range form:

R<sub>max</sub> = [ P<sub>t</sub>G²λ²σ / ((4π)³S<sub>min</sub>L) ]<sup>1/4</sup>

Rmax is maximum detectable range in m; the other symbols are as above; L is the system loss ratio.

With effective aperture directly, Rmax = [PtAe²σ/(4πλ²SminL)]1/4. Use whichever antenna description you have. Do not mix the two forms: the (4π)³ form needs G, the other needs Ae.

The fourth root governs performance. Double the range and R⁴ rises by 2⁴ = 16, so the transmitter needs 16 times the power, or 12 dB more. Increase σ one hundredfold and Rmax rises by 1001/4 = 3.16, not 100. Plotted against range, the echo power falls as 1/R⁴; plotted against σ, range rises as σ1/4, as the figure shows.

A search radar must cover a volume, not one target. Its equation replaces single-pulse power by average power and adds search solid angle and search time. Only a few universities teach this form; the three-line idea is that the search equation answers 'how far can I search a volume per second', while the range equation answers 'how far can I detect one target'.

Worked example

Question. A monostatic radar transmits Pt = 500 kW at 10 GHz. The antenna gain is 30 dB. It must detect a target with radar cross section σ = 1 m². Exam traps

  • Using received power Pr in place of Smin when solving for Rmax. The equation gives maximum range only when Pr equals the weakest detectable echo.
  • Forgetting that G is squared. In the monostatic form one G is the transmit beam and one is the receive capture; a 3 dB gain error becomes 6 dB in the echo.
  • Doubling range needs 16 times the power, not 2 times. In dB that is 12 dB more transmit power.
  • Putting F in dB into Smin = kT0FB. F must be a ratio; 10 dB means F = 10.
  • Mixing kilometres and metres, or using λ in cm. R and λ must be in the same length unit, preferably metres.

6.3 Radar cross section of targets

A radar sees the echo a target returns, not its outline. Two objects of the same physical size can return echoes differing by a factor of ten thousand, because echo depends on shape, aspect angle and wavelength. Engineers describe a target by one number, radar cross section σ, an equivalent area in square metres. It sits in the range equation of 6.2 and sets how far a radar can see an object.

You need Chapter 6.2 for σ in the range equation, and Unit 2 for effective aperture and the receiving antenna.

The definition: an equivalent area

A target intercepts incident power and reradiates it in many directions. Replace it by a fictitious area that takes the same power and reradiates equally in all directions. Choose that area so the echo density at the radar matches the real echo. That area is σ.

σ = 4πR² · S_back / S_inc

σ is radar cross section in square metres (m²); R is target-to-radar distance in m; S_back is echo power density at the radar in W/m²; S_inc is incident power density at the target in W/m². The factor 4πR² is the sphere area over which the equivalent isotropic reradiator spreads its power.

Turned around, S_back = S_inc σ / (4πR²). That is the target term in 6.2. The 1/R² is spreading loss on the return path.

σ spans many powers of ten, so it is often quoted in dBsm: σ(dBsm) = 10 log₁₀(σ / 1 m²). Thus 1 m² is 0 dBsm, 100 m² is 20 dBsm and 0.01 m² is −20 dBsm.

σ is not shadow area. A thin wire, plate edge or cavity can have σ far larger than its outline; a shaped body can have σ far smaller.

Sphere, plate and corner reflector

The metal sphere is the one target with an exact cross section. Let a be radius in m and λ wavelength in m; form ka = 2πa/λ, where k = 2π/λ is wavenumber in rad/m.

  • Rayleigh region, ka ≪ 1: sphere much smaller than a wavelength; σ grows as a⁶ and falls as λ⁴. Rain drops and insects live here, so weather radar uses short wavelength.
  • Resonance or Mie region, ka roughly 1: σ oscillates with peaks and nulls around the optical value; no simple formula holds.
  • Optical region, ka ≫ 1: σ = πa², the projected area, independent of wavelength. Large aircraft or ship at X band lies here for big smooth parts.

A flat metal plate broadside is the opposite extreme. Every point reradiates in phase, so fields add coherently and power grows as area squared.

σ = 4πA² / λ²

A is plate area in m²; λ is wavelength in m. Valid only near normal incidence. The A² shows coherent addition; 1/λ² is the same factor that gives a large aperture antenna its gain.

A corner reflector is two or three mutually perpendicular metal plates. A wave bounces once from each plate and leaves almost parallel to the way it came, so a large cross section is available over a wide angle range. Small boats carry corner reflectors so marine radar sees them; calibration targets use them because σ is trusted.

A half-wave dipole at resonance has σ of order 0.86λ². A short dipole is far smaller and its σ drops quickly as the wire is shortened.

Aspect, fluctuation and hiding a target

σ depends on direction. A few degrees off broadside, a plate's σ falls by tens of decibels because the coherent sum now cancels. Real targets have flat panels, edges and cavities, so RCS swings wildly as they turn. This echo fluctuation is covered by the Swerling models in 6.8.

TargetTypical σComment

These are orders of magnitude only. The same target can change by 20 dB or more between bands and between nose-on and broadside.

Reduce σ by shaping, radar absorbing material and aspect control. Shaping removes flat panels and right angles, tilts vertical surfaces so the flash goes upward, and buries engine faces behind bends. Absorbing material turns the incident wave into heat in a lossy layer, but is usually narrow band or thick. Stealth combines these with reduced platform emission.

Polarisation matters. A flat plate returns the same polarisation; a dihedral corner rotates it by 90°, so receive polarisation changes echo.

Worked example

Question. A flat metal plate of area 1 m² is seen broadside by an X-band radar at 10 GHz. (a) Find σ in m² and dBsm. (b) A metal sphere has the same projected area of 1 m². Find its radius, ka, and σ at the same frequency. (c) Compare the echoes.

  1. Wavelength: λ = c/f = (3 × 10⁸ m/s) / (10 × 10⁹ Hz) = 0.03 m, the 3 cm X band.
  2. Plate broadside: σ = 4πA²/λ² = (4π × (1 m²)²) / (0.03 m)² = 12.566 / (9 × 10⁻⁴) = 1.3963 × 10⁴ m².
  3. In decibels: σ(dBsm) = 10 log₁₀(1.3963 × 10⁴) = 41.45 dBsm.
  4. Sphere with πa² = 1 m² gives a = √(1/π) = 0.564 m.
  5. k = 2π/λ = 2π/0.03 = 209.4 rad/m, so ka = 209.4 × 0.564 = 118.2 ≫ 1. The sphere is in the optical region, so σ = πa² = 1.0 m², that is 0 dBsm.
  6. Ratio: 13963 / 1.0 ≈ 1.4 × 10⁴. The plate returns more than ten thousand times the power of the sphere, although both present 1 m² of projected area.

Answer. The plate has σ ≈ 13963 m² (41.45 dBsm) and the sphere of radius 0.564 m has σ = 1.0 m² (0 dBsm); the plate's echo is about 1.4 × 10⁴ times stronger.

Exam traps

  • Reading σ as physical area. A 1 m² plate gives about 14000 m² of cross section at one angle and almost nothing a few degrees away, so 'the target is 1 m²' is meaningless without band and aspect.
  • Carrying an RCS value from one frequency to another. The same aircraft nose may be in the resonance region on VHF and the optical region on X band, with very different σ.
  • Dropping the reference of a decibel. 0 dBsm means 1 m², not zero area; −20 dBsm is 0.01 m². Do not confuse dBsm (area) with dB (a pure ratio).
  • Using the flat-plate formula off broadside. σ = 4πA²/λ² holds only at normal incidence; tilted, the coherent sum collapses and the pattern shows narrow spikes.
  • Forgetting that σ assumes isotropic reradiation. Use the real bistatic pattern instead and the number depends on receiver direction, so it is no longer the same σ.

6.4 Pulse repetition frequency and range ambiguities

A pulsed radar cannot listen while it transmits, so it sends a pulse and waits a fixed time for the echo. The pulse repetition frequency (PRF) sets that waiting time, and therefore the longest range the radar can measure without confusing one pulse's echo with another. This is why PRF is chosen before waveform timing, and why it controls both range ambiguity and the radar's power budget. Air-traffic radars, weather radars and military search radars all pick PRF around this compromise.

You need: Chapter 6.2 for maximum range Rmax, and Chapter 6.1 for echo timing.

PRF sets the listening window

A pulsed radar sends a short pulse, then switches to receive. It waits one pulse repetition time Tr before the next pulse. PRF is the number of pulses per second. The period is Tr = 1 / PRF. The transmitter is on for τ of that period, so the receiver listens for Tr − τ. An echo must return inside this listening time. If it returns later, the next pulse has already gone out and the receiver cannot tell which pulse produced it.

T<sub>r</sub> = 1 / PRF,   R<sub>unamb</sub> = c T<sub>r</sub> / 2 = c / (2 PRF)

Tr is the pulse repetition time in seconds; PRF is in hertz; c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s is the speed of light; Runamb is the unambiguous range in metres. The factor 2 is the out-and-back path. In a simple pulsed radar τ is much smaller than Tr, so the listening time Tr − τ is taken as Tr itself.

The pulse width τ is the on-time of each pulse. Duty cycle is the fraction of time the transmitter is on: τ / Tr = τ PRF. Average power is Pav = Pt τ PRF, where Pt is peak power. A radar with Pt = 1 MW, τ = 1 μs and PRF = 1 kHz has duty cycle 0.001 and Pav = 1 kW.

Second-time-around echoes and blind range

A target beyond Runamb still gives an echo. The echo arrives after the next pulse is sent. The radar measures only the delay inside one period, so the target folds back. For one extra period, apparent range Rapp = R − Runamb. For n extra periods, Rapp = R − n Runamb, with n a whole number. A target at 200 km with Runamb = 150 km appears at 50 km.

Eclipsing happens when the echo arrives while the radar is transmitting. The receiver is blanked or sees only the strong leakage pulse, so the target is missed. The smallest measurable range is set by pulse width and receiver recovery: Rblind = c τ / 2 plus recovery distance. A 1 μs pulse gives Rblind = 150 m before recovery is added.

R<sub>blind</sub> = c τ / 2  (+ c t<sub>rec</sub> / 2)

τ is the pulse width in seconds; trec is the receiver recovery time in seconds; Rblind is the blind range in metres.

Range gating divides the listening window into bins. Each gate is a short sampling window of width τ (the pulse width) in time, which corresponds to a range-bin width of c τ / 2 metres. Bin k covers range near k c τ / 2. The display or processor uses these bins for thresholding, tracking and clutter maps. A long pulse gives coarse bins and a large blind range; a short pulse gives fine bins but less energy per pulse.

The PRF trade-off

High PRF means short Tr, so Runamb is small and range is ambiguous. But pulses come often, so Doppler shift is sampled often and target velocity can be unambiguous over a wide span. Low PRF gives long Tr, large Runamb, but Doppler is ambiguous. Medium PRF sits between. Search radars often use low or medium PRF; pulse Doppler fire-control radars use high or medium PRF.

  • High PRF: good Doppler, poor range.
  • Low PRF: good range, poor Doppler.
  • Medium PRF: both are partly ambiguous.

Multiple PRF sets resolve both. The radar transmits groups of pulses at two or more PRFs. A true target appears at a consistent true range after unfolding; a false fold does not. Staggered PRF changes the period from pulse to pulse so second-time-around echoes land at different apparent ranges and can be rejected. See Chapter 6.6 for blind speeds and staggered PRF, and Chapter 6.5 for Doppler ambiguity.

6.5 CW and FMCW radar; Doppler effect

A continuous-wave radar transmits all the time and never times a pulse. It listens for the small frequency shift that a moving target puts on the echo: the Doppler shift. That gives speed only. Sweep the transmit frequency linearly and the echo arrives with a delay, so mixing echo and reference produces a beat frequency proportional to range. This is FMCW radar, used in aircraft radio altimeters, tank level gauges and cheap intruder sensors.

You need: Chapter 6.1 for the radar block diagram, and Unit 5 for mixer operation and oscillator phase noise.

CW radar: speed only

A CW radar has one oscillator running continuously at f0. The transmit antenna radiates a pure tone and a separate receive antenna collects the echo; separate antennas keep the strong transmitter from saturating the receiver. For a stationary target the echo has the same frequency. For a target moving toward or away from the radar, the echo frequency shifts by the Doppler frequency:

fd = 2 vr / λ

fd is the Doppler frequency in Hz; vr is the radial velocity in m/s, positive for a closing target; λ = c / f0 is the wavelength in m.

A mixer multiplies the echo with a sample of the transmitter. The output has a sum term near 2f0 and a difference term at fd; a low-pass Doppler filter keeps only fd, and a counter or FFT shows it. A closing target gives positive fd and a higher echo frequency. An opening target gives negative fd.

A single-frequency CW radar has no timing mark on the transmitted wave, so it cannot measure range. It also cannot see a stationary target, whose fd is zero, and it cannot separate two closing targets at different ranges if their radial speeds are equal: both produce one Doppler tone. It is a speed sensor, not a range sensor.

FMCW: add a sweep, get range

FMCW radar sweeps the oscillator frequency in a sawtooth from f0 to f0+Δf over a sweep period Tm, then repeats. The echo from a target at range R comes back after delay τ = 2R/c. By then the oscillator has moved on, so the echo frequency differs from the current transmit frequency. The mixer multiplies the echo with a sample of the current sweep and produces a low beat frequency fb. During the delay τ the sweep has advanced by Δf(τ/Tm), so

fb = 2 R Δf / (c Tm)

fb is the beat frequency in Hz; R is range in m; Δf is the sweep bandwidth in Hz; Tm is the sweep period in s; c = 3 × 108 m/s.

The beat is constant during the ramp; counting it gives range. Range resolution is set by sweep bandwidth:

ΔR = c / (2 Δf)

ΔR is the range resolution in m; Δf is the sweep bandwidth in Hz.

A larger Δf gives finer range bins. Maximum range is limited by the largest beat frequency the receiver and ADC can handle, and by the sampling rate; it is not limited by pulse repetition frequency because there is no pulse. Triangular modulation uses an up-ramp and a down-ramp. A moving target adds the Doppler shift to the beat: the up and down beat frequencies differ by 2fd, so averaging them gives range and their difference gives radial speed.

Multiple tones and where this is used

Two or more CW tones can give range without a sweep. Transmit f1 and f2, receive both echoes, and measure the phase of each. The round-trip phase is φ = 4πR/λ, so the phase difference between the tones changes with R. The range is unambiguous up to Rmax = c / (2 Δf), where Δf is the spacing between the two tones in Hz; wider spacing gives a shorter unambiguous range. Only a few universities teach this as multiple-frequency radar.

Applications follow directly. A police speed gun is CW: it reads fd. A radio altimeter is FMCW: it reads fb downward to the ground. A tank level gauge is usually FMCW: it measures surface distance from fb. A CW version can only flag that the surface has reached a set level, not how far away it is. A proximity fuse and an intruder sensor use the same Doppler or beat signal.

6.6 MTI and pulse Doppler radar

A radar sees strong echoes from ground, sea, buildings and rain. These echoes are clutter. Clutter can hide an aircraft because it is much stronger and has no Doppler shift. MTI and pulse Doppler radar solve this by comparing successive pulses and cancelling anything stationary. The delay-line canceller gives zeros at Doppler frequencies called blind speeds, fixed by wavelength and PRF. Pulse Doppler radar adds a bank of Doppler filters, so it can also measure velocity. These ideas are used in airport surveillance, weather radar and fighter fire control.

You need: the Doppler shift from 6.5, and PRF and range gating from 6.4.

Clutter and the moving-target problem

An echo from ground, sea, buildings or rain is called clutter. Clutter can be 40–60 dB stronger than an aircraft echo. A fixed threshold high enough to reject clutter also rejects the aircraft. The Doppler shift separates them. A target with radial velocity vr gives fd = 2vr/λ, where λ is the wavelength and fd the Doppler frequency in hertz. Stationary clutter has fd = 0. MTI stores the video of one pulse, compares it with the next pulse, and subtracts. Any echo with the same phase cancels. A moving target changes phase, so a residue remains. The subtraction is done separately in each range bin: the sample at delay 2R/c is kept and cancelled bin by bin. This is range gating. Coherent MTI uses a stable transmitted phase reference; noncoherent MTI uses echo amplitude changes and suits magnetron transmitters.

Single and double delay-line cancellers

A single delay-line canceller subtracts the previous pulse from the present pulse after a delay Tr. Tr is the pulse repetition time in seconds. Its transfer function magnitude is

H(fd) = 2 |sin(π fd Tr)|

H(fd) is the voltage response at Doppler frequency fd in Hz; Tr is the pulse repetition time in s.

The response is zero when fd = n/Tr = n PRF, with n = 0, 1, 2, ... and PRF the pulse repetition frequency in hertz. Those Doppler frequencies are blind speeds. In terms of target speed,

vb = n λ PRF / 2

vb is the blind radial speed in m/s; λ is the wavelength in m; PRF is in Hz; n = 1, 2, 3, ...

The response peaks at odd multiples of PRF/2 with value 2. A double canceller cascades two single cancellers. Its magnitude is |H| = 4 sin²(π fd Tr). The notch around each zero is wider, so slow clutter is rejected better, but low-Doppler targets lose more signal and phase errors hurt more. Improvement factor I is the output signal-to-clutter ratio divided by the input signal-to-clutter ratio, usually in dB. Staggered PRF changes the PRF pulse to pulse, so the zeros move and no single speed is blind all the time; the price is range-ambiguity bookkeeping.

Pulse Doppler radar and MTD

Pulse Doppler radar sends many pulses per dwell and passes each range bin through a bank of Doppler filters. Each filter covers a Doppler band. The output is a range-Doppler map. The filter with the largest output gives the target velocity; a longer dwell gives finer velocity resolution. Platform motion spreads the clutter spectrum, so airborne and spaceborne radars need clutter compensation. MTD, the moving target detector, is a practical combination: a canceller followed by a small Doppler filter bank, common in airport surveillance. Coherent MTI uses the transmitter phase reference; noncoherent MTI uses amplitude only and works with magnetron transmitters. Uses include airport surveillance, weather Doppler radar for rain motion, and fighter fire control.

  • MTI removes stationary clutter; it does not measure velocity.
  • Pulse Doppler measures velocity by which filter is strongest.
  • Blind speeds come from Doppler nulls; range ambiguities come from PRF.

6.7 Tracking radar: monopulse, conical scan, sequential lobing

A search radar sweeps a volume and reports whatever it finds. A tracking radar stares at one target and follows it with a servo loop that keeps the antenna pointed. That loop needs an angle error signal: a voltage that is zero when the antenna points exactly at the target and changes sign when the target drifts off. Three classic methods build it: sequential lobing, conical scan, and monopulse. Monopulse gets the error from a single pulse, which is why airport approach radars and missile seekers use it.

You need 6.1 for the antenna and receiver chain, and Unit 3 for beam squint and array patterns.

From search to track: the error signal

A search radar sweeps its beam through a volume and reports every echo, with an angle only as fine as the beamwidth. A tracking radar picks one target and stays pointed at it. It closes a loop: antenna, receiver, angle error, servo motor, back to the antenna.

Boresight is the electrical axis of the antenna: the direction in which the angle error signal is zero. A little to one side the error takes one sign, a little to the other side the opposite sign, and its size grows with the angle. A clean zero crossing at boresight is the whole requirement.

ε = k θ

ε is the angle error voltage in volts; θ is the off-boresight angle in degrees; k is the error slope in volts per degree, set by the beam shape — a narrow beam gives a steeper slope.

  1. The antenna dwells on the predicted target position for one or more pulses.
  2. The receiver measures the echo and the angle sensor produces ε.
  3. The servo amplifies ε and turns the antenna until ε falls to zero.
  4. The loop keeps the antenna in step with the target's motion.

The loop is a control system. Its bandwidth must be wide enough to follow a manoeuvring aircraft and narrow enough to average out receiver noise.

Sequential lobing and conical scan

Sequential lobing, also called lobe switching, uses two beams squinted to either side of boresight and switches between them pulse by pulse. A target on boresight gives equal echoes; a target off to one side gives a bigger echo in the nearer beam, and the difference carries the angle.

ε = (A_L − A_R) / (A_L + A_R) ≈ k θ

A_L and A_R are the echo amplitudes on the left and right beams; ε is zero on boresight and changes sign across it; θ is the off-boresight angle and k absorbs the proportionality.

  • The beams are sampled on different pulses, so a target whose echo strength fluctuates looks like a moving target and produces angle noise.
  • Each beam gets only half the dwell time, which costs signal-to-noise ratio.
  • It is simple and needs one receiver, but it is slow.

Conical scan uses one beam, squinted off axis and rotated about the boresight at the scan rate — a few tens of hertz — so the beam traces a cone. The echo amplitude then rises and falls once per revolution. The depth of the modulation gives the size of the angle error; its phase, compared with the rotation, gives the direction.

  • One receiver and one feed, so it is cheap and was standard on early airborne and shipborne radars.
  • The scan-rate modulation is a known signature: a jammer that repeats the echo amplitude-modulated at the scan rate creates a false angle error and pulls the antenna off target.
  • The scan rate lies in the band where target fading and glint carry most of their energy, so a filter tuned to the scan rate cannot separate real angle modulation from amplitude noise. Doppler processing does not help either, because the scan-rate sidebands sit far below the target Doppler shift, which is in kilohertz and above.

Monopulse: sum and difference from one pulse

Monopulse means simultaneous lobing. Four horns, each squinted into one quadrant, feed a comparator built from magic tees or hybrid rings (see waveguide tees and directional couplers). The comparator adds and subtracts the four signals to form three channels.

Σ = A + B + C + D;  Δaz = (A + B) − (C + D);  Δel = (A + C) − (B + D)

A, B, C, D are the signals from the four squinted beams; Σ is the sum channel used for detection and range; Δaz and Δel are the difference channels, each zero when the target is on boresight in that plane. Number the horns A top-left, B bottom-left, C top-right, D bottom-right. Then A + B is the left column, C + D the right column, A + C the top row, B + D the bottom row.

θ = (Δ / Σ) / k

θ is the angle error in degrees; Δ/Σ is the difference-to-sum ratio, a pure number; k is the error slope in per degree, measured in calibration.

Dividing by Σ is what makes the reading useful. A strong echo and a weak echo from the same direction give very different Δ but the same Δ/Σ, so the indicated angle does not depend on range, target size or transmitter power. Because all four beams are sampled by the same pulse, target fluctuation cannot create an angle error. The cost is hardware: three matched receivers, or one receiver switched fast between channels.

Phase-comparison monopulse uses two parallel beams from separate apertures instead of squinted beams. The angle comes from the phase difference between the received signals, needing phase detectors that share one reference oscillator. Only a few universities teach it in detail; the normalisation and the servo are the same.

Range tracking, memory and accuracy limits

Angle is half the job; the range gate must track too. A split gate places an early gate and a late gate either side of the echo. If the echo is centred, both collect equal energy; if it drifts, the difference drives the gate delay and the range reading follows.

  • Acquisition puts the target inside the gates and switches the loop to track; loss of echo puts the loop into memory, where it coasts on the last velocity until the target reappears.
  • Thermal noise sets a random angle error that falls as the error slope rises and as signal-to-noise ratio rises.
  • Glint is worse than noise: when two scattering points on an aircraft add and cancel, the apparent position wanders by metres.

Worked example

Question. A monopulse radar reads Δ = 1.2 V and Σ = 10 V on the same pulse, with error slope k = 1.8 per degree. (a) Find Δ/Σ. (b) Find the angle error. (c) Find the cross-range offset at range 20 km. (d) What does the servo do next?

  1. Δ/Σ = 1.2 V / 10 V = 0.12, a pure ratio.
  2. θ = (Δ/Σ) / k = 0.12 / 1.8 per degree = 0.0667 degrees.
  3. Cross-range offset = R tan θ = 20 000 m × tan(0.0667°) = 20 000 × 1.1636 × 10⁻³ = 23.27 m.
  4. The servo drives the antenna 0.0667° toward the target so that Δaz returns to zero.

Answer. Δ/Σ = 0.12, the angle error is 0.0667° (a cross-range offset of 23.27 m at 20 km), and the servo turns the antenna 0.0667° to null the difference channel.

Exam traps

  • Using Δ alone as the error. Without dividing by Σ the indicated angle changes when echo strength, range or target size changes. The error signal is Δ/Σ.
  • Monopulse means one pulse, not one beam: it needs several squinted beams or apertures, a comparator, and parallel receiver channels.
  • In conical scan the modulation depth gives the size of the angle error and the phase gives the direction. Swapping these two inverts the correction.
  • The error slope k is in volts per degree in the lobing form and per degree in the ratio form. Mixing degrees and radians throws the answer off by 57.3.
  • Sequential lobing and conical scan compare echoes taken at different times, so a fluctuating target looks like angle motion. Monopulse does not.

6.8 Detection in noise: matched filter and CFAR

A radar receiver never sees a clean echo; it sees echo plus noise. Detection is the decision: target present or absent, from one noisy observation. The matched filter is the receiver that gives the largest possible peak signal-to-noise ratio for a known pulse shape. A constant false alarm rate (CFAR) threshold then keeps false alarms steady when clutter changes. This chain is used in airport surveillance, weather radars and missile seekers.

You need: echo power and minimum detectable signal from 6.2, and noise figure and bandwidth from Unit 5, Noise figure, cascaded noise and sensitivity.

The detection problem and matched filter

Detection is a two-choice problem. Under H0 the input is r(t)=n(t). Under H1 it is r(t)=s(t)+n(t). Here s(t) is the known received pulse in volts; n(t) is white Gaussian noise with two-sided power spectral density N0/2 in W/Hz. N0 is the one-sided noise density in W/Hz. The receiver must decide H1 or H0 from one observation of length T.

Pass r(t) through a linear filter h(t) and sample at time T. The peak signal-to-noise ratio is the signal power at T divided by the noise power at T. The filter that maximises this ratio is the matched filter.

h(t) = s(T − t)

h(t) is the filter impulse response; s(T − t) is the received pulse reversed in time and shifted to end at the sampling instant T.

SNRmax = 2E / N0

SNRmax is the peak power signal-to-noise ratio at the filter output; E is the pulse energy in joules, E = ∫ s²(t) dt over the pulse; N0 is the one-sided noise power spectral density in W/Hz.

The pulse shape cancels out. Only the energy E matters, not whether the pulse is rectangular, triangular or Gaussian. A correlation receiver multiplies r(t) by s(t), integrates over T and samples at T. It gives the same number, so matched filtering and correlation are two views of one operation.

For a rectangular pulse of average power Pt in watts and width τ in seconds, E=Ptτ. Then SNRmax=2Ptτ/N0. The matched filter is an integrate-and-dump over τ seconds.

Threshold, false alarm and integration

After the filter, the detector decides. An envelope detector gives |y(T)|. A square-law detector gives |y(T)|². Noise alone gives a Rayleigh-distributed envelope. For envelope voltage V and threshold VT, Pfa=exp(−VT²/(2σ²)). Pfa is the probability of false alarm; σ² is the variance of each quadrature noise component in V².

Raising VT lowers Pfa but also lowers Pd, the probability of detecting a real target. The Neyman–Pearson rule fixes Pfa first and then chooses the threshold that gives the largest Pd. Plotting Pd against Pfa gives the receiver operating characteristic, or ROC. Signal plus noise has a Rician envelope, and Pd comes from the Rician or Marcum Q function.

Coherent integration adds complex matched-filter outputs from N pulses before detection. For equal pulses the SNR rises about N times. Noncoherent integration adds envelope outputs after detection. It is simpler but loses some SNR. Swerling models (Swerling 1 to 4) only name how target cross section fluctuates; they change the ROC, not the matched filter.

CFAR and pulse compression

A fixed threshold fails when clutter or receiver noise changes. A strong rain cell raises the noise mean, so the same VT gives a high Pfa. CFAR estimates the local noise from neighbouring range cells. In cell-averaging CFAR, the cell under test (CUT) is the cell being tested. Guard cells on each side are left out so target spill-over does not bias the estimate. Training cells further out are averaged to give noise estimate Z.

Pfa = (1 + α/N)^−N

Pfa is the required false-alarm probability; α is the threshold multiplier, so the threshold is αZ; N is the number of training cells used in the average Z.

Solve this for α when Pfa is specified. The estimate Z is noisy, so CFAR needs extra SNR compared with a known fixed threshold. That extra SNR is CFAR loss. Ordered-statistic and greatest-of CFAR are common variants.

Waveform design adds resolution without shortening the pulse. A linear FM chirp sweeps frequency by B Hz over τ seconds. The matched filter compresses it to a pulse of width about 1/B seconds. The time-bandwidth product τB is the compression ratio. Range resolution is ΔR=c/(2B); c is the speed of light, 3×10⁸ m/s, and B is the chirp bandwidth in Hz. Long τ gives energy for detection range; large B gives fine range resolution.

Worked example

Question. A radar echo at the receiver input has Pr = 1×10⁻¹³ W. The pulse width is τ = 1 μs. The receiver has noise bandwidth B = 1 MHz, noise figure F = 10 dB (a ratio of 10), at T0 = 290 K. Find the input SNR and the matched-filter output SNR. Use k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K.

  1. Convert the noise figure to a ratio: F = 10 dB gives F = 10.
  2. Find the receiver noise power: N = k T0 F B = 1.38×10⁻²³ × 290 × 10 × 1×10⁶ = 4.002×10⁻¹⁴ W ≈ 4.0×10⁻¹⁴ W.
  3. Input SNR = Pr / N = 1×10⁻¹³ / 4.002×10⁻¹⁴ = 2.499 ≈ 2.5. In decibels, 10 log₁₀(2.5) = 3.98 dB ≈ 4.0 dB.
  4. Find the one-sided noise density: N0 = k T0 F = 1.38×10⁻²³ × 290 × 10 = 4.002×10⁻²⁰ W/Hz ≈ 4.0×10⁻²⁰ W/Hz.
  5. Find the pulse energy: E = Pr τ = 1×10⁻¹³ × 1×10⁻⁶ = 1.0×10⁻¹⁹ J.
  6. Matched-filter output SNR = 2E / N0 = 2×1.0×10⁻¹⁹ / 4.002×10⁻²⁰ = 4.998 ≈ 5.0. In decibels, 10 log₁₀(5.0) = 6.99 dB ≈ 7.0 dB.
  7. The gain over the input SNR is 5.0 / 2.5 = 2, that is 3 dB. The matched filter buys a factor of 2 over filtering with bandwidth 1/τ.

Answer. The receiver noise is 4.0×10⁻¹⁴ W, input SNR is 2.5 (4.0 dB), N0 is 4.0×10⁻²⁰ W/Hz, pulse energy is 1.0×10⁻¹⁹ J, and matched-filter output SNR is 5.0 (7.0 dB).

Exam traps

  • The matched filter maximises peak SNR, not Pd directly. Pd follows only after the threshold is set by Pfa, and CFAR loss is counted.
  • Do not mix N0 and N0/2. Use the one-sided N0 in 2E/N0, and note that the two-sided density is N0/2.
  • Input SNR uses receiver noise bandwidth B. Matched-filter output SNR uses pulse energy E and N0; do not plug B into 2E/N0.
  • The CFAR multiplier α is not an SNR. It is the factor that multiplies the local noise estimate to set the threshold.
  • Pulse compression improves range resolution through bandwidth B. It does not create extra pulse energy; energy still comes from pulse width τ.

6.9 Radar antennas, transmitters, receivers and duplexers

A radar is not only a block diagram. The antenna must form a narrow beam and scan it, the transmitter must deliver a short high-power pulse, and the receiver must hear a very weak echo. A duplexer lets both share one antenna without burning the receiver. Modulators shape the pulse, displays turn echoes into a map, and the atmosphere bends and attenuates the wave. This chapter connects the blocks to real hardware: dish and array antennas, tubes and solid-state transmitters, superheterodyne receivers, TR tubes, PPI displays, and the propagation effects an operator sees.

You need: Chapter 6.1 for the block diagram, Unit 3 for reflectors and arrays, Unit 4 for tubes and circulators, and Unit 5 for receivers and noise.

Antennas: dish, shaped beam and array

A radar needs a high-gain antenna with low sidelobes. A parabolic reflector puts a small feed at the focus. The feed illuminates the dish, and the dish forms a narrow pencil beam. Some radars scan by moving the feed instead of the heavy dish, for example a nodding feed used for height finding while the dish rotates in azimuth. Many simple search radars just rotate the whole antenna. Beamwidth is roughly 70λ/D degrees, where λ is wavelength and D is dish diameter. A search radar often uses a cosecant-squared pattern: gain falls as csc²θ above the horizon. Here θ is elevation angle above the horizon. This gives a more even echo from a target at constant height as its range changes.

A lens antenna bends rays to a focus and can give a shaped beam. A slotted waveguide array is a flat high-gain antenna made of slots cut in a waveguide. A phased array uses many elements and phase shifters. The beam is steered by changing phase, with no moving parts. A passive array shares one transmitter, while an active array has a transmit/receive module at every element. Unit 3 gives the pattern details and Unit 4 gives ferrite and diode phase shifters. A radome covers the antenna and must pass the radar band with low loss.

Transmitters and modulators

Transmitters supply short high-power pulses. The magnetron is a crossed-field oscillator that gives high pulse power at low cost; many marine and weather radars use it. A klystron or travelling wave tube (TWT) is an amplifier, so it can follow a stable low-power source and keep phase coherence for MTI. Solid-state transmitters use many gallium nitride (GaN) modules and combine their outputs. They are reliable and need lower voltage, but a single module has modest peak power.

Peak power Ppeak and average power Pavg are linked by duty cycle: Pavg = Ppeak × duty, where duty = τ/T = τ fprf. Here τ is pulse width, T is pulse repetition interval and fprf is pulse repetition frequency. Cooling must remove the average heat, not the peak. The modulator forms the pulse. A line-type modulator charges a pulse forming network (PFN) and discharges it through a switch into the tube; a hard-tube modulator uses an active tube to shape the pulse. Rise time and flat top matter because they set the pulse spectrum; the smallest usable range is set mainly by the pulse width and the duplexer recovery time. Unit 4 covers tube operation.

Receiver and display

The receiver is superheterodyne. A low-noise RF stage sets the noise figure. A mixer converts RF to IF using the STALO, a stable local oscillator. An image-reject filter before the mixer removes the image. IF gain and a detector produce video. A log amplifier compresses a wide dynamic range so weak echoes and strong clutter fit one display. Dynamic range is the ratio of largest to smallest signal handled without distortion. I and Q outputs keep amplitude and phase for Doppler and MTI. CFAR (constant false alarm rate) is a threshold that adapts to the local clutter level. An A-scope plots echo amplitude against range. A B-scope plots range against azimuth. A PPI is a plan view with a rotating sweep. Modern radars use raster displays and scan conversion, with synthetic video from the processor.

Duplexer and propagation

The duplexer connects one antenna to the transmitter and the receiver. During transmit, the transmit-receive (TR) tube fires and shorts the receiver; the anti-transmit-receive (ATR) tube detunes the transmitter branch during receive. Gas tubes recover in microseconds. A circulator duplexer uses a ferrite circulator. The isolation needed is IdB = 10 log10(Ptx / Psafe), where IdB is isolation in decibels, Ptx is transmitter peak power in watts and Psafe is the maximum power the receiver can absorb in watts. During the pulse, spike leakage can exceed the steady isolation, so a receiver protector clips it. Recovery must be shorter than the round-trip time to the nearest target. In propagation, standard refraction is modelled by a 4/3 earth radius. Ducting traps energy and gives anomalous long-range echoes. Rain attenuates millimetre waves. Near the ground, multipath causes lobing.

Worked example

Question. A radar transmitter delivers 100 kW peak power. The receiver front end can safely absorb 10 mW. Find the duplexer isolation needed in decibels. A practical TR tube plus circulator gives 60 to 70 dB. Explain why a receiver protector is still used.

  1. Write the powers in watts: Ptx = 100 kW = 100 × 10³ W; Psafe = 10 mW = 10 × 10⁻³ W.
  2. Required power ratio is Ptx / Psafe = (100 × 10³) / (10 × 10⁻³) = 1 × 10⁷.
  3. Isolation in decibels is IdB = 10 log10(1 × 10⁷) = 10 × 7 = 70 dB.
  4. A practical duplexer gives about 60 to 70 dB. The remaining leakage appears as a short spike at the start of the pulse, so a receiver protector clips it.

Answer. The duplexer must provide 70 dB of isolation; practical hardware is close to this, so spike leakage still needs a receiver protector.

Exam traps

  • Duplexer isolation is not one fixed number for all time. Leakage during the pulse and the recovery period is far larger than in the listening period, so spike leakage and recovery time decide receiver damage and minimum range.
  • Peak power and average power are not the same. Average power is peak power times duty cycle, and cooling depends on average power.
  • A magnetron is an oscillator, not an amplifier; a klystron and a TWT are amplifiers that can keep phase coherence.
  • A cosecant-squared pattern is not a pencil beam. It gives up gain at high elevation angles (near overhead, short range) so that echoes from targets at constant altitude are more uniform as range changes.
  • A radome is not a simple waterproof cover; a wet or lossy radome can reduce gain and raise sidelobes.

Takeaways

  1. A radar makes one basic measurement—echo delay gives range, antenna pointing angle gives direction—and every other block supports that measurement.
  2. Echo power falls as the fourth power of range, so long range is bought with high transmit power, high antenna gain, low receiver noise or a larger target cross section.

Azimuth
The horizontal angle of a target measured from a radar reference direction.
Blind speed
A radial target speed at which the Doppler shift looks like a multiple of the PRF and an MTI filter cancels the target.
Boresight
The direction along the antenna axis where gain is maximum.
Circulator
A ferrite three-port device that routes microwave power in one direction around its ports.

Unwanted radar echoes from ground, sea, rain, birds or other non-target objects.
Coherent radar
A radar that preserves a phase reference from pulse to pulse so Doppler can be measured.
Conical scan
A tracking method that rotates a squinted beam around boresight and uses the echo amplitude modulation for angle error.
Constant false alarm rate (CFAR)
An adaptive threshold method that keeps the false-alarm probability fixed as the noise level changes.
Delay-line canceller
An MTI filter that subtracts a previous pulse echo from the current echo to remove stationary clutter.
Difference channel
In monopulse, the antenna output proportional to the angle of the target off boresight.
Duplexer
A device that lets one antenna serve both transmitter and receiver while protecting the receiver from transmit power.
Duty cycle
The fraction of time the transmitter is on, D = τ/T_p = τ f_p.
Elevation
The vertical angle of a target above or below the horizontal plane.
Low-noise amplifier (LNA)
The receiver front-end amplifier designed to add very little noise to the weak echo.
Matched filter
A filter matched to a known pulse that maximizes peak signal-to-noise ratio in white noise.
Noncoherent radar
A radar that detects the echo envelope without keeping pulse-to-pulse phase information.
Phased array
An antenna whose beam is steered electronically by controlling the phase of many radiating elements.
Probability of detection
The probability that a target echo crosses the detection threshold.
Propagation ducting
Bending of radar waves in atmospheric layers that can extend range beyond the normal horizon.
Pulse compression
A technique that transmits a long coded pulse and compresses it in the receiver to get short-pulse range resolution.

Radome
A protective cover over an antenna that is transparent to radar waves.

A tracking method that switches between squinted beams and compares their echo amplitudes.
