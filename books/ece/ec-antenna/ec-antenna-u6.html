6.1 Radar principle, block diagram and frequency bands

A radar sends out a burst of radio energy and listens for the echo. The round-trip delay of the echo gives the range of the target, and the direction the antenna points gives its bearing. Every other part of a radar exists to make those two measurements reliably in noise and clutter. The same idea runs air traffic control at an airport, weather radar over the Bay of Bengal, a police speed gun on the highway and the radio altimeter of an aircraft. This chapter builds the pulse radar block diagram and then chooses a frequency band for the job.

You need antenna gain and beamwidth from Antenna fundamentals and radiation, and the speed of light, c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s.

Delay gives range, pointing gives direction

Radar is short for radio detection and ranging. The transmitter sends a short pulse, the antenna focuses it into a beam, and any target in the beam reflects a tiny part of it back. The receiver notes when the echo arrives.

R = c t / 2

R is the target range in metres; c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s is the speed of light; t is the round-trip delay of the echo in seconds.

The 2 is there because the wave goes out and comes back, so only half of the delay belongs to the outward trip. A handy rule: every 1 µs of delay is 150 m of range, so 124 µs is 18.6 km.

Direction comes from the antenna. A narrow beam gives a precise bearing in azimuth and elevation, so the beamwidth sets the angle accuracy. A moving target also shifts the echo frequency; that Doppler shift gives speed and is the subject of CW and FMCW radar.

A pulse of width τ occupies cτ metres of space. Two targets on the same bearing are seen as two only if their echoes do not overlap, which needs a range difference of at least half the pulse length:

ΔR = c τ / 2

ΔR is the range resolution in metres; τ is the pulse width in seconds.

A monostatic radar uses one antenna for transmit and receive, which is the usual case and the one this unit assumes. A bistatic radar has its transmitter and receiver at separate sites.

The pulse radar block diagram

The figure shows the blocks and the path of the pulse and the echo. Each block has one job:

  • Synchronizer (timer): the master clock. It sets the pulse repetition frequency (PRF), triggers the modulator, and starts the range count at the instant the pulse leaves.
  • Modulator: in a radar built around a magnetron or klystron, it is the high-voltage pulse generator that switches the transmitter tube on for exactly τ seconds. In a solid-state radar a low-power generator forms the pulse and the amplifiers raise it.
  • Transmitter: produces the high-power pulse, from kilowatts to megawatts peak with a magnetron, klystron, TWT or solid-state amplifiers.
  • Duplexer: a switch that connects the antenna to the transmitter during the pulse and to the receiver between pulses. It uses TR and ATR gas tubes or a ferrite circulator with a limiter.
  • Antenna: a high-gain reflector or array that forms the beam and scans it, mechanically or electronically.
  • Receiver: a superheterodyne chain (low-noise amplifier, mixer, IF amplifier, detector); the echo reaches it only through the duplexer.
  • Signal processor: matched filtering, clutter rejection (MTI or pulse Doppler) and the detection threshold.
  • Display: a plan position indicator (PPI) shows each target at its range and bearing.

Choose the band by the job, not by a ranking. Lower bands lose less to the air and to rain, and they see less rain clutter, because rain backscatter rises steeply with frequency. Their cost is size: beamwidth grows with wavelength, so a narrow low-band beam needs a big antenna, and the wider beam lights up a larger patch of ground. Higher bands give a narrow beam from a small antenna and support short pulses for fine resolution, but rain attenuation rises and high transmitter power is harder to build.

So long-range air surveillance sits at L band, airport and weather radar at S band, airborne radar at X band, and car radar at 77 GHz, where a hand-sized antenna still gives a narrow beam. Refraction, ducting and rain loss are covered in Antenna arrays, types and propagation.

Worked example

Question. A pulse radar transmits a 1 µs pulse. The echo from a target arrives 124 µs after the pulse leaves the antenna. Find (a) the target range, (b) the length of the pulse in space and (c) the range resolution.

  1. Data: c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s, t = 124 µs = 124 × 10⁻⁶ s, τ = 1 µs = 1 × 10⁻⁶ s.
  2. Range: R = c t / 2 = (3 × 10⁸ × 124 × 10⁻⁶) / 2 = 37 200 / 2 = 18 600 m = 18.6 km.
  3. Pulse length in space: c τ = 3 × 10⁸ × 1 × 10⁻⁶ = 300 m.
  4. Range resolution: ΔR = c τ / 2 = 300 / 2 = 150 m.
  5. Check with the rule of thumb: 124 µs × 150 m per µs = 18 600 m.

Answer. R = 18.6 km; the pulse is 300 m long in space; two targets on the same bearing must be at least 150 m apart in range to be seen as two.

Worked example 2

Question. An air surveillance radar needs a 1.5° beamwidth. Using θ ≈ 70λ/D degrees for a dish of diameter D, find the dish size at L band (1.3 GHz), S band (2.8 GHz) and X band (9.4 GHz), and say which band a rooftop installation would choose.

  1. Rearrange: D = 70λ/θ, with θ in degrees and λ = c/f.
  2. L band: λ = 3 × 10⁸ / 1.3 × 10⁹ = 0.2308 m; D = 70 × 0.2308 / 1.5 = 10.8 m.
  3. S band: λ = 3 × 10⁸ / 2.8 × 10⁹ = 0.1071 m; D = 70 × 0.1071 / 1.5 = 5.0 m.
  4. X band: λ = 3 × 10⁸ / 9.4 × 10⁹ = 0.0319 m; D = 70 × 0.0319 / 1.5 = 1.49 m.
  5. The same beam needs a dish about 7 times larger at L band than at X band, because D grows in proportion to λ.

Answer. D ≈ 10.8 m at L band, 5.0 m at S band and 1.49 m at X band. A rooftop set would take X band for its small antenna, accepting more rain loss; a long-range airport radar accepts the larger S-band antenna to keep rain loss low.

Exam traps

  • Writing R = c t instead of R = c t / 2. The delay covers the trip out and back.
  • Mixing up the pulse length in space, cτ, with the range resolution, cτ/2. The resolution is half the pulse length.
  • Drawing the antenna straight into the receiver. On receive the echo passes back through the duplexer; on transmit the duplexer cuts the receiver off.
  • Treating band letters as exact limits, or assuming a higher band is always better. Rain loss and transmitter power can make a high band worse for long range.

6.2 Radar range equation and range performance

The echo from a target is extremely weak: a radar may send hundreds of kilowatts and get back a fraction of a picowatt. The radar range equation follows the power from the transmitter, through the spreading beam, off the target, back through space and into the antenna. It ties transmit power, antenna gain, wavelength, target cross section and receiver noise to one number: the greatest range at which the target can be detected. Every radar design starts from this budget.

You need antenna gain and effective aperture (G = 4πAe/λ²) from Antenna fundamentals and radiation, and noise figure from RF circuits and transceivers.

Building the echo-power equation

Take a monostatic radar. The transmitter delivers Pt watts to an antenna of gain G (a ratio, not dB). On the beam axis at range R metres the power density is PtG/(4πR²) W/m²: the power spread over a sphere of area 4πR², multiplied by the gain in the beam direction.

The target intercepts power equal to this density times its radar cross section σ (in m²), and reradiates it. σ is defined so that the reradiation can be treated as equal in all directions (see Radar cross section of targets). Spread over a second sphere on the way back, the echo density at the radar is PtGσ/((4π)²R⁴). Two spreadings of R² give the R⁴.

The antenna collects this density over its effective aperture Ae. With Ae = Gλ²/(4π) for the same antenna:

Pr = PtG²λ²σ / ((4π)³R⁴)

Pr is the received echo power in W; Pt the peak transmit power in W; G the antenna gain as a ratio; λ the wavelength in m; σ the radar cross section in m²; R the range in m.

In terms of aperture the same result is Pr = PtAe²σ/(4πλ²R⁴). G appears squared because it acts twice: once to focus the transmitted beam and once to capture the echo.

The minimum detectable signal

The receiver adds its own noise. Referred to the input, the noise power in the receiver bandwidth B is kT0BF, where k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K, T0 = 290 K and F is the noise figure as a ratio. That is the noise floor, not yet a detection. To declare a target with the wanted probabilities of detection and false alarm, the echo must exceed the noise by a required signal-to-noise ratio (S/N)min, typically 10 to 13 dB for one pulse:

Smin = kT0BF·(S/N)min
Here Smin is the minimum detectable signal in W; B is the receiver noise bandwidth in Hz, about 1/τ for a pulse of width τ; F and (S/N)min are ratios.

Losses in the waveguide, duplexer, radome and signal processing are gathered into a system loss L, a ratio greater than 1, which divides the echo: the useful power is Pr/L.

A radar rarely decides from one pulse. Integrating n pulses raises the signal-to-noise ratio, so the (S/N)min needed per pulse falls. Coherent integration (adding the pulses with their phases lined up) gains exactly n. Noncoherent integration (adding after the detector) gains less than n, close to √n when each pulse is weak. In the equations below, (S/N)min is the single-pulse value; with integration, divide it by the integration gain.

Maximum range and the fourth-root law

Set Pr/L = Smin and solve for R:

Rmax = [PtG²λ²σ / ((4π)³SminL)]1/4
Here Rmax is in metres when every other quantity is in SI units; with apertures, Rmax = [PtAe²σ/(4πλ²SminL)]1/4.

Use the G form or the Ae form, never a mixture. The fourth root rules radar design. Doubling the range needs 2⁴ = 16 times the power (12 dB). A target with 100 times the cross section is seen only 1001/4 = 3.16 times farther. The figure plots the echo power of the worked example against range for two targets.

In decibels the equation becomes a budget: 40 log10 Rmax = Pt(dBW) + 2G(dB) + 20 log10 λ + σ(dBsm) − 33.0 − Smin(dBW) − L(dB), where 33.0 dB is 10 log10(4π)³ = 10 log10 1984.4. Each line is easy to check, which is why exam answers lay it out as a table.

A search radar must cover a whole volume, not one target. Not every syllabus covers its search form, which uses the average power and the aperture: the product PavAe sets how large a volume can be searched in a given time at a given range.

Worked example

Question. A monostatic radar transmits Pt = 500 kW at 10 GHz with an antenna gain of 30 dB. Its minimum detectable signal is Smin = 1 × 10⁻¹³ W and the losses are 0 dB. (a) Find Rmax for a target of σ = 1 m². (b) Find the power needed to double this range.

  1. λ = c/f = 3 × 10⁸ / 10 × 10⁹ = 0.03 m. G = 1030/10 = 1000.
  2. Numerator: PtG²λ²σ = 5 × 10⁵ × 10⁶ × 9 × 10⁻⁴ × 1 = 4.5 × 10⁸ W·m⁴.
  3. Denominator: (4π)³SminL = 1984.4 × 1 × 10⁻¹³ × 1 = 1.984 × 10⁻¹⁰ W.
  4. Rmax⁴ = 4.5 × 10⁸ / 1.984 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 2.268 × 10¹⁸ m⁴, so Rmax = 38 806 m = 38.8 km.
  5. Doubling the range multiplies R⁴ by 16, so Pt = 16 × 500 kW = 8 MW.

Answer. Rmax = 38.8 km; doubling it needs 8 MW, 12 dB more power.

Question. An S-band surveillance radar works at 3 GHz with Pt = 1.5 MW, G = 33 dB, receiver bandwidth 1 MHz, noise figure 4 dB, required single-pulse (S/N)min = 13 dB and system loss 6 dB. Find Smin, then Rmax for a 2 m² aircraft. How far does it reach if 10 pulses are integrated coherently?

Exam traps

  • Taking the noise floor kT0BF as the minimum detectable signal. Detection needs the floor times (S/N)min; leaving it out makes Rmax too large by (S/N)min1/4, a factor of 2.1 for 13 dB.
  • Forgetting that G is squared. A 3 dB gain error becomes 6 dB in the echo.
  • Doubling range with doubled power. It takes 16 times the power, 12 dB more.
  • Putting F or G in dB into a ratio formula. 10 dB means a ratio of 10, and 30 dB a ratio of 1000.
  • Mixing units: λ in cm or R in km in a formula written for metres.

6.3 Radar cross section of targets

A radar sees the echo a target sends back, not its outline. Two objects of the same size can return echoes ten thousand times apart, because the echo depends on shape, aspect angle and wavelength. Radar engineers describe a target by one number, its radar cross section σ: an equivalent area in square metres. It sits in the radar range equation and decides how far away a target can be seen; stealth design is the art of making it small.

You need the radar range equation from Radar range equation and range performance.

The definition: an equivalent area

A target intercepts some of the incident power and scatters it in many directions. Replace it by an imaginary area that intercepts the incident power density and reradiates it equally in all directions, sized so that the echo density at the radar matches the real one. That area is σ:

σ = 4πR² · Ss / Si

σ is the radar cross section in m²; R is the target range in m; Si is the incident power density at the target and Ss the scattered power density back at the radar, both in W/m². Strictly the limit as R → ∞ is taken, so σ does not depend on range.

σ spans many powers of ten, so it is often given in decibels relative to 1 m²: σ(dBsm) = 10 log10(σ / 1 m²). Thus 1 m² is 0 dBsm, 100 m² is 20 dBsm and 0.01 m² is −20 dBsm.

σ is not the shadow area. A flat plate facing the radar has a σ thousands of times its area; a carefully shaped body can have far less than its area.

Sphere, plate and corner reflector

The metal sphere is the one target whose σ is known exactly at every frequency, and it looks the same from every side. With radius a and wavelength λ, the size is measured by ka = 2πa/λ. The figure shows the exact curve; it has three regions:

  • Rayleigh region, ka ≪ 1 (sphere much smaller than λ): σ = 9πa²(ka)⁴, so σ grows as a⁶ and as 1/λ⁴. Raindrops and insects sit here, which is why a shorter wavelength sees rain far more strongly.
  • Resonance (Mie) region, ka from about 1 to 10: σ swings above and below πa² as the creeping wave around the back adds to or cancels the front reflection. The highest peak, about 3.65πa², comes near ka = 1.
  • Optical region, ka above about 10: σ = πa², the projected area, independent of λ.

A flat metal plate seen face-on is the opposite extreme. Every point of it reflects in phase, so the fields add and the power grows as the area squared:

σ = 4πA² / λ²

A is the plate area in m²; λ is the wavelength in m. It holds only at normal incidence: a few degrees off, the in-phase sum cancels and σ falls by tens of dB.

A corner reflector is three metal plates at right angles (a trihedral). A ray bounces once off each face and leaves parallel to the way it came, so a large echo comes back over a wide cone of angles. For a triangular trihedral of edge a, σ = 4πa⁴/(3λ²); with a = 0.3 m at 9.4 GHz (λ = 0.0319 m), σ = 33.3 m², larger than the echo of many aircraft, from a frame you can carry. Small boats hoist one so marine radar sees them, and radar test ranges use them as calibration targets.

Aspect, fluctuation and stealth

Real targets are collections of panels, edges, cavities and engine inlets. As the target turns, these add and cancel, so σ swings by 10 to 20 dB over a few degrees. Detection theory treats σ as a random number; the fluctuation models are in Detection in noise.

These are orders of magnitude only; the same target changes by 20 dB between nose-on and broadside. Stealth lowers σ in three ways. Shaping removes flat faces and right angles facing likely radars and hides engine faces behind curved ducts. Radar-absorbing material turns the wave into heat in a lossy layer, but works well only over a band of frequencies and adds weight. Low-band radars, whose wavelength is close to the size of tail fins, push the target into the resonance region where shaping helps least.

Worked example

Question. A flat metal plate of area 1 m² faces an X-band radar at 10 GHz. (a) Find its σ in m² and dBsm. (b) A metal sphere has the same projected area, 1 m². Find its radius, ka and σ. (c) Compare the two echoes.

  1. λ = c/f = 3 × 10⁸ / 10 × 10⁹ = 0.03 m.
  2. Plate: σ = 4πA²/λ² = 4π × 1² / (0.03)² = 12.566 / 9 × 10⁻⁴ = 13 963 m², and 10 log10 13 963 = 41.4 dBsm.
  3. Sphere: πa² = 1 m² gives a = √(1/π) = 0.564 m.
  4. ka = (2π/0.03) × 0.564 = 209.4 × 0.564 = 118.2, far above 10: optical region, so σ = πa² = 1.0 m² = 0 dBsm.
  5. Ratio: 13 963 / 1.0 = 1.4 × 10⁴, that is 41.4 dB.

Answer. The plate has σ = 13 963 m² (41.4 dBsm) and the sphere 1.0 m² (0 dBsm). Facing the radar, the plate returns about 14 000 times the sphere's echo from the same projected area.

Worked example 2

Question. A raindrop is a sphere of radius 1 mm. Find its σ at 3 GHz and at 10 GHz, using the Rayleigh formula σ = 9πa²(ka)⁴, and the ratio of the two.

  1. At 3 GHz: λ = 0.1 m; ka = 2π × 0.001 / 0.1 = 0.0628 ≪ 1, so the Rayleigh formula applies.

Exam traps

  • Reading σ as physical area. "The target is 1 m²" means nothing without the band and the aspect.
  • Carrying a σ value from one band to another. A 1 m nose is in the resonance region at VHF (λ of a few metres) and in the optical region at X band, with very different σ.
  • Reading 0 dBsm as zero area. It is 1 m²; −20 dBsm is 0.01 m².
  • Using σ = 4πA²/λ² off normal incidence. A few degrees away the in-phase sum collapses.
  • Calling ka = 5 the optical region. The optical formula πa² is reliable only above ka ≈ 10.

6.4 Pulse repetition frequency and range ambiguities

A pulse radar sends a pulse, listens, and sends the next one. The pulse repetition frequency (PRF) sets how long it listens, and so the longest range it can measure without mixing up one pulse's echo with the next. PRF also sets the average power and how often the Doppler shift is sampled, so it is one of the first numbers chosen in any radar design. Air traffic, weather and airborne fire-control radars all pick their PRF around this compromise.

You need R = c t / 2 and the range resolution cτ/2 from Radar principle, block diagram and frequency bands.

PRF sets the listening window

PRF is the number of pulses sent per second. The time from one pulse to the next is the pulse repetition interval, Tr = 1/PRF. The transmitter is on for τ of each interval, and the receiver listens for the rest, Tr − τ. An echo is placed correctly only if it returns before the next pulse leaves:

Runamb = c Tr / 2 = c / (2 PRF)
Here Runamb is the maximum unambiguous range in m; Tr is in s; PRF is in Hz; c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. Because τ is far shorter than Tr, the small loss of listening time is usually neglected.

Turned round, a radar that must place targets correctly out to Rmax needs PRF ≤ c/(2Rmax): 150 km allows at most 1 kHz.

The fraction of time the transmitter is on is the duty cycle, d = τ/Tr = τ·PRF. The average power is Pav = Pt·d, with Pt the peak power. A 1 MW, 1 µs radar at 1 kHz has d = 0.001 and Pav = 1 kW. The cooling and the power supply are sized for Pav; the tube and the duplexer must survive Pt.

Second-time-around echoes and the blind zone

A target beyond Runamb still returns an echo, but it arrives after the next pulse has left. The radar measures delay from the latest pulse, so it shows the target too close. This is a second-time-around (or multiple-time-around) echo:

Rapp = R − n Runamb

Rapp is the apparent range; R is the true range; n is the whole number of intervals the echo is late, chosen so that 0 ≤ Rapp < Runamb.

The figure draws this for the worked example: a target at 200 km, at a PRF of 1 kHz, shows up at 50 km.

At the near end, the receiver is switched off while the pulse is sent and while the duplexer recovers. Echoes arriving then are lost, so there is a blind zone close to the radar:

Rmin = c (τ + trec) / 2

Rmin is the minimum range in m; τ is the pulse width and trec the duplexer and receiver recovery time, both in s. A 1 µs pulse alone blinds the first 150 m.

An echo can also be lost in the same way far out, when it arrives exactly while a later pulse is being sent; this is called eclipsing. The receiver timeline is cut into range gates, each one pulse width τ long in time and therefore cτ/2 long in range, and the processor examines each gate separately.

The PRF trade-off and multiple PRFs

PRF also samples the Doppler shift: a radar measuring phase once per pulse can only tell Doppler frequencies apart over a span of one PRF. So:

  • Low PRF (a few hundred hertz to a few kHz): long unambiguous range, but Doppler is badly ambiguous. Used by search and air traffic radars.
  • High PRF (tens to hundreds of kHz): Doppler is unambiguous for fast targets, but range is heavily ambiguous. Used by airborne pulse Doppler radars to see fast closing targets.
  • Medium PRF: both are ambiguous, but each only a little, and several PRFs together sort them out.

Multiple PRFs remove the ambiguity. The radar sends bursts at two or more PRFs. A real target folds to a different apparent range at each PRF, but only one true range fits every reading. With two PRFs whose unambiguous ranges are R1 and R2, the pair is unambiguous out to their least common multiple. Staggered PRF, which changes Tr from pulse to pulse, makes second-time-around echoes jump about while true echoes stay put; it also moves the blind speeds of MTI radar. The Doppler side of the trade is in CW and FMCW radar.

Worked example

Question. A radar has PRF = 1000 Hz, pulse width 1 µs and peak power 1 MW. Find Tr, the duty cycle, the average power, the unambiguous range and the blind range from the pulse alone. A target is really at 200 km: where does the radar show it?

  1. Tr = 1/PRF = 1/1000 = 1 ms.
  2. Duty cycle d = τ/Tr = 1 × 10⁻⁶ / 1 × 10⁻³ = 0.001 (0.1 %); Pav = 10⁶ × 0.001 = 1 kW.
  3. Runamb = c Tr/2 = 3 × 10⁸ × 10⁻³ / 2 = 150 000 m = 150 km.
  4. Blind range from the pulse: cτ/2 = 3 × 10⁸ × 10⁻⁶ / 2 = 150 m.
  5. True range 200 km lies one interval beyond: n = 1, Rapp = 200 − 150 = 50 km.

Answer. Tr = 1 ms, d = 0.001, Pav = 1 kW, Runamb = 150 km, blind range 150 m; the 200 km target is shown at 50 km.

Exam traps

  • Writing Runamb = c/PRF. The echo must go out and back within one interval, so it is c/(2 PRF).
  • Using τ where Tr belongs. τ sets the blind zone and the range resolution; Tr sets the unambiguous range.
  • Treating a second-time-around echo as noise. It is a real target, shown at R − nRunamb.
  • Saying a high PRF is always better. It improves Doppler coverage and average power, but shortens the unambiguous range.
  • Sizing the cooling for peak power. Heat follows the average power, Pt × duty cycle.

6.5 CW and FMCW radar; Doppler effect

A continuous-wave (CW) radar transmits all the time and never times a pulse. It listens for the small frequency shift that a moving target puts on the echo, the Doppler shift, and so it measures speed only. If the transmit frequency is swept in a straight line, the echo returns with a delay, and mixing echo with transmitter gives a beat frequency proportional to range. That is frequency-modulated CW (FMCW) radar, used in aircraft radio altimeters, tank level gauges and car radar; the plain CW radar is the police speed gun.

You need R = c t / 2 from Radar principle, block diagram and frequency bands and the idea of a mixer from RF circuits and transceivers.

The Doppler shift and the CW radar

The echo from a target at range R has travelled 2R, so its phase lags the transmitter by φ = 2π(2R/λ) = 4πR/λ radians. If the target moves, R changes and the phase turns; a turning phase is a frequency. With radial velocity vr = −dR/dt (positive when the target closes):

fd = 2 vr / λ = 2 vr f0 / c
Here fd is the Doppler shift in Hz; vr is the radial velocity in m/s, the part of the target velocity along the line of sight (v cos θ for a target moving at angle θ to that line); λ = c/f0 is the wavelength in m.

A closing target raises the echo frequency; an opening target lowers it. A target crossing the beam at right angles has vr = 0 and gives no shift at all.

A CW radar has one oscillator at f0 running continuously. Because the transmitter never stops, it usually has separate transmit and receive antennas, so the strong transmit signal does not swamp the receiver. A mixer multiplies the echo by a sample of the transmitter; a filter keeps the difference frequency fd, and a counter or FFT reads it. Better sets mix down to an intermediate frequency first, avoiding the flicker noise of the mixer at low frequencies, and use I and Q outputs to tell closing from opening targets.

A CW radar cannot measure range: the wave has no timing mark. It also ignores anything stationary (fd = 0), and two cars at the same speed at different distances give one tone.

FMCW radar: a sweep gives range

An FMCW radar sweeps its frequency linearly by Δf over a time Tm. The echo from range R returns after t = 2R/c, by which time the transmitter has moved on by Δf·t/Tm. Mixing echo with transmitter gives that difference, the beat frequency:

fb = 2 R Δf / (c Tm)

fb is the beat frequency in Hz; R the range in m; Δf the sweep width in Hz; Tm the duration of one sweep in s.

For a triangular sweep with modulation frequency fm (one up-sweep and one down-sweep each period), each sweep lasts Tm = 1/(2fm) and the formula becomes fb = 4RΔf·fm/c, the form many textbooks print. The range resolution depends only on the sweep width:

ΔR = c / (2 Δf)

ΔR is the range resolution in m. A 150 MHz sweep resolves 1 m.

A moving target adds its Doppler shift to the beat. On the up-sweep the two subtract, on the down-sweep they add (for a closing target), so the triangular sweep separates them:

fR = (fup + fdown) / 2
Here fR is the range part of the beat; fup = fR − fd and fdown = fR + fd are the measured beats, so fd = (fdown − fup)/2.

The figure draws both sweeps and both beats for the second worked example.

Multiple-frequency CW and where each is used

Range can also come from two CW tones f1 and f2. Each echo lags by 4πR/λ, so the phase difference between the two echoes is Δφ = 4πRΔf/c, with Δf = f2 − f1. Measuring Δφ gives R, unambiguously while Δφ stays below 2π, that is up to R = c/(2Δf): 1 MHz spacing gives 150 m. Not every syllabus covers this multiple-frequency method; it is used in survey instruments.

6.6 MTI and pulse Doppler radar

A radar beam also hits the ground, the sea, buildings and hills, and their echoes, called clutter, can be thousands of times stronger than an aircraft. Fixed clutter has no Doppler shift, while a moving target does. Moving target indication (MTI) compares successive pulses and cancels whatever has not changed; pulse Doppler radar goes further and sorts echoes into Doppler bins, so it also measures speed. The price is a set of blind speeds fixed by the wavelength and the PRF. Airport surveillance radars, weather radars and fighter radars all depend on these ideas.

You need the Doppler shift fd = 2vr/λ from CW and FMCW radar and the PRF from Pulse repetition frequency and range ambiguities.

Clutter and the coherent MTI radar

Clutter can be 40 to 60 dB stronger than an aircraft echo, so no threshold can pass the aircraft and stop the ground. The Doppler shift separates them. Fixed clutter (ground, buildings) has fd ≈ 0. Sea and rain clutter move with waves and wind, so they have a small Doppler spread that is often off zero; they are harder to cancel.

A pulse radar sees Doppler as a change of phase from pulse to pulse. A coherent MTI radar keeps a phase reference for this. A stable local oscillator (STALO) and a coherent oscillator (COHO) at the intermediate frequency drive the transmitter chain; on receive, a phase detector compares each echo with the COHO. A fixed target gives the same output on every pulse; a moving target's output changes by 2π fdTr radians from one pulse to the next.

The comparison is done separately for every range gate: the sample at delay 2R/c from one pulse is compared with the sample at the same delay from the previous pulse, exactly one pulse interval Tr earlier. A magnetron starts with a random phase on each pulse, so a magnetron radar either locks its COHO to each transmitted pulse or uses noncoherent MTI, which uses the clutter itself as the reference.

Delay-line cancellers and blind speeds

A single delay-line canceller subtracts the previous pulse's output from the present one. Fixed echoes cancel; a moving target leaves a residue whose size depends on fd:

|H(fd)| = 2 |sin(π fd Tr)|
Here H is the voltage response of the canceller (input amplitude 1); fd is the Doppler shift in Hz; Tr = 1/PRF is the pulse interval in s.

The response is zero whenever fd = n·PRF: the target then moves a whole number of half-wavelengths between pulses, its phase returns to the same value, and it cancels like clutter. The radial speeds at which this happens are the blind speeds:

vb = n λ PRF / 2

vb is the n-th blind speed in m/s, n = 1, 2, 3, …; λ is the wavelength in m; PRF is in Hz.

The response peaks at 2 when fd is an odd multiple of PRF/2. A double canceller (two in cascade) has |H| = 4 sin²(π fd Tr): its notch around zero is wider, so it rejects clutter with some spread better, but it also loses more of the slow targets. The quality of an MTI is given by its improvement factor: the signal-to-clutter ratio at the output divided by that at the input, averaged over all target speeds, in dB.

Staggered PRF changes Tr from pulse to pulse. A target is then blind only if its speed is blind at every interval. With two intervals in the ratio T1/T2 = a/b (whole numbers), the first common blind Doppler is a/T1, so the first blind speed rises to (a + b)/2 times the blind speed of a constant PRF with the average interval. The figure shows this for the second worked example. The cost: clutter cancellation is a little poorer, and the processing must allow for the changing interval.

Pulse Doppler radar and the MTD

A pulse Doppler radar sends a burst of N coherent pulses on each beam position and passes each range gate through a bank of N Doppler filters, usually an FFT. Each filter is PRF/N wide in Doppler; clutter falls in the filters near zero and is thrown away, and the filter with the largest output gives the target's speed. The result is a range-Doppler map.

For example, N = 16 pulses at PRF = 10 kHz give filters 10 000/16 = 625 Hz wide; at λ = 3 cm that is 625 × 0.03/2 = 9.4 m/s per filter, with speeds unambiguous over ±75 m/s. The same 10 kHz PRF leaves only 15 km of unambiguous range, which is why pulse Doppler radars use medium or high PRF and resolve range with several PRFs.

The moving target detector (MTD), used in airport surveillance radars, combines the two: a short canceller, then a small Doppler filter bank, with a separate threshold for each filter and a clutter map for the zero-Doppler filter. In short:

  • MTI removes fixed clutter; it does not measure speed.
  • Pulse Doppler measures speed from which filter is strongest.
  • Blind speeds come from the Doppler sampling by the PRF; range ambiguity comes from the listening time.

6.7 Tracking radar: monopulse, conical scan, sequential lobing

A search radar sweeps a volume and reports whatever it finds. A tracking radar stays on one target and follows it, with a servo loop that keeps the antenna pointed at it. The loop needs an angle error signal: zero when the antenna points exactly at the target, changing sign when the target drifts to the other side. Sequential lobing, conical scan and monopulse are three ways to make it; monopulse gets it from a single pulse, which is why missile seekers and precision instrumentation radars use it.

You need antenna beams and beamwidth from Antenna fundamentals and radiation, and magic tees and hybrids from Microwave components, devices and measurements.

The angle error signal

A tracking radar closes a loop: antenna, receiver, angle error detector, servo amplifier, motor and back to the antenna. The boresight (tracking axis) is the direction in which the error is zero. For a tracker this is not the peak of one beam: it is the crossover of the squinted beams, or the rotation axis of a conical scan.

ε ≈ k θ

ε is the angle error signal (a voltage, or a ratio in monopulse); θ is the angle of the target off boresight in degrees; k is the error slope, steeper for a narrower beam.

The servo turns the antenna until ε is zero, and keeps doing so as the target moves. Its bandwidth must be wide enough to follow a manoeuvring aircraft and narrow enough to average out receiver noise.

Sequential lobing and conical scan

Sequential lobing (lobe switching) switches between two beams squinted either side of boresight. A target on boresight gives equal echoes in both; off to one side, the nearer beam gives the larger echo:

ε = (A1 − A2) / (A1 + A2)
Here A1 and A2 are the echo amplitudes in the two beams; for example 1.1 and 0.9 give ε = 0.2/2.0 = 0.1. Dividing by the sum makes ε independent of echo strength. A second pair of beams gives the other plane.

Conical scan uses one beam, squinted off axis and rotated about the boresight at a few tens of revolutions per second, so the beam traces a cone. A target on the axis gives a steady echo. Off axis, the echo rises and falls once per revolution: the depth of this modulation gives how far off the target is, and its phase against the rotation gives which way.

Both methods compare echoes taken at different times. If the target's echo fluctuates between those times, that fluctuation looks like an angle error and adds angle noise; target fluctuations often have energy at a few to tens of hertz, right where the scan rate sits. Conical scan also has a known weakness: a jammer that repeats the echo modulated at the scan rate creates a false error and pulls the antenna away. Randomising the scan rate, or scanning only the receive beam while the transmit beam stays fixed (COSRO, conical scan on receive only), hides the scan rate from the jammer.

Monopulse: sum and difference from one pulse

Monopulse (simultaneous lobing) forms all the beams at once. Four horns at the focus of a reflector give four beams squinted into the four quadrants. A comparator of magic tees or hybrid rings adds and subtracts their outputs. Number the horns, seen from behind the feed, A top-left, B bottom-left, C top-right and D bottom-right:

Σ = A + B + C + D

Σ is the sum channel, a normal beam on boresight, used for detection and range.

Δaz = (A + B) − (C + D)

Δaz is the azimuth difference: left column minus right column; zero when the target is on boresight in azimuth.

Δel = (A + C) − (B + D)

Δel is the elevation difference: top row minus bottom row.

The angle comes from the ratio θ = (Δ/Σ)/k, with k now a slope in "per degree" found by calibration. Dividing by Σ makes the reading independent of range, target size and transmitter power, and the sign of Δ/Σ gives the side. Because every channel sees the same pulse, echo fluctuation cannot create an angle error. The cost is three matched receiver channels and a more complex feed. Phase-comparison monopulse uses two side-by-side apertures with parallel beams and reads the angle from the phase difference between them; the normalisation and the servo are the same.

Range tracking and accuracy

The range must be tracked too. A split gate puts an early gate and a late gate on either side of the echo; if the echo slides later, the late gate collects more energy, and the difference moves both gates until they balance. If the echo is lost, the tracker coasts on its last speed (memory) until the target returns. Thermal noise sets a random angle error that shrinks as S/N and the slope k grow. Glint, the wandering of the apparent centre as echoes from different parts of an aircraft add and cancel, is often larger at short range.

Worked example

Question. A monopulse radar reads Δaz = 1.2 V and Σ = 10 V on the same pulse; its calibrated slope is k = 1.8 per degree. (a) Find Δ/Σ and the angle error. (b) Find the cross-range offset of the target at 20 km. (c) What does the servo do?

  1. Δ/Σ = 1.2/10 = 0.12, a pure number.
  2. θ = (Δ/Σ)/k = 0.12/1.8 = 0.0667°.
  3. In radians: 0.0667 × π/180 = 1.164 × 10⁻³ rad. Offset = R tan θ ≈ Rθ = 20 000 × 1.164 × 10⁻³ = 23.3 m.
  4. The servo turns the antenna 0.0667° toward the side given by the sign of Δ/Σ, until Δaz is zero.

Answer. Δ/Σ = 0.12; the target is 0.0667° (23.3 m at 20 km) off boresight, and the servo turns the antenna by that amount to null the difference channel.

Exam traps

  • Using Δ alone as the error. Without dividing by Σ the reading changes with echo strength, range and target size.
  • Reading "monopulse" as one beam. It means one pulse; it needs several squinted beams, a comparator and parallel receivers.
  • Swapping depth and phase in conical scan. The depth gives how far off, the phase gives which way.
  • Mixing degrees and radians in the slope k, an error of 57.3 times.
  • Defining boresight as the gain peak of the antenna. For a tracker it is the axis where the error signal is zero.

6.8 Detection in noise: matched filter and CFAR

A radar receiver never sees a clean echo; it sees echo plus noise, and must decide in each range cell: target or no target. The matched filter is the receiver filter that gives the highest possible peak signal-to-noise ratio for a known pulse, and so the best chance of detection. A threshold then makes the decision, and a constant false alarm rate (CFAR) detector moves that threshold with the local noise and clutter so that false alarms stay steady. This chain runs in every surveillance, weather and tracking radar.

You need the noise power kT0BF and Smin from Radar range equation and range performance.

The matched filter

Detection is a choice between two cases: noise alone, r(t) = n(t), or echo plus noise, r(t) = s(t) + n(t). Here s(t) is the known echo pulse and n(t) is white noise of one-sided power spectral density N0 = kT0F in W/Hz. The receiver passes r(t) through a filter h(t) and samples the output at an instant T. The filter that makes the peak signal-to-noise ratio at T as large as possible is the matched filter:

h(t) = s(T − t)

h(t) is the impulse response: the expected pulse reversed in time and delayed so that it ends at the sampling instant T.

(S/N)max = 2E / N0

E = ∫s²(t) dt is the energy of the received pulse in J; N0 is in W/Hz. The 2E/N0 form counts the peak power of the IF carrier; on an average-power basis the same output is E/N0.

Only the energy matters, not the pulse shape. A long pulse of low power does as well as a short pulse of high power with the same energy, which is the basis of pulse compression below. A correlation receiver, which multiplies r(t) by a stored copy of s(t) and integrates, gives exactly the same output: matched filtering and correlation are two views of one operation. For a rectangular pulse of power Pr and width τ, E = Prτ, and an ordinary IF filter of bandwidth about 1.4/τ comes within 1 dB of the matched filter.

Threshold, false alarm and detection

After the filter, a detector takes the envelope |y|, or its square |y|² (a square-law detector), and compares it with a threshold. Noise alone gives a Rayleigh-distributed envelope. If each quadrature noise component has variance σn², the probability of a false alarm is:

Pfa = exp(−VT² / 2σn²)
Here VT is the threshold on the envelope voltage. For a square-law detector the same law reads Pfa = exp(−T/μ), where T is the power threshold and μ = 2σn² the mean noise power.

Raising the threshold lowers Pfa but also the probability of detection Pd. Radars fix Pfa first, often 10⁻⁶ per cell, and then need enough S/N to reach the wanted Pd: this is the Neyman–Pearson rule, and the curves of Pd against S/N at fixed Pfa (the receiver operating characteristic) give the (S/N)min of the range equation. Echo plus noise has a Rician envelope, and Pd comes from the Marcum Q function. A fluctuating target (described by the Swerling models, which give the probability distribution of σ) needs several dB more S/N for a high Pd.

Integration helps here too. Coherent integration of N pulses adds the matched-filter outputs with their phases aligned and raises S/N exactly N times for equal pulses; noncoherent integration adds the detector outputs and gains less.

CFAR and pulse compression

A fixed threshold fails when the noise level changes: a rain cell or a jammer raises the background, and the false alarms flood in. The cell-averaging CFAR estimates the background around each cell. The cell under test (CUT) is the range cell whose decision is being made; on each side of it a few guard cells are skipped, so that the target's own energy does not leak into the estimate; beyond them, N training cells are averaged to give the noise estimate Z. The threshold is αZ. For a square-law detector in Gaussian noise:

Pfa = (1 + α/N)−N

α is the threshold multiplier; N is the number of training cells. Solved for α: α = N(Pfa−1/N − 1).

Because Z is itself noisy, α must be larger than the threshold that a known noise level would need, and the extra S/N needed is the CFAR loss. More training cells shrink the loss but make the detector slower to follow a clutter edge.

Pulse compression separates energy from resolution. A linear FM (chirp) pulse sweeps a bandwidth B over a long width τ; its matched filter compresses it to about 1/B. Range resolution becomes c/(2B), and the compression ratio is the time-bandwidth product τB. A 50 µs chirp of 5 MHz gives 30 m resolution with τB = 250 (24 dB), where an uncoded 50 µs pulse would resolve only 7.5 km.

Worked example

Question. An echo of Pr = 1 × 10⁻¹³ W and width τ = 1 µs reaches a receiver of noise figure 10 dB at T0 = 290 K with a 1 MHz noise bandwidth. Find the noise power and input S/N, N0, the pulse energy, and the matched-filter output E/N0 and 2E/N0.

  • Saying the matched filter maximises Pd directly. It maximises the peak S/N; Pd follows once the threshold is set by Pfa.

6.9 Radar antennas, transmitters, receivers and duplexers

The block diagram of a radar becomes real hardware here. The antenna must form a narrow beam and scan it; the transmitter must deliver short pulses of high power; the receiver must hear an echo near its own noise; and the duplexer must let one antenna serve both without burning the receiver. This chapter connects each block to the parts used in practice: dish and array antennas, magnetron, klystron and solid-state transmitters, superheterodyne receivers, displays, and TR-tube and circulator duplexers.

You need the block diagram from Radar principle, block diagram and frequency bands; microwave tubes and ferrite devices are covered in Microwave components, devices and measurements.

Antennas: dish, shaped beam and array

A radar antenna needs high gain and low sidelobes. A parabolic reflector with a feed at its focus forms a narrow pencil beam; its beamwidth is about 70λ/D degrees and its gain G = η(πD/λ)², where D is the diameter and η the aperture efficiency, about 0.55 to 0.7. Most search radars rotate the whole antenna in azimuth. Some move only the feed, for example a nodding feed for height finding on a rotating dish.

An air search radar often shapes its elevation beam as cosecant squared: the gain stays high near the horizon and falls as csc²θ above it, where θ is the elevation angle. Long-range targets near the horizon get full gain; close targets at high elevation get less, which they can afford. An aircraft flying at constant height then gives a nearly constant echo as it approaches.

A slotted-waveguide array, cut with rows of slots, is the flat antenna of most marine radars. A phased array uses many elements, each behind a phase shifter, and steers the beam by changing the phases, with no moving parts. A passive array shares one transmitter; an active array (AESA) has a transmit/receive module behind every element. A radome protects the antenna and must pass the radar band with little loss.

Transmitters and modulators

The magnetron is a crossed-field oscillator that gives high peak power cheaply, so marine and many weather radars use it. Its phase is random from pulse to pulse, so coherent processing needs special care. The klystron and the travelling-wave tube (TWT) are amplifiers that follow a stable low-power source, so they keep the phase coherence that MTI and pulse Doppler need. Solid-state transmitters combine many gallium nitride (GaN) or silicon modules; each gives modest peak power, so they use longer pulses with pulse compression.

Peak and average power are linked by the duty cycle: Pav = Pt × τ × PRF. The tube, the waveguide and the duplexer must survive Pt; the cooling and the power supply are sized for Pav.

The modulator switches the tube on for each pulse. A line-type modulator charges a pulse-forming network (PFN, a ladder of inductors and capacitors that acts as an artificial transmission line) and discharges it through a thyratron or solid-state switch into the tube through a pulse transformer; the pulse width is set by the network. A hard-tube (active-switch) modulator switches a stored high voltage on and off directly, so the pulse width can be changed. The rise time and the flat top shape the transmitted spectrum and its splatter into nearby channels.

Receiver and display

The receiver is a superheterodyne. A low-noise amplifier (LNA) sets the noise figure. A mixer driven by the STALO (stable local oscillator) shifts the echo to the intermediate frequency (IF), often 30 or 60 MHz; an image filter before the mixer rejects the image band. The IF amplifier sets the bandwidth, about 1/τ, near the matched filter. Coherent radars then use I and Q phase detectors against the COHO to keep amplitude and phase; simple radars use a logarithmic amplifier, which squeezes the wide range from weak echoes to strong clutter into one display, and an envelope detector. A CFAR (constant false alarm rate) threshold then decides target or no target.

An A-scope plots echo amplitude against range for one direction. A B-scope plots range against azimuth on a rectangle. The PPI (plan position indicator) is a map: range outward from the centre, azimuth around it, with a sweep that rotates with the antenna. Modern displays are computer screens that show processed tracks over a map.

Duplexers and receiver protection

In a branch-type duplexer, the TR (transmit-receive) gas tube sits in the receiver branch and the ATR (anti-transmit-receive) tube in the transmitter branch. During the pulse both fire: the TR tube shorts the receiver branch, and a quarter-wave length of line turns that short into an open circuit at the junction, so the pulse goes to the antenna. Between pulses both are off: the unfired ATR tube, a quarter wave from the junction, blocks the transmitter branch, and the echo goes to the receiver. A balanced duplexer uses two hybrid couplers and two TR tubes and handles more power over a wider band. Modern sets use a ferrite circulator, with a limiter in front of the receiver.

The isolation the duplexer must give is set by the transmitter power and the power the receiver front end can survive:

I = 10 log10(Pt / Psafe)
Here I is the isolation in dB; Pt is the peak transmitter power in W; Psafe is the largest power the receiver can absorb in W.

At the start of each pulse, before the gas fully ionises, a short spike leaks through that is larger than the steady leakage, so a receiver protector (a diode limiter) clips it. After the pulse the TR tube takes a few microseconds to deionise; until it does, the receiver is deaf, and this recovery time adds to the minimum range c(τ + trec)/2. Question. A radar transmitter gives 100 kW peak, and the receiver front end can safely absorb 10 mW. Find the duplexer isolation needed. A TR tube gives 60 to 70 dB: why is a receiver protector still fitted?

Exam traps

  • Treating duplexer isolation as one number for all time. The leading-edge spike and the recovery period decide receiver damage and the minimum range.
  • Mixing up peak and average power. Average power is peak times duty cycle, and cooling follows the average.
  • Calling the magnetron an amplifier. It is an oscillator; the klystron and the TWT are amplifiers that keep phase coherence.
  • Saying a cosecant-squared beam gives up gain near the horizon. It keeps high gain near the horizon (far targets) and gives up gain at high elevation (near targets).
  • Thinking a radome is just a rain cover. A wet or badly designed radome lowers gain and raises sidelobes.

Takeaways

  1. A radar makes one basic measurement, the echo delay, which gives range as R = ct/2; the antenna angle gives direction and the Doppler shift gives radial speed.
  2. Echo power falls as 1/R⁴, so range grows only as the fourth root of power, gain squared, σ and 1/Smin: 16 times the power doubles the range.
  3. The minimum detectable signal is the noise floor kT0BF times the S/N that detection needs; integrating pulses lowers the S/N needed per pulse.
  4. Radar cross section is an equivalent area set by shape, aspect and wavelength, not the physical size: a face-on plate returns far more than a sphere of the same area.
  5. The PRF fixes the unambiguous range c/(2·PRF) and the Doppler coverage, so low PRF suits search and high PRF suits fast targets; several PRFs remove both ambiguities.
  6. A CW radar measures only speed; an FMCW sweep adds range from the beat frequency, and a triangular sweep separates range and Doppler.
  7. MTI cancels fixed clutter by comparing pulses, at the price of blind speeds nλ·PRF/2; staggered PRF pushes them out, and pulse Doppler filter banks also measure speed.
  8. Tracking radars need an error signal that is zero on boresight; monopulse forms it as Δ/Σ from one pulse, so echo fluctuation cannot disturb it.
  9. The matched filter gives the best peak S/N, 2E/N0, for a known pulse; CFAR keeps the false-alarm rate fixed as the background changes.
  10. Real radars build the block diagram from dish or array antennas, magnetron, klystron, TWT or solid-state transmitters, superheterodyne receivers and TR-tube or circulator duplexers.

Exam question bank

Most syllabi ask for the pulse radar block diagram with the job of each block, the derivation of the range equation, and short numerical problems on unambiguous range, Doppler shift, FMCW beat frequency and MTI blind speeds; tracking and detection questions want a labelled sketch and a comparison.

Understand Define radar cross section. Explain how the RCS of a metal sphere varies with ka in the Rayleigh, resonance and optical regions, and compare the sphere with a flat plate and a corner reflector. Find σ for a sphere of radius 0.5 m at 100 MHz, 1 GHz and 10 GHz. 13 marks

Points to cover. σ = 4πR²Ss/Si, an equivalent isotropic area, in m² or dBsm; Rayleigh σ = 9πa²(ka)⁴ (∝ 1/λ⁴), resonance peaks near ka = 1, optical πa²; Plate 4πA²/λ² face-on; trihedral 4πa⁴/(3λ²) over a wide angle; Sphere a = 0.5 m: ka = 1.05, 10.5, 105; σ = 2.86, 0.785, 0.785 m²; Aspect, fluctuation and stealth.

Model answer.

  1. Definition: σ = limR→∞ 4πR² Ss/Si, where Si is the incident power density at the target and Ss the scattered density back at the radar. It is the area that, intercepting Si and reradiating equally in all directions, would give the observed echo. Unit m²; dBsm = 10 log10(σ/1 m²).
  2. σ is not the physical area: it depends on shape, aspect, material, polarisation and wavelength.
  3. Sphere: it looks the same from every direction, so it is the calibration standard. The size parameter is ka = 2πa/λ.
  4. Rayleigh region (ka ≪ 1): σ = 9πa²(ka)⁴, so σ ∝ a⁶/λ⁴. Raindrops and insects lie here, which is why rain clutter rises steeply with frequency.
  5. Resonance (Mie) region (ka ≈ 1 to 10): the wave reflected from the front interferes with a creeping wave around the back, so σ/πa² swings; the first and highest peak is 3.65 at ka ≈ 1.
  6. Optical region (ka > 10): σ = πa², the projected area, independent of λ.
  7. Sphere of radius 0.5 m (πa² = 0.785 m²):
    fλka = 2πa/λRegionσ
    100 MHz3 m1.05resonance, near the peak3.65 × 0.785 = 2.86 m² (4.6 dBsm)
    1 GHz0.3 m10.5optical0.785 m² (−1.0 dBsm)
    10 GHz0.03 m105optical0.785 m² (−1.0 dBsm)
  8. The same sphere returns 3.65 times more at 100 MHz than in the optical region: the resonance is why an RCS figure must always name its band.
  9. Flat plate, face-on: every point reflects in phase, so σ = 4πA²/λ². A 0.25 m² plate at 10 GHz gives 4π × 0.0625/9 × 10⁻⁴ = 873 m² (29.4 dBsm), over a thousand times its area, but only within a degree or so of normal incidence.
  10. Corner reflector (trihedral): three mutually perpendicular plates return the wave parallel to its arrival over a wide cone, with σ = 4πa⁴/(3λ²) for edge a. An a = 0.2 m trihedral at 10 GHz gives 4π × 0.0016/(3 × 9 × 10⁻⁴) = 7.4 m². Boats carry them; test ranges calibrate with them.
  11. Real targets: many scatterers add and cancel, so σ fluctuates by 10 to 20 dB with small changes of aspect; detection uses statistical (Swerling) models. Stealth lowers σ by shaping (no faces or right angles toward the radar, hidden engine faces) and absorbing materials.
  12. Closing check: at 100 MHz the Rayleigh formula would give 9π × 0.25 × 1.047⁴ = 8.5 m², three times the true 2.86 m²; at ka ≈ 1 the sphere is already out of the Rayleigh region, so the formula must not be used there.
Understand Compare sequential lobing, conical scan and amplitude-comparison monopulse tracking. Explain the monopulse comparator, and find the angle error when Δ = 0.9 V, Σ = 12 V and the slope is 1.5 per degree, with the cross-range offset at 15 km. 13 marks

Points to cover. Error signal zero on boresight, sign gives side; Sequential lobing: switched squinted beams, (A1 − A2)/(A1 + A2); Conical scan: rotating squinted beam, modulation depth and phase; Monopulse: four horns, comparator, Σ, Δaz, Δel; Δ/Σ; Comparison table; θ = 0.05°, offset 13.1 m.

Model answer.

  1. All three make an angle error signal ε ≈ kθ that is zero on the boresight (tracking axis) and changes sign across it; a servo turns the antenna until ε = 0.
  2. Sequential lobing: one feed is switched between two beam positions squinted either side of boresight (and two more for the other plane). ε = (A1 − A2)/(A1 + A2); for example echoes of 1.08 and 0.92 give 0.16/2.00 = 0.08.
  3. Conical scan: one beam, squinted off axis, rotates about the boresight at a few tens of hertz. On axis the echo is steady; off axis it is modulated once per rotation, the depth giving the size of the error and the phase giving the direction.
  4. Amplitude-comparison monopulse: four horns at the focus give four beams squinted into the quadrants. With A top-left, B bottom-left, C top-right, D bottom-right, a comparator of four magic tees or rat-race hybrids forms:
  5. Comparator outputs:
    Σ    = A + B + C + D          (detection, range, reference)
Δaz = (A + B) − (C + D)      (left column − right column)
Δel = (A + C) − (B + D)      (top row − bottom row)
  6. Each channel has its own receiver; the angle is read as θ = (Δ/Σ)/k. Dividing by Σ removes the dependence on echo strength, and the phase of Δ relative to Σ (0° or 180°) gives the side.
  7. Comparison:

  9. Numbers: Δ/Σ = 0.9/12 = 0.075; θ = 0.075/1.5 = 0.050°.
  10. Cross-range offset at 15 km: 0.050° = 8.73 × 10⁻⁴ rad, so x = Rθ = 15 000 × 8.73 × 10⁻⁴ = 13.1 m.
  11. The servo turns the antenna 0.050° toward the side given by the sign of Δ until Δaz is zero; the range gate is tracked separately with split early and late gates.
  12. Closing check: with the antenna corrected, Δ = 0 and Δ/Σ = 0 whatever the value of Σ, which is the defining property of boresight.

Apply Explain the Doppler effect and the operation of CW and FMCW radar. (a) A 24.15 GHz speed gun watches a car travelling at 72 km/h on a line 30° from the beam. Find the Doppler shift and the speed the gun reads. (b) An FMCW radio altimeter at 4.3 GHz uses a triangular sweep of 100 MHz at fm = 100 Hz. Find the beat frequency at a height of 600 m, the up- and down-sweep beats if the aircraft descends at 5 m/s, and the range resolution. 13 marks

Points to cover. fd = 2vr/λ from the phase 4πR/λ; CW radar: mixer, Doppler filter, no range; FMCW: fb = 2RΔf/(cTm) = 4RΔf·fm/c, triangular sweep separates fd; (a) fd = 2789 Hz, reads 62.4 km/h; (b) fR = 80 kHz, fd = 143 Hz, beats 79.86 and 80.14 kHz, ΔR = 1.5 m.

Model answer.

  1. Doppler effect: the echo from range R lags the transmitter by φ = 4πR/λ. If R changes at the rate −vr, the phase changes at 4πvr/λ rad/s, which is a frequency shift fd = 2vr/λ (positive, higher echo frequency, for a closing target). Only the radial component vr = v cos θ counts.
  2. CW radar: an oscillator runs continuously; separate transmit and receive antennas keep the transmitter out of the receiver; a mixer with a transmitter sample gives fd, which a filter and counter read. It cannot measure range (no timing mark) and does not see stationary targets.
  3. FMCW radar: the frequency is swept linearly by Δf; the echo, delayed by 2R/c, differs from the transmitter by the beat fb = 2RΔf/(cTm), where Tm is one sweep. For a triangular sweep at modulation frequency fm, Tm = 1/(2fm), so fb = 4RΔf·fm/c. For a closing target, fup = fR − fd and fdown = fR + fd.
  4. (a) λ = 3 × 10⁸ / 24.15 × 10⁹ = 0.01242 m. v = 72/3.6 = 20 m/s; vr = 20 cos 30° = 17.32 m/s.
  5. fd = 2 × 17.32 / 0.01242 = 2789 Hz. Head-on the shift would be 2 × 20 / 0.01242 = 3220 Hz.
  6. The gun converts fd to speed assuming a head-on car: v = fdλ/2 = 17.32 m/s = 62.4 km/h. The cosine error always reads low, in the driver's favour.
  7. (b) λ = 3 × 10⁸ / 4.3 × 10⁹ = 0.06977 m; each sweep lasts Tm = 1/(2 × 100) = 5 ms.
  8. fR = 4RΔf·fm/c = 4 × 600 × 10⁸ × 100 / 3 × 10⁸ = 80 000 Hz. Check with the other form: 2 × 600 × 10⁸ / (3 × 10⁸ × 5 × 10⁻³) = 80 000 Hz.

  12. Closing check: going back from the beats, R = 80 000 × 3 × 10⁸ / (4 × 10⁸ × 100) = 600 m and v = 143.3 × 0.06977/2 = 5.0 m/s, the values given.

Apply Explain the branch-type duplexer with TR and ATR tubes and the need for a receiver protector. A 9.4 GHz radar has 500 kW peak power, a receiver that can take 50 mW, a 1 µs pulse, a TR recovery time of 2 µs and a PRF of 2 kHz, with a 3 m dish of efficiency 0.6. Points to cover. TR tube in the receiver arm, ATR in the transmitter arm, quarter-wave spacings; Transmit and receive states; Leading-edge spike and recovery; limiter; I = 70 dB; Rmin = 450 m; Pav = 1 kW; θ = 0.74°, G = 47.2 dB.

Model answer.

  1. Layout: the transmitter, antenna and receiver meet at a junction. The TR tube sits in the receiver arm and the ATR tube in a side arm of the transmitter line, each placed a quarter wavelength from the junction.
  2. Transmit: the high-power pulse fires both gas tubes. The fired TR tube is a short circuit across the receiver arm, which the quarter-wave line turns into an open circuit at the junction, so no power enters the receiver arm and the receiver is shielded. The fired ATR tube is a short at the end of a quarter-wave stub, which looks like an open across the main line and lets the pulse pass to the antenna.
  3. Receive: the weak echo cannot fire either tube. The unfired ATR arm now presents a short a quarter wave from the junction, which appears as an open there and blocks the transmitter arm; the echo goes into the receiver.
  4. A balanced duplexer uses two 3 dB hybrids and two TR tubes for more power and bandwidth; a ferrite circulator with a limiter does the same job in modern sets.
  5. Receiver protection: at the start of each pulse the gas takes a short time to ionise, so a spike leaks through that is larger than the steady leakage; after the pulse the TR tube needs time to deionise (recovery). A diode limiter after the duplexer clips the spike.

Analyze Compare CW, FMCW, low-PRF pulse and pulse Doppler radar for measuring range and radial speed. For an X-band radar (λ = 3 cm) that must measure closing speeds up to 300 m/s without Doppler ambiguity, find the PRF needed and the resulting unambiguous range, and explain how medium-PRF radars resolve the conflict. 13 marks

Points to cover. CW speed only; FMCW range and speed; low PRF range; pulse Doppler speed; fd = 20 kHz at 300 m/s; ±fd needs PRF ≥ 40 kHz; Runamb = 3.75 km; Low PRF 1 kHz: Doppler span only 15 m/s; Medium PRF and several PRFs; trade-off table.

Model answer.

  1. CW radar: measures radial speed precisely from fd; no range, no stationary targets. Simple and low power: speed guns, intruder alarms.
  2. FMCW radar: a linear sweep gives range from the beat, and a triangular sweep gives speed as well; low peak power, fine resolution c/(2Δf), but short range and strong transmitter leakage: altimeters, level gauges, car radar.
  3. Low-PRF pulse radar: measures range unambiguously over a long distance; Doppler is sampled only at the PRF, so speed is ambiguous. Search and air traffic radars, with MTI for clutter.
  4. Pulse Doppler radar: a high or medium PRF and a Doppler filter bank measure speed and reject clutter; range is ambiguous and resolved with several PRFs. Airborne fire-control radars.
  5. Doppler needed: fd = 2vr/λ = 2 × 300 / 0.03 = 20 000 Hz.
  6. Unambiguous Doppler: phase samples once per pulse can tell Doppler frequencies apart only over a span of one PRF. To measure both closing and opening targets up to ±20 kHz the span must be 40 kHz: PRF ≥ 40 kHz (20 kHz would do for closing targets only).
  7. Unambiguous range at 40 kHz: c/(2 × 40 000) = 3750 m = 3.75 km, useless for search on its own.
  8. At a low PRF of 1 kHz the unambiguous range is 150 km, but the Doppler span of 1000 Hz covers only 1000 × 0.03/2 = 15 m/s of speed, so a 300 m/s target folds many times.
  9. Trade-off:
    PRF classRangeDopplerTypical use
    Low (≈ 1 kHz)unambiguous to 150 kmambiguous every 15 m/ssearch, ATC
    Medium (≈ 10 kHz)ambiguous every 15 kmambiguous every 150 m/sairborne multi-role
    High (≥ 40 kHz)ambiguous every 3.75 kmunambiguous to ±300 m/sairborne, fast closing targets
  10. Medium PRF: the radar cycles through several PRFs near 10 kHz (for example three of a set of eight). Each is ambiguous in both range and Doppler, but only mildly, and the true range and the true speed are the single values consistent with all the readings, as with two PRFs in range unfolding. Blind zones in range and speed differ from PRF to PRF, so a target hidden in one is seen in another.
  11. Choice: a ground speed gun is CW; a car radar that must see a stopped car is FMCW; an airport search radar is low PRF with MTI; a fighter radar uses medium and high PRF pulse Doppler.
  12. Closing check: at PRF 40 kHz, a 300 m/s target's 20 kHz shift is exactly half the PRF, the edge of the unambiguous span ±PRF/2, consistent with the requirement.
Evaluate Evaluate pulse compression. A radar with 1 MW peak power uses a 1 µs uncoded pulse. It is replaced by a 20 µs linear FM pulse of 1 MHz bandwidth at the same peak power, and then by the same chirp at 50 kW peak. Compare the pulse energy, S/N, detection range, range resolution and average power (PRF 1 kHz) in the three cases, and state the costs of compression. 13 marks

Points to cover. Matched filter S/N depends on E = Ptτ; Chirp at 1 MW: 20 × energy, +13 dB, range × 2.11, resolution still 150 m; Chirp at 50 kW: same energy and range as the short pulse at 1/20 of the peak power; Average power 1 kW, 20 kW, 1 kW; Costs: range sidelobes, longer blind zone, Doppler mismatch, processing.

Model answer.

  1. Principle: the matched-filter output S/N is 2E/N0, set by the pulse energy E = Ptτ, not by the shape. Range resolution is set by the bandwidth, c/(2B). An uncoded pulse ties the two together (B ≈ 1/τ); a chirp breaks the tie by sweeping B over a long τ, and its matched filter compresses it to about 1/B.
  2. Comparison (PRF 1 kHz):

    Energy Ptτ1 J20 J1 J
    S/N relative0 dB+13.0 dB0 dB

  3. Same peak power: the chirp has 20 times the energy, so 10 log10 20 = 13.0 dB more S/N, and the range grows as the fourth root: 201/4 = 2.11 times, with the same 150 m resolution. But the average power rises to 20 kW, which the transmitter and cooling must supply.
  4. Same energy: at 50 kW the chirp matches the detection range of the 1 MW short pulse. This is why solid-state transmitters, whose modules give modest peak power, depend on pulse compression: they trade peak power for pulse length.
  5. Costs: (1) the compressed pulse has range sidelobes about 13 dB down for a plain chirp, which can hide a small target next to a large one, so a weighting window is used at the cost of a slightly wider main lobe and about 1 dB of S/N; (2) the long pulse blinds the receiver for longer, here 3 km instead of 150 m, so a short pulse is often added to cover close range; (3) a Doppler shift moves the compressed peak slightly in range (range-Doppler coupling); (4) the matched filter needs more processing, now cheap in digital form.
  6. Verdict: compression is worth it whenever peak power is limited or resolution must be fine at long range; it is used in nearly every modern surveillance and tracking radar.
  7. Closing check: compression ratio τB = 20 × 10⁻⁶ × 10⁶ = 20, the same factor as the energy ratio at equal peak power, and the S/N gain of the matched filter over the input S/N in a 1 MHz bandwidth is also 20 (13.0 dB).

Glossary

Azimuth
The horizontal angle of a target, measured from a reference direction such as north.
Blind speed
A radial speed at which the Doppler shift is a multiple of the PRF, so an MTI canceller removes the target.
Boresight
The tracking axis of an antenna: the direction in which its angle error signal is zero.
Circulator
A ferrite three-port device that passes power from port to port in one direction only.
Clutter
Unwanted echoes from ground, sea, rain, birds or other objects that are not targets.
COHO
Coherent oscillator: the IF phase reference against which each echo is phase-detected in a coherent radar.
Conical scan
Tracking by rotating a squinted beam about the boresight and reading the echo modulation.
Constant false alarm rate (CFAR)
A detector whose threshold follows a local estimate of the background, so the false-alarm probability stays fixed.
Cosecant-squared beam
An elevation pattern whose gain falls as csc²θ above the horizon, giving a steady echo from a target at constant height.
Delay-line canceller
An MTI filter that subtracts the previous pulse's output from the present one, range gate by range gate.
Difference channel
In monopulse, the difference of beam pairs: zero on boresight, with a sign that gives the side.
Duplexer
The switch that lets one antenna serve the transmitter and the receiver while protecting the receiver.
Duty cycle
The fraction of time the transmitter is on, τ/Tr = τ·PRF.
Elevation
The angle of a target above the horizontal.
Improvement factor
The output signal-to-clutter ratio of an MTI divided by the input ratio, averaged over target speeds.
Matched filter
The filter whose impulse response is the time-reversed pulse; it gives the highest peak S/N in white noise.
PPI
Plan position indicator: a map display with range out from the centre and azimuth around it.
Probability of false alarm
The probability that noise alone crosses the threshold in one cell.
Pulse compression
Sending a long coded pulse and compressing it in the receiver, giving the energy of a long pulse with the resolution of a short one.
Pulse repetition interval
The time between the starts of two successive pulses, Tr = 1/PRF.
Radome
A cover over an antenna that must be transparent in the radar band.
Range gate
A time window, one pulse width long, that selects the echoes from one small band of range.
Second-time-around echo
An echo from beyond the unambiguous range that arrives after the next pulse and is shown too close.
Sequential lobing
Tracking by switching between squinted beams and comparing their echoes.
Staggered PRF
Changing the pulse interval from pulse to pulse so that blind speeds move out and second-time-around echoes jump about.
STALO
Stable local oscillator: the low-noise oscillator that drives the receiver mixer.
Sum channel
In monopulse, the sum of the four beams: a normal beam used for detection and range.
TR tube
A gas tube that fires during the pulse and shorts the receiver branch of a duplexer.
