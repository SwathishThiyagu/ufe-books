3.1 Absorption, spontaneous and stimulated emission

Light meets atoms in three ways. A photon can be absorbed, lifting an electron to a higher level. An excited atom can drop by itself, sending out a photon in a random direction: spontaneous emission. Or a passing photon can trigger the drop, adding a second identical photon: stimulated emission. The third process is the seed of laser action and of every optical transmitter that needs coherent light. LEDs use the second; lasers use the third. Only a few universities teach this at full depth. This chapter names the rates and shows why lasers need a population inversion.

You need: photon energy E = hf and two-level atomic energy levels from Unit 1.

Absorption and spontaneous emission

Take a two-level atom with lower level 1 and upper level 2. Its energy gap is ΔE = E2 − E1. A photon of frequency f carries energy hf. If hf equals ΔE, the atom can absorb the photon and move from 1 to 2; the photon is lost. Absorption raises the population of level 2. An excited atom in level 2 can also fall to 1 on its own, with no incoming photon, and release a photon in a random direction and random phase. This is spontaneous emission. Its average delay is the spontaneous lifetime τ_sp, and the rate per atom is A21 = 1/τ_sp. LEDs work by spontaneous emission; see LED structures.

E = hf = hc/λ

E is photon energy in joules; h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J s is Planck's constant; f is frequency in hertz; c = 3.00×10⁸ m/s is the speed of light; λ is wavelength in metres.

Stimulated emission and Einstein coefficients

A second photon of energy hf can trigger a 2→1 drop if the atom is already in level 2. The emitted photon has the same frequency, phase and direction as the trigger photon. One photon goes in, two identical photons come out: this is stimulated emission. Einstein described the three processes with coefficients. B12 is the absorption coefficient per unit spectral energy density ρ(f), where ρ(f) is the energy density per unit frequency in J m⁻³ Hz⁻¹. B21 is the stimulated-emission coefficient, and A21 is the spontaneous-emission coefficient. For simple non-degenerate levels, B12 = B21. The coefficients are linked by A21 = (8π h f³ / c³) B21. A21 is a rate per second, not an energy.

A21 = 1/τ_sp = (8π h f³/c³) B21

A21 is spontaneous emission rate per atom per second; τ_sp is spontaneous lifetime in seconds; B21 is the stimulated-emission coefficient; h, f and c are as above.

In thermal equilibrium N2/N1 = exp(−hf/kT) < 1, so N2 < N1 and absorption dominates. Here k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann's constant and T is temperature in kelvin. Laser action needs N2 > N1, an inverted population. That is why a laser needs pumping; see laser action.

Worked example

Question. An atom has spontaneous lifetime τ_sp = 5 ns at λ = 850 nm. Find the photon energy in eV and the Einstein A coefficient. If the excited-state population is twice the ground-state population, state whether net stimulated emission or absorption dominates. Use h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00×10⁸ m/s, 1 eV = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ J.

  1. Convert wavelength: λ = 850 nm = 850×10⁻⁹ m.
  2. Frequency: f = c/λ = 3.00×10⁸ / (850×10⁻⁹) = 3.5294×10¹⁴ Hz.
  3. Photon energy: E = hf = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ × 3.5294×10¹⁴ = 2.340×10⁻¹⁹ J.
  4. In eV: E = 2.340×10⁻¹⁹ / 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.4625 eV.
  5. Einstein A: A21 = 1/τ_sp = 1/(5×10⁻⁹) = 2.00×10⁸ s⁻¹.
  6. N2 = 2N1 and B12 = B21. Net stimulated rate ∝ N2 − N1 = N1 > 0, so net stimulated emission dominates over absorption.

Answer. E = 1.4625 eV (about 1.46 eV), A21 = 2.00×10⁸ s⁻¹, and net stimulated emission dominates because N2 > N1.

Exam traps

  • Spontaneous emission does not need a second photon; it happens at a random time whose average is τ_sp.
  • A21 is a rate per second, not an energy; do not add it to photon energy.
  • Stimulated emission needs an inverted population; at thermal equilibrium N2 < N1.
  • B12 = B21 holds for simple non-degenerate levels; degeneracy changes the ratio, not the need for inversion.
  • Convert nanometres to metres before using E = hc/λ; 850 nm is infrared.

3.2 LED structures, characteristics and efficiency

An LED is a forward-biased p-n junction that turns injected electrons into spontaneous photons. Its chip structure decides how many photons escape, and its efficiency decides how much optical power reaches a fiber. LEDs are used in short-distance links, local area networks, sensors and displays because they are cheap, reliable and need simple drive circuits. This chapter covers planar, dome, surface-emitter and edge-emitter LEDs, then the efficiencies, I-P curve, spectrum, modulation bandwidth and temperature behaviour that set their use.

You need spontaneous emission and direct-bandgap recombination from 3.1; numerical aperture from Unit 1 is used later in 3.6.

LED junction and chip structures

An LED is a forward-biased p-n junction. In a direct-bandgap semiconductor such as GaAs, electrons and holes recombine spontaneously and release photons. A double heterostructure places a thin active layer between wider-bandgap p and n confining layers. This traps carriers in the active layer and improves injection. The chip shape then decides how light leaves.

A planar LED is the simplest surface emitter. The active layer sits near the top surface and light exits through that surface. Total internal reflection traps much of the light because the semiconductor has a high refractive index. A dome LED shapes the chip into a hemisphere or adds a dome lens. Rays meet the surface more nearly at right angles, so more light escapes.

A higher-radiance surface emitter, called a Burrus LED in some syllabi, uses a small active area and a substrate window or well so light leaves from one face. It gives a small, bright emitting spot and couples well to a fiber. An edge emitter LED guides light along a stripe to a cleaved edge. Its emitting area is narrow and directional, so coupling is better than a simple planar LED. It still emits spontaneous light, not laser light.

From electrons to escaping photons

Three efficiencies are easy to mix. Internal quantum efficiency η_int is the number of photons generated in the active region per electron injected. Extraction efficiency η_extraction is the number of photons escaping the chip per photon generated inside. External quantum efficiency η_ext is the number of photons escaping per electron injected:

η_int = N_ph(int)/N_e,   η_ext = η_int × η_extraction,   η_power = P_out/(I V)

N_ph(int) is photons generated per second; N_e is electrons injected per second; P_out is escaping optical power in W; I is forward current in A; V is forward voltage in V. η_power is wall-plug power efficiency.

Power efficiency includes electrical losses, so it is lower than η_ext. The I-P curve plots P_out against I. Above turn-on it is almost linear. The slope is:

P_out = η_ext (I/e) E, dP_out/dI = η_ext E/e

E is photon energy in J; e = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C is the electron charge.

Linear power against current lets you modulate an LED by changing its drive current. A planar LED with η_int = 0.70 and η_extraction = 0.20 has η_ext = 0.14; 14% of injected electrons give escaping photons. The rest become heat or trapped light.

Spectrum, speed and temperature

An LED emits over a band of wavelengths, not one line. Recombination involves a spread of carrier energies and a thermal distribution, so the spectrum has a full width at half maximum of tens of nanometres. This width causes chromatic dispersion in a fiber, so LEDs suit short links. See Unit 2 for dispersion.

Speed is limited by the carrier lifetime τ. A rough modulation bandwidth is f₃dB ≈ 1/(2πτ), where τ is in seconds and f₃dB is in hertz. A shorter lifetime means faster modulation. LEDs reach tens to a few hundred MHz, well below a laser diode.

Temperature changes both power and wavelength. As temperature rises, non-radiative recombination increases, so output power falls. The bandgap shrinks, so the peak wavelength shifts to longer wavelengths. Drive circuits use temperature compensation or a monitor photodiode when output must stay steady.

Worked example

Question. A planar LED has internal quantum efficiency 0.70 and extraction efficiency 0.20. It is driven by a forward current of 50 mA. Each emitted photon has energy 1.4 eV. Take e = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C and 1 eV = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ J. Find (a) the number of photons generated per second inside the chip, (b) the number leaving the chip per second, and (c) the external optical power in mW.

  1. Convert current: I = 50 mA = 50×10⁻³ A = 0.050 A.
  2. Electrons injected per second = I/e = 0.050/(1.60×10⁻¹⁹) = 3.125×10¹⁷ s⁻¹.
  3. Photons generated inside = η_int × electrons/s = 0.70 × 3.125×10¹⁷ = 2.1875×10¹⁷ s⁻¹.
  4. Photons escaping = η_extraction × photons inside = 0.20 × 2.1875×10¹⁷ = 4.375×10¹⁶ s⁻¹. External quantum efficiency = 0.70 × 0.20 = 0.14.
  5. Photon energy = 1.4 eV × 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ J/eV = 2.24×10⁻¹⁹ J.
  6. P_out = escaping photons/s × photon energy = 4.375×10¹⁶ × 2.24×10⁻¹⁹ = 9.80×10⁻³ W = 9.80 mW.

Answer. The external optical power is about 9.8 mW.

Exam traps

  • Do not multiply by extraction efficiency twice. External efficiency already contains η_int and η_extraction; use P_out = η_ext (I/e) E once.
  • Keep current in amperes when using e = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C. 50 mA is 0.050 A, not 50.
  • Convert photon energy from eV to joules before calculating watts: 1 eV = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • External quantum efficiency and power efficiency are different. η_ext counts photons per electron; η_power divides optical watts by electrical watts.
  • An edge emitter LED is not a laser. It is still spontaneous emission; laser action needs feedback and threshold, covered in 3.3–3.4.

3.3 Laser action: population inversion, feedback, threshold

Laser action turns a pumped gain medium into an oscillator. Three things must happen together: the pump must create population inversion so stimulated emission beats absorption; mirrors must feed light back through the medium; and the pump must be strong enough to cross a threshold where round-trip gain equals round-trip loss. Below threshold the device behaves like an LED. Above threshold it emits narrow-linewidth, coherent light. Fiber links use this for long-haul transmitters, where launch power and spectral width matter. This chapter gives the conditions; 3.4 builds the diode.

You need: 3.1 for stimulated emission, 3.2 for recombination, and Unit 1 for cavity modes.

Population inversion gives gain

At thermal equilibrium, N2/N1 = exp[−(E2−E1)/(kBT)], where kB is the Boltzmann constant (1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K) and T is the absolute temperature in kelvin. The lower level N1 is fuller, so absorption beats stimulated emission. A pump lifts atoms to an upper level. A metastable upper level holds them long enough for a photon to arrive. Population inversion means N2 > N1. Then stimulated emission exceeds absorption and the medium has gain. A semiconductor uses carrier density and quasi-Fermi levels instead of two discrete levels, but the test is the same: pump the active region until stimulated emission beats absorption. Only a few universities teach the full derivation; exams ask the condition and the gain formula.

g = σ(N2 − N1)

g is gain per unit length (cm⁻¹); σ is the stimulated-emission cross-section (cm²); N2 and N1 are populations per unit volume (cm⁻³). Both N values are densities, not total counts.

Feedback and threshold

Gain alone gives a single-pass amplifier. A Fabry-Perot cavity puts two mirrors around the gain medium and sends light back and forth. At threshold, round-trip gain equals total loss. Above threshold gain clamps and extra pump becomes output. The cavity also selects longitudinal modes: 2nL = mλ, where n is refractive index, λ is vacuum wavelength and m is an integer. Mode spacing is Δν = c/(2ngL), with c the speed of light and ng the group index (ng ≈ n far from band edges). Threshold current is the pump current where the gain condition is first met. Threshold current density falls with lower αi, longer L, higher R1R2, or higher Γ. For a fixed stripe width the total threshold current still rises with L, because the pumped volume grows. This is why laser diodes confine light and carriers tightly.

Γg<sub>th</sub> = α<sub>i</sub> + (1/(2L)) ln[1/(R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>)]

Γ is the confinement factor (fraction of the mode in the gain medium); gth is threshold material gain (cm⁻¹); αi is internal loss (cm⁻¹); L is cavity length (cm); R1 and R2 are mirror power reflectances (dimensionless).

Worked example

Question. A Fabry-Perot semiconductor laser has L = 300 μm, R1 = R2 = 0.32, internal loss α_i = 10 cm⁻¹ and confinement factor Γ = 0.30. Find the threshold modal gain and threshold material gain.

  1. Convert length: 300 μm = 300 × 10⁻⁶ m = 0.03 cm.
  2. R1R2 = 0.32 × 0.32 = 0.1024; 1/(R1R2) = 9.765625; ln(9.765625) = 2.2789.
  3. Mirror loss = 2.2789/(2 × 0.03 cm) = 2.2789/0.06 = 37.98 cm⁻¹.
  4. Threshold modal gain Γg_th = α_i + mirror loss = 10 + 37.98 = 47.98 cm⁻¹.
  5. Threshold material gain g_th = 47.98/0.30 = 159.94 cm⁻¹.

Answer. Threshold modal gain is 47.98 cm⁻¹ and threshold material gain is 159.94 cm⁻¹.

Exam traps

  • Mixing centimetres and metres in 1/(2L): convert L to the unit of α_i first. In the example, 300 μm = 0.03 cm.
  • Forgetting Γ: the threshold condition limits modal gain Γg_th, so material gain is g_th = Γg_th/Γ.
  • Using R instead of R1R2: mirror loss needs the product, not one mirror.
  • Calling any strong pump inversion: inversion requires N2 > N1, not merely a large N2.
  • Saying gain grows without limit above threshold: gain clamps near threshold and extra pump becomes output.

3.4 Semiconductor laser diodes

A semiconductor laser diode is a forward-biased p-n junction that supplies its own light and its own mirrors. Cleaved crystal faces at the two ends form a Fabry–Perot cavity. Below a threshold current the diode behaves like an expensive LED; above it, stimulated emission wins and the output becomes narrow-band, directional and coherent. Every long-haul link, metro ring and FTTH feeder in India ends in one of these devices. Some syllabi call it an injection laser diode (ILD).

You need: 3.1 for stimulated emission, 3.2 for the forward-biased emitting junction, and 3.3 for population inversion, feedback and the threshold gain condition.

The diode is its own cavity

A laser diode is a p-n junction with a very thin active layer — tens of nanometres to about 0.1 µm — between two wider-bandgap cladding layers. Forward bias injects electrons from the n-side and holes from the p-side into that layer, and they recombine there. At low current the device emits spontaneous light exactly as the LED of 3.2 does.

Two things change the picture. The active layer is thin and the current density high, so carriers are injected faster than spontaneous recombination alone can remove them. The carrier density builds until the recombination rate matches the injection rate, and at that density population inversion exists between the bands — the condition from 3.3. And the crystal is cleaved along natural planes at both ends, leaving two flat parallel faces. The semiconductor index is near 3.5 and air is 1, so each face reflects a fraction R = ((n − 1)/(n + 1))² ≈ 0.30, where n is the refractive index.

Thirty per cent per facet is enough. Light travelling along the cavity is amplified by stimulated emission on each pass, reflects at a facet and comes back. Gain per pass must exceed loss per pass — mirror loss plus internal loss — and that is the threshold of 3.3.

Threshold, slope efficiency and the L-I curve

Written as a current density, the threshold condition in the linear-gain approximation is:

J_th = (α_i + α_m) / (Γ β)

J_th is threshold current density in A/m² (device data sheets quote A/cm²); α_i is internal loss per metre; α_m = (1/2L)·ln(1/(R₁R₂)) is mirror loss per metre for cavity length L in metres and facet reflectivities R₁, R₂; Γ is the confinement factor, the fraction of the optical mode overlapping the active layer; β is gain per metre per A/m².

A double heterostructure confines both carriers and the optical field to the thin active layer. The carrier confinement raises the gain coefficient β and the heterobarriers keep α_i small, so J_th falls even though Γ is only a few tenths (typically 0.1–0.4 for a bulk active layer). Quantum wells go further (see 3.5).

A shorter cavity raises α_m and so raises J_th, but the active area shrinks, so the threshold current I_th = J_th × area falls. Lengthening the cavity lowers α_m and J_th, but raises I_th. Cavity length trades threshold current against mirror loss.

Above threshold the light-current curve is a straight line:

P = η_s (I − I_th)

P is emitted optical power in W; I is forward current in A; I_th is threshold current in A; η_s is slope efficiency in W/A, and η_s = η_d (hν/q) with η_d the differential quantum efficiency (a pure number, at most 1), hν the photon energy in J and q = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

Typical values for a Fabry–Perot laser at 1.3–1.55 µm are I_th = 10–30 mA and η_s = 0.1–0.3 W/A. Subtract I_th, never I. Below threshold the idealised curve of the figure is zero; a real diode still shows a weak spontaneous tail of a few microwatts.

Modes, temperature and direct modulation

The cavity is a few hundred micrometres long, so many longitudinal modes fit inside the gain band. Their spacing is:

Δλ = λ² / (2 n_g L)

Δλ is mode spacing in m; λ is centre wavelength in m; n_g is group index, about 3.5–4; L is cavity length in m. For λ = 1.55 µm, n_g = 3.5, L = 300 µm, Δλ ≈ 1.14 nm.

Transverse modes are set by waveguide width and layer structure. A ridge a few micrometres wide supports one transverse mode, which is what a single-mode fiber needs; a broad stripe oscillates in several and is harder to couple (see 3.6).

I_th(T) = I_th(T₀) · exp[(T − T₀)/T_c]

T and T₀ are temperatures in kelvin and T_c is the characteristic temperature in kelvin. T_c is 50–70 K for InGaAsP devices near 1.3 µm and above 100 K for GaAs-based ones; a small T_c means the laser needs a cooler or a bias-tracking circuit.

Direct modulation adds the signal to the bias current. The diode must stay biased above threshold, otherwise every pulse starts with a turn-on delay. Carrier density and refractive index move together, so the wavelength chirps during each pulse, and that chirp turns into dispersion penalty in the fiber (see Unit 2, Chromatic dispersion). Directly modulated DFB lasers reach a few Gb/s; beyond that an external modulator is used (see 3.8).

Worked example

Question. A Fabry–Perot laser diode has threshold current 20 mA and slope efficiency 0.30 W/A. (a) Find the output optical power at 40 mA and at 60 mA. (b) At 60 mA the forward voltage is 1.8 V; find the electrical input power and the overall efficiency.

  1. Convert currents to amperes: I_th = 0.020 A, 40 mA = 0.040 A, 60 mA = 0.060 A.
  2. At 40 mA: I − I_th = 0.040 − 0.020 = 0.020 A. P = 0.30 × 0.020 = 0.0060 W = 6.0 mW.
  3. At 60 mA: I − I_th = 0.060 − 0.020 = 0.040 A. P = 0.30 × 0.040 = 0.0120 W = 12.0 mW.
  4. Electrical input at 60 mA: P_elec = V·I = 1.8 × 0.060 = 0.108 W = 108 mW.
  5. Overall efficiency: η = 12.0 mW / 108 mW = 0.111, i.e. 11.1%. Most of the input is heat, which is why a laser package sits on a heat sink.

Answer. 6.0 mW at 40 mA and 12.0 mW at 60 mA; at 60 mA the input is 108 mW and the overall efficiency is 11.1%.

Exam traps

  • Using the whole current instead of (I − I_th): at 60 mA with η_s = 0.30 W/A you get 18 mW, not 12 mW.
  • Slope efficiency is a slope in W/A, not a percentage. The percentage is the differential quantum efficiency η_d, and the two differ by the factor hν/q.
  • Threshold current (mA) and threshold current density (A/cm²) are different quantities; J_th multiplied by active area gives I_th.
  • Longitudinal mode spacing comes from cavity length, transverse modes come from waveguide width. Examiners swap the two words.
  • Mirror loss α_m and internal loss α_i are separate terms that add inside J_th; a small R lowers reflectivity and raises threshold, it does not raise output.

It confines both carriers and the optical mode to a thin active layer. Carrier confinement greatly increases the gain β per unit current density and keeps the internal loss α_i small; Γ is only moderate (a few tenths) but the product Γβ still rises, so J_th = (α_i + α_m)/(Γβ) drops. The thin layer also shrinks the active volume, so the threshold current I_th falls further.

3.5 Heterojunction and quantum-well lasers

A heterojunction laser puts a thin active semiconductor between cladding layers of a different material. The bandgap step traps electrons and holes; the refractive-index step traps light. A quantum well goes further: the active layer is only about 10 nm thick, so electron energy levels become quantized. This lowers threshold current and shifts the wavelength. These lasers sit inside transmitters for metro links, data centers and fiber-to-the-home. Only a few universities teach this advanced structure in detail.

You need 3.3 laser action and 3.4 laser diodes; Unit 1 waveguide modes are assumed.

Heterojunction: trapping carriers and light

A homojunction uses one semiconductor on both sides. Carriers and light spread widely, so threshold current is high. A heterojunction joins materials of different bandgap. In a double heterostructure (DH), a thin GaAs active layer sits between p-AlGaAs and n-AlGaAs cladding. AlGaAs has a wider bandgap and a lower refractive index than GaAs.

The bandgap step blocks electrons and holes from leaving the active layer: carrier confinement. The refractive-index step, about 0.2–0.3 for GaAs/AlGaAs, guides the optical mode along the same layer: optical confinement. Both raise gain per unit volume and lower threshold current.

A separate confinement heterostructure (SCH) adds two undoped layers between active layer and cladding. Their bandgap is wider than the active layer but narrower than the cladding. The optical mode spreads into them, away from doped cladding, while carriers stay in the active layer. Light and carriers are controlled separately, which reduces loss.

Quantum wells and threshold reduction

Below about 20 nm, the active layer becomes a quantum well. Electron motion across the well is restricted, so energies above the band edge are discrete:

E_n = n²h²/(8m*L²)

n = 1, 2, 3… is the level number; h is Planck's constant in J s; m* is the electron effective mass in kg; L is the well width in m.

The lowest level E₁ adds to the bandgap. The emitted photon energy is E_g + E₁e + E₁h, so the wavelength is shorter than a bulk laser of the same material. A 10 nm GaAs well shifts the wavelength by tens of nanometres.

The two-dimensional density of states is step-like. Fewer carriers are needed for a given gain, so threshold current density falls, often by a factor of two or more. A multiple quantum well (MQW) stacks several wells with barrier layers; it gives more gain and a narrower spectrum.

Straining the well, such as InGaAs on GaAs, changes the band structure. Compressive strain lowers the hole effective mass and threshold, and extends the wavelength range. Feedback still comes from cleaved facets, or from a grating in DFB and DBR lasers; those are feedback variants, not different gain media.

Worked example

Question. A GaAs quantum well is 10 nm wide. The electron effective mass is 0.067m0. Find the lowest confined electron energy above the band edge. Use h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J s and m0 = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg.

  1. Convert the width: L = 10 nm = 10 × 10⁻⁹ m = 1.0 × 10⁻⁸ m.
  2. Effective mass: m* = 0.067 × 9.11×10⁻³¹ = 6.104×10⁻³² kg.
  3. Use the infinite-well formula E₁ = h²/(8m*L²).
  4. Numerator: h² = (6.63×10⁻³⁴)² = 4.396×10⁻⁶⁷ J² s².
  5. Denominator: 8m*L² = 8 × 6.104×10⁻³² × (1.0×10⁻⁸)² = 4.883×10⁻⁴⁷ kg m².
  6. Divide: E₁ = 4.396×10⁻⁶⁷ / 4.883×10⁻⁴⁷ = 9.002×10⁻²¹ J.
  7. Convert to eV: 9.002×10⁻²¹ / 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ = 0.0562 eV.

Answer. The lowest confined electron energy is 9.002×10⁻²¹ J, or 0.0562 eV, above the band edge.

Exam traps

  • Putting L = 10 directly into the formula instead of 10×10⁻⁹ m. The energy varies as 1/L², so the error is huge.
  • Using the free-electron mass m0 instead of the effective mass 0.067m0. The computed energy is then about 15 times smaller.
  • Calling every p-n laser a heterojunction laser. A homojunction has no bandgap step, so it has weak carrier confinement.
  • Expecting a narrower well to give a longer wavelength. Confinement raises the energy, so the wavelength becomes shorter.

3.6 Source-to-fiber power launching and coupling

An optical source emits light into a wide cone, but a fiber accepts only a narrow cone and a small core area. Source-to-fiber coupling gets the most light from the source into the guided mode. Coupling efficiency η is the ratio of coupled power to emitted power. It depends on source radiance, fiber numerical aperture (NA), core area and alignment. Only a few universities teach the full calculation. The same ideas set transmitter design, pigtailing and package tolerances.

You need: 3.2 LED emission and Unit 1 numerical aperture and modes.

Direct coupling and efficiency

Direct coupling puts the fiber face close to the source with no lens. The source emits over area A_s and into a cone. The fiber accepts only rays inside its acceptance cone and over its core area A_c. Rays outside either limit are lost.

Radiance L is power per area per solid angle, in W m⁻² sr⁻¹. A high-L source packs more power into the small cone and spot a fiber accepts.

η = P_f / P_s

η is coupling efficiency (0 to 1); P_f is power in the guided mode (W); P_s is power emitted by the source (W).

η ≈ NA²

NA = sin θ_a is the fiber numerical aperture; θ_a is the acceptance angle in air; the source area is no larger than the core and fills a hemisphere.

For a Lambertian source the fraction of emitted power inside the acceptance cone is sin²θ_a = NA², so NA enters squared. The small-angle approximation of the accepted solid angle gives the same number. A laser's narrow beam can beat this LED estimate.

Fresnel reflection at an uncoated air-glass face (n ≈ 1.5) removes near 4% of the light, R = ((n − 1)/(n + 1))² ≈ 0.04, even with perfect alignment.

Large source area and coupling improvement

When the source area is larger than the core, only the part of the source that images onto the core can couple. For uniform emission, add the area ratio:

η ≈ (r_c / r_s)² NA²

r_c is core radius (m); r_s is source emitting radius (m); NA is numerical aperture. Use radii in the same unit. The area factor is a power ratio, not an amplitude ratio.

A lens can match the source image to the core, but shrinking the image widens the ray angles at the fiber face, so for a large Lambertian source a lens cannot raise η above the (r_c/r_s)² NA² limit. A lens helps most with a small source: it spreads the image to fill the core and reduces ray angles, pushing η above NA². A tapered or lensed fiber does the same gradually. Pigtailing fixes the fiber to the source in one package; index-matching glue or an anti-reflection coating cuts Fresnel loss.

Alignment tolerance follows spot size. A single-mode core is near 9 µm, so lateral error must be under about 1 µm. A 50 µm multimode core is looser. Coupling loss is quoted in dB; 3 dB means half the power is lost.

Worked example

Question. A Lambertian LED has emitting-area radius 100 µm. A step-index fiber has core radius 50 µm and NA = 0.20. Total LED power is 2.0 mW. The source area is larger and uniform. Find the coupled power.

  1. Both radii are in µm: r_s = 100 µm, r_c = 50 µm.
  2. Area factor = (r_c / r_s)² = (50 / 100)² = 0.25.
  3. NA² = 0.20² = 0.04.
  4. η ≈ 0.25 × 0.04 = 0.01, which is 1%.
  5. P_f = η P_s = 0.01 × 2.0 mW = 0.02 mW = 20 µW.

Answer. The coupled power is 20 µW, with coupling efficiency 0.01.

Exam traps

  • Using NA instead of NA² makes the estimate far too large.
  • Forgetting the area factor (r_c / r_s)² ignores the source-to-core size mismatch.
  • dB is a ratio, not a power: 3 dB loss halves the power.
  • Mixing radius and diameter changes the area factor by a factor of four.
  • Ignoring Fresnel reflection misses the near 4% loss at an uncoated air-glass face with n ≈ 1.5, where R = ((n − 1)/(n + 1))² ≈ 0.04.

3.7 Fiber joints, connectors and misalignment losses

A fiber joint is any place where light leaves one fiber and enters another. A splice is permanent; a connector can be plugged and unplugged. No joint is perfect. Light is lost by fiber mismatch and by misalignment. Engineers describe each loss as a fraction and then convert to dB, because dB losses add along a link. This chapter explains fusion and mechanical splices, connectors such as SC, ST and LC, and the lateral, angular and gap errors that set insertion loss and return loss in telecom links, FTTH and data centres.

You need: 3.6 for coupling efficiency, and Unit 1 for numerical aperture and core radius.

Splices, connectors and what loss means

A splice joins two fibers permanently. In a fusion splice, an electric arc melts the cleaved ends and they fuse. It gives the lowest loss, often below 0.1 dB, because there is no air gap and no ferrule. A mechanical splice holds two prepared ends in a small alignment channel, often with index-matching gel; it is faster in the field but loss is higher.

A connector is a demountable joint. The fiber is fixed in a ferrule, usually 2.5 mm for SC and ST, or 1.25 mm for LC. SC is square push-pull; ST is bayonet; LC is small form factor. Connectors are used at patch panels, transmitters and receivers, and in FTTH drops.

L<sub>dB</sub> = −10 log<sub>10</sub>(η<sub>j</sub>)

LdB is insertion loss in dB; ηj is the fraction of power that passes through the joint, from 0 to 1.

RLdB = −10 log10(R)

RLdB is return loss in dB; R is the reflected power fraction. High return loss is good: 40 dB means only 0.0001 of the power is reflected.

Intrinsic loss: built-in fiber mismatch

Intrinsic loss comes from differences in the two fibers, even with perfect alignment. If core diameters differ and the NAs are equal, core-size loss appears mainly when light goes from the larger core into the smaller one. The smaller core acts as a stop: with uniform (over-filled) launch the coupled fraction is about (asmall/alarge)². For 62.5 μm into 50 μm, (50/62.5)² ≈ 0.64, about 1.9 dB. Going 50 μm into 62.5 μm the light enters the larger core fully, so core-size loss is negligible. Angular acceptance loss appears when NAs differ, not from core diameter alone.

If numerical aperture (NA) differs, the fiber with smaller NA accepts less of the cone. Rays outside its acceptance angle become cladding modes. For step-index multimode fibers, the loss depends on the overlap of the two angular distributions. In single-mode fibers, mode-field diameter mismatch replaces core diameter mismatch.

A difference in refractive-index profile changes Fresnel reflection and mode shape. Same type and same manufacturer reduce intrinsic loss. In the field, you cannot tune a splice away; you select matched fibers.

Extrinsic loss: misalignment and end finish

Extrinsic loss comes from how the ends are held. Lateral offset is sideways shift d. Angular tilt is angle θ between axes. Longitudinal gap is the separation between the two fiber ends; gap loss grows with the gap and is small for gaps below about 10 μm. End finish is cleave angle, roughness, and dome or undercut.

η = (2/π)[arccos x − x√(1 − x²)], x = d/(2a)

η is coupling efficiency for step-index multimode fiber with equal cores; d is lateral offset; a is core radius; x is offset divided by core diameter. At d = 0, η = 1. At d = 2a, η = 0.

A small lateral offset loses little because the two circles still overlap. The denominator uses 2a, the core diameter, because d/(2a) is the shift as a fraction of diameter. Angular tilt tilts the output cone so part misses the receiving core; loss rises with tilt angle and falls with larger NA. A gap adds diffraction spreading and two Fresnel reflections; index-matching gel reduces reflection but not the gap spreading.

At a glass-air interface with refractive index about 1.5, normal-incidence Fresnel reflection is about 4% per surface. A rough or angled end scatters light. Angled physical contact (APC) connectors use an 8° angled polish to reduce back reflection, giving high return loss.

Worked example

Question. Two step-index multimode fibers have core radius a = 50 μm and NA = 0.22. A lateral offset d = 10 μm is the only misalignment. Estimate the coupling efficiency and the loss in dB.

  1. List the data: a = 50 μm, d = 10 μm. NA is not needed for pure lateral offset between equal cores.
  2. Find the core diameter: 2a = 2 × 50 μm = 100 μm.
  3. Find x = d/(2a) = 10 μm / 100 μm = 0.1.
  4. Compute arccos(0.1) = 1.4706 rad and √(1 − 0.1²) = √0.99 = 0.99499.
  5. Bracket = 1.4706 − (0.1 × 0.99499) = 1.4706 − 0.0995 = 1.3711.
  6. Efficiency η = (2/π) × 1.3711 = 0.8729.
  7. Loss LdB = −10 log10(0.8729) = 0.5904 dB.

Answer. The coupling efficiency is 0.8729 (87.29%) and the lateral-offset loss is 0.5904 dB.

Exam traps

  • Do not add two joint losses by multiplying their fractions and stopping there. Convert each fraction to dB and add the dB values; the product is the overall fraction.
  • In the overlap formula use d/(2a), not d/a. Here 2a is the core diameter, so a 10 μm offset in a 100 μm core is x = 0.1, not 0.2.
  • Check whether the question gives radius or diameter. A 50 μm radius means a 100 μm core diameter; using 50 μm as diameter doubles x and changes the loss.
  • Insertion loss and return loss are different. Insertion loss is light lost through the joint; return loss is light reflected back. High return loss in dB is good.
  • Fresnel reflection and lateral offset are separate causes. Index-matching gel reduces Fresnel reflection but does not correct a sideways shift.

3.8 Optical modulators and Mach-Zehnder devices

A laser diode can be switched by its current, but that changes its wavelength and adds chirp. An optical modulator changes light after the laser, so the laser runs steady and only the light is coded. The Mach-Zehnder modulator splits light into two waveguide arms, shifts the phase in each arm by the electro-optic effect, and recombines them. Interference turns phase difference into output power. These devices sit in high-speed metro and long-haul links, where clean, low-chirp pulses matter.

You need: 3.4 semiconductor laser diodes, waveguide modes from Unit 1, and WDM ideas from Unit 6.

Why put the modulator after the laser

Direct modulation changes the laser current to switch the light. The current changes carrier density, gain and refractive index together. The output frequency therefore shifts during each pulse; this is chirp. Carrier lifetime and relaxation oscillation also limit speed and cause pattern-dependent output. A steady current avoids these problems. The laser runs continuous wave (CW), and a separate optical modulator after it sets the light level. The modulator works by the electro-optic or Pockels effect: an electric field E changes refractive index n in a material without inversion symmetry, such as lithium niobate (LiNbO3) or indium phosphide (InP).

φ = 2πnL/λ

φ is phase in radians; n is refractive index; L is arm length in metres; λ is free-space wavelength in metres.

Δφ = πV/Vπ

Δφ is phase difference in radians; V is applied voltage in volts; Vπ is the voltage that gives a phase shift of π radians at the operating wavelength.

A phase modulator alone changes phase, not power. To get an on-off optical signal, the phase change must be converted into an intensity change. Some syllabi call the resulting device a Mach-Zehnder modulator (MZM).

Mach-Zehnder interferometer

An input waveguide splits light equally into two arms. Each arm carries a phase φ1 or φ2. A combiner adds the two optical fields. If the fields are equal in amplitude and the splitter is ideal, the output field is the sum of the two arm fields.

Eout = (Ein/2)(e^(jφ1) + e^(jφ2))

Eout is output electric field; Ein is input electric field; j is the imaginary unit; φ1 and φ2 are the arm phases in radians.

Pout = Pin cos²((φ1 − φ2)/2)

Pout is output optical power in watts; Pin is input optical power in watts; φ1 − φ2 is the phase difference in radians.

In push-pull drive, the two arms are driven in opposite directions: φ1 = φ0 + Δφ/2 and φ2 = φ0 − Δφ/2. The common phase φ0 does not affect interference, so only Δφ remains. With Δφ = πV/Vπ, the transfer function becomes Pout = Pin cos²(πV/(2Vπ)). At V = 0 the output is maximum. At V = Vπ the output is the first null. At V = 2Vπ it is maximum again.

Pout = Pin cos²(πV/(2Vπ))

Pout is output power in watts; Pin is input power in watts; V is drive voltage in volts; Vπ is the half-wave voltage in volts.

A useful bias point is V = Vπ/2, called quadrature. There the slope is maximum, so a small signal gives the largest change. The curve is still cos², not a straight line, so large swings add harmonics. The extinction ratio is ER = 10 log10(Pmax/Pmin) in dB; finite ER means the off state still leaks light. Insertion loss compares output with input when the device is on.

Chirp and electro-absorption

Chirp is unwanted change of optical frequency during modulation. A push-pull MZM can have very low chirp because the two arms change phase in opposite directions. A single-arm drive does not cancel the phase change and can chirp. An electro-absorption modulator (EAM) works differently: an electric field increases absorption near the band edge, using the Franz-Keldysh effect in bulk material or the quantum-confined Stark effect in quantum wells. An EAM is compact and needs a low drive voltage, and it can be integrated with a distributed-feedback laser as an electro-absorption modulated laser. It is wavelength dependent and can add some chirp. A travelling-wave MZM uses electrodes that let the electrical and optical waves travel together, keeping the drive voltage low at high bit rates.

Worked example

Question. A push-pull Mach-Zehnder modulator has Vπ = 4.0 V. Input optical power is 2.0 mW. The phase difference is Δφ = πV/Vπ. Find the output power at V = 2.0 V and at V = 4.0 V.

  1. At V = 2.0 V: Δφ = π × 2.0 / 4.0 = π/2 rad.
  2. Pout = Pin cos²(Δφ/2) = 2.0 mW × cos²(π/4).
  3. cos²(π/4) = 0.5, so Pout = 2.0 mW × 0.5 = 1.0 mW.
  4. At V = 4.0 V: Δφ = π × 4.0 / 4.0 = π rad.
  5. Pout = 2.0 mW × cos²(π/2) = 2.0 mW × 0 = 0.0 mW.

Answer. At 2.0 V, Pout = 1.0 mW; at 4.0 V, Pout = 0.0 mW.

Exam traps

  • Using P proportional to cos(Δφ) instead of cos²(Δφ/2) gives wrong nulls and wrong power at quadrature.
  • Calling Vπ the voltage for maximum transmission or for a 2π phase shift. Vπ gives the first null, a π phase difference.
  • Forgetting the push-pull factor: each arm gets ±Δφ/2, while interference depends on the total difference Δφ.
  • Mixing extinction ratio with insertion loss. Extinction ratio compares on and off levels of one modulator; insertion loss compares output with input.
  • Assuming an external modulator has zero chirp in every drive scheme. Single-arm drive and electro-absorption modulators can still chirp.

Takeaways

  1. Absorption removes light, spontaneous emission gives incoherent light, and stimulated emission gives coherent light when a second photon triggers emission.
  2. An LED is a spontaneous emitter; its launched power depends on internal quantum efficiency, extraction efficiency and coupling.
  3. Laser oscillation requires population inversion, optical feedback and pump current above threshold gain.
  4. A semiconductor laser diode uses cleaved facets as a Fabry-Perot cavity and emits coherent light above threshold current.
  5. Heterojunctions and quantum wells shrink the active region, confine light and carriers, and reduce threshold current.
  6. Source-to-fiber coupling efficiency depends on source radiance, fiber numerical aperture, core area and alignment.
  7. Joint and connector losses from lateral, angular and longitudinal misalignment are added in dB.
  8. A Mach-Zehnder modulator changes phase and then converts it to an intensity change with a cos²(Δφ/2) response.

Glossary

Absorption
Loss of a photon when an electron or atom moves from a lower to a higher energy state.
Active region
Semiconductor region where carriers recombine and light is generated or amplified.
Angular misalignment
Angle between the axes of two fibers.
Cleaved facet
Mirror-like end face formed by cleaving a semiconductor crystal along a crystal plane.
Coupling efficiency
Ratio of power launched into the fiber to power emitted by the source.
Direct modulation
Changing laser drive current to modulate output power.
External quantum efficiency
Photons emitted outside the device per injected electron.
Extinction ratio
Ratio of high to low optical power in a modulated signal.
Extraction efficiency
Fraction of generated light that leaves the chip.
Fabry–Perot cavity
Two parallel mirrors, such as cleaved facets, that provide optical feedback at resonant wavelengths.
Heterojunction
Junction of two semiconductors with different band gaps that can confine carriers and light.
Insertion loss
Loss added by a component, in dB, equal to 10 log₁₀(P_in/P_out).
Internal quantum efficiency
Fraction of injected carriers that recombine radiatively.
Lambertian source
Source whose radiance is constant with angle and whose intensity falls as cos θ.
Lateral misalignment
Sideways offset between two fiber cores.
Longitudinal misalignment
Gap between fiber ends along the axis.
Mach–Zehnder interferometer
Two-path interferometer that converts phase difference into intensity change.
Numerical aperture
NA = √(n₁² − n₂²) for step-index fiber; it measures the light-gathering cone.
Optical feedback
Return of part of the light to the gain medium by mirrors or facets to sustain laser oscillation.
Population inversion
Condition where the upper energy level has more carriers than the lower level, so stimulated emission exceeds absorption.
Quantum well
Very thin semiconductor layer that confines carriers and creates quantised energy levels.
Radiance
Power emitted per unit area per unit solid angle, in W/(m²·sr).
Spontaneous emission
Random emission of a photon by an excited atom without an incoming photon.
Stimulated emission
Emission of a photon coherent with an incoming photon when the atom is already excited.
Threshold gain
Gain that makes round-trip gain equal round-trip loss and starts laser action.
