3.1 Absorption, spontaneous and stimulated emission

Light meets atoms in three ways. A photon can be absorbed, lifting an electron to a higher level. An excited atom can drop by itself, sending out a photon in a random direction: spontaneous emission. Or a passing photon can trigger the drop, adding a second identical photon: stimulated emission. The third process is the seed of laser action and of every optical transmitter that needs coherent light. LEDs use the second; lasers use the third. Some syllabi cover this at full depth. This chapter names the rates and shows why lasers need a population inversion.

You need: photon energy E = hf and the idea that an atom or a semiconductor has discrete energy levels (or bands) that electrons can occupy.

Absorption and spontaneous emission

Take a two-level atom with lower level 1 and upper level 2. Its energy gap is ΔE = E2 − E1. A photon of frequency f carries energy hf. If hf equals ΔE, the atom can absorb the photon and move from 1 to 2; the photon is lost. Absorption raises the population of level 2. An excited atom in level 2 can also fall to 1 on its own, with no incoming photon, and release a photon in a random direction and random phase. This is spontaneous emission. Its average delay is the spontaneous lifetime τsp, and the rate per atom is A21 = 1/τsp. LEDs work by spontaneous emission; see LED structures.

E = hf = hc/λ

E is photon energy in joules; h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J s is Planck's constant; f is frequency in hertz; c = 3.00×10⁸ m/s is the speed of light; λ is wavelength in metres.

A handy form for optical work is λ(µm) = 1.24/E(eV), because hc/q is about 1.24 µm·eV. A 1.46 eV photon has λ = 1.24/1.46 = 0.85 µm. The same rule turns a semiconductor bandgap Eg into its emission wavelength; see heterojunction and quantum-well lasers.

Stimulated emission and Einstein coefficients

A second photon of energy hf can trigger a 2→1 drop if the atom is already in level 2. The emitted photon has the same frequency, phase and direction as the trigger photon. One photon goes in, two identical photons come out: this is stimulated emission. Einstein described the three processes with coefficients. B12 is the absorption coefficient per unit spectral energy density ρ(f), where ρ(f) is the energy density per unit frequency in J m⁻³ Hz⁻¹. B21 is the stimulated-emission coefficient, and A21 is the spontaneous-emission coefficient. For simple non-degenerate levels, B12 = B21. The coefficients are linked by A21 = (8π h f³ / c³) B21. A21 is a rate per second, not an energy.

A21 = 1/τsp = (8π h f³/c³) B21

A21 is spontaneous emission rate per atom per second; τsp is spontaneous lifetime in seconds; B21 is the stimulated-emission coefficient; h, f and c are as above.

In thermal equilibrium N2/N1 = exp(−hf/kT) < 1, so N2 < N1 and absorption dominates. Here k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann's constant and T is temperature in kelvin. Laser action needs N2 > N1, an inverted population. That is why a laser needs pumping; see laser action.

Einstein’s relations also give the ratio of stimulated to spontaneous emission for atoms bathed in thermal radiation at temperature T: R = B21ρ(f)/A21 = 1/[exp(hf/kT) − 1]. At optical frequencies and room temperature hf/kT is about 57, so R is near 10⁻²⁵ and spontaneous emission wins by an enormous margin. Ordinary thermal light can never make a laser; the device needs pumping into inversion and a cavity that builds up its own photon density.

Worked example

Question. An atom has spontaneous lifetime τsp = 5 ns at λ = 850 nm. Find the photon energy in eV and the Einstein A coefficient. If the excited-state population is twice the ground-state population, state whether net stimulated emission or absorption dominates. Use h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00×10⁸ m/s, 1 eV = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ J.

  1. Convert wavelength: λ = 850 nm = 850×10⁻⁹ m.
  2. Frequency: f = c/λ = 3.00×10⁸ / (850×10⁻⁹) = 3.5294×10¹⁴ Hz.
  3. Photon energy: E = hf = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ × 3.5294×10¹⁴ = 2.340×10⁻¹⁹ J.
  4. In eV: E = 2.340×10⁻¹⁹ / 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.4625 eV. Check with the short rule: λ = 1.24/1.4625 = 0.848 µm, which is 850 nm.
  5. Einstein A: A21 = 1/τsp = 1/(5×10⁻⁹) = 2.00×10⁸ s⁻¹.
  6. N2 = 2N1 and B12 = B21. Net stimulated rate ∝ N2 − N1 = N1 > 0, so net stimulated emission dominates over absorption.

Answer. E = 1.4625 eV (about 1.46 eV), A21 = 2.00×10⁸ s⁻¹, and net stimulated emission dominates because N2 > N1.

Worked example 2

Question. For the 850 nm transition of worked example 1, find kT, the population ratio N2/N1 and the ratio R of stimulated to spontaneous emission at T = 300 K. At what wavelength would R reach 1 at the same temperature? Use k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K and the photon energy 2.340×10⁻¹⁹ J found there.

  1. Thermal energy: kT = 1.38×10⁻²³ × 300 = 4.14×10⁻²¹ J, which is 0.0259 eV.
  2. Exponent: hf/kT = 2.340×10⁻¹⁹ / 4.14×10⁻²¹ = 56.52.
  3. Population ratio: N2/N1 = exp(−56.52) = 2.84×10⁻²⁵. The upper level is practically empty.
  4. Rate ratio: R = 1/[exp(56.52) − 1] = 2.84×10⁻²⁵, the same number because exp(56.52) is huge.
  5. R = 1 needs exp(hf/kT) = 2, so hf = kT ln 2 = 4.14×10⁻²¹ × 0.693 = 2.87×10⁻²¹ J.
  6. Then f = 2.87×10⁻²¹ / 6.63×10⁻³⁴ = 4.33×10¹² Hz and λ = c/f = 3.00×10⁸ / 4.33×10¹² = 6.9×10⁻⁵ m = 69 µm, in the far infrared.

Answer. kT = 4.14×10⁻²¹ J (0.0259 eV), N2/N1 = R = 2.84×10⁻²⁵, and R = 1 only near λ = 69 µm. At 850 nm a source must be pumped into inversion.

Exam traps

  • Spontaneous emission does not need a second photon; it happens at a random time whose average is τsp.
  • A21 is a rate per second, not an energy; do not add it to photon energy.
  • Stimulated emission needs an inverted population; at thermal equilibrium N2 < N1.
  • B12 = B21 holds for simple non-degenerate levels; degeneracy changes the ratio, not the need for inversion.
  • Convert nanometres to metres before using E = hc/λ; 850 nm is infrared.

3.2 LED structures, characteristics and efficiency

An LED is a forward-biased p-n junction that turns injected electrons into spontaneous photons. Its chip structure decides how many photons escape, and its efficiency decides how much optical power reaches a fiber. LEDs are used in short-distance links, local area networks, sensors and displays because they are cheap, reliable and need simple drive circuits. This chapter covers surface emitters (planar, dome and the high-radiance Burrus type) and edge emitters, then the efficiencies, I-P curve, spectrum, modulation bandwidth and temperature behaviour that set their use.

You need spontaneous emission and direct-bandgap recombination from 3.1; numerical aperture, from the study of light propagation in fibers, is used later in 3.6.

LED junction and chip structures

An LED is a forward-biased p-n junction. In a direct-bandgap semiconductor such as GaAs, electrons and holes recombine spontaneously and release photons. A double heterostructure places a thin active layer between wider-bandgap p and n confining layers. This traps carriers in the active layer and improves injection. The chip shape then decides how light leaves.

A planar LED is the simplest surface emitter. The active layer sits near the top surface and light exits through that surface. Total internal reflection traps much of the light because the semiconductor has a high refractive index. A dome LED shapes the chip into a hemisphere or adds a dome lens. Rays meet the surface more nearly at right angles, so more light escapes.

A higher-radiance surface emitter, called a Burrus LED in some syllabi, uses a small active area and a substrate window or well so light leaves from one face. It gives a small, bright emitting spot and couples well to a fiber. An edge emitter LED guides light along a stripe to a cleaved edge. Its emitting area is narrow and directional, so coupling is better than a simple planar LED. It still emits spontaneous light, not laser light.

From electrons to escaping photons

Four efficiencies are easy to mix. Internal quantum efficiency ηint is the number of photons generated in the active region per electron injected. Extraction efficiency ηextraction is the number of photons escaping the chip per photon generated inside. External quantum efficiency ηext is the number of photons escaping per electron injected:

ηint = Nph(int)/Ne, ηext = ηint × ηextraction, ηpower = Pout/(I V)

Nph(int) is photons generated per second; Ne is electrons injected per second; Pout is escaping optical power in W; I is forward current in A; V is forward voltage in V. ηpower is wall-plug power efficiency.

Power efficiency includes electrical losses, so it is lower than ηext. The I-P curve plots Pout against I. Once the diode conducts, the curve is close to a straight line through the origin, because every injected electron has the same chance of giving an escaping photon. The slope is:

Pout = ηext (I/e) E, dPout/dI = ηext E/e

E is photon energy in J; e = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C is the electron charge.

Linear power against current lets you modulate an LED by changing its drive current. A dome LED with ηint = 0.70 and ηextraction = 0.20 has ηext = 0.14; 14% of injected electrons give escaping photons. The rest become heat or trapped light.

Both factors can be found from device data. With radiative lifetime τr and non-radiative lifetime τnr, the total carrier lifetime τ obeys 1/τ = 1/τr + 1/τnr, and ηint = τ/τr. The optical power made inside the chip is then:

Pint = ηint (hc/qλ) I

Pint is internal optical power in W; hc/qλ is the photon energy in eV written as volts; I is drive current in A.

A flat chip loses most of this. Only rays inside the critical-angle cone reach air, and Fresnel reflection takes part of those, so the extraction efficiency of a flat surface emitting into air is about ηextraction ≈ 1/[n(n + 1)²], where n is the semiconductor index. For n = 3.6 that is only 1.3%, which is why domes, textured surfaces and index-matched epoxy exist.

Spectrum, speed and temperature

An LED emits over a band of wavelengths, not one line. Recombination involves a spread of carrier energies and a thermal distribution, so the spectrum has a full width at half maximum of tens of nanometres. This width causes chromatic dispersion in a fiber (treated with fiber signal degradation), so LEDs suit short links.

Speed is limited by the carrier lifetime τ. Driven at frequency f, the optical output falls as P(f)/P(0) = 1/√(1 + (2πfτ)²). The electrical 3 dB bandwidth, where the detected electrical power halves, is f3dB = 1/(2πτ); the optical 3 dB bandwidth, where the optical power halves, is √3 times larger. A shorter lifetime means faster modulation. LEDs reach tens to a few hundred MHz, well below a laser diode.

Temperature changes both power and wavelength. As temperature rises, non-radiative recombination increases, so output power falls. The bandgap shrinks, so the peak wavelength shifts to longer wavelengths. Drive circuits use temperature compensation or a monitor photodiode when output must stay steady.

Worked example

Question. A dome LED has internal quantum efficiency 0.70 and extraction efficiency 0.20. It is driven by a forward current of 50 mA. Each emitted photon has energy 1.4 eV. Take e = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C and 1 eV = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ J. Find (a) the number of photons generated per second inside the chip, (b) the number leaving the chip per second, and (c) the external optical power in mW.

  1. Convert current: I = 50 mA = 50×10⁻³ A = 0.050 A.
  2. Electrons injected per second = I/e = 0.050/(1.60×10⁻¹⁹) = 3.125×10¹⁷ s⁻¹.
  3. Photons generated inside = ηint × electrons/s = 0.70 × 3.125×10¹⁷ = 2.1875×10¹⁷ s⁻¹.
  4. Photons escaping = ηextraction × photons inside = 0.20 × 2.1875×10¹⁷ = 4.375×10¹⁶ s⁻¹. External quantum efficiency = 0.70 × 0.20 = 0.14.
  5. Photon energy = 1.4 eV × 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ J/eV = 2.24×10⁻¹⁹ J.
  6. Pout = escaping photons/s × photon energy = 4.375×10¹⁶ × 2.24×10⁻¹⁹ = 9.80×10⁻³ W = 9.80 mW.

Answer. The external optical power is about 9.8 mW.

Question. A double-heterostructure LED emits at 0.85 µm. Its radiative lifetime is 50 ns and its non-radiative lifetime is 80 ns, and it is driven at 30 mA. The chip surface is flat, with refractive index 3.6, and emits into air. Find (a) the internal quantum efficiency, (b) the internal optical power, (c) the power that leaves the chip, and (d) the electrical 3 dB modulation bandwidth. Use h = 6.63×10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00×10⁸ m/s, q = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ C.

  1. Total lifetime: 1/τ = 1/50 + 1/80 = 0.0200 + 0.0125 = 0.0325 ns⁻¹, so τ = 30.8 ns.
  2. Internal quantum efficiency: ηint = τ/τr = 30.8/50 = 0.615.
  3. Photon energy: hc/(qλ) = (6.63×10⁻³⁴ × 3.00×10⁸)/(1.60×10⁻¹⁹ × 0.85×10⁻⁶) = 1.4625 eV, so each electron that recombines radiatively gives 1.4625 V × q of light.

Exam traps

  • Do not multiply by extraction efficiency twice. External efficiency already contains ηint and ηextraction; use Pout = ηext (I/e) E once.
  • Keep current in amperes and photon energy in joules: 50 mA is 0.050 A, and 1 eV = 1.60×10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • External quantum efficiency and power efficiency are different. ηext counts photons per electron; ηpower divides optical watts by electrical watts.
  • f3dB = 1/(2πτ) is the electrical 3 dB bandwidth. The optical 3 dB bandwidth is √3 times larger; say which one you give.
  • An edge emitter LED is not a laser. It is still spontaneous emission; laser action needs feedback and threshold, covered in laser action.

3.3 Laser action: population inversion, feedback, threshold

Laser action turns a pumped gain medium into an oscillator. Three things must happen together: the pump must create population inversion so stimulated emission beats absorption; mirrors must feed light back through the medium; and the pump must be strong enough to cross a threshold where round-trip gain equals round-trip loss. Below threshold the device behaves like an LED. Above threshold it emits narrow-linewidth, coherent light. Fiber links use this for long-haul transmitters, where launch power and spectral width matter. This chapter gives the conditions; 3.4 builds the diode.

You need: 3.1 for stimulated emission, 3.2 for recombination, and the idea of standing waves and modes from fiber propagation.

Population inversion gives gain

At thermal equilibrium, N2/N1 = exp[−(E2−E1)/(kBT)], where kB is the Boltzmann constant (1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K) and T is the absolute temperature in kelvin. The lower level N1 is fuller, so absorption beats stimulated emission. A pump lifts atoms to an upper level. A metastable upper level holds them long enough for a photon to arrive. Population inversion means N2 > N1. Then stimulated emission exceeds absorption and the medium has gain. A semiconductor uses carrier density and quasi-Fermi levels instead of two discrete levels, but the test is the same: pump the active region until stimulated emission beats absorption. Some syllabi cover the full derivation; exams ask the condition and the gain formula.

g = σ(N2 − N1)

g is gain per unit length (cm⁻¹); σ is the stimulated-emission cross-section (cm²); N2 and N1 are populations per unit volume (cm⁻³). Both N values are densities, not total counts.

Feedback and threshold

Gain alone gives a single-pass amplifier. A Fabry-Perot cavity puts two mirrors around the gain medium and sends light back and forth. At threshold, round-trip gain equals total loss: R1R2·exp[2L(Γgth − αi)] = 1, so the light returns to its starting point with the same power. Taking the natural log of both sides gives the formula below. Above threshold gain clamps and extra pump becomes output. The cavity also selects longitudinal modes: 2nL = mλ, where n is refractive index, λ is vacuum wavelength and m is an integer. Mode spacing is Δν = c/(2ngL), with c the speed of light and ng the group index (ng ≈ n far from band edges). Threshold current is the pump current where the gain condition is first met. Threshold current density falls with lower αi, longer L, higher R1R2, or higher Γ. For a fixed stripe width the total threshold current still rises with L, because the pumped volume grows. This is why laser diodes confine light and carriers tightly.

Γgth = αi + (1/(2L)) ln[1/(R1R2)]

Γ is the confinement factor (fraction of the mode in the gain medium); gth is threshold material gain (cm⁻¹); αi is internal loss (cm⁻¹); L is cavity length (cm); R1 and R2 are mirror power reflectances (dimensionless).

Worked example

Question. A Fabry-Perot semiconductor laser has L = 300 μm, R1 = R2 = 0.32, internal loss αi = 10 cm⁻¹ and confinement factor Γ = 0.30. Find the threshold modal gain and threshold material gain. Also find the longitudinal mode spacing in frequency for group index ng = 3.6.

  1. Convert length: 300 μm = 300 × 10⁻⁶ m = 0.03 cm.
  2. R1R2 = 0.32 × 0.32 = 0.1024; 1/(R1R2) = 9.765625; ln(9.765625) = 2.2789.
  3. Mirror loss = 2.2789/(2 × 0.03 cm) = 2.2789/0.06 = 37.98 cm⁻¹.
  4. Threshold modal gain Γgth = αi + mirror loss = 10 + 37.98 = 47.98 cm⁻¹.
  5. Threshold material gain gth = 47.98/0.30 = 159.94 cm⁻¹.
  6. Mode spacing: Δν = c/(2ngL) = 3.00×10⁸/(2 × 3.6 × 300×10⁻⁶ m) = 1.389×10¹¹ Hz = 139 GHz. At 1.3 µm this is Δλ = λ²Δν/c = 0.78 nm.

Answer. Threshold modal gain is 47.98 cm⁻¹, threshold material gain is 159.94 cm⁻¹, and the modes are 139 GHz apart.

Exam traps

  • Mixing centimetres and metres in 1/(2L): convert L to the unit of αi first. In the example, 300 μm = 0.03 cm.
  • Forgetting Γ: the threshold condition limits modal gain Γgth, so material gain is gth = Γgth/Γ.
  • Using R instead of R1R2: mirror loss needs the product, not one mirror.
  • Calling any strong pump inversion: inversion requires N2 > N1, not merely a large N2.
  • Saying gain grows without limit above threshold: gain clamps near threshold and extra pump becomes output.

3.4 Semiconductor laser diodes

A semiconductor laser diode is a forward-biased p-n junction that supplies its own light and its own mirrors. Cleaved crystal faces at the two ends form a Fabry–Perot cavity. Below a threshold current the diode behaves like an expensive LED; above it, stimulated emission wins and the output becomes narrow-band, directional and coherent. Every long-haul link, metro ring and FTTH feeder in India starts from one of these devices. Some syllabi call it an injection laser diode (ILD).

You need: 3.1 for stimulated emission, 3.2 for the forward-biased emitting junction, and 3.3 for population inversion, feedback and the threshold gain condition.

The diode is its own cavity

A laser diode is a p-n junction with a very thin active layer — tens of nanometres to about 0.1 µm — between two wider-bandgap cladding layers. Forward bias injects electrons from the n-side and holes from the p-side into that layer, and they recombine there. At low current the device emits spontaneous light exactly as the LED of 3.2 does.

Two things change the picture. The active layer is thin and the current density high, so carriers are injected faster than spontaneous recombination alone can remove them. The carrier density builds until the recombination rate matches the injection rate, and at that density population inversion exists between the bands — the condition from 3.3. And the crystal is cleaved along natural planes at both ends, leaving two flat parallel faces. The semiconductor index is near 3.5 and air is 1, so each face reflects a fraction R = ((n − 1)/(n + 1))² ≈ 0.30, where n is the refractive index.

Thirty per cent per facet is enough. Light travelling along the cavity is amplified by stimulated emission on each pass, reflects at a facet and comes back. Gain per pass must exceed loss per pass — mirror loss plus internal loss — and that is the threshold of 3.3.

Threshold, slope efficiency and the L-I curve

Written as a current density, the threshold condition in the linear-gain approximation is:

Jth = (αi + αm) / (Γ β)

Jth is threshold current density in A/m² (device data sheets quote A/cm²); αi is internal loss per metre; αm = (1/2L)·ln(1/(R₁R₂)) is mirror loss per metre for cavity length L in metres and facet reflectivities R₁, R₂; Γ is the confinement factor, the fraction of the optical mode overlapping the active layer; β is gain per metre per A/m².

A double heterostructure confines both carriers and the optical field to the thin active layer. The carrier confinement raises the gain coefficient β and the heterobarriers keep αi small, so Jth falls even though Γ is only a few tenths (typically 0.1–0.4 for a bulk active layer). Quantum wells go further (see 3.5).

A shorter cavity raises αm and so raises Jth, but the active area shrinks, so the threshold current Ith = Jth × area falls. Lengthening the cavity lowers αm and Jth, but raises Ith. Cavity length trades threshold current against mirror loss.

Above threshold the light-current curve is a straight line:

P = ηs (I − Ith)

P is emitted optical power in W; I is forward current in A; Ith is threshold current in A; ηs is slope efficiency in W/A, and ηs = ηd (hν/q) with ηd the differential quantum efficiency (a pure number, at most 1), hν the photon energy in J and q = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

Typical values for a Fabry–Perot laser at 1.3–1.55 µm are Ith = 10–30 mA and ηs = 0.1–0.3 W/A. Subtract Ith, never I. Below threshold the curve in the figure sits almost on the axis: the diode gives only a weak spontaneous tail of a few microwatts.

Modes, temperature and direct modulation

The cavity is a few hundred micrometres long, so many longitudinal modes fit inside the gain band. Their spacing is:

Δλ = λ² / (2 ng L)

Δλ is mode spacing in m; λ is centre wavelength in m; ng is group index, about 3.5–4; L is cavity length in m. For λ = 1.55 µm, ng = 3.5, L = 300 µm, Δλ ≈ 1.14 nm.

Transverse modes are set by waveguide width and layer structure. A ridge a few micrometres wide supports one transverse mode, which is what a single-mode fiber needs; a broad stripe oscillates in several and is harder to couple (see 3.6).

Ith(T) = Ith(T₀) · exp[(T − T₀)/Tc]

T and T₀ are temperatures in kelvin and Tc is the characteristic temperature in kelvin. Tc is 50–70 K for InGaAsP devices near 1.3 µm and above 100 K for GaAs-based ones; a small Tc means the laser needs a cooler or a bias-tracking circuit.

Direct modulation adds the signal to the bias current. The diode must stay biased above threshold, otherwise every pulse starts with a turn-on delay. Carrier density and refractive index move together, so the wavelength chirps during each pulse, and that chirp turns into dispersion penalty in the fiber (see chromatic dispersion, with fiber signal degradation). Directly modulated DFB lasers serve short and metro links at up to about 10 Gb/s; for longer reach or higher rates an external modulator is used (see 3.8).

Worked example

Question. A Fabry–Perot laser diode has threshold current 20 mA and slope efficiency 0.30 W/A. (a) Find the output optical power at 40 mA and at 60 mA. (b) At 60 mA the forward voltage is 1.8 V; find the electrical input power and the overall efficiency. (c) The laser emits at 1.31 µm; find its differential quantum efficiency ηd.

  1. Convert currents to amperes: Ith = 0.020 A, 40 mA = 0.040 A, 60 mA = 0.060 A.
  2. At 40 mA: I − Ith = 0.040 − 0.020 = 0.020 A. P = 0.30 × 0.020 = 0.0060 W = 6.0 mW.
  3. At 60 mA: I − Ith = 0.060 − 0.020 = 0.040 A. P = 0.30 × 0.040 = 0.0120 W = 12.0 mW.
  4. Electrical input at 60 mA: Pelec = V·I = 1.8 × 0.060 = 0.108 W = 108 mW.
  5. Overall efficiency: η = 12.0 mW / 108 mW = 0.111, i.e. 11.1%. Most of the input is heat, which is why a laser package sits on a heat sink.
  6. Photon energy in volts: hν/q = 1.24/1.31 = 0.947 V. Then ηd = ηs/(hν/q) = 0.30/0.947 = 0.317: about 32% of the extra electrons above threshold give an output photon.

Answer. 6.0 mW at 40 mA and 12.0 mW at 60 mA; at 60 mA the input is 108 mW and the overall efficiency is 11.1%. The differential quantum efficiency is 0.317.

Exam traps

  • Using the whole current instead of (I − Ith): at 60 mA with ηs = 0.30 W/A you get 18 mW, not 12 mW.
  • Slope efficiency is a slope in W/A, not a percentage. The percentage is the differential quantum efficiency ηd, and the two differ by the factor hν/q.
  • Threshold current (mA) and threshold current density (A/cm²) are different quantities; Jth multiplied by active area gives Ith.
  • Longitudinal mode spacing comes from cavity length, transverse modes come from waveguide width. Examiners swap the two words.
  • Mirror loss αm and internal loss αi are separate terms that add inside Jth; a small R lowers reflectivity and raises threshold, it does not raise output.

3.5 Heterojunction and quantum-well lasers

A heterojunction laser puts a thin active semiconductor between cladding layers of a different material. The bandgap step traps electrons and holes; the refractive-index step traps light. A quantum well goes further: the active layer is only about 10 nm thick, so electron energy levels become quantized. This lowers threshold current and shifts the wavelength. These lasers sit inside transmitters for metro links, data centers and fiber-to-the-home. Some syllabi cover this advanced structure in detail.

You need 3.3 laser action and 3.4 laser diodes; waveguide modes from fiber propagation are assumed.

Heterojunction: trapping carriers and light

A homojunction uses one semiconductor on both sides. Carriers and light spread widely, so threshold current is high. A heterojunction joins materials of different bandgap. In a double heterostructure (DH), a thin GaAs active layer sits between p-AlGaAs and n-AlGaAs cladding. AlGaAs has a wider bandgap and a lower refractive index than GaAs. For AlxGa1−xAs with x up to about 0.37 the bandgap is close to Eg = 1.424 + 1.266x + 0.266x² eV. Lasers for 1.3 and 1.55 µm use In1−xGaxAsyP1−y grown on InP, with Eg ≈ 1.35 − 0.72y + 0.12y² eV. The emission wavelength follows from λ(µm) = 1.24/Eg(eV).

The bandgap step blocks electrons and holes from leaving the active layer: carrier confinement. The refractive-index step, about 0.2–0.3 for GaAs/AlGaAs, guides the optical mode along the same layer: optical confinement. Carrier confinement packs the injected carriers into the thin layer, which raises the gain per unit volume. Optical confinement raises the confinement factor Γ, the share of the mode that overlaps the active layer, so the modal gain Γg rises. Both lower the threshold current.

A separate confinement heterostructure (SCH) adds two undoped layers between active layer and cladding. Their bandgap is wider than the active layer but narrower than the cladding. The optical mode spreads into them, away from doped cladding, while carriers stay in the active layer. Light and carriers are controlled separately, which reduces loss.

Quantum wells and threshold reduction

Below about 20 nm, the active layer becomes a quantum well. Electron motion across the well is restricted, so energies above the band edge are discrete:

En = n²h²/(8m*L²)

n = 1, 2, 3… is the level number; h is Planck's constant in J s; m* is the electron effective mass in kg; L is the well width in m.

The lowest level E₁ adds to the bandgap. The emitted photon energy is Eg + E1e + E1h, where E1e and E1h are the lowest electron and hole levels, each found from the formula with its own effective mass. So the wavelength is shorter than a bulk laser of the same material. The infinite-well formula slightly overestimates the levels; a real well of finite depth gives somewhat smaller values. A 10 nm GaAs well shifts the wavelength by tens of nanometres.

The two-dimensional density of states is step-like. Fewer carriers are needed for a given gain, so threshold current density falls, often by a factor of two or more. A multiple quantum well (MQW) stacks several wells with barrier layers; it gives more gain and a narrower spectrum.

Straining the well, such as InGaAs on GaAs, changes the band structure. Compressive strain lowers the hole effective mass and threshold, and extends the wavelength range. Feedback still comes from cleaved facets, or from a grating in DFB and DBR lasers; those are feedback variants, not different gain media.

3.6 Source-to-fiber power launching and coupling

An optical source emits light into a wide cone, but a fiber accepts only a narrow cone and a small core area. Source-to-fiber coupling gets the most light from the source into the guided mode. Coupling efficiency η is the ratio of coupled power to emitted power. It depends on source radiance, fiber numerical aperture (NA), core area and alignment. Some syllabi cover the full calculation. The same ideas set transmitter design, pigtailing and package tolerances.

You need: 3.2 LED emission and numerical aperture and acceptance angle from fiber basics.

Direct coupling and efficiency

Direct coupling puts the fiber face close to the source with no lens. The source emits over area As and into a cone. The fiber accepts only rays inside its acceptance cone and over its core area Ac. Rays outside either limit are lost.

Radiance L is power per area per solid angle, in W m⁻² sr⁻¹. A high-L source packs more power into the small cone and spot a fiber accepts.

η = Pf / Ps

η is coupling efficiency (0 to 1); Pf is power in the guided mode (W); Ps is power emitted by the source (W).

η ≈ NA²

NA = sin θa is the fiber numerical aperture; θa is the acceptance angle in air; the source area is no larger than the core and fills a hemisphere.

For a Lambertian source the fraction of emitted power inside the acceptance cone is sin²θa = NA², so NA enters squared. The small-angle approximation of the accepted solid angle gives the same number. A laser's narrow beam can beat this LED estimate.

In terms of the source radiance B0 (W cm⁻² sr⁻¹) and emitting radius rs, a Lambertian surface emits Ps = π²rs²B0 in all. A step-index fiber of core radius a takes P = π²rs²B0NA² when rs ≤ a, and P = π²a²B0NA² when rs > a. Dividing by Ps gives the two efficiency formulas of this chapter.

Fresnel reflection at an uncoated air-glass face (n ≈ 1.5) removes near 4% of the light, R = ((n − 1)/(n + 1))² ≈ 0.04, even with perfect alignment. In dB that is −10 log₁₀(1 − R) ≈ 0.18 dB per face.

Large source area and coupling improvement

When the source area is larger than the core, only the part of the source that images onto the core can couple. For uniform emission, add the area ratio:

η ≈ (rc / rs)² NA²

rc is core radius (m); rs is source emitting radius (m); NA is numerical aperture. Use radii in the same unit. The area factor is a power ratio, not an amplitude ratio.

A lens can match the source image to the core, but shrinking the image widens the ray angles at the fiber face, so for a large Lambertian source a lens cannot raise η above the (rc/rs)² NA² limit. A lens helps most with a small source: it spreads the image to fill the core and reduces ray angles, pushing η above NA². A tapered or lensed fiber does the same gradually. Pigtailing fixes the fiber to the source in one package; index-matching glue or an anti-reflection coating cuts Fresnel loss.

Alignment tolerance follows spot size. A single-mode core is near 9 µm, so lateral error must be under about 1 µm. A 50 µm multimode core is looser. Coupling loss is quoted in dB; 3 dB means half the power is lost.

Worked example

Question. A Lambertian LED has emitting-area radius 100 µm. A step-index fiber has core radius 50 µm and NA = 0.20. Total LED power is 2.0 mW. The source area is larger and uniform. Find the coupled power.

  1. Both radii are in µm: rs = 100 µm, rc = 50 µm.
  2. Area factor = (rc / rs)² = (50 / 100)² = 0.25.
  3. NA² = 0.20² = 0.04.
  4. η ≈ 0.25 × 0.04 = 0.01, which is 1%.
  5. Pf = η Ps = 0.01 × 2.0 mW = 0.02 mW = 20 µW.

Answer. The coupled power is 20 µW, with coupling efficiency 0.01.

3.7 Fiber joints, connectors and misalignment losses

A fiber joint is any place where light leaves one fiber and enters another. A splice is permanent; a connector can be plugged and unplugged. No joint is perfect. Light is lost by fiber mismatch and by misalignment. Engineers describe each loss as a fraction and then convert to dB, because dB losses add along a link. This chapter explains fusion and mechanical splices, connectors such as SC, ST and LC, and the lateral, angular and gap errors that set insertion loss and return loss in telecom links, FTTH and data centres.

You need: 3.6 for coupling efficiency, and numerical aperture and core radius from fiber basics.

Splices, connectors and what loss means

A splice joins two fibers permanently. In a fusion splice, an electric arc melts the cleaved ends and they fuse. It gives the lowest loss, often below 0.1 dB, because there is no air gap and no ferrule. A mechanical splice holds two prepared ends in a small alignment channel, often with index-matching gel; it is faster in the field but loss is higher.

A connector is a demountable joint. The fiber is fixed in a ferrule, usually 2.5 mm for SC and ST, or 1.25 mm for LC. SC is square push-pull; ST is bayonet; LC is small form factor. Connectors are used at patch panels, transmitters and receivers, and in FTTH drops.

LdB = −10 log10(ηj)

LdB is insertion loss in dB; ηj is the fraction of power that passes through the joint, from 0 to 1.

RLdB = −10 log10(R)

RLdB is return loss in dB; R is the reflected power fraction. High return loss is good: 40 dB means only 0.0001 of the power is reflected.

Intrinsic loss: built-in fiber mismatch

Intrinsic loss comes from differences in the two fibers, even with perfect alignment. If core diameters differ and the NAs are equal, core-size loss appears mainly when light goes from the larger core into the smaller one. The smaller core acts as a stop: with uniform (over-filled) launch the coupled fraction is about (asmall/alarge)². For 62.5 μm into 50 μm, (50/62.5)² ≈ 0.64, about 1.9 dB. Going 50 μm into 62.5 μm the light enters the larger core fully, so core-size loss is negligible. Angular acceptance loss appears when NAs differ, not from core diameter alone: light from a fiber of NAE into one of smaller NAR loses about −10 log₁₀(NAR/NAE)², so 0.20 into 0.18 costs 0.92 dB.

If numerical aperture (NA) differs, the fiber with smaller NA accepts less of the cone. Rays outside its acceptance angle become cladding modes. For step-index multimode fibers, the loss depends on the overlap of the two angular distributions. In single-mode fibers, mode-field diameter mismatch replaces core diameter mismatch.

A difference in refractive-index profile changes Fresnel reflection and mode shape. Same type and same manufacturer reduce intrinsic loss. In the field, you cannot tune a splice away; you select matched fibers.

Extrinsic loss: misalignment and end finish

Extrinsic loss comes from how the ends are held. Lateral offset is sideways shift d. Angular tilt is angle θ between axes. Longitudinal gap s is the separation between the two fiber ends; the loss grows with s. End finish is cleave angle, roughness, and dome or undercut.

η = (2/π)[arccos x − x√(1 − x²)], x = d/(2a)

η is coupling efficiency for step-index multimode fiber with equal cores; d is lateral offset; a is core radius; x is offset divided by core diameter. At d = 0, η = 1. At d = 2a, η = 0.

A small lateral offset loses little because the two circles still overlap. The denominator uses 2a, the core diameter, because d/(2a) is the shift as a fraction of diameter. Angular tilt tilts the output cone so part misses the receiving core; loss rises with tilt angle and falls with larger NA. A gap adds diffraction spreading and two Fresnel reflections; index-matching gel reduces reflection but not the gap spreading.

For step-index multimode fibers with the same uniform launch, the gap and tilt losses are:

Lgap = −10 log₁₀[a/(a + s tan θc)]², θc = sin⁻¹(NA/n)

Lang = −10 log₁₀[1 − nθ/(π NA)]

Here s is the gap, n is the index of the medium in the gap (1 for air), θc is the half-angle of the cone leaving the first fiber, and θ is the tilt in radians. Each glass face in contact with the gap adds a Fresnel loss −10 log₁₀(1 − R) with R = ((n1 − n)/(n1 + n))², where n1 is the core index.

At a glass-air interface with refractive index about 1.5, normal-incidence Fresnel reflection is about 4% per surface. A rough or angled end scatters light. Angled physical contact (APC) connectors use an 8° angled polish to reduce back reflection, giving high return loss.

Worked example

Question. Two step-index multimode fibers have core radius a = 50 μm and NA = 0.22. A lateral offset d = 10 μm is the only misalignment. Estimate the coupling efficiency and the loss in dB.

  1. List the data: a = 50 μm, d = 10 μm. NA is not needed for pure lateral offset between equal cores.
  2. Find the core diameter: 2a = 2 × 50 μm = 100 μm.
  3. Find x = d/(2a) = 10 μm / 100 μm = 0.1.
  4. Compute arccos(0.1) = 1.4706 rad and √(1 − 0.1²) = √0.99 = 0.99499.
  5. Bracket = 1.4706 − (0.1 × 0.99499) = 1.4706 − 0.0995 = 1.3711.
  6. Efficiency η = (2/π) × 1.3711 = 0.8729.
  7. Loss LdB = −10 log10(0.8729) = 0.5904 dB.

Answer. The coupling efficiency is 0.8729 (87.29%) and the lateral-offset loss is 0.5904 dB.

Question. Two step-index multimode fibers have core radius 25 µm, NA = 0.20 and core index 1.48, and are joined in air. Find the loss of (a) a 10 µm end gap alone, (b) a 1° tilt alone, and (c) the two glass–air faces. Then find the total loss of a joint that has all three.

Exam traps

  • Do not add two joint losses by multiplying their fractions and stopping there. Convert each fraction to dB and add the dB values; the product is the overall fraction.
  • In the overlap formula use d/(2a), not d/a, and check whether the question gives a radius or a diameter. A 10 μm offset in a 100 μm core (a = 50 μm) is x = 0.1, not 0.2.
  • Put the tilt angle in radians in Lang: 1° is 0.01745 rad. Using 1 gives a nonsense loss.
  • Insertion loss and return loss are different. Insertion loss is light lost through the joint; return loss is light reflected back. High return loss in dB is good.
  • Fresnel reflection and lateral offset are separate causes. Index-matching gel reduces Fresnel reflection but does not correct a sideways shift.

3.8 Optical modulators and Mach-Zehnder devices

A laser diode can be switched by its current, but that changes its wavelength and adds chirp. An optical modulator changes light after the laser, so the laser runs steady and only the light is coded. The Mach-Zehnder modulator splits light into two waveguide arms, shifts the phase in each arm by the electro-optic effect, and recombines them. Interference turns phase difference into output power. These devices sit in high-speed metro and long-haul links, where clean, low-chirp pulses matter.

You need: 3.4 semiconductor laser diodes, waveguide modes from fiber propagation, and the idea of WDM (see WDM concepts and components).

Why put the modulator after the laser

Direct modulation changes the laser current to switch the light. The current changes carrier density, gain and refractive index together. The output frequency therefore shifts during each pulse; this is chirp. Carrier lifetime and relaxation oscillation also limit speed and cause pattern-dependent output. A steady current avoids these problems. The laser runs continuous wave (CW), and a separate optical modulator after it sets the light level. Phase and Mach-Zehnder modulators use the linear electro-optic (Pockels) effect: an electric field E changes the refractive index of a crystal without inversion symmetry, such as lithium niobate (LiNbO3) or indium phosphide (InP), by Δn = ½n³rE, where r is the electro-optic coefficient in m/V. The electro-absorption modulator described below works by field-induced absorption instead.

φ = 2πnL/λ

φ is phase in radians; n is refractive index; L is arm length in metres; λ is free-space wavelength in metres.

Δφ = πV/Vπ

Δφ is phase difference in radians; V is applied voltage in volts; Vπ, the half-wave voltage, is the voltage that changes the phase difference between the two arms by π radians. In push-pull drive each arm shifts by π/2.

A phase modulator alone changes phase, not power. To get an on-off optical signal, the phase change must be converted into an intensity change. The device that does this is the Mach-Zehnder modulator (MZM).

Mach-Zehnder interferometer

An input waveguide splits light equally into two arms. Each arm carries a phase φ1 or φ2. A combiner adds the two optical fields. If the fields are equal in amplitude and the splitter is ideal, the output field is the sum of the two arm fields.

Eout = (Ein/2)(ejφ1 + ejφ2)

Eout is output electric field; Ein is input electric field; j is the imaginary unit; φ1 and φ2 are the arm phases in radians.

Pout = Pin cos²((φ1 − φ2)/2)

Pout is output optical power in watts; Pin is input optical power in watts; φ1 − φ2 is the phase difference in radians.

In push-pull drive, the two arms are driven in opposite directions: φ1 = φ0 + Δφ/2 and φ2 = φ0 − Δφ/2. The common phase φ0 does not affect interference, so only Δφ remains. With Δφ = πV/Vπ, the transfer function becomes Pout = Pin cos²(πV/(2Vπ)). At V = 0 the output is maximum. At V = Vπ the output is the first null. At V = 2Vπ it is maximum again.

Pout = Pin cos²(πV/(2Vπ))

Pout is output power in watts; Pin is input power in watts; V is drive voltage in volts; Vπ is the half-wave voltage in volts.

A useful bias point is V = Vπ/2, called quadrature. There the slope is maximum, so a small signal gives the largest change. The curve is still cos², not a straight line, so large swings add harmonics. The extinction ratio is ER = 10 log10(Pmax/Pmin) in dB; finite ER means the off state still leaks light. Insertion loss compares output with input when the device is on.

Vπ follows from the Pockels effect. Each arm of length L sees a field V/d across an electrode gap d, and only a fraction Γ of the field overlaps the light. Adding the two opposite arm shifts gives, for push-pull drive:

Vπ = λd/(2n³r33ΓL)

λ is the free-space wavelength; d is the electrode gap; n is the crystal index; r33 is the electro-optic coefficient in m/V; Γ is the field–light overlap (0 to 1); L is the electrode length. Single-arm drive needs twice this voltage.

An unequal splitter leaves light at the null. With field amplitudes a and b in the two arms, Pmax/Pmin = ((a + b)/(a − b))², so a 55:45 power split already limits the extinction ratio to 26 dB.

Chirp and electro-absorption

Chirp is unwanted change of optical frequency during modulation. A push-pull MZM can have very low chirp because the two arms change phase in opposite directions. A single-arm drive does not cancel the phase change and can chirp. An electro-absorption modulator (EAM) works differently: an electric field increases absorption near the band edge, using the Franz-Keldysh effect in bulk material or the quantum-confined Stark effect in quantum wells. An EAM is compact and needs a low drive voltage, and it can be integrated with a distributed-feedback laser as an electro-absorption modulated laser. It is wavelength dependent and can add some chirp. A travelling-wave MZM uses electrodes that let the electrical and optical waves travel together, keeping the drive voltage low at high bit rates.

Worked example

Question. A push-pull Mach-Zehnder modulator has Vπ = 4.0 V. Input optical power is 2.0 mW. The phase difference is Δφ = πV/Vπ. Find the output power at V = 2.0 V and at V = 4.0 V.

  1. At V = 2.0 V: Δφ = π × 2.0 / 4.0 = π/2 rad.
  2. Pout = Pin cos²(Δφ/2) = 2.0 mW × cos²(π/4).
  3. cos²(π/4) = 0.5, so Pout = 2.0 mW × 0.5 = 1.0 mW.
  4. At V = 4.0 V: Δφ = π × 4.0 / 4.0 = π rad.
  5. Pout = 2.0 mW × cos²(π/2) = 2.0 mW × 0 = 0.0 mW.

Answer. At 2.0 V, Pout = 1.0 mW; at 4.0 V, Pout = 0.0 mW.

Worked example 2

Question. A push-pull LiNbO3 Mach-Zehnder modulator works at 1.55 µm with n = 2.14, r33 = 30.8 pm/V, electrode gap d = 10 µm, electrode length L = 2.0 cm and overlap Γ = 0.50. Find Vπ, the single-arm value, and the output at the quadrature bias for Pin = 2.0 mW.

  1. Index term: n³ = 2.14³ = 9.80; n³r33 = 9.80 × 30.8×10⁻¹² = 3.02×10⁻¹⁰ m/V.
  2. Denominator: 2n³r33ΓL = 2 × 3.02×10⁻¹⁰ × 0.50 × 0.020 = 6.04×10⁻¹² m²/V.
  3. Numerator: λd = 1.55×10⁻⁶ × 10×10⁻⁶ = 1.55×10⁻¹¹ m².
  4. Vπ = 1.55×10⁻¹¹ / 6.04×10⁻¹² = 2.57 V. Driving one arm only needs 2 × 2.57 = 5.13 V.
  5. Quadrature: V = Vπ/2 = 1.28 V; Δφ = π/2, so Pout = 2.0 mW × cos²(π/4) = 1.0 mW.

Answer. Vπ = 2.57 V in push-pull (5.13 V single-arm); at the 1.28 V quadrature bias the output is 1.0 mW.

Exam traps

  • Using P proportional to cos(Δφ) instead of cos²(Δφ/2) gives wrong nulls and wrong power at quadrature.
  • Calling Vπ the voltage for maximum transmission or for a 2π phase shift. Vπ gives the first null, a π phase difference.
  • Forgetting the push-pull factor: each arm gets ±Δφ/2, while interference depends on the total difference Δφ.
  • Mixing extinction ratio with insertion loss. Extinction ratio compares on and off levels of one modulator; insertion loss compares output with input.
  • Assuming an external modulator has zero chirp in every drive scheme. Single-arm drive and electro-absorption modulators can still chirp.

Takeaways

  1. Absorption removes light, spontaneous emission gives incoherent light, and stimulated emission gives coherent light when a second photon triggers emission.
  2. An LED is a spontaneous emitter; its launched power depends on internal quantum efficiency, extraction efficiency and coupling.
  3. Laser oscillation requires population inversion, optical feedback and pump current above threshold gain.
  4. A semiconductor laser diode uses cleaved facets as a Fabry-Perot cavity and emits coherent light above threshold current.
  5. Heterojunctions and quantum wells shrink the active region, confine light and carriers, and reduce threshold current.
  6. Source-to-fiber coupling efficiency depends on source radiance, fiber numerical aperture, core area and alignment.
  7. Joint and connector losses from lateral, angular and longitudinal misalignment are added in dB.
  8. A Mach-Zehnder modulator changes phase and then converts it to an intensity change with a cos²(Δφ/2) response.

Understand Explain absorption, spontaneous emission and stimulated emission. Use simple energy-level diagrams and state why population inversion is needed for net optical gain. 12 marks

Points to cover. Define each of the three processes; Show lower and upper levels for each process; Spontaneous emission is random in phase and direction; Stimulated emission is coherent with the incoming photon; Absorption removes photons and opposes gain; Population inversion makes stimulated emission exceed absorption.

Model answer.

  1. Model. Take a two-level system: lower level E1 with N1 atoms per m³ and upper level E2 with N2 per m³. Light of frequency f interacts only when hf = E2 − E1. For an 850 nm transition hf = 2.34×10⁻¹⁹ J = 1.46 eV. Let ρ(f) be the spectral energy density of the light in J m⁻³ Hz⁻¹.
  2. Absorption. A photon of energy hf is taken up and an electron moves from E1 to E2. The photon disappears, so absorption removes light from the beam. Its rate is B12ρ(f)N1 per m³ per second: it needs light and atoms in the lower level.
  3. Spontaneous emission. An electron in E2 falls to E1 by itself, after a random delay whose average is the spontaneous lifetime τsp, and emits one photon in a random direction with random phase. Its rate is A21N2, with A21 = 1/τsp; for τsp = 5 ns, A21 = 2.0×10⁸ s⁻¹. This incoherent light is what an LED gives.
  4. Stimulated emission. A passing photon of energy hf makes an electron in E2 fall to E1 at once. The new photon has the same frequency, phase, direction and polarization as the trigger, so one photon in gives two identical photons out. Its rate is B21ρ(f)N2. This is the process a laser amplifies.
  5. Einstein relations. In thermal equilibrium the three rates must balance with blackbody radiation, which gives:
    B12 = B21                     (non-degenerate levels)
A21/B21 = 8πhf³/c³
N2/N1 = exp(−hf/kT)            (Boltzmann)
  6. Comparison of the three processes:
    ProcessNeedsPhotons afterRateUsed in
    Absorptionphoton + atom in E1one fewerB12ρN1photodetectors

The medium has material gain g (per cm) when pumped, of which only the fraction Γ in the active layer acts on the mode, so the modal gain is Γg. The internal loss αi (scattering, free-carrier absorption) acts all along the path.

after reflection at facet 2: I0·R2·exp[(Γg − αi)L]

    R1R2·exp[2L(Γgth − αi)] = 1

  4. Longitudinal gap s. Light spreads at the cone half-angle θc = sin⁻¹(NA/n) across the gap, so part of it lands outside the far core: Lgap = −10 log₁₀[a/(a + s tan θc)]².   5. NA mismatch. If the receiving fiber has a smaller NA, rays steeper than its acceptance angle are not guided: loss ≈ −10 log₁₀(NAR/NAE)², so 0.20 into 0.18 costs 0.92 dB. Into a larger NA there is no loss. Core-size mismatch works the same way with (aR/aE)²: 62.5 µm into 50 µm costs 1.94 dB.

Index-matching gel in a mechanical joint removes the Fresnel loss but cannot fix an offset.

Evaluate Compare direct modulation of a laser diode with external Mach-Zehnder modulation for high-bit-rate links. Discuss chirp, extinction ratio, bias control and insertion loss. 13 marks

Points to cover. Direct modulation changes the laser current; It causes chirp and relaxation effects; External modulator keeps the laser at constant bias; Mach-Zehnder gives low chirp and a high extinction ratio; External modulator adds insertion loss and needs bias control; Choose by reach, bit rate and cost.

Model answer.

  1. Direct modulation. The data current is added to a bias above threshold, and output follows P = ηs(I − Ith). No extra optical part is needed, so it is cheap and compact.
  2. Chirp. Changing the current changes the carrier density, and with it the refractive index, so the laser frequency swings during each pulse. The chirped pulse has a wider spectrum, and fiber dispersion turns that into pulse spreading; this, not the laser’s speed, limits directly modulated links at 10 Gb/s and above over long fiber.
  3. Extinction ratio of direct modulation. To avoid turn-on delay and the worst chirp the laser is kept above threshold in the off state. For Ith = 20 mA and ηs = 0.30 W/A, driving between 25 mA and 60 mA gives 1.5 mW and 12 mW: ER = 10 log₁₀(12/1.5) = 9.0 dB.
  4. External Mach-Zehnder modulation. The laser runs CW at constant current, so its line stays narrow; the MZM sets the intensity through Pout = Pincos²(πV/2Vπ). In push-pull drive the two arm phases change in opposite directions and the common phase cancels, so the output is nearly chirp-free.
  5. Extinction ratio of the MZM. The null is set by the splitter balance: a 55:45 split gives Pmax/Pmin = ((0.742 + 0.671)/(0.742 − 0.671))² = 398, i.e. 26 dB, far better than the 9 dB above.
  6. Bias control. The MZM must sit at a set point (quadrature or the null) on a cos² curve that drifts with temperature and charge build-up in LiNbO3, so it needs a feedback bias loop. The laser in direct modulation needs bias tracking because Ith drifts as exp(T/Tc).
  7. Drive voltage and insertion loss. A LiNbO3 MZM with d = 10 µm, L = 2 cm and Γ = 0.5 has Vπ = λd/(2n³r33ΓL) = 2.57 V in push-pull, so it needs a broadband driver of a few volts. Waveguide and fiber-coupling losses add a few dB of insertion loss, which the power budget must cover, and the device is centimetres long.

  9. Verdict. Direct modulation suits short and metro links and cost-sensitive access networks, where reach is short enough that chirp does little harm. External MZM modulation is preferred for long-haul and high-rate links, and for the phase-coded formats of coherent systems, because only it gives clean, low-chirp pulses with a high extinction ratio.
  10. Closing check. Raising the direct-modulation off level to 30 mA gives 3 mW and ER = 10 log₁₀(12/3) = 6.0 dB, showing the trade: more bias means less chirp and turn-on delay but a poorer extinction ratio.
Analyze Trace the steps from carrier injection to coherent output in a semiconductor laser: emission processes, population inversion, gain, feedback, threshold and heterojunction confinement. Explain what limits the output power. 15 marks

Points to cover. Forward bias injects electrons and holes into the active layer; Recombination gives spontaneous and stimulated emission; Population inversion gives net gain; Cleaved facets provide optical feedback; Oscillation starts above threshold gain and threshold current; Heterojunction confines carriers and light; Output power is limited by heating, gain saturation and facet damage.

Model answer.

  1. Injection. Forward bias drives electrons from the n-side and holes from the p-side into a thin active layer. In a double heterostructure the wider-gap claddings (for GaAs/Al0.30Ga0.70As, 1.828 eV against 1.424 eV) stop them leaving, so the carrier density builds until recombination balances injection.
  2. Spontaneous emission first. At low current the carriers recombine spontaneously, each emitting a photon of energy close to Eg in a random direction: λ = 1.24/1.424 = 0.871 µm for bulk GaAs. The device behaves like an LED, giving microwatts.
  3. Population inversion. As the density rises, the quasi-Fermi levels separate. Once their separation exceeds the photon energy, more states are filled at the upper energy than at the lower, which is inversion. Stimulated emission now beats absorption and the medium has gain g, roughly g = βJ in the linear model.
  4. Optical confinement. The claddings also have a lower index, so the layer is a waveguide; a fraction Γ of the mode overlaps the gain, and the modal gain is Γg.
  5. Feedback. The cleaved facets reflect R = ((3.5 − 1)/(3.5 + 1))² = 0.31 each, forming a Fabry–Perot cavity. Photons from spontaneous emission travelling along the axis are amplified by stimulated emission on each pass, reflect, and return.
  6. Threshold. Oscillation starts when the round-trip gain equals the losses:
    R1R2·exp[2L(Γgth − αi)] = 1  →  Γgth = αi + (1/2L)·ln[1/(R1R2)]
L = 300 µm, R = 0.32, αi = 10 cm⁻¹, Γ = 0.30:
αm = 37.98 cm⁻¹,  Γgth = 47.98 cm⁻¹,  gth = 159.9 cm⁻¹
  7. Coherent output. Above threshold the gain clamps at gth; each extra electron gives a stimulated photon that copies the existing field, so the output is narrow-band and directional, in modes 2nL = mλ spaced c/(2ngL) = 139 GHz apart for ng = 3.6. The light-current line is P = ηs(I − Ith).
  8. Numbers for Ith = 20 mA, ηs = 0.30 W/A, V = 1.8 V:
    CurrentOutputElectrical inputOverall efficiency

  9. What limits the output power. (1) Heating: 96 of the 108 mW at 60 mA becomes heat; temperature raises Ith as exp(T/Tc), lowers ηs, and the L-I curve bends over (thermal rollover). (2) Gain saturation and spatial hole burning at high photon density, which make the output kink and go multimode. (3) Catastrophic optical damage: the facet absorbs light, heats and melts above a power density limit; coatings and wider stripes raise it. (4) Lifetime: high current and temperature speed up degradation.
  10. Role of confinement in the trace. Because carriers and light share the same thin layer, threshold falls; quantum wells (5–20 nm) go further by quantising the levels, which lowers Jth again and shifts λ (833 nm for a 10 nm GaAs well).
  11. Closing check. At 60 mA, P = 0.30 × (0.060 − 0.020) = 12.0 mW ✓, and the differential quantum efficiency at 0.85 µm would be ηd = 0.30/(1.24/0.85) = 0.21, well below 1 as it must be.

Glossary

Absorption
Loss of a photon when an electron or atom moves from a lower to a higher energy state.
Active region
Semiconductor region where carriers recombine and light is generated or amplified.
Angular misalignment
Angle between the axes of two fibers.
Characteristic temperature
Tc in Ith ∝ exp(T/Tc); a small Tc means a strongly temperature-sensitive laser.
Cleaved facet
Mirror-like end face formed by cleaving a semiconductor crystal along a crystal plane.
Confinement factor
Fraction Γ of the optical mode power that lies in the active layer; modal gain is Γg.
Coupling efficiency
Ratio of power launched into the fiber to power emitted by the source.
Differential quantum efficiency
Extra output photons per extra injected electron above threshold, ηd = ηsq/(hν).
Direct modulation
Changing laser drive current to modulate output power.
External quantum efficiency
Photons emitted outside the device per injected electron.
Extinction ratio
Ratio of high to low optical power in a modulated signal.
Extraction efficiency
Fraction of generated light that leaves the chip.
Fabry–Perot cavity
Two parallel mirrors, such as cleaved facets, that provide optical feedback at resonant wavelengths.
Half-wave voltage
Vπ, the drive voltage that changes the phase difference between the two arms of a Mach-Zehnder modulator by π.
Heterojunction
Junction of two semiconductors with different band gaps that can confine carriers and light.
Insertion loss
Loss added by a component, in dB, equal to 10 log₁₀(Pin/Pout).
Internal quantum efficiency
Fraction of injected carriers that recombine radiatively.
Lambertian source
Source whose radiance is constant with angle and whose intensity falls as cos θ.
Lateral misalignment
Sideways offset between two fiber cores.
Longitudinal misalignment
Gap between fiber ends along the axis.
Mach–Zehnder interferometer
Two-path interferometer that converts phase difference into intensity change.
Numerical aperture
NA = √(n₁² − n₂²) for step-index fiber; it measures the light-gathering cone.
Optical feedback
Return of part of the light to the gain medium by mirrors or facets to sustain laser oscillation.
Population inversion
Condition where the upper energy level has more carriers than the lower level, so stimulated emission exceeds absorption.
Quantum well
Very thin semiconductor layer that confines carriers and creates quantised energy levels.
Radiance
Power emitted per unit area per unit solid angle, in W/(m²·sr).
Return loss
Reflected power below the incident power, in dB, −10 log₁₀(R); a high value means little reflection.
Spontaneous emission
Random emission of a photon by an excited atom without an incoming photon.
Stimulated emission
Emission of a photon coherent with an incoming photon when the atom is already excited.
Threshold gain
Gain that makes round-trip gain equal round-trip loss and starts laser action.
