6.1 WDM concepts and components

Wavelength division multiplexing (WDM) sends several light signals through one fiber at the same time, each on its own wavelength. One laser and its modulator give only a few Gb/s, while the 1550 nm window holds tens of THz. WDM uses that window as many separate channels, so capacity grows with the number of channels N, not with electronic speed. Telephone backbones, cable TV feeds and data-centre links use it.

You need Unit 1 (single-mode fiber, V-number) and Unit 3 (laser sources, external modulators).

Why one fiber carries many colours

WDM assigns each signal a different wavelength λ, called a channel. All channels share one fiber. If there are N channels and each carries B bit/s, total capacity is C = N × B, as long as the fiber passband and the receivers allow it. This is the main reason WDM exists: one laser and modulator give a few Gb/s, but the 1550 nm window spans tens of THz.

C = N × B

C is total bit rate in bit/s; N is the number of wavelength channels; B is the bit rate per channel in bit/s.

Δλ ≈ λ² Δf / c

Δλ is channel spacing in wavelength (m); λ is the operating wavelength (m); Δf is channel spacing in frequency (Hz); c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s is the speed of light in vacuum.

At 1550 nm, a 100 GHz slot is about 0.8 nm. The same 100 GHz slot is wider in nanometres at longer wavelengths because Δλ grows as λ². ITU-T grids put channels on exact frequencies near 193.1 THz. 100 GHz spacing is common; 50 GHz doubles the channel count in the same band. DWDM uses tight spacing, often 50 or 100 GHz, with temperature-controlled lasers. CWDM uses 20 nm spacing from 1270 nm to 1610 nm, uncooled lasers and short reach.

Mux and demux: sorting colours

A multiplexer combines λ1…λN into one fiber; a demultiplexer separates them. Some syllabi say “wavelength multiplexer” or “WDM mux/demux” for the same pair. Five common parts do this job.

  • Prism: refraction angle changes with wavelength; simple, low channel count.
  • Diffraction grating: periodic grooves send each wavelength to a different angle; good for many channels.
  • Thin-film dielectric filter: a stack of layers passes some wavelengths and reflects others; used in mux/demux and add/drop.
  • Arrayed waveguide grating (AWG): waveguides with fixed path differences interfere so each wavelength focuses on a different output; common in integrated optics.
  • Fiber Bragg grating (FBG): a periodic index pattern in the fiber reflects one wavelength and passes the rest.

Four parameters describe a mux or demux. Insertion loss is the drop in dB from input to wanted output; lower is better. Adjacent-channel crosstalk is the leakage power from the next channel divided by the wanted channel power, in dB; the leakage is smaller, so the value is negative and more negative is better. Channel uniformity is the spread in dB between the strongest and weakest output. Passband width is the range of wavelengths over which loss stays low. Coupler and grating physics is in 6.2.

Reading a WDM specification

The comb is the set of channel frequencies. Its width is roughly (N − 1)Δf, not NΔf, because N channels have N − 1 gaps. Capacity grows with N, but N is limited by amplifier gain band (see 6.3), laser wavelength drift, filter passband and crosstalk. A mux with 1.5 dB insertion loss removes 1.5 dB from every channel; a demux adds more. Passband width must cover laser drift and modulation chirp. Adjacent-channel crosstalk sets how close channels can be. In a network, each channel becomes a lightpath, see 6.5.

Worked example

Question. A DWDM system uses 40 channels on the ITU grid near 1550 nm, spaced 100 GHz apart, each carrying 10 Gb/s. (a) Find the total capacity. (b) Find the spacing in nanometres using Δλ = λ²Δf/c. (c) Find the width of the whole comb. (d) In a 3.2 THz band with 100 GHz spacing, how many 10 Gb/s channels fit?

  1. Step 1: N = 40 channels and B = 10 Gb/s per channel. Total capacity C = N × B = 40 × 10 = 400 Gb/s.
  2. Step 2: λ = 1550 nm = 1550 × 10⁻⁹ m. Δf = 100 GHz = 100 × 10⁹ Hz. c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. First λ² = (1550 × 10⁻⁹)² = 2.4025 × 10⁻¹² m².
  3. Step 3: λ²Δf = 2.4025 × 10⁻¹² × 100 × 10⁹ = 0.24025. Divide by c: Δλ = 0.24025 ÷ (3 × 10⁸) = 8.008 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.8008 nm.
  4. Step 4: N channels have N − 1 = 39 gaps. Comb width = 39 × 0.8008 = 31.2 nm.
  5. Step 5: Band = 3.2 THz = 3.2 × 10¹² Hz. Number of channels = 3.2 × 10¹² ÷ (100 × 10⁹) = 32. Capacity = 32 × 10 = 320 Gb/s.

Answer. The answers are 400 Gb/s total capacity, 0.8008 nm spacing, 31.2 nm comb width, 32 channels and 320 Gb/s in the 3.2 THz band.

Exam traps

  • 100 GHz is not always 0.8 nm: Δλ = λ²Δf/c, so the same frequency spacing is wider at 1600 nm than at 1300 nm.
  • Total capacity is N × B per direction; multiply by 2 for a duplex pair only if the question says both directions are counted, and never multiply by N a second time.
  • CWDM is 20 nm spacing over 1270–1610 nm; DWDM is tight 50/100 GHz spacing. Do not mix the names.
  • Crosstalk is given as a negative dB ratio; −30 dB is better than −20 dB. Insertion loss is positive dB.
  • Mux and demux are named from the direction of the wanted signal; a passive part can often work either way.

6.2 Passive devices: couplers, isolators and gratings

A coupler splits light into two or more arms, an isolator passes light one way only, a circulator sends light round a fixed 1→2→3 route, and a Bragg grating reflects one wavelength and passes the rest. None needs electrical power: they are passive. They set how much power a WDM link loses and how much of one channel leaks into its neighbour, so every WDM node is built from them.

You need total internal reflection and single-mode fiber from Unit 1, the back-reflection problem of laser diodes from Unit 3, and the WDM channel picture from 6.1.

Couplers: sharing light with no power supply

A coupler takes light in at one port and shares it among two or more output ports; run backwards, it combines several inputs into one output. Only glass is involved, so it needs no supply and never wears out.

The fused biconical taper (FBT) coupler is made by placing or twisting two single-mode fibers together, fusing the joint and tapering (stretching) it. Over the fused length the cores lie so close that light leaks from one core into the other. The planar coupler does the same with waveguides patterned on a silica substrate; coupling is fixed by waveguide spacing and length, so it drifts less with temperature.

Shapes include the 1×2 splitter, 2×2 coupler, N×N star that mixes many inputs into many outputs, and the tree built from 1×2 stages. Splitting costs power: a perfect 1×2 gives half to each arm, which is 3 dB.

  • Coupling ratio CR = P₃/(P₃ + P₄) × 100 percent: share of total output leaving one arm. A 50:50 coupler has CR = 50 percent.
  • Excess loss = 10 log₁₀(Pin/(P₃ + P₄)) dB: power lost inside the coupler, found by adding all outputs.
  • Insertion loss = 10 log₁₀(Pin/P₃) dB: loss from input to one chosen output port; it contains both split ratio and excess loss.
  • Directivity = 10 log₁₀(P3,forward/P1,leak) dB: P3,forward is the output at port 3 for light fed into port 1; P1,leak is the small power appearing at port 1 when light is fed into port 3. High directivity keeps echoes out of the link.
  • Uniformity = 10 log₁₀(Pmax/Pmin) dB over the output ports: how evenly a 1×N splitter shares power.

Pin is input power at port 1; P₃ and P₄ are output powers, all in the same unit (mW or W). A power ratio in a log is dimensionless, so dB is the same with mW or W.

Isolators and circulators: controlling direction

A laser diode is disturbed by light reflected back into it: the reflected wave pulls the gain and adds noise (Unit 3). An isolator is the one-way valve that stops it.

An isolator is a polarizer, a Faraday rotator and a second polarizer. The first polarizer passes one plane. The Faraday rotator, a magneto-optic crystal in a magnetic field, turns that plane by 45°; the second polarizer sits at 45° and passes the light. The key: the crystal rotates the plane in the same sense viewed from the lab, whatever the direction of travel. Light reflected from the fiber turns another 45°, arrives at 90° to the first polarizer, and is blocked.

ParameterTypical value
Insertion loss (forward)0.5 to 1 dB
Isolation (reverse)40 to 60 dB

A circulator is the three-port version: light in at port 1 leaves by port 2, in at port 2 leaves by port 3, in at port 3 leaves by port 1. The reverse route 2→1 is heavily attenuated. Because a circulator routes light instead of splitting it, no 3 dB is thrown away. That makes it the natural partner of a Bragg grating.

Fiber Bragg gratings and filters

A fiber Bragg grating is a short piece of fiber whose core refractive index rises and falls periodically along the axis with pitch Λ. The forward wave reflects weakly at every period. Those reflections add in phase for one wavelength only, the Bragg wavelength.

λ_B = 2 n_eff Λ

λ_B is the reflected Bragg wavelength (m); n_eff is the effective index of the guided mode, about 1.45 in silica; Λ is the grating pitch (m). The factor 2 is the round trip: the wave travels one pitch forward and one pitch back.

All other wavelengths pass almost untouched, so the grating is a band-stop (notch) filter with bandwidth from a fraction of a nanometre to a few nanometres.

Apodization means tapering the modulation strength gradually to zero at the two ends. A sharp start and stop produce side lobes: small unwanted reflection peaks just outside the main reflected band, sitting right on neighbouring WDM channels. Tapering removes them.

Uses: notch filter in front of a pump laser, a WDM demultiplexer (a chain of gratings, each reflecting one channel), gain flattening, and chirped gratings for dispersion compensation.

With a circulator the grating becomes an add-drop multiplexer. Channels λ1 to λ4 enter port 1 and leave at port 2, where the grating reflects λ2 back; λ1, λ3 and λ4 pass straight through to the output line, and the reflected λ2 re-enters port 2 and leaves at port 3 as the dropped channel. To add a channel, feed the new λ2 into port 3; the circulator sends it back along the same reflection path.

Thin-film filters do the same in free space: a stack of dielectric layers, each a quarter-wave thick at the design wavelength, on glass. Reflections at layer boundaries add for one band and cancel for the rest. A demultiplexer is a chain of such filters, each dropping one channel. A tunable Fabry-Perot filter is a cavity between two mirrors; it passes a comb of wavelengths, and moving the mirrors slides the comb so one channel is chosen.

Worked example

Question. A fused 2×2 coupler receives 1.00 mW at port 1. Ports 3 and 4 each give 0.40 mW. (a) Find the coupling ratio. (b) Find the excess loss. (c) Find the insertion loss of one output port. (d) Find the grating pitch that reflects 1550 nm in a fiber with n_eff = 1.45.

  1. (a) Total output = P₃ + P₄ = 0.40 + 0.40 = 0.80 mW. CR = P₃/(P₃ + P₄) × 100 = 0.40/0.80 × 100 = 50 percent, a 50:50 coupler.
  2. (b) Excess loss = 10 log₁₀(Pin/(P₃ + P₄)) = 10 log₁₀(1.00/0.80) = 10 log₁₀(1.25) = 10 × 0.0969 = 0.97 dB.
  3. (c) Insertion loss = 10 log₁₀(Pin/P₃) = 10 log₁₀(1.00/0.40) = 10 log₁₀(2.5) = 10 × 0.3979 = 3.98 dB.
  4. (d) From λ_B = 2 n_eff Λ, Λ = λ_B/(2 n_eff) = 1550/(2 × 1.45) = 1550/2.90 = 534.48 nm.
  5. Check: 3.98 dB is close to the ideal 3.01 dB of a 50:50 split plus the 0.97 dB excess loss.

Answer. CR = 50:50, excess loss = 0.97 dB, insertion loss = 3.98 dB, grating pitch = 534.48 nm.

Exam traps

  • Dropping the factor 2 in λ_B = 2 n_eff Λ. The wave goes one pitch forward and one back, so the 2 is always there; without it the pitch comes out double.
  • Treating excess loss and insertion loss as the same number. Excess loss compares total output with input; insertion loss compares one port with input.
  • Measuring the coupling ratio against the input power instead of total output power. CR is a share of what comes out.
  • Swapping isolation and insertion loss on an isolator. Isolation is the reverse attenuation (40 to 60 dB); insertion loss is the small forward penalty (0.5 to 1 dB).
  • Assuming a circulator splits power like a coupler. It routes full power in one fixed direction, which is why it wastes no 3 dB.

Analyze A grating used inside a WDM demultiplexer has strong side lobes. Explain the cause and the cure, and say what happens to the neighbouring channels without the cure. 5 marks

A grating whose modulation starts and stops abruptly behaves like a rectangular window; its reflection spectrum shows side lobes just outside the main reflected band. Analyze Explain how a circulator and a Bragg grating together form an add-drop multiplexer, and why a 2×2 coupler is a poorer choice for the same node. 6 marks

Light enters circulator port 1 and leaves at port 2 into the grating. The grating reflects only λ2; the other channels pass to the output line. The reflected λ2 returns to port 2 and the circulator sends it out at port 3 as the dropped channel. Adding works along the same reflection path. A 2×2 coupler would split each channel in both directions, losing at least 3 dB per pass, and would also send part of every other channel into the drop port, adding crosstalk.

6.3 Optical amplifiers: SOA and EDFA

An optical amplifier boosts light directly, without converting it to electrons. A pump feeds energy into a gain medium, and incoming photons trigger stimulated emission that copies them. It exists because fiber loses about 0.2 dB per km: an 80 km span arrives 16 dB weaker, so eight spans would cost 128 dB. Before amplifiers, every span ended at a receiver and a fresh transmitter started the next. Now one amplifier per span carries the same light across a country or an ocean. EDFAs serve 1550 nm long-haul links; SOAs serve shorter and cheaper links.

You need Unit 3 (stimulated emission, laser diode) and Unit 2 (attenuation, dB arithmetic); 6.1 explains why one amplifier serves many wavelengths.

Why amplify, and the five numbers

A single-mode fiber at 1550 nm loses about 0.2 dB per km. An 80 km span therefore arrives 0.2 × 80 = 16 dB weaker. Eight such spans lose 128 dB, which no laser can launch and no receiver can detect. An amplifier at each span restores the power, so the same light continues instead of being detected and re-created.

G(dB) = 10 log₁₀(P_out / P_in)

G(dB) is the gain in decibels; P_out is the output power in mW; P_in is the input power in mW. A 20 dB gain means P_out = 100 × P_in.

Five numbers appear on every amplifier data sheet.

  • Gain: 20-40 dB typical; it sets how much span loss one amplifier can pay back.
  • Gain bandwidth: the wavelength range where gain stays within a stated drop, say 1 dB; the EDFA C-band is about 35 nm wide.
  • Saturation output power: the output at which gain falls by 3 dB; beyond it the amplifier stops being linear.
  • Noise figure NF: the dB degradation of signal-to-noise ratio; 3 dB is the theoretical minimum, and lower is better.
  • Polarization dependence: the gain difference between input polarizations, in dB; it is large in SOAs and small in EDFAs.

SOA: a semiconductor gain chip

A semiconductor optical amplifier (SOA) is a laser diode chip run below threshold. Anti-reflection coatings on both facets stop feedback, so light passes once through the active region and is amplified by stimulated emission. Different material compositions cover 1310 nm and 1550 nm. Gain is 20-30 dB and noise figure 6-8 dB.

Two weaknesses limit its use. Gain depends strongly on polarization, often by several dB, because the active region is asymmetric. Second, the carrier density saturates, so a strong channel reduces gain for its neighbours: cross-gain modulation, which causes crosstalk between WDM channels (see 6.1).

SOAs therefore serve as boosters after a single transmitter, as inline amplifiers in short access links where cost matters more than OSNR, and as gates or wavelength converters, because their gain responds in nanoseconds.

EDFA: erbium-doped fiber amplifier

An EDFA is a few metres of silica fiber whose core is doped with Er³⁺ ions, giving a three-level system. A pump photon lifts an ion from level 1 to level 3, which decays quickly to the metastable level 2. A 1550 nm signal photon then stimulates the 2 → 1 transition, adding an identical photon. Pumping at 980 nm reaches level 3; pumping at 1480 nm reaches level 2 directly. The 980 nm pump gives the lower noise figure, 1480 nm the higher saturation power.

The parts are a pump laser, a WDM coupler that merges pump and signal, 10-30 m of doped fiber, isolators before and after to block backward amplified spontaneous emission (ASE) and reflections, and a gain-flattening filter that equalises channels across the band. The C-band runs 1530-1565 nm with 30-40 dB gain and NF 4-6 dB; a longer doped fiber extends gain to the L-band, 1570-1610 nm.

OSNR(dB) = 58 + P_in(dBm) − NF(dB) − 10 log₁₀ N

OSNR is the optical signal-to-noise ratio in a 0.1 nm reference bandwidth at 1550 nm; P_in is the power into each amplifier in dBm; NF is its noise figure in dB; N is the number of identical amplifiers in the chain. The constant 58 folds in the reference bandwidth and photon energy.

Every amplifier adds ASE, so noise accumulates while the chain holds signal power fixed. That is why OSNR falls as 10 log₁₀ N: eight amplifiers cost 9.0 dB. Raman amplification, a distributed alternative that pumps the fiber itself, belongs to Unit 2.

Worked example

Question. A 10 Gb/s channel enters each EDFA at −20 dBm. Every amplifier gives 20 dB gain with a noise figure of 5 dB, and there are 8 spans. (a) Show that the input power to every amplifier is the same. (b) Estimate the OSNR after the last amplifier in 0.1 nm using OSNR = 58 + P_in − NF − 10 log₁₀ N.

  1. Each span loses 20 dB and each amplifier restores 20 dB, so the net change per span is 0 dB. Every amplifier therefore receives −20 dBm.
  2. Compute the chain penalty: 10 log₁₀ 8 = 9.0 dB.
  3. Substitute in OSNR = 58 + P_in − NF − 10 log₁₀ N = 58 − 20 − 5 − 9.0 = 24.0 dB.
  4. 10 Gb/s at a bit error rate of 10⁻¹² needs roughly 15-16 dB, so 24.0 dB clears it with about 8 dB margin.

Answer. Every amplifier input is −20 dBm, and the OSNR after 8 spans is 24.0 dB, with 9.0 dB of the loss coming from noise accumulation.

Exam traps

  • An amplifier is not a regenerator: it restores power but not pulse shape, so dispersion and jitter pass straight through.
  • Noise adds up: N identical amplifiers drop OSNR by 10 log₁₀ N, not by a fixed amount per amplifier.
  • Do not swap noise figure and saturation power; NF is about the weak input end, saturation about the strong output end.
  • The EDFA gain band is 1530-1565 nm, not the whole low-loss window from 1260 to 1625 nm.
  • SOA polarization dependence is several dB, not zero; assuming zero gives wrong crosstalk and gain budgets.

6.4 Nonlinear effects: self-phase modulation and solitons

At high power the refractive index of silica rises with the light intensity. A pulse then changes its own phase as it travels, a chirp that spreads its spectrum. This is self-phase modulation. When the fiber has anomalous dispersion, that chirp can cancel the dispersion broadening exactly, and the pulse travels unchanged as a soliton. Only a few universities teach this. It sets the power ceiling of a WDM channel, and it is why solitons were studied for long-haul links that could carry pulses for thousands of kilometres without regeneration.

You need: chromatic dispersion and β₂ (see Unit 2, Signal Degradation in Optical Fibers) and the amplifier power levels of 6.3 Optical amplifiers.

The Kerr effect and the nonlinear length

Silica is not perfectly linear. Its refractive index follows the light intensity: n = n₀ + n₂I. n₀ is the small-signal index, I is the intensity in W/m², and n₂ is the Kerr coefficient, about 2.6×10⁻²⁰ m²/W for silica. The change is tiny, but a long fiber accumulates it.

The intensity is not uniform across the mode. A single number, the effective area A_eff, averages it. The nonlinear coefficient is γ = 2πn₂/(λA_eff). λ is the wavelength in metres, A_eff is in m², and n₂ is in m²/W; this gives γ in W⁻¹m⁻¹. Multiply by 1000 to get W⁻¹km⁻¹: γ = 2πn₂×10³/(λA_eff), typically 1 to 3 W⁻¹km⁻¹. A small A_eff gives a large γ. The nonlinear length L_NL = 1/(γP₀) is then in km; it is the distance over which the nonlinear phase reaches 1 rad, and P₀ is the peak power in W.

Self-phase modulation

A pulse carries its own index change, so it phase-modulates itself. Over an effective length L_eff (close to 1/α for a long lossy fiber), the nonlinear phase is φ_NL = γP₀L_eff. The leading edge of the pulse sees a rising intensity and gets a red shift; the trailing edge sees a falling intensity and gets a blue shift. The pulse acquires a chirp without any modulator. The spectrum broadens while the pulse shape is still narrow.

This is the practical limit on power per WDM channel. Push more power into one channel and its broadened spectrum spills into its neighbours, causing crosstalk. Amplifiers (see 6.3) make this easy to hit.

Solitons and the nonlinear Schrödinger equation

With dispersion the pulse envelope A(z,T) obeys the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLS): ∂A/∂z + (α/2)A + (iβ₂/2)∂²A/∂T² = iγ|A|²A. z is distance, T is time in the pulse frame, α is the loss in nepers per unit length, β₂ is the group-velocity dispersion, and the last term is self-phase modulation. Do not solve it here; read the terms.

Two lengths compete. The dispersion length L_D = T₀²/|β₂| is the distance over which dispersion broadens a pulse of width T₀. The soliton order is N² = L_D/L_NL. When N = 1 the two effects balance exactly. The pulse keeps a sech² shape and travels without changing. Its peak power is P₀ = |β₂|/(γT₀²). The fundamental soliton keeps its shape at all distances while its phase grows with z. The soliton period z₀ = (π/2)L_D is the distance after which a higher-order soliton (N ≥ 2) recovers its original shape. N = 2, 3 pulses breathe: they narrow and split periodically. Dispersion-managed solitons let the dispersion alternate in sign along the link, and this is used in long-haul submarine and terrestrial systems.

Worked example

Question. A fiber has β₂ = −20 ps²/km, γ = 2 W⁻¹km⁻¹ and a pulse parameter T₀ = 10 ps, so the full width at half maximum is 1.763 T₀ = 17.6 ps. (a) Find L_D. (b) Find the peak power of the fundamental soliton. (c) Check that L_NL = L_D. (d) Find the soliton period.

  1. L_D = T₀²/|β₂| = (10 ps)²/(20 ps²/km) = 100/20 = 5 km.
  2. P₀ = |β₂|/(γT₀²) = 20/(2 × 100) = 0.1 W = 100 mW.
  3. L_NL = 1/(γP₀) = 1/(2 × 0.1) = 5 km, so N² = 5/5 = 1.
  4. z₀ = (π/2)L_D = 1.571 × 5 = 7.85 km.

Answer. L_D = 5 km, P₀ = 0.1 W (100 mW), L_NL = 5 km and z₀ = 7.85 km.

Exam traps

  • Using average power in γP₀ instead of the peak power P₀. For a short pulse the average power is much smaller, so the nonlinearity is badly underestimated.
  • Forgetting the sign of β₂. A fundamental soliton needs anomalous dispersion, β₂ < 0; with normal dispersion the chirps add and the pulse spreads faster.
  • Writing z₀ = L_D. The soliton period is (π/2)L_D, about 1.571 L_D.
  • Confusing γ (nonlinear coefficient, W⁻¹km⁻¹) with n₂ (Kerr coefficient, m²/W).

6.5 Optical network concepts and terminology

An optical network is a set of nodes joined by fiber links. A connection that stays in the optical domain from one end to the other is a lightpath, and it holds one wavelength on every link it crosses. Networks exist because one fiber between two points is not enough: many users, many cities and many services must share the same fiber plant. Telephone companies use these ideas in access, metro and core networks; the same vocabulary describes a campus fiber ring or a data-centre link.

You need: <a href='#wdm-concepts-and-components'>6.1 for the wavelength grid and Unit 5 for span loss and power budget.

Nodes, links and lightpaths

A node is a point where traffic is added, dropped, switched or regenerated. A link is the fiber between adjacent nodes. A span is one amplified section inside a link; its loss must fit the power budget (Unit 5). A path is the ordered set of nodes and links between a source and a destination. A channel is one wavelength on one link. A wavelength is the optical carrier taken from the grid in <a href='#wdm-concepts-and-components'>6.1.

A lightpath is an end-to-end connection that uses one wavelength on every link of its path and does not convert to electronics in between. Some syllabi call it a wavelength path or an optical channel. The physical topology is the actual graph of nodes and links. The logical or lightpath topology is the graph of lightpaths seen by the routers. A mesh of fibers can present a ring of lightpaths, and a ring of fibers can present a full mesh.

A transparent network keeps the signal optical end to end. Impairments add up (Unit 2), so reach is limited. An opaque network regenerates at nodes: it receives, reamplifies, reshapes and retimes, then retransmits (3R). Regeneration restores the signal but costs a transponder at each side. A static lightpath is set up once and left; a switched lightpath is set up on demand.

Topologies and the three tiers

Common physical topologies are mesh, ring, star and bus. Mesh has many links and survives cuts, but needs more ports. Ring is a closed loop; two fibers often form a self-healing ring. Star has one central node; bus shares one fiber. The public telecommunication network is built in three tiers. The access network joins homes, offices and mobile towers to the first exchange. Its reach is a few kilometres up to about 20 km; a passive optical network splits one fiber among many users. The metro network joins exchanges inside a city, typically tens of kilometres. It is usually a ring because a city has rights-of-way along roads and a ring gives a simple backup path. The core or long-haul network joins cities and countries, typically hundreds to thousands of kilometres. It is a mesh of cross-connects and amplifiers; a Delhi–Mumbai route is a core link.

The tier sets what matters: access fights cost per user, metro fights fast protection, core fights capacity and reach. The logical topology can be richer than the physical one. Six metro nodes on one ring can still have 15 lightpaths, one per pair, as the example shows.

Node functions, protection and switching

An optical node performs four jobs. Add/drop puts local wavelengths into or out of the fiber (OADM). Cross-connect moves a wavelength from an input fiber to a chosen output fiber (OXC). Regeneration recreates a clean signal when the path is too long. Switching sets or changes the route under control. Simple nodes do only add/drop; larger nodes do all four. The node hardware is in <a href='#wdm-networks-optical-nodes-and-switching'>6.7.

Protection reserves capacity before a failure. In 1+1 the signal is sent on two paths at once and the receiver selects the better; no extra traffic rides the backup. In 1:1 one backup path serves one working path and may carry low-priority extra traffic when healthy. In 1:N one backup serves N working paths. Restoration finds a new route after the failure, so it is slower and needs a control plane. Rings dominate metro because a fiber cut becomes a loop-back at the two nodes next to the cut, and protection can finish in milliseconds.

A broadcast-and-select network sends all wavelengths to all nodes; each receiver filters the one it wants. A wavelength-routed network sends each wavelength through cross-connects along its own path. Static lightpaths are fixed; switched lightpaths are set up and torn down by signalling. The wavelength must be the same on every link unless the node has a wavelength converter.

Worked example

Question. A metro ring has 6 nodes. Each node must send one 10 Gb/s lightpath to each of the other five. (a) How many node pairs are there? (d) What is the total carried working traffic?

  2. With no wavelength reuse, each pair needs its own wavelength on every link: 15 wavelengths.
  3. 1+1 protection sends a copy on a second wavelength for each pair: 2 × 15 = 30 wavelengths. The transponder count also doubles.
  4. Each pair carries 10 Gb/s of working traffic: total working traffic = 15 × 10 Gb/s = 150 Gb/s. The protection copy repeats the same data, so it is not extra working traffic.

Answer. The ring has 15 pairs, 15 working wavelengths, 30 wavelengths with 1+1 protection, and 150 Gb/s of working traffic.

Exam traps

  • Physical topology is not the lightpath topology. A ring of fiber can carry a mesh of lightpaths, so do not copy the fiber graph into the logical graph.
  • Protection doubles both wavelengths and transponders. Count the second copy on every link of the protected path, not just on one link.
  • A lightpath needs the same wavelength on every link unless a converter is present. A free wavelength on one link is not enough.
  • 1+1 and 1:1 look like backups, but 1+1 is always bridged and its backup carries no extra traffic; a 1:1 backup can carry extra traffic when healthy.
  • Link, span, path and channel are not synonyms. A channel is one wavelength on one link; a lightpath is end to end.

6.6 SONET/SDH and synchronous networks

SONET in North America and SDH elsewhere define one synchronous frame that carries many low-rate channels on a single optical line. One master clock and byte interleaving let any channel be added or dropped without splitting the whole signal. The frame repeats every 125 μs, so rates are exact multiples of 51.84 Mb/s. Telephone companies use these networks for voice, leased lines and protected rings; data networks use them as a reliable pipe under packet traffic.

You need: Chapter 6.1 for the wavelength layer and Unit 3 for why electronics cannot simply run at the line rate.

Why one clock and pointers

Early plesiochronous multiplexers gave every channel its own clock. To add one 2 Mb/s telephone line, the operator demultiplexed the high-rate signal down to that line and rebuilt everything. SONET (North America) and SDH (Europe and most of the world) replace this with one synchronous frame. All nodes lock to a primary reference clock, and bytes from many channels are interleaved in fixed positions.

The basic SONET rate is OC-1 = 51.84 Mb/s. Higher rates are exact multiples: OC-n = n × 51.84 Mb/s. SDH uses STM-1 = 155.52 Mb/s = OC-3. The table gives the common steps; the optical line rate is slightly higher than the payload because overhead bytes are added at every layer.

A pointer in the line overhead marks where the synchronous payload envelope (SPE) starts inside the frame. If a node's clock runs slightly fast or slow, the pointer moves by one byte and a stuffing byte is added or removed. The payload therefore floats without a slip. Concatenation, written STS-3c, ties three STS-1 payloads into one container with one pointer; STM-1/VC-4 does the same job in SDH.

Frame, envelope and payload rates

The frame is a rectangle: 90 columns × 9 rows = 810 bytes. It lasts 125 μs, so 8000 frames pass per second. Of the 90 columns, 3 are transport overhead: section and line. The other 87 columns form the SPE; the first column of the SPE is path overhead. The SPE is 87 × 9 = 783 bytes. The pointer bytes H1, H2 and H3 are in the transport overhead, never in the payload.

R = 810 × 8 × 8000 = 51.84 Mb/s

R is the STS-1 line rate; 810 is bytes per frame, 8 bits per byte, and 8000 frames per second.

For STS-1 the SPE rate is 50.112 Mb/s. This envelope includes path overhead and fixed stuff; the user payload is 48.384 Mb/s. An STM-1 carries a VC-4 with 149.76 Mb/s payload capacity. An OC-3 frame is not 810 bytes: it is three interleaved STS-1 frames, 2430 bytes, in the same 125 μs.

Layers, add-drop and rings

SONET/SDH is divided into layers. The section layer runs between regenerators and monitors framing and section error bytes. The line layer runs between add-drop multiplexers, carries the pointer and protection signalling, and monitors line errors. The path layer runs end to end between the nodes that create and read the payload; it monitors path errors and the path trace. Each layer checks its own overhead, so a fault can be located without taking the whole signal apart.

An add-drop multiplexer (ADM) uses byte positions. It drops selected columns, inserts new traffic into the same positions, and passes the rest. It does not need to demultiplex every channel. In a UPSR (unidirectional path switched ring) or SDH SNCP, the signal is bridged on both directions and the receiver selects the better copy. In a BLSR (bidirectional line switched ring) or MS-SPRing, protection capacity is shared and working traffic loops back at a failure. Both are designed to restore within 50 ms.

Jitter is short-term phase variation, above about 10 Hz. Wander is slow phase drift, below about 10 Hz. Pointers absorb wander; interface specifications limit jitter.

Worked example

Question. An STS-1 frame is 810 bytes and repeats 8000 times per second. (a) Find the bit rate. (b) Find the SPE rate for the 783-byte envelope. 6.7 WDM networks, optical nodes and switching

A WDM network carries many lightpaths, each on a wavelength. The nodes must add, drop and cross-connect those wavelengths without turning every signal into electronics. An optical add-drop multiplexer handles a few local wavelengths; an optical cross-connect switches many wavelengths between fiber pairs. A lightpath normally keeps one wavelength from end to end, called the wavelength continuity constraint, and converters are used only when that fails. Routing and wavelength assignment choose the path and the wavelength. These ideas run metro rings, data-centre links and long-haul mesh networks.

You need: the wavelength grid and components from 6.1 and 6.2, the lightpath idea from 6.5, and amplifier basics from 6.3.

Nodes that add, drop and cross-connect

An optical node does more than join fibers. It must add local wavelengths, drop wavelengths for local receivers, and pass the rest through. An optical add-drop multiplexer (OADM) does this for a few wavelengths. A fixed OADM drops one or two wavelengths using filters or gratings and circulators (see 6.2). A reconfigurable OADM uses a wavelength-selective switch (WSS), which sends each wavelength from an input to any chosen output. The WSS changes the path, not the wavelength.

An optical cross-connect (OXC) switches many wavelengths between many fiber pairs, so it can rearrange lightpaths. A metro ring uses OADMs to drop one wavelength for an apartment ISP while other wavelengths pass untouched. A long-haul mesh node uses an OXC.

Cross-connect architectures and switch elements

Two OXC architectures are common. In broadcast-and-select, each input fiber is split into N copies, one copy for each output port. From each copy a filter picks the wanted wavelength, and a gate after the filter passes that wavelength or blocks it. A combiner then sums the one wavelength arriving from each input fiber to build that output port. Loss is splitter loss plus filter and gate loss plus combiner loss. A blocked gate still leaks a little, so the extinction ratio sets the crosstalk. In a WSS fabric, each input WSS routes each wavelength to a chosen output, and output combiners merge them. It filters instead of splitting all power to all outputs, so loss and crosstalk are lower, but cost is higher.

Wavelength continuity and converters

A lightpath keeps one wavelength from source to destination unless a converter changes it. This is the wavelength continuity constraint. For a path with links 1 to k, let S_i be the set of free wavelengths on link i.

S = S₁ ∩ S₂ ∩ … ∩ S_k

S_i is the set of free wavelengths on link i; k is the number of links; S is the set of wavelengths free on every link. The path works without conversion only if S is not empty.

Checking only pairs of links is wrong. Converters relax the rule. Optoelectronic regeneration receives the signal, reshapes and retimes it, and retransmits on a new wavelength; it may be electrical (see 6.3). Cross-gain modulation in a semiconductor optical amplifier (SOA) uses a strong pump at λ1 to modulate the gain seen by a probe at λ2. Four-wave mixing uses two pumps in a nonlinear medium to create an idler at a new wavelength. Converters are placed sparsely because each adds cost, loss and noise.

Routing, protection, bursts and packets

Routing and wavelength assignment (RWA) picks a path and a wavelength for each lightpath. In route-first-then-assign, find a route with Dijkstra, list the free wavelengths common to all its links, and take the first fit in a fixed order. If none is free, try the next route or block the request. Static traffic is known in advance and can be planned; dynamic traffic arrives and leaves, so blocking probability matters. RWA is graph colouring: each lightpath is a vertex, two vertices are joined if their paths share a fiber link, and colours are wavelengths. First-fit is a simple colouring heuristic.

Protection from 6.6 is reused: ring UPSR/BLSR and mesh 1+1 or shared backup. An OXC makes restoration fast by switching wavelengths. Optical burst switching (OBS) sends a control packet ahead on a separate wavelength, then a data burst after an offset time. Nodes configure before the burst arrives; contention can drop a burst. Optical packet switching (OPS) switches each packet, so it needs nanosecond switching, fast header processing and synchronization.

Worked example

Question. A lightpath must run A-B-C-D. Link A-B has λ1 and λ2 free, B-C has λ2 and λ3 free, C-D has λ1 and λ3 free. (a) Can it be set up with wavelength continuity? (b) What happens if node B has a converter? (c) How many switch elements does a full optical cross-connect need with 4 fiber pairs and 8 wavelengths per pair?

  1. Write the free sets: S_AB = {λ1, λ2}, S_BC = {λ2, λ3}, S_CD = {λ1, λ3}.
  2. For continuity, intersect all three: {λ1, λ2} ∩ {λ2, λ3} = {λ2}; then {λ2} ∩ {λ1, λ3} = ∅. The intersection is empty, so no single wavelength is free on all three links. The path is blocked without conversion.
  3. With one converter at B: use λ2 on A-B, because λ2 is free on A-B. At B, convert λ2 to λ3. λ3 is free on B-C and also free on C-D, so the rest of the path runs on λ3 without a second conversion. One converter at B is enough.
  4. Full OXC: incoming wavelengths = 4 fiber pairs × 8 wavelengths per pair = 32. Outgoing ports = 32. A full cross-connect needs one switch element for each input-output pair: 32 × 32 = 1024 elements.

Answer. The path is blocked without conversion (0 common wavelengths). A single converter at B (λ2 on A-B, converted to λ3 for B-C and C-D) makes it work. A full 4×8 OXC needs 1024 switch elements.

  • A WSS switches a wavelength; it does not change it. Only a wavelength converter changes λ1 to λ2.
  • For a full OXC, multiply fiber pairs by wavelengths per pair first, then square. 4×8 gives 32×32, not 4×8 elements.
  • Splitter loss in dB adds: a 1:16 splitter gives 10 log₁₀ 16 ≈ 12 dB, and an output combiner adds another 12 dB.
  • OADM and OXC are not the same: an OADM touches only local add/drop wavelengths, while an OXC can switch every wavelength between every port.

6.8 Network layers, protocols and ATM

Layering splits a network job into levels, so a change at one level leaves the others alone. Each level talks to its peer at the far end and hands data down through a clear interface. Optics is the bottom level and carries bits for every layer above. Asynchronous Transfer Mode (ATM) is a fixed-cell layer-2 design that once promised voice, video and data on one optical pipe. This chapter maps layers, data units and ATM, and shows why IP won.

You need: 6.5 for nodes, links and lightpaths, and 6.6 for SONET as the carrier underneath.

Layers and protocol data units

Only a few universities teach this at this depth. The Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) model has seven layers; each layer adds a header or trailer and passes a protocol data unit (PDU) down.

  • Application: user services such as web and e-mail.
  • Presentation: format, compression and encryption.
  • Session: start and end a dialogue.
  • Transport: end-to-end delivery and flow control.
  • Network: addressing and routing, as IP does.
  • Data link: frames on one link, with error checks.
  • Physical: bits, wavelength, connector and power.

The PDU is a bit at physical, a frame at data link, a packet at network, a segment at transport and a message at application. TCP/IP folds presentation and session into application and uses application, transport, internet and link. Optics touches the physical layer only; a photodiode does not read an IP address.

IP runs over SONET (6.6), over WDM lightpaths, or over Ethernet. Multiprotocol Label Switching (MPLS) puts a short fixed label before each packet; routers switch on the label, not the full IP header.

ATM cells, layers and survival

ATM sends all information in a cell: 53 bytes, a 5-byte header plus a 48-byte payload. The header has a virtual path identifier (VPI) and virtual channel identifier (VCI) to label the connection, a payload type field, a cell loss priority bit and a header error check byte. Short fixed cells suit voice because a sample cannot wait for a long packet. They also let hardware switch fast, with one lookup per cell.

ATM has three layers. Physical moves bits over SONET or fiber. ATM adds and removes the 5-byte header and switches by VPI/VCI. The ATM adaptation layer (AAL) breaks data into cells and rebuilds it. AAL1 carries constant-bit-rate voice and circuit emulation. AAL2 carries compressed voice. AAL5 carries data; it adds an 8-byte trailer, then pads to a multiple of 48 bytes.

Service classes give quality of service (QoS). Constant bit rate (CBR): fixed rate, tight delay, like a call. Real-time variable bit rate (VBR-rt): variable rate, real-time delay. Non-real-time variable bit rate (VBR-nrt): variable rate, no real-time delay. Available bit rate (ABR): feedback shares spare bandwidth. Unspecified bit rate (UBR): no guarantee, like a file download.

ATM lost to IP and Ethernet. IP was already on every computer, Ethernet was cheap and kept getting faster, and ATM paid a cell tax: 5 header bytes per 48 payload bytes. IP over WDM now carries most long-haul traffic. ATM remains in some legacy access and telephone backbones; its QoS classes and label switching ideas survived. Generalized MPLS (GMPLS) extends labels to wavelengths. The control plane is signalling software that finds a path and sets cross-connects; the data plane is the light.

Worked example

Question. An IP packet of 1500 bytes travels over AAL5, which adds an 8-byte trailer, with a 48-byte cell payload. (a) How many cells are needed? (b) How many bytes go on the link? (c) What is the efficiency?

  1. Payload with trailer = 1500 + 8 = 1508 bytes.
  2. Cells = ceil(1508 ÷ 48) = ceil(31.4167) = 32 cells.
  3. Link bytes = 32 × 53 = 1696 bytes.
  4. Efficiency = 1500 ÷ 1696 = 0.8844 = 88.4 percent.

Answer. 32 cells, 1696 bytes on the link, efficiency 88.4 percent.

Exam traps

  • Count the 5-byte header: a 48-byte payload costs 53 bytes, not 48.
  • Add the AAL5 8-byte trailer and pad before dividing by 48.
  • Do not confuse VPI with VCI: VPI is the path, VCI is the channel inside it.
  • AAL1 is constant bit rate voice; AAL5 is data. Swapping them is a common slip.
  • UBR gives no QoS guarantee; CBR does.

Takeaways

  1. WDM raises fiber capacity by adding wavelengths, not by making the electronics faster, so one fiber behaves like many parallel channels.
  2. Passive components — couplers, isolators, circulators and gratings — set the insertion loss, crosstalk and reflection performance of a WDM link.
  3. An optical amplifier boosts light directly by stimulated emission, replacing the receiver–electronics–transmitter chain at every span.
  4. EDFA dominates 1550 nm links with high gain, low noise figure and no polarisation dependence, while SOA is smaller, cheaper and works near 1300 nm.
  5. At high power the Kerr effect makes the pulse chirp itself through SPM; balanced against GVD, this produces a soliton that keeps its shape.
  6. A lightpath is one wavelength held end to end, and finding wavelengths for many lightpaths is the central design problem of a WDM network.
  7. SONET/SDH multiplexes low-rate tributaries into a frame locked to one master clock, so a single channel can be added or dropped cleanly.
  8. WDM nodes combine add-drop, cross-connection and sometimes wavelength conversion, and the choice of converter placement decides network blocking.
  9. Layering keeps transmission, framing and user services independent, which is why SONET/SDH, ATM and IP can share the same fiber.

Apply A WDM link carries 40 channels spaced 100 GHz apart around 1550 nm. Find the channel spacing in nm and the total usable bandwidth in THz. 10 marks

Apply An EDFA gives 25 dB gain for an input of −20 dBm. Find the output power in dBm and mW. If the input signal-to-noise ratio is 40 dB and the output is 35 dB, find the noise figure. 10 marks

P_out(dBm) = P_in(dBm) + G(dB) = −20 + 25 = +5 dBm; P_out = 10^(0.5) mW ≈ 3.16 mW; NF = SNR_in − SNR_out = 40 − 35 = 5 dB; State that NF = 10 log₁₀ F, so F ≈ 3.16; Comment: amplifier adds spontaneous emission noise.

Glossary

Anomalous dispersion
The regime where β₂ < 0 and longer wavelengths travel faster; required for bright solitons.
Blocking probability
The chance that a requested lightpath cannot be set up because no wavelength is free.
Chirp
A change of instantaneous frequency across a pulse, so different parts of the pulse have different wavelengths.
Circulator
A multi-port passive device that passes light in one rotational direction only, e.g. port 1 to 2 to 3.
Cross-gain modulation
One channel's power changes the SOA gain seen by other channels, causing crosstalk.
Crosstalk
Leakage of one WDM channel into a neighbouring channel, measured in dB.
Directivity
The fraction of input power that leaks backwards to the input-side port of a coupler.
Dispersion length
L_D = T₀²/|β₂|, the distance over which GVD broadens a pulse noticeably.
Excess loss
The part of a coupler's loss not explained by the intended splitting ratio.
Gain bandwidth
The range of wavelengths over which an amplifier gives useful gain.
Insertion loss
The drop in optical power caused by putting a component into the link, in dB.
Nonlinear length
L_NL = 1/(γP₀), the distance over which SPM becomes significant.
Nonlinear Schrödinger equation
The propagation equation combining GVD and SPM that governs pulse shape in a fiber.
Optical cross-connect (OXC)
A node that switches wavelengths from any input fiber to any output fiber.
Plesiochronous
Nearly but not exactly synchronous, so bit stuffing is needed before multiplexing, as in the PDH hierarchy.
Polarisation-dependent gain
Gain that differs for different input polarisations, a drawback of ordinary SOAs.
Population inversion
A state where more ions or carriers sit in the upper energy level than the lower one, so stimulated emission exceeds absorption.
Protocol layer
One level of a layered model that performs a defined job and uses the services of the level below.
Pump laser
A laser at 980 nm or 1480 nm whose power excites the erbium ions in an EDFA.
Saturation power
The output power at which amplifier gain falls 3 dB below its small-signal value.
Section, line and path overhead
SONET/SDH overhead bytes used for framing, error checking and management at each level.
Spontaneous emission
Random photon emission that seeds amplifier noise and limits the noise figure.
Synchronous multiplexing
Packing tributary channels into fixed positions of a frame locked to one master clock.
Thin-film filter
Dielectric multilayer filter that passes one wavelength band and reflects the rest, used in add-drop modules.
Virtual circuit
A connection whose route is fixed at setup time and identified by a short label in each cell.
Wavelength converter
A device that shifts a lightpath from one wavelength to another, removing the continuity constraint.
