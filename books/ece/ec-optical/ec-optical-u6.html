6.1 WDM concepts and components

Wavelength division multiplexing (WDM) sends several light signals through one fiber at the same time, each on its own wavelength. One laser and its modulator give only a few Gb/s, while the 1550 nm window holds several THz of usable bandwidth. WDM uses that window as many separate channels, so capacity grows with the number of channels N, not with electronic speed. Telephone backbones, cable TV feeds and data-centre links use it.

You need: single-mode fiber and its low-loss 1550 nm window (see Single-mode and multimode fibers; cutoff wavelength) and the laser transmitter (see Semiconductor laser diodes).

Why one fiber carries many colours

WDM gives each signal a different wavelength λ. One wavelength with its own data is called a channel, and all channels share one fiber. If there are N channels and each carries B bit/s, the total capacity is C = N × B, as long as the amplifiers, filters and receivers allow it.

C = N × B

C is the total bit rate in bit/s; N is the number of wavelength channels; B is the bit rate of one channel in bit/s.

Channel spacing can be given in frequency (GHz) or in wavelength (nm). Since f = c/λ, a small step in frequency Δf matches a small step in wavelength Δλ:

Δλ = λ²Δf / c and Δf = cΔλ / λ²

Δλ is the spacing in wavelength (m); λ is the operating wavelength (m); Δf is the spacing in frequency (Hz); c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s is the speed of light in vacuum.

At 1550 nm a 100 GHz step is 0.80 nm. The same 100 GHz step is 0.57 nm at 1310 nm and 0.86 nm at 1610 nm, because Δλ grows as λ². The ITU-T DWDM grid (G.694.1) anchors channels at 193.1 THz and steps in 100 GHz, 50 GHz or finer slots; dense WDM (DWDM) uses these tight spacings with temperature-controlled lasers. Coarse WDM (CWDM, G.694.2) uses 20 nm spacing: 18 channels from 1270 nm to 1610 nm, uncooled lasers and short reach.

Mux and demux: sorting colours

A multiplexer combines λ1…λN into one fiber; a demultiplexer separates them. Some syllabi say “wavelength multiplexer” or “WDM mux/demux” for the same pair. Five common parts do this job.

  • Prism: the refraction angle changes with wavelength; simple, few channels.
  • Diffraction grating: periodic grooves send each wavelength to a different angle; good for many channels.
  • Thin-film dielectric filter: a stack of layers passes one band and reflects the rest; used in mux/demux and add/drop.
  • Arrayed waveguide grating (AWG): waveguides with fixed path differences interfere so each wavelength focuses on a different output; common in integrated optics.
  • Fiber Bragg grating (FBG): a periodic index pattern in the fiber reflects one wavelength and passes the rest.

Four numbers describe a mux or demux. Insertion loss is the drop in dB from the input to the wanted output; lower is better. Adjacent-channel crosstalk is 10 log₁₀(leaked power from the next channel ÷ wanted channel power); the leak is smaller than the wanted power, so the value is negative, and more negative is better. Channel uniformity is the spread in dB between the strongest and weakest output. Passband width is the range of wavelengths over which the loss stays low. The couplers and gratings inside these parts are in Passive devices.

Reading a WDM specification

The comb is the set of channel frequencies. From the first channel centre to the last it is (N − 1)Δf wide, not NΔf, because N channels have N − 1 gaps. When a question asks how many channels fit in a band, divide the band by the spacing: each channel takes one slot of width Δf.

Capacity grows with N, but N is limited by the amplifier gain band (the erbium C-band is about 1530 to 1565 nm, see Optical amplifiers), laser wavelength drift, filter passband and crosstalk. A mux with 1.5 dB insertion loss removes 1.5 dB from every channel, and the demux at the far end removes its own share. The passband must cover laser drift and modulation chirp, and the crosstalk sets how close the channels can sit. In a network each channel becomes a lightpath, see Optical network concepts.

Worked example

Question. A DWDM system uses 40 channels on the ITU grid near 1550 nm, spaced 100 GHz apart, each carrying 10 Gb/s. (a) Find the total capacity. (b) Find the spacing in nanometres. (c) Find the width of the comb from the first to the last channel centre. (d) The erbium C-band runs from 1530 nm to 1565 nm. How many 100 GHz slots does it hold, and what capacity is that at 10 Gb/s per channel?

  1. N = 40 channels and B = 10 Gb/s per channel. Total capacity C = N × B = 40 × 10 = 400 Gb/s.
  2. λ = 1550 nm = 1550 × 10⁻⁹ m, Δf = 100 GHz = 10¹¹ Hz, c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. λ² = (1550 × 10⁻⁹)² = 2.4025 × 10⁻¹² m².
  3. Δλ = λ²Δf/c = 2.4025 × 10⁻¹² × 10¹¹ ÷ (3 × 10⁸) = 0.24025 ÷ (3 × 10⁸) = 8.008 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.8008 nm.
  4. The comb has N − 1 = 39 gaps: width = 39 × 100 GHz = 3.9 THz, or 39 × 0.8008 = 31.23 nm.
  5. Band edges in frequency: f = c/λ. At 1530 nm, f = 3 × 10⁸ ÷ (1530 × 10⁻⁹) = 196.08 THz; at 1565 nm, f = 191.69 THz. Band width = 196.08 − 191.69 = 4.385 THz.
  6. Slots = 4.385 THz ÷ 0.1 THz = 43.85, so 43 whole slots. Capacity = 43 × 10 = 430 Gb/s.
  7. Check: 35 nm ÷ 0.80 nm per slot ≈ 43.7, which also gives 43 whole slots.

Answer. (a) 400 Gb/s. (b) 0.8008 nm. (c) 3.9 THz, or 31.23 nm. (d) The C-band is 4.385 THz wide and holds 43 slots of 100 GHz, which is 430 Gb/s at 10 Gb/s per channel.

Exam traps

  • 100 GHz is not always 0.8 nm: Δλ = λ²Δf/c, so the same frequency spacing is 0.57 nm at 1310 nm and 0.86 nm at 1610 nm.
  • Comb width uses N − 1 gaps; the number of channels in a band uses band ÷ spacing. Mixing the two gives answers off by one channel.
  • Total capacity is N × B in each direction. Multiply by 2 only when the question counts both directions of a duplex pair.
  • CWDM is 20 nm spacing over 1270 to 1610 nm; DWDM is 100 GHz, 50 GHz or finer. Do not mix the names.
  • Crosstalk is a negative dB ratio, so −30 dB is better than −20 dB. Insertion loss is a positive dB number.

6.2 Passive devices: couplers, isolators and gratings

A coupler splits light into two or more arms, an isolator passes light one way only, a circulator sends light round a fixed 1→2→3 route, and a fiber Bragg grating reflects one wavelength and passes the rest. None needs electrical power. They set how much power a WDM node loses and how much one channel leaks into its neighbour.

You need: the WDM channel and its spacing (WDM concepts) and decibels of power ratio.

Couplers: sharing light with no power supply

A coupler takes light in at one port and shares it among two or more output ports; run backwards, it combines several inputs into one output. The fused biconical taper (FBT) coupler is made by twisting two single-mode fibers together, fusing the joint and stretching it into a taper. Over the fused length the cores lie so close that light leaks from one core into the other. A planar coupler does the same with waveguides on a silica chip.

Name the ports of a 2×2 coupler this way: ports 1 and 2 on the input side, ports 3 and 4 on the output side. Light enters at port 1 with power Pin; P₃ and P₄ come out, and a tiny P₂ comes back out of port 2. Shapes include the 1×2 splitter, the 2×2 coupler and the N×N star that shares every input among all N outputs. Splitting costs power: an ideal 1×N splitter gives each arm Pin/N, a splitting loss of 10 log₁₀ N dB (3.01 dB for N = 2, 15.05 dB for N = 32).

  • Coupling ratio CR = P₃/(P₃ + P₄) × 100 percent: the share of the total output that leaves port 3.
  • Excess loss = 10 log₁₀(Pin/(P₃ + P₄)) dB: power lost inside the coupler, found by adding all outputs.
  • Insertion loss (1→3) = 10 log₁₀(Pin/P₃) dB: loss from the input to one chosen output; it contains both the split and the excess loss.
  • Directivity = 10 log₁₀(Pin/P₂) dB: how little light comes back out of the other input-side port. A good fused coupler gives 40 dB or more, which keeps echoes out of the link.

Put all powers in the same unit (mW or µW); the ratio inside the log has no unit.

Isolators and circulators: controlling direction

A laser diode is disturbed by light reflected back into it: the returning wave pulls its wavelength and adds noise (see Semiconductor laser diodes). An isolator is the one-way valve that stops it.

An isolator is a polarizer, a Faraday rotator and a second polarizer set at 45° to the first. The Faraday rotator, a magneto-optic crystal in a magnetic field, turns the plane of polarization by 45°, so forward light passes the second polarizer. The key fact: the crystal turns the plane in the same sense, viewed from the lab, whichever way the light travels. Light reflected back from the fiber passes the 45° polarizer, turns another 45°, arrives at 90° to the first polarizer and is blocked.

Isolator parameterTypical value
Insertion loss (forward)0.5 to 1 dB
Isolation (reverse)30 to 60 dB (one or two stages)

A circulator is the three-port version: light in at port 1 leaves by port 2, in at port 2 leaves by port 3, and in at port 3 leaves by port 1. Because a circulator routes light instead of splitting it, no 3 dB is thrown away. That makes it the natural partner of a Bragg grating.

Fiber Bragg gratings and the add-drop node

A fiber Bragg grating is a short piece of fiber whose core refractive index rises and falls periodically along the axis with pitch Λ. The forward wave reflects weakly at every period. The reflections add in phase for one wavelength only, the Bragg wavelength.

λB = 2 neff Λ

λB is the reflected Bragg wavelength (m); neff is the effective index of the guided mode, about 1.45 in silica; Λ is the grating pitch (m). The factor 2 is the round trip: the reflection from one period must travel one pitch forward and one pitch back to stay in step.

All other wavelengths pass almost untouched, so in transmission the grating is a band-stop (notch) filter, with a stop band from a fraction of a nanometre to a few nanometres wide. A sharp start and stop of the index pattern produce side lobes: small unwanted reflection peaks just outside the main band, sitting on the neighbouring WDM channels. Apodization, tapering the modulation strength gradually to zero at both ends, removes them. Gratings also serve as gain-flattening filters and, with a chirped pitch, as dispersion compensators.

With two circulators the grating becomes an optical add-drop multiplexer. Channels λ1 to λ4 enter port 1 of circulator C1 and leave at its port 2 into the grating. The grating reflects λ2 back into C1 port 2, and C1 sends it out of port 3: this is the drop. λ1, λ3 and λ4 pass through the grating into port 2 of circulator C2 and leave at C2 port 3, the output line. A new signal on λ2 enters C2 port 1, leaves at C2 port 2 towards the grating, is reflected back into C2 port 2, and joins the through channels at C2 port 3: this is the add.

Worked example

Question. A fused 2×2 coupler receives Pin = 250 µW at port 1. Port 3 gives 118 µW, port 4 gives 112 µW, and 4.0 nW comes back out of port 2. (a) Find the coupling ratio. (b) Find the excess loss. (c) Find the insertion loss from port 1 to port 3 and to port 4. (d) Find the directivity. (e) Find the grating pitch that reflects 1550 nm in a fiber with neff = 1.45.

  1. (a) Total output P₃ + P₄ = 118 + 112 = 230 µW. CR = 118/230 × 100 = 51.3 percent, so the coupler is close to 50:50.
  2. (b) Excess loss = 10 log₁₀(250/230) = 10 log₁₀(1.0870) = 10 × 0.0362 = 0.36 dB.
  3. (c) Insertion loss 1→3 = 10 log₁₀(250/118) = 10 log₁₀(2.1186) = 3.26 dB. Insertion loss 1→4 = 10 log₁₀(250/112) = 10 log₁₀(2.2321) = 3.49 dB.
  4. (d) Directivity = 10 log₁₀(Pin/P₂) = 10 log₁₀(250 × 10⁻⁶ ÷ 4.0 × 10⁻⁹) = 10 log₁₀(62 500) = 47.96 dB.
  5. (e) From λB = 2 neff Λ: Λ = λB/(2 neff) = 1550/(2 × 1.45) = 1550/2.90 = 534.5 nm.
  6. Check: the insertion loss 1→3 splits into the share part 10 log₁₀(230/118) = 2.90 dB plus the excess loss 0.36 dB, and 2.90 + 0.36 = 3.26 dB.

Answer. CR = 51.3 percent, excess loss = 0.36 dB, insertion loss = 3.26 dB (to port 3) and 3.49 dB (to port 4), directivity = 47.96 dB, grating pitch = 534.5 nm.

Exam traps

  • Dropping the factor 2 in λB = 2 neff Λ. Without it the pitch comes out double (1069 nm instead of 534.5 nm for 1550 nm).
  • Treating excess loss and insertion loss as the same number. Excess loss compares the total output with the input; insertion loss compares one port with the input.
  • Measuring the coupling ratio against the input power instead of the total output. CR is a share of what comes out: 118/230, not 118/250.
  • Swapping isolation and insertion loss on an isolator. Isolation is the large reverse attenuation (30 to 60 dB); insertion loss is the small forward penalty (0.5 to 1 dB).
  • Drawing an add-drop node with one circulator and feeding the add channel into its port 3. Light into port 3 goes to port 1, back towards the source; the add path needs the second circulator after the grating.

Analyze Explain how two circulators and a Bragg grating form an add-drop multiplexer, and why a 2×2 coupler is a poorer choice. 6 marks

Channels enter C1 port 1 and leave at port 2 into the grating; the grating reflects λ2, which returns to C1 port 2 and is dropped at port 3. The other channels pass the grating into C2 port 2 and leave at C2 port 3. The added λ2 enters C2 port 1, goes out of port 2, is reflected by the grating and leaves at C2 port 3 with the through channels. A 2×2 coupler splits every channel, losing at least 3 dB per pass, and sends part of every channel into the drop port, which adds crosstalk.

6.3 Optical amplifiers: SOA and EDFA

An optical amplifier boosts light directly, without converting it to electrons. A pump feeds energy into a gain medium, and signal photons trigger stimulated emission that copies them. Before amplifiers, every span ended at a receiver and a fresh transmitter; now one amplifier per span lifts every WDM channel at once. Only a few syllabi teach amplifiers in this depth. EDFAs serve 1550 nm long-haul links; SOAs serve shorter and cheaper links.

You need: stimulated emission and population inversion (see Absorption, spontaneous and stimulated emission), dBm power units, and the WDM coupler and isolator (Passive devices).

Why amplify, and the five numbers

Fiber at 1550 nm loses about 0.2 dB per km, so an 80 km span loses 16 dB and eight spans 128 dB, far beyond any power budget. An amplifier after each span restores the power, and the same light continues.

G = 10 log₁₀(Pout/Pin) dB

G is the gain; Pout and Pin are the signal powers out and in, in the same unit. In dBm, Pout(dBm) = Pin(dBm) + G(dB); a 20 dB gain means Pout = 100 × Pin.

Five numbers appear on every amplifier data sheet.

  • Gain: 20 to 40 dB; it sets how much span loss one amplifier can pay back.
  • Gain bandwidth: the wavelength range where the gain stays within a stated drop; the EDFA C-band is about 35 nm wide.
  • Saturation output power: the output at which the gain has fallen by 3 dB from its small-signal value.
  • Noise figure NF = 10 log₁₀(SNRin/SNRout): the SNR lost in the amplifier; about 3 dB is the limit for a high-gain amplifier, and lower is better.
  • Polarization dependence: the gain difference between input polarizations, in dB; large in plain SOAs and small in EDFAs.

SOA: a semiconductor gain chip

A semiconductor optical amplifier (SOA) is a laser diode chip driven by current but stopped from lasing. Anti-reflection coatings on both facets remove the feedback, so light passes once through the active region and is amplified by stimulated emission. Different material compositions cover 1310 nm and 1550 nm. Gain is 20 to 30 dB and the noise figure is usually 6 to 9 dB.

Two weaknesses limit its use. The gain depends on polarization, often by several dB, because the active region is thin and flat. And the carrier lifetime is below a nanosecond, so a strong channel modulates the gain seen by its neighbours: cross-gain modulation, a crosstalk between WDM channels (WDM concepts). The same fast response makes SOAs useful as gates and wavelength converters, and as cheap boosters in short links.

EDFA: erbium-doped fiber amplifier

An EDFA is a length of silica fiber, typically 10 to 30 m, whose core is doped with Er³⁺ ions. A 980 nm pump photon lifts an ion from the ground level 1 to level 3, which decays within microseconds to the metastable level 2 (lifetime about 10 ms). A signal photon near 1550 nm then stimulates the 2 → 1 transition and gains an identical copy. A 1480 nm pump lifts ions straight into the top of level 2. The 980 nm pump gives the more complete inversion and so the lower noise figure; the 1480 nm pump gives more output power per pump watt.

The parts in signal order are an input isolator, a WDM coupler that merges pump and signal, the doped fiber, an output isolator and a gain-flattening filter. The isolators stop reflections and backward amplified spontaneous emission (ASE) from turning the amplifier into a laser. The C-band runs from 1530 to 1565 nm with 20 to 40 dB gain and NF of 4 to 6 dB; a longer doped fiber gives gain in the L-band, 1570 to 1610 nm.

Energy conservation caps the output. Each pump photon can make at most one signal photon, and a signal photon carries less energy (E = hc/λ). So the added signal power can be at most (λp/λs) times the pump power:

PCE = (Ps,out − Ps,in) / Pp

PCE is the power conversion efficiency; Ps,out and Ps,in are the signal powers out and in; Pp is the pump power. The limit is PCE ≤ λp/λs, which is 980/1550 = 0.632 for a 980 nm pump. The quantum conversion efficiency QCE = PCE × λs/λp is the share of pump photons turned into signal photons, at most 1.

Each amplifier in a chain adds ASE noise. For N identical spans the optical signal-to-noise ratio in 0.1 nm at 1550 nm is about OSNR(dB) = 58 + Pin(dBm) − NF(dB) − 10 log₁₀ N, where Pin is the power of one channel at each amplifier input and 58 dB comes from the photon energy and the 0.1 nm bandwidth. Noise adds up, so OSNR falls by 10 log₁₀ N: eight amplifiers cost 9.03 dB. Raman amplification, which pumps the transmission fiber itself, is covered with Nonlinear scattering: stimulated Brillouin and Raman.

Worked example

Question. A 10 Gb/s channel is launched into a chain of 8 spans. Each span loses 20 dB and is followed by an EDFA with 20 dB gain and a noise figure of 5 dB. The power into the first amplifier is −20 dBm. (a) Show that every amplifier sees the same input. (b) Estimate the OSNR after the last amplifier with OSNR = 58 + Pin − NF − 10 log₁₀ N.

  1. Each span takes away 20 dB and each amplifier gives back 20 dB, so the net change per span is 0 dB. Amplifier 1 sees −20 dBm and puts out 0 dBm; span 2 brings it back to −20 dBm, and so on. Every amplifier input is −20 dBm.
  2. Chain penalty: 10 log₁₀ 8 = 9.03 dB.
  3. OSNR = 58 + (−20) − 5 − 9.03 = 23.97 dB, about 24.0 dB.
  4. Check: one amplifier alone would give 58 − 20 − 5 = 33 dB, and 33 − 9.03 = 23.97 dB.

Answer. Every amplifier input is −20 dBm, and the OSNR after 8 spans is about 24.0 dB, of which 9.03 dB is lost to noise accumulation.

Worked example 2

Question. An EDFA is pumped with 60 mW at 980 nm. A 1550 nm signal of 0.5 mW goes in and 25 mW comes out. Find (a) the gain in dB, (b) the power conversion efficiency, (c) the quantum conversion efficiency, and (d) the largest output and gain energy conservation allows with this pump.

  1. (a) G = 10 log₁₀(25/0.5) = 10 log₁₀ 50 = 16.99 dB.
  2. (b) PCE = (25 − 0.5)/60 = 24.5/60 = 0.408, or 40.8 percent.
  3. (c) QCE = PCE × λs/λp = 0.408 × 1550/980 = 0.408 × 1.582 = 0.646, so 64.6 percent of the pump photons become signal photons.
  4. (d) Limit: Ps,out ≤ Ps,in + (λp/λs)Pp = 0.5 + 0.632 × 60 = 0.5 + 37.94 = 38.44 mW. Maximum gain = 10 log₁₀(38.44/0.5) = 10 log₁₀ 76.9 = 18.86 dB.
  5. Check: the measured 25 mW is below the 38.44 mW limit, and the QCE is below 1, as it must be.

Answer. G = 16.99 dB, PCE = 40.8 percent, QCE = 64.6 percent; at most 38.44 mW out, a gain of 18.86 dB.

Exam traps

  • An amplifier is not a regenerator: it restores power but not pulse shape, so dispersion and jitter pass straight through.
  • Noise adds up: N identical amplifiers lower the OSNR by 10 log₁₀ N, so 8 amplifiers cost 9.03 dB, not 8 times one amplifier’s penalty.
  • PCE cannot reach 100 percent: with a 980 nm pump and a 1550 nm signal the ceiling is 980/1550 = 63.2 percent, because each signal photon carries less energy than a pump photon.
  • The EDFA C-band is 1530 to 1565 nm, not the whole low-loss window from 1260 to 1625 nm.
  • An SOA facet without its anti-reflection coating gives feedback, and the chip lases instead of amplifying.

6.4 Nonlinear effects: self-phase modulation and solitons

At high power the refractive index of silica rises with the light intensity. A pulse then changes its own phase as it travels, a chirp that widens its spectrum: self-phase modulation (SPM). When the fiber has anomalous dispersion, that chirp can cancel the dispersion broadening exactly, and the pulse travels unchanged as a soliton. Only a few syllabi teach this topic. SPM sets the power ceiling of a WDM channel, and solitons show how nonlinearity and dispersion can be made to work together.

You need: chromatic dispersion and the group-velocity dispersion β₂ (see Chromatic dispersion: material and waveguide) and fiber attenuation in dB/km (see Attenuation in optical fibers).

The Kerr effect and the nonlinear length

Silica is not perfectly linear. Its refractive index follows the light intensity: n = n₀ + n₂I. Here n₀ is the low-power index, I is the intensity in W/m², and n₂ is the Kerr coefficient, about 2.6 × 10⁻²⁰ m²/W for silica. The change is tiny, but a long fiber adds it up.

The intensity is not uniform across the mode, so one number, the effective area Aeff, stands for the mode size (about 80 µm² in standard single-mode fiber). The nonlinear coefficient is γ = 2πn₂/(λAeff). With λ in m, Aeff in m² and n₂ in m²/W, γ comes out in W⁻¹m⁻¹; multiply by 1000 for W⁻¹km⁻¹, the usual unit, typically 1 to 3 W⁻¹km⁻¹. The nonlinear length LNL = 1/(γP₀) is the distance over which the nonlinear phase reaches 1 rad for a peak power P₀.

A lossy fiber weakens the light as it goes, so the nonlinearity acts over an effective length Leff = (1 − e−αL)/α, with α the loss in 1/km (α = loss in dB/km ÷ 4.343). For a long span Leff is close to 1/α, about 21.7 km at 0.2 dB/km.

Self-phase modulation

A pulse carries its own index change, so it phase-modulates itself. The nonlinear phase is φNL = γP(T)Leff, largest at the pulse peak. A changing phase means a shifted frequency, δω = −dφNL/dT. On the leading edge the power rises (dP/dT > 0), so δω is negative: a red shift. On the trailing edge the power falls, so the shift is to the blue. The pulse acquires a chirp without any modulator, and its spectrum widens while its shape is unchanged at first.

This is the practical limit on power per WDM channel. Push more power into one channel and its widened spectrum spills towards its neighbours, and with dispersion the chirp turns into distortion. EDFAs (Optical amplifiers) make these powers easy to reach in every span.

Solitons and the nonlinear Schrödinger equation

With dispersion the pulse envelope A(z,T) obeys the nonlinear Schrödinger equation (NLS): ∂A/∂z + (α/2)A + (iβ₂/2)∂²A/∂T² = iγ|A|²A. Here z is distance, T is time in a frame moving with the pulse, α is the loss, β₂ is the group-velocity dispersion and the right side is SPM. Read the terms rather than solving it: loss, dispersion and nonlinearity act together.

Two lengths compete. The dispersion length LD = T₀²/|β₂| is the distance over which dispersion broadens a pulse of width parameter T₀. The soliton order N is set by N² = LD/LNL. In anomalous dispersion (β₂ < 0, where higher frequencies travel faster) the blue trailing edge catches up and the red leading edge falls back, so the SPM chirp undoes the dispersion. When N = 1 the balance is exact: the fundamental soliton has a sech-shaped amplitude (sech² intensity), FWHM = 1.763 T₀, peak power P₀ = |β₂|/(γT₀²), and keeps its shape at every distance while its phase grows steadily. Higher-order solitons (N = 2, 3, …) narrow, split and recover their shape after the soliton period z₀ = (π/2)LD. Soliton systems were demonstrated over thousands of kilometres with amplifiers, but installed long-haul links use dispersion management and coherent detection instead.

Worked example

Question. A fiber has β₂ = −20 ps²/km and γ = 2 W⁻¹km⁻¹. A sech pulse has T₀ = 10 ps, so its FWHM is 1.763 T₀ = 17.6 ps. (a) Find LD. (b) Find the peak power of the fundamental soliton. (c) Check that LNL = LD. (d) Find the soliton period.

  1. LD = T₀²/|β₂| = (10 ps)²/(20 ps²/km) = 100/20 = 5 km.
  2. P₀ = |β₂|/(γT₀²) = 20/(2 × 100) = 0.1 W = 100 mW. The units: ps²/km ÷ (W⁻¹km⁻¹ × ps²) = W.
  3. LNL = 1/(γP₀) = 1/(2 × 0.1) = 5 km, so N² = 5/5 = 1, the fundamental soliton.
  4. z₀ = (π/2)LD = 1.571 × 5 = 7.85 km.
  5. Check: without the nonlinearity this sech pulse would be 1.54 times wider after 5 km and 2.14 times wider after 10 km (Figure 6.4.1); the soliton stays at 17.6 ps.

Answer. LD = 5 km, P₀ = 0.1 W (100 mW), LNL = 5 km so N = 1, and z₀ = 7.85 km.

Exam traps

  • Using average power in γP₀ instead of the peak power P₀. For a short pulse the average power is much smaller, so the nonlinearity is badly underestimated.
  • Forgetting the sign of β₂. A bright soliton needs anomalous dispersion, β₂ < 0; with normal dispersion the SPM chirp and the dispersion chirp add and the pulse spreads faster.
  • Writing z₀ = LD. The soliton period is (π/2)LD, about 1.571 LD, and it is the period of the higher-order solitons; the fundamental soliton never changes shape.
  • Putting λ in nm or Aeff in µm² into γ = 2πn₂/(λAeff) without converting. Use metres, then multiply W⁻¹m⁻¹ by 1000 for W⁻¹km⁻¹.

6.5 Optical network concepts and terminology

An optical network is a set of nodes joined by fiber links. A connection that stays in the optical domain from one end to the other is a lightpath, and it holds one wavelength on every link it crosses. Networks exist because one fiber between two points is not enough: many users, many cities and many services must share the same fiber plant. Telephone companies use these ideas in access, metro and core networks; the same words describe a campus fiber ring or a data-centre link.

You need: WDM channels on one fiber (WDM concepts) and the point-to-point link and its power budget (see Link power budget).

Nodes, links and lightpaths

A node is a point where traffic is added, dropped, switched or regenerated. A link is the fiber between two adjacent nodes. In an amplified link, a span is the fiber between two amplifiers, so one long link can hold several spans; some books use span and link for the same thing on short unamplified routes. A path is the ordered list of nodes and links from a source to a destination. A channel is one wavelength on one link.

A lightpath is an end-to-end connection that uses one wavelength on every link of its path and is not converted to electronics in between; some books call it a wavelength path. The physical topology is the actual graph of nodes and fibers. The logical (lightpath) topology is the graph of lightpaths that the routers see. A ring of fibers can carry a full mesh of lightpaths, as the worked example shows.

A transparent network keeps the signal optical end to end; impairments add up along the path (see Signal degradation in optical fibers), so the reach is limited. An opaque network regenerates at nodes: it receives, reamplifies, reshapes and retimes the signal, then retransmits it (3R). Regeneration restores the signal but costs a transponder pair at each node.

Topologies, tiers and transmission modes

Common physical topologies are mesh, ring, star and bus. A mesh has many links and survives cuts but needs more ports. A ring is a closed loop; two fibers in opposite directions form a self-healing ring. A star has one central node, and a bus shares one fiber.

The public telecommunication network has three tiers. The access network joins homes, offices and mobile towers to the first exchange, over a few kilometres up to about 20 km; a passive optical network splits one fiber among many users. The metro network joins the exchanges of a city over tens of kilometres, usually as a ring, because ducts follow the roads and a ring gives a second route. The core (long-haul) network joins cities and countries over hundreds to thousands of kilometres as a mesh of cross-connects and amplifiers; a Delhi–Mumbai route is a core link. Access fights cost per user, metro fights fast protection, and core fights capacity and reach.

Traffic crosses these networks in two transmission modes. In circuit switching a fixed path and capacity are set up before any data flows and held until the call ends: a SONET/SDH time slot (SONET/SDH) or a whole lightpath. In packet switching the data is cut into packets that share the links on demand, each carrying an address (IP) or a short connection label (an ATM virtual circuit, Network layers and ATM). Circuits give fixed delay; packets use spare capacity better.

Node functions and protection

An optical node does four jobs. Add/drop puts local wavelengths into or out of the fiber (an optical add-drop multiplexer, OADM). Cross-connect moves a wavelength from an input fiber to a chosen output fiber (an optical cross-connect, OXC). Regeneration recreates a clean signal when the path is too long. Switching sets or changes routes under control. The node hardware is in WDM networks, optical nodes and switching.

Protection reserves capacity before a failure. In 1+1 the signal is sent on two paths at once and the receiver selects the better copy; the backup carries no other traffic. In 1:1 one backup path stands by for one working path and may carry low-priority extra traffic while the working path is healthy. In 1:N one backup serves N working paths. Restoration finds a new route only after the failure, so it is slower and needs a control plane. Metro networks favour rings because a cut is handled by switching at the two nodes next to it, within milliseconds.

A broadcast-and-select network sends every wavelength to every node, and each receiver filters the one it wants. A wavelength-routed network sends each wavelength through cross-connects along its own path, so the same wavelength can be reused on links that do not overlap. Static lightpaths are set up once; switched lightpaths are set up and torn down by signalling.

Worked example

Question. A metro ring has 6 nodes, A to F. Every pair of nodes needs one bidirectional 10 Gb/s lightpath, which uses the same wavelength on both fibers of each link it crosses.   2. (b) With no reuse, each lightpath has its own wavelength on the whole ring: 15 wavelengths.
  3. (c) Dedicated 1+1 sends a second copy on a separate wavelength over the other side of the ring: 2 × 15 = 30 wavelengths.
  4. (d) Each lightpath carries 10 Gb/s in each direction: 15 × 10 = 150 Gb/s in each direction, 300 Gb/s counting both. The protection copies repeat the same data, so they add no working traffic.
  5. (e) Shortest routes: the 6 neighbour pairs use 1 link each, the 6 pairs two apart use 2 links, and the 3 opposite pairs use 3 links. Link uses = 6 × 1 + 6 × 2 + 3 × 3 = 27 over 6 links, an average of 4.5, so the busiest link must carry at least 5 lightpaths and at least 5 wavelengths are needed.
  6. Five are enough: route the opposite pairs A–D via B, B–E via A, C–F via D, and colour the lightpaths so that no two sharing a link get the same wavelength. One such set is λ1 = {A–D, A–E, D–E}, λ2 = {B–D, B–E}, λ3 = {A–C, A–F, C–F}, λ4 = {B–C, B–F, C–E, E–F}, λ5 = {A–B, C–D, D–F}.
  7. Check: links A–B, C–D and E–F each carry 5 lightpaths and the other three links carry 4, so 4 × 3 + 5 × 3 = 27 link uses, as counted.

Exam traps

  • Physical topology is not the lightpath topology. A ring of fiber can carry a full mesh of lightpaths, so do not copy the fiber graph into the logical graph.
  • Count a bidirectional lightpath once per pair, N(N − 1)/2, and give its traffic per direction. Counting each node’s demands separately gives 30 demands and doubles the traffic.

  • A lightpath needs the same wavelength on every link unless a converter is present. A free wavelength on one link is not enough.
  • 1+1 and 1:1 both have a backup, but in 1+1 the signal is always sent on both paths; only a 1:1 backup can carry extra traffic while healthy.

6.6 SONET/SDH and synchronous networks

SONET in North America and SDH elsewhere define one synchronous frame that carries many low-rate channels on a single optical line. One master clock and byte interleaving let any channel be added or dropped without taking the whole signal apart. The frame repeats every 125 µs, so every rate is an exact multiple of 51.84 Mb/s. Telephone companies built voice, leased-line and protected-ring networks on it, and packet networks still use it as a reliable pipe.

You need: time-division multiplexing of digital voice channels into the E1 (2.048 Mb/s) and T1 (1.544 Mb/s) carriers, and the ring and protection terms of Optical network concepts.

Why one clock and pointers

The older plesiochronous digital hierarchy (PDH) let every multiplexer run on its own, nearly equal clock and used bit stuffing to make up the difference. To pick one 2 Mb/s line out of a 140 Mb/s stream, the operator had to demultiplex step by step down to that line and build everything up again. SONET (North America) and SDH (Europe, India and most of the world) replace this with one synchronous frame: every node locks to a primary reference clock, and bytes from many channels sit in fixed, known positions.

The SONET building block is the STS-1 electrical signal at 51.84 Mb/s; its optical form is OC-1. STS-n and OC-n run at n × 51.84 Mb/s. The SDH building block is STM-1 at 155.52 Mb/s, which equals STS-3/OC-3, and STM-N runs at N × 155.52 Mb/s.

A pointer in the line overhead marks where the synchronous payload envelope (SPE) starts inside the frame. If a tributary clock runs slightly fast or slow, the pointer moves by one byte and a stuffing byte is added or removed, so the payload floats in the frame without a slip. Concatenation, written STS-3c, ties three STS-1 payloads into one container with one pointer; the SDH VC-4 does the same job.

Frames, envelopes and payload rates

The STS-1 frame is a rectangle of 90 columns × 9 rows = 810 bytes, sent row by row. It lasts 125 µs, so 8000 frames pass per second. The first 3 columns are transport overhead: section overhead in rows 1 to 3 and line overhead in rows 4 to 9, with the pointer bytes H1, H2 and H3 in row 4. The other 87 columns form the SPE, 87 × 9 = 783 bytes, whose first column is the path overhead.

R = bytes per frame × 8 × 8000

R is the bit rate in b/s; 8 bits per byte; 8000 frames per second. For STS-1, R = 810 × 8 × 8000 = 51.84 Mb/s; each column of 9 bytes is worth 9 × 8 × 8000 = 576 kb/s.

So the SPE carries 783 × 64 000 = 50.112 Mb/s. Take away the path overhead column and two fixed-stuff columns and 84 columns of user payload remain: 48.384 Mb/s, room for 28 T1s or one DS3 (44.736 Mb/s).

The STM-1 frame is 270 columns × 9 rows = 2430 bytes in the same 125 µs. Its first 9 columns are the section overhead: the regenerator section overhead in rows 1 to 3, the AU pointer in row 4 and the multiplex section overhead in rows 5 to 9. The other 261 columns carry the VC-4, whose first column is path overhead and whose remaining 260 columns are the C-4 container, 149.76 Mb/s. E1s enter the VC-4 through TU-12s grouped 3 into a TUG-2, 7 TUG-2s into a TUG-3 and 3 TUG-3s into the VC-4: 3 × 7 × 3 = 63 E1s.

Layers, add-drop and rings

SONET has four layers. The photonic layer is the light on the fiber. The section layer (SDH: regenerator section) runs between regenerators and handles framing and section error checks. The line layer (SDH: multiplex section) runs between multiplexers and carries the pointer, protection switching signals and line error checks. The path layer runs end to end between the nodes that build and read the payload. Each layer checks its own overhead, so a fault can be located to one section of the route.

An add-drop multiplexer (ADM) uses the fixed byte positions: it drops selected tributaries, inserts new traffic into the same positions and passes the rest, with no full demultiplexing. In a UPSR (unidirectional path-switched ring), called SNCP in SDH, each signal is sent both ways round the ring and the receiver selects the better copy. In a two-fiber BLSR (bidirectional line-switched ring), called MS-SPRing in SDH, half the capacity of each fiber is kept as shared protection, and at a cut the nodes beside it loop the working traffic back onto that protection capacity. Both restore traffic within 50 ms.

Jitter is short-term variation of the clock phase, at 10 Hz and above; wander is slow drift below 10 Hz. Pointers absorb wander, and interface specifications limit jitter.

6.7 WDM networks, optical nodes and switching

A WDM network carries many lightpaths, each on a wavelength. Its nodes must add, drop and cross-connect those wavelengths without turning every signal into electronics. An optical add-drop multiplexer handles a few local wavelengths; an optical cross-connect switches many wavelengths between many fibers. A lightpath normally keeps one wavelength from end to end, the wavelength continuity constraint, and converters are used only where that fails. Routing and wavelength assignment choose the path and the wavelength. These ideas run metro rings, data-centre links and long-haul mesh networks.

You need: lightpaths, links and protection (Optical network concepts) and the circulator and Bragg grating (Passive devices).

Nodes that add, drop and cross-connect

An optical node does more than join fibers. It adds local wavelengths, drops wavelengths for local receivers, and passes the rest through. An optical add-drop multiplexer (OADM) does this for a few wavelengths. A fixed OADM drops chosen wavelengths with gratings and circulators or thin-film filters. A reconfigurable OADM (ROADM) uses a wavelength-selective switch (WSS), which sends each wavelength from its input to any chosen output port. The WSS changes the route of a wavelength, not the wavelength itself.

An optical cross-connect (OXC) switches many wavelengths between many input and output fibers, so it can rearrange lightpaths across a mesh. A metro ring uses OADMs to drop one wavelength at a city exchange while the others pass untouched; a long-haul mesh node uses an OXC.

Cross-connect architectures and switch elements

Size first. Take an OXC with F fiber pairs and W wavelengths per fiber. Each input fiber is demultiplexed into its W wavelengths. Without wavelength converters, wavelength λi can only leave on λi, so the node is W separate F × F switches, one per wavelength plane: W × F² crosspoints in crossbar form. With full wavelength interchange, any of the F × W inputs can reach any of the F × W outputs, which needs one (FW) × (FW) switch: (FW)² crosspoints, plus converters.

In broadcast-and-select, each input fiber is split into as many copies as there are output fibers. At each output, a filter and a gate on each copy keep the wanted wavelength from that input and block the rest, and a combiner sums the copies. Loss is the splitter loss plus filter and gate loss plus the combiner loss, and a closed gate that still leaks a little sets the crosstalk. A WSS-based node routes each wavelength instead of sending all power everywhere, so its loss and crosstalk are lower, at higher cost.

Wavelength continuity and converters

A lightpath keeps one wavelength from source to destination unless a converter changes it. For a path with links 1 to k, let Si be the set of free wavelengths on link i.

S = S₁ ∩ S₂ ∩ … ∩ Sk

Si is the set of free wavelengths on link i; k is the number of links; S is the set of wavelengths free on every link. Without conversion the path can be set up only if S is not empty.

Checking only neighbouring pairs of links is wrong: every link must share the wavelength. Converters relax the rule. Optoelectronic (O-E-O) conversion receives the signal, regenerates it and retransmits it on a new wavelength. Cross-gain modulation in an SOA (Optical amplifiers) lets a strong signal at λ1 modulate the gain seen by a probe at λ2, copying the data onto λ2. Four-wave mixing (Nonlinear effects covers the Kerr effect behind it) creates a copy at a new wavelength in a nonlinear medium. Converters are placed sparsely, because each adds cost, loss and noise.

Routing, protection, bursts and packets

Routing and wavelength assignment (RWA) picks a path and a wavelength for each lightpath. In route-first-then-assign, find a route (for example the shortest path by Dijkstra), list the wavelengths free on all its links, and take the lowest-numbered one (first fit). If none is free, try the next route or block the request. Static traffic is known in advance and can be planned; dynamic traffic comes and goes, so the blocking probability matters. Static RWA is a graph colouring: each lightpath is a vertex, two vertices are joined when their paths share a link, and colours are wavelengths.

Protection reuses the ring ideas of SONET/SDH (UPSR and BLSR) and the mesh schemes 1+1 and shared backup paths; an OXC speeds restoration by switching whole wavelengths. Optical burst switching (OBS) sends a control packet ahead on a separate wavelength and the data burst after an offset time, so each node can set its switch before the burst arrives; contention can drop a burst. Optical packet switching (OPS) switches every packet, so it needs nanosecond switching, fast header processing and optical buffering.

Worked example

Question. A lightpath must run A–B–C–D. Link A–B has λ1 and λ2 free, B–C has λ2 and λ3 free, and C–D has λ1 and λ3 free. (a) Can it be set up with wavelength continuity? (b) Node B has a wavelength converter. Find a working assignment. (c) A node has 4 fiber pairs with 8 wavelengths each. How many crosspoints does it need without converters, and with full wavelength interchange?

  1. Free sets: SAB = {λ1, λ2}, SBC = {λ2, λ3}, SCD = {λ1, λ3}.
  2. (a) {λ1, λ2} ∩ {λ2, λ3} = {λ2}; then {λ2} ∩ {λ1, λ3} = ∅. No wavelength is free on all three links, so the request is blocked without conversion, even though every link has two free wavelengths.
  3. (b) The converter at B splits the path into A–B and B–C–D. On A–B use λ2 (free). For B–C–D intersect {λ2, λ3} ∩ {λ1, λ3} = {λ3}, so use λ3 on both links. At B convert λ2 → λ3. One conversion is enough.
  4. (c) Without converters: 8 wavelength planes, each a 4 × 4 switch with 4² = 16 crosspoints, so 8 × 16 = 128 crosspoints.
  5. With full interchange: 4 × 8 = 32 inputs and 32 outputs in one 32 × 32 switch, 32² = 1024 crosspoints, plus up to 32 converters.
  6. Check: 1024 ÷ 128 = 8 = W. Freedom to change wavelength costs W times more crosspoints.

Answer. (a) Blocked: the free sets have no common wavelength. (b) λ2 on A–B, convert to λ3 at B, λ3 on B–C and C–D. (c) 128 crosspoints without converters; 1024 with full wavelength interchange.

Exam traps

  • A common wavelength on each pair of neighbouring links is not enough. The same wavelength must be free on every link of the path.
  • A WSS switches a wavelength to another port; it does not change it. Only a wavelength converter turns λ2 into λ3.
  • Without converters an OXC is W small F × F switches (W × F² crosspoints), not one big FW × FW switch. For 4 fibers and 8 wavelengths that is 128, not 1024.
  • Splitter losses in dB add: a 1:16 splitter costs 10 log₁₀ 16 = 12.04 dB, and a 16:1 combiner costs another 12.04 dB.
  • OADM and OXC are not the same: an OADM adds and drops a few local wavelengths on one fiber line, while an OXC can switch any wavelength between many fibers.

6.8 Network layers, protocols and ATM

Layering splits a network job into levels, so a change at one level leaves the others alone. Each level talks to its peer at the far end and hands data down through a clear interface. Optics is the bottom level and carries bits for every layer above. Asynchronous Transfer Mode (ATM) is a fixed-cell layer-2 design that once promised voice, video and data on one optical pipe. Only a few syllabi teach this topic. The chapter maps layers, data units and ATM, and shows why IP won.

You need: circuit and packet transmission modes (Optical network concepts) and the SONET/SDH frame (SONET/SDH).

Layers and protocol data units

The Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) model has seven layers. Each layer adds its own header (and sometimes a trailer) to what it receives from above and passes the result, its protocol data unit (PDU), down.

  • Application: user services such as web and e-mail.
  • Presentation: data format, compression and encryption.
  • Session: starting and ending a dialogue.
  • Transport: end-to-end delivery, ordering and flow control.
  • Network: addressing and routing across many links, as IP does.
  • Data link: frames on one link, with error checks.
  • Physical: bits on the medium: wavelength, connector, power and line code.

The PDU is a bit at the physical layer, a frame at the data link layer, a packet at the network layer, a segment at the transport layer for TCP (a datagram for UDP) and a message at the application layer. The TCP/IP model folds session and presentation into the application and has four layers: application, transport, internet and link. Optics works at the physical layer: a photodiode does not read an IP address.

IP can ride on SONET/SDH, on Ethernet, or straight on WDM lightpaths (WDM networks). Multiprotocol Label Switching (MPLS) puts a short fixed label in front of each packet, so routers switch on the label rather than the full IP header; generalized MPLS (GMPLS) extends the label idea to time slots, wavelengths and fibers.

ATM cells, layers and service classes

ATM sends everything in cells of 53 bytes: a 5-byte header and a 48-byte payload. At the user-network interface the header holds a 4-bit generic flow control field, an 8-bit virtual path identifier (VPI), a 16-bit virtual channel identifier (VCI), a 3-bit payload type, a 1-bit cell loss priority and an 8-bit header error control byte; inside the network the flow control bits join the VPI, giving 12 bits. Short fixed cells keep voice delay small, because a voice sample never waits behind a long packet, and they let hardware switch with one table lookup per cell.

ATM has three layers. The physical layer moves cells over SONET/SDH or fiber. The ATM layer adds and removes the header and switches cells by VPI/VCI. The ATM adaptation layer (AAL) cuts user data into 48-byte pieces and rebuilds it at the far end. AAL1 carries constant-bit-rate voice and circuit emulation, AAL2 carries compressed voice, and AAL5 carries data: it adds padding and then an 8-byte trailer (with the length and a CRC-32), so that data + pad + trailer fill a whole number of 48-byte payloads and the trailer sits in the last 8 bytes of the last cell.

Service classes give quality of service (QoS). Constant bit rate (CBR) is a fixed rate with tight delay, like a call. Real-time variable bit rate (VBR-rt) varies in rate but keeps delay tight, like compressed video. Non-real-time VBR (VBR-nrt) varies with looser delay. Available bit rate (ABR) shares spare capacity using feedback. Unspecified bit rate (UBR) gives no guarantee, like a file download.

ATM lost to IP and Ethernet. IP was already on every computer, Ethernet was cheap and kept getting faster, and ATM paid a cell tax of 5 header bytes on every 48 payload bytes plus AAL padding. IP over WDM, often with MPLS, now carries most backbone traffic; ATM survives mainly in older access and telephone networks, and its virtual-circuit labels and QoS classes live on in MPLS.

Worked example

Question. An IP packet of 1500 bytes is sent over AAL5, which adds an 8-byte trailer and pads the result to whole 48-byte cell payloads. (a) How many cells are needed? (b) How many pad bytes are added? (c) How many bytes go on the link? (d) What is the efficiency? (e) How long does this take on an STM-1 carrying 149.76 Mb/s of cells?

  1. (a) Data plus trailer = 1500 + 8 = 1508 bytes. Cells = ⌈1508/48⌉ = ⌈31.42⌉ = 32 cells.
  2. (b) 32 payloads hold 32 × 48 = 1536 bytes, so the pad is 1536 − 1508 = 28 bytes, placed before the trailer.
  3. (c) Link bytes = 32 × 53 = 1696 bytes.
  4. (d) Efficiency = 1500/1696 = 0.8844, or 88.4 percent. The headers cost 32 × 5 = 160 bytes and pad plus trailer 36 bytes.
  5. (e) Time = 1696 × 8 bits ÷ 149.76 × 10⁶ b/s = 13 568 ÷ 149.76 × 10⁶ = 90.6 µs.
  6. Check: 1500 + 28 + 8 + 160 = 1696 bytes.

Answer. 32 cells with 28 pad bytes, 1696 bytes on the link, 88.4 percent efficiency, 90.6 µs on the STM-1 payload.

Exam traps

  • Count the 5-byte header: a 48-byte payload costs 53 bytes on the link, not 48.
  • Add the 8-byte AAL5 trailer before dividing by 48: a 90-byte packet seems to need ⌈90/48⌉ = 2 cells, but 90 + 8 = 98 bytes need 3.
  • AAL5 pads first and puts the trailer last, in the final 8 bytes of the last cell; the trailer is not between data and pad.
  • VPI names the path and VCI the channel inside it; do not swap them. AAL1 carries constant-bit-rate voice and AAL5 carries data.
  • The transport PDU is a segment for TCP but a datagram for UDP; a frame belongs to the data link layer.

Takeaways

  1. WDM raises fiber capacity by adding wavelengths, C = N × B, not by making the electronics faster; 100 GHz spacing is 0.80 nm at 1550 nm.
  2. Couplers, isolators, circulators and Bragg gratings set the loss and crosstalk of every WDM node; two circulators and a grating make an add-drop node.
  3. An optical amplifier boosts all channels at once by stimulated emission; noise adds as 10 log₁₀ N over N amplifiers, and the pump-to-signal photon ratio caps the output.
  4. The EDFA rules 1550 nm links with high gain, low noise figure and little polarization dependence; the SOA is smaller and cheaper, works near 1310 nm too, and suits gates and converters.
  5. At high power the Kerr effect makes a pulse chirp itself through SPM; balanced against anomalous dispersion at P₀ = |β₂|/(γT₀²) it forms a soliton that keeps its shape.
  6. A lightpath is one wavelength held end to end; a ring of fibers can carry a full mesh of lightpaths, and wavelength reuse cuts the wavelengths needed.
  7. SONET/SDH packs tributaries into a 125 µs frame locked to one clock, so rates are exact multiples of 51.84 Mb/s and pointers let one channel be added or dropped cleanly.
  8. WDM nodes combine add-drop, cross-connection and sometimes wavelength conversion; converters remove the continuity constraint at W times the switch size.
  9. Layering keeps transmission, framing and user services independent, which is why IP, ATM and SONET/SDH can share the same fiber.

Diagram: input isolator, WDM coupler with pump, Er-doped fiber, output isolator, gain-flattening filter; Three-level picture: pump to level 3, fast decay to metastable level 2, stimulated emission 2 → 1 at 1530–1565 nm; Pumps at 980 nm (low noise) and 1480 nm (more output per pump watt); Isolators stop reflections and backward ASE; Typical gain 20–40 dB, NF 4–6 dB, C-band 1530–1565 nm; Power conversion efficiency and a numeric check.

Model answer.

  1. Block diagram:
    signal in → [isolator] → [WDM coupler] → [Er-doped fiber, 10–30 m] → [isolator] → [gain-flattening filter] → out
                              ↑
                   [pump laser 980 or 1480 nm]
  2. Doped fiber. The core of 10 to 30 m of silica fiber holds Er³⁺ ions. These ions have a long-lived (metastable) level 2 about 0.8 eV above the ground level 1, matching photons of 1530 to 1565 nm. When more ions sit in level 2 than in level 1 (population inversion), a signal photon passing an excited ion stimulates it to fall to level 1 and emit an identical photon. Many such events along the fiber give the gain.
  3. Pump laser. The pump creates the inversion. A 980 nm photon lifts an ion from level 1 to level 3; the ion drops within microseconds to level 2, where it stays about 10 ms, long enough to wait for a signal photon. A 1480 nm photon lifts the ion straight into the top of level 2. The WDM coupler merges the pump and the signal into the doped fiber with little loss to either. The 980 nm pump gives the more complete inversion and so the lower noise figure; the 1480 nm pump gives more signal power per pump watt.
  4. Isolators. The doped fiber also emits spontaneously in all directions, and that light is amplified too (ASE). Reflections from connectors would send signal and ASE back through the gain and could make the amplifier oscillate like a laser. The input isolator blocks backward ASE from reaching the transmitter side; the output isolator blocks reflections from the line. The gain-flattening filter evens out the gain across the band so all WDM channels leave at about the same power.

    QuantityTypical value
    Gain bandC-band 1530 to 1565 nm (L-band 1570 to 1610 nm with longer fiber)
    Small-signal gain20 to 40 dB
    Noise figure4 to 6 dB (about 3 dB is the limit)
    Pump wavelengths980 nm and 1480 nm
    Polarization dependencesmall

Model answer.

  1. Kerr effect. In silica the refractive index depends on the light intensity I: n = n₀ + n₂I, where n₀ is the low-power index and n₂ ≈ 2.6 × 10⁻²⁰ m²/W is the Kerr coefficient.
  2. Phase after a length L:
    φ = (2π/λ) n L = (2π/λ) n₀ L + (2π/λ) n₂ I L
                 linear part      nonlinear part φNL
  3. Intensity in terms of power. The mode carries power P over an effective area Aeff, so I = P/Aeff. Then φNL = (2πn₂/(λAeff)) P L = γPL, with the nonlinear coefficient γ = 2πn₂/(λAeff).
  4. Loss. The power falls as P(z) = P e−αz, so the phase adds up over φNL = γP ∫₀L e−αz dz = γP Leff, with Leff = (1 − e−αL)/α. For a pulse, P becomes the instantaneous power P(T), so φNL(T) = γP(T)Leff: the peak of the pulse gets the most phase.

On the leading edge the power rises (dP/dT > 0), so δω < 0: the front of the pulse shifts to the red. On the trailing edge dP/dT < 0, so δω > 0: the back shifts to the blue. Near the peak dP/dT = 0 and there is no shift.
  6. Consequence. New frequencies appear on both sides, so the spectrum widens while the pulse shape is unchanged at first. Through dispersion this chirp later changes the shape: in normal dispersion it speeds the broadening; in anomalous dispersion it can cancel it (the soliton). In a WDM system it limits the power per channel.

Model answer.

  1. Synchronous principle. Every node is locked to one primary reference clock, and each tributary occupies fixed byte positions in a frame that repeats every 125 µs (8000 frames per second). Rates are therefore exact multiples: STS-n = n × 51.84 Mb/s and STM-N = N × 155.52 Mb/s.

    | 3 columns transport overhead | 87 columns SPE                         |
| rows 1–3: section overhead   | col 1: path overhead | 86 cols payload |
| row 4: H1 H2 H3 (pointer)    |                      | (2 fixed stuff) |
| rows 4–9: line overhead      |                      |                 |
  3. Rates from the frame:
    PartBytes per frameRate
    Whole STS-1 frame90 × 9 = 810810 × 8 × 8000 = 51.84 Mb/s
    Transport overhead3 × 9 = 271.728 Mb/s
    SPE (with path overhead)87 × 9 = 78350.112 Mb/s
    User payload84 × 9 = 75648.384 Mb/s
    STM-1 frame270 × 9 = 2430155.52 Mb/s
  4. SDH form. The STM-1 frame is 270 columns × 9 rows. Its first 9 columns are section overhead: regenerator section overhead in rows 1 to 3, the AU pointer in row 4 and multiplex section overhead in rows 5 to 9. The other 261 columns carry the VC-4 (path overhead plus the 149.76 Mb/s C-4).
  5. Pointers. A tributary clock may differ slightly from the network clock. The pointer bytes (H1, H2) give the position where the payload envelope starts, and H3 is a spare byte for adjustment. If the tributary runs fast, one extra byte is carried in H3 and the pointer moves back by one; if slow, a stuff byte is inserted and the pointer moves forward. The payload floats, but its start is always known, so no data slips.
  6. Add and drop. Because each tributary sits at known byte positions, an add-drop multiplexer reads the pointer, takes out the bytes of the channel to be dropped, writes the new channel into the same positions and passes every other byte straight through. In the older plesiochronous hierarchy, bit stuffing at every level hid each channel inside the higher rate, so the whole stream had to be demultiplexed step by step.
  7. Layers. Section overhead is checked between regenerators, line (multiplex section) overhead between multiplexers, and path overhead end to end, so a fault is located to one part of the route. Line overhead also carries the protection-switching bytes for rings that restore traffic within 50 ms.
  8. Closing check. 810 × 8 × 8000 = 51.84 Mb/s, and 3 × 51.84 = 155.52 Mb/s = 2430 × 8 × 8000, so one STM-1 carries exactly three STS-1 frames; transport overhead is 27/810 = 3.33 percent of the STS-1.

  10. Closing check: 5.184 + 150.336 = 155.52 Mb/s and 0.576 + 149.76 = 150.336 Mb/s, so the parts add up to the whole at both levels.

Evaluate Compare SOA and EDFA for a 10-channel WDM link in gain band, noise figure, polarization dependence, crosstalk, size and cost. Recommend one for a long-haul link of 10 spans and justify with numbers. 13 marks

Points to cover. EDFA: C-band 1530–1565 nm, gain 20–40 dB, NF 4–6 dB, small polarization dependence, slow (ms) gain dynamics so little inter-channel crosstalk, needs pump and metres of fiber; SOA: 1310 or 1550 nm, gain 20–30 dB, NF 6–9 dB, polarization dependent, sub-ns gain dynamics give cross-gain modulation, small and cheap; OSNR over 10 spans with NF 5 dB and 8 dB; Recommend EDFA for long haul; SOA for short links, gates and converters.

Model answer.

  1. Side-by-side comparison:

  2. Crosstalk reasoning. In a 10-channel link each channel carries a changing bit pattern. In an SOA the gain recovers in under a nanosecond, so when one channel sends a 1 the gain seen by the other nine dips, and their bits pick up a copy of its pattern. In an EDFA the excited ions live about 10 ms, millions of bit periods at Gb/s rates, so the gain sees only the average power and the channels do not disturb each other.
  3. Noise reasoning. NF depends on how complete the population inversion is. A 980 nm pumped EDFA reaches almost full inversion and has low coupling loss to the fiber; an SOA has partial inversion and loses power at the chip-to-fiber joints, so its NF is higher.

  6. Recommendation: EDFA for the 10-span long-haul link. It amplifies all 10 channels together in the C-band with low noise, negligible crosstalk and stable gain whatever the polarization; its higher cost is shared by all channels on every span.
  7. Where the SOA wins: short metro and access links where cost and size matter more than noise, the 1310 nm band where erbium gives no gain, and switching uses (optical gates, wavelength converters by cross-gain modulation), where its fast gain is the point.

Analyze Explain how SPM and GVD interact in a fiber. Derive the condition for soliton formation and discuss the practical limits on soliton communication systems. 13 marks

Points to cover. GVD: in anomalous dispersion (β₂ < 0) higher frequencies (blue) travel faster; SPM: red leading edge, blue trailing edge; Normal dispersion: chirps add, faster broadening; anomalous: they cancel; NLS equation and LD = T₀²/|β₂|, LNL = 1/(γP₀), N² = LD/LNL; N = 1: P₀ = |β₂|/(γT₀²), sech amplitude; higher orders breathe with z₀ = (π/2)LD; Limits: loss and amplifiers, ASE timing jitter, soliton interaction, high peak power; numbers.

Model answer.

  1. GVD alone. Group-velocity dispersion β₂ makes different frequencies travel at different speeds. In anomalous dispersion (β₂ < 0, the 1550 nm region of standard fiber) higher frequencies (blue) travel faster than lower ones (red); in normal dispersion (β₂ > 0) red is faster. Either way a pulse of width T₀ broadens over the dispersion length LD = T₀²/|β₂|.
  2. SPM alone. The Kerr effect gives φNL = γP(T)L, and the frequency shift δω = −γL dP/dT puts red on the leading edge and blue on the trailing edge. The spectrum widens but the shape does not change by itself. The phase reaches 1 rad over the nonlinear length LNL = 1/(γP₀).
  3. Together:
    DispersionLeading edge (red)Trailing edge (blue)Result
    Normal, β₂ > 0red is faster: pulls aheadblue is slower: falls behindchirps add; faster broadening
    Anomalous, β₂ < 0red is slower: falls backblue is faster: catches upchirps cancel; pulse can hold its shape
  4. Governing equation. The envelope A(z,T) obeys the nonlinear Schrödinger equation ∂A/∂z + (α/2)A + (iβ₂/2)∂²A/∂T² = iγ|A|²A. Ignoring loss and writing it with z in units of LD and the power in units of P₀ leaves one number, the soliton order N, with N² = LD/LNL = γP₀T₀²/|β₂|.
  5. Soliton condition. Balance needs equal lengths, N = 1:
    LD = LNL  →  T₀²/|β₂| = 1/(γP₀)  →  P₀ = |β₂|/(γT₀²),  with β₂ < 0
  6. The exact solution is A(z,T) = √P₀ sech(T/T₀) eiz/(2LD): the amplitude is sech-shaped (intensity sech²), the FWHM is 1.763 T₀, and only the phase grows with z. For N = 2, 3, … the pulse narrows, splits and recovers its shape after the soliton period z₀ = (π/2)LD.
  7. Numbers. For β₂ = −20 ps²/km, γ = 2 W⁻¹km⁻¹ and T₀ = 10 ps: LD = 100/20 = 5 km, P₀ = 20/(2 × 100) = 0.1 W, LNL = 1/(2 × 0.1) = 5 km, so N = 1, and z₀ = 7.85 km.
  8. Practical limits. (1) Loss: the fiber lowers the power, so SPM weakens between amplifiers and the balance holds only on average; amplifier spacing must be well below LD. (2) Amplifier noise: ASE shifts each soliton’s frequency at random, and dispersion turns this into timing jitter (the Gordon-Haus effect) that grows with distance. (3) Interaction: neighbouring solitons attract or repel, so the bit slot must be several pulse widths. (4) Power: 100 mW peaks per channel are high and bring other nonlinear crosstalk in WDM. These are why installed long-haul links use dispersion management and coherent detection instead.
  9. Closing check: γP₀LD = 2 × 0.1 × 5 = 1 = N², and halving T₀ to 5 ps raises P₀ to 0.4 W, four times higher, which shows why short soliton pulses are costly in power.
Analyze Explain the wavelength continuity constraint and blocking in WDM networks. Compare networks with no converters, sparse converters and full conversion at every node, using a worked path and the switch size of a node. 14 marks

Points to cover. Lightpath keeps one wavelength unless converted; S = S₁ ∩ … ∩ Sk; Example A–B–C–D blocked with every link half free; converter at B fixes it; Blocking probability; RWA as graph colouring; first fit; Converter types: O-E-O, SOA cross-gain, four-wave mixing; Switch size W × F² against (FW)²; 128 against 1024; No, sparse and full conversion compared; recommendation.

Model answer.

  1. Constraint. Without converters a lightpath must use the same wavelength on every link of its route. For links 1 to k with free sets Si, the request can be met only if S = S₁ ∩ S₂ ∩ … ∩ Sk is not empty. Blocking is the event that S is empty (and no other route helps), and the blocking probability is the share of requests refused.
  2. Worked path A–B–C–D:
    LinkFree wavelengths
    A–Bλ1, λ2
    B–Cλ2, λ3
    C–Dλ1, λ3
  3. Intersection: {λ1, λ2} ∩ {λ2, λ3} = {λ2}; {λ2} ∩ {λ1, λ3} = ∅. The request is blocked although every link is only one-third busy. This is the cost of the constraint: free capacity exists but on the wrong wavelengths.
  4. With a converter at B the path splits into A–B and B–C–D. A–B uses λ2; for B–C–D, {λ2, λ3} ∩ {λ1, λ3} = {λ3}. Convert λ2 → λ3 at B and the request is served with one conversion.
  5. Converter types. O-E-O: detect, regenerate and retransmit on a new wavelength; mature, also cleans the signal, but tied to bit rate and format. SOA cross-gain modulation: the data on λ1 modulates the gain seen by a probe on λ2; all-optical but inverts and adds noise. Four-wave mixing: transparent to format but weak.
  6. Switch size for F = 4 fiber pairs, W = 8 wavelengths:
    no converters:   W planes of F × F  = 8 × 4² = 128 crosspoints
full conversion: (F W) × (F W)       = 32²     = 1024 crosspoints (+ up to 32 converters)
  7. Comparison:
    DesignBlockingCost and complexityWavelength assignment
    No convertershighestlowest: W small switcheshard: graph colouring, first fit
    Sparse converters at chosen nodesclose to full conversion in many networksmoderate: converters only where paths meetper segment between converters
    Full conversionlowest: same as a circuit network with no wavelength rulehighest: (FW)² switch and FW converterstrivial: any free wavelength per link
  8. Analysis. Most blocking caused by the constraint comes from a few busy nodes where many long paths cross, so converters placed there remove most of it; adding them everywhere costs W times more crosspoints for little extra gain. Rerouting and reassigning existing lightpaths also lowers blocking without hardware.
  9. Recommendation: sparse conversion at the high-degree, heavily loaded nodes, with first-fit assignment elsewhere; full conversion only where traffic is very dynamic and cost is secondary.
  10. Closing check: 1024 ÷ 128 = 8 = W, the price of full conversion in this node, while one converter at B was enough to serve the blocked request.
Evaluate Explain the OSI seven-layer model, place SONET/SDH and ATM in it, and discuss why ATM used fixed 53-byte cells. Evaluate the cost of the cells for a 1500-byte IP packet. 12 marks

Points to cover. Seven layers and their PDUs; SONET/SDH mainly physical layer with framing; ATM layer 2 over it; ATM cell 5 + 48 bytes; header fields; Short fixed cells: low voice delay, simple fast switching, QoS classes; AAL5 on 1500 bytes: 32 cells, 1696 bytes, 88.4 %; Evaluation: lost to IP and Ethernet; ideas live on in MPLS.

Model answer.

  1. The OSI model:
    LayerJobPDU
    7 Applicationuser services (web, e-mail)message
    6 Presentationformat, compression, encryptionmessage
    5 Sessionstart and end dialoguesmessage
    4 Transportend-to-end delivery, flow controlsegment (TCP) or datagram (UDP)
    3 Networkaddressing and routing (IP)packet
    2 Data linkframes on one link, error checkframe (cell in ATM)
    1 Physicalbits on the medium: light, wavelength, powerbit
  2. Placing SONET/SDH. SONET/SDH defines the optical interface, line rates and the framed byte structure with its overheads, so it sits at the physical layer, with framing and its own section, line and path layers inside it. The fiber and laser are its photonic layer.
  3. Placing ATM. ATM switches cells between links using VPI/VCI labels, so it acts at layer 2 (some books place its virtual circuits partly at layer 3). The ATM adaptation layer cuts higher-layer data into cells. A typical stack is IP over AAL5 over ATM over SONET/SDH over fiber.
  4. Cell format. 53 bytes: a 5-byte header (at the user interface 4-bit GFC, 8-bit VPI, 16-bit VCI, 3-bit payload type, 1-bit cell loss priority, 8-bit header error control) and a 48-byte payload.
  5. Why short and fixed. (1) Voice delay: a 64 kb/s voice channel fills 48 bytes in 48 × 8 ÷ 64 000 = 6 ms; a longer cell would mean a longer fill delay and echo problems. (2) Switching: a fixed size lets hardware switch every cell in the same time with one table lookup, at line rate. (3) Queueing: a voice cell never waits behind a 1500-byte packet, so delay is bounded and the CBR and VBR-rt classes can be met.
  6. Cost for a 1500-byte IP packet over AAL5:
    data + trailer = 1500 + 8 = 1508 B
cells          = ⌈1508/48⌉ = ⌈31.42⌉ = 32
pad            = 32 × 48 − 1508 = 28 B
link bytes     = 32 × 53 = 1696 B
efficiency     = 1500/1696 = 88.4 %
  7. Evaluation. Headers alone take 5/53 = 9.4 percent of every cell, and padding adds more for packets that do not fill the last cell: 11.6 percent of the link carries no user data here. IP was on every computer, Ethernet was cheap and kept getting faster, and carrying IP packets directly in Ethernet or on SONET/SDH and WDM avoided both the cell tax and ATM’s connection set-up. ATM lost the network core to IP, but its label switching and service classes live on in MPLS, and SONET/SDH and OTN still carry IP below.
  8. Closing check: 1500 + 28 + 8 + 32 × 5 = 1696 bytes, the link total.

Glossary

AAL5
The ATM adaptation layer for data: pads the packet and adds an 8-byte trailer so it fills whole 48-byte payloads.
Anomalous dispersion
The regime where β₂ < 0 and higher frequencies (shorter wavelengths) travel faster; required for bright solitons.
Blocking probability
The chance that a requested lightpath cannot be set up because no wavelength is free.
Chirp
A change of instantaneous frequency across a pulse, so different parts of the pulse have different wavelengths.
Circulator
A multi-port passive device that passes light in one rotational direction only, e.g. port 1 to 2 to 3.
Coupling ratio
The share of a coupler’s total output that leaves one chosen port, in percent.
Cross-gain modulation
One channel's power changes the SOA gain seen by other channels, causing crosstalk.
Crosstalk
Leakage of one WDM channel into a neighbouring channel, measured in dB.
Directivity
How little of the input power leaks back out of the other input-side port of a coupler, 10 log₁₀(Pin/P₂) in dB.
Dispersion length
LD = T₀²/|β₂|, the distance over which group-velocity dispersion noticeably broadens a pulse of width parameter T₀.
Excess loss
The part of a coupler's loss not explained by the intended splitting ratio.
Gain bandwidth
The range of wavelengths over which an amplifier gives useful gain.
Insertion loss
The drop in optical power caused by putting a component into the link, in dB.
Nonlinear length
LNL = 1/(γP₀), the distance over which the SPM phase reaches 1 rad at peak power P₀.
Nonlinear Schrödinger equation
The propagation equation combining GVD and SPM that governs pulse shape in a fiber.
Optical cross-connect (OXC)
A node that switches wavelengths from any input fiber to any output fiber.
Plesiochronous
Nearly but not exactly synchronous, so bit stuffing is needed before multiplexing, as in the PDH hierarchy.
Polarization-dependent gain
Gain that differs for different input polarizations, a drawback of ordinary SOAs.
Population inversion
A state where more ions or carriers sit in the upper energy level than the lower one, so stimulated emission exceeds absorption.
Power conversion efficiency
The signal power an amplifier adds divided by the pump power it uses.
Protocol layer
One level of a layered model that performs a defined job and uses the services of the level below.
Pump laser
A laser at 980 nm or 1480 nm whose power excites the erbium ions in an EDFA.
Saturation power
The output power at which amplifier gain falls 3 dB below its small-signal value.
Section, line and path overhead
SONET/SDH overhead bytes used for framing, error checking and management at each level.
Spontaneous emission
Random photon emission that seeds amplifier noise and limits the noise figure.
Synchronous multiplexing
Packing tributary channels into fixed positions of a frame locked to one master clock.
Synchronous payload envelope
The 87-column part of a SONET frame that carries the path overhead and the user payload.
Thin-film filter
Dielectric multilayer filter that passes one wavelength band and reflects the rest, used in add-drop modules.
Virtual circuit
A connection whose route is fixed at setup time and identified by a short label in each cell.
Wavelength converter
A device that shifts a lightpath from one wavelength to another, removing the continuity constraint.
