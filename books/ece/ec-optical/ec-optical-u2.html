2.1 Attenuation in optical fibers

Attenuation is the loss of optical power as light travels down a fiber. It is quoted as a coefficient in dB per kilometre, because a receiver needs a minimum power and every kilometre takes a fixed share away. The coefficient decides how far a link can reach before an amplifier or repeater is needed. A 1550 nm long-haul system, a 1310 nm metro link and a short 850 nm data link all use the same arithmetic, which this chapter sets up for the whole unit.

Loss in decibels

Let Pin be the optical power launched into a fiber of length L km, and Pout the power that leaves the far end. Both powers use the same unit, for example mW or µW. The attenuation coefficient is the loss per kilometre:

α = (10/L) log₁₀(Pin/Pout)
α is the attenuation coefficient in dB/km; L is the fiber length in km; Pin and Pout are the input and output powers in the same unit; log₁₀ is the base-10 logarithm.

The loss over the whole length is A = αL in dB. To go the other way, Pout = Pin × 10−A/10, that is, Pin times ten raised to the power −A/10.

Two anchor points make the decibel easy. A loss of 3 dB halves the power, because 10 log₁₀2 = 3.01 dB. A loss of 10 dB divides the power by ten, and 20 dB by a hundred. A 3 dB coupler is named this way because each of its two outputs carries half the input power, so each output port is 3 dB below the input.

Absolute power is quoted in dBm, decibels relative to 1 mW: P(dBm) = 10 log₁₀(P/1 mW). So 1 mW is 0 dBm, 0.5 mW is −3 dBm and 1 µW is −30 dBm. A launch power in dBm minus the losses in dB gives the received power in dBm, with no multiplication at all.

Four loss families add in dB

Four families cover almost every loss in a fiber link. Each gives its own dB value, and the total loss is their sum.

  • Absorption: light turned into heat by the glass and its impurities (see absorption losses).
  • Scattering: light thrown out of the core by tiny index fluctuations (see Rayleigh and Mie scattering).
  • Bending: light that radiates out where the fiber is curved (see bending losses).
  • Joint loss: light lost where two fibers meet at a splice or connector (see fiber joints and connectors).

Atotal = Aabs + Ascat + Abend + Ajoint
Atotal is the total loss in dB; each term on the right is that family’s loss in dB.

The fiber terms are usually lumped into one coefficient α in dB/km, and the joints are counted as so many dB each. The sum works because dB is logarithmic: powers multiply along the link, and the logarithm turns that product into a sum. Never add powers in mW and then convert; add the dB losses, then convert once. The full link power budget, with receiver sensitivity and margin, comes with link design.

Standard windows and the water peak

Silica fiber is not equally clear at all wavelengths. Three transmission windows are used. The table also lists the water peak, which is a loss feature to avoid, not a window.

BandWavelengthTypical α

Second window (near zero dispersion)1310 nm0.32 to 0.35 dB/km
Third window (lowest loss)1550 nm0.18 to 0.2 dB/km

Long-haul links work at 1550 nm because each kilometre costs the fewest dB, so the spacing between amplifiers is longest; erbium-doped fiber amplifiers also work in this band. Low-water-peak fiber removes most of the 1383 nm bump, so the whole 1260 to 1625 nm range becomes usable.

Some books write the coefficient in nepers. From Pout = Pine−α′L, the neper coefficient α′ and the dB coefficient α are related by α = 4.343 α′, so α′ = 0.2303 α. A dB/km value times 0.2303 gives Np/km; divide that by 1000 for Np/m.

Worked example

Question. A 1550 nm link uses fiber with α = 0.2 dB/km and launches 1 mW. (a) Find the loss over 80 km. (b) Find the power at the far end in dBm and in µW. (c) Find the length at which the power falls to 1 µW.

  1. Loss: A = αL = 0.2 dB/km × 80 km = 16 dB.
  2. Launch power: 1 mW = 0 dBm, so the received power is 0 − 16 = −16 dBm.
  3. In watts: Pout = 1 mW × 10−16/10 = 1 mW × 10−1.6 = 1 mW × 0.0251 = 0.0251 mW = 25.1 µW.
  4. For part (c), 1 µW is −30 dBm, so the allowed loss is 0 − (−30) = 30 dB.
  5. Length: L = 30 dB ÷ 0.2 dB/km = 150 km.

Answer. 16 dB over 80 km; −16 dBm, which is 25.1 µW; the power reaches 1 µW after 150 km.

Worked example 2

Question. A 6 km fiber is tested at 1310 nm. The launched power is 120 µW and the power at the far end is 36 µW. Find the attenuation coefficient, and give both powers in dBm.

  1. Ratio: Pin/Pout = 120/36 = 3.333.
  2. log₁₀ 3.333 = 0.5229, so the total loss is A = 10 × 0.5229 = 5.229 dB.
  3. Coefficient: α = A/L = 5.229 dB ÷ 6 km = 0.871 dB/km.
  4. In dBm: 120 µW = 0.120 mW gives 10 log₁₀ 0.120 = −9.21 dBm; 36 µW gives 10 log₁₀ 0.036 = −14.44 dBm.
  5. Check: −9.21 − (−14.44) = 5.23 dB, the same loss as step 2.

Answer. α = 0.87 dB/km; the powers are −9.21 dBm in and −14.44 dBm out. A value this high at 1310 nm points to a cable with extra bend or joint loss, not to clean fiber.

Exam traps

  • Writing A = α/L instead of A = αL. α is per kilometre, so multiplying by L km leaves dB.
  • Adding a dB/km coefficient to a dB loss. Multiply each coefficient by its length first, then add the dB values.
  • Adding powers in mW and then taking a logarithm. Losses add in dB; convert to mW only at the end.
  • A sign slip in the formula. With Pin/Pout inside the logarithm the loss is positive; if you write Pout/Pin you get the same number with a minus sign.
  • Mixing dB and dBm. dB is a ratio (a loss or a gain); dBm is an absolute power. Received power in dBm = launch power in dBm − loss in dB.

2.2 Absorption losses: intrinsic and extrinsic

Absorption loss is light turned into heat inside the glass. Pure silica absorbs strongly in the ultraviolet and the infrared; this is intrinsic absorption, a property of the glass itself. Impurities, above all the hydroxyl ion OH⁻, add extrinsic absorption bands in between. The two silica tails leave a clear region with its lowest absorption near 1.55 µm, and the OH⁻ band near 1383 nm sits inside it. Only a few syllabi list this topic on its own; they ask for the origins, the wavelengths and sometimes the tail formulas.

Intrinsic absorption: two silica tails

Intrinsic absorption comes from the SiO₂ network itself, so even perfectly pure glass has it. It has two parts.

The ultraviolet tail is electronic. Photons with enough energy lift electrons across the band gap of silica, and the edge of that absorption trails into the visible and near infrared. It falls as wavelength rises.

The infrared tail is vibrational. The Si–O bond vibrates near 9 µm, and a photon can excite several vibration quanta at once (multiphonon absorption). This tail is tiny at 1.3 µm and rises steeply beyond about 1.6 µm, which is why silica fiber is not used much past 1.65 µm.

For a germania-doped silica core, two empirical formulas give the tails in dB/km, with λ in µm and x the mole fraction of GeO₂:

αUV = [154.2x/(46.6x + 60)] × 10−2 e4.63/λ
αUV is the ultraviolet absorption in dB/km; x is the GeO₂ mole fraction (0.06 means 6 %); λ is the wavelength in µm.

αIR = 7.81 × 1011 e−48.48/λ
αIR is the infrared absorption in dB/km; λ is in µm.

Extrinsic absorption: impurities and the water peak

Extrinsic absorption comes from impurities. Transition-metal ions (Fe²⁺, Cu²⁺, Cr³⁺, Ni²⁺, V³⁺) give broad bands across the visible and near infrared. Vapour-phase preform processes keep them at the parts-per-billion level, so in modern fiber they no longer matter.

The hydroxyl ion OH⁻ remains. Its fundamental O–H stretch vibration lies near 2.73 µm, outside the window, but its overtones fall inside it: near 1.38 µm (the strongest, the water peak), 0.95 µm and 0.72 µm. A combination band of the O–H and Si–O vibrations adds a smaller peak near 1.24 µm.

Two other sources are worth one line each. Atomic defects in the glass, made for example by nuclear radiation, absorb strongly, which matters for fiber in reactors and space. Hydrogen that diffuses into an installed cable forms new OH⁻ and slowly raises the water peak.

The water peak is not a wall. Low-water-peak fiber is drawn from a dried preform and cuts the 1383 nm loss to about the level at 1310 nm. A system on older fiber simply avoids the band and works at 1310 nm or 1550 nm. Some syllabi call the two kinds fundamental and impurity absorption.

Worked example

Question. A single-mode fiber has a germania-doped core with x = 0.06. Using the two empirical formulas, find the intrinsic absorption at 1.55 µm and at 1.70 µm, and compare it with a Rayleigh loss of 0.14 dB/km at 1.55 µm.

  1. At 1.55 µm, UV tail: 154.2 × 0.06 = 9.252 and 46.6 × 0.06 + 60 = 62.796, so the prefactor is (9.252/62.796) × 10−2 = 1.473 × 10−3. With e4.63/1.55 = e2.987 = 19.83, αUV = 0.029 dB/km.
  2. At 1.55 µm, IR tail: 48.48/1.55 = 31.28 and e−31.28 = 2.61 × 10−14, so αIR = 7.81 × 1011 × 2.61 × 10−14 = 0.020 dB/km.
  3. Intrinsic total at 1.55 µm: 0.029 + 0.020 = 0.049 dB/km, about a third of the 0.14 dB/km Rayleigh loss.
  4. At 1.70 µm: e4.63/1.70 = 15.23 gives αUV = 0.022 dB/km; e−48.48/1.70 = 4.12 × 10−13 gives αIR = 0.32 dB/km.
  5. From 1.55 to 1.70 µm the IR term grows sixteenfold, from 0.020 to 0.32 dB/km, while the UV term barely changes.

Answer. Intrinsic absorption is about 0.05 dB/km at 1.55 µm, well below Rayleigh scattering; at 1.70 µm the infrared tail alone is 0.32 dB/km. This is why the low-loss window ends near 1.6 µm.

Worked example 2

Question. An old cable measures 1.10 dB/km at 1383 nm. A low-water-peak fiber measures 0.26 dB/km at the same wavelength. For a 20 km coarse-WDM channel at 1383 nm, find the extra loss caused by the water peak and the fraction of power it removes.

  1. Excess coefficient: 1.10 − 0.26 = 0.84 dB/km.

Answer. The water peak costs 16.8 dB on this channel and removes 97.9 % of the power that the low-water-peak fiber would deliver.

Exam traps

  • Putting the water peak at 1550 nm. Its strongest overtone is near 1383 nm; the fundamental is at 2.73 µm.
  • Calling OH⁻ or metal-ion bands intrinsic. Intrinsic means the silica network itself; impurities are extrinsic.
  • Treating the whole 1300 to 1500 nm range as unusable. The water peak is a band a few tens of nm wide, and low-water-peak fiber removes most of it.
  • Blaming absorption for the 1550 nm loss floor. At 1550 nm Rayleigh scattering is the larger part; the infrared tail takes over only beyond about 1.6 µm.

2.3 Scattering losses: Rayleigh and Mie

Scattering redirects light out of the core instead of absorbing it on the spot. Two linear mechanisms matter: Rayleigh scattering from density and composition fluctuations frozen into the glass, far smaller than the wavelength, and Mie scattering from imperfections about the size of the wavelength. Rayleigh loss falls as 1/λ⁴, so it sets the loss floor near 1550 nm and explains why early 850 nm systems needed short spans. Mie loss is kept small by clean fabrication.

The mechanism: light leaves the core

Glass is a frozen liquid. As the fiber cools on the draw tower, tiny regions lock in with slightly different density and composition from their neighbours, so the refractive index varies on a scale far below the wavelength. Each small index step re-radiates a little of the passing wave in all directions. Most of that light falls outside the acceptance angle and leaves the core; a small part travels backward.

The scattering event is elastic: the scattered light keeps the wavelength of the incident light, and the glass is not heated where it happens. The light is still lost to the link, and it ends up absorbed in the coating or jacket. Both Rayleigh and Mie scattering are called linear because the loss in dB/km does not depend on the power. Power-dependent scattering is a separate effect, covered in stimulated Brillouin and Raman scattering.

Rayleigh scattering and the 1/λ⁴ law

For a single-component glass the Rayleigh scattering coefficient is

γR = (8π³/3λ⁴) n⁸ p² βc k TF
γR is the Rayleigh scattering coefficient in m⁻¹ (nepers per metre); λ is the free-space wavelength in m; n is the refractive index; p is the photoelastic coefficient; βc is the isothermal compressibility in m²/N; k = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann’s constant; TF is the fictive temperature in K, at which the glass structure froze.

Over a length L the power falls by the factor e−γRL, so the loss in dB is 10 log₁₀(1/e−γRL) = 4.343 γRL. The physics behind 1/λ⁴: each fluctuation acts as a tiny dipole whose radiated field grows as 1/λ², so the scattered power grows as 1/λ⁴.

In practice the loss is written αR = C/λ⁴ with λ in µm. For germania-doped single-mode fiber C is about 0.8 dB·km⁻¹·µm⁴, which gives 1.53 dB/km at 850 nm, 0.27 dB/km at 1310 nm and 0.14 dB/km at 1550 nm. Double the wavelength and the loss falls 2⁴ = 16 times. Rayleigh loss can be lowered but not removed: a lower fictive temperature (slower cooling, or annealing) and less dopant in the core reduce it, which is how pure-silica-core fibers reach about 0.15 dB/km in total.

Mie scattering: size matters

Imperfections comparable in size to the wavelength scatter by the Mie process: a rough or wavy core–cladding boundary, small bubbles, unreacted particles in the preform, strain, and fluctuations in core diameter. Mie scattering is strongest in the forward direction, and its wavelength dependence is weaker than Rayleigh’s. A simple model treats it as 1/λ²: a term of 0.05 dB/km at 1 µm drops to 0.021 dB/km at 1.55 µm, a factor of 2.4 against 5.8 for Rayleigh over the same range.

Mie loss is not a property of the glass. Careful preform making, control of the core diameter, a smoother core–cladding interface and a larger index difference keep it below the Rayleigh term in telecom fiber. A large measured Mie term points to a process fault, not to the material.

Worked example

Question. A single-mode fiber has a Rayleigh loss of 0.14 dB/km at 1550 nm. (a) Estimate the Rayleigh loss at 1310 nm. (b) Estimate it at 850 nm. (c) The measured total loss at 850 nm is 2.0 dB/km; how much is left for absorption, Mie scattering and other terms?

  1. Scaling: αR(λ) = αR(1550) × (1550/λ)⁴, with λ in nm.
  2. (a) (1550/1310)⁴ = 1.1832⁴ = 1.960, so αR = 0.14 × 1.960 = 0.274 dB/km.
  3. (b) (1550/850)⁴ = 1.8235⁴ = 11.06, so αR = 0.14 × 11.06 = 1.55 dB/km.
  4. (c) Remainder at 850 nm: 2.0 − 1.55 = 0.45 dB/km. Without more data this is an upper bound on the sum of Mie scattering, the UV absorption tail and waveguide imperfections.

Answer. 0.27 dB/km at 1310 nm, 1.55 dB/km at 850 nm, and at most 0.45 dB/km left for the other terms at 850 nm.

Worked example 2

Question. Silica has a fictive temperature of 1400 K, isothermal compressibility 7 × 10⁻¹¹ m²/N, refractive index 1.46 and photoelastic coefficient 0.286. Find the Rayleigh scattering loss in dB/km at 0.85 µm and at 1.55 µm.

  1. Constant part: n⁸ = 1.46⁸ = 20.66, p² = 0.0818, and βckTF = 7 × 10⁻¹¹ × 1.381 × 10⁻²³ × 1400 = 1.353 × 10⁻³⁰ m³. Their product is 2.286 × 10⁻³⁰ m³.
  2. At 0.85 µm: λ⁴ = (0.85 × 10⁻⁶)⁴ = 5.220 × 10⁻²⁵ m⁴, and 8π³/3 = 82.68. So γR = 82.68 × 2.286 × 10⁻³⁰ ÷ 5.220 × 10⁻²⁵ = 3.62 × 10⁻⁴ m⁻¹.
  3. Over 1 km: γRL = 0.362, transmission e−0.362 = 0.696, loss 10 log₁₀(1/0.696) = 1.57 dB/km.
  4. At 1.55 µm: γR falls by (1.55/0.85)⁴ = 11.06, to 3.27 × 10⁻⁵ m⁻¹. Over 1 km γRL = 0.0327, and the loss is 4.343 × 0.0327 = 0.142 dB/km.

Answer. About 1.57 dB/km at 0.85 µm and 0.14 dB/km at 1.55 µm, the same values that C = 0.8 dB·km⁻¹·µm⁴ gives.

Exam traps

  • Writing λ⁴ instead of 1/λ⁴. Rayleigh loss falls as the wavelength rises.
  • Saying scattering loses no power because it is elastic. The wavelength is unchanged, but the redirected light still leaves the link.
  • Saying Mie scattering dominates an ordinary telecom fiber. Good fiber keeps the Mie term well below Rayleigh.
  • Leaving γR in m⁻¹ as if it were dB/km. Multiply by 1000 for km⁻¹, then by 4.343 for dB/km.
  • Calling Rayleigh loss power-dependent. Linear scattering is fixed by the glass; the stimulated kinds appear only at high power.

2.4 Bending losses

Bending loss is the extra attenuation caused when a fiber is curved. On a bend the outer part of the guided field would have to travel faster than light can in the cladding, so that part breaks away and radiates. Two forms exist: macrobending, where the whole fiber follows a visible curve, and microbending, where tiny random wiggles of the axis couple light out along the length. Every installed cable bends at trays, cabinets and wall corners, and the bend rules tell the installer how tight is safe.

Macrobending: one large curve

Picture the mode field as a wheel spoke turning round the centre of the bend. To keep its phase front straight, the field further out must move faster. At some radial distance, called the radiation caustic, the required speed exceeds c/n₂, the speed of light in the cladding. The field beyond that point cannot keep up and radiates away. A tighter bend moves the caustic closer to the core, where the field is stronger, so the loss climbs very quickly.

Measured loss per turn follows an exponential law well, written αr = c₁e−c₂R, where R is the bend radius and c₁ (dB per turn) and c₂ (per mm) are fitted from measurements on one fiber at one wavelength. The fit holds over the radii where it was measured; it is not a universal curve.

A rough scale for the radius where loss becomes large is the critical radius. For a multimode fiber:

Rc ≈ 3n₁²λ / [4π(n₁² − n₂²)3/2]
Rc is the critical radius, in the unit of λ; n₁ and n₂ are the core and cladding indices; λ is the free-space wavelength.

For a single-mode fiber the field reaches much further into the cladding, and the estimate becomes

Rcs ≈ [20λ/(n₁ − n₂)3/2] (2.748 − 0.996λ/λc)−3
Rcs is the single-mode critical radius; λc is the cutoff wavelength of the fiber; λ, Rcs and λc share one length unit.

Both formulas say the same thing: a large index difference holds the field tightly and allows sharp bends, and a longer wavelength, which spreads the field, needs gentler ones. Multimode fiber with Δ ≈ 1 % has Rc of only tens of micrometres; standard single-mode fiber at 1550 nm has Rcs of a few centimetres.

Microbending and wavelength

Microbending comes from many small random bends of the axis, with periods of a millimetre or less and amplitudes of micrometres. Uneven coating, cabling pressure, a rough drum surface and thermal contraction of the coating at low temperature all cause them. Each wiggle is tiny, but together they act like a weak grating that couples the guided mode to cladding and radiation modes, so power leaks out evenly along the length. A soft inner coating and a stiff outer coating, which smooths the outside forces, is the usual cure.

Bending loss rises with wavelength. At 1550 nm the mode field is wider than at 1310 nm, so more of it sits beyond the caustic, and the same bend costs more. Installers therefore test bends at 1550 nm or 1625 nm, the worst case. Bend-insensitive fiber adds a low-index trench in the cladding that pulls the mode back toward the core; it is used in drop cables, patch cords and crowded racks.

Using bend loss in a link budget

Macrobend loss is quoted per turn at one radius and one wavelength, so multiply by the number of turns before adding it to the budget. Microbend loss is already spread along the cable and appears inside the cable’s dB/km figure. A mandrel test wraps a set number of turns on a rod of known size and measures the added loss; note that mandrels are specified by diameter, so a 30 mm mandrel gives a 15 mm bend radius.

  1. Find the tightest bend radius R on the route.
  2. Read the loss per turn at the working wavelength from the data sheet or a measured curve.
  3. Count the turns N at that radius and add N × αr dB to the budget.
  4. If the margin is small, open up R, re-route, or use bend-insensitive fiber.

At 0.2 dB/km, 1 dB of bend loss costs as much as 5 km of fiber, which is why bend rules matter as much as the fiber’s own attenuation.

Worked example

Question. A 1550 nm fiber has a measured bend loss αr = 8e−0.6R dB per turn, R in mm, valid from 4 to 15 mm. (a) Find the loss of one turn at R = 5 mm and at R = 10 mm. 2.5 Intermodal dispersion

A short pulse launched into a multimode fiber comes out longer, because its power is shared among many modes and each mode takes a different time to cross the fiber. This is intermodal (or modal) dispersion. It grows with length L and with the fractional index difference Δ, and it caps the bit rate of a multimode link. A graded-index core cuts it by a factor of about 8/Δ, eight hundred times at Δ = 0.01, and a single-mode fiber removes it completely.

You need: total internal reflection, the critical angle and the fractional index difference Δ = (n₁ − n₂)/n₁ from the study of the optical fiber structure.

Step index: two rays, two arrival times

In a step-index fiber the core has one index n₁, so every ray travels at c/n₁ and only the path length differs. The two extremes set the spread. The axial ray goes straight and arrives after tmin = Ln₁/c. The steepest guided ray meets the core boundary at exactly the critical angle, where sin φc = n₂/n₁; its zig-zag path is L n₁/n₂ long, so it arrives after tmax = Ln₁²/(cn₂).

δTs = (Ln₁²/cn₂)Δ ≈ Ln₁Δ/c

δTs is the delay difference between the slowest and the fastest ray in s; L is the length in m; n₁ and n₂ are the core and cladding indices; Δ = (n₁ − n₂)/n₁; c = 2.998 × 10⁸ m/s (3 × 10⁸ is used here).

The approximation drops a factor n₁/n₂ ≈ 1.01. With n₁ = 1.48 and Δ = 0.01, δTs/L = 1.48 × 0.01/(3 × 10⁸) = 4.93 × 10⁻¹¹ s/m, that is 49.3 ns per kilometre. The spread grows in proportion to L.

Graded index: fast rays through slow glass

A graded-index core has its highest index on the axis and a smooth fall to n₂ at the core radius a:

n(r) = n₁[1 − 2Δ(r/a)α]1/2 for r < a
n(r) is the index at radius r; a is the core radius; α is the profile parameter (α = 1 triangular, α = 2 parabolic, α → ∞ step).

A ray that swings far from the axis travels a longer path, but most of it lies in lower-index glass where light is faster. With a near-parabolic profile the extra speed almost cancels the extra path, so all rays arrive nearly together. For the near-parabolic profile the delay difference is

δTg ≈ Ln₁Δ²/(8c)

δTg is the intermodal delay difference of the graded fiber in s; the symbols are as above.

The ratio to the step-index value is Δ/8, so for Δ = 0.01 the 49.3 ns/km of step index falls to 61.7 ps/km. Some books give Ln₁Δ²/(2c) for an exactly parabolic profile analysed by a different ray method; it is of the same order, so quote the form your textbook uses and say which one.

RMS width and the bit-rate limit

A real pulse is not rectangular, so engineers also quote its RMS width σ, the standard deviation of its arrival times. For a rectangular spread of total width δT, σ = δT/(2√3). The two standard results are

σs = Ln₁Δ/(2√3 c)

σs is the RMS intermodal broadening of a step-index fiber in s.

σg = Ln₁Δ²/(20√3 c)

σg is the RMS broadening of a graded fiber with the optimum profile, α ≈ 2 − 12Δ/5, in s.

The ratio σs/σg is 10/Δ, a thousand at Δ = 0.01. Two bit-rate rules follow, one for each way of measuring the spread:

  • From the full spread: BT ≤ 1/(2δT), so that a pulse spreads over at most half of a bit period of a return-to-zero signal.
  • From the RMS width: BT(max) ≈ 0.2/σ, the usual rule for a Gaussian pulse.

Because the spread grows with L, the product of bit rate and length is roughly fixed for a fiber type. This bandwidth–length product is quoted in Mbit/s·km or MHz·km: doubling the reach halves the bit rate. Independent spreads such as intermodal and chromatic dispersion combine as σ = √(σmodal² + σchromatic²). A single-mode fiber guides one mode, so its intermodal term is zero.

2.6 Chromatic dispersion: material and waveguide

No source emits one pure wavelength: a DFB laser spans a fraction of a nanometre, an LED tens of nanometres. Each wavelength in a pulse travels at its own group velocity, so the pulse arrives stretched. This is chromatic (intramodal) dispersion, and it happens even in a single-mode fiber. The fiber is described by a dispersion coefficient D in ps/(nm·km); the spread it causes, Δτ, is a time in ps. D has two parts: a material part from the glass and a waveguide part from the guiding geometry.

Where the spread comes from

Light energy travels at the group velocity vg = dω/dβ, where β is the propagation constant of the mode and ω the angular frequency. The group delay over a length L is τ = L/vg. Because τ changes with wavelength, two wavelengths Δλ apart arrive at different times. The dispersion coefficient is the change of group delay per kilometre per nanometre:

D = (1/L) dτ/dλ

D is the dispersion coefficient in ps/(nm·km); τ is the group delay in ps; L is in km; λ is in nm.

Δτ = |D| L Δλ

Δτ is the pulse spread in ps; D in ps/(nm·km); L in km; Δλ is the spectral width of the source in nm.

This book takes D > 0 when the longer wavelengths arrive later (the “anomalous” region of standard fiber at 1550 nm). Some books use the opposite sign; fix one convention for a whole calculation. The two parts add with their signs:

D = Dm + Dw

Dm is the material dispersion and Dw the waveguide dispersion, both in ps/(nm·km).

Material dispersion

The index of silica falls as wavelength rises, and the curve n(λ) bends. Material dispersion depends on that bend:

Dm = −(λ/c) d²n/dλ²

λ is the wavelength in m; c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s; d²n/dλ² is the curvature of the index curve in m⁻². The result is in s/m²; 1 s/m² = 10⁶ ps/(nm·km).

For pure silica Dm crosses zero near 1270 nm; germania in the core moves this a little longer. Below the zero Dm is negative, about −85 ps/(nm·km) at 850 nm, where the longer wavelengths arrive first. Above it Dm is positive, about +21 ps/(nm·km) at 1550 nm. Some books write the material spread directly as σm = (σλL/c)|λ d²n/dλ²|, where σλ is the RMS spectral width; it is the same formula.

Waveguide dispersion

In a single-mode fiber part of the power travels in the cladding, and that share depends on the normalised frequency V = (2πa/λ)√(n₁² − n₂²), where a is the core radius. As λ rises, V falls, more of the mode spreads into the lower-index cladding, and its effective index moves from n₁ toward n₂. That change of β with wavelength, caused by geometry alone, is waveguide dispersion.

For a standard step-index single-mode fiber Dw is negative, about −4 ps/(nm·km) across 1300 to 1600 nm. Adding it to Dm pulls the zero of the total from about 1270 nm to about 1310 nm. Dw is the part a designer controls through a and Δ: a smaller core and a larger Δ make it more negative, which is how dispersion-shifted fibers move the zero to 1550 nm.

Near its zero λ₀ standard fiber is described by its zero-dispersion slope S₀, about 0.092 ps/(nm²·km):

D(λ) ≈ (S₀/4)(λ − λ₀⁴/λ³)

D in ps/(nm·km); S₀ is the slope at λ₀ in ps/(nm²·km); λ and λ₀ in nm.

With λ₀ = 1310 nm this gives D = 17.5 ps/(nm·km) at 1550 nm, the familiar “about 17”. If a laser linewidth is given in frequency, convert it with Δλ = λ²Δf/c; at 1550 nm, 10 GHz is 0.080 nm. The bit-rate rule used in this book for a full spread is B ≤ 1/(2Δτ), as for intermodal dispersion.

2.7 Polarization mode dispersion

A single-mode fiber actually carries two modes: the same field pattern polarized along two perpendicular axes. In a perfectly round, stress-free fiber they travel at the same speed. Real fiber is slightly elliptical and stressed, so the two polarizations see slightly different indices and drift apart in time. That delay is polarization mode dispersion (PMD). Along a cable the axes twist at random, so the mean delay grows as the square root of length. Only a few syllabi list PMD, but it limits links at 10 Gbit/s and above.

Birefringence splits the two modes

Drawing and cabling leave the core slightly non-circular and add stress that is not the same in all directions. The index is then nx along one transverse axis and ny along the other. Their difference is the birefringence, written here δn = |nx − ny| (the letter B is kept for the bit rate). The two axes are the principal axes.

Birefringence makes the polarization state change along the fiber. The state repeats after one beat length:

LB = λ/δn

LB is the beat length in m; λ is the wavelength in m; δn is the birefringence (a pure number).

In a short piece of fiber whose axes stay fixed, the delay between the two polarizations, the differential group delay (DGD), grows in proportion to length: Δτ ≈ L δn/c when the dispersion of δn is ignored. With δn = 5 × 10⁻⁷ at 1550 nm the beat length is 3.1 m and the DGD is 1.67 ps per kilometre. Polarization-maintaining fiber does the opposite on purpose: stress rods give δn ≈ 3 × 10⁻⁴, a 5 mm beat length, and the two axes stay locked.

Random axes give a square-root law

In an installed cable the principal axes do not stay fixed. Bends, twists and temperature rotate them every few tens of metres, so power swaps between the two polarizations again and again. Each section adds a small delay that may add to or subtract from the total, like the steps of a random walk. The mean DGD therefore grows as √L:

⟨Δτ⟩ = DPMD √L

⟨Δτ⟩ is the mean DGD in ps; DPMD is the PMD coefficient in ps/√km; L is the length in km.

Modern fiber has DPMD of about 0.02 to 0.1 ps/√km; cable laid before the late 1990s can exceed 0.5 ps/√km. The DGD at any moment is random and follows a Maxwellian distribution. The chance that it exceeds twice the mean is 1.7 %, and three times the mean only 4.2 × 10⁻⁵, but over years of service a link does reach those values for minutes at a time.

A common design rule for direct-detection links keeps the mean DGD below a tenth of the bit period:

DPMD √L ≤ 0.1 Tb

both sides in ps; Tb = 1/B is the bit period, 100 ps at 10 Gbit/s and 25 ps at 40 Gbit/s.

PMD differs from chromatic dispersion in three ways: it scales as √L, not L; it does not depend on the source spectral width; and it changes with time, so it cannot be cancelled by a fixed spool of fiber.

  2. (b) At 10 Gbit/s: Tb = 100 ps, limit 10 ps. The mean of 2 ps passes easily.
  3. At 40 Gbit/s: Tb = 25 ps, limit 2.5 ps. The mean of 2 ps passes, with only 0.5 ps to spare.
  4. (c) Three times the mean is 6 ps, above the 2.5 ps limit. The Maxwellian chance of exceeding 3⟨Δτ⟩ is 4.2 × 10⁻⁵, about 22 minutes a year (4.2 × 10⁻⁵ × 525 600 min).
  5. Even 2⟨Δτ⟩ = 4 ps breaks the limit, with a chance of 1.7 %, so at 40 Gbit/s this link has frequent PMD outages.

Answer. Mean DGD 2 ps: safe at 10 Gbit/s; at 40 Gbit/s the mean passes but the random excursions above 2.5 ps make the link unreliable without PMD compensation.

Worked example 2

Question. A 40 Gbit/s link must keep ⟨Δτ⟩ ≤ 2.5 ps. Find the longest link on fiber with DPMD = 0.1 ps/√km and on older fiber with 0.5 ps/√km.

  1. Rule: √L ≤ 2.5/DPMD, so L ≤ (2.5/DPMD)².
  2. New fiber: 2.5/0.1 = 25, L ≤ 625 km.
  3. Old fiber: 2.5/0.5 = 5, L ≤ 25 km.
  4. The coefficient is five times larger, and the reach is 5² = 25 times shorter.

Answer. 625 km on the new fiber but only 25 km on the old one, which is why old cable is measured for PMD before an upgrade.

Exam traps

  • Using Δτ proportional to L for a cable. Linear growth holds only for a short piece with fixed axes; a real cable gives DPMD√L.
  • Writing DPMD in ps/km. Its unit is ps/√km.
  • Comparing the mean DGD with the whole bit period. The usual rule uses one tenth of it.
  • Treating the mean as the worst case. The DGD is random and its Maxwellian tail exceeds twice the mean 1.7 % of the time.
  • Thinking a narrower laser reduces PMD. PMD does not depend on the spectral width; chromatic dispersion does.

2.8 Dispersion-shifted and dispersion-compensating fibers

Standard single-mode fiber loses least light at 1550 nm but has its dispersion zero near 1310 nm, so a 1550 nm link gets low loss and about 17 ps/(nm·km) of dispersion together. Two fixes exist. Redesign the fiber so its zero moves to 1550 nm (dispersion-shifted fiber), or keep the line fiber and add a short length of fiber with strong negative dispersion that cancels the total (dispersion-compensating fiber). Only a few syllabi list these fibers; the design rule is short and the numbers are easy.

You need: D = Dm + Dw and Δτ = |D|LΔλ from chromatic dispersion.

Dispersion-shifted fiber

At 1550 nm the material term Dm is about +21 ps/(nm·km) and fixed by the glass. To cancel it there, the waveguide term must reach about −21 ps/(nm·km), five times its value in standard fiber. A smaller core (radius about 2 to 3 µm) and a larger index difference do this, often with a triangular or segmented core profile. The result is dispersion-shifted fiber (DSF), with its zero-dispersion wavelength λ₀ near 1550 nm, where the loss is also lowest. It suits one high-speed channel per fiber.

DSF has a weakness with wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM). When every channel sits near zero dispersion, all channels travel at almost the same group velocity and stay in step. Four-wave mixing between them is then phase-matched: three channels mix to create light at new frequencies that land on other channels as crosstalk.

Non-zero dispersion-shifted fiber

Non-zero dispersion-shifted fiber (NZDSF) keeps a small dispersion of about 2 to 6 ps/(nm·km) across the 1530 to 1565 nm band, positive or negative depending on the type. It is small enough that the accumulated spread over a span is a third or less of standard fiber’s, and large enough that channels 0.8 nm apart (100 GHz) drift out of step quickly. With D = 4 ps/(nm·km) two such channels move 4 × 0.8 = 3.2 ps apart per kilometre, so they lose step within a few hundred metres and four-wave mixing stays weak.

The price is a smaller effective area Aeff, about 55 to 72 µm² against 80 µm² for standard fiber, so the light is more intense and other nonlinear effects are a little stronger. How much accumulated dispersion a receiver tolerates depends on the bit rate: of order 1000 ps/nm at 10 Gbit/s for direct detection, and about 16 times less at 40 Gbit/s, since the tolerance scales as 1/B².

Dispersion-compensating fiber

Dispersion-compensating fiber (DCF) leaves the line fiber alone and adds a spool with strong negative dispersion, made with a small core and high Δ: D of about −80 to −100 ps/(nm·km) in common modules. It sits in a module at an amplifier or receiver site. Dispersion accumulates with length, so the span is compensated when

D₁L₁ + D₂L₂ = 0

D₁, L₁ are the dispersion (ps/(nm·km)) and length (km) of the line fiber; D₂, L₂ those of the DCF, with D₂ negative.

For 100 km of standard fiber at +17 ps/(nm·km), 1700 ps/nm must be cancelled: 17 to 20 km of DCF at −100 to −85. Two costs follow. Loss: DCF runs near 0.5 dB/km, so a 20 km module adds about 10 dB. Nonlinearity: its effective area is only about 20 µm². And the cancellation is exact at one wavelength only, unless the DCF slope is matched to the line fiber: the ratio S/D must be the same for both fibers. Always check the edge channels, not only the centre.

Worked example

Question. A 100 km span of standard fiber has D = +17 ps/(nm·km) and α = 0.2 dB/km. The DCF has D = −85 ps/(nm·km) and α = 0.5 dB/km. (a) Find the DCF length for zero total dispersion. (b) Find the span loss. (c) Only 15 km of DCF is available; find the residual dispersion and the spread for a 0.1 nm source.

  1. Line dispersion: 17 × 100 = 1700 ps/nm.
  2. (a) 1700 + (−85)L₂ = 0, so L₂ = 1700/85 = 20 km.
  3. (b) Loss: 0.2 × 100 + 0.5 × 20 = 20 + 10 = 30 dB.
  4. (c) The 15 km spool gives −85 × 15 = −1275 ps/nm; residual 1700 − 1275 = +425 ps/nm.
  5. Spread: 425 ps/nm × 0.1 nm = 42.5 ps, under half the 100 ps bit period at 10 Gbit/s, and well within a 1000 ps/nm tolerance.

Answer. 20 km of DCF for full compensation; 30 dB span loss; with 15 km the residual is 425 ps/nm, a 42.5 ps spread for a 0.1 nm source.

Worked example 2

Question. The 100 km span above has a dispersion slope S = +0.058 ps/(nm²·km). The 20 km DCF that cancels it at 1550 nm has slope −0.10 ps/(nm²·km). Find the residual dispersion at 1530 nm and 1565 nm, and the DCF slope that would cancel both edges.

  1. At wavelength λ, each fiber has D(λ) = D(1550) + S(λ − 1550).
  2. At 1530 nm (λ − 1550 = −20 nm): line 100 × (17 − 1.16) = 1584 ps/nm; DCF 20 × (−85 + 2.0) = −1660 ps/nm; residual −76 ps/nm.
  3. At 1565 nm (+15 nm): line 100 × (17 + 0.87) = 1787 ps/nm; DCF 20 × (−85 − 1.5) = −1730 ps/nm; residual +57 ps/nm.
  4. Slope matching needs SDCF/DDCF = S/D: 0.058/17 = 0.00341 nm⁻¹, so SDCF = −85 × 0.00341 = −0.29 ps/(nm²·km).
  5. Check at 1530 nm: 20 × (−85 + 0.29 × 20) = 20 × (−79.2) = −1584 ps/nm, which cancels the line’s +1584 exactly.

Answer. Residuals of −76 ps/nm at 1530 nm and +57 ps/nm at 1565 nm; a DCF slope of −0.29 ps/(nm²·km) cancels the whole band.

Exam traps

  • Comparing D values without their lengths. 20 km at −85 ps/(nm·km) gives −1700 ps/nm, exactly cancelling 100 km at +17; neither “outweighs” the other.
  • Forgetting the DCF’s own loss (about 0.5 dB/km) and its small effective area.
  • Mixing up DSF (zero at 1550 nm) and NZDSF (small but non-zero dispersion across the band).
  • Dropping the sign: D₂ is negative inside D₁L₁ + D₂L₂ = 0.
  • Assuming perfect compensation for every channel. Without slope matching the band edges keep a residual.

2.9 Nonlinear scattering: stimulated Brillouin and Raman

Above a certain power, silica stops being a passive pipe: the light itself drives two scattering processes. Stimulated Brillouin scattering (SBS) sends a frequency-shifted copy of the signal backward; stimulated Raman scattering (SRS) sends one mainly forward. Both take power from the signal, so each sets a ceiling on the power a fiber can carry. Only a few syllabi list these effects, and the exam asks for the mechanism, the threshold and the comparison. The same physics is used on purpose in Brillouin sensors and Raman amplifiers.

You need: the attenuation coefficient in dB/km and nepers from attenuation, and linear Rayleigh scattering.

Two effects, two directions

Rayleigh scattering is linear and elastic: the scattered light has the signal’s frequency and its share does not depend on power. The nonlinear kinds are inelastic: the scattered photon, called the Stokes wave, has less energy than the signal photon, and the difference goes into the glass. Below threshold this happens spontaneously and weakly. Above threshold the Stokes wave grows exponentially, fed by the signal.

SBS. The signal and the backward Stokes wave beat together, and their interference pattern compresses the glass by electrostriction, driving an acoustic wave. That acoustic wave is a moving index grating which reflects more signal backward, Doppler-shifted down by νB = 2nvA/λ, where vA ≈ 5960 m/s is the speed of sound in silica. At 1550 nm this is about 11 GHz. The Brillouin gain band is only about 20 to 50 MHz wide, so a narrow-line laser puts all its power inside it and reaches threshold at a few milliwatts.

SRS. The signal scatters off molecular vibrations of silica (optical phonons). The Stokes wave is shifted down by about 13 THz, roughly 100 nm at 1550 nm, and travels in both directions, the forward wave growing along with the signal. The Raman gain band is very wide, about 40 THz, and the gain coefficient is small, so the threshold is hundreds of milliwatts.

Threshold power

Only the first part of a span is bright enough to drive these effects, because the signal fades along the fiber. The effective length counts that part:

Leff = (1 − e−αL)/α

Leff is the effective length in m; α is the attenuation coefficient in nepers per metre (dB/km ÷ 4343); L is the physical length in m. For a long span Leff → 1/α, about 21.7 km at 0.2 dB/km.

The threshold is the launch power at which the Stokes wave grows as large as the signal at the output. For a narrow-line source the usual estimates are

Pth,B ≈ 21 Aeff/(gBLeff)
Pth,B is the SBS threshold in W; Aeff is the effective core area in m²; gB ≈ 5 × 10⁻¹¹ m/W is the peak Brillouin gain coefficient.

Pth,R ≈ 16 Aeff/(gRLeff)
Pth,R is the SRS threshold in W; gR ≈ 7 × 10⁻¹⁴ m/W is the peak Raman gain coefficient near 1550 nm.

Many textbooks also give empirical forms in practical units, with d the core diameter in µm, λ in µm, αdB in dB/km and ν the source bandwidth in GHz: PB = 4.4 × 10⁻³ d²λ²αdBν watts and PR = 5.9 × 10⁻² d²λαdB watts. The Brillouin form is for a source much wider than the Brillouin band, so it grows with ν; widening the source spreads its power over many Brillouin bands.

What they do in service

  • SBS caps the power per channel. Above threshold extra launch power is reflected back toward the transmitter, adding noise. Cures: stay below threshold, or dither the laser to widen its spectrum beyond the Brillouin band.
  • SRS acts on the whole WDM band. Short-wavelength channels pump long-wavelength ones, so the spectrum tilts; the limit is on the total launched power.
  • Used on purpose: Brillouin sensors read strain and temperature along a fiber from shifts of νB, and Raman amplifiers pump the transmission fiber about 13 THz above the signal.

Worked example

Question. An 80 km span at 1550 nm has α = 0.2 dB/km and Aeff = 80 µm². Take gB = 5 × 10⁻¹¹ m/W and gR = 7 × 10⁻¹⁴ m/W. (a) Find Leff. (b) Find the SBS and SRS thresholds for a narrow-line source. (c) A 40-channel WDM system launches 4 mW per channel; comment.

  1. (a) α = 0.2 ÷ 4343 = 4.605 × 10⁻⁵ Np/m; αL = 4.605 × 10⁻⁵ × 80 000 = 3.684; e−3.684 = 0.0251.
  2. Leff = (1 − 0.0251) ÷ 4.605 × 10⁻⁵ = 21 170 m = 21.2 km, close to the limit 1/α = 21.7 km.
  3. (b) SBS: 21 × 80 × 10⁻¹² ÷ (5 × 10⁻¹¹ × 21 170) = 1.68 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 1.059 × 10⁻⁶ = 1.59 × 10⁻³ W = 1.6 mW.
  4. SRS: 16 × 80 × 10⁻¹² ÷ (7 × 10⁻¹⁴ × 21 170) = 1.28 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 1.482 × 10⁻⁹ = 0.86 W.
  5. (c) Each channel at 4 mW is above the 1.6 mW SBS threshold, so the lasers must be dithered. The total 40 × 4 = 160 mW is below the 0.86 W single-channel SRS threshold, but across a wide WDM band Raman tilt becomes noticeable at total powers of this order, so the edge channels need gain equalisation.

Answer. Leff = 21.2 km; Pth,B ≈ 1.6 mW and Pth,R ≈ 0.86 W; the link is limited per channel by SBS.

  1. At 1.55 µm: d² = 64 and λ² = 2.4025, so PB = 4.4 × 10⁻³ × 64 × 2.4025 × 0.2 × 0.5 = 0.0677 W = 67.7 mW.
  2. PR = 5.9 × 10⁻² × 64 × 1.55 × 0.2 = 1.17 W.
  3. At 1.31 µm: λ² = 1.7161, so PB = 4.4 × 10⁻³ × 64 × 1.7161 × 0.35 × 0.5 = 0.0846 W = 84.6 mW, and PR = 5.9 × 10⁻² × 64 × 1.31 × 0.35 = 1.73 W.

Exam traps

  • Using the physical length where the formula needs Leff. An 80 km span at 0.2 dB/km acts like 21.2 km.
  • Converting dB/km to nepers per metre wrongly. Divide by 4.343 for Np/km, and by 4343 for Np/m.
  • Swapping the two: Brillouin is backward, about 11 GHz, a band of tens of MHz; Raman is about 13 THz, a band of tens of THz.
  • Converting Aeff wrongly: 80 µm² is 80 × 10⁻¹² m², not 80 × 10⁻⁶ m².
  • Saying SRS limits each channel separately. Its wide band couples all channels, so it limits the total power.

Takeaways

  1. Attenuation is quoted in dB/km so that fiber, splice, connector and bend losses simply add; received dBm = launch dBm − total dB.
  2. Intrinsic absorption belongs to silica itself (UV and IR tails); extrinsic absorption comes from impurities, above all the OH⁻ water peak near 1383 nm.
  3. Rayleigh scattering falls as 1/λ⁴ and the infrared tail rises past 1.6 µm, so the loss minimum, about 0.2 dB/km, sits near 1550 nm.
  4. Bend loss climbs exponentially as the radius falls toward the critical radius, and it is worse at longer wavelengths.
  5. In multimode fiber the spread comes from mode delays: Ln₁Δ/c for step index, about Ln₁Δ²/(8c) for a near-parabolic graded core.
  6. In single-mode fiber the spread is chromatic, Δτ = |D|LΔλ, with D = Dm + Dw, zero near 1310 nm and about 17 ps/(nm·km) at 1550 nm.
  7. PMD is random and grows as √L; it matters for long links at 10 Gbit/s and above.
  8. Dispersion-shifted fiber moves the zero to 1550 nm, NZDSF keeps a little dispersion for WDM, and DCF cancels the accumulated dispersion of a span.
  9. Stimulated Brillouin scattering limits the power per channel at a few mW; stimulated Raman scattering limits the total power at near 1 W.

Understand Define intermodal dispersion and chromatic dispersion. State the unit in which each is quoted, the fibers in which each occurs, and give one number for each. 8 marks

Model answer.

  1. Dispersion is the spreading of a light pulse in time as it travels. A spread pulse overlaps its neighbours and limits the bit rate.
  2. Intermodal dispersion. In a multimode fiber the pulse power is shared among many modes, and each mode (or ray) takes a different time to cross the fiber. In a step-index fiber the axial ray takes Ln₁/c and the steepest guided ray Ln₁²/(cn₂), so δTs ≈ Ln₁Δ/c. It is quoted as a delay per length, in ns/km, or as a bandwidth–length product in MHz·km. It exists only in multimode fiber.
  3. Chromatic (intramodal) dispersion. Within one mode, the different wavelengths of the source spectrum travel at different group velocities. It is described by the coefficient D = (1/L) dτ/dλ in ps/(nm·km), and the spread is Δτ = |D|LΔλ in ps. It occurs in every fiber, single-mode included.
  4. Chromatic dispersion has two parts: material dispersion Dm = −(λ/c) d²n/dλ² from the glass, and waveguide dispersion Dw from the change of mode confinement with λ. In standard fiber they cancel near 1310 nm.
  5. One number for each.
    MechanismExampleResult
    Intermodal, step indexn₁ = 1.48, Δ = 0.01, 1 km1000 × 1.48 × 0.01 ÷ 3 × 10⁸ = 49.3 ns
    Chromatic, single-modeD = 17 ps/(nm·km), 100 km, 0.1 nm17 × 100 × 0.1 = 170 ps
  6. A third mechanism. Polarization mode dispersion comes from the two polarizations of the single mode travelling at slightly different speeds; it is random and grows as √L.
  7. Why it matters. Each spread sets a bit-rate ceiling through B ≤ 1/(2 × spread). The 49.3 ns of one kilometre of step-index fiber allows only 1/(2 × 49.3 ns) = 10.1 Mbit/s, while the 170 ps of the single-mode example allows 1/(2 × 170 ps) = 2.94 Gbit/s. A ray that swings far from the axis travels a longer, curved path, but most of it lies in lower-index glass where the speed c/n(r) is higher. With α near 2 the extra speed nearly cancels the extra path, so the residual difference is second order in Δ: δTg ≈ Ln₁Δ²/(8c). (Some books quote Ln₁Δ²/(2c) for an exactly parabolic profile by another ray method; the order is the same.)
  4. RMS forms. σs = Ln₁Δ/(2√3 c) and, for the optimum profile α ≈ 2 − 12Δ/5, σg = Ln₁Δ²/(20√3 c). The ratio σs/σg = 10/Δ.

  7. Practical limits. The optimum profile depends on wavelength (profile dispersion), so a fiber optimised at 850 nm is worse at 1300 nm. Small errors in α, a dip or a spike of index on the axis, and differential mode attenuation spoil the cancellation. Real graded fiber reaches a few hundred MHz·km to a few GHz·km, not the ideal 8 Gbit/s·km.

Evaluate Why is 1550 nm the preferred window for long-haul links even though standard fiber has its zero-dispersion wavelength near 1310 nm? Evaluate the loss and dispersion of a 100 km span in each window and discuss how the trade-off is resolved. 16 marks

Points to cover. Loss mechanisms against wavelength: Rayleigh, IR tail, water peak; Span loss at 1310 and 1550 nm; Dispersion at each window, D = Dm + Dw; Amplifiers at 1550 nm; Resolutions: DSF, NZDSF, DCF, coherent DSP; FWM penalty; Judgement with numbers.

Model answer.

  1. Loss against wavelength. Rayleigh scattering falls as 1/λ⁴ (about 0.27 dB/km at 1310 nm and 0.14 dB/km at 1550 nm for C = 0.8 dB·km⁻¹·µm⁴). The infrared multiphonon tail is negligible at 1310 nm, about 0.02 dB/km at 1550 nm, and rises steeply beyond 1600 nm. The water peak near 1383 nm lies between the two windows. So the loss minimum, about 0.2 dB/km, is near 1550 nm.
  2. Dispersion against wavelength. Material dispersion of silica crosses zero near 1270 nm and reaches about +21 ps/(nm·km) at 1550 nm. Waveguide dispersion of a standard step-index fiber is about −4 ps/(nm·km). Their sum is zero near 1310 nm and about +17 ps/(nm·km) at 1550 nm.

  4. Near 1310 nm the slope formula gives D(1300) = −0.93 and D(1320) = +0.91 ps/(nm·km), so a 100 km span collects under 100 ps/nm. At 1550 nm the 15 dB loss saving per 100 km is the decisive gain: amplifier spans of 80 to 100 km instead of about 55 km.
  5. The amplifier argument. Erbium-doped fiber amplifiers amplify the whole 1530 to 1565 nm band at once, so many WDM channels share one amplifier. Once amplification is cheap, loss sets the spacing of amplifier sites and dispersion becomes a design problem to solve, not a wall.
  6. Resolutions. (1) Dispersion-shifted fiber: a small core and large Δ make Dw about −21 ps/(nm·km), moving the zero to 1550 nm; ideal for one channel, but in WDM every channel travels in step and four-wave mixing grows. (2) NZDSF: 2 to 6 ps/(nm·km) across the band keeps channels walking off (3.2 ps per km for 0.8 nm spacing at 4 ps/(nm·km)) while the accumulated dispersion is a third or less of standard fiber’s. (3) DCF: 20 km at −85 ps/(nm·km) cancels the 1700 ps/nm of the span, at the cost of 10 dB of extra loss, made up by the amplifier. (4) Coherent detection with digital equalisation undoes dispersion in the receiver, so new links use plain standard fiber.
  7. Evaluation. At 10 Gbit/s the receiver tolerates about 1000 ps/nm, so an uncompensated 1550 nm span is limited to about 1000/17 ≈ 59 km; with DCF or digital compensation the reach becomes thousands of kilometres. At 1310 nm the dispersion would allow long spans, but 35 dB per 100 km with no practical amplifier makes repeaters necessary every 50 to 60 km.
  8. Conclusion. Loss can be fixed only by amplification, and the amplifier works at 1550 nm; dispersion can be fixed by fiber design or signal processing. So the long-haul industry chose 1550 nm and manages its dispersion.
  9. A 400 km system, both ways. At 1550 nm: four spans of 100 km, 20 dB each, with three in-line EDFAs and DCF (or a coherent receiver) for the 6800 ps/nm in total. At 1310 nm with a 0 dBm launch and a −28 dBm receiver: each regenerator section can be 28 ÷ 0.35 = 80 km, so five sections and four electronic regenerators, each tied to one bit rate and one channel. The amplified 1550 nm system can later carry many more WDM channels through the same amplifiers.
  10. Role of the water peak. In older fiber the OH⁻ band near 1383 nm, about 1 dB/km or more, separated the two windows; low-water-peak fiber now makes the whole 1260 to 1625 nm range usable for coarse WDM in metro links, where spans are short and dispersion matters less.

Evaluate Explain stimulated Brillouin and stimulated Raman scattering in optical fibers. Compare their mechanisms, directions, shifts and thresholds, evaluate both thresholds for an 80 km span, and state how each affects a WDM link. 13 marks

Points to cover. Inelastic scattering, Stokes wave, threshold behaviour; SBS: electrostriction, acoustic grating, backward, about 11 GHz, narrow band; SRS: optical phonons, about 13 THz, broad band; Leff and threshold formulas with numbers; Effects on WDM and remedies.

Model answer.

  1. Common idea. Both are inelastic: a signal photon gives part of its energy to the glass as a phonon and leaves as a lower-frequency Stokes photon. At low power this is weak and spontaneous; above a threshold the Stokes wave is amplified exponentially by the signal and takes a large share of its power.
  2. SBS. The signal and a backward Stokes wave interfere; the moving intensity pattern compresses the glass by electrostriction and drives an acoustic wave. That wave is a moving Bragg grating that reflects more signal backward, Doppler-shifted down by νB = 2nvA/λ = 2 × 1.45 × 5960 ÷ 1.55 × 10⁻⁶ = 11.2 GHz. The gain bandwidth is only 20 to 50 MHz, set by the short life of the acoustic phonon.
  3. SRS. The signal scatters off molecular vibrations of silica (optical phonons). The shift is about 13 THz (about 100 nm at 1550 nm), the gain band about 40 THz wide, and the Stokes wave grows mainly forward with the signal. The Raman gain coefficient is about 700 times smaller than the Brillouin one.

SBS: each channel’s band lies inside one Brillouin gain band, so each channel is limited separately; above about 1.6 mW the extra launch power is reflected toward the transmitter, adding noise and possibly destabilising the laser. SRS: the Raman band covers the whole C-band, so short-wavelength channels pump long-wavelength ones; the spectrum tilts and channels exchange crosstalk, and the limit is on the total power.

For SBS: dither or phase-modulate the laser so its spectrum is GHz wide (the empirical threshold 4.4 × 10⁻³ d²λ²αdBν grows with the source bandwidth ν: 68 mW for d = 8 µm, 0.2 dB/km and ν = 0.5 GHz), or use a larger Aeff. Glossary

Attenuation
The fall of optical power with distance along a fiber, quoted as a coefficient in dB per kilometre.
Birefringence
The index difference δn between the two principal polarization axes of a fiber; it sets the beat length λ/δn.
Chromatic dispersion
Pulse spreading because the group velocity depends on wavelength; measured in ps/(nm·km).
Critical radius
The bend radius below which bending loss rises sharply; about 3n₁²λ/[4π(n₁² − n₂²)3/2] for multimode fiber and a few centimetres for single-mode fiber at 1550 nm.
dBm
An absolute power level referred to 1 mW, so 0 dBm = 1 mW and −3 dBm ≈ 0.5 mW.
Decibel (dB)
A logarithmic power ratio, 10·log₁₀(P₂/P₁), used so that cascaded losses add instead of multiply.
Differential group delay
The arrival-time difference between the two polarization modes, in picoseconds.
Dispersion-compensating fiber
Fiber with large negative dispersion, spliced in to cancel the accumulated positive dispersion of a link.
Dispersion-shifted fiber
Fiber designed so that its zero-dispersion wavelength falls at 1550 nm, in the lowest-loss window.
Effective core area
The area Aeff over which the mode power is effectively spread, about 80 µm² in standard single-mode fiber; it sets the intensity and so the nonlinear thresholds.
Effective length
The length Leff = (1 − e−αL)/α over which the signal is strong enough to drive nonlinear effects; about 21 km for a long span at 0.2 dB/km.
Extrinsic absorption
Absorption caused by impurities in the glass, chiefly OH⁻ ions and transition-metal ions.
Graded-index fiber
Multimode fiber whose core index decreases smoothly from the axis, equalising modal delays.
Intermodal dispersion
Pulse spreading in multimode fiber because different modes take different paths and arrive at different times.
Intrinsic absorption
Absorption caused by the silica network itself, through ultraviolet electronic transitions and infrared vibrational transitions.
Low-water-peak fiber
Fiber drawn from a dried preform so that the 1383 nm OH⁻ peak falls to about the 1310 nm loss, making 1260 to 1625 nm usable.
Macrobending
Loss caused by curving the whole fiber to a small radius, so the field on the outside of the bend radiates away.
Material dispersion
The part of chromatic dispersion coming from the wavelength dependence of the glass refractive index.
Microbending
Loss caused by small random kinks along the fiber axis, usually from cabling or coating stress.
Mie scattering
Scattering from defects or bubbles comparable to the wavelength, with a much weaker wavelength dependence than Rayleigh scattering.
Mode
One field pattern that can travel along the fiber, with its own propagation constant and group velocity.
Non-zero dispersion-shifted fiber
Fiber with a small dispersion, about 2 to 6 ps/(nm·km), across the 1550 nm band, so WDM channels walk off and four-wave mixing stays weak.
OH⁻ absorption peak
The strongest absorption band of the hydroxyl ion, near 1383 nm; weaker OH⁻ peaks lie near 950 and 720 nm, with a combination band near 1240 nm.
Optical window
A wavelength band where fiber loss is low, notably around 850 nm, 1310 nm and 1550 nm.
Polarization mode dispersion
Random delay between the two polarization modes of a real, slightly non-circular fiber; grows as the square root of length.
Rayleigh scattering
Scattering from density fluctuations much smaller than the wavelength, with loss proportional to 1/λ⁴.
Stimulated Brillouin scattering
Backward scattering from a light-driven acoustic wave, shifted down by about 11 GHz at 1550 nm, with a threshold of a few milliwatts for a narrow-line source.
Stimulated Raman scattering
Scattering from molecular vibrations, shifted down by about 13 THz, with a threshold near 1 W in one channel; useful for amplification but a source of crosstalk in WDM.
Waveguide dispersion
The part of chromatic dispersion coming from the way the mode's propagation constant depends on wavelength in a given core geometry.
Zero-dispersion wavelength
The wavelength at which material and waveguide dispersion cancel; about 1310 nm for standard single-mode fiber, while silica’s material zero alone is near 1270 nm.
