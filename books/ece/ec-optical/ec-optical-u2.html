2.1 Attenuation in optical fibers

Attenuation is the loss of optical power as light travels down a fiber. It is quoted as a coefficient in dB per kilometre, because a receiver needs a minimum power and every kilometre takes some away. The coefficient decides how far a link can reach before an amplifier or repeater is needed. A 1550 nm long-haul system, a 1310 nm campus link and a short 850 nm data link all use the same arithmetic.

You need: Unit 1 for the ray picture, total internal reflection and the meaning of guided power.

Loss in decibels

Let Pin be the optical power launched into a fiber of length L km, and Pout the power that leaves the far end. Both powers use the same unit, for example mW or µW. The attenuation coefficient is the loss per kilometre:

α = (10 / L) log₁₀(P<sub>in</sub> / P<sub>out</sub>)

α is the attenuation coefficient in dB/km; L is the fiber length in km; Pin is the input power; Pout is the output power in the same unit; log₁₀ is the base-10 logarithm.

The loss over the whole length is A = α L dB, with A the total loss in dB. To go the other way, Pout = Pin × 10−A/10. Read 10−A/10 as ten raised to the power (−A/10), not as 10 minus A/10.

Two anchor points make the decibel useful. A loss of 3 dB halves the power: 10 log₁₀(2) = 3.01 dB, so 3 dB means Pout/Pin is about 0.5. A loss of 10 dB divides power by ten. A gain of 3 dB doubles power. A 3 dB coupler is called that because each output is 3 dB (about half the power) below the input, which is an equal split between two ports.

Four loss families add in dB

Four families cover almost every loss in a fiber link. Each family gives its own dB value, and the total loss is the sum.

  • Absorption: intrinsic silica absorption and impurity absorption (see 2.2).
  • Scattering: Rayleigh and Mie scattering (see 2.3).
  • Bending: radiation loss when the fiber is curved (see 2.4).
  • Joint and connector loss: loss where two fibers meet (see Unit 3, Fiber joints, connectors and misalignment losses).
A<sub>total</sub> = A<sub>abs</sub> + A<sub>scat</sub> + A<sub>bend</sub> + A<sub>joint</sub>

Atotal is the total loss in dB; each term on the right is that family's loss in dB.

The same addition works for coefficients in dB/km: αtotal = αabs + αscat + αbend + αjoint. This works because dB is logarithmic: multiplying powers becomes adding dB. Do not add powers in mW and then convert; add the dB losses first, then convert once. A full link power budget, which combines these dB values with receiver sensitivity, is Unit 5.

Standard windows and the water peak

Silica fiber is not equally clear at all wavelengths. Three transmission windows matter in practice. The table also lists the water peak, which is a loss feature, not a usable window.

Some syllabi give α in nepers per metre. Using the power definition Pout = Pin e−αNpL, 1 dB = 0.2303 nepers, and 1 Np = 4.343 dB. To convert a dB/km value to Np/km, multiply by 0.2303; to Np/m, divide by 1000 after that.

Worked example

Question. A 1550 nm link uses fiber with α = 0.2 dB/km and launches 1 mW. (a) Find the loss over 80 km. (b) Find the power arriving at the far end. (c) Find the maximum length before the power falls to 1 µW.

  1. The loss over length L is A = α L. Here α = 0.2 dB/km and L = 80 km, so A = 0.2 × 80 = 16 dB.
  2. The output power is Pout = Pin × 10^(−A/10). With Pin = 1 mW, Pout = 1 mW × 10^(−16/10) = 1 mW × 10^(−1.6).
  3. 10^(−1.6) = 0.025118..., so Pout = 0.0251 mW, which is about 25 µW.
  4. For the last part, the allowed loss is the dB ratio between 1 mW and 1 µW. Convert 1 µW to 0.001 mW, so allowed loss = 10 log₁₀(1 mW / 0.001 mW) = 10 log₁₀(1000) = 30 dB.
  5. With the same fiber, Lmax = 30 dB / 0.2 dB/km = 150 km.

Answer. The loss over 80 km is 16 dB; the received power is about 0.0251 mW ≈ 25 µW; the maximum length before the power falls to 1 µW is 150 km.

Exam traps

  • Writing A = α / L instead of A = α L. α is per kilometre, so multiplying by L kilometres cancels the km and leaves dB.
  • Mixing dB and dB/km in the same sum. Convert each loss to dB first; α values in dB/km must be multiplied by their lengths before adding.
  • Adding powers in mW and then taking a logarithm. Powers multiply through the fiber, so losses add in dB; convert only at the end.
  • Forgetting that a loss is positive. Since Pout is less than Pin, log₁₀(Pin/Pout) is positive; the formula with Pout/Pin gives a negative dB and must be read as loss.
  • Using 3 dB as exactly half. It is 3.01 dB; in a chain of many components the 0.01 dB can add up.

2.2 Absorption losses: intrinsic and extrinsic

Absorption loss is light turned into heat inside the glass. Pure silica absorbs strongly in the ultraviolet and infrared, and impurities add narrow or broad bands in between. The two silica tails leave a transparent window from roughly 0.4 µm to 1.6 µm, and the OH⁻ ion puts a famous peak near 1380 nm inside it. Engineers use this picture to choose a wavelength and judge whether a fiber is old or modern.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the attenuation coefficient in dB/km, and Unit 1 for the picture of silica as a random SiO₂ network.

Intrinsic absorption: two silica tails

Intrinsic absorption comes from the SiO₂ network itself, with no impurity needed. It has two parts.

The ultraviolet tail is electronic. Photons with enough energy lift electrons across the band gap of silica. This absorption is very strong at short wavelengths and falls steeply as wavelength rises. By about 0.4 µm it has dropped enough to stop dominating.

The infrared tail is vibrational. The Si–O bond has resonant vibrations, and multiphonon absorption lets one photon excite several vibrational quanta. This tail is weak at 1.3 µm, then rises steeply beyond about 1.6 µm.

Between the two tails silica is transparent from roughly 0.4 µm to 1.6 µm. The loss minimum of silica sits near 1.55 µm, between the two tails. Only a few universities teach the tail physics in detail; the exam usually asks the origin and the wavelength range.

P(z) = P₀ exp(−αz)

P₀ is the launched power in watts; P(z) is the power after distance z in metres; α is the absorption coefficient in nepers per metre.

Extrinsic absorption: impurities and the water peak

Extrinsic absorption comes from impurities in the glass. The most important is the hydroxyl ion, OH⁻. Its fundamental vibration sits near 2.73 µm. Overtones of that vibration appear near 1.38 µm, 0.95 µm and 0.72 µm. The 1.38 µm overtone is the strongest and is called the water peak. It adds loss where systems may want to work.

Metal ions also absorb. Fe²⁺, Cu²⁺, Cr³⁺ and Ni²⁺ give broad bands across the visible and near infrared. Old glass could lose several dB/km from them. Modern processing has pushed metal absorption below 0.01 dB/km, so in today's fiber only the OH⁻ overtones matter.

The water peak is not a wall. A low-water-peak fiber reduces the OH⁻ band, and a system can avoid 1380 nm by using 1310 nm or 1550 nm. The choice then depends on scattering and dispersion, not on absorption alone.

Some syllabi call these fundamental and impurity absorption.

2.3 Scattering losses: Rayleigh and Mie

Scattering throws light out of the fiber core without absorbing it. Two linear mechanisms matter: Rayleigh scattering from density and composition fluctuations frozen into the glass, and Mie scattering from imperfections about the size of the wavelength. Rayleigh loss falls steeply as 1/λ⁴, so it sets the loss floor near 1550 nm and explains why early 850 nm systems needed short spans. Mie loss is weaker and is kept small by good fabrication. Every link budget uses these numbers; see Unit 5 for the budget itself.

You need: the attenuation coefficient and dB/km from Chapter 2.1, and where scattering sits beside absorption from Chapter 2.2.

The mechanism: light leaves the core

An optical fiber is not perfectly uniform. When silica glass cools, tiny regions freeze with slightly different density and composition from their neighbours. Each region has a slightly different refractive index. Light passing through sees a random array of weak index steps. Each step redirects a small part of the wave in another direction. Most of that redirected light leaves the core and is lost. The process is elastic: the scattered light keeps the same wavelength as the incident light. No energy goes into heat.

Rayleigh scattering uses fluctuations much smaller than the wavelength, roughly molecular scale. Mie scattering uses larger imperfections, comparable to the wavelength: rough core–cladding boundary, bubbles, unreacted particles or stress. Both are called linear scattering because the loss does not depend on the optical power. Raise the input power and the dB/km stays the same. Power-dependent scattering is a different topic; see Chapter 2.9.

Rayleigh scattering and the 1/λ⁴ law

The standard Rayleigh coefficient for a glass is given below. The exact constants differ with glass composition. The part to carry into an exam is αR ∝ 1/λ⁴. Double the wavelength and the Rayleigh loss falls by 2⁴ = 16.

α<sub>R</sub> = (8π³ / 3λ⁴) · n⁸ p² β<sub>c</sub> k T<sub>f</sub>

αR is the Rayleigh scattering loss coefficient; λ is the free-space wavelength; n is the refractive index; p is the photoelastic coefficient; βc is the isothermal compressibility; k is Boltzmann's constant; Tf is the fictive temperature at which the glass froze.

Typical silica fiber numbers: about 1.9 dB/km at 850 nm, 0.34 dB/km at 1310 nm, and 0.17 dB/km at 1550 nm. These are consistent with 1/λ⁴ scaling. At 1550 nm, Rayleigh plus the infrared absorption tail gives the familiar floor near 0.2 dB/km. That floor is why long-haul links use the 1550 nm window, and why the same fiber is worse at 850 nm. Rayleigh is a lower limit for a given glass; better drawing cannot remove it because it comes from frozen disorder.

Mie scattering: size matters

Imperfections of the order of the wavelength scatter by the Mie process. Sources include a rough core–cladding boundary, bubbles, unreacted particles in the preform, and stress. Mie loss has a weaker wavelength dependence than Rayleigh, roughly 1/λ² in the worst case, and sometimes almost flat over a short band. It is not a fundamental floor of pure glass. Good fabrication keeps it below the Rayleigh term in ordinary telecom fiber. A measured Mie term that is large usually points to a process problem, not to the glass itself.

Compare the two on a log plot. Rayleigh dives as wavelength rises; Mie falls slowly and stays low. At short wavelengths, Rayleigh can dominate strongly. At long wavelengths, the infrared absorption tail takes over. Between them lies the low-loss window.

Worked example

Question. A silica fiber has a Rayleigh loss of 0.17 dB/km at 1550 nm and no measurable Mie term. (a) Estimate the Rayleigh loss at 1310 nm. (b) Estimate it at 850 nm. (c) The measured total loss at 850 nm is 2.5 dB/km; how much loss is left for other mechanisms? Use αR ∝ 1/λ⁴.

  1. Write the scaling: αR(λ) = αR(1550) × (1550/λ)⁴, with λ in nanometres.
  2. (a) For λ = 1310 nm, the ratio is (1550/1310)⁴ = (1.1832)⁴ = 1.958. Then αR = 0.17 × 1.958 = 0.333 dB/km ≈ 0.33 dB/km.
  3. (b) For λ = 850 nm, the ratio is (1550/850)⁴ = (1.8235)⁴ = 11.06. Then αR = 0.17 × 11.06 = 1.88 dB/km ≈ 1.9 dB/km.
  4. (c) Other loss = 2.5 − 1.88 = 0.62 dB/km ≈ 0.6 dB/km. This is an upper bound on Mie plus absorption and other terms at 850 nm.

Answer. About 0.33 dB/km at 1310 nm, 1.9 dB/km at 850 nm, and 0.6 dB/km left for other mechanisms at 850 nm.

Exam traps

  • Writing λ⁴ instead of 1/λ⁴: loss falls as wavelength rises, not rises.
  • Saying Mie scattering dominates an ordinary telecom fiber. Good fiber keeps Mie below Rayleigh.
  • Confusing scattering with absorption: scattering redirects light, absorption turns it into heat.
  • Mixing dB and dB/km. The coefficient is per kilometre; a 10 km span multiplies the dB loss, not the dB/km figure.
  • Treating Rayleigh loss as power-dependent. Linear scattering is fixed by the glass; stimulated scattering is a high-power effect in Chapter 2.9.

2.4 Bending losses

Bending loss is the extra attenuation caused when an optical fiber is curved. The guided light no longer sees a perfectly straight core, so part of its field reaches the cladding and escapes. Two forms exist: macro bending, where the whole fiber follows a large curve, and micro bending, where small random wiggles disturb the core. Every installed cable has bends at trays, cabinets and wall corners. Bending loss tells the designer how tight a bend is safe before the received power falls below the receiver's sensitivity.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for dB and loss budgets, and Unit 1 for total internal reflection, critical angle and numerical aperture.

Macro bending: one large curve

Macro bending, also called macrocurvature loss, happens when the whole fiber follows a curve of radius R. On the outside of the curve, the guided mode must bend around the corner. Its phase front tilts and its tail extends further into the cladding. If that tail is beyond the region where total internal reflection can hold it, light tunnels out and radiates away. The loss grows as R falls.

α_bend = A e^(−BR)

α_bend is the bend loss per turn in dB; R is the bend radius in mm; A is a constant in dB per turn for that fiber and wavelength; B is a constant in mm⁻¹. A and B come from measurement, not from a universal table.

The curve is flat at large R and turns upward sharply near a critical radius R_c. A useful estimate is:

R_c ≈ (3 n₁² λ) / (4π (n₁² − n₂²)^(3/2))

R_c is the critical bend radius; n₁ is the core refractive index; n₂ is the cladding refractive index; λ is the free-space wavelength in the same length unit as R_c; π is the constant 3.1416.

Below R_c, loss per turn becomes large; above it, loss is usually small. A common shop-floor check wraps a few turns around a 10 mm mandrel and measures the added loss. A 10 mm radius may be harmless; a 5 mm radius can already cost noticeable loss at 1550 nm. Cable trays, splice cassettes, wall corners and spare-fiber loops are the usual places where R becomes too small.

Micro bending and wavelength

Micro bending, or microcurvature loss, comes from many small random bends along the fiber. The coating, cabling, temperature changes and lateral pressure can all create them. Each wiggle is tiny, but together they act like a weak grating. They couple the guided core mode to cladding modes and radiation modes, so power leaks out along the length.

Bending loss rises with wavelength. At 1550 nm the mode field is wider and reaches further into the cladding than at 1310 nm, so a 1550 nm system suffers more from the same bend. This is one reason 1310 nm links tolerate tighter installation than 1550 nm links.

Bend-insensitive fiber adds a low-index trench in the cladding. The trench pulls the mode back toward the core, so less field reaches the outer cladding at a bend. Such fiber is used in drop cables, patch cords and crowded data-center trays.

The standard test is still simple: wrap a few turns around a mandrel, measure power with and without the wrap, and divide by the number of turns. Record the wavelength, radius and number of turns with the result.

Using bend loss in a link budget

Bend loss is quoted as dB per turn at a stated radius and wavelength. It is not a loss per kilometre. Before adding it to a link budget, multiply by the number of turns. A single turn may be a fraction of a dB; several turns can add more than 1 dB, which can exceed the margin of a short link.

  1. Find the tightest bend radius R in the route.
  2. Read α_bend per turn at the operating wavelength from the fiber data or a measured curve.
  3. Count the turns N at that radius.
  4. Add N α_bend dB to the loss budget.
  5. If the margin is small, increase R, shorten the route or use bend-insensitive fiber.

Compare this with Chapter 2.1: fiber attenuation is about 0.2 dB/km at 1550 nm, so 1 dB of bend loss equals the attenuation of roughly 5 km of fiber. That is why installation rules matter as much as the fiber's dB/km figure. See Unit 5, Link Design and Fiber Measurements, for the full power budget.

Worked example

Question. A 1550 nm fiber has a measured bend-loss curve α = 8 e^(−0.6R) dB per turn, with R in mm. (a) Find the loss for one turn at R = 5 mm and at R = 10 mm. (b) Find the extra loss when a cable tray forces four turns at R = 5 mm. (c) Say why the same tray hurts less at 1310 nm.

  1. Given: α(R) = 8 e^(−0.6R) dB per turn, with R in mm.
  2. At R = 5 mm: exponent = −0.6 × 5 = −3; e^(−3) = 0.04979; α = 8 × 0.04979 = 0.3983 dB per turn ≈ 0.40 dB per turn.
  3. At R = 10 mm: exponent = −0.6 × 10 = −6; e^(−6) = 0.002479; α = 8 × 0.002479 = 0.01983 dB per turn ≈ 0.020 dB per turn.
  4. Four turns at R = 5 mm: total extra loss = 4 × 0.3983 = 1.5932 dB ≈ 1.6 dB.
  5. At 1310 nm the same tray hurts less because bending loss rises with wavelength; the shorter 1310 nm wavelength sees lower loss than 1550 nm at the same radius.

Answer. One turn loses 0.40 dB at 5 mm and 0.020 dB at 10 mm; four turns at 5 mm add 1.6 dB, and the same tray is less lossy at 1310 nm because bend loss rises with wavelength.

Exam traps

  • Bend loss is not worse at shorter wavelength: it rises with wavelength, so 1550 nm suffers more than 1310 nm at the same radius.
  • α is per turn, not total: multiply by the number of turns before adding it to the link budget.
  • Macro bending is one large curve; micro bending is many small random curves. Do not treat a tight mandrel test as a micro-bending measurement.
  • Keep R and B in matching units; if B is per mm, R must be in mm.
  • Do not add a bend loss in dB to an attenuation coefficient in dB/km as if both were per km.

2.5 Intermodal dispersion

One short pulse of light entering a multimode fiber leaves stretched. Different modes take different zig-zag paths, so they arrive at different times: the axial ray first, the steepest guided ray last. This is intermodal dispersion, also called modal dispersion. It grows with length L and fractional index difference Δ, and caps the bit rate of a multimode link. Graded-index profiles shrink it by a factor of about 2/Δ — roughly two hundred times at Δ = 0.01 — and a single-mode fiber removes it completely.

You need: Guided modes, numerical aperture, and the step-versus-graded index profile from Unit 1, Foundations: Light Propagation in Fibers.

Two rays, two arrival times

A multimode step-index fiber carries many modes. Only the two extremes set the spread. The axial ray runs straight along the core. Its path is the fiber length L, so it arrives at t₁ = L n₁ / c. Here n₁ is the core refractive index (a pure number), L is length in metres, and c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s.

The steepest guided ray still obeys total internal reflection. At the limiting angle, cos θ = n₂ / n₁, where n₂ is the cladding index. Its zig-zag path is L / cos θ = L n₁ / n₂, and it travels at speed c / n₁. So t₂ = (L n₁ / n₂)(n₁ / c) = L n₁² / (c n₂).

ΔT = t₂ − t₁ = (L n₁ / c) × (n₁ / n₂ − 1) ≈ L n₁ Δ / c

ΔT is the delay difference in seconds; t₁, t₂ are arrival times in seconds; L is length in metres; n₁, n₂ are core and cladding indices; Δ = (n₁ − n₂)/n₁ is the fractional index difference, a pure number of order 0.01; c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s.

The approximation is safe because n₂ is within 1–2 % of n₁. Put in n₁ = 1.48 and Δ = 0.01: ΔT / L = 1.48 × 0.01 / (3 × 10⁸) = 4.93 × 10⁻¹¹ s per metre, about 50 ns per kilometre. A 5 km link smears each pulse by roughly 250 ns, too wide for a 100 ns bit slot.

Graded index: fast rays through slow glass

A graded-index core has highest index on the axis and a smooth fall toward the cladding. Rays far from the axis travel longer paths in lower-index glass, where speed c / n is higher. The longer path is partly cancelled by the higher average speed, so those rays catch up with the axial ray.

ΔT = L n₁ Δ² / (2 c)

ΔT is delay spread in seconds; L is length in metres; n₁ is axial core index; Δ is fractional index difference; c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s. Valid for the best profile shape, α ≈ 2, where n(r) = n₁[1 − 2Δ(r/a)^α]^(1/2), r is radial distance and a is core radius.

Compare the two results. The ratio is Δ/2, so Δ = 0.01 gives about 200 times improvement. Per kilometre: 1.48 × (0.01)² / (2 × 3 × 10⁸) = 2.47 × 10⁻¹³ s per metre, about 0.25 ns per kilometre instead of 50. The figure plots both lines. This is why graded-index multimode fiber served campus LANs and data centres for years; the cost is a tightly controlled index profile during drawing.

Bit rate limit, RMS width, and adding spreads

A bit slot must be longer than the spread, or pulse 1 spills into slot 2. The working rule below uses factor 2 as a safety margin. Because ΔT grows with L, B·L is roughly constant for a fiber type — the bandwidth–length product, quoted in MHz·km. Doubling reach halves bit rate.

B ≤ 1 / (2 ΔT)

B is bit rate in bit/s; ΔT is total pulse spread in seconds. Both times — the spread ΔT and the bit period 1/B — must be in the same unit (both ns, or both s) before you compare them.

A real pulse is not rectangular. Engineers quote RMS width σ, the standard deviation in time of its optical power. Independent spreads add in quadrature, never directly.

σ_total = √(σ_modal² + σ_chromatic²)

σ_total, σ_modal, σ_chromatic are RMS pulse widths in seconds; σ_modal is this chapter's intermodal term (about ΔT/√12 for a rectangular pulse); σ_chromatic is the source-spectral-width term, covered in 2.6.

A single-mode fiber guides only one mode, so σ_modal = 0 and no intermodal term exists. That is why single-mode fiber wins on long haul. Unit 5, Link Design and Fiber Measurements, combines all terms into the rise-time budget.

Worked example

Question. A step-index multimode fiber has core index n₁ = 1.48, fractional index difference Δ = 0.01 and length L = 5 km. (a) Find the intermodal delay difference ΔT. (b) Estimate the maximum bit rate using B ≤ 1/(2ΔT). (c) Repeat both parts for a graded-index fiber with the optimum near-parabolic profile.

  1. Use SI units: L = 5000 m, n₁ = 1.48, Δ = 0.01, c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s.

  6. (c) B ≤ 1 / (2 × 1.233 × 10⁻⁹) = 4.054 × 10⁸ bit/s ≈ 405 Mbit/s, about 200 times the step-index figure.

Exam traps

  • Using Δ = n₁ − n₂ instead of Δ = (n₁ − n₂)/n₁. For n₁ = 1.48 and n₂ = 1.465, Δ is 0.01, not 0.015.

2.6 Chromatic dispersion: material and waveguide

A source never emits one pure wavelength. A laser spans a narrow band; an LED spans tens of nanometres. Glass bends each wavelength slightly differently, and in a single-mode fiber the guided mode also sits a little differently in the core at each wavelength. So the wavelengths inside one pulse travel at different group velocities and arrive at different times. That spreading is chromatic dispersion; the spread Δτ is in ps, and the fiber is described by a dispersion coefficient D in ps/(nm·km). It is not a loss, it is a timing error, and on long single-mode links it sets the bit-rate limit. The coefficient has two parts: a material part from the glass and a waveguide part from the profile.

You need: group velocity and the V number from Unit 1 (see Unit 1, Foundations: Light Propagation in Fibers), and the idea of a pulse spreading in time from 2.5.

Where the spread comes from

A pulse is not one wavelength. Mode theory gives a propagation constant β for the guided mode. The group velocity is v_g = dω/dβ, so the delay after a length L is τ = L/v_g = L dβ/dω. Because β depends on wavelength, every wavelength in the pulse's band gets a slightly different delay, and the pulse arrives stretched in time.

The dispersion coefficient is that delay change per kilometre per nanometre:

D = (1/L) dτ/dλ

D is the dispersion coefficient in ps/(nm·km); L is the fiber length in km; τ is the group delay in ps; λ is the wavelength in nm. D > 0 means longer wavelengths are delayed more and arrive later.

Δτ = D L Δλ

Δτ is the pulse spread in ps; D is in ps/(nm·km); L is in km; Δλ is the full spectral width of the source in nm.

Sign conventions differ between books: some books take D positive when the longer wavelength arrives first; this book takes it positive when the longer wavelength arrives later. Fix one convention and keep it through a whole calculation. Chromatic dispersion has two sources. Material dispersion comes from the glass index and its dependence on wavelength. Waveguide dispersion comes from how tightly the mode is confined to the core, which also changes with wavelength. The two add:

D = D_m + D_w

D_m is the material part and D_w the waveguide part, both in ps/(nm·km), each with its own sign.

Material dispersion

The refractive index of silica falls as wavelength rises. Both n and dn/dλ enter the group delay, so the spread depends on the curvature of n(λ):

D_m = −(λ/c)(d²n/dλ²)

λ is the wavelength in m; c = 3×10⁸ m/s; n is the core refractive index. The result is in s/m²; multiply by 10⁶ to get ps/(nm·km).

For silica, D_m crosses zero near 1300 nm. It is about −100 ps/(nm·km) at 850 nm, so there the longer wavelength arrives first. It is about +20 ps/(nm·km) at 1550 nm, so there the longer wavelength arrives last. This curve belongs to the glass. Core doping shifts it a little and nothing more, which is why designers attack the other term.

Waveguide dispersion

In a single-mode fiber some power travels in the cladding. How much is set by the V number:

V = (2πa/λ)√(n₁² − n₂²)

a is the core radius; λ is the wavelength in the same unit; n₁ and n₂ are the core and cladding refractive indices.

As λ rises, V falls, the mode spreads further into the cladding, and its effective index drops toward n₂. That wavelength-dependent confinement changes the group delay, which is waveguide dispersion D_w. For a standard step-index profile it is negative across 1300-1600 nm and only a few ps/(nm·km) in size.

D_w is the part a designer controls, through the core radius a and the index step Δ = (n₁ − n₂)/n₁. Because it is negative, it cancels the rising D_m, and the fiber's zero lands near 1310 nm rather than at the material zero near 1300 nm. Fibers built to move that zero all the way to 1550 nm are covered in 2.8.

The slope near 1310 nm is about 0.09 ps/(nm²·km), so D climbs steeply as you move away from the zero. Δλ is the full spectral width of the source, not its modulation rate. If a laser is quoted in frequency, convert:

Δλ = (λ²/c) Δf

λ is the operating wavelength in m; c = 3×10⁸ m/s; Δf is the source linewidth in Hz. At 1550 nm, 10 GHz is about 0.08 nm.

A 0.1 nm laser over 100 km with D = 17 gives 170 ps of spread. An LED with 40 nm at the same D gives 68 ns, which limits the link to a few Mbit/s. That is why wide sources pair with short links. In a multimode link, chromatic spread adds to intermodal spread from 2.5; the two are independent, so combine them root-sum-square, Δτ = √(Δτ_ch² + Δτ_mod²). Polarization mode dispersion (2.7) is a separate, random term.

2.7 Polarization mode dispersion

A perfectly round fiber would carry the two polarization modes at the same speed. Real fiber is slightly elliptical and stressed, so its refractive index differs along two transverse axes. That difference is birefringence. The two polarization modes then arrive at slightly different times, and the delay is polarization mode dispersion (PMD). Along a cabled link the axes twist at random, so the delay grows as the square root of length, not linearly. PMD matters at 10 Gbit/s and above, and in coherent systems it still limits reach.

You need Unit 1 for modes and polarization, and chapter 2.6 for group delay differences.

Birefringence splits the two modes

Drawing and cabling leave the core slightly elliptical, and they add stress that is not the same in all directions. The refractive index is then not one number: it is n_x along one transverse axis and n_y along the other. The difference B = |n_x − n_y| is the birefringence. The two polarization modes see different indices, so they have different propagation constants and different group delays. In a short, uniform piece of fiber the differential group delay (DGD) grows linearly with length, as the formula below shows. A round, stress-free fiber has B = 0, and the two modes travel together. The axes are called the principal axes. Birefringence is tiny in good fiber, but it is never exactly zero. Most links ignore it below 10 Gbit/s. The delay is small in a short jumper but can matter over hundreds of kilometres.

Δτ = |β₁ₓ − β₁ᵧ| L

Δτ is the DGD in ps; β₁ₓ and β₁ᵧ are group delays per unit length in ps/km; L is the fiber length in km.

Random axes give a square-root law

In a real cable the elliptical axes do not stay fixed. Bending, twisting and temperature rotate them along the length. The delay therefore does not add as L; it adds like a random walk, because each short section adds a small delay with a random sign. The mean DGD follows the first formula below. Modern fiber has D_PMD about 0.05 to 0.2 ps/√km; older cable can exceed 1 ps/√km. At 100 km with 0.1 ps/√km the mean is 1 ps, not 10 ps. The instantaneous DGD follows a Maxwellian distribution. Most values sit below the mean, but rare values near three times the mean cause outages. Keep the mean below about 10% of the bit period, as the second formula states. This is why PMD is often a 40 Gbit/s problem.

⟨Δτ⟩ = D_PMD √L

⟨Δτ⟩ is the mean DGD in ps; D_PMD is the PMD coefficient in ps/√km; L is the length in km.

D_PMD √L ≤ 0.1 T_b

D_PMD is the PMD coefficient in ps/√km; L is the length in km; T_b is the bit period in ps; R_b is the bit rate in Gbit/s, with T_b = 1000/R_b. Both sides are in ps.

Worked example

Question. A 400 km link uses fiber with D_PMD = 0.1 ps/√km. (a) Find the mean DGD. (b) Compare it with the bit period at 10 Gbit/s and 40 Gbit/s, using 10% of the bit period as the limit. (c) Comment on the Maxwellian tail.

  1. ⟨Δτ⟩ = D_PMD √L = 0.1 × √400.
  2. √400 = 20, so ⟨Δτ⟩ = 0.1 × 20 = 2 ps.

  4. At 40 Gbit/s, T_b = 25 ps and the limit is 2.5 ps. The mean 2 ps is close to it.
  5. The Maxwellian tail reaches about 3 × 2 = 6 ps, which exceeds 2.5 ps, so errors are possible at 40 Gbit/s.

  • Treating the mean DGD as the whole story. The Maxwellian tail reaches about three times the mean.
  • Comparing mean DGD with the bit period instead of 10% of it.
  • Confusing PMD with chromatic dispersion: both spread pulses, but chromatic dispersion scales as L and comes from wavelength, while PMD scales as √L and comes from polarization.

2.8 Dispersion-shifted and dispersion-compensating fibers

Standard single-mode fiber loses least light at 1550 nm but has zero dispersion only near 1310 nm. A 1550 nm link therefore pays low loss and high dispersion together. Two fixes exist: redesign the fiber so the zero-dispersion wavelength moves to 1550 nm, or splice in a short spool of strongly negative-dispersion fiber that cancels the link's positive dispersion. Only a few universities teach this.

You need Chapter 2.6 for D and its material and waveguide parts.

Dispersion-shifted fiber

Dispersion has two parts, D = D_m + D_w. D_m is the material term from the glass, positive at 1550 nm. D_w is the waveguide term, negative there. Standard fiber cancels them near 1310 nm. To cancel at 1550 nm instead, make D_w more negative at that wavelength: shrink the core and raise Δ, the fractional core-cladding index difference. A dispersion-shifted fiber (DSF) does exactly that, placing its zero-dispersion wavelength λ₀ at 1550 nm, where loss is also lowest.

D = D_m + D_w

D, D_m, D_w are total, material and waveguide dispersion in ps/(nm·km).

That suits a single-channel long-haul link. The cost appears with WDM. With every channel at nearly zero dispersion, all channels travel at nearly the same group velocity, phase matching holds, and four-wave mixing grows between them, stealing power and adding crosstalk (see Unit 6).

Non-zero dispersion-shifted fiber

Engineers gave up on the exact zero. A non-zero dispersion-shifted fiber (NZDSF) keeps a small dispersion at 1550 nm, about +2 to +6 ps/(nm·km). Small enough that the spread over a span stays within the receiver's tolerance, large enough that neighbouring channels drift out of phase within a few kilometres, so four-wave mixing stays weak. A negative-dispersion version also exists; the sign matters when a span is compensated. The price is a smaller effective area A_eff, the area the guided mode fills, and slightly more loss, so NZDSF is a little more nonlinear. Designers accept that, because channel crosstalk hurts more than a moderate dispersion penalty.

Dispersion-compensating fiber

A dispersion-compensating fiber (DCF) leaves the line fiber alone and adds a short length with strong negative dispersion: small core, high Δ, D of order −85 to −100 ps/(nm·km). It sits on a spool at an amplifier or receiver site. Dispersion is a delay per nanometre per kilometre, so it adds with length, and the design rule is Σ D_i L_i = 0 over the span, with D_i in ps/(nm·km) and L_i in km. Ten to twenty kilometres of DCF cancels a hundred kilometres of standard fiber.

Two costs. Loss: DCF runs near 0.5 dB/km, so a 20 km spool throws away about 10 dB. Nonlinearity: the small core means high intensity at ordinary power. And cancellation is exact at one wavelength only, because DCF and line fiber have different dispersion slopes; the band edges keep a residual. Check the worst channel, not the centre.

Worked example

  1. Line dispersion = 17 × 100 = 1700 ps/nm.
  2. (a) 1700 − 85L = 0, so L = 1700/85 = 20 km of DCF.
  3. (b) Loss = 0.2 × 100 + 0.5 × 20 = 20 + 10 = 30 dB.
  4. (c) 85 × 15 = 1275 ps/nm; residual = 1700 − 1275 = 425 ps/nm.

Answer. L = 20 km, total loss 30 dB, and with 15 km a residual of 425 ps/nm to check against the bit-rate limit.

Worked example 2

Question. An 80 km span has D = +16 ps/(nm·km) and α = 0.22 dB/km. The DCF has D = −80 ps/(nm·km) and α = 0.5 dB/km. Find the DCF length and the span loss.

  1. Span dispersion = 16 × 80 = 1280 ps/nm.
  2. 1280/80 = 16 km of DCF.
  3. Loss = 0.22 × 80 + 0.5 × 16 = 17.6 + 8 = 25.6 dB.

Answer. 16 km of DCF and a span loss of 25.6 dB.

Exam traps

  • Comparing D values without weighting by length: 20 km at −85 outweighs 100 km at +17.
  • Forgetting the DCF's own 0.5 dB/km loss and its extra nonlinearity.
  • Mixing up DSF (zero at 1550 nm) and NZDSF (small but nonzero).
  • Sign slips: the DCF term is negative inside Σ D_i L_i = 0.
  • Assuming perfect compensation across the whole band; slope mismatch leaves residual dispersion at the edges.

2.9 Nonlinear scattering: stimulated Brillouin and Raman

Above a certain power, silica stops being a passive pipe. The light itself drives two scattering processes. Stimulated Brillouin scattering (SBS) sends a frequency-shifted copy of the signal backward. Stimulated Raman scattering (SRS) sends one forward. Both steal power, so they cap what a link can carry. The same physics is used on purpose in Brillouin sensors and Raman amplifiers. Only a few universities teach this.

You need the loss coefficient α from Chapter 2.1 and scattering from Chapter 2.3: Rayleigh scattering is linear, this is not.

Two effects, two directions

Rayleigh scattering is linear: double the input power and the scattered power doubles. Nonlinear scattering differs. Past a threshold the scattered wave is amplified by the signal itself and settles at a fixed share of the power.

In SBS the signal and the backward Stokes wave beat together, and their beat pattern drives an acoustic wave in the glass through electrostriction. That acoustic wave acts as a moving grating and reflects light backward. The reflected wave is shifted down by about 11 GHz at 1550 nm. The Brillouin gain band is narrow, tens of MHz, so a narrow-linewidth laser reaches threshold at a few milliwatts.

In SRS the signal scatters off vibrations of silica molecules. The scattered wave travels forward, shifted down by about 13 THz. Its gain band is tens of THz wide, so it draws power from many wavelengths at once. The threshold is far higher: hundreds of milliwatts.

Threshold power

Both effects switch on when the gain collected over the effective length, g·(P/A_eff)·L_eff, reaches a fixed factor G above unity: about 21 for Brillouin and about 16 for Raman.

P_th ≈ G · A_eff / (g · L_eff)

P_th is the threshold power in W; G ≈ 21 for Brillouin and ≈ 16 for Raman; A_eff is the effective core area in m²; g is the gain coefficient in m/W; L_eff is the effective length in m.

L_eff is shorter than L because the signal fades along the span; only the near end is bright enough to drive the effect.

L_eff = (1 − e^(−αL)) / α

α is the attenuation coefficient in nepers per metre (divide dB/km by 4343) and L is the physical length in m. For a long span L_eff → 1/α.

At 1550 nm, 0.2 dB/km gives α ≈ 4.6×10⁻⁵ Np/m, so 1/α ≈ 22 km: an 80 km span behaves like 22 km. Broadening the laser beyond the narrow Brillouin band raises P_th; the Raman band is so wide that linewidth barely matters.

What they do in service

  • SBS caps power per WDM channel. The backward Stokes wave returns to the laser and adds noise. Cure: stay below P_th, or broaden the line beyond the gain band.
  • SRS moves power from short-wavelength channels to long-wavelength ones. Short-λ channels droop, long-λ channels gain; the limit is on total power.
  • Used on purpose: Brillouin sensors read strain and temperature from the frequency shift; a Raman amplifier pumps a signal with light about 13 THz above it. Unit 6 covers amplifiers.

Worked example

Question. A 1550 nm span has A_eff = 50 µm², g_B = 5×10⁻¹¹ m/W, g_R = 1×10⁻¹³ m/W, and L_eff = 20 km. (a) Find the Brillouin threshold. (b) Find the Raman threshold. (c) A WDM system runs 4 mW per channel. Comment.

  1. A_eff = 50 µm² = 50 × 10⁻¹² m²; L_eff = 20 km = 2 × 10⁴ m.
  2. g_B L_eff = 5×10⁻¹¹ × 2×10⁴ = 1×10⁻⁶ m²/W.
  3. P_th,B = 21 × 50×10⁻¹² / 1×10⁻⁶ = 1.05×10⁻³ W.
  4. g_R L_eff = 1×10⁻¹³ × 2×10⁴ = 2×10⁻⁹ m²/W.
  5. P_th,R = 16 × 50×10⁻¹² / 2×10⁻⁹ = 0.4 W.
  6. 4 mW exceeds 1.05 mW, so each channel is above the Brillouin threshold. Eight channels total 32 mW, far below 400 mW.

Answer. P_th,B ≈ 1.05 mW and P_th,R ≈ 400 mW, so at 4 mW per channel the link is limited by SBS, not SRS.

Worked example 2

Question. Interpretation only: why does doubling the laser linewidth help a link that is Brillouin-limited?

  1. SBS gain exists only within tens of MHz around the 11 GHz shift.
  2. A narrow line sits inside that band, so all the power pumps one acoustic wave.
  3. Spreading the power over several GHz leaves only a small fraction inside the band.
  4. The effective g_B falls, so P_th rises and the Stokes wave stops building up.

Answer. Broadening the line lowers the power density inside the narrow Brillouin band, which raises the SBS threshold.

Exam traps

  • Putting physical length L where the formula needs L_eff. An 80 km span at 1550 nm behaves like about 22 km.
  • Swapping the two: Brillouin is backward, GHz shift, narrow band; Raman is forward, THz shift, wide band.
  • Converting A_eff wrongly. 50 µm² is 50 × 10⁻¹² m²; a slip to 50 × 10⁻⁶ m² makes P_th a million times too large.
  • Using G = 21 for Raman. It is ≈ 21 for Brillouin, ≈ 16 for Raman.
  • Saying SRS limits per-channel power. SRS limits total launched power across all channels.

Remember Which scattering is backward and which is forward, and by how much is each shifted at 1550 nm? 2 marks

Brillouin backward, about 11 GHz; Raman forward, about 13 THz.

Remember Give the order of magnitude of the SBS and SRS thresholds at 1550 nm for a narrow-line laser. 2 marks

A few milliwatts for SBS; hundreds of milliwatts for SRS.

Understand Why does the threshold formula use L_eff instead of the physical length L? 3 marks

The signal decays as e^(−αz), so only about the first 1/α of the span is strong enough to drive the effect; extra length adds almost nothing.

Apply A span has A_eff = 80 µm², g_B = 5×10⁻¹¹ m/W and L_eff = 20 km. Find the Brillouin threshold. 4 marks

P_th = 21 × 80×10⁻¹² / (5×10⁻¹¹ × 2×10⁴) = 1.68×10⁻⁹ / 1×10⁻⁶ = 1.68×10⁻³ W, about 1.7 mW.

Analyze Why does SBS limit power per channel while SRS limits total power? 5 marks

The Brillouin band is tens of MHz, narrower than one channel, so each channel acts alone. The Raman band is tens of THz and covers every channel, so they pump one another.

Analyze Why does broadening the laser linewidth raise the SBS threshold but not the SRS threshold? 4 marks

SBS gain exists only within tens of MHz, so a broad line puts little power in the band. The Raman band is tens of THz wide, so the line still lies inside it.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Distributed Brillouin sensing turns a whole fiber into a strain and temperature sensor for bridges and pipelines. Raman amplification uses the same 13 THz shift to pump a signal with shorter-wavelength light. Both effects are why high-power WDM links need careful power management.

Takeaways

  1. Attenuation sets the reach of a link, and in silica it is quoted as a coefficient in dB/km rather than as a plain ratio.
  2. Intrinsic absorption is a property of the glass itself, while extrinsic absorption comes from impurities such as OH⁻ and metal ions.
  3. Rayleigh scattering falls as 1/λ⁴ and is the physical reason the 1550 nm window is the lowest-loss window.
  4. Bending loss is negligible until the bend radius approaches a critical value, then it grows exponentially with further tightening.
  5. In a multimode fiber the pulse spread is set by path-length differences between modes, and grading the core cuts it by roughly a factor of Δ/8.
  6. In a single-mode fiber the spread comes from chromatic dispersion, the sum of a material part and a waveguide part.
  7. Standard single-mode fiber has its zero-dispersion wavelength near 1310 nm, so a 1550 nm link needs dispersion management.
  8. Polarization mode dispersion is random and grows as √L, so it matters for long, high-bit-rate links rather than short ones.
  9. Dispersion-shifted and dispersion-compensating fibers treat dispersion as a design choice rather than a fixed property.
  10. Nonlinear scattering puts a ceiling on launched power, since beyond a threshold the signal pumps a scattered wave and loses power.

Remember and understand

Understand Define the attenuation coefficient of an optical fiber. Derive its expression in dB/km and explain why loss is quoted this way. 8 marks

State α = (10/L)log₁₀(P_in/P_out) with L in km and both powers in the same unit; Show the step from the natural logarithm form to the base-10 decibel form; Explain that the coefficient is per kilometre, so link loss is αL, and that it allows simple addition of fiber, splice and connector losses; Give typical values: about 0.2 dB/km at 1550 nm, 0.35 dB/km at 1310 nm, 2 to 3 dB/km at 850 nm.

Understand Distinguish intrinsic and extrinsic absorption in silica glass fibers and name the wavelengths where each is serious. 10 marks

Intrinsic: electronic absorption in the ultraviolet below about 0.4 μm and vibrational absorption in the infrared beyond about 1.6 μm; both tails reach into the usable window; Extrinsic: OH⁻ ion peaks near 950 nm, 1240 nm and 1380 nm, the last being the strongest; Metal ion impurities such as Fe, Cu, Cr, Mn add broadband loss; Removal by purification and by drying the preform, giving low-water-peak fiber; Both together set part of the loss floor of the fiber alongside Rayleigh scattering.

Understand Explain Rayleigh scattering in an optical fiber and show why its loss falls as 1/λ⁴. 10 marks

Origin: density and composition fluctuations frozen into the glass during drawing, on a scale much smaller than the wavelength; Each fluctuation acts as a tiny dipole scatterer; scattered power follows the λ⁻⁴ law of Rayleigh scattering; Write α_R = C/λ⁴, C ≈ 0.8 dB·km⁻¹·μm⁴ for silica; at 1.55 μm this is about 0.15 dB/km; It is the dominant loss mechanism in modern fiber between about 1.3 μm and 1.6 μm and cannot be removed, only reduced by longer wavelength; Compare with Mie scattering from larger defects, which is flatter in wavelength and is reduced by better fabrication.

Understand Explain bending losses in optical fibers. Distinguish macrobending from microbending and state what happens as the bend radius is reduced. 10 marks

Macrobending: a visible curve of a few millimetres radius or less; Microbending: random microscopic kinks along the axis from coating, cabling or thermal stress; Physical cause: on the outside of the bend the field must travel faster than the plane wave in the cladding, so the tail beyond the caustic radiates away; Loss stays tiny above a critical radius R_c ≈ 3n₁²λ/(4π(n₁² − n₂²)^{3/2}) and then rises exponentially as R falls below it; Longer wavelength and smaller Δ both make bending loss worse; single-mode fiber in the 1550 nm band is the most sensitive.

Understand Define intermodal dispersion and chromatic dispersion and state the units in which each is measured. 8 marks

Intermodal: different modes have different path lengths, so one pulse splits into a train of arrivals; spread is proportional to length, in ns or ps per km; Chromatic: within one mode, different wavelengths travel at different group velocities; measured as D in ps/(nm·km); Chromatic = material dispersion plus waveguide dispersion; Intermodal dispersion exists only in multimode fiber; chromatic exists in both; Polarization mode dispersion is a third, random, mechanism in single-mode fiber.

Apply

Apply A 12 km link uses fiber with α = 0.5 dB/km and has two splices of 0.1 dB each. The launched power is 1 mW. Find the loss in dB and the received power in dBm and in mW. 8 marks

Fiber loss = 0.5 × 12 = 6 dB; splice loss = 0.2 dB; total 6.2 dB; 1 mW is 0 dBm, so received power = 0 − 6.2 = −6.2 dBm; Convert: 10^(−0.62) ≈ 0.24 mW; State that losses in dB add directly, which is why the dB scale is used.

Apply A step-index multimode fiber has n₁ = 1.48, Δ = 0.01, core radius 50 μm and length 1 km. Estimate the pulse broadening from intermodal dispersion and the maximum bit rate the fiber could support. 10 marks

Apply A standard single-mode link is 80 km long at 1550 nm with D = 17 ps/(nm·km). The source has a spectral width of 1 nm. Find the pulse broadening and compare it with a 10 Gbit/s bit slot. 12 marks

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare pulse broadening in multimode step-index and multimode graded-index fibers. Why does the graded profile reduce the spread so strongly? 13 marks

Step index: all modes see the same index, so path length alone sets the delay; ΔT/L = n₁Δ/c, about 50 ns/km for Δ = 0.01; Graded index: rays that swing far from the axis travel through lower-index glass, so their higher speed partly cancels their longer path; With the near-parabolic profile (α ≈ 2), ΔT/L ≈ n₁Δ²/(8c); the ratio to the step-index case is Δ/8; Numbers for Δ = 0.01: about 50 ns/km falls to about 60 ps/km; Practical limits: profile errors, mode mixing and the difficulty of holding α near 2 across the core.

Evaluate Why is 1550 nm the preferred window for long-haul links even though the zero-dispersion wavelength is near 1310 nm? Discuss how the loss–dispersion trade-off is resolved. 16 marks

Loss: Rayleigh scattering falls as 1/λ⁴, giving about 0.2 dB/km at 1550 nm against 0.35 dB/km at 1310 nm; the infrared absorption tail sets the upper limit; Dispersion: standard fiber has D ≈ +17 ps/(nm·km) at 1550 nm but nearly zero at 1310 nm; Erbium-doped fiber amplifiers work in the 1530–1565 nm band, which favours 1550 nm; Resolution: dispersion-shifted fiber moving the zero to 1550 nm, non-zero-dispersion fiber, dispersion-compensating fiber, or electronic compensation; Note the four-wave-mixing penalty that pure dispersion-shifted fiber suffers in WDM systems; Conclude that the loss saving of roughly 0.15 dB/km over 100 km (15 dB) outweighs the need to manage dispersion.

Evaluate Explain stimulated Brillouin and stimulated Raman scattering. Compare their thresholds, directions and shifts, and state how each affects a WDM link. 13 marks

Both are inelastic scattering processes in which a pump photon gives energy to a shifted wave; they become stimulated above a threshold power; Brillouin: backward, shift about 11 GHz at 1550 nm, narrow gain bandwidth, threshold of a few milliwatts; P_th ≈ 21A_eff/(g_B L_eff); Raman: forward, shift about 13 THz, broad gain band, threshold in the hundreds of milliwatts; the gain is used in distributed Raman amplifiers; Effects on a link: SBS clips the launched power and causes back-reflection; SRS transfers power between WDM channels (channel crosstalk) at high total power; Mitigations: keep power below threshold, widen the source linewidth to raise the SBS threshold, use large effective area fiber, space channels to limit Raman crosstalk.

Glossary

Attenuation
The fall of optical power with distance along a fiber, quoted as a coefficient in dB per kilometre.
Chromatic dispersion
Pulse spreading because the group velocity depends on wavelength; measured in ps/(nm·km).
Critical radius
The bend radius below which bending loss rises sharply, roughly 3n₁²λ/(4π(n₁² − n₂²)^{3/2}).
dBm
An absolute power level referred to 1 mW, so 0 dBm = 1 mW and −3 dBm ≈ 0.5 mW.
Decibel (dB)
A logarithmic power ratio, 10·log₁₀(P₂/P₁), used so that cascaded losses add instead of multiply.
Differential group delay
The arrival-time difference between the two polarization modes, in picoseconds.
Dispersion-compensating fiber
Fiber with large negative dispersion, spliced in to cancel the accumulated positive dispersion of a link.
Dispersion-shifted fiber
Fiber designed so that its zero-dispersion wavelength falls at 1550 nm, in the lowest-loss window.
Effective core area
The area A_eff over which the mode intensity is effectively spread; it sets the power density and hence the nonlinear thresholds.
Extrinsic absorption
Absorption caused by impurities in the glass, chiefly OH⁻ ions and transition-metal ions.
Graded-index fiber
Multimode fiber whose core index decreases smoothly from the axis, equalising modal delays.
Intermodal dispersion
Pulse spreading in multimode fiber because different modes take different paths and arrive at different times.
Intrinsic absorption
Absorption caused by the silica network itself, through ultraviolet electronic transitions and infrared vibrational transitions.
Low-water-peak fiber
Fiber drawn from a dried preform so that the 1380 nm OH⁻ peak is reduced and the whole 1300–1600 nm range is usable.
Macrobending
Loss caused by curving the whole fiber to a small radius, so the field on the outside of the bend radiates away.
Material dispersion
The part of chromatic dispersion coming from the wavelength dependence of the glass refractive index.
Microbending
Loss caused by small random kinks along the fiber axis, usually from cabling or coating stress.
Mie scattering
Scattering from defects or bubbles comparable to the wavelength, with a much weaker wavelength dependence than Rayleigh scattering.
Mode
One field pattern that can travel along the fiber, with its own propagation constant and group velocity.
OH⁻ absorption peak
A strong absorption band of the hydroxyl ion near 1380 nm, with weaker peaks near 950 nm and 1240 nm.
Optical window
A wavelength band where fiber loss is low, notably around 850 nm, 1310 nm and 1550 nm.
Polarization mode dispersion
Random delay between the two polarization modes of a real, slightly non-circular fiber; grows as the square root of length.
Rayleigh scattering
Scattering from density fluctuations much smaller than the wavelength, with loss proportional to 1/λ⁴.
Stimulated Brillouin scattering
Backward inelastic scattering above a few milliwatts, with a shift of about 11 GHz at 1550 nm.
Stimulated Raman scattering
Forward inelastic scattering with a shift of about 13 THz, useful for amplification but harmful as channel crosstalk.
Waveguide dispersion
The part of chromatic dispersion coming from the way the mode's propagation constant depends on wavelength in a given core geometry.
Zero-dispersion wavelength
The wavelength at which material and waveguide dispersion cancel; about 1310 nm for standard single-mode fiber.
