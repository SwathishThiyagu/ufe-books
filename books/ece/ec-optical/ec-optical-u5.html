5.1 Digital and analog point-to-point links

A point-to-point link is one transmitter, one fiber path and one receiver, and nothing else between them. It exists because most real systems are exactly that: a trunk between two telephone exchanges, a cable-TV headend feeding a node, a 10 km run across a campus. The same five blocks serve both link types. What changes is the question asked at the end. A digital link carries bits and is judged by how many arrive wrong. An analog link carries a waveform and is judged by how much noise rides on it. That one difference drives the whole design.

You need Unit 3 (sources and power launching) and Unit 4 (photodiodes, receiver sensitivity, bit error rate).

The five blocks of a point-to-point link

The signal path is fixed: light leaves the source, loses power in the fiber, lands on a photodiode, is amplified, and is judged. Draw it once and both link types fit the same drawing.

  • Source and driver. An LED or laser diode plus the circuit that pumps current into it. In a digital link the driver sends current pulses; in an analog link it sends a continuously varying current. The output is modulated optical power.
  • Fiber with splices and connectors. The cable plus every joint on the route. Each joint adds a fixed loss, and the run adds αL, where α is the attenuation in dB/km and L is the length in km.
  • Photodiode. Converts optical power P to current I = R·P, where R is the responsivity in A/W.
  • Preamplifier and filter. Raises the small current to a usable level and limits the bandwidth. That bandwidth decides how much noise arrives along with the signal.
  • Decision circuit or measurement stage. In a digital link it samples the filtered pulse at the centre of the bit and decides 1 or 0. In an analog link the output is kept as a waveform and measured for carrier-to-noise ratio.

Every loss before the photodiode simply reduces the current. Noise added by the preamplifier sets a floor that no later stage can remove; the only cure is more received optical power.

Digital link: four system considerations

For a digital link the design settles four numbers. Bit rate B, in bits/s, sets the bandwidth the receiver must pass and the rise time the pulse is allowed. Distance L, in km, sets the loss αL. Received power P_rx, in dBm or watts, must stay above the receiver sensitivity for the required error rate. Pulse shape must still be open at the sampling instant after the fiber has broadened it.

The score card is the bit error rate, BER: the fraction of bits decided wrongly. A target of 10⁻⁹ means one error per thousand million bits, the usual telephony-class figure. At 2.5 Gbit/s that is roughly one error every 0.4 s. BER falls very steeply as received power rises, so a link either clears its target with margin or fails badly; there is little middle ground.

In practice you check two budgets. The power budget asks whether enough light arrives. The rise-time budget asks whether the pulse is still sharp enough. The link passes only if both do, and the shorter of the two limits wins. Power usually limits long links; rise time usually limits fast ones.

Analog link: linear modulation and carrier-to-noise ratio

In an analog link the light intensity follows the waveform itself, so the source must be modulated linearly. The modulation index m, no unit, is the peak swing of the optical power divided by its mean value. One fiber may carry a single video channel or many subcarriers, each a narrow band placed at a different frequency.

CNR = (mI)²/2 ÷ i_n²

CNR is a power ratio, no unit; m is the modulation index (no unit); I is the mean photocurrent in amperes; i_n is the total RMS noise current in amperes inside the channel bandwidth. Both currents are squared, so their units cancel. In decibels, CNR(dB) = 10 log₁₀ CNR.

The signal current is an RMS value, mI/√2, so its power is (mI)²/2 in A². The noise power is i_n², where i_n collects shot noise, the thermal noise of the preamplifier and any relative intensity noise of the source. Widening the filter raises i_n in proportion to the square root of bandwidth, so CNR drops.

Noise is only half the analog story. When two or more subcarriers share the fiber, the square-law detector mixes them. Sums and differences of two carriers give second-order beats, quoted as composite second order (CSO). Combinations of three give third-order beats, quoted as composite triple beat (CTB). Both land inside channels and cannot be filtered out. Dynamic range is the useful span of m, from the CNR floor at the bottom to the distortion ceiling at the top.

Which measure to report

Link typeMeasureTypical targetEffect of noise or distortion
DigitalBER10⁻⁹ for telephony-class linkswrong decisions; BER climbs steeply as received power falls
AnalogCNRabout 50 dB for one video channelvisible snow or sparkle in the picture
AnalogCSO, CTBbeats kept far below the carriernot noise at all; distortion stays even when more light arrives

Fix the measure before you design. A digital power budget starts from the receiver sensitivity written for a stated BER. An analog budget starts from the CNR the customer needs at the far end, and then checks that the modulation index chosen stays below the distortion limit.

Worked example

Question. An analog link carries one 6 MHz video channel at 0.85 μm. The received optical power is −20 dBm, the photodiode responsivity is 0.5 A/W, the modulation index is m = 0.7, and the total RMS noise current in the 6 MHz band is 5 nA. Find the mean photocurrent, the RMS signal current and the CNR in dB.

  1. Convert the optical power out of dBm. P = 10^(−20/10) mW = 0.01 mW = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ W.
  2. Mean photocurrent: I = R·P = 0.5 A/W × 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ W = 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ A = 5 μA.
  3. RMS signal current: mI/√2 = 0.7 × 5 μA ÷ 1.414 = 3.5 ÷ 1.414 = 2.475 μA.
  4. Signal power in the channel: (2.475 μA)² = 6.125 μA².
  5. Noise current 5 nA = 0.005 μA, so noise power = (0.005 μA)² = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ μA².
  6. CNR = 6.125 ÷ 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ = 2.45 × 10⁵. Working both currents in μA keeps the ratio unitless.
  7. In decibels: CNR(dB) = 10 log₁₀ (2.45 × 10⁵) = 10 × 5.389 = 53.9 dB.

Answer. Mean photocurrent 5 μA, RMS signal current 2.475 μA, and CNR = 2.45 × 10⁵, that is 53.9 dB.

Exam traps

  • dBm is optical power, not current. Convert to mW or W first, then multiply by responsivity in A/W. Multiplying a dBm figure straight by R is the commonest slip.
  • Signal is an RMS current, so divide the peak mI by √2 before squaring. Noise adds in power, not in current: never add a signal current and a noise current as plain numbers.
  • CNR is a power ratio, so it goes to dB with 10 log₁₀. Using 20 log₁₀ doubles the answer and looks plausible.
  • CSO and CTB are distortion, not noise. Adding more received power lifts CNR but leaves the beat products sitting right where they were, relative to the carrier.
  • Examiners like the pair BER and CNR side by side. Remember BER belongs to a decision on bits and CNR to a continuous waveform; a link can pass one and fail the other.

5.2 Link power budget

The link power budget is a single addition. It adds every loss and margin between the transmitter and the receiver, then compares the total with the power difference available. The idea exists because a link must still work after connectors age, splices add loss and the laser weakens. It is used at design time to choose the source, fiber, connector count and receiver, and to leave a clear spare margin. If the sum fits, the link closes; if not, the design changes.

You need: 5.1 for the five-block link chain, Unit 2 for fiber loss mechanisms, and Unit 3 for connector and splice losses.

The budget equation

Start with the transmitter mean launched power P_T in dBm and the receiver sensitivity P_R in dBm. The difference P_T − P_R is the power you can afford to lose, in dB. Put every loss and reserve on the right side. The budget equation is:

P_T − P_R = αL + α_c N_c + α_s N_s + M

P_T is mean launched power in dBm; P_R is receiver sensitivity in dBm; α is fiber attenuation in dB/km; L is fiber length in km; α_c is loss per connector in dB; N_c is the number of connectors; α_s is loss per splice in dB; N_s is the number of splices; M is the system margin in dB.

The left side is a power difference in dB. Every term on the right is a loss in dB. A positive M is an allowance for ageing, temperature, repairs and extra connectors; it is not a measured loss. The budget closes when the right side is less than or equal to the left. The actual received power is P_T minus the physical losses only. The margin is then held back as reserve.

Do not add a dBm value to a dB value. P_T and P_R are absolute powers in dBm; αL, α_cN_c, α_sN_s and M are ratios in dB. The received power in dBm is P_T − αL − α_cN_c − α_sN_s. Compare that with P_R; the difference is the headroom. Of that headroom, M is reserved as margin and the rest is spare.

Typical values and the extra penalty

Use planning values, not the best laboratory number. Typical values are:

ItemTypical valueUnit
1550 nm single-mode fiber0.20–0.25dB/km
1310 nm single-mode fiber0.35–0.50dB/km
850 nm multimode fiber2.5–3.5dB/km
Connector0.5–1.0dB each
Splice0.1–0.2dB each
System margin4–6dB

A connector count includes both ends and every patch panel in the path. A splice count counts joints inside the span; a 60 km span spliced every 10 km has five splices, not six, unless the ends are spliced to pigtails. System margin is commonly 4–6 dB. For a short indoor link a smaller margin may be acceptable; for an outdoor span use the larger end.

Sensitivity is the minimum usable power at the required bit error rate. Overload is the maximum power the receiver can accept before it saturates. The budget must satisfy P_R ≤ received power ≤ P_overload. A very short link with a strong laser can fail from overload; add a fixed attenuator. A link near its dispersion limit also needs extra power because spreading pulses close the eye. This dispersion power penalty P_d is in dB and is added as one more loss term: P_T − P_R = αL + α_cN_c + α_sN_s + M + P_d.

Design rule and reading the result

Compute in this order: fiber loss, connector loss, splice loss, system margin. Then subtract from available power. Spare = (P_T − P_R) − (αL + α_cN_c + α_sN_s + M). A positive spare means the budget closes. A negative spare means it does not; reducing M to make it fit is not design, it is hiding risk. Change a real term: shorter L, lower-loss fiber, fewer splices, better connectors, a higher-power source, or a more sensitive receiver. Each change has a cost.

The design rule is economic: pick the cheapest source and fiber that still close the budget with a sensible margin. An 850 nm multimode LED link is often cheapest for a building; a 1310 nm single-mode link fits medium distances; a 1550 nm single-mode laser fits long spans. After the loss budget closes, check the rise-time budget in 5.3. Loss decides reach; rise time decides speed. The shorter limit wins.

A spare margin is useful, but too much spare can mean you paid for a laser or fiber you did not need. Keep enough margin for repairs and ageing, and no more than the link economics justify.

Worked example

Question. Design a 2.5 Gb/s point-to-point link: a 1550 nm laser with 0 dBm mean launched power, 60 km of single-mode fiber with α = 0.22 dB/km, two connectors at 0.5 dB each, one splice every 10 km at 0.1 dB each, receiver sensitivity −28 dBm at 2.5 Gb/s, and a 6 dB system margin. Does the budget close? Find the spare margin and the worst-case received power.

  1. Count splices inside the 60 km span: 60/10 − 1 = 5 splices. The two end connections are connectors, not splices.
  2. Fiber loss = 60 km × 0.22 dB/km = 13.2 dB.
  3. Connector loss = 2 × 0.5 dB = 1.0 dB.
  4. Splice loss = 5 × 0.1 dB = 0.5 dB.
  5. Physical loss = 13.2 + 1.0 + 0.5 = 14.7 dB.
  6. Add system margin: total budget used = 14.7 + 6 = 20.7 dB.
  7. Available power = P_T − P_R = 0 − (−28) = 28 dB.
  8. Spare margin = 28 − 20.7 = 7.3 dB.
  9. Worst-case received power = 0 − 14.7 = −14.7 dBm.
  10. Headroom above sensitivity = −14.7 − (−28) = 13.3 dB. Of this, 6 dB is reserved margin, leaving 7.3 dB spare.

Answer. Yes: total used = 20.7 dB, available = 28 dB, spare = 7.3 dB, and worst-case received power = −14.7 dBm, which is above the −28 dBm sensitivity.

Exam traps

  • Adding a dBm power to a dB loss. Keep P_T, sensitivity and received power in dBm; keep losses and margins in dB.
  • Forgetting the system margin or one connector. Count both ends and every patch panel; margin is a real budget line, not optional.
  • Miscounting splices: a 60 km span spliced every 10 km has five splices inside, not six, unless the ends are spliced to pigtails.
  • Confusing sensitivity with overload. Sensitivity is the minimum usable power; overload is the maximum. A short link can fail from too much power.
  • Leaving out the dispersion power penalty when the link is near its dispersion limit; it behaves like one more dB loss in the budget.

5.3 Rise-time budget; attenuation and dispersion limits

A link must carry a signal far enough and fast enough. Loss sets how far: every kilometre removes power, and the receiver needs a minimum. Rise time sets how fast: a pulse that spreads too much overlaps its neighbour. This chapter builds the rise-time budget, turns it into a maximum length from dispersion, compares that length with the attenuation limit, and shows that the shorter of the two is the real reach. It is used in choosing fiber, source and receiver for a new route.

You need: 5.2 for PT, PR and margin M; Unit 2 for chromatic and modal dispersion.

Two clocks in one link

Every digital link has a clock. The bit period Tb is the time allowed for one bit. If the bit rate is B bit/s, then Tb = 1/B. At 1 Gbit/s, Tb = 1 ns = 1000 ps. At 10 Gbit/s it is 100 ps. A pulse must rise and fall inside this slot. Its 10% to 90% rise time is Tr. If Tr is too large, the pulse smears into the next slot and the receiver cannot decide between 0 and 1. This overlap is inter-symbol interference, ISI.

A non-return-to-zero (NRZ) pulse stays high for the whole bit slot. A return-to-zero (RZ) pulse is high for part of the slot and returns to zero, so it needs a faster transition. The usual rule is: the system rise time Tsys must be at most 0.7Tb for NRZ and 0.35Tb for RZ. The 0.7 factor lets the received pulse fill most of the slot without touching its neighbour. Some syllabi call the same check the bandwidth budget.

T<sub>sys</sub> = √(T<sub>tx</sub>² + T<sub>fiber</sub>² + T<sub>rx</sub>²) ≤ 0.7 T<sub>b</sub> for NRZ, and ≤ 0.35 T<sub>b</sub> for RZ

Tsys is the total system rise time (10–90%) in ps; Ttx, Tfiber and Trx are the transmitter, fiber and receiver rise times in ps; Tb = 1/B is the bit period in ps; B is the bit rate in bit/s.

Tsys is not the sum of the parts. Three independent broadenings add as squares: transmitter Ttx, fiber Tfiber, receiver Trx. This square-root rule comes from adding variances of independent Gaussian-like responses. It is why a fast receiver cannot fully cure a slow fiber. Each term must be a 10–90% rise time in the same unit, usually picoseconds.

The fiber term

Tfiber has two possible sources. In multimode fiber, different modes travel different paths and arrive at different times. That is modal dispersion, covered in Unit 2. In single-mode fiber there is only one mode, but different wavelengths travel at different speeds. That is chromatic dispersion. A source is never perfectly monochromatic. Its spectral width Δλ is the spread of wavelengths. Use the root-mean-square (RMS) width, not the full width at half maximum, unless the data sheet says otherwise.

Tchrom = |D| L Δλ

Tchrom is the chromatic rise time in ps; D is the dispersion coefficient in ps/(nm·km); L is fiber length in km; Δλ is the RMS source spectral width in nm. The absolute value handles negative D.

If L = 20 km, D = 17 ps/(nm·km) and Δλ = 2 nm, then Tchrom = 17 × 20 × 2 = 680 ps. A laser with Δλ = 0.1 nm would give 34 ps, twenty times smaller. That is why narrow-linewidth lasers matter at high bit rates.

If a fiber has both modal and chromatic broadening, combine them in quadrature: Tfiber = √(Tmodal² + Tchrom²). In pure single-mode fiber Tmodal is zero. Polarization-mode dispersion also broadens pulses, but its statistical nature is treated separately.

Attenuation limit and dispersion limit

The attenuation limit asks how long the fiber can be before received power falls below sensitivity. Rearrange the power budget of 5.2: Latt = (PT − PR − M)/α. PT is launched power in dBm, PR is receiver sensitivity in dBm, M is system margin in dB, and α is fiber attenuation in dB/km. The numerator is in dB, so Latt is in km. If PT = 0 dBm, PR = −28 dBm, M = 0 dB and α = 0.22 dB/km, then Latt = 28/0.22 = 127 km. Connectors, splices and ageing margin must already be inside M or subtracted separately.

The dispersion limit asks how far a pulse can travel before its spread reaches one bit slot. A simple criterion is B L |D| Δλ ≤ 1. Here B is bit rate in bit/s, D is in s/(nm·km) when using SI units, and Δλ is in nm. Solving gives Ldisp = 1/(B D Δλ). With B in Gbit/s and D in ps/(nm·km), multiply by 1000: Ldisp = 1000/(BGb Dps Δλ). The figure uses D = 17 ps/(nm·km) and Δλ = 0.1 nm, so Ldisp = 588/BGb km: 588 km at 1 Gbit/s and 58.8 km at 10 Gbit/s. A 100 km loss limit crosses this near 5.9 Gbit/s.

The real reach is the smaller of Latt and Ldisp. At low bit rates loss usually wins; at high bit rates dispersion wins. If the reach is too short, first reduce Δλ or use a lower-dispersion fiber, then increase power or sensitivity. The rise-time budget is stricter than the simple dispersion limit because it includes transmitter and receiver rise times and the 0.7 factor. The dispersion limit is a quick first estimate.

Worked example

Question. A 1 Gbit/s NRZ link uses 20 km of standard single-mode fiber at 1550 nm. The transmitter rise time is 250 ps, the receiver rise time is 350 ps, the source RMS spectral width is 2 nm, and the fiber dispersion is D = 17 ps/(nm·km). The launched power is 0 dBm, receiver sensitivity is −28 dBm, margin is 0 dB, and attenuation is 0.22 dB/km. Does the link pass the rise-time budget? If not, what is the maximum fiber length allowed by the rise-time budget? Check whether loss is the limiting factor.

  1. Bit period: Tb = 1/B = 1/(1 × 109) s = 1 ns = 1000 ps.
  2. Allowed system rise time for NRZ: 0.7Tb = 0.7 × 1000 = 700 ps.
  3. Fiber rise time allowed: Tfiber ≤ √(700² − 250² − 350²) = √(490000 − 62500 − 122500) = √305000 = 552.3 ps.
  4. Actual chromatic rise time: Tchrom = |D|LΔλ = 17 × 20 × 2 = 680 ps.
  5. Compare: 680 ps > 552.3 ps, so the link fails the rise-time budget.
  6. Maximum length from rise time: L = 552.3/(17 × 2) = 16.2 km.
  7. Loss check: fiber loss = 20 × 0.22 = 4.4 dB. Available budget = PT − PR − M = 0 − (−28) − 0 = 28 dB. Since 4.4 dB < 28 dB, loss passes easily.

Answer. The link fails the rise-time budget; the maximum length is 16.2 km, while the loss check needs only 4.4 dB of the 28 dB available, so rise time sets the reach.

Exam traps

  • Adding Ttx, Tfiber and Trx directly. They add in quadrature, not as a plain sum.
  • Using the full spectral width instead of the RMS width in Tchrom = |D|LΔλ.
  • Using 0.7Tb for RZ. RZ uses 0.35Tb.
  • Mixing ps and ns: Tb = 1/B gives seconds; multiply by 1012 for ps.
  • Forgetting to compare Ldisp with Latt. The reach is the smaller, not the dispersion limit alone.

5.4 Attenuation and dispersion measurements

A datasheet value is a claim; a measurement is a fact. Attenuation is loss per kilometre. Dispersion is pulse spread per nanometre of source width per kilometre. Installers check both. Attenuation uses cutback or an OTDR. Dispersion uses pulse delay or phase shift. Only a few universities teach these measurements. The budgets in 5.2 and 5.3 depend on these numbers.

You need: Unit 2 gives the physics. 5.2 and 5.3 show why α and D are needed.

Attenuation by the cutback method

Cutback compares output power from a long fiber with output power from a short piece. Source, launch and meter stay fixed. The difference is the loss of the removed piece.

  1. Launch one fixed wavelength through a mode scrambler into the full length L₁.
  2. Record output P₁.
  3. Without moving source or launch, cut to a short length L₂ of a few metres.
  4. Record P₂ and compute α.

α = 10 log₁₀(P₂/P₁) ÷ (L₁ − L₂)

α is attenuation in dB/km; L₁ and L₂ are long and short lengths in km; P₁ and P₂ are their output powers in the same unit.

P₂ is larger, so α is positive. Cutting keeps launch identical; a new connector would add its own loss.

Attenuation from an OTDR trace

An optical time-domain reflectometer (OTDR) sends a pulse and plots Rayleigh backscatter against distance. The trace falls at 2α per kilometre because the light travels out and back; the OTDR halves this, so the α it reports, and the slope of the display, is the one-way value in dB/km. Quiz readings are taken from that corrected display. A step down is splice or connector loss. A spike is reflection; its height gives reflectance. Dead zone is the blind stretch in metres after a strong reflection.

A splice reads differently from the two ends; average both directions for accuracy (see 5.5).

Dispersion in time and frequency domain

Time domain, also called pulse-delay or time-of-flight: send short pulses at several wavelengths, record arrival times, then plot group delay τ in ps against λ in nm. Its slope at the working wavelength gives dispersion.

D = (1/L) · dτ/dλ

D is dispersion in ps/(nm·km); L is fiber length in km; τ is group delay in ps; λ is wavelength in nm.

Frequency domain, or phase-shift method: modulate the source at f_m, sweep λ, read phase φ. A delay τ gives φ = 360° × f_m × τ, so τ = φ ÷ (360 f_m). No fast pulse detector is needed; production uses this method.

Worked example

Question. A 1550 nm cutback measurement: with 10 km of fiber the meter reads 200 μW. The launch is untouched, the fiber is cut to 2 m, and the meter reads 1000 μW. (a) Find α. (b) Find total loss of the original 10 km.

  1. L₁ = 10 km, P₁ = 200 μW; L₂ = 2 m = 0.002 km, P₂ = 1000 μW.
  2. Length removed = 9.998 km.
  3. P₂/P₁ = 5; loss = 10 log₁₀ 5 = 6.9897 dB.
  4. α = 6.9897 ÷ 9.998 = 0.6991 dB/km.
  5. Total loss of 10 km = 0.6991 × 10 = 6.99 dB.
  6. This is much higher than the ~0.2 dB/km of modern single-mode fiber at 1550 nm, more like old or poor fiber. The arithmetic is what the question asks. An OTDR on the same fiber must show the same slope, 0.6991 dB/km.

Answer. α = 0.6991 dB/km (about 0.70 dB/km); 10 km of this fiber loses 6.99 dB.

Worked example 2

Question. The same fiber in a pulse-delay (time-of-flight) dispersion set-up: relative group delay reads 500.0 ps at 1548 nm and 506.8 ps at 1552 nm over 10 km. Find D.

  1. Wavelength step dλ = 1552 − 1548 = 4 nm.
  2. Relative delay step dτ = 506.8 − 500.0 = 6.8 ps.
  3. dτ/dλ = 6.8 ÷ 4 = 1.7 ps/nm.
  4. D = (1/L) · dτ/dλ = 1.7 ÷ 10 = 0.17 ps/(nm·km).
  5. This near-zero D at 1550 nm means the sample is dispersion-shifted fiber; ordinary single-mode fiber would show about 17 ps/(nm·km) here.

Answer. D = 0.17 ps/(nm·km); the sample is dispersion-shifted fiber.

Exam traps

  • Dividing by total length L₁ instead of L₁ − L₂. The cut piece was not measured.
  • One reading in dBm and the other in μW, or lengths in metres. Use the same power unit and lengths in km.
  • Reading a splice straight after a connector spike. It sits in the dead zone.
  • Mixing units: α is dB/km, D is ps/(nm·km). 40 ps/nm on a 10 km spool is a total, not a coefficient; divide by 10 km to get 4 ps/(nm·km).
  • Confusing total loss in dB with the coefficient in dB/km.

5.5 Measuring NA, cutoff wavelength and diameter; field tests

Numerical aperture, cutoff wavelength and cladding diameter decide whether a fiber behaves as a single-mode guide at the operating wavelength. All three are read from light leaving the fiber: a far-field pattern, a bend-loss trace, or an index profile. The same instruments travel to the field, where an installed cable is tested with a source and power meter, an OTDR, a visual fault locator, dispersion testers and a bit-error test. Only a few universities teach this measurement detail.

You need Unit 1 for numerical aperture and cutoff wavelength, and 5.4 for the measurement bench.

Cutoff and numerical aperture

Cutoff wavelength is the wavelength above which the fiber carries only the fundamental mode. The bend, or transmission, method uses a 2 m length. Launch the broad-spectrum source into one length of fiber and record the trace straight, then with one loop, comparing the two traces of the same fiber. Record output power against wavelength for both. Below cutoff the fiber guides the fundamental mode and also a weakly confined higher-order mode (for example LP11); the bend strips the higher-order mode, so the looped trace loses power. Above cutoff that higher-order mode is no longer guided, only the well-confined fundamental remains, and the bend removes very little, so the gap closes. Cutoff is the wavelength where the looped trace sits a fixed amount below the straight one; a common criterion is 0.1 dB. The standard transmission method uses a loop of 60 mm radius. The 2 m measurement gives the fiber cutoff wavelength; a longer cabled length gives the cable cutoff, which is shorter. Bend sensitivity of the fundamental itself grows with wavelength because the mode field widens. The result depends on loop diameter and launch condition, so report both. Do not call the link's operating wavelength the cutoff wavelength.

Numerical aperture NA is the sine of the half-angle θ at which the far-field intensity falls to 5% of its peak: NA = sin θ. Use the 5% level, not the 50% half-power level. The index profile gives approximately the same value: NA = √(n1² − n2²), where n1 is the core index and n2 the cladding index. The far-field NA usually comes out a little larger because of launch and mode-excitation effects, so expect a small difference from the profile number. Measured in air, the acceptance half-angle θa equals θ.

Diameter, reflectance and field tests

Core diameter comes from a refracted near-field index profile. Cladding diameter comes from a video microscope or a grey-scale image; 125 μm is standard for single-mode and most multimode fiber. Calibrate the microscope scale before measuring. A wrong diameter changes connector and splice loss. Reflectance is R = 10 log₁₀(P_r/P_i), where P_r is reflected power and P_i is incident power; R is negative, for example −30 dB. Optical return loss is ORL = −10 log₁₀(P_r/P_i); it is positive, so the same joint has ORL = 30 dB.

In the field, an installed link is tested without cutting it. A source and power meter give end-to-end loss. An OTDR sends a pulse and plots backscatter against distance; the trace locates splice loss, connector loss, bend loss and a break. A visual fault locator injects visible red light to find a tight bend or a cracked connector. Chromatic dispersion and polarization-mode dispersion testers check high-bit-rate routes. A bit-error-rate test runs known data and counts errors. Measure loss at the operating wavelength. Compare results with the power budget in 5.2 and the attenuation measurement in 5.4.

Worked example

Question. A step-index fiber has n1 = 1.46. In the far field the intensity falls to 5% of its peak at a half-angle of 12° from the axis. Find the numerical aperture, the acceptance angle in air and n2.

  1. NA = sin 12° = 0.2079 ≈ 0.208.
  2. Measured in air, the acceptance angle θa equals this far-field half-angle, so θa = 12°.
  3. n2 = √(1.46² − 0.208²) = √(2.1316 − 0.0433) = √2.0883 = 1.445.

Answer. NA = 0.208, θa = 12°, and n2 = 1.445.

Exam traps

  • Calling the operating wavelength the cutoff wavelength.
  • Using the 50% far-field half-angle instead of the 5% level.
  • Giving reflectance a positive sign and ORL a negative sign.
  • Reporting cutoff without loop diameter and launch condition.
  • Measuring cladding diameter from the core index profile, or skipping microscope calibration.

Takeaways

  1. A point-to-point link is designed block by block; every dB lost between source and detector must be paid for by source power or receiver sensitivity.
  2. Digital links are judged by bit error rate, analog links by carrier-to-noise ratio, so the same fiber gives different answers for the two.
  3. The power budget answers how far the signal reaches, the rise-time budget answers how fast it can go, and the link must pass both.
  4. The shorter of the attenuation-limited and dispersion-limited lengths is the real maximum span.
  5. Attenuation is a difference of two power readings, so the cutback method removes connector and launch uncertainties.
  6. Dispersion is measured either by pulse broadening in time or by phase shift in frequency; both need a long fiber and a narrow source.
  7. NA, cutoff wavelength and MFD are all read from the light that leaves the fiber, so they describe the fiber as the network sees it.
  8. Field tests use the same ideas as lab tests, but with an OTDR, an optical power meter and a stable source that fit in a van.

Glossary

Attenuation
Loss of optical power along the fiber, quoted in dB per kilometre at a stated wavelength.
Bit period (T)
The time occupied by one bit; T = 1 ÷ bit rate, measured in seconds.
Cladding mode
Light travelling in the cladding rather than the core; it must be stripped before a measurement or the reading is too high.
Connector
A removable joint between two fibers or a fiber and a device, typically losing 0.3 to 1 dB.
Cutoff wavelength
The wavelength below which a single-mode fiber begins to carry a second mode.
Dead zone
The length after a strong reflection in an OTDR trace where a second event cannot be seen.
Dispersion coefficient (D)
Pulse spread per unit length per unit source linewidth, in ps/(nm·km).
Far-field pattern
The spread of light intensity measured far from the fiber end face; it gives NA and MFD.
Fusion splice
A permanent joint made by melting two fiber ends together, typically losing 0.05 to 0.1 dB.
Index difference (Δ)
Fractional step between core and cladding index, Δ = (n₁ − n₂) ÷ n₁.
Launch condition
How light enters the fiber — spot size, angle and mode filling — which changes measured loss and NA.
Mode-field diameter (MFD)
Width of the optical power distribution in a single-mode fiber, close to but not equal to the core diameter.
Numerical aperture (NA)
The sine of the largest half-angle of a cone that the fiber guides; NA = √(n₁² − n₂²).
Optical return loss (ORL)
Ratio of reflected to incident power at a joint, expressed as a positive dB number.
OTDR
Optical time-domain reflectometer; it sends pulses and reads backscatter to give loss, events and distance.
Power penalty
Extra decibels of power needed to hold the same BER when a new impairment such as dispersion is added.
Reflectance
The fraction of incident power reflected at a joint or end face; ORL in dB is its negative logarithm times ten.
Relative intensity noise (RIN)
Laser power fluctuation quoted in dB per hertz; it limits CNR at high received power.
Rise time
Time for a pulse to swing from 10 percent to 90 percent of its final value.
Spectral width (Δλ)
Range of wavelengths emitted by the source; it multiplies with dispersion to spread the pulse.
System margin
Decibels reserved in the power budget for ageing, repairs and temperature, usually 4 to 6 dB.
Wavelength division multiplexing (WDM)
Sending several wavelengths on one fiber; it adds multiplexers and demultiplexers to the power budget.
