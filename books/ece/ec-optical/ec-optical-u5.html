5.1 Digital and analog point-to-point links

A point-to-point link is one transmitter, one fiber path and one receiver, with only splices and connectors between them: a trunk between two telephone exchanges, a cable-TV headend feeding a street node, a run between two buildings. Digital and analog links use the same blocks. A digital link is judged by how many bits arrive wrong, an analog link by how much noise rides on the waveform, and that difference decides what the receiver is designed for.

You need optical sources (LED and laser), photodetectors and responsivity, and receiver noise and bit error rate.

The blocks of a point-to-point link

Both link types use the same chain of blocks.

  • Driver and source. An LED or laser diode and the circuit that drives it: current pulses for a digital link, a bias plus a varying signal current for an analog one. The output is the mean launched power PT in dBm.
  • Fiber, splices and connectors. The cable loses αL dB (α in dB/km, L in km); each joint adds a fixed loss.
  • Photodiode. A pin photodiode or avalanche photodiode (APD) turns received power P into current Ip = ℛP, with ℛ the responsivity in A/W.
  • Preamplifier and filter. Raises the current and limits the bandwidth B, which sets how much noise passes.
  • Decision circuit or measurement stage. A digital receiver samples each bit and decides 1 or 0; an analog receiver keeps the waveform, measured for carrier-to-noise ratio.

Every loss before the photodiode lowers the current, but the preamplifier's thermal noise does not shrink with it, so received power is the first thing a design checks.

Digital links: the system considerations

A digital link design starts from three requirements: the bit rate B in bit/s, the distance L in km, and the bit error rate (BER) the user will accept. BER is the fraction of bits decided wrongly. A target of 10⁻⁹ at 2.5 Gbit/s still means 2.5 errors every second, so faster links ask for 10⁻¹² or use error-correcting codes.

The designer then chooses the parts; some syllabi call these the system considerations:

Every design then faces two checks. The link power budget asks whether enough light arrives. The rise-time budget asks whether the pulses are still sharp enough. The link works only if it passes both.

Analog links: modulation index and carrier-to-noise ratio

In an analog link the light intensity follows the signal: the laser is biased at a steady power and the signal swings it up and down, so the source must be linear. The modulation index m is the peak deviation of the power from its mean, divided by the mean: a swing from 0.5P to 1.5P is m = 0.5, not 1.

The photocurrent has a mean Ip = ℛP and a signal of peak mIp, whose mean-square value is (mIp)²/2. Three noises add, each a mean-square current in A²:

CNR = ½(m ℛ P)² ÷ [ RIN·(ℛP)²·B + 2q(Ip + ID)B + 4kBTBFt/Req ]

P is mean received power in W; RIN is the laser's relative intensity noise per Hz (−143 dB/Hz = 10−14.3/Hz); B is bandwidth in Hz; q = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; ID is dark current; kB = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K; T in K; Ft is the preamplifier noise figure as a ratio; Req is the load in Ω. CNR(dB) = 10 log₁₀ CNR.

RIN noise grows as P², like the signal, so it sets a CNR ceiling that more power cannot lift. Shot noise grows as P, so a shot-limited CNR changes 10 dB per 10 dB of power. Thermal noise does not depend on P, so a thermal-limited CNR changes 20 dB per 10 dB. A cable-TV channel needs about 50 dB.

When many channels ride on separate subcarriers, the laser's nonlinearity mixes them: two-carrier beats are quoted as composite second order (CSO), three-carrier beats as composite triple beat (CTB). They fall inside other channels and no filter removes them, so m per channel is kept to a few percent.

Worked example

Question. An analog video link uses a laser with RIN = −143 dB/Hz. The receiver has a pin photodiode of responsivity 0.6 A/W, negligible dark current, an equivalent load of 750 Ω and a preamplifier noise figure of 3 dB, at T = 300 K. The channel bandwidth is 6 MHz and the modulation index is m = 0.5. (a) For a received power of −10 dBm, find each noise term and the CNR in dB. (b) Find the CNR if the received power falls to −20 dBm, and say which noise limits each case.

  1. Received power: P = 10−10/10 mW = 0.1 mW = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ W. Photocurrent Ip = ℛP = 0.6 × 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ A = 60 µA.
  2. Signal power: ½(mIp)² = ½ × (0.5 × 6.0 × 10⁻⁵)² = ½ × (3.0 × 10⁻⁵)² = 4.50 × 10⁻¹⁰ A². (The RMS signal current is 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ ÷ √2 = 21.2 µA.)
  3. RIN noise: RIN = 10−14.3 = 5.01 × 10⁻¹⁵ per Hz. RIN·Ip²·B = 5.01 × 10⁻¹⁵ × (6.0 × 10⁻⁵)² × 6 × 10⁶ = 1.08 × 10⁻¹⁶ A².
  4. Shot noise: 2qIpB = 2 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ × 6 × 10⁶ = 1.15 × 10⁻¹⁶ A².
  5. Thermal noise: Ft = 100.3 = 2.0. 4kBTBFt/Req = 4 × 1.381 × 10⁻²³ × 300 × 6 × 10⁶ × 2.0 ÷ 750 = 2.65 × 10⁻¹⁶ A².
  6. Total noise = 1.08 + 1.15 + 2.65 = 4.88 × 10⁻¹⁶ A². CNR = 4.50 × 10⁻¹⁰ ÷ 4.88 × 10⁻¹⁶ = 9.22 × 10⁵, so CNR = 10 log₁₀(9.22 × 10⁵) = 59.6 dB. Thermal noise is the largest of the three.
  7. (b) At −20 dBm, Ip = 6.0 µA. Signal and RIN fall 100 times (4.50 × 10⁻¹² and 1.08 × 10⁻¹⁸ A²), shot noise 10 times (1.15 × 10⁻¹⁷ A²); thermal noise stays at 2.65 × 10⁻¹⁶ A².
  8. Total noise = 0.0108 + 0.115 + 2.65 = 2.77 × 10⁻¹⁶ A², 96 % of it thermal. CNR = 4.50 × 10⁻¹² ÷ 2.77 × 10⁻¹⁶ = 1.62 × 10⁴ = 42.1 dB.

Answer. (a) CNR = 59.6 dB, with thermal noise the largest term. (b) CNR = 42.1 dB: 10 dB less light cost 17.5 dB, because the receiver is now almost purely thermal-noise limited, and a 50 dB video target is missed.

Exam traps

  • dBm is optical power: convert to watts before multiplying by ℛ.
  • Signal power is ½(mIp)²; leaving out the ½ makes the CNR 3 dB too high.
  • Noises add as mean-square currents (A²), never as currents.
  • CNR is a power ratio: use 10 log₁₀. Using 20 log₁₀ doubles the dB value.
  • More power does not cure RIN, CSO or CTB.

5.2 Link power budget

The link power budget adds every loss between transmitter and receiver, plus a reserve, and compares the total with the power the transmitter gives and the receiver needs. The reserve keeps the link working after connectors wear, repair splices are added and the laser ages. Designers use the budget to choose the source, fiber, joints and receiver; if the losses fit, the link closes.

You need fiber attenuation in dB/km, splice and connector losses, and receiver sensitivity.

The budget equation

PT is the mean power launched into the fiber, in dBm. PR is the receiver sensitivity: the smallest mean power, in dBm, that gives the required BER at the bit rate. PT − PR is the loss you can afford, in dB.

PT − PR = αL + lcNc + lsNs + M

PT is launched power in dBm; PR is receiver sensitivity in dBm; α is fiber attenuation in dB/km; L is fiber length in km; lc is the loss per connector in dB and Nc the number of connectors; ls is the loss per splice in dB and Ns the number of splices; M is the system margin in dB.

Both sides are in dB. M is not a loss you can measure on a new link but a reserve for source ageing, temperature, repair splices and extra patch cords: usually 6 dB, 3 to 4 dB for a short indoor link.

The received power is PT minus the physical losses only, in dBm. The spare margin is what is left after the reserve: spare = (PT − PR) − (αL + lcNc + lsNs + M). A spare of zero or more closes the budget.

The receiver has a ceiling too: overload, the largest power before the preamplifier saturates. The received power must lie between PR and overload; a short link with a strong laser needs a fixed attenuator.

Worked example

Question. Design check for a 2.5 Gbit/s link: a 1550 nm laser launches 0 dBm into 60 km of single-mode fiber with α = 0.22 dB/km, laid in 10 km reels joined by fusion splices of 0.1 dB each. There is one connector of 0.5 dB at each end. The receiver sensitivity is −28 dBm at 2.5 Gbit/s and its overload level is −8 dBm. A 6 dB system margin is required. Does the budget close? Find the received power and the spare margin.

  1. Splices: 60 km in 10 km reels is 6 reels, so Ns = 6 − 1 = 5 splices inside the span. The two ends are connectors, Nc = 2.
  2. Losses: fiber 0.22 × 60 = 13.2 dB, connectors 2 × 0.5 = 1.0 dB, splices 5 × 0.1 = 0.5 dB; physical total 14.7 dB, so the received power is −14.7 dBm.
  3. Overload: −14.7 dBm is below −8 dBm, so no attenuator is needed. Available 0 − (−28) = 28 dB; used with margin 14.7 + 6 = 20.7 dB.
  4. Spare = 28 − 20.7 = 7.3 dB. Check: −14.7 dBm is 13.3 dB above sensitivity, 6 dB reserve plus 7.3 dB spare.

Answer. It closes: 20.7 dB used of 28 dB, a spare of 7.3 dB, and −14.7 dBm received, between sensitivity and overload.

Question. Exam traps

  • Adding a dBm value to a dB value. PT, PR and the received power are in dBm; the losses and the margin are in dB.
  • Leaving the margin out of the used total, or putting it into the received power. The received power counts physical losses only; the margin is held back from the spare.
  • Miscounting joints: 60 km in 10 km reels has five splices, not six, and both end connectors count.
  • Treating sensitivity as the only limit. A short link with a strong laser can exceed the overload level and fail from too much light.
  • Leaving out the dispersion power penalty when the link is close to its dispersion limit.

5.3 Rise-time budget; attenuation and dispersion limits

Loss sets how far a link reaches; rise time sets how fast it can run. Each part of the link slows the edges of a pulse, and a pulse that spreads too much runs into its neighbour. The rise-time budget adds these slowdowns and compares the total with the bit period. With the power budget it gives two maximum lengths, one set by attenuation and one by dispersion; the shorter is the real reach.

You need the link power budget, and modal and chromatic dispersion in fibers.

The rise-time budget

A rise time is the 10 % to 90 % time of a step response. Four independent slowdowns act: the transmitter (ttx), modal dispersion (tmod), chromatic dispersion (tchrom) and the receiver (trx). For near-Gaussian responses they add in quadrature:

tsys = √(ttx² + tmod² + tchrom² + trx²)

tsys is the system rise time; all four terms in the same unit (ns or ps).

With bit period Tb = 1/B, the rule is tsys ≤ 0.7Tb for NRZ and tsys ≤ 0.35Tb for RZ, whose pulse fills only half the slot. Turned round, the highest bit rate is Bmax = 0.7/tsys (NRZ) or 0.35/tsys (RZ). Some syllabi call this the bandwidth budget.

The four terms

  • Transmitter ttx. From the data sheet: a few ns for an LED, tens to hundreds of ps for a laser.
  • Receiver trx. trx = 350/Brx ns, with Brx the receiver's 3 dB bandwidth in MHz.
  • Modal tmod. Multimode fiber only: tmod = 440 Lq/B0 ns, with B0 the bandwidth of 1 km in MHz·km and L in km. Mode mixing makes q lie between 0.5 and 1 (0.7 is typical). Zero in single-mode fiber.
  • Chromatic tchrom. Every fiber: tchrom = |D| L σλ, with D in ps/(nm·km), L in km and σλ the source spectral width in nm (the half-power width in most link-design texts; use the width the question gives).

The source width dominates. Over 20 km at 1550 nm (D = 17 ps/(nm·km)) a 2 nm Fabry–Perot laser gives 680 ps but a 0.1 nm DFB laser 34 ps; a 40 nm LED at 850 nm (|D| ≈ 90 ps/(nm·km)) gives 18 ns over just 5 km.

Attenuation limit and dispersion limit

Solving the power budget for L gives the attenuation limit Latt = (PT − PR − joint losses − M) ÷ α: with 28 dB available, no joints or margin and 0.22 dB/km, 127 km. It is flat in B for a fixed receiver; real sensitivity worsens as B rises, so it falls slowly.

The dispersion limit is where the fiber term uses up the allowance: Ldisp = √((0.7/B)² − ttx² − trx²) ÷ (|D|σλ) for single-mode fiber. Ignoring ttx and trx gives the quick bound Ldisp ≈ 0.7/(B|D|σλ), falling as 1/B: 20.6 km at 1 Gbit/s for |D|σλ = 34 ps/km (Fabry–Perot), 412 km for 1.7 ps/km (DFB).

The reach is the smaller of the two: loss wins at low bit rates, dispersion at high ones, crossing near 160 Mbit/s for the Fabry–Perot laser and 3.2 Gbit/s for the DFB. A dispersion-limited link needs a narrower source or a wavelength nearer the zero-dispersion point; a loss-limited one needs more power, a better receiver or an amplifier.

Worked example

Question. A 20 Mbit/s NRZ link runs 5 km over graded-index multimode fiber at 850 nm. The LED has a rise time of 12 ns and a spectral width of 40 nm. The fiber has |D| = 90 ps/(nm·km) and B0 = 500 MHz·km (take q = 0.7). The receiver bandwidth is 30 MHz. Does the link pass the rise-time budget? Find the highest NRZ and RZ bit rates it can carry.

  1. Allowed: Tb = 1/(20 × 10⁶) = 50 ns, so tsys ≤ 35 ns.
  2. Terms: tchrom = 0.090 × 5 × 40 = 18.0 ns; tmod = 440 × 50.7 ÷ 500 = 440 × 3.085 ÷ 500 = 2.71 ns; trx = 350 ÷ 30 = 11.67 ns; ttx = 12 ns.
  3. tsys = √(12² + 2.71² + 18.0² + 11.67²) = √(144 + 7.4 + 324 + 136.1) = √611.5 = 24.7 ns.
  4. 24.7 ns ≤ 35 ns: it passes, and the LED's spectral width is the largest term.
  5. Highest bit rates: NRZ Bmax = 0.7 ÷ 24.7 ns = 28.3 Mbit/s; RZ Bmax = 0.35 ÷ 24.7 ns = 14.2 Mbit/s.

Answer. It passes (24.7 ns against 35 ns), and carries up to 28.3 Mbit/s NRZ or 14.2 Mbit/s RZ, so RZ at 20 Mbit/s would fail.

Worked example 2

Question. A 1 Gbit/s NRZ link uses 20 km of standard single-mode fiber at 1550 nm (D = 17 ps/(nm·km)) and a Fabry–Perot laser of spectral width 2 nm. The transmitter rise time is 250 ps and the receiver rise time 350 ps. The launched power is 0 dBm, the sensitivity −28 dBm, α = 0.22 dB/km, with no joints or margin. Does the link pass?   1. Allowed: Tb = 1000 ps, so tsys ≤ 700 ps. tchrom = 17 × 20 × 2 = 680 ps; tmod = 0.
  2. tsys = √(250² + 680² + 350²) = √647 400 = 804.6 ps > 700 ps: it fails, and could carry only 0.7 ÷ 804.6 ps = 0.87 Gbit/s NRZ.
  3. Allowed fiber rise time: √(700² − 250² − 350²) = √(490 000 − 62 500 − 122 500) = √305 000 = 552.3 ps.
  4. Longest length from rise time: L = 552.3 ÷ (17 × 2) = 16.2 km.

Exam traps

  • Adding rise times directly: 12 + 2.7 + 18 + 11.7 = 44.4 ns would wrongly fail the first example.
  • Using 0.7Tb for RZ. RZ allows only 0.35Tb.
  • Mixing units: trx = 350/Brx gives ns only with Brx in MHz; D in ps/(nm·km) gives tchrom in ps.
  • Quoting the dispersion limit alone as the reach. The reach is the smaller of Latt and Ldisp.

5.4 Attenuation and dispersion measurements

The two budgets rest on two fiber numbers, attenuation and dispersion, and both must be measured on the fiber actually bought or laid. Attenuation is measured by the cut-back method on the bench and by an optical time-domain reflectometer (OTDR) on installed cable. Dispersion is measured in the time domain, from how much a pulse broadens, or in the frequency domain. Only a few syllabi teach these measurements in this detail.

You need fiber attenuation and dispersion, and the rise-time budget that uses them.

Total attenuation by the cut-back method

The cut-back method compares the power out of a long fiber with the power out of a short piece of it, with the launch untouched, so launch and detector cancel and the difference is the loss of the removed length.

  1. Launch light at the test wavelength through a mode scrambler, so the modes are filled the same way every time.
  2. Strip cladding modes at the far end and read P1 through the full length L1 on a large-area detector.
  3. Cut the fiber about 2 m from the input and read P2 through the short length L2.
  4. Compute α; repeat at other wavelengths for a loss spectrum.
α = 10 log₁₀(P2/P1) ÷ (L1 − L2)

α is the attenuation in dB/km; P1 and P2 are the powers out of the long and the short length, in the same unit; L1 and L2 are those lengths in km.

Cut-back destroys fiber, so it suits the factory. For cable with connectors the insertion-loss method reads a reference through a short patch cord, inserts the cable and reads again; the difference includes the connectors.

Attenuation from an OTDR

An OTDR launches pulses into one end and records the Rayleigh backscatter against time. Light from distance z returns after t, so z = ct/(2n), with c = 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s and n the group index (about 1.47). The backscatter loses α going out and α coming back, so the raw trace falls at 2α; the display is halved, and its slope is α.

On the raw trace, α = 10 log₁₀(P(z1)/P(z2)) ÷ 2(z2 − z1). It needs one end only and also shows the splices and breaks used in the field tests.

Dispersion in the time domain and in the frequency domain

Time domain (pulse broadening). Record the half-maximum width of a narrow pulse at the input (τi) and output (τo). For near-Gaussian pulses the fiber's own broadening is τ = √(τo² − τi²) and its 3 dB optical bandwidth Bopt ≈ 0.44/τ. It suits multimode fiber.

Frequency domain (baseband response). Sweep a sinusoidal modulation and compare the received power through the long fiber and a short reference; the 3 dB (optical) point of the ratio is Bopt, with no pulse shape assumed.

Chromatic dispersion of single-mode fiber. The group delay τg is measured at several wavelengths, either as pulse arrival times (pulse delay) or as the phase of a sine wave modulated at fm (phase shift), where Δφ = 360° × fm × Δτ. Then D = (1/L) × dτg/dλ in ps/(nm·km). The phase-shift method needs no picosecond pulses and is the usual production method.

Worked example

Question. A cut-back measurement at 850 nm on multimode fiber reads 6.3 µW through 2.0 km. With the launch untouched, the fiber is cut to 2 m and the meter reads 20.0 µW. Find α and the total loss of the 2.0 km length.

  1. L1 = 2.0 km and L2 = 2 m = 0.002 km, so L1 − L2 = 1.998 km.
  2. Power ratio P2/P1 = 20.0 ÷ 6.3 = 3.175; loss = 10 log₁₀ 3.175 = 5.02 dB.
  3. α = 5.02 ÷ 1.998 = 2.51 dB/km.
  4. Total loss of the full 2.0 km: 2.51 × 2.0 = 5.02 dB (the 2 m stub adds only 0.005 dB).
  5. Check: 2.51 dB/km is normal for multimode fiber at 850 nm.

Answer. α = 2.51 dB/km; the 2.0 km length loses 5.02 dB.

Worked example 2

Question. A 0.4 ns (FWHM) pulse is launched into 2.0 km of graded-index fiber and leaves it 2.5 ns wide. Find the pulse broadening due to the fiber, the broadening per km, the 3 dB optical bandwidth and the bandwidth-length product.

  1. Fiber broadening τ = √(τo² − τi²) = √(2.5² − 0.4²) = √(6.25 − 0.16) = √6.09 = 2.47 ns.
  2. Per kilometre: 2.47 ÷ 2.0 = 1.23 ns/km.
  3. Optical bandwidth: Bopt = 0.44 ÷ 2.47 ns = 178 MHz.
  4. Bandwidth-length product: 178 MHz × 2.0 km = 357 MHz·km, a typical value for graded-index fiber.

Answer. τ = 2.47 ns (1.23 ns/km), Bopt = 178 MHz and a bandwidth-length product of about 357 MHz·km.

Exam traps

  • Dividing by L1 instead of L1 − L2. The short piece was never part of the measured difference.
  • Taking the raw OTDR backscatter slope as α. Light goes out and back, so the raw slope is 2α; the displayed trace is already halved.
  • Subtracting pulse widths directly: 2.5 − 0.4 = 2.1 ns is wrong; widths subtract in quadrature.
  • The baseband response gives bandwidth; the phase shift against wavelength gives D.

5.5 Measuring NA, cutoff wavelength and diameter; field tests

Numerical aperture, cutoff wavelength and diameter decide how a fiber couples to sources, whether it is single-mode at the working wavelength, and how much a joint loses. All three are read from light leaving the fiber or from an image of its end face. Field tests then check the laid route without cutting it. Only a few syllabi teach this measurement detail.

You need numerical aperture and acceptance angle, single-mode condition and the V number, and the OTDR from attenuation and dispersion measurements.

Numerical aperture from the far field

Light leaves a fiber in a cone whose half-angle is the acceptance angle θa, so NA = sin θa. A scanning detector records far-field intensity against angle, and θa is read where it has fallen to 5 % of the peak (not the 50 % level). The simpler trigonometric method throws the spot onto a screen at distance D and measures its diameter A at the same edge:

NA = sin θa = A ÷ √(A² + 4D²)

A is the far-field spot diameter on the screen and D is the distance from the fiber end to the screen, in the same unit; tan θa = (A/2)/D.

The index profile gives NA = √(n1² − n2²) (core n1, cladding n2), which agrees only approximately: the far-field value depends on the launch, so it must fill the modes steadily, with cladding modes stripped and a few metres of fiber.

Cutoff wavelength and diameter

The cutoff wavelength λc is the wavelength above which only the fundamental mode LP01 is guided. Below it LP11 is guided too, but weakly, so a bend strips it. In the bend-reference method a 2 m sample in one loose loop is scanned in wavelength, then scanned again with a small extra loop that strips LP11. Their ratio in dB is flat at long wavelengths and rises below cutoff; λc is where it is 0.1 dB above the flat level.

Cutoff depends on length and bending, so the method must be stated. Installed cable cuts off at a shorter wavelength than the 2 m fiber (the cable cutoff λcc); standard single-mode fiber has λcc ≤ 1260 nm, so it is single-mode at 1310 and 1550 nm.

The cladding diameter (125 µm for telecom fiber) and core concentricity are measured on a calibrated video microscope image of the end face, or, during drawing, by timing the shadow of a scanning laser beam. The core diameter comes from the near-field intensity or a refracted near-field index profile. A diameter error offsets the cores at every joint and shows up as joint loss.

Reflectance, return loss and field tests

Every joint reflects some light back toward the laser. Reflectance R = 10 log₁₀(Pr/Pi) (reflected over incident power) is negative, −50 dB or lower for a good connector. Optical return loss ORL = −10 log₁₀(Pr/Pi) is positive; the higher it is, the less the laser is disturbed.

  • Stable source and power meter. End-to-end loss at the working wavelength, compared with the link power budget.
  • OTDR. Locates every event at z = ct/(2n): a step at a splice or bend, a spike at a connector or break. A splice can read as a gain from one end because the fibers backscatter differently; the true loss is the average of both directions. A strong reflection blinds the detector for a short dead zone.
  • Visual fault locator. Red light (about 650 nm) glows through the jacket at a break or tight bend.
  • Dispersion and BER testers. Check high-speed routes; a bit-error test counts errors in known data.

Worked example

Question. In a trigonometric NA test, the far-field spot of a step-index multimode fiber falls on a screen 10.0 cm from the end face. The spot diameter at the 5 % intensity edge is 4.6 cm. The core index is 1.48. Find the NA, the acceptance angle, the cladding index and the relative index difference Δ.

  1. A² + 4D² = 4.6² + 4 × 10.0² = 21.16 + 400 = 421.16, and √421.16 = 20.52 cm.
  2. NA = A ÷ √(A² + 4D²) = 4.6 ÷ 20.52 = 0.224.
  3. Acceptance angle θa = sin⁻¹ 0.224 = 12.95°. Check: tan θa = 2.3 ÷ 10.0 = 0.230, and tan⁻¹ 0.230 = 12.95°.
  4. Cladding index: n2 = √(n1² − NA²) = √(1.48² − 0.224²) = √(2.1904 − 0.0502) = √2.1402 = 1.463.
  5. Δ = (n1 − n2) ÷ n1 = (1.48 − 1.463) ÷ 1.48 = 0.0115, about 1.2 %.

Answer. NA = 0.224, θa = 12.95° (a full cone of 25.9°), n2 = 1.463 and Δ ≈ 1.2 %.

Exam traps

  • Using the 50 % (half-power) points of the far field for NA. The standard uses the 5 % level.
  • Putting the spot radius for A: A is the diameter.
  • Calling the operating wavelength the cutoff wavelength, or forgetting that cable cutoff is shorter than the 2 m fiber cutoff.
  • Reflectance and ORL signs: reflectance is negative (−50 dB), ORL is positive (50 dB).
  • Trusting a one-direction OTDR splice reading, or reading an event inside the dead zone after a connector.

Takeaways

  1. A point-to-point link is one chain of blocks; a digital link is judged by BER, an analog link by CNR (and CSO and CTB when it carries many channels).
  2. Analog CNR has three noises: RIN grows with P², shot noise with P, thermal noise not at all, so a thermal-limited link loses 20 dB of CNR for every 10 dB of power lost.
  3. The power budget subtracts every loss and a margin from PT − PR; what is left is the spare, and the received power must also stay below overload.
  4. Rise times add in quadrature, and the total must stay within 0.7 of the bit period for NRZ or 0.35 for RZ; the same rule gives the highest bit rate.
  5. Loss sets Latt, dispersion sets Ldisp, and the link reaches only the smaller; dispersion wins at high bit rates and with wide sources.
  6. Cut-back attenuation keeps the launch fixed so it cancels; the OTDR measures from one end, with distance z = ct/(2n).
  7. Dispersion is measured by pulse broadening or baseband response (bandwidth), and by pulse delay or phase shift against wavelength (chromatic D).
  8. NA comes from the far-field cone at the 5 % level, cutoff from the bend-reference ratio at 0.1 dB, and diameter from an end-face image.
  9. Field tests use a source and power meter for total loss, an OTDR for events (averaged from both ends), return-loss readings and a visual fault locator.

Glossary

Attenuation
Loss of optical power along the fiber, in dB/km at a stated wavelength.
Avalanche photodiode (APD)
A photodiode with internal gain; about 5 to 10 dB more sensitive than a pin photodiode, at the cost of a high bias voltage.
Bit period (Tb)
Time taken by one bit, Tb = 1/B.
Cable cutoff wavelength (λcc)
Cutoff of the fiber in its cable; shorter than the 2 m fiber cutoff, at most 1260 nm for standard single-mode fiber.
Cladding-mode stripper
A short section that removes light travelling in the cladding before a measurement.
Composite second order (CSO) and composite triple beat (CTB)
Distortion products from two and three subcarriers mixing in a multichannel analog link.
Cut-back method
Attenuation measurement that reads the power through a long fiber and then through a short piece of it, with the launch untouched.
Dead zone
Length after a strong reflection on an OTDR trace in which no other event can be read.
Dispersion coefficient (D)
Change of group delay with wavelength per unit length, in ps/(nm·km).
Dispersion power penalty
Extra received power needed to hold the BER when dispersion closes the eye.
Far-field pattern
Intensity of the light leaving the fiber, measured far from the end face against angle.
Insertion-loss method
Loss test that compares a reference reading through a short cord with a reading through the cable under test.
Mode scrambler
A device that fills the fiber's modes in a fixed, repeatable way at the launch.
Numerical aperture (NA)
Sine of the acceptance half-angle; NA = √(n1² − n2²).
Optical return loss (ORL)
Incident over reflected power in dB, a positive number; higher is better.
OTDR
Optical time-domain reflectometer: sends pulses and plots backscatter against distance to show loss and events.
Overload
Largest received power a receiver accepts before saturating.
Phase-shift method
Chromatic dispersion measurement that reads the phase of a modulated signal as the wavelength is stepped.
Receiver sensitivity
Smallest mean received power that gives the required BER at the bit rate.
Reflectance
Reflected over incident power at one joint, in dB; a negative number.
Relative intensity noise (RIN)
Laser power fluctuation relative to the mean power, in dB/Hz.
Rise time
Time a response takes to go from 10 % to 90 % of its final value.
Spectral width (σλ)
Range of wavelengths a source emits; it multiplies with D and L to spread a pulse.
System margin
Decibels held back in the power budget for ageing, temperature and repairs.
Visual fault locator
A red laser source whose light leaks out at a break or tight bend, showing where it is.
