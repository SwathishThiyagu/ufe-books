1.1 Optical fiber communication system and its advantages

An optical fiber communication system carries information as light. A transmitter converts an electrical data signal into light, a glass fiber guides the light over distance, and a receiver converts the light back into an electrical signal. This idea exists because light in silica glass can travel far with low loss and can be switched very fast. Telephone trunks, internet backbones, cable TV, mobile towers and bank links use it. At 1550 nm, one fiber can carry many high-speed channels at once, which copper cables cannot do over long distances.

The three blocks of a fiber link

Every fiber link has three blocks: transmitter, fiber channel, receiver. The transmitter takes an electrical input, such as a digital bit stream from a router or an analog video signal from a cable head-end, and drives an optical source. The optical source is a light-emitting diode (LED) or a semiconductor laser. It emits light whose power follows the electrical signal. The fiber is a thin strand of silica glass with a core of higher refractive index and a cladding of lower refractive index. The core guides the light by total internal reflection; see 1.2. The receiver uses a photodetector, usually a PIN photodiode or an avalanche photodiode (APD), to convert incident light into current. A receiver circuit then amplifies and reshapes the signal to give the electrical output.

For a digital link, the source is switched between two light levels, often on and off, and the receiver decides each bit. For an analog link, the light power varies continuously with the input waveform, as in older cable TV distribution. The same three-block picture applies to both. The usual wavelength windows are named by their centre wavelengths: 850 nm for short multimode links, 1300 nm for metro links, and 1550 nm for long-haul links. Loss values for these windows belong to Unit 2.

Advantages over copper cable

Copper cable carries data as an electric current. Fiber carries data as light. This change gives several practical advantages.

  • Wide bandwidth. Light has a very high frequency, so the fiber channel can be modulated at very high rates. A single silica fiber can carry many gigabits per second, and with wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) it can carry many wavelengths at once. A copper pair has much lower bandwidth and loses more at high frequency.
  • Low loss. Silica glass is very transparent at the infrared windows. A signal can travel tens of kilometres before it needs amplification or regeneration. Copper suffers higher loss, so long copper links need repeaters frequently. Exact loss values are in Unit 2.
  • Immunity to electromagnetic interference. A fiber is a dielectric, not a conductor. It does not pick up currents from nearby power lines, lightning or electric motors. It also does not radiate, so it does not disturb other circuits. This helps near railway traction or heavy motors.
  • Small size and weight. One fiber is thinner than a human hair. A cable with many fibers has much less weight and bulk than a copper cable with the same capacity. This reduces duct and tower loading.
  • Electrical isolation. There is no metallic path between the two ends. This breaks ground loops and protects equipment from voltage differences and lightning surges.
  • Security. Light stays inside the core. Tapping a fiber is harder than tapping a copper pair, and a tap can change the loss and be detected. Fiber does not broadcast a signal that a nearby antenna can pick up.

These advantages explain why backbone routes, undersea cables, UPI payment data centres and mobile tower links use fiber.

Analog, digital, and the wavelength windows

At block level, an analog fiber link maps a continuous input voltage to a continuous light power. The receiver recovers a continuous voltage. Analog links are used for cable TV and some radio-over-fiber feeds. A digital fiber link maps bits to light pulses. The receiver samples each bit interval and decides whether the light was high or low. Almost all telephone and internet traffic is digital.

The link can also be described by its wavelength window. The window name is the centre wavelength of the source. 850 nm is used with short multimode links inside buildings. 1300 nm is used in metro links because it has low dispersion; dispersion is covered in Unit 2. 1550 nm is used in long-haul links because silica loss is lowest there. These are names only here; the loss and dispersion numbers come later.

L_dB = α × z

L_dB is total fiber loss in decibels (dB); α is loss per kilometre in dB/km; z is fiber length in kilometres (km).

P_dBm = 10 log₁₀(P_mW / 1 mW)

P_dBm is power in dBm; P_mW is power in milliwatts (mW).

Loss in dB adds with distance. The worked example uses these two relations.

Worked example

Question. A 20 km fiber link uses a 1550 nm source and has fiber loss 0.2 dB/km. Find the total fiber loss. The input power is 1 mW. Find the output power in mW and in dBm.

  1. Identify the given values: loss per kilometre α = 0.2 dB/km and length z = 20 km.
  2. Find total fiber loss: L_dB = α × z = 0.2 dB/km × 20 km = 4 dB.
  3. Convert input power to dBm: P_in,dBm = 10 log₁₀(1 mW / 1 mW) = 10 log₁₀(1) = 0 dBm.
  4. Subtract the loss: P_out,dBm = P_in,dBm − L_dB = 0 dBm − 4 dB = −4 dBm.
  5. Convert back to milliwatts: P_out,mW = 1 mW × 10^(−4/10) = 0.398107 mW.

Answer. The total fiber loss is 4 dB, and with 1 mW input the output is −4 dBm, which is 0.398 mW (about 0.4 mW).

Exam traps

  • Loss in dB adds with distance. Do not treat 0.2 dB/km as the total loss, and do not multiply powers in dB; add or subtract dB values.
  • dBm is an absolute power; dB is a ratio. A loss of 4 dB reduces 0 dBm to −4 dBm.
  • The source is the electrical-to-optical converter; the photodetector is the optical-to-electrical converter. Do not swap them.
  • 850 nm, 1300 nm and 1550 nm are wavelength window names here. They are not loss values; loss numbers come in Unit 2.
  • Fiber immunity to EMI comes from being dielectric, not from carrying a larger current. A fiber carries no current along the data path.

1.2 Ray theory: total internal reflection, acceptance angle

Light can be steered inside a fiber by bouncing it at the core–cladding boundary. The core carries a higher refractive index than the cladding. When a ray strikes the boundary steeply enough, the boundary acts like a mirror. This is total internal reflection. It keeps the light in the core over long distances, so a transmitter can launch power into a narrow input cone and a receiver at the far end still gets signal. The same idea appears in endoscopes, optical sensors and chip waveguides.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for the source–fiber–detector picture and the meaning of a core and cladding.

Refractive index and the critical angle

Refractive index n is the ratio of light speed in vacuum c to light speed in a material v: n = c/v. It has no unit. A larger n means slower light. In a step-index fiber the core has index n1 and the cladding has index n2, with n1 > n2. The medium outside the fiber has index n0; for air n0 = 1.

At a flat boundary between two materials, Snell's law links the angles: n1 sin θ1 = n2 sin θ2. Here θ1 is the angle in the first material measured from the normal, and θ2 is the angle in the second material measured from the same normal. The normal is the line perpendicular to the boundary.

When light goes from the denser core to the rarer cladding (n1 > n2), it bends away from the normal: θ2 > θ1. Increase θ1 and θ2 reaches 90° first. That value is the critical angle θc. Put θ2 = 90° in Snell's law: n1 sin θc = n2 × 1, so θc = sin⁻¹(n2/n1). For θ1 > θc there is no real refracted angle. All the light reflects back into the core. This is total internal reflection, TIR.

θc = sin⁻¹(n2/n1)

θc is the critical angle measured from the normal; n1 is the core refractive index; n2 is the cladding refractive index; n1 > n2.

From the end face to the acceptance cone

Most rays we draw are meridional rays: they pass through the fiber axis. A meridional ray enters the flat end face from air at angle θ0 to the fiber axis. The normal to that end face is the axis itself, so θ0 is also the angle of incidence. Snell's law at the end face is n0 sin θ0 = n1 sin θr, where θr is the ray angle inside the core, measured from the axis.

Inside the core the ray travels in a straight line until it hits the core–cladding boundary. The normal at that side boundary is radial. The angle of incidence at the side boundary is therefore 90° − θr. TIR needs 90° − θr > θc, or θr < 90° − θc.

The largest launch angle that still satisfies this is the acceptance angle θa. At the limit, θr = 90° − θc. Use Snell's law at the end face: n0 sin θa = n1 sin(90° − θc) = n1 cos θc = n1√(1 − sin²θc) = n1√(1 − (n2/n1)²) = √(n1² − n2²). For air outside, n0 = 1, so sin θa = √(n1² − n2²). The acceptance angle is the half-angle of the cone of rays that the fiber accepts. Numerical aperture is NA = sin θa; Chapter 1.3 treats it fully and adds skew rays.

n0 sin θa = √(n1² − n2²)

θa is the acceptance angle in the outside medium; n0 is the outside refractive index; n1 and n2 are core and cladding indices.

What the cone controls

Use the acceptance cone when you couple a source to a fiber. An LED or laser must sit inside the cone for its rays to be guided. A wider cone, meaning larger n1² − n2², accepts more rays. That helps coupling but usually means a larger core and more modes, which can spread the pulse; see Unit 2, Intermodal dispersion. The cone also explains alignment: tilt the fiber or the source beyond θa and launched power falls.

Only rays that are meridional and inside the cone are covered by this simple picture. Rays that miss the axis, called skew rays, follow helical paths and have a different bound condition; see Chapter 1.3. The step-index profile used here has a sudden index jump; Chapter 1.5 shows how grading the index changes ray paths. For a short link, this ray test is enough to decide whether a launched ray stays in the core.

Worked example

Question. A step-index fiber has core index n1 = 1.50 and cladding index n2 = 1.45. The outside medium is air, n0 = 1. Find the critical angle at the core–cladding boundary, the numerical aperture, and the acceptance angle.

  1. Use θc = sin⁻¹(n2/n1). Substitute n2/n1 = 1.45/1.50 = 0.9667.
  2. θc = sin⁻¹(0.9667) = 75.2°.
  3. Compute n1² = 1.50² = 2.2500 and n2² = 1.45² = 2.1025.
  4. n1² − n2² = 2.2500 − 2.1025 = 0.1475.
  5. Numerical aperture NA = √(n1² − n2²) = √0.1475 = 0.3841. With n0 = 1, NA = sin θa = 0.3841.
  6. θa = sin⁻¹(0.3841) = 22.6°.

Answer. The critical angle is 75.2°, the numerical aperture is 0.384, and the acceptance angle is 22.6°.

Exam traps

  • Use n2/n1 for the critical angle, not n1/n2. Since n2 < n1, sin θc is less than 1.
  • TIR needs light to travel from the higher-index core to the lower-index cladding. If n2 > n1, no TIR occurs at that boundary.
  • Measure angles from the normal, not from the surface. A ray 70° from the boundary surface is only 20° from the normal.
  • The acceptance angle is measured in the outside medium, usually air, not inside the core. With n0 ≠ 1, use sin θa = √(n1² − n2²)/n0.
  • Do not call θc the acceptance angle. θc is inside at the core–cladding boundary; θa is at the input face.

1.3 Numerical aperture and skew rays

Numerical aperture (NA) measures how wide a cone of light a fiber accepts. It turns the critical-angle rule from Chapter 1.2 into one number for comparing fibers and estimating launch loss. Inside the fiber, meridional rays cross the axis, while skew rays spiral around it. Skew rays can be bound or leak, and the simple cone does not fully describe them. Only a few universities teach skew-ray detail; the NA formula is standard exam material.

You need: Chapter 1.2 for total internal reflection and the acceptance angle.

Numerical aperture: one number for the cone

A step-index fiber has core index n₁ and cladding index n₂. Light arrives from an outside medium with index n₀. The largest half-angle that can be accepted is θₐ, measured outside. NA is defined so that this cone becomes a single dimensionless number.

NA = n₀ sin θₐ = √(n₁² − n₂²)

NA is numerical aperture, dimensionless; n₀ is the outside refractive index (air n₀ ≈ 1); θₐ is the acceptance half-angle in that outside medium, in degrees or radians; n₁ is core index; n₂ is cladding index.

With air outside, n₀ = 1, so NA = sin θₐ and θₐ = sin⁻¹(NA). In another medium, divide by n₀: sin θₐ = √(n₁² − n₂²)/n₀. A larger NA gives a wider cone and easier coupling, but also more guided modes.

Δ = (n₁ − n₂)/n₁, NA ≈ n₁√(2Δ)

Δ is the relative index difference, dimensionless; the approximation holds for small Δ, as in most communication fibers.

Meridional rays, skew rays and the bound condition

A meridional ray lies in a plane through the fiber axis and crosses the axis after reflections. The acceptance cone in Chapter 1.2 is a meridional result: launch within θₐ and total internal reflection can bind the ray.

A skew ray misses the axis. It travels as a helix, reflecting while turning around the core. Its path needs two angles: slope along the axis and rotation around it. One launch angle cannot describe it.

  • Meridional: crosses axis; simple NA cone applies.
  • Skew: helical; bound or unbound depends on both angles.
  • For a skew ray, the incidence angle at the core–cladding boundary is still constant along the path, but it is larger than for a meridional ray with the same axial slope, so the bound-ray condition is modified (skew rays are trapped more easily). Only a few universities teach the full inequality; this course stops at the idea.

Use NA for source coupling and meridional acceptance. Treat skew rays as extra paths in multimode fiber. Exact skew-ray acceptance is left to advanced texts; their loss and dispersion belong to Unit 2, Signal Degradation in Optical Fibers.

Worked example

Question. A step-index fiber has core index n₁ = 1.48, cladding index n₂ = 1.46, and air outside (n₀ = 1). Find the numerical aperture, the acceptance angle in air, the relative index difference Δ, and check NA with the small-Δ formula.

  1. Square the indices: n₁² = 1.48² = 2.1904 and n₂² = 1.46² = 2.1316.
  2. Subtract: n₁² − n₂² = 2.1904 − 2.1316 = 0.0588. Then NA = √0.0588 = 0.2425.
  3. With air outside, sin θₐ = NA/n₀ = 0.2425/1 = 0.2425. So θₐ = sin⁻¹(0.2425) = 14.0°.
  4. Find Δ = (n₁ − n₂)/n₁ = (1.48 − 1.46)/1.48 = 0.02/1.48 = 0.0135.
  5. Check: NA ≈ n₁√(2Δ) = 1.48√(2 × 0.0135) = 1.48√0.0270 = 1.48 × 0.1644 = 0.243. The exact value 0.2425 is close, so the small-Δ form is valid.

Answer. NA = 0.2425, θₐ = 14.0°, Δ = 0.0135, and the small-Δ check gives NA ≈ 0.243.

Exam traps

  • NA is dimensionless. Do not write NA = 14.0°; the angle is θₐ.
  • θₐ is measured in the outside medium, usually air. It is not the angle inside the core; inside, the ray angle is smaller.
  • Use NA = √(n₁² − n₂²) for a step-index fiber with a sharp core–cladding boundary. Graded-index fiber needs a local definition.
  • NA ≈ n₁√(2Δ) is a small-Δ approximation. Compare exact and approximate values when Δ is not small.
  • Skew rays do not obey the simple meridional acceptance cone. Do not use NA alone to prove a skew ray is bound.

1.4 Electromagnetic mode theory; modes in planar guides

Ray optics tells you whether light is trapped by total internal reflection. It cannot tell you how many distinct light patterns the guide carries, or how tightly each is held. Maxwell's equations answer both: in a flat slab of high-index core between low-index cladding, the field settles into a few fixed shapes called modes, each travelling along z without changing shape. This slab is a planar guide, the flat version of an optical fibre. Planar guides appear in chip waveguides, splitters and modulator arms.

You need: Chapter 1.2 for total internal reflection and Chapter 1.3 for the idea of a bound ray, plus the standard Maxwell's equations in a dielectric.

Maxwell's equations in a layered slab

A planar guide is three flat layers: a core of refractive index n1 and thickness d, sitting between two claddings of refractive index n2, with n1 > n2. The layers are wide in y, so nothing varies along y. Light travels along z, and all the variation we must solve for is across x, the thickness direction.

Inside a medium of refractive index n, Maxwell's equations combine into the wave equation.

∇²E = μ ε ∂²E/∂t²

E is the electric field in V/m; μ is the permeability in H/m; ε = ε₀n² is the permittivity in F/m; t is time in s.

For a wave travelling along z at angular frequency ω, write E(x,z,t) = E(x)e^(j(ωt − βz)), where β is the propagation constant in rad/m. The equation for the core becomes:

d²E/dx² + (k0²n1² − β²)E = 0

k0 = 2π/λ is the free-space wavenumber in rad/m; λ is the vacuum wavelength in m; β is the mode's propagation constant in rad/m; n1 is the core index.

The cladding obeys the same equation with n2 in place of n1. The two solutions must join at x = ±d/2, the core–cladding interfaces, where the tangential components of E and H are continuous.

That continuity is a strong filter. Only a few values of β let the field oscillate in the core, decay in the cladding, and still match at both interfaces. Those few are the guided modes: field patterns that keep their shape as they travel and carry power along z.

TE, TM, mode number m and the V number

Two polarizations exist. A TE mode (transverse electric) has E only along y, with H having x and z parts. A TM mode (transverse magnetic) has H only along y, with E having x and z parts. Each polarization gets its own set of allowed β values from the same boundary conditions.

In the core the field oscillates as cos(κx) or sin(κx), where κ = √(k0²n1² − β²) is the transverse wavenumber in rad/m. In the cladding it decays as A·e^(−γ(|x| − d/2)), where γ = √(β² − k0²n2²) in 1/m and A is the field value at the interface. A guided mode needs κ and γ both real, which means n2k0 < β < n1k0.

The number of nulls across the core labels the mode: m = 0, 1, 2, … The m = 0 mode is a single central peak, the fundamental. m = 1 has one null, and higher m means a steeper zigzag and weaker confinement.

V = (2πd/λ)√(n1² − n2²)

V is the normalized frequency, dimensionless; d is the core thickness in m; λ is the free-space wavelength in m; n1 and n2 are the core and cladding refractive indices.

V packs thickness, wavelength and index step into one number. The m-th mode just fits when V = mπ, so it is guided when V > mπ. The total count for one polarization is about floor(V/π) + 1. Keeping only the m = 0 mode needs V < π.

Reading the profile; where planar guides are used

Near cutoff the mode is loose: much of its power travels in the cladding. As V grows the field pulls into the core and the confinement factor — the fraction of power inside the core — rises towards one. Designing a guide is mostly choosing d so the wanted mode sits well above cutoff.

V ties back to the numerical aperture. For a step-index guide NA = √(n1² − n2²) exactly, so V = (2πd/λ)·NA. A bigger launch cone means a bigger V and more modes, the trade-off met in Chapter 1.3. The small-index-step form is NA ≈ n1√(2Δ), with Δ = (n1 − n2)/n1.

Planar guides are the base of integrated optics. Modulator arms, silicon photonics strip waveguides, directional couplers and arrayed waveguide gratings all start from this three-layer slab. Etching the slab into a ridge confines light in two directions instead of one — that is Chapter 1.8.

A round fibre is the next step: the same wave equation in cylindrical coordinates with core radius a. The slab thickness d is replaced by the radius a, not by 2a — a slab of thickness d matches a fibre of diameter d, radius a = d/2, and that fibre has half the slab's V. Mode labels become LP modes, covered in Chapter 1.6.

Worked example

Question. A symmetric planar guide has core index n1 = 1.50, cladding index n2 = 1.48, core thickness d = 5 µm and operates at wavelength λ = 1.3 µm. Find the normalized frequency V and the number of guided modes.

Exam traps

  • Thickness versus radius: a planar guide uses core thickness d in V; a round fibre uses core radius a. Feeding d into the fibre formula roughly doubles your answer.
  • V is dimensionless. Keep λ and d in the same unit (both µm is fine) and never write a unit after V.
  • floor(V/π) + 1 counts modes for one polarization. In a symmetric guide the TE and TM sets have the same cutoffs, so the total is 2 × (floor(V/π) + 1) if the question wants both polarizations. Do not multiply again for 'two polarizations' — that is the same factor.
  • Cutoff of mode m is at V = mπ, so the m = 1 mode appears at V = π, not at V = 2π. V just below π means single mode.
  • A guided mode needs n2k0 < β < n1k0. If your algebra gives β below n2k0, the field radiates into the cladding; it is not a guided mode.

1.5 Step-index and graded-index fibers

An optical fiber guides light in a core surrounded by cladding of lower refractive index. A step-index fiber changes index suddenly at the core edge. A graded-index fiber lowers the index smoothly from centre to edge. The smooth profile bends rays back before they hit the boundary, so different rays take more similar times. This chapter compares both profiles, writes their index formulas, defines Δ and numerical aperture, and finds the V number and approximate mode count for a step-index fiber.

You need ray theory and total internal reflection and the idea of a mode.

Fiber structure and index profiles

A fiber has three layers. The core, radius a, carries most of the light. The cladding, radius b, has lower index and keeps light inside by total internal reflection. The jacket is a plastic coating for mechanical protection. Core and cladding are glass or silica; the jacket is not an optical layer.

A step-index fiber has one index in the core and a lower index in the cladding: n(r)=n1 for 0≤r<a, and n(r)=n2 for r≥a. The change at r=a is sudden. Here n1 is the core index, n2 is the cladding index, r is radial distance from the axis, and a is the core radius in metres or micrometres.

A graded-index fiber makes the index decrease continuously inside the core.

n(r) = n<sub>1</sub>[1 − 2Δ(r/a)<sup>α</sup>]<sup>1/2</sup>, 0 ≤ r &lt; a

n1 is the on-axis index; n2 is the cladding index; a is the core radius; r is radial distance; Δ is the relative index difference; α is the profile parameter (dimensionless). For r ≥ a, n(r)=n2.

The relative index difference is approximately Δ ≈ (n1 − n2)/n1. The exact form is Δ = (n1² − n2²)/(2n1²). For weak guidance the two values are close. This chapter uses the approximate form for numerical work unless stated. Inside the profile formula use the exact Δ = (n1² − n2²)/(2n1²); for weakly guiding fibres the approximate Δ gives n(a) within about 0.001 of n2, so the difference is negligible in practice. The numerical aperture is NA = √(n12 − n22), dimensionless. α=2 gives a parabolic profile; larger α makes the profile closer to a step.

Ray paths in the two fibers

In a step-index fiber a meridional ray travels in straight segments and reflects at the core–cladding boundary. It follows a zig-zag path. Rays at different angles have different path lengths inside the core. They reach the far end at different times, which spreads a pulse. That spreading is modal dispersion; Unit 2 treats it in detail.

In a graded-index fiber the index falls with radius, so the speed v = c/n rises away from the axis. A ray bends continuously instead of reflecting sharply. It curves back towards the axis and crosses it, then repeats. The path looks like a smooth wave, not a zig-zag.

The curved path helps timing. A ray that swings far from the axis travels a longer path, but it spends more time in lower-index material, where light is faster. The extra speed partly cancels the extra length. Rays then arrive at more similar times. A parabolic profile with α=2 gives the simplest useful correction. Skew rays follow helical paths and need the bound-ray condition from the skew-ray chapter.

V number and approximate mode count

The V number combines wavelength, core size and numerical aperture: V = (2πa/λ) NA. Here a is the core radius in µm or m, λ is the vacuum wavelength in the same unit, and NA is dimensionless. V is dimensionless. V is a normalised frequency. It measures, roughly, how many half-wavelength mode patterns fit across the core diameter; roughly V/2 half-wavelengths per direction, so large V means many modes. A step-index fiber is single-mode only below about V=2.405; cutoff wavelength is treated later.

For a step-index multimode fiber with large V, the approximate number of guided modes is M ≈ V²/2. This is a count, so it has no unit. The formula is not exact near cutoff. A parabolic graded-index fiber with the same V carries about half as many modes, M ≈ V²/4, because the smooth profile separates the mode propagation constants.

Use the same unit for a and λ. V grows with a and NA, and falls as λ grows.

Worked example

Question. A step-index fiber has n1 = 1.48, n2 = 1.46, core radius a = 25 µm and operating wavelength λ = 1.30 µm. Find the approximate relative index difference Δ, the numerical aperture NA, the V number, and the approximate number of modes.

  4. V = 29.3 > 2.405, so the fiber is multimode. M ≈ V²/2 = (29.3)²/2 = 858.5/2 = 429.2, so about 429 modes.

Answer. Δ ≈ 0.0135, NA ≈ 0.2425, V ≈ 29.3 and M ≈ 429 modes.

1.6 Single-mode and multimode fibers; cutoff wavelength

A fiber does not decide on its own whether it is single-mode. The wavelength does. Compare the core size with the wavelength through one dimensionless number, V, and the fiber is single-mode only while V stays below 2.405. Push the wavelength shorter and V rises, more field patterns appear, and they interfere. This one inequality sets the cutoff wavelength of every step-index fiber, and it decides whether a link uses a cheap LED over 300 m or a laser over 80 km.

You need: Chapter 1.4 for what a mode is, and Chapter 1.5 for the core radius and index step.

The V number and the single-mode condition

A step-index fiber has a core of radius a and index n1, inside a cladding of index n2. The numerical aperture NA measures how strongly the guide pulls light in (Chapter 1.3). One dimensionless number collects the geometry and the wavelength:

V = (2πa / λ) · NA

V is the normalized frequency (dimensionless); a is the core radius in µm; λ is the free-space wavelength in µm; NA is the numerical aperture, dimensionless.

V compares the core circumference 2πa with the wavelength, weighted by NA. A large V means the core is many wavelengths wide, so many modes fit. A small V means the field barely fits, so only one mode survives.

For a step-index fiber at large V, the number of guided modes is about M ≈ V²/2. Each mode is a distinct field pattern from Chapter 1.4. The exact condition for single-mode operation is:

V < 2.405

2.405 is the first zero of the Bessel function J₀. It is the V value at which the second mode, LP11, reaches cutoff.

Below V = 2.405 only the fundamental mode LP01 travels. Single-mode does not mean one ray: many rays still bounce inside the core, but they all add up to the same field pattern.

Cutoff wavelength

The cutoff wavelength is the λ at which V equals 2.405. Put V = 2.405 and solve for λ:

λc = 2πa · NA / 2.405

λc is the cutoff wavelength in µm; a is the core radius in µm; NA is the numerical aperture.

Above cutoff, λ > λc, V falls below 2.405 and the fiber is single-mode. Below cutoff, λ < λc, V is larger, several modes travel, and they interfere at the receiver as modal noise (Chapter 1.5).

So a fiber is not single-mode by itself. It is single-mode at wavelengths longer than its own cutoff, and the same fiber used at a shorter wavelength becomes multimode.

Take a = 4 µm and NA = 0.11. Then 2πa·NA = 2.7646 µm and λc = 1.15 µm. At 1.31 µm, V = 2.11, so the fiber is single-mode. At 0.85 µm, V = 3.25, so it is multimode. Single-mode fiber is designed with λc near 1.1–1.3 µm so that both the 1.31 µm and the 1.55 µm windows stay single-mode.

Mode field diameter and the multimode family

In a single-mode fiber the field is not strictly inside the core. The mode field diameter (MFD) is the diameter at which the intensity falls to 1/e² of its peak on axis. It is typically 9–10 µm at 1.55 µm, slightly larger than the core. MFD, not core diameter, sets splice loss and bending loss (Unit 5, Link Design and Fiber Measurements). With one mode there is no intermodal dispersion, so single-mode fiber carries the highest bit rates over long distances. It needs a laser and tight connector tolerances.

Multimode fiber has V well above 2.405, so hundreds or thousands of modes travel.

FiberCore diameterBehaviourTypical use

In graded-index fiber the index falls smoothly from centre to cladding. A ray that swings far from the axis spends its time in lower-index glass, where light travels faster, so it catches up with the axial ray. Intermodal dispersion shrinks, and a few hundred metres at multi-Gb/s becomes possible with a cheap source.

1.7 Phase velocity and group velocity

A light wave in a fiber carries two speeds at once. The crests of its sine wave travel at the phase velocity. The pulse that carries the bits travels at the group velocity. Timing in a link — how long a bit takes to reach the receiver — follows the group velocity, not the phase velocity. This chapter defines both speeds, ties them to the fiber's effective index, and shows how to compute pulse delay over a length of fiber.

You need: Chapter 1.4 for the propagation constant β, and Chapter 1.6 for the effective index of a mode.

Crests and envelopes move at different speeds

A sine wave of one frequency has a definite propagation constant β, measured in rad/m. Its crests move forward at the phase velocity.

vp = ω/β = c/n_eff

vp is phase velocity in m/s; ω is angular frequency in rad/s; β is the propagation constant in rad/m; c = 3.00 × 10^8 m/s is the speed of light in vacuum; n_eff is the dimensionless effective index of the mode.

A single frequency carries no information. A pulse, meaning a short burst of light, is a band of frequencies around a carrier. The whole band travels as one envelope, at the group velocity. Only a few universities teach this in detail.

vg = dω/dβ

vg is group velocity in m/s; dω/dβ is the derivative of angular frequency with respect to propagation constant, taken at the carrier frequency.

Group index and pulse delay

Rewriting the derivative against wavelength gives the working form used in link design.

vg = c/N_g N_g = n_eff − λ (dn_eff/dλ)

N_g is the dimensionless group index; λ is the vacuum wavelength in metres; dn_eff/dλ is the slope of effective index against wavelength, in 1/m. For silica, that slope is negative, so N_g is slightly larger than n_eff.

A pulse launched at time zero reaches the far end after a group delay.

T = L/vg = L N_g / c

T is the group delay in seconds; L is the fiber length in metres; N_g is the group index; c is 3.00 × 10^8 m/s. This T, not L/vp, is the number a rise-time budget uses.

Reading the numbers in a link

In a typical fiber n_eff = 1.46 and N_g = 1.48 differ by about 1 percent, so vp and vg sit close together. Over 10 km that 1 percent is roughly 0.5 µs of extra delay — small once, but repeated in every span of a long route.

  • Use vg for anything that carries bits: bit slots, rise-time budget, synchronisation.
  • Use vp only for the phase of one continuous wave, as in interference and grating formulas.
  • Both n_eff and N_g shift when the waveguide geometry changes; that is waveguide dispersion, handled in Unit 2, Signal Degradation in Optical Fibers.

Worked example

Question. At λ = 1550 nm a fiber has effective index n_eff = 1.46 and group index N_g = 1.48. Take c = 3.00 × 10^8 m/s. Find (a) the phase velocity, (b) the group velocity, (c) the pulse delay over L = 10 km.

  1. Phase velocity: vp = c/n_eff = (3.00 × 10^8)/(1.46) = 2.05 × 10^8 m/s.
  2. Group velocity: vg = c/N_g = (3.00 × 10^8)/(1.48) = 2.03 × 10^8 m/s.
  3. Length in metres: L = 10 km = 10,000 m.
  4. Delay: T = L/vg = 10,000/(2.03 × 10^8) = 4.93 × 10^−5 s = 49.3 µs.

Answer. vp = 2.05 × 10^8 m/s, vg = 2.03 × 10^8 m/s, and the pulse delay over 10 km is 49.3 µs.

Exam traps

  • Using n_eff instead of N_g in L N_g/c understates the delay; the group index governs pulses.
  • Phase velocity is not the speed of information — a monochromatic wave has no bits on it.
  • In silica N_g is slightly above n_eff; a smaller N_g points to a sign slip in dn_eff/dλ.
  • Put L in metres and λ in metres before dividing; 10 km is 10,000 m, not 10.

1.8 Integrated optical waveguides: strip and channel

An integrated optical waveguide traps light inside a narrow high-index strip or channel built on a chip. It uses total internal reflection, but confines light in two directions: vertically by the layer stack and horizontally by the strip edges. The core has index n1; substrate and cladding have lower index n2. Many optical functions then share one small chip, a photonic integrated circuit. Only a few universities teach this topic in detail. It is the geometry behind splitters, filters and Mach–Zehnder modulators (Unit 3).

You need: Chapter 1.4 for modes in a planar layer and Chapter 1.6 for the single-mode cutoff idea.

Strip and channel geometry

An integrated waveguide is a dielectric layer stack on a chip. The core has refractive index n1. The substrate and cladding have refractive index n2, with n1 > n2. Light is guided when it strikes the core–cladding boundary above the critical angle, as in Chapter 1.2.

A planar guide confines light only in the vertical direction. Patterning the core into a strip adds horizontal confinement. A 3D guide is often called a channel waveguide; some syllabi use that name only for the buried rectangular core, and call the raised version a strip or ridge guide. The figure shows the layer order, not the width.

  • Raised strip or ridge: raised core; air or glass above; strong sidewall index step.
  • Buried channel: rectangular core inside cladding on all sides; smoother top surface.
  • Both need n1 > n2 at every boundary that guides the mode.

The substrate is the lower cladding. The upper cladding may be air or a deposited glass. In a channel guide the core is embedded, so top and sides see cladding.

Effective index and single-mode check

Two index steps act together: vertical and horizontal. The field is not fully inside the core. Part travels as an evanescent tail in the cladding. The mode behaves as if it sees an effective index n_eff, with n2 < n_eff < n1. n_eff is the phase index of the guided mode.

V = (2πt/λ)√(n1² − n2²)

V is the normalized frequency (dimensionless); t is core thickness in µm; λ is free-space wavelength in µm; n1 is core index; n2 is cladding or substrate index.

The numerical aperture is NA = √(n1² − n2²), dimensionless; here it measures the index step, not a launch angle in air.

A quick single-mode check in the thickness direction uses this V. For a symmetric slab stack, the thickness direction is approximately single-mode when V < π/2 ≈ 1.57. An asymmetric stack pushes the limit up towards π. The value V < 2.405 belongs to a step-index fibre (first zero of J0), not to this slab V. For a rectangular guide this is only an estimate because the width also matters.

Photonic integrated circuits place waveguides, splitters, gratings and modulators on one substrate. The same chip can route several wavelengths and modulate light. A Mach–Zehnder modulator splits a guided wave into two arms, changes phase in one arm and recombines the arms; see Unit 3, Optical modulators and Mach–Zehnder devices.

The width w also sets horizontal confinement. A guide can be single-mode vertically but multimode horizontally if w is large. Full design uses a mode solver. Go to Chapter 1.9 for periodic guides.

Worked example

Question. A strip waveguide has core thickness t = 0.5 µm, width w = 2 µm, n1 = 1.50, n2 = 1.45 and λ = 1.55 µm. Find NA and the approximate V for the thickness direction. State whether the thickness direction is approximately single-mode.

  1. Square the indices: n1² = 2.2500 and n2² = 2.1025.
  2. Index difference: n1² − n2² = 0.1475. NA = √0.1475 = 0.384 (dimensionless).
  3. Thickness ratio: t/λ = 0.5/1.55 = 0.32258 (dimensionless).
  4. Prefactor: 2π × 0.32258 = 2.027.
  5. V = 2.027 × 0.384 = 0.778 (dimensionless).
  6. Compare: 0.778 < 1.57, so the thickness direction is approximately single-mode. The width w = 2 µm is not checked by this formula.

Answer. NA ≈ 0.384, V ≈ 0.778, so the thickness direction is approximately single-mode because V < 1.57 (slab limit).

Exam traps

  • Terminology differs: some books call every 3D guide a channel waveguide; others reserve channel for a buried core and call the raised one a strip or ridge. Check which convention your paper uses.
  • Do not use the fibre cutoff V < 2.405 for a slab. For a symmetric stack the thickness direction is approximately single-mode when V < π/2 ≈ 1.57; asymmetric stacks approach π. For a rectangular guide even this is only an estimate.
  • Use consistent units for t and λ. The ratio t/λ is dimensionless; mixing µm and nm gives a wrong V.
  • Do not confuse effective index n_eff with core index n1. n_eff lies between n2 and n1.
  • A guide can be single-mode vertically and multimode horizontally. The width matters.

1.9 Photonic crystals and photonic band gap

Photonic crystals are dielectric structures whose refractive index repeats in one, two or three dimensions. The repetition makes some wavelengths reflect strongly and prevents them from travelling through the crystal. That blocked range is the photonic band gap. Engineers use it to make compact mirrors, traps for light, sharp bends on a chip, and fibres that guide light in a hollow core. The same Bragg-reflection idea appears in thin-film coatings, but a photonic crystal extends it from a stack of layers to a full periodic lattice.

You need: <a href='#electromagnetic-mode-theory-modes-in-planar-guides'>Chapter 1.4 for modes in a layered dielectric, and <a href='#integrated-optical-waveguides-strip-and-channel'>Chapter 1.8 for guided strips on a chip.

One-dimensional photonic crystal and Bragg reflection

A one-dimensional photonic crystal is a stack of alternating dielectric layers. Let a be the period, one full pair of layers, and n_eff be the average refractive index of that pair. Light travelling down the stack is partly reflected at every interface. When the reflected waves return in phase, they add. This Bragg condition gives a stop band: wavelengths near λB are reflected and cannot pass through a long stack.

λB ≈ 2 n_eff a

λB is the first-order band-gap centre wavelength; n_eff is the average refractive index of one period; a is the period, one full layer pair.

The band gap is a range of wavelengths, not a single line. Its width and strength depend on the index contrast between the two materials. A high-contrast stack can reflect a wide band over a wide range of angles; such a design is called an omnidirectional reflector. A low-contrast stack gives a narrow band. In a fibre or chip, the same stack can be grown as thin films, etched as trenches, or written by lithography. The reflector works because the periodic medium has no propagating mode inside the gap.

  1. Choose the centre wavelength λB.
  2. Estimate n_eff from the layer indices.
  3. Find the period a = λB/(2 n_eff).
  4. Simulate or measure reflectance across the band.

Two-dimensional and three-dimensional crystals; defects

A two-dimensional photonic crystal has a periodic lattice in a plane, for example a grid of air holes in silicon. A three-dimensional crystal repeats in all three axes, such as a stack of spheres or a woodpile structure. The band gap can block light along chosen directions. A defect breaks the period: remove one row of holes and the missing row becomes a line-defect waveguide. Remove a few holes and you make a microcavity that traps a wavelength for many cycles. These defects are the working parts of photonic-crystal chips.

Fabrication follows the same logic. Lithography and etching make 2D slabs and line defects. Self-assembled crystals form by letting spheres settle into an ordered lattice; holographic fabrication uses interfering laser beams to write a periodic pattern in photoresist. Each method trades precision against cost and area. A photonic crystal fibre uses a periodic pattern of air holes running along the fibre length to confine light in a core.

At block level, two special effects appear. Negative refraction bends a beam to the same side of the normal in a photonic crystal. Self-collimation lets a beam travel straight without a guiding core because the lattice makes the group velocity point along one axis. Both are useful for dense optical circuits, but they need careful design.

Photonic crystal fibre and practical limits

Photonic crystal fibre (PCF) is a glass fibre with a regular array of air holes along its length. In an index-guiding PCF, the solid core has a higher average index than the holey cladding, so total internal reflection still guides light. In a band-gap PCF, the cladding's periodic holes create a gap and a hollow core can guide light inside that gap. Hollow-core fibre can carry high power and has low material loss, but coupling and fabrication tolerances are tight.

A photonic crystal is not a magic low-loss material. Scattering from rough holes, imperfect period, and coupling from a normal fibre add loss. The useful band must overlap the source wavelength. For 1550 nm links, the period is set so λB sits near 1550 nm, often around 500 nm for n_eff near 1.5. The same design rule appears in thin-film filters, Bragg gratings, and laser mirrors. Plasmonics and metamaterials are related advanced topics beyond this unit.

Worked example

Question. A one-dimensional photonic crystal is made from alternating layers with average refractive index n_eff = 1.5. The period is a = 500 nm, where one period is one full layer pair. Find the first-order band-gap centre wavelength in nanometres and in micrometres.

  1. Write the first-order Bragg condition: λB ≈ 2 n_eff a.
  2. Substitute the data: n_eff = 1.5 and a = 500 nm.
  3. Multiply: 2 × 1.5 = 3.0.
  4. Then λB = 3.0 × 500 nm = 1500 nm.
  5. Convert: 1500 nm = 1.5 μm.

Answer. The first-order band-gap centre is 1500 nm, or 1.5 μm.

Exam traps

  • The band gap is a range of wavelengths, not one exact wavelength. The centre λB is a label for the middle of the stop band.
  • The period a is one full layer pair. A single layer is not the period, even if the two layers have equal thickness.
  • Use n_eff, the average index of the period, in λB ≈ 2 n_eff a. Using only the high-index value shifts the predicted centre.
  • A 1D stack is not automatically an omnidirectional reflector. Wide-angle reflection needs high index contrast and careful layer design.
  • Do not mix the photonic band gap with the electronic band gap of a semiconductor. They are different gaps for different waves.

Takeaways

  1. A fiber link is just a light source, a glass guide and a detector, but the glass guide removes the bandwidth limit of copper.
  2. Light stays in the core because of total internal reflection, and the acceptance angle tells you how well you can launch into it.
  3. Numerical aperture is the single number that links the refractive indices to the launch cone.
  4. Maxwell's equations in a layered dielectric allow only discrete field patterns, which we call modes.
  5. Step-index fiber is simple; graded-index fiber trades a harder profile for much less modal dispersion.
  6. Whether a fiber is single-mode depends on the wavelength you use, not on the fiber alone.
  7. The speed of your data is set by group velocity; phase velocity is not what the bits follow.
  8. On a chip, the same reflection idea is squeezed into a strip a few hundred nanometres wide.
  9. Periodic dielectrics can forbid light in a band of wavelengths and so guide or trap it.
  10. Numbers matter in this unit: NA, V, λc and delay are all short calculations an examiner will ask for.

Glossary

Acceptance cone
The cone of input rays, of half-angle θa, that the fiber can guide.
Channel waveguide
A raised strip or rib of high index that confines light in two transverse directions.
Cladding
The lower-index layer around the core that makes total internal reflection possible.
Core
The central high-index region of a fiber that carries the light.
Cutoff wavelength
The wavelength below which a fiber stops being single-mode.
Effective index
β/k₀, the equivalent index seen by a mode; used to reduce 2D problems to 1D.
Graded-index fiber
A fiber whose core index decreases smoothly from the axis to the cladding.
Group velocity
The speed of the pulse envelope, v_g = dω/dβ.
Jacket
The protective plastic coating outside the cladding; it carries no light.
LP mode
Linearly polarised mode; the weakly guiding approximation used to label fiber modes such as LP₀₁.
Meridional ray
A ray whose path crosses the fiber axis and lies in one plane.
Modal dispersion
Pulse broadening caused by different modes travelling at different group velocities.
Mode
A discrete, self-consistent field distribution that propagates without changing shape.
Multimode fiber
A fiber that guides many modes, with a core of roughly 50–100 μm.
Numerical aperture
The sine of the acceptance angle in air; a measure of light-gathering power.
Phase velocity
The speed of a single crest of the wave, v_p = ω/β.
Photonic band gap
A band of wavelengths that cannot propagate through a photonic crystal in a given direction.
Photonic crystal
A dielectric structure whose refractive index varies periodically with a period near the wavelength of light.
Photonic crystal fiber
A fiber whose cladding contains a regular array of air holes, guiding by index contrast or by band gap.
Planar waveguide
A slab of high-index material that confines light in one transverse direction.
Refractive index
n = c/v, the ratio of light speed in vacuum to that in the medium.
Relative index difference
Δ = (n₁ − n₂)/n₁, typically 0.01 for multimode and 0.003 for single-mode fiber.
Single-mode fiber
A fiber that guides only the LP₀₁ mode at the working wavelength.
Skew ray
A ray that never crosses the axis and follows a helical path.
Step-index fiber
A fiber whose core index is uniform and changes abruptly at the cladding.
V number
V = 2πa·NA/λ, the normalized frequency that sets the mode count.
