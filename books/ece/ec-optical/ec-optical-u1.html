1.1 Optical fiber communication system and its advantages

An optical fiber communication system carries information as light. A transmitter turns an electrical signal into light, a glass fiber guides that light over distance, and a receiver turns it back into an electrical signal. Fiber is used because light in silica glass travels tens of kilometres with little loss, and because one fiber can carry many high-speed channels at once. Internet backbones, undersea cables, mobile tower links, cable TV and the fiber that reaches homes all use it.

The blocks of a fiber link

Every fiber link has three parts: a transmitter, the fiber channel and a receiver. The transmitter takes the electrical input, such as a bit stream from a router or an analog video signal, and a drive circuit turns it into a current that drives an optical source. The source is a light-emitting diode (LED) or a semiconductor laser, and the light power it emits follows the drive current.

The fiber is a thin strand of silica glass. Its central core has a slightly higher refractive index than the cladding around it, and that small index step keeps the light inside the core by total internal reflection (see Ray theory: total internal reflection, acceptance angle). A plastic coating protects the glass; it carries no light. Long links join fiber lengths with splices and connectors, and very long links add optical amplifiers or regenerators along the route.

The receiver uses a photodetector, usually a PIN photodiode or an avalanche photodiode (APD), to turn the arriving light into a current. An amplifier raises that small current, and a decision or filter circuit reshapes it into the electrical output.

  1. Electrical input (information source).
  2. Drive circuit and optical source: electrical to optical (E/O) conversion.
  3. Fiber cable with splices, connectors and, on long routes, amplifiers or regenerators.
  4. Photodetector: optical to electrical (O/E) conversion.
  5. Amplifier and signal processing, then the electrical output.

Advantages over copper cable

Copper cable carries the signal as an electric current. Fiber carries it as light in glass. That one change gives these advantages.

  • Wide bandwidth. The optical carrier near 1550 nm has a frequency of about 1.94 × 10¹⁴ Hz, so even a small fraction of it is a huge band; a single fiber carries tens of gigabits per second on one wavelength, and wavelength-division multiplexing (WDM) puts many wavelengths on the same fiber. The usable rate on one channel is set by the source, detector and electronics, but the fiber itself is not the bottleneck. A copper pair loses more and more signal as frequency rises.
  • Low loss. Silica is very transparent in the near infrared, so a signal travels tens of kilometres before it needs amplification. Copper links at high data rates need repeaters every few kilometres. The loss numbers are in the chapter Attenuation in optical fibers.
  • Immunity to electromagnetic interference. Glass is a dielectric, not a conductor, so nearby power lines, lightning and traction motors induce no noise in it, and the fiber radiates nothing that could disturb other circuits.
  • Electrical isolation. No metal joins the two ends, so there are no ground loops and no path for surge currents between buildings.
  • Safety. A fiber carries no current and makes no spark, so it can run through fuel depots, refineries and chemical plants.
  • Small size and weight. The bare glass is 125 µm across, about the thickness of a hair, and 250 µm with its coating. A cable holding dozens of fibers is lighter and thinner than a copper cable of far lower capacity, which eases duct and tower loading.
  • Security. Light stays in the core and nothing radiates, so the signal cannot be picked up by a nearby antenna. Tapping a fiber needs physical access and adds loss that monitoring can detect.
  • Cheap, plentiful raw material. Silica comes from sand, while copper is a costly metal.

Fiber also has drawbacks an examiner may ask for: splicing and connectors need precision tools, the glass breaks if bent too tightly, and the ends need E/O and O/E converters.

Analog and digital links, windows and the decibel

In an analog link the light power follows the input waveform continuously; radio-over-fiber feeds and older cable TV networks work this way. In a digital link the source is switched between two light levels and the receiver decides, in each bit slot, whether the light was high or low. Almost all telephone and internet traffic is digital.

Links are named by their wavelength window. 850 nm serves short multimode links inside buildings with cheap sources. 1310 nm is where pulse spreading in standard single-mode fiber is smallest, so it suits short and medium single-mode links. 1550 nm is where silica loss is lowest, so long-haul links use it. Loss and dispersion values come with signal degradation in fibers.

Loss in a fiber is quoted in decibels per kilometre, and the total loss in dB grows in proportion to length:

Loss (dB) = α × L

α is the attenuation in dB/km and L is the fiber length in km.

P (dBm) = 10 log₁₀(P / 1 mW)

P is the optical power in mW; 0 dBm means exactly 1 mW. Powers in dBm and losses in dB add and subtract: Pout(dBm) = Pin(dBm) − loss (dB).

Worked example

Question. A 20 km link at 1550 nm uses fiber with attenuation 0.2 dB/km. The source launches 1 mW. Find the total fiber loss, and the power reaching the receiver in dBm and in mW.

  1. Total loss: α × L = 0.2 dB/km × 20 km = 4 dB.
  2. Launch power in dBm: 10 log₁₀(1 mW / 1 mW) = 10 log₁₀(1) = 0 dBm.
  3. Received power in dBm: 0 dBm − 4 dB = −4 dBm.
  4. Back to milliwatts: P = 1 mW × 10−4/10 = 1 mW × 0.398 = 0.398 mW.
  5. Check: a 3 dB loss halves the power, and 4 dB is a little more than that, so 0.398 mW (a little under half of 1 mW) is sensible.

Answer. The fiber loss is 4 dB; the receiver gets −4 dBm, which is 0.398 mW.

Exam traps

  • Loss in dB grows with length: 0.2 dB/km is not the total loss. Add and subtract dB values; never multiply powers written in dB.
  • dBm is an absolute power (referred to 1 mW); dB is a ratio. 4 dB of loss takes 0 dBm to −4 dBm, not to 0.4 dBm.
  • The source is the E/O converter and the photodetector is the O/E converter. Do not swap them in the block diagram.
  • 1310 nm is the low-dispersion window and 1550 nm the low-loss window; students often swap the two reasons.
  • Fiber's immunity to interference comes from glass being a dielectric. It does not come from the light being 'shielded' by the cladding.

1.2 Ray theory: total internal reflection, acceptance angle

Ray theory treats light in a fiber as straight lines that bend at boundaries by Snell's law. The core has a higher refractive index than the cladding, and a ray that meets the core–cladding boundary at a steep enough angle is reflected back completely: total internal reflection (TIR). Working backwards from that condition to the end face gives the acceptance angle, the half-angle of the cone of light a fiber can take in. The same reasoning sizes lenses and alignment when a source is coupled to a fiber.

Snell's law and the critical angle

The refractive index n of a material is the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum c to its speed v in the material: n = c/v. It has no unit; a larger n means slower light. In a step-index fiber the core index is n₁ and the cladding index is n₂, with n₁ > n₂. The medium outside the fiber end has index n₀ (air, n₀ = 1).

At a boundary, Snell's law links the angles on the two sides: n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. Both angles are measured from the normal, the line perpendicular to the boundary. The reflected ray leaves at the same angle as the incident ray (law of reflection).

Going from the denser core into the rarer cladding, the ray bends away from the normal, so θ₂ > θ₁. As θ₁ grows, θ₂ reaches 90° first: the refracted ray grazes the boundary. That value of θ₁ is the critical angle θc. Put θ₂ = 90° in Snell's law: n₁ sin θc = n₂.

sin θc = n₂/n₁

θc is measured from the normal at the core–cladding boundary; it exists only when n₁ > n₂.

For any angle of incidence above θc, Snell's law would need sin θ₂ > 1, which no real angle gives, so no light is transmitted and all of it is reflected back into the core. That is total internal reflection. Its two conditions are: light travels from the higher index to the lower index (n₁ > n₂), and the angle of incidence exceeds θc. Even then a weak evanescent field reaches a short way into the cladding, but it carries no power away.

From TIR at the wall to the acceptance angle

Take a meridional ray, one that passes through the fiber axis. It enters the flat end face from the outside medium at angle θ₀ to the axis. The end face is perpendicular to the axis, so the axis is its normal. Snell's law at the end face gives n₀ sin θ₀ = n₁ sin θr, where θr is the ray's angle to the axis inside the core.

The ray then meets the core–cladding wall. The wall runs parallel to the axis, so its normal is at right angles to the axis, and the angle of incidence on the wall is φ = 90° − θr. TIR needs φ ≥ θc, which means θr ≤ 90° − θc.

The acceptance angle θa is the largest outside angle θ₀ that still meets this, so at the limit θr = 90° − θc:

  1. n₀ sin θa = n₁ sin(90° − θc) = n₁ cos θc.
  2. cos θc = √(1 − sin²θc) = √(1 − n₂²/n₁²).
  3. So n₀ sin θa = n₁ √(1 − n₂²/n₁²) = √(n₁² − n₂²).

n₀ sin θa = √(n₁² − n₂²)

θa is the acceptance half-angle in the outside medium, measured from the fiber axis. The quantity n₀ sin θa is the numerical aperture, treated fully in Numerical aperture and skew rays.

Rotating θa about the axis sweeps out the acceptance cone. A meridional ray launched inside the cone (θ₀ < θa) meets the wall above θc and is guided; a ray launched outside it meets the wall below θc, loses part of its power into the cladding at every reflection and dies out within a short length.

What the cone controls

The cone decides how much of a source's light enters the fiber. An LED spreads light over a wide angle, so only the part inside the cone is captured; a laser's narrow beam fits inside easily. A wider cone (larger n₁² − n₂²) collects more light, but the same index step also lets more modes travel, which spreads pulses (see Intermodal dispersion with signal degradation in fibers). Tilting the fiber end relative to the source moves part of the beam outside the cone, which is why angular misalignment costs power at joints.

This picture holds for meridional rays in a fiber whose core is many wavelengths wide. Rays that never cross the axis, called skew rays, follow helical paths and are accepted over a slightly wider cone; see Numerical aperture and skew rays. When the core is only a few wavelengths wide, rays give way to modes: Electromagnetic mode theory; modes in planar guides.

Worked example

Question. A step-index fiber has core index n₁ = 1.50 and cladding index n₂ = 1.45, with air outside (n₀ = 1). Find the critical angle at the core–cladding boundary, the numerical aperture and the acceptance angle. Then find the ray angle inside the core for a ray launched exactly at the acceptance angle, and check that it meets the wall at θc.

  1. Critical angle: sin θc = n₂/n₁ = 1.45/1.50 = 0.9667, so θc = sin⁻¹(0.9667) = 75.16°.
  2. Squares: n₁² = 2.2500 and n₂² = 2.1025, so n₁² − n₂² = 0.1475.
  3. NA = √0.1475 = 0.3841. With n₀ = 1, sin θa = 0.3841, so θa = sin⁻¹(0.3841) = 22.59°.
  4. Inside the core: sin θr = sin θa/n₁ = 0.3841/1.50 = 0.2560, so θr = 14.84°.
  5. Angle on the wall: φ = 90° − 14.84° = 75.16°, which equals θc. The limiting ray just meets the TIR condition, as the derivation says.

Answer. θc = 75.2°, NA = 0.384 and θa = 22.6°; the limiting ray travels at 14.8° to the axis inside the core and strikes the wall at exactly 75.2°.

Exam traps

  • Use sin θc = n₂/n₁, not n₁/n₂; with n₂ < n₁ the ratio is below 1, as a sine must be.
  • Angles in Snell's law are measured from the normal. A ray 15° from the wall is 75° from the wall's normal.
  • θc is inside the fiber at the core–cladding wall; θa is outside, at the end face, measured from the axis. Do not call θc the acceptance angle.
  • With a medium other than air at the end face, divide by n₀: sin θa = √(n₁² − n₂²)/n₀. The cone in water is narrower than in air.
  • A ray launched at a larger angle hits the wall at a smaller angle of incidence (φ = 90° − θr), so it is the steep launch that escapes, not the shallow one.

1.3 Numerical aperture and skew rays

Numerical aperture (NA) is the one number that says how wide a cone of light a fiber accepts. It is built from the two refractive indices alone, so it is the first figure on any fiber data sheet and the starting point for coupling, mode count and cutoff calculations. The relative index difference Δ says the same thing as a fraction. Skew rays, which spiral round the axis without crossing it, are accepted over a wider cone than meridional rays, which matters when multimode fiber is launched from a broad source. Only a few universities teach the skew-ray part in detail; the NA formula is standard exam material.

You need: the acceptance angle from Ray theory: total internal reflection, acceptance angle.

Numerical aperture and the relative index difference

The acceptance derivation ended with n₀ sin θa = √(n₁² − n₂²). That quantity is defined as the numerical aperture:

NA = n₀ sin θa = √(n₁² − n₂²)

NA is dimensionless; n₀ is the index outside the end face (air, 1); θa is the acceptance half-angle; n₁ and n₂ are the core and cladding indices. In air θa = sin⁻¹(NA).

NA does not depend on the outside medium: put the fiber in water and θa shrinks, but n₀ sin θa stays the same. Typical values are about 0.1 for single-mode fiber, about 0.2 for graded-index multimode fiber and 0.3 or more for step-index multimode and plastic fibers.

The relative index difference Δ expresses the index step as a fraction. Its exact definition and its usual approximation are:

Δ = (n₁² − n₂²)/(2n₁²) ≈ (n₁ − n₂)/n₁

The approximation uses n₁² − n₂² = (n₁ + n₂)(n₁ − n₂) ≈ 2n₁(n₁ − n₂), valid when n₁ and n₂ are close (weak guidance, Δ ≪ 1). Communication fibers have Δ of about 0.003 (single-mode) to 0.01–0.02 (multimode).

Rearranging the exact form gives n₁² − n₂² = 2n₁²Δ, so NA = n₁√(2Δ). With the exact Δ this is exact; with the approximate Δ it is very close.

Meridional and skew rays

A meridional ray lies in a plane that contains the fiber axis. Seen end-on it runs along a diameter, crossing the axis between reflections. The acceptance cone of the previous chapter is the result for these rays.

A skew ray enters off the axis and never crosses it. Seen end-on it traces a polygon of chords round the axis, and along the fiber it follows a helix. Two angles describe it: its angle θ to the axis, and the angle γ between its end-on projection and the radius at the point where it meets the wall. A meridional ray has γ = 0.

In a straight step-index fiber every reflection of a given skew ray happens at the same angle of incidence, so a skew ray that meets the TIR condition at one reflection meets it at all of them. For the same axial angle that incidence angle is larger than a meridional ray's, because the ray strikes the round wall obliquely. TIR is therefore met more easily, and the acceptance condition for skew rays becomes:

n₀ sin θas · cos γ = √(n₁² − n₂²) = NA
θas is the acceptance angle for a skew ray whose end-on projection makes angle γ with the radius. Since cos γ ≤ 1, θas ≥ θa: skew rays are accepted over a wider cone. γ = 0 gives back the meridional result.

Two practical effects follow. A broad source such as an LED launches many skew rays, so a multimode fiber collects a little more light than the meridional NA predicts, and a measured far-field NA can come out larger than √(n₁² − n₂²) over a short length. And skew rays travel longer helical paths, so they add to the spread of arrival times in multimode fiber (see Intermodal dispersion with signal degradation in fibers).

Worked example

Question. A step-index fiber has n₁ = 1.48 and n₂ = 1.46 with air outside. Find (a) NA and the meridional acceptance angle; (b) Δ, exactly and by the approximation, and NA from n₁√(2Δ) with each; (c) the acceptance angle for a skew ray with γ = 45°.

  1. (a) n₁² = 2.1904, n₂² = 2.1316, n₁² − n₂² = 0.0588. NA = √0.0588 = 0.2425. θa = sin⁻¹(0.2425) = 14.03°.
  2. (b) Exact: Δ = 0.0588/(2 × 2.1904) = 0.0588/4.3808 = 0.01342. Approximate: Δ ≈ (1.48 − 1.46)/1.48 = 0.02/1.48 = 0.01351.
  3. NA from the exact Δ: 1.48 × √(2 × 0.01342) = 1.48 × √0.02684 = 1.48 × 0.1638 = 0.2425, the same as (a). From the approximate Δ: 1.48 × √0.02703 = 1.48 × 0.1644 = 0.2433, only 0.3 % high.
  4. (c) sin θas = NA/cos γ = 0.2425/cos 45° = 0.2425/0.7071 = 0.3429, so θas = sin⁻¹(0.3429) = 20.06°.
  5. Check: 20.06° > 14.03°, as it must be, since cos γ < 1 widens the cone for skew rays.

Answer. NA = 0.2425 and θa = 14.0°; Δ = 0.01342 exactly (0.01351 approximately, giving NA ≈ 0.2433); a skew ray with γ = 45° is accepted up to 20.1°.

Exam traps

  • NA is a pure number. Writing NA = 14.0° mixes it up with θa.
  • NA = √(n₁² − n₂²), not n₁ − n₂: for 1.48 and 1.46 the difference is 0.02 but NA is 0.2425.
  • State which Δ you use. (n₁ − n₂)/n₁ is the approximation; (n₁² − n₂²)/(2n₁²) is exact and makes NA = n₁√(2Δ) exact.
  • Skew rays are accepted over a wider cone, not a narrower one: sin θas = NA/cos γ is larger than NA.
  • A large NA does not make a fiber single-mode; it raises the V number and the mode count.

1.4 Electromagnetic mode theory; modes in planar guides

Ray optics says whether light is trapped. It cannot say how many distinct light patterns a guide carries or how much of each pattern spills into the cladding. Maxwell's equations answer both. In a planar guide, a flat high-index layer between lower-index layers, the field can only take a few fixed shapes called modes, each travelling along the guide without changing shape. The planar guide is the simplest case to solve, and it is the base of every chip waveguide, splitter and modulator arm.

You need: total internal reflection from Ray theory: total internal reflection, acceptance angle.

The wave equation in a three-layer slab

A symmetric planar guide is a core layer of index n₁ and thickness d between two claddings of index n₂, with n₁ > n₂. The layers are wide in y, so nothing changes along y. Light travels along z, and everything we solve for varies across x, the thickness direction, with the core from x = −d/2 to x = +d/2.

In each layer Maxwell's equations reduce to the wave equation. For a wave of angular frequency ω travelling along z, write the field as E(x) ej(ωt − βz), where β is the propagation constant in rad/m. The wave equation becomes:

d²E/dx² + (k₀²n² − β²) E = 0

k₀ = 2π/λ is the free-space wavenumber (λ the vacuum wavelength) and n is n₁ in the core and n₂ in the cladding.

At x = ±d/2 the tangential components of E and H must be continuous. A guided mode oscillates in the core and decays in the cladding, which needs k₀n₂ < β < k₀n₁. Then:

  • In the core, E varies as cos(κx) or sin(κx), with transverse wavenumber κ = √(k₀²n₁² − β²).
  • In the cladding, E decays as e−γ(|x| − d/2), scaled to match the core field at the wall, with decay constant γ = √(β² − k₀²n₂²).
  • Matching the field and its slope at the wall gives the eigenvalue equations tan(κd/2) = γ/κ for the even (cosine) modes and −cot(κd/2) = γ/κ for the odd (sine) modes.

These equations have only a few roots, so only a few values of β are allowed. Each root is a mode: a fixed transverse field pattern that keeps its shape as it travels. The effective index neff = β/k₀ of a mode always lies between n₂ and n₁.

TE and TM modes, mode number and the V number

Two families exist. A TE mode (transverse electric) has its electric field only along y; a TM mode (transverse magnetic) has its magnetic field only along y. Each family has its own set of β values from the same kind of matching, and in a weakly guiding slab the two sets are nearly equal.

The mode number m counts the zeros of the field across the core. TE₀ has a single central peak and no zero; TE₁ has one zero at the centre; higher m means more zeros, a smaller β and a longer evanescent tail. In ray language, a higher mode is a ray zig-zagging at a steeper angle.

One number collects thickness, wavelength and index step:

V = (2πd/λ) √(n₁² − n₂²)

V is the normalized frequency (dimensionless); d is the full core thickness; λ is the vacuum wavelength in the same unit as d; √(n₁² − n₂²) is the NA of the index step.

With this V, mode m is guided when V > mπ, so a symmetric guide carries floor(V/π) + 1 modes of each family, and it is single-mode when V < π. TE₀ has no cutoff: a symmetric slab always guides at least one mode. Some books use the half-thickness d/2 in V; their V is half of this one and their cutoffs are V = mπ/2. Check which form a question uses.

The fraction of a mode's power inside the core is its confinement factor Γ. Near cutoff Γ is small and most of the power travels in the cladding; well above cutoff Γ approaches 1.

From the slab to chips and fibers

The planar guide is the base of integrated optics. Etching the slab into a strip adds confinement in the second direction, the subject of Integrated optical waveguides: strip and channel. Splitters, directional couplers and Mach–Zehnder modulator arms are all built from such guides.

A round fiber is the same problem in cylindrical coordinates. The fields become Bessel functions, the modes are labelled LPlm, and the fiber's V uses the core radius a: V = (2πa/λ)√(n₁² − n₂²). Its single-mode limit is V < 2.405, treated in Single-mode and multimode fibers; cutoff wavelength. The slab rule V < π does not carry over to fibers.

Worked example

Question. A symmetric planar guide has n₁ = 1.50, n₂ = 1.48 and core thickness d = 5 µm, used at λ = 1.3 µm. (a) Find V and the number of TE modes. (b) The eigenvalue equations, solved numerically, give effective indices 1.4969 for TE₀ and 1.4882 for TE₁. Check that both are guided, and find the decay length 1/γ of each in the cladding.

  2. Size term: 2πd/λ = 2π × 5/1.3 = 31.416/1.3 = 24.17 (µm cancels).
  3. V = 24.17 × 0.2441 = 5.90.
  4. Mode count: V/π = 5.90/3.142 = 1.88, so floor(1.88) + 1 = 2 TE modes (TE₀ and TE₁). TE₂ would need V > 2π = 6.28.
  5. Guidance check: n₂ = 1.48 < 1.4882 < 1.4969 < n₁ = 1.50, so both effective indices lie in the guided range.

Exam traps

  • The slab V uses the full thickness d here, with cutoffs at mπ. If your book puts d/2 in V, the cutoffs are mπ/2. Mixing the two halves or doubles the answer.
  • floor(V/π) + 1 counts one family (TE). Counting TE and TM together doubles it; do not double again 'for polarization', because TE and TM already are the two polarizations.
  • Mode m appears when V passes mπ: TE₁ appears at V = π, not 2π. A symmetric slab with V just below π is single-mode.
  • A guided mode needs n₂ < neff < n₁. A value below n₂ means the field radiates into the cladding.
  • A fiber uses its core radius a and the limit 2.405; a slab uses thickness d and the limit π. Do not carry one rule into the other.

1.5 Step-index and graded-index fibers

Fibers are classed by how the refractive index changes across the core. In a step-index fiber the index is constant in the core and drops suddenly at the cladding. In a graded-index fiber it falls smoothly from the axis to the cladding, so rays curve back towards the axis instead of bouncing off a wall, and rays on different paths arrive at nearly the same time. This chapter writes both profiles, finds the V number, and counts the modes each type carries: about V²/2 for step index and V²/4 for a parabolic graded index.

You need: NA and Δ from Numerical aperture and skew rays.

Structure and the two index profiles

A fiber has a core of radius a, a cladding of lower index out to radius b (125 µm diameter for standard fiber), and a plastic coating that protects the glass but is not part of the optics. Let r be the distance from the axis.

Step index: n(r) = n₁ for r < a, and n(r) = n₂ for r ≥ a. The jump at r = a is sudden.

Graded index: the index falls continuously inside the core and equals n₂ in the cladding:

n(r) = n₁ [1 − 2Δ (r/a)α]½
for r < a, and n(r) = n₂ for r ≥ a. n₁ is the index on the axis; Δ = (n₁² − n₂²)/(2n₁²) is the exact relative index difference, which makes n(a) = n₂ exactly; α is the profile parameter. α = 1 is triangular, α = 2 is parabolic, and α → ∞ gives back the step profile.

In a graded fiber the light-gathering power depends on where light enters. The local NA at radius r is √(n(r)² − n₂²): it equals the step-index value on the axis and falls to zero at the core edge. Data sheets quote the on-axis value.

FiberCore diameterTypical NATypical use
Step-index multimode50–200 µm0.2–0.5short links, plastic fiber, sensors
Graded-index multimode50 or 62.5 µmabout 0.2LANs and data centres
Step-index single-mode8–10 µmabout 0.1metro and long haul

Ray paths in the two fibers

In a step-index fiber a meridional ray travels in straight lines and reflects sharply at the core–cladding wall, a zig-zag. A steep ray travels a longer path than an axial ray at the same speed c/n₁, so it arrives later. That spread of arrival times is intermodal (modal) dispersion, worked out with signal degradation in fibers.

In a graded-index fiber the index falls away from the axis, so light travels faster (v = c/n) away from it. A ray leaving the axis bends continuously back towards it, crosses it and swings out on the other side: a smooth wave instead of a zig-zag. The ray that swings out furthest travels the longest path, but it spends that extra length in lower-index glass where it moves faster. With a near-parabolic profile (α ≈ 2) the two effects almost cancel, and rays arrive close together. Skew rays in a graded fiber follow helical paths that stay within a ring of the core.

V number and the number of modes

For a fiber the normalized frequency uses the core radius:

V = (2πa/λ) · NA = (2πa/λ) √(n₁² − n₂²)

a is the core radius and λ the vacuum wavelength, in the same unit; V is dimensionless. For a graded fiber NA is the on-axis value.

V grows with core size and NA and falls as the wavelength rises. A step-index fiber is single-mode only for V < 2.405 (Single-mode and multimode fibers; cutoff wavelength). For a multimode fiber with large V, the number of guided modes is approximately:

M ≈ [α/(α + 2)] · V²/2

This counts both polarizations. Step index (α → ∞): M ≈ V²/2. Parabolic graded index (α = 2): M ≈ V²/4, half as many for the same core radius and on-axis NA, because the local NA shrinks towards the core edge.

These are large-V estimates: near cutoff, count the LP modes one by one instead.

(e) A parabolic graded-index fiber has the same n₁, n₂ and a. How many modes does it carry, and what is its index at r = 10 µm?

  1. (a) Exact: Δ = (2.1904 − 2.1316)/(2 × 2.1904) = 0.0588/4.3808 = 0.01342. Approximate: (1.48 − 1.46)/1.48 = 0.01351.

  5. (e) Graded, α = 2, same on-axis NA and radius, so the same V: M ≈ V²/4 = 858.5/4 = 215 modes.

Exam traps

  • V uses the core radius. A '50 µm fiber' has a = 25 µm; putting 50 µm in V doubles V and quadruples the mode count.
  • M ≈ V²/2 is for step index and M ≈ V²/4 for parabolic graded index, both at large V. Near V = 2.405 neither formula holds.

1.6 Single-mode and multimode fibers; cutoff wavelength

Whether a fiber is single-mode is not fixed by the fiber alone: it depends on the wavelength used. The V number compares the core size with the wavelength, and a step-index fiber carries only one mode while V < 2.405. The wavelength at which V equals 2.405 is the cutoff wavelength: longer wavelengths give single-mode operation, shorter ones give several modes. This one inequality decides whether a link can use a cheap LED and multimode fiber over a few hundred metres, or needs a laser and single-mode fiber over tens of kilometres.

You need: the V number from Step-index and graded-index fibers.

LP modes and the single-mode condition

In communication fibers n₁ and n₂ differ by well under 1 %, so the fiber is weakly guiding and its exact modes group into nearly degenerate sets called linearly polarized (LP) modes, labelled LPlm. Each LP mode has its own cutoff value of V, set by zeros of Bessel functions.

LP modeCutoff VGuided when
LP₀₁ (fundamental)0 (no cutoff)always
LP₁₁2.405 (first zero of J₀)V > 2.405
LP₂₁ and LP₀₂3.832 (first zero of J₁)V > 3.832

So a step-index fiber is single-mode when

V = (2πa/λ) · NA < 2.405

a is the core radius, λ the vacuum wavelength (same unit), NA = √(n₁² − n₂²).

Below 2.405 only LP₀₁ travels, in two polarizations. Single-mode does not mean the light runs in one straight ray; it means the field has one fixed shape across the core. With large V the fiber is multimode and carries about V²/2 modes.

Cutoff wavelength

Set V = 2.405 and solve for λ. The result is the cutoff wavelength λc, the shortest wavelength at which the fiber is single-mode:

λc = 2πa · NA / 2.405

λc has the unit of a. Since V = 2.405 λc/λ, the fiber is single-mode for λ > λc (V < 2.405) and multimode for λ < λc (V > 2.405).

Take a = 4 µm and NA = 0.11. Then 2πa·NA = 2.765 µm and λc = 2.765/2.405 = 1.150 µm. At 1.31 µm, V = 2.765/1.31 = 2.11 (single-mode); at 1.55 µm, V = 1.78 (single-mode); at 0.85 µm, V = 3.25, so LP₁₁ also travels and the fiber is multimode there.

Designers place λc below the shortest working wavelength with some margin. Standard single-mode fiber is specified with a cabled cutoff of at most 1260 nm, so both the 1310 nm and 1550 nm windows are single-mode. The cutoff should not be pushed far below the window, though: a small V means a weakly held mode that is lost easily at bends.

Mode field diameter and choosing a fiber

The LP₀₁ field is close to a Gaussian and reaches into the cladding. The mode field diameter (MFD) is the diameter at which its intensity falls to 1/e² of the peak. For standard single-mode fiber it is about 9 µm at 1310 nm and about 10 µm at 1550 nm, larger than the 8 to 9 µm core, and it grows as V falls. MFD, not the core diameter, sets splice loss between fibers and the coupling from a laser.

With one mode there is no intermodal dispersion, so single-mode fiber carries the highest bit rates over the longest spans; the price is a laser source and tight alignment of a core only about 9 µm wide. Multimode fiber (V in the tens) accepts light from LEDs and VCSELs and uses cheap connectors, but its many modes spread pulses, so it serves links of a few hundred metres. When several modes arrive together at a receiver, the speckle pattern they make shifts with vibration and temperature, adding modal noise.

1.7 Phase velocity and group velocity

A light wave in a fiber has two speeds. The crests of the carrier move at the phase velocity; the envelope of a pulse, which carries the bits, moves at the group velocity. Link timing, the delay of a bit from end to end and the spreading of pulses all follow the group velocity. The two speeds differ because the effective index changes with wavelength, and the group index Ng captures that difference in one number. Only a few universities teach this topic in detail, but delay calculations from it are common short questions.

You need: the propagation constant β and effective index from Electromagnetic mode theory; modes in planar guides.

Phase velocity: how fast the crests move

A guided mode at one frequency varies as ej(ωt − βz). A point of constant phase, a crest, satisfies ωt − βz = constant, so it moves at

vp = ω/β = c/neff

vp is the phase velocity in m/s; ω the angular frequency in rad/s; β the propagation constant in rad/m; c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s; neff = β/k₀ the mode's effective index. For a plane wave in bulk glass neff is just n.

A single unchanging frequency carries no information: it has no start, no end and no bits. Information needs modulation, and a modulated signal contains a band of frequencies.

Group velocity and the group index

Two waves at nearby frequencies ω ± Δω add to a carrier inside a slowly varying envelope. The crests travel at ω/β, but the envelope peak, where the two waves stay in step, travels at Δω/Δβ. In the limit this is the group velocity:

vg = dω/dβ

the slope of the ω–β curve at the carrier frequency.

Write β = 2πneff/λ and differentiate with respect to ω (using dλ/dω = −λ²/2πc). The result is 1/vg = dβ/dω = (1/c)(neff − λ dneff/dλ), so

vg = c/Ng, Ng = neff − λ dneff/dλ
Ng is the group index (dimensionless); dneff/dλ is the slope of the effective index with wavelength. In silica near 1550 nm the slope is negative, so Ng is larger than neff and pulses travel slower than crests.

A pulse launched into a fiber of length L arrives after the group delay

τ = L/vg = L · Ng / c

τ in seconds, L in metres. This delay, not L/vp, is what a timing or rise-time budget uses.

If Ng itself changes with wavelength, the different wavelengths in a pulse arrive at different times and the pulse spreads. That is chromatic dispersion, which comes with signal degradation in fibers (Chromatic dispersion: material and waveguide).

Reading the numbers

In silica fibers Ng and neff differ by only 1 to 2 %, so vp and vg are close, but over long spans the difference adds up; the worked example finds it for 10 km. With Ng near 1.48, each kilometre of fiber delays a pulse by about 4.9 µs, a number used when timing distant sites.

  • Use vg and Ng for anything that carries bits: delay, bit slots, synchronisation, dispersion.
  • Use vp and neff for phase: interference, Bragg gratings, the phase shift in a modulator arm.
  • Both change with waveguide geometry as well as with the glass, because neff depends on how much of the mode runs in the cladding.

Worked example

1.8 Integrated optical waveguides: strip and channel

An integrated optical waveguide is a narrow strip of high-index material on a chip that holds light in two directions: vertically by the layer stack and sideways by the strip edges. Many such guides, with splitters, couplers and modulators, share one substrate as a photonic integrated circuit. The guide is a planar slab cut to a finite width, so its design reuses the slab V number, and the effective index method turns the two-dimensional problem into two slab problems. Only a few universities teach this topic in detail.

You need: the slab V number and mode count from Electromagnetic mode theory; modes in planar guides.

Strip, ridge and channel guides

A planar guide confines light only across its thickness. To route light on a chip it must be confined sideways as well, which needs a core of finite width w as well as thickness t. The core has index n₁; the substrate below and the cladding around it have lower indices (here both n₂). Light is held wherever the core meets lower-index material.

  • Strip (or raised strip) guide: the core layer is etched into a strip standing on the substrate, with air or a deposited cladding over it. The sidewalls give a strong index step.
  • Ridge or rib guide: the core is only partly etched, leaving a thin slab on each side of a raised ridge; the side confinement is weaker, which keeps the guide single-mode at larger widths and lowers sidewall scattering.
  • Channel (buried or embedded) guide: the core is a rectangle buried in cladding on all sides, made by diffusion or ion exchange into the substrate or by depositing cladding over a strip.

Names vary between books: many use 'channel waveguide' for any guide that confines in two directions, strips and ridges included, and others reserve it for the buried core. Say which you mean when you answer.

Single-mode check and the effective index method

The thickness direction is a slab, so the slab V of the mode-theory chapter applies, with the same full-thickness convention:

V = (2πt/λ) √(n₁² − n₂²)

t is the core thickness and λ the vacuum wavelength in the same unit; √(n₁² − n₂²) is the NA of the index step, here a measure of contrast rather than a launch angle. A symmetric stack is single-mode across its thickness when V < π. In an asymmetric stack (air above) even the fundamental mode has a cutoff, and the single-mode range moves up by that amount.

The effective index method handles the width. Step 1: solve the vertical slab (thickness t) for its mode; its effective index neff,1 lies between n₂ and n₁. Step 2: treat the guide, seen from above, as a lateral slab of width w with core index neff,1 and side index n₂ (for a buried channel), and apply the slab rule again with Vw = (2πw/λ)√(neff,1² − n₂²). The guide is single-mode only if both checks pass.

Because neff,1 is below n₁, the lateral contrast is weaker than the material contrast, so a guide is often much wider than it is thick before a second lateral mode appears. A high-contrast platform such as silicon on silica holds light in a core about 0.22 µm thick and 0.5 µm wide and allows bends of a few micrometres radius; low-contrast glass guides are several micrometres across and need millimetre bends.

Where integrated guides are used

Photonic integrated circuits combine guides with Y-branch splitters, directional couplers, ring resonators, arrayed waveguide gratings and modulators on one chip. A Mach–Zehnder modulator splits the guided light into two arms, shifts the phase in one arm with an applied voltage, and recombines them so that the output intensity follows the voltage (see Optical modulators and Mach-Zehnder devices). Practical limits are loss from sidewall roughness, coupling loss to a fiber whose mode is far larger than the chip's, and different behaviour for the two polarizations.

Worked example

Question. A buried channel guide has core thickness t = 0.5 µm, width w = 2 µm, n₁ = 1.50 and n₂ = 1.45 all round, used at λ = 1.55 µm. Find the NA of the index step and V for the thickness direction, and decide whether the thickness direction is single-mode.

Exam traps

  • Do not use the fiber limit 2.405 for a slab or strip. With V built on the full thickness, a symmetric slab is single-mode for V < π (half-thickness books write π/2).
  • The thickness check says nothing about the width. A guide can be single-mode vertically and multimode sideways; check the width with the effective index.
  • In the lateral step use neff,1 from the vertical slab, not n₁; using n₁ overstates the lateral contrast and the mode count.
  • Effective indices obey n₂ < neff < n₁, and each added confinement lowers neff a little more.
  • Keep t, w and λ in one unit; t/λ and w/λ must be pure numbers.

1.9 Photonic crystals and photonic band gap

A photonic crystal is a dielectric whose refractive index repeats with a period comparable to the wavelength of light, in one, two or three dimensions. Waves reflected from the many repeated boundaries add in phase for a band of wavelengths, so light in that band cannot travel through the structure: that band is the photonic band gap. Breaking the pattern on purpose makes a defect that traps or guides light inside the gap. These ideas give near-perfect mirrors, tiny cavities, sharp bends on chips and fibers that guide light in a hollow core.

A one-dimensional crystal: the Bragg stack

The simplest photonic crystal is a stack of alternating layers: a high-index layer nH of thickness dH, then a low-index layer nL of thickness dL, repeated. The period a = dH + dL is one full pair of layers. At every interface a little light is reflected. When the reflections from successive periods return in phase, they add up and the stack reflects strongly. This is Bragg reflection, and at normal incidence it is centred on

λB = 2 n̄ a, n̄ = (nHdH + nLdL)/a
λB is the first-order centre wavelength of the reflection band (in vacuum); n̄ is the average index of one period, weighted by layer thickness; a is the period.

The strongest design is the quarter-wave stack, where each layer is a quarter wavelength thick in its own material: nHdH = nLdL = λB/4. Inside the band no wave can propagate through the periodic medium; the field decays from layer to layer, so a long enough stack reflects almost all of the light. Outside the band the stack passes most of the light, with small ripples.

The band is a range, not a line. For a quarter-wave stack its width in frequency is Δf/f₀ = (4/π) sin⁻¹[(nH − nL)/(nH + nL)], so a larger index contrast gives a wider gap. The peak reflectance rises with the number of pairs N. A stack with enough contrast can reflect a band for all angles of incidence and both polarizations: an omnidirectional reflector.

Two and three dimensions, and defects

A two-dimensional photonic crystal repeats in a plane, for example a triangular lattice of air holes etched through a thin silicon slab. A three-dimensional crystal repeats in all three directions, as in a woodpile of crossed rods or an opal of packed spheres; only a 3D crystal can have a complete gap that blocks every direction. In a 2D slab, light is held vertically by total internal reflection and in the plane by the gap.

A defect breaks the period and creates an allowed state inside the gap. Leave out one row of holes and the missing row becomes a line-defect waveguide: light at a gap wavelength cannot leak into the crystal on either side, so it follows the line, even round sharp corners. Leave out one or a few holes and you get a point-defect microcavity that stores light for many cycles in a volume about a wavelength across.

Fabrication methods follow the dimension: lithography and etching for 2D slabs; layer-by-layer stacking, self-assembly of spheres, and holographic lithography with interfering laser beams for 3D. Each trades precision against cost and area.

Photonic crystal fiber and practical limits

A photonic crystal fiber (PCF) has a regular array of air holes running along its length. In an index-guiding PCF the core is solid glass, the holey cladding has a lower average index, and a modified form of total internal reflection guides the light; the hole size and spacing set the mode area and dispersion, and some designs stay single-mode at every wavelength. In a band-gap PCF the periodic cladding forms a gap, so light can be guided in a hollow core of lower index than the glass, which no total-internal-reflection fiber can do. Hollow-core fibers carry high power, have very low nonlinearity and lower latency, because light travels mostly in air.

A photonic crystal is not loss-free by itself. Rough hole walls and errors in the period scatter light, the gap must sit on the source wavelength, and coupling to an ordinary fiber adds loss. For a 1550 nm gap with n̄ = 1.5 the period must be 1550/(2 × 1.5) = 517 nm, so the structure must be made to a few nanometres. The same Bragg rule designs thin-film filters, fiber Bragg gratings and laser mirrors.

Worked example

Question. A quarter-wave mirror for 1550 nm is made of silicon nitride (nH = 2.0) and silica (nL = 1.45). Find (a) the two layer thicknesses and the period, (b) the average index and the Bragg wavelength, as a check, (c) the width of the stop band, (d) the peak reflectance of 8 pairs between air and a silica substrate, which a transfer-matrix calculation gives as 98.4 %, and the fraction transmitted.

  1. (a) dH = λB/(4nH) = 1550/(4 × 2.0) = 193.8 nm; dL = 1550/(4 × 1.45) = 267.2 nm; a = 193.8 + 267.2 = 461.0 nm.
  2. (b) n̄ = (2.0 × 193.8 + 1.45 × 267.2)/461.0 = (387.5 + 387.5)/461.0 = 1.681. λB = 2 × 1.681 × 461.0 = 1550 nm, as designed.
  3. (c) (nH − nL)/(nH + nL) = 0.55/3.45 = 0.1594; sin⁻¹(0.1594) = 0.1601 rad; Δf/f₀ = (4/π) × 0.1601 = 0.204.
  4. The band edges sit at f₀(1 ± 0.102), that is at λ = 1550/1.102 = 1407 nm and 1550/0.898 = 1726 nm: a stop band about 320 nm wide.
  5. (d) R = 98.4 % at 1550 nm, so 1.6 % is transmitted. Fewer pairs reflect less: the same calculation gives 81 % for 4 pairs and 99.6 % for 10.
  6. Check: using the plain mean (2.0 + 1.45)/2 = 1.725 instead of the thickness-weighted n̄ would predict 2 × 1.725 × 461.0 = 1590 nm, 40 nm off.

Answer. dH = 193.8 nm, dL = 267.2 nm, a = 461.0 nm, n̄ = 1.681 and λB = 1550 nm; the stop band runs from about 1407 to 1726 nm, and 8 pairs reflect 98.4 % at its centre.

Exam traps

  • The period a is one full pair of layers, not one layer.
  • n̄ in λB = 2n̄a is weighted by thickness. The plain mean of nH and nL is correct only when both layers have equal thickness.
  • The gap is a band of wavelengths, not one wavelength; λB is its centre.
  • Do not confuse the photonic band gap (a range of light wavelengths that cannot propagate) with a semiconductor's electronic band gap (an energy range with no electron states).
  • A band-gap fiber can guide in a hollow, lower-index core; an index-guiding PCF still needs the core index above the cladding's average.

In the weakly guiding fiber the LP₁₁ field in the core varies as J₁(ur/a); at cutoff the cladding decay constant goes to zero, and the matching condition reduces to J₀(V) = 0. The first zero of J₀ is 2.405, so LP₁₁ exists only for V > 2.405. LP₀₁'s condition at cutoff is J₁(V) = 0 with root V = 0, so it is always guided.
  9. Number of modes at 850 nm. LP₀₁ has 2 field patterns (two polarizations) and LP₁₁ has 4 (two orientations, two polarizations), so 6 patterns travel. The large-V estimate V²/2 = 3.40²/2 = 5.8 happens to agree here, but near cutoff the exact count must be used.
  10. Practical note. As V falls, the LP₀₁ field spreads further into the cladding (the mode field diameter grows) and the mode is held more weakly, so bend loss rises. That is why λc is kept only moderately below the working window instead of far below it.
  11. Closing check. The fiber is single-mode for every λ > λc = 1.20 µm and multimode below it; 0.85 µm is below and 1.55 µm above, matching the V values 3.40 and 1.86.

Multimode fiber's large core makes field splicing and testing easier and tolerates dust and misalignment better. Evaluate Describe photonic crystals and the photonic band gap. Design a quarter-wave silicon nitride/silica mirror for 1550 nm and find its stop band. Contrast index guiding with band-gap guiding, and explain how a photonic crystal fiber and a photonic crystal waveguide use these ideas. 13 marks

Points to cover. Periodic dielectric with period near λ; Bragg reflection from every period; Band gap: wavelengths with no propagating mode; from periodicity, not absorption; Quarter-wave stack: dH = 193.8 nm, dL = 267.2 nm, a = 461.0 nm, λB = 2n̄a = 1550 nm, band 1407–1726 nm; Defects create states in the gap: line-defect waveguides and point-defect cavities; Index-guiding PCF (solid core, modified TIR) against band-gap PCF (hollow core).

Model answer.

  1. Photonic crystal. A dielectric whose refractive index repeats in one, two or three dimensions with a period a comparable to the wavelength. Light is partly reflected at every boundary; at certain wavelengths the reflections from successive periods return in phase and add.
  2. Photonic band gap. For a band of wavelengths around the Bragg condition, no wave can propagate through the periodic medium: the field decays from period to period and the light is reflected. The gap comes from the periodicity alone, not from absorption, much as the periodic lattice of a crystal makes an electronic band gap for electrons.
  3. Quarter-wave mirror for 1550 nm with nH = 2.0 (silicon nitride) and nL = 1.45 (silica):

    peak R, 8 pairstransfer-matrix calculation98.4 %
  4. Defects. Breaking the period creates an allowed state inside the gap. A missing row of holes in a 2D crystal is a line defect: light at a gap wavelength cannot enter the crystal on either side, so it is guided along the line, even round 60° or 90° bends of a few micrometres. A missing hole is a point defect, a microcavity that stores light for many cycles in about a cubic wavelength; it is used for filters and tiny lasers.
  5. Index guiding. The core has a higher index than the cladding and light is held by total internal reflection. An index-guiding photonic crystal fiber has a solid glass core inside a cladding of air holes; the holes lower the cladding's average index, so a modified TIR guides the light. Hole size and spacing set the mode area and dispersion, and some designs stay single-mode over a very wide band.
  6. Band-gap guiding. The cladding is a photonic crystal whose gap forbids light at the working wavelength, so light is trapped in a core of lower index than the cladding, even air. A hollow-core band-gap fiber guides light mostly in air, giving low nonlinearity, high power handling and lower latency, which TIR cannot do.
  7. Photonic crystal waveguide on a chip. A line defect in a silicon slab with a lattice of air holes: in-plane light is held by the gap, vertical light by TIR in the slab. Its strength is sharp bends and slow light (large group index) in a small area.
  8. Evaluation. Band-gap structures give compact mirrors, cavities and bends that ordinary TIR guides cannot, but they demand nanometre accuracy (a 1550 nm gap needs a period near 460 to 520 nm), suffer scattering from rough holes, and work only inside the gap.
  9. Closing check. The plain mean (2.0 + 1.45)/2 = 1.725 would put λB at 2 × 1.725 × 461.0 = 1590 nm; only the thickness-weighted n̄ = 1.681 gives the design wavelength 1550 nm.
Analyze Explain strip, ridge and channel waveguides used in integrated optics. Why is light confined in two dimensions, and how is a single-mode guide designed? Use a buried guide 0.5 µm thick and 2 µm wide with n₁ = 1.50, n₂ = 1.45 at 1.55 µm. What difficulties appear when the guide is made very small? 10 marks

Points to cover. Strip: etched core; ridge: partly etched; channel: buried core; Vertical confinement by the layer stack, lateral by the side index step; Effective index method: thickness slab V = 0.778 → neff,1 = 1.4564; width slab Vw = 1.11 < π; Single-mode up to about 5.7 µm width; Difficulties: sidewall scattering, fiber coupling loss, polarization dependence.

Model answer.

  1. Types. A strip guide is a core layer etched into a strip on the substrate, covered by air or a deposited cladding. A ridge (rib) guide is only partly etched, leaving thin slabs at the sides that weaken lateral confinement. A channel (buried) guide has a rectangular core surrounded by cladding on all sides, made by diffusion, ion exchange or deposition. Some books use 'channel waveguide' for all of these.
  2. Two-dimensional confinement. A planar slab holds light only across its thickness. Cutting the core to a finite width adds an index step at each side, so light is held vertically by the layer stack and sideways by the strip edges, and can be routed round a chip.
  3. Thickness check (slab V with full thickness t):
    NA = √(1.50² − 1.45²) = √0.1475 = 0.3841
V = (2πt/λ)·NA = (2π × 0.5/1.55) × 0.3841 = 2.027 × 0.3841 = 0.778 < π
  4. The stack is symmetric, so V < π means one mode across the thickness; solving the slab gives neff,1 = 1.4564.
  5. Width check by the effective index method. Treat the guide from above as a slab of width w with core index neff,1 and side index n₂:
    lateral NA = √(1.4564² − 1.45²) = √0.01860 = 0.1364
Vw = (2π × 2/1.55) × 0.1364 = 8.107 × 0.1364 = 1.11 < π
single-mode width limit: w < λ/(2 × 0.1364) = 1.55/0.2728 = 5.7 µm
  6. Both checks pass, so the guide is single-mode, with effective index about 1.451. The lateral contrast (0.136) is much weaker than the material contrast (0.384), which is why the guide can be four times wider than thick and still single-mode.
  7. Design steps. Choose the index contrast (sets bend radius and mode size); choose t so the vertical V is below π; find neff,1; choose w so the lateral V is below π; confirm with a numerical mode solver, because the effective index method is an estimate.
  8. Difficulties when the guide is small. (1) Sidewall roughness from etching scatters light, and loss rises as the mode presses harder on the walls of a small, high-contrast core. (2) The chip's mode is far smaller than a fiber's (about 10 µm), so butt coupling loses much light unless tapers or grating couplers are used. (3) A thin, wide guide treats TE and TM differently, so the device becomes polarization dependent. (4) Small dimension errors shift neff and detune filters and interferometers.
  9. Closing check. The guide index 1.4514 lies between n₂ = 1.45 and neff,1 = 1.4564, as each added confinement must lower neff.
Analyze Explain how a skew ray differs from a meridional ray, derive or state the acceptance condition for skew rays, and show with numbers for a fiber of NA = 0.2425 why skew rays are accepted over a wider cone. What practical effects follow in a multimode fiber? 10 marks

Points to cover. Meridional: in a plane through the axis, crosses it; skew: never crosses it, helical path; Skew acceptance n₀ sin θas cos γ = NA, so θas ≥ θa; Numbers: γ = 0: 14.03°; 45°: 20.06°; 60°: 29.01°; Effects: more light collected from broad sources, measured NA larger over short lengths, longer paths add to modal spread.

Model answer.

  1. Meridional ray. It lies in a plane that contains the fiber axis; seen end-on it runs along a diameter, crossing the axis between reflections. Its acceptance condition is n₀ sin θa = NA.
  2. Skew ray. It enters off-axis and never crosses the axis. Seen end-on it traces a polygon of chords inside the core, each touching an inner circle; along the fiber it follows a helix. Two angles describe it: θ, its angle to the axis, and γ, the angle between its end-on projection and the core radius at the point of reflection. A meridional ray has γ = 0.
  3. Constant reflection angle. In a straight, uniform step-index fiber every chord of a given skew ray is the same length and meets the wall at the same angle, so if the first reflection satisfies TIR, every reflection does.
  4. Acceptance condition. The direction cosine of the ray along the wall's normal is sin θr cos γ (θr inside the core), so TIR needs n₁ sin θr cos γ ≤ n₁ cos θc = √(n₁² − n₂²). With Snell's law at the end face, n₀ sin θ = n₁ sin θr:
    n₀ sin θas · cos γ = √(n₁² − n₂²) = NA
sin θas = NA / cos γ  ≥  NA = sin θa

  7. Practical effects. (1) A broad source such as an LED, or an off-axis launch, puts much power into skew rays, so a multimode fiber collects a little more light than the meridional NA predicts. (2) A far-field NA measured on a short length can come out larger than √(n₁² − n₂²), because skew and leaky rays leave at wider angles before they have been lost. (3) Skew rays follow longer helical paths, so they add to the spread of arrival times in multimode fiber. (4) Skew rays near the edge of the cone are hard to strip; test launches use mode scramblers and mandrel wraps to set a known launch.

Glossary

Acceptance angle
The largest launch angle, from the axis, at which a meridional ray is guided.
Channel waveguide
A guide that confines light in both transverse directions; in some books only the buried kind.
Cladding
The lower-index glass around the core that makes total internal reflection possible.
Coating
The plastic layer outside the cladding that protects the glass; it carries no light.
Confinement factor
The fraction of a mode's power that travels inside the core.
Core
The central, higher-index region of a fiber that carries the light.
Critical angle
The angle of incidence at which the refracted ray grazes the boundary; sin θc = n₂/n₁.
Cutoff wavelength
The wavelength above which a fiber is single-mode; λc = 2πa·NA/2.405.
Effective index
neff = β/k₀; it lies between the cladding and core indices.
Evanescent field
The part of a guided field that decays exponentially into the cladding and carries no power away.
Graded-index fiber
A fiber whose core index falls smoothly from the axis to the cladding.
Group index
Ng = neff − λ dneff/dλ.
Group velocity
vg = dω/dβ = c/Ng, the speed of a pulse envelope.
LP mode
A linearly polarized mode, the label used for weakly guiding fibers, such as LP₀₁ and LP₁₁.
Meridional ray
A ray that lies in a plane containing the fiber axis and crosses the axis.
Mode
A field pattern that satisfies Maxwell's equations and the boundary conditions and keeps its shape as it travels.
Mode field diameter
The diameter at which the intensity of the fundamental mode falls to 1/e² of its peak.
Numerical aperture
NA = n₀ sin θa = √(n₁² − n₂²); a dimensionless measure of light-gathering power.
Phase velocity
vp = ω/β, the speed of a crest.
Photonic band gap
A band of wavelengths that cannot propagate through a photonic crystal.
Photonic crystal
A dielectric whose index repeats with a period comparable to the wavelength.
Photonic crystal fiber
A fiber with a regular array of air holes along its length, guiding by index contrast or by a band gap.
Propagation constant
β, the phase change per metre of a mode along the guide, in rad/m.
Refractive index
n = c/v, the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to its speed in the material.
Relative index difference
Δ = (n₁² − n₂²)/(2n₁²), about 0.003 for single-mode and 0.01 to 0.02 for multimode fiber.
Skew ray
A ray that never crosses the axis and follows a helical path; it is accepted over a wider cone.
Step-index fiber
A fiber with a constant core index and a sudden drop to the cladding index.
Total internal reflection
Complete reflection of light going into a lower-index medium at an angle of incidence above the critical angle.
V number
The normalized frequency; for a fiber V = (2πa/λ)·NA, for a slab V = (2πd/λ)·NA.
