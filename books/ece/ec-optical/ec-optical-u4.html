4.1 Detection principles: quantum efficiency and responsivity

A photodiode turns light into current at a fixed rate. Quantum efficiency η counts how many of the arriving photons give a collected electron; responsivity R gives the current in amperes for each watt of light. Together they connect the optical power in a fiber to the electrical signal a receiver sees, so a link designer can say "1 µW in gives about 0.5 µA out". Some syllabi cover this as a topic of its own; most fold it into the photodiode chapter.

You need photon energy and the bandgap from the chapter on optical sources: E = hν = hc/λ and λ = 1.24/Eg.

Photon energy and the photon rate

A photon of wavelength λ carries energy E = hν = hc/λ. Here h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s is Planck's constant, ν is the optical frequency in hertz, c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s is the speed of light and λ is in metres. At λ = 0.85 µm, E = 2.34 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; at 1.55 µm, E = 1.28 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. A longer wavelength means a weaker photon.

An optical power of P watts is a stream of photons. Each second P/E photons arrive. So 1 µW at 0.85 µm is about 4.3 × 10¹² photons per second, and 1 µW at 1.55 µm is about 7.8 × 10¹² photons per second, because each photon at 1.55 µm carries less energy.

Quantum efficiency and the cutoff wavelength

η = (electrons collected per second) ÷ (photons arriving per second)

η is a pure ratio with no units, between 0 and 1. A good PIN photodiode has η of about 0.7 to 0.9.

Photons are lost in three ways. Some are reflected at the surface before they enter the semiconductor (an antireflection coating keeps this to a few percent). Some pass through the absorbing layer without being absorbed. Some create electron–hole pairs that recombine before the junction field collects them. Each loss lowers η.

A photon is absorbed only if its energy is at least the bandgap Eg. So every material has a cutoff wavelength λc = 1.24/Eg (λc in µm, Eg in eV). Beyond λc the material is transparent and η = 0. In a real diode η starts to fall a little before λc, because absorption grows weak near the band edge. The cutoff is a property of the material, not of the circuit.

This is why silicon cannot be used in the 1.3 µm and 1.55 µm fiber windows: its cutoff is 1.11 µm.

Responsivity: amperes per watt

Each collected electron carries charge q = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. The primary photocurrent Ip is q times the number of electrons collected per second, which is η times the photon rate P/(hν).

Ip = ηqP/(hν) = ηqλP/(hc)

Ip is the photocurrent in A and P the optical power falling on the diode in W.

R = Ip/P = ηqλ/(hc) = ηλ/1.24

R is the responsivity in A/W. In the last form λ is in micrometres: 1.24 µm·V is hc/q worked out.

R grows in proportion to λ for a fixed η, because at a longer wavelength one watt holds more photons. Since η ≤ 1, a photodiode without gain has R ≤ λ/1.24: about 0.69 A/W at 0.85 µm, 1.05 A/W at 1.3 µm and 1.25 A/W at 1.55 µm. This ceiling holds for the primary current of a PN or PIN diode. An avalanche photodiode multiplies the primary current by its gain M, so its measured responsivity MR can be many times the ceiling; the primary R inside it still obeys R ≤ λ/1.24.

Some syllabi ask for quantum efficiency and responsivity as two separate definitions; others ask for the derivation of R = ηqλ/(hc). The junction that collects the carriers is the subject of PN and PIN photodiodes, and gain is the subject of Avalanche photodiodes.

Worked example

Question. A silicon PIN photodiode has η = 0.8 at λ = 0.85 µm and receives P = 1 µW. Find the photon arrival rate, the collected electron rate, the photocurrent and the responsivity. Take h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s and q = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

  1. Photon energy: E = hc/λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3 × 10⁸)/(0.85 × 10⁻⁶) = 2.339 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  2. Photon rate: Np = P/E = (1 × 10⁻⁶ W)/(2.339 × 10⁻¹⁹ J) = 4.28 × 10¹² photons per second.
  3. Collected electron rate: Ne = ηNp = 0.8 × 4.28 × 10¹² = 3.42 × 10¹² electrons per second.
  4. Photocurrent: Ip = qNe = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 3.42 × 10¹² = 5.48 × 10⁻⁷ A = 0.548 µA.
  5. Responsivity: R = Ip/P = 0.548 µA/1 µW = 0.548 A/W. Check with the shortcut: R = ηλ/1.24 = 0.8 × 0.85/1.24 = 0.548 A/W. The two routes agree.
  6. Compare with the ceiling at 0.85 µm: λ/1.24 = 0.685 A/W, and 0.548/0.685 = 0.8 = η, as it must be.

Answer. Np = 4.28 × 10¹² s⁻¹, Ne = 3.42 × 10¹² s⁻¹, Ip = 0.548 µA and R = 0.548 A/W.

Worked example 2

Question. An InGaAs photodiode gives 0.90 µA when 1.0 µW of 1.55 µm light falls on it. Find its responsivity and quantum efficiency. Would the same diode respond at 1.70 µm? (Eg of InGaAs = 0.75 eV.)

  1. Responsivity: R = Ip/P = 0.90 µA/1.0 µW = 0.90 A/W.
  2. Quantum efficiency: η = 1.24R/λ = 1.24 × 0.90/1.55 = 0.72. So 72 % of the photons give a collected electron.
  3. Check by counting: photons per second = P/E = 1 × 10⁻⁶/1.282 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 7.80 × 10¹²; electrons per second = Ip/q = 0.90 × 10⁻⁶/1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 5.62 × 10¹²; ratio 5.62/7.80 = 0.72.
  4. Cutoff: λc = 1.24/0.75 = 1.65 µm. At 1.70 µm the photon energy is 1.24/1.70 = 0.73 eV, below the bandgap, so the light is not absorbed and R = 0.

Answer. R = 0.90 A/W and η = 0.72; at 1.70 µm the diode does not respond, because 1.70 µm is beyond its 1.65 µm cutoff.

Exam traps

  • Putting λ in metres into R = ηλ/1.24. The 1.24 shortcut needs λ in micrometres; with λ in metres use R = ηqλ/(hc).
  • Mixing up the units: η has no units, R is in A/W. "η = 0.8 A/W" loses the mark.
  • Saying no detector can ever show R above λ/1.24. The ceiling is for the primary current; an APD with gain M shows MR, which can be far above it.
  • Taking η = 1 by default. Surface reflection, light that passes through, and recombination all make η less than 1.
  • Using R = ηλ/1.24 past the cutoff wavelength. Beyond λc = 1.24/Eg the material does not absorb, so R = 0, not a larger value.

4.2 PN and PIN photodiodes

A reverse-biased p–n junction turns absorbed light into current: each absorbed photon makes an electron–hole pair, and the junction field sweeps the pair out before it recombines. A plain PN diode collects light only in its thin depletion layer, so it is inefficient and slow. Putting a wide intrinsic (i) layer between the p and n sides gives the PIN photodiode, the standard detector of fiber links from 0.85 µm to 1.55 µm. This chapter covers how it collects carriers, the trade-off between efficiency, capacitance and speed, and the numbers a receiver designer works out.

You need quantum efficiency and responsivity from Detection principles.

Reverse bias turns photons into drift current

A PN photodiode is a p–n junction held in reverse bias. The reverse voltage widens the depletion region and makes its electric field strong. When a photon with energy hν ≥ Eg is absorbed, it creates an electron–hole pair. If this happens inside the depletion region, the field separates the pair at once: the electron drifts to the n side and the hole to the p side. This drift of charge is the photocurrent, and it flows in the external circuit.

A pair created in the neutral p or n region feels almost no field. It moves only by slow diffusion and often recombines before it reaches the junction. Any carrier that does diffuse in arrives late and gives a slow tail on the pulse. So in a plain PN diode, with a depletion width of about a micrometre, much of the light is wasted and the response is slow.

η = (1 − r)(1 − e−αw)

η is the quantum efficiency; r is the fraction of light reflected at the surface; α is the absorption coefficient (in m⁻¹ or cm⁻¹); w is the width of the depleted layer. The factor (1 − r) is the light that enters, and (1 − e−αw) is the part of it absorbed within w. The formula assumes every pair made in w is collected.

The PIN structure widens the collecting region

A PIN photodiode places an undoped (intrinsic) i layer between a heavily doped p⁺ layer and an n⁺ layer. A small reverse bias depletes the whole i layer, so the field acts across its full width and almost all the light is absorbed where carriers are collected. Light enters through a thin p⁺ layer, kept thin so that little light is absorbed there, and an antireflection coating brings r down to a few percent. The i width is now chosen for the optics rather than set by doping.

How wide the i layer must be depends on α. InGaAs at 1.55 µm absorbs strongly (α ≈ 10⁴ cm⁻¹, so 1/α = 1 µm), and an i layer of 2 to 5 µm absorbs 86 % to 99 % of the light. Silicon at 0.85 µm absorbs weakly (α of the order of 10³ cm⁻¹ or less), so a silicon PIN needs an i layer of 20 to 50 µm and is slower.

  • Silicon covers 0.8–0.9 µm, with responsivity around 0.5–0.6 A/W.
  • Germanium and InGaAs cover 1.3 µm and 1.55 µm. A packaged InGaAs PIN typically gives about 0.9 to 1.0 A/W at 1.55 µm; the ideal (1 − r)(1 − e−αw) estimate is higher because it ignores light lost in the p⁺ layer and under the contacts.

A wide i layer lowers the junction capacitance, which helps speed, but carriers take longer to cross it, which hurts speed. The next section puts numbers on both.

Two bandwidth limits: transit time and RC

Carriers cross the i layer at the saturation velocity vsat, about 1 × 10⁵ m/s in silicon and about 6.5 × 10⁴ m/s for the slower carriers (holes) in InGaAs. The transit time is ttr = w/vsat. For light absorbed evenly across the layer, the current response falls by 3 dB at ftr ≈ 0.44/ttr. Some textbooks use the rougher estimate vsat/(2w) = 0.5/ttr; say which one you use.

The junction is also a parallel-plate capacitor, C = εrε0A/w, with ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m and A the diode area. With the load resistor RL it forms a low-pass filter with corner fRC = 1/(2πRLC).

f3dB ≈ 1/√(1/ftr² + 1/fRC²)

The two limits act together, so the diode's overall bandwidth is lower than either of them. The smaller of the two tells you which one to improve.

Design is a push and pull. A wider w raises η and lowers C (fRC rises), but ttr grows (ftr falls). A larger area A makes fiber alignment easy but raises C. The bias must be high enough to deplete the whole i layer and keep carriers at vsat. For a high-speed receiver, choose w so that both limits are well above the bandwidth needed, then accept the η that results. When even a good PIN gives too little current, internal gain helps: see Avalanche photodiodes.

Worked example

Question. An InGaAs PIN photodiode for 1.55 µm has i-layer width w = 4 µm, absorption coefficient α = 1 × 10⁴ cm⁻¹, surface reflectivity r = 0.03, area A = 1 × 10⁻⁸ m² (a 100 µm × 100 µm square), relative permittivity εr = 13.9 and load resistance RL = 50 Ω. The carriers cross at vsat = 6.5 × 10⁴ m/s. Find η and R, the transit time and ftr, the capacitance and fRC, and the overall bandwidth. Which limit controls?

  1. Same units for αw: w = 4 µm = 4 × 10⁻⁴ cm, so αw = (1 × 10⁴ cm⁻¹)(4 × 10⁻⁴ cm) = 4.0.
  2. Absorbed fraction: 1 − e−4 = 1 − 0.0183 = 0.9817.
  3. η = (1 − r)(1 − e−αw) = 0.97 × 0.9817 = 0.952. Responsivity R = ηλ/1.24 = 0.952 × 1.55/1.24 = 1.19 A/W (an ideal estimate; a packaged diode gives less).
  4. Transit time: ttr = w/vsat = (4 × 10⁻⁶ m)/(6.5 × 10⁴ m/s) = 6.15 × 10⁻¹¹ s = 61.5 ps.
  5. Transit-time bandwidth: ftr = 0.44/ttr = 0.44/(6.15 × 10⁻¹¹ s) = 7.15 GHz.
  6. Capacitance: C = εrε0A/w = (13.9 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 1 × 10⁻⁸)/(4 × 10⁻⁶) = 3.08 × 10⁻¹³ F = 0.308 pF.
  7. RC bandwidth: fRC = 1/(2πRLC) = 1/(2π × 50 × 3.08 × 10⁻¹³) = 10.35 GHz.
  8. Overall: f3dB ≈ 1/√(1/7.15² + 1/10.35²) GHz = 5.88 GHz. The smaller limit is ftr = 7.15 GHz, so this diode is transit-time limited: a thinner i layer would make it faster, at some cost in η.

Answer. η = 0.952 (R ≈ 1.19 A/W), ttr = 61.5 ps, ftr = 7.15 GHz, C = 0.308 pF, fRC = 10.35 GHz, overall bandwidth about 5.9 GHz; transit time limits it.

Exam traps

  • Thinking a thicker i layer is always better. It raises η and lowers C, but it lengthens the transit time and lowers ftr.
  • Dropping (1 − r). With r = 0.03, 3 % of the light is lost before any absorption.
  • Mixing units in αw: α in cm⁻¹ needs w in cm, α in m⁻¹ needs w in m. Mixing them gives an exponent 100 times wrong.
  • Reporting the smaller limit as the bandwidth. The two limits combine, so the real bandwidth (5.9 GHz in the example) is below both.
  • Forward biasing a photodiode. It must be reverse biased (or at zero bias); forward bias injects its own current and swamps the photocurrent.

4.3 Avalanche photodiodes

An avalanche photodiode (APD) gives the photodiode internal current gain. One photon still creates one primary electron–hole pair, but a high-field region near breakdown makes each carrier knock out more pairs by impact ionisation, so the current leaves multiplied by a gain M. The multiplication is random and adds excess noise, so there is a best gain rather than "the more the better". APDs are used where the received light is weak and the amplifier noise would otherwise swamp the signal: long links without optical amplifiers, and optical time-domain reflectometers.

You need responsivity from Detection principles and the PIN structure from PN and PIN photodiodes.

Gain from impact ionisation

A photon absorbed in the depleted absorption layer creates one primary electron–hole pair, exactly as in a PIN. In a PIN that pair gives one electron of external current. In an APD the carrier then drifts into a thin gain region where the field is close to the breakdown field (more than about 10⁵ V/cm). Between collisions it gains enough energy to knock a bound electron into the conduction band, creating a new electron–hole pair. This is impact ionisation.

The new carriers are accelerated too and can ionise again, so one primary pair sets off a cascade. A common structure, the reach-through APD, is a stack n⁺–p–π–p⁺: light is absorbed in the wide, lightly doped π layer, and electrons drift into the thin p layer next to the n⁺ contact, where the field is highest and the gain happens.

M = IM/Ip

M is the avalanche gain (multiplication factor); IM is the multiplied output current and Ip = RP is the primary photocurrent the same light would give with no gain. Typical APDs run at M = 10 to 100.

M ≈ 1/[1 − (V/VB)n]

This is an empirical fit. V is the reverse bias, VB the breakdown voltage and n a fitting number, about 3 to 6. Far below VB, M stays near 1; close to VB it rises very steeply. With VB = 50 V and n = 3, M = 10 needs V = 50 × 0.91/3 = 48.3 V.

Excess noise and the best gain

Multiplication is random: two primary carriers seldom produce the same number of secondary pairs. So the multiplied current fluctuates more than plain shot noise of the same current. This extra spread is the excess noise factor F(M) ≥ 1. It depends on k, the ratio of the hole ionisation rate to the electron ionisation rate (for electrons starting the avalanche).

F(M) = kM + (1 − k)(2 − 1/M)

F is the excess noise factor, M the gain and k the ionisation-rate ratio. If only electrons ionise, k = 0 and F = 2 − 1/M, never more than 2. If both carriers ionise equally, k = 1 and F = M, the worst case. Silicon has k ≈ 0.02 to 0.05; InGaAs APDs with InP gain regions have k ≈ 0.5; germanium is near 1. A rough fit sometimes used is F ≈ Mx, with x ≈ 0.3 for Si and 0.7 for InGaAs.

ish² = 2qIpM²F(M)B

ish² is the mean-square shot-noise current at the APD output in A², q = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and B is the receiver bandwidth in Hz. Dark current adds its own term; see Photodetector noise.

The receiver also has thermal noise ith² from its load or amplifier, which does not depend on M. The signal power is (MIp)². At small M the thermal noise dominates, and SNR grows almost as M². At large M the multiplied shot noise dominates: signal and noise both carry M², but F keeps growing with M, so SNR falls. In between is an optimum Mopt. For a thermal-noise-limited receiver a good estimate is Mopt ≈ [4kBT/(kqRLIp)]1/3, with kB = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K Boltzmann's constant. A smaller k gives a larger Mopt.

Materials, structure and limits

Silicon APDs have low k and low excess noise, and they work well at 0.8–0.9 µm. At 1.3–1.55 µm the absorber must be InGaAs, but avalanche gain inside InGaAs is noisy and leaks at the high fields needed. The separate absorption and multiplication (SAM) APD solves this: an InGaAs layer absorbs the light at a moderate field, and an InP layer next to it does the multiplication at high field.

An APD needs a much higher bias than a PIN: tens of volts for InGaAs/InP and up to a few hundred volts for some silicon reach-through APDs, always just below breakdown. Breakdown voltage rises with temperature, so at a fixed bias M falls as the diode warms. An APD receiver therefore has a temperature-compensated bias supply or a gain-control loop. The avalanche also takes time to build up, and the gain–bandwidth product is roughly fixed: higher M means lower bandwidth.

Worked example

Question. An InGaAs/InP APD has η = 0.8 at λ = 1.3 µm, gain M = 10 and ionisation ratio k = 0.3. It receives P = 100 nW, and the receiver bandwidth is B = 1 GHz. The amplifier's thermal noise is that of RL = 1 kΩ at T = 300 K. Find the multiplied current, F, the shot and thermal noise, and the SNR. Compare with a PIN (M = 1) of the same η.

  1. Responsivity: R = ηλ/1.24 = 0.8 × 1.3/1.24 = 0.839 A/W.
  2. Primary photocurrent: Ip = RP = 0.839 × 100 × 10⁻⁹ = 83.9 nA. Multiplied current: IM = MIp = 839 nA.
  3. Excess noise factor: F = kM + (1 − k)(2 − 1/M) = 0.3 × 10 + 0.7 × 1.9 = 3 + 1.33 = 4.33.
  4. Shot noise: ish² = 2qIpM²FB = 2 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 83.9 × 10⁻⁹ × 100 × 4.33 × 10⁹ = 1.16 × 10⁻¹⁴ A² (rms 108 nA).
  5. Thermal noise: ith² = 4kBTB/RL = 4 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 300 × 10⁹/1000 = 1.66 × 10⁻¹⁴ A² (rms 129 nA).
  6. SNR = IM²/(ish² + ith²) = (839 × 10⁻⁹)²/(2.82 × 10⁻¹⁴) = 7.04 × 10⁻¹³/2.82 × 10⁻¹⁴ = 25.0, or 10 log₁₀ 25.0 = 14.0 dB. (Shot noise alone would give 60.5, or 17.8 dB; leaving out the thermal noise overstates the SNR.)
  7. PIN, M = 1: signal (83.9 nA)² = 7.04 × 10⁻¹⁵ A²; shot 2qIpB = 2.69 × 10⁻¹⁷ A²; thermal 1.66 × 10⁻¹⁴ A². SNR = 7.04 × 10⁻¹⁵/1.66 × 10⁻¹⁴ = 0.424, or −3.7 dB. The APD gains 14.0 − (−3.7) = 17.7 dB.
  8. Is M = 10 the best? Mopt ≈ [4kBT/(kqRLIp)]1/3 = [1.656 × 10⁻²⁰/(0.3 × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1000 × 83.9 × 10⁻⁹)]1/3 = 41081/3 = 16. At M = 16, F = 6.16 and SNR = 14.9 dB, only 0.9 dB better than at M = 10.

Answer. IM = 0.839 µA, F = 4.33, shot noise 108 nA rms, thermal noise 129 nA rms, SNR = 25.0 (14.0 dB) against −3.7 dB for the PIN. The best gain is about 16, giving 14.9 dB.

Exam traps

  • Multiplying only the signal and forgetting the excess noise. Signal power grows as M², shot-noise power as M²F.
  • Saying a bigger M always helps. Past Mopt the extra excess noise outweighs the extra gain, and SNR falls (in the example, 14.7 dB at M = 20 against 14.9 dB at M = 16).
  • Confusing the ionisation ratio k with the gain M, or with Boltzmann's constant kB.
  • Writing the SNR from shot noise only. At moderate gain the amplifier's thermal noise is often the larger term.
  • Using F = M for every APD. That is the worst case, k = 1; a silicon APD with k = 0.02 at M = 100 has F = 0.02 × 100 + 0.98 × 1.99 = 3.95.

4.4 Photodetector noise

Photodetector noise sets the smallest signal a receiver can trust. Shot noise comes from the random arrival of carriers, so it grows with the photocurrent and the dark current. Thermal noise comes from the load resistor and does not depend on the light at all. The two add as powers, and their ratio to the signal power is the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), which tells you whether a receiver is shot-noise limited or thermal-noise limited. Some syllabi cover this as a topic of its own.

You need responsivity from Detection principles and the gain M and excess noise factor F from Avalanche photodiodes.

Shot noise, dark current and thermal noise

Current is a stream of separate electrons. A steady 1 µA is about 6.2 × 10¹² electrons per second, each arriving at a random moment (Poisson statistics), so the current flickers about its mean. This is shot noise. Its mean-square value grows with the current and with the bandwidth B of the receiver after the photodiode (the electrical bandwidth, not the optical frequency).

ish² = 2q(Ip + Id)B

ish² is the mean-square shot-noise current in A²; q = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; Ip is the photocurrent and Id the dark current, both in A; B is in Hz. This is the PIN form.

Dark current is the current that flows with no light: carriers made by heat in the depleted volume (the bulk part, Idb) plus leakage along the surface of the chip (Ids). In an APD the bulk dark current passes through the gain region and is multiplied, while the surface leakage goes around it and is not. So the APD output dark current is MIdb + Ids, and the APD shot noise is

ish² = 2q(Ip + Idb)M²FB + 2qIdsB

M is the avalanche gain and F the excess noise factor. Put M = F = 1 for a PIN to get back the first formula.

Thermal (Johnson) noise is the random motion of electrons in the load resistor RL. It is there with no light at all, and it is set by the resistor, the temperature and the bandwidth. A real amplifier adds its own noise, often written as an amplifier noise figure Fn that multiplies the thermal term.

ith² = 4kBTB/RL

kB = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann's constant, T the temperature in K and RL the load resistance in Ω.

Adding noise and the signal-to-noise ratio

The noise sources are independent, so their mean squares (powers) add: σ² = ish² + ith², where σ is the total rms noise current. Never add rms currents: 5.07 nA and 40.7 nA give √(5.07² + 40.7²) = 41.0 nA, not 45.8 nA.

SNR = Ip²/σ², SNR(dB) = 10 log₁₀ SNR

Ip² is the signal power (as a mean-square current) and σ² the total noise power, both in A². For an APD the signal is (MIp)².

Two limits are worth knowing. Thermal-noise limited: SNR ≈ Ip²RL/(4kBTB). Since Ip ∝ P, the SNR grows as P²: 10 dB more optical power gives 20 dB more SNR. Shot-noise limited: SNR ≈ Ip/(2qB), which grows only as P: 10 dB more power gives 10 dB more SNR. The two terms are equal when IpRL = 2kBT/q, which is 51.7 mV at 300 K. So a PIN with a 1 kΩ load is thermal-noise limited until Ip reaches about 52 µA, far more than a receiver normally sees.

A larger RL lowers thermal noise but raises the RLC time constant and so narrows the bandwidth; this is the problem of Optical receiver structure and sensitivity. Low-frequency 1/f noise also exists below a few kilohertz, but it is negligible at data rates.

Noise equivalent power

Datasheets give the noise as a power per root hertz. Divide the noise mean square by B to get the noise spectral density in A²/Hz; its square root is in A/√Hz.

NEP = (σ/√B)/R

NEP is the noise equivalent power in W/√Hz: the optical power that would give SNR = 1 in a 1 Hz bandwidth. σ/√B is the rms noise current per root hertz in A/√Hz and R the responsivity in A/W. A smaller NEP means a quieter detector.

For the 1 kΩ load at 300 K, √(4kBT/RL) = 4.07 pA/√Hz; with R = 0.8 A/W, NEP = 5.09 pW/√Hz. Over B = 100 MHz the power for SNR = 1 is NEP × √B = 5.09 × 10⁻¹² × 10⁴ = 50.9 nW.

Worked example

Question. A PIN receiver has responsivity R = 0.8 A/W, received power P = 1 µW, dark current Id = 1 nA, bandwidth B = 100 MHz and load RL = 1 kΩ at T = 300 K. Find the rms shot noise, the rms thermal noise, the total rms noise and the SNR. Is the receiver shot-noise or thermal-noise limited?

4.5 Optical receiver structure and sensitivity

An optical receiver turns a weak, noisy train of light pulses back into clean bits. In every bit period it must decide whether the light was on or off, and it must do so at a stated error rate with as little power as possible. The chain is always the same: photodiode, low-noise preamplifier, main amplifier and equaliser, low-pass filter, and a decision circuit clocked by a recovered timing signal. Its key specification is the sensitivity, the weakest average power that still meets the target bit error rate; every link power budget starts from this number.

You need the noise formulas of Photodetector noise; the Q factor used here is explained in Bit error rate and receiver performance.

The chain from photon to bit

A receiver does four jobs in order: convert light to current, amplify that current while adding as little noise as possible, shape the pulses so that each bit stays in its own time slot, and decide one or zero near the centre of each slot.

  • Photodiode (PIN or APD): delivers a current I = RP, where R is the responsivity in A/W and P the received optical power in W.
  • Preamplifier (front end): a low-noise stage that turns the current into a voltage. Its noise usually sets the noise of the whole receiver.
  • Main amplifier and equaliser: raises the signal to a level the decision circuit can use, and corrects pulse spreading from the fiber and the front end.
  • Low-pass filter: removes noise outside the signal band. For NRZ data at bit rate BT its bandwidth is usually about 0.5BT to 0.7BT; wider passes more noise, narrower smears neighbouring bits together.
  • Decision circuit: a comparator that samples the filtered signal once per bit, at t = nT with bit period T = 1/BT and bit number n, and outputs 1 if the sample is above the threshold.
  • Clock recovery: extracts a clock at BT from the transitions in the data and times the decision circuit. Without it the sampling point drifts.
  • Automatic gain control (AGC): a loop that holds the output level steady when the received power changes, so the threshold stays in the right place.

AGC matters because a receiver must work over a dynamic range: the ratio of the strongest to the weakest input it accepts. Too strong a signal overloads the amplifiers, the pulses flatten and errors appear even though there is plenty of light.

Three front ends

The preamplifier sets both the bandwidth and the noise. CT is the total input capacitance (photodiode plus amplifier).

In a transimpedance amplifier (TIA) the resistor Rf sits in the feedback path of an inverting amplifier with open-loop gain A. The output voltage is V = −IRf, so the transimpedance is Rf ohms. Feedback makes the input look like a small resistance Rf/(A + 1), so the bandwidth is about A times wider than a high-impedance front end with RL = Rf, while the thermal noise is still that of the large Rf.

B ≈ A/(2πRfCT)

B is the closed-loop bandwidth in Hz, A the amplifier's open-loop voltage gain, Rf the feedback resistance in Ω and CT the total input capacitance in F.

Doubling Rf lowers the noise current by a factor √2 but halves the bandwidth. That trade-off is the design problem of the front end. With Rf = 10 kΩ, CT = 1 pF and A = 20, B ≈ 318 MHz; a high-impedance front end with the same resistor would give only 15.9 MHz.

Sensitivity

Sensitivity is the minimum average received optical power needed to reach a stated bit error rate (BER) at a stated bit rate. Without both numbers it means nothing. It is usually quoted in dBm: P(dBm) = 10 log₁₀(P/1 mW), so 1 µW is −30 dBm and 100 nW is −40 dBm.

Take a PIN receiver limited by thermal noise, so that a one and a zero have the same rms noise σ. With on–off keying the zero level is (ideally) zero current. For the target BER the two levels must be Qσ above and below the threshold, so the one level must be I1 = 2Qσ. The average current is half of that, Qσ, and the average power follows.

Psens ≈ Qσ/R

Psens is the sensitivity as average received power in W; Q is about 6 for BER = 10⁻⁹ and about 7 for 10⁻¹²; σ is the rms noise current in A; R is the responsivity in A/W.

An APD multiplies the signal by M before the preamplifier, while the preamplifier's noise stays the same, but it adds signal-dependent shot noise. For a zero level of zero and thermal noise σT, the sensitivity becomes

Psens ≈ (Q/R)(qFBQ + σT/M)

F is the excess noise factor at gain M and B the receiver bandwidth. Put M = F = 1 and drop the small shot term to get back QσT/R. The best M makes this smallest; APD receivers are typically 5 to 10 dB more sensitive than PIN receivers of the same speed.

There is also an absolute floor, the quantum limit. Even an ideal detector with no thermal noise makes an error when a "one" pulse happens to contain no photon. For BER = 10⁻⁹ a one must carry about 20 photons on average, so the average is about 10 photons per bit, whatever the bit rate. At 1.55 µm and 2.5 Gbit/s that is 10 × 1.28 × 10⁻¹⁹ J × 2.5 × 10⁹ s⁻¹ = 3.2 nW (−54.9 dBm). Real receivers sit 20 dB or more above it.

  • Mixing peak and average power. Psens ≈ Qσ/R is the average power; the one level needs twice that.
  • Losing the dBm reference: 0 dBm is 1 mW, −30 dBm is 1 µW, and every 10 dB is a factor of ten in power.
  • Writing the transimpedance bandwidth as 1/(2πRfCT). That is the high-impedance value; feedback makes the TIA about A times wider.
  • Setting the filter bandwidth equal to the bit rate "to be safe". About 0.5BT to 0.7BT is enough for NRZ; a wider filter only adds noise.

4.6 Bit error rate and receiver performance

Every optical receiver guesses each bit. Noise makes the sampled current wander, so a 1 can fall below the decision threshold or a 0 can rise above it. The bit error rate (BER) is the probability of a wrong guess. One number, the Q factor, measures how far apart the two levels are compared with their noise, and Q alone fixes the BER. The eye diagram shows the same thing on an oscilloscope, and the power penalty says how much extra light a degraded receiver needs to get back to the target BER. Some syllabi cover this as a topic of its own.

You need the noise currents of Photodetector noise and the receiver chain of Optical receiver structure and sensitivity.

Signal, noise and one number Q

At the sampling instant the receiver holds a current. For a 1 bit its mean is I1, for a 0 bit I0, and the rms noise on each is σ1 and σ0, all in amperes. Noise is taken as Gaussian. The decision threshold ID lies between the levels: a sample above ID is called 1, below it 0.

Errors happen in two ways: a 1 whose sample falls below ID, and a 0 whose sample rises above it. The 1 level is (I1 − ID)/σ1 standard deviations above the threshold, and the 0 level is (ID − I0)/σ0 below it. The best threshold makes these two numbers equal; that common value is Q.

Q = (I1 − I0)/(σ1 + σ0)

Q has no units. It is the gap between the levels measured in units of the sum of the two noises.

ID = (σ0I1 + σ1I0)/(σ0 + σ1)

This is the best threshold (very nearly exact for Gaussian noise). With σ1 = σ0 it is halfway between the levels. When the 1 level is noisier (shot noise, APD noise), the threshold moves down towards the quieter 0 level.

BER from Q, and the eye diagram

With equally likely ones and zeros and the best threshold, each error type has probability ½erfc(Q/√2), and the BER is

BER = ½ erfc(Q/√2) ≈ e−Q²/2/(Q√(2π))

erfc is the complementary error function. The approximation is within about 10 % for Q above 3 and within 3 % for Q ≥ 6.

The eye diagram overlays many bit periods on an oscilloscope. At the sampling instant its vertical opening is the gap I1 − I0; the margins Qσ1 below the 1 level and Qσ0 above the 0 level must fit inside that gap. Noise thickens the traces; intersymbol interference from dispersion or a slow receiver pulls the levels towards each other; timing jitter moves the sampling point sideways. All three close the eye and raise the BER.

Power penalty and non-ideal effects

To compare receivers, ask how much more optical power brings the BER back to the target. The power penalty is

Penalty (dB) = 10 log₁₀(P2/P1)

P1 is the average power needed before the change and P2 after it, in the same unit.

If the noise does not depend on the signal (thermal noise), Q is proportional to P, so the penalty for moving from Q1 to Q2 is 10 log₁₀(Q2/Q1). If shot noise dominates, σ ∝ √P, so Q ∝ √P and the penalty is 20 log₁₀(Q2/Q1). Always state the noise regime.

A finite extinction ratio rex = P0/P1 (light left in the zero) shrinks the gap for the same average power. With thermal noise the penalty is 10 log₁₀[(1 + rex)/(1 − rex)]: 0.87 dB for rex = 0.1. A BER floor is different: the curve of BER against power flattens and more power stops helping. It comes from disturbances that do not shrink as the signal grows, such as interference, mode-partition noise, modal noise and pattern-dependent jitter. APD excess noise does not cause a floor: it enlarges σ1 and σ0 and moves the threshold, but more power still lowers the BER. Dispersion and jitter penalties of whole links are part of link design (the power and rise-time budgets).

The best threshold makes both levels the same number of their own standard deviations away. When σ1 > σ0 that point is closer to I0: ID = (σ0I1 + σ1I0)/(σ0 + σ1).

Apply I1 = 1.2 µA, I0 = 0.1 µA, σ1 = σ0 = 0.1 µA. Find Q and the BER. 4 marks

Q = 1.1/0.2 = 5.5. BER = ½erfc(5.5/√2) = 1.9 × 10⁻⁸.

Analyze At low power the BER of a receiver falls as expected, but above some power it stays at 10⁻¹⁰ however much light is added. What does that tell you? 5 marks

There is a BER floor: an error source that does not shrink as the signal grows, such as interference, mode-partition noise or pattern-dependent jitter. More power will not help; that impairment must be removed.

BER ≈ e−Q²/2/(Q√(2π)), good for Q above about 3.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Forward error correction lets a link run at a raw BER near 10⁻³ and still deliver 10⁻¹² or better after decoding, so the Q needed before decoding drops sharply. Coherent receivers use multilevel formats and soft decisions, where Q is an intermediate measure rather than the final one. BER testers send pseudo-random bit sequences and count errors; Q is also estimated from eye measurements when errors are too rare to count. Takeaways

  1. A photodiode is a current source driven by light: Ip = RP with R = ηλ/1.24 A/W, and R ≤ λ/1.24 for any diode without gain.
  2. Every material has a cutoff λc = 1.24/Eg: silicon stops at 1.11 µm, so the 1.3 and 1.55 µm windows need InGaAs or germanium.
  3. A PIN puts a wide depleted i layer where the light is absorbed: η = (1 − r)(1 − e−αw) rises with w, but so does the transit time, while the capacitance falls; the best width balances ftr and fRC.
  4. An APD multiplies the signal by M and the shot noise by M²F, with F = kM + (1 − k)(2 − 1/M); this gives an optimum gain, larger when k is small.
  5. Noise powers add, never rms currents: σ² = ish² + ith². A PIN receiver is almost always thermal-noise limited, because shot noise wins only when IpRL exceeds 2kBT/q ≈ 52 mV.
  6. A transimpedance front end gives the low noise of a large Rf with a bandwidth about A times wider than a high-impedance stage, which is why it is the standard choice.
  7. Sensitivity is the average power for a target BER at a stated bit rate: Qσ/R for a thermal-limited PIN; an APD at its best gain is typically 5 to 10 dB better.
  8. Q = (I1 − I0)/(σ1 + σ0) alone fixes the BER = ½erfc(Q/√2): Q = 6 gives 10⁻⁹ and Q = 7.03 gives 10⁻¹², provided the threshold is set where both levels are Q noise widths away.
  9. Power penalties turn impairments into decibels: 10 log₁₀(Q2/Q1) with fixed noise, 20 log₁₀(Q2/Q1) when shot-limited; a BER floor is a different problem that more power cannot fix.

Understand Define quantum efficiency and responsivity of a photodetector. Derive R = ηqλ/(hc) and explain why the responsivity rises with wavelength and then falls to zero. 8 marks

Points to cover. η = electrons collected per incident photon; R = Ip/P; Ip = q × ηP/(hν) with hν = hc/λ; so R = ηqλ/(hc) = ηλ/1.24, linear in λ; ceiling for η = 1: 0.69 A/W at 0.85 µm, 1.05 A/W at 1.3 µm; beyond λc = 1.24/Eg no absorption, R = 0.

Model answer.

  1. Quantum efficiency: η = (number of electrons collected per second)/(number of photons arriving per second). It has no units and lies between 0 and 1; a good PIN photodiode has η ≈ 0.7 to 0.9. It is less than 1 because some light is reflected at the surface, some passes through the absorbing layer, and some pairs recombine before they are collected.
  2. Responsivity: R = Ip/P, the primary photocurrent Ip (in A) per watt of optical power P falling on the detector. Its unit is A/W.
  3. Derivation. Each photon carries energy hν = hc/λ, so a power P is a stream of P/(hν) photons per second. A fraction η of them gives a collected electron of charge q, so the current is
    Ip = q × η × P/(hν) = ηqPλ/(hc)
R = Ip/P = ηqλ/(hc)
With hc/q = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3 × 10⁸)/(1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 1.24 × 10⁻⁶ V·m = 1.24 µm·V:
R = ηλ/1.24   (λ in µm, R in A/W)
  4. Why R rises with λ: at a fixed η, one watt of longer-wavelength light contains more photons, because each photon carries less energy. More photons per watt means more electrons per watt, so R grows in proportion to λ.
  5. The ceiling. Because η ≤ 1, a PIN or PN photodiode always has R ≤ λ/1.24:
    λ (µm)R for η = 1 (A/W)R for η = 0.8 (A/W)

  6. Why R then falls to zero: absorption needs hν ≥ Eg. Beyond the cutoff wavelength λc = 1.24/Eg the photons are too weak to lift an electron across the bandgap, the material becomes transparent and η → 0. For silicon (Eg = 1.12 eV) λc = 1.11 µm; for InGaAs (Eg = 0.75 eV) λc = 1.65 µm. So the R(λ) curve is a rising line that drops sharply near λc (in a real diode the drop starts slightly before λc, where absorption becomes weak).

Understand Draw the structure of a PIN photodiode and explain how the intrinsic layer improves performance. Why is the device reverse biased? 8 marks

Points to cover. p⁺–i–n⁺ sketch, light entering through a thin p⁺ layer with an antireflection coating; reverse bias depletes the whole i layer; most light absorbed inside the high-field region, so pairs are swept out by drift; high η: η = (1 − r)(1 − e−αw); wide i layer lowers capacitance, which helps the RC bandwidth; the η-versus-transit-time compromise.

Model answer.

  1. Structure (sketch below): a heavily doped p⁺ layer, a wide undoped intrinsic (i) layer, and a heavily doped n⁺ layer, with metal contacts. Light enters through a thin p⁺ layer that carries an antireflection coating. The diode is reverse biased through a load resistor RL, across which the signal voltage appears.
  2. How it works: an absorbed photon with hν ≥ Eg creates an electron–hole pair. In the depleted i layer a strong field separates the pair at once; the electron drifts to the n⁺ side and the hole to the p⁺ side. This drift current is the photocurrent.
  3. What the i layer improves. (1) Efficiency: in a plain PN diode the depletion layer is thin, so most light is absorbed in the neutral regions, where pairs move by slow diffusion and many recombine. In the PIN the depleted width w is the whole i layer, chosen so that αw is large, and η = (1 − r)(1 − e−αw) is close to 1 − r. (2) Speed: carriers made in the i layer move by fast drift, so there is no slow diffusion tail. (3) Capacitance: C = εrε0A/w is small because w is wide, which raises the RC bandwidth 1/(2πRLC).
  4. Why reverse bias: (a) it depletes the whole i layer and makes the field strong enough to move carriers at the saturation velocity; (b) it keeps the dark current small (only thermally generated carriers flow); (c) it makes the output current proportional to the light. Forward bias would inject a large diode current of its own and swamp the photocurrent.
  5. The compromise: a wider i layer raises η and lowers C, but the transit time ttr = w/vsat grows, so the transit-time bandwidth ftr ≈ 0.44/ttr falls. The designer picks w so that both bandwidth limits are above what the bit rate needs.
  6. Check with numbers (the InGaAs PIN of the chapter): w = 4 µm, α = 10⁴ cm⁻¹, r = 0.03 give αw = 4 and η = 0.97 × (1 − e−4) = 0.952; ttr = 4 × 10⁻⁶/6.5 × 10⁴ = 61.5 ps, so ftr = 7.15 GHz; C = 0.308 pF with 50 Ω gives fRC = 10.35 GHz; together about 5.9 GHz.
Understand Explain impact ionisation in an avalanche photodiode. Define the multiplication factor M, the ionisation ratio k and the excess noise factor F, and say why a small k is wanted. 8 marks

Points to cover. strong field near breakdown accelerates carriers to ionising energy; each ionising collision creates a new pair, giving a chain; M = IM/Ip; M ≈ 1/[1 − (V/VB)n] rises steeply near breakdown; k = hole rate/electron rate; F = kM + (1 − k)(2 − 1/M); small k: avalanche carried by one carrier type, low F; typical M = 10 to 100, limited by noise and breakdown.

Model answer.

  1. Impact ionisation: in the gain region of an APD the reverse bias makes the field close to the breakdown field. A carrier accelerated there gains, between collisions with the lattice, more energy than the bandgap. When it collides it can lift a valence electron into the conduction band, creating a new electron–hole pair. The new carriers are accelerated too and can ionise again, so one primary pair triggers an avalanche.
  2. In a reach-through APD (n⁺–p–π–p⁺) light is absorbed in the wide, lightly doped π layer at moderate field, and the electrons drift into the thin p layer next to the n⁺ contact, where the field is highest and multiplication happens. In an InGaAs/InP SAM APD, InGaAs absorbs and InP multiplies.

    M = IM/Ip
M ≈ 1/[1 − (V/VB)n]   (empirical, n ≈ 3 to 6)

Understand State the two main noise currents of an optical receiver and give their mean-square expressions. For a PIN with Ip = 1 µA, say which dominates at 100 Mb/s (RL = 1 kΩ, B = 70 MHz) and at 2.5 Gb/s (RL = 200 Ω, B = 1.75 GHz). 8 marks

Points to cover. shot noise ish² = 2q(Ip + Id)B, from random carrier arrival; thermal noise ith² = 4kBTB/RL, from the load resistor; both mean-square currents in A² within bandwidth B; powers add; ratio ish²/ith² = qIpRL/(2kBT), independent of B; thermal noise dominates in both cases, more so at 2.5 Gb/s where RL must be small; dark current adds to shot noise; an APD makes shot noise large.

Model answer.

  1. Shot noise: the photocurrent and dark current are streams of separate carriers arriving at random times (Poisson statistics). Within the receiver bandwidth B the mean-square fluctuation is ish² = 2q(Ip + Id)B, where q = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, Ip is the photocurrent and Id the dark current. For an APD it becomes 2q(Ip + Idb)M²FB + 2qIdsB.
  2. Thermal (Johnson) noise: random thermal motion of electrons in the load resistor RL gives ith² = 4kBTB/RL, with kB = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K and T in kelvin. It does not depend on the light. An amplifier noise figure multiplies it.
  3. The two are independent, so their mean squares add: σ² = ish² + ith², and SNR = Ip²/σ².
  4. Which dominates: the ratio is ish²/ith² = 2qIpB/(4kBTB/RL) = qIpRL/(2kBT). B cancels, so the answer depends on IpRL: shot noise wins only when IpRL > 2kBT/q = 51.7 mV at 300 K.

Front end and detector: InGaAs/InP SAM APD (InGaAs absorbs at 1.55 µm, InP multiplies) with η giving R = 0.9 A/W, feeding the transimpedance amplifier (its bandwidth A/(2πRfCT) must cover about 1.75 GHz). A bias supply of a few tens of volts, temperature-compensated or in a gain-control loop, holds M near 10 because VB rises with temperature.
  9. Effects to check: dark current (the bulk part is multiplied and adds shot noise 2qIdbM²FB; it is negligible only while Idb is far below the average primary photocurrent at sensitivity, 0.9 × 183 nW = 165 nA, which a good InGaAs APD with a few nA meets); dispersion at 1.55 µm over 120 km at 2.5 Gbit/s (standard fiber disperses about 17 ps/(nm·km), so a narrow-linewidth laser is needed and a dispersion penalty must come out of the margin); and the extinction ratio of the transmitter (rex = 0.1 costs about 0.9 dB with thermal noise).
  10. Against the alternatives: the PIN is cheaper and simpler but cannot close this budget. An optical preamplifier (an EDFA) in front of a PIN would give more sensitivity than the APD but costs more and adds its own noise. The quantum limit, about 13.5 photons per bit on average at 10⁻¹² (4.3 nW, −53.6 dBm), shows how far any real receiver is from the ideal: the APD design is 16 dB above it.
  11. Conclusion: use an InGaAs/InP APD with a transimpedance front end at M ≈ 10, sensitivity −37.4 dBm, giving 2.4 dB beyond the 3 dB system margin. Risk: if the temperature control fails and M falls to 5, the sensitivity drops to −35.7 dBm and the spare margin shrinks to 0.7 dB.

Glossary

Avalanche photodiode
A photodiode biased near breakdown so that each photon-generated pair triggers a chain of impact ionisations, giving internal gain.
Bit error rate
The probability that a bit is decided wrongly; errors per second = BER × bit rate.
Cutoff wavelength
The longest wavelength a material can absorb, λc = 1.24/Eg µm with Eg in eV.
Dark current
The small current that flows in a photodiode with no light, from carriers generated by heat in the bulk and leakage along the surface.
dBm
Optical power relative to 1 mW: P(dBm) = 10 log₁₀(P/1 mW).
Decision circuit
The comparator that samples the filtered signal once per bit and outputs a 1 or a 0.
Depletion region
The zone of a reverse-biased junction that holds almost no free carriers but a strong electric field.
Equaliser
A filter that corrects the receiver's frequency response so the pulses reach the decision circuit with the right shape.
Excess noise factor
The factor F ≥ 1 by which the random avalanche raises the shot noise above that of a noiseless gain M.
Extinction ratio
The ratio rex = P0/P1 of the power sent for a 0 to the power sent for a 1.
Eye diagram
An overlay of many received bit periods on an oscilloscope, used to judge noise, jitter and intersymbol interference.
Impact ionisation
The process in which a fast carrier in a strong field knocks a bound electron free, creating an extra electron–hole pair.
Ionisation ratio
The ratio k of hole to electron ionisation rates; a small k gives a quieter avalanche.
Multiplication factor
The ratio M of the multiplied output current of an APD to the primary photocurrent.
Noise equivalent power
The optical power that gives a signal equal to the noise (SNR = 1) in a 1 Hz bandwidth, in W/√Hz.
Photodetector
A device that turns incident optical power into an electrical current, usually a reverse-biased semiconductor junction.
Photodiode
A p–n junction run in reverse bias so that light-generated electron–hole pairs are swept out as current.
PIN photodiode
A photodiode with a wide intrinsic layer between p and n regions, giving high efficiency, fast drift collection and low capacitance.
Power penalty
The extra received power, in dB, needed to restore the target BER after an impairment.
Preamplifier
The first amplifier after the photodiode; its noise usually sets the noise of the whole receiver.
Q factor
The ratio (I1 − I0)/(σ1 + σ0) that measures how far apart the two levels are compared with their noise.
Quantum efficiency
The fraction of incident photons that produce a collected electron–hole pair, written η.
Quantum limit
The sensitivity of an ideal noiseless detector: about 10 photons per bit on average for BER = 10⁻⁹.
Receiver sensitivity
The minimum average received optical power that gives the specified BER at the specified bit rate.
Responsivity
The photocurrent produced per unit incident optical power, in amperes per watt.
SAM APD
A separate absorption and multiplication APD: InGaAs absorbs the light and InP multiplies the carriers.
Shot noise
Current noise from the random arrival of individual carriers; its mean square is 2qIB.
Thermal noise
Current noise from random thermal motion of electrons in a resistor; its mean square is 4kBTB/R.
Transimpedance amplifier
A current-to-voltage amplifier with a feedback resistor Rf; it combines low noise with wide bandwidth.
Transit time
The time carriers take to cross the depleted layer, w/vsat; it limits the photodiode bandwidth.
