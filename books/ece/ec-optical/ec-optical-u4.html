4.1 Detection principles: quantum efficiency and responsivity

A photodiode turns light into current at a fixed rate. Quantum efficiency η counts how many incident photons yield collected electrons; responsivity R gives the current in amperes for each watt of light. Both connect an optical power in a fiber to the electrical signal a receiver sees. The idea exists because link design needs one number for the detector: microamperes per microwatt. It is used in specifying PIN and avalanche photodiodes, in receiver sensitivity budgets, and in choosing a wavelength for a given material. Only a few universities teach it as a separate topic; most fold it into the photodiode chapter.

You need Unit 3 on photon energy, bandgap and absorption, since η depends on how strongly the material absorbs at that wavelength.

Photon energy and the photon rate

A photon of wavelength λ carries energy E = hν = hc/λ. Here h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s is Planck's constant, ν is frequency in hertz, c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s is the speed of light, and λ is in metres. At λ = 0.85 µm, E ≈ 2.34 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

Incident power P watts means P/E photons arrive every second. Facet reflection loses a fraction ρ; absorption depth, recombination and slow transit lose more. Quantum efficiency η is electrons collected per second divided by photons incident per second. It is a pure ratio, no units. A good antireflection coating raises η by lowering ρ.

Photons with energy below the bandgap E_g are not absorbed. So η falls to zero beyond the cutoff wavelength λ_c = 1.24/E_g, with λ_c in micrometres and E_g in electronvolts. This cutoff is a material property, not a circuit adjustment.

Responsivity: amperes per watt

The primary photocurrent is set by how many collected electrons cross the junction each second. Responsivity is that current divided by the incident optical power.

I_ph = ηqP/hν = ηqPλ/(hc);  R = I_ph/P = ηλ/1.24

I_ph is photocurrent in A; η is quantum efficiency (no units); q = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C is the electron charge; P is incident power in W; h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s; ν is frequency in Hz; λ is wavelength in µm for the last form; c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s; R is responsivity in A/W.

Because η ≤ 1, R ≤ λ/1.24 A/W. At 1.3 µm the ceiling is about 1.05 A/W; at 1.55 µm it is about 1.25 A/W. This is a hard ceiling set by photon energy, not by circuit design. Some syllabi treat quantum efficiency and responsivity as two separate marks.

The junction that collects these carriers is described in 4.2; gain in 4.3 multiplies this primary current.

Worked example

Question. A silicon PIN photodiode has η = 0.8 at λ = 0.85 µm and receives P = 1 µW. Find the photon arrival rate, the collected electron rate, the responsivity and the photocurrent.

  1. λ = 0.85 × 10⁻⁶ m. Photon energy E = hc/λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3 × 10⁸)/(0.85 × 10⁻⁶) = 2.34 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  2. Photon rate N_p = P/E = 1 × 10⁻⁶/2.34 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 4.27 × 10¹² s⁻¹.
  3. Collected electron rate N_e = ηN_p = 0.8 × 4.27 × 10¹² = 3.42 × 10¹² s⁻¹.
  4. Photocurrent I_ph = qN_e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 3.42 × 10¹² = 5.48 × 10⁻⁷ A.
  5. Responsivity R = ηλ/1.24 = 0.8 × 0.85/1.24 = 0.548 A/W. Then I_ph = RP = 0.548 × 1 × 10⁻⁶ = 5.48 × 10⁻⁷ A.

Answer. R ≈ 0.548 A/W, photon rate ≈ 4.28 × 10¹² s⁻¹, collected electron rate ≈ 3.42 × 10¹² s⁻¹, and I_ph ≈ 5.48 × 10⁻⁷ A.

Exam traps

  • Using λ in metres in R = ηλ/1.24; the 1.24 shortcut needs λ in micrometres.
  • Treating η as A/W or R as a pure ratio; η has no units, R has A/W.
  • Claiming a better design can push R above λ/1.24; that is the η = 1 ceiling.
  • Assuming η = 1 by ignoring facet reflection and recombination.
  • Using R = ηλ/1.24 past the cutoff; beyond λ_c the material does not absorb, so R = 0.

4.2 PN and PIN photodiodes

A reverse-biased p–n junction turns absorbed light into current: photons make electron–hole pairs, and the junction field sweeps the pairs out before they recombine. A plain PN diode collects only its thin depletion layer, so it is inefficient. Widening that layer into an intrinsic i region gives the PIN photodiode, the standard detector in fiber links from 0.85 µm to 1.55 µm. The collection mechanism, the efficiency–capacitance–speed trade-off, and the numbers a receiver designer uses follow.

You need Chapter 4.1 for η, R and photon energy; Unit 3 helps for absorption in direct-bandgap material.

Reverse bias turns photons into drift current

A PN photodiode is a p–n junction held in reverse bias. The reverse voltage widens the depletion region and strengthens its electric field. A photon with energy hν at least the bandgap Eg is absorbed and creates an electron–hole pair. Inside the depletion region the field separates the pair at once: the electron drifts to the n side, the hole to the p side, and the movement is photocurrent. A pair created in the neutral p or n region has no strong field. It diffuses slowly and often recombines before reaching the junction, so it contributes little or nothing.

Efficiency follows from where absorption happens. A thin PN depletion gives small αw and low η. It also gives high junction capacitance C = εA/w, where ε = εrε0, A is area, and ε0 = 8.85 × 10−12 F/m. Thin w is fast but inefficient.

η = (1 − R)(1 − e−αw)

η is the fraction of incident photons that produce collected carriers; R is the facet reflectivity (fraction reflected); α is the absorption coefficient in m⁻¹; w is the depletion width in m. (1 − R) enters the semiconductor; (1 − e−αw) is absorbed in width w.

The PIN stack widens the collecting region

A PIN photodiode inserts an undoped i layer between p+ and n+ layers. Reverse bias depletes the i layer fully, so the field acts across the whole width. Light enters through a thin p+ layer because it must reach the i layer. An antireflection coating reduces R, often to a few percent. The i width can now be chosen for optics, not set by doping. Typical values are a few micrometres to about ten micrometres, giving large αw and η near 1 after allowing for reflection.

  • Silicon covers 0.85 µm, with responsivity around 0.5–0.6 A/W.
  • Germanium and InGaAs cover 1.3 µm and 1.55 µm.
  • InGaAs at 1.55 µm gives about 0.9 A/W.

A wide i layer lowers C, which helps the RC limit, but it raises transit time. The next section turns that trade-off into two bandwidth formulas.

Two bandwidth limits: transit time and RC

Carriers drift across the i layer at the saturation velocity vsat. The transit time is t = w/vsat. vsat is about 1 × 10⁵ m/s in Si and 6.5 × 10⁴ m/s in InGaAs. The junction capacitance C and load resistor RL form an RC limit. The smaller of the transit-time bandwidth and the RC bandwidth controls the diode.

t = w / v<sub>sat</sub>,   f<sub>t</sub> ≈ 0.4 / t,   C = εA / w,   f<sub>RC</sub> = 1 / (2πR<sub>L</sub>C)

t is transit time in s; w is i-layer width in m; vsat is saturation drift velocity in m/s; ft and fRC are bandwidths in Hz; C is capacitance in F; ε = εrε0; A is area in m²; RL is load resistance in Ω.

Design is a push–pull. Increasing w raises η and lowers C, so fRC improves; but t rises, so ft falls. Increasing A raises C and lowers fRC. A small area helps speed but makes fiber alignment harder. Reverse bias must be high enough to deplete the i layer and keep the field near saturation. For a high-speed receiver, choose w so ft and fRC are both above the bit rate, then accept the resulting η. Avalanche gain can raise sensitivity later (see Chapter 4.3).

Worked example

Question. An InGaAs PIN photodiode for 1.55 µm has i-layer width w = 4 µm, absorption coefficient α = 1 × 10⁴ cm⁻¹, facet reflectivity R = 0.03, area A = 1 × 10⁻⁸ m², relative permittivity εr = 13.9, and load resistance RL = 50 Ω. The saturation drift velocity is 6.5 × 10⁴ m/s. Find η, the transit time, the transit-time bandwidth, the junction capacitance, and the RC bandwidth. Which limit controls?

  1. Convert w to cm for the αw product: w = 4 µm = 4 × 10⁻⁴ cm. Then αw = (1 × 10⁴ cm⁻¹)(4 × 10⁻⁴ cm) = 4.0, dimensionless.
  2. Absorbed fraction in the i layer = 1 − e−4 = 1 − 0.0183 = 0.9817.
  3. η = (1 − R)(1 − e−αw) = (1 − 0.03)(0.9817) = 0.97 × 0.9817 = 0.952.
  4. Transit time t = w/vsat = (4 × 10⁻⁶ m)/(6.5 × 10⁴ m/s) = 6.154 × 10⁻¹¹ s = 61.54 ps.
  5. Transit-time bandwidth ft ≈ 0.4/t = 0.4/(6.154 × 10⁻¹¹ s) = 6.50 × 10⁹ Hz = 6.50 GHz.
  6. Capacitance C = εrε0A/w = (13.9 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F/m × 1 × 10⁻⁸ m²)/(4 × 10⁻⁶ m) = 3.075 × 10⁻¹³ F ≈ 0.308 pF.
  7. RC bandwidth fRC = 1/(2πRLC) = 1/(2π × 50 Ω × 3.075 × 10⁻¹³ F) = 1.035 × 10¹⁰ Hz = 10.35 GHz.
  8. The smaller bandwidth is ft = 6.50 GHz, so transit time limits this diode.

Answer. η = 0.952, t = 61.54 ps, ft = 6.50 GHz, C = 0.308 pF, fRC = 10.35 GHz; the diode is transit-time limited.

Exam traps

  • A thicker i layer is not automatically better: it raises η but increases transit time and lowers ft.
  • Do not drop (1 − R). A 3% reflection loses 3% of the incident power before absorption.
  • Convert units before using αw: α in m⁻¹ with w in m, or α in cm⁻¹ with w in cm. Mixing cm and m gives a wrong exponent.
  • The RC limit is 1/(2πRLC), not 0.4/t. Check both and take the smaller.
  • A photodiode works in reverse bias; forward bias injects current and destroys photodetection.

4.3 Avalanche photodiodes

An avalanche photodiode (APD) gives an optical receiver internal current gain. One photon still creates one primary electron–hole pair, but a high-field region near breakdown makes each carrier knock out more pairs by impact ionisation. The gain M raises the signal current, yet the multiplication is random and adds excess noise. APDs are used where the received light is weak and every extra ampere before the amplifier helps, such as long-haul links and some optical time-domain reflectometers.

You need Chapter 4.2 for the primary photocurrent and the absorption region, and Unit 3 for carrier behaviour in a strongly reverse-biased junction.

Gain from impact ionisation

Impact ionisation gives the APD its gain. A photon absorbed in the depletion region creates one electron–hole pair, the primary pair. In a PIN this pair gives at most one electron of external current. In an APD the pair drifts into a narrow high-field region held just below breakdown. A carrier there gains enough energy between collisions to knock a bound electron into the conduction band, creating a new electron–hole pair.

Each new carrier can repeat the process. One primary pair therefore produces a cascade, and the current is multiplied. The gain is M = I_M / I_ph, where I_M is the multiplied current and I_ph is the primary photocurrent before multiplication. A typical APD has M from 10 to 100.

M = 1 / (1 − (V / V_B)^n)

M is the avalanche gain; V is the reverse bias; V_B is the breakdown voltage at which M would diverge; n is a fitting number, usually about 3 to 6. At V well below V_B, M stays near 1; as V approaches V_B, M rises sharply.

Excess noise and the optimum gain

Multiplication is random. Not every primary carrier creates the same number of secondary pairs, so the multiplied current fluctuates beyond ordinary shot noise. The extra fluctuation is described by the excess noise factor F(M). For an APD with electron ionisation rate α and hole ionisation rate β, k = β/α is the ratio of hole to electron ionisation rates.

F(M) = kM + (2 − 1/M)(1 − k)

F(M) is the excess noise factor; M is the gain; k is the hole-to-electron ionisation-rate ratio. Low k is good. If only electrons ionise, k = 0 and F ≈ 2 − 1/M, the lowest possible. If both carriers ionise equally, k = 1 and F = M, the worst case.

With primary photocurrent I_ph, the multiplied signal current is M I_ph. Its mean-square shot noise in bandwidth B is <i²>_shot = 2q I_ph M² F(M) B, where q = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C is the electron charge. At low M, receiver thermal noise dominates and signal power grows as M², so SNR rises. At high M, multiplied shot noise dominates; signal and shot noise both carry M², and F grows with M, so SNR falls. The best M balances the two.

Materials, structure and limits

Silicon APDs have low k and low excess noise. They work well at 0.85 µm, a wavelength silicon absorbs. InGaAs absorbs at 1.3–1.55 µm, the low-loss windows, but its avalanche region has poor noise. The separate absorption and multiplication (SAM) structure solves this: an InGaAs layer absorbs the light, and an InP layer next to it provides the high-field multiplication.

An APD needs a bias far higher than a PIN, often tens of volts, and close to breakdown. Breakdown voltage and gain change with temperature, so a receiver using an APD needs temperature control or a bias-control loop. Speed is limited by the time the avalanche takes to build up. The gain-bandwidth product is roughly fixed: high M comes with lower bandwidth.

Worked example

Question. An APD has quantum efficiency η = 0.8 at wavelength λ = 1.3 µm, gain M = 10, and ionisation ratio k = 0.3. It receives optical power P = 100 nW in a bandwidth B = 1 GHz. Find the multiplied current, the excess noise factor and the SNR. Use q = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

  1. Responsivity: R = ηλ / 1.24 = 0.8 × 1.3 / 1.24 = 0.839 A/W, where 1.24 is the product hc/q in µm·V.
  2. Primary photocurrent: I_ph = R P = 0.839 × 100 × 10⁻⁹ = 83.9 nA.
  3. Multiplied current: I_M = M I_ph = 10 × 83.9 nA = 839 nA = 0.839 µA.
  4. Excess noise factor: F = kM + (2 − 1/M)(1 − k) = 0.3 × 10 + (2 − 0.1)(0.7) = 3 + 1.33 = 4.33.
  5. Mean-square shot noise: <i²> = 2q I_ph M² F B = 2 × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 83.9 × 10⁻⁹ × 100 × 4.33 × 10⁹ = 1.16 × 10⁻¹⁴ A².
  6. Rms noise current: √(1.16 × 10⁻¹⁴) = 1.08 × 10⁻⁷ A = 108 nA.
  7. SNR = (0.839 × 10⁻⁶)² / (1.16 × 10⁻¹⁴) = 60.7. In decibels, 10 log₁₀(60.7) = 17.8 dB.

Answer. The multiplied current is 0.839 µA, the excess noise factor is 4.33, and the SNR is 60.7, or 17.8 dB.

Exam traps

  • Multiplying only the signal and forgetting the excess noise: the shot noise grows as M²F, not as M².
  • Saying a bigger M always helps. Past the optimum M, the added excess noise beats the added signal gain.
  • Confusing k with the gain: k is the ratio of hole to electron ionisation rates, not the multiplication factor.
  • Treating an APD bias like a PIN bias: an APD sits close to breakdown and needs temperature control or bias control.
  • Using F = M for every APD. F = M is the worst case k = 1; silicon has small k and much lower F.

Analyze Explain why an APD has an optimum gain rather than a gain that is always better when larger. 4 marks

Apply If the bias of an APD is increased so that M doubles, and F is unchanged, what happens to the signal power and the shot-noise power? 2 marks

The signal current doubles, so signal power becomes four times larger. The shot-noise mean square also becomes four times larger because it contains M².

Analyze Why does an APD receiver need temperature control or a bias-control loop? 3 marks

Breakdown voltage and gain change with temperature. A fixed bias can then move M away from its optimum or too close to breakdown, so the receiver controls temperature or adjusts the bias.

The avalanche takes time to build up. When M is made larger, the build-up time increases and the bandwidth falls, so the product of gain and bandwidth is roughly fixed.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Modern APD work includes low-noise InP and antimony-based materials, APD arrays for imaging, and single-photon avalanche diodes (SPADs) used in lidar and quantum optics. 4.4 Photodetector noise

Photodetector noise sets the smallest current a receiver can trust. Shot noise comes from random carrier arrival. Thermal noise comes from the load resistor. Shot noise follows photocurrent plus dark current; thermal noise follows resistance and temperature. Add noise powers, not rms currents. The signal-to-noise ratio tells whether the receiver is shot-limited or thermal-limited. Only a few universities teach this as a stand-alone topic.

You need Chapter 4.1 for responsivity, Chapter 4.2 for photocurrent, and Chapter 4.3 for APD gain and excess noise factor.

Shot noise and thermal noise

Shot noise comes from random arrival times. A 1 µA photocurrent is about 6 × 10¹² electrons per second, each crossing unpredictably. Carriers obey Poisson statistics because each arrives independently. Shot noise grows with current and bandwidth.

<i>i</i><sub>sh</sub><sup>2</sup> = 2q(I<sub>ph</sub> + I<sub>d</sub>)B

q = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C is electron charge; Iph is photocurrent in A; Id is dark current in A; B is post-detection bandwidth in Hz. For an APD, photocurrent and bulk dark current carry M²F; surface dark current adds directly. M is gain, F is excess noise factor.

Thermal noise is the load resistor's current jitter. It exists with no light; it is set by the resistor and amplifier input. For a PIN, put M = 1 and F = 1 in the shot formula. B is post-detection bandwidth, not optical frequency.

<i>i</i><sub>th</sub><sup>2</sup> = 4kTB/R<sub>L</sub>

k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K; T is temperature in K; RL is load resistance in Ω.

Adding noise and the signal-to-noise ratio

Noise currents are independent, so powers add: σ² = ish2 + ith2. Here σ² is total mean-square noise current in A²; σ is rms in A. Add powers, not rms currents: 5.06 nA and 40.7 nA give about 41 nA because 40.7² dominates.

SNR = I<sub>ph</sub>²/σ²,   SNR(dB) = 10 log₁₀(SNR)

Iph is photocurrent in A; σ² is total mean-square noise current in A².

If thermal noise dominates, SNR ≈ Iph²RL/(4kTB). Since Iph ∝ P, SNR rises as P², or 20 dB per decade. If shot noise dominates, SNR ≈ Iph/(2qB), so it rises as P, or 10 dB per decade.

Dark current has a bulk part, proportional to depleted volume, and a surface leakage part. Only the bulk part is multiplied by M in an APD; the surface part adds directly. Write Id = M·Idb + Ids, with Idb the bulk part in A, Ids the surface part in A, and M the gain. Low-frequency 1/f noise appears below a few kHz and is small in a good diode. A larger RL lowers thermal noise but raises the RLC time constant, so bandwidth falls.

NEP = σrms/R

σrms is rms noise current per unit bandwidth in A/√Hz; R is responsivity in A/W; NEP is in W/√Hz, the optical power giving SNR = 1 in a 1 Hz bandwidth.

Worked example

Question. A PIN receiver has responsivity R = 0.8 A/W, received optical power P = 1 µW, dark current Id = 1 nA, post-detection bandwidth B = 100 MHz, load RL = 1 kΩ at T = 300 K. Find the shot-noise rms current, thermal-noise rms current, total rms noise, SNR and SNR in dB.

  1. Iph = RP = 0.8 × 10⁻⁶ = 8.00 × 10⁻⁷ A; Iph + Id = 8.01 × 10⁻⁷ A.
  2. Shot: ish² = 2(1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)(8.01 × 10⁻⁷)(1.00 × 10⁸) = 2.56 × 10⁻¹⁷ A²; rms = 5.06 nA.
  3. Thermal: ith² = 4(1.38 × 10⁻²³)(300)(1.00 × 10⁸)/1000 = 1.66 × 10⁻¹⁵ A²; rms = 40.7 nA.
  4. Total σ² = 1.68 × 10⁻¹⁵ A²; rms = 41.0 nA. SNR = (8.00 × 10⁻⁷)²/1.68 × 10⁻¹⁵ = 381; SNR(dB) = 25.8 dB.

Answer. Shot noise 5.06 nA rms, thermal 40.7 nA rms, total 41.0 nA rms, SNR 381 or 25.8 dB. The receiver is thermal-noise limited.

Exam traps

  • Adding rms noise currents. Add mean-square powers, then take one square root.
  • Using Iph in shot noise and forgetting Id.
  • For an APD, putting M or M²F in the wrong place. Signal current is M Iph; shot-noise power carries M²F for photocurrent and bulk dark current only.
  • Treating a bigger RL as free. It lowers thermal noise but raises RLC and narrows B.
  • Quoting SNR in dB as 20 log₁₀ instead of 10 log₁₀ of a power ratio.

4.5 Optical receiver structure and sensitivity

An optical receiver turns a weak, noisy pulse train of light into clean digital bits. Every bit period it must decide whether the light was on or off, and it must do so at a stated error rate using as little power as possible. The chain is fixed: photodiode, low-noise preamplifier, main amplifier and equaliser, low-pass filter, and a decision circuit clocked by a recovered timing signal. Its key specification is sensitivity, the weakest average power that still meets the target bit error rate. Every link budget in Unit 5 starts from this number.

You need: chapter 4.1 for responsivity and chapter 4.4 for the noise current σ.

The chain from photon to bit

A receiver does four jobs in order: convert light to current, amplify that current with as little added noise as possible, shape the pulses so each bit sits in its own time slot, and decide one or zero at the centre of each slot.

  1. Photodiode: absorbs the light and delivers a current I = R·P, where R is the responsivity in A/W and P is the received optical power in W.
  2. Preamplifier: a low-noise current-to-voltage stage. Its noise dominates the whole receiver.
  3. Main amplifier and equaliser: raises the signal to a few volts and compensates pulse spreading from the fiber and the front end.
  4. Low-pass filter: removes out-of-band noise. Its bandwidth is set near the bit rate so noise is limited without smearing the pulses.
  5. Decision circuit: a comparator that samples the filtered signal at t = nT, where T = 1/B is the bit period, B is the bit rate and n is the bit index. It outputs one if the sample is above the threshold.
  6. Timing recovery: extracts a clock at B from the data transitions and feeds it to the decision circuit. Without it the sample point drifts.
  7. AGC: automatic gain control, a loop that holds the output level constant when the received power changes, so the threshold stays valid.

AGC matters because a receiver must work over a dynamic range, the ratio between the strongest and weakest power it accepts. Above the upper limit the amplifiers overload: pulses flatten and the receiver fails on strong signals, not weak ones.

Three front ends

The preamplifier choice fixes both bandwidth and noise.

Front endBandwidthNoiseComment
Low impedanceWide, set by R_LHigh, about 4kT/R_LSimple, used in fast short links
High impedanceNarrow, about 1/(2πR_L C)Low, R_L is largeNeeds equalisation; poor dynamic range
TransimpedanceAbout 1/(2πR_f C)Low, gain set by R_fThe standard choice

In a transimpedance amplifier a resistor R_f sits in the feedback path of an inverting amplifier. The output is V = −I·R_f, so the transresistance equals R_f in ohms. A larger R_f raises the output and lowers the input-referred thermal noise current, which falls as √(4kT/R_f). The price is bandwidth.

B ≈ 1 / (2π R_f C)

B is the closed-loop bandwidth in Hz; R_f is the feedback resistance in Ω; C is the total input capacitance in F, from photodiode and amplifier.

Doubling R_f halves the noise current but halves the bandwidth. That trade-off is the whole design problem of the front end.

Sensitivity

Sensitivity is the minimum average received optical power needed to reach a stated bit error rate at a stated bit rate. It is meaningless without both numbers attached.

P_dBm = 10 log₁₀(P / 1 mW)

P is the average optical power in W; P_dBm is the same power in decibel-milliwatts.

For a PIN receiver limited by thermal noise, with equal rms noise σ on a one and on a zero, the two levels must be separated by 2Qσ. The signal photocurrent is then about Qσ, giving the average power below.

P ≈ Q σ / R

P is the average received power in W; Q is the factor defined in chapter 4.6, about 6 at BER 10⁻⁹; σ is the rms noise current in A; R is the responsivity in A/W.

Practical PIN receivers reach about −25 to −40 dBm at a few hundred Mbit/s. An APD adds gain M before the preamplifier: the signal rises M times while the preamplifier noise stays fixed, buying roughly 5 to 10 dB. The gain needs a bias supply of tens of volts and a control loop against temperature drift, so an APD receiver costs more.

Worked example

Question. A PIN receiver has responsivity R = 0.9 A/W and a total input noise current σ = 40 nA rms. It must reach BER 10⁻⁹, which needs Q = 6. (a) Find the sensitivity in watts and in dBm. (b) If the fiber and connectors lose 30 dB, what average power must the transmitter launch?

  1. Write the thermal-noise-limited sensitivity: P = Qσ/R.
  2. Substitute: P = 6 × 40 × 10⁻⁹ / 0.9 = 2.67 × 10⁻⁷ W.
  3. Convert to nanawatts: 2.67 × 10⁻⁷ W = 267 nW.
  4. Convert to dBm: 10 log₁₀(267 × 10⁻⁹ / 1 × 10⁻³) = 10 log₁₀(2.67 × 10⁻⁴) = −35.7 dBm.
  5. Add the loss: launch power = −35.7 dBm + 30 dB = −5.7 dBm.

Answer. The sensitivity is 2.67 × 10⁻⁷ W (267 nW, −35.7 dBm), so a link with 30 dB of loss needs at least −5.7 dBm launched.

Exam traps

  • Quoting a sensitivity figure without the bit rate and the BER it was measured at. The same receiver is far less sensitive at a higher rate.
  • Mixing peak and average power. The formulas use average power; some datasheets quote peak.
  • Losing track of the dBm reference. 0 dBm is 1 mW, so −30 dBm is 1 µW, and every 10 dB is a factor of ten in power.
  • Assuming APD gain always helps. Past the optimum M the multiplied shot noise, which grows as M²F, outweighs the gain and sensitivity falls.
  • Adding noise currents directly. Independent noise currents add as squares: σ_total² = σ₁² + σ₂².

4.6 Bit error rate and receiver performance

Every optical receiver guesses each bit. Noise makes the current sample wander, so a 1 can fall below the decision threshold or a 0 can rise above it. Bit error rate (BER) is the probability of a wrong guess. One number, Q, captures the competition between signal separation and noise. Q turns into BER, maps onto the eye diagram, and fixes the power penalty a degraded receiver needs to reach the target BER. Only a few universities teach this as a separate advanced topic.

You need: Chapter 4.4 for noise σ and Chapter 4.5 for receiver sensitivity.

Signal, noise, and one number Q

At the sampling instant the receiver holds a current. A 1 bit has mean current I₁; a 0 bit has mean current I₀. Both are in amperes. Their rms noise currents are σ₁ and σ₀. The decision threshold I_D sits between them. If σ₁ = σ₀, the best I_D is halfway. With unequal noises it moves away from the noisier level.

Q is the separation of the means in units of combined noise:

Q = (I₁ − I₀)/(σ₁ + σ₀)

I₁ is the mean current for a 1 bit, I₀ for a 0 bit, σ₁ and σ₀ are their rms noise currents, all in amperes; Q is dimensionless.

Why the sum? The 1 must stay above I_D by Qσ₁, and the 0 below it by Qσ₀. The gap between means covers both margins, so the margins add. Large Q means a wide eye compared with noise. The same Q describes both error directions because the threshold makes their Q values equal.

BER from Q, and the eye diagram

For Gaussian noise the two error probabilities add to

BER = ½ erfc(Q/√2) ≈ e^(−Q²/2)/(√(2π) Q)

BER is the average fraction of wrong bits, erfc is the complementary error function, Q is from the previous section; the approximation is good for Q above about 3.

Anchor points: Q = 6 gives BER ≈ 1.0×10⁻⁹; Q = 7 gives ≈ 1.3×10⁻¹²; Q = 4.5 is in the 10⁻⁶ decade. Multiply BER by bit rate to get errors per second. At 10 Gbit/s, BER 10⁻⁹ means about 10 errors per second.

The eye diagram is the same information in time. It overlays many bit periods. At the sampling instant the eye opening equals I₁−I₀, the separation of the two mean levels. For a given BER, the margins Qσ₁ below the 1 level and Qσ₀ above the 0 level must fit inside that opening; noise or interference that eats the opening raises BER. Timing jitter moves the sampling point; intersymbol interference from dispersion or a slow receiver fills the opening. Both close the eye and raise BER. A closed eye means errors; an open eye means margin.

Power penalty and non-ideal effects

To compare receivers ask how much more optical power restores the target BER. Power penalty in dB is

10 log₁₀(P₂/P₁)

P₁ is the optical power at the first BER and P₂ at the second; both in watts or both in the same unit.

If thermal noise is fixed, Q ∝ P, so penalty = 10 log₁₀(Q₂/Q₁). If shot noise dominates, σ ∝ √P, so Q ∝ √P and penalty = 20 log₁₀(Q₂/Q₁). State the noise regime.

A finite extinction ratio r = P₀/P₁ leaves light in the zero. Then I₀ rises, the eye shrinks, and with fixed noise the penalty is about 10 log₁₀((1+r)/(1−r)) dB. A BER floor appears when the disturbance is not Gaussian or not reduced by more signal power—interference, modal noise, or pattern-dependent jitter—so the curve flattens and extra Q does not help. APD excess noise raises σ₁ and σ₀ and shifts the threshold; it changes the slope of the BER curve, not the floor. Link-level jitter and dispersion penalties belong to Unit 5. Quote bit rate and extinction ratio with any sensitivity. Otherwise the number is not comparable.

Worked example

Question. A receiver has I₁=1.0 µA, I₀=0.10 µA, σ₁=σ₀=0.075 µA. Treat this first part as fixed (thermal) noise with equal σ₁=σ₀. Find Q and BER. Now suppose the same link is shot-limited, so σ ∝ √P, and it starts at Q=6.0. Find Q for BER 10⁻¹² and the power penalty. Compare with the fixed-noise penalty.

  1. I₁−I₀ = 0.90 µA; σ₁+σ₀ = 0.150 µA.
  2. Q = 0.90/0.150 = 6.0.
  3. BER = ½ erfc(6/√2) ≈ 1.0×10⁻⁹.
  4. With true shot noise σ ∝ √I, σ₁/σ₀ = √(I₁/I₀) = √10 ≈ 3.2; then Q must be recomputed from the actual σ values. The penalty below uses the measured starting Q=6.0.
  5. Solve ½ erfc(Q₂/√2)=10⁻¹²; Q₂=7.03.
  6. Shot-limited: P₂/P₁=(7.03/6.0)²=1.373; penalty=10 log₁₀1.373=1.38 dB.
  7. Fixed noise: P₂/P₁=7.03/6.0=1.172; penalty=10 log₁₀1.172=0.69 dB.

Answer. Q=6.0, BER≈1.0×10⁻⁹, Q₂=7.03; penalty is 1.38 dB shot-limited and 0.69 dB with fixed noise.

Exam traps

  • Using only σ₁ or only σ₀ in Q. Q uses σ₁+σ₀.
  • Quoting BER without bit rate. Errors per second = BER × bit rate.
  • Using 20 log₁₀(Q₂/Q₁) when noise is fixed. Fixed thermal noise gives 10 log₁₀; shot-limited noise gives 20 log₁₀.
  • Forgetting that APD noise depends on signal, so σ₁ and σ₀ differ and the threshold is not halfway.
  • Confusing extinction ratio r with ER in dB; use linear r=P₀/P₁ in the penalty formula.

Takeaways

  1. A photodiode is a current source driven by light, and R = ηqλ/(hc) turns optical power into amperes.
  2. Quantum efficiency needs a thick absorbing layer, but transit time needs a thin one, so every detector design is a trade-off between η and bandwidth.
  3. A PIN puts a wide intrinsic layer in the junction so both goals are partly met at low capacitance.
  4. An APD buys sensitivity with gain: signal rises as M, shot noise as M²F, so an optimum M exists and it is well below the maximum gain.
  5. Silicon APDs are quiet (k of a few percent) but blind beyond about 1.1 μm; InGaAs APDs work at 1.3 and 1.55 μm with much noisier gain.
  6. Shot noise and thermal noise set the receiver floor; below roughly 1 Gb/s the resistor dominates, above it the shot noise of the gain does.
  7. Sensitivity is quoted as the average power for a target BER, so it always travels with a bit rate and a wavelength.
  8. BER is a probability, and Q = (I₁ − I₀)/(σ₁ + σ₀) is the single number that fixes it: Q = 6 gives about 10⁻⁹, Q = 7 gives about 10⁻¹².
  9. An APD receiver beats a PIN receiver by roughly 5 to 10 dB of sensitivity, but only if the gain is set near its optimum.

Glossary

Avalanche photodiode
A photodiode biased near breakdown so that each photon-generated pair triggers a chain of impact ionisations, giving internal gain.
Bit error rate
The average fraction of bits decided wrongly; it is a probability, not a count.
Dark current
The small current that flows in a photodiode with no light on it, caused by thermal generation and leakage.
dBm
Optical power expressed relative to 1 mW: P(dBm) = 10 log₁₀(P/1 mW).
Decision circuit
The comparator that samples the filtered signal at the middle of each bit and outputs a 1 or a 0.
Depletion region
The zone around a reverse-biased junction that holds almost no free carriers but a strong electric field.
Equaliser
A filter that flattens the receiver response so that pulses arrive as narrow, non-overlapping shapes at the decision circuit.
Excess noise factor F
A number greater than one that expresses how much extra shot noise the random avalanche gain process adds.
Eye diagram
An overlay of many received bit periods on an oscilloscope, used to judge margin, jitter and noise.
Impact ionisation
The process in which a fast carrier in a strong field knocks a bound electron free, creating an extra electron–hole pair.
Ionisation ratio k
The ratio of hole to electron ionisation rates; a small k means the avalanche is dominated by one carrier type and is quieter.
Multiplication factor M
The ratio of the multiplied output current of an APD to the primary photocurrent before gain.
Noise equivalent power
The optical power that produces a photocurrent equal to the detector's own noise current in a 1 Hz bandwidth.
Photodetector
A device that turns incident optical power into an electrical current, usually a reverse-biased semiconductor junction.
Photodiode
A p–n junction run in reverse bias so that light-generated electron–hole pairs are swept out as current.
PIN photodiode
A photodiode with a wide intrinsic layer between p and n regions, giving high efficiency and low capacitance.
Preamplifier
The first amplifier after the photodiode; its noise adds directly to the signal and so sets the receiver floor.
Q factor
The ratio (I₁ − I₀)/(σ₁ + σ₀) that measures how far the two signal levels are apart compared with their noise.
Quantum efficiency
The fraction of incident photons that produce a collected electron–hole pair, usually written η.
Receiver sensitivity
The minimum average received optical power that gives the specified bit error rate at the specified bit rate.
Responsivity
The photocurrent produced per unit incident optical power, in amperes per watt.
Shot noise
Current noise caused by the discrete, randomly timed arrival of charge carriers; its mean square is 2qIB.
Thermal noise
Voltage or current noise from the random thermal motion of electrons in a resistor; its current mean square is 4kTB/R.
Transimpedance amplifier
A current-to-voltage amplifier whose feedback resistor sets the gain and, with the diode capacitance, the bandwidth.
