5.1 Initial and final conditions in RL, RC, RLC circuits

A switch closes at t = 0. The inductor current and the capacitor voltage cannot change in zero time, because that would need a jump in the stored energy ½Li² or ½Cv², which needs infinite power. So the state just before switching, at t = 0⁻, fixes the state just after, at t = 0⁺. Everything after that is an ordinary resistive circuit problem. This one idea lets you start any transient problem correctly — from the inductor in a phone charger to the capacitor bank that holds up a router through a Chennai power cut.

You need: the V-I relations of R, L and C, and KVL and KCL, from Unit 1, Foundations: Circuit Elements and Laws.

Nothing stored can change instantly

Three instants matter. At t = 0⁻ the circuit is still in its old state. At t = 0 the switch moves. At t = 0⁺ the new circuit has just started. The minus and plus signs are not arithmetic; they mean the left limit and the right limit of time around the switching instant.

An inductor stores energy ½Li_L², where L is the inductance in henries and i_L is the current through it in amperes. A jump in i_L would change that energy in zero time, and changing energy in zero time needs infinite power. No real source can supply that, so the current through an inductor is continuous: i_L(0⁺) = i_L(0⁻).

A capacitor stores ½Cv_C², where C is the capacitance in farads and v_C is the voltage across it in volts. The same argument gives v_C(0⁺) = v_C(0⁻).

Everything else may jump. The voltage across an inductor can change instantly, the current through a capacitor can change instantly, and a resistor has no memory at all — its voltage and current obey Ohm's law at every instant.

i_L(0⁺) = i_L(0⁻) and v_C(0⁺) = v_C(0⁻)

i_L is the inductor current in amperes, positive in the chosen reference direction; v_C is the capacitor voltage in volts, positive at the chosen + terminal; 0⁻ is the instant just before switching and 0⁺ the instant just after.

The equivalent circuit at 0⁺ and at ∞

Once the two numbers i_L(0⁻) and v_C(0⁻) are known, the circuit at t = 0⁺ is a plain resistive problem. Replace the inductor by a current source of value i_L(0⁻), pointing the same way as the original current. Replace the capacitor by a voltage source of value v_C(0⁻), with the same polarity as the original voltage. Solve with KVL, KCL and series-parallel reduction.

For the far end, let time run to infinity with a DC source. The circuit settles. The inductor voltage v_L = L di/dt becomes zero, so the inductor is a short circuit. The capacitor current i_C = C dv/dt becomes zero, so the capacitor is an open circuit. Solve that resistive circuit for i(∞) and v(∞).

  1. Redraw the circuit for t < 0 with the switch in its old position. For DC, the inductor is a short and the capacitor is open. Find i_L(0⁻) and v_C(0⁻).
  2. Carry them across the switch: i_L(0⁺) = i_L(0⁻) and v_C(0⁺) = v_C(0⁻).
  3. Redraw for t = 0⁺ with the inductor as a current source and the capacitor as a voltage source. Find the voltages and currents you need.
  4. Redraw for t → ∞ with the inductor short and the capacitor open. Find the final values.
  5. Use the two end values plus the initial slope to shape the answer (Chapters 5.2 to 5.4).

If nothing was stored before switching, the two rules look like opposites. At t = 0⁺ a 0 A inductor source behaves like an open branch and a 0 V capacitor source behaves like a short. At t → ∞ they swap: inductor short, capacitor open. Keep the two instants apart.

The first slope at 0⁺

The two end values fix the answer, but the first slope tells you how the answer leaves. Take the defining relations v_L = L di_L/dt and i_C = C dv_C/dt and read them at t = 0⁺.

di_L/dt at 0⁺ = v_L(0⁺) / L

di_L/dt is the rate of change of inductor current in amperes per second, v_L(0⁺) is the inductor voltage just after switching in volts, and L is the inductance in henries.

dv_C/dt at 0⁺ = i_C(0⁺) / C

dv_C/dt is the rate of change of capacitor voltage in volts per second, i_C(0⁺) is the capacitor current just after switching in amperes, and C is the capacitance in farads.

You get v_L(0⁺) from KVL on the 0⁺ circuit and i_C(0⁺) from KCL. Neither is continuous, so both must be found fresh after the switch moves. These slopes are the constants that appear when the differential equation is solved — see Chapter 5.3. They let you sketch the answer before doing any algebra, and catch a wrong one.

Worked example

Question. A 12 V DC source, a switch, R = 6 Ω, L = 2 H and C = 0.5 F are in series. Nothing is stored before t = 0 and the switch closes at t = 0. Find i(0⁺), v_C(0⁺), di/dt at 0⁺, i(∞) and v_C(∞).

  1. Before t = 0 the switch is open, so no current flows and no energy is stored: i_L(0⁻) = 0 A and v_C(0⁻) = 0 V.
  2. Carry the state across the switch: i_L(0⁺) = 0 A and v_C(0⁺) = 0 V. All three elements are in series, so the loop current i(0⁺) = i_L(0⁺) = 0 A.
  3. Draw the 0⁺ circuit: the inductor is a 0 A current source (an open branch), the capacitor is a 0 V voltage source (a short). KVL round the loop gives 12 = 6(0) + v_L(0⁺) + 0, so v_L(0⁺) = 12 V.
  4. First slope: di/dt at 0⁺ = v_L(0⁺)/L = 12 V ÷ 2 H = 6 A/s.
  5. Final state: DC source, inductor short, capacitor open. The capacitor blocks the loop, so i(∞) = 0 A.
  6. With no current there is no drop across R or L, so the whole 12 V sits on the capacitor: v_C(∞) = 12 V.

Answer. i(0⁺) = 0 A, v_C(0⁺) = 0 V, di/dt at 0⁺ = 6 A/s, i(∞) = 0 A, v_C(∞) = 12 V.

Exam traps

  • Treating the inductor as an open circuit or the capacitor as a short circuit at t = 0⁺ in every case. In general replace them by a current source i_L(0⁻) and a voltage source v_C(0⁻); they look like an open and a short only when nothing was stored (i_L(0⁻) = 0, v_C(0⁻) = 0). The unconditional short/open rule belongs to t → ∞ and to the DC steady state only.
  • Believing a big source forces i_L or v_C to jump. Only an impulse could do that; a 12 V source gives a finite slope, not a jump.
  • Applying continuity to the wrong variable. Inductor current and capacitor voltage are continuous; inductor voltage and capacitor current are not.
  • Solving the t < 0 circuit with the switch already in its new position, so the stored energy is wrong from the first line.
  • Unit slips: di/dt = v_L/L comes out in A/s only when v_L is in volts and L in henries. Using mH without converting gives a factor of 1000 error.

5.2 Source-free RL and RC circuits; time constant

Unplug a circuit and its stored energy does not vanish at once. An inductor's current and a capacitor's voltage fall through the resistance in a smooth exponential. The speed is set by one number, the time constant τ. This is the source-free natural response: no battery, no generator, only L or C and R. It explains why a fan slows gradually, why a camera flash dims, and why a coil's current collapses in milliseconds. The same maths runs sensor circuits, filters and snubbers.

You need: Unit 1 for the V-I relations of L and C, and Chapter 5.1 for the starting value at t = 0.

The first-order circuit and its equation

Source-free means the independent source is removed or switched out at t = 0. Only the storage element and R remain. In an RL circuit, an inductor L with initial current I₀ is connected across a resistor R. KVL gives v_L + v_R = 0. Use v_L = L di/dt and v_R = R i:

L di/dt + R i = 0 ⇒ di/dt = −(R/L) i

L is inductance in henry (H); R is resistance in ohm (Ω); i is the loop current in ampere (A) at time t in second (s).

The solution is i(t) = I₀ e^(−t/τ) with τ = L/R. I₀ is the current at t = 0 in A. For an RC circuit, a capacitor C charged to V₀ is connected across R. KCL at the node gives C dv/dt + v/R = 0, so RC dv/dt + v = 0. The voltage is v(t) = V₀ e^(−t/τ) with τ = RC. V₀ is the capacitor voltage at t = 0 in V. Both are first-order because one storage element sets the answer. The final value is zero. Some syllabi call this the natural response or free response.

The time constant and the tangent

τ is the time constant, measured in seconds. At t = τ, e^(−1) = 0.368. The current or voltage has fallen to 36.8 % of its starting value. After 2τ it is 13.5 %, after 3τ 5.0 %, and after 5τ 0.67 %. The 5τ rule says the transient is practically zero; it is not exactly zero. The half-life is τ ln 2 ≈ 0.693τ.

Differentiate x(t) = x(0)e^(−t/τ) at t = 0: slope = −x(0)/τ. The tangent line is x = x(0) − [x(0)/τ]t. It meets zero at t = τ. That is the geometric meaning of τ: follow the starting slope and you reach zero one time constant later.

R changes the two circuits in opposite ways. For RL, τ = L/R: a larger R gives a smaller τ, so the current dies faster. More resistance removes more energy per ampere. For RC, τ = RC: a larger R gives a larger τ, so the voltage dies slower. The capacitor drains through a smaller current. This is a favourite comparison question.

Energy goes into the resistor

Stored energy is ½ L I₀² in the inductor and ½ C V₀² in the capacitor. The resistor has no storage; it turns that energy into heat. In RL, p_R = i²R = I₀²R e^(−2t/τ). Integrate from 0 to ∞: total = I₀²R (τ/2) = ½ L I₀². In RC, p_R = v²/R = (V₀²/R) e^(−2t/τ). Total = (V₀²/R)(RC/2) = ½ C V₀². All the initial stored energy ends in R as t → ∞. At t = τ, the fraction burnt is 1 − e^(−2) = 0.865, so 86.5 % is gone and 13.5 % remains. This energy view explains why a relay coil needs a diode: the inductor's ½LI² must go somewhere when the switch opens.

Worked example

Question. An inductor L = 0.5 H carries a steady current I₀ = 2 A. At t = 0 it is connected across a resistor R = 100 Ω. Find τ, i(t), and the time to reach 0.2 A.

  1. This is a source-free RL circuit. The time constant is τ = L/R.
  2. τ = 0.5 H ÷ 100 Ω = 0.005 s = 5 ms.
  3. The current decay is i(t) = I₀ e^(−t/τ) = 2 e^(−t/0.005) = 2 e^(−200t) A. Here 200 s⁻¹ = 1/τ.
  4. Set i(t) = 0.2 A: 0.2 = 2 e^(−200t). Divide by 2: 0.1 = e^(−200t).
  5. Take natural logarithms: ln 0.1 = −200t. So t = −ln 0.1 ÷ 200 = ln 10 ÷ 200.
  6. ln 10 = 2.302585. t = 2.302585 ÷ 200 = 0.0115129 s. Using τ directly: t = τ ln 10 = 0.005 × 2.302585 = 0.0115129 s.

Answer. τ = 0.005 s = 5 ms; i(t) = 2e^(−200t) A; the current is 0.2 A at t = 0.011512925464970229 s ≈ 11.5 ms.

Exam traps

  • Writing τ = R/L for an RL circuit or τ = 1/(RC) for an RC circuit. Check units: τ must come out in seconds.
  • Thinking at t = τ the value is 63.2 % of the start. That is the charging curve; the source-free decay reaches 36.8 %.
  • Treating 5τ as exactly zero. About 0.67 % is left, so an exam may ask for the exact expression.
  • Forgetting that I₀ or V₀ comes from the instant before switching. The inductor current and capacitor voltage cannot jump; see Chapter 5.1.
  • Saying a large R always slows decay. In RL, τ = L/R falls as R rises; in RC, τ = RC rises as R rises.

5.3 Step response of first-order circuits; natural and forced response

Switch a DC source onto a circuit with one capacitor or one inductor and every voltage and current settles along the same exponential curve. The curve is the sum of two parts: a forced part set by the source and a natural part set by the energy already stored. Engineers use this split to read a datasheet, size a delay, or explain why a relay drops out late. This chapter shows the split, the one-line formula that joins the start and end values, and the sketches that follow from it.

You need: Chapter 5.2 for τ and the source-free exponential, and Unit 1 for KVL and KCL.

Complete response = natural + forced

A first-order circuit has one storage element, so after switching its differential equation is first order. Its general solution has two pieces. The forced response (also called particular or steady-state response) is any solution that satisfies the driven equation and follows the source. The natural response (also called homogeneous, complementary or transient response) solves the source set to zero and always has the form A e−t/τ. The complete response is their sum.

For a constant DC source, the forced response is simply the final value x(∞), because after the exponential dies nothing changes. The natural part must carry the difference between the initial value x(0⁺) and that final value. Hence the shortcut:

x(t) = x(∞) + [x(0⁺) − x(∞)] e−t/τ

x(t) is any current or voltage for t ≥ 0⁺; x(∞) is its steady value after the switch; x(0⁺) is its value just after switching; τ is the time constant, τ = ReqC or L/Req, with Req the resistance seen by the storage element with sources killed.

At t = 0⁺ the exponential equals 1, so x(0⁺) = x(∞) + [x(0⁺) − x(∞)]. As t → ∞ it goes to 0, so x → x(∞). The bracket is the initial mismatch. If x(0⁺) = x(∞), the natural part is zero and nothing changes.

RC and RL step responses

For a series RC circuit with a DC voltage Vs switched on at t = 0, KVL gives Vs = R i + vC and i = C dvC/dt. With τ = RC,

v<sub>C</sub>(t) = V<sub>s</sub> + [v<sub>C</sub>(0⁺) − V<sub>s</sub>] e<sup>−t/τ</sup>,   i(t) = [(V<sub>s</sub> − v<sub>C</sub>(0⁺))/R] e<sup>−t/τ</sup>

vC is capacitor voltage; i is series current; Vs is the step voltage; vC(0⁺) is the capacitor voltage just after switching. For an uncharged capacitor, vC = Vs(1 − e−t/τ) and i = (Vs/R)e−t/τ. Capacitor voltage cannot jump, but capacitor current can: it jumps to (Vs − vC(0⁺))/R at t = 0⁺ and decays to 0.

For a series RL circuit driven by Vs, KVL gives Vs = R i + L di/dt. With τ = L/R,

i(t) = I∞ + [i(0⁺) − I∞] e<sup>−t/τ</sup>,   I∞ = V<sub>s</sub>/R

i is inductor current; I∞ is its final value; i(0⁺) is inductor current just after switching. Inductor current cannot jump; inductor voltage vL = L di/dt can, and vL(t) = [Vs − R i(0⁺)] e−t/τ. For zero initial current, i = (Vs/R)(1 − e−t/τ) and vL = Vse−t/τ.

Sketching, slope and pulses

To sketch any first-order answer, mark x(0⁺) and x(∞) on the vertical axis. Draw an exponential from the first to the second. If x(∞) > x(0⁺), the curve rises; if x(∞) < x(0⁺), it falls. The initial slope is:

dx/dt at t = 0⁺ = [x(∞) − x(0⁺)] / τ

the slope has units of x per second; it is positive for a rising step and negative for a falling step. The tangent at t = 0 reaches the final value at t = τ. After 5τ the error is about 0.7% of the initial mismatch.

A pulse is two steps in the same circuit. First the source switches on at t = 0 and the answer runs toward its on-state final value. At t = Tp it switches off, and the same formula restarts with x(Tp⁺) as the new initial value and a new final value, often zero. If Tp is much larger than τ, the capacitor or inductor fully charges and then fully discharges; if Tp is much smaller than τ, it barely moves and the output looks like a distorted copy of the pulse. A repeating pulse train builds or droops according to this ratio.

With two storage elements the single exponential splits into two, covered in Chapter 5.4.

Worked example

Question. A 10 V DC source is switched at t = 0 across a series combination of R = 50 kΩ and C = 2 μF. The capacitor is uncharged before switching. Find τ, v_C(t), i(t), and v_C at t = 0.1 s.

  1. τ = RC = (50 × 10³ Ω)(2 × 10⁻⁶ F) = 0.1 s.
  2. For an uncharged capacitor, v_C(0⁺) = 0 V. After the transient, the capacitor is open to DC, so v_C(∞) = 10 V.
  3. Use v_C(t) = v_C(∞) + [v_C(0⁺) − v_C(∞)] e−t/τ = 10 + (0 − 10)e−10t = 10(1 − e−10t) V.
  4. i(t) = C dv_C/dt = (2 × 10⁻⁶)(10)(10)e−10t A = 2 × 10⁻⁴ e−10t A = 0.2 e−10t mA.
  5. At t = 0.1 s, t/τ = 1. e−1 = 0.3679. v_C(0.1) = 10(1 − 0.3679) = 6.321 V ≈ 6.32 V.

Answer. τ = 0.1 s, v_C(t) = 10(1 − e−10t) V, i(t) = 0.2 e−10t mA, and v_C(0.1 s) = 6.32 V.

Exam traps

  • Using the source value as the final value of every variable. In an RC step v_C ends at V_s but i_C ends at 0; in an RL step i_L ends at V_s/R but v_L ends at 0.
  • Putting x(0⁻) into the shortcut for a quantity that jumps at switching. Use x(0⁺); only v_C and i_L are guaranteed continuous.
  • Forgetting that τ uses the Thévenin resistance seen by C or L after switching, with sources killed.
  • Dropping the minus sign in the natural part. For a rising step x(0⁺) < x(∞), the natural term x(0⁺) − x(∞) is negative.
  • Treating a pulse as one step. A pulse has two switching instants; restart the formula at the second one.

5.4 Second-order RLC circuits: overdamped, critically damped, underdamped

A circuit with both an inductor and a capacitor has two energy stores, so after a switch it can do more than decay: it can overshoot and ring. The governing equation is second order, and its two roots decide the shape. Overdamped means two real roots and a slow creep; critically damped means one repeated root and the fastest rise without overshoot; underdamped means complex roots and a damped sine. This explains relay chatter, filter settling, motor drive overshoot and the ring you see on a lab oscilloscope.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for the t = 0 state and Chapter 5.3 for forced plus natural thinking.

The characteristic equation and the three shapes

A series RLC circuit with a DC step source has two energy stores, L and C. Write v_C(t) for the capacitor voltage and V_s for the step height. After the switch the circuit satisfies LC v_C'' + RC v_C' + v_C = V_s. Divide by LC. The forced part is V_s; the natural part is set by s² + (R/L)s + 1/(LC) = 0. Define α and ω₀ so the same equation reads s² + 2αs + ω₀² = 0.

s₁,₂ = −α ± √(α² − ω₀²)

s₁ and s₂ are the natural frequencies in s⁻¹; α is the damping coefficient in s⁻¹; ω₀ is the undamped natural frequency in rad/s.

  • α > ω₀: two different real negative roots. The circuit is overdamped; v_C creeps to V_s with two decaying exponentials.
  • α = ω₀: one repeated real root −α. The circuit is critically damped; v_C rises as fast as possible without overshoot.
  • α < ω₀: complex roots −α ± jω_d with ω_d = √(ω₀² − α²). The circuit is underdamped; v_C overshoots and rings at ω_d.

Compare α with ω₀, or, if you prefer, α² with ω₀²; the two tests are the same because both numbers are positive. α = 12 s⁻¹ with ω₀ = 10 rad/s is overdamped: 12 > 10, and also 144 > 100. Equality is one point, not a range. Some syllabi write over-damped, critically damped and under-damped.

The curves are labelled by the damping ratio ζ = α/ω₀, a dimensionless number: ζ < 1 underdamped, ζ = 1 critical, ζ > 1 overdamped.

Series and parallel circuits

The same quadratic appears in series and parallel RLC, but α is read differently. For a series RLC with resistance R, inductance L and capacitance C, α = R/(2L). For a parallel RLC, α = 1/(2RC). In both, ω₀ = 1/√(LC). Use the series value only for series circuits and the parallel value only for parallel circuits.

α_series = R/(2L),   α_parallel = 1/(2RC),   ω₀ = 1/√(LC)

α_series and α_parallel are damping coefficients in s⁻¹; R is resistance in ohms; L is inductance in henries; C is capacitance in farads; ω₀ is in rad/s.

Write V_f for the final capacitor voltage; for the series voltage step V_f = V_s. With a DC step from zero initial energy, the full capacitor voltage takes one of three forms. Overdamped: v_C(t) = V_f + A₁e^{s₁t} + A₂e^{s₂t}. Critically damped: v_C(t) = V_f + (A₁ + A₂t)e^{−αt}. Underdamped: v_C(t) = V_f + e^{−αt}(A₁cos ω_d t + A₂sin ω_d t). The constants A₁ and A₂ are fixed by v_C(0) and v_C'(0).

Initial conditions fix the constants

The initial conditions come from the two storage elements. Capacitor voltage cannot jump: v_C(0⁺) = v_C(0⁻). Inductor current cannot jump: i_L(0⁺) = i_L(0⁻). For a series circuit the current through C is the same as the inductor current, so v_C'(0⁺) = i_L(0⁺)/C. For a parallel circuit, find the capacitor current at 0⁺ by KCL, then use v_C'(0⁺) = i_C(0⁺)/C.

The shape has a physical meaning. In the underdamped case energy swaps between L and C, so v_C overshoots V_f and rings; R removes energy each cycle, so the ring decays as e^{−αt}. In the critically damped case the circuit returns to rest as fast as it can without a second overshoot. In the overdamped case one exponential dies quickly and the other lingers, so the rise is slow. A larger R in a series circuit increases α and pushes the same circuit from underdamped to critical to overdamped.

Use the response to choose R. A contactor coil or a filter that must not ring is made overdamped or critical. A radio front end may want a sharp underdamped response. The same three words describe mechanical suspension and control loops.

Worked example

Question. A series RLC circuit has L = 1 H, C = 0.01 F and a 10 V DC step source. The capacitor is initially uncharged and the inductor current is zero. Find ω₀ and α for R = 4 Ω, R = 20 Ω and R = 40 Ω. Name the damping case in each. For R = 4 Ω, also find the damped frequency ω_d.

  1. Compute ω₀ = 1/√(LC) = 1/√(1 × 0.01) = 1/0.1 = 10 rad/s.
  2. For a series circuit use α = R/(2L) = R/(2 × 1) = R/2 s⁻¹.
  3. R = 4 Ω: α = 2 s⁻¹. α² = 4 and ω₀² = 100, so α < ω₀. Underdamped.
  4. R = 20 Ω: α = 10 s⁻¹. α² = 100 = ω₀². Critically damped.
  5. R = 40 Ω: α = 20 s⁻¹. α² = 400 > 100. Overdamped.
  6. For R = 4 Ω, ω_d = √(ω₀² − α²) = √(100 − 4) = √96 = 9.798 rad/s.

Answer. ω₀ = 10 rad/s; for R = 4, 20 and 40 Ω, α = 2, 10 and 20 s⁻¹, so α/ω₀ = 0.2, 1 and 2 and the cases are underdamped, critical and overdamped; the R = 4 Ω case has ω_d ≈ 9.798 rad/s.

Exam traps

  • Using α = 1/(2RC) in a series RLC circuit; the series value is R/(2L).
  • Squaring only one side. Compare α with ω₀ (the easy test), or α² with ω₀², but never α² with ω₀. α = 12 s⁻¹ with ω₀ = 10 rad/s is overdamped: 12 > 10 and 144 > 100.
  • Letting v_C or i_L jump at the switch; both are continuous, and their 0⁻ values set the constants.
  • Writing ω_d = √(α² − ω₀²); for underdamped it is √(ω₀² − α²), a real positive number.

5.5 Natural frequency and damping ratio

Two numbers describe every second-order circuit. The natural frequency ω_n (rad/s) says how fast the circuit would oscillate with no energy loss. The damping ratio ζ (no unit) says how strongly resistance kills that oscillation. From them you get the pole positions, the ringing frequency, the overshoot, the peak time and the settling time. Designers use this language for filters, speaker crossovers, control loops and power supplies, where ringing wastes time and can trip protection. You can tune L and C for ω_n and R for ζ.

You need: Chapter 5.4 for the RLC characteristic equation, and Unit 1 for the element laws of R, L and C.

Two numbers replace the component values

A second-order circuit has two storage elements, so its characteristic equation is quadratic. Write it with two numbers: the natural frequency ω_n and the damping ratio ζ. The symbol s is the complex frequency in s⁻¹. ω_n, in rad/s, is the frequency the circuit would oscillate at if no energy were lost. ζ, with no unit, is how strongly energy loss kills that oscillation.

s² + 2ζω_n s + ω_n² = 0

s is the complex frequency in s⁻¹; ω_n is the natural frequency in rad/s; ζ is the damping ratio (no unit).

For a series RLC, ω_n = 1/√(LC) and α = R/(2L), where α is the neper frequency in s⁻¹. For a parallel RLC, α = 1/(2RC). Then ζ = α/ω_n. Do not use the series α in a parallel circuit; each α belongs to its own topology. Some syllabi write ω₀ for ω_n and call ζ the damping factor. A useful inverse is Q = 1/(2ζ); high Q means light damping and a long ring. These two numbers are not extra components; they are a relabelling of R, L and C.

Roots and the damped frequency

Solve the characteristic equation for the poles: s = −ζω_n ± ω_n√(ζ² − 1). The square root changes character at ζ = 1. For ζ < 1 it is imaginary, so the roots are complex conjugates. Rewrite them as s = −ζω_n ± jω_n√(1 − ζ²). The real part −ζω_n is the decay rate in s⁻¹. The imaginary part is the damped frequency ω_d = ω_n√(1 − ζ²) rad/s. The circuit rings at ω_d, not at ω_n. They differ only when ζ is large.

s = −ζω_n ± jω_d, ω_d = ω_n√(1 − ζ²)

s is the complex pole in s⁻¹; ζ is the damping ratio; ω_n is the natural frequency in rad/s; ω_d is the damped frequency in rad/s; j = √(−1).

At ζ = 1 the two roots meet at s = −ω_n. For ζ > 1 both roots are real and negative. At ζ = 0 the roots sit on the jω axis and the oscillation never dies. A negative ζ puts a root in the right half-plane and the response grows. The common error is to write ω_d = ω_n√(ζ² − 1); that expression is imaginary for ζ < 1 and is not a measurable frequency.

Overshoot, speed and settling

Step-response specifications follow from the same two numbers. Percentage overshoot is the largest rise above the final value: overshoot = 100 e^(−πζ/√(1−ζ²)) %. Peak time t_p = π/ω_d seconds is when the first peak occurs. The 2 % settling time t_s ≈ 4/(ζω_n) seconds is when the envelope has fallen to about 2 % of its starting size. These formulas assume a standard second-order low-pass response with zero initial energy and a unit step. They are estimates when the circuit has zeros.

M_p = 100 e^(−πζ/√(1−ζ²)) %,   t_p = π/ω_d,   t_s ≈ 4/(ζω_n)

M_p is percentage overshoot; ζ is the damping ratio; ω_d is the damped frequency in rad/s; t_p is peak time in s; t_s is 2 % settling time in s; ω_n is the natural frequency in rad/s.

Use them to choose L and C for ω_n and R for ζ. In a series circuit, raising R raises α and ζ, so ringing dies faster but the response creeps once ζ passes 1. In a parallel circuit, raising R lowers α and ζ, so a large parallel resistor lets the tank ring longer. On the s-plane the poles lie on a circle of radius ω_n and move along it as ζ changes; Chapter 5.8 reads that picture.

Worked example

Question. A series RLC circuit has R = 20 Ω, L = 0.1 H and C = 10 μF. A 10 V step is applied at t = 0 with zero initial energy. Find ω_n, α, ζ, ω_d, percentage overshoot, peak time and 2 % settling time.

  1. Compute LC = (0.1 H)(10 × 10⁻⁶ F) = 1 × 10⁻⁶ s². Then ω_n = 1/√(LC) = 1/√(1 × 10⁻⁶) = 1000 rad/s.
  2. For a series RLC, α = R/(2L) = 20 Ω/(2 × 0.1 H) = 100 s⁻¹.
  3. ζ = α/ω_n = 100/1000 = 0.1. The circuit is underdamped.
  4. √(1 − ζ²) = √(1 − 0.01) = √0.99 = 0.994987. ω_d = 1000 × 0.994987 = 994.987 rad/s ≈ 995 rad/s.
  5. Exponent = πζ/√(1 − ζ²) = (3.14159 × 0.1)/0.994987 = 0.315741. e^(−0.315741) = 0.7293. Overshoot = 100 × 0.7293 = 72.93 % ≈ 72.9 %.
  6. t_p = π/ω_d = 3.14159/994.987 = 0.003157 s = 3.157 ms ≈ 3.16 ms.
  7. t_s ≈ 4/(ζω_n) = 4/(0.1 × 1000) = 4/100 = 0.04 s = 40 ms.

  • Using Q = 2ζ instead of Q = 1/(2ζ).

5.6 Transient response with sinusoidal excitation

A sine source switched on at t = 0 gives two currents at once: a steady sine set by the phasor answer, and a decaying exponential left over from the switching instant. The exponential is not optional. Its starting size K depends on where the sine was when the switch closed. This explains why a transformer or motor can draw a large first peak even though its steady current is modest. The same calculation covers relay coils, capacitor charging through a resistor, and any AC load energised by a switch.

You need: Unit 3 for phasors and impedance, Chapter 5.2 for the time constant τ, and Chapter 5.3 for natural plus forced response.

The complete response is a sine plus an exponential

Write the source as v_s = V_m sin(ωt + φ). V_m is peak volts, ω is angular frequency in rad/s, t is seconds, φ is the phase at switching in radians. For a series RL circuit, the phasor impedance is Z = R + jωL. Its magnitude is |Z| = √(R² + (ωL)²), and its angle is θ = tan⁻¹(ωL/R). The steady-state current phasor is I = (V_m∠φ)/Z. In time form, i_ss(t) = I_m sin(ωt + φ − θ), where I_m = V_m/|Z|.

Inductor current cannot jump. The total current is i(t) = i_ss(t) + K e−t/τ, with τ = L/R in seconds. K is an ampere constant fixed by the initial current. At t = 0⁺, i(0⁺) = I_m sin(φ − θ) + K, so K = i(0⁺) − I_m sin(φ − θ). This is the whole method.

Series RC works the same way. Now Z = R − j/(ωC), θ = tan⁻¹(1/(ωRC)), and i_ss(t) = I_m sin(ωt + φ + θ), because current leads voltage. The time constant is τ = RC. In RC the capacitor voltage cannot jump: v_C(0⁺) = v_C(0⁻). Get the initial current from KVL at t = 0⁺, i(0⁺) = (v_s(0⁺) − v_C(0⁺))/R, and then put it in K = i(0⁺) − I_m sin(φ + θ).

i(t) = I_m sin(ωt + φ − θ) + K e<sup>−t/τ</sup>,  K = i(0⁺) − I_m sin(φ − θ)

I_m = V_m/|Z| is peak steady current in A; θ is the impedance angle; τ = L/R is the time constant in s; K is the initial offset in A. For RC replace −θ by +θ and τ by RC.

Where the offset comes from: the switching instant

At the switch, the sine part has some value. If that value is not equal to the actual initial current, the difference must be supplied by K e−t/τ. So the size of K depends on φ, the source phase at switching. Some syllabi call this transient response under AC excitation.

The largest offset occurs when i_ss(0) is at a peak, ±I_m. For a mainly resistive RL circuit this is close to switching at the source peak. For a purely inductive circuit it is switching at the source zero crossing, because current lags by 90°. The gentlest case is i_ss(0) = 0, which gives K = 0 and no transient. Keep the general rule: compare i_ss(0) with i(0⁺), not the source value.

After one time constant the exponential has fallen to 37% of K. After 5τ it is below 1% and is treated as gone. In the example below τ = 0.25 s, so the transient is finished in about 1.25 s.

An RLC circuit under AC behaves in the same pattern: the steady state is still a phasor, and the transient is a second-order solution from Chapter 5.4. It may ring instead of decaying as one exponential.

Switching at a bad instant can double the first peak. A 2 A steady current with a 2 A offset reaches a first peak of nearly 4 A when the exponential has not decayed much by the first steady peak; at other instants the offset partly cancels the sine. The total current still starts at i(0⁺), which is 0 A for a circuit switched on from rest. Fuses react to the first peak, so the instant matters in real equipment.

5.7 Laplace transform and s-domain circuit analysis

Every transient so far has been a differential equation with an initial condition bolted on. The Laplace transform removes both difficulties at once: it turns derivatives into multiplication by s, and it swallows the initial condition into the transformed circuit as an extra source. What is left is a network of impedances in the complex variable s, solved with the nodal and mesh tools of Unit 2. The inverse transform at the end returns the time answer. Switched-mode power supplies, motor drives and every SPICE run use this step.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for i_L(0⁻) and v_C(0⁻), and Chapter 5.3 for the time-domain answers you will check against.

The one-sided transform and the derivative rule

The Laplace transform converts a time function f(t) into a function of the complex variable s = σ + jω: F(s) = ∫₀⁻^∞ f(t) e^(−st) dt. The lower limit is 0⁻, the instant just before switching, so a jump at t = 0 lies inside the integral. That single choice is why initial conditions survive as ordinary numbers instead of constants of integration.

Differentiation becomes multiplication. Apply the rule twice to the RLC loop equation and a second-order differential equation collapses into a quadratic in s — the same characteristic equation that Chapter 5.4 solved by guessing e^(st).

L{df/dt} = sF(s) − f(0⁻)   ,   L{d²f/dt²} = s²F(s) − s f(0⁻) − f′(0⁻)

F(s) is the transform of f(t); f(0⁻) is the value just before the switch; f′(0⁻) is the value of its derivative just before the switch.

s-domain element models: memory becomes a source

A resistor has no memory: v = Ri holds at every instant, so V(s) = R I(s). The inductor and capacitor store energy, and their derivative relations pull the stored value into the transform as a source.

Inductor: v = L di/dt, so V(s) = sL I(s) − L i_L(0⁻). Model it as an impedance sL in series with a voltage source of value L i_L(0⁻), poled to push current the way the initial current was already flowing.

Capacitor: i = C dv/dt, so I(s) = sC V(s) − C v_C(0⁻), which rearranges to V(s) = (1/sC) I(s) + v_C(0⁻)/s. Model it as an impedance 1/sC in series with a source v_C(0⁻)/s. Note the 1/s: a stored 4 V becomes 4/s, because in the transformed picture that source must act for all time.

Solve in s, then walk back to time

  1. Freeze the circuit at t = 0⁻ and find i_L(0⁻) and v_C(0⁻).
  2. Transform the sources: a DC source of A volts becomes A/s, A sin ωt becomes Aω/(s² + ω²), A cos ωt becomes As/(s² + ω²).
  3. Replace R, L and C by the models above, initial-condition sources included.
  4. Write KCL at each node (sum of V(s)/Z leaving the node = 0) or KVL round each mesh (sum of Z I(s) = source). It is plain algebra in s.
  5. Solve for the wanted V(s) or I(s) as one rational function.
  6. Split it into partial fractions and invert each term from the pair table.
  7. Check with the initial and final value theorems, then against the answer from Chapter 5.3 or 5.4.

First check that the degree of the numerator is less than the degree of the denominator. If it is not, divide first, handle the polynomial part separately, and only then write the remainder as Σ K_i/(s − p_i). For distinct poles p₁, p₂, …, K_i = [(s − p_i)F(s)] evaluated at s = p_i, and each term inverts to K_i e^(p_i t). A pole repeated r times needs K/(s − p)^r terms, which invert to K t^(r−1) e^(pt)/(r−1)!; for r = 2 that is K t e^(pt), the t e^(−at) shape of critical damping.

The method never changes the physics. Same differential equation, same initial condition, same answer — the worked example below is the RC step of Chapter 5.3 done in s.

Exam traps

  • Sign and scale of the initial-condition source. An inductor needs L i_L(0⁻) volts and a capacitor needs v_C(0⁻)/s volts in a series branch. Writing C v_C(0⁻) there is the parallel-form current source in the wrong place. The slip is silent and surfaces only at t = 0⁺.
  • Using f(0⁺) instead of f(0⁻) in the derivative rule. The theorem is stated with 0⁻ precisely because inductor current and capacitor voltage cannot jump; any other quantity can, and then the two values differ.
  • Applying the final value theorem to a circuit that never settles. If F(s) has a pole in the right half plane, or a pair on the jω axis as in an undamped LC, the limit does not exist and the 'steady state' you quote is fiction.
  • Dropping the 1/s or the ω. A constant A volts is A/s, not A, and A sin ωt is Aω/(s² + ω²), not A/(s² + ω²). A missing factor of ω is the commonest slip in the sinusoidal case.

5.8 Network functions, transfer functions, poles and zeros

A network function is a ratio of one response transform to one excitation transform. Kill every independent source, set all initial energy to zero, and the circuit becomes a pure ratio H(s). That single function carries the poles that decide the natural response and the zeros that shape how the steady-state response rises or falls. Designers use it to read stability from a pole-zero plot, to get the impulse response by inverse transform, and to find the sinusoidal steady-state gain as H(jω). Filters, amplifiers and control loops are all specified this way.

You need: Chapter 5.7 for s-domain algebra, and Unit 4 for resonance and Q.

From a circuit to H(s)

Write the circuit in the s-domain with the Laplace transform. Element impedances become: R stays R, L becomes sL, C becomes 1/(sC). Set every independent source to zero: voltage sources become short circuits, current sources become open circuits. Set every initial inductor current and initial capacitor voltage to zero. Now excite one port with a test source X(s) and compute the response Y(s) at the chosen port.

The network function is H(s) = Y(s)/X(s). X(s) is the input transform; Y(s) is the output transform; H(s) has units of output per input. If input and output are at the same port, H is a driving-point function, such as input impedance Z(s) = V(s)/I(s). If they are at different ports, H is a transfer function, such as voltage gain V₂(s)/V₁(s).

H(s) = Y(s)/X(s) = N(s)/D(s)

N(s) and D(s) are polynomials in s with real coefficients; N(s) is the numerator, D(s) is the denominator after cancelling common factors.

The denominator D(s) is the characteristic polynomial of the circuit. Its roots are the poles. They depend only on the circuit elements and on which storage elements are present, not on where the input is applied. The numerator N(s) gives the zeros. The zeros do depend on where the output is taken.

  1. Draw the s-domain circuit with all independent sources killed and zero initial conditions.
  2. Apply a test source X(s) at the input port.
  3. Solve for the response Y(s) at the output port.
  4. Form H(s) = Y(s)/X(s), then factor N(s) and D(s).

Poles, zeros and the s-plane

A pole is a value of s that makes H(s) infinite. A zero is a value of s that makes H(s) zero. Write s = σ + jω. σ is the real part, in s⁻¹; ω is the imaginary part, in rad/s; j is the imaginary unit. Mark poles by × and zeros by ○ in the complex s-plane. The real part σ sets growth or decay; the imaginary part ω sets oscillation.

H(s) = K (s − z₁)(s − z₂)… / ((s − p₁)(s − p₂)…)

K is a real gain; z₁, z₂, … are zeros; p₁, p₂, … are poles. A repeated pole or zero is counted by its multiplicity.

With zero initial conditions, the impulse response is h(t) = L⁻¹{H(s)}. Each simple pole pᵢ contributes a term Aᵢ e^(pᵢt). A pole with negative real part gives a decaying term. A pole on the jω axis gives a constant-amplitude sinusoid. A pole with positive real part gives growth. So stability needs all poles strictly in the left half plane.

Put s = jω to get the sinusoidal steady-state ratio. |H(jω)| is the gain at frequency ω, and ∠H(jω) is the phase shift. If a pole sits close to the jω axis, the denominator becomes small near that frequency and |H(jω)| peaks. This is the same resonance and Q effect from Unit 4: small damping means a tall narrow peak.

|H(jω)| = |N(jω)| / |D(jω)|

N(jω) and D(jω) are the numerator and denominator evaluated at s = jω; | | means magnitude, and ω is in rad/s.

Stability and reading a pole-zero plot

Only poles decide stability. Zeros change the size and phase of the response but cannot turn a growing exponential into a decaying one. A pole at s = 0 is a constant offset; a pair at s = ±jω₀ is a sustained oscillation; a pole at s = +2 gives e^(2t). A zero near a pole can cancel its visible effect, but exact cancellation is rare and small errors leave the pole.

For a second-order low-pass H(s) = ω_n² / (s² + 2ζω_n s + ω_n²), poles are s = −ζω_n ± jω_n√(1 − ζ²) for 0 < ζ < 1. The damping ratio ζ sets the real part; the natural frequency ω_n sets the distance from the origin. For ζ < 1/√2 the magnitude has a peak at ω_r = ω_n√(1 − 2ζ²) with peak 1/[2ζ√(1 − ζ²)].

Worked example

Question. A series RLC circuit has L = 1 H, C = 0.01 F, and the output is the capacitor voltage. The input is an ideal voltage source. Find H(s) = V_C(s)/V_s(s), its poles and zeros, and the peak magnitude for R = 2 Ω and R = 10 Ω. Use ω_n and ζ.

With L = 1 H and C = 0.01 F, LC = 0.01 s². Divide by 0.01: H(s) = 100 / (s² + R s + 100), where R is the resistance value in ohms.
  2. The natural frequency is ω_n = √(1/(LC)) = √(100) = 10 rad/s. The denominator is s² + 2ζω_n s + ω_n², so 2ζω_n = R/L = R, giving ζ = R/(2ω_n) = R/20. For R = 2 Ω, ζ = 0.1. For R = 10 Ω, ζ = 0.5.
  3. The numerator is the constant 100, so there are no finite zeros.
  4. For R = 2 Ω, solve s² + 2s + 100 = 0. The poles are s = −1 ± j√99 = −1 ± j9.9499. The peak is at ω_r = 10√(1 − 2(0.1)²) = 10√0.98 = 9.8995 rad/s. Peak |H| = 1/[2(0.1)√(1 − 0.01)] = 5.0252.

Exam traps

  • Stability comes only from poles. A zero in the right half plane does not make an unstable circuit; a pole in the right half plane does.
  • A pole on the jω axis is marginal, not stable. It gives sustained oscillation; any small positive real part grows without limit.
  • Do not forget to kill sources and zero initial conditions before forming H(s). A live source or an initial capacitor voltage adds terms that are not part of H(s).
  • H(jω) is not H(s) with s replaced by ω. Put s = jω, including the j. Dropping j gives the wrong sign and phase.
  • Cancelling a pole with a zero hides it mathematically only; exact cancellation is not robust in real components.

5.9 State-variable analysis of circuits

Some circuits have many storage elements, and solving each differential equation separately gets messy. State-variable analysis collects the inductor currents and capacitor voltages into one vector x. The circuit then becomes two matrix equations, ẋ = Ax + Bu and y = Cx + Du. The eigenvalues of A are the natural frequencies, so you know whether the circuit rings without solving anything. Only a few universities teach this chapter. Circuit simulators and control engineers use it daily.

You need: 5.4 for the RLC characteristic equation, 5.8 for poles, and basic matrix algebra.

What the state vector holds

State variables are the quantities that cannot jump when a switch moves: inductor currents iL and capacitor voltages vC (see 5.1). Stack them as x₁, x₂, …, into the state vector x. A circuit with n storage elements has n states.

The circuit becomes two matrix equations:

ẋ = Ax + Bu

x is the n × 1 state vector; u holds the m source values; A is n × n; B is n × m.

y = Cx + Du

y holds p outputs; C is p × n; D is p × m; D is the direct feedthrough through resistors.

Build the matrices:

  1. Label each iL and vC as a state.
  2. Write KVL around inductor loops and KCL at capacitor nodes.
  3. Divide until each derivative stands alone.
  4. Collect coefficients into A and B.
  5. Write each output as a sum of states and sources to read C and D.

Eigenvalues give the natural frequencies

The eigenvalues of A are the natural frequencies. Solve:

det(sI − A) = 0

I is the identity matrix; s is the complex frequency; the roots are the natural frequencies.

This is the same characteristic polynomial as in 5.4. The roots are the poles, so you know whether the circuit rings without solving the differential equation. Real negative roots decay. A pair −σ ± jωd rings at ωd rad/s while decaying. A passive RLC never gives a positive real part. For a 2 × 2 A, compare det(sI − A) with s² + 2ζωns + ωn² to read ωn and ζ.

Reading the block diagram

The figure shows the same algebra. The source u enters through B and adds to the feedback Ax. The integrator turns ẋ into x, so x cannot jump. The output y is read through C. Every circuit simulator builds this loop.

Takeaways

  1. Inductor current and capacitor voltage are the two quantities that cannot jump, so the circuit state at t = 0⁻ decides the whole answer after switching.
  2. With no source, energy drains as a pure exponential e^(−t/τ) whose speed is fixed only by R and the storage element.
  3. Every first-order answer is final value plus (initial value minus final value) times e^(−t/τ); find the two end values and τ and the work is done.
  4. Two storage elements give a quadratic characteristic equation, and its two roots decide whether the answer creeps, settles cleanly, or rings.
  5. The pair ω_n and ζ replaces R, L and C as the description of a second-order circuit: ω_n sets how fast it wants to oscillate, ζ sets how fast the oscillation is killed.
  6. With a sine source the complete response is a steady-state sine plus a decaying exponential whose size depends on the instant of switching.
  7. The Laplace transform turns the differential equation into algebra and puts the initial conditions inside the circuit as extra sources.
  8. With sources killed and zero initial energy the circuit is a single ratio H(s); its poles give the natural response and its zeros shape the steady-state response.
  9. State variables turn any circuit into ẋ = Ax + Bu and y = Cx + Du, where the eigenvalues of A are the natural frequencies.

Remember and understand

Understand State and explain the continuity conditions for an inductor and a capacitor. Why can these two quantities not change instantaneously, while resistor current and voltage can? 8 marks

Inductor: v_L = L di/dt, so a jump in i would need infinite voltage — not possible in a physical circuit; Capacitor: i_C = C dv/dt, so a jump in v would need infinite current; Hence i_L(0⁺) = i_L(0⁻) and v_C(0⁺) = v_C(0⁻); Resistor obeys v = iR with no memory, so it may jump freely; Stored energy ½Li² and ½Cv² cannot change in zero time.

Remember Define the time constant of an RC circuit and of an RL circuit. Give its unit, and state the meaning of one, three and five time constants. 8 marks

τ = RC seconds; τ = L/R seconds; Unit: seconds when R in ohms, C in farads, L in henries; One τ: change reaches 63.2% of final value (rises to 36.8% of initial gap remaining); Three τ: about 95%; five τ: about 99.3%, treated as steady state; Sketch of the exponential and the tangent at t = 0 cutting the final value at t = τ.

Understand Distinguish between natural response, forced response and complete response of a linear circuit. Show how the three are added for a first-order circuit with a DC source. 10 marks

Natural: source set to zero, set by circuit roots, always decays in a passive circuit; Forced: source kept, found as the steady state; constant for DC input; Complete = natural + forced; Working form x(t) = x(∞) + [x(0⁺) − x(∞)]e^(−t/τ); Verify by checking t = 0⁺ and t → ∞.

Understand Explain how the roots of the characteristic equation of a series RLC circuit classify the response as overdamped, critically damped or underdamped. State the condition on R in each case. 10 marks

Characteristic equation s² + (R/L)s + 1/(LC) = 0; Roots s₁,₂ = −α ± √(α² − ω_n²) with α = R/(2L), ω_n = 1/√(LC); α > ω_n i.e. R > 2√(L/C): overdamped, two real roots; α = ω_n i.e. R = 2√(L/C): critically damped, repeated root; α < ω_n i.e. R < 2√(L/C): underdamped, complex conjugate roots, response rings; Sketch the three waveforms.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze For a series RLC circuit with L = 1 H and C = 1 F, find the value of R that gives critical damping. Plot the shape of the capacitor voltage for R above and below this value, and explain the physical reason for the difference. 13 marks

Critical damping when R = 2√(L/C) = 2 Ω; α = R/(2L), ω_n = 1 rad/s, ζ = R/2; R > 2 Ω: overdamped, slow creep to final value, no overshoot; R < 2 Ω: underdamped, ringing at ω_d = √(1 − ζ²), overshoot above final value; Physical reason: larger R dissipates the stored energy faster than it can be exchanged between L and C; Comment on the envelope e^(−αt) and on settling time.

Evaluate A sinusoidal source is switched onto an RL circuit. Show that the size of the transient term depends on the switching angle, and find the switching instant at which the transient is zero. 14 marks

i(t) = I_m sin(ωt + θ − φ) + Ke^(−t/τ), with φ = tan⁻¹(ωL/R); i(0⁺) = 0 fixes K = −I_m sin(θ − φ); K = 0 when θ = φ, i.e. switching at the instant the steady-state current is passing through zero going positive; Worst case is θ − φ = ±90°, where the transient has peak amplitude I_m; Practical comment: this is why inrush current in transformers and motors depends on the instant of switching.

Analyze For the circuit with a series RLC branch, write the state equations in the form ẋ = Ax + Bu, identify the state variables, and show how the eigenvalues of A relate to the natural response. 14 marks

Evaluate Define the transfer function of a network. For a given two-port network, explain how the pole-zero plot predicts stability and the shape of the impulse response. 15 marks

H(s) = Y(s)/X(s) with sources killed and zero initial energy; Poles are roots of the denominator, zeros of the numerator; All poles in the left half plane → stable; poles on the jω axis → marginal; right half plane → unstable; Real pole gives e^(−σt); complex pair gives e^(−σt)cos(ω_d t + φ); Zero near the jω axis reduces the response at that frequency, i.e. it notches the steady-state gain; Impulse response is the inverse Laplace transform of H(s).

Glossary

Characteristic equation
The polynomial equation whose roots are the natural frequencies of the circuit.
Characteristic root
A value of s that makes the natural response possible; written s₁ and s₂ for a second-order circuit.
Critically damped
The boundary case with equal real roots; the fastest settling without overshoot.
Damped frequency ω_d
The actual oscillation frequency ω_n√(1 − ζ²) when the circuit is underdamped.
Damping
The loss of oscillatory energy caused by resistance, which makes a second-order response settle.
Damping ratio ζ
The dimensionless number α/ω_n that measures how strongly oscillation is suppressed.
Exponential decay
A fall of the form Ae^(−t/τ), in which equal time steps give equal fractional drops.
Forced response
The response forced by the source, equal to the steady state for DC and sine inputs.
Impulse response
The output when the input is a unit impulse; it is the inverse Laplace transform of H(s).
Inverse Laplace transform
The step that recovers f(t) from F(s), usually by partial fractions and a table.
Laplace transform
A rule that maps a time function f(t) to a complex-frequency function F(s).
Natural frequency ω_n
The frequency in rad/s at which the circuit would oscillate with no resistance.
Natural response
The response produced by the circuit's own poles with independent sources set to zero.
Overdamped
A second-order case with two distinct real roots; the response settles without oscillating.
Partial fraction expansion
Splitting a ratio of polynomials into simple terms whose inverse transforms are known.
Pole
A value of s where the transfer function becomes infinite and the natural response lives.
s-plane
The complex plane with σ on the horizontal axis and jω on the vertical axis, used to plot poles and zeros.
Source-free response
The response of a circuit after its source is removed, driven only by stored energy.
State variable
One of the minimum set of quantities, here i_L and v_C, that fixes the future behaviour of the circuit.
Steady state
The condition reached after the transient has vanished, when every quantity repeats or stays constant.
Switching
An abrupt change in the circuit at t = 0, usually by closing or opening a switch.
Time constant
The value τ in e^(−t/τ); the time for the response to fall to 36.8% of its starting value.
Transfer function
The ratio Y(s)/X(s) of output to input transform for zero initial energy.
Transient
The part of a response that dies away with time after a change in the circuit.
Underdamped
A second-order case with complex roots; the response oscillates while it decays.
Zero
A value of s where the transfer function becomes zero, blocking transmission at that frequency.
