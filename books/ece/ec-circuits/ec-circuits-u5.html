About this unit

This unit is about what a circuit does in the moments after a switch moves. It starts by fixing the values just after the switch, follows the free decay of one storage element and its time constant, and then adds a step source to split every answer into natural and forced parts. Two storage elements bring the three damping cases, described by the natural frequency and the damping ratio, and a sine source shows the transient riding on the steady-state wave. The Laplace transform then replaces the differential equations with algebra, network functions read the answer through poles and zeros, and state equations, which only a few universities teach, write the same circuit in matrix form.

5.1 Initial and final conditions in RL, RC, RLC circuits

A switch moves at t = 0. The inductor current and the capacitor voltage cannot change in zero time, because that would need a jump in the stored energy ½Li² or ½Cv², and so infinite power. So the state just before switching, at t = 0⁻, fixes the state just after, at t = 0⁺, and everything after that is an ordinary resistive circuit problem. This one idea starts every transient problem correctly, from the inductor in a phone charger to the capacitor that holds up a router through a Chennai power cut.

You need: the V-I relations of R, L and C, and KVL and KCL, from circuit elements and laws.

Nothing stored can change instantly

Three instants matter. At t = 0⁻ the circuit is still in its old state, at t = 0 the switch moves, and at t = 0⁺ the new circuit has just started. The signs are not arithmetic: they mean the left and right limits of time around the switching instant.

An inductor stores energy ½LiL², where L is the inductance in henries and iL the current through it in amperes. A jump in iL would change that energy in zero time, which needs infinite power. No real source supplies that, so the inductor current is continuous. A capacitor stores ½CvC², with C in farads and vC in volts, and the same argument makes the capacitor voltage continuous.

Everything else may jump. The inductor voltage can change instantly, so can the capacitor current, and a resistor has no memory at all: it obeys Ohm's law at every instant.

iL(0⁺) = iL(0⁻) and vC(0⁺) = vC(0⁻)

Both are measured with the reference direction and polarity chosen before the switch.

The equivalent circuit at 0⁺ and at ∞

At t = 0⁺ replace the inductor by a current source of value iL(0⁻), pointing the way the current flowed, and the capacitor by a voltage source of value vC(0⁻), with the same polarity. What is left is a resistive circuit: solve it with KVL and KCL.

At t → ∞ with a DC source the circuit settles. Then vL = L di/dt = 0, so the inductor is a short circuit, and iC = C dv/dt = 0, so the capacitor is an open circuit.

  1. Draw the circuit for t < 0 with the switch in its old position; with DC, L is a short and C is open. Find iL(0⁻) and vC(0⁻).
  2. Carry them across the switch unchanged.
  3. Draw the t = 0⁺ circuit with L as a current source and C as a voltage source, and find the currents and voltages you need.
  4. Draw the t → ∞ circuit with L short and C open, and find the final values.

If nothing was stored, the two pictures look like opposites: at 0⁺ a 0 A inductor source is an open branch and a 0 V capacitor source is a short, while at ∞ the inductor is a short and the capacitor is open. Keep the two instants apart.

The first slope at 0⁺

The end values fix where the answer starts and stops; the first slope tells you how it leaves. Read the element laws vL = L diL/dt and iC = C dvC/dt at t = 0⁺:

diL/dt (0⁺) = vL(0⁺)/L

in A/s, with vL(0⁺) from KVL on the 0⁺ circuit.

dvC/dt (0⁺) = iC(0⁺)/C

in V/s, with iC(0⁺) from KCL on the 0⁺ circuit.

Neither vL nor iC is continuous, so both are found fresh after the switch. In a second-order circuit the initial value and this first slope are the two conditions that fix the two constants of the solution (see second-order circuits). They also let you sketch the answer before any algebra and catch a wrong one.

Worked example

Question. In the circuit of Figure 5.1.1, a 10 V DC source feeds R₁ = 2 Ω and L = 1 H in series to node a. From node a to the return line sit C = 0.25 F and R₂ = 3 Ω in parallel. The circuit has been in this state for a long time. At t = 0 a switch S across R₁ closes and shorts it out. Find iL(0⁺), vC(0⁺), iC(0⁺), vL(0⁺), the two first slopes, and the final values iL(∞) and vC(∞).

  1. Before the switch. DC steady state: L is a short and C is open, so one current flows through R₁, L and R₂: 10/(2 + 3) = 2 A. So iL(0⁻) = 2 A, and C sits across R₂: vC(0⁻) = 2 × 3 = 6 V.
  2. Across the switch. iL(0⁺) = 2 A and vC(0⁺) = 6 V.
  3. The 0⁺ circuit. R₁ is shorted, L is a 2 A current source and C is a 6 V source. R₂ has 6 V across it, so its current is 6/3 = 2 A.
  4. Capacitor current. KCL at node a: iL = iC + iR2, so iC(0⁺) = 2 − 2 = 0 A.
  5. Inductor voltage. KVL round the source, L and C: 10 = vL(0⁺) + 6, so vL(0⁺) = 4 V. It was 0 V before the switch: the inductor voltage jumped, which is allowed.
  6. First slopes. diL/dt = 4/1 = 4 A/s. dvC/dt = 0/0.25 = 0 V/s, so vC leaves 6 V with a flat start.
  7. Final state. L short, C open, R₁ still shorted: iL(∞) = 10/3 = 3.33 A and vC(∞) = 3.33 × 3 = 10 V.

Answer. iL(0⁺) = 2 A, vC(0⁺) = 6 V, iC(0⁺) = 0 A, vL(0⁺) = 4 V, diL/dt = 4 A/s, dvC/dt = 0 V/s, iL(∞) = 3.33 A, vC(∞) = 10 V.

Exam traps

  • Treating L as an open and C as a short at t = 0⁺ in every case. In general they are sources of value iL(0⁻) and vC(0⁻); they look like an open and a short only when nothing was stored. The unconditional short/open rule belongs to DC steady state.
  • Believing a big source forces iL or vC to jump. Only an impulse could; a finite source gives a finite slope, not a jump.
  • Applying continuity to the wrong variable: inductor voltage, capacitor current and every resistor quantity may jump.
  • Solving the t < 0 circuit with the switch already in its new position, so the stored values are wrong from the first line.
  • Unit slips: di/dt = vL/L is in A/s only with vL in volts and L in henries; forgetting to convert mH gives a factor of 1000.

5.2 Source-free RL and RC circuits; time constant

Disconnect the source and the stored energy does not vanish at once: an inductor's current and a capacitor's voltage fall through the resistance along a smooth exponential. The speed is set by one number, the time constant τ. This source-free (natural) response, with no battery or generator, only L or C and R, explains why a camera-flash capacitor still holds charge after switch-off and why a relay coil's current collapses in milliseconds. The same maths runs sensor circuits, filters and snubbers.

You need: the V-I relations of L and C from circuit elements and laws, and initial and final conditions for the starting value at t = 0.

The first-order circuit and its equation

Source-free means the independent source is removed or switched out at t = 0. Only the storage element and R remain. In an RL circuit, an inductor L with initial current I₀ is connected across a resistor R. KVL gives vL + vR = 0. Use vL = L di/dt and vR = R i:

L di/dt + R i = 0 ⇒ di/dt = −(R/L) i

L is inductance in henry (H); R is resistance in ohm (Ω); i is the loop current in ampere (A) at time t in second (s).

The solution is i(t) = I₀ e−t/τ with τ = L/R. I₀ is the current at t = 0 in A. For an RC circuit, a capacitor C charged to V₀ is connected across R. KCL at the node gives C dv/dt + v/R = 0, so RC dv/dt + v = 0. The voltage is v(t) = V₀ e−t/τ with τ = RC. V₀ is the capacitor voltage at t = 0 in V. Both are first-order because one storage element sets the answer, and the final value is zero. When several resistors remain, R is the equivalent resistance Req seen from the terminals of L or C. Some syllabi call this the natural response or free response.

The time constant and the tangent

τ is the time constant, measured in seconds. At t = τ, e−1 = 0.368. The current or voltage has fallen to 36.8 % of its starting value. After 2τ it is 13.5 %, after 3τ 5.0 %, and after 5τ 0.67 %. The 5τ rule says the transient is practically zero; it is not exactly zero. The half-life is τ ln 2 ≈ 0.693τ.

Differentiate x(t) = x(0)e−t/τ at t = 0: slope = −x(0)/τ. The tangent line is x = x(0) − [x(0)/τ]t. It meets zero at t = τ. That is the geometric meaning of τ: follow the starting slope and you reach zero one time constant later.

R changes the two circuits in opposite ways. For RL, τ = L/R: a larger R gives a smaller τ, because the same current through a larger resistor burns the stored ½LI₀² faster (power i²R). For RC, τ = RC: a larger R gives a larger τ, because the capacitor can push only a smaller current v/R through it. This is a favourite comparison question.

Energy goes into the resistor

Stored energy is ½ L I₀² in the inductor and ½ C V₀² in the capacitor. The resistor has no storage; it turns that energy into heat. In RL, pR = i²R = I₀²R e−2t/τ. Integrate from 0 to ∞: total = I₀²R (τ/2) = ½ L I₀². In RC, pR = v²/R = (V₀²/R) e−2t/τ. Total = (V₀²/R)(RC/2) = ½ C V₀². All the initial stored energy ends in R as t → ∞. At t = τ, the fraction burnt is 1 − e−2 = 0.865, so 86.5 % is gone and 13.5 % remains. This energy view explains why a relay coil needs a diode: the inductor's ½LI² must go somewhere when the switch opens.

Worked example

Question. An inductor L = 0.5 H carries a steady current I₀ = 2 A. At t = 0 it is connected across a resistor R = 100 Ω. Find τ, i(t), and the time to reach 0.2 A.

  1. This is a source-free RL circuit. The time constant is τ = L/R.
  2. τ = 0.5 H ÷ 100 Ω = 0.005 s = 5 ms.
  3. The current decay is i(t) = I₀ e−t/τ = 2 e−t/0.005 = 2 e−200t A. Here 200 s⁻¹ = 1/τ.
  4. Set i(t) = 0.2 A: 0.2 = 2 e−200t. Divide by 2: 0.1 = e−200t.
  5. Take natural logarithms: ln 0.1 = −200t. So t = −ln 0.1 ÷ 200 = ln 10 ÷ 200.
  6. ln 10 = 2.302585. t = 2.302585 ÷ 200 = 0.01151 s = 11.5 ms. Using τ directly: t = τ ln 10 = 5 ms × 2.3026 = 11.5 ms.

Answer. τ = 0.005 s = 5 ms; i(t) = 2e−200t A; the current is 0.2 A at t = 11.5 ms.

Worked example 2

Question. A 50 μF capacitor has been charged to 24 V. At t = 0 it is switched across a 6 kΩ and a 3 kΩ resistor in parallel. Find τ, v(t), the current in the 6 kΩ resistor at t = 50 ms, and the energy each resistor absorbs in the end.

  1. Equivalent resistance seen by C: Req = (6 × 3)/(6 + 3) = 2 kΩ.
  2. τ = Req C = 2000 × 50 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.1 s, so 1/τ = 10 s⁻¹.
  3. v(t) = 24 e−10t V for t ≥ 0.
  4. At t = 50 ms: v = 24 e−0.5 = 24 × 0.6065 = 14.56 V. Current in 6 kΩ: 14.56/6000 = 2.43 mA.
  5. Stored energy: ½CV₀² = 0.5 × 50 × 10⁻⁶ × 24² = 14.4 mJ.
  6. Both resistors see the same voltage, so their powers v²/R are in the ratio 1/6 : 1/3 = 1 : 2. The 6 kΩ resistor absorbs 14.4/3 = 4.8 mJ and the 3 kΩ resistor 9.6 mJ; together 14.4 mJ, all the stored energy.

Answer. τ = 0.1 s, v(t) = 24e−10t V, 2.43 mA in the 6 kΩ resistor at 50 ms, and 4.8 mJ and 9.6 mJ absorbed by the 6 kΩ and 3 kΩ resistors.

Exam traps

  • Writing τ = R/L for an RL circuit or τ = 1/(RC) for an RC circuit. Check units: τ must come out in seconds.
  • Writing 63.2 % at t = τ for a decay. A source-free decay has 36.8 % of its starting value left at t = τ; 63.2 % of the final value belongs to a charging curve.
  • Treating 5τ as exactly zero. About 0.67 % is left, so an exam may ask for the exact expression.
  • Forgetting that I₀ or V₀ comes from the instant before switching. The inductor current and capacitor voltage cannot jump; see initial and final conditions.
  • Saying a large R always slows decay. In RL, τ = L/R falls as R rises; in RC, τ = RC rises as R rises.

5.3 Step response of first-order circuits; natural and forced response

Switch a DC source onto a circuit with one capacitor or one inductor and every voltage and current moves from its starting value to its final value with the same time constant τ; some rise, some fall. Each answer is the sum of a forced part set by the source and a natural part set by the mismatch at the switching instant. Engineers use this split to size a delay, read a datasheet or explain why a relay drops out late. This chapter shows the split, the one-line formula that joins the start and end values, and the sketch that follows from it.

You need: source-free circuits for τ and the decaying exponential, and KVL and KCL from circuit elements and laws.

Complete response = natural + forced

A first-order circuit has one storage element, so after switching it obeys a first-order differential equation whose solution has two pieces. The forced (particular, steady-state) response follows the source. The natural (homogeneous, transient) response solves the equation with the source set to zero and always has the form A e−t/τ. The complete response is their sum:

  1. For a series RL circuit, KVL for t > 0 gives L di/dt + R i = Vs.
  2. Natural part: set the source to zero, L di/dt + R i = 0, whose solution is in = A e−Rt/L = A e−t/τ with τ = L/R.
  3. Forced part: a constant source forces a constant current; put di/dt = 0 to get if = Vs/R.
  4. Complete response: i = Vs/R + A e−t/τ. The initial current fixes A: A = i(0⁺) − Vs/R.

For a constant DC source, the forced response is simply the final value x(∞), because after the exponential dies nothing changes. The natural part must carry the difference between the initial value x(0⁺) and that final value. Hence the shortcut:

x(t) = x(∞) + [x(0⁺) − x(∞)] e−t/τ

x is any current or voltage for t ≥ 0⁺; τ = ReqC or L/Req, with Req the resistance seen by the storage element with independent sources killed.

At t = 0⁺ the exponential is 1 and the formula gives x(0⁺); as t → ∞ it gives x(∞). The bracket is the initial mismatch, and if it is zero nothing moves.

RC and RL step responses

For a series RC circuit with a DC voltage Vs switched on at t = 0, KVL gives Vs = R i + vC and i = C dvC/dt. With τ = RC,

vC(t) = Vs + [vC(0⁺) − Vs] e−t/τ, i(t) = [(Vs − vC(0⁺))/R] e−t/τ

For an uncharged capacitor, vC = Vs(1 − e−t/τ) and i = (Vs/R)e−t/τ. The capacitor voltage cannot jump, but its current can: it jumps to (Vs − vC(0⁺))/R at t = 0⁺ and decays to 0.

For a series RL circuit driven by Vs, KVL gives Vs = R i + L di/dt. With τ = L/R,

i(t) = I∞ + [i(0⁺) − I∞] e−t/τ, I∞ = Vs/R

I∞ is the final current. The inductor voltage can jump: vL(t) = [Vs − R i(0⁺)] e−t/τ. For zero initial current, i = (Vs/R)(1 − e−t/τ) and vL = Vse−t/τ.

Sketching, slope and pulses

To sketch any first-order answer, mark x(0⁺) and x(∞) on the vertical axis. Draw an exponential from the first to the second. If x(∞) > x(0⁺), the curve rises; if x(∞) < x(0⁺), it falls. The initial slope is:

dx/dt at t = 0⁺ = [x(∞) − x(0⁺)] / τ

positive for a rising answer, negative for a falling one. The tangent at t = 0 reaches the final value at t = τ; after 5τ about 0.7 % of the mismatch is left.

A pulse is two steps in the same circuit. First the source switches on at t = 0 and the answer runs toward its on-state final value. At t = Tp it switches off, and the same formula restarts with x(Tp⁺) as the new initial value and a new final value, often zero. If Tp is much larger than τ, the capacitor or inductor fully charges and then fully discharges; if Tp is much smaller than τ, the capacitor voltage barely moves, so almost the whole pulse appears across the resistor: vR is a near copy of the pulse and vC a small rounded ramp. A repeating pulse train builds or droops according to this ratio.

With two storage elements the single exponential becomes two exponentials or a damped sine; see second-order RLC circuits.

Worked example

Question. A 10 V DC source is switched at t = 0 across a series combination of R = 50 kΩ and C = 2 μF. The capacitor is uncharged before switching. Find τ, vC(t), i(t), and vC at t = 0.1 s.

  1. τ = RC = (50 × 10³ Ω)(2 × 10⁻⁶ F) = 0.1 s.
  2. For an uncharged capacitor, vC(0⁺) = 0 V. After the transient, the capacitor is open to DC, so vC(∞) = 10 V.
  3. Use vC(t) = vC(∞) + [vC(0⁺) − vC(∞)] e−t/τ = 10 + (0 − 10)e−10t = 10(1 − e−10t) V.
  4. i(t) = C dvC/dt = (2 × 10⁻⁶)(10)(10)e−10t A = 2 × 10⁻⁴ e−10t A = 0.2 e−10t mA.
  5. At t = 0.1 s, t/τ = 1. e−1 = 0.3679. vC(0.1) = 10(1 − 0.3679) = 6.321 V ≈ 6.32 V.

Answer. τ = 0.1 s, vC(t) = 10(1 − e−10t) V, i(t) = 0.2 e−10t mA, and vC(0.1 s) = 6.32 V.

Worked example 2

Question. For a long time a 12 V source has driven R₁ = 4 Ω, R₂ = 2 Ω and L = 0.6 H in series. At t = 0 a switch shorts R₂. Find i(t) and vL(t) for t ≥ 0, name the forced and natural parts, and find when the current reaches 2.9 A.

  1. Before the switch the inductor is a short: i(0⁻) = 12/(4 + 2) = 2 A, and continuity gives i(0⁺) = 2 A.
  2. After the switch only R₁ remains: i(∞) = 12/4 = 3 A and τ = L/R₁ = 0.6/4 = 0.15 s, so 1/τ = 6.67 s⁻¹.

Exam traps

  • Using the source value as the final value of every variable. In an RC step vC ends at Vs but iC ends at 0; in an RL step iL ends at Vs/R but vL ends at 0.
  • Putting x(0⁻) into the shortcut for a quantity that jumps at switching. Use x(0⁺); only vC and iL are guaranteed continuous.
  • Forgetting that τ uses the Thévenin resistance seen by C or L after switching, with sources killed.
  • Dropping the minus sign in the natural part. For a rising step x(0⁺) < x(∞), the natural term x(0⁺) − x(∞) is negative.
  • Treating a pulse as one step. A pulse has two switching instants; restart the formula at the second one.

5.4 Second-order RLC circuits: overdamped, critically damped, underdamped

A circuit with both an inductor and a capacitor has two energy stores, so after a switch it can do more than decay: it can overshoot and ring. The governing equation is second order, and its two roots decide the shape. Overdamped means two real roots and a slow creep; critically damped means one repeated root and the fastest rise without overshoot; underdamped means complex roots and a damped sine. This explains relay chatter, filter settling, motor drive overshoot and the ring you see on a lab oscilloscope.

You need: initial and final conditions for the t = 0 state and first-order step response for forced plus natural thinking.

The characteristic equation and the three shapes

A series RLC circuit with a DC step source has two energy stores, L and C. Write vC(t) for the capacitor voltage and Vs for the step height. After the switch the circuit satisfies LC vC'' + RC vC' + vC = Vs. Divide by LC. The forced part is Vs; the natural part is set by s² + (R/L)s + 1/(LC) = 0. Define α and ω₀ so the same equation reads s² + 2αs + ω₀² = 0.

s₁,₂ = −α ± √(α² − ω₀²)

s₁ and s₂ are the natural frequencies in s⁻¹; α is the damping coefficient in s⁻¹; ω₀ is the undamped natural frequency in rad/s.

  • α > ω₀: two different real negative roots. The circuit is overdamped; vC creeps to Vs with two decaying exponentials.
  • α = ω₀: one repeated real root −α. The circuit is critically damped; vC rises as fast as possible without overshoot.
  • α < ω₀: complex roots −α ± jω_d with ωd = √(ω₀² − α²). The circuit is underdamped; vC overshoots and rings at ωd.

Compare α with ω₀, or, if you prefer, α² with ω₀²; the two tests are the same because both numbers are positive. α = 12 s⁻¹ with ω₀ = 10 rad/s is overdamped: 12 > 10, and also 144 > 100. Equality is one point, not a range. Some syllabi write over-damped, critically damped and under-damped.

The same test is often written with the damping ratio ζ = α/ω₀, a pure number, which Figure 5.4.1 gives for each curve: ζ < 1 underdamped, ζ = 1 critical, ζ > 1 overdamped.

Series and parallel circuits

The same quadratic appears in series and parallel RLC, but α is read differently. For a series RLC with resistance R, inductance L and capacitance C, α = R/(2L). For a parallel RLC, α = 1/(2RC). In both, ω₀ = 1/√(LC). Use the series value only for series circuits and the parallel value only for parallel circuits.

αseries = R/(2L), αparallel = 1/(2RC), ω₀ = 1/√(LC)

αseries and αparallel are damping coefficients in s⁻¹; R is resistance in ohms; L is inductance in henries; C is capacitance in farads; ω₀ is in rad/s.

Write Vf for the final capacitor voltage; for the series voltage step Vf = Vs. With a DC step from zero initial energy, the full capacitor voltage takes one of three forms. Overdamped: vC(t) = Vf + A₁es₁t + A₂es₂t. Critically damped: vC(t) = Vf + (A₁ + A₂t)e−αt. Underdamped: vC(t) = Vf + e−αt(A₁cos ωd t + A₂sin ωd t). The constants A₁ and A₂ are fixed by vC(0) and vC'(0).

Initial conditions fix the constants

The initial conditions come from the two storage elements. Capacitor voltage cannot jump: vC(0⁺) = vC(0⁻). Inductor current cannot jump: iL(0⁺) = iL(0⁻). For a series circuit the current through C is the same as the inductor current, so vC'(0⁺) = iL(0⁺)/C. For a parallel circuit, find the capacitor current at 0⁺ by KCL, then use vC'(0⁺) = iC(0⁺)/C.

The shape has a physical meaning. In the underdamped case energy swaps between L and C, so vC overshoots Vf and rings; R removes energy each cycle, so the ring decays as e−αt. In the critically damped case the circuit returns to rest as fast as it can without a second overshoot. In the overdamped case one exponential dies quickly and the other lingers, so the rise is slow. A larger R in a series circuit increases α and pushes the same circuit from underdamped to critical to overdamped.

Use the response to choose R. A contactor coil or a filter that must not ring is made overdamped or critical. A radio front end may want a sharp underdamped response. The same three words describe mechanical suspension and control loops.

5.5 Natural frequency and damping ratio

Two numbers describe every second-order circuit. The natural frequency ωn (rad/s) says how fast the circuit would oscillate with no energy loss. The damping ratio ζ (no unit) says how strongly resistance kills that oscillation. From them you get the pole positions, the ringing frequency, the overshoot, the peak time and the settling time. Designers use this language for filters, speaker crossovers, control loops and power supplies, where ringing wastes time and can trip protection. You can tune L and C for ωn and R for ζ.

You need: second-order RLC circuits for the characteristic equation and the three damping cases.

Two numbers replace the component values

A second-order circuit has two storage elements, so its characteristic equation is quadratic. Write it with two numbers: the natural frequency ωn and the damping ratio ζ. The symbol s is the complex frequency in s⁻¹. ωn, in rad/s, is the frequency the circuit would oscillate at if no energy were lost. ζ, with no unit, is how strongly energy loss kills that oscillation.

s² + 2ζωn s + ωn² = 0

s is the complex frequency in s⁻¹; ωn is the natural frequency in rad/s; ζ is the damping ratio (no unit).

For a series RLC, ωn = 1/√(LC) and α = R/(2L), where α is the neper frequency in s⁻¹. For a parallel RLC, α = 1/(2RC). Then ζ = α/ωn. Do not use the series α in a parallel circuit; each α belongs to its own topology. ωn is the same number as the ω₀ of second-order RLC circuits; some syllabi call ζ the damping factor. A useful inverse is Q = 1/(2ζ); high Q means light damping and a long ring. These two numbers are not extra components; they are a relabelling of R, L and C.

Roots and the damped frequency

Solve the characteristic equation for the poles: s = −ζωn ± ωn√(ζ² − 1). The square root changes character at ζ = 1. For ζ < 1 it is imaginary, so the roots are complex conjugates. Rewrite them as s = −ζωn ± jω_n√(1 − ζ²). The real part −ζωn is negative; its size ζωn = α, in s⁻¹, is the decay rate of the envelope e−ζωn t. The imaginary part is the damped frequency ωd = ωn√(1 − ζ²) rad/s. The circuit rings at ωd, not at ωn. The two differ for every ζ > 0, but only slightly when ζ is small: at ζ = 0.1 the difference is 0.5 %.

s = −ζωn ± jω_d, ωd = ωn√(1 − ζ²)

s is the complex pole in s⁻¹; ζ is the damping ratio; ωn is the natural frequency in rad/s; ωd is the damped frequency in rad/s; j = √(−1).

At ζ = 1 the two roots meet at s = −ωn. For ζ > 1 both roots are real and negative. At ζ = 0 the roots sit on the jω axis and the oscillation never dies. A negative ζ puts a root in the right half-plane and the response grows. The common error is to write ωd = ωn√(ζ² − 1); that expression is imaginary for ζ < 1 and is not a measurable frequency.

Overshoot, speed and settling

Step-response specifications follow from the same two numbers. Percentage overshoot is the largest rise above the final value: overshoot = 100 e−πζ/√(1−ζ²) %. Peak time tp = π/ωd seconds is when the first peak occurs. The 2 % settling time ts ≈ 4/(ζωn) seconds is when the envelope has fallen to about 2 % of its starting size. These formulas assume the standard second-order low-pass response, such as the capacitor voltage of a series RLC, with zero initial energy and a step input. They are estimates when the circuit has zeros.

Mp = 100 e−πζ/√(1−ζ²) %, tp = π/ωd, ts ≈ 4/(ζωn)

Mp is percentage overshoot; ζ is the damping ratio; ωd is the damped frequency in rad/s; tp is peak time in s; ts is 2 % settling time in s; ωn is the natural frequency in rad/s.

Use them to choose L and C for ωn and R for ζ. In a series circuit, raising R raises α and ζ, so ringing dies faster but the response creeps once ζ passes 1. In a parallel circuit, raising R lowers α and ζ, so a large parallel resistor lets the tank ring longer. On the s-plane the poles lie on a circle of radius ωn and move along it as ζ changes; network functions reads that picture.

Question. A series RLC circuit has R = 20 Ω, L = 0.1 H and C = 10 μF. A 10 V step is applied at t = 0 with zero initial energy. Find ωn, α, ζ, ωd, percentage overshoot, peak time and 2 % settling time.

  1. Compute LC = (0.1 H)(10 × 10⁻⁶ F) = 1 × 10⁻⁶ s². Then ωn = 1/√(LC) = 1/√(1 × 10⁻⁶) = 1000 rad/s.

5.6 Transient response with sinusoidal excitation

A sine source switched on at t = 0 gives two currents at once: a steady sine set by the phasor answer, and a decaying exponential left over from the switching instant. The exponential is not optional. Its starting size K depends on where the sine was when the switch closed. This explains why a transformer or motor can draw a large first peak even though its steady current is modest. The same calculation covers relay coils, capacitor charging through a resistor, and any AC load energised by a switch.

You need: phasors and impedance from sinusoidal steady state, source-free circuits for the time constant τ, and first-order step response for natural plus forced response.

The complete response is a sine plus an exponential

Write the source as vs = Vm sin(ωt + φ). Vm is peak volts, ω is angular frequency in rad/s, t is seconds, φ is the phase at switching in radians. For a series RL circuit, the phasor impedance is Z = R + jωL. Its magnitude is |Z| = √(R² + (ωL)²), and its angle is θ = tan⁻¹(ωL/R). The steady-state current phasor is I = (Vm∠φ)/Z. In time form, iss(t) = Im sin(ωt + φ − θ), where Im = Vm/|Z|.

Inductor current cannot jump. The total current is i(t) = iss(t) + K e−t/τ, with τ = L/R in seconds. K is an ampere constant fixed by the initial current. At t = 0⁺, i(0⁺) = Im sin(φ − θ) + K, so K = i(0⁺) − Im sin(φ − θ). This is the whole method.

Series RC works the same way. Now Z = R − j/(ωC), θ = tan⁻¹(1/(ωRC)), and iss(t) = Im sin(ωt + φ + θ), because current leads voltage. The time constant is τ = RC. In RC the capacitor voltage cannot jump: vC(0⁺) = vC(0⁻). Get the initial current from KVL at t = 0⁺, i(0⁺) = (vs(0⁺) − vC(0⁺))/R, and then put it in K = i(0⁺) − Im sin(φ + θ).

i(t) = Im sin(ωt + φ − θ) + K e−t/τ, K = i(0⁺) − Im sin(φ − θ)

Im = Vm/|Z| is peak steady current in A; θ is the impedance angle; τ = L/R is the time constant in s; K is the initial offset in A. For RC replace −θ by +θ and τ by RC.

Where the offset comes from: the switching instant

At the switch, the sine part has some value. If that value is not equal to the actual initial current, the difference must be supplied by K e−t/τ. So the size of K depends on φ, the source phase at switching. Some syllabi call this transient response under AC excitation.

The largest offset occurs when iss(0) is at a peak, ±Im. For a mainly resistive RL circuit this is close to switching at the source peak. For a purely inductive circuit it is switching at the source zero crossing, because current lags by 90°. The gentlest case is iss(0) = 0, which gives K = 0 and no transient. Keep the general rule: compare iss(0) with i(0⁺), not the source value.

After 5τ the exponential is below 1 % of K and is treated as gone; in the example below τ = 0.25 s, so the transient is over in about 1.25 s.

An RLC circuit under AC behaves in the same pattern: the steady state is still a phasor, and the transient is a second-order solution from second-order RLC circuits. It may ring instead of decaying as one exponential.

Reading the plotted waveform

Switching at the worst instant can nearly double the first peak, but only when τ is long compared with the period, so that K e−t/τ has hardly decayed when the sine reaches its first peak. In the worked example it has: the first steady peak comes at t = 0.74 s, where 1.2e−4 × 0.74 = 0.06 A, so the first peak is only about 2.06 A. The total still starts at i(0⁺) = 0 A. Fuses react to the first peak, so the switching instant matters in real equipment.

5.7 Laplace transform and s-domain circuit analysis

Every transient so far has been a differential equation with an initial condition bolted on. The Laplace transform removes both difficulties at once: it turns derivatives into multiplication by s, and it puts the initial condition into the transformed circuit as an extra source. What is left is a network of impedances in the complex variable s, solved with the same nodal and mesh analysis used for resistive networks, and the inverse transform at the end returns the time answer. Filter design, control loops and the AC analysis of circuit simulators all work in s.

You need: initial and final conditions for iL(0⁻) and vC(0⁻), and first-order step response for the time-domain answers you will check against.

The one-sided transform and the derivative rule

The Laplace transform converts a time function f(t) into a function of the complex variable s = σ + jω: F(s) = ∫ f(t) e−st dt, taken from t = 0⁻ to ∞. The lower limit is 0⁻, the instant just before switching, so a jump at t = 0 lies inside the integral. That single choice is why initial conditions survive as ordinary numbers instead of constants of integration.

Differentiation becomes multiplication. Apply the rule twice to the RLC loop equation and a second-order differential equation collapses into a quadratic in s — the same characteristic equation that second-order RLC circuits solved by guessing est.

F(s) is the transform of f(t); f(0⁻) is the value just before the switch; f′(0⁻) is the value of its derivative just before the switch.

Two theorems check the two ends of an answer without inverting it:

the first is the initial value theorem; the second, the final value theorem, holds only when every pole of sF(s) has a negative real part, apart from at most one pole at s = 0. An undamped LC circuit has poles at ±jω, so its final value does not exist.

s-domain element models: memory becomes a source

A resistor has no memory: v = Ri holds at every instant, so V(s) = R I(s). The inductor and capacitor store energy, and their derivative relations pull the stored value into the transform as a source.

Inductor: v = L di/dt, so V(s) = sL I(s) − L iL(0⁻). Model it as an impedance sL in series with a voltage source of value L iL(0⁻), poled to push current the way the initial current was already flowing.

Capacitor: i = C dv/dt, so I(s) = sC V(s) − C vC(0⁻), which rearranges to V(s) = (1/sC) I(s) + vC(0⁻)/s. Model it as an impedance 1/sC in series with a source vC(0⁻)/s. Note the 1/s: a stored 4 V becomes 4/s, because in the transformed picture that source must act for all time.

Exam traps

  • Sign and scale of the initial-condition source. An inductor needs L iL(0⁻) volts and a capacitor needs vC(0⁻)/s volts in a series branch. Writing C vC(0⁻) there is the parallel-form current source in the wrong place. The slip is silent and surfaces only at t = 0⁺.
  • Using f(0⁺) instead of f(0⁻) in the derivative rule. The theorem is stated with 0⁻ precisely because inductor current and capacitor voltage cannot jump; any other quantity can, and then the two values differ.
  • Applying the final value theorem to a circuit that never settles. If F(s) has a pole in the right half plane, or a pair on the jω axis as in an undamped LC, the limit does not exist and the 'steady state' you quote is fiction.

5.8 Network functions, transfer functions, poles and zeros

A network function is a ratio of one response transform to one excitation transform. Kill every independent source, set all initial energy to zero, and the circuit becomes a pure ratio H(s). That single function carries the poles that decide the natural response and the zeros that shape how the steady-state response rises or falls. Designers use it to read stability from a pole-zero plot, to get the impulse response by inverse transform, and to find the sinusoidal steady-state gain as H(jω). Filters, amplifiers and control loops are all specified this way.

You need: Laplace analysis for s-domain algebra, and series resonance and Q from resonance and coupled circuits.

From a circuit to H(s)

Write the circuit in the s-domain with the Laplace transform. Element impedances become: R stays R, L becomes sL, C becomes 1/(sC). Set every independent source to zero: voltage sources become short circuits, current sources become open circuits. Set every initial inductor current and initial capacitor voltage to zero. Now excite one port with a test source X(s) and compute the response Y(s) at the chosen port.

The network function is H(s) = Y(s)/X(s). X(s) is the input transform; Y(s) is the output transform; H(s) has units of output per input. If input and output are at the same port, H is a driving-point function, such as input impedance Z(s) = V(s)/I(s). If they are at different ports, H is a transfer function, such as voltage gain V₂(s)/V₁(s).

H(s) = Y(s)/X(s) = N(s)/D(s)

N(s) and D(s) are polynomials in s with real coefficients; N(s) is the numerator, D(s) is the denominator after cancelling common factors.

The denominator D(s) is the characteristic polynomial of the circuit. Its roots are the poles: they are the natural frequencies of the circuit with its sources killed, so many network functions of one circuit share them. The roots of the numerator N(s) are the zeros, and they depend on both where the input is applied and where the output is taken.

  1. Draw the s-domain circuit with all independent sources killed and zero initial conditions.
  2. Apply a test source X(s) at the input port.
  3. Solve for the response Y(s) at the output port.
  4. Form H(s) = Y(s)/X(s), then factor N(s) and D(s).

Poles, zeros and the s-plane

A pole is a value of s that makes H(s) infinite. A zero is a value of s that makes H(s) zero. Write s = σ + jω. σ is the real part, in s⁻¹; ω is the imaginary part, in rad/s; j is the imaginary unit. Mark poles by × and zeros by ○ in the complex s-plane. The real part σ sets growth or decay; the imaginary part ω sets oscillation.

H(s) = K (s − z₁)(s − z₂)… / ((s − p₁)(s − p₂)…)

K is a real gain; z₁, z₂, … are zeros; p₁, p₂, … are poles. A repeated pole or zero is counted by its multiplicity.

With zero initial conditions, the impulse response is h(t) = L⁻¹{H(s)}. Each simple pole pᵢ contributes a term Aᵢ epᵢt. A pole with negative real part gives a decaying term. A pole on the jω axis gives a constant-amplitude sinusoid. A pole with positive real part gives growth. So stability needs all poles strictly in the left half plane.

Put s = jω to get the sinusoidal steady-state ratio. |H(jω)| is the gain at frequency ω, and ∠H(jω) is the phase shift. If a pole sits close to the jω axis, the denominator becomes small near that frequency and |H(jω)| peaks. This is the resonance peak of a high-Q circuit: small damping means a tall, narrow peak.

|H(jω)| = |N(jω)| / |D(jω)|

N(jω) and D(jω) are the numerator and denominator evaluated at s = jω; | | means magnitude, and ω is in rad/s.

Stability and reading a pole-zero plot

Only poles decide stability. Zeros change the size and phase of the response but cannot turn a growing exponential into a decaying one. A simple pole at s = 0 puts a constant, non-decaying term in the impulse response, so the step response ramps without limit; a pair at s = ±jω₀ is a sustained oscillation; a pole at s = +2 gives e2t. Only a zero exactly on a pole removes that pole's term. A zero that is merely near it leaves a small residue, and a slow pole still sets the long tail of the response.

For a second-order low-pass H(s) = ωn² / (s² + 2ζωn s + ωn²), poles are s = −ζωn ± jω_n√(1 − ζ²) for 0 < ζ < 1. The damping ratio ζ sets the real part; the natural frequency ωn sets the distance from the origin. For ζ < 1/√2 the magnitude has a peak at ωr = ωn√(1 − 2ζ²) with peak 1/[2ζ√(1 − ζ²)].

Worked example

Exam traps

  • Stability comes only from poles. A zero in the right half plane does not make an unstable circuit; a pole in the right half plane does.
  • A pole on the jω axis is marginal, not stable. It gives sustained oscillation; any small positive real part grows without limit.
  • Do not forget to kill sources and zero initial conditions before forming H(s). A live source or an initial capacitor voltage adds terms that are not part of H(s).
  • H(jω) is not H(s) with s replaced by ω. Put s = jω, including the j. Dropping j gives the wrong sign and phase.
  • Cancelling a pole with a zero hides it mathematically only; exact cancellation is not robust in real components.

5.9 State-variable analysis of circuits

Some circuits have many storage elements, and solving each differential equation separately gets messy. State-variable analysis collects the inductor currents and capacitor voltages into one vector x. The circuit then becomes two matrix equations, ẋ = Ax + Bu and y = Cx + Du. The eigenvalues of A are the natural frequencies, so you know whether the circuit rings without solving anything. Only a few universities teach this chapter. Circuit simulators and control engineers use it daily.

You need: second-order RLC circuits for the characteristic equation, network functions for poles, and basic matrix algebra.

What the state vector holds

State variables are the quantities that cannot jump when a switch moves: inductor currents iL and capacitor voltages vC (see initial and final conditions). Stack them as x₁, x₂, … into the state vector x. Usually there is one state per storage element; a loop made only of capacitors (and voltage sources), or a cut-set made only of inductors (and current sources), makes one of them dependent and lowers the count.

ẋ = Ax + Bu

x is the n × 1 state vector; u holds the m source values; A is n × n and B is n × m.

y = Cx + Du

y holds the p chosen outputs; C is p × n. D is p × m and carries the direct path from the sources to the outputs: it is nonzero only when an output depends on a source at the same instant.

  1. Label each independent iL and vC as a state.
  2. Write KVL round a loop containing each inductor and KCL at a node of each capacitor.
  3. Rearrange until each derivative stands alone, with only states and sources on the right.
  4. Collect the coefficients into A and B.
  5. Write each output in terms of states and sources to read C and D.

Eigenvalues give the natural frequencies

The eigenvalues of A are the natural frequencies of the circuit, the roots of

det(sI − A) = 0

I is the identity matrix of the same size as A.

For a series RLC this is the characteristic polynomial s² + (R/L)s + 1/(LC) of second-order RLC circuits, so the eigenvalues show whether the circuit rings without solving any differential equation. Real negative roots decay; a pair −σ ± jω_d rings at ωd while decaying; a passive RLC never gives a positive real part. For a 2 × 2 A, match det(sI − A) with s² + 2ζωn s + ωn² to read ωn and ζ.

Reading the state model

The source enters only the inductor equation, through B; the two states feed each other through A; and the output picks out vC through C. Each state is the integral of its derivative, which is why x cannot jump. With a DC input the final state follows from ẋ = 0, that is Ax(∞) + Bu = 0, without solving anything else.

  • Making a resistor voltage or a capacitor current a state. Only iL and vC store energy and stay continuous.
  • Sign slips in A: in the series RLC the iL row has −R/L on the diagonal and −1/L against vC.
  • Writing 1 instead of 1/L in B. A voltage source enters the inductor equation divided by L.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. State-space matrices A, B, C, D are the standard language of control engineering; search state-space representation and controllability and observability. Circuit simulators assemble the same matrices internally, which is why SPICE handles thousands of nodes. Power-electronics engineers average the equations over a switching cycle, called state-space averaging. A later course solves ẋ = Ax + Bu with the state-transition matrix eAt.

Takeaways

  1. Inductor current and capacitor voltage are the two quantities that cannot jump, so the circuit state at t = 0⁻ decides the whole answer after switching.
  2. With no source, energy drains as a pure exponential e−t/τ whose speed is fixed only by R and the storage element.
  3. Every first-order answer is final value plus (initial value minus final value) times e−t/τ; find the two end values and τ and the work is done.
  4. Two storage elements give a quadratic characteristic equation, and its two roots decide whether the answer creeps, settles cleanly, or rings.
  5. The pair ωn and ζ replaces R, L and C as the description of a second-order circuit: ωn sets how fast it wants to oscillate, ζ sets how fast the oscillation is killed.
  6. With a sine source the complete response is a steady-state sine plus a decaying exponential whose size depends on the instant of switching.
  7. The Laplace transform turns the differential equation into algebra and puts the initial conditions inside the circuit as extra sources.
  8. With sources killed and zero initial energy the circuit is a single ratio H(s); its poles give the natural response and its zeros shape the steady-state response.
  9. State variables turn any circuit into ẋ = Ax + Bu and y = Cx + Du, where the eigenvalues of A are the natural frequencies.

Model answer.

  1. Definition. A network function is H(s) = Y(s)/X(s): the transform of one chosen response divided by the transform of one excitation, with all other independent sources killed and all initial conditions zero. If input and output share a port it is a driving-point function; otherwise a transfer function.
  2. H(s). Voltage division in the s-domain:
    H(s) = R / (R + sL + 1/(sC)) = R s C / (LC s² + RC s + 1)
     = (R/L) s / (s² + (R/L) s + 1/(LC))
     = 2s / (s² + 2s + 5)
  3. Zeros and poles. Numerator 2s: one zero at s = 0. Denominator s² + 2s + 5 = (s + 1)² + 4: poles at s = −1 ± j2.
  4. Pole-zero plot. × at −1 + j2 and −1 − j2, ○ at the origin.
  5. Stability. Both poles have real part −1 < 0, so every natural term decays: the circuit is stable. The zero at the origin does not affect stability; it only says the circuit passes no DC (the capacitor blocks it).

Glossary

Characteristic equation
The polynomial equation whose roots are the natural frequencies of the circuit.
Characteristic root
A value of s that makes the natural response possible; written s₁ and s₂ for a second-order circuit.
Critically damped
The boundary case with equal real roots; the fastest settling without overshoot.
Damped frequency ωd
The actual oscillation frequency ωn√(1 − ζ²) when the circuit is underdamped.
Damping
The loss of oscillatory energy caused by resistance, which makes a second-order response settle.
Damping ratio ζ
The dimensionless number α/ωn that measures how strongly oscillation is suppressed.
Exponential decay
A fall of the form Ae−t/τ, in which equal time steps give equal fractional drops.
Forced response
The response forced by the source, equal to the steady state for DC and sine inputs.
Impulse response
The output when the input is a unit impulse; it is the inverse Laplace transform of H(s).
Inverse Laplace transform
The step that recovers f(t) from F(s), usually by partial fractions and a table.
Laplace transform
A rule that maps a time function f(t) to a complex-frequency function F(s).
Natural frequency ωn
The frequency in rad/s at which the circuit would oscillate with no resistance.
Natural response
The response produced by the circuit's own poles with independent sources set to zero.
Overdamped
A second-order case with two distinct real roots; the response settles without oscillating.
Partial fraction expansion
Splitting a ratio of polynomials into simple terms whose inverse transforms are known.
Pole
A value of s where the transfer function becomes infinite and the natural response lives.
s-plane
The complex plane with σ on the horizontal axis and jω on the vertical axis, used to plot poles and zeros.
Source-free response
The response of a circuit after its source is removed, driven only by stored energy.
State variable
One of the minimum set of quantities, here iL and vC, that fixes the future behaviour of the circuit.
Steady state
The condition reached after the transient has vanished, when every quantity repeats or stays constant.
Switching
An abrupt change in the circuit at t = 0, usually by closing or opening a switch.
Time constant
The value τ in e−t/τ; the time for the response to fall to 36.8% of its starting value.
Transfer function
The ratio Y(s)/X(s) of output to input transform for zero initial energy.
Transient
The part of a response that dies away with time after a change in the circuit.
Underdamped
A second-order case with complex roots; the response oscillates while it decays.
Zero
A value of s where the transfer function becomes zero, blocking transmission at that frequency.
