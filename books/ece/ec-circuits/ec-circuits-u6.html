6.1 Two-port network concept and terminology

A two-port network is a circuit seen only through two pairs of terminals: one pair where the signal goes in and one pair where it comes out. Each pair is a port, so the current that enters one terminal of the pair leaves by the other. The inside is hidden, and the outside is described by four quantities, V₁, I₁, V₂ and I₂, linked by two equations. Transistor models, filters, transformers and transmission lines are all handled this way, because only their input and output behaviour matters to the rest of the circuit.

You need Kirchhoff's laws, series and parallel resistance, and the voltage and current division rules.

Ports and terminal pairs

A terminal is a point where an outside wire connects. A port is a pair of terminals with one condition: the current entering one terminal equals the current leaving the other. Then the pair carries one voltage (across it) and one current (through it). This is called the port condition.

A one-port has one such pair; a resistor, or any network judged by its input impedance alone, is a one-port. A two-port has two pairs, labelled 1–1′ (port 1, the input) and 2–2′ (port 2, the output). A multi-port has three or more ports, such as a three-winding transformer. Some syllabi call a two-port a four-terminal network or a quadripole.

Many two-ports have only three terminals, because terminals 1′ and 2′ are the same wire. A T network and a transistor in common-emitter use are examples: the common terminal returns I₁ from port 1 and I₂ from port 2, so both port conditions still hold. The port condition fails only when an outside path joins the two ports, for example a wire from terminal 1 to terminal 2′ in another part of the circuit; then a terminal pair no longer carries equal and opposite currents and the two-port description breaks.

To describe a two-port by four fixed constants it must be linear (no powers or products of the port variables), contain no independent sources, and start with zero stored energy in its inductors and capacitors. Dependent sources are allowed; they are how a transistor model gets its gain.

Sign convention and power

This book uses the convention found in most textbooks. V₁ is the voltage of terminal 1 with respect to 1′, and I₁ is the current entering terminal 1. V₂ is the voltage of terminal 2 with respect to 2′, and I₂ is the current entering terminal 2. Both currents are drawn into the network. The arrows define the positive direction; the real current may flow the other way, and then the value is negative.

With both currents entering, the power entering the two-port from outside is

P = V₁I₁ + V₂I₂

where P in watts is positive when the network absorbs power and negative when it delivers power to the outside (a passive network can never give P < 0).

A load ZL at port 2 carries the current that leaves terminal 2, which is −I₂. Ohm's law on the load therefore reads V₂ = −I₂ZL. This one minus sign appears in every terminated two-port calculation of this unit.

Four variables, two equations

A linear two-port with no internal sources fixes two linear relations among V₁, I₁, V₂ and I₂. So only two of the four are free: pick any two as the inputs and the network decides the other two. Each choice of the free pair gives one parameter set of four constants:

Free (independent) pairFound from themParameter set
I₁, I₂V₁, V₂Z (impedance)
V₁, V₂I₁, I₂Y (admittance)
I₁, V₂V₁, I₂h (hybrid)
V₁, I₂I₁, V₂g (inverse hybrid)
V₂, −I₂V₁, I₁ABCD (transmission)

When a source and a load are connected, the outside circuit adds two more equations (one at each port), and the four port variables become definite numbers, as the worked example shows.

Worked example

Question. A T network has a series arm Z₁ = 2 Ω from terminal 1 to a centre node, a series arm Z₂ = 3 Ω from the centre node to terminal 2, and a shunt arm Z₃ = 4 Ω from the centre node to the common terminal 1′–2′. A 12 V source is connected at port 1 and a 1 Ω load at port 2. Find V₁, I₁, V₂ and I₂ with the standard convention, and the power entering the two-port. Check the power by adding the resistor losses.

  1. Seen from the centre node, the branch towards port 2 is Z₂ + ZL = 3 + 1 = 4 Ω. It is in parallel with Z₃ = 4 Ω: 4 × 4/(4 + 4) = 2 Ω.
  2. Input resistance: Rin = Z₁ + 2 = 2 + 2 = 4 Ω. The source fixes V₁ = 12 V, so I₁ = 12/4 = 3 A (entering terminal 1, so positive).
  3. Centre-node voltage: 12 − 3 × 2 = 6 V. The current down Z₃ is 6/4 = 1.5 A; the current towards the load is 6/4 = 1.5 A. KCL at the centre: 3 = 1.5 + 1.5 ✓.
  4. The load current 1.5 A leaves terminal 2, so V₂ = 1.5 × 1 = 1.5 V and I₂ = −1.5 A (it enters terminal 2 as −1.5 A). Check V₂ = −I₂ZL = −(−1.5)(1) = 1.5 V ✓.
  5. Power entering the two-port: P = V₁I₁ + V₂I₂ = 12 × 3 + 1.5 × (−1.5) = 36 − 2.25 = 33.75 W.
  6. Resistor losses: Z₁: 3² × 2 = 18 W; Z₃: 1.5² × 4 = 9 W; Z₂: 1.5² × 3 = 6.75 W. Total 18 + 9 + 6.75 = 33.75 W ✓. The source gives 36 W; 33.75 W is lost inside and the 2.25 W that leaves port 2 heats the load.

Answer. V₁ = 12 V, I₁ = 3 A, V₂ = 1.5 V, I₂ = −1.5 A; the two-port absorbs P = 33.75 W, equal to its resistor losses, and passes 2.25 W to the load.

Exam traps

  • I₂ is defined entering port 2. The current a load draws is −I₂, so a load at port 2 gives V₂ = −I₂ZL, not +I₂ZL. In the worked example I₂ = −1.5 A, and writing +1.5 A makes P come out 38.25 W, more than the source gives.
  • A port is a pair of terminals with equal and opposite currents, not any two terminals. If an outside wire joins the input side to the output side, the pair condition fails and the parameters no longer describe the network.
  • A three-terminal network (common 1′–2′, as in a T network or a transistor) is a valid two-port. Its common terminal carries I₁ + I₂, and that is allowed.
  • The four port variables are not four free inputs. A linear two-port fixes two of them once the other two are chosen; a question that gives three port variables can be checked for consistency.
  • Fixed parameters need a linear network with no independent sources inside and zero initial stored energy. Dependent sources are fine.

With the arrow leaving, the measured current is I₂′ = −I₂, so the power entering is V₁I₁ − V₂I₂′. Using +V₂I₂′ adds the load power instead of subtracting it: in the worked example it gives 38.25 W instead of 33.75 W.

Analyze Can a network that contains an independent source be described by four fixed parameters? Explain. 3 marks

No. The source adds a constant term to each port equation (V₁ = z₁₁I₁ + z₁₂I₂ + V₀₁, for example), so the port variables are no longer proportional to the free pair. It needs four parameters plus two source terms, as in a Thévenin model at each port.

6.2 Impedance (Z) and admittance (Y) parameters

Z parameters describe a two-port by writing its two port voltages in terms of its two port currents. Y parameters do the opposite: they write the two port currents in terms of the two port voltages. The Z set is measured with one port open, the Y set with one port shorted, and for the same network the Y matrix is the inverse of the Z matrix. Z parameters are the natural choice when two-ports are joined in series, Y parameters when they are joined in parallel, and both are the starting point for every other parameter set.

You need the port variables and the sign convention from two-port concept and terminology, impedance and admittance of R, L and C, and the inverse of a 2 × 2 matrix.

Open-circuit impedance (Z) parameters

Take the two port currents I₁ and I₂ as the free variables. Because the network is linear, each port voltage is a weighted sum of the two currents:

V₁ = z₁₁I₁ + z₁₂I₂, V₂ = z₂₁I₁ + z₂₂I₂

where z₁₁, z₁₂, z₂₁ and z₂₂ are the Z parameters, all in ohms; in matrix form [V] = [Z][I].

To isolate one parameter, make the other current zero by leaving that port open. With port 2 open (I₂ = 0), z₁₁ = V₁/I₁ is the input impedance and z₂₁ = V₂/I₁ is the forward transfer impedance. With port 1 open (I₁ = 0), z₂₂ = V₂/I₂ is the output impedance and z₁₂ = V₁/I₂ is the reverse transfer impedance. Every Z test uses an open circuit, so they are called open-circuit impedance parameters.

The subscripts follow one rule: the first index is the port where the response is read, the second is the port that is driven.

Short-circuit admittance (Y) parameters

Now take V₁ and V₂ as the free variables:

I₁ = y₁₁V₁ + y₁₂V₂, I₂ = y₂₁V₁ + y₂₂V₂

where the y parameters are in siemens (S); in matrix form [I] = [Y][V].

To make a voltage zero, short that port. With port 2 shorted (V₂ = 0), y₁₁ = I₁/V₁ is the input admittance and y₂₁ = I₂/V₁ is the forward transfer admittance. With port 1 shorted (V₁ = 0), y₂₂ = I₂/V₂ and y₁₂ = I₁/V₂. These are the short-circuit admittance parameters. In a passive network y₂₁ is usually negative: with port 2 shorted, current flows out of terminal 2 into the short, and I₂ is defined entering.

Both sets describe the same box. Substituting [V] = [Z][I] into [I] = [Y][V] gives [Y] = [Z]⁻¹, so

y₁₁ = z₂₂/Δz,  y₁₂ = −z₁₂/Δz,  y₂₁ = −z₂₁/Δz,  y₂₂ = z₁₁/Δz

where Δz = z₁₁z₂₂ − z₁₂z₂₁ is the determinant of [Z], in Ω². The same rule run backwards gives z₁₁ = y₂₂/Δy, z₁₂ = −y₁₂/Δy, z₂₁ = −y₂₁/Δy, z₂₂ = y₁₁/Δy with Δy = y₁₁y₂₂ − y₁₂y₂₁ in S².

The diagonal terms swap places, and both off-diagonal terms change sign. z₁₂ is not 1/y₁₂: the inverse of a matrix is not the matrix of reciprocals.

Which set exists, and which to use

  • A T network (series arms Z₁, Z₂, shunt arm Z₃) gives its Z parameters by inspection: z₁₁ = Z₁ + Z₃, z₂₂ = Z₂ + Z₃, z₁₂ = z₂₁ = Z₃. A π network (shunt arms YA, YC, series arm YB) gives its Y parameters by inspection: y₁₁ = YA + YB, y₂₂ = YB + YC, y₁₂ = y₂₁ = −YB.
  • If Δz = 0 no Y matrix exists. A lone series impedance Z between terminals 1 and 2 has no Z matrix but has y₁₁ = y₂₂ = 1/Z, y₁₂ = y₂₁ = −1/Z; a lone shunt impedance has a Z matrix (all four entries Z) but no Y matrix; an ideal transformer has neither. The ABCD parameters handle all three.
  • Z suits series connections and Y suits parallel connections; see interconnection of two-ports. Equal off-diagonal terms (z₁₂ = z₂₁) mark a reciprocal network; see symmetry and reciprocity.

Worked example

Question. A T network has series arms Z₁ = 2 Ω (port 1 side) and Z₂ = 3 Ω (port 2 side) and a shunt arm Z₃ = 4 Ω to the common terminal. (a) Find the Z parameters from the open-circuit tests. (b) Find Δz and the Y parameters. (c) Check y₁₁ and y₂₁ directly with port 2 shorted and 1 V applied at port 1.

  1. Port 2 open (I₂ = 0): no current in Z₂, so I₁ flows through Z₁ and Z₃. V₁ = I₁(2 + 4) = 6I₁, so z₁₁ = 6 Ω. V₂ is the drop across Z₃ (no drop on the idle Z₂): V₂ = 4I₁, so z₂₁ = 4 Ω.
  2. Port 1 open (I₁ = 0): I₂ flows through Z₂ and Z₃. V₂ = I₂(3 + 4) = 7I₂, so z₂₂ = 7 Ω, and V₁ = 4I₂, so z₁₂ = 4 Ω.
  3. Δz = z₁₁z₂₂ − z₁₂z₂₁ = 6 × 7 − 4 × 4 = 42 − 16 = 26 Ω².
  4. y₁₁ = z₂₂/Δz = 7/26 = 0.2692 S; y₁₂ = y₂₁ = −4/26 = −0.1538 S; y₂₂ = z₁₁/Δz = 6/26 = 0.2308 S.
  5. Direct check: with port 2 shorted, Z₂ ∥ Z₃ = 3 × 4/7 = 1.7143 Ω, so the input resistance is 2 + 1.7143 = 3.7143 Ω and I₁ = 1/3.7143 = 0.2692 A. Then y₁₁ = I₁/V₁ = 0.2692 S ✓.
  6. Centre-node voltage = 1 − 2 × 0.2692 = 0.4615 V. The current through Z₂ into the short is 0.4615/3 = 0.1538 A, leaving terminal 2, so I₂ = −0.1538 A and y₂₁ = I₂/V₁ = −0.1538 S ✓.

Answer. z₁₁ = 6 Ω, z₁₂ = z₂₁ = 4 Ω, z₂₂ = 7 Ω; Δz = 26 Ω²; y₁₁ = 0.2692 S, y₁₂ = y₂₁ = −0.1538 S, y₂₂ = 0.2308 S, confirmed by the short-circuit test.

Worked example 2

Question. A π network has a 2 Ω shunt resistor across port 1, a 4 Ω series resistor between terminals 1 and 2, and a 5 Ω shunt resistor across port 2. Find its Y parameters by inspection, then its z₁₁ and z₂₂, and check them by series-parallel reduction.

  1. Admittances: YA = 1/2 = 0.5 S, YB = 1/4 = 0.25 S, YC = 1/5 = 0.2 S.
  2. y₁₁ = YA + YB = 0.75 S; y₂₂ = YB + YC = 0.45 S; y₁₂ = y₂₁ = −YB = −0.25 S.
  3. Δy = y₁₁y₂₂ − y₁₂y₂₁ = 0.75 × 0.45 − 0.0625 = 0.3375 − 0.0625 = 0.275 S².
  4. z₁₁ = y₂₂/Δy = 0.45/0.275 = 1.6364 Ω and z₂₂ = y₁₁/Δy = 0.75/0.275 = 2.7273 Ω.
  5. Check z₁₁: with port 2 open, port 1 sees 2 Ω in parallel with (4 + 5) Ω: 2 × 9/11 = 1.6364 Ω ✓. Check z₂₂: port 2 sees 5 Ω in parallel with (4 + 2) Ω: 30/11 = 2.7273 Ω ✓.

Answer. y₁₁ = 0.75 S, y₁₂ = y₂₁ = −0.25 S, y₂₂ = 0.45 S; z₁₁ = 1.6364 Ω and z₂₂ = 2.7273 Ω, matching the direct reductions.

Exam traps

  • z₁₁ is measured with port 2 open, not shorted. For the T network, shorting port 2 gives 3.7143 Ω, not z₁₁ = 6 Ω; 3.7143 Ω is 1/y₁₁.
  • z₁₂ is not 1/y₁₂. Here z₁₂ = 4 Ω but 1/y₁₂ = 1/(−0.1538) = −6.5 Ω. Invert the whole matrix.
  • The off-diagonal terms change sign in the inversion: y₁₂ = −z₁₂/Δz. Writing +z₁₂/Δz gives y₂₁ = +0.1538 S, which says current enters terminal 2 from the short, which is impossible for a passive network driven at port 1.
  • Units: z in ohms, y in siemens, Δz in Ω², Δy in S². A determinant written in plain ohms is marked wrong.
  • Not every network has both sets: a lone series element has no Z matrix and a lone shunt element has no Y matrix.

6.3 Hybrid (h) and inverse hybrid (g) parameters

Hybrid (h) parameters take the input current I₁ and the output voltage V₂ as the free variables, and give the input voltage V₁ and the output current I₂. One free variable is a current and the other a voltage, so the four constants have mixed units: one in ohms, one in siemens and two plain ratios. That mix fits a transistor amplifier, whose input is driven by a base current and whose output sits across a collector voltage, which is why transistor data sheets quote h parameters. The inverse hybrid (g) set swaps the roles and takes V₁ and I₂ as the free pair.

You need the port variables and their directions, Z and Y parameters and dependent sources.

The hybrid equations

V₁ = h₁₁I₁ + h₁₂V₂, I₂ = h₂₁I₁ + h₂₂V₂

where both currents enter their ports, as everywhere in this unit; in matrix form [V₁; I₂] = [h][I₁; V₂].

The equations draw a simple model. The first is KVL at the input: a resistance h₁₁ carrying I₁, in series with a dependent voltage source h₁₂V₂ fed back from the output. The second is KCL at the output: a dependent current source h₂₁I₁ in parallel with a conductance h₂₂. This is the h-parameter equivalent circuit of the figure, and it is the small-signal model of a bipolar transistor.

Measuring each h parameter

Each parameter is a ratio with one free variable set to zero: V₂ = 0 means the output is shorted, I₁ = 0 means the input is open.

ParameterRatioConditionNameUnit
h₁₁V₁/I₁V₂ = 0 (output short)input impedanceΩ
h₂₁I₂/I₁V₂ = 0 (output short)forward current gainnone
h₁₂V₁/V₂I₁ = 0 (input open)reverse voltage gainnone
h₂₂I₂/V₂I₁ = 0 (input open)output admittanceS

In transistor work the four numbers carry letter names: hi (input), hr (reverse), hf (forward) and ho (output), with a second letter for the configuration, so hie, hre, hfe, hoe in common emitter. With I₂ entering the collector, hfe = β is positive. hre is small, of the order of 10⁻⁴, and is often neglected.

From the Z equations, short-circuiting the output (V₂ = 0) gives I₂ = −(z₂₁/z₂₂)I₁ and V₁ = (Δz/z₂₂)I₁; opening the input gives V₁ = (z₁₂/z₂₂)V₂ and I₂ = V₂/z₂₂. So h₁₁ = Δz/z₂₂, h₁₂ = z₁₂/z₂₂, h₂₁ = −z₂₁/z₂₂ and h₂₂ = 1/z₂₂. For a passive network h₁₂ is positive and h₂₁ negative, and a reciprocal network has h₁₂ = −h₂₁.

Inverse hybrid (g) parameters

I₁ = g₁₁V₁ + g₁₂I₂, V₂ = g₂₁V₁ + g₂₂I₂

where g₁₁ = I₁/V₁ with I₂ = 0 (siemens), g₁₂ = I₁/I₂ with V₁ = 0, g₂₁ = V₂/V₁ with I₂ = 0, and g₂₂ = V₂/I₂ with V₁ = 0 (ohms).

The g matrix is the inverse of the h matrix, [g] = [h]⁻¹:

g₁₁ = h₂₂/Δh,  g₁₂ = −h₁₂/Δh,  g₂₁ = −h₂₁/Δh,  g₂₂ = h₁₁/Δh

where Δh = h₁₁h₂₂ − h₁₂h₂₁ is dimensionless (ohms × siemens). The same pattern with g and h swapped gives h from g.

The g set suits a voltage amplifier model: g₂₁ is the open-circuit voltage gain, as in an op-amp stage. g₂₂ is an impedance and h₂₂ an admittance; they are not reciprocals of each other, because only whole matrices invert.

Worked example

Question. A transistor at its bias point has h₁₁ = 1 kΩ, h₁₂ = 0.002, h₂₁ = 50 and h₂₂ = 20 μS. The input current is I₁ = 10 μA and the output port sits at V₂ = −5 V. Find V₁ and I₂, and the load resistance that produces this V₂.

  1. Base units: h₁₁ = 1000 Ω, h₂₂ = 20 × 10⁻⁶ S, I₁ = 10 × 10⁻⁶ A, V₂ = −5 V.
  2. Input drop: h₁₁I₁ = 1000 × 10 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.01 V. Feedback term: h₁₂V₂ = 0.002 × (−5) = −0.01 V.
  3. V₁ = 0.01 + (−0.01) = 0 V. The two terms cancel exactly for these numbers.
  4. Controlled source: h₂₁I₁ = 50 × 10 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.5 mA. Output conductance term: h₂₂V₂ = 20 × 10⁻⁶ × (−5) = −0.1 mA.
  5. I₂ = 0.5 − 0.1 = 0.4 mA, entering terminal 2.
  6. A passive load obeys V₂ = −I₂RL, so RL = −V₂/I₂ = 5/(0.4 × 10⁻³) = 12.5 kΩ. The negative V₂ is simply the inverting output of the amplifier across its load.

Answer. V₁ = 0 V, I₂ = 0.4 mA, and the load is 12.5 kΩ.

Exam traps

  • h₁₁ needs the output shorted (V₂ = 0), not open. For the T network the open-output value would be z₁₁ = 6 Ω, not h₁₁ = 3.7143 Ω.
  • Only h₁₁ (ohms) and h₂₂ (siemens) have units. h₁₂ and h₂₁ are pure numbers; writing h₂₂ in ohms or h₁₂ in volts loses the mark.
  • Do not drop h₁₂V₂ unless the question says so. In the transistor example it cancels the whole input drop.
  • A passive network has h₂₁ < 0 with the entering convention (−4/7 for the T network). A transistor has h₂₁ = +β because its dependent source pushes current into the collector terminal.
  • One test gives only two parameters: the output-short test gives h₁₁ and h₂₁; h₁₂ and h₂₂ need the input open.

6.4 Transmission (ABCD) parameters

Transmission parameters, also called ABCD or chain parameters, give the input voltage and current of a two-port in terms of the output voltage and the current that flows out to the load. They were first used for telephone and power lines, where the sending-end quantities are wanted from the receiving-end ones. Their real strength is the cascade: when the output of one two-port feeds the input of the next, the overall ABCD matrix is simply the product of the individual matrices.

You need the port variables and their directions, Z and Y parameters, and 2 × 2 matrix multiplication.

The chain equations and the minus sign

The free pair is the output voltage V₂ and the current that leaves port 2 towards the load. I₂ is defined entering port 2, so the leaving current is −I₂:

V₁ = AV₂ − BI₂, I₁ = CV₂ − DI₂

where A and D are pure numbers, B is in ohms and C is in siemens; in matrix form [V₁; I₁] = [T][V₂; −I₂] with [T] = [A B; C D].

The minus sign belongs to the definition, not to the network. Books that draw the output current leaving port 2 write V₁ = AV₂ + BI₂′ with I₂′ = −I₂; the numbers A, B, C and D are the same either way. What changes the numbers is keeping the entering current and dropping the minus sign.

ParameterDefinitionTest at port 2Unit
AV₁/V₂ (reverse voltage ratio)open, I₂ = 0none
CI₁/V₂ (transfer admittance)open, I₂ = 0S
BV₁/(−I₂) (transfer impedance)short, V₂ = 0Ω
DI₁/(−I₂) (reverse current ratio)short, V₂ = 0none

Three building blocks are worth knowing by heart: a series impedance Z gives [1 Z; 0 1], a shunt admittance Y gives [1 0; Y 1], and an ideal transformer of turns ratio n : 1 gives [n 0; 0 1/n]. Each has AD − BC = 1. The transformer has no Z or Y matrix at all, so ABCD is the only one of these sets that can describe it.

Terminated two-port and inverse transmission parameters

With a load ZL at port 2, V₂ = −I₂ZL. Dividing the two chain equations gives the input impedance:

Zin = V₁/I₁ = (AZL + B)/(CZL + D)

The limits give the four tests back: an open load (ZL → ∞) gives A/C, which equals z₁₁, and a shorted load gives B/D, which equals 1/y₁₁. The voltage gain is V₂/V₁ = ZL/(AZL + B), and with the load open it is 1/A.

The inverse transmission parameters run the other way: V₂ = A′V₁ − B′I₁ and I₂ = C′V₁ − D′I₁. Inverting [T] gives A′ = D/ΔT, B′ = B/ΔT, C′ = C/ΔT, D′ = A/ΔT, with ΔT = AD − BC. For a reciprocal network ΔT = 1, so A′ = D, B′ = B, C′ = C and D′ = A.

Cascading two-ports

Connect port 2 of network a to port 1 of network b. Then V₂a = V₁b, and the current leaving a is the current entering b: −I₂a = I₁b. The output vector of a is the input vector of b, so

[T] = [Ta] × [Tb]

where the matrix nearest the source is on the left. In full: A = AaAb + BaCb, B = AaBb + BaDb, C = CaAb + DaCb, D = CaBb + DaDb.

Order matters: [Tb][Ta] describes the circuit turned round. Any ladder can be built as a product of series and shunt blocks, which is how the second worked example rebuilds the T network. Series and parallel connections, and the conditions they need, are in interconnection of two-ports.

Worked example

Question. Find the ABCD parameters of the T network with Z₁ = 2 Ω, Z₂ = 3 Ω and Z₃ = 4 Ω from the open and short tests at port 2, and check AD − BC.

  1. Port 2 open (I₂ = 0): I₁ flows through Z₁ and Z₃, so V₁ = 6I₁ and V₂ = 4I₁.
  2. A = V₁/V₂ = 6I₁/4I₁ = 1.5; C = I₁/V₂ = 1/4 = 0.25 S.
  3. Port 2 shorted (V₂ = 0): V₁ = I₁(2 + 3 ∥ 4) = (26/7)I₁, and the current into the short is −I₂ = I₁ × 4/7.
  4. B = V₁/(−I₂) = (26/7)/(4/7) = 6.5 Ω; D = I₁/(−I₂) = 7/4 = 1.75.
  5. AD − BC = 1.5 × 1.75 − 6.5 × 0.25 = 2.625 − 1.625 = 1.

Answer. A = 1.5, B = 6.5 Ω, C = 0.25 S, D = 1.75, and AD − BC = 1.

Worked example 2

Question. Build the same T network as a cascade of a 2 Ω series arm, a 0.25 S shunt arm and a 3 Ω series arm. Multiply the matrices, then find the input impedance with a 10 Ω load and check it by series-parallel reduction.

  1. Blocks: series 2 Ω = [1 2; 0 1], shunt 0.25 S = [1 0; 0.25 1], series 3 Ω = [1 3; 0 1].
  2. First two: [1 2; 0 1][1 0; 0.25 1] = [1 + 2 × 0.25, 2; 0.25, 1] = [1.5 2; 0.25 1].
  3. Times the third: [1.5 2; 0.25 1][1 3; 0 1] = [1.5, 1.5 × 3 + 2; 0.25, 0.25 × 3 + 1] = [1.5 6.5; 0.25 1.75]. This matches the first example.

  5. Check: Z₂ + ZL = 13 Ω, in parallel with Z₃: 13 × 4/17 = 3.0588 Ω; add Z₁: 5.0588 Ω ✓.
  6. Voltage gain: V₂/V₁ = ZL/(AZL + B) = 10/21.5 = 0.4651.

Answer. [T] = [1.5 6.5 Ω; 0.25 S 1.75], Zin = 5.0588 Ω and V₂/V₁ = 0.4651 with the 10 Ω load.

Exam traps

  • Keep the minus sign: V₁ = AV₂ − BI₂. Writing +BI₂ with I₂ entering flips the signs of B and D, and the cascade product then comes out wrong.
  • A and C come from the open-output test, B and D from the short-output test. Mixing them gives B = V₁/(−I₂) measured with port 2 open, where I₂ = 0 and the ratio is infinite.
  • B is in ohms and C in siemens; A and D have no unit. In the T network, B = 6.5 Ω and C = 0.25 S.
  • AD − BC = 1 tests reciprocity, not symmetry. The T network has AD − BC = 1 but A = 1.5 ≠ D = 1.75, so it is reciprocal and not symmetric.
  • Matrix order in a cascade follows the signal path: [Ta][Tb] with a nearest the source. [Tb][Ta] for the T network gives [1.75 6.5; 0.25 1.5], which is the network turned round.

6.5 Relationships and conversions between parameter sets

Every two-port parameter set is the same pair of linear equations, solved for a different pair of variables. So one set follows from another by algebra, with no new measurement. This matters every day: a transistor data sheet gives h parameters but a cascade needs ABCD; a series connection needs Z but the network was measured as Y. The conversion is exact whenever the parameter it divides by is not zero; when it is zero, the target set does not exist for that network.

You need Z and Y parameters, h and g parameters, ABCD parameters, and 2 × 2 determinants.

How a conversion is derived

Write the known set, solve one of its equations for the variable that the target set wants on the right, and substitute into the other equation. Here is Z to ABCD, the derivation examiners ask for most.

  1. Start from V₁ = z₁₁I₁ + z₁₂I₂ and V₂ = z₂₁I₁ + z₂₂I₂. ABCD wants V₁ and I₁ in terms of V₂ and −I₂.
  2. Solve the second equation for I₁: I₁ = (1/z₂₁)V₂ − (z₂₂/z₂₁)I₂. Comparing with I₁ = CV₂ − DI₂ gives C = 1/z₂₁ and D = z₂₂/z₂₁.
  3. Substitute I₁ into the first equation: V₁ = (z₁₁/z₂₁)V₂ − (z₁₁z₂₂/z₂₁)I₂ + z₁₂I₂ = (z₁₁/z₂₁)V₂ − (Δz/z₂₁)I₂.
  4. Compare with V₁ = AV₂ − BI₂: A = z₁₁/z₂₁ and B = Δz/z₂₁, where Δz = z₁₁z₂₂ − z₁₂z₂₁.
  5. Every term divides by z₂₁, so the conversion needs z₂₁ ≠ 0.

The other conversions come the same way, or by matrix inversion where the two sets swap all four variables: [Y] = [Z]⁻¹ and [g] = [h]⁻¹. The determinants that appear are Δz (Ω²), Δy (S²), Δh and Δg (no unit) and ΔT = AD − BC (no unit).

Checks and zero divisors

  • Units: z in Ω, y in S, h₁₁ in Ω, h₂₂ in S, B in Ω, C in S; h₁₂, h₂₁, A and D have none. A B in siemens means a row was misread.
  • Reciprocity: z₁₂ = z₂₁ must come out as y₁₂ = y₂₁, h₁₂ = −h₂₁ and ΔT = 1. In general ΔT = z₁₂/z₂₁, so this is a one-line check on any Z-to-ABCD result.
  • Zero divisors: the Z column needs Δz ≠ 0 for Y and z₂₂ ≠ 0 for h; the ABCD row needs z₂₁ ≠ 0. A network with z₂₁ = 0 (no forward transmission, a unilateral network) has y₂₁ = h₂₁ = 0 too, so it has no ABCD matrix by any route. Work with its Z, Y or h equations directly.
  • A second route is a free check: compute the target from two different known sets and compare, as the first worked example does.

Worked example

Question. A transistor has h₁₁ = 1 kΩ, h₁₂ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴, h₂₁ = 50 and h₂₂ = 25 μS. Find its Z parameters, then its ABCD parameters, and check the ABCD set by the direct h-to-ABCD row.

  1. Δh = h₁₁h₂₂ − h₁₂h₂₁ = 1000 × 25 × 10⁻⁶ − 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ × 50 = 0.025 − 0.0125 = 0.0125.
  2. z₁₁ = Δh/h₂₂ = 0.0125/(25 × 10⁻⁶) = 500 Ω; z₁₂ = h₁₂/h₂₂ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴/(25 × 10⁻⁶) = 10 Ω.
  3. z₂₁ = −h₂₁/h₂₂ = −50/(25 × 10⁻⁶) = −2 × 10⁶ Ω; z₂₂ = 1/h₂₂ = 40 kΩ.
  4. Δz = z₁₁z₂₂ − z₁₂z₂₁ = 500 × 40 000 − 10 × (−2 × 10⁶) = 2 × 10⁷ + 2 × 10⁷ = 4 × 10⁷ Ω².
  5. From Z: A = z₁₁/z₂₁ = 500/(−2 × 10⁶) = −2.5 × 10⁻⁴; B = Δz/z₂₁ = −20 Ω; C = 1/z₂₁ = −5 × 10⁻⁷ S; D = z₂₂/z₂₁ = −0.02.
  6. Direct from h: A = −Δh/h₂₁ = −0.0125/50 = −2.5 × 10⁻⁴ ✓; B = −h₁₁/h₂₁ = −20 Ω ✓; C = −h₂₂/h₂₁ = −5 × 10⁻⁷ S ✓; D = −1/h₂₁ = −0.02 ✓.

  • [Y] = [Z]⁻¹ is a matrix inverse: y₁₁ = z₂₂/Δz, not 1/z₁₁. For the T network 1/z₁₁ = 0.1667 S but y₁₁ = 0.2692 S.

Only when z₁₂z₂₁ = 0, so that Δz = z₁₁z₂₂. In general the off-diagonal terms enter through Δz: y₁₁ = z₂₂/Δz.

6.6 Interconnection of two-ports: series, parallel and cascade

Large networks are often built by wiring small two-ports together: an amplifier with a feedback network, a bridged-T attenuator, a chain of filter sections. Three connections cover most cases. In series the port voltages add, so the Z matrices add. In parallel the port currents add, so the Y matrices add. In cascade the output of one two-port feeds the next, so the ABCD matrices multiply. The series and parallel rules hold only if the connection keeps each network's port condition, and checking that is part of every answer.

You need Z and Y parameters, ABCD parameters and conversions between parameter sets.

Series connection: Z matrices add

In a series (series–series) connection, port 1 of the combination is formed by terminal 1 of network a and terminal 1′ of network b, with 1′ of a joined to 1 of b; port 2 is made the same way. The same current I₁ flows through port 1 of both networks, and the same I₂ through port 2, while the voltages add: V₁ = V₁a + V₁b and V₂ = V₂a + V₂b. Adding [Va] = [Za][I] and [Vb] = [Zb][I] gives

[Z] = [Za] + [Zb]

Port condition. The rule assumes the current entering terminal 1 of each network leaves by its own terminal 1′. Two T networks with their common terminals at the bottom break this: joining 1′ of a to 1 of b and 2′ of a to 2 of b also joins terminals 1 and 2 of b through the common wire of a, and a circulating current flows. Turning b upside down, so that the two common terminals meet, keeps both port conditions. The general check is the Brune test: with the combination's ports open and one port driven, the voltage across the open junction between the two networks at the other side must be zero. An ideal 1 : 1 isolating transformer at one port always makes the rule valid.

Parallel connection: Y matrices add

In a parallel (parallel–parallel) connection both input ports are joined across one pair of terminals and both output ports across another. Each network sees the same V₁ and V₂, and the currents add: I₁ = I₁a + I₁b, I₂ = I₂a + I₂b. So

[Y] = [Ya] + [Yb]

If the data are in Z form, convert each network to Y, add, and convert back only if the question asks for Z. Port condition: no current may circulate between the networks through their return terminals. Two three-terminal networks whose common terminals are joined, such as a T network and a bridging resistor in a bridged-T, always satisfy it.

Two mixed connections complete the set and appear in feedback amplifiers: series at the input and parallel at the output adds h matrices, and parallel at the input and series at the output adds g matrices.

Cascade: ABCD matrices multiply

In cascade, port 2 of network a feeds port 1 of network b: V₂a = V₁b and −I₂a = I₁b. The output vector [V₂a; −I₂a] of a is the input vector [V₁b; I₁b] of b, so

[T] = [Ta][Tb]

where the network nearest the source is on the left and three or more stages multiply in signal order.

The cascade needs no extra condition: the current leaving one network is, by the wiring itself, the current entering the next. This is why chains of sections, from L-pads to transmission-line models, are always analysed with ABCD.

Exam traps

  • Cascade multiplies ABCD matrices; it never adds them. Adding [Ta] + [Tb] is the most common wrong answer in this topic.

6.7 Symmetry and reciprocity conditions

Reciprocity and symmetry are two properties you can test from the parameters alone, without opening the box. A reciprocal network transfers equally in both directions: a current into port 1 produces the same open-circuit voltage at port 2 as the same current into port 2 produces at port 1. A symmetric network looks the same from either port, so the two ports can be swapped without any change outside. Each property removes one independent parameter, and both simplify the input impedance and gain of a terminated two-port.

You need Z and Y parameters, h and g parameters, ABCD parameters, the conversion table and the reciprocity theorem from network theorems.

Reciprocity: equal transfer both ways

Drive port 1 with a current I with port 2 open: the voltage at port 2 is z₂₁I. Drive port 2 with the same I with port 1 open: the voltage at port 1 is z₁₂I. The reciprocity theorem says that in a linear network of R, L, C, coupled coils and ideal transformers the two responses are equal. So a reciprocal network has z₁₂ = z₂₁, and the conversion table turns this into a condition for every set:

SetReciprocal whenWhy
Zz₁₂ = z₂₁the reciprocity theorem

The minus sign in the h test comes from the short-circuit measurement: h₂₁ = I₂/I₁ with port 2 shorted, and in a passive network the current flows out of terminal 2 into the short, against the entering arrow. Dependent sources usually break reciprocity; a transistor model is not reciprocal. A reciprocal two-port has three independent parameters.

Symmetry: the same from either port

A two-port is (electrically) symmetric when swapping ports 1 and 2 changes nothing at the terminals. The open-circuit input impedance then equals the open-circuit output impedance, z₁₁ = z₂₂. Through the table:

The h test follows from h₁₁ = Δz/z₂₂, h₂₂ = 1/z₂₂, h₁₂h₂₁ = −z₁₂z₂₁/z₂₂², which give Δh = z₁₁/z₂₂. The ABCD test follows from A = z₁₁/z₂₁ and D = z₂₂/z₂₁. A passive network that is symmetric and reciprocal is fixed by only two parameters, for example z₁₁ and z₁₂. A network drawn with mirror symmetry (a T with Z₁ = Z₂, a lattice) is always electrically symmetric, but a reciprocal network need not be symmetric: the T network of this unit has z₁₂ = z₂₁ = 4 Ω but z₁₁ = 6 Ω ≠ z₂₂ = 7 Ω.

The terminated two-port

Put a source of internal impedance ZS at port 1 and a load ZL at port 2. The load gives V₂ = −I₂ZL; substituting in the Z equations and eliminating I₂:

Zin = z₁₁ − z₁₂z₂₁/(z₂₂ + ZL) and, looking back into port 2, Zout = z₂₂ − z₁₂z₂₁/(z₁₁ + ZS).

Current gain I₂/I₁ = −z₂₁/(z₂₂ + ZL) and voltage gain V₂/V₁ = z₂₁ZL/(Δz + z₁₁ZL).

For a reciprocal network z₁₂z₂₁ = z₁₂², and for a symmetric one Zin and Zout have the same form. The same quantities in ABCD form are Zin = (AZL + B)/(CZL + D) and Zout = (DZS + B)/(CZS + A); note that A and D swap when you look in from the other end.

6.8 Network synthesis: RC and LC functions, Foster and Cauer

Network synthesis is analysis run backwards. Analysis finds the driving-point impedance Z(s) of a given circuit; synthesis starts with Z(s), asks whether passive R, L and C can produce it, and builds the circuit. Z(s) must be positive real, and for LC or RC networks its poles and zeros must follow a strict pattern. Foster's method then builds the network by partial fractions and Cauer's by continued fractions, giving four standard circuits with the same impedance.

You need the Laplace transform, network functions with their poles and zeros, and the impedances sL, 1/(sC) and R of the three elements.

Driving-point functions and the positive-real test

A driving-point impedance Z(s) = V(s)/I(s) is measured at one port, with zero initial conditions; the driving-point admittance is Y(s) = 1/Z(s). For R, L and C it is a ratio of two polynomials in s with real, non-negative coefficients.

Z(s) can be built from passive elements only if it is positive real (PR): (1) Z(s) is real when s is real, and (2) Re Z(s) ≥ 0 whenever Re s ≥ 0. For a rational function this is tested in three steps:

  1. The numerator and denominator are Hurwitz polynomials (no roots in the right half plane), so Z(s) has no poles or zeros with Re s > 0.
  2. Poles on the jω axis (including s = 0 and s = ∞) are simple, with real, positive residues.
  3. Re Z(jω) ≥ 0 for every ω.

The degrees of numerator and denominator differ by at most one. Condition 3 says the one-port can only absorb average power. Every PR function can be built from R, L, C and coupled coils (Brune's method).

LC and RC driving-point functions

An LC (lossless, reactance) function is an odd rational function: even polynomial over odd, or odd over even, with degrees differing by exactly one. Its poles and zeros are simple, lie on the jω axis, and alternate; s = 0 and s = ∞ are always critical frequencies (a pole or a zero). Its partial fractions are

Z(s) = k∞s + k₀/s + Σ 2kᵢs/(s² + ωᵢ²)

where k∞, k₀ and every kᵢ are positive (or zero for the first two). Re Z(jω) = 0 everywhere, and the reactance X(ω) always rises with ω.

An RC impedance has simple, alternating poles and zeros on the negative real axis (s = −σ). The critical frequency nearest the origin is a pole (possibly at s = 0) and the one farthest away is a zero (possibly at ∞), so Z(0) > Z(∞). Its expansion is Z(s) = K∞ + K₀/s + Σ Kᵢ/(s + σᵢ) with all constants positive.

Foster and Cauer realizations

Foster I expands Z(s) in partial fractions: the terms are elements in series. For LC, k∞s is an inductor L = k∞, k₀/s a capacitor C = 1/k₀, and 2kᵢs/(s² + ωᵢ²) a parallel LC tank with C = 1/(2kᵢ) and L = 2kᵢ/ωᵢ². Foster II expands Y(s): the terms are branches in parallel; k∞s is a capacitor, k₀/s an inductor, and 2kᵢs/(s² + ωᵢ²) a series LC branch with L = 1/(2kᵢ) and C = 2kᵢ/ωᵢ².

Cauer I removes the pole at infinity again and again by long division with the polynomials in descending powers. Each quotient is a series inductor or a shunt capacitor, giving a ladder. Cauer II removes the pole at s = 0 again and again by dividing with the polynomials in ascending powers, giving series capacitors and shunt inductors. Every form of an LC function uses the same number of elements, equal to the highest power of s in the function.

Worked example 2

Question. Show that Z(s) = (s + 2)(s + 4)/[(s + 1)(s + 3)] is an RC impedance and realize it in Foster I form.

  1. From the origin: pole −1, zero −2, pole −3, zero −4. They alternate on the negative real axis and the nearest is a pole: an RC impedance. Z(0) = 8/3 Ω > Z(∞) = 1 Ω ✓.
  2. K∞ = Z(∞) = 1. At s = −1: (s + 2)(s + 4)/(s + 3) = (1)(3)/2 = 1.5. At s = −3: (s + 2)(s + 4)/(s + 1) = (−1)(1)/(−2) = 0.5. So Z = 1 + 1.5/(s + 1) + 0.5/(s + 3).
  3. A term K/(s + σ) is a parallel RC: Z = (1/C)/(s + 1/(RC)), so C = 1/K and R = K/σ.
  4. 1.5/(s + 1): C = 1/1.5 = 2/3 F, R = 1.5 Ω. 0.5/(s + 3): C = 2 F, R = 0.5/3 = 1/6 Ω.
  5. Check at s = 0: 1 + 1.5 + 1/6 = 2.667 Ω = Z(0) ✓ (every capacitor open, the resistors in series).

Answer. A 1 Ω resistor in series with (1.5 Ω ∥ 2/3 F) and (1/6 Ω ∥ 2 F).

Exam traps

  • Positive coefficients alone do not make a function PR or LC. Z(s) = (s² + 4)/[s(s² + 1)] has positive coefficients, but its critical frequencies run pole 0, pole 1, zero 2; its residue at ±j1 is −3, so it is not an LC impedance.
  • (s² + 4)/(s² + 1) is not an LC impedance: it is even, and Re Z(jω) < 0 between 1 and 2 rad/s, so it is not even PR.
  • Foster I expands Z(s) into series elements; Foster II expands Y(s) into parallel branches. Expanding Y but drawing series elements gives the dual circuit, not the asked one.
  • In Foster I a term k₀/s is a capacitor of 1/k₀, not k₀: 1.5/s is 2/3 F.
  • For an RC impedance the critical frequency nearest the origin must be a pole. (s + 1)/(s + 2) has a zero nearest the origin, so it is an RL impedance (or an RC admittance), not an RC impedance.

Remember State the conditions for a rational Z(s) to be positive real. 3 marks

Z(s) is real for real s; it has no poles or zeros in the right half plane; poles on the jω axis are simple with positive real residues; and Re Z(jω) ≥ 0 for all ω.

Understand Why must the poles and zeros of an LC impedance alternate? 3 marks

The reactance X(ω) of an LC one-port always rises with frequency. Between two poles X runs from −∞ up to +∞, so it must cross zero once; between two zeros it must pass through a pole. So they interlace.

Apply Realize Z(s) = (s² + 1)/[s(s² + 4)] in Foster I form. 4 marks

k₀ = 1/4 and, at s² = −4, (s² + 1)/s² = 3/4, so Z = 0.25/s + 0.75s/(s² + 4). Elements in series: C = 1/0.25 = 4 F and a tank with C = 1/0.75 = 4/3 F, L = 0.75/4 = 3/16 H.

Apply Realize the same Z(s) = (s² + 1)/[s(s² + 4)] in Cauer I form. 4 marks

Z has a zero at ∞, so start with Y = (s³ + 4s)/(s² + 1). Divide: s, remainder 3s → shunt C = 1 F. (s² + 1) ÷ 3s = s/3, remainder 1 → series L = 1/3 H. 3s ÷ 1 = 3s → shunt C = 3 F.

Analyze Can Z(s) = 2(s + 3)/(s + 1) be an RC impedance? If so, realize it. 4 marks

Yes: pole −1 nearest the origin, zero −3 beyond it, Z(0) = 6 Ω > Z(∞) = 2 Ω. Z = 2 + 4/(s + 1): a 2 Ω resistor in series with a parallel RC of R = 4 Ω and C = 1/4 F.

Takeaways

  1. A linear two-port with no internal sources fixes two relations among V₁, I₁, V₂ and I₂, so any two of them can be chosen as the free pair; each choice is a parameter set of four constants.
  2. Draw both port currents entering the network. Then a load gives V₂ = −I₂ZL and the power into the two-port is V₁I₁ + V₂I₂.
  3. Z parameters need an open circuit at the far port, Y parameters a short circuit, h parameters one of each; ABCD uses the output open (A, C) and shorted (B, D).
  4. Every set follows from another by algebra; a conversion exists only when the parameter it divides by is not zero.
  5. Series connections add Z matrices and parallel connections add Y matrices only if the port condition survives the wiring; cascades multiply ABCD matrices in signal order with no extra condition.
  6. Reciprocity (z₁₂ = z₂₁, AD − BC = 1) holds for any network of R, L, C and coupled coils; symmetry (z₁₁ = z₂₂, A = D) is a separate property.
  7. For the T network of 2, 3 and 4 Ω: Z = [6 4; 4 7] Ω, Y = [0.2692 −0.1538; −0.1538 0.2308] S, h₁₁ = 3.714 Ω, h₂₁ = −0.571 and ABCD = [1.5 6.5; 0.25 1.75].
  8. A driving-point function is realizable with passive elements only if it is positive real; LC and RC functions have simple, alternating poles and zeros, and Foster and Cauer forms turn them into circuits with the same number of elements.

Remember Define a port and a two-port network. State the port condition and the sign convention for the four port variables, explain why a linear two-port needs only four parameters, and list the parameter sets with their free variables. 8 marks

Points to cover. Port: a terminal pair with equal and opposite currents; Two-port: ports 1–1′ and 2–2′; three-terminal networks qualify; Convention: V across each port, both currents entering; load gives V₂ = −I₂ZL; Linear, no independent sources, zero initial energy; Four variables, two relations, two free variables, four constants; Six sets: Z, Y, h, g, ABCD, inverse ABCD.

Model answer.

  1. Port. A port is a pair of terminals through which the current entering one terminal equals the current leaving the other (the port condition). A port therefore carries one voltage and one current.
  2. Two-port. A two-port network has two such pairs: port 1 (terminals 1–1′, the input) and port 2 (terminals 2–2′, the output). A three-terminal network with 1′ and 2′ joined, such as a T network or a transistor, is still a two-port: its common terminal returns both port currents.
  3. Sign convention. V₁ is the voltage of terminal 1 with respect to 1′ and V₂ that of 2 with respect to 2′. I₁ enters terminal 1 and I₂ enters terminal 2: both currents are drawn into the network. The power entering the two-port is P = V₁I₁ + V₂I₂, and a load ZL at port 2 obeys V₂ = −I₂ZL.
  4. Conditions for fixed parameters. The network must be linear, contain no independent sources, and start with no stored energy. Dependent sources are allowed.
  5. Why four parameters. There are four port variables, but the network imposes two linear relations among them. So two variables are free and two are dependent; each dependent variable is a linear combination of the two free ones, with two constants each: four constants in all.
  6. Parameter sets. The six ways of choosing the free pair give six sets:
    SetFree pairEquations
    ZI₁, I₂V₁ = z₁₁I₁ + z₁₂I₂, V₂ = z₂₁I₁ + z₂₂I₂
    YV₁, V₂I₁ = y₁₁V₁ + y₁₂V₂, I₂ = y₂₁V₁ + y₂₂V₂
    hI₁, V₂V₁ = h₁₁I₁ + h₁₂V₂, I₂ = h₂₁I₁ + h₂₂V₂
    gV₁, I₂I₁ = g₁₁V₁ + g₁₂I₂, V₂ = g₂₁V₁ + g₂₂I₂
    ABCDV₂, −I₂V₁ = AV₂ − BI₂, I₁ = CV₂ − DI₂

  2. Parallel. In a parallel connection both networks see the same V₁ and V₂ and the currents add, so [Y] = [Yₐ] + [Yᵦ]. This needs no circulating current between the networks through their return terminals.
  3. Cascade. [T] = [Tₐ][Tᵦ] needs no extra condition, because the current leaving port 2 of one network is, by the wiring, the current entering port 1 of the next.
  4. Series, done wrongly. Take two grounded T networks (common terminal at the bottom) and join 1′ of a to 1 of b and 2′ of a to 2 of b. Network a's common wire now also joins terminals 1 and 2 of b, so the 2 Ω and 3 Ω arms of b are in parallel and b collapses to one impedance in the common lead:
    Zx = 4 + (2 × 3)/(2 + 3) = 5.2 Ω
[Z] = [Zₐ] + Zx[1 1; 1 1] = [6 + 5.2, 4 + 5.2; 4 + 5.2, 7 + 5.2] = [11.2 9.2; 9.2 12.2] Ω
  5. The rule would have given [Zₐ] + [Zᵦ] = [12 8; 8 14] Ω, which is wrong: the port currents of b are not I₁ and I₂ any more.
  6. Series, done rightly. Turn network b over, so its common terminal is at the top and meets the common terminal of a. Now only the two common terminals are joined, each network still carries I₁ through its port 1 and I₂ through its port 2, and
    [Z] = [6 4; 4 7] + [6 4; 4 7] = [12 8; 8 14] Ω
  7. Check of z₁₁: with port 2 open, I₁ runs through 2 + 4 Ω of a and 4 + 2 Ω of b: 12 Ω ✓. Check of z₁₂: a current into port 2 passes through both 4 Ω shunt arms, and port 1 reads that drop: 8 Ω ✓.
  8. The two arrangements.
  9. Brune test. Connect port 1 of the two networks in series as intended, drive it with I₁, and leave the port-2 junction open (do not join 2′ of a to 2 of b). The voltage across that gap must be zero; if it is not, joining the gap makes a current circulate. In the wrong arrangement, with I₁ = 1 A, terminal 2′ of a is at the potential of terminal 1 of b, and terminal 2 of b sits z₁₁ − z₂₁ = 6 − 4 = 2 V away from it: the gap has 2 V, so the test fails. In the right arrangement both sides of the gap are the joined common terminals: 0 V, so the test passes. The same test with the ports exchanged is also needed.
  10. Parallel and cascade for these networks. Two grounded three-terminal networks in parallel share their common terminal anyway, so no current can circulate and the Y rule always holds (for example the bridged-T: T network plus a 5 Ω bridge gives [0.4692 −0.3538; −0.3538 0.4308] S). Cascade never needs a check: two T networks in cascade give [1.5 6.5; 0.25 1.75]² = [3.875 21.125; 0.8125 4.6875].
  11. Fix when the test fails. An ideal 1 : 1 isolating transformer at one port of one network forces equal and opposite currents in that port's terminals, so the rule then holds.
  12. Closing check. The wrong-arrangement matrix is still reciprocal (9.2 = 9.2) and its z₁₁ = 11.2 Ω equals 6 Ω (network a) plus the 5.2 Ω common-lead impedance, as the collapsed circuit predicts.
Evaluate State the conditions for a driving-point function to be positive real and the properties of LC and RC impedances. Judge each of the following and realize those that are LC or RC: (a) (s + 2)/(s + 1); (b) (s + 1)/(s + 2); (c) (s² + 4)/[s(s² + 1)]; (d) (s² + 1)/(s² + 4); (e) (s² + 3s + 2)/(s² + s + 1). 13 marks

Points to cover. PR: real for real s, no RHP poles or zeros, simple jω poles with positive residues, Re Z(jω) ≥ 0; LC: simple alternating poles and zeros on the jω axis; RC: alternating on the negative real axis, pole nearest the origin; (a) RC, (b) RL not RC, (c) not PR, (d) not PR, (e) PR but needs R, L and C.

Model answer.

  1. Positive-real conditions. Z(s) is real for real s; it has no poles or zeros with Re s > 0 (numerator and denominator Hurwitz); any pole on the jω axis, including s = 0 and s = ∞, is simple with a real positive residue; and Re Z(jω) ≥ 0 for every ω. Only PR functions can be built from passive elements.
  2. LC impedance. An odd rational function (even over odd or odd over even, degrees differing by one); simple poles and zeros on the jω axis that alternate; a critical frequency at s = 0 and at s = ∞; Re Z(jω) = 0.
  3. RC impedance. Simple poles and zeros on the negative real axis that alternate; the critical frequency nearest the origin is a pole and the farthest is a zero, so Z(0) > Z(∞); all residues positive.
  4. (a) Z = (s + 2)/(s + 1). Pole −1, zero −2: alternating, pole nearest the origin; Z(0) = 2 Ω > Z(∞) = 1 Ω. An RC impedance:
    Z = 1 + 1/(s + 1)  → 1 Ω in series with (R = 1 Ω ∥ C = 1 F)
  5. (b) Z = (s + 1)/(s + 2). Zero −1 is nearest the origin and Z(0) = 0.5 Ω < Z(∞) = 1 Ω, so it is not an RC impedance (its RC expansion 1 − 1/(s + 2) has a negative residue). It is PR, and it is an RL impedance:
    Z/s = (s + 1)/[s(s + 2)] = 0.5/s + 0.5/(s + 2)
Z = 0.5 + 0.5s/(s + 2)  → 0.5 Ω in series with (R = 0.5 Ω ∥ L = 0.25 H)
  6. Check: a parallel RL has Z = Rs/(s + R/L); with R = 0.5 Ω and R/L = 2, L = 0.25 H ✓. At s = 0 the inductor shorts the 0.5 Ω, leaving 0.5 Ω = Z(0) ✓.
  7. (c) Z = (s² + 4)/[s(s² + 1)]. Poles 0, ±j1; zeros ±j2. On the jω axis: pole 0, pole 1, zero 2: two poles in a row, so not LC. The residue at s² = −1 is negative:
    Z = 4/s + k s/(s² + 1),  k = [(s² + 4)/s²] at s² = −1 = 3/(−1) = −3
  8. A jω-axis pole with a negative residue fails the PR test: not realizable. (Its reciprocal is an LC impedance, so it is an LC admittance.)
  9. (d) Z = (s² + 1)/(s² + 4). Even function, so not LC. On the jω axis Z(jω) = (1 − ω²)/(4 − ω²) is real:

Glossary

Brune test
A check that a series or parallel connection keeps every port condition, made by measuring the voltage across the open junction.
Cascade connection
Port 2 of one two-port feeding port 1 of the next; their ABCD matrices multiply.
Cauer form
A ladder realization from a continued fraction, removing poles at infinity (first form) or at zero (second form).
Driving-point impedance
The ratio V/I at one port, with the other port in a stated condition.
Foster form
A realization from the partial fractions of Z(s) (first form, series elements) or Y(s) (second form, parallel branches).
Hurwitz polynomial
A polynomial with no roots in the right half of the s plane.
Hybrid parameters
The h parameters, with I₁ and V₂ as free variables; used for transistors.
Inverse hybrid parameters
The g parameters, with V₁ and I₂ as free variables; [g] = [h]⁻¹.
Inverse transmission parameters
The set giving V₂ and I₂ in terms of V₁ and −I₁; the inverse of the ABCD matrix.
Ladder network
A chain of alternating series and shunt arms.
Open-circuit impedance parameters
The Z parameters, each measured with the other port open.
Parallel connection
Two two-ports with common port voltages and added port currents; their Y matrices add.
Port
A pair of terminals through which the current entering one terminal equals the current leaving the other.
Port condition
The requirement that each port carries equal and opposite currents in its two terminals.
Positive-real function
A function real for real s with Re Z(s) ≥ 0 for Re s ≥ 0; the condition for passive realizability.
Reactance function
The driving-point impedance of an LC network; an odd rational function with alternating poles and zeros on the jω axis.
Reciprocal network
A network whose transfer is the same in both directions: z₁₂ = z₂₁, AD − BC = 1.
Series connection
Two two-ports with common port currents and added port voltages; their Z matrices add.
Short-circuit admittance parameters
The Y parameters, each measured with the other port shorted.
Symmetric network
A network whose ports can be exchanged without any change at the terminals: z₁₁ = z₂₂, A = D.
Terminated two-port
A two-port with a source impedance at port 1 and a load at port 2.
Transfer impedance
The ratio of a voltage at one port to a current at the other, such as z₂₁.
Transmission parameters
The ABCD (chain) parameters: V₁ = AV₂ − BI₂, I₁ = CV₂ − DI₂.
Two-port network
A network with an input port and an output port, described by two linear equations in V₁, I₁, V₂ and I₂.
