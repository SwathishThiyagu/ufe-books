6.1 Two-port network concept and terminology

A two-port network is a circuit seen only through two pairs of terminals, one pair at the input and one pair at the output. Each pair is a port, so current entering one terminal leaves its partner. This black-box view hides the inside and describes the outside with four quantities: V1, I1, V2 and I2. Engineers use it for transistors, filters, transformers and transmission lines, where only input and output behaviour matters.

You need Kirchhoff's laws and Thévenin and Norton equivalents from earlier units; they are used only as background.

Ports and terminal pairs

A terminal is a point where a wire connects. A port is a pair of terminals with one condition: the current entering one terminal must equal the current leaving the other. That pair carries one voltage and one current.

A one-port has one such pair; a resistor driven from two wires is a one-port. A two-port has two pairs: port 1 at the input and port 2 at the output. A multi-port has three or more ports. A transistor has three terminals, but in small-signal use we often treat base-emitter as port 1 and collector-emitter as port 2; the shared emitter is common to both ports.

A two-port is linear when its equations contain no powers, products or constant terms in the port variables. To describe it by four fixed constants, two more conditions are needed: no independent sources inside, and zero initial stored energy in any capacitors or inductors, so the response comes only from the applied port signals.

  • Transistor small-signal model.
  • Filter and matching network.
  • Transformer and transmission line.
  • Amplifier input and output.

Sign convention and power

Use this convention. V1 is the voltage across port 1, positive at the marked terminal. I1 enters that positive terminal and leaves the return terminal. V2 is across port 2 with its own marked positive terminal. I2 enters that positive terminal and leaves its return. The arrows do not mean the actual current direction; they define the positive sign.

With this convention the power entering the whole two-port is:

P = V1 I1 + V2 I2

P is the total power entering the two-port in watts (W); V1 and V2 are port voltages in volts (V); I1 and I2 are port currents in amperes (A), both defined as entering their ports.

Positive P means the network absorbs power. Negative P means it delivers power. For a load at port 2, the load current usually leaves port 2; that current equals −I2.

Four variables, two equations

The outside world gives four quantities: V1, I1, V2, I2. For a linear two-port with no independent sources, only two are independent. The network imposes two linear equations. Choose any two variables as inputs; the equations give the other two. Each choice produces a parameter set with four constants.

If I1 and I2 are chosen as inputs, the equations are V1 = z11 I1 + z12 I2 and V2 = z21 I1 + z22 I2. The four z constants are the Z parameters. Choosing V1 and V2 as inputs gives the Y parameters. Choosing I1 and V2 gives the h parameters. Choosing V2 and I2 gives the ABCD parameters. ABCD parameters are normally written with I2 taken as leaving port 2; with the entering convention used here, I2 is replaced by −I2 in those equations. All are rearrangements of the same two equations; see conversions between parameter sets.

The port description is used for a transistor data sheet, a filter design, a transformer model and a long transmission line. Some syllabi call the same idea a quadripole or a four-terminal network. A true four-terminal network is not always a two-port: the port current condition must hold.

Worked example

Question. A two-port has V1 = 10 V, I1 = 2 A entering port 1, V2 = −4 V, and I2 = 1 A entering port 2. Find the power entering the two-port and state whether it absorbs or delivers power.

  1. Write the power entering the two-port: P = V1 I1 + V2 I2.
  2. Put in port 1 values: V1 I1 = 10 V × 2 A = 20 W.
  3. Put in port 2 values: V2 I2 = (−4 V) × 1 A = −4 W.
  4. Add the two terms: P = 20 W + (−4 W) = 16 W.
  5. The result is positive, so power enters the network.

Answer. The two-port absorbs 16 W.

Exam traps

  • I2 is defined entering port 2. A load current leaving port 2 is −I2. A wrong sign flips the V2 I2 term.
  • A port is not any two terminals. The current entering one terminal must equal the current leaving the other.
  • A four-terminal network is not automatically a two-port. If the shared terminal also carries current from a third, external path, the port condition fails. A three-terminal device such as a transistor is a valid two-port, because its common terminal carries only I1 and I2.
  • The four port variables are not four independent inputs. A linear two-port has only two independent port variables.
  • Fixed parameters need no independent sources inside and zero initial stored energy. An internal source adds extra terms.

Not for the exam. At radio frequencies, two-ports are measured as S parameters because open and short circuits are hard to make at high frequency. 6.2 Impedance (Z) and admittance (Y) parameters

A two-port is a box with an input port and an output port. Z parameters describe it by writing the two port voltages as sums of terms proportional to the two port currents. Y parameters do the opposite: they write the two port currents in terms of the two port voltages. Both descriptions carry the same information, because the Y matrix is the inverse of the Z matrix. Data sheets of transformers, filters and small RF blocks quote them. They also let you combine networks: series connections add Z matrices and parallel connections add Y matrices (see 6.6).

You need: 6.1 for the four port variables V₁, I₁, V₂, I₂, Ohm's law and KVL/KCL from Unit 1, and impedance from Unit 3.

Open-circuit Z parameters

Z parameters, also called open-circuit impedance parameters, treat the two port currents I₁ and I₂ as the causes and the two port voltages V₁ and V₂ as the effects. Both currents enter their ports, following the sign convention fixed in 6.1. The network is linear, so the relations are linear.

V₁ = z₁₁I₁ + z₁₂I₂    and    V₂ = z₂₁I₁ + z₂₂I₂

I₁, I₂ are the port currents in amperes, entering the top terminals; V₁, V₂ are the port voltages in volts; z₁₁, z₁₂, z₂₁, z₂₂ are the Z parameters, each in ohms (V/A). The four numbers form the matrix Z = [[z₁₁, z₁₂], [z₂₁, z₂₂]].

To isolate z₁₁ you must make I₂ zero, which means leaving port 2 open. Then V₁ = z₁₁I₁, so z₁₁ = V₁/I₁. It is simply the input impedance seen at port 1 with the output open. In that same test V₂ = z₂₁I₁, so z₂₁ = V₂/I₁: the voltage appearing at port 2 for each ampere pushed into port 1.

Swap the roles for the other two. Open port 1 so I₁ = 0, drive port 2, and read V₂ = z₂₂I₂ and V₁ = z₁₂I₂. Here z₂₂ = V₂/I₂ is the output impedance with the input open, and z₁₂ = V₁/I₂ is the transfer impedance measured backwards. An ohmmeter placed on one port with the other left open reads the diagonal terms directly.

  1. Open port 2, so I₂ = 0.
  2. Apply a known test current I₁ at port 1.
  3. Measure V₁ and V₂.
  4. Compute z₁₁ = V₁/I₁ and z₂₁ = V₂/I₁, both in ohms.
  5. Open port 1, drive port 2, and compute z₁₂ = V₁/I₂ and z₂₂ = V₂/I₂ the same way.

Short-circuit Y parameters

Y parameters, or short-circuit admittance parameters, turn the equations around: voltages now drive and currents respond.

I₁ = y₁₁V₁ + y₁₂V₂    and    I₂ = y₂₁V₁ + y₂₂V₂

V₁, V₂ are the port voltages in volts; I₁, I₂ are the port currents in amperes; y₁₁, y₁₂, y₂₁, y₂₂ are the Y parameters, each in siemens (A/V), written S. The matrix is Y = [[y₁₁, y₁₂], [y₂₁, y₂₂]].

Set V₂ = 0 by short-circuiting port 2. Then I₁ = y₁₁V₁, so y₁₁ = I₁/V₁ is the input admittance with the output shorted. Also I₂ = y₂₁V₁, so y₂₁ = I₂/V₁ is the short-circuit transfer admittance. Short port 1 instead (V₁ = 0) and drive port 2 to get y₁₂ = I₁/V₂ and y₂₂ = I₂/V₂.

Because the two sets describe the same box, Y is the matrix inverse of Z.

y₁₁ = z₂₂/Δz,  y₁₂ = −z₁₂/Δz,  y₂₁ = −z₂₁/Δz,  y₂₂ = z₁₁/Δz,  where Δz = z₁₁z₂₂ − z₁₂z₂₁

Δz is the determinant of the Z matrix, in Ω². Each y comes out in S.

Watch the signs. The diagonal terms keep their sign, but both off-diagonal terms pick up a minus sign from the inverse. And z₁₂ is not 1/y₁₂: the inverse of a matrix is not the matrix of reciprocals.

Which set exists, and which to use

  • Z needs a good open circuit at the far port; Y needs a good short. At high frequency stray capacitance spoils an open, so RF engineers prefer short-circuit tests or scattering parameters.
  • If Δz = 0 the Z matrix is singular and no Y matrix exists. A passive T network of ordinary elements never hits this, but an ideal transformer does: it forces V₁ = nV₂ and I₁ = −I₂/n, so the port variables are not free and neither a Z nor a Y matrix can be written. The transmission parameters of 6.4 handle it.
  • A reciprocal network has z₁₂ = z₂₁ and y₁₂ = y₂₁; a symmetric one has z₁₁ = z₂₂ and y₁₁ = y₂₂. See 6.7.
  • Conversion to h or ABCD parameters is pure algebra on the same two equations; see 6.5.

Worked example

Question. A T network has a series arm Z₁ = 2 Ω on the port 1 side, a series arm Z₂ = 3 Ω on the port 2 side, and a shunt arm Z₃ = 4 Ω from the centre node to the common return. (a) Find z₁₁, z₁₂, z₂₁ and z₂₂. (b) Find Δz and all four Y parameters. (c) Find the input impedance with port 2 shorted and compare it with z₁₁.

  1. With port 2 open, I₂ = 0, so no current flows in Z₂. The current I₁ passes through Z₁ and Z₃ in series: V₁ = I₁(Z₁ + Z₃) = I₁(2 + 4) = 6I₁, giving z₁₁ = 6 Ω.
  2. The same current develops V₂ across Z₃ only: V₂ = I₁Z₃ = 4I₁, giving z₂₁ = 4 Ω.
  3. With port 1 open, I₁ = 0 and I₂ passes through Z₂ and Z₃: V₂ = I₂(Z₂ + Z₃) = I₂(3 + 4) = 7I₂, giving z₂₂ = 7 Ω.
  4. The same I₂ develops V₁ across Z₃: V₁ = I₂Z₃ = 4I₂, giving z₁₂ = 4 Ω. Since z₁₂ = z₂₁, the network is reciprocal.
  5. Δz = z₁₁z₂₂ − z₁₂z₂₁ = (6)(7) − (4)(4) = 42 − 16 = 26 Ω².
  6. y₁₁ = z₂₂/Δz = 7/26 = 0.2692 S.
  7. y₁₂ = −z₁₂/Δz = −4/26 = −0.1538 S and y₂₁ = −z₂₁/Δz = −0.1538 S.
  8. y₂₂ = z₁₁/Δz = 6/26 = 0.2308 S.
  9. With port 2 shorted, Z₂ and Z₃ are in parallel: their combination is (3)(4)/(3 + 4) = 1.7143 Ω, and the input impedance is Z₁ + 1.7143 = 2 + 1.7143 = 3.7143 Ω. This is not z₁₁ = 6 Ω, because the port condition is different.

Exam traps

  • z₁₁ is not measured with port 2 shorted. For the example network, shorting port 2 gives 3.7143 Ω, not 6 Ω. Z parameters always need an open circuit; Y parameters always need a short circuit.
  • z₁₂ is not 1/y₁₂. Here z₁₂ = 4 Ω while 1/y₁₂ = 1/(−0.1538) = −6.5 Ω. The inverse of a matrix is not the matrix of reciprocals.
  • Sign slip: y₁₂ and y₂₁ carry a minus sign from the inverse; z₁₂ and z₂₁ do not. Writing y₁₂ = +z₁₂/Δz is the most common lost mark.
  • Units: Z parameters in ohms, Y parameters in siemens, Δz in Ω². A determinant written in plain ohms loses a mark.
  • Forcing a matrix on a network that has none: an ideal transformer has no finite Z or Y matrix, because its ports are not independent. Use ABCD parameters there.

6.3 Hybrid (h) and inverse hybrid (g) parameters

Any two of the four two-port variables can be chosen to drive the other two. Hybrid parameters choose the input current I1 and the output voltage V2 as drivers, and produce the input voltage V1 and the output current I2. One driver is a current, the other is a voltage, so the set is called hybrid. The four constants carry mixed units: one ohm, one siemens, two plain ratios. That mix matches a transistor exactly, where the base is current-driven and the collector is voltage-loaded. The inverse hybrid (g) set turns the same two equations inside out, driving I1 and V2 from V1 and I2.

You need: 6.1 for the four port variables and their assumed current directions, 6.2 for the matrix view of a two-port, and dependent sources from Unit 1.

The two hybrid equations

A two-port is seen only through two terminal pairs, so it has four external variables: V1, I1 at port 1 and V2, I2 at port 2. Z parameters drive the port with I1 and I2; Y parameters drive it with V1 and V2. Neither fits an amplifier, whose input is fed by a current and whose output is loaded by a voltage. The hybrid set drives with I1 and V2.

V₁ = h₁₁ I₁ + h₁₂ V₂        I₂ = h₂₁ I₁ + h₂₂ V₂

V₁, I₁ are the port-1 voltage and current; V₂, I₂ are the port-2 voltage and current, with I₂ taken as entering port 2; h₁₁ is the short-circuit input impedance in Ω; h₁₂ is the open-circuit reverse voltage gain, dimensionless; h₂₁ is the short-circuit forward current gain, dimensionless; h₂₂ is the open-circuit output admittance in S.

The two equations do different jobs. The first builds the input voltage from a drop (h11 I1) and a feed-through from the output (h12 V2). The second builds the output current from a controlled source (h21 I1) and a local admittance (h22 V2). Stacked as matrices, [V₁; I₂] = [h] × [I₁; V₂], where [h] is the 2×2 hybrid matrix.

Because I2 is taken as entering port 2, a passive load is written V2 = −I2 Z_L. Keep that sign; it decides whether h21 comes out positive or negative in a test.

Measuring each h parameter

Each parameter is a ratio taken with one driver set to zero. V2 = 0 means shorting the output port with a wire. I1 = 0 means leaving the input port open. Two tests, four numbers.

The subscripts follow one rule: the first index is the port where you measure, the second is the port you excite. So h₁₂ reads "volts at port 1 per volt at port 2" with port 1 open. Read the table that way and you never have to memorise it.

In transistor work the same four numbers get different names: h₂₁ is the forward current gain β, written h_fe; h₁₁ is the input impedance h_ie; h₁₂ is the reverse voltage gain h_re, usually around 10⁻⁴; h₂₂ is the output admittance h_oe. All four are small-signal values measured at a stated bias point.

Inverse hybrid (g) parameters

Solve the same two equations for I1 and V2 instead, and you get the inverse hybrid set.

I₁ = g₁₁ V₁ + g₁₂ I₂        V₂ = g₂₁ V₁ + g₂₂ I₂

g₁₁ is the open-circuit input admittance in S (measure with I₂ = 0); g₁₂ is the short-circuit reverse current gain, dimensionless (V₁ = 0); g₂₁ is the open-circuit forward voltage gain, dimensionless (I₂ = 0); g₂₂ is the short-circuit output impedance in Ω (V₁ = 0).

The two matrices are inverses of each other: [h] = [g]⁻¹.

h₁₁ = g₂₂ / Δ_g,  h₁₂ = −g₁₂ / Δ_g,  h₂₁ = −g₂₁ / Δ_g,  h₂₂ = g₁₁ / Δ_g

Δ_g = g₁₁ g₂₂ − g₁₂ g₂₁ is the determinant of the g matrix; the minus signs appear on both transfer terms. Full conversion algebra is in 6.5.

Watch the units across the swap: h₂₂ is an admittance in siemens while g₂₂ is an impedance in ohms. They are not reciprocals of each other. Only the whole matrices are inverses. Some syllabi call h the hybrid parameters and g the inverse hybrid parameters; only a few courses go past reading the two matrices off a datasheet.

Worked example

Question. A transistor at its bias point has h11 = 1 kΩ, h12 = 0.002, h21 = 50 and h22 = 20 μS. The input current is I1 = 10 μA and the output port is held at V2 = −5 V. Find the input voltage V1 and the output current I2.

  1. Write the hybrid equations: V1 = h11 I1 + h12 V2 and I2 = h21 I1 + h22 V2.
  2. Convert to base units: h11 = 1000 Ω, h22 = 20 × 10⁻⁶ S, I1 = 10 × 10⁻⁶ A, V2 = −5 V.
  3. Input drop term: h11 I1 = 1000 × 10 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.01 V.
  4. Reverse feed-through term: h12 V2 = 0.002 × (−5) = −0.01 V.
  5. Add: V1 = 0.01 + (−0.01) = 0 V.
  6. Controlled source term: h21 I1 = 50 × 10 × 10⁻⁶ = 5 × 10⁻⁴ A = 0.5 mA.
  7. Output admittance term: h22 V2 = 20 × 10⁻⁶ × (−5) = −1 × 10⁻⁴ A = −0.1 mA.
  8. Add: I2 = 0.5 − 0.1 = 0.4 mA = 4 × 10⁻⁴ A.

Answer. V1 = 0 V and I2 = 0.4 mA (4 × 10⁻⁴ A); at port 1 the forward drop and the reverse feed-through cancel exactly.

Exam traps

  • h11 is measured with the output shorted (V2 = 0), not open. Opening the output changes the number completely; the short is what makes h11 a pure input impedance.
  • Unit slip: h11 is in ohms and h22 is in siemens, while h12 and h21 are plain ratios. Writing h11 in siemens, h22 in ohms, or h12 in volts costs marks.
  • Do not throw away the h12 V2 term as negligible unless the question says so. With V2 negative it can cancel the h11 I1 term exactly, and the cancellation is often the point of the sum.
  • g22 is an impedance while h22 is an admittance. They are not reciprocals; only the full matrices are inverses, and the transfer terms pick up minus signs.
  • The short-circuit output test gives h11 and h21 only. h12 and h22 need the input left open, so a single test cannot give all four.

6.4 Transmission (ABCD) parameters

Transmission parameters describe a two-port from the input side: they give the input voltage and current in terms of the output voltage and the current leaving port 2. The matrix is also called the chain or ABCD matrix. It exists because cascaded blocks are common: in a receiver chain, a filter followed by an amplifier, you can multiply the matrices instead of re-solving the whole circuit. Some syllabi call these transmission or chain parameters.

You need: Chapter 6.1 for port variables, 6.2 for the matrix idea, and Unit 1 for KVL and KCL.

The chain relation and its sign convention

A two-port has four external variables: V1, I1, V2 and I2. The ABCD set chooses V2 and the current leaving port 2 as the independent pair. To keep I2 defined as entering port 2, that leaving current is −I2. The relation is:

[V1; I1] = [[A, B], [C, D]] [V2; −I2]

V1 is the port-1 voltage in V; I1 is the current entering port 1 in A; V2 is the port-2 voltage in V; I2 is the current entering port 2 in A; A, B, C and D are the transmission parameters.

The matrix equation means V1 = A V2 + B(−I2) and I1 = C V2 + D(−I2). Every term is a phasor or a real number; the same equations work for AC at one frequency.

A and D are dimensionless because they relate a voltage to a voltage or a current to a current. B has ohms because it relates a voltage to a current. C has siemens because it relates a current to a voltage.

Measuring the four parameters

Each parameter is found by forcing one output condition: open circuit (I2 = 0) or short circuit (V2 = 0). This is why the definitions are called open-circuit and short-circuit parameters.

  1. Set I2 = 0, so port 2 is open. Then A = V1/V2 and C = I1/V2.
  2. Set V2 = 0, so port 2 is short. Then B = V1/(−I2) and D = I1/(−I2).
  3. Record the units: A and D are dimensionless; B is in ohms; C is in siemens.
  4. For a reciprocal network, AD − BC = 1; for a symmetric network, A = D.

Reciprocity and symmetry are separate tests. A reciprocal network can be unsymmetric, so AD − BC = 1 may hold while A is not equal to D. A symmetric network has A = D, but reciprocity still needs its own check.

AD − BC = 1 (reciprocal), A = D (symmetric)

The first condition marks a reciprocal two-port; the second marks a symmetric two-port. A and D are dimensionless, B is in ohms and C is in siemens.

Cascading two-ports

The main reason for ABCD parameters is cascade. Connect port 2 of the first network to port 1 of the second. The output variables of the first become the input variables of the second, so the matrices multiply in signal order.

ABCD = ABCD1 × ABCD2

ABCD1 is the matrix of the block nearest port 1; ABCD2 is the matrix of the block nearest port 2. Multiplication is ordinary matrix multiplication, not element by element.

  1. Write each block as [[A, B], [C, D]] with its own values.
  2. Multiply in signal order: first block on the left, second on the right.
  3. Compute A = A1A2 + B1C2, B = A1B2 + B1D2, C = C1A2 + D1C2, D = C1B2 + D1D2.
  4. Check AD − BC = 1 if the total network is reciprocal.

Swapping the order gives a different matrix unless the two blocks are identical or commute. In a cascade, order is part of the circuit.

Worked example

Question. Two two-ports are cascaded in signal order. The first has A1 = 2, B1 = 3 Ω, C1 = 1 S, D1 = 2. The second has A2 = 1, B2 = 0 Ω, C2 = 0.5 S, D2 = 1. Find the overall ABCD matrix. Also verify reciprocity of the cascade.

  1. Write the matrices: M1 = [[2, 3], [1, 2]] and M2 = [[1, 0], [0.5, 1]].
  2. Compute A = A1A2 + B1C2 = 2×1 + 3×0.5 = 2 + 1.5 = 3.5.
  3. Compute B = A1B2 + B1D2 = 2×0 + 3×1 = 0 + 3 = 3 Ω.
  4. Compute C = C1A2 + D1C2 = 1×1 + 2×0.5 = 1 + 1 = 2 S.
  5. Compute D = C1B2 + D1D2 = 1×0 + 2×1 = 0 + 2 = 2.
  6. Check reciprocity: AD − BC = 3.5×2 − 3×2 = 7 − 6 = 1. Both blocks are reciprocal, so the cascade is reciprocal.

Answer. ABCD = [[3.5, 3 Ω], [2 S, 2]]; the cascade is reciprocal because AD − BC = 1.

Exam traps

  • The minus sign on I2 is part of the convention. If you keep I2 as entering port 2 but drop the minus sign and write [V1; I1] = [[A, B], [C, D]][V2; I2], then B and D change sign. Drawing I2 as leaving port 2 and writing the equation with +I2 leaves the values of A, B, C, D unchanged.
  • Matrix multiplication order matters. ABCD1 × ABCD2 is not the same as ABCD2 × ABCD1 in general.
  • Do not swap the units: B is an impedance in ohms, while C is an admittance in siemens.
  • Open and short conditions get mixed up. A and C use I2 = 0 (open output); B and D use V2 = 0 (short output).
  • AD − BC = 1 tests reciprocity, not symmetry. Symmetry is A = D, and the two tests can pass or fail independently.

6.5 Relationships and conversions between parameter sets

Every two-port parameter set is the same pair of linear equations written in a different order. Z, Y, h, g and ABCD each choose different independent and dependent variables, so one matrix follows from another by algebra. This matters when a device data sheet gives h parameters but a cascade calculation needs ABCD, or when a measured Z matrix must be checked against a Y matrix. The conversion is exact when the denominator parameter is nonzero; if it is zero, that route does not exist.

You need: Z and Y parameters, h and g parameters, ABCD parameters, plus matrix algebra and determinants.

One network, five ways to write it

A linear two-port has only two independent equations. Each parameter set chooses a different pair of variables to put on the right side. The physics does not change; only the algebra changes.

V<sub>1</sub> = z<sub>11</sub> I<sub>1</sub> + z<sub>12</sub> I<sub>2</sub>; V<sub>2</sub> = z<sub>21</sub> I<sub>1</sub> + z<sub>22</sub> I<sub>2</sub>

V1, V2 are port voltages in volts; I1, I2 are port currents in amperes; z11, z12, z21, z22 are Z parameters in ohms.

I<sub>1</sub> = y<sub>11</sub> V<sub>1</sub> + y<sub>12</sub> V<sub>2</sub>; I<sub>2</sub> = y<sub>21</sub> V<sub>1</sub> + y<sub>22</sub> V<sub>2</sub>

y parameters are admittances in siemens (S); the same V and I symbols apply.

V<sub>1</sub> = h<sub>11</sub> I<sub>1</sub> + h<sub>12</sub> V<sub>2</sub>; I<sub>2</sub> = h<sub>21</sub> I<sub>1</sub> + h<sub>22</sub> V<sub>2</sub>

h11 is in ohms, h22 in siemens, h12 and h21 are dimensionless.

I<sub>1</sub> = g<sub>11</sub> V<sub>1</sub> + g<sub>12</sub> I<sub>2</sub>; V<sub>2</sub> = g<sub>21</sub> V<sub>1</sub> + g<sub>22</sub> I<sub>2</sub>

g11 is in siemens, g22 in ohms, g12 and g21 are dimensionless.

V<sub>1</sub> = A V<sub>2</sub> − B I<sub>2</sub>; I<sub>1</sub> = C V<sub>2</sub> − D I<sub>2</sub>

A and D are dimensionless, B is in ohms, C is in siemens; I2 is the current entering port 2.

The determinants measure coupling: Δz = z11z22 − z12z21 (Ω²), Δy = y11y22 − y12y21 (S²), Δh = h11h22 − h12h21 (dimensionless), Δg = g11g22 − g12g21 (dimensionless).

Any two sets are linked by solving one pair of equations for the other pair. A matrix inverse works only when the determinant is nonzero; a conversion formula may fail earlier when its divisor is zero.

6.6 Interconnection of two-ports: series, parallel and cascade

A filter, an attenuator pad, a length of transmission line — each is a two-port, and a large network is often built by wiring small two-ports together. Three connections cover almost every case. In series the port voltages add, so the Z matrices add. In parallel the port currents add, so the Y matrices add. In cascade the output of one two-port feeds the input of the next, so the ABCD matrices multiply. Each rule is a shortcut that saves you from solving the whole network from scratch, and the series and parallel rules each carry a port condition that must be checked first.

You need: 6.2 for Z and Y parameters, 6.4 for ABCD parameters, and 6.5 for converting between them.

Series connection: Z matrices add

Join port 1 of network A in series with port 1 of network B, and port 2 of A in series with port 2 of B. This is a series–series connection. The two networks sit back to back, so the current entering the pair at port 1 must pass through both of them.

The port currents are therefore shared: I1 flows through A and through B, and I2 flows through both on the output side. The port voltages split: V1 = V1a + V1b and V2 = V2a + V2b. Each network obeys V = ZI, so adding the two voltage equations gives the combined matrix.

Z = Z1 + Z2

Z, Z1, Z2 are 2×2 impedance matrices in ohms, Z1 for the first network and Z2 for the second; the addition is element by element, so z11 = z11a + z11b, z12 = z12a + z12b, z21 = z21a + z21b, z22 = z22a + z22b.

Port condition. The rule holds only if no current circulates between the two networks at the junctions. The only conducting paths between A and B must be the two port wires. If a third path exists — a common ground wire, a shared resistor — current loops through it, the port currents of A and B are no longer equal, and Z1 + Z2 is wrong.

Parallel connection: Y matrices add

Now connect port 1 of A across port 1 of B, and port 2 of A across port 2 of B. Both inputs see the same voltage V1 and both outputs see the same voltage V2. This is a parallel–parallel connection.

The port voltages are shared and the currents split: I1 = I1a + I1b and I2 = I2a + I2b. Each network obeys I = YV, so adding the two current equations gives the combined Y matrix.

Y = Y1 + Y2

Y, Y1, Y2 are 2×2 admittance matrices in siemens; the addition is element by element, exactly like the series case.

If your data is in Z form, do not add Z matrices for a parallel connection. Convert each network to Y using 6.5, add, and convert back only if the question asks for Z.

Port condition. Both networks must really see the same port voltage. If a circulating current can flow around the loop formed by the two input ports, or by the two output ports, then the currents you split into I1a and I1b are not the true branch currents and Y1 + Y2 fails. Check that the only connections are the two port pairs.

Cascade: ABCD matrices multiply

In cascade the output port of the first network becomes the input port of the second: V2a = V1b, and the current leaving port 2 of A equals the current entering port 1 of B, so I2a = −I1b with the usual sign convention. Filter chains and attenuator pads are built this way, stage after stage.

ABCD parameters are designed for this. With I2 defined as entering port 2 (the usual two-port convention), the chain matrix T satisfies [V1; I1] = T [V2; −I2]. The minus sign turns the entering current into the current drawn outward at port 2, which is what the next stage receives.

T = T1 × T2

T is the ABCD matrix of the pair, T1 belongs to the network nearest the input, T2 to the network nearest the output, and × is ordinary 2×2 matrix multiplication, not element-by-element addition.

Order matters. With T1 = [[A1, B1], [C1, D1]] and T2 = [[A2, B2], [C2, D2]]: A = A1A2 + B1C2, B = A1B2 + B1D2, C = C1A2 + D1C2, D = C1B2 + D1D2. The input-side matrix sits on the left.

Cascade has no port condition. The output current of one network is the input current of the next by construction, so the product rule always holds for linear two-ports. Three or more stages multiply left to right.

6.7 Symmetry and reciprocity conditions

Reciprocity and symmetry are two tests you can run on a two-port without opening it. A reciprocal network sends a signal equally well in both directions: the transfer term from port 1 to port 2 equals the transfer term from port 2 to port 1. A symmetric network looks the same from either port. A general two-port is fixed by four independent parameters, for example z11, z12, z21 and z22. Reciprocity removes one, leaving three; symmetry removes one more, leaving two. These conditions also simplify the input impedance, output impedance and gain of a terminated two-port.

You need Z and Y parameters, h parameters, ABCD parameters, and the conversion rules; see also Unit 2, Reciprocity theorem.

Reciprocity: equal transfer both ways

Reciprocity is a property of a passive linear two-port made of R, L, C and mutual inductance. In such a network, the transfer from port 1 to port 2 equals the transfer from port 2 to port 1. Drive port 1 and open-circuit port 2: the voltage V2 divided by I1 is z21. Drive port 2 and open-circuit port 1: V1 divided by I2 is z12. Reciprocity says z12 = z21.

z12 = z21, y12 = y21, h12 = −h21, AD − BC = 1

z12 and z21 are open-circuit transfer impedances in Ω; y12 and y21 are short-circuit transfer admittances in S; h12 and h21 are hybrid reverse voltage ratio and forward current gain; A, B, C, D are ABCD parameters. The minus sign in h12 = −h21 comes from I2 being defined as leaving port 2, whereas the z and y sets define both currents as entering the two-port. For ABCD, AD − BC = 1 is the reciprocity test, not the symmetry test.

Use reciprocity as a quick check. Measure z12 and z21 on a passive network; a large difference means a dependent source or a measurement error. See Z and Y parameters and ABCD parameters.

Symmetry: same from either port

Symmetry is a port-interchange property. Swap port 1 and port 2, and the two-port looks identical. Its input impedance with the output open equals its output impedance with the input open.

z11 = z22, y11 = y22, A = D, Δh = h11 h22 − h12 h21 = 1

z11 is the input impedance at port 1 with port 2 open, in Ω; z22 is the output impedance at port 2 with port 1 open, in Ω; y11 and y22 are short-circuit input and output admittances in S; A and D are ABCD voltage and current ratios; h11, h12, h21, h22 are hybrid parameters and Δh is their determinant. In h parameters the symmetry test is Δh = 1, not Δh = 0.

A symmetric passive linear network is reciprocal. The reverse is false: reciprocity does not force symmetry. A reciprocal T network can have z11 = 6 Ω and z22 = 7 Ω. It passes signals equally both ways, but it does not look the same from both ports.

Using the conditions on a terminated two-port

These conditions reduce the algebra when a two-port is terminated. Put a load ZL at port 2 and a source resistance ZS at port 1.

Zin = z11 − z12 z21/(z22 + ZL)

Zin is the input impedance looking into port 1, in Ω; z11, z12, z21, z22 are the Z parameters in Ω; ZL is the load impedance at port 2 in Ω. The term z12 z21/(z22 + ZL) is the loading correction. If the network is reciprocal, z12 z21 = z12². If it is symmetric, z11 = z22.

Zout = z22 − z12 z21/(z11 + ZS)

Zout is the output impedance looking into port 2 with the source killed and ZS connected at port 1, in Ω; ZS is the source resistance in Ω. The correction now uses z11 + ZS.

V2/V1 = z21 ZL/(Δz + z11 ZL)

V2/V1 is the voltage gain from port 1 to port 2 with load ZL; Δz = z11 z22 − z12 z21, in Ω²; ZL is in Ω. For a reciprocal network z12 = z21, so Δz = z11 z22 − z12².

Derivation of Zin: the load gives V2 = −I2 ZL. Substitute this into the Z equations, eliminate I2, and divide V1 by I1. The same steps give Zout. Hybrid h parameters are used in negative-feedback amplifier analysis; the design detail belongs to electronics courses.

Worked example

Question. A T network has z11 = 6 Ω, z12 = 4 Ω, z21 = 4 Ω and z22 = 7 Ω. Test reciprocity and symmetry. Find Zin when a 3 Ω load is connected at port 2.

  1. List the data: z11 = 6 Ω, z12 = 4 Ω, z21 = 4 Ω, z22 = 7 Ω, ZL = 3 Ω.
  2. Test reciprocity by comparing z12 and z21: z12 = 4 Ω and z21 = 4 Ω. They are equal, so the network is reciprocal.
  3. Test symmetry by comparing z11 and z22: z11 = 6 Ω and z22 = 7 Ω. They are not equal, so the network is not symmetric.
  4. Compute z22 + ZL = 7 Ω + 3 Ω = 10 Ω.
  5. Compute z12 z21 = 4 Ω × 4 Ω = 16 Ω².
  6. Divide: 16 Ω² / 10 Ω = 1.6 Ω.
  7. Zin = z11 − z12 z21/(z22 + ZL) = 6 Ω − 1.6 Ω = 4.4 Ω.

Answer. The network is reciprocal, not symmetric, and its input impedance with the 3 Ω load is 4.4 Ω.

Exam traps

  • Reciprocity is not symmetry. A reciprocal two-port may have z11 ≠ z22.
  • In h parameters reciprocity is h12 = −h21. The minus sign is part of the test.
  • AD − BC = 1 tests reciprocity, not symmetry. Symmetry in ABCD is A = D.
  • In h parameters symmetry is Δh = h11 h22 − h12 h21 = 1, not Δh = 0.
  • Zin uses z22 + ZL in the denominator. Zout uses z11 + ZS. Swapping them is a common error.

6.8 Network synthesis: RC and LC functions, Foster and Cauer

Network synthesis is the reverse of circuit analysis. Analysis starts with a circuit and finds its driving-point impedance Z(s). Synthesis starts with Z(s) and asks whether a passive R, L, C one-port can produce it, and how. The answer uses two tests: the positive-real test, and the placement of poles and zeros. LC and RC one-ports have strict patterns, and Foster and Cauer forms turn those patterns into actual circuits. This matters when you must design, not just analyse, a filter or matching network.

You need the Laplace transform and network functions from Unit 5, and impedance from Unit 3.

Driving-point impedance and the positive-real test

A one-port is a network seen through one pair of terminals. Its driving-point impedance is Z(s) = V(s)/I(s), where V(s) and I(s) are the Laplace transforms of terminal voltage and current. Its driving-point admittance is Y(s) = I(s)/V(s) = 1/Z(s).

A rational function Z(s) is positive-real, written PR, when two conditions hold. First, Z(s) is real for every real value of s. Second, Re Z(s) ≥ 0 for every s with Re s > 0. For a rational function this is equivalent to: Z(s) has no poles in Re s > 0, and Re Z(jω) ≥ 0 at every frequency where Z(jω) is finite on the jω axis. A pole on the jω axis is allowed only if it is simple and its residue is non-negative. The first condition makes the coefficients real. The second says the one-port never delivers average power to a sine source.

PR is the gatekeeper test for a passive one-port. A resistor Z(s) = R passes: Re R = R ≥ 0. A capacitor Z(s) = 1/(sC) gives Re Z(jω) = 0. An inductor Z(s) = sL also gives Re Z(jω) = 0. Any network built only from R, L and C must pass the PR test.

LC and RC realizability

For an LC one-port, Z(s) is a reactance function. Its finite nonzero poles and zeros are simple and lie on the jω axis. They alternate along that axis. An LC impedance may also have a pole or zero at s = 0, and may have a pole or zero at infinity; these endpoints are allowed. The finite nonzero residues are positive, and the origin and infinity terms in the Foster expansion are non-negative. The general Foster expansion is Z(s) = k∞s + k0/s + Σ 2k_i s/(s² + ω_i²), where k∞, k0 and each k_i are non-negative. The sum runs over the finite nonzero pole frequencies ω_i.

For an RC one-port, all finite nonzero poles and zeros lie on the negative real axis, at s = −σ with σ > 0. They are simple and alternate. An RC impedance may have a pole at s = 0 and a zero at infinity; these endpoints are allowed. Every finite nonzero residue is positive, and the endpoint terms have non-negative coefficients. A typical RC impedance has the form Z(s) = K∞ + K0/s + Σ K_i/(s + σ_i), with all constants non-negative.

One-portWhere poles and zeros lie
LCFinite nonzero critical frequencies on jω axis; simple; alternate; positive finite residues; pole or zero at 0 or ∞ allowed
RCFinite nonzero critical frequencies on negative real axis; simple; alternate; positive finite residues; pole at 0 and zero at ∞ allowed

Positive coefficients alone do not prove realizability. A function can have all positive coefficients and still fail because its poles or zeros are in the wrong place. Check the axis and the alternation, then check the residues.

Foster and Cauer forms

Foster forms use partial fractions. Foster I expands Z(s) into a sum of series branches. A term k0/s is a capacitor, k∞s is an inductor, and 2k_i s/(s² + ω_i²) is a parallel LC branch. Foster II expands Y(s) into a sum of parallel branches.

Cauer forms use a continued fraction. Cauer I removes a pole at infinity repeatedly, giving a ladder of series inductors and shunt capacitors. Cauer II removes a pole at zero repeatedly. The ladder is convenient when you want a physical circuit with few element values.

Worked example

Question. Test whether Z(s) = (s² + 1)/(s(s² + 4)) is an LC driving-point impedance. If yes, write its Foster I form and give element values. Use s in rad/s, so element values are normalized.

  1. Write the denominator as s(s² + 4). The poles are s = 0 and s = ±j2. The numerator gives zeros at s² + 1 = 0, so s = ±j1.
  2. The critical frequencies on the jω axis are: pole at 0, zero at 1, pole at 2. They alternate, and all are simple. This matches an LC impedance.
  3. Expand in partial fractions: Z(s) = A/s + (Bs + C)/(s² + 4). Multiply out: A(s² + 4) + s(Bs + C) = s² + 1. Compare coefficients: 4A = 1, so A = 1/4; A + B = 1, so B = 3/4; C = 0.
  4. The expansion is Z(s) = 1/(4s) + (3/4)s/(s² + 4). Both residues, 1/4 and 3/4, are positive, so the function is realizable.
  5. Read the Foster I branches. The first branch 1/(4s) is a series capacitor: 1/(sC) = 1/(4s) gives C = 4 F. The second branch (3/4)s/(s² + 4) is a parallel LC branch. For parallel L and C, Z = (s/C)/(s² + 1/(LC)). Comparing, 1/C = 3/4 gives C = 4/3 F, and 1/(LC) = 4 gives L = 3/16 H.

Answer. Yes, it is an LC driving-point impedance. Foster I is a 4 F capacitor in series with a parallel LC branch of L = 3/16 H and C = 4/3 F, with normalized values for s in rad/s.

Exam traps

  • Positive coefficients do not prove an LC or RC function. Check that finite nonzero poles and zeros lie on the correct axis and alternate, with the allowed origin and infinity exceptions.
  • For an LC impedance, the finite nonzero residues in the partial-fraction expansion must be positive; endpoint coefficients must be non-negative. A negative residue means the function is not passive.
  • Do not mix up Foster I and Foster II. Foster I sums Z(s) into series branches; Foster II sums Y(s) into parallel branches.
  • For an RC impedance, finite nonzero critical frequencies must lie on the negative real axis. A pole at the origin and a zero at infinity are allowed; a nonzero pole on the jω axis is not.
  • In Foster I, the term k0/s gives C = 1/k0. Students often write C = k0 and lose the reciprocal.

Takeaways

  1. A two-port is a black box described by only two equations linking four external variables, so any two of the four can be chosen as inputs.
  2. All six parameter sets are the same two linear equations rearranged; pick the set whose measurements are easiest in the lab or whose interconnection rule is simplest.
  3. Z parameters are measured with a port open, Y parameters with a port shorted, h parameters with a port shorted and open mixed, ABCD with the output open and shorted.
  4. Series adds Z matrices, parallel adds Y matrices, cascade multiplies ABCD matrices, and each rule holds only if the interconnection does not disturb the port currents or voltages.
  5. Reciprocity is a property of networks without dependent sources; it shows up as equal off-diagonal terms in Z and Y and as AD - BC = 1 in ABCD.
  6. Symmetry is about the network looking the same from both ends; it gives z11 = z22, y11 = y22 and A = D but says nothing about the transfer terms.
  7. Network synthesis is the reverse of analysis: it asks whether a given Z(s) or Y(s) can be built, and the answer is yes only if the function is positive real.
  8. Foster and Cauer forms turn a positive real reactance function into a circuit of L and C by partial fractions or by continued fractions, and both give the same input impedance.

Glossary

Cascade
A connection in which the output port of one two-port becomes the input port of the next.
Cauer form
A ladder realisation obtained by continued-fraction expansion of an impedance or admittance function.

The ratio V/I at the same port, with the other port terminated in a stated way.
Foster form
A realisation obtained by partial-fraction expansion of a reactance or susceptance function.
Hurwitz polynomial
A polynomial whose roots all lie in the left half of the s plane.

A parameter of the set giving output variables in terms of input variables, the inverse of the ABCD matrix.

A chain of series arms and shunt arms, the natural result of Cauer expansion.

The design of a network of R, L and C elements that realises a prescribed driving-point or transfer function.

A Z parameter measured with one port left open so that its current is zero.
Parallel connection
A connection in which the corresponding port currents add while the port voltages stay equal.
Pole-zero alternation
The property of LC and RC functions that poles and zeros interleave along the negative real axis or the imaginary axis.
Port
A pair of terminals carrying equal and opposite currents.

A rational function that is real for real s and has non-negative real part in the right half plane.

The property that the transfer parameter from port 1 to 2 equals the transfer parameter from 2 to 1.

A connection in which the corresponding port voltages of two networks add while the port currents stay equal.
