About this unit

The first two chapters turn Kirchhoff's laws into methods: mesh analysis writes KVL with mesh currents and handles current sources with a supermesh; nodal analysis writes KCL with node voltages and handles voltage sources with a supernode. 2.1 Mesh analysis and supermesh

Mesh analysis turns Kirchhoff's voltage law into a short set of simultaneous equations. Every window of a flat (planar) circuit gets its own circulating mesh current, and you write one KVL equation per window. When a current source sits on a branch shared by two meshes, the two are treated together as a supermesh. Mesh analysis suits circuits with many series elements and voltage sources, such as battery packs and bridges.

You need: Ohm's law, Kirchhoff's voltage and current laws, and the passive sign convention (circuit elements and laws).

Meshes, mesh currents and how many equations

A circuit is planar when you can draw it on paper with no branch crossing another. A mesh is a loop that has no other loop inside it: one window of the drawing. A circuit with b branches and n nodes has exactly b − n + 1 meshes, so mesh analysis needs b − n + 1 equations.

Give each mesh a clockwise current i₁, i₂, and so on. An outside branch belongs to one mesh, so its current is that mesh current. A shared branch carries two mesh currents in opposite directions, so its current is the difference. In the top circuit of the figure the 4 Ω resistor is shared: its downward current is i₁ − i₂.

Why it works: KCL is satisfied automatically, because each mesh current enters and leaves every node it passes. Only KVL is left to write, one equation per mesh, and each equation contains only mesh currents.

Writing the mesh equations by inspection

  1. Check that the circuit is planar, number its meshes and draw every mesh current clockwise.
  2. For mesh k, walk round clockwise and add the voltage drops: each resistor in the mesh gives R × ik, and a shared resistor R gives R × (ik − ij), where j is the neighbouring mesh.
  3. Put the sources on the right side: a source counts positive when the walk goes from its − terminal to its + terminal (a rise in the clockwise direction).
  4. Solve the equations, then find branch currents and voltages from the mesh currents.

Together the equations form R i = v. The diagonal entry Rkk, the self-resistance, is the sum of the resistances in mesh k. The off-diagonal entry Rkj is minus the mutual resistance shared by meshes k and j. The right side vk is the net source rise in mesh k. With independent sources only the matrix is symmetric, a quick check on your working.

Dependent sources. A controlled source stays in the KVL equation, with its value written in mesh currents: in the top circuit 2iₓ = 2(i₁ − i₂). Move every mesh-current term to the left. The matrix is then usually not symmetric, which is correct.

Current sources and the supermesh

A current source fixes a current, but its voltage is set by the rest of the circuit, so a KVL equation through it would carry a new unknown.

  • Source on an outside branch (in one mesh only): it fixes that mesh current directly, for example i₃ = −2 A if a 2 A source points against the clockwise direction. One equation fewer to solve.
  • Source on a shared branch: join the two meshes into a supermesh. Write one KVL equation round their outer path, leaving out the shared branch, plus the constraint the source sets.

Sign rule for the constraint: compare the source arrow with each mesh current where it passes through the shared branch. In the bottom circuit of the figure the 3 A source points up. Clockwise i₂ goes up through that branch and clockwise i₁ goes down, so the upward current is i₂ − i₁ = 3 A. A resistor in series with the current source (the 5 Ω) carries the fixed 3 A and lies off the supermesh path, so it never enters the KVL equation.

The same steps work for AC circuits with impedances in place of resistances (see applying network theorems to AC circuits).

Worked example

Question. In the top circuit of Figure 2.1.1 (24 V source, 2 Ω top-left and top-right, 4 Ω in the shared branch, and a controlled source 2iₓ, + at the top, in the right branch, where iₓ is the downward current in the 4 Ω), find the mesh currents and iₓ, and check the power balance.

  1. Draw i₁ (left) and i₂ (right) clockwise. The downward current in the 4 Ω resistor is iₓ = i₁ − i₂, so the controlled source is 2(i₁ − i₂) V.
  2. Mesh 1, walking clockwise: the 24 V source is a rise; drops are 2i₁ and 4(i₁ − i₂). So 2i₁ + 4(i₁ − i₂) = 24, that is 6i₁ − 4i₂ = 24.
  3. Mesh 2, clockwise: 2i₂ in the top-right resistor, 2(i₁ − i₂) down through the controlled source from + to −, and 4(i₂ − i₁) up through the 4 Ω. So 2i₂ + 2(i₁ − i₂) + 4(i₂ − i₁) = 0, that is −2i₁ + 4i₂ = 0.
  4. From mesh 2, i₁ = 2i₂. Substitute in mesh 1: 6(2i₂) − 4i₂ = 24, so 8i₂ = 24 and i₂ = 3 A. Then i₁ = 6 A.
  5. iₓ = i₁ − i₂ = 3 A, so the controlled source is 2 × 3 = 6 V.
  6. Power check: the 24 V source delivers 24 × 6 = 144 W. Absorbed: 6² × 2 = 72 W, 3² × 4 = 36 W, 3² × 2 = 18 W, and the controlled source takes 3 A into its + terminal, 6 × 3 = 18 W. Total 144 W.

Answer. i₁ = 6 A, i₂ = 3 A, iₓ = 3 A (controlled source 6 V); 144 W delivered and 144 W absorbed.

Worked example 2

Question. In the bottom circuit of Figure 2.1.1 (24 V source, 2 Ω top-left, 5 Ω in series with a 3 A source pointing up in the shared branch, 3 Ω top-right, 1 Ω on the right), find the mesh currents and the voltage across the current source.

  1. The 3 A source is on the shared branch, so meshes 1 and 2 form a supermesh.
  2. Constraint: the source points up; i₂ flows up and i₁ flows down in that branch, so i₂ − i₁ = 3 A.
  3. Supermesh KVL round the outer path (24 V source, 2 Ω, 3 Ω, 1 Ω): 2i₁ + 3i₂ + 1i₂ = 24, that is 2i₁ + 4i₂ = 24. The 5 Ω is not on this path.
  4. Substitute i₂ = i₁ + 3: 2i₁ + 4i₁ + 12 = 24, so 6i₁ = 12, i₁ = 2 A and i₂ = 5 A.
  5. Voltage of the top junction above the bottom wire: 24 − 2 × 2 = 20 V (check from the right: 5 × (3 + 1) = 20 V).
  6. The 3 A flows up through the 5 Ω, so the bottom of the 5 Ω is 5 × 3 = 15 V above its top: the current source's upper terminal is at 20 + 15 = 35 V. The source has 35 V across it, upper end positive.
  7. Power check: the 24 V source delivers 24 × 2 = 48 W and the current source delivers 35 × 3 = 105 W, total 153 W. Absorbed: 2² × 2 + 5² × 3 + 5² × 1 + 3² × 5 = 8 + 75 + 25 + 45 = 153 W.

Answer. i₁ = 2 A, i₂ = 5 A; the current source has 35 V across it and delivers 105 W.

Exam traps

  • Writing KVL through a current source as if its voltage were zero. Use the supermesh, or fix the mesh current for an outside branch.
  • Getting the constraint sign backwards. In worked example 2 it is i₂ − i₁ = 3 A; i₁ − i₂ = 3 A solves a different circuit.
  • Putting the resistor in series with the current source into the supermesh equation. It is off the outer path.
  • Writing the mutual term with a plus sign. With all mesh currents clockwise, a shared resistor always enters as R(ik − ij) in mesh k.
  • Expecting a symmetric matrix when a dependent source is present, and 'correcting' a correct answer.

2.2 Nodal analysis and supernode

Nodal analysis turns Kirchhoff's current law into simultaneous equations whose unknowns are node voltages. Pick one node as the reference (0 V), give every other node a voltage, and write KCL at each of them using Ohm's law for the resistor currents. A circuit with n nodes needs only n − 1 equations, however many branches it has. When an ideal voltage source joins two non-reference nodes, its current is unknown, so the two nodes are treated together as a supernode: one KCL equation for the pair plus the equation the source fixes. Nodal analysis is what circuit simulators use, and it is the quicker hand method for circuits with many parallel branches and current sources.

You need: Ohm's law, KCL, conductance G = 1/R, and the idea of a reference node (circuit elements and laws). Mesh analysis and supermesh is the companion method.

Node voltages and the KCL equations

Choose the reference node, usually the bottom wire or the node with the most branches, and call it 0 V (ground). The node voltage vk is the voltage of node k above the reference. The voltage across any resistor is then a difference of two node voltages, so its current follows from Ohm's law: a resistor R from node k to node j carries (vk − vj)/R out of node k.

  1. Mark the reference node and label the other n − 1 node voltages v₁, v₂, ….
  2. At each non-reference node write KCL: the sum of currents leaving through resistors equals the sum of source currents entering.
  3. Write every resistor current as (own node voltage − neighbour's voltage)/R, always leaving the node you are at. This one habit removes most sign errors.
  4. Write each dependent source in node voltages.
  5. Solve, then find any branch current from two node voltages.

In matrix form the equations are G v = i. The diagonal entry Gkk is the sum of the conductances touching node k; the off-diagonal Gkj is minus the conductance joining nodes k and j; ik is the source current injected into node k. With resistors and independent current sources only, G is symmetric. Some syllabi call this the node-voltage method.

Dependent sources in nodal analysis

A controlled source stays in the KCL equation and its value is written in node voltages. In worked example 1 the controlled current is 0.5vₓ, where vₓ is the voltage across the 2 Ω resistor, + at node 1. So vₓ = v₁ − v₂ and the source injects 0.5(v₁ − v₂) into node 2. Moving that term to the left side changes two entries of row 2, so G loses its symmetry. That is expected.

If the controlling quantity is a current, write it from node voltages first: a current iy through a resistor R from node a to node b is (va − vb)/R. Then substitute. The number of unknowns stays n − 1.

Voltage sources and the supernode

  • Voltage source from a node to the reference: it fixes that node voltage, for example v₁ = 12 V. That node needs no KCL equation. This is not a supernode.
  • Voltage source between two non-reference nodes (a floating source): its current is unknown. Draw a closed boundary round both nodes and the source, the supernode. Write one KCL for every current crossing the boundary (the source current stays inside and never appears), and add the constraint from the source.

v+ − v− = Vs

v+ is the voltage of the node at the source's + terminal, v− the voltage of the node at its − terminal, and Vs the source voltage in volts.

Counting check: the supernode replaces two KCL equations by one KCL plus one constraint, so the number of equations still equals the number of unknown node voltages. After solving, KCL at either end of the floating source gives its current. For AC circuits the same steps work with admittances Y = 1/Z in place of conductances (see applying network theorems to AC circuits).

Worked example

Question. In the top circuit of Figure 2.2.1, a 2 A source drives current from the reference into node 1. Node 1 has 8 Ω to the reference and 2 Ω to node 2. Node 2 has 3 Ω to the reference and a voltage-controlled current source of 0.5vₓ amperes driving current from the reference into node 2, where vₓ is the voltage across the 2 Ω resistor, + at node 1. Find v₁, v₂ and check the power balance.

  1. Write the control voltage in node voltages: vₓ = v₁ − v₂.
  2. KCL at node 1 (leaving = entering): v₁/8 + (v₁ − v₂)/2 = 2.
  3. KCL at node 2: (v₂ − v₁)/2 + v₂/3 = 0.5(v₁ − v₂).
  4. Clear fractions. Node 1 × 8: v₁ + 4v₁ − 4v₂ = 16, so 5v₁ − 4v₂ = 16. Node 2 × 6: 3v₂ − 3v₁ + 2v₂ = 3v₁ − 3v₂, so −6v₁ + 8v₂ = 0, that is v₁ = (4/3)v₂.
  5. Substitute: 5(4/3)v₂ − 4v₂ = 16, so (20/3 − 12/3)v₂ = 16, (8/3)v₂ = 16 and v₂ = 6 V. Then v₁ = 8 V.
  6. vₓ = 8 − 6 = 2 V, so the controlled source injects 0.5 × 2 = 1 A into node 2.
  7. Power check: the 2 A source delivers 2 × 8 = 16 W and the controlled source delivers 1 × 6 = 6 W, total 22 W. Absorbed: 8²/8 + 2²/2 + 6²/3 = 8 + 2 + 12 = 22 W.

Answer. v₁ = 8 V, v₂ = 6 V; the controlled source supplies 1 A; 22 W delivered and absorbed.

Exam traps

  • Calling a source from a node to the reference a supernode. It simply fixes that node voltage; a supernode needs a source between two non-reference nodes.
  • Writing KCL separately at the two ends of a floating voltage source. Each equation would contain the source's unknown current; write one supernode KCL instead.
  • Reversing the constraint. In worked example 2, v₂ − v₃ = 6 instead of v₃ − v₂ = 6 gives v₂ = 8.77 V and v₃ = 2.77 V, the voltages of a different circuit.
  • Mixing directions inside one KCL equation. Write every resistor current as leaving the node you are at, (own − neighbour)/R, and put source currents entering on the right side.
  • Leaving the control variable as an extra unknown. Write vₓ or iₓ in node voltages before solving.

2.3 Linearity and superposition theorem

A circuit with several sources can be solved one source at a time. In a linear circuit, every voltage and current is a weighted sum of the independent source values, so the response to all sources together equals the sum of the responses to each source acting alone. That is the superposition theorem. It explains why a small signal can ride on a DC bias, why an audio mixer adds its inputs, and why one source can be studied while the others are switched off. It applies to voltages and currents, never directly to power.

You need: mesh analysis or nodal analysis, and series-parallel reduction.

Linearity: scaling and adding

Treat each independent source as an input x and any chosen voltage or current as the output y. A circuit is linear when it has two properties.

  • Homogeneity (scaling): multiply every input by k and the output is multiplied by k.
  • Additivity: the output for inputs x₁ and x₂ together is the output for x₁ alone plus the output for x₂ alone.

Resistors (v = Ri), inductors (v = L di/dt) and capacitors (i = C dv/dt) are linear elements: each relation is linear in the element's own variables. A dependent source with a constant gain (μ, g, r or β) is linear too. Diodes, transistors driven into saturation and lamps whose resistance changes with temperature are not.

y = a₁x₁ + a₂x₂ + … + aNxN

y is any voltage or current in the circuit; xk is the value of independent source k; ak is a constant set by the resistances and dependent-source gains; N is the number of independent sources.

Why it holds: nodal or mesh analysis of a linear circuit gives equations whose coefficients are fixed numbers and whose right sides contain only the source values. The solution of such equations is a weighted sum of the right sides. Homogeneity also gives a short method for ladder networks, the unit-output method: assume 1 A (or 1 V) in the far branch, work back to the source value this needs, then scale everything by the true source value.

The superposition procedure

  1. List the independent sources. Keep one and switch off all the others.
  2. Switch off a voltage source by making it 0 V: replace it by a short circuit (a wire). Switch off a current source by making it 0 A: replace it by an open circuit (a gap). Any internal resistance stays in place.
  3. Leave every dependent source in the circuit, still controlled by its variable.
  4. Solve the reduced circuit for the wanted voltage or current, marking its sign against a fixed reference direction.
  5. Repeat for each independent source in turn, then add the partial answers with their signs.

y = y′ + y″ + … (one term per source)

y′ is the response with only the first independent source on, y″ with only the second, and so on.

Sources may also be switched on in groups, as long as every source is on in exactly one run. Superposition usually takes more work than one nodal solution for a plain DC circuit; its real uses are to show how much each source contributes, to handle sources of different frequencies (see applying network theorems to AC circuits), and to prove the other theorems of this unit.

Where superposition stops

Power does not superpose. Power is P = I²R or V²/R, a square, and (I′ + I″)²R = I′²R + 2I′I″R + I″²R. The cross term 2I′I″R is missing if you add the partial powers. In worked example 1 the partial powers add to 27 W, but the true power is 54 W. Superpose the current or voltage first, then compute power once.

Dependent sources must not be switched off. Their value is set by a voltage or current that changes from run to run, so they stay in every reduced circuit. In worked example 2, switching off the controlled source gives 9.33 V instead of the correct 9 V.

Nonlinear elements break it. A diode's current is not a weighted sum of the source values, so superposition holds only for small changes about a fixed operating point (the small-signal model).

Worked example

Question. A 12 V source in series with a 2 Ω resistor feeds node A. A 6 Ω resistor joins A to the reference, and a 6 A current source drives current from the reference into A (Figure 2.3.1). Find VA by superposition, then the power in the 6 Ω resistor. Compare with the sum of the two partial powers.

  1. Run 1, 12 V alone: open the 6 A source. The 2 Ω and 6 Ω are in series: I = 12/(2 + 6) = 1.5 A, so VA′ = 1.5 × 6 = 9 V.
  2. Run 2, 6 A alone: short the 12 V source. The 2 Ω now runs from A to the reference, in parallel with the 6 Ω: 2 × 6/(2 + 6) = 1.5 Ω. VA″ = 6 × 1.5 = 9 V.
  3. Add: VA = 9 + 9 = 18 V (both runs make A positive).
  4. Check by nodal analysis with both sources on: (VA − 12)/2 + VA/6 = 6. Times 6: 3VA − 36 + VA = 36, so VA = 18 V.
  5. Power in the 6 Ω: 18²/6 = 54 W.
  6. Partial powers: 9²/6 = 13.5 W in each run, sum 27 W. The difference, 27 W, is the cross term 2 × (9 × 9)/6 = 27 W.

Worked example 2

Question. A 10 V source in series with 2 Ω feeds node a; iₓ is the current in the 2 Ω towards a. From a to the reference there are a 4 Ω resistor and a current-controlled current source of 0.5iₓ that draws current out of a. A 2 A source drives current from the reference into a. Find the voltage v of node a by superposition.

  1. KCL at a in every run: current in through the 2 Ω + source current in = current out through the 4 Ω + 0.5iₓ, with iₓ = (Vs − v)/2 where Vs is the value of the 10 V source in that run.
  2. Run 1, 10 V alone (2 A source open): iₓ = (10 − v)/2 and iₓ = v/4 + 0.5iₓ, so 0.5iₓ = v/4, giving (10 − v)/4 = v/4 and v′ = 5 V (iₓ′ = 2.5 A).
  3. Run 2, 2 A alone (10 V source shorted): iₓ = (0 − v)/2 = −v/2 and iₓ + 2 = v/4 + 0.5iₓ, so 0.5iₓ + 2 = v/4, giving −v/4 + 2 = v/4 and v″ = 4 V (iₓ″ = −2 A). The controlled source stays in this run.
  4. Add: v = 5 + 4 = 9 V, and iₓ = 2.5 − 2 = 0.5 A.
  5. Check with both sources: iₓ = (10 − 9)/2 = 0.5 A; in = 0.5 + 2 = 2.5 A; out = 9/4 + 0.5 × 0.5 = 2.25 + 0.25 = 2.5 A. KCL holds.

Exam traps

  • Shorting a current source or opening a voltage source to switch it off. Off means 0 V (short) for a voltage source and 0 A (open) for a current source.
  • Switching off a dependent source. It stays in every run; worked example 2 shows the error (9.33 V instead of 9 V).
  • Adding powers. Add currents or voltages, then square once: 54 W, not 27 W, in worked example 1.
  • Dropping a sign. Draw one reference direction for the wanted current before the first run and give every partial answer a sign against it.
  • Removing a source's internal resistance along with the source. Only the ideal source is replaced; a 0.5 Ω internal resistance stays.

2.4 Thevenin's and Norton's theorems

However many sources and resistors a linear network holds, at any two of its terminals it behaves exactly like one voltage source in series with one resistor. Thevenin's theorem names them Vth and Rth. Norton's theorem describes the same terminals as one current source IN in parallel with RN = Rth. Once a network is reduced to two numbers, the current in any load follows from one line, which is why engineers model a car battery, a sensor bridge, a mains supply or an amplifier output this way.

You need: linearity and superposition, nodal or mesh analysis, and source transformation (circuit elements and laws).

Thevenin's theorem

Statement. Any linear two-terminal network can be replaced, at its terminals a-b, by a voltage source Vth in series with a resistance Rth. Vth is the open-circuit voltage Voc at a-b. Rth is the resistance seen at a-b with every independent source switched off (voltage sources shorted, current sources opened) and every dependent source left in place.

  1. Decide which element is the load and remove it. Everything else stays: the network is all that remains.
  2. Find Voc = Vth across the open terminals, by any method, and mark its polarity (a positive with respect to b).
  3. Switch off the independent sources and find Rth looking into a-b. With no dependent sources, series-parallel reduction is enough.
  4. Draw Vth in series with Rth, put the load back, and find the load current IL = Vth/(Rth + RL).

Why it works. Replace the load by a current source of value I. By superposition the terminal voltage is the part due to the internal sources, which is Voc (the I run is switched off), plus the part due to I alone, which is −RthI. So V = Voc − RthI for every load: a straight line, and two numbers fix a straight line. A real network is linear only while its elements are; a battery's internal resistance changes with charge and temperature, so its Thevenin values hold for one condition.

Norton's theorem and source transformation

Statement. The same network can be replaced by a current source IN in parallel with RN. IN is the short-circuit current Isc that flows from a to b through a wire joining them, and RN = Rth.

Vth = INRth, Rth = Voc/Isc

Vth in volts, IN = Isc in amperes, Rth = RN in ohms. Any two of Voc, Isc and Rth give the third.

This is source transformation applied to the whole network: a voltage source in series with R becomes a current source V/R in parallel with the same R. Choose whichever form makes the next step short: Thevenin for series loads, Norton when loads are added in parallel. Fix the direction of IN as the current leaving terminal a through the external short; then both forms push current the same way through a load.

Networks with dependent sources

A dependent source cannot be switched off and cannot be merged into a resistance, so series-parallel reduction fails. Use one of two methods.

  • Open circuit and short circuit: find Voc and Isc with all sources on, then Rth = Voc/Isc. Works when the network has at least one independent source.
  • Test source: switch off the independent sources, keep the dependent ones, apply a test voltage Vt at a-b (or a test current), solve for the current It entering terminal a, and take Rth = Vt/It. Needed when the network has only dependent sources, where Vth = 0 and Isc = 0.

With dependent sources Rth can come out smaller than any single resistor, or even negative, which means the network can deliver power from its controlled source. That is a real result, not an arithmetic slip; a negative value from a network of resistors and independent sources only, however, is a slip.

Exam traps

  • Switching sources off the wrong way. A voltage source becomes a short and a current source an open; reversing them changes Rth.

  • Using the equivalent to find power lost inside the original network. The equivalent is exact only at the terminals; its Rth loss is not the network's internal loss.

2.5 Maximum power transfer theorem

When a load is connected to a fixed source, a very small load resistance takes a large current but little voltage, and a very large one takes a large voltage but little current. Somewhere between, the load power is greatest. The maximum power transfer theorem says where: a load takes the most power from a fixed linear network when the load resistance equals the network's Thevenin resistance, RL = Rth, and that power is Vth²/(4Rth). Communication circuits, sensors and antennas are designed for it; power systems deliberately avoid it, because at the maximum-power point half the power or more is lost.

You need: Thevenin's and Norton's theorems and the power formula P = I²R.

Load power from a Thevenin source

Reduce the fixed network to Vth in series with Rth and connect a load RL. One current flows round the loop, I = Vth/(Rth + RL), so the load power is

PL = I²RL = Vth²RL/(Rth + RL)²

where Vth is in volts, Rth and RL in ohms and PL in watts. At RL = 0 the load is a short and takes no power (no voltage). As RL grows very large the current falls towards zero and the power falls with it. The power is positive in between, so it has a peak. For the network of Thevenin's and Norton's theorems (24 V, 4 Ω) the load powers are 23.04 W at 1 Ω, 32 W at 2 Ω, 36 W at 4 Ω, 32 W at 8 Ω and 23.04 W at 16 Ω (Figure 2.5.1).

Proof: the peak is at RL = Rth

  1. Hold Vth and Rth fixed and differentiate PL with respect to RL by the quotient rule: dPL/dRL = Vth²[(Rth + RL)² − 2RL(Rth + RL)]/(Rth + RL)⁴.
  2. Cancel one factor (Rth + RL): dPL/dRL = Vth²(Rth − RL)/(Rth + RL)³.
  3. Set it to zero: Rth − RL = 0, so RL = Rth.
  4. It is a maximum: the derivative is positive for RL below Rth (power rising) and negative above it (power falling).
  5. Substitute RL = Rth: Pmax = Vth²Rth/(2Rth)² = Vth²/(4Rth).

In Norton form the same result reads Pmax = IN²RN/4. Two loads give equal power on either side of the peak when their product is Rth²: 2 Ω × 8 Ω = 16 = 4² in the figure. The curve is flat near its top, so a load within about a factor of two of Rth still takes close to 90 % of Pmax (32 of 36 W).

Maximum power is not maximum efficiency

In the equivalent circuit the source delivers VthI and the load takes I²RL, so the efficiency is η = RL/(Rth + RL). At the maximum-power point η = 50 %: as much power heats Rth as reaches the load. A large load gives a high efficiency but little power.

Two cautions. First, the 50 % belongs to the equivalent circuit only; the real network can waste more, because the theorem says nothing about its internal losses (worked example 1 finds 26.5 %). Second, the theorem fixes the source and varies the load. If the load is fixed and the source resistance can be chosen, the load power is greatest with the smallest possible source resistance, not with Rs = RL.

So the theorem fits weak-signal circuits, where every microwatt counts: an antenna feeding a receiver, a microphone feeding a preamplifier, a solar panel driven to its maximum power point. Mains supplies and power stations are built for high efficiency, with source resistance far below the load. For AC sources the condition becomes the conjugate match ZL = Zth* (see applying network theorems to AC circuits).

2.6 Reciprocity, Millman's and Tellegen's theorems

Three more theorems give shortcuts and checks. Reciprocity: in a linear bilateral network with one source, swapping the source and the ammeter leaves the reading unchanged. Millman's theorem: any number of parallel branches, each a voltage source behind a resistance, collapse to one source behind one resistance, so the common voltage takes one line. Tellegen's theorem: in any circuit that obeys KCL and KVL, the branch powers vkik add up to zero, linear elements or not. Reciprocity underlies antenna and two-port measurements, Millman's theorem fits batteries and generators in parallel, and Tellegen's theorem is the power-balance check used in this book's worked examples.

You need: nodal analysis, source transformation and the passive sign convention.

Reciprocity theorem

Statement. In a linear, bilateral network with a single independent source and no dependent sources, if a voltage source V in branch 1 produces a current I in branch 2, then the same source moved to branch 2 produces the same current I in branch 1. The ratio I/V, the transfer conductance, is the same both ways. A current-source form also holds: a current source in branch 1 gives a voltage across open-circuited branch 2, and swapping source and voltmeter gives the same voltage.

Bilateral means each element behaves the same for either direction of current: R, L and C are bilateral; diodes and transistors are not. All other independent sources must be switched off during the test, and the ammeter is ideal (zero resistance), so the branch it leaves behind is a short.

Why it holds: with R, L and C only, the nodal matrix G is symmetric (Gkj = Gjk), and the transfer from node j to node k is the same as from k to j. A dependent source puts a one-way term into G and breaks the symmetry. In the lab, a two-port that reads differently after a swap contains an active device or a wiring fault.

Millman's theorem

Take n branches in parallel between nodes A and B; branch k is a voltage source Vk (+ towards A) in series with Rk, or a plain resistor (Vk = 0). Source transformation turns each branch into a current Vk/Rk in parallel with Rk; the currents add and the conductances add. So the whole group equals one source VAB behind one resistance Req:

VAB = Σ(Vk/Rk) / Σ(1/Rk)

VAB is the voltage of A above B (volts); Vk is the source in branch k, entered with a minus sign if its + faces B; Rk is the branch resistance (ohms). The equivalent resistance is Req = 1/Σ(1/Rk).

With conductances, VAB = ΣVkGk/ΣGk. A branch that is a current source Ik adds Ik to the top and nothing to the bottom (its resistance is infinite). Branch currents then follow from VAB: the current into A from branch k is (Vk − VAB)/Rk, and these add to zero. Millman's theorem is nodal analysis for a circuit with one unknown node.

Tellegen's theorem

Statement. For any network of b branches whose branch voltages satisfy KVL and whose branch currents satisfy KCL, with each current taken entering the + terminal of its branch voltage (passive convention), Σ vkik = 0 over all b branches. Each term is the power absorbed by branch k, so the theorem is conservation of power: sources' negative terms balance the loads' positive ones.

  1. Proof. Give each node a potential em. KVL holds, so the voltage of a branch from node m to node n is vk = em − en.
  2. Then Σ vkik = Σ (em − en)ik. Collect the terms of each node: node m's potential is multiplied by the total current leaving node m.
  3. By KCL that total is zero at every node, so every group is zero and the sum is zero.

The proof uses only KCL, KVL and the shape of the network (its graph), never what the elements are. So the theorem holds for nonlinear and time-varying elements, at every instant. It even holds with the voltages of one network and the currents of another network of the same graph. Example: keep the voltages of worked example 1 in mesh analysis (24, 12, 12, 6 and 6 V) and take the currents of the same graph with a 12 V source and a 2 Ω resistor in place of the controlled source (3, 3, 1.5, 1.5 and 1.5 A): −24 × 3 + 12 × 3 + 12 × 1.5 + 6 × 1.5 + 6 × 1.5 = −72 + 36 + 18 + 9 + 9 = 0. Reciprocity can be proved from this form.

As a check: in worked example 1 of mesh analysis the branch powers are −144 W (source), 72, 36, 18 and 18 W, which add to zero. A nonzero total after solving a circuit means a sign slip or a missing branch.

2.7 Compensation theorem

Change one resistor in a solved circuit, or insert an ammeter, and every current shifts a little. The compensation theorem finds those shifts without solving the whole circuit again. It replaces the change by one small voltage source, the compensating source Vc = IΔZ, and the currents this source drives on its own are exactly the changes. Engineers use it to find the error an ammeter causes, the effect of a resistor's tolerance or ageing, and the effect of a fault or a change of load. Only a few syllabi list it, and where it is listed it is examined with the other network theorems, for DC and AC circuits.

You need: superposition and Thevenin's theorem.

Statement of the compensation theorem

In a linear network, let a branch of impedance Z carry a current I. If that impedance changes to Z + ΔZ, the change in the current of every branch equals the current that a voltage source Vc = IΔZ would produce in that branch, when

  • Vc is placed in series with the changed branch (which now has Z + ΔZ), with its polarity opposing I: + where I enters the branch, like the extra drop IΔZ;
  • every independent source is switched off (voltage sources shorted, current sources opened, internal impedances kept);
  • dependent sources are left in place.

Then each new current is the old current plus the change. For DC circuits Z is a resistance R and ΔZ a resistance change ΔR. A current-source form exists too (a current source V ΔY across a branch whose admittance changes), but the voltage form above is the one examined. The theorem is built on the substitution theorem: any branch whose voltage and current are known can be replaced by a source of that voltage (or current) without changing anything else in the network.

Proof using Thevenin's theorem

  1. Look into the network from the two ends of the branch. The rest of the network is Vth behind Zth, so the original current is I = Vth/(Zth + Z).
  2. After the change the current is I′ = Vth/(Zth + Z + ΔZ).
  3. The change is ΔI = I′ − I = Vth[(Zth + Z) − (Zth + Z + ΔZ)]/[(Zth + Z)(Zth + Z + ΔZ)].
  4. Simplify, using Vth/(Zth + Z) = I: ΔI = −IΔZ/(Zth + Z + ΔZ).
  5. That is exactly the current a source IΔZ, opposing I, drives round a loop of impedance Zth + Z + ΔZ, which is the compensation network with the independent sources off.
  6. Every other branch current is a linear response to the same change, so by superposition its change is the current the same source drives in that branch.

The minus sign says an increase in impedance (ΔZ positive) reduces the branch current, as expected. The result also explains when the change is small: if ΔZ is much less than Zth + Z, the relative change ΔI/I is about −ΔZ/(Zth + Z).

Using the theorem

  1. Solve the original circuit for the current I in the branch that will change (often it is already known).
  2. Draw the compensation network: switch off the independent sources, put Z + ΔZ in the branch, and put Vc = IΔZ in series with it, + where I enters.
  3. Solve this one-source circuit for the changes ΔI in the branches you need.
  4. Add each change to the old current, and check one current by solving the changed circuit directly.

Ammeter error. An ammeter of resistance RA inserted in a branch is a change ΔR = RA. The meter therefore reads I + ΔI instead of I, and the compensation theorem gives the error at once (worked example 2). AC circuits. The theorem holds for phasors with complex Z and ΔZ (see applying network theorems to AC circuits); then Vc = IΔZ is a phasor too.

Exam traps

  • Using the new current I′ in Vc = IΔZ together with the changed impedance Z + ΔZ. With Z + ΔZ in the compensation network, Vc uses the original current I.
  • Leaving the original sources on in the compensation network. Only Vc acts; every independent source is switched off.
  • Giving Vc the wrong polarity. It opposes the original current (+ where I enters), so a larger resistance always reduces that branch current.
  • Forgetting that the changes spread to other branches. In worked example 1 the 6 Ω current rises by 0.227 A even though that resistor did not change.
  • Switching off a dependent source in the compensation network. It stays, as in every theorem of this unit.

2.8 Applying network theorems to AC circuits

Every method and theorem of this unit also works for AC circuits in the sinusoidal steady state. Replace each sinusoidal source by its phasor and each element by its impedance, and the DC steps go through unchanged with complex numbers: mesh and nodal analysis (with supermesh, supernode and dependent sources), superposition, Thevenin and Norton, compensation and the rest. Two things are new. Phasors of different frequencies cannot be added, so superposition must then be done in the time domain. And maximum power needs a conjugate match, ZL = Zth*, because the load must cancel the source's reactance as well as match its resistance.

You need: phasors, impedance and RMS values (sinusoidal steady state), and the DC methods of this unit: mesh, nodal, Thevenin and Norton.

Phasors and impedances: the DC methods carry over

At one angular frequency ω (rad/s, ω = 2πf), a sinusoid is represented by a phasor, a complex number with the RMS value as its size and the phase as its angle: 20∠0° V. Each element becomes an impedance Z in ohms:

  • resistor: Z = R;
  • inductor: Z = jωL, a positive reactance (L in henry);
  • capacitor: Z = 1/(jωC) = −j/(ωC), a negative reactance (C in farad).

Here j = √(−1). KVL and KCL hold for phasors, and V = ZI replaces Ohm's law, so every DC method applies word for word. Mesh analysis gives a matrix of impedances, Z I = V, with self-impedances on the diagonal and minus the shared impedances off it. Nodal analysis gives Y V = I with admittances Y = 1/Z in siemens. A current source on a shared branch still makes a supermesh, a floating voltage source still makes a supernode, and a dependent source is still written in the phasor of its controlling variable. Only the arithmetic is complex: keep real and imaginary parts separate, and convert to polar form at the end.

To return to the time domain, an RMS phasor |V|∠θ means v(t) = √2|V|cos(ωt + θ). Some books use peak-value phasors; then the √2 is dropped and every average power picks up a factor ½. State which you use and keep to it.

Superposition with one or several frequencies

If all sources have the same frequency, superposition works on phasors: switch off all but one independent source (voltage source shorted, current source opened, dependent sources kept), solve, repeat, and add the phasors.

If the sources have different frequencies, a phasor at 50 Hz and a phasor at 150 Hz rotate at different rates and cannot be added. Solve one circuit per frequency, with the impedances recalculated at that frequency (an inductor is 3 times larger in reactance at 150 Hz than at 50 Hz, and a capacitor 3 times smaller). Turn each answer into a time function and add the time functions. A DC source is the case ω = 0: inductors become shorts and capacitors opens.

Average power, unlike instantaneous power, does add across different frequencies, because the cross terms between two different frequencies average to zero over time. It still does not add across sources of the same frequency.

Thevenin, Norton and maximum average power

At one frequency a linear AC network seen from two terminals is a phasor source Vth (the open-circuit voltage) in series with Zth = Rth + jXth, found with the independent sources switched off; or a Norton current IN = Vth/Zth in parallel with Zth. With dependent sources, use Voc/Isc or a test source, exactly as for DC.

A load ZL = RL + jXL draws |I| = |Vth|/|Zth + ZL| and takes average power |I|²RL:

P = |Vth|²RL/[(Rth + RL)² + (Xth + XL)²]

  1. The reactance appears only in the denominator, so choose XL = −Xth to make that term zero.
  2. What is left is the DC problem, so RL = Rth.
  3. Together: ZL = Rth − jXth = Zth*, the complex conjugate, and Pmax = |Vth|²/(4Rth) with RMS Vth.

If the load can only be a resistor, the best value is RL = |Zth|, which gives less than Pmax. Millman's theorem uses admittances, V = ΣVkYk/ΣYk; reciprocity holds for networks of R, L and C; and the compensation source becomes the phasor IΔZ.

Exam traps

  • Matching RL = Rth and ignoring the reactance. The reactances must cancel too: ZL = Zth*, not ZL = Zth.
  • Adding phasors of different frequencies. Solve each frequency separately and add the time functions.
  • Mixing peak and RMS phasors. Pmax = |Vth|²/(4Rth) needs RMS; with a peak phasor it is |Vth|²/(8Rth).
  • Keeping one reactance value at every frequency. XL = ωL and XC = −1/(ωC) must be recalculated for each frequency.
  • Adding magnitudes instead of complex numbers: |2 + j1| + |1 − j2| is not |(2 + j1) + (1 − j2)|.

Takeaways

  1. Mesh analysis writes one KVL per window with clockwise mesh currents; a current source on a shared branch makes a supermesh, and a dependent source is written in mesh currents.
  2. Nodal analysis writes one KCL per non-reference node; a voltage source between two such nodes makes a supernode, and a source to the reference simply fixes a node voltage.
  3. Superposition adds voltages and currents, one independent source at a time, with dependent sources kept on in every run; power is found once, from the total.
  4. Any linear two-terminal network is Vth behind Rth, or IN across Rth; with dependent sources use Voc/Isc or a test source.
  5. A load takes the most power at RL = Rth, Vth²/(4Rth), but at no more than 50 % efficiency, so power systems avoid it.
  6. Reciprocity and Tellegen's theorem are quick checks; Millman's theorem solves parallel source branches in one line.
  7. The compensation theorem gives the effect of a changed element or an inserted meter from one small source, Vc = IΔZ.
  8. With phasors and impedances every method holds in AC; different frequencies are solved separately, and maximum power needs ZL = Zth*.

    12 ∥ 2 = 1.714 Ω;  4 + 1.714 = 5.714 Ω;  6 ∥ 5.714 = 2.927 Ω
source current = 30/(3 + 2.927) = 5.062 A
into the 4 Ω: 5.062 × 6/(6 + 5.714) = 2.593 A
into branch 2: 2.593 × 12/(12 + 2) = 2.222 A
  6. Swapped: source in branch 2 (30 V in series with the 2 Ω), ammeter in branch 1 (in series with the 3 Ω):
    6 ∥ 3 = 2 Ω;  4 + 2 = 6 Ω;  12 ∥ 6 = 4 Ω
source current = 30/(2 + 4) = 5 A
into the 4 Ω: 5 × 12/(12 + 6) = 3.333 A
into branch 1: 3.333 × 6/(6 + 3) = 2.222 A
  7. Reciprocity verified. The ammeter reads 2.222 A both times; the transfer conductance is 2.222/30 = 0.0741 S both ways. The source currents differ (5.062 A and 5 A), as expected: reciprocity says nothing about them.
  8. Conditions. The ladder has one independent source, only resistors (linear, bilateral) and no dependent sources, and the ammeter is ideal, so reciprocity must hold. Adding a dependent source, or leaving a second source on, would break it.
  9. Answer. (a) 11 V across the load; 1.333 A, 0.5 A and 1.833 A; equivalent 13.2 V behind 1.2 Ω. (b) 2.222 A in both positions.
Apply A 100 V RMS, 50 Hz source in series with 10 Ω feeds a 318.3 µF capacitor connected across the output terminals a-b. Find the Thevenin equivalent at a-b, the load that takes maximum average power (give its resistance and its inductance or capacitance), the maximum power, the current and voltage of that load, and the best purely resistive load with its power. 13 marks

Points to cover. XC = 1/(2π × 50 × 318.3 µF) = 10 Ω; Vth = 100(−j10)/(10 − j10) = 50 − j50 V; Zth = 10 ∥ (−j10) = 5 − j5 Ω; Conjugate load 5 + j5 Ω: 5 Ω with 15.92 mH; Pmax = 250 W; Resistor only: R = |Zth| = 7.07 Ω, 207.1 W.

  1. Capacitor reactance. XC = 1/(ωC) = 1/(2π × 50 × 318.3 × 10⁻⁶) = 1/0.1 = 10.0 Ω, so ZC = −j10 Ω.
  2. Thevenin voltage (open circuit, voltage division):
    Vth = 100 × (−j10)/(10 − j10)
    = −j1000 (10 + j10)/(10² + 10²)
    = (10 000 − j10 000)/200 = 50 − j50 V = 70.71∠−45° V
  3. Thevenin impedance (source shorted: 10 Ω in parallel with −j10 Ω):
    Zth = 10(−j10)/(10 − j10) = −j100(10 + j10)/200
    = (1000 − j1000)/200 = 5 − j5 Ω
  4. Norton check. IN = Vth/Zth = (50 − j50)/(5 − j5) = 10 A, the current a short across a-b would draw: 100/10 = 10 A.
  5. Best load. ZL = Zth* = 5 + j5 Ω: a 5 Ω resistor in series with an inductor of reactance 5 Ω, L = 5/(2π × 50) = 15.92 mH.
  6. Maximum power.

I = Vth/(Zth + ZL) = (50 − j50)/10 = 5 − j5 A = 7.071∠−45° A
check: |I|²RL = 50 × 5 = 250 W   ✓
VL = I ZL = (5 − j5)(5 + j5) = 50 V = 50∠0° V
  7. The source then sees 10 Ω in series with the parallel pair, and the reactances cancel at the terminals: the total impedance seen by the Thevenin source is 10 Ω, purely resistive.
  8. Resistor-only load. With XL = 0, P = |Vth|²R/[(5 + R)² + 5²] is greatest at R = |Zth| = √(5² + 5²) = 7.071 Ω:
    P = 5000 × 7.071 / [(12.071)² + 25] = 35 355/170.71 = 207.1 W

Apply A 100 V RMS, 50 Hz source in series with 10 Ω has a capacitor of reactance −j10 Ω across its output terminals, and a load of 5 + j5 Ω is connected there. The load's inductive reactance then rises from 5 Ω to 8 Ω. Use the compensation theorem to find the new load current and power, and check by direct solution. 13 marks

Points to cover. Thevenin at the load: 50 − j50 V behind 5 − j5 Ω; Original I = 5 − j5 A (250 W); Vc = IΔZ = (5 − j5)(j3) = 15 + j15 V; ΔI = −Vc/(Zth + ZL + ΔZ) = −1.789 − j0.963 A; New I = 3.211 − j5.963 A = 6.773∠−61.7° A, 229.4 W; direct check.

Model answer.

  1. Thevenin equivalent seen by the load. Open circuit: Vth = 100(−j10)/(10 − j10) = 50 − j50 V. Source shorted: Zth = 10 ∥ (−j10) = 5 − j5 Ω.
  2. Original current. I = (50 − j50)/[(5 − j5) + (5 + j5)] = (50 − j50)/10 = 5 − j5 A = 7.071∠−45° A. Load power |I|² × 5 = 50 × 5 = 250 W.
  3. The change. ΔZ = (5 + j8) − (5 + j5) = j3 Ω.
  4. Compensating source.
    Vc = I ΔZ = (5 − j5)(j3) = j15 − j²15 = 15 + j15 V = 21.21∠45° V
  5. Compensation network. Short the 100 V source, put the changed load 5 + j8 Ω in its branch with Vc in series, opposing I. The loop impedance is Zth + ZL + ΔZ = (5 − j5) + (5 + j8) = 10 + j3 Ω.
  6. Change in the load current.
    ΔI = −Vc/(10 + j3) = −(15 + j15)(10 − j3)/(10² + 3²)
   = −(150 − j45 + j150 + 45)/109 = −(195 + j105)/109
   = −1.789 − j0.963 A   (2.032∠−151.7° A)
  7. New load current and power.
    I′ = I + ΔI = (5 − 1.789) + j(−5 − 0.963) = 3.211 − j5.963 A
|I′| = √(3.211² + 5.963²) = 6.773 A,  angle −61.7°
P′ = |I′|² × 5 = 45.87 × 5 = 229.4 W
  8. Direct check.
    I′ = (50 − j50)/(10 + j3) = (50 − j50)(10 − j3)/109
   = (500 − j150 − j500 − 150)/109 = (350 − j650)/109
   = 3.211 − j5.963 A   ✓
  9. Interpretation. The extra 3 Ω of reactance spoils the conjugate match: the reactances no longer cancel (net +j3 Ω in the loop), the current falls from 7.071 A to 6.773 A and the load power from 250 W to 229.4 W, a loss of 8.3 %. Because the power curve is flat near the match, a small drift costs little.
  10. Other currents. The same compensating source, acting alone, gives the change in the capacitor and source currents too, by the current divider between the 10 Ω and the −j10 Ω.
  11. Answer. The load current changes from 5 − j5 A to 3.211 − j5.963 A (6.773∠−61.7° A) and its power from 250 W to 229.4 W.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze For the ladder of the first question (a 20 V source, + at the top, in the left branch; 2 Ω, 6 Ω and 4 Ω along the top from left to right; 4 Ω and 3 Ω vertical resistors from the two inner top junctions to the bottom wire; a 10 V source, + at the top, in the right branch), compare mesh and nodal analysis: count the equations each needs, solve the circuit by nodal analysis, and show that it agrees with the mesh currents i₁ = 4 A, i₂ = 1 A, i₃ = −1 A. State in general when each method is the better choice. 13 marks

Points to cover. b = 7, n = 5: mesh needs 3 equations, nodal 4 − 2 = 2 (two nodes fixed by sources); Nodal: v₂ = 12 V, v₃ = 6 V; Mesh currents recovered from node voltages; Effort comparison; General guidance: sources, counts, dependent sources, AC, simulators.

Model answer.

  1. Counting. Branches: the two sources and five resistors, b = 7. Nodes: the bottom wire (reference) and four top junctions, n = 5. Mesh analysis needs b − n + 1 = 3 equations. Nodal analysis needs n − 1 = 4 unknowns, but the 20 V and 10 V sources each join a node to the reference, which fixes those node voltages: v₁ = 20 V (top of the 20 V source) and v₄ = 10 V (top of the 10 V source). Only v₂ (top of the 4 Ω) and v₃ (top of the 3 Ω) are unknown: 2 equations.
  2. Nodal equations (currents leaving each node):
    node 2: (v₂ − 20)/2 + v₂/4 + (v₂ − v₃)/6 = 0
node 3: (v₃ − v₂)/6 + v₃/3 + (v₃ − 10)/4 = 0
  3. Clear fractions (× 12 each):
    node 2: 6(v₂ − 20) + 3v₂ + 2(v₂ − v₃) = 0   →   11v₂ − 2v₃ = 120
node 3: 2(v₃ − v₂) + 4v₃ + 3(v₃ − 10) = 0   →   −2v₂ + 9v₃ = 30
  4. Solve.
    from node 3: v₂ = (9v₃ − 30)/2
11(9v₃ − 30)/2 − 2v₃ = 120   →   99v₃ − 330 − 4v₃ = 240
95v₃ = 570   →   v₃ = 6 V,   v₂ = (54 − 30)/2 = 12 V
  5. Agreement with the mesh currents.
    QuantityFrom node voltagesFrom mesh currents
    2 Ω current(20 − 12)/2 = 4 Ai₁ = 4 A
    4 Ω (vertical) current12/4 = 3 Ai₁ − i₂ = 3 A
    6 Ω current(12 − 6)/6 = 1 Ai₂ = 1 A
    3 Ω current6/3 = 2 Ai₂ − i₃ = 2 A
    Top 4 Ω current, left to right(6 − 10)/4 = −1 Ai₃ = −1 A
  6. Effort here. Nodal: 2 equations in 2 unknowns. Mesh: 3 equations in 3 unknowns (a 3 × 3 solution). Nodal is shorter for this ladder because both voltage sources are tied to the reference and remove unknowns for free.
  7. When mesh analysis is better. Fewer meshes than independent nodes (long series chains); voltage sources that would otherwise make supernodes; current-controlled sources whose control current is a mesh current; when branch currents are the answer.
  8. When nodal analysis is better. Fewer independent nodes than meshes (many parallel branches); current sources, which would otherwise make supermeshes; voltage-controlled sources; non-planar circuits, where windows do not exist; and large circuits, which is why simulators use modified nodal analysis.
  9. Rule of thumb. Count b − n + 1 and n − 1, subtract one equation for each current source on an outside mesh branch (mesh) or each voltage source to the reference (nodal), and choose the smaller system. Both methods give the same answers, as the table shows, and both work for AC with impedances.
  10. Answer. Mesh needs 3 equations and nodal 2; nodal gives v₂ = 12 V and v₃ = 6 V, which reproduce i₁ = 4 A, i₂ = 1 A and i₃ = −1 A.
Evaluate A 24 V battery feeds a workshop lamp through a long cable. With nothing connected the far end reads 24.0 V; with the lamp on it reads 23.0 V and the lamp draws 2.0 A. A 4 Ω heater is then switched on in parallel with the lamp. (a) Model the battery and cable by Thevenin's theorem. (b) Find the new lamp voltage and power, the heater power and the cable loss, assuming the lamp's resistance stays constant. (c) Would choosing the load for maximum power transfer be sensible here? (d) Evaluate replacing the cable by one with 0.2 Ω resistance. 13 marks

Points to cover. Vth = 24 V, Rth = (24 − 23)/2 = 0.5 Ω; lamp 11.5 Ω, 46 W; Lamp ∥ heater = 2.968 Ω; terminal voltage 20.54 V; Lamp 36.7 W (dims by 20 %), heater 105.5 W, cable 23.95 W, η = 85.6 %; Maximum power would need 0.5 Ω: 288 W at 50 % efficiency and 12 V; 0.2 Ω cable: 22.48 V, lamp 43.96 W.

Model answer.

  1. (a) Thevenin model. The open-circuit reading is Vth = 24 V. Loaded, the terminal voltage obeys V = Vth − RthI, so Rth = (24 − 23)/2 = 0.5 Ω (battery and cable together). The lamp's resistance is 23/2 = 11.5 Ω and it takes 23 × 2 = 46 W.
  2. (b) Heater on. The load is now 11.5 Ω in parallel with 4 Ω:
    Rp = 11.5 × 4/15.5 = 2.968 Ω
I = 24/(0.5 + 2.968) = 6.921 A
V = 24 × 2.968/3.468 = 20.54 V
  3. Powers.
    ItemBefore heaterWith heater
    Terminal voltage23.0 V20.54 V
    Lamp power (V²/11.5)46.0 W36.7 W
    Heater power (V²/4)0105.5 W
    Cable and battery loss (I² × 0.5)2.0 W23.95 W
    Battery output (24 × I)48 W166.1 W
    Efficiency95.8 %85.6 %
  4. Check: 36.7 + 105.5 + 23.95 = 166.1 W. The lamp's power falls by about 20 %, which is the dimming seen. A real filament cools and its resistance falls a little, so the true dip is slightly smaller; the linear model is a good first estimate.
  5. (c) Maximum power transfer? The theorem says the load would take the most power at RL = Rth = 0.5 Ω: 24²/(4 × 0.5) = 288 W, but only at 12 V across the load and with another 288 W heating the battery and cable (50 % efficiency). Lamps and heaters are made for a fixed voltage, not a fixed source, so the aim is to keep the terminal voltage near 24 V, which means RL much larger than Rth. Maximum power transfer is the wrong criterion for supplying power; it suits weak-signal sources.
  6. (d) Thicker cable, Rth = 0.2 Ω.
    V = 24 × 2.968/(0.2 + 2.968) = 22.48 V
lamp: 22.48²/11.5 = 43.96 W;   heater: 22.48²/4 = 126.4 W
  7. With the heater on, the lamp now gets 44 W instead of 36.7 W, only 4.4 % below its 46 W, so the dimming is hardly visible, and the heater gives 126 W instead of 105 W. The cost is a heavier, dearer cable. The alternative, a separate cable for the heater, removes the shared Rth entirely.
  8. Evaluation. Thevenin's theorem turned two meter readings into a model that predicts every load. The fix is to reduce Rth (thicker or shorter cable, separate circuits), which raises efficiency and terminal voltage together; maximising power transfer would do the opposite.
  9. Answer. Rth = 0.5 Ω; with the heater the lamp gets 20.54 V and 36.7 W; maximum power transfer is not sensible; a 0.2 Ω cable restores 22.48 V and 44 W.

Glossary

Additivity
The response to several sources together is the sum of the responses to each alone.
Admittance
Y = 1/Z in siemens; used for nodal analysis and Millman's theorem in AC.
Bilateral element
An element that behaves the same for either direction of current, such as R, L or C.
Branch
A single two-terminal element, or elements in series carrying one current, between two nodes.
Compensating source
The voltage source Vc = IΔZ that stands for a change ΔZ in a branch carrying I.
Conductance
G = 1/R, in siemens (S).
Conjugate match
A load ZL = Zth*, same resistance and opposite reactance, which takes the most average power.
Dependent source
A voltage or current source whose value is set by a voltage or current elsewhere in the circuit; never switched off in a theorem.
Efficiency
Power reaching the load divided by the power the sources supply.
Homogeneity
Scaling every source by k scales every response by k.
Impedance
Z = V/I for phasors, Z = R + jX in ohms.
Loop
Any closed path through a circuit; a mesh is a loop with no other loop inside it.
Mesh current
A current assigned to circulate round one mesh, used as the unknown in mesh analysis.
Node
A point where two or more branches meet.
Open-circuit voltage
The voltage across two terminals with nothing connected; equal to Vth.
Phasor
A complex number giving the RMS size and the phase of a sinusoid at one frequency.
Planar circuit
A circuit that can be drawn on a flat sheet with no branch crossing another.
Reference node
The node chosen as 0 V; every node voltage is measured from it.
Short-circuit current
The current through a wire joining two terminals; equal to IN.
Source transformation
Replacing a voltage source V in series with R by a current source V/R in parallel with R, or the reverse.
Substitution theorem
A branch with known voltage (or current) can be replaced by a source of that voltage (or current) without changing the rest of the network.
Supermesh
Two meshes treated together because a current source sits on their shared branch.
Supernode
Two non-reference nodes and the voltage source between them, treated as one node for KCL.
Transfer conductance
Current in one branch divided by the source voltage in another; equal both ways in a reciprocal network.
Unit-output method
Assume 1 A or 1 V at the far end of a ladder, work back to the source, then scale by homogeneity.
