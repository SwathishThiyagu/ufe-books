4.1 Series resonance

Series resonance is the frequency in a series RLC circuit where the inductive reactance and the capacitive reactance have equal magnitude and cancel. The impedance becomes pure resistance, the current reaches its maximum, and the source voltage and current are in phase. This sharp frequency choice is used in radio tuners, intermediate-frequency filters, wireless receivers, and any circuit that must pick one band and reject others. It also matters in power systems, where unwanted resonance can amplify harmonic currents and overheat capacitors.

You need Unit 1 for the R, L and C voltage-current relations, and Unit 3 for impedance, phasors and series AC circuits.

Reactance cancellation sets the resonant frequency

In a series RLC circuit the same current I flows through R, L and C. At a sinusoidal source frequency f, in hertz, the angular frequency is ω = 2πf in rad/s. The inductive reactance is X_L = ωL and the capacitive reactance is X_C = 1/(ωC). Both are in ohms. The series impedance is:

Z = R + j(X_L − X_C) = R + j(ωL − 1/(ωC))

Z is the series impedance in Ω; R is resistance in Ω; X_L = ωL is inductive reactance in Ω; X_C = 1/(ωC) is capacitive reactance in Ω; ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s; f is frequency in Hz; j is the imaginary unit.

At low frequency X_C is large and X_L is small, so the circuit looks capacitive and the current is small. At high frequency X_L is large and X_C is small, so the circuit looks inductive. At one frequency the two reactances are equal: X_L = X_C. They cancel in the impedance, leaving Z = R. The phase angle is zero, and the current I = V/R is the largest possible for a fixed source voltage V. This condition is series resonance.

ω₀ = 1/√(LC), f₀ = ω₀/(2π) = 1/(2π√(LC))

ω₀ is resonant angular frequency in rad/s; f₀ is resonant frequency in Hz; L is inductance in H; C is capacitance in F.

At resonance the phasors V_L and V_C are equal in magnitude and opposite in phase: V_L leads the current by 90°, V_C lags by 90°. Their sum is zero. The source voltage appears across R, which sets the current. Some syllabi call f₀ the natural frequency; the transient meaning of natural frequency is treated in Unit 5.

The resonance curve and half-power points

A plot of current against frequency peaks at f₀. The figure normalises current to I₀ = V/R and frequency to f₀, so every series RLC circuit has the same shape for a given Q. The quality factor of a series RLC circuit is:

Q = ω₀L/R = 1/(ω₀CR) = (1/R)√(L/C)

Q is the quality factor, dimensionless; R is series resistance in Ω; L is inductance in H; C is capacitance in F; ω₀ is resonant angular frequency in rad/s.

Q is dimensionless. A high Q gives a tall, narrow peak; a low Q gives a broad peak. The half-power points are the two frequencies f₁ and f₂ where the current falls to I₀/√2, about 0.707 I₀. At these points the power in R is half its maximum, because P = I²R. The bandwidth is BW = f₂ − f₁ = f₀/Q. This is the same Q that measures selectivity; see 4.3 for the general Q and bandwidth treatment.

At resonance X_L = X_C = √(L/C). Therefore Q = X_L/R = X_C/R. The figure uses Q = 3 and Q = 10; the Q = 10 curve matches the worked example and has a much narrower bandwidth.

Voltage magnification and circuit names

At resonance the current is I = V/R. The voltage across the inductor is V_L = I X_L = (V/R) X_L. Because Q = X_L/R, this becomes V_L = QV. The capacitor voltage has the same magnitude: V_C = QV. The two voltages are 180° apart, so they cancel in the Kirchhoff voltage loop, but each one individually can be much larger than the source voltage. This is voltage magnification.

V_L = V_C = QV

V_L is rms voltage across L in V; V_C is rms voltage across C in V; Q is the series quality factor, dimensionless; V is rms source voltage in V.

A series RLC circuit at resonance is called an acceptor circuit, because it accepts current from the source most easily at f₀. The same behaviour is called voltage resonance, because the voltages across L and C are magnified. The damping ratio ζ = R/(2ω₀L) = 1/(2Q) links this phasor picture to the transient response; overdamped, critically damped and underdamped cases belong to Unit 5.

Worked example

Question. A series RLC circuit has R = 10 Ω, L = 10 mH and C = 1 µF. A 10 V rms sinusoidal source is connected at the resonant frequency. Find f₀, Q, the bandwidth, and the rms voltage across the capacitor.

  1. Convert: L = 10 mH = 10 × 10⁻³ H, C = 1 µF = 1 × 10⁻⁶ F, V = 10 V rms.
  2. LC = (10 × 10⁻³)(1 × 10⁻⁶) = 1 × 10⁻⁸ s². √(LC) = 1 × 10⁻⁴ s.
  3. f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)) = 1/(2π × 1 × 10⁻⁴) = 1591.5 Hz.
  4. Q = (1/R)√(L/C) = (1/10)√(10 × 10⁻³ / 1 × 10⁻⁶) = (1/10)√(10⁴) = 10.
  5. Bandwidth = f₀/Q = 1591.5/10 = 159.2 Hz.
  6. ω₀ = 2πf₀ = 10000 rad/s. X_L = ω₀L = 10000 × 10 × 10⁻³ = 100 Ω. X_C = 1/(ω₀C) = 1/(10000 × 1 × 10⁻⁶) = 100 Ω.
  7. At resonance Z = R = 10 Ω, so I = V/R = 10/10 = 1 A rms.
  8. V_C = I X_C = 1 × 100 = 100 V rms. V_L is also 100 V rms and opposite in phase.

Answer. f₀ ≈ 1591.5 Hz, Q = 10, bandwidth ≈ 159.2 Hz, and V_C = 100 V rms; V_L is also 100 V rms.

Exam traps

  • Do not set X_L = X_C at an arbitrary frequency. The equality X_L = X_C holds only at ω₀, so recompute both reactances whenever the source frequency changes.
  • In Q = ω₀L/R use ω₀ in rad/s, not f₀ in Hz. Using f₀ in place of ω₀ introduces a 2π error.
  • V_L and V_C cancel in the source loop, but each can be Q times the source voltage. Do not add their magnitudes as if they were in phase.
  • The half-power current is I₀/√2, about 0.707 I₀, not zero and not I₀. Half power means P/Pmax = 1/2.
  • At resonance the impedance is R, not zero. If R is very small, the ideal model predicts a very large current, but real coils and sources have resistance.

4.2 Parallel resonance

Parallel resonance happens when the capacitive and inductive susceptances in a parallel network are equal and opposite. They cancel, the tank looks purely resistive, and its impedance reaches a maximum. The supply then delivers minimum current, while the coil and capacitor exchange a much larger current between themselves. This idea sits inside every tuned radio stage, oscillator tank and filter that must pass one band and reject the rest. This chapter finds the resonant frequency, defines Q for the parallel case, and then handles a real coil with resistance.

You need Chapter 4.1 for the resonance condition and Q, and Unit 3 for admittance and parallel AC circuits.

Ideal parallel RLC: admittance does the work

Connect R, L and C in parallel across one AC source. All three carry the same voltage V, so currents add and admittances add. The input admittance is:

Y = 1/R + j(ωC − 1/(ωL))

Y is admittance in siemens (S), R is resistance in ohms (Ω), L is inductance in henries (H), C is capacitance in farads (F), and ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s.

At resonance the imaginary part is zero: ω₀C = 1/(ω₀L). This gives the same resonant frequency as the series case:

ω₀ = 1/√(LC), f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC))

ω₀ is resonant angular frequency in rad/s; f₀ is resonant frequency in Hz; L is inductance in H; C is capacitance in F.

Now Y = 1/R, which is real. The impedance is Z = R and is maximum; the supply current I_s = V/R is minimum. At lower f the term 1/(ωL) dominates and the tank looks inductive. At higher f the term ωC dominates and it looks capacitive.

The parallel quality factor is:

Q = R/(ω₀L) = ω₀CR = R√(C/L)

Q is dimensionless; R is parallel resistance in Ω; ω₀ is in rad/s; L is in H; C is in F.

At f₀ the branch currents are I_L = V/(ω₀L) and I_C = Vω₀C. Their magnitudes equal Q I_s and their phasors are opposite, so they cancel in the supply line. This is current magnification.

Practical tank: coil resistance shifts f₀

A real inductor is a coil with resistance r in series with L. Connect C across the coil. The coil branch has admittance:

Y_coil = 1/(r + jωL) = (r − jωL)/(r² + ω²L²)

r is coil resistance in Ω; L is coil inductance in H; ω is angular frequency in rad/s; j is the imaginary unit.

Add jωC. Resonance occurs when the total imaginary part is zero:

ωC = ωL/(r² + ω²L²)

ω is angular frequency in rad/s; C is capacitance in F; r is coil resistance in Ω; L is inductance in H.

Solving gives the low-loss resonant frequency:

f₀ = (1/(2π))√(1/(LC) − (r/L)²)

f₀ is resonant frequency in Hz; r is coil resistance in Ω; L is inductance in H; C is capacitance in F.

At this frequency the input impedance is resistive. Its value, the dynamic resistance, is R_D = L/(rC). A small r gives a large R_D and a small supply current. The circuit is called a rejector or current-resonant tank because it rejects the resonant current from the source while a large current circulates in the coil and capacitor.

Use the ideal formula only when r is small compared with ω₀L. The coil resistance pulls f₀ below 1/(2π√(LC)). If 1/(LC) ≤ (r/L)², this low-loss formula has no real solution.

Solving a parallel tank

  1. Convert the source to a phasor and write each branch as an admittance.
  2. Add the admittances and set the imaginary part to zero to find ω₀ or f₀.
  3. Put ω₀ back into Y. The remaining real part gives Z at resonance; I_s = V/Z.
  4. Find coil and capacitor currents separately. At resonance they are nearly equal and opposite, each about Q times I_s.

The figure plots |Z|/R against f/f₀. The peak is at f₀. A higher Q gives a sharper peak and a narrower band; the Q = 10 curve is visibly sharper than Q = 3. Chapter 4.3 turns this sharpness into bandwidth.

  • At f₀ the tank power factor is unity because Y is real. The source sees R or R_D.
  • For a current-source drive, the tank voltage peaks at f₀.
  • For a voltage-source drive, the supply current dips at f₀.
  • A meter in the supply line reads the small current; a meter in the coil branch reads the large circulating current.

Worked example

Question. A parallel RLC circuit has R = 1 kΩ, L = 10 mH and C = 1 µF. A 10 V rms source drives it. Find f₀, Q, the bandwidth, the supply current at resonance and the circulating inductor current at resonance.

  1. LC = (10 × 10⁻³)(1 × 10⁻⁶) = 1 × 10⁻⁸ s². √(LC) = 1 × 10⁻⁴ s. ω₀ = 1/(1 × 10⁻⁴) = 10 000 rad/s.
  2. f₀ = ω₀/(2π) = 10 000/(2π) = 1591.55 Hz.
  3. Q = R/(ω₀L) = 1000/(10 000 × 0.01) = 10. Check: ω₀CR = 10 000 × 10⁻⁶ × 1000 = 10.
  4. Bandwidth = f₀/Q = 1591.55/10 = 159.15 Hz.
  5. At resonance Z = R = 1 kΩ, so I_s = 10/1000 = 0.01 A = 10 mA.
  6. I_L = V/(ω₀L) = 10/(10 000 × 0.01) = 10/100 = 0.1 A = 100 mA. I_C = Vω₀C = 10 × 10 000 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.1 A. I_L/I_s = 100/10 = Q = 10.

Answer. f₀ = 1591.55 Hz, Q = 10, bandwidth = 159.15 Hz, supply current = 10 mA, and circulating current = 100 mA rms.

Exam traps

  • Impedance is maximum at parallel resonance, not minimum. Series resonance gives minimum impedance and maximum supply current.
  • At resonance the supply current is small, but the coil and capacitor currents are large and opposite. Do not assume branch current is zero.
  • Parallel Q has R in the numerator: Q = R/(ω₀L). The series formula Q = ω₀L/R is the reciprocal.
  • A real coil's resistance lowers f₀ below 1/(2π√(LC)) and gives a finite dynamic resistance R_D = L/(rC); the ideal formula can fail when r is large.

4.3 Quality factor, bandwidth and selectivity

Quality factor Q measures how much energy a resonant circuit stores compared with what it loses each cycle. It is a single number that sets two practical properties: bandwidth and selectivity. High Q gives a narrow bandwidth, so the circuit responds strongly only near its resonant frequency and rejects nearby frequencies. Low Q gives a broad, gentle response. This idea is used in radio tuning, filters, wireless receivers and any circuit where one frequency must be picked out from many. This chapter defines Q from energy, connects it to bandwidth and selectivity, and gives the series and parallel formulas.

You need: Chapter 4.1 and Chapter 4.2 for series and parallel resonance, and Unit 3 for AC power and the half-power idea.

Quality factor from energy

At resonance, a series RLC circuit stores energy in the inductor's magnetic field and the capacitor's electric field. The current and voltage oscillate at f₀, while the energy stored in L and C oscillates and is exchanged at 2f₀. In the ideal lossless case the total stored energy is constant. The resistor removes energy as heat every cycle. Q compares the energy kept to the energy lost.

Q = 2π × W<sub>stored,peak</sub> / W<sub>lost,cycle</sub>

Wstored,peak is the peak energy stored in L or C in joules (J); Wlost,cycle is the energy lost in R per cycle in joules (J); Q is dimensionless.

For a series RLC at resonance, Wstored,peak = ½ L I₀² and the energy lost per cycle is (I₀²/2) R T, where I₀ is peak current in amperes (A), T = 1/f₀ is the period in seconds (s), L is inductance in henries (H), C is capacitance in farads (F), and R is resistance in ohms (Ω). Substituting into the energy definition gives the series result.

Qseries = ω₀L / R = 1 / (ω₀CR)

ω₀ = 2πf₀ is the resonant angular frequency in rad/s; f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)) is the resonant frequency in Hz.

For a parallel RLC near resonance, the same energy definition gives a reciprocal use of R. The shunt resistor removes less energy when R is large, so Q rises with R.

Qparallel = R / (ω₀L) = ω₀CR

R is the shunt resistance in ohms (Ω); L and C are the same as above.

A larger series R lowers Q and widens the response. A larger parallel R raises Q and narrows the response. Check which circuit you have before substituting R.

Bandwidth and half-power frequencies

Bandwidth BW is the frequency range between the two half-power frequencies f₁ and f₂. At these frequencies the power in R is half its maximum. In a series circuit the current magnitude is I₀/√2 ≈ 0.707I₀; in a parallel circuit the voltage magnitude is V₀/√2, where V₀ is the peak voltage across the parallel RLC circuit at resonance. The half-power points are also called the −3 dB points.

BW = f₂ − f₁

f₁ is the lower half-power frequency in Hz; f₂ is the upper half-power frequency in Hz; BW is in Hz.

At resonance a series RLC has impedance R. At a half-power point the impedance magnitude is |Z| = √(R² + (ωL − 1/(ωC))²) = R√2. Solving this condition gives the exact half-power angular frequencies.

ω₁,₂ = ω₀(√(1 + 1/(4Q²)) ∓ 1/(2Q))

ω₁ is the lower half-power angular frequency; ω₂ is the upper half-power angular frequency; Q is the resonant quality factor.

The exact half-power frequencies are not exactly evenly spaced around f₀; they are symmetric about f₀√(1 + 1/(4Q²)). For Q ≥ 10 that factor is almost 1, so the half-power frequencies are close to f₀ and approximately evenly spaced.

f₁ ≈ f₀ − BW/2, f₂ ≈ f₀ + BW/2

f₀ is the resonant frequency in Hz; BW is the bandwidth in Hz; the approximation is valid for Q ≥ 10.

The bandwidth follows directly from Q. In angular units the bandwidth is ωBW = ω₂ − ω₁. For a series RLC, ωBW = R/L exactly; for a parallel RLC, ωBW = 1/(RC) exactly.

BW = f₀ / Q, ωBW = ω₀ / Q

f₀ and BW are in Hz; ω₀ and ωBW are in rad/s; Q is dimensionless.

Selectivity and the Q link

Selectivity is how well a circuit picks the wanted frequency and rejects nearby unwanted frequencies. It is measured by the selectivity ratio BW/f₀ = 1/Q. A small ratio means a sharp peak and good selectivity; a large ratio means a broad response and poor selectivity.

Two radio stations 10 kHz apart can be separated by a filter with BW = 5 kHz. A filter with BW = 50 kHz passes both and cannot separate them. The narrower filter has the higher Q.

High Q gives narrow bandwidth and high selectivity. Low Q gives wide bandwidth and low selectivity. Very high Q also makes the tuned frequency sensitive to component tolerance, temperature and loading, so receivers often use moderate Q with careful tuning.

For a series RLC the damping ratio is ζ = 1/(2Q). A high-Q circuit has small ζ and rings for many cycles after a disturbance. The transient meaning of ζ belongs to Unit 5; here the link only shows how Q controls damping.

Worked example

Question. A series RLC circuit has a resonant frequency f₀ = 1 kHz and a quality factor Q = 50. Find the bandwidth, the two half-power frequencies, and the selectivity ratio. Use the high-Q approximation.

  1. Write the given values: f₀ = 1 kHz = 1000 Hz, Q = 50. The approximation is allowed because Q ≥ 10.
  2. Bandwidth: BW = f₀/Q = 1000/50 = 20 Hz.
  3. Half-power spacing: BW/2 = 20/2 = 10 Hz.
  4. Lower half-power frequency: f₁ ≈ f₀ − BW/2 = 1000 − 10 = 990 Hz.
  5. Upper half-power frequency: f₂ ≈ f₀ + BW/2 = 1000 + 10 = 1010 Hz.
  6. Selectivity ratio: BW/f₀ = 20/1000 = 0.02 = 2%.

Answer. The bandwidth is 20 Hz, the half-power frequencies are about 990 Hz and 1010 Hz, and the selectivity ratio is 0.02 (2%).

Exam traps

  • Inverse relation: Higher Q means smaller BW, not larger. If BW falls by a factor of 2, Q rises by a factor of 2 at the same f₀.
  • Unit mix-up: BW = f₀/Q gives hertz only when f₀ is in hertz. ωBW = ω₀/Q gives rad/s. Multiplying or dividing by 2π at the wrong step changes the answer.
  • Series and parallel R: Qseries = ω₀L/R falls as R rises, while Qparallel = R/(ω₀L) rises as R rises. Using the wrong formula reverses the effect of R.
  • Approximation limit: f₁,₂ = f₀ ± BW/2 is only good for Q ≥ 10. For low Q the exact half-power frequencies are not evenly spaced around f₀.
  • Selectivity ratio confusion: BW/f₀ = 1/Q is a fraction; 0.02 is 2%, not 0.02%. Also do not call the ratio a bandwidth.

4.4 Frequency response of RC and RLC circuits

Frequency response is the graph of gain and phase against frequency. It answers a practical question: a circuit may work at 1 kHz but fail at 1 MHz. Capacitor and inductor reactances change with frequency, so the output changes too. The RC circuit gives a gentle first-order cutoff. The series RLC circuit gives a sharp band-pass peak. These two shapes sit inside radio tuning, audio filters and every phone's radio front end.

You need phasors and impedance from Unit 3, and resonance, Q and bandwidth from Chapter 4.1 and Chapter 4.3.

The transfer function and the RC low-pass

Write the input as a phasor V_in and the output as V_out. The transfer function is H(jω) = V_out/V_in. It is a complex number at each frequency. Its size |H| is the gain; its angle is the phase shift. Gain in decibels is 20 log₁₀|H|. A gain of 1 is 0 dB. A gain of 0.707 is −3 dB.

For the RC low-pass, take the output across C. The input divider gives H(jω) = (1/(jωC)) / (R + 1/(jωC)). Multiply top and bottom by jωC: H(jω) = 1/(1 + jωRC).

H(jω) = 1 / (1 + jωRC),  |H| = 1 / √(1 + (ωRC)²),  φ = −arctan(ωRC)

ω is angular frequency in rad/s; R is resistance in Ω; C is capacitance in F; |H| is gain; φ is output phase minus input phase in degrees.

The cutoff is where ωRC = 1. Define ω_c = 1/(RC) and f_c = 1/(2πRC). At this frequency |H| = 1/√2 = 0.707 and φ = −45°. The output lags the input. Below f_c the gain is near 1. Above f_c the gain falls. Far above f_c, |H| ≈ 1/(ωRC), so every tenfold rise in frequency drops the gain by a factor 10. That is −20 dB per decade.

The first-order RL low-pass has the same shape with time constant L/R. Take output across R with input across R and L in series: H = 1/(1 + jωL/R). Replace RC by L/R and the formulas carry over.

The RC high-pass and first-order symmetry

Take the output across R. The divider gives H(jω) = R / (R + 1/(jωC)) = jωRC / (1 + jωRC). Its magnitude is |H| = ωRC / √(1 + (ωRC)²) and its phase is φ = 90° − arctan(ωRC). At cutoff ω = ω_c, |H| = 0.707 and φ = +45°. Far below cutoff |H| ≈ ωRC, so gain rises by 20 dB per decade. Far above cutoff |H| ≈ 1. The low-pass and high-pass magnitudes satisfy |H_LP|² + |H_HP|² = 1 for the same R and C.

H(jω) = jωRC / (1 + jωRC),  |H| = ωRC / √(1 + (ωRC)²),  φ = 90° − arctan(ωRC)

ω_c = 1/(RC) is the cutoff; at ω = ω_c the gain is 0.707 and the phase is +45°.

A high-pass RC removes DC and low-frequency hum. In an audio coupling circuit it passes voice or music while blocking a steady offset. The same first-order shape appears in an RL high-pass when the output is across L.

At f = 10 f_c the high-pass gain is 0.995. At f = 0.1 f_c it is 0.0995. These two numbers show the slow first-order rise and the fast approach to 1.

Series RLC band-pass from the resistor voltage

Connect R, L and C in series and take the output across R. The input phasor sees impedance Z = R + j(ωL − 1/(ωC)). The transfer function is H(jω) = R / Z = 1 / [1 + j(ωL/R − 1/(ωCR))]. Define ω₀ = 1/√(LC), the resonant frequency, and Q = ω₀L/R = 1/(ω₀CR). Then H(jω) = 1 / [1 + jQ(ω/ω₀ − ω₀/ω)].

|H| = 1 / √(1 + Q²(ω/ω₀ − ω₀/ω)²),  φ = −arctan[Q(ω/ω₀ − ω₀/ω)]

ω₀ is the resonant angular frequency in rad/s; Q is the quality factor from Chapter 4.3; |H| is gain; φ is phase in degrees.

At ω = ω₀, the imaginary part is zero, so |H| = 1 and φ = 0°. The resistor voltage equals the input. Below resonance φ tends to +90°; above resonance φ tends to −90°. The curve has a peak at f₀. The half-power points are where |H| = 1/√2 = 0.707. They satisfy Q(ω/ω₀ − ω₀/ω) = ±1. Their separation is the bandwidth: Δω = ω₀/Q, or Δf = f₀/Q. A high Q gives a narrow, sharp band-pass; a low Q gives a wide, gentle one.

Use this circuit as a frequency-selective filter. A TV tuner or radio front end picks one channel by tuning f₀ and fixing Q. The antenna may carry many stations; the band-pass passes the wanted band and rejects the rest. A narrow bandwidth means high Q and sharp selection, but it also means the tuning must be accurate. This is the same Q and bandwidth idea as Chapter 4.3.

Worked example

Question. An RC low-pass filter has R = 1 kΩ and C = 100 nF. (a) Find f_c. (b) Find gain and phase at 1 kHz. (c) A series RLC band-pass with f₀ = 1 kHz and Q = 10: find its bandwidth.

  1. Convert: R = 1 kΩ = 1000 Ω, C = 100 nF = 100 × 10⁻⁹ F, f = 1 kHz = 1000 Hz.
  2. Cutoff: f_c = 1/(2πRC) = 1/(2π × 1000 × 100 × 10⁻⁹) = 1/(6.2832 × 10⁻⁴) = 1591.5 Hz.
  3. Frequency ratio: f/f_c = 1000/1591.5 = 0.6283.
  4. Gain: |H| = 1/√(1 + 0.6283²) = 1/√(1.3948) = 1/1.1810 = 0.8467 ≈ 0.847.
  5. Phase: φ = −arctan(0.6283) = −32.14° ≈ −32.1°.
  6. RLC bandwidth: BW = f₀/Q = 1000/10 = 100 Hz. Approximate half-power points: 950 Hz and 1050 Hz.

Answer. f_c ≈ 1591.5 Hz, gain at 1 kHz ≈ 0.847 with phase ≈ −32.1°, and the RLC band-pass bandwidth is 100 Hz.

Exam traps

  • −3 dB is gain 0.707, not 0.5. Half-power means power falls to half, and power goes as gain squared.
  • Do not mix ω in rad/s with f in Hz. Convert with ω = 2πf before using ω_c = 1/RC.
  • In a series RLC band-pass, the voltage across R peaks at f₀, but the voltage across C peaks below f₀ and across L peaks above f₀. The examiner may ask which output is taken.
  • The formula BW = f₀/Q uses the half-power bandwidth of the band-pass. A first-order RC has no Q and no such bandwidth; its cutoff is a single −3 dB point.
  • Phase signs: RC low-pass output lags (−φ), RC high-pass output leads (+φ) at the same frequency. At cutoff the magnitudes are both 0.707, but the phases are −45° and +45°.

4.5 Self and mutual inductance; coefficient of coupling

A changing current in a coil creates a changing magnetic flux. The coil itself opposes that change with a self-induced voltage, set by its self-inductance L. Some of the same flux can pass through a nearby coil and induce a voltage there; this link is mutual inductance M. The coefficient of coupling k says how much of the flux is shared, from none to all. These ideas explain transformers, induction motors, wireless chargers and unwanted coupling between wires on a circuit board.

You need Unit 1 for the inductor relation v = L di/dt and Unit 3 for AC inductor behaviour; see 4.1 for how inductance sets resonance.

Self-inductance: the coil reacts to its own current

A current i through a coil sets up a magnetic flux. If i changes, the flux changes, and Faraday's law gives a voltage in the same coil. This is self-induction. Let N be the number of turns and Φ the flux per turn in weber (Wb). The flux linkage is NΦ. Self-inductance is flux linkage per ampere: L = NΦ/i. The unit is the henry (H); 1 H = 1 Wb/A = 1 V·s/A.

For a linear magnetic core, L is constant. The self-induced emf (electromotive force, a voltage) is e = −L di/dt. The minus sign comes from Lenz's law: the emf opposes the change in current. For magnitude, |e| = L |di/dt|. A large L means the coil fights current changes strongly; this is why inductors smooth current in filters.

L = NΦ/i, |e| = L |di/dt|

L is self-inductance in henry (H); N is turns; Φ is flux per turn in weber (Wb); i is current in ampere (A); e is self-induced emf in volt (V); di/dt is current change rate in A/s.

  1. Find Φ for the given current i.
  2. Multiply by N to get flux linkage NΦ.
  3. Divide by i to get L in henry.
  4. For a current change, use |e| = L |di/dt|.
  • Energy stored in the field is ½Li², in joule.
  • Inductor voltage leads current by 90° in AC; see Unit 3.
  • Chokes and relay coils use self-inductance.

Mutual inductance: one coil drives another

Put coil 2 near coil 1. Only part of coil 1's flux may pass through coil 2. Call that shared part Φ₂₁, the mutual flux. When i₁ changes, Φ₂₁ changes and an emf appears in coil 2. Mutual inductance is M = N₂Φ₂₁/i₁, where N₂ is turns of coil 2. M is in henry (H).

The induced emf in coil 2 is e₂ = −M di₁/dt; its magnitude is |e₂| = M |di₁/dt|. Notice the cause: e₂ depends on the change of current in coil 1, not on coil 2's own current. If i₁ is steady, e₂ is zero even when i₁ is large. M depends on coil size, turns, spacing, orientation and core material. It can be zero if no net flux from coil 1 links coil 2.

M = N₂Φ₂₁/i₁, |e₂| = M |di₁/dt|

M is mutual inductance in henry (H); N₂ is turns of coil 2; Φ₂₁ is flux from coil 1 linking coil 2 in weber (Wb); i₁ is coil 1 current in ampere (A); e₂ is emf in coil 2 in volt (V); di₁/dt is coil 1 current change rate in A/s.

  • Changing i₁ also gives self-emf e₁ = −L₁ di₁/dt in coil 1.
  • Signs of M terms need the dot convention; see 4.6.
  • Transformers use mutual inductance; see 4.7.

Coefficient of coupling: how much flux is shared

M alone does not tell how well two coils are coupled: a big coil can have a big M even with poor sharing. The coefficient of coupling compares M with the largest possible value. Define k = M/√(L₁L₂). k has no unit. Its range is 0 ≤ k ≤ 1. Rearranged, M = k√(L₁L₂).

Flux from coil 1 that does not link coil 2 is leakage flux Φℓ1. If all flux links both coils, there is no leakage and k = 1. If no flux links, k = 0. Loose coupling means small k; tight coupling means k close to 1. Iron-core transformers are tightly coupled; separated air-core coils are loosely coupled.

k = M/√(L₁L₂), 0 ≤ k ≤ 1, M = k√(L₁L₂)

k is coefficient of coupling (dimensionless); M is mutual inductance in henry (H); L₁ and L₂ are self-inductances of the two coils in henry (H).

Because M ≤ √(L₁L₂), you can never get M larger than the geometric mean of the two self-inductances. This bound is a quick check on any calculation. k is the number to quote when comparing coupling quality; M is the number used in the voltage formula.

Worked example

Question. Two coils have L₁ = 4 H, L₂ = 1 H and coefficient of coupling k = 0.5. (a) Find M. (b) If the current in coil 1 changes at di₁/dt = 2 A/s, find the magnitude of the induced voltage in coil 2. (c) Find the magnitude of the self-induced voltage in coil 1.

  1. Data: L₁ = 4 H, L₂ = 1 H, k = 0.5, di₁/dt = 2 A/s.
  2. Product: L₁L₂ = 4 × 1 = 4 H².
  3. Square root: √(L₁L₂) = √4 = 2 H.
  4. Mutual inductance: M = k√(L₁L₂) = 0.5 × 2 = 1 H.
  5. Mutual emf magnitude: |e₂| = M |di₁/dt| = 1 H × 2 A/s = 2 V.
  6. Self emf magnitude: |e₁| = L₁ |di₁/dt| = 4 H × 2 A/s = 8 V.
  7. Signs: e₁ and e₂ oppose the change by Lenz's law; exact signs need dots in 4.6.

Answer. M = 1 H, the induced voltage in coil 2 has magnitude 2 V, and the self-induced voltage in coil 1 has magnitude 8 V.

Exam traps

  • M is not k(L₁ + L₂) and not the average of L₁ and L₂; use M = k√(L₁L₂). k is dimensionless and cannot be greater than 1.
  • Mutual voltage in coil 2 uses the current change in coil 1. Coil 2's own current creates its own self-emf, not its mutual emf from coil 1.
  • M can be zero even when the coils are physically close: perpendicular axes, cancellation of flux, or a shield can leave no net mutual flux.
  • Unit slips: L and M are in henry (H); k has no unit; in e = M di/dt the current rate must be in A/s, not A.
  • k = 1 means all flux links both coils. Real iron-core devices can be close to this, but leakage flux prevents a perfect 1 in most practical coils.

4.6 Dot convention and analysis of coupled coils

Two coils on one magnetic path share flux. A changing current in coil 1 induces a voltage in coil 2, and that mutual voltage may add to or subtract from each coil's self-induced voltage. The dot convention is a terminal mark that fixes the sign without drawing the magnetic core. It is used in transformer polarity, SPICE coupled-inductor models, and circuits where coils are wound on a common core.

You need: Chapter 4.5 for M and k; Unit 1 for KVL; Unit 2 for mesh analysis.

The dot fixes the sign

A dotted terminal is the terminal marked with a dot on a coupled coil. The dot is not a component and has no voltage by itself. It is a reference mark. It states that current entering the dotted terminal of a coil produces flux in a defined direction. If two currents both enter dotted terminals, their fluxes aid. If one enters a dotted terminal and the other enters an undotted terminal, their fluxes oppose.

For two coils, let i₁ and i₂ enter dotted terminals. KVL gives the two coil voltages below. If a current enters an undotted terminal, the sign of its M term flips. This sign rule defines the dot convention. Moving a dot to the other terminal of a coil reverses the sign of that coil's mutual term; some books call that dot conversion.

v₁ = L₁ di₁/dt + M di₂/dt
v₂ = M di₁/dt + L₂ di₂/dt

v₁ and v₂ are the voltages of coils 1 and 2 measured from the dotted terminal to the undotted terminal; i₁ and i₂ enter those dotted terminals; L₁ and L₂ are the self-inductances; M is the mutual inductance; t is time.

Series coils: +2M or −2M

In series, one current i flows through both coils. If i enters both dots, both mutual terms are positive. The total voltage is v = (L₁ + L₂ + 2M) di/dt. The equivalent inductance is L₁ + L₂ + 2M, called series aiding.

If i enters one dot and leaves the other dot, both mutual terms become negative. The total voltage is v = (L₁ + L₂ − 2M) di/dt, called series opposing. The equivalent inductance is L₁ + L₂ − 2M. The mutual contribution is 2M because the same current links both coils.

L_eq = L₁ + L₂ + 2M  (both currents enter dots)
L_eq = L₁ + L₂ − 2M  (one enters, one leaves)

L_eq is the equivalent series inductance; L₁ and L₂ are the self-inductances; M is the mutual inductance. The sign follows the dot rule for the actual current directions.

Parallel coils and the limit on M

In parallel, both coils have the same terminal voltage v. For aiding, the two coil currents enter dotted terminals for that voltage. KVL gives v = L₁ di₁/dt + M di₂/dt and v = M di₁/dt + L₂ di₂/dt. Solving for the total current gives the first formula below. For opposing, one current enters an undotted terminal, so both M terms change sign and the denominator has +2M.

L_eq = (L₁L₂ − M²)/(L₁ + L₂ − 2M)  (aiding)
L_eq = (L₁L₂ − M²)/(L₁ + L₂ + 2M)  (opposing)

L_eq is the equivalent inductance of the parallel pair; L₁ and L₂ are the self-inductances; M is the mutual inductance.

The coefficient of coupling is k = M/√(L₁L₂). Energy storage requires k ≤ 1, so M ≤ √(L₁L₂). This makes L₁L₂ − M² non-negative, so the numerator of each parallel formula is not negative. For k < 1 the denominator is positive, so L_eq is positive. At k = 1 with equal coils the aiding formula is 0/0; taking the limit gives L_eq → L(1 + k)/2 = L.

Worked example

Question. Two coils have L₁ = 2 H, L₂ = 3 H and M = 1 H. They are connected in series. Find the equivalent inductance when their fluxes aid and when they oppose. Also state which value applies if current enters the dot of coil 1 and leaves the dot of coil 2.

  1. The coils are in series, so the same current flows through both.
  2. Aiding case: both currents enter dotted terminals. L_eq = L₁ + L₂ + 2M = 2 + 3 + 2(1) = 7 H.
  3. Opposing case: one current enters a dot and the other leaves a dot. L_eq = L₁ + L₂ − 2M = 2 + 3 − 2(1) = 3 H.
  4. Check the coupling limit: M = 1 H and √(L₁L₂) = √(2 × 3) = 2.449 H, so M ≤ √(L₁L₂) holds.
  5. Current entering the dot of coil 1 and leaving the dot of coil 2 is the opposing case, so 3 H applies.

Answer. The equivalent inductance is 7 H when the fluxes aid and 3 H when they oppose; the stated current direction gives 3 H.

Exam traps

  • The dot is a terminal mark, not a component. Moving a dot changes the sign of the mutual term, not the self-inductance value.
  • Dots on the same side do not always mean aiding. The actual current directions decide whether the mutual term is positive or negative.
  • The series mutual contribution is 2M, not M. The same current flows through both coils, so both mutual voltages appear.
  • Do not use the series formulas for parallel coils. In the parallel formulas the sign of 2M in the denominator reverses between aiding and opposing.
  • If a calculation gives M > √(L₁L₂), the coupling coefficient k is greater than 1. Check the sign, the given values, or the dot positions.

4.7 Ideal and linear transformers

An ideal transformer is a lossless two-coil block that changes AC voltage, current and impedance by the turns ratio alone. It assumes no winding resistance, no core loss, infinite core permeability and perfect coupling. A linear transformer is the practical coupled-coil model: finite self-inductances L₁ and L₂ and mutual inductance M. The ideal case is its limit. Transformers appear in phone chargers, adapters, isolation units and distribution, where they step voltage down and match impedance.

You need Chapter 4.5 and Chapter 4.6 for M and dots, and Unit 3 for AC impedance.

Ideal transformer: voltage, current and impedance

Let N₁ and N₂ be the turns on the primary and secondary coils. Define the turns ratio n = N₂/N₁. With the dots at the same polarity, the secondary voltage is V₂ = nV₁. If the secondary current I₂ leaves the dotted terminal and the primary current I₁ enters its dot, ampere-turn balance N₁I₁ = N₂I₂ gives I₂/I₁ = N₁/N₂ = 1/n. Power in equals power out: V₁I₁ = V₂I₂ for resistive loading.

n = N₂/N₁;  V₂/V₁ = n;  I₂/I₁ = 1/n;  Z_in = (N₁/N₂)² Z_L

n is the turns ratio (dimensionless); N₁ and N₂ are primary and secondary turns; V₁ and V₂ are rms voltages; I₁ and I₂ are rms currents with I₂ leaving the dotted secondary terminal; Z_in is the impedance seen at the primary; Z_L is the load impedance on the secondary, in ohms.

  • No winding resistance: copper loss is zero.
  • No core loss: hysteresis and eddy-current loss are zero.
  • Infinite core permeability: finite flux needs zero magnetizing current.
  • Perfect coupling k = 1: no leakage flux links only one coil.

The ideal transformer is a limiting case, not a component you can buy. It fixes voltage, current and impedance ratios but has no magnetizing current and no stored energy.

Linear transformer model

A linear transformer keeps finite L₁, L₂ and M. Define M = k√(L₁L₂), where k is the coefficient of coupling and ω is the angular frequency in rad/s. Take both dots at the top and let mesh current I₂ enter the dotted secondary terminal. Winding resistances are omitted here; add R₁ and R₂ in series with L₁ and L₂ when they matter.

V₁ = jωL₁I₁ + jωM I₂
0 = jωM I₁ + (jωL₂ + Z_L)I₂
Z_in = V₁/I₁ = jωL₁ + (ωM)²/(jωL₂ + Z_L)

V₁ is the primary source voltage; I₁ is the primary mesh current; I₂ is the secondary mesh current entering the dotted terminal; L₁ and L₂ are self-inductances in henries; M is mutual inductance in henries; ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s; Z_L is the load impedance in ohms; Z_in is the input impedance in ohms.

Eliminate I₂: I₂ = −jωM I₁/(jωL₂ + Z_L). Substitution gives the input impedance above. If either dot is reversed, the sign of every jωM term flips. The magnitudes of the ratios stay the same, but the phase of V₂ changes by 180°.

Magnetizing current is the primary current that sets the core flux. A finite L₁ draws this current even with no load. In the ideal limit L₁ → ∞ with k = 1, the magnetizing current goes to zero.

Use, limits and a step-change note

A charger transformer steps 230 V AC down to a low AC voltage, then rectifies it. A distribution transformer steps voltage down for homes and isolates the load from the feeder. Impedance scaling is useful: a 10 Ω load on the secondary can look like 250 Ω at the primary when N₁/N₂ = 5.

A real transformer has copper loss, core loss, leakage flux and finite magnetizing inductance. The linear model keeps finite L₁, L₂ and M; the ideal model is the limit of infinite inductances with k = 1. Magnetic-circuit details such as reluctance and flux are in Chapter 4.8.

With a sudden voltage step, the ideal model lets currents jump instantly in the ratio set by the turns, because ampere-turn balance must hold at every instant. A real coil has finite L, so current rises with a time constant set by L/R; that transient belongs to Unit 5.

Worked example

Question. An ideal transformer has N₁ = 500 turns, N₂ = 100 turns, primary voltage 230 V rms and a 10 Ω load on the secondary. Find V₂, I₂, I₁ and Z_in.

  1. Turns ratio n = N₂/N₁ = 100/500 = 0.2.
  2. Secondary voltage V₂ = nV₁ = 0.2 × 230 V = 46 V rms.
  3. Secondary current I₂ = V₂/Z_L = 46 V / 10 Ω = 4.6 A rms.
  4. Primary current I₁ = nI₂ = 0.2 × 4.6 A = 0.92 A rms.
  5. Input impedance Z_in = (N₁/N₂)²Z_L = (500/100)² × 10 Ω = 25 × 10 Ω = 250 Ω.
  6. Power check: V₁I₁ = 230 × 0.92 = 211.6 W and V₂I₂ = 46 × 4.6 = 211.6 W.

Answer. V₂ = 46 V rms, I₂ = 4.6 A rms, I₁ = 0.92 A rms and Z_in = 250 Ω.

Exam traps

  • Turns ratio direction: n = N₂/N₁ for voltage, but the current ratio is 1/n. With the secondary current leaving the dot, I₂/I₁ = N₁/N₂. Students often invert both.
  • Impedance scales as n², not n. Z_in = (N₁/N₂)²Z_L. Check with power: voltage rises by n while current falls by n, so V/I changes by n².
  • The ideal transformer is the limit of a linear transformer as L₁, L₂ and M go to infinity with k = 1. Do not use the ideal ratio on a finite-L linear transformer without checking magnetizing current.
  • Dot reversal changes the sign of jωM in the mesh equations and the phase of V₂ by 180°. Magnitudes stay the same, so a dot question is usually about sign and phase.
  • Keep units: n is dimensionless, Z_in and Z_L are in ohms, V is in volts rms, I is in amperes rms and ω is in rad/s.

4.8 Magnetic circuits: MMF, flux and reluctance

A magnetic circuit is the path that magnetic flux takes through iron and air. A coil with N turns and current I supplies a magnetomotive force F = NI. The iron and air oppose flux with reluctance R = l/(μA). The result is Φ = F/R, the magnetic form of Ohm's law. Motors, transformers, relays, inductors and door sensors use this model. It lets you find flux from coil data and geometry before using a field solver.

You need: Ohm's law and series/parallel resistor rules from Unit 1; basic area and length units.

MMF, flux and reluctance

A coil on a core is a source of magnetic pressure. The magnetomotive force (MMF) is F = NI. N is the number of turns, a count. I is the coil current in ampere (A). F is measured in ampere-turns (A·t), often written simply as A. MMF drives flux Φ through the magnetic path. Φ is the total magnetic flux in weber (Wb).

The path opposes flux. Reluctance is R = l/(μA). l is the mean path length in metre (m). A is the cross-sectional area in square metre (m²). μ is the permeability of the material in henry per metre (H/m). Permeability is μ = μ₀μr, where μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m is the permeability of free space. μr is the dimensionless relative permeability. For air, μr = 1. For iron, μr is hundreds to thousands.

Φ = F/R = NI / (l/(μA))

Φ is flux in Wb; F is MMF in A; R is reluctance in A/Wb; N is turns; I is current in A; l is path length in m; A is area in m²; μ is permeability in H/m.

Flux density B is flux per area: B = Φ/A, in tesla (T). Magnetic field strength H is MMF per length: H = NI/l, in ampere per metre (A/m). The material links them: B = μH. The same reluctance gives inductance L = N²/R, treated in 4.5.

  • MMF F ↔ voltage V.
  • Flux Φ ↔ current I.
  • Reluctance R ↔ resistance R.
  • Φ = F/R ↔ I = V/R.

Series paths, parallel paths and air gaps

In a series magnetic circuit, the same flux passes through every part. The core, the air gap and any other section carry Φ. Reluctances add: Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ + ... . Then Φ = F/Rtotal. A common case is an iron core with one air gap: Rtotal = Rcore + Rgap.

In a parallel magnetic circuit, two or more paths share the same MMF between the same two points. Flux divides: Φtotal = Φ₁ + Φ₂ + ... . Reluctances combine like parallel resistors: 1/Req = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ... . Use this for a core with a side limb or a shunt path.

An air gap has μr = 1, so Rgap = lg/(μ₀A). Even a gap of 1 mm can have reluctance comparable to a metre of iron, because μ₀ is very small and iron μr is large. The gap often sets the flux. Ignore fringing, the spreading of flux at gap edges, and saturation, the fall of μr at high B, unless the question states them.

R<sub>core</sub> = l<sub>c</sub>/(μ₀μ<sub>r</sub>A),  R<sub>gap</sub> = l<sub>g</sub>/(μ₀A)

lc is core length in m; lg is gap length in m; A is area in m²; μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m; μr is relative permeability; R is in A/Wb.

Worked example

Question. A ring core has mean core length 0.4 m, cross-section 4×10⁻⁴ m² and relative permeability 500. It has an air gap of 1 mm. The coil has N = 500 turns and carries I = 2 A. Ignore fringing and saturation. Find the core reluctance, gap reluctance, total reluctance, MMF, flux and flux density. Use μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m.

  1. Given: lc = 0.4 m, A = 4×10⁻⁴ m², μr = 500, lg = 1 mm = 1×10⁻³ m, N = 500, I = 2 A, μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m.
  2. MMF: F = NI = 500 × 2 = 1000 A.
  3. Core reluctance: Rc = lc/(μ₀μrA) = 0.4/(4π×10⁻⁷ × 500 × 4×10⁻⁴) = 1.59×10⁶ A/Wb.
  4. Gap reluctance: Rg = lg/(μ₀A) = 1×10⁻³/(4π×10⁻⁷ × 4×10⁻⁴) = 1.99×10⁶ A/Wb.
  5. Series total: Rtotal = Rc + Rg = 1.59×10⁶ + 1.99×10⁶ = 3.58×10⁶ A/Wb.
  6. Flux: Φ = F/Rtotal = 1000/(3.58×10⁶) = 2.79×10⁻⁴ Wb.
  7. Flux density: B = Φ/A = 2.79×10⁻⁴/(4×10⁻⁴) = 0.698 T.

Answer. Rc = 1.59×10⁶ A/Wb, Rg = 1.99×10⁶ A/Wb, Rtotal = 3.58×10⁶ A/Wb, Φ = 2.79×10⁻⁴ Wb and B = 0.698 T.

Exam traps

  • Air-gap reluctance uses μ₀ alone, not μ₀μr. Iron uses μ₀μr. Swapping them is the most common unit-and-physics slip.
  • Convert every length to metre before dividing. 1 mm = 1×10⁻³ m and 1 cm = 1×10⁻² m. Mixing mm with m² gives a reluctance off by 1000.
  • The analogy is not identity. MMF is not voltage, flux is not current, and reluctance is not resistance. Only the algebraic rules carry over.
  • In series, the same flux passes through all parts, so reluctances add. In parallel, the same MMF appears across branches, so reluctances combine reciprocally. Do not mix the two rules.
  • Saturation and fringing are ignored unless stated. Do not change μr mid-problem or add extra gap area without being told.

Takeaways

  1. Series resonance happens when X_L = X_C, the impedance collapses to R, and the current peaks at f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)).
  2. Parallel resonance is the dual: susceptances cancel, impedance peaks, and the supply current dips to a minimum.
  3. Q = ω₀L/R ties together stored energy, bandwidth (BW = f₀/Q) and selectivity, so one number controls three ideas.
  4. An RC circuit gives one first-order −3 dB corner, while an RLC circuit gives a band-pass peak whose sharpness is set by Q.
  5. Self-inductance is a coil reacting to its own current; mutual inductance is the same flux reaching a second coil.
  6. The coefficient of coupling k = M/√(L₁L₂) says how much of the flux is shared, from 0 to a maximum of 1.
  7. The dot convention is a bookkeeping rule: it decides whether the mutual voltage adds (+M) or subtracts (−M) in the KVL equation.
  8. An ideal transformer scales voltage by a, current by 1/a and impedance by a², with no loss; a linear transformer keeps finite magnetizing inductance.
  9. Magnetic circuits follow Ohm's law in magnetic form: flux Φ = MMF/reluctance, with reluctance R = l/(μA).

Glossary

Air gap
A short non-magnetic length in a magnetic core; it has high reluctance and stores most of the magnetic energy.
Bandwidth
The difference between the two half-power frequencies, equal to f₀/Q in hertz.
Coefficient of coupling
The fraction k = M/√(L₁L₂) of the total flux of one coil that links the other; it lies between 0 and 1.
Cutoff frequency
The frequency at which the gain of a first-order circuit drops to 0.707 of its maximum, a 3 dB fall.
Decade
A tenfold change in frequency; first-order filters change gain by 20 dB per decade beyond cutoff.
Dot convention
A pair of marks on coupled coils such that currents entering both dots create flux in the same direction.
Dynamic resistance
The equivalent resistance L/(CR) that a practical parallel tank presents at resonance.
Flux linkage
The product NΦ, the total flux threading all turns of a coil, measured in weber-turns.
Half-power point
A frequency where the power delivered is half the resonant value, which means the current is 1/√2 of its peak.
Ideal transformer
A lossless transformer with infinite magnetizing inductance and unity coupling, giving exact turns-ratio scaling of voltage and current.
Linear transformer
A coupled-coil model with finite self and mutual inductances, analysed by mesh equations; it is linear but not ideal.
Magnetomotive force
The driving force of a magnetic circuit, MMF = NI, measured in ampere-turns.
Mutual inductance
The property by which a changing current in one coil induces a voltage in a second coil linked by common flux.
Permeability
The property μ = μ₀μ_r of a material showing how easily it carries magnetic flux, in henry per metre.
Phase margin of a response
The phase shift between input and output at a given frequency, read from the phase plot of the frequency response.
Quality factor
A dimensionless number comparing energy stored per cycle with energy lost per cycle; higher Q means a sharper, narrower response.
Reactance
The imaginary part of impedance: X_L = 2πfL for a coil and X_C = 1/(2πfC) for a capacitor, both in ohm.
Reluctance
The opposition a magnetic path offers to flux, R = l/(μA), in ampere-turns per weber.
Resonance
The condition in a circuit containing L and C where the two reactances cancel and the circuit behaves purely resistively.
Resonant frequency
The frequency f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)) at which cancellation of reactances occurs.
Selectivity
How well a tuned circuit separates the wanted frequency band from unwanted nearby frequencies.
Self-inductance
The property of a coil that opposes change of its own current by inducing a voltage proportional to di/dt.
Series aiding
A connection where the two coupled coils carry current into the dotted terminals, giving L_eq = L₁ + L₂ + 2M.
Series opposing
A connection where one coil is reversed so its flux opposes the other, giving L_eq = L₁ + L₂ − 2M.
Susceptance
The imaginary part of admittance, in siemens; parallel resonance occurs when the total susceptance is zero.
