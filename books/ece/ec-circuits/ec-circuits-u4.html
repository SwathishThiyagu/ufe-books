4.1 Series resonance

Series resonance is the frequency in a series RLC circuit where the inductive reactance and the capacitive reactance have equal magnitude and cancel. The impedance becomes pure resistance, the current reaches its maximum, and the source voltage and current are in phase. This sharp frequency choice is used in radio tuners, intermediate-frequency filters, wireless receivers, and any circuit that must pick one band and reject others. It also matters in power systems, where unwanted resonance can amplify harmonic currents and overheat capacitors.

You need: the V-I relations of R, L and C, and phasors and impedance from sinusoidal steady-state analysis.

Reactance cancellation sets the resonant frequency

In a series RLC circuit the same current I flows through R, L and C. At a sinusoidal source frequency f, in hertz, the angular frequency is ω = 2πf in rad/s. The inductive reactance is XL = ωL and the capacitive reactance is XC = 1/(ωC). Both are in ohms. The series impedance is:

Z = R + j(XL − XC) = R + j(ωL − 1/(ωC))

Here z is the series impedance in Ω; R is resistance in Ω; XL = ωL is inductive reactance in Ω; XC = 1/(ωC) is capacitive reactance in Ω; ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s; f is frequency in Hz; j is the imaginary unit.

At low frequency XC is large and XL is small, so the circuit looks capacitive and the current is small. At high frequency XL is large and XC is small, so the circuit looks inductive. At one frequency the two reactances are equal: XL = XC. They cancel in the impedance, leaving Z = R. The phase angle is zero, and the current I = V/R is the largest possible for a fixed source voltage V. This condition is series resonance.

ω₀ = 1/√(LC), f₀ = ω₀/(2π) = 1/(2π√(LC))

Here ω₀ is resonant angular frequency in rad/s; f₀ is resonant frequency in Hz; L is inductance in H; C is capacitance in F.

At resonance the phasors VL and VC are equal in magnitude and opposite in phase: VL leads the current by 90°, VC lags by 90°. Their sum is zero, so the whole source voltage appears across R, which sets the current. Some syllabi call ω₀ the natural frequency of the circuit; the same ω₀ returns, with the damping ratio, in the transient response of second-order RLC circuits.

The resonance curve and half-power points

A plot of current against frequency peaks at f₀, where I₀ = V/R. Away from f₀ the net reactance grows and the current falls on both sides. The sharpness of that fall is set by the quality factor of the series circuit:

Q = ω₀L/R = 1/(ω₀CR) = (1/R)√(L/C)

Here q is the quality factor, dimensionless; R is series resistance in Ω; L is inductance in H; C is capacitance in F; ω₀ is resonant angular frequency in rad/s.

Q is dimensionless. With the same L and C, a smaller R gives a larger Q and a narrower peak; it also raises the peak, because I₀ = V/R. The half-power points are the two frequencies f₁ and f₂ where the current falls to I₀/√2, about 0.707 I₀. There the power in R is half its maximum, because P = I²R. The bandwidth is BW = f₂ − f₁ = f₀/Q; the chapter on quality factor and bandwidth derives it.

At resonance XL = XC = √(L/C), the characteristic resistance of the pair. Therefore Q = XL/R = XC/R. The figure plots the current of the worked example circuit with R = 10 Ω (Q = 10) and with R = 20 Ω (Q = 5): doubling R halves the peak from 1 A to 0.5 A and doubles the bandwidth from 159 Hz to 318 Hz.

Voltage magnification and circuit names

At resonance the current is I = V/R. The voltage across the inductor is VL = I XL = (V/R) XL. Because Q = XL/R, this becomes VL = QV. The capacitor voltage has the same magnitude: VC = QV. The two voltages are 180° apart, so they cancel in the Kirchhoff voltage loop, but each one individually can be much larger than the source voltage. This is voltage magnification.

VL = VC = QV

Here vL is rms voltage across L in V; VC is rms voltage across C in V; Q is the series quality factor, dimensionless; V is rms source voltage in V.

A series RLC circuit at resonance is called an acceptor circuit, because it accepts current from the source most easily at f₀. The same behaviour is called voltage resonance, because the voltages across L and C are magnified. The damping ratio ζ = R/(2ω₀L) = 1/(2Q) links this phasor picture to the transient response; the overdamped, critically damped and underdamped cases belong to second-order transient analysis.

Worked example

Question. A series RLC circuit has R = 10 Ω, L = 10 mH and C = 1 µF. A 10 V rms sinusoidal source is connected at the resonant frequency. Find f₀, Q, the bandwidth, and the rms voltage across the capacitor.

  1. Convert: L = 10 mH = 10 × 10⁻³ H, C = 1 µF = 1 × 10⁻⁶ F, V = 10 V rms.
  2. LC = (10 × 10⁻³)(1 × 10⁻⁶) = 1 × 10⁻⁸ s². √(LC) = 1 × 10⁻⁴ s.
  3. f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)) = 1/(2π × 1 × 10⁻⁴) = 1591.5 Hz.
  4. Q = (1/R)√(L/C) = (1/10)√(10 × 10⁻³ / 1 × 10⁻⁶) = (1/10)√(10⁴) = 10.
  5. Bandwidth = f₀/Q = 1591.5/10 = 159.2 Hz.
  6. ω₀ = 2πf₀ = 10000 rad/s. XL = ω₀L = 10000 × 10 × 10⁻³ = 100 Ω. XC = 1/(ω₀C) = 1/(10000 × 1 × 10⁻⁶) = 100 Ω.
  7. At resonance Z = R = 10 Ω, so I = V/R = 10/10 = 1 A rms.
  8. VC = I XC = 1 × 100 = 100 V rms. VL = I XL = 100 V rms too, 180° away from VC. Check: VC/V = 100/10 = 10 = Q, the voltage magnification.
  9. Half-power currents: I₀/√2 = 0.707 A, reached at about f₀ ± BW/2 = 1512 Hz and 1671 Hz.

Answer. f₀ ≈ 1591.5 Hz, Q = 10, bandwidth ≈ 159.2 Hz, I = 1 A, and VC = VL = 100 V rms, ten times the source voltage.

Exam traps

  • Do not set XL = XC at an arbitrary frequency. The equality XL = XC holds only at ω₀, so recompute both reactances whenever the source frequency changes.
  • In Q = ω₀L/R use ω₀ in rad/s, not f₀ in Hz. Using f₀ in place of ω₀ introduces a 2π error.
  • VL and VC cancel in the source loop, but each can be Q times the source voltage. Do not add their magnitudes as if they were in phase.
  • The half-power current is I₀/√2, about 0.707 I₀, not zero and not I₀. Half power means P/Pmax = 1/2.
  • At resonance the impedance is R, not zero. If R is very small, the ideal model predicts a very large current and very large VL and VC; real coils and sources always add some resistance.

4.2 Parallel resonance

Parallel resonance happens when the capacitive and inductive susceptances in a parallel network are equal and opposite. They cancel, the tank looks purely resistive, and its impedance reaches a maximum. The supply then delivers minimum current, while the coil and capacitor exchange a much larger current between themselves. This idea sits inside every tuned radio stage, oscillator tank and filter that must pass one band and reject the rest. A real tank, a coil with resistance across a capacitor, is a series-parallel circuit with its own formulas.

You need: the resonance condition and Q from series resonance, and admittance of parallel AC branches.

Ideal parallel RLC: admittance does the work

Connect R, L and C in parallel across one AC source. All three carry the same voltage V, so currents add and admittances add. The input admittance is:

Y = 1/R + j(ωC − 1/(ωL))

Here y is admittance in siemens (S), R is resistance in ohms (Ω), L is inductance in henries (H), C is capacitance in farads (F), and ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s.

At resonance the imaginary part is zero: ω₀C = 1/(ω₀L). This gives the same resonant frequency as the series case:

ω₀ = 1/√(LC), f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC))

Here ω₀ is resonant angular frequency in rad/s; f₀ is resonant frequency in Hz; L is inductance in H; C is capacitance in F.

Now Y = 1/R, which is real. The impedance is Z = R and is maximum; the supply current Is = V/R is minimum. At lower f the term 1/(ωL) dominates and the tank looks inductive. At higher f the term ωC dominates and it looks capacitive.

The parallel quality factor is:

Q = R/(ω₀L) = ω₀CR = R√(C/L)

Here q is dimensionless; R is parallel resistance in Ω; ω₀ is in rad/s; L is in H; C is in F.

At f₀ the branch currents are IL = V/(ω₀L) and IC = Vω₀C. Their magnitudes equal Q Is and their phasors are opposite, so they cancel in the supply line. This is current magnification.

Practical tank: coil resistance shifts f₀

A real inductor is a coil with resistance r in series with L. Connect C across the coil. The coil branch has admittance:

Ycoil = 1/(r + jωL) = (r − jωL)/(r² + ω²L²)

Here r is coil resistance in Ω; L is coil inductance in H; ω is angular frequency in rad/s; j is the imaginary unit.

Add jωC. Resonance occurs when the total imaginary part is zero:

ωC = ωL/(r² + ω²L²)

Here ω is angular frequency in rad/s; C is capacitance in F; r is coil resistance in Ω; L is inductance in H.

Solving for ω gives the resonant frequency of the practical tank, the frequency at which the tank has unity power factor:

f₀ = (1/(2π))√(1/(LC) − (r/L)²)

Here f₀ is resonant frequency in Hz; r is coil resistance in Ω; L is inductance in H; C is capacitance in F.

At this frequency the input impedance is resistive. Its value, the dynamic resistance, is RD = L/(rC). With the coil quality factor Q = ω₀L/r this is the same as RD = r(1 + Q²), about Q²r. A small r gives a large RD and a small supply current. The circuit is called a rejector or current-resonant tank: it rejects current from the source while a large current circulates in the coil and capacitor.

The coil resistance pulls f₀ below 1/(2π√(LC)); for r much smaller than ω₀L the shift is tiny and the ideal formula is close enough. If 1/(LC) ≤ (r/L)², the formula has no real solution: the tank never reaches unity power factor.

If the capacitor branch also has a resistance RC in series with C, set the total susceptance to zero in the same way. The result is the general series-parallel resonance condition:

ω₀ = (1/√(LC)) √[(r² − L/C)/(RC² − L/C)]

Here r is the coil resistance and RC the capacitor-branch resistance, in Ω. With RC = 0 it reduces to the practical-tank formula; if r = RC = √(L/C) the tank is resistive at every frequency.

Solving a parallel tank

  1. Convert the source to a phasor and write each branch as an admittance.
  2. Add the admittances and set the imaginary part to zero to find ω₀ or f₀.
  3. Put ω₀ back into Y. The remaining real part gives Z at resonance; Is = V/Z.
  4. Find coil and capacitor currents separately. At resonance they are nearly equal and opposite, each about Q times Is, with Q = ω₀L/r for a real coil.

The figure plots |Z| of the first worked example against frequency. The peak of 1 kΩ sits at f₀ = 1591.5 Hz, and the impedance falls to 707 Ω at the half-power points, 159 Hz apart. The right half shows the branch currents of the practical tank in the second example. The chapter on Q and bandwidth turns this sharpness into numbers.

  • At f₀ the tank power factor is unity because Y is real. The source sees R or RD.
  • A meter in the supply line reads the small current; a meter in the coil branch reads the large circulating current.

Worked example

Question. A parallel RLC circuit has R = 1 kΩ, L = 10 mH and C = 1 µF. A 10 V rms source drives it. Find f₀, Q, the bandwidth, the supply current at resonance and the circulating inductor current at resonance.

  1. LC = (10 × 10⁻³)(1 × 10⁻⁶) = 1 × 10⁻⁸ s². √(LC) = 1 × 10⁻⁴ s. ω₀ = 1/(1 × 10⁻⁴) = 10 000 rad/s.
  2. f₀ = ω₀/(2π) = 10 000/(2π) = 1591.55 Hz.
  3. Q = R/(ω₀L) = 1000/(10 000 × 0.01) = 10. Check: ω₀CR = 10 000 × 10⁻⁶ × 1000 = 10.
  4. Bandwidth = f₀/Q = 1591.55/10 = 159.15 Hz.
  5. At resonance Z = R = 1 kΩ, so Is = 10/1000 = 0.01 A = 10 mA.
  6. IL = V/(ω₀L) = 10/(10 000 × 0.01) = 10/100 = 0.1 A = 100 mA. IC = Vω₀C = 10 × 10 000 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.1 A. IL/Is = 100/10 = Q = 10.

Answer. f₀ = 1591.55 Hz, Q = 10, bandwidth = 159.15 Hz, supply current = 10 mA, and circulating current = 100 mA rms.

Worked example 2

Question. A coil of resistance 10 Ω and inductance 10 mH is connected in parallel with a 1 µF capacitor across a 10 V rms variable-frequency supply. Find the resonant frequency, the dynamic resistance, the supply current, the coil current and the capacitor current at resonance.

  1. Data: r = 10 Ω, L = 10 mH = 0.01 H, C = 1 µF = 10⁻⁶ F, V = 10 V rms.
  2. 1/(LC) = 1/(10⁻⁸) = 10⁸ s⁻². (r/L)² = (10/0.01)² = 10⁶ s⁻².
  3. ω₀ = √(10⁸ − 10⁶) = √(9.9 × 10⁷) = 9949.9 rad/s, so f₀ = 9949.9/(2π) = 1583.6 Hz, a little below the ideal 1591.5 Hz.
  4. Dynamic resistance: RD = L/(rC) = 0.01/(10 × 10⁻⁶) = 1000 Ω.
  5. Supply current: Is = V/RD = 10/1000 = 10 mA, in phase with V.
  6. Coil impedance at ω₀: ω₀L = 99.50 Ω, |Zcoil| = √(10² + 99.50²) = 100.0 Ω, so Icoil = 10/100 = 0.100 A, lagging V by arctan(99.50/10) = 84.3°.
  7. Capacitor current: IC = Vω₀C = 10 × 9949.9 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.0995 A, leading V by 90°.
  8. Check: the in-phase part of Icoil is 0.100 × cos 84.3° = 0.0100 A = Is, and its reactive part 0.100 × sin 84.3° = 0.0995 A cancels IC. Q = ω₀L/r = 9.95 ≈ IC/Is.

Answer. f₀ ≈ 1583.6 Hz, RD = 1 kΩ, Is = 10 mA, Icoil = 100 mA and IC = 99.5 mA.

Exam traps

  • Impedance is maximum at parallel resonance, not minimum. Series resonance gives minimum impedance and maximum supply current.
  • At resonance the supply current is small, but the coil and capacitor currents are large and opposite. Do not assume branch current is zero.
  • Parallel Q has R in the numerator: Q = R/(ω₀L). Do not invert the series formula blindly; the two use different resistances (the shunt R of an ideal tank, the series r of a real coil, where Q = ω₀L/r).
  • A real coil's resistance lowers f₀ below 1/(2π√(LC)) and gives a finite dynamic resistance RD = L/(rC); the ideal formula can fail when r is large.

4.3 Quality factor, bandwidth and selectivity

Quality factor Q measures how much energy a resonant circuit stores compared with what it loses each cycle. That one number sets the bandwidth and the selectivity: high Q gives a narrow band that picks one frequency out of many, low Q a broad, gentle response. Radio tuning, filters and receivers all rest on it.

You need: series resonance and parallel resonance, and average power P = I²R in a resistor.

Quality factor from energy

At resonance, a series RLC circuit stores energy in the inductor's magnetic field and the capacitor's electric field. The current and voltage oscillate at f₀, while the energy in L and in C each swings at 2f₀, passing back and forth between the two; at resonance their total stays constant. The resistor removes energy as heat every cycle. Q compares the energy kept to the energy lost.

Q = 2π × Wmax / Wlost

Here wmax is the maximum energy stored in the circuit, in joules (J); Wlost is the energy turned into heat in R in one period, in J; Q has no unit.

For a series RLC at resonance, Wmax = ½ L Im² and Wlost = (Im²/2) R T, where Im is the peak current in A and T = 1/f₀ is the period in s. Substituting gives Q = 2π f₀ L/R, the series result.

Qseries = ω₀L / R = 1 / (ω₀CR)

Here ω₀ = 2πf₀ is the resonant angular frequency in rad/s; f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)) is the resonant frequency in Hz.

For a parallel RLC near resonance, the same energy definition gives a reciprocal use of R. The shunt resistor removes less energy when R is large, so Q rises with R.

Qparallel = R / (ω₀L) = ω₀CR

Here r is the shunt resistance in ohms (Ω); L and C are the same as above.

Bandwidth and half-power frequencies

Bandwidth BW is the frequency range between the two half-power frequencies f₁ and f₂. At these frequencies the power in R is half its maximum. In a series circuit the current is I₀/√2 ≈ 0.707 I₀; in a parallel circuit the tank voltage is V₀/√2, where V₀ is the tank voltage at resonance. The half-power points are also called the −3 dB points.

BW = f₂ − f₁

Here f₁ is the lower half-power frequency in Hz; f₂ is the upper half-power frequency in Hz; BW is in Hz.

At resonance a series RLC has impedance R. At a half-power point the impedance magnitude is |Z| = √(R² + (ωL − 1/(ωC))²) = R√2. Solving this condition gives the exact half-power angular frequencies.

ω₁,₂ = ω₀(√(1 + 1/(4Q²)) ∓ 1/(2Q))

Here ω₁ is the lower half-power angular frequency; ω₂ is the upper half-power angular frequency; Q is the resonant quality factor.

The two half-power frequencies satisfy f₁f₂ = f₀², so f₀ is their geometric mean, not their average. Their average is f₀√(1 + 1/(4Q²)), slightly above f₀. For Q ≥ 10 the difference is under 0.2 %, so the points sit almost evenly on each side:

f₁ ≈ f₀ − BW/2, f₂ ≈ f₀ + BW/2

Here f₀ is the resonant frequency in Hz; BW is the bandwidth in Hz; the approximation is valid for Q ≥ 10.

Subtracting the exact pair gives the bandwidth with no approximation: ω₂ − ω₁ = ω₀/Q. For a series RLC that is R/L, so BW = R/(2πL), independent of C; for a parallel RLC it is 1/(RC).

BW = f₀ / Q, ωBW = ω₀ / Q

Here f₀ and BW are in Hz; ω₀ and ωBW are in rad/s; Q is dimensionless.

Selectivity and the Q link

Selectivity is how well a circuit picks the wanted frequency and rejects nearby unwanted frequencies. Some books quote it as f₀/BW, which is Q itself; others use the fractional bandwidth BW/f₀ = 1/Q. A sharp peak means a high f₀/BW and good selectivity.

Two radio stations 10 kHz apart can be separated by a filter with BW = 5 kHz. A filter with BW = 50 kHz passes both and cannot separate them. The narrower filter has the higher Q.

High Q gives narrow bandwidth and high selectivity. Low Q gives wide bandwidth and low selectivity. Very high Q also makes the tuned frequency sensitive to component tolerance, temperature and loading, so receivers often use moderate Q with careful tuning.

Worked example

Question. A series RLC circuit has a resonant frequency f₀ = 1 kHz and a quality factor Q = 50. Find the bandwidth, the two half-power frequencies, and the selectivity ratio. Use the high-Q approximation.

  1. Write the given values: f₀ = 1 kHz = 1000 Hz, Q = 50. The approximation is allowed because Q ≥ 10.
  2. Bandwidth: BW = f₀/Q = 1000/50 = 20 Hz.
  3. Half-power spacing: BW/2 = 20/2 = 10 Hz.
  4. Lower half-power frequency: f₁ ≈ f₀ − BW/2 = 1000 − 10 = 990 Hz.
  5. Upper half-power frequency: f₂ ≈ f₀ + BW/2 = 1000 + 10 = 1010 Hz.
  6. Selectivity ratio: BW/f₀ = 20/1000 = 0.02 = 2%.

Answer. The bandwidth is 20 Hz, the half-power frequencies are about 990 Hz and 1010 Hz, and the selectivity ratio is 0.02 (2%).

Exam traps

  • Inverse relation: Higher Q means smaller BW, not larger. If BW falls by a factor of 2, Q rises by a factor of 2 at the same f₀.
  • Unit mix-up: BW = f₀/Q gives hertz only when f₀ is in hertz. ωBW = ω₀/Q gives rad/s. Multiplying or dividing by 2π at the wrong step changes the answer.
  • Series and parallel R: Qseries = ω₀L/R falls as R rises, while Qparallel = R/(ω₀L) rises as R rises. Using the wrong formula reverses the effect of R.
  • Approximation limit: f₁,₂ = f₀ ± BW/2 is only good for Q ≥ 10. For low Q the exact half-power frequencies are not evenly spaced around f₀.
  • Selectivity confusion: f₀/BW = Q and BW/f₀ = 1/Q are both quoted. A fractional bandwidth of 0.02 is 2 %, not 0.02 %. State which one you use.

4.4 Frequency response of RC and RLC circuits

Frequency response is the graph of gain and phase against frequency. It answers a practical question: a circuit may work at 1 kHz but fail at 1 MHz. Capacitor and inductor reactances change with frequency, so the output changes too. The RC circuit gives a gentle first-order cutoff. The series RLC circuit gives a sharp band-pass peak. These two shapes sit inside radio tuning, audio filters and every phone's radio front end.

You need: phasors and impedance, and resonance, Q and bandwidth from series resonance and quality factor, bandwidth and selectivity.

The transfer function and the RC low-pass

Write the input as a phasor Vin and the output as Vout. The transfer function is H(jω) = Vout/Vin. It is a complex number at each frequency. Its size |H| is the gain; its angle is the phase shift. Gain in decibels is 20 log₁₀|H|. A gain of 1 is 0 dB. A gain of 0.707 is −3 dB.

For the RC low-pass, take the output across C. The input divider gives H(jω) = (1/(jωC)) / (R + 1/(jωC)). Multiply top and bottom by jωC: H(jω) = 1/(1 + jωRC).

H(jω) = 1 / (1 + jωRC), |H| = 1 / √(1 + (ωRC)²), φ = −arctan(ωRC)

Here ω is angular frequency in rad/s; R is resistance in Ω; C is capacitance in F; |H| is gain; φ is output phase minus input phase in degrees.

The cutoff is where ωRC = 1. Define ωc = 1/(RC) and fc = 1/(2πRC). At this frequency |H| = 1/√2 = 0.707 and φ = −45°. The output lags the input. Below fc the gain is near 1. Above fc the gain falls. Far above fc, |H| ≈ 1/(ωRC), so every tenfold rise in frequency drops the gain by a factor 10. That is −20 dB per decade.

The first-order RL low-pass has the same shape with time constant L/R. Take output across R with input across R and L in series: H = 1/(1 + jωL/R). Replace RC by L/R and the formulas carry over.

The RC high-pass and first-order symmetry

Take the output across R. The divider gives H(jω) = R / (R + 1/(jωC)) = jωRC / (1 + jωRC). Its magnitude is |H| = ωRC / √(1 + (ωRC)²) and its phase is φ = 90° − arctan(ωRC). At cutoff ω = ωc, |H| = 0.707 and φ = +45°. Far below cutoff |H| ≈ ωRC, so gain rises by 20 dB per decade. Far above cutoff |H| ≈ 1. The low-pass and high-pass magnitudes satisfy |HLP|² + |HHP|² = 1 for the same R and C.

H(jω) = jωRC / (1 + jωRC), |H| = ωRC / √(1 + (ωRC)²), φ = 90° − arctan(ωRC)

Here ωc = 1/(RC) is the cutoff; at ω = ωc the gain is 0.707 and the phase is +45°.

A high-pass RC removes DC and low-frequency hum. In an audio coupling circuit it passes voice or music while blocking a steady offset. The same first-order shape appears in an RL high-pass when the output is across L.

At f = 0.1 fc the high-pass gain is 0.0995 (−20 dB); at f = 10 fc it is 0.995 (−0.04 dB). So one decade below cutoff it passes about a tenth of the input, and one decade above it passes almost all of it.

Series RLC band-pass from the resistor voltage

Connect R, L and C in series and take the output across R. The input phasor sees impedance Z = R + j(ωL − 1/(ωC)). The transfer function is H(jω) = R / Z = 1 / [1 + j(ωL/R − 1/(ωCR))]. Define ω₀ = 1/√(LC), the resonant frequency, and Q = ω₀L/R = 1/(ω₀CR). Then H(jω) = 1 / [1 + jQ(ω/ω₀ − ω₀/ω)].

|H| = 1 / √(1 + Q²(ω/ω₀ − ω₀/ω)²), φ = −arctan[Q(ω/ω₀ − ω₀/ω)]

Here ω₀ is the resonant angular frequency in rad/s; Q is the series quality factor; |H| is gain; φ is phase in degrees.

At ω = ω₀, the imaginary part is zero, so |H| = 1 and φ = 0°. The resistor voltage equals the input. Below resonance φ tends to +90°; above resonance φ tends to −90°. The curve has a peak at f₀. The half-power points are where |H| = 1/√2 = 0.707. They satisfy Q(ω/ω₀ − ω₀/ω) = ±1. Their separation is the bandwidth: Δω = ω₀/Q, or Δf = f₀/Q. A high Q gives a narrow, sharp band-pass; a low Q gives a wide, gentle one.

Use this circuit as a frequency-selective filter. A TV tuner or radio front end picks one channel by tuning f₀ and fixing Q. The antenna may carry many stations; the band-pass passes the wanted band and rejects the rest. A narrow bandwidth means high Q and sharp selection, but it also means the tuning must be accurate (quality factor, bandwidth and selectivity).

The figure draws both shapes for the worked example on a log frequency axis: the RC low-pass and high-pass with R = 1 kΩ and C = 100 nF cross at −3 dB at fc = 1591.5 Hz, and the band-pass with f₀ = 1 kHz and Q = 10 peaks at 0 dB and is down 3 dB at about 950 Hz and 1050 Hz.

Worked example

Question. An RC low-pass filter has R = 1 kΩ and C = 100 nF. (a) Find fc. (b) Find gain and phase at 1 kHz. (c) A series RLC band-pass has f₀ = 1 kHz and Q = 10: find its bandwidth and its gain at 1.2 kHz.

  3. Frequency ratio: f/fc = 1000/1591.5 = 0.6283.
  4. Gain: |H| = 1/√(1 + 0.6283²) = 1/√(1.3948) = 1/1.1810 = 0.8467 ≈ 0.847.
  5. Phase: φ = −arctan(0.6283) = −32.14° ≈ −32.1°.
  6. RLC bandwidth: BW = f₀/Q = 1000/10 = 100 Hz. Approximate half-power points: 950 Hz and 1050 Hz.

Exam traps

  • −3 dB is gain 0.707, not 0.5. Half-power means power falls to half, and power goes as gain squared.
  • Do not mix ω in rad/s with f in Hz. Convert with ω = 2πf before using ωc = 1/RC.
  • In a series RLC band-pass, the voltage across R peaks at f₀, but the voltage across C peaks below f₀ and across L peaks above f₀. The examiner may ask which output is taken.
  • The formula BW = f₀/Q uses the half-power bandwidth of the band-pass. A first-order RC has no Q and no such bandwidth; its cutoff is a single −3 dB point.
  • Phase signs: RC low-pass output lags (−φ), RC high-pass output leads (+φ) at the same frequency. At cutoff the magnitudes are both 0.707, but the phases are −45° and +45°.

4.5 Locus diagrams of RL, RC and RLC circuits

A locus diagram is the path traced by the tip of a phasor when one circuit quantity is varied and the source voltage is held fixed. For series RL, RC and RLC circuits the current traces part of a circle, so one sketch shows the largest current, the power factor at every setting, the value for maximum power, and the settings that make a parallel circuit resonate. Some syllabi place locus diagrams with AC circuit analysis rather than with resonance.

You need: phasors, impedance and admittance, and the resonance condition from series resonance and parallel resonance.

Impedance lines become current circles

Take the supply voltage V as the reference phasor, along the positive real axis. In a series circuit I = V/Z. If only R changes, Z = R + jX keeps its imaginary part, so the tip of Z moves along a horizontal line; if only X changes, it moves along a vertical line at distance R.

The current is V times the inverse of that line, and a straight line that misses the origin inverts into a circle through the origin. Only the part reached by positive values of the varied element is drawn.

To draw a current locus in an exam:

  1. Draw V along the horizontal axis; lagging currents go below it, leading currents above it.
  2. Find the end points: the current when the varied element is zero and when it is infinite.
  3. Draw the semicircle on the diameter V/(fixed element), below V for an inductive circuit, above for a capacitive one.
  4. Check one middle point by calculating V/Z.

Series RL and RC circuits

Series RL with R varied and XL fixed. With θ the angle by which the current lags V, tan θ = XL/R and |I| = V/√(R² + XL²) = (V/XL) sin θ. In polar form this is a circle of diameter V/XL whose diameter lies along the −90° axis. At R = 0 the current is V/XL at −90°; as R → ∞ it shrinks to zero along the V axis. The power P = VI cos θ is the horizontal coordinate of the tip, so it is largest at the right-most point of the semicircle, where θ = 45° and R = XL.

|I| = (V/XL) sin θ (R varied), |I| = (V/R) cos θ (XL varied)

Here θ is the angle between I and V; the first is a circle of diameter V/XL on the −90° axis, the second a circle of diameter V/R on the V axis.

Series RL with XL varied and R fixed. Now |I| = (V/R) cos θ, a circle of diameter V/R on the V axis: from V/R in phase at XL = 0, round the lower half toward the origin.

Series RLC with frequency varied, and parallel branches

In a series RLC circuit with R fixed, the net reactance X = ωL − 1/(ωC) runs from −∞ to +∞ as ω rises from 0 to ∞. So the current traces the whole circle of diameter V/R on the V axis. Below ω₀ the circuit is capacitive and the tip is on the upper (leading) half; at ω₀ it reaches I = V/R in phase with V; above ω₀ it is on the lower half. The half-power frequencies are the two points at ±45°, where |I| = V/(R√2).

In a parallel circuit the branch currents add. If one branch is fixed (for example a capacitor carrying IC = V/XC at +90°), the total current locus is the varied branch's semicircle shifted by IC. Wherever the shifted circle crosses the V axis, the total current is in phase with V: the circuit is resonant.

For a coil branch R + jXL across a capacitor XC, the susceptances cancel when XL/(R² + XL²) = 1/XC. With XL varied this gives XL² − XC XL + R² = 0: two resonant values of XL if XC > 2R, one if XC = 2R, none if XC < 2R. With R varied it gives R² = XL(XC − XL), one value, which exists only when XC > XL.

Worked example

Question. A series RL circuit has XL = 10 Ω and a variable resistor R, fed from 100 V. Draw the current locus, find the current for R = 0, 5, 10, 20 and 40 Ω, and find the value of R for maximum power and that power.

  1. Fixed element XL = 10 Ω, so the diameter is V/XL = 100/10 = 10 A, along the −90° axis. Centre at 5 A below the origin.

  5. R = 20 Ω: I = 4.47∠−26.6° A = 4 − j2 A. R = 40 Ω: I = 2.43∠−14.0° A.
  6. Check that the points lie on the circle: 4 − j8 is √(4² + 3²) = 5 A from the centre (0, −5); so are 5 − j5 and 4 − j2.
  7. Power P = V × Re(I) is largest at the right-most point, R = XL = 10 Ω: P = 100 × 5 = 500 W = V²/(2XL).

4.6 Self and mutual inductance; coefficient of coupling

A changing current in a coil creates a changing magnetic flux. The coil itself opposes that change with a self-induced voltage, set by its self-inductance L. Some of the same flux can pass through a nearby coil and induce a voltage there; this link is mutual inductance M. The coefficient of coupling k says how much of the flux is shared, from none to all. These ideas explain transformers, induction motors, wireless chargers and unwanted coupling between wires on a circuit board.

You need: the inductor relation v = L di/dt and Faraday's law of induction.

Self-inductance: the coil reacts to its own current

A current i through a coil sets up a magnetic flux. If i changes, the flux changes, and Faraday's law gives a voltage in the same coil. This is self-induction. Let N be the number of turns and Φ the flux per turn in weber (Wb). The flux linkage is NΦ. Self-inductance is flux linkage per ampere: L = NΦ/i. The unit is the henry (H); 1 H = 1 Wb/A = 1 V·s/A.

For a linear magnetic core, L is constant. The self-induced emf (electromotive force, a voltage) is e = −L di/dt. The minus sign comes from Lenz's law: the emf opposes the change in current. For magnitude, |e| = L |di/dt|. A large L means the coil fights current changes strongly; this is why inductors smooth current in filters.

L = NΦ/i, |e| = L |di/dt|

Here l is self-inductance in henry (H); N is turns; Φ is flux per turn in weber (Wb); i is current in ampere (A); e is self-induced emf in volt (V); di/dt is current change rate in A/s.

  1. Find Φ for the given current i.
  2. Multiply by N to get flux linkage NΦ.
  3. Divide by i to get L in henry.
  4. For a current change, use |e| = L |di/dt|.
  • Energy stored in the field is ½Li², in joule.
  • Chokes and relay coils use self-inductance.
  • In AC steady state the inductor voltage leads its current by 90°.

Mutual inductance: one coil drives another

Put coil 2 near coil 1. Only part of coil 1's flux may pass through coil 2. Call that shared part Φ₂₁, the mutual flux. When i₁ changes, Φ₂₁ changes and an emf appears in coil 2. Mutual inductance is M = N₂Φ₂₁/i₁, where N₂ is turns of coil 2. M is in henry (H).

The induced emf in coil 2 is e₂ = −M di₁/dt; its magnitude is |e₂| = M |di₁/dt|. Notice the cause: e₂ depends on the change of current in coil 1, not on coil 2's own current. If i₁ is steady, e₂ is zero even when i₁ is large. M depends on coil size, turns, spacing, orientation and core material. It can be zero if no net flux from coil 1 links coil 2.

M = N₂Φ₂₁/i₁, |e₂| = M |di₁/dt|

Here m is mutual inductance in henry (H); N₂ is turns of coil 2; Φ₂₁ is flux from coil 1 linking coil 2 in weber (Wb); i₁ is coil 1 current in ampere (A); e₂ is emf in coil 2 in volt (V); di₁/dt is coil 1 current change rate in A/s.

  • Changing i₁ also gives self-emf e₁ = −L₁ di₁/dt in coil 1.
  • The signs of M terms in a circuit come from the dot convention.
  • Transformers are coupled coils on one core: see ideal and linear transformers.

Coefficient of coupling: how much flux is shared

M alone does not tell how well two coils are coupled: a big coil can have a big M even with poor sharing. The coefficient of coupling compares M with the largest possible value. Define k = M/√(L₁L₂). k has no unit. Its range is 0 ≤ k ≤ 1. Rearranged, M = k√(L₁L₂).

Flux from coil 1 that does not link coil 2 is leakage flux Φℓ1. If all flux links both coils, there is no leakage and k = 1. If no flux links, k = 0. Loose coupling means small k; tight coupling means k close to 1. Iron-core transformers are tightly coupled; separated air-core coils are loosely coupled.

k = M/√(L₁L₂), 0 ≤ k ≤ 1, M = k√(L₁L₂)

Here k is coefficient of coupling (dimensionless); M is mutual inductance in henry (H); L₁ and L₂ are self-inductances of the two coils in henry (H).

Why M ≤ √(L₁L₂): the energy stored in two coupled coils is W = ½L₁i₁² + ½L₂i₂² ± Mi₁i₂. It can never be negative, whatever the two currents, and that holds only when M² ≤ L₁L₂. Worked example

Question. Two coils have L₁ = 4 H, L₂ = 1 H and coefficient of coupling k = 0.5. (a) Find M. (b) If the current in coil 1 changes at di₁/dt = 2 A/s, find the magnitude of the induced voltage in coil 2. (c) Find the magnitude of the self-induced voltage in coil 1.

  1. Data: L₁ = 4 H, L₂ = 1 H, k = 0.5, di₁/dt = 2 A/s.
  2. Product: L₁L₂ = 4 × 1 = 4 H².
  3. Square root: √(L₁L₂) = √4 = 2 H.
  4. Mutual inductance: M = k√(L₁L₂) = 0.5 × 2 = 1 H.
  5. Mutual emf magnitude: |e₂| = M |di₁/dt| = 1 H × 2 A/s = 2 V.
  6. Self emf magnitude: |e₁| = L₁ |di₁/dt| = 4 H × 2 A/s = 8 V.
  7. Signs: e₁ and e₂ oppose the change by Lenz's law; the sign each takes in a circuit equation comes from the dot convention.

Answer. M = 1 H, the induced voltage in coil 2 has magnitude 2 V, and the self-induced voltage in coil 1 has magnitude 8 V.

  • M is not k(L₁ + L₂) and not the average of L₁ and L₂; use M = k√(L₁L₂). k is dimensionless and cannot be greater than 1.
  • Mutual voltage in coil 2 uses the current change in coil 1. Coil 2's own current creates its own self-emf, not its mutual emf from coil 1.
  • M can be zero even when the coils are physically close: perpendicular axes, cancellation of flux, or a shield can leave no net mutual flux.
  • Unit slips: L and M are in henry (H); k has no unit; in |e| = M |di/dt| the current rate must be in A/s, not A.
  • k = 1 means all flux links both coils. Real iron-core devices can be close to this, but leakage flux prevents a perfect 1 in most practical coils.

4.7 Dot convention and analysis of coupled coils

Two coils on one magnetic path share flux. A changing current in coil 1 induces a voltage in coil 2, and that mutual voltage may add to or subtract from each coil's self-induced voltage. The dot convention is a terminal mark that fixes the sign without drawing the magnetic core. It is used in transformer polarity, SPICE coupled-inductor models, and circuits where coils are wound on a common core.

You need: M and k from self and mutual inductance, KVL, and mesh analysis with phasors.

The dot fixes the sign

A dotted terminal is the terminal marked with a dot on a coupled coil. The dot is not a component and has no voltage by itself. It is a reference mark. It states that current entering the dotted terminal of a coil produces flux in a defined direction. If two currents both enter dotted terminals, their fluxes aid. If one enters a dotted terminal and the other enters an undotted terminal, their fluxes oppose.

For two coils, let i₁ and i₂ enter dotted terminals. KVL gives the two coil voltages below. If a current enters an undotted terminal, the sign of its M term flips. This sign rule defines the dot convention. Moving a dot to the other terminal of a coil reverses the sign of that coil's mutual term; some books call that dot conversion.

Placing the dots from the windings, when the core is drawn:

  1. Pick a terminal of coil 1 and mark it with a dot. Imagine a current entering there.
  2. Use the right-hand rule on coil 1's winding to find the direction of the flux that current makes in the core.
  3. On coil 2, find the terminal where an entering current would make flux in the same direction round the core. Mark that terminal with a dot.
  4. Test: switch a battery on into coil 1's dot; a voltmeter on coil 2 kicks positive at coil 2's dot.

v₁ = L₁ di₁/dt + M di₂/dt
v₂ = M di₁/dt + L₂ di₂/dt

Here v₁ and v₂ are the voltages of coils 1 and 2 measured from the dotted terminal to the undotted terminal; i₁ and i₂ enter those dotted terminals; L₁ and L₂ are the self-inductances; M is the mutual inductance; t is time.

Series coils: +2M or −2M

In series, one current i flows through both coils. If i enters both dots, both mutual terms are positive. The total voltage is v = (L₁ + L₂ + 2M) di/dt. The equivalent inductance is L₁ + L₂ + 2M, called series aiding.

If i enters one dot and leaves the other dot, both mutual terms become negative. The total voltage is v = (L₁ + L₂ − 2M) di/dt, called series opposing. The equivalent inductance is L₁ + L₂ − 2M. The mutual contribution is 2M because the same current links both coils.

Leq = L₁ + L₂ + 2M (both currents enter dots)
Leq = L₁ + L₂ − 2M (one enters, one leaves)

Here leq is the equivalent series inductance; L₁ and L₂ are the self-inductances; M is the mutual inductance. The sign follows the dot rule for the actual current directions.

Parallel coils and the limit on M

In parallel, both coils have the same terminal voltage v. For aiding, the two coil currents enter dotted terminals for that voltage. KVL gives v = L₁ di₁/dt + M di₂/dt and v = M di₁/dt + L₂ di₂/dt. Solving for the total current gives the first formula below. For opposing, one current enters an undotted terminal, so both M terms change sign and the denominator has +2M.

Leq = (L₁L₂ − M²)/(L₁ + L₂ − 2M) (aiding)
Leq = (L₁L₂ − M²)/(L₁ + L₂ + 2M) (opposing)

Here leq is the equivalent inductance of the parallel pair; L₁ and L₂ are the self-inductances; M is the mutual inductance.

The coefficient of coupling is k = M/√(L₁L₂). Energy storage requires k ≤ 1, so M ≤ √(L₁L₂). This makes L₁L₂ − M² non-negative, so the numerator of each parallel formula is not negative. For k < 1 both denominators are positive, because L₁ + L₂ − 2M ≥ (√L₁ − √L₂)² ≥ 0, so Leq is positive. At k = 1 with equal coils the aiding formula is 0/0; for L₁ = L₂ = L and M = kL it equals L(1 + k)/2, which tends to L.

Coupled coils in a mesh. In AC steady state each mutual term becomes ±jωM times the other coil's current, with the sign from the dots. Write KVL round each mesh as usual, adding the mutual voltage of every coupled coil that the mesh passes through. The figure draws the second worked example: a primary mesh with 5 Ω and j10 Ω, a secondary mesh with j20 Ω and a 10 Ω load, and ωM = 5 Ω.

Worked example

Question. Two coils have L₁ = 2 H, L₂ = 3 H and M = 1 H. They are connected in series. Find the equivalent inductance when their fluxes aid and when they oppose. Also state which value applies if current enters the dot of coil 1 and leaves the dot of coil 2.

  1. The coils are in series, so the same current flows through both.
  2. Aiding case: both currents enter dotted terminals. Leq = L₁ + L₂ + 2M = 2 + 3 + 2(1) = 7 H.
  3. Opposing case: one current enters a dot and the other leaves a dot. Leq = L₁ + L₂ − 2M = 2 + 3 − 2(1) = 3 H.
  4. Check the coupling limit: M = 1 H and √(L₁L₂) = √(2 × 3) = 2.449 H, so M ≤ √(L₁L₂) holds.
  5. Current entering the dot of coil 1 and leaving the dot of coil 2 is the opposing case, so 3 H applies.

Answer. The equivalent inductance is 7 H when the fluxes aid and 3 H when they oppose; the stated current direction gives 3 H.

Exam traps

  • The dot is a terminal mark, not a component. Moving a dot changes the sign of the mutual term, not the self-inductance value.

4.8 Ideal and linear transformers

An ideal transformer is a lossless two-coil block that changes AC voltage, current and impedance by the turns ratio alone. It assumes no winding resistance, no core loss, infinite core permeability and perfect coupling. A linear transformer is the practical coupled-coil model: finite self-inductances L₁ and L₂ and mutual inductance M. The ideal case is its limit. Transformers appear in phone chargers, adapters, isolation units and distribution, where they step voltage down and match impedance.

You need: M, k and the dot convention, and phasor mesh analysis.

Ideal transformer: voltage, current and impedance

Let N₁ and N₂ be the turns on the primary and secondary coils. Define the turns ratio n = N₂/N₁. With the dots at the same polarity, the secondary voltage is V₂ = nV₁. If the secondary current I₂ leaves the dotted terminal and the primary current I₁ enters its dot, ampere-turn balance N₁I₁ = N₂I₂ gives I₂/I₁ = N₁/N₂ = 1/n. Power in equals power out: V₁I₁ = V₂I₂ for resistive loading.

n = N₂/N₁; V₂/V₁ = n; I₂/I₁ = 1/n; Zin = (N₁/N₂)² ZL

Here n is the turns ratio (dimensionless); N₁ and N₂ are primary and secondary turns; V₁ and V₂ are rms voltages; I₁ and I₂ are rms currents with I₂ leaving the dotted secondary terminal; Zin is the impedance seen at the primary; ZL is the load impedance on the secondary, in ohms.

  • No winding resistance: copper loss is zero.
  • No core loss: hysteresis and eddy-current loss are zero.
  • Infinite core permeability: finite flux needs zero magnetizing current.
  • Perfect coupling k = 1: no leakage flux links only one coil.

The ideal transformer is a limiting case, not a component you can buy. It fixes voltage, current and impedance ratios but draws no magnetizing current and stores no energy. Seen from the primary, a load ZL looks like ZL/n²; this is the referred (reflected) load used for impedance matching.

Linear transformer model

A linear transformer keeps finite L₁, L₂ and M = k√(L₁L₂), where k is the coefficient of coupling; ω is the source's angular frequency in rad/s. Take both dots at the top and let mesh current I₂ enter the dotted secondary terminal. This is the opposite of the load current used for the ideal ratios above, so the load current is −I₂. Winding resistances are omitted here; add R₁ and R₂ in series with L₁ and L₂ when they matter.

V₁ = jωL₁I₁ + jωM I₂
0 = jωM I₁ + (jωL₂ + ZL)I₂
Zin = V₁/I₁ = jωL₁ + (ωM)²/(jωL₂ + ZL)

Here v₁ is the primary source voltage; I₁ is the primary mesh current; I₂ is the secondary mesh current entering the dotted terminal; L₁ and L₂ are self-inductances in henries; M is mutual inductance in henries; ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s; ZL is the load impedance in ohms; Zin is the input impedance in ohms.

Eliminate I₂: I₂ = −jωM I₁/(jωL₂ + ZL). Substitution gives the input impedance above. The second term, (ωM)²/(jωL₂ + ZL), is the reflected impedance: the secondary as the primary sees it. If either dot is reversed, the sign of every jωM term flips; Zin does not change because M appears squared, but I₂ and V₂ change phase by 180°.

Magnetizing current is the primary current that sets the core flux. A finite L₁ draws this current even with no load. Because L grows as N², the ideal transformer is the limit L₁ → ∞ with k = 1 and L₂/L₁ = n² held fixed; then the magnetizing current goes to zero and the turns-ratio laws hold exactly.

Use, limits and a step-change note

A charger transformer steps 230 V AC down to a low AC voltage, then rectifies it. A distribution transformer steps voltage down for homes and isolates the load from the feeder. Impedance scaling is useful: a 10 Ω load on the secondary can look like 250 Ω at the primary when N₁/N₂ = 5.

A real transformer has copper loss, core loss, leakage flux and finite magnetizing inductance. The linear model keeps finite L₁, L₂ and M; the ideal model is its limit. Reluctance and flux in the core are worked out in magnetic circuits.

With a sudden voltage step, the ideal model lets currents jump instantly in the ratio set by the turns, because ampere-turn balance must hold at every instant. A real coil has finite L, so its current rises with a time constant of order L/R; that is a first-order transient.

Question. An ideal transformer has N₁ = 500 turns, N₂ = 100 turns, primary voltage 230 V rms and a 10 Ω load on the secondary. Find V₂, I₂, I₁ and Zin.

4.9 Magnetic circuits: MMF, flux and reluctance

A magnetic circuit is the path that magnetic flux takes through iron and air. A coil with N turns and current I supplies a magnetomotive force F = NI. The iron and air oppose flux with reluctance R = l/(μA). The result is Φ = F/R, the magnetic form of Ohm's law. Motors, transformers, relays, inductors and door sensors use this model. It lets you find flux from coil data and geometry before using a field solver.

You need: Ohm's law and the series and parallel resistor rules, and SI units of length and area.

MMF, flux and reluctance

A coil on a core drives flux round the core. The magnetomotive force (MMF) is F = NI. N is the number of turns, a count. I is the coil current in ampere (A). F is measured in ampere-turns (A·t), often written simply as A. MMF drives flux Φ through the magnetic path. Φ is the total magnetic flux in weber (Wb).

The path opposes flux. Reluctance is R = l/(μA). l is the mean path length in metre (m). A is the cross-sectional area in square metre (m²). μ is the permeability of the material in henry per metre (H/m). Permeability is μ = μ₀μr, where μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m is the permeability of free space. μr is the dimensionless relative permeability. For air, μr = 1. For iron, μr is hundreds to thousands.

Φ = F/R = NI / (l/(μA))

Here φ is flux in Wb; F is MMF in A; R is reluctance in A/Wb; N is turns; I is current in A; l is path length in m; A is area in m²; μ is permeability in H/m.

Flux density B is flux per area: B = Φ/A, in tesla (T). The material links B to the magnetic field strength H by B = μH, with H in ampere per metre (A/m). In one uniform path of length l, H = NI/l. In a path made of parts (iron plus an air gap), each part has its own H = B/μ, and the MMF equals the sum of the drops: NI = H₁l₁ + H₂l₂ + …. The same reluctance gives the coil inductance L = N²/R (see self and mutual inductance).

  • MMF F ↔ voltage V.
  • Flux Φ ↔ current I.
  • Reluctance R ↔ resistance R.
  • Φ = F/R ↔ I = V/R.

Series paths, parallel paths and air gaps

In a series magnetic circuit, the same flux passes through every part. The core, the air gap and any other section carry Φ. Reluctances add: Rtotal = R₁ + R₂ + ... . Then Φ = F/Rtotal. A common case is an iron core with one air gap: Rtotal = Rcore + Rgap.

In a parallel magnetic circuit, two or more paths share the same MMF between the same two points. Flux divides: Φtotal = Φ₁ + Φ₂ + ... . Reluctances combine like parallel resistors: 1/Req = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ... . Use this for a core with a side limb or a shunt path.

An air gap has μr = 1, so Rgap = lg/(μ₀A). Even a gap of 1 mm can have reluctance comparable to a metre of iron, because μ₀ is very small and iron μr is large. The gap often sets the flux. Ignore fringing, the spreading of flux at gap edges, and saturation, the fall of μr at high B, unless the question states them.

Rcore = lc/(μ₀μrA), Rgap = lg/(μ₀A)

Here lc is core length in m; lg is gap length in m; A is area in m²; μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m; μr is relative permeability; R is in A/Wb.

  • Electric and magnetic circuits differ. Current is a flow of charge; flux is not a flow of anything.
  • Energy is needed to set up flux but not to hold it steady; a steady current in R dissipates I²R all the time.
  • Resistance hardly changes with current, but μ falls as the iron saturates, so reluctance depends on B.
  • Air is a good electric insulator but a fair magnetic conductor, so leakage flux has no easy cure.

Worked example

Question. A ring core has mean core length 0.4 m, cross-section 4×10⁻⁴ m² and relative permeability 500. It has an air gap of 1 mm. The coil has N = 500 turns and carries I = 2 A. Ignore fringing and saturation. Find the core reluctance, gap reluctance, total reluctance, MMF, flux and flux density. Use μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m.

  1. Given: lc = 0.4 m, A = 4×10⁻⁴ m², μr = 500, lg = 1 mm = 1×10⁻³ m, N = 500, I = 2 A, μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m.
  2. MMF: F = NI = 500 × 2 = 1000 A.
  3. Core reluctance: Rc = lc/(μ₀μrA) = 0.4/(4π×10⁻⁷ × 500 × 4×10⁻⁴) = 1.59×10⁶ A/Wb.
  4. Gap reluctance: Rg = lg/(μ₀A) = 1×10⁻³/(4π×10⁻⁷ × 4×10⁻⁴) = 1.99×10⁶ A/Wb.
  5. Series total: Rtotal = Rc + Rg = 1.59×10⁶ + 1.99×10⁶ = 3.58×10⁶ A/Wb.
  6. Flux: Φ = F/Rtotal = 1000/(3.58×10⁶) = 2.79×10⁻⁴ Wb.
  7. Flux density: B = Φ/A = 2.79×10⁻⁴/(4×10⁻⁴) = 0.698 T.
  8. Check by MMF drops: Hcore = B/(μ₀μr) = 0.698/(6.283×10⁻⁴) = 1111 A/m, drop 1111 × 0.4 = 444 A; Hgap = B/μ₀ = 5.56×10⁵ A/m, drop 5.56×10⁵ × 10⁻³ = 556 A; 444 + 556 = 1000 A = NI.
  9. Coil inductance: L = N²/Rtotal = 500²/(3.58×10⁶) = 0.0698 H, about 70 mH.

Answer. Rc = 1.59×10⁶ A/Wb, Rg = 1.99×10⁶ A/Wb, Rtotal = 3.58×10⁶ A/Wb, F = 1000 A, Φ = 2.79×10⁻⁴ Wb, B = 0.698 T; the 1 mm gap takes 556 A of the 1000 A, and L ≈ 70 mH.

Worked example 2

Question. A three-limb core has the coil on the centre limb: length 0.1 m, area 8×10⁻⁴ m², with a 0.5 mm air gap. Each outer limb has length 0.3 m and area 4×10⁻⁴ m². μr = 1000 for the iron. Find the current in a 500-turn coil that gives 1 mWb in the centre limb. Ignore fringing and leakage.

  1. Centre iron: R = 0.1/(4π×10⁻⁷ × 1000 × 8×10⁻⁴) = 9.95×10⁴ A/Wb.
  2. Centre gap: R = 0.5×10⁻³/(4π×10⁻⁷ × 8×10⁻⁴) = 4.97×10⁵ A/Wb.
  3. Each outer limb: R = 0.3/(4π×10⁻⁷ × 1000 × 4×10⁻⁴) = 5.97×10⁵ A/Wb. The two are in parallel: 5.97×10⁵/2 = 2.98×10⁵ A/Wb.
  4. The centre limb is in series with that pair: Rtotal = 9.95×10⁴ + 4.97×10⁵ + 2.98×10⁵ = 8.95×10⁵ A/Wb.
  5. MMF = Φ × Rtotal = 10⁻³ × 8.95×10⁵ = 895 A, so I = 895/500 = 1.79 A.
  6. By symmetry each outer limb carries 0.5 mWb; B = 10⁻³/(8×10⁻⁴) = 1.25 T in the centre and 0.5×10⁻³/(4×10⁻⁴) = 1.25 T in each outer limb.

Answer. I ≈ 1.79 A; each outer limb carries 0.5 mWb, and B = 1.25 T everywhere in the iron.

Exam traps

  • Air-gap reluctance uses μ₀ alone, not μ₀μr. Iron uses μ₀μr. Swapping them is the most common unit-and-physics slip.
  • Convert every length to metre before dividing. 1 mm = 1×10⁻³ m and 1 cm = 1×10⁻² m. Mixing mm with m² gives a reluctance off by 1000.
  • The analogy is not identity. MMF is not voltage, flux is not current, and reluctance is not resistance. Only the algebraic rules carry over.
  • In series, the same flux passes through all parts, so reluctances add. In parallel, the same MMF appears across the branches, so reluctances combine reciprocally and the fluxes add. In a three-limb core the two outer limbs are in parallel with each other and in series with the centre limb.
  • Saturation and fringing are ignored unless stated. Do not change μr mid-problem or add extra gap area without being told.

Takeaways

  1. Series resonance: XL = XC at f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)); the impedance drops to R, the current peaks and VL = VC = QV.
  2. Parallel resonance is the dual: susceptances cancel, the tank impedance peaks and the supply current dips; a real coil lowers f₀ and gives RD = L/(rC).
  3. One number, Q, sets bandwidth (BW = f₀/Q) and selectivity; the half-power frequencies satisfy f₁f₂ = f₀².
  4. An RC section gives one −3 dB corner at fc = 1/(2πRC) and a 20 dB per decade slope; a series RLC gives a band-pass peak whose width is f₀/Q.
  5. Vary one element and the current phasor of a series RL or RC circuit runs on a semicircle of diameter V over the fixed element; parallel resonance happens where the shifted circle cuts the V axis.
  6. Self-inductance is a coil reacting to its own current; mutual inductance is the shared flux reaching a second coil, with M = k√(L₁L₂) and k ≤ 1.
  7. The dot convention fixes the sign of every mutual term: aiding gives L₁ + L₂ + 2M, opposing L₁ + L₂ − 2M.
  8. An ideal transformer scales voltage by n, current by 1/n and impedance by n²; a linear transformer adds the reflected impedance (ωM)²/(jωL₂ + ZL).
  9. Magnetic circuits follow Ohm's law in magnetic form: Φ = NI/Rtotal, and a 1 mm air gap can take more MMF than 0.4 m of iron.

Model answer.

  1. Definition. A series RLC circuit is in resonance when its inductive and capacitive reactances are equal, XL = XC. The net reactance is then zero, the circuit is purely resistive, and the source voltage and current are in phase.
  2. Impedance. With one current I through R, L and C at angular frequency ω = 2πf:
    Z = R + jXL − jXC = R + j(ωL − 1/(ωC))
|Z| = √(R² + (ωL − 1/(ωC))²)
φ = arctan[(ωL − 1/(ωC))/R]

    ω₀L = 1/(ω₀C)
ω₀² = 1/(LC)

Remember Define self-inductance, mutual inductance and the coefficient of coupling. Derive M = k√(L₁L₂) and explain why k cannot exceed 1. 8 marks

Points to cover. L = NΦ/i; e = −L di/dt; M = N₂Φ₂₁/i₁ = N₁Φ₁₂/i₂; Φ₂₁ = k₁Φ₁, Φ₁₂ = k₂Φ₂; M² = k₁k₂L₁L₂, so M = k√(L₁L₂) with k = √(k₁k₂); Energy argument for k ≤ 1.

Model answer.

  1. Self-inductance. A current i in a coil of N turns sets up flux Φ per turn. L = NΦ/i, the flux linkage per ampere, in henry. A changing current induces e = −L di/dt in the same coil, opposing the change (Lenz's law).
  2. Mutual inductance. If coil 1 carries i₁ and a part Φ₂₁ of its flux links the N₂ turns of coil 2, M = N₂Φ₂₁/i₁, and a changing i₁ induces e₂ = −M di₁/dt in coil 2. For linear media the same M results when coil 2 drives coil 1: M = N₁Φ₁₂/i₂.
  3. Derivation. Let a fraction k₁ of coil 1's flux link coil 2, and k₂ of coil 2's flux link coil 1:
    Φ₂₁ = k₁Φ₁,  Φ₁₂ = k₂Φ₂
M = N₂k₁Φ₁/i₁   and   M = N₁k₂Φ₂/i₂
M² = k₁k₂ (N₁Φ₁/i₁)(N₂Φ₂/i₂) = k₁k₂ L₁L₂
M = k√(L₁L₂),   k = √(k₁k₂)
  4. Coefficient of coupling. k = M/√(L₁L₂) is the fraction of flux shared, with no unit. Iron-core transformers reach k close to 1; separated air-core coils may have k of 0.1 or less.
  5. Why k ≤ 1. Each k₁, k₂ is a fraction of a flux, so neither exceeds 1, and so neither does √(k₁k₂). The energy view gives the same bound:
    W = ½L₁i₁² + ½L₂i₂² − M i₁i₂     (the worst sign)
put i₂ = (M/L₂) i₁:   W = ½ i₁² (L₁ − M²/L₂)
W ≥ 0 for every current  →  M² ≤ L₁L₂  →  k ≤ 1
  6. Check with numbers. L₁ = 4 H, L₂ = 1 H, k = 0.5: M = 0.5 × √4 = 1 H, below √(L₁L₂) = 2 H. With di₁/dt = 2 A/s, |e₁| = 8 V and |e₂| = 2 V.

Glossary

Air gap
A short non-magnetic length in a magnetic core; its reluctance is high, so it often takes most of the MMF and stores most of the energy.
Bandwidth
The difference between the two half-power frequencies, equal to f₀/Q in hertz.
Coefficient of coupling
k = M/√(L₁L₂), the measure of how much flux two coils share; it lies between 0 and 1.
Cutoff frequency
The frequency at which the gain of a first-order circuit drops to 0.707 of its maximum, a 3 dB fall.
Decade
A tenfold change in frequency; first-order filters change gain by 20 dB per decade beyond cutoff.
Dot convention
A pair of marks on coupled coils such that currents entering both dots create flux in the same direction.
Dynamic resistance
The resistance L/(rC) that a practical parallel tank presents at resonance, with r the coil resistance.
Flux linkage
The product NΦ, the total flux threading all turns of a coil, measured in weber-turns.
Half-power point
A frequency where the power delivered is half the resonant value, which means the current is 1/√2 of its peak.
Ideal transformer
A lossless transformer with infinite magnetizing inductance and unity coupling, giving exact turns-ratio scaling of voltage and current.
Linear transformer
A coupled-coil model with finite self and mutual inductances, analysed by mesh equations; it is linear but not ideal.
Locus diagram
The path traced by the tip of a phasor when one circuit element or the frequency varies and the supply voltage is fixed.
Magnetomotive force
The driving force of a magnetic circuit, MMF = NI, measured in ampere-turns.
Mutual inductance
The property by which a changing current in one coil induces a voltage in a second coil linked by common flux.
Permeability
The property μ = μ₀μr of a material showing how easily it carries magnetic flux, in henry per metre.
Phase response
The angle of H(jω) against frequency: how far the output leads or lags the input.
Quality factor
A dimensionless number comparing energy stored per cycle with energy lost per cycle; higher Q means a sharper, narrower response.
Reactance
The imaginary part of impedance: XL = 2πfL for a coil and XC = 1/(2πfC) for a capacitor, both in ohm.
Reflected impedance
The impedance (ωM)²/(jωL₂ + ZL) that a loaded secondary adds to the primary of a linear transformer.
Rejector circuit
A parallel resonant tank, so called because it draws minimum current from the supply at f₀.
Reluctance
The opposition a magnetic path offers to flux, R = l/(μA), in ampere-turns per weber.
Resonance
The condition in a circuit containing L and C where the two reactances cancel and the circuit behaves purely resistively.
Resonant frequency
The frequency f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)) at which cancellation of reactances occurs.
Selectivity
How well a tuned circuit separates the wanted frequency band from unwanted nearby frequencies.
Self-inductance
The property of a coil that opposes change of its own current by inducing a voltage proportional to di/dt.
Series aiding
A connection where the two coupled coils carry current into the dotted terminals, giving Leq = L₁ + L₂ + 2M.
Series opposing
A connection where one coil is reversed so its flux opposes the other, giving Leq = L₁ + L₂ − 2M.
Susceptance
The imaginary part of admittance, in siemens; parallel resonance occurs when the total susceptance is zero.
