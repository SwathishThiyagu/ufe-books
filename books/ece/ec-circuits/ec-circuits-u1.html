1.1 Circuit elements R, L, C and their V-I relations

Every circuit is built from three passive elements: the resistor R, the inductor L and the capacitor C. Each element fixes one relation between its voltage v and its current i. That relation decides whether the element turns electrical energy into heat or stores it in a field. The same three laws let you read a phone charger, a motor drive or a filter on paper. This chapter gives the laws, their units, the power and energy behind them, and the ideal-versus-real gap you must watch in exams.

Voltage, current and the three element laws

Voltage v is the energy per charge across an element, measured in volts (V). Current i is the charge flow through it, measured in amperes (A). Use the passive sign convention: current enters the positive voltage terminal. A passive element has no internal source.

A resistor obeys v = iR. R is resistance in ohms (Ω); 1 Ω = 1 V/A. An inductor obeys v = L di/dt. L is inductance in henries (H); 1 H = 1 V·s/A. A capacitor obeys i = C dv/dt. C is capacitance in farads (F); 1 F = 1 A·s/V. The derivative means the element reacts to change, not to the present value alone.

v_R = iR, v_L = L di/dt, i_C = C dv/dt

v_R is resistor voltage in V; i is current in A; R is resistance in Ω; v_L is inductor voltage in V; L is inductance in H; t is time in s; i_C is capacitor current in A; C is capacitance in F; v is capacitor voltage in V.

For a resistor, the V-I characteristic is a straight line through the origin; some syllabi call it the volt-ampere characteristic. All three elements are linear when R, L and C are constant, passive because they cannot supply net energy, and bilateral because reversing the terminals does not change the law. Ohm's law gets its full treatment in 1.3.

Power and energy: dissipate or store

Power p = vi is the rate of energy flow, measured in watts (W). For a resistor, p = vi = i²R = v²/R. This power is never negative, so a resistor turns electrical energy into heat. A filament lamp and a heater are resistors doing useful work.

For an inductor, p = vi = L i di/dt = d/dt(½Li²). The stored magnetic energy is W_L = ½Li² joules (J). For a capacitor, p = vi = C v dv/dt = d/dt(½Cv²). The stored electric energy is W_C = ½Cv² J. Ideal L and C store energy and return it later; they do not dissipate it.

Real elements are not perfect. A real inductor has winding resistance, so model it as an ideal L in series with a small resistance. That resistance dissipates i²R_w and limits the quality of filters and transformers. A real capacitor has leakage and equivalent series resistance, but the ideal law still sets the main behaviour.

Reading the laws correctly

  1. Name the element and the known variable: voltage v or current i.
  2. Write the matching law: v = iR for R, v = L di/dt for L, i = C dv/dt for C.
  3. Convert prefixes before calculating: 100 μF = 100 × 10⁻⁶ F, 50 mH = 50 × 10⁻³ H.
  4. Check units: H × A/s gives V; F × V/s gives A; V × A gives W.

At DC steady state, di/dt = 0 makes an ideal inductor a short circuit (v = 0). dv/dt = 0 makes an ideal capacitor an open circuit (i = 0). A resistor still obeys v = iR at DC. Do not use v = iR for L or C; their laws are differential. For a sinusoidal signal, the derivative also brings a 90° phase shift; phasors in Unit 3 turn that calculus into algebra. Time-domain transients and time constants are in Unit 5, Transient Analysis and Laplace Transform.

Series and parallel combinations, and voltage and current division, come in 1.5. For now, treat each element alone and trust its law.

Worked example

Question. A 12 Ω resistor carries 2 A. A 0.5 H inductor carries 2 A and has di/dt = 40 A/s. A 100 μF capacitor has 10 V across it and dv/dt = 200 V/s. Find the resistor voltage v_R, the inductor voltage v_L, the capacitor current i_C, and the energy stored in the inductor and capacitor.

  1. Resistor law: v_R = iR = 2 A × 12 Ω = 24 V.
  2. Inductor law: v_L = L di/dt = 0.5 H × 40 A/s = 20 V.
  3. Capacitor law: i_C = C dv/dt = 100 × 10⁻⁶ F × 200 V/s = 0.02 A = 20 mA.
  4. Inductor stored energy: W_L = ½Li² = ½ × 0.5 H × (2 A)² = 1 J.
  5. Capacitor stored energy: W_C = ½Cv² = ½ × 100 × 10⁻⁶ F × (10 V)² = 0.005 J = 5 mJ.

Answer. v_R = 24 V, v_L = 20 V, i_C = 0.02 A (20 mA), W_L = 1 J and W_C = 0.005 J (5 mJ).

Exam traps

  • Using v = iR for an inductor or capacitor. Their laws are v = L di/dt and i = C dv/dt.
  • Swapping the two differential laws: inductor voltage uses di/dt; capacitor current uses dv/dt.
  • Forgetting prefix powers: 100 μF is 100 × 10⁻⁶ F, not 100 × 10⁻³ F.
  • Treating ideal L and C as energy dissipaters. They store and return energy; R dissipates it.
  • Ignoring the winding resistance of a real inductor, which adds a resistive voltage drop.

1.2 Independent and dependent voltage and current sources

Sources are the parts of a circuit that push energy into it. An independent source fixes its own voltage or current, no matter what the rest of the circuit does. A dependent source fixes its voltage or current as a multiple of a voltage or current somewhere else. This difference matters because dependent sources model transistors, op-amps and amplifiers, where one part controls another. Phone chargers, UPI payment terminals and power supplies all use these ideas.

You need circuit elements R, L, C and their V-I relations for voltage, current and the passive sign convention.

Independent sources set their own value

An ideal independent voltage source keeps its terminal voltage fixed at Vs volts. Its current is set by the circuit outside it. The source can supply any current, so its V-I line is vertical at v = Vs. Vs is the fixed source voltage in volts; v is the terminal voltage in volts. The symbol is a circle with + and − marks.

An ideal independent current source keeps its current fixed at Is amperes. Its voltage is set by the circuit outside it, so its V-I line is horizontal at i = Is. Is is the fixed source current in amperes; i is the terminal current in amperes. The symbol is a circle with an arrow. The arrow gives the direction of positive current.

An ideal voltage source has zero internal resistance. An ideal current source has infinite internal resistance. A practical source is an ideal source plus an internal resistance Rs in ohms. A practical voltage source is Vs in series with Rs; its terminal voltage falls as load current rises: v = Vs − i Rs. A practical current source is Is in parallel with Rs; part of Is goes through Rs, so load current falls as load voltage rises. See source transformation for the link between the two practical models.

Dependent sources follow a control

A dependent source has a control port and an output port. The control port measures a voltage vx in volts or a current ix in amperes. The output port acts as a voltage source or a current source. The output is a fixed multiple of the control variable. The symbol is a diamond, not a circle, so it is not mistaken for an independent source.

VCVS means voltage-controlled voltage source. VCCS means voltage-controlled current source. CCVS means current-controlled voltage source. CCCS means current-controlled current source. The gain μ is a pure number. The gain β is a pure number. The gain g is a conductance in siemens; 1 S = 1 A/V. The gain r is a resistance in ohms; r is also called transresistance.

A dependent source is an active element: it can deliver power. A transistor small-signal model uses a VCCS or a CCCS. An op-amp is often drawn as a VCVS. The control may be far away in the circuit. Substitute the control relation when you write circuit equations; the full method comes in Unit 2, mesh and nodal analysis.

Worked example: four dependent sources

Question. A circuit has four dependent sources. (a) A VCVS has μ = 5 and control voltage vx = 3 V. Find the output voltage. (b) A VCCS has g = 2 mS and vx = 4 V. Find the output current in mA. (c) A CCVS has r = 10 Ω and control current ix = 0.5 A. Find the output voltage. (d) A CCCS has β = 20 and ix = 20 mA. Find the output current in mA.

  1. VCVS: output voltage v = μ vx = 5 × 3 V = 15 V.
  2. VCCS: g = 2 mS = 2 × 10−3 S. Output current i = g vx = 2 × 10−3 S × 4 V = 8 × 10−3 A = 8 mA.
  3. CCVS: output voltage v = r ix = 10 Ω × 0.5 A = 5 V.
  4. CCCS: output current i = β ix = 20 × 20 mA = 400 mA.

Answer. The outputs are 15 V, 8 mA, 5 V and 400 mA.

Exam traps

  • Treating a dependent source as independent: its output changes whenever the control voltage or control current changes.
  • Dropping the gain unit: μ and β are pure numbers, g is in siemens, r is in ohms. Convert 2 mS to 0.002 S before multiplying.
  • Using a circle for a dependent source. The diamond is the dependent-source symbol; the circle is for an independent source.
  • Ignoring the arrow or polarity: a negative μ, g, r or β reverses the output polarity or current direction.
  • Forgetting internal resistance in a practical source: the terminal voltage or current is not the ideal value under load.

1.3 Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws

Ohm's law fixes the voltage across a resistor once its current is known. Kirchhoff's two laws do the bookkeeping for everything else: charge cannot pile up at a node, and energy per charge must return to its starting value after a closed loop. Together the three rules turn any circuit into a set of linear equations you can solve. Every practical check — the 220 Ω series resistor for an LED, the divider that feeds a 5 V regulator, the feeder currents in a Chennai street — ends in these laws.

You need Chapter 1.1 for what R, L, C do, and Chapter 1.2 for the sources that push current round a loop.

Ohm's law

A resistor is a two-terminal element. Ohm's law says the voltage across it is proportional to the current through it, and the constant of proportionality is the resistance. It holds for an ohmic element: a metal wire at fixed temperature, or a carbon film resistor well inside its power rating. A diode, a filament lamp or an LED is not ohmic, and the same formula does not describe it.

v = i R i = v / R = v G

v is the voltage across the resistor in volts (V); i is the current through it in amperes (A); R is the resistance in ohms (Ω); G = 1/R is the conductance in siemens (S), so 1 S = 1 A/V.

Current enters the resistor at the terminal of higher potential and leaves at the lower one. Draw the arrow from the + terminal to the − terminal through the element; then v and i come out positive together. The resistor converts electrical energy into heat at the rate p = i²R watts, which is why every resistor carries a power rating.

Kirchhoff's current law

A node is a point where two or more elements meet (Chapter 1.4). Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) says the algebraic sum of the currents at a node is zero. Charge does not accumulate at a point inside a conducting circuit: every coulomb that arrives must leave. That is conservation of charge, written at a node.

Σ i = 0

i is each branch current at the node, in amperes, written with + if it enters and − if it leaves — or the reverse, but the same choice for every term.

  1. Mark a reference arrow on every branch that joins the node. Each arrow is that current's positive direction; a negative answer only means the real flow is opposite.
  2. Choose one convention: entering positive and leaving negative, or leaving positive and entering negative.
  3. Write every current with its sign and set the total to zero.
  4. Solve, then redraw any arrow whose answer came out negative.

The same rule works for a whole region of a circuit: the sum of currents leaving any closed boundary is zero. That is how a supernode is handled in Unit 2.

Kirchhoff's voltage law

Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL) says the algebraic sum of the voltages around any closed loop is zero. A charge that travels round a loop and returns to its starting point has the same potential energy per coulomb as when it started. Voltage is energy per unit charge, so the rises and the drops must cancel exactly.

Σ v = 0

v is each element voltage round the loop, in volts, written with + for a rise and − for a drop while you walk the loop in one chosen direction.

  1. Choose a loop: a closed path that does not pass through the same node twice.
  2. Choose a walking direction, clockwise or anticlockwise, and keep it for the whole loop.
  3. Entering a terminal marked − and leaving at + is a rise: write +v.
  4. Entering + and leaving at − is a drop: write −v.
  5. Sum to zero. Equivalently, sum of rises = sum of drops.

KVL also holds along an open path if you add the voltage between the two ends. That reading gives the loop equations of mesh analysis in Unit 2.

Open circuit, short circuit, and AC

Ohm's law has two degenerate cases. An open circuit is a break: no current flows whatever voltage stands across the gap, so i = 0 and R = ∞. A short circuit is a zero-resistance link: the voltage across it is zero whatever current it carries, so v = 0 and R = 0. Swapping the two ruins a whole solution.

Both Kirchhoff laws are general. They hold at every instant with instantaneous values, so they describe AC as well as DC. In sinusoidal steady state you write them for phasors: ΣI = 0 at a node and ΣV = 0 round a loop, with complex I and V (Unit 3). Ohm's law becomes V = I Z, where Z is the impedance in ohms.

Counting equations: a connected network with n nodes and b branches gives n − 1 independent KCL equations and b − n + 1 independent KVL equations. Chapter 1.8 explains where those counts come from.

Worked example

Question. (a) One branch carries 2 A into a node, and two other branches carry 0.8 A and 0.5 A out of it. A third leaving branch carries I3; find I3. (b) A 12 V source drives a loop current through elements with voltage drops of 3 V and 5 V, and through one more element with voltage Vx; find Vx. (c) A 12 Ω resistor carries 0.5 A; find the voltage across it.

  1. Take entering as positive and leaving as negative, and apply KCL at the node: 2 − 0.8 − 0.5 − I3 = 0.
  2. I3 = 2 − 0.8 − 0.5 = 0.7 A. The sign is positive, so it leaves the node as drawn.
  3. Walk the loop in the direction of the source. The 12 V source is a rise and the three element voltages are drops, so KVL gives 12 − 3 − 5 − Vx = 0.
  4. Vx = 12 − 3 − 5 = 4 V, a drop in the chosen direction.
  5. Ohm's law on the 12 Ω resistor: v = i R = 0.5 A × 12 Ω = 6 V.
  6. Check: 2 − 0.8 − 0.5 − 0.7 = 0 for KCL; 12 − 3 − 5 − 4 = 0 for KVL; and 6 V across 12 Ω gives 6/12 = 0.5 A.

Answer. I3 = 0.7 A leaving the node, Vx = 4 V, and the 12 Ω resistor has 6 V across it.

Exam traps

  • Mixing entering and leaving currents in the same sum: a leaving current added with a plus sign next to entering ones can never give zero. Fix the convention before writing the first term.
  • Adding a rise and a drop with the same sign. A 12 V source is +12 V walked one way and −12 V walked the other; the total is still zero, but every individual answer flips sign.
  • Using the source voltage in Ohm's law instead of the voltage across that resistor. Ohm's law wants v across the element, not the battery behind it.
  • Unit slip with conductance: a 4 Ω resistor has G = 0.25 S, not 0.25 Ω. Siemens is the reciprocal unit, and the exam often asks for G to catch this.
  • Confusing the two degenerate cases: an open circuit has i = 0 for any v (R = ∞), a short circuit has v = 0 for any i (R = 0).

1.4 Network terminology: node, branch, loop, mesh

A circuit diagram looks like a tangle of wires, but in the ideal model it has only four named parts: node, branch, loop, mesh. Name them and you can count them, and the counts tell you exactly how many independent equations the circuit needs. Kirchhoff's current law is written at nodes and Kirchhoff's voltage law around loops, so this counting is the first step of every nodal or mesh solution. The same counts let you check a circuit file before a simulator runs it, and they set up the graph methods in 1.8.

You need 1.3 Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws: KCL lives at nodes and KVL lives around loops, so the names come first.

Nodes and branches

A node is a point where two or more circuit elements meet. In the ideal model the wire between those meeting points has zero resistance, so the whole connected wire is one node and sits at one voltage. Mark it with a dot and a letter, such as A or B.

A junction is a node where three or more elements meet. Some syllabi make this distinction; others use node and junction as synonyms. Choose one meaning, write it down, and stay with it through the answer sheet.

A branch is one element, or a series path of elements, between two nodes. The whole branch carries the same current. Two resistors in series with nothing tapped between them form one branch, not two.

  • Node: two or more elements meet; one voltage.
  • Junction: three or more elements meet (in that convention).
  • Branch: one element or a series path; one current.
  • Reference node: the node chosen as 0 V, the ground of the calculation.

Loops, meshes and planar circuits

A loop is any closed path. Start at a node, walk along branches, and return to the same node without using a branch twice. The direction you walk is your choice; it only fixes the sign of the terms in a KVL equation.

A mesh is a loop that encloses no other loop. In a flat drawing, the meshes are the window panes of the diagram.

Only planar circuits have meshes. A circuit is planar if you can redraw it on a flat sheet with no branch crossing another. Where two branches cross in your sketch, that crossing is not a connection unless a dot says so — draw a small hop to show it.

m = b − n + 1

m is the number of meshes, b the number of branches, n the number of nodes, for a connected planar circuit.

Why it holds: pick a tree, a set of n − 1 branches that touches every node and contains no loop. The remaining b − (n − 1) branches are called chords. Each chord closes exactly one loop; the number of chords equals the number of meshes, m = b − n + 1, in a connected planar circuit. Unit 1.8 builds tie-sets and cut-sets on this same tree.

Counting the independent equations

KCL at all n nodes gives n equations, but they are not independent: add them and every current appears twice, once positive and once negative, so the sum is 0. Fix one node as the 0 V reference and write KCL at the other n − 1 nodes.

n − 1  node equations,   m = b − n + 1  mesh equations

n is the number of nodes, b the number of branches, m the number of meshes of a connected planar circuit.

Add them: (n − 1) + (b − n + 1) = b. Exactly b independent equations for b unknown branch currents. That balance is the reason for counting.

Open and short circuits are limiting branches. An open circuit carries zero current at any voltage: remove the branch. If it was the only path between two points, one node becomes two. A short circuit holds zero volts at any current: the two nodes it joins become one node. Redraw the circuit, then count.

Worked example

Question. In the circuit shown in the figure, four nodes A, B, C, D are joined by five branches numbered 1 to 5, with branch 5 running from A to C. Find (a) the number of nodes n and branches b, (b) the number of branches in a tree, (c) the number of chords and hence the number of meshes m, (d) the number of independent node equations, and (e) check that the node and mesh equations together equal b.

  1. Count the nodes: A, B, C, D give n = 4.
  2. Branches 1 and 2 meet at B and nothing else connects there, so they are in series; the same holds for 3 and 4 at D. Counting each drawn element as one branch gives n = 4, b = 5. Merging each series pair gives n = 2, b = 3, and m = 3 − 2 + 1 = 2, the same mesh count.
  3. A tree has n − 1 = 4 − 1 = 3 branches.
  4. The chords are the branches left out of the tree: b − (n − 1) = 5 − 3 = 2.
  5. Each chord closes one mesh, so m = 2. Reading the drawing, the meshes are triangle ABC (branches 1, 2, 5) and triangle ACD (branches 3, 4, 5).
  6. The outer square ABCD (branches 1, 2, 3, 4) is a loop, but it encloses both triangles, so it is not a mesh. This confirms m = 2, not 3.
  7. Independent node equations = n − 1 = 4 − 1 = 3. Take one node, say D, as the 0 V reference and write KCL at A, B and C.
  8. Check: node equations + mesh equations = 3 + 2 = 5, and b = 5. Equal, so the count is consistent.

Answer. n = 4 nodes and b = 5 branches; the tree has 3 branches, leaving 2 chords, so m = 2 meshes; the circuit needs 3 independent node equations, and 3 + 2 = 5 = b.

Exam traps

  • Using node and junction for the same thing in one answer. If your syllabus separates them, say which node you mean before you count n.
  • Counting meshes straight off a drawing where two branches cross. Redraw so the branches do not cross, or use m = b − n + 1, because a crossing without a dot is not a connection.
  • Treating two dots joined by a plain wire as two nodes. The wire has zero resistance in the model, so it is one node with one voltage.
  • Calling the outer boundary a mesh. The outer path is a loop; a mesh must enclose no other loop inside it.
  • Counting before redrawing an open or a short. A short merges two nodes into one and removes the shorted element; an open splits a node in two. Count n and b after the redraw, not before.

1.5 Series-parallel circuits; voltage and current division

Many circuits are not one resistor. They are groups in series, groups in parallel, and groups inside groups. Two reduction rules replace each group by one equivalent resistance. The voltage divider gives one series element's voltage; the current divider gives one parallel branch's current. With these, a 12 V supply feeding a small resistor network becomes one resistance, one source current, and a few branch values. Phone chargers, LED strings, sensor dividers and current shunts use the same arithmetic. The rules come from Ohm's law plus Kirchhoff's laws.

You need: 1.1 for R, L, C and 1.3 for Ohm's law, KCL and KVL.

Series and parallel reduction

Two elements are in series when the same current must pass through both, with no other path at the joining node. Resistors R1, R2, ..., in series add:

R<sub>eq</sub> = R<sub>1</sub> + R<sub>2</sub> + ... + R<sub>n</sub>

Req is the one resistance that would draw the same current from the same source; each R is in ohms (Ω).

Two elements are in parallel when they share the same two nodes, so both have the same voltage. For resistors in parallel:

1/R<sub>eq</sub> = 1/R<sub>1</sub> + 1/R<sub>2</sub> + ... + 1/R<sub>n</sub>

Req is the equivalent resistance in ohms; each R is a branch resistance in ohms.

For exactly two resistors this becomes Req = R1 × R2 / (R1 + R2). For n equal resistors of value R, Req = R/n. The parallel equivalent is always smaller than the smallest branch resistance.

  1. Mark nodes and label each resistor.
  2. Combine a clear series chain by adding.
  3. Combine a clear parallel group by reciprocals or the two-resistor form.
  4. Redraw with the new single resistor.
  5. Repeat until one source and one Req remain.

Reduction is valid while the group really shares one current (series) or one voltage (parallel). If a dependent source or a tapped connection makes that shared current or voltage depend on something outside the group, replace the group only with care. KCL and KVL from 1.3 justify the rules. Some syllabi call this network reduction.

Voltage and current division

A voltage divider is a series chain. The same current I flows through every resistor. Ohm's law gives I = Vs/Rtotal. The voltage across Rx is:

V<sub>x</sub> = I R<sub>x</sub> = V<sub>s</sub> R<sub>x</sub>/R<sub>total</sub>

Vx is the voltage across Rx in volts (V); Vs is the source voltage in volts; Rtotal is the series sum in ohms; Rx is one series resistance in ohms.

For two series resistors, V1 = Vs R1/(R1+R2). The larger resistor takes the larger share of voltage.

A current divider is a parallel group. The same voltage sits across every branch. For two resistors carrying total current Is:

I<sub>1</sub> = I<sub>s</sub> R<sub>2</sub>/(R<sub>1</sub>+R<sub>2</sub>), I<sub>2</sub> = I<sub>s</sub> R<sub>1</sub>/(R<sub>1</sub>+R<sub>2</sub>)

I1 is the current in R1 in amperes (A); I2 is the current in R2 in A; Is is the total current entering the pair in A; R1, R2 are in ohms.

Notice the reversal: the current in R1 uses R2 on top. The smaller resistance takes the larger current. For more than two branches use conductance G = 1/R, in siemens (S): Ik = Is Gk/Gtotal, where Gtotal = G1 + G2 + ... is the sum of the branch conductances. Divider rules save solving KCL and KVL when only one branch value is wanted. Sensor voltage dividers, LED current sharing and shunt resistors use them daily.

Inductors, capacitors and where reduction stops

The same series and parallel patterns apply to ideal inductors and capacitors, but with swapped roles. From the V-I relations in 1.1:

ElementSeriesParallel
ResistorReq = ΣR1/Req = Σ1/R
InductorLeq = ΣL1/Leq = Σ1/L
Capacitor1/Ceq = Σ1/CCeq = ΣC

So series inductors add, parallel inductors use reciprocals. Parallel capacitors add, series capacitors use reciprocals. These results are for ideal components; real coils have resistance and real capacitors have leakage, handled later.

Reduction stops at a bridge or ladder where no pair is simply series or parallel. Use star-delta in 1.6, source transformation in 1.7, or mesh and nodal analysis in Unit 2. Time-domain RL and RC behaviour is Unit 5. AC impedance dividers are Unit 3.

Worked example

Question. A 12 V source feeds R1 = 6 Ω in series with a parallel pair R2 = 12 Ω and R3 = 6 Ω. Find the equivalent resistance, the source current, the voltage across the parallel pair, and the current in each parallel resistor.

  1. The parallel pair R2 and R3 has R23 = R2R3/(R2+R3) = (12×6)/(12+6) = 72/18 = 4 Ω.
  2. Add the series R1: Req = R1 + R23 = 6 + 4 = 10 Ω.
  3. Source current Is = Vs/Req = 12/10 = 1.2 A.
  4. This current flows through R1. Voltage across the parallel pair is V23 = IsR23 = 1.2×4 = 4.8 V. Check: V1 = 1.2×6 = 7.2 V; 7.2+4.8=12 V.
  5. Current in R2: I2 = V23/R2 = 4.8/12 = 0.4 A.
  6. Current in R3: I3 = V23/R3 = 4.8/6 = 0.8 A. Check: 0.4+0.8=1.2 A.

Answer. Req = 10 Ω; source current = 1.2 A; parallel-pair voltage = 4.8 V; I2 = 0.4 A; I3 = 0.8 A.

Exam traps

  • Using R2/(R1+R2) for the current through R2 in a parallel pair. The current divider is reversed: I2 = Is R1/(R1+R2).
  • Adding parallel resistors directly: 12 Ω with 6 Ω is not 18 Ω; use the reciprocal or product-over-sum.
  • Using Req = R1+R2 for a group that shares two nodes. Series addition needs the same current, not just same drawing line.
  • Applying a voltage divider to a parallel group. The divider formula is for series resistors carrying one common current.
  • Writing series capacitors as C1+C2 or parallel inductors as L1+L2. These use reciprocals.

1.6 Star-delta transformation

Some resistor groups have three terminals and no simple series or parallel path. A star joins three resistors at one centre node; a delta joins them as a triangle. At the three outside terminals the two can behave the same. The star-delta transformation swaps one for the other using fixed resistor formulas. It exists so that a bridge or a three-terminal network can be reduced to series and parallel parts. Power supplies, three-phase loads and bridge sensors use the same three-terminal idea.

You need: series and parallel reduction and the divider rules from 1.5, and KCL/KVL from 1.3.

When three terminals are not series or parallel

A star is three resistors RA, RB, RC joined at one centre node N; their free ends are terminals A, B, C. A delta is three resistors RAB, RBC, RCA joined as a closed triangle between the same three terminals. If the outside circuit touches only A, B, C, the inside is a three-terminal network. Some syllabi call the star a Y or wye and the delta a Δ.

Series and parallel rules fail in a bridge because one resistor sits between two branches. A star or delta inside the bridge blocks the reduction. Replacing that group by its other form removes the block and turns the circuit into series-parallel parts.

The replacement is equivalent at the terminals: the same voltages at A, B, C give the same currents into A, B, C. The internal currents are not the same. Do not use the equivalent star/delta arms to find the power in each individual resistor of the original group; total terminal power is unchanged.

Delta to star

Name the delta resistors RAB between A and B, RBC between B and C, and RCA between C and A. Let SΔ = RAB + RBC + RCA. The equivalent star has centre N and arms RA, RB, RC.

R<sub>A</sub> = R<sub>AB</sub> R<sub>CA</sub> / S<sub>Δ</sub>; R<sub>B</sub> = R<sub>AB</sub> R<sub>BC</sub> / S<sub>Δ</sub>; R<sub>C</sub> = R<sub>BC</sub> R<sub>CA</sub> / S<sub>Δ</sub>

All resistors are in Ω. RA is the star arm at terminal A. SΔ is the sum of the three delta resistors, in Ω.

Look at the terminal letter. RA uses the two delta resistors that touch A, namely RAB and RCA. RB uses RAB and RBC. RC uses RBC and RCA. The denominator is always the full delta sum.

Star to delta and the balanced case

Go the other way with the star arms RA, RB, RC. Let P = RARB + RBRC + RCRA. The delta resistors are:

R<sub>AB</sub> = P / R<sub>C</sub>; R<sub>BC</sub> = P / R<sub>A</sub>; R<sub>CA</sub> = P / R<sub>B</sub>

P is the sum of the three products of star arms, in Ω². Each result is in Ω. The denominator is the star arm opposite the delta side: RC for side AB.

Balanced case: all star arms equal RY, all delta sides equal RΔ. Then P = 3RY² and RΔ = 3RY. The reverse is RY = RΔ/3. Use this as a quick check: 9 Ω star arms give 27 Ω delta sides, and 30 Ω delta sides give 10 Ω star arms.

In a bridge, pick the star or delta whose replacement creates a series or parallel pair. Convert once, redraw, then use series-parallel reduction from 1.5. Do not convert again unless the redrawn circuit still has a three-terminal block.

Worked example

Question. A delta has RAB = 10 Ω, RBC = 20 Ω, RCA = 30 Ω. Find the equivalent star resistors.

  1. Sum the delta resistors: SΔ = 10 + 20 + 30 = 60 Ω.
  2. RA = RAB RCA / SΔ = 10 × 30 / 60 = 300 / 60 = 5 Ω.
  3. RB = RAB RBC / SΔ = 10 × 20 / 60 = 200 / 60 = 10/3 Ω = 3.333 Ω.
  4. RC = RBC RCA / SΔ = 20 × 30 / 60 = 600 / 60 = 10 Ω.

Answer. The equivalent star has RA = 5 Ω, RB = 3.333 Ω, and RC = 10 Ω.

Exam traps

  • Wrong numerator order in delta-to-star: RA must use RAB and RCA, because those are the two delta resistors touching terminal A.
  • Using the delta sum as the denominator in star-to-delta. There the denominator is one star arm, the arm opposite the delta side being found.
  • Saying the two networks are the same everywhere. They are equivalent only at the three outside terminals. Internal currents and the power in each individual original resistor can differ. Total power entering the terminals is unchanged.
  • Reversing the balanced formulas. RY = RΔ/3, but RΔ = 3RY; the factor 3 is not used both ways.
  • Mixing units in one formula. Convert all resistances to Ω before adding or dividing, then attach Ω to the answer.

1.7 Source transformation

A practical source has internal resistance, so its terminal voltage sags as the load draws more current. Two circuits reproduce that sag exactly: a voltage source in series with a resistor, or a current source in parallel with the same resistor. Swapping one for the other, using I_s = V_s/R_s, is source transformation. It exists to make circuits easier to reduce before mesh or nodal analysis. It is used whenever a circuit mixes current sources and voltage sources, and it is the step behind Norton and Thevenin equivalents.

You need: source symbols and reference directions from 1.2, and series-parallel reduction with the current divider from 1.5.

Two models, one pair of terminals

A real source is not ideal. A cell, a lead-acid battery, a bench supply, each has some internal resistance. Charge pushed through that resistance loses part of the EMF as heat, so the terminal voltage falls as the load draws more current. Two circuits reproduce this fall, and from outside they are indistinguishable.

Model A is an ideal voltage source V_s in series with a resistance R_s. Write KVL round the loop: V = V_s − I·R_s. V is the terminal voltage in volts, V_s is the source EMF in volts, I is the current delivered to the load in amperes, and R_s is the internal resistance in ohms.

Model B is an ideal current source I_s in parallel with the same R_s. KCL at the top node gives I = I_s − V/R_s. Multiply by R_s and rearrange: V = I_s·R_s − I·R_s.

Compare the two lines. They are the same line when V_s = I_s·R_s. So the pair (V_s with R_s in series) and the pair (I_s with R_s in parallel) share one terminal V-I curve, one open-circuit voltage, one short-circuit current. Only the inside differs, and the load never sees the inside.

The rule, the arrow, the steps

I_s = V_s / R_s V_s = I_s · R_s

I_s is the transformed current source in amperes; V_s is the original voltage source in volts; R_s is the same resistance in ohms, moved from series to parallel or the reverse. Some syllabi call this source conversion.

  1. Find the practical source: an ideal source with a resistance in series (voltage form) or in parallel (current form).
  2. Read off R_s. Its value does not change; only its position changes.
  3. Compute the new source value with I_s = V_s/R_s, or V_s = I_s·R_s.
  4. Redraw: the series resistor becomes a parallel resistor, the parallel resistor becomes a series resistor.
  5. Point the current arrow at the node that was the + terminal of the voltage source. Reversing that gives the same magnitude with the wrong sign everywhere later.
  6. Continue with series-parallel reduction from 1.5, or with mesh and nodal analysis from Unit 2.

Direction loses the most marks. Inside a current source the arrow points from the negative terminal to the positive terminal, so the current leaves the + terminal into the external circuit, exactly as the + terminal of a voltage source delivers current outwards. The node that was + stays +.

Two cases refuse the swap. An ideal voltage source has R_s = 0, so I_s = V_s/0 is infinite and no finite current source matches it. An ideal current source has nothing in parallel, R_s = infinity, so V_s = infinity. Only practical sources with a finite, non-zero resistance transform.

Where it earns its place

Its job is to make everything look alike before you solve. Nodal analysis likes current sources feeding nodes; mesh analysis likes voltage sources round loops. Convert, merge, then write equations.

  • Voltage sources in series add. Current sources in parallel add, provided their arrows point the same way; opposite arrows subtract.
  • A branch with a voltage source and a series resistor becomes a current source whose parallel resistor joins the neighbouring parallel group directly.
  • The Norton current of a one-port is the short-circuit current, which is the I_s you get by transforming the Thevenin form. Dependent-source versions are handled in Unit 2.
  • The load stays outside the transformation. The equivalence is only what the two kept terminals see; a resistor you transform cannot also be the load.
  • Terminal V and I are preserved, internal power is not. In the worked example the voltage model's source delivers 64 W and the current model's delivers 32 W, while the load takes 21.33 W in both.

Worked example

Question. A 24 V source has 6 Ω in series with it and feeds a 3 Ω load. (a) Find the load current and load voltage directly. (b) Transform the source and find the same two quantities from the current-source model. (c) Confirm the two models agree.

  1. The 6 Ω series resistor and the 3 Ω load are in series. Total resistance = 6 + 3 = 9 Ω.
  2. Load current I = V_s / (R_s + R_load) = 24/9 = 2.6667 A.
  3. Load voltage V_load = I × R_load = 2.6667 × 3 = 8 V.
  4. Transform: I_s = V_s / R_s = 24/6 = 4 A, placed in parallel with the same 6 Ω.
  5. Now the 6 Ω and the 3 Ω are in parallel across the current source. The current divider gives load current = 4 × 6/(6 + 3) = 4 × 0.6667 = 2.6667 A.
  6. Load voltage = 2.6667 × 3 = 8 V.
  7. The arrow points at the node that was the + terminal of the 24 V source, that is, towards the load.
  8. Both models give 2.6667 A and 8 V, so the terminal behaviour is unchanged.

Answer. Load current is 2.6667 A and load voltage is 8 V from both models; the equivalent current source is I_s = 4 A in parallel with 6 Ω.

Exam traps

  • Reversing the current-source arrow. The head must point at the node that was the + terminal of the voltage source; flip it and every later current carries the wrong sign.
  • Transforming an ideal source. With R_s = 0 the equivalent current is infinite; with nothing in parallel the equivalent voltage is infinite. Only practical sources transform.
  • Leaving the resistor in both places. The series R_s and the parallel R_s are one and the same resistor, now moved; it is no longer in series after the swap.
  • Swallowing the load into the transformation. The equivalence holds only at the two kept terminals, so the load stays outside.
  • Comparing internal power instead of terminal quantities. In the example the voltage model's source gives 64 W and the current model's gives 32 W, yet the load takes 21.33 W in both cases.

1.8 Network topology: graphs, tie-sets and cut-sets

A circuit diagram is a graph: nodes are vertices and branches are edges. Network topology studies only this shape, not the element values. A spanning tree is a small connected skeleton with no loop. Every branch outside the tree, called a chord, closes exactly one fundamental tie-set. Every tree branch defines exactly one fundamental cut-set. These sets give the smallest independent set of KVL and KCL equations. Circuit simulators use the same idea to build sparse equations. Only a few universities teach this as a separate topic.

You need 1.4 for node, branch, loop and mesh, and 1.3 for KVL and KCL.

Graph, tree and co-tree

A graph G = (V, E) has a set V of nodes and a set E of branches. A node is a point where branches meet. A branch is one element or one wire between two nodes. A connected graph has a path between every pair of nodes. In an oriented graph every branch carries an arrow; the arrow fixes the positive current direction and later gives +1 or −1 in a matrix.

Tree branches = n − 1; chords = b − n + 1

n is the number of nodes; b is the number of branches; a chord is a co-tree branch.

A tree is a connected subgraph that contains all n nodes and has no closed path. A tree has exactly n − 1 branches. The co-tree is the rest of the graph. Each co-tree branch is a chord. The number of chords is b − (n − 1) = b − n + 1. The tree is not unique; any valid choice gives the same counts.

In the figure, n = 4 and b = 5. The solid edges A-B, B-C and C-D form one tree. Tree branches = 4 − 1 = 3. Chords = 5 − 4 + 1 = 2; they are D-A and A-C.

Tie-sets: one chord, one loop

A tie-set is a set of branches that forms a closed path, also called a loop. Pick a tree. Remove one chord and keep all tree branches. There is exactly one path in the tree between the two endpoints of the chord. That tree path plus the chord is a fundamental tie-set. Each chord gives one fundamental tie-set, so the count is b − n + 1.

Fundamental tie-sets = b − n + 1

b is the number of branches; n is the number of nodes; each tie-set uses one chord and its unique tree path.

Cut-sets: one tree branch, one split

A cut-set is a minimal set of branches whose removal splits a connected graph into two separate parts. Minimal means no smaller subset does the job. A fundamental cut-set is made by removing one tree branch. That removal splits the tree, and therefore the graph, into two parts. Collect every branch with one end in each part. The set contains that one tree branch and zero or more chords. Each tree branch gives one fundamental cut-set, so the count is n − 1.

n is the number of nodes; each fundamental cut-set contains exactly one tree branch.

Worked example

Question. A connected graph has n = 5 nodes and b = 8 branches. Find the number of tree branches, chords, fundamental tie-sets and fundamental cut-sets. State the rule for one fundamental tie-set.

  1. Tree branches = n − 1 = 5 − 1 = 4.
  2. Chords = b − n + 1 = 8 − 5 + 1 = 4. The value b − n = 3 is wrong because it misses one chord.
  3. Fundamental tie-sets = number of chords = 4.
  4. Fundamental cut-sets = number of tree branches = 4.
  5. One fundamental tie-set is one chord plus the unique tree path between the two endpoints of that chord.

Answer. The graph has 4 tree branches, 4 chords, 4 fundamental tie-sets and 4 fundamental cut-sets.

Exam traps

  • Using n instead of n − 1 for the number of tree branches.
  • Using b − n instead of b − n + 1 for the number of chords. For n = 5 and b = 8, b − n gives 3 but the correct count is 4.
  • Calling any loop a fundamental tie-set. A fundamental tie-set must be one chord plus its unique tree path.
  • Calling any group of branches a cut-set. A cut-set must be minimal and must split the graph into two separate parts.
  • Mixing the counts: fundamental tie-sets equal chords, while fundamental cut-sets equal tree branches, for a connected graph.

Takeaways

  1. Every passive element is defined by its V-I relation, not by its shape or name.
  2. Resistors dissipate energy, inductors store it in a magnetic field, and capacitors store it in an electric field.
  3. An independent source fixes its own value; a dependent source is controlled by another voltage or current.
  4. KCL follows charge conservation at a node; KVL follows energy conservation around a closed path.
  5. Counting nodes, branches and loops tells how many independent equations a circuit needs.
  6. Series and parallel reduction plus divider rules solve many circuits without full analysis.
  7. Star-delta and source transformations change the diagram, not the terminal behaviour.
  8. A tree gives a systematic way to choose independent tie-set and cut-set equations.

Glossary

Branch
A single element or a series path between two nodes, carrying one current.
Chord
A branch of the graph that is not in the chosen tree; each chord closes one tie-set.
Current division
The rule that in parallel branches each branch current is proportional to the conductance of that branch.
Cut-set
A minimal set of branches whose removal separates the graph into two parts.
Delta network
Three resistors connected in a closed triangle between three terminals.
Dependent source
A source whose voltage or current is controlled by another circuit voltage or current.
Independent source
A source that maintains a specified voltage or current regardless of other circuit variables.
Junction
A point where three or more branches meet; often used for a node with more than two connections.
Kirchhoff's current law
The algebraic sum of currents entering a node is zero.
Kirchhoff's voltage law
The algebraic sum of voltages around a closed loop is zero.
Loop
Any closed path in a circuit that starts and ends at the same node.
Mesh
A loop that encloses no other loop; used in planar mesh analysis.
Node
A point where two or more circuit elements meet.
Ohm's law
For a resistor, voltage equals current times resistance at constant temperature.
Open circuit
A path with zero current because the resistance between its terminals is infinite.
Parallel connection
Elements connected across the same two nodes, so they share the same voltage.
Planar graph
A circuit graph that can be drawn on a plane without branches crossing.
Series connection
Elements connected end to end so the same current flows through each.
Short circuit
A path with zero voltage because its resistance is zero.
Source transformation
Replacing a voltage source in series with a resistor by an equivalent current source in parallel with the same resistor, or the reverse.
Star network
Three resistors joined at one common node and brought out to three terminals.
Star-delta transformation
A conversion between a three-terminal star and an equivalent three-terminal delta.
Tie-set
One chord plus the tree path that closes it; it gives an independent KVL equation.
Tree
A connected set of branches that touches every node and contains no closed path.
Voltage division
The rule that in a series string each resistor takes a voltage proportional to its resistance.
