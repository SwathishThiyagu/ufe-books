1.1 Circuit elements R, L, C and their V-I relations

Every circuit is a set of elements joined by ideal wires. The three passive elements are the resistor R, the inductor L and the capacitor C, and each is defined by one rule that ties the voltage v across it to the current i through it. The resistor turns electrical energy into heat; the inductor and the capacitor store energy and give it back later. Every later method in the subject is built on these three rules.

Charge, current, voltage and power

Charge q is measured in coulombs (C). Current is the rate at which charge flows past a point: i = dq/dt, in amperes (A), where 1 A = 1 C/s. Voltage between two points is the energy needed to move one coulomb of charge from one point to the other: v = dw/dq, in volts (V), where 1 V = 1 J/C.

Power is the rate of energy flow: p = dw/dt = (dw/dq)(dq/dt) = v·i, in watts (W). Use the passive sign convention: draw the current reference arrow so that it enters the + terminal of the element. Then p = vi is the power the element absorbs. A negative p means the element is delivering power, which is what a source normally does.

A DC quantity is constant in time (a 12 V car battery); an AC quantity changes with time, usually as a sinusoid (the 230 V, 50 Hz wall socket). The element laws below hold at every instant, so they serve both.

The three element laws

Resistance R is the opposition to current that turns electrical energy into heat. For a uniform wire of resistivity ρ, length l and area A, R = ρl/A. Inductance L is the flux linkage per ampere of a coil, L = Nφ/i, where N is the number of turns and φ the flux in webers. Capacitance C is the charge stored per volt, C = q/v; for two parallel plates of area A, gap d and permittivity ε, C = εA/d.

G = 1/R is the conductance in siemens (S); the integrals run from t₀ to t. Units follow from the laws: 1 Ω = 1 V/A, 1 H = 1 V·s/A, 1 F = 1 A·s/V. Use the integral form when the other quantity is known, and add the starting value i(t₀) or v(t₀).

The energy formulas come from the power: for the inductor p = Li di/dt = d(½Li²)/dt, and for the capacitor p = Cv dv/dt = d(½Cv²)/dt. Ideal L and C only store energy and return it. All three elements are linear (R, L, C constant), passive (they cannot deliver more energy than they received) and bilateral (the law is the same with the terminals swapped).

What cannot jump, and real components

Because v = L di/dt, a sudden jump in inductor current would need an infinite voltage. So the current through an inductor is continuous: iL(0⁺) = iL(0⁻). In the same way the voltage across a capacitor is continuous: vC(0⁺) = vC(0⁻). The inductor's voltage and the capacitor's current can jump. These two facts give the initial conditions used later in transient analysis.

Real components are not ideal. Model a real inductor as an ideal L in series with its winding resistance Rw, which dissipates i²Rw. A real capacitor has leakage and an equivalent series resistance (ESR). A filament lamp, whose resistance rises as it heats, is not linear.

Worked example

Question. A 12 Ω resistor carries 2 A. A 0.5 H inductor carries 2 A with di/dt = 40 A/s. A 100 μF capacitor has 10 V across it with dv/dt = 200 V/s. Using the passive sign convention, find vR, vL, iC, the power absorbed by each element and the energy stored in L and C.

  1. Resistor: vR = Ri = 12 Ω × 2 A = 24 V. Power pR = i²R = (2 A)² × 12 Ω = 48 W, all turned into heat.
  2. Inductor: vL = L di/dt = 0.5 H × 40 A/s = 20 V. Power pL = vLi = 20 V × 2 A = 40 W, flowing into the magnetic field.
  3. Capacitor: convert first, 100 μF = 100 × 10⁻⁶ F = 10⁻⁴ F. Then iC = C dv/dt = 10⁻⁴ F × 200 V/s = 0.02 A = 20 mA. Power pC = 10 V × 0.02 A = 0.2 W, flowing into the electric field.
  4. Stored energy: wL = ½Li² = ½ × 0.5 × 2² = 1 J; wC = ½Cv² = ½ × 10⁻⁴ × 10² = 0.005 J = 5 mJ.
  5. Check: d(½Li²)/dt = Li di/dt = 0.5 × 2 × 40 = 40 W, the same as pL.

Answer. vR = 24 V, vL = 20 V, iC = 20 mA; powers 48 W, 40 W and 0.2 W absorbed; stored energies 1 J in L and 5 mJ in C.

Worked example 2

Question. (a) An uncharged 20 μF capacitor is charged by a constant current of 10 mA for 20 ms, after which the current is zero. Find its voltage at 20 ms and at 1 s, and the energy stored. (b) A 0.2 H inductor carries 0 A at t = 0. A constant 6 V is applied across it for 0.1 s. Find the current at 0.1 s and the stored energy.

  1. Capacitor, integral form: v(t) = v(0) + (1/C)∫i dt. With v(0) = 0 and a constant current, the integral is i × t = 10 × 10⁻³ A × 20 × 10⁻³ s = 2 × 10⁻⁴ C.
  2. v(20 ms) = 2 × 10⁻⁴ C / (20 × 10⁻⁶ F) = 10 V.
  3. After 20 ms the current is zero, so dv/dt = 0 and the ideal capacitor holds 10 V; at 1 s it still reads 10 V. Energy w = ½Cv² = ½ × 20 × 10⁻⁶ × 10² = 1 × 10⁻³ J = 1 mJ.
  4. Inductor, integral form: i(t) = i(0) + (1/L)∫v dt = 0 + (6 V × 0.1 s)/0.2 H = 0.6/0.2 = 3 A.
  5. Energy w = ½Li² = ½ × 0.2 × 3² = 0.9 J.

Answer. (a) 10 V at 20 ms and still 10 V at 1 s, storing 1 mJ. (b) 3 A at 0.1 s, storing 0.9 J.

Exam traps

  • Using v = iR for an inductor or a capacitor. Their laws are v = L di/dt and i = C dv/dt; a constant current gives zero inductor voltage, not iR.
  • Swapping the two derivative laws: the inductor's voltage depends on di/dt; the capacitor's current depends on dv/dt.
  • Saying that capacitor current cannot change suddenly. It is the capacitor voltage that is continuous; for the inductor it is the current.
  • Forgetting the prefix: 100 μF is 100 × 10⁻⁶ F, not 100 × 10⁻³ F; 50 mH is 50 × 10⁻³ H.
  • Calling ideal L and C lossy. They store and return energy; only resistance (including a coil's winding resistance) turns it into heat.

1.2 Independent and dependent voltage and current sources

Sources push energy into a circuit. An independent source fixes its own voltage or current, whatever the rest of the circuit does. A dependent (controlled) source sets its voltage or current as a fixed multiple of a voltage or current somewhere else in the circuit; it is how transistors and op-amps appear in circuit equations. Real batteries and supplies are neither perfect, so each ideal source also has a practical model with an internal resistance.

You need: the passive sign convention and power p = vi from Circuit elements R, L, C.

Ideal independent sources

An ideal voltage source keeps its terminal voltage at Vs volts for any current; the external circuit decides the current. Its symbol is a circle with + and − marks (a circle with a ∼ for a sinusoidal source). On a graph of terminal voltage v against current i its characteristic is a horizontal line v = Vs, so its internal resistance is zero.

An ideal current source keeps its current at Is amperes for any terminal voltage; the external circuit decides the voltage. Its symbol is a circle with an arrow showing the direction of the current. On the same graph its characteristic is a vertical line i = Is, so its internal resistance is infinite.

  • Never short-circuit an ideal voltage source: i = Vs/0 has no finite value. Never open-circuit an ideal current source: the voltage needed to push Is through a break has no finite value.
  • Two ideal voltage sources of different values cannot sit in parallel (KVL fails), and two ideal current sources of different values cannot sit in series (KCL fails).
  • A voltage source set to zero is a short circuit; a current source set to zero is an open circuit. The network theorems use this when they switch independent sources off.

Practical sources

A real battery loses part of its EMF inside itself. Model it as an ideal Vs in series with an internal resistance Rs. KVL round the source gives the terminal voltage v = Vs − iRs: it equals Vs on open circuit (i = 0) and falls linearly as the load current rises, reaching zero at the short-circuit current isc = Vs/Rs. A good voltage source has Rs much smaller than the load.

A practical current source is an ideal Is in parallel with a resistance Rp. KCL at its terminal gives i = Is − v/Rp: part of Is leaks through Rp, more as the terminal voltage rises. A good current source has Rp much larger than the load. When Is = Vs/Rs and Rp = Rs the two models give the same terminal line; that is source transformation.

Dependent sources

A dependent source has a control (a voltage vx or a current ix measured elsewhere) and an output that behaves as a voltage source or a current source. Its symbol is a diamond, with + and − for a voltage output or an arrow for a current output, and the control expression written beside it.

Worked example

Question. A 2 V source drives a 1 kΩ resistor; the voltage across this resistor is vx. A VCCS of gain g = 2 mS, controlled by vx, pushes its current into a 2.5 kΩ load. Find the output voltage, the voltage gain, the power the VCCS delivers and the power the 2 V source delivers.

  1. Input side: the 1 kΩ resistor sits directly across the 2 V source, so vx = 2 V and the input current is 2 V / 1000 Ω = 2 mA.
  2. VCCS output: i = g vx = 2 × 10⁻³ S × 2 V = 4 × 10⁻³ A = 4 mA.
  3. This current flows through the 2.5 kΩ load: vout = 4 × 10⁻³ A × 2500 Ω = 10 V.
  4. Voltage gain = vout/vx = 10 V / 2 V = 5.
  5. Power delivered by the VCCS = 10 V × 4 mA = 40 mW. Power delivered by the 2 V source = 2 V × 2 mA = 4 mW. The dependent source delivers ten times the input power: it is active, and the extra energy comes from the supply that powers the real amplifier.

Answer. vout = 10 V, gain 5; the VCCS delivers 40 mW while the input source delivers only 4 mW.

Question. A 12 V battery has an internal resistance of 0.5 Ω. Find the terminal voltage, the load power and the power lost inside for loads of 11.5 Ω, 5.5 Ω and 1.5 Ω, and the short-circuit current.

Exam traps

  • Treating a dependent source as independent: its value changes whenever its control changes, so keep the control as an unknown.
  • Dropping the gain unit: μ and β are pure numbers, g is in siemens and r in ohms. Convert 2 mS to 0.002 S before multiplying.
  • Drawing a dependent source as a circle. The diamond marks a dependent source; the circle is for an independent one.
  • Switching a source off the wrong way: a dead voltage source becomes a short circuit, a dead current source becomes an open circuit.
  • Quoting the EMF as the terminal voltage of a loaded practical source. Under load v = Vs − iRs, which is less than Vs.

1.3 Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws

Ohm's law gives the voltage across a resistor once its current is known. Kirchhoff's two laws do the bookkeeping for the whole circuit: charge cannot pile up at a node, and the voltages round any closed path must add to zero. With these three rules any circuit becomes a set of linear equations. The 220 Ω resistor in series with an LED, the divider in front of a 5 V regulator and the feeder currents in a city distribution network are all checked with exactly these laws.

You need: the element laws and the passive sign convention from Circuit elements R, L, C.

Ohm's law

Ohm's law: at constant temperature the voltage across a conductor is proportional to the current through it, v = iR, or i = Gv with the conductance G = 1/R in siemens. With the passive sign convention (current arrow entering the + terminal) v and i are both positive and the resistor absorbs p = vi = i²R = v²/R watts, which is why every resistor carries a power rating.

It holds for ohmic elements: a metal wire at fixed temperature, a carbon or metal-film resistor inside its rating. A diode, an LED and a filament lamp are not ohmic, so v = iR does not describe them. Two limiting cases matter: an open circuit carries i = 0 for any voltage (R → ∞), and a short circuit has v = 0 for any current (R = 0).

Kirchhoff's current law (KCL)

A node is a point, or a whole connected piece of ideal wire, where two or more elements meet; every point of it is at one potential. KCL: the algebraic sum of the currents at a node is zero, Σi = 0. Equivalently, the total current entering equals the total current leaving. Charge is conserved and cannot pile up at a point of a conducting circuit.

  1. Mark a reference arrow on every branch at the node; a negative answer later only means the real flow is opposite to the arrow.
  2. Choose one sign rule (entering +, leaving −) and use it for every term.
  3. Write the sum, set it to zero and solve.

KCL also holds for any closed boundary drawn round part of a circuit: the net current crossing the boundary is zero. Mesh and nodal analysis use this for the supernode.

Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL)

KVL: the algebraic sum of the voltages round any closed path is zero, Σv = 0. A charge carried round a closed path returns to its starting potential, so the rises and the drops cancel; KVL is conservation of energy written per coulomb. The path may be any closed path, simple or not.

  1. Choose a closed path and a direction to walk round it, and keep that direction.
  2. Entering an element at its − mark and leaving at + is a rise: write +v. Entering at + and leaving at − is a drop: write −v.
  3. For a resistor, mark the + end where the current arrow enters, so a walk along the current meets a drop of iR.
  4. Set the sum to zero: the sum of rises equals the sum of drops.

Both laws hold at every instant, so they apply to AC as well as DC; in sinusoidal steady state they are written with phasors, ΣI = 0 and ΣV = 0, and Ohm's law becomes V = IZ. For a circuit with n nodes and b branches, n − 1 KCL equations and b − n + 1 KVL equations are independent (see Network terminology). A final check on any answer is the power balance: the power delivered by the sources equals the power absorbed by the rest.

Worked example

Question. A 12 V supply with a 2 Ω lead resistance (R₁) charges a 4 V cell through 4 Ω (R₂), and an 8 Ω load (R₃) hangs from the junction A of the two resistors, as in the figure. Using KCL and KVL, find the three branch currents, the voltage of A and the power of every element.

  1. Unknowns: I₁ from the 12 V source into A through R₁; I₂ from the 4 V cell into A through R₂; I₃ from A down through R₃. Take the bottom wire B as 0 V.
  2. KCL at A (entering = leaving): I₁ + I₂ = I₃. (1)
  3. KVL, left window, walking along I₁: +12 − 2I₁ − 8I₃ = 0, so 2I₁ + 8I₃ = 12. (2)
  4. KVL, right window, walking along I₂: +4 − 4I₂ − 8I₃ = 0, so 4I₂ + 8I₃ = 4. (3)
  5. Put (1) into (2) and (3): 10I₁ + 8I₂ = 12 and 8I₁ + 12I₂ = 4. Multiply the first by 1.5: 15I₁ + 12I₂ = 18; subtract the second: 7I₁ = 14, so I₁ = 2 A. Then 8I₂ = 12 − 20 = −8, so I₂ = −1 A, and I₃ = 2 − 1 = 1 A.
  6. VA = I₃R₃ = 1 × 8 = 8 V. Check from the left: 12 − 2 × 2 = 8 V. From the right: 4 − 4 × (−1) = 8 V.
  7. I₂ is negative, so 1 A really flows from A into the + terminal of the 4 V cell: the cell is being charged and absorbs 4 × 1 = 4 W.
  8. Power balance: the 12 V source delivers 12 × 2 = 24 W. Absorbed: R₁ 2² × 2 = 8 W, R₂ 1² × 4 = 4 W, R₃ 1² × 8 = 8 W, cell 4 W; total 24 W.

Answer. I₁ = 2 A, I₂ = −1 A (1 A charging the cell), I₃ = 1 A, VA = 8 V; 24 W delivered = 8 + 4 + 8 + 4 W absorbed.

Worked example 2

Question. A single loop holds a 10 V source, R₁ = 2 Ω, R₂ = 4 Ω and a voltage-controlled voltage source of value 2v₁, where v₁ is the voltage across R₁. The VCVS opposes the 10 V source (its + terminal is where the loop current enters it). Find the loop current i and the power of each element.

  1. Walk round the loop along i. KVL: +10 − v₁ − 2v₁ − 4i = 0.
  2. Control equation: v₁ = 2i (Ohm's law on R₁). Substitute: 10 − 2i − 4i − 4i = 0, so 10i = 10 and i = 1 A.
  3. Then v₁ = 2 V and the VCVS is 2v₁ = 4 V.
  4. Powers: source delivers 10 × 1 = 10 W; R₁ absorbs 2 W, R₂ absorbs 4 W, the VCVS absorbs 4 × 1 = 4 W. Check: 2 + 4 + 4 = 10 W.

Answer. i = 1 A; the VCVS is 4 V and absorbs 4 W; the 10 V source delivers 10 W.

Exam traps

  • Mixing sign rules inside one KCL sum. Fix entering-positive (or leaving-positive) before writing the first term.
  • Giving a rise and a drop the same sign in KVL. Walk in one direction and read each element's polarity marks as you meet them.
  • Using the source voltage in Ohm's law instead of the voltage across that resistor.
  • Writing conductance in ohms: a 4 Ω resistor has G = 0.25 S, not 0.25 Ω.
  • Treating a dependent source's value as a known number. Write it in terms of its control (2v₁) and add the control equation.

1.4 Network terminology: node, branch, loop, mesh

A circuit diagram looks like a tangle of wires, but it is built from a few named parts: nodes, junctions, branches, loops and meshes. Name them and you can count them, and the counts tell you exactly how many independent equations the circuit needs before you write any. KCL is written at nodes and KVL round loops, so this counting is the first step of every nodal or mesh solution, and the graph methods of Network topology start from the same counts.

You need: KCL and KVL from Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws.

Nodes, junctions and branches

A node is a point where two or more elements meet. Ideal wire has zero resistance, so a whole connected piece of wire, however long it is drawn, is one node at one potential. Mark it with a dot and a letter. A junction (also called an essential node) is a node where three or more elements meet; current divides only at junctions.

A branch is a single two-terminal element, a source or a passive element, between two nodes; it carries one current. Some textbooks also call a chain of elements in series between two junctions one branch. Both counts work if you keep to one of them through the whole answer: count every element (and every node between elements), or count series chains (and only junctions).

  • Node: two or more elements meet; one potential.
  • Junction: three or more elements meet; currents divide here.
  • Branch: one element (or one series chain); one current.
  • Reference node: the node chosen as 0 V, often marked with a ground symbol.

Loops, meshes and planar circuits

A loop is any closed path: start at a node, follow branches and come back to the same node without using a branch twice. A mesh is a loop that has no other branch inside it; in a flat drawing the meshes are the window panes. Every mesh is a loop, but not every loop is a mesh.

Meshes exist only for a planar circuit, one that can be redrawn on paper with no branch crossing another. Where two wires cross without a dot, they are not connected. For a connected planar circuit the number of meshes is

m = b − n + 1

m is the number of meshes, b the number of branches and n the number of nodes.

Why: choose a tree, a set of n − 1 branches that touches every node and closes no loop. Each of the remaining b − n + 1 branches, the chords, closes exactly one loop with the tree, and these loops are independent. For a planar circuit Euler's formula for flat drawings gives the same number of window panes, so the meshes are also a full independent set. A chord's loop need not itself be a mesh; only the counts agree.

Counting the independent equations

KCL at all n nodes gives n equations, but they are not independent: add them all and every branch current appears once entering and once leaving, so the sum is 0 = 0. Take one node as the reference and write KCL at the other n − 1 nodes. KVL gives b − n + 1 independent equations, one per mesh. Together (n − 1) + (b − n + 1) = b: exactly enough for the b unknown branch currents.

Open and short circuits change the counts, so redraw first. An open circuit removes a branch (and may leave two elements in series). A short circuit merges the two nodes it joins into one and removes every element connected directly across those two nodes, since each now has zero voltage and carries no current.

1.5 Series-parallel circuits; voltage and current division

Most circuits are groups of elements in series, groups in parallel, and groups inside groups. Two reduction rules replace each group by one equivalent element, and two divider rules then give the voltage of one series element or the current of one parallel branch without solving the whole circuit. With them a 12 V supply feeding a small network becomes one resistance, one source current and a few branch values. Sensor dividers, LED strings and ammeter shunts use the same arithmetic, which follows directly from Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws.

You need: Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's laws.

Series and parallel reduction

Elements are in series when the same current must pass through each of them: they meet at nodes that nothing else touches. KVL adds their voltages, so series resistances add: Req = R₁ + R₂ + … + Rn. The equivalent is larger than the largest of them.

Elements are in parallel when they are connected across the same two nodes, so they share one voltage. KCL adds their currents, so conductances add: 1/Req = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … + 1/Rn, or Geq = G₁ + G₂ + … with G = 1/R in siemens (S). For two resistors Req = R₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂) (product over sum); for n equal resistors Req = R/n. A parallel equivalent is smaller than the smallest branch.

  1. Mark and letter every node.
  2. Combine any clear series chain by adding, and any clear parallel group by product over sum or conductances.
  3. Redraw with the single equivalent and repeat until one equivalent faces the source.
  4. For a ladder (series arms with shunt arms to a common wire), start at the end far from the source and work back towards it.

A group may be replaced only while it truly shares one current (series) or one voltage (parallel). A bridge has a cross arm that makes no pair series or parallel; that needs star-delta transformation or mesh and nodal analysis.

Voltage and current division

Voltage divider. In a series chain across Vs the common current is I = Vs/Rtotal, so the voltage across one resistor Rx is Vx = Vs Rx/Rtotal. For two resistors V₁ = VsR₁/(R₁ + R₂): the larger resistor takes the larger share.

Current divider. Two resistors in parallel share a total current Is. Their common voltage is IsR₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂), and dividing by each resistance gives I₁ = Is R₂/(R₁ + R₂) and I₂ = Is R₁/(R₁ + R₂). Note the swap: the current in R₁ has the other resistance on top, so the smaller resistance takes the larger current. For more than two branches use conductances: Ik = IsGk/(G₁ + G₂ + …).

The divider rules hold only when no current is drawn from the junction between series resistors (an unloaded divider) and when the parallel branches share the stated total current. A load hung on the middle of a divider is in parallel with the lower resistor and changes the ratio.

Inductors and capacitors in series and parallel

Inductors combine like resistors, because v = L di/dt adds the same way as v = Ri. Capacitors combine the other way round, because for them it is the charges (currents) that are shared in series and the voltages that are shared in parallel.

ElementSeriesParallelDivider
ResistorReq = ΣR1/Req = Σ1/RV₁ = V R₁/(R₁ + R₂)
InductorLeq = ΣL1/Leq = Σ1/LV₁ = V L₁/(L₁ + L₂)
Capacitor1/Ceq = Σ1/CCeq = ΣCV₁ = V C₂/(C₁ + C₂)

Capacitors in series all carry the same charge Q, so V = Q/C: the smaller capacitor takes the larger voltage, which is why the capacitor divider has the other capacitance on top. These rules assume no magnetic coupling between the coils and capacitors that start uncharged. AC impedance dividers use the same pattern with Z in place of R.

Worked example

Question. A 12 V source feeds R₁ = 6 Ω in series with a parallel pair R₂ = 12 Ω and R₃ = 6 Ω. Find the equivalent resistance, the source current, the voltage across each part and the current in R₂ and R₃.

  1. Parallel pair: R₂₃ = R₂R₃/(R₂ + R₃) = (12 × 6)/(12 + 6) = 72/18 = 4 Ω.
  2. Series with R₁: Req = 6 + 4 = 10 Ω.
  3. Source current I = 12 V / 10 Ω = 1.2 A; it all flows through R₁.
  4. Voltage divider: V₁ = 12 × 6/10 = 7.2 V across R₁ and V₂₃ = 12 × 4/10 = 4.8 V across the pair. Check: 7.2 + 4.8 = 12 V.
  5. Current divider: I₂ = 1.2 × 6/(12 + 6) = 0.4 A in the 12 Ω and I₃ = 1.2 × 12/(12 + 6) = 0.8 A in the 6 Ω. Check with Ohm's law: 4.8/12 = 0.4 A and 4.8/6 = 0.8 A; KCL: 0.4 + 0.8 = 1.2 A.

Answer. Req = 10 Ω; I = 1.2 A; 7.2 V across R₁ and 4.8 V across the pair; I₂ = 0.4 A, I₃ = 0.8 A.

Worked example 2

Question. A 6 μF capacitor C₁ is in series with a parallel pair C₂ = 4 μF and C₃ = 8 μF, across 60 V DC. All start uncharged. Find the equivalent capacitance, the charge drawn from the supply, the voltage on each part and the charge on C₂ and C₃.

  • Adding parallel resistors directly: 12 Ω with 6 Ω in parallel is 4 Ω, not 18 Ω.
  • Calling two resistors in series because they are drawn in a line. Series needs one shared current with nothing else at the joining node.
  • Using the voltage divider on a loaded divider. A load at the tap is in parallel with the lower resistor; combine them first.
  • Treating capacitors like resistors. Series capacitors combine by reciprocals and parallel capacitors add.

1.6 Star-delta transformation

Some groups of three resistors have three terminals and no pair in series or in parallel. A star (also called Y, wye or T) joins three resistors at one centre node; a delta (Δ, mesh or π) joins three resistors in a triangle. Seen from the three terminals, a star and a delta with the right values behave identically, so one can replace the other. The swap turns a bridge circuit into plain series and parallel parts, and the same idea gives per-phase equivalents of three-phase loads.

You need: Series-parallel reduction.

Why a three-terminal group blocks reduction

In the bridge of Network terminology, the 10 Ω arm from B to C joins the two halves. No two resistors share one current or one voltage, so series-parallel reduction stops. But the three resistors A-B, B-C and C-A form a delta, and the three resistors meeting at B also form a star. Replacing either group by its other form opens the way to series and parallel steps.

The replacement is equivalent at the three terminals only: the same terminal voltages give the same terminal currents, and the total power entering the group is the same. The currents and powers inside the group are different, so after reducing, go back to the original circuit to find the current in any resistor of the replaced group.

Delta to star

Call the delta resistors RAB, RBC and RCA, and the star arms RA, RB, RC from the centre N to the terminals. Matching the resistance seen between each pair of terminals (with the third terminal open) gives three equations whose solution is:

RA = RABRCA/S, RB = RABRBC/S, RC = RBCRCA/S, where S = RAB + RBC + RCA.

Memory rule: star arm = product of the two delta sides that touch that terminal ÷ sum of all three. For example, the pair condition between A and B is RA + RB = RAB ∥ (RBC + RCA); adding and subtracting the three such conditions gives the formulas.

Star to delta, and the balanced cases

Going back, let P = RARB + RBRC + RCRA (the sum of the products taken two at a time). Then RAB = P/RC, RBC = P/RA, RCA = P/RB. Memory rule: delta side = P ÷ the star arm opposite that side (the arm that touches neither of its terminals).

Balanced case: when all three resistors are equal, RY = RΔ/3 and RΔ = 3RY; for example 30 Ω delta sides become 10 Ω star arms. Balanced bridge: if the four outer arms satisfy RAB/RBD = RAC/RCD, then B and C are at the same potential, the cross arm carries no current and can be removed; no transformation is needed. Check for balance first.

  1. Find a delta (triangle) or star (three arms at one node) whose replacement creates a series or parallel pair.
  2. Convert it with the formulas, keeping the terminal letters.
  3. Redraw and reduce with series-parallel rules.
  4. For currents inside the replaced group, find the terminal voltages and return to the original circuit.

Worked example

Question. A delta has RAB = 10 Ω, RBC = 20 Ω and RCA = 30 Ω. Find the equivalent star and convert it back as a check.

1.7 Source transformation

A practical source has internal resistance, so its terminal voltage sags as the load draws more current. Two circuits reproduce that sag exactly: a voltage source in series with a resistor, and a current source in parallel with the same resistor. Swapping one for the other, with Is = Vs/R, is source transformation (some syllabi call it source conversion). It lets you merge sources, prepare a circuit for mesh or nodal analysis, and it is the step behind the Thevenin and Norton equivalents of the network theorems.

You need: practical sources from Independent and dependent sources, and the current divider from Series-parallel circuits.

Two models, one pair of terminals

Voltage form: an ideal Vs in series with R. KVL gives the terminal voltage v = Vs − iR, where i is the current delivered to the load.

Current form: an ideal Is in parallel with R. KCL at the top terminal gives i = Is − v/R; multiply by R and rearrange: v = IsR − iR.

The two lines are identical when Vs = IsR. Then both forms have the same open-circuit voltage Vs, the same short-circuit current Is and the same terminal line between them, so no load connected to the two terminals can tell them apart. Only the inside differs.

The rule and the arrow

Is = Vs/R (voltage form to current form) and Vs = IsR (current form to voltage form). R keeps its value; it moves from series to parallel or back.

  1. Find a practical source: an ideal voltage source with a resistor in series, or an ideal current source with a resistor in parallel.
  2. Compute the new source value; keep R.
  3. Set the direction: the current arrow points towards the terminal that was the + terminal of the voltage source. Going back, the + terminal goes on the side the arrow points to.
  4. Redraw, then merge with neighbouring elements and continue reducing.

The direction rule follows from the short-circuit current: shorting the voltage form drives current out of its + terminal and round through the short, so the current source must push current out of the same terminal. Only practical sources transform. An ideal voltage source (no series R) and an ideal current source (no parallel R) have no equivalent in the other form.

Merging sources

  • Voltage sources in series add with their signs: aiding sources add, opposing sources subtract. Their series resistors add.
  • Current sources in parallel add with their signs: arrows the same way add, opposite arrows subtract. Their parallel resistors combine in parallel.
  • So to merge practical sources in parallel, convert each to the current form, add; to merge practical sources in series, convert each to the voltage form, add.
  • A dependent source with its resistor transforms in the same way (I = μvx/R, for example), provided the control variable is not inside the part being transformed.
  • The load stays outside the transformation; the equivalence holds only at the two kept terminals. Terminal voltage and current carry over, but the power inside the source model does not.

Worked example

Question. A 24 V source with 6 Ω in series feeds a 3 Ω load. (a) Find the load current and voltage directly. (b) Transform the source and find them again. (c) Compare the power delivered by the ideal source in each model.

  1. (a) The 6 Ω and the 3 Ω are in series: I = 24/(6 + 3) = 24/9 = 2.667 A; load voltage V = 2.667 × 3 = 8 V.

Exam traps

  • Reversing the current arrow. It points to the terminal that was +; flip it and every later current changes sign.
  • Transforming an ideal source. Without a series R (voltage) or a parallel R (current) there is nothing to transform.
  • Keeping R in both places. The series R moves to parallel; it is the same resistor, not a second one.
  • Swallowing the load into the transformation. Keep the element whose current you want outside.
  • Comparing internal powers. In the first example the ideal sources deliver 64 W and 32 W, yet the load takes 21.33 W either way.

1.8 Network topology: graphs, tie-sets and cut-sets

Network topology studies only the shape of a circuit: which nodes each branch joins, not what the branch contains. Drawn this way a circuit is a graph. A spanning tree picked from the graph gives, in a fixed recipe, a complete set of independent KVL equations (one tie-set per chord) and of independent KCL equations (one cut-set per tree branch). The recipe always gives the right number of equations, which is why circuit simulators use it, and it covers circuits that cannot be drawn flat.

You need: node, branch, tree and chord from Network terminology.

Graph, tree and co-tree

Replace every element by a line (a branch or edge) between its two nodes (vertices). Some textbooks first replace ideal voltage sources by short circuits and ideal current sources by open circuits; here every element, source included, stays a branch. Give each branch an arrow for its positive current direction and the graph is oriented. A graph is connected if there is a path between every pair of nodes.

A tree is a connected set of branches that touches all n nodes and contains no closed path. Its branches are twigs; the remaining branches form the co-tree, and each of them is a link (chord). For a connected graph with n nodes and b branches:

twigs = n − 1, links = b − n + 1

These hold for a connected graph; a graph in k separate parts has n − k twigs. Many different trees exist, but every tree has the same counts.

The incidence matrix has one row per node and one column per branch, with +1 where the branch leaves the node, −1 where it enters, 0 elsewhere. Delete the reference node's row to get the reduced incidence matrix A; then A i = 0 is KCL at the other n − 1 nodes.

Tie-sets: one link, one loop, one KVL equation

Add one link to the tree and exactly one closed path appears: the link plus the unique tree path between its ends. This is a fundamental loop, and its set of branches is a fundamental tie-set. Orient the loop the same way as its link. There are b − n + 1 of them, and each contains a link that no other contains, so their KVL equations are independent.

Write them as a tie-set matrix B (rows: tie-sets; columns: branches; +1 if the branch arrow agrees with the loop direction, −1 if it opposes, 0 if absent). KVL is B v = 0. The link currents act as loop currents, and every branch current follows from them: i = Bᵀ IL. For the bridge in the figure (tree 4, 5, 6; links 1, 2, 3):

Cut-sets: one twig, one split, one KCL equation

A cut-set is a minimal set of branches whose removal splits a connected graph into exactly two parts; minimal means that putting back any one of them reconnects the graph. Remove one twig from the tree and the tree falls into two parts; the twig together with every link that joins the two parts is a fundamental cut-set. Orient it like its twig. There are n − 1 of them, one per twig.

Exam traps

  • Using n instead of n − 1 for the number of twigs, or b − n instead of b − n + 1 for the number of links.
  • Calling any loop a fundamental tie-set. A fundamental tie-set has exactly one link; the rest are twigs.
  • Calling any set of branches that separates the graph a cut-set. It must be minimal and leave exactly two parts.
  • Swapping the counts: tie-sets equal links (b − n + 1), cut-sets equal twigs (n − 1).
  • Ignoring orientation. In B and Q each entry is +1 or −1 by comparing the branch arrow with the loop or cut direction; a wrong sign corrupts the equation.

Takeaways

  1. Each passive element is defined by its V-I law: v = Ri, v = L di/dt, i = C dv/dt.
  2. Resistors dissipate energy; ideal inductors and capacitors store it (½Li², ½Cv²) and their current and voltage respectively cannot jump.
  3. An independent source fixes its own value; a dependent source is set by a voltage or current elsewhere and must keep its control equation.
  4. KCL is charge conservation at a node; KVL is energy conservation round a closed path; a power balance checks every answer.
  5. A connected circuit needs n − 1 KCL and b − n + 1 KVL equations, b in all.
  6. Series-parallel reduction with voltage and current division solves many circuits without simultaneous equations.
  7. Star-delta and source transformations change the inside of a network, never what its terminals see.
  8. A tree picks a complete independent set of loop (tie-set) and node (cut-set) equations, even for circuits that cannot be drawn flat.

  1. Resistance. The opposition to current that converts electrical energy into heat. R = v/i, unit ohm (1 Ω = 1 V/A); for a uniform wire R = ρl/A.

  3. Capacitance. The charge stored per volt, C = q/v, unit farad (1 F = 1 C/V = 1 A·s/V); for parallel plates C = εA/d.

link 3:  10I₃ + 2(I₁ + I₃) − 8(I₂ − I₃) = 0   →  2I₁ − 8I₂ + 20I₃ = 0
  7. Solve.
    I₁ = (36 − 2I₃)/22,   I₂ = (36 + 8I₃)/28
× 308:  14(72 − 4I₃) − 11(288 + 64I₃) + 6160I₃ = 0
         1008 − 3168 + 5400I₃ = 0   →   I₃ = 0.4 A
I₁ = 35.2/22 = 1.6 A,   I₂ = 39.2/28 = 1.4 A
  8. Branch currents. i₁ = 1.6 A, i₂ = 1.4 A, i₃ = 0.4 A, i₄ = 2 A, i₅ = 1 A, i₆ = 3 A.
  9. Cut-set check (Q i = 0):
    twig 4:  −i₁ − i₃ + i₄ = −1.6 − 0.4 + 2 = 0
twig 5:  −i₂ + i₃ + i₅ = −1.4 + 0.4 + 1 = 0
twig 6:  −i₁ − i₂ + i₆ = −1.6 − 1.4 + 3 = 0
  10. Nodal comparison. With D as reference the source fixes VA = 36 V, so nodal analysis needs only VB and VC:
    at B:  (36 − VB)/20 + (VC − VB)/10 = VB/2
at C:  (36 − VC)/20 = (VC − VB)/10 + VC/8
VB = 4 V, VC = 8 V:  1.6 + 0.4 = 2 ✓   1.4 = 0.4 + 1 ✓
  11. Count check. 3 tie-set + 3 cut-set = 6 = b equations, the same as 3 mesh + 3 nodal equations for this planar circuit (the voltage source removes one nodal unknown, as it fixes VA). The source sees 36/3 = 12 Ω, the value star-delta reduction gives.

Glossary

Active element
An element that can deliver net energy to a circuit, such as an independent or dependent source.
Branch
A single element (or one series chain) between two nodes, carrying one current.
Capacitance
Charge stored per volt, C = q/v, in farads.
Conductance
The reciprocal of resistance, G = 1/R, in siemens (S).
Current divider
The rule that splits a current between parallel branches in inverse proportion to their resistances.
Cut-set
A minimal set of branches whose removal splits a connected graph into exactly two parts; it gives one KCL equation.
Delta network
Three resistors connected in a closed triangle between three terminals.
Dependent source
A source whose voltage or current is a fixed multiple of a voltage or current elsewhere in the circuit.
Ideal current source
A source that keeps its current fixed whatever the terminal voltage; infinite internal resistance.
Ideal voltage source
A source that keeps its terminal voltage fixed whatever the current; zero internal resistance.
Incidence matrix
A node-by-branch matrix with +1 where a branch leaves a node and −1 where it enters.
Inductance
Flux linkage per ampere of a coil, L = Nφ/i, in henries.
Junction
A node where three or more elements meet.
Kirchhoff's current law
The algebraic sum of the currents at a node is zero.
Kirchhoff's voltage law
The algebraic sum of the voltages round any closed path is zero.
Link
A branch not in the chosen tree, also called a chord; each link closes one fundamental loop.
Loop
Any closed path in a circuit that does not use a branch twice.
Mesh
A loop with no branch inside it; a window pane of a planar drawing.
Node
A point, or a connected piece of ideal wire, where two or more elements meet.
Ohm's law
For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature, v = iR.
Open circuit
A break carrying zero current for any voltage (R → ∞).
Passive sign convention
Current reference entering the + terminal, so that p = vi is the power absorbed.
Planar circuit
A circuit that can be drawn on paper with no branch crossing another.
Practical source
An ideal source with an internal resistance, in series for a voltage source or in parallel for a current source.
Short circuit
A connection with zero voltage for any current (R = 0).
Source transformation
Replacing Vs in series with R by Is = Vs/R in parallel with R, or the reverse.
Star network
Three resistors joined at a common centre node, with their free ends as the terminals.
Tie-set
The branches of a fundamental loop: one link plus its tree path; it gives one KVL equation.
Tree
A connected set of branches touching every node with no closed path; its branches are twigs.
Voltage divider
The rule that shares a voltage between series resistors in proportion to their resistances.
