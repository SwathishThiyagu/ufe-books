3.1 Sinusoids: RMS and average values, form factor

A sinusoid is a voltage or current that rises and falls as a sine wave. Three numbers fix it: peak Vm in volts, angular frequency ω in rad/s, and phase φ in degrees or radians. Its heating effect is not the peak. It is the RMS value, the steady DC that would heat a resistor equally. For a pure sine, RMS is peak divided by √2. Every AC meter, appliance rating and power calculation uses this value, so reading 230 V at a socket means an RMS value near 230 V and a peak near 325 V.

You need: Unit 1 for the V-I relations of R, L and C, and school trigonometry with the idea of an average taken by an integral.

Peak, frequency and phase

Write a sinusoidal voltage as v(t) = Vm sin(ωt + φ). Vm is the peak value in volts, the largest value in either direction. ω is the angular frequency in rad/s. φ is the phase angle in radians or degrees. The same form works for current with Im and i(t).

v(t) = Vm sin(ωt + φ), T = 1/f, ω = 2πf

v(t) is the instantaneous voltage in volts; Vm is the peak voltage in volts; ω is the angular frequency in rad/s; t is time in seconds; φ is the phase angle; T is the period in seconds; f is the frequency in hertz.

Frequency f is in hertz, cycles per second. One cycle takes T = 1/f seconds. Angular frequency is ω = 2πf, so a 50 Hz supply has ω = 2π × 50 = 314 rad/s. A meter shows RMS, not peak.

Two sinusoids at the same frequency can be compared by phase. If v₁ reaches its peak before v₂, v₁ leads v₂. If it reaches later, v₁ lags v₂. The phase difference is measured in degrees or radians; 360° is one period.

Average value and RMS value

Average value means area under the curve over one interval divided by the interval length. For a pure sine over a full period, the positive and negative halves cancel, so the full-cycle average is 0 V. Many exam questions mean the average over one half cycle, from 0 to T/2. That value is not zero.

Vavg = (2/T) ∫₀^(T/2) Vm sin(ωt) dt = 2Vm/π = 0.637 Vm

Vavg is the half-cycle average voltage in volts; Vm is the peak voltage in volts; T is the period in seconds; ω is the angular frequency in rad/s.

RMS value is the square root of the mean of the square. Square the wave first, so sign disappears. Take the mean over one period, then the square root. This is the DC value that gives the same heating in a resistor. RMS is also called the effective value.

Vrms = √( (1/T) ∫₀^T v² dt ) = Vm/√2 = 0.707 Vm

Vrms is the RMS voltage in volts; v is the instantaneous voltage; T is the period in seconds; Vm is the peak voltage in volts.

  1. Square the sine: v² = Vm² sin²(ωt).
  2. Use sin²θ = (1 − cos 2θ)/2. Over one period the cos 2θ term averages to zero.
  3. The mean of the square is Vm²/2. Its square root is Vm/√2.

Form factor and peak factor

Form factor compares RMS with average: form factor = Vrms / Vavg. For a pure sine it is 0.707/0.637 = 1.11. Some syllabi call it shape factor. Peak factor, also called crest factor, compares peak with RMS: peak factor = Vm / Vrms. For a sine it is 1.414.

A multimeter on AC range is calibrated to show RMS for a sine. On a distorted wave, a cheap meter may read the average and scale it by 1.11, so it lies. A true-RMS meter computes the heating value directly. Use RMS for power: P = Vrms² / R for a resistor, or P = Vrms Irms cos φ for a general AC load; power formulas come in 3.5.

Phasor diagrams use the same peak or RMS values consistently. Keep one choice. Most power work uses RMS; some textbooks draw phasors with peak. See 3.2 for phasors.

Worked example

Question. An electric heater is connected to a supply v(t) = 325 sin(314t) V. Find the peak value, the RMS value, the average value over a half cycle, the form factor, the peak factor and the frequency.

  1. Compare v(t) with Vm sin(ωt). The multiplier of the sine is Vm = 325 V.
  2. The coefficient of t is ω = 314 rad/s. Frequency f = ω/(2π) = 314/(2π) = 49.97 Hz ≈ 50 Hz.
  3. RMS value Vrms = Vm/√2 = 325/1.414 = 229.8 V. This is the value an AC meter would show, close to the nominal 230 V.
  4. Half-cycle average Vavg = 2Vm/π = (2 × 325)/π = 206.9 V.
  5. Form factor = Vrms/Vavg = 229.8/206.9 = 1.11.
  6. Peak factor = Vm/Vrms = 325/229.8 = 1.414.

Answer. Vm = 325 V, Vrms ≈ 229.8 V, Vavg(half cycle) ≈ 206.9 V, form factor ≈ 1.11, peak factor ≈ 1.414, f ≈ 50 Hz.

Exam traps

  • Using the peak value where RMS belongs, such as in power, meter readings or impedance current.
  • Quoting zero as the average of a sine when the question means the half-cycle average; the full-cycle average of a pure sine is zero.
  • Mixing form factor and peak factor: form factor = RMS/average ≈ 1.11, peak factor = peak/RMS ≈ 1.414.
  • Forgetting that 230 V mains is RMS; the peak is about 325 V.
  • Applying sine factors to square, triangular or rectified waves; use their own table values.

3.2 Phasors and phasor diagrams

At one fixed frequency, every sinusoid in a circuit has the same ω. Only its peak and its phase change from point to point. A phasor stores those two numbers as one complex number: a vector frozen at t = 0. Adding two sinusoids then becomes adding two vectors. This turns differential equations into algebra with j. Phasors are used in AC circuit analysis, power calculations, transformers, machines and three-phase systems. The price is that the method works only in the sinusoidal steady state, at one frequency at a time.

You need: 3.1 for peak, ω and phase, and Unit 1 for the R, L, C voltage-current relations.

Euler's relation freezes the wave

Euler's relation is the bridge. It says ejθ = cos θ + j sin θ, with j² = −1. A cosine wave is v(t) = Vm cos(ωt + φ). Write it as Re{Vm ejφ ejωt}. The factor ejωt spins at the supply frequency ω and is the same for every sinusoid in the circuit. Drop it. What remains is the phasor V = Vm∠φ. Here Vm is the peak voltage in volts, φ is the phase angle in degrees or radians, ω is the angular frequency in rad/s, and t is time in seconds. The phasor is not the waveform. It is the waveform frozen at t = 0, with only magnitude and phase kept. To return, multiply by ejωt and take the real part for cosine reference, or the imaginary part for sine reference.

Sine reference: v(t) = Vm sin(ωt + φ), so the phasor is Vm∠φ on sine reference. Cosine reference: v(t) = Vm cos(ωt + φ), so the phasor is Vm∠φ on cosine reference. The two conventions differ by 90°. Since sin θ = cos(θ − 90°), a sine-reference phasor of 20∠0° is 20∠−90° on cosine reference. Pick the convention your textbook uses and state it. Some Indian syllabi use sine; many use cosine. Never switch midway.

RMS phasor: magnitude = Vm/√2. Use RMS magnitudes when the answer feeds a power calculation (see 3.5). Peak and RMS values cannot be added in one sum. Below, magnitudes are peak unless marked RMS.

v(t) = Re{V<sub>m</sub> e<sup>jφ</sup> e<sup>jωt</sup>} → V = V<sub>m</sub>∠φ

Vm is peak volts; φ is phase in degrees or radians; ω is rad/s; t is seconds; V is the phasor.

j is a 90° rotation

j is the imaginary unit, j = √−1. On the complex plane, multiplying any phasor by j rotates it counterclockwise by 90°. Check: 1 × j = j, and j × j = −1, which is 1 rotated by 180°. So jV has the same magnitude as V and angle φ + 90°.

Differentiation becomes multiplication by jω. Start with Vm ej(ωt+φ). Its time derivative is jω times the same expression. The common ejωt cancels, so d/dt → jω on phasors. Integration → 1/(jω) = −j/ω. This is why the inductor relation v = L di/dt becomes V = jωL I, and the capacitor relation i = C dv/dt becomes I = jωC V. Those element relations become impedances in 3.3.

V = a + jb = r∠θ; r = √(a² + b²); θ = tan<sup>−1</sup>(b/a)

a is the real part, b is the imaginary part, r is the magnitude, θ is the angle; choose the quadrant from the signs of a and b.

Complex algebra refresher. Add or subtract by real parts and imaginary parts separately. Multiply by multiplying magnitudes and adding angles. Divide by dividing magnitudes and subtracting angles. Some syllabi call all this the j-notation or complex representation.

Phasor diagrams and lead/lag

A phasor diagram draws each phasor as an arrow from the origin. Arrow length is magnitude; arrow angle is phase. The diagram is a snapshot at t = 0, not a picture of the wires.

Choose the common quantity as reference. In a series circuit the current is the same through every element, so draw I at 0°. Then place each element voltage at its angle: VR in phase with I, VL leading I by 90°, VC lagging I by 90°. In a parallel circuit the voltage is common, so draw V at 0° and place the branch currents by their element relations. For a capacitor, I leads V by 90°, so the current arrow is at +90° when V is at 0°. For an inductor, I lags V by 90°.

Lead and lag come from the sign of φv − φi. If φv − φi > 0, voltage leads current. If φv − φi < 0, voltage lags current. The words alone confuse; the sign does not. For a pure inductor, φv − φi = +90°. For a pure capacitor, φv − φi = −90°. For a resistor, it is 0°.

Add phasors tip to tail. Put the tail of the second at the head of the first. The resultant runs from the origin to the final head. Subtraction adds the negative phasor. The figure draws the worked example: 20 V at 0° plus 15 V at 60° gives a dashed resultant of 30.4 V at 25.3°. The resultant is the diagonal of the parallelogram formed by the two phasors.

Worked example

Question. Two series-connected AC sources apply v1 = 20 sin(314t) V and v2 = 15 sin(314t + 60°) V. Find the total voltage v = v1 + v2 as a single sinusoid. Use the sine reference.

  1. At ω = 314 rad/s both waves have the same frequency. Write phasors on sine reference: V1 = 20∠0° V peak; V2 = 15∠60° V peak.
  2. Convert V2 to rectangular: V2 = 15 cos 60° + j15 sin 60° = 7.5 + j12.99 V.
  3. Add real and imaginary parts: V = V1 + V2 = (20 + 7.5) + j12.99 = 27.5 + j12.99 V.
  4. Magnitude: |V| = √(27.5² + 12.99²) = √(756.25 + 168.75) = √925 = 30.4 V peak.
  5. Angle: φ = tan⁻¹(12.99/27.5) = 25.3°. Both parts are positive, so the angle is in the first quadrant.
  6. Return to time domain: v(t) = 30.4 sin(314t + 25.3°) V.

Answer. The total voltage is 30.4 V peak with phase 25.3°, so v(t) = 30.4 sin(314t + 25.3°) V.

Exam traps

  • Switching from sine to cosine reference in the middle of a solution and forgetting the 90° shift. A waveform that is 10∠30° on sine reference is 10∠−60° on cosine reference.
  • Adding a peak phasor to an RMS phasor, or adding magnitudes 20 and 15 to get 35. Phasors add as vectors; 20∠0° + 15∠60° is 30.4∠25.3°, not 35∠60°.
  • Using tan⁻¹(b/a) without the quadrant. If a is negative, the angle is in the second or third quadrant, so add or subtract 180° as needed.
  • Mixing up lead and lag. Use the sign of φ_v − φ_i: positive means voltage leads current; negative means voltage lags current. A capacitor has φ_v − φ_i = −90°, so its current leads its voltage.

3.3 Impedance and admittance

A resistor opposes current by a fixed amount. An inductor and a capacitor also oppose, but they delay or advance the current, so a single number cannot describe them. Impedance Z = V/I is the fix: a complex number whose magnitude is the size ratio and whose angle is the phase shift. Written as Z = R + jX, it combines in series and parallel exactly like resistance. Every AC design — a motor starter, a 50 Ω antenna feed, a filter on a power supply — is an impedance calculation. Admittance Y = 1/Z is the same fact upside down.

You need: phasors and the j operator from 3.2, plus complex number arithmetic.

Impedance: resistance that remembers phase

In DC the ratio V/I is R. At one fixed frequency the ratio is still meaningful, because every voltage and current is a phasor. That ratio is the impedance.

Each element gives one impedance. A resistor does not shift phase, so Z_R = R at 0°. An inductor's voltage leads its current by 90°, so Z_L = jωL. A capacitor's current leads by 90°, so Z_C = 1/(jωC) = −j/(ωC). All three are in ohms (Ω).

Z = R + jX, |Z| = √(R² + X²), θ = tan⁻¹(X/R)

Z is impedance in Ω; R is resistance in Ω; X is reactance in Ω; θ is the angle of Z in degrees, equal to the angle by which voltage leads current.

X is the part that stores and returns energy instead of burning it. Positive X is inductive and the current lags; negative X is capacitive and the current leads. Keep the j: 8 + j6 Ω is 10 Ω at 36.9°, not 14 Ω.

Series, parallel and the conjugate

Series impedances add: Z = Z₁ + Z₂ + …, because the current is common. The voltages divide in proportion: V₁ = V × Z₁/(Z₁ + Z₂).

Parallel admittances add: Y = Y₁ + Y₂ + …, or Z = Z₁Z₂/(Z₁ + Z₂) for two branches. The current divides as I₁ = I × Z₂/(Z₁ + Z₂); the larger impedance takes the smaller current.

  1. Write every element as R + jX at the source frequency ω = 2πf.
  2. Combine series by adding, parallel by adding reciprocals.
  3. To invert a + jb, multiply top and bottom by its conjugate a − jb: 1/(a + jb) = (a − jb)/(a² + b²). The denominator is now real.
  4. Use rectangular form to add, polar form |Z|∠θ to multiply and divide.

For example, 1/(6 + j8) = (6 − j8)/100 = 0.06 − j0.08 S.

Admittance and frequency behaviour

Admittance is the reciprocal: Y = 1/Z = G + jB, in siemens (S); older texts say mho. G is conductance, B is susceptance. Y_R = 1/R, Y_L = −j/(ωL), Y_C = jωC. Series work is easier in Z, parallel work in Y.

Frequency changes the picture. |Z_L| = ωL grows with ω, so an inductor blocks high frequencies. |Z_C| = 1/(ωC) shrinks, so a capacitor passes them. At ω → 0 the capacitor is an open circuit and the inductor a short; at high ω the roles swap. This is the mechanism behind every filter.

Worked example

Question. A coil of resistance 8 Ω and inductance 0.02 H is in series with a 100 µF capacitor across a 230 V, 50 Hz supply. Find the total impedance, the current, and the voltage across the coil.

  1. ω = 2πf = 2π × 50 = 314.16 rad/s.
  2. X_L = ωL = 314.16 × 0.02 = 6.28 Ω.
  3. X_C = 1/(ωC) = 1/(314.16 × 100 × 10⁻⁶) = 31.83 Ω.
  4. X = X_L − X_C = 6.28 − 31.83 = −25.55 Ω (capacitive overall).
  5. Z = R + jX = 8 − j25.55 Ω.
  6. |Z| = √(8² + 25.55²) = √(64 + 652.8) = 26.77 Ω.
  7. θ = tan⁻¹(−25.55/8) = −72.6°, so the current leads the supply voltage by 72.6°.
  8. I = V/|Z| = 230/26.77 = 8.59 A.
  9. Coil alone: Z_coil = 8 + j6.28 Ω, |Z_coil| = √(64 + 39.4) = 10.17 Ω.
  10. V_coil = I × |Z_coil| = 8.59 × 10.17 = 87.4 V.

Answer. Z = 8 − j25.55 Ω (26.77 Ω at −72.6°), I = 8.59 A leading, and V_coil = 87.4 V.

Exam traps

  • Sign of reactance: X_L is +ωL, but X_C enters as −1/(ωC). A capacitive branch has negative X, so the angle of Z is negative and the current leads.
  • Adding R and X as plain numbers: 8 + j6 Ω is 10 Ω at 36.9°, not 14 Ω.
  • Forgetting the quadrant: tan⁻¹ alone loses the sign, so use atan2(X, R) and keep degrees or radians consistent.
  • Writing 'impedance of a capacitor is 1/ωC'. That is only the magnitude; the −j carries the −90° phase.
  • Unit slip: admittance is in siemens, not ohms. Y = 0.06 − j0.08 S has magnitude 0.1 S, which matches Z of magnitude 10 Ω.

3.4 Steady-state analysis of RL, RC and RLC circuits

Every AC circuit at one fixed frequency can be solved with the DC toolkit you already know — mesh, nodal, Thevenin, superposition — once each element is written as an impedance and each source and answer as a phasor. The method skips the switching transient and keeps only the response that survives: the steady state. Electricity boards, motor designers and anyone holding a clamp meter work this way, because at 50 Hz the transient is over in milliseconds.

You need: phasors and impedance from 3.2 and 3.3, plus mesh, nodal and Thevenin analysis from Unit 2.

The four steps

The method rests on one fact: at a single frequency ω, every voltage and current in a linear circuit is a sinusoid of that same frequency. Only two numbers change from point to point — magnitude and phase. A phasor carries exactly those two numbers, and an impedance carries how each element scales and shifts them.

So the circuit becomes a complex-number circuit at ω. Kirchhoff's laws are untouched: they hold at every instant, so they also hold for the phasors.

  1. Write every source as a phasor V = V∠φ, with φ measured against your chosen reference.
  2. Replace each element by its impedance: R stays R, L becomes jωL, C becomes 1/(jωC) = −j/(ωC).
  3. Solve with mesh analysis, nodal analysis or any network theorem, using ordinary complex arithmetic.
  4. Convert the complex answer back: I = |I|∠ψ means i(t) = √2|I| cos(ωt + ψ) when |I| is RMS. Use the same reference as the source: if v(t) = √2|V| cos(ωt + φ) then i(t) = √2|I| cos(ωt + ψ); if the source is written with sin, write the answer with sin.

Keep magnitudes as RMS. Then |V| reads the same as a voltmeter and |I| the same as an ammeter. A supply called 230 V means 230 V RMS, whose peak is 230√2 = 325 V; the √2 appears only when you write the time function.

Series RL, RC and RLC

Z = R + jX,  X = ωL − 1/(ωC),  I = V/Z,  |Z| = √(R² + X²),  θ = tan⁻¹(X/R)

Z is impedance in Ω; R is resistance in Ω; X is net reactance in Ω; ω is angular frequency in rad/s; L is inductance in H; C is capacitance in F; V and I are RMS phasors in V and A; θ is the impedance angle in degrees. Because I = V/Z, the current angle = (angle of V) − θ.

In a series circuit one current flows through all three elements, so you find I first and then multiply by each impedance. The voltages add as phasors, not as numbers.

The sign of θ tells you the story. If X > 0 the circuit is inductive and the current lags the source. If X < 0 the circuit is capacitive and the current leads the source. X = 0 is series resonance, which belongs to Unit 4; there Z = R and the current is largest and in phase.

Two element voltages can each be bigger than the source. Near resonance X_L and X_C are large and nearly equal, so V_L and V_C are large and nearly opposite. Their phasor difference V_L − V_C is small, and that small difference combined with V_R rebuilds the source exactly. A capacitor in a tuned filter can read 338 V while the supply is 230 V, which is why such circuits are handled with care.

Parallel case, real coils, and the Laplace check

In parallel, admittances add: Y = 1/R + jωC + 1/(jωL), and Z = 1/Y. Series-parallel combinations reduce by the same rules as resistors, provided you never drop the j.

Parallel check: R = 30 Ω in parallel with L = 0.1 H at ω = 314.16 rad/s. X_L = 31.42 Ω, so Z = (30 × j31.42)/(30 + j31.42) = 15.69 + j14.99 Ω, that is 21.70 Ω at 43.7°. Adding admittances gives the same: Y = 0.0333 − j0.0318 S.

A practical coil is never pure inductance; the winding has resistance. Model it as one impedance R + jωL with R the measured DC resistance. Its angle is then less than 90°, and ignoring that resistance is a common source of wrong answers in power-factor problems.

Unit 5 solves the same circuit with the Laplace transform. Its partial-fraction answer has a transient that decays like e^(−t/τ) plus a steady sinusoid. As t → ∞ the transient vanishes and only the phasor answer remains. Both roads agree; the phasor road is shorter when the steady state is all you want.

Worked example

Question. A series RLC circuit has R = 40 Ω, L = 0.15 H and C = 50 µF. The source is v(t) = 230√2 sin(314.16t) V. Find i(t) and the RMS voltages across R, L and C, and check that the element voltages rebuild the source.

  1. ω = 314.16 rad/s. The source as an RMS phasor is V = 230∠0° V (peak 230√2 = 325.3 V).
  2. X_L = ωL = 314.16 × 0.15 = 47.12 Ω.
  3. X_C = 1/(ωC) = 1/(314.16 × 50×10⁻⁶) = 63.66 Ω.
  4. Net reactance X = X_L − X_C = 47.12 − 63.66 = −16.54 Ω. Negative, so the circuit is capacitive.
  5. Z = R + jX = 40 − j16.54 Ω. |Z| = √(40² + 16.54²) = 43.28 Ω, and θ = tan⁻¹(−16.54/40) = −22.5°.
  6. I = V/Z = 230∠0° ÷ 43.28∠−22.5° = 5.31∠+22.5° A RMS. Peak = 5.31 × 1.414 = 7.51 A.
  7. The current angle is 0 − (−22.5°) = +22.5°, so the current leads the source: i(t) = 7.51 sin(314.16t + 22.5°) A.
  8. V_R = I R = 5.31 × 40 = 212.5 V, in phase with I.
  9. V_L = I X_L = 5.31 × 47.12 = 250.4 V, leading I by 90°.
  10. V_C = I X_C = 5.31 × 63.66 = 338.3 V, lagging I by 90°.

Exam traps

  • Stopping at Z or I in complex form. The examiner wants the time function with its phase; a bare 5.31∠22.5° A can lose half the marks.
  • Subtracting V_L from V_C as if the two were in phase. They are 180° apart, so they oppose as phasors; written as 250.4 − 338.3 they only look like a subtraction because both are taken along the same axis.
  • Mixing peak and RMS. Phasors are kept in RMS so they match meter readings, and √2 is applied only when writing i(t). Mixing the two scales gives answers wrong by 41%.
  • Treating a real coil as pure jωL. Its winding resistance must sit in the same impedance, so the coil angle is below 90°.
  • Reversing lead and lag. Check the sign: X > 0 means inductive and the current lags; X < 0 means capacitive and the current leads.

3.5 Real, reactive, apparent and complex power; power factor

AC power has two parts. The real part, P, does useful work and becomes heat, light or torque. The reactive part, Q, only swings energy between the source and the load's electric or magnetic field. Together they form complex power S = P + jQ; its magnitude is apparent power, measured in volt-amperes. This idea lets you size cables and generators, read an electricity bill, and correct a low power factor with a capacitor.

You need RMS values from 3.1, the impedance angle from 3.3, and phasor V and I from 3.4.

Instantaneous and average power

With v(t)=Vm sin(ωt+θv) and i(t)=Im sin(ωt+θi), instantaneous power is p(t)=v(t)i(t). Let φ=θv−θi. Using sin A sin B = ½[cos(A−B)−cos(A+B)] gives

p(t) = Vrms Irms cos φ − Vrms Irms cos(2ωt + θv + θi)

Vm, Im are peak volts and amps; Vrms=Vm/√2 and Irms=Im/√2; ω=2πf; φ=θv−θi is the impedance angle.

The second term has frequency 2f, so power pulses twice per supply cycle. Its average over a cycle is zero. The average is P=Vrms Irms cosφ, in watts (W). A positive P means net energy flows from source to load. When cosφ < 1, p(t) can go negative for part of each cycle; energy then returns to the source. The figure uses φ=60° lagging. It shows p/(VmIm) dipping below zero and averaging 0.25. Only resistance absorbs real power: P=Irms²R. The double-frequency component has zero average and does not add to P. Its quadrature part, the Q sin 2ωt part, represents exchange with stored energy. Its in-phase part is the twice-frequency pulsation of real power. In a pure resistor the oscillation is real-power pulsation, not stored energy.

Reactive, apparent and complex power

Reactive power is Q=Vrms Irms sinφ, in volt-ampere reactive (VAR). It is not heat; it is the rate at which energy is exchanged with inductance or capacitance. By convention, an inductive load has current lagging voltage, φ>0, so Q is positive. A capacitive load has current leading, φ<0, so Q is negative. Apparent power is |S|=Vrms Irms, in volt-ampere (VA). It is the product of RMS readings and sets the current a cable must carry. Complex power is S=V I*, where I* is the complex conjugate of the current phasor and V is the voltage phasor. With V=Vrms∠0 and I=Irms∠−φ, S=Vrms Irms∠φ=P+jQ.

P = |S| cosφ, Q = |S| sinφ, |S| = √(P² + Q²), φ = tan⁻¹(Q/P)

P is real power in W; Q is reactive power in VAR; |S| is apparent power in VA; φ is the power-factor angle; j is the imaginary unit.

The power triangle is a right triangle with P on the adjacent side, Q on the opposite side and |S| on the hypotenuse. Across parallel branches, real powers add and reactive powers add with sign: P_total=ΣP, Q_total=ΣQ. Apparent powers do not add arithmetically. Two loads of 100 VA each at different power factors give |S_total|=√(P_total²+Q_total²), not 200 VA unless their power factors are equal and of the same sign (both lagging or both leading).

Power factor and correction

Power factor is cosφ=P/|S|. It is called lagging when current lags voltage (inductive) and leading when current leads voltage (capacitive). A low power factor means a larger current for the same P, so cables, transformers and switchgear must be bigger, and losses I²R rise. Utilities therefore charge industrial consumers for poor power factor; the exact tariff varies. Correction means adding a parallel capacitor that supplies Qc while P stays the same. For a load Q1 at φ1 changed to Q2 at φ2, Qc=Q1−Q2=P(tanφ1−tanφ2). The capacitor is C=Qc/(ωVrms²), with Vrms the supply voltage and ω=2πf. The capacitor's own reactive power is −Qc, so it cancels part of the inductive Q. After correction the apparent power falls from P/cosφ1 to P/cosφ2, but the real power P is unchanged. The supply current falls because it carries less reactive component. This is why a factory can avoid a penalty without changing its machines.

Worked example

Question. A 230 V, 50 Hz supply feeds a motor taking 10 A at 0.8 power factor lagging. Find the real power P, the reactive power Q and the apparent power |S|. Find the parallel capacitor that raises the power factor to 0.95 lagging.

  1. Apparent power: |S|=V×I=230×10=2300 VA.
  2. Real power: P=|S| cosφ1=2300×0.8=1840 W. Also φ1=acos(0.8)=36.87°.
  3. Reactive power: Q1=|S| sinφ1=2300×0.6=1380 VAR. It is positive because the load is inductive and lagging.
  4. At the new power factor 0.95: φ2=acos(0.95)=18.19°. The real power stays 1840 W, so Q2=P tanφ2=1840×0.3287=604.8 VAR.
  5. The capacitor must supply Qc=Q1−Q2=1380−604.8=775.2 VAR.
  6. ω=2πf=2π×50=314.16 rad/s. C=Qc/(ωV²)=775.2/(314.16×230²)=46.6×10⁻⁶ F.
  7. The capacitor value is 46.6 µF, connected in parallel with the motor.

  • Using peak values in V I: average power uses RMS. For a sine, Vrms=Vm/√2, so P=Vrms Irms cosφ=(Vm Im/2)cosφ.
  • Adding apparent powers: 100 VA at 0.6 pf plus 100 VA at 0.8 pf is not 200 VA. Add P and Q separately, then |S|=√(P²+Q²).
  • Getting the sign of Q wrong: inductive lagging has Q positive; capacitive leading has Q negative. A capacitor's Qc is negative in the sum S_load+S_C.
  • Forgetting that power-factor correction leaves P unchanged: the capacitor changes Q and line current, not the motor's watts.
  • Mixing units: W for P, VAR for Q, VA for |S|. Do not write VAR for apparent power or W for reactive power.

3.6 Balanced and unbalanced three-phase circuits

A three-phase supply is three sinusoids of equal RMS value and frequency, spaced 120° apart. At every instant their powers add to a constant, and in a balanced load their currents cancel in the neutral. That lets a balanced system be solved as one phase. An unbalanced load breaks the cancellation: the neutral point shifts, currents differ, and you must solve each phase and add phasors. The idea runs office wiring, 400 V motors, data-centre UPS systems and the grid itself.

You need: phasors for 120° shifts, impedance for per-phase Z, and single-phase power for cos φ.

Phase sequence, star and delta quantities

A three-phase source has three voltages of the same RMS value and frequency. Their phase angles are 0°, −120° and +120°. The order in which they reach their peaks is the phase sequence: a-b-c means phase a peaks first, then b 120° later, then c. Reverse two lines and the sequence becomes a-c-b; motors then run backwards.

V<sub>L</sub> = √3 V<sub>ph</sub> and I<sub>L</sub> = I<sub>ph</sub> (star); V<sub>L</sub> = V<sub>ph</sub> and I<sub>L</sub> = √3 I<sub>ph</sub> (delta)

Vph is the RMS phase voltage across one branch; VL is the RMS line-to-line voltage; Iph is the RMS current inside one phase branch; IL is the RMS current in one line conductor.

In a star (wye) connection each phase branch sits between one line and the neutral. The phase voltage Vph is line-to-neutral; the line voltage VL is between two lines. In a delta connection each branch sits between two lines, so the phase voltage equals the line voltage. There is no neutral in a three-wire delta.

For balanced positive sequence, the star line voltage Vab leads Van by 30°. In delta, the line current Ia lags the phase current Iab by 30°. Keep the √3 with the line quantity that is larger: voltage in star, current in delta.

Balanced load: solve one phase

A balanced load has equal impedance in every phase: same |Z| and same angle φ. The three phase currents are equal in RMS and 120° apart. Their phasor sum is zero, so the neutral current is zero in a four-wire star system.

  1. Identify the connection and write the per-phase impedance Z = R + jX.
  2. Choose phase a. Find Vph: VL/√3 for star, VL for delta.
  3. Find Iph = Vph/Z and the angle φ = angle of Z. In star IL = Iph; in delta IL = √3 Iph.
  4. Find total real power P = 3 Vph Iph cos φ = √3 VL IL cos φ and reactive power Q = √3 VL IL sin φ.

The per-phase equivalent is a single-phase circuit with the phase voltage and one Z. Total power is three times the per-phase power. The formula √3 VL IL cos φ works for both star and delta because the √3 and the current or voltage ratio cancel. If a delta load must be replaced by an equivalent star, use the star-delta transformation from Unit 1, Foundations.

Unbalanced load: neutral shift

An unbalanced load has different Z in the three phases, or different phase angles. The currents no longer add to zero. In a four-wire star system with a solid neutral, each phase voltage is fixed by the supply, so each phase current is found on its own: Ia = Va/Za, Ib = Vb/Zb, Ic = Vc/Zc. The neutral current is the phasor sum IN = Ia + Ib + Ic. It is generally not zero.

V<sub>N′N</sub> = (V<sub>a</sub>Y<sub>a</sub> + V<sub>b</sub>Y<sub>b</sub> + V<sub>c</sub>Y<sub>c</sub>) / (Y<sub>a</sub> + Y<sub>b</sub> + Y<sub>c</sub>)

VN′N is the shift of the load star point N′ from the source neutral N; Va, Vb, Vc are source phase voltages; Ya = 1/Za, Yb = 1/Zb, Yc = 1/Zc are phase admittances.

When the neutral is absent or has impedance, the star point N′ of the load shifts. Use Millman's theorem to find VN′N, then the load phase voltage is Va − VN′N. Compute each current and add them phasor-wise.

A three-wire delta has no neutral. Solve it by mesh currents around the three loops, or convert the delta load to an equivalent star and use the neutral-shift method. For any general unbalanced network, symmetrical components split the system into positive, negative and zero sequences; that method belongs to a power-systems course. This chapter stops at per-phase and mesh solutions.

3.7 Three-phase power measurement: two-wattmeter method

A three-wire three-phase load has no neutral, so you cannot multiply one phase voltage by one phase current. Two wattmeters, wired into any two lines, solve the problem. Their current coils carry two line currents; their voltage coils measure the two line-to-line voltages referred to the third line. The sum of the two readings is the total real power, for a balanced or an unbalanced load. The difference gives the reactive power and the power factor when the load is balanced. Industry uses this method on motors, feeders and distribution panels because it needs only two meters and no neutral connection.

You need real and reactive power and balanced three-phase quantities.

The connection

The connection. A three-wire system has lines R, Y and B, and no neutral. Each wattmeter has a current coil (CC) and a voltage coil (VC). Put the CC of W1 in line R and the CC of W2 in line Y. Connect the VC of W1 between R and B; connect the VC of W2 between Y and B. Line B is the common reference. A wattmeter reads V I cos θ, where V is the RMS voltage across its VC, I is the RMS current through its CC, and θ is the angle between them. In this connection each meter sees a line-to-line voltage VL and a line current IL. The total real power is P = W1 + W2. This sum is correct for a balanced load and also for an unbalanced load, as long as the system has three wires. The two meters need not be in R and Y; any two lines work, with the third line as the common voltage reference.

Balanced load: where the 30° comes from

Let VL be the RMS line voltage and IL the RMS line current. Let φ be the angle by which each line current lags its phase voltage. In a balanced three-phase system each line-to-line voltage differs in phase from the corresponding phase voltage by 30° (see phasor diagrams). For W1, with CC in R and VC across R and B, the angle between VRB and IR is 30° − φ. For W2, with CC in Y and VC across Y and B, the angle is 30° + φ. Therefore W1 = VL IL cos(30° − φ) and W2 = VL IL cos(30° + φ). Add them: cos(30° − φ) + cos(30° + φ) = 2 cos 30° cos φ = √3 cos φ. So P = W1 + W2 = √3 VL IL cos φ. Subtract: W1 − W2 = 2 VL IL sin 30° sin φ = VL IL sin φ. The total reactive power is Q = √3 VL IL sin φ, so Q = √3 (W1 − W2) in VAR. The power-factor angle follows from tan φ = Q/P = √3 (W1 − W2) / (W1 + W2). The power factor is pf = cos φ. These equations use lagging φ as positive; for a leading load, φ is negative and the signs of Q and W1−W2 reverse.

Reading the meters

Follow these steps for a measurement.

  1. Check that the load is three-wire. A four-wire system with neutral needs three wattmeters or a different method.
  2. Insert the current coils in any two lines. Connect each voltage coil from its own line to the third line.
  3. Read W1 and W2 with their signs. The algebraic sum is P.
  4. If one reading is negative, swap the two terminals of that meter's voltage coil. Read the magnitude, then restore the negative sign in your calculation. The true reading is the negative of the displayed value.
  5. For a balanced load, compute tan φ, then φ, then pf = cos φ. Compute Q = √3(W1−W2) in VAR.

At φ = 60°, cos(30° + 60°) = cos 90° = 0, so W2 reads zero and pf = 0.5. Below 0.5 pf, W2 is negative. A negative reading does not mean the load is capacitive; it only says the power factor is below 0.5, lagging or leading. For a four-wire system, use three wattmeters, one per phase. A single wattmeter can also be switched to take both readings, but it is slower.

Worked example

Question. Two wattmeters in a balanced three-phase circuit read W1 = 5.5 kW and W2 = 2.5 kW. Find the total power, the power factor and the reactive power. Assume the load is lagging.

  1. Total real power: P = W1 + W2 = 5.5 + 2.5 = 8.0 kW.
  2. Difference: W1 − W2 = 5.5 − 2.5 = 3.0 kW.
  3. tan φ = √3(W1 − W2)/(W1 + W2) = 1.732 × 3.0/8.0 = 0.6495.
  4. φ = arctan(0.6495) = 33.0°.
  5. pf = cos 33.0° = 0.839 lagging.
  6. Reactive power: Q = √3(W1 − W2) = 1.732 × 3.0 = 5.20 kVAR.
  7. Check: S = P/pf = 8.0/0.839 = 9.54 kVA, and √(9.54² − 8.0²) = 5.20 kVAR.

Answer. P = 8.0 kW, pf = 0.839 lagging, Q = 5.20 kVAR.

Exam traps

  • Using phase voltage Vp instead of line voltage VL in W1 = VL IL cos(30° − φ). The meter sees line-to-line voltage.
  • Dropping the √3 in tan φ = √3(W1 − W2)/(W1 + W2).
  • Treating a negative reading as proof of a capacitive load. It only says the power factor is below 0.5.
  • Adding the magnitudes when one reading is negative. Use the algebraic sum; a swapped voltage coil reverses the sign.
  • Connecting the voltage coils to neutral in a three-wire system. The common point must be the third line.

Check yourself

Remember In the two-wattmeter method, where are the current coils and voltage coils connected in a three-wire system? 2 marks

Understand Why must the voltage coils be connected to the third line and not to neutral in a three-wire system? 3 marks

A three-wire system has no neutral. The third line is the only common reference available, and the method is built on line-to-line voltages.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. In substations, the two-wattmeter idea appears inside panel meters and power-quality analysers that compute P, Q and pf from two voltage and two current channels. 3.8 Non-sinusoidal waveforms: Fourier series, RMS value, THD

Real voltages are rarely pure sines. A rectifier, a triac dimmer or a phone charger chops the mains sine and leaves a periodic wave of a different shape. Fourier's theorem says any periodic wave of period T is a DC term plus sines and cosines at whole multiples of the fundamental ω = 2π/T. Each harmonic is then its own AC problem, solved with phasors at its own frequency, and the results combine by root-sum-square. Only a few universities teach this chapter; the rest stop at single-frequency AC.

You need: 3.1 for RMS, 3.2 for phasors, 3.5 for average power.

The Fourier series of a periodic wave

Let v(t) be periodic with period T and fundamental ω = 2π/T rad/s. Fourier's theorem writes it as a DC term plus harmonics at nω:

v(t) = a<sub>0</sub>/2 + Σ<sub>n=1</sub><sup>∞</sup> (a<sub>n</sub> cos nωt + b<sub>n</sub> sin nωt)

a0/2 is the DC (average) value in V; an and bn are the cosine and sine coefficients in V; n is the harmonic order; nω is the nth harmonic frequency in rad/s.

The coefficients come from one period of the wave:

a<sub>n</sub> = (2/T)∫<sub>0</sub><sup>T</sup> v(t) cos nωt dt,  b<sub>n</sub> = (2/T)∫<sub>0</sub><sup>T</sup> v(t) sin nωt dt

T is the period in s; the integral runs over any full period.

Amplitude–phase form packs each harmonic into one cosine:

v(t) = c<sub>0</sub> + Σ c<sub>n</sub> cos(nωt + φ<sub>n</sub>)

c0 = a0/2 in V; cn = √(an² + bn²) is the peak of harmonic n in V; φn = −tan−1(bn/an) in rad.

  • Even wave, v(−t) = v(t): every bn = 0, so cosine terms only.
  • Odd wave, v(−t) = −v(t): every an = 0, so sine terms only.
  • Half-wave symmetry, v(t + T/2) = −v(t): a0 = 0 and all even harmonics vanish; only odd n survive.

A square wave has half-wave symmetry, so it holds the 1st, 3rd, 5th … harmonics only, with peaks falling as 1/n.

RMS, average power and THD

Square v(t) and average over one period. Every cross term 2VmVn cos(mωt) cos(nωt) with m ≠ n averages to zero. So harmonics add in power, not in amplitude:

V<sub>rms</sub> = √(V<sub>0</sub>² + V<sub>1,rms</sub>² + V<sub>2,rms</sub>² + ⋯)

V0 is the DC value in V; Vn,rms = cn/√2 is the RMS of harmonic n in V.

Average power follows the same rule — only voltage and current of the same frequency make a nonzero average:

P = V<sub>0</sub>I<sub>0</sub> + Σ<sub>n=1</sub><sup>∞</sup> V<sub>n,rms</sub> I<sub>n,rms</sub> cos φ<sub>n</sub>

P is in W; φn is the phase between the nth-harmonic voltage and current in rad.

Form factor F = Vrms/Vavg, where Vavg is the average of |v(t)| over a period. Total harmonic distortion compares the harmonics with the fundamental:

THD = √(V<sub>2,rms</sub>² + V<sub>3,rms</sub>² + ⋯) / V<sub>1,rms</sub>

THD is a pure ratio, quoted as a fraction or a percentage; the denominator is the fundamental RMS only, never the total RMS. With no DC, THD = √(Vrms² − V1,rms²)/V1,rms.

Filters decide which harmonics survive: a filter that attenuates a harmonic also removes the power and the distortion that harmonic carried.

Worked example

Question. A voltage v(t) = 100 sin(314t) + 30 sin(942t + 30°) V is applied to a circuit. Find its RMS value and its THD.

  1. Match with v(t) = V0 + V1 sin(ω1t + θ1) + V3 sin(3ω1t + θ3). Here V0 = 0, V1 = 100 V, ω1 = 314 rad/s, V3 = 30 V, and 942 = 3 × 314, so the second term is the third harmonic.
  2. Fundamental RMS = 100/√2 = 70.71 V.
  3. Third-harmonic RMS = 30/√2 = 21.21 V.
  4. Total RMS = √(70.71² + 21.21²) = √(5000 + 450) = √5450 = 73.82 V.
  5. THD = 21.21/70.71 = 0.3000, that is 30.0 %.

Answer. Vrms = 73.8 V and THD = 0.30 (30 %).

Exam traps

  • Adding harmonic RMS values directly: 70.7 + 21.2 = 91.9 V is wrong, because RMS combines as the root of the sum of squares.
  • Expecting a third-harmonic current to produce average power with the fundamental voltage. sin(ωt)·sin(3ωt) averages to zero over a period.
  • Leaving the DC term out of the RMS: Vrms² contains V0², not harmonics alone.
  • Putting total RMS in the THD denominator. THD is always referred to the fundamental RMS.
  • Assuming half-wave symmetry keeps even harmonics. It removes them, and the DC, leaving odd orders only.

Check yourself

Remember State the trigonometric Fourier series of a periodic voltage. 2 marks

Understand Why do harmonic RMS values combine by root-sum-square rather than by simple addition? 3 marks

The square of the sum contains cross terms 2VₘVₙ cos(mωt)cos(nωt) with m ≠ n; each averages to zero over a period, so only the squared terms survive.

Apply v(t) = 50 + 40 sin(ωt) + 12 sin(3ωt) V. Find Vrms. 4 marks

Vrms = √(50² + 40²/2 + 12²/2) = √(2500 + 800 + 72) = √3372 = 58.07 V.

Apply A distorted voltage has Vrms = 230 V and fundamental RMS = 225 V. Find the THD. 4 marks

Harmonic RMS = √(230² − 225²) = √2275 = 47.70 V; THD = 47.70/225 = 0.212, that is 21.2 %.

Analyze A wave has half-wave symmetry and no DC. Which harmonics can it contain, and why? 5 marks

Only odd harmonics. Shifting by T/2 negates the wave, which is exactly what odd harmonics do; even harmonics repeat after T/2 and would not be negated.

Understand An even periodic wave has only cosine terms. What does that say about the wave at t = 0? 3 marks

Every cosine peaks at t = 0, so the wave sits at a maximum or minimum there; it is mirror-symmetric about t = 0.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Power-quality standards limit voltage THD at the point of supply, and metering instruments compute THD from a sampled waveform rather than from coefficients. Switch-mode converters are the usual source of odd harmonics in Indian distribution feeders, and passive or active filters are chosen to remove them. Takeaways

  1. A sinusoid is fully described by three numbers — peak, frequency and phase — and for a pure sine the heating value is the peak divided by √2.
  2. At one fixed frequency a phasor replaces the whole time function, so adding sinusoids becomes adding complex numbers.
  3. Impedance Z = R + jX lets every DC rule (series, parallel, mesh, nodal, Thevenin) work unchanged, provided you keep the j.
  4. In an AC steady-state solution the answer is the particular solution; the transient has already died out.
  5. Complex power S = P + jQ separates the useful watts from the reactive vars, and the power factor is the cosine of the angle between them.
  6. A balanced three-phase supply delivers constant total power at every instant and cancels in the neutral, so one phase can be solved alone.
  7. Two wattmeters in any two lines give both total power and the power-factor angle, and they work for balanced and unbalanced loads alike.
  8. A non-sinusoidal periodic wave breaks into harmonics, and each harmonic is solved as its own AC problem before the RMS values combine root-sum-square.

Exam question bank

Long answers are expected to set up the circuit in phasor or complex form, show the algebra with units, and then interpret the result physically — power factor, phase angle, wattmeter readings or harmonic content — rather than stopping at a number.

Remember and understand

Understand Define peak value, RMS value, average value and form factor for a periodic waveform. Derive the RMS and half-cycle average values of a pure sine wave and hence show that its form factor is 1.11. 10 marks

State the definition of RMS as the square root of the mean of the square over one period; Square the sine, use sin²θ = (1 − cos 2θ)/2, integrate over one period, divide by the period; Result V_rms = V_m/√2 = 0.707 V_m; Half-cycle average = (2/π)V_m = 0.637 V_m; full-cycle average of a sine is zero; Form factor k = 0.707/0.637 = 1.11; note that k > 1 for every waveform except a pure DC.

Understand Explain the phasor concept. Why can a sinusoid be represented by a complex number only when the frequency is fixed? Draw the phasor diagrams for a pure resistor, a pure inductor and a pure capacitor. 10 marks

A phasor is a rotating vector frozen at t = 0; its length is the RMS (or peak) value and its angle is the phase; The e^(jωt) factor is common to every signal in the circuit, so it can be dropped and restored at the end; Different frequencies would rotate at different rates, so a single frozen snapshot would be meaningless; Resistor: V and I in phase. Inductor: I lags V by 90°. Capacitor: I leads V by 90°; State the corresponding impedances R, jωL and 1/(jωC).

Understand Define real power, reactive power, apparent power and complex power. Draw the power triangle and explain the meaning of the power factor. 10 marks

Understand For a balanced star-connected three-phase supply, derive the relations between line and phase voltages and between line and phase currents. Show that the neutral current is zero for a balanced load. 12 marks

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Show that the two-wattmeter method gives the total power and the power-factor angle of a three-phase load whether the load is balanced or unbalanced. Derive tan φ = √3(W₁ − W₂)/(W₁ + W₂) for the balanced case and explain a negative reading. 14 marks

Evaluate Compare phasor steady-state analysis with the Laplace transform method for an RL circuit switched onto an AC source. When does the transient matter and when can it be ignored? 13 marks

The reciprocal of impedance, Y = G + jB, measured in siemens.

RMS value divided by average value; 1.11 for a pure sine.

A sinusoidal component whose frequency is an integer multiple of the fundamental.
Impedance
The complex ratio V/I, written R + jX, measured in ohms.

The voltage measured between any two lines of a three-phase system.
Neutral
The fourth conductor of a star system that carries the unbalanced current back to the source.
Period
The time T = 1/f for one complete cycle, in seconds.
Phase angle
The angle φ that fixes where the waveform starts at t = 0, in degrees or radians.
Phase sequence
The order in which the three phase voltages of a supply reach their maximum values.
Phasor
A complex number representing the amplitude and phase of a sinusoid at a fixed frequency.
Power factor
The cosine of the angle between voltage and current; also real power divided by apparent power.
Power triangle
A right triangle with P, Q and S as its sides, showing how the three powers relate.
Reactance
The imaginary part of impedance; ωL for an inductor, −1/(ωC) for a capacitor.
Reactive power
The power that oscillates between source and reactance with zero net transfer, Q = VI sin φ, in var.

The average power actually converted to work or heat, P = VI cos φ, in watts.

The condition ωL = 1/(ωC) at which the net reactance is zero and current is limited only by resistance.
