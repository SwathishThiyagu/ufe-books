5.1 Sampling theorem and reconstruction of signals

A continuous signal can be replaced by a list of numbers if it has no frequency above f_max and we sample faster than twice f_max. The samples keep all the information, and a low-pass filter turns them back into a smooth waveform. This is the bridge from continuous-time to discrete-time processing: phone audio, ECG recorders, digital control and payment logs all store finite samples instead of continuous voltage. Sampling too slowly loses information, a failure studied in 5.2.

You need: Fourier transform, sinc spectrum and ideal filters (see Unit 3, Fourier Series and Fourier Transform); impulse and standard signals (see Unit 1, Classification of Signals and Systems).

The sampling theorem and its numbers

A signal is band-limited when its Fourier transform is zero above fmax, the highest frequency in hertz. Sample it at instants nTs, where n = 0, 1, 2, ... and Ts is the sampling interval in seconds. The sampling rate is fs = 1/Ts samples per second, also called hertz. The sampling theorem says: if fs > 2 fmax, the samples x(nTs) determine x(t) exactly. The number 2 fmax is the Nyquist rate. The number fs/2 is the Nyquist frequency, the highest frequency a sampler at fs can represent. The inequality is strict. At fs = 2 fmax, a sinusoid sitting exactly at fmax is sampled at the same phase every period, so its amplitude and phase cannot be recovered. Some syllabi call the result the Nyquist–Shannon or Whittaker–Shannon theorem.

f<sub>s</sub> = 1/T<sub>s</sub> > 2 f<sub>max</sub>

fs is the sampling rate in Hz; Ts is the sampling interval in seconds; fmax is the highest frequency in the signal in Hz; 2 fmax is the Nyquist rate in Hz; fs/2 is the Nyquist frequency in Hz.

Impulse-train model and the repeated spectrum

Multiply x(t) by an impulse train p(t) = Σ δ(t − nTs). The product is the sampled signal. Each impulse carries one sample value as its area. In frequency, the impulse train has lines at multiples of fs. Multiplication in time becomes convolution in frequency, so the spectrum of the samples is a sum of shifted copies of X(f).

x<sub>s</sub>(t) = x(t) p(t) = Σ x(nT<sub>s</sub>) δ(t − nT<sub>s</sub>)

xs(t) is the sampled signal; x(nTs) is the sample value at time nTs; δ(t − nTs) is a unit impulse at t = nTs; the sum runs over all integers n.

X<sub>s</sub>(f) = (1/T<sub>s</sub>) Σ X(f − k f<sub>s</sub>)

Xs(f) is the spectrum of the samples; X(f) is the original spectrum; fs is the sampling rate in Hz; Ts is the sampling interval in seconds; k runs over all integers.

The original spectrum X(f) is copied at k fs = 0, ±fs, ±2fs, ... and every copy is scaled by 1/Ts. Sketch: draw X(f) from −fmax to fmax, then repeat it every fs hertz. If fs > 2 fmax, the copies do not overlap. The empty gap between the baseband copy and its neighbours is the guard band. If copies overlap, high frequencies fold down as aliasing; see 5.2.

Ideal reconstruction and sinc interpolation

An ideal low-pass filter with cutoff fs/2 and gain Ts selects the baseband copy and removes all other copies. The gain Ts cancels the 1/Ts scaling in Xs(f), so the output has the original amplitude. In time, the same operation is ideal interpolation: each sample is multiplied by a sinc pulse centred at its sampling instant, and the pulses add to rebuild the smooth signal.

H(f) = T<sub>s</sub> for |f| < f<sub>s</sub>/2, and H(f) = 0 outside

H(f) is the reconstruction filter frequency response; Ts is the sampling interval in seconds; fs/2 is the cutoff frequency in Hz.

x(t) = Σ x(nT<sub>s</sub>) sinc((t − nT<sub>s</sub>)/T<sub>s</sub>)

sinc(u) = sin(πu)/(πu) and sinc(0) = 1; x(nTs) are the samples; Ts is the sampling interval in seconds; the sum runs over all integers n.

This is an ideal formula. Real filters have a transition band, and a sample-and-hold circuit attenuates high frequencies. The band-pass sampling case is in 5.3.

Worked example

Question. A voltage x(t) = 3 cos(2π·200t) + 2 cos(2π·450t) volts is sampled at f_s = 1000 Hz. Find f_max, the Nyquist rate, T_s, and the ideal reconstruction filter cutoff. Check whether the spectral copies overlap and state the guard band.

  1. Each cosine has frequency equal to the number multiplying t inside 2π(...). The two frequencies are 200 Hz and 450 Hz, so f_max = 450 Hz.
  2. Nyquist rate = 2 f_max = 2 × 450 Hz = 900 Hz. The actual rate is 1000 Hz, and 1000 Hz > 900 Hz, so the strict sampling condition is met.
  3. T_s = 1/f_s = 1/1000 Hz = 0.001 s = 1 ms.
  4. The reconstruction filter cutoff is f_s/2 = 1000/2 = 500 Hz. Its passband gain must be T_s.
  5. Sampled-spectrum copies are centred at k f_s = 0, ±1000, ±2000, ... Hz. The baseband copy has components at 200 Hz and 450 Hz. The closest component from the upper copy is 1000 − 450 = 550 Hz.
  6. The gap is 550 Hz − 450 Hz = 100 Hz. No copy overlaps the baseband, so ideal reconstruction returns x(t).

Answer. f_max = 450 Hz, Nyquist rate = 900 Hz, T_s = 0.001 s (1 ms), filter cutoff = 500 Hz, lowest upper-copy component = 550 Hz, and guard band = 100 Hz; sampling at 1000 Hz is safe.

Exam traps

  • Nyquist rate is 2 f_max in Hz; Nyquist frequency is f_s/2 in Hz. They are not the same number.
  • Do not drop the T_s gain of the reconstruction filter; without it the rebuilt signal is scaled by 1/T_s.
  • f_s = 2 f_max is not safe for a sinusoid at f_max; use f_s > 2 f_max.
  • Keep units straight: T_s in seconds, f_s in hertz, and 1 ms = 0.001 s.
  • Examiners often ask for the guard band: lowest upper-copy frequency minus f_max.

5.2 Aliasing and anti-aliasing filters

Sampling a signal makes copies of its spectrum every f_s hertz. If the signal has energy above f_s/2, neighbouring copies overlap and a high-frequency component appears at a low frequency. That false low frequency is an alias, and no digital processing after sampling can tell it from a real low tone. The cure is an analog anti-aliasing filter placed before the sampler, with cutoff at or below f_s/2. Every audio codec, ADC card and software-defined radio uses this filter, because once an alias is sampled it stays in the data.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for the sampling theorem and spectrum copies, and Unit 3 for the ideal low-pass response.

Spectral copies overlap

A sampler multiplies x(t) by an impulse train of period T_s = 1/f_s. In frequency, this repeats the spectrum X(f) at multiples of f_s.

X_s(f) = f_s Σ_{k=−∞}^{∞} X(f − k f_s)

X_s(f) is the spectrum of the sampled signal; X(f) is the spectrum of x(t); f_s is the sampling rate in Hz; k is any integer.

When X(f)=0 above f_s/2, the copies do not overlap. A low-pass filter with cutoff f_s/2 rebuilds the signal, as in Chapter 5.1.

When a component sits at f_max > f_s/2, the copies overlap. For f_s/2 < f_max < f_s, the copy centred at f_s reaches down to f_s − f_max inside the baseband, and that copy adds to the baseband spectrum. For f_max ≥ f_s, the alias comes from a different copy; take the nearest multiple of f_s, as the folding rule below does. The condition f_s < 2 f_max is exactly when this overlap starts. The overlap is aliasing; some syllabi call it fold-over. It is a permanent mixing, not a rounding error.

The folding rule

After sampling, every frequency f appears as the frequency nearest to zero among |f − k f_s|, for integer k. That is the folding rule.

Original f (Hz)Nearest k f_s (Hz)Observed alias (Hz)
1000100
3000300
7001000300
9001000100
13001000300
17002000300
25002000500

Take f_s=1000 Hz. A 700 Hz tone is above f_s/2=500 Hz. Its nearest multiple is 1000 Hz, so the alias is |700−1000|=300 Hz. The identity below shows why.

cos(2π·700 n/1000) = cos(2π n − 2π·300 n/1000) = cos(2π·300 n/1000)

n is the sample index; the sample times are t = n/1000 s; the two continuous-time cosines cos(2π·700t) and cos(2π·300t) give these same samples.

So cos(2π·700t) and cos(2π·300t) give identical samples at 1000 Hz. After sampling they are indistinguishable. A 1300 Hz tone also folds to 300 Hz, because |1300−1000|=300 Hz.

The anti-aliasing filter

An anti-aliasing filter is an analog low-pass filter placed before the sampler. It removes components above f_s/2 before they can fold. Some syllabi call it a prefilter.

Choose cutoff f_c with f_max ≤ f_c ≤ f_s/2. A cutoff f_c = 2 f_max is wrong only when it exceeds f_s/2; the hard constraint is f_c ≤ f_s/2. If 2 f_max ≤ f_s/2, that cutoff is allowed but not necessarily a good design. The transition band runs from f_max to f_s/2, so its width is Δf = f_s/2 − f_max.

|H(f)|² = 1 / (1 + (f/f_c)^(2N))

|H(f)| is the magnitude response of an N-th order Butterworth low-pass filter; f_c is the cutoff in Hz; N is the filter order.

The order N follows from the stopband attenuation A in dB and Δf. Solve for N from the attenuation at the stopband edge. Large A and small Δf need high N.

No digital filter after the sampler can remove an alias. The sampler has already merged the two frequencies, and the digital filter sees only the merged value. Sampling faster after an alias is equally useless; the alias is already in the samples.

Worked example

Question. A speech signal occupies 300 Hz to 3400 Hz and is sampled at 8000 Hz. Is it safe from aliasing, and what guard band remains? A tone cos(2π·700t) volts is then sampled at 1000 Hz. Find the frequency of the reconstructed tone. Which other tone gives the same samples?

  1. For the speech, f_max = 3400 Hz. The Nyquist rate is 2 f_max = 6800 Hz. The given f_s = 8000 Hz is greater than 6800 Hz, so the speech does not alias.
  2. The Nyquist frequency is f_s/2 = 8000/2 = 4000 Hz. The guard band is 4000 − 3400 = 600 Hz.
  3. For the tone, f_s = 1000 Hz, so f_s/2 = 500 Hz. The tone frequency 700 Hz is greater than 500 Hz, so it aliases.
  4. Fold 700 Hz to the nearest multiple of 1000 Hz. |700 − 1000| = 300 Hz. The reconstructed tone is cos(2π·300t) volts with the same amplitude, 1 V.
  5. A tone at 1300 Hz gives |1300 − 1000| = 300 Hz, so it produces the same samples as the 700 Hz tone.

Answer. At f_s = 8000 Hz, f_s/2 = 4000 Hz; the speech band is safe with a 600 Hz guard band; the 700 Hz tone is reconstructed as cos(2π·300t) volts, and a 1300 Hz tone gives the same samples.

Exam traps

  • Saying the anti-aliasing cutoff must be 2 f_max; the cutoff is at most f_s/2.
  • Placing the anti-aliasing filter after the sampler; it is then a digital filter and the alias is already present.
  • Thinking a faster sampler removes an alias that is already in the samples; the overlap has already happened.
  • Forgetting that for a single isolated tone the alias keeps the amplitude; for a general signal, overlapping aliases add complexly and can change the amplitude.
  • Measuring the transition width to f_c instead of the stopband edge; for a filter whose stopband edge is f_s/2 the transition width equals the guard band f_s/2 − f_max, and a lower stopband edge changes it.

5.3 Low-pass, band-pass and practical sampling

Low-pass sampling needs a clock faster than twice the highest frequency. A signal that occupies only f_L to f_H, with nothing near zero, is different: the empty band from 0 to f_L gives the shifted copies room, so the clock can be far below 2 f_H. Real samplers also hold each sample for a short time. That hold multiplies the spectrum by a sinc, which droops at high frequencies. Band-pass sampling is used in radio receivers and intermediate-frequency digitisation; the hold correction is used in every practical digitiser.

You need: the low-pass theorem and folding picture from 5.1 and 5.2, plus sinc spectra and band-pass filters from Unit 3.

Band-pass sampling: use the empty gaps

Let a real signal have energy only from f_L to f_H. f_L is the lowest frequency in Hz, f_H is the highest frequency in Hz, and the bandwidth is B = f_H - f_L. If no component lies below f_L, the spectrum from 0 to f_L is empty. Sampling repeats this spectrum every f_s Hz. A shifted copy can use that empty space without touching the original.

The low-pass rule f_s > 2 f_max would demand f_s > 2 f_H. The band-pass rule removes that waste. Choose an integer m with 1 ≤ m ≤ floor(f_H/B), and set f_s = 2 f_H/m. For that m, the shifted band must fit inside 0 to f_s/2. Two inequalities test this: f_H ≤ m f_s/2 and f_L ≥ (m - 1) f_s/2. If both hold, no copy overlaps another. The largest m that passes both tests gives the lowest rate.

f_s = 2 f_H / m, 1 ≤ m ≤ floor(f_H / B)

f_H is the upper band edge in Hz; B = f_H - f_L is the bandwidth in Hz; m is an integer; f_s is the sampling rate in samples per second.

At equality in the two checks, adjacent copies touch at 0 or f_s/2. That is mathematically clean but leaves no guard band for real filters. In practice pick a slightly higher f_s or allow a wider filter transition. After sampling, a band-pass filter selects the copy that landed near zero. The original high-frequency band is not lost; it has been translated down by a multiple of f_s.

Practical sampling: the aperture effect

An ideal sampler takes an impulse of zero width. A real circuit holds the sample for τ seconds. τ is the hold time in seconds, often equal to the clock period T_s = 1/f_s. The held pulse is a rectangle of width τ. The held sample train is the impulse train convolved with that rectangle. In frequency this multiplies the spectrum by H(f) = τ sinc(f τ) in magnitude; the phase delay is omitted. Multiplication in time by a window would instead convolve the spectrum.

H(f) = τ sinc(f τ) = τ · sin(π f τ)/(π f τ)

H(f) is the aperture frequency response; τ is the hold time in seconds; f is frequency in Hz; sinc(x) = sin(πx)/(πx).

The scale factor τ is constant. The normalized magnitude is |sinc(f τ)|. It is 1 at 0 Hz, first reaches zero at f = 1/τ, and droops in between. For a full-period hold τ = T_s, the gain at f_s/2 is sinc(1/2) = 2/π ≈ 0.637, or about -3.9 dB. That is a real amplitude error at the top of the band. An inverse-sinc filter after the sampler boosts high frequencies by 1/|sinc(f τ)| to flatten the response. It also boosts high-frequency noise, so the correction is limited to the signal band.

The zero-order hold is also the first step of practical interpolation. Hold each sample, then pass the stair-step waveform through a smoothing low-pass filter. The filter removes the copies above f_s/2 and leaves the reconstructed signal. The same hold that makes the stair step causes the sinc droop. The figure uses a 4 kHz clock with τ = 0.25 ms, so the zero is at 4 kHz; at 2 kHz the gain is 0.637. Quantization of the held samples is covered in 5.4.

Worked example

Question. A signal has components only between 5 kHz and 6 kHz. Find a valid band-pass sampling rate well below the low-pass requirement of 12 kHz, and check that the copies do not overlap.

  1. The band edges are f_L = 5 kHz and f_H = 6 kHz. The bandwidth is B = f_H - f_L = 1 kHz.
  2. A low-pass sampler would need f_s > 2 f_H = 12 kHz. The band-pass formula can go much lower.
  3. Find the largest integer m ≤ floor(f_H/B) = floor(6/1) = 6. Take m = 6.
  4. Compute f_s = 2 f_H/m = 12/6 = 2 kHz.
  5. Check the upper edge: f_H ≤ m f_s/2 gives 6 ≤ 6 × 2/2 = 6 kHz. True, with equality.
  6. Check the lower edge: f_L ≥ (m - 1) f_s/2 gives 5 ≥ 5 × 2/2 = 5 kHz. True, with equality.
  7. The band lands in 0 to f_s/2 = 0 to 1 kHz. One copy is the original 5-6 kHz band shifted by -3f_s = -6 kHz, giving -1 kHz to 0 kHz; its absolute frequency is 0 to 1 kHz. No copy overlaps another.
  8. A low-pass filter after the sampler, with cutoff 1 kHz, selects the 0-1 kHz image and recovers the signal.

Answer. The valid band-pass sampling rate is f_s = 2 kHz, with B = 1 kHz, m = 6, upper check 6 kHz and lower check 5 kHz.

Exam traps

  • Using f_s > 2 f_H on a band-pass signal and rejecting a perfectly valid low rate. The low-pass formula is not the band-pass formula.
  • Taking f_s = 2B and stopping there. You must test f_H ≤ m f_s/2 and f_L ≥ (m - 1) f_s/2; otherwise copies overlap.
  • Forgetting aperture droop. A full-period hold has only 0.637 gain at f_s/2, about -3.9 dB, so the band edge is attenuated.
  • Confusing hold time τ with sampling period T_s. The first zero is at 1/τ, which is 1/T_s only for a full-period hold.
  • Ignoring guard band at equality. Mathematical equality works, but real anti-alias and reconstruction filters need transition space.

5.4 Quantization of sampled signals

An analog sample can take any value. A real ADC stores each sample in a fixed number of bits, so it must round the sample to one of a finite set of levels. That rounding is quantization. The difference between the true sample and the rounded value is quantization error, modelled as added noise. The model connects bit count to accuracy: it predicts audio noise, sensor resolution and the dynamic range of digital systems.

You need: 5.1 to 5.3 for where the samples come from, and Unit 1 for signal power and average value.

Levels and step size

Uniform quantization divides the full input range x_min to x_max into L equal steps, with L = 2ⁿ for an n-bit quantizer. The step size is Δ = (x_max − x_min)/L. Δ is also called the resolution: the smallest output change the quantizer can represent. A mid-rise quantizer puts its L levels at x_min + (k + ½)Δ for k = 0 to L − 1. For a 0 V to 8 V, 3-bit quantizer, L = 8 and Δ = 1 V, so the levels sit at 0.5 V, 1.5 V, …, 7.5 V. A mid-tread quantizer instead places a level at zero. Only a few universities teach these quantizer types; most exams ask for Δ, L, one rounded output and the SQNR formula.

Δ = (x_max − x_min)/L

x_min and x_max are the input range limits in V; L = 2ⁿ is the number of levels; n is the word length in bits; Δ is the step size in V.

Error, SQNR and overload

Quantization error is e_q = Q(x) − x, where Q(x) is the chosen output level. For a mid-rise quantizer, if x stays inside the range, |e_q| ≤ Δ/2. The error is deterministic for one sample, but for a busy signal it is modelled as uniform random noise on −Δ/2 to +Δ/2, with zero mean and power Δ²/12. A full-scale sinusoid has amplitude A = (x_max − x_min)/2 and power A²/2. Dividing by the noise power gives SQNR = 6.02n + 1.76 dB when L = 2ⁿ and the sine fills the range. Each extra bit adds about 6 dB. Inputs outside the range cause overload or clipping error, which can exceed Δ/2; the noise model fails. Companding with μ-law or A-law compresses large amplitudes before quantization and expands after, giving small signals finer relative resolution. Oversampling by M reduces in-band quantization noise by about 10 log₁₀ M dB, or 3 dB per doubling, when the noise is white.

SQNR = 6.02n + 1.76 dB

n is the number of bits; SQNR is the signal-to-quantization-noise ratio for a full-scale sinusoid with uniform quantization error. For another sine amplitude A, add 20 log₁₀(A/A_fs) dB, where A_fs = (x_max − x_min)/2.

Worked example

Question. A 3-bit uniform mid-rise quantizer covers 0 V to 8 V. Find L, the step size Δ, the maximum quantization error, the output for an input sample of 3.4 V, the error, and the SQNR for a full-scale sinusoid.

  1. L = 2³ = 8 levels.
  2. Δ = (8 − 0)/8 = 1 V; resolution is 1 V.
  3. Maximum error = Δ/2 = 0.5 V.
  4. Mid-rise levels are 0.5, 1.5, …, 7.5 V. For x = 3.4 V, the distances are 0.9 V to 2.5 V and 0.1 V to 3.5 V; output = 3.5 V and error = +0.1 V.
  5. Full-scale sine amplitude A = 4 V; signal power = A²/2 = 8 V².
  6. Noise power = Δ²/12 = 1/12 ≈ 0.0833 V².
  7. SQNR = 8/(1/12) = 96; 10 log₁₀ 96 = 19.82 dB. Formula: 6.02 × 3 + 1.76 = 19.82 dB.

Answer. L = 8 levels, Δ = 1 V, maximum error = 0.5 V, output = 3.5 V for 3.4 V input with error +0.1 V, and SQNR = 19.82 dB.

Exam traps

  • Using Δ as the maximum error; the bound is ±Δ/2.
  • Writing L = 2ⁿ − 1; that is the largest code value, not the number of levels.
  • Applying 6.02n + 1.76 dB to a small signal or a non-sinusoid; it is for a full-scale sinusoid with uniform error.
  • Ignoring overload: inputs outside x_min to x_max clip, and the Δ²/12 noise formula no longer applies.
  • Confusing resolution (Δ, in volts) with word length (n bits).

5.5 Discrete-time LTI systems and convolution sum

A discrete-time LTI system is a rule that turns one sequence into another. Linearity plus time invariance lets one short sequence describe it completely: the impulse response h[n]. The output is then the convolution sum of input x[n] and h[n]. This is the discrete twin of the convolution integral. Phones, audio filters, ECG smoothing and fraud filters all run such sample-by-sample rules.

You need Chapter 5.1 for how x[n] arises, Unit 1 for δ[n], u[n] and system properties, and Unit 2 for the convolution integral that this chapter mirrors.

Sequences, impulse and step

A discrete-time signal is a sequence x[n]. Here n is an integer sample index, not a continuous time. A periodic sequence has x[n + N] = x[n] for every n; the smallest positive N is its period.

The unit impulse is δ[n] = 1 at n = 0 and δ[n] = 0 elsewhere. The unit step is u[n] = 1 for n ≥ 0 and u[n] = 0 for n < 0. They relate by δ[n] = u[n] − u[n − 1]. A shifted impulse δ[n − m] is nonzero only at n = m.

The impulse response h[n] is the output of an LTI system when the input is δ[n], with the system at rest. Because the system is linear and time-invariant, an input x[n] = Σk x[k] δ[n − k] produces a sum of shifted, scaled copies of h[n]. That sum is the convolution sum.

The convolution sum and two ways to evaluate it

y[n] = Σ<sub>k=−∞</sub><sup>∞</sup> x[k] h[n − k] = Σ<sub>k=−∞</sub><sup>∞</sup> h[k] x[n − k]

y[n] is the output sample at index n; x[k] is the input sample at index k; h[n − k] is the impulse response reversed and shifted by n; k is the dummy summation index.

For finite sequences, keep only overlapping nonzero terms. If x[n] has length N₁ and h[n] has length N₂, then y[n] has length N₁ + N₂ − 1.

  1. Sliding method: plot x[k] and h[n − k]. For n = 0, flip h[k] to h[−k], place it at k = 0, multiply overlapping samples and add.
  2. Increase n by 1. The flipped h[n − k] shifts right by one sample. Multiply the new overlap and add. Repeat until no overlap remains.
  3. Table method: for each output index n, list the products x[k] h[n − k] for all k, then add the row. The row sums are y[n].

Properties, causality, stability and frequency response

  • Commutative: x[n] * h[n] = h[n] * x[n].
  • Associative: (x * h₁) * h₂ = x * (h₁ * h₂).
  • Distributive: x * (h₁ + h₂) = x * h₁ + x * h₂.
  • Identity element: convolving with δ[n] returns the original sequence.

A causal discrete-time LTI system has h[n] = 0 for n < 0. Its output at n depends only on present and past input samples. A BIBO-stable system has Σ |h[n]| finite; every bounded input then gives a bounded output.

H(e<sup>jω</sup>) = Σ<sub>n=−∞</sub><sup>∞</sup> h[n] e<sup>−jωn</sup>

H(ejω) is the frequency response; ω is angular frequency in radians per sample; h[n] is the impulse response; j is the imaginary unit. If x[n] = ejωn, then y[n] = H(ejω) ejωn.

The step response is s[n] = Σk=−∞n h[k]. It is the running sum of the impulse response. The frequency response gives the complex gain at each ω; its magnitude is amplitude gain and its angle is phase shift.

Worked example

Question. For x[n] = [2, 1, 3] at n = 0, 1, 2 and h[n] = [1, 2] at n = 0, 1, use y[n] = Σ x[k]h[n − k] to find y[0] to y[3]. Then test h[n] = (0.5)nu[n] for BIBO stability.

  1. List nonzero samples: x[0] = 2, x[1] = 1, x[2] = 3; h[0] = 1, h[1] = 2.
  2. y[0] = x[0]h[0] = 2·1 = 2. Other k give no overlap.
  3. y[1] = x[0]h[1] + x[1]h[0] = 2·2 + 1·1 = 4 + 1 = 5.
  4. y[2] = x[1]h[1] + x[2]h[0] = 1·2 + 3·1 = 2 + 3 = 5.
  5. y[3] = x[2]h[1] = 3·2 = 6.
  6. Thus y[n] = [2, 5, 5, 6]. Its length is N₁ + N₂ − 1 = 3 + 2 − 1 = 4, which matches.
  7. For h[n] = (0.5)nu[n], Σ |h[n]| = 1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + … = 1/(1 − 0.5) = 2.
  8. The sum 2 is finite, so the system is BIBO stable.

Answer. y[n] = [2, 5, 5, 6] with length 4 samples, and the system with h[n] = (0.5)nu[n] is BIBO stable because Σ |h[n]| = 2 is finite.

Exam traps

  • Not flipping h[n] in the sliding method. You must use h[n − k], not h[k], while sliding.
  • Losing the first or last output sample. For finite sequences check the length N₁ + N₂ − 1.
  • Mixing indices n and k: n is the output sample being found, k is the summation index.
  • Testing only the first few terms of h[n] for stability. The whole sum Σ |h[n]| must be finite.
  • Confusing the impulse response h[n] with the frequency response H(ejω). One is a sequence in n; the other is a function of ω.

5.6 Linear constant-coefficient difference equations

A difference equation is the discrete-time twin of a differential equation. It ties the present output sample to a few past output samples and to present and past input samples, using fixed numbers as coefficients. Give it a handful of starting values and you can compute every later sample with a loop — which is exactly what a digital filter does inside a phone, a hearing aid or an ECG monitor. The roots of its characteristic equation decide whether the output settles or blows up. This chapter works entirely in the time domain.

You need: 5.5 for the convolution sum and impulse response, and Unit 1 for the unit step, unit impulse and signal shifting.

The equation, its order and its two cases

A discrete-time LTI system can be described by a linear constant-coefficient difference equation. Writing every output term on the left and every input term on the right gives the standard form:

y[n] = −Σ<sub>k=1…N</sub> a<sub>k</sub> y[n − k] + Σ<sub>m=0…M</sub> b<sub>m</sub> x[n − m]

y[n] is the output sample at index n; x[n] is the input sample; ak and bm are real constants, so the system is time-invariant; N is the order; M is the number of past input samples used.

The order N is the largest delay of the output in the equation. It is also the number of past outputs you must store and the number of unit delays in the hardware. The coefficients must be plain numbers: a term such as n·y[n−1] or y[n]·y[n−1] destroys linearity or time-invariance.

If at least one ak is nonzero, the output feeds back into itself. The system is recursive, and its impulse response lasts forever, so it is called IIR (infinite impulse response). If every ak = 0, then y[n] = Σ bm x[n − m], which is the convolution sum of 5.5 with h[n] = bn for 0 ≤ n ≤ M. That is a non-recursive, or FIR (finite impulse response), filter.

To start the recursion you need the N past outputs y[−1], y[−2], …, y[−N]. These are the initial conditions. Rest initial conditions means y[n] = 0 for all n < 0: the system was silent before the input arrived.

  1. Move each ak y[n − k] to the right side with its sign changed, so y[n] stands alone.
  2. Write down the initial conditions y[−1] … y[−N].
  3. Put n = 0, substitute the input sample and the initial conditions, and get y[0].
  4. Raise n by one, substitute the value just found, and repeat. Every step uses only numbers you already have.

Block diagram: adders, delays and multipliers

Three blocks build every such equation. An adder (Σ) sums its inputs. A multiplier scales a signal by a constant. A unit delay holds one sample: its output is y[n−1] when its input is y[n]. Textbooks label the delay z⁻¹, read as z inverse; the symbol comes from the Z-transform in Unit 6, but here it only means one sample of memory.

A direct-form diagram of order N uses N delays, one adder and up to N + M + 1 multipliers. The figure shows the first-order case y[n] = x[n] + 0.5y[n−1], so b₀ = 1, a₁ = −0.5 and the feedback path multiplies by −a₁ = 0.5. Each new output sample is delayed, scaled and added back to the input. A non-recursive FIR system has no feedback path at all: only a chain of delays and multipliers feeding one large adder.

Closed form: natural plus forced response

Iteration gives numbers, not a formula. The closed form has two parts: the natural response, the system's own behaviour with the input removed, and the forced response, its steady reaction to the input.

Natural response. Set the input to zero and try y[n] = λⁿ. Substituting and multiplying by λN gives the characteristic equation:

a₀λ<sup>N</sup> + a₁λ<sup>N−1</sup> + … + a<sub>N−1</sub>λ + a<sub>N</sub> = 0

λ are the natural modes; a₀ is the coefficient of y[n], normally 1; N is the order, so there are N roots counting repeats.

Each distinct root λi contributes a term Ciλin. That same λi is a pole of the system. A root repeated r times contributes (C₀ + C₁n + … + Cr−1nr−1)λⁿ.

Forced response. Guess a form like the input and substitute: a constant input gives a constant guess A, an input 2ⁿ gives A·2ⁿ, a sinusoid gives A cos(ωn) + B sin(ωn). Solve for the constants algebraically.

Total response: y[n] = ynat[n] + yforced[n] for n ≥ 0. Find the N unknown Ci from y[−1] … y[−N], or from y[0] … y[N−1] if you obtained those by iterating first. N conditions fix N constants.

Stability from the root positions

A causal system is BIBO stable if every bounded input gives a bounded output. For a difference equation that happens exactly when every natural mode decays:

|λi| < 1 for every root i ⟺ stable

λi are the characteristic roots, also called poles; |·| is the magnitude of a complex number; the statement is for a causal system, whose modes start at n = 0.

Root positionMode λⁿVerdict
|λ| < 1, insidedecays to 0stable
|λ| = 1, single rootfixed amplitude, may oscillatemarginally stable
|λ| = 1, repeated rootgrows like nunstable
|λ| > 1, outsidegrows like |λ|ⁿunstable

The unit circle is |z| = 1 in the z-plane: inside means stable, on means marginal, outside means unstable. A real negative root close to −1 gives an alternating slow decay — the ringing heard after a click in an audio filter. Complex roots on the circle give a pure oscillation at ω = angle of the root.

The Z-transform in Unit 6 gets the same answer faster: H(z) = B(z)/A(z), poles at the roots of A(z), and a table gives the inverse. The time-domain work here shows what the machine does sample by sample, and it is the method to use when only the first few outputs are wanted.

Worked example

Question. A causal discrete-time system is described by y[n] − 0.5y[n − 1] = x[n], with input x[n] = u[n] and rest initial conditions y[n] = 0 for n < 0. (a) Iterate the equation for n = 0 to 3. (b) Find the closed form y[n]. (c) State whether the system is stable.

  1. Rewrite in recursive form: y[n] = x[n] + 0.5y[n − 1]. Here a₁ = −0.5, so the feedback coefficient is −a₁ = 0.5.
  2. n = 0: y[0] = x[0] + 0.5y[−1] = 1 + 0.5(0) = 1.
  3. n = 1: y[1] = 1 + 0.5(1) = 1.5.
  4. n = 2: y[2] = 1 + 0.5(1.5) = 1.75.
  5. n = 3: y[3] = 1 + 0.5(1.75) = 1.875.
  6. Characteristic equation: λ − 0.5 = 0, so the single root is λ = 0.5. Natural response: C(0.5)ⁿ.
  7. Forced response: try the constant A. Then A − 0.5A = 1, giving 0.5A = 1 and A = 2. Check: 2 − 0.5(2) = 1, correct.
  8. Total: y[n] = 2 + C(0.5)ⁿ. Apply y[0] = 1: 2 + C = 1, so C = −1.
  9. Closed form: y[n] = 2 − (0.5)ⁿ for n ≥ 0. At n = 3 this gives 2 − 0.125 = 1.875, matching the iteration.
  10. The pole is at 0.5, and |0.5| < 1, so it lies inside the unit circle: the system is stable and y[n] settles at 2.

Exam traps

  • Sign slip: y[n] + a₁y[n−1] = x[n] becomes y[n] = −a₁y[n−1] + x[n]. The multiplier in the feedback path is −a₁, never a₁.
  • Wrong starting sample: begin from y[−1], not y[0]. Starting at y[0] shifts the entire output sequence by one place.
  • Characteristic equation for y[n] − 0.5y[n−1] = x[n] is λ − 0.5 = 0, so λ = 0.5. Writing 1 − 0.5λ = 0 gives λ = 2 and a false unstable answer.
  • Order versus input length: N counts output delays, M counts input delays. A second-order equation can carry any number of b coefficients.
  • A repeated root on the unit circle grows like n and is unstable. Only single roots on the circle are marginally stable — the examiner's favourite trap.

Takeaways

  1. Sampling above the Nyquist rate preserves a band-limited signal; sampling below it creates irreversible aliasing.
  2. An anti-aliasing filter must act before the sampler; digital filtering after sampling cannot remove aliases.
  3. Band-pass sampling can use a rate below twice the top frequency when the signal bandwidth is narrow.
  4. Sample-and-hold introduces sinc attenuation called the aperture effect, which can be compensated by inverse sinc filtering.
  5. Quantization adds noise with variance Δ²/12, and each extra bit lowers quantization noise by about 6 dB.
  6. A discrete-time LTI system is fully specified by h[n], and its output is the convolution sum x[n]*h[n].
  7. Difference equations compute outputs recursively, and pole positions inside the unit circle give stability.
  8. Sampling links continuous and discrete time through x[n]=x(nT), while reconstruction restores x(t) under the sampling condition.

Use f_L=20 kHz, f_H=24 kHz, B=4 kHz; Use the band-pass condition 2f_H/m ≤ f_s ≤ 2f_L/(m−1); Choose m=3, giving 16 kHz ≤ f_s ≤ 20 kHz; Pick f_s=18 kHz as a valid rate; Check that translated bands do not overlap 20–24 kHz.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare ideal sinc reconstruction with zero-order hold reconstruction. Derive the aperture-effect sinc factor and discuss a compensation method. 15 marks

Explain ideal low-pass reconstruction and its sinc kernel; Model zero-order hold by a rectangular pulse; Derive the sinc factor sin(πfτ)/(πfτ); Discuss high-frequency attenuation and phase effects; Suggest inverse sinc equalization or a smaller aperture.

Analyze A sampled system shows a false 300 Hz tone when the input contains a 3.7 kHz component. Determine a possible sampling frequency, explain the alias, and specify the anti-aliasing filter. 12 marks

Try f_s=4 kHz; The alias is |3700−4000|=300 Hz; Explain folding about multiples of f_s; An anti-aliasing low-pass filter before the sampler must remove 3.7 kHz; Set cutoff below 2 kHz for f_s=4 kHz.

Evaluate For a second-order difference equation with poles at z=0.8 and z=−0.6, discuss stability and natural response. What changes if a pole moves to z=1.2? 12 marks

Both poles lie inside the unit circle, so the causal system is stable; Natural response contains terms proportional to 0.8^n and (−0.6)^n; Both terms decay as n increases; A pole at z=1.2 lies outside the unit circle; The system becomes unstable and the response grows.

Evaluate Analyse an end-to-end chain: analog anti-aliasing filter, sampler, n-bit quantizer, discrete-time LTI filter, and reconstruction filter. Explain how each block affects aliasing, quantization noise, and stability, and give design trade-offs. 16 marks

Anti-aliasing filter limits input bandwidth before sampling; Sampler must satisfy the Nyquist condition or aliasing appears; n-bit quantizer adds noise of variance Δ²/12; Discrete-time LTI filter shapes signal and noise and must be stable; Reconstruction filter removes spectral replicas and smooths the output; Trade-offs include filter order, sampling rate, word length and cost.

Glossary

Alias
A false low-frequency component produced when spectral replicas overlap.
Anti-aliasing filter
An analog low-pass filter placed before a sampler to limit the input bandwidth.
Aperture time
The duration for which a sampler holds or averages the input sample.
Band-limited signal
A signal whose Fourier transform is zero above some highest frequency f_max.
Band-pass sampling
Sampling a narrow high-frequency band at a rate lower than twice its top frequency.
BIBO stability
Every bounded input gives a bounded output; for a causal LTI system, all poles must lie inside the unit circle.
Convolution sum
y[n]=Σ x[k]h[n−k], the output of a discrete-time LTI system.
Difference equation
An equation relating the current output sample to past outputs and present or past inputs.
Discrete-time signal
A sequence x[n] defined only at integer indices n.
Guard band
An unused frequency interval between the signal band and the Nyquist frequency or filter transition band.
Impulse response
The output h[n] of an LTI system to the unit impulse δ[n].
Nyquist frequency
Half the sampling rate, f_s/2; it is the highest frequency that can be represented without aliasing.
Nyquist rate
The minimum sampling rate 2f_max for a signal band-limited to f_max.
Pole
A value of z that makes the denominator of H(z) zero; it controls the natural response and stability.
Quantization
Mapping continuous sample amplitudes to a finite set of levels.
Quantization error
The difference between the actual sample value and the quantized value; it is modelled as noise.
Reconstruction
Recovering a continuous-time signal from its samples, usually by low-pass filtering.
Sample-and-hold
A circuit that samples an input and holds the value for an aperture time.
Sampling
The process of taking values of a continuous-time signal at discrete instants.
Signal-to-quantization-noise ratio
The ratio of signal power to quantization noise power; it improves by about 6 dB per extra bit.
Sinc function
sinc(u)=sin(πu)/(πu), with sinc(0)=1; it is the ideal interpolation kernel.
Spectral replica
A copy of the signal spectrum shifted by multiples of the sampling frequency f_s.
Transfer function
H(z)=Y(z)/X(z) for an LTI system, the ratio of Z-transforms of output and input.
Uniform quantizer
A quantizer with equal step size Δ between adjacent levels.
Unit impulse
δ[n]=1 at n=0 and 0 otherwise; it is the identity for convolution.
Zero-order hold
A reconstruction method that holds each sample constant until the next sample.
