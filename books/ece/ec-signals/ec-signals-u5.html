5.1 Sampling theorem and reconstruction of signals

A continuous signal can be replaced by a list of numbers if it has no frequency above fmax and we sample faster than 2fmax. The samples then keep all the information, and an ideal low-pass filter turns them back into the smooth waveform. This is the bridge from continuous-time to discrete-time processing: phone audio, ECG recorders and digital controllers all store samples instead of a continuous voltage. Sampling too slowly loses information for good; that failure is the next chapter, aliasing and anti-aliasing filters.

You need: the Fourier transform, the sinc spectrum and ideal filters (see Fourier series and Fourier transform); the impulse and standard signals (see Classification of signals and systems).

The sampling theorem and its numbers

A signal is band-limited when its Fourier transform X(f) is zero for |f| > fmax, where fmax is the highest frequency in hertz. Sampling takes its values at the instants t = nTs, where n = …, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, … is any integer and Ts is the sampling interval in seconds. The sampling rate is fs = 1/Ts samples per second, written in hertz.

Sampling theorem. A signal band-limited to fmax is completely determined by its samples x(nTs) if fs > 2fmax. The rate 2fmax is the Nyquist rate, a property of the signal. The longest allowed spacing, 1/(2fmax), is the Nyquist interval. Half the sampling rate, fs/2, is the Nyquist frequency, a property of the sampler: components below it are kept as they are, and a component above it folds onto a lower frequency.

fs = 1/Ts > 2fmax

Here fs is the sampling rate in Hz, Ts the sampling interval in s and fmax the highest frequency in the signal in Hz.

The inequality is strict. At fs = 2fmax, a cosine exactly at fmax is sampled twice per period at the same two phases. Sampled at its peaks it looks fine, but sampled at its zero crossings every sample is 0, so its amplitude and phase cannot be recovered. Some syllabi call the result the Nyquist–Shannon or Whittaker–Kotelnikov–Shannon theorem.

Impulse-train model and the repeated spectrum

To see why the theorem holds, model the sampler as multiplication by an impulse train p(t) = Σ δ(t − nTs), the sum running over all integers n. Each impulse in the product carries one sample value as its area.

xs(t) = Σ x(nTs) δ(t − nTs)

Here xs(t) is the impulse-sampled signal and x(nTs) the sample at t = nTs.

The Fourier transform of the impulse train is another impulse train, of strength 1/Ts at every multiple of fs. Multiplication in time becomes convolution in frequency, and convolving X(f) with an impulse at kfs simply shifts it there. So the spectrum of the samples is the original spectrum repeated every fs hertz:

Xs(f) = (1/Ts) Σ X(f − kfs)

Here Xs(f) is the spectrum of the samples, X(f) the original spectrum, and k runs over all integers. Every copy is scaled by 1/Ts = fs.

The copy at k = 0 is the baseband copy; it runs from −fmax to fmax. The next copy starts at fs − fmax. The copies stay apart when fs − fmax > fmax, which is exactly fs > 2fmax. The empty gap between them, from fmax to fs − fmax, is the guard band, of width fs − 2fmax. If the copies overlap, high frequencies land on low ones: that is aliasing, the subject of aliasing and anti-aliasing filters.

Ideal reconstruction and sinc interpolation

An ideal low-pass filter with cutoff fs/2 and passband gain Ts keeps the baseband copy and removes all the others. The gain Ts cancels the 1/Ts scaling, so the output is x(t) with its original amplitude. Any cutoff inside the guard band works; fs/2 is the usual choice because it sits in the middle.

H(f) = Ts for |f| < fs/2, and 0 outside

Here H(f) is the frequency response of the reconstruction filter.

In time, this filter's impulse response is h(t) = sinc(t/Ts), the inverse transform of a rectangle of height Ts and width fs. Passing the impulse train through it replaces each impulse by a sinc pulse of height x(nTs) centred at nTs. Adding the pulses gives the interpolation formula:

x(t) = Σ x(nTs) sinc((t − nTs)/Ts)

Here sinc(u) = sin(πu)/(πu) with sinc(0) = 1, and the sum runs over all integers n.

Each sinc is 1 at its own sampling instant and 0 at every other sampling instant, so at t = nTs the sum returns exactly the sample x(nTs); between instants the pulses fill in the smooth curve. This filter is ideal: it is non-causal and has a vertical edge. Real circuits hold each sample and then smooth the staircase, which droops at high frequencies; that practical side and the band-pass case are in low-pass, band-pass and practical sampling.

Worked example

Question. A voltage x(t) = 3 cos(2π·200t) + 2 cos(2π·450t) volts is sampled at fs = 1000 Hz. Find fmax, the Nyquist rate, the Nyquist interval, Ts and the reconstruction filter. List the spectral lines between 0 and 1500 Hz, and find the guard band.

  1. The frequency of each cosine is the number multiplying t inside 2π(…): 200 Hz and 450 Hz. So fmax = 450 Hz.
  2. Nyquist rate = 2fmax = 2 × 450 = 900 Hz. Nyquist interval = 1/900 s ≈ 1.11 ms. Since 1000 Hz > 900 Hz, the condition fs > 2fmax holds.
  3. Ts = 1/fs = 1/1000 = 0.001 s = 1 ms.
  4. Reconstruction filter: ideal low-pass, cutoff fs/2 = 500 Hz, passband gain Ts = 0.001.
  5. Spectral lines on the positive axis: baseband at 200 and 450 Hz; the copy at 1000 Hz gives 1000 − 450 = 550, 1000 − 200 = 800, 1000 + 200 = 1200 and 1000 + 450 = 1450 Hz.
  6. Guard band: from fmax = 450 Hz up to the lowest copy line 550 Hz, width 550 − 450 = 100 Hz = fs − 2fmax. The 500 Hz cutoff sits in the middle of it.
  7. The filter passes 200 and 450 Hz and stops 550 Hz and above, so the output is the original x(t).

Answer. fmax = 450 Hz, Nyquist rate = 900 Hz, Nyquist interval ≈ 1.11 ms, Ts = 1 ms, filter cutoff 500 Hz with gain 0.001, copy lines at 550, 800, 1200 and 1450 Hz, guard band 100 Hz; sampling at 1000 Hz is safe.

Exam traps

  • Nyquist rate (2fmax, fixed by the signal) and Nyquist frequency (fs/2, fixed by the sampler) are different ideas. They are equal only when fs = 2fmax, the borderline case.
  • Dropping the gain Ts of the reconstruction filter: the output is then scaled by 1/Ts.
  • Writing fs ≥ 2fmax. A sinusoid exactly at fmax is not safe at fs = 2fmax; the condition is strict.
  • Unit slips: Ts in seconds, fs in hertz; 1 ms = 0.001 s, and 1/(1 kHz) = 1 ms, not 1 s.
  • Guard band is fs − 2fmax, the gap between the top of the baseband and the bottom of the first copy, not fs − fmax.

5.2 Aliasing and anti-aliasing filters

Sampling makes copies of a spectrum every fs hertz. If the signal has energy above fs/2, neighbouring copies overlap and a high-frequency component shows up at a low frequency. That false low frequency is an alias, and nothing done to the samples afterwards can tell it from a real low tone. The cure is an analog anti-aliasing filter before the sampler. Every sound card, data-acquisition board and digital radio has one, because once an alias is in the samples it stays there.

You need: the repeated spectrum and the guard band from sampling theorem and reconstruction, and the ideal low-pass response (see Distortionless transmission, ideal filters and realizability).

Spectral copies overlap

Sampling at fs = 1/Ts repeats the spectrum X(f) at every multiple of fs:

Xs(f) = fs Σ X(f − kfs)

Here Xs(f) is the spectrum of the samples, X(f) the spectrum of x(t), fs the sampling rate in Hz and k any integer.

If X(f) = 0 for |f| ≥ fs/2, the copies sit apart and the low-pass filter of the previous chapter rebuilds x(t). If the signal reaches past fs/2, that is if fs < 2fmax, a copy reaches into the band from 0 to fs/2 and adds to the baseband copy. For a component between fs/2 and fs, the intruder is the copy centred at fs, which puts it at fs − f. For a component above fs, it is a copy at a higher multiple of fs; the folding rule below handles every case.

The overlap is aliasing; some syllabi call it fold-over or frequency folding. It is a permanent mixing of two frequencies in the same numbers, not a rounding error.

The folding rule

After sampling at fs, a component at frequency f looks like the frequency |f − kfs| that is nearest to zero, over all integers k. The result always lies between 0 and fs/2.

Original f (Hz)Nearest kfs (Hz)Seen as (Hz)
3000300
7001000300
9001000100
13001000300
17002000300
25002000 or 3000500

The table uses fs = 1000 Hz. A 700 Hz tone is above fs/2 = 500 Hz; its nearest multiple is 1000 Hz, so it is seen at |700 − 1000| = 300 Hz. The identity behind it, with t = n/1000 s:

cos(2π·700n/1000) = cos(2πn − 2π·300n/1000) = cos(2π·300n/1000)

The middle step uses cos(2πn − θ) = cos θ for every integer n. So cos(2π·700t) and cos(2π·300t) give the same samples at 1000 Hz, and so does cos(2π·1300t).

For a single cosine the alias keeps its amplitude. For a real signal, several components can fold onto the same frequency and add or cancel there, so the aliased spectrum is not just a shifted copy of the original.

The anti-aliasing filter

An anti-aliasing filter (also called a prefilter) is an analog low-pass filter placed before the sampler. It must be analog: after sampling, the alias and the real tone are the same numbers, so no digital filter can separate them, and sampling faster later does not help either.

Strict rule. To stop any folding at all, the filter must remove everything at or above fs/2. Its passband edge is fmax and its stopband edge is fs/2, so the transition band is fs/2 − fmax wide, half the guard band. Usual design rule. A component between fs/2 and fs − fmax folds to a frequency between fmax and fs/2, outside the wanted band, and a digital filter can remove it later. Only components above fs − fmax fold into the wanted band, so the stopband edge can be put at fs − fmax, which gives the filter the whole guard band as its transition band.

A Butterworth low-pass filter of order N and 3 dB cutoff fc has a flat passband and attenuation that grows with frequency:

|H(f)|² = 1/(1 + (f/fc)2N)

Here |H(f)| is the magnitude response and N the filter order. For attenuation A dB at the stopband edge fstop, the order is the smallest integer N ≥ log₁₀(10A/10 − 1) / (2 log₁₀(fstop/fc)).

A narrow transition band needs a high order; sampling faster widens the guard band and lets a simple filter do the job. This is why most converters oversample.

Worked example

Question. A speech signal occupies 300 Hz to 3400 Hz and is sampled at 8000 Hz. Is it safe from aliasing, and how wide is the guard band? A tone cos(2π·700t) volts is then sampled at 1000 Hz. What tone does the reconstruction filter give, and which other tone gives the same samples?

  1. Speech: fmax = 3400 Hz, so the Nyquist rate is 2 × 3400 = 6800 Hz. Since 8000 > 6800, the speech does not alias.
  2. The first copy starts at fs − fmax = 8000 − 3400 = 4600 Hz. Guard band = 4600 − 3400 = 1200 Hz = fs − 2fmax. The Nyquist frequency 4000 Hz sits in the middle of it.
  3. Tone: fs/2 = 1000/2 = 500 Hz, and 700 Hz > 500 Hz, so the tone aliases.
  4. Folding: the nearest multiple of 1000 Hz is 1000 Hz, and |700 − 1000| = 300 Hz. The 500 Hz reconstruction filter therefore outputs cos(2π·300t) volts, a 1 V tone at the wrong frequency.
  5. Other tones with the same samples: any f with |f − k·1000| = 300 Hz, for example 1300 Hz (k = 1) or 1700 Hz (k = 2); and the genuine 300 Hz tone itself.

Answer. The speech is safe at 8000 Hz with a 1200 Hz guard band (3400 Hz to 4600 Hz). The 700 Hz tone comes out as cos(2π·300t) volts, and a 1300 Hz tone gives the same samples.

Worked example 2

Question. The same speech (passband edge fc = 3.4 kHz) needs at least 30 dB of attenuation on every component that would fold into the speech band. Find the Butterworth order needed at fs = 8 kHz and at fs = 16 kHz.

  1. The stopband edge is fstop = fs − fmax. At 8 kHz, fstop = 8 − 3.4 = 4.6 kHz; at 16 kHz, fstop = 16 − 3.4 = 12.6 kHz.
  2. 10A/10 − 1 = 10³ − 1 = 999, and log₁₀ 999 = 2.9996.
  3. At 8 kHz: fstop/fc = 4.6/3.4 = 1.353, log₁₀ 1.353 = 0.1313, so N ≥ 2.9996/(2 × 0.1313) = 11.42. Take N = 12.
  4. At 16 kHz: fstop/fc = 12.6/3.4 = 3.706, log₁₀ 3.706 = 0.5689, so N ≥ 2.9996/(2 × 0.5689) = 2.64. Take N = 3.
  5. Check N = 3 at 12.6 kHz: 10 log₁₀(1 + 3.706⁶) = 10 log₁₀(2591) = 34.1 dB, which is above 30 dB.

Answer. N = 12 at fs = 8 kHz, but only N = 3 at fs = 16 kHz: doubling the sampling rate turns a 12th-order analog filter into a 3rd-order one.

Exam traps

  • Placing the anti-aliasing filter after the sampler. It is then a digital filter, and the alias is already inside the samples.
  • Thinking a faster sampler can remove an alias that is already in recorded samples; the mixing has already happened.
  • Reading the alias of 1300 Hz at fs = 1000 Hz as 1300 − 500. Fold about multiples of fs, not about fs/2: |1300 − 1000| = 300 Hz.
  • Saying every alias keeps its amplitude. That holds for one isolated cosine; components folding onto the same frequency add or cancel.
  • Mixing up the guard band (fs − 2fmax) with the strict transition band (fs/2 − fmax), which is half of it.

5.3 Low-pass, band-pass and practical sampling

The sampling theorem of the first chapter is the low-pass sampling theorem: a signal from 0 to fmax needs fs > 2fmax. A signal that occupies only a band from fL to fH, with nothing near zero, can be sampled far below 2fH, because the empty space below fL gives the copies room. Real samplers also take samples of finite width, and a held sample flattens the top of the band: that droop is the aperture effect. Radio receivers use band-pass sampling; every practical converter has to live with the aperture effect.

You need: the repeated spectrum from sampling theorem and reconstruction and the folding rule from aliasing and anti-aliasing filters, plus the Fourier transform of a rectangular pulse (see Fourier transform of aperiodic signals).

Band-pass sampling: use the empty gaps

Let a real signal have energy only between fL and fH, with bandwidth B = fH − fL. Sampling repeats the band (and its mirror image at negative frequencies) every fs. The copies do not overlap if the whole band fits inside one Nyquist zone, a strip of width fs/2 between (m − 1)fs/2 and mfs/2 for some integer m. Writing that condition for both band edges gives the band-pass sampling theorem:

2fH/m ≤ fs ≤ 2fL/(m − 1)

Here m is an integer with 1 ≤ m ≤ ⌊fH/B⌋. Each allowed m gives a range of valid rates; m = 1 is the low-pass case fs ≥ 2fH.

The largest m gives the lowest rate, fs = 2fH/m, which equals 2B when fH is an exact multiple of B. At the ends of a range the copies touch, with no guard band, so a real design picks a rate inside a range. A band in an odd zone (m odd) lands near zero the right way round; in an even zone it lands mirrored, with high and low frequencies swapped. To rebuild the original signal, filter the samples with a band-pass filter from fL to fH; for digital processing, the copy near zero already holds all the information.

Natural and flat-top sampling

A real sampler cannot take an impulse. Natural sampling multiplies x(t) by a train of narrow pulses of width τ, so each pulse top follows the signal. Its spectrum is a set of copies of X(f) at kfs with constant weights (τ/Ts) sinc(kτ/Ts): each copy keeps its shape, and a low-pass filter still recovers x(t) exactly.

Flat-top sampling, done by a sample-and-hold circuit, holds each sample value constant for τ seconds. The held train is the impulse train convolved with a rectangle of width τ, so its spectrum is Xs(f) multiplied by the rectangle's transform:

H(f) = τ sinc(fτ) e−jπfτ

Here τ is the hold time in s, f the frequency in Hz and sinc(u) = sin(πu)/(πu). The factor e−jπfτ is a delay of τ/2; it changes no amplitude.

The magnitude |sinc(fτ)| is 1 at 0 Hz, falls across the band and reaches zero at f = 1/τ. This frequency-dependent loss is the aperture effect: unlike natural sampling, the baseband copy itself is distorted. For a full-period hold (τ = Ts, the zero-order hold used to drive a DAC), the gain at fs/2 is sinc(0.5) = 2/π ≈ 0.637, about −3.9 dB.

Two remedies are used. Make τ small compared with Ts, so that fτ stays near zero across the band. Or follow the hold with an equalizer of gain 1/|sinc(fτ)| over the signal band, often called an inverse-sinc filter. The equalizer also boosts noise near the band edge, so it is used only up to fmax. The held values are then rounded to a fixed number of levels, which is the next chapter, quantization of sampled signals.

Worked example

Question. A signal has components only between 5 kHz and 6 kHz. Find every range of valid sampling rates, the lowest rate, and a practical rate with a guard band. For the practical rate, find where the band lands between 0 and fs/2.

  1. fL = 5 kHz, fH = 6 kHz, B = 1 kHz, so m runs from 1 to ⌊6/1⌋ = 6. Low-pass sampling would need fs > 2fH = 12 kHz.
  2. Ranges 2fH/m ≤ fs ≤ 2fL/(m − 1): m = 2 gives 6 to 10 kHz; m = 3 gives 4 to 5 kHz; m = 4 gives 3 to 3.33 kHz; m = 5 gives 2.4 to 2.5 kHz; m = 6 gives 2 to 2 kHz.
  3. Lowest rate: m = 6 gives fs = 12/6 = 2 kHz = 2B, but only that single value works, with the copies touching at both edges.
  4. Practical rate: take m = 5 and fs = 2.45 kHz, inside 2.4 to 2.5 kHz. Zone 5 runs from 4 × 2.45/2 = 4.9 kHz to 5 × 2.45/2 = 6.125 kHz, and 5 to 6 kHz lies inside it.
  5. The band lands at 5 − 2 × 2.45 = 0.1 kHz to 6 − 4.9 = 1.1 kHz, inside 0 to fs/2 = 1.225 kHz, the right way round because m = 5 is odd.
  6. Margins: 0.1 kHz below the band image and 1.225 − 1.1 = 0.125 kHz above it.

Answer. Valid ranges are 6–10, 4–5, 3–3.33, 2.4–2.5 kHz and exactly 2 kHz. The lowest rate is 2 kHz (no margin); 2.45 kHz is a practical choice, and the band then appears at 0.1–1.1 kHz, against 12 kHz for low-pass sampling.

Worked example 2

Question. Speech up to 3.4 kHz is sampled at 8 kHz with flat-top pulses. Find the aperture gain at 3.4 kHz and the equalizer gain needed there (a) for a full-period hold τ = Ts, (b) for τ = 10 μs.

  1. (a) τ = Ts = 1/8000 = 125 μs. fτ = 3400 × 125 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.425.
  2. sinc(0.425) = sin(0.425π)/(0.425π) = sin(1.335)/1.335 = 0.9724/1.335 = 0.728, which is 20 log₁₀ 0.728 = −2.75 dB.
  3. Equalizer gain at 3.4 kHz = 1/0.728 = 1.37, a boost of 2.75 dB.
  4. (b) τ = 10 μs: fτ = 3400 × 10 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.034, and sinc(0.034) = 0.998, only −0.017 dB.
  5. So a pulse 10 μs wide, 8% of the period, makes the aperture effect negligible.

Answer. (a) Gain 0.728 (−2.75 dB), equalizer gain 1.37 at 3.4 kHz. (b) Gain 0.998 (−0.017 dB), no equalizer needed.

Exam traps

  • Rejecting a valid band-pass rate because it is below 2fH. The low-pass condition is not the band-pass condition.
  • Taking fs = 2B and stopping there. It works only when fH is a multiple of B, and even then the copies touch; check 2fH/m ≤ fs ≤ 2fL/(m − 1).
  • Saying natural sampling causes the aperture effect. Natural sampling only scales each copy; flat-top (sample-and-hold) sampling distorts the band.
  • Confusing the hold time τ with the sampling period Ts. The first zero of sinc(fτ) is at 1/τ, which is fs only for a full-period hold.
  • Forgetting that 0.637 at fs/2 is for τ = Ts; at the top of a real signal band (below fs/2) the droop is smaller.

At such high input frequencies, random timing errors of the sampling clock (aperture jitter) start to limit the signal-to-noise ratio. Inverse-sinc filters are built into many high-speed DACs. 5.4 Quantization of sampled signals

A held sample can take any value, but a converter stores it in a fixed number of bits, so it must round the sample to one of a finite set of levels. That rounding is quantization. The quantization error eq = Q(x) − x, the rounded value minus the true sample, behaves like a small added noise. That noise model links the number of bits to accuracy: it predicts the hiss of digital audio, the resolution of a sensor and the dynamic range of a converter. Only a few syllabi list quantization in this subject; where it is listed, exams ask for the step size, one rounded sample and the SQNR.

You need: where the held samples come from (see practical sampling), and the average power of a sinusoid, A²/2 (see Classification: energy, power, periodic, even and odd signals).

Levels and step size

A uniform quantizer with n bits has L = 2ⁿ output levels. If its L steps exactly tile the input range from xmin to xmax, each step has width

Δ = (xmax − xmin)/L

Here Δ is the step size, also called the resolution, in volts; L = 2ⁿ is the number of levels and n the word length in bits.

This is the mid-rise quantizer: its levels sit at the middle of each step, at xmin + (k + ½)Δ for k = 0, 1, …, L − 1. For a 0 V to 8 V, 3-bit quantizer, L = 8, Δ = 1 V and the levels are 0.5 V, 1.5 V, …, 7.5 V. A mid-tread quantizer instead has a level at zero (levels at kΔ), so a zero input gives an exact zero output; its staircase is shifted by half a step, and a bipolar mid-tread quantizer uses an odd number of levels.

Error, noise power and SQNR

While the input stays inside the range, rounding to the nearest level gives |eq| ≤ Δ/2. For a busy signal the error changes from sample to sample in an unpredictable way, so it is modelled as random noise spread uniformly between −Δ/2 and +Δ/2. Its mean is 0 and its power (variance) is

Pq = Δ²/12

Here Pq is the quantization noise power in V². It follows from the mean of e² over a uniform spread: (1/Δ) ∫ e² de from −Δ/2 to Δ/2 = Δ²/12.

A full-scale sinusoid has amplitude A = (xmax − xmin)/2 = LΔ/2 and power A²/2 = L²Δ²/8. Dividing by Δ²/12 gives SQNR = 1.5L² = 1.5 × 4ⁿ. In decibels:

SQNR = 6.02n + 1.76 dB

This holds for a full-scale sinusoid and uniform error. For a sine of smaller amplitude A, subtract 20 log₁₀(Afs/A) dB, where Afs = (xmax − xmin)/2.

Each extra bit halves Δ and adds about 6 dB. Inputs outside the range overload the quantizer: the output clips and the error can exceed Δ/2, so the noise model fails. Companding (μ-law in North America and Japan, A-law in India and Europe) compresses large amplitudes before a uniform quantizer and expands them after, which gives small signals smaller steps in volts; the SQNR then stays nearly constant over a wide range of input levels.

Worked example

Question. A 3-bit uniform mid-rise quantizer covers 0 V to 8 V. Find L, Δ, the maximum error, the output and the error for an input sample of 3.4 V, the noise power, and the SQNR for a full-scale sinusoid.

  1. L = 2³ = 8 levels.
  2. Δ = (8 − 0)/8 = 1 V. The levels are 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, …, 7.5 V.
  3. Maximum error inside the range = Δ/2 = 0.5 V.
  4. 3.4 V lies in the step from 3 V to 4 V, whose level is 3.5 V. Its distance to 3.5 V is 0.1 V and to 2.5 V is 0.9 V, so Q(3.4) = 3.5 V and eq = 3.5 − 3.4 = +0.1 V.
  5. Noise power Pq = Δ²/12 = 1/12 = 0.0833 V².
  6. Full-scale sine: A = 4 V, power A²/2 = 8 V². SQNR = 8/(1/12) = 96, and 10 log₁₀ 96 = 19.82 dB. Check with the formula: 6.02 × 3 + 1.76 = 19.82 dB.

Answer. L = 8, Δ = 1 V, maximum error 0.5 V, Q(3.4 V) = 3.5 V with eq = +0.1 V, Pq = 0.0833 V², SQNR = 19.82 dB.

Exam traps

  • Using Δ as the maximum error. Inside the range the bound is Δ/2.
  • Writing L = 2ⁿ − 1. That is the largest code value; the number of levels is 2ⁿ.
  • Using 6.02n + 1.76 dB for a small or non-sinusoidal signal. It is for a full-scale sine with uniform error.
  • Mixing up the sign of the error: this book uses eq = Q(x) − x, so rounding 3.4 V up to 3.5 V gives +0.1 V.
  • Ignoring overload: outside xmin to xmax the output clips and Δ²/12 no longer describes the error.

5.5 Discrete-time LTI systems and convolution sum

A discrete-time system turns one sequence into another. If it is linear and time-invariant (LTI), a single sequence describes it completely: its impulse response h[n]. The output for any input x[n] is then the convolution sum of x[n] and h[n], the discrete twin of the convolution integral. Phone audio filters, ECG smoothing and moving averages on sensor data all run this sum sample by sample.

You need: the samples x[n] = x(nTs) from sampling theorem and reconstruction; δ[n], u[n] and the system properties (see System properties: linearity, time invariance, causality, stability); and the convolution integral it mirrors (see Convolution integral and its evaluation).

Sequences, impulse and step

A discrete-time signal is a sequence x[n], defined only at integer n. A sequence is periodic if x[n + N] = x[n] for every n; the smallest such positive integer N is its period. A discrete-time system maps an input sequence x[n] to an output y[n]; it is LTI when it obeys superposition and a delayed input gives an equally delayed output.

The unit impulse is δ[n] = 1 at n = 0 and 0 elsewhere. The unit step is u[n] = 1 for n ≥ 0 and 0 for n < 0. They are related by δ[n] = u[n] − u[n − 1]. Any sequence is a sum of shifted, scaled impulses, the sum running over all integers k:

x[n] = Σk=−∞∞ x[k] δ[n − k]

Here x[k] is the value of the input at index k, and δ[n − k] is an impulse placed at n = k.

The impulse response h[n] is the output when the input is δ[n] and the system starts at rest. By time invariance, δ[n − k] gives h[n − k]; by linearity, x[k]δ[n − k] gives x[k]h[n − k]; adding over k gives the output.

The convolution sum and how to evaluate it

y[n] = Σk=−∞∞ x[k] h[n − k]

Here y[n] is the output at index n, x[k] the input at index k, h[n − k] the impulse response flipped and shifted by n, and k the summation index. Swapping the roles gives the same result: y[n] = Σ h[k] x[n − k].

For finite sequences only the overlapping terms are nonzero. If x[n] has N₁ samples and h[n] has N₂ samples, y[n] has N₁ + N₂ − 1 samples, starting at the sum of the two start indices.

  1. Flip and slide: draw x[k]; draw h[−k], the impulse response flipped about k = 0.
  2. Shift the flipped sequence right by n to get h[n − k]. Multiply the overlapping samples and add: that sum is y[n].
  3. Increase n by one and repeat until the sequences no longer overlap.
  4. Table check: write x along the top and h down the side, fill each cell with the product, and add along the anti-diagonals; the sums are y[0], y[1], ….

For an infinite h[n] such as aⁿu[n], the same sum is a geometric series; a finite series from 0 to n has the closed form (1 − an+1)/(1 − a).

Properties, causality, stability and frequency response

  • Commutative: x[n] * h[n] = h[n] * x[n].
  • Associative: (x * h₁) * h₂ = x * (h₁ * h₂), so two systems in cascade act like one with h₁ * h₂.
  • Distributive: x * (h₁ + h₂) = x * h₁ + x * h₂, so two systems in parallel add their impulse responses.
  • Identity: x[n] * δ[n] = x[n], and x[n] * δ[n − m] = x[n − m], a delay of m samples.

An LTI system is causal exactly when h[n] = 0 for n < 0: the output then uses only present and past inputs. It is BIBO stable exactly when Σ|h[n]| over all n is finite; every bounded input then gives a bounded output. The step response is the running sum s[n] = Σk=−∞n h[k].

H(ejω) = Σn=−∞∞ h[n] e−jωn

Here H(ejω) is the frequency response, ω the frequency in radians per sample and j = √−1. The sum converges when Σ|h[n]| is finite, so every stable system has one. For the input ejωn, the output is H(ejω) ejωn: the magnitude of H is the gain and its angle the phase shift at ω. It is studied fully under the discrete-time Fourier transform.

5.6 Linear constant-coefficient difference equations

A difference equation is the discrete-time twin of a differential equation. It ties the present output sample to a few past outputs and to present and past inputs, through fixed coefficients. Give it its starting values and a loop computes every later sample, which is what a digital filter does inside a phone or an ECG monitor. The roots of its characteristic equation decide whether the output settles or blows up.

You need: the convolution sum and impulse response from discrete-time LTI systems and convolution sum, and the unit step, unit impulse and time shifting (see Standard signals: impulse, step, ramp, exponential).

The equation, its order and its two kinds

A linear constant-coefficient difference equation (LCCDE) of order N is

Σk=0N ak y[n − k] = Σm=0M bm x[n − m]

Here y[n] is the output, x[n] the input, ak and bm are constants with a0 = 1, N is the order (the largest output delay) and M the largest input delay.

Moving the past outputs to the right gives the recursive form y[n] = −a1y[n − 1] − … − aNy[n − N] + b0x[n] + … + bMx[n − M], which computes each sample from numbers already known. The coefficients must be plain numbers: a term such as n·y[n − 1] breaks time invariance and y[n]·y[n − 1] breaks linearity.

If some ak (k ≥ 1) is nonzero, the output feeds back and the system is recursive; its impulse response normally lasts forever, so it is an IIR (infinite impulse response) system. (A pole cancelled by a zero is the rare exception.) If every ak = 0 for k ≥ 1, then y[n] = Σ bm x[n − m], the convolution sum with h[n] = bn for 0 ≤ n ≤ M: a non-recursive or FIR system.

To start the recursion you need N past outputs y[−1], …, y[−N], the initial conditions. Initial rest means y[n] = 0 for n < 0. Under initial rest an LCCDE describes a causal LTI system.

Block diagram: adders, delays and multipliers

Three blocks build every LCCDE: an adder (Σ), a multiplier by a constant, and a unit delay, drawn as z⁻¹ (the symbol comes from Z-transform analysis; here it only means one sample of memory: input y[n], output y[n − 1]). Direct form I draws the input side and the output side separately and uses M + N delays. Direct form II shares one chain of delays between both sides and uses only max(N, M) delays, the least possible; both use N + M + 1 multipliers.

The figure draws worked example 2, y[n] = x[n] + 0.75y[n − 1] − 0.125y[n − 2]. The output passes through two delays; the taps are multiplied by −a1 = 0.75 and −a2 = −0.125 and fed back to the adder.

Closed form: natural plus forced response

Natural response. With the input set to zero, try y[n] = λⁿ. Dividing out λn−N gives the characteristic equation:

λN + a1λN−1 + … + aN = 0

Its N roots λi (counting repeats) are the natural modes; without pole–zero cancellation they are the poles of the system.

Each distinct root gives a term Ciλin. A root repeated r times gives (C0 + C1n + … + Cr−1nr−1)λⁿ.

Forced response. Guess a form like the input: a constant for a constant input, A·2ⁿ for 2ⁿ, A cos ωn + B sin ωn for a sinusoid. If the guess is itself a natural mode (a constant input with λ = 1 a root, or 2ⁿ with λ = 2 a root), multiply the guess by n. Substitute and solve for the constants.

Total response: y[n] = ynat[n] + yforced[n] for n ≥ 0. Fix the N constants Ci from N known values, either y[−1], …, y[−N] or y[0], …, y[N − 1] found by iterating first.

Stability from the root positions

|λi| < 1 for every root ⟺ stable

This is for a causal system; λi are the characteristic roots (poles) and |·| is the magnitude of a complex number.

A marginally stable system's own modes stay bounded, but an input at the mode's frequency makes the output grow: u[n] into y[n] = y[n − 1] + x[n] gives n + 1. A negative root such as −0.9 gives an alternating, slowly decaying mode.

Takeaways

  1. Sampling at fs copies the spectrum every fs; if fs > 2fmax the copies stay apart and an ideal low-pass filter with cutoff fs/2 and gain Ts rebuilds x(t).
  2. Below the Nyquist rate a component at f is seen at the nearest |f − kfs|, and no processing of the samples can undo it.
  3. The anti-aliasing filter is analog and sits before the sampler; sampling faster widens the guard band and lowers the filter order it needs.
  4. A band from fL to fH can be sampled at 2fH/m ≤ fs ≤ 2fL/(m − 1), often far below 2fH.
  5. Flat-top (sample-and-hold) sampling droops by sinc(fτ), the aperture effect; short pulses or an inverse-sinc equalizer correct it.
  6. An n-bit uniform quantizer has step Δ = range/2ⁿ, noise power Δ²/12 and SQNR = 6.02n + 1.76 dB for a full-scale sine.
  7. A discrete-time LTI system is described completely by h[n]; its output is the convolution sum, and it is stable when Σ|h[n]| is finite.
  8. A difference equation is solved by iteration or as natural plus forced response; it is stable when every characteristic root is inside the unit circle.

Understand State the sampling theorem for a band-limited signal and derive the ideal reconstruction formula using sinc interpolation. 12 marks

Points to cover. Define a band-limited signal and fmax; Model sampling by an impulse train and show the spectrum repeats every fs; State the condition fs > 2fmax; Show that an ideal low-pass filter with cutoff fs/2 and gain Ts recovers X(f); Derive x(t) = Σ x(nTs) sinc((t − nTs)/Ts).

Model answer.

  1. Band-limited signal. A signal x(t) is band-limited to fmax if its Fourier transform X(f) = 0 for |f| > fmax. Samples are taken at t = nTs for every integer n, with sampling interval Ts and sampling rate fs = 1/Ts.
  2. Statement. A signal band-limited to fmax is uniquely determined by its samples x(nTs) if fs > 2fmax. The minimum rate 2fmax is the Nyquist rate and the largest spacing 1/(2fmax) is the Nyquist interval.
  3. Impulse-train model. Write the sampled signal as xs(t) = x(t)p(t), with p(t) = Σ δ(t − nTs). Because x(t)δ(t − nTs) = x(nTs)δ(t − nTs), this is xs(t) = Σ x(nTs)δ(t − nTs): each impulse carries one sample as its area.
  4. Spectrum of the impulse train. p(t) is periodic with period Ts. Its exponential Fourier series has every coefficient equal to 1/Ts, so p(t) = (1/Ts) Σ ej2πkfst, and its transform is P(f) = (1/Ts) Σ δ(f − kfs).
  5. Spectrum of the samples. Multiplication in time is convolution in frequency: Xs(f) = X(f) * P(f) = (1/Ts) Σ X(f − kfs). The spectrum is X(f), scaled by 1/Ts, repeated at every multiple of fs.
  6. No overlap. The k = 0 copy occupies −fmax to fmax; the k = 1 copy starts at fs − fmax. They do not overlap when fs − fmax > fmax, that is fs > 2fmax. The strict sign matters: at fs = 2fmax a cosine at fmax can be sampled at its zero crossings and give all-zero samples.
  7. Recovery in frequency. Let H(f) = Ts for |f| < fs/2 and 0 elsewhere. With no overlap, only the k = 0 copy lies inside the passband, so Xs(f)H(f) = (1/Ts)X(f)·Ts = X(f). The filter output is exactly x(t).
  8. Impulse response of the filter. h(t) = ∫ H(f)ej2πft df over −fs/2 to fs/2 = Ts·sin(πfst)/(πt) = sinc(t/Ts), using sinc(u) = sin(πu)/(πu).
  9. Interpolation formula. The output is xs(t) * h(t) = Σ x(nTs) h(t − nTs), so x(t) = Σ x(nTs) sinc((t − nTs)/Ts). Each pulse is 1 at its own instant and 0 at every other instant nTs, so the formula returns the samples exactly and fills in the curve between them.
  10. Numerical check. For x(t) = 3 cos(2π·200t) + 2 cos(2π·450t) sampled at fs = 1000 Hz (fmax = 450 Hz, Nyquist rate 900 Hz), the spectral lines and the filter are:
    Line (Hz)FromInside the 500 Hz filter?

    800copy at 1000 Hzno
    1200, 1450copy at 1000 Hzno
  11. Closing check. The guard band is 550 − 450 = 100 Hz = fs − 2fmax, so the filter separates the copies. Summing the sinc series at t = 0.5 ms (between two samples) with many terms gives 2.740 V, and the formula 3 cos(0.2π) + 2 cos(0.45π) gives 2.427 + 0.313 = 2.740 V.
Understand Explain aliasing using the spectrum of a sampled signal. Define Nyquist rate and Nyquist frequency with an example. 10 marks

Points to cover. Draw the baseband spectrum and its copies every fs; Show the overlap when fs < 2fmax; Define the Nyquist rate 2fmax and the Nyquist frequency fs/2; Give one numerical alias with the folding rule; State that aliasing cannot be undone after sampling and needs an anti-aliasing filter.

Model answer.

  1. Spectrum of the samples. Sampling at fs gives Xs(f) = fs Σ X(f − kfs): the spectrum X(f) of width −fmax to fmax is copied at 0, ±fs, ±2fs, …. Sketch the baseband triangle and the same triangle centred at fs.
  2. Overlap. The first copy starts at fs − fmax. If fs > 2fmax this is above fmax and a guard band of width fs − 2fmax separates the copies. If fs < 2fmax, the copy reaches below fmax into the baseband and the two add. That overlap is aliasing: a frequency above fs/2 appears in the samples as a lower one.
  3. Nyquist rate. The minimum sampling rate for a signal band-limited to fmax, 2fmax. It belongs to the signal.
  4. Nyquist frequency. Half the sampling rate, fs/2. It belongs to the sampler: components below it are kept; components above it fold.
  5. Folding rule. A component at f is seen at the value of |f − kfs| nearest to zero over integers k. In the time domain, cos(2πf nTs) = cos(2π(f − kfs)nTs) for every integer n, so the two frequencies give the same samples.
  6. Example. Take fs = 1000 Hz, so the Nyquist frequency is 500 Hz.
    Tone (Hz)Nearest kfs (Hz)Seen at (Hz)Aliased?
    3000300no
    7001000300yes
    9001000100yes
    13001000300yes
  7. Reading the table. The 700 Hz and 1300 Hz tones give exactly the samples of a 300 Hz tone. A signal containing 300 Hz and 700 Hz would need fs > 2 × 700 = 1400 Hz (its Nyquist rate) to be sampled correctly.
  8. Irreversible. After sampling, the alias and a real 300 Hz tone are the same numbers, so no digital filter can separate them. The only cure is an analog anti-aliasing low-pass filter before the sampler, which removes components that would fold into the wanted band.
  9. Sketch. The figure shows a triangular spectrum of half-width fmax = 700 Hz sampled at fs = 1000 Hz: the copy centred at 1000 Hz reaches down to 300 Hz, so the band from 300 Hz to 700 Hz is covered twice and the sum (dashed) is no longer the original triangle.
  10. Where the alias lands. A line at f inside the overlap, say 700 Hz, has an image at fs − f = 300 Hz from the copy at fs. The baseband filter (cutoff fs/2 = 500 Hz) passes that image, so the output contains a 300 Hz line that was never in x(t), and loses the true 700 Hz line.
  11. Effect on amplitude. A single aliased cosine keeps its amplitude. When a real component and an aliased one land on the same frequency they add with their phases, so the measured amplitude there can grow or shrink: the original spectrum cannot be read back from the sum.
  12. Closing check. With fs = 1000 Hz: cos(2π·700·n/1000) at n = 1 is cos(1.4π) = −0.309 and cos(2π·300·n/1000) at n = 1 is cos(0.6π) = −0.309; equal, as the folding rule says.
Remember Define uniform quantization. Derive the step size and show that quantization noise variance is Δ²/12 for a uniform error model. 10 marks

Points to cover. Define n-bit uniform quantization and L = 2ⁿ levels; Write Δ = (xmax − xmin)/2ⁿ; Model the error eq = Q(x) − x as uniform on −Δ/2 to Δ/2; Compute its mean (0) and variance (Δ²/12); State the assumptions of the model and derive SQNR = 6.02n + 1.76 dB.

Model answer.

  1. Definition. Quantization maps each sample to the nearest of a finite set of output levels. In an n-bit uniform quantizer the L = 2ⁿ levels are equally spaced, Δ apart.
  2. Step size. If the L steps tile the range xmin to xmax (mid-rise), L steps of width Δ cover xmax − xmin, so Δ = (xmax − xmin)/L = (xmax − xmin)/2ⁿ. The levels sit at xmin + (k + ½)Δ for k = 0, …, L − 1.
  3. Error bound. Rounding to the nearest level gives eq = Q(x) − x with −Δ/2 ≤ eq ≤ Δ/2 while x is inside the range.
  4. Noise model. For a busy signal that crosses many levels, eq is treated as a random variable spread uniformly on (−Δ/2, Δ/2), with probability density 1/Δ there, independent of the signal.
  5. Mean. E[eq] = (1/Δ) ∫ e de from −Δ/2 to Δ/2 = (1/Δ)[e²/2] = (1/Δ)(Δ²/8 − Δ²/8) = 0.
  6. Variance. E[eq²] = (1/Δ) ∫ e² de from −Δ/2 to Δ/2 = (1/Δ)[e³/3] = (1/Δ)(Δ³/24 + Δ³/24) = Δ²/12. Since the mean is zero, the noise power is Pq = Δ²/12.
  7. SQNR. A full-scale sine has A = (xmax − xmin)/2 = LΔ/2 and power A²/2 = L²Δ²/8. SQNR = (L²Δ²/8)/(Δ²/12) = 1.5L² = 1.5 × 4ⁿ, so in dB SQNR = 10 log₁₀ 1.5 + 20n log₁₀ 2 = 1.76 + 6.02n dB.
  8. Numbers for a 3-bit, 0 V to 8 V quantizer.
    QuantityWorkingValue
    L2³8
    Δ8/81 V
    Pq1²/120.0833 V²
    Sine power4²/28 V²
    SQNR10 log₁₀(8/0.0833)19.82 dB
  9. Assumptions. No overload (x stays inside the range), many levels crossed between samples, and an error uncorrelated with the signal. A constant or very slow input breaks the model; dither restores it.
  10. Why uniform is reasonable. When the signal changes by many steps between samples, the position of x inside its step is equally likely anywhere, so eq is equally likely anywhere in ±Δ/2. A sine that spans the whole range at a frequency unrelated to fs behaves this way closely.
  11. Why the noise is called white. Under the same conditions successive errors are nearly uncorrelated, so their power Δ²/12 is spread evenly from 0 to fs/2. This is what makes oversampling useful: only the part of the noise inside the signal band matters.
  12. Mid-tread note. A mid-tread quantizer has a level at zero, so a zero input gives zero error; its noise power is the same Δ²/12 for a busy signal because the error is still spread over one step.
  13. Closing check. 6.02 × 3 + 1.76 = 19.82 dB, the same as 10 log₁₀ 96 = 19.82 dB from the table.
Understand Define a discrete-time LTI system and derive its convolution-sum output from the impulse response. 10 marks

Points to cover. Define linearity and time invariance in discrete time; Write x[n] = Σ x[k]δ[n − k]; Use time invariance and superposition; Obtain y[n] = Σ x[k]h[n − k]; State the properties and check with a short example.

Model answer.

  1. Linearity. A discrete-time system T is linear if T{a x1[n] + b x2[n]} = a y1[n] + b y2[n] for all inputs and constants: it obeys additivity and scaling.
  2. Time invariance. If x[n] gives y[n], then x[n − n0] gives y[n − n0] for every integer shift n0.
  3. Impulse response. h[n] = T{δ[n]} with the system at rest.
  4. Step 1: decompose the input. Every sequence is a sum of shifted, scaled impulses: x[n] = Σk=−∞∞ x[k]δ[n − k], because δ[n − k] picks out the value at n = k.
  5. Step 2: time invariance. T{δ[n − k]} = h[n − k].
  6. Step 3: linearity. x[k] is a constant for each k, so T{x[k]δ[n − k]} = x[k]h[n − k], and the response to the sum is the sum of responses.
  7. Result. y[n] = Σk=−∞∞ x[k]h[n − k] = x[n] * h[n], the convolution sum. Putting m = n − k gives the equivalent form Σ h[m]x[n − m].
  8. Properties. Commutative (x * h = h * x), associative (cascaded systems combine as h1 * h2), distributive (parallel systems add as h1 + h2), identity (x * δ = x). Causal if h[n] = 0 for n < 0; BIBO stable if Σ|h[n]| is finite.
  9. Short example. x[n] = [2, 1, 3] and h[n] = [1, 2], both from n = 0:
    nterms x[k]h[n − k]y[n]
    02·12
    12·2 + 1·15
    21·2 + 3·15
    33·26
  10. Why both conditions are needed. Without time invariance the response to δ[n − k] would be a different sequence hk[n] for each k, and the output would be Σ x[k]hk[n], which is not a convolution. Without linearity the response to the sum would not be the sum of responses.
  11. Reading the sum. For a fixed n, the term x[k]h[n − k] is the input at time k weighted by how strongly the system still responds n − k samples later. For a causal system h[n − k] = 0 when k > n, so the sum runs only over the present and past inputs, k ≤ n.
  12. Graphical evaluation. Flip h[k] to h[−k], shift it by n to get h[n − k], multiply sample by sample with x[k] and add. For y[1] in the example below, h[1 − k] has the value 2 at k = 0 and 1 at k = 1, so y[1] = 2·2 + 1·1 = 5.
  13. Closing check. Length 3 + 2 − 1 = 4 samples, and the sum 2 + 5 + 5 + 6 = 18 equals (2 + 1 + 3)(1 + 2) = 18.

  8. Stability. The single pole (root) is λ = 0.5 and |0.5| < 1, so the causal system is BIBO stable. Equivalently Σ|h[n]| = 1/(1 − 0.5) = 2 is finite.
  9. Final value. As n → ∞, (0.5)ⁿ → 0 and y[n] → 2, which is the DC gain 1/(1 − 0.5) times the unit input.
  10. Characteristic root versus pole. In Z-transform language H(z) = 1/(1 − 0.5z⁻¹), with a pole at z = 0.5; the root λ = 0.5 of the characteristic equation is the same number, so both routes give the same stability verdict.
  11. Transient and steady state. The output splits into the steady part 2 (the forced response) and the transient −(0.5)ⁿ (the natural response). The transient halves every sample: after 7 samples it is 0.0078, under 0.4% of the final value.
  12. A non-zero start. If instead y[−1] = 2, then y[0] = 1 + 0.5 × 2 = 2, so C = 0 and y[n] = 2 for all n ≥ 0: the system starts already at its final value and shows no transient.
  13. Closing check. n = 4: 2 − 0.0625 = 1.9375, the same as the iteration table.

Analyze Compare ideal sinc reconstruction with zero-order hold reconstruction. Derive the aperture-effect sinc factor and discuss a compensation method. 15 marks

Points to cover. Ideal reconstruction: low-pass filter with cutoff fs/2 and gain Ts, sinc kernel; Zero-order hold: rectangular pulse of width τ; Derive H(f) = τ sinc(fτ)e−jπfτ; Give the droop and phase numbers and what is left of the images; Compensate with an inverse-sinc equalizer, shorter pulses or oversampling.

Model answer.

  1. Ideal reconstruction. The impulse samples pass through H(f) = Ts for |f| < fs/2, 0 elsewhere. In time this convolves the samples with h(t) = sinc(t/Ts), giving x(t) = Σ x(nTs) sinc((t − nTs)/Ts). It is exact for fs > 2fmax but needs a non-causal, infinitely long kernel and a vertical filter edge, so it cannot be built.
  2. Zero-order hold. A DAC holds each sample constant for τ seconds (τ = Ts for a full hold), producing a staircase. Its impulse response is h0(t) = 1 for 0 ≤ t < τ and 0 elsewhere, so the staircase is xs(t) * h0(t).
  3. Derivation. H0(f) = ∫ e−j2πft dt from 0 to τ = (1 − e−j2πfτ)/(j2πf).
  4. Factor out e−jπfτ: H0(f) = e−jπfτ(ejπfτ − e−jπfτ)/(j2πf) = e−jπfτ·2j sin(πfτ)/(j2πf) = e−jπfτ sin(πfτ)/(πf).
  5. Multiply and divide by τ: H0(f) = τ sinc(fτ) e−jπfτ. The magnitude τ|sinc(fτ)| is the aperture effect; the phase −πfτ is a pure delay of τ/2, which shapes nothing.
  6. Output spectrum. The staircase spectrum is (1/Ts) Σ X(f − kfs) · τ sinc(fτ)e−jπfτ. The baseband copy is distorted by sinc(fτ), and the images are only partly removed.
  7. Numbers for fs = 8 kHz, τ = Ts = 125 μs.

Evaluate For a second-order difference equation with poles at z = 0.8 and z = −0.6, discuss stability and natural response. What changes if a pole moves to z = 1.2? 12 marks

  3. Natural response. ynat[n] = C1(0.8)ⁿ + C2(−0.6)ⁿ. The first term decays smoothly; the second alternates in sign and decays faster. After 10 samples (0.8)10 = 0.107 and (0.6)10 = 0.006, so the 0.8 mode dominates.

Evaluate Analyse an end-to-end chain: analog anti-aliasing filter, sampler, n-bit quantizer, discrete-time LTI filter, and reconstruction filter. Explain how each block affects aliasing, quantization noise, and stability, and give design trade-offs. 16 marks

Points to cover. Anti-aliasing filter limits the input band before sampling; Sampler must satisfy fs > 2fmax or aliasing appears; n-bit quantizer adds noise of power Δ²/12; SQNR = 6.02n + 1.76 dB; The discrete-time filter shapes signal and noise and must be stable; The hold and reconstruction filter remove images and droop by sinc(fτ); Trade-offs: filter order, sampling rate, word length and cost.

Model answer.

  1. The chain. x(t) → analog anti-aliasing filter → sampler at fs → n-bit quantizer → discrete-time LTI filter → DAC with zero-order hold → analog reconstruction filter → output. Take speech up to fmax = 3.4 kHz as the running example.
  2. 1. Anti-aliasing filter (aliasing). It removes everything that would fold into 0 to 3.4 kHz, that is above fs − fmax. For 30 dB at that edge with a Butterworth filter (fc = 3.4 kHz): at fs = 8 kHz the edge is 4.6 kHz and N = 12; at fs = 16 kHz the edge is 12.6 kHz and N = 3.
  3. 2. Sampler (aliasing). fs must exceed 2fmax = 6.8 kHz. At 8 kHz the guard band is 1.2 kHz; at 16 kHz it is 9.2 kHz. Without the filter, a 4.7 kHz noise component at fs = 8 kHz would appear at 3.3 kHz, inside the speech band.
  4. 3. Quantizer (noise). For an n-bit uniform quantizer over ±1 V, Δ = 2/2ⁿ and Pq = Δ²/12. With n = 8: Δ = 7.81 mV and SQNR = 6.02 × 8 + 1.76 = 49.9 dB for a full-scale sine; with n = 12: 74.0 dB. Glossary

Alias
A false lower frequency produced when spectral copies overlap.
Anti-aliasing filter
An analog low-pass filter before the sampler that removes components that would fold into the wanted band.
Aperture effect
The high-frequency droop sinc(fτ) caused by flat-top sampling or a zero-order hold.
Band-limited signal
A signal whose Fourier transform is zero above some highest frequency fmax.
Band-pass sampling
Sampling a band from fL to fH at a rate below 2fH, allowed when 2fH/m ≤ fs ≤ 2fL/(m − 1).
BIBO stability
Every bounded input gives a bounded output; for an LTI system Σ|h[n]| is finite, for a causal LCCDE every |λi| < 1.
Characteristic roots
The roots of λN + a1λN−1 + … + aN = 0; the natural modes, or poles, of the system.
Companding
Compressing large amplitudes before uniform quantization and expanding them after (μ-law, A-law).
Convolution sum
y[n] = Σ x[k]h[n − k], the output of a discrete-time LTI system.
Difference equation
An equation relating the present output to past outputs and to present and past inputs with constant coefficients.
FIR and IIR
Finite and infinite impulse response: non-recursive and recursive difference equations.
Flat-top sampling
Sampling in which each value is held constant for τ seconds, as in a sample-and-hold circuit.
Guard band
The empty gap from fmax to fs − fmax between the baseband and the first spectral copy; its width is fs − 2fmax.
Impulse response
The output h[n] of an LTI system to δ[n], with the system at rest.
Natural sampling
Sampling with narrow pulses whose tops follow the signal; each spectral copy keeps its shape.
Nyquist frequency
Half the sampling rate, fs/2; components below it are kept, components above it fold.
Nyquist interval
The longest allowed sample spacing, 1/(2fmax).
Nyquist rate
The minimum sampling rate 2fmax for a signal band-limited to fmax.
Nyquist zone
A strip of width fs/2, from (m − 1)fs/2 to mfs/2; a band inside one zone can be sampled without overlap.
Quantization
Mapping each sample amplitude to the nearest of a finite set of levels.
Quantization error
eq = Q(x) − x, the quantized value minus the true sample; within ±Δ/2 without overload.
Reconstruction
Recovering the continuous-time signal from its samples, ideally with a low-pass filter of cutoff fs/2 and gain Ts.
Sampling
Taking the values of a continuous-time signal at the instants nTs.
Sinc function
sinc(u) = sin(πu)/(πu), with sinc(0) = 1; the ideal interpolation kernel.
SQNR
Signal-to-quantization-noise ratio; 6.02n + 1.76 dB for a full-scale sine and n bits.
Step size
The spacing Δ between adjacent levels of a uniform quantizer, also called its resolution.
Unit impulse
δ[n] = 1 at n = 0 and 0 elsewhere; the identity element of convolution.
Zero-order hold
Holding each sample constant until the next one; its response is τ sinc(fτ)e−jπfτ.
