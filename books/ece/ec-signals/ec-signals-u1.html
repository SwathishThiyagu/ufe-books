1.1 Signals and systems defined; CT and DT signals

A signal carries information as a function of an independent variable. A system takes an input signal and produces an output signal. In continuous time the variable is a real number t, usually seconds. In discrete time it is an integer n, a plain count with no unit. Every phone, ECG monitor and power grid runs on this pair: measure a signal, pass it through a system, read the result. This chapter fixes the words and the notation x(t), x[n], y(t) and y[n] that the rest of the subject uses.

You need: basic trigonometry (sin and cos at standard angles such as π/2 and π) and the school idea of a function; no earlier chapter of this book.

A signal is a function that carries information

A signal is a function of an independent variable that carries information. Write x(t). Here x is the signal, t is the independent variable, and the number x(t) at one particular t is the amplitude. The amplitude carries units: volts on a microphone lead, amperes in a motor winding, °C in a room, rupees for a share price. Some syllabi call a signal a function of one or more independent variables; a photograph has two, row and column.

A continuous-time (CT) signal has a value for every real t in its range. Speech voltage from a microphone is CT: between t = 0.001 s and t = 0.0011 s there are infinitely many instants, and a voltage exists at each one.

A discrete-time (DT) signal has a value only at integer n. Write x[n]. The number n is a count, not a time: n = 0, 1, 2, … Nothing is defined at n = 2.5. One value x[n] is called a sample when it came from a measurement.

  • Daily closing price of one share on the NSE: one value per trading day, index n = 0, 1, 2, …
  • Number of UPI transactions completed each day: discrete by nature, no sampling needed.
  • Room temperature read by a sensor every 10 ms: discrete in the record, though the room itself is continuous.

x[n] = x(nTs), n = 0, ±1, ±2, …

x(t) is the CT signal; Ts is the sampling interval in seconds; n is the integer index; x[n] is the resulting sequence. This equality holds only for ideal sampling.

A system maps an input signal to an output signal

A system is an operator that turns an input signal into an output signal. Write y(t) = T{x(t)} for CT and y[n] = T{x[n]} for DT. T is the rule, x is the input, y is the output. Input and output share the same independent variable.

Treat the system as a black box. What matters is the rule from input to output, not the circuit inside. The box may be an op-amp board, a few lines of Python, or a physical room whose temperature reacts to a heater. Systems are split into CT and DT in 1.5.

Two examples fix the idea. An audio amplifier with y(t) = 100 x(t) takes an input of 20 mV and gives 2 V. A moving average that replaces each reading by the mean of the last five readings is a DT system: input x[n], output y[n], index n in both.

  1. Read the independent variable on the input. Real t in seconds means CT; integer n means DT.
  2. Check that the output uses the same variable. A CT input with a DT output is a conversion, not a plain CT or DT system.
  3. State the rule T in one line, then test it. Samplers and reconstruction belong to Unit 5.

Question. A temperature sensor gives the voltage x(t) = 4 sin(20πt) V with t in seconds. A sampler records it every Ts = 10 ms, producing x[n] = 4 sin(0.2πn) V. Exam traps

  • x[n] is not x(nTs) in general. That equality needs ideal sampling; n is an integer count, never a time in seconds.
  • Brackets and parentheses are not decoration: x(t) is CT, x[n] is DT. Writing x(5) for a sequence loses marks.
  • The argument of sin and cos is in radians. 0.5π rad is 90°, not 0.5°; a calculator left in degree mode gives wrong answers.
  • A system is CT or DT by the variable on its input and output, not by its hardware. A filter running on a phone is still a DT system.
  • x(t) is the whole function; x(2) is one number. Saying 'the signal is 4 V' describes one amplitude, not the signal.

1.2 Classification: energy, power, periodic, even and odd signals

A signal is measured by its size, its repetition and its symmetry. Energy and average power answer how large the signal is over all time. Periodicity answers whether it repeats. Even and odd symmetry answers whether it is a mirror image about zero. These labels decide which transform applies, whether a Fourier series exists, and which terms vanish. You meet them in a mains voltage waveform, a heart-rate trace, and a log of UPI transactions sampled every second.

You need signal notation and the continuous-time (CT) versus discrete-time (DT) distinction from Chapter 1.1.

Energy and average power measure size

Total energy adds the squared signal over all time. Average power divides energy by the length of the observation window, then lets the window grow without limit. A finite pulse has finite energy and zero average power. A never-ending finite-amplitude wave has infinite energy and finite nonzero average power.

E = ∫−∞∞ |x(t)|² dt

E is total energy; x(t) is the CT signal; |x(t)|² is instantaneous power; if x is in volts across 1 Ω, E is in joules.

P = lim<sub>T→∞</sub> (1/T) ∫<sub>−T/2</sub><sup>T/2</sup> |x(t)|² dt

P is average power in watts; T is the length of the averaging window in seconds; the window is centred on zero.

For DT signals replace the integral by a sum and the window length by the number of samples.

E = Σ<sub>n=−∞</sub><sup>∞</sup> |x[n]|²,  P = lim<sub>N→∞</sub> (1/(2N+1)) Σ<sub>n=−N</sub><sup>N</sup> |x[n]|²

n is the integer sample index; 2N+1 is the number of samples from −N to N; E has units of (sample value)² and P the same units per sample.

  • Energy signal: 0 < E < ∞ and P = 0. Example: a single rectangular pulse.
  • Power signal: 0 < P < ∞ and E = ∞. Example: a sine wave that never stops.
  • Neither: both E and P are infinite. Example: x(t) = t for all t.
  • Some syllabi call energy and power the size of a signal.

Periodic and aperiodic signals

A periodic signal repeats after a fixed shift. The smallest positive shift is the fundamental period. A signal that never repeats is aperiodic.

x(t) = x(t + T) for all t

T is any positive period in seconds; the fundamental period T0 is the smallest positive T; the fundamental frequency is f0 = 1/T0 Hz and the angular frequency is ω0 = 2π/T0 rad/s.

In DT the shift must be an integer number of samples.

x[n] = x[n + N] for all integer n

N is a positive integer period in samples; N0 is the smallest positive integer period.

A DT sinusoid cos(Ωn + φ) is periodic only when Ω/(2π) is a rational number. If Ω/(2π) = m/N in lowest terms, then N0 = N samples. A sum of periodic signals is periodic when the ratio of their periods is rational; its fundamental period is the least common multiple of the individual periods.

Even and odd signals

An even signal is unchanged by time reversal. An odd signal changes sign under time reversal. The definitions use the same rule for CT and DT, with −t replaced by −n.

Even: x(−t) = x(t); Odd: x(−t) = −x(t)

x(t) is the signal value at time t; −t is the reversed time; an odd signal must satisfy x(0) = 0.

Every signal splits into an even part and an odd part. Add and subtract the signal and its reversed copy.

x<sub>e</sub>(t) = (x(t) + x(−t))/2,  x<sub>o</sub>(t) = (x(t) − x(−t))/2

x_e is the even part; x_o is the odd part; x(t) = x_e(t) + x_o(t) for every t.

  • cos(ωt) and a constant are even; sin(ωt) and t are odd.
  • even × even = even; odd × odd = even; even × odd = odd.
  • A signal with a nonzero average has a nonzero even part.

Worked example

Question. For x(t) = 6 cos(200πt) + 3 V, find the fundamental period T0, the average power P normalized to 1 Ω, the total energy E, and the even and odd parts.

  1. Compare with A cos(ωt) + DC. Here A = 6 V, ω = 200π rad/s, and DC = 3 V.
  2. T0 = 2π/ω = 2π/(200π) = 0.01 s = 10 ms. The fundamental frequency is f0 = 1/T0 = 100 Hz.
  3. Average power of A cos(ωt) is A²/2 = 6²/2 = 18 W. Average power of the DC term is 3² = 9 W. The cross term averages to zero over whole periods.
  4. P = 18 + 9 = 27 W.
  5. Energy over one period is P × T0 = 27 × 0.01 = 0.27 J. The signal repeats forever, so E = 0.27 + 0.27 + … = ∞.
  6. cos(200πt) is even and the constant 3 is even, so x(−t) = x(t). Therefore x_e(t) = x(t) and x_o(t) = 0.

Answer. T0 = 0.01 s, P = 27 W, E = ∞, x_e(t) = x(t), and x_o(t) = 0.

Exam traps

  • A periodic finite-amplitude signal is a power signal, not an energy signal. Its energy is infinite, not zero.
  • Do not confuse zero average value with zero average power. sin(t) has zero mean but P = 1/2 for unit amplitude.
  • For DT sinusoids, periodic in n needs Ω/(2π) rational. A sampled sine can look periodic on paper but be aperiodic in the sample index.
  • In the even-part formula, divide by 2. Forgetting the 2 gives twice the even part.
  • An odd signal must pass through zero at the origin, so x(0) = 0. A signal with x(0) ≠ 0 cannot be purely odd.

1.3 Standard signals: impulse, step, ramp, exponential

Standard signals are a small set of functions used as building blocks: the impulse δ(t), step u(t), ramp r(t)=t u(t), and exponential e^(at). Any piecewise signal can be written as a sum of shifted steps, ramps or exponentials. They are also the test inputs for a system, because the response to an impulse or step describes the system completely. In practice they model a switch closing (step), a quantity rising at a steady rate such as constant-velocity motion (ramp), a capacitor discharging (exponential), and a very short hit (impulse).

You need Chapter 1.1 for the meaning of t, n, CT and DT; Chapter 1.2 helps for energy and symmetry.

Impulse and step

Unit step u(t) is 0 for t<0 and 1 for t≥0. This book uses u(0)=1. In discrete time u[n]=1 for n≥0 and 0 for n<0. The step is a switch: it turns a signal on at t=0. A shifted step u(t−a) turns on at t=a; a negative step −u(t−a) turns off.

∫<sub>−∞</sub><sup>∞</sup> x(t) δ(t−t<sub>0</sub>) dt = x(t<sub>0</sub>)

x(t) is the signal being sampled; it must be continuous at t=t0 for this to hold. t0 is the shifting instant; the integral is over all time.

Unit impulse δ(t) is not an ordinary function. It has δ(t)=0 for t≠0 and unit area ∫ δ(t) dt=1. The value δ(0) is not an ordinary number. Its meaning is the sifting property above: the impulse picks out one value of x.

In discrete time δ[n]=1 at n=0 and 0 elsewhere. It equals u[n]−u[n−1]. The scale rule is δ(at)=δ(t)/|a| for a≠0. The absolute value matters: δ(−2t)=δ(t)/2. Some syllabi call these singularity functions, elementary signals or commonly used signal models.

Ramp and exponential

r(t)=t u(t), r[n]=n u[n]

r(t) is the continuous-time unit ramp; r[n] is the discrete-time unit ramp; u is the unit step.

Unit ramp r(t)=t u(t): r(t)=0 for t<0 and r(t)=t for t≥0. In discrete time r[n]=n u[n]. The ramp is the integral of the step, so r′(t)=u(t). In the generalized sense u′(t)=δ(t). These derivative relations let you differentiate piecewise signals without splitting them into intervals.

ejωt = cos(ωt) + j sin(ωt)

e is the base of natural logarithms; j=√−1; ω is angular frequency in rad/s; t is time in s.

Real exponential x(t)=A eat u(t) with A and a real. If a<0 the exponential decays; if a>0 it grows; if a=0 it is the constant A. The quantity 1/|a| has units of time and is the time constant for decay. Complex exponential x(t)=ejωt has real part a cosine and imaginary part a sine.

In discrete time the complex exponential is ejΩn, with Ω in rad/sample; it is periodic only when Ω/(2π) is rational. Complex exponentials are the eigenfunctions of LTI systems, which is why Unit 3 uses them.

Building signals and test inputs

Any piecewise signal made of straight segments and jumps can be written with steps and ramps. A jump of height K at t=a is K u(t−a). A pulse of height 2 from t=1 to t=3 is 2u(t−1)−2u(t−3). A slope change at t=a uses a shifted ramp.

Exponentials describe charging, discharging and decay. The product eatu(t) means the exponential starts at t=0; before that it is zero. A short pulse can be approximated by an impulse when the system is much slower than the pulse width.

These signals are test inputs. The impulse response h(t) is the output to δ(t); the step response is the output to u(t). They are linked by differentiation and integration. See Unit 2, Continuous-Time LTI Systems, for impulse response and convolution. See Unit 3, Fourier Series and Fourier Transform, for transforms of these signals; see Unit 4, Laplace Transform Analysis, for Laplace transforms.

Exam traps

  • δ(t) is not a finite-height pulse. Its value at t=0 is not an ordinary number; use area and sifting only.
  • This book uses u(0)=1 and u[n]=1 for n≥0. A different convention changes values at the jump but not the area or sifting result.
  • The scale rule uses |a|: δ(at)=δ(t)/|a|, so δ(−2t)=δ(t)/2, not −δ(t)/2.
  • When differentiating x(t)u(t), apply the product rule; the δ(t) term comes from the jump at t=0, not from the derivative of x(t).
  • In DT, δ[n] is 1 only at n=0; it is not a sampled δ(t), and u[n]−u[n−1] gives δ[n].

1.4 Operations on signals: shifting, scaling, reversal

A signal's shape can be moved along the time axis, squeezed or stretched, and flipped left-to-right — all by changing the independent variable inside the bracket. These three edits are shifting, scaling and reversal. Every delay line, playback-speed change and time-reversed echo is one of them. They also explain why the delay inside x(2t − 1) is 0.5 s, not 1 s. Reading the order correctly earns easy marks and gives correct code in a digital filter.

You need Chapter 1.1 for the notation x(t) and x[n], and Chapter 1.3 for the pulse and step shapes used here.

Shift: slide the signal along the time axis

Write y(t) = x(t − t₀). Here t₀ is the shift, in seconds. A feature that sits at time t₁ in x(t) sits at t₁ + t₀ in y(t).

For t₀ > 0 every feature moves right, so the signal is delayed. For t₀ < 0 it moves left, so the signal is advanced. A train announcement recorded at 9:00 and played 3 s later is x(t − 3). Only the position changes, never the shape.

Discrete time uses the same rule with an integer shift: y[n] = x[n − n₀], with n₀ a whole number. Samples keep their values and spacing; only their positions change. n₀ must be an integer because x[n] exists only at integer n, so x[n − 0.5] is not defined.

Shifting models every delay: an audio echo, a satellite hop, the queue buffer inside a router, a UPI confirmation that lands two seconds late.

Scale: squeeze or stretch the time axis

Write y(t) = x(at), with a a nonzero real number. The value that x had at t₁ now appears at t = t₁/a.

y(t) = x(at)  →  a feature at t₁ moves to t₁ / a

a is the scale factor; a > 1 compresses time (faster, shorter signal), 0 < a < 1 stretches time (slower, longer signal), and a < 0 combines scaling with reversal.

A four-minute song played at double speed is x(2t); it finishes in two minutes. A pulse of width 2 s becomes a pulse of width 2/a s.

Discrete time needs an integer index, so scaling splits in two. y[n] = x[Mn] with M a positive integer keeps every M-th sample — downsampling, or decimation — and the record becomes M times shorter. Upsampling inserts M − 1 zeros between samples and stretches the record M times. x[Mn] discards samples and can fold high frequencies downwards (Unit 5).

Reverse: mirror about t = 0

Write y(t) = x(−t). Every feature at t₁ moves to −t₁. A pulse on 0 to 2 s becomes the same pulse on −2 to 0 s, with the same height 2.

x(−t) flips the time axis, not the amplitude. The vertical flip is −x(t), which turns a positive pulse into a negative one. Reversal answers 'when', inversion answers 'how much'.

Reversal appears in playing a recording backwards, in the even part (x(t) + x(−t))/2 from Chapter 1.2, and in correlation, where one signal is reversed before sliding past the other (Unit 2).

Combined operations: choose one order and keep it

A general inside form is x(at − b). Both recipes below are correct; mixing them is the usual mistake.

  1. Scale first, then shift. x(at − b) = x(a(t − b/a)). Apply x(at), then delay the result by b/a; if b/a > 0 the move is to the right.
  2. Shift first, then scale. Start from x(t − b), which is x delayed by b seconds, then replace t by at to get x(at − b). The shift stays b, then the whole picture is compressed by a.
  3. Check with the edges. If x is nonzero for A ≤ argument ≤ B, solve A ≤ at − b ≤ B for t.

Both recipes agree when each is followed fully. For x(2t − 1): scale first gives x(2t), then a right shift of 0.5 s; shift first gives a 1 s delay, then compression by 2. The delay in seconds is b/a = 0.5 s, not 1 s.

Worked example

Question. A continuous-time signal is x(t) = 2 for 0 ≤ t ≤ 2 s and x(t) = 0 elsewhere. (a) Find the nonzero time interval and the amplitude of y(t) = x(2t − 1). (b) Find the nonzero time interval and the amplitude of z(t) = x(−t + 1). (c) Sketch both against x(t).

  1. y is nonzero only when the argument lies inside the pulse: 0 ≤ 2t − 1 ≤ 2.
  2. Add 1 to all three parts: 1 ≤ 2t ≤ 3. Divide by 2: 0.5 ≤ t ≤ 1.5 s.
  3. The height is copied, so y(t) = 2 on this interval and 0 outside.
  4. Check: x(2t − 1) = x(2(t − 0.5)) — compress 0–2 s to 0–1 s, then shift right 0.5 s. Agrees.
  5. For z the argument is −t + 1: 0 ≤ −t + 1 ≤ 2 gives −1 ≤ −t ≤ 1, and flipping both inequalities gives −1 ≤ t ≤ 1 s.
  6. z(t) = 2 on −1 ≤ t ≤ 1 s and 0 outside.
  7. Check: x(−t + 1) = x(−(t − 1)) — reverse to −2 to 0 s, then shift right 1 s. Agrees.
  8. Sketch: x(t) is a height-2 rectangle of width 2 s from 0 to 2; y(t) has width 1 s from 0.5 to 1.5 s; z(t) has width 2 s from −1 to 1 s.

Answer. y(t) = 2 on 0.5 ≤ t ≤ 1.5 s, and z(t) = 2 on −1 ≤ t ≤ 1 s; both are zero outside, with amplitude 2.

Exam traps

  • x(2t − 1) does not mean a delay of 1 s. Shifting x right by 1 s gives x(t − 1); replacing t by 2t then gives x(2t − 1), which is the same waveform, but the compression shrinks the 1 s delay to 0.5 s. Compressing first by 2 and then shifting right by 0.5 s gives the same x(2t − 1). Quoting the delay as 1 s is the mistake.
  • In x(at − b) the delay is b/a seconds, not b.
  • x(−t) is not −x(t). The first mirrors the signal in time, the second flips it upside down. A pulse on 0 to 2 s becomes a pulse on −2 to 0 s, not a dip on 0 to 2 s.
  • For discrete time, the shift n₀ must be an integer and the scale M must be a positive integer. x[2n] keeps samples 0, 2, 4, … and is not interpolation; zero insertion is the separate upsampling step.
  • Sign slip on shifts: x(t + 3) moves the signal 3 s to the left, because the feature at t₁ now occurs at t₁ − 3.

1.5 Classification of systems: CT and DT systems

A system is an operator that turns an input signal into an output signal. Write it as y(t)=T{x(t)} in continuous time and y[n]=T{x[n]} in discrete time. The same idea covers an RC filter, a car suspension and a monthly savings balance. Engineers classify systems by memory, causality, stability, linearity, time invariance and invertibility because each label predicts how the system will behave in analysis and design. This chapter sets out those labels and shows how blocks connect in series and parallel.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for CT and DT signals, and Chapter 1.3 for the unit step used in the example.

A system maps input to output

A system is any rule, device or process that acts on an input signal and produces an output signal. Write the action as an operator T. For a continuous-time (CT) input x(t), the output is y(t)=T{x(t)}. For a discrete-time (DT) input x[n], the output is y[n]=T{x[n]}. Here t is real time in seconds; n is an integer sample index with no unit. The set of allowed inputs is the input set, and the set of resulting outputs is the output set.

The label CT or DT comes from the independent variable of the signals, not from the hardware. An RC low-pass circuit with voltage vᵢ(t) in and vₒ(t) out is CT. A car suspension with road height as input and body height as output is CT. The on/off command sequence for a room heater, recorded once per minute, is DT. A bank balance updated once per month, with balance B[n], deposits d[n] and interest rate r, is DT: B[n]=B[n−1]+d[n]+rB[n−1].

A computer can simulate a CT model by taking small time steps. The model is still CT because its signals are defined for every real t. This distinction matters when you sample a CT signal for processing; see Unit 5, Sampling and Discrete-Time Systems.

Series and parallel connection

Large systems are built from smaller blocks. Two connections appear everywhere. In a series or cascade connection, the output of the first system feeds the second: y(t)=T₂{T₁{x(t)}}. In a parallel connection, the same input drives both blocks and the outputs add: y(t)=T₁{x(t)}+T₂{x(t)}.

The DT forms are identical with square brackets: y[n]=T₂{T₁{x[n]}} for series, and y[n]=T₁{x[n]}+T₂{x[n]} for parallel. The order matters in series. A filter followed by an amplifier is not the same as the amplifier followed by the filter when the amplifier clips.

An audio chain is a series connection: microphone, equaliser, amplifier, speaker. A stereo mix is a parallel connection: left and right channels add into a sum. In a control loop the blocks also connect in feedback, but that needs the transfer-function tools of Unit 4.

When blocks are linear and time-invariant, let h₁(t) and h₂(t) be their impulse responses. A series connection has overall impulse response h₁(t)*h₂(t), a convolution; in DT use h₁[n]*h₂[n]. A parallel connection has h₁(t)+h₂(t), or h₁[n]+h₂[n] in DT. In the Laplace domain these become the product H₁(s)H₂(s) and the sum H₁(s)+H₂(s); in the z domain use H₁(z)H₂(z) and H₁(z)+H₂(z). Unit 2 covers convolution.

Six labels used to classify systems

Classification answers practical questions before any calculation. The labels below are independent: a system can be, for example, causal and nonlinear.

  • Memoryless (static) or with memory (dynamic). A memoryless system uses only the present input. y(t)=3x(t)+2 is memoryless. A system with memory uses past or future inputs, or past outputs. y[n]=x[n]−x[n−1] has memory because it needs x[n−1].
  • Causal or noncausal. A causal system output at any time depends only on present and past inputs, never future inputs. y[n]=x[n]+x[n+1] is noncausal because it looks one step ahead. All real-time physical systems are causal; stored data can be processed noncausally.
  • Stable or unstable. A system is bounded-input bounded-output (BIBO) stable if every bounded input gives a bounded output. An audio amplifier with a fixed gain is stable; a microphone too close to its own speaker can become unstable.
  • Linear or nonlinear. A system is linear if it obeys superposition: T{a x₁+b x₂}=a T{x₁}+b T{x₂} for any inputs x₁ and x₂ and constants a and b. y(t)=3x(t)+2 is nonlinear because of the +2. y[n]=x[n]−x[n−1] is linear.
  • Time-invariant or time-varying. A time-invariant system does not change its rule with time: a shift in the input produces the same shift in the output. A resistor is time-invariant; a circuit switched on at t=0 may be time-varying during start-up.
  • Invertible or noninvertible. A system is invertible if different inputs always give different outputs, so an inverse system can recover the input. y(t)=3x(t) is invertible with inverse x(t)=y(t)/3. y[n]=x[n]² is not invertible over all real inputs because x and −x give the same output.

The formal tests for linearity, time invariance, causality and stability are in Chapter 1.6. Here the aim is the meaning, not the test routine. Some syllabi call this system classification and speak of a system as an interconnection of elements.

Reading a system equation

To classify a system from its equation, ask three questions in order. First, is the independent variable t or n? That fixes CT or DT. Second, does the output at an index use any other index? If yes, the system has memory; if no, it is memoryless. Third, does the output use a future index? If yes, it is noncausal.

  1. Write the output for a general input, not for one numerical input.
  2. List every input value that appears on the right-hand side.
  3. If only plain x(t) or plain x[n] appears, the system is memoryless. Any argument other than plain t or plain n — a shift x(t−a), an advance x[n+k], or a scale x(2t), x[2n] — means the system has memory. Scaling or advancing the index also makes the system noncausal.
  4. If x(t−a), x[n−k] or x[n+k] appears, it has memory; x[n+k] with k>0 also makes it noncausal.
  5. Check any added constant or product of inputs: either can make the system nonlinear.

A CT model is used for a physical device described by differential equations. A DT model is used for software, sampled data and sequences. A single system, such as a digitally controlled motor, may need both: a CT model of the motor and a DT controller. Sampling links the two in Unit 5.

Worked example

Question. A CT system is y(t)=3x(t)+2 and a DT system is y[n]=x[n]−x[n−1]. Classify each as CT or DT, and as memoryless or with memory. For x(t)=u(t), find y(1); for x[n]=n u[n], find y[2]. Justify each label in one line.

  1. Read the independent variable. The first system uses t, so it is CT. The second uses n, so it is DT.
  2. Check memory. In y(t)=3x(t)+2 the output uses only x(t), so the CT system is memoryless. In y[n]=x[n]−x[n−1] the output uses x[n−1], so the DT system has memory.
  3. For the CT input x(t)=u(t), at t=1 the unit step value is x(1)=u(1)=1 because 1 is positive.
  4. Substitute: y(1)=3×1+2=5.
  5. For the DT input x[n]=n u[n], x[2]=2u[2]=2×1=2 and x[1]=1u[1]=1×1=1.
  6. Substitute: y[2]=x[2]−x[1]=2−1=1.

Answer. The CT system is memoryless with y(1)=5, and the DT system has memory with y[2]=1, in the units of the signals.

Exam traps

  • CT and DT describe the independent variable. A digital computer that simulates a CT model does not make the model DT.
  • Causal is not the same as memoryless. y[n]=x[n−1] is causal but has memory; y[n]=x[n+1] is noncausal.
  • A constant term breaks linearity. y(t)=3x(t)+2 is nonlinear and time-invariant, not linear.
  • Stability and time invariance are independent. A system can be time-invariant and unstable, for example y[n]=2y[n−1]+x[n], an accumulator whose loop gain 2 makes the output grow without bound.
  • Squaring is not a small detail: y[n]=x[n]² is nonlinear over real inputs and not invertible, because x and −x give the same output.

1.6 System properties: linearity, time invariance, causality, stability

A system is judged by four separate questions. Does it obey superposition (linearity)? Does a delayed input give only a delayed output (time invariance)? Does the output at an instant depend only on past and present input (causality)? Does every bounded input give a bounded output (BIBO stability)? Each question has a short definition and is answered with a counterexample when the system fails. These four tests decide whether the heavy tools of Unit 2 and Unit 4 apply, so they are asked early in every signals and systems exam.

You need the system operator idea and CT/DT notation from Chapter 1.1, and the impulse response symbol h(t) or h[n] from Chapter 1.3.

Linearity: additivity plus scaling

Write the system as y = T{x}, where T is the operator, x is the input signal and y is the output signal. The system is linear when two inputs together give two outputs together, with constants kept outside.

T{a x₁ + b x₂} = a T{x₁} + b T{x₂}

x₁ and x₂ are any two input signals; a and b are any constants (real or complex); T{x₁} and T{x₂} are the outputs for those inputs.

The formula packs two rules. Set a = b = 1 to get additivity: T{x₁ + x₂} = T{x₁} + T{x₂}. Set b = 0 to get scaling (homogeneity): T{a x₁} = a T{x₁}. A system must pass both to be linear.

Linearity exists so that a complicated input can be split into simple pieces, each piece pushed through the system, and the outputs added. The impulse, step and exponential of Chapter 1.3 are exactly such pieces.

A quick test: a linear system must give zero output for zero input, because T{0} = T{0·x} = 0·T{x} = 0. So y(t) = x(t) + 2 is not linear, while y(t) = t x(t) is linear even though its coefficient changes with time.

Time invariance and causality

Time invariance is about shifts, not about the system ageing. Delay the input by t₀ seconds and the whole output must delay by the same t₀, unchanged in shape.

x(t − t₀) → y(t − t₀)   for every t₀;   x[n − n₀] → y[n − n₀] for every integer n₀

t₀ is the delay in seconds for continuous time (CT); n₀ is the delay in samples for discrete time (DT); y is the output produced by x.

  1. Take a test input x and find the output y = T{x}.
  2. Form the shifted input x(t − t₀) and compute T{x(t − t₀)}.
  3. Shift the original output to get y(t − t₀).
  4. Compare the two results symbolically. Equal for all t₀ means time-invariant; one t₀ with unequal results is a counterexample.

Causality: the output at a chosen instant t₀ may depend only on input values at times t ≤ t₀, never on future input. A system that needs the next 5 ms of input is non-causal. Live systems such as a phone call or a motor controller must be causal. Recorded-data work, such as smoothing a stored ECG file, is allowed to be non-causal.

For an LTI system, causality and BIBO stability both reduce to tests on the impulse response h(t). Causality holds if and only if h(t) = 0 for t < 0 (h[n] = 0 for n < 0 in DT); the stability test comes in Section 3. Convolution and these LTI tests are developed in Unit 2.

BIBO stability

BIBO means bounded-input bounded-output. The system is BIBO stable when every input that never exceeds some finite size produces an output that also never exceeds some finite size.

if |x(t)| ≤ Mₓ for all t, then |y(t)| ≤ M_y for all t

Mₓ is a finite bound on the input magnitude; M_y is a finite bound on the output magnitude; the same statement with n in place of t covers DT.

For an LTI system the test moves onto h alone.

The absolute value matters. A bounded input can be shaped to match the sign of h at every instant, so the output can reach the full sum of magnitudes. If that sum is infinite, one bounded input drives the output to infinity. Example: h(t) = u(t) (a pure integrator) is causal but not stable, since ∫|h(t)|dt over all time is infinite.

The four tests are independent. A system can be linear and unstable, causal and time-varying, or stable and non-causal. Answer them one by one.

Exam traps

1.7 Signals as vectors: orthogonal signal space

A signal defined on an interval behaves like a vector: every instant of time is one component. Two tools carry over from vector algebra. The inner product measures how much two signals resemble each other, and the norm measures a signal's size, which here is its energy. When the inner product is zero the signals are orthogonal, and a complete orthogonal set acts as a coordinate system. Each coefficient is a single inner product, and Parseval's relation rebuilds the total energy from those coefficients. Fourier series, QAM and OFDM modems, and image compression all rest on this picture.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for signal notation and Chapter 1.2 for the energy of a signal.

Inner product: similarity as one number

A vector v = (v₁, v₂, …, v_N) has N components, and its dot product with another vector is a sum of component-by-component products. A signal has one component for every instant of time. Replace the sum by an integral and the dot product becomes the inner product.

⟨x, y⟩ = ∫ₐᵇ x(t) y*(t) dt

a and b are the interval limits, in seconds; y*(t) is the complex conjugate of y(t), obtained by flipping the sign of its imaginary part; for real signals y*(t) = y(t).

⟨x, y⟩ = Σ x[n] y*[n]

the sum runs over the sample indices in the chosen range; n is the integer sample index; this is the discrete-time version.

The inner product is a single number. A large value means the two signals rise and fall together on that interval; a value near zero means they share no common pattern there.

‖x‖ = √⟨x, x⟩ = √(∫ₐᵇ |x(t)|² dt)

‖x‖ is the norm of x; |x(t)|² is the squared magnitude; ‖x‖² is exactly the energy of x on [a, b], so the norm is the square root of energy.

The distance between two signals is ‖x − y‖, the square root of the energy of the error signal. Two signals are orthogonal when ⟨x, y⟩ = 0.

  • ⟨x, y⟩ = ⟨y, x⟩* and ⟨x, x⟩ is real and never negative.
  • The inner product is linear in its first slot: ⟨ax₁ + bx₂, y⟩ = a⟨x₁, y⟩ + b⟨x₂, y⟩.
  • ⟨x, x⟩ = 0 only for a signal that is zero almost everywhere.

Orthogonal sets and the coefficient formula

A set of signals φ₁, φ₂, … is orthogonal on an interval when ⟨φ_m, φ_n⟩ = 0 for every m ≠ n. It is orthonormal when each signal also has unit norm, ‖φ_n‖ = 1; then ⟨φ_m, φ_n⟩ = δ_mn, which equals 1 when m = n and 0 otherwise.

Orthogonality is what makes coefficients easy. Write x = Σ_n c_n φ_n and take the inner product of both sides with φ_m. Every term with n ≠ m gives zero, so one term survives and the coefficient falls out.

c_n = ⟨x, φ_n⟩ / ‖φ_n‖²

c_n is the coefficient of φ_n in the expansion of x; the numerator is one inner product; the denominator is the energy of that basis signal. For an orthonormal set the denominator is 1 and c_n = ⟨x, φ_n⟩.

  1. Fix the interval; orthogonality is meaningless without it.
  2. Check the set is orthogonal on that interval.
  3. Compute each ‖φ_n‖² by integrating the squared basis signal.
  4. Compute each inner product ⟨x, φ_n⟩.
  5. Divide to get c_n, then sum Σ c_n φ_n to rebuild x.

A complete set leaves nothing behind: no nonzero signal is orthogonal to every φ_n. With a complete set the sum Σ c_n φ_n reproduces x in energy, and the residual x − Σ c_n φ_n has zero norm. With an incomplete set the residual is a genuine signal, orthogonal to the whole set, that the basis simply cannot describe.

Parseval: energy from the coefficients

Energy is the inner product of a signal with itself. Substitute the expansion and expand: the cross terms ⟨φ_m, φ_n⟩ with m ≠ n all vanish, leaving only the diagonal terms.

∫ₐᵇ |x(t)|² dt = Σₙ |c_n|² ‖φ_n‖²

the left side is the total energy of x on [a, b]; |c_n|²‖φ_n‖² is the energy carried by the n-th component; the discrete-time form replaces the integral by Σ |x[n]|².

So the energy splits cleanly among the basis signals, with no cross-energy terms. Each coefficient squared, times the energy of its basis signal, is that component's share. This is the same statement as Pythagoras' theorem in a right triangle.

Worked example

Question. On the interval 0 ≤ t ≤ 1, take φ₁(t) = 1 and φ₂(t) = 2t − 1. (a) Show they are orthogonal. (b) Find ‖φ₁‖² and ‖φ₂‖². (c) For x(t) = 3φ₁(t) + 4φ₂(t), find the energy using Parseval, and verify by direct integration.

  1. Interval: [0, 1]. Basis signals: φ₁(t) = 1, φ₂(t) = 2t − 1.
  2. (a) ⟨φ₁, φ₂⟩ = ∫₀¹ 1·(2t − 1) dt = [t² − t]₀¹ = (1 − 1) − (0 − 0) = 0. Nonzero signals with zero inner product, so they are orthogonal on [0, 1].
  3. (b) ‖φ₁‖² = ∫₀¹ 1² dt = [t]₀¹ = 1.
  4. ‖φ₂‖² = ∫₀¹ (2t − 1)² dt = ∫₀¹ (4t² − 4t + 1) dt = [4t³/3 − 2t² + t]₀¹ = 4/3 − 2 + 1 = 1/3.
  5. (c) Since x is already written in the basis, c₁ = 3 and c₂ = 4. Parseval: energy = c₁²‖φ₁‖² + c₂²‖φ₂‖² = 9(1) + 16(1/3) = 9 + 16/3 = 43/3 ≈ 14.33.
  6. Check directly: x(t) = 3 + 4(2t − 1) = 8t − 1, so ∫₀¹ (8t − 1)² dt = ∫₀¹ (64t² − 16t + 1) dt = 64/3 − 8 + 1 = 64/3 − 7 = 43/3. Both routes agree.

Answer. φ₁ and φ₂ are orthogonal on [0, 1]; ‖φ₂‖² = 1/3; the energy of x(t) = 3φ₁ + 4φ₂ is 43/3 ≈ 14.33 energy units.

Exam traps

  • Orthogonality depends on the interval. φ₁(t) = 1 and φ₂(t) = 2t − 1 give inner product 0 on [0, 1] but ∫₀²(2t − 1)dt = 2 on [0, 2], so they are not orthogonal there.
  • For complex signals use the conjugate: ⟨x, y⟩ = ∫x(t)y*(t)dt, not ∫x(t)y(t)dt. Dropping the star gives a different number and can make a self inner product complex.
  • Divide by ‖φ_n‖² whenever the set is not orthonormal. In the worked example ‖φ₂‖² = 1/3, so a coefficient computed as ⟨x, φ₂⟩ alone is 3 times too small.
  • Orthogonality is a property of a pair of signals on an interval, not of one signal alone; a single function is never 'orthogonal' without a partner.
  • Zero energy means the zero signal, not a zero inner product: ⟨x, y⟩ = 0 does not imply x = 0 or y = 0.

Taking ⟨x, φ_m⟩ with x = Σc_nφ_n leaves c_m⟨φ_m, φ_m⟩ = c_m‖φ_m‖². Energy appears squared, so the division is by ‖φ_m‖². If the set is orthonormal, ‖φ_m‖² = 1 and no division is visible.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. In an OFDM modem the subcarriers are made orthogonal over one symbol period, so each subcarrier's data is recovered by one inner product at the receiver. QAM constellations are points in a two-dimensional signal space built from two orthonormal waveforms. Compressed sensing goes further and asks how few inner products are needed to recover a sparse signal. Wavelets replace the single sinusoid basis with scaled and shifted versions that stay orthogonal, which is why JPEG 2000 works. Search for 'signal space representation', 'Gram–Schmidt for signals' and 'orthogonal frequency division multiplexing' to go deeper.

Takeaways

  1. A signal is a function of an independent variable that carries information, and a system maps input signals to output signals.
  2. Continuous-time signals use real t and discrete-time signals use integer n; a sample is x[n]=x(nTₛ).
  3. Energy and power classify signals by finite energy or finite nonzero average power, while periodic, even, and odd properties simplify later work.
  4. The impulse, step, ramp, and exponential are standard building blocks and common test inputs for systems.
  5. Operations on the independent variable change the graph: shifting moves it, scaling compresses or stretches it, and reversal flips it.
  6. A system is CT or DT by the variable of its input and output, and its properties must be tested from definitions.
  7. Linearity, time invariance, causality, and BIBO stability are separate tests; one does not automatically imply another.
  8. Signals on an interval behave like vectors: inner products measure similarity and orthogonal sets form a basis.
  9. Parseval's relation gives signal energy from the coefficients of an orthogonal expansion.
