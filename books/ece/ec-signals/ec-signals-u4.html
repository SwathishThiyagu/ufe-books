4.1 Laplace transform and region of convergence

The Laplace transform turns a time signal x(t) into a function X(s) of complex frequency s=σ+jω. It multiplies x(t) by a damping factor e⁻ˢᵗ before integrating, so signals that grow too fast for the Fourier transform can still be handled. The integral converges only for some s values; that set is the region of convergence, ROC. Engineers use it to solve circuits and control problems with differential equations, to read stability from poles, and to design filters. The ROC is as important as X(s): it tells which time signal you actually have.

You need: Unit 1 standard signals u(t) and exponentials; Unit 3 Fourier transform for the damping idea; properties come later in Chapter 4.2.

The transform and complex frequency

Start with a continuous-time signal x(t). The one-sided Laplace transform is defined for t≥0. It is written X(s).

X(s) = ∫₀∞ x(t)e−stdt

x(t) is the time signal; s=σ+jω is complex frequency; σ is damping in s⁻¹; ω is angular frequency in rad/s; X(s) is the transform.

Write e−st=e−σte−jωt. The factor e−jωt rotates the signal at frequency ω. The factor e−σt damps or grows it. The Fourier transform is the special case σ=0. For a causal signal (x(t)=0 for t<0), X(jω)=X(s) at s=jω when the ROC includes the jω axis. The extra σ is why the Laplace transform can handle e2tu(t), which has no ordinary Fourier transform.

A bilateral form integrates from −∞ to ∞: X(s)=∫−∞∞ x(t)e−stdt. The one-sided form is used for causal signals, which are zero for t<0. The left-sided and two-sided examples below state that they use the bilateral form.

Convergence and the region of convergence

The integral is improper. It converges when the limit at ∞ is finite. Test with x(t)=e−atu(t), where a is real. Some syllabi call the convergence set the region of convergence.

X(s)=∫₀<sup>∞</sup> e<sup>−(s+a)t</sup>dt = 1/(s+a)

a is a real decay rate in s⁻¹; the result holds when Re(s+a)>0, that is Re(s)>−a. Re(s) means the real part σ.

That inequality is the ROC. For a=2, X(s)=1/(s+2) and ROC Re(s)>−2. The pole is at s=−2. The ROC is an open half-plane to the right of the pole. It never contains the pole: at s=−2 the integrand becomes e0t=1 and the integral diverges.

A left-sided signal gives the same algebra with a different region. Using the bilateral transform, take x(t)=−e−atu(−t). Then X(s)=1/(s+a), but the integral converges for Re(s)<−a. The minus sign makes the two time signals different. For a two-sided signal such as x(t)=e−2tu(t)+e3tu(−t), X(s)=1/(s+2)−1/(s−3) and ROC is −2<Re(s)<3.

For the bilateral transform, poles alone do not fix the signal. X(s)=1/(s+2) can come from e−2tu(t) with ROC Re(s)>−2, or from −e−2tu(−t) with ROC Re(s)<−2. The ROC chooses the side. A right-sided signal has ROC to the right of its rightmost pole. A left-sided signal has ROC to the left of its leftmost pole. A two-sided signal has a strip between poles.

Reading a transform with its ROC

The table's left-sided and two-sided rows use the bilateral transform.

SignalX(s)ROC
e−2tu(t)1/(s+2)Re(s)>−2

e−2tu(t)+e3tu(−t)1/(s+2)−1/(s−3)−2<Re(s)<3

Check each row by the integral. For row 1, the exponential decays for t≥0. For row 2, the signal is nonzero for t≤0 and the minus sign is part of x(t). For row 3, the first term needs Re(s)>−2 and the second needs Re(s)<3; both must hold, so the ROC is the overlap.

When you meet X(s), factor the denominator to find poles. Mark poles on the s-plane: σ is horizontal, jω is vertical. Use the stated ROC to choose left or right sides. The ROC cannot cross a pole. For a causal system, the ROC is the half-plane to the right of the rightmost pole; stability needs the jω axis inside the ROC. System use is in Chapter 4.4.

Worked example

Question. Find the Laplace transform and ROC of x(t)=e−2tu(t). State the pole location.

  1. The unit step u(t) is 0 for t<0 and 1 for t≥0. Thus x(t)=e−2t for t≥0 and x(t)=0 for t<0.
  2. Write the one-sided transform: X(s)=∫₀∞ e−2te−stdt.
  3. Combine exponents: e−2te−st=e−(s+2)t.
  4. Integrate: ∫₀∞ e−(s+2)tdt = 1/(s+2), provided Re(s+2)>0.
  5. So Re(s)>−2. The pole is s=−2. The ROC is Re(s)>−2, so the pole is on the boundary and not inside.

Answer. X(s)=1/(s+2), pole at s=−2, and ROC Re(s)>−2.

Exam traps

  • Writing X(s)=1/(s+2) but omitting Re(s)>−2: the transform alone does not identify the time signal.
  • Putting the pole s=−2 inside the ROC: the ROC is always open and never contains a pole.
  • Using the right-sided ROC for a left-sided signal: −e−2tu(−t) shares 1/(s+2) but has Re(s)<−2.
  • Treating s as jω only: s=σ+jω; the Fourier transform is the special case σ=0 and only when the ROC includes the jω axis.
  • Adding transforms without intersecting their ROCs; for a two-sided sum use the bilateral transform and take the overlap strip.

4.2 Properties of the Laplace transform

The Laplace transform properties are shortcuts. Instead of integrating from zero to infinity every time, you reuse a known pair and change it: add signals, shift time, shift s, differentiate, integrate, or convolve. Each rule also states how the region of convergence (ROC) changes, because the ROC decides which time signal belongs to X(s). These rules build X(s) for e^(−3t)cos(4t)u(t) in one line, and they work backwards from X(s) to x(t). Circuit analysis, control loops and filter design all use them.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for X(s)=∫x(t)e^(−st)dt and the ROC, and the cosine pair from Unit 3, Fourier Series and Fourier Transform.

Rules that shift and scale

Write the one-sided Laplace transform as X(s)=∫0−∞x(t)e−stdt. The lower limit 0− includes an impulse at t=0. s is a complex variable, s=σ+jω. The ROC is the set of s values where the integral converges.

L{a x₁(t)+b x₂(t)} = a X₁(s)+b X₂(s)

L{} means Laplace transform; x₁(t), x₂(t) are signals with transforms X₁(s), X₂(s); a, b are real or complex constants. The ROC contains the overlap of the two individual ROCs.

L{x(t−t₀)u(t−t₀)} = e^(−s t₀) X(s)

t₀>0 is a delay in seconds, u(t) is the unit step, and X(s) is the transform of x(t)u(t). The ROC is unchanged. The factor e^(−s t₀) is a pure time delay.

L{e^(s₀t)x(t)} = X(s−s₀)

s₀ is a complex shift. The new ROC is the old ROC shifted by Re(s₀). Some syllabi call this the frequency-shifting property. For s₀=−3, replace s by s+3.

L{x(at)} = (1/a) X(s/a), a>0

For the one-sided transform, take a>0 only. a is the time-scaling constant; time is compressed by a when a>1. The ROC is scaled so that s/a lies in the old ROC. The 1/|a| form with both signs of a is valid only for the bilateral transform, or when time reversal is handled separately.

Calculus rules and convolution

Differentiation in time becomes multiplication by s, but the one-sided transform must subtract the initial value. The bilateral transform, with no starting point, uses sX(s) alone.

L{dx/dt} = sX(s) − x(0−)

x(0−) is the value just before t=0. For the bilateral transform the term x(0−) is absent. The ROC contains the original ROC.

L{d²x/dt²} = s²X(s) − s x(0−) − x′(0−)

x′(0−) is the derivative just before t=0. Each extra derivative adds one more initial-condition term.

L{t x(t)} = −dX(s)/ds

dX/ds is the derivative of X with respect to complex s. The ROC is unchanged, though boundary points may move. The minus sign is easy to lose.

L{∫0−tx(τ)dτ} = X(s)/s

τ is a dummy time variable. The ROC is the old ROC intersected with Re(s)>0, because 1/s has a pole at s=0.

L{x₁(t)*x₂(t)} = X₁(s) X₂(s)

* is convolution, x₁(t)*x₂(t)=∫x₁(τ)x₂(t−τ)dτ. The ROC contains the overlap of the two individual ROCs.

Initial value, final value, and reverse use

Two limit theorems read time-domain endpoints from X(s) without inversion. They only work when the stated conditions hold.

x(0+) = lims→∞ sX(s)

x(0+) is the value just after t=0. The limit must exist. If x(t) has an impulse at t=0, use the unilateral form carefully.

x(∞) = lims→0 sX(s)

x(∞) is the steady final value. All poles of sX(s) must lie in the left half-plane, Re(s)<0, except possibly a simple pole at s=0. Otherwise the final value does not exist.

Use the rules backwards too. A factor e^(−2s) in X(s) delays x(t) by 2 seconds. A denominator (s+3) means a factor e^(−3t) in time. A factor 1/s means integration. For a rational X(s), partial fractions in Chapter 4.3 finish the inversion.

Worked example

Question. Find the Laplace transform of x(t)=e^(−3t)cos(4t)u(t). State the ROC. Use the standard pair L{cos(4t)u(t)}=s/(s²+16) with ROC Re(s)>0.

  1. Start with the cosine pair: X₀(s)=s/(s²+16). Its ROC is R₀: Re(s)>0.
  2. Identify the multiplier e^(−3t), so s₀=−3 in the s-shift rule L{e^(s₀t)x₀(t)}=X₀(s−s₀).
  3. Replace s by s−(−3)=s+3: X(s)=(s+3)/((s+3)²+16).
  4. Expand the denominator: (s+3)²+16=s²+6s+9+16=s²+6s+25. So X(s)=(s+3)/(s²+6s+25).
  5. Shift the ROC by Re(s₀)=−3: Re(s)>0−3, so Re(s)>−3.

Exam traps

  • Using s→s−3 for e^(−3t) instead of s→s+3. The rule is s→s−s₀ with s₀=−3, so the plus sign is correct.
  • Dropping the initial-condition term in a unilateral derivative: L{dx/dt}=sX(s)−x(0−), not sX(s) alone.
  • Forgetting that s-shift moves the ROC while time shift leaves it unchanged. A factor e^(−3t) shifts the ROC to Re(s)>−3 in the example.
  • Applying the final-value theorem when sX(s) has a pole in the right half-plane or on the imaginary axis. Then x(∞) does not settle to a finite constant.
  • Sign slip in the s-derivative: L{t x(t)}=−dX(s)/ds, not +dX/ds.

4.3 Inverse Laplace transform by partial fractions

A rational Laplace transform X(s) is a ratio of two polynomials. Partial fractions rewrite it as a sum of simple terms such as A/(s+a). Each simple term has a known inverse from the Laplace table. The region of convergence (ROC) then decides whether a term is right-sided, left-sided or two-sided. The method turns circuit and control models back into time functions without evaluating the Bromwich integral. It also exposes each natural mode and its coefficient.

You need: Laplace transform and ROC, properties of the Laplace transform, and the Unit 1 step and exponential signals.

Proper fractions and distinct real poles

Write a rational transform as X(s)=N(s)/D(s). N(s) is the numerator polynomial and D(s) is the denominator polynomial. The fraction is proper when the degree of N(s) is smaller than the degree of D(s). If it is improper, divide N(s) by D(s) first. Division gives a polynomial plus a proper remainder. Partial fractions apply only to the proper remainder.

For a proper X(s), factor D(s). A pole is a value of s that makes D(s)=0. For distinct real poles at s=−p₁, s=−p₂, …, s=−p_n, write X(s)=A₁/(s+p₁)+A₂/(s+p₂)+…+A_n/(s+p_n). The cover-up rule finds each coefficient. Equating coefficients in powers of s gives the same A_k and is a good check.

A_k = [(s+p_k) X(s)] at s = −p_k

A_k is the residue at the pole s=−p_k. Here p_k is the pole location with the sign flipped: the pole sits at s=−p_k, and p_k itself may be positive, negative or zero.

Once the terms are simple, each inverse comes from a table. The ROC is attached to X(s), not to the individual terms. Some syllabi call this partial-fraction expansion.

Repeated and complex-conjugate poles

If D(s) has a repeated factor (s+a)^r, include all powers from 1 to r. Write X(s)=A₁/(s+a)+A₂/(s+a)²+…+A_r/(s+a)^r+…. The highest coefficient is A_r=[(s+a)^r X(s)] at s=−a. Get the lower coefficients by differentiating that product or by equating coefficients. Missing a lower power is a common exam error.

1/(s+a) ↔ e^(−at)u(t),   1/(s+a)² ↔ t e^(−at)u(t),   1/(s+a)^n ↔ t^(n−1)e^(−at)u(t)/(n−1)!

a is a real number; u(t) is the unit step, u(t)=0 for t<0 and u(t)=1 for t≥0. These pairs are for a right-sided ROC.

For complex-conjugate poles, keep a quadratic term. If s²+bs+c has b²−4c<0, complete the square as (s+α)²+ω², where α=b/2 and ω=√(c−b²/4). Split the numerator into a multiple of (s+α) plus a constant times ω. For a right-sided ROC, the inverse terms are e^(−αt)cos(ωt)u(t) and e^(−αt)sin(ωt)u(t). Do not split complex poles into real first-order terms with real coefficients.

The ROC chooses the side

The ROC fixes the time side. For a pole at s=−a, 1/(s+a) gives e^(−at)u(t) when Re(s)>−a, and gives −e^(−at)u(−t) when Re(s)<−a. Here u(−t) is the reversed step: 1 for t≤0 and 0 for t>0. For 1/(s+a)², the right-sided inverse is t e^(−at)u(t), and the left-sided inverse is −t e^(−at)u(−t). If the ROC is a vertical strip between two poles, some terms are right-sided and some are left-sided. The same X(s) can describe a growing, decaying or two-sided signal. The algebra does not choose the side; the ROC does.

TermROCInverse
1/(s+a)Re(s) > −ae^(−at)u(t)
1/(s+a)Re(s) < −a−e^(−at)u(−t)
1/(s+a)²Re(s) > −at e^(−at)u(t)
1/(s+a)²Re(s) < −a−t e^(−at)u(−t)

The pole positions and the ROC also decide system stability; see Analysis of LTI systems.

Worked example

Question. Find x(t) for X(s)=(3s+5)/(s²+4s+3) with ROC Re(s)>−1. State the poles and the coefficient of each mode.

  1. Degrees: numerator degree 1, denominator degree 2. The fraction is proper, so no division is needed.
  2. Factor the denominator: s²+4s+3=(s+1)(s+3). Poles are at s=−1 and s=−3, both in s⁻¹.
  3. Set X(s)=A/(s+1)+B/(s+3).
  4. Cover-up for A: multiply by (s+1), so A=(3s+5)/(s+3) at s=−1. A=(−3+5)/(−1+3)=2/2=1.
  5. Cover-up for B: multiply by (s+3), so B=(3s+5)/(s+1) at s=−3. B=(−9+5)/(−3+1)=(−4)/(−2)=2.
  6. So X(s)=1/(s+1)+2/(s+3).
  7. The ROC Re(s)>−1 is to the right of both poles −1 and −3. Both terms are right-sided. Use 1/(s+a) ↔ e^(−at)u(t). First term a=1; second term a=3.
  8. Thus x(t)=e^(−t)u(t)+2e^(−3t)u(t)=(e^(−t)+2e^(−3t))u(t), with t in seconds and pole values in s⁻¹.

Answer. x(t)=(e^(−t)+2e^(−3t))u(t), with coefficients 1 and 2 and poles at −1 s⁻¹ and −3 s⁻¹.

Exam traps

  • Ignoring the ROC and always writing u(t). The same X(s) gives a different x(t) for a different ROC.
  • Using partial fractions on an improper fraction without dividing first. Divide N(s) by D(s), split only the proper remainder, and invert the polynomial part separately.
  • A sign slip in cover-up: use the factor (s+p_k), then evaluate at s=−p_k. The denominator often changes sign in this step.
  • For a repeated pole, writing only A/(s+a) and missing A₂/(s+a)². Include every power up to the multiplicity.
  • For complex poles, splitting into real first-order terms with real coefficients. Keep the quadratic term and complete the square.

4.4 Analysis of LTI systems: transfer function, poles and zeros

A transfer function turns an LTI circuit or control loop into one rational function H(s)=Y(s)/X(s). For initial rest, the Laplace transform of the impulse response gives H(s), so algebra replaces differential equations. Its poles and zeros show the shape of the response, and the ROC decides stability. Engineers use it to check whether an amplifier or motor controller settles or blows up before building it. Some syllabi call H(s) the network function.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for X(s) and ROC, Chapter 4.2 for convolution and differentiation, Unit 2 for impulse response and differential-equation models, and Chapter 4.3 for inverse transforms.

From impulse response to H(s)

An LTI system at initial rest has zero initial stored energy. Take the Laplace transform of y(t)=h(t)*x(t). The convolution property gives Y(s)=H(s)X(s). Divide: H(s)=Y(s)/X(s)=L{h(t)}. H(s) is the transfer function. Initial rest means x(t)=0 for t<0 and every initial condition is zero; without it, extra terms appear. To find H(s): write the differential equation; transform with zero initial conditions; solve for Y(s)/X(s); factor numerator and denominator. For a circuit, use impedance methods and treat L as sL and C as 1/(sC). If the ROC includes the jω axis, set s=jω to get the frequency response H(jω). Frequency-response plots are in Unit 3.

H(s) = Y(s)/X(s) = L{h(t)}

H(s) is the transfer function; Y(s) and X(s) are Laplace transforms of output and input; h(t) is the impulse response; L{·} is the Laplace transform.

Poles, zeros and the ROC

For a rational transfer function, write H(s)=N(s)/D(s). N(s) and D(s) are polynomials in s. A zero is a root of N(s)=0. A pole is a root of D(s)=0. Poles set natural modes such as e^(p t). Zeros set which inputs are blocked or attenuated. The ROC completes the description. For a causal system, the ROC is Re(s)>p_max, right of the rightmost pole p_max. The causal system is stable exactly when every pole is strictly in the left half-plane, Re(p)<0. Equivalently, the ROC includes the jω axis, Re(s)=0. A pole on the jω axis gives marginal behaviour: a bounded input can produce a sustained oscillation or a ramp. Repeated jω-axis poles grow. A denominator with all positive coefficients is necessary for left-half-plane roots but not sufficient. Check pole locations. Do not cancel a common factor before the stability check. In H(s)=(s−2)/((s+1)(s−2)), careless cancellation gives 1/(s+1), which looks stable. The pole at s=2 still exists and is excited whenever the input reaches that mode.

Step response and differential equations

For a unit step x(t)=u(t), X(s)=1/s. The step response transform is Y(s)=H(s)/s. Split Y(s) by partial fractions, then invert using Chapter 4.3. The result has a forced part from the step and natural parts from the poles. For y''(t)+3y'(t)+2y(t)=x(t) at initial rest, transform: (s²+3s+2)Y(s)=X(s). Hence H(s)=1/(s²+3s+2). The denominator roots −1 and −2 give modes e^(−t) and e^(−2t). The unit-step response is computed in the worked example below. The impulse response is h(t)=L⁻¹{H(s)}. For a causal stable system, h(t) decays to zero as t→∞. The unit-step response settles to H(0) when all poles of H(s) are strictly in the left half-plane.

Worked example

Question. For H(s)=1/(s²+3s+2), find the zeros, poles, stability and the unit-step response y(t). Assume a causal system at initial rest.

  1. Write H(s)=N(s)/D(s). Here N(s)=1 and D(s)=s²+3s+2.
  2. Find zeros: N(s)=1 has no finite root. So there are no finite zeros.
  3. Factor D(s)=s²+3s+2=(s+1)(s+2). Poles: s=−1 and s=−2.
  4. Causal ROC: Re(s)>−1, right of the rightmost pole. It includes the jω axis Re(s)=0. Both poles have negative real parts, so the system is stable.
  5. For unit step, X(s)=1/s. Y(s)=H(s)/s=1/[s(s+1)(s+2)].
  6. Partial fractions: 1/[s(s+1)(s+2)] = A/s + B/(s+1) + C/(s+2). A=1/[(1)(2)]=1/2. B=1/[(-1)(1)]=−1. C=1/[(-2)(-1)]=1/2.
  7. Invert: y(t)=(1/2 − e^(−t) + (1/2)e^(−2t))u(t). Check t=0: 1/2−1+1/2=0. As t→∞: 1/2.

Answer. No finite zeros; poles at s=−1 and s=−2; causal system is stable; y(t)=(1/2−e^(−t)+(1/2)e^(−2t))u(t).

Exam traps

  • Do not call a system stable just because all denominator coefficients are positive. That is only a necessary sign test, not a pole-location proof.
  • Do not ignore the ROC. H(s) alone does not fix stability; the ROC, usually from causality, does.
  • Do not cancel poles and zeros before checking stability. A cancelled unstable pole can still be excited.
  • A pole on the jω axis is marginal, not strictly stable. A simple pole pair at s=±jω with ω≠0 gives a sustained bounded oscillation. A simple pole at s=0 gives a constant mode, and a step input produces a ramp (unbounded). Repeated jω-axis poles grow.
  • Zeros are roots of N(s), not D(s); a zero does not create a natural mode.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Control engineers use H(s) poles to design closed-loop controllers: root locus moves poles as gain changes, and Bode and Nyquist plots check margins. Circuit simulators such as SPICE compute small-signal transfer functions from the same N(s)/D(s). Search terms: pole-zero placement, Routh–Hurwitz criterion, root locus, Nyquist stability criterion, loop gain.

Takeaways

  1. The Laplace transform turns a differential equation in t into an algebraic equation in s, which is why it is used for circuits and control.
  2. X(s) alone is not the answer: the ROC must be stated, because one X(s) can belong to two different time signals.
  3. A pole on the jω axis means a sustained oscillation; a pole in the right half-plane means a growing signal.
  4. Causality and stability are separate questions; a causal system is stable only if all its poles lie in the left half of the s-plane.
  5. Partial fractions plus a table of standard pairs is the practical way to invert X(s), and the ROC fixes the sign and side of each term.
  6. The initial and final value theorems give x(0⁺) and x(∞) from X(s) without any inversion.
  7. For an LTI system at rest, H(s) = Y(s)/X(s), and multiplying by 1/s gives the step response.

Understand State the time-shift and time-differentiation properties of the Laplace transform and derive the differentiation result from the defining integral. 8 marks

Time shift: x(t − t₀) → e^(−s t₀)X(s), ROC unchanged; Differentiation: dx/dt → sX(s) − x(0⁻); Derive by integrating by parts, showing where the initial-condition term appears; Extend to the second derivative: s²X(s) − s·x(0⁻) − x′(0⁻).

Understand Explain the initial value theorem and the final value theorem. Give one valid use of each and one case where the final value theorem gives a wrong answer. 8 marks

x(0⁺) = lim sX(s) as s → ∞; lim x(t) = lim sX(s) as s → 0, provided poles of sX(s) are in the left half-plane; Example: X(s) = 1/(s(s + 2)) gives x(∞) = 1/2; Failure case: X(s) = ω/(s² + ω²) has poles on the jω axis, so the limit does not exist.

Understand Define transfer function, pole, zero and BIBO stability for a continuous-time LTI system. State the pole condition for stability. 8 marks

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze A system has H(s) = (s + 2)/((s + 1)(s − 1)). List the three possible ROCs, and for each say whether the system is causal and whether it is stable. 13 marks

Poles at s = −1 and s = +1; three ROCs: Re(s) > 1, −1 < Re(s) < 1, Re(s) < −1; Re(s) > 1: causal but unstable, because of the pole at s = +1; −1 < Re(s) < 1: stable but non-causal; Re(s) < −1: anti-causal and unstable; Conclusion: no ROC makes the system both causal and stable.

Evaluate Compare the Fourier transform and the Laplace transform. Explain when the Laplace transform reduces to the Fourier transform and how the ROC separates BIBO stability from ordinary bounded response. 13 marks

Fourier: X(jω) = ∫ x(t)e^(−jωt)dt, exists only if x(t) is absolutely integrable; Laplace: put s = σ + jω; with σ = 0 the two integrals agree; Laplace exists for signals such as e^(2t)u(t) where Fourier does not, because σ can be chosen; Fourier transform exists exactly when the ROC of X(s) contains the jω axis; A pole on the jω axis gives a bounded but not absolutely integrable signal, so it is not BIBO stable for a system.

Analyze A second-order system has H(s) = ωₙ²/(s² + 2ζωₙs + ωₙ²). Draw the pole locations for ζ > 1, ζ = 1, 0 < ζ < 1 and ζ = 0, and explain how the step response shape and stability follow from the poles. 12 marks

Poles at s = −ζωₙ ± jωₙ√(1 − ζ²); ζ > 1: two distinct real left-half poles, overdamped, no overshoot; ζ = 1: double real pole, critically damped; 0 < ζ < 1: complex pair, damped ringing at ω_d = ωₙ√(1 − ζ²); ζ = 0: poles on the jω axis, sustained oscillation, not BIBO stable; Settling time grows as the real part −ζωₙ moves closer to the origin.

Evaluate A first-order system y′(t) + a·y(t) = x(t) is built with a = 0 by mistake. Discuss what happens to the impulse response, the ROC and the stability, and what a practical fix is. 10 marks

H(s) = 1/s; h(t) = u(t) is not absolutely integrable, so the system is not BIBO stable; ROC is Re(s) > 0 for the causal choice and does not include the jω axis, which has a pole at s = 0; A bounded input such as a step gives an unbounded output that grows with t; Fix: add damping so the pole moves to s = −a with a > 0, and check that the ROC now contains the jω axis.

Glossary

Asymptotic stability
A system whose natural response decays to zero as t → ∞, which happens when all poles are strictly in the left half-plane.
BIBO stability
A system is stable in this sense when every bounded input produces a bounded output; in the s-domain all poles must lie in the left half-plane.
Causal system
A system whose impulse response h(t) is zero for t < 0.
Characteristic polynomial
The denominator of H(s); its roots are the poles, which decide the natural response.
Final value theorem
The result lim x(t) as t → ∞ = lim sX(s) as s → 0, valid only when the poles of sX(s) are all in the left half-plane.
Impulse response
h(t), the output of an LTI system when the input is a unit impulse δ(t); its Laplace transform is H(s).
Initial value theorem
The result x(0⁺) = lim sX(s) as s → ∞, used to read the starting value without inversion.
Laplace transform
The operation X(s) = ∫ x(t)e^(−st)dt that maps a time signal to a function of the complex variable s.
Left-sided signal
A signal that is zero after some time; its ROC is a half-plane to the left of the leftmost pole.
One-sided transform
A Laplace transform integrated from 0⁻ to ∞, used for solving differential equations with initial conditions.
Partial fraction expansion
Rewriting a proper rational X(s) as a sum of simple fractions that match known inverse pairs.
Pole
A complex value of s at which X(s) or H(s) becomes infinite.
Proper rational function
A rational function whose numerator degree is smaller than its denominator degree.
Rational function
A ratio of two polynomials in s.
Region of convergence
The set of s values for which the Laplace integral converges; it must be stated with every transform.
Residue
The coefficient A in the term A/(s − p); for a simple pole, A = (s − p)X(s) evaluated at s = p.
Right-sided signal
A signal that is zero before some time; its ROC is a half-plane to the right of the rightmost pole.
s-plane
The complex plane of s = σ + jω used to plot poles and zeros.
Step response
The output when the input is u(t); in the s-domain it is H(s)/s.
Transfer function
H(s) = Y(s)/X(s) for an LTI system at rest; it multiplies the input transform to give the output transform.
Two-sided signal
A signal nonzero for a long range on both sides; its ROC is a vertical strip between two poles.
Zero
A complex value of s at which X(s) or H(s) equals zero.
Zero-input response
The part of the output caused by initial conditions alone, with the input set to zero.
Zero-state response
The part of the output caused by the input alone, with all initial conditions set to zero.
