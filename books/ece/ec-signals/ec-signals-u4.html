4.1 Laplace transform and region of convergence

The Laplace transform turns a time signal x(t) into a function X(s) of a complex frequency s = σ + jω. Before integrating, it multiplies x(t) by a decaying factor e−σt, so signals that grow too fast for the Fourier transform, such as e2tu(t), still have a transform. The integral converges only for some values of s. That set is the region of convergence (ROC), and it is half of the answer: the same X(s) with a different ROC belongs to a different time signal. Circuit, control and filter engineers use the transform because it turns differential equations into algebra.

You need: the unit step u(t), the impulse δ(t) and real exponentials from the standard signals, and the Fourier transform integral X(jω) = ∫x(t)e−jωtdt.

The transform and complex frequency

The bilateral (two-sided) Laplace transform of a signal x(t) integrates over all time:

X(s) = ∫ x(t)·e⁻ˢᵗ dt, t from −∞ to ∞

x(t) is the time signal; s = σ + jω is the complex frequency; σ = Re(s) is the damping in s⁻¹; ω = Im(s) is the angular frequency in rad/s; X(s) is the transform. We write x(t) ↔ X(s) for a transform pair.

Split the kernel: e−st = e−σt·e−jωt. The factor e−jωt is the Fourier kernel. The factor e−σt damps the signal for t > 0 when σ > 0. So the Laplace transform of x(t) is the Fourier transform of x(t)e−σt. Put σ = 0 and you get the Fourier transform: X(jω) = X(s) at s = jω, but only when the ROC contains the jω axis.

The unilateral (one-sided) transform starts at t = 0⁻, just before zero: X(s) = ∫0⁻∞ x(t)e−stdt. For a causal signal (x(t) = 0 for t < 0) the two forms give the same X(s). The one-sided form is the one used to solve differential equations with initial conditions in the properties and LTI-analysis chapters. In this chapter, signals that live at t < 0 always use the bilateral form.

Convergence and the region of convergence

Take the right-sided exponential x(t) = e−atu(t), with a real. Only t ≥ 0 contributes:

X(s) = ∫ e⁻⁽ˢ⁺ᵃ⁾ᵗ dt (t from 0 to ∞) = 1/(s + a)

The upper limit gives zero only if e−(s+a)t → 0 as t → ∞, that is Re(s + a) > 0. So the ROC is Re(s) > −a, a half-plane to the right of the pole at s = −a.

Now the left-sided signal x(t) = −e−atu(−t), which is nonzero only for t < 0. Its integral is −∫−∞0 e−(s+a)tdt. The antiderivative is e−(s+a)t/(s + a), and at t → −∞ the term e−(s+a)t vanishes only if Re(s + a) < 0. The result is again 1/(s + a), but with ROC Re(s) < −a, the half-plane to the left of the pole.

So one algebraic expression, 1/(s + a), belongs to two different signals. The ROC tells them apart. This is why an examiner gives no marks for X(s) without its ROC.

Properties of the ROC and standard pairs

Convergence depends only on σ = Re(s), because |e−st| = e−σt. That one fact gives the rules below. Some syllabi list them as the properties of the ROC.

  1. The ROC is a vertical strip σ₁ < Re(s) < σ₂ in the s-plane; σ₁ may be −∞ and σ₂ may be +∞.
  2. The ROC never contains a pole: at a pole X(s) is infinite, so the integral cannot converge there.
  3. A finite-duration signal that is absolutely integrable has the whole s-plane as its ROC.
  4. A right-sided signal has ROC Re(s) > σ₁, to the right of its rightmost pole.
  5. A left-sided signal has ROC Re(s) < σ₂, to the left of its leftmost pole.
  6. A two-sided signal has a strip between two poles, or no ROC at all if the strip is empty.

Worked example

Question. Find the Laplace transform and the ROC of the two-sided signal x(t) = e−2tu(t) + e3tu(−t). Does its Fourier transform exist? Check X(0) against the area under x(t).

  1. Use the bilateral transform, because the second term lives at t < 0.
  2. First term: e−2tu(t) ↔ 1/(s + 2), ROC Re(s) > −2.
  3. Second term: ∫−∞0 e3te−stdt = ∫−∞0 e(3−s)tdt = 1/(3 − s), which needs Re(3 − s) > 0, that is Re(s) < 3. Note 1/(3 − s) = −1/(s − 3).
  4. Add: X(s) = 1/(s + 2) − 1/(s − 3) = [(s − 3) − (s + 2)]/((s + 2)(s − 3)) = −5/((s + 2)(s − 3)) = −5/(s² − s − 6).
  5. ROC: both terms must converge, so −2 < Re(s) < 3. The strip is not empty, so the transform exists.
  6. Fourier transform: the strip contains σ = 0, so X(jω) = −5/((jω + 2)(jω − 3)) exists.
  7. Check: X(0) = −5/((2)(−3)) = 5/6. The area under x(t) is ∫₀∞e−2tdt + ∫−∞0e3tdt = 1/2 + 1/3 = 5/6. The two agree, as they must, because X(0) = ∫x(t)dt.

Answer. X(s) = −5/((s + 2)(s − 3)), ROC −2 < Re(s) < 3; the Fourier transform exists because the strip contains the jω axis; X(0) = 5/6 = area under x(t).

Worked example 2

Question. Find X(s) and the ROC of the pulse x(t) = u(t) − u(t − 2) (height 1, width 2 s).

  1. x(t) = 1 for 0 ≤ t < 2 and 0 elsewhere, so X(s) = ∫₀² e−stdt = (1 − e−2s)/s.
  2. The signal has finite duration and finite area, so the ROC is the whole s-plane.
  3. At s = 0 the formula reads 0/0. Expand e−2s ≈ 1 − 2s for small s: X(s) ≈ 2s/s = 2. So s = 0 is not a pole, and X(0) = 2 = the area of the pulse.

Answer. X(s) = (1 − e−2s)/s, ROC all s; there is no pole at s = 0 and X(0) = 2.

Exam traps

  • Writing X(s) = 1/(s + 2) without Re(s) > −2. Without the ROC the answer could equally be −e−2tu(−t).
  • Putting the pole inside the ROC. The boundary of the ROC passes through a pole; the pole itself is never inside.
  • Using the one-sided integral for a signal such as e3tu(−t). The one-sided integral ignores t < 0 and gives 0 for that term; use the bilateral form.
  • Saying 'the ROC is to the right of the outermost pole'. For a right-sided signal it is to the right of the pole with the largest real part, whatever the size of its imaginary part.
  • Setting s = jω when the ROC does not contain the jω axis: e2tu(t) has X(s) = 1/(s − 2) with ROC Re(s) > 2, and no Fourier transform.

4.2 Properties of the Laplace transform

The properties of the Laplace transform are shortcuts. Instead of integrating every new signal from scratch, you start from a known pair and change it: add signals, delay them, multiply by an exponential, differentiate, integrate or convolve. Each rule also says what happens to the region of convergence (ROC), because X(s) without its ROC is not a full answer. With these rules e−3tcos(4t)u(t) takes one line, a differential equation with initial conditions becomes algebra, and the starting and final values of a signal can be read from X(s) without inverting it.

You need: the Laplace transform and its ROC, with the table of standard pairs, and the convolution integral of LTI systems.

'Contains' means the ROC can be larger, when a pole cancels. For example, u(t) − u(t − 2) has two terms with ROC Re(s) > 0 each, but the difference (1 − e−2s)/s converges for all s.

The shift in s is also called frequency shifting. Multiplying by e−3t means s₀ = −3, so every s in X(s) becomes s + 3 and every pole moves 3 units to the left. A time shift only multiplies X(s) by e−st₀, which has no poles, so the ROC stays where it was. With the one-sided transform, use the time shift only as x(t − t₀)u(t − t₀) ↔ e−st₀X(s) with t₀ > 0 (a delay), and time scaling only with a > 0: a negative a would move the signal into t < 0, which the one-sided integral never sees.

Derivatives with initial conditions

The one-sided transform is used for systems that start at t = 0 with stored energy. Integrate by parts to see where the initial value comes from:

L{dx/dt} = ∫ x′(t)e⁻ˢᵗ dt        (t from 0⁻ to ∞)
         = [x(t)e⁻ˢᵗ] from 0⁻ to ∞ + s∫ x(t)e⁻ˢᵗ dt
         = 0 − x(0⁻) + s X(s)

The upper limit gives 0 inside the ROC. x(0⁻) is the value just before t = 0, for example the voltage on a capacitor when a switch closes.

L{d²x/dt²} = s²X(s) − s x(0⁻) − x′(0⁻)

x′(0⁻) is the slope just before t = 0. Each further derivative adds one more initial-condition term. In the bilateral transform there is no starting point, and the rule is simply dx/dt ↔ sX(s).

Two more rules complete the set. Multiplying by t differentiates in s: t x(t) ↔ −dX/ds, so t e−2tu(t) ↔ −d/ds[1/(s + 2)] = 1/(s + 2)². Convolution becomes multiplication: if y(t) = x(t) * h(t), then Y(s) = X(s)H(s). This last rule is why an LTI system is described by one function H(s).

Initial and final value theorems

x(0⁺) = lim sX(s) as s → ∞

x(0⁺) is the value just after t = 0. Valid when X(s) is strictly proper (numerator degree less than denominator degree), so that x(t) has no impulse at t = 0.

x(∞) = lim sX(s) as s → 0

Valid only when every pole of sX(s) lies strictly in the left half-plane, Re(s) < 0. A simple pole of X(s) at s = 0 is allowed, because the factor s cancels it.

Both theorems are for one-sided transforms of signals that start at t = 0. The final value theorem gives a wrong number, not an error message, when its condition fails: for sin(4t)u(t), sX(s) = 4s/(s² + 16) → 0 as s → 0, but the sine never settles. Always check the poles first.

Worked example

Question. Use the properties to find the Laplace transform and ROC of x(t) = e−3tcos(4t)u(t). Check x(0⁺) and x(∞) with the limit theorems.

  1. Start from the pair cos(4t)u(t) ↔ s/(s² + 16), ROC Re(s) > 0. Its poles are at s = ±j4.
  2. The multiplier is e−3t = es₀t with s₀ = −3. Replace s by s − s₀ = s + 3: X(s) = (s + 3)/((s + 3)² + 16).
  3. Expand: (s + 3)² + 16 = s² + 6s + 9 + 16 = s² + 6s + 25. So X(s) = (s + 3)/(s² + 6s + 25).
  4. ROC: shift Re(s) > 0 by Re(s₀) = −3, giving Re(s) > −3. The poles are at −3 ± j4, on the boundary line.
  5. Initial value: sX(s) = (s² + 3s)/(s² + 6s + 25) → 1 as s → ∞. Directly, x(0⁺) = e⁰cos 0 = 1. They agree.
  6. Final value: the poles of sX(s) are −3 ± j4, both with real part −3 < 0, so the theorem applies. sX(s) → 0 as s → 0, so x(∞) = 0, as the decaying envelope says.

Answer. X(s) = (s + 3)/(s² + 6s + 25), ROC Re(s) > −3; x(0⁺) = 1 and x(∞) = 0.

Worked example 2

Question. A one-sided transform is X(s) = (5s + 6)/(s(s + 2)(s + 3)). Find x(0⁺) and x(∞) from the limit theorems, then confirm both by finding x(t). What signal has the transform e−sX(s)?

  1. X(s) is strictly proper (degree 1 over degree 3), so the initial value theorem applies: sX(s) = (5s + 6)/((s + 2)(s + 3)) → 0 as s → ∞. So x(0⁺) = 0.
  2. The poles of sX(s) are −2 and −3, both in the left half-plane, so the final value theorem applies: sX(s) at s = 0 is 6/(2·3) = 1. So x(∞) = 1.
  3. Confirm by partial fractions: X(s) = A/s + B/(s + 2) + C/(s + 3) with A = 6/(2·3) = 1, B = (5(−2) + 6)/((−2)(1)) = (−4)/(−2) = 2, C = (5(−3) + 6)/((−3)(−1)) = (−9)/3 = −3.

Exam traps

  • Using s → s − 3 for e−3t. The rule is s → s − s₀ with s₀ = −3, so s becomes s + 3 and the poles move left.
  • Dropping the initial-condition terms of the one-sided derivative: L{dx/dt} = sX(s) − x(0⁻), not sX(s).
  • Losing the minus sign in t x(t) ↔ −dX/ds. Check with t u(t): −d/ds(1/s) = 1/s², which is right.
  • Applying the final value theorem to sin(4t)u(t) or e2tu(t). For both, sX(s) → 0 as s → 0, yet neither signal settles to 0. Check that every pole of sX(s) is in the left half-plane first.
  • Applying the initial value theorem to an improper X(s) such as (s + 2)/(s + 1) = 1 + 1/(s + 1). The constant 1 is an impulse δ(t) at t = 0; take the theorem on the proper part only, which gives x(0⁺) = 1.

4.3 Inverse Laplace transform by partial fractions

Most transforms met in circuits and control are rational: X(s) = N(s)/D(s), a ratio of two polynomials in s. Partial fractions rewrite such an X(s) as a sum of simple terms like A/(s + a), each of which is a row of the transform table. The region of convergence (ROC) then decides, term by term, whether the inverse is right-sided or left-sided. This is how a transfer function becomes an impulse response or a step response, and every coefficient you find is the size of one natural mode of the system.

You need: the transform table and the ROC rules, and the properties, mainly the time shift and differentiation in s.

Proper fractions and distinct poles

N(s) is the numerator and D(s) the denominator. X(s) is proper when the degree of N is less than the degree of D. If it is not, divide first. For example, (s² + 5s + 9)/(s² + 4s + 3) = 1 + (s + 6)/(s² + 4s + 3). The constant 1 inverts to an impulse δ(t) (an s term would give δ′(t)), and only the proper remainder goes to partial fractions.

Factor D(s) to find the poles, the roots of D(s) = 0. For distinct poles at s = −p₁, −p₂, …, write X(s) = A₁/(s + p₁) + A₂/(s + p₂) + …. Each coefficient comes from the cover-up rule:

Aₖ = [(s + pₖ)·X(s)] at s = −pₖ

Multiply X(s) by the factor of that pole, cancel, then put s equal to the pole. Aₖ is also called the residue at the pole s = −pₖ. Here pₖ is just a number; the pole is at −pₖ, whatever the sign of pₖ.

In the division example, (s + 6)/((s + 1)(s + 3)) gives A = (−1 + 6)/(−1 + 3) = 5/2 and B = (−3 + 6)/(−3 + 1) = −3/2. With a right-sided ROC, the full inverse is δ(t) + (2.5e−t − 1.5e−3t)u(t). Comparing coefficients after clearing the denominator gives the same numbers and is a good check.

Repeated and complex-conjugate poles

A repeated factor (s + a)r needs r terms: B₁/(s + a) + B₂/(s + a)² + … + Br/(s + a)r. Let G(s) = (s + a)rX(s). Then Br = G(−a), Br−1 = G′(−a), and in general Br−k = G(k)(−a)/k!. The inverse pairs for a right-sided ROC are:

1/(s + a)ⁿ ↔ tⁿ⁻¹ e⁻ᵃᵗ u(t) / (n − 1)!

So 1/(s + a) ↔ e−atu(t) and 1/(s + a)² ↔ t e−atu(t). These come from differentiation in s.

Complex-conjugate poles come from a quadratic s² + bs + c with b² < 4c. Keep it whole and complete the square: s² + bs + c = (s + α)² + ω² with α = b/2 and ω = √(c − α²). Split the numerator into a multiple of (s + α) plus a multiple of ω, and use e−αtcos(ωt)u(t) ↔ (s + α)/((s + α)² + ω²) and e−αtsin(ωt)u(t) ↔ ω/((s + α)² + ω²).

Example: X(s) = (2s + 12)/(s² + 2s + 5) with ROC Re(s) > −1. Here (s + 1)² + 2², so α = 1 and ω = 2. Write 2s + 12 = 2(s + 1) + 10 = 2(s + 1) + 5·2. Then x(t) = e−t[2cos(2t) + 5sin(2t)]u(t). Check: x(0⁺) = 2, and lim sX(s) as s → ∞ is also 2.

A single term 1/(s + a) has two inverses, and the ROC picks one. If the ROC lies to the right of the pole −a, the term is right-sided; if the ROC lies to the left, the term is left-sided. Decide each term separately by asking: is the ROC to the right or to the left of this term's pole?

4.4 Analysis of LTI systems: transfer function, poles and zeros

An LTI system is described in the s-domain by one rational function, its transfer function H(s) = Y(s)/X(s), the Laplace transform of its impulse response. Convolution becomes multiplication, so outputs come from algebra and partial fractions. Its zeros and poles, with the ROC, show at a glance whether the system is causal, whether it is stable, and how its response will look. Some syllabi call H(s) the system function or network function.

You need: the convolution and differentiation properties, partial fractions, and the impulse response, convolution and BIBO stability of continuous-time LTI systems.

The transfer function

For an LTI system with impulse response h(t), the output is y(t) = x(t) * h(t). The convolution property turns this into Y(s) = H(s)X(s), with H(s) = L{h(t)}. Three routes lead to H(s):

  1. From h(t): transform it. h(t) = e−tu(t) gives H(s) = 1/(s + 1).
  2. From a differential equation at initial rest (all initial conditions zero): transform term by term with d/dt → s and solve for Y(s)/X(s). For y″ + 3y′ + 2y = x(t), (s² + 3s + 2)Y(s) = X(s), so H(s) = 1/(s² + 3s + 2).
  3. From a circuit: replace R by R, an inductor by sL and a capacitor by 1/(sC), then use the voltage divider or nodal analysis. A series RC low-pass filter with output across C gives H(s) = (1/sC)/(R + 1/sC) = 1/(1 + sRC).

Systems connect by simple algebra. In cascade (one after the other), H(s) = H₁(s)H₂(s). In parallel (outputs added), H(s) = H₁(s) + H₂(s). With negative feedback, a forward path G(s) and a feedback path F(s) give H(s) = G(s)/(1 + G(s)F(s)).

Poles, zeros, causality and stability

Write H(s) = N(s)/D(s). A zero is a root of N(s) = 0 and a pole is a root of D(s) = 0. On a pole-zero plot, poles are marked × and zeros ○. Each pole p gives a natural mode ept in h(t); zeros only set how much of each mode appears. The ROC then settles two questions:

  1. Causality: h(t) = 0 for t < 0 means a right-sided h(t), so the ROC is a right half-plane. For a rational H(s), causal means the ROC is to the right of the rightmost pole.
  2. BIBO stability: h(t) is absolutely integrable exactly when the ROC contains the jω axis.
  3. Both together: a causal system with rational H(s) is stable exactly when every pole has a negative real part.

Positive denominator coefficients are necessary for left-half-plane poles but not sufficient above second order: s³ + s² + s + 1 = (s + 1)(s² + 1) has poles at ±j. Be careful with cancellation too. H(s) = (s − 2)/((s + 1)(s − 2)) reduces to 1/(s + 1), so the input-to-output map is BIBO stable; but a circuit built this way still contains the mode e2t, which any initial charge or small component mismatch sets growing. Such a system is called internally unstable.

Complete response and frequency response

With stored energy at t = 0, transform the differential equation with the one-sided derivative rules. The initial conditions add terms to Y(s), and the output splits in two: the zero-input response (from the initial conditions alone) plus the zero-state response (from the input alone, Y = HX). The second worked example does this with numbers.

When the ROC contains the jω axis, put s = jω to get the frequency response H(jω), which links this unit to Fourier analysis. For H(s) = 1/(s² + 3s + 2) at ω = 1 rad/s: H(j1) = 1/(−1 + 3j + 2) = 1/(1 + 3j), so the gain is 1/√10 ≈ 0.316 and the phase is −tan⁻¹3 ≈ −71.6°. A sinusoid cos(t) entering this system leaves as 0.316 cos(t − 71.6°) in the steady state.

Worked example

Question. A causal LTI system has H(s) = 1/(s² + 3s + 2). Find its zeros, poles, ROC and stability, its impulse response h(t), and its unit-step response y(t).

  1. N(s) = 1 has no roots, so there are no finite zeros.
  2. D(s) = s² + 3s + 2 = (s + 1)(s + 2), so the poles are s = −1 and s = −2.
  3. Causal, so the ROC is Re(s) > −1, to the right of the rightmost pole. It contains the jω axis, so the system is stable; both poles have negative real parts.
  4. Impulse response: H(s) = 1/(s + 1) − 1/(s + 2) (cover-up: 1/(−1 + 2) = 1 and 1/(−2 + 1) = −1), so h(t) = (e−t − e−2t)u(t).
  5. Step input: X(s) = 1/s, so Y(s) = 1/(s(s + 1)(s + 2)) = A/s + B/(s + 1) + C/(s + 2).
  6. A = 1/((1)(2)) = 1/2; B = 1/((−1)(1)) = −1; C = 1/((−2)(−1)) = 1/2.
  7. y(t) = (1/2 − e−t + (1/2)e−2t)u(t).
  8. Checks: y(0) = 1/2 − 1 + 1/2 = 0 (the system starts at rest); y(∞) = 1/2 = H(0); y′(t) = e−t − e−2t = h(t), as it must be.

Answer. No finite zeros; poles −1, −2; ROC Re(s) > −1; stable; h(t) = (e−t − e−2t)u(t); y(t) = (1/2 − e−t + ½e−2t)u(t).

Question. The same system, y″ + 3y′ + 2y = x(t), starts with y(0⁻) = 1 and y′(0⁻) = 0, and the input is x(t) = u(t). Find the zero-input, zero-state and complete responses.

Exam traps

  • Calling a system stable because all denominator coefficients are positive. (s + 1)(s² + 1) = s³ + s² + s + 1 has poles on the jω axis.
  • Forgetting the ROC: H(s) = 1/(s − 1) is unstable if causal, but stable (and non-causal) with ROC Re(s) < 1.
  • Calling a pole on the jω axis 'stable' because the impulse response stays bounded. BIBO stability needs ∫|h(t)|dt to be finite; a simple pole at s = 0 makes a step input give a ramp.
  • Writing H(s) = Y(s)/X(s) from an equation with nonzero initial conditions. H(s) is defined at initial rest; the initial conditions belong to the zero-input response.
  • Treating zeros as modes. Zeros do not create terms in h(t); they change the coefficients of the modes that the poles create.

Takeaways

  1. The Laplace transform weights x(t) by e−σt before the Fourier integral, so it exists for growing signals too, inside its region of convergence.
  2. X(s) without its ROC is not an answer: 1/(s + a) is e−atu(t) for Re(s) > −a and −e−atu(−t) for Re(s) < −a.
  3. The ROC is a vertical strip that never contains a pole: right of the rightmost pole for right-sided signals, left of the leftmost for left-sided ones, between poles for two-sided ones.
  4. The properties build transforms from a few table pairs; the one-sided derivative rule brings the initial conditions into the algebra.
  5. The final value theorem only works when every pole of sX(s) is in the left half-plane; check the poles before using it.
  6. Partial fractions split a proper rational X(s) into table terms; repeated poles need every power, complex poles are kept as damped cosines and sines.
  7. For an LTI system Y(s) = H(s)X(s); causal means the ROC is to the right of the rightmost pole, stable means the ROC contains the jω axis.

Analyze A system has H(s) = (s + 2)/((s + 1)(s − 1)). List the possible ROCs. For each, find h(t) and say whether the system is causal and whether it is stable. Can this system be both causal and stable? 13 marks

Points to cover. Poles −1 and +1, zero −2; Partial fractions: −½/(s + 1) + (3/2)/(s − 1); Re(s) > 1: causal, unstable; −1 < Re(s) < 1: two-sided, stable, not causal; Re(s) < −1: anti-causal, unstable; No ROC gives both; check ∫h = H(0) for the stable case.

Model answer.

  1. Poles and zero. Zero at s = −2; poles at s = −1 and s = +1. The poles split the s-plane into three ROCs: Re(s) > 1, −1 < Re(s) < 1 and Re(s) < −1.

Evaluate Compare the Fourier transform and the Laplace transform for signal and system analysis. When does the Laplace transform reduce to the Fourier transform? Evaluate which tool suits each of these tasks: a growing signal, the response of a circuit switched on with stored energy, and the steady-state gain of a filter. 13 marks

Points to cover. Definitions; X(jω) = X(s) at s = jω when the ROC contains the jω axis; Laplace converges for more signals because of e−σt; Table of signals: e−2tu(t), e2tu(t), u(t), cos(ω₀t); Systems: H(s) gives transient, stability and initial conditions; H(jω) gives steady-state gain and phase; Verdict for each task, with a number.

Model answer.

  1. Definitions. Fourier: X(jω) = ∫−∞∞x(t)e−jωtdt. Laplace: X(s) = ∫−∞∞x(t)e−stdt with s = σ + jω. The Laplace transform is the Fourier transform of x(t)e−σt.
  2. When they coincide. Putting σ = 0 gives s = jω. If the ROC of X(s) contains the jω axis, the Fourier transform exists and X(jω) = X(s) at s = jω. If the ROC does not contain the axis, the two are not related by this substitution.

  4. For systems. H(s) holds the poles, so it shows causality and stability (through the ROC), the transient modes, and, with the one-sided transform, the effect of initial conditions. H(jω) is the frequency response: the gain |H(jω)| and phase ∠H(jω) that a sinusoid meets in the steady state. H(jω) exists only for a stable system.
  5. Task 1, a growing signal such as e2tu(t). Only the Laplace transform works: 1/(s − 2) with ROC Re(s) > 2. Fourier analysis has nothing to offer.
  6. Task 2, a circuit switched on with stored energy. Use the one-sided Laplace transform. Glossary

BIBO stability
Every bounded input gives a bounded output; for an LTI system, ∫|h(t)|dt is finite and the ROC of H(s) contains the jω axis.
Bilateral transform
The Laplace transform with the integral over all time, −∞ to ∞; needed for signals that are nonzero for t < 0.
Causal system
A system whose impulse response is zero for t < 0; for rational H(s) its ROC is to the right of the rightmost pole.
Damping ratio
ζ in s² + 2ζωₙs + ωₙ²; ζ > 1 overdamped, ζ = 1 critically damped, 0 < ζ < 1 underdamped, ζ = 0 undamped.
Final value theorem
x(∞) = lim sX(s) as s → 0, valid only when every pole of sX(s) is in the left half-plane.
Frequency response
H(jω), the transfer function on the jω axis; it exists when the ROC contains the axis and gives gain and phase.
Initial value theorem
x(0⁺) = lim sX(s) as s → ∞, valid when X(s) is strictly proper.
Internal stability
Stability of every mode inside a system, including modes hidden by a pole-zero cancellation in H(s).
Laplace transform
The operation X(s) = ∫x(t)e−stdt that maps a time signal to a function of the complex frequency s = σ + jω.
Left-sided signal
A signal that is zero after some time; its ROC lies to the left of its leftmost pole.
Marginal stability
A causal system with simple poles on the jω axis and none to the right; its modes neither grow nor decay, but it is not BIBO stable.
One-sided transform
The Laplace transform with the integral from 0⁻ to ∞; it brings initial conditions into differential equations.
Partial fraction expansion
Rewriting a proper rational X(s) as a sum of simple terms, each matching a table pair.
Pole
A value of s at which X(s) or H(s) becomes infinite: a root of the denominator.
Proper rational function
A ratio of polynomials whose numerator degree is less than its denominator degree.
Region of convergence (ROC)
The set of s for which the Laplace integral converges; a vertical strip in the s-plane that contains no pole.
Residue
The coefficient A of a term A/(s + p) for a simple pole; found by the cover-up rule.
Right-sided signal
A signal that is zero before some time; its ROC lies to the right of its rightmost pole.
s-plane
The complex plane of s = σ + jω, with σ across and jω up, on which poles, zeros and the ROC are drawn.
Transfer function
H(s) = Y(s)/X(s) for an LTI system at initial rest; the Laplace transform of the impulse response.
Two-sided signal
A signal that extends to both t → −∞ and t → ∞; its ROC is a strip between two poles, if it exists at all.
Zero
A value of s at which X(s) or H(s) equals zero: a root of the numerator.
Zero-input response
The part of the output caused by the initial conditions alone, with the input set to zero.
Zero-state response
The part of the output caused by the input alone, with all initial conditions zero: Y(s) = H(s)X(s).
