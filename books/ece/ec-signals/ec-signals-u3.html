3.1 Fourier series: trigonometric and exponential forms, properties

Any periodic signal, however awkward its shape, is a sum of sinusoids at the fundamental frequency and its multiples. Orthogonality lets you pull out each sinusoid's share with one integral over one period. That share is the Fourier coefficient. Engineers use this to read a waveform's spectrum, predict what a filter outputs for a periodic input, and describe the harmonic currents a rectifier or fan regulator pushes into the mains. This chapter builds both forms and lists the properties that make them quick to apply.

You need: periodic signals, even and odd symmetry, and orthogonal signals (see the topics Classification: energy, power, periodic, even and odd signals and Signals as vectors: orthogonal signal space).

Period, harmonics and orthogonality

A signal is periodic when x(t) = x(t + T₀) for every t, where T₀ is the smallest positive value that works: the period, in seconds. The fundamental frequency f₀ = 1/T₀ is in hertz. The fundamental angular frequency ω₀ = 2π/T₀ is in radians per second. The n-th harmonic is the sinusoid at n f₀ hertz, or nω₀ rad/s, for n = 1, 2, 3, …

Fourier's claim is that x(t) equals a sum of these harmonics. Write ∫T₀ … dt for an integral over any interval of length T₀. Products of harmonics behave like this:

∫T₀ cos(nω₀t)cos(mω₀t)dt = T₀/2 if n = m ≥ 1, T₀ if n = m = 0, and 0 if n ≠ m

Here n and m are harmonic numbers; ω₀ = 2π/T₀ rad/s; the integral runs over one period of T₀ seconds. The same holds for sines, and ∫T₀ sin(nω₀t)cos(mω₀t)dt = 0 for all n and m.

This is orthogonality: different harmonics do not overlap. Multiply x(t) by cos(nω₀t) and integrate over a period; every harmonic except the n-th averages to zero, and the n-th survives with a factor T₀/2. Each coefficient needs one integral, not a search.

Two forms of the same series

x(t) = a₀/2 + Σn=1∞ [aₙcos(nω₀t) + bₙsin(nω₀t)]

Here x(t) is the periodic signal with period T₀ seconds; ω₀ = 2π/T₀ rad/s; aₙ and bₙ are real coefficients in the units of x(t); a₀/2 is the average or DC value.

aₙ = (2/T₀)∫T₀ x(t)cos(nω₀t)dt, bₙ = (2/T₀)∫T₀ x(t)sin(nω₀t)dt, a₀ = (2/T₀)∫T₀ x(t)dt

Here the factor 2/T₀ cancels the T₀/2 from orthogonality; all three integrals run over one period. Every non-zero aₙ or bₙ shows a sinusoid actually present in x(t).

x(t) = Σn=−∞∞ cₙejnω₀t, cₙ = (1/T₀)∫T₀ x(t)e−jnω₀tdt

Here cₙ is complex in general and has the units of x(t); the sum runs over negative as well as positive n; ejnω₀t = cos(nω₀t) + j sin(nω₀t). This is the exponential form.

c₀ = a₀/2, cₙ = (aₙ − jbₙ)/2, c−n = cₙ* for real x(t)

Here the star means complex conjugate; aₙ and bₙ are the trigonometric coefficients. For real x(t), |cₙ| = ½√(aₙ² + bₙ²), so a cosine of amplitude Aₙ = √(aₙ² + bₙ²) appears as two lines of height Aₙ/2 at +nω₀ and −nω₀.

The exponential form is compact: one integral, one symbol, and a time shift or derivative becomes a plain multiplication. The trigonometric form speaks in real cosines and sines, which is what a spectrum display shows on its positive-frequency side. Dirichlet conditions: if x(t) is absolutely integrable over one period, with finitely many maxima, minima and discontinuities there, the series converges to x(t) at each point of continuity and to the midpoint of a jump at a discontinuity. Near a jump the partial sums ring, overshooting by about 9% of the jump height; adding terms narrows the ringing but never removes that overshoot. This is the Gibbs phenomenon.

Symmetry saves arithmetic. A real even signal has every bₙ = 0 and real, even cₙ: a cosine-only series. A real odd signal has every aₙ = 0, hence a₀ = 0, and purely imaginary, odd cₙ: a sine-only series. Half-wave symmetry, x(t ± T₀/2) = −x(t), kills the DC term and all even harmonics, leaving n = 1, 3, 5, …. The square-wave current drawn from a 50 Hz supply by a bridge rectifier with a large inductive load behaves this way: its harmonics sit at 50, 150, 250 Hz, and the even ones are absent.

Worked example

Question. The periodic pulse x(t) = 1 for −1 < t < 1 s and x(t) = 0 for 1 < |t| < 2 s repeats with period T₀ = 4 s. Find c₀ and the general cₙ, then write the exponential Fourier series and the trigonometric Fourier series with the first three non-zero cosine terms.

  1. Period T₀ = 4 s, so ω₀ = 2π/T₀ = 2π/4 = π/2 rad/s ≈ 1.5708 rad/s.
  2. DC term: c₀ = (1/T₀)∫T₀x(t)dt = (1/4)∫−111 dt = (1/4)(2 s) = 0.5. The pulse is high for 2 s of every 4 s, so the average is 0.5.
  3. For n ≠ 0: cₙ = (1/4)∫−11e−jn(π/2)tdt.
  4. Inner integral: ∫−11e−jn(π/2)tdt = (e−jnπ/2 − ejnπ/2)/(−jnπ/2) = (−2j sin(nπ/2))/(−jnπ/2) = 4 sin(nπ/2)/(nπ).
  5. Divide by T₀ = 4: cₙ = sin(nπ/2)/(nπ) for n ≠ 0.
  6. Numbers: c₁ = sin(π/2)/π = 1/π ≈ 0.3183; c₂ = sin(π)/(2π) = 0; c₃ = sin(3π/2)/(3π) = −1/(3π) ≈ −0.1061; c₅ = 1/(5π) ≈ 0.0637. Even n give zero because the pulse is centered and high for exactly half the period, so sin(nπ/2) = 0 for even n.
  7. Exponential series: x(t) = 0.5 + Σn≠0 [sin(nπ/2)/(nπ)]ejn(π/2)t, with n running over ±1, ±3, ±5, …
  8. x(t) is real and even, so bₙ = 0 and aₙ = 2cₙ = 2 sin(nπ/2)/(nπ): a₁ = 0.6366, a₃ = −0.2122.
  9. Trigonometric series: x(t) = 0.5 + (2/π)cos(πt/2) − (2/(3π))cos(3πt/2) + (2/(5π))cos(5πt/2) − …

Answer. c₀ = 0.5 and cₙ = sin(nπ/2)/(nπ) for n ≠ 0, giving x(t) = 0.5 + Σn≠0[sin(nπ/2)/(nπ)]ejn(π/2)t = 0.5 + (2/π)cos(πt/2) − (2/(3π))cos(3πt/2) + (2/(5π))cos(5πt/2) − …, with cosine amplitudes 0.6366, 0.2122 and 0.1273.

Exam traps

  • Dropping the 1/T₀ in front of the cₙ integral, or using 2/T₀ for cₙ because the trigonometric form uses 2/T₀ for aₙ. Dropping 1/T₀ multiplies every cₙ by T₀; using 2/T₀ instead of 1/T₀ doubles every cₙ.
  • Writing ω₀ = 1/T₀. The fundamental frequency is f₀ = 1/T₀ hertz, but the angular frequency is ω₀ = 2π/T₀ rad/s, and every harmonic multiple must use ω₀.
  • Mixing the two DC conventions. With x(t) = a₀/2 + Σ… and a₀ = (2/T₀)∫x(t)dt, as in this book, the DC value is a₀/2 = c₀. Some books write x(t) = a₀ + Σ… with a₀ = (1/T₀)∫x(t)dt, and then a₀ itself is the DC value. Pick one convention and keep it through the answer.
  • Summing the exponential series only from n = 1. The sum runs from −∞ to ∞, and for real signals c−n = cₙ*, so the cosine amplitude is 2|cₙ|, not |cₙ|.
  • Getting the sign wrong in a time shift: x(t − t₀) multiplies cₙ by e−jnω₀t₀. A delay gives a negative phase; e+jnω₀t₀ is a time advance.

3.2 Fourier transform of aperiodic signals

A periodic signal has a line spectrum. An aperiodic signal has no single period, so its spectrum becomes continuous. The Fourier transform is the limit of the exponential Fourier series as the period T₀ goes to infinity. It maps a time signal x(t) to a complex function X(ω) of angular frequency ω in rad/s. Engineers use it to see which frequencies a pulse, a transient or a message occupies, and to compute filtering, modulation and bandwidth. This chapter derives the transform, states when it exists, and builds the standard pairs used everywhere.

You need: Fourier series, the standard signals (impulse, step, exponential, signum) and limits from first-year calculus.

From Fourier series to Fourier transform

Start with a periodic signal xT₀(t), period T₀. Its exponential Fourier series is xT₀(t)=Σ cn ejnω₀t, where ω₀=2π/T₀ rad/s and cn=(1/T₀)∫xT₀(t)e−jnω₀tdt over one period. Define Xn=cnT₀. Then xT₀(t)=(1/T₀)Σ Xn ejnω₀t. The line spacing is Δω=ω₀=2π/T₀. As T₀→∞ the signal loses its period, Δω→0, the lines merge, and the sum becomes an integral.

X(ω) = ∫−∞∞ x(t)e−jωtdt

Here x(t) is the time signal (V if voltage); ω is angular frequency in rad/s; j=√−1; X(ω) is the complex spectrum.

x(t) = (1/2π)∫−∞∞ X(ω)ejωtdω

Here X(ω) is the spectrum and dω is a small step of angular frequency. The 1/2π comes from the line spacing: the sum (1/T₀)Σ… becomes (1/2π)Σ…Δω, and Δω becomes dω. If you work with f in hertz (ω = 2πf, dω = 2π df), the factor cancels and x(t) = ∫X(f)ej2πftdf.

The transform exists when x(t) meets the Dirichlet conditions: ∫|x(t)|dt<∞, and only finitely many maxima, minima and finite jumps in any finite interval. X(ω) is then continuous in ω. These conditions are sufficient, not necessary: the constant 1, the step u(t), sgn(t) and cos ω₀t fail them, yet they get transforms that contain impulses (the table below). Some syllabi write X(jω) for the same function.

Time width and bandwidth

A signal is time-limited if x(t)=0 outside a finite interval. It is band-limited if X(ω)=0 for |ω|>B, where B is the bandwidth in rad/s. A nonzero signal cannot be both. If X(ω) is zero above B, the inverse integral runs over finite limits and produces a smooth function that cannot be zero on an interval unless it is zero everywhere. In the other direction, a rectangular pulse of width τ has a sinc spectrum with first zero at ω=2π/τ. Narrower τ gives a wider main lobe. The product τB stays roughly constant. This trade is why a short pulse needs more bandwidth than a long one: a 1 μs pulse has its first spectral zero at ω = 2π×10⁶ rad/s (1 MHz), a 1 ms pulse at 2π×10³ rad/s (1 kHz).

Worked example

Question. For x(t)=e−2tu(t), find X(ω). Hence find |X(ω)| at ω=0 and ω=2 rad/s.

  1. Write u(t)=1 for t≥0 and 0 for t<0, so the integral runs from 0 to ∞.
  2. X(ω)=∫0∞ e−2te−jωtdt = ∫0∞ e−(2+jω)tdt.
  3. Integrate: X(ω)=[−1/(2+jω)]e−(2+jω)t evaluated from t=0 to t=∞.
  4. At t→∞, e−2t→0 and |e−jωt|=1, so the upper limit gives 0. At t=0 the value is 1. Thus X(ω)=1/(2+jω).
  5. Magnitude: |X(ω)|=1/√(2²+ω²)=1/√(4+ω²).
  6. At ω=0: |X(0)|=1/√4=0.5. At ω=2 rad/s: |X(2)|=1/√(4+4)=1/√8≈0.354.

Answer. X(ω)=1/(2+jω); |X(0)|=0.5 and |X(2 rad/s)|≈0.354.

Worked example 2

Question. Find the Fourier transforms of the signum function sgn(t) and the unit step u(t). Check the signum result numerically at ω = 2 rad/s.

  1. sgn(t) is not absolutely integrable (∫|sgn t|dt is infinite), so write it as a limit of a signal that is: sgn(t) = lima→0 [e−atu(t) − eatu(−t)], with a > 0.
  2. Transform the bracket: e−atu(t) ↔ 1/(a+jω), and eatu(−t) ↔ ∫−∞0eate−jωtdt = 1/(a−jω).
  3. Difference: 1/(a+jω) − 1/(a−jω) = [(a−jω) − (a+jω)]/(a²+ω²) = −2jω/(a²+ω²).
  4. Let a → 0: −2jω/ω² = −2j/ω = 2/(jω). So sgn(t) ↔ 2/(jω).
  5. Numerical check at ω = 2 rad/s with a = 0.1: −2j(2)/(0.01+4) = −j0.998, already within 0.3% of 2/(j2) = −j1.
  6. Unit step: u(t) = ½ + ½ sgn(t). The constant ½ transforms to ½·2πδ(ω) = πδ(ω), and ½ sgn(t) to ½·2/(jω) = 1/(jω).
  7. So u(t) ↔ πδ(ω) + 1/(jω). The impulse carries the average value ½ of the step; the 1/(jω) part carries the jump.

Answer. sgn(t) ↔ 2/(jω), with |2/(jω)| = 1 at ω = 2 rad/s; u(t) ↔ πδ(ω) + 1/(jω).

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the 1/(2π) factor in the inverse transform. The forward transform has no such factor in this convention.
  • Mixing f in Hz with ω in rad/s. Use X(ω)=∫x(t)e−jωtdt and remember ω=2πf.
  • Using the wrong denominator for e−atu(t). It is 1/(a+jω), not 1/(a−jω), and the lower limit is 0 because of u(t).
  • Assuming a time-limited pulse is band-limited. A rectangular pulse has sinc tails that never end, so its spectrum is nonzero at arbitrarily high ω.
  • Treating δ(ω−ω₀) as a finite value. It is a density with unit area; the spectrum of a sinusoid is an impulse, not a tall finite spike.

3.3 Fourier transform properties; convolution and modulation theorems

A Fourier transform turns a signal x(t) into a spectrum X(ω). Many operations that are hard in time become easy algebra in frequency. Shifting a signal adds a phase ramp. Convolving with an impulse response becomes multiplying spectra. Multiplying by a cosine copies and halves the spectrum. Engineers use these rules for filters, radio transmitters, sound tones, and power-supply noise checks. This chapter lists the main properties, proves the convolution and modulation theorems, and shows how to compute transforms without long integrals.

You need: the Fourier transform and its standard pairs and the convolution integral (see Convolution integral and its evaluation).

Convolution theorem: time convolution becomes spectral product

Convolution in time is defined as y(t)=x(t)*h(t)=∫−∞∞x(τ)h(t−τ)dτ. The convolution theorem says the transform of y(t) is the product of the two transforms.

x(t)*h(t) ↔ X(ω)H(ω)

Here x(t) is the input; h(t) is the impulse response; X(ω) and H(ω) are their spectra; * means convolution.

  1. Write Y(ω)=∫∫ x(τ)h(t−τ)e−jωtdτdt.
  2. Substitute u=t−τ, so t=u+τ and dt=du.
  3. Then e−jω(u+τ)=e−jωue−jωτ.
  4. The double integral separates into ∫x(τ)e−jωτdτ times ∫h(u)e−jωudu.
  5. The result is Y(ω)=X(ω)H(ω).

The dual rule is multiplication in time: x(t)y(t) ↔ (1/2π)X(ω)*Y(ω). The factor 1/2π comes from the inverse-transform convention. This rule explains why a short window smears a spectrum.

Modulation theorem: cosine makes two shifted copies

Use Euler's identity cos(ω₀t)=(ejω₀t+e−jω₀t)/2. Multiply x(t) by this cosine. The frequency-shift rule gives one copy moved to ω₀ and one copy moved to −ω₀.

x(t)cos(ω₀t) ↔ ½[X(ω−ω₀)+X(ω+ω₀)]

Here ω₀ is the carrier frequency in rad/s; X(ω) is the original spectrum; each shifted copy has half the original height.

The shift rule itself is ejω₀tx(t) ↔ X(ω−ω₀). The modulation theorem uses a cosine, so it applies two shifts. Some syllabi call modulation the frequency-shifting property. The cosine version is the one used for amplitude modulation.

A radio station multiplies voice by a carrier cosine. The voice spectrum moves to the carrier frequency. A receiver multiplies by a local cosine and moves it back. The half-size copies carry the same information.

Worked example

Question. Find the Fourier transform of x(t)=e−2tcos(10t)u(t), where t is in seconds and x(t) is in volts. Also give X(0) in V·s.

3.4 Parseval's theorem, energy and power spectrum

Parseval's theorem is an energy accounting rule. The energy or average power of a signal can be computed either from its time waveform or from its Fourier spectrum, and the two answers match. Engineers use it to find signal energy without integrating in time, to read how energy is spread over frequency, and to define bandwidth for filters and communication links. This chapter states the rule for continuous-time Fourier transforms and Fourier series, then defines the energy spectral density and power spectral density.

You need: energy and power signals (see Classification: energy, power, periodic, even and odd signals), Fourier series, the Fourier transform and its properties.

Energy and power signals

For a continuous-time signal x(t), define the total energy E and the average power P by the limits below. The signal may be real or complex; |x(t)|² is the squared magnitude.

E = ∫−∞∞ |x(t)|² dt, P = limT→∞ (1/T) ∫−T/2T/2 |x(t)|² dt

Here x(t) is the signal; E is energy in joules (J) if x is in volts across 1 Ω; P is average power in watts (W); T is the averaging window length in seconds (s).

An energy signal has 0 < E < ∞. Its average power is zero. A power signal has 0 < P < ∞. Its total energy is infinite. A pulse e−3tu(t) is an energy signal, where u(t) is the unit step. A sine wave and a step are power signals.

These names decide which Parseval form applies. Use the energy form for pulses and finite-duration signals. Use the power form for periodic signals and signals that run forever with finite average power.

Parseval's theorem

Take the Fourier transform pair X(ω)=∫x(t)e−jωtdt and x(t)=(1/2π)∫X(ω)ejωtdω. Here ω is angular frequency in rad/s. Parseval's theorem for an energy signal says the energy computed in time equals the scaled area under |X(ω)|² on the frequency axis.

E = ∫−∞∞ |x(t)|² dt = (1/2π) ∫−∞∞ |X(ω)|² dω

Here E is total energy in J; X(ω) is the Fourier transform of x(t); |X(ω)|² is the energy spectral density; the factor 1/2π comes from the inverse transform convention above.

For a periodic signal with period T₀ and exponential Fourier series x(t)=Σ cₙ ej n ω₀ t, average power is the sum of squared coefficient magnitudes. Here ω₀=2π/T₀ is the fundamental angular frequency in rad/s, and cₙ is the n-th Fourier coefficient.

P = (1/T₀) ∫T₀ |x(t)|² dt = Σn=−∞∞ |cₙ|²

Here P is average power in W; T₀ is the period in s; cₙ=(1/T₀)∫T₀x(t)e−j n ω₀ tdt; the sum is over all integers n.

Energy and power spectrum

The energy spectral density (ESD) of an energy signal is |X(ω)|². It tells how much energy lies in a small band dω around ω. Its area, after the 1/2π scale, is total energy.

ESD(ω)=|X(ω)|², E=(1/2π)∫ESD(ω)dω

Here ESD(ω) has units J per (rad/s) for a 1 Ω reference; ω is in rad/s.

For a power signal, use a truncated version xT(t) that is zero outside |t|≤T/2. Its Fourier transform is XT(ω). The PSD is the limit below. Average power is the 1/2π-scaled area under PSD.

PSD(ω)=limT→∞ |XT(ω)|²/T, P=(1/2π)∫PSD(ω)dω

Here PSD(ω) has units W per (rad/s); T is the truncation length in s; XT(ω) is the transform of xT(t).

For a periodic signal the spectrum is a line spectrum at nω₀, and each line carries power |cₙ|². Bandwidth from cumulative energy: choose B so the band |ω|≤B holds a fraction η of total energy, with 0<η<1. Then ∫−BBESD(ω)dω = η(2πE). The smallest such B is the η-energy bandwidth. The same idea with PSD gives power bandwidth.

Worked example

Question. For x(t)=e−3tu(t), compute the total energy E from the time waveform. Then verify Parseval's theorem using X(ω)=1/(3+jω), and find the band |ω| ≤ B that holds 90% of the energy.

  1. For t≥0, |x(t)|²=e−6t; for t<0 it is zero. Thus E=∫0∞ e−6tdt.

  5. (1/2π)(π/3)=1/6 J. Both methods give 0.1667 J.
  6. Energy inside |ω| ≤ B: (1/2π)∫−BBdω/(9+ω²) = (1/2π)(2/3)tan−1(B/3). As a fraction of E = 1/6 this is (2/π)tan−1(B/3).

3.5 Frequency response of LTI systems

An LTI system can be described by how it treats each frequency. If x(t) has Fourier transform X(ω), the output has Y(ω)=H(ω)X(ω). The complex number H(ω) is the frequency response. It scales amplitude by |H(ω)| and shifts phase by ∠H(ω). This turns time-domain convolution into multiplication in frequency. Engineers use it to read RC filters, audio tone controls, communication channels and control loops. The same idea explains why a bass note and a treble note leave a filter with different strengths and delays.

You need: the Fourier transform, the convolution theorem, and impulse response and convolution (see Impulse response and step response).

Definition and sinusoidal steady state

For an LTI system with impulse response h(t), the frequency response is the Fourier transform of h(t).

H(ω)=Y(ω)/X(ω)=∫−∞∞ h(t)e−jωtdt

Here H(ω) is the complex frequency response; Y(ω) and X(ω) are Fourier transforms of output y(t) and input x(t); h(t) is the impulse response; j=√−1; ω is angular frequency in rad/s.

H(ω) is complex at each ω. Its magnitude |H(ω)| is amplitude gain. Its angle ∠H(ω) is phase shift.

x(t)=A cos(ω₀t+φ) → y(t)=A|H(ω₀)| cos(ω₀t+φ+∠H(ω₀))

Here A is input amplitude in volts or another unit; ω₀ is input angular frequency in rad/s; φ is input phase in radians or degrees; y(t) is the steady-state output. The rule needs a stable system (h(t) absolutely integrable), so that H(ω) exists and the transient dies away; an unstable system never settles to this sinusoid.

  1. Evaluate H(ω) at the input frequency ω₀.
  2. Multiply the input amplitude A by |H(ω₀)|.
  3. Add ∠H(ω₀) to the input phase φ.

Some syllabi call this frequency-domain system analysis. It gives the steady-state sinusoidal output directly, without solving a differential equation in time.

Computing H(ω)

Use h(t) when it is known. Integrate h(t)e−jωt from −∞ to ∞. The result is a function of ω, not a single number.

Use the differential equation when it is known. For a constant-coefficient linear ordinary differential equation, replace d/dt by jω.

y′(t)+a y(t)=b x(t) → H(ω)=b/(jω+a)

Here y′ is dy/dt; a and b are real constants; y(t) is output; x(t) is input; j=√−1; ω is angular frequency in rad/s.

For the RC low-pass, the equation is y′(t)+(1/RC)y(t)=(1/RC)x(t). Its frequency response is H(ω)=1/(1+jωRC). Here R is resistance in ohms, C is capacitance in farads, and RC is the time constant in seconds.

Do not confuse H(ω) with h(t). H(ω) is a complex gain at each frequency. h(t) is the time-domain impulse response. They are a Fourier-transform pair.

Magnitude, phase and filter shape

A magnitude sketch plots |H(ω)| against ω on linear axes. A phase sketch plots ∠H(ω). Figure 3.5.1 shows the RC low-pass of the worked examples on linear axes, with the two input frequencies marked.

Low-pass: |H| is largest near ω = 0 and falls as ω rises (|H(0)| = 1 only when the pass-band gain is normalised; 5/(1+jωRC) is still a low-pass, with |H(0)| = 5). High-pass: |H(0)|=0 and |H| rises as ω rises. Band-pass: |H| peaks near a centre frequency and is small on both sides.

Read the output at the input frequency only. A low-pass does not remove all high frequencies; it attenuates them by the factor |H(ω)| and shifts them by ∠H(ω).

For distortionless transmission, |H(ω)| must be constant and ∠H(ω) must be a straight line through the origin, ∠H(ω) = −ωtd; see distortionless transmission and ideal filters.

3.6 Distortionless transmission, ideal filters and realizability

A signal that travels through a channel should come out looking the same, only weaker and a little later. That happens when the channel's gain is flat and its phase is a straight line across the frequencies the signal uses. Such a channel is distortionless. Push the same idea to its limit and you get the ideal filter: perfect gain inside a band, exactly zero outside. Telephone lines, audio amplifiers and radio front ends all aim at these two targets, and both are limits that real hardware only approaches.

You need: frequency response (magnitude and phase of H(ω)) and the rectangular-pulse and sinc pair of the Fourier transform.

Distortionless transmission: flat gain, straight-line phase

A channel is distortionless when its output is a scaled, delayed copy of its input: y(t) = K x(t − td). K is the gain (a positive constant), td is the delay in seconds, x(t) is the input and y(t) is the output. No new frequencies appear, no shape changes, nothing else happens.

In frequency terms this is two conditions on H(ω), the frequency response. The magnitude must be flat, |H(ω)| = K, and the phase must be a straight line through the origin, ∠H(ω) = −ω td. ω is angular frequency in rad/s. Both conditions must hold over the band the signal occupies, not over all ω.

H(ω) = K e−jω td ⟺ y(t) = K x(t − td)

Here H(ω) is the channel frequency response; K is the constant gain; td is the delay in seconds; the two forms are equivalent for every ω in the signal band.

Break either condition and you get distortion. Amplitude distortion means |H(ω)| is not flat, so some frequencies are scaled more than others; an amplifier with a peak near 3 kHz makes speech sound harsh. Phase distortion means ∠H(ω) is not a straight line, so different frequencies are delayed by different amounts; a pulse sent down a long cable arrives smeared because its high-frequency parts arrive at a different time from its low-frequency parts.

  • Phase delay: tp = −∠H(ω)/ω seconds. The time shift felt by the single frequency ω.
  • Group delay: tg = −d∠H(ω)/dω seconds. The time shift felt by a narrow band of frequencies around ω.
  • For linear phase both equal td, so every frequency is delayed by the same amount.

Ideal filters and the sinc impulse response

An ideal filter passes one band of frequencies with constant gain K and linear phase −ω td, and gives exactly zero gain everywhere else. The magnitude plot looks like a brick wall, so these are called brick-wall filters.

FilterPassband, |H(ω)| = KStopband, |H(ω)| = 0
Ideal low-pass|ω| ≤ ωc|ω| > ωc
Ideal high-pass|ω| > ωc|ω| ≤ ωc
Ideal band-passω₁ ≤ |ω| ≤ ω₂all other ω
Ideal band-stopall other ωω₁ ≤ |ω| ≤ ω₂

h(t) = sin(ωc t)/(π t) = (ωc/π) sinc(ωc t/π)

Here h(t) is the impulse response of an ideal low-pass with K = 1 and td = 0; ωc is the cut-off in rad/s; sinc(x) = sin(πx)/(πx); the zeros are at t = kπ/ωc for k = ±1, ±2, …

The peak is ωc/π at t = 0 and the ripples die away only as 1/t. Two consequences follow. First, h(t) is nonzero for t < 0 as well as for t > 0, so the filter is noncausal: it must answer before the input arrives. Second, h(t) never dies out completely, so the filter has infinite memory. A delay td slides the sinc to the right, but the left tail is still infinite, so no finite delay makes it causal. Ideal high-pass and band-stop responses contain a δ(t) term (1 minus the corresponding low-pass brick wall); the ideal band-pass response is the difference of two low-pass brick walls and has no δ(t). All of them are noncausal in the same way.

Why ideal filters cannot be built, and what we build instead

The formal test is the Paley–Wiener condition. If a causal system has an impulse response with finite energy, the integral of |ln|H(ω)|| / (1 + ω²) over all ω must be finite. A band of exact zeros still makes it diverge. Where |H(ω)| = 0 over a band, ln|H| is −∞ there, the integral blows up, and no causal finite-energy system can have that magnitude. Isolated zeros are allowed; a whole band of exact zero is not. So a realisable magnitude may be small, but never exactly zero over a range, and the magnitude plot can never have a truly vertical edge.

Practical filters are therefore specified by tolerances, not by exact equalities. A low-pass specification gives a pass-band edge ωp with ripple there (say |H| within ±0.5 dB of K), a stop-band edge ωs beyond which |H| stays below a level (say −40 dB), and the transition band from ωp to ωs between them. Decibels are 20 log₁₀ of a voltage ratio. Group-delay variation across the pass band is specified too, because a filter with flat magnitude can still smear a pulse. Butterworth, Chebyshev and elliptic designs trade ripple against transition sharpness. Some syllabi call this topic distortion-free transmission and realizability of frequency response.

Worked example

Question. An ideal filter has gain K = 2 and delay td = 1 ms over |ω| ≤ 2000 rad/s, and zero gain outside. The input is x(t) = cos(1000t) V. (a) Check that the input frequency lies in the pass band. (b) Find |H| and ∠H at that frequency. (c) Write y(t). (d) Find y(0).

  1. The input has ω = 1000 rad/s. Since 1000 ≤ 2000, the frequency lies in the pass band, where |H(ω)| = K = 2 and ∠H(ω) = −ω td.
  2. Phase at ω = 1000 rad/s: ∠H = −ω td = −(1000 rad/s)(1 × 10⁻³ s) = −1 rad, which is about −57.3°.
  3. Magnitude at ω = 1000 rad/s: |H| = 2, so the output amplitude is 2 × 1 V = 2 V.
  4. A phase of −1 rad at 1000 rad/s is a time delay of 1 rad ÷ 1000 rad/s = 1 ms, which matches td. So the output is 2 cos(1000t − 1) V.
  5. At t = 0: y(0) = 2 cos(−1) = 2 × 0.5403 = 1.0806 V.

Answer. y(t) = 2 cos(1000t − 1) V, a 2 V wave delayed by 1 ms, and y(0) = 1.0806 V.

Exam traps

  • Flat magnitude is not enough. Many answers check |H| = K and stop; a nonlinear phase still distorts the waveform, so both conditions must be checked.
  • Sign of the phase. A delay gives ∠H = −ω td. Writing +ω td describes an advance, which is noncausal and shifts the signal the wrong way.
  • Phase delay and group delay are equal only for linear phase. For any other phase they differ, so quoting one when the other is asked loses marks.
  • Ideal filters are not 'costly but buildable'. No amount of hardware makes them causal; the exact brick wall violates the Paley–Wiener condition.
  • Distortionless does not mean unity gain. K may be 0.1 or 50; only the flatness of |H| and the linearity of ∠H decide it.

3.7 Correlation and spectral density of signals

Correlation asks how much one signal looks like another signal after a shift. Autocorrelation compares a signal with its own delayed copy; cross-correlation compares two different signals. The Fourier transform of autocorrelation is the spectral density, the same |X(ω)|² you met in Parseval's theorem. Engineers use this to find echo delay in radar, align received bits in a modem, locate a fault from vibration, and measure how much of a signal's energy sits near each frequency. This chapter defines these averages and proves the Fourier pair that connects them.

You need: the Fourier transform, the convolution theorem and energy and power spectra.

Autocorrelation and cross-correlation

Autocorrelation measures how much a signal x(t) matches a shifted copy of itself. For a real energy signal, define Rx(τ)=∫−∞∞ x(t)x(t+τ)dt. Here τ is the lag in seconds; positive τ compares x(t) with a copy advanced by τ. The value Rx(0)=∫x²(t)dt is the total energy E of the signal.

Rx(τ)=∫−∞∞ x(t)x(t+τ)dt

Here Rx(τ) is the autocorrelation at lag τ; x(t) is a real energy signal; τ is in seconds.

For a power signal, energy is infinite, so use the time average: Rx(τ)=limT→∞(1/T)∫−T/2T/2 x(t)x(t+τ)dt. For a periodic signal of period T₀, replace the limit by (1/T₀)∫ over one period. Now Rx(0) is average power P.

Cross-correlation compares two different real signals: Rxy(τ)=∫x(t)y(t+τ)dt for energy signals; for power signals use the same time average with x(t)y(t+τ). It is not generally even. Rxy(τ)=Ryx(−τ).

  • Rx(0)=E for an energy signal, or P for a power signal.
  • |Rx(τ)|≤Rx(0): no shift matches better than zero shift.
  • For real x, Rx(−τ)=Rx(τ), so the plot is symmetric.
  • Rx(τ)=x(τ)*x(−τ); autocorrelation is convolution with a reversed copy.

Wiener–Khinchin relation

The autocorrelation and the magnitude spectrum carry the same information. This is the Wiener–Khinchin theorem. For an energy signal with Fourier transform X(ω), Rx(τ) ↔ |X(ω)|². The double arrow means a Fourier-transform pair. |X(ω)|² is the energy spectral density: it tells how energy is spread over angular frequency ω in rad/s.

Rx(τ) ↔ |X(ω)|²

Here X(ω)=∫x(t)e−jωtdt is the Fourier transform; |X(ω)|² has units of energy per (rad/s); ω is in rad/s.

Taking the inverse transform at τ=0 gives Parseval's theorem: E=Rx(0)=(1/2π)∫|X(ω)|²dω. For a power signal the (generalized) Fourier transform of the time-average autocorrelation is the power spectral density Sx(ω): Rx(τ) ↔ Sx(ω), and P=(1/2π)∫Sx(ω)dω. For a periodic signal this transform is a sum of impulses at the harmonic frequencies.

  • Sx(ω) is real and nonnegative because it equals |X(ω)|² for energy signals.
  • For real x, Rx is even, so Sx is real and even.
  • Some syllabi call Sx the energy spectrum or power spectrum.
  • Do not confuse X(ω) with |X(ω)|²; the phase is lost in spectral density.

Finding a delay by correlation

A received signal is often a delayed, noisy copy of a known signal: y(t)=a x(t−d)+n(t). Cross-correlation with x gives a peak near lag d. At that lag the shifted copy lines up with y, so the product x(t)y(t+τ) adds coherently; at other lags it averages toward zero. The delay estimate is the τ that maximises Rxy(τ).

  1. Record x[n] and y[n] over the same time window.
  2. Compute Rxy[m]=Σ x[n]y[n+m] for a range of integer lags m.
  3. Find m0 where Rxy is largest.
  4. Convert lag to seconds: d≈m0Ts, where Ts is the sampling interval.
  5. Check the sign: if y is delayed relative to x, the peak sits at positive lag under this definition.

The same idea locates a radar echo, synchronises a frame in a receiver, or finds the travel time of a vibration wave. Autocorrelation is the special case x=y; its peak at τ=0 confirms the signal matches itself with no delay. Noise lowers the peak but does not move it if the noise is uncorrelated with x.

Worked example

Question. For x(t)=e−2tu(t), (a) find Rx(τ) for all τ, (b) evaluate Rx at τ=0, 1, 2, 3 s, and (c) find the Fourier transform of Rx(τ) and show it equals |X(ω)|².

  1. x(t)=0 for t<0 and x(t)=e−2t for t≥0. The energy is finite: E=∫0∞e−4tdt=1/4=0.25.
  2. Use Rx(τ)=∫0∞x(t)x(t+τ)dt, treating τ≥0 first.
  3. For τ≥0, x(t+τ)=e−2(t+τ) for t≥0. So Rx(τ)=e−2τ∫0∞e−4tdt=e−2τ/4.
  4. For τ<0, put τ=−σ with σ>0. By direct computation Rx(−σ)=e−2σ/4, and for a real x, Rx(−τ)=Rx(τ). Hence Rx(τ)=e−2|τ|/4 for all τ.
  5. Values: Rx(0)=1/4=0.25; Rx(1)=e−2/4=0.0338; Rx(2)=e−4/4=0.00458; Rx(3)=e−6/4=0.00062.
  6. X(ω)=∫0∞e−2te−jωtdt=1/(2+jω). Hence |X(ω)|²=1/(4+ω²).
  7. Fourier transform of e−2|τ| is 4/(4+ω²). Therefore F{Rx(τ)}=(1/4)×4/(4+ω²)=1/(4+ω²). This equals |X(ω)|², as Wiener–Khinchin predicts.

Answer. Rx(τ)=e−2|τ|/4 with values 0.25, 0.0338, 0.00458 and 0.00062 at τ=0, 1, 2, 3 s; its Fourier transform is 1/(4+ω²), equal to |X(ω)|².

Exam traps

  • Autocorrelation is not convolution with itself: Rx(τ)=x(τ)*x(−τ). Using x(τ)*x(τ) gives a different, non-even function.
  • For a power signal, Rx(0) is average power, not total energy. Total energy is infinite; do not write E=Rx(0).
  • Wiener–Khinchin uses |X(ω)|², not X(ω). The spectral density has no phase information.
  • Rxy(τ)=Ryx(−τ). Reversing the order without reversing the lag gives the wrong delay sign.
  • A peak in cross-correlation is at the lag that aligns the signals; do not read the peak height as the delay. Peak height measures similarity, not time.

3.8 Hilbert transform and its properties

The Hilbert transform is a filter that keeps every frequency's amplitude and shifts its phase by 90°. Positive frequencies get −90°; negative frequencies get +90°. In time, it turns cos(ω₀t) into sin(ω₀t). It exists because many systems need a quadrature copy of a signal: a version delayed by a quarter period. Single-sideband radio, envelope detection and instantaneous-frequency measurement use it. The same operation builds the analytic signal x(t)+jx̂(t). Some syllabi list it as an advanced Fourier-transform topic.

You need: the cosine pair of the Fourier transform, the phase response from frequency response, and the signum function sgn(ω).

Definition and frequency response

The Hilbert transform of a real signal x(t) is another real signal x̂(t), read “x hat”:

x̂(t) = (1/π) PV ∫−∞∞ x(τ)/(t−τ) dτ

Here x(t) is the input signal; x̂(t) is its Hilbert transform; τ is the integration time; t is the output time; PV means Cauchy principal value, which takes equal limits on both sides of the singularity at τ=t and cancels the infinite parts.

The Hilbert transformer is an LTI system. Its frequency response is HH(ω)=−j sgn(ω). The term sgn(ω) is the signum function: +1 for ω>0, 0 at ω=0, and −1 for ω<0. So |HH(ω)|=1 for every ω≠0. The phase is ∠HH(ω)=−90° for ω>0 and +90° for ω<0. At ω=0 the response is zero.

This is an all-pass filter with a quadrature phase shift. It does not change the size of any frequency component; it only moves positive-frequency components back by a quarter period and negative-frequency components forward by a quarter period. That is why a cosine becomes a sine, not a scaled cosine. The impulse response is h(t)=1/(πt), which is non-causal; an ideal Hilbert transformer cannot be built exactly in real time.

Properties, pairs and analytic signal

Write ℋ{x(t)} for the Hilbert transform of x(t). These properties follow from HH(ω)=−j sgn(ω).

  • Linearity: ℋ{a x₁(t)+b x₂(t)}=a ℋ{x₁(t)}+b ℋ{x₂(t)} for constants a and b.
  • Energy preservation: ∫|x̂(t)|²dt = ∫|x(t)|²dt. The reason is |HH(ω)|=1 at all ω≠0; the zero at ω=0 has zero area.
  • Orthogonality: for a real finite-energy signal x(t), ∫ x(t)x̂(t)dt=0. The two signals are 90° apart in phase, so their product has zero average.
  • Double transform: ℋ{ℋ{x(t)}}=−x(t) for a signal with no DC component. Squaring the frequency response gives (−j sgn ω)²=−1 for ω≠0. A DC term is removed by the first transform and cannot return.
  • Transform pairs for ω₀>0: cos(ω₀t) → sin(ω₀t) and sin(ω₀t) → −cos(ω₀t). The arrow means “Hilbert transform of”. The sign comes from multiplying the positive-frequency term by −j, which is a −90° rotation.
  • Same magnitude spectrum: |X̂(ω)| = |X(ω)| for ω≠0, so x(t) and x̂(t) have the same energy spectral density and the same autocorrelation.
  • Symmetry: the Hilbert transform of an even signal is odd, and of an odd signal is even (the kernel 1/(πt) is odd).
  • Modulated signals: if m(t) is low-pass with no spectrum above W and ωc > W, then ℋ{m(t)cos ωct} = m(t)sin ωct. Single-sideband modulation relies on this.
  • More pairs: δ(t) → 1/(πt); 1/(1+t²) → t/(1+t²) (worked example 2).

Analytic signal, in one line: xa(t)=x(t)+j x̂(t), with j=√−1. For x(t)=cos(ω₀t), xa(t)=cos(ω₀t)+j sin(ω₀t)=exp(jω₀t), so it contains only the positive frequency ω₀. This complex signal is the starting point for envelope and instantaneous-frequency ideas; those applications are outside this chapter.

Worked example

Question. A signal x(t)=cos(2t), with t in seconds, passes through an ideal Hilbert transformer. Using the exp(−jωt) Fourier convention, find x̂(t) and x̂(1 s).

  1. Write ω₀=2 rad/s.
  2. The Fourier transform of cos(2t) is X(ω)=π[δ(ω−2)+δ(ω+2)].
  3. The Hilbert frequency response is HH(ω)=−j sgn(ω). At ω=2, sgn(2)=+1, so HH(2)=−j. At ω=−2, sgn(−2)=−1, so HH(−2)=+j.
  4. Multiply the spectrum: X̂(ω)=π[−jδ(ω−2)+jδ(ω+2)].
  5. Take the inverse transform: x̂(t)=(1/2π)[−jπ exp(j2t)+jπ exp(−j2t)]=sin(2t).
  6. At t=1 s, x̂(1)=sin(2)=0.9093.

Answer. x̂(t)=sin(2t); at t=1 s, x̂(1)=0.9093 (dimensionless amplitude).

Worked example 2

Question. Find the Hilbert transform of x(t) = 1/(1+t²). Check that x and x̂ have the same energy and are orthogonal.

  1. Spectrum: e−|t| ↔ 2/(1+ω²); by duality, 2/(1+t²) ↔ 2π e−|ω|, so X(ω) = π e−|ω|.
  2. Multiply by −j sgn(ω): X̂(ω) = −jπ sgn(ω) e−|ω|.
  3. Recognise this with the frequency-derivative rule t x(t) ↔ j dX/dω: j d(πe−|ω|)/dω = j·π·(−sgn ω)e−|ω| = −jπ sgn(ω)e−|ω|. That is exactly X̂(ω).
  4. So x̂(t) = t x(t) = t/(1+t²). At t = 1 s: x(1) = 0.5 and x̂(1) = 0.5; at t = 0: x = 1 and x̂ = 0.
  5. Energy: ∫dt/(1+t²)² = π/2 and ∫t²dt/(1+t²)² = π/2, so both energies are π/2 ≈ 1.571.
  6. Orthogonality: ∫x x̂ dt = ∫t/(1+t²)² dt = 0, because the integrand is odd. An even input gave an odd output, as the symmetry property says.

Answer. ℋ{1/(1+t²)} = t/(1+t²); both signals have energy π/2 ≈ 1.571 and their product integrates to zero.

Exam traps

  • Choosing the sign of the 90° shift. Under the exp(−jωt) convention, ℋ{cos(ω₀t)}=+sin(ω₀t), not −sin(ω₀t).
  • Forgetting that the Hilbert transformer has zero response at ω=0. A DC term is removed, so ℋ{ℋ{x}}=−x only for zero-mean signals.
  • Swapping the sine pair. For ω₀>0, ℋ{sin(ω₀t)}=−cos(ω₀t).
  • Thinking the Hilbert transform turns a real signal into a complex one. The transform x̂(t) is real; the analytic signal x(t)+jx̂(t) is the complex one.
  • Using magnitude 1 at every frequency and ignoring the phase. The magnitude is flat, but the phase is the whole operation.

Takeaways

  1. Any reasonably well-behaved periodic signal can be built from harmonically related sinusoids, and orthogonality gives a direct formula for each coefficient.
  2. Letting the period T₀ go to infinity turns the discrete harmonic lines of the Fourier series into the continuous spectrum of the Fourier transform.
  3. Signals that are not absolutely integrable, such as sgn(t), u(t) and cos ω₀t, still have Fourier transforms once impulses are allowed: 2/(jω), πδ(ω) + 1/(jω) and π[δ(ω−ω₀) + δ(ω+ω₀)].
  4. Properties such as time shift, scaling, convolution and modulation let you get new transform pairs from known ones instead of integrating again.
  5. Parseval's theorem lets you compute energy or power in whichever domain is easier, and tells you where in frequency the energy sits.
  6. An LTI system cannot create new frequencies, so a sinusoid in gives a sinusoid out, changed only in amplitude by |H(ω)| and in phase by ∠H(ω).
  7. Perfect transmission needs a flat magnitude and a linear phase over the signal band; brick-wall filters satisfy this but are not causal, so real designs approximate them.
  8. Correlation and spectral density are a Fourier pair, so similarity-versus-lag in time and energy-versus-frequency carry the same information.
  9. The Hilbert transform is an all-pass 90° phase shifter (−j sgn ω); adding j times it to a real signal gives the analytic signal, with a one-sided spectrum, used in SSB and envelope detection.

Understand Explain how the Fourier transform is obtained from the Fourier series by letting the period tend to infinity, state the conditions for its existence, and interpret the resulting X(ω). 12 marks

Points to cover. Start from cₙ of a periodic extension xT₀(t); Define X(ω) as the envelope T₀cₙ = X(nω₀); Let T₀ → ∞: ω₀ → dω, the sum becomes an integral; Write the forward and inverse transforms; Dirichlet conditions; transforms with impulses for power signals; X(ω) is a density, check with a pulse.

Model answer.

  1. Take an aperiodic pulse x(t) that is zero outside |t| < T/2. Build its periodic extension xT₀(t) by repeating it every T₀ > T seconds. Inside one period the two signals are the same.
  2. The periodic signal has a Fourier series with ω₀ = 2π/T₀ and cₙ = (1/T₀)∫−T₀/2T₀/2x(t)e−jnω₀tdt. Because x(t) is zero outside |t| < T/2, the limits can be stretched to ±∞.
  3. Define the envelope X(ω) = ∫−∞∞x(t)e−jωtdt. Then T₀cₙ = X(nω₀): the scaled coefficients are samples of one fixed function, taken every ω₀ rad/s.
  4. Rewrite the synthesis equation with 1/T₀ = ω₀/2π: xT₀(t) = Σ cₙejnω₀t = (1/2π)Σ X(nω₀)ejnω₀tω₀.
  5. Now let T₀ → ∞. The copies move away to infinity, so xT₀(t) → x(t). The spacing ω₀ shrinks to dω, nω₀ becomes a continuous variable ω, and the sum becomes an integral: x(t) = (1/2π)∫−∞∞X(ω)ejωtdω, with X(ω) = ∫−∞∞x(t)e−jωtdt.
  6. Existence (Dirichlet conditions, sufficient): x(t) absolutely integrable, ∫|x(t)|dt < ∞, with finitely many maxima, minima and finite jumps in any finite interval. Then X(ω) exists and is continuous; at a jump the inverse transform returns the midpoint. Power signals such as 1, u(t), sgn(t) and cos ω₀t fail the first condition but get transforms with impulses: 2πδ(ω), πδ(ω) + 1/(jω), 2/(jω), π[δ(ω−ω₀) + δ(ω+ω₀)].
  7. Interpretation. The Fourier series has lines with finite heights cₙ. The transform has no lines: X(ω) is a spectral density in (units of x)·s, that is, amplitude per unit of frequency. |X(ω)| says how strongly frequency ω is present relative to its neighbours, ∠X(ω) gives its phase, and (1/2π)|X(ω)|²dω is the energy in the band dω.
  8. Two facts follow at once from the definition. X(0) = ∫x(t)dt is the area under the signal; for x(t) = e−2tu(t), X(ω) = 1/(2 + jω) and X(0) = 0.5 V·s, the area of the exponential. And for real x(t), X(−ω) = X*(ω), so only ω ≥ 0 needs to be plotted: |X| is even and ∠X is odd.
  9. Check with a pulse of height 1 and width 2 s (|t| < 1). Its transform is X(ω) = 2 sin ω/ω = 2Sa(ω). Its periodic extension with T₀ = 4 s has cₙ = sin(nπ/2)/(nπ), so T₀cₙ should equal X(nω₀):
    T₀ω₀ (rad/s)T₀c₁X(ω₀) = 2 sin ω₀/ω₀
    4 sπ/2 = 1.5714/π = 1.2731.273
    8 sπ/4 = 0.7851.8011.801
  10. Practical reading: a signal that lasts longer in time has a narrower X(ω), and a short one a wider X(ω). The pulse of width 2 s has its first spectral zero at ω = π rad/s; a pulse of width 1 s would have it at 2π rad/s.
  11. Closing check: doubling the period halves the line spacing (1.571 → 0.785 rad/s) while every scaled line still sits on the same curve 2 sin ω/ω. In the limit the lines fill the curve, which is the Fourier transform.

Points to cover. Write the defining integral once; Each property: statement, then a two-line proof by substitution; Name the condition (a ≠ 0, differentiable, transforms exist); Verify two properties on e−2tu(t) with numbers.

Model answer.

  1. Definition used throughout: X(ω) = ∫−∞∞x(t)e−jωtdt, written x(t) ↔ X(ω); ω is in rad/s.
  2. Linearity: a x₁(t) + b x₂(t) ↔ aX₁(ω) + bX₂(ω) for constants a, b. Proof: the integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals, and constants come out of the integral.

  4. Frequency shifting: ejω₀tx(t) ↔ X(ω − ω₀). Proof: ∫x(t)ejω₀te−jωtdt = ∫x(t)e−j(ω−ω₀)tdt = X(ω − ω₀). With cos ω₀t = (ejω₀t + e−jω₀t)/2 this gives the modulation theorem x(t)cos ω₀t ↔ ½[X(ω−ω₀) + X(ω+ω₀)].

Glossary

Amplitude distortion
Change in pulse shape caused by a magnitude response that is not constant over the signal band.
Aperiodic signal
A signal that does not repeat, so its spectrum is continuous rather than a set of lines.
Causal system
A system whose output at any time depends only on present and past inputs, so h(t) = 0 for t < 0.
Convergence of Fourier series
The property that the partial sums approach the signal at every point of continuity and to the midpoint at a jump.
Convolution
The operation y(t) = ∫x(τ)h(t − τ)dτ giving the response of an LTI system to an input.
Cross-correlation
Rxy(τ) = ∫x(t)y(t + τ)dt, measuring how much one signal resembles a shifted copy of another.
DC component
The average value of the signal over one period, given by c₀ or a₀.
Dirichlet conditions
Sufficient conditions for a Fourier series or transform to converge: absolute integrability and finitely many extrema and finite jumps in any finite interval.
Duality
The symmetry that lets a known transform pair be reused with the roles of time and frequency exchanged.
Energy spectral density
|X(ω)|², the energy per rad/s of an energy signal; (1/2π) times its area is the total energy.
Envelope
The slowly varying amplitude |xa(t)| of a bandpass signal, obtained from the analytic signal.
Fourier transform pair
Two functions x(t) and X(ω) related by the forward and inverse transform integrals.
Fundamental period
The smallest positive value of T₀ for which x(t) = x(t + T₀) holds.
Group delay
The negative derivative of phase with respect to frequency, −d∠H/dω, giving the delay of a narrow band of frequencies.
Harmonic
A sinusoid whose frequency is an integer multiple of the fundamental frequency.
Ideal filter
A filter whose magnitude is exactly one in the passband and exactly zero in the stopband, with sharp edges.
Impulse response
The output h(t) of an LTI system when the input is a unit impulse δ(t).
Line spectrum
A plot of the harmonic amplitudes or phases at discrete frequencies kω₀.
Orthogonal functions
A set of functions whose cross-integral over an interval is zero unless the two functions are the same member.
Periodic signal
A signal that repeats exactly after a fixed interval T₀, so x(t) = x(t + T₀) for all t.
Phase distortion
Change in pulse shape caused by a phase response that is not linear in frequency.
Pre-envelope
Another name for the analytic signal x(t) + jx̂(t), whose spectrum is zero for negative frequencies.
Quadrature signal
The Hilbert transform of a signal, in phase quadrature (90° apart) with it.
Signum function
sgn(t) = +1 for t > 0 and −1 for t < 0; its Fourier transform is 2/(jω).
Single-sideband modulation
Amplitude modulation in which one sideband is removed, halving the bandwidth, usually with a Hilbert transformer.
Spectrum
The description of a signal by the amplitude and phase of each frequency it contains.
Transfer function
The ratio H(ω) = Y(ω)/X(ω), equal to the Fourier transform of the impulse response.
