6.1 Discrete-time Fourier series

A periodic discrete-time sequence repeats every N samples. The discrete-time Fourier series writes it as a sum of exactly N complex exponentials. The coefficients X[k] tell how much of each discrete frequency is present. This matters because digital filters, OFDM radios, and spectrum displays process blocks of samples, and the transform must respect the fact that a sampled sinusoid has only N distinct frequencies. The next chapters turn this finite series into the discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT) and the discrete Fourier transform (DFT).

You need periodic signals and even/odd symmetry from Unit 1, the continuous-time Fourier series idea from Unit 3, and discrete-time sequences from Unit 5; the aperiodic case is in chapter 6.2.

N frequencies, N coefficients

Take a sequence x[n] that repeats every N samples: x[n+N] = x[n]. N is the period in samples. The discrete-time Fourier series (DTFS) writes one period as a sum of N complex exponentials ej2πkn/N, k = 0, 1, …, N−1. The integer k is the harmonic index: k=1 makes one cycle across N samples, k=2 makes two cycles, and so on. X[k] is a complex coefficient. |X[k]| is its amplitude and ∠X[k] is its phase in radians. j is the imaginary unit, j² = −1. The symbol Σ means sum.

X[k] = Σ<sub>n=0</sub><sup>N−1</sup> x[n] e<sup>−j2πkn/N</sup>

X[k] is the coefficient of harmonic k; x[n] is one period; N is the period in samples; n runs from 0 to N−1.

x[n] = (1/N) Σ<sub>k=0</sub><sup>N−1</sup> X[k] e<sup>j2πkn/N</sup>

The 1/N factor is the convention used here; k runs over the N harmonics.

The analysis equation gives X[k] from one period. The synthesis equation returns x[n] from all N coefficients. They are a DTFS pair.

Only N distinct exponentials exist. Add N to k: ej2π(k+N)n/N = ej2πkn/N ej2πn = ej2πkn/N, because n is an integer. Therefore X[k+N] = X[k]. The coefficients repeat every N. A continuous-time Fourier series has infinitely many harmonics; a sampled periodic sequence has exactly N.

Real signals, power, pulse train

For real x[n], X[N−k] = X*[k]. The star means complex conjugate: negate the imaginary part. X*[k] = Σ x[n] ej2πkn/N; the exponent equals e−j2π(N−k)n/N, so the sum is X[N−k]. This halves the independent coefficients. X[0] is real; for even N, X[N/2] is also real.

X[N−k] = X*[k]

Holds when x[n] is real; X*[k] is the complex conjugate of X[k].

Parseval's relation fixes average power: Pavg = (1/N) Σn=0N−1 |x[n]|² = (1/N²) Σk=0N−1 |X[k]|². The first sum uses time samples; the second uses harmonics. The 1/N², not 1/N, follows from the 1/N in synthesis. One period's energy is N·Pavg.

Pulse train: x[n]=1 for n=0,1,2 and 0 for n=3,…,7, repeated with N=8. Its average power is 3/8 = 0.375. The worked example computes its coefficients; the figure lists |X[k]| for k=0,…,7. The marked values at k=1 and k=7 are equal because X[7]=X*[1].

Reading the frequency axis

Only k=0,…,N−1 are new. The discrete frequency of harmonic k is Ωk = 2πk/N radians per sample. For even N, k=N/2 gives π radians per sample, the fastest change a sampled sequence can show. k>N/2 repeats negative frequencies: k=N−1 is −2π/N radians per sample.

Some syllabi call this the discrete Fourier series (DFS). Some put 1/N on the analysis equation. Both conventions give the same x[n]. State your convention first. Here 1/N is on synthesis, so X[0] is the sum of one period, not the average.

To compute by hand: write one period x[0] to x[N−1]; compute X[0] as the plain sum; compute X[k] for k=1,…,N−1; use X[N−k]=X*[k] for real signals; check synthesis. The next chapter 6.2 lets N go to infinity and replaces the finite sum with the DTFT. The DFT appears in 6.3.

Worked example

Question. A real periodic sequence has period N = 8. One period is x[0]=x[1]=x[2]=1 and x[3]=…=x[7]=0. Compute X[0], X[1], X[2], X[3] using X[k]=Σn=07 x[n]e−j2πkn/8. Then verify X[7]=X*[1].

  1. Write the three nonzero terms: X[k] = 1 + e−jπk/4 + e−jπk/2, because 2πkn/8 = πkn/4.
  2. X[0] = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3.
  3. X[1] = 1 + e−jπ/4 + e−jπ/2 = 1 + (0.7071 − j0.7071) − j = 1.7071 − j1.7071. Magnitude = 2.4142. Phase = −0.7854 rad = −π/4.
  4. X[2] = 1 + e−jπ/2 + e−jπ = 1 − j − 1 = −j. Real part 0, imaginary part −1.
  5. X[3] = 1 + e−j3π/4 + e−j3π/2 = 1 + (−0.7071 − j0.7071) + j = 0.2929 + j0.2929.
  6. X[7] = 1 + e−j7π/4 + e−j7π/2 = 1 + (0.7071 + j0.7071) + j = 1.7071 + j1.7071, which equals X*[1].

Answer. X[0]=3; X[1]=2.4142 e−jπ/4 (real 1.7071, imaginary −1.7071); X[2]=−j; X[3]=0.2929+j0.2929; and X[7]=1.7071+j1.7071=X*[1].

Exam traps

  • Reading k past N−1 and expecting zero: X[N]=X[0], X[N+1]=X[1], and so on. The coefficient sequence is periodic.
  • Putting the 1/N factor on the wrong equation. Some books put it on analysis, so X[0] becomes the average. State the convention before computing.
  • Forgetting that with 1/N on synthesis, X[0] is the sum of one period, not the average. For the pulse train X[0]=3, not 0.375.
  • Using continuous-time harmonic counting. A sampled period has only N distinct frequencies; k=N/2 is the highest for even N.
  • Applying X[N−k]=X*[k] to a complex-valued x[n]. The conjugate symmetry holds only for real sequences.

6.2 Discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT) and properties

A periodic sequence has a line spectrum: a few frequencies, each with a coefficient. An aperiodic sequence has no period to pick out those lines, so its spectrum becomes a continuous curve X(e^(jω)) of frequency. That curve is the discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT). It exists for every absolutely summable sequence, it repeats every 2π radians per sample, and one period holds all the information. Filter design, audio processing, radar and every DFT chip rest on it.

You need: the continuous-time Fourier transform and its convolution theorem (see Unit 3, Fourier Series and Fourier Transform), sampling and the spectrum of a sampled signal (see Unit 5, Sampling and Discrete-Time Systems), and the DTFS (6.1).

Definition and the inverse integral

Take a sequence x[n] defined for every integer n. Its DTFT is the sum of x[n] weighted by the complex exponential e^(−jωn). The variable ω is frequency in radians per sample, not in rad/s. The two are tied by ω = ΩT, where Ω is the continuous-time frequency in rad/s and T is the sampling interval in seconds. The notation X(e^(jω)), with e^(jω) inside the bracket, is a reminder that the transform depends on ω only through the point e^(jω) on the unit circle.

X(e^(jω)) = Σₙ₌₋∞^∞ x[n] e^(−jωn)

x[n] is the sequence; ω is frequency in rad/sample; the sum runs over every integer n.

The inverse turns the curve back into the sequence. Integrate X(e^(jω)) e^(jωn) over any stretch of length 2π, for example ω from −π to π, and divide by 2π. The factor 1/2π belongs to the definition; drop it and the round trip no longer closes.

x[n] = (1/2π) ∫₂π X(e^(jω)) e^(jωn) dω

the integral runs over any interval of length 2π in ω; n is an integer.

Because n is an integer, e^(−j(ω+2π)n) = e^(−jωn) e^(−j2πn) = e^(−jωn). Every term of the sum repeats, so X(e^(j(ω+2π))) = X(e^(jω)). The spectrum is 2π-periodic in ω even though x[n] is not periodic at all. One period, ω in (−π, π], carries everything. The point ω = π is the Nyquist frequency, half the sampling rate.

Convergence and the pairs you must know

The sum converges uniformly, and X is a continuous function of ω, when x[n] is absolutely summable: Σ|x[n]| < ∞. Finite-length sequences qualify trivially. So does aⁿu[n] with |a| < 1, since the geometric series adds to 1/(1−|a|). Sequences with finite energy, Σ|x[n]|² < ∞, also converge, in the mean-square sense.

Some useful sequences are not absolutely summable. The unit step u[n] is the standard example. Its algebraic transform 1/(1−e^(−jω)) is finite everywhere except ω = 2πk, where it blows up. The full DTFT adds impulses of area π at those points. Writing only the fraction is a frequent error.

The rectangular pulse gives a Dirichlet kernel, not a sinc. For length N, X(e^(jω)) = e^(−jω(N−1)/2) sin(ωN/2)/sin(ω/2). The zeros are at ω = 2πm/N for m = 1, …, N−1 modulo N; at ω = 2πk the value is N. This kernel is the discrete cousin of the continuous rect-to-sinc pair, and it is the shape every window in spectral analysis inherits.

The convolution property is the working tool: an LTI system multiplies the spectrum point by point, so |Y| = |X||H| and the phases add. Multiplication in time becomes periodic convolution in ω, with the 1/2π factor; that is exactly how a window smears a spectrum. Parseval says the energy summed over n equals (1/2π)∫|X|²dω, so |X(e^(jω))|² is an energy density per rad/sample.

For samples x[n] = x_c(nT) of a continuous-time signal, put ω = ΩT in the DTFT. The answer is the continuous-time spectrum copied every Ω_s = 2π/T and scaled by 1/T.

X(e^(jΩT)) = (1/T) Σₖ X_c(j(Ω − kΩ_s))

Ω is the continuous-time frequency in rad/s, T is the sampling interval in seconds, and Ω_s = 2π/T is the sampling frequency in rad/s.

So the DTFT of the samples is not X_c; it is the sum of all its shifted copies. If X_c has content above Ω_s/2, the copies overlap and the overlap is aliasing (see Unit 5). The 2π repeat in ω and the Ω_s repeat in Ω are the same statement.

Worked example

Question. A discrete-time signal is x[n] = (0.8)ⁿu[n]. (a) Find X(e^(jω)). (b) Write |X(e^(jω))| in closed form. (c) Evaluate the magnitude at ω = 0, ω = π/2 and ω = π rad/sample.

  1. (a) The step makes the sum start at n = 0: X(e^(jω)) = Σₙ₌₀^∞ (0.8)ⁿ e^(−jωn) = Σₙ₌₀^∞ (0.8 e^(−jω))ⁿ.
  2. This is a geometric series with ratio r = 0.8 e^(−jω). Its modulus is |r| = 0.8 < 1, so the series converges and X(e^(jω)) = 1/(1 − 0.8 e^(−jω)).
  3. (b) |X|² = 1/|1 − 0.8 e^(−jω)|². Expand the denominator: 1 − 1.6 cos ω + 0.64 = 1.64 − 1.6 cos ω. Hence |X| = 1/√(1.64 − 1.6 cos ω).
  4. (c) At ω = 0: cos 0 = 1, denominator 1.64 − 1.6 = 0.04, so |X| = 1/√0.04 = 1/0.2 = 5.
  5. At ω = π/2: cos(π/2) = 0, denominator 1.64, √1.64 = 1.2806, so |X| = 0.7809 ≈ 0.781.
  6. At ω = π: cos π = −1, denominator 1.64 + 1.6 = 3.24, √3.24 = 1.8, so |X| = 1/1.8 = 0.5556 ≈ 0.556.
  7. Sanity check: the peak sits at ω = 0 because all terms of x[n] are positive and add in phase there; the dip at ω = π comes from the alternating signs of e^(−jπn) = (−1)ⁿ.

Answer. X(e^(jω)) = 1/(1 − 0.8e^(−jω)); |X| = 5 at ω = 0, 0.781 at ω = π/2 and 0.556 at ω = π, with ω in rad/sample.

Exam traps

  • Writing X(jω) or X(ω). The DTFT is X(e^(jω)); the argument is a point on the unit circle and ω is in rad/sample, not rad/s.
  • Treating X(e^(jω)) as aperiodic and quoting one branch. The curve repeats every 2π, so the value at ω = 3π/2 equals the value at ω = −π/2.
  • Using 1/(1 − e^(−jω)) as the whole DTFT of u[n]. The impulses of area π at ω = 2πk are missing, and the expression is infinite there anyway.
  • Forgetting the 1/2π in multiplication and in Parseval. Both come straight from the inverse transform; the convolution property itself has no such factor.
  • Mixing up DTFT and DFT. The N-point DFT is N samples of X(e^(jω)), not X itself; a feature between the samples is invisible (6.3).

6.3 Introduction to the discrete Fourier transform (DFT)

The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) turns N numbers x[n] into N numbers X[k]. It is the DTFT sampled at ω = 2πk/N, so a computer can calculate a spectrum with finite arithmetic. The same finite sum is the Fourier-series analysis sum of the periodic extension of x[n]. The DFT is used in spectrum analysers, audio frames, OFDM in 4G and 5G, and any phone that runs a fast Fourier transform (FFT). This chapter defines the N-point DFT, its matrix form, circular operations, zero padding, Parseval and symmetry.

You need: Chapter 6.1, Chapter 6.2, and Unit 5 on discrete-time signals.

Definition and matrix form

Let x[n] be a length-N sequence, n = 0, 1, ..., N−1. Define the twiddle factor WN = e−j2π/N. It is a point on the unit circle at angle −2π/N. The N-point DFT is the finite sum below. Equivalently, X[k] is the DTFS analysis sum of the periodic extension of x[n], with the 1/N factor moved to synthesis.

X[k] = Σ<sub>n=0</sub><sup>N−1</sup> x[n] W<sub>N</sub><sup>nk</sup>

X[k] is the kth DFT value, k = 0, ..., N−1; x[n] is the input sequence; WN = e−j2π/N is the twiddle factor.

The inverse DFT returns x[n] from X[k]. The factor 1/N belongs to synthesis.

x[n] = (1/N) Σ<sub>k=0</sub><sup>N−1</sup> X[k] W<sub>N</sub><sup>−nk</sup>

x[n] is the recovered sequence; X[k] is the DFT; 1/N is the synthesis scale factor.

For a length-N sequence, X[k] equals the DTFT X(ejω) at ω = 2πk/N. These are N equally spaced samples of one 2π period. The DFT is exact at those points; it says nothing between them.

In matrix form X = W x. The row k, column n entry of W is WNnk. The inverse is x = (1/N) WH X, because WH W = N I. A computer therefore needs only a finite matrix-vector product.

Circular shift, circular convolution, zero padding

The DFT treats x[n] as one period of a periodic sequence of period N. A shift must wrap around. The index (n−m) mod N is a circular shift, not an ordinary delay.

y[n] = x[(n−m) mod N]  →  Y[k] = W<sub>N</sub><sup>km</sup> X[k]

m is an integer shift; mod N wraps indices into 0 to N−1; WN = e−j2π/N.

Circular convolution is defined next. Multiplication in the DFT domain gives circular convolution, because the sum wraps at N.

y[n] = Σ<sub>m=0</sub><sup>N−1</sup> x[m] h[(n−m) mod N]  →  Y[k] = X[k] H[k]

x[n] and h[n] both have length N; (n−m) mod N wraps the tail of h to the start; X[k] and H[k] are N-point DFTs.

Linear convolution of lengths N and M has length N+M−1. To get it from an L-point DFT, zero-pad both sequences to L ≥ N+M−1. For two length-N sequences, use L ≥ 2N−1. Without enough zeros, the tail wraps and adds to the start.

Zero padding to length L gives an L-point DFT, which is the DTFT of the original finite sequence sampled at L frequencies. More zeros give a finer frequency grid. They do not create new time information, and they do not improve true resolution set by the observation length.

Parseval, symmetry and computation

Parseval for the DFT relates time-domain energy to DFT-bin energy.

Σ<sub>n=0</sub><sup>N−1</sup> |x[n]|² = (1/N) Σ<sub>k=0</sub><sup>N−1</sup> |X[k]|²

|x[n]|² is the energy of sample n; |X[k]|² is the energy of bin k; N is the DFT length.

If x[n] is real, X[k] = X*[(-k) mod N]. Equivalently X[N−k] = X*[k] for k = 1, ..., N−1, and X[0] is real. Half the spectrum therefore carries the full information.

These symmetries explain why a real audio frame can be stored as about N/2+1 complex bins: the rest are conjugates. They also warn you that a peak in |X[k]| lies on the N-point grid; the true peak may lie between bins.

Fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithms compute the same X[k] with about N log₂ N operations instead of N². They change speed, not the definition. Spectrum analysers use DFT bins, OFDM in 4G and 5G uses inverse DFT to make subcarriers, and a phone computes FFTs on short audio frames.

Worked example

Question. An N = 4 sequence is x[0] = 1, x[1] = 2, x[2] = 3, x[3] = 4. (a) Find X[k] for k = 0, 1, 2, 3 using W4. (b) Recover x[0] from the inverse DFT.

  1. N = 4 and W4 = e−j2π/4 = e−jπ/2 = −j. Its powers are W40 = 1, W41 = −j, W42 = −1, W43 = j.
  2. k = 0: X[0] = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 = 10.
  3. k = 1: X[1] = 1 + 2(−j) + 3(−1) + 4(j) = 1 − 2j − 3 + 4j = −2 + 2j.
  4. k = 2: X[2] = 1 + 2(−1) + 3(1) + 4(−1) = 1 − 2 + 3 − 4 = −2.
  5. k = 3: X[3] = 1 + 2(j) + 3(−1) + 4(−j) = 1 + 2j − 3 − 4j = −2 − 2j.
  6. x[0] = (1/4)(X[0] + X[1] + X[2] + X[3]) = (1/4)(10 − 2 + 2j − 2 − 2 − 2j) = (1/4)(4) = 1.

Answer. X[0] = 10, X[1] = −2 + 2j, X[2] = −2, X[3] = −2 − 2j, and the inverse gives x[0] = 1.

6.4 Z-transform and its region of convergence

The z-transform turns a sequence x[n] into a function X(z) of a complex variable z. It exists because the DTFT does not converge for every sequence. Adding the real factor r = |z| lets the sum converge in a ring of the z-plane. That ring, the region of convergence or ROC, is part of the answer. Without it, the algebra cannot tell a right-sided sequence from a left-sided one. Engineers use this in difference-equation solving, digital filter design, control, and any place where a sampled signal needs a compact frequency-domain description.

You need: Unit 4 (Laplace transform and ROC) and the DTFT definition in chapter 6.2.

Definition and absolute convergence

X(z) = Σ<sub>n=−∞</sub><sup>∞</sup> x[n] z<sup>−n</sup>

x[n] is the sequence; z is a complex number; n is an integer index.

Write z = r ejθ, where r = |z| is the radius and θ is the angle. The magnitude of a term is |x[n]| r−n, because |z−n| = r−n. The series converges absolutely when Σ |x[n]| r−n < ∞. The ROC is the set of all z for which this inequality holds. Absolute convergence is stronger than ordinary convergence, and it is the standard definition here.

If z = ejω, then r = 1 and X(ejω) = Σ x[n] e−jωn, the DTFT of chapter 6.2. This equality holds only when the unit circle |z| = 1 lies inside the ROC.

Shape of the ROC

Convergence depends on r, not on θ. So the ROC is built from circles centred at z = 0. It is a ring (an annulus), the outside of one circle, the inside of one circle, or the whole plane with possible holes at 0 or ∞. It is connected, and it contains no poles of X(z). A pole is a value of z where X(z) becomes infinite; absolute convergence cannot hold there.

Four cases cover most exam questions. A finite-length sequence is nonzero only for n1 ≤ n ≤ n2. Its ROC is the whole z-plane except possibly z = 0 (when n2 > 0) and z = ∞ (when n1 < 0). A right-sided sequence is zero for n < N0; its ROC is |z| > r2, outside the outermost pole radius. A left-sided sequence is zero for n > N0; its ROC is |z| < r1, inside the innermost pole radius. A two-sided sequence has an annulus r2 < |z| < r1. If the two inequalities do not overlap, the z-transform does not exist.

The unit circle is the set |z| = 1. If it lies inside the ROC, the DTFT exists and equals X(ejω). If it does not, the DTFT does not exist as an ordinary function, even though the z-transform algebra may look finite.

Standard pairs and the pole-zero plot

  • δ[n] ↔ 1 for all z. The unit impulse has no pole.
  • u[n] ↔ z/(z − 1) for |z| > 1. The pole is at z = 1.
  • anu[n] ↔ z/(z − a) for |z| > |a|. This is right-sided.
  • −anu[−n − 1] ↔ z/(z − a) for |z| < |a|. This is left-sided.

For a = 1/3, both sequences give z/(z − 1/3), but the right-sided one has ROC |z| > 1/3 and the left-sided one has ROC |z| < 1/3. In a pole-zero plot, poles are marked × and zeros ○. The ROC is a region that stops at poles; it never crosses one. The mapping z = esT connects this picture to Unit 4. Here s = σ + jω is the Laplace variable and T is the sampling interval. The left half of the s-plane, σ < 0, maps to |z| < 1 inside the unit circle; σ = 0 maps to |z| = 1.

Worked example

Question. Find X(z), the poles, the zeros and the ROC for x[n] = (1/3)nu[n] − (1/2)nu[−n − 1]. State whether the DTFT exists.

Exam traps

  • Two different sequences share the same X(z). The pair anu[n] and −anu[−n−1] both give z/(z−a). An answer with no ROC is incomplete.
  • A pole can sit at z = 0 or z = ∞ after a shift. For δ[n−1], X(z) = z−1, so z = 0 is excluded even though the sequence is finite length.
  • The ROC never includes a pole. If your region contains z = 1/3 or z = 1/2 in the worked example, it is wrong.
  • The unit circle test is not optional. If |z| = 1 is outside the ROC, do not write X(ejω); say the DTFT does not exist.
  • Right-sided gives |z| > r2 and left-sided gives |z| < r1; do not swap the inequalities. A two-sided sequence needs an annulus, not a half-plane.

6.5 Properties of the Z-transform

Delay a sequence, scale it, multiply it by n, or pass it through a filter, and the z-domain answer changes in a small fixed way. These rules mean you never re-sum a power series by hand: apply the rule to X(z), then fix the region of convergence (ROC) with the same rule. They turn difference equations into algebra and give the output of a digital filter. One caution runs through the chapter: an ROC statement is always 'at least', because a cancelled pole can enlarge the region.

You need: chapter 6.4 — the definition X(z) = Σ x[n]z⁻ⁿ, and how the ROC, not the algebra alone, identifies the sequence.

Linearity, time shift and z-scaling

Every proof here starts from X(z) = Σ x[n]z⁻ⁿ, summed over all integers n. Here x[n] is the sequence, z is a complex number, and the ROC is the set of z where the sum converges.

a·x₁[n] + b·x₂[n] ↔ a·X₁(z) + b·X₂(z)

a and b are constants; X₁(z) and X₂(z) are the transforms of x₁[n] and x₂[n]; the ROC contains the overlap of the two individual ROCs.

Linearity is the sum split into two sums. Each piece converges on its own ROC, so both converge together on the overlap: ROC ⊇ ROC₁ ∩ ROC₂. The word 'contains' matters. A zero of one transform can cancel a pole of the other, and then the real ROC is bigger than the overlap.

x[n − n₀] ↔ z⁻ⁿ⁰ X(z)

n₀ is an integer delay in samples; the ROC is unchanged, except that z = 0 or z = ∞ may drop out or come in, because z⁻ⁿ⁰ adds a pole or a zero there.

Shift proof: put m = n − n₀. Then Σ x[n − n₀]z⁻ⁿ = z⁻ⁿ⁰ Σ x[m]z⁻ᵐ = z⁻ⁿ⁰X(z).

aⁿ x[n] ↔ X(z/a)

a is a nonzero complex constant; if the old ROC is r₁ < |z| < r₂, the new ROC is |a|r₁ < |z| < |a|r₂, so both radii scale by the modulus |a|.

Proof: Σ aⁿx[n]z⁻ⁿ = Σ x[n](z/a)⁻ⁿ = X(z/a). Only |a| enters the radii, so a negative or complex a rescales the ROC without changing its shape.

Reversal, multiplication by n, convolution, accumulation

x[−n] ↔ X(1/z)

the ROC is inverted: 1/r₂ < |z| < 1/r₁. Substitute m = −n in the definition to see it.

n·x[n] ↔ −z·dX/dz

the ROC is unchanged. Differentiate X(z) term by term to get dX/dz = Σ (−n)x[n]z⁻ⁿ⁻¹, then multiply by −z.

x[n] * h[n] ↔ X(z)·H(z)

x[n] * h[n] = Σ x[k]h[n−k]; the ROC contains the overlap of the two ROCs. Put m = n − k, swap the sums, and the inner sum is H(z).

Σ_{k=−∞}^{n} x[k] ↔ X(z)/(1 − z⁻¹)

the ROC contains the overlap of the ROC of X(z) with |z| > 1; the pole at z = 1 comes from the running sum.

The accumulation rule is not new work. A running sum is convolution with the unit step u[n], and u[n] ↔ 1/(1 − z⁻¹) on |z| > 1. Multiply the two transforms, exactly as the convolution rule says.

Cross-correlation follows the same pattern. For r_xy[l] = Σ x[n]y[n − l], the transform is X(z)Y(z⁻¹), with ROC containing the ROC of X(z) and the inverted ROC of Y(z). Set y = x and you get the autocorrelation.

Keep signs straight. Multiplication by n carries a minus sign and a factor z; reversal inverts the ROC instead of keeping it.

Initial and final value theorems

For a causal sequence, x[n] = 0 for n < 0, so X(z) = x[0] + x[1]z⁻¹ + x[2]z⁻² + … As |z| → ∞ every term with z⁻ᵏ, k ≥ 1, dies, so only x[0] survives.

x[0] = lim_{z→∞} X(z)

x[n] must be causal; otherwise X(z) holds positive powers of z and the limit is not x[0].

Check it on x[n] = (0.5)ⁿu[n]. Here X(z) = 1/(1 − 0.5z⁻¹) → 1 as z → ∞, and x[0] = 1. Correct.

lim_{n→∞} x[n] = lim_{z→1} (1 − z⁻¹)X(z)

x[n] is causal, and all its poles lie inside |z| < 1 except at most one simple pole at z = 1. If there is no pole at z = 1, the limit is 0.

Why: (1 − z⁻¹)X(z) is the transform of x[n] − x[n−1]. At z = 1 that transform becomes the total of the differences, a telescoping sum ending at the final value. The step is valid only when the differences die out, which is exactly the pole condition.

Example: X(z) = 1/(1 − 0.5z⁻¹) has its only pole at 0.5, so x[∞] = 0, matching (0.5)ⁿ → 0. For X(z) = 1/(1 − z⁻¹) the pole at z = 1 is simple and no other pole lies on or outside the unit circle, so x[∞] = lim_{z→1} (1 − z⁻¹)/(1 − z⁻¹) = 1, matching u[n] → 1. The theorem fails when a pole other than the single simple one at z = 1 lies on or outside the unit circle, for example z = 2 in 2ⁿu[n].

Exam traps

  • Quoting the ROC as exactly ROC₁ ∩ ROC₂. A cancelled pole can enlarge it: u[n] − u[n−1] = δ[n], and the two transforms differ by exactly one factor z⁻¹, giving 1 for every z ≠ 0.
  • Using the initial value theorem on a non-causal sequence. If x[n] is non-zero for n < 0, X(z) contains positive powers of z and lim as z → ∞ is not x[0].
  • Dropping the modulus in z-scaling. The ROC radii multiply by |a|, not by a, so a = −3 gives |a| = 3 and the ROC widens, it does not flip sign.
  • Saying the ROC is 'unchanged' under a time shift without the escape clause: z = 0 or z = ∞ can enter or leave.
  • Applying the final value theorem when a pole lies on or outside the unit circle. For x[n] = 2ⁿu[n] the formula quietly returns 0, but the sequence diverges.

6.6 Inverse Z-transform

The inverse Z-transform recovers the sequence x[n] from its z-domain expression X(z). The algebra alone is not enough: the region of convergence (ROC) fixes whether each pole produces a right-sided or a left-sided term. The formal inverse is a contour integral, but three practical methods do the work: power series, partial fractions and residues. Engineers use it to turn a transfer function or a difference-equation solution back into a discrete-time signal, for example the samples of a speech frame or the impulse response of a digital filter.

You need: chapter 6.4 for X(z) and the ROC, chapter 6.5 for standard pairs, and Unit 4, Inverse Laplace transform by partial fractions, for the algebra.

The inversion formula and what the ROC does

The formal inverse is a contour integral. Evaluate it inside the ROC, not on a pole.

x[n] = (1/(2πj)) ∮<sub>C</sub> X(z) z<sup>n−1</sup> dz

C is a closed counterclockwise contour inside the ROC; j = √(−1); n is the sample index; X(z) is the z-transform.

Direct integration is rare. Power series, or long division, is the first practical method. If the ROC is |z| > r, the sequence is right-sided, so divide to get a series in z⁻¹: X(z) = Σ x[n] z−n. Read x[n] as the coefficient of z−n. If the ROC is |z| < r, the sequence is left-sided, so divide to get a series in positive powers of z: X(z) = Σ x[−m] zm. Read x[−m] as the coefficient of zm. Partial fractions and residues are the other two methods.

Partial fractions: split X(z)/z and let the ROC choose the side

Partial fractions turn X(z) into first-order terms whose inverse pairs are known. The ROC decides the side of each term.

  1. Factor the denominator of X(z) to find the poles pk.
  2. Form X(z)/z and write it as Σ Ak/(z − pk).
  3. Find each coefficient from Ak = (z − pk) X(z)/z evaluated at z = pk.
  4. Multiply back by z: X(z) = Σ Ak z/(z − pk) = Σ Ak/(1 − pk z−1).
  5. Use the ROC of each pole: |z| > |pk| gives pknu[n]; |z| < |pk| gives −pknu[−n−1].
TermROCx[n]
1/(1 − a z⁻¹)|z| > |a|aⁿu[n]
1/(1 − a z⁻¹)|z| < |a|−aⁿu[−n−1]

The same term 1/(1 − 0.5z⁻¹) gives 0.5ⁿu[n] when |z| > 0.5, and −0.5ⁿu[−n−1] when |z| < 0.5. The algebra is identical; only the ROC changes the answer.

Residues, repeated poles and complex poles

The residue method sums residues of X(z) zn−1 at the poles inside C. For a simple pole at z = p, the residue is limz→p (z − p) X(z) zn−1.

x[n] = Σ Res[X(z) z<sup>n−1</sup> at poles inside C]

the sum runs over every pole of X(z) zn−1 that lies inside the contour C.

For a pole of order m at z = p, use the repeated-pole formula: Res = (1/(m−1)!) limz→p dm−1/dzm−1 [(z − p)m X(z) zn−1]. Complex poles appear in conjugate pairs; combine them into a quadratic factor and use rncos(ωn) and rnsin(ωn) pairs. Include a pole at z = 0 when it lies inside C; for n < 0 the factor zn−1 is a negative power of z, so z = 0 becomes a pole of X(z) zn−1 and adds a residue.

  2. Form X(z)/z = z/[(z − 0.5)(z − 2)] = A/(z − 0.5) + B/(z − 2).

6.7 Analysis of discrete-time LTI systems using Z-transform

The z-transform turns a difference equation into algebra. For zero initial conditions the ratio H(z) = Y(z)/X(z) is the transfer function, and the impulse response h[n] is its inverse transform. The region of convergence (ROC) decides causality and BIBO stability. Setting z = e^{jω} gives the frequency response, so one function gives a digital filter's gain and phase. Engineers use this before coding a UPI fraud filter, an audio equaliser or a motor controller: poles near the unit circle warn of slow ringing.

You need: the z-transform and ROC, inverse z-transform, difference equations from Unit 5, and BIBO stability and causality from Unit 2.

From difference equation to H(z)

A discrete-time LTI system obeys Σk=0N ak y[n-k] = Σk=0M bk x[n-k]. The ak and bk are real constants; a0 is usually 1. Take the two-sided z-transform with zero initial conditions. A shift by k multiplies a transform by z−k, so the equation becomes Σ ak z−kY(z)=Σ bk z−kX(z).

H(z) = Y(z)/X(z) = (Σ<sub>k=0</sub><sup>M</sup> b<sub>k</sub> z<sup>−k</sup>) / (Σ<sub>k=0</sub><sup>N</sup> a<sub>k</sub> z<sup>−k</sup>)

Y(z) and X(z) are z-transforms of output and input; ak, bk are real coefficients; M and N are non-negative integers; H(z) is the transfer function.

The denominator roots are poles; numerator roots are zeros. The impulse response is h[n]=Z−1{H(z)}, because x[n]=δ[n] gives X(z)=1 and Y(z)=H(z). Always quote the ROC with H(z). The ROC of Y(z) is at least the intersection of the ROCs of X(z) and H(z), and can be larger if poles and zeros cancel. The ROC of H(z) itself is fixed by its own poles — for example |z| > r for a causal system. For y[n] − 0.5y[n−1] = x[n], H(z) = 1/(1 − 0.5z−1), pole z = 0.5.

ROC decides causality and stability

Causality means h[n] = 0 for n < 0. For a causal rational H(z) the ROC is |z| > r, where r is the largest pole magnitude; it includes z = ∞ when H(∞) is finite. BIBO stability means every bounded input gives a bounded output. It holds when Σ|h[n]|<∞, which for rational H(z) is exactly when the ROC contains the unit circle |z|=1.

For a causal system the ROC is |z|>r. Stability then needs r<1, so every pole lies strictly inside the unit circle. A pole at 0.5 is stable; a pole at 1.2 is causal but unstable. A stable non-causal system can have an annular ROC such as 0.5<|z|<2 that contains |z|=1. Read stability from the ROC, not from poles alone.

The same H(z) with |z|>0.5 gives h[n]=(0.5)nu[n], causal and stable. With |z| < 0.5 the algebra gives h[n] = −(0.5)nu[−n−1], non-causal and unstable because |z| = 1 is outside the ROC.

Frequency response and sinusoidal steady state

If the ROC contains |z|=1, put z=ejω. H(ejω) is the frequency response; ω is in radians per sample and the response repeats every 2π. |H(ejω)| is gain, ∠H(ejω) is phase in radians. For H(z)=1/(1−a z−1), |H(ejω)|=1/√(1+a²−2a cos ω). A pole at a=0.9 gives gain 10 at ω=0; a=0.5 gives gain 2. The nearer pole gives a sharper low-pass peak.

y<sub>ss</sub>[n] = A |H(e<sup>jω₀</sup>)| cos(ω₀ n + φ + ∠H(e<sup>jω₀</sup>))

A is input amplitude; ω₀ is input frequency in rad/sample; φ is input phase in radians; yss is the steady-state output; the system must be stable.

For a=0.5 and ω0=π/3, |H|=1/√(1.25−cos(π/3))≈1.1547 and ∠H=−π/6. Thus x[n]=3 cos(πn/3) gives yss[n]≈3.464 cos(πn/3−π/6).

Initial conditions and inverse systems

Initial conditions use the one-sided transform Y⁺(z)=Σn=0∞ y[n]z−n. Shifts: y[n−1]→z−1Y⁺(z)+y[−1]; y[n−2]→z−2Y⁺(z)+z−1y[−1]+y[−2]. Substitute y[−1], y[−2] before solving.

For y[n]−0.5y[n−1]=x[n] with y[−1]=2, Y⁺(z)−0.5(z−1Y⁺(z)+2)=X(z). So Y⁺(z)=[X(z)+1]/(1−0.5z−1). The constant 1 is zero-input; X(z)/(1−0.5z−1) is zero-state. With x[n]=u[n], inverting gives (2−(0.5)n)u[n] + (0.5)nu[n] = 2u[n].

An inverse system has Hi(z)=1/H(z). Its poles are zeros of H(z). If H(z)=(1−2z−1)/(1−0.5z−1), H is stable (pole 0.5) but the causal inverse has a pole at 2, so it is unstable. Only a few syllabi list deconvolution.

Worked example

Question. A system obeys y[n] - 0.5 y[n-1] = x[n] with zero initial conditions. Find H(z) and its ROC, decide causality and stability, give h[n], and find the step response for x[n] = u[n].

  1. Take the z-transform with zero initial conditions: Y(z) - 0.5z⁻¹Y(z) = X(z).
  2. Solve the ratio: H(z) = Y(z)/X(z) = 1/(1 - 0.5z⁻¹).
  3. Set the denominator to zero: 1 - 0.5z⁻¹ = 0 gives pole z = 0.5.
  4. For a causal impulse response the ROC is the exterior of the pole circle: |z| > 0.5.
  5. The unit circle |z| = 1 lies inside |z| > 0.5, so the system is causal and BIBO stable.
  6. The inverse transform of H(z) with ROC |z| > 0.5 is h[n] = (0.5)^n u[n].
  7. For x[n] = u[n], X(z) = 1/(1 - z⁻¹), so Y(z) = 1/[(1 - z⁻¹)(1 - 0.5z⁻¹)].
  8. Partial fractions: Y(z) = 2/(1 - z⁻¹) - 1/(1 - 0.5z⁻¹).
  9. Inverse transform: y[n] = (2 - (0.5)^n)u[n]. Then y[0] = 1, y[1] = 1.5, y[2] = 1.75, and the steady-state value is 2.

Answer. H(z) = 1/(1 - 0.5z⁻¹), ROC |z| > 0.5, causal and BIBO stable, h[n] = (0.5)^n u[n], and the step response is y[n] = (2 - (0.5)^n)u[n] with steady-state value 2.

Exam traps

  • Reading stability from pole positions alone: stability means the ROC contains |z|=1; for a causal system every pole must be strictly inside the unit circle.
  • Marking a causal system stable when a pole sits exactly on |z|=1. The ROC then starts at that circle and does not contain it, so BIBO stability fails.
  • Using H(e^{jω}) when the ROC excludes the unit circle. The algebra still evaluates, but it is not a steady-state frequency response.
  • Dropping the y[−1] term in one-sided transforms. y[n−1] becomes z⁻¹Y⁺(z)+y[−1], not just z⁻¹Y⁺(z).
  • Assuming 1/H(z) is stable because H(z) is. Zeros of H outside the unit circle become unstable poles of the causal inverse.

Takeaways

  1. A period-N sequence carries only N independent complex exponentials, so its DTFS has exactly N distinct coefficients and then repeats.
  2. The DTFT is continuous in ω but always periodic with period 2π, because discrete time makes frequency a wrapped, circular quantity.
  3. The N-point DFT is just the DTFT sampled at ω = 2πk/N, which is why it can be computed as a finite N × N matrix product.
  4. The z-transform is a power series; the algebraic formula alone is incomplete, and the ROC is what pins down the sequence.
  5. Every operation you do in time — shift, scale, convolve, difference — has a one-line z-domain counterpart with a predictable effect on the ROC.
  6. Inverse z-transform is two steps: invert the algebra, then use the ROC to choose causal or anti-causal terms for each pole.
  7. The poles of H(z) relative to the unit circle decide stability and shape the frequency response, without ever solving the difference equation in time.
  8. A causal system is stable only if every pole is strictly inside |z| = 1; a single pole on or outside the circle breaks it.

Glossary

Absolutely summable
Property that the sum of the magnitudes of the sequence terms is finite.
BIBO stability
The property that every bounded input produces a bounded output; for causal z-transforms it means all poles inside the unit circle.
Causal sequence
A sequence that is zero for all negative values of n.
Circular convolution
The convolution of two periodic sequences taken over one period; it is what multiplication of DFTs corresponds to.
Difference equation
A relation expressing the present output sample in terms of past outputs and present and past inputs.
Discrete Fourier transform (DFT)
An N-point transform that maps N time samples to N frequency samples equally spaced over one full 2π turn.
Discrete-time Fourier series (DTFS)
The representation of a periodic discrete-time sequence as a finite sum of N complex exponentials.
Discrete-time Fourier transform (DTFT)
The representation of an aperiodic sequence as a continuous, 2π-periodic function of frequency ω.
Frequency response
The complex gain H(e^(jω)) that an LTI system applies to a complex exponential input at frequency ω.
Linear convolution
The ordinary convolution sum of two aperiodic sequences, equal to circular convolution when the period is long enough.
Minimum-phase system
A causal, stable system whose zeros also all lie inside the unit circle, so a causal stable inverse exists.
Partial fraction expansion
A way of writing a rational transform as a sum of simple fractions whose inverse transforms are known.
Pole
A value of z at which the transfer function or transform becomes infinite.
Region of convergence (ROC)
The set of complex z values for which the z-transform sum converges absolutely.
Right-sided sequence
A sequence that is zero for n less than some finite index, not necessarily zero.
Spectral leakage
The spreading of energy from one frequency bin into neighbouring bins when the record length is not an integer number of periods.
Transfer function H(z)
The z-transform of the impulse response of an LTI system; it equals Y(z)/X(z) for zero initial conditions.
Twiddle factor
The constant W_N = e^(−j2π/N) raised to integer powers inside the DFT sum.
Unit circle
The set |z| = 1 in the z-plane; on it the z-transform becomes the DTFT.
Z-transform
The power series X(z) = Σ x[n] z^(−n) that generalises the DTFT to complex values of z.
Zero
A value of z at which the numerator, and hence the transform, equals zero.
Zero padding
Appending zeros to a finite record before the DFT, which interpolates the spectrum without improving resolution.
