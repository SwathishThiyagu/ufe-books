3.1 Low-frequency response and lower cut-off frequency

An amplifier does not amplify DC and very low frequencies the same way it amplifies midband signals. Coupling capacitors block DC and bypass capacitors hold the emitter or source at AC ground, so the signal does not see the emitter or source resistor. At low frequency these capacitors no longer look like short circuits. Each capacitor with the resistance around it forms an RC high-pass filter. The gain falls below the midband value. The lower cut-off frequency fL is where gain is 0.707 of midband, the -3 dB point. This matters in audio preamplifiers, ECG and other sensor front ends, and seismic recorders, where signals extend down to tens of hertz.

You need Unit 2: RC coupling, CE amplifier, input and output resistance.

Each capacitor is an RC high-pass

An RC-coupled amplifier has capacitors between stages and at the input and output. These capacitors stop DC from shifting the bias point. At midband, their reactance X_C = 1/(2πfC) is small, so they act almost as short circuits. At low frequency X_C rises. The capacitor and the resistance around it then form a high-pass filter, and the gain falls. The frequency where the gain is 0.707 of its midband value Am is the lower cut-off fL; some syllabi write f1. The figure compares two amplifiers with Am = 40 dB, one with fL = 50 Hz and one with fL = 200 Hz. The 200 Hz curve is still 3 dB down at 200 Hz; the 50 Hz curve is already near midband there.

fL = 1/(2πRC)

fL is the lower cut-off frequency in hertz; R is the resistance seen by the capacitor in ohms; C is the capacitance in farads.

On a Bode magnitude plot, the vertical axis is 20 log10 |A| in dB. At fL the curve is 3 dB below the flat midband level, because 20 log10(0.707) = -3 dB. Below fL the curve rises at about +20 dB per decade as frequency increases toward midband.

Finding the overall lower cut-off

Three capacitors usually set the low-frequency response of a BJT stage. The input coupling capacitor sees Rs + Rin, where Rs is the source resistance and Rin is the amplifier input resistance. The output coupling capacitor sees Ro + RL, where Ro is the output resistance and RL is the load. The emitter bypass capacitor CE sees a resistance near RE in parallel with the path through the base, often written RE || (re + (RB||Rs)/β). Each gives its own fL. The overall lower cut-off is not the smallest of these values. It is set mainly by the largest one. Reason: each capacitor multiplies the gain by (f/fi)/√(1+(f/fi)²). The highest corner is the last to reach midband, so it pulls the total 3 dB down first. If one corner is much larger than the others, the overall fL is close to that largest value. The same idea applies to a FET: the gate coupling capacitor, drain coupling capacitor and source bypass capacitor each produce an RC high-pass corner. The midband gain is the flat gain between fL and fH. Bandwidth is fH - fL, so fL is the start of the bandwidth; <a href='#high-frequency-response-and-miller-effect'>Chapter 3.2 covers fH.

Bypass capacitor and emitter resistance

The emitter bypass capacitor changes the emitter resistance seen by the signal. At midband CE is a short, so it bypasses RE. The emitter resistance is then only the small dynamic resistance re, and the stage has high gain. At low frequency CE is open. The emitter resistance becomes RE + re, negative feedback increases, and gain falls. The corner for this effect is fE = 1/(2πRCE CE), where RCE is the resistance seen by CE: roughly RE in parallel with (re + (RB||Rs)/β). If RE is large, CE must also be large to keep fE low. In a FET stage the source bypass capacitor CS does the same job: it shorts RS at midband and raises gain; at low frequency it opens and gain falls. The output coupling capacitor has a similar role but it sees Ro + RL, not the emitter. Use each capacitor's own resistance when you compute its corner. A common exam mistake is to use RE for the input coupling capacitor, or to use CE with the output resistance.

Worked example

Question. A common-emitter amplifier has input resistance 10 kΩ, input coupling capacitor 1 µF, output resistance 50 kΩ and output coupling capacitor 0.1 µF. Midband gain is 100. Find each lower cut-off, the overall fL, and the gain at 10 Hz.

  1. Input coupling: R1 = 10 kΩ = 1×10^4 Ω and C1 = 1 µF = 1×10^-6 F. f1 = 1/(2πR1C1) = 1/(2π×10^4×10^-6) = 15.9 Hz.
  2. Output coupling: R2 = 50 kΩ = 5×10^4 Ω and C2 = 0.1 µF = 1×10^-7 F. f2 = 1/(2πR2C2) = 1/(2π×5×10^4×1×10^-7) = 31.8 Hz.
  3. The larger individual corner is close to the overall fL, but it is not exact. At 31.8 Hz the output capacitor is exactly at its corner and contributes 3 dB of loss. The input capacitor is a factor of two above its own corner and contributes only about 1 dB of loss, so the total is about 4 dB down. The combined -3 dB point is therefore slightly above 31.8 Hz, about 38 Hz. Quote fL ≈ 38 Hz, or say 31.8 Hz is a lower bound.
  4. At f = 10 Hz, the input capacitor is far from a short, so use both high-pass factors. Gain = Am × [(f/f1)/√(1+(f/f1)²)] × [(f/f2)/√(1+(f/f2)²)] = 100 × [(10/15.9)/√(1+(10/15.9)²)] × [(10/31.8)/√(1+(10/31.8)²)] = 100 × 0.532 × 0.300 ≈ 16.0. A value near 30.0 is only a rough upper estimate that pretends the input capacitor is already a short.

Answer. The input coupling corner is 15.9 Hz, the output coupling corner is 31.8 Hz, the overall fL is about 38 Hz, and the gain at 10 Hz is about 16.0.

Exam traps

  • Taking the smallest individual lower cut-off as the overall fL. The largest corner dominates.
  • Saying the gain at fL is full midband gain. At fL it is 0.707 of midband, that is 3 dB down.
  • Forgetting to convert microfarads to farads. A capacitor marked 1 µF is 1×10^-6 F in the formula.
  • Using the wrong resistance in fL = 1/(2πRC). Use the resistance seen by that particular capacitor, not RE or Ro by habit.
  • Treating the emitter bypass capacitor as open at midband. At midband it is a short and removes emitter degeneration.

3.2 High-frequency response and Miller effect

At 1 kHz a transistor's internal capacitors are open circuits, so gain is flat. At 1 MHz they shunt the signal and gain falls. The capacitor from output back to input, Cμ, is the worst: its ends move oppositely, so it draws much more current than its size suggests. This is the Miller effect. It sets the upper cut-off frequency fH, where gain drops to 0.707 of midband.

You need the hybrid-pi model, rπ and gm from Unit 2, Transistor Biasing and Small-Signal Amplifiers, plus midband gain and RC time constants from Chapter 3.1.

The hybrid-pi model above midband

The hybrid-pi model of Unit 2 is a midband model. It keeps rπ and gm and discards every capacitor inside the transistor. That works while each capacitor has reactance far larger than nearby resistors. At higher frequency the reactance falls and these capacitors shunt the signal.

A BJT needs two capacitors.

  • Cπ, base to emitter. Typical 10 pF to 200 pF; grows with DC collector current. It is mostly charge stored in the base by the forward-biased emitter junction.
  • Cμ, base to collector. Typical 1 pF to 10 pF; shrinks as collector-base voltage rises. It is mostly depletion charge of the reverse-biased collector-base junction.

Some syllabi call Cπ diffusion capacitance and Cμ transition capacitance. The model still has only two capacitors.

rπ is base-emitter resistance in ohms: rπ = V_T/I_B, where V_T ≈ 26 mV at room temperature and I_B is DC base current. gm is transconductance in siemens: gm = I_C/V_T, where I_C is DC collector current. Also gm rπ = β, where β is DC current gain.

The FET model has the same shape but no rπ. At low frequency its insulated gate draws no current. Its capacitors are Cgs, gate to source, and Cgd, gate to drain, both a few pF. Above midband these two dominate.

The Miller effect

Current through a capacitor C is jωC times the voltage across it, where ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s. Connect C between input and output of an inverting amplifier with gain −A, where A > 0 is gain magnitude. The output moves opposite to the input, so the capacitor voltage exceeds the input voltage.

  1. Input node is V_in; output node is V_out = −A V_in.
  2. Voltage across Cμ = V_in − V_out = V_in(1 + A).
  3. Current from input node through Cμ = jωCμ V_in(1 + A).
  4. A capacitor C_eq to ground would draw jωC_eq V_in.
  5. Equate currents: C_eq = Cμ(1 + A).

C_eq is the Miller capacitance. Nothing was added; the input node behaves as if a much larger capacitor sat on it.

C_M = Cμ (1 + |A_v|)

C_M is Miller input capacitance in farads; Cμ is physical collector-base or gate-drain capacitance in farads; |A_v| is midband voltage gain magnitude between those nodes, dimensionless.

The same capacitor loads the output less: seen from output, the Miller output capacitance is C_out ≈ Cμ(1 + 1/|A_v|) ≈ Cμ. For A = 100 the factor is 1.01.

C_out ≈ Cμ (1 + 1/|A_v|)

C_out is extra capacitance at the output node in farads; other symbols are as above. For |A_v| ≥ 10 this is within 10 % of Cμ.

Use (1 + |A_v|), never |A_v| alone. At A = 100 they differ by 1 %; at A = 4 they differ by 20 %. A FET obeys C_M = Cgd(1 + |A_v|); Cgd of 1–5 pF with gain 50 becomes 50–250 pF. Miller multiplication applies to any impedance between input and output: a resistor there is divided by (1 + A).

Upper cut-off frequency and why the CE stage is slow

  1. Replace source and bias resistors by one input resistance: R_eq = R_s ‖ R_B ‖ rπ, in ohms, where R_s is source resistance, R_B is bias network seen from base, rπ is base-emitter resistance.
  2. Add input capacitances: C_eq = Cπ + C_M, in farads.
  3. Input time constant: τ_in = R_eq C_eq, in seconds.
  4. Input pole: f_in = 1/(2π R_eq C_eq), in hertz.
  5. At output: R_out = R_C ‖ R_L ‖ r_o and C_out' = Cμ(1 + 1/|A_v|), giving f_out = 1/(2π R_out C_out'). R_C is collector resistor, R_L load, r_o transistor output resistance.
  6. fH is the lower of f_in and f_out, because the lower pole bends gain down first.

fH = 1 / (2π R_eq C_eq)

fH is upper cut-off frequency in hertz, where |A_v| falls to 0.707 of midband (3 dB down); R_eq is input-node resistance in ohms; C_eq = Cπ + Cμ(1 + |A_v|) is input-node capacitance in farads.

The lower pole is the dominant pole, and fH ≈ f_dominant. Two close poles pull gain down slightly earlier; the estimate is standard and good when poles are far apart.

A CE stage is slow because Cμ is multiplied by full gain while rπ is only a few kΩ. Hundreds of pF times a few kΩ gives microseconds, so fH is tens of kHz. A CB stage escapes: input is emitter and base is grounded, so Cμ runs from collector to ground and sees no gain multiplication. A CS stage has the CE problem exactly, with Cgd replacing Cμ.

The standard cure is the cascode from Unit 2. A common-base transistor sits above the input device and holds its collector almost still, so gain across its own Cμ is near 1 and Miller multiplication almost vanishes. Chapter 3.4 builds on this.

Worked example

Question. A CE stage has |A_v| = 100, Cμ = 2 pF, Cπ = 10 pF, R_s = 1 kΩ. Bias network and rπ are large enough to ignore. Find (a) Miller input capacitance, (b) total input capacitance, (c) upper cut-off set by input pole, (d) Miller output capacitance.

  1. C_M = Cμ(1 + |A_v|) = 2 pF × 101 = 202 pF.
  2. C_eq = Cπ + C_M = 10 pF + 202 pF = 212 pF. Cπ is added once because it goes to ground.
  3. R_eq = R_s = 1 kΩ = 1000 Ω.
  4. τ_in = R_eq C_eq = 1000 Ω × 212×10⁻¹² F = 2.12×10⁻⁷ s = 212 ns.
  5. fH = 1/(2π τ_in) = 1/(2π × 2.12×10⁻⁷ s) = 750.7 kHz ≈ 751 kHz.
  6. C_out = Cμ(1 + 1/|A_v|) = 2 pF × 1.01 = 2.02 pF.

Answer. C_M = 202 pF, total input capacitance 212 pF, C_out = 2.02 pF, and fH ≈ 751 kHz.

Exam traps

  • Using |A_v| instead of (1 + |A_v|). At gain 4, C_M becomes 8 pF instead of 10 pF, a 20 % error.
  • Adding Cπ to Cμ and then multiplying the sum by gain. Only Cμ bridges input and output; Cπ goes to ground and is added once.
  • Using the signed gain. With A_v = −100, (1 − 100) = −99 gives negative capacitance. Use magnitude.
  • Multiplying output capacitance by full gain. Output sees Cμ(1 + 1/|A_v|) ≈ Cμ, not Cμ(1 + |A_v|).
  • Quoting fH as fT. fH is where this wired stage's gain falls to 0.707 of midband; fT is where transistor open-loop current gain reaches 1 — see Chapter 3.3.

3.3 Gain-bandwidth product and transition frequency

An amplifier cannot give high gain over a wide band of frequencies. In a single-pole amplifier the gain falls at 20 dB per decade above its dominant pole, so gain multiplied by bandwidth stays fixed. That constant is the gain-bandwidth product, GBW. The frequency where the open-loop gain falls to 1 is the transition frequency fT, and for a single-pole amplifier fT = GBW. Datasheets quote fT, and a designer uses it to find the closed-loop bandwidth at any gain: bandwidth = fT ÷ closed-loop gain.

You need: the upper cut-off frequency and the 0.707 point from Chapter 3.2, and the ideal op-amp model from Unit 6.

The gain falls, the product stays

Every amplifier has internal capacitance, so its gain falls at high frequency. In a single-pole amplifier one pole dominates: the gain is flat at A₀ up to the pole frequency f_p, and above f_p it falls at 20 dB per decade. A fall of 20 dB per decade means the gain halves every time the frequency doubles.

A(f) = A₀ ÷ √(1 + (f/f_p)²)

A(f) is the open-loop gain magnitude at frequency f, in V/V; A₀ is the low-frequency gain in V/V; f_p is the dominant pole frequency in Hz, the point where A = 0.707 A₀; f is the signal frequency in Hz.

When f is much larger than f_p, the 1 under the square root can be dropped, and the gain becomes A(f) ≈ A₀ f_p / f. Multiply both sides by f: A(f) × f ≈ A₀ f_p. The product is a constant, independent of frequency. That constant is the gain-bandwidth product, written GBW = A₀ × f_p, measured in hertz.

The product stays fixed because the slope is fixed. Every extra unit of gain costs the same factor of bandwidth, so you can trade one for the other but cannot get both. An op-amp with A₀ = 2×10⁵ and f_p = 10 Hz has GBW = 2 MHz; its gain is about 200 at 10 kHz and about 20 at 100 kHz.

Transition frequency f_T

Put A(f) = 1 in the falling part of the curve: 1 = A₀ f_p / f_T, so f_T = A₀ × f_p = GBW. At f_T the open-loop gain is exactly 1 (0 dB). A signal passed through the bare op-amp comes out with the same amplitude it went in; above f_T the op-amp attenuates.

f_T is the same number as GBW, written as a frequency. Some syllabi call it the unity-gain frequency, unity-gain bandwidth, or the cut-off frequency. A datasheet may print "gain bandwidth product 2 MHz" or "unity gain bandwidth 2 MHz". Keep it separate from f_p: f_p is the −3 dB point of the open-loop curve, f_T is the 0 dB point.

Engineers treat f_T as the figure of merit for speed. Two op-amps with the same low-frequency gain can differ tenfold in f_T, and the faster one gives ten times the closed-loop bandwidth at every gain.

Closed-loop bandwidth and the trade-off

A_CL = 1 + R_f / R_1

A_CL is the closed-loop gain of the non-inverting amplifier in V/V; R_f is the feedback resistor in Ω; R_1 is the resistor from the inverting input to ground in Ω.

Negative feedback pushes the closed-loop pole up by the same factor that it pushes the gain down. The closed-loop response is still single-pole, with bandwidth BW_CL = f_T / A_CL. The gain-bandwidth product of the closed-loop amplifier is A_CL × BW_CL = f_T, unchanged from the open-loop value. See Unit 4, Feedback Amplifiers and Oscillators, for the full derivation.

At f_T = 2 MHz, a gain of 20 gives 100 kHz and a gain of 200 gives 10 kHz. A voltage follower, A_CL = 1, gives the full 2 MHz and is the fastest configuration. A closed-loop gain set lower than 1 is not possible without extra circuitry, so f_T is the ceiling for closed-loop bandwidth.

For the inverting amplifier, the bandwidth is set by the noise gain 1 + R_f/R_1, not by the signal gain R_f/R_1. A gain-of-10 inverting stage behaves like a non-inverting stage of gain 11 for bandwidth.

One forward pointer: at large signal amplitudes the output cannot move faster than the slew rate, so the usable full-power bandwidth is lower than f_T/A_CL even in a single-pole part. That limit, and its link to f_T, is covered in Unit 5.

Worked example

Question. A single-pole op-amp has a low-frequency open-loop gain A₀ = 200,000 and a transition frequency f_T = 2 MHz. (a) Find the dominant pole frequency f_p. (b) State the gain-bandwidth product. (c) Find the closed-loop bandwidth when the op-amp is used in a non-inverting amplifier of gain 20.

  1. For a single-pole amplifier GBW = f_T = A₀ × f_p, so f_p = f_T / A₀.
  2. f_p = 2×10⁶ Hz ÷ 2×10⁵ = 10 Hz.
  3. GBW = f_T = 2 MHz = 2×10⁶ Hz. Check: A₀ × f_p = 2×10⁵ × 10 Hz = 2×10⁶ Hz.
  4. The closed-loop gain is A_CL = 20, so BW_CL = f_T / A_CL.
  5. BW_CL = 2×10⁶ Hz ÷ 20 = 1×10⁵ Hz = 100 kHz.
  6. Check the meaning: at 100 kHz the open-loop gain is f_T/f = 2×10⁶/1×10⁵ = 20, exactly the closed-loop gain. The loop gain has fallen to 1 there, which is where the closed-loop response is 3 dB down.

Answer. f_p = 10 Hz, GBW = 2 MHz, and the closed-loop bandwidth at gain 20 is 100 kHz.

Exam traps

  • Using f_T as the closed-loop bandwidth at every gain. The bandwidth is f_T divided by the closed-loop gain: at gain 20 it is 100 kHz, not 2 MHz.
  • Mixing up f_p and f_T. f_p is the open-loop −3 dB point, where gain is 0.707 A₀; f_T is the 0 dB point, where gain is 1. They are linked by f_T = A₀ × f_p.
  • Applying gain × bandwidth = f_T when a second pole sits near the working frequency. The product then falls, and the simple formula overestimates the real bandwidth.
  • Feeding a pole in rad/s into a formula that expects hertz. If ω_p = 62.8 rad/s is given, convert first: f_p = ω_p/2π = 10 Hz.
  • For an inverting amplifier, putting the signal gain R_f/R_1 into f_T/A_CL. Use the noise gain 1 + R_f/R_1 instead.

3.4 Wideband and tuned amplifiers

Wideband amplifiers keep gain flat over a large frequency range; tuned amplifiers do the opposite, giving high gain only around a chosen centre frequency. Wideband design fights the internal capacitances that cause the Miller effect. Tuned design uses an LC tank as the load and trades bandwidth for selectivity. These circuits appear in video drivers, oscilloscope front ends, radio receivers, TV tuners and mobile-phone RF front ends. The same transistor can be made wideband or narrowband by changing the load and the stage connection.

You need: Chapter 3.2 for the Miller effect and Chapter 3.3 for the gain–bandwidth trade-off; Unit 2 for cascode and Darlington connections.

Wideband amplifiers and the cascode

A wideband amplifier has a flat gain from its lower cut-off fL to its upper cut-off fH. Its figure of merit is the gain–bandwidth product (Chapter 3.3). To widen the band, keep the midband gain but stop the internal capacitances from forming a low-frequency pole. The main enemy is the Miller effect.

C<sub>M</sub> = C<sub>µ</sub>(1 + |A<sub>v</sub>|)

CM is the Miller capacitance at the input; Cµ is the collector–base capacitance; Av is the voltage gain from base to collector. A large Av makes CM large and the input pole low.

The cascode connection uses a common-emitter (CE) transistor Q1 followed by a common-base (CB) transistor Q2. The base of Q2 is held at AC ground. Q2 has a low input resistance, about 1/gm2, so the voltage gain of Q1 is small, close to −gm1/gm2 ≈ −1. The collector voltage of Q1 therefore moves very little. Its Miller capacitance is about Cµ(1+1)=2Cµ, not Cµ(1+|Av,total|). The overall voltage gain can still be high, about −gm1RL, because Q2 passes the current to a high load resistance RL. Here gm is the transistor transconductance, and RL is the load resistance. The input pole moves up and the bandwidth increases. Cascodes appear in video drivers, oscilloscope front ends and RF low-noise amplifiers.

Other widebanding techniques

A common-collector (CC) stage followed by a CE stage is the CC-CE cascade. The CC stage is an emitter follower: its voltage gain is about 1, so its own Miller multiplication is small. It gives a high input resistance and a low output resistance, which drives the CE stage from a stiff source. The CE stage supplies the voltage gain. The CC-CE cascade is wider than a single CE stage because the input capacitance is isolated from the high-gain node. The FET version is the CD-CS cascade, with a common-drain stage driving a common-source stage.

A Darlington pair connects two transistors as CC-CC. The current gain is β1β2, and the input resistance is very high. As an emitter follower its voltage gain is slightly less than 1. It is used as a buffer before a wideband gain stage, not as the gain stage itself. A small capacitor CE across the emitter resistor RE gives emitter peaking. At low frequency CE is open and the gain is set by RE. At high frequency CE bypasses part of RE, raising the gain and compensating the high-frequency roll-off. Too much peaking causes overshoot.

Tuned amplifiers

A tuned amplifier uses an LC tank as its load. The tank stores energy at one frequency and rejects other frequencies. The centre frequency is where the reactances cancel.

f0 = 1/(2π√(LC))

f0 is the centre frequency in hertz; L is the inductance in henry; C is the capacitance in farad.

Q = (1/R)√(L/C) = ω0L/R

Q is the quality factor, dimensionless; R is the series resistance of the coil in ohm; ω0 = 2πf0.

For a coil with series resistance R, a high Q means low loss and a sharp resonance. The bandwidth is the band between the two 3 dB points:

BW = f0/Q

BW is the 3 dB bandwidth in hertz; f0 is the centre frequency in hertz; Q is the quality factor.

At resonance the tank looks like a resistance Rp = L/(C R) = Qω0L = Q²R. A CE stage with transconductance gm has voltage gain |Av| = gmRp at resonance. Away from f0 the impedance falls and the gain falls.

A single tuned amplifier has one LC tank. It is simple, but its passband is rounded and its skirts are not very steep. A double tuned amplifier couples two tanks. It gives a flatter top and steeper skirts, at the cost of more components and harder alignment. Some syllabi call the whole family an RF tuned amplifier. Tuned stages are used in radio IF amplifiers, TV tuners and as the load of class C stages (Chapter 3.7).

Worked example

Question. A tuned amplifier uses a coil of L = 100 µH with series resistance R = 10 Ω, in parallel with C = 100 pF. Find the centre frequency f0 in MHz, the quality factor Q, and the 3 dB bandwidth in kHz.

  1. Convert units: L = 100×10−6 H, C = 100×10−12 F, R = 10 Ω.
  2. Compute LC = (100×10−6)(100×10−12) = 1.0×10−14 s2.
  3. √(LC) = 1.0×10−7 s. f0 = 1/(2π×1.0×10−7) = 1.591549×106 Hz = 1.591549 MHz.
  4. L/C = (100×10−6)/(100×10−12) = 1.0×106. √(L/C)=1000 Ω. Q=(1/R)√(L/C)=1000/10=100.0.
  5. BW = f0/Q = 1.591549×106/100 = 15915.49 Hz = 15.91549 kHz.

Answer. The centre frequency is 1.591549 MHz, Q is 100.0, and the bandwidth is 15.91549 kHz.

Exam traps

  • Q is not found from Q = f0/BW when the question gives L, C and R. First Q=(1/R)√(L/C); then BW=f0/Q.
  • Cascode does not remove Cµ; it reduces the voltage across it, so the Miller multiplication falls.
  • A tuned amplifier is narrowband, not wideband. Its high gain exists only near f0.
  • In f0, L must be in henry and C in farad: 100 µH = 100×10−6 H and 100 pF = 100×10−12 F.
  • Darlington pair gives high current gain and high input resistance, but voltage gain is slightly less than 1 when used as an emitter follower.

3.5 Power amplifier classes and conversion efficiency

A power amplifier is the last stage before the load. It must deliver watts, not millivolts, so the transistor swings over a large part of its load line. What separates one class from another is the conduction angle: the part of the input cycle during which collector current flows. Class A conducts for all 360°, class B for half of it, class C for less. Conversion efficiency rises as the conduction angle falls, and distortion rises with it. Audio systems, RF transmitters and motor drivers all pick from this menu.

You need: the DC load line, quiescent point and CE amplifier from Unit 2, Transistor Biasing and Small-Signal Amplifiers, plus the gain idea from Chapter 3.1.

Conduction angle, quiescent power and efficiency

The conduction angle is the number of degrees of the 360° input cycle for which the transistor carries collector current. Class A: 360°. Class B: 180°. Class AB: between 180° and 360°. Class C: less than 180°.

The quiescent point Q = (V_CEQ, I_CQ) is the DC operating point with no signal applied. It fixes how much power the supply hands over before any signal arrives.

η = (P_out / P_DC) × 100%

P_out is the average AC power delivered to the load in W; P_DC is the average DC power drawn from the supply in W; η is the conversion efficiency in percent. Some syllabi call this the collector efficiency.

P_out = (V_p × I_p) / 2

V_p is the peak output voltage in V and I_p the peak output current in A, for a sine wave. The factor ½ comes from averaging a sine, not from the DC values.

  1. Draw the AC load line and mark Q on it.
  2. Find the largest undistorted swing: peak voltage V_p and peak current I_p, limited by cut-off at one end and saturation at the other.
  3. Compute P_out = V_p I_p / 2.
  4. Compute P_DC = V_CC × (average current drawn from the supply).
  5. Divide: η = P_out / P_DC.

Efficiency depends on drive level. At a small signal the circuit still draws nearly the same DC power but returns little AC power, so η falls far below its maximum.

Class A: series-fed and transformer-coupled

In the series-fed class A stage the load resistor R_C carries the full collector current. For the largest swing, bias at the midpoint: V_CEQ = V_CC/2 and I_CQ = V_CC/(2R_C). The collector voltage can then swing up to V_CC and down to about 0 V, so the peak AC voltage is V_CC/2, and the peak AC current is I_CQ.

P_out(max) = [(V_CC/2) × I_CQ] / 2 = V_CC I_CQ / 4

V_CC is the supply voltage in V and I_CQ the quiescent collector current in A.

The supply delivers V_CC continuously while the average current is I_CQ, so P_DC = V_CC I_CQ. Dividing gives η = 1/4, that is 25%.

In the transformer-coupled class A stage the primary has almost no DC resistance, so there is no DC drop across it and Q sits at V_CEQ ≈ V_CC. The collector voltage can now swing from 0 V to 2V_CC, giving a peak of V_CC, while the peak current is still I_CQ.

P_out(max) = (V_CC × I_CQ) / 2 , η = 50%

P_DC is again V_CC I_CQ in W, so the ratio doubles to one half.

The transformer also keeps DC out of the speaker, but it is heavy, lossy and cannot be put on a chip. Class A in any form wastes three-quarters or half of the supply power as heat, which is why it suits only low-power or high-fidelity stages.

Class B, class AB and class C

Class B conducts for 180°. Two transistors share the cycle, one for each half (the push-pull circuit is in Chapter 3.6). Each half carries a half sine, so each transistor's average current is I_p/π and the pair draws 2I_p/π.

P_out(max) = V_CC² / (2R_L) ,  P_DC = 2V_CC I_p / π ,  η = π V_p / (4 V_CC)

R_L is the load resistance in Ω, I_p = V_p/R_L the peak load current in A, and V_p the peak load voltage in V.

Put V_p = V_CC for full swing: η = π/4 = 78.5%. That is the highest efficiency any linear class can reach, because the transistor is cut off for half the cycle and burns no power then.

Class AB biases both transistors slightly on, so the conduction angle is a little over 180°, typically 200°. The small standing current removes the crossover notch. Efficiency sits between 50% and 78.5%, usually 60–70% in real audio chips.

Class C conducts for well under 180°, producing current pulses. The pulses are rich in harmonics. A tuned tank at the load filters those harmonics, so the output is nearly a sine. But class C cannot follow a changing envelope, so it is used only for constant-envelope RF signals. Efficiency can approach 85–90%, and distortion is high. RF transmitters accept that trade; audio does not. Class D switches instead of amplifying and is covered in Chapter 3.7.

Worked example

Question. A series-fed class A amplifier has V_CC = 12 V, R_C = 1 kΩ, and is biased exactly at midpoint so that V_CEQ = 6 V and I_CQ = 6 mA. Find the DC input power, the maximum AC output power and the conversion efficiency.

  1. Quiescent values: V_CEQ = 6 V, I_CQ = 6 mA. Check: I_CQ = V_CC/(2R_C) = 12/(2 × 1 kΩ) = 6 mA, so the bias is indeed at midpoint.
  2. DC power: P_DC = V_CC × I_CQ = 12 V × 6 mA = 72 mW.
  3. Largest swing: the collector voltage can rise to 12 V and fall to 0 V, so the peak AC voltage is V_p = 6 V. Cut-off limits the peak current to I_p = 6 mA.
  4. AC output: P_out(max) = (V_p × I_p)/2 = (6 V × 6 mA)/2 = 18 mW.
  5. Efficiency: η = 18 mW / 72 mW = 0.25 = 25%.

Answer. P_DC = 72 mW, P_out(max) = 18 mW, and η = 25%, which is exactly the series-fed class A limit.

Worked example 2

Question. A push-pull class B stage runs from V_CC = 12 V into an 8 Ω loudspeaker and is driven to full swing. Find the peak load current, the maximum output power, the DC power drawn and the efficiency.

  1. Peak load current: I_p = V_CC / R_L = 12 V / 8 Ω = 1.5 A.
  2. Maximum output power: P_out = V_CC × I_p / 2 = 12 V × 1.5 A / 2 = 9 W.
  3. DC power: P_DC = 2 V_CC I_p / π = (2 × 12 V × 1.5 A) / π = 11.46 W.

Exam traps

  • Dropping the factor ½ and writing P_out = V_p × I_p. That doubles the answer and can push efficiency past 100%.
  • Treating V_CC × I_CQ as AC power. It is the DC input power; the AC output is always smaller.
  • Mixing the two class A limits: series-fed gives 25%, transformer-coupled gives 50%. Write which circuit you mean before quoting a number.
  • Quoting 78.5% for class B at any drive. The formula η = πV_p/(4V_CC) falls with swing; 78.5% holds only at V_p = V_CC.
  • Saying class AB removes distortion. It removes the crossover notch only; clipping and harmonic distortion remain (see Chapter 3.8).

3.6 Push-pull class B and AB amplifiers; crossover distortion

A single transistor biased in class A burns current even in silence. A push-pull stage splits the work: one transistor carries the positive half-cycle, a second carries the negative half. Each rests for half the cycle, so the stage draws almost no supply current at idle and reaches a theoretical efficiency of 78.5 %. Class B does this but leaves a flat spot near zero volts, called crossover distortion. Class AB biases both transistors slightly on, spending a little idle current to remove that flat spot. Radio, TV and phone audio power stages use this idea.

You need: class A and class B conduction angles and efficiency from Chapter 3.5, and the complementary emitter follower from Unit 2, Transistor Biasing and Small-Signal Amplifiers.

Two transistors share one cycle

Class B means a device conducts for 180°, exactly half a cycle. A push-pull stage gives each half to its own transistor: one for the positive swing, one for the negative. With zero input both are off, so the stage draws no supply current. A class A stage, by contrast, burns current even in silence.

In the transformer-coupled form, an input transformer with a centre-tapped secondary feeds antiphase base drives to two NPN transistors. Their emitters are grounded and their collectors drive the two halves of an output transformer primary. The output transformer adds the half-sine waves back into a full sine. The load gets no DC, but transformers are heavy and block low frequencies.

The transformerless form is what real equipment uses, called OTL (output transformer-less). An NPN and a PNP sit in complementary symmetry as emitter followers, emitters joined at the output node, fed from +Vcc and −Vcc. The NPN sources current into RL on the positive half; the PNP sinks current on the negative half. A single supply with a large output coupling capacitor also works, and a bridge (BTL) connection doubles the swing.

When good power PNPs were hard to make, designers used quasi-complementary output: two NPN power devices, the lower one in Darlington with a PNP driver, so it behaves like a PNP. Modern devices make true complementary pairs normal.

Class A push-pull is the alternative: both transistors conduct all the time, there is no dead zone, but the best efficiency is 50 % against 78.5 % for class B.

Power and the 78.5 % ceiling

Assume the output swings to the supply rail, ignoring V_CE(sat). Then the peak output voltage is V_peak = Vcc and the peak output current is I_peak = Vcc/RL.

P_out(max) = V_peak × I_peak / 2 = Vcc² / (2RL)

Vcc is the magnitude of each supply in volts, RL is the load resistance in ohms, and P_out(max) is the largest sine-wave power delivered to the load in watts.

I_dc = I_peak / π

I_dc is the average current one supply delivers over a full cycle in amperes; each supply passes a half-sine once per cycle, whose average over the full period is I_peak/π.

P_DC = 2 × Vcc × I_dc = 2Vcc² / (πRL)

P_DC is the total DC power taken from both supplies in watts. The factor 2 appears because the positive half comes from the +Vcc rail and the negative half from the −Vcc rail, and both carry current.

η = P_out / P_DC = π/4 = 0.785 = 78.5 %

η is the conversion efficiency, the fraction of DC power turned into signal power.

78.5 % is a ceiling, reached only at full drive. At a smaller swing V_peak the output power falls as V_peak² but the DC power falls only as V_peak, so η = (π/4)(V_peak/Vcc) drops quickly. Quiet passages of music run at low efficiency even in a class B stage.

Crossover distortion, and how class AB removes it

A silicon BJT stays off until its base-emitter voltage reaches about 0.7 V. At the zero crossing the input must rise 0.7 V before the NPN turns on, and fall 0.7 V before the PNP turns on. The output therefore has a flat step of about ±0.7 V around zero. That flat spot is crossover distortion. It is worst for quiet signals, looks like a kink in the sine wave, and adds odd harmonics (see Chapter 3.8).

Class AB fixes it by biasing the two bases apart by roughly 2V_BE, about 1.4 V, so both devices idle at a small quiescent current I_Q of a few mA to tens of mA. There are two bias circuits. Two forward-biased diodes give about 1.4 V. A V_BE multiplier uses one transistor with a base divider R1, R2 to give V_BB = V_BE(1 + R1/R2); it is adjustable and can be mounted on the heat sink so the bias falls as the output devices heat up.

The cost is idle dissipation, roughly P_Q = 2Vcc I_Q on a split supply. Small emitter resistors of 0.1 Ω to 0.47 Ω in series with each emitter steady the quiescent current against device mismatch and thermal runaway.

Each device now conducts for more than 180° but less than 360°, so efficiency lies between 50 % and 78.5 %, typically 60 % to 70 % in a well-set-up audio stage.

Question. A complementary (OTL) class B push-pull amplifier runs from ±12 V supplies into an 8 Ω loudspeaker. 3.7 Class C and class D amplifiers

Class C conducts for less than half a cycle, and class D conducts either fully on or fully off. Both keep the transistor out of the region where it carries high current and high voltage at the same time, so efficiency rises. Class C uses a tuned LC tank to rebuild the sine wave and drives radio transmitters. Class D switches at a high frequency, carries the signal as pulse width, and filters the result; it drives audio amplifiers, phone speakers and motor drives.

You need: the push-pull idea of two devices driving one load (Chapter 3.6), the conduction-angle and efficiency comparison of classes A, B and C (Chapter 3.5), and the diode and switch basics from Unit 1.

Class C: less than half a cycle, and a tuned load

A class C stage is biased far below cut-off. In an npn transistor the base is held below the emitter, so the device stays off until the input peak swings it on. Current then flows in a short pulse once per cycle, for less than half the input cycle. The conduction angle is the part of the 360° input cycle during which the device conducts. For class C it is less than 180°, and RF designs commonly use 90° to 150°.

Efficiency rises because a transistor dissipates power only when voltage across it and current through it are both present. In class C the device carries zero current for most of the cycle, so the average dissipation is small. Practical class C stages reach 60 % to 80 %. For comparison, a class B stage peaks near 78.5 % at full drive and a class A stage peaks at about 25 % with a resistive load (up to 50 % with a transformer-coupled load).

The transistor current is a narrow pulse train, not a sine. The load is a parallel LC tank resonant at the carrier frequency f₀. The tank has high impedance at f₀ and low impedance elsewhere, so the voltage across it is almost a pure sine at f₀. The tank does the wave shaping; the transistor only supplies pulses of energy.

f₀ = 1 / (2π√(LC))

f₀ is the resonant frequency in hertz, L is the tank inductance in henries, C is the tank capacitance in farads. At f₀ the tank impedance is maximum and the output is a sine.

Class C works only over a narrow band around f₀. The transistor current is a narrow pulse train rich in harmonics, and only the f₀ component appears as a large sine at the tank; the harmonics are mostly suppressed. Any signal whose energy moves outside that narrow band appears as severe distortion. FM and CW transmitters keep a constant envelope and a fixed carrier, so class C is often their final stage. Audio cannot use it: speech and music spread over 20 Hz to 20 kHz and change amplitude constantly, so a tank tuned to one frequency would distort them.

Class D: switch, do not amplify

In class D the transistor never sits between on and off. It is either fully on, with the voltage across it near zero and current set by the load, or fully off, with current zero and full voltage across it. Instantaneous dissipation p = v × i is small in both states, so almost no heat is made and almost all the DC input reaches the load.

The signal is carried as pulse width modulation (PWM). A comparator compares the audio input with a triangle carrier at the switching frequency, usually a few hundred kilohertz. A large audio sample gives a wide pulse, a small sample a narrow pulse, so the duty cycle D follows the audio.

v_avg = D × V_DD

v_avg is the average of the switched waveform over one switching period in volts, D is the duty cycle (0 to 1, no unit), V_DD is the supply voltage in volts. The LC filter produces this average at the load.

The PWM drives two MOSFETs in a half-bridge, one pulling the output to V_DD and the other to ground — the push-pull action of Chapter 3.6. Both must never conduct together, so a small dead time separates them. The square wave then enters an LC low-pass filter whose cut-off lies between the audio band (20 kHz) and the switching frequency. The filter averages the pulses, the carrier is removed, and only audio reaches the speaker.

Ideal efficiency is 100 %, because ideal switches dissipate nothing. Real losses are switching loss during each transition, conduction loss in the on-resistance R_ds(on), gate-drive power, inductor resistance and the dead time. Practical class D amplifiers reach 85 % to 95 %, against 50 % to 70 % for a class AB amplifier of the same output power.

Two more switching classes exist. Class E shapes the voltage across the switch with a resonant load so that it falls to zero before the device turns on, called zero-voltage switching. Class F tunes the harmonics of its output network to build a flatter voltage waveform. Both are RF techniques; the names are enough here.

  • Battery life: a 90 % efficient amplifier turns one-tenth of the DC power into heat, so a phone speaker plays longer and stays cool.
  • Motor drives: the same comparator and bridge form an inverter, and the PWM averages into a sine current in the motor winding.
  • Bandwidth: the LC low-pass passes the whole audio band, while a class C tank passes only a narrow slice around f₀.

Worked example

Question. A class D audio amplifier delivers 20 W of audio to a load. Its switching MOSFETs dissipate 1 W and its LC filter dissipates 0.5 W. Find the total loss, the DC input power and the conversion efficiency.

  1. Losses add up: P_loss = P_MOS + P_filter = 1 W + 0.5 W = 1.5 W.
  2. Energy conservation: P_DC = P_load + P_loss = 20 W + 1.5 W = 21.5 W.
  3. η = (P_load / P_DC) × 100 = (20 / 21.5) × 100 = 93.0 %.
  4. Check: 1.5 W lost is 6.98 % of the input, a realistic class D figure and far better than the 50 % to 70 % of class AB.

Answer. The loss is 1.5 W, the DC input power is 21.5 W, and the conversion efficiency is 93.0 %.

Exam traps

  • Quoting class D as 100 % efficient. That is the ideal-switch figure; switching, conduction, gate-drive and filter losses bring real circuits to 85 % to 95 %.
  • Swapping the two load networks. A class C tank is resonant at the carrier and rebuilds the sine; a class D LC section is a low-pass filter that removes the carrier and passes the audio.
  • Forgetting that P_DC = P_load + P_loss. Putting P_DC = P_load gives 100 %, and inverting the ratio (P_DC / P_load) gives a number above 1.
  • Claiming class C suits audio because its efficiency is high. Its output is a pulse train restored by a narrowband tank, so amplitude and frequency changes are destroyed.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Gallium nitride (GaN) MOSFETs switch faster with less charge, pushing switching frequencies into the megahertz range and shrinking the filter. 3.8 Harmonic distortion, THD and heat sinks

A pure sine wave has one frequency. A real amplifier adds small copies at 2f, 3f and so on; these are harmonics, and their relative size is harmonic distortion. Heat is the second cost of power: the transistor chip must stay below its maximum junction temperature. A heat sink does this by giving heat a low-resistance path to air. The ideas matter in audio amplifiers, inverters, and any power stage in a sealed box on a Chennai summer day.

You need: power amplifier classes, class B and AB stages, and the power limits of a transistor from Unit 2.

Harmonics: unwanted multiples of the signal

Any repeating waveform can be split into a sum of sine waves at f, 2f, 3f, ... This is the Fourier idea. f is the fundamental; 2f is the second harmonic; nf is the nth harmonic. A perfectly linear amplifier changes only the size of the input sine, so its output has only f. A nonlinear transfer curve bends the sine. The bend adds new frequencies at multiples of f. Even harmonics (2f, 4f) come from asymmetry; odd harmonics (3f, 5f) come from symmetric compression. In a Hi-Fi amplifier, harmonics above the audio band are less audible, but they still show in a measurement. A spectrum analyser or FFT plots each harmonic as a spike. The height of each spike is an rms voltage. Use V1 for fundamental rms, V2 for second-harmonic rms, and so on. Harmonic distortion is not the same as clipping: clipping is severe distortion with many harmonics. A small bend gives small THD. In radio, a tuned load filters harmonics; in audio, the load is wideband, so they reach the speaker.

Total harmonic distortion

THD stands for total harmonic distortion. Some syllabi call it total harmonic distortion; the words are the same. For voltages, THD = √(V2² + V3² + ... + Vn²) / V1. V1 is rms fundamental; V2, V3 are rms harmonics; n is the highest harmonic you include. The result is a ratio; multiply by 100 to get percent. The same formula holds for currents. To express in decibels, use THD(dB) = 20 log10(THD ratio). A THD of 0.01 (1 %) is −40 dB. Do not add V2 and V3 as plain numbers. They are sine waves at different frequencies, so their powers add: V2² + V3². If you add voltages, you overestimate distortion. Useful THD often quotes a bandwidth, for example 20 Hz to 20 kHz, because a wider measurement catches more harmonics. THD alone does not include noise; THD+N adds noise. For a quick exam answer, state the formula with square root of squares. For a quick check, 1 % THD is a ratio of 0.01.

Heat sinks and thermal resistance

Power lost in a transistor becomes heat at the junction, the tiny chip inside the package. Heat flows junction → case → heat sink → ambient. Each step has thermal resistance Rθ, in °C/W. Rθ is the temperature rise per watt: 2 °C/W means 2 °C hotter for every watt. The steady-state junction temperature is Tj = Ta + PD(Rθjc + Rθcs + Rθsa). Tj is junction temperature in °C; Ta is ambient in °C; PD is dissipation in W; Rθjc is junction-to-case; Rθcs is case-to-sink (thermal pad or paste); Rθsa is sink-to-ambient. To choose a sink, rearrange: Rθsa ≤ (Tjmax − Ta)/PD − (Rθjc + Rθcs). Tjmax is the maximum junction temperature, often 150 °C. Use the worst ambient, not room temperature. A larger sink has lower Rθsa. Derating means the allowed PD falls as Ta rises. Thermal runaway is a loop: higher Tj raises collector current, which raises PD and Tj again. Stable bias and emitter resistors stop it. This chapter only selects the sink; feedback to cut distortion is in Unit 4.

Worked example

Question. A 1 kHz amplifier output has fundamental 10 V rms, second harmonic 0.5 V rms and third harmonic 0.3 V rms. The transistor dissipates 8 W. Rθjc = 2 °C/W, Rθcs = 1 °C/W, ambient 40 °C, maximum junction 150 °C. Find THD and the maximum allowed heat-sink thermal resistance.

  1. Given: V1 = 10 V rms, V2 = 0.5 V rms, V3 = 0.3 V rms, PD = 8 W, Rθjc = 2 °C/W, Rθcs = 1 °C/W, Ta = 40 °C, Tjmax = 150 °C.
  2. Harmonic numerator: √(V2² + V3²) = √(0.5² + 0.3²) = √(0.25 + 0.09) = √0.34 = 0.5831 V rms.
  3. THD = 0.5831 / 10 = 0.05831 = 5.83 %.
  4. Allowed total thermal resistance: (Tjmax − Ta) / PD = (150 − 40) / 8 = 110 / 8 = 13.75 °C/W.
  5. Fixed path resistance: Rθjc + Rθcs = 2 + 1 = 3 °C/W.
  6. Maximum sink resistance: Rθsa ≤ 13.75 − 3 = 10.75 °C/W.

Answer. THD is 5.83 % and the maximum allowed heat-sink thermal resistance is 10.75 °C/W.

Exam traps

  • Adding harmonic rms voltages directly; add their squares and take the square root.
  • Putting V1 into the THD numerator; the denominator is only the fundamental.
  • Forgetting Rθcs; thermal paste or an insulating pad adds to the path.
  • Using 25 °C room ambient for a box that runs at 40 °C or more.
  • Mixing THD percent and THD in dB; 5.83 % is a ratio of 0.0583, about −24.7 dB.

Takeaways

  1. Coupling and bypass capacitors set the lower cut-off frequency; above fL they act nearly as short circuits.
  2. Internal Cπ and Cμ set the upper cut-off frequency; the Miller effect multiplies Cμ at the input.
  3. A dominant-pole amplifier has a constant gain-bandwidth product, and fT is its unity-gain frequency.
  4. Wideband design trades gain for bandwidth using cascode or CC-CE stages, while tuned design trades bandwidth for gain at f0.
  5. Power amplifier classes are distinguished by conduction angle, and efficiency rises as conduction angle falls.
  6. A push-pull class B stage uses two devices for opposite half-cycles, and class AB bias removes crossover distortion.
  7. Class C uses short conduction and a tuned load for RF, while class D switches with PWM for high efficiency.
  8. THD measures unwanted harmonics, and a heat sink controls junction temperature and device reliability.
