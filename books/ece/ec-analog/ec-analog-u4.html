4.1 Negative feedback: effect on gain, bandwidth and impedance

Negative feedback returns a fraction β of the output to the input so that it subtracts from the source. The amplifier gain A falls, but the fall buys a wider bandwidth, steadier gain and less distortion. This is the everyday reason an op-amp circuit with two resistors gives a gain you can trust, even when the transistor inside has a gain spread of 2:1. In a phone charger, an audio preamp or a sensor front end, the same idea sets the gain by resistor ratio β rather than by the active device.

You need: Unit 2 (common-emitter gain and loading) and Unit 3 (gain-bandwidth product and cut-off frequency). Impedance effects are in 4.2; stability is in 4.3.

The loop that subtracts

The amplifier is a block with open-loop gain A (V/V). The feedback network returns v_f = β v_o to a summing node; β is the feedback factor (V/V). The node subtracts: v_i = v_s − v_f, where v_s is the source and v_i is the amplifier input. The minus sign makes the feedback negative.

A_f = v_o / v_s = A / (1 + Aβ)

A_f is closed-loop gain (V/V); A is open-loop gain (V/V); β is feedback factor (V/V); Aβ is loop gain (dimensionless).

The loop gain is Aβ. The denominator D = 1 + Aβ is the desensitivity. With A = 1000 V/V and β = 0.01 V/V, Aβ = 10, D = 11, and A_f = 1000/11 = 90.9 V/V. The gain has fallen by 11 times.

When Aβ is much greater than 1, A_f ≈ 1/β, so the gain is set by the feedback network. With β = 0.01 V/V, 1/β = 100 V/V. The exact 90.9 V/V is close because Aβ = 10. At Aβ = 3 or 4 the approximation is poor.

Steady gain, less distortion

D spends the lost gain on stability. A small open-loop change ΔA gives a closed-loop change ΔA_f/A_f = (ΔA/A)/D. The same D reduces the feedback-corrected part of harmonic distortion and noise. Noise already at the input is not reduced.

ΔA_f / A_f = (ΔA / A) / (1 + Aβ)

ΔA/A is the fractional open-loop change; ΔA_f/A_f is the fractional closed-loop change; D = 1 + Aβ.

In the worked example A rises by 10 %. D = 11, so A_f rises by 10 %/11 = 0.91 %. A transistor gain that varies by ±20 % becomes a closed-loop gain that varies by about ±1.8 %. This is why resistor ratios, not transistor spreads, set the gain of an audio preamp.

Distortion works the same way. If the open-loop amplifier adds 8 % THD, the feedback-corrected output distortion is about 8 %/11 = 0.73 %. This applies to distortion generated inside the loop.

Bandwidth and the gain-bandwidth product

Negative feedback raises the upper cut-off and lowers the lower cut-off. The closed-loop bandwidth is BW_f ≈ BW × (1 + Aβ) = BW × D. The gain falls by D, so A_f × BW_f ≈ A × BW.

BW_f = BW (1 + Aβ), A_f × BW_f ≈ A × BW

BW is open-loop bandwidth (Hz); BW_f is closed-loop bandwidth (Hz).

From the example: BW = 10 kHz, D = 11, so BW_f = 10 kHz × 11 = 110 kHz. The open-loop product is 1000 × 10 kHz = 10⁷ Hz. The closed-loop product is 90.9 × 110 kHz ≈ 10⁷ Hz. The gain falls by 20 log₁₀(11) ≈ 20.8 dB while bandwidth rises by 11.

This is a first-order picture with one dominant pole, as in Unit 3. When β changes the poles move; stability is 4.3. Which impedance rises or falls depends on the feedback topology, not on β alone; see 4.2.

Worked example

Question. An amplifier has open-loop gain A = 1000 V/V and open-loop bandwidth BW = 10 kHz. Negative feedback with β = 0.01 V/V is applied. Find the loop gain and desensitivity. Find the closed-loop gain. Find the closed-loop bandwidth. If A rises by 10 %, find the percentage change in closed-loop gain.

  1. Loop gain: Aβ = 1000 V/V × 0.01 V/V = 10 (dimensionless).
  2. Desensitivity: D = 1 + Aβ = 1 + 10 = 11.
  3. Closed-loop gain: A_f = A/D = 1000/11 = 90.909... V/V, rounded to 90.9 V/V.
  4. Closed-loop bandwidth: BW_f = BW × D = 10 kHz × 11 = 110 kHz.
  5. Gain change: ΔA_f/A_f = (ΔA/A)/D = 10 %/11 = 0.909...%, rounded to 0.91 %.

Answer. Aβ = 10, D = 11, A_f = 90.9 V/V, BW_f = 110 kHz, and the closed-loop gain changes by about 0.91 % when A changes by 10 %.

Exam traps

  • Using A_f = A/(1 − Aβ): negative feedback subtracts, so the denominator is 1 + Aβ. The minus sign belongs to positive feedback (4.4).
  • Using A_f ≈ 1/β too early: check Aβ. At Aβ = 3 the error is 33 %, at Aβ = 4 it is 25 %.
  • Writing β = 1 for 1 %: β is a voltage ratio, so 1 % = 0.01 V/V.
  • Saying feedback raises the gain-bandwidth product: it keeps A_f × BW_f roughly equal to A × BW.
  • Assuming all impedances fall: the direction depends on the feedback topology (4.2).

4.2 Feedback topologies: voltage and current, series and shunt

An amplifier output has a voltage and a current; a feedback network can return a sample of either. That output choice is sampling. At the input the returned signal can subtract as a voltage in the input loop or as a current at the input node; that input choice is mixing. Two independent choices give four topologies. Mixing sets the direction of the input-resistance change; sampling sets the direction of the output-resistance change. Every topology moves both resistances, each by D. Ideal closed-loop gain is 1/β with its own unit.

You need: 4.1 for A, β and D, and Unit 2 for amplifier input and output resistance.

Sampling and mixing: two independent choices

The output carries voltage and current at once. A feedback network can return a sample of either. That is sampling.

  • Voltage sampling: the network connects across the output terminals, parallel to the load. The returned signal is proportional to vo, the output voltage. The amplifier behaves like a voltage source.
  • Current sampling: the network connects in series with the output loop, so load current io flows through it. The returned signal is proportional to io. The amplifier behaves like a current source.

At the input the returned signal must oppose the source. That is mixing.

  • Series mixing: the network sits in series with the input loop. The feedback voltage vf = βvo (or βio) subtracts from source voltage vs.
  • Shunt mixing: the network sits across the input node, parallel to the source. The feedback current if = βvo (or βio) subtracts from source current is, so less current enters the amplifier.

The choices are independent. Two output answers and two input answers give four topologies. Mixing sets the direction of the input-resistance change; sampling sets the direction of the output-resistance change. Every topology moves both resistances, each by D. The gain formula has the same shape, but A and β carry different units.

A_f = A / (1 + Aβ) = A / D

A is the open-loop gain, β the feedback factor, Aβ the loop gain (dimensionless) and D = 1 + Aβ the desensitivity.

The four topologies and both naming styles

Two naming styles are in use. The sampling–mixing name states output quantity first and input connection second: voltage-series, voltage-shunt, current-series, current-shunt. The connection name states input connection first and output second: series-shunt, shunt-shunt, series-series, shunt-series. Voltage-series and series-shunt are the same circuit.

The ideal gain comes from A → ∞: A_f → 1/β. Its unit is the topology's unit — V/V, V/A, A/V, A/A. A current-series stage gives output current for input voltage, so a transconductance amplifier uses it.

Recognisable circuits: two CE stages with a resistor from the second collector to the first emitter give voltage-series. One CE stage with a resistor from collector to base gives voltage-shunt. A CE stage with an unbypassed emitter resistor R_E gives current-series, because emitter current develops the feedback voltage across R_E.

Which resistance moves, and why

Mixing moves R_in by D in the direction it sets; sampling moves R_out by D in the direction it sets.

R_if = R_i (1 + Aβ) for series mixing;  R_if = R_i / (1 + Aβ) for shunt mixing

R_i is input resistance without feedback and R_if with it. D = 1 + Aβ is the same number as in the gain formula.

R_of = R_o / (1 + Aβ) for voltage sampling;  R_of = R_o (1 + Aβ) for current sampling

R_o is output resistance without feedback and R_of with it.

Series mixing puts feedback voltage inside the input loop, so less input current flows for the same source voltage: input resistance looks larger. Shunt mixing pulls extra current at the input node, so input resistance looks smaller. Voltage sampling holds output voltage nearly constant against load changes: low R_out. Current sampling holds output current nearly constant: high R_out.

Reading a circuit in two looks

Identification is practical. Take two looks.

  1. Look at the output. Does the feedback network connect across the output terminals (voltage sampling) or in series with the load current path (current sampling)? A resistor carrying load current samples current, even though a voltage appears across it.
  2. Look at the input. Does feedback return in series with the source loop (series mixing) or into the input node alongside the source (shunt mixing)?
  3. Name the topology in both styles, and write ideal gain 1/β with its unit.
  4. Compute D = 1 + Aβ. Move both resistances, each by D, in the directions set by mixing and sampling.

The feedback network is not a free observer. It draws current and drops voltage, so use loaded values of A, R_i and R_o, not the bare amplifier. Rigorous analysis absorbs the network into the amplifier with two-port parameters, then applies the same formulas.

Worked example

Question. A voltage-series feedback amplifier has open-loop gain A = 10⁴ V/V, feedback factor β = 0.01 V/V, input resistance R_i = 10 kΩ and output resistance R_o = 1 kΩ. Find D, A_f, R_if and R_of.

  1. Voltage sampling and series mixing: R_if rises by D and R_of falls by D.
  2. Aβ = 10⁴ × 0.01 = 100. D = 1 + 100 = 101.
  3. A_f = A/D = 10⁴ / 101 = 99.01 V/V, close to ideal 1/β = 100 V/V.
  4. R_if = R_i × D = 10 kΩ × 101 = 1.01 MΩ.
  5. R_of = R_o / D = 1 kΩ / 101 = 9.90 Ω.

Answer. D = 101, A_f = 99.01 V/V, R_if = 1.01 MΩ and R_of = 9.90 Ω.

  • 'Negative feedback raises input resistance' holds only for series mixing; shunt mixing lowers it. State the mixing type first.
  • Sampling sets R_out and mixing sets R_in. Swapping these is the most common lost mark.
  • Voltage-series is series-shunt. The names list sampling and mixing in opposite order, so they are one topology.
  • The ideal gain is 1/β but not always V/V: voltage-shunt gives V/A, current-series A/V, current-shunt A/A. A unit slip makes the answer wrong.
  • A resistor carrying output current samples current even though a voltage appears across it. Look at series or parallel connection, not what you measure.

Analyze A student writes: 'Negative feedback always raises input impedance and lowers output impedance.' Correct the statement topology by topology. 5 marks

4.3 Loop gain, stability, gain and phase margins

An amplifier with negative feedback is stable only if its loop gain Aβ falls below 1 before the total loop phase reaches −180°. The loop gain is a complex number at every frequency, so stability is a question about a Bode plot, not a single number. This chapter reads the magnitude and phase of Aβ(jω), defines the two crossover frequencies, and turns them into gain margin and phase margin. These margins are checked in every op-amp circuit, audio power amplifier and control loop that must not self-oscillate.

You need: 4.1 and 4.2 for β and loop gain; Unit 3 for Bode plots, poles and cut-off frequencies.

Loop gain and the characteristic equation

Write the forward gain as A(s) and the feedback fraction as β(s). β is a pure number or a network ratio; it has no units. The loop gain is the gain around the complete loop, forward path times feedback path.

T(s) = A(s) β(s)

T(s) is the loop gain; A(s) is the forward gain; β(s) is the feedback fraction; s = jω = j2πf.

With negative feedback the closed-loop gain is A/(1 + T). The closed-loop poles are the roots of the characteristic equation.

1 + T(s) = 0

T(s) is the loop gain; the roots of this equation are the closed-loop poles.

At β = 0 the closed-loop poles are the open-loop poles. Increasing β moves them. In a two-pole amplifier the closed-loop poles become complex and ring more as β rises, but they never cross the imaginary axis. With three or more poles they can cross into the right half plane when β is too large. That movement is the stability question. The open-loop Bode plot of T(jω) answers it because T is measured with the loop broken but correctly loaded. Deliberate positive feedback with |T| = 1 and ∠T = 0° is the oscillator case in 4.4.

Bode plot and the two crossings

A Bode magnitude plot draws 20 log₁₀|T| in dB against log f. Each pole adds a fall of 20 dB/decade above its cut-off frequency. Each zero adds a rise of 20 dB/decade. At a pole frequency the phase is −45°. Well above a pole the phase approaches −90°.

Two frequencies matter. The gain crossover frequency f_c is where |T| = 1, that is 0 dB. The phase crossover frequency f_180 is where ∠T = −180°. The margins are read at the opposite crossing: phase margin at f_c, gain margin at f_180.

PM = 180° + ∠T(jω_c)

PM is the phase margin in degrees; ∠T(jω_c) is the loop-gain phase at the gain crossover frequency where |T| = 1.

GM = −20 log₁₀|T(jω_180)| dB

GM is the gain margin in dB; |T(jω_180)| is the loop-gain magnitude at the phase crossover frequency where ∠T = −180°.

A positive phase margin means the phase at f_c is above −180°. A positive gain margin means the magnitude at f_180 is below 1, that is below 0 dB. Negative margins mean the loop is unstable.

Reading margins and pole count

Phase margin measures how much extra phase lag the loop can take before the phase reaches −180° while |T| is still 1. A design target is 45° to 60°. Below about 30° the closed-loop response shows overshoot and ringing. A negative phase margin means oscillation.

Gain margin measures how much the loop gain can rise before the phase-crossover magnitude reaches 0 dB. One pole gives at most −90° of phase, so a one-pole loop is always stable and its gain margin is infinite on paper. Two poles can be made safe with 45° to 60° of phase margin. Three or more poles bring the phase near −180° or beyond, so such loops ring easily and need careful design.

Adding feedback moves the amplifier poles. That is why a Bode plot of the open-loop loop gain T answers a closed-loop stability question. In an op-amp datasheet the open-loop gain curve becomes T once you choose β with external resistors. Compensation networks that reshape this curve are covered in Unit 5.

Worked example

Question. An amplifier has open-loop gain A₀ = 10⁵, poles at 10 Hz and 100 kHz, and β = 0.01. Find the low-frequency loop gain in linear and dB form, the gain crossover frequency, the phase margin and the gain margin.

  1. Low-frequency loop gain: A₀β = 10⁵ × 0.01 = 1000. In dB: 20 log₁₀(1000) = 60 dB.
  2. Above 10 Hz the first pole makes |A| ≈ 10⁵/(f/10) = 10⁶/f. Set |Aβ| = 1, so |A| = 1/β = 100. Then 10⁶/f = 100, giving f = 10⁴ Hz = 10 kHz. This is below 100 kHz, so the second pole is small at the crossing.
  3. Phase at 10 kHz: first pole gives about −90°. Second pole gives −arctan(10⁴/10⁵) = −5.7°. Total phase = −95.7°.
  4. Phase margin = 180° + (−95.7°) = 84.3°.
  5. The phase approaches −180° only at infinite frequency, where |T| → 0. So the gain margin is infinite on paper.

Answer. Aβ at low frequency = 1000 (60 dB), gain crossover = 10 kHz, phase at crossover = −95.7°, phase margin = 84.3°, gain margin is infinite on paper.

Exam traps

  • Measuring gain margin at the gain crossover and phase margin at the phase crossover. Each margin belongs to the other crossing: PM at |T| = 1, GM at ∠T = −180°.
  • Forgetting the sign convention. A stable amplifier has positive gain margin because GM = −20 log|T| at the phase crossover. If |T| > 1 there, GM is negative.
  • Using the open-loop gain A₀ instead of the loop gain Aβ. β changes the gain crossover and therefore changes both margins.
  • Treating every pole as exactly −90° of phase at the crossover. A pole is −45° at its cut-off and approaches −90° only well above it.
  • Assuming infinite gain margin means no ringing. A two-pole loop can have infinite GM on paper and still ring badly if its phase margin is small.

4.4 Barkhausen criterion and condition for oscillation

An oscillator makes a sine wave from a DC supply alone. It takes a feedback amplifier, returns a fraction β of the output to the input, and arranges that the returned signal arrives exactly in phase and exactly as large as needed. The circuit then feeds itself forever at one frequency f₀. This is positive feedback; the rule fixing f₀ and the minimum gain is the Barkhausen criterion. Transmitters, clocks, function generators and the local oscillator in every radio receiver depend on it.

You need: 4.1 for β and loop gain, and 4.3 for loop phase.

Positive feedback makes an amplifier feed itself

Negative feedback returns a fraction of the output so that it opposes the input, dropping gain to A/(1 + Aβ). Positive feedback returns it so that it adds. Add enough and the amplifier supplies its own input: the circuit oscillates.

Draw the loop as a summing node, an amplifier of gain A, and a network β that samples the output and returns part of it.

v_o / v_s = A / (1 − Aβ)

A is the open-loop voltage gain; β is the feedback fraction, the part of v_o returned; Aβ is the loop gain, dimensionless; v_o is the output voltage and v_s the external input, both in volts.

Set v_s to zero and allow v_o to be finite. That needs 1 − Aβ = 0, that is Aβ = 1. The closed-loop gain is then infinite: the smallest disturbance grows into a full-size sine.

The Barkhausen criterion: magnitude one, phase zero

Aβ is a complex number with a magnitude and a phase angle. The signal travelling once around the loop must come back with the same size and the same phase it started with, or each trip changes it.

|A(jω₀)| · |β(jω₀)| = 1    and    ∠A(jω₀) + ∠β(jω₀) = n × 360°, n = 0, 1, 2, …

ω₀ = 2πf₀ is the oscillation angular frequency in rad/s; f₀ is the oscillation frequency in Hz; ∠ means phase shift in degrees; n counts whole turns around the phase circle. Many books write the phase condition as 0°, which means the same thing.

The phase condition chooses f₀: only at one frequency does the total loop phase reach a whole multiple of 360°, so only there does the signal reinforce itself. The magnitude condition then sets the minimum gain, A ≥ 1/β.

Watch the sign bookkeeping. A common-emitter stage already inverts, giving 180°; its β network must supply the other 180° at f₀. A non-inverting stage starts at 0°, so its network supplies 0° or 360°.

Start-up, amplitude limiting, and what Barkhausen does not promise

A circuit with |Aβ| exactly 1 has no reason to start: zero output also satisfies the condition. Real circuits use |Aβ| slightly above 1 at f₀. Thermal noise and the switch-on transient contain every frequency; the f₀ component grows on each trip around the loop, the others do not.

Growth cannot continue forever. Something must pull the loop gain back to exactly 1 at the final amplitude:

  • The transistor cuts off on peaks and saturates on troughs, so the waveform clips and average gain falls.
  • Rectified base current charges the coupling capacitor and shifts the bias point, lowering gain as amplitude rises.
  • Diode limiters across the tank or the feedback path clip the return signal.
  • A thermistor or a small lamp in the β network changes β with amplitude, as in the Wien bridge.

Barkhausen is necessary but not sufficient for a clean sine. It says where and whether the circuit can oscillate; purity depends on how gently the limiting acts. Hard clipping gives a large third harmonic, a slow amplitude-dependent β a few percent distortion.

Which oscillator for which job

Worked example

Question. A feedback amplifier has an inverting gain A = −50. Its feedback network has β = 0.02 and adds a further 180° of phase shift at 5 kHz. (a) Test the Barkhausen criterion and state the oscillation frequency. (b) The network is changed so that β = 0.03 at the same frequency. What happens to the amplitude and to the loop gain?

  1. Loop gain: Aβ = (−50) × 0.02 = −1.0.
  2. Magnitude: |Aβ| = 1.0, so the magnitude condition is met exactly.
  3. Phase: 180° from the inverting amplifier plus 180° from the network gives 360°, a whole multiple. The phase condition holds at 5 kHz.
  4. Both conditions hold at 5 kHz, so the circuit oscillates at f₀ = 5 kHz.
  5. For part (b), Aβ = (−50) × 0.03 = −1.5, so |Aβ| = 1.5, greater than 1.
  6. Phase is unchanged, so f₀ stays 5 kHz but the amplitude grows on every trip: 1.5, 2.25, 3.375, and so on.
  7. Growth stops when clipping or bias shift reduces the effective gain. The average |Aβ| then settles at 1, and the output is a steady but somewhat distorted sine.

Answer. The circuit satisfies Barkhausen at f₀ = 5 kHz with |Aβ| = 1 and total loop phase 360°; with β = 0.03 the loop gain magnitude becomes 1.5, so the amplitude grows until limiting brings the average loop gain back to 1.

Worked example 2

Question. A non-inverting amplifier (phase shift 0°) drives a feedback network that returns 5% of the output with no extra phase shift at 1.2 kHz. Find the loop gain if the amplifier gain is 15, and the minimum gain needed for oscillation.

  1. Feedback fraction β = 5% = 0.05.
  2. Loop gain with A = 15: Aβ = 15 × 0.05 = 0.75.
  3. |Aβ| = 0.75 is less than 1, so the oscillation dies out even though the phase condition is met at 1.2 kHz.
  4. Minimum gain: A ≥ 1/β = 1/0.05 = 20.
  5. With A = 20 the loop gain is exactly 1 and the circuit sustains oscillation at 1.2 kHz.

Answer. The loop gain is 0.75, below one, so it does not oscillate; the minimum amplifier gain for oscillation is 20.

Exam traps

  • Checking only |Aβ| = 1 and forgetting the phase. Both conditions are needed; either alone proves nothing.
  • Leaving the amplifier's own 180° inversion out of the loop phase. A common-emitter stage plus a 180° network gives 360°, not 180°.
  • Using the negative-feedback denominator 1 + Aβ for a positive-feedback loop. Positive feedback gives 1 − Aβ, and the gain blows up as Aβ approaches 1.
  • Confusing this with the stability test of 4.3, where |Aβ| must fall below 1 by the time the phase reaches −180°. Here |Aβ| must reach 1 exactly at 0° or 360°.
  • Forgetting that the steady-state average |Aβ| equals 1, while the small-signal value is deliberately a little above 1 to start the oscillation.

4.5 RC phase shift and Wien bridge oscillators

An RC oscillator chooses its frequency by phase, not by resonance. Three RC sections can shift a signal by 180° at one frequency, and an inverting amplifier adds the other 180°. A Wien network does the same with one lead-lag pair at 0° but returns only one third of the output. Both satisfy the Barkhausen condition at a single frequency set by R and C. They are the standard audio sine sources in function generators and the first oscillator built in most analog labs.

You need 4.4 for the loop conditions, 4.1 for the feedback fraction, and the RC frequency response and inverting amplifier from Units 3 and 2.

Three identical RC sections make 180° at one frequency

An RC phase-shift oscillator uses only resistors and capacitors. Three identical sections are connected as a ladder. Each section has a resistor and a capacitor, and the sections load each other. At very low frequency the capacitors block the signal. At very high frequency the capacitors short it. Somewhere between, the total phase shift through the three sections passes through 180°.

f₀ = 1/(2πRC√6)

R is the resistance in each section in ohms (Ω); C is the capacitance in each section in farads (F); f₀ is the oscillation frequency in hertz (Hz).

At f₀ the network also attenuates the signal: it returns only 1/29 of the output to the amplifier input. That number comes from the same ladder solution, not from three isolated filters.

The amplifier must therefore have a voltage gain magnitude of at least 29. It must also invert, because the RC network already supplies 180°; the amplifier's 180° makes the total loop phase 360°. A single BJT in common emitter inverts and can give a gain above 29 if its bias and hfe allow it. An op-amp inverting amplifier with Rf/Ri = 29 is the cleaner choice; see Unit 2 for the inverting amplifier.

The circuit is simple and needs no inductor, but it needs high gain and three matched RC sections. Tuning is awkward: changing frequency means changing all three resistors or all three capacitors together. It is used as a fixed-frequency audio source in labs.

The Wien bridge: one lead-lag pair, gain of three

The Wien bridge uses one series RC branch and one parallel RC branch. The series branch is R and C in series from the output to the amplifier input. The parallel branch is R and C in parallel from that input to ground. The two branches form a lead-lag network: one branch leads, the other lags.

β = 1/√(9 + (ωRC − 1/(ωRC))²)

β is the fraction of the output fed back; ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s; R and C are the matched resistance in ohms and capacitance in farads.

The phase of β is zero at ωRC = 1. At that point β = 1/3 exactly. So f₀ = 1/(2πRC). The amplifier must be non-inverting with a gain of 3: 1 + Rf/Ri = 3, so Rf = 2Ri. At f₀ the loop phase is 0° from the network plus 0° from the non-inverting amplifier, a total of 0° or 360°.

The Wien bridge needs only one R-C pair to set frequency, has low gain and low distortion, and tunes easily with a ganged variable resistor. It is the standard audio sine oscillator in function generators.

Starting, stabilising and choosing between them

If the loop gain is exactly 1, the oscillator sits on the edge. Any small drift stops it. In practice the gain is set a little above the threshold so oscillation starts from noise and grows. Then a nonlinear element reduces the gain to 1 at the desired amplitude.

A common method uses a small incandescent lamp in the Wien bridge feedback divider. The lamp is Ri, the resistor to ground. Gain is 1 + Rf/R_lamp. When the output amplitude rises, the lamp heats, its resistance rises, and the gain falls. When the output falls, the lamp cools and the gain rises. The amplitude settles where the average gain is 3.

A thermistor works the same way. A diode limiter is cheaper but clips the peaks and adds distortion. The lamp or thermistor is gentler and keeps a clean sine wave.

Exam traps

  • Using 1/(2πRC) for the three-section phase-shift oscillator. The √6 belongs there; the Wien bridge has no √6.
  • Forgetting that the phase-shift amplifier must invert. Without the extra 180°, the loop phase is wrong and the circuit does not oscillate.
  • Setting the Wien gain to 2 or 4. At f₀ β = 1/3, so the non-inverting gain must be 3: Rf = 2Ri.
  • Assuming the RC network is unloaded. The 1/29 and the frequency formula come from the loaded ladder; a high-impedance amplifier input and a low-impedance output are needed.
  • Designing gain exactly 29 or 3 and expecting oscillation to start. Component tolerance and drift mean you start slightly above and use amplitude limiting.

4.6 LC oscillators: Hartley, Colpitts and Clapp

An LC oscillator holds a transistor amplifier at one frequency by returning a fraction of its output through a parallel tank. The tank impedance peaks at f₀, and a tap on the inductor or on the capacitor divider returns just enough signal to satisfy Barkhausen. Hartley taps the inductor, Colpitts taps the capacitor, and Clapp adds a small series capacitor for stability. These are the tuned oscillators inside radio transmitters, TV tuners and signal generators.

You need: 4.4 for the Barkhausen criterion, Unit 2 for BJT gain, and Unit 3 for tuned amplifiers and Q.

The parallel tank chooses the frequency

A parallel LC tank has an inductor L and a capacitor C side by side. At one frequency the inductive and capacitive currents cancel. That frequency is resonance: f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)). L is the inductance in henries (H); C is the capacitance in farads (F); f₀ is in hertz (Hz).

Near f₀ the tank impedance is large; far from f₀ it is small. The peak value is R, the loss resistance in parallel with the tank. Q measures sharpness: Q = R/(ω₀L) = ω₀CR, where ω₀ = 2πf₀. A high Q gives a tall, narrow peak. The figure plots |Z|/R against f/f₀ for Q = 10 and Q = 50; the higher-Q curve is much sharper.

Phase matters. Exactly at f₀ the tank is resistive and its phase is 0°. Below f₀ it looks inductive (+90°); above f₀ it looks capacitive (−90°). The amplifier and the feedback connections supply the remaining phase so that the total loop phase is 360° at f₀. Away from f₀ the tank phase moves quickly, the loop phase is wrong, and the oscillation dies out. So the tank selects one frequency.

Hartley: a tap between two coils

A Hartley oscillator uses one coil split into two parts, L1 and L2, with the tap between them. The amplifier output drives the whole coil. The tap returns a fraction of the output to the input. The two coils are in series for AC, so the equivalent inductance is L_eq = L1 + L2. If the coils share a magnetic core, add 2M, where M is the mutual inductance: L_eq = L1 + L2 + 2M. Then f₀ = 1/(2π√(L_eq C)).

The tap fraction is β = L2/(L1 + L2). This is the part of the output returned to the input. For oscillation the amplifier gain A must be at least 1/β = (L1 + L2)/L2. If the gain is lower, the loop gain magnitude is below 1 and the oscillation decays. If it is higher, the amplitude grows until the transistor limits it.

The trap: the frequency depends on L1 + L2, never on L1 or L2 alone. Using one coil gives a wrong, higher frequency. Hartley oscillators are used from tens of kilohertz to tens of megahertz, for example in simple radio receivers and signal generators.

Colpitts: a tap between two capacitors

A Colpitts oscillator taps the capacitor instead of the inductor. The tank has one inductor L and two capacitors C1 and C2 in series. The tap between the capacitors returns the feedback. The equivalent capacitance is C_eq = C1C2/(C1 + C2). Then f₀ = 1/(2π√(L C_eq)).

The divider returns a fraction β = C1/(C1 + C2) of the output. The amplifier gain must be at least 1/β = (C1 + C2)/C1.

Colpitts is preferred at high frequency. The capacitive divider swamps the transistor's own small capacitances (C_be, C_bc), which otherwise shift f₀. The capacitors also give a low-impedance path at the tap, so the transistor's input capacitance matters less. Colpitts oscillators are common in FM transmitters, RF signal generators and oscillators above a few megahertz.

Clapp: a Colpitts with a small series capacitor

A Clapp oscillator adds a capacitor C3 in series with the inductor L. The equivalent capacitance is 1/C_eq = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + 1/C3. If C3 is much smaller than C1 and C2, then C_eq ≈ C3. The frequency becomes f₀ ≈ 1/(2π√(L C3)).

Because C_eq is nearly C3, the frequency hardly depends on the transistor's internal capacitances or on C1 and C2. That makes Clapp more stable than Colpitts. The cost is a narrower tuning range. C3 must stay small compared with C1 and C2, so it can be varied only over a limited band before the approximation C_eq ≈ C3 (and the feedback fraction set by C1, C2) changes. Q is higher, so the output is cleaner. Clapp is used in stable RF oscillators and in test equipment.

  • Unit slips. Convert µH to 10⁻⁶ H and pF to 10⁻¹² F before putting numbers into f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)).

4.7 Crystal oscillators

A quartz crystal is a piezoelectric resonator: a thin slice of quartz with metal plates on both faces. It behaves as an electrical tank with an extremely high quality factor: Q is typically 10⁴ to 10⁶, roughly a thousand times higher than a coil-and-capacitor tank (Q about 10² to 10³). An oscillator built around it holds its frequency to a few parts per million. Watches, real-time clocks, microcontrollers, radios and test instruments all use crystal oscillators. This chapter explains the equivalent circuit, the two resonances, and the circuits that use them.

You need 4.4 for the Barkhausen criterion and 4.6 for the LC tank and Q.

Piezoelectric effect and the model

Quartz is piezoelectric. Squeeze a quartz slice and a voltage appears across it; apply a voltage and the slice changes shape. A crystal resonator uses the second effect. Metal electrodes on both faces form a capacitor, and the electric field makes the quartz vibrate mechanically. The vibration is strongest at a mechanical resonance fixed by the cut and size of the slice.

Electrically, the crystal is modelled by a series branch R, L, C in parallel with C₀. C₀ is the capacitance of the electrodes, a few picofarads. The branch R-L-C is the motional branch: it represents the mechanical vibration as an electrical resonance. L can be henries, C a few femtofarads, and R tens to hundreds of ohms. The quality factor is Q = ω_s L / R, where ω_s = 2πf_s is the angular series resonant frequency in rad/s. Because L is large and C is tiny, Q is very high.

f_s = 1/(2π√(LC)), Q = (1/R)√(L/C) = ω_s L/R

f_s is the series resonant frequency in hertz; L is the motional inductance in henries; C is the motional capacitance in farads; R is the motional resistance in ohms; Q is the quality factor, a pure number.

Series and parallel resonance

At f_s the series branch has zero reactance, so the crystal impedance is at its minimum, approximately R (strictly R in parallel with C₀, and C₀'s reactance is much larger than R). Above f_s the series branch is inductive. The crystal then looks like an inductor in parallel with C₀, and the parallel resonance occurs at f_p = f_s√(1+C/C₀). Here f_p is the parallel resonant frequency in hertz, f_s is the series resonant frequency in hertz, C is the motional capacitance in farads, and C₀ is the electrode capacitance in farads.

Between f_s and f_p the crystal is inductive. That is the region used by Pierce and Colpitts crystal oscillators, where the crystal replaces the inductor of the tank. At f_p the impedance is maximum. The two frequencies lie only a fraction of a percent apart because C/C₀ is very small: C is in femtofarads and C₀ is in picofarads.

An external load capacitor or trim capacitor adds to C₀. More capacitance lowers f_p and pulls the frequency slightly. Manufacturers specify a load capacitance, often 8 pF, 12 pF or 20 pF, at which the nominal frequency is measured.

Crystal oscillator circuits

Two circuit styles dominate. In series-resonant mode the crystal sits in the feedback path. At f_s it has low resistance, so feedback is maximum and the amplifier oscillates there. A small series capacitor can adjust the exact frequency. This mode is common in discrete BJT oscillators.

In parallel-resonant mode the crystal works as the inductor of a Colpitts or Pierce tank. Two capacitors C₁ and C₂ across the crystal provide the feedback tap, exactly as in the LC Colpitts circuit of 4.6. The Pierce circuit puts the crystal between the amplifier input and output and the capacitors from each end to ground. A CMOS inverter Pierce circuit is the standard clock oscillator inside microcontrollers.

Common frequencies: 32.768 kHz for watches and real-time clocks, because 2¹⁵ = 32768 and fifteen binary divider stages give 1 Hz. Microcontrollers and radios use 1 MHz to 30 MHz, often 4 MHz, 8 MHz, 16 MHz or 25 MHz. Tolerance is typically 10 to 100 ppm at 25 °C, plus temperature drift. A 20 ppm crystal at 10 MHz can be off by 200 Hz.

Compare with an LC tank. The crystal has far higher Q, needs no coil, and is far more stable. Its drawbacks are a fixed frequency, a fragile package, and a limited drive level. Some crystals are cut to vibrate at an odd overtone, such as the third overtone, and the circuit is tuned to that overtone to reach a higher frequency. The loop still obeys the Barkhausen condition of 4.4.

  2. From Q = ω_s L/R, L = QR/ω_s = (10⁵ × 100)/(6.2832 × 10⁶) = 1.5915 H.
  3. C = 1/(ω_s²L) = 1/((6.2832 × 10⁶)² × 1.5915) = 1.5915 × 10⁻¹⁴ F = 0.0159 pF.
  4. C/C₀ = 0.0159/3 = 0.00530.
  5. f_p = f_s√(1+C/C₀) = 1 MHz × √1.00530 = 1.00265 MHz.
  6. f_p − f_s = 0.00265 MHz = 2.65 kHz.

Answer. L = 1.5915 H, C = 0.0159 pF, f_p = 1.00265 MHz, and the parallel resonance lies 2.65 kHz above the series resonance.

Exam traps

  • Assuming a crystal has a single resonant frequency. Series and parallel resonance differ, and C₀ must be included whenever f_p is asked.
  • Using the electrode capacitance C₀ in place of the motional capacitance C inside the square-root formula for f_p.
  • Forgetting that the crystal is inductive only between f_s and f_p. In a Pierce circuit it replaces the inductor in that region, not at f_s itself.
  • Sliding a decimal in ppm. 1 ppm means 1 part in 10⁶, so 20 ppm at 10 MHz is 200 Hz, not 20 Hz or 2 kHz.
  • Ignoring the specified load capacitance. A crystal marked 12 pF is not guaranteed at its nominal frequency when the circuit presents 20 pF.

Remember What is the piezoelectric effect? 2 marks

A voltage applied across a quartz slice makes it change shape, and squeezing the slice produces a voltage across it.

Remember Which frequency is standard for watches and real-time clocks? 2 marks

32.768 kHz, because 2¹⁵ = 32768 and fifteen binary divider stages produce 1 Hz.

Understand Why does a quartz crystal have a much higher Q than an LC tank? 3 marks

Its motional inductance is very large and its motional capacitance is a few femtofarads, so Q = (1/R)√(L/C) is very large. Mechanical vibration loses little energy, so R is small.

Understand Explain the difference between series resonance and parallel resonance in a crystal. 3 marks

At f_s the series R-L-C branch is resistive and the crystal impedance is minimum, about R. At f_p the series branch is inductive and resonates with C₀, giving maximum impedance. The two frequencies are separated by a fraction of a percent.

Apply A crystal has f_s = 4 MHz, motional capacitance C = 0.02 pF and electrode capacitance C₀ = 5 pF. Find f_p. 4 marks

C/C₀ = 0.02/5 = 0.004. √(1.004) = 1.001999. f_p = 4 MHz × 1.001999 = 4.008 MHz, about 8 kHz above f_s.

Analyze In a Pierce oscillator the crystal is said to work as an inductor. In which frequency range does it do so, and why? 4 marks

Between f_s and f_p. There the series branch is inductive and, together with C₀ and the external capacitors, it forms a parallel resonant tank. Below f_s it is capacitive; above f_p it is capacitive again.

Analyze Why is a 32.768 kHz crystal preferred over a 1 MHz crystal for a watch? 3 marks

32768 = 2¹⁵, so a simple chain of fifteen flip-flop dividers gives exactly 1 Hz. A 1 MHz crystal would need a more complex divider chain and would draw more current.

Takeaways

  1. Negative feedback trades gain for everything else: bandwidth, distortion, impedance control and gain that depends only on β.
  2. The factor (1+Aβ) is the single number that sets how much gain is lost and how much bandwidth and linearity are gained.
  3. Sampling the output voltage lowers output resistance; sampling the output current raises it; series mixing raises input resistance and shunt mixing lowers it.
  4. An amplifier is stable while the loop-gain magnitude falls below 1 before the loop phase reaches −180°; the two margins measure how close it gets.
  5. An oscillator is a positive-feedback amplifier whose loop gain is exactly 1 at one frequency and less than 1 at every other frequency.
  6. Excess loop gain above 1 is what starts oscillation; a nonlinear or temperature-dependent element is what holds the amplitude steady at 1.
  7. RC oscillators suit audio frequencies because resistor and capacitor values stay practical; LC oscillators suit radio frequencies because the tank sets a sharp resonant frequency.
  8. A quartz crystal gives frequency stability in parts per million, which is why clocks, watches and radio transmitters use it.

Understand Name the four negative-feedback topologies. For each, state what is sampled at the output, what is mixed at the input, and the effect on input and output resistance. 12 marks

Remember State the Barkhausen criterion for oscillation. Explain why a practical oscillator needs the loop gain to be slightly more than 1 at the start. 10 marks

Understand Define gain margin and phase margin. How are they read from a Bode plot, and what values are normally considered safe? 10 marks

Understand Explain the piezoelectric effect in quartz. Why does a crystal oscillator hold its frequency so well, and what is the difference between its series and parallel resonant frequencies? 10 marks

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze A three-stage amplifier has a loop gain whose magnitude is 0 dB at 100 kHz while the loop phase there is −150°, and whose phase reaches −180° at 1 MHz where the magnitude is −30 dB. Find the phase margin and gain margin and comment on stability. 13 marks

PM = 180° − 150° = 30°; GM = 30 dB at the −180° crossing; Stable, because the magnitude is below 1 before the phase reaches −180°; PM of 30° is small; expect ringing and overshoot in the step response; Aim to raise PM towards 45°–60° by moving the dominant pole or adding compensation; Gain margin is comfortable, so instability would come from phase, not magnitude.

Evaluate Compare the four feedback topologies for input resistance, output resistance and typical use. For a sensor with a high source resistance driving a low-resistance load, which topology would you choose and why? 14 marks

Voltage-series raises R_in and lowers R_out; Voltage-shunt lowers R_in and lowers R_out; Current-series raises R_in and raises R_out; Current-shunt lowers R_in and raises R_out; High source resistance needs a large R_in, so use series mixing; Low load resistance needs a small R_out, so use voltage sampling; Choice: voltage-series feedback; note the cost in gain and in stability margins.

Evaluate Compare an RC phase-shift oscillator, a Wien bridge oscillator, a Colpitts oscillator and a crystal oscillator for frequency stability, tuning range and typical application. Which one would you pick for a fixed 10 MHz clock and why? 14 marks

Analyze Explain how negative feedback affects the poles of an amplifier and how this decides stability. Describe pole splitting and its cost. 13 marks

Transfer function with poles p₁, p₂; feedback moves them in the complex plane; Aβ is a complex number, so the −180° phase shift comes from the poles; As Aβ grows the poles move together, then break away and turn towards the imaginary axis; At the value where a pole pair crosses into the right half plane the circuit oscillates; Dominant-pole compensation pushes one pole low so the gain reaches 0 dB while the phase is far from −180°; Cost: reduced bandwidth and reduced slew rate.

Glossary

Amplitude limiting
Any nonlinearity or gain-changing element that stops the oscillation growing and holds the loop gain at 1.
Barkhausen criterion
The pair of conditions (loop magnitude 1 and loop phase a multiple of 360°) that allows steady oscillation.
Bode plot
Two graphs of 20 log₁₀|Aβ| in dB and phase in degrees, both plotted against log frequency.
Clapp oscillator
A Colpitts oscillator with a small extra capacitor in series with the inductor, giving improved frequency stability.
Closed-loop gain
The gain with feedback connected, A/(1+Aβ) for negative feedback and A/(1−Aβ) for positive feedback.
Colpitts oscillator
An LC oscillator whose feedback comes from a tap on a capacitive divider.
Crystal oscillator
An oscillator whose frequency is set by a vibrating quartz plate of very high Q.
Current-shunt feedback
Output current sampled and feedback mixed in shunt at the input; low input resistance and high output resistance.
Desensitivity factor
The quantity (1+Aβ) by which gain is divided and stability of gain against device changes is multiplied.
Dominant pole
The lowest-frequency pole of the amplifier, which sets where the gain starts to fall at 20 dB per decade.
Feedback factor
The ratio β = feedback signal ÷ output signal, a number with no unit.
Gain margin
How many dB the loop gain could still rise before the circuit becomes unstable at the −180° phase point.
Hartley oscillator
An LC oscillator whose feedback comes from a tap on the inductor.
Input resistance
The resistance seen looking into the amplifier input; raised by series mixing and lowered by shunt mixing.
LC tank
An inductor and capacitor connected together, storing energy at f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)).
Loop gain
The product Aβ, the total gain a signal sees in going round the amplifier and the feedback path once.
Negative feedback
Returning a fraction of the output so that it subtracts from the input signal.
Oscillator
An amplifier with positive feedback that produces a steady sine wave without any external input signal.
Output resistance
The resistance seen looking back into the output; lowered by voltage sampling and raised by current sampling.
Phase margin
How many degrees of extra phase lag the loop can take at the 0 dB point before it oscillates.
Piezoelectricity
The property of certain crystals to produce a voltage when strained and to strain when a voltage is applied.
RC phase-shift oscillator
An oscillator in which three RC sections produce 180° of phase shift at the oscillating frequency.
Voltage-series feedback
Output voltage sampled and feedback mixed in series at the input; high input resistance and low output resistance.
Wien bridge oscillator
An oscillator using a series RC and a parallel RC arm to give zero phase shift at f₀ = 1/(2πRC).
