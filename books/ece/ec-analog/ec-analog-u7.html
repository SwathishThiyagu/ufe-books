7.1 Active filters: low-pass, high-pass, band-pass, band-stop

An active filter uses an op-amp with resistors and capacitors to pass some frequencies and block others. The op-amp supplies gain and a low output impedance, so the cut-off frequency and the pass-band gain can be set independently. Two first-order responses, low-pass and high-pass, plus two second-order responses, band-pass and band-stop, cover most needs. The second-order responses are built by combining the first-order sections. They appear in audio tone controls, anti-alias stages before an ADC, and mains-hum removal in instrumentation. A Bode plot gives the cut-off frequency, the pass-band gain and the roll-off.

You need: the inverting and non-inverting amplifiers and virtual ground from Unit 6; cut-off frequency and Bode magnitude plots from Unit 3.

First-order low-pass and high-pass sections

Build a low-pass from an op-amp in the non-inverting connection. Put R in series with the input and C from the + input to ground. The feedback resistors R_f (from output to − input) and R_1 (from − input to ground) set the pass-band gain A0 = 1 + R_f/R_1. The RC network makes the input to the op-amp V+ = V_in/(1 + j2πfRC). The output magnitude is A0/√(1 + (f/f_c)²). The cut-off is the −3 dB point, where the gain is A0/√2.

f_c = 1/(2πRC)

f_c is the cut-off frequency in hertz; R is the series resistance in ohms; C is the capacitance in farads.

Swap R and C for a high-pass: C in series, R to ground. The cut-off is the same. The gain is A0 for f ≫ f_c and falls below f_c. Above f_c the low-pass falls by 20 dB per decade (about 6 dB per octave) because one RC section is one pole. The high-pass rises by 20 dB per decade below f_c. The op-amp's own gain-bandwidth product limits how high f_c can go; see Unit 3.

Band-pass and band-stop

Cascade a high-pass and a low-pass to make a band-pass. Each section contributes one pole, so the band-pass is second-order. The high-pass sets the lower cut-off f_L; the low-pass sets the upper cut-off f_H. The pass band lies between them. The centre frequency is the geometric mean f0 = √(f_L f_H), and the bandwidth is B = f_H − f_L. The quality factor Q = f0/B measures sharpness; Chapter 7.2 treats Q properly.

For a band-stop, the low-pass and high-pass outputs must cancel at the notch; the band-stop is second-order. Subtract one from the other in a difference amplifier, or use a twin-T notch. A twin-T has two T networks, one RC and one CR, and rejects f0 = 1/(2πRC). The output is near zero at f0 and passes other frequencies.

The band-pass and band-stop curves in the figure are drawn for Q = 1. For a cascade of two first-order sections with f_L = f_H, Q = 1/3, so the peak at f0 is only 0.5 before normalising; separating f_L and f_H raises Q.

Ideal filters have flat pass bands and infinite roll-off. Practical first-order sections have rounded corners at f_c, finite roll-off, and gain errors from resistor and capacitor tolerances. The op-amp adds its own high-frequency roll-off. Keep f_c well below the op-amp's gain-bandwidth product. Use 1% resistors and stable capacitors such as polypropylene for predictable cut-offs.

Reading the magnitude plot

Normalise frequency as x = f/f_c. Plot |H| against x on a log axis. For A0 = 1, the low-pass curve is 1 at x = 0.1, 0.707 at x = 1 and 0.1 at x = 10. The high-pass is 0.1 at x = 0.1, 0.707 at x = 1 and 1 at x = 10. The band-pass peaks at x = 1; the band-stop falls to zero at x = 1.

To sketch, draw a flat pass band at A0. At f_c, drop or rise by 3 dB. Then draw a straight asymptote with slope 20 dB per decade per pole. For the low-pass, the asymptote is flat below f_c and falls at 20 dB/decade above. The true curve is 3 dB below the corner. For the high-pass, mirror it.

|H| = A0/√(1 + (f/f_c)²)

|H| is the output magnitude divided by input magnitude; A0 is the pass-band gain; f is the signal frequency in hertz; f_c is the cut-off in hertz. For the high-pass use |H| = A0 (f/f_c)/√(1 + (f/f_c)²).

To compute output amplitude at any frequency, substitute f/f_c into the magnitude formula. Convert to dB with 20 log10 |H|. The figure shows all four shapes normalised to A0 = 1.

Worked example

Question. Design a first-order active low-pass filter with a cut-off frequency of 1 kHz and a pass-band gain of magnitude 2. Use C = 10 nF. Find R and the gain in dB at 10 kHz.

  1. The cut-off formula gives R = 1/(2π f_c C).
  2. Substitute f_c = 1000 Hz and C = 10 nF = 10 × 10⁻⁹ F: R = 1/(2π × 1000 × 10 × 10⁻⁹) = 15.92 kΩ.
  3. The pass-band gain is 2. In decibels: 20 log10 2 = 6.02 dB, about 6 dB.
  4. At 10 kHz, f/f_c = 10. The magnitude is |H| = 2/√(1 + 10²) = 2/√101 = 0.199.
  5. In decibels: 20 log10 0.199 = −14.0 dB, or 6.02 − 20.04 = −14.02 dB.

Answer. R = 15.92 kΩ; the gain at 10 kHz is 0.199, i.e. −14.0 dB.

Exam traps

  • Putting R in kΩ and C in µF straight into 1/(2πRC) without converting to ohms and farads.
  • Writing the roll-off as 20 dB per octave instead of 20 dB per decade.
  • Forgetting that f_c is the −3 dB point, not the frequency where the gain reaches zero.
  • Mixing the pass-band gain A0 with the gain at f_c; at f_c the gain is A0/√2.
  • In a band-pass, using the arithmetic mean (f_L + f_H)/2 instead of the geometric mean √(f_L f_H); in a band-stop, forgetting that the two paths must cancel at the notch.

7.2 Butterworth and Chebyshev filters; Sallen-Key circuits

Real filters are specified by a cut-off frequency, a stop-band attenuation and an allowed pass-band ripple. An ideal brick-wall response cannot be built, so we choose a mathematical approximation: Butterworth for a flat pass band, Chebyshev for a sharper transition with ripple. A high-order approximation is split into second-order sections, each with a known Q, and each section is built with one op-amp in a Sallen-Key circuit. The same blocks appear in audio anti-alias filters, ECG front ends and sensor conditioning.

You need: Chapter 7.1 for the four responses, Unit 3 for Bode plots and roll-off, and Unit 4 for gain margin and the stability limit.

Butterworth and Chebyshev approximations

A low-pass filter passes frequencies below ωc and attenuates above it. The order n sets the ultimate roll-off at 20n dB per decade. The Butterworth magnitude is |H(jω)|² = 1/(1 + (ω/ωc)2n). Here ω is the angular frequency in rad/s and ωc is the cut-off in rad/s. The response is maximally flat at ω = 0: the first 2n − 1 derivatives of |H|² are zero. There is no pass-band ripple. A second-order Butterworth section has damping ratio ζ = 1/√2 and pole Q = 1/(2ζ) = 0.707.

A fourth-order Butterworth is not four identical sections. It splits into two second-order sections with Q = 0.541 and Q = 1.307. The low-Q section is flat; the high-Q section peaks slightly near fc, and the cascade gives the flat Butterworth shape. Some syllabi call d = 1/Q = 2ζ the dissipation factor (some say damping); for Q = 0.707, d = 1.414, twice ζ.

The Chebyshev type-I magnitude is |H(jω)|² = 1/(1 + ε² Tn²(ω/ωc)). Tn is the Chebyshev polynomial of order n, and ε sets the pass-band ripple. If the allowed ripple is Ap dB, ε = √(10Ap/10 − 1). The response ripples between 1 and 1/√(1 + ε²) in the pass band and then falls faster than Butterworth of the same order. A 1 dB Chebyshev meets a given stop-band specification with a lower order than Butterworth, at the cost of ripple and a poorer phase response. Its section Q values come from tables or a design program.

Sallen-Key low-pass and high-pass

The equal-component Sallen-Key low-pass uses two equal resistors R and two equal capacitors C. The op-amp is a non-inverting amplifier with gain K = 1 + Rf/R1. Rf is the feedback resistor from output to inverting input, and R1 goes from inverting input to ground. The cut-off and pole Q are fc = 1/(2πRC) and Q = 1/(3 − K). These hold for K < 3. The second capacitor feeds a fraction of the output back to the RC node; this positive feedback raises Q without an inductor.

For a high-pass response, swap each R with C. The cut-off is still fc = 1/(2πRC) and Q = 1/(3 − K). The low-pass to high-pass transformation replaces s/ωc by ωc/s in the normalized low-pass function, where s is the complex frequency variable. In components, resistors become capacitors and capacitors become resistors, and the gain stage is unchanged.

  1. Pick C as a convenient value, usually 1 nF to 100 nF.
  2. Compute R = 1/(2π fc C).
  3. Find K from K = 3 − 1/Q.
  4. Choose R1 and set Rf = (K − 1)R1. Keep K below 3.

Frequency scaling to a new cut-off fc′: divide every capacitor by k or divide every resistor by k, where k = fc′/fc; fc becomes fc′ and Q is unchanged. Impedance scaling: multiply every resistor by m and divide every capacitor by m; fc and Q stay the same and the impedance level rises by m. To move a designed filter from fc to fc′, scale all capacitors by fc/fc′ or scale all resistors by fc/fc′ (equivalently, divide the resistors by fc′/fc).

From specification to second-order sections

Start with fc, the stop-band frequency fs, the stop-band attenuation As in dB, and the pass-band ripple Ap in dB. For Butterworth, the order needed is n ≥ log10(10As/10 − 1) / (2 log10(fs/fc)). Round n up to an integer. For Chebyshev, use the ripple ε and the Chebyshev order formula, or read n from a table. A higher n means more sections and a steeper roll-off.

For an even-order Butterworth, split n into n/2 second-order sections. The Q values are Qk = 1/(2 cos((2k − 1)π/(2n))), for k = 1 to n/2. For n = 4, k = 1 gives Q = 0.541 and k = 2 gives Q = 1.307. For odd n, one section is first-order and the rest are second-order. Cascade the sections in any order; put the lowest Q first if you want to avoid internal peaking.

For a high-pass filter, use the same Q values and swap R and C in every section. For a narrow band-pass, apply the low-pass to band-pass transformation s/ωc → (s² + ω0²)/(B s). Here ω0 is the centre frequency in rad/s and B is the bandwidth in rad/s. The result has centre ω0 and quality factor Qbp = ω0/B. It can be built as a Sallen-Key band-pass or as the single-op-amp positive-feedback circuit some syllabi call Deliyannis-Friend.

Worked example

Question. Design a second-order Butterworth low-pass filter with fc = 2 kHz using the equal-component Sallen-Key circuit. Take C = 10 nF. Choose standard 1% resistors and R1 = 10 kΩ. Find R, K and Rf.

  1. For the equal-component low-pass, R = 1/(2π fc C). Substitute fc = 2000 Hz and C = 10 × 10−9 F: R = 1/(2π × 2000 × 10 × 10−9) = 7957.7 Ω = 7.96 kΩ. Use the standard 1% value 8.2 kΩ.
  2. A second-order Butterworth has Q = 1/√2 = 0.707. The gain must be K = 3 − 1/Q = 3 − 1.414 = 1.586.
  3. With R1 = 10 kΩ, Rf = (K − 1)R1 = 0.586 × 10 kΩ = 5.86 kΩ. Use the standard 1% value 5.9 kΩ.
  4. The final section uses R = 8.2 kΩ, C = 10 nF, R1 = 10 kΩ and Rf = 5.9 kΩ, giving Q ≈ 0.71 and K ≈ 1.59. With R = 8.2 kΩ and C = 10 nF the actual cut-off is 1/(2π × 8.2 kΩ × 10 nF) ≈ 1.94 kHz, about 3% low; use 7.96 kΩ (or trim R) if the 2 kHz value matters.

Answer. Use R = 8.2 kΩ, C = 10 nF, K = 1.586 and Rf = 5.9 kΩ with R1 = 10 kΩ.

Exam traps

  • Taking Q = 1 for a Butterworth section. The second-order Butterworth Q is 0.707; a fourth-order Butterworth needs Q = 0.541 and Q = 1.307.
  • Forgetting that the Sallen-Key gain must stay below 3. At K = 3, Q becomes infinite and the filter oscillates; above 3 the positive feedback wins.
  • Using fc = 1/(2πRC) when the two resistors or two capacitors are not equal. The simple formula is only for the equal-component circuit.
  • Confusing pass-band ripple in dB with stop-band attenuation in dB. Chebyshev ripple is allowed inside the pass band; it does not replace the stop-band specification.
  • Ignoring resistor tolerance. A 5% error in R shifts fc by about 5%; Q is set by the gain K = 1 + Rf/R1, so it is mismatch between the two filter resistors or capacitors, and the tolerance of Rf and R1, that changes Q. High-Q sections are the sensitive ones. Use 1% parts for a Q near 1.3.

7.3 State-variable, universal and switched-capacitor filters

A state-variable filter wires a summer and two integrators into a loop. The summer output is high-pass, the first integrator gives band-pass, the second gives low-pass. The same circuit therefore supplies three responses at once, and adding them gives a notch. On an integrated circuit a switched capacitor driven by a clock replaces the resistor, so the cut-off becomes a capacitor ratio times the clock frequency. These filters sit in audio crossovers, anti-alias stages and tunable instrumentation where one accurate clock must set many cut-offs.

You need: the op-amp integrator, virtual ground and summing amplifier from Unit 6, and the second-order response and Q from Chapter 7.2.

The state-variable loop and its three outputs

Take an inverting summer, then two inverting integrators. Connect the second integrator's output back to the summer. Let both integrators use the same resistor R and capacitor C. Let Vin be the input, and VHP, VBP, VLP be the summer, first-integrator and second-integrator outputs. Some syllabi call this the Kerwin–Huelsman–Newcomb (KHN) filter.

An inverting integrator with input x gives output −x/(sRC), where s is the complex frequency and RC is its time constant in seconds. Two such integrators invert twice, so the loop produces the standard second-order denominator s² + (k/RC)s + 1/(R²C²). Here k is the fraction of VBP fed back to the summer. Comparing with s² + (ω0/Q)s + ω0² gives the design equations.

f0 = 1/(2πRC) Q = 1/k

f0 is the centre frequency in hertz; R is each integrator resistor in ohms; C is each integrator capacitor in farads; k is the gain from the band-pass output back to the summer, a resistor ratio with no unit; Q is the quality factor, with no unit.

VHP is the summer output because a high-pass response falls at low frequency and the loop has not yet integrated it. VBP is the first integrator output: one integration of high-pass gives a band-pass shape. VLP is the second integrator output. The centre frequency f0 is set by R and C only, while Q is set by k. Universal filter and the Tow-Thomas biquad

Because all three responses are available, the block is called a universal filter. A fourth op-amp can sum VHP, VBP and VLP with weighted resistors. Choose the weights so the high-pass and low-pass parts cancel at one frequency; the result is a notch (band-stop) at f0. This is how one chip can provide low-pass, band-pass, high-pass and notch outputs from the same tuning.

The Tow-Thomas biquad is another multi-op-amp form. It uses an input summer, two integrators and a feedback inverter. It also gives low-pass and band-pass outputs directly; a third input adds high-pass or notch. Its design also separates f0 from Q, but the resistor names differ from the state-variable circuit. Only a few universities teach the Tow-Thomas by name; the state-variable loop is the one to recognise first.

Multi-op-amp biquads are used when a filter section must be tuned electronically. Replace the integrator resistors with voltage-controlled resistors or with switched capacitors, and one control voltage or one clock retunes f0. The cost is more op-amps, more resistors and a wider spread of component values than a Sallen-Key section from Chapter 7.2.

Switched-capacitor filters

A resistor is hard to make accurately on a CMOS chip; a capacitor ratio is easy. Take a capacitor C, charge it to voltage V, then dump the charge into the next node every clock period Tclk = 1/fclk. The charge moved per second is C V fclk, so the structure looks like a resistor.

Req = 1/(fclk C)

Req is the equivalent resistance in ohms; fclk is the clock frequency in hertz; C is the switched capacitance in farads.

Put this Req into an integrator with feedback capacitor Ci. The integrator's unity-gain frequency is 1/(2π Req Ci) = fclk C/(2π Ci). The cut-off is therefore a ratio of two on-chip capacitors times fclk. Capacitor ratios match to about 0.1% on a chip, while absolute resistors may vary by 20%. The clock can be crystal-controlled, so the filter is accurate and tunable: change fclk and every cut-off moves in proportion.

The input must stay below fclk/2. The switched capacitor samples the input, so a higher input frequency folds down (aliases) into the passband. In a real chip an anti-alias low-pass filter sits before the switched-capacitor stage. Clock feedthrough and charge injection also limit dynamic range; those details belong to later courses.

Worked example

Question. A switched-capacitor resistor uses C = 1 pF switched at fclk = 100 kHz. It is the input resistor of an inverting integrator whose feedback capacitor is also 1 pF. (a) Find Req. (b) Find the integrator unity-gain frequency. (c) Show that this frequency equals fclk/(2π).

  1. Step 1. Convert C to farads: C = 1 pF = 1 × 10⁻¹² F.
  2. Step 2. Convert fclk to hertz: 100 kHz = 100 × 10³ Hz = 1 × 10⁵ Hz.
  3. Step 3. Req = 1/(fclk C) = 1/(1 × 10⁵ × 1 × 10⁻¹²) = 1/(1 × 10⁻⁷) = 1 × 10⁷ Ω = 10 MΩ.
  4. Step 4. Unity-gain frequency fu = 1/(2π Req Ci). Here Ci = 1 pF = 1 × 10⁻¹² F, so Req Ci = 1 × 10⁷ × 1 × 10⁻¹² = 1 × 10⁻⁵ s.
  5. Step 5. fu = 1/(2π × 1 × 10⁻⁵) = 1/(6.283185 × 10⁻⁵) = 15 915 Hz = 15.9 kHz.
  6. Step 6. fclk/(2π) = (1 × 10⁵)/(6.283185) = 15 915 Hz, the same value.

Answer. Req = 10 MΩ and the unity-gain frequency is 15.9 kHz, equal to fclk/(2π).

Exam traps

  • The summer output is high-pass, not low-pass. The second integrator output is low-pass; swapping them reverses the whole cascade.
  • Q falls as the band-pass feedback k rises. Do not use Q = k; the relation is Q = 1/k.
  • In a switched-capacitor filter the cut-off is not fclk. It is (C/(2π Ci)) fclk, a small fraction of the clock.
  • A switched-capacitor input above fclk/2 aliases; the clock is a sampling rate, not just a tuning knob.
  • Do not compare an RC filter's absolute resistor tolerance with a switched-capacitor filter's capacitor ratio; ratio matching is the reason the switched-capacitor version is accurate.

7.4 Waveform generators: square, triangular and sawtooth

Two op-amps and a few passive parts make a square wave, a triangle wave and a sawtooth wave. A Schmitt trigger snaps between two fixed levels; an integrator turns those levels into straight ramps; the ramps come back and trip the trigger again. The loop starts by itself and needs no tuned LC circuit. Bench function generators, the ramp inside a PWM motor drive, an oscilloscope timebase and the clock of a successive-approximation ADC all use this idea. Because the frequency depends only on resistor and capacitor values, it is easy to predict and easy to set.

You need: the comparator and Schmitt trigger from Unit 6 (Op-Amp Linear and Nonlinear Applications), and the op-amp integrator from 7.3.

Square wave from a Schmitt trigger

A Schmitt trigger is a comparator with two thresholds (Unit 6). Its output cannot stop in between: it sits at +Vsat or −Vsat, within a volt or two of the supplies, because positive feedback drives it all the way.

R2 runs from the output to the non-inverting input, and R1 runs from that input to ground. The non-inverting input therefore sits at a fixed fraction of the output.

β = R<sub>1</sub>/(R<sub>1</sub> + R<sub>2</sub>),  thresholds = ±βV<sub>sat</sub>

β is the feedback fraction (no unit); R1 is the resistor from the non-inverting input to ground, in Ω; R2 is the resistor from the output to the non-inverting input, in Ω; Vsat is the saturated output magnitude, in V.

Put the integrator output on the inverting input. The integrator is non-inverting, so the ramp rises while the square is at +Vsat. While the ramping voltage stays below +βVsat, the output stays at +Vsat. When the ramp crosses +βVsat the comparator flips to −Vsat and the threshold drops to −βVsat. The next flip needs the ramp to climb down to −βVsat. The output is a square wave whose edges are spaced by the time the ramp takes to cross between the two thresholds.

Triangle wave: integrate the square

A non-inverting integrator gives vout = +(1/RC)∫vin dt, so a constant input makes a straight ramp of slope +vin/(RC). (The inverting integrator from 7.3 followed by an inverter also works.) The square wave is ±Vsat, so the slope is ±Vsat/(RC) in V/s. The sign flips at every edge, so the integrator traces up and down between the thresholds, and its peak is exactly the threshold that caused the switch, ±βVsat.

One half period is the time to travel 2βVsat at slope Vsat/(RC). That time is 2βRC, so the full period is 4βRC.

f = 1/(4βRC)

f is the output frequency in Hz; β is the feedback fraction R1/(R1 + R2); R is the integrator input resistor in Ω; C is the integrator capacitor in F.

Vsat cancels. The ramp slope and the thresholds both scale with the supply, so supply drift leaves the frequency alone; only β, R and C set it.

The triangle peak lines up with a square edge. When the ramp reaches +βVsat, the square has just fallen to −Vsat; when the ramp reaches −βVsat, the square has just risen to +Vsat. Sketch the square on top and the triangle below, and every corner of the triangle sits under a transition of the square. The two slopes are equal, so the duty cycle is exactly 50 %.

Sawtooth and duty-cycle control

A sawtooth has unequal rise and fall. The capacitor must charge through one resistance and discharge through another. Split the integrator input resistor into RA and RB, each in series with a diode pointing opposite ways. The diode that conducts while the square is at +Vsat sets the rising-path resistance RA. The other diode, which conducts while the square is at −Vsat, sets the falling-path resistance RB.

duty = R<sub>A</sub>/(R<sub>A</sub> + R<sub>B</sub>),  f = 1/(2βC(R<sub>A</sub> + R<sub>B</sub>))

duty is the fraction of the period spent rising; RA is the resistance on the rising path in Ω; RB is the resistance on the falling path in Ω; β, C as before.

If a potentiometer splits one fixed resistance, the total RA + RB stays constant. The duty knob then moves the duty cycle and leaves the frequency almost unchanged. With RA = 5 kΩ rising and RB = 20 kΩ falling, the duty cycle is 20 %.

A single op-amp relaxation oscillator needs no separate integrator: the capacitor charges straight from the output, so the ramps are exponential and T = 2RC ln((1+β)/(1−β)). The 555 timer charges one capacitor between two fixed fractions of the supply in the same way (7.6). Exponential ramps are cheap; a straight ramp from an integrator is used when the sawtooth must be linear, as in a timebase or a PWM ramp.

Worked example

Question. A function generator is built from a Schmitt trigger and an integrator. The Schmitt trigger runs on ±12 V supplies and saturates at ±12 V. R1 = 10 kΩ goes from the non-inverting input to ground and R2 = 20 kΩ goes from the output to that input. The integrator uses R = 10 kΩ and C = 10 nF. Find (a) β and the thresholds, (b) the frequency, (c) the triangle amplitude peak-to-peak, (d) the ramp slope and the half period, and (e) check whether an op-amp with 0.5 V/µs slew rate is fast enough.

  1. Feedback fraction: β = R1/(R1 + R2) = 10 kΩ/(10 kΩ + 20 kΩ) = 1/3 = 0.3333.
  2. Thresholds: ±βVsat = ±0.3333 × 12 V = ±4 V. The triangle swings from −4 V to +4 V.
  3. Peak-to-peak triangle amplitude: 2βVsat = 2 × 4 V = 8 V.
  4. Integrator time constant: RC = 10 kΩ × 10 nF = 10 × 10³ × 10 × 10⁻⁹ s = 100 µs.
  5. Frequency: f = 1/(4βRC) = 1/(4 × 0.3333 × 100 × 10⁻⁶ s) = 1/(1.3333 × 10⁻⁴ s) = 7500 Hz = 7.5 kHz.
  6. Ramp slope: Vsat/(RC) = 12 V/100 µs = 0.12 V/µs = 1.2 × 10⁵ V/s.
  7. Half period: the ramp travels 8 V at 0.12 V/µs, so T/2 = 8 V ÷ 0.12 V/µs = 66.7 µs and T = 133.3 µs. Then 1/T = 7.5 kHz, which matches step 5.
  8. Slew-rate check: the op-amp must follow 0.12 V/µs. With 0.5 V/µs available the margin is about 4 times, so the triangle stays straight at this frequency.

Answer. β = 1/3, thresholds ±4 V, f = 7.5 kHz, triangle ±4 V (8 V peak-to-peak), slope 0.12 V/µs, half period 66.7 µs; a 0.5 V/µs op-amp is fast enough.

Exam traps

  • Swapping the outputs: the square wave comes from the Schmitt trigger and the triangle from the integrator. Labelling them the other way loses the marks for the whole sketch.
  • Dropping β: f = 1/(2RC) or 1/(2πRC) is wrong here. The threshold fraction must appear as f = 1/(4βRC).
  • Reading amplitude as peak-to-peak: the triangle swings ±βVsat, so its peak-to-peak value is 2βVsat. Answer whichever the question asks.
  • Expecting the supply to set the frequency: Vsat cancels out. Changing the supply moves the amplitude and the slope, not f (to first order).
  • Assuming every duty control keeps f fixed: diode steering changes f as duty changes, while a potentiometer across a fixed total resistance keeps f nearly constant. State which one the circuit uses.

7.5 Astable, monostable and bistable multivibrators

A multivibrator is a two-state circuit built from two cross-coupled transistors or two cross-coupled logic gates. Positive feedback forces each side to oppose the other, so one side is on while the other is off. How the circuit moves between these states defines the three types: astable has no stable state and runs freely, monostable has one stable state and gives one timed pulse, bistable has two stable states and remembers the last input. These circuits make clocks, one-shot delays and latches in timers, counters and power-on reset circuits.

You need: the BJT as a switch and capacitor charging through a resistor (Unit 2), and positive feedback (Unit 4); the comparator with hysteresis is in Chapter 7.4.

Two cross-coupled sides make two states

Build the circuit from two inverting stages and connect the output of each to the input of the other. Each stage is an NPN transistor with a collector resistor, or a logic inverter. The loop has positive feedback: if one side starts to conduct more, its collector voltage falls, it drives the other side off, and the other collector voltage rises, which drives the first side on harder. The loop settles with Q1 ON and Q2 OFF, or Q1 OFF and Q2 ON. Both are stable.

For BJTs, Q1 ON means its collector is near 0.2 V and Q2 base is pulled low through the coupling resistor, so Q2 is OFF. Q2 collector is near VCC and feeds Q1 base through the other resistor, so Q1 stays ON. The second state is the mirror image. A trigger or a capacitor can force a base past about 0.7 V or below 0 V, and the loop then jumps to the other state. The three multivibrators differ only in how many stable states the loop is allowed to keep.

Astable: no stable state, free-running

An astable has no stable state. Two capacitors cross-couple the collectors to the opposite bases, so each side can hold the other off only for a limited time.

  1. Start with Q1 ON and Q2 OFF. The capacitor at Q2 base is charged with a polarity that holds Q2 base below 0 V, keeping Q2 OFF.
  2. That capacitor charges through RB2 toward VCC. When Q2 base reaches about 0 V, Q2 turns ON, its collector falls, and the step is coupled through the other capacitor to Q1 base, turning Q1 OFF.
  3. Now the capacitor at Q1 base charges through RB1. When Q1 base reaches about 0 V, Q1 turns ON and the roles swap again.
T = 0.69(R<sub>B1</sub>C<sub>1</sub> + R<sub>B2</sub>C<sub>2</sub>)

T is the full period in seconds; RB1 and RB2 are the base resistors in ohms; C1 and C2 are the coupling capacitors in farads; 0.69 is ln 2 from the capacitor charging from about −VCC to 0 V.

Each half period is 0.69RC. With RB1=RB2=R and C1=C2=C, T=1.38RC and f=1/T≈0.72/(RC). This is the discrete transistor form of the square-wave generator in Chapter 7.4.

Monostable, bistable and the emitter-coupled form

A monostable keeps one stable state. A trigger edge turns the OFF transistor ON and the ON transistor OFF. One coupling capacitor now charges through its base resistor, and when its base reaches about 0 V the loop falls back to the stable state. The output is one pulse of width T=0.69RC, where R is the base resistor and C the coupling capacitor. The trigger must be shorter than T; a long trigger holds the circuit in the quasi-stable state, so the pulse lasts as long as the trigger, not 0.69RC. After the pulse, the capacitor must recharge through R before the next trigger.

A bistable removes the timing capacitors and keeps both cross-couplings direct. It has two stable states and no natural return. A pulse at SET forces Q1 ON, Q2 OFF; a pulse at RESET forces the opposite. The state remains after the input is removed, so the circuit is a one-bit memory or latch. Cross-coupled NOR gates give an active-high SET/RESET latch; cross-coupled NAND gates give active-low inputs. A single-input bistable with different up and down thresholds is a Schmitt trigger, covered in Unit 6.

The emitter-coupled monostable shares one emitter resistor. Its cross-coupling capacitor charges through that resistor and the base resistor, and the pulse width is again close to 0.69RC. It gives a cleaner pulse and better supply rejection. Timer IC versions of all three are in Chapter 7.6.

Worked example

Question. A collector-coupled astable multivibrator uses C1 = C2 = 10 nF and R_B1 = R_B2 = 47 kΩ. (a) Find the period and frequency. (b) By switching one base resistor to a trigger input, the same loop is used as a monostable. Find its output pulse width.

  1. Write the astable period: T = 0.69(R_B1C1 + R_B2C2).
  2. Compute each RC product: R_B1C1 = 47 × 10³ Ω × 10 × 10⁻⁹ F = 4.7 × 10⁻⁴ s; R_B2C2 is the same.
  3. Sum the two products: 4.7 × 10⁻⁴ s + 4.7 × 10⁻⁴ s = 9.4 × 10⁻⁴ s. Multiply by 0.69: T = 6.486 × 10⁻⁴ s = 0.6486 ms ≈ 0.65 ms.
  4. Frequency: f = 1/T = 1/(6.486 × 10⁻⁴ s) = 1541.8 Hz ≈ 1.54 kHz.
  5. For the monostable use one RC pair: T_p = 0.69 R_B C = 0.69 × 47 × 10³ Ω × 10 × 10⁻⁹ F = 3.243 × 10⁻⁴ s = 0.3243 ms ≈ 0.32 ms.

Answer. The astable period is 0.65 ms, its frequency is 1.54 kHz, and the monostable pulse width is 0.32 ms.

Exam traps

  • Using 0.69RC as the full astable period. It is one half period; the full period is 0.69(R_B1C1 + R_B2C2).
  • Forgetting that a monostable trigger must be shorter than the output pulse. A long trigger holds the quasi-stable state and the pulse is not set by RC alone.
  • Pairing the wrong resistor and capacitor in an astable. Each half period uses the base resistor and coupling capacitor of the transistor that is OFF.
  • Treating the bistable as a timed circuit. It has no timing capacitor and does not return by itself; it changes only when SET or RESET is pulsed.
  • Assuming the astable frequency is exactly independent of supply voltage. The 0.69RC result assumes switching near 0 V and V_CC much larger than the base-emitter drop.

7.6 555 timer IC and its applications

The 555 is an eight-pin IC that turns two resistors and one capacitor into a timer. Three 5 kΩ resistors set 2/3 V_CC and 1/3 V_CC; two comparators check a capacitor against them; a latch holds the result. It works as an astable oscillator, a monostable one-shot, a PWM generator and a missing-pulse alarm. It costs a few rupees and outputs about 200 mA.

You need multivibrators from Chapter 7.5 and comparators from Unit 6, Op-Amp Linear and Nonlinear Applications.

Inside the chip: three resistors, two comparators, one latch

Pin 1 is ground; pin 8 is V_CC (4.5 V to 16 V for the bipolar NE555). Pin 2 is TRIGGER, pin 6 is THRESHOLD, pin 7 is DISCHARGE, pin 3 is OUTPUT, pin 4 is RESET and pin 5 is CONTROL VOLTAGE.

Three identical 5 kΩ resistors from V_CC to ground fix 2/3 V_CC and 1/3 V_CC. The name 555 comes from them.

The upper comparator compares pin 6 with 2/3 V_CC and resets the latch above that level. The lower comparator compares pin 2 with 1/3 V_CC and sets the latch below that level.

The latch is an RS flip-flop. Its Q output drives an inverter and push-pull pair — sourcing or sinking about 200 mA for an LED or small relay — and the discharge transistor Q1, whose collector is pin 7. When Q is high, Q1 is on and pin 7 is grounded.

Pin 4 is active-low reset: tie it to V_CC, or pull it low to force the output low. Pin 5 exposes the 2/3 V_CC node; a voltage there moves the upper threshold, so a 10 nF capacitor from pin 5 to ground keeps noise off it.

Astable mode: charge through R_A + R_B, discharge through R_B

Connect R_A from V_CC to pin 7, R_B from pin 7 to pins 2 and 6 joined, and C from those pins to ground. If C starts below 1/3 V_CC, the lower comparator sets the latch: output high, Q1 off, C charges through R_A and R_B. At 2/3 V_CC the upper comparator resets the latch: output low, Q1 on, C discharges through R_B only into pin 7. At 1/3 V_CC the cycle restarts. The paths differ, so the half-periods differ.

T_H = 0.693 (R_A + R_B) C

T_H is output high time in seconds; R_A and R_B in ohms; C in farads; 0.693 = ln 2, the factor for charging from 1/3 V_CC to 2/3 V_CC.

T_L = 0.693 R_B C

T_L is output low time in seconds. Only R_B carries discharge current.

f = 1.44 / ((R_A + 2 R_B) C)   and   D = (R_A + R_B) / (R_A + 2 R_B)

f is in hertz; 1.44 ≈ 1/0.693; D is duty cycle, the fraction of the period high. R_A is in the charge path only, so T_H > T_L and D > 50%.

For D near 50%, connect a diode across R_B with anode at pin 7 and cathode at the pins 2/6 node. It bypasses R_B while C charges, so T_H ≈ 0.693 R_A C, while discharge still uses R_B, so T_L ≈ 0.693 R_B C. With R_A = R_B, D ≈ R_A/(R_A + R_B) = 50%.

Pick C first, from 1 nF to 10 µF, then solve for R_A and R_B. Keep R_B below about 1 MΩ; comparator bias current and capacitor leakage spoil long timings.

Monostable mode and the applications that follow

Connect R from V_CC to pin 7 and also to pin 6, C from pin 6 to ground, and leave pin 2 separate. A negative pulse on pin 2 below 1/3 V_CC sets the latch: output high, Q1 off, C charges through R. At 2/3 V_CC the latch resets and the output falls.

T = 1.1 R C

T is output pulse width in seconds; R in ohms; C in farads; 1.1 = ln 3, for charging from 0 V to 2/3 V_CC.

If the trigger stays below 1/3 V_CC longer than T, the lower comparator keeps setting the latch: the output goes high again immediately and repeats, so it keeps pulsing until the trigger is released. Keep the trigger shorter than T. Pin 4 stays at V_CC.

  • LED flasher: astable with R_A = 1 kΩ, R_B = 68 kΩ, C = 10 µF and an LED plus 470 Ω on pin 3 gives about a 1 Hz blink.
  • Tone generator: astable at audio frequency; pin 3 through a 100 µF or larger capacitor into an 8 Ω speaker, with a 100 Ω series resistor to limit pin 3 current.
  • Missing-pulse detector: each pulse triggers a monostable, so output stays high while pulses arrive; a missing pulse lets output fall low after T and drives the alarm. A plain 555 does not retrigger during T, so set T slightly longer than one pulse period and use a retriggerable arrangement, such as an extra discharge transistor. Useful in a UPI payment terminal watching a heartbeat line from the host.
  • PWM: a slowly varying voltage on pin 5 moves the upper threshold, changing T_H and the duty cycle while frequency stays roughly constant. This dims an LED or sets DC motor speed.

Tie pin 4 to V_CC directly or through 10 kΩ. An open pin 4 is bad practice on the bipolar NE555 — it usually runs — and unreliable on CMOS versions such as the 7555, where noise on the floating high-impedance input resets the timer at random and raises supply current.

Worked example

  6. Check: f = 1.44/((10 kΩ + 94 kΩ) × 10 nF) = 1385 Hz, within rounding.

7.7 Phase-locked loop and voltage-controlled oscillator

A phase-locked loop (PLL) makes an oscillator follow the frequency of an input signal. It compares the phase of a voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) with the incoming signal and adjusts the oscillator until the two match. The loop is used to demodulate FM, to multiply a frequency by an integer, and to recover a clock from serial data. This chapter explains the four blocks, the VCO formula f_out = f_0 + K_vco V_c, and the capture and lock ranges.

You need: Chapter 7.6 for a 555-based VCO, and Unit 4 for oscillator feedback and free-running frequency.

Four blocks in a closed loop

The input signal and the VCO output both enter the phase detector. The detector produces an error voltage proportional to their phase difference: V_e = K_pd (θ_in − θ_out). K_pd is the detector gain in volts per radian; θ_in and θ_out are the phases in radians.

The loop filter is a low-pass filter. It removes the sum-frequency term from the detector and passes the slowly varying error. It sets the loop's speed and stability. Its output is the control voltage V_c.

The VCO is an oscillator whose frequency is set by V_c. The feedback path returns the VCO output to the detector. If the VCO is slower than the input, the phase error grows in one direction; the detector and filter push V_c until the VCO speeds up.

  1. Compare input and VCO phase; the detector gives an error voltage.
  2. Low-pass filter the error to get V_c.
  3. The VCO frequency shifts by K_vco V_c.
  4. Feedback corrects until the two frequencies match.

In lock, the VCO frequency equals the input frequency. The phase error settles to a constant value. In a simple first-order loop, a steady phase error is needed to hold a non-zero V_c; in an ideal integrator (type-II) loop, the filter can hold V_c with zero steady phase error. The steady offset in the first-order case is the static phase error.

VCO line and the two ranges

f_out = f_0 + K_vco V_c

f_out is the output frequency in Hz; f_0 is the free-running frequency in Hz when V_c = 0; K_vco is the VCO gain in Hz/V; V_c is the control voltage in V.

The relation is linear over a limited range. Outside it the VCO saturates and the loop cannot lock. For the 565 PLL the free-running frequency is set by one resistor and one capacitor: f_0 = 1.2 / (4 R_1 C_1). R_1 is in ohms, C_1 is in farads, and f_0 is in hertz. The 4046 is a CMOS PLL with an XOR phase detector and a VCO; its frequency is also set by external R and C.

The capture range is the band of input frequencies over which an unlocked loop can acquire lock. It is narrow and roughly set by the loop bandwidth, which depends on the loop filter and the loop gain. The lock range is the wider band over which a loop that is already locked keeps lock. So lock range > capture range. Lock time is the time taken to acquire lock.

Where the PLL is used

FM demodulation: the FM input drives the PLL. The VCO follows the instantaneous input frequency, and V_c is a copy of the modulating message.

Frequency multiplication: put a divider by N in the feedback path. The loop forces the divided output to equal the input, so f_out = N f_in. A 10 kHz reference and a divider by 8 give a 80 kHz output.

Clock recovery: serial data has transitions at the bit rate but no separate clock. The PLL locks to those transitions and its VCO output becomes the clock. The same idea times read channels in disks and receivers in digital links.

Worked example

Question. A 565 PLL has R_1 = 12 kΩ and C_1 = 2.5 nF. (a) Find its free-running frequency. (b) The VCO gain is 6 kHz/V. Find the output frequency for V_c = +0.5 V. (c) State whether the loop locks to a 13 kHz input that lies inside the capture range.

  1. Compute the time constant: 4 R_1 C_1 = 4 × 12 × 10³ × 2.5 × 10⁻⁹ = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴ s.
  2. f_0 = 1.2 / (1.2 × 10⁻⁴ s) = 10,000 Hz = 10 kHz.
  3. Frequency shift: Δf = K_vco V_c = 6 kHz/V × 0.5 V = 3 kHz.
  4. Output frequency: f_out = 10 kHz + 3 kHz = 13 kHz.
  5. The 13 kHz input is inside the capture range, so the loop acquires lock and holds it with V_c ≈ 0.5 V.

Answer. f_0 = 10,000 Hz (10 kHz); f_out = 13,000 Hz (13 kHz); the loop locks to the 13 kHz input because it lies inside the capture range.

Exam traps

  • Swapping capture range and lock range: capture is the narrower band where an unlocked loop can acquire lock; lock is the wider band where it holds lock.
  • Expecting lock on an input far outside both ranges: the VCO cannot reach that frequency with the available control voltage.
  • Mixing units: K_vco in kHz/V times V_c in volts gives kHz; add that to f_0 in kHz, not to f_0 in Hz.
  • Thinking the loop filter is just any low-pass: its cut-off sets capture range, lock time and stability.
  • Confusing the free-running frequency f_0 with the input frequency: f_0 applies when V_c = 0.

7.8 DACs: weighted resistor and R-2R ladder

A DAC turns an n-bit digital word into a voltage by adding binary-weighted currents at a virtual ground. The weighted-resistor network gives every bit its own resistor, which is easy to understand but needs a resistor spread of 2^(n−1) to 1, so it stops at about 4 bits. The R-2R ladder reaches 10 to 12 bits with only two resistor values, which is why it sits inside almost every DAC chip and inside every successive-approximation ADC (Chapter 7.9).

You need the inverting summing amplifier and virtual ground from Unit 6, Op-Amp Linear and Nonlinear Applications, and the offset and settling limits of real op-amps from Unit 5, Differential Amplifiers and Op-Amp Internals.

Weighted-resistor DAC: one resistor per bit

A DAC takes an n-bit word and gives a voltage proportional to its value. Write the bits b_(n−1) down to b_0, where b_(n−1) is the most significant bit (MSB) and b_0 the least significant bit (LSB). Each bit drives a switch: bit = 1 connects its resistor to V_ref, bit = 0 connects it to ground. All the resistors meet at the inverting input of an op-amp, which is a virtual ground because the non-inverting input is at 0 V. Each branch then carries a current V_ref/R_branch that does not depend on the other branches, and the op-amp adds them.

V_out = −V_ref (R_f/R) × (b_(n−1) + b_(n−2)/2 + ⋯ + b_0/2^(n−1))

V_ref is the reference voltage in volts; R_f is the op-amp feedback resistor in ohms; R is the resistor of the MSB branch in ohms; b_i is the bit value, 0 or 1. The next branch uses 2R, the next 4R, and the LSB branch 2^(n−1)R.

The network is simple, but the resistors must be graded. The MSB branch carries V_ref/R and the LSB branch V_ref/(2^(n−1)R), a spread of 2^(n−1) to 1. The MSB resistor must also hold its value to one part in 2^n, otherwise its current alone is wrong by a whole LSB. On a chip, resistors match well only when they are identical in shape and size, so a graded set cannot be kept accurate. Weighted-resistor DACs use trimmed thin-film resistors and are impractical beyond about 4 bits.

R-2R ladder: two values, any number of bits

The ladder uses only R and 2R. A chain of series R runs from a 2R termination at the far end to the op-amp's virtual ground. At every node of the chain a 2R resistor is switched to V_ref or to ground by one bit. The bit currents add at the virtual ground and the feedback resistor R_f sets the output voltage.

The ladder works because the resistance seen looking toward the termination from any node is exactly 2R. At the last node, the 2R termination in parallel with that node's switched 2R gives R; adding the series R gives 2R again. The same step repeats node by node toward the op-amp. So the current arriving at a node splits into two equal halves, and the bit currents fall by a factor 2 at every step. Only two resistor shapes exist, repeated, so matching depends on geometry and not on absolute value.

I_i = V_ref/(2^(n−i) R)    and    V_out = −V_ref × (R_f/R) × D/2^n

I_i is the current that the bit in position i (i = 0 is the LSB) sends into the summing node when it is 1, in amperes; D = b_(n−1)2^(n−1) + ⋯ + b_0 is the decimal value of the input word; the rest of the symbols are as before. With R_f = R the output is simply V_out = −V_ref × D/2^n.

An n-bit ladder needs 2n resistors against n for the weighted network, and its settling time is set by the RC of the ladder and the switch resistances. A typical CMOS ladder settles in tens of nanoseconds and holds 10 to 12 bits.

Resolution is the number of input bits n. It fixes the step size, not the accuracy: a badly matched 12-bit part can be less accurate than a good 10-bit one.

Worked example

Question. An 8-bit R-2R ladder DAC has V_ref = 5 V and R_f = R. The input word is 10110010. Find the decimal value D, the LSB size, the output voltage, and the largest output magnitude the DAC can produce.

  1. Write the bit weights for n = 8: b7 = 2^7 = 128, b6 = 64, b5 = 32, b4 = 16, b3 = 8, b2 = 4, b1 = 2, b0 = 1.
  2. Read the word 1 0 1 1 0 0 1 0 from MSB to LSB. The bits that are 1 are b7, b5, b4 and b1.
  3. D = 128 + 32 + 16 + 2 = 178.
  4. One LSB = V_ref/2^n = 5 V/256 = 19.53 mV.
  5. V_out = −V_ref × D/2^n = −5 V × 178/256 = −890/256 V = −3.4766 V.
  6. The largest output comes from the all-ones word: −5 V × 255/256 = −4.9805 V, which is one LSB below V_ref in magnitude.
  7. The sign is negative because the ladder feeds the virtual ground of an inverting summer. Add a unity-gain inverting stage if a positive output is needed.

Answer. D = 178, LSB = 19.53 mV, V_out = −3.4766 V, and the largest output magnitude is 4.9805 V.

Exam traps

  • Assuming the maximum output equals V_ref. Full scale is (2^n − 1)/2^n × V_ref, one LSB lower, and its sign is negative.
  • Dropping the minus sign. The ladder works into a virtual ground, so the op-amp inverts; a positive output needs a second inverting stage.
  • Confusing resolution with accuracy. Eight bits means 19.53 mV steps with V_ref = 5 V, but resistor mismatch, offset and gain error decide whether those steps are all present and in order.
  • Reading the word the wrong way round. 10110010 is 178 only when the leftmost bit is the MSB; read the other way it is 77.
  • Mixing the two denominators. The step comes from 2^n; the largest code is 2^n − 1.

7.9 ADCs: flash, successive approximation, dual-slope, sigma-delta

An analog-to-digital converter turns a voltage into a binary code. Four circuits do this in different ways: flash compares the input with every tap of a resistor string at once; successive approximation searches one bit at a time; dual-slope measures a time ratio; sigma-delta oversamples and filters. The choice is a trade of speed, resolution and accuracy. ADCs sit in digital meters, phone charger controllers, ECG front ends and motor drives. This chapter shows how each architecture works and how to compute its code and timing.

You need: Chapter 7.8 for the DAC inside the SAR and dual-slope converters, and Unit 6 for the comparator and sample-and-hold.

What an ADC must do

An ADC samples the input, holds it, and converts it to an n-bit code. The sampling rate f_s is how many conversions per second. The conversion time T_conv is the time from the sample instant to the valid code; it sets the maximum f_s. Aperture time is the small window in which the sample-and-hold must keep the input still. A changing input during that window adds an error.

Q = V_FS / 2ⁿ

Q is the step size in volts; V_FS is the full-scale input range in volts; n is the number of bits.

The code is D = round(V_in/Q), clipped to 0 … 2ⁿ − 1. The quantisation error is at most ±Q/2 if the input is centred. It is not the same as accuracy. Resolution is the step size or bit count; accuracy is how close the code is to the true value after offset, gain and linearity errors. A 12-bit ADC can be less accurate than a well-trimmed 8-bit ADC.

Four families trade these terms against one another. Flash uses many comparators, SAR uses one comparator and a DAC, dual-slope uses an integrator and a counter, and sigma-delta uses oversampling and a digital filter. The figure compares comparator count, speed and typical resolution.

Flash and successive approximation

Flash (simultaneous) ADC has a resistor string of 2ⁿ equal resistors between V_ref and ground. It gives 2ⁿ − 1 tap voltages. Each tap drives one comparator; the other input is V_in. The comparator outputs form a thermometer code: all comparators below V_in are 1, all above are 0. A priority encoder turns that into an n-bit binary code.

Conversion takes one clock, so flash is the fastest. It needs 2ⁿ − 1 comparators: 255 for 8 bits, 1023 for 10 bits. The input drives all comparators, so input capacitance and kickback are high. Flash is used for low resolution and high speed, such as video and radar front ends.

Successive approximation (SAR) uses one comparator, a sample-and-hold, and an internal n-bit DAC. It performs a binary search. First it sets the MSB and compares V_in with V_ref/2. If V_in is larger it keeps the bit; otherwise it clears it. Then it tests the next bit, and so on. Each clock decides one bit, so an n-bit conversion takes n clocks plus a little overhead.

T_conv = n T_clk

T_conv is the conversion time in seconds; n is the bit count; T_clk is the clock period in seconds.

A 10-bit SAR at 1 MHz takes about 10 µs, which is 100 kSa/s. SAR is the workhorse for 8 to 16 bits at moderate speed: data loggers, motor control, battery monitors. It is not slow; it is just slower than flash.

Dual-slope and sigma-delta

Dual-slope ADC integrates first, then counts back. In run-up, the integrator input is V_in for a fixed time T1 = 2ⁿ T_clk. The counter runs through 2ⁿ clocks. The integrator output at the end is V_o = −V_in T1/(RC). In de-integration, the input switches to −V_ref. The integrator returns to zero. The time T2 satisfies V_ref T2/(RC) = V_in T1/(RC).

T2 = T1 V_in / V_ref

T2 is the de-integration time in seconds; T1 is the fixed run-up time in seconds; V_in is the input voltage in volts; V_ref is the reference voltage in volts.

The counter counts T2/T_clk. If V_in = V_ref, T2 = T1 and the count is 2ⁿ. The code is D = round(2ⁿ V_in/V_ref), clipped to 2ⁿ − 1. The same clock and capacitor appear in both phases, so slow drift in clock frequency or RC cancels in the ratio. That makes dual-slope accurate and immune to component drift.

Dual-slope is slow: a 10-bit conversion needs up to 2 × 2ⁿ clocks, and it rejects mains hum if T1 is a multiple of 20 ms. It is used in panel meters, digital multimeters and weigh scales.

Sigma-delta (Σ-Δ) ADC oversamples far above the Nyquist rate, uses a 1-bit comparator inside a feedback loop, and shapes quantisation noise to high frequency. A digital decimation filter removes the out-of-band noise and produces a high-resolution code. Resolution of 16 to 24 bits is common at low speed: audio, strain gauges, temperature and energy metering. Only a few universities teach the loop analysis; the idea is oversampling plus noise shaping plus digital filtering.

Worked example

Question. A 10-bit dual-slope ADC has V_ref = 2 V and a 100 kHz clock. (a) Find T_clk. (b) Find the run-up time T1. (c) For V_in = 1.5 V find the de-integration time T2. (d) Find the output code.

  1. T_clk = 1/f_clk = 1/(100 kHz) = 10 µs.
  2. T1 = 2ⁿ T_clk = 2¹⁰ × 10 µs = 1024 × 10 µs = 10 240 µs = 10.24 ms.
  3. V_in/V_ref = 1.5 V / 2 V = 0.75.
  4. T2 = T1 × V_in/V_ref = 10.24 ms × 0.75 = 7.68 ms.
  5. Count = T2/T_clk = 7.68 ms / 10 µs = 768.
  6. The code is 768 out of 1024, so it fits in 10 bits.

Answer. The clock period is 10 µs, the run-up time is 10.24 ms, the de-integration time is 7.68 ms, and the output code is 768 out of 1024.

Exam traps

  • Counting 2ⁿ comparators in a flash ADC instead of 2ⁿ − 1; an 8-bit flash needs 255, not 256.
  • Calling an SAR ADC slow because it takes n clocks; it is fast, just not as fast as flash.
  • Inverting the dual-slope ratio, using V_ref/V_in instead of V_in/V_ref, which gives a code above full scale.
  • Mixing resolution with accuracy; a 12-bit converter can have larger error than a trimmed 8-bit part.
  • Forgetting that dual-slope cancels clock and capacitor drift only because the same clock and capacitor are used in both phases.

Takeaways

  1. An active filter uses an op-amp with resistors and capacitors to shape gain versus frequency without inductors.
  2. A Bode plot reads a filter by its passband gain, cut-off frequency and roll-off slope.
  3. Real filter specifications are met by choosing an approximation such as Butterworth or Chebyshev and then setting the required order.
  4. A second-order section has a Q value, and a Sallen-Key circuit realizes that section with one op-amp.
  5. A state-variable or universal filter gives low-pass, band-pass and high-pass outputs from the same circuit.
  6. A switched-capacitor filter replaces resistors with clocked capacitors, so its cut-off is tuned by a clock ratio.
  7. A comparator with hysteresis plus an integrator forms a square-triangle generator, and unequal charge paths make a sawtooth.
  8. Astable, monostable and bistable multivibrators differ by the number of stable states and by how they leave a state.
  9. The 555 timer uses a resistor divider, two comparators, a latch and a discharge transistor to set timing from two resistors and one capacitor.
  10. A PLL locks a VCO to an input, and DAC and ADC architectures trade speed, resolution, accuracy and cost.

Glossary

555 timer
Versatile timer IC used for astable and monostable timing, with comparators, latch and discharge transistor.
Active filter
Filter built with an op-amp plus resistors and capacitors, so it can provide gain and avoid inductors.
Astable multivibrator
Oscillator circuit with no stable state that continuously switches between two states.
Bistable multivibrator
Latch circuit with two stable states, set and reset by external signals.
Bode plot
Graph of magnitude or phase of a transfer function against frequency on a logarithmic frequency axis.
Butterworth filter
Filter approximation with a maximally flat passband and monotonic response.
Chebyshev filter
Filter approximation with ripple in the passband or stopband and a sharper transition than Butterworth.
Cut-off frequency
Frequency at which the filter gain falls to 1/√2 of the passband gain, about −3 dB.
DAC
Digital-to-analog converter that turns a binary code into a proportional analog voltage or current.
Dual-slope ADC
ADC that integrates the input for a fixed time and then integrates a reference until zero; accurate but slower.
Filter order
Number of poles in the filter transfer function; it sets the ultimate roll-off steepness.
Flash ADC
ADC that compares the input with many reference levels at once and encodes the result; very fast.
Monostable multivibrator
One-shot circuit with one stable state that produces a single pulse after a trigger.
Phase-locked loop
Control loop that locks a VCO to the phase and frequency of an input signal.
Q factor
Quality factor of a second-order section; it controls peaking near cut-off and damping.
R-2R ladder
DAC resistor network using only R and 2R values to create binary-weighted currents.
Roll-off
Rate at which gain falls beyond cut-off, usually stated in dB per decade or dB per octave.
Sallen-Key filter
Single-op-amp second-order active filter using an RC network and positive feedback.
Schmitt trigger
Comparator with hysteresis, giving two switching thresholds and clean output transitions.
Sigma-delta ADC
Oversampling ADC that uses noise shaping and digital filtering to obtain high resolution at low bandwidth.
State-variable filter
Active filter using integrators and a summer to provide low-pass, band-pass and high-pass outputs together.
Successive-approximation ADC
ADC that finds the output code bit by bit using a DAC, comparator and binary search.
Switched-capacitor filter
Active filter in which clocked capacitors replace resistors, making cut-off depend on the clock frequency.
Universal filter
Active filter configuration that can produce several standard responses from the same circuit.
Voltage-controlled oscillator
Oscillator whose frequency changes with an applied control voltage.
