2.1 Load line, operating point and bias stabilization

An amplifier works well only if its transistor sits at a steady DC current and voltage before any signal arrives. That steady point is chosen on the DC load line, a straight line drawn on the output characteristics from the supply voltage to the maximum possible collector current. Temperature and transistor gain drift, so the bias network must hold the point nearly fixed. Every audio stage, sensor front end and radio block begins with this choice, and a bad Q-point shows up as clipping, heat or a dead signal.

You need: BJT structure, regions of operation and output characteristics from the devices course; the DC supply and rectifier ideas behind V_CC from Unit 1.

The DC load line and the Q-point

Draw the output characteristics with V_CE on the horizontal axis and I_C on the vertical axis. For a fixed base current I_B the curve is almost flat. The DC load line comes from the collector loop. Kirchhoff's voltage law gives V_CC = I_C R_C + V_CE, so I_C = (V_CC − V_CE)/R_C. On the graph this is a straight line with slope −1/R_C. At V_CE = 0, I_C(sat) = V_CC/R_C. At I_C = 0, V_CE(cutoff) = V_CC. These intercepts fix the line. The supply sets the voltage end; the resistor sets the current end. Doubling R_C halves I_C(sat) and leaves V_CE(cutoff) unchanged. Raising V_CC moves both ends outward. The Q-point is where the load line cuts the output curve for the chosen I_B; some syllabi call it the quiescent point. In the active region the base-emitter junction is forward biased and the collector-base junction is reverse biased, with V_CE above about 0.3 V for a silicon BJT. At cutoff I_C is nearly zero and V_CE is near V_CC. At saturation V_CE is small and I_C is set by the external resistors, not by β. An amplifier must sit in the active region, away from both ends, so the signal has room to swing.

V_CE = V_CC − I_C R_C,  I_C(sat) = V_CC / R_C,  V_CE(cutoff) = V_CC

V_CC is the collector supply in volts; I_C is the collector current in amperes; R_C is the collector resistor in ohms; V_CE is the collector-emitter voltage in volts; I_C(sat) is the saturation current in amperes; V_CE(cutoff) is the cutoff voltage in volts.

Bias stabilization and thermal runaway

A bias circuit sets the Q-point and holds it. Transistor parameters move with temperature and from unit to unit. The reverse saturation current I_CO rises steeply with temperature. V_BE falls by about 2 mV for each 1 °C rise. β spreads widely even within one part number. If base current is fixed, a larger β gives a larger I_C and the Q-point slides toward saturation. Bias stabilization means choosing resistors so that I_C changes little when these parameters change. The stability factors measure sensitivity: S = ∂I_C/∂I_CO, S' = ∂I_C/∂V_BE and S'' = ∂I_C/∂β. A small value is good; S = 1 is the best possible for the first factor. Thermal runaway is the dangerous loop: I_C rises, the transistor dissipates more power, its temperature rises, I_CO rises, and I_C rises again. The collector resistor limits the current rise because a larger I_C drops more voltage across R_C and leaves less V_CE. Emitter resistance helps more by feeding back against the change; that is the idea behind the voltage-divider bias in the next chapter. A good design keeps V_CE near the middle of the load line at the expected temperature, not only at 25 °C on the bench.

The Early effect and output resistance

Real output curves are not perfectly flat. As V_CE rises, the reverse-biased collector-base junction widens and the effective base width shrinks. This is base-width modulation, or the Early effect. A narrower base means less recombination, so I_C rises slightly with V_CE even when I_B is fixed. The curves tilt upward. Extended backward, they meet the voltage axis at a negative value called the Early voltage V_A. The small-signal output resistance is r_o = ΔV_CE/ΔI_C at constant I_B, approximately r_o = V_A/I_C. Here V_A is in volts, I_C in amperes and r_o in ohms. A typical V_A is tens of volts, so r_o is tens of kilohms at 1 mA. The Early effect does not move the DC load line, but it makes the Q-point slightly dependent on V_CE and gives the transistor a finite output resistance. That resistance appears in the hybrid-π model in Chapter 2.4.

Worked example

Question. A silicon npn transistor is biased with V_CC = 12 V, R_C = 2 kΩ, R_B = 240 kΩ and β = 100. Take V_BE = 0.7 V and V_CE(sat) = 0.3 V. (a) Find I_B, I_C and V_CE. (b) Find the two ends of the DC load line and mark the Q-point. (c) State whether the transistor is in the active region.

  1. Base loop: V_CC = I_B R_B + V_BE. I_B = (12 − 0.7) V / 240 kΩ = 11.3 / 240000 A = 47.08 µA.
  2. Collector current: I_C = β I_B = 100 × 47.08 µA = 4708 µA = 4.71 mA.
  3. Collector loop: V_CE = V_CC − I_C R_C = 12 V − 4.71 mA × 2 kΩ = 12 − 9.42 = 2.58 V.
  4. Load line ends: I_C(sat) = 12 V / 2 kΩ = 6 mA; V_CE(cutoff) = 12 V. Q = (2.58 V, 4.71 mA).
  5. Active check: V_CE = 2.58 V > 0.3 V, so the transistor is active. The downward swing to saturation is only 2.58 − 0.3 = 2.28 V.

Answer. I_B = 47.08 µA, I_C = 4.71 mA, V_CE = 2.58 V; the load line runs from 6 mA to 12 V, and Q = (2.58 V, 4.71 mA) lies in the active region with little downward swing room.

Exam traps

  • Marking a Q-point active without checking V_CE against V_CE(sat). A transistor with V_CE = 0.2 V is saturated even if β I_B suggests a large current.
  • Using R_C in parallel with the load to draw the DC load line. The DC line uses only the DC collector resistor; the AC load line belongs to Unit 3.
  • Treating a smaller stability factor as worse. For S, S' and S'', smaller means the Q-point moves less.
  • Forgetting that β is not fixed. A design that works at β = 100 can saturate at β = 300.
  • Mixing kΩ and Ω. I_C(sat) is in milliamperes when V_CC is in volts and R_C is in kilohms.

2.2 BJT biasing: fixed, collector feedback and voltage divider

An amplifier needs a steady DC current and voltage before any signal arrives. A resistor network sets that point. Three standard networks are fixed bias, collector-to-base feedback bias and voltage divider bias. They differ in how far the collector current moves when β or temperature changes. Fixed bias moves most; the divider least. This chapter gives each circuit, its DC equations, stability factor and design steps.

You need the DC load line, the Q-point and the stability factor S from Chapter 2.1.

Fixed bias (base bias): one resistor, no feedback

RB runs from VCC to the base, RC from VCC to the collector, and the emitter goes to ground. IB is set by VCC and RB alone. Nothing senses IC, so nothing corrects it.

I<sub>B</sub> = (V<sub>CC</sub> − V<sub>BE</sub>)/R<sub>B</sub>,  I<sub>C</sub> = βI<sub>B</sub>,  V<sub>CE</sub> = V<sub>CC</sub> − I<sub>C</sub>R<sub>C</sub>

VCC is the supply in volts; VBE ≈ 0.7 V for a silicon transistor at room temperature; RB and RC are in ohms; IB and IC are in amperes. Germanium needs about 0.3 V; a Darlington pair about 1.4 V.

The stability factor is S = 1 + β. At β = 100, S = 101, the worst of any bias circuit: leakage is multiplied 101 times and doubling β doubles IC. β rises about 0.5% per °C; VBE falls about 2 mV per °C.

Use it for a hard-driven switch or the cheapest circuit, not a linear amplifier.

Collector feedback bias: the resistor watches the output

Return RB to the collector, not the supply. RC now carries IC + IB.

V<sub>CC</sub> = (I<sub>C</sub> + I<sub>B</sub>)R<sub>C</sub> + I<sub>B</sub>R<sub>B</sub> + V<sub>BE</sub>  →  I<sub>C</sub> = β(V<sub>CC</sub> − V<sub>BE</sub>)/(R<sub>B</sub> + (1+β)R<sub>C</sub>)

the first term is the drop across RC carrying both currents, the second is the drop across RB, and VBE ≈ 0.7 V. Solve with IC = βIB.

If IC rises, the RC drop rises, collector voltage falls, and less current enters the base through RB. That is negative feedback. The stability factor is S = (1+β)(RB + RC)/(RB + (1+β)RC). With RB = 560 kΩ, RC = 2.2 kΩ and β = 100, S ≈ 73.

RB is an output-to-input path, so it lowers input resistance and gain; see Chapter 2.4.

Voltage divider bias: fix the base, let R_E set the current

R1 from VCC to base, R2 from base to ground, RE from emitter to ground, RC from VCC to collector. RE carries IE, turning voltage into current and fighting change: if IC rises, VE rises, VBE falls, and the transistor conducts less. Some syllabi call this self-bias or emitter bias.

V<sub>B</sub> = V<sub>CC</sub>R<sub>2</sub>/(R<sub>1</sub> + R<sub>2</sub>),  V<sub>E</sub> = V<sub>B</sub> − V<sub>BE</sub>,  I<sub>E</sub> = V<sub>E</sub>/R<sub>E</sub>,  I<sub>C</sub> ≈ I<sub>E</sub>,  V<sub>CE</sub> = V<sub>CC</sub> − I<sub>C</sub>(R<sub>C</sub> + R<sub>E</sub>)

VCC is the supply in volts; R1, R2, RE, RC are in ohms; VBE ≈ 0.7 V; currents are in amperes. IC ≈ IE because α = β/(1+β) is within 1% of unity for β above 100.

The 10× rule: make the divider current through R2 at least ten times IB. Then the base current barely loads the divider and the VB formula is good enough for an exam answer.

  1. Choose VE about a tenth of VCC, so RE stabilises the point without eating the output swing.
  2. RE = VE/ICQ.
  3. VB = VE + 0.7 V; then R2 = VB/(10IB) and R1 = (VCC − VB)/(11IB).
  4. RC = (VCC − VCEQ − VE)/ICQ, with VCEQ near half of VCC.
  5. Check that VCE stays above about 1 V, so the transistor never saturates on signal peaks.

S = (1+β)(1 + Rth/RE)/(1+β + Rth/RE) with Rth = R1 ∥ R2. The worked circuit gives S ≈ 8.5, and S falls towards 1 as Rth becomes small against RE.

With CE across RE, the DC design is unchanged and the signal sees ground at the emitter. Remove CE and you have emitter-stabilized bias: same DC point, but gain drops to roughly RC/(RE + re). See Chapter 2.5.

Worked example

Question. A voltage divider bias circuit uses VCC = 12 V, R1 = 47 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ, RC = 2.2 kΩ, RE = 1 kΩ, β = 100 and VBE = 0.7 V. (a) Find the Q-point. (b) Check the 10× rule. (c) Find IC when β rises to 200 using the exact Thevenin solution, and compare with fixed bias.

  1. Neglect base current first. VB = 12 × 10/(47 + 10) = 2.105 V.
  2. VE = 2.105 − 0.700 = 1.405 V.
  3. IE = 1.405 V/1 kΩ = 1.405 mA, so IC ≈ 1.405 mA.
  4. VC = 12 − 1.405 mA × 2.2 kΩ = 12 − 3.09 = 8.91 V.
  5. VCE = 8.91 − 1.405 = 7.50 V. The transistor is active since VCE is well above 1 V.
  6. Divider current I2 = 2.105 V/10 kΩ = 210.5 µA. IB = 1.405 mA/100 = 14.05 µA. The ratio is 15, so the 10× rule holds.
  7. Exact check: VTH = 2.105 V and Rth = 47 kΩ ∥ 10 kΩ = 8.246 kΩ, so IB = (VTH − VBE)/(Rth + (1+β)RE). At β = 100: IB = 1.405/(8.246 k + 101 k) = 12.86 µA, IC = 1.286 mA. At β = 200: IB = 1.405/(8.246 k + 201 k) = 6.72 µA, IC = 1.343 mA. That is a 4% rise; fixed bias would double IC.

Exam traps

  • Using VB = VCCR2/(R1 + R2) when IB is comparable to divider current. Check the 10× rule, or solve the Thevenin circuit.
  • Thinking VB sets the current. VE = VB − 0.7 V drives RE, so 0.1 V error in VB is about 7% error in IC.
  • In collector feedback, writing IB = (VCC − 0.7)/RB and forgetting that RC carries IC + IB and that IC sets collector voltage.
  • Mixing kΩ with mA (they cancel and give volts) but pairing ohms with mA, which throws the answer off by 1000.
  • Calling a circuit with CE across RE 'emitter-stabilized' without saying for which quantity: the capacitor is open at DC and a short at signal frequency.

2.3 FET and MOSFET biasing circuits

A bipolar transistor is biased by a current; a field-effect transistor is biased by a voltage. The gate draws almost no DC current, so no resistor network can set a gate current — it must set a gate-source voltage V_GS, and the device's transfer characteristic turns that into a drain current I_D. Biasing a FET therefore means solving two equations together: the device curve (Shockley's equation or the square law) and the straight bias line drawn by the source and gate resistors. Every JFET or MOSFET amplifier starts here — a microphone preamplifier, an oscilloscope front end, the input stage of an op-amp.

You need: the load-line and Q-point idea from Chapter 2.1, and FET construction, the transfer characteristic and Shockley's equation from your devices course.

The gate voltage sets the current

A BJT's collector current is set by its base current. A FET has no such input current: at DC the gate oxide or the reverse-biased gate junction passes nanoamperes. The quantity the bias network must supply is a voltage, V_GS.

I_D = I_DSS(1 − V_GS/V_P)²

I_D is the drain current in mA; I_DSS is the saturation drain current measured with V_GS = 0; V_P is the pinch-off voltage, negative for an n-channel JFET; V_GS is the gate-source voltage in volts.

For an enhancement NMOS the square law replaces Shockley's equation.

I_D = K_n(V_GS − V_TN)²

K_n is the transconductance parameter in mA/V²; V_TN is the threshold voltage, positive for an n-channel enhancement device; V_GS must be greater than V_TN for any current to flow.

Each equation holds two unknowns, I_D and V_GS, so one equation cannot fix both. The second equation comes from the resistors. Any gate voltage V_G and source resistor R_S give a straight line on the transfer plot:

I_D = (V_G − V_GS)/R_S

V_G is the DC gate voltage in volts (0 V for self-bias); R_S is the source resistor in kilohm if I_D is in mA. This is the bias line, and where it cuts the device curve is the operating point — the same meeting of a line and a curve as in Chapter 2.1.

JFET: self-bias, fixed bias, gate divider

Self-bias is the standard JFET circuit. The gate goes to ground through a large R_G (1 MΩ is typical), so V_G = 0 and V_GS = −I_D R_S. The bias line I_D = −V_GS/R_S passes through the origin with slope −1/R_S. It also self-corrects: if I_D tries to rise, the drop across R_S grows, V_GS becomes more negative, and the JFET conducts less, so a drift in I_DSS is partly cancelled.

Fixed bias puts a separate battery V_GG in the gate lead. Then V_GS = −V_GG is fixed and I_D follows from Shockley's equation directly. It is easy to solve but needs a second supply and has no self-correction. Gate (voltage-divider) bias adds R1 and R2 from V_DD to ground; the gate current is zero, so the divider is unloaded and V_G = V_DD R2/(R1 + R2). The Q-point can then be placed anywhere between V_P and 0 V.

  1. Find V_G from the gate connection: 0 V for self-bias, −V_GG for fixed bias, V_DD R2/(R1 + R2) for divider bias.
  2. Write the bias line I_D = (V_G − V_GS)/R_S.
  3. Substitute it into Shockley's equation to get a quadratic in V_GS.
  4. Solve the quadratic and keep only the root with V_P ≤ V_GS ≤ 0 for an n-channel JFET.
  5. Get I_D from the bias line, then V_S = I_D R_S, V_D = V_DD − I_D R_D and V_DS = V_D − V_S.

Depletion and enhancement MOSFETs

The depletion MOSFET behaves like a JFET whose gate may also be driven positive, because the channel is already there at V_GS = 0. Shockley's equation still applies with a negative V_P for the n-channel type, so all three JFET circuits work unchanged. Divider bias is usual because it allows V_GS above zero, where the device delivers more current.

The enhancement MOSFET has no channel at V_GS = 0; it needs V_GS > V_TN with V_TN positive. Plain self-bias fails, since R_S produces only a negative V_GS and keeps the device off. The gate must be lifted by a divider, giving I_D = (V_G − V_GS)/R_S with V_G = V_DD R2/(R1 + R2). Equating this to K_n(V_GS − V_TN)² gives a quadratic; keep the root with V_GS > V_TN.

Drain-to-gate feedback bias needs no divider, only R_G from drain to gate. The gate current is zero, so V_G = V_D and, with the source grounded, V_GS = V_DD − I_D R_D. The bias line is I_D = (V_DD − V_GS)/R_D. It self-corrects: a rise in I_D lowers V_D, which lowers V_GS and pushes I_D back down. Two resistors set the whole Q-point.

Finally check the region. The device stays in saturation, where it acts as a controlled current source, only while V_DS > V_GS − V_P for a JFET, or V_DS > V_GS − V_TN for an enhancement MOSFET. If that fails, the bias line and the transfer curve no longer describe the circuit.

Worked example

Question. A JFET has I_DSS = 12 mA and V_P = −6 V. It is self-biased with R_S = 1 kΩ and R_D = 2 kΩ from V_DD = 20 V. Find V_GS, I_D, V_D, V_S and V_DS, and state the region of operation.

  1. Self-bias means the gate is at 0 V, so V_GS = −I_D R_S, that is I_D = −V_GS/1 kΩ with I_D in mA and V_GS in volts.
  2. Shockley: I_D = 12 mA × (1 − V_GS/(−6 V))² = 12(1 + V_GS/6)² mA.
  3. Equate the two: 12(1 + V_GS/6)² = −V_GS. Expanding, 12 + 4V_GS + V_GS²/3 = −V_GS, so V_GS² + 15V_GS + 36 = 0.
  4. Roots: V_GS = (−15 ± √(225 − 144))/2 = (−15 ± 9)/2, giving −3 V or −12 V.
  5. With V_P = −6 V the valid range is −6 V ≤ V_GS ≤ 0. The root −12 V lies beyond pinch-off and is rejected, so V_GS = −3 V.
  6. I_D = −V_GS/R_S = 3 V/1 kΩ = 3 mA.
  7. V_S = I_D R_S = 3 mA × 1 kΩ = 3 V.
  8. V_D = V_DD − I_D R_D = 20 V − (3 mA × 2 kΩ) = 20 − 6 = 14 V.
  9. V_DS = V_D − V_S = 14 V − 3 V = 11 V.
  10. Saturation check: V_GS − V_P = −3 V + 6 V = 3 V, and V_DS = 11 V > 3 V, so the JFET is in saturation.

Answer. V_GS = −3 V, I_D = 3 mA, V_S = 3 V, V_D = 14 V, V_DS = 11 V, and the JFET is in saturation.

Exam traps

  • Writing V_GS = +I_D R_S in self-bias. The gate is at ground and the source sits above it, so the gate is negative with respect to the source: V_GS = −I_D R_S.
  • Keeping both roots of the quadratic. The second root usually lies outside V_P ≤ V_GS ≤ 0, or below V_TN, and must be rejected.
  • Forgetting that an enhancement NMOS needs V_GS > V_TN with V_TN positive; a source resistor alone gives a negative V_GS and never turns it on.
  • Treating R1 and R2 as if they carried gate current. The gate current is zero, so they form an unloaded divider and R_G may be 1 MΩ or more.
  • Skipping the saturation check, or inserting V_P as a positive number in V_DS > V_GS − V_P.

Bias line I_D = −V_GS/1 kΩ. Shockley: 8(1 + V_GS/4)² = −V_GS, which gives V_GS² + 10V_GS + 16 = 0, roots −2 V and −8 V. Reject −8 V (beyond V_P = −4 V). So V_GS = −2 V and I_D = 2 mA.

Apply An enhancement NMOS has K_n = 0.5 mA/V² and V_TN = 2 V. Its gate divider gives V_G = 6 V and R_S = 1 kΩ. Find V_GS and I_D. 5 marks

0.5(V_GS − 2)² = (6 − V_GS)/1, so V_GS² − 2V_GS − 8 = 0, roots 4 V and −2 V. Reject −2 V (below V_TN). V_GS = 4 V, I_D = (6 − 4)/1 kΩ = 2 mA, which the square law confirms: 0.5(4 − 2)² = 2 mA.

Analyze In drain-to-gate feedback bias, why does the circuit settle at one Q-point even when I_D drifts with temperature? 4 marks

V_GS = V_DD − I_D R_D, so a rise in I_D lowers V_D and hence V_GS, reducing the current; a fall does the opposite. The feedback is negative and holds the operating point near the curve intersection.

Analyze A student solves a self-bias quadratic for V_P = −6 V and gets V_GS = −9 V and I_D = 9 mA. What is wrong? 3 marks

V_GS = −9 V is more negative than V_P = −6 V, where Shockley's equation gives zero current, not 9 mA. The root is physically meaningless and must be discarded; the other root is the operating point.

Understand Write the bias line for gate divider bias and define every term. 2 marks

I_D = (V_G − V_GS)/R_S, with V_G = V_DD R2/(R1 + R2) in volts, R_S the source resistor in kilohm, V_GS in volts and I_D in mA.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. On an integrated circuit a resistor-accurate Q-point is expensive, so bias is usually set by a MOSFET current mirror or a constant-gm (beta-multiplier) reference; see Unit 5, Differential Amplifiers and Op-Amp Internals. Drain-to-gate feedback reappears inside self-biased cascode stages and inverter-like gain cells, which need no divider at all. How V_TN and K_n drift with temperature decides whether a bias point stays put, which is why data sheets plot them. Useful searches: MOSFET current mirror, beta multiplier reference, constant-gm bias, self-biased cascode.

2.4 Small-signal models: h-parameter, hybrid-pi and T-model

Bias a transistor at a fixed Q-point, add a signal of a few millivolts, and over that small swing the device behaves as a linear two-port. Three equivalent pictures describe it. The h-parameter model is what you measure in the lab, the hybrid-π model is what device physics gives you, and the T-model puts the resistance in the emitter where the emitter follower and common-base stage are easiest to read. Every gain, input resistance and output resistance in this unit comes from one of these three, and you convert between them with simple arithmetic.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the Q-point and Chapter 2.2 for the DC currents that fix g_m, r_π and r_e.

The same device as a two-port

Bias the transistor and the currents settle at a Q-point. Add a signal of a few millivolts on top and, over that small swing, the curves are straight lines. The transistor is now a linear two-port: a base-emitter input port and a collector-emitter output port. All three models in this chapter describe that one two-port, and any one converts into the others.

v_be = h_ie·i_b + h_re·v_ce      i_c = h_fe·i_b + h_oe·v_ce

v_be and i_b are the signal voltage and current at the input port; v_ce and i_c are the same at the output port. Lowercase letters mean small-signal changes around the Q-point, never DC totals.

  • h_ie — input resistance measured with the output shorted (v_ce = 0), in ohms.
  • h_fe — forward current gain measured with the output shorted; this is the β of the datasheet.
  • h_re — reverse voltage feedback measured with the input open (i_b = 0); a small number, about 10⁻⁴.
  • h_oe — output admittance measured with the input open, in siemens; it equals 1/r_o.

The name hybrid is honest: the four numbers carry mixed units, ohms, a pure ratio, a pure ratio, siemens. The subscript tells you the test: the first letter is the common terminal (e for emitter), the second is the quantity (i input, f forward, r reverse, o output).

The full box needs a feedback voltage source h_re v_ce in the input loop and a current source h_fe i_b at the output. Hand analysis uses the CE approximate model instead: h_re is about 10⁻⁴ so it is dropped, and h_oe is dropped when R_L is far below r_o. What remains is h_ie in series with the base and one current source h_fe i_b at the collector.

The hybrid-π model

The hybrid-π model is built from device physics, so its elements follow the bias current instead of being re-measured at every Q-point. Some syllabi call it the Giacoletto model.

g_m = I_C / V_T

g_m is the transconductance in siemens; I_C is the DC collector current at the Q-point in amperes; V_T = kT/q where k is the Boltzmann constant (1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K), T is the absolute temperature in kelvin and q is the electron charge (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C); at T ≈ 300 K, V_T ≈ 26 mV.

r_π = β / g_m = β V_T / I_C

r_π is the base-emitter resistance of the model in ohms; β is the DC current gain; g_m and I_C are as above.

r_o = V_A / I_C

r_o is the output resistance in ohms; V_A is the Early voltage, typically 50 V to 150 V; I_C is in amperes.

At I_C = 1 mA and V_T = 26 mV, g_m = 38.5 mS. Every milliamp of collector current buys roughly 38.5 mS, so g_m is fixed by bias alone and β never enters it.

Three more elements sit in the full model. r_x is the base spreading resistance between the base terminal and the active region under the emitter, roughly 10 Ω to 300 Ω. C_π and C_µ are capacitances that do nothing at low frequency and take over at high frequency (see Unit 3, Frequency Response and Power Amplifiers).

The controlled source is g_m v_be, and v_be is the voltage across r_π. Once r_x is present, the base terminal voltage is not v_be; that difference matters only when the source resistance is very small. Exams normally set r_x = 0.

The T-model and the conversions

The T-model puts the resistance in the emitter leg. It is the natural picture for the emitter follower and the common-base stage (Chapter 2.5), and for any stage where the emitter is not at signal ground.

r_e = α / g_m ≈ V_T / I_E

r_e is the emitter resistance in ohms; α = β/(β+1) is the common-base current gain, just under 1; I_E is the DC emitter current, (β+1)I_B, nearly equal to I_C.

The source in the T-model is α i_e. With I_C = 1 mA, r_e = 0.99/38.5 mS = 26 Ω, the familiar number r_e ≈ 26 mV / I_E. The two models agree because the base and emitter resistances are the same resistance seen from different terminals:

r_π = (β + 1) r_e

r_π is the hybrid-π input resistance in ohms; β is the DC current gain; r_e is the T-model emitter resistance in ohms. Check: 101 × 26 Ω = 2.6 kΩ.

Worked example

Question. An npn transistor is biased at I_C = 1 mA with β = 100 and Early voltage V_A = 100 V. Take V_T = 26 mV. (a) Find g_m, r_π and r_o.   2. r_π = β / g_m = 100 / 38.46 mS = 2600 Ω = 2.6 kΩ.
  3. r_o = V_A / I_C = 100 V / 1 mA = 100 kΩ.
  4. R_L ∥ r_o = (5 kΩ × 100 kΩ) / (5 kΩ + 100 kΩ) = 500/105 kΩ = 4.762 kΩ.
  5. Stage voltage gain A_v = −g_m (R_L ∥ r_o) = −38.46 mS × 4.762 kΩ = −183.2. The minus sign says the CE stage inverts.

Exam traps

  • Mixing the models: quoting h_ie from a datasheet at one bias current and r_π from a different I_C, then using both in the same calculation.
  • Dropping the minus sign. The common-emitter stage inverts, so A_v = −g_m(R_L ∥ r_o); a positive answer means you lost it.
  • Using R_L alone when r_o is given. The gain formula needs R_L ∥ r_o, and the two differ by 5 % here, more when R_L is close to r_o.
  • Confusing α with β, and r_e with r_π. The bridge is r_π = (β + 1) r_e, not r_π = β r_e.
  • Treating 26 mV as a constant. V_T = kT/q rises with temperature, so g_m falls and r_e rises unless the bias current is raised in proportion to V_T (a PTAT bias). With I_C ∝ V_T, g_m = I_C/V_T and r_e = V_T/I_E stay fixed.

2.5 CE, CB and CC (emitter follower) amplifiers

One transistor becomes three amplifiers depending on which terminal is signal ground. CE: voltage gain, output inverted. CB: gain without inversion, very low input resistance. CC (emitter follower): gain just under one, tiny output resistance. Bias and Q-point are unchanged; only the signal ports move. One device covers audio preamps, RF inputs and buffers.

You need Chapter 2.4 for the small-signal models, and Chapter 2.2 for the DC operating point these formulas sit on.

One device, three signal ports

An amplifier has an input port, an output port and a common terminal. In a BJT the emitter, base and collector are the candidates. Ground one for signals — directly or through a capacitor that is a short at signal frequency — and the other two become input and output.

  • CE: emitter common; input at base, output at collector.
  • CB: base common; input at emitter, output at collector.
  • CC: collector common (AC ground through V_CC); input at base, output at emitter. This is the emitter follower.

The bias network is unchanged (Chapter 2.2), so the Q-point is the same. Coupling capacitors pass the signal and block DC; a bypass capacitor across R_E shorts it for signals, so R_E leaves the AC path.

r_e = V_T / I_E

r_e is the small-signal emitter resistance in ohms; V_T ≈ 26 mV is the thermal voltage at room temperature; I_E is the DC emitter current in A. At I_C ≈ I_E = 1 mA, r_e = 26 Ω.

Every derivation needs r_e, β and the resistors. Take r_o as infinite.

Common emitter: gain and 180° inversion

Signal enters the base through a coupling capacitor and leaves the collector. R1 and R2 set the base voltage, R_C is the collector resistor, and C_E bypasses R_E so the emitter is at AC ground.

  1. With the emitter AC-grounded, looking into the base the transistor shows β r_e: base current i_b flows through r_e carrying i_e ≈ β i_b.
  2. Collector current i_c = β i_b flows through R_C in parallel with the load R_L.
  3. Output v_o = −i_c (R_C ∥ R_L); input v_i = i_b β r_e.
  4. Divide: β cancels and the minus sign appears.

A_v = −(R_C ∥ R_L) / r_e

A_v is voltage gain (no units); R_C is the collector resistor and R_L the external load, both in ohms; r_e in ohms. The minus sign means 180° inversion: a rising input gives a falling collector.

R_in = R1 ∥ R2 ∥ β r_e , R_out ≈ R_C

R_in is resistance seen by the source at the base, R_out resistance seen looking back into the output with the source zeroed. Bias resistors appear in parallel with the transistor's own β r_e.

Without C_E, R_E stays in the signal path: A_v = −(R_C ∥ R_L)/(r_e + R_E) and R_in = R1 ∥ R2 ∥ β(r_e + R_E). Gain falls, input resistance rises, distortion falls — local feedback. CE has the highest voltage and current gain, so it is the usual first stage (Chapter 2.7).

Common base: no inversion, low input resistance

The base is held at AC ground by a bypass capacitor; the signal enters the emitter and leaves the collector through R_C.

Looking into the emitter you see r_e — about 26 Ω at 1 mA. Current gain is i_c/i_e = α = β/(β+1), below 1 (0.99 at β = 100). Voltage gain has the same size as CE but is positive.

A_v = +α (R_C ∥ R_L) / r_e ≈ (R_C ∥ R_L) / r_e ,  R_out ≈ R_C

A_v is positive, so there is no phase inversion. A rising emitter voltage lowers v_BE, so i_c falls, the drop across R_C falls, and the collector voltage rises.

CB is useful because its low input resistance matches a coaxial cable or antenna, and its input at the emitter avoids the Miller effect that limits CE at high frequency (Unit 3). It is also the upper transistor of a cascode (Chapter 2.8).

Common collector: the emitter follower

The collector is tied to V_CC, which is AC ground. Input at base, output at emitter across R_E ∥ R_L. The emitter follows the base, one v_BE below it.

A_v = (R_E ∥ R_L) / (r_e + R_E ∥ R_L)

A_v is just under 1 (0.987 at R_E = 2 kΩ, r_e = 26 Ω) and in phase with the input. The whole signal appears across the load except the small drop on r_e.

R_in = R1 ∥ R2 ∥ β (r_e + R_E ∥ R_L) ,  R_out ≈ r_e + (R1 ∥ R2 ∥ R_S) / β

R_S is the resistance of the source driving the base. A resistance at the emitter is multiplied by β when seen from the base; a resistance at the base is divided by β when seen from the emitter.

At I_C = 1 mA (r_e = 26 Ω), R1 = 47 kΩ, R2 = 10 kΩ and R_S = 1 kΩ: R1 ∥ R2 = 8.25 kΩ; with R_S in parallel, 892 Ω; divided by β = 100 gives 8.9 Ω; R_out ≈ 26 + 8.9 = 34.9 Ω. CE would be about 3 kΩ.

That is why the follower is a buffer: high R_in, low R_out, no voltage loss. It drives cables, small loudspeakers through a power stage, or a low-resistance next block. It has current gain, so it amplifies power. It appears inside op-amps (Unit 5) and class AB drivers (Unit 3).

2.6 CS, CG and CD (source follower) amplifiers

Replace the BJT base with a MOSFET gate and three signal-ground choices remain. Common source (CS) grounds the source, common gate (CG) grounds the gate, and common drain (CD) grounds the drain; CD is also called a source follower. The gate draws almost no current, so the BJT input-resistance problem disappears. CS gives voltage gain and inversion, CG gives low input resistance and current gain near one, and CD gives gain just below one with low output resistance. These stages appear in sensor front ends, radio-frequency input stages, and output buffers inside op-amps.

You need: Chapter 2.3 for the bias point and the square law, and Chapter 2.4 for the small-signal idea.

The MOSFET small-signal model

At the bias point the MOSFET sits in saturation. Write the square law as ID = Kn(VGS − VTN)². Here ID is the DC drain current in A, VGS is the DC gate-source voltage in V, VTN is the threshold voltage in V, and Kn is the device parameter in A/V². Some books put a factor ½ inside Kn; check the definition before using a formula.

g<sub>m</sub> = 2√(K<sub>n</sub> I<sub>D</sub>) = 2I<sub>D</sub>/(V<sub>GS</sub> − V<sub>TN</sub>)

gm is transconductance in A/V (also called siemens, S); Kn is the device parameter in A/V²; ID is the DC drain current in A; VGS is the DC gate-source voltage in V; VTN is the threshold voltage in V.

For small signals, let vgs be a small change in gate-source voltage and vds a small change in drain-source voltage. The drain current gets an AC part gmvgs plus a part vds/ro. The gate current is zero at low frequency, so the gate looks like an open circuit. This one fact separates MOSFET stages from BJT stages.

ro = 1/(λ ID)

ro is the small-signal output resistance in Ω; λ is the channel-length modulation parameter in V⁻¹; ID is the DC drain current in A.

The model is a dependent current source gmvgs from drain to source, in parallel with ro. Bias sets gm and ro; the model predicts gain, input resistance, and output resistance. In a long-channel device ro is large. In a short-channel device λ is larger, ro is smaller, and you cannot drop it.

Common-source stage

In a CS stage the input is at the gate, the source is at AC ground, and the output is at the drain through RD to VDD. RL stands for the next stage or load. Bias from Chapter 2.3 sets ID and VDS.

A<sub>v</sub> = −g<sub>m</sub>(R<sub>D</sub> ∥ R<sub>L</sub> ∥ r<sub>o</sub>)

Av is the small-signal voltage gain, unitless; gm is transconductance in S; RD is the drain bias resistor in Ω; RL is the load resistance in Ω; ro is the MOSFET output resistance in Ω.

The minus sign means inversion: a rising gate voltage increases drain current, so the drain voltage falls. Input resistance is very high because the gate draws no current; bias resistors set it in practice. Output resistance is RD ∥ ro. Keep ro when it is comparable to RD ∥ RL. Drop it only when ro is much larger.

The CS stage is the workhorse voltage amplifier. It is used after a sensor, after a filter, and inside op-amp gain stages. Its response is flat in the middle band and falls at high frequency because of MOSFET capacitances, covered in Unit 3. The figure normalizes both curves to 40 dB and compares the corner: a typical CS stage rolls off near 100 kHz, while a source follower can hold its response to about 10 MHz because its gain is below one and its output resistance is low.

Common-gate and common-drain stages

In a CG stage the input is at the source, the gate is at AC ground, and the output is at the drain. Input resistance is low, about 1/gm. The signal current entering the source leaves the drain almost unchanged, so current gain is near one. Voltage gain is positive, approximately +gm(RD ∥ RL ∥ ro), so the stage does not invert. A CG stage is useful as a low-noise input stage and as the input half of a cascode, see Chapter 2.8.

In a CD stage, also called a source follower, the input is at the gate, the drain is at AC ground, and the output is at the source across Rs. Use Rs ∥ RL when a load is present.

A<sub>v</sub> = g<sub>m</sub>R<sub>s</sub>/(1 + g<sub>m</sub>R<sub>s</sub>)

Av is the small-signal voltage gain, unitless and below 1; gm is transconductance in S; Rs is the source resistance in Ω, replaced by Rs ∥ RL when a load is connected.

The source follower does not invert, has a gain just below one, and has a low output resistance, about 1/gm ∥ Rs. It is used as a buffer: it takes a high-resistance signal and drives a low-resistance load without loading the source. The body is a fourth terminal. If the body is not tied to the source, VBS changes VTN, so bias and gain shift. Only a few universities teach body effect in depth; this chapter ignores it.

2.7 Multistage amplifiers: RC, direct and transformer coupling

One transistor stage gives gain in the tens. A microphone or sensor often needs hundreds or thousands. Chaining stages multiplies gain, and the coupling between stages decides what else changes: DC level, bandwidth and impedance. RC coupling uses a capacitor, transformer coupling uses a coil, and direct coupling uses a wire. The same choice appears in audio preamps, radio receivers and op-amp internal circuits.

You need: 2.5 and 2.6 for single-stage gain, input resistance and output resistance.

RC coupling: a capacitor between stages

A coupling capacitor CC sits between the collector of one stage and the base of the next. The capacitor blocks DC, so each stage keeps its own bias point from 2.2. For the AC signal, CC is almost a short at midband. The signal passes, and the voltage gain of the two stages multiplies.

  • Input resistance of the next stage and CC form a high-pass corner. Below it, gain falls.
  • Output resistance of the first stage and input resistance of the second form a divider. This divider lowers gain unless the second stage has high input resistance.
  • RC coupling is cheap, small and wideband. Audio preamps and low-frequency amplifiers use RC coupling.

Use one stage when gain is enough. Use a cascade when the required gain is higher than one stage can give with acceptable distortion.

Direct and transformer coupling

Direct coupling joins the collector of one stage to the base of the next with a wire. No capacitor, so DC flows. The DC collector voltage of stage 1 is not the base voltage stage 2 needs. A level shifter or a second supply can fix this. Temperature drift and offset also pass on.

Transformer coupling uses a coil. The primary is in the collector, the secondary goes to the base. No DC path between primary and secondary; each side is biased separately. The turns ratio N2/N1 changes impedance by (N2/N1)². It is bulky, costly and passes only a narrow band. RF and tuned amplifiers still use it.

  • Direct: used inside op-amps and ICs because large capacitors are hard to build on chip.
  • Transformer: used for impedance matching and isolation, not for DC.

Gain and bandwidth of a cascade

2.8 Cascode and Darlington connections

One transistor forces a trade. A common-emitter (CE) stage gives voltage gain, but its Miller capacitance limits bandwidth. An emitter follower gives high input resistance, but only one transistor's β. Two-transistor pairs remove both weaknesses. A common-base (CB) stage stacked on a CE stage shields the lower collector capacitance: the cascode, a wideband gain stage. Two emitter followers stacked multiply current gain: the Darlington pair, a high-resistance buffer. Both appear inside op-amps, regulators and power drivers.

You need Chapter 2.5 for CE, CB and CC gains, and Chapter 2.7 for direct coupling.

The cascode: a common-emitter feeding a common-base

A CE amplifier loses high-frequency gain because of the base-collector capacitance Cbc, a few picofarads. Base and collector swing in antiphase, so the voltage across Cbc is (1 + |Av|) times the input. The input then sees Miller capacitance CM = Cbc(1 + gmRL). Here gm is transconductance in siemens (A/V), and RL is the collector load in ohms. With source resistance Rs, this gives a pole at f = 1/(2πRsCM), which sets bandwidth. Remove the collector swing and you remove the problem.

The cascode uses a second transistor. Q1 is a CE stage. Its collector drives the emitter of Q2, whose base is held at fixed DC voltage, so Q2 works as a CB stage. The load RL is at Q2's collector. Q1's signal current ic1 = gm1vbe1 flows into Q2's emitter and out of its collector almost unchanged, because α ≈ 1.

A<sub>v</sub> = v<sub>o</sub> / v<sub>be1</sub> ≈ −g<sub>m1</sub>R<sub>L</sub>

gm1 is Q1's transconductance in A/V, RL is the load at Q2's collector in ohms, and the minus sign means the output is inverted.

Q1's collector looks into Q2's emitter, a low-resistance node of about 1/gm2, roughly 25 Ω at 1 mA. So Q1's collector swings by only vo1 ≈ −gm1vbe1/gm2 ≈ −vbe1. The Miller factor falls from (1 + gmRL) to about 2. Input capacitance falls from hundreds of picofarads to a few, and the input pole moves out by about the gain ratio.

The price is headroom: about 1 V extra between supply and output. The reward is very high output resistance. Looking into Q2's collector, Rout ≈ gm2ro2ro1 ≈ β2ro2. That is poor for driving a load and excellent for a current source, which is why folded-cascode op-amps use it.

The Darlington pair: two emitter followers

An emitter follower has Rin ≈ βRE. When that is not enough, feed Q1's emitter into Q2's base. Both collectors go to VCC; the signal enters Q1's base and leaves Q2's emitter. This is two common-collector stages in direct cascade: a Darlington pair.

β<sub>D</sub> ≈ β1β2   (exactly β1 + β2 + β1β2)

βD is the current gain of the pair, β1 of Q1, and β2 of Q2.

Q1's emitter current is Q2's base current, IE1 = IB2. So IE2 = (β2 + 1)(β1 + 1)IB1. A base current of 10 µA can control 100 mA. For small signals the pair acts as one transistor with current gain βD, input resistance Rin ≈ βDRE, and VBE(total) = VBE1 + VBE2 ≈ 1.4 V.

Q1 never saturates. Its collector-emitter voltage is only VCE1 = VBE2 ≈ 0.7 V, above VCE(sat) ≈ 0.2 V. The pair saturates at about VBE2 + VCE2(sat) ≈ 0.9 V. The 1.4 V offset is the cost: on a 3.3 V supply it eats almost half the swing.

Uses: buffering a high-resistance sensor into a low-resistance load, voltmeter inputs, and driver ICs such as the ULN2003 switching relays and stepper windings. A complementary Darlington, some syllabi call it the Sziklai pair, uses one PNP and one NPN, gives the same βD, and needs only about 0.7 V offset.

Exam traps

  • Writing βD = β1 + β2 instead of β1β2. Base currents add, emitter currents multiply.
  • Forgetting the Darlington's ≈ 1.4 V base-emitter drop. It eats headroom in a 3.3 V or 5 V supply and clips the output early.
  • Calling any two-transistor stack a cascode. The upper device must be common base; taking output from Q1's collector gives an ordinary CE stage with no bandwidth benefit.
  • Expecting the cascode to raise input resistance. It raises output resistance; input still sees rπ of Q1.
  • Sign slip on cascode gain: Av ≈ −gm1RL. The stage inverts like the CE stage it replaces.

Takeaways

  1. The DC load line fixes the set of allowed operating points; biasing means choosing one that stays put.
  2. A good bias network makes I_C depend on resistor values and V_BE, not on β.
  3. The voltage divider with an emitter resistor is preferred because its stability factor is the smallest of the simple circuits.
  4. A FET is biased by voltage, not current, so its Q-point is found graphically on the transfer characteristic.
  5. The h-parameter, hybrid-π and T models describe the same transistor and must give the same answers.
  6. CE gives voltage gain with inversion; CB gives current gain with no inversion; CC gives voltage gain near 1 and a low output resistance.
  7. Because the gate draws no current, a CS amplifier can have an input resistance in the megohms without any special design.
  8. Multistage coupling decides everything: RC coupling isolates DC, transformer coupling matches impedance, direct coupling passes DC.
  9. The cascode trades a second transistor for bandwidth; the Darlington trades two base-emitter drops for current gain.

A_v = (1+β)(R_E ∥ R_L)/[r_π + (1+β)(R_E ∥ R_L)]; R_E ∥ R_L = 0.5 kΩ, so (1+β)(0.5 kΩ) = 50.5 kΩ; A_v ≈ 50.5/52.5 ≈ 0.96; R_o = R_E ∥ [(R_s ∥ R_B + r_π)/(1+β)] ≈ 20–25 Ω; Conclude: no inversion, gain near unity, output resistance in tens of ohms.

Apply A MOSFET with k_n = 1 mA/V² and V_t = 1 V is biased with R_D = 2 kΩ, R_S = 1 kΩ and a gate divider giving V_G = 3 V, with V_DD = 10 V. Find I_D, V_DS and g_m. 12 marks

V_GS = V_G − I_D·R_S and I_D = k_n(V_GS − V_t)²; Solve the quadratic to get I_D ≈ 1 mA and V_GS = 2 V; V_DS = V_DD − I_D(R_D + R_S) = 7 V, so saturation holds; V_OV = V_GS − V_t = 1 V, g_m = 2I_D/V_OV = 2 mS; Check I_D against the data-sheet parameter if given.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare RC coupling, transformer coupling and direct coupling for multistage amplifiers on voltage gain, bandwidth, DC level shifting, size and cost. State one application for each. 12 marks

RC coupling: simple, cheap, wide band, poor low-frequency response, DC blocked; Transformer coupling: good impedance matching and efficiency, bulky and costly, poor high-frequency response; Direct coupling: passes DC, used in op-amp and audio IC stages, but Q-point drift propagates; Gain in dB adds: A_total = A1·A2·A3; Applications: RC in audio preamps, transformer in RF and power stages, direct in op-amps.

Analyze A three-stage RC-coupled amplifier has stage gains of 20, 15 and 10, with each stage having f_L = 100 Hz and f_H = 100 kHz. Find the overall gain in dB and discuss how the overall cut-off frequencies relate to those of the individual stages. 14 marks

A_total = 20 × 15 × 10 = 3000, that is 69.5 dB; Each stage contributes a pole; the overall lower cut-off moves higher than 100 Hz; The overall upper cut-off moves lower than 100 kHz; Bandwidth therefore shrinks as stages are added, roughly as 1/√(2^(1/n) − 1) times the single-stage value; State the gain-bandwidth trade-off.

Evaluate Explain the cascode amplifier. Derive its voltage gain and show why its bandwidth is far larger than that of a single CE stage with the same gain. 14 marks

Circuit: CE transistor with a CB transistor stacked above it; Gain ≈ −g_m1·R_L, the same as a CE stage, but the CB stage holds the CE collector nearly fixed; Miller capacitance is (1 + A_v)C_μ in a plain CE stage; in the cascode the first stage sees a gain near −1; Input pole therefore moves to a much higher frequency; Costs: extra supply headroom and a second bias branch.

Evaluate Explain the Darlington pair. Derive its current gain, input resistance and effective base-emitter voltage, and state the drawbacks. 12 marks

Emitter of Q1 drives base of Q2; I_C ≈ β1·β2·I_B1; R_i ≈ β1·β2·R_E, which is why it is used as a buffer; V_BE(total) ≈ 1.4 V, so the input must rise by two diode drops; Drawbacks: leakage current is also multiplied by β, slower turn-off, higher saturation voltage; Use: series regulator pass element, audio output stage, touch sensors.

Glossary

Biasing
Setting the DC currents and voltages of a device so that it sits in the active region before any signal arrives.
Cascode
CE stage stacked on a CB stage to reduce Miller effect and increase bandwidth.
Coupling capacitor
A capacitor between two stages that passes the signal but blocks DC, so each stage keeps its own Q-point.
Current mirror
A circuit that copies a reference current into another branch using matched transistors.
Cut-off
The state where the transistor carries almost no current because the input junction is not forward biased.
Darlington pair
Two cascaded emitter followers giving a current gain equal to the product of the two β values.
Early voltage
The extrapolated voltage V_A at which the BJT output curves meet the axis; it sets r_o = V_A/I_C.
Emitter bypass capacitor
A capacitor placed across R_E so that AC signal current bypasses the resistor while DC bias still flows through it.
Emitter follower
Common-collector amplifier whose output at the emitter follows the input with gain just below one.
h-parameter
One of four hybrid two-port numbers (h_ie, h_re, h_fe, h_oe) describing a transistor for small signals.
h_fe
Forward current gain in CE, equal to β; about 100 for a small-signal BJT.
Hybrid-π model
Small-signal BJT model built from r_π, a controlled current source g_m·v_be, r_o and the junction capacitances.
Input resistance
The resistance seen looking into the amplifier input; it decides how much the source is loaded.
Load line
A straight line drawn on the device characteristic showing every (voltage, current) pair allowed by the external resistors.
Output resistance
The resistance seen looking back into the amplifier output; it decides how far the gain drops under load.
Q-point
The DC collector current and collector-emitter voltage of a transistor when the signal is zero.
RC coupling
Joining two stages through a capacitor and a resistor so that the signal passes but DC does not.
Saturation
The state where the transistor is fully on and V_CE has fallen to a small value such as 0.2 V.
Small-signal condition
The signal amplitude is small enough that the device behaves linearly about its Q-point.
Source follower
Common-drain FET amplifier whose output at the source follows the gate input.
Stability factor
The rate at which collector current changes when the reverse saturation current changes, with β and V_BE fixed.
T-model
Small-signal model that places r_e = V_T/I_E in the emitter lead and a current source α·i_e in the collector lead.
Thermal runaway
Self-heating that increases current, which increases heating again, ending in device failure.
Transconductance
The small-signal change in output current divided by the change in input voltage that causes it; unit siemens.
