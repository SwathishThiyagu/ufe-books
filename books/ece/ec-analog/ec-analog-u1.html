1.1 Half-wave, full-wave and bridge rectifiers

Mains arrives as 230 V, 50 Hz AC. Almost every circuit inside a phone, TV or laptop needs steady DC. A rectifier bridges the two: it uses diodes, which conduct in one direction only, to convert AC into pulsating DC. A filter then flattens the pulses (chapter 1.2) and a regulator holds the level steady. Half-wave, centre-tapped full-wave and bridge are the three standard circuits. The bridge is the most common in real products because it needs no centre-tapped transformer. Some syllabi call the bridge the Graetz rectifier.

You need: the PN junction diode's one-way conduction and its 0.7 V silicon drop, the ideal-diode model, and the link between rms and peak for a sine wave, Vm = √2 × Vrms.

The half-wave rectifier: one diode, one half cycle

Connect the transformer secondary, one diode and the load resistor R in series. In the positive half cycle the anode is positive, the diode conducts, and the secondary voltage appears across R minus the diode drop. In the negative half cycle the diode is reverse biased, no current flows, and the load voltage is zero. The output is a row of positive humps, not a steady DC.

Let Vm be the peak secondary voltage in volts, R the load in ohms, and Vd the diode forward drop: 0.7 V for silicon, 0.3 V for germanium. The transformer is rated in rms, so Vm = 1.414 × Vrms. The humps repeat once per supply cycle, so the ripple frequency equals the supply frequency.

Vdc = Vm / π = 0.318 Vm

Vdc is the average load voltage in volts; Vm is the peak secondary voltage in volts; π = 3.1416. With a real diode, replace Vm by (Vm − Vd).

Irms = Im / 2, Im = (Vm − Vd) / R

Im is the peak load current in amperes; R is the load resistance in ohms; Irms is the rms current in the diode and load over one full cycle.

  • Peak inverse voltage: PIV = Vm. The reverse-biased diode must survive the whole secondary peak.
  • Ripple frequency = 50 Hz for a 50 Hz supply.
  • Maximum rectification efficiency η = Pdc/Pac = 40.6 %, the worst of the three circuits.
  • Transformer utilisation factor TUF = DC power delivered ÷ secondary VA rating = 0.287.

Full-wave rectifiers: both halves used

The centre-tap circuit uses a secondary with a middle connection, giving two equal halves of peak Vm each. D1 conducts on the positive half cycle and D2 on the negative, but both push current through R in the same direction. The load now sees one hump every half cycle, so the ripple frequency is 2f = 100 Hz and the DC output doubles.

Vdc = 2Vm / π = 0.636 Vm

Vm is the peak of one half of the secondary, measured from the centre tap to one end, not the whole secondary.

The PIV here is 2Vm, not Vm. When D1 conducts, the other end of the secondary swings to −Vm, so the non-conducting D2 faces Vm from its own half plus Vm from the conducting half.

The bridge rectifier removes the centre tap. Four diodes form a ring; opposite pairs conduct on alternate half cycles, so the load current keeps the same direction. The secondary is used fully in both halves, which is why its TUF (0.812) beats the centre tap (0.693). The price is two diode drops in the conducting path instead of one.

Bridge: Vdc = 2(Vm − 2Vd) / π, PIV = Vm

Vm is the peak of the full secondary; the 2Vd accounts for two forward-biased diodes in series with the load. Ideal full-wave efficiency is 81.2 %, but the bridge's is a little lower in practice because of that extra drop.

Exam traps

  • Putting the rms value into Vdc = Vm/π. The formula wants the peak; 23 V rms gives 32.53 V peak, and the DC is 10.13 V, not 7.32 V.
  • Forgetting the diode drop. Subtract 0.7 V from the peak before dividing by π; subtracting it after division gives the wrong answer.
  • Saying the bridge PIV is 2Vm. In a bridge, each reverse diode sees only Vm. The 2Vm figure belongs to the centre-tap circuit.
  • Using 0.636 Vm for a half-wave rectifier. That constant is for full-wave; half-wave is 0.318 Vm.
  • Reporting 50 Hz ripple for a full-wave circuit. Both full-wave types double the ripple frequency to 100 Hz.

1.2 Capacitor and LC filters; ripple factor

A rectifier gives DC that still pulses. Ripple is the unwanted AC part left on that DC. A filter reduces ripple by storing energy or by blocking alternating current. The commonest is a capacitor across the load: it charges near the peak and then discharges slowly through the load while the rectifier output falls. An LC section then removes the remaining high-frequency ripple. Every power supply that charges a phone or runs a router uses this idea before a regulator, because a regulator needs a fairly steady input to hold its output clean.

You need: <a href='#half-wave-full-wave-and-bridge-rectifiers'>Chapter 1.1 rectifier waveforms and the RC time constant and impedance.

How a capacitor-input filter works

The capacitor-input filter sits directly after the rectifier. During each pulse, the rectifier output rises. The diode conducts and charges C. C stores charge and its voltage reaches about Vm, the peak rectified voltage. When the pulse falls, the diode turns off. C now supplies the load current through R. Its voltage falls slowly. The next pulse charges C again. This charge-discharge cycle repeats at f: twice per cycle for full-wave, once per cycle for half-wave.

The discharge is set by the time constant RC. A large C or a large R makes the droop small, so the load sees nearly steady DC. The average output is below the peak by about half the peak-to-peak ripple.

  1. Rectifier output rises; diode conducts and C charges toward Vm.
  2. Output falls below capacitor voltage; diode turns off.
  3. C discharges through R; load voltage droops.
  4. Next pulse recharges C to near Vm.

Vdc ≈ Vm − Vr(pp)/2

Vdc is the average DC output in V; Vm is the peak rectified voltage in V; Vr(pp) is the peak-to-peak ripple voltage in V.

Ripple voltage and ripple factor

During discharge, load current is about I = Vdc/R. The capacitor loses charge ΔQ = I × Δt. For full-wave, the discharge time is about 1/(2f); for half-wave it is 1/f. Since Vr(pp) = ΔQ/C, the approximate formulas follow. They assume small ripple, Vr(pp) much less than Vdc, and steady load current.

Full-wave: Vr(pp) ≈ Vdc/(2fCR). Half-wave: Vr(pp) ≈ Vdc/(fCR).

f is supply frequency in Hz; C is filter capacitance in farad; R is load resistance in ohm; Vdc is average DC in V; Vr(pp) is peak-to-peak ripple in V.

Ripple factor compares the AC part with the DC part. Use rms values: r = Vr(rms)/Vdc. A sawtooth-like ripple has Vr(rms) = Vr(pp)/(2√3). So r ≈ 1/(4√3 fCR) for full-wave and 1/(2√3 fCR) for half-wave. Some notes quote the peak ratio Vr(pp)/Vdc and call it ripple factor; in an exam, state which definition you use.

RC and LC passive filters

A single capacitor is a first-order filter. Its shunt impedance at ripple frequency is 1/(2πfC). A series resistor and shunt capacitor form an RC low-pass filter. Above cut-off, gain falls about 20 dB per decade. The resistor also drops DC voltage, so an RC filter wastes power in a supply.

An LC section uses a series inductor and shunt capacitor. The inductor blocks high-frequency ripple and the capacitor bypasses it. Above its natural frequency, a simple LC low-pass falls about 40 dB per decade. RLC adds damping and avoids a sharp resonance peak. Some syllabi call these passive filters. Active filters, including Gm-C filters, are in Unit 7. A regulator in <a href='#zener-diode-voltage-regulator'>Chapter 1.4 or <a href='#series-and-shunt-transistor-regulators'>Chapter 1.5 needs this clean DC.

RC cut-off: fc = 1/(2πRC). LC natural frequency: f0 = 1/(2π√(LC)).

R is series resistance in ohm; C is capacitance in farad; L is inductance in henry; fc and f0 are in Hz. Exact LC cut-off also depends on source and load.

Worked example

Question. A full-wave bridge rectifier with Vm = 12 V, f = 50 Hz feeds a capacitor C = 1000 µF and load R = 100 Ω. Use the approximate capacitor-filter formulas and ideal diodes. Find the peak-to-peak ripple voltage, the DC output and the rms ripple factor.

  1. C = 1000 µF = 1000 × 10⁻⁶ F = 1.0 × 10⁻³ F.
  2. Full-wave factor: 2fCR = 2 × 50 Hz × 1.0 × 10⁻³ F × 100 Ω = 10.0.
  3. So Vr(pp) ≈ Vdc/10.0.
  4. DC output: Vdc ≈ Vm − Vr(pp)/2 = 12 V − Vdc/20. Thus 1.05 Vdc = 12 V, giving Vdc = 11.43 V.
  5. Ripple: Vr(pp) = 11.43 V / 10.0 = 1.14 V.
  6. rms ripple: Vr(rms) = Vr(pp)/(2√3) = 1.14 V / 3.464 = 0.330 V.
  7. Ripple factor: r = Vr(rms)/Vdc = 0.330 V / 11.43 V = 0.0289.
  8. Peak ratio: Vr(pp)/Vdc = 1.14 V / 11.43 V = 0.10.

Answer. The peak-to-peak ripple is 1.14 V, the DC output is 11.43 V, and the rms ripple factor is 0.0289 (2.89%); the peak ratio is 0.10. A real bridge subtracts about 1.4 V of diode drop, which lowers Vdc but leaves this ripple-factor formula almost unchanged.

Exam traps

  • Full-wave has two pulses per cycle. Use 2fCR, not fCR, in the ripple formula.
  • Ripple factor uses rms ripple. Vr(pp)/Vdc is the peak ratio; divide by 2√3 for the true ripple factor.
  • A real bridge has two diode drops. Subtract about 1.4 V from Vm before finding Vdc; otherwise Vdc is high by about 1.4 V.
  • The capacitor formulas assume Vr(pp) much less than Vdc and steady load current. At heavy load, small C or small R, the droop is large and the formulas fail.
  • Do not mix RC cut-off 1/(2πRC) with LC natural frequency 1/(2π√(LC)); exact LC cut-off also depends on source and load.

1.3 Clippers and clampers

A clipper removes the part of a waveform that lies above or below a chosen level. A clamper moves the whole waveform up or down without changing its peak-to-peak value. Both use a diode as a switch. A clipper uses a battery (or Zener) to set the clip level; a clamper uses a capacitor to store the DC shift and a diode returned to a reference. They protect inputs, reshape signals and set DC levels in radios, sensors and audio gear. Some syllabi call clippers limiting circuits. A sensor input that must not see a spike above 4 V, for example, uses a clipper. The next sections show the circuits, their transfer characteristics and the time-constant rule for clampers.

You need: the diode switch model and sine-wave peak values from Chapter 1.1, plus the RC time constant from Chapter 1.2.

Clipping: cutting off a peak

A clipper is a diode network that passes one part of the input and cuts the rest. In a shunt clipper the diode sits across the output. When it conducts, it holds the output at a reference level. In a series clipper the diode sits in the signal path. When it is off, no signal reaches the output. The diode acts as a switch: about 0.7 V forward drop for silicon, open in reverse, or 0 V in the ideal model.

V<sub>out</sub> = V<sub>ref</sub> + V<sub>D</sub>   (diode on, shunt clipper)

Vref is the battery reference voltage in volts; VD is the diode forward drop, about 0.7 V for silicon and 0 V for an ideal diode.

A bias battery sets the clip level. A positive clipper removes positive peaks above Vref. A negative clipper removes negative peaks below −Vref. Reversing the diode or the battery changes which peak is cut and at what level. Two branches make a double clipper that cuts both peaks.

  • Positive shunt clipper: output is flat at Vref + VD (≈ Vref + 0.7 V with a silicon diode) when Vin is above it.
  • Negative shunt clipper: output is flat at −(Vref + VD) when Vin is below it.
  • Biased clipper: the battery moves the clip level away from zero.
  • Double clipper: one branch cuts the top, the other cuts the bottom.

These levels assume the diode is on. Vref alone is the level only for an ideal diode.

The transfer characteristic plots Vout against Vin. Inside the pass region the slope is 1. On a clipped peak the slope is 0, so the curve is flat.

Clamping: shifting the DC level

A clamper, also called a DC restorer, uses a capacitor in series with the signal and a diode to a reference. On the first half-cycle the diode conducts and charges the capacitor to the peak of the input minus the diode drop. Then the diode turns off and the capacitor holds that voltage. The output is the input plus the capacitor voltage, so the whole waveform shifts. The peak-to-peak value does not change; the average value does.

V<sub>C</sub> = V<sub>p</sub> − V<sub>D</sub> ;   V<sub>out</sub> = V<sub>in</sub> + V<sub>C</sub>

VC is the capacitor voltage in volts; Vp is the input peak in volts; VD is the diode drop in volts. Use VC ≈ −(Vp − VD) for a negative clamper.

A positive clamper pushes the waveform upward so its negative peak sits near 0 V. With an ideal diode and a 10 V peak sine, the output swings from 0 V to +20 V. A negative clamper pushes the waveform downward so its positive peak sits near 0 V, swinging from −20 V to 0 V.

The capacitor must hold its charge during the whole cycle. Choose the load resistance RL and capacitance C so that RLC is much larger than the period T. A common design rule is RLC ≥ 10T, where T = 1/f. If the time constant is too small, the output droops or tilts.

Practical checks and limits

A real diode turns on gradually near 0.7 V, so a clipper corner is rounded rather than sharp. Above the knee the diode has a small resistance, so the clipped top is not perfectly flat. A clamper output also loses about 0.7 V because of the diode drop. The capacitor must be connected with the polarity that matches its charging voltage; an electrolytic capacitor placed the wrong way can fail.

Check a clipper by comparing the output peak with the reference level. Check a clamper by comparing peak-to-peak values: they must be equal at input and output. The average output equals the average input plus VC. Clampers are used as DC restorers in video and as level shifters before ADCs. Clippers protect RF mixers and comparator inputs. For precision clipping, op-amp circuits appear in Unit 6; Zener limiting is covered in Chapter 1.4.

Question. A 10 V peak, 50 Hz sine wave drives a shunt positive clipper with an ideal diode in series with a 4 V battery. The diode conducts when the input exceeds 4 V. Sketch the input and output for one cycle and state the output peak-to-peak voltage.

  1. Input peak Vp = 10 V. Input minimum = −10 V. Input peak-to-peak = 10 − (−10) = 20 V.
  2. Period T = 1/f = 1/50 = 0.020 s = 20 ms.
  3. The battery reference is 4 V and the diode is ideal, so the clip level is 4 V.
  4. When Vin > 4 V, the diode conducts and Vout = 4 V. This starts at θ = sin−1(4/10) = 23.6° and ends at 180° − 23.6° = 156.4°.
  5. When Vin ≤ 4 V, the diode is off and Vout = Vin. The negative peak reaches −10 V.
  6. Output maximum = +4 V. Output minimum = −10 V. Output peak-to-peak = 4 − (−10) = 14 V.

Answer. The output peak-to-peak voltage is 14 V.

Exam traps

  • Reversing the diode changes the clipper from positive to negative; reversing the battery moves the clip level but does not change the peak that is cut.
  • A clamper changes the DC average but never the peak-to-peak value; an answer that reports a smaller peak-to-peak for an ideal clamper is wrong.
  • A practical clamper output is shifted by about 0.7 V less than the ideal value because of the diode drop.
  • If RLC is not much larger than T, the capacitor discharges and the clamped waveform droops or tilts.
  • In a shunt clipper the diode conducts and shorts the output to the reference; in a series clipper the diode opens the signal path. Do not mix the two.

1.4 Zener diode voltage regulator

A Zener diode is a silicon diode built to work in reverse breakdown. At its breakdown voltage VZ the voltage across it stays nearly constant while the current through it changes widely. Put one in parallel with a load, feed both through a series resistor Rs, and the load sees a steady VZ even when the input or load current moves. It is the cheapest way to get a fixed 6 V or 9 V rail, used in small chargers, sensor boards and reference circuits.

You need: a steady DC input from Chapter 1.2, and diode behaviour from Chapter 1.1.

The Zener works in reverse breakdown

A Zener is a heavily doped silicon p-n junction. Forward biased, it conducts at about 0.7 V like any diode. Reverse biased, it blocks with microamperes of leakage until the reverse voltage reaches VZ. At VZ the current rises steeply while the voltage across the diode stays almost fixed. It is used reverse biased: forward gives 0.7 V, reverse gives a chosen VZ from about 2.4 V to over 100 V.

Below about 5 V the depletion layer is thin and electrons tunnel through it — Zener breakdown. Above about 6 V carriers knock out more carriers — avalanche breakdown. Neither destroys the diode if current is limited and heat is removed.

The reverse characteristic bends at knee current IZ(min). Below it the voltage is not pinned at VZ, so there is no regulation. Above it the curve is almost vertical. Data sheets state VZ at test current IZT.

r<sub>z</sub> = ΔV<sub>Z</sub> / ΔI<sub>Z</sub>

rz is the dynamic resistance in ohms, ΔVZ the small change in Zener voltage in volts and ΔIZ the small change in Zener current in amperes. rz is a few ohms to tens of ohms, and causes the output to move slightly when current changes.

Rs drops the surplus voltage

The regulator has a series resistor Rs from the input, a Zener from the output node to ground, and the load in parallel with the Zener. The input must be larger than VZ. Rs carries the difference, and whatever current the load does not take flows through the Zener.

I<sub>Rs</sub> = (V<sub>in</sub> − V<sub>Z</sub>) / R<sub>s</sub>,   I<sub>Z</sub> = I<sub>Rs</sub> − I<sub>L</sub> = (V<sub>in</sub> − V<sub>Z</sub>)/R<sub>s</sub> − V<sub>Z</sub>/R<sub>L</sub>,   P<sub>Z</sub> = V<sub>Z</sub>·I<sub>Z</sub>

Vin is the DC input in volts, VZ the Zener voltage in volts, Rs the series resistor and RL the load resistor, both in ohms; IRs, IZ and IL are resistor, Zener and load currents in amperes; PZ is Zener dissipation in watts.

Regulation holds only while IZ(min) ≤ IZ ≤ IZ(max). A heavier load lowers IZ; below the knee the output sags to Vin·RL/(Rs + RL). A higher input or lighter load raises IZ; above IZ(max) the Zener overheats.

Line regulation is ΔVout/ΔVin ≈ rz/(Rs + rz) volts per volt; a larger Rs or smaller rz gives a stiffer output. Load regulation is (VNL − VFL)/VFL × 100%, where VNL is no-load output and VFL full-load output.

Designing Rs and the power rating

Choose Rs from the worst case that drains the Zener, then check the case that overloads it.

  1. Heaviest load, lowest input: Rs must still pass IZ(min), so Rs ≤ (Vin(min) − VZ)/(IZ(min) + IL(max)), with IL(max) = VZ/RL(min).
  2. Lightest load, highest input: IZ(max) = (Vin(max) − VZ)/Rs − IL(min), with IL(min) = 0 at no load.
  3. Power: PZ = VZ·IZ(max) must stay below the derated rating.
  4. If step 3 fails, use a larger-wattage Zener, a lower Vin(max), or a larger Rs at the cost of load current.

A 1 W Zener rated at 25 °C cannot shed 1 W at 100 °C. Data sheets give linear derating to zero at a maximum junction temperature. If a 1 W part derates to zero at 150 °C, the allowed dissipation at 100 °C is 1 W × (150 − 100)/(150 − 25) = 0.40 W. Design with the derated figure and leave margin for the hottest day inside the cabinet.

Worked example

Question. A Zener shunt regulator has Vin = 12 V, VZ = 6 V, Rs = 220 Ω and RL = 1 kΩ. The Zener needs IZ ≥ 5 mA to stay in breakdown. (a) Find the Zener current. (b) Find the Zener power dissipation. (c) Find the smallest load resistance that still keeps IZ ≥ 5 mA.

  6. Check at RL = 269.4 Ω: IL = 22.27 mA, IZ = 5 mA exactly. Any lower RL pushes IZ below the knee and the output falls below 6 V.

1.5 Series and shunt transistor regulators

A Zener regulator is open loop: it sets a voltage but cannot correct for slope resistance or a sagging input. A transistor regulator closes the loop. It samples the output, compares it with a fixed reference, and drives a control transistor that absorbs the left-over voltage. The output then depends almost only on the reference and two resistors. Bench supplies and 78xx chips work this way. This chapter builds discrete series and shunt regulators and their protection.

You need the Zener reference in Chapter 1.4 and BJT biasing from Unit 2.

Why the loop is needed

A Zener regulator cannot correct for slope resistance or input changes. A transistor regulator closes the loop: it measures a fraction of the output, compares it with a reference, and adjusts a control device until the two agree.

Four blocks do this. A reference gives a steady DC voltage, usually a Zener or bandgap cell. An error amplifier compares the reference with an output sample. A pass device, a BJT or MOSFET, sits between input and load. A feedback divider scales the output down.

  1. Load current rises and Vout dips.
  2. The sample Vfb = Vout × R2/(R1+R2) dips with it.
  3. The error amplifier sees Vfb below Vref and swings its output up.
  4. The pass transistor conducts harder, its collector-emitter voltage falls, and Vout rises back.
  5. The loop balances when Vfb equals Vref.

The loop works only while the pass device is not fully on. That margin is the dropout voltage: the smallest Vin − Vout for which Vout still holds. Below it the pass transistor saturates and Vout follows the input down.

The series pass regulator

In a series regulator the pass transistor is in series with the load, so all load current flows through it. The error amplifier drives its base or gate; the output is at its emitter or source.

Vfb = Vout × R2 / (R1 + R2)

Vfb is the voltage at the error-amplifier input in volts; Vout is the regulated output in volts; R1 is the resistor from Vout to the feedback node in ohms; R2 is the resistor from the feedback node to ground in ohms.

Negative feedback holds Vfb at Vref. Substitute and rearrange.

Vout = Vref × (1 + R1/R2)

Vref is the reference voltage in volts; R1 and R2 are the feedback resistors in ohms, R1 being the upper one; Vout is the regulated output in volts. R1 = 0 gives Vout = Vref.

  1. Fix Vref from the Zener or bandgap cell, for example 5.6 V.
  2. Choose R2 between 1 kΩ and 10 kΩ. A small R2 draws more divider current, costing efficiency but improving noise immunity.
  3. Solve R1 = R2 × (Vout/Vref − 1). Round to a standard value.
  4. Check dropout: Vin must exceed Vout by the headroom — about 0.3 V for a PMOS LDO, about 1 V for a plain NPN, and 1.5 V to 2 V for a Darlington.

All load current passes through the pass transistor, so it burns power even at light load.

P<sub>pass</sub> = (Vin − Vout) × I<sub>L</sub>

Ppass is power wasted in the pass transistor in watts; Vin is the unregulated input in volts; Vout is the regulated output in volts; IL is load current in amperes. Vin − Vout is the voltage across the transistor, never Vout.

The shunt regulator

A shunt regulator puts the control device in parallel with the load. Resistor RS drops the input to the wanted output. The error amplifier drives a transistor that pulls extra current through RS when Vout tries to rise. RS current stays roughly constant; the transistor takes what the load does not.

That wastes power. At light load almost all RS current goes through the shunt transistor, so input power barely falls. Efficiency is roughly the fraction of total current reaching the load times Vout/Vin. A series regulator passes only load current, so its dissipation falls with load.

Protection: current limit and thermal shutdown

A shorted output is worst. Vout collapses to 0 V, so the full input voltage appears across the pass transistor. Without protection the current is limited only by the source, and P = Vin × ISC destroys the device in milliseconds.

Current limiting adds a small sense resistor Rsense in the pass path. When its drop reaches about 0.7 V, a second transistor turns on and steals base drive from the pass device.

Ilimit ≈ 0.7 V / Rsense

Ilimit is maximum output current in amperes; Rsense is the sense resistor in ohms; 0.7 V is the base-emitter turn-on voltage of the sensing BJT.

Foldback limiting lowers the limit current as Vout falls, often to a third of full load, so a short dissipates far less than a full-load overload. Thermal shutdown is the last defence: a die sensor turns the pass device off near 150 °C and restarts it after cooling by a hysteresis band. Chapter 1.6 shows these circuits inside a chip.

Question. A discrete series regulator uses a 5.6 V reference. R1 = 10 kΩ connects Vout to the error-amplifier input; R2 = 20 kΩ connects that input to ground. Vin = 12 V and the load draws 84 mA. Find (a) Vout, (b) pass-transistor dissipation, (c) efficiency.

1.6 IC voltage regulators: 78xx, 79xx, LM317, LDO

A three-terminal IC regulator holds its output at a set voltage with almost no external parts. Inside one chip sit a voltage reference, an error amplifier and a pass transistor — the three blocks you built from discrete parts, now trimmed at the factory and guarded by current limit and thermal shutdown. A 7805 turns a rough 12 V adapter into a clean 5 V rail for a microcontroller; an LM317 sets any voltage a bench supply needs; an LDO keeps 3.3 V alive while a lithium cell falls to 3.6 V. Cheap, small, and on almost every board you will open.

You need: Chapter 1.5 for the reference–error amplifier–pass transistor loop, negative feedback from Unit 4, and the thermal resistance idea behind heat sinks from Unit 3.

Inside the three-terminal regulator

Chapter 1.5 built a regulator from a Zener reference, an error amplifier and a series pass transistor. The IC version puts all three on one die, adds a temperature-stable internal reference, current limiting and thermal shutdown, and brings out three pins: IN, OUT, and a common pin — GND on fixed types, ADJ on adjustable ones.

The loop is unchanged. An internal divider samples a fraction of Vout, the error amplifier compares it with the internal reference and drives the pass transistor's base. If Vout falls, base current rises, the transistor conducts harder, and Vout comes back. The divider ratio is trimmed on the die, so the part number fixes the output and no external resistor sets it. In symbols Vout = Vref × (1 + R2/R1), where Vref is the internal reference voltage, R1 is the internal resistor from OUT down to the tap, and R2 is the resistor from the tap to ground. For the LM317 the same formula holds with external resistors and Vref = 1.25 V, the OUT-to-ADJ voltage.

Two capacitors keep this loop stable. A 0.33 µF capacitor at the input stops oscillation when the regulator sits far from the rectifier and filter, because the wire between them adds inductance. A 0.1 µF capacitor at the output gives the loop a low-impedance path at high frequency and covers fast load steps. Some low-dropout types instead specify an equivalent series resistance (ESR) window for the output capacitor; outside that window they oscillate.

78xx and 79xx: the fixed families

The 78xx family is fixed and positive. The last two digits give the output: 7805 is +5 V, 7809 is +9 V, 7812 is +12 V, and 7815, 7824 continue the pattern. A plain 78xx in a TO-220 package passes about 1 A; the middle letter gives the current, so 78L05 is 100 mA and 78M05 is 500 mA. Output tolerance is a few percent and dropout is about 2 V, meaning a 7805 needs at least 7 V at its input to hold 5 V.

The 79xx family is the negative twin: 7905 is −5 V, 7912 is −12 V. It regulates a negative rail and pairs with a 78xx to make the split supply a dual op-amp needs. Watch the pin order. In TO-220 a 78xx is IN–GND–OUT, while a 79xx is GND–IN–OUT. Swapping them destroys the part, and the pin order is a favourite exam question.

LM317: adjustable output

The LM317 is adjustable and positive, with pins IN, OUT and ADJ. Its only internal job is to hold exactly 1.25 V between OUT and ADJ; it has no connection to ground. Two external resistors turn that fixed 1.25 V into any output: R1 from OUT to ADJ (the upper resistor), R2 from ADJ to ground (the lower resistor).

Vout = 1.25 × (1 + R2/R1) + I_ADJ × R2

Vout is the output in volts; R1 is in ohms from OUT to ADJ (upper); R2 is in ohms from ADJ to ground (lower); I_ADJ ≈ 50 µA is the current out of the ADJ pin, usually dropped because I_ADJ × R2 is only a few millivolts.

R1 is normally 240 Ω. It sets a minimum load current of 1.25/240 ≈ 5 mA, which the LM317 needs before it regulates cleanly; with no load the output can drift up. Output spans 1.25 V to 37 V, input may reach 40 V, and current reaches 1.5 A with a heatsink. The negative adjustable version is the LM337. Because the reference floats, the same chip also makes a current source: a resistor R from OUT to ADJ passes 1.25/R to the load.

Dropout, quiescent current and heat

Dropout voltage is the smallest input-to-output difference at which regulation still holds. Below it the pass device saturates and the output simply follows the input. A 78xx needs about 2 V; a low-dropout (LDO) regulator uses a PNP or PMOS pass device and needs 0.1 V to 0.5 V. That headroom decides whether a 3.3 V rail survives when a lithium cell sags to 3.5 V.

Quiescent current Iq is what the regulator draws at its ground or ADJ pin with no load: about 5 mA for a 78xx, a few hundred microamps or less for an LDO. It is wasted from the input and heats the chip.

Pd = (Vin − Vout) × I_load + Vin × Iq

Pd is the power turned into heat in watts; Vin and Vout are in volts; I_load and Iq are in amperes.

Heat leaves the junction through three thermal resistances in series: junction to case θjc, case to heatsink θcs (0.5 to 1.5 °C/W with thermal paste), and heatsink to ambient θsa. Add them.

Tj = Ta + Pd × (θjc + θcs + θsa)

Tj is junction temperature in °C; Ta is ambient in °C; Pd is in W; each θ is in °C/W.

Keep Tj below the datasheet limit, usually 125 °C or 150 °C. Efficiency is roughly Vout/Vin, so a 7805 running on 12 V turns 58% of the input power into heat. Above a few watts, use an SMPS instead.

Worked example

Question. A 7805 has Vin = 12 V, Vout = 5 V and I_load = 500 mA. Its junction-to-case thermal resistance is 5 °C/W and case-to-heatsink is 1 °C/W. Ambient is 40 °C and the maximum junction temperature is 125 °C. Find the power dissipation and the largest heatsink thermal resistance allowed. Neglect quiescent current.

  1. Voltage across the pass transistor: Vin − Vout = 12 − 5 = 7 V.
  2. Load current in amperes: 500 mA = 0.5 A.
  3. Power dissipation: Pd = 7 V × 0.5 A = 3.5 W.
  4. Allowed temperature rise: Tj,max − Ta = 125 − 40 = 85 °C.
  5. Total thermal resistance allowed: 85 °C ÷ 3.5 W = 24.29 °C/W.

1.7 Switched-mode power supplies

A switched-mode power supply (SMPS) does not burn off excess voltage as heat. It switches the input on and off thousands of times a second, stores energy in an inductor or capacitor, and releases it to the load at the wanted voltage. Feedback adjusts the on-time fraction, called duty cycle, to hold the output steady. Phone chargers, laptop adapters, TV sets and UPI payment terminals all use an SMPS. Its efficiency often exceeds 85 percent, which is why it replaced bulky linear supplies in most mains-powered equipment.

You need: Chapter 1.6 on IC regulators, and the energy-storage idea for an inductor (½Li²) and capacitor (½Cv²).

The switch replaces the pass transistor

The problem with linear regulators. A series regulator in Chapter 1.5 holds the output by dropping the extra voltage across a pass transistor. If Vin = 24 V and Vout = 9.6 V at 1 A, the transistor burns (24 − 9.6) × 1 = 14.4 W as heat. The best possible efficiency is Vout/Vin = 40 percent.

A switched-mode power supply, or SMPS, avoids that heat. It uses a transistor as a switch that is either fully on (low voltage across it) or fully off (no current). In both states the power wasted in the switch is small. The switch chops the input into pulses. An inductor and capacitor store the pulse energy and smooth it into steady DC. Some syllabi call these circuits switching regulators.

D = Ton / T = Ton × fsw

D is duty cycle (0 to 1); Ton is on-time in seconds; T is switching period in seconds; fsw = 1/T is switching frequency in hertz.

A pulse-width modulator (PWM) compares a sample of Vout with a reference and sets D. If Vout falls, D rises and more energy moves to the output.

Buck, boost and buck-boost

Three topologies cover most needs. All formulas assume continuous conduction mode (CCM): the inductor current never reaches zero during a cycle.

Buck (step-down): Vout = D Vin. Here Vout and Vin are in volts and D is dimensionless. The switch connects Vin to the inductor for D T. The freewheeling diode carries inductor current for the rest. With Vin = 24 V and D = 0.4, Vout = 0.4 × 24 = 9.6 V.

Boost (step-up): Vout = Vin / (1 − D). The inductor is first connected across Vin to store energy; then the switch opens and the inductor pushes current through a diode into the output capacitor. With Vin = 12 V and D = 0.5, Vout = 12/(1 − 0.5) = 24 V. The output is always greater than Vin.

Buck-boost (inverting): Vout = −Vin × D/(1 − D). The magnitude can be lower or higher than Vin; the sign reverses. Many isolated supplies use a transformer instead, which also gives isolation.

ΔIL = (Vin − Vout) D T / L

ΔIL is peak-to-peak inductor current in amperes; Vin and Vout are in volts; D is dimensionless; T is in seconds; L is inductance in henries. The off-time expression Vout (1 − D) T / L gives the same ripple, because the average inductor voltage over one cycle is zero.

Component roles: the inductor limits current change and stores ½ L i²; the output capacitor stores ½ C v² and holds Vout between pulses. Higher fsw allows smaller L and C.

Losses, ripple and rectification

Losses and efficiency. Conduction loss in the switch is approximately I² Rds(on) D, where I is switch current in amperes and Rds(on) is the MOSFET on-resistance in ohms. Switching loss occurs while the switch passes through the linear region: roughly ½ Vin I (tr + tf) fsw, where tr and tf are rise and fall times in seconds. At high fsw this term grows, so there is a practical limit around a few hundred kilohertz for silicon MOSFETs.

The freewheeling diode in a buck converter drops about 0.4 to 0.7 V. At Vout = 1.2 V this diode loss can dominate. Synchronous rectification replaces the diode with a MOSFET that is switched on when the diode would conduct. Its drop is I Rds(on), often 50 mV or less, so efficiency rises, especially at low output voltages.

ΔVout ≈ ΔIL / (8 fsw C)

ΔVout is peak-to-peak output ripple in volts; ΔIL is inductor ripple in amperes; fsw is in hertz; C is output capacitance in farads. This ignores capacitor ESR; real ripple has an extra term ΔIL × ESR.

Control-loop compensation — setting the error amplifier's gain and phase so the loop does not oscillate — is left to advanced power electronics.

Worked example

Question. A buck converter has Vin = 24 V, duty cycle D = 0.4, switching frequency fsw = 100 kHz and inductor L = 100 µH. Find the ideal output voltage and the inductor current ripple in continuous conduction.

  1. Period T = 1/fsw = 1/(100 × 10³) = 10 × 10⁻⁶ s = 10 µs.
  2. Ideal output Vout = D Vin = 0.4 × 24 = 9.6 V.
  3. On-time Ton = D T = 0.4 × 10 µs = 4 µs.
  4. Voltage across the inductor during on-time = Vin − Vout = 24 − 9.6 = 14.4 V.
  5. Ripple ΔIL = V_L Ton / L = 14.4 × 4 × 10⁻⁶ / (100 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.576 A.
  6. Check with off-time: Vout (1 − D) T / L = 9.6 × 0.6 × 10 × 10⁻⁶ / (100 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.576 A. The two answers agree.

Answer. Ideal output voltage is 9.6 V and inductor current ripple is 0.576 A peak-to-peak.

Exam traps

  • Swapping buck and boost formulas. Buck gives Vout = D Vin, less than Vin; boost gives Vin/(1 − D), more than Vin. Write the topology name before substituting.
  • Using D as 40 instead of 0.4 in a formula. Duty cycle is a fraction; multiply by 100 only when you report percent.
  • Treating the ideal formula as the real output. Diode drop, switch drop and losses lower the measured Vout; an unloaded converter may sit slightly high.
  • Applying the CCM ripple formula at light load. If the inductor current reaches zero, the converter is in DCM and the formula overestimates ripple.
  • Forgetting that the buck-boost output is inverted. The magnitude is D Vin/(1 − D); the minus sign matters in polarity and in feedback sensing.

Check yourself

Remember Define duty cycle and write its formula. 2 marks

Duty cycle is the fraction of the switching period for which the switch is on. D = Ton/T = Ton fsw, where Ton is on-time, T is period and fsw is switching frequency. D lies between 0 and 1.

Understand Why can an SMPS be more efficient than a series linear regulator? 3 marks

The switch is either off (no current) or on (small voltage), so it does not continuously dissipate (Vin − Vout)I. Energy is temporarily stored in L and C. The linear pass transistor always carries load current with the full difference voltage across it.

Apply A boost converter has Vin = 12 V and D = 0.5 in CCM. Find Vout. 3 marks

Vout = Vin/(1 − D) = 12/(1 − 0.5) = 12/0.5 = 24 V.

Apply A buck converter has Vin = 20 V, D = 0.25, fsw = 200 kHz and L = 50 µH. Find Vout and the inductor current ripple in CCM. 4 marks

Vout = D Vin = 0.25 × 20 = 5 V. T = 1/200 kHz = 5 µs. Ton = 0.25 × 5 µs = 1.25 µs. ΔIL = (20 − 5) × 1.25 × 10⁻⁶ / (50 × 10⁻⁶) = 15 × 1.25/50 = 0.375 A peak-to-peak.

Analyze A buck converter in CCM suddenly gets a heavier load. Describe what happens to Vout and how PWM restores it. 4 marks

Vout dips because inductor current cannot change instantly. The error amplifier sees the fall and increases D. The switch on-time lengthens, average inductor current rises until it matches the new load, and Vout returns to the set value. Loop compensation sets the speed and stability of this recovery.

Analyze Why does raising fsw shrink L and C but hurt efficiency? 4 marks

Inductor ripple ΔIL is proportional to 1/(fsw L), so smaller L gives the same ripple at higher fsw. Output ripple is proportional to 1/(fsw C), so C can also shrink. But switching loss is proportional to fsw, and gate-drive loss rises too.

Understand What is synchronous rectification and where does it help most? 3 marks

It replaces the freewheeling diode with a MOSFET switched on when the diode would conduct. The drop becomes I Rds(on) instead of a fixed diode voltage. It helps most at low output voltages, where a 0.5 V diode drop is a large fraction of Vout.

1.8 Voltage multipliers: doubler, tripler, quadrupler

A voltage multiplier is a ladder of diodes and capacitors. Capacitors charge on one half-cycle and stack in series on the next, so the DC output is a multiple of the input peak Vp. Each diode–capacitor pair adds one Vp: a doubler uses two pairs (2Vp), a tripler three (3Vp), a quadrupler four (4Vp). The multiplication factor equals the number n of diode–capacitor pairs. No transformer is needed to reach a few hundred volts. Multipliers bias photomultiplier tubes, laser and X-ray heads, precipitators and older CRT anodes. They give high voltage at low current, and that current limit drives the design.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for rectifier action, Chapter 1.2 for capacitor charging and ripple, and Chapter 1.3 for the clamp that starts the ladder.

The clamp and the peak detector

A multiplier uses two diode–capacitor habits. A diode with a capacitor charges to Vp and holds it: a peak detector. Put that charged capacitor in series with the source and its Vp adds to the source, lifting the waveform by Vp: a clamp. A multiplier is a chain of clamps.

  1. Negative half-cycle: D1 conducts, C1 charges to Vp with its left plate negative.
  2. Positive half-cycle: source and C1 add, so D2 anode reaches about 2Vp.
  3. D2 conducts and C2 charges to 2Vp; D1 is reverse biased.
  4. The steps repeat every cycle; no-load output stays at 2Vp.

Two diodes and two capacitors give no-load 2Vp. This is the half-wave doubler.

From doubler to tripler and quadrupler

Add one diode and one capacitor and you add one more Vp. The new capacitor sits on the previous DC level. C3 charges to 3Vp through D3, and C4 to 4Vp through D4. A cascade of n pairs gives n·Vp at no load.

Vout ≈ n·Vp − n·Vd

Vout is no-load DC output in volts; n is number of diode–capacitor pairs, also the multiplication factor (2 doubler, 3 tripler, 4 quadrupler); Vp is peak input in volts, not rms; Vd is one diode forward drop in volts.

A bridge or full-wave doubler uses two diodes and two capacitors, charging one capacitor on each half-cycle, so its ripple is at 2f. The cascade above is half-wave: charge reaches the output once per cycle. Only a few universities teach these cascade details.

Load, ripple and limits

Each pair adds a diode drop and series resistance, and charge arrives once per cycle. n·Vd is lost even at no load. On load the output falls further by resistive and charge-transfer drop, roughly I·Reff, where Reff is effective series resistance. For an n-pair half-wave cascade the ripple grows roughly as n(n+1)·I/(2fC); the one-pair value is ΔV ≈ I/(fC).

  • Ripple frequency is f (50 Hz from mains), not 2f, for the half-wave cascade.
  • Load regulation is poor; multipliers suit microamp to milliamp loads.
  • C1 has only Vp across it: rate it above Vp. D1 must withstand 2Vp in reverse. The doubler output capacitor C2 must be rated above 2Vp. In an n-pair ladder the last capacitor top plate stands at n·Vp above ground, so its rating and insulation must exceed n·Vp.
  • Design from peak values: a 10 V rms winding gives Vp = 14.1 V, not 10 V.

Worked example

Question. A cascade multiplier runs from a 10 V peak, 50 Hz source with ideal diodes. Find the no-load DC output of a doubler, tripler and quadrupler. Repeat the doubler with Vd = 0.7 V.

  1. Input is already peak: Vp = 10 V.
  2. Doubler: n = 2, Vout = 2 × 10 = 20 V.
  3. Tripler: n = 3, Vout = 30 V.
  4. Quadrupler: n = 4, Vout = 40 V.
  5. Doubler with drops: Vout = 2 × 10 − 2 × 0.7 = 18.6 V.

Answer. Ideal no-load outputs are 20 V, 30 V and 40 V; the doubler with 0.7 V diodes gives 18.6 V.

Exam traps

  • Using rms input as Vp: a 10 V rms winding gives 14.1 V peak and a 28.3 V doubler, not 20 V.
  • Forgetting diode drops: each pair costs one Vd, so a quadrupler with 0.7 V diodes loses 2.8 V even at no load.
  • Expecting the output to hold at 100 mA; the cascade sags and ripples long before that.
  • Assuming ripple is at 2f; the half-wave cascade ripples at f, and only the bridge doubler gives 2f.
  • Under-rating capacitors: C1 sits at Vp, not 2Vp; D1 must withstand 2Vp reverse. Doubler output C2 sits at 2Vp; in an n-pair ladder the last capacitor sees n·Vp.

Takeaways

  1. Rectifiers use diode one-way conduction to make pulsating DC; a bridge rectifier gives full-wave output without a centre tap.
  2. A filter capacitor reduces ripple by storing energy, and ripple factor compares AC ripple with DC output.
  3. Clippers cut off waveform peaks, while clampers shift the whole waveform in DC level without changing its shape.
  4. A Zener regulator holds load voltage near VZ by shunting excess current when the input or load changes.
  5. Series and shunt transistor regulators use feedback to reduce output changes caused by input and load variation.
  6. IC regulators integrate a reference, error amplifier and pass device; 78xx and 79xx give fixed outputs and LM317 gives an adjustable output.
  7. Linear regulators are simple and low-noise but waste power as heat when the input-output difference is large.
  8. Switched-mode supplies chop at high frequency and control duty cycle for high efficiency and small size.
  9. Voltage multipliers add capacitor voltages to produce about 2×, 3× or 4× the input peak for high-voltage low-current loads.
