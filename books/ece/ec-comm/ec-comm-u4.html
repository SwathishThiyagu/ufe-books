4.1 Sampling theorem, aliasing and reconstruction

Sampling turns a continuous signal into numbers taken at fixed instants, which is what PCM speech, digital storage and software radio need. If the samples are close enough, the signal can be rebuilt exactly. If they are too far apart, high frequencies fold down and pose as low ones, and no later filter can undo it. This chapter states the theorem, computes aliases, and covers ideal and flat-top reconstruction.

You need: the Fourier transform and band-pass spectra (see Fourier transform review and band-pass signals). Pulse modulation follows in PAM, PWM and PPM.

The theorem and the sample rate

A lowpass signal m(t) is bandlimited to fm hertz when its spectrum M(f) is zero for |f| > fm. The sampling theorem: such a signal is fixed completely by its samples m(nTs) if the sampling rate fs = 1/Ts ≥ 2fm. The lowest allowed rate 2fm is the Nyquist rate (samples/s); the largest spacing 1/(2fm) is the Nyquist interval (seconds).

Ideal sampling multiplies m(t) by an impulse train Σ δ(t − nTs). That train is periodic, so its spectrum is an impulse train fs Σ δ(f − kfs). Multiplication in time is convolution in frequency, so every spectral impulse places a copy of M(f) at kfs:

S(f) = fs Σ M(f − kfs)

S(f) is the spectrum of the sampled signal s(t) = Σ m(nTs) δ(t − nTs); k runs over all integers. The copies are fs apart and scaled by fs.

The copy at 0 ends at fm; the copy at fs starts at fs − fm. They stay apart when fs ≥ 2fm, which is the theorem, and the gap fs − 2fm is the guard band. A lowpass filter with gain Ts (to cancel the factor fs) and any cutoff from fm to fs − fm keeps only the copy at 0 and returns m(t). At exactly 2fm the guard band is zero and a tone at fm can be sampled at its zero crossings, so real systems sample faster.

Aliasing and folding

A tone at f0 above fs/2 does not vanish. Its samples equal those of a lower tone, the alias, at fa = |f0 − kfs|, with the integer k chosen so that 0 ≤ fa ≤ fs/2. The spectrum folds about fs/2, the folding frequency.

With fs = 7 kHz, a 1 kHz tone stays at 1 kHz, but a 4 kHz tone is above 3.5 kHz and folds to |4 − 7| = 3 kHz. The figure shows both views: a 4 kHz and a 3 kHz cosine through the same samples, and the spectral lines of the sampled signal. A 3.5 kHz reconstruction filter passes 1 and 3 kHz, so a 3 kHz tone that was never sent comes out.

So aliasing is stopped before the sampler. An anti-aliasing filter passes 0 to fm. A component at f folds to fs − f, which lands inside the message band when f ≥ fs − fm, so the filter must reach its stopband by fs − fm. The guard band is the room a real filter has to roll off; telephone speech uses fm = 3.4 kHz with fs = 8 kHz for this reason.

Reconstruction and the aperture effect

Ideal reconstruction is sinc interpolation: m(t) = Σ m(nTs) sinc((t − nTs)/Ts), with sinc(x) = sin(πx)/(πx). Each sinc pulse is 1 at its own instant and 0 at every other sample instant, so the sum passes through every sample. It needs an ideal lowpass filter, which real circuits only approach.

In natural sampling the pulse top follows m(t); in flat-top sampling a sample-and-hold keeps the top at m(nTs) for a width τ ≤ Ts. Holding filters the spectrum by H(f) = τ sinc(fτ), which droops the top of the message band: the aperture effect. A zero-order hold in a DAC is the worst case, τ = Ts. A shorter τ or a 1/sinc equalizer reduces it.

A band-pass signal of bandwidth B = fH − fL can be sampled below 2fH if the shifted copies interleave. When fH is a whole multiple of B, fs = 2B works; receivers use this to digitise an IF band directly.

Worked example

Question. m(t) = 3 cos(2π·1000t) + 2 cos(2π·4000t) volts is sampled at fs = 7000 samples/s and passed through an ideal reconstruction filter with cutoff fs/2. Find the Nyquist rate, decide whether aliasing occurs, and list the output frequencies.

  1. fm = 4000 Hz, so the Nyquist rate is 2 × 4000 = 8000 samples/s (Nyquist interval 125 µs).
  2. 7000 < 8000 samples/s, so aliasing occurs. Folding frequency fs/2 = 3500 Hz.
  3. Sampled spectrum: 1 kHz gives lines at 1, 7 − 1 = 6, 7 + 1 = 8 kHz, …; 4 kHz gives 4, 7 − 4 = 3, 7 + 4 = 11 and 14 − 4 = 10 kHz, ….
  4. The 3500 Hz filter keeps 1 kHz and 3 kHz only.
  5. Check at n = 1: 2 cos(2π·4/7) = 2 cos(3.590) = −1.802 V and 2 cos(2π·3/7) = 2 cos(2.693) = −1.802 V, the same sample.

Answer. Nyquist rate 8000 samples/s; aliasing occurs; the output is 3 cos(2π·1000t) plus a false 2 cos(2π·3000t).

Worked example 2

Question. Speech bandlimited to 3.4 kHz is sampled at 8 kHz and rebuilt by a zero-order hold. (a) Find the guard band and the stopband edge of the anti-aliasing filter. (b) Where does a 10.5 kHz interferer appear? (c) Find the hold loss at 3.4 kHz.

  1. Guard band = fs − 2fm = 8000 − 6800 = 1200 Hz. Stopband edge = fs − fm = 8000 − 3400 = 4600 Hz.
  2. Interferer: fa = |10 500 − 8000| = 2500 Hz, inside 0 to 4000 Hz and inside the speech band.
  3. Hold: τ = Ts = 125 µs, so the relative gain is sinc(3400/8000) = sinc(0.425) = sin(1.3352)/1.3352 = 0.9724/1.3352 = 0.728.
  4. Loss = 20 log10(0.728) = −2.75 dB.

Answer. Guard band 1.2 kHz, stopband by 4.6 kHz; the interferer appears at 2.5 kHz; the hold loses 2.75 dB at 3.4 kHz, which a 1/sinc equalizer restores.

Exam traps

  • fs = 2fm is the limit, not a design value: it leaves no guard band and can miss a tone at fm.
  • The alias must lie in 0 to fs/2. For 18 kHz at 8 kHz one subtraction (10 kHz) is not enough; two give 2 kHz.
  • Do not swap the Nyquist rate 2fm (samples/s), the Nyquist interval 1/(2fm) (s) and the folding frequency fs/2 (Hz).
  • The aperture effect is a droop of the wanted band by sinc(fτ), not aliasing; it shrinks with τ, while aliasing depends only on fs.

4.2 PAM, PWM and PPM

Analog pulse modulation sends a pulse every Ts seconds and lets one pulse property follow the sample: height in PAM, width in PWM, position in PPM. The pulse rate stays fixed at the sampling rate. PAM is the first step towards PCM and TDM; PWM drives motors, lamps and class-D amplifiers; PPM appears in optical and infrared links. This chapter shows how each is made, what bandwidth it needs, and why constant-height pulses resist amplitude noise.

You need: sampling and the aperture effect and the spectrum of a rectangular pulse.

PAM: pulse height follows the sample

Take a pulse train with period Ts = 1/fs, pulse width τ and duty cycle d = τ/Ts, with fs ≥ 2fm. In pulse amplitude modulation (PAM) pulse n has height m(nTs), or A + m(nTs) when an offset A keeps every pulse positive. Natural PAM lets the top follow m(t) during the pulse; flat-top PAM holds the sample for τ, so a sample-and-hold makes it.

Flat-top PAM is the sampled message filtered by one rectangular pulse, so its spectrum is

P(f) = fs τ sinc(fτ) Σ M(f − kfs)

Each copy of M(f), at 0, ±fs, …, is shaped by τ sinc(fτ), which has its first null at 1/τ. Natural PAM instead gives undistorted copies weighted by d sinc(kd), so only flat-top PAM has the aperture effect.

The receiver lowpass filters at fm; for flat-top PAM it adds a 1/sinc equalizer if τ is not small. PAM is still analog: it becomes digital only after quantization and coding in PCM.

PWM and PPM: timing carries the sample

Pulse width modulation (PWM), also called pulse duration modulation (PDM), keeps the height fixed and sets the width τn = τ0 + kw m(nTs), where τ0 is the unmodulated width and kw the width sensitivity in s/V. A comparator against a sawtooth of period Ts makes it: the output is high until the sawtooth crosses the message, so one edge (here the leading edge) stays fixed and the trailing edge moves. A lowpass filter recovers m(t), because the average of a PWM wave is proportional to its duty cycle.

Pulse position modulation (PPM) keeps height and width fixed and shifts the whole pulse from its nominal position by kp m(nTs). It is made from PWM: differentiate the PWM wave, keep only the trailing-edge spikes, and let each trigger a monostable that gives one short pulse of fixed width. That is one pulse per sampling period, placed where the PWM pulse ended. The receiver needs a timing reference, usually from a sync pulse, to measure each shift.

Bandwidth and noise compared

The bandwidth of a pulse train is set by its fastest feature. PAM pulses of width τ need roughly 1/(2τ) to 1/τ. PWM and PPM carry the message in edge timing, so the edges must be sharp: a rise time tr needs about 1/(2tr) of bandwidth, and tr must be far shorter than the smallest width or shift to be resolved. PPM, with the narrowest pulses, needs the most.

With constant height, a receiver can slice the pulses at half height and ignore most amplitude noise; noise then matters only through the timing jitter it causes on a sloping edge. PWM wastes power because wide pulses carry energy without extra information, which PPM avoids. Since every PAM pulse uses only τ of each Ts, other channels can fill the gaps: that is time division multiplexing.

Worked example

Question. A 1 kHz tone is sampled at 8 kHz and sent as flat-top PAM with 25 µs pulses. Find Ts, the duty cycle, the first spectral null and the aperture loss at 1 kHz and at 3.4 kHz.

  1. Ts = 1/fs = 1/(8 × 10³ Hz) = 125 × 10⁻⁶ s = 125 µs.
  2. d = τ/Ts = 25/125 = 0.20, or 20 %.
  3. First null of τ sinc(fτ): f = 1/τ = 1/(25 × 10⁻⁶ s) = 40 × 10³ Hz = 40 kHz.
  4. Loss at 1 kHz: sinc(1000 × 25 × 10⁻⁶) = sinc(0.025) = 0.9990, that is −0.009 dB.
  5. Loss at 3.4 kHz: sinc(0.085) = 0.9882, that is −0.10 dB. Compare the 2.75 dB of a full-width hold (τ = Ts) in the sampling chapter.

Answer. Ts = 125 µs, d = 20 %, first null 40 kHz; with τ only a fifth of Ts the aperture loss is under 0.1 dB, so no equalizer is needed.

Question. PWM uses fs = 10 kHz, τ0 = 50 µs and kw = 10 µs/V, with the leading edge at the start of each slot. Four samples are +2, +1, −1 and −3 V. Find each width, each trailing-edge time, and the shift of each PPM pulse made from those edges.

Exam traps

  • PAM is not PCM: PAM is analog until it is quantized and coded.
  • PPM shifts a fixed-width pulse; PWM changes the width. PPM from PWM uses one edge per period, not both edges, which would double the pulse rate.
  • Duty cycle is τ/Ts (dimensionless), not τ·Ts.
  • The first null of a rectangular pulse of width τ is at 1/τ, not 1/(2τ).

Analyze Why are PWM and PPM less sensitive to amplitude noise than PAM? 4 marks

Their height is constant, so the receiver slices at half height and reads only edge times. Noise affects them only through the jitter it adds on a sloping edge, which sharp edges keep small. PAM puts the message in the height, so the same noise adds straight to the sample.

4.3 Uniform and non-uniform quantization; companding

Quantization rounds each sample to one of L allowed levels, so a sample becomes one of a finite set of values. A uniform quantizer spaces the levels equally, so a quiet signal gets the same absolute error as a loud one and a much worse signal-to-noise ratio. Speech is mostly quiet, so telephony gives small samples fine steps and large samples coarse steps; companding is the practical way to do this. Telephone PCM and VoIP gateways still use the μ-law and A-law curves.

You need: sampling, and mean-square value and variance (see Probability and random variables review).

Uniform quantization and its noise

A uniform quantizer covering −V to +V with L = 2ⁿ levels, for an n-bit code, has step size Δ = 2V/L. Each sample x is replaced by the nearest level q(x); the quantization error e = q(x) − x lies between −Δ/2 and +Δ/2 while |x| ≤ V. A sample beyond ±V is clipped to the end level, which is overload distortion.

For a busy signal and a fine step, e is equally likely anywhere in one step, with density 1/Δ. Its mean is zero and its mean square is ∫ e²(1/Δ) de from −Δ/2 to Δ/2 = Δ²/12. This granular noise is present even on a perfect channel.

Pq = Δ²/12, Δ = 2V/2ⁿ

Pq is the quantization noise power in V² (into 1 Ω), V the peak of the range and n the bits per sample.

The signal-to-quantization-noise ratio is SQNR = Px/Pq, with Px the mean-square signal. Substituting Δ gives SQNR = 3·2²ⁿ·Px/V², or in decibels

SQNR = 4.77 + 6.02n − 20 log(V/σ) dB

σ = √Px is the rms value of the signal and V/σ its loading factor. For a full-scale sine, V/σ = √2 and the rule becomes the familiar 6.02n + 1.76 dB. For speech peaks held at 4σ, it becomes 6.02n − 7.27 dB. Each extra bit adds 6.02 dB; halving the signal costs 6.02 dB.

Non-uniform quantization and companding

A non-uniform quantizer uses small steps near zero and large steps at large |x|, so the error grows with the signal and SQNR stays nearly constant. The usual way to build one is companding: a compressor y = C(x) lifts small inputs, a uniform quantizer follows, and an expander C⁻¹ at the receiver undoes the curve. Near zero the compressor slope C′(0) divides the effective step by that factor. Telephone codecs build the smooth curves from 8 straight segments per polarity.

y = ln(1 + μ|x|/V) / ln(1 + μ)

This is the μ-law for x ≥ 0 (odd symmetry for x < 0), with y the compressed output normalised to 1. μ = 255 is the standard value, used in North America and Japan. Its slope at zero is μ/ln(1 + μ) = 255/5.545 = 46.0, a 33.3 dB gain for small signals.

The A-law, standard in Europe, India and on international links, is linear for small inputs and logarithmic above: y = A(|x|/V)/(1 + ln A) for |x|/V ≤ 1/A, and y = [1 + ln(A|x|/V)]/(1 + ln A) for 1/A ≤ |x|/V ≤ 1, with A = 87.6, so 1/A = 0.0114 and 1 + ln A = 5.473. Its small-signal slope is 87.6/5.473 = 16.0, or 24.1 dB. The two laws are not compatible, so calls between the regions are converted. Some syllabi call non-uniform quantization logarithmic quantization.

What companding buys and what it cannot do

For large inputs (μ|x| ≫ V) the μ-law quantization noise becomes proportional to signal power, and SQNR ≈ 3L²/[ln(1 + μ)]². With 8 bits that is 3 × 65 536/30.75 = 6394, or 38.1 dB, whatever the level. Uniform 8-bit PCM gives 49.9 dB at full scale but only 9.9 dB for a sine 40 dB down; μ-law gives about 34 dB there. Companding trades about 12 dB at full scale for a flat SQNR over a 40 dB range of talkers.

Companding cannot stop overload: a sample beyond ±V is still clipped. The noise left during silence, idle-channel noise, is reduced by the fine steps near zero but not removed. And Δ²/12 is quantization noise only; thermal noise and bit errors add their own powers.

4.4 Pulse code modulation (PCM)

Pulse code modulation (PCM) sends each quantized sample as a fixed-length binary code word. The message is filtered, sampled, rounded to one of L levels and written as n bits, so the bit rate is Rb = n fs: telephone speech at 8 kHz and 8 bits gives 64 kbit/s. Because the channel carries only 0s and 1s, repeaters can regenerate clean pulses, and noise does not pile up along the route. PCM is the base of digital telephony and of every audio file format.

You need: sampling and aliasing and quantization and companding.

The PCM chain and its bit rate

  1. An anti-aliasing lowpass filter limits m(t) to fm.
  2. A sampler takes values at fs ≥ 2fm.
  3. A quantizer rounds each sample to one of L = 2ⁿ levels (uniform, or companded for speech).
  4. An encoder writes the level number as an n-bit code word and sends the bits, each lasting Tb = 1/Rb.
  5. Regenerative repeaters along the line decide each bit and send it on as a fresh pulse.
  6. The receiver decodes each word back to its level, and a reconstruction lowpass filter smooths the steps into m(t).

Rb = n fs, Tb = 1/Rb

Rb in bit/s, n bits per sample, fs in samples/s, Tb in seconds.

The figure traces three bits through the chain: a 1 kHz tone of peak 3.5 V sampled at 8 kHz, a 3-bit quantizer over ±4 V (Δ = 1 V, levels ±0.5, ±1.5, ±2.5, ±3.5 V) and natural binary codes 000 for −3.5 V up to 111 for +3.5 V. Some syllabi add a Gray code, in which neighbouring levels differ in one bit, so a single bit error moves the sample by only one step.

Bandwidth and the SNR that n buys

Two bandwidth figures are easy to mix. With ideal Nyquist pulses and no intersymbol interference, the minimum baseband bandwidth is Bmin = Rb/2 = n fs/2; at the Nyquist rate this is n fm, so PCM needs n times the message bandwidth. Rectangular NRZ pulses have their first spectral null at Rb, so their main lobe needs Rb. For 64 kbit/s speech: 32 kHz minimum, 64 kHz to the first null.

Each extra bit doubles L and adds 6.02 dB of SQNR, but also adds fs bit/s of rate and fs/2 Hz of bandwidth. So SQNR in dB grows linearly with bandwidth, an exponential trade of bandwidth for SNR that analog FM cannot match. To meet a target, solve 6.02n + 1.76 ≥ SQNR for the smallest whole n.

Regeneration and channel errors

A regenerative repeater equalizes the pulse, recovers the clock, decides 0 or 1 and sends a fresh pulse. Noise picked up on one span is discarded instead of amplified, so a long line behaves like its worst span. A wrong decision is a bit error and travels on; regeneration is not error correction.

A bit error moves the decoded sample to a wrong level, and an error in the most significant bit moves it by half the range: a click. For a uniformly distributed signal and natural binary code, the output SNR including bit errors with probability Pe is

(S/N)o = L² / (1 + 4L²Pe)

L = 2ⁿ. With no errors it is L² (the SQNR of a uniform signal, 6.02n dB); the error term takes over once 4L²Pe nears 1.

4.5 Differential PCM and adaptive DPCM

Differential pulse code modulation (DPCM) sends the difference between each sample and a prediction made from past samples, instead of the sample itself. Speech and most sensor signals change little from one sample to the next, so the difference is small and needs fewer bits for the same quality. The receiver adds the same prediction back. Adaptive DPCM (ADPCM) also adjusts the quantizer step to the signal’s short-term level; it carries telephone speech at 32 kbit/s, half the PCM rate.

You need: PCM and correlation between samples (see Random processes, stationarity, correlation and power spectral density).

Predict, subtract, quantize

Let x[n] be the sample at time index n, x̂[n] its prediction from earlier samples and x̃[n] the reconstructed sample that both ends can compute. DPCM quantizes the prediction error e[n] = x[n] − x̂[n] to q[n] and sends the level number as a code word of N bits, from L = 2ᴺ levels. The bit rate is N fs, the PCM formula with N bits for the difference. N can be smaller than the PCM word length because e[n] has a smaller spread than x[n].

e[n] = x[n] − x̂[n], x̃[n] = x̂[n] + q[n]

The reconstructed sample differs from the input only by the quantization error of the difference: x[n] − x̃[n] = e[n] − q[n].

The simplest predictor uses the last reconstructed sample, x̂[n] = x̃[n − 1]. For a signal with variance σ² and correlation ρ between neighbouring samples, the error variance is then σe² = 2σ²(1 − ρ). The best single-coefficient predictor x̂[n] = a x̃[n − 1] uses a = ρ and gives σe² = σ²(1 − ρ²). Practical speech coders use several past samples.

Why the predictor sits inside the loop

The figure shows the transmitter and the receiver separately. The receiver has only q[n], so it can form only x̃. The transmitter therefore contains a copy of the receiver, its local decoder: it adds q[n] to x̂[n] to get x̃[n], and predicts the next sample from x̃, never from the true x.

  1. Predict x̂[n] from stored reconstructed samples.
  2. Subtract: e[n] = x[n] − x̂[n].
  3. Quantize e[n] to q[n] and send its code.
  4. Both ends add: x̃[n] = x̂[n] + q[n], and store it for the next prediction.

Because both predictors see the same x̃, they stay identical, and the error in each output sample is just one quantization error, e[n] − q[n]. If the transmitter predicted from x instead (open loop), the receiver’s predictions would drift away, and quantization errors would add up from sample to sample.

Prediction gain and ADPCM

Gp = 10 log10(σ²/σe²) dB

Gp is the prediction gain: the SQNR improvement over PCM with the same number of bits, since the quantizer now covers a range set by σe, not σ. With x̂[n] = x̃[n − 1] it is 10 log[1/(2(1 − ρ))]; each 6.02 dB of gain saves one bit.

The gain is not automatic. For ρ = 0.5 the simple predictor gives 0 dB, and for ρ = 0 it gives −3 dB: subtracting an unrelated sample doubles the variance. Telephone speech sampled at 8 kHz typically has ρ of about 0.85 to 0.9.

ADPCM makes the quantizer step Δ[n] follow the short-term level: loud passages get a large step to avoid overload, quiet ones a small step to cut granular noise. The step and often the predictor coefficients are computed from past code words, so the receiver tracks them with no side information. The ITU-T G.726 coder sends 4-bit differences at 8 kHz, 32 kbit/s. This is adaptation over time, not companding, which is a fixed curve.

4.6 Delta and adaptive delta modulation

Delta modulation (DM) is the simplest differential coder: one bit per sample. At each clock tick it compares the input with a staircase built from past bits and sends 1 if the input is higher, 0 if lower; the staircase then moves one step Δ up or down. The hardware is a comparator, a clock and an integrator. Adaptive delta modulation (ADM) changes Δ with the signal slope, which makes DM usable for speech in low-cost voice links.

You need: DPCM, of which DM is the 1-bit case, and the slope of a sinusoid.

How delta modulation works

The transmitter keeps a staircase approximation x̃ of the input. At sample n it compares x[n] with the previous staircase value x̃[n − 1] and sends b[n] = 1 if x[n] ≥ x̃[n − 1], else 0. The staircase then moves: x̃[n] = x̃[n − 1] + Δ for a 1 and − Δ for a 0. This is DPCM with the prediction x̂[n] = x̃[n − 1] and a two-level quantizer ±Δ.

Rb = fs

One bit per sample, so the bit rate equals the sampling rate. To track speech, fs is many times the Nyquist rate (32 to 64 kHz for telephone speech).

The receiver rebuilds the same staircase from the bits with an accumulator (an integrator in analog form), then a lowpass filter at fm smooths it. It needs only the bit clock: there are no word boundaries. Because each bit only says “higher” or “lower”, the staircase never settles on a steady input: when the gap is smaller than Δ, a step overshoots and the next one comes back.

Slope overload and granular noise

The staircase can rise at most Δ per sample, a slope of Δfs volts per second. When the input rises faster, the staircase falls behind and sends a long run of equal bits while the gap grows: slope overload. For x(t) = A sin(2πft) the steepest slope is 2πfA, at the zero crossings, so there is no overload when

Δ fs ≥ 2π f A

Δ in volts, fs in samples/s, f the tone frequency in Hz, A the peak amplitude in volts.

On a slow or flat input the staircase hunts up and down by Δ around it: granular noise, the DM form of quantization noise. A large Δ cures overload but raises granular noise; a small Δ does the reverse. The figure and the second worked example show both on one tone: the staircase hunts at the peak and lags on the falling slope.

Adaptive DM and the comparison with PCM

ADM changes the step from the bit pattern. A run of equal bits means the staircase is falling behind, so the step is multiplied by a factor K > 1 (often 1.5 or 2); alternating bits mean it is hunting, so the step is divided by K, within limits Δmin and Δmax. The receiver sees the same bits and repeats the same rule, so no extra bits are sent. CVSD, continuously variable slope DM, is the version used for voice.

For a tone at the largest amplitude that avoids overload, DM gives SNR = 3fs³/(8π²f2W), where W is the receiver filter bandwidth. With f = 1 kHz, W = 4 kHz and fs = 64 kHz this is 2490, or 34.0 dB, while 8-bit PCM at the same 64 kbit/s gives 49.9 dB. Doubling fs adds only 9 dB to DM. So DM wins on simplicity and robustness to single bit errors (one error moves the output by only 2Δ), and PCM wins on quality per bit.

4.7 Time division multiplexing

Time division multiplexing (TDM) lets many signals share one link by giving each a repeating slice of time, its slot. Each channel uses the whole bandwidth of the link, but only during its own slot. A frame marker tells the receiver where the round of slots starts, so it can hand each sample back to its channel. Digital telephone exchanges and trunks work this way: an E1 line at 2.048 Mbit/s carries 32 slots, 30 of them speech, and a T1 line carries 24 calls at 1.544 Mbit/s.

You need: PAM and PCM code words.

Frame, slot and the commutator

A commutator, a rotating switch in the classic picture and a multiplexer chip today, visits the N channels in turn and takes one sample from each per revolution. One revolution is a frame, and its duration is Tf = 1/fs, because each channel must be sampled at fs. For speech sampled at 8 kHz, Tf = 125 µs whatever the number of channels. Inside the frame each channel owns one time slot, and a synchronization bit or word marks the frame start. A guard time between slots absorbs timing error at the cost of some capacity.

All channels should be bandlimited by their own anti-aliasing filters before the commutator. If channels have different bandwidths, a wideband one can take several slots per frame (super-commutation) and a slow one a slot every few frames (sub-commutation). The receiver’s decommutator must turn in step with the transmitter’s, which is the whole difficulty of TDM.

PAM-TDM, PCM-TDM and the bit rate

In PAM-TDM each slot holds one analog sample. N channels sampled at fs give N fs pulses per second, and keeping them apart needs a minimum bandwidth of N fs/2; at the Nyquist rate that is N fm, the same total as FDM without guard bands. In PCM-TDM each slot holds an n-bit word, either kept together (word interleaving) or spread one bit per round (bit interleaving).

Rb = (N n + S) fs

N data channels of n bits each, plus S framing and signalling bits per frame, at fs frames per second.

TDM, which multiplexes signals on one link, should not be confused with TDMA, a multiple-access method in which separate users take turns on a shared radio channel.

Synchronization, timing errors and crosstalk

A single sync bit per frame is a weak marker, because data bits at the same position can imitate it for a while; T1 therefore spreads a fixed pattern over 12 or 24 frames, and E1 sends a 7-bit alignment word in slot 0 of alternate frames. The bit clock is recovered from transitions in the line signal, which the line code must supply.

If the receiver samples early it reads the tail of the previous slot; if late, the start of the next. Either way one channel leaks into its neighbour, which is TDM crosstalk; in PAM-TDM it also comes from a channel too narrow to let one pulse die out before the next. Losing frame alignment shifts every slot, so channel 1 is delivered as channel 2 until sync is found again. A frame slip is different: a whole frame is lost or repeated because the two clocks drift apart. TDM shares time, while frequency division multiplexing (see Frequency division multiplexing) shares frequency.

Each sampling instant comes a little earlier than the last, so the error grows. Once it exceeds the guard time, the receiver reads the tail of the previous slot and one channel leaks into another, until the sync pattern pulls the timing back.

A single bit can be imitated by data at the same position, so the receiver may lock on a false position. A longer pattern, checked over several frames, is very unlikely to appear by chance, at the cost of a few extra bits.

4.8 Line coding: NRZ, RZ, Manchester and their spectra

A line code is the rule that turns a bit stream into voltage levels on a wire. The receiver must recover both the bits and the bit clock from that waveform, and the waveform may pass through transformers and AC-coupled amplifiers that block DC. NRZ, RZ, bipolar and Manchester codes trade three things: DC content, clock content and bandwidth. Their power spectral densities show the trade at a glance, and you meet the codes in PCM trunks, TDM backplanes and older Ethernet.

You need: the PCM bit stream from PCM, TDM line rates from Time division multiplexing, and power spectral density (see Random processes, stationarity, correlation and power spectral density).

Mapping bits to levels

Bits last Tb = 1/Rb seconds and V is the pulse level. Unipolar codes use 0 and +V; polar codes use +V and −V; bipolar or AMI (alternate mark inversion) codes send 0 as 0 V and each 1 as +V and −V in turn.

  • NRZ (non-return-to-zero): the level lasts the whole bit. Polar NRZ-L: 1 = +V, 0 = −V. NRZ-I changes level at the start of each 1, so data sits in transitions.
  • RZ (return-to-zero): the pulse lasts Tb/2 and the line returns to 0 V for the second half. Polar RZ: 1 = +V then 0, 0 = −V then 0.
  • Manchester (split-phase, biphase): each bit has a transition at its centre. In the IEEE 802.3 convention used here, 1 = low-to-high and 0 = high-to-low; some books use the opposite, so state yours. Differential Manchester always has the centre transition and carries the data in the presence (0) or absence (1) of a transition at the start of the bit.

Manchester’s two half-bit levels always cancel, so its sin² factor forces zero power at DC whatever the data, and its peak sits near 0.74Rb. AMI also has no DC, because successive 1s cancel, and keeps the NRZ bandwidth. Unipolar RZ adds discrete lines at DC and at multiples of Rb, which makes clock extraction easy but wastes power. Integrating each PSD shows that about 90 % of the power of polar NRZ lies below its first null.

Clock recovery and the choice of code

The receiver samples each bit at its centre, using a clock recovered, often by a phase-locked loop, from transitions in the signal. A long run of equal bits in NRZ has no transitions, so the clock drifts; NRZ links add scrambling or a coding rule to break long runs. Polar RZ has a pulse edge in every bit. Manchester has a guaranteed centre transition in every bit, so it is self-clocking, and with no DC it passes through transformers: hence its use in 10 Mbit/s Ethernet.

Swapping the two wires inverts every level. NRZ-L and plain Manchester then read every bit inverted; NRZ-I and differential Manchester, which carry data in transitions, read the same bits. AMI tolerates inversion too, and a 1 with the same polarity as the previous 1, a bipolar violation, reveals an error; its weakness is long runs of 0s, which HDB3 (in E1) and B8ZS (in T1) replace by patterns with deliberate violations.

Worked example

Question. The bits 1 0 1 1 0 are sent at 1 kbit/s with level V. Give the levels in each half-bit for polar NRZ, polar RZ, AMI and Manchester (1 = low-to-high), and find Tb and the first spectral null of each code. Which codes have no DC for any data?

  1. Tb = 1/Rb = 1/1000 s = 1 ms, so each half-bit lasts 0.5 ms.

Exam traps

  • Manchester’s first null is at 2Rb, not Rb: each bit has two half-bit levels.
  • Unipolar NRZ is not DC-free: with equally likely bits its mean is V/2.
  • Plain Manchester is not immune to swapped wires; differential Manchester and NRZ-I are.
  • AMI has the NRZ bandwidth (first null Rb) although its pulses are RZ, because the sin²(πfTb) factor sets the null.

Takeaways

  1. Sample at fs ≥ 2fm with a guard band; anything above fs/2 that reaches the sampler folds to |f − kfs| and cannot be removed later.
  2. Flat-top sampling droops the band by sinc(fτ): 2.75 dB at 3.4 kHz for a full 125 µs hold, under 0.1 dB for a 25 µs pulse.
  3. PAM, PWM and PPM carry the sample in height, width or position; constant-height PWM and PPM buy noise immunity with bandwidth.
  4. Uniform quantization: Pq = Δ²/12 and 6.02 dB per bit; a quiet signal loses 6 dB for every halving of its level.
  5. μ-law and A-law companding hold an 8-bit SQNR near 34 to 38 dB over a 40 dB range of input levels, at the cost of about 12 dB at full scale.
  6. PCM sends n fs bit/s (64 kbit/s for telephone speech) and needs at least half that in hertz; regeneration removes noise but not bit errors.
  7. DPCM and ADPCM send prediction errors; with neighbour correlation 0.9 the gain is 7 dB, about one bit, and ADPCM halves the speech rate to 32 kbit/s.
  8. Delta modulation is 1-bit DPCM: slope overload unless Δfs ≥ 2πfA, granular noise when Δ is large; ADM adapts Δ from the bits.
  9. TDM frames last 1/fs; count every bit with (N n + S) fs: T1 is 1.544 Mbit/s and E1 2.048 Mbit/s.
  10. Line codes trade DC, clock and bandwidth: NRZ is narrowest, Manchester is DC-free and self-clocking at twice the bandwidth, AMI is DC-free at NRZ bandwidth.

Understand State the sampling theorem for a lowpass signal and explain, with a spectrum sketch, why sampling below 2fm causes aliasing that cannot be removed later. 10 marks

Points to cover. Define fm, fs, Ts; state fs ≥ 2fm, the Nyquist rate and interval; Spectrum of the sampled signal: copies of M(f) at every multiple of fs; If fs < 2fm the copies overlap; the overlap is the alias; A tone above fs/2 appears at |f − kfs|, with a numerical example; Once folded, no filter can separate the alias: hence the anti-aliasing filter.

Model answer.

  1. Statement. A signal m(t) whose spectrum M(f) is zero for |f| > fm is determined completely by its samples m(nTs) if the sampling rate fs = 1/Ts ≥ 2fm. The minimum rate 2fm is the Nyquist rate and the largest interval 1/(2fm) is the Nyquist interval.
  2. Ideal sampling. Model the sampler as multiplication by an impulse train: s(t) = m(t) Σ δ(t − nTs) = Σ m(nTs) δ(t − nTs). The impulse train is periodic with period Ts; all its Fourier-series coefficients equal 1/Ts, so its transform is fs Σ δ(f − kfs).
  3. Spectrum of the samples. Multiplication in time is convolution in frequency, so S(f) = fs Σ M(f − kfs). Sketch: the baseband triangle from −fm to fm, and identical triangles centred at ±fs, ±2fs, … .
  4. Condition for no overlap. The copy at fs starts at fs − fm. It clears the baseband when fs − fm ≥ fm, that is fs ≥ 2fm. The gap fs − 2fm is the guard band, and a lowpass filter of gain Ts with its cutoff anywhere from fm to fs − fm returns M(f) exactly.
  5. Overlap when fs < 2fm. The copy at fs now reaches below fm, so its upper part lies on top of the baseband. Seen from the baseband, every component above fs/2 folds about fs/2 and appears at fa = |f − kfs|, with k chosen so that 0 ≤ fa ≤ fs/2.
  6. Numerical example. Take x(t) = 2 cos(2π·3000t), so fm = 3 kHz and the Nyquist rate is 6 kHz.

Model answer.

  2. Transmitter. x[n] and x̂[n] enter a subtractor; e[n] is quantized to q[n] and encoded in N bits. A local decoder adds q[n] to x̂[n] to form x̃[n] = x̂[n] + q[n], which goes into the predictor for the next sample.
  3. Receiver. The decoder recovers q[n]; an adder forms x̃[n] = x̂[n] + q[n] with an identical predictor fed from its own output; a lowpass filter smooths x̃.

Remember Describe delta modulation with a block diagram, and define slope overload and granular noise with the condition to avoid overload. 8 marks

Points to cover. 1-bit coder: comparator, sampler, integrator in feedback; Staircase moves ±Δ per sample; Rb = fs; Slope overload and the condition Δfs ≥ 2πfA; Granular noise and the step-size trade-off; ADM as the remedy.

Model answer.

  1. Transmitter. Draw x(t) into a comparator whose other input is the staircase x̃; the comparator output is sampled at fs to give one bit b[n]; the bit drives an integrator (accumulator) that adds +Δ for a 1 and −Δ for a 0, closing the loop.
  2. Receiver. An identical integrator rebuilds the staircase from the bits, and a lowpass filter at fm smooths it. Since there is one bit per sample, Rb = fs and no word framing is needed.
  3. Rule. b[n] = 1 if x[n] ≥ x̃[n − 1], else 0; then x̃[n] = x̃[n − 1] ± Δ. DM is DPCM with a 1-bit quantizer and the prediction x̃[n − 1].
  4. Slope overload. The staircase can change by at most Δ per sample, a slope of Δfs. For x(t) = A sin(2πft) the steepest slope is 2πfA, so overload is avoided only if Δfs ≥ 2πfA. Otherwise the staircase lags and a long run of equal bits appears.
  5. Example. A = 2 V, f = 1 kHz, Δ = 0.1 V, fs = 32 kHz: the input slope is 2π × 1000 × 2 = 12 566 V/s and the staircase slope 0.1 × 32 000 = 3200 V/s, so the coder overloads. It needs fs ≥ 12 566/0.1 = 125.7 kHz, or Δ ≥ 12 566/32 000 = 0.393 V.
  6. Granular noise. On a flat or slow input the staircase hunts ±Δ around it, giving a noise of peak about Δ. A large Δ cures overload but raises granular noise; a small Δ does the opposite. So a fixed-step DM must compromise.
  7. Remedy. Adaptive DM multiplies Δ by K > 1 after a run of equal bits and divides it after alternating bits, between set limits; the receiver applies the same rule to the same bits.
  8. Closing check. With Δ = 0.393 V and 32 kHz, Δfs = 12 576 V/s ≥ 12 566 V/s, so the overload condition is just met.

Evaluate Explain why companding is used in speech PCM. Draw the μ-law and A-law compression curves, and compare the SQNR of uniform and μ-law 8-bit PCM for weak and strong talkers. 12 marks

Points to cover. Uniform PCM loses 6 dB per halving of level, so weak talkers suffer; Compressor, uniform quantizer, expander; small signals get fine steps; μ-law formula, μ = 255; A-law formula, A = 87.6; the curves; SQNR table against level for uniform and μ-law; μ-law large-signal SQNR ≈ 3L²/[ln(1 + μ)]² = 38 dB; Costs: about 12 dB at full scale, non-compatible laws, overload still possible.

Model answer.

  1. The problem. Uniform quantization gives the same Δ²/12 noise to every sample, so SQNR is proportional to signal power. Talkers differ by 30 dB or more, and speech is small most of the time, so a quiet talker on 8-bit uniform PCM would get only 10 to 20 dB.
  2. Companding. A compressor y = C(x) before a uniform quantizer lifts small inputs; an expander C⁻¹ after the decoder restores them. The effective step at an input x is Δ/C′(x): fine near zero, coarse near full scale. With a logarithmic C, the noise grows in proportion to the signal and SQNR becomes nearly independent of level.
  3. μ-law. y = ln(1 + μ|x|/V)/ln(1 + μ) with odd symmetry, μ = 255 (North America, Japan). Slope at zero 255/ln 256 = 46.0: small signals see a step 46 times finer, a 33.3 dB gain.
  4. A-law. y = A(|x|/V)/(1 + ln A) for |x|/V ≤ 1/A, and [1 + ln(A|x|/V)]/(1 + ln A) above, A = 87.6 (Europe, India). Slope at zero 87.6/5.473 = 16.0, a 24.1 dB gain.
  5. Curves. Both rise steeply from zero and bend over; sample values: μ-law 0.01 → 0.229, 0.1 → 0.591, 0.5 → 0.876; A-law 0.01 → 0.160, 0.1 → 0.579, 0.5 → 0.873.

Glossary

A-law
Companding law, linear below 1/A and logarithmic above, with A = 87.6, used in Europe and India.
AMI
Alternate mark inversion: a bipolar line code sending 0 as 0 V and 1s as alternate +V and −V.
Aperture effect
Droop of the message band by sinc(fτ) caused by holding samples; corrected by a 1/sinc equalizer.
Code word
The n-bit binary number that stands for one quantized sample in PCM.
Compander
A compressor at the transmitter and an expander at the receiver that together give non-uniform quantization.
Crosstalk
Leakage of one TDM channel into a neighbouring slot through timing error or pulse spreading.
Duty cycle
τ/Ts, the fraction of each period a pulse occupies.
Flat-top sampling
PAM in which each pulse is held at the sample value for τ; causes the aperture effect.
Folding frequency
fs/2, about which components above it fold down as aliases.
Frame
One complete round of TDM slots, lasting 1/fs.
Frame alignment word
A fixed bit pattern by which a TDM receiver finds the start of each frame.
Granular noise
The ±Δ hunting of a delta-modulation staircase around a slow input.
Guard band
The gap fs − 2fm between the baseband and the first spectral copy, where filters roll off.
Idle-channel noise
Quantization noise heard when the input is silent.
Loading factor
V/σ, the ratio of the quantizer range to the rms signal; it sets the SQNR for a given n.
Local decoder
The copy of the receiver inside a DPCM or DM transmitter that forms the reconstructed samples for prediction.
Manchester code
A line code with a transition at the centre of every bit; DC-free and self-clocking.
Natural sampling
PAM in which the pulse top follows the message during the pulse.
Nyquist rate
The lowest sampling rate, 2fm, that avoids aliasing for a signal bandlimited to fm.
Overload
Clipping of samples beyond the quantizer range ±V.
Power spectral density
The distribution of a signal’s power over frequency, in W/Hz.
Prediction gain
The SQNR improvement of DPCM over PCM, 10 log(σ²/σe²).
Quantization noise
The rounding error of a quantizer; Δ²/12 in power for a uniform quantizer.
Regenerative repeater
A unit that decides each received bit and sends a fresh pulse, so noise does not accumulate.
Sampling
Taking the values of a continuous signal at instants Ts = 1/fs apart.
Slope overload
Delta-modulation distortion when the input changes faster than Δfs.
Step size
The spacing Δ between neighbouring quantization levels.
μ-law
Logarithmic companding law with μ = 255, used in North America and Japan.
