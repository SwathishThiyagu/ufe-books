2.1 AM: modulation index, spectrum and power

Amplitude modulation (AM) makes a high-frequency carrier grow and shrink in step with a low-frequency message. The carrier carries the signal through radio; the message rides on its envelope. One number, the modulation index m, sets how deep the swing is, how large the two sidebands become, and how much extra power the transmitter spends. Bandwidth is set by the message frequency, not by m. AM is still used in medium-wave and shortwave broadcast, aviation voice links, and simple envelope-detector receivers. This chapter derives the waveform, line spectrum, power and efficiency for a single tone.

You need: Fourier transform, band-pass signals, and the carrier and sideband picture from Unit 1, Foundations.

Waveform and modulation index

Start with an unmodulated carrier A_c cos(2πf_c t). A_c is the peak carrier amplitude in volts; f_c is the carrier frequency in hertz. The message is a single tone A_m cos(2πf_m t). A_m is the peak message amplitude in volts and f_m is the message frequency in hertz. Conventional AM multiplies the carrier by (1 + m cos 2πf_m t), not by the message alone.

s(t) = A_c [1 + m cos(2π f_m t)] cos(2π f_c t)

s(t) is the transmitted voltage in volts at time t seconds; A_c is peak carrier amplitude in volts; m is the modulation index, dimensionless; f_m is tone frequency in hertz; f_c is carrier frequency in hertz.

The modulation index is m = A_m / A_c. It is dimensionless. Percentage modulation is 100m %. With m = 0.4, modulation is 40 %. The factor 1 + m cos(2πf_m t) is the envelope. When m < 1 it stays positive. When m = 1 it just touches zero. When m > 1 it goes negative, so the envelope crosses zero. An envelope detector then distorts, and a real modulator creates extra sidebands not shown by the single-tone line spectrum. Keep m ≤ 1.

Some syllabi call this conventional AM or linear CW (continuous-wave) modulation. Here linear refers to the spectrum shift, not to power. The message is carried by the sidebands. The carrier carries no message but makes envelope detection easy. Generation circuits are in 2.4; detection is in 2.5.

Line spectrum and bandwidth

Expanding the product gives three lines. The carrier line is at f_c with height A_c. The upper sideband is at f_c + f_m and the lower sideband is at f_c − f_m. Each sideband has height mA_c/2. Heights here are peak volts. A spectrum analyzer may show rms volts, which are peak divided by √2.

s(t) = A_c cos(2πf_c t) + (mA_c/2) cos[2π(f_c + f_m)t] + (mA_c/2) cos[2π(f_c − f_m)t]

the three terms are carrier, upper sideband and lower sideband voltages in volts; all other symbols are as defined above.

For a single tone, bandwidth = (f_c + f_m) − (f_c − f_m) = 2f_m. The sidebands sit symmetrically around f_c. Speech and music contain many tones. An AM station using message frequencies up to W hertz needs bandwidth 2W hertz. With several tones, draw one sideband pair per tone; powers add. See 2.2 where the carrier is removed, and 2.3 where one sideband is removed.

For the worked example, the carrier is at 1000 kHz with height 10 V. The two sidebands are at 999 kHz and 1001 kHz with height 2 V each. The line spacing is 1 kHz, so the occupied band is 2 kHz wide.

Power, efficiency and current

Carrier power depends on peak carrier voltage and load resistance: P_c = A_c²/(2R). A_c is in volts peak, R is in ohms, and P_c is in watts. This is the power of the unmodulated carrier. The sidebands add power. Each sideband carries m²P_c/4, so both together carry m²P_c/2.

P_total = P_c (1 + m²/2)

P_total is total AM power in watts; P_c is unmodulated carrier power in watts; m is the modulation index, dimensionless.

Total power is the sum: P_total = P_c + m²P_c/2 = P_c(1 + m²/2). The carrier is most of the power at small m. Efficiency is useful sideband power divided by total power: η = (m²P_c/2) / [P_c(1 + m²/2)] = m²/(2 + m²). At m = 1, η = 1/3 = 33.3 %; that is the best a single-tone conventional AM can do. At m = 0.4, η = 0.16/2.16 = 7.41 %. Extra carrier power is wasted but buys simple receivers.

If the load is fixed, power is proportional to current squared. With I_c the unmodulated carrier current and I_t the total current, I_t = I_c√(1 + m²/2). Use rms ammeter readings consistently. For several tones, replace m² by Σm_i² in the power formulas. The peak envelope still must satisfy ΣA_i ≤ A_c to avoid overmodulation.

Worked example

Question. A 10 V carrier at 1 MHz is amplitude-modulated by a 1 kHz tone of amplitude 4 V across a 50 Ω load. Find m, the sideband amplitudes, the bandwidth, the carrier power, the total power and the efficiency.

  1. m = A_m/A_c = 4 V / 10 V = 0.4. Percentage modulation is 40 %.
  2. Each sideband amplitude = mA_c/2 = 0.4 × 10 V / 2 = 2 V. They are at 1 MHz ± 1 kHz, that is, 999 kHz and 1001 kHz.
  3. Bandwidth = 2f_m = 2 × 1 kHz = 2 kHz = 2000 Hz.
  4. P_c = A_c²/(2R) = (10 V)² / (2 × 50 Ω) = 100/100 W = 1 W.
  5. Each sideband power = m²P_c/4 = 0.16 × 1 W / 4 = 0.04 W. Total sideband power = 0.08 W.
  6. P_total = P_c(1 + m²/2) = 1 W × (1 + 0.16/2) = 1 W × 1.08 = 1.08 W.
  7. η = m²/(2 + m²) = 0.16/2.16 = 0.074074 = 7.41 %.

Answer. m = 0.4 (40 %), sidebands 2 V each at 999 kHz and 1001 kHz, bandwidth 2 kHz, carrier power 1 W, total power 1.08 W, efficiency 7.41 %.

Exam traps

  • Using peak-to-peak message amplitude as A_m. If the data give 8 V peak-to-peak, then A_m = 4 V, so halve before finding m.
  • Writing each sideband height as mA_c rather than mA_c/2; the factor 1/2 comes from expanding the product of cosines.
  • Applying P_total = P_c(1 + m²/2) when m > 1. That formula is for one tone with m ≤ 1; overmodulation adds extra lines and invalidates the simple result.
  • Calling carrier power total power. Efficiency counts only sidebands; at m = 1 it is 33.3 %, not 100 %.
  • Using f_m instead of 2f_m as the bandwidth for a single tone, or doubling every individual tone in a multitone message instead of using twice the highest message frequency.

2.2 DSB-SC modulation and coherent detection

DSB-SC sends the two sidebands of an amplitude-modulated wave and removes the carrier. The message rides on a carrier that changes sign with the message, so the envelope is not the message. The receiver must generate a local carrier at exactly the same frequency and phase, multiply, and low-pass filter. This saves the carrier power of plain AM. It is used in colour television chrominance, FM stereo difference channels and some data links.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the AM spectrum and Unit 1 for the Fourier transform and multiplication of signals.

The DSB-SC signal and its spectrum

Let m(t) be the message with bandwidth W in hertz, A_c the carrier amplitude in volts, and f_c the carrier frequency in hertz. DSB-SC is s(t) = A_c m(t) cos(2πf_c t). The message has zero average in the usual case, so m(t) has no DC term.

S(f) = (A_c/2)[M(f − f_c) + M(f + f_c)]

S(f) is the Fourier transform of s(t); M(f) is the Fourier transform of m(t); A_c is the carrier amplitude in volts; f_c is the carrier frequency in hertz; W is the message bandwidth in hertz.

There is no impulse at ±f_c. The carrier line is suppressed. The occupied band is f_c − W to f_c + W, so the bandwidth is 2W. Use 2 cos A cos B = cos(A−B) + cos(A+B). For a tone m(t) = A_m cos(2πf_m t), the two sidebands are (A_c A_m/2) cos(2π(f_c − f_m)t) and (A_c A_m/2) cos(2π(f_c + f_m)t). Some syllabi call this DSB/SC or double-sideband suppressed carrier. See Chapter 2.3 for the one-sideband case that halves this bandwidth.

Coherent detection step by step

A coherent detector multiplies s(t) by a local carrier and low-pass filters the result. With local carrier A_lo cos(2πf_c t), the product is A_c A_lo m(t) cos²(2πf_c t) = (A_c A_lo/2)m(t) + (A_c A_lo/2)m(t) cos(4πf_c t). The low-pass filter removes the double-frequency term.

y(t) = (A_c A_lo / 2) m(t)

y(t) is the low-pass filter output in volts; A_c is the received carrier amplitude in volts; A_lo is the local carrier amplitude in volts; m(t) is the message voltage.

With A_lo = 1 and zero phase error, y(t) = (A_c/2)m(t). The output is the baseband message, scaled. The local carrier must match the received carrier in frequency and phase. This is why the method is called coherent detection. Carrier recovery can use a small pilot tone or a Costas loop; the loop locks a local oscillator to the received carrier phase.

An envelope detector does not work here. The DSB-SC wave changes sign, so its envelope is |A_c m(t)|, not m(t). See Chapter 2.5 for the envelope detector used with plain AM.

Phase error and frequency error

Let the local carrier be A_lo cos(2πf_c t + φ), where φ is the phase error in radians. After multiplication and low-pass filtering, the output is scaled by cos φ.

y(t) = (A_c A_lo / 2) m(t) cos φ

φ is the phase difference between the received carrier and the local carrier, in radians; cos φ is the loss factor.

A phase error of 90° gives cos φ = 0, so the output is zero. A phase error of 180° inverts the message. Small phase errors lose little: at 30°, cos φ = 0.866, a 13.4% loss.

Let the local frequency be f_c + Δf, where Δf is the frequency error in hertz. The low-pass output contains m(t) cos(2πΔf t). The message is multiplied by a slow beat. For a tone this gives two tones at f_m ± Δf. Oscillator stability and carrier recovery are therefore part of the receiver design.

Worked example

Question. A 2 V peak, 1 kHz tone modulates a 10 V peak, 100 kHz carrier in DSB-SC. Write the two sideband terms. Find the output after coherent detection with a local carrier of amplitude 1 V and zero phase error.

  1. Message: m(t) = 2 cos(2π × 1000t) V. Carrier: A_c = 10 V, f_c = 100000 Hz.
  2. DSB-SC wave: s(t) = 10 × 2 cos(2π1000t) cos(2π100000t) = 20 cos(2π1000t) cos(2π100000t) V.
  3. Use 20 cos A cos B = 10[cos(A − B) + cos(A + B)]. Lower sideband: 10 cos(2π(100000 − 1000)t) = 10 cos(2π99000t) V. Upper sideband: 10 cos(2π(100000 + 1000)t) = 10 cos(2π101000t) V.
  4. So the sidebands are 10 V peak at 99 kHz and 10 V peak at 101 kHz.
  5. Coherent detector output amplitude: (A_c A_lo/2) × A_m = (10 × 1/2) × 2 = 10 V. Output: 10 cos(2π1000t) V.

Answer. The sidebands are 10 V peak at 99 kHz and 10 V peak at 101 kHz; coherent detection with a 1 V local carrier gives 10 cos(2π1000t) V, a 10 V peak 1 kHz tone.

Exam traps

  • DSB-SC is not plain AM with a smaller carrier: the carrier is zero, so the envelope is |m(t)| and an envelope detector gives distortion.
  • A 90° phase error gives zero output, not half output. Half output comes from a 60° phase error, because cos 60° = 0.5.
  • Do not draw a spectral line at f_c. For a tone the only lines are at f_c − f_m and f_c + f_m.
  • DSB-SC bandwidth is 2W, not W. The one-sideband case in Chapter 2.3 has bandwidth W.
  • A frequency error gives a beat factor cos(2πΔf t), not a fixed attenuation. A phase error gives a fixed cos φ factor.

2.3 SSB and VSB modulation

Single-sideband (SSB) and vestigial-sideband (VSB) modulation are bandwidth-saving children of double-sideband suppressed-carrier (DSB-SC) modulation. DSB-SC sends two sidebands, but each sideband carries the same message information. SSB transmits only one sideband, so bandwidth falls from 2W to W. VSB transmits one full sideband plus a narrow vestige of the other, so a real filter can build it. Radio links, point-to-point voice, and analog TV video use these ideas.

You need: Chapter 2.2 DSB-SC modulation and coherent detection and Unit 1 Hilbert transform and complex envelope.

One sideband carries the message

Let the message m(t) have bandwidth W hertz and let the carrier be cos 2πfct, where fc is the carrier frequency in hertz. DSB-SC forms s(t) = m(t) cos 2πfct. Its spectrum has an upper sideband from fc to fc + W and a lower sideband from fc − W to fc. For a real message these two sidebands are mirror images. Each one contains the full message spectrum, so one can be removed and the receiver can still recover m(t).

SSB sends only one sideband. Its bandwidth is BSSB = W. The upper-sideband signal occupies fc to fc + W; the lower-sideband signal occupies fc − W to fc. A 3 kHz audio band therefore needs 3 kHz of radio spectrum in SSB, against 6 kHz in DSB-SC. The price is a sharper filter or a more careful modulator, and a coherent receiver with a well synchronized local carrier.

B<sub>DSB</sub> = 2W, B<sub>SSB</sub> = W, B<sub>VSB</sub> = W + f<sub>v</sub>

W is message bandwidth in Hz; fv is vestige width in Hz; B is occupied RF bandwidth in Hz.

Coherent detection multiplies the received SSB signal by 2 cos 2πfct and low-pass filters with cutoff W. A phase error φ in the local carrier attenuates the message by cos φ and adds Hilbert-transform crosstalk; a frequency error makes the output beat and sound distorted. Some syllabi call SSB single-sideband suppressed carrier, or SSB-SC.

Three ways to make SSB

The filter method starts from a DSB-SC signal. A band-pass filter keeps one sideband and rejects the other. The two sidebands meet at fc, so the filter must change from stop to pass within the width of the message. For 3 kHz audio at a high carrier frequency this is a very sharp filter, and every decibel of opposite-sideband leakage becomes interference.

The phase-shift method cancels one sideband by adding two DSB-SC terms in quadrature. One branch multiplies m(t) by cos 2πfct. The other multiplies m̂(t) by sin 2πfct, where m̂(t) is the Hilbert transform of m(t): every frequency component is shifted by 90°, as in Unit 1. Adding or subtracting cancels the unwanted sideband.

s<sub>USB</sub>(t) = (A<sub>c</sub>/2)[m(t) cos 2πf<sub>c</sub>t − m̂(t) sin 2πf<sub>c</sub>t]
s<sub>LSB</sub>(t) = (A<sub>c</sub>/2)[m(t) cos 2πf<sub>c</sub>t + m̂(t) sin 2πf<sub>c</sub>t]

Ac is the carrier amplitude in volts; m(t) is the message; m̂(t) is its Hilbert transform; fc is the carrier frequency in Hz; USB is upper sideband and LSB is lower sideband.

Practical cancellation needs the two branches to match in amplitude and phase. A small mismatch leaves a residual sideband. The Weaver method uses two stages of quadrature modulation and low-pass filtering. It avoids both a sharp RF filter and a wideband 90° phase shifter, at the cost of more mixers and filters.

SSB is used in point-to-point HF radio, in some telephone multiplexing, and in any link where spectrum is scarce. It is not a good fit for a simple envelope detector because it has no carrier.

VSB: one full sideband and a vestige

VSB (vestigial sideband) keeps one sideband in full and a narrow vestige of the other sideband near the carrier. Let fv be the vestige width in hertz. Then BVSB = W + fv. The vestige is small, so VSB bandwidth sits between SSB and DSB. A 3 kHz message with a 0.5 kHz vestige occupies 3.5 kHz, not 6 kHz.

The reason is practical filtering. A real band-pass filter has a gradual transition from passband to stopband. VSB uses that transition as the vestige instead of demanding an almost ideal edge at fc. The receiver can equalize the vestige, or, in television, use a large transmitted carrier and an envelope detector. Analog TV video is the standard example: it sends one video sideband fully and a small vestige of the other, with the carrier present.

VSB is not SSB with an accidentally wide filter. The vestige is a defined part of the spectrum and must be accounted for in bandwidth and detection. It is also not DSB: the far sideband is rejected. The occupied band is W + fv, so a 0.5 kHz vestige adds 0.5 kHz to the SSB bandwidth, not 3 kHz.

Worked example

Question. A 0-3 kHz audio band modulates a 100 kHz carrier. Compare the DSB, SSB and VSB bandwidths if the VSB filter keeps a 0.5 kHz vestige. Give the occupied RF range for the SSB upper sideband and for the VSB signal.

  1. Message bandwidth W = 3 kHz, carrier fc = 100 kHz, vestige width fv = 0.5 kHz.
  2. DSB bandwidth BDSB = 2W = 2 × 3 = 6 kHz. It occupies 100 − 3 = 97 kHz to 100 + 3 = 103 kHz.
  3. SSB bandwidth BSSB = W = 3 kHz. The upper sideband occupies fc to fc + W = 100 kHz to 103 kHz.
  4. VSB bandwidth BVSB = W + fv = 3 + 0.5 = 3.5 kHz. The filter keeps the full upper sideband 100-103 kHz and a 0.5 kHz lower-sideband vestige 99.5-100 kHz.
  5. The VSB occupied range is therefore 99.5 kHz to 103 kHz. Against DSB, SSB saves 3 kHz (50%) and VSB saves 2.5 kHz (about 41.7%).

Answer. DSB needs 6 kHz, SSB needs 3 kHz, and VSB needs 3.5 kHz; the SSB upper sideband occupies 100-103 kHz and the VSB signal occupies about 99.5-103 kHz.

Exam traps

  • SSB is not one fixed spectrum: say upper sideband or lower sideband. 'SSB' alone does not tell the examiner which half of the DSB spectrum remains.
  • The filter method is judged by opposite-sideband rejection in decibels. A finite filter always leaks some of the unwanted sideband; ideal brick-wall filters do not exist.
  • VSB bandwidth is W + fv, not W and not 2W. The vestige width fv is measured near the carrier, not the full message bandwidth.
  • Coherent SSB detection needs carrier synchronization. A phase error is not just a gain error: it adds crosstalk; a frequency error causes a beat.
  • VSB is not SSB with a sloppy filter. The vestige is deliberate and is included in the bandwidth and receiver design.

2.4 AM generation: square-law and balanced modulators

A square-law device — a diode or transistor driven with a small signal — has a current containing the square of its input voltage. Carrier plus message at its input produces sidebands. A band-pass filter then gives AM with carrier. A balanced modulator uses two such devices with the message in antiphase; subtracting outputs cancels the carrier and leaves DSB-SC. Both methods are cheap, need no crystal, and appear in low-power transmitters and radio mixers.

You need the AM expression, modulation index and spectrum from 2.1, and the idea that a diode or transistor has a nonlinear current–voltage relation.

The square law multiplies two signals

A resistor is linear. A diode or bipolar transistor is not. Around its bias point, its current follows a power series in input voltage.

i = a₀ + a₁v + a₂v² + a₃v³ + …

i is device current in amperes; v is input voltage in volts; a₀ is DC bias current in amperes; a₁ is linear slope in A/V; a₂ is square-law coefficient in A/V²; a₃ and higher terms describe the rest of the curvature.

For a small signal, cube and higher terms are negligible. The device is then square-law. Feed it carrier plus message.

v = A_c cos ω_c t + A_m cos ω_m t

A_c is carrier amplitude in volts; ω_c = 2πf_c is carrier angular frequency in rad/s; A_m is message amplitude in volts; ω_m = 2πf_m is message angular frequency in rad/s.

Substituting v into i = a₀ + a₁v + a₂v² and using cos²θ = (1 + cos 2θ)/2 and 2cosAcosB = cos(A − B) + cos(A + B) splits the square term into four groups.

a₂v² = a₂A_c²/2 + (a₂A_c²/2)cos 2ω_c t + a₂A_cA_m[cos(ω_c − ω_m)t + cos(ω_c + ω_m)t] + a₂A_m²/2 + (a₂A_m²/2)cos 2ω_m t

these are parts of the square term in amperes: first pair is DC and carrier second harmonic; middle pair is the two sidebands; last pair is DC and message second harmonic.

  • Linear term a₁v: copies of carrier at f_c and message at f_m. No new frequencies.
  • Square term: DC, sidebands at f_c − f_m and f_c + f_m, second harmonics at 2f_c and 2f_m.
  • Only the cross product A_c cos ω_c t × A_m cos ω_m t makes sidebands. Squaring is the cheapest multiplier.

Sidebands are useful. Everything else is filtered out.

Square-law AM generator

A band-pass filter centred on f_c keeps carrier and sidebands, rejects the rest.

  1. Add carrier and message: v = A_c cos ω_c t + A_m cos ω_m t. Figure uses 2 V carrier and 0.5 V tone.
  2. Apply v to the square-law device. Figure uses i = v + 0.2v², so a₁ = 1 A/V and a₂ = 0.2 A/V².
  3. Band-pass filter: centre f_c = 100 kHz, bandwidth 2f_m = 2 kHz. Passes 99–101 kHz; rejects DC, 1 kHz message, 2 kHz harmonic, 200 kHz second harmonic.
  4. Output is AM with carrier amplitude a₁A_c and each sideband amplitude a₂A_cA_m.

m = 2a₂A_m / a₁

m is modulation index, a pure number; a₂ is in A/V²; A_m is in volts; a₁ is in A/V. Standard AM, i = A_c(1 + m cos ω_m t)cos ω_c t, has sidebands A_c m/2 each.

  • m does not depend on A_c. Raising carrier amplitude raises output power, not modulation index.
  • m rises with message amplitude only while a₃v³ stays small. Practical square-law modulators run at m ≈ 0.2 to 0.5; larger m distorts.
  • Efficiency is poor: most output power sits in the carrier, which carries no information (see 2.1).
  • Nearest unwanted product is carrier second harmonic at 2f_c, one octave away, so a simple tuned circuit separates it.

Balanced modulator: cancelling the carrier

Two identical square-law devices, outputs subtracted. Carrier is fed in phase to both, message in antiphase.

v₁ = A_c cos ω_c t + A_m cos ω_m t,   v₂ = A_c cos ω_c t − A_m cos ω_m t

v₁ and v₂ are the two input voltages in volts; symbols carry the same meaning as before.

i₁ − i₂ = 2a₁A_m cos ω_m t + 2a₂A_cA_m[cos(ω_c − ω_m)t + cos(ω_c + ω_m)t]

i₁ − i₂ is difference current in amperes. First term is message at f_m; second term is the pair of sidebands.

Common terms cancel: DC, second harmonic at 2ω_c, second harmonic at 2ω_m, and carrier at ω_c. Message at ω_m survives but is far below f_c and removed by output band-pass filter. Output is DSB-SC.

DSB-SC carries no carrier: saves power but needs coherent detection (see 2.2 and 2.5).

Ring modulator: four diodes in a ring, switched by carrier, message through centre-tapped transformer. Passive, no bias supply, works to hundreds of megahertz.

Matching decides everything. A few percent mismatch leaves residual carrier, wasting power and upsetting the detector.

Exam traps

  • The carrier at ω_c comes from the linear term a₁v, not from squaring. The square term alone gives only sidebands, DC and second harmonics.
  • m = 2a₂A_m/a₁ contains no A_c. Raising carrier amplitude only raises power, not modulation index.
  • A balanced modulator cancels carrier only when the two halves are matched. Mismatch leaves carrier leakage, a favourite one-mark question.
  • The message term at ω_m is still present at a balanced modulator output. It is removed by the output band-pass filter, not by balancing.
  • Square law holds only for a small signal. Large A_m brings in a₃v³, adding sidebands at f_c ± 2f_m and distorting the envelope; keep m small.

2.5 AM demodulation: envelope and coherent detectors

AM demodulation turns the received radio-frequency waveform back into the message. A cheap envelope detector uses a diode and an RC filter to follow the positive peaks of AM. A coherent detector multiplies by a locally generated carrier and low-pass filters; it also works when no carrier is sent. Envelope detection is common in medium-wave AM broadcast and simple receivers. Coherent detection is used in DSB-SC, SSB and laboratory systems. The choice depends on cost, carrier availability and the need for synchronization.

You need: 2.1 AM for the envelope Ac[1 + m(t)], 2.2 coherent detection for multiplication and filtering, and Unit 1 for low-pass filters.

Envelope detector: diode and RC load

An AM wave with carrier amplitude Ac and normalized message m(t) is s(t) = Ac[1 + m(t)] cos(2π fc t), where |m(t)| ≤ 1 and fc is the carrier frequency in Hz. The envelope E(t) = Ac[1 + m(t)] is the slow outline of the carrier. The diode conducts on positive half-cycles and charges the capacitor toward the peak. Between peaks the capacitor discharges through R. When RC is much larger than the carrier period, the output stays near the peak and follows E(t). A DC block removes the average Ac and leaves Acm(t), the audio. The figure shows the order: diode, RC filter, DC block.

s(t) = A<sub>c</sub>[1 + m(t)] cos(2π f<sub>c</sub> t),   E(t) = A<sub>c</sub>[1 + m(t)]

s(t) is the AM voltage in V; Ac is the unmodulated carrier amplitude in V; m(t) is the normalized message, |m(t)| ≤ 1; fc is the carrier frequency in Hz; E(t) is the envelope voltage in V.

  1. The diode rectifies the AM wave and passes positive peaks.
  2. The capacitor charges to the peak and discharges slowly through R.
  3. The RC low-pass filter removes the carrier, leaving the envelope.
  4. The DC block removes Ac and leaves the audio Acm(t).

RC design: ripple and diagonal clipping

RC must satisfy two limits: 1/(2π fc) ≪ RC ≪ 1/(2π W), where fc is carrier frequency in Hz and W is the highest modulating frequency in Hz. For small carrier ripple the practical rule is stronger: RC ≫ 1/fc, the carrier period. If RC is too small, the output has carrier ripple at fc. The upper limit lets the output fall with the fastest envelope; if RC is too large, the output cannot follow a fast downward slope, so diagonal clipping appears. For fc = 100 kHz and W = 1 kHz, 1.59 µs ≪ RC ≪ 159.15 µs, and the carrier period is 1/fc = 10 µs. Choose RC = 100 µs: the capacitor falls to e−10/100 ≈ 0.90 of the peak between carrier peaks, about 10 % ripple, and 100 µs is below 159.15 µs, so there is no diagonal clipping at m = 0.5. Overmodulation with m > 1 makes 1 + m(t) negative. The envelope no longer matches the message, and a simple diode detector distorts. A DC block is needed because the detected output contains Ac as well as the audio.

1/(2π fc) ≪ RC ≪ 1/(2π W)

fc is the carrier frequency in Hz; W is the highest modulating frequency in Hz; R is the load resistance in Ω; C is the filter capacitance in F; RC is the discharge time constant in s.

  • RC too small: carrier ripple remains at fc.
  • RC too large: diagonal clipping on fast downward envelope slopes.
  • m > 1: a simple envelope detector cannot recover the message without distortion.

Coherent detector for AM

A coherent detector multiplies the received AM by a local carrier cos(2π fc t) and low-pass filters the product. With s(t) = Ac[1 + m(t)] cos(2π fc t), s(t)cos(2π fc t) = (Ac/2)[1 + m(t)] + (Ac/2)[1 + m(t)]cos(4π fc t). The low-pass filter removes the double-frequency term; the output is (Ac/2)[1 + m(t)]. A DC block then leaves (Ac/2)m(t). The local carrier must match frequency and phase. A phase error φ reduces amplitude by cos φ; a frequency error smears the message. Coherent detection costs an oscillator and synchronization. It is necessary for DSB-SC and SSB, where the carrier is suppressed and the envelope is not the message. Broadcast AM can use the cheaper envelope detector.

y(t) = LPF{s(t) cos(2π f<sub>c</sub> t)} = (A<sub>c</sub>/2)[1 + m(t)]

y(t) is the low-pass filter output in V; LPF means low-pass filter; s(t) is the received AM voltage in V; fc is the local carrier frequency in Hz; Ac is the carrier amplitude in V; m(t) is the normalized message.

2.6 AM transmitters: low-level and high-level

An AM transmitter must create a high-power RF carrier, vary its amplitude with audio, and send it to an antenna. Two architectures do this. A low-level transmitter modulates a small RF signal and then amplifies the AM wave with a linear amplifier. A high-level transmitter amplifies an unmodulated carrier to full power and modulates the final stage. The choice sets linearity, efficiency, cost and adjustment difficulty. Low-level designs suit small or wideband systems; high-level designs suit large broadcast transmitters where efficiency matters.

You need: the power relations in Chapter 2.1 and the modulator circuits in Chapter 2.4.

Low-level transmitter chain

A low-level transmitter modulates the carrier before the final power amplifier. The chain is: RF oscillator → buffer → low-level modulator → linear RF power amplifier → antenna. The audio input goes to the modulator. The oscillator sets the carrier frequency. The buffer isolates the oscillator from later load changes, so the frequency stays stable. The modulator makes a small AM wave; in the worked example its carrier is 2 W. The linear amplifier raises the power without changing the envelope shape. Class B push-pull is common: each device conducts for half a cycle and the pair reproduces the full wave with acceptable linearity. Efficiency is lower than class C, so this chain is used at low and medium power or where wideband linearity is needed.

G_dB = 10 log₁₀(P_out / P_in)

G_dB is power gain in decibels; P_out is output power in W; P_in is input power in W. A 20 dB amplifier gives P_out/P_in = 10^(20/10) = 100.

High-level transmitter chain

High-level modulation is applied at the final RF stage. The chain is: RF oscillator → buffer → RF driver → class C final amplifier → antenna, with a high-level modulator feeding the final stage. The driver raises the carrier to the drive level the final stage needs. The final class C amplifier is nonlinear but efficient: it conducts in short pulses. It cannot amplify an already modulated AM wave; it would distort the envelope. Instead the modulator, often a collector modulator, varies the DC supply voltage of the final stage at the audio rate. A higher supply gives a larger carrier amplitude; a lower supply gives a smaller one. The RF output is therefore amplitude-modulated. The modulator is an audio power amplifier and must supply substantial power. Tuning is more critical than in a low-level set.

P_t = P_c(1 + m²/2), P_sb = P_c m²/2

P_t is total transmitted power in W; P_c is unmodulated carrier power in W; m is modulation index, 0 ≤ m ≤ 1; P_sb is total sideband power in W. At m = 0.5, the sidebands add 12.5% to the carrier power.

Question. A 1 kW carrier is amplitude-modulated with m = 0.5. Find the total power and total sideband power. A low-level modulator produces 2 W; a following linear amplifier has 20 dB gain. Find the final carrier power.

2.7 Superheterodyne receiver

The superheterodyne receiver, or superhet, converts every incoming radio signal to one fixed intermediate frequency (IF) before detection. Why: amplifiers and filters give high gain and sharp selectivity easily at a low fixed frequency, not at every station's frequency. A local oscillator (LO) and mixer do the conversion; the radio-frequency (RF) filter before the mixer rejects the image, a second unwanted frequency that would otherwise land on the same IF. Every AM/FM radio, TV tuner and many wireless receivers uses this plan.

You need: envelope detection from Chapter 2.5, and frequency translation and band-pass filters from Unit 1.

Why shift to a fixed IF

An antenna receives many weak signals. The RF amplifier (radio-frequency amplifier) selects the wanted band and raises the signal a little. It also gives the first rejection of unwanted frequencies. RF means the incoming radio frequency; fRF is its value in hertz.

A receiver could filter and amplify directly at fRF. That is hard: the tuned circuits must move over the whole band, and high gain at high frequency can oscillate. The superheterodyne plan avoids this. A mixer and local oscillator (LO) translate every station to one fixed intermediate frequency (IF). The IF is the frequency where the receiver does most of its filtering and amplification. This plan is what some syllabi call the superhet receiver. In AM broadcast the IF is commonly 455 kHz.

The IF amplifier gives most of the gain and the IF filter gives the sharp bandwidth. Both are fixed, so they can be designed once for best performance. A detector then recovers the audio, and an audio amplifier drives the speaker. An automatic gain control (AGC) loop senses the carrier level and adjusts RF and IF gain so weak and strong stations sound similar.

Local oscillator, mixing and the image

The local oscillator runs at fLO. The mixer multiplies RF and LO, producing sum and difference frequencies. The difference is set equal to the IF, and the sum is filtered out:

High-side injection (LO above the signal): f<sub>LO</sub> = f<sub>RF</sub> + f<sub>IF</sub>,   f<sub>image</sub> = f<sub>RF</sub> + 2f<sub>IF</sub>

fLO is the local oscillator frequency; fRF is the wanted radio frequency; fIF is the intermediate frequency; fimage is the image frequency, the unwanted second RF that also gives fIF.

This is high-side injection: the LO is above the signal. Then fLO − fRF = fIF. Low-side injection puts the LO below: fLO = fRF − fIF, and the image becomes fRF − 2fIF. The image is a second RF that also gives fIF at the mixer output. For high-side injection, fimage − fLO = fIF. The mixer cannot tell which one is wanted.

The RF filter before the mixer must reject the image. It passes fRF and stops fimage. If the image reaches the mixer, it lands on the same IF and no later filter can separate it. The RF filter's passband therefore sets image rejection; a narrow filter near the antenna helps.

Detector, audio, AGC and tracking

After the IF filter, the detector recovers the message. For AM this is an envelope detector, covered in Chapter 2.5. The audio amplifier raises the audio to speaker level. AGC takes a DC voltage from the detector, filters it, and feeds it back to the RF and IF amplifiers. A strong station increases AGC voltage and lowers gain; a weak station raises gain. This keeps the audio output nearly constant.

The tuning knob must move the RF filter and the LO together. When fRF changes by Δf, fLO must change by the same Δf so the difference stays at fIF. This is tracking. Alignment is the service adjustment that sets the RF and LO tuned circuits to track across the band. Small tracking errors reduce gain and image rejection at band edges. The IF alignment is set once at the factory and is not tuned by the listener.

Worked example

Question. An AM broadcast receiver has an RF signal at 1.000 MHz and an IF of 455 kHz with high-side injection. Find the local oscillator frequency and the image frequency. If the RF filter passband is 20 kHz wide around 1.000 MHz, does it reject the image?

  1. Convert: fRF = 1.000 MHz, fIF = 455 kHz = 0.455 MHz.
  2. High-side injection: fLO = fRF + fIF = 1.000 + 0.455 = 1.455 MHz.
  3. Image: fimage = fRF + 2fIF = 1.000 + 2(0.455) = 1.000 + 0.910 = 1.910 MHz. This is also fLO + fIF = 1.455 + 0.455.
  4. RF passband: 20 kHz = 0.020 MHz wide, so 0.990 MHz to 1.010 MHz. The image at 1.910 MHz is 0.910 MHz above the passband centre and far outside the 0.990 MHz to 1.010 MHz passband. An ideal RF filter rejects it; a real filter needs enough attenuation there.

Answer. fLO = 1.455 MHz and fimage = 1.910 MHz; the 20 kHz RF passband does not include the image, so the RF filter rejects it.

Exam traps

  • The image is not the local oscillator. For high-side injection fimage = fRF + 2fIF, which is also fLO + fIF.
  • High-side and low-side injection give different fLO and different images. Do not use fRF + 2fIF when fLO = fRF − fIF.
  • The IF is not the audio frequency. 455 kHz is the fixed band before the detector; audio is a few kHz after it.
  • Image rejection belongs to the RF filter before the mixer. After mixing, the image and wanted signal share the same IF.
  • Most gain and selectivity come from the IF stages. The RF amplifier mainly selects the band and lowers the image.

2.8 Frequency division multiplexing

Frequency division multiplexing (FDM) places each message in its own frequency slot and adds all slots to one cable, wire or radio band. A different carrier shifts each baseband signal into its slot. At the far end, a band-pass filter picks one slot and a demodulator brings that message back to baseband. FDM exists because one medium, such as a coaxial cable or a microwave link, can carry many telephone calls at once. Telephone trunk routes, cable television and satellite links use it.

You need: Chapter 2.3 for SSB, Chapter 2.7 for receiver frequency translation, and Unit 1 for spectra.

Frequency slots and guard bands

One telephone voice signal occupies about 0.3 kHz to 3.4 kHz, a width of 3.1 kHz. In FDM, this baseband signal is not sent at 0 Hz. A carrier multiplies it, and an SSB filter keeps one sideband. The sideband is placed inside a reserved frequency slot. A slot is the total frequency range given to one channel. The message does not fill the whole slot. The empty strip at the edge is the guard band. For example, a 3.1 kHz voice signal in a 4 kHz slot leaves 0.9 kHz as guard band.

B<sub>slot</sub> = B<sub>m</sub> + B<sub>g</sub>

Bslot is the slot width in kHz; Bm is the message bandwidth in kHz; Bg is the guard band in kHz.

For N channels, the composite bandwidth is N times the slot width. The total is not N times the voice bandwidth, because every channel also needs guard band.

B<sub>FDM</sub> = N × B<sub>slot</sub> = N × (B<sub>m</sub> + B<sub>g</sub>)

BFDM is the total composite bandwidth in kHz; N is the number of channels; Bslot is one slot width in kHz; Bm is message bandwidth; Bg is guard band.

If DSB is used instead of SSB, the sideband pair needs 2Bm, so the slot becomes 2Bm plus guard band. Voice FDM normally uses SSB because it halves the spectrum per call. Slots must not overlap. Overlap lets one message enter another channel, which is crosstalk.

Transmitter and receiver

The transmitter works channel by channel. First, a low-pass filter limits each message to 3.1 kHz. Second, each message multiplies a different carrier: fc1, fc2, and so on. Third, an SSB filter keeps one sideband. Fourth, a band-pass filter trims the result to its assigned slot. A linear summer then adds all slot signals to form the composite FDM signal.

  1. Limit each message to Bm with a low-pass filter.
  2. Multiply message i by carrier fci and keep one SSB sideband.
  3. Band-pass filter channel i to its slot from fi to fi + Bslot.
  4. Add all filtered slot signals in a linear summer.
  5. Send the composite signal on the cable, link or radio carrier.

The receiver reverses the process. A band-pass filter selects one slot and rejects the others. In a superheterodyne receiver, a mixer then translates that slot to a fixed intermediate frequency for easy filtering. An SSB demodulator multiplies by a local carrier and a low-pass filter recovers the voice. Crystal, ceramic and SAW filters are common because they have sharp edges near a slot boundary.

Filters are never perfect. A real band-pass filter has finite stop-band attenuation, so a small part of channel 1 can leak into channel 2. The guard band lowers this leakage but cannot remove it. Carrier drift also moves a slot and causes overlap. A nonlinear summer or amplifier is another danger: it creates intermodulation products that can fall inside other slots. Keep the summing and amplification linear, and leave enough guard band.

Telephony groups and broadcast use

Telephone systems were the first large FDM users. A basic group carries 12 voice channels in 4 kHz slots from 60 kHz to 108 kHz. That is 12 × 4 = 48 kHz of composite band. Five groups can form a supergroup, and larger hierarchies continue from there. The same idea appears in cable television, where many TV channels share one coaxial cable, and in satellite transponders, where each uplink carrier gets a frequency slot.

When each user or terminal keeps a permanent frequency slot, some syllabi call the scheme frequency division multiple access, or FDMA. FM stereo puts the L+R sum at baseband, a 19 kHz pilot, and the L-R difference around a 38 kHz subcarrier inside one FM band; Unit 3 covers that receiver. FDM separates channels by frequency. Time division multiplexing separates channels by time and belongs to Unit 4. OFDM overlaps subcarriers so they stay orthogonal, and Unit 6 covers it.

Worked example

Question. Four voice channels, each 3.1 kHz wide, use FDM with 4 kHz slots starting at 60 kHz. Find each channel's signal band, the guard band, and the total composite bandwidth.

  1. Given: number of channels N = 4; message bandwidth B_m = 3.1 kHz; slot width B_slot = 4 kHz; first slot starts at 60 kHz.
  2. Channel 1 slot: 60 kHz to 60 + 4 = 64 kHz. Its 3.1 kHz signal occupies 60.0 kHz to 60.0 + 3.1 = 63.1 kHz. Guard band: 63.1 kHz to 64.0 kHz, width 64.0 - 63.1 = 0.9 kHz.
  3. Channel 2 slot: 64 kHz to 68 kHz. Signal band: 64.0 kHz to 67.1 kHz. Guard band: 67.1 kHz to 68.0 kHz, width 0.9 kHz.
  4. Channel 3 slot: 68 kHz to 72 kHz. Signal band: 68.0 kHz to 71.1 kHz. Guard band: 71.1 kHz to 72.0 kHz, width 0.9 kHz.
  5. Channel 4 slot: 72 kHz to 76 kHz. Signal band: 72.0 kHz to 75.1 kHz. Guard band: 75.1 kHz to 76.0 kHz, width 0.9 kHz.
  6. Guard band = B_slot - B_m = 4 - 3.1 = 0.9 kHz.
  7. Total composite bandwidth = N × B_slot = 4 × 4 = 16 kHz. Equivalently, 76 kHz - 60 kHz = 16 kHz.

Answer. Each slot is 4 kHz wide, the guard band is 0.9 kHz, and the composite FDM bandwidth is 16 kHz from 60 kHz to 76 kHz.

Exam traps

  • The guard band is not message bandwidth. Total FDM bandwidth is N times slot width, not N times voice bandwidth.
  • Using DSB instead of SSB needs 2Bm for the sidebands, so the slot becomes 2Bm + Bg. The slot is wider, but not exactly double.
  • Real filters leak. A guard band reduces crosstalk but does not remove it; finite stop-band attenuation and carrier drift still cause overlap.
  • A nonlinear summer or amplifier creates intermodulation products that can land in other slots. Keep the combining linear.
  • FDM separates channels by frequency; TDM separates channels by time. Do not use the names interchangeably.

Remember Define frequency division multiplexing in one sentence. 2 marks

Remember What is a guard band in FDM? 2 marks

A guard band is the unused frequency strip between adjacent message bands in neighbouring slots, kept to reduce crosstalk from imperfect filters.

Understand Why is SSB preferred over DSB for voice FDM? 3 marks

SSB needs only Bm for the sideband, while DSB needs 2Bm. With guard band Bg, the slots are Bm + Bg and 2Bm + Bg, so SSB uses a much narrower slot but not exactly half.

Apply Three voice channels, each 3.1 kHz wide, use 4 kHz slots starting at 100 kHz. Find the guard band, the three slot ranges, and the total composite bandwidth. 4 marks

Analyze Channel 2 in an FDM link suffers crosstalk from channel 1. Give two likely causes and one practical fix. 4 marks

Causes: channel 1 band-pass filter has insufficient stop-band attenuation, or carrier drift moved channel 1 toward channel 2, or the summer/amplifier is nonlinear. Fix: increase guard band, use sharper filters such as SAW or crystal filters, and stabilise carriers.

Analyze Why must the summer in an FDM transmitter be linear? 5 marks

A linear summer adds the slot signals without creating new frequencies. A nonlinear summer mixes slots and produces intermodulation products that can fall inside other slots; those products appear as crosstalk and cannot be removed by the receiver band-pass filter.

Takeaways

  1. AM adds a carrier to the message; the carrier carries no information but makes envelope detection simple.
  2. The modulation index m sets sideband amplitude; AM bandwidth is 2f_m and total power is P_c(1 + m²/2).
  3. DSB-SC removes the carrier, saving power but requiring coherent detection with a synchronized local carrier.
  4. SSB sends one sideband and uses half the bandwidth of DSB; VSB keeps a small vestige so practical filters can be built.
  5. Square-law and balanced modulators use nonlinear devices; the balanced connection cancels the carrier and gives DSB-SC.
  6. An envelope detector is simple and cheap but fails for DSB-SC and for overmodulated AM with m > 1.
  7. Low-level transmitters modulate before power amplification; high-level transmitters modulate the final RF stage and suit high-power AM broadcast.
  8. A superheterodyne receiver converts every RF channel to a fixed IF, giving good selectivity and gain; image rejection is a key design issue.
  9. FDM stacks messages in frequency; filters and guard bands separate channels without overlap.
