2.1 AM: modulation index, spectrum and power

Amplitude modulation (AM) makes the peak value of a high-frequency carrier rise and fall in step with a low-frequency message. The message rides on the outline of the carrier, the envelope, and a simple diode circuit can read it back. One number, the modulation index m, says how deep the swing is. It sets the height of the two sidebands and the share of power that carries the message. It does not set the bandwidth, which is twice the highest message frequency. Medium-wave and shortwave broadcasting and aviation voice radio still use AM.

You need: the Fourier transform of a cosine and the idea of a band-pass signal, from the Fourier transform review and band-pass signals.

The AM wave and the modulation index

Take a carrier Ac cos 2πfct, with peak Ac in volts and frequency fc in hertz, and a tone message Am cos 2πfmt, with peak Am and frequency fm much smaller than fc. Conventional AM adds the message to the carrier's amplitude:

s(t) = Ac[1 + m cos 2πfmt] cos 2πfct

s(t) is the AM voltage in volts at time t in seconds; m = Am/Ac is the modulation index, a pure number. Percentage modulation is 100m %.

The bracket Ac[1 + m cos 2πfmt] is the envelope. It swings between Vmax = Ac(1 + m) and Vmin = Ac(1 − m). An oscilloscope shows both, so m is usually measured from them:

m = (Vmax − Vmin)/(Vmax + Vmin)

Vmax and Vmin are the largest and smallest envelope peaks in volts; half their sum is Ac and half their difference is Am.

If m > 1 (overmodulation), 1 + m cos 2πfmt goes negative for part of each cycle. The carrier flips phase, the envelope loses the shape of the message, an envelope detector distorts, and a real transmitter clips and sprays extra sidebands into neighbouring channels. So AM keeps m ≤ 1, with only the loudest peaks near 1.

Line spectrum and bandwidth

Expanding with 2 cos A cos B = cos(A − B) + cos(A + B) gives three cosines:

s(t) = Ac cos 2πfct + (mAc/2) cos 2π(fc + fm)t + (mAc/2) cos 2π(fc − fm)t

These are the carrier at fc with amplitude Ac, the upper sideband (USB) at fc + fm and the lower sideband (LSB) at fc − fm, each of amplitude mAc/2. The sidebands carry the message: their distance from the carrier is the message frequency and their height follows the message amplitude. The carrier line is the same with or without a message. The figure draws the envelope and these three lines for the first worked example.

BAM = 2fm (a band of tones: 2W)

BAM is the bandwidth in hertz and W the highest message frequency. Medium-wave stations in India sit 9 kHz apart, room for about 4.5 kHz of audio.

A larger m makes the sidebands taller but does not move them, so the bandwidth stays 2fm.

Power, efficiency, antenna current and several tones

A cosine of peak V across a resistance R delivers V²/(2R). Applied to each line:

Pc = Ac²/(2R), PUSB = PLSB = m²Pc/4

Pc is the carrier power in watts and R the load (antenna) resistance in ohms.

Pt = Pc(1 + m²/2)

Pt is the total power: the carrier plus two sidebands of m²Pc/4 each.

The transmission efficiency η is the share of power in the sidebands: η = (m²Pc/2)/Pt = m²/(2 + m²). At m = 1, η = 1/3 = 33.3 %, the best tone-modulated AM can do; at m = 0.5 it is 11.1 %. The wasted carrier buys a cheap receiver.

Power in a fixed antenna resistance goes as the square of the rms antenna current, so It = Ic√(1 + m²/2), where Ic is the current with the carrier alone and It the modulated current. An RF ammeter therefore measures m: m = √(2[(It/Ic)² − 1]).

With several tones of indices m1, m2, … the sideband powers add, so the total index is mt = √(m1² + m2² + …) and Pt = Pc(1 + mt²/2). The envelope stays positive even when all tones peak together only if m1 + m2 + … ≤ 1.

Worked example

Question. An AM wave on a 1 MHz carrier has an envelope that peaks at Vmax = 12 V and dips to Vmin = 4 V. The tone is 5 kHz and the load is 50 Ω. Find m, Ac, Am, the sidebands, the bandwidth, the powers and the efficiency.

  1. m = (12 − 4)/(12 + 4) = 8/16 = 0.5 (50 %).
  2. Ac = (12 + 4)/2 = 8 V and Am = (12 − 4)/2 = 4 V; check Am/Ac = 0.5.
  3. USB at 1000 + 5 = 1005 kHz, LSB at 995 kHz, each mAc/2 = 0.5 × 8/2 = 2 V.
  4. B = 2fm = 10 kHz, from 995 kHz to 1005 kHz.
  5. Pc = 8²/(2 × 50) = 0.64 W. Each sideband: 2²/(2 × 50) = 0.04 W, which equals m²Pc/4 = 0.25 × 0.64/4.
  6. Pt = 0.64 × (1 + 0.25/2) = 0.72 W = 0.64 + 2 × 0.04 W.
  7. η = 0.25/2.25 = 11.1 %; check 0.08/0.72 = 0.111.

Answer. m = 0.5, Ac = 8 V, Am = 4 V; 2 V sidebands at 995 and 1005 kHz; B = 10 kHz; Pc = 0.64 W, 0.04 W per sideband, Pt = 0.72 W; η = 11.1 %.

Exam traps

  • Using peak-to-peak readings as peaks. An envelope of 24 V and 8 V peak-to-peak means Vmax = 12 V and Vmin = 4 V; m is still 0.5, but Ac is 8 V, not 16 V.
  • Writing the sideband amplitude as mAc. It is mAc/2: 2 V, not 4 V, in the first example.
  • Adding indices for several tones. Indices 0.577 and 0.4 give mt = 0.702, not 0.977.
  • Using I²R/2 with an ammeter reading. Ammeters read rms, so 10 A in 50 Ω is 5000 W, not 2500 W.

2.2 DSB-SC modulation and coherent detection

Double-sideband suppressed-carrier modulation (DSB-SC) sends the two sidebands of AM and leaves out the carrier. Since the carrier carries no message, every watt now goes into the sidebands. The price is at the receiver: the envelope of DSB-SC is not the message, so the receiver must make its own copy of the carrier, at exactly the right frequency and phase, multiply and low-pass filter. This is coherent (synchronous) detection. DSB-SC carries the stereo difference signal in FM broadcasting and the colour signals of analog television, and it is the parent of SSB and QAM.

You need: the AM spectrum and power from AM: modulation index, spectrum and power.

The DSB-SC wave and its spectrum

A product modulator multiplies the message m(t), in volts, by the carrier cos 2πfct. Its output is

s(t) = Ac m(t) cos 2πfct

Ac is the multiplier's gain, a pure number, so s(t) is in volts like m(t); fc is the carrier frequency in hertz.

S(f) = (Ac/2)[M(f − fc) + M(f + fc)]

S(f) and M(f) are the Fourier transforms of s(t) and m(t). The message spectrum is copied to ±fc at half height.

There is no impulse at ±fc. The reason is that the message has zero average value, so M(f) has no impulse at f = 0 to be shifted up. A message band from 0 to W hertz becomes a band from fc − W to fc + W, so the bandwidth is 2W, the same as AM. For a tone m(t) = Am cos 2πfmt the wave has just two lines, each of amplitude AcAm/2, at fc − fm and fc + fm.

In time, s(t) is a carrier whose amplitude follows m(t) and goes negative with it. At every zero of the message the carrier phase jumps by 180°, as the figure shows. The envelope is |Ac m(t)|, a rectified copy of the message, so an envelope detector gives a distorted output.

Coherent detection and the effect of phase and frequency errors

The receiver multiplies the incoming wave by a local carrier cos(2πfct + φ), where φ is the phase error in radians, and passes the product through a low-pass filter (LPF) of cutoff W. Using cos A cos B = ½[cos(A − B) + cos(A + B)]:

s(t) cos(2πfct + φ) = (Ac/2) m(t) cos φ + (Ac/2) m(t) cos(4πfct + φ)

y(t) = (Ac/2) m(t) cos φ

y(t) is the LPF output in volts: the term around 2fc is removed, the baseband term stays.

  1. φ = 0: full output (Ac/2) m(t).
  2. φ = 60°: half output, since cos 60° = 0.5.
  3. φ = 90°: zero output. This is the quadrature null effect.
  4. φ drifting slowly: the output fades up and down as cos φ changes.

A frequency error Δf makes the local carrier cos 2π(fc + Δf)t, and the output becomes (Ac/2) m(t) cos 2πΔft: the message multiplied by a slow beat. A 1 kHz tone with Δf = 5 Hz comes out as two tones at 995 Hz and 1005 Hz, heard as a tone that swells and fades five times a second. Both errors mean the local carrier must be locked to the incoming one.

The quadrature null effect has a use. Two messages can share one carrier: m1(t) cos 2πfct + m2(t) sin 2πfct. A receiver multiplying by cos gets m1 with zero m2, and by sin gets m2. Some syllabi call this quadrature-carrier multiplexing (QCM) or quadrature amplitude modulation; it doubles the use of a 2W band.

Getting the carrier back: pilot, squaring loop and Costas loop

Because no carrier is sent, the receiver must recover it. Three methods are common:

  • Pilot carrier. The transmitter sends a small, low-power carrier. The receiver filters it out and uses it, after amplification, as the local carrier. FM stereo sends a 19 kHz pilot and doubles it to 38 kHz.
  • Squaring loop. Squaring s(t) gives Ac²m²(t)(1 + cos 4πfct)/2. Since m²(t) has a nonzero average, there is a line at 2fc. A narrow filter or phase-locked loop (PLL) picks it out and a divide-by-2 gives fc, with a 180° ambiguity.
  • Costas loop. Two multipliers use a voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) output, one in phase (I branch) and one shifted by 90° (Q branch). After low-pass filtering the I arm gives (Ac/2)m(t) cos φ and the Q arm (Ac/2)m(t) sin φ. Their product, (Ac²/8)m²(t) sin 2φ, is a control voltage that is zero only when φ = 0 and drives the VCO into lock. The I arm is then the demodulated output.

DSB-SC against AM: for the same sidebands DSB-SC needs only the sideband power, but its receiver needs a locked oscillator. The bandwidth is 2W in both.

Worked example

Question. A 2 V peak, 1 kHz tone drives a product modulator of gain Ac = 10 with a 100 kHz carrier. The output feeds 50 Ω. Write the DSB-SC wave and its sidebands, find the transmitted power, and compare it with AM that has the same sidebands at m = 1. Find the coherent detector output for φ = 0 and φ = 30°.

  1. s(t) = 10 × 2 cos 2π1000t × cos 2π100000t = 20 cos 2π1000t cos 2π100000t V.
  2. 20 cos A cos B = 10 cos(A − B) + 10 cos(A + B): LSB 10 cos 2π99000t V and USB 10 cos 2π101000t V, that is 10 V lines at 99 kHz and 101 kHz; bandwidth 2 kHz.
  3. Power: each sideband 10²/(2 × 50) = 1 W, total 2 W. No carrier term.
  4. AM with the same 10 V sidebands at m = 1 needs mAc/2 = 10 V, so a 20 V carrier with Pc = 20²/(2 × 50) = 4 W and Pt = 4 × 1.5 = 6 W.
  5. Saving: (6 − 2)/6 = 66.7 % of the AM power.
  6. Detector, φ = 0: y(t) = (Ac/2) m(t) = 5 × 2 cos 2π1000t = 10 cos 2π1000t V.
  7. φ = 30°: y(t) = 10 cos 30° cos 2π1000t = 8.66 cos 2π1000t V, a 13.4 % loss in amplitude.

Answer. Sidebands of 10 V at 99 and 101 kHz; 2 W against 6 W for AM (66.7 % saved); output 10 cos 2π1000t V at φ = 0 and 8.66 cos 2π1000t V at φ = 30°.

Exam traps

  • DSB-SC is not AM with a small carrier. The carrier is zero, the envelope is |Ac m(t)| and an envelope detector distorts.
  • A 90° phase error gives zero output, not half. Half output needs 60°, since cos 60° = 0.5.
  • A frequency error is not a fixed loss: the output is m(t) multiplied by cos 2πΔft, a beat.
  • Do not draw a line at fc. A tone gives only fc − fm and fc + fm; the bandwidth is still 2W.
  • The squaring loop output is at 2fc, so it must be divided by 2, and the recovered phase can be 180° off.

2.3 SSB and VSB modulation

The two sidebands of DSB-SC are mirror images, so each one alone holds the whole message. Single-sideband modulation (SSB) sends just one of them, halving the bandwidth to W and the power to a quarter of a fully modulated AM carrier's share. It needs a sharp filter or a 90° phase-shift network at the transmitter and a coherent detector at the receiver. Vestigial-sideband modulation (VSB) sends one sideband plus a small slice, the vestige, of the other. It suits messages with energy down to 0 Hz, such as television video.

You need: DSB-SC and coherent detection from DSB-SC modulation and coherent detection, and the Hilbert transform from Hilbert transform and complex envelope.

One sideband: bandwidth and power

Let the message m(t) occupy fL to W hertz. DSB-SC puts an upper sideband from fc + fL to fc + W and a lower sideband from fc − W to fc − fL. SSB keeps one of them: an upper-sideband (USB) signal or a lower-sideband (LSB) signal, and the name must say which.

BDSB = 2W, BSSB = W, BVSB = W + fv

W is the highest message frequency in hertz and fv the width of the vestige in hertz.

For one test tone, AM at m = 1 radiates 1.5Pc, DSB-SC radiates the two sidebands, 0.5Pc, and SSB one sideband, 0.25Pc. So against full AM, DSB-SC saves 66.7 % and SSB 83.3 % of the power, and SSB also halves the band. SSB serves crowded HF voice links; some syllabi call it SSB-SC.

Making SSB: filter, phase-shift and Weaver methods

Filter method. Make DSB-SC, then a band-pass filter keeps one sideband. Speech has almost nothing below 300 Hz, so the two sidebands are separated by a gap of 2 × 300 = 600 Hz around fc. The filter must pass fully on one side of that gap and reject strongly on the other. Such a narrow transition is practical only at a low carrier, so the sideband is made there and then mixed up to the final frequency.

Phase-shift (Hartley) method. Two product modulators are fed in quadrature. One multiplies m(t) by cos 2πfct. The other multiplies m̂(t) by sin 2πfct, where m̂(t) is the Hilbert transform: every positive-frequency component delayed by 90°, so cos 2πfmt becomes sin 2πfmt. Adding or subtracting the branches cancels one sideband:

sUSB(t) = m(t) cos 2πfct − m̂(t) sin 2πfct

the upper sideband; a plus sign gives the lower sideband. The gain Ac/2 is left out.

For a tone, cos 2πfmt cos 2πfct − sin 2πfmt sin 2πfct = cos 2π(fc + fm)t, one line at fc + fm. No sharp filter is needed, but the 90° shift must hold across the whole audio band, and any amplitude or phase mismatch between the branches leaves part of the unwanted sideband.

Weaver (third) method. Two quadrature branches mix the message with an audio tone at the band centre, low-pass filter, then mix with the carrier. It needs neither a wideband 90° network nor a sharp filter.

Detection. SSB has no carrier, so it needs a coherent detector. A phase error φ leaves the output amplitude unchanged and shifts the phase of every tone by φ, which the ear hardly notices. A frequency error Δf shifts every tone by Δf: with Δf = 10 Hz, a 1 kHz tone comes out at 1.01 kHz or 0.99 kHz. Speech then sounds high- or low-pitched. This differs from DSB-SC, where the same error gives a beat.

VSB: one sideband plus a vestige

Television video has energy from almost 0 Hz up to several megahertz, so there is no gap between its sidebands and the filter method cannot work. VSB keeps the upper sideband, a vestige of width fv of the lower one, and lets the filter roll off gradually through fc. In the PAL-B system once used for Indian terrestrial television the video band is 5 MHz and the vestige 0.75 MHz, so the video occupies 5.75 MHz of the 7 MHz channel.

The roll-off must be shaped. After coherent detection, the part of the vestige folded down from below fc adds to the part of the upper sideband near fc. The message comes out undistorted only if the two filter gains add to a constant:

H(fc + x) + H(fc − x) = 1, |x| ≤ fv

H is the VSB filter gain and x the distance from the carrier in hertz. The filter is 0.5 at fc and its slope is skew-symmetric about fc.

Broadcast TV adds a large carrier so that receivers can use an envelope detector. The figure draws the spectra of DSB-SC, USB and VSB for the first worked example.

2.4 AM generation: square-law and balanced modulators

A modulator must make new frequencies, fc + fm and fc − fm, that are in neither input. A linear circuit cannot do this; a nonlinear one can. A diode or transistor run on the curved part of its characteristic has a current with a term in the square of its input voltage, and that square contains the product of carrier and message. A square-law modulator uses one such device and a band-pass filter to make AM with carrier. A balanced modulator uses two devices in push-pull so that the carrier cancels, giving DSB-SC.

You need: the AM and DSB-SC spectra from AM: modulation index, spectrum and power and DSB-SC modulation and coherent detection.

How a square law multiplies

Near its bias point the current i of a nonlinear device can be written as a power series in its input voltage v. For a small input the cube and higher terms are negligible:

i = a0 + a1v + a2v²

i in mA, v in volts; a0 is the bias current (mA), a1 the slope (mA/V) and a2 the square-law coefficient (mA/V²).

Apply v = Ac cos ωct + Am cos ωmt, where ω = 2πf. The linear term a1v only copies the inputs. The square term gives, with cos²θ = (1 + cos 2θ)/2 and 2 cos A cos B = cos(A − B) + cos(A + B):

  • DC: a2(Ac² + Am²)/2.
  • Second harmonics: (a2Ac²/2) cos 2ωct and (a2Am²/2) cos 2ωmt.
  • The cross product 2a2AcAm cos ωct cos ωmt = a2AcAm[cos(ωc − ωm)t + cos(ωc + ωm)t]: the two sidebands.

Only the cross product makes sidebands. The figure plots every output line of the worked example on one frequency axis.

The square-law AM modulator

Carrier and message are added and applied to the device. A band-pass filter (a tuned LC tank) centred on fc with bandwidth 2W keeps the carrier a1Ac cos ωct, which comes from the linear term, and the two sidebands of a2AcAm each, which come from the square term. Comparing with Ac'[1 + m cos ωmt] cos ωct, whose sidebands are mAc'/2:

m = 2a2Am / a1

m is the modulation index. It contains no Ac: a bigger carrier raises the output, not the modulation depth.

The filter must reject DC, the message at fm and its harmonic at 2fm, and the harmonic at 2fc. The lowest wanted frequency is fc − W and the highest unwanted one below it is 2W, so fc − W > 2W, that is fc > 3W. Above, 2fc is far from fc + W. For a large message the a3v³ term adds lines at fc ± 2fm, so these modulators run at small m.

The balanced modulator and the diode ring

Two matched square-law devices receive the same carrier but opposite message: v1 = c(t) + m(t) and v2 = c(t) − m(t), with c(t) = Ac cos ωct. A centre-tapped output transformer takes the difference of their currents:

i1 − i2 = 2a1m(t) + 4a2c(t)m(t)

a1c(t) appears equally in i1 and i2, so the subtraction removes it; so do a0, a2c² and a2m². What is left is the message and the product term.

With a tone, 4a2AcAm cos ωct cos ωmt = 2a2AcAm[cos(ωc − ωm)t + cos(ωc + ωm)t]. A band-pass filter at fc removes the message term 2a1m(t), and the output is DSB-SC. If the devices are not matched, part of the carrier survives (carrier leakage), so practical balanced modulators have a balance control.

The ring modulator uses four diodes in a ring with centre-tapped transformers. A large carrier switches the diodes so that the message is multiplied by a square wave p(t) that flips between +1 and −1 at fc. Since p(t) = (4/π)[cos ωct − (1/3) cos 3ωct + …], the output holds DSB-SC at fc with amplitude (2/π)Am per sideband and weaker copies around 3fc, 5fc, which a band-pass filter removes. The ring is passive and works into the hundreds of megahertz; sold as a double-balanced mixer it is also the mixer of most receivers.

2.5 AM demodulation: envelope and coherent detectors

Demodulation recovers the message from the received carrier. For AM with a full carrier and m ≤ 1, the envelope is the message plus a constant, and a diode, a resistor and a capacitor can follow it: this envelope detector is in almost every AM radio. Its time constant must be long enough to bridge the gaps between carrier peaks and short enough to follow the fastest fall of the envelope. A coherent detector multiplies by a local carrier and low-pass filters instead. It costs an oscillator locked to the signal, but it works for DSB-SC, SSB and overmodulated AM, where the envelope is not the message.

You need: the AM wave from AM: modulation index, spectrum and power and the coherent detector from DSB-SC modulation and coherent detection.

The diode envelope detector

The AM wave s(t) = Ac[1 + m cos 2πfmt] cos 2πfct is applied to a diode in series with a load R shunted by a capacitor C. On each positive carrier peak the diode conducts and charges C almost to the peak, through the small diode and source resistance. Between peaks the diode is off and C discharges through R. When RC is many carrier periods long, the capacitor voltage drops only a little before the next peak tops it up, so it follows the envelope Ac[1 + m cos 2πfmt] with a small sawtooth ripple at fc.

The output has a DC part Ac and the audio Acm cos 2πfmt. A coupling capacitor Cc blocks the DC and passes the audio to the next stage. The DC part is useful too: it measures the carrier level and drives the automatic gain control of the receiver. The figure shows the circuit and the waveforms of the worked example.

Choosing RC: ripple, diagonal clipping and negative-peak clipping

Ripple. Between two peaks, 1/fc apart, C falls by the fraction 1 − e−1/(fcRC) ≈ 1/(fcRC). So RC ≫ 1/fc. RC = 20 carrier periods gives about 5 % ripple, which the next stage filters out.

Diagonal clipping. If RC is too long, C cannot discharge as fast as the envelope falls, and the output slides down a straight exponential instead of following the envelope. For a tone, the output follows the envelope at every instant only if

RC ≤ √(1 − m²) / (2πfm m)

fm is the highest modulating frequency in hertz and m the modulation index. A deeper m or a higher fm needs a shorter RC.

So the window is 1/fc ≪ RC ≤ √(1 − m²)/(2πfmm). The rough rule 1/fc ≪ RC ≪ 1/W is a weaker form of the same idea. Near m = 1 the upper limit tends to zero, which is why detectors are designed for the m expected in practice.

Negative-peak clipping. For the audio, the next stage's input resistance Ri is in parallel with R through Cc, so the AC load Rac = R ∥ Ri is smaller than the DC load Rdc = R. The audio swing on Rac cannot exceed what the DC level allows, and the troughs of the output are cut flat unless m ≤ Rac/Rdc.

Overmodulation (m > 1) cannot be cured by any RC: the envelope itself has lost the shape of the message.

The coherent (synchronous) detector

Multiply the received AM by a local carrier cos(2πfct + φ) and low-pass filter:

y(t) = (Ac/2)[1 + m cos 2πfmt] cos φ

y(t) is the low-pass output in volts; φ is the phase error of the local carrier.

After the DC block the audio is (Acm/2) cos 2πfmt · cos φ. This output is linear in the message for any m, even m > 1, because no part of the wave is clipped by a diode. It works equally for DSB-SC and SSB. The local carrier is usually taken from the strong AM carrier itself: an amplifier-limiter or a phase-locked loop strips off the modulation and leaves a clean cos 2πfct. The cost is the oscillator and its lock, which is why cheap AM radios use the envelope detector instead.

2.6 AM transmitters: low-level and high-level

An AM transmitter makes a stable carrier, impresses the audio on its amplitude and delivers kilowatts to the antenna. Where the audio enters decides the design. In a low-level transmitter the carrier is modulated at a few watts and the AM wave is then raised to full power by linear amplifiers, which keep the envelope but waste power. In a high-level transmitter the carrier is amplified unmodulated by efficient class C stages, and the audio varies the supply of the final stage, so the modulator must itself be a large audio amplifier. Large medium-wave broadcast stations use high-level modulation; small and wideband transmitters use low-level.

You need: the power formula Pt = Pc(1 + m²/2) from AM: modulation index, spectrum and power and the modulators of AM generation: square-law and balanced modulators.

The common front end

Both designs start the same way. A crystal oscillator sets the carrier frequency to within a few parts per million. A buffer amplifier follows it, so that later stages, whose load changes with tuning and modulation, cannot pull the oscillator off frequency. Where the crystal is cut for a lower frequency, class C frequency multipliers (doublers or triplers) bring the carrier to fc. A class C amplifier conducts for less than half a cycle and delivers current pulses into a tuned tank, which rings at the carrier frequency. It reaches 70 % to 80 % efficiency because its transistor or valve is either off or fully on, but its output amplitude is not proportional to its input. That last fact shapes everything that follows.

Low-level and high-level modulation

Low-level. The carrier is modulated in an early stage at small power, for example with a balanced or base modulator, and the AM wave is then amplified to full power. Every stage after the modulator must be linear, or the envelope distorts and splatters into neighbouring channels. These linear amplifiers are class A, B or AB; a practical design uses class AB push-pull with negative feedback, since pure class B has crossover distortion. Their efficiency is lower than class C. The audio stage is small, since it only drives a low-power modulator.

High-level. The carrier is amplified unmodulated through class C driver stages, and the audio is applied to the final stage. In collector (or anode) modulation, the audio power amplifier, usually class B push-pull, is connected through a modulation transformer in series with the DC supply of the final class C stage. The supply then becomes VCC[1 + m cos 2πfmt]. A class C stage with a tuned tank gives an RF output amplitude proportional to its supply voltage, so the output is AM.

The sidebands get their power from the modulator. If the class C stage takes Pdc from the supply with efficiency η, the carrier is Pc = ηPdc. Varying the supply by the factor [1 + m cos 2πfmt] raises its average input power to Pdc(1 + m²/2), so the modulator must supply

Paudio = Pdc m²/2 = Pc m²/(2η)

Paudio is the audio power in watts the modulator delivers to the final stage; η is the final stage's efficiency. At m = 1 this is half the DC input, a very large audio amplifier.

2.7 Superheterodyne receiver

A superheterodyne receiver (superhet) shifts every station it tunes to one fixed intermediate frequency (IF), 455 kHz in AM broadcast radios, and does most of its amplifying and filtering there. Fixed-frequency stages are easy to make with high gain and a sharp, flat passband, which tuned stages that must sweep a whole band are not. The shift is done by a mixer fed from a local oscillator (LO) that is tuned together with the input. The cost is the image: a second input frequency that the mixer also turns into the IF, and that only the tuned stages before the mixer can reject.

You need: mixing as multiplication (sum and difference frequencies), as in AM generation: square-law and balanced modulators, and the envelope detector of AM demodulation: envelope and coherent detectors.

The blocks and what each one does

  1. RF stage: a tuned circuit, often with an amplifier, selects the wanted signal fs, rejects the image and improves the noise figure.
  2. Mixer and local oscillator: the mixer multiplies fs by fLO and its output tank keeps the difference fLO − fs = fIF.
  3. IF amplifier: two or three fixed tuned stages, or a ceramic filter, give most of the gain and set the selectivity (about 10 kHz wide for AM broadcast).
  4. Detector: an envelope detector recovers the audio and a DC level proportional to the carrier.
  5. AGC: automatic gain control filters that DC level and feeds it back to cut the RF and IF gain on strong stations, so loud and weak stations come out at similar volume.
  6. Audio amplifier and loudspeaker.

Receivers are judged on four properties. Sensitivity is the weakest signal that gives a standard output. Selectivity is the ability to reject a neighbouring channel; the IF stage sets it. Fidelity is how faithfully the audio band is reproduced. Image rejection is the subject of the next section. The figure draws the chain with the frequencies of the worked example.

The local oscillator, the image and image rejection

Most broadcast receivers use high-side injection: the LO runs above the signal.

fLO = fs + fIF, fsi = fs + 2fIF

for high-side injection: fs is the wanted signal, fLO the local oscillator and fsi the image, all in hertz.

The image fsi lies fIF above the LO, so it too gives fsi − fLO = fIF at the mixer output. Once both are at 455 kHz nothing can separate them; the rejection must happen in the tuned RF stages, which are tuned to fs and see the image 2fIF away. With low-side injection fLO = fs − fIF and the image is fs − 2fIF.

A single tuned circuit of loaded quality factor Q, tuned to fs, passes the image weaker than the signal by the image-rejection ratio α:

α = √(1 + Q²ρ²), ρ = fsi/fs − fs/fsi

α is a voltage ratio (20 log α in dB); ρ is the fractional detuning of the image. Two tuned stages multiply: α = α1α2.

For a fixed IF, ρ shrinks as fs rises, because the image is a smaller fraction away. A shortwave receiver with a 455 kHz IF therefore rejects images poorly, as the worked example shows. The cure is a higher IF, or double conversion: a high first IF (several megahertz) to push the image far away, then a low second IF for selectivity.

Tracking, choice of IF and double spotting

One knob tunes the RF circuit and the LO together on a ganged capacitor, keeping fLO − fs = fIF across the band. This is tracking. For 540 to 1650 kHz the RF circuit spans a frequency ratio of 3.06, which needs a capacitance ratio of 3.06² = 9.34. The LO spans 995 to 2105 kHz, a ratio of only 2.12 (capacitance ratio 4.48). The LO section therefore has a series padder capacitor and a parallel trimmer, and the error is made zero at three points of the band (three-point tracking). Low-side injection would need the LO to span 85 to 1195 kHz, a ratio of 14, which a variable capacitor cannot cover: one reason for high-side injection.

Choosing the IF is a compromise. A high IF puts the image far away (good image rejection) and eases tracking. A low IF gives narrow, steep IF filters (good selectivity) and more stable gain. The IF must not fall inside the tuning band, or a strong station there would pass straight through. Double spotting, hearing a strong station at two dial settings 2fIF apart, is the image problem seen from the dial and points to a weak RF stage.

2.8 Frequency division multiplexing

Frequency division multiplexing (FDM) sends many messages over one cable, fibre or radio link at the same time by giving each its own band of frequencies. Each message modulates its own carrier, usually as SSB, the bands are placed side by side with small guard bands between them, and the sum is sent as one composite signal. At the receiver, band-pass filters pick each band out and a demodulator brings it back to baseband. Analog telephone trunks carried thousands of calls this way. Radio and TV broadcasting, cable TV and FM stereo are FDM too.

You need: SSB from SSB and VSB modulation and coherent detection from DSB-SC modulation and coherent detection.

Slots, guard bands and the total bandwidth

Each channel is given a slot of the spectrum. The modulated message fills part of it and the rest is the guard band, an empty strip that lets real filters move from pass to stop without letting a neighbour in. Two ways of counting are in use, and an answer must say which one it uses:

BT = N·Bch + (N − 1)·Bg

guard bands only between channels: N channels of modulated bandwidth Bch each, with N − 1 guard bands of Bg hertz.

BT = N(Bch + Bg)

the slot convention of telephone systems: every channel owns a slot of Bch + Bg, with its guard band inside.

With SSB, Bch = W; with DSB-SC or AM, Bch = 2W. Switching from SSB to DSB doubles Bch but not the guard bands, so the total less than doubles: for a 3.1 kHz voice with a 0.9 kHz guard band, the slot is 4 kHz with SSB and 2 × 3.1 + 0.9 = 7.1 kHz with DSB, not 8 kHz.

Transmitter and receiver

  1. Each message passes a low-pass filter that limits it to W (0.3 to 3.4 kHz for telephone speech).
  2. A balanced modulator multiplies it by its own carrier fci, giving DSB-SC.
  3. A band-pass filter keeps one sideband and trims it to the channel's slot.
  4. A linear summer adds all the channels into the composite FDM signal, which may itself modulate a radio carrier.

The receiver reverses this: a bank of band-pass filters separates the slots, each slot is coherently detected with its own carrier fci, and a low-pass filter returns the voice. The carriers at both ends must agree closely, since an SSB frequency error moves every tone of the voice; telephone systems derived all carriers from one master oscillator.

Crosstalk, one channel heard in another, has three causes: filters with too little stop-band rejection, carriers that drift towards a neighbour, and nonlinearity in the summer or line amplifiers, whose intermodulation products fall into other slots. Wider guard bands, sharper crystal or ceramic filters, stable carriers and linear amplifiers are the cures.

The telephone hierarchy and other uses

Analog telephony built FDM in stages. A basic group puts 12 voice channels in 60 to 108 kHz: carriers at 64, 68, …, 108 kHz, lower sideband kept, so each channel owns a 4 kHz slot. The figure draws it with the band of each channel. Five groups make a supergroup of 60 channels in 312 to 552 kHz, and larger groupings followed for coaxial cable and microwave links.

The same idea is everywhere in radio. Broadcast stations are FDM with 9 kHz slots on medium wave. FM stereo multiplexes within one station: the sum L + R at 0 to 15 kHz, a 19 kHz pilot, and the difference L − R as DSB-SC on a 38 kHz subcarrier at 23 to 53 kHz (see FM stereo multiplexing and FM receivers). When each user of a shared medium owns a band, the access scheme is called FDMA. Time division multiplexing shares a channel in time instead, and OFDM overlaps orthogonal subcarriers with no guard bands (see the chapters Time division multiplexing, and OFDM and fading channels).

Worked example

Question. Ten voice channels, each band-limited to 3.4 kHz, are frequency multiplexed with a 0.6 kHz guard band between adjacent channels. Find the total bandwidth with SSB and with DSB-SC, and the ratio.

  4. In the slot convention the answers would be 10 × (3.4 + 0.6) = 40 kHz and 10 × (6.8 + 0.6) = 74 kHz.

Answer. 39.4 kHz with SSB and 73.4 kHz with DSB-SC, a ratio of 1.86 (40 kHz and 74 kHz if each channel owns its guard band).

Glossary

Amplitude modulation (AM)
Modulation in which the carrier's amplitude follows the message, with the carrier itself transmitted.
Automatic gain control (AGC)
Feedback from the detector's DC level that lowers RF and IF gain on strong signals.
Balanced modulator
Two matched nonlinear devices in push-pull whose difference output cancels the carrier, giving DSB-SC.
Coherent detection
Demodulation by multiplying with a local carrier of the same frequency and phase, then low-pass filtering.
Collector modulation
High-level AM in which the audio varies the supply voltage of a class C final stage.
Costas loop
A carrier-recovery loop with in-phase and quadrature arms that locks a VCO to a suppressed carrier.
Diagonal clipping
Envelope-detector distortion when RC is too long to follow a falling envelope.
DSB-SC
Double-sideband suppressed-carrier modulation: both sidebands, no carrier.
Envelope
The curve joining the peaks of a modulated carrier; Ac[1 + m cos 2πfmt] for tone AM.
Envelope detector
A diode with an RC load that follows the peaks of an AM wave.
FDM
Frequency division multiplexing: messages share a channel in separate frequency bands.
Guard band
An unused strip between FDM channels that lets real filters separate them.
Hilbert transform
A filter that delays every positive-frequency component by 90°; used in the phase-shift SSB method.
Image frequency
The second input that mixes to the IF: fs + 2fIF with high-side injection.
Intermediate frequency (IF)
The fixed frequency of a superhet's IF stages, 455 kHz in AM broadcast sets.
Modulation index
m = Am/Ac, the depth of amplitude modulation; m > 1 is overmodulation.
Negative-peak clipping
Flattening of the audio troughs when m exceeds Rac/Rdc of the detector load.
Quadrature null effect
Zero output of a coherent detector whose local carrier is 90° out of phase.
Ring modulator
Four diodes in a ring switched by the carrier; a balanced modulator and mixer.
Sideband
The band of frequencies above (USB) or below (LSB) the carrier that carries the message.
Square-law modulator
A modulator that uses the v² term of a nonlinear device to form the product of carrier and message.
SSB
Single-sideband modulation: one sideband only, bandwidth W.
Superheterodyne receiver
A receiver that converts every station to a fixed intermediate frequency before amplification and detection.
Tracking
Keeping the LO exactly fIF above the RF tuning across the band.
Transmission efficiency
The fraction of AM power in the sidebands, m²/(2 + m²).
VSB
Vestigial-sideband modulation: one sideband plus a small part (the vestige) of the other.
