3.1 Frequency and phase modulation concepts

Angle modulation carries the message in the phase of a sine carrier, not in its amplitude. The carrier envelope stays fixed, so a cheap limiter can remove amplitude noise before detection. Phase modulation (PM) moves the phase in step with the message. Frequency modulation (FM) moves the phase in step with the integral of the message, so the instantaneous frequency follows the message itself. FM radio, TV sound, cordless phones and two-way radios use this idea. This chapter defines the two waves, their sensitivities and the modulation index, and shows how one modulator can produce the other.

You need: phasors, the complex envelope and the Fourier transform from Unit 1, Foundations: Signals, Noise and Random Processes.

Phase is the angle that moves

A sine carrier is s(t)=Ac cos θ(t). Ac is the peak amplitude in volts; θ(t) is the instantaneous phase in radians. For an unmodulated carrier θ(t)=2πfc t, with fc in hertz. The angle grows at 2πfc radians per second. If θ(t) changes with time, that rate of change carries information. Define instantaneous frequency fi(t) = (1/2π) dθ/dt. It is the frequency the carrier would show over a very short window, in hertz. The factor 1/2π converts radians per second to cycles per second. For the unmodulated carrier, fi = fc. In angle modulation, θ(t)=2πfc t + φ(t), where φ(t) is the excess phase in radians. Then fi(t)=fc + (1/2π)dφ/dt. The message can enter φ(t) directly (PM), or it can enter the rate of change dφ/dt (FM), which is what the instantaneous frequency is. The phasor picture: a vector of length Ac rotates at fc; modulation makes it speed up and slow down, but its tip never moves off the circle of radius Ac. Hence the envelope Ac stays constant, even while the zero crossings shift.

s(t) = Ac cos θ(t), fi(t) = (1/2π) dθ/dt

Ac is the peak carrier amplitude in V; θ(t) is the instantaneous phase in rad; fi(t) is the instantaneous frequency in Hz.

PM and FM side by side

In PM the excess phase is proportional to the message: φ(t)=kp m(t). The PM wave is s(t)=Ac cos(2πfc t + kp m(t)). kp is the phase sensitivity in radians per volt. For a tone m(t)=Am cos(2πfm t), the peak phase deviation is Δθp=kp Am, in radians. The instantaneous frequency shifts by (1/2π)dφ/dt = -(kp Am fm) sin(2πfm t); its peak size is Δf=kp Am fm. Thus a PM tone with higher fm has a larger frequency swing for the same phase swing.

In FM the excess phase is proportional to the integral of the message: φ(t)=2πkf ∫ from 0 to t m(τ)dτ. The FM wave is s(t)=Ac cos(2πfc t + 2πkf∫m(τ)dτ). kf is the frequency sensitivity in hertz per volt. For the same tone, fi(t)=fc + kf Am cos(2πfm t), so peak deviation Δf=kf Am. The phase term becomes (kf Am/fm) sin(2πfm t). The modulation index β=Δf/fm is the peak phase deviation in radians. For PM with a tone, the equivalent index is kp Am; it does not change when fm changes.

PM: s(t)=Ac cos(2πfc t + kp m(t)); FM: s(t)=Ac cos(2πfc t + 2πkf∫m(τ)dτ)

kp is phase sensitivity in rad/V; kf is frequency sensitivity in Hz/V; m(t) is the message in V; the integral runs from 0 to t.

Δf_FM = kf Am,   β = Δf/fm,   Δθ_PM = kp Am,   Δf_PM = kp Am fm

Am is the tone peak in V; fm is the tone frequency in Hz; Δf is in Hz; β and Δθ are in rad.

One modulator builds the other

PM and FM are close relatives. A phase modulator fed with the integral of m(t) produces FM, because the phase becomes proportional to ∫m. So an integrator followed by a phase modulator is an FM generator. A frequency modulator fed with the derivative of m(t) produces PM, because the modulator's phase is the integral of its input, and the integral of dm/dt is m(t) itself. The phase therefore follows m(t), which is phase modulation. So a differentiator followed by a frequency modulator is a PM generator. This is the standard PM-from-FM and FM-from-PM conversion. The two sensitivities also convert: for a tone, an FM modulator with deviation Δf is equivalent to a PM modulator with peak phase kp Am = Δf/fm.

Some syllabi call kf the frequency sensitivity and kp the phase sensitivity. Others call them deviation constants. Keep the units straight: kf is Hz/V, so kf times volts gives hertz. kp is rad/V, so kp times volts gives radians. In the FM formula the 2π is outside the integral; if the message is a tone, the 2π cancels with the 1/2π in the frequency definition and leaves Δf=kf Am.

Constant envelope does not mean the spectrum is narrow. A large deviation spreads the carrier into many sidebands; chapter 3.2 handles that. The key distinction is: PM ties phase to m(t), FM ties frequency to m(t).

  • Integrator + phase modulator → FM.
  • Differentiator + frequency modulator → PM.
  • Envelope fixed at Ac; instantaneous frequency moves.

Worked example

Question. A 5 kHz tone of amplitude 2 V modulates an FM modulator with kf = 3 kHz/V. Find Δf and β. Then the same tone feeds a PM modulator with kp = 0.8 rad/V: find the peak phase deviation and the equivalent Δf.

  1. For the FM modulator, peak deviation Δf = kf Am = 3 kHz/V × 2 V = 6 kHz.
  2. Modulation index β = Δf/fm = 6 kHz / 5 kHz = 1.2 (dimensionless).
  3. For PM, peak phase deviation Δθp = kp Am = 0.8 rad/V × 2 V = 1.6 rad.
  4. Equivalent peak frequency deviation for PM: Δf = Δθp fm = 1.6 rad × 5000 Hz = 8000 Hz = 8 kHz. Equivalently kp Am fm = 0.8 × 2 × 5000 = 8 kHz.

Answer. FM: Δf = 6 kHz and β = 1.2. PM: peak phase deviation = 1.6 rad and equivalent Δf = 8 kHz.

Exam traps

  • β is not Δf. Δf=kfAm depends on message amplitude only; β=Δf/fm also depends on message frequency. In FM, β falls as fm rises; in PM with a tone, peak phase deviation kpAm is fixed and equivalent Δf rises with fm.
  • The FM phase argument carries 2πkf∫m dt. Without the 2π the phase is in cycles, not radians, so the computed deviation is 2π times too small and β comes out wrong. Keep kf in Hz/V and keep the 2π in the phase.
  • PM and FM both have constant envelope, but neither has constant instantaneous frequency. An envelope detector cannot recover the message; a frequency or phase detector is needed.
  • Integrator and differentiator directions are easy to flip: integrator then phase modulator gives FM; differentiator then frequency modulator gives PM.
  • For a tone, PM phase deviation is kpAm radians; FM peak phase deviation is β=kfAm/fm radians. These two numbers are not interchangeable unless you also state fm.

3.2 Narrowband and wideband FM; Carson's rule

Angle modulation hides the message in the carrier's phase, and the bandwidth it needs grows with the modulation index β = Δf / f_m. For small β, narrowband FM uses a carrier and one sideband pair, with the pair in anti-phase; an envelope detector cannot recover the tone. For large β, wideband FM spreads many Bessel sidebands spaced f_m apart. Carson's rule, B_T ≈ 2(β + 1)f_m, gives the channel width you must allow. Broadcast FM, two-way radio and FM links use this estimate to plan spectrum.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for β and Δf, and Unit 1 for Fourier series and spectra.

Narrowband FM: a carrier and one sideband pair

Narrowband FM (NBFM) is the small-β case. Take β = Δf / f_m ≤ about 0.2. The FM wave is s(t) = A_c cos(2πf_c t + β sin(2πf_m t)). For small β, use cos(θ + φ) ≈ cos θ − φ sin θ.

s_NBFM(t) ≈ A_c cos(2πf_c t) − A_c β sin(2πf_m t) sin(2πf_c t)

A_c is carrier amplitude in V; f_c is carrier frequency in Hz; f_m is the single modulating tone in Hz; β = Δf / f_m is dimensionless.

Expanding the product gives a carrier plus one sideband pair: A_c cos(2πf_c t) + (A_c β/2)[cos(2π(f_c + f_m)t) − cos(2π(f_c − f_m)t)]. Each sideband has amplitude A_c β/2. The upper and lower sidebands have opposite signs, so the sidebands are in anti-phase with each other. Their resultant is at 90° to the carrier. The envelope stays close to A_c, so an envelope detector gives almost no message. The message sits in phase and frequency.

The bandwidth for one tone is 2f_m. For a message with highest frequency W, replace f_m by W. NBFM appears in narrowband voice radios and as the first stage of indirect FM generation; see Chapter 3.3.

Wideband FM: Bessel sidebands

Wideband FM (WBFM) is the case β above about 0.2, and practical systems often use β from 2 to 10. The exact spectrum comes from a Bessel expansion.

s_FM(t) = A_c Σ J_n(β) cos[2π(f_c + n f_m)t], summed over n = −∞ to ∞

J_n(β) is the Bessel function of the first kind, order n; sideband n sits at f_c + n f_m; its amplitude is A_c |J_n(β)|; sideband spacing is f_m.

A negative J_n(β) is a 180° phase reversal, not negative power. Power uses J_n². The carrier amplitude is A_c J_0(β), which can vanish when J_0(β) = 0; the first carrier null is β = 2.405. The total power stays A_c²/2 (in W, across a 1 Ω resistor) because Σ J_n²(β) = 1, so the sidebands simply take power from the carrier. Sidebands are spaced by f_m, not 2f_m. Significant sideband pairs are roughly β + 1 for β ≥ 1.

Carson's rule and the NBFM/WBFM comparison

Count the significant sideband pairs as about β + 1. Each pair adds f_m above and f_m below the carrier, so the total span is 2(β + 1)f_m. This is Carson's rule.

B_T ≈ 2(β + 1)f_m = 2(Δf + f_m)

B_T is transmission bandwidth in Hz; β is modulation index; f_m is highest modulating frequency in Hz; Δf is peak frequency deviation in Hz.

For large β, β + 1 ≈ β, so B_T ≈ 2βf_m = 2Δf. This is the quasi-static bound. Carson sits above 2Δf by 2f_m. Broadcast FM with Δf = 75 kHz and f_m = 15 kHz gives β = 5, B_T = 180 kHz and 2Δf = 150 kHz. The figure plots both estimates for f_m = 15 kHz. They stay 2f_m = 30 kHz apart at every β; only the relative gap 2f_m/(2Δf) = 1/β shrinks as β grows, so 2Δf becomes a good approximation for large β. Some syllabi call WBFM wide-band FM and Carson's rule the transmission bandwidth.

Worked example

Question. An FM broadcast transmitter has peak deviation Δf = 75 kHz and the highest modulating tone f_m = 15 kHz. Find β, the number of significant sideband pairs by β + 1, the Carson bandwidth, and the quasi-static bandwidth 2Δf.

  1. β = Δf / f_m = 75 kHz / 15 kHz = 5. β has no unit.
  2. Significant sideband pairs ≈ β + 1 = 5 + 1 = 6.
  3. Carson bandwidth B_T = 2(β + 1)f_m = 2 × 6 × 15 kHz = 180 kHz.
  4. Check with the other form: 2(Δf + f_m) = 2(75 + 15) kHz = 180 kHz.
  5. Quasi-static bandwidth = 2Δf = 2 × 75 kHz = 150 kHz. This is not the channel allowance; Carson gives 180 kHz.

Answer. β = 5, about 6 significant sideband pairs, Carson bandwidth 180 kHz, and quasi-static bandwidth 150 kHz.

Exam traps

  • Carson's rule is 2(β + 1)f_m, not 2Δf and not 2βf_m. The form 2βf_m is only the large-β limit, equal to 2Δf.
  • β = Δf / f_m is dimensionless. Do not compute Δf / f_c; the carrier frequency sets where the spectrum sits, not the sideband spacing.
  • NBFM sidebands are anti-phase with each other and in quadrature with the carrier. An envelope detector gives almost no message; use a discriminator or coherent detector.
  • Sidebands are spaced f_m apart, not 2f_m. The pair at f_c ± f_m is one pair, and the full Carson span is 2(β + 1)f_m.
  • J_n(β) can be negative. A negative value is a 180° phase reversal, not negative power; power uses J_n².

3.3 FM generation: direct and indirect methods

FM generation is the process of making a carrier's instantaneous frequency follow the message. There are two routes. A direct generator pulls the frequency of an oscillator with a voltage, using a varactor diode, a reactance modulator or a voltage-controlled oscillator. An indirect generator starts with narrowband FM, multiplies carrier and deviation together, then mixes the carrier to the final channel while leaving deviation alone. Direct circuits are simple but drift; indirect circuits are stable because a crystal sets the initial carrier. FM broadcast transmitters, two-way radios and software-defined radios all use one of these ideas.

You need: Chapter 3.2 for β and Carson's rule, and Unit 2 for the mixer and superheterodyne idea.

Direct FM: voltage controls the oscillator

In direct FM the message voltage is applied inside the oscillator. The tuned circuit that sets the frequency contains a component whose value changes with voltage. The oscillator frequency then changes at the same rate as the message. This is a voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO).

The common component is a varactor diode. It is a p-n junction operated in reverse bias, so it acts as a capacitor. Its capacitance falls as reverse voltage rises. Connect it across the LC tank; the message changes C, so the resonant frequency changes. The relation is:

f₀ = 1 / (2π√(L C(V)))

f₀ is the centre frequency in hertz; L is the tank inductance in henry; C(V) is the total tank capacitance in farad, including the varactor; V is the message voltage in volt.

A reactance modulator uses a transistor or FET with a feedback network so that its input looks like a reactance across the tank. The message changes the device transconductance, which changes the effective reactance and hence frequency. It can give larger deviation than a simple varactor.

Two practical limits matter. First, C(V) is nonlinear, so equal message steps do not give equal frequency steps. Keep deviation small or linearise with a correction network. Second, the centre frequency drifts with temperature, bias and supply. An automatic frequency control (AFC) loop compares the oscillator with a stable crystal reference and corrects the bias. Without AFC, a direct FM transmitter can wander off channel.

Indirect FM: Armstrong's multiplier-and-mixer route

The Armstrong method avoids a drifting power oscillator. A crystal oscillator feeds a narrowband FM (NBFM) modulator. NBFM has small β, so it is easy to make linearly by a phase modulator. The initial deviation is tiny.

A frequency multiplier by N multiplies every frequency in the wave: carrier and deviation. So:

f_c′ = N f_c, Δf′ = N Δf, β′ = N β

f_c is the input carrier in hertz; Δf is the input peak deviation in hertz; β is the modulation index; N is the integer multiplier; primed values are after multiplication.

A mixer then adds or subtracts a local oscillator frequency. It shifts the carrier only: f_c″ = |f_c′ ± f_LO|, while Δf″ = Δf′. The deviation does not grow because the mixer is a frequency translation, not a frequency multiplication. A band-pass filter selects the sum or difference, and an RF amplifier feeds the antenna.

The order matters: multiply first to get the deviation you need, then mix to the assigned channel. If you mix first, later multiplication would multiply the already-correct deviation and overdeviate.

Design arithmetic and FM/PM conversion rules

The design uses the tone relation Δf = β f_m. Here Δf is peak deviation in hertz, β is modulation index, and f_m is message frequency in hertz. The multiplier needed is N = Δf_target / Δf_NBFM. The carrier after multiplication is N f_c, and after mixing is N f_c ± f_LO. β after multiplication is N β_NBFM.

For example, a 15 kHz message with β = 0.1 at 100 kHz has NBFM deviation 1.5 kHz. A multiplier of 50 gives 5 MHz and 75 kHz deviation. A 95 MHz local oscillator then gives 100 MHz by the sum, while deviation stays 75 kHz. The worked example below shows every step.

The same hardware can convert FM and PM. To generate FM with a phase modulator, integrate the message first: feed m(t) through an integrator, because phase is the integral of frequency. To generate PM with a frequency modulator, differentiate the message first, because frequency is the derivative of phase. These two rules explain why the same modulator block can be labelled FM or PM.

In a software-defined radio the VCO is replaced by a numerically controlled oscillator. The message updates a frequency control word, so the phase accumulator runs faster or slower. The arithmetic is the same as direct FM; the limits are digital word length and DAC clocks. Simple varactor circuits remain common in low-cost transmitters.

Exam traps

  • A multiplier scales carrier and deviation together. A mixer shifts carrier only. Mixing cannot increase deviation; only a multiplier or a higher-β modulator can.
  • β = Δf / f_m, not Δf × f_m. Doubling message frequency at fixed deviation halves β.
  • Varactor capacitance versus voltage is nonlinear; a large direct-FM deviation produces distortion unless linearised.
  • Centre-frequency drift and deviation are different faults. AFC corrects slow drift of the centre, not the audio deviation.
  • In Armstrong design, multiply before mixing. If the final mixer is placed before the multiplier, the multiplier will multiply the already-final deviation and overdeviate.

3.4 FM demodulation: slope, Foster-Seeley, ratio detector

An FM signal carries its message in frequency, yet every practical detector works on amplitude. The trick is to convert the frequency deviation into an amplitude change and then envelope-detect it, as Unit 2 taught. One detuned tuned circuit does this — the slope detector. Two of them, balanced, do it better. The Foster-Seeley discriminator does it with a transformer whose secondary phase rotates with frequency, and the ratio detector adds one large capacitor for free limiting. Every FM broadcast receiver and every 10.7 MHz IF strip ends with one of these circuits.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for instantaneous frequency, Chapter 3.2 for deviation and bandwidth, and Unit 2 for the IF stage and the envelope detector.

Slope detector: one detuned tuned circuit

An FM detector is two blocks: a frequency-to-amplitude converter, then the diode envelope detector from Unit 2. The converter is a resonant circuit. Its voltage against frequency peaks at the resonant frequency f_r and falls away on both flanks, and near f_r each flank is almost a straight line.

Place the carrier f_c on a flank by tuning f_r a little to one side of f_c. As the FM signal swings around f_c, the output swings with it. Frequency deviation has become amplitude.

v_o = S (f − f_r)

v_o is the detector output in volts; S is the slope sensitivity in volts per hertz (often written mV/kHz); f is the instantaneous frequency in Hz; f_r is the resonant frequency of the tuned circuit in Hz.

Two faults. The straight part is short, about the tuned-circuit bandwidth B = f_r/Q wide, so the circuit suits narrowband FM and distorts wideband broadcast FM with ±75 kHz deviation. And it is single-ended: AM on the incoming carrier, from noise or fading, comes straight out as audio. That is why an FM IF strip always ends in a limiter.

Raising Q steepens the slope, giving more volts per kHz, but shortens the straight part. That trade never goes away.

The balanced slope detector uses two tuned circuits, one resonant above f_c and one below, each with its own diode, and subtracts the outputs. The frequency terms have opposite signs, so the difference is steeper and straighter; the AM terms are equal and cancel. Output is zero at f_c. The cost is alignment: two primaries must be trimmed against each other.

Foster-Seeley discriminator

One transformer, a tuned primary, and a secondary that is both tuned to f_c and centre-tapped. Let e_p be the primary voltage and e_s the secondary voltage. The centre tap splits e_s into two halves of e_s/2, in antiphase with each other.

A tuned secondary driven through mutual inductance does not hold its voltage in phase with e_p. At f_c, e_s is 90° out of phase with e_p. Above f_c the angle grows past 90°; below f_c it falls short. The secondary phase rotates with frequency, and that rotation is the mechanism.

Diode D1 sees the vector sum e_p + e_s/2; diode D2 sees e_p − e_s/2. Each diode with its RC load is an envelope detector, so the load voltages are the magnitudes V1 = |e_p + e_s/2| and V2 = |e_p − e_s/2|. The audio output is their difference, v_o = V1 − V2.

At f_c the two vectors have equal length and v_o = 0. Above f_c one grows while the other shrinks, so v_o > 0; below f_c the reverse, v_o < 0. Plotted against frequency this is an S-curve, odd about f_c, so the polarity of the output tells you which side of f_c the signal lies on. Hence the name frequency discriminator.

Linearity beats the slope detector. Near resonance the secondary phase varies almost linearly with frequency, and near 90° the difference of the two magnitudes varies almost linearly with the phase angle. Two linear laws multiplied give a linear detector over a wide band.

Amplitude: both V1 and V2 scale with input amplitude, so at f_c an amplitude change cancels in the subtraction. Away from f_c a residual AM term survives, so a limiter still precedes the circuit. Feeding the output through an integrator recovers the phase message, which is how the same circuit demodulates PM.

Ratio detector, and how the family compares

Reverse one diode and merge the two loads into a single resistor with a large capacitor C across all of it. Both diodes now conduct the same way round the loop, so the voltages add: C charges to V1 + V2, not V1 − V2.

The time constant R_L C is long compared with one audio cycle. V1 + V2 is therefore held almost constant. A sudden rise in input amplitude tries to raise both V1 and V2, but C absorbs it; only a slow drift is followed. The output, tapped from part of the load, then depends only on the ratio V1/V2, which depends only on frequency. That is the name, and that is the limiting: AM is squeezed out inside the detector, without a separate limiter stage.

The price: the load carries the sum, so audio output is roughly half that of a Foster-Seeley for the same input; the S-curve is slightly asymmetric; and the two diodes must be matched, because an imbalance charges C wrongly and shifts the operating point. For NBFM the Foster-Seeley is the usual choice, since narrow deviation already gives a small output.

Exam traps

  • The S-curve is linear only over a limited range. Beyond it the output flattens and then folds back, so a larger deviation does not give a larger output.
  • The Foster-Seeley secondary is tuned to the same f_c as the primary, not offset. The 90° phase shift comes from resonance, not from detuning.
  • The ratio detector gives about half the output and only modest limiting. Do not claim it removes all AM, or that it needs no limiter in a demanding receiver.

3.5 Noise in AM and FM receivers; figure of merit

Noise enters a receiver with the signal, and every detector treats it differently. The figure of merit (FOM) puts that difference into one number: output signal-to-noise ratio divided by input carrier-to-noise ratio, for the same total received signal power. It lets you compare AM, DSB-SC, SSB and FM before building anything. AM with an envelope detector tops out at 1/3. FM can reach (3/2)β² because a discriminator turns flat noise into a rising spectrum, and wider deviation buys a higher output SNR. Broadcast, satellite and two-way radio link budgets use this number.

You need Unit 1 for AWGN and noise power, and chapters 3.2 and 3.4 for bandwidth and detector behaviour.

What the figure of merit measures

Take a message tone of bandwidth fm hertz. Let PC be the total received signal power in the message band, in watts. For AM this is carrier plus sidebands, PC = PT = (Ac²/2)(1 + m²/2), where Ac is the unmodulated carrier amplitude and m is the modulation index. For FM the envelope is constant, so PC = Ac²/2. Let N0/2 be the two-sided power spectral density of the channel noise in W/Hz. Define the input carrier-to-noise ratio as (S/N)c = PC/(N0fm). Define the output signal-to-noise ratio (S/N)o as the signal power after the detector divided by the noise power after the detector, both measured in the same message band fm. The figure of merit is FOM = (S/N)o/(S/N)c. Use the same PC and N0 for every scheme you compare. A FOM above 1 means the detector improves SNR; below 1 it degrades SNR.

The definition hides one choice: the bandwidth used for the input noise. This chapter measures input noise in fm, the common convention that gives the neat AM and FM formulas. If a book measures FM input noise in the transmission bandwidth B = 2(β + 1)fm, its FOM is larger by B/fm = 2(β + 1). Always check the bandwidth before comparing numbers.

AM, DSB-SC and SSB

AM with an envelope detector is the baseline. The received signal is s(t) = Ac[1 + m cos(2πfmt)]cos(2πfct), where m is the modulation index, 0 ≤ m ≤ 1. Its total input power is PC = PT = (Ac²/2)(1 + m²/2). After the dc block, the detector output signal is Acm cos(2πfmt), with power Ac²m²/2. The in-phase noise nc(t) passes to the output with two-sided PSD N0 over |f| ≤ fm, so output noise power is 2N0fm. The figure of merit is then FOM = (Ac²m²/2 ÷ 2N0fm) ÷ (PT ÷ N0fm) = m²/(2 + m²). At m = 1 the maximum is 1/3, that is −4.77 dB. The carrier carries no information but adds input power, and the envelope detector passes noise; both push the FOM down.

DSB-SC with coherent detection has FOM = 1. SSB with coherent detection also has FOM = 1, while using only fm of bandwidth instead of 2fm. Coherent multiplication rejects the quadrature noise component, so these systems do not pay the AM penalty.

FM: the discriminator shapes the noise

FM behaves differently because the detector is a discriminator. The input noise is flat across the IF band. The discriminator output is proportional to the derivative of the noise phase, so its noise power spectrum is Sno(f) = N0f²/Ac² for |f| ≤ fm. A flat input spectrum becomes a parabolic power spectrum, and the voltage spectrum is triangular. Integrating over that band gives output noise power No = 2N0fm³/(3Ac²). The tone after the discriminator is Δf cos(2πfmt), where Δf = βfm, so signal power is β²fm²/2. Output SNR is (3/2)β²PC/(N0fm). Divide by input CNR PC/(N0fm) to get FOM = (3/2)β². At β = 5 this is 37.5, or 15.74 dB. The result needs the carrier above threshold; below it the linear analysis fails (chapter 3.6).

The input-noise bandwidth matters: with noise measured in B = 2(β + 1)fm, the same output SNR gives FOM = 3β²(β + 1). Table 1 lists both the bandwidth and the FOM under the fm convention.

Question. Exam traps

  • AM's maximum is 1/3, not 1. At m = 1, FOM = 1/3 (−4.77 dB); only DSB-SC and SSB reach 1.

  • Do not mix bandwidth conventions. (3/2)β² uses input noise in f_m; if the paper puts input noise in B = 2(β + 1)f_m, the FOM is 3β²(β + 1).
  • In dB, add. Output SNR(dB) = input CNR(dB) + 10 log10(FOM). Do not add the linear FOM to a dB value.
  • The FM formula is a small-noise result. Below threshold the output SNR falls faster than the input CNR; chapter 3.6 covers this.

3.6 Threshold and capture effects in FM

FM gives a large noise improvement only while the received carrier is well above the noise. Below an input carrier-to-noise ratio of about 10 dB the detector output breaks into jumps and clicks, and the output SNR falls far faster than the ideal law predicts. This is the threshold effect. The same nonlinear detector behaviour gives the capture effect: of two co-channel FM signals, the stronger one takes over the detector output and the weaker is effectively rejected, provided the stronger exceeds it by the capture ratio, typically a few dB. Threshold and capture decide FM broadcast quality and co-channel reuse.

You need Chapter 3.5 for the above-threshold output SNR law, and Unit 1 for noise power in a bandwidth.

Why the threshold exists

FM noise performance has two regimes. In the good regime the carrier is much larger than the noise in the IF bandwidth. CNR means carrier power divided by noise power in that bandwidth. The limiter removes amplitude noise, and the discriminator output noise is small. The output SNR then follows a straight-line law: every extra dB of input CNR gives one extra dB of output SNR, plus a fixed FM improvement set by the modulation index.

(S/N)<sub>out</sub> = (C/N)<sub>in</sub> + I<sub>FM</sub>

(S/N)out is output signal-to-noise ratio in dB; (C/N)in is input carrier-to-noise ratio in dB; IFM is the FM improvement in dB, fixed by the modulation index and bandwidth.

Below about 10 dB input CNR the carrier no longer dominates. For short intervals noise exceeds the carrier. The limiter follows the noise, the estimated instantaneous frequency jumps, and the discriminator output shows spikes called clicks. A click is a large error, not a small one. The output SNR now falls steeply, much faster than linearly. This collapse is the threshold effect.

Define the threshold as the input CNR at which the output SNR is 1 dB below the above-threshold law. Practical receivers put this knee near 10 dB. Threshold is a property of the input CNR, not of the output SNR. A receiver with a higher modulation index has a better above-threshold law but usually a higher threshold.

The two-region curve and threshold extension

The figure plots output SNR against input CNR. The straight line is the above-threshold law; here it is x + 15.7 dB. The measured points sit on the line at 12 dB input and above, where the actual output is 27.7 dB. At 10 dB input the actual output is already about 20 dB, roughly 6 dB below the ideal 25.7 dB, so this receiver's 1 dB threshold lies near 11–12 dB. At 8 dB input the ideal line would give 23.7 dB, but the actual output is only about 6 dB. The curve has a sharp knee, which is why engineers speak of an FM threshold.

Threshold extension methods move the knee to lower input CNR. An FM feedback loop (FMFB) uses a narrow IF filter that follows the instantaneous carrier, so less noise enters. A phase-locked loop (PLL) detector tracks carrier phase and does not produce the same click spikes. Only a few universities teach these methods in detail. Know the names, the aim, and that they trade complexity for a lower threshold.

Capture effect

Capture effect appears when two FM signals share one channel. The receiver limiter sees the sum. If one signal is stronger by more than the capture ratio, the discriminator output follows only the stronger signal; the weaker is suppressed. Capture ratio is the minimum input power ratio, in dB, of the stronger to the weaker signal for the stronger to win. It is typically a few dB.

This helps FM broadcasting. A distant co-channel station is weaker at your receiver, so it is captured out and you hear only the local station, with less co-channel interference than AM would give. It also helps cellular reuse. The same channel can be reused in another cell if the local base station is stronger than the distant co-channel base station by the capture ratio. If the interferer becomes stronger, the receiver captures the wrong signal. Capture ratio is a two-signal specification; threshold is a signal-to-noise specification.

Worked example

Question. An FM receiver has an IF bandwidth B = 180 kHz, a noise figure F = 1 (0 dB), and an antenna temperature T0 = 290 K. Use kT0 = 4 × 10⁻²¹ W/Hz. The threshold is taken as an input CNR of 10 dB. Find the input noise power in W and dBm, and the input carrier power at threshold in W and dBm.

Exam traps

  • The threshold is an input CNR of about 10 dB; do not quote it as an output SNR or as a fixed carrier power in watts.
  • Below threshold the output SNR does not follow the above-threshold line; do not use (S/N)out = (C/N)in + IFM there.
  • Capture ratio is not threshold. Capture ratio compares two co-channel signal powers; threshold compares carrier with noise.
  • A larger modulation index improves the above-threshold output SNR but raises the threshold; do not claim it always improves reception.
  • Noise figure 0 dB means F = 1, not zero noise; antenna noise kT0B still exists.

3.7 Pre-emphasis and de-emphasis

FM reception has a hidden problem: after the discriminator, noise power grows with audio frequency, so the treble sounds hissy. Pre-emphasis boosts the treble in the transmitter before modulation; de-emphasis cuts the treble in the receiver after detection. The voice or music returns to normal, but the high-frequency noise is cut too. Broadcast FM in the Americas uses a 75 µs time constant; Europe, India and most of the world use 50 µs. The idea is used in FM radio, TV sound and wireless microphones.

You need: Chapter 3.5 for the noise triangle and figure of merit, and Chapter 3.4 for the discriminator output.

Why the treble is noisiest

The input noise at an FM receiver is white: its power spectral density is flat across the RF band. The discriminator does not keep it flat. It converts a frequency offset into a voltage, so a noise component that sits f hertz away from the carrier produces an output voltage proportional to f. Power is proportional to the square of voltage. The output noise power spectral density therefore rises as f² across the audio band. Plotted against audio frequency, the amplitude spectrum is a triangle and the power spectrum is a parabola. Engineers call it the noise triangle.

Speech and music do not have equal power at all audio frequencies. Most energy is at low and middle frequencies; high notes and consonants are weaker. At 10 kHz the signal may be 20 dB below its low-frequency level while the noise is much higher. The treble signal-to-noise ratio is poor even when the overall SNR looks good.

Pre-emphasis fixes this before transmission. The transmitter passes the audio through a network that boosts high frequencies. The receiver passes the discriminator output through the inverse network. The signal is boosted then cut back, so it ends where it started. The noise is not boosted at the transmitter. At the receiver it is cut along with the treble. The net effect is a large SNR gain at high frequencies.

One condition: the boost must be modest and the deviation must be set after the pre-emphasis network, because boosting treble raises the peak audio voltage and with it the FM deviation.

The two responses

The pre-emphasis response is a first-order boost. A resistor and capacitor set the time constant τ = RC, where R is resistance in ohms and C is capacitance in farads.

|H_pre(f)| = √(1 + (f/f1)²), f1 = 1/(2πτ)

H_pre(f) is the voltage gain at audio frequency f (Hz); f1 is the corner frequency (Hz); τ = RC is the time constant in seconds.

Below f1 the gain is about 1. Above f1 the gain rises at 6 dB per octave. De-emphasis is the mirror image.

|H_de(f)| = 1 / √(1 + (f/f1)²)

H_de(f) is the de-emphasis voltage gain; the same f1 is used at both ends, so the two responses multiply to 1 at every frequency.

With de-emphasis, the high-frequency noise is reduced. For an audio band extending to fm, with fm much greater than f1, the output SNR improvement is approximately:

SNR improvement ≈ (1/3)(fm/f1)²

fm is the highest audio frequency in Hz; f1 is the corner frequency; the result is a power ratio when fm is much greater than f1.

The American 75 µs system with fm = 15 kHz gives f1 = 2122 Hz, ratio 7.07, improvement about 16.7, or 12.2 dB. The formula counts the reduction of the parabolic noise. It does not apply when fm is close to f1.

Placing the networks

Put pre-emphasis in the audio path before the FM modulator. Put de-emphasis after the FM discriminator, before the audio power amplifier. The transmitter network must see the microphone or line signal, not the modulated carrier. The receiver network must see the recovered audio, not the IF signal.

Time constants must match at the two ends. A 75 µs transmitter needs a 75 µs receiver. If the receiver uses 50 µs, the treble is cut too little, so the sound is bright and noisy. If the transmitter uses 50 µs and the receiver 75 µs, the treble is cut too much and the sound is dull.

In a broadcast chain the pre-emphasis is inside the exciter. The receiver chip or software-defined radio applies de-emphasis after the discriminator. In a stereo receiver the de-emphasis is placed after the stereo decoder, so it acts on the recovered left and right channels. Placing it before the decoder would cut the L+R baseband and the L−R subcarrier by different amounts and spoil the stereo separation; that decoder is the subject of Chapter 3.8.

Worked example

Question. An American broadcast FM system uses a 75 µs pre-emphasis. Find the corner frequency, the voltage boost at 2.122 kHz and at 15 kHz in dB, and estimate the SNR improvement for a 15 kHz audio band in ratio and dB.

  1. τ = 75 µs = 75 × 10⁻⁶ s. f1 = 1/(2πτ) = 1/(2π × 75 × 10⁻⁶) = 2122 Hz.
  2. At f = 2122 Hz, f/f1 = 1. Gain = √(1 + 1²) = √2 = 1.414. Boost in dB = 20 log₁₀ 1.414 = 3.01 dB.
  3. At f = 15000 Hz, f/f1 = 15000/2122 = 7.07. Gain = √(1 + 7.07²) = √50.98 = 7.14. Boost in dB = 20 log₁₀ 7.14 = 17.1 dB.
  4. SNR improvement ratio = (1/3)(fm/f1)² = (1/3)(7.07)² = 16.7. In dB = 10 log₁₀ 16.7 = 12.2 dB.

Answer. f1 = 2122 Hz; boost = 3.01 dB at 2.122 kHz and 17.1 dB at 15 kHz; SNR improvement ≈ 16.7, that is 12.2 dB.

Exam traps

  • Swapping the two networks puts the treble boost at the receiver, so the noise triangle is boosted instead of cut; the signal shape is still restored but the SNR at high frequencies gets roughly 12 dB worse instead of better. Pre-emphasis belongs at the transmitter, de-emphasis at the receiver.
  • Using 10 log instead of 20 log for a voltage gain gives half the correct boost in dB.
  • Forgetting to convert microseconds to seconds in f1 = 1/(2πτ) puts the corner out by a factor of 10⁶.
  • The improvement (1/3)(fm/f1)² is a power ratio; quote 10 log₁₀ of it in dB, not 20 log₁₀.
  • Pre-emphasis does not remove the noise triangle; it reshapes the signal before transmission so de-emphasis can cut the noise afterward.

3.8 FM stereo multiplexing and FM receivers

A stereo FM station must send two audio signals, left and right, through one 200 kHz channel. It does this by building a composite baseband: the sum L + R in the ordinary audio band so old mono radios still work, a small 19 kHz pilot tone, and the difference L − R on a suppressed 38 kHz subcarrier. The receiver recovers the carrier from the pilot, detects the difference, and matrixes sum and difference back into left and right. The same idea runs in every FM stereo chip and in software-defined radio.

You need: the discriminator from Chapter 3.4, de-emphasis from Chapter 3.7, and the superheterodyne front end with its 10.7 MHz IF from Unit 2.

The composite baseband

One carrier, one 200 kHz channel, two audio signals. Sending left (L) and right (R) on two separate carriers would waste spectrum, so the transmitter combines them into one audio-frequency signal called the composite baseband and modulates the carrier with it. Stereo FM is frequency-division multiplexing applied to FM (see Unit 2, Frequency division multiplexing).

Compatibility drives the layout. A mono receiver tuned to a stereo station must still sound right. So the transmitter forms the sum L + R and puts it in the plain audio band, 0 to 15 kHz. A mono receiver low-pass filters the discriminator output at 15 kHz and hears L + R, which for a listener between the speakers is the whole programme. Nothing is lost.

The difference L − R carries the stereo information and needs its own band, placed above the audio. It modulates a 38 kHz subcarrier with both sidebands and no carrier, so it occupies (38 − 15) = 23 kHz to (38 + 15) = 53 kHz. That 30 kHz block is empty when L = R, since the difference is then zero. Anything above 53 kHz, such as a 67 kHz subsidiary channel, is optional extra.

L = [(L + R) + (L − R)] / 2        R = [(L + R) − (L − R)] / 2

L and R are the left and right audio voltages; (L + R) comes from the 0–15 kHz band and (L − R) from the synchronous detector. This adding and subtracting is the matrix.

The pilot at 19 kHz

The difference channel is double-sideband suppressed-carrier. Suppressing the carrier saves nearly all the transmitted power, but coherent detection needs a reference at exactly 38 kHz with the right phase. So the transmitter sends a small unmodulated tone at half that frequency, 19 kHz, called the pilot. It sits in the empty gap between 15 kHz audio and the 23 kHz lower edge of the difference channel, where it interferes with nothing and costs little: about 8 to 10 percent of the total deviation.

The receiver doubles the pilot to get 38 kHz. A frequency doubler or a phase-locked loop does this exactly: 2 × 19 kHz = 38 kHz, and the output phase is locked to the pilot. The transmitter's own subcarrier was produced by doubling the same 19 kHz oscillator, so the receiver's locally generated 38 kHz stays phase-coherent with the suppressed carrier. That is the whole reason the pilot is at half the subcarrier frequency and not at 38 kHz itself.

Phase matters. If the recovered 38 kHz is off by an angle φ, the synchronous detector scales the wanted difference by cos φ and leaks some of the sum channel into it.

S ≈ 20 log₁₀(cot φ)

S is the left-right separation in dB and φ is the pilot phase error in radians. φ = 10° gives cot φ = 5.67 and S ≈ 15 dB, poor; φ = 1° gives about 35 dB, acceptable.

The FM receiver chain

FM broadcasting uses 88 to 108 MHz with channels spaced 200 kHz apart, so a station's deviation of 75 kHz and its sidebands fit with room to spare. The receiver is a superheterodyne, and every stage has a job.

  1. Antenna picks up 88–108 MHz and the RF amplifier selects the wanted channel and sets the noise figure.
  2. The mixer with a local oscillator at f_LO = f_c + 10.7 MHz shifts the channel down to the intermediate frequency of 10.7 MHz.
  3. The IF amplifier, with a filter about 200 kHz wide, provides most of the gain and the selectivity.
  4. The limiter strips amplitude variations, so noise that arrived as AM never reaches the detector.
  5. The discriminator converts frequency deviation into voltage, giving back the composite baseband.
  6. The stereo decoder runs: a narrow 19 kHz pilot filter, a doubler, a synchronous detector for L − R, and a matrix.
  7. De-emphasis and low-pass filtering on each output cut the treble that pre-emphasis boosted, restoring flat audio response and, at the same time, cutting much of the high-frequency noise picked up in transmission.
  8. Two audio power amplifiers drive the loudspeakers.

De-emphasis must be applied to baseband signals. Because de-emphasis and the matrix are both linear, putting one de-emphasis network on each output after the matrix gives the same result as putting one network on L + R and one on L − R after detection but before the matrix. It is not equivalent to filtering the composite. A mono receiver skips the decoder entirely and applies de-emphasis to L + R.

Worked example

Question. A stereo FM transmitter is fed test tones of L = 1 V and R = 0.5 V peak. Find the matrix outputs, the frequency bands present in the composite signal, the total composite bandwidth, and the signals the receiver recovers after the matrix.

  1. Matrix sum: L + R = 1 + 0.5 = 1.5 V.
  2. Matrix difference: L − R = 1 − 0.5 = 0.5 V.
  3. The sum occupies the audio band 0 to 15 kHz. A mono receiver hears only this.
  4. The difference is DSB-SC on 38 kHz, so its sidebands span 38 − 15 = 23 kHz to 38 + 15 = 53 kHz.
  5. The pilot is added at 19 kHz, in the gap between 15 kHz and 23 kHz. Total composite bandwidth = 53 kHz.
  6. The receiver filters the pilot, doubles 19 kHz to 38 kHz, and synchronously detects, recovering L − R = 0.5 V.
  7. Matrix: L = (1.5 + 0.5)/2 = 1.0 V and R = (1.5 − 0.5)/2 = 0.5 V, matching the inputs.

Answer. L + R = 1.5 V, L − R = 0.5 V, composite bands 0–15 kHz, 19 kHz pilot and 23–53 kHz, total bandwidth 53 kHz, and the receiver recovers L = 1.0 V and R = 0.5 V.

Exam traps

  • Pilot is 19 kHz and the subcarrier is 38 kHz. Writing it the other way round is the commonest mistake; the subcarrier itself is suppressed and never transmitted.
  • The difference channel is DSB-SC, not SSB and not FM. It needs both sidebands, 23 to 53 kHz, so the composite is 53 kHz wide.
  • A mono receiver loses nothing. It hears L + R, which is the full mono programme; its 15 kHz low-pass removes the pilot and the difference channel.
  • A phase error in the doubled pilot leaks the sum into the difference. Amplitude error only changes level; phase error destroys separation.
  • The pilot and the difference channel share the 75 kHz deviation. They do not sit outside it, and the 200 kHz channel spacing is set by Carson's rule plus guard band.

Takeaways

  1. In angle modulation the message moves the instantaneous phase, and the derivative of that phase is the instantaneous frequency, so FM is the integral of the message and PM is the message itself.
  2. Narrowband FM carries one sideband pair and looks like AM, while wideband FM spreads power over many sidebands set by Bessel functions J_n(β).
  3. Carson's rule B ≈ 2(Δf + f_m) is the bandwidth you must allocate in practice, and it grows in direct proportion to how much you widen the deviation.
  4. Direct FM pulls an LC oscillator with a varactor; indirect FM builds a tiny deviation in a stable crystal oscillator and multiplies it up, which is why it holds the carrier frequency steady.
  5. Every FM detector is a frequency-to-amplitude converter followed by an envelope detector; the balanced Foster–Seeley and ratio detector versions cancel the amplitude noise that comes along.
  6. FM wins on figure of merit, but the prize is bought with bandwidth: the output SNR grows roughly as β³ while the bandwidth grows only as β.
  7. FM behaves exactly as the formulas promise only above threshold, near 10 dB carrier-to-noise; below it the output collapses and the receiver goes quiet.
  8. Pre-emphasis at the transmitter and de-emphasis at the receiver flatten the audio while pushing down the high-frequency noise that FM produces unevenly.
  9. FM stereo keeps mono receivers working by putting L+R in the baseband, L−R on a suppressed 38 kHz subcarrier, and a 19 kHz pilot to keep the two synchronized.

Glossary

Amplitude limiter
A hard-clipping IF stage that strips amplitude variations from the FM wave before the discriminator.
Angle modulation
Any modulation in which the message changes the phase angle of the carrier rather than its amplitude; FM and PM are its two forms.
Armstrong method
The indirect FM scheme: make a small-index NBFM at a stable low frequency, multiply, then mix to the final carrier.
Bessel function
The function J_n(β) that gives the amplitude of the n-th sideband pair in a sinusoidally modulated FM wave.
Capture ratio
The level difference in dB by which a wanted FM signal must exceed a co-channel unwanted one before it captures the receiver.
Carson's rule
The practical bandwidth estimate B ≈ 2(Δf + f_m) hertz for an FM signal.
De-emphasis
The matching high-frequency cut at the FM receiver that restores flat audio and removes the boosted noise.
Discriminator
A circuit that converts frequency deviation into a proportional voltage; the heart of an FM demodulator.
Figure of merit
The ratio of output signal-to-noise ratio to input carrier-to-noise ratio of a receiver, used to compare modulation schemes.
FM stereo multiplex
The composite baseband of L+R, the DSB-SC L−R on 38 kHz, and the 19 kHz pilot, all sent on one FM carrier.
Foster–Seeley discriminator
A balanced tuned-transformer diode detector whose output is an S-curve of voltage against frequency, zero at f_c.
Frequency deviation Δf
The peak amount by which the carrier frequency moves away from f_c, in hertz.
Frequency modulation
Modulation in which the instantaneous frequency of the carrier is f_c + k_f m(t), so the phase carries the integral of the message.
Instantaneous frequency
The rate at which the phase of the carrier advances, f_i = (1/2π)dθ/dt, in hertz.
Modulation index β
The ratio Δf/f_m, dimensionless; it decides how many sidebands carry significant power.
Narrowband FM
FM with β much less than 1, whose spectrum is a carrier and one sideband pair inside 2f_m.
Phase modulation
Modulation in which the instantaneous phase is shifted in proportion to the message, so the frequency deviation follows the derivative of the message.
Pilot tone
The unmodulated 19 kHz signal in FM stereo that synchronises the receiver's 38 kHz subcarrier generator.
Pre-emphasis
Boosting of high audio frequencies at the FM transmitter to equalise the rising noise spectrum at the receiver.
Ratio detector
A balanced FM detector that responds to the ratio of two tuned-circuit voltages, so it rejects amplitude noise without a separate limiter.
Subcarrier
A carrier placed above the audio baseband to carry extra information, such as the L−R stereo difference at 38 kHz.
Threshold effect
The sudden fall in FM output signal-to-noise ratio once the carrier-to-noise ratio drops to roughly 10 dB.
Varactor diode
A reverse-biased diode used as a voltage-controlled capacitor, the usual tuning element in direct FM modulators.
Wideband FM
FM with β of order 1 or more, whose spectrum spreads over many sideband pairs.
