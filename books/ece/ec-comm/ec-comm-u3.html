3.1 Frequency and phase modulation concepts

Angle modulation carries the message in the phase of a sine carrier, not in its amplitude. The envelope stays fixed, so a limiter can strip amplitude noise before detection. Phase modulation (PM) moves the phase in step with the message. Frequency modulation (FM) moves the phase in step with the integral of the message, so the instantaneous frequency follows the message itself. FM radio, TV sound, cordless phones and two-way radios use this idea. This chapter defines the two waves, their sensitivities and the modulation index, and shows how one modulator can produce the other.

You need: phasors, the Fourier transform and the complex envelope (see Hilbert transform and complex envelope).

Phase is the angle that moves

A sine carrier is s(t) = Ac cos θ(t). Ac is the peak amplitude in volts; θ(t) is the instantaneous phase in radians. For an unmodulated carrier θ(t) = 2πfc t, with fc in hertz, so the angle grows at 2πfc radians per second. If θ(t) is made to change in some other way, that change can carry information.

Define the instantaneous frequency fi(t) = (1/2π) dθ/dt. It is the frequency the carrier would show over a very short window, in hertz; the factor 1/2π turns radians per second into cycles per second. For the unmodulated carrier, fi = fc. In angle modulation θ(t) = 2πfc t + φ(t), where φ(t) is the excess phase in radians, so fi(t) = fc + (1/2π) dφ/dt.

There are two ways to put the message in. In PM the message sets the excess phase φ(t) itself. In FM the message sets the rate of change dφ/dt, so the instantaneous frequency follows the message and φ(t) holds the integral of the message.

As a phasor: a vector of length Ac rotates at fc; modulation speeds it up and slows it down, but its tip stays on the circle of radius Ac, so the envelope stays Ac.

s(t) = Ac cos θ(t), fi(t) = (1/2π) dθ/dt

Ac is the peak carrier amplitude in V; θ(t) is the instantaneous phase in rad; fi(t) is the instantaneous frequency in Hz.

PM and FM side by side

In PM the excess phase is proportional to the message: φ(t) = kp m(t), so s(t) = Ac cos(2πfc t + kp m(t)). kp is the phase sensitivity in radians per volt. For a tone m(t) = Am cos(2πfm t), the peak phase deviation is Δθ = kp Am radians. The frequency shifts by (1/2π) dφ/dt = −kp Am fm sin(2πfm t), so the peak frequency deviation is Δf = kp Am fm. For the same phase swing, a higher fm gives a larger frequency swing.

In FM the excess phase is proportional to the integral of the message: φ(t) = 2πkf ∫ m(τ) dτ, taken from 0 to t. kf is the frequency sensitivity in hertz per volt. For the same tone, fi(t) = fc + kf Am cos(2πfm t), so the peak deviation is Δf = kf Am. The phase term becomes (kf Am/fm) sin(2πfm t). Its peak, the modulation index β = Δf/fm, is the peak phase deviation of the FM wave in radians. For PM with a tone the equivalent index is kp Am, and it does not change when fm changes.

PM: s(t) = Ac cos(2πfc t + kp m(t))
FM: s(t) = Ac cos(2πfc t + 2πkf ∫m(τ)dτ)

kp is the phase sensitivity in rad/V; kf is the frequency sensitivity in Hz/V; m(t) is the message in V; the integral runs from 0 to t.

ΔfFM = kf Am, β = Δf/fm, ΔθPM = kp Am, ΔfPM = kp Am fm

Am is the tone peak in V; fm is the tone frequency in Hz; Δf is in Hz; β and Δθ are in rad.

One modulator builds the other

PM and FM are close relatives. A phase modulator fed with the integral of m(t) produces FM, because its phase becomes proportional to ∫m. So an integrator followed by a phase modulator is an FM generator; this is the start of the indirect method in FM generation. A frequency modulator fed with dm/dt produces PM, because a frequency modulator's phase is the integral of its input, and the integral of dm/dt is m(t) itself. So a differentiator followed by a frequency modulator is a PM generator.

Some syllabi call kf and kp deviation constants. Keep the units straight: kf times volts gives hertz, kp times volts gives radians.

Constant envelope does not mean a narrow spectrum: a large deviation spreads the carrier into many sidebands (see Narrowband and wideband FM). The one line to remember: PM ties phase to m(t), FM ties frequency to m(t).

Worked example

Question. A 5 kHz tone of amplitude 2 V drives an FM modulator with kf = 3 kHz/V. Find Δf and β. The same tone then drives a PM modulator with kp = 0.8 rad/V: find the peak phase deviation and the equivalent peak frequency deviation.

  1. FM: Δf = kf Am = 3 kHz/V × 2 V = 6 kHz.
  2. β = Δf/fm = 6 kHz / 5 kHz = 1.2 (no unit; it is the peak phase swing in radians).
  3. PM: Δθ = kp Am = 0.8 rad/V × 2 V = 1.6 rad.
  4. Equivalent Δf for PM = Δθ × fm = 1.6 × 5000 Hz = 8000 Hz = 8 kHz.
  5. The FM swing peaks when m(t) = +2 V; the PM swing peaks where m(t) crosses zero (Figure 3.1.1).

Answer. FM: Δf = 6 kHz, β = 1.2. PM: Δθ = 1.6 rad, equivalent Δf = 8 kHz.

Worked example 2

Question. An angle-modulated wave is s(t) = 10 cos(2π × 10⁸ t + 5 sin 2π × 10³ t) V. Find the instantaneous frequency and its largest and smallest values, Δf, β and the power in a 1 Ω load. Then find the message m(t) if the wave is FM with kf = 2.5 kHz/V, and if it is PM with kp = 2.5 rad/V.

  1. θ(t) = 2π × 10⁸ t + 5 sin(2π × 10³ t), so fi = (1/2π) dθ/dt = 10⁸ + 5 × 10³ cos(2π × 10³ t) Hz.
  2. fi swings between 100.005 MHz and 99.995 MHz, so Δf = 5 kHz.
  3. fm = 1 kHz, so β = Δf/fm = 5, the same as the peak of the phase term.
  4. Power = Ac²/2 = 10²/2 = 50 W in 1 Ω. Angle modulation does not change it.
  5. If FM: fi − fc = kf m(t), so m(t) = 5000 cos(2π × 10³ t)/2500 = 2 cos(2π × 10³ t) V.
  6. If PM: φ(t) = kp m(t), so m(t) = 5 sin(2π × 10³ t)/2.5 = 2 sin(2π × 10³ t) V.

Answer. fi from 99.995 to 100.005 MHz; Δf = 5 kHz, β = 5, P = 50 W. FM message 2 cos(2π × 10³ t) V; PM message 2 sin(2π × 10³ t) V.

Exam traps

  • β is not Δf. Δf = kf Am depends on the message amplitude only; β = Δf/fm also depends on the message frequency. In FM, β falls as fm rises; in PM with a tone, kp Am is fixed and the equivalent Δf rises with fm.
  • The FM phase carries 2πkf ∫m dt. Without the 2π the phase is in cycles, not radians, so the deviation worked out from it is 2π times too small. Keep kf in Hz/V and keep the 2π.
  • PM and FM both have a constant envelope, but neither has a constant instantaneous frequency. An envelope detector cannot recover the message; a frequency or phase detector is needed.
  • Integrator and differentiator are easy to swap: integrator then phase modulator gives FM; differentiator then frequency modulator gives PM.
  • A tone-modulated wave written as Ac cos(2πfc t + β sin 2πfm t) could be FM or PM. Find the message only from the modulator type and its constant (worked example 2).

3.2 Narrowband and wideband FM; Carson's rule

The bandwidth an FM wave needs grows with its modulation index β = Δf/fm. For small β, narrowband FM has a carrier and one sideband pair, like AM but with the pair turned 90° to the carrier, so an envelope detector cannot recover the tone. For large β, wideband FM spreads into many sidebands spaced fm apart, sized by Bessel functions. Carson's rule, BT ≈ 2(Δf + fm), gives the channel width to allow. Broadcast FM, two-way radio and FM links use this estimate to plan spectrum.

You need: the FM wave and the modulation index β (see Frequency and phase modulation concepts).

Narrowband FM: a carrier and one sideband pair

Take a tone-modulated FM wave s(t) = Ac cos(2πfc t + β sin 2πfm t). If β is small, cos(β sin x) ≈ 1 and sin(β sin x) ≈ β sin x, so:

s(t) ≈ Ac cos 2πfc t − βAc sin 2πfm t · sin 2πfc t

Ac is the carrier amplitude in V; fc the carrier and fm the tone frequency in Hz; β = Δf/fm.

Expanding the product gives a carrier plus one pair: Ac cos 2πfc t + (βAc/2)[cos 2π(fc + fm)t − cos 2π(fc − fm)t]. Each sideband has amplitude βAc/2. The lower one carries a minus sign, so the pair adds up at 90° to the carrier. The envelope therefore stays close to Ac and holds almost no message; the message is in the phase. (AM has the same three lines but with both sidebands positive, which is why AM moves the envelope.)

This is narrowband FM (NBFM). The bandwidth for one tone is 2fm, or 2W for a message band of width W. A working test: β ≤ 0.2, where the second sideband pair is only 5 % of the first (J2(0.2)/J1(0.2) = 0.050). Some books allow up to 0.3. NBFM is used in voice radios and as the first stage of indirect FM generation.

Wideband FM: Bessel sidebands

For larger β the small-angle steps fail and the exact expansion is needed. The wave becomes a sum of lines at fc + n fm, for every whole number n, positive and negative.

s(t) = Ac Σ Jn(β) cos 2π(fc + n fm)t

Jn(β) is the Bessel function of the first kind, order n; line n sits at fc + n fm with amplitude Ac |Jn(β)|; lines are spaced fm apart; J−n(β) = (−1)ⁿ Jn(β).

A negative Jn(β) is a 180° phase reversal, not negative power; power uses Jn². The carrier line is Ac J0(β), which vanishes where J0(β) = 0; the first such β is 2.405, used to calibrate deviation. Because Σ Jn²(β) = 1 over all n, the total power stays Ac²/2 (watts in a 1 Ω load) whatever β is: modulation only moves power from the carrier to the sidebands. Carson's rule and the comparison

Beyond about n = β + 1 the lines fall away fast. Counting β + 1 significant pairs, each spaced fm, gives a total span of 2(β + 1)fm. This is Carson's rule; it holds about 98 % or more of the power.

BT ≈ 2(β + 1)fm = 2(Δf + fm)

BT is the transmission bandwidth in Hz; β the modulation index; fm the highest modulating frequency in Hz; Δf the peak deviation in Hz. With a message band W, β becomes the deviation ratio D = Δf/W.

For large β, BT ≈ 2Δf, the band the carrier sweeps; for small β, BT ≈ 2fm, the NBFM band. Carson's value is always 2fm above 2Δf, so 2Δf becomes a good estimate only when β is large. Broadcast FM with Δf = 75 kHz and W = 15 kHz has D = 5 and BT = 180 kHz. Some syllabi call this the transmission bandwidth of FM.

Worked example

Question. An FM broadcast transmitter has peak deviation Δf = 75 kHz and the highest modulating tone fm = 15 kHz. Find β, the significant sideband pairs by β + 1, the Carson bandwidth and the swept band 2Δf.

  1. β = Δf/fm = 75/15 = 5.
  2. Significant pairs ≈ β + 1 = 6.
  3. BT = 2(β + 1)fm = 2 × 6 × 15 kHz = 180 kHz.
  4. Check with the other form: 2(Δf + fm) = 2(75 + 15) = 180 kHz.
  5. 2Δf = 150 kHz. This is only the band the carrier sweeps; the allocation follows Carson, 180 kHz.

Answer. β = 5, about 6 pairs, BT = 180 kHz, 2Δf = 150 kHz.

Exam traps

  • Carson's rule is 2(β + 1)fm = 2(Δf + fm), not 2Δf and not 2βfm; those are only its large-β limit.
  • β = Δf/fm is dimensionless. Do not use Δf/fc: the carrier sets where the spectrum sits, not how wide it is.
  • NBFM and AM both show three lines, but the NBFM lower sideband is negative. An envelope detector gives almost no message from NBFM.
  • Sidebands are spaced fm apart, not 2fm. The pair at fc ± fm is one pair.
  • Jn(β) can be negative. That is a 180° phase reversal; the power of a line uses Jn², and the total stays Ac²/2.

3.3 FM generation: direct and indirect methods

FM generation makes a carrier's instantaneous frequency follow the message. There are two routes. A direct generator pulls the frequency of an oscillator with the message voltage, using a varactor diode or a reactance modulator. An indirect (Armstrong) generator makes narrowband FM from a stable crystal, then multiplies carrier and deviation together and uses a mixer to move the carrier to the channel. Direct circuits are simple but drift; indirect ones are stable because a crystal sets every frequency. Broadcast exciters, two-way radios and phase-locked synthesisers use one of these ideas.

You need: β and the narrowband test (see Narrowband and wideband FM).

Direct FM: the message tunes the oscillator

In direct FM the message voltage acts inside the oscillator. The tank that sets the frequency contains a part whose value changes with voltage, so the oscillator frequency follows the message. This is a voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO).

The usual part is a varactor diode: a p-n junction held in reverse bias, which behaves as a capacitor whose value falls as the reverse voltage rises. Across the LC tank it makes the resonant frequency depend on the message:

f0 = 1 / (2π√(L C))

f0 is the oscillator frequency in Hz; L is the tank inductance in H; C is the total tank capacitance in F, including the varactor.

For a small change ΔC, f0 changes by Δf ≈ −f0 ΔC/(2C): a 1 % rise in C lowers the frequency by 0.5 %. A reactance modulator does the same job with a transistor whose feedback network makes it look like a capacitor of value proportional to its transconductance gm; the message changes gm and so the reactance across the tank.

Two limits matter. First, f0 depends on 1/√C and C on the voltage in a curved way, so equal message steps do not give equal frequency steps; keep the deviation small or correct it with a network (worked example 1 shows the effect). Second, the centre frequency drifts with temperature, bias and supply. An automatic frequency control (AFC) loop compares the oscillator with a crystal reference and feeds back a slow correcting voltage. AFC corrects slow drift, not the audio deviation.

Indirect FM: Armstrong's multiplier chain

Edwin Armstrong's method keeps the carrier crystal-stable. The audio is integrated and fed to a narrowband phase modulator driven by a crystal oscillator, which gives NBFM (the integrator turns PM into FM, as in Frequency and phase modulation concepts). The phase modulator is linear only for a small angle, so the deviation it makes is tiny: β must stay below about 0.2 even at the lowest audio frequency, where β = Δf/fm is largest.

A frequency multiplier (a nonlinear stage tuned to the Nth harmonic) multiplies the instantaneous frequency by N, so carrier and deviation both scale:

fc' = N fc, Δf' = N Δf, β' = N β

fc is the input carrier and Δf the input deviation in Hz; N is a whole number; primed values are after the multiplier; fm does not change.

A mixer adds or subtracts a local oscillator frequency fLO. It shifts the carrier only: fc″ = |fc′ ± fLO| while Δf″ = Δf′, because shifting every frequency by the same amount does not change how far the instantaneous frequency swings. A band-pass filter keeps the wanted sum or difference.

The deviation needed (75 kHz from tens of hertz) calls for N in the thousands, which would push the carrier far above the channel. So the chain is split: multiply by N1, mix the carrier down, then multiply by N2. The total deviation gain is N1N2; the mixer sets the final carrier. Worked example

Question. A direct FM oscillator has a 1 µH coil and a total tank capacitance of 25 pF at rest. The message swings the varactor capacitance by ±0.5 pF. Find the rest frequency, the frequencies at the two extremes, the deviation from the small-change formula, and comment on linearity.

  1. f0 = 1/(2π√(1 × 10⁻⁶ × 25 × 10⁻¹²)) = 1/(2π × 5 × 10⁻⁹) = 31.831 MHz.
  2. C = 24.5 pF (capacitance falls): f = 32.154 MHz, a rise of +323.2 kHz.
  3. C = 25.5 pF: f = 31.517 MHz, a fall of −313.6 kHz.
  4. Small-change formula: Δf ≈ f0 ΔC/(2C) = 31.831 MHz × 0.5/(2 × 25) = 318.3 kHz.
  5. The upward swing is 9.6 kHz larger than the downward one, about 3 % of the deviation. Equal capacitance steps give unequal frequency steps, which is second-harmonic distortion of the audio; a smaller swing or a correcting network reduces it.

Answer. f0 = 31.83 MHz; extremes 32.154 and 31.517 MHz (+323.2 kHz, −313.6 kHz); Δf ≈ 318.3 kHz; the 3 % mismatch is the nonlinearity of direct FM.

Worked example 2

Question. Design an Armstrong transmitter for a 96 MHz carrier with 75 kHz deviation. The crystal is 100 kHz; the narrowband phase modulator gives 20 Hz deviation, and the lowest audio frequency is 100 Hz. Use two multipliers, ×75 and ×50, with a mixer between them. Check the narrowband condition, then find the frequencies at each stage and the local oscillator.

  1. Narrowband check at the lowest audio frequency: β = 20/100 = 0.2, the limit; at 15 kHz, β = 20/15 000 = 0.0013.
  2. Total multiplication needed: N = 75 000/20 = 3750 = 75 × 50.
  3. After ×75: carrier 100 kHz × 75 = 7.5 MHz; deviation 20 × 75 = 1.5 kHz.
  4. The final ×50 must start from 96 MHz/50 = 1.92 MHz.
  5. Mixer: fLO = 7.5 − 1.92 = 5.58 MHz, taking the difference (9.42 MHz with the other sideband would also work). Deviation stays 1.5 kHz.
  6. After ×50: carrier 1.92 × 50 = 96 MHz; deviation 1.5 kHz × 50 = 75 kHz. β at 15 kHz = 75/15 = 5.

Answer. Multipliers 75 × 50 = 3750; 7.5 MHz and 1.5 kHz after the first, 5.58 MHz LO to 1.92 MHz, then 96 MHz with 75 kHz deviation.

Exam traps

  • A multiplier scales carrier and deviation together. A mixer shifts the carrier only; it can never change the deviation.
  • Check the narrowband condition at the lowest audio frequency, where β = Δf/fm is largest, not at 15 kHz.
  • Do not use one long multiplier and then complain the carrier overshoots: the mixer between the two multipliers is what lets the deviation reach 75 kHz while the carrier lands on the channel.
  • Varactor capacitance falls as reverse voltage rises, so the frequency rises with reverse voltage. Equal capacitance steps do not give equal frequency steps.
  • Centre-frequency drift and deviation are different. AFC corrects slow drift of the centre, not the audio deviation.

3.4 FM demodulation: slope, Foster-Seeley, ratio detector

An FM receiver must turn frequency into voltage. The classic circuits do it in two steps: a tuned circuit turns the frequency swing into an amplitude or phase change, and diodes turn that into a voltage. One detuned tuned circuit gives the slope detector. Two balanced circuits do better. The Foster-Seeley discriminator uses a transformer whose secondary phase turns with frequency, and the ratio detector adds a large capacitor that gives it built-in limiting. These circuits fill the 10.7 MHz IF strips of older broadcast receivers; most newer chips use quadrature or digital detectors.

You need: the diode envelope detector (see AM demodulation: envelope and coherent detectors) and the FM wave (see Frequency and phase modulation concepts).

Slope detector: one detuned tuned circuit

A parallel tuned circuit's voltage peaks at its resonant frequency fr and falls away on both flanks. Tune fr a little to one side of the carrier fc, so the carrier sits on a flank. As the FM wave swings around fc, the tank voltage swings with it, and a diode envelope detector reads that voltage. Frequency deviation has become amplitude.

|V/Vpeak| = 1/√(1 + (2Q(f − fr)/fr)²)

Q is the quality factor of the tank; f is the instantaneous frequency and fr the resonant frequency, both in Hz; the formula holds close to resonance.

Two faults. The flank is straight only over a small part of the bandwidth fr/Q, so a wide deviation is distorted (worked example 1). And the circuit is single-ended: any AM on the incoming wave, from noise or fading, passes straight to the audio, so a limiter must come first. A higher Q gives a steeper slope, more volts per kilohertz, but a shorter straight part.

The balanced slope detector uses one primary tuned to fc and two secondary tanks, one tuned above fc and one below, each with its own diode. The outputs are subtracted. The frequency effects have opposite signs and add, giving a steeper and straighter curve that is zero at fc; equal amplitude changes cancel. Its cost is alignment: the three tuned circuits must be trimmed against each other.

Foster-Seeley discriminator

One transformer: the primary is tuned to fc, and the secondary is also tuned to fc and centre-tapped. A coupling capacitor and an RF choke put the primary voltage ep between the centre tap and the middle of the two diode loads. Each half of the secondary carries es/2, the two halves in antiphase.

A tuned secondary driven through mutual inductance is 90° out of phase with the primary at resonance. Off resonance the angle changes: with es/2 = j ep (k/(1 + jx)), where x = 2Q(f − fc)/fc, the angle is 90° − arctan x. Above fc it falls below 90°; below fc it rises above 90°. The secondary phase turns with frequency, and that is the mechanism.

Diode D1 sees the phasor sum ep + es/2 and diode D2 sees ep − es/2. Each diode with its RC load is an envelope detector, so V1 = |ep + es/2| and V2 = |ep − es/2|. The output is vo = V1 − V2. At fc the two sums have equal length and vo = 0. Above fc, V1 grows and V2 shrinks, so vo > 0; below fc the reverse. Plotted against frequency this is the S-curve, odd about fc, so the sign of the output says which side of fc the signal is on (Figure 3.4.1).

Near fc the secondary phase changes almost linearly with frequency, and near 90° the difference of the two lengths changes almost linearly with the phase; one nearly linear law applied after the other gives a wide straight region. Both V1 and V2 scale with the input amplitude, so at fc an amplitude change cancels, but away from fc it does not: a limiter still goes before the Foster-Seeley. An integrator after the output turns the same circuit into a PM detector.

Ratio detector, and the family compared

Turn one diode round and connect a large capacitor C across both loads. Now the diodes charge C to V1 + V2, not V1 − V2, and the audio is taken between the load midpoint and the capacitor midpoint, giving (V1 − V2)/2. The time constant of C with the loads is long, many audio cycles, so V1 + V2 is held almost constant. A sudden rise in input amplitude tries to raise both voltages; the capacitor soaks it up, and the output, which now depends only on the ratio V1/V2, follows frequency alone.

That is the built-in limiting, and many receivers use the ratio detector with only a light limiter or none. The price: half the output of a Foster-Seeley for the same input, limiting that fails for fast or deep amplitude changes, and diodes that must be matched.

Exam traps

  • A single slope detector's output is not zero at the carrier: it sits at a bias point on the flank. The balanced slope, Foster-Seeley and ratio detectors give zero at fc.
  • The S-curve is straight only over a limited range; beyond its peaks (±328 kHz in worked example 2) the output falls again, so more deviation does not give more output.
  • The Foster-Seeley secondary is tuned to fc, the same as the primary. The 90° comes from the tuned secondary, not from detuning.
  • The ratio detector limits only slow or moderate amplitude changes. Do not write that it removes all AM.

3.5 Noise in AM and FM receivers; figure of merit

Noise enters a receiver with the signal, and each detector treats it differently. The figure of merit (FOM) puts that difference into one number: the output signal-to-noise ratio divided by the channel signal-to-noise ratio, for the same received power and the same noise. It lets you compare AM, DSB-SC, SSB and FM before building anything. AM with an envelope detector reaches at most 1/3; DSB-SC and SSB with coherent detection reach 1; FM reaches (3/2)β², because wider deviation makes the message stand further above the discriminator noise. Link budgets for broadcast, satellite and two-way radio start from these numbers.

You need: white noise and its power spectral density (see Noise sources, white noise and AWGN), and the AM and DSB-SC detectors (see AM demodulation: envelope and coherent detectors).

The receiver model and the figure of merit

Model the receiver as a band-pass filter of the transmission bandwidth, a detector, and a low-pass filter of the message bandwidth W. The channel adds white noise of two-sided power spectral density N0/2 W/Hz. After the band-pass filter the noise is narrowband and can be written n(t) = nc(t) cos 2πfc t − ns(t) sin 2πfc t. The in-phase part nc(t) and the quadrature part ns(t) are low-pass noises, each with power spectral density N0 over the filter band, and each carries the same power as n(t).

Let P be the average received signal power in watts, carrier and sidebands together. The channel signal-to-noise ratio is the received power over the noise power in the message band, (S/N)c = P/(N0 W): the SNR a plain baseband system would get with the same power. The output signal-to-noise ratio (S/N)o is the message power over the noise power after the detector and the low-pass filter. Then:

FOM = (S/N)o / (S/N)c

both ratios as powers, for the same P, N0 and W; FOM above 1 means the scheme does better than plain baseband, below 1 worse.

Some books measure the FM input noise in the transmission bandwidth BT instead of W. Their input SNR is smaller by BT/W, so their FOM is larger by BT/W = 2(β + 1). Always check which bandwidth a quoted FOM uses.

AM, DSB-SC and SSB

AM with an envelope detector: the signal is Ac[1 + m cos 2πfm t] cos 2πfc t, with total power P = (Ac²/2)(1 + m²/2). With a carrier well above the noise, the envelope is about Ac + Ac m cos 2πfm t + nc(t). After the dc block the message is Ac m cos 2πfm t, power Ac²m²/2, and the noise is nc(t) over |f| ≤ W, power 2N0W. So (S/N)o = Ac²m²/(4N0W), while (S/N)c = Ac²(1 + m²/2)/(2N0W). Dividing:

FOMAM = m² / (2 + m²)

m is the modulation index, 0 ≤ m ≤ 1; the largest value, at m = 1, is 1/3 (−4.77 dB).

The carrier takes power without carrying the message, and that is the whole loss. With a weak carrier the envelope detector also has its own threshold, which the coherent detector does not.

DSB-SC with coherent detection: the signal Ac m(t) cos 2πfc t has power Ac²Pm/2, where Pm is the message power. Multiplying by cos 2πfc t and low-pass filtering gives (Ac/2)m(t) + (1/2)nc(t): signal power Ac²Pm/4 and noise power 2N0W/4. So (S/N)o = Ac²Pm/(2N0W) = P/(N0W), and FOM = 1. The coherent product rejects ns.

SSB with coherent detection: the receiver filter is only W wide, so half the noise enters, but the detected message power is also halved. The two halves cancel: FOM = 1, in half the bandwidth.

FM: the discriminator shapes the noise

With a strong carrier the noise mainly tilts the carrier phasor; the phase error is about ns(t)/Ac. The discriminator outputs (1/2π) times the rate of change of phase, and differentiating multiplies each noise component at frequency f by f. So the output noise power spectral density is N0f²/Ac² across the IF band: flat noise in, parabolic noise out. Only |f| ≤ W gets through the low-pass filter, so:

No = ∫ N0f²/Ac² df (−W to W) = 2N0W³/(3Ac²)

No is the output noise power; the output signal is the frequency swing Δf cos 2πfm t, power Δf²/2.

So (S/N)o = (Δf²/2)/(2N0W³/3Ac²) = (3/2)β² × (Ac²/2)/(N0W) with fm = W, and (S/N)c = (Ac²/2)/(N0W). Dividing gives FOMFM = (3/2)β². At β = 5 this is 37.5, or 15.74 dB. Doubling β quadruples the FOM but roughly doubles the bandwidth: FM trades bandwidth for SNR. The result needs the carrier above the FM threshold (see Threshold and capture effects).

3.6 Threshold and capture effects in FM

FM gives its large noise improvement only while the received carrier is well above the noise. When the carrier-to-noise ratio in the IF bandwidth falls to about 10 dB, the output starts to crackle with clicks and the output SNR drops far faster than the ideal law. This is the threshold effect. The same nonlinear behaviour of the limiter and discriminator gives the capture effect: of two FM signals on one channel, the stronger takes over the output once it exceeds the weaker by the capture ratio, and the weaker is largely suppressed. Threshold and capture decide FM coverage and how closely channels can be reused.

You need: the FM figure of merit (see Noise in AM and FM receivers).

Why the threshold exists

Measure the received carrier against the noise in the IF bandwidth BT: the carrier-to-noise ratio ρ = C/(N0BT). (The channel SNR of the figure-of-merit chapter measures the noise in W instead, so it is larger by BT/W.) Above threshold the output SNR follows a straight line: every extra dB of ρ gives one extra dB out, plus a fixed FM improvement.

(S/N)o = 3β²(β + 1) ρ

ρ = C/(N0BT) is the carrier-to-noise ratio in BT = 2(β + 1)W; for β = 5 the factor 3β²(β + 1) = 450, a 26.5 dB improvement.

This is the (3/2)β² of the figure of merit rewritten with the noise in BT. It assumes the carrier phasor is much longer than the noise phasor, so the noise only tilts it a little. When the noise is nearly as large as the carrier, now and then it swings the resultant phasor right round the origin. The phase jumps by 2π in a moment, and the discriminator, which differentiates the phase, gives a sharp spike: a click. Clicks carry large power, so the output SNR collapses.

The threshold is usually defined as the ρ at which the output SNR is 1 dB below the straight line. For a tone with moderate β it lies near ρ = 10 dB; some books use 13 dB (ρ = 20) as the onset of clicks. Because BT grows with β, the carrier power at threshold grows too: in channel-SNR terms the threshold is about 20(β + 1) for ρ = 10. Wider deviation gives a better output above threshold but needs a stronger carrier to reach it.

The two-region picture and threshold extension

Figure 3.6.1 draws the straight-line law for the two receivers of the worked examples against received carrier power. Each line starts at its threshold point; below it, the dashed arrows only show that the output falls steeply, since the straight-line law no longer gives values there.

Threshold extension moves the knee to a lower ρ. An FM feedback (FMFB) receiver feeds the demodulated output back to its local oscillator, so the IF sees a compressed deviation and can use a narrower filter, letting in less noise. A phase-locked loop (PLL) detector tracks the carrier phase with a narrow loop and produces fewer clicks. Both trade circuit complexity for a few dB of threshold; few syllabi ask for more than the names, the aim and a block diagram.

Capture effect

When two FM signals share a channel, the limiter sees their sum. The resultant phasor follows the stronger one, and the weaker only wobbles its phase a little; after the discriminator the weaker programme is pushed far down. The capture ratio is the smallest power ratio, in dB, of the stronger to the weaker signal at which the receiver follows the stronger cleanly. Good broadcast receivers have capture ratios of a few dB.

This helps FM broadcasting: a distant co-channel station is weaker at your receiver, so it is captured out, with far less co-channel interference than AM would suffer. It also helped analog cellular reuse: the same channel could be used again in a cell far enough away for the wanted base station to exceed the interferer by the capture ratio. The catch: if the interferer becomes stronger, for instance as a car drives between two stations, the receiver suddenly switches to it. Capture ratio is a two-signal specification; threshold is a signal-to-noise specification.

3.7 Pre-emphasis and de-emphasis

After the FM discriminator, noise power grows with audio frequency, so the treble sounds hissy. Pre-emphasis boosts the treble in the transmitter before modulation; de-emphasis cuts it by the same amount in the receiver after detection. The programme comes back flat, but the high-frequency noise is cut on the way. Broadcast FM uses a 50 µs time constant in most of the world and 75 µs in the Americas and South Korea. The same trick is used in TV sound, wireless microphones and analogue tape.

You need: the parabolic noise spectrum of FM (see Noise in AM and FM receivers).

Why the treble is noisiest

The noise at the receiver input is white. The discriminator turns a frequency offset into a voltage, so a noise component f hertz from the carrier gives an output voltage proportional to f, and a power proportional to f². Across the audio band the noise power density rises as a parabola; the amplitude rises as a straight line, the noise triangle.

Speech and music have most of their energy at low and middle frequencies; high notes and consonants are weaker. So the treble has the least signal exactly where the noise is greatest, and the treble SNR is poor even when the overall SNR looks good.

Pre-emphasis fixes this before transmission. The transmitter passes the audio through a network that boosts the high frequencies; the receiver passes the discriminator output through the inverse network. The programme is boosted and then cut back, so it ends where it started. The noise is added in between, so it is only cut. One condition: the boost raises the peak audio voltage and hence the deviation, so the deviation limit must be set after the pre-emphasis network.

The two responses and the improvement

The pre-emphasis network is a first-order boost set by a time constant τ = RC.

|Hpre(f)| = √(1 + (f/f1)²), f1 = 1/(2πτ)

f is the audio frequency in Hz; f1 the corner frequency in Hz; τ in seconds. Below f1 the gain is about 1; above it the gain rises at 6 dB per octave.

|Hde(f)| = 1/√(1 + (f/f1)²)

the same f1 at both ends, so the two responses multiply to 1 at every frequency.

A practical pre-emphasis network (R1 in parallel with C, then a shunt R2) has a second corner well above the audio band, so the boost stops rising there. The table gives the two standards.

Time constant τCorner f1Used in

With de-emphasis, the output noise is the parabola f² weighted by 1/(1 + (f/f1)²). Integrating both over 0 to W and dividing gives the improvement in output SNR (the deviation is reset so the signal power is unchanged):

I = (W/f1)³ / [3((W/f1) − arctan(W/f1))]

W is the highest audio frequency in Hz; arctan in radians; I is a power ratio. For W much larger than f1 it tends to (1/3)(W/f1)², which underestimates I by 1 to 1.5 dB at broadcast values.

Placing the networks

Put pre-emphasis in the audio path before the FM modulator, and de-emphasis after the discriminator, before the audio amplifier. The transmitter network must see the microphone or line signal, not the modulated carrier; the receiver network must see the recovered audio, not the IF.

The time constants must match. With a 75 µs transmitter and a 50 µs receiver, the treble is cut too little, so the sound is bright and hissy. With a 50 µs transmitter and a 75 µs receiver, the treble is cut too much and the sound is dull.

In a stereo receiver de-emphasis goes after the stereo decoder, on the recovered left and right signals (or on L + R and L − R after detection). It must not act on the composite baseband, where the L − R signal sits at 23 to 53 kHz and would be cut almost flat, and the 19 kHz pilot would be weakened (see FM stereo multiplexing).

Worked example

Question. A broadcast FM system uses 75 µs pre-emphasis and a 15 kHz audio band. Find the corner frequency, the boost at the corner and at 15 kHz, and the SNR improvement from the exact formula and from the large-W approximation.

3.8 FM stereo multiplexing and FM receivers

A stereo FM station must send two audio signals, left and right, on one carrier. It builds a composite baseband: the sum L + R in the ordinary audio band, so mono radios still work; a small 19 kHz pilot tone; and the difference L − R on a suppressed 38 kHz subcarrier. The receiver rebuilds the 38 kHz carrier from the pilot, detects the difference, and adds and subtracts to get L and R back. This chapter also follows the whole superheterodyne FM receiver from antenna to loudspeakers.

You need: DSB-SC and coherent detection, frequency division multiplexing and the superheterodyne receiver (see the units on amplitude modulation systems), and Pre-emphasis and de-emphasis.

The composite baseband

Sending L and R on two carriers would double the spectrum, so the transmitter combines them into one baseband signal and frequency modulates the carrier with it. Stereo FM is frequency-division multiplexing done before the modulator.

Compatibility decides the layout. A mono receiver tuned to a stereo station must still sound right, so the sum L + R goes in the plain audio band, 0 to 15 kHz. A mono set low-pass filters its discriminator output at 15 kHz and plays L + R, the whole programme.

The difference L − R carries the stereo information. It modulates a 38 kHz subcarrier as DSB-SC, so it occupies 38 − 15 = 23 kHz to 38 + 15 = 53 kHz. The stereo baseband is therefore 0 to 53 kHz (Figure 3.8.1). Services above 53 kHz, such as RDS data at 57 kHz, are optional extras.

L = [(L + R) + (L − R)]/2, R = [(L + R) − (L − R)]/2

L and R are the left and right audio voltages; (L + R) comes from the 0–15 kHz band and (L − R) from the 38 kHz detector. This adding and subtracting is the matrix.

The 19 kHz pilot

The difference channel is DSB-SC, which saves the power a 38 kHz carrier would take, but coherent detection needs a 38 kHz reference of the right phase. So the transmitter sends a small unmodulated tone at half that frequency, 19 kHz: the pilot. It sits in the empty gap between 15 kHz and 23 kHz and uses about 8 to 10 % of the total modulation.

The receiver filters out the pilot and doubles it with a phase-locked loop or a doubler: 2 × 19 = 38 kHz. The transmitter made its own 38 kHz subcarrier by doubling the same 19 kHz oscillator, so the receiver's 38 kHz is locked in phase to the suppressed carrier. A pilot at half the frequency also cannot be confused with anything in the L − R band, which starts at 23 kHz.

Phase matters. If the recovered 38 kHz is off by an angle φ, the synchronous detector delivers (L − R)cos φ instead of L − R. Then the left output becomes L(1 + cos φ)/2 + R(1 − cos φ)/2, and some right signal leaks into the left channel.

separation = 20 log[(1 + cos φ)/(1 − cos φ)] dB

φ is the phase error of the recovered 38 kHz subcarrier. φ = 10° gives 42.3 dB; φ = 30° gives 22.9 dB.

The FM receiver chain

FM broadcasting uses 88 to 108 MHz with a peak deviation of 75 kHz. The receiver is a superheterodyne:

  • The RF amplifier selects the band, sets the noise figure and gives some image rejection.
  • The mixer with a local oscillator, usually fLO = fc + 10.7 MHz, moves the station to the 10.7 MHz intermediate frequency.
  • The IF amplifier, with a filter about 200 kHz wide, gives most of the gain and the selectivity.
  • The limiter strips amplitude changes, so noise that arrived as AM never reaches the detector.
  • The discriminator (ratio detector, quadrature detector or a digital equivalent) gives back the composite baseband.
  • The stereo decoder: a 19 kHz pilot filter and doubler, a synchronous detector for L − R, and the matrix.
  • De-emphasis on each output restores a flat response and cuts the treble noise; two audio amplifiers drive the loudspeakers.

Because the IF is 10.7 MHz, the image of a station lies 21.4 MHz above it, outside the 20 MHz wide FM band, so a modest RF filter rejects it. Stereo widens the signal: by Carson's rule a mono signal needs 2(75 + 15) = 180 kHz, but with the 53 kHz baseband it needs 2(75 + 53) = 256 kHz. Stations in one area are therefore kept several channels apart. De-emphasis must act on the separate outputs (or on L + R and L − R after detection), never on the composite, where it would crush the 23–53 kHz difference band and the pilot.

Worked example

Question. A stereo FM transmitter is fed test tones of L = 1 V and R = 0.5 V peak. Find the matrix outputs, the bands present in the composite signal and its total width, and the signals the receiver recovers.

Exam traps

  • The pilot is 19 kHz and the subcarrier 38 kHz. The 38 kHz carrier itself is suppressed and never sent.
  • The difference channel is DSB-SC, not SSB and not FM. It needs both sidebands, 23 to 53 kHz.

  • The pilot and L − R share the 75 kHz peak deviation; they do not add to it. In Carson's rule use the highest baseband frequency, 53 kHz, for stereo.

Takeaways

  1. In angle modulation the message moves the instantaneous phase; PM ties the phase to m(t), FM ties the frequency to m(t), so the FM phase holds the integral of m(t).
  2. Narrowband FM has one sideband pair turned 90° to the carrier; wideband FM has many lines spaced fm apart, sized by Jn(β), with the total power fixed at Ac²/2.
  3. Carson's rule 2(Δf + fm) is the bandwidth to allow: 180 kHz for mono broadcast FM and 256 kHz with the 53 kHz stereo baseband.
  4. Direct FM pulls an LC oscillator with a varactor and needs AFC; Armstrong FM starts from a crystal with a tiny deviation and multiplies it, with a mixer between the multipliers to land on the channel.
  5. Classic FM detectors turn frequency into amplitude or phase with tuned circuits, then use diodes; the Foster-Seeley gives an S-curve zero at fc, and the ratio detector trades half the output for built-in limiting.
  6. With the noise measured in the message band, the figure of merit is 1/3 at best for AM, 1 for DSB-SC and SSB, and (3/2)β² for FM: FM buys output SNR with bandwidth.
  7. FM keeps its advantage only above threshold, near a 10 dB carrier-to-noise ratio in the IF band; wider deviation needs a stronger carrier to reach it.
  8. Pre-emphasis at the transmitter and de-emphasis at the receiver leave the programme flat and cut the treble noise, 13.2 dB for 75 µs and a 15 kHz band.
  9. FM stereo keeps mono sets working by sending L + R at baseband, L − R on a suppressed 38 kHz subcarrier, and a 19 kHz pilot that the receiver doubles.

  1. Instantaneous phase. Write the wave as v(t) = A cos θ(t). The whole angle θ(t), in radians, is the instantaneous phase. For an unmodulated carrier θ(t) = 2πfc t, a straight line of slope 2πfc.

  3. Angle-modulated wave. With θ(t) = 2πfc t + φ(t):
    fi(t) = (1/2π) d/dt [2πfc t + φ(t)]

Analyze Derive the figure of merit of an FM receiver with a tone message and compare it with AM. Explain why FM trades bandwidth for noise performance, and state the limit of this trade. 16 marks

Points to cover. Receiver model: band-pass BT, limiter-discriminator, low-pass W; noise N0/2; Carrier plus narrowband noise; the quadrature noise sets the phase error ns/Ac; Output noise density N0f²/Ac²; power 2N0W³/(3Ac²); Signal Δf²/2; FOM = (3/2)β² (noise in W), or 3β²(β + 1) with noise in BT; AM: m²/(2 + m²), at most 1/3; Output SNR grows as β² for a fixed received power while the bandwidth grows as β; The trade stops at threshold, about 10 dB carrier-to-noise in BT.

Model answer.

  1. Receiver model. A band-pass filter of width BT = 2(β + 1)W passes the FM wave and the noise; a limiter and an ideal discriminator follow, then a low-pass filter of width W. The channel noise is white with two-sided density N0/2. The received wave is Ac cos[2πfc t + φ(t)] with φ(t) = β sin 2πfm t, fm = W.

    n(t) = nc(t) cos 2πfc t − ns(t) sin 2πfc t
nc, ns: low-pass, density N0 each over |f| ≤ BT/2
  3. Phase error. With Ac much larger than the noise (above threshold), the noise adds a small phasor to the carrier; only its component at right angles to the carrier changes the phase. Taking the noise statistics as unaffected by the modulation:
    θ(t) ≈ φ(t) + ns(t)/Ac
  4. Discriminator output. It gives (1/2π) dθ/dt:
    y(t) = (1/2π) dφ/dt + (1/2πAc) dn_s/dt

Since BT = 2(β + 1)W, threshold needs a channel SNR of about 20(β + 1): 120 (20.8 dB) for β = 5, 220 (23.4 dB) for β = 10.
  11. So for a given received power there is a best β: the largest one that keeps the carrier above threshold. Threshold-extension receivers (FMFB, PLL) push this limit down by a few dB.
  12. Closing check: β = 5, channel SNR 120: (S/N)o = 120 × 37.5 = 4500 = 36.5 dB, and ρ = 120/12 = 10 (10 dB) with 3β²(β + 1)ρ = 450 × 10 = 4500, the same value.
Analyze Explain the threshold effect in FM. Define threshold and capture ratio, sketch the output SNR curve, and describe two methods of extending the threshold. 13 marks

Points to cover. Threshold: the carrier-to-noise ratio in BT at which the output SNR falls 1 dB below the straight-line law, about 10 dB; Cause: noise swings the resultant phasor round the origin, giving 2π phase jumps and clicks; Sketch: straight line above threshold, sharp knee, steep fall below; Capture ratio: the power ratio needed for the stronger of two co-channel signals to take over; Extension: FMFB narrows the IF bandwidth by feedback; a PLL detector tracks the phase with a narrow loop; Cost: complexity, loop stability, limited deviation range.

Model answer.

  1. Above threshold. With ρ = C/(N0BT), the carrier-to-noise ratio in the IF bandwidth, the output follows (S/N)o = 3β²(β + 1)ρ: a straight line of slope 1 on a dB plot.
  2. Cause of the threshold. The received phasor is the carrier plus a noise phasor. While the carrier is much longer, the noise only tilts it slightly. As ρ falls toward 10 dB, the noise phasor is now and then longer than the carrier and swings the resultant right round the origin. The phase jumps by 2π in a short time; the discriminator, which differentiates the phase, outputs a spike of area 1 cycle: a click. The click rate rises steeply as ρ falls, and clicks carry far more power than the smooth noise.
  3. Definition. The threshold is the ρ at which the output SNR is 1 dB below the straight-line law; for usual tone-modulated receivers it is about 10 dB (some books use 13 dB, ρ = 20, as the onset of clicks). In channel-SNR terms it is about 20(β + 1).
  4. Numbers for the sketch (β = 5 and β = 2, W = 15 kHz, N0 = 4 × 10⁻²¹ W/Hz):
    βBTnoise N0BTC at thresholdoutput at threshold
    5180 kHz−121.4 dBm−111.4 dBm36.5 dB
    290 kHz−124.4 dBm−114.4 dBm25.6 dB
  5. Sketch (Figure 3.6.1). Output SNR against carrier power: each line rises 1 dB per dB above its threshold point, then falls steeply to the left of it. The β = 5 line is higher but starts 3 dB further right: wide deviation needs a stronger carrier.
  6. Capture effect. With two FM signals on one channel, the limiter output follows the stronger, and the weaker only wobbles its phase slightly. The capture ratio is the smallest power ratio, in dB, of the stronger to the weaker at which the receiver follows the stronger cleanly; a few dB in good receivers. Example: −70 dBm against −75 dBm with a 3 dB capture ratio: 5 dB > 3 dB, so the −70 dBm station is heard. Capture lets FM reuse channels at shorter distances than AM, but a receiver can switch suddenly when the two levels cross.
  7. Threshold extension 1, FM feedback (FMFB). The discriminator output drives the local oscillator so that it follows the incoming frequency. The IF signal then has a compressed deviation and can pass through a narrower filter, so less noise enters and the threshold falls. The loop must be stable and fast enough for the audio.
  8. Threshold extension 2, phase-locked loop detector. A VCO is locked to the incoming wave; the loop control voltage is the demodulated audio. The loop bandwidth needs only cover the message, not BT, so it sees less noise and slips cycles (clicks) less often. A loop that is too narrow fails on large deviation.
  9. Cost. Both trade circuit complexity and a limited deviation range for a few dB of threshold, which mattered most for weak links such as early satellite and space receivers.
  10. Closing check: −111.4 − (−121.4) = 10 dB, and 10 + 10 log(3 × 25 × 6) = 10 + 26.5 = 36.5 dB.
Evaluate An FM broadcast service is to be planned for a city. Using deviation, Carson bandwidth, transmitter and detector choice, threshold, pre-emphasis and stereo multiplexing, justify the choice of each block in the transmitter and receiver chain and state which blocks decide the final audio quality. 16 marks

Points to cover. Deviation ±75 kHz, audio 15 kHz: β = 5; Carson 180 kHz mono, 256 kHz stereo; Transmitter: crystal-referenced exciter (PLL or Armstrong), pre-emphasis 50 µs, stereo coder; Receiver: RF stage, mixer to 10.7 MHz, IF filter, limiter, discriminator, stereo decoder, de-emphasis; Threshold sets the coverage edge; capture sets co-channel reuse; Quality is decided by deviation and emphasis, IF bandwidth and linearity, limiter, discriminator linearity, pilot phase.

Model answer.

  1. Deviation and audio band. Choose the standard ±75 kHz peak deviation and W = 15 kHz: β = D = 5. With noise in the message band, FOM = (3/2) × 25 = 37.5 (15.7 dB) over plain baseband, and 20.5 dB over AM at m = 1.
  2. Bandwidth:
    mono:   BT = 2(75 + 15) = 180 kHz
stereo: BT = 2(75 + 53) = 256 kHz
  3. The stereo signal is wider than one 200 kHz channel, so stations in one city are placed several channels apart and the IF filter of the receiver is about 200 kHz wide, accepting a little loss of the outer sidebands.
  4. Transmitter exciter. A crystal-referenced exciter is needed to hold the carrier within a few hundred hertz. Today this is a VCO in a phase-locked loop (direct FM with crystal stability); the Armstrong chain (for example 100 kHz, 20 Hz, ×75, mixer, ×50 to 96 MHz with 75 kHz) does the same by multiplication. Direct FM without AFC is ruled out by drift.
  5. Pre-emphasis. 50 µs (corner 3183 Hz) as used in most countries; the receivers sold in the city must match it. It improves the output SNR by 10.4 (10.2 dB) for a 15 kHz band, at the cost of a higher peak deviation on bright music, so an audio limiter after the pre-emphasis keeps the peak at 75 kHz.
  6. Stereo coder. L + R at 0–15 kHz for mono listeners, a 19 kHz pilot at about 9 % of the modulation, L − R on a suppressed 38 kHz subcarrier, all within the 75 kHz peak. Optionally RDS at 57 kHz for the station name.
  7. Receiver. RF amplifier for noise figure and image rejection; mixer to 10.7 MHz, which puts the image 21.4 MHz away, outside the 20 MHz band; ceramic IF filter about 200 kHz wide for adjacent-channel selectivity; limiter to remove AM; discriminator (ratio, quadrature or digital) with a straight S-curve over ±75 kHz plus margin; stereo decoder with PLL pilot recovery; de-emphasis on L and R; audio amplifiers.
  8. Coverage. With N0 = kT0 = 4 × 10⁻²¹ W/Hz and BT = 180 kHz, the noise is −121.4 dBm and the threshold carrier −111.4 dBm for an ideal receiver. A receiver with noise figure F raises both by F dB. Inside the coverage the target output SNR, say 50 dB for good stereo, sets the needed carrier:
    needed ρ = 50 − 26.5 = 23.5 dB
C = −121.4 + 23.5 = −97.9 dBm (plus F)
  9. Stereo costs SNR: the L − R channel is noisier than L + R because the 23–53 kHz band sits higher on the noise parabola, so the stereo service area is smaller than the mono one; receivers blend to mono on weak signals.
  10. Co-channel and adjacent planning. Capture lets a co-channel station be reused once its signal is a capture ratio (a few dB) below the wanted one everywhere in the service area; adjacent channels are kept away by the IF selectivity and by spacing.

Glossary

Angle modulation
Modulation in which the message changes the phase angle of the carrier, not its amplitude; FM and PM are its two forms.
Armstrong method
Indirect FM: integrate the audio, make NBFM from a crystal with a phase modulator, then multiply and mix.
Automatic frequency control
A slow feedback loop that holds a direct FM oscillator's centre frequency to a crystal reference.
Bessel function
Jn(β), the amplitude factor of the n-th sideband of a tone-modulated FM wave.
Capture ratio
The power ratio in dB by which a co-channel FM signal must exceed another to take over the receiver.
Carson's rule
The bandwidth estimate BT ≈ 2(Δf + fm).
Click
A spike at the discriminator output when noise swings the resultant phasor round the origin.
Composite baseband
The FM stereo modulating signal: L + R, the pilot, and L − R on a suppressed 38 kHz subcarrier.
De-emphasis
The matching treble cut after the discriminator; it restores flat audio and cuts the treble noise.
Discriminator
A circuit whose output voltage is proportional to the frequency offset from fc.
Figure of merit
Output SNR over channel SNR, used to compare modulation schemes for the same received power and noise.
Foster-Seeley discriminator
A balanced tuned-transformer diode detector whose output against frequency is an S-curve, zero at fc.
Frequency deviation Δf
The largest amount by which the instantaneous frequency moves away from fc, in hertz.
Frequency modulation
Modulation in which the instantaneous frequency is fc + kf m(t), so the phase carries the integral of the message.
Frequency multiplier
A nonlinear stage tuned to the N-th harmonic; it multiplies carrier and deviation by N.
Instantaneous frequency
The rate at which the carrier phase advances, fi = (1/2π) dθ/dt, in hertz.
Limiter
An IF stage that clips the FM wave to a constant amplitude before the discriminator.
Modulation index β
Δf/fm for a tone; it sets how many sideband pairs carry significant power.
Narrowband FM
FM with β up to about 0.2, whose spectrum is a carrier and one sideband pair in 2fm.
Phase modulation
Modulation in which the phase is shifted by kp m(t), so the frequency deviation follows the slope of the message.
Pilot tone
The 19 kHz tone in FM stereo that the receiver doubles to rebuild the 38 kHz subcarrier.
Pre-emphasis
A treble boost of time constant τ at the FM transmitter, before the modulator.
Ratio detector
A balanced FM detector with a large capacitor across the diode loads; its output follows V1/V2, giving partial limiting.
Threshold effect
The sharp fall of FM output SNR, with clicks, once the carrier-to-noise ratio in the IF band drops to about 10 dB.
Varactor diode
A reverse-biased diode used as a voltage-controlled capacitor in direct FM oscillators.
Wideband FM
FM with β of order 1 or more, whose spectrum spreads over many sideband pairs.
