1.1 Elements of a communication system; need for modulation

A communication system carries information from one point to another: a source produces a message, a transmitter conditions it, a channel carries it, and a receiver hands it to a destination. The channel is rarely kind — it attenuates the signal, adds noise, and is shared with other users. Modulation is the deliberate shift of the message spectrum onto a carrier frequency so the signal fits the channel, the antenna and the spectrum rules. Every phone call, UPI confirmation and cricket live stream uses these same blocks.

You need: a sinusoid, frequency, wavelength and the relation v = fλ from first-year physics.

The block schematic of a communication system

A communication system moves information from a source to a destination. Information is whatever the destination does not already know: a voice, a temperature reading, a UPI transaction record, a frame of video. The system is drawn as blocks in a line, and some syllabi call this drawing the communication model.

The source produces the message. The transducer converts it into an electrical voltage — a microphone turns sound pressure into a voltage, a camera sensor turns light into charge. The result is the message signal m(t), a voltage that varies with time t measured in seconds.

The transmitter prepares the signal for the channel: it amplifies, filters and modulates. The channel is the physical medium — a copper pair, a coaxial cable, an optical fibre, or free space. The receiver amplifies what arrives, removes the carrier and produces an estimate of m(t). The destination is a human ear, a screen or a storage device.

r(t) = s(t) + n(t)

r(t) is the received voltage; s(t) is the transmitted signal after the channel has attenuated it; n(t) is the unwanted noise voltage added by the channel and by the receiver's own circuits.

Noise is random, so it is described by statistics, not by a formula — see 1.6. Digital systems insert two more blocks between source and transmitter: a source encoder that compresses bits and a channel encoder that adds protection; see Unit 4 and Unit 7.

Baseband, passband, and modulation as frequency translation

A signal whose spectrum lies from 0 Hz up to some maximum B hertz is a baseband signal. Speech, music and sensor outputs are baseband: their energy sits near 0 Hz. A passband signal has its spectrum concentrated around a carrier frequency fc far above 0 Hz.

Modulation translates the message spectrum to sit around fc. It creates no new information; it only moves the information to a new place in the spectrum.

s(t) = A(t) cos(2π fc t + φ(t))

s(t) is the modulated (passband) signal; A(t) is its slowly varying envelope in volts; fc is the carrier frequency in hertz; φ(t) is its slowly varying phase in radians; t is time in seconds.

The carrier is the sinusoid cos(2π fc t). It carries no information by itself; it only provides a place in the spectrum. The message rides on it through A(t) or φ(t).

If A(t) varies in proportion to m(t), the scheme is amplitude modulation. If the instantaneous frequency or phase varies with m(t), it is angle modulation, that is, frequency or phase modulation. These are the analog modulations. In digital modulation the same two quantities are switched between a finite set of values (see Unit 6). The bandwidth Bs of the modulated signal is the width of spectrum it occupies; for amplitude modulation Bs ≈ 2B. The bandwidth is set by the message and the scheme, not by fc.

Why modulation is needed

  • Antenna size. An antenna radiates efficiently when its length is a good fraction of the wavelength λ = c/f, where c = 3×108 m/s is the speed of light and f is the radiated frequency in hertz. A quarter-wave antenna is λ/4 long. At audio frequencies this is kilometres; at radio frequencies it is centimetres. The worked example below computes both.
  • Frequency assignment. Free space is shared by everyone. Regulators license bands (mobile services in India use bands around 900 MHz, 1800 MHz and 2.5 GHz) so that each transmitter stays inside its own slot. Modulation is what puts a signal into its assigned slot.
  • Multiplexing. Many messages share one channel when each is placed on a different carrier: frequency division multiplexing (see Unit 2). Time division multiplexing shares the time axis instead (see Unit 4).
  • Noise and interference. Modulation does not remove noise. It moves the signal to a band where interference is low, and it lets the receiver filter out everything outside fc ± Bs. Wideband schemes then trade bandwidth for noise immunity (see Unit 3).

The receiver's band-pass filter passes only the band around fc. Signals at other frequencies are rejected before they reach the detector, which is the practical gain of putting the message on a carrier.

Worked example

Question. A 1 kHz audio tone has to be radiated from an antenna. (a) Find its wavelength and the length of a quarter-wave antenna. (b) The same tone now modulates a 100 MHz carrier. Find the wavelength and quarter-wave antenna length of the radiated wave. Take c = 3×10⁸ m/s.

  1. Use λ = c/f. At f = 1 kHz = 1×10³ Hz: λ = (3×10⁸ m/s) ÷ (1×10³ Hz) = 3×10⁵ m = 300 km.
  2. Quarter-wave length = λ/4 = 300 km ÷ 4 = 75 km. An antenna 75 km long cannot be built on a tower, so direct radiation of the audio tone is impossible.
  3. At the carrier fc = 100 MHz = 1×10⁸ Hz: λ = (3×10⁸ m/s) ÷ (1×10⁸ Hz) = 3 m.
  4. Quarter-wave length = λ/4 = 3 m ÷ 4 = 0.75 m, that is 75 cm. This is a small, practical antenna.
  5. The radiated wave is now at 100 MHz; the audio information rides on it as a variation of the envelope A(t). The antenna shrank by a factor 75 km ÷ 0.75 m = 1×10⁵, exactly the ratio of the two frequencies.

Answer. At 1 kHz: λ = 300 km and λ/4 = 75 km. At 100 MHz: λ = 3 m and λ/4 = 0.75 m — modulation shortens the antenna by a factor of 10⁵.

Exam traps

  • Modulation does not create information and does not by itself improve the signal-to-noise ratio at the receiver input. It only shifts the message spectrum and makes the antenna and multiplexing practical.
  • Do not confuse the carrier frequency with the message frequency. The carrier decides where the signal sits in the spectrum; the message and the scheme decide the bandwidth.
  • In λ = c/f, put the frequency that is actually radiated. After modulation that is fc, not the audio frequency.
  • Noise enters at the channel and in the receiver's front end. It is not generated mainly by the transmitter, and a strong transmitter does not remove it.
  • Baseband does not mean audio. Any signal whose spectrum starts at 0 Hz, including a digital NRZ pulse train, is a baseband signal.

1.2 Fourier transform review and band-pass signals

A Fourier transform turns a time waveform into a frequency recipe: how much of each frequency the signal contains. Filters, antennas and cables act on frequency, not on time samples. A band-pass signal has all its content in a narrow band around a carrier, as in a radio station at 100 MHz or Wi-Fi at 2.4 GHz. This chapter reviews the transform and its key properties, then describes band-pass signals by an easier low-pass equivalent.

You need baseband, carrier and bandwidth from 1.1.

The transform and its inverse

A signal x(t) is a function of time. The Fourier transform asks how much of each frequency it contains. It writes x(t) as a sum of complex exponentials ej2πft, each with complex weight X(f). The forward transform analyses; the inverse synthesises.

X(f) = ∫<sub>−∞</sub><sup>∞</sup> x(t) e<sup>−j2πft</sup> dt

X(f) is the Fourier transform in volts per hertz (V/Hz) when x(t) is in volts; f is frequency in hertz; j = √−1; t is time in seconds.

x(t) = ∫<sub>−∞</sub><sup>∞</sup> X(f) e<sup>j2πft</sup> df

x(t) is the time signal in volts; X(f) is its transform; f is frequency in hertz; the integral adds all frequency components.

X(f) is complex. |X(f)| is the magnitude spectrum, ∠X(f) the phase spectrum. For a voltage pulse, |X(f)| has units V/Hz: it is a density, not a voltage. Multiply by a filter width in hertz to get volts.

The transform exists for finite-energy signals and for common power signals treated with impulses.

Properties used in every problem

Six properties cover most exam questions. Each pairs a time operation with a frequency operation.

  • Linearity: a x₁(t) + b x₂(t) ↔ a X₁(f) + b X₂(f). Superposition holds.
  • Time shift: x(t − t₀) ↔ X(f) e−j2πf t₀. Magnitude unchanged; only phase changes.
  • Frequency shift: x(t) ej2πf_c t ↔ X(f − f_c): a complex exponential shifts the spectrum one way, to +f_c. Multiplying by a real carrier cos(2πf_c t) = ½(ej2πf_c t + e−j2πf_c t) makes two half-size copies at ±f_c; that is modulation.
  • Convolution: x(t) * h(t) ↔ X(f) H(f). Time convolution becomes frequency multiplication.
  • Scaling: x(at) ↔ (1/|a|) X(f/a). Compressing in time spreads the spectrum in frequency.
  • Parseval (Rayleigh energy) theorem: total energy in time equals total energy in frequency.
∫<sub>−∞</sub><sup>∞</sup> |x(t)|² dt = ∫<sub>−∞</sub><sup>∞</sup> |X(f)|² df

With x(t) in volts and f in hertz, both sides have units V²·s, energy per ohm; |X(f)|² is the energy spectral density.

Define sinc(u) = sin(πu)/(πu) for u ≠ 0 and sinc(0) = 1. A rectangular pulse of width T has transform A T sinc(fT).

LTI systems and distortionless transmission

A linear time-invariant (LTI) system is fixed by its impulse response h(t). The output is y(t) = x(t) * h(t). In frequency, this is simple multiplication.

Y(f) = X(f) H(f)

Y(f), X(f) and H(f) are the Fourier transforms of output, input and impulse response; H(f) is the frequency response.

H(f) = |H(f)| ejθ(f). |H(f)| scales each frequency; θ(f) delays it. A system is distortionless when every frequency gets the same gain and the same time delay. In phase terms θ(f) must be a straight line through the origin, θ(f) = −2πf t_d: the phase lag in radians grows linearly with frequency, while the delay t_d stays constant.

H(f) = K e−j2πf t_d

K is a constant gain; t_d is the delay in seconds; θ(f) = −2πf t_d radians.

Phase delay is the delay of one frequency. Group delay is the delay of the envelope around that frequency.

τ_p = −θ(f)/(2πf), τ_g = −(1/(2π)) dθ(f)/df

τ_p is phase delay in seconds; τ_g is group delay in seconds; θ(f) is in radians; f is in hertz.

If θ(f) = −2πf t_d, both delays equal t_d. If θ(f) is not a straight line, frequencies arrive at different times and a pulse spreads even when |H(f)| is flat. Use radians for θ(f).

Band-pass signals and the low-pass equivalent

A band-pass signal has a spectrum zero outside a band of width B centred at +f_c and −f_c, with f_c much larger than B. A radio signal at 100 MHz with 200 kHz bandwidth is band-pass. Its positive-frequency content lies between f_c − B/2 and f_c + B/2.

Any such signal can be written in canonical form with two low-pass signals.

x(t) = x_I(t) cos(2πf_c t) − x_Q(t) sin(2πf_c t)

x_I(t) is the in-phase component in volts; x_Q(t) is the quadrature component in volts; f_c is the carrier in hertz; both components are low-pass with bandwidth B/2.

x_I is in-phase, x_Q quadrature. Both are real low-pass with bandwidth B/2 and together carry all information of x(t). The pair is the low-pass equivalent (baseband equivalent).

Why bother? Sampling a 100 MHz carrier directly needs more than 200 million samples per second. x_I needs B samples per second and x_Q needs B samples per second, so the pair needs 2B real samples per second, far below the 2f_c samples per second the carrier itself would demand. The spectrum picture is a copy of the message spectrum moved to ±f_c.

The Hilbert transform builds x_I and x_Q from x(t); that construction is in 1.3.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular pulse is x(t) = 2 V for |t| < 0.5 ms and x(t) = 0 elsewhere. (a) Find X(f). (b) Find X(0). (c) Find the first null and the null-to-null bandwidth. State why the 3-dB bandwidth is a different number.

  1. The pulse width is T = 1 ms = 1×10−3 s. The amplitude is A = 2 V.
  2. X(f) = ∫−T/2T/2 A e−j2πft dt = A T sinc(fT), with sinc(u) = sin(πu)/(πu).
  3. A T = 2 V × 1×10−3 s = 2×10−3 V/Hz.
  4. So X(f) = 2×10−3 sinc(103 f) V/Hz.
  5. At f = 0, X(0) = 2×10−3 V/Hz.
  6. The first null is at fT = 1, so f = 1/T = 1 kHz.
  7. The null-to-null bandwidth is 2/T = 2 kHz.
  8. |X(f)| falls to 1/√2 of its peak at f = ±0.443/T, so the 3-dB bandwidth, peak to peak between these two points, is 0.886/T ≈ 0.89/T. That is different from the null-to-null 2/T.

Answer. X(f) = 2×10−3 sinc(103 f) V/Hz, X(0) = 2×10−3 V/Hz, and the null-to-null bandwidth is 2 kHz.

Exam traps

  • Bandwidth is not one number. Say first-null, 3-dB or null-to-null; for a rectangular pulse null-to-null is 2/T, not 1/T.
  • X(f) is a density in V/Hz. Multiply it by the filter width in hertz to get volts. Around f = 0 the pulse has 2 mV/Hz, so a flat 1 Hz filter passes a peak of 2 mV; a filter many times wider passes proportionally more, because the density is being added over a wider band.
  • A time shift multiplies X(f) by e−j2πf t₀; the magnitude |X(f)| does not change.
  • Use radians in delay formulas. θ(f) in degrees gives a wrong delay by a factor 180/π.
  • Phase delay and group delay are equal only when phase is linear; nonlinear phase distorts the envelope even if |H(f)| is flat.
  • Time convolution pairs with frequency multiplication, not division. Writing Y(f) = X(f)/H(f) is deconvolution, not normal transmission.

1.3 Hilbert transform and complex envelope

A real band-pass signal carries information in a narrow band around a carrier, so the carrier is only a vehicle. The Hilbert transform turns the real signal into an analytic signal that has only positive frequencies. Removing the carrier from that analytic signal leaves the complex envelope, a low-pass complex signal whose real and imaginary parts are the in-phase (I) and quadrature (Q) components. This idea is used in SSB generation, I/Q modulators, digital receivers and simulation of band-pass systems.

You need the Fourier transform and band-pass spectra from 1.2; modulation basics are in 1.1.

The Hilbert transform

Real signals have a conjugate-symmetric spectrum, X(−f) = X*(f), so the negative-frequency half carries no new information and the positive half alone can describe the signal. The Hilbert transform produces a companion signal x̂(t) that is 90° shifted in phase without changing the magnitude spectrum.

x̂(t) = (1/π) PV ∫<sub>−∞</sub><sup>∞</sup> x(τ)/(t − τ) dτ

x̂(t) is the Hilbert transform of x(t) in volts; PV means the Cauchy principal value, which avoids the singular point τ = t; τ is the integration variable in seconds.

H(f) = −j sgn(f)

H(f) is the frequency response; sgn(f) = 1 for f > 0, −1 for f < 0 and 0 at f = 0; f is frequency in hertz; j is the imaginary unit.

For positive frequencies, H(f) = −j gives a −90° phase shift. For negative frequencies it gives +90°. At f = 0 the response is zero. The transform is all-pass: every frequency keeps its amplitude.

  • H[cos(2πfct + θ)] = sin(2πfct + θ) for fc > 0. Cos becomes sin, not −sin.
  • Applying H twice gives −x(t): H[H[x(t)]] = −x(t).
  • x(t) and x̂(t) have the same energy. For finite-energy real signals, their inner product is zero.
  • The ideal Hilbert transformer is non-causal; practical 90° phase-shifting networks approximate it over a band.

Analytic signal and complex envelope

The analytic signal, also called the pre-envelope, is xa(t) = x(t) + j x̂(t). For a real band-pass signal, xa has only positive frequencies: its spectrum is 2X(f) for f > 0 and 0 for f < 0. The factor 2 keeps the real part unchanged.

xa(t) = x(t) + j x̂(t)

xa(t) is the analytic signal in volts; x(t) is the real band-pass signal in volts; x̂(t) is its Hilbert transform in volts; j is the imaginary unit.

The complex envelope removes the carrier: x̃(t) = xa(t)e−j2πfct. It is low-pass and complex. It is not the analytic signal; xa still contains ej2πfct.

x̃(t) = x<sub>a</sub>(t)e<sup>−j2πf<sub>c</sub>t</sup>

x̃(t) is the complex envelope in volts; xa(t) is the analytic signal in volts; fc is the carrier frequency in hertz; t is time in seconds.

Canonical representation of a band-pass signal uses the in-phase and quadrature components:

x(t) = Re{x̃(t)e<sup>j2πf<sub>c</sub>t</sup>} = x<sub>I</sub>(t) cos(2πf<sub>c</sub>t) − x<sub>Q</sub>(t) sin(2πf<sub>c</sub>t)

xI(t) = Re{x̃(t)} is the in-phase component in volts; xQ(t) = Im{x̃(t)} is the quadrature component in volts; Re{·} means real part; fc is the carrier frequency in hertz; t is time in seconds.

Downconversion multiplies xa by e−j2πfct and low-pass filters to get x̃. Upconversion takes Re{x̃ ej2πfct} to rebuild x(t). In a receiver, multiply x(t) by 2cos(2πfct) and −2sin(2πfct), then low-pass filter, to get I and Q.

Low-pass equivalent and phase shifting

A band-pass system has a low-pass equivalent. Let h(t) be the real band-pass impulse response. Its complex envelope h̃(t) is defined by h(t) = Re{h̃(t)ej2πfct}; that is, h̃(t) is the complex envelope of h(t). The output complex envelope is:

ỹ(t) = (1/2) x̃(t) * h̃(t)

ỹ(t) is the output complex envelope in volts; x̃(t) is the input complex envelope in volts; h̃(t) is the complex envelope of the band-pass impulse response h(t), that is, h(t) = Re{h̃(t)ej2πfct}; * is convolution; the 1/2 follows from the analytic-signal convention.

The output is y(t) = Re{ỹ(t)ej2πfct} with ỹ = (1/2) x̃ * h̃. Keep the 1/2 factor consistent; dropping it changes the gain by 6 dB. Some syllabi call the complex envelope the low-pass equivalent. This view reduces simulation and design to low-pass filters and complex arithmetic.

Phase shifting: a 90° phase shifter over the band approximates the Hilbert transformer. In single-sideband (SSB) generation, one path is direct and the other is Hilbert-transformed; adding or subtracting cancels one sideband. See Unit 2, SSB and VSB modulation, for the circuit.

Worked example

Question. A band-pass voltage is x(t) = 2 cos(2πfct + 30°) V with fc > 0. Find the Hilbert transform x̂(t), the analytic signal xa(t), the complex envelope x̃(t), and the I and Q values at t = 0.

  1. The Hilbert transform of cos is sin for a positive carrier. So x̂(t) = 2 sin(2πfct + 30°) V.
  2. Form the analytic signal: xa(t) = x(t) + j x̂(t) = 2[cos(2πfct + 30°) + j sin(2πfct + 30°)] = 2ej(2πfct + 30°) V.
  3. Remove the carrier: x̃(t) = xa(t)e−j2πfct = 2ej30° V.
  4. Convert to rectangular form: 2 cos 30° = 2(√3/2) = 1.732 V; 2 sin 30° = 2(0.5) = 1.000 V. Thus x̃(t) = 1.732 + j1.000 V. The complex envelope is constant for this pure tone, so at t = 0 it has the same value.
  5. I = Re{x̃} = 1.732 V and Q = Im{x̃} = 1.000 V. Check: 1.732 cos(2πfct) − 1.000 sin(2πfct) equals 2 cos(2πfct + 30°) V.

Answer. x̂(t) = 2 sin(2πfct + 30°) V; xa(t) = 2ej(2πfct + 30°) V; x̃(t) = 1.732 + j1.000 V; I = 1.732 V, Q = 1.000 V.

Exam traps

  • The Hilbert transform of cos is sin, not −sin. The −j factor shifts the positive-frequency phasor by −90°, and cos(θ − 90°) = sin θ.
  • x̃(t) is not the analytic signal xa(t). The analytic signal still contains ej2πfct; the complex envelope has the carrier removed.
  • H(f) = −j sgn(f), not −j for all frequencies. Negative frequencies get +90°, which keeps the time signal real.
  • In low-pass equivalents, keep the 1/2 factor from the analytic-signal convention. Dropping it changes the gain by 6 dB.

1.4 Probability and random variables review

A random variable assigns a real number to each outcome of an experiment. Its cumulative distribution and density or mass function let you compute the probability that a measured voltage, current or bit error count lies in a range. Communication receivers work with such measurements under noise, so they need these tools to predict error rates and set thresholds. The same ideas price risk in finance and score credit in banking. This chapter reviews the rules, the common distributions and the transformation of random variables that later chapters use for noise and detection.

You need first-year probability and integration, plus Fourier transform review only for the characteristic function.

Probability rules and conditioning

An experiment has a sample space S, the set of all outcomes. An event A is a subset of S. Probability P(A) follows three axioms: P(A) ≥ 0; P(S) = 1; and for disjoint events A₁, A₂, ..., P(A₁ ∪ A₂ ∪ ...) = P(A₁) + P(A₂) + ... . These axioms give the complement rule P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A) and the addition rule P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).

P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

P(A|B) is the probability of event A given that event B occurred; P(A ∩ B) is the probability that both occur; P(B) > 0.

Conditonal probability updates a probability after new information. Rearranging the formula gives the multiplication rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A|B)P(B). Events A and B are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B), which makes P(A|B) = P(A).

Bayes' rule reverses a conditional: P(Aᵢ|B) = P(B|Aᵢ)P(Aᵢ) / Σⱼ P(B|Aⱼ)P(Aⱼ). The denominator is the total probability of B. In a receiver, Aᵢ may be 'bit 1 sent' or 'bit 0 sent', and B is the measured voltage. Bayes combines the prior P(Aᵢ) with the likelihood P(B|Aᵢ) to give the posterior. Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together; independent events can occur together but do not affect each other's probability.

Random variables, CDF, PDF and moments

A random variable X is a function X: S → ℝ. It maps each outcome to a real number. The cumulative distribution function (CDF) is F_X(x) = P(X ≤ x). It is nondecreasing, right-continuous, F_X(−∞) = 0 and F_X(∞) = 1. The CDF works for discrete and continuous variables.

P(a < X ≤ b) = F_X(b) − F_X(a) = ∫ₐᵇ f_X(x) dx

F_X is the CDF; f_X is the probability density function (PDF); a and b are real limits with a < b.

For a discrete variable, the probability mass function (PMF) is p_X(x) = P(X = x). For a continuous variable, the PDF f_X(x) satisfies F_X(x) = ∫_{−∞}^{x} f_X(t) dt and ∫_{−∞}^{∞} f_X(x) dx = 1. A continuous variable has P(X = x) = 0 for every x. The PDF height f_X(x) is density per unit x, not a probability. Only an area gives probability.

The mean or expected value is μ = E[X] = ∫ x f_X(x) dx for continuous X, or Σ x p_X(x) for discrete X. The variance is σ² = E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − μ². The standard deviation σ is in the same units as X; variance is in units squared. The n-th moment is E[Xⁿ]. For nonzero mean, E[X²] = σ² + μ², not σ² alone. For zero-mean noise, E[V²] = σ², the average power in a 1 Ω resistor.

Common distributions in communication

The uniform distribution on [a, b] has f_X(x) = 1/(b − a) inside the interval and 0 outside. Its mean is (a + b)/2 and variance is (b − a)²/12. The Gaussian (normal) distribution N(μ, σ²) has f_X(x) = 1/(σ√(2π)) exp(−(x − μ)²/(2σ²)). Its mean is μ and variance is σ². The standard Gaussian has μ = 0 and σ = 1; its peak height is 1/√(2π) ≈ 0.399.

The binomial distribution counts successes in n independent trials with success probability p: P(K = k) = C(n, k) pᵏ (1 − p)^{n−k}, for k = 0, 1, ..., n. C(n, k) is the number of ways to choose k items from n. Its mean is np and variance is np(1 − p). The Poisson distribution counts rare events in a fixed interval: P(K = k) = e^{−λ} λᵏ / k!, with mean λ and variance λ. The exponential distribution models waiting times: f_X(x) = λ e^{−λx} for x ≥ 0, with mean 1/λ and variance 1/λ². Here λ is the rate, in events per unit time.

For a standard Gaussian, Φ(z) = P(Z ≤ z) and Q(z) = P(Z > z) = 1 − Φ(z). Tables give Q(2) = 0.0228. These tails set detection thresholds and error rates.

Joint variables, transformations and the CLT

Two random variables X and Y have a joint PDF f_{X,Y}(x, y). The marginal PDF is f_X(x) = ∫ f_{X,Y}(x, y) dy. The conditional PDF is f_{X|Y}(x|y) = f_{X,Y}(x, y) / f_Y(y), with f_Y(y) > 0. X and Y are independent when f_{X,Y}(x, y) = f_X(x) f_Y(y). For discrete variables, replace integrals by sums and PDFs by PMFs. Independence is stronger than zero correlation; zero correlation only removes the linear part.

A function of a random variable is Y = g(X). If g is monotonic and differentiable, f_Y(y) = f_X(x) / |g'(x)|, where x = g^{−1}(y). If g is not one-to-one, sum this expression over all roots x that give the same y. The CDF method is safer: F_Y(y) = P(g(X) ≤ y), then differentiate. Some syllabi call this 'function of random variables' or 'transformation of random variables'; it is the same idea.

The central limit theorem (CLT) says that the sum of many independent random variables, each with finite mean and variance, tends to a Gaussian. The sum's mean is the sum of the means; its variance is the sum of the variances. This is why thermal noise and many interference sources are modelled as Gaussian. The characteristic function φ_X(ω) = E[e^{jωX}] is the Fourier transform of the PDF and generates moments. See Fourier transform review for the transform pair.

Worked example

Question. A Gaussian noise voltage V has mean 0 mV and standard deviation 2 mV. Find the probability that a sample exceeds 4 mV. Use the standard normal table value Q(2) = 0.0228.

  1. Write the given values: μ = 0 mV, σ = 2 mV, threshold v = 4 mV.
  2. Standardize: z = (v − μ)/σ = (4 − 0)/2 = 2.0. The value 2.0 is in standard deviations; it is dimensionless.
  3. For a standard Gaussian, P(Z > z) = Q(z) = 1 − Φ(z). From the table, Q(2) = 0.0228.
  4. Convert to percent: 0.0228 × 100% = 2.28%, about 2.3%.
  5. Because the Gaussian is continuous, P(V > 4 mV) equals P(V ≥ 4 mV); the point 4 mV has probability 0.

Answer. The standardized value is z = 2.0; P(V > 4 mV) = 0.0228, about 2.3%.

Exam traps

  • PDF height can exceed 1; only area is probability. For a continuous variable, P(X = x) = 0.
  • For nonzero mean, E[X²] = σ² + μ², not σ². Variance is in units squared; standard deviation is in original units.
  • Independent events can occur together; mutually exclusive events cannot when both have nonzero probability. Do not use P(A|B) and P(B|A) interchangeably.
  • In the Gaussian exponent use σ², not σ. Standardize with z = (x − μ)/σ, and do not mix Q(z) with Φ(z).
  • The CLT needs independent terms with finite variance. It gives an approximation for a sum, not an exact result for every sample.

1.5 Random processes, stationarity, correlation and power spectral density

A random process is a family of random signals indexed by time. One outcome gives one waveform; the ensemble gives all possible waveforms. Communication noise, fading and speech are modelled this way. For a wide-sense stationary process the mean is constant and the autocorrelation depends only on the time gap τ. The Fourier transform of that autocorrelation is the power spectral density (PSD), which shows how average power is spread over frequency. This chapter builds these tools and applies them to a linear filter, where output PSD is |H(f)|² times input PSD.

You need expectation and distributions from Chapter 1.4 and the Fourier transform from Chapter 1.2.

Random process, mean and autocorrelation

A random process X(t) assigns a random variable to every time t. Fix one outcome in the sample space and you get a sample function x(t), also called a realization. Fix t and you get a random variable X(t). The ensemble is the set of all sample functions.

The mean is μX(t)=E[X(t)]. The variance is σX²(t)=E[(X(t)−μX(t))²]. The autocorrelation compares values at two times:

R<sub>X</sub>(t<sub>1</sub>,t<sub>2</sub>)=E[X(t<sub>1</sub>)X(t<sub>2</sub>)]

E[·] is expectation; X(t1) and X(t2) are random variables at times t1 and t2; RX has units of power if X is a voltage across 1 Ω.

The autocovariance removes the mean: CX(t1,t2)=RX(t1,t2)−μX(t1)μX(t2). When the mean is zero, autocorrelation and autocovariance are the same.

  • RX(0,0)=E[X²(t)] is average power at time t.
  • For real X, RX(t1,t2)=RX(t2,t1).
  • If the mean is zero, CX(0,0) equals the variance.

For two processes X(t) and Y(t), the cross-correlation is RXY(t1,t2)=E[X(t1)Y(t2)]. It measures how one signal follows another at a delay. In general RXY(τ) is not the same as RYX(τ). The standard relation is RXY(τ)=RYX(−τ). They are equal only in special cases.

Stationarity and ergodicity

Strict-sense stationary means every joint distribution depends only on time differences, not on absolute time. This is hard to prove. Wide-sense stationary (WSS) keeps only the first two moments fixed. A process is WSS when μX(t)=μX (constant) and RX(t1,t2)=RX(τ) with τ=t1−t2.

RX(τ)=E[X(t)X(t+τ)]

τ is the time gap in seconds; RX(τ) is the autocorrelation of a WSS process; the mean μX is constant.

Ergodicity means a long time average of one sample function equals the ensemble average. Then you can measure μX and RX(τ) from one recorded waveform. WSS does not imply ergodicity. A process with a random DC level is WSS but not ergodic in the mean.

For WSS, RX(τ) has three useful facts: RX(0)=E[X²]=average power; RX(−τ)=RX(τ) for real X; and |RX(τ)|≤RX(0). The last fact says no delay gives more correlation than zero delay.

PSD, Wiener–Khinchine and filtering

A stationary random signal is a power signal, not an energy signal, so the ordinary Fourier transform of the process does not exist. Its average power in frequency is therefore described by the power spectral density SX(f). The Wiener–Khinchine theorem links it to autocorrelation:

S<sub>X</sub>(f)=∫<sub>−∞</sub><sup>∞</sup> R<sub>X</sub>(τ)e<sup>−j2πfτ</sup>dτ

f is frequency in hertz; τ is time delay in seconds; SX(f) is two-sided PSD in W/Hz; RX(τ) is in watts.

The inverse relation is RX(τ)=∫−∞∞SX(f)ej2πfτdf. Average power is the area under PSD: P=RX(0)=∫−∞∞SX(f)df. For a real process SX(f) is real, nonnegative and even: SX(−f)=SX(f). Some syllabi call it the power density spectrum. A one-sided PSD folds negative frequencies onto positive frequencies, so it is twice the two-sided value for f>0.

Pass X(t) through a linear time-invariant filter with frequency response H(f). The output Y(t) is WSS if X(t) is WSS. Its PSD is

SY(f)=|H(f)|²SX(f)

H(f) is filter frequency response; |H(f)|² is power gain; SX(f) is input PSD; SY(f) is output PSD in W/Hz.

The output mean is μY=H(0)μX. Output average power is PY=∫SY(f)df=RY(0). For an ideal low-pass filter of bandwidth B hertz, H(f)=1 for |f|≤B and 0 elsewhere. White noise of two-sided PSD N0/2 then gives PY=(N0/2)(2B)=N0B.

Exam traps

  • RX(0) is average power E[X²], not variance. Variance is RX(0)−μX². Only zero-mean processes make them equal.
  • Two-sided PSD integrates from −∞ to ∞. One-sided PSD integrates from 0 to ∞ and is twice as tall at positive frequencies. Mixing the two halves or doubles the answer.
  • WSS fixes only mean and autocorrelation. Strict stationarity fixes all joint distributions. WSS does not imply strict stationarity, except for Gaussian processes.
  • The output PSD uses |H(f)|², not H(f). A filter with voltage gain 2 gives power gain 4.
  • A random process has no ordinary Fourier transform. Do not write SX(f)=|F{X(t)}|² for one realization; use the autocorrelation transform.

1.6 Noise sources, white noise and AWGN

Every receiver picks up unwanted random voltage along with the signal. Electrons jostle in resistors, charges cross junctions at random, and the antenna collects whatever the city radiates. We cannot predict this noise sample by sample; we can only describe its statistics. Modelling it as additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) — flat in frequency, Gaussian in amplitude, added to the signal — reduces the whole physical mess to one number, the noise power inside the receiver bandwidth. Every signal-to-noise ratio and error-probability result in this subject starts from that model.

You need: 1.4 for the Gaussian PDF and 1.5 for power spectral density and autocorrelation.

Where noise comes from

Every resistor generates a random voltage because its electrons are in constant thermal motion. This is thermal noise, also called Johnson noise. It needs no current to flow and cannot be designed away — only cooling the resistor or narrowing the bandwidth reduces it. It sets the noise floor of every receiver, from an FM radio in a Chennai flat to a satellite earth station.

P = kTB, vn² = 4kTRB

P is the available thermal noise power in watts; k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann's constant; T is the absolute temperature in kelvin; B is the bandwidth in hertz; R is the resistance in ohms; vn is the RMS noise voltage in volts. Power does not depend on R, but voltage does: a larger R delivers the same available power at a higher voltage.

  • Shot noise: charges cross a junction at random instants. i² = 2qIB, where q = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C is the electron charge, I is the average DC current in amperes and B is the bandwidth in hertz. It appears in diodes, bipolar transistors and photodiodes.
  • Flicker noise: surface traps capture and release carriers, giving a PSD that rises as 1/f. It dominates below a few kilohertz; the frequency where it meets thermal noise is the flicker corner.
  • Burst noise: a defect switches current between two levels at random, heard as popping in a speaker. It is a faulty device, not a law of physics.
  • External interference: lightning, motor ignition, nearby transmitters. These enter through the antenna and are fought with shielding and filtering.

White noise: PSD, autocorrelation, bandwidth

White noise is the ideal noise whose PSD is the same at every frequency, as white light holds every colour. Its two-sided PSD is S(f) = N0/2 W/Hz for all f. N0 is the one-sided PSD in W/Hz, the density you get when only positive frequencies are drawn. For a resistor at temperature T, N0 = kT.

S(f) = N0/2, R(τ) = (N0/2)·δ(τ)

S(f) is the two-sided PSD in W/Hz; N0 is the one-sided PSD in W/Hz, equal to kT for thermal noise; R(τ) is the autocorrelation of the noise; δ(τ) is the Dirac delta, infinite at τ = 0 and zero elsewhere.

The autocorrelation is a delta. Any two samples of white noise, however close in time, are uncorrelated. A delta is infinitely tall and infinitely narrow, so the total power is infinite. White noise is a model, valid only over the band the receiver actually uses.

Power in a bandwidth. Noise that passes through a filter with positive-frequency width B carries power P = N0B. The same formula holds for a low-pass filter and for a band-pass filter far up at 100 MHz — only the width matters, not the position.

Equivalent noise bandwidth. Real filters have sloping skirts. A single-pole RC low-pass with 3 dB bandwidth B_3dB collects noise over the whole tail; its equivalent noise bandwidth is B_n = (π/2)B_3dB ≈ 1.571 B_3dB. Put B_n, not B_3dB, into N0B or kTB.

Gaussian noise, the AWGN channel and SNR

Thermal noise is the sum of an enormous number of independent electron motions. The central limit theorem makes that sum Gaussian, with zero mean and variance σ² equal to the average noise power.

p(v) = (1/√(2πσ²))·exp(−v²/(2σ²))

p(v) is the probability density of the instantaneous noise voltage v in volts; σ² is the average noise power (in watts across 1 Ω); σ is the RMS noise voltage in volts.

White plus Gaussian gives AWGN. The model makes three commitments: the noise adds to the signal, its amplitude is Gaussian, and its PSD is flat. The received waveform is r(t) = s(t) + n(t), with s(t) the wanted signal and n(t) the noise. This additive white Gaussian noise channel is the most used channel model in the subject; every error-probability formula in Unit 5 and Unit 7 assumes it. It fits a thermal-noise-limited receiver. It does not fit impulsive interference, and it does not fit fading, which multiplies the signal instead of adding to it (see Unit 6, Digital Passband Modulation).

SNR. Signal-to-noise ratio is average signal power divided by average noise power in the same bandwidth: SNR = P_s/(N0B), where P_s is the signal power in watts. In dB, SNR(dB) = 10 log10(P_s/P_n). A receiver with 1 mW of signal and 4×10⁻¹⁵ W of noise has SNR = 2.5×10¹¹, that is 114 dB. Digital work usually quotes Eb/N0, energy per bit over N0, which is independent of bandwidth (Unit 5 and Unit 7).

Worked example

Question. A 1 kΩ resistor at T = 290 K feeds a receiver whose equivalent noise bandwidth is 1 MHz. Take k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K. (a) Find the available thermal noise power in watts and in dBm. (b) Find the RMS noise voltage. (c) Find the one-sided noise PSD N0 in W/Hz and dBm/Hz.

  1. Given: R = 1 kΩ = 1000 Ω, T = 290 K, B = 1 MHz = 1×10⁶ Hz, k = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K.
  2. Available noise power: P = kTB = 1.38×10⁻²³ × 290 × 1×10⁶ = 4.002×10⁻¹⁵ W.
  3. In dBm: P(dBm) = 10 log10(P / 1 mW) = 10 log10(4.002×10⁻¹⁵ / 1×10⁻³) = 10 log10(4.002×10⁻¹²) = −114.0 dBm.
  4. RMS noise voltage: vn = √(4kTRB) = √(4 × 1.38×10⁻²³ × 290 × 1000 × 1×10⁶) = √(1.6008×10⁻¹¹) = 4.00×10⁻⁶ V = 4.0 µV.
  5. One-sided PSD: N0 = kT = 1.38×10⁻²³ × 290 = 4.002×10⁻²¹ W/Hz, that is −174.0 dBm/Hz. Check: N0B = 4.002×10⁻²¹ × 1×10⁶ = 4.002×10⁻¹⁵ W, which matches step 2.

Answer. The available thermal noise power is 4.00×10⁻¹⁵ W (−114.0 dBm), the RMS noise voltage is 4.0 µV, and N0 = 4.002×10⁻²¹ W/Hz (−174.0 dBm/Hz).

Exam traps

  • White noise has infinite total power. A statement like 'the noise power of this resistor is 4 fW' is meaningless until a bandwidth is fixed; look for the B or N0 the question forgot to give.
  • N0 and N0/2 differ by 3 dB. Integrating the two-sided PSD from −∞ to ∞ uses N0/2; integrating the one-sided PSD from 0 to ∞ uses N0. Mixing them makes the answer wrong by a factor of 2.
  • Noise power P = kTB does not contain R, but noise voltage vn = √(4kTRB) does. Doubling R doubles vn² and leaves P unchanged — examiners test exactly this.
  • dBm and dBW differ by 30 dB. 4×10⁻¹⁵ W is −114 dBm but −144 dBW. dBm is referenced to 1 mW, not 1 W.
  • 'White' and 'Gaussian' describe different things: white is the shape of the PSD, Gaussian is the amplitude distribution. Noise can be white and non-Gaussian (impulsive), or Gaussian and band-limited (not white).

1.7 Noise figure and noise temperature

A receiver adds its own noise, so the signal-to-noise ratio at its output is lower than at its input. Noise figure and noise temperature state that degradation as a single number. They let an engineer compare amplifiers, choose the first stage of a receiver, and predict the SNR that reaches the demodulator. The idea is used in satellite receivers, mobile handsets, Wi-Fi front ends and radio astronomy. This chapter defines both measures, connects them, and shows how they combine when stages are cascaded.

You need: Chapter 1.6 for noise power, SNR and AWGN.

Noise factor and noise figure

Every amplifier adds noise of its own. The signal-to-noise ratio at the input is measured with a source at a stated temperature. The signal-to-noise ratio at the output is measured at the load. The ratio of these two ratios is the noise factor F:

F = SNRin / SNRout

F is the linear noise factor (no unit); SNRin is input signal-to-noise ratio as a power ratio; SNRout is output signal-to-noise ratio as a power ratio.

F is always at least 1. A noiseless device has F = 1. F is not a gain and is not in decibels. Some syllabi call this linear F the noise factor. The noise figure NF is the same number in decibels:

NF = 10 log10 F

NF is the noise figure in dB; F is the linear noise factor; log10 is the base-10 logarithm.

A device with F = 2 has NF = 3.01 dB. Datasheets quote NF in dB because decibels compress a wide range. Formulas use F because noise powers add and gains multiply. The definition needs a source temperature. The standard reference is T0 = 290 K, about 17 °C. It is a convention, not exactly room temperature. F compares SNR, not output noise power alone.

Equivalent noise temperature

Noise temperature Te is the temperature of a matched resistor at the device input that would produce the same added noise power as the device. The added noise power referred to the input is k Te B, where k is Boltzmann's constant, Te is in kelvin, and B is the noise bandwidth in hertz. At the output, this added noise power is multiplied by the device power gain G. This is a bookkeeping temperature, not a physical temperature you can touch.

F = 1 + Te / T0

F is the linear noise factor; Te is the equivalent noise temperature in kelvin; T0 = 290 K.

Te = (F − 1) T0

Te is the equivalent noise temperature in kelvin; F is the linear noise factor; T0 = 290 K.

A device with F = 2 has Te = 290 K. A low-noise amplifier can have Te below 100 K. A lossy cable can have Te of several hundred kelvin.

A lossy component is an attenuator or cable with power loss L, where L is input power divided by output power and L ≥ 1. At physical temperature T0, its noise factor is F = L and its noise temperature is Te = (L − 1)T0. A 3 dB loss means L = 2, so F = 2 and Te = 290 K. Loss placed before an amplifier raises the overall noise figure.

Cascaded stages: Friis formula

A receiver is a chain of stages. Stage 1 has linear gain G1 and linear noise factor F1. Stage 2 has G2 and F2. Gains and noise factors must be linear, not in dB. The overall noise factor for two stages is:

F = F<sub>1</sub> + (F<sub>2</sub> − 1) / G<sub>1</sub>

F is the overall linear noise factor; F1, F2 are stage noise factors; G1 is stage 1 linear power gain.

For three stages the formula extends:

F = F<sub>1</sub> + (F<sub>2</sub> − 1)/G<sub>1</sub> + (F<sub>3</sub> − 1)/(G<sub>1</sub>G<sub>2</sub>)

F is overall noise factor; Fi is stage i noise factor; Gi is stage i linear power gain; the pattern continues for more stages.

The second stage's excess noise F2 − 1 is divided by G1. Stage 1 amplifies the signal and its own noise before stage 2 adds noise. A large G1 makes the second term small. The first stage dominates the overall noise figure. This is why a receiver puts a low-noise amplifier at the antenna and why loss before that amplifier is costly.

Procedure: convert every NF in dB to F = 10^(NF/10). Convert every gain in dB to G = 10^(GdB/10). Put these linear values into Friis. Convert the result back with NF = 10 log10 F. The figure shows the two-stage chain used in the example.

Worked example

Question. A two-stage receiver has stage 1 gain 20 dB and noise figure 3 dB. Stage 2 has gain 20 dB and noise figure 6 dB. The source is at T0 = 290 K. Find the overall noise factor F, the overall noise figure NF in dB, and the equivalent noise temperature Te.

  1. Convert stage 1 gain: G1 = 10^(20/10) = 100 (linear).
  2. Convert stage 2 gain: G2 = 10^(20/10) = 100 (linear).
  3. Convert stage 1 noise figure: F1 = 10^(3/10) = 1.995, taken as 2.00 (linear).
  4. Convert stage 2 noise figure: F2 = 10^(6/10) = 3.981, taken as 4.00 (linear).
  5. Apply Friis for two stages: F = F1 + (F2 − 1)/G1 = 2 + (4 − 1)/100 = 2 + 0.03 = 2.03.
  6. Convert overall noise factor to dB: NF = 10 log10(2.03) = 3.07 dB.
  7. Find equivalent noise temperature: Te = (F − 1)T0 = (2.03 − 1) × 290 = 1.03 × 290 = 298.7 K.

Answer. Overall noise factor F = 2.03 (linear), overall noise figure NF = 3.07 dB, and equivalent noise temperature Te = 298.7 K.

Exam traps

  • Do not add noise figures in dB. Friis needs linear F and linear G. Convert first, apply the formula, then convert back.
  • The reference temperature is T0 = 290 K, not 300 K. Using 300 K shifts Te and is a common marking error.
  • F is linear and NF is in dB. F cannot be below 1, so a real device cannot have a negative NF in dB.
  • A lossy cable is not noiseless. At T0, F = L and G = 1/L; loss before the first amplifier can dominate the system noise.
  • Noise temperature is not the physical temperature of the amplifier. It is the temperature of a matched source that would add the same noise power.

Takeaways

  1. A communication system is source, transmitter, channel, receiver and destination; the channel always adds noise and limits bandwidth, so the design goal is to keep error small at low power and low cost.
  2. Modulation exists for four practical reasons: antenna size at f_c, frequency assignment by a regulator, multiplexing many users on one medium, and moving the signal to a band where noise and interference are easier to handle.
  3. The Fourier transform turns filtering, modulation and bandwidth into multiplication and shifting in the frequency domain, which is why it is the main tool of the subject.
  4. A narrowband band-pass signal needs only its complex envelope x̃(t), a low-pass pair (x_I, x_Q), because the carrier f_c is known and can be put back at the receiver.
  5. The Hilbert transform supplies the 90-degree shifted version of a signal, so every real band-pass signal has the canonical form x_c(t)cos(2πf_c t) − x_s(t)sin(2πf_c t).
  6. CDF and PDF answer the only question a receiver really asks: what is the probability that the measured value falls past a decision threshold?
  7. For a WSS random process the autocorrelation R_X(τ) describes power in time and its Fourier transform S_X(f) describes power in frequency; the pair is the Wiener–Khinchine relation.
  8. White noise is a model, not a physical signal: it has infinite power, so it is always used with a finite bandwidth, and over that band the power is N₀B.
  9. Cascaded stages are dominated by the first stage: Friis gives F_total = F₁ + (F₂ − 1)/G₁ + (F₃ − 1)/(G₁G₂) + …, which is why the front-end amplifier sets receiver sensitivity.

Glossary

Attenuation
The reduction in signal power as it travels through a channel, usually expressed in decibels.
Bandwidth
The width in hertz of the frequency range that a signal or channel actually occupies or passes.
Baseband signal
A signal whose spectrum begins near 0 Hz and ends at some finite upper frequency W.
Burst noise
Step-like noise jumps caused by impurities or defects, also called popcorn noise.
Decision threshold
The amplitude level at which a receiver decides which symbol was sent, fixed by the noise statistics.
Ergodic process
A stationary process in which the time average of one long sample equals the ensemble average.
Expectation
The probability-weighted average of a random variable, E[X] = ∫x f_X(x)dx.
Flicker noise
Noise whose PSD falls roughly as 1/f, important in semiconductor devices at low frequencies.
Frequency translation
Moving a signal's spectrum from one band to another, done by multiplying with a carrier or a local oscillator.
Friis formula
The cascade relation F = F₁ + (F₂ − 1)/G₁ + (F₃ − 1)/(G₁G₂) + … for the total noise figure of stages in series.
Gaussian random variable
A variable whose PDF is (1/(σ√(2π)))·e^(−(x−m)²/(2σ²)), fully described by its mean m and variance σ².
In-phase component
The part of a band-pass signal that multiplies cos(2πf_c t), written x_I(t).
Modulation
The process of varying a parameter of a high-frequency carrier in proportion to a message signal.
Noise equivalent bandwidth
The width of an ideal rectangular filter that would pass the same total white-noise power as the real filter.
Passband signal
A signal whose spectrum is concentrated around a carrier frequency f_c far above 0 Hz.
Pre-envelope
Another name for the analytic signal x(t) + jx̂(t), which contains only positive frequencies.
Quadrature component
The part of a band-pass signal that multiplies sin(2πf_c t), written x_Q(t), shifted by 90 degrees from the in-phase part.
Rayleigh energy theorem
The statement that the energy of a signal equals the integral of its spectral energy density over frequency.
Reference temperature
The standard temperature T₀ = 290 K used to convert between noise figure and noise temperature.
Shot noise
Noise caused by the discrete arrival of charge carriers, with mean square current 2eI·B.
Signal-to-noise ratio
The ratio of signal power to noise power at a stated point, in watts per watt or in decibels.
Thermal noise
Noise produced by the random motion of electrons in a conductor, with two-sided PSD about kT/2 watts per hertz.
Transducer
A device that converts one form of energy to another, such as a microphone turning sound into voltage.
Variance
The average squared deviation from the mean, σ² = E[X²] − (E[X])²; its square root is the standard deviation.
