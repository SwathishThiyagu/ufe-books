1.1 Elements of a communication system; need for modulation

A communication system carries a message from a source to a destination through a channel that weakens the signal, adds noise and is shared with other users. Modulation moves the message spectrum up to a carrier frequency so that the signal suits the antenna, its licensed frequency slot and the channel. Every phone call, FM broadcast and UPI payment alert passes through the same chain of blocks.

You need: a sinusoid, frequency, wavelength and the relation c = fλ from first-year physics.

The block schematic of a communication system

A communication system moves information from a source to a destination. Information is whatever the destination does not already know: a voice, a temperature reading, a payment record, a frame of video. The system is drawn as a chain of blocks; some syllabi call this drawing the block schematic or the communication model.

The information source produces the message. An input transducer converts it into an electrical voltage: a microphone turns sound pressure into a voltage, a camera sensor turns light into charge. The result is the message signal m(t), a voltage that varies with time t in seconds. In Figure 1.1.1 the input transducer sits inside the source block, and the output transducer (a loudspeaker or a screen) sits inside the destination block.

The transmitter prepares the signal for the channel: it filters, modulates and amplifies. The channel is the physical medium: a copper pair, a coaxial cable, an optical fibre or free space. The receiver filters and amplifies what arrives, removes the carrier and produces an estimate m̂(t) of the message. The destination is a listener, a screen or a storage device.

r(t) = s(t) + n(t)

r(t) is the received voltage; s(t) is the transmitted signal after the channel has attenuated it; n(t) is the unwanted noise voltage added by the channel and by the receiver's own circuits.

Noise is random, so it is described by statistics rather than by a formula; see Noise sources, white noise and AWGN. When m(t) is sent as a continuous waveform the system is analog. When the message is first turned into bits and the transmitter sends one of a finite set of waveforms, the system is digital; it adds a source encoder (compression) and a channel encoder (error protection) after the source, which are covered with information theory and coding.

Baseband, passband and modulation as frequency translation

A signal whose spectrum lies between 0 Hz and some highest frequency B hertz is a baseband signal. Speech, music and sensor outputs are baseband. A passband signal has its spectrum concentrated around a carrier frequency fc far above 0 Hz.

s(t) = A(t) cos(2πfct + φ(t))

s(t) is the modulated (passband) signal; A(t) is its slowly varying envelope in volts; fc is the carrier frequency in hertz; φ(t) is its slowly varying phase in radians.

The carrier is the sinusoid cos(2πfct). It carries no information by itself; it only gives the signal a place in the spectrum. Modulation varies the carrier with m(t). If the amplitude A(t) follows m(t), the scheme is amplitude modulation (AM, with its forms DSB-SC, SSB and VSB). If the frequency or the phase φ(t) follows m(t), it is angle modulation: frequency modulation (FM) or phase modulation (PM). In digital modulation the same quantities are switched between a finite set of values.

Multiplying m(t) by cos(2πfct) copies the message spectrum to −fc and +fc; Fourier transform review and band-pass signals proves this. Modulation creates no new information; it moves the information to a new place in the spectrum. The width Bs of the band the modulated signal occupies is its bandwidth; for AM, Bs = 2B. The bandwidth is set by the message and the scheme, not by fc.

Why modulation is needed

  • Antenna size. An antenna radiates well when its length is a good fraction of the wavelength λ = c/f, where c = 3×108 m/s and f is the radiated frequency in hertz. A quarter-wave antenna is λ/4 long: kilometres at audio frequencies, centimetres to metres at radio frequencies. The worked example computes both.
  • Frequency assignment. Free space is shared by everyone. Regulators license bands (mobile services in India use bands near 900 MHz, 1800 MHz and 3.5 GHz) so that each transmitter stays inside its own slot. Modulation puts a signal into its assigned slot.
  • Multiplexing. Many messages share one channel when each sits on a different carrier: frequency division multiplexing (FDM). Time division multiplexing shares the time axis instead and is covered with pulse modulation.
  • Noise and interference. Modulation does not remove noise. It moves the signal to a band where interference is low, and the receiver's band-pass filter keeps only fc − Bs/2 to fc + Bs/2. Wideband FM goes further and trades extra bandwidth for a better output signal-to-noise ratio.
  • Propagation. Each frequency range travels differently: medium-wave broadcasts near 1 MHz follow the ground for long distances, while VHF and higher bands travel in near line of sight. The carrier is chosen to suit the distance to be covered.

Worked example

Question. A 1 kHz audio tone has to be radiated from an antenna. (a) Find its wavelength and the length of a quarter-wave antenna. (b) The same tone now modulates a 100 MHz carrier. Find the wavelength and the quarter-wave antenna length of the radiated wave. Take c = 3×108 m/s.

  1. Use λ = c/f. At f = 1 kHz = 1×103 Hz: λ = (3×108 m/s) ÷ (1×103 Hz) = 3×105 m = 300 km.
  2. Quarter-wave length = λ/4 = 300 km ÷ 4 = 75 km. No tower can hold a 75 km antenna, so the audio tone cannot be radiated directly.
  3. At the carrier fc = 100 MHz = 1×108 Hz: λ = (3×108 m/s) ÷ (1×108 Hz) = 3 m.
  4. Quarter-wave length = λ/4 = 3 m ÷ 4 = 0.75 m, that is 75 cm: a small, practical antenna.
  5. Check: the antenna shrank by 75 km ÷ 0.75 m = 1×105, which is exactly the ratio of the two frequencies, 100 MHz ÷ 1 kHz. The audio still reaches the listener, carried as a variation of the envelope A(t) or the phase φ(t).

Answer. At 1 kHz: λ = 300 km and λ/4 = 75 km. At 100 MHz: λ = 3 m and λ/4 = 0.75 m. Modulation shortens the antenna by a factor of 105.

Exam traps

  • Modulation does not create information and does not by itself improve the signal-to-noise ratio at the receiver input. It shifts the message spectrum so that the antenna, the frequency plan and multiplexing become practical.
  • Do not confuse the carrier frequency with the message frequency. The carrier decides where the signal sits in the spectrum; the message and the scheme decide the bandwidth.
  • In λ = c/f, put the frequency that is actually radiated. After modulation that is fc, not the audio frequency.
  • The receiver filter is centred on fc and is Bs wide in total, from fc − Bs/2 to fc + Bs/2, not fc ± Bs.
  • Baseband does not mean audio. Any signal whose spectrum starts at 0 Hz, including a train of digital pulses, is a baseband signal.

1.2 Fourier transform review and band-pass signals

A Fourier transform turns a time waveform into a frequency recipe: how much of each frequency the signal contains. Filters, antennas and cables act on frequency, so communication problems are easier there. A band-pass signal has all its content in a narrow band around a carrier, as an FM station near 100 MHz does. This chapter reviews the transform, its pairs and properties, and the low-pass description of band-pass signals.

You need: complex exponentials ejθ = cos θ + j sin θ and integration; the transform is taught in full with signals and systems.

The transform and its inverse

The Fourier transform writes a signal x(t) as a sum of complex exponentials ej2πft, each with a complex weight X(f); the inverse builds x(t) back.

X(f) = ∫ x(t) e−j2πft dt

the integral runs over all t from −∞ to ∞; X(f) is in volts per hertz (V/Hz) when x(t) is in volts; f is frequency in hertz; j = √−1.

x(t) = ∫ X(f) ej2πft df

the integral runs over all f; it adds every frequency component back into the time signal.

|X(f)| is the magnitude spectrum and ∠X(f) the phase spectrum. The unit V/Hz shows that X(f) is a density: only an integral over a band gives volts. Finite-energy signals always have a transform; power signals (cos, a constant) are handled with impulses δ(f).

LTI systems and distortionless transmission

A linear time-invariant (LTI) system with impulse response h(t) gives y(t) = x(t) * h(t), so Y(f) = X(f) H(f), where H(f) is the frequency response.

Write H(f) = |H(f)| ejθ(f). A system is distortionless when every frequency gets the same gain K and the same time delay td: y(t) = K x(t − td). In frequency this is H(f) = K e−j2πftd. The gain is flat, and the phase θ(f) = −2πftd is a straight line through the origin: the phase lag in radians grows with frequency, while the delay stays the same for every frequency.

Phase delay τp = −θ(f)/(2πf) is the delay of one frequency; group delay τg = −(1/2π) dθ/df is the delay of the envelope (θ in radians). With linear phase both equal td; otherwise a pulse spreads even when |H(f)| is flat.

Band-pass signals and the low-pass equivalent

A band-pass signal has a spectrum that is zero outside a band of width B centred at +fc and at −fc, with fc much larger than B. Its positive-frequency content lies between fc − B/2 and fc + B/2. Any such signal can be written in canonical form with two low-pass signals:

x(t) = xI(t) cos(2πfct) − xQ(t) sin(2πfct)

Here xI(t) (in-phase) and xQ(t) (quadrature) are real low-pass signals of bandwidth B/2; the pair, the low-pass equivalent, carries everything in x(t). Each can be sampled at B samples per second, 2B in total, far below the more than 2(fc + B/2) per second the band-pass signal itself would need. Hilbert transform and complex envelope shows how to find xI and xQ.

Name the bandwidth. A baseband spectrum is measured on positive frequencies: a pulse of width T has first-null bandwidth 1/T and 3-dB bandwidth 0.443/T. After modulation both sides sit around fc, so the band-pass values double: 2/T null to null and 0.886/T between half-power points.

Worked example

Question. A rectangular pulse is x(t) = 2 V for |t| < 0.5 ms and 0 elsewhere. (a) Find X(f) and X(0). (b) Find the first-null and the 3-dB bandwidths. (c) Find the pulse energy (per ohm) and the share of it inside the main lobe |f| < 1/T.

  1. Width T = 1 ms = 1×10−3 s and height A = 2 V.
  2. From the table, X(f) = A T sinc(fT). A T = 2 V × 1×10−3 s = 2×10−3 V/Hz, so X(f) = 2×10−3 sinc(10−3f) V/Hz and X(0) = 2 mV/Hz.
  3. The first null is at fT = 1, so f = 1/T = 1 kHz: the first-null bandwidth is 1 kHz.
  4. Half power means |X(f)| = X(0)/√2, that is sinc(fT) = 0.707. Solving gives fT = 0.443, so the 3-dB bandwidth is 0.443/T = 443 Hz.
  5. Energy in time: E = A²T = (2 V)² × 1×10−3 s = 4×10−3 V²·s.
  6. Main lobe (Parseval): ∫ |X(f)|² df over ±1 kHz = A²T ∫ sinc²(u) du over ±1 = 4×10−3 × 0.903 = 3.61×10−3 V²·s, 90.3 % of the energy.

Answer. X(f) = 2×10−3 sinc(10−3f) V/Hz with X(0) = 2 mV/Hz; first-null bandwidth 1 kHz and 3-dB bandwidth 443 Hz; energy 4×10−3 V²·s, of which 90.3 % lies in the main lobe.

Question. The same pulse modulates a 10 kHz carrier: y(t) = x(t) cos(2π×104t). Find Y(f), its peak value, the band it occupies (null to null), its energy and its in-phase and quadrature components about fc = 10 kHz.

Exam traps

  • Bandwidth is not one number: say first-null, 3-dB or null-to-null, and baseband (1/T for the pulse) or band-pass (2/T).
  • x(t) cos(2πfct) gives two copies of half height, ½X(f ∓ fc). Writing X(f − fc) alone is the shift by ej2πfct, not by a real carrier.
  • A time shift multiplies X(f) by e−j2πft0; |X(f)| does not change.
  • Distortionless means constant delay for every frequency. It is the phase, θ(f) = −2πftd, that grows with f. Put θ in radians in τp and τg; degrees give an answer wrong by 180/π.
  • Time convolution pairs with frequency multiplication. Y(f) = X(f)/H(f) is undoing a filter (equalization), not passing a signal through it.

1.3 Hilbert transform and complex envelope

A real band-pass signal carries its information in a narrow band around a carrier, so the carrier itself is only a vehicle. The Hilbert transform gives a 90° shifted copy of the signal; adding it as an imaginary part makes the analytic signal, which has only positive frequencies. Removing the carrier from the analytic signal leaves the complex envelope, a low-pass signal whose real and imaginary parts are the in-phase (I) and quadrature (Q) components. SSB transmitters, I/Q modulators, digital receivers and simulators all use this idea.

You need: Fourier transform review and band-pass signals, in particular the canonical form xI cos − xQ sin.

The Hilbert transform

A real signal has a conjugate-symmetric spectrum, X(−f) = X*(f): the negative-frequency half is the mirror image of the positive half and carries nothing new. The Hilbert transform produces a companion signal x̂(t) whose every frequency component is shifted by 90° while its magnitude stays the same.

x̂(t) = (1/π) ∫ x(τ)/(t − τ) dτ

the integral runs over all τ and is taken as a Cauchy principal value, which steps symmetrically round the point τ = t; x̂(t) is in volts. In words, x̂(t) = x(t) * 1/(πt).

H(f) = −j sgn(f)

sgn(f) = 1 for f > 0, −1 for f < 0 and 0 at f = 0. So H(f) = −j (a −90° shift) for positive frequencies and +j (a +90° shift) for negative frequencies.

  • Cosine becomes sine: the Hilbert transform of cos(2πf0t + θ) is sin(2πf0t + θ), for f0 > 0.
  • Sine becomes minus cosine: the transform of sin(2πf0t + θ) is −cos(2πf0t + θ).
  • A constant (f = 0) has transform zero, and applying the transform twice gives −x(t).
  • x(t) and x̂(t) have the same energy (or power) and are orthogonal: ∫ x(t) x̂(t) dt = 0.
  • For a message m(t) band-limited below fc, the transform of m(t) cos(2πfct) is m(t) sin(2πfct): only the carrier is shifted.
  • The ideal transformer is non-causal; a practical 90° phase-shift network or digital filter approximates it over the band of interest.

Analytic signal and complex envelope

The analytic signal, also called the pre-envelope, is xa(t) = x(t) + j x̂(t). Its spectrum is X(f) + j(−j sgn f)X(f) = [1 + sgn(f)]X(f): 2X(f) for f > 0 and 0 for f < 0. The negative frequencies cancel and the positive ones double, so Re{xa(t)} is still x(t).

The complex envelope removes the carrier: x̃(t) = xa(t) e−j2πfct. In frequency, X̃(f) = Xa(f + fc): the doubled positive band slides down to sit around 0 Hz. It is low-pass and complex, and it is not the analytic signal, which still contains ej2πfct.

Writing x̃(t) = xI(t) + j xQ(t) gives the canonical representation, x(t) = Re{x̃(t) ej2πfct} = xI(t) cos(2πfct) − xQ(t) sin(2πfct). In polar form, the natural envelope is a(t) = |x̃(t)| = |xa(t)| = √(xI² + xQ²) and the phase is φ(t) = tan−1(xQ/xI), so x(t) = a(t) cos(2πfct + φ(t)).

A receiver finds I and Q by downconversion: multiply x(t) by 2cos(2πfct) and by −2sin(2πfct), then low-pass filter each product. For the first, 2x cos = xI(1 + cos 4πfct) − xQ sin 4πfct, and the filter keeps xI. Upconversion runs the other way: the transmitter forms xI cos − xQ sin.

Low-pass equivalent of a band-pass system

A band-pass filter with real impulse response h(t) also has a complex envelope h̃(t), defined by h(t) = Re{h̃(t) ej2πfct}. With that definition the output complex envelope is ỹ(t) = ½ x̃(t) * h̃(t), and the real output is y(t) = Re{ỹ(t) ej2πfct}. The ½ comes from writing both signals with the same Re{·} rule; some books define h̃ with a factor 2 and then drop the ½. Keep one convention: mixing them changes the gain by a factor 2, which is 6 dB.

Some syllabi call the complex envelope the low-pass equivalent. It turns band-pass filtering, modulation and noise into low-pass problems with complex numbers, and a simulator can run at a sample rate set by the bandwidth instead of by the carrier. In single-sideband generation one path carries m(t) cos(2πfct) and the other the Hilbert-transformed m̂(t) sin(2πfct); subtracting or adding them cancels one sideband (the phase-shift method of SSB modulation).

Worked example

Question. A band-pass voltage is x(t) = 2 cos(2πfct + 30°) V with fc > 0. Find x̂(t), the analytic signal xa(t), the complex envelope x̃(t), and the I and Q components.

  1. The Hilbert transform of a cosine is the sine with the same phase: x̂(t) = 2 sin(2πfct + 30°) V.
  2. xa(t) = x + j x̂ = 2[cos(2πfct + 30°) + j sin(2πfct + 30°)] = 2ej(2πfct + 30°) V.
  3. Remove the carrier: x̃(t) = xa(t) e−j2πfct = 2ej30° V, a constant for this pure tone.
  4. Rectangular form: 2 cos 30° = 1.732 V and 2 sin 30° = 1.000 V, so x̃ = 1.732 + j1.000 V: I = 1.732 V, Q = 1.000 V.
  5. Check: 1.732 cos(2πfct) − 1.000 sin(2πfct) = 2[cos 30° cos(2πfct) − sin 30° sin(2πfct)] = 2 cos(2πfct + 30°) V. The envelope is |x̃| = √(1.732² + 1²) = 2 V.

Answer. x̂ = 2 sin(2πfct + 30°) V; xa = 2ej(2πfct + 30°) V; x̃ = 1.732 + j1.000 V; I = 1.732 V, Q = 1.000 V; envelope 2 V.

1.4 Probability and random variables review

A random variable assigns a real number to each outcome of an experiment, such as a noise voltage sampled at one instant or the number of bit errors in a packet. Its distribution gives the probability that the number falls in any range. A receiver compares noisy samples with a threshold, so these tools predict its error rate. This chapter reviews probability rules, random variables and their moments, the common distributions, and functions of random variables.

You need: sets, sums and integrals; no earlier chapter of this book.

Probability rules, conditioning and Bayes' rule

An experiment has a sample space S, the set of all outcomes. An event A is a subset of S. Probability obeys three axioms: P(A) ≥ 0; P(S) = 1; and for disjoint events, P(A1 ∪ A2 ∪ …) = P(A1) + P(A2) + …. From them follow P(Ac) = 1 − P(A) and P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B).

P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)

P(A|B) is the probability of A given that B occurred; it is defined only when P(B) > 0.

Rearranged, it gives the multiplication rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A|B)P(B). Events A and B are independent when P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B); then, if P(B) > 0, P(A|B) = P(A). Mutually exclusive events cannot happen together, so two exclusive events with non-zero probabilities are never independent.

If A1, …, An split S into disjoint pieces, the total probability of B is P(B) = Σ P(B|Ai)P(Ai), and Bayes' rule reverses the condition: P(Ai|B) = P(B|Ai)P(Ai) / P(B). In a receiver Ai is "symbol i was sent" (the prior), B is what was received, P(B|Ai) is the likelihood and P(Ai|B) the posterior. Worked example 2 uses it on a binary channel.

Random variables, CDF, PDF and moments

A random variable X maps each outcome to a real number. Its cumulative distribution function (CDF) is FX(x) = P(X ≤ x): it never decreases, is right-continuous, and runs from FX(−∞) = 0 to FX(∞) = 1. A discrete variable has a probability mass function pX(x) = P(X = x). A continuous variable has a probability density function (PDF) fX(x) = dFX/dx, with total area 1 and P(a < X ≤ b) = FX(b) − FX(a) = ∫ fX(x) dx from a to b. The height fX(x) is a density, not a probability, and P(X = x) = 0 for every single x.

The mean is μ = E[X] = ∫ x fX(x) dx (or Σ x pX(x)). The variance is σ² = E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − μ², and σ is the standard deviation, in the units of X. E[X²] is the mean-square value: for a noise voltage it is the average power in 1 Ω, and it equals σ² only when μ = 0. A linear change Y = aX + b gives E[Y] = aμ + b and var(Y) = a²σ².

Joint variables, functions of a variable and the CLT

Two variables have a joint PDF fXY(x, y). The marginal is fX(x) = ∫ fXY(x, y) dy and the conditional is fX|Y(x|y) = fXY(x, y)/fY(y) where fY(y) > 0. X and Y are independent when fXY = fXfY, and uncorrelated when E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]. Independence implies uncorrelated, but not the reverse; only for jointly Gaussian variables does zero correlation imply independence.

For a function Y = g(X) that is monotonic and differentiable, fY(y) = fX(x)/|g′(x)| with x = g−1(y); if several x give the same y, add the term for each root. The CDF method, FY(y) = P(g(X) ≤ y) and then differentiate, always works. Some syllabi call this transformation of random variables. The characteristic function φX(ω) = E[ejωX] is the Fourier transform of the PDF with the sign of the exponent reversed, and its derivatives at ω = 0 give the moments.

The central limit theorem (CLT): the sum of n independent, identically distributed variables with finite variance tends to a Gaussian as n grows, with mean and variance equal to the sums of the means and variances. For terms that are not identical, an extra condition (Lindeberg's) must keep any one term from dominating. Thermal noise, a sum of countless tiny electron contributions, is therefore Gaussian.

Question. A Gaussian noise voltage V has mean 0 mV and standard deviation 2 mV. Find the probability that a sample exceeds 4 mV, and the probability that it lies between −2 mV and 2 mV.

Question. A binary channel carries 1s with probability 0.6 and 0s with probability 0.4. A sent 1 is received as 0 with probability 0.05; a sent 0 is received as 1 with probability 0.1.   • In the Gaussian exponent use σ², and standardize with z = (x − μ)/σ. Q(z) is the upper tail and Φ(z) the lower area; do not mix them.
  • Uncorrelated does not mean independent, except for jointly Gaussian variables. Exclusive is not the same as independent.

1.5 Random processes, stationarity, correlation and power spectral density

A random process is a family of random waveforms indexed by time: one outcome gives one waveform, and the whole family is the ensemble. Noise, speech and fading are modelled this way. For a wide-sense stationary process the mean is constant and the autocorrelation depends only on the time gap τ; its Fourier transform, the power spectral density (PSD), shows how the average power is spread over frequency, and a filter multiplies it by |H(f)|².

You need: Probability and random variables review (mean, variance, joint variables) and the Fourier pairs in Fourier transform review and band-pass signals.

Random process, mean and autocorrelation

A random process X(t) assigns a random variable to every time t. Fix one outcome and you get a sample function x(t), one realization. Fix a time t and you get a random variable X(t). The set of all sample functions is the ensemble.

The mean is μX(t) = E[X(t)], an average across the ensemble at one instant. The autocorrelation compares the process at two instants: RX(t1, t2) = E[X(t1)X(t2)]. For a voltage across 1 Ω its unit is watts (strictly V²). The autocovariance removes the mean: CX(t1, t2) = RX(t1, t2) − μX(t1)μX(t2), and CX(t, t) is the variance at time t.

For two processes the cross-correlation is RXY(t1, t2) = E[X(t1)Y(t2)]. It measures how one signal follows the other at a delay. RXY and RYX are different functions, but for jointly stationary processes they are time-reversed copies: RXY(τ) = RYX(−τ).

Stationarity and ergodicity

A process is strict-sense stationary when every joint distribution depends only on time differences, not on absolute time. That is hard to check, so communication work uses wide-sense stationarity (WSS): the mean is a constant μX, and the autocorrelation depends only on the gap τ.

RX(τ) = E[X(t + τ) X(t)]

τ is the time gap in seconds; the same RX(τ) results whatever t is chosen.

  • RX(0) = E[X²(t)], the average power. The variance is RX(0) − μX².
  • RX(−τ) = RX(τ): the autocorrelation of a real process is even.
  • |RX(τ)| ≤ RX(0): no delay gives more correlation than zero delay.
  • A periodic component in X(t) gives a periodic component in RX(τ).

A strict-sense stationary process is also WSS; the reverse holds only for Gaussian processes. A process is ergodic when time averages of one long sample function equal the ensemble averages, so μX and RX(τ) can be measured from a single recording. WSS does not imply ergodic: a random DC level (each sample function is a different constant) is WSS, yet one recording never shows the ensemble mean.

PSD, the Wiener–Khinchin theorem and filtering

A stationary process lasts for ever with finite average power, so each sample function has infinite energy and no ordinary Fourier transform. Its power is described instead by the power spectral density SX(f) in W/Hz. The Wiener–Khinchin theorem (also spelt Wiener–Khinchine; some syllabi call SX the power density spectrum) says that RX(τ) and SX(f) are a Fourier pair:

S<sub>X</sub>(f) = ∫ R<sub>X</sub>(τ) e<sup>−j2πfτ</sup> dτ

the integral runs over all τ; SX(f) is the two-sided PSD; the inverse is RX(τ) = ∫ SX(f) ej2πfτ df.

Setting τ = 0 in the inverse gives the power: P = RX(0) = ∫ SX(f) df over all f. For a real process SX(f) is real, never negative and even. A one-sided PSD folds the negative frequencies onto the positive ones, so it is twice the two-sided value for f > 0 and is integrated from 0 only.

Pass a WSS process X(t) through an LTI filter H(f). The output Y(t) is WSS, its mean is μY = H(0)μX, and

SY(f) = |H(f)|² SX(f)

|H(f)|² is the power gain at frequency f; the output power is PY = ∫ SY(f) df = RY(0).

For example, white noise of two-sided PSD N0/2 through an ideal low-pass filter (gain 1 for |f| ≤ B) gives PY = (N0/2)(2B) = N0B, and its autocorrelation is the inverse transform of a rectangle: RY(τ) = N0B sinc(2Bτ), which first crosses zero at τ = 1/(2B). Samples of this noise taken 1/(2B) apart are uncorrelated.

1.6 Noise sources, white noise and AWGN

Every receiver picks up unwanted random voltage along with the signal: electrons jostle in resistors, charges cross junctions at random instants, and the antenna collects what the sky and the city radiate. Noise can only be described by its statistics. Modelling it as additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) reduces it to one number, the noise power inside the receiver bandwidth, where every later signal-to-noise and error-rate result starts.

You need: power spectral density and filtering from Random processes, stationarity, correlation and power spectral density, and the Gaussian PDF.

Where noise comes from

External noise enters through the antenna. Atmospheric noise comes from lightning and is strongest below about 30 MHz. Extraterrestrial noise comes from the Sun (solar noise) and from distant stars and the galaxy (cosmic noise). Industrial or man-made noise comes from vehicle ignition, motors, switching power supplies and power lines. Shielding, filtering and the choice of band are the defences.

Internal noise is made inside the receiver's own components. The main kind is thermal noise (Johnson or Johnson–Nyquist noise): every resistor at absolute temperature T makes a random voltage because its electrons are in thermal motion. It needs no current, and only cooling or a narrower bandwidth reduces it.

P = kTB, vn² = 4kTRB

P is the available noise power in watts, delivered to a matched load; k = 1.38×10−23 J/K is Boltzmann's constant; T is in kelvin; B is the noise bandwidth in hertz; R is the resistance in ohms; vn is the RMS open-circuit noise voltage. A resistor is drawn as a noise source vn in series with a noiseless R.

  • Shot noise: charge carriers cross a junction at random instants, so a DC current I carries a fluctuation of mean-square value in² = 2qIB, where in is the RMS noise current in amperes and q = 1.6×10−19 C. It appears in diodes, bipolar transistors and photodiodes.
  • Partition noise: in a device with several electrodes the current divides between them at random (between base and collector in a transistor).
  • Flicker (1/f) noise: traps capture and release carriers, giving a PSD that rises as 1/f; it dominates below a corner frequency of a few kilohertz in many devices.
  • Transit-time noise: at very high frequencies the carrier transit time becomes comparable to the signal period, and the device noise rises with frequency.
  • Burst (popcorn) noise: a defect switches the current between two levels at random.

Resistors in series add their mean-square voltages, because their noises are independent: vn² = 4kT(R1 + R2)B. In parallel, use the parallel resistance R1R2/(R1 + R2). Worked example 2 does both.

White noise: PSD, autocorrelation and noise bandwidth

White noise has the same power spectral density (PSD) at every frequency, as white light holds every colour. Its two-sided PSD is S(f) = N0/2 W/Hz for all f, and its autocorrelation is the inverse transform of a constant, R(τ) = (N0/2)δ(τ): any two samples, however close, are uncorrelated. For thermal noise N0 = kT (available power per hertz).

Its total power would be infinite, so white noise is a model, valid over the band the receiver actually uses (thermal noise at room temperature stays nearly flat up to about 1 THz). Through a filter of bandwidth B, low-pass or band-pass at any centre, the power is P = N0B: only the width matters.

A real filter has sloping skirts. Its equivalent noise bandwidth Bn is the width of an ideal filter, with the same peak power gain, that passes the same white-noise power: Bn = ∫0∞ |H(f)|² df / |H|max². For a single-pole RC low-pass, Bn = 1/(4RC) = (π/2)f3dB = 1.571 f3dB. Put Bn, not f3dB, into kTB.

Gaussian noise, narrowband noise, AWGN and SNR

Thermal noise is the sum of an enormous number of independent electron motions, so by the central limit theorem it is Gaussian with zero mean. Its PDF is p(v) = e−v²/2σ²/√(2πσ²), where σ is the RMS noise voltage in volts and σ² is the mean-square voltage in V², which equals the average power only across 1 Ω.

After the receiver's band-pass filter the noise is narrowband noise, centred on fc with bandwidth B. Like any band-pass signal it has a canonical form, n(t) = nI(t) cos(2πfct) − nQ(t) sin(2πfct). For a flat band, nI and nQ are zero-mean Gaussian, low-pass to B/2, each with PSD N0 and the same power N0B as n(t); their envelope √(nI² + nQ²) has a Rayleigh PDF. This is what an AM or FM demodulator sees.

AWGN joins the pieces: the noise adds to the signal, r(t) = s(t) + n(t); its amplitude is Gaussian; its PSD is flat. The model fits a receiver limited by thermal noise. It does not fit impulsive interference, nor fading, which multiplies the signal instead of adding to it.

Signal-to-noise ratio is the average signal power over the average noise power in the same bandwidth: SNR = Ps/(N0B), and SNR(dB) = 10 log10(Ps/Pn). Noise powers are quoted in dBm, decibels relative to 1 mW. Digital work quotes Eb/N0, the energy per bit over N0, which does not depend on the bandwidth.

Worked example

Question. A 1 kΩ resistor at T = 290 K feeds a receiver whose equivalent noise bandwidth is 1 MHz. Take k = 1.38×10−23 J/K. Find (a) the available noise power in watts and dBm, (b) the RMS open-circuit noise voltage, (c) N0 in W/Hz and dBm/Hz, and (d) the SNR for a 1 mW signal.

  1. P = kTB = 1.38×10−23 × 290 × 106 = 4.002×10−15 W.
  2. In dBm: 10 log10(4.002×10−15 / 10−3) = 10 log10(4.002×10−12) = −114.0 dBm.
  3. vn = √(4kTRB) = √(4 × 1.38×10−23 × 290 × 1000 × 106) = √(1.601×10−11) = 4.00×10−6 V = 4.0 µV.
  4. N0 = kT = 4.002×10−21 W/Hz = −174.0 dBm/Hz. Check: N0B = 4.002×10−15 W, as in step 1.
  5. SNR = 10−3/4.002×10−15 = 2.50×1011, that is 0 dBm − (−114.0 dBm) = 114.0 dB.
  6. Check: a matched 1 kΩ load sees vn/2 = 2.0 µV, and (2.0 µV)²/1000 Ω = 4.0×10−15 W = kTB.

Answer. P = 4.00×10−15 W = −114.0 dBm; vn = 4.0 µV; N0 = 4.0×10−21 W/Hz = −174.0 dBm/Hz; SNR = 114.0 dB.

Exam traps

  • White noise has infinite total power. A noise power means nothing until a bandwidth is fixed; look for the B or N0 the question gives.
  • N0 and N0/2 differ by 3 dB. Integrate N0/2 over −∞ to ∞ or N0 over 0 to ∞, never N0 over both sides.
  • Available power kTB has no R in it; the voltage √(4kTRB) does. Doubling R doubles vn² and leaves the available power unchanged.
  • Independent noise sources add in mean square (v²), not in RMS volts.
  • dBm and dBW differ by 30 dB: 4×10−15 W is −114 dBm but −144 dBW. White describes the spectrum, Gaussian the amplitude; noise can be one without the other.

1.7 Noise figure and noise temperature

A receiver adds noise of its own, so the signal-to-noise ratio at its output is lower than at its input. Noise figure and noise temperature put that loss into one number. With them an engineer compares amplifiers, decides what goes first in a receiver chain, and predicts the SNR that reaches the demodulator. This chapter defines both, relates them, and combines stages in cascade with the Friis formula.

You need: thermal noise power kTB and noise bandwidth from Noise sources, white noise and AWGN, and decibels.

Noise factor and noise figure

Feed an amplifier of power gain G from a matched source at the standard temperature T0 = 290 K. The input noise is kT0B. The output noise No is G kT0B (amplified input noise) plus Na, the noise the amplifier adds. The noise factor F compares the two SNRs:

F = SNR<sub>in</sub> / SNR<sub>out</sub> = N<sub>o</sub> / (G kT<sub>0</sub>B)

F is a linear ratio with no unit; the second form follows because the signal is amplified by G as well; F = 1 + Na/(G kT0B).

F is at least 1, and a noiseless device has F = 1. The noise figure is the same quantity in decibels, NF = 10 log10 F: F = 2 is NF = 3.01 dB. Some syllabi use "noise figure" for both. Datasheets quote NF in dB; formulas need linear F, because noise powers add and gains multiply. T0 = 290 K (about 17 °C) is a fixed convention, not the room temperature of the day.

Equivalent noise temperature

The equivalent noise temperature Te describes the added noise as if it came from an extra matched resistor at the input: the added noise power, referred to the input, is kTeB, and at the output it is G kTeB. Te is a bookkeeping temperature, not the physical temperature of the device. Putting Na = G kTeB into the formula for F gives

F = 1 + T<sub>e</sub>/T<sub>0</sub>,   T<sub>e</sub> = (F − 1)T<sub>0</sub>

Te and T0 = 290 K are in kelvin; F is linear.

F = 2 means Te = 290 K. Noise temperature suits very quiet devices: an LNA with NF = 1 dB has Te = 75 K, and a few kelvin are easier to compare than the second decimal of a dB value.

A lossy cable or attenuator with power loss L (input power over output power, L ≥ 1) has gain G = 1/L. At physical temperature T0 its noise factor equals its loss, F = L, so Te = (L − 1)T0. A 3 dB loss (L = 2) is F = 2 and Te = 290 K; a 6 dB loss (L = 3.981) is Te = 864.5 K.

Cascaded stages: the Friis formula

A receiver is a chain of stages with linear gains G1, G2, … and noise factors F1, F2, …. Each stage's added noise, referred back to the chain input, is divided by the total gain in front of it. That gives the Friis formula, in two equivalent forms:

F = F1 + (F2 − 1)/G1 + (F3 − 1)/(G1G2) + …

Te = Te1 + Te2/G1 + Te3/(G1G2) + …

A large G1 makes every later term small, so the first stage sets the noise of the whole chain. That is why a receiver puts a low-noise amplifier (LNA) with good gain first, and why loss in front of it is costly: a cable before the LNA has G1 < 1, which multiplies every later term instead of dividing it.

  1. Convert every NF to F = 10NF/10 and every gain to G = 10G(dB)/10; a loss of L dB is F = 10L/10 and G = 1/F.
  2. Put the linear values into Friis, keeping at least four significant figures.
  3. Convert back: NF = 10 log10 F, or Te = (F − 1)T0.

The antenna delivers noise of its own, described by the antenna temperature Ta (sky and ground seen by the beam). The system noise temperature is Tsys = Ta + Te, and the noise power at the receiver input is kTsysB.

Worked example

Question. A two-stage receiver has stage 1 with gain 20 dB and noise figure 3 dB, and stage 2 with gain 20 dB and noise figure 6 dB. Find the overall noise factor, noise figure and equivalent noise temperature (T0 = 290 K).

  1. G1 = 1020/10 = 100. F1 = 103/10 = 1.995. F2 = 106/10 = 3.981.
  2. Friis: F = F1 + (F2 − 1)/G1 = 1.995 + 2.981/100 = 1.995 + 0.0298 = 2.025.
  3. NF = 10 log10 2.025 = 3.06 dB, only 0.06 dB worse than stage 1 alone.
  4. Te = (2.025 − 1) × 290 = 297.3 K.
  5. Check with the temperature form: Te1 = 0.995 × 290 = 288.6 K and Te2 = 2.981 × 290 = 864.5 K, so Te = 288.6 + 864.5/100 = 288.6 + 8.6 = 297.3 K. The gain of stage 2 does not enter: nothing follows it.

Answer. F = 2.025, NF = 3.06 dB, Te = 297.3 K.

Exam traps

  • Do not add noise figures in dB, and do not put dB values into Friis. Convert to linear F and G, apply the formula, then convert back.
  • T0 is 290 K, not 300 K. Using 300 K shifts every Te and is a common marking error.
  • F is linear and NF is in dB. F cannot be below 1, so NF cannot be negative.
  • A lossy cable at T0 has F = L and gain 1/L. Before the LNA it adds its own noise and also multiplies the later stages' terms by L.
  • The last stage's gain never appears in Friis. And Te is referred to the input: at the output the added noise is G kTeB.

Takeaways

  1. A communication system is source, transmitter, channel, receiver and destination; the channel attenuates the signal and adds noise, r(t) = s(t) + n(t).
  2. Modulation moves the message spectrum to a carrier for antenna size, frequency assignment, multiplexing, interference rejection and propagation; it creates no information and does not by itself raise the input SNR.
  3. The Fourier transform turns filtering into multiplication and modulation into shifting: x(t)cos(2πfct) has two half-height copies at ±fc, and its band-pass bandwidth is twice the baseband one.
  4. A band-pass signal is fully described by its complex envelope x̃ = xI + j xQ, found with the Hilbert transform (cos → sin) and a shift by fc.
  5. Probability is an area under the PDF; the Gaussian tail Q(z) with z = (x − μ)/σ gives threshold and error probabilities, and Bayes' rule turns likelihoods into posteriors.
  6. For a WSS process the autocorrelation and the PSD are a Fourier pair; RX(0) is the power, and a filter multiplies the PSD by |H(f)|².
  7. White noise is a model with infinite power; in a bandwidth B it gives N0B, which for thermal noise is kTB, −174 dBm/Hz + 10 log10B. Use the equivalent noise bandwidth for real filters.
  8. Noise figure and noise temperature measure what a stage adds; in a cascade the first stage dominates (Friis), so a low-noise amplifier goes first and loss before it is costly.

Understand Draw the block diagram of a communication system and explain, with numbers, why modulation is needed. 10 marks

Model answer.

  1. Block diagram (draw left to right): information source and input transducer → transmitter → channel → receiver → destination and output transducer, with a noise source n(t) feeding the channel. The source gives the message m(t); the transmitter filters, modulates and amplifies it into s(t); the channel attenuates s(t) and adds noise, so the receiver gets r(t) = s(t) + n(t); the receiver filters, amplifies and demodulates to give the estimate m̂(t).
  2. State the idea: modulation varies a carrier cos(2πfct) with m(t). It copies the message spectrum to ±fc; it adds no information. A message of bandwidth B becomes, for AM, a signal of bandwidth Bs = 2B centred on fc.
  3. Reason 1, antenna size. An antenna radiates well when it is about a quarter wavelength long, λ/4 = c/(4f) with c = 3×108 m/s:
    Frequency radiatedλ = c/fλ/4
    1 kHz (audio tone)300 km75 km
    1 MHz (medium wave)300 m75 m
    100 MHz (FM band)3 m0.75 m
  4. So a 1 kHz tone would need a 75 km antenna, while on a 100 MHz carrier it needs 0.75 m: smaller by 75 km ÷ 0.75 m = 105, the ratio of the frequencies.
  5. Reason 2, frequency assignment. Every baseband audio signal occupies the same range, about 0 to 5 kHz. If all stations radiated at baseband they would overlap. Regulators give each service its own band, and modulation places the signal in it.
  6. Reason 3, multiplexing (FDM). With AM, a 5 kHz message takes 2B = 10 kHz, so carriers 10 kHz apart do not overlap. One megahertz of spectrum then holds 1 MHz ÷ 10 kHz = 100 programmes, which one cable or one antenna can carry at once.
  7. Reason 4, noise and interference. Modulation does not remove noise, but the receiver's band-pass filter passes only fc − Bs/2 to fc + Bs/2 (for a 1 MHz AM station with B = 5 kHz, 0.995 to 1.005 MHz) and rejects every signal outside it. Wideband schemes such as FM go further and trade bandwidth for a better output SNR.
  8. Reason 5, propagation. The carrier frequency decides how the wave travels: medium-wave signals near 1 MHz follow the ground for long distances, while VHF and higher frequencies travel in near line of sight. The carrier is chosen to suit the range needed.
  9. Close with the limit: modulation shifts the spectrum and makes antennas, frequency plans and multiplexing practical; the SNR at the receiver input is still set by signal power and noise power in the band. Check: the antenna ratio 105 equals 100 MHz ÷ 1 kHz, as λ ∝ 1/f requires.

Model answer.

  9. What band-limiting does in time: white noise through an ideal low-pass filter of bandwidth B has
    S(f) = N₀/2 for |f| ≤ B   →   R(τ) = N₀B sinc(2Bτ)
R(0) = N₀B (finite power);  zeros at τ = 1/(2B), 2/(2B), …
as B → ∞, N₀B sinc(2Bτ) → (N₀/2)δ(τ), the white-noise autocorrelation
  10. So band-limited white noise is the physical object: its samples taken 1/(2B) apart are uncorrelated (and, being Gaussian, independent). For B = 10 kHz that spacing is 50 µs.
  11. Quick rule in dBm: thermal noise power = −174 dBm/Hz + 10 log10B at 290 K. For B = 10 kHz: −174.0 + 40.0 = −134.0 dBm, which is 4.0×10−17 W, the ideal-filter row of the table.

Analyze Explain the complex-envelope or low-pass equivalent approach for band-pass signals and systems, and show why it simplifies the analysis of modulation, filtering and noise. 13 marks

Points to cover. Analytic signal, then shift by fc to get x̃(t); Canonical form x = xIcos − xQsin; envelope and phase; Band-pass filter → low-pass filter on x̃, with ỹ = ½x̃ * h̃; Modulation and demodulation become multiplication by e±j2πfct; Noise: nI, nQ Gaussian with the same power; Sample-rate saving with numbers; narrowband condition B ≪ fc.

Model answer.

  1. Start: a real band-pass signal x(t) with spectrum in a band B around ±fc, B ≪ fc. Form the analytic signal xa = x + j x̂ (positive frequencies only, doubled), then remove the carrier: x̃(t) = xa(t)e−j2πfct = xI(t) + j xQ(t). Its spectrum lies in −B/2 to B/2.
  2. Representation: x(t) = Re{x̃(t)ej2πfct} = xI cos(2πfct) − xQ sin(2πfct) = a(t) cos(2πfct + φ(t)), with envelope a = |x̃| and phase φ = ∠x̃. The carrier is known, so x̃ carries all the information.
  3. Modulation in this language: each scheme is just a choice of x̃(t).
    SchemeComplex envelope x̃(t)
    AMAc[1 + k m(t)]
    DSB-SCAc m(t)
    PMAc ejk m(t)
    tone offset by ΔA ej2πΔt (rotates at Δ)
  4. Filtering: a band-pass filter h(t) = Re{h̃(t)ej2πfct} acts on the envelope as ỹ = ½ x̃ * h̃, a low-pass convolution; the real output is y = Re{ỹ ej2πfct}. No carrier terms appear in the working.
  5. Up- and downconversion: the transmitter forms xIcos − xQsin; the receiver multiplies by 2cos(2πfct) and −2sin(2πfct) and low-pass filters to recover xI and xQ. Example: a 2 V tone at 1.001 MHz, seen about fc = 1 MHz, gives x̃ = 2ej2π(1000)t: at t = 0.125 ms, I = Q = 1.414 V.
  6. Noise: narrowband Gaussian noise of PSD N₀/2 in the band becomes nI + j nQ, each Gaussian, low-pass to B/2, PSD N₀, power N₀B, the same as n(t). So SNR calculations for AM, FM and digital receivers can be done entirely at baseband.
  7. Sample-rate saving, for one FM channel of B = 200 kHz at fc = 100 MHz:
    band-pass signal sampled directly: more than 2(fc + B/2) = 2 × 100.1 MHz = 200.2 Msamples/s
I and Q, each low-pass to 100 kHz: 2 × 200 ksamples/s = 400 ksamples/s
ratio 200.2×106 / 4×105 ≈ 500

  9. Without the ½ the same filter would appear to double the envelope, a 6 dB error; this check is the quickest way to confirm which convention a textbook uses.
  10. Limits: the method needs B ≪ fc so the two spectral halves do not overlap, a fixed reference fc (the envelope changes with the choice), and one consistent convention for the ½.
  11. Check on the canonical form: for x = 2cos(2πfct + 30°), x̃ = 1.732 + j1, and 1.732cos − 1·sin = 2cos(2πfct + 30°).

Points to cover. Analytic signal and complex envelope; x = Re{x̃ej2πfct}; Expand to xIcos − xQsin; xI = x cos + x̂ sin, xQ = x̂ cos − x sin; Recover by multiplying by 2cos and −2sin and low-pass filtering; Spectra: low-pass, width B/2; XI(f) = X(f − fc) + X(f + fc) when the spectrum is symmetric about fc; powers; Numerical check.

Model answer.

  1. Step 1, analytic signal: xa(t) = x(t) + j x̂(t), whose spectrum is 2X(f) for f > 0 and 0 for f < 0. It contains the carrier.
  2. Step 2, complex envelope: x̃(t) = xa(t)e−j2πfct, spectrum X̃(f) = 2X(f + fc) for f + fc > 0, a low-pass spectrum in −B/2 to B/2. Write x̃ = xI + j xQ.
  3. Step 3, back to x(t): since x = Re{xa} = Re{x̃ ej2πfct},
    x(t) = Re{(xI + j xQ)(cos 2πfct + j sin 2πfct)}
     = xI(t) cos(2πfct) − xQ(t) sin(2πfct)
  4. Step 4, I and Q in terms of x and x̂ (take real and imaginary parts of xae−j2πfct):
    xI(t) = x(t) cos(2πfct) + x̂(t) sin(2πfct)
xQ(t) = x̂(t) cos(2πfct) − x(t) sin(2πfct)
  5. Step 5, recovery in hardware: 2x cos(2πfct) = xI + xIcos(4πfct) − xQsin(4πfct); a low-pass filter keeps xI. Likewise −2x sin(2πfct) = xQ − xQcos(4πfct) − xIsin(4πfct), and the filter keeps xQ. The minus sign in the canonical form is why the quadrature branch uses −2sin.

Evaluate Distinguish between uncorrelated and statistically independent random variables, and justify the Gaussian model for receiver noise using the central limit theorem. 12 marks

Points to cover. Uncorrelated: E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]; independent: joint PDF factorises; Independence implies uncorrelated, not the reverse; counterexample Y = X²; Jointly Gaussian: uncorrelated implies independent; CLT statement and conditions; numerical check with a sum of uniforms; Thermal noise as a sum of many electron contributions; Gaussian preserved by linear filters; Limits: impulsive and burst noise, interference.

Model answer.

  1. Uncorrelated: cov(X, Y) = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y] = 0. Independent: fXY(x, y) = fX(x)fY(y) for all x, y, so every function of X is independent of every function of Y.
  2. Independence implies uncorrelated: E[XY] = ∫∫ xy fXfY dx dy = E[X]E[Y]. The reverse fails. Counterexample: X uniform on [−1, 1] and Y = X². E[X] = 0 and E[XY] = E[X³] = 0, so cov = 0, yet Y is completely fixed by X: P(Y > 0.25 | X = 0.9) = 1 while P(Y > 0.25) = P(|X| > 0.5) = 0.5.
  3. Special case: if X and Y are jointly Gaussian and uncorrelated, the joint PDF factorises into the two marginals, so they are independent. Two variables that are each Gaussian but not jointly Gaussian need not be.
  4. Central limit theorem: the sum of n independent, identically distributed variables with finite variance, standardised, tends to N(0, 1) as n grows; for unequal terms a condition such as Lindeberg's keeps any single term from dominating.
  5. Numerical check: S = sum of 12 independent uniforms on [−0.5, 0.5] has mean 0 and variance 12 × 1/12 = 1. Its exact tails (Irwin–Hall distribution) against the Gaussian:
    EventExact for the sumGaussian N(0, 1)
    |S| > 20.04460.0455
    |S| > 30.002010.00270
  6. Twelve terms already match near the centre; the far tails converge more slowly. A noise voltage is the sum of the tiny contributions of an enormous number of electrons, so its distribution is Gaussian to high accuracy. A linear filter forms weighted sums of Gaussian samples, so filtered Gaussian noise stays Gaussian, and only its mean and autocorrelation (or PSD) are needed.
  7. Limits of the model: impulsive noise (ignition, lightning) and burst noise are dominated by a few large events, so the CLT does not apply and their tails are far heavier; strong interference from one transmitter is a single term, not a sum. Error-rate formulas built on AWGN are then optimistic.
  8. Why jointly Gaussian variables are special: their joint PDF with correlation coefficient ρ is
    fXY(x, y) = exp{−[x²/σx² − 2ρxy/(σxσy) + y²/σy²] / (2(1 − ρ²))} / (2πσxσy√(1 − ρ²))
ρ = 0:  fXY = [e−x²/2σx²/(σx√(2π))] · [e−y²/2σy²/(σy√(2π))] = fX(x) fY(y)
  9. This is what lets a receiver treat uncorrelated noise samples, such as nI and nQ at one instant, as independent.
  10. Using the model: a 1 kΩ resistor at 290 K over 1 MHz has σ = 4.0 µV rms. The Gaussian model then predicts that the noise exceeds 12 µV (3σ) in magnitude a fraction 2Q(3) = 0.0027 of the time, about 1 sample in 370.
  11. Check: 2Q(2) = 2 × 0.0228 = 0.0455 and 2Q(3) = 2 × 0.00135 = 0.0027, the Gaussian column above.

Glossary

Antenna temperature
The temperature of a matched resistor that would give the same noise power as the antenna delivers.
Autocorrelation
RX(τ) = E[X(t + τ)X(t)] for a WSS process; RX(0) is the average power.
AWGN
Additive white Gaussian noise: added to the signal, flat in frequency, Gaussian in amplitude.
Bandwidth
The width in hertz of the band a signal occupies or a filter passes; always name the definition (first-null, 3-dB, null-to-null, noise).
Baseband signal
A signal whose spectrum starts at or near 0 Hz and ends at some highest frequency B.
Burst noise
Random switching of a device current between two levels, caused by defects; also called popcorn noise.
Ergodic process
A stationary process whose time averages over one long sample function equal its ensemble averages.
Expectation
The probability-weighted average of a random variable, E[X] = ∫ x fX(x) dx.
Flicker noise
Low-frequency device noise whose PSD rises as 1/f below a corner frequency.
Frequency division multiplexing
Sharing one channel by giving each message its own carrier and frequency slot.
Friis formula
The cascade relation for noise: F = F1 + (F2 − 1)/G1 + (F3 − 1)/(G1G2) + ….
Gaussian random variable
A variable with PDF e−(x−μ)²/2σ²/(σ√(2π)), fixed by its mean μ and variance σ².
Group delay
τg = −(1/2π)dθ/df, the delay of the envelope of a narrow band of frequencies.
In-phase component
xI(t), the low-pass part of a band-pass signal that multiplies cos(2πfct).
Modulation
Varying the amplitude, frequency or phase of a carrier in step with a message signal.
Narrowband noise
Noise after a band-pass filter, written nIcos(2πfct) − nQsin(2πfct).
Noise equivalent bandwidth
The width of an ideal filter with the same peak gain that passes the same white-noise power as the real filter.
Passband signal
A signal whose spectrum is concentrated around a carrier frequency fc far above 0 Hz.
Power spectral density
SX(f), the power per hertz of a random process; the Fourier transform of RX(τ).
Pre-envelope
Another name for the analytic signal x(t) + j x̂(t), which has only positive frequencies.
Quadrature component
xQ(t), the low-pass part of a band-pass signal that multiplies −sin(2πfct).
Rayleigh distribution
The PDF (r/σ²)e−r²/2σ² of the envelope of narrowband Gaussian noise; mean 1.253σ.
Rayleigh energy theorem
Parseval's theorem for energy signals: ∫|x(t)|²dt = ∫|X(f)|²df.
Reference temperature
T0 = 290 K, the standard source temperature for noise figure and noise temperature.
Shot noise
Noise from the random crossing of a junction by charge carriers; mean-square current 2qIB.
Signal-to-noise ratio
Signal power over noise power at a stated point and in the same bandwidth, as a ratio or in dB.
sinc function
sinc(u) = sin(πu)/(πu), with sinc(0) = 1 and zeros at nonzero integers; the spectrum shape of a rectangular pulse.
Thermal noise
Johnson noise from random electron motion in a resistance; available power kTB, two-sided PSD kT/2.
Transducer
A device that converts one form of energy to another, such as a microphone turning sound into voltage.
Variance
σ² = E[X²] − (E[X])², the mean-square spread about the mean; σ is the standard deviation.
