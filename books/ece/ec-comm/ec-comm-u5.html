Analog and Digital Communication Unit 5 · Baseband Data Transmission and Detection

Baseband data transmission means sending a stream of bits as pulses through a cable or any low-pass channel, and getting the same bits back at the far end. This unit explains why fast pulses spill into their neighbours, how to shape and equalise them, how to picture signals as points in space, and how a matched filter with a nearest-point decision gives the smallest possible error rate. After it you can compute the Nyquist bandwidth of a link, design a raised cosine or duobinary scheme, and write the probability of error for binary and M-ary PAM.

5.1 Intersymbol interference and Nyquist criterion

Send symbols fast enough and each pulse is still ringing when the next one is sampled. The tail of every other symbol lands on this instant as intersymbol interference, or ISI, and it pushes the sample towards the wrong decision. Nyquist's criterion states exactly when the spill is zero: the overall pulse must cross zero at every other sampling instant. Every baseband modem, Ethernet PHY and DSL link is designed around this one condition.

You need: the Fourier transform and power spectral density (see Unit 1, Foundations), and the symbol period, PAM and line codes (see Unit 4, Sampling and Pulse Modulation).

The model: one pulse per symbol

A baseband link sends one symbol every T seconds. T is the symbol period, in seconds. The k-th symbol ak is one of M real levels: for binary, ±1; for 4-level PAM, ±1 and ±3. The symbol rate is Rs = 1/T baud, where one baud is one symbol per second. Each symbol carries log₂M bits, so the bit rate is Rb = Rs log₂M bit/s.

Three blocks shape the pulse. The transmit filter gT(t) turns each symbol into a waveform. The channel c(t) is the cable, fibre or radio path. The receive filter gR(t) limits noise before the sampler. Their cascade is one overall pulse p(t) = gT(t) * c(t) * gR(t), where * means convolution and p(t) is the shape a single symbol leaves at the sampler.

r(t) = Σ<sub>n</sub> a<sub>n</sub> p(t − nT) + n(t)

r(t) is the received signal before sampling; an is the n-th symbol level; p(t) is the overall pulse; T is the symbol period in seconds; n(t) is additive noise.

y(kT) = a<sub>k</sub> p(0) + Σ<sub>n≠k</sub> a<sub>n</sub> p((k − n)T) + n(kT)

y(kT) is the sample taken at t = kT; ak p(0) is the wanted term, p(0) being the peak height; the middle sum is the intersymbol interference (ISI); n(kT) is the noise sample.

The middle sum is the damage: tails of all other symbols landing on this instant. Peak distortion D = (1/p(0)) Σn≠k |p(nT)| is the worst-case ISI as a fraction of the wanted amplitude. D = 0.25 means ISI can reach a quarter of the signal, shrinking the margin to the decision threshold by that much.

Nyquist's first criterion

Kill every term of the ISI sum: require p(nT) = 0 for every integer n ≠ 0. This is Nyquist's first criterion, also called the zero-ISI condition. The overall pulse must pass through zero at every sampling instant except its own.

The pulse that meets it exactly is the sinc: p(t) = sinc(t/T) = sin(πt/T)/(πt/T), with p(0) = 1. The numerator vanishes whenever t/T is a nonzero integer, so all zeros fall on the neighbouring sampling instants. The figure shows two such pulses one period apart and their sum.

The criterion is a condition on p(t), the whole chain. A clean transmit filter with a distorting channel still gives ISI, which is why an equaliser at the receiver is often needed.

The sinc spectrum is a rectangle of width 1/(2T) = Rs/2 Hz on each side of zero. Zero ISI therefore needs at least W = Rs/2 Hz, the Nyquist bandwidth. For binary, Rs = Rb, so the ceiling is 2 bit/s/Hz; with M levels it becomes 2 log₂M bit/s/Hz.

Two catches. The sinc stretches from −∞ to +∞, so it is non-causal and cannot be built. Its tails decay only as 1/t, so truncating it, or sampling slightly off the ideal instant, breaks the zeros and ISI returns. The next chapter shows the raised cosine pulse that pays a little extra bandwidth for fast-decaying tails.

The bandwidth floor in numbers

The Nyquist bandwidth is a floor, not a target. A binary link at 2400 bit/s cannot fit in less than 1200 Hz of baseband without ISI, because 2400 symbols per second each need to be resolved and the sinc that does it occupies Rs/2 = 1200 Hz. Real designs spend 10% to 30% extra bandwidth on a raised cosine roll-off, so a practical 2400 bit/s link uses roughly 1320 Hz to 1560 Hz.

Raising M buys bit rate inside the same band. 4-level PAM at 2400 baud carries 4800 bit/s in the same 1200 Hz floor, that is 4 bit/s/Hz. The price is that four levels sit closer together, so the same noise causes more errors; the error probability of 5.6 depends on that spacing.

  • The criterion does not demand a sinc; any p(t) with those zeros works.
  • It removes ISI only. Noise, crosstalk and interference remain.
  • It fixes where to sample, not when the clock actually arrives; timing error shifts the zeros away from the sampling instants.

Exam traps

  • Using Rs where Rs/2 belongs. The minimum baseband bandwidth is half the symbol rate, so 2400 baud needs 1200 Hz.
  • Mixing bit rate with symbol rate. Rb = Rs log₂M, so 2 bit/s/Hz holds for binary only; M-level PAM reaches 2 log₂M bit/s/Hz.
  • Blaming the transmit filter for ISI. The zeros are set by the overall response gT * c * gR, and a bad channel alone can create ISI.
  • Sign slips in the ISI sum. ISI adds algebraically with the sign of each neighbour, so a positive neighbour on a −1 symbol pulls the sample up towards the threshold.
  • Assuming the sinc can be built. It is non-causal and infinite; truncating it or mis-timing the sampler brings the ISI back.

5.2 Pulse shaping, raised cosine spectrum and eye pattern

The sinc pulse satisfies Nyquist's zero-ISI condition, but its spectrum is a perfect rectangle. A rectangle cannot be built, and its slowly decaying 1/t tails turn a small timing error into interference at the next sample. The raised cosine pulse rounds the corners of that rectangle with a cosine roll-off controlled by one number, α, between 0 and 1. It pays a known extra bandwidth and gets tails that die as 1/t³. The eye pattern is the oscilloscope picture that shows whether the shaped pulse is still clean where the sampler fires. DSL, cable modems and every mobile baseband use this daily.

You need: 5.1 for the zero-ISI condition, the sinc pulse, and the meaning of symbol rate R_s and symbol period T.

From a brick-wall spectrum to a cosine roll-off

In 5.1 the fastest pulse with zero ISI is the sinc, and its spectrum is a perfect rectangle: flat up to R_s/2 hertz, then nothing. A perfect rectangle needs an infinite, non-causal impulse response. No circuit builds it, and a real cable would chop its corners anyway.

Nyquist's criterion allows the corners to be rounded, provided the transition band is odd-symmetric about R_s/2. The raised cosine spectrum does exactly that. It is flat from 0 to (1 − α)R_s/2, then falls smoothly, like half a cosine, to zero at (1 + α)R_s/2.

B = (R_s/2)(1 + α)

B is the baseband bandwidth in hertz, measured from zero to the highest frequency used; R_s is the symbol rate in baud, equal to 1/T, with T the symbol period in seconds; α is the roll-off factor, also called the excess bandwidth factor, with 0 ≤ α ≤ 1.

excess bandwidth = B − R_s/2 = αR_s/2

the extra hertz spent above the Nyquist minimum, in hertz; α and R_s as above.

Two extremes frame the choice. α = 0 returns the sinc and the Nyquist minimum B = R_s/2. α = 1 gives B = R_s, double the minimum, with the smoothest and most buildable spectrum. Practical links sit between: a DSL line on α = 0.25 pays 25 % extra bandwidth above the Nyquist minimum and buys a filter that can actually be manufactured.

The pulse in time, and the root raised cosine pair

p(t) = sinc(t/T) · cos(παt/T) / (1 − (2αt/T)²)

p(t) is the pulse amplitude at time t seconds; T is the symbol period in seconds; α is the roll-off factor; sinc(x) means sin(πx)/(πx).

At t = 0 the pulse is 1. At t = kT (k ≠ 0), p(kT) = 0; where the denominator also vanishes, take the limit. At t = ±T/(2α) the ratio cos(παt/T)/(1 − (2αt/T)²) tends to π/4, so p = (π/4) sinc(1/(2α)).

The tails decide practical success. A sinc falls off as 1/t. A raised cosine with α > 0 falls off as 1/t³. Sample 10 % of a symbol late and the raised cosine contributes almost nothing, while the sinc still contributes visibly.

The pulse the sampler sees is the cascade of transmit filter, channel and receive filter. Matched-filter theory (see 5.5) says the receive filter should match the incoming pulse to maximise signal-to-noise ratio at the sampling instant. Both demands are met by splitting the raised cosine: put the square root of its spectrum at the transmitter and the same square root at the receiver. Each is a root raised cosine (RRC) filter. Two RRC filters in cascade give exactly the raised cosine, so zero ISI plus matched filtering for free. An RRC filter alone is not ISI-free; only the pair is.

Reading the eye pattern

Pulse shaping is judged on an oscilloscope. The eye pattern, also called the eye diagram, is made by triggering the display on the symbol clock and overlaying the received waveform for two symbol periods, over thousands of random sequences. The traces pile up into a shape with a clear opening, like an eye.

  • Vertical opening: the gap between the worst-case top and bottom traces at the sampling instant. It is the noise margin; noise beyond it causes errors.
  • Horizontal opening: how far the sampling clock may drift, in time, and still hit an open eye. It is the tolerance to timing jitter.
  • Slope at the zero crossing: a steep crossing gives a wide horizontal opening, so the eye tolerates more timing jitter. A gradual crossing narrows the opening and makes the eye timing-sensitive.
  • A closing eye means growing ISI — from a channel that cuts high frequencies, a wrong α, or a clock firing at the wrong instant.

The sampler must fire in the middle of the horizontal opening, where the vertical opening is widest. An eye monitor keeps checking that instant. Raising α shortens the tails, so the eye stays open over a wider range of sampling phase, at the price of bandwidth.

Worked example

Question. A binary baseband link carries 4800 bit/s through a raised cosine system with roll-off α = 0.5. Find the symbol rate, the symbol period, the Nyquist minimum bandwidth, the actual bandwidth and the excess bandwidth. State what α = 1 would cost on the same link.

  1. The data is binary, so one bit rides on each symbol: R_s = R_b = 4800 baud.
  2. Symbol period T = 1/R_s = 1/4800 s = 208.3 µs.
  3. Nyquist minimum bandwidth = R_s/2 = 4800/2 = 2400 Hz.
  4. Actual bandwidth B = (R_s/2)(1 + α) = 2400 × (1 + 0.5) = 2400 × 1.5 = 3600 Hz.
  5. Excess bandwidth = B − R_s/2 = 3600 − 2400 = 1200 Hz. Check: αR_s/2 = 0.5 × 4800/2 = 1200 Hz, same value.
  6. With α = 1, B = 2400 × 2 = 4800 Hz, that is 1200 Hz more than the α = 0.5 design.

Answer. R_s = 4800 baud, T = 208.3 µs, minimum bandwidth 2400 Hz, actual bandwidth 3600 Hz, excess bandwidth 1200 Hz; α = 1 would need 4800 Hz.

Exam traps

  • Choosing α = 0 because it is the narrowest. The sinc cannot be built and its 1/t tails flood the sample under a small timing error. Practical links use α near 0.2 to 0.5, and exams want that justification, not a minimum.
  • Dropping the half: writing B = R_s(1 + α) or excess = αR_s. Correct forms are B = (R_s/2)(1 + α) and excess = αR_s/2.
  • Expecting one root raised cosine filter to give zero ISI. Only the cascade of transmit and receive RRC gives the raised cosine; each alone does not.
  • Reading the eye at its widest point on the picture instead of at the instant the sampler actually fires, and forgetting that the eye is taken after the receive filter, not at the channel output.

5.3 Equalization: zero-forcing and adaptive equalizers

An equaliser is a receiver filter that cancels the intersymbol interference left by a non-ideal channel. It stores recent samples on a tapped delay line, multiplies them by adjustable weights and adds them, so that at each symbol instant the wanted symbol appears with little or no spill from its neighbours. The zero-forcing equaliser inverts the channel exactly. The adaptive equaliser learns the weights from a known training sequence and then from its own decisions. These filters appear in DSL, cable modems, Ethernet and mobile receivers.

You need: 5.1 and 5.2 for the sampled overall response and the zero-ISI target.

The equaliser undoes the channel at sampling instants

A real channel does not deliver a pulse in one symbol slot. A pulse sent at time 0 arrives as a waveform h(t) that leaks across several symbol periods T. At the sampling instants kT the channel is described by its samples hk. The sample at time nT contains the wanted term plus tails from earlier symbols. That spill is intersymbol interference, ISI. <a href="#5.1">Section 5.1 gives the Nyquist condition for zero ISI when the transmitter pulse can be designed freely. A cable or radio channel adds its own hk, so the receiver must repair the damage.

A linear transversal equaliser is a tapped delay line with tap spacing T, weights c−N to cN and one adder. Its input is the received sample sequence x(n). Its output is y(n) = Σk=−NN ck x(n−k). Here ck is the weight on tap k, x(n−k) is the input stored for k symbol periods, and 2N+1 is the number of taps. The equaliser sits after the receive filter and before the threshold device.

The useful measure is the combined sampled response. If hm is the channel sample at mT, then vm = Σk ck hm−k. The overall response vm is the height at mT of one symbol after channel and equaliser. Zero ISI needs v0 = 1 and vm = 0 for every m ≠ 0. The taps are chosen to make this true as closely as the available taps allow.

Zero-forcing: invert the channel

The zero-forcing equaliser, also called the inverse filter, sets the weights so that v0 = 1 and vm = 0 for all m ≠ 0. In the frequency domain the channel is H(f) and the equaliser is C(f). The cascade must be flat, so C(f)H(f) = 1 and C(f) = 1/H(f). The zero-forcing equaliser is the inverse of the channel response.

With a finite number of taps the equaliser cannot null every tail. A common choice uses 2N+1 taps and solves 2N+1 linear equations: Σk=−NN ck hm−k = 1 for m = 0, and 0 for m = ±1, …, ±N. In matrix form H c = δ. Here c is the column vector of tap weights, H is the matrix built from the channel samples hm, and δ is a column vector with 1 in the centre and 0 elsewhere. Solve c = H−1 δ by matrix inversion. An iterative zero-forcing algorithm does the same job without inversion: it adjusts one tap at a time to null one postcursor, then repeats.

The price is noise enhancement. Where |H(f)| is small, |C(f)| = 1/|H(f)| is large, so noise at those frequencies is amplified along with the signal. At a spectral null, H(f) = 0 and the zero-forcing filter would need infinite gain, so it cannot be built. The minimum mean-square error equaliser is the standard cure: it minimises the combined power of ISI and noise instead of forcing ISI to zero.

Adaptive equaliser: learn weights with LMS

A fixed zero-forcing filter needs the channel response H(f). In a telephone line, a radio link or a cable modem the channel is unknown at start-up and drifts with temperature and ageing. An adaptive equaliser starts with arbitrary weights and updates them from the received data. The usual rule is the least mean square, LMS, algorithm: ck(n+1) = ck(n) + μ e(n) x(n−k).

Here ck(n) is the weight on tap k at iteration n, μ is the step size (a small positive number), e(n) is the error, x(n−k) is the stored input sample on that tap, and ck(n+1) is the new weight. The error is e(n) = d(n) − y(n). Here y(n) = Σk ck x(n−k) is the equaliser output sample at the decision instant, before the threshold. In training mode d(n) is the known training symbol. In decision-directed mode d(n) = â(n), the slicer output, so e(n) = â(n) − y(n). The update moves each weight in the direction that reduces the squared error.

Training mode sends a known preamble. The receiver knows d(n), computes the error and adapts. After the preamble, decision-directed mode uses the receiver's own decision as d(n). This works when decisions are mostly right; a burst of errors can push the weights the wrong way. The step size is a trade-off. A large μ converges in fewer symbols but the weights jitter around the optimum. A small μ gives a steady setting but takes longer to converge.

Worked example

Question. A channel has sampled response h0 = 1, h1 = 0.5, h2 = 0.25 and hm = 0 for all other m. Design a two-tap zero-forcing equaliser with weights c0 and c1. Find the overall sampled response vm for m = 0, 1, 2, 3 and state the residual ISI.

  1. For a two-tap equaliser y(n) = c0x(n) + c1x(n−1), where the second tap is delayed by one symbol period T. The overall response is vm = c0hm + c1hm−1, with hm = 0 outside m = 0, 1, 2.
  2. Set v0 = 1. v0 = c0h0 = c0 × 1 = c0, so c0 = 1.
  3. Set v1 = 0. v1 = c0h1 + c1h0 = 1 × 0.5 + c1 × 1 = 0.5 + c1, so c1 = −0.5.
  4. Check v2. v2 = c0h2 + c1h1 = 1 × 0.25 + (−0.5) × 0.5 = 0.25 − 0.25 = 0.
  5. Check v3. v3 = c0h3 + c1h2 = 1 × 0 + (−0.5) × 0.25 = −0.125.
  6. For m > 3 all terms are zero because hm = 0. The only non-zero unwanted sample is v3 = −0.125, so the residual ISI is −0.125.

Answer. The two-tap zero-forcing equaliser is c0 = 1 and c1 = −0.5. The overall response is v0 = 1, v1 = 0, v2 = 0 and v3 = −0.125, leaving a residual ISI of −0.125 at 3T.

Exam traps

  • Zero-forcing cancels ISI, not noise. Near a null in |H(f)| its gain is huge, so the noise at the decision point grows. It is not the low-SNR optimum.
  • Do not mix hk and ck. hk is the channel sample; ck is the equaliser tap. The combined response is the convolution vm = Σ ckhm−k.
  • In LMS the error is d(n) − y(n), not y(n) − d(n), where y(n) is the equaliser output before the slicer. In decision-directed mode d(n) = â(n), so e(n) = â(n) − y(n). A sign flip makes the weights move away from the optimum and the filter diverges.
  • More taps do not automatically mean a better zero-forcing receiver. More taps can null more ISI but also raise the noise gain and need a larger matrix inversion.

5.4 Signal space representation and Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization

A digital modulator picks one of M waveforms, each lasting T seconds. Carrying M functions is clumsy. Instead each waveform is drawn as a point. Two signals are compared by an inner product, an integral over one symbol. The M points live in at most M dimensions, and Gram-Schmidt builds the axes. Energy becomes squared length, distance becomes separation, and the matched filter and error probability (see 5.5 and 5.6) are geometry on that picture.

You need orthogonality of sinusoids and the Fourier transform from Unit 1, Foundations: Signals, Noise and Random Processes.

Inner product, norm and coordinates

Two real signals lasting 0 ≤ t < T are compared by an inner product: multiply and integrate.

⟨x, y⟩ = ∫₀ᵀ x(t) y(t) dt

⟨x,y⟩ is the inner product; T is symbol duration in seconds; x,y in volts give V²·s (or V²·ms).

The norm is length; its square is energy.

‖x‖ = √⟨x, x⟩ = √E_x

‖x‖ is norm in V·√s; E_x = ∫₀ᵀ x²(t) dt is energy in V²·s, the energy a 1 Ω resistor would absorb.

An orthonormal basis has unit-norm, mutually orthogonal axes φᵢ.

⟨φᵢ, φⱼ⟩ = 1 if i = j, and 0 if i ≠ j

φᵢ,φⱼ are axes; each φᵢ carries 1/√s when signals are in volts, so its energy is the pure number 1.

With N axes, a signal is a weighted sum; each weight is an inner product.

s_m(t) = Σᵢ₌₁ᴺ s_mi φᵢ(t), s_mi = ⟨s_m, φᵢ⟩

s_m(t) is the mth signal; s_mi is its ith coordinate in V·√s; N is the number of axes.

Energy is the sum of squared coordinates. Squared distance is the sum of squared coordinate differences.

E_m = Σᵢ s_mi², d²(m, n) = Σᵢ (s_mi − s_ni)²

E_m is energy in V²·s; d(m,n) is distance in V·√s; s_mi,s_ni are coordinates. Distance is not E_m − E_n.

Gram-Schmidt: building the axes

Gram-Schmidt takes signals one at a time and removes the part already explained by existing axes.

  1. First signal: if ‖s₁‖ ≠ 0, φ₁ = s₁/‖s₁‖.
  2. Next signal s_m: cᵢ = ⟨s_m, φᵢ⟩ on every existing axis.
  3. Residual: g_m = s_m − Σᵢ cᵢ φᵢ.
  4. If ‖g_m‖ = 0, s_m is already explained; skip it.
  5. If ‖g_m‖ ≠ 0, add φ_new = g_m/‖g_m‖ and increase N.
  6. Repeat for m = 2, 3, …, M. The final dimension count obeys N ≤ M.

Check orthogonality: ⟨g_m, φ_k⟩ = ⟨s_m, φ_k⟩ − Σᵢ cᵢ⟨φᵢ, φ_k⟩ = c_k − c_k = 0. All φ_k content was subtracted; nothing across it changed.

Signal order changes which axes appear, not energies or distances between points.

Cauchy-Schwarz and the standard pictures

No inner product exceeds the product of the norms. This is Cauchy-Schwarz.

|⟨x, y⟩| ≤ ‖x‖ ‖y‖

|⟨x,y⟩| is absolute inner product; ‖x‖,‖y‖ are norms. Equality holds only when y = c x for a constant c.

For received r, the filter sample is ⟨r, s⟩ = ⟨s, s⟩ + ⟨n, s⟩. For a fixed received energy and a fixed reference energy ‖s‖², ⟨r, s⟩ is largest when s has exactly the incoming shape; the largest value is ‖r‖‖s‖. No filter beats it; see 5.5.

PAM uses one shape p(t): φ₁ = p/‖p‖, and level A_m becomes the scalar A_m‖p‖. BPSK has two points on one line, +√E and −√E, separated by 2√E.

QPSK needs cosine and sine axes at the same carrier frequency. Its four points lie on a circle of radius √E at π/4, 3π/4, 5π/4, 7π/4. Neighbours are √(2E) apart; opposites are 2√E apart. Passband constellations belong to Unit 6.

Worked example

Question. Over 0 ≤ t < T with T = 1 ms, s₁(t) = 1 V for the whole interval and s₂(t) = 2 V for the first half and 0 V for the second half. Work in units of V·√ms. (a) Find φ₁ and φ₂ by Gram-Schmidt. (b) Give the coordinates of s₁ and s₂. (c) Find both energies and the distance between the points. (d) Check Cauchy-Schwarz for the pair.

  4. ‖g₂‖² = 1²×0.5 + (−1)²×0.5 = 1 V²·ms, so ‖g₂‖ = 1 V·√ms. Non-zero: φ₂ = g₂/‖g₂‖ = +1/√ms over the first half and −1/√ms over the second.
  5. Coordinates: s₁ = (1, 0) V·√ms; s₂ = (1, 1) V·√ms.

  7. Distance: d² = (1 − 1)² + (0 − 1)² = 1, so d = 1 V·√ms.

Exam traps

  • Using N = M in the error formula. Dependent signals are skipped, so N can be smaller than the number of signals; N ≤ M, not equal to M.
  • Confusing energy and squared distance. Energy is Σ s_mi² from the origin; squared distance is Σ (s_mi − s_ni)². It is never E_m − E_n.
  • Forgetting normalisation. Using g_m as an axis instead of g_m/‖g_m‖ makes every later coordinate, energy and distance wrong.
  • Dropping units. Inner products carry V²·s, norms and coordinates carry V·√s, and axes carry 1/√s. Unitless answers lose marks.
  • Believing the basis is unique. A different signal order gives different axes and hence different coordinates; only the energies and the distances between points stay the same.

5.5 Matched filter and correlation receiver

The receiver must decide which known pulse arrived while noise blurs it. At the sampling instant t = T, one filter gives the largest signal-to-noise ratio: the matched filter, whose impulse response is the pulse reversed in time and delayed by T. A multiplier followed by an integrator produces the same sample and is called the correlation receiver. Digital modems, radar and optical receivers use this idea to squeeze the best error rate from a given pulse energy.

You need: 5.4 for the inner product and Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, and Unit 1 for white noise and power spectral density.

The filter that maximises the sample

A known pulse s(t) lasts T seconds and has energy E = ∫₀ᵀ s²(t) dt. It arrives as r(t) = s(t) + n(t), where n(t) is white Gaussian noise with two-sided power spectral density N₀/2 W/Hz. Pass r(t) through a linear filter h(t), and sample the output at t = T. The signal part at that instant is ys(T) = ∫₀ᵀ h(τ)s(T − τ)dτ. The output noise variance is σ² = (N₀/2)∫₀ᵀ h²(τ)dτ. We want the largest possible ratio ys²(T)/σ².

SNR(T) = [∫₀ᵀ h(τ)s(T − τ)dτ]² ÷ [(N₀/2)∫₀ᵀ h²(τ)dτ] ≤ 2E/N₀

h(τ) is the filter impulse response; s(t) is the known pulse; T is the pulse duration in seconds; E = ∫₀ᵀ s²(t)dt is the pulse energy in joules; N₀ is the one-sided noise density in W/Hz, so N₀/2 is the two-sided density; SNR(T) is the signal-to-noise ratio at the sampling instant.

Cauchy-Schwarz gives the upper bound. Equality needs h(τ) proportional to s(T − τ). Choose gain 1: h(t) = s(T − t). Its transfer function is H(f) = S*(f)e^(−j2πfT). This is the matched filter, also called the optimum filter for white noise. At t = T the signal output is E, the noise variance is N₀E/2, and the peak SNR is 2E/N₀ = E/(N₀/2).

The output for the signal alone is the autocorrelation Rs(t − T) = ∫ s(u)s(u + t − T)du, shifted so its peak sits at t = T. The filter folds the pulse about T, so every part of the pulse adds in phase at the sampling instant. Its output at other times is not optimum; an early or late clock loses SNR.

Correlation receiver gives the same sample

Multiply r(t) by a stored copy of s(t) and integrate from 0 to T. The result at the dump instant is z(T) = ∫₀ᵀ r(t)s(t)dt = E + ∫₀ᵀ n(t)s(t)dt. The noise term has zero mean and variance (N₀/2)∫₀ᵀs²(t)dt = N₀E/2. The signal sample is E, exactly as with the matched filter. The multiplier, integrator and dump form the correlation receiver, also called the correlation detector; the dump resets the integrator for the next pulse.

z(T) = ∫₀ᵀ r(t)s(t)dt = E + n<sub>s</sub>,   Var(n<sub>s</sub>) = N₀E/2

z(T) is the correlator output at time T; r(t) is the received signal; s(t) is the stored pulse; E is the pulse energy in joules; ns is the noise component at the sample; N₀ is the one-sided noise density in W/Hz.

Equivalence is one change of variable. The matched filter output at T is y(T) = ∫ h(τ)r(T − τ)dτ = ∫₀ᵀ s(u)r(u)du = z(T). The two receivers make the same decision. The matched filter is convenient in hardware because it is a fixed filter; the correlator is convenient in analysis because it is an inner product, the same picture as 5.4.

Reading the result correctly

  • The maximum SNR depends only on E and N₀, not on the pulse shape. For fixed energy in white noise, shape changes bandwidth and implementation, not the bound.
  • The factor 2 belongs there. With two-sided density N₀/2, the bound is E/(N₀/2) = 2E/N₀. Reporting E/N₀ is 3 dB low.
  • The bound is reached only at t = T. Sampling early or late, or using the filter output between samples, is suboptimal.
  • The matched filter maximises SNR; it does not remove intersymbol interference. Zero-ISI pulse shaping and equalisation are separate jobs (see 5.1 and 5.3).
  • For coloured noise, whiten the noise first; the plain matched filter is optimum only for white noise.

The next step, comparing this sample with a threshold to choose a symbol, is in 5.6.

  1. For a rectangular pulse, s(t) = A for 0 ≤ t ≤ T, so E = A²T = (1 V)² × (1 × 10⁻³ s) = 1 × 10⁻³ J = 1 mJ. The unit check is V²·s = W·s = J.

5.6 Optimum detection and probability of error in AWGN

In white Gaussian noise, the best receiver does not guess. It treats the received sample as a point near one of the allowed signal points and chooses the closest one. This chapter derives that maximum-likelihood rule, draws the decision regions, and gives the error probability in terms of distance and N₀. The same formulas size a UPI link, a Wi-Fi hop or a telecom backhaul: how much signal energy per bit buys how few bit errors. The answer depends only on the spacing of the signal points and on the noise density.

You need: 5.5 for the matched-filter sample and its noise variance, and Unit 1 for the Gaussian density and AWGN.

Received vector and maximum-likelihood rule

After the correlator of 5.5, the receiver holds a vector r. Under hypothesis m, r = s_m + n. Here s_m is the m-th signal vector from 5.4, with energy E_m = ||s_m||², and n is the noise vector. For additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN), the components of n are independent Gaussian random variables with mean 0 and variance N₀/2 per dimension. N₀ is the one-sided noise power spectral density in W/Hz.

The maximum-likelihood (ML) rule chooses the m that makes r most likely. For Gaussian noise, the density p(r|s_m) has exponent −||r−s_m||²/N₀. Expanding gives ||r−s_m||² = ||r||² − 2⟨r,s_m⟩ + E_m. The terms ||r||² and 1/N₀ are common, so the rule is: choose m maximizing ⟨r,s_m⟩ − E_m/2.

m̂ = arg max_m (⟨r, s_m⟩ − E_m/2)

m̂ is the decided index; r is the received vector; s_m is the m-th signal vector; E_m = ||s_m||² is its energy; ⟨ , ⟩ is the inner product.

When all E_m are equal, the −E_m/2 term is common. The rule becomes: choose the signal vector with the largest inner product with r. That is the same as choosing the nearest point, because ||r−s_m||² = ||r||² − 2⟨r,s_m⟩ + E. So for equal energies ML is the minimum-distance rule.

Decision regions and two-point error

The decision region of s_m is the set of r for which the receiver decides m. For equal-energy points these regions are the Voronoi cells: every r goes to its nearest point. Two neighbouring regions meet on a line in 2-D or a hyperplane in higher dimensions. That boundary is the perpendicular bisector of the line joining the two points. On the boundary the two distances are equal, so either decision is equally likely.

Q(x) = (1/√(2π)) ∫ₓ^∞ e^(−u²/2) du

Q(x) is the Gaussian tail probability; x is a real number; u is the integration variable. Q(0)=0.5, Q(−x)=1−Q(x), and Q(x) decreases as x increases.

For two equally likely points at distance d and equal energy, an error happens when noise pushes r across the boundary. The component of noise along the line joining the points decides the side. That component has variance N₀/2, so the error probability is Q(d/√(2N₀)).

P_e = Q(d/√(2N₀))

d is the distance between the two points; N₀ is the one-sided noise PSD in W/Hz; P_e is the probability of choosing the wrong point.

For antipodal binary signalling the two points are +√E_b and −√E_b on one axis. Their distance is d = 2√E_b. Substituting gives P_e = Q(√(2E_b/N₀)) = ½ erfc(√(E_b/N₀)). E_b is the energy per bit in joules; N₀ is in W/Hz, so E_b/N₀ is dimensionless. erfc is the complementary error function. This is the matched-filter bit error rate for BPSK on AWGN; Unit 6 uses it for passband BPSK.

M-PAM error and the union bound

For M-PAM the signal points sit on a line with equal spacing. Let E_s be the average symbol energy. The spacing a between adjacent levels satisfies E_s = (M²−1)a²/12. An inner point is mistaken when noise crosses either of its two boundaries. The two end points have only one boundary. Weighting by the fraction of points gives the symbol error probability.

P_e = (2(M−1)/M) Q(√(6E_s/((M²−1)N₀)))

M is the number of PAM levels; E_s is the average symbol energy in joules; N₀ is the one-sided noise PSD in W/Hz; P_e is the symbol error probability.

For M=2 this reduces to Q(√(2E_s/N₀)), the antipodal binary result with E_s=E_b. For large M, P_e grows because the levels are closer for the same average energy. A union bound handles general M-ary sets: P_e ≤ Σ_{m'≠m} Q(d_{mm'}/√(2N₀)), where d_{mm'} is the distance from the sent point m to another point m'. It is simple but loose when many points are close.

Worked example

Question. Binary antipodal signalling (BPSK) uses E_b/N₀ = 10 in linear value, that is 10 dB. (a) Find the bit error probability. (b) Find the E_b/N₀ in dB needed for P_e = 10⁻⁵ and the saving in dB.

  1. For antipodal binary, P_e = Q(√(2E_b/N₀)).
  2. With E_b/N₀ = 10, 2E_b/N₀ = 20. So x = √20 = 4.472.
  3. Q(4.472) = 0.5 erfc(4.472/√2) = 0.5 erfc(3.162) ≈ 3.9×10⁻⁶. Check: Q(4.5) = 3.4×10⁻⁶, and Q falls as x rises.
  4. For P_e = 10⁻⁵ solve Q(x) = 10⁻⁵. By search, x ≈ 4.27.
  5. Then x² = 2E_b/N₀, so E_b/N₀ = x²/2 ≈ 18.2/2 = 9.1.
  6. In dB: 10 log₁₀(9.1) = 9.6 dB. Saving = 10 − 9.6 = 0.4 dB.

Answer. P_e ≈ 3.9×10⁻⁶ per bit; to reach 10⁻⁵ use E_b/N₀ ≈ 9.1 (9.6 dB), saving about 0.4 dB.

Exam traps

  • Swapping E_s and E_b and losing the 2 in the binary formula: antipodal binary gives Q(√(2E_b/N₀)), not Q(√(E_b/N₀)). A missing 2 is a silent 3 dB error in every number that follows.
  • Using Q(d/√N₀) instead of Q(d/√(2N₀)). The 2 comes from the fact that the noise variance along the decision axis is N₀/2, not N₀.
  • Using N₀ per dimension instead of N₀/2 per dimension in the received vector model. That doubles the noise variance and gives the wrong constant in every error formula.
  • Calling the ML rule a nearest-point rule for unequal energies. Nearest-point is correct only when all signal energies are equal; otherwise use ⟨r,s_m⟩ − E_m/2.
  • Plugging E_b/N₀ in dB directly into Q. The Q formula needs the linear ratio. Convert dB first with E_b/N₀ = 10^(dB/10).

5.7 Correlative coding and M-ary PAM

Correlative coding, also called partial response signalling, deliberately adds a small known intersymbol interference so that a binary stream can fit a realisable Nyquist filter. M-ary PAM takes the other route to higher speed: it sends log₂M bits on each symbol by using M amplitude levels. Duobinary is the standard example: cₖ = aₖ + aₖ₋₁ gives three levels at the Nyquist bandwidth. These ideas appear in wireline modems, digital subscriber lines, and high-speed serial links where the ideal sinc pulse cannot be built.

You need: 5.1 and 5.2 for Nyquist bandwidth and pulse shaping; 5.6 for the error probability formula extended to M levels.

Partial response signalling: duobinary

A Nyquist pulse with zero ISI needs a sinc spectrum, which is flat to R_s/2 and then zero. A real filter cannot make that sharp edge. Correlative coding accepts a small, known ISI instead. The simplest scheme is duobinary, or class I partial response. Its coder adds each symbol to the previous one: cₖ = aₖ + aₖ₋₁. If aₖ is 0 or 1, cₖ takes three values: 0, 1, 2. The symbol rate is still R_s, and the required bandwidth is R_s/2, so the bit rate per hertz is 2 bit/s/Hz. That equals the ideal binary Nyquist rate, now with a gradual, realisable filter.

A direct duobinary receiver must subtract the previous decision to recover aₖ. One wrong decision then corrupts every later bit. The precoder removes that memory. Before the coder, form pₖ = bₖ ⊕ pₖ₋₁, where bₖ is the input bit, pₖ is the precoded bit, and ⊕ is XOR. Start with p₀ = 0 (one bit of memory held in the precoder) before the first input bit b₁. The coder sends cₖ = pₖ + pₖ₋₁. The receiver decides the three levels and then applies b̂ₖ = cₖ mod 2. Because (pₖ + pₖ₋₁) mod 2 = pₖ ⊕ pₖ₋₁ = bₖ, the decode uses only the current level. A single level error gives one bit error, not a burst.

  1. Precode each input bit with one bit of memory: pₖ = bₖ ⊕ pₖ₋₁, starting from p₀ = 0.
  2. Add the precoded bit to its delayed copy: cₖ = pₖ + pₖ₋₁.
  3. Send the three-level symbols through the Nyquist channel.
  4. Slice at two thresholds, midway between 0 and 1 and between 1 and 2.
  5. Decode each decided level modulo 2: b̂ₖ = cₖ mod 2.

cₖ = pₖ + pₖ₋₁, b̂ₖ = cₖ mod 2

cₖ is the three-level channel symbol at time k; pₖ is the precoded binary symbol; b̂ₖ is the recovered bit; mod 2 gives the remainder after division by 2.

Modified duobinary and its cost

Duobinary has a DC component because its levels 0, 1, 2 are not symmetric. Modified duobinary uses cₖ = aₖ − aₖ₋₂. The delay is two symbols, and the subtraction gives levels −1, 0, 1 when aₖ is 0 or 1. The DC term cancels, which suits transformer-coupled lines and some magnetic recording channels. In delay-operator notation, duobinary is 1 + D and modified duobinary is 1 − D², where D means one symbol delay.

Duobinary gives up noise margin. Binary PAM has one threshold; duobinary has two thresholds and three levels. At the same average transmitted power its adjacent levels sit closer together than binary's, and its 1 + D receive filter also passes more noise than an ideal Nyquist filter. Its error probability is therefore much higher than ideal binary at the same power — far more than a factor of two at high SNR — a real SNR penalty. The gain is a realisable filter at bandwidth R_s/2, not extra bits per symbol. One input bit still produces one three-level symbol.

M-ary PAM

M-ary PAM sends log₂M bits on each symbol by using M amplitude levels. Binary PAM is M = 2; 4-PAM has four levels and sends two bits per symbol. The symbol rate falls for the same bit rate: R_s = R_b / log₂M. With a raised cosine filter of roll-off α, the bandwidth is B = (R_s/2)(1 + α). The receiver needs M − 1 decision thresholds, placed midway between adjacent levels.

Space the M levels as ±d, ±3d, ..., so the gap between neighbours is 2d. The average symbol energy is E_avg = (M² − 1)d²/3. For binary PAM this gives d², and for 4-PAM it gives 5d². If you keep the same gap d and raise M, the average symbol energy becomes (M² − 1)/3 times the binary value, so the power cost grows with the square of M.

Use Gray labelling: assign bit patterns so that neighbouring levels differ in only one bit. Then a noise spike that moves the decision to the next level usually causes one bit error, not several. The error probability for M-PAM still follows the nearest-neighbour idea from 5.6: it depends on the distance between adjacent levels and on N₀.

R_s = R_b / log₂M, B = (R_s/2)(1 + α)

R_s is symbol rate in baud; R_b is bit rate in bit/s; M is the number of PAM levels; α is the raised cosine roll-off, 0 ≤ α ≤ 1; B is the required bandwidth in Hz.

E_avg = (M² − 1)d²/3

E_avg is the average symbol energy; M is the number of levels; d is half the spacing between adjacent levels, with levels at ±d, ±3d, ...

Worked example

Question. A link must carry 9600 bit/s with a raised cosine roll-off α = 0.25. Compare binary PAM and 4-PAM: find the symbol rate, the 4-PAM symbol duration, the bandwidth, the number of decision thresholds, and the average-energy penalty for the same level spacing.

  1. Binary PAM: log₂2 = 1 bit per symbol, so R_s = 9600/1 = 9600 baud. Bandwidth B = (9600/2)(1 + 0.25) = 4800 × 1.25 = 6000 Hz. Thresholds = M − 1 = 2 − 1 = 1.
  2. 4-PAM: log₂4 = 2 bits per symbol, so R_s = 9600/2 = 4800 baud. Symbol duration T = 1/4800 = 208.33 µs. Bandwidth B = (4800/2)(1 + 0.25) = 2400 × 1.25 = 3000 Hz. Thresholds = M − 1 = 4 − 1 = 3.
  3. Average energy for the same spacing d: binary E_avg = (2² − 1)d²/3 = d². 4-PAM E_avg = (4² − 1)d²/3 = 15d²/3 = 5d². The ratio is 5.
  4. Power penalty in decibels: 10 log₁₀(5) = 6.99 dB, about 7 dB.

Answer. Binary PAM uses 9600 baud, 6000 Hz and one threshold; 4-PAM uses 4800 baud, 208.33 µs per symbol, 3000 Hz and three thresholds, costing 5 times (about 6.99 dB) average energy for the same spacing.

Exam traps

  • Running duobinary without a precoder: the receiver must subtract the previous decision, so one wrong three-level decision corrupts every later bit.
  • Decoding duobinary by taking cₖ mod 3 or by subtracting the previous level: with the precoder the correct decode is simply b̂ₖ = cₖ mod 2.
  • Thinking duobinary's three levels carry more bits per symbol: one binary input bit still produces one three-level symbol; the gain is a realisable Nyquist filter.
  • Using M thresholds for M-ary PAM: there are M − 1 thresholds. Also remember the power penalty (M² − 1)/3 for the same spacing, so lower bandwidth is not free.
  • Mixing duobinary 1 + D with modified duobinary 1 − D²: the minus sign removes the DC component and uses a two-symbol delay.

Takeaways

  1. Intersymbol interference is the spill of neighbouring pulses into the current sample; it is measured by the sampled values h(kT) of the overall pulse.
  2. Nyquist's criterion h(kT) = 0 for k ≠ 0 is the exact condition for zero ISI, and it fixes the minimum bandwidth at half the symbol rate.
  3. A raised-cosine pulse meets Nyquist's condition with a gradual, realisable spectrum, trading α·Rₛ/2 hertz of excess bandwidth for easier filters and a wider eye.
  4. The eye pattern is the practical test of pulse shaping: its opening measures noise margin, its width measures timing-jitter tolerance.
  5. A zero-forcing equalizer inverts the channel exactly but boosts noise where the channel is weak; an adaptive LMS equalizer trades a little residual ISI for stability and tracking.
  6. Gram-Schmidt shows that M signals of duration T live in at most M dimensions, so a receiver only needs those coordinates, not the whole waveform.
  7. The matched filter and the correlation receiver give the same sample; the matched filter reaches the maximum SNR of 2E/N₀ at t = T.
  8. In AWGN the optimum detector picks the nearest signal point, so P(e) depends only on the distances between points and on N₀.
  9. M-ary PAM multiplies the bit rate by log₂M at the same symbol rate but needs more power per bit; the error probability expression carries both effects.

Glossary

AWGN channel
A channel that adds white Gaussian noise of flat spectral density to the transmitted signal.
Baud
One symbol per second; the unit of symbol rate Rₛ = 1/T.
Correlation receiver
A multiplier followed by an integrate-and-dump that computes the inner product of the received signal with a reference signal.
Correlative coding
Signalling that deliberately adds a known, controlled ISI so that the spectrum fits a realisable Nyquist filter.
Decision region
The set of received points that the detector maps to one particular transmitted signal.
Duobinary signalling
A correlative scheme using a 1 + D response that sends binary data in the Nyquist bandwidth with three received levels.
Equalizer
A filter, usually a tapped delay line, placed at the receiver to cancel the ISI introduced by the channel.
Excess bandwidth
The bandwidth used beyond the Nyquist minimum, equal to αRₛ/2 hertz.
Eye pattern
A display formed by overlaying received symbol-length segments, whose opening shows the margin against noise and ISI.
Gram-Schmidt procedure
A constructive method that produces an orthonormal basis from any set of signals by subtracting projections.
Intersymbol interference
The contamination of one symbol's sample by the tails of pulses belonging to other symbols.
LMS algorithm
The least-mean-square update wₖ₊₁ = wₖ + μ·eₖ·rₖ that adapts equalizer taps from the error between the received and decided symbol.
M-ary PAM
Pulse amplitude modulation with M levels, carrying log₂M bits per symbol.
Matched filter
The filter whose impulse response is the signal reversed in time and delayed by the symbol period, giving maximum output SNR.
Minimum distance
The smallest separation between two signal points; it sets the error probability in AWGN.
Nyquist bandwidth
Half the symbol rate, 1/(2T) hertz, the least bandwidth in which zero-ISI signalling is possible.
Nyquist pulse
Any pulse shape whose sampled values are 1 at the centre and exactly 0 at every other symbol instant.
Orthonormal basis
A set of waveforms of unit energy that are mutually orthogonal, used as the axes of the signal space.
Q-function
The Gaussian tail probability Q(x); every error probability in this unit is written in terms of it.
Roll-off factor
The parameter α between 0 and 1 that fixes how much extra bandwidth a raised-cosine pulse uses.
Signal space
A geometric view in which each signal of duration T is a point whose coordinates are its projections on the basis functions.
Sinc pulse
The pulse sin(πt/T)/(πt/T); it is the ideal Nyquist pulse but needs infinite delay and a brick-wall spectrum.
Timing jitter
Random variation of the sampling instant about the ideal point, which reduces the eye opening.
Zero-forcing equalizer
An equalizer that makes the overall sampled response exactly 1 at the centre and 0 at all other symbol instants.
