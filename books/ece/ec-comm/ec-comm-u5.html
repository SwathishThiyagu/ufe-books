Analog and Digital Communication Unit 5 · Baseband Data Transmission and Detection

Baseband data transmission means sending a stream of bits as pulses through a cable or any low-pass channel, and getting the same bits back at the far end. This unit explains why fast pulses spill into their neighbours, how to shape and equalise them, how to picture signals as points in space, and how a matched filter with a nearest-point decision gives the smallest possible error rate. After it you can compute the Nyquist bandwidth of a link, design a raised cosine or duobinary scheme, and write the probability of error for binary and M-ary PAM.

5.1 Intersymbol interference and Nyquist criterion

Send pulses fast enough and each one is still ringing when the next one is sampled. The tails of the other symbols land on the sampling instant as intersymbol interference (ISI) and push the sample towards the wrong decision. Nyquist's first criterion states exactly when the spill is zero: the overall pulse must pass through zero at every sampling instant except its own. It also sets the floor on bandwidth, half the symbol rate. Every baseband modem and DSL line is designed around it.

You need: the Fourier transform and power spectral density (see Foundations: signals, noise and random processes), and PAM and line codes (see Sampling and pulse modulation).

The model: one pulse per symbol

A baseband link sends one symbol every T seconds. T is the symbol period. The k-th symbol ak is one of M real levels: ±1 for binary, ±1 and ±3 for 4-level PAM. The symbol rate is Rs = 1/T baud, where one baud is one symbol per second. Each symbol carries log₂M bits, so the bit rate is Rb = Rs log₂M bit/s.

The transmit filter gT(t), the channel c(t) and the receive filter gR(t) shape the pulse. Their cascade is one overall pulse p(t) = gT(t) * c(t) * gR(t), where * is convolution. Take p(0) as the peak height of one symbol at the sampler.

r(t) = Σ an p(t − nT) + n(t)

Here r(t) is the waveform reaching the sampler, the sum runs over every symbol n, and n(t) is the noise.

Sample at t = kT to decide symbol k. Split the sum into the term n = k and all the others:

y(kT) = ak p(0) + Σn≠k an p((k − n)T) + n(kT)

Here the first term is the wanted one, the middle sum is the ISI, and n(kT) is the noise sample.

The ISI depends on the samples p(±T), p(±2T), … of the overall pulse. The worst case comes when every neighbour has the sign that hurts. Its size, as a fraction of the wanted height, is the peak distortion D = Σn≠0 |p(nT)| / p(0). D ≥ 1 means some data patterns are decided wrongly even with no noise.

Nyquist's first criterion

Make every ISI term zero: require p(0) ≠ 0 and p(nT) = 0 for every integer n ≠ 0. This is Nyquist's first criterion, the zero-ISI condition. Between the instants the pulse is free.

In frequency the same condition reads: the spectrum P(f), copied at every multiple of 1/T and added, must be flat.

Σm P(f − m/T) = T·p(0) for all f

Here P(f) is the Fourier transform of p(t) and the sum runs over all integers m.

The narrowest spectrum that can be flat after this folding is a rectangle of height T from −1/(2T) to +1/(2T). Its pulse is the sinc: p(t) = sin(πt/T)/(πt/T), which is 1 at t = 0 and zero at every t = nT, n ≠ 0. So zero ISI needs at least

BN = Rs/2 = 1/(2T) Hz

Here BN is the Nyquist bandwidth: half the symbol rate. Any narrower spectrum leaves gaps after folding and cannot meet the criterion.

For binary, Rs = Rb, so the best is 2 bit/s per hertz; with M levels it becomes 2 log₂M bit/s/Hz. Figure 5.1.1 shows the worked example's three pulses and the sample they build.

Why the sinc is not the answer

The sinc meets the criterion in the narrowest band, but it fails in practice for two reasons. It runs from −∞ to +∞, so it is non-causal and can only be approximated with a long delay. Its tails fall only as 1/t, so a small timing error leaves many neighbours far from their zeros, and their contributions add up. The second worked example puts numbers on this.

A pulse can meet the criterion in other ways. An NRZ rectangle of width T is zero at every other sampling instant, so it has no ISI on paper; but its spectrum T·sinc(fT) never ends, and a band-limited channel rounds and spreads it. The practical answer is a pulse with a little more bandwidth than BN and fast-dying tails: the raised cosine of pulse shaping and the eye pattern.

  • The criterion is on the overall pulse p(t). A clean transmit filter followed by a distorting channel still gives ISI; that is the job of the equalizer.
  • It removes ISI only. Noise, crosstalk and interference remain.
  • It holds at the ideal sampling instants. A clock that samples early or late moves every neighbour off its zero.

Worked example

Question. A binary baseband link sends 2400 bit/s. The overall pulse has p(0) = 1 and p(±T) = 0.25, and all other samples p(nT) are zero. Around the k-th symbol the levels are ak−1 = +1, ak = −1, ak+1 = +1. Find the symbol rate, the symbol period and the Nyquist bandwidth. Then find the ISI and the sample y(kT) with no noise, the peak distortion, and the margin left to the decision threshold at 0.

  1. Binary: log₂2 = 1 bit per symbol, so Rs = Rb = 2400 baud.
  2. T = 1/Rs = 1/2400 s = 416.7 µs.
  3. BN = Rs/2 = 2400/2 = 1200 Hz.
  4. ISI = ak−1p(T) + ak+1p(−T) = (+1)(0.25) + (+1)(0.25) = +0.5.
  5. y(kT) = akp(0) + ISI = (−1)(1) + 0.5 = −0.5. The sample is still negative, so the decision −1 is right.
  6. Peak distortion D = (|p(−T)| + |p(T)|)/p(0) = (0.25 + 0.25)/1 = 0.5.
  7. Without ISI the sample would be −1, at distance 1 from the threshold. Now it is −0.5, at distance 0.5: this data pattern, the worst case, has eaten half the noise margin, exactly D = 0.5.

Answer. Rs = 2400 baud, T = 416.7 µs, BN = 1200 Hz; ISI = +0.5, y(kT) = −0.5, D = 0.5, margin halved from 1 to 0.5.

Worked example 2

Question. A link uses the ideal sinc pulse, but its sampler fires 0.1T late. Take the three nearest neighbours on each side (n = ±1, ±2, ±3) and find the wanted sample p(0.1T), each neighbour's contribution p((n + 0.1)T), and the worst-case peak distortion.

  1. Wanted sample: p(0.1T) = sin(0.1π)/(0.1π) = 0.3090/0.3142 = 0.9836.
  2. Neighbours, p((n + 0.1)T) = sin(π(n + 0.1))/(π(n + 0.1)): n = −1 gives 0.1093, n = +1 gives −0.0894, n = −2 gives −0.0518, n = +2 gives 0.0468, n = −3 gives 0.0339, n = +3 gives −0.0317.
  3. Sum of magnitudes = 0.1093 + 0.0894 + 0.0518 + 0.0468 + 0.0339 + 0.0317 = 0.3630.
  4. D = 0.3630/0.9836 = 0.369. With the worst data pattern the sample of a +1 symbol falls to 0.9836 − 0.3630 = 0.621.
  5. More neighbours make it worse: the magnitudes fall only as 1/n, so the sum keeps growing.

Answer. A 10 % timing error turns zero ISI into D = 0.369 from six neighbours alone, and the worst sample drops from 1 to 0.62. This is why the sinc is not used.

Exam traps

  • Using Rs where Rs/2 belongs. The minimum bandwidth is half the symbol rate: 2400 baud needs 1200 Hz, not 2400 Hz.
  • Mixing bit rate and symbol rate. BN = Rs/2 uses the symbol rate; for 4-PAM at 4800 bit/s, Rs = 2400 baud and BN = 1200 Hz.
  • Dropping the signs in the ISI sum. ISI adds with the sign of each neighbour; only the peak distortion uses magnitudes.
  • Writing the criterion as p(t) = 0 for t ≠ 0. It is only at the instants t = nT; between them the pulse is free.

Analyze An NRZ rectangle of width T is zero at every other sampling instant. Explain why it still causes ISI in a real link. 5 marks

5.2 Pulse shaping, raised cosine spectrum and eye pattern

The sinc pulse meets Nyquist's zero-ISI condition in the least bandwidth, but its rectangular spectrum cannot be built and its 1/t tails turn a small timing error into large ISI. The raised cosine pulse rounds the corners of that rectangle with a cosine roll-off set by one number, the roll-off factor α between 0 and 1. It spends a known extra bandwidth and gets tails that die as 1/t³. The eye pattern is the oscilloscope picture that shows whether the shaped pulses are still clean where the sampler fires.

You need: ISI and the Nyquist criterion (the Nyquist bandwidth Rs/2).

From a brick wall to a cosine roll-off

The criterion asks that the copies of P(f), spaced 1/T apart, add up to a constant. A rectangle does this, but so does any spectrum whose fall around Rs/2 is odd-symmetric: what one copy loses just below Rs/2, the next copy adds back just above it. The raised cosine spectrum uses half a cosine for that fall. It is flat from 0 to (1 − α)Rs/2, then falls smoothly to zero at (1 + α)Rs/2.

B = (Rs/2)(1 + α)

Here B is the baseband bandwidth in hertz, Rs = 1/T is the symbol rate in baud and α is the roll-off factor, 0 ≤ α ≤ 1.

excess bandwidth = B − Rs/2 = αRs/2

Here α is the excess as a fraction of the Nyquist bandwidth: α = 0.25 spends 25 % more than Rs/2.

α = 0 is the sinc with B = Rs/2. α = 1 doubles the band to B = Rs and gives the gentlest filter. Real links sit between, typically α = 0.2 to 0.5. Read the other way, a channel of bandwidth B carries at most Rs = 2B/(1 + α) symbols per second.

The pulse in time, and the root raised cosine pair

p(t) = sinc(t/T) · cos(παt/T) / [1 − (2αt/T)²]

Here sinc(x) = sin(πx)/(πx), so p(0) = 1. The sinc factor keeps every zero at t = nT, which keeps zero ISI. The second factor makes the tails fall as 1/t³ instead of 1/t.

Where the bracket is zero, at t = ±T/(2α), the formula reads 0/0 and the limit is p = (π/4)·sinc(1/(2α)). For α = 0.5 that point is t = T, where p is zero anyway; for α = 0.35 it is t = 1.43T, where p = −0.171.

The tails decide practical success. Halfway between instants, at t = 2.5T, the sinc is still 0.127, the raised cosine with α = 0.25 is 0.087, with α = 0.5 only 0.017, and with α = 1 exactly zero. Small tails mean a late sample picks up little from the neighbours.

The receive filter also has a second job: it should be matched to the incoming pulse to give the best signal-to-noise ratio (see the matched filter). Both demands are met by splitting the spectrum: a root raised cosine (RRC) filter, with |G(f)| = √P(f), at the transmitter and the same RRC at the receiver. Their cascade is the raised cosine, so the sampler sees zero ISI and a matched filter at once. One RRC alone does not give zero ISI; only the pair does.

Reading the eye pattern

The eye pattern (eye diagram) is made by feeding the receive-filter output to an oscilloscope triggered by the symbol clock, with a sweep of about two symbol periods. Thousands of random symbol sequences pile up on the screen, and for binary data the traces leave an open space shaped like an eye. Figure 5.2.1 shows the eye of the worked example's α = 0.5 pulse.

  • Vertical opening at the sampling instant: the distance between the lowest +1 trace and the highest −1 trace. It is the noise margin. ISI shrinks it.
  • Horizontal opening: the range of sampling instants over which the eye is still open. It is the tolerance to timing error and jitter.
  • Slope of the traces near the sampling instant: a steep slope means the opening shrinks fast when the clock is off, so the system is sensitive to timing error.
  • Spread of the zero crossings: their width is the timing jitter that the clock recovery circuit sees.

Sample in the middle of the eye, where the vertical opening is largest. For the α = 0.5 pulse, binary data and every possible neighbour pattern, the worst +1 sample is 1 at the centre, 0.785 at 0.1T off and 0.535 at 0.2T off. For α = 0.25 the same offsets give 0.652 and 0.289: less bandwidth buys a narrower eye. A channel that cuts high frequencies, a wrong α or a badly placed clock all show up as a closing eye.

Worked example

Question. A binary baseband link carries 4800 bit/s through a raised cosine system with α = 0.5. Find the symbol rate, the symbol period, the Nyquist bandwidth, the transmission bandwidth and the excess bandwidth. What would α = 0 and α = 1 need?

  1. Binary: one bit per symbol, so Rs = Rb = 4800 baud.
  2. T = 1/4800 s = 208.3 µs.
  3. Nyquist bandwidth Rs/2 = 4800/2 = 2400 Hz.
  4. B = (Rs/2)(1 + α) = 2400 × 1.5 = 3600 Hz.
  5. Excess = B − 2400 = 1200 Hz. Check: αRs/2 = 0.5 × 2400 = 1200 Hz.
  6. α = 0: B = 2400 Hz, but the filter is the unbuildable brick wall. α = 1: B = 2400 × 2 = 4800 Hz, 1200 Hz more than α = 0.5.

Answer. Rs = 4800 baud, T = 208.3 µs, Nyquist bandwidth 2400 Hz, B = 3600 Hz, excess 1200 Hz; α = 0 needs 2400 Hz and α = 1 needs 4800 Hz.

Worked example 2

Question. A cable passes 0 to 3600 Hz. (a) What is the highest binary bit rate with α = 0.2? (b) The link must carry 9600 bit/s. Can binary do it? If 4-level PAM is used, what is the largest α allowed?

  1. (a) Rs = 2B/(1 + α) = 2 × 3600/1.2 = 6000 baud. Binary gives Rb = 6000 bit/s.
  2. (b) Binary at 9600 bit/s means Rs = 9600 baud, which needs at least Rs/2 = 4800 Hz even with α = 0. 4800 Hz > 3600 Hz, so binary cannot do it.
  3. 4-PAM carries 2 bits per symbol: Rs = 9600/2 = 4800 baud, Nyquist bandwidth 2400 Hz.
  4. Need (4800/2)(1 + α) ≤ 3600, so 1 + α ≤ 1.5 and α ≤ 0.5.

Answer. (a) 6000 bit/s; (b) binary would need at least 4800 Hz, so no; 4-PAM at 4800 baud fits with α up to 0.5.

Exam traps

  • Dropping the half: writing B = Rs(1 + α) or excess = αRs. The correct forms are B = (Rs/2)(1 + α) and excess = αRs/2.
  • Putting the bit rate where the symbol rate belongs. For M-PAM use Rs = Rb/log₂M in the bandwidth formula.
  • Choosing α = 0 because it is the narrowest. The brick wall cannot be built and its eye closes under the smallest timing error; justify a practical α of 0.2 to 0.5.
  • Expecting one root raised cosine filter to give zero ISI. Only the transmit and receive RRC filters in cascade make the raised cosine.

Analyze An eye pattern has a wide horizontal opening but a small vertical opening at the centre. What is the likely cause? 4 marks

The timing is fine, since the eye is open over a wide range of instants. The small vertical opening points to amplitude trouble at the sampling instant: additive noise, or residual ISI from a channel that does not give the zeros at nT. An equalizer helps the ISI; more signal power helps the noise.

Analyze Why does a link use two root raised cosine filters instead of one raised cosine filter at the transmitter? 4 marks

The overall pulse must be a raised cosine for zero ISI, and the receive filter must be matched to the incoming pulse for the best SNR. With an RRC at each end both hold: the receive RRC is matched to the transmit RRC, and their product is the raised cosine. A raised cosine at the transmitter alone leaves the receive filter free to be either wide (more noise) or narrow (more ISI).

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Broadcast and satellite standards offer a short menu of roll-off factors so operators can trade bandwidth against out-of-band emission; DVB-S2 is a good example to read about. Very high-speed copper and optical links push α low and repair the rest with equalizers. Search for root raised cosine filter design and eye mask testing.

5.3 Equalization: zero-forcing and adaptive equalizers

An equalizer is a receiver filter that cancels the ISI a real channel leaves behind. It keeps the last few received samples on a tapped delay line, multiplies them by adjustable weights and adds them, so that at each sampling instant the wanted symbol comes out with little spill from its neighbours. The zero-forcing equalizer solves for the weights that force the ISI to zero at chosen instants. The adaptive equalizer learns its weights from a known training sequence and then from its own decisions. DSL modems and mobile receivers all contain one.

You need: ISI and the Nyquist criterion (the sampled overall pulse and peak distortion).

The transversal equalizer

A channel smears each pulse over several symbol periods. At the sampling instants the smear is described by the samples hm of the received pulse at t = mT: h0 is the wanted height, and h−1, h1, h2, … are the precursor and postcursor ISI. The received sample sequence is x(n) = Σm hm a(n − m) + noise.

A linear transversal equalizer is a delay line with taps T apart, weights c−N … cN and one adder. With 2N + 1 taps its output is

y(n) = Σk=−N…N ck x(n − k)

Here ck is the weight on tap k and x(n − k) is the sample held on that tap; the centre tap c0 holds the sample being decided.

The channel and equalizer in cascade have the combined sampled response vm = Σk ck hm−k, the convolution of the two sequences. Zero ISI means v0 = 1 and vm = 0 for every m ≠ 0. The equalizer sits after the receive filter and before the decision device (the slicer).

Zero-forcing: solve for the taps

A zero-forcing (ZF) equalizer with 2N + 1 taps forces v0 = 1 and vm = 0 for m = ±1, …, ±N. That is 2N + 1 linear equations in 2N + 1 unknowns:

Σk ck hm−k = 1 for m = 0, and 0 for m = ±1 … ±N

Here in matrix form H·c = [0 … 0 1 0 … 0]ᵀ, where row m of H holds hm+N … hm−N. Solve by elimination or by inverting H.

A finite equalizer cannot null everything: the zeros are forced only for |m| ≤ N, and new, smaller ISI appears at m = ±(N + 1) and beyond. The first worked example shows this. With unlimited taps the ZF equalizer becomes C(f) = 1/H(f): the inverse of the channel.

The price is noise enhancement. Where |H(f)| is small, |C(f)| = 1/|H(f)| is large, and noise at those frequencies is amplified with the signal. For white noise samples the equalizer multiplies the noise variance by Σ ck². At a spectral null (H(f) = 0) the ZF equalizer would need infinite gain. The minimum mean-square error (MMSE) equalizer avoids this by minimising the total power of ISI plus noise instead of forcing ISI to zero.

Adaptive equalizer: learn the taps with LMS

A ZF design needs the channel samples, which are unknown at start-up and drift. An adaptive equalizer starts from simple weights (centre tap 1, others 0) and updates them after every symbol. The usual rule is the least mean square (LMS) algorithm:

ck(n+1) = ck(n) + μ e(n) x(n − k)

Here μ is the step size (a small positive number), x(n − k) is the sample on tap k and e(n) = d(n) − y(n) is the error between the desired symbol d(n) and the equalizer output y(n) taken before the slicer.

  • Training mode. The transmitter first sends a sequence the receiver already knows, so d(n) is the known training symbol.
  • Decision-directed mode. After training, d(n) is the slicer's decision â(n), and e(n) = â(n) − y(n). The error is not zero, because y(n) is the analogue value before the slicer, for example 0.93 when â(n) = +1.
  • Step size. A large μ converges fast but the taps jitter; a small μ settles closer but tracks changes slowly.

Each update steps the taps down the slope of e²(n). Over many symbols LMS approaches the MMSE setting, not the ZF one. Figure 5.3.1 shows the three-tap equalizer of the first worked example and the response before and after it.

Worked example

Question. A channel has sampled response h−1 = 0.2, h0 = 1, h1 = 0.3, all other hm = 0. Design a three-tap zero-forcing equalizer (c−1, c0, c1). Find the equalized response vm for m = −2 … 2 and the peak distortion before and after. By what factor does the equalizer multiply white noise power?

  1. Write vm = c−1hm+1 + c0hm + c1hm−1.
  2. m = −1: c−1(1) + c0(0.2) + c1(0) = 0, so c−1 = −0.2c0.
  3. m = +1: c−1(0) + c0(0.3) + c1(1) = 0, so c1 = −0.3c0.
  4. m = 0: c−1(0.3) + c0(1) + c1(0.2) = 1. Substitute: c0(1 − 0.06 − 0.06) = 0.88c0 = 1, so c0 = 1.1364.
  5. Then c−1 = −0.2 × 1.1364 = −0.2273 and c1 = −0.3 × 1.1364 = −0.3409.
  6. New ISI outside the forced range: v−2 = c−1h−1 = −0.2273 × 0.2 = −0.0455 and v2 = c1h1 = −0.3409 × 0.3 = −0.1023.
  7. Peak distortion before: (0.2 + 0.3)/1 = 0.5. After: (0.0455 + 0.1023)/1 = 0.148.
  8. Noise gain Σck² = 0.2273² + 1.1364² + 0.3409² = 0.0517 + 1.2913 + 0.1162 = 1.459, so the noise power rises by 1.459 times (1.64 dB).

Answer. c = (−0.2273, 1.1364, −0.3409); v = (−0.0455, 0, 1, 0, −0.1023); peak distortion falls from 0.5 to 0.148, at a noise power cost of 1.459 times.

Question. The same channel is equalized adaptively by LMS in training mode with μ = 0.1 and starting taps (c−1, c0, c1) = (0, 1, 0). The training symbols are a(0) … a(5) = +1, +1, −1, +1, +1, −1 and there is no noise. Do the updates at n = 2 and n = 3.

  1. Received samples x(n) = 0.2a(n+1) + a(n) + 0.3a(n−1): x(1) = 1.1, x(2) = −0.5, x(3) = 0.9, x(4) = 1.1.

5.4 Signal space representation and Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization

A digital transmitter sends one of M known waveforms in each symbol interval of T seconds. The receiver can treat each waveform as a point in a space of at most M dimensions, with axes built by the Gram-Schmidt procedure. Energy becomes squared length and the difference between two signals becomes a distance. The matched filter, the optimum detector and every error-probability formula later in this unit are geometry on that picture.

You need: integration of piecewise-constant signals, and vectors (dot product, length).

Inner product, energy and coordinates

Two real signals x(t) and y(t) on 0 ≤ t ≤ T are compared by their inner product, the integral of their product:

⟨x, y⟩ = ∫₀ᵀ x(t) y(t) dt

Here the unit is V²·s when x and y are voltages. ⟨x, y⟩ = 0 means x and y are orthogonal.

The energy of x is Ex = ⟨x, x⟩, the energy it would deliver to a 1 Ω resistor, and its norm (length) is ‖x‖ = √Ex, in V·√s.

An orthonormal basis is a set of N functions φ1(t) … φN(t) with ⟨φi, φj⟩ = 1 when i = j and 0 otherwise. Each φ has unit energy, so when time is in ms its height is in 1/√ms. If every signal of the set is a weighted sum of the φ's,

sm(t) = Σi smi φi(t), smi = ⟨sm, φi⟩

Here smi is the i-th coordinate of signal m, in V·√s. The vector sm = (sm1, …, smN) is the signal's point.

Then all the integrals become vector arithmetic: the energy is Em = Σi smi² (the squared length), the inner product is the dot product, and the squared distance between two signals is d²mn = Σi(smi − sni)² = ∫₀ᵀ [sm(t) − sn(t)]² dt.

Gram-Schmidt: building the axes

Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization builds the basis from the signals themselves, one signal at a time. Each new signal keeps only the part that the axes found so far cannot explain.

  1. First axis: φ1 = s1/‖s1‖ (take the first signal with nonzero energy).
  2. For the next signal sm, find its projection on every existing axis: ci = ⟨sm, φi⟩.
  3. Subtract them to get the residual: gm(t) = sm(t) − Σi ci φi(t).
  4. If ‖gm‖ = 0, sm is a combination of the old axes: add no axis.
  5. If ‖gm‖ ≠ 0, add the new axis φ = gm/‖gm‖.
  6. Repeat for m = 2, 3, …, M. Each signal adds at most one axis, so the dimension N ≤ M.

The residual is orthogonal to every old axis: ⟨gm, φk⟩ = ⟨sm, φk⟩ − Σi ci⟨φi, φk⟩ = ck − ck = 0, because only the term i = k survives. Taking the signals in a different order gives different axes and different coordinates, but the energies and the distances between points stay the same; they are properties of the waveforms, not of the axes.

Cauchy-Schwarz and the standard signal sets

No inner product can be larger than the product of the two lengths:

|⟨x, y⟩| ≤ ‖x‖ ‖y‖

Here this is the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Equality holds only when y(t) = c·x(t) for a constant c. It is the step that proves the matched filter is optimum (see the matched filter).

  • Binary antipodal (polar NRZ, BPSK): s2 = −s1. One axis, points +√E and −√E, distance 2√E.
  • On-off (unipolar): points 0 and √E on one axis, distance √E.
  • Binary orthogonal: ⟨s1, s2⟩ = 0 with equal energy E. Two axes, points (√E, 0) and (0, √E), distance √(2E).
  • M-ary PAM: one pulse shape p(t) and M amplitudes, so one axis φ1 = p/‖p‖ and M points on a line.

For the same energy antipodal points are farther apart (2√E) than orthogonal ones (√(2E)), so they give the lower error rate (see optimum detection). Carrier sets such as QPSK belong to digital passband modulation.

5.5 Matched filter and correlation receiver

The receiver knows the shape of the pulse that was sent but sees it buried in noise. One linear filter gives the largest possible signal-to-noise ratio at the sampling instant t = T: the matched filter, whose impulse response is the pulse reversed in time and delayed by T. A multiplier followed by an integrator gives the same sample and is called the correlation receiver. The best SNR it reaches is 2E/N₀, and it depends only on the pulse energy, not its shape. Modems, radar and GPS receivers are built on this.

You need: signal space (inner product and Cauchy-Schwarz), and white noise with power spectral density N₀/2 (see Foundations: signals, noise and random processes).

The filter that maximises the sample

A known pulse s(t), lasting 0 ≤ t ≤ T with energy E = ∫₀ᵀ s²(t) dt, arrives as r(t) = s(t) + w(t). The noise w(t) is white and Gaussian with two-sided power spectral density N₀/2. Pass r(t) through a filter with impulse response h(t) and sample its output at t = T. The signal part of the sample and the variance of the noise part are

ys(T) = ∫ h(τ) s(T − τ) dτ, σ² = (N₀/2) ∫ h²(τ) dτ

Here both integrals run over every τ where h(τ) is nonzero. The noise variance is the same at every sampling time, because the noise is stationary.

The output SNR is ys²(T)/σ². By Cauchy-Schwarz, [∫h(τ)s(T − τ)dτ]² ≤ ∫h²(τ)dτ · ∫s²(T − τ)dτ = ∫h²dτ · E. Dividing by σ² gives

SNR(T) ≤ 2E/N₀

Here E is the pulse energy in joules (across 1 Ω for a voltage pulse) and N₀ the one-sided noise density in W/Hz, so 2E/N₀ = E/(N₀/2) has no unit.

Equality needs h(τ) proportional to s(T − τ). With gain 1 that is the matched filter, h(t) = s(T − t): the pulse reversed in time, then delayed by T so that the filter is causal. In frequency, H(f) = S*(f)e−j2πfT. At t = T its signal output is E and its noise variance is (N₀/2)E, so the SNR is E²/(N₀E/2) = 2E/N₀.

For the signal alone, the output is the pulse's autocorrelation shifted to peak at t = T. For a rectangular pulse of height A this is a triangle: it rises linearly from 0 at t = 0 to A²T at t = T and falls back to 0 at t = 2T (Figure 5.5.1). Sampling early or late takes a lower point of the triangle, while the noise stays the same.

The correlation receiver gives the same sample

Multiply r(t) by a stored copy of s(t) and integrate from 0 to T:

z(T) = ∫₀ᵀ r(t) s(t) dt = E + ∫₀ᵀ w(t) s(t) dt

Here the noise term has mean 0 and variance (N₀/2)E, exactly as for the matched filter.

The multiplier, integrator and sampler form the correlation receiver (correlator); the integrator is cleared after each symbol, so it is also called integrate and dump. Put h(τ) = s(T − τ) into the matched-filter output and substitute u = T − τ: y(T) = ∫ s(T − τ) r(T − τ) dτ = ∫₀ᵀ s(u) r(u) du = z(T). The two receivers give the same number at t = T and so make the same decisions.

They differ between samples: the matched filter's output is the triangle, the correlator's is a ramp that is dumped at T. The matched filter is a fixed filter and suits hardware; the correlator is an inner product ⟨r, s⟩, which is what the signal-space picture uses. With N basis functions, a bank of N correlators gives the coordinates of r.

Reading the result correctly

  • The bound 2E/N₀ depends only on E and N₀. Two pulses of equal energy give the same peak SNR, whatever their shape.
  • The factor 2 is real. With two-sided density N₀/2 the bound is E/(N₀/2); writing E/N₀ is 3 dB low.
  • The bound holds only at t = T. A mis-timed sample or a filter of the wrong shape loses SNR; the second worked example measures both losses.
  • The matched filter maximises SNR; it does not remove ISI. Pulse shaping and equalization are separate jobs.
  • It is optimum for white noise. For coloured noise, whiten the noise first, then match.

A mismatched filter loses SNR by the factor ρ² = ⟨hr, s⟩²/(‖hr‖²‖s‖²), where hr(t) = h(T − t) is the filter's reference shape. This is the squared correlation coefficient of the two shapes, which Cauchy-Schwarz keeps at or below 1. The comparison of this sample with a threshold, which turns SNR into an error probability, is the subject of optimum detection.

5.6 Optimum detection and probability of error in AWGN

After the correlators the receiver holds a point r in signal space, near one of the M signal points but pushed off it by noise. The optimum detector chooses the signal that most probably produced r. In white Gaussian noise with equally likely signals this is simply the nearest signal point, so the decision regions are bounded by perpendicular bisectors. The error probability then depends only on the distances between points and on N₀, through the Gaussian tail function Q(x). These formulas tell a link designer how much energy per bit buys how few bit errors.

You need: signal space (coordinates and distances) and the correlation receiver.

The received vector and the maximum-likelihood rule

A bank of N correlators, one per basis function, turns r(t) into a vector r = sm + w when signal m was sent. In additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN) of two-sided density N₀/2, the noise components w1 … wN are independent Gaussian variables with mean 0 and variance N₀/2 each.

The maximum-likelihood (ML) rule chooses the m for which r is most likely. The likelihood of r given sm is proportional to exp(−‖r − sm‖²/N₀), which is largest when ‖r − sm‖ is smallest. So, for equally likely signals:

m̂ = the m with the smallest ‖r − sm‖

Here this is the minimum-distance rule. It is the ML rule in AWGN for any energies; with equal priors ML is also the minimum-error (MAP) rule.

Expanding the distance, ‖r − sm‖² = ‖r‖² − 2⟨r, sm⟩ + Em. The term ‖r‖² is the same for every m, so the receiver can equally compute the correlation metric ⟨r, sm⟩ − Em/2 and choose the largest. Only when all energies Em are equal may the −Em/2 term be dropped, leaving the largest correlation. The worked example shows a case where dropping it gives the wrong answer.

Decision regions and the error of two points

The decision region of sm is the set of all r that are decided as m. Under the minimum-distance rule the boundary between two points is the perpendicular bisector of the line joining them: on it the two distances are equal. Figure 5.6.1 draws the regions for the three-point set from the Gram-Schmidt example.

Q(x) = (1/√(2π)) ∫ₓ^∞ e−u²/2 du

Here Q(x) is the probability that a zero-mean, unit-variance Gaussian exceeds x. Q(0) = 0.5, Q(1) = 0.159, Q(2) = 0.0228, Q(3) = 1.35 × 10⁻³, Q(4) = 3.17 × 10⁻⁵, and Q(x) = ½ erfc(x/√2).

For two points a distance d apart, an error happens when the noise component along the line joining them exceeds d/2. That component has variance N₀/2, so

Pe = Q( (d/2)/√(N₀/2) ) = Q( d/√(2N₀) )

Here d is in V·√s and N₀ in V²·s (W/Hz across 1 Ω), so the argument has no unit.

Polar (antipodal) signalling has points ±√Eb, so d = 2√Eb and Pe = Q(√(2Eb/N₀)). On-off signalling with points 0 and √E has d = √E but an average energy per bit Eb = E/2, so Pe = Q(√(Eb/N₀)): the same error rate needs 3 dB more Eb/N₀. Binary orthogonal signals sit in between at d = √(2Eb), giving the same Q(√(Eb/N₀)) as on-off.

More than two points: the union bound

With M points, signal m is decided wrongly if r lands in any other region. The exact probability needs an integral over the region, but a simple upper bound adds the pairwise errors:

Pe|m ≤ Σk≠m Q( dmk/√(2N₀) )

Here dmk is the distance from point m to point k. This is the union bound; it counts twice the noise that crosses two boundaries at once.

At high SNR the nearest neighbours dominate, so Pe ≈ N̄min Q(dmin/√(2N₀)), with dmin the smallest distance in the set and N̄min the average number of neighbours at that distance. Points on a line (M-ary PAM) are worked out in full in correlative coding and M-ary PAM.

5.7 Correlative coding and M-ary PAM

Correlative coding (partial-response signalling) adds a small, known ISI on purpose, so binary data can be sent at the Nyquist rate through a filter that can actually be built; duobinary, ck = ak + ak−1, is the standard example. M-ary PAM instead sends log₂M bits on each symbol by using M amplitude levels, cutting the bandwidth for a given bit rate at the cost of more power. PAM4 now carries 400G Ethernet.

You need: raised cosine bandwidth and error probability with Q(x).

Duobinary signalling and the precoder

Zero ISI in Rs/2 needs the brick-wall sinc. Duobinary lets each sample hold the current symbol plus one copy of the previous one. With polar symbols ak = ±1 the coder output is

ck = ak + ak−1 (levels −2, 0, +2)

Here in delay-operator notation the coder is 1 + D, where D is a delay of one symbol.

With an ideal Nyquist filter the overall response is H(f) = 2T cos(πfT) e−jπfT for |f| ≤ 1/(2T). It falls smoothly to zero at the band edge, so a real filter can approach it, and the band is still Rs/2. In time it is two sinc pulses T apart, whose sum decays as 1/t².

Undoing the coder by subtraction, âk = ck − âk−1, lets one wrong decision corrupt every later one: error propagation. The precoder prevents it. Before the coder, form the binary sequence pk = bk ⊕ pk−1 (⊕ is XOR), starting from p0 = 0, and send ak = 2pk − 1.

  1. Precode: pk = bk ⊕ pk−1, with p0 = 0.
  2. Map to polar: ak = +1 if pk = 1, −1 if pk = 0.
  3. Duobinary coder: ck = ak + ak−1.
  4. Receiver: if |ck| < 1 decide b̂k = 1; if |ck| > 1 decide b̂k = 0.

It works because ck = 0 exactly when ak ≠ ak−1, that is when pk ⊕ pk−1 = bk = 1. Each decision uses only the current sample, so one level error gives one bit error.

Modified duobinary and the noise cost

Duobinary's spectrum is largest at f = 0, which transformer-coupled lines cannot pass. Modified duobinary uses ck = ak − ak−2, the operator 1 − D²: its response 2T sin(2πfT) is zero at both f = 0 and f = 1/(2T). Its precoder is pk = bk ⊕ pk−2, and the receiver decides b̂k = 1 when |ck| > 1.

The price is noise margin. The middle level has two thresholds to cross instead of one, and the 1 + D shaping, split equally between the transmit and receive filters, costs extra transmitted energy for the same level spacing. With that split and symbol-by-symbol decisions, the error probability is

Pe = 1.5 Q( (π/4) √(2Eb/N₀) )

Here the 1.5 counts the two thresholds of the middle level, and (π/4)² is an SNR loss of 20 log₁₀(4/π) = 2.1 dB against polar binary.

At Eb/N₀ = 10 dB this gives 1.5 Q(3.512) = 3.33 × 10⁻⁴, against 3.87 × 10⁻⁶ for ideal binary. A sequence detector (Viterbi) instead of the slicer wins most of it back.

M-ary PAM

M-ary PAM sends one of M amplitudes per symbol, ±d, ±3d, …, ±(M − 1)d, so neighbours are 2d apart. Rs = Rb/log₂M and B = (Rs/2)(1 + α). The receiver needs M − 1 thresholds midway between levels. With Gray labelling neighbours differ in one bit, so BER ≈ Pe/log₂M.

Es = (M² − 1)d²/3

Here Es is the average symbol energy (d² for binary, 5d² for 4-PAM), with Es = Eb log₂M.

Inner levels have two boundaries d away, end levels one. With noise variance N₀/2,

Pe = 2(1 − 1/M) Q( √( 6Es / ((M² − 1)N₀) ) )

Here for M = 2 this is Q(√(2Eb/N₀)), the polar result.

For the same error rate Eb must grow by (M² − 1)/(3 log₂M): 2.5 times (4.0 dB) for 4-PAM, 7 times (8.5 dB) for 8-PAM, 21.25 times (13.3 dB) for 16-PAM. The table gives Eb/N₀ for Pe = 10⁻⁵.

  • Decoding duobinary without a precoder by subtraction: one wrong level then corrupts every later bit.

Takeaways

  1. ISI is the sum of the neighbours' pulse samples at the sampling instant; peak distortion measures its worst case.
  2. Nyquist's criterion p(nT) = 0 for n ≠ 0 gives zero ISI and sets the least bandwidth at Rs/2.
  3. The raised cosine spends αRs/2 extra hertz for a buildable filter and tails that fall as 1/t³; split as two root raised cosines it also gives a matched receiver.
  4. The eye pattern shows the noise margin (vertical opening) and the timing margin (horizontal opening) at a glance.
  5. A zero-forcing equalizer solves linear equations for its taps and removes ISI near the centre at the cost of noise gain Σck²; LMS learns the taps and tends to the MMSE setting.
  6. Gram-Schmidt turns M waveforms into points in N ≤ M dimensions, where energy is squared length and distance decides errors.
  7. The matched filter and the correlator give the same sample, with the largest possible SNR 2E/N₀, at t = T only.
  8. In AWGN the ML detector picks the nearest point; Pe = Q(d/√(2N₀)) per pair, Q(√(2Eb/N₀)) for polar binary.
  9. Precoded duobinary sends binary data in Rs/2 with a buildable filter at a 2.1 dB cost; M-PAM divides the bandwidth by log₂M at about 4 dB per extra bit for small M.

Understand Define intersymbol interference. Derive the condition for zero ISI at the sampling instants, state Nyquist's criterion in the time and frequency domains, and find the least bandwidth it allows. 10 marks

Points to cover. Received signal r(t) = Σ anp(t − nT) + n(t) and its sample y(kT); The ISI term and peak distortion; Zero ISI: p(nT) = 0 for n ≠ 0; Frequency form Σm P(f − m/T) = T·p(0); Least bandwidth Rs/2 with the sinc pulse, and why the sinc is impractical.

Model answer.

  1. Model. A baseband link sends symbols an every T seconds. The transmit filter, channel and receive filter together give one overall pulse p(t), so the waveform at the sampler is r(t) = Σn anp(t − nT) + n(t), where n(t) is noise.
  2. Sample. Sampling at t = kT to decide symbol k and separating the term n = k:
    y(kT) = ak·p(0)  +  Σn≠k an·p((k − n)T)  +  n(kT)
          wanted            intersymbol interference      noise
  3. Definition. Intersymbol interference (ISI) is the middle sum: the contribution of the tails of other symbols' pulses at the instant symbol k is sampled. In the worst data pattern every term pushes the same way, so the worst-case ISI as a fraction of the wanted sample is the peak distortion D = Σn≠0|p(nT)|/p(0). If D ≥ 1, some patterns are decided wrongly even without noise.
  4. Condition for zero ISI. The middle sum is zero for every data pattern only if each coefficient is zero: p(nT) = 0 for every integer n ≠ 0, with p(0) ≠ 0. This is Nyquist's first criterion in the time domain. It says nothing about p(t) between the sampling instants.
  5. Frequency domain. The samples p(nT) form the sequence p(0)δ[n]. Sampling p(t) every T seconds makes its spectrum periodic: the spectrum of the samples is (1/T)ΣmP(f − m/T). A sequence that is p(0) at n = 0 and zero elsewhere has a flat spectrum p(0). Equating the two:
    Σm P(f − m/T) = T·p(0)    for all f
  6. So the copies of P(f), shifted by every multiple of 1/T, must add up to a constant.
  7. Least bandwidth. If P(f) were zero above some W < 1/(2T), copies spaced 1/T apart would leave gaps between W and 1/T − W where the sum is zero, not constant. So W ≥ 1/(2T). The only spectrum that reaches W = 1/(2T) is the rectangle P(f) = T·p(0) for |f| < 1/(2T), whose pulse is p(t) = p(0)·sin(πt/T)/(πt/T), the sinc. The Nyquist bandwidth is therefore BN = 1/(2T) = Rs/2.
  8. Numbers. A binary link at 2400 bit/s has Rs = 2400 baud and needs at least 1200 Hz. With p(0) = 1, p(±T) = 0.25 and neighbours +1, +1 around a −1 symbol, the ISI is +0.5 and the sample is −0.5: the decision is still right, but half the noise margin is gone (D = 0.5).
  9. Why not the sinc. It is non-causal (it extends to −∞), and its tails fall only as 1/t. With a sampling error of 0.1T the six nearest neighbours alone give D = 0.369, and the sum keeps growing with more neighbours. Practical systems therefore use a raised cosine, which meets the same criterion with bandwidth (Rs/2)(1 + α).
  10. Check. For the sinc, p(nT) = sin(nπ)/(nπ) = 0 for n ≠ 0, and its rectangle of height T and width 1/T, copied every 1/T, tiles the frequency axis to the constant T, as the criterion requires.

ZF: solve for taps; forces zero ISI at the forced instants; Noise gain Σck² and the MSE split into ISI and noise; LMS: c(n + 1) = c(n) + μe(n)x(n); tends to the MMSE taps; MMSE taps and MSE against ZF at σ² = 0.1; Convergence, step size, tracking, training and decision-directed modes.

Model answer.

  1. ZF taps. Forcing v−1 = 0, v0 = 1, v1 = 0 gives c = (−0.2273, 1.1364, −0.3409), leaving v−2 = −0.0455 and v2 = −0.1023. Peak distortion drops from 0.5 to 0.148.
  2. ZF noise enhancement. Output noise = σ²Σck² = 0.1 × 1.459 = 0.1459. The channel's |H(f)| = |1 + 0.2ej2πfT + 0.3e−j2πfT| falls to 0.5 at f = 1/(2T), where ZF has gain about 2: that is where the noise is boosted.
  3. Mean-square error of ZF (symbols ±1, independent):
    MSE = residual ISI power + output noise
    = (0.0455² + 0.1023²) + 0.1459
    = 0.0125 + 0.1459 = 0.1584
  4. LMS. The update ck(n + 1) = ck(n) + μe(n)x(n − k), with e(n) = d(n) − y(n), is a stochastic-gradient descent on E[e²]. Its average end point is the MMSE (Wiener) solution c = R⁻¹p, with R the correlation matrix of the tap inputs (noise included) and p their correlation with the wanted symbol. For this channel:

  6. Convergence. ZF is computed once from a known channel (a matrix solve). LMS starts from (0, 1, 0) and needs many symbols; in the chapter's example two training updates with μ = 0.1 moved the taps to (−0.034, 1.034, −0.060) and the squared error from 0.25 to 0.0099. A larger μ converges faster but leaves more tap jitter (excess MSE); too large a μ diverges.
  7. Time-varying channels. ZF must re-measure the channel and re-solve. LMS tracks a slow change automatically, first with a training sequence and then decision-directed, using â(n) − y(n) as the error. A burst of wrong decisions can mislead it, so systems retrain when the error grows.

Evaluate Explain why the matched filter is optimum in AWGN. Then evaluate the loss when (a) a rectangular pulse's matched filter is sampled at 0.9T, (b) a ramp pulse s(t) = At/T is received with an integrate-and-dump filter. Find what each loss does to a polar link designed for Pe = 10⁻⁵ (Eb/N₀ = 9.59 dB). 12 marks

Points to cover. Cauchy-Schwarz: SNR ≤ 2E/N₀, equality only for h = s(T − t) at t = T; Mismatch factor ρ² = ⟨hr, s⟩²/(‖hr‖²‖s‖²); (a) triangle output 0.9E: loss 0.92 dB; (b) ramp with flat filter: ρ² = 3/4, loss 1.25 dB; New Pe from Q(√(2Eb/N₀)) with the loss.

Model answer.

  1. Why optimum. For any filter, the output SNR at t = T is [∫h(τ)s(T − τ)dτ]²/((N₀/2)∫h²dτ). Cauchy-Schwarz bounds the numerator by ∫h²dτ · E, so SNR ≤ 2E/N₀, with equality only when h(τ) ∝ s(T − τ). No linear filter can do better, and the bound is reached only at t = T.
  2. Mismatch in general. Write hr(t) = h(T − t), the filter's reference shape. Then
    SNR = (2E/N₀) · ρ²,    ρ² = ⟨hr, s⟩² / (‖hr‖² ‖s‖²) ≤ 1
  3. ρ is the correlation coefficient between the reference and the pulse; the loss in dB is 10 log₁₀ρ².
  4. (a) Late sample. For a rectangle of height A, the matched-filter output is the triangle y(t) = A²t for 0 ≤ t ≤ T, so y(0.9T) = 0.9E. The noise variance is still (N₀/2)E, because the noise is stationary. SNR = (0.9E)²/((N₀/2)E) = 0.81 × 2E/N₀: a loss of 10 log₁₀ 0.81 = −0.92 dB.

Glossary

AWGN
Additive white Gaussian noise: flat power spectral density N₀/2 and Gaussian amplitudes.
Baud
One symbol per second, the unit of symbol rate Rs = 1/T.
Correlation receiver
A multiplier and integrate-and-dump that forms ∫₀ᵀ r(t)s(t)dt, the matched filter's sample at T.
Decision region
The set of received points decided as one signal; in AWGN bounded by perpendicular bisectors.
Duobinary signalling
Correlative coding with ck = ak + ak−1: binary data in Rs/2 with three levels.
Equalizer
A receiver filter, usually a tapped delay line, that cancels ISI caused by the channel.
Eye pattern
Received traces overlaid in step with the symbol clock; the opening shows noise and timing margins.
Gram-Schmidt procedure
Builds an orthonormal basis from a set of signals by subtracting projections and normalising what is left.
Gray labelling
Assigning bit patterns so that neighbouring levels differ in one bit.
Intersymbol interference (ISI)
The part of a symbol's sample that comes from the tails of other symbols' pulses.
LMS algorithm
The update ck(n + 1) = ck(n) + μe(n)x(n − k), with e(n) the desired symbol minus the equalizer output.
M-ary PAM
Pulse amplitude modulation with M equally spaced levels, carrying log₂M bits per symbol.
Matched filter
The filter h(t) = s(T − t), which gives the largest SNR, 2E/N₀, at t = T in white noise.
Maximum-likelihood detection
Choosing the signal that makes the received vector most likely; in AWGN, the nearest signal point.
MMSE equalizer
An equalizer that minimises the mean-square error from ISI and noise together.
Noise enhancement
The rise in output noise when an equalizer has large gain where the channel is weak; Σck² for white noise.
Nyquist bandwidth
Rs/2 = 1/(2T) hertz, the least bandwidth in which zero-ISI signalling is possible.
Nyquist pulse
Any pulse that is zero at every sampling instant except its own, so it causes no ISI.
Orthonormal basis
Unit-energy, mutually orthogonal functions used as the axes of the signal space.
Peak distortion
D = Σn≠0|p(nT)|/p(0), the worst-case ISI as a fraction of the wanted sample.
Precoder
pk = bk ⊕ pk−1 before a duobinary coder, so each bit is read from one sample and errors do not propagate.
Q-function
Q(x), the probability that a unit Gaussian exceeds x; Q(x) = ½ erfc(x/√2).
Roll-off factor
α, 0 ≤ α ≤ 1: the excess bandwidth of a raised cosine as a fraction of Rs/2.
Root raised cosine filter
A filter with |G(f)| = √P(f); one at each end of the link gives a raised cosine overall and a matched receiver.
Signal space
A view in which each signal is a point whose coordinates are its inner products with orthonormal basis functions.
Sinc pulse
sin(πt/T)/(πt/T): the Nyquist pulse of least bandwidth, non-causal and with slowly decaying 1/t tails.
Union bound
An upper bound on symbol error probability: the sum of the pairwise error probabilities.
Zero-forcing equalizer
An equalizer whose taps force the combined response to 1 at the centre and 0 at chosen neighbouring instants.
