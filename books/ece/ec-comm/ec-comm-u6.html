6.1 ASK and FSK: generation and detection

Passband modulation puts bits onto a sine-wave carrier so they can travel through air, cable or fibre. Binary amplitude shift keying (ASK) changes the carrier amplitude for the two bits; binary frequency shift keying (BFSK) keeps the amplitude and switches between two frequencies. Both are cheap, so they run car key fobs, RFID tags, 433 MHz remotes and low-rate telemetry.

You need: the correlator (matched filter) receiver and signal-space points (see baseband data transmission and detection), and white Gaussian noise of two-sided density N0/2 (see signals, noise and random processes).

Binary ASK and on-off keying

Binary ASK sends the carrier at one amplitude for bit 1 and another for bit 0. When the second amplitude is zero it is on-off keying (OOK): bit 1 is a carrier burst, bit 0 is silence. Exams usually mean OOK by ASK.

s(t) = A·b(t)·cos(2πfc t)

A is the amplitude in volts, b(t) is 1 or 0 during the bit, fc is the carrier frequency in Hz and Tb the bit time in s; the bit rate is Rb = 1/Tb.

Generation. Multiply unipolar NRZ data (level 1 or 0) by the carrier in a product modulator, or simply switch the carrier on and off.

Coherent detection. Multiply by a phase-locked local carrier 2cos(2πfc t), integrate over one bit and sample at Tb. Without noise the output is A·Tb for a 1 and 0 for a 0, so the threshold is A·Tb/2.

Energy (1 Ω reference). A burst has energy A²Tb/2. With equally likely bits the average is Eb = A²Tb/4 and the average power A²/4, half the peak A²/2. The signal-space points are 0 and √(2Eb).

BFSK: two tones

BFSK sends bit 1 at the mark frequency f1 and bit 0 at the space frequency f0, both at amplitude A, so every bit has energy Eb = A²Tb/2.

Generation. A voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) driven by the data swings between f0 and f1 with continuous phase (the root of MSK). Switching between two oscillators also works, but the phase jumps widen the spectrum.

Coherent detection. Two correlators, at f1 and at f0, each integrate over Tb and the larger output wins. In signal space the points are (√Eb, 0) and (0, √Eb), √(2Eb) apart, with the 45° line between them as the boundary.

Orthogonal tones. Each correlator ignores the other tone only if the tones are orthogonal over a bit. Coherent detection needs |f1 − f0| = nRb/2 with n a positive whole number (carriers well above Rb); the smallest is Rb/2. Non-coherent detection needs nRb, at least Rb (see non-coherent detection). Being above the minimum is not enough.

Δf = |f1 − f0|/2, h = 2Δf/Rb

Δf is the deviation from the centre (f1 + f0)/2 and h is the modulation index.

B ≈ 2(Δf + Rb)

Carson's rule for BFSK, with Rb standing in for the baseband bandwidth of the data.

Spectra and error probability

The OOK spectrum is a sinc² lobe on fc plus a carrier line (the unipolar data has a non-zero mean); its nulls at fc ± Rb give a null-to-null bandwidth of 2Rb. BFSK looks like two such lobes, one per tone.

In AWGN of two-sided density N0/2, two signal points a distance d apart give Pb = Q(d/√(2N0)), where Q(x) is the normal tail area beyond x. Both schemes have d = √(2Eb), so

Pb = Q(√(Eb/N0))

for coherent OOK (Eb = A²Tb/4, the average) and for coherent orthogonal BFSK (Eb = A²Tb/2). N0 is the one-sided noise density in W/Hz.

Both are 3 dB worse than BPSK, whose points are 2√Eb apart. At the same peak amplitude BFSK has twice the Eb of OOK. BFSK also has a constant envelope, so a saturating amplifier is fine, while the OOK threshold depends on the received amplitude and moves when the signal fades.

Worked example

Question. A BFSK transmitter sends Rb = 10 kb/s with a 30 kHz mark tone (bit 1) and a 20 kHz space tone (bit 0). Find Δf, h and the Carson bandwidth. Are the tones orthogonal for coherent and for non-coherent detection? What changes with a 27.5 kHz mark tone?

  1. Tb = 1/Rb = 100 µs, so the mark tone makes 3 cycles per bit and the space tone 2.
  2. Spacing |f1 − f0| = 10 kHz, so Δf = 5 kHz around a centre of 25 kHz.
  3. h = 2Δf/Rb = 10/10 = 1.
  4. B = 2(Δf + Rb) = 2(5 + 10) = 30 kHz.
  5. Coherent test: Rb/2 = 5 kHz and 10 = 2 × 5, so n = 2: orthogonal.
  6. Non-coherent test: Rb = 10 kHz and 10 = 1 × 10: orthogonal too.
  7. At 27.5 kHz the spacing is 7.5 kHz = 1.5 × (Rb/2): above the minimum but not a whole multiple. The tones' correlation over a bit is about −0.25 of the full value, so each correlator picks up a quarter of the wrong tone.

Answer. Δf = 5 kHz, h = 1, B = 30 kHz; the 10 kHz spacing is orthogonal for both receivers. A 7.5 kHz spacing is not orthogonal although it exceeds Rb/2.

Worked example 2

Question. OOK and BFSK links both use peak amplitude A = 10 mV (1 Ω), Rb = 10 kb/s and N0 = 2.5 × 10−10 W/Hz. Find the peak power and, for each, Eb, Eb/N0 and the coherent Pb.

  1. Peak power A²/2 = (0.01)²/2 = 50 µW for both; Tb = 100 µs.
  2. OOK: Eb = A²Tb/4 = 2.5 × 10−9 J, so Eb/N0 = 10 (10 dB) and Pb = Q(√10) = Q(3.162) = 7.83 × 10−4.
  3. BFSK: Eb = A²Tb/2 = 5 × 10−9 J, so Eb/N0 = 20 (13 dB) and Pb = Q(√20) = Q(4.472) = 3.87 × 10−6.
  4. Check: OOK is silent half the time, so its Eb is half that of BFSK; those 3 dB cost a factor of about 200 in Pb.

Answer. Peak power 50 µW. OOK: 2.5 nJ, 10 dB, Pb = 7.83 × 10−4. BFSK: 5 nJ, 13 dB, Pb = 3.87 × 10−6.

Exam traps

  • Δf is half the tone spacing: for 30 kHz and 20 kHz tones Δf = 5 kHz, not 10 kHz, and h = 1.
  • Orthogonality needs a whole multiple: nRb/2 for coherent and nRb for non-coherent detection. 7.5 kHz at 10 kb/s is above the minimum and still not orthogonal.
  • In B = 2(Δf + Rb), Rb is the bit rate in bit/s; putting in Tb gives nonsense.
  • For OOK, Eb is the average A²Tb/4. Using the burst energy A²Tb/2 makes OOK look 3 dB better than it is.
  • Coherent detection needs a phase-locked carrier; an envelope detector is non-coherent and has other formulas.

6.2 BPSK: generation, detection and error probability

Binary phase shift keying (BPSK) carries one bit in the phase of the carrier: cos(2πfc t) for a 1 and its negative for a 0. The two signals are exact opposites (antipodal), which puts them as far apart as possible for a given energy, so BPSK has the lowest bit error probability of any binary scheme in white Gaussian noise. GPS, deep-space links and the control channels of mobile systems use it where power matters more than bandwidth.

You need: the correlator receiver and the Q-function (see baseband data transmission and detection), and ASK and FSK for comparison.

Waveform and generation

s(t) = ±√(2Eb/Tb) cos(2πfc t), 0 ≤ t < Tb

Eb is the bit energy in joules (1 Ω reference), Tb the bit time in s and fc the carrier in Hz; + sends bit 1 (phase 0) and − sends bit 0 (phase π). Squaring and integrating over a bit gives Eb, which is why the amplitude is √(2Eb/Tb).

  1. Convert the bits to bipolar NRZ: 1 → +1, 0 → −1.
  2. Multiply by the carrier √(2Eb/Tb)cos(2πfc t) in a balanced (product) modulator.
  3. The output flips its phase by 180° at every change of data.

The envelope is constant, so the power amplifier can run near saturation. Some syllabi call BPSK phase reversal keying (PRK).

Coherent detection and the signal space

The receiver multiplies r(t) = s(t) + w(t), where w(t) is white noise of two-sided density N0/2, by the unit-energy basis function φ(t) = √(2/Tb)cos(2πfc t) and integrates over the bit:

y = ∫ r(t)φ(t) dt = ±√Eb + n

The integral runs over 0 to Tb. y is in √J (volts × √seconds with a 1 Ω reference), not volts. n is Gaussian with mean 0 and variance N0/2.

The decision is the sign of y: y > 0 → 1, y < 0 → 0. On the signal-space line the points are +√Eb and −√Eb, a distance d = 2√Eb apart, and the boundary is y = 0.

The local carrier must match the incoming phase. A squaring loop or a Costas loop recovers it from the received signal itself; both lock with a 180° ambiguity, which DPSK removes. If the local phase is off by θ, the useful term becomes ±√Eb·cos θ, so the effective energy falls to Eb cos²θ.

Error probability and bandwidth

A 1 is misread when the noise pulls y below zero, which needs n < −√Eb. With σ = √(N0/2),

Pb = Q(√Eb/σ) = Q(√(2Eb/N0))

N0 is the one-sided noise density in W/Hz and Q(x) = (1/√(2π))∫ e−u²/2 du from x to ∞. In erfc form Pb = ½erfc(√(Eb/N0)), since Q(x) = ½erfc(x/√2).

Coherent BFSK and OOK have Q(√(Eb/N0)) (see ASK and FSK), so for the same Pb they need twice the Eb/N0: BPSK is 3 dB better. The cost is carrier recovery.

With rectangular bits the power spectral density is sinc² shaped around fc, S(f) ∝ sinc²((f − fc)Tb). Its first nulls are at fc ± Rb, so the null-to-null bandwidth is 2Rb and the bandwidth efficiency 0.5 b/s/Hz. There is no carrier line, because the bipolar data has zero mean.

Worked example

Question. A BPSK link runs at Rb = 1 Mb/s with received Eb/N0 = 10 dB. (a) Find Pb. (b) How many errors are expected in 108 bits, and per second? (c) The recovered carrier is 25° off. Find the new Pb.

  1. Eb/N0 = 1010/10 = 10.
  2. Argument √(2 × 10) = √20 = 4.472; Pb = Q(4.472) = 3.87 × 10−6.
  3. Errors in 108 bits = 108 × 3.87 × 10−6 = 387. At 106 bit/s that is 3.87 errors per second on average.
  4. With θ = 25°: cos²25° = 0.8214, so the effective Eb/N0 = 10 × 0.8214 = 8.214 (9.15 dB).
  5. New argument √(2 × 8.214) = 4.053; Pb = Q(4.053) = 2.53 × 10−5, about 6.5 times worse.

Answer. (a) 3.87 × 10−6; (b) about 387 errors in 108 bits, 3.87 per second; (c) 2.53 × 10−5 with a 25° phase error.

Exam traps

  • BPSK is Q(√(2Eb/N0)); Q(√(Eb/N0)) is coherent BFSK or OOK. Dropping the 2 costs 3 dB.
  • Q(x) and erfc differ by a factor and a √2: Q(x) = ½erfc(x/√2), so Pb = ½erfc(√(Eb/N0)). Mixing the two forms is the commonest numeric slip.
  • The null-to-null bandwidth of rectangular BPSK is 2Rb, not Rb.
  • A carrier phase error θ scales the amplitude by cos θ and the energy by cos²θ; at 90° BPSK fails completely.
  • The correlator output is in √J, not volts; the noise on it has variance N0/2, not N0.

6.3 Non-coherent detection and DPSK

A coherent receiver needs a local carrier locked to the phase of the incoming one. When the oscillators drift, or the receiver must be cheap, non-coherent detection does without that phase. For FSK and OOK it measures envelopes; for phase keying it puts the data in the change of phase from one bit to the next, which is differential phase shift keying (DPSK). The price is a small loss in error probability, often under 1 dB.

You need: ASK and FSK and BPSK.

Non-coherent BFSK and OOK

The envelope of a bandpass signal is its slowly varying amplitude. It is never negative and does not change when the carrier phase changes, so a receiver that looks only at envelopes needs no phase reference.

  1. Pass the received signal through two bandpass filters, one centred on f1 and one on f0 (or use I and Q correlators at each tone and add their squares).
  2. Envelope-detect each filter output.
  3. Sample both envelopes at the end of the bit.
  4. Decide 1 if the f1 envelope is larger, otherwise 0.

The tones must be orthogonal for every phase difference, which needs a spacing that is a whole multiple of Rb = 1/Tb: at least 1/Tb, twice the coherent minimum (see ASK and FSK).

Non-coherent BFSK: Pb = ½ e−Eb/2N0

Eb is the energy per bit in joules and N0 the one-sided noise density in W/Hz.

Non-coherent OOK uses one bandpass filter, an envelope detector and a threshold near half the received amplitude. At high Eb/N0 its error probability is close to the same ½e−Eb/2N0, with Eb the average energy per bit (half the energy of an on burst). Its best threshold moves with the signal level, a weakness in fading.

DPSK: the data rides on phase changes

DPSK encodes each bit bk into a transmitted bit dk that depends on the previous transmitted bit:

dk = bk ⊕ dk−1

⊕ is exclusive OR and d0 is a known reference bit. Then dk = 1 is sent with phase π and dk = 0 with phase 0. A data 1 flips the phase and a data 0 keeps it. (Some books use the complement, where a 1 keeps the phase; the receiver must match.)

  1. Split the received signal into a direct path and a path delayed by exactly Tb.
  2. Multiply the two (or, in the optimum form, correlate each bit with the previous bit's I and Q outputs).
  3. Integrate or lowpass-filter over the bit.
  4. A negative result means the phase changed, so bk = 1; a positive result means bk = 0.

The previous bit acts as the phase reference, so the receiver needs no carrier recovery, only a phase that stays steady over two bits. A constant phase offset, even 180°, cancels in the comparison, which also removes the 180° ambiguity of a BPSK carrier loop. For this receiver in white Gaussian noise the result is exact, not an approximation:

DPSK: Pb = ½ e−Eb/N0

Because the reference is itself noisy, DPSK is slightly worse than coherent BPSK, and one wrong bit spoils two decisions, so errors tend to come in pairs.

Worked example

Question. A DPSK transmitter uses dk = bk ⊕ dk−1 with reference d0 = 0, phase 0 for d = 0 and π for d = 1. Encode the data 1 1 0 1, list the transmitted phases, and show the receiver's decisions. Then rotate every received phase by 180° and decode again.

  1. d1 = 1 ⊕ 0 = 1; d2 = 1 ⊕ 1 = 0; d3 = 0 ⊕ 0 = 0; d4 = 1 ⊕ 0 = 1.
  2. Sent sequence including the reference: d = 0 | 1 0 0 1, phases 0 | π 0 0 π.
  3. Receiver compares each phase with the one before: π−0 changed → 1; 0−π changed → 1; 0−0 same → 0; π−0 changed → 1. Decoded 1 1 0 1.
  4. Rotated by 180°: phases π | 0 π π 0. Changes: changed, changed, same, changed. Decoded 1 1 0 1 again.
  5. Check with XOR: bk = dk ⊕ dk−1 = 1⊕0, 0⊕1, 0⊕0, 1⊕0 = 1 1 0 1.

Answer. Encoded 1 0 0 1 after the reference 0; phases 0 | π 0 0 π. The decisions give 1 1 0 1 both before and after a 180° rotation, because only phase changes carry data.

Exam traps

  • Non-coherent BFSK tones need a spacing of n/Tb (at least Rb), not n/(2Tb).
  • The exponents differ: DPSK is ½e−Eb/N0; non-coherent BFSK and OOK are ½e−Eb/2N0.
  • DPSK needs a known reference bit d0 at the start, and the receiver must use the same XOR convention as the transmitter.
  • Do not decode DPSK from absolute phases; only the change between neighbouring bits carries data, so a 180° carrier slip does no harm.
  • ½e−Eb/N0 is the exact DPSK result in white Gaussian noise, not a high-SNR approximation.

6.4 QPSK and M-ary PSK

QPSK carries two bits in each symbol by choosing one of four carrier phases. It is two BPSK signals on carriers 90° apart, so it sends twice the bits of BPSK in the same bandwidth with the same bit error probability. M-ary PSK goes further: with M = 2k phases each symbol carries k bits and the bandwidth per bit shrinks, but the points crowd together and need more power. QPSK runs satellite TV, LTE and Wi-Fi at their lower rates.

You need: BPSK and the idea of a two-dimensional signal space (see baseband data transmission and detection).

QPSK: two BPSK links on quadrature carriers

s(t) = A[I·cos(2πfc t) − Q·sin(2πfc t)]

A is the amplitude in volts; I and Q are the two bits in bipolar form, each +1 or −1 (pure numbers); the symbol lasts Ts = 2Tb. The phase is arctan(Q/I) and the symbol energy is Es = A²Ts = 2Eb (1 Ω reference).

With the first bit of each pair setting I and the second setting Q (1 → +1, 0 → −1), the pairs land at 11 → 45°, 01 → 135°, 00 → 225° and 10 → 315°. Neighbours 90° apart differ in one bit only: this is Gray labelling.

  1. Split the serial bits into two streams: odd-numbered bits to I, even-numbered bits to Q.
  2. Make each stream bipolar NRZ and hold each level for Ts = 2Tb.
  3. Multiply I by cos(2πfc t) and Q by −sin(2πfc t).
  4. Add the two products and transmit.

Each branch runs at Rs = Rb/2 symbols per second, so with rectangular pulses the null-to-null bandwidth is 2Rs = Rb: half that of BPSK at the same bit rate. Some syllabi call QPSK 4-PSK or quadriphase shift keying.

Coherent detection and error probability

The receiver multiplies r(t) by cos(2πfc t) and by −sin(2πfc t), integrates each over Ts and takes the sign of each output: the I output gives the first bit and the Q output the second. Because the carriers are orthogonal over a symbol, each branch is a BPSK detector carrying one bit with energy Eb.

Pb = Q(√(2Eb/N0))

Exactly the BPSK result. The symbol is right only if both bits are right, so Ps = 1 − (1 − Pb)² = 2Pb − Pb² ≈ 2Pb.

A carrier phase error θ rotates the received constellation against the decision axes. The I output becomes A(I cos θ − Q sin θ)·Ts/2, so each decision picks up a part of the other bit (crosstalk) and the margin to the nearest boundary shrinks; at θ = 45° the points sit on the boundaries. Offset QPSK delays the Q stream by Tb so that the phase never jumps by 180°; it leads to MSK.

M-ary PSK: more phases, fewer hertz

M-ary PSK uses M equal-amplitude phases 2πm/M, m = 0, 1, …, M − 1, with M = 2k. Each symbol carries k = log2 M bits (k = 1 is BPSK, k = 2 QPSK, k = 3 8-PSK). The symbol rate is Rs = Rb/k, the rectangular-pulse null-to-null bandwidth B = 2Rs = 2Rb/k and the bandwidth efficiency Rb/B = k/2 b/s/Hz.

d = 2√Es·sin(π/M)

d is the distance between neighbouring points and Es = kEb the symbol energy. d = 1.414√Es for QPSK, 0.765√Es for 8-PSK, 0.390√Es for 16-PSK.

Ps ≈ 2Q(√(2Es/N0)·sin(π/M))

the symbol error probability for M ≥ 4 at high Es/N0. With Gray labels a symbol error usually changes one bit, so Pb ≈ Ps/k.

Going from k to k + 1 bits per symbol doubles M but multiplies the bandwidth per bit only by k/(k + 1): from QPSK to 8-PSK saves a third of the bandwidth, from 8-PSK to 16-PSK a quarter. Each step costs several dB, because d shrinks roughly in proportion to 1/M while Es grows only as log2 M.

6.5 M-ary FSK and QAM

M-ary FSK and QAM both send several bits per symbol, but they spend different resources. M-ary FSK picks one of M orthogonal tones: constant envelope and low power per bit, but a band that grows with M. QAM picks one point on a grid of amplitude and phase: many bits per hertz, but it needs a linear amplifier and a cleaner channel. Telemetry and some military and underwater links use M-ary FSK; Wi-Fi, cable modems and microwave backhaul use QAM.

You need: BFSK and QPSK.

M-ary FSK: one tone per symbol

M-ary FSK sends k = log2 M bits by choosing one of M tones for a symbol time Ts = kTb. The symbol rate is Rs = Rb/k.

The tones must be orthogonal over Ts: the spacing is 1/(2Ts) = Rs/2 for coherent detection and 1/Ts = Rs for non-coherent detection. The receiver is a bank of M correlators (or M filters and envelope detectors); the largest output names the symbol, which maps back to k bits.

B ≈ M·Rs/2 (coherent), B ≈ M·Rs (non-coherent)

B is the occupied band in Hz. The bandwidth efficiency Rb/B = 2k/M (coherent) falls as M grows: 1 b/s/Hz for M = 2 and 4, 0.75 for 8, 0.5 for 16.

Ps ≤ (M − 1)·Q(√(Es/N0))

the union bound for coherent orthogonal MFSK, with Es = kEb the symbol energy. Every wrong symbol is equally likely, so Pb = Ps·(M/2)/(M − 1).

At a fixed Eb/N0 the error rate of orthogonal MFSK falls as M grows, because Es = kEb grows while the distance between any two tones stays √(2Es). That makes MFSK power-efficient and bandwidth-hungry, the opposite of PSK and QAM.

QAM: amplitude and phase together

s(t) = I(t)cos(2πfc t) − Q(t)sin(2πfc t)

I and Q are the in-phase and quadrature amplitudes in volts, chosen from a set of levels each symbol.

In square M-QAM, I and Q are independent √M-level amplitudes. For 16-QAM the levels are ±a and ±3a on each axis, a 4 × 4 grid; 4-QAM is QPSK. With Gray labels on each axis (00 → −3a, 01 → −a, 11 → +a, 10 → +3a), neighbours in the grid differ in one bit.

  1. Group the bits in fours (for 16-QAM); the first two pick I and the last two pick Q.
  2. Map each pair to its level with the Gray table above.
  3. Multiply I by cos(2πfc t) and Q by sin(2πfc t) in two balanced modulators.
  4. Subtract the Q product from the I product: s(t) = I cos − Q sin.

The coherent receiver has two correlators, one per carrier, and slices each output with thresholds at −2a, 0 and +2a. The average symbol energy of square M-QAM is Es = 2(M − 1)a²/3: 10a² for 16-QAM and 42a² for 64-QAM, in units where a point (I, Q) has energy I² + Q².

Ps ≈ 4(1 − 1/√M)·Q(√(3Es/((M − 1)N0)))

for square M-QAM at moderate to high Es/N0, with Gray labels Pb ≈ Ps/log2 M.

With rectangular pulses the passband null-to-null bandwidth is 2Rs, so the efficiency is k/2 b/s/Hz: 2 for 16-QAM, 3 for 64-QAM. Ideal Nyquist (sinc) pulses would need only Rs, giving k b/s/Hz.

6.6 MSK and GMSK

Minimum shift keying (MSK) is binary FSK with the two tones as close as orthogonality allows, half the bit rate apart, and with a phase that never jumps. The envelope is constant and the spectrum falls off fast, so a cheap saturating amplifier can send a compact signal. Gaussian MSK (GMSK) smooths the data with a Gaussian filter first, narrowing the spectrum further at the cost of some intersymbol interference; it is the modulation of GSM.

You need: BFSK and the modulation index and QPSK.

MSK: continuous-phase FSK with h = 0.5

f1 = fc + Rb/4, f2 = fc − Rb/4

fc is the centre (carrier) frequency and Rb = 1/Tb the bit rate. The deviation is Δf = Rb/4, the tone spacing Rb/2 and the modulation index h = 2Δf/Rb = 0.5.

Rb/2 is the smallest spacing at which two tones are coherently orthogonal over a bit (see BFSK); hence "minimum" shift. Some syllabi call MSK fast FSK.

Write the signal as s(t) = A cos(2πfc t + θ(t)). During a 1 the frequency is fc + Rb/4, so θ rises by 2π(Rb/4)Tb = π/2 over the bit; during a 0 it falls by π/2. Each bit starts from where the last one ended, so θ(t) is a continuous zig-zag of straight segments, the phase trellis. At the end of every bit θ is a multiple of π/2.

I/Q form, generation and detection

s(t) = A[aI(t)cos(πt/2Tb)cos(2πfc t) − aQ(t)sin(πt/2Tb)sin(2πfc t)]

aI and aQ are ±1 streams built from alternate bits; each value lasts 2Tb, and the Q stream is offset from the I stream by Tb. The cosine and sine factors are half-sine pulse shapes of length 2Tb.

So MSK is offset QPSK with half-sine pulses instead of rectangles: because the two rails change at different instants and each pulse eases in and out, the envelope stays exactly constant.

  • Generation: drive a VCO between f2 and f1 (phase continuous by construction), or split the bits into I and Q rails, offset Q by Tb, weight with half-sines and modulate cos and sin carriers.
  • Coherent detection: correlate each rail with its half-sine-weighted carrier over 2Tb; the I rail is sampled every 2Tb, and the Q rail too, Tb later. Each rail decides one bit with energy Eb, so Pb = Q(√(2Eb/N0)), the same as BPSK and QPSK.
  • Non-coherent detection: a limiter followed by a frequency discriminator reads the instantaneous frequency; it is simple but makes more errors than coherent detection.

The MSK spectrum has a main lobe 1.5Rb wide between nulls, against Rb for QPSK and 2Rb for BPSK, but its first side lobe is 23 dB down (13 dB for QPSK) and the lobes fall as 1/f⁴ instead of 1/f². 99% of the MSK power lies within about 1.2Rb, against roughly 10Rb for QPSK.

GMSK: Gaussian filter and the BT product

BT = B·Tb, B = (BT)·Rb

B is the 3 dB bandwidth of the Gaussian lowpass filter in Hz; BT is a pure number.

The filter sits before the frequency modulator, so the output is still constant-envelope MSK-like FM. A smaller BT gives a narrower spectrum with lower side lobes, but the Gaussian pulse then spreads each bit's frequency change over neighbouring bits: intersymbol interference that a plain sample-by-sample detector feels as a loss and a Viterbi (MLSE) equaliser can undo. GSM uses BT = 0.3, which spreads each bit over about three bit times and still fits the 270.833 kb/s signal into 200 kHz channels.

6.7 Signal constellations and bandwidth efficiency

A signal constellation draws every waveform a modulator can send as a point in a signal space built from orthonormal basis functions. The distances between the points set the error probability; the number of points sets the bits per symbol and, with the pulse shape, the bandwidth. Put the spectra of the schemes side by side and the picture also shows how much band each one really occupies. Every modem, from a GPS receiver to 1024-QAM Wi-Fi, is designed on these two pictures.

You need: the schemes of BPSK, M-ary PSK, QAM and MFSK and MSK.

Waveforms become points

For passband symbols of length Ts, two orthonormal basis functions carry everything: φ1(t) = √(2/Ts)cos(2πfc t) and φ2(t) = −√(2/Ts)sin(2πfc t). Each has unit energy and their product integrates to zero over a symbol. A symbol si(t) = si1φ1(t) + si2φ2(t) is the point (si1, si2), with energy Ei = si1² + si2²; the average over M equally likely symbols is Es.

The receiver correlates with φ1 and φ2, gets a noisy point, and picks the nearest constellation point. The set of points nearer to si than to any other is its decision region.

Minimum distance decides the errors

Ps ≲ Nnb·Q(dmin/√(2N0))

dmin is the smallest distance between two points, Nnb the number of nearest neighbours of a point and N0/2 the two-sided noise density. A symbol is lost when noise pushes it across a boundary dmin/2 away.

Size sets the bits and the bandwidth

M points carry k = log2 M bits, so Rb = kRs. The bandwidth efficiency η = Rb/B is measured in b/s/Hz, never in bits per symbol. With rectangular pulses the passband main lobe is B = 2Rs wide, so η = k/2; with ideal Nyquist pulses B = Rs and η = k. Orthogonal MFSK needs about M·Rs/2, so η = 2k/M falls as M grows.

On a plane of η across and required Eb/N0 up, PSK and QAM move right and up as M grows (more bits per hertz, more power per bit), MFSK moves left and down. The Shannon limit bounds the plane: η ≤ log2(1 + SNR), or per bit Eb/N0 ≥ (2η − 1)/η, which tends to −1.59 dB as η → 0. Coding (see information theory and error-control coding) is what closes the gap.

  • QPSK's main lobe is narrower than MSK's, but MSK has far less power outside it; quote both facts in a spectrum comparison.

6.8 Spread spectrum: DSSS and frequency hopping

Spread spectrum takes a narrowband data signal and deliberately spreads it over a far wider band with a fast pseudo-noise (PN) code that the receiver also knows. The receiver undoes the spreading for the wanted signal, while a jammer, an interferer or another user stays spread and mostly falls outside the data band. Direct sequence (DSSS) multiplies the data by the code; frequency hopping (FHSS) moves the carrier from channel to channel. GPS, CDMA mobile, Bluetooth and anti-jam military radios are built on these ideas.

You need: BPSK and the idea of autocorrelation (see signals, noise and random processes).

PN sequences, chips and processing gain

A PN sequence is a deterministic binary sequence that looks random. A shift register of m stages with exclusive-OR feedback makes one; when the feedback taps are chosen well, it runs through all 2m − 1 non-zero states before repeating, a maximal-length sequence (m-sequence). Each PN bit is called a chip, of length Tc, and the chip rate Rc = 1/Tc is much higher than the bit rate.

This 3-stage register, started at 111, gives 1 1 1 0 0 1 0, period 2³ − 1 = 7. It shows the three m-sequence properties: balance (four 1s, three 0s), runs (half the runs have length 1, a quarter length 2, and so on) and a two-valued autocorrelation: in ±1 form the sequence agrees with itself in all 7 places at zero shift and gives −1 at every other shift. That sharp peak lets the receiver find the code phase.

Gp = Rc/Rb = Tb/Tc

the processing gain of DSSS, the number of chips per bit; in decibels Gp(dB) = 10 log10(Rc/Rb). Rc is in chips/s and Rb in bit/s.

DSSS: spread, send, despread

s(t) = A·d(t)·c(t)·cos(2πfc t)

d(t) is the bipolar data (±1) at Rb and c(t) the bipolar PN code (±1) at Rc. The product d·c is a ±1 wave at the chip rate, so the BPSK signal now occupies about 2Rc instead of 2Rb.

The receiver multiplies by the same carrier and by the same code, aligned to the chip. Since c(t)² = 1 the data returns, and the correlator integrates over Tb as for BPSK. With a different code, or the right code a chip out of step, the correlation is close to zero.

A narrowband jammer at the carrier meets the code only once, at the receiver, so the multiplication spreads it over about 2Rc; the data filter then keeps only about 1/Gp of its power. The jamming margin is how many dB the jammer power J may exceed the signal power S at the receiver input while the despread Eb/N0 still meets its target:

Mj = Gp(dB) − (Eb/N0)req(dB) − Lsys(dB)

Lsys is the implementation loss of the system, often taken as 0 in exam questions.

Frequency hopping and CDMA

FHSS keeps a narrowband modulation (usually M-FSK) but the PN code drives a frequency synthesiser that picks one of N channels at each hop. The receiver's synthesiser follows the same pattern. If the hop band is Wss and each channel B wide, N = Wss/B and the processing gain is Gp ≈ N.

  • Slow hopping: several symbols per hop (hop rate below the symbol rate); simple synthesiser, but a hit channel loses a burst of symbols.
  • Fast hopping: several hops per symbol; each symbol is spread over several channels, so a jammer on one channel spoils only part of it; needs a fast synthesiser and tight timing.

DSSS spreads every symbol over the whole band at once; FHSS occupies one channel at a time but visits the whole band over many hops. Code-division multiple access (CDMA) gives each user its own code. The receiver correlates with one code and the other users, having low cross-correlation, look like added noise. A strong nearby user can still swamp a distant one (the near-far problem), so CDMA needs tight power control.

6.9 OFDM and fading channels

A radio signal reaches the receiver along many paths, each with its own delay, strength and Doppler shift, so the received signal fades and smears. OFDM answers the smearing by splitting one fast data stream into many slow ones on closely spaced, orthogonal subcarriers, built with an inverse FFT and separated with an FFT. A cyclic prefix soaks up the echoes, and each narrow subcarrier then sees a simple flat gain. Wi-Fi, 4G LTE, 5G NR and digital TV all use it.

You need: QAM, the discrete Fourier transform and intersymbol interference (see baseband data transmission and detection).

Multipath fading

The delay spread is how long the echoes keep arriving: τmax from first to last, or the rms value στ. Its frequency-domain twin is the coherence bandwidth Bc, the range over which the channel gain stays nearly the same; a common rule is Bc ≈ 1/(5στ). If the signal bandwidth is well below Bc the fading is flat (all frequencies rise and fall together); if it is wider, the fading is frequency-selective and the echoes cause intersymbol interference.

Motion adds the time dimension. The maximum Doppler shift is fd = vfc/c (v the speed, c = 3 × 108 m/s), and the coherence time, over which the channel stays nearly constant, is roughly Tc ≈ 0.423/fd. Symbols much shorter than Tc see slow fading; symbols comparable to it see fast fading.

Rayleigh, BPSK: Pb = ½[1 − √(γ̄/(1 + γ̄))] ≈ 1/(4γ̄)

γ̄ is the mean Eb/N0. With no direct path the amplitude is Rayleigh distributed and the error rate falls only as 1/γ̄, against an exponential fall in AWGN.

Diversity sends or receives the same bit over independently fading paths: different times (coding with interleaving), frequencies, or antennas. Maximal-ratio combining (MRC) weights each branch by its own gain and adds them. With two branches BPSK gives Pb ≈ 3/(16γ̄²), falling as 1/γ̄²: deep fades must now happen on both branches at once.

OFDM: many slow subcarriers

Send N QAM symbols X[0] … X[N − 1] at the same time, each on its own subcarrier. Each lasts the useful time Tu, N times longer than one serial symbol, so the same echoes are a small fraction of it.

Δf = 1/Tu

Δf is the subcarrier spacing in Hz. At this spacing every subcarrier makes a whole number of cycles in Tu, so they are orthogonal: the spectra overlap, but at each subcarrier's centre all the others pass through zero.

  1. Map the bits to QAM symbols X[k], k = 0, 1, …, N − 1.
  2. Take an N-point IFFT: x[n] = (1/N)Σ X[k]ej2πkn/N, n = 0, …, N − 1.
  3. Copy the last G samples to the front as the cyclic prefix and send N + G samples.
  4. At the receiver drop the first G samples and take an N-point FFT to get Y[k].
  5. Divide Y[k] by the channel gain H[k] and slice each subcarrier's QAM point.

Y[k] = H[k]·X[k] + W[k]

H[k] is the channel's frequency response at subcarrier k and W[k] the noise. Equalisation is one complex division per subcarrier instead of a long time-domain filter. The FFT needs about N log2 N operations instead of N².

The cyclic prefix and practical limits

The physical channel convolves the signal with its impulse response. Because the prefix repeats the end of the block, the useful block the receiver keeps equals a circular convolution of x[n] with the channel, provided the prefix is at least as long as the channel's delay spread. The FFT turns a circular convolution into the product H[k]X[k], and the previous symbol's echoes die inside the prefix: no ISI, no inter-carrier interference (ICI).

T = Tu + Tcp, Tcp ≥ τmax

T is the full symbol time; Tcp = G/B with B = NΔf the sample rate. A prefix shorter than τmax lets ISI and ICI in.

  • Overhead: the prefix carries no new data, so rates use T, not Tu; Tcp/T is lost.
  • Peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR): many subcarriers can add in phase, so the amplifier must back off.
  • Synchronisation: a carrier frequency offset of a fraction of Δf breaks orthogonality and causes ICI.
  • Fading per subcarrier: OFDM removes ISI, not fading; a subcarrier in a deep notch is lost, so OFDM systems add coding across subcarriers (frequency diversity). OFDMA shares subcarriers among users, as in the LTE downlink.

Worked example

Exam traps

  • Bits per OFDM symbol = (number of data subcarriers) × log2 M; forgetting either factor is common.

Evaluate A 20 MHz channel has a delay spread of 5 µs. Compare a single-carrier QAM link at 20 Msymbol/s with an OFDM link in the same band: intersymbol interference, equalisation effort, cyclic-prefix overhead, peak-to-average power and synchronisation. Which would you choose? 15 marks

Points to cover. Single carrier: 5 µs × 20 Msymbol/s = 100 symbols of ISI; long equaliser; OFDM with N = 1024: Δf = 19.53 kHz, Tu = 51.2 µs, Tcp = 5 µs, overhead 8.9%; One complex division per subcarrier after a 1024-point FFT; OFDM: high PAPR, sensitive to frequency offset; Choose OFDM for this channel.

Model answer.

  1. Single carrier. Symbol time 1/20 MHz = 50 ns. The echoes last 5 µs, so each received sample mixes 5 µs × 20 × 106 = 100 symbols. The channel is strongly frequency-selective (coherence bandwidth about 1/(5στ); with στ ≈ 1 µs that is roughly 200 kHz, a hundredth of the band).
  2. Single-carrier equalisation. A time-domain equaliser needs at least about 100 taps, often several hundred, each a complex multiply per symbol, and it must adapt as the channel changes; a maximum-likelihood sequence detector would be far too complex for 16-QAM over 100 symbols.
  3. OFDM design. Pick the prefix first, then make Tu long enough that the prefix is a small overhead:
    NΔfTuTcpOverhead Tcp/T
    51239.06 kHz25.6 µs5 µs16.3%
    102419.53 kHz51.2 µs5 µs8.9%
    20489.77 kHz102.4 µs5 µs4.7%
  4. N = 1024 is a reasonable choice: 8.9% overhead, and a 19.5 kHz subcarrier is much narrower than the coherence bandwidth, so each sees flat fading.
  5. OFDM equalisation. A 1024-point FFT costs about (N/2)log2 N = 5120 complex multiplies per OFDM symbol, about 5 per subcarrier, plus one complex division per subcarrier: roughly 6 operations per data symbol instead of 100 or more.
  6. Peak-to-average power. With 1024 subcarriers adding with random phases the signal looks Gaussian; rare peaks far above the mean force the amplifier to back off several dB, costing power efficiency. Single-carrier 16-QAM has a much lower PAPR (2.55 dB for the constellation itself: peak 18a² over average 10a²).
  7. Synchronisation. OFDM subcarriers 19.5 kHz apart lose orthogonality if the carrier is off by a noticeable fraction of that. A 2 GHz oscillator with 2 ppm error is 4 kHz off, 20% of Δf, so OFDM needs frequency-offset estimation and correction; Doppler at 30 m/s (200 Hz) is only 1% of Δf. Timing is relaxed by the prefix.
  8. Fading still matters. OFDM removes the ISI but some subcarriers fall into deep notches; coding and interleaving across subcarriers turn the frequency selectivity into diversity.
  9. Verdict. For a 100-symbol delay spread OFDM wins clearly on equaliser complexity, at the cost of 9% overhead, a linear amplifier with back-off, and careful frequency synchronisation. That is the choice made by Wi-Fi, LTE and DVB-T. Single carrier (with frequency-domain equalisation) remains attractive for uplinks where amplifier efficiency matters, which is why LTE uses DFT-spread OFDM there.
  10. Closing check. 1/19.53 kHz = 51.2 µs and 5/(51.2 + 5) = 0.089, consistent with the table.
Evaluate Compare DSSS and FHSS for interference rejection and multiple access when both spread a 10 kb/s signal over 25 MHz. Include processing gain, chip rate, hop rate and the near-far problem. 13 marks

Points to cover. DSSS: Rc ≈ 12.5 Mchip/s, Gp = 1250 (31 dB); FHSS: 1000 channels of 25 kHz, Gp ≈ 1000 (30 dB); Narrowband jammer: DSSS spreads it; FHSS avoids it most of the time; CDMA by codes vs by hop patterns; Near-far: 40 dB for a 10× distance ratio; DSSS needs power control, FHSS suffers only collisions.

Model answer.

  1. DSSS numbers. To fill about 25 MHz with BPSK chips the main lobe 2Rc must be 25 MHz:
    Rc = 12.5 Mchip/s;  Gp = Rc/Rb = 12.5 × 106/104 = 1250  →  30.97 dB
  2. FHSS numbers. With BFSK in 25 kHz channels:
    N = 25 MHz/25 kHz = 1000 channels;  Gp ≈ 1000  →  30.0 dB
slow hopping at 1000 hops/s: 10 bits per hop
fast hopping at 4 hops/bit: 40 000 hops/s, 25 µs dwell
  3. Processing gain. Almost the same, about 30 dB, because both occupy the same total band for the same data rate.
  4. Against a narrowband jammer. DSSS: the receiver's code multiplication spreads the jammer over 25 MHz, leaving about 1/1250 of its power in the data band, all the time. FHSS: the radio lands on the jammed channel 1 hop in 1000; that hop may be lost, but coding and interleaving repair it. A partial-band jammer covering 10% of the band hits 10% of FHSS hops but is spread just as well by DSSS.
  5. Against a follower jammer. FHSS with fast hopping leaves each channel in 25 µs, before a jammer can measure and follow. DSSS has no such weakness, but it has no way to avoid a strong wideband jammer either.
  6. Multiple access. DSSS-CDMA: each user has its own code; others appear as noise reduced by Gp, so capacity falls gently as users are added. FH multiple access: each user has its own hop pattern; two users collide only when they land on the same channel in the same dwell, probability about 1/1000 per hop per interfering user.
  7. Near-far problem. With a path-loss exponent of 4, a user 10 times closer is 40 dB stronger:
    10 log(104) = 40 dB  >  Gp = 31 dB
  8. In DSSS that near user swamps the far one, since despreading suppresses it by only 31 dB; tight power control (as in CDMA mobiles) is essential. In FHSS the near user hurts only when the two collide on a channel, so the problem is much milder.
  9. Practical cost. DSSS needs chip-level synchronisation (80 ns chips) and a 25 MHz-wide front end; FHSS needs a fast-settling synthesiser and only a 25 kHz-wide receiver at any instant.
  10. Verdict. DSSS suits cellular CDMA and GPS, where power control and precise timing are available and steady suppression of everything else is wanted. FHSS suits military radios and Bluetooth, where avoiding interference and tolerating strong nearby users matter more.
  11. Closing check. 12.5 Mchip/s / 10 kb/s = 1250 and 25 MHz / 25 kHz = 1000: the two gains differ by 10 log 1.25 = 0.97 dB, so the comparison is fair.
Evaluate Evaluate the claim: "Higher-order QAM always improves performance because it sends more bits per symbol." Use a 10 MHz channel (B = 2Rs) with a fixed received S/N0 that gives Eb/N0 = 12 dB for QPSK, and compare QPSK, 16-QAM and 64-QAM. 12 marks

Points to cover. Rs = 5 Msymbol/s: 10, 20, 30 Mb/s; Fixed S/N0 = 82 dB-Hz: Eb/N0 = 12.0, 9.0, 7.2 dB; Pb = 9.0 × 10−9, 4.4 × 10−3, 6.4 × 10−2; More bits per symbol cost power; the claim is false when SNR is limited; Adaptive modulation picks M from the measured SNR.

Model answer.

  1. Bit rates. Rs = B/2 = 5 Msymbol/s for all three. QPSK 2 × 5 = 10 Mb/s; 16-QAM 20 Mb/s; 64-QAM 30 Mb/s. So far the claim looks right.
  2. Fixed received power. The transmitter and the path set S/N0, not Eb/N0:
    S/N0 = (Eb/N0)·Rb = 15.85 × 107 = 1.585 × 108 Hz  (82.0 dB-Hz)
Eb/N0 = (S/N0)/Rb:  QPSK 15.85 (12.0 dB), 16-QAM 7.92 (9.0 dB), 64-QAM 5.28 (7.2 dB)
  3. Every extra bit per symbol spreads the same energy over more bits, so Eb falls.
  4. Bit error probabilities.
    QPSK:   Q(√(2 × 15.85)) = Q(5.630) = 9.0 × 10−9
16-QAM: arg = √(3 × 4 × 7.92/15) = 2.518;  Pb ≈ 3Q(2.518)/4 = 4.4 × 10−3
64-QAM: arg = √(3 × 6 × 5.28/63) = 1.229;  Pb ≈ 4(7/8)Q(1.229)/6 = 6.4 × 10−2
  5. Comparison.
    SchemeRbEb/N0PbEb/N0 needed for 10−5
    QPSK10 Mb/s12.0 dB9.0 × 10−99.6 dB
    16-QAM20 Mb/s9.0 dB4.4 × 10−313.4 dB
    64-QAM30 Mb/s7.2 dB6.4 × 10−217.8 dB
  6. Evaluation. At this SNR 64-QAM makes an error in about every 16th bit, useless even with strong coding; 16-QAM's 4.4 × 10−3 needs heavy coding that eats much of its rate gain; QPSK is nearly error-free. So the claim is false: higher order raises the bandwidth efficiency but lowers the power efficiency, and it pays only when the SNR is high enough.
  7. When it is true. If S/N0 rises by about 10.6 dB (to 92.6 dB-Hz, a phone close to the router), 64-QAM reaches its 17.8 dB at 30 Mb/s and triples the throughput in the same band. That is why real systems use adaptive modulation: they measure the SNR and choose QPSK, 16-QAM, 64-QAM or higher accordingly.
  8. Other limits. Higher-order QAM also needs a more linear amplifier (higher peak-to-average power), a cleaner oscillator (phase noise rotates closely spaced points across boundaries) and finer converters.
  9. Closing check. Eb/N0 falls by 10 log 2 = 3.0 dB from QPSK to 16-QAM and by 10 log 1.5 = 1.8 dB more to 64-QAM, matching 12.0 → 9.0 → 7.2 dB.

Glossary

Antipodal signals
Two signals that are negatives of each other, such as the two BPSK waveforms.
AWGN
Additive white Gaussian noise, with power spectral density N0/2 at all frequencies.
BT product
The Gaussian filter's 3 dB bandwidth times the bit time in GMSK.
Chip
One bit of a spreading code, of duration Tc.
Coherence bandwidth
The frequency range over which the channel gain stays nearly constant.
Coherence time
The time over which the channel gain stays nearly constant.
Coherent detection
Detection with a local carrier locked in frequency and phase to the received one.
Continuous-phase FSK
FSK whose phase does not jump at bit boundaries.
Correlator
Multiplies the received signal by a reference waveform and integrates over a symbol; equivalent to a matched filter sampled at the symbol end.
Cyclic prefix
A copy of the end of an OFDM symbol placed before it to absorb echoes.
Decision region
The set of received points nearer to one constellation point than to any other.
Delay spread
The time between the first and last significant echoes of a multipath channel.
Diversity
Sending or receiving a symbol over independently fading paths, then combining them.
Eb/N0
Energy per bit over one-sided noise density; the fair measure for comparing schemes.
Envelope detector
A circuit that outputs the slowly varying amplitude of a bandpass signal.
Gray labelling
A bit mapping in which neighbouring constellation points differ in one bit.
Matched filter
A filter with impulse response s(T − t); it maximises the output signal-to-noise ratio at t = T in white noise.
Modulation index (FSK)
h = 2Δf/Rb, the tone spacing divided by the bit rate; 0.5 for MSK.
Near-far problem
In CDMA, a strong nearby user swamping a weak distant one; cured by power control.
Non-coherent detection
Detection that does not need the carrier phase, using envelopes or phase differences.
Orthogonal signals
Signals whose product integrates to zero over a symbol.
PAPR
Peak-to-average power ratio; high in OFDM and QAM, it forces the amplifier to back off.
Passband signal
A signal whose spectrum sits around a carrier frequency fc rather than around zero.
PN sequence
A deterministic binary sequence with noise-like properties, made by a shift register with feedback.
Processing gain
The spreading factor of a spread-spectrum system: chips per bit in DSSS, hop channels in FHSS.
Rayleigh fading
Fading of a channel with many scattered paths and no direct one; the amplitude follows a Rayleigh distribution.
Signal constellation
The set of symbol points plotted in signal space.
Subcarrier
One of the many narrowband carriers of an OFDM symbol.
Symbol
One waveform chosen from a finite set and sent for Ts seconds; it carries k = log2 M bits.
