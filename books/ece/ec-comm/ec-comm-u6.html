6.1 ASK and FSK: generation and detection

Digital passband modulation puts bits onto a carrier that can travel through a cable or air. Binary ASK changes carrier amplitude; this chapter studies the zero-level form, OOK: bit 1 is a carrier burst and bit 0 is silence. Binary ASK can also use two nonzero amplitudes. BFSK changes carrier frequency between two tones. The receiver knows the bit clock and uses a matched filter or correlator to decide each bit in noise. These schemes are simple and cheap, so they appear in remote keyless entry, RFID readers, low-rate telemetry and older telephone modems. This chapter shows generation, coherent detection, bandwidth and bit error probability for binary ASK and binary FSK.

You need: Unit 1 complex envelope and AWGN, and Unit 5 matched filter and signal space (see Unit 5, Matched filter and correlation receiver).

Binary ASK and on-off keying

Binary ASK changes carrier amplitude between two levels. This chapter studies the zero-level form, OOK: bit 1 is a carrier burst and bit 0 is silence. Binary ASK can also use two nonzero amplitudes. The passband signal is

s(t) = A b(t) cos(2πfct)

A is carrier amplitude in volts; b(t) is 1 or 0 during a bit; fc is carrier frequency in hertz; Tb is bit duration in seconds.

Generation uses a multiplier (balanced modulator) or a switch. The NRZ data has levels 0 and 1. The switch passes the carrier for 1 and blocks it for 0; a multiplier gives cleaner multiplication.

Coherent detection multiplies the received signal by 2cos(2πfct) and integrates over one bit Tb. For bit 1 the correlator output is A Tb; for bit 0 it is 0. The threshold is A Tb/2. The factor 2 cancels the 1/2 from cos².

For equiprobable bits, peak carrier power is A²/2 W, average signal power is A²/4 W, and average bit energy is Eb = A²Tb/4 J. The null-to-null bandwidth is 2Rb, where Rb = 1/Tb is the bit rate.

BFSK: two tones

BFSK sends bit 1 as frequency f1 (mark) and bit 0 as f0 (space), both with amplitude A:

s<sub>1</sub>(t) = A cos(2πf<sub>1</sub>t), s<sub>0</sub>(t) = A cos(2πf<sub>0</sub>t)

f1 is mark frequency; f0 is space frequency; both in hertz; A is amplitude in volts.

Generation uses a voltage-controlled oscillator whose frequency shifts with the input voltage, or two switched oscillators. Switching can cause phase jumps; continuous-phase FSK avoids them, as in MSK (see 6.6).

Coherent detection uses two correlators, one at f0 and one at f1. Each multiplies by 2cos and integrates over Tb. The larger output wins.

Tones are coherently orthogonal over Tb when the spacing is n/(2Tb) for integer n; the minimum is 1/(2Tb) = Rb/2. Noncoherent detection needs spacing 1/Tb (see 6.3).

Δf = |f<sub>1</sub> − f<sub>0</sub>|/2, h = 2Δf/R<sub>b</sub>, B = 2(Δf + R<sub>b</sub>)

Δf is frequency deviation in hertz; Rb = 1/Tb is bit rate in bits per second; h is modulation index; B is Carson bandwidth in hertz.

Spectra and error probability

The ASK power spectral density has a carrier line and sinc² lobes around fc. Its first nulls are at fc ± Rb, so the null-to-null bandwidth is 2Rb. The BFSK spectrum is the sum of two such lobes at f0 and f1; Carson bandwidth 2(Δf + Rb) contains most of the power.

For coherent OOK and coherent orthogonal BFSK in AWGN, the bit error probability is

P<sub>b</sub> = Q(√(E<sub>b</sub>/N<sub>0</sub>))

Pb is bit error probability; Eb is average bit energy in joules; N0 is one-sided noise power spectral density in W/Hz; Q(x) = ∫x∞ (1/√(2π)) e−u²/2 du.

For BFSK Eb = A²Tb/2. For equiprobable OOK average Eb = A²Tb/4. BFSK is constant envelope, so a nonlinear power amplifier can be used. OOK average power is half its peak.

Worked example

Question. A binary FSK transmitter sends data at Rb = 10 kbps. The mark frequency is 100 kHz and the space frequency is 110 kHz. Find the frequency deviation Δf, the modulation index h, the Carson bandwidth, and the minimum coherent tone spacing for orthogonality. State whether the actual tones are orthogonal.

  1. Bit duration Tb = 1/Rb = 1/(10×10³) = 100×10⁻⁶ s = 100 µs.
  2. Tone spacing = |110 − 100| kHz = 10 kHz. Frequency deviation Δf = spacing/2 = 5 kHz = 5000 Hz.
  3. Modulation index h = 2Δf/Rb = (2×5 kHz)/(10 kbps) = 10/10 = 1.
  4. Carson bandwidth B = 2(Δf + Rb) = 2(5 + 10) kHz = 30 kHz = 30,000 Hz.
  5. Minimum coherent spacing = 1/(2Tb) = Rb/2 = 10 kbps/2 = 5 kHz = 5000 Hz.
  6. Actual spacing = 10 kHz = 2 × 5 kHz = 2(Rb/2), so n = 2. Therefore the tones are orthogonal. Being merely above the minimum does not guarantee orthogonality; the spacing must equal nRb/2 for a positive integer n.

Answer. Δf = 5 kHz, h = 1, Carson bandwidth = 30 kHz, minimum coherent spacing = 5 kHz; actual spacing 10 kHz = 2(Rb/2), n = 2, so the tones are orthogonal.

Exam traps

  • In B = 2(Δf + Rb), Rb is bits per second, not Tb seconds. Substituting Tb breaks the units.
  • Δf is half the tone spacing. For 100 kHz and 110 kHz, Δf = 5 kHz, not 10 kHz. Then h = 2Δf/Rb.
  • BFSK needs two tones. Coherent orthogonality requires spacing = nRb/2 for positive integer n (minimum Rb/2 when n = 1); noncoherent orthogonality requires spacing = nRb (minimum Rb when n = 1). Zero spacing is not orthogonal. A spacing merely above the minimum is not automatically orthogonal.
  • For equiprobable OOK, average power is A²/4 W, half the peak A²/2 W. Using peak power doubles Eb and gives the wrong Q argument.
  • Coherent detection needs a phase-locked local carrier. Envelope detection is noncoherent; see 6.3.

6.2 BPSK: generation, detection and error probability

BPSK carries one bit in the phase of a carrier. The transmitter sends cos(2πfct) for a 1 and its negative for a 0, so the two signals are exact opposites. This antipodal pair gives the smallest binary error probability in white Gaussian noise. BPSK appears in GPS navigation, deep-space links and low-rate control channels where power matters more than bandwidth.

You need Unit 5 on matched filters, signal space and AWGN error analysis, plus 6.1 for binary passband signals.

Waveform and generation

BPSK sends one bit per symbol. For bit 1 the transmitter sends a carrier with phase 0; for bit 0 it sends the same carrier with phase π. The two waveforms are negatives of each other.

s(t) = ±√(2E<sub>b</sub>/T<sub>b</sub>) cos(2πf<sub>c</sub>t), 0 ≤ t < T<sub>b</sub>

Eb is the bit energy in joules, Tb is the bit duration in seconds, fc is the carrier frequency in hertz, and the plus sign sends bit 1 while the minus sign sends bit 0.

The amplitude √(2Eb/Tb) is not arbitrary. Squaring s(t) and integrating over one bit gives Eb. The factor 2 cancels the average of cos² over a whole number of cycles.

  1. Convert the input bits to bipolar NRZ: bit 1 becomes +1, bit 0 becomes −1.
  2. Multiply this bipolar waveform by the carrier A cos(2πfct), where A = √(2Eb/Tb).
  3. The product is the BPSK signal. A data change from +1 to −1 flips the carrier phase by 180°.

The multiplier is a balanced modulator. The output has constant envelope, so a nonlinear power amplifier can be used. Some syllabi call BPSK phase reversal keying (PRK).

Coherent detection and the constellation

The receiver must know the carrier phase. It multiplies the received signal by a local carrier and integrates over one bit.

y = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>T<sub>b</sub></sup> r(t) √(2/T<sub>b</sub>) cos(2πf<sub>c</sub>t) dt = ±√E<sub>b</sub> + n

r(t) is the received signal plus noise, y is the test statistic in volts, Eb is the bit energy, and n is the noise component after integration.

The integrator output is compared with a threshold at zero. If y > 0 the receiver decides bit 1; if y < 0 it decides bit 0. The local carrier must be phase-locked to the incoming carrier. A squaring loop or a Costas loop can recover it.

On a signal-space diagram BPSK has two points on one axis: +√Eb for bit 1 and −√Eb for bit 0. The distance between the points is 2√Eb. The decision boundary is the line through zero, so each point owns a half-plane.

A phase error θ in the local carrier multiplies the useful signal by cos θ. The error probability rises because the effective bit energy falls to Eb cos²θ. 6.3 treats the case where the phase is unknown.

Error probability and bandwidth

In AWGN the bit error probability is set by the distance between the two constellation points.

P<sub>b</sub> = Q(√(2E<sub>b</sub>/N<sub>0</sub>)) = ½ erfc(√(E<sub>b</sub>/N<sub>0</sub>))

Pb is the bit error probability (dimensionless), Eb is the received bit energy in joules, N0 is the one-sided noise power spectral density in watts per hertz, Q is the Gaussian tail probability, and erfc is the complementary error function.

The Q function is Q(x) = (1/√(2π)) ∫x∞ e−u²/2 du. The complementary error function is erfc(x) = (2/√π) ∫x∞ e−u² du. They are related by Q(x) = ½ erfc(x/√2).

With rectangular pulses the BPSK spectrum is a sinc-shaped band around fc. The null-to-null bandwidth is 2Rb, where Rb is the bit rate in bits per second. The spectral efficiency is Rb/(2Rb) = 0.5 bit per second per hertz.

Coherent orthogonal BFSK has Pb = Q(√(Eb/N0)). To reach the same Pb as BPSK, BFSK needs twice the Eb/N0. That is a 3 dB energy advantage for BPSK. The price is that BPSK needs carrier phase recovery.

Worked example

Question. A BPSK link sends data at Rb = 1 Mbps. The received Eb/N0 is 10 dB. (a) Find the bit error probability. (b) Find the expected number of bit errors in 108 transmitted bits.

  1. Eb/N0 = 10 dB = 1010/10 = 10 (linear ratio).
  2. The argument of Q is √(2Eb/N0) = √(2×10) = √20 = 4.472.
  3. From the Q table, Q(4.472) ≈ 3.87×10−6.
  4. Expected errors = 108 × 3.87×10−6 = 387.

Answer. The bit error probability is about 3.87×10−6, so about 387 bit errors are expected in 108 bits.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the factor 2: BPSK uses Q(√(2Eb/N0)), not Q(√(Eb/N0)). The second form belongs to coherent orthogonal BFSK.
  • Using symbol energy Es in place of bit energy Eb. For BPSK one symbol carries one bit, so Es = Eb. Confusing them with QPSK gives wrong numbers.
  • Saying BPSK and QPSK have different bit error probability for the same Eb. With Gray coding they have the same Pb; their symbol error probabilities differ.
  • Taking the null-to-null bandwidth as Rb. For rectangular BPSK it is 2Rb, so the spectral efficiency is 0.5 bps/Hz.
  • Ignoring carrier phase. BPSK detection is coherent; a phase error θ reduces the effective Eb by cos²θ.

6.3 Non-coherent detection and DPSK

Coherent receivers need a carrier phase reference. In many links the phase is unknown or drifts, because the receiver oscillator is not locked to the transmitter. Non-coherent detection avoids phase recovery. For FSK and OOK it uses envelope detectors. For PSK it encodes data in phase changes and detects those changes with a one-bit delay (DPSK). The price is a small loss in error probability. These schemes give simple, robust receivers for low-cost wireless links, optical links and legacy data systems.

You need: 6.1 ASK and FSK, 6.2 BPSK, and the matched-filter and decision-region ideas from Unit 5.

Non-coherent BFSK and OOK

Non-coherent detection means the receiver does not know the carrier phase. It uses only the envelope, the slowly varying amplitude of the bandpass signal. The envelope is non-negative. A phase reversal does not change it.

For binary FSK (BFSK), bit 1 is a tone at f1 and bit 0 is a tone at f2. The receiver has two bandpass filters, one at each frequency. Each filter output goes to an envelope detector. A comparator decides 1 when the f1 envelope is larger and 0 otherwise. The tones must be orthogonal. The minimum spacing for non-coherent orthogonal BFSK is 1/Tb, where Tb is the bit duration in seconds. This is twice the coherent spacing 1/(2Tb).

P<sub>b</sub> = ½ exp(−E<sub>b</sub>/(2N<sub>0</sub>))

Pb is bit error probability; Eb is received energy per bit in joules; N0 is the AWGN power spectral density in W/Hz. This is for orthogonal non-coherent BFSK.

Non-coherent OOK sends bit 1 as a carrier burst and bit 0 as silence. A bandpass filter, envelope detector and threshold recover the bit. The same error form applies, with Eb the energy of the on burst. The threshold must be set from the noise level, not from a phase reference.

  1. Filter the received signal at f1 and at f2.
  2. Envelope-detect each filter output.
  3. Compare the two envelopes at the end of the bit.
  4. Decide the larger envelope; no phase reference is used.

DPSK: data in phase changes

DPSK is differential phase shift keying. Some syllabi call DPSK differential phase shift keying. It avoids absolute phase recovery by making each transmitted phase depend on the current bit and the previous transmitted phase.

d<sub>k</sub> = b<sub>k</sub> ⊕ d<sub>k−1</sub>

bk is the input bit at time k; dk is the encoded bit; dk−1 is the previous encoded bit; ⊕ is XOR. The receiver knows the initial reference d0.

A BPSK modulator maps dk = 1 to phase 180° and dk = 0 to phase 0° (or the opposite convention). With this convention, the phase change from bit k−1 to k is 180° when bk = 1 and 0° when bk = 0. The absolute phase may drift; the change over one bit carries the data.

  1. Split the received signal into a direct path and a delayed path.
  2. Delay one copy by exactly Tb.
  3. Multiply the direct signal by the delayed copy.
  4. Lowpass filter the product.
  5. Threshold the filtered product at the end of the bit to recover the phase change.
P<sub>b</sub> ≈ ½ exp(−E<sub>b</sub>/N<sub>0</sub>)

Pb is bit error probability; Eb is energy per bit in joules; N0 is AWGN power spectral density in W/Hz. This is a high-SNR approximation for DPSK in AWGN.

DPSK is slightly worse than coherent BPSK at the same Eb/N0; coherent BPSK is in 6.2. DPSK needs no carrier recovery, so it tolerates slow phase drift. Fast phase noise changes the phase over one bit and raises errors; treat this effect qualitatively.

Choosing between the simple receivers

The three non-coherent options differ in what they assume and what they cost.

  • Non-coherent BFSK: no phase reference; tone spacing 1/Tb; Pb = ½ exp(−Eb/(2N0)).
  • Non-coherent OOK: no phase reference; one threshold; same Pb form as BFSK.
  • DPSK: no absolute phase; one-bit delay; Pb ≈ ½ exp(−Eb/N0).
  • Coherent BPSK: needs carrier recovery; best binary error probability; see 6.2.

Non-coherent BFSK suits low-cost sensor links and simple telemetry. OOK suits very cheap on-off remote modules. DPSK suits links with phase drift and a simple delay detector, such as some optical and wireless systems. M-ary non-coherent schemes are in 6.5; fading is in 6.9.

In a phone link the phase can spin because of oscillator offset. A non-coherent receiver still works if the phase is constant over one bit; DPSK assumes exactly that.

Worked example

Question. A DPSK system uses the rule dk = bk ⊕ dk−1 with initial d0 = 0. The input bit stream is 1 1 0 1. Find the encoded bits dk and the phase changes if bit 1 gives 180° and bit 0 gives 0°.

  1. d1 = b1 ⊕ d0 = 1 ⊕ 0 = 1.
  2. d2 = b2 ⊕ d1 = 1 ⊕ 1 = 0.
  3. d3 = b3 ⊕ d2 = 0 ⊕ 0 = 0.
  4. d4 = b4 ⊕ d3 = 1 ⊕ 0 = 1.
  5. Encoded bits d = 1 0 0 1.
  6. Phase changes follow b: b = 1 1 0 1 gives 180°, 180°, 0°, 180°.
  7. Decoding is bk = dk ⊕ dk−1.

Answer. Encoded bits d = 1 0 0 1; phase changes are 180°, 180°, 0°, 180°.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting d0: DPSK encoding needs an initial reference bit, fixed and known at the receiver.
  • Using the coherent BPSK formula Q(√(2Eb/N0)) for DPSK. Use Pb ≈ ½ exp(−Eb/N0).
  • Using tone spacing 1/(2Tb) for non-coherent BFSK. Non-coherent orthogonal BFSK needs 1/Tb.
  • Mixing the exponents: non-coherent BFSK and OOK have Eb/(2N0); DPSK has Eb/N0.
  • Reading DPSK phase changes as absolute phases. The data is in the change between adjacent bits, not in one phase alone.

6.4 QPSK and M-ary PSK

QPSK carries two bits per symbol by choosing one of four carrier phases. It is built from two BPSK signals on carriers 90° apart, so it doubles the bit rate of BPSK in the same bandwidth. M-ary PSK pushes the idea further: with M = 2^k phases each symbol carries k bits, the symbol rate falls by a factor k, and the bandwidth falls with it. The price is a smaller spacing between constellation points, so more power is needed for the same error rate. QPSK sits inside satellite links, LTE and Wi-Fi.

You need BPSK and its error probability (6.2) and the correlator view of optimum detection from Unit 5, Optimum detection and probability of error in AWGN.

QPSK: two BPSK links on quadrature carriers

A QPSK symbol lasts Ts seconds and carries two bits, one on each of two carriers. The in-phase carrier is cos(2πfct) and the quadrature carrier is sin(2πfct). They are 90° apart, so a receiver that multiplies by one of them and integrates wipes the other out.

s(t) = A·I·cos(2πf<sub>c</sub>t) − A·Q·sin(2πf<sub>c</sub>t),   0 ≤ t < T<sub>s</sub>

A is the carrier amplitude in volts, fc the carrier frequency in Hz, Ts the symbol duration in seconds, and I and Q are the two bits in bipolar form, each equal to +1 or −1. The minus sign makes the phase equal arctan(Q/I).

The four combinations of (I, Q) give four phases: (+,+) → 45°, (−,+) → 135°, (−,−) → 225°, (+,−) → 315°. The bits are Gray labelled, so points next to each other differ in one bit only.

  1. Split the serial bit stream into two: even-indexed bits go to the I branch, odd-indexed bits to the Q branch.
  2. Convert each stream to bipolar NRZ: bit 1 is +1 and bit 0 is −1, unitless, with any ±1 V levels absorbed into A. Hold each level for Ts.
  3. Multiply the I stream by cos(2πfct) and the Q stream by −sin(2πfct).
  4. Add the two products and send the sum to the antenna, after any pulse shaping.

Each branch runs at half the bit rate, so the symbol rate is Rs = Rb/2 and Ts = 2Tb. With rectangular pulses each branch has a null-to-null width of 2Rs = Rb, so QPSK fits twice the bits of BPSK into the same bandwidth. Some syllabi call QPSK 4-PSK.

Coherent detection and bit error probability

The receiver recovers the carrier, multiplies the received signal by cos(2πfct) and by −sin(2πfct), integrates each product over one symbol, and samples at t = Ts. The two correlator outputs are rI and rQ. A decision device takes the sign of each: positive → bit 1, negative → bit 0. The pair is then read as the Gray label of the nearest constellation point. Because the carriers are orthogonal, the I decision depends only on the I bit plus noise; the Q branch does not leak into it.

P<sub>b</sub> = Q(√(2E<sub>b</sub>/N<sub>0</sub>))

Pb is the bit error probability, Q(x) the Gaussian tail probability, Eb the energy per bit in joules, N0 the one-sided noise power spectral density in W/Hz, and Eb/N0 a pure ratio, usually quoted in dB.

This is exactly the BPSK result at the same Eb, because each QPSK branch is a BPSK system carrying one bit. The symbol energy is Es = 2Eb, and the two branches fail independently, so Ps = 2Pb − Pb² ≈ 2Pb.

A carrier phase error rotates the received constellation relative to the decision axes and causes I/Q crosstalk; small errors shrink the decision margin, and large errors cause slips to a neighbouring point. Offset QPSK, which delays the Q branch by half a symbol, is covered in 6.6.

M-ary PSK: more phases, fewer hertz

In M-ary PSK the carrier takes one of M phases, 2πm/M for m = 0, 1, …, M−1, all with the same amplitude. M = 2^k with k = 1, 2, 3, …; k = 1 is BPSK, and M-ary PSK usually means k ≥ 2. Each symbol carries k = log₂M bits. The symbol rate falls to Rs = Rb/k, and the null-to-null bandwidth of rectangular MPSK is B = 2Rs = 2Rb/k. The bandwidth efficiency is Rb/B = k/2 bps/Hz: 1 bps/Hz for QPSK, 1.5 for 8-PSK, 2 for 16-PSK.

P<sub>s</sub> ≈ 2·Q( √(2E<sub>s</sub>/N<sub>0</sub>) · sin(π/M) )

Ps is the symbol (not bit) error probability, Es = k·Eb the energy per symbol in joules, N0 the noise density in W/Hz, and M the number of phases. The approximation is good for large M and high Es/N0.

Adjacent points sit a distance d = 2√Es·sin(π/M) apart, so as M grows the points crowd together and each symbol needs more energy to keep the same spacing in noise. With Gray labelling a symbol error usually flips one bit, giving Pb ≈ Ps/k. Doubling M adds one bit per symbol and reduces bandwidth per bit by the factor k/(k+1), not by half. Doubling k is what halves the bandwidth per bit. Each extra bit still costs several dB of power.

6.5 M-ary FSK and QAM

M-ary FSK and QAM both send more than one bit per symbol. M-ary FSK picks one of M orthogonal tones; QAM picks one point on a two-dimensional grid of amplitude and phase. The first is simple and constant-envelope but uses wide bandwidth. The second is bandwidth-efficient but needs a linear amplifier and a cleaner channel. Telemetry, some military links, Wi-Fi, digital cable TV and microwave backhaul use these schemes.

You need: 6.1 ASK and FSK, 6.4 QPSK and M-ary PSK, and Unit 5 on signal space and the matched filter.

M-ary FSK: one tone per symbol

M-ary FSK sends k = log₂ M bits by choosing one of M carrier frequencies for T_s seconds. Each chosen tone is one symbol. The symbol rate is R_s = R_b / k, where R_b is the bit rate in bits per second and R_s is the symbol rate in symbols per second. The symbol time is T_s = 1 / R_s.

The M tones must be orthogonal over each symbol. For coherent detection, the minimum tone spacing is Δf = 1 / (2T_s). Then the inner product of any two different tones over T_s is zero. Noncoherent detection needs wider spacing, typically Δf = 1 / T_s.

The receiver is a bank of M matched filters or correlators. Filter i is matched to tone i. At time T_s each filter gives one number. The comparator picks the largest output. That index is the detected symbol, which maps back to k bits. The same idea appears in 6.1 for M = 2.

P_s ≤ (M − 1) Q(√(E_s / N₀))

P_s is the symbol error probability; M is the number of tones; E_s is the energy per symbol in joules; N₀ is the one-sided noise power spectral density in W/Hz; Q(x) is the Gaussian tail probability. This is an elementary union bound for coherent orthogonal MFSK.

MFSK bandwidth grows with M. The M tones occupy a band of order M R_s, so doubling M adds one bit per symbol but roughly doubles bandwidth. MFSK is therefore a power-efficient, bandwidth-expensive scheme. Its constant envelope also suits nonlinear amplifiers.

QAM: amplitude and phase together

Quadrature amplitude modulation, or QAM, sends data on two carriers in quadrature. The passband signal is s(t) = I(t) cos(2πf_c t) − Q(t) sin(2πf_c t), where f_c is the carrier frequency in Hz, and I(t) and Q(t) are the in-phase and quadrature baseband waveforms.

In square M-QAM, I and Q are independent √M-level PAM signals. For 16-QAM, each axis uses levels −3, −1, 1, 3. The point (I, Q) carries k = log₂ M bits. 4-QAM is the same as QPSK. 16-QAM and 64-QAM are common in Wi-Fi and cable modems.

  1. Split the incoming bit stream into k-bit groups. Each group is one symbol.
  2. Divide the k bits into two groups for the I and Q axes.
  3. Map each group to a PAM level. For 16-QAM, use the four levels −3, −1, 1, 3.
  4. Multiply the I level by cos(2πf_c t) and the Q level by sin(2πf_c t) in two balanced modulators.
  5. Subtract the Q product from the I product and transmit the difference, I cos(2πf_c t) − Q sin(2πf_c t).

The coherent receiver uses two correlators, one with cos and one with sin. It recovers I and Q, then applies multilevel threshold decisions. For 16-QAM the I thresholds are at −2, 0 and 2; the Q thresholds are the same. A decision error on either axis can flip one or more bits.

P_s ≈ 4(1 − 1/√M) Q(√(3E_s / ((M − 1)N₀)))

P_s is the symbol error probability for square QAM; M is the constellation size; E_s is the average symbol energy in joules; N₀ is the one-sided noise power spectral density in W/Hz. The bit error probability is P_b ≈ P_s / log₂ M.

With rectangular pulses, the baseband null-to-null bandwidth is R_s, so the passband null-to-null bandwidth is B = 2R_s. The Nyquist minimum R_s / 2 needs ideal sinc pulses with zero excess bandwidth, which cannot be built. The bandwidth efficiency is R_b / B = k / 2 bits per second per hertz. For 16-QAM, k = 4, so the efficiency is 2 bps/Hz.

Choosing between MFSK and QAM

MFSK and QAM trade different resources. MFSK spends bandwidth to gain power efficiency and a constant envelope. QAM spends power and linearity to gain bandwidth efficiency. The table compares them for the same bit rate and the same M.

QAM fails badly if the amplifier compresses peaks. The outer constellation points move inward, so the received point lands in a wrong decision region. MFSK does not have this problem because every tone has the same envelope. The price is a wide band and many filters or correlators in the receiver.

A system designer chooses QAM when spectrum is costly and the channel has good signal-to-noise ratio. The designer chooses MFSK when the channel is poor, the amplifier is nonlinear, or noncoherent detection is needed.

6.6 MSK and GMSK

MSK sends bits as two frequencies that are only half a bit rate apart, with phase that never jumps. That makes the envelope constant and the spectrum tidy. GMSK passes the data through a Gaussian lowpass filter before the MSK modulator. The filter narrows the spectrum further but smears each bit into its neighbours. GSM uses GMSK; Bluetooth and some satellite links use related schemes. MSK and GMSK matter when a cheap nonlinear amplifier must send a compact spectrum.

You need: 6.1 FSK for tone spacing and 6.4 QPSK for the I/Q view.

MSK: continuous-phase FSK with h = 0.5

MSK is continuous-phase FSK with modulation index 0.5. Let Rb be the bit rate in bits per second and Tb = 1/Rb the bit duration in seconds. The two transmitted frequencies are fc − Δf and fc + Δf, where fc is the carrier in Hz and Δf is the peak frequency deviation in Hz. The modulation index is h = 2Δf/Rb.

h = 2Δf / R<sub>b</sub>,  2Δf = h R<sub>b</sub>

h is the modulation index (dimensionless); Δf is the peak frequency deviation in Hz; Rb is the bit rate in bit/s.

For MSK h = 0.5. Then 2Δf = Rb/2, so the tone spacing is Rb/2 and Δf = Rb/4. The phase changes by ±πh = ±π/2 over each bit. That phase never jumps at a bit boundary: it is continuous. The envelope stays constant. The spacing is the smallest that keeps the two tones orthogonal over one bit; some syllabi call MSK fast FSK.

I/Q view, generation and detection

MSK has an I/Q form:

s(t) = A [ I(t) cos(πt/(2T<sub>b</sub>)) cos(2πf<sub>c</sub>t) − Q(t) sin(πt/(2T<sub>b</sub>)) sin(2πf<sub>c</sub>t) ]

s(t) is the transmitted signal; A is the amplitude; fc is the carrier in Hz; Tb is the bit duration in s; I(t) and Q(t) are ±1 data streams offset by Tb; the sine and cosine factors are half-sinusoid pulse weights of length 2Tb.

This is OQPSK with sinusoidal pulse weighting. The I and Q streams are offset by Tb, so their transitions do not coincide.

  • VCO generation: the data drives a voltage-controlled oscillator between fc − Δf and fc + Δf. The oscillator phase is continuous by construction.
  • I/Q generation: split the bits into even and odd streams, delay Q by Tb, multiply by the half-sine weights, modulate cos and sin carriers, and add.

Coherent detection mixes the received signal with cos and sin carriers, lowpass filters the two outputs, samples at the bit instants, and decides I and Q. A limiter-discriminator can detect MSK non-coherently, but coherent I/Q detection gives better error performance. The constant envelope allows a nonlinear power amplifier.

GMSK: Gaussian filter and the BT product

GMSK adds a Gaussian lowpass filter before the MSK modulator. The filter has a 3 dB bandwidth B Hz. With bit duration Tb s, the BT product is BT = B Tb. For a given BT, B = (BT)/Tb = (BT) Rb.

BT = B Tb, B = (BT) Rb

B is the Gaussian filter 3 dB bandwidth in Hz; Tb is the bit duration in s; Rb = 1/Tb is the bit rate in bit/s; BT is dimensionless.

Smaller BT gives a narrower spectrum and lower sidelobes. It also gives a longer filter impulse response, so each bit leaks into its neighbours and ISI grows. BT = 0.3 is a common value. The Gaussian pulse is not a Nyquist pulse, so the samples at the receiver are not ISI-free.

GMSK keeps a constant envelope because the filter is before the phase modulator. The filter does not increase the data rate by itself. It trades bandwidth for ISI. A receiver may use matched filters and symbol-by-symbol decisions, or a Viterbi/MLSE detector to handle the ISI.

Worked example

  • Saying a smaller BT raises the data rate or lowers error probability. Smaller BT narrows the spectrum but adds ISI.
  • Using B as the bit rate. B is the Gaussian filter 3 dB bandwidth; B = BT × Rb.

6.7 Signal constellations and bandwidth efficiency

A signal constellation is a drawing of every waveform a modulator can send, plotted as points in a two-dimensional signal space built from two orthonormal basis functions. That one picture answers two exam questions at once: how far apart the points are, which sets the error probability, and how many points there are, which sets bits per symbol and bandwidth efficiency. Every passband modem, from BPSK on a satellite link to 1024-QAM in Wi-Fi, is designed on this picture.

You need: signal-space vectors and the orthonormal basis from Unit 5, plus the BPSK, QPSK and QAM modulators of 6.2, 6.4 and 6.5.

Waveforms become points

A passband symbol lasts T_s seconds at carrier frequency f_c. Two orthonormal basis functions carry all of it: φ₁(t) = √(2/T_s)·cos(2πf_c t) and φ₂(t) = −√(2/T_s)·sin(2πf_c t), defined for 0 ≤ t < T_s. Each has unit energy, and their product integrates to zero over one symbol.

Any symbol waveform is s_i(t) = s_i1·φ₁(t) + s_i2·φ₂(t). The pair (s_i1, s_i2) plots as one point on the I–Q plane: I is the in-phase axis, Q the quadrature axis. Symbol energy is E_si = s_i1² + s_i2², the squared length of that vector. Averaged over M equally likely symbols, E_s = (1/M)·Σ E_si.

The receiver correlates the incoming signal with both basis functions and gets a noisy point r = (r₁, r₂). It decides for the nearest constellation point. All received points closer to s_i than to any other symbol form the decision region of s_i; these regions are the Voronoi cells of the constellation.

d_ij = ||s_i − s_j|| = √((s_i1 − s_j1)² + (s_i2 − s_j2)²)

d_ij is the Euclidean distance between symbols i and j; s_i1, s_i2 are the I and Q components of symbol i (volts or √joule, depending on normalization).

Minimum distance decides the errors

The smallest of those distances is the minimum Euclidean distance, d_min = min over i≠j of d_ij. It is the one number that sets error probability: a symbol is misread when noise pushes the received point across the nearest decision boundary, which sits d_min/2 away.

P_e ≤ N_nb · Q(d_min / √(2N₀))

N_nb is the number of nearest neighbours of a point; N₀/2 is the two-sided power spectral density of the AWGN; Q(x) = (1/√(2π))·∫ₓ^∞ e^(−u²/2) du is the Gaussian tail probability.

BPSK has two points at ±√E_b, so d_min = 2√E_b and P_b = Q(√(2E_b/N₀)), the exact result of 6.2. QPSK has four points at (±√(E_s/2), ±√(E_s/2)), so d_min = √(2E_s) = 2√E_b, because E_s = 2E_b. In terms of E_b it is the same distance as BPSK, hence the same P_b, but it carries two bits per symbol.

16-QAM takes I and Q from ±a, ±3a. Then E_s = 10a² and d_min = 2a, so d_min² = 0.4E_s = 1.6E_b. Against 4E_b for BPSK the ratio is 2.5, which is 4 dB. That is the price of four bits per symbol.

Size sets the bits and the bandwidth

Constellation size M sets the bits per symbol k = log₂ M, and the bit rate R_b = k·R_s, where R_s = 1/T_s is the symbol rate in symbols per second (baud). Bandwidth efficiency is η = R_b/B, where B is the occupied channel bandwidth in hertz. It is bits per second per hertz, never bits per symbol.

With rectangular pulses the baseband envelope is a sinc whose first null is at R_s; after modulation the passband main lobe is 2R_s wide, so B = 2R_s and η = k·R_s/(2R_s) = k/2. With ideal Nyquist shaping (sinc pulses, roll-off zero) the passband bandwidth is only R_s, giving η = k.

Schemes on the power–bandwidth plane

Plot η across and required E_b/N₀ up the side. That plane shows the trade directly: moving right means more bits per hertz, moving down means less power per bit.

QAM and PSK sit on the bandwidth-efficient side, reaching η = k/2 with rectangular pulses. The power bill is steep: at the same P_b, going from QPSK to 16-QAM to 64-QAM costs about 4 dB from QPSK to 16-QAM and about 4.5 dB from 16-QAM to 64-QAM, because d_min² drops from 4E_b to 1.6E_b to 0.57E_b. A 256-QAM Wi-Fi link therefore runs only when the phone is close to the router.

MFSK sits on the other side. Its M orthogonal tones need bandwidth that grows with M, so η falls as k rises, but its d_min grows with M, so the required E_b/N₀ falls. Deep-space and low-power telemetry links choose it.

The Shannon limit is the boundary of this plane: η ≤ log₂(1 + SNR), which rewritten per bit gives E_b/N₀ ≥ (2^η − 1)/η, with a floor of −1.59 dB as η → 0. It marks what is impossible, not how to build a modem.

  3. QPSK: k = log₂4 = 2 bits/symbol. R_b = 2 × 5 × 10⁶ = 10 Mbps. η = 10 × 10⁶ / (10 × 10⁶) = 1 bps/Hz.

  5. Power check: d_min² = 4E_b for BPSK and QPSK, but 1.6E_b for 16-QAM. The ratio 4/1.6 = 2.5 is 10·log₁₀2.5 ≈ 4 dB, so 16-QAM needs about 4 dB more E_b/N₀ for the same P_b.

Exam traps

  • Do not use the baseband Nyquist bandwidth R_s/2 for a passband channel. The passband minimum is R_s, so η = k with ideal shaping and only k/2 with a rectangular pulse.
  • η is bits per second per hertz, not bits per symbol. QPSK carries 2 bits per symbol but has η = 1 bps/Hz on a rectangular-pulse channel.
  • Compare schemes at the same E_b/N₀, not the same E_s/N₀. 16-QAM at the same E_s as QPSK has 3 dB less energy per bit, because E_s = 2E_b for QPSK but E_s = 4E_b for 16-QAM, so E_b halves; its P_b is far worse. The 6 dB figure applies only when 16-QAM is compared with BPSK at the same E_s.
  • d_min is a distance; d_min² is what most tables list. The Q-function argument uses d_min/√(2N₀), not d_min².
  • QPSK is not noisier than BPSK. At equal E_b/N₀ the two have the same P_b; QPSK simply carries twice the bits in the same bandwidth.

6.8 Spread spectrum: DSSS and frequency hopping

Spread spectrum takes a narrowband signal and deliberately spreads it over a much wider band using a fast pseudorandom code. The receiver knows the code and collapses the signal back, while interference stays spread. This gives resistance to jamming, hiding in noise, and multiple users in one band. DSSS multiplies data by a code; FHSS hops the carrier. GPS, Bluetooth and CDMA use these ideas.

You need: BPSK from 6.2 and noise and random processes from Unit 1.

PN code, chips and processing gain

A pseudorandom (PN) sequence is a binary sequence that looks random but repeats after a fixed length. A shift register with feedback generates it. In spread spectrum the PN sequence runs much faster than the data. Each PN bit is called a chip. One data bit is multiplied by many chips.

G_p = R_c / R_b    and    G_p(dB) = 10 log10(R_c / R_b)

G_p is the processing gain, unitless; R_c is the chip rate in chips per second; R_b is the data bit rate in bits per second; G_p(dB) is the processing gain in decibels.

G_p is the number of chips per data bit. A larger G_p spreads the signal wider, needs more bandwidth, and suppresses interference better. A PN code should have a sharp autocorrelation peak at zero shift and small cross-correlation with other codes.

Typical processing gains run from tens to thousands. The spreading does not change the data rate; it changes the bandwidth occupied and the receiver's ability to reject unwanted signals.

DSSS: multiply, modulate, despread

Direct-sequence spread spectrum (DSSS) multiplies the bipolar data d(t) by a PN code c(t). Both take values +1 and −1. The product d(t)c(t) is then BPSK modulated.

s(t) = A d(t) c(t) cos(2π f_c t)

s(t) is the transmitted signal; A is the carrier amplitude; d(t) is the bipolar data at bit rate R_b; c(t) is the PN code at chip rate R_c; f_c is the carrier frequency in hertz.

The receiver multiplies by the same carrier and the same code. Since c²(t)=1, the data bit returns. The receiver integrates over one bit period T_b and decides. The same code and chip timing are required; a wrong code gives near-zero correlation.

A narrowband jammer at the carrier frequency enters the receiver. Multiplication by c(t) spreads the jammer over R_c, so only about R_b/R_c = 1/G_p of its power falls in the data band. This is the processing gain. The jamming margin is the extra jammer power the link can tolerate.

M_j = G_p(dB) − (E_b/N_0)_req(dB)

M_j is the jamming margin in dB; G_p(dB) is the processing gain in dB; (E_b/N_0)_req is the required bit energy to noise density ratio in dB for the target error rate; losses are ignored.

Code acquisition finds the code phase. Tracking keeps chip alignment after acquisition.

Frequency hopping and CDMA

Frequency-hopping spread spectrum (FHSS) keeps data modulation narrowband but changes the carrier frequency. A PN code drives a frequency synthesizer that selects one frequency from a hop set at each hop. The receiver uses the same hop pattern and synthesizer, so it follows the transmitter.

Slow hopping uses one hop for several data symbols. Fast hopping uses several hops within one symbol; the receiver must combine the hops coherently or noncoherently.

FHSS avoids a narrowband jammer because the carrier sits on any one frequency only briefly. A jammer must cover the whole hop band. DSSS spreads each symbol in frequency by the code; FHSS spreads over time by hopping the carrier.

CDMA multiple access lets many DSSS users share one band. Each user has a distinct PN code. The receiver correlates with one code; other users' signals have small cross-correlation and appear as noise. This is code-division multiple access. Capacity analysis is left to advanced courses.

Worked example

Question. A DSSS link sends data at R_b = 9.6 kbps using a PN code at R_c = 1.2288 Mcps. (a) Find the processing gain as a ratio and in dB. (b) The required E_b/N_0 for the target bit error rate is 7 dB. Find the jamming margin in dB, ignoring losses.

  1. G_p = R_c / R_b = 1.2288×10⁶ chips/s ÷ 9.6×10³ bits/s = 128.
  2. G_p(dB) = 10 log10(128) = 10 × 2.1072 = 21.07 dB.
  3. M_j = G_p(dB) − (E_b/N_0)_req = 21.07 dB − 7 dB = 14.07 dB.

Answer. The processing gain is 128, or about 21.07 dB; the jamming margin is about 14.07 dB.

Exam traps

  • Chip rate is not bit rate. R_c counts chips per second and is larger than R_b by the processing gain.
  • Processing gains add in dB. Do not add linear ratios and then convert unless you are multiplying them.
  • Jamming margin subtracts the required E_b/N_0 in dB: M_j = G_p(dB) − (E_b/N_0)_req. A common slip is to add them.
  • Despreading needs the same PN code and chip timing. A different code, or a code delayed by one chip, does not recover the data.
  • Slow and fast hopping differ by hops per symbol: slow has one hop per several symbols, fast has several hops per symbol.

6.9 OFDM and fading channels

OFDM splits one high-rate data stream into many low-rate streams, each on its own closely spaced subcarrier. The subcarriers are orthogonal over one symbol, so they overlap in frequency without interfering. An IFFT builds the transmitted symbol and an FFT separates the subcarriers at the receiver. A cyclic prefix absorbs delay spread, making a frequency-selective channel look flat on each subcarrier. OFDM is the base of LTE and Wi-Fi, where it is called OFDMA when subcarriers are shared among users.

You need: QPSK and M-ary PSK, M-ary QAM, ISI and the Nyquist criterion from Unit 5, and random processes from Unit 1.

One wide band becomes many narrow bands

A high-rate single carrier over a multipath channel has a symbol time shorter than the delay spread. Echoes from buildings and hills then smear one symbol into the next, giving intersymbol interference (ISI). A wideband equalizer can undo this, but it is complex. OFDM takes another route: it sends N low-rate streams in parallel, one on each subcarrier. Each stream has a long useful symbol duration T_u, so the same delay spread is a small fraction of a symbol. The subcarriers are spaced by Δf = 1/T_u and are orthogonal over T_u, so their spectra may overlap without cross-talk. N subcarriers occupy about N Δf hertz. A 20 MHz Wi-Fi channel can carry 64 subcarriers at 312.5 kHz spacing, each much narrower than the channel's coherence bandwidth.

Δf = 1 / T_u

Δf is the subcarrier spacing in hertz; T_u is the useful OFDM symbol duration in seconds; N is the number of subcarriers.

IFFT at the transmitter, FFT at the receiver

The transmitter first maps bits to complex QAM symbols. For one OFDM symbol it collects N such symbols, X[0] to X[N−1], one per subcarrier. An inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT) turns these frequency-domain values into N time samples. The receiver applies a fast Fourier transform (FFT) to return to the frequency domain. The FFT is efficient: N log₂ N operations instead of N² for a direct transform.

  1. Map incoming bits to X[k] using QPSK or QAM; k = 0, 1, ..., N−1.
  2. Compute x[n] by an N-point IFFT of X[k].
  3. Copy the last G time samples to the front as the cyclic prefix; send the N+G samples.
  4. At the receiver, discard the first G samples and take an N-point FFT to get Y[k].
  5. Multiply Y[k] by 1/H[k] to undo the channel on subcarrier k; then QAM-demap.
x[n] = (1/N) Σₖ₌₀ᴺ⁻¹ X[k] e^(j2πkn/N),  n = 0, ..., N−1

x[n] is the nth time sample; X[k] is the complex QAM symbol on subcarrier k; N is the number of subcarriers; j = √−1.

Y[k] = H[k] X[k] + W[k]

Y[k] is the received value on subcarrier k; H[k] is the channel gain on subcarrier k; W[k] is noise on subcarrier k.

After the cyclic prefix is removed, the channel acts as a circular convolution. The FFT turns that into a multiplication on each subcarrier: Y[k] = H[k]X[k] + W[k]. The equalizer is therefore one complex multiply per subcarrier, not a long filter. This is the main reason OFDM is cheap in the receiver.

Cyclic prefix, delay spread and fading

The cyclic prefix (CP) is a copy of the tail of the IFFT output placed before it. It costs extra time but protects the useful symbol. Let G be the CP length in samples and T_s = 1/(N Δf) the sample time. Then T_cp = G T_s and the total symbol time is T = T_u + T_cp. The rule is T_cp > τ_max, where τ_max is the maximum channel delay spread. If this holds, echoes from the previous symbol die inside the CP; after CP removal there is no ISI. If T_cp is too short, ISI and intercarrier interference (ICI) appear.

T = T_u + T_cp

T is the total OFDM symbol time in seconds; T_u is the useful symbol duration; T_cp is the cyclic prefix duration.

OFDM does not delete fading. It changes its shape. A channel is flat if its coherence bandwidth B_c is much larger than the signal bandwidth; all frequencies have nearly the same gain. It is frequency-selective if B_c is smaller than the signal bandwidth; different frequencies fade differently. In OFDM the whole signal may be frequency-selective, but each subcarrier is narrow, so each subcarrier sees almost flat fading and gets one equalizer gain H[k].

Slow and fast fading use time. Coherence time T_c is the time over which the channel stays roughly constant. If T_c is much larger than the OFDM symbol T, fading is slow: H[k] changes over many symbols. If T_c is comparable to or smaller than T, fading is fast and channel estimates go stale. Doppler spread f_d = v f_c / c sets T_c roughly as 1/f_d, where v is speed, f_c is carrier frequency and c is the speed of light. Only a few syllabi treat fading in detail; the exam usually asks for these names and the CP rule.

Practical problems and where OFDM is used

Two practical problems dominate. First, the peak-to-average power ratio (PAPR): many subcarriers adding in phase can give a large peak, so the power amplifier must back off and loses efficiency. Second, synchronisation: the receiver must align its FFT window and carrier frequency; a small frequency error breaks orthogonality and causes ICI. Channel estimation is also needed to find H[k].

  • OFDMA shares groups of subcarriers among users; LTE downlink uses it.
  • Wi-Fi uses OFDM in its high-rate physical layers.
  • The CP is overhead: raw bit rate must use T, not T_u.

Worked example

Question. An OFDM system uses 64 subcarriers in 20 MHz, a 16-sample cyclic prefix, and 64-QAM on every subcarrier. Find the subcarrier spacing, useful symbol duration, cyclic prefix duration, total symbol duration, and uncoded bit rate.

  1. Subcarrier spacing: Δf = B/N = 20 MHz / 64 = 0.3125 MHz = 312.5 kHz.
  2. Useful symbol duration: T_u = 1/Δf = 1/(312.5 kHz) = 3.2 μs.
  3. Sample time: T_s = 1/B = 1/(20 MHz) = 50 ns.
  4. Cyclic prefix duration: T_cp = 16 × T_s = 16 × 50 ns = 800 ns = 0.8 μs.
  5. Total symbol duration: T = T_u + T_cp = 3.2 μs + 0.8 μs = 4.0 μs.
  6. 64-QAM carries log₂ 64 = 6 bits per subcarrier. Bits per OFDM symbol = 64 × 6 = 384 bits.
  7. Uncoded bit rate: R_b = 384 bits / 4.0 μs = 96 × 10⁶ bit/s = 96 Mbps.

Answer. The system has Δf = 312.5 kHz, T_u = 3.2 μs, T_cp = 0.8 μs, T = 4.0 μs, and an uncoded bit rate of 96 Mbps.

Exam traps

  • Using T_u instead of T = T_u + T_cp in the bit-rate calculation; the cyclic prefix is overhead and lowers the raw rate.
  • Saying OFDM removes fading. It removes ISI when the CP is long enough; fading remains as a flat gain H[k] on each subcarrier.
  • Confusing subcarrier spacing with the total symbol rate. Δf = 1/T_u, but the transmitted OFDM symbol lasts T = T_u + T_cp.
  • Making the cyclic prefix shorter than the channel delay spread. The CP must be longer than τ_max to avoid ISI and ICI.
  • Forgetting the factor N or log₂ M in bits per OFDM symbol: bits per symbol = N log₂ M for M-QAM on every subcarrier.

Takeaways

  1. ASK and FSK map bits to amplitude or frequency; coherent detection with matched filters or correlators needs carrier phase, while non-coherent detection avoids it.
  2. BPSK is the best binary scheme in AWGN because antipodal signals have the largest distance for fixed bit energy, with P_b=Q(√(2E_b/N0)).
  3. DPSK trades a small error penalty for no absolute phase recovery, which helps when carrier phase wanders.
  4. QPSK sends two bits per symbol and, with Gray coding, has the same bit error probability as BPSK at the same E_b/N0.
  5. MFSK and QAM offer opposite trade-offs: MFSK spends bandwidth to gain power efficiency, while QAM spends power to gain bandwidth efficiency.
  6. MSK keeps phase continuous and the envelope constant; GMSK filters data to reduce out-of-band spectrum at the cost of ISI.
  7. Constellation minimum distance controls error probability, while constellation size controls bits per symbol and bandwidth efficiency.
  8. DSSS and FHSS spread a signal with a pseudorandom code to resist interference, and OFDM turns a frequency-selective channel into parallel flat subchannels using IFFT/FFT and a cyclic prefix.

Apply An OFDM system has 64 subcarriers and useful symbol time 3.2 μs. Find subcarrier spacing and choose a cyclic prefix for a channel delay spread of 0.8 μs. Explain the choice. 10 marks

Subcarrier spacing Δf=1/T_u=1/(3.2 μs)=312.5 kHz; Cyclic prefix length must exceed 0.8 μs; choose 0.8–1.0 μs with margin; Cyclic prefix prevents inter-block interference; Overhead is T_cp/(T_u+T_cp); with 0.8 μs it is 20%; Longer prefix gives more protection but lower efficiency.

Apply A DSSS system uses 127 chips per bit and BPSK chips. Find processing gain in dB and explain how it helps against a narrowband interferer. 10 marks

Processing gain G_p=10 log10(127)=21.0 dB; DSSS spreads the data over a wide band; Despreading adds the desired signal coherently; A narrowband interferer is spread and then filtered; Jamming margin and near-far effects limit practical gain.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare BPSK, QPSK, 16-PSK, and 16-QAM at equal average bit energy. Discuss error probability, spectrum, and suitability for a power-limited versus bandwidth-limited link. 15 marks

At equal E_b/N0, BPSK and Gray QPSK have the same BER; 16-PSK has smaller angular spacing than 16-QAM at equal average energy; Bandwidth efficiency rises with constellation size; Power efficiency falls as minimum distance shrinks; Power-limited links favour BPSK/QPSK; bandwidth-limited links favour QAM.

Analyze Explain why MSK has constant envelope and continuous phase, and evaluate the GMSK filter trade-off between spectral containment and ISI in a nonlinear link. 13 marks

MSK is CPFSK with h=0.5 and phase continuity; Tone separation 1/(2T_b) gives orthogonal signalling; Constant envelope suits nonlinear amplifiers; GMSK Gaussian filter reduces spectral sidelobes; Filtering adds ISI; BT product trades spectrum against eye closure.

Evaluate A frequency-selective channel has delay spread comparable to the symbol time. Compare a single-carrier QAM link with an OFDM link using a cyclic prefix; discuss equalization, PAPR, and synchronization. 15 marks

Single-carrier QAM suffers ISI and needs an equalizer; OFDM converts the channel into flat subcarriers with one-tap equalizers; Cyclic prefix longer than delay spread removes inter-block interference; OFDM has high PAPR and is sensitive to frequency and timing errors; Compare complexity, spectral efficiency, and adaptive loading.

Evaluate Compare DSSS and FHSS for interference rejection and multiple access. Include processing gain, chip rate, hopping rate, and near-far issues. 13 marks

DSSS uses a fast chip code and has processing gain; FHSS hops the carrier and resists partial-band interference; DSSS supports CDMA by code separation; FHSS supports multiple access by hop patterns; Near-far problems are severe in DSSS; synchronization is critical in both.

Evaluate Evaluate the statement: Higher-order QAM always improves performance because it sends more bits per symbol. Use constellation distance and bandwidth efficiency in your answer. 12 marks

Higher-order QAM raises bits per symbol and bandwidth efficiency; At fixed average power, constellation points crowd and d_min falls; Error probability rises unless SNR is increased; Distinguish bandwidth-limited from power-limited operation; Practical limits include linearity, PAPR, phase noise, and converter resolution.

Glossary

AWGN
Additive white Gaussian noise, a model with constant power spectral density N0/2 over all frequencies.
Baseband signal
A signal whose spectrum is centered at zero frequency.
Bit
A binary digit, 0 or 1.
Carrier
A sinusoidal wave at f_c that carries data by amplitude, phase, or frequency changes.
Chip
One pulse of a pseudorandom spreading code; chip rate is usually much larger than bit rate.
Coherence bandwidth
The frequency range over which the channel response is approximately flat.
Coherent detection
Detection that uses a locally recovered carrier with known phase and frequency.
Constellation
The set of signal vectors plotted in an orthonormal signal space.
Correlator
A receiver that multiplies the received signal by a reference waveform and integrates over the symbol interval.
Cyclic prefix
A guard interval made by copying the tail of an OFDM symbol to its start to prevent inter-block interference.
Delay spread
The time spread between the earliest and latest significant multipath arrivals.
DPSK
Differential phase-shift keying, where information is carried by phase changes between successive symbols.
E_b/N0
Bit energy divided by one-sided noise power spectral density; a standard power-efficiency measure.
Envelope detector
A circuit that extracts the slowly varying amplitude of a bandpass signal, used in non-coherent ASK and FSK.
Gray coding
A bit mapping in which adjacent constellation points differ by one bit, reducing bit errors per symbol error.
ISI
Intersymbol interference, the overlap of one symbol pulse with neighbouring symbols after a band-limited channel.
Matched filter
A linear filter matched to the pulse shape that maximizes sampled SNR in AWGN.
Modulation index
In CPFSK, h=2Δf T_b, where Δf is frequency deviation and T_b is bit duration; MSK uses h=0.5.
Non-coherent detection
Detection that does not need the absolute carrier phase, often using envelope or square-law detection.
Orthogonal signals
Signals whose inner product is zero over the symbol interval; they do not interfere in an ideal correlator.
PAPR
Peak-to-average power ratio, a measure of waveform peaks relative to average power.
Passband signal
A signal whose spectrum is centered on a nonzero carrier frequency f_c.
Processing gain
The ratio of spread bandwidth to data bandwidth, often 10 log10(chips per bit) dB.
Signal-to-noise ratio (SNR)
Ratio of signal power to noise power, often expressed per bit as E_b/N0.
Subcarrier
One of many narrowband carriers in OFDM or multicarrier modulation.
Symbol
One transmitted waveform chosen from a finite set; it may carry one or more bits.
