1.1 Circuit elements: resistance, inductance and capacitance

A circuit is a network of three ideal elements: resistance, inductance and capacitance. Each one is completely described by one fixed relation between its voltage and current. That relation tells you what the element does at every instant, including after a long time when nothing changes. A resistor keeps a voltage drop, an inductor becomes a short, a capacitor becomes an open. The same three rules model a heater coil, a transformer winding and the capacitor inside a fan regulator.

Charge, current, voltage and power

Charge Q is quantity of electricity, in coulombs (C). One coulomb is the charge of about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons. Current is the rate of flow of charge: I = dQ/dt, where I is current in amperes (A), Q is charge in coulombs, t is time in seconds. One ampere is one coulomb per second.

V = dW/dQ

V is voltage in volts (V); W is energy in joules (J); Q is charge in coulombs (C). One volt is one joule per coulomb.

Voltage is energy per unit charge. Power is the rate of energy transfer: P = VI, where P is power in watts (W), V is voltage in volts, I is current in amperes. Energy is the integral of power over time, E = ∫P dt, in joules. In a DC circuit the quantities are constant, so E = Pt and I = Q/t for steady flow. These four quantities describe any two-terminal element without knowing what is inside it.

Resistor, inductor, capacitor: one rule each

An ideal resistor obeys v = iR at every instant. Here v is voltage across the resistor in volts, i is current through it in amperes, R is resistance in ohms (Ω). It converts electrical energy to heat and stores none. Its power is P = vi = i²R = v²/R.

An ideal inductor obeys v = L di/dt. L is inductance in henry (H); di/dt is the rate of change of current in A/s. If current is constant, di/dt = 0, so v = 0: in DC steady state the inductor is a short circuit. It stores magnetic energy ½Li².

An ideal capacitor obeys i = C dv/dt. C is capacitance in farad (F); dv/dt is the rate of change of voltage in V/s. If voltage is constant, dv/dt = 0, so i = 0: in DC steady state the capacitor is an open circuit. It stores electric energy ½Cv².

These rules are the whole element. A 5 Ω resistor is not “a resistor plus something else” in the lumped model; its rule is v = 5i. The same applies to L and C. Real components have small extra effects, but the ideal rule is the exam model.

Linear, passive and lumped

An element is linear if its v-i graph is a straight line through the origin. A constant resistance is linear. A filament lamp is non-linear because its resistance rises as it heats. An element is passive if it cannot deliver net energy to the rest of the circuit; R, L and C are passive. Ideal voltage and current sources are active; for those see independent and dependent sources.

In the lumped model, each element’s property is concentrated at one point and the wires are ideal. A real coil has both L and R; a real capacitor has some leakage R. At 50 Hz these extra effects are small, so the lumped model works. At high frequency, a wire itself behaves as a distributed element. Only a few universities teach the lumped/distributed distinction formally.

Worked example

Question. A 10 V DC source is connected in series with a 5 Ω resistor and one unknown element. After a long time the circuit has settled to DC steady state. Find the current I, the voltage V_x across the unknown element and the energy E stored in it when the element is (a) a 5 Ω resistor, (b) a 2 H inductor, (c) a 2 mF capacitor. For (a) also give the power in the unknown resistor.

  1. After a long time the circuit is in DC steady state: all currents and voltages are constant. So di/dt = 0 for any inductor and dv/dt = 0 for any capacitor.
  2. (a) The unknown element is 5 Ω. Total resistance = 5 + 5 = 10 Ω. I = 10/10 = 1 A. V_x = I × 5 = 1 × 5 = 5 V. P = I²R = 1² × 5 = 5 W. A resistor stores no energy: E = 0 J.
  3. (b) An inductor is a short in DC steady state, so V_x = 0 V. The only resistance is 5 Ω. I = 10/5 = 2 A. E = ½Li² = ½ × 2 × 2² = 4 J.
  4. (c) A capacitor is an open in DC steady state, so I = 0 A. No current flows in the 5 Ω resistor, so its drop is 0 V. The full source voltage appears across the capacitor: V_x = 10 V. E = ½Cv² = ½ × 2×10⁻³ × 10² = 0.1 J.

Answer. The results are (a) I = 1 A, V_x = 5 V, P = 5 W, E = 0 J; (b) I = 2 A, V_x = 0 V, E = 4 J; (c) I = 0 A, V_x = 10 V, E = 0.1 J.

Exam traps

  • After a long time, an inductor is a short circuit (0 V), not an open circuit. A capacitor is an open circuit (0 A), not a short circuit.
  • Use i²R or v²/R with the voltage across that resistor, not the source voltage, when other elements are in series.
  • Convert prefixes before calculating: 2 mF = 2 × 10⁻³ F, not 2 × 10⁻⁶ F; 2 H stays 2 H.
  • Do not swap the stored-energy formulas: inductor energy is ½Li², capacitor energy is ½Cv².
  • An ideal resistor stores no energy even though it dissipates power; that power becomes heat.

1.2 Independent and dependent voltage and current sources

A source is the element that pushes energy into a circuit. An ideal voltage source holds its terminal voltage fixed whatever current it supplies; an ideal current source holds its current fixed whatever voltage appears across it. A real battery or phone charger is a practical source: an ideal source plus an internal resistance, so its terminal voltage sags as the load draws more current. A dependent source takes its value from another branch, which is how transistors and op-amps are modelled. Every bias calculation, sensor interface and power-supply design starts by getting the source right.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for the v–i relation of a resistor, which supplies the controlling voltage or current in most dependent-source problems.

Ideal and practical sources

An ideal voltage source holds the same voltage across its terminals whatever current flows through it. Its internal resistance is zero, so the terminal voltage never sags. It is drawn as a circle with + and − inside. An ideal current source holds the same current through itself whatever voltage appears across its terminals. Its internal resistance is infinite. It is drawn as a circle with an arrow inside, and the arrow points the way the current is forced through the source, from − to + inside the source.

On a v–i plot, an ideal voltage source is a vertical line at v = V and an ideal current source is a horizontal line at i = I. Both are straight lines, so both are linear elements and superposition works with them.

A practical source is one ideal source plus one internal resistance. For a voltage source, R_s sits in series with the ideal emf, so the terminal voltage falls as the load current rises.

V_t = E − I R_s

V_t is the terminal voltage in volts, E is the internal emf in volts, I is the load current in amperes (positive when leaving the + terminal), and R_s is the internal resistance in ohms.

A 12 V battery with R_s = 0.5 Ω delivering 2 A shows 12 − 0.5 × 2 = 11 V at its terminals. This is why a car headlamp dims when the starter motor cranks: the same internal resistance drops more volts at the larger current.

Independent and dependent sources

An independent source sets its own value. A 9 V battery, a bench supply set to 5 V, a current source set to 2 mA — the value does not depend on anything else in the circuit. A dependent source, also called a controlled source, takes its value from a voltage or a current somewhere else in the circuit. It has two output terminals like any source, plus a controlling pair. Textbooks draw it as a diamond, not a circle, to warn you that you cannot set it by hand.

These four elements are the only way to write a transistor or an op-amp as a circuit element, so they reappear in every amplifier problem (see Unit 4, Semiconductor Devices and Analog Circuits).

Combining sources

Voltage sources in series carry the same current, so their voltages add algebraically, with signs taken from each + terminal: V_total = V_1 + V_2 + … A 5 V source in series with a 3 V source opposing it gives 2 V.

Current sources in parallel share the same voltage across them, so their currents add with arrow directions: I_total = I_1 + I_2 + … A 4 A source in parallel with a 1 A source pointing the other way gives 3 A.

Two voltage sources in parallel must have equal values. If they differ, Kirchhoff's voltage law round the loop through the two sources gives a non-zero sum round a closed loop, which is impossible. Two current sources in series must also be equal, for the same reason with Kirchhoff's current law at the junction.

Never add a voltage source to a current source. One fixes a voltage and the other fixes a current; they are different kinds of constraint. If you need to swap a practical source for the other kind, that is source transformation, Chapter 1.5.

Worked example

Question. A 5 Ω resistor carries 3 A. A voltage-controlled voltage source of gain μ = 4 senses the voltage across this resistor and drives a 10 Ω load. Find the controlling voltage, the source output voltage, the load current and the power delivered to the load.

  1. The controlling voltage is the voltage across the 5 Ω resistor. By Ohm's law, v_x = I R = 3 A × 5 Ω = 15 V.
  2. The source is a VCVS with gain μ = 4 V/V, so its output is v_out = μ v_x = 4 × 15 V = 60 V.
  3. This 60 V appears across the 10 Ω load. The load current is i_L = v_out / R_L = 60 V / 10 Ω = 6 A.
  4. The power delivered to the load is P = v_out × i_L = 60 V × 6 A = 360 W.

Answer. Controlling voltage 15 V, output voltage 60 V, load current 6 A, load power 360 W.

Exam traps

  • Drawing a dependent source as a circle. The diamond is the examiner's signal that the source is controlled, and that you must write it in terms of v_x or i_x before solving.
  • Treating a dependent source like an independent one during superposition. You can never switch it off on its own; you can only set its controlling voltage or current to zero.
  • Adding two voltage sources in parallel with different values, or two current sources in series with different values. Kirchhoff's laws forbid both; only equal sources may be joined that way.
  • Unit slips in the gain: g is in siemens (A/V), r is in ohms (V/A), while μ and β are dimensionless. Mixing r and g gives an answer off by a factor of ohms squared.
  • Forgetting that the arrow of a current source marks the direction inside the source. Adding a 4 A source and a 1 A source in parallel by simple arithmetic gives 5 A, but opposing arrows give 3 A.

1.3 Ohm's law and Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws

Ohm's law fixes how voltage and current behave inside a resistor. Kirchhoff's current law (KCL) says charge does not pile up at a node. Kirchhoff's voltage law (KVL) says energy gained and lost round a closed loop balance. These three rules turn a DC circuit into simple equations. You meet them in every branch, from a USB charger to a fuse box, and in all the network theorems that follow.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for V-I relations and Chapter 1.2 for source symbols.

Ohm's law and where it stops

Ohm's law: V = IR. V is the voltage across the resistor in volts (V), I is the current through it in amperes (A), and R is the resistance in ohms (Ω). Rearranged, I = V/R and R = V/I.

It holds for a linear resistor at constant temperature. Linear means doubling V doubles I. Bilateral means swapping the terminals reverses I but not the relation. The resistance does not depend on the voltage level or current direction.

Many real parts are not ohmic. A filament lamp heats up; its resistance rises with temperature, so its V-I graph curves. A thermistor is made to change resistance sharply with temperature. For those, use the measured V-I curve, not one fixed R.

P = VI = I²R = V²/R

P is power in watts (W); V is voltage in volts; I is current in amperes; R is resistance in ohms. Use the form that matches the known values.

KCL and KVL with signs

A node is a point where two or more elements meet. A branch is one element between two nodes. A loop is any closed path round the circuit.

Σ I = 0 at a node

I is current leaving the node in amperes. Currents entering count negative. Equivalently, sum of currents entering = sum of currents leaving.

Σ V = 0 round a loop

V is voltage across each element in volts, with a drop counted positive and a rise counted negative. Equivalently, sum of rises = sum of drops.

Sign discipline decides the answer. If the loop passes through a source from − to +, that is a rise; count it negative when drops are positive. If it passes + to −, that is a drop; count it positive.

KCL is charge conservation: charge cannot collect at a node in steady DC. KVL is energy conservation: a charge that returns to its starting node must have the same energy.

Series, parallel, equation count, power check

In series, the same current flows through every resistor. KVL gives V_source = V₁ + V₂ + ... = I(R₁ + R₂ + ...). So R_series = R₁ + R₂ + ... .

In parallel, the same voltage appears across every resistor. KCL gives I = I₁ + I₂ + ... = V(1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ...). So 1/R_parallel = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ... . For two resistors, R_parallel = R₁R₂/(R₁ + R₂). Chapter 1.4 applies these rules to larger networks.

For a circuit with n nodes and b branches, KCL gives n − 1 independent current equations. KVL gives b − n + 1 independent voltage equations. The total is b independent equations, enough for b branch currents. Chapter 1.6 writes this minimum set systematically.

Power balance is the final check. Total power delivered by sources equals total power absorbed by resistors and other elements. If absorbed power is positive, a source delivering power is negative, so ΣP = 0 over the whole circuit.

Worked example

Question. A 20 V DC source drives 2 Ω, 3 Ω and 5 Ω in series. Find the current, the voltage across each resistor, and verify both KVL and power balance.

  1. Step 1: The resistors are in series, so the same current I flows through all three. R_total = 2 Ω + 3 Ω + 5 Ω = 10 Ω.
  2. Step 2: Use Ohm's law for the whole series path. I = V_source / R_total = 20 V / 10 Ω = 2 A.
  3. Step 3: Voltage across each resistor. V₁ = I R₁ = 2 A × 2 Ω = 4 V. V₂ = 2 A × 3 Ω = 6 V. V₃ = 2 A × 5 Ω = 10 V.
  4. Step 4: KVL check. Sum of drops = 4 V + 6 V + 10 V = 20 V, equal to the source rise. With drops positive and source rise negative, −20 + 4 + 6 + 10 = 0.
  5. Step 5: Power supplied by the source. P_source = V_source × I = 20 V × 2 A = 40 W.
  6. Step 6: Power absorbed by resistors. P₁ = I²R₁ = (2 A)² × 2 Ω = 8 W. P₂ = 4 × 3 = 12 W. P₃ = 4 × 5 = 20 W. Total absorbed = 8 + 12 + 20 = 40 W.
  7. Step 7: The supplied power 40 W equals the absorbed power 40 W. Power balance holds.

Exam traps

  • KVL sign slip: passing through a source from − to + is a rise. If you call drops positive, write that source term as negative, not positive.
  • Using V = IR as a fixed law for a lamp or thermistor. Their resistance changes with temperature; use the measured V-I curve.
  • Mixing KCL sign conventions in one equation. Either take all leaving currents as positive, or all entering, but do not mix without changing signs.
  • Writing parallel resistance as an arithmetic average. For parallel, use reciprocals; the product-over-sum shortcut works only for two resistors.
  • Writing all n KCL equations or all b KVL equations. Use n − 1 independent KCL equations and b − n + 1 independent KVL equations.

1.4 Series-parallel circuits; voltage and current divider rules

Real circuits mix series branches and parallel branches. Elements in series share one current; elements in parallel share one voltage. Reduction folds the whole network into a single resistance, finds the source current from Ohm's law, then unfolds step by step with the voltage and current divider rules to reach any branch quantity. An electrician tracing a faulty branch of house wiring, or an engineer reading a sensor bridge, uses exactly this. Some syllabi call the topic network reduction techniques.

You need Chapter 1.1 for element values and Chapter 1.3 for Ohm's law and the KVL and KCL proofs behind both divider rules.

Series and parallel: one current, one voltage

Two elements are in series when the same current must pass through both — they sit on one path with no junction between them. They are in parallel when both connect across the same two nodes, so both see the same voltage.

R_s = R1 + R2 + … + Rn

R_s is the total series resistance in ohms (Ω); R1 … Rn are the individual resistances in ohms. The same current I = V/R_s flows through every element, and the drops add to V by KVL.

Parallel branches add as reciprocals.

1/R_p = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + … + 1/Rn

R_p is the equivalent parallel resistance in ohms; each R is a branch resistance in ohms. For exactly two resistors, R_p = R1R2/(R1+R2), the product over the sum.

Conductance G = 1/R is measured in siemens (S); 1 S = 1 A/V. It adds directly: G_p = G1 + G2, in siemens. That is why adding a branch always lowers the equivalent resistance and raises the source current.

  • R_p is always smaller than the smallest branch resistance.
  • n equal resistors R in parallel give R/n; two equal resistors give R/2.
  • Convert kΩ to Ω before combining — never add 4 Ω to 12 kΩ.

Voltage divider and current divider rules

Two series resistors across a source V carry the same current I = V/(R1+R2). Ohm's law on R1 gives the voltage divider.

V1 = V × R1/(R1 + R2)

V1 is the voltage across R1 in volts; V is the source voltage in volts; R1 and R2 are in ohms. The bigger resistor takes the bigger share.

Two parallel resistors carrying a total current I share it in inverse proportion to resistance. The current divider puts the other resistance on top.

I1 = I × R2/(R1 + R2)

I1 is the current through R1 in amperes; I is the total current entering the pair in amperes; R1 and R2 are in ohms. The smaller resistor carries the larger current.

For three or more branches, first find R_p, then I_k = I × R_p/R_k, where R_k is the branch whose current you want. The voltage across the bank is I R_p, and each branch current is that voltage divided by its own resistance.

Inductors follow resistors: series L = L1 + L2 + …, parallel 1/L = 1/L1 + 1/L2 + …. Capacitors run the other way: series 1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + …, parallel C = C1 + C2 + …. A capacitive divider splits voltage inversely with capacitance.

Ladder networks, open branches and short branches

A ladder network alternates series arms and shunt arms. Work from the end farthest from the source toward it.

  1. Circle each parallel group and mark its two nodes.
  2. Combine the group farthest from the source into one resistance.
  3. Add that to the series arm feeding it.
  4. Repeat until one resistance R_total remains; the source current is V/R_total.
  5. Unfold outward: current divider at each parallel node, voltage divider along each series arm.

An open branch has infinite resistance. Delete it and recompute the network: its own current becomes zero, and the currents and voltages elsewhere usually change. A branch connected directly across an ideal source is the exception — removing it leaves the other branch values unchanged. A short branch has zero resistance. Placed across a parallel group it forces that group's equivalent resistance to zero and the group voltage to zero, so every element in parallel with the short is bypassed — no current through it, no voltage across it. Elements elsewhere, including the series arms feeding the shorted section, still carry current, and their current rises.

A loaded divider is not the unloaded one. Connect a load R_L across R2 and R_L sits in parallel with R2. The section resistance falls, so the output voltage falls. Reduce R2 ∥ R_L first, then apply the divider rule; the unloaded value is only an upper bound.

Exam traps

  • Current divider upside down: I1 = I × R2/(R1+R2), not I × R1/(R1+R2). The resistance whose current you want sits at the bottom; the other one is on top.
  • Forgetting to invert the reciprocal: 1/R_p = 1/4 + 1/12 gives 1/3, so R_p = 3 Ω, not 1/3 Ω.
  • Capacitors follow the opposite pattern to resistors: series adds reciprocals, parallel adds directly. Memorising only the resistor pattern fails on capacitors.
  • Using the unloaded voltage divider when a load is connected. The load sits in parallel with R2 and pulls the output down; include it before dividing.
  • Mixing units: 4 Ω and 12 kΩ cannot be combined until both are in the same unit. A factor-of-1000 slip is the usual cause of a wrong source current.

1.5 Source transformation and star-delta transformation

A practical source can be drawn as a voltage source with a series resistance or as a current source with a parallel resistance. Both give the same voltage and current at the terminals. A star of three resistors and a delta of three resistors are also two drawings of one three-terminal network. These two swaps turn awkward circuits into simple series-parallel circuits. You use them before mesh or node analysis, and inside Thevenin and Norton work. The arithmetic is small; the skill is choosing the right swap and keeping the source arrow correct.

You need: Chapter 1.2 for practical and ideal sources, and Chapter 1.4 for series-parallel reduction and dividers.

One source, two drawings

Every real source has some internal resistance. A 12 V battery with 0.5 Ω inside is not ideal. At the two terminals, a voltage source V_s in series with R and a current source I_s in parallel with the same R look identical if I_s = V_s ÷ R. That is the source transformation.

Open circuit: the voltage source gives V_s across the terminals; the current source gives I_s × R = V_s. Short circuit: the voltage source gives V_s ÷ R = I_s; the current source gives I_s. Same two tests, same terminal behaviour.

  1. Identify a practical voltage source: value V_s volts, series resistance R ohms.
  2. Compute I_s = V_s ÷ R amperes.
  3. Draw current source I_s in parallel with the same R.
  4. Point the arrow inside the current source from − to +, toward the terminal that acts as +. Current leaves that terminal into the outside circuit and returns to −.
  5. Reverse: V_s = I_s × R, keep R in series, and put the positive terminal where the arrow leaves the source.

V_s = I_s R and I_s = V_s / R

V_s is the source voltage in volts; I_s is the source current in amperes; R is the internal resistance in ohms. The same R changes position, not value.

Only practical sources transform. An ideal voltage source has R = 0, so I_s would be infinite; no finite current source replaces it. An ideal current source has R = ∞, so V_s would be infinite; no finite voltage source replaces it. Use the swap to combine parallel current sources or series voltage sources, and to simplify a circuit before applying Chapter 1.4.

Star and delta at three terminals

Three resistors can meet at one node, a star, or form a closed triangle, a delta. A star has arms R_a, R_b, R_c from terminals a, b, c to the common node n. A delta has sides R_ab between a and b, R_bc between b and c, R_ca between c and a. At terminals a, b, c the two networks are equivalent when their resistance between every pair of terminals matches.

R_a = (R_ab R_ca) / (R_ab + R_bc + R_ca)

Delta to star. R_a, R_b, R_c are star arms in ohms at terminals a, b, c. R_ab, R_bc, R_ca are delta sides in ohms. The numerator uses the two delta sides touching terminal a. R_b and R_c follow by rotating the letters.

R_ab = (R_a R_b + R_b R_c + R_c R_a) / R_c

Star to delta. R_ab is the delta side between a and b in ohms; R_c is the star arm opposite side ab in ohms. R_bc uses R_a, and R_ca uses R_b.

Balanced case: all three star arms equal R_star, or all three delta sides equal R_delta. Then R_delta = 3 R_star, and R_star = R_delta ÷ 3. Never use both factors together.

  1. Mark the three terminals a, b, c.
  2. For delta to star, add the three sides, then divide the product of the two sides meeting at each terminal by that sum.
  3. For star to delta, add the three pair products, then divide by the star arm opposite the side you want.
  4. Use these when a bridge blocks series-parallel reduction; convert one star or delta, then apply Chapter 1.4.

Worked example

Question. A 12 V source in series with 6 Ω feeds a 3 Ω load. (a) Convert the source and find the load current from the converted circuit. (b) Convert a star of three 9 Ω resistors into its equivalent delta.

  1. Label V_s = 12 V, R = 6 Ω, load R_L = 3 Ω.
  2. Converted current: I_s = V_s ÷ R = 12 ÷ 6 = 2 A. The 6 Ω stays in parallel with the 2 A source.
  3. In the converted circuit, the 6 Ω source resistance is in parallel with the 3 Ω load: R_p = (6 × 3) ÷ (6 + 3) = 18 ÷ 9 = 2 Ω.
  4. Current divider for the load: I_L = I_s × R ÷ (R + R_L) = 2 × 6 ÷ (6 + 3) = 1.333 A.
  5. Check in the original series circuit: R_total = 6 + 3 = 9 Ω, so I = 12 ÷ 9 = 1.333 A. The two answers match.
  6. Balanced star to delta: R_delta = 3 × R_star = 3 × 9 = 27 Ω on each side, so R_ab = R_bc = R_ca = 27 Ω.

Answer. The converted source is 2 A in parallel with 6 Ω; the load current is 1.333 A, matching the original 1.333 A; each side of the equivalent delta is 27 Ω.

Exam traps

  • Reversing the current-source arrow. Inside the current source the arrow points from − to +, toward the source's + terminal. Current leaves that + terminal, flows through the outside circuit, and returns to −. The + terminal of the equivalent voltage source is the terminal the arrow points to.
  • Trying to transform an ideal source. R = 0 for an ideal voltage source and R = ∞ for an ideal current source leave no finite series or parallel resistor, so no partner source exists.
  • Using the series resistance as a parallel resistance, or the reverse. The same number R changes position, not value.
  • In star-delta work, dividing by the wrong arm. For R_ab use the star arm opposite side ab, which is R_c; for R_a use the product of the two delta sides touching node a.
  • Mixing the balanced factors: R_delta = 3 R_star, but R_star = R_delta ÷ 3. A factor-of-3 error is common.

1.6 Mesh current and node voltage analysis

Mesh current analysis gives each window of a circuit one invented current and writes KVL round that window. Node voltage analysis gives each node one voltage above a 0 V reference and writes KCL there. Both produce the smallest set of simultaneous equations — one per mesh, or one per non-reference node — instead of one equation per element. Every circuit simulator builds these same matrices internally, and anyone solving a bridge or a two-source network by hand uses them before reaching for superposition or Thevenin's theorem.

You need: Chapter 1.3 for KVL and KCL, and Chapter 1.4 for reading resistances and branches off a circuit.

Mesh current analysis: one unknown per window

Series-parallel reduction works only when resistors combine. A bridge, a ladder or a network with two sources in different branches does not fold down. You need a method that always works and gives the fewest equations.

A mesh is a window of a flat (planar) circuit: a loop with no other loop inside it. In mesh current analysis you invent one current for each window and draw all of them clockwise. That mesh current is a real branch current only in branches on the outer edge. In a shared branch the real current is the difference of the two mesh currents.

Why invent currents? Once the mesh currents are known every branch current follows, and KCL holds at every node by itself, because each node has mesh currents entering and leaving in pairs. Only KVL is left to enforce, and there are exactly as many independent loops as windows.

  1. Redraw the circuit flat and count the windows m. Draw i₁ to i_m all clockwise, in amperes.
  2. Self resistance R₁₁: add every resistor inside mesh 1. It multiplies i₁ with a plus sign.
  3. Shared resistance: the resistor common to meshes 1 and 2 enters as −R_shared, because the two clockwise currents oppose each other there.
  4. Right side: add the voltage sources that drive current clockwise in that mesh. A source pushing anticlockwise enters negative.
  5. Solve the m equations, then convert to the branch currents asked for.
R₁₁ i₁ + R₁₂ i₂ = V₁   and   R₂₁ i₁ + R₂₂ i₂ = V₂

R₁₁ and R₂₂ are the total resistances inside mesh 1 and mesh 2 (Ω); R₁₂ = R₂₁ = minus the resistance shared by the two meshes (Ω); i₁, i₂ are the clockwise mesh currents (A); V₁, V₂ are the sums of the source voltages driving current clockwise in mesh 1 and mesh 2 (V).

Current sources and the supermesh

A current source on the outer edge belongs to one mesh only, and it fixes that mesh current outright: a 5 A source driving clockwise in mesh 2 means i₂ = 5 A, and one unknown disappears.

A current source shared by two meshes is harder. Its voltage is unknown, so KVL round either mesh cannot be written — you would carry a symbol with no value. Use a supermesh. Remove the branch holding the source, treat the two windows as one big loop, and write a single KVL round the outer path, skipping the source. The unknown source voltage cancels between the two meshes and never appears. That is equation one. Equation two is the constraint the source itself imposes: i₁ − i₂ = I_s, with the sign fixed by the arrow of the source.

Two unknowns, two equations, always solvable.

Node voltage analysis: one unknown per node

Node voltage analysis uses KCL instead of KVL. Choose one node as the reference and call its voltage 0 V — usually the bottom wire, or the node with the most connections. Every other node then has a voltage measured from it. A node voltage is not a branch voltage: the voltage across a resistor is the difference of the two node voltages at its ends.

  1. Mark the reference node 0 V.
  2. Label the other n − 1 nodes V₁, V₂ … in volts. These are the unknowns.
  3. At each non-reference node write KCL: currents leaving through resistors = currents entering from sources.
  4. A voltage source joined to the reference node fixes that node voltage directly, so no equation is needed for it.
  5. Solve, then take differences to get branch voltages.

Σ (V_node − V_neighbour) / R = Σ I entering

each term is one resistor R (Ω) between the node being written and a neighbour node; V_node and V_neighbour are their voltages above the reference (V); the right side adds the source currents feeding that node (A).

A voltage source sitting between two non-reference nodes has an unknown current, so KCL at either end cannot be written. Draw a closed surface round both nodes — a supernode — write one KCL for the surface, and add the constraint V_a − V_b = V_s. If that source touches the reference node, skip the supernode: the node voltage is simply V_s.

Matrix form and which method to pick

Both methods give linear equations, so write them as a resistance matrix times a current vector equals a source vector. For two unknowns solve by substitution; for three or more use elimination or Cramer's rule, where the determinant is Δ = R₁₁R₂₂ − R₁₂R₂₁ and i₁ = (V₁R₂₂ − V₂R₁₂)/Δ.

Count before you start. A connected flat circuit with b branches and n nodes has m = b − n + 1 meshes and n − 1 independent node equations. Use whichever is smaller. If they tie, avoid work: node analysis suits many parallel branches and many current sources; mesh analysis suits a planar ladder with several voltage sources.

Worked example

Question. Two windows of a circuit share a 4 Ω resistor. Mesh 1 also contains an 8 V source and a 2 Ω resistor; mesh 2 also contains a 2 V source and a 6 Ω resistor. Both sources drive current clockwise in their own mesh. Find i₁, i₂ and the current in the shared resistor.

  1. Mesh 1 self resistance = 2 + 4 = 6 Ω. Shared resistor with mesh 2 = 4 Ω, entered as −4i₂. Source sum = +8 V, because the 8 V source drives clockwise. Equation: 6i₁ − 4i₂ = 8.
  2. Mesh 2 self resistance = 4 + 6 = 10 Ω. Shared resistor with mesh 1 = −4i₁. Source sum = +2 V. Equation: −4i₁ + 10i₂ = 2.
  3. Determinant Δ = (6)(10) − (−4)(−4) = 60 − 16 = 44 Ω².
  4. i₁ = [(8)(10) − (2)(−4)] / 44 = (80 + 8)/44 = 88/44 = 2 A.
  5. i₂ = [(6)(2) − (−4)(8)] / 44 = (12 + 32)/44 = 44/44 = 1 A.
  6. Shared 4 Ω resistor carries i₁ − i₂ = 2 − 1 = 1 A, from the mesh 1 side towards the mesh 2 side.

Answer. i₁ = 2 A, i₂ = 1 A, and the shared 4 Ω resistor carries 1 A from the mesh 1 side to the mesh 2 side.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the minus sign on the shared resistance term. With both mesh currents clockwise, the neighbour's current always opposes your own through the shared resistor, so the term is −R_shared.
  • Wrong sign on a source. A source whose rise is in the direction of the mesh current is positive; if it drives anticlockwise it enters the right side as negative.
  • Writing an ordinary mesh equation or node equation across a source that has no resistance in series with it. That branch needs a supermesh or a supernode plus the constraint equation.
  • Reporting a mesh current as the branch current. In any shared branch the real current is the difference (or sum) of the two mesh currents.
  • In node analysis, missing a resistor connected to the node, or adding a source current on the wrong side. Current entering a node is positive on the right-hand side; current leaving through a resistor is (V_node − V_neighbour)/R.

1.7 Superposition theorem

Superposition turns one hard circuit into several easy ones. In a linear circuit carrying more than one independent source, the voltage across or the current through any element equals the sum of the values that each source would produce on its own, with every other source set to zero. It exists so that a set of simultaneous equations can be replaced by a few small circuits solved with dividers. It is used in small-signal amplifier analysis, where a bias source and a signal add, and in fault studies, where several generators feed one point.

You need: 1.2 for independent and dependent sources, 1.3 for KCL and KVL, 1.4 for the divider rules, and 1.6 for one reliable solution method to check your answer against.

The idea and its two conditions

A circuit can carry more than one independent source: a 12 V supply, a 3 A current source, a signal generator. Superposition says you do not have to solve them together. Keep one source, set every other independent source to zero, and find the voltage or current you want. Repeat for each source. Add the results with their signs. The sum is the answer for the complete circuit.

Why it exists: a circuit with three sources usually needs one nodal or mesh solution with several unknowns, and every source sits inside every equation. Splitting it into three one-source circuits replaces that with three small reductions, each done quickly by series-parallel work. The theorem is the formal reason those small answers may be added.

  • Linear: every element has a straight-line relation between voltage and current, such as v = iR. A diode does not, so superposition fails there.
  • Bilateral: the element behaves the same with current in either direction. A resistor is bilateral; a transistor is not.

Switching a source off means setting its value to zero, not cutting the wire. A 0 V voltage source is a short circuit. A 0 A current source is an open circuit. These two source types are described in 1.2.

V = V′ + V″ + … and I = I′ + I″ + …

V and I are the voltage and current in the complete circuit; V′ and I′ are the parts produced by the first independent source acting alone, V″ and I″ by the second, and so on. The primes are labels, not derivatives.

The procedure, and where dependent sources go

Fix one reference direction first. Draw the arrow for the current you want on the full circuit and keep that same arrow in every partial circuit.

  1. Pick the element whose voltage or current you want.
  2. Keep one independent source. Replace every other independent voltage source by a short circuit and every other independent current source by an open circuit.
  3. Solve the reduced circuit by series-parallel reduction, a divider, or one node equation.
  4. Repeat for each independent source left in the original circuit.
  5. Add the partial results with their signs, in the fixed reference direction.

Dependent sources stay in place in every partial circuit. A dependent source is not a driving source; its value is fixed by a voltage or current somewhere else, so it must be carried through every pass. Setting it to zero deletes part of the circuit and gives a wrong answer.

Watch the signs. If a partial current comes out opposite to your reference arrow, enter it as negative before adding. Two partial currents of 1.2 A in the same direction give 2.4 A; in opposite directions they cancel.

Voltage and current add, power does not

Superposition works because the circuit equations are linear. Power is not linear. If two partial currents of 1.2 A each flow in a 6 Ω resistor, the total current is 2.4 A and the power is 2.4² × 6 = 34.56 W. Adding the two partial powers gives 2 × (1.2² × 6) = 17.28 W, which is only half the true value.

So find the total current or voltage first, then square it. Never superpose power.

P = I²R = (I′ + I″)² R ≠ I′²R + I″²R

I′ and I″ are the partial currents from each source acting alone, in amperes; R is the resistance in ohms; P is the power in watts. The missing power is the cross term 2I′I″R; in the 6 Ω example above it is 2 × 1.2 × 1.2 × 6 = 17.28 W.

One node equation is the check. In the figure the 12 V source feeds node A through 4 Ω, the 3 A source injects into A, and 6 Ω runs from A to ground. KCL at A gives (V − 12)/4 + V/6 = 3. Multiply by 12: 3V − 36 + 2V = 36, so 5V = 72 and V = 14.4 V. The superposition sum must land on the same number.

Worked example

  1. Reference: let I be the current in the 6 Ω resistor from A downwards, and V_A the node voltage. Keep both directions in every partial circuit.

  3. Source 2 alone, 3 A on and 12 V off: the voltage source becomes a short circuit, so the 4 Ω resistor now runs from node A to ground, in parallel with the 6 Ω. The 3 A divides between them: I″ = 3 × 4/(4 + 6) = 3 × 0.4 = 1.2 A. V_A″ = 1.2 × 6 = 7.2 V.
  4. Add the partial results: I = 1.2 + 1.2 = 2.4 A downwards, V_A = 7.2 + 7.2 = 14.4 V.
  5. Check with one node equation: (V_A − 12)/4 + V_A/6 = 3 gives 3V_A − 36 + 2V_A = 36, so 5V_A = 72, V_A = 14.4 V and I = 14.4/6 = 2.4 A. The two methods agree.

Answer. The current in the 6 Ω resistor is 2.4 A from A to ground, and the voltage at node A is 14.4 V.

Exam traps

  • Superposing power. P = I²R is not linear; add the currents first, then square.
  • Switching off a dependent source. Dependent sources stay in every partial circuit because they are controlled by the circuit itself.
  • Deleting a source instead of zeroing it. A dead voltage source is a short circuit (0 V) and a dead current source is an open circuit (0 A); removing the branch changes the network.
  • Losing the sign. Fix one reference arrow at the start and enter a partial current as negative when it flows the other way.
  • Using the theorem on a circuit with a diode, a lamp or any non-linear element. The two conditions, linear and bilateral, must both hold.

1.8 Thevenin's and Norton's theorems

Thevenin's and Norton's theorems replace a whole linear two-terminal network by one source and one resistance. The load then sees the same voltage and current as with the full circuit. This matters because a load often changes while the rest of the circuit stays fixed: a new fan, a different sensor, another stage of an amplifier. Compute the equivalent once, then get the new load current in one division. The same idea turns a complicated circuit into a source transformation and makes cascaded stages easy to analyse.

You need: Chapters 1.4, 1.6 and 1.7 for reduction, equation solving and adding source responses.

Thevenin equivalent: open circuit, then look back

A linear two-terminal network has any number of resistors and independent or dependent sources. Linear means every element obeys superposition with fixed values. Thevenin's theorem says the whole network, seen from terminals A and B, can be replaced by one voltage source Vth in series with one resistance Rth. The load across A and B then sees the same voltage and current as before.

  1. Remove the load RL from A and B. Leave the terminals open.
  2. Find the open-circuit voltage Voc across A and B. This is Vth. Choose A positive if Voc is higher at A.
  3. Kill every independent source: a voltage source becomes a short of 0 V; a current source becomes an open of 0 A. Keep dependent sources.
  4. Find the resistance Rth looking into A and B with the independent sources killed.
  5. Replace the network by Vth in series with Rth, then reconnect the load.
I<sub>L</sub> = V<sub>th</sub> / (R<sub>th</sub> + R<sub>L</sub>)

IL is the load current in amperes; Vth is the open-circuit terminal voltage in volts; Rth is the equivalent resistance in ohms; RL is the load resistance in ohms.

The open-circuit voltage is measured with no load. Rth is the resistance seen from the terminals, not the resistance of the whole original circuit including the load.

Norton equivalent and dependent sources

Norton's theorem is the same statement in current form. Replace the network by a current source IN in parallel with a resistance RN. RN equals Rth. IN is the short-circuit current from A to B when the load is replaced by a wire. The two equivalents are source transformations of each other, as in Chapter 1.5.

I<sub>N</sub> = V<sub>th</sub> / R<sub>th</sub>,  R<sub>N</sub> = R<sub>th</sub>

IN is the Norton current in amperes, the short-circuit current from A to B; Vth and Rth are the Thevenin voltage and resistance; RN is the Norton resistance in ohms.

To find IN directly, short A to B and solve the circuit. The short carries IN. If Vth and Rth are already known, use IN = Vth/Rth. Then the load current from the Norton equivalent is found by current division between RN and RL.

With dependent sources, Rth cannot be found by killing all sources and reducing resistors alone. Kill only independent sources, apply a test source across A and B, and measure the other quantity. Use Rth = Vtest/Itest, keeping signs. A 1 V test source or a 1 A test source is convenient.

  • Apply Vtest, measure Itest, then Rth = Vtest/Itest.
  • Apply Itest, measure Vtest, then Rth = Vtest/Itest.
  • Dependent sources stay active; only independent sources are killed.

Load line, limits and two related theorems

The Thevenin equivalent gives the load line V = Vth − I Rth. The load fixes its own relation between V and I, such as V = I RL for a resistor. The intersection is the operating point. Change RL and only the load characteristic changes; the load line V = Vth − I Rth and hence Vth and Rth stay fixed. The new operating point is where the new load characteristic cuts the same load line.

The equivalent is valid at the terminals for a linear network. The load itself may be nonlinear, such as a diode, because the theorem replaces only the linear network behind the terminals. The equivalent does not give internal currents or internal power; it gives only terminal behaviour.

Some syllabi add the reciprocity theorem here. In a linear bilateral network, a voltage source in branch 1 producing current in branch 2 gives the same current in branch 1 when the source is moved to branch 2. Tellegen's theorem says the sum of vkik over all branches is zero at any instant for any network obeying KCL and KVL. Only a few universities teach these in this slot.

Σ vk ik = 0

vk and ik are the voltage and current of branch k, with each current entering the positive terminal; the sum runs over every branch.

Worked example

Question. A 3 Ω resistor is connected across terminals A and B and is fed from a 12 V source through 6 Ω, with the 3 Ω itself forming the shunt to B. Find the Thevenin and Norton equivalents seen by a load across A and B, then find the load current for a 2 Ω load.

  1. Remove the load across A and B. The circuit is a 12 V source through 6 Ω to A, then 3 Ω from A to B.
  2. The open-circuit voltage is the voltage across the 3 Ω resistor. By voltage division, Vth = 12 × 3/(6 + 3) = 12 × 3/9 = 4 V. Terminal A is positive with respect to B.
  3. Kill the 12 V source: replace it by a short. Looking into A and B, the 6 Ω and 3 Ω resistors are in parallel. Rth = 6 ∥ 3 = (6 × 3)/(6 + 3) = 18/9 = 2 Ω.
  4. Short A to B to find the Norton current. The 3 Ω resistor is bypassed, so the source current is 12/6 = 2 A. Thus IN = 2 A from A to B through the short. Check: IN = Vth/Rth = 4/2 = 2 A.
  5. The Norton resistance is RN = Rth = 2 Ω, in parallel with IN = 2 A.
  6. Connect RL = 2 Ω to the Thevenin equivalent. IL = Vth/(Rth + RL) = 4/(2 + 2) = 1 A. Load voltage = 1 × 2 = 2 V.
  7. Check with Norton: current division gives IL = 2 × 2/(2 + 2) = 1 A.

Answer. Vth = 4 V, Rth = 2 Ω, IN = 2 A in parallel with RN = 2 Ω, and the 2 Ω load current is IL = 1 A.

Exam traps

  • Including the load resistor inside Rth. Remove the load first; Rth is seen looking into the open terminals.
  • Killing a source the wrong way: a voltage source becomes a short of 0 V, a current source becomes an open of 0 A.
  • Using Vth as the original supply voltage. Vth is the open-circuit terminal voltage after the load is removed, not the 12 V source in the example.
  • Finding Rth with a dependent source by killing all sources and reducing resistors. Dependent sources stay alive; apply a test source and use Rth = Vtest/Itest. The examiner's favourite is exactly this dependent-source case.

1.9 Maximum power transfer theorem

A source with fixed open-circuit voltage and fixed internal resistance gives its largest possible power to one particular load resistance. That load resistance equals the resistance seen looking back into the source. This is the maximum power transfer theorem. It sets the load value in audio, radio and sensor circuits. It also warns power engineers: at the matched point, half the source power is wasted in the source resistance.

You need Chapter 1.8 for the Thevenin equivalent that gives V_th and R_th.

Match the load to the source resistance

Replace everything to the left of the load by its Thevenin equivalent: a voltage source V_th in series with a resistance R_th. That replacement is the work of Chapter 1.8. The load is a resistance R_L. The power delivered to R_L changes when R_L changes.

P_L = I² R_L = V_th² R_L / (R_th + R_L)²

P_L is load power in watts (W); I is load current in amperes (A); V_th is Thevenin voltage in volts (V); R_th is Thevenin resistance in ohms (Ω); R_L is load resistance in ohms (Ω).

V_th and R_th stay fixed. The current I = V_th / (R_th + R_L) falls as R_L grows, but the factor R_L grows. The product has a peak between the two extremes.

dP_L/dR_L = V_th² (R_th − R_L) / (R_th + R_L)³

dP_L/dR_L is the change in load power per ohm of load resistance, in W/Ω; V_th, R_th and R_L are as above.

Set dP_L/dR_L = 0. V_th² is positive, and (R_th + R_L)³ is finite, so the only zero comes from R_th − R_L = 0. Thus R_L = R_th. This is the maximum power transfer condition.

P_max = V_th² / (4R_th)

P_max is the largest load power in watts (W); V_th is the Thevenin voltage in volts (V); R_th is the Thevenin resistance in ohms (Ω).

The maximum possible load power is V_th²/(4R_th). It depends on the source, not on the chosen load beyond the match.

The power curve and the 50 percent efficiency

The figure plots P_L against R_L for V_th = 20 V and R_th = 2 Ω. At R_L = 0 Ω, the current is 10 A but the load voltage is zero, so P_L = 0 W. As R_L becomes very large, the current goes to zero, so P_L again goes to 0 W.

At R_L = 2 Ω the curve peaks at 50 W. On the left of 2 Ω the current is larger but the load resistance is smaller. On the right the load resistance is larger but the current is smaller. The peak is exactly at R_L = R_th.

At this peak, R_th and R_L carry the same current and have equal resistance. Both absorb I²R. Half of the source power is lost in R_th. Efficiency η = (P_L / P_source) × 100% = 50%. Here η is efficiency in percent, P_L is load power in W, and P_source is total source power in W.

A mains power system must not work at this point. If a 230 V supply had R_th equal to R_L, half the generated energy would heat the source or the line. Power systems use small R_th so η is high. Audio amplifiers and radio antenna feeds use matching to pull the largest signal power from a weak source. See Unit 2, Single-Phase and Three-Phase AC Circuits for the AC impedance version.

Using the theorem correctly

The theorem is for a resistive load. If R_L is fixed and R_th can be chosen, the load power rises when R_th falls. A stiff source with small R_th gives the best transfer to that fixed load. Matching is the answer when R_th is fixed and R_L is free.

V_th and R_th may include dependent sources. In that case find R_th with a test source or with the short-circuit current, as in Chapter 1.8. Do not use the original source voltage or its internal resistance without reducing the network.

  1. Remove R_L and mark its two terminals.
  2. Find V_th, the open-circuit voltage across those terminals.
  3. Find R_th, the resistance seen looking back with independent sources set to zero.
  4. Set R_L = R_th for maximum power.
  5. Compute P_max = V_th² / (4R_th); report watts.

At the matched point, load power is maximum but efficiency is only 50 percent. That is the trade-off the theorem makes.

Worked example

Question. A source has an open-circuit voltage V_th = 20 V and a Thevenin resistance R_th = 2 Ω. Find the load resistance for maximum power, the maximum power delivered, and the efficiency at that point.

  1. The Thevenin values are V_th = 20 V and R_th = 2 Ω.
  2. Maximum power transfer needs R_L = R_th, so R_L = 2 Ω.
  3. The total series resistance is R_th + R_L = 2 Ω + 2 Ω = 4 Ω.
  4. The current is I = V_th / 4 Ω = 20 V / 4 Ω = 5 A.
  5. The load power is P_L = I² R_L = (5 A)² × 2 Ω = 50 W.
  6. The formula gives P_max = V_th² / (4R_th) = (20 V)² / (4 × 2 Ω) = 400/8 W = 50 W.
  7. Source power is P_source = V_th I = 20 V × 5 A = 100 W. Efficiency η = (50 W / 100 W) × 100% = 50%.

Answer. R_L = 2 Ω gives P_max = 50 W at 50 percent efficiency.

Exam traps

  • Maximum power is not maximum efficiency. At R_L = R_th the efficiency is 50 percent, not 100 percent.
  • Do not set R_L = 0 for maximum power. Zero load resistance gives zero load voltage and zero load power.
  • Use the Thevenin V_th and R_th of the network, not the nameplate values of the original source, especially when dependent sources are present.
  • P_max = V_th²/(4R_th) assumes a resistive load and fixed V_th and R_th. For AC reactance, matching uses the complex conjugate; see Unit 2.
  • For a fixed R_L, a small R_th gives more load power. Matching R_L to R_th applies only when R_th is fixed and R_L is adjustable.

Takeaways

  1. Every lumped element is one fixed relation between its own voltage and current; R, L and C differ only in which of the two is differentiated.
  2. An ideal source fixes one quantity and lets the other be whatever the load demands, which is why shorting an ideal voltage source or opening an ideal current source is harmless.
  3. KCL is charge conservation at a node and KVL is energy conservation round a loop; together with Ohm's law they solve any DC network.
  4. Series-parallel reduction, dividers, source transformation and star-delta are all bookkeeping tricks that replace a network by a simpler one with identical terminal behaviour.
  5. Mesh analysis writes KVL, node analysis writes KCL; choose mesh when the circuit has many current sources or few windows, node when it has many voltage sources or few nodes.
  6. Superposition, Thevenin, Norton and maximum power transfer all depend on linearity, and all fail once a diode, a saturated amplifier or a nonlinear load enters.
  7. A dependent source does not break Thevenin's theorem, but it can make R_th negative, so the maximum power transfer condition needs a physical check.
  8. Two solutions by different methods must agree; the examiner reads that agreement as proof of correctness, not as extra work.

Apply A delta-connected set of resistors of 6 Ω, 12 Ω and 18 Ω is to be replaced by a star. Find the three star arms, then use the result to find the resistance between two corners of the original delta and check the answer against a direct calculation. 12 marks

Apply A circuit contains a 12 V independent source, a 2 A independent source and a current-controlled voltage source 3I_x. Find the Thevenin equivalent across the terminals of a 4 Ω load, then find the load current. 13 marks

Compute V_th as the open-circuit voltage, keeping the dependent source in place; Find I_x from the open-circuit condition, not from the loaded circuit; Compute R_th by applying a test source of 1 V or 1 A at the terminals with independent sources dead and the dependent source alive; R_th = V_test/I_test; state whether it is positive or negative and what a negative value would mean physically; Reconnect the load: I_L = V_th/(R_th + 4); Note that superposition can be used for V_th but never for R_th as a direct sum of resistances.

Apply A source of 20 V with an internal resistance of 5 Ω feeds a variable load. Find the load resistance for maximum power, the maximum power, the terminal voltage and the efficiency at that point. 10 marks

R_L = R_th = 5 Ω; Total resistance 10 Ω, current 2 A; P_max = I²R_L = 20 W, or V_th²/(4R_th) = 400/20 = 20 W, both agreeing; Terminal voltage = 10 V, exactly half the source voltage; Efficiency = 50 percent — the reason power systems are not designed at maximum power transfer; Sketch of P_L against R_L showing the peak at R_L = R_th.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze A two-source resistive circuit is given. Solve it twice: once by superposition and once by Thevenin's theorem applied to the branch whose current is asked for. Compare the two answers, and say which method you would choose if the load resistance were to be varied over a range, with reasons. 15 marks

Superposition: kill each source in turn, compute the branch current, add with signs; Thevenin: open-circuit voltage and R_th looking into the load terminals, then I = V_th/(R_th + R_L); Both answers identical, which validates the work; Superposition needs one full solution per source and gives one load condition only; Thevenin gives one equivalent that answers every load value in one line, so it is preferred when the load varies; Note that a nonlinear load would break both methods.

Analyze Using mesh analysis, node analysis and superposition, find the same branch current in a three-mesh circuit of your choice, and compare the number of simultaneous equations each method needs. State a rule for choosing a method from the circuit topology alone. 15 marks

Count windows and nodes; the smaller count usually wins; Mesh: b − n + 1 equations; node: n − 1 equations; Superposition: one full solution per independent source, useful only for a few sources and a single wanted quantity; All three results must agree; show at least one cross-check; Current sources favour mesh with a supermesh; voltage sources favour node with a supernode; Final recommendation with a reason, not a memorised sentence.

Evaluate A student claims that maximum power transfer always gives the most efficient operating point for a circuit. Examine the claim using a source of V_th and R_th feeding a load, derive P_L and the efficiency as functions of R_L, and state the practical design rule that follows. 12 marks

P_L = V_th²R_L/(R_th + R_L)²; differentiate and set to zero, giving R_L = R_th; P_max = V_th²/(4R_th); Efficiency = R_L/(R_th + R_L), which is 0.5 at maximum power and rises towards 1 as R_L grows; So maximum power and maximum efficiency occur at different loads; Communication and power systems choose R_L ≫ R_th for efficiency; electronic and signal circuits choose matching for power; Conclusion: the claim is false as stated; the correct statement names the quantity being maximised.

Glossary

Capacitor
Element in which i = C dv/dt, storing energy in an electric field.
Circuit element
A two-terminal device with a defined voltage-current relation, treated as a single lumped component.
Delta connection
Three elements joined end to end in a closed triangle, the three corners forming the terminals.
Dependent source
A source whose value is a fixed multiple of another voltage or current in the circuit.
Efficiency
Ratio of power delivered to the load to total power supplied by the source, usually given as a percentage.
Farad
Unit of capacitance, equal to one coulomb per volt.
Henry
Unit of inductance, equal to one volt-second per ampere.
Ideal source
A source whose voltage or current is fixed regardless of the load connected to it.
Inductor
Element in which v = L di/dt, storing energy in a magnetic field.
Linear circuit
A circuit made only of resistances, inductances, capacitances and independent or dependent sources, obeying superposition.
Loop
Any closed path through a circuit that returns to its starting node without repeating a branch.
Lumped element
An element whose physical size is small compared with the wavelength of the signals, so its behaviour can be described by one relation between terminal voltage and current.
Mesh
A loop that contains no other loop inside it, i.e. one window of a planar circuit.
Node
A point in a circuit where two or more elements meet; all points joined by ideal wire form one node.
Ohm
Unit of resistance, equal to one volt per ampere.
Open-circuit voltage
The voltage appearing across two terminals when nothing is connected between them; it equals V_th.
Power
Rate of energy transfer, p = vi, measured in watts.
Reference node
The node chosen as 0 V in node analysis, against which all other node voltages are measured.
Resistor
Element in which v = iR, dissipating electrical energy as heat.
Short-circuit current
The current that flows through an ideal wire joining two terminals; it equals I_N.
Source transformation
Replacing a voltage source in series with a resistance by a current source in parallel with the same resistance, or the reverse.
Star connection
Three elements joined at one common point, each free end forming a terminal of the three-terminal network.
Thevenin resistance
The resistance seen looking into two terminals with all independent sources set to zero and dependent sources left in place.
