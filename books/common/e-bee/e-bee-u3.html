3.1 Magnetic circuits: MMF, flux and reluctance

A magnetic circuit is a path built to carry magnetic flux, usually through iron. A coil current creates a magnetomotive force (MMF) that drives flux around the path, while reluctance opposes it. The relation is the same as Ohm's law: flux equals MMF divided by reluctance. This idea explains why a small air gap can control the flux in an inductor, relay or motor, and it gives a quick way to estimate flux before using field software. The chapter builds the quantities, the analogy and one series calculation.

You need: Unit 1 for Ohm's law and series resistance, which make the magnetic-circuit analogy easy to see.

Flux, flux density and MMF

A magnetic circuit is a closed path that carries magnetic flux, usually through iron. Flux Φ is the total magnetic field through a cross-section, measured in weber (Wb). Flux density B is flux per unit area: B = Φ/A, in tesla (T), with A in square metres. For a uniform core, B is the same over the cross-section.

The source is a coil. A current I through N turns produces magnetomotive force F = N I, measured in ampere-turns (A·t). MMF is the driving pressure that pushes flux around the path. The right-hand grip rule gives its direction.

We assume no leakage: all flux stays inside the core. Real cores leak a little, especially at joints. Iron is used because its high permeability guides flux with little MMF. Transformers and motors depend on this guided flux; see transformers and DC machines.

Reluctance and the circuit analogy

Reluctance S is the opposition to flux. For a uniform part of the path:

S = l / (μ₀ μᵣ A)

l is the path length in metres; A is the cross-section area in square metres; μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m is the permeability of free space; μᵣ is the relative permeability (1 for air, thousands for iron); S is in ampere-turns per weber (A·t/Wb). Turns are dimensionless, so A/Wb is the same unit.

Permeability μ = μ₀ μᵣ tells how easily a material carries flux. The magnetic form of Ohm's law is:

Φ = F / S = N I / S

Φ is flux in Wb; F is MMF in A·t; S is reluctance in A·t/Wb; N is turns; I is current in A.

Series parts carry the same flux, so their reluctances add: S_total = S₁ + S₂ + ... . Parallel paths share the same MMF, so 1/S_eq = 1/S₁ + 1/S₂ + ... . Some syllabi call reluctance magnetic resistance and MMF magnetising force; standard books reserve magnetising force for H = F/l, in A/m.

The analogy is a calculation aid, not a full model. Iron saturates, so μᵣ falls at high B, and leakage breaks the simple picture.

The air gap controls the flux

Put an iron core and an air gap in series. The same flux Φ passes both. MMF divides: F_iron = Φ S_iron, F_gap = Φ S_gap, and total F = Φ(S_iron + S_gap). Air has μᵣ = 1 while iron may have μᵣ = 2000 or more, so a short gap can have larger reluctance than a long iron path. In the worked example, 1 mm of air is about four times as reluctant as 50 cm of iron.

Design consequence: the air gap controls flux. Machines keep it as small as mechanical clearance allows. A relay or loudspeaker uses a gap where movement is wanted.

For a core with a central limb, flux splits into parallel limbs. Reluctances combine as parallel resistances. Flux takes the easier path: more flux goes through the limb with lower reluctance, in inverse ratio.

Assumptions: no leakage, no fringing at the gap, uniform area. Fringing makes the effective gap area larger, so real gap reluctance is a little lower. Saturation reduces μᵣ at high flux. A changing flux links later work on Faraday's laws and inductance.

Worked example

Question. An iron ring of mean length 50 cm and cross-section 4 cm² has a 1 mm air gap cut in it. The relative permeability of the iron is 2000 and the coil has 400 turns carrying 1 A. Find the reluctance of the iron, the reluctance of the air gap, the total reluctance, the flux and the flux density. Take μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m.

  1. Convert units: l_iron = 50 cm = 0.50 m; l_gap = 1 mm = 1 × 10⁻³ m; A = 4 cm² = 4 × 10⁻⁴ m².
  2. Find permeability: μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ = 1.2566 × 10⁻⁶ H/m. For iron, μ = μ₀ μᵣ = 1.2566 × 10⁻⁶ × 2000 = 2.513 × 10⁻³ H/m. For air, μ = μ₀.
  3. Iron reluctance: S_iron = l_iron / (μ A) = 0.50 / (2.513 × 10⁻³ × 4 × 10⁻⁴) = 0.50 / (1.005 × 10⁻⁶) = 4.97359 × 10⁵ A·t/Wb.
  4. Air-gap reluctance: S_gap = l_gap / (μ₀ A) = 1 × 10⁻³ / (1.2566 × 10⁻⁶ × 4 × 10⁻⁴) = 1 × 10⁻³ / (5.0265 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 1.98944 × 10⁶ A·t/Wb.
  5. Total reluctance: S_total = S_iron + S_gap = 4.97359 × 10⁵ + 1.98944 × 10⁶ = 2.48680 × 10⁶ A·t/Wb.
  6. MMF: F = N I = 400 × 1 = 400 A·t.
  7. Flux: Φ = F / S_total = 400 / (2.48680 × 10⁶) = 1.6085 × 10⁻⁴ Wb = 0.16085 mWb.
  8. Flux density: B = Φ / A = 1.6085 × 10⁻⁴ / (4 × 10⁻⁴) = 0.4021 T.

Answer. S_iron = 4.97 × 10⁵ A·t/Wb, S_gap = 1.99 × 10⁶ A·t/Wb, S_total = 2.49 × 10⁶ A·t/Wb, Φ = 0.161 mWb and B = 0.40 T.

Exam traps

  • Using μᵣ = 2000 for the air gap. Air has μᵣ = 1; the gap reluctance comes from μ₀ alone.
  • Leaving length in cm and area in cm². Convert to metres and square metres before dividing; otherwise the answer moves by powers of ten.
  • Treating MMF N I and magnetising force H = N I / l as the same. MMF is total ampere-turns; H is ampere-turns per metre.
  • In a series circuit the same flux passes every part, so the part MMFs are not known until the flux is found. Do not try to add part MMFs first; find S_total, then Φ = F/S_total, then F_iron = ΦS_iron and F_gap = ΦS_gap.
  • For parallel limbs, adding reluctances as if they were in series. Use reciprocals: 1/S_eq = 1/S₁ + 1/S₂.

3.2 Faraday's laws; self and mutual inductance

Move a magnet into a coil and the meter needle jumps; hold the magnet still and the needle falls back to zero. The change in flux, not the flux itself, drives the EMF. Faraday's law states this, and Lenz's law fixes the direction. Inductance turns the idea into a number: flux linkage per ampere. One coil gives self-inductance L; two coils sharing flux give mutual inductance M. This is how transformers, chokes, ignition coils, motor windings and wireless chargers behave, and it is why a winding opposes any sudden change in its current.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for flux, flux density and reluctance, and the phasor picture from Unit 2 for the 90° shift.

Faraday's two laws and Lenz's law

Faraday's first law: an EMF is induced in a coil only while the magnetic flux linked with it is changing. A magnet lying still inside a coil induces nothing. The same magnet pushed in or pulled out induces a pulse, and the faster the push, the bigger the pulse.

e = N · ΔΦ/Δt

e is the induced EMF in volts (V); N is the number of turns of the coil; ΔΦ is the change in flux in webers (Wb); Δt is the time taken in seconds (s).

The product NΦ is called the flux linkage λ, measured in weber-turns. One weber-turn per second is one volt, so the units work out directly. Lenz's law fixes the direction: the induced current sets up its own flux that opposes the change that produced it, which is why the law is written with a minus sign, e = −N·ΔΦ/Δt. Push a north pole towards a coil and the near face turns north and pushes back. Pull it away and the face flips to south and pulls back. Induction always resists.

In a sinusoid this opposition shows up as a 90° shift: the EMF is a cosine while the flux is a sine. Let Φ = Φₘ sin ωt, where Φₘ is the peak flux in webers and ω = 2πf is the angular frequency in rad/s. Then e = −N·Φₘ·ω·cos ωt = N·Φₘ·ω·sin(ωt − 90°). Because of the minus sign, the EMF is a negative cosine, so it lags the flux by 90°. Its magnitude peaks when the flux is zero, and it is zero when the flux is at its peak, because that is the instant the flux stops changing. In a 50 Hz cycle that puts the EMF peaks at 0 ms and 10 ms, and its zeros at 5 ms and 15 ms. See the figure.

Self-inductance L

L = NΦ/I

L is the self-inductance in henries (H); N is the number of turns; Φ is the flux produced by the coil's own current in webers (Wb); I is that current in amperes (A).

One henry is one weber-turn per ampere, which is the same as one volt-second per ampere. A coil of 1 H develops 1 V across itself when its current changes at 1 A/s.

Chapter 3.1 gives Φ = NI/S, where S is the reluctance of the magnetic path in ampere-turns per weber. Substituting, L = N²/S. More turns help twice, once in the MMF and once in the linking, so L grows as N squared. An iron core raises L because it lowers S.

e = −L · di/dt

e is the EMF induced in the coil in volts (V); L is the self-inductance in henries (H); di/dt is the rate of change of the coil's own current in amperes per second (A/s).

The minus sign is Lenz's law again: the coil fights any change in its own current. Rising current gives an EMF opposing the rise; falling current gives an EMF trying to keep it flowing. This is why a motor winding or a transformer primary cannot be switched off instantly — the sudden di/dt makes a large EMF, which arcs across the switch. The energy held is ½Li² joules, and it has to go somewhere.

Mutual inductance M and coupling k

Two coils on one core share flux. When the current in coil 1 changes, part of its flux links coil 2 and induces an EMF there, with no wire between them.

M = N₂Φ₂₁/I₁ and e₂ = −M · di₁/dt

M is the mutual inductance in henries (H); N₂ is the turns of coil 2; Φ₂₁ is the flux from coil 1 that links coil 2 in webers (Wb); I₁ is the current in coil 1 in amperes (A); e₂ is the EMF induced in coil 2 in volts (V); di₁/dt is the rate of change of I₁ in A/s.

Only the part of the flux that actually links both coils counts. The coefficient of coupling k measures that sharing: k = M/√(L₁L₂), with 0 ≤ k ≤ 1. Here k is a pure number, L₁ and L₂ are the self-inductances in henries. k = 1 means all the flux of one coil links the other; k = 0 means none. From this, M can never exceed √(L₁L₂). An iron-core transformer has k close to 1; two small air-cored coils a metre apart have k near 0.

Two coils in series give L = L₁ + L₂ ± 2M, plus when their fluxes aid and minus when they oppose. The dot convention marks this on a diagram: dots go on the two terminals that are the same end, and current entering both dotted ends produces aiding flux. One-line rule — both currents into the dotted ends: use +2M; one into a dot and one out of a dot: use −2M. Only a few syllabi go further into dot-rule algebra; that belongs to a circuits course.

Worked example

Question. (a) A coil of 500 turns has its flux changed uniformly from 2 mWb to 6 mWb in 8 ms. Find the induced EMF. (b) Two coils on one core have L₁ = 0.4 H, L₂ = 0.1 H and k = 0.6. Find M, and the EMF induced in coil 2 when the current in coil 1 rises at 50 A/s.

  1. Convert the units first. Change of flux: ΔΦ = 6 − 2 = 4 mWb = 4 × 10⁻³ Wb. Time: Δt = 8 ms = 8 × 10⁻³ s.
  2. Apply Faraday's second law: e = N·ΔΦ/Δt = 500 × (4 × 10⁻³) ÷ (8 × 10⁻³).
  3. The 10⁻³ factors cancel: 4 ÷ 8 = 0.5, so e = 500 × 0.5 = 250 V.
  4. For M, use the coupling formula: M = k·√(L₁L₂) = 0.6 × √(0.4 × 0.1) = 0.6 × √0.04 = 0.6 × 0.2 = 0.12 H.
  5. The mutual EMF magnitude is e₂ = M·di₁/dt = 0.12 × 50 = 6 V. By Lenz's law it opposes the rise of current in coil 1.

Answer. The induced EMF is 250 V; the mutual inductance is M = 0.12 H and the EMF in coil 2 is 6 V.

Exam traps

  • Dropping N. Using ΔΦ/Δt alone gives 0.5 V instead of 250 V; the turns multiply the answer.
  • Unit slip: mWb must become ×10⁻³ Wb and ms must become ×10⁻³ s. Missing one factor puts the answer a thousand times off, and examiners deduct the unit mark too.
  • 'Uniformly' means the answer from ΔΦ/Δt is the average EMF. For a non-uniform change you must use e = −N·dΦ/dt at the instant asked for.
  • Mixing up M and k. k is a dimensionless number between 0 and 1; M is in henries and can never exceed √(L₁L₂). Also M is the same both ways: M₁₂ = M₂₁.
  • Writing L = L₁ + L₂ + 2M without looking at the dots. If the two fluxes oppose, the correct total is L₁ + L₂ − 2M, and the sign is the whole point of the question.

3.3 Transformers: construction and working principle

A transformer moves AC power from one coil to another through an iron core, with no moving parts. Both coils share one alternating flux, so each turn gets the same induced EMF. Hence voltage ratio equals turns ratio, current ratio is its inverse, and power passes almost unchanged. Transformers sit at power stations, substations and inside phone chargers.

You need 3.1 for the iron core as a magnetic circuit, 3.2 for mutual inductance, and Unit 2 for RMS value and form factor.

Construction: core and windings

A transformer has two coils on one closed iron core. The supply coil is the primary; the load coil is the secondary. Power crosses by mutual inductance; nothing moves. The core is stacked from 0.35–0.5 mm silicon-steel sheets, varnished to make a laminated core. Laminations break eddy-current paths, cutting eddy loss; silicon raises resistivity and cuts hysteresis loss. The core is the magnetic circuit of 3.1 with no air gap.

In core type, windings sit on vertical limbs and the iron forms one loop. In shell type, the core wraps the windings and flux splits into two outer paths. Core type suits high voltage; shell type is stronger and common in small units. The low-voltage winding sits next to the core, high-voltage outside: less insulation, better cooling.

Working principle and the EMF equation

Connect the primary to AC. Primary current makes an alternating flux in the core. That flux links both windings, so every turn, primary or secondary, gets the same EMF. That one fact decides the design.

  1. Flux is sinusoidal: Φ = Φm sin(ωt), with peak Φm in Wb and ω = 2πf.
  2. EMF per turn: e = −dΦ/dt = −ωΦm cos(ωt).
  3. Peak EMF per turn: ωΦm = 2πfΦm.
  4. RMS EMF per turn: 2πfΦm/√2 = 4.44 fΦm.
  5. For N turns in series: E = 4.44 f N Φm.

E = 4.44 f N Φm

E is RMS induced EMF in volts; f is supply frequency in Hz; N is turns; Φm is peak core flux in Wb; 4.44 = 4 × 1.11, the sine form factor times the half-cycle average 4fΦm.

The 4.44 is 4 × 1.11. Flux swings from −Φm to +Φm in half a cycle, giving average EMF per turn 4fΦm. The sine form factor 1.11 makes it RMS. It is a property of the sine wave, not the transformer.

The primary EMF opposes the supply, so it is a counter EMF. At no load it almost equals supply voltage; net voltage across primary resistance is tiny, so only small magnetising current flows. On load, secondary current weakens flux, counter EMF drops, and primary current rises to restore flux. Primary current follows the load.

Turns ratio, kVA rating and losses

Divide the secondary EMF equation by the primary: E₁/E₂ = N₁/N₂, the turns ratio. N₂ > N₁ is step-up; N₂ < N₁ is step-down. An ideal transformer has no winding resistance, leakage flux or iron loss, so input power equals output power and I₂/I₁ = N₁/N₂.

E₁/E₂ = N₁/N₂ = I₂/I₁

Subscripts 1 and 2 are primary and secondary; all values are RMS; the transformer is ideal.

A real transformer is rated in kVA, not kW: its limit is winding current and insulation voltage, not load phase angle. A 100 kVA unit delivers 100 kVA at power factor 1 or 0.8, but different kW.

  • Copper loss = I²R in windings; it rises with load current.
  • Iron loss = hysteresis + eddy loss; roughly constant at fixed voltage and frequency.
  • Transmission uses step-up transformers (Unit 6): high voltage, low current, lower I²R line loss.
  • A phone charger is a small step-down unit; a welding set uses a step-down transformer with loose coupling for low voltage at large adjustable current.

Worked example

Question. A single-phase transformer is rated 230 V / 23 V, 50 Hz, and its primary has 460 turns. (a) Find the secondary turns. (b) Find the peak core flux. (c) If the secondary supplies 2 A, find the primary current, treating the transformer as ideal.

  1. Given: V₁ = 230 V, V₂ = 23 V, f = 50 Hz, N₁ = 460 turns, I₂ = 2 A.
  2. (a) N₂ = N₁ × V₂/V₁ = 460 × 23/230 = 46 turns.
  3. (b) Φm = V₁/(4.44 f N₁) = 230/(4.44 × 50 × 460) = 2.25 × 10⁻³ Wb = 2.25 mWb.
  4. (c) Ideal: I₁ = I₂ × V₂/V₁ = 2 × 23/230 = 0.2 A.
  5. Check: E₁ = 4.44 × 50 × 460 × 2.25 × 10⁻³ = 230 V; E₂ = 23 V.

Answer. N₂ = 46 turns, peak core flux Φm = 2.25 mWb, and primary current I₁ = 0.2 A.

Worked example 2

Question. A 50 Hz transformer has 200 primary turns and is connected to a 230 V supply. Find the peak core flux.

  1. Given: V₁ = 230 V, f = 50 Hz, N₁ = 200 turns.
  2. Φm = V₁/(4.44 f N₁) = 230/(4.44 × 50 × 200) = 5.18 × 10⁻³ Wb = 5.18 mWb.

Answer. The peak core flux is 5.18 mWb.

Exam traps

  • Putting RMS voltage into E = 4.44 f N Φm and calling Φm RMS. Φm is always peak; RMS flux is Φm/√2.
  • Writing 4 instead of 4.44. 4 gives average EMF per turn; the 1.11 form factor makes it RMS.
  • Inverting the current ratio. Voltage ratio is N₁/N₂; current ratio is N₂/N₁; a step-up steps current down.
  • Assuming kVA rating falls with load power factor. Rating is fixed by winding current and insulation; only kW changes.
  • Thinking primary current is set by primary resistance. It is set by load: more secondary current gives more primary current to restore flux.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Real transformers use an equivalent circuit with winding resistance, leakage reactance and a magnetising branch; machines courses use it for regulation and efficiency. Switching on can draw large inrush current for a few cycles if the core saturates on the first flux peak. 3.4 DC generators and DC motors

A DC machine is one device with two jobs. Spin it and it generates a DC voltage; feed it DC and it produces torque. The same parts do both: a field winding makes flux, a rotating armature carries conductors, and a commutator with brushes turns the coil's alternating EMF into direct voltage at the terminals. A motor's own rotation creates a back EMF that sets its current and torque. Shunt machines hold speed; series machines give high starting torque for traction and cranes.

You need: magnetic circuits for the field path, Faraday's law for the generated EMF, and KVL from Unit 1.

Construction: the parts that make a DC machine

A DC machine has a stationary field and a rotating armature. The yoke is a cast-iron or steel ring. It carries the magnetic flux from pole to pole and holds the machine together. Field poles are bolted inside the yoke. Each pole carries a field winding. When DC flows in the field winding, it sets up flux Φ per pole, as in magnetic circuits.

The armature is a laminated steel core with slots. Insulated conductors sit in the slots and form the armature winding. Laminations reduce eddy-current loss. The armature is the rotor.

The commutator is a ring of copper segments on the shaft, insulated from each other. Carbon brushes press on the segments and connect the rotating winding to the outside circuit. The commutator is a mechanical rectifier: it reverses the connection of each coil twice per revolution, so the coil's alternating EMF appears as direct voltage between the brushes. The brushes are stationary; the segments rotate.

Generator action: rotation makes DC at the terminals

Faraday's law says a conductor cutting flux gets an EMF. In a DC generator the prime mover turns the armature. Each conductor slides past the north pole and then the south pole, so the EMF in a single coil reverses every half turn. The commutator swaps the coil's terminals at the same instant, so the brush marked positive always touches the conductor under the north pole. The terminal voltage is unidirectional, though it ripples slightly. More coils and segments make the ripple small.

E = PΦNZ/(60A)

E is the generated EMF in volts; P is the number of poles; Φ is the flux per pole in webers; N is the speed in revolutions per minute; Z is the total number of armature conductors; A is the number of parallel paths in the armature winding. The 60 converts minutes to seconds.

A depends only on the winding. In lap winding the coils are connected so that each pole has its own parallel path: A = P. In wave winding the coils are connected in a single wave that always gives two parallel paths: A = 2, whatever the pole count. Lap winding suits low voltage and high current. Wave winding suits higher voltage and lower current.

Motor action: back EMF limits the current

Connect DC to the field and to the armature. Current in the armature conductors sits in the field's magnetic field, so each conductor feels a force F = BIl. The forces on the two sides of a coil form a torque, and the armature turns. This is motor action.

As soon as the armature turns, its conductors cut the same flux, so they generate an EMF. By Lenz's law this EMF opposes the supply. It is called the back EMF E_b. KVL around the armature gives V = E_b + I_aR_a, so I_a = (V − E_b)/R_a. At standstill E_b = 0 and the current is limited only by R_a, which is small. A starter resistance limits this starting current.

T = PΦI_aZ/(2πA)

T is the torque in newton-metres; I_a is the armature current in amperes; P, Φ, Z and A are as in the EMF equation.

Torque rises with flux and with armature current. The motor is self-regulating: add load, speed dips, E_b falls, I_a rises, torque rises to match the load.

  • Separately excited: field from a separate DC supply.
  • Shunt: field winding in parallel with the armature; flux is almost constant, so speed is nearly constant from no load to full load.
  • Series: field winding in series with the armature; flux rises with current, giving very high starting torque; speed falls sharply with load, and the motor must never be run unloaded.
  • Compound: both shunt and series windings, a compromise between the two.

Uses: a shunt generator charges batteries and feeds lighting; a shunt motor drives lathes and fans; a series motor drives trains, cranes and lifts; a compound motor drives rolling mills and presses.

Worked example

Question. A 4-pole lap-wound DC generator has 400 armature conductors, flux 0.03 Wb per pole and runs at 1500 rpm. Find the generated EMF. The same machine runs as a motor from 230 V, draws 20 A, and its armature resistance is 0.5 Ω; find the back EMF.

  1. Data: P = 4 poles, Φ = 0.03 Wb, N = 1500 rpm, Z = 400 conductors. Lap winding, so A = P = 4.
  2. Generator: E = PΦNZ/(60A) = (4 × 0.03 × 1500 × 400) ÷ (60 × 4).
  3. The 4 in the numerator cancels the 4 in the denominator: E = (0.03 × 1500 × 400) ÷ 60.
  4. 0.03 × 1500 = 45; 45 × 400 = 18000; 18000 ÷ 60 = 300 V.
  5. Motor: V = 230 V, I_a = 20 A, R_a = 0.5 Ω. E_b = V − I_aR_a = 230 − (20 × 0.5) = 230 − 10 = 220 V.

Answer. Generated EMF = 300 V; back EMF as a motor = 220 V.

Exam traps

  • Confusing generated EMF E with terminal voltage. In a generator V = E − I_aR_a; in a motor E_b = V − I_aR_a.
  • Thinking the back EMF is a separate battery. It is induced in the same armature conductors that carry the current.
  • Running a series motor at no load. With tiny flux the speed can rise to a dangerous value.

3.5 Three-phase induction motor: torque-slip characteristics

Three fixed windings, fed from a three-phase supply, make a magnetic field that turns at one fixed speed. The rotor has no wire from the supply; the field induces currents in it, and those currents make torque. So the rotor chases the field, always a little slower. That small gap, the slip, controls everything: rotor current, torque, and where the motor settles on the torque-slip curve. Industry runs on this motor: water pumps, fans, lathe spindles, compressors, textile machines.

You need: Chapter 3.2 for induced EMF, and Unit 2 for the three-phase supply and the rotating field.

Stator, rotor and the rotating field

The stator carries a three-phase winding: three coil groups, one per phase, placed 120° apart around the bore. The three phase currents differ in time by 120°, so the three pulsating fluxes add up to one flux of constant magnitude that turns round the bore. This is the rotating field.

The rotor is a cage or a winding. A squirrel-cage rotor is a set of copper or aluminium bars in a laminated iron cylinder, short-circuited at both ends by rings. A wound (slip-ring) rotor carries a real three-phase winding brought out through slip rings and brushes, so external resistance can be added to help starting under heavy load. The cage rotor has no brushes and almost no maintenance, so it drives most pumps, fans, lathes, compressors and textile machines.

N_s = 120 f / P

N_s is the synchronous speed of the field in rpm; f is the supply frequency in Hz; P is the number of poles of the machine.

The field speed is fixed by supply frequency and pole count. A 4-pole, 50 Hz motor has its field at 1500 rpm whether the shaft is loaded or not.

  1. Three-phase currents in three windings set up a flux that rotates at N_s.
  2. The rotating flux cuts the rotor bars and induces an EMF in them (Chapter 3.2).
  3. The bars are shorted by the end rings, so current flows; each current-carrying bar in the field feels a force.
  4. The forces add to a torque that drags the rotor along behind the field.

Slip and rotor frequency

The rotor never quite catches the field. If it did, the flux would stand still relative to the bars, no EMF would be induced, there would be no rotor current and no torque, and the rotor would slow again. So it settles at a speed N slightly below N_s.

s = (N_s − N) / N_s = f_r / f

s is the slip, a pure fraction from 0 to 1; N is the rotor speed in rpm; N_s is the synchronous speed in rpm; f_r is the rotor frequency in Hz; f is the supply frequency in Hz.

At standstill N = 0, so s = 1. At full load a typical motor runs at s = 0.02 to 0.05, that is 2% to 5%. A 1500 rpm motor therefore turns at 1425 to 1470 rpm.

The rotor bars see the field slip past at the difference speed, so the induced rotor EMF and current have frequency f_r = s f. At standstill that is the full 50 Hz; at 4% slip it is 2 Hz. Rotor reactance rises with this frequency, X_r = s X_ro, where X_ro is the standstill rotor reactance. That one fact shapes the whole torque curve.

Torque-slip curve

Air-gap power P_ag crosses from stator to rotor through the magnetic field. Torque is that power divided by the speed at which the field delivers it.

T = P_ag / ω_s, ω_s = 2π N_s / 60

T is the torque in N·m; P_ag is the air-gap power in W; ω_s is the synchronous angular speed in rad/s; N_s is in rpm.

At slip s the rotor copper loss is s·P_ag and the mechanical power is (1 − s)·P_ag. Small slip means small loss, which is why the motor is efficient at full load.

At small slip the rotor circuit is almost resistive, so rotor current and torque are both proportional to s: the curve is a straight line from the origin. As slip grows, the rotor reactance s X_ro grows, the rotor current lags further, and each ampere gives less torque. The curve bends over, peaks at the pull-out slip s_m, then falls.

T = 2 T_max / (s/s_m + s_m/s)

T is torque at slip s; T_max is the peak (pull-out) torque; s_m is the slip at which that peak occurs, typically 0.1 to 0.25.

This is the approximate form; it neglects stator resistance and reactance, so it is accurate near the peak and on the running part of the curve, not at standstill.

On this curve the standstill torque is 0.38 T_max and full load sits at 0.47 T_max, so the starting torque comes to only about 0.8 times full load. A motor like that could not start a loaded pump or hoist. Real cage motors lift the starting point to 1.5 to 2 times full load by using a deep-bar or double-cage rotor, whose resistance is high at standstill and low when running. Pull-out torque is about twice full load, and the motor runs on the near-linear part between no load and full load.

Stability follows from the slope. If the load rises, the rotor slows, slip rises, torque rises, and the motor settles at a new point: this holds for s below s_m. Beyond s_m more slip gives less torque, so the rotor keeps slowing and the motor stalls. That peak is the breakdown torque.

Worked example

Question. A 4-pole, 50 Hz, three-phase induction motor runs at 4% slip. Find the synchronous speed, the rotor speed and the rotor frequency. If the air-gap power is 12 kW, find the torque developed.

  • Writing s = 4 instead of s = 0.04. Slip is a fraction of synchronous speed, never a percentage inside the formula.
  • Calling 1440 rpm the synchronous speed. The field always turns at 1500 rpm; only the shaft turns slower.
  • Using the rotor speed in ω_s when computing torque. T = P_ag/ω_s uses the synchronous angular speed at which the field delivers the power.
  • Assuming rotor frequency stays at 50 Hz. It is f_r = s f, which falls as the motor speeds up.
  • Claiming the motor can run at N_s. At N_s there is no relative speed, no rotor EMF, no rotor current, and therefore no torque.

3.6 Single-phase induction motor and universal motor

A single-phase induction motor has one stator winding on the supply. Its field pulses along one axis, not a smooth rotating field. The motor therefore has zero starting torque and needs an auxiliary winding or a shaded pole to begin turning. After it starts, the same winding keeps it running. The universal motor is a series DC motor rebuilt with laminated iron and a compensating winding, so it runs from AC or DC at high speed in small appliances. These are the fractional-kilowatt motors inside fans, refrigerators, mixers and drills.

You need: Chapter 3.5 for the rotating field and slip, and Unit 2 for phase difference and impedance angle.

A pulsating field is two opposite fields

A three-phase winding has three coils spaced 120° apart. Their currents rise and fall in sequence, so the combined field rotates at synchronous speed Ns = 120f / P, where f is supply frequency in hertz and P is the number of poles. A single-phase motor has only one winding. Its current alternates, so its flux grows, falls and reverses along the same axis. The field pulsates; it does not rotate.

Φ(θ, t) = Φ<sub>m</sub> cos(ωt) cos θ = (Φ<sub>m</sub>/2) cos(θ − ωt) + (Φ<sub>m</sub>/2) cos(θ + ωt)

Φm is peak pulsating flux in weber; θ is space angle around the air gap; ω is angular frequency in rad/s; t is time in second. The first term is the forward field, the second is the backward field.

At standstill the rotor has slip 1 with respect to both fields. The two torques are equal and opposite, so the net starting torque is zero. The motor cannot start alone. If the shaft is pushed, the rotor speed n makes the forward slip s = (Ns − n)/Ns and the backward slip sb = (Ns + n)/Ns = 2 − s. The forward torque then exceeds the backward torque, and the motor accelerates in the direction of the push.

Starting methods

A second winding, called the auxiliary or starting winding, is placed 90 electrical degrees from the main winding in space. If its current is also near 90° away from the main current in time, the two pulsating fields add to a rotating field. The phase split is made by giving the auxiliary winding a different resistance-to-reactance ratio, or by adding a capacitor. A centrifugal switch cuts the auxiliary winding out at about 75% of full speed in most designs.

Universal motor: one series motor for AC and DC

A DC series motor has its field winding in series with the armature, so the same current flows through both. Torque is proportional to Φ Ia, where Φ is the field flux in weber and Ia is the armature current in ampere. On AC, the field current and armature current reverse together. Flux and armature current both change sign, so the torque keeps the same direction. The machine therefore runs on AC as well as DC.

AC operation needs changes. The field magnetic circuit is laminated to reduce eddy currents, because the flux alternates. A compensating winding is placed in the stator to cancel armature magnetomotive force and improve commutation. The motor has brushes and a commutator, so it is not an induction motor. It gives high speed, high starting torque and high power for its size. It is used in mixers, drills, vacuum cleaners and sewing machines. On no load, a series motor can overspeed, so the load should not be removed.

3.7 Synchronous generators and synchronous motors

A synchronous machine keeps its rotor at one speed fixed by the supply frequency. The rotor carries a DC field; the stator carries a three-phase armature. The two fields lock together, so speed does not fall as load rises. That gives steady frequency for generation and exact speed for drives. The same machine works as a generator in power stations and as a motor for constant-speed loads or power-factor correction. It cannot start as a motor without help.

You need: Chapter 3.2 for the induced-EMF form, Chapter 3.5 for synchronous speed, and Unit 2 for star connections.

One speed, set by the supply

Construction puts the field on the rotor and the three-phase armature on the stator. The rotor field is fed with direct current (DC) through slip rings, or is made with permanent magnets. Stator currents produce a rotating magnetic field. The rotor poles lock to that field, so the rotor turns at the field speed. This is synchronous speed.

Ns = 120 f / P

Ns is synchronous speed in rpm; f is supply frequency in Hz; P is number of poles.

Pole shape follows speed. A cylindrical rotor is long and thin for high-speed turbines. A salient-pole rotor has projecting poles for low-speed hydro sets. For a generator, the prime mover turns the rotor, and the frequency is set by speed and poles.

f = P N / 120

f is generated frequency in Hz; P is number of poles; N is rotor speed in rpm.

A motor needs help to start. At standstill the alternating torque averages zero, so the rotor cannot pull into step by itself. A damper winding, which is a shorted cage on the rotor, lets it start like an induction motor. A small helper motor, called a pony motor, or a variable-frequency drive can also bring it near synchronous speed.

Field current sets power factor. With constant load, armature current is least at unity power factor, where current is in phase with voltage. It rises when field current is too low (lagging) or too high (leading). A plot of armature current against field current is the V-curve. Its dip is the unity-power-factor point. Here pu means per unit, a multiple of a chosen base value.

Generator EMF and line voltage

Each phase of the armature has T turns in series. The rotating flux per pole Φ induces an RMS phase EMF. The equation assumes a sinusoidal flux wave.

Eph = 4.44 f Φ T

Eph is RMS phase EMF in volts; f is frequency in Hz; Φ is flux per pole in weber; T is turns per phase in series.

Use Eph with phase voltage, never line voltage. At no load, in a star connection the line EMF is √3 times the phase EMF, and line current equals phase current. In a delta connection line EMF equals phase EMF.

VL = √3 Eph

VL is open-circuit line EMF in volts; Eph is generated phase EMF in volts. This holds for a star-connected armature at no load. Under load, armature reaction and synchronous impedance reduce terminal voltage; use Vph = Eph − Ia Zs in phasor form, where Vph is terminal phase voltage, Ia is armature current and Zs is synchronous impedance.

Starting, control and where they are used

On start, the damper winding produces induction-motor torque and brings the rotor close to synchronous speed. The DC field is then switched on, and the rotor pulls into synchronism. Once locked, steady speed stays Ns; extra load changes the torque angle, not the speed.

An overexcited synchronous motor draws a leading current. At no load it acts as a synchronous condenser, supplying reactive power and correcting power factor. An underexcited motor draws a lagging current. Field current is varied continuously by a field rheostat or automatic exciter/AVR (automatic voltage regulator) to set the power factor.

Large alternators in thermal, hydro and nuclear stations are synchronous generators. Synchronous condensers sit at substations for voltage and power-factor support. Constant-speed drives such as large compressors and mills use synchronous motors. Permanent-magnet synchronous machines, covered with special machines in Chapter 3.8, are common in efficient variable-speed drives.

At standstill the stator field sweeps past the rotor poles, so the torque reverses every half cycle and averages zero. The rotor cannot accelerate and lock. A damper winding, pony motor or variable-frequency drive brings it near synchronous speed first.

Apply A 6-pole synchronous generator runs at 1000 rpm. Find its frequency. 3 marks

f = P N / 120 = 6 × 1000 / 120 = 50 Hz.

Apply A star-connected alternator has a phase EMF of 400 V at no load. Find the open-circuit line voltage. 3 marks

VL = √3 × 400 V = 693 V to the nearest volt. This is the no-load line EMF; under load the terminal line voltage differs because of armature reaction and synchronous impedance.

Analyze Why does the armature current of a synchronous motor show a minimum in the V-curve? 5 marks

At the minimum, field current is just enough to give unity power factor. The motor draws only active current. Too little field current makes the current lag; too much makes it lead. Both add a reactive component, so the armature current rises on either side of the dip.

Analyze A synchronous motor is overexcited at constant load. State the effect on armature current and power factor. 4 marks

The motor draws a leading current. Power factor becomes leading, and armature current rises above its unity-power-factor value because the reactive component increases.

Understand Why is a salient-pole rotor used in a low-speed hydro generator? 3 marks

Many projecting poles give the large pole number needed for 50 Hz at low speed. They also give a sturdy mechanical shape for a large slow rotor.

3.8 Special machines: stepper, servo and BLDC motors

A stepper motor moves in fixed angular steps when pulses arrive, so a controller can count steps instead of measuring position. A servo motor closes a feedback loop around a normal motor, so it corrects any error between commanded and actual position. A brushless DC (BLDC) motor uses an inverter to switch stator coils in step with a permanent-magnet rotor, so it avoids brushes. These machines trade the simplicity of a plain DC motor for precise control in printers, robot arms and e-rickshaws.

You need: Chapter 3.4 for torque and commutation, Chapter 3.6 for the universal motor, and Chapter 3.7 for fixed-speed synchronous behaviour.

Stepper motor: fixed steps in open loop

A stepper motor has a stator with m phase windings and a rotor with Nr teeth. The controller energises the phases in sequence, so the rotor jumps from one aligned position to the next. Each jump is one step. The step angle β is the angle moved per step:

β = 360° ÷ (m × Nr)

β is the step angle in degrees per step; m is the number of phases; Nr is the number of rotor teeth.

The number of steps per revolution is 360° ÷ β, which equals m × Nr. Position is open loop: the driver counts pulses from a known start. Pull-out torque is the largest torque the motor can carry at a given stepping rate without losing synchronism (losing steps). No sensor is needed if the load never exceeds the pull-out torque. Holding torque is the torque available with the windings energised and the shaft stationary. It needs current; with the current off, the motor does not hold the rated load. Speed depends on the pulse rate fp: speed = β × fp. Here β is in degrees per step, fp is in pulses per second, and speed is in degrees per second. Divide degrees per second by 6 to get rpm.

  • 3D printer axes and filament feed: a 1.8° motor moves a known distance by counting pulses.
  • CNC table and scanner: open-loop position control from a pulse train.
  • Only a few syllabi teach stepper driver circuits; switching and pulse generation details belong to Units 4 and 5.

Servo motor: feedback corrects the error

A servo motor is not one motor type. It is a DC or AC motor plus a position or speed sensor and a controller. The controller compares the command with the measured value and drives the motor to reduce the error. This is closed-loop control. A potentiometer, encoder or Hall sensor gives position. The error is e = θcmd − θact, where θcmd is the commanded angle and θact is the actual angle, both in degrees. The controller output is a voltage or current command to the motor drive.

If θact is too small, the drive increases torque in the forward direction; if θact is too large, the drive applies torque in the reverse direction. In a DC servo, the motor is a normal DC motor from Chapter 3.4; the commutator and brushes still exist. In an AC servo, an inverter drives a synchronous or induction motor. The sensor is what makes the machine a servo, not a special winding.

  • Robot arm joint: holds an angle under changing load.
  • CNC axis: follows a position command along a path.
  • Rudder or camera gimbal: rejects wind disturbance.

BLDC motor: brushes replaced by electronics

A BLDC motor has a permanent-magnet rotor and a stator with phase windings. There is no commutator. An inverter switches the stator phases in a sequence. The sequence must follow the rotor position, measured by Hall sensors or estimated from back EMF. This is electronic commutation. The inverter applies DC in blocks to two phases at a time. The current pattern makes a stator field that jumps in steps, and the magnet rotor follows the nearest stable alignment.

Because the switching is done by semiconductors, there are no brush sparks and no brush wear. Efficiency is high, life is long, and speed is set by the DC voltage the inverter applies to the windings and by the load; the commutation (switching) frequency follows the rotor position, so it is a result of the speed, not its setting. A PMSM is the sinusoidally driven cousin of the BLDC: it uses smoother three-phase currents and is often chosen for quiet, low-torque-ripple drives.

  • Ceiling fan: low-speed BLDC with a small inverter.
  • E-rickshaw: BLDC hub motor with battery and controller.
  • Drone: BLDC with an electronic speed controller.

Worked example

Question. A stepper motor has 4 phases and a rotor with 50 teeth. Find the step angle and the number of steps per revolution. How many pulses move the shaft through 90°, and what is the speed at 300 pulses per second? Give the speed in degrees per second and in rpm.

  1. m = 4 phases and Nr = 50 teeth.
  2. β = 360° ÷ (m × Nr) = 360° ÷ (4 × 50) = 360° ÷ 200 = 1.8° per step.
  3. Steps per revolution = 360° ÷ β = 360° ÷ 1.8° = 200 steps. Also m × Nr = 4 × 50 = 200.
  4. Pulses for 90° = 90° ÷ β = 90° ÷ 1.8° = 50 pulses.
  5. Speed = β × fp = 1.8° × 300 pulses/s = 540°/s.
  6. In rpm: 540°/s ÷ 6 = 90 rpm, because 360°/s equals 60 rpm.

Answer. β = 1.8°, 200 steps per revolution, 50 pulses for 90°, and 540°/s = 90 rpm at 300 pulses per second.

Exam traps

  • Step angle is not steps per revolution. β = 1.8° gives 200 steps per revolution; 200 is not the step angle.
  • A stepper does not hold its rated load with power removed. Holding torque needs current in the windings; permanent-magnet detent torque is much smaller.
  • Open-loop counting fails if the load torque exceeds the pull-out torque. The motor loses steps and the controller does not know.
  • A servo is a complete drive system, not a special motor winding. The motor inside can be a DC motor with brushes.
  • BLDC and PMSM are not identical in drive. BLDC usually uses block commutation; PMSM uses sinusoidal currents.

Takeaways

  1. A magnetic circuit obeys the same rule as a resistive one, MMF = flux × reluctance, and one millimetre of air can outweigh a metre of iron.
  2. Inductance is only flux linkage per ampere, so any coil's L is fixed by its turns and the reluctance of its path.
  3. A transformer transfers power by shared flux, not by contact, so volts per turn are equal on both windings and V₂/V₁ = N₂/N₁.
  4. A DC machine is one hardware with two behaviours: spin it to generate, feed it to drive, and back EMF is what limits the current it takes.
  5. A three-phase winding alone produces a rotating field; the rotor just chases it, and slip is the small lag that keeps torque flowing.
  6. A single-phase winding gives no starting torque on its own, so every single-phase motor needs a start winding, capacitor or shaded pole.
  7. A synchronous machine locks to supply frequency, which gives exact speed and adjustable power factor but zero self-starting torque.
  8. Special machines are chosen by the control they allow: stepper for open-loop steps, servo for closed-loop position, BLDC for efficient electronic commutation.

Glossary

Back EMF
The EMF generated by a motor's own armature rotation, which opposes the supply and sets the current drawn.
Brushless DC motor
A permanent-magnet rotor motor whose stator currents are switched electronically, so no brushes or commutator are needed.
Coefficient of coupling
The fraction of flux shared by two coils, k = M/√(L₁L₂), from 0 to 1.
Commutator
A segmented cylinder with carbon brushes that reverses armature coil connections at the right instant.
Eddy current
Circulating current induced inside a solid iron core by changing flux; it heats the core and is cut by lamination.
Excitation
The DC current fed to a field winding, which sets the flux and hence the internal EMF of a machine.
Faraday's law
The induced EMF in a circuit equals the negative rate of change of flux linkage through it.
Flux density
Flux per unit area, B = Φ/A, measured in tesla (T).
Hysteresis loss
Energy lost per cycle as the core is magnetised back and forth; reduced by using silicon steel.
Leakage flux
Flux that links one coil but not the other, so it does useful work in neither.
Lenz's law
The induced EMF drives a current whose own field opposes the change that produced it.
Magnetic flux
The total magnetic field passing through an area, measured in weber (Wb), symbol Φ.
Magnetic saturation
The state where extra MMF barely increases flux because the iron's domains are all aligned.
Mutual inductance
Flux linkage in one coil per ampere of current in another, M, also in henry.
Permeability
A material's ability to carry flux; μ = μ₀μᵣ, with μ₀ = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m for free space.
Reluctance
The opposition a magnetic path offers to flux, S = l/(μ₀μᵣA), in A/Wb.
Self-inductance
Flux linkage per ampere in the same coil, L = NΦ/I, measured in henry (H).
Servo motor
A motor packaged with a position or speed sensor and driven in closed loop so the shaft follows a command.
Slip
The fractional shortfall of rotor speed below synchronous speed, s = (N_s − N)/N_s.
Squirrel cage rotor
Bars shorted by end rings, with no external circuit; simple and rugged but slip cannot be added externally.
Step angle
The fixed shaft rotation produced by one pulse of a stepper drive, 360° divided by steps per revolution.
Synchronous speed
The speed of the rotating magnetic field, N_s = 120f/P, fixed by supply frequency and pole count.
Transformer
A static device with two windings on one core that changes AC voltage level at nearly constant power.
Turns ratio
The ratio of primary to secondary turns, which also equals the voltage ratio of an ideal transformer.
