4.1 Semiconductor basics and PN junction diode characteristics

A diode is a two-terminal device that lets current pass easily one way and blocks it the other. It exists because of a junction between two differently doped pieces of silicon. Below about 0.7 V forward it conducts almost nothing; above that the current rises steeply. This one-way behaviour turns alternating current into direct current, protects a circuit from a reversed battery, and clips voltage spikes. Every phone charger you plug in has diodes doing exactly this job. This chapter builds the diode from the silicon atom upward, then reads its voltage-current curve.

You need: Ohm's law, KVL and series circuits from Unit 1, DC Circuits and Network Theorems.

Silicon, holes and doping

Silicon has four electrons in its outer shell. In a crystal each atom shares one electron with each of four neighbours, forming covalent bonds. At room temperature a few bonds break, releasing a free electron and leaving a vacancy called a hole. Both carry current: the electron moves through the crystal, and the hole moves when a neighbouring electron fills it. Pure silicon, called intrinsic silicon, has very few such pairs, so it conducts poorly.

Doping adds a tiny amount of impurity, roughly one atom in a million, to make the silicon useful. The added atoms are called dopants.

  • Trivalent dopant (boron, aluminium, gallium) has three outer electrons. One bond stays incomplete, so each dopant atom gives one hole. Holes become the majority carriers and electrons the minority carriers. The result is p-type silicon.
  • Pentavalent dopant (phosphorus, arsenic, antimony) has five outer electrons. Four fit the bonds and the fifth is loosely held, so each atom gives one free electron. Electrons become the majority carriers and holes the minority carriers. The result is n-type silicon.
  • Doped silicon is still electrically neutral; the dopant atom brings its own nucleus. Doping changes only which carrier is plentiful, not the net charge.

The junction at rest

Join a p piece and an n piece in one crystal and the boundary is a PN junction. Holes on the p side diffuse across into the n side, and electrons diffuse the other way. Each hole that leaves behind leaves a fixed negative acceptor ion; each electron that leaves leaves a fixed positive donor ion. A thin layer near the junction is left with no mobile carriers. It is called the depletion region.

The exposed ions create an electric field pointing from the n side to the p side. This field pushes carriers back and opposes the diffusion. Equilibrium arrives when the drift current equals the diffusion current, so with nothing connected the net current is zero.

A voltage still sits across the depletion region at equilibrium. It is the barrier potential, about 0.7 V for silicon and 0.3 V for germanium at room temperature. It falls slightly as temperature rises. The depletion width is a few micrometres; it widens in reverse bias and narrows in forward bias, and that is the whole mechanism of the diode.

Forward bias, reverse bias and the V-I characteristic

Forward bias connects the p side to the positive terminal and the n side to the negative. The applied field opposes the barrier, the depletion region narrows, and current begins. Below the knee voltage, about 0.6 V for silicon and 0.3 V for germanium, the current is negligible. Above it the current rises steeply and the diode behaves like a fixed voltage drop. The series resistor, not the diode, then decides the current.

I = I_s × (e^(V / (n × V_T)) − 1)

I is the diode current in A; V is the voltage across the diode in V, positive in forward bias; I_s is the reverse saturation current in A; n is the ideality factor, about 1 for germanium and 1 to 2 for silicon; V_T = kT/q ≈ 26 mV at room temperature, where k is Boltzmann's constant, T the absolute temperature and q the electron charge.

Reverse bias connects p to the negative terminal and n to the positive. The barrier grows, the depletion region widens, and only minority carriers cross. The result is a small reverse saturation current, a few nanoamperes for silicon and a few microamperes for germanium, almost independent of the reverse voltage. Enough reverse voltage causes breakdown; the voltage then stays nearly constant while current rises, which is the Zener effect (see Zener diode and linear voltage regulators).

  • Ideal diode: 0 V drop when forward biased, open circuit when reverse biased. Use it only for quick estimates.
  • Practical diode: 0.7 V drop for silicon or 0.3 V for germanium forward, small leakage reverse, and a stated breakdown voltage.
  • The triangle of the diode symbol points from anode (p side) to cathode (n side), which is the direction of forward current.

Rectifiers and clippers

A rectifier converts AC to DC. A half-wave rectifier uses one diode, conducts on one half cycle only, and the load sees a series of pulses. A full-wave rectifier uses two diodes with a centre-tapped transformer, or four in a bridge, and conducts on both halves.

r = √((I_rms / I_dc)² − 1)  and  η = (P_dc / P_ac) × 100%

r is the ripple factor, a measure of how much AC is left in the DC output (smaller is better); I_rms is the RMS value of the output in A; I_dc is the average output in A; η is the rectification efficiency in percent; P_dc is the DC power delivered to the load in W; P_ac is the AC power taken from the source in W.

Worked example

Question. A silicon diode in series with a 220 Ω resistor is connected to the 5 V output of a phone charger. Take the diode drop as 0.7 V. Find the diode current and the power the diode dissipates.

  1. The supply and the diode and the resistor are in series, so KVL gives V_supply = V_diode + V_R.
  2. Voltage across the resistor: V_R = 5 V − 0.7 V = 4.3 V.
  3. Ohm's law gives the series current, which is also the diode current: I = V_R / R = 4.3 V / 220 Ω = 0.01955 A = 19.55 mA.
  4. Power in the diode: P = V_diode × I = 0.7 V × 0.01955 A = 0.01368 W = 13.68 mW.
  5. Check the power in the resistor for comparison: P_R = 4.3 V × 19.55 mA = 84.06 mW, so most of the heat is in the resistor, not the diode.

Answer. The diode current is 19.55 mA and the diode dissipates 13.68 mW.

Exam traps

  • Using an ideal diode with 0 V drop when the 0.7 V decides the current. In the 5 V, 220 Ω circuit above, 0 V gives 22.7 mA and 0.7 V gives 19.55 mA, a 15% error.
  • Drawing the diode arrow backwards. The triangle points from anode (p side) to cathode (n side); forward current flows that way.
  • Calling p-type silicon positively charged. It is neutral; the p only means holes are the majority carriers.
  • Swapping rectifier numbers. Half-wave: ripple factor 1.21, efficiency 40.6%. Full-wave: 0.482, 81.2%. Ripple frequency doubles to 100 Hz for full-wave on a 50 Hz supply.
  • Quoting reverse saturation current in mA, or saying it rises with reverse voltage. It is nA for silicon and µA for germanium, and stays almost flat until breakdown.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Plain silicon PN diodes rarely rectify in modern chargers: Schottky diodes switch faster with a lower forward drop, and wide-bandgap materials such as silicon carbide and gallium nitride hold off hundreds of volts at high switching frequency, which is why laptop chargers have shrunk so much. The same junction physics runs the light-emitting diodes in your room and the perovskite solar cells in research labs. Useful search terms: Schottky barrier diode, SiC power diode, power factor correction boost converter.

4.2 Zener diode and linear voltage regulators

A reverse-biased Zener diode enters controlled breakdown at a set voltage, then holds almost that same voltage across itself even when its current changes. That makes it a cheap voltage reference. Put a resistor in series and a Zener across the load, and a rough 12 V adapter can feed a steady 6 V circuit. You find this inside small chargers and sensor boards. Older regulator ICs used a Zener as the voltage reference; the 78xx family and the LM317 use a bandgap reference instead.

You need 4.1: the reverse characteristic of a PN junction and what breakdown means.

How a Zener diode holds a voltage

A Zener diode is a PN junction doped much more heavily than an ordinary diode. The heavy doping makes the depletion layer very thin, so even a small reverse voltage creates a large electric field across it. At a designed reverse voltage Vz, the Zener voltage, the field tears electrons out of their bonds and current jumps. This is Zener breakdown, and it happens below about 5 V. Above about 5 V the same sudden current comes from avalanche breakdown: carriers gain enough energy to knock more carriers loose. Both are reversible if the current is limited, and both hold the voltage nearly constant.

Useful ratings: Vz is the nominal voltage at a test current; Iz(min) is the knee current below which regulation fails; Iz(max) is the largest current the package can take; Zz = ΔVz/ΔIz is the dynamic resistance, a few ohms to tens of ohms in breakdown. Power dissipated is Pz = Vz × Iz. A 6 V Zener passing 68 mA burns 0.41 W, so it needs a 1 W part, not a 0.5 W part. Below about 5 V, Vz falls as temperature rises; above about 5 V, Vz rises slightly with temperature.

Pz = Vz × Iz

Pz is the Zener power in watts, Vz is the Zener voltage in volts, and Iz is the Zener current in amperes.

Designing a shunt regulator

The Zener sits in parallel with the load, and a series resistor Rs drops the difference between the unregulated input Vin and Vz. Kirchhoff's current law at the output node gives I_Rs = Iz + IL, where IL is the load current. The resistor must be small enough to keep Iz above Iz(min) at the lowest Vin and the largest IL, yet large enough to keep Iz below Iz(max) at the highest Vin and the smallest IL.

  1. Find the largest allowed Rs from the worst low-input, high-load case: Rs(max) = (Vin(min) − Vz)/(Iz(min) + IL(max)).
  2. Find the smallest allowed Rs from the worst high-input, low-load case: Rs(min) = (Vin(max) − Vz)/(Iz(max) + IL(min)).
  3. Choose a standard resistor value between Rs(min) and Rs(max).
  4. Check Iz at both extremes, then compute the worst-case Zener power Pz = Vz × Iz(worst), where Iz(worst) is the largest actual Zener current found, at Vin(max) and IL(min).

Rs = (Vin − Vz) / (Iz + IL)

Rs is the series resistor in ohms, Vin is the unregulated input in volts, Vz is the Zener voltage in volts, Iz is the Zener current in amperes, and IL is the load current in amperes.

line regulation = ΔVout / ΔVin;   load regulation = (V_no-load − V_full-load) / V_full-load × 100%

ΔVout is the change in output voltage for a change ΔVin in input voltage; V_no-load and V_full-load are the output voltages at no load and full load. Line regulation is often quoted in mV/V, load regulation in percent.

Three-terminal regulators: 78xx, 79xx and LM317

A three-terminal regulator hides a voltage reference, an error amplifier and a pass transistor inside one package. The 78xx family gives a fixed positive output: 7805 is 5 V, 7812 is 12 V. The 79xx family gives the same fixed values but negative output. The input must stay above the output by the dropout voltage, about 2 V for the classic 78xx and less for low-dropout types. Input and output capacitors keep the chip stable and improve ripple rejection, the ratio of input ripple to output ripple, often 60 dB to 70 dB for a 78xx.

The LM317 is adjustable. It holds 1.25 V between its OUT and ADJ pins, so R1 from OUT to ADJ carries a fixed current and R2 from ADJ to ground sets the output. Vout = 1.25(1 + R2/R1). With R1 = 240 Ω and R2 = 1 kΩ, Vout = 1.25(1 + 1000/240) = 6.46 V. Keep a minimum load of a few milliamperes. The LM317 also needs about 2 V dropout, so a 6 V output needs at least about 8 V at the input.

Vout = 1.25 × (1 + R2/R1)

Vout is the regulated output in volts, R1 is the resistor from OUT to ADJ in ohms, and R2 is the resistor from ADJ to ground in ohms; 1.25 V is the internal reference.

Worked example

Question. A 6 V Zener must feed a load current varying from 20 mA to 50 mA from an unregulated 12 V supply that can sag to 10 V. Take Iz(min) = 5 mA and Iz(max) = 100 mA. Choose Rs and the Zener power rating. Use a standard resistor if it lies inside the allowed range.

  1. Data: Vz = 6 V; Vin(min) = 10 V; Vin(max) = 12 V; IL(min) = 20 mA; IL(max) = 50 mA; Iz(min) = 5 mA; Iz(max) = 100 mA.
  2. Largest Rs: at Vin = 10 V and IL = 50 mA, I_Rs must be at least 5 mA + 50 mA = 55 mA. Rs(max) = (10 − 6) V / 0.055 A = 4 / 0.055 = 72.7 Ω.
  3. Smallest Rs: at Vin = 12 V and IL = 20 mA, I_Rs must not exceed 100 mA + 20 mA = 120 mA. Rs(min) = (12 − 6) V / 0.120 A = 6 / 0.120 = 50 Ω.
  4. Choose the standard 68 Ω, which lies between 50 Ω and 72.7 Ω.
  5. Low-input check: at 10 V and full load, I_Rs = (10 − 6) / 68 = 58.82 mA. Iz = 58.82 − 50 = 8.82 mA, above 5 mA.
  6. High-input check: at 12 V and light load, I_Rs = (12 − 6) / 68 = 88.24 mA. Iz = 88.24 − 20 = 68.24 mA, below 100 mA.
  7. Worst Zener power: Pz = 6 V × 0.06824 A = 0.409 W. A 0.5 W part is too close, so fit a 1 W Zener.

Answer. Rs = 68 Ω, inside the allowed 50 Ω to 72.7 Ω range; worst-case Zener dissipation is 0.41 W, so use a 1 W Zener.

Exam traps

  • Sizing Rs from the no-load case only. Then at full load Iz falls below Iz(min) and the output sags.
  • Assuming regulation continues when Vin < Vz, or when Iz < Iz(min). The output then just follows the input, reduced by the divider.
  • Using Pz = Vz × Iz(test) instead of the worst case at maximum Vin and minimum load, where the Zener burns the most power.
  • Swapping the families: 78xx is positive, 79xx is negative. The LM317 reference is 1.25 V between OUT and ADJ, not 0 V.
  • Forgetting dropout voltage. A 7805 needs roughly 7 V or more at its input to hold 5 V; below that the output falls.

4.3 BJT: structure, working and CE, CB characteristics

A bipolar junction transistor (BJT) is a three-layer device in which a small base current controls a much larger collector current. That is why it behaves as a current-controlled current source. Two junctions share the middle layer: one is forward biased, the other reverse biased, and charge carriers flow from emitter to collector. The same idea gives amplification in audio and radio circuits and fast switching in inverters and logic. NPN and PNP are the two polarities. It is also called a bipolar transistor, or simply a transistor.

You need: 4.1 for the diode-like base-emitter junction, and Unit 1 for KVL and load lines.

Structure and active-mode current

An NPN transistor has three regions: emitter, base, collector. The emitter is heavily doped with n-type silicon. The base is a thin, lightly doped p-type layer. The collector is a larger n-type region that collects carriers and dissipates heat. A PNP transistor swaps every polarity.

Two junctions exist. The base-emitter (BE) junction is forward biased at about 0.7 V for silicon. The collector-base (CB) junction is reverse biased. In this active mode the emitter pushes electrons into the thin base. Few electrons recombine with holes there; most are swept into the collector by the reverse-biased CB junction. The small recombination current is the base current IB, and the large swept current is the collector current IC.

IE = IB + IC

IE is emitter current, IB is base current and IC is collector current, all in amperes (A).

Two ratios describe the device. α = IC/IE is the common-base current gain; it lies between 0.95 and 0.99. β = IC/IB is the common-emitter current gain; it lies between 50 and 300. They are linked by β = α/(1 − α).

β = α/(1 − α)

α is the fraction of emitter current that reaches the collector, and β is the collector-to-base current ratio. Both are pure numbers.

Regions, biasing and the load line

Three regions appear on the output curves. In cut-off the BE junction is not forward biased, IB is zero and IC is only leakage. In the active region BE is forward biased and CB is reverse biased; IC = βIB. In saturation both junctions are forward biased and IC is set by the external resistor, not by β.

Biasing fixes a steady base current and collector voltage. Four common methods: fixed base uses one resistor from VCC to base; collector-to-base feedback connects a resistor from collector to base; voltage divider holds the base voltage with two resistors; emitter bias adds a resistor in the emitter to steady the current. The first is simple but β-sensitive. The last two are preferred when β varies.

I<sub>B</sub> = (V<sub>CC</sub> − V<sub>BE</sub>)/R<sub>B</sub>,   I<sub>C</sub> = βI<sub>B</sub>,   V<sub>CE</sub> = V<sub>CC</sub> − I<sub>C</sub>R<sub>C</sub>

VCC is supply voltage in volts, VBE ≈ 0.7 V, RB is base resistor in ohms, RC is collector resistor in ohms, and VCE is collector-emitter voltage in volts.

The dc load line is the line VCE = VCC − ICRC drawn on the output curves. Its ends are cut-off (IC = 0, VCE = VCC) and saturation (VCE ≈ 0.2 V, IC = (VCC − 0.2)/RC). The Q-point is where the chosen IB curve crosses the load line.

CE and CB characteristics

The common-emitter (CE) connection uses the emitter as the common terminal. Its input characteristic plots IB against VBE for a fixed VCE. It looks like a diode curve: IB is almost zero below about 0.6 V and rises steeply after 0.7 V.

The CE output characteristic plots IC against VCE for fixed IB. Near VCE = 0 the curve rises steeply: this is saturation. Above about 0.2 V the curve is almost flat: this is the active region, where IC ≈ βIB. The flat part has a slight upward slope because a higher VCE widens the collector depletion layer and narrows the base, so β rises a little. At very high VCE breakdown occurs. The figure plots three curves for IB = 20 µA, 40 µA and 60 µA.

The common-base (CB) connection uses the base as the common terminal. Its input characteristic plots IE against VEB at fixed VCB, again a diode curve. Its output characteristic plots IC against VCB at fixed IE. That curve is even flatter than the CE curve, because IC = αIE and α is close to 1. The CB current gain is below 1, but the CB connection has low input impedance and is used in high-frequency circuits.

Worked example

Question. A fixed-base NPN circuit has VCC = 12 V, RB = 470 kΩ, RC = 2.2 kΩ, β = 100 and VBE = 0.7 V. Find IB, IC and VCE, and state the region.

  1. Write the base loop: VCC = IBRB + VBE. So IB = (VCC − VBE)/RB.
  2. Put values: IB = (12 V − 0.7 V)/470 kΩ = 11.3 V/470,000 Ω = 2.40 × 10−5 A = 24.0 µA.
  3. Assume active region: IC = βIB = 100 × 24.0 µA = 2400 µA = 2.40 mA.
  4. Collector loop: VCE = VCC − ICRC = 12 V − (2.40 mA × 2.2 kΩ). 2.40 mA × 2.2 kΩ = 5.28 V. So VCE = 6.72 V ≈ 6.7 V.
  5. Check saturation: IC(sat) ≈ VCC/RC = 12 V/2.2 kΩ = 5.45 mA. The calculated 2.40 mA is below 5.45 mA, and VCE = 6.7 V is well above 0.2 V. The active-region assumption is correct.

Answer. IB = 24.0 µA, IC = 2.40 mA, VCE = 6.7 V, so the transistor is in the active region.

Exam traps

  • Do not use IC = βIB before checking the region. It holds only while VCE is above about 0.2 V. If the calculated VCE falls below that, the transistor is saturated and IC = (VCC − 0.2)/RC.
  • Do not swap α and β. α = IC/IE is below 1; β = IC/IB is usually 50 to 300.
  • Subtract VBE before dividing by RB. Using IB = VCC/RB overestimates the base current.
  • In the CE output curve, the steep part near the origin is saturation, not cut-off. Cut-off is the bottom curve where IB = 0.
  • Keep kΩ and mA consistent: mA × kΩ gives volts. Mixing ohms and milliamperes gives a factor-of-1000 error.

Most emitter electrons cross the base without recombining, so IB is small while IC is almost equal to IE. Since β = IC/IB, a small IB gives a large β.

Analyze Why is the active part of the CE output curve not perfectly horizontal? 4 marks

Increasing VCE increases the reverse bias of the CB junction. Its depletion layer widens into the thin base, reducing base recombination. IC rises slightly for the same IB; this is base-width modulation, or the Early effect.

Analyze A fixed-base circuit is designed with β = 100 and sits at VCE = 5 V. The transistor is replaced by one with β = 300. What happens to IB, IC and VCE? 5 marks

IB stays the same because it is set by VCC, VBE and RB. The circuit tries to make IC three times larger, but the collector resistor limits it. The transistor moves into saturation, IC ≈ (VCC − 0.2)/RC, and VCE falls to about 0.2 V.

Understand Why is the CB output characteristic flatter than the CE output characteristic? 3 marks

In CB, IC = αIE with IE fixed and α close to 1, so IC barely changes with VCB. In CE, the base current is fixed, but VCE changes the base width and β slightly, so IC changes a little more.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. BJTs remain in analog ICs as gain stages, in power circuits as switches, and in bandgap voltage references. BiCMOS chips combine bipolar speed with CMOS logic. The IGBT is a BJT output stage driven by a MOSFET input. Silicon-germanium heterojunction BJTs work at microwave frequencies in phone radios. 4.4 Field effect transistors: JFET and MOSFET

A field effect transistor (FET) controls current by an electric field, not by feeding current into a control terminal. In an n-channel FET, a negative or positive gate voltage changes the width of a conducting channel between source and drain. A wider channel carries more drain current; a narrower channel carries less. The gate draws almost no current, so input resistance is very high, often around 10¹⁰ Ω. FETs appear in sensors, radio front ends, power supplies and digital circuits. The two common families are the junction FET (JFET) and the metal-oxide-semiconductor FET (MOSFET).

You need: 4.1 for the reverse-biased junction and depletion region, and 4.3 for the BJT comparison.

How an n-channel JFET works

An n-channel JFET is a bar of n-type silicon called the channel. Two p-type regions are diffused into its sides and joined as the gate. One end of the channel is the source, the other end is the drain. The gate and channel form a p–n junction. In normal operation the gate is negative with respect to the source, so this junction is reverse biased. The reverse bias creates a depletion region around the gate. This region has few free electrons and acts as an insulator. As VGS becomes more negative, the depletion region grows and the channel narrows. A narrow channel has higher resistance, so drain current ID falls. At one negative gate voltage the depletion regions meet and close the channel. That voltage is VGS(off), also called the pinch-off voltage VP. For an n-channel JFET, VGS(off) is negative, often −2 V to −8 V. The saturation current at VGS = 0 is IDSS. The gate current is only reverse leakage, so gate input resistance is very high. The device is unipolar: channel current uses one carrier type, electrons here.

JFET transfer and drain characteristics

With VDS high enough, ID depends mainly on VGS. The transfer characteristic is a square law:

I<sub>D</sub> = I<sub>DSS</sub>(1 − V<sub>GS</sub>/V<sub>GS(off)</sub>)²

ID is drain current in mA; IDSS is the saturation current at VGS = 0 in mA; VGS is gate-source voltage in V; VGS(off) is the pinch-off voltage in V, negative for an n-channel JFET.

At VGS = 0, ID = IDSS. At VGS = VGS(off), ID = 0. The figure plots this curve for IDSS = 8 mA and VGS(off) = −4 V. The drain characteristics plot ID against VDS for fixed VGS. At small VDS, the channel acts as a voltage-controlled resistor; this is the ohmic region. As VDS rises, the drain end of the channel pinches first, and ID almost stops rising. This is the saturation region, where the square law is used. The gate-channel junction must stay reverse biased, so VGS should not go positive by more than about 0.5 V; otherwise gate current flows. The symbol for an n-channel JFET has a vertical channel line, source and drain leads, and a gate arrow pointing inward. The arrow shows the p-to-n direction of the gate junction. Some syllabi call the JFET a voltage-controlled resistor in the ohmic region.

MOSFET: insulated gate and threshold

An n-channel enhancement MOSFET has two n+ regions, source and drain, in a p-type substrate. A thin silicon dioxide layer covers the area between them, and a metal or poly-silicon gate sits on top. The oxide is an insulator, so gate and channel form a capacitor. At VGS = 0, no conducting path exists between source and drain; the device is off. When VGS is positive, it attracts electrons to the substrate surface under the oxide. Above a threshold voltage VT, enough electrons form an n-type inversion layer. This layer joins source and drain and conducts. A larger VGS makes the inversion layer richer and raises ID. In saturation:

I<sub>D</sub> = k(V<sub>GS</sub> − V<sub>T</sub>)²

ID is drain current in A or mA; k is a device constant in A/V²; VGS is gate-source voltage in V; VT is threshold voltage in V.

For an n-channel enhancement MOSFET, VT is positive, often 1 V to 3 V. A depletion MOSFET has a built-in channel, so it conducts at VGS = 0 and can be turned off by a negative VGS. The gate oxide gives input resistance of order 10¹⁰ Ω or higher. The device is also called an IGFET or MOS device. Its symbol uses a broken channel line for enhancement mode, with a gap between gate and channel; the substrate arrow points inward for n-channel.

BJT versus FET

A BJT is current-controlled: base current controls collector current. Its base-emitter junction is forward biased, so the base draws current. A FET is voltage-controlled: gate-source voltage controls drain current, and the gate draws almost no current. A BJT uses electrons and holes, so it is bipolar. A FET uses mainly one carrier type, so it is unipolar. FETs give higher input resistance and lower input current. BJTs give higher transconductance for the same bias current and are often cheaper for simple analog circuits. The choice depends on the circuit: a BJT suits a simple audio amplifier, while a MOSFET suits a high-impedance sensor input or a digital gate. In exam answers, do not say a MOSFET has gate current; the oxide blocks it. Do not say a JFET gate junction is forward biased in normal operation.

Worked example

Question. An n-channel JFET has IDSS = 8 mA and VGS(off) = −4 V. It is biased at VGS = −2 V with RD = 2.2 kΩ and VDD = 15 V. Find ID, VDS and the transconductance gm. Use the saturation region.

  1. Check bias: VGS = −2 V lies between VGS(off) = −4 V and 0 V, so the JFET is on and the square law applies.
  2. ID = IDSS(1 − VGS/VGS(off))² = 8 mA × (1 − (−2 V)/(−4 V))² = 8 mA × (1 − 0.5)² = 8 mA × 0.25 = 2 mA.
  3. Drain resistor drop VRD = IDRD = 2 mA × 2.2 kΩ = 4.4 V.
  4. VDS = VDD − VRD = 15 V − 4.4 V = 10.6 V. This is greater than VGS − VGS(off) = −2 V − (−4 V) = 2 V, so saturation is confirmed.
  5. gm is the change in ID for a small change in VGS. First gm0 = 2IDSS/|VGS(off)| = 2 × 8 mA / 4 V = 4 mS. Then gm = gm0(1 − VGS/VGS(off)) = 4 mS × (1 − 0.5) = 2 mS.

Answer. The JFET has ID = 2 mA, VDS = 10.6 V and gm = 2 mS.

Exam traps

  • VGS(off) is negative for an n-channel JFET. Writing +4 V instead of −4 V flips the square-law result.
  • The JFET square law holds in saturation, not in the ohmic region at very small VDS.
  • IDSS is the saturation current at VGS = 0, not the current at any VDS.
  • Do not expect gate current in a MOSFET; the oxide insulates the gate. Gate current appears only through leakage or oxide damage.
  • VT and VGS(off) are not the same: VT is the enhancement-MOSFET threshold, while VGS(off) is the JFET pinch-off voltage.

4.5 Operational amplifiers: ideal op-amp and its configurations

An operational amplifier, or op-amp, is a complete high-gain difference amplifier on one silicon chip. It senses the voltage difference Vd = V+ − V− between two inputs and multiplies it by a very large open-loop gain A, typically 2 × 10⁵ for the LM741. That raw gain is too large to use directly: a few microvolts at the input drive the output to its supply rail. Negative feedback, built with two resistors, trades the huge gain for a precise gain set by resistance ratios. Op-amps sit inside sensor boards, audio mixers, filters and comparators. Some syllabi call the part an opamp or a linear IC.

You need: Kirchhoff's current law, Ohm's law and the potential divider (see Unit 1), plus the transistor stages inside the chip from 4.3.

The symbol and the eight pins

The symbol is a triangle with two inputs on the flat side and one output at the point. The non-inverting input V+ keeps the output sign; the inverting input V− reverses it. The chip needs a positive supply at pin 7 and a negative supply at pin 4, often +12 V and −12 V. Pin 6 is the output. Pins 1 and 5 are offset-null terminals. Pin 8 has no connection on the LM741. Pin 2 is the inverting input and pin 3 is the non-inverting input.

Inside, a difference stage subtracts the two input voltages, a gain stage multiplies the result, and an output stage drives the load. The gain stage gives the large A. The output stage stops the output passing the supply rails.

Ideal op-amp and the equivalent circuit

For hand analysis, treat the op-amp as ideal: infinite open-loop gain, infinite input resistance, zero output resistance, infinite bandwidth and zero offset. The first two assumptions matter most. Infinite input resistance means no current enters V+ or V−. Infinite gain means a finite output needs almost zero input difference.

The real equivalent circuit has an input resistance Rin between the two input terminals, a dependent voltage source A × Vd at the output, and an output resistance Ro in series with that source. For the 741, Rin = 2 MΩ, A = 2 × 10⁵ and Ro = 75 Ω. The dependent source is why the op-amp is an active device: it supplies load power from the supplies, controlled by Vd.

Vout = A × Vd = A × (V+ − V−)

Vout is the output voltage; A is the open-loop voltage gain; Vd is the differential input voltage; V+ and V− are the non-inverting and inverting input voltages. This holds only while the output is between the supply rails.

Without feedback the circuit is open-loop. A tiny Vd drives the output into saturation. With ±12 V supplies and A = 2 × 10⁵, the linear input range is only about ±11 V ÷ (2 × 10⁵) = ±55 µV. A 1 mV difference asks for 200 V, so the output clips near ±11 V, about 1 V below each rail.

Negative feedback: inverting and non-inverting

Negative feedback returns a fraction of the output to the inverting input. Two rules follow from the ideal model and negative feedback: (1) no current flows into either input, and (2) the op-amp adjusts its output until V+ = V−. Rule 2 is the virtual short. When one input is grounded, the other input sits at almost 0 V; that is a virtual ground. Both rules fail if feedback is absent or positive.

In the inverting amplifier, Vin goes through R1 to the inverting input. Rf connects the output back to that same input. The non-inverting input is grounded. The inverting node is a virtual ground, so the current in R1 is Vin/R1. That current cannot enter the op-amp, so it all flows in Rf. The output must therefore be Vout = −(Rf/R1) × Vin.

Vout = −(Rf / R1) × Vin

R1 is the input resistor from Vin to the inverting input; Rf is the feedback resistor from output to the inverting input; Vin is the input voltage; Vout is the output voltage. The minus sign means inversion.

In the non-inverting amplifier, Vin goes to V+. R1 goes from the inverting input to ground, and Rf goes from the output to the inverting input. The virtual short makes V− = V+ = Vin. The divider R1–Rf then gives Vout = (1 + Rf/R1) × Vin. The output stays in phase with the input.

Vout = (1 + Rf / R1) × Vin

R1 is the resistor from the inverting input to ground; Rf is the feedback resistor; Vin is the input voltage at V+; Vout is the output voltage. The gain is always at least 1.

Worked example

Question. An inverting amplifier uses R1 = 10 kΩ and Rf = 100 kΩ. The supplies are +12 V and −12 V. The input is Vin = 0.5 V. Find the closed-loop gain, the output voltage, and the current in R1. State whether the output clips.

  1. Closed-loop gain: Av = −Rf/R1 = −100 kΩ / 10 kΩ = −10.
  2. Output voltage: Vout = Av × Vin = −10 × 0.5 V = −5 V.
  3. Check the rails: −5 V lies between −11 V and +11 V, so the output does not clip.
  4. Current in R1: I = Vin/R1 = 0.5 V / 10 kΩ = 50 µA.
  5. The inverting input draws no ideal current, so the same 50 µA flows in Rf. The voltage across Rf is 50 µA × 100 kΩ = 5 V, which agrees with Vout − 0 V = −5 V.

Answer. The closed-loop gain is −10, the output is −5 V, and the current in R1 is 50 µA; the output is inside the rails, so there is no clipping.

Exam traps

  • Do not apply V+ = V− to an open-loop or positive-feedback op-amp. Without negative feedback the output sits at saturation and the input difference is not zero.
  • Do not drop the minus sign in the inverting gain. −Rf/R1 inverts; 1 + Rf/R1 does not.
  • Do not confuse the open-loop gain A with the closed-loop gain. A is a chip property near 2 × 10⁵; the closed-loop gain is set by Rf and R1.
  • Do not let a calculated output pass the rails. With ±12 V supplies a 741 clips near ±11 V; a calculated −15 V appears as about −11 V.
  • Do not use the ideal zero-input-current rule for very high resistor values on a real 741. Its input bias current is small but not zero, and it creates an offset.

4.6 Op-amp applications: follower, adder, subtractor, integrator, comparator

An operational amplifier (op-amp) with negative feedback obeys two golden rules: no current enters its input terminals, and its two input terminals sit at almost the same voltage. Those rules turn a bare high-gain amplifier into precise building blocks. This chapter shows the voltage follower, adder, subtractor, integrator and comparator. A follower buffers a sensor; an adder and subtractor combine signals with resistors; an integrator uses one capacitor; a comparator uses no feedback and gives only two output levels. The same parts appear in sensor front ends, audio mixers, waveshaping and trip circuits.

You need: Chapter 4.5, especially the virtual short rule and the saturation limits.

Follower and inverting adder

Voltage follower: connect the output directly to the inverting input, and drive the non-inverting input with Vin. Negative feedback forces V− = V+, so Vout = Vin. The gain is exactly 1. The input resistance is very high and the output resistance is very low, so the follower takes almost no current from a sensor and can drive a heavier load. Use it between a high-resistance sensor and a low-resistance load, or between stages.

Inverting adder: each input V1, V2, V3 goes through its own resistor R1, R2, R3 to the inverting input. Rf goes from output to that same input. The non-inverting input is grounded. Negative feedback and the virtual short make the inverting node a virtual ground, V− ≈ 0 V, while no current enters the op-amp input. Kirchhoff's current law at that node gives:

Vout = −Rf(V1/R1 + V2/R2 + V3/R3)

Vout is the output voltage in volts; V1, V2, V3 are input voltages in volts; R1, R2, R3 are input resistors in ohms; Rf is the feedback resistor in ohms.

With equal input resistors R and Rf = R/N for N inputs, the output is the negative average. With Rf = R, the output is the negative sum. The minus sign comes from the inverting input.

A non-inverting adder feeds the inputs through equal resistors to V+. With equal input resistors the non-inverting input sits at the average (V1 + V2 + … + VN)/N, so Vout = (1 + Rf/R1) × average, positive and with no minus sign.

Subtractor (difference amplifier)

A difference amplifier subtracts two voltages. V1 goes through R1 to the inverting input; Rf is the feedback resistor from output to that input. V2 goes through R2 to the non-inverting input; R3 connects that input to ground. Set Rf/R1 = R3/R2. The circuit is balanced. Then the output is:

Vout = (Rf/R1)(V2 − V1)

Vout is the output voltage in volts; V1 and V2 are input voltages in volts; Rf, R1, R2 and R3 are resistors in ohms.

R1 and Rf set the gain; R2 and R3 divide V2 by the same factor. If the resistor ratios are not equal, V2 is scaled differently from V1; the circuit amplifies common-mode voltage and rejects less. In the balanced case, a common voltage on both inputs gives zero output. Difference amplifiers read a small voltage between two sensor wires while rejecting noise that appears on both. A common use is the front end of an ECG or a current-sense circuit.

Integrator and differentiator

Swap the feedback resistor Rf for a capacitor C. The input resistor is R1. The inverting node stays at virtual ground. The current through R1 is Vin/R1. That current charges C. With the sign of the inverting input, the output is:

Vout(t) = Vout(0) − (1/(R1C))∫₀ᵗ Vin dt

Vout(t) is the output voltage in volts at time t; Vout(0) is the output voltage at t = 0 (0 V in the worked figure); Vin is the input voltage in volts; R1 is the input resistor in ohms; C is the feedback capacitor in farads; t is time in seconds; ∫₀ᵗ Vin dt is in volt-seconds; R1C is the time constant in seconds.

The output changes at a slope of −Vin/(R1C). A constant positive input gives a falling ramp; a constant negative input gives a rising ramp. A square wave therefore becomes a triangle wave. If the input is +1 V for 1 ms and R1C = 1 ms, the slope is −1000 V/s, so the output falls by 1 V in 1 ms. A sine becomes a cosine scaled by 1/(ωR1C), with no extra minus sign in the result: Vout = (A/(ωR1C))cos(ωt) for Vin = A sin(ωt). The only minus sign is the one already inside the formula Vout = −(1/(R1C))∫Vin dt. The differentiator is the mirror: capacitor C at input, resistor R in feedback, giving Vout = −RC dVin/dt. It responds to edges and is noisy, so practical versions add a small input resistor.

Comparator and Schmitt trigger

A comparator uses the op-amp without feedback. The output cannot settle between the rails; it hits near +Vsat or −Vsat. Vsat is the saturation voltage, a little below the supply voltage. With Vin on V+ and a reference Vref on V−, Vout is high when Vin > Vref and low when Vin < Vref. This is a level detector. With Vref = 0 V it is a zero-crossing detector: the output changes state each time the input crosses 0 V. It is used to convert a slow analog signal into a clean logic edge, in trip circuits and in zero-crossing dimmers.

A Schmitt trigger adds positive feedback from output to V+. It has two thresholds: an upper threshold VUT and a lower threshold VLT. The output switches high only after the input rises above VUT, and low only after it falls below VLT. The gap VUT − VLT is the hysteresis. Hysteresis stops a noisy input near one threshold from making many fast output changes. The 555 timer uses two such levels internally; see Chapter 4.7.

Worked example

Question. An inverting adder has Rf = 20 kΩ and three input resistors of 10 kΩ each, fed with V1 = 1 V, V2 = 2 V and V3 = 0.5 V. Supplies are ±12 V. Find Vout and the current through Rf.

  1. The non-inverting input is grounded, so the virtual short gives V− ≈ 0 V at the inverting node.
  2. Current through R1: I1 = V1/R1 = 1 V / 10 kΩ = 0.10 mA.
  3. Current through R2: I2 = V2/R2 = 2 V / 10 kΩ = 0.20 mA.
  4. Current through R3: I3 = V3/R3 = 0.5 V / 10 kΩ = 0.05 mA.
  5. Total current into the node: I = I1 + I2 + I3 = 0.10 + 0.20 + 0.05 = 0.35 mA. This current flows through Rf.
  6. Output: Vout = 0 − I × Rf = −0.35 mA × 20 kΩ = −7 V.
  7. The supply is ±12 V, and −7 V lies between the rails, so the op-amp does not saturate.
  8. Current through Rf = 0.35 mA, the same as the total input current.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the minus sign in the inverting adder or integrator. The input goes to the inverting input, so Vout is negative for positive inputs.
  • Thinking a comparator is a linear amplifier. With no feedback there is no virtual short; the output is only near +Vsat or −Vsat.
  • Using the balanced difference-amplifier formula when Rf/R1 ≠ R3/R2. Then V2 is scaled differently and common-mode rejection is poor.
  • Mixing up average and sum in an adder. Equal input resistors R with Rf = R give the negative sum; for N inputs the negative average needs Rf = R/N.
  • Forgetting the unit of R1C in the integrator. R1 in ohms times C in farads gives seconds; the slope is volts per second.

4.7 Timers and their applications

The 555 timer is a cheap eight-pin IC that turns a resistor and a capacitor into a clock. Inside, three equal resistors split the supply into ⅓ VCC and ⅔ VCC. Two comparators watch the capacitor voltage and flip a flip-flop at those levels. It runs LED blinkers, time delays, tone generators and PWM drives.

You need: The comparator idea from 4.6, and RC charging with time constant τ = RC from Unit 1.

Inside the 555

A popular explanation of the name is three 5 kΩ resistors, so 555. Three equal resistors sit in a chain from VCC to ground, giving two reference levels: ⅓ VCC at the lower tap and ⅔ VCC at the upper tap.

The lower comparator watches the trigger pin (pin 2). A trigger below ⅓ VCC sets an RS flip-flop. The upper comparator watches the threshold pin (pin 6). A threshold above ⅔ VCC resets the flip-flop.

Set means output pin 3 is high. Reset means pin 3 is low and the discharge transistor pulls pin 7 to ground, emptying the timing capacitor. The output stage sources or sinks about 200 mA, enough for an LED.

Only a few universities teach this topic, so treat it as a short visit: one chip, two formulas, a few uses.

Monostable: one delay per pulse

One stable state: the output rests low, the discharge transistor is on, and the capacitor is held at 0 V.

  1. A negative pulse below ⅓ VCC arrives at the trigger pin.
  2. The flip-flop sets, the output goes high, and the discharge transistor switches off.
  3. The capacitor charges towards VCC through resistor R.
  4. At ⅔ VCC the upper comparator resets the flip-flop.
  5. The output falls, the transistor turns on, and the capacitor dumps to ground. One pulse out per pulse in.

T = 1.1 R C

T is the output pulse width in seconds; R is the timing resistor in ohms; C is the capacitor in farads. The 1.1 is ln 3, from charging from 0 towards VCC and stopping at ⅔ VCC.

Use it for a staircase lamp that stays on for a fixed time after a button press, or a relay that keeps a pump running for 30 s.

Astable: a free-running oscillator

There is no stable state; the output flips forever. R1 runs from VCC to pin 7, R2 from pin 7 to pin 6, and C from pin 6 to ground. Charging goes through R1 + R2 and is slow; discharging goes through R2 only and is fast, so the output stays high longer.

T = 0.693 (R1 + 2R2) C,  f = 1.44 / ((R1 + 2R2) C)

T is the full period in seconds; f is the frequency in hertz; R1 and R2 are in ohms; C is in farads; 0.693 is ln 2.

Duty cycle = (R1 + R2) / (R1 + 2R2)

the fraction of the period the output spends high; always above 50%, because charging uses R1 + R2 but discharging uses R2 alone.

  • LED blinker: pick f ≈ 1–2 Hz so the eye sees flashes.
  • Tone generator: pick f in the audio band, feed pin 3 to a speaker through a capacitor.
  • PWM: a diode across R2 makes high and low times independent, giving duty cycles below 50%.
  • Switch debouncing: a monostable set to 20 ms ignores the chatter of a push button.

Worked example

Question. A 555 timer is wired as an astable multivibrator with R1 = 10 kΩ, R2 = 47 kΩ and C = 10 µF. Find the period, the frequency and the duty cycle.

  1. Effective resistance for the period: R1 + 2R2 = 10 kΩ + 2 × 47 kΩ = 104 kΩ.
  2. Charging uses R1 + R2 = 57 kΩ, discharging uses R2 = 47 kΩ, and R1 + 2R2 = 57 + 47 = 104 kΩ is the combination that gives the full period.
  3. C = 10 µF = 10 × 10⁻⁶ F.
  4. T = 0.693 × 104 × 10³ × 10 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.693 × 1.04 = 0.72 s.
  5. f = 1.44 / (104 × 10³ × 10 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.44 / 1.04 = 1.38 Hz.
  6. Duty cycle = (10 + 47)/104 = 57/104 = 0.548 = 54.8%. High time = 0.693 × 57 × 10³ × 10 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.395 s, which is 54.8% of 0.72 s.

Exam traps

  • Unit slips: put R in ohms and C in farads before multiplying. 10 kΩ × 10 µF is 0.1 s, not 100 s.
  • Expecting below 50% duty from the plain astable. Charging uses R1 + R2, discharging uses R2 only, so high time is always longer.
  • Swapping the formulas: monostable is 1.1RC with one resistor; astable is 0.693(R1 + 2R2)C with two.
  • Forgetting that trigger is active low (below ⅓ VCC) and threshold is active high. Pin numbers: 2 trigger, 6 threshold, 7 discharge, 3 output.

Analyze A student builds an LED blinker but the LED glows steady instead of flashing. The 555 is fine. Give two likely causes and the fix. 4 marks

First, the RC product is too small, so f is in the kilohertz and the eye sees a steady glow; raise C. Second, the timing capacitor is open, wrongly valued, or not connected between pin 6 and ground, so the voltage never climbs to ⅔ VCC and the output never resets; check the value and solder joints with a multimeter. The diode bypasses R2 during charging, so the capacitor charges through R1 only and discharges through R2 as before. High time becomes 0.693 R1 C, which can be made smaller than the low time 0.693 R2 C.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. In industry the 555 is often used as a cheap comparator with a strong output — debouncing a keypad, driving a small relay in a washing machine board. Microcontroller timers with a crystal now give parts-per-million accuracy where the 555 gives a few percent. Hobbyists still use it for simple tone and PWM generation. Look up the CMOS version (7555), which draws far less current and works down to 2 V.

Takeaways

  1. A PN junction conducts easily above about 0.7 V forward and blocks almost completely in reverse, and that single property gives rectifiers, clippers and protection diodes.
  2. A reverse-biased Zener holds nearly constant voltage while its current changes, so it can act as a simple shunt regulator, and an IC such as the 7805 does the same job with better regulation and current limit.
  3. In a BJT the base current controls the collector current (IC = βIB), which makes it a current-controlled current source and the standard small-signal amplifier.
  4. In a JFET or MOSFET the gate voltage controls the channel width, so drain current is voltage-controlled and the gate draws almost no current, which suits digital and low-power design.
  5. Negative feedback plus two resistors and two golden rules (no input current, inputs at the same voltage) turn an op-amp into precise amplifiers, adders, subtractors and integrators.
  6. An op-amp without feedback becomes a comparator, switching its output between the supply rails to indicate which input is larger.
  7. One 555 timer chip, one resistor and one capacitor set a delay or a square-wave frequency, which is why the 555 appears in timers, flashers and pulse generators.
  8. Choosing a device is a design decision: Zener for cheap low-current regulation, 78xx for simple fixed rails, BJT for current drive, MOSFET for high-speed switching, op-amp for signal mathematics.

Glossary

Acceptor impurity
A trivalent atom such as boron that creates a hole and makes the silicon p-type.
Avalanche breakdown
Reverse breakdown in a lightly doped junction where carriers gain enough energy to knock out more carriers, creating a chain multiplication.
Channel (FET)
The conducting path between source and drain whose width is controlled by the gate voltage.
Clipper
A diode circuit that removes the part of a waveform above or below a chosen level without changing the rest.
CMRR
Common-mode rejection ratio, the ratio of differential gain to common-mode gain; ideally infinite, practically about 90 dB for a 741.
Cut-off region
The BJT state where the base-emitter junction is not forward biased, so collector current is almost zero and the device acts as an open switch.
Depletion mode
A FET type that conducts at zero gate-source voltage and is turned off by driving the gate voltage towards pinch-off.
Donor impurity
A pentavalent atom such as phosphorus or arsenic that donates a free electron and makes the silicon n-type.
Duty cycle
The fraction of one period for which a pulse output stays high, expressed as a percentage.
Enhancement mode
A MOSFET type that is off at zero gate-source voltage and needs VGS above the threshold to form a channel.
Forward bias
Connecting the p side to the positive terminal and the n side to the negative terminal, which narrows the depletion region and allows current above the knee voltage.
Hole
The vacancy left when an electron leaves a covalent bond; it behaves as a positive charge carrier.
Input offset voltage
The small differential voltage (a few millivolts) needed at the inputs to make the output exactly zero, caused by imperfect matching inside the op-amp.
Intrinsic semiconductor
Pure silicon with no added impurity, in which the number of free electrons equals the number of holes and conductivity is very low.
Load regulation
The change in output voltage when load current changes from no load to full load, usually given as a percentage or in millivolts.
Negative feedback
Feeding a fraction of the output back to the inverting input so that the closed-loop gain is set by external components and becomes stable and predictable.
Open-loop gain
The very large voltage gain (typically 10⁵ or about 100 dB) of an op-amp with no feedback.
PN junction
The boundary formed when p-type and n-type silicon are joined in one crystal, with a depletion region across it.
Quiescent point (Q-point)
The d.c. values of collector current and collector-emitter voltage about which a transistor amplifier swings.
Rectifier
A circuit of diodes that converts alternating voltage into a one-directional (pulsating d.c.) voltage.
Reverse bias
Connecting the p side to the negative terminal, which widens the depletion region and blocks current except for a small leakage.
Saturation region (BJT)
The BJT state where both junctions are forward biased, VCE falls to about 0.2 V and the device acts as a closed switch.
