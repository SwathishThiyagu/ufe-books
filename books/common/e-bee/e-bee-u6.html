6.1 Measuring instruments: moving coil and moving iron

A pointer that swings across a scale is pushed by a magnetic force on a current-carrying part. In a moving-coil meter that part is a coil inside a permanent magnet, and the swing is proportional to current. In a moving-iron meter that part is a piece of soft iron pulled into a coil, and the swing is proportional to current squared. The first suits DC and laboratory work. The second also works on AC and sits on cheap panel boards. Both still exist behind the digital display.

You need: Unit 1 current, resistance and Ohm's law; Unit 2 RMS value and phasors for the AC behaviour of these meters.

The three torques, and the moving-coil meter

An indicating instrument needs three torques. The deflecting torque moves the pointer away from zero. The controlling torque pulls it back, so the pointer stops where the two balance. The damping torque acts only while the pointer is moving, so it settles quickly instead of swinging.

In a permanent-magnet moving-coil (PMMC) meter a light coil of N turns and area A m² sits in the gap of a permanent magnet. A soft iron cylinder fills the gap, so the flux density B (tesla) is radial and always cuts the coil sides at right angles. A current I (ampere) through the coil gives the deflecting torque below.

T = N B I A    and    T = k θ    so    θ = (N B A / k) I

T is deflecting torque in N m; N is the number of turns; B is flux density in T; I is coil current in A; A is coil area in m²; k is the spring constant in N m per radian; θ is deflection in radians.

A hairspring gives a controlling torque proportional to the angle and also carries current into the coil. At balance the two torques are equal, so deflection is proportional to current and the scale is uniform. An aluminium former on the coil produces eddy currents as it moves; that is the damping. Jewelled bearings keep friction low, so a movement of 50 μA full scale is practical. Some syllabi call this the D'Arsonval movement.

Moving iron: attraction and repulsion

A moving-iron meter has a stationary coil and a small piece of soft iron that moves. In the attraction type the iron disc is pulled into the coil, and the pointer turns with it. In the repulsion type two iron vanes sit inside the coil, one fixed and one on the spindle. The coil magnetises both vanes in the same direction, so they repel and the moving vane swings.

T = ½ I² (dL/dθ)

T is deflecting torque in N m; I is coil current in A; L is the inductance of the coil in H, which changes as the iron vane moves; dL/dθ is the rate of change of inductance with deflection θ in radians. Torque therefore goes as I².

The square law has two consequences. First, reversing the current leaves the torque in the same direction, so the meter works on AC. Second, on AC the average torque follows the mean square of the current, so the meter reads the RMS value directly and gives the same reading on AC and DC for the same heating effect.

The cost is the scale: deflection goes as I², so divisions are cramped near zero and wide near full scale. Iron adds hysteresis and eddy-current errors, the reading drifts with frequency, and stray fields disturb it. Against that, the meter is cheap, robust, has no magnet to weaken, and can be built for large currents.

Range extension, and a moving-coil meter on AC

A PMMC movement has a small full-scale current I_m, often 50 μA to 1 mA, and its own resistance R_m. To read a larger current, connect a low resistance R_sh in parallel. This is the shunt, and most of the current bypasses the coil.

R_sh = I_m R_m / (I − I_m)

I is the current to be measured in A; I_m is the full-scale current of the movement in A; R_m is the movement resistance in Ω; R_sh is the shunt resistance in Ω.

R_se = V / I_m − R_m

V is the voltage to be measured in V; I_m is the full-scale current in A; R_m is the movement resistance in Ω; R_se is the series multiplier resistance in Ω.

The multiplier R_se is connected in series, so the full-scale current flows when the rated voltage is applied. On AC the current reverses every half cycle, the torque reverses, and the pointer stays at zero. A bridge rectifier in front of the coil fixes the direction, and the meter then reads the average of the rectified current. For a sine wave, average = 0.637 I_peak and RMS = 0.707 I_peak; their ratio, the form factor 1.11, is built into the scale. That reading is correct only for a sine wave. A moving-iron meter needs no rectifier and reads RMS of any waveform.

PMMC movements sit in laboratory ammeters and voltmeters, analog multimeters and galvanometers. Moving-iron meters appear on switchboard panel meters and cheap clamp meters, where ruggedness matters more than accuracy.

Worked example

Question. Two ammeters have the same full-scale current of 5 A. One is a moving-coil meter with θ ∝ I; the other is a moving-iron meter with θ ∝ I². (a) Find the deflection as a fraction of full scale at 2 A for each. (b) Find the current at which the moving-iron meter reaches half scale.

  1. Moving coil: fraction of full scale = θ/θ_FS = I/I_FS = 2 A / 5 A = 0.4, that is 40% of full scale.
  2. Moving iron: fraction = (I/I_FS)² = (2/5)² = 0.4² = 0.16, that is 16% of full scale.
  3. Half scale on the moving-iron meter: (I/5)² = 0.5, so I/5 = √0.5 = 0.7071.
  4. Therefore I = 5 × 0.7071 = 3.54 A.

Answer. At 2 A the moving-coil meter shows 0.4 of full scale (40%) and the moving-iron meter 0.16 (16%); the moving-iron meter reaches half scale only at 3.54 A.

Exam traps

  • Writing θ ∝ 2I for a moving-iron meter. The torque goes as current squared, not twice the current: at 2 A on a 5 A meter the fraction is (2/5)² = 0.16, not 0.4.
  • Expecting a PMMC meter to read AC directly. Without a rectifier the torque reverses every half cycle and the pointer stays at zero; with a rectifier it reads 1.11 × average, which equals RMS only for a sine wave.
  • Swapping shunt and multiplier. The shunt goes in parallel with the movement (ammeter) and is small; the multiplier goes in series (voltmeter) and is large. The wrong one gives a wrong reading or a burnt coil.
  • Assuming a moving-iron meter reads average on AC. It responds to I², so it reads RMS.
  • Using only the full-scale current or only the coil resistance in a range formula. Both I_m and R_m are needed: R_sh = I_m R_m/(I − I_m) and R_se = V/I_m − R_m.

Analyze A moving-iron meter and a rectifier-type moving-coil meter are both calibrated on a sine wave and read 230 V. What does each read on a square wave of the same RMS value? 5 marks

The moving-iron meter still reads 230 V because it responds to mean square, which is true RMS. The rectified moving-coil meter reads 1.11 × average; for a square wave average equals RMS, so it reads about 1.11 × 230 = 255 V, roughly 11% high.

Analyze Why does reversing the current not reverse the reading of a moving-iron meter, while it drives a PMMC pointer backwards? 3 marks

Moving-iron torque depends on I², which stays positive when I reverses, so direction does not matter. PMMC torque depends on the sign of I, so a reversed current reverses the torque and pushes the pointer off scale.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Analog movements survive where a cheap, always-on indication matters: switchboard panel meters, automotive ammeters and analog multimeters. Modern designs sense current with a shunt plus an ADC, or with a Hall-effect sensor, and compute the value digitally. Every commercial instrument carries an accuracy class, and calibration laboratories check it against a standard. True-RMS meters do the squaring and averaging electronically, which is exactly what the moving-iron vane did with mechanics. 6.2 Energy meter, megger, digital multimeter and DSO

Four instruments answer four different questions. An energy meter counts how much energy a house has used, in kilowatt-hours. A megger checks whether insulation can still hold off a high voltage. A digital multimeter reads voltage, current or resistance at one point. A digital storage oscilloscope shows how a signal changes with time. An electrician, a wiring inspector and a final-year project team all need these tools, and each tool answers only its own question. Using the wrong one gives a number that looks correct and means nothing.

You need: Chapter 6.1 for how a coil produces deflection, and Unit 2 for RMS values and power factor.

Induction energy meter: counting kWh

Power tells you how fast energy is used. A bill needs the total, so the meter must add power over time. The induction energy meter, the drum-shaped meter on a wall, does this with a rotating aluminium disc.

Two coils drive the disc. The voltage coil, also called the pressure coil, is connected across the supply. It has many turns of thin wire, so its current is proportional to the supply voltage V. The current coil is in series with the load. It has a few turns of thick wire, so it carries the load current I. Both coils make alternating flux that cuts the disc and drives eddy currents inside it. Those eddy currents react with the flux and produce a torque proportional to V × I × cos φ, which is the power P.

A permanent brake magnet sits over the disc. Its field produces a drag that rises with disc speed. The disc speeds up until the drag equals the driving torque, so the steady speed is proportional to power. Speed proportional to power means the number of turns N is proportional to energy.

A worm gear drives the register, whose dials show kilowatt-hours. The meter constant K is the number of disc revolutions per kWh, typically 600 to 1200 for a domestic meter.

E = N / K

E is the energy in kWh (1 unit = 1 kWh); N is the number of disc revolutions; K is the meter constant in revolutions per kWh.

Megger and digital multimeter

The megger measures insulation resistance. Insulation must hold off the working voltage, so a megger applies a high DC test voltage, commonly 500 V or 1000 V, from a hand-cranked generator or a battery-driven inverter. It shows resistance in MΩ. A common ohmmeter uses about 2 V, far too low to expose a weak spot that would break down at 230 V. Some syllabi call the megger an insulation resistance tester.

Isolate the circuit first, and disconnect loads and sensitive electronics. Connect the megger between live and earth, then between live and neutral. Never use a megger on a live circuit: the high voltage can shock a person and destroy the instrument. Discharge the circuit after the test, because cable capacitance holds charge. The scale runs from 0 to ∞ and is cramped near ∞. Many codes accept about 1 MΩ as the minimum for a 230 V installation; healthy new wiring reads several MΩ.

The digital multimeter (DMM) converts an analog quantity into a number with an ADC and shows it on a display. Voltage ranges have a high input impedance, typically 10 MΩ, so the meter draws almost no current and disturbs the circuit very little. Auto-ranging selects the range by itself. Continuity mode beeps when resistance is below a few tens of ohms. Diode test shows the forward drop, about 0.6 V for silicon. Current ranges have a very low resistance, so the meter must go in series with the load, never across the supply.

Digital storage oscilloscope

A DSO plots voltage against time. It shows shape, not just a number, so you can see distortion, spikes and timing.

  1. The probe signal passes through an attenuator and amplifier, which scale it to suit the ADC.
  2. The ADC samples the signal at the sample rate and writes the samples into memory.
  3. The trigger decides when to record; a rising edge on a chosen level is the usual choice.
  4. The time base sets seconds per division on the screen, and the memory holds the record so a single event can be studied.

The probe ground clip is connected to the oscilloscope chassis, which is earthed. It is not a floating input. Clipping that ground to a live wire shorts the live wire to earth through the probe. Use a differential probe or an isolation transformer when neither point is at earth potential. Keep the sample rate at least twice the highest signal frequency you want to see.

Exam traps

  • Energy is in kWh, not kW. 5.52 kWh is energy; 0.92 kW is power. Writing kWh as kW loses the whole idea of the meter.
  • A megger must never be used on a live circuit. The high DC test voltage is dangerous to the user and destroys the instrument.
  • A DSO ground clip is earthed, not a floating input. Clipping it to a live conductor short-circuits the supply through the probe.
  • A DMM in current mode has almost zero resistance. Connected across the supply instead of in series, it draws a huge current and blows its fuse.
  • The meter constant is in revolutions per kWh, not per kW. Rev/kW would describe speed, not energy.

6.3 Sensors and transducers: Hall effect and proximity sensors

A sensor notices a physical change; a transducer turns that change into an electrical signal an instrument or controller can read. The Hall effect does this with a magnetic field: a thin semiconductor carrying current produces a small transverse voltage when a magnet comes near. A proximity sensor does it without contact, switching its output when a metal, plastic or other target enters range. You meet both in a phone flip cover, a motor speed wheel, a card reader and a factory conveyor.

You need: magnetic flux density and the force on a moving charge from Unit 3, and the op-amp comparator from Unit 4; 6.1 reminds you how a deflection becomes a reading.

Sensor, transducer and signal type

A sensor responds to a physical quantity such as temperature, light, pressure or magnetic field. A transducer converts one form of energy into another. An electrical transducer converts the physical quantity into a voltage, current or resistance change. Every sensor with an electrical output is a transducer. A loudspeaker is a transducer but not a sensor, because it converts electrical energy to sound, not the other way.

Transducers are grouped by how they get their output energy.

  • Active transducer: it generates its own voltage from the physical quantity. A thermocouple gives a small voltage from a temperature difference; a piezoelectric disc gives a voltage when pressed.
  • Passive transducer: it needs an external supply and changes its resistance, capacitance or voltage under that supply. A strain gauge, an RTD and a Hall element are passive.

The output may be analog (a continuous voltage, such as 0.04 V from a Hall element) or digital (a switch that is HIGH or LOW). A digital output is easier for a microcontroller to count, so many sensors include a comparator inside the package. The useful specifications are range (the physical span it can measure), sensitivity (output change per unit input), resolution (smallest detectable change), accuracy (closeness to the true value) and hysteresis (the difference between switch-on and switch-off points).

The Hall effect

Take a thin rectangular slab of semiconductor. Pass a steady current I along its length. Apply a magnetic field B perpendicular to the face of the slab. The moving charge carriers feel a sideways force, so electrons pile up on one edge and leave positive charge on the other. This charge builds a transverse electric field until its force balances the magnetic force. The steady voltage across the width is the Hall voltage.

VH = KH × I × B

VH is the Hall voltage in volts (V); KH is the Hall sensitivity in V/(A·T); I is the current through the element in amperes (A); B is the magnetic flux density in tesla (T). For a slab of thickness t, carrier density n and carrier charge q, KH = 1/(n q t), so a thin, lightly doped semiconductor gives a larger voltage.

The sign of VH reverses when B reverses or when I reverses. That sign carries direction information: a north pole and a south pole give opposite readings. The voltage is small, often tens of millivolts, so the signal chain is: Hall element, amplifier, comparator. The amplifier raises the level and the comparator turns it into a clean HIGH or LOW at a chosen threshold. The figure shows this chain with the worked example's values.

Hall sensors measure magnetic field, and through it they measure speed, position and current. A toothed wheel or a ring of magnets on a motor shaft passes the sensor and produces one pulse per tooth or magnet. A phone flip cover uses a small magnet and a Hall switch to blank the screen. A clamp-type current sensor uses the field around a wire; no electrical connection to the wire is needed. Brushless DC motors use Hall sensors to know rotor position before switching the coils (see Unit 3, special machines).

Proximity sensors

A proximity sensor detects an object without touching it. The target comes within a rated distance, called the sensing range, and the output changes state. Three common types use different physics.

Inductive. A coil and oscillator create a high-frequency field at the face. A metal target nearby carries eddy currents, which load the oscillator and reduce its amplitude. A comparator detects the drop and switches the output. Inductive sensors detect metals only, with ranges of a few millimetres. They suit a steel can on a conveyor or a metal cam on a rotating shaft.

Capacitive. Two plates at the face form a capacitor with the air in front. Any material whose dielectric constant differs from air changes the capacitance when it enters the field. Capacitive sensors detect metal, plastic, glass, wood and even liquid level through a wall, with ranges up to about 25 mm.

Optical. An emitter sends light and a receiver looks for it. In through-beam types the target blocks the beam; in diffuse types the target reflects light back. Optical sensors see almost any object and reach much farther, but dust, rain or a transparent bottle can fool them.

The three-wire output is named after the transistor inside. An NPN output sinks current to 0 V when active, so the load connects between the output and the positive supply. A PNP output sources current from the positive supply when active, so the load connects between the output and 0 V. Reversing the expected type gives a load that never switches. Hysteresis makes the switch-on distance larger than the switch-off distance; without it, a target resting at the threshold would chatter. Signal conditioning is therefore an amplifier or oscillator, then a comparator with a small hysteresis band, as in Unit 4.

Worked example

Question. A Hall sensor has sensitivity KH = 20 V/(A·T). It carries a current I = 10 mA in a magnetic field B = 0.2 T. Find the Hall voltage in millivolts. The amplifier after it has a gain of 100; find the amplifier output.

  1. Write the given values with units: KH = 20 V/(A·T), I = 10 mA = 10 × 10-3 A = 0.01 A, B = 0.2 T, gain A = 100.
  2. Use VH = KH × I × B.
  3. Substitute: VH = 20 × 0.01 × 0.2. First 20 × 0.01 = 0.2 V/T; then 0.2 × 0.2 = 0.04 V.
  4. Convert to millivolts: 0.04 V × 1000 = 40 mV.
  5. Amplifier output = gain × VH = 100 × 0.04 V = 4 V.

Answer. The Hall voltage is 0.04 V, that is 40 mV, and the amplifier output is 4 V.

Exam traps

  • The Hall voltage reverses when either B or I reverses. A sign slip means the sensor reports the wrong pole or the wrong direction of rotation.
  • Inductive proximity sensors detect metals only; capacitive types detect non-metals too. An examiner often asks which one sees a plastic bottle.
  • Keep the units of KH as V/(A·T) and put I in amperes, not milliamperes. Substituting 10 instead of 0.01 gives an answer 1000 times too large.
  • Nominal sensing range is not the guaranteed operating point. Hysteresis and temperature drift mean you should work inside the range, not at its edge.
  • An NPN output sinks current and a PNP output sources current. Wiring the load to the wrong rail gives no switching and is a common lab mistake.

6.4 Sources of electric power: conventional and renewable

Most electric power is produced by turning an energy source into mechanical rotation, then into alternating voltage. Solar photovoltaic is the exception: light is converted straight into DC electricity, with no moving part. Coal and nuclear are non-renewable: the fuel is used once. Solar and wind are renewable: nature refills the source. The choice affects fuel cost, emissions, land and reliability. In India, coal supplies most generation, while solar and wind are growing additions. This chapter follows the main energy converters and compares them.

You need Unit 3, Magnetic Circuits and Electrical Machines, for the synchronous generator, and Unit 2, Single-Phase and Three-Phase AC Circuits, for AC generation.

Coal thermal and nuclear plants

A coal thermal plant burns coal to make steam, and steam drives a turbine. The turbine turns a synchronous generator. The generator makes AC. This chain is a heat engine: fuel heat becomes steam, then shaft work, then electrical energy.

  1. Coal is burned in the furnace. Water in the boiler becomes high-pressure steam.
  2. Steam expands through the turbine and spins its shaft.
  3. The shaft drives a synchronous generator, which produces AC.
  4. Exhaust steam goes to a condenser, becomes water, and is pumped back.
  5. The generator output is stepped up by a transformer; see 6.5.

A nuclear plant replaces the coal furnace with a reactor. Fission of uranium releases heat. A primary coolant carries heat to a steam generator. Steam then drives a turbine and generator. Nuclear fuel is finite, so nuclear is non-renewable. The condenser and steam loop work as in a coal plant.

η = Eout ÷ Ein

η is overall efficiency, no unit, often written as a percentage; Eout is electrical energy delivered in kWh or J; Ein is fuel energy input in the same unit.

Solar photovoltaic and wind

A photovoltaic (PV) cell is a large PN junction. Light creates electron-hole pairs; the junction separates them and gives DC voltage. Cells are connected in series for voltage and in parallel for current to form a module. Modules form an array. Irradiance is sunlight power per unit area, in W/m². Standard test irradiance is 1000 W/m². High cell temperature lowers voltage and output.

A wind turbine converts kinetic energy of moving air. Blades turn a hub. A gearbox matches low blade speed to generator speed. The generator makes AC. Cut-in speed is the lowest wind speed at which output starts. Rated speed is the wind speed at which the turbine reaches rated power. Above cut-out speed the turbine stops to protect itself.

Pwind = ½ ρ A v³

Pwind is wind power in W; ρ is air density in kg/m³; A is swept rotor area in m²; v is wind speed in m/s. Actual electrical power is lower by the power coefficient Cp.

Hydro uses falling water to spin a turbine. Biomass burns plant or waste fuel to make steam. Both are renewable when managed. Rajasthan solar parks and Tamil Nadu wind farms are Indian examples.

Comparing sources

Capital cost is the build cost. Fuel cost is the running cost. Availability is how firm the output is. Emissions and land are side effects. Coal has a large fuel bill per kWh and high emissions; fuel is cheap per kg but used in bulk. Nuclear has high build cost and radioactive waste. Solar and wind have no fuel cost but weather-dependent output.

6.5 Layout of generation, transmission and distribution

Electric power is made in a few large plants and used in millions of small places. The layout of generation, transmission and distribution is the chain of transformers and lines that joins the two: generate at 11–25 kV, step up for the long run, step down at substations, and step down once more to 400 V near the load. The reason is loss. A higher voltage means a smaller current for the same power, and line loss falls with the square of the current.

You need: three-phase star and delta connections and line/phase values (see Unit 2, Single-Phase and Three-Phase AC Circuits) and the transformer working principle (see Unit 3, Magnetic Circuits and Electrical Machines).

Why the voltage goes up and down

A plant generator produces 11 kV to 25 kV. Homes and shops need 230 V, often 200 km away. Sending 11 kV that far turns most of the power into heat in the line. So the voltage is raised at the plant, carried high, and lowered in steps near the load.

P = √3 V_L I_L cos φ

P is the total three-phase active power in W; V_L is the line-to-line voltage in V; I_L is the line current in A; cos φ is the power factor.

P_loss = 3 I_L² R = R P² / (V_L² cos²φ)

P_loss is the total heat lost in all three line conductors in W; R is the resistance of one conductor in Ω; the other symbols are as above.

The loss falls as the square of the voltage. Doubling V_L halves I_L and divides the loss by four. At 400 kV the line loss is a small fraction of the power sent; at 11 kV it would be a large share. This is why long-distance transmission is never done at the generator voltage.

The chain, stage by stage

  1. Generation: the alternator produces 11–25 kV. Building it for 400 kV would need insulation that cannot be cooled and far costlier windings.
  2. Step-up transformer: raises the voltage to 400 kV, 220 kV or 132 kV. The current falls by the same ratio, so a thinner conductor carries the same power.
  3. Transmission line: carries power to the city. Standard AC levels in India are 765 kV, 400 kV, 220 kV, 132 kV and 66 kV; 66 kV is subtransmission in some regions.
  4. Step-down substations: 400/220 kV, then 220/66 kV, then 66/33 kV, then 33/11 kV. Each one feeds a smaller area than the one before.
  5. Primary distribution: 11 kV lines run along the roads on poles to each neighbourhood.
  6. Distribution transformer: 11 kV/400 V, mounted on a pole or in a small yard, one per street or building block.
  7. Consumer: receives 230 V single-phase or 400 V three-phase.

The 11 kV and 400 V networks are usually radial, meaning one path with no loop back. Protection is then simple, but one fault can darken a whole street.

Inside the 11 kV/400 V substation

  1. Incoming 11 kV feeder, overhead or underground, with an isolator and a circuit breaker.
  2. 11 kV busbar: a metal bar that feeds several transformer bays.
  3. Transformer: primary winding in delta on the 11 kV side, secondary in star with the neutral brought out. The delta lets triplen harmonic currents circulate inside it, so they do not reach the line voltages; the star point can be earthed.
  4. 400 V LT busbar: receives the secondary. Three phases and a neutral leave it.
  5. Outgoing feeders: each street gets a fuse-switch unit or an MCB, and live, neutral and earth run to the consumer.

The star secondary gives two voltages. Between any two phases the line voltage is 400 V. From one phase to the neutral the phase voltage is 400 / √3 = 231 V, written 230 V. A single-phase load goes between one phase and the neutral; a three-phase motor goes on all three phases plus earth.

Current transformers on the 11 kV side feed an overcurrent relay that trips the breaker. On the 400 V side the MCBs do the same job street by street. Earthing the star point holds the neutral near earth potential (see 6.8).

Reading a single-line diagram

A single-line diagram (SLD) draws a three-phase circuit as one line, because all three phases normally behave alike. It is the drawing an engineer checks before switching.

  • Busbar: a thick horizontal bar.
  • Transformer: two interlocked circles, with Δ or Y written beside them.
  • Circuit breaker: a small square. Isolator: a diagonal line (knife switch), often drawn open. It has no arc-quenching, so it is opened only after the circuit breaker has cleared the current.
  • Rating beside each item: 11 kV/400 V, 100 kVA, 50 Hz.
  • Read from source to load, checking the voltage at every step. Voltage rises once, at the step-up transformer, and then falls at every step-down substation and at the distribution transformer. A rise where a step-down is expected means an arrow or a winding symbol is reversed.

Exam traps

  • 400 V is the line voltage of the three-phase four-wire supply and 230 V is the phase voltage: 400 / √3 = 231 V, rounded to 230 V. A single-phase consumer gets one phase and the neutral, never 400 V.
  • A transformer changes voltage and current, not power. 100 kVA in at 11 kV is 100 kVA out at 400 V minus small loss; the currents are in inverse ratio to the voltages.
  • In P_loss = 3 I_L² R the 3 counts the three phase conductors. Writing I²R alone for a three-phase line gives one third of the true loss.
  • Generation is at 11–25 kV, not at 220 kV. The step-up transformer sits between the generator and the line; saying the generator produces 220 kV is a standard exam error.

6.6 Electricity tariff, energy audit and rooftop solar

An electricity bill is not just units used. The tariff turns meter units into rupees using fixed and energy charges, often with slabs. An energy audit lists loads, measures kWh and finds where money leaks. A rooftop solar plant is sized from monthly kWh, panel wattage and peak sun hours, and net metering lets the grid absorb surplus. One unit on the meter is 1 kWh: the energy of a 1 kW load running for 1 hour.

You need: Chapter 6.2 for energy meter reading, Chapter 6.4 for solar as a source, and Chapter 6.5 for distribution and the meter.

Tariff: from meter units to rupees

A tariff is the rule book that converts meter units into a bill. It exists because the electricity board must recover fixed costs such as wires and meters, plus running costs such as fuel, even when a home uses little power. A factory and a home pay different tariffs because their load shapes differ.

Bill = fixed charge + Σ(slab rate × slab kWh) + fuel surcharge + PF penalty − rebate

Σ means sum over slabs; slab rate is ₹ per kWh in that slab; slab kWh is the units in that slab; PF penalty is the power-factor charge; rebate is any credit.

  • Fixed charge is ₹ per month for the connection. A home with zero units still pays it.
  • Energy charge is ₹ per kWh, often in slabs: the first 100 kWh at one rate, the next 100 kWh at a higher rate.
  • Fuel surcharge is a ₹ per kWh adjustment when fuel prices change.
  • Power factor penalty applies to large consumers. Low power factor means more current for the same kW, so the tariff penalises PF below a limit, often 0.90.

A 100 W fan running for 10 hours uses 1 kWh. If the energy charge is ₹7 per kWh, that fan costs ₹7 for those 10 hours, before fixed charges. Tariff orders are public documents; read the one for your state rather than guessing slab rates.

Energy audit: find the high-use loads

An energy audit is a systematic check of where electricity is used and what can be cut without hurting comfort or work. It exists because most homes do not know which appliance drives the bill. The audit turns guesses into ranked kWh numbers.

  1. List every load: lamp, fan, refrigerator, geyser, air conditioner, pump. Note its wattage from the nameplate and its hours per day.
  2. Compute monthly kWh for each load from the formula below.
  3. Read the energy meter at the start and end of a normal week. Compare the meter kWh with your list to catch missing loads.
  4. Rank loads by monthly kWh. Usually the air conditioner, geyser and refrigerator come first, not lamps.
  5. Estimate saving for each fix: LED lamps, five-star fans, AC setpoint at 26 °C, removing standby power, or a solar water heater.

Monthly kWh = (W × h × d) ÷ 1000

W is load power in watts; h is hours per day; d is days per month; 1000 converts Wh to kWh.

A 1500 W geyser used 1 hour daily for 30 days uses (1500 × 1 × 30) ÷ 1000 = 45 kWh per month. At ₹7 per kWh it costs ₹315. A 60 W fan used 8 hours daily uses 14.4 kWh, about ₹101. The geyser is the better audit target.

Rooftop solar: size, inverter and net metering

A rooftop solar plant has panels that make DC, an inverter that changes DC to AC, and a net meter that records both import from the grid and export to it. Size the array from daily kWh need and local sun, not from roof area alone.

Daily kWh = array kW × PSH × PR

array kW is panel capacity in kW; PSH is peak sun hours, numerically hours at 1 kW/m²; PR is performance ratio, a fraction such as 0.80 for heat and wiring losses.

Panel count = array kW ÷ panel kW

panel kW is one panel's rating in kW, for example 500 W = 0.5 kW.

The inverter's AC rating is chosen close to the array kW, after checking its datasheet and DC/AC ratio. A grid-tied inverter is needed for net metering. A subsidy is often ₹ per kW but may be capped by kW or by a total amount. Net cost = cost − subsidy.

Net import kWh = import kWh − export kWh

import is grid energy used; export is surplus solar sent to the grid. The energy charge is normally on net import, not on total solar generation. Some syllabi call net metering net billing loosely; strictly, net billing pays export at a separate rate.

Payback in months = net cost ÷ monthly saving

net cost is ₹ after subsidy; monthly saving is ₹ per month from avoided grid units.

Worked example

Question. A home uses 300 kWh per month. It plans a 3 kW rooftop system using 500 W panels. Average generation is 4 kWh per kW per day for 30 days. Find the panel count, monthly generation and simple payback. Cost is ₹60,000 per kW, subsidy is ₹15,000 per kW, saved energy is valued at ₹7 per kWh for 300 kWh per month, and export credit is ignored.

  1. Array size = 3 kW. One panel = 500 W = 0.5 kW. Panel count = 3 ÷ 0.5 = 6 panels.
  2. Daily generation = 3 kW × 4 kWh per kW per day = 12 kWh per day. Monthly generation = 12 × 30 = 360 kWh.
  3. Gross cost = 3 × ₹60,000 = ₹1,80,000. Subsidy = 3 × ₹15,000 = ₹45,000. Net cost = ₹1,80,000 − ₹45,000 = ₹1,35,000.
  4. Billed saving uses 300 kWh per month; the extra 60 kWh is export and is ignored. Monthly saving = 300 × ₹7 = ₹2,100.
  5. Payback = ₹1,35,000 ÷ ₹2,100 = 64.2857 months ≈ 64.3 months.

Answer. The plant needs 6 panels of 500 W, generates 360 kWh per month, costs ₹1,35,000 after subsidy, saves ₹2,100 per month, and has a simple payback of 64.3 months.

Exam traps

  • kW is power and kWh is energy. A 3 kW array does not make 3 kWh per hour unless the sun is at 1 kW/m² and losses are ignored.
  • A subsidy of ₹15,000 per kW may be capped after a certain kW or by a total amount. Read the scheme, not just the per-kW rate.
  • Net metering needs a grid-tied inverter and a bidirectional net meter. An off-grid solar system gets no export credit.
  • The energy charge is on net import, not on total solar generation. Export may be credited at a lower rate or carried forward.
  • Fixed charges and fuel surcharge can remain even when solar cuts units to zero. Power factor penalty is not for a typical home.

6.7 Residential wiring, safety measures and safety devices

Residential wiring carries electric power from the supply authority's service entrance to every lamp, socket, geyser and air conditioner in a home. Three wires run together: live at about 230 V, neutral at near earth potential, and earth for protection. The meter records energy, a main switch isolates the house, a distribution board splits the load into sub-circuits, and each sub-circuit has a fuse or miniature circuit breaker (MCB). A residual current circuit breaker (RCCB) watches for current leaking to earth. The purpose is simple: keep wires from overheating and keep people from shock.

You need: Ohm's law and power from Unit 1, DC Circuits and Network Theorems; Chapter 6.6 for load and bill; Chapter 6.2 for the energy meter.

Service entrance to sub-circuit

The service entrance is where the utility's cable meets the building. It brings live (also called phase or line), neutral and protective earth. Live and neutral pass through the energy meter, which counts kilowatt-hours (kWh) for billing; see Chapter 6.2. Next is the main switch, a two-pole switch that disconnects both live and neutral so the board can be worked on safely. The supply then reaches the distribution board, where an RCCB sits first and a row of MCBs follows. Each MCB feeds one sub-circuit: lights and fans, 5 A sockets, a 16 A socket for a geyser or air conditioner.

  1. Identify each sub-circuit and add the wattage of every appliance on it.
  2. Apply a diversity factor D, because not all appliances run together; D is often 0.5 to 0.8 for homes.
  3. Find design current I_b = (D × ΣP) / V, with ΣP in watts and V = 230 V.
  4. Choose cable whose current capacity I_z is at least I_b.
  5. Choose MCB rating I_n so that I_b ≤ I_n ≤ I_z.
  • Wire colour code: live brown or red, neutral blue or black, earth green or yellow-green. Older installations may use red live, black neutral and green earth.
  • A light circuit is wired with 1.0 or 1.5 mm² copper and a 5 A or 6 A MCB. A 16 A power circuit uses thicker copper.
  • The earth wire goes to every socket and metal body. It must never pass through a fuse or a switch.

Fuse and MCB: protecting the wiring

A fuse is a thin wire or strip that melts when current exceeds its rating. The rewirable fuse has a porcelain holder and a replaceable wire; it is cheap but slow and can be miswired. An HRC (high rupturing capacity) fuse has a ceramic body filled with sand and a silver element; it clears a short circuit in a few milliseconds without flame. Both protect the cable from overheating. They do not protect a person from shock.

An MCB (miniature circuit breaker) does the same job and resets. It has two trips. A bimetallic strip bends slowly on overload and trips after seconds or minutes. A magnetic coil trips instantly on a large short-circuit current. Standard ratings are 6, 10, 16, 20, 25 and 32 A. Type B, C and D curves set the instantaneous trip level.

I = P / V

I is current in amperes (A), P is power in watts (W), and V is supply voltage in volts (V), taken as 230 V for a single-phase home.

I_n ≥ 1.25 × I

I_n is the nominal MCB rating in amperes and the 1.25 factor gives a 25 % margin for continuous load. Also I_n ≤ I_z, the cable current capacity.

RCCB and safety measures

An RCCB (residual current circuit breaker) compares the current in live and neutral. In a healthy circuit the two currents are equal, so their difference is zero. If a person touches live, some current flows through the body to earth and returns through the soil, not through the neutral. The difference, called residual current, operates a small current transformer and trips the breaker. A 30 mA RCCB is used for human protection; 100 mA or 300 mA units protect against fire. Some syllabi call this device an RCD (residual current device); an ELCB (earth leakage circuit breaker) is the older name.

An RCCB does not detect overload or a live-to-neutral short circuit, because those currents still return through neutral and cancel. It must be used with an MCB or fuse. Safety measures in a home include insulated conductors, closed boxes, earthing of metal bodies (see Chapter 6.8), a main switch that can be locked off, and never putting the earth wire through a fuse or switch. Before touching a circuit, switch off and test.

Arc fault detection devices (AFDDs) are appearing in newer codes to catch loose connections that start fires.

6.8 Earthing: need, types and working

Earthing (some syllabi say grounding) is a deliberate low-resistance connection from exposed metal and from the supply neutral point to the soil. It exists so a live-to-metal fault sends a large current back to the source and blows the fuse or trips the MCB. It also gives lightning and surge currents a path to earth. You meet it at every three-pin socket, water pump, inverter body and distribution transformer. The earth connection must be real, not the neutral wire.

You need: Chapter 6.7 for live, neutral and earth wiring; Chapter 6.2 for the megger; and Ohm's law from Unit 1, DC Circuits and Network Theorems.

Why earthing is needed

Earthing is a safety connection. The exposed metal case of a fan, washing machine or inverter is normally not live. If insulation fails, the live wire touches the case. Without earthing, the case stays at 230 V. A person who touches it and stands on a wet floor completes a path through the body to earth. With earthing, the case is wired to an earth electrode buried in soil. The fault current gets a low-resistance path back to the supply. The current becomes large, so the fuse or MCB trips and removes the supply. The danger lasts for a short time. When there is no fault, the metal case sits at earth potential.

Earthing also holds metalwork at a steady reference potential. It gives lightning and switching surges a path to soil instead of through equipment. It stabilises the voltage of a system whose neutral is earthed at the transformer. Safety of persons is the first need; protection of equipment is the second. In Indian homes, the third pin of a 230 V socket is the earth pin. It must go to a real earth pit, never to the neutral.

Types of earth electrodes

An earth electrode is the metal part buried in soil. The earth wire joins it to the equipment. The choice depends on soil resistivity, fault current and space. Plate and pipe electrodes are common in homes; mesh is for high-current sites. Common types are listed below.

A plate electrode uses a copper or GI plate in a pit filled with charcoal and salt. A pipe electrode is a GI pipe driven vertically into the ground. A rod electrode is a copper-bonded steel rod, often driven deep. A strip electrode is a GI or copper strip laid in a trench. A mesh electrode is a grid of rods and strips, used where the current is large. Chemical or maintenance-free earthing surrounds the electrode with a backfill such as bentonite, which holds moisture, or graphite, which is conductive. The bentonite backfill holds moisture and keeps the earth resistance stable in dry soil.

How the fault path works

The fault path is a loop. It runs from the live wire, through the fault, into the metal body, along the earth wire, into the earth electrode, through the soil, and back to the earthed neutral of the source. The earth wire and electrode must have low resistance.

Let R_e be the total resistance of this earth path in ohms. Let V be the supply voltage to earth in volts. The fault current is:

I_f ≈ V / R_e

I_f is the fault current in amperes; V is the supply voltage to earth in volts; R_e is the total earth path resistance in ohms, including the earth wire, electrode and soil return.

For a 230 V supply and R_e = 5 Ω, I_f = 230 / 5 = 46 A. A 16 A MCB sees 46 A as an overcurrent above its rating and trips. If R_e is 100 Ω, I_f is only 2.3 A. A 16 A MCB may not trip, and the metal body stays live. Touch voltage is the voltage a person feels between the metal body and the ground. A low R_e makes the fault current large, so the trip is fast. When the fault itself has some resistance, a low R_e also keeps the touch voltage lower. The earth connection does not absorb current. It provides a return path.

Earth resistance, testing and care

Earth resistance is measured with an earth tester. A three-terminal tester passes a current through the electrode and a current probe, and measures the voltage between the electrode and a potential probe. A clamp-on earth tester measures the earth resistance without disconnecting the electrode. A megger-type instrument is used in some tests. Measure in dry weather, because dry soil gives the worst value. The exact limit comes from the local wiring rule; domestic pits are usually kept to a few ohms or less.

  • Do keep the earth wire joints tight and clean.
  • Do water the earth pit in summer, if it is a conventional pit.
  • Do test the earth resistance at fixed intervals.
  • Do not use the neutral wire as earth. Neutral carries load current and can be at a voltage.
  • Do not disconnect the earth wire to stop nuisance tripping. Find the fault instead.
  • Do not use a plastic pipe or a painted metal part as an electrode.

Worked example

Question. A 230 V metal-bodied appliance has a fault from live to body. The earth wire and electrode have a total resistance of 5 Ω. Find the fault current. Will a 16 A MCB see this as a large fault? Take the supply voltage to earth as 230 V.

  1. Write the data: V = 230 V, R_e = 5 Ω.
  2. Use I_f = V / R_e.
  3. Substitute: I_f = 230 / 5 = 46 A.
  4. Compare with the MCB rating: 46 A > 16 A. The ratio is 46/16 = 2.875, so the current is well above the rating.
  5. The MCB sees this as an overcurrent and trips, clearing the fault.

Answer. The fault current is 46 A, and yes, the 16 A MCB sees it as a large fault and trips.

Exam traps

  • Earthing is not a current absorber. It is a low-resistance return path that makes the fuse or MCB trip.
  • A high earth resistance lowers the fault current. The MCB may not trip, so an RCCB is added for shock protection.
  • Never use neutral as earth. Neutral carries load current, so its voltage is not zero.
  • Do not confuse earth resistance with insulation resistance. The megger checks insulation; an earth tester checks the electrode.
  • In I_f = V/R_e, use R_e in ohms, not kΩ. A 5 kΩ earth path gives only 0.046 A, not 46 A.

Takeaways

  1. A moving-coil meter gives a deflection proportional to current and suits DC, while a moving-iron meter gives a deflection proportional to current squared and therefore works on AC and DC both.
  2. Every indicating instrument needs three torques: deflecting to move the pointer, controlling to bring it back, and damping to stop the swing.
  3. Energy meter, megger, DMM and DSO answer different questions: how much energy, how good is the insulation, what are V, I and R, and what does the waveform look like.
  4. A transducer turns a physical quantity into an electrical signal; Hall sensors use a magnetic field and proximity sensors detect objects without touching them.
  5. Electric power comes from non-renewable sources such as coal thermal and nuclear, and from renewable sources such as solar and wind.
  6. Power is generated at 11-25 kV, stepped up for transmission, stepped down at substations, and delivered at 230 V single-phase or 400 V three-phase.
  7. The bill is fixed charge plus energy charge plus fuel surcharge, with a penalty if power factor is low; an energy audit and rooftop solar both reduce the units bought.
  8. Residential wiring uses live, neutral and earth with an MCB for overload and short circuit and an RCCB for earth leakage, and earthing is what makes those devices work.

Remember and understand

Understand Draw the construction of a moving-coil (permanent-magnet moving-coil) instrument and explain how the deflecting, controlling and damping torques are produced. Explain why it indicates only on DC. 13 marks

Parts: permanent magnet with curved poles, soft-iron core, coil on an aluminium former, spiral springs, pointer and scale; Deflecting torque from force F = B·I·l·N on coil sides, so torque is proportional to current — a linear scale; Controlling torque from the spiral springs, so deflection θ = k·I; Damping by eddy currents induced in the aluminium former, giving torque proportional to speed; On AC the torque reverses every half cycle and the inertia averages it to zero, so the pointer stays at zero; a rectifier is needed for AC.

Understand Explain the construction and working of a repulsion-type moving-iron instrument with a sketch. Show that its deflecting torque is proportional to the square of the current, and state why it can be used on AC as well as DC. 13 marks

A fixed coil, a fixed iron vane inside it, and a movable iron vane on the spindle; Both vanes are magnetised in the same direction by the coil field, so like poles face each other and they repel; Force depends on the strength of both magnets, hence torque proportional to I², giving a non-uniform (squared) scale; Reversing the current reverses both magnetisms, so the direction of repulsion stays the same — this is why it works on AC and DC; Damping by air friction and control by springs or gravity; mention the advantages: robust, cheap, can carry overload.

Understand Describe the construction and working of a megger. How is it used to test the insulation resistance of a domestic wiring installation, and what readings would you call acceptable? 10 marks

Understand List the main conventional and renewable sources of electric power and explain the energy conversion in each. Give two advantages and two limitations of solar and of wind power. 13 marks

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare moving-coil and moving-iron instruments on scale, sensitivity, AC/DC capability, effect of stray fields, cost and overload capacity. Which would you choose on a panel that must show both AC and DC values, and why? 13 marks

Scale: uniform for moving coil, cramped at the start for moving iron because torque depends on I²; Sensitivity: moving coil is more sensitive; moving iron draws more power from the circuit; AC/DC: moving coil is DC only (or rectified AC); moving iron reads both because the torque depends on I²; Moving coil needs a strong permanent magnet and is costlier; moving iron is cheaper, simpler and more robust, with better overload capacity; Moving coil is affected by stray fields unless shielded; moving iron has weaker fields and is less affected; Conclusion: moving iron for a general AC/DC panel, moving coil where accuracy and a linear scale matter.

Evaluate A fuse, an MCB and an RCCB are all called protective devices, yet they protect different things. Compare their operating principle, typical breaking time, and the fault each does not protect against. Explain why an RCCB is needed even when MCBs are fitted. 13 marks

Glossary

Accuracy
How close the indicated value is to the true value, usually quoted as a percentage of full-scale deflection.
Ammeter
An instrument connected in series with a circuit to measure current; it must have very low resistance.
Capacitive proximity sensor
A non-contact sensor that detects any material by the change in capacitance when an object enters its field.
Creep (energy meter)
Slow rotation of the disc with no load connected, controlled by a small brake magnet or a shading coil.
Digital multimeter
A meter that displays DC and AC volts, current and resistance as digits, usually with a 3½ or 4½ digit display.
Earth electrode
The metal plate or pipe buried in soil that connects the earthing conductor to the general mass of earth.
ELCB
Earth leakage circuit breaker; an older name for a leakage-current-operated protective device, often voltage-operated in old installations.
Fuel surcharge
A per-unit charge that recovers changes in the cost of fuel used by the generating stations.
Inductive proximity sensor
A non-contact sensor that detects metal targets by the eddy-current change in its oscillator coil.
Instrument
A device that measures and indicates the value of an electrical quantity such as current, voltage or power.
LVDT
Linear variable differential transformer; a passive inductive transducer that gives an AC output proportional to core displacement.
Multiplying factor
The number by which the meter reading is multiplied to get the actual value when a shunt or series resistance is used.
Oscilloscope
An instrument that plots amplitude against time so the shape, peak value, frequency and distortion of a waveform can be seen.
PMMC
Permanent-magnet moving-coil instrument; the standard moving-coil meter used for DC measurement.
RCCB
Residual current circuit breaker; it trips when the current in live and neutral differs by more than its sensitivity, typically 30 mA.
Resolution
The smallest change a digital instrument can display, fixed by the number of digits.
RTD
Resistance temperature detector, a passive transducer (such as platinum) whose resistance rises predictably with temperature.
Sanctioned load
The maximum load in kW or kVA that the utility agrees to supply; it sets the fixed charge.
Sensitivity
The smallest change in input that produces a detectable deflection, often quoted in ohms per volt for a voltmeter.
Shunt
A low-value resistor connected in parallel with a meter to carry most of the current and extend its range.
Sinking and sourcing
Two output wiring styles: NPN sensors sink current to 0 V, PNP sensors source current from the positive supply.
Strain gauge
A passive transducer whose resistance changes when it is stretched or compressed with the surface it is bonded to.
Substation
An assembly of transformers, switchgear and busbars that changes voltage level and routes power.
Thermocouple
An active transducer made of two dissimilar metals that produces a small emf proportional to the temperature difference between its junctions.
Touch voltage
The potential difference a person would feel between an exposed metal part and the earth under fault conditions.
Voltmeter
An instrument connected in parallel across two points to measure potential difference; it must have very high resistance.
