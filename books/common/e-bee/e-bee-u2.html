2.1 Generation of sinusoidal voltage; average and RMS values

A coil turning at constant speed in a uniform magnetic field produces a sinusoidal voltage. This is the basic idea of an AC generator. The mains in India is a 50 Hz sine wave with RMS value 230 V. Peak, average and RMS values describe that wave and let us compare it with steady DC. They are used for meter readings, insulation ratings, rectifier design and power calculations.

You need: resistance, voltage and current (see Unit 1), plus basic sine, cosine and radians.

A rotating coil gives a sine wave

A coil of N turns and area A rotates at constant angular speed ω in a uniform magnetic field of flux density B. The field passes through the coil at a changing angle. Flux linkage changes as cos ωt, so the induced voltage changes as sin ωt. Slip rings and brushes connect the rotating coil to the outside circuit. The law of induction itself is treated with magnetic circuits in Unit 3, Magnetic Circuits and Electrical Machines.

  1. A coil rotates at constant ω in a uniform magnetic field B.
  2. The flux linking the coil is B A cos ωt.
  3. The induced voltage is e = N B A ω sin ωt.
  4. The peak is Em = N B A ω; the wave is a sine because the coil turns steadily.
e = E<sub>m</sub> sin ωt = N B A ω sin ωt, with E<sub>m</sub> = N B A ω

e is instantaneous voltage in V; Em is peak voltage in V; N is number of turns; B is flux density in T; A is coil area in m²; ω is angular frequency in rad/s; t is time in s.

One complete positive and negative swing is a cycle. The time for one cycle is the period T in s. Frequency f = 1/T in Hz is cycles per second. Angular frequency is ω = 2πf rad/s. In India f = 50 Hz, so T = 20 ms and ω = 314 rad/s.

Average and RMS values

The instantaneous value is the voltage at one instant t. The peak value Vm is the largest magnitude. The full-cycle average of a pure sine is zero because the positive and negative halves cancel. The useful average is taken over one half cycle: Vavg = 2Vm/π = 0.637Vm.

V<sub>avg</sub> = (2/π) V<sub>m</sub> ≈ 0.637 V<sub>m</sub>

Vavg is half-cycle average voltage in V; Vm is peak voltage in V; π ≈ 3.1416.

The RMS value is the square root of the mean of the squared wave over one full cycle. For a sine, Vrms = Vm/√2 = 0.707Vm. It is the DC voltage that gives the same heating in a resistor. Some syllabi call it the effective value. The ratio Vrms/Vavg is the form factor; see 2.2.

V<sub>rms</sub> = V<sub>m</sub>/√2 ≈ 0.707 V<sub>m</sub>

Vrms is RMS voltage in V; Vm is peak voltage in V.

  • Peak value: use for insulation and device voltage ratings. A 230 V RMS mains has 325 V peak.
  • Half-cycle average: use in rectifier calculations. The full-cycle average of a sine is zero.
  • RMS: use on AC meters and in power. A 230 V label means 230 V RMS.
  • The factors 0.637 and 0.707 apply only to a pure sine. For other waveforms use the definitions.

Reading a sine wave correctly

For v = Vm sin ωt, the wave repeats every 2π radians. At ωt = π/2 the value is Vm; at ωt = π it is zero; at ωt = 3π/2 it is −Vm. The meter needle or digital display shows RMS for a sine, not peak. A cheap meter may be calibrated for sine and read wrongly on distorted waves. Meter construction is in Unit 6, Measurements, Power Systems and Electrical Safety.

Power in a resistor is P = Vrms²/R = Irms²R. If you use peak values by mistake, the power comes out twice too large. For a 230 V RMS supply across 100 Ω, P = 230²/100 = 529 W. The peak voltage is 325 V, but 325²/100 is not the average power. Active, reactive and apparent power are covered in 2.5.

P = V<sub>rms</sub><sup>2</sup>/R = I<sub>rms</sub><sup>2</sup>R

P is average power in W; Vrms and Irms are RMS voltage and current; R is resistance in Ω.

Worked example

Question. A sinusoidal voltage is v = 325 sin(314t) V. Find its peak value, RMS value, half-cycle average value and frequency.

  1. Compare with v = Vm sin ωt. The peak value is Vm = 325 V.
  2. The angular frequency is ω = 314 rad/s.
  3. RMS value: Vrms = 325/√2 = 229.8 V ≈ 230 V.
  4. Half-cycle average: Vavg = 2 × 325/π = 206.9 V.
  5. Frequency: f = ω/(2π) = 314/(2π) = 49.97 Hz ≈ 50 Hz.

Answer. Peak 325 V, RMS 229.8 V (about 230 V), half-cycle average 206.9 V and frequency 50 Hz.

Exam traps

  • The full-cycle average of a pure sine is zero. If a question asks for average value without saying half cycle, state the half-cycle value and say the full-cycle value is zero.
  • Peak and RMS are not interchangeable. Use Vm for insulation or a stated peak; use Vrms in P = V²/R and meter readings.
  • ω is in rad/s, not Hz. In v = 325 sin(314t), f = 314/(2π) = 50 Hz, not 314 Hz.
  • A meter marked AC volts reads RMS for a sine. On a distorted wave, a cheap average-sensing meter can be wrong; a true-RMS meter is needed.

2.2 Form factor and peak factor

Two AC waveforms can have the same peak but deliver different heating. Form factor and peak factor are ratios built from the RMS, average and peak values; they describe the shape of a waveform with plain numbers. Form factor is Vrms/Vavg; peak factor is Vm/Vrms. Meter scaling, rectifier design and insulation checks use them. For a sine wave the standard values are 1.11 and 1.414.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for peak, average and RMS values of a sine wave.

Definitions and the average value

Three numbers describe a periodic voltage: peak Vm (largest magnitude), RMS Vrms (the DC voltage that gives the same heating), and average Vavg. For a pure sine, the full-cycle average is zero because the positive and negative halves cancel. So we take the average of one half cycle, or of the rectified waveform. For a rectified wave, we take the average over a full cycle of the rectified shape. This choice matters: using zero makes the form factor infinite, which is meaningless.

F<sub>F</sub> = V<sub>rms</sub> / V<sub>avg</sub>,   F<sub>P</sub> = V<sub>m</sub> / V<sub>rms</sub>

Vm is peak voltage in volts (V); Vrms is RMS voltage in volts (V); Vavg is the average of the rectified voltage in volts (V). FF and FP are dimensionless.

The RMS value is the steady DC value that would produce the same power in a resistor. The average value is the area under the rectified curve divided by its base. Peak factor tells you how far the waveform reaches above its RMS; form factor tells you how much the RMS exceeds the average.

  • Vm is peak voltage in volts (V).
  • Vrms is RMS voltage in volts (V).
  • Vavg is average of the rectified voltage in volts (V).
  • FF and FP are pure numbers, no units.

Values for sine and rectified sine

For a sine v = Vm sin θ, the half-cycle average is Vavg = (2/π)Vm = 0.637 Vm. The RMS is Vrms = Vm/√2 = 0.707 Vm. Then FF = 0.707/0.637 = π/(2√2) = 1.11. FP = 1/0.707 = √2 = 1.414.

A half-wave rectified sine keeps one half of each cycle. Its average over a full cycle is Vm/π = 0.318 Vm; its RMS is Vm/2 = 0.5 Vm. So FF = 0.5/0.318 = π/2 = 1.571 and FP = 2.00.

A full-wave rectified sine folds the negative half upward. The average is 2Vm/π = 0.637 Vm; the RMS is still Vm/√2 = 0.707 Vm. Its form and peak factors are the same as the sine: 1.11 and 1.414.

A square wave switches between +Vm and −Vm. Its rectified average is Vm and its RMS is Vm, so both factors are 1.00. A symmetrical triangular wave of peak Vm has Vavg = Vm/2 and Vrms = Vm/√3, giving 1.15 and 1.73.

Where the ratios are used

A moving-coil meter responds to average value, while a moving-iron meter responds to RMS. If a coil meter is calibrated to show RMS on a sine wave, it multiplies the average by 1.11. On a square wave that same meter reads about 11 % high, because the true form factor is 1.00. This is why the waveform shape must be known before trusting an AC meter. A true-RMS meter computes heating directly and avoids this error.

Peak factor sizes insulation and semiconductor ratings. A 230 V RMS sine has peak 230 × 1.414 = 325 V. A capacitor rated 250 V DC therefore cannot be used on this supply, because the peak exceeds its DC rating. An AC rating is an RMS rating, so a 250 V AC part is safe at 230 V RMS. Rectifier diodes in a capacitor-input filter must withstand the peak, not the RMS. In power quality, a high crest factor warns of pulse currents whose peaks stress components, so the equipment heats more than a resistor carrying the same RMS would.

Worked example

Question. A full-wave rectified sine voltage has a peak value Vm = 100 V. Find its average value, RMS value, form factor and peak factor.

  1. For a full-wave rectified sine, Vavg = 2Vm/π = 2 × 100 V / 3.1416 = 63.66 V.
  2. Vrms = Vm/√2 = 100 V / 1.4142 = 70.71 V.
  3. FF = Vrms/Vavg = 70.71 V / 63.66 V = 1.111.
  4. FP = Vm/Vrms = 100 V / 70.71 V = 1.414.

Answer. Vavg = 63.7 V, Vrms = 70.7 V, form factor = 1.11, peak factor = 1.41.

Exam traps

  • Full-cycle average of a pure sine is zero, not 0.637Vm. Use the half-cycle average or the rectified average in the denominator.
  • Average value is not RMS value. For a sine, average is 0.637Vm, RMS is 0.707Vm; swapping them gives 0.90 instead of 1.11.
  • Form factor and peak factor have no unit. Writing 1.11 V is a unit slip.
  • Half-wave rectified sine has form factor 1.57 and peak factor 2.00. Do not copy the full-wave values 1.11 and 1.41.
  • For a square wave, RMS equals peak. The sine's Vm/√2 does not apply.

2.3 Phasor representation of sinusoidal quantities

A phasor is a fixed complex number obtained by freezing a rotating arrow at t = 0; the rotating arrow is only the picture used to derive it. It stands for a sine wave of fixed frequency. Its length is the peak or RMS value; its angle is the phase. Phasor notation turns the addition of sine waves into the addition of complex numbers. This is used in AC circuit analysis, power systems and signal processing. In India, the same idea describes the phase difference between voltage and current in a household fan or a factory motor. The frequency stays the same, so the angle carries the timing information.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for peak and RMS values, and basic complex numbers (a+jb, r∠θ).

From a rotating arrow to a phasor

v(t) = Vm sin(ωt + θ)

Vm is peak voltage in V; ω is angular frequency in rad/s; t is time in s; θ is the phase angle in degrees or radians.

At t = 0 the sine has the value Vm sin θ. Draw an arrow of length Vm at angle θ, rotate it anticlockwise at ω, and take its vertical projection. The projection is the sine wave. Freeze the rotation and keep only the arrow: that arrow is the phasor V = Vm∠θ. A full cycle is 360° or 2π rad. Phase difference is φ = θ1 − θ2. If φ > 0, v1 leads v2 by φ; if φ < 0, v1 lags v2 by |φ|. Lead means the wave reaches the same point earlier in time. Example: sin(ωt + 90°) leads sin(ωt) by 90°.

Three ways to write a phasor

Use the complex plane. The horizontal axis is real; the vertical axis is imaginary. The letter j means √(−1), so j² = −1. A phasor has three forms. Polar: r∠θ, where r is magnitude and θ is angle. Rectangular: a + jb, where a is the real part and b is the imaginary part. Exponential: r ejθ (equivalently r(cos θ + j sin θ)). Convert polar to rectangular with a = r cos θ and b = r sin θ. Convert rectangular to polar with r = √(a² + b²) and θ = tan−1(b/a), choosing the quadrant from the signs of a and b. Example: 10∠30° gives a = 10 cos 30° = 8.66 and b = 10 sin 30° = 5.00, so 8.66 + j5.00. The angle can be in degrees or radians, but keep one unit throughout a calculation.

This chapter uses peak phasors: length is Vm. An RMS phasor has length Vrms and the same angle; never mix the two in one sum.

Adding sinusoids with phasors

  1. Write each wave as a phasor with the same magnitude convention.
  2. Convert each phasor to rectangular form.
  3. Add the real parts and add the imaginary parts.
  4. Convert the sum back to polar form.
  5. Write the time-domain sine with the sum magnitude and angle.

Subtraction uses the same steps with a minus sign. The figure shows the flow. Phasors add like vectors: 10∠30° plus 20∠−45° is not 30∠−15°. The correct rectangular sum is 22.80 − j9.14, which is 24.56∠−21.8°. Only same-frequency waves can be added this way; different frequencies need separate phasors. Some syllabi call this symbolic representation. For a series circuit, the same phasor diagram shows voltage and current with a phase difference; circuit impedance is covered in Chapter 2.4.

Worked example

Question. Two voltages are v₁(t) = 10 sin(ωt + 30°) V and v₂(t) = 20 sin(ωt − 45°) V. Find v₁ + v₂ using peak phasors.

  1. Both waves have the same ω, so they can be added as phasors. The peak-phasor convention is used, so the lengths are 10 V and 20 V.
  2. V₁ = 10∠30° = 10 cos 30° + j10 sin 30° = 8.66 + j5.00 V.
  3. V₂ = 20∠−45° = 20 cos(−45°) + j20 sin(−45°) = 14.14 − j14.14 V.
  4. Sum = (8.66 + 14.14) + j(5.00 − 14.14) = 22.80 − j9.14 V.
  5. Magnitude = √(22.80² + 9.14²) = √(519.8 + 83.5) = √603.3 = 24.56 V.
  6. Angle = tan⁻¹(−9.14 / 22.80) = −21.8°. The real part is positive and the imaginary part is negative, so the angle is in the fourth quadrant.
  7. Convert back: v(t) = 24.56 sin(ωt − 21.8°) V.

Exam traps

  • Never add peak values directly when phases differ; 10 V + 20 V is not the peak of the sum.
  • Keep peak and RMS phasors separate; if a sum is a peak phasor, convert to RMS only at the end with Vrms = Vm/√2.
  • Use degrees for both the given angle and the inverse tangent, or radians for both; do not mix 30° with π/6 in one sum.
  • Check the quadrant of tan−1(b/a); a negative real part needs +180°, so atan2 is safer.
  • Different frequencies cannot share one phasor sum; treat them separately in time.

2.4 Analysis of R-L, R-C and R-L-C circuits

A resistor opposes current at every frequency. Inductors and capacitors oppose AC by amounts that change with frequency and shift current in time against voltage. As complex numbers all three form impedance Z. Then I = V/Z gives current size and phase. Fan regulators, tube-light chokes and power-factor capacitors use this analysis.

You need: Chapter 2.3 for phasors and the j-operator, and Unit 1 for resistance, series circuits and Ohm's law.

Reactance and impedance

Inductive reactance XL is opposition from an inductor. It grows with frequency because faster current changes induce larger back emf.

XL = ωL = 2πfL

XL is inductive reactance in ohm (Ω); ω is angular frequency in rad/s; f is frequency in hertz (Hz); L is inductance in henry (H).

Capacitive reactance XC falls as frequency rises; the capacitor has less time to charge before supply reverses.

XC = 1/(ωC) = 1/(2πfC)

XC is capacitive reactance in Ω; C is capacitance in farad (F).

Reactance is magnitude only; phase is separate. Voltage across L leads current by 90°, so ZL = jXL. Voltage across C lags by 90°, so ZC = −jXC.

R and net reactance are 90° apart and combine like right-triangle legs.

Z = R + jX = R + j(X<sub>L</sub> − X<sub>C</sub>),   |Z| = √(R² + X²),   φ = tan⁻¹(X/R)

Z is impedance in Ω; R is resistance in Ω; X is net reactance XL − XC in Ω; |Z| is magnitude in Ω; φ is angle of Z in degrees, positive when XL > XC.

Admittance Y = 1/Z = G + jB, in siemens (S). Parallel branches use Y because branch admittances add.

Series R-L and R-C circuits

In series, same current flows. Take current as reference at 0°; supply voltage is phasor sum of element voltages.

  1. ω = 2πf rad/s.
  2. XL = ωL and XC = 1/(ωC) in Ω.
  3. Z = R + j(XL − XC); |Z| = √(R² + X²).
  4. I = V/Z; magnitude V/|Z| in ampere (A).
  5. VR = IR, VL = IXL, VC = IXC in volt (V).
  6. φ = tan⁻¹(X/R); state lead or lag.

R-L: XC = 0, X positive, current lags; fan motor near no load. R-C: XL = 0, X negative, current leads; capacitor-run fan.

Element voltages do not add as plain numbers; they form a right triangle with base VR and height VL − VC.

V = √(V<sub>R</sub>² + (V<sub>L</sub> − V<sub>C</sub>)²)

V is RMS supply voltage in V; VR, VL, VC are RMS element voltages in V.

Series R-L-C: which one wins

In series R-L-C, XL and XC have opposite phase effects. Net reactance is their difference.

ConditionNet reactance XAngle φCircuit is
XL > XCpositivepositiveinductive, current lags
XL < XCnegativenegativecapacitive, current leads
XL = XCzerozeroresistive, current in phase

Last row is series resonance: current limited by R alone and largest. Some syllabi treat resonance separately; sign of X gives the behaviour.

VL or VC can exceed supply voltage. Kirchhoff's law holds: the two are nearly opposite in phase, so phasors partly cancel. A 230 V supply can hold 332 V across a capacitor, so rating matters.

Parallel circuits through admittance

In parallel, each branch sees full supply voltage. Convert each branch to admittance, add, then divide once.

I = V·Y, Y = Y₁ + Y₂ + …

I is total RMS current in A; V is RMS supply voltage in V; Y is total admittance in S; Y₁, Y₂ are branch admittances in S.

  • Resistor branch: Y = 1/R, real and positive.
  • Inductor branch: Y = 1/(jXL) = −j/XL, so B = −1/XL.
  • Capacitor branch: Y = 1/(−jXC) = +j/XC, so B = +1/XC.

Conductances add; susceptances add with signs. Sign of total susceptance decides lead or lag. A capacitor across a motor winding corrects power factor.

2.5 Active, reactive and apparent power; power factor

AC power has three parts. A sinusoidal voltage and current make instantaneous power pulse twice per cycle. The average of that pulse is active power P; it does useful work or produces heat. Reactive power Q swings energy into and out of inductors and capacitors with no net transfer. Apparent power S is the plain product of RMS voltage and current; it sizes wires, fuses and transformers. Power factor tells what fraction of S becomes P, and utilities charge extra for poor power factor. This chapter defines these quantities and links them in the power triangle.

You need: RMS values from Chapter 2.1 and impedance phase angle from Chapter 2.4.

Three powers

Let v(t) = Vm sin(ωt) and i(t) = Im sin(ωt − φ). Instantaneous power is p(t) = v(t)i(t), in watts (W). It changes with time. Its average over one cycle is active power P. For sine waves, P = VrmsIrmscos φ. Vrms and Irms are RMS values (Chapter 2.1). φ is the angle by which current lags voltage. P is in W. It leaves the source as heat, light, shaft work or battery charging.

Reactive power is Q = VrmsIrmssin φ, in volt-ampere reactive (var). It is not fake power. An inductor stores energy in its magnetic field; a capacitor stores energy in its electric field. That energy returns to the source each half cycle. Net transfer over a full cycle is zero. Q measures this exchange. Some syllabi call Q reactive volt-amperes.

Apparent power is S = VrmsIrms, in volt-ampere (VA). It is the product of the voltmeter and ammeter readings, ignoring phase. S sizes cables, fuses, switches and transformer windings, because their limits depend on current and voltage magnitude.

Power factor and lead-lag

Power factor is PF = cos φ = P/S. It is a pure number from 0 to 1 for normal loads. A pure resistor has φ = 0, PF = 1, P = S, Q = 0. A pure inductor has current lagging by 90°, PF = 0 lagging, P = 0, Q = S. A pure capacitor has current leading by 90°, PF = 0 leading, Q = −S under the lagging-positive convention. Real motors and transformers are inductive: current lags voltage, so PF is lagging. Capacitor banks make current lead and give leading PF.

Current angle = −φ with voltage as reference. An inductor has φ = +90° and current at −90°; a capacitor has φ = −90° and current at +90°.

With voltage as reference, V = Vrms∠0°. A lagging current is I = Irms∠−φ for φ > 0. A leading current has a negative φ. Fix this sign convention before you call Q positive or negative. The formulas use RMS values. Do not mix peak and RMS values: VmIrmscos φ is √2 times too large, so divide by √2 (multiply by 1/√2). The 1/2 factor applies only when both voltage and current are peak values: P = (VmIm/2)cos φ.

Complex power and sizing

Complex power is S = P + jQ. Here j is the imaginary unit. The same letter S also means the magnitude: |S| = S = VrmsIrms. P is the real part in W; Q is the imaginary part in var. The power triangle has |S| as hypotenuse, P adjacent to φ, and Q opposite φ: P = S cos φ, Q = S sin φ, S = √(P² + Q²). The angle of complex S is φ. Lagging loads have Q > 0; leading loads have Q < 0 under the same convention.

The triangle for S = 2300 VA at PF 0.8 lagging has P = 1840 W and Q = 1380 var. The figure shows this split. The cable carries the full 2300 VA, not just 1840 W. A transformer rated 2300 VA can deliver only 1840 W to this load. Raising PF toward 1 raises the useful P available from the same VA rating. Three-phase power uses the same cos φ term; see Chapter 2.7.

Worked example

Question. A 230 V rms, 50 Hz source supplies a load that draws 10 A rms at 0.8 power factor lagging. (a) Find S, P, the phase angle φ and Q. (b) With V = 230∠0° V rms, write the current phasor. (c) State whether Q is positive or negative under the lagging-positive convention.

  1. Given: Vrms = 230 V, Irms = 10 A, PF = 0.8 lagging.
  2. Apparent power: S = VrmsIrms = 230 × 10 = 2300 VA.
  3. Active power: P = S cos φ = 2300 × 0.8 = 1840 W.
  4. Phase angle: φ = cos⁻¹(0.8) = 36.87°. Current lags, so its angle is −36.87° with V as reference.
  5. Reactive power: Q = S sin φ = 2300 × 0.6 = 1380 var. Lagging current gives Q = +1380 var.
  6. Current phasor: I = 10∠−36.87° A rms when V = 230∠0° V rms.
  7. Check: P = 230 × 10 × 0.8 = 1840 W.

Answer. S = 2300 VA, P = 1840 W, φ = 36.87°, Q = +1380 var (lagging), and I = 10∠−36.87° A rms with V = 230∠0° V rms.

Exam traps

  • Peak versus RMS: P = VrmsIrmscos φ. Using peak values gives a result too large by a factor of 2; RMS is the standard for power.
  • Cos/sin swap: P uses cos φ, Q uses sin φ. At PF 0.8, P is 0.8S, not 0.6S.
  • Unit slip: P in W, Q in var, S in VA. PF has no unit. Do not write Q in W or S in W.
  • Sign convention: lagging current is not automatically positive Q. It is positive only if you define Q = V I sin φ with φ positive for lagging. Fix the convention first.
  • PF angle: PF = 0.8 means φ = cos⁻¹0.8 = 36.87°, not 0.8°. PF is not an angle and not a percentage unless written as 80%.

2.6 Three-phase systems: star and delta connections

A three-phase supply is three sine voltages of equal RMS magnitude and frequency, displaced by 120°. One rotating field in a generator produces all three at once. Industry uses it because three-phase motors turn smoothly, cables carry less current for the same power, and a star winding gives two voltage levels. In a star connection the three winding ends meet at a neutral point. In a delta connection they form a closed loop with no neutral. The names to keep straight are phase voltage, line voltage, phase current and line current; each connection fixes which pairs are equal.

You need: phasors for 120° displacement and complex impedance for the worked example.

Three voltages from one rotating field

A three-phase generator has three identical windings A, B and C on the stator, spaced 120 electrical degrees apart. The rotor field sweeps past them in turn. Each winding gets an emf of the same peak and frequency. The emfs reach their peaks in a fixed order called the phase sequence, written R-Y-B or A-B-C. Reversing any two lines reverses the sequence and reverses a three-phase motor.

v<sub>A</sub> = V<sub>m</sub> sin(ωt), v<sub>B</sub> = V<sub>m</sub> sin(ωt − 120°), v<sub>C</sub> = V<sub>m</sub> sin(ωt + 120°)

Vm is peak voltage in V; ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s; f is frequency in Hz; t is time in s; the three voltages have equal Vm and f.

The three voltages sum to zero at every instant in a balanced set. That is why a three-wire system can work without a return wire for balanced loads. The four quantities used from here on are: phase voltage Vph, the voltage across one phase winding; line voltage VL, the voltage between two line conductors; phase current Iph, the current through one winding; and line current IL, the current in one line conductor. A balanced load has equal impedances in all three phases, so the three phase currents also have equal magnitude and are 120° apart.

The exact √3 relations between line and phase quantities are derived in Chapter 2.7. Here keep the equalities straight for each connection. Phase sequence and balanced phasors use the ideas from Chapter 2.3.

Star connection: a neutral point appears

Join one end of each winding A, B and C to a common point N. Leave the other ends as the three line terminals. This is the star or Y connection. Point N is the neutral point. Figure 1 shows the star on the left. The neutral wire, when brought out, is the fourth wire of a four-wire star system.

  • Line current equals phase current: IL = Iph. The same current flows from the line into a winding.
  • Line voltage is not equal to phase voltage. In a balanced star, VL is larger than Vph; the √3 factor is proved in Chapter 2.7.
  • Two voltage levels are available: VL between any two lines and Vph between one line and neutral. A 400 V line-to-line star supply gives about 230 V line-to-neutral.
  • The neutral carries the return current of unbalanced loads. In a balanced star load, the three equal phase currents are 120° apart, so their sum is zero and neutral current is zero.

Domestic single-phase 230 V in India is one line and neutral of a 400 V star distribution. The same star winding also feeds three-phase motors at 400 V. The neutral point is often earthed for safety; earthing practice belongs to Unit 6.

Delta connection: a closed three-wire loop

Connect the end of winding A to the start of winding B, the end of B to the start of C, and the end of C back to the start of A. The three windings form a closed triangle. This is the delta or mesh connection. The three corners are the line terminals D1, D2 and D3 in Figure 1. There is no neutral point and no fourth wire.

  • Line voltage equals phase voltage: VL = Vph. Each pair of lines is connected directly across one winding.
  • Line current is not equal to phase current. Each line current is the phasor difference of the two winding currents at that corner; the √3 relation for a balanced delta is in Chapter 2.7.
  • The closed loop has no neutral. Never connect a neutral wire to a delta load: there is no neutral terminal, and forcing one can unbalance the winding voltages.
  • In a balanced set the three emfs sum to zero, so no circulating current flows around the closed triangle. Slight unbalance can cause a small circulating current.

Delta is common for three-phase motors and for transformer windings where a neutral is not needed. Some syllabi call star the Y connection and delta the mesh connection; both names describe the same drawings.

Worked example

Question. A balanced 400 V line-to-line, 50 Hz supply feeds a star-connected load with per-phase impedance Z = 20∠30° Ω. Find the phase voltage, the phase current magnitude and the line current magnitude. State the phase angle of the current relative to the phase voltage.

  1. Connection is star. Given line voltage VL = 400 V and per-phase impedance magnitude |Z| = 20 Ω with angle +30°.
  2. In star the phase voltage is line-to-neutral: Vph = VL / √3 = 400 / 1.732 = 230.9 V.
  3. Phase current magnitude: Iph = Vph / |Z| = 230.9 / 20 = 11.55 A.
  4. The impedance angle is +30°, so the current lags the phase voltage by 30°.
  5. In star, line current equals phase current: IL = Iph = 11.55 A.

Answer. The phase voltage is 230.9 V; the phase current and line current are 11.55 A each, and the current lags the phase voltage by 30°.

Exam traps

  • Line voltage is between two lines; phase voltage is across one phase winding. A 400 V three-phase label normally means line-to-line, not 400 V per phase.
  • In star, line current equals phase current; in delta, line voltage equals phase voltage. Do not mix the two equalities.
  • Delta has no neutral point. Never connect a neutral wire to a delta load; there is no fourth terminal to take it.
  • Phase sequence matters. Swapping any two lines reverses R-Y-B to R-B-Y and reverses a three-phase motor.
  • Do not use 400/√3 for a delta load. The √3 factor belongs to balanced star line voltage, not to every three-phase circuit.

2.7 Line and phase voltages and currents; three-phase power

In a balanced three-phase circuit, every phase carries the same current magnitude and the same phase angle. The supply gives two sets of numbers: line values at the terminals and phase values inside each winding or load branch. They differ by √3, but the total power follows one formula, P = √3 VLILcos φ, whether the load is star or delta. This lets a 400 V motor, a factory feeder and a distribution transformer be rated from a few terminal measurements.

You need Chapter 2.6 for star and delta connections and Chapter 2.5 for active, reactive and apparent power.

Star connection: line voltage is √3 times phase voltage

Line voltage VL is measured between any two line terminals. Phase voltage Vph is measured across one phase winding or one load branch; in star it is the voltage from one line to the neutral point. Line current IL flows in a line conductor. Phase current Iph flows inside one phase branch. Voltages are in volts (V) and currents in amperes (A).

In a star connection, the three phase branches meet at the neutral point. Each line feeds exactly one branch, so the line current is the branch current: IL = Iph.

Take two phase voltages from the neutral to two lines. They have equal magnitude and are 120° apart. Their difference is the line voltage between those two lines. The resultant magnitude is 2Vphcos 30° = √3 Vph. The line voltage also leads the corresponding phase voltage (neutral to line) by 30°; in delta, the line current lags the corresponding phase current by 30°.

V<sub>L</sub> = √3 V<sub>ph</sub>,  I<sub>L</sub> = I<sub>ph</sub>

VL is line voltage in V; Vph is phase voltage in V; IL is line current in A; Iph is phase current in A. Some syllabi write VLL for VL.

For a 400 V line, Vph = 400/√3 = 231 V. This is the usual single-phase level in a 400 V three-phase distribution system.

Delta connection: line current is √3 times phase current

In a delta connection, each phase branch is connected between two lines. There is no neutral point. The voltage across a branch is therefore a line voltage: Vph = VL.

The line current is not the branch current. At a line terminal, two branch currents meet. By Kirchhoff's current law, the line current is their vector difference. The two phase currents have equal magnitude and are 120° apart. Their difference has magnitude 2Iphcos 30° = √3 Iph.

V<sub>L</sub> = V<sub>ph</sub>,  I<sub>L</sub> = √3 I<sub>ph</sub>

VL is line voltage in V; Vph is phase voltage in V; IL is line current in A; Iph is phase current in A.

The √3 factor moves in opposite ways in the two connections. Star gives a larger line voltage and the same current. Delta gives the same voltage and a larger line current. Both statements assume a balanced load: equal phase impedances and equal phase angles.

Total active, reactive and apparent power

For one phase, active power is VphIphcos φ. Here φ is the angle by which the phase current lags or leads the phase voltage in that branch. For three identical phases, total active power is three times the per-phase value.

P = 3V<sub>ph</sub>I<sub>ph</sub>cos φ = √3V<sub>L</sub>I<sub>L</sub>cos φ

P is active power in watts (W); φ is the angle between phase voltage and phase current; cos φ is the power factor; Vph and Iph are phase voltage and phase current; VL and IL are line voltage and line current.

For a star load, substitute Vph = VL/√3 and Iph = IL: 3VphIph = 3(VL/√3)IL = √3VLIL. For a delta load, substitute Vph = VL and Iph = IL/√3: 3VphIph = 3VL(IL/√3) = √3VLIL. The line formula is the same for both.

Q = √3V<sub>L</sub>I<sub>L</sub>sin φ,  S = √3V<sub>L</sub>I<sub>L</sub>

Q is reactive power in volt-amperes reactive (var); S is apparent power in volt-amperes (VA); φ is the same phase angle between phase voltage and phase current; VL and IL are line voltage and line current.

The power factor is cos φ = P/S. Use line values in the √3 formulas and phase values in the 3VphIph formula. Mixing the two sets is the most common calculation error.

Worked example

Question. A balanced delta load has phase impedance 15∠45° Ω and line voltage 400 V. Find the phase current, line current, active power and apparent power.

  1. For a delta load, phase voltage equals line voltage: Vph = VL = 400 V.
  2. The phase impedance magnitude is 15 Ω and its angle is 45°. Phase current magnitude: Iph = Vph/|Z| = 400/15 = 26.67 A.
  3. In delta, line current: IL = √3 Iph = 1.732 × 26.67 = 46.19 A.
  4. Power factor: cos φ = cos 45° = 0.7071. Active power: P = 3VphIphcos φ = 3 × 400 × 26.67 × 0.7071 = 22,627 W = 22.6 kW.
  5. Check with line values: P = √3VLILcos φ = 1.732 × 400 × 46.19 × 0.7071 = 22.6 kW.
  6. Apparent power: S = 3VphIph = 3 × 400 × 26.67 = 32,000 VA = 32.0 kVA. Or S = √3 × 400 × 46.19 = 32.0 kVA.

Answer. The phase current is 26.67 A, the line current is 46.19 A, the active power is 22.6 kW and the apparent power is 32.0 kVA.

Exam traps

  • Do not put line current into P = 3VphIphcos φ. That formula uses phase voltage and phase current only.
  • In the √3 formula, φ is the phase angle of the load, the angle between phase voltage and phase current. In a balanced circuit the angle between line voltage and line current is the same φ, so either measurement gives the same power factor. Only with an unbalanced load does each phase have its own angle.
  • In star, Vph = VL/√3, not VL. In delta, Vph = VL, not VL/√3.
  • Do not mix Vph with IL in one formula. Use either all phase quantities with the factor 3, or all line quantities with the factor √3.
  • The √3 relations and total-power formulas here assume a balanced load. For an unbalanced load, solve each phase separately or use another method.

Star (Y) connectionThree windings joined at a common neutral point; gives two voltage levels and a fourth wire.2.6
Delta (Δ) connectionThree windings joined end to end in a closed loop; three wires, no neutral.2.6
Line and phase quantitiesLine values are measured between lines; phase values are measured across or through one winding.2.7

Takeaways

  1. A coil turning at constant speed in a uniform magnetic field produces e = Vm sin ωt, so the sine wave is built into every generator.
  2. RMS value is the DC value that would heat a resistor equally; that is why 230 V mains means 230 V RMS and about 325 V peak.
  3. Form factor and peak factor are shape numbers: 1.11 and 1.414 for a sine, 1.0 and 1.0 for a square, and different again for a triangular wave.
  4. A phasor freezes the rotation: length gives magnitude, angle gives phase, and adding phasors by vector rules gives the sum of sinusoids.
  5. In series R-L-C, impedance Z = R + j(XL − XC) carries both size and phase, so one complex number answers the whole circuit.
  6. Real power P = VI cos φ does work; reactive power Q = VI sin φ only shuttles back and forth; apparent power S = VI sizes the wiring and the transformer.
  7. Star gives √3 times the phase voltage at the lines and a neutral for unbalanced loads; delta gives √3 times the phase current in the lines.
  8. For any balanced three-phase load, total power is √3 VL IL cos φ, whether the load is in star or in delta.

Exam question bank

Long answers are expected to combine a labelled phasor or circuit diagram, a clean derivation of the formula, and a numerical check — with star/delta relations and the √3 power formula asked almost every time.

Remember and understand

Understand Explain how a coil rotating at constant speed in a uniform magnetic field produces a sinusoidal voltage. Write the instantaneous voltage expression and define every symbol in it. 10 marks

Faraday's law: changing flux linkage induces emf; Flux through coil Φ = BA cos θ with θ = ωt as the coil turns; Result e = NBAω sin ωt, so the waveform is sinusoidal; Define Vm = NBAω, ω = 2πf, t in seconds; Peak occurs when the coil plane is parallel to the field; One full turn gives one complete cycle; f = Nrpm/60 for a two-pole machine.

Remember Define average value and RMS value of a periodic waveform. Derive both for a sine wave and explain why RMS, not average, is used to rate equipment. 10 marks

Average over half cycle: (1/π)∫Vm sin θ dθ = 2Vm/π = 0.637 Vm; RMS: square, average over full cycle, square root → Vm/√2 = 0.707 Vm; Average over a full cycle of a sine is zero, so half-cycle average is used; Heating in a resistor goes as i², so the square-root-of-mean-square is the DC equivalent; 230 V mains is RMS; peak is 230√2 ≈ 325 V; Insulation is chosen from peak, heating and rating from RMS.

Understand Define form factor and peak factor. Compute both for a sinusoidal, a square and a triangular waveform and comment on what the numbers tell you. 8 marks

Form factor = RMS/average; peak factor = peak/RMS; Sine: 1.11 and 1.414; Square: 1.0 and 1.0 (RMS = peak = average of half cycle); Triangular: RMS = Vm/√3, average = Vm/2, so form factor 1.155 and peak factor √3 ≈ 1.732; A meter calibrated on sine reads wrong on other shapes; Peak factor matters for insulation and for rectifier peak inverse voltage.

Understand Explain phasor representation of sinusoidal quantities. Why can a sinusoid of fixed frequency be drawn as a stationary arrow, and how are phasors added and subtracted? 8 marks

A sinusoid has three data: magnitude, frequency, phase; Frequency is the same for all quantities in a linear circuit at steady state, so it can be dropped; Rotating vector of length Vm at ω, projected on the vertical axis gives v(t); Freeze the picture at t = 0: length = RMS or peak, angle = phase; Same-frequency phasors add by parallelogram or by resolving into x and y components; Subtraction = add the reversed phasor; results valid only for the same frequency.

Apply

Apply A coil of resistance 8 Ω and inductance 30 mH is connected to a 230 V, 50 Hz supply. Find the impedance, current, phase angle and power factor, and draw the phasor diagram. 12 marks

XL = 2π × 50 × 0.030 = 9.42 Ω; Z = √(8² + 9.42²) = 12.36 Ω; I = 230/12.36 = 18.6 A; φ = tan⁻¹(9.42/8) = 49.7°, current lags; Power factor = cos 49.7° = 0.647 lagging; Phasor diagram: I reference, VR in phase, VL leading by 90°, supply V the vector sum.

Apply A series R-L-C circuit has R = 10 Ω, L = 0.1 H, C = 50 µF and is connected to 230 V, 50 Hz. Find Z, I, VR, VL, VC and verify Kirchhoff's voltage law in phasor form. 13 marks

XL = 31.4 Ω, XC = 63.7 Ω; X = XL − XC = −32.3 Ω, so the circuit is capacitive; Z = √(10² + 32.3²) = 33.8 Ω, I = 6.8 A; VR = 68 V, VL = 214 V, VC = 433 V; Phasor sum √(VR² + (VL − VC)²) = 230 V, so KVL holds; Note VC exceeds the supply voltage — possible because the voltages are out of phase.

Apply A 5 kW load runs at 230 V, 50 Hz with a power factor of 0.7 lagging. Find the current and apparent power, then calculate the capacitance needed to raise the power factor to 0.95 lagging. 14 marks

S = P/cos φ = 5/0.7 = 7.14 kVA, I = S/V = 31.1 A; Reactive power Q1 = P tan φ1 = 5 × tan 45.6° = 5.10 kVAR; At 0.95 pf, tan φ2 = 0.329, Q2 = 1.64 kVAR; Capacitor must supply QC = 5.10 − 1.64 = 3.46 kVAR; C = QC/(2πfV²) = 3460/(2π × 50 × 230²) = 208 µF; Current falls from 31.1 A to about 22.9 A; cable and transformer loading drop.

Apply Three identical coils of impedance (6 + j8) Ω per phase are connected in star to a 400 V, 50 Hz three-phase supply. Find phase voltage, phase current, line current and total power. 12 marks

Star: Vph = 400/√3 = 231 V; |Z| = √(6² + 8²) = 10 Ω, Iph = 23.1 A; In star IL = Iph = 23.1 A; cos φ = R/|Z| = 0.6; P = √3 VL IL cos φ = √3 × 400 × 23.1 × 0.6 = 9.6 kW; Check: P = 3 Iph²R = 3 × 23.1² × 6 = 9.6 kW.

Apply Draw and explain star and delta connections of three-phase windings. State the voltage and current relations for each and give one practical situation where each is used. 10 marks

Star: three ends joined at neutral N; line-to-line voltage √3 times phase voltage; IL = Iph; Phase difference of 30° between line and phase voltage, with a clear phasor diagram; Delta: windings closed in a loop; VL = Vph; IL = √3 Iph; Star gives two voltage levels and a neutral — used for distribution and motor starters; Delta used where only one voltage is available and for running motors; a delta-connected winding must never be opened under load.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Derive the relation between line and phase quantities for a balanced star and a balanced delta load, and hence show that total three-phase power is √3 VL IL cos φ in both cases. 14 marks

Balanced phasors 120° apart; vector difference of two phase voltages gives √3 Vph; Star: VL = √3 Vph, IL = Iph, power = 3 Vph Iph cos φ; Delta: VL = Vph, IL = √3 Iph, power = 3 Vph Iph cos φ; Substitute in each case and both reduce to √3 VL IL cos φ; Conclusion: a wattmeter sees the same power whatever the internal connection; Neutral current in a balanced star load is zero, so the neutral can be omitted.

Analyze Compare single-phase and three-phase supply for delivering the same power at the same line voltage. Discuss line current, copper required, torque smoothness and why industry prefers three-phase. 12 marks

For the same P and V, a single-phase line carries P/(V cos φ); three-phase lines carry P/(√3 VL cos φ) each; Three-phase current per conductor is 1/√3 of the single-phase value, so thinner conductors; Total copper for the same loss falls roughly by half; two conductors saved where neutral is not needed; Instantaneous three-phase power is constant, giving steady torque in motors; Three-phase motors are self-starting; single-phase motors need auxiliary windings; Domestic supply still uses single phase because loads are small.

Analyze A series R-L-C circuit is brought to resonance. Explain what happens to impedance, current, the voltages across L and C, and the power factor. Why can VL be several times the supply voltage? 12 marks

At resonance XL = XC, so X = 0 and Z = R (minimum); Current is maximum, I = V/R, and in phase with supply; power factor unity; VL and VC are individually Q times the supply voltage, where Q = XL/R; They are 180° apart, so their phasor sum cancels; KVL is still satisfied; Resonant frequency fr = 1/(2π√(LC)); Danger: insulation and capacitor ratings must allow for the Q-times voltage; this is how a radio tunes.

Evaluate A factory draws 100 kW at a power factor of 0.6 lagging from the supply authority. Explain why the authority penalises this, and evaluate adding a capacitor bank against replacing the motors with high-efficiency ones. 13 marks

Load current is P/(V cos φ); low pf means larger current for the same kW; Larger current causes higher I²R losses, bigger cables, transformer and switchgear rated in kVA; Apparent power here is 167 kVA for only 100 kW of useful work; Capacitor bank: cheap, static, no moving parts, reduces Q directly; needs switching as load varies and can cause over-correction; Motor replacement: costs more, improves efficiency as well, but does not fix reactive demand unless pf is also improved; Recommend measurement of load profile before choosing; a mixed solution is usually best; Give a sample calculation showing current and kVA before and after correction.

Glossary

Balanced load
A three-phase load whose three phase impedances are identical in magnitude and angle.
Balanced three-phase supply
Three voltages of equal magnitude, 120° apart, from one generator.
Complex impedance
Z = R + jX, where j = √(−1); both magnitude and phase in one number.
Conductance G
The real part of admittance, G = R/(R² + X²), in siemens.
Cycle
One complete set of positive and negative values of a periodic wave.
Frequency f
Number of cycles per second, in hertz (Hz).
Instantaneous power
p = vi at one instant; for a sine supply it swings at twice the supply frequency.
Leading and lagging
A current leads when its peak comes earlier than the voltage's, and lags when it comes later.
Line current
Current flowing in a line conductor supplying the load.
Line voltage
Voltage between any two line conductors.
Neutral wire
The fourth wire in a star system, joined to the common point of the three windings.
Phase angle
The angle ωt + φ that fixes where the wave is in its cycle at time t.
Phase current
Current flowing through one phase winding of the load.
Phase voltage
Voltage across one phase winding, measured between a line and the neutral in star.
Reactance
The part of impedance that stores and returns energy, X = XL − XC, in ohms.
Resistance R
The part of impedance that converts electrical energy into heat, in ohms.
Resonance
The condition XL = XC at which impedance is purely resistive and current is maximum.
Sine wave
A waveform of the form Vm sin(ωt + φ), produced by a coil rotating in a uniform magnetic field.
Susceptance B
The imaginary part of admittance, B = −X/(R² + X²), in siemens.
Time period T
Time taken for one complete cycle, in seconds; T = 1/f.
Volt-ampere
The unit of apparent power, written VA; kVA is 1000 VA.
Waveform
The graph of a quantity against time.
