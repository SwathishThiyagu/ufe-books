6.1 Gradient and directional derivative

A scalar field assigns one number to each point in space: temperature in a room, pressure over a wing, height on a hill. The gradient turns that field into a vector. It points toward the steepest rise, and its length is the rate of that rise. The directional derivative asks a narrower question: if you walk along a chosen unit direction, how fast does the number change? The answer is the gradient projected on that direction. Engineers use it to follow heat, pressure and potential, and to drive gradient descent in machine learning.

You need partial derivatives from Unit 4, partial derivatives and the normal to a surface from Unit 4, tangent planes and normal lines.

Scalar field, gradient, level surface

Write a scalar field as φ(x, y, z). Each point (x, y, z) gives one number φ. The gradient of φ is the vector of its three first partial derivatives.

∇φ = φ<sub>x</sub> i + φ<sub>y</sub> j + φ<sub>z</sub> k = (φ<sub>x</sub>, φ<sub>y</sub>, φ<sub>z</sub>)

φx = ∂φ/∂x, and i, j, k are unit vectors along x, y, z.

The symbol ∇ is read "del" or "nabla". On a scalar it gives a vector; on a vector it has two other meanings, divergence and curl (see 6.2). A level surface is the set of points where φ(x, y, z) = c, with c a constant. For φ = x² + y² + z², the level surfaces are spheres centred at the origin.

The key geometric fact: ∇φ at a point is normal (perpendicular) to the level surface through that point. Moving along the surface keeps φ fixed, so the change in φ is zero in every tangent direction. The gradient has no component along those tangents. This is why the unit normal to a surface f(x, y, z) = c is

n̂ = ∇f / |∇f|

n̂ is a unit normal vector; |∇f| = √(fx² + fy² + fz²).

Use n̂ to write tangent planes and normal lines; those constructions stay in Unit 4. Here n̂ matters because it turns any surface into a direction.

Directional derivative and its extreme values

Let û = (u₁, u₂, u₃) be a unit vector, so u₁² + u₂² + u₃² = 1. The directional derivative of φ at P in the direction û is the rate of change of φ as you move from P along û.

D<sub>û</sub> φ = ∇φ · û = φ<sub>x</sub>u₁ + φ<sub>y</sub>u₂ + φ<sub>z</sub>u₃

∇φ is evaluated at P; the dot means the sum of component products.

If a problem gives a direction vector a rather than a unit vector, divide first: û = a/|a|. Then Dû φ = (∇φ · a)/|a|. Some books write the directional derivative along a as ∇φ · a without normalising; read the definition in your question. The normalised form gives rate per unit length and is the safe default.

Because ∇φ · û = |∇φ| cos θ, where θ is the angle between ∇φ and û, the directional derivative is largest when û points along ∇φ. Then cos θ = 1 and the maximum rate is |∇φ|. The minimum rate is −|∇φ|, opposite to ∇φ. Along any direction perpendicular to ∇φ, the rate is zero, which is the same as saying that direction lies in the level surface.

For two surfaces f = c₁ and g = c₂, their normals are ∇f and ∇g. The angle between the surfaces is the angle between these normals.

cos θ = (∇f · ∇g) / (|∇f| |∇g|)

θ is the angle between the normals; take the acute angle if the question asks for the angle between surfaces.

Working order and a two-variable case

  1. Find ∇φ at the given point; keep it as a vector.
  2. Turn the given direction a into a unit vector û = a/|a|; if û is already unit, skip this.
  3. Compute Dû φ = ∇φ · û. Include units of φ per unit length.
  4. For the maximum rate, take |∇φ| and give the direction ∇φ.
  5. For a surface f = c, use n̂ = ∇f/|∇f|; for an angle between surfaces, use the two normals.

In two variables, φ(x, y) has gradient (φx, φy). Level sets φ = c are curves, and the gradient is normal to the curve. The directional derivative in a unit direction (u₁, u₂) is φxu₁ + φyu₂. Every formula above drops the z-component.

Worked example

Question. A scalar field is φ = x²y + 2yz. Find the directional derivative of φ at P(1, 2, −1) in the direction of a = i + 2j + 2k, and the maximum rate of increase at P.

  1. At P(1, 2, −1), compute φx = 2xy = 2×1×2 = 4.
  2. φy = x² + 2z = 1² + 2(−1) = −1.
  3. φz = 2y = 2×2 = 4. So ∇φ = (4, −1, 4) at P.
  4. Length of a = i + 2j + 2k: |a| = √(1² + 2² + 2²) = √9 = 3.
  5. Unit direction û = a/|a| = (1/3, 2/3, 2/3).
  6. Dû φ = ∇φ · û = 4(1/3) + (−1)(2/3) + 4(2/3) = (4 − 2 + 8)/3 = 10/3 ≈ 3.33 per unit length.
  7. Maximum rate = |∇φ| = √(4² + (−1)² + 4²) = √33 ≈ 5.74 per unit length; it occurs along ∇φ = (4, −1, 4).

Answer. The directional derivative is 10/3 ≈ 3.33 per unit length, and the maximum rate is √33 ≈ 5.74 per unit length, in the direction of ∇φ = (4, −1, 4).

Exam traps

  • Forgetting to divide a direction vector by its length. In the example |a| = 3, not 1; using a directly gives 10, not 10/3.
  • Reading the maximum rate as the directional derivative along a. The maximum is |∇φ| and its direction is ∇φ; the given direction a may be different.
  • Taking the normal to a surface as the tangent. For f = c, the normal is ∇f; the tangent plane is perpendicular to it.
  • Mixing up the angle between surfaces with the angle between their tangents. Use cos θ = (∇f·∇g)/(|∇f||∇g|).
  • Losing the sign: opposite to ∇φ gives −|∇φ|, and a direction perpendicular to ∇φ gives zero.

6.2 Divergence, curl and vector identities

Physics gives you fields: temperature across a room, wind velocity over a city, water speed inside a pipe. Each has a value at every point of space. Two questions then come up again and again. Is the field being created or destroyed at a point? Is it swirling there? The del operator ∇ answers both. Dotted with a vector field it gives divergence, a scalar. Crossed with it, curl, a vector. (Applied to a scalar it gives the gradient, from the previous chapter.) These two quantities per point drive fluid flow, heat flow and the big integral theorems of this unit.

You need partial derivatives and the equality of mixed partials (see Unit 4, Partial derivatives and higher order partials), and the del operator and gradient from 6.1.

Del, divergence, and what spreading means

Del is the operator ∇ = i ∂/∂x + j ∂/∂y + k ∂/∂z. Here i, j, k are the unit vectors along the x, y, z axes, and ∂/∂x means differentiate with respect to x while y and z are held fixed. Del alone does nothing. It waits for a field and then acts in one of three ways: on a scalar it gives the gradient, dotted with a vector it gives divergence, crossed with a vector it gives curl.

div F = ∇·F = ∂F₁/∂x + ∂F₂/∂y + ∂F₃/∂z

F = F₁i + F₂j + F₃k is a vector field; F₁, F₂, F₃ are its three components, each a function of x, y, z; the output is a scalar field, one number at each point.

Divergence is flux per unit volume. Put a tiny closed surface around a point, add up the outward flow of F through it, and divide by the volume inside. As the surface shrinks, that ratio tends to div F at the point. Where div F is positive the point is a source: fluid leaves faster than it arrives. Where div F is negative it is a sink. In water flowing steadily along a straight pipe, div F = 0, because every small box has as much entering as leaving.

The word is well chosen. A field that spreads out from a point diverges there; a field that closes in on a point converges. The same reading applies to heat leaving a hot spot of metal, or to charge spraying out of a small region.

Curl and the paddle wheel

curl F = ∇×F = | i  j  k ; ∂/∂x  ∂/∂y  ∂/∂z ; F₁  F₂  F₃ | = (∂F₃/∂y − ∂F₂/∂z) i + (∂F₁/∂z − ∂F₃/∂x) j + (∂F₂/∂x − ∂F₁/∂y) k

the 3×3 determinant is expanded along the first row; F₁, F₂, F₃ and the derivatives are as above; the output is a vector field.

Expand the determinant every time. Do not memorise the components, because the j component runs the other way: it is ∂F₁/∂z − ∂F₃/∂x, the term containing F₁ first and differentiated with respect to z. A single swapped sign ruins the whole vector.

Physical reading: curl is circulation per unit area. Place a tiny paddle wheel in the flow at a point. The wheel spins, and curl F is twice its angular velocity vector; the axis it spins about is the direction of curl F. A whirlpool turns the wheel fast; water sliding straight down a pipe does not turn it at all. Take F = (−y, x, 0), the velocity of water going round a drain. Then curl F = (0, 0, 2), so the wheel turns at 1 radian per second about the z axis. This is why curl is also called the rotation of the field.

Product rules and the three identities

div(φF) = φ div F + F·∇φ

φ = φ(x, y, z) is a scalar field, F is a vector field, and both terms on the right are scalars.

curl(φF) = φ curl F + ∇φ × F

same symbols; note the cross product in the second term, so the order ∇φ × F matters.

Two identities are exam favourites and are always worded prove that.

  • div(curl F) = 0. Write out the divergence of the curl. The six terms pair off and cancel because mixed partials are equal: ∂²/∂x∂y = ∂²/∂y∂x.
  • curl(∇φ) = 0, the zero vector. Same cancellation, taken in the other order.

The divergence of a gradient defines the Laplacian: div(∇φ) = ∇²φ = ∂²φ/∂x² + ∂²φ/∂y² + ∂²φ/∂z². The symbol ∇² is the Laplacian.

One more identity, usually quoted rather than proved at this stage: curl(curl F) = ∇(∇·F) − ∇²F, where ∇²F means the Laplacian applied to each component separately. Note that div(curl F) is always zero, while curl(curl F) is generally not.

Worked example

Question. For the vector field F = (xy, yz, zx), find div F and curl F at the point (1, 2, 3). Hence verify that div(curl F) = 0 at that point.

  1. Write the components: F₁ = xy, F₂ = yz, F₃ = zx.
  2. div F = ∂(xy)/∂x + ∂(yz)/∂y + ∂(zx)/∂z = y + z + x.
  3. At (1, 2, 3): div F = 2 + 3 + 1 = 6.
  4. curl F = (∂F₃/∂y − ∂F₂/∂z, ∂F₁/∂z − ∂F₃/∂x, ∂F₂/∂x − ∂F₁/∂y).
  5. The six partials: ∂F₃/∂y = 0, ∂F₂/∂z = y, ∂F₁/∂z = 0, ∂F₃/∂x = z, ∂F₂/∂x = 0, ∂F₁/∂y = x.
  6. So curl F = (0 − y, 0 − z, 0 − x) = (−y, −z, −x).
  7. At (1, 2, 3): curl F = (−2, −3, −1).
  8. Check: let G = curl F = (−y, −z, −x). Then div G = ∂(−y)/∂x + ∂(−z)/∂y + ∂(−x)/∂z = 0 + 0 + 0 = 0.

Answer. div F = 6 (a scalar), curl F = (−2, −3, −1) (a vector), and div(curl F) = 0 at (1, 2, 3), as the identity promises.

Exam traps

  • The middle component of curl is ∂F₁/∂z − ∂F₃/∂x. Swapping the two terms flips that component's sign; expand the determinant instead of reciting it.
  • div F is one number at each point and curl F is three. An answer that writes divergence with i, j, k, or a curl as a single scalar, is wrong whatever the arithmetic.
  • curl F = 0 does not mean F = 0. The field F = (x, y, z) has zero curl yet grows without limit; likewise div F = 0 does not kill a field (see <a href="#6.3">6.3).
  • div(curl F) is always zero, but curl(curl F) is not. Dropping the ∇(∇·F) term in the identity curl(curl F) = ∇(∇·F) − ∇²F is a standard slip.
  • ∇²φ means ∂²φ/∂x² + ∂²φ/∂y² + ∂²φ/∂z², a sum of second partials. It is not (∇φ)·(∇φ), which is the square of the gradient's length.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Divergence and curl are the language of two famous systems: the Navier–Stokes equations for fluid flow, and Maxwell's equations for electricity and magnetism, two of whose four equations are written with div and two with curl. Computational fluid dynamics packages use these operators to simulate aircraft wings, car bodies and monsoon winds. The Helmholtz decomposition says any well-behaved field splits into a curl-free part plus a divergence-free part, which is why the words irrotational and solenoidal cover everything. 6.3 Solenoidal and irrotational fields; scalar potential

Some fields spread out from a point, some curl around it, and some do neither. Zero divergence is called solenoidal; zero curl is irrotational. An irrotational field is the gradient of a scalar function φ, its potential, and φ carries everything the field carries. Engineers use this daily: electric potential instead of the E field, work between two points depending only on the endpoints. This chapter gives the two tests and the method that builds φ from the field.

You need: divergence and curl from 6.2, and partial integration from Unit 2, Indefinite integrals and techniques of integration.

Two tests, two names

A vector field F = (F₁, F₂, F₃) gives a vector at each point of a region. Two derivatives of F say what the field does there. Divergence is a scalar: how much the field spreads out of the point, like a tap or a drain. Curl is a vector: how much the field turns around the point, like a tiny paddle wheel.

div F = ∂F₁/∂x + ∂F₂/∂y + ∂F₃/∂z

F₁, F₂, F₃ are the x, y, z components of F; ∂F₁/∂x is the partial derivative of F₁ in x with y, z held fixed, and so on.

curl F = (∂F₃/∂y − ∂F₂/∂z, ∂F₁/∂z − ∂F₃/∂x, ∂F₂/∂x − ∂F₁/∂y)

the three entries are the x, y, z components of the curl vector; keep this order, the minus signs matter.

F is solenoidal in a region when div F = 0 at every point of it. F is irrotational in a region when curl F = 0 at every point of it.

  • Solenoidal, from solenoid: a current coil has magnetic field B with div B = 0. Field lines close; nothing is born or destroyed inside. Fluid flow with div v = 0 is incompressible.
  • Irrotational: every small blob moves without spinning about its centre. Steady flow along a straight pipe is irrotational; swirl in a wash basin is not.
  • The tests are independent. A field may be one, both, or neither.

The condition is on a region, never on one point. One point where curl F = 0 proves nothing.

Irrotational fields have a scalar potential

For an irrotational field there is a scalar function φ with F = ∇φ, the scalar potential of F.

F = ∇φ = (∂φ/∂x, ∂φ/∂y, ∂φ/∂z)

φ is a scalar function of x, y, z; ∇ is the gradient operator from 6.1.

If F = ∇φ then curl F = 0. The first curl component is ∂/∂y(∂φ/∂z) − ∂/∂z(∂φ/∂y) = ∂²φ/∂y∂z − ∂²φ/∂z∂y, and mixed partials are equal, so it is 0. The same happens in the other components. Every gradient field is irrotational.

The converse needs a region with no holes. Only a few syllabi press this point. On a region with a hole it can fail: F = (−y, x, 0)/(x² + y²) has curl F = 0 except on the z-axis, yet its line integral round the unit circle is 2π, not 0, so no single-valued φ exists.

φ is not unique. Adding any constant leaves ∇φ unchanged, so x²y and x²y + 7 are both potentials of the same field. Report φ + C.

An irrotational field is conservative: the line integral between two points depends only on the endpoints and equals φ(B) − φ(A). That result belongs to 6.4. In electrostatics E = −∇V, where V is potential in volts and E in volts per metre; the minus sign makes V fall along the field.

Finding φ: integrate, then match

  1. Write F₁, F₂, F₃ and test curl F and div F first.
  2. If curl F ≠ 0 at any point of the region, no scalar potential exists. Stop.
  3. Set ∂φ/∂x = F₁ and integrate in x, holding y and z fixed. The constant is an unknown function g(y, z), not a number.
  4. Differentiate φ in y, set it equal to F₂, and integrate in y to get g(y, z) = (a function of y, z) + h(z).
  5. Differentiate φ in z, set it equal to F₃. This fixes h′(z), hence h(z).
  6. Add C and verify by computing ∇φ and getting F back.

For an irrotational field, div F = div(∇φ) = ∇²φ, the Laplacian. If the field is also solenoidal then ∇²φ = 0, Laplace's equation.

∇²φ = ∂²φ/∂x² + ∂²φ/∂y² + ∂²φ/∂z² = 0

∇² is the Laplacian operator; φ is the scalar potential of a field that is both irrotational and solenoidal.

Worked example

Question. Show that F = (2xy + z²) i + (x² + 2yz) j + (y² + 2zx) k is irrotational, decide whether it is solenoidal, and find its scalar potential φ. Hence give φ at (1, 1, 1).

  1. F₁ = 2xy + z², F₂ = x² + 2yz, F₃ = y² + 2zx.
  2. First curl component: ∂F₃/∂y − ∂F₂/∂z = 2y − 2y = 0.
  3. Second: ∂F₁/∂z − ∂F₃/∂x = 2z − 2z = 0.
  4. Third: ∂F₂/∂x − ∂F₁/∂y = 2x − 2x = 0.
  5. curl F = (0, 0, 0) everywhere, so F is irrotational and a potential exists.
  6. div F = 2y + 2z + 2x = 2(x + y + z). This is 6 at (1, 1, 1), not 0, so F is not solenoidal.
  7. Set ∂φ/∂x = 2xy + z². Integrate in x: φ = x²y + xz² + g(y, z).
  8. ∂φ/∂y = x² + ∂g/∂y = x² + 2yz, so ∂g/∂y = 2yz and g = y²z + h(z).
  9. φ = x²y + xz² + y²z + h(z). Then ∂φ/∂z = 2xz + y² + h′(z) = y² + 2zx, so h′(z) = 0 and h = C.
  10. φ = x²y + y²z + z²x + C. Check: ∇φ = (2xy + z², x² + 2yz, y² + 2zx) = F.
  11. At (1, 1, 1): φ = 1 + 1 + 1 + C = 3 + C.

Output

0.0
0.0
0.0
6.0
3.0

Answer. F is irrotational but not solenoidal, with scalar potential φ = x²y + y²z + z²x + C; at (1, 1, 1) the potential is 3 + C.

Exam traps

  • Testing div or curl at one point, usually the origin, and declaring the whole field solenoidal or irrotational. The condition must hold at every point of the region.
  • Swapping the names: solenoidal is div F = 0, irrotational is curl F = 0.
  • Writing the unknown after the first integration as g(y) only. It must be g(y, z), because x-integration holds y and z fixed.
  • Dropping the + C, or adding a constant twice. One constant at the end is enough.
  • Sign slips in the curl: the first component is ∂F₃/∂y − ∂F₂/∂z, not the reverse.

6.4 Line integrals and work done

A line integral adds a field along a curve instead of across an interval. Chop the curve into tiny steps dr, dot each step with the field F at that point, and sum. For a force field this sum is work: only the part of the force along the road counts. Engineers use it for forces on a moving part, circulation around a wing section, and voltage around a circuit loop.

You need: definite integrals from Unit 2 and the gradient from 6.1; the potential shortcut is built in 6.3.

Adding a field along a curve

Let C be a curve in space and F = (F₁, F₂, F₃) a vector field. At a point on C, take the small displacement dr = (dx, dy, dz). The line integral of F along C is the sum of the component of F along each step:

∫_C F·dr = ∫_C (F₁ dx + F₂ dy + F₃ dz)

F₁, F₂, F₃ are the field components; dx, dy, dz are changes in x, y, z along C. The dot product F·dr uses only the part of F along the curve.

To compute it, parameterise C: r(t) = (x(t), y(t), z(t)), a ≤ t ≤ b. Then dr = r′(t) dt, so

∫_C F·dr = ∫_a^b F(r(t))·r′(t) dt

r(t) is the position on C; r′(t) = (x′(t), y′(t), z′(t)); a and b are the parameter values at the start and end. Reversing the direction changes t from b to a and flips the sign.

  1. Choose a parameter t and write x(t), y(t), z(t) for C.
  2. Write the start and end values of t, in order of travel.
  3. Compute r′(t) dt = (x′(t), y′(t), z′(t)) dt.
  4. Substitute x(t), y(t), z(t) into F to get F(r(t)).
  5. Dot F(r(t)) with r′(t), integrate from start t to end t.

Work done and circulation

Work done by a force F moving a particle along C is W = ∫_C F·dr. If F is in newtons and r in metres, W is in joules. A force at right angles to the motion does no work because F·dr = 0. A force opposite to dr gives negative work.

When C is closed, the line integral is written ∮_C F·dr and called the circulation. Circulation measures how much the field pushes around the loop. A fan blade, a wing section and a wire loop all have a natural circulation.

The value can depend on the path. In the worked example below, the same force gives 2/3 J along a parabola and 1 J along a straight line between the same two points. So work is not always just a difference of endpoints. 6.6 converts circulation round a closed plane curve into a double integral.

Path independence and the potential shortcut

Path independence means the integral from A to B is the same for every curve from A to B. For a plane field F = (M, N), a quick test is ∂N/∂x = ∂M/∂y. For a space field F = (F₁, F₂, F₃), the test is curl F = 0. If the test holds on a region with no holes, F is conservative and F = ∇φ for a scalar potential φ.

Then the fundamental-theorem shortcut applies:

∫_C F·dr = φ(B) − φ(A)

A is the start point, B is the end point, and φ is the potential. You do not integrate along C; you evaluate φ at two points. For a closed curve, the answer is zero.

If the test fails, the field is not conservative and the path matters. The worked example checks this: ∂N/∂x = −1 but ∂M/∂y = 1, so no potential exists and the two paths disagree. Find potentials as in 6.3.

Worked example

Question. A force F(x,y) = (x + y) i + (y − x) j acts on a particle. Find the work done from A(0,0) to B(1,1) along (a) the parabola y = x², (b) the straight line y = x. Then state whether the field is conservative. Take force in newtons and coordinates in metres.

  1. Take x = t, then y = t² on y = x². The start (0,0) is t = 0 and the end (1,1) is t = 1.
  2. Then r(t) = (t, t²) and dr = r′(t) dt = (1, 2t) dt.
  3. At r(t), F = (x + y, y − x) = (t + t², t² − t).
  4. Dot product: F·dr = (t + t²)(1) + (t² − t)(2t) = t + t² + 2t³ − 2t² = t − t² + 2t³.
  5. Integrate: W₁ = ∫₀¹ (t − t² + 2t³) dt = 1/2 − 1/3 + 1/2 = 2/3 J.
  6. Straight line y = x: take x = t, y = t, 0 ≤ t ≤ 1. Then F = (2t, 0) and dr = (1, 1) dt, so F·dr = 2t. W₂ = ∫₀¹ 2t dt = 1 J.
  7. Test: M = x + y, N = y − x. ∂N/∂x = −1 and ∂M/∂y = 1. They differ, so F is not conservative; the two works legitimately differ.

  • Reversing the direction of travel. Going from B to A changes the sign: ∫ from B to A = −∫ from A to B.
  • Assuming two paths give the same work without checking curl F = 0 or ∂N/∂x = ∂M/∂y. If the test fails, path matters.
  • Substituting y = x² into F but leaving dx and dy mixed. Replace both x and y and both dx and dy by their t-forms.
  • Ignoring the sign when force and motion oppose. F·dr negative means the field takes energy out along that step.

6.5 Surface and volume integrals

A surface integral adds values over a curved sheet. A volume integral adds values through a solid. Both look new, but each becomes an ordinary double or triple integral once you write the geometry as z = f(x, y) or as limits on x, y, z. Engineers use them for flux through a wing or a phone casing, charge in a cloud, and heat through a wall. The field supplies the value; the shape supplies the area or volume element.

You need: Unit 5 for double and triple integrals, Fubini, and coordinate limits; 6.1 for the gradient and unit normal.

Scalar surface integrals

A surface S is a two-sided sheet in space. A scalar surface integral adds a number φ at each point of S over the area of S: ∬_S φ dS. Here dS is a small patch of area on S. If φ = 1, the integral gives the area of S.

dS = √(1 + f_x² + f_y²) dA

S is the graph z = f(x, y); f_x = ∂f/∂x and f_y = ∂f/∂y; dA = dx dy is the area element in the shadow D on the xy-plane.

The shadow D gives the limits. Project S onto the xy-plane, integrate over D, and replace dS by the formula. For a plane x + y + z = 1, take z = 1 − x − y, so f_x = −1 and f_y = −1.

  1. Write S as z = f(x, y) or as g(x, y, z) = c.
  2. Find dS or the unit normal n.
  3. Project S to a region D with constant outer limits.
  4. Put the limits and integrate the resulting double integral.

Flux through an oriented surface

A vector surface integral, or flux integral, adds the part of F along the normal to S: ∬_S F·n dS. F = (F_x, F_y, F_z) is a vector field; n is a unit normal; F·n is the normal component. The value counts field crossing S per unit area. Some syllabi call this a flux integral.

F·n dS = (−F_x f_x − F_y f_y + F_z) dA

S is z = f(x, y), n points upward, f_x and f_y are the partial derivatives, and dA = dx dy. For a downward normal, multiply the right side by −1.

Orientation is a choice of one side of S. Reverse n and the flux changes sign. For a closed surface, take n pointing outward. If S is g(x, y, z) = c, a unit normal is n = ∇g/|∇g|, with ∇g = (g_x, g_y, g_z). Then F·n = F·∇g/|∇g|.

  • Scalar integral ∬ φ dS: no normal, no sign change.
  • Flux integral ∬ F·n dS: normal direction matters.
  • Use a unit normal; a non-unit normal scales the answer wrongly.

Volume integrals and limits

A volume integral adds a value f(x, y, z) through a solid V: ∭_V f dV. In Cartesian coordinates dV = dx dy dz, so it is a triple integral. If f = 1, the integral gives the volume of V.

∭_V f dV = ∫∫∫_V f(x, y, z) dz dy dx

V is the solid; the order of dz, dy, dx can change by Fubini's theorem; the limits come from the boundary of V.

Read limits from the shape. Project V onto a coordinate plane, give the inner variable limits from the lower to the upper surface, and keep the outer limits constant. For a tetrahedron x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0, z ≥ 0, x + y + z ≤ 1, use z from 0 to 1 − x − y, y from 0 to 1 − x, x from 0 to 1.

  1. Choose an order that keeps limits simple.
  2. For a box all six limits are constants.
  3. For a disk base use polar coordinates from Unit 5.
  4. Check f = 1 gives the known volume.

Worked example

Question. Find the flux ∬_S F·n dS for F = (x, y, z) across the part S of the plane x + y + z = 1 lying in the first octant, with n pointing away from the origin.

  1. Let g(x, y, z) = x + y + z. Then ∇g = (1, 1, 1) and |∇g| = √(1² + 1² + 1²) = √3. The unit normal away from the origin is n = (1, 1, 1)/√3.
  2. On S, x + y + z = 1, so F·n = (x, y, z)·(1, 1, 1)/√3 = (x + y + z)/√3 = 1/√3.
  3. Write S as z = 1 − x − y. Then f_x = −1, f_y = −1, and dS = √(1 + (−1)² + (−1)²) dA = √3 dA. D is the triangle x ≥ 0, y ≥ 0, x + y ≤ 1.
  4. Flux = ∬_S F·n dS = ∬_D (1/√3)(√3) dA = ∬_D dA. The area of D is (1/2)(1)(1) = 0.5 square units. So flux = 0.5.
  5. Check by 6.8: div F = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3. The tetrahedron volume is 1/6 cubic units, so ∭_V div F dV = 3 × (1/6) = 0.5. This matches.

Answer. The flux is ∬_S F·n dS = 0.5 in field × area units.

Exam traps

  • Using dA instead of dS: for a slope, dS = √(1 + f_x² + f_y²) dA, so the area is larger than its shadow.
  • Using a non-unit normal: n = ∇g is not unit; divide by |∇g|, otherwise F·n is too large by that factor.
  • Sign of n: reversing n reverses the flux; for a closed surface use outward.
  • Mixing scalar and vector surface integrals: ∬ φ dS has no normal; ∬ F·n dS needs orientation.
  • Wrong projection: the first-octant part of x + y + z = 1 projects to a triangle, not the unit square.

6.6 Green's theorem in the plane

Green's theorem turns a line integral around a closed plane curve into a double integral over the region inside it. For a curve C that runs anticlockwise and a region R, the integral of M dx + N dy along C equals the integral of ∂N/∂x − ∂M/∂y over R. The theorem saves work when the boundary has many pieces. It also gives area formulas and shows when a line integral depends only on endpoints.

You need line integrals from 6.4 and double integrals over rectangles and triangles from Unit 5, Multiple Integrals.

The statement and positive orientation

Let R be a closed plane region with boundary C. Let M(x,y) and N(x,y) have continuous first partial derivatives on R and on C. If C is piecewise smooth, closed, and taken anticlockwise, then the line integral and the double integral are equal.

∮<sub>C</sub> (M dx + N dy) = ∬<sub>R</sub> (∂N/∂x − ∂M/∂y) dA

M and N are functions of x and y; C is the anticlockwise boundary of R; dA is the area element dx dy; the left side is a line integral round C and the right side is a double integral over R.

Positive orientation means this: walk along C in the chosen direction. The region R stays on your left. For a simple rectangle, that is anticlockwise: bottom edge left to right, right edge upward, top edge right to left, left edge downward.

The curve may have corners. A triangle or a rectangle is fine. Split a cornered curve into smooth pieces and add the line integrals. The statement needs C closed. An open curve cannot be used directly.

Two uses and the area formula

Most exam questions use the theorem forward. The line integral has many edges and awkward limits. The double integral has one region and simpler limits. Compute ∂N/∂x and ∂M/∂y first. Keep the order: N first, then M.

Use it backward when you want an area. Pick M and N so that ∂N/∂x − ∂M/∂y = 1. Then the double integral is just the area of R. One choice is M = −y/2 and N = x/2. Check: ∂N/∂x = 1/2 and ∂M/∂y = −1/2, so the difference is 1. Green's theorem then gives the area formula.

A = ∬<sub>R</sub> dA = ½ ∮<sub>C</sub> (x dy − y dx)

A is the area of R; C is the anticlockwise boundary; x and y are the coordinates on C.

Another choice is M = 0 and N = x. Then A = ∮C x dy. The formula A = ½∮(x dy − y dx) is symmetric and often easier on a circle.

Corners, holes, and sign

The theorem still holds when R has a hole. Let C2 be the outer boundary and C1 the inner boundary. Orient C2 anticlockwise and C1 clockwise. Then R stays on the left on both curves. The formula becomes ∮C2 + ∮C1 = ∬R (∂N/∂x − ∂M/∂y) dA.

For a hole, a common mistake is to orient both boundaries anticlockwise. That leaves the hole on the left for the inner curve, so the sign is wrong. Draw an arrow on each boundary before you compute.

If you reverse the direction of C, every dx and dy changes sign. The line integral changes sign, and the double integral does not. So a clockwise curve gives the negative of the anticlockwise answer. State the orientation in your answer.

Continuity matters. If M or N or a first partial derivative blows up inside R, do not apply the theorem across that point. Cut a small circle around the bad point and take a limit.

6.7 Stokes' theorem

Stokes' theorem turns a line integral round a closed loop into a surface integral over any sheet that the loop bounds. The loop integral measures circulation; the sheet integral measures how much the field curls through the sheet. The two are equal. It exists because circulation and curl are the same idea seen at different scales. Engineers use it to compute work round a wing section, induced voltage round a circuit, and fluid swirl through a pipe mouth, often by swapping a hard integral for an easy one.

You need: curl from 6.2, line integrals from 6.4, surface integrals from 6.5, and the flat case from 6.6.

The statement and the right-hand rule

Take an open surface S: a sheet with a rim, not a closed bag. Give it a unit normal n at every point. Its boundary is a closed curve C. Orient C by the right-hand rule: curl the fingers of your right hand along C; your thumb points along n. Reverse C, and n flips too.

∮C F·dr = ∬S (∇×F)·n dS

C is the closed boundary curve of the open surface S; F is a vector field with continuous partial derivatives; dr is a small step along C; ∇×F is the curl of F; n is the unit normal to S; dS is a small area element on S; the dot product (∇×F)·n keeps only the normal part of the curl.

The left side is a single integral along the rim. The right side is a double integral over the sheet. Both give the same number.

S may be curved, tilted, or wavy. Any surface that has C as its boundary gives the same right side. So you may replace a difficult sheet by a flat disk with the same rim. That freedom is the working power of the theorem.

The orientation rule links the two sides. Flipping n flips the sign of the surface integral. The matching flip of C flips the line integral. Both sides change together, so the equality survives.

Why a flat surface gives Green's theorem

Put S in the plane z = 0, so n = k. Write F = P i + Q j + R k with P, Q, R functions of x, y, z.

∇×F = (∂R/∂y − ∂Q/∂z) i + (∂P/∂z − ∂R/∂x) j + (∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y) k

P, Q, R are the components of F, and each partial derivative is taken with the other two variables held constant.

On the flat sheet the normal is k, so only the k-component survives: (∇×F)·k = ∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y. The area element is dS = dx dy. The surface integral becomes ∬D (∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y) dx dy over the plane region D inside C.

On the same sheet dz = 0, so F·dr = P dx + Q dy. The line integral is ∮C (P dx + Q dy). Matching the two sides gives Green's theorem. Stokes' theorem is Green's theorem lifted off the plane onto a curved surface. The normal component of the curl is the local spin density through the sheet; adding it over the sheet gives the total circulation round the rim.

Choosing the easier side

Two moves solve most problems. To evaluate a line integral ∮C F·dr on a nasty space curve, find any surface S with that curve as boundary, compute ∇×F, dot with n, and integrate over S. A flat disk or triangle is easiest. To evaluate a surface integral ∬S (∇×F)·n dS on a curved cap, replace the cap by a flat surface with the same boundary and integrate there.

  1. Sketch S and C. Mark n and the direction of C using the right-hand rule.
  2. Compute ∇×F.
  3. Choose coordinates for S. A plane z = constant gives dS = dx dy; a disk in the xy-plane gives polar coordinates.
  4. Dot the curl with n at each point before integrating. If n is constant and the curl is constant, the integral is just (∇×F)·n times the area.
  5. Integrate over the chosen surface.

The shortcut in step 4 is common in exams: a disk of radius 2 with curl (0, 0, 2) and n = k gives 2 × π(2²) at once.

Worked example

Question. Verify Stokes' theorem for F = (−y, x, 0) on the disk S: x² + y² ≤ 4 in the plane z = 0, with n = k and C the circle x² + y² = 4 traversed anticlockwise viewed from above.

  1. Write the components: P = −y, Q = x, R = 0.
  2. Compute the curl: ∇×F = (∂R/∂y − ∂Q/∂z, ∂P/∂z − ∂R/∂x, ∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y) = (0 − 0, 0 − 0, 1 − (−1)) = (0, 0, 2).
  3. Dot with n = k = (0, 0, 1): (∇×F)·n = 2.
  4. The disk has radius 2, so its area is π(2²) = 4π ≈ 12.5664 square units.
  5. Surface integral: ∬S (∇×F)·n dS = 2 × 4π = 8π ≈ 25.1327.
  6. Parametrize the boundary: r(t) = (2cos t, 2sin t, 0) with 0 ≤ t ≤ 2π, anticlockwise. Then dr = (−2sin t, 2cos t, 0) dt.
  7. On C, F(r(t)) = (−2sin t, 2cos t, 0). So F·dr = (−2sin t)(−2sin t) + (2cos t)(2cos t) + 0 = 4sin²t + 4cos²t = 4.
  8. Line integral: ∮C F·dr = ∫02π 4 dt = 4(2π) = 8π ≈ 25.1327.

Answer. Both sides equal 8π ≈ 25.1327, so Stokes' theorem holds for this field and disk.

Exam traps

  • Mismatching the orientation of C and n: reversing one side but not the other flips the sign of one integral only, so the two sides differ by a factor of −1.
  • Plugging n = k into a curved surface: on a tilted or curved sheet n changes from point to point, so write the local normal or switch to a flat surface with the same boundary.
  • Taking a component of F and then curling it: compute ∇×F first, then dot with n. The order matters because n may vary over S.
  • Applying Stokes' theorem to a closed surface such as a sphere: there is no boundary curve, and the closed-surface result is Gauss' theorem, not Stokes'.
  • Using a curve that is not the boundary of the chosen surface: the line integral must go round the rim of the same surface used on the right side.

6.8 Gauss divergence theorem

Gauss's divergence theorem turns a flux through a closed surface into a volume integral of the divergence. For a solid V with boundary S and outward unit normal n, the outward flux ∬_S F·n dS equals ∭_V (∇·F) dV. It exists because divergence measures source density: adding up sources inside must give the net flow out. Engineers use it to replace six awkward face integrals by one triple integral, and to derive Gauss's law in electrostatics and the continuity equation for water, charge or traffic.

You need: divergence from 6.2 and surface and volume integrals from 6.5.

The statement and its conditions

Take a solid V in space. Its boundary S is a closed surface: a balloon with no rim. The unit normal n points outward, away from V. Let F = (P, Q, R) have continuous first partial derivatives on V. Then

∬_S F·n dS = ∭_V (∇·F) dV

S is the closed boundary of V; n is the outward unit normal; F = (P, Q, R) is a smooth vector field; ∇·F = ∂P/∂x + ∂Q/∂y + ∂R/∂z; dS is a surface area element; dV is a volume element.

On the left, F·n is the component of F along the outward normal, dS is a small patch of area, and the double integral adds this over the whole surface. It is the net outward flux. On the right, ∇·F = ∂P/∂x + ∂Q/∂y + ∂R/∂z is the divergence, dV is a small volume, and the triple integral adds the divergence through V. The theorem says these two numbers are equal. S must be piecewise smooth, and F must be smooth inside; a singularity at a point breaks the result. Orientation is part of the statement: use the outward normal. Choosing inward flips the sign of the left side.

Why internal faces cancel

Cut V into many small boxes. For one tiny box of volume ΔV, the outward flux is approximately (∇·F) ΔV. This is the meaning of divergence: flux per unit volume. Now add the fluxes of all boxes. A face shared by two boxes is crossed twice in opposite directions, because the outward normals of the two boxes point opposite ways on that shared face. The two contributions cancel. What remains is the flux through the outer boundary S. As the boxes shrink, the approximation becomes exact:

Σ (∇·F) ΔV → ∭_V ∇·F dV

Σ adds over small boxes of volume ΔV; the arrow means the limit as the boxes shrink.

Hence flux out of S equals the total divergence inside. Use the theorem in either direction. If a problem gives six face integrals, compute one volume integral. If a problem gives a volume integral of a divergence, compute a flux. The left side needs a closed S; an open disc has a rim and no enclosed volume.

Source density, Gauss's law and continuity

Divergence is source density. Positive divergence at a point means more field leaves a tiny box around it than enters; negative divergence means a sink. The theorem is the global statement of that local fact. Two standard one-line appearances:

  • Gauss's law for electricity: ∮_S E·n dS = Q/ε₀, where E is the electric field, Q is the charge inside S and ε₀ is the permittivity of free space. Writing Q = ∭_V ρ dV gives ∇·E = ρ/ε₀.
  • Continuity equation: ∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρu) = 0, where ρ is density and u is velocity. It says mass leaving a region equals the rate of decrease inside; the same form tracks charge or traffic.

In a tank, the flux of water across the walls plus the rate of change of stored water balance; the divergence form states this locally. For an incompressible fluid with constant ρ, this reduces to ∇·u = 0. A pipe with no leaks has zero divergence of the mass flux. A field with ∇·F = 0 is solenoidal: every closed surface has zero net flux. See 6.3.

Worked example

Question. Verify Gauss's divergence theorem for F = (x², y², z²) over the unit cube V = [0, 1] × [0, 1] × [0, 1], with S its boundary and outward normal.

  1. The surface S is the six faces of the cube. The field has P = x², Q = y², R = z², so ∇·F = ∂(x²)/∂x + ∂(y²)/∂y + ∂(z²)/∂z = 2x + 2y + 2z.
  2. Volume side: ∭_V (2x + 2y + 2z) dV = ∫₀¹∫₀¹∫₀¹ (2x + 2y + 2z) dz dy dx. Split into three terms. For the x term, 2∫₀¹ x dx = 2(1/2) = 1, and ∫₀¹dy = 1, ∫₀¹dz = 1, so its value is 1. The y and z terms also equal 1 each. Total = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3.
  3. Surface side, face x = 1: n = i, F·n = x² = 1 on that face, and the face area is 1, so flux = 1. Face x = 0: n = −i, F·n = −x² = 0, so flux = 0.
  4. Face y = 1: n = j, F·n = y² = 1, area 1, flux = 1. Face y = 0: flux = 0. Face z = 1: n = k, F·n = z² = 1, area 1, flux = 1. Face z = 0: flux = 0.
  5. Add all six faces: 1 + 0 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 0 = 3.

Answer. Both the outward flux through S and the volume integral of ∇·F equal 3, so the divergence theorem is verified for this field and cube.

Exam traps

  • Using the inward normal. The theorem is stated with outward n; an inward choice changes the sign of the surface integral.
  • Applying it to an open surface. A disc or a hemisphere without a base does not enclose a volume; the theorem does not apply.
  • On a cube, forgetting that the two faces with zero flux contribute nothing, or counting a face twice. Pair opposite faces and use their normals.
  • Ignoring a singularity inside V. A field like r/|r|³ has zero divergence away from the origin but is not smooth at the origin, so the theorem does not apply across a surface enclosing it.
  • Mixing up Gauss and Stokes. Gauss converts a closed surface integral to a volume integral; Stokes converts a closed curve integral to a surface integral.

6.9 Space curves: tangent, normal, curvature and torsion

Most curves in real life are not flat: a wire, a roller-coaster track, a helix. To describe how such a space curve bends and twists, attach three perpendicular unit vectors at each point — tangent, principal normal, binormal. Two numbers then do the work. Curvature measures how fast the tangent turns per unit length. Torsion measures how fast the curve leaves its own plane. Engineers use these numbers for robot paths, railway alignments and computer graphics. Only a few universities teach this chapter.

You need: arc length from Unit 2 and derivatives of vector-valued functions from Unit 4.

The moving frame

Write a space curve as r(t) = (x(t), y(t), z(t)). Here t is the parameter and r(t) is the position vector, in metres if the coordinates are metres. The derivative r′(t) = (x′(t), y′(t), z′(t)) points along the curve, and |r′(t)| is the speed.

Arc length s = ∫₀ᵗ |r′(u)| du measures distance along the curve in metres, so ds/dt = |r′|. Divide r′ by its length to get the unit tangent T = r′/|r′|. T has no units and points forward.

As s increases, T turns. Its derivative dT/ds is perpendicular to T. The unit vector N = (dT/ds)/|dT/ds| is the principal normal. Set B = T × N; this is the binormal, a unit vector. T, N, B form a right-handed frame that travels with the curve.

For a plane curve, B is constant and N lies in the plane. For a space curve, B can rotate, which is the new feature.

Curvature, torsion and the Frenet–Serret formulas

Curvature κ = |dT/ds| measures how fast T turns per unit length. In terms of t, κ = |r′ × r″| / |r′|³, with units 1/length. A straight line has κ = 0; a circle of radius a has κ = 1/a.

κ = |dT/ds| = |r′ × r″| / |r′|³

κ is curvature in 1/length; T is unit tangent; s is arc length in metres; r′ and r″ are derivatives with respect to t.

Torsion τ = (r′ × r″) · r‴ / |r′ × r″|² measures twisting out of the plane of T and N. Units are 1/length. A plane curve has τ = 0; a circular helix has constant nonzero τ.

τ = (r′ × r″) · r‴ / |r′ × r″|²

τ is torsion in 1/length; r‴ is the third derivative; the dot is the scalar triple product.

Frenet–Serret: T′ = κN, N′ = −κT + τB, B′ = −τN. Derivatives are with respect to s. The first says T turns towards N at rate κ; the third says B turns towards −N at rate τ.

Worked example

Question. For the helix r(t) = (2 cos t, 2 sin t, t), with coordinates in metres and t in radians, find the curvature κ and the torsion τ. Show that both are independent of t.

  1. r′(t) = (−2 sin t, 2 cos t, 1), so |r′| = √(4 sin²t + 4 cos²t + 1) = √5 m.
  2. r″(t) = (−2 cos t, −2 sin t, 0), and r‴(t) = (2 sin t, −2 cos t, 0).
  3. r′ × r″ = (2 sin t, −2 cos t, 4). Its length is √(4 sin²t + 4 cos²t + 16) = √20 = 2√5 m².
  4. κ = |r′ × r″| / |r′|³ = 2√5 / (√5)³ = 2√5 / (5√5) = 2/5 = 0.4 m⁻¹.
  5. (r′ × r″) · r‴ = (2 sin t)(2 sin t) + (−2 cos t)(−2 cos t) + 4·0 = 4 m³.
  6. |r′ × r″|² = 20 m⁴, so τ = 4/20 = 1/5 = 0.2 m⁻¹. Both values are unchanged when t changes.

Answer. κ = 0.4 m⁻¹ and τ = 0.2 m⁻¹ at every point of the helix.

Exam traps

  • Putting |r′|² in the denominator of κ; the correct denominator is |r′|³.
  • Differentiating r′ directly instead of first making T = r′/|r′|; κ uses dT/ds.
  • Swapping the order in (r′ × r″) · r‴ flips the sign of τ.
  • Writing κ in metres; curvature and torsion have units 1/length, so m⁻¹.
  • Assuming every helix has τ = 0; a circular helix has nonzero τ, and τ = 0 only when the pitch is zero (a circle).

Takeaways

  1. The gradient turns a scalar field into a vector; the directional derivative is just that vector dotted with a unit direction, so the maximum rate of increase is |∇φ|.
  2. All three first-order operators come from the same ∇, so their product rules and the identities ∇ × ∇φ = 0 and ∇ · (∇ × F) = 0 follow from ordinary differentiation.
  3. Zero divergence means no sources (solenoidal); zero curl means no rotation (irrotational) and guarantees a scalar potential.
  4. A line integral of a force field is work, and for an irrotational field the path does not matter — only the two endpoints do.
  5. Surface and volume integrals are not new calculus; write the surface or solid in parameters, put in the Jacobian factor, and integrate.
  6. Green, Stokes and Gauss are one idea in three settings: an integral over a boundary equals an integral of a derivative over the inside.
  7. Green's theorem is Stokes' theorem restricted to a flat region in the xy-plane.
  8. For space curves, curvature measures bending and torsion measures twisting; both come from derivatives of the position vector with respect to arc length.

State the theorem with the closed-surface and outward-normal conditions; Choose F whose divergence is simple and set up the triple integral with correct limits; Compute and compare with the direct surface calculation for one face as a check; For an open surface the theorem fails; close it with a cap or use Stokes instead.

Analyze Obtain Green's theorem as the plane case of Stokes' theorem, and use it to express the area of a plane region as a line integral round its boundary. 12 marks

Take F = M i + N j with no z dependence and a flat surface in the xy-plane; Reduce the surface integral of the curl to a double integral of ∂N/∂x − ∂M/∂y; Choose M = −y/2, N = x/2 to get area = ½∮(x dy − y dx); Apply to one region, for example an ellipse, as a check.

Glossary

Arc length
The distance measured along a curve, s = ∫|r′(t)| dt.
Circulation
The line integral of a vector field round a closed curve, ∮_C F · dr.
Conservative field
A field whose line integral between two points does not depend on the path taken.
Curl
∇ × F, a vector giving the local rotation of a vector field.
Curvature
κ = |dT̂/ds|, measuring how quickly the curve bends; its reciprocal is the radius of curvature.
Del operator
The symbolic vector ∇ = i∂/∂x + j∂/∂y + k∂/∂z used to define gradient, divergence and curl.
Directional derivative
The rate of change of a scalar field per unit distance along a chosen unit direction.
Divergence
∇ · F, a scalar giving the net outflow of a vector field per unit volume at a point.
Flux
The total amount of a vector field passing through a surface, counting only the normal component.
Gradient
∇φ, the vector of partial derivatives of a scalar field, normal to the level surfaces.
Irrotational
Having zero curl everywhere, so that the field has no local spin.
Laplacian
∇²φ = ∇ · (∇φ) = ∂²φ/∂x² + ∂²φ/∂y² + ∂²φ/∂z².
Level surface
The set of points where a scalar field equals a constant; the gradient is normal to it.
Line integral
An integral of a field along a curve, written ∫_C F · dr.
Orientation
The choice of direction of the normal to a surface, or of travel along a curve, fixed by convention.
Osculating plane
The plane at a point of a space curve that contains both the tangent and the principal normal.
Principal normal
N̂ = (dT̂/ds)/|dT̂/ds|, the direction in which the curve is turning.
Scalar field
A function that assigns one number to each point of space, such as temperature or pressure.
Scalar potential
A scalar φ with F = ∇φ; it exists when F is irrotational on a simply connected region.
Simple closed curve
A curve that starts and ends at the same point and does not cross itself in between.
Solenoidal
Having zero divergence everywhere, so that no sources or sinks exist inside the region.
Surface integral
An integral of a field over a surface, written ∬_S F · n̂ dS for the flux form.
Torsion
τ, measuring how quickly a space curve twists out of its osculating plane; zero for plane curves.
Unit tangent
T̂ = dr/ds, the direction of travel along a curve in terms of arc length.
Vector field
A function that assigns a vector to each point of space, such as fluid velocity or electric field.
Volume integral
An integral of a function over a solid region, written ∭_V f dV.
