2.1 Definite integrals and the fundamental theorem of calculus

A definite integral measures accumulated change: area under a curve, distance from speed, charge from current. Cut the interval into thin strips, add rectangle areas, and let strip width shrink to zero. The limit of those sums is the definite integral. The fundamental theorem of calculus connects this limit to the reverse of differentiation: evaluate an antiderivative at the two ends and subtract. That turns a long summing process into two substitutions. Engineers use it to compute work, energy and total flow; a UPI transaction log summed over a day is the same idea.

You need Unit 1: limits, continuity and derivative rules from Derivative as a function.

From thin strips to a definite integral

Take a continuous function f on a closed interval [a, b]. Here f(x) is the height at x, a is the left end and b is the right end. Divide [a, b] into n small pieces of width Δx = (b − a)/n. In piece i choose any sample point xᵢ*. The rectangle there has height f(xᵢ*) and area f(xᵢ*)Δx. Add all rectangles: Sₙ = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ f(xᵢ*) Δx. Sₙ is a Riemann sum. It overestimates where f rises and underestimates where f falls, but the errors cancel as strips get thin.

Let n → ∞, so Δx → 0. If all sums approach one number independent of the sample points, that number is the definite integral.

∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = lim (n→∞) Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ f(xᵢ*) Δx

f is the integrand, a is the lower limit, b is the upper limit, Δx = (b − a)/n, and xᵢ* is a sample point in strip i.

Read ∫ₐᵇ as integral from a to b. x is a dummy variable: ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx = ∫ₐᵇ f(t)dt. The value is a number, not a function. If f(x) ≥ 0, the integral is the area under the curve. If f goes below the x-axis, area below counts negative. That signed area is why the integral gives net change.

Properties and the first fundamental theorem

Three properties follow directly from sums. Linearity: ∫ₐᵇ [c f(x) + d g(x)] dx = c∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx + d∫ₐᵇ g(x)dx for constants c and d. Additivity: ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx = ∫ₐᶜ f(x)dx + ∫_cᵇ f(x)dx when c lies between a and b. Reversal: ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx = −∫_bᵃ f(x)dx. These let you split a beam, a day, or a piecewise path into simpler intervals.

Now fix a and let the upper end move. Define the area function F(x) = ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt. Here t is the dummy integration variable and x is the moving upper limit. Increase x by a tiny h. The extra area is roughly a thin strip of height f(x) and width h, so F(x+h) − F(x) ≈ f(x)h. Divide by h and let h → 0: F′(x) = lim (h→0) [F(x+h) − F(x)]/h = f(x). This is FTC part 1.

d/dx ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt = f(x)

F(x) is the accumulated area from a to x, f is continuous, and x is the upper limit.

The derivative of the accumulated area is the current height. Continuity of f makes the strip error vanish. The lower limit a does not appear in the derivative; changing a shifts F by a constant, and constants differentiate to zero.

The second fundamental theorem and net change

FTC part 2 evaluates the integral without summing. If F is any antiderivative of f on [a, b], meaning F′(x) = f(x) there, then ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a). The notation [F(x)]ₐᵇ means F(b) − F(a). The theorem works because all antiderivatives differ by a constant; that constant cancels in the subtraction. Procedure: find one antiderivative, substitute the upper limit, substitute the lower limit, subtract in that order. A common slip is writing F(b) alone or F(a) − F(b). The order matters: swapping gives the negative.

∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a), when F′ = f

F is an antiderivative of f on [a, b], a is the lower limit, and b is the upper limit.

The applied form is the net change theorem. For any differentiable function Q, ∫ₐᵇ Q′(x) dx = Q(b) − Q(a). The integral of a rate gives the net change in the quantity. If v(t) is velocity in m/s, ∫ₜ₁ᵗ₂ v(t)dt is displacement in metres; total distance uses |v(t)|. If P′(t) is a production rate in units/hour, the integral gives units produced. In an electricity bill, integrating power over time gives energy in kWh. Antiderivatives come from reversing derivative rules; see Indefinite integrals and techniques of integration.

Worked example

Question. Evaluate ∫₀² (3x² + 2x) dx by the fundamental theorem of calculus. State what the number represents as an area.

  1. Identify f(x) = 3x² + 2x, a = 0, b = 2. The integrand is continuous on [0, 2], so FTC applies.
  2. Find F with F′(x) = f(x). Reverse the power rule: ∫3x² dx = x³ and ∫2x dx = x². Choose F(x) = x³ + x². Check: F′(x) = 3x² + 2x.
  3. Evaluate at the upper limit: F(2) = 2³ + 2² = 8 + 4 = 12.
  4. Evaluate at the lower limit: F(0) = 0³ + 0² = 0.
  5. Subtract in order: F(2) − F(0) = 12 − 0 = 12.
  6. Since f(x) > 0 for x > 0, the integral is the area under the curve from 0 to 2, so the area is 12 square units.

Answer. ∫₀² (3x² + 2x) dx = 12 square units.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the lower limit: ∫ₐᵇ f = F(b) − F(a), not F(b).
  • Reversing the order: F(a) − F(b) gives the negative; ∫ₐᵇ = −∫_bᵃ.
  • Treating a definite integral as a family: it is a number. An indefinite integral is a family of functions; see Chapter 2.2.
  • Using the same letter as dummy and limit: d/dx ∫ₐˣ f(x)dt is confusing; write d/dx ∫ₐˣ f(t)dt.
  • Applying FTC across a break: for piecewise or discontinuous integrands, split the interval at the break and apply FTC on each continuous piece.

2.2 Indefinite integrals and techniques of integration

An indefinite integral is the whole family of functions whose derivative is a given function. It exists because differentiation loses information: many functions share one derivative, so the answer carries a constant C. Each integration technique reverses one differentiation rule. Substitution reverses the chain rule, parts reverses the product rule, and partial fractions split rational functions into pieces you can integrate. Engineers use these tools to recover a quantity from its rate, such as distance from speed, charge from current, or total cost from a marginal cost. The same antiderivatives evaluate definite integrals in Chapter 2.1.

You need: derivative rules, the product rule and the chain rule from Unit 1, and the definite integral from Chapter 2.1.

The antiderivative family and standard forms

Integration asks: which function F has derivative f? If F′(x)=f(x), then every function F(x)+C also has derivative f(x), because the derivative of a constant is zero. Write ∫ f(x) dx = F(x)+C. The symbol ∫ is the integral sign, f(x) is the integrand, dx names the variable, and C is the constant of integration. C is not decoration: without it you have one antiderivative, not the family. Check every answer by differentiating it; you should recover f(x). The table lists forms you should know. Each is a differentiation rule read backwards. In applications, if r(t) is a rate, an antiderivative gives an accumulated total up to a constant. The definite integral over an interval gives the net change; see Chapter 2.1.

Substitution: reversing the chain rule

Substitution reverses the chain rule. It is useful when an integrand contains an inner function g(x) and, up to a constant, its derivative g′(x). The rule is ∫ f(g(x)) g′(x) dx = ∫ f(u) du, where u = g(x) and du = g′(x) dx. Some syllabi call this change of variable. If the derivative is off by a constant, adjust that constant outside the integral.

  1. Choose u as the inner function whose derivative also appears.
  2. Compute du = g′(x) dx.
  3. Replace every x and dx by u and du.
  4. Integrate in u, then substitute back u = g(x).

For example, ∫ 2x cos(x²) dx has u=x² and du=2x dx, so it becomes ∫ cos u du = sin u + C = sin(x²)+C. Differentiate sin(x²)+C to get 2x cos(x²). For a definite integral, change the limits to u-values and do not substitute back; the method is the same as in Chapter 2.1.

Integration by parts: reversing the product rule

Integration by parts reverses the product rule. From (uv)′ = u′v + uv′, integrate both sides and rearrange: ∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du. Here u and v are functions of x, du and dv their differentials. Choose u so that du is simpler than u, and dv so that v is easy to find. For ∫ x e2x dx, take u=x and dv=e2x dx; then du=dx and v=½e2x. The new integral ∫ v du = ∫ ½e2x dx is simpler. If your choice makes the new integral harder, swap roles. A common guide is LIATE: logarithm, inverse trig, algebraic, trig, exponential, choosing u from the left. For ∫ ln x dx, take u=ln x and dv=dx; then du=(1/x)dx and v=x, giving x ln x − x + C. Check by differentiating the final answer. The worked example below uses this method.

Partial fractions and simple trigonometric integrals

Partial fractions turn a rational function P(x)/Q(x) into simpler fractions you can integrate. First make the degree of P less than Q by division if needed. Factor Q into linear and irreducible quadratic factors. For each distinct linear factor x−a, write A/(x−a); for a repeated factor (x−a)², write A/(x−a)+B/(x−a)²; for an irreducible quadratic, write (Ax+B)/(quadratic). Solve for the constants by multiplying out or substituting convenient x-values. Check the partial-fraction sum by putting it over a common denominator. Then integrate each term using 1/(x−a) and ln, or arctan for quadratics. Example: 1/(x²−1)=1/2[1/(x−1)−1/(x+1)], so ∫ dx/(x²−1)=½ln|x−1|−½ln|x+1|+C. For trigonometric integrals, use identities. sin²x=(1−cos2x)/2 and cos²x=(1+cos2x)/2 remove powers. Also ∫tan x dx=ln|sec x|+C.

Worked example

Question. Evaluate ∫ x e2x dx by integration by parts. Show every step and verify the result by differentiation.

  1. Choose u = x and dv = e2x dx.
  2. Then du = dx and v = ∫ e2x dx = ½e2x. Do not add C at this stage.
  3. Use ∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du: ∫ x e2x dx = x·½e2x − ∫ ½e2x dx.
  4. The remaining integral is ½∫ e2x dx = ½·½e2x = ¼e2x.
  5. So ∫ x e2x dx = ½x e2x − ¼e2x + C.
  6. Check: d/dx[½x e2x − ¼e2x + C] = ½e2x + x e2x − ½e2x = x e2x.
  7. At x = 1, both the derivative of the answer and x e2x equal 7.389056098931 (12 d.p.).

Answer. ∫ x e2x dx = ½x e2x − ¼e2x + C, and differentiation returns x e2x.

Exam traps

  • Dropping +C in an indefinite integral. The answer is a family of functions, not one function.
  • Choosing u and dv badly in parts. For ∫x e2x dx, taking u=e2x and dv=x dx makes the new integral harder; take u=x.
  • In substitution, forgetting to replace dx or forgetting to substitute back at the end. In a definite integral, change the limits instead.
  • In partial fractions, using the wrong denominator factor or missing a repeated factor term such as B/(x−a)².
  • Sign slips in standard forms. ∫sin ax dx = −(1/a)cos ax + C because the derivative of cos ax is −a sin ax.

2.3 Improper integrals and their convergence

A definite integral needs a finite interval and a bounded integrand. If either fails, the integral is improper. Replace the bad end by a variable and take a limit: R→∞ for an infinite interval, or c→a⁺ for a function that blows up at a. The limit is the integral. This idea is used in probability densities with infinite support, in the gamma function, and in Laplace transforms. This chapter decides convergence without always finding a value, using p-integrals and comparison.

You need definite integrals and the fundamental theorem, integration techniques, and limits.

Two ways an integral breaks

An improper integral is a limit of proper integrals. First kind: the interval is infinite. For a function f continuous on [a, ∞), define ∫ₐ^∞ f(x) dx = lim R→∞ ∫ₐ^R f(x) dx. R is a real number with R > a. If the limit is a finite real number, the integral converges; otherwise it diverges. A similar definition holds for (−∞, b]. Some syllabi call these improper integrals of the first kind.

Second kind: the integrand is unbounded. If f blows up at b and is continuous on [a, b), define ∫ₐ^b f(x) dx = lim c→b⁻ ∫ₐ^c f(x) dx. Here c approaches b from below. If f blows up at a, use c→a⁺. If f blows up at an interior point, split the interval at that point. Both pieces must converge. Take the limit after integration, never by substituting ∞ or the blow-up point as a number.

The p-integrals set the benchmark

For a real constant p, the two p-integrals are the reference tests. ∫₁^∞ x^(−p) dx converges exactly when p > 1. For p ≠ 1, ∫ x^(−p) dx = x^(1−p)/(1−p). At R, x^(1−p)/(1−p) tends to 0 when 1−p < 0, that is when p > 1, giving the value 0 − 1/(1−p) = 1/(p−1). When p < 1, 1−p > 0 and the expression grows without bound. For p=1, ∫₁^R x⁻¹ dx = ln R, which tends to ∞. Hence p=1 diverges.

∫₀¹ x^(−p) dx converges exactly when p < 1. For p ≠ 1, the proper integral from c to 1 equals 1/(1−p) − c^(1−p)/(1−p). For p < 1, c^(1−p) → 0 as c→0⁺, so the limit is 1/(1−p). For p > 1, 1−p < 0 and c^(1−p) grows without bound. For p = 1, the value ln(1/c) grows. Hence p = 1 diverges. The rules look opposite because the intervals are opposite: near ∞, a larger p makes x^(−p) decay faster; near 0, a smaller p makes x^(−p) blow up more slowly.

Comparison and absolute convergence

Comparison test. Let f and g be positive on the same improper interval, with 0 ≤ f(x) ≤ g(x). If ∫g converges, then ∫f converges. If ∫f diverges, then ∫g diverges. The reason is monotonicity: the proper integral of a positive f increases as R grows or as c moves toward the blow-up point. If it stays below a finite bound, it has a finite limit. Compare with a p-integral whose exponent you can read.

Example: ∫₁^∞ 1/(x²+1) dx converges because 1/(x²+1) ≤ 1/x² and ∫₁^∞ x^(−2) dx converges (p=2). Absolute convergence: if ∫|f(x)| dx converges, then ∫f(x) dx converges. Call the original integral absolutely convergent. The converse fails: an integral can converge without its absolute version converging. Test absolute convergence first when signs oscillate; if it fails, the original may still converge, so check separately. The comparison and absolute-convergence statements hold for both kinds.

Worked example

Question. Show that ∫₁^∞ x^(−2) dx converges and find its value.

  1. The upper limit is infinite, so write the definition: I = lim R→∞ ∫₁^R x^(−2) dx, with R > 1.
  2. An antiderivative is −x^(−1), because d/dx(−x^(−1)) = x^(−2).
  3. Evaluate the proper integral: [−x^(−1)]₁^R = −1/R − (−1/1) = 1 − 1/R.
  4. Take the limit: lim R→∞ (1 − 1/R) = 1 − 0 = 1.
  5. The limit is finite, so the improper integral converges. Its value is 1 square unit.

Answer. The integral converges to 1 square unit.

Exam traps

  • Treating ∞ as a number and writing F(∞) − F(1) directly. Write a limit and take it after integration.
  • Checking only one bad endpoint. If the integrand blows up inside the interval, split there and test both pieces.
  • Mixing the p-rules: ∫₁^∞ x^(−p) dx converges for p > 1, but ∫₀¹ x^(−p) dx converges for p < 1.
  • Using comparison in the wrong direction. A smaller divergent integrand proves nothing; a larger convergent integrand proves nothing.
  • Assuming absolute convergence is necessary for convergence. For oscillating integrands, the original may converge while ∫|f| diverges.

2.4 Beta and Gamma functions

Two integrals, improper at the endpoints or at infinity for the parameter values that matter here, do a surprising amount of work. The gamma function turns the factorial into a smooth curve. The beta function measures how two powers of t and (1 − t) share the interval from 0 to 1. Together they evaluate a whole family of definite integrals that resist ordinary substitution, and they carry the constants of probability, statistics and physics. Some syllabi call them the Eulerian integrals. This chapter defines both, gives their main identities, and shows how to reduce a given integral to one of these two standard shapes.

You need: improper integrals and their convergence from Chapter 2.3, substitution from Chapter 2.2, and factorial notation.

The gamma function

Start with an integral over the whole positive axis.

Γ(x) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>∞</sup> t<sup>x−1</sup> e<sup>−t</sup> dt

t is the integration variable (t > 0); x is a real parameter; e−t is the decaying exponential; Γ is read "gamma of x".

The integral is improper at both ends. Near t = 0 the integrand behaves like tx−1, which is integrable exactly when x > 0. As t grows, e−t falls faster than any power of t, so the tail always converges. The limit comparison test of Chapter 2.3 gives this, and the domain is x > 0.

Two facts follow by direct work. Put x = 1: the integrand becomes e−t, and the integral is 1. Now take Γ(x+1) and integrate by parts with u = tx and dv = e−tdt. The bracket txe−t vanishes at 0 and at ∞, and what remains is x times Γ(x).

Γ(x+1) = x Γ(x), Γ(1) = 1

the recurrence steps the argument up by 1 and multiplies by the old argument; Γ(1) = 1 is the starting value.

Apply the recurrence from Γ(1) again and again: Γ(n+1) = n! for every whole number n ≥ 0. So the gamma function is the factorial with the argument shifted by one. Γ(5) = 4! = 24, not 5!.

The beta function

The second function lives on the unit interval.

B(p,q) = ∫<sub>0</sub><sup>1</sup> t<sup>p−1</sup>(1−t)<sup>q−1</sup> dt

p > 0 and q > 0 are parameters; t is the integration variable; B is read "beta of p, q".

The interval is finite, but the integrand can blow up at an endpoint. Near t = 0 it behaves like tp−1, so we need p > 0. Near t = 1, write s = 1 − t; the integrand behaves like sq−1, so we need q > 0.

Put s = 1 − t, so dt = −ds. When t = 0, s = 1, and when t = 1, s = 0. The minus sign flips the limits 1 to 0 back to 0 to 1, and the two powers trade places. That proves the symmetry B(p,q) = B(q,p).

The beta function is not a new object at all. It is built from two gamma values.

B(p,q) = Γ(p) Γ(q) / Γ(p+q)

the numerator holds the two separate gamma values and the denominator holds the gamma of the sum. The proof uses a double integral, so it is deferred; see Unit 5, Multiple Integrals.

Some easy checks: B(1,1) = 1, B(p,1) = 1/p, and B(1/2,1/2) = Γ(1/2)²/Γ(1) = π. Also B(2,3) = 1!·2!/4! = 2/24 = 1/12.

Reducing an integral to beta or gamma form

  1. Check the limits. 0 to 1 means beta; 0 to ∞ means gamma. Any other upper limit needs a substitution first.
  2. For beta, force the integrand into the shape tp−1(1−t)q−1 and read the two exponents. Add 1 to each exponent to get p and q.
  3. For gamma, force the shape tx−1e−t. If the exponential is e−at, put u = at and divide the whole integral by ax.
  4. Apply the formula, convert Γ(n+1) to n!, and simplify the fraction.
  • ∫01 x⁴(1−x)³ dx = B(5,4) = Γ(5)Γ(4)/Γ(9) = 4!·3!/8! = 144/40320 = 1/280.
  • ∫0∞ x⁵ e−2x dx = Γ(6)/2⁶ = 120/64 = 15/8.

These two shapes cover a lot of ground. The normalising constant of the normal distribution, the density of the beta distribution in statistics, and many physics normalisations are beta or gamma integrals written in disguise.

Worked example

Question. Using the gamma function, evaluate B(2,3) = ∫01 t(1−t)² dt, and confirm the value by expanding the integrand directly.

  1. Match the integrand with the beta definition: tp−1 = t gives p = 2, and (1−t)q−1 = (1−t)² gives q = 3. Both are positive, so the integral converges.
  2. Apply the formula: B(2,3) = Γ(2)Γ(3)/Γ(5).

  4. Divide: B(2,3) = (1 × 2)/24 = 1/12.
  5. Direct check: (1−t)² = 1 − 2t + t², so the integrand is t − 2t² + t³. Its integral is t²/2 − 2t³/3 + t⁴/4 from 0 to 1, which is 1/2 − 2/3 + 1/4 = (6 − 8 + 3)/12 = 1/12.

Exam traps

  • Shift error on the factorial: Γ(n+1) = n!, so Γ(5) = 4! = 24 and Γ(2) = 1!, not 2!. Both the numerator and the denominator of the beta formula shift.
  • Reading p and q straight off the exponents: the exponent in the integrand is p − 1, so x² needs p = 3, not p = 2.
  • Missing the scale factor in the gamma form: ∫0∞ xne−axdx = Γ(n+1)/an+1, so the power of a goes with the exponential, not just a plain division.
  • Using the beta formula when the limits are not 0 and 1, or the gamma formula when the upper limit is finite. Substitute first to reach the standard limits.
  • Treating Γ(0) as a number. Γ(x) → ∞ as x → 0+, so Γ(0) does not exist; the domain starts strictly above 0.

2.5 Area of a region between curves

The area between two curves is the integral of the upper curve minus the lower curve over the interval between their intersection points. Picture thin vertical strips: each strip has height top minus bottom and width dx, so the strips sum to a definite integral. Engineers use it to measure clearance between profiles, the gap between revenue and cost curves, and visible area between plotted boundaries. Some syllabi call this area of a region between curves. Only a few universities teach this as a separate advanced topic. Vertical slices, horizontal slices, intersections, splitting, and symmetry follow.

You need: definite integrals and antiderivatives from Chapter 2.1 and Chapter 2.2.

Vertical slices: top minus bottom

Slice the region into thin vertical strips of width dx. At a fixed x, yT(x) is the top curve height and yB(x) is the bottom curve height. One strip has height yT(x) − yB(x) and area dA = [yT(x) − yB(x)] dx.

A = ∫<sub>a</sub><sup>b</sup> [y<sub>T</sub>(x) − y<sub>B</sub>(x)] dx

a and b are left and right x-limits; yT is top and yB is bottom; the integrand must be ≥0.

The limits a and b may be intersection points or given directly. If the curves cross, the top curve can become the bottom curve. Then the integral gives a signed net, not an area.

For y=x and y=x² on 0≤x≤1, top is y=x, bottom is y=x². Area = ∫01(x−x²)dx = [x²/2 − x³/3]01 = 1/6 square unit. The figure shows this.

Horizontal slices: right minus left

Some regions are easier as thin horizontal strips of height dy. Solve each curve for x. Let xR(y) be the right curve and xL(y) the left curve. A strip has width xR(y) − xL(y) and area [xR(y) − xL(y)] dy.

A = ∫<sub>c</sub><sup>d</sup> [x<sub>R</sub>(y) − x<sub>L</sub>(y)] dy

c and d are bottom and top y-limits; xR is right and xL is left.

Use horizontal slices when the curves come as x=g(y), or when a vertical slice would need two top formulas. Check that right minus left is nonnegative on c≤y≤d. A positive width keeps the integral nonnegative.

For x=y² and x=4, y runs −2 to 2. Area = ∫−22(4−y²)dy = 32/3 square units. This is the same idea turned sideways; use it when vertical slices would split.

Intersections, splitting and symmetry

  1. Set f(x)=g(x) and solve. These roots are possible limits or split points.
  2. Sort the roots. Test one x in each interval to find which curve is on top.
  3. Integrate top minus bottom on each interval and add the pieces.

A = ∫ab |f(x) − g(x)| dx

|...| is absolute value; split at every crossing.

Symmetry can halve the work. For mirror symmetry about the y-axis, area = 2∫0a[top−bottom]dx for the right half from 0 to a. For symmetry about the x-axis, use horizontal slices and double the upper half. Symmetry must match both curves and limits.

When a question says 'bounded by', solve for intersections. If limits are given, still check for crossings inside them. A crossing splits the region into separate pieces; each piece has its own top and bottom.

Worked example

Question. Find the area of the region bounded by y = x and y = x².

  1. Solve x = x²: x(1−x)=0, so x=0 and x=1. The intersection points are (0,0) and (1,1).
  2. Test x=1/2. y=x=0.5 and y=x²=0.25, so y=x is top and y=x² is bottom on 0<x<1.
  3. Write area A = ∫01(x − x²)dx.
  4. Antiderivative: x²/2 − x³/3.
  5. At x=1: 1/2 − 1/3 = 1/6. At x=0: 0. Difference = 1/6.
  6. The area is positive, so the order is correct.

Answer. The area is 1/6 square unit, with intersection x-values 0 and 1.

Exam traps

  • Integrating top minus bottom without checking which curve is on top; after an intersection the order may swap.
  • Using x-limits as y-limits for horizontal slices.
  • Forgetting to split at a crossing; the integral then gives a signed net, not area.
  • Using given endpoints without checking whether curves cross inside.
  • Mixing dx and dy: vertical slices need y as a function of x; horizontal slices need x as a function of y.

2.6 Arc length of a curve

Arc length is the distance you would walk along a curve, not the straight distance between its ends. Cut the curve into tiny pieces; each piece is almost a straight chord. Add the chord lengths and take a limit. The limit is a definite integral. Surveyors, road designers, cable installers and computer-graphics programs use it to measure curved paths. The same integral answers questions about a bent wire, a railway curve or a plotted function.

You need: definite integrals and the fundamental theorem, integration techniques, and derivative rules with the chain rule from Unit 1.

From chords to an integral

Take y=f(x) on an interval a≤x≤b, with f smooth there. Split [a,b] into small pieces of width Δx. Over one piece the curve rises by Δy. The chord between the piece's ends has length √((Δx)²+(Δy)²). Factor out Δx: chord = √(1+(Δy/Δx)²) Δx. As Δx→0, the slope Δy/Δx tends to f′(x). The sum of chord lengths tends to the definite integral below. A smooth curve has a continuous derivative, so it has no sharp corner.

L = ∫ₐᵇ √(1 + (f′(x))²) dx

L is the arc length; f′(x) is the derivative of f at x; a is the left x-limit and b is the right x-limit, with a<b; y=f(x) is smooth on [a,b].

Some syllabi call this the length of a curve. Use it only when y is a function of x on the whole interval.

Parametric form and simplification

Use a parameter t when the curve is given as x=x(t), y=y(t). The chord over a small change Δt has length √((Δx)²+(Δy)²). Divide and multiply by Δt: chord = √((Δx/Δt)²+(Δy/Δt)²) Δt. Taking Δt→0 gives the parametric formula. This form also handles vertical tangents, where dy/dx is infinite.

L = ∫ₐᵝ √((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²) dt

L is the arc length; x(t) and y(t) are the coordinate functions; t is the parameter; α is the starting parameter and β is the ending parameter, with α<β; dx/dt and dy/dt are the derivatives.

Simplify before integrating. Expand the squares, add, and use identities. For a circle x=r cos t, y=r sin t, the derivatives are −r sin t and r cos t; the sum of squares is r², so the integrand is r. For a piecewise smooth curve, split at corners, apply the formula to each smooth piece, and add the lengths. Space curves and curvature are in Unit 6.

Procedure for an exam answer

  1. Write the curve in y=f(x) form or in x(t), y(t) form. State the x-interval or t-interval.
  2. Differentiate. Square the derivative, or square both parametric derivatives.
  3. Add 1 for y=f(x), or add the two squares for parametric form. Take the square root.
  4. Simplify the integrand before integrating; look for a substitution or a standard integral.
  5. Check for corners. Split a piecewise smooth curve at each corner and add the pieces.
  6. Integrate, evaluate at the limits, and attach length units.

A quick sanity check: the arc length cannot be less than the straight-line distance between the endpoints. Equality happens only for a straight line. If your answer is smaller, check the square, the limits or the algebra.

Worked example

Question. Find the arc length of y=(2/3)x^(3/2) from x=0 to x=3. Show that L=14/3.

  1. Here f(x)=(2/3)x^(3/2), a=0, b=3.
  2. Differentiate: f′(x)=(2/3)(3/2)x^(1/2)=√x.
  3. Square: (f′(x))²=x. So 1+(f′(x))²=1+x.
  4. The length is L=∫₀³ √(1+x) dx.
  5. Let u=1+x, so du=dx. The antiderivative is (2/3)u^(3/2).
  6. Evaluate: L=(2/3)[(1+3)^(3/2)−(1+0)^(3/2)] = (2/3)(4^(3/2)−1^(3/2)).
  7. 4^(3/2)=8 and 1^(3/2)=1, so L=(2/3)(8−1)=14/3≈4.666666666666667 length units.

Answer. The arc length is 14/3 length units, approximately 4.666666666666667 length units.

Exam traps

  • Squaring only f′(x) and then forgetting the square root, or writing √(1+f′(x)) instead of √(1+(f′(x))²). The square is inside.
  • Using the y=f(x) formula on a curve that is not a function of x, such as a full circle. Use parametric form or split into arcs.
  • Running the formula through a sharp corner. The derivative may not exist there; split into smooth pieces and add.
  • Reversing the limits α and β in a parametric integral. For length take α<β; reversing changes the sign.
  • Dropping units. dx, dy, dt and L must use one consistent length unit before you state the answer.

2.7 Volume and surface area of revolution

Spin a flat curve around a line and it sweeps out a solid: a shaft, a dome, a wine glass. Slice that solid perpendicular to the axis and every slice is a disk, or a washer — a disk with a hole. A disk's area is π times a radius squared, so the volume becomes an integral of πR². The same curve, spun around, also makes a surface out of thin bands, each of area 2πy times a small slant length. Engineers need these integrals for machined volumes and for the paint or cooling area of a tank. Only a few universities teach this chapter.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the definite integral, Chapter 2.5 for upper minus lower, and Chapter 2.6 for the slant length √(1+(dy/dx)²).

Disks about the x-axis

Take y = f(x) ≥ 0 on [a, b] and rotate it about the x-axis. Cut the solid at x with a slice of thickness dx. That slice is a disk of radius R(x) = f(x), so its volume is π(R(x))² dx. Add the slices and let dx → 0.

V = π∫ₐᵇ (R(x))² dx

R(x) is the outer radius, the distance from the axis to the curve, in units of length; a and b are the end values of x; V is the volume in cubic units.

  1. Sketch the curve and mark the axis of rotation.
  2. Write R(x), the distance from the axis to the curve.
  3. Integrate π(R(x))² from a to b.

Washers, and the y-axis

If the region between an outer curve R(x) and an inner curve r(x) rotates, the slice is a washer. Subtract the hole, not the radius.

V = π∫ₐᵇ ((R(x))² − (r(x))²) dx

R is the outer radius and r the inner radius, both measured from the axis; r = 0 brings back the disk formula.

For rotation about the y-axis, disks need x written as a function of y. The alternative is shells: V = 2π∫ₐᵇ x f(x) dx, where x is the distance of the shell from the axis and f(x) is its height.

Surface of revolution

Rotate y = f(x) ≥ 0 about the x-axis. A short piece of curve of length ds sweeps a thin band. The band is nearly a cylinder of circumference 2πy and width ds, so its area is 2πy ds. From arc length, ds = √(1+(dy/dx)²) dx.

S = 2π∫ₐᵇ y√(1+(dy/dx)²) dx

y = f(x) is the height of the curve above the axis; dy/dx is its slope; S is the surface area in square units.

The square root is the price of the curve being slanted. Drop it and you add up flat rings, which always gives too small an answer.

Worked example

Question. The curve y = √x, 0 ≤ x ≤ 4, is rotated about the x-axis. Find the volume of the solid formed.

  1. Here R(x) = √x, a = 0, b = 4.
  2. Square the radius: (R(x))² = (√x)² = x.
  3. V = π∫₀⁴ x dx = π[x²/2]₀⁴.
  4. At x = 4, x²/2 = 16/2 = 8; at x = 0 it is 0.
  5. V = π(8 − 0) = 8π ≈ 25.1327.

Answer. V = 8π ≈ 25.13 cubic units.

Worked example 2

Question. The same curve y = √x, 0 ≤ x ≤ 4, is rotated about the x-axis. Find its surface area.

  1. dy/dx = 1/(2√x), so 1 + (dy/dx)² = 1 + 1/(4x) = (4x+1)/(4x).
  2. √(1+(dy/dx)²) = √(4x+1)/(2√x).
  3. S = 2π∫₀⁴ √x · √(4x+1)/(2√x) dx = π∫₀⁴ √(4x+1) dx.
  4. ∫√(4x+1) dx = (4x+1)^(3/2)/6, so S = π[(17)^(3/2) − 1]/6.
  5. 17^(3/2) ≈ 70.0928, so S ≈ π(69.0928)/6 ≈ 36.18.

Answer. S ≈ 36.18 square units.

Exam traps

  • Squaring only part of the radius: the integrand is (R(x))², and π(∫R)² is a different, wrong number.
  • Using the surface formula without √(1+(dy/dx)²); that gives a stack of cylinders, not the slanted band.
  • Swapping R and r in a washer; R is the outer, larger radius, so R² − r² is never negative.
  • Rotating about the y-axis but keeping the disk formula in x; either solve for x(y) or switch to shells.

Takeaways

  1. A definite integral is a limit of sums, so it always measures an accumulated quantity such as area, distance or work.
  2. The fundamental theorem is the bridge: differentiating and integrating are inverse operations, and ∫ₐᵇ f = F(b) − F(a) turns a limit into arithmetic.
  3. An indefinite integral is not a single function; the +C records that every antiderivative differs from every other by a constant.
  4. Every integration technique is a differentiation rule read backwards: substitution undoes the chain rule, parts undoes the product rule, partial fractions undoes a common denominator.
  5. An improper integral is defined by a limit; it is said to converge only when that limit is a finite number.
  6. The gamma and beta functions turn many awkward definite integrals into factorial and ratio arithmetic, and B(m,n) = Γ(m)Γ(n)/Γ(m+n) links the two.
  7. For area, always integrate upper minus lower; for volume, always square the radius; the difficulty is choosing the slice, not doing the integral.
  8. Arc length, volume and surface area all come from the same move: cut the object into thin pieces, write the piece length or area, then integrate.
  9. Choose the variable of integration to match the slice: dx for vertical slices, dy for horizontal slices, and pick whichever gives simpler limits.

Remember and understand

Remember Define an improper integral of the first kind and of the second kind. Give one example of each and state what it means for such an integral to converge. 8 marks

First kind: infinite interval, written as lim_{b→∞} ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx (and split at an interior point if both ends are infinite); Second kind: integrand has an infinite discontinuity at an endpoint or interior point, written as a one-sided limit, e.g. lim_{c→0⁺} ∫_c¹ x^(−1/2) dx; If the limit is a finite number the integral converges and equals that number; if the limit fails to exist or is infinite it diverges; Examples: ∫₁^∞ dx/x² = 1 (converges); ∫₀¹ dx/x diverges.

Understand State both parts of the fundamental theorem of calculus. Explain how the definite integral is defined as a limit of Riemann sums and how the net change theorem follows. 10 marks

Part 1: if f is continuous, then F(x) = ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt satisfies F′(x) = f(x); differentiating an accumulation function returns the rate; Part 2: if F is any antiderivative of f on [a,b], then ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a); Riemann sum: divide [a,b] into n parts of width Δx = (b−a)/n, pick xᵢ* in each part, and take the limit of Σ f(xᵢ*) Δx; Net change: ∫ₐᵇ F′(x) dx = F(b) − F(a) says the integral of a rate of change is the total change; example, integrating velocity gives displacement.

Remember Write down the standard formulas for arc length, volume of a solid of revolution (disk and washer), and area of a surface of revolution, defining every symbol used. 8 marks

Arc length: L = ∫ₐᵇ √(1 + (dy/dx)²) dx; parametric form L = ∫ √((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²) dt; Disk: V = π∫ₐᵇ [R(x)]² dx when rotating y = R(x) about the x-axis; Washer: V = π∫ₐᵇ ([R(x)]² − [r(x)]²) dx, R outer radius, r inner radius; Surface: S = 2π∫ₐᵇ y √(1 + (dy/dx)²) dx about the x-axis, and 2π∫ₐᵇ x √(1 + (dy/dx)²) dx about the y-axis; Say clearly that the square root is the length of a small chord, not the function value, and name the limits.

Apply Evaluate (a) ∫ x² e^x dx and (b) ∫ dx / ((x+1)(x+2)). Name the technique used in each case. 8 marks

Choose horizontal slices: the variable is y and y runs from 0 to 4; At height y the horizontal slice is a disk of radius x = √y, since y = x² inverted gives x = √y; V = π∫₀⁴ (√y)² dy = π∫₀⁴ y dy; V = π[y²/2]₀⁴ = 8π cubic units; Explain why dy was chosen: the axis of rotation is vertical, so slices perpendicular to it are horizontal.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Discuss the convergence of ∫₁^∞ dx/x^p and ∫₀¹ dx/x^p for all real values of p. 12 marks

For p ≠ 1, ∫₁^b x^(−p) dx = (b^(1−p) − 1)/(1 − p); take b → ∞; It converges exactly when 1 − p < 0, that is p > 1, with value 1/(p − 1); p = 1 gives log b → ∞, divergence; Near zero, ∫_c¹ x^(−p) dx = (1 − c^(1−p))/(1 − p), which converges exactly when p < 1; Summary: infinity needs a fast enough decay (p > 1), a blow-up at zero needs a mild enough one (p < 1); call these the p-test; Use the comparison test to extend the result to integrands bounded by a multiple of x^(−p).

Analyze Prove that Γ(n+1) = nΓ(n) for n > 0, deduce Γ(n+1) = n! for whole n, and show Γ(1/2) = √π. 14 marks

Start from Γ(n+1) = ∫₀^∞ x^n e^(−x) dx and integrate by parts with u = x^n, dv = e^(−x) dx; The boundary term x^n e^(−x) vanishes at 0 and at ∞ for n > 0, leaving n∫₀^∞ x^(n−1) e^(−x) dx = nΓ(n); With Γ(1) = ∫₀^∞ e^(−x) dx = 1, induction gives Γ(n+1) = n!; For Γ(1/2), substitute x = t² in Γ(1/2) = ∫₀^∞ x^(−1/2) e^(−x) dx to reach 2∫₀^∞ e^(−t²) dt; Evaluate the Gaussian integral (by squaring and using polar coordinates) to get Γ(1/2) = √π.

Evaluate Prove that B(m,n) = Γ(m)Γ(n)/Γ(m+n) for m > 0, n > 0, and hence evaluate ∫₀¹ x³(1 − x)² dx. 14 marks

Write Γ(m)Γ(n) as a double integral over the first quadrant and substitute x = u v, y = u(1 − v) with Jacobian u; The u-integral gives Γ(m+n) and the v-integral gives B(m,n); rearrange; Alternatively derive it in the form B(m,n) = ∫₀^∞ t^(m−1)/(1+t)^(m+n) dt; For ∫₀¹ x³(1−x)² dx, identify B(4,3) = Γ(4)Γ(3)/Γ(7) = (6·2)/720 = 1/60; Check the answer by direct expansion of x³(1 − x)².

Evaluate The region bounded by y = x² and y = x is rotated about the x-axis. Find the area of the region and the volume of the solid generated, and explain how the two set-ups differ. 16 marks

Intersections: x² = x gives x = 0 and x = 1, so the interval is [0,1]; Area = ∫₀¹ (x − x²) dx = 1/2 − 1/3 = 1/6; Volume by washers: outer radius R = x, inner radius r = x², so V = π∫₀¹ (x² − x⁴) dx; V = π(1/3 − 1/5) = 2π/15; State the difference: area integrates a difference of lengths, volume integrates a difference of squared lengths; both use upper minus lower.

Evaluate Evaluate ∫₀^∞ dx/(1 + x²) by substitution and a limit, justify each step with the fundamental theorem and the definition of an improper integral, and state whether the integral converges. 14 marks

Write the integral as lim_{b→∞} ∫₀^b dx/(1 + x²), by definition of an improper integral of the first kind; Use the antiderivative arctan x, justified by the fundamental theorem since 1/(1 + x²) is continuous on [0,b]; Get arctan b − arctan 0 = arctan b; Take the limit using arctan b → π/2 as b → ∞; The integral converges to π/2; note the integrand is positive and bounded, so no singularity arises.

Glossary

Antiderivative
A function F whose derivative equals the given function f.
Arc length
The length of a curve, obtained as the limit of lengths of inscribed chords.
Area between curves
The integral of the vertical distance between an upper and a lower curve over their common interval.
Beta function
B(m,n) = ∫₀¹ x^(m−1)(1−x)^(n−1) dx, expressible through gamma functions.
Comparison test
If 0 ≤ f ≤ g and ∫ g converges then ∫ f converges; if ∫ f diverges then ∫ g diverges.
Constant of integration
The arbitrary constant C added to an indefinite integral.
Convergence
The property that the defining limit of an improper integral exists and is finite.
Definite integral
The limit of Riemann sums over an interval; a single number.
Factorial
n! = n(n−1)···1, equal to Γ(n+1) for whole n.
Fundamental theorem of calculus
The pair of results linking differentiation and integration as inverse processes.
Gamma function
Γ(n) = ∫₀^∞ x^(n−1)e^(−x) dx, the continuous extension of the factorial.
Improper integral
An integral defined as a limit because the interval is infinite or the integrand is unbounded.
Indefinite integral
The family F(x) + C of all antiderivatives of f.
Integrand
The function f(x) being integrated in ∫ f(x) dx.
Integration by parts
Rule ∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du that reverses the product rule.
Limits of integration
The numbers a and b in ∫ₐᵇ that fix the interval of integration.
Net change theorem
The statement that the integral of a derivative over an interval equals the total change of the function.
p-integral
An integral of the form ∫ x^(−p) dx used as a benchmark in convergence tests.
Partial fraction decomposition
Rewriting a rational function as a sum of simpler fractions before integrating.
Riemann sum
A finite sum Σ f(xᵢ*) Δx of rectangle areas that approximates a definite integral.
Singularity
A point where the integrand becomes unbounded, forcing a limit definition of the integral.
Solid of revolution
The three-dimensional solid obtained by rotating a plane region about an axis.
Substitution rule
Change of variable that reverses the chain rule in integration.
Surface of revolution
The surface traced by rotating a curve about an axis, whose area is an integral of thin bands.
Washer
A slice of a solid of revolution shaped like a ring, with outer radius R and inner radius r.
