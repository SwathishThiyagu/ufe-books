5.1 Double integrals as iterated integrals; Fubini's theorem

A double integral adds up a quantity over a flat region. Its definition as a limit of sums is correct but slow. Fubini's theorem says that for a continuous function on a rectangle you can add up in strips: first integrate in one variable, then in the other. Two ordinary integrals replace one two-dimensional limit. Engineers use this to find total load on a plate, average temperature over a chip, and mass from a density map. The same idea extends to curved regions by letting the inner limits depend on the outer variable.

You need: definite integrals and the fundamental theorem from Unit 2, Definite integrals, and continuity of f(x,y) from Unit 4.

The double integral as a limit of sums

Take a rectangle R = [a,b]×[c,d]. This means a ≤ x ≤ b and c ≤ y ≤ d. Cut [a,b] into m pieces, each of width Δx = (b−a)/m. Cut [c,d] into n pieces, each of height Δy = (d−c)/n. The grid makes m n small rectangles, each of area ΔA = Δx Δy.

Σ<sub>i=1</sub><sup>m</sup> Σ<sub>j=1</sub><sup>n</sup> f(x<sub>ij</sub>*, y<sub>ij</sub>*) ΔA

xij*, yij* is one sample point inside small rectangle (i,j); ΔA is that rectangle's area.

The double integral is the limit of this sum as the grid becomes fine.

∬<sub>R</sub> f(x,y) dA = lim<sub>Δx,Δy→0</sub> Σ<sub>i,j</sub> f(x<sub>ij</sub>*, y<sub>ij</sub>*) ΔA

R is the rectangle [a,b]×[c,d]; dA is the area element, and in cartesian coordinates dA = dx dy.

If f(x,y) ≥ 0, the integral equals the volume under the surface z = f(x,y) and above R. If f is a density in kg/m², the integral is total mass in kg. The definition is correct but impractical: it asks for a limit in two directions at once.

Fubini's theorem on a rectangle

Fubini's theorem turns the limit into two ordinary integrals. If f is continuous on R = [a,b]×[c,d], then

∬<sub>R</sub> f(x,y) dA = ∫<sub>c</sub><sup>d</sup> (∫<sub>a</sub><sup>b</sup> f(x,y) dx) dy = ∫<sub>a</sub><sup>b</sup> (∫<sub>c</sub><sup>d</sup> f(x,y) dy) dx

the inner integral is done first; its limits belong to the inner variable only. The outer integral uses the remaining variable.

Read the middle expression from inside out. The inner integral ∫ab f(x,y) dx freezes y, integrates with respect to x, and gives a function of y alone. The outer integral then integrates that function from c to d. The right-hand expression does the same with the roles swapped. Both orders give the same number.

The proof idea: for each fixed y, the inner integral is an ordinary definite integral, so the fundamental theorem of calculus applies. Integrating the slice areas over y adds the slices back into the whole.

Some syllabi call these repeated integrals or iterated integrals. The name does not change the rule.

  1. Sketch R and mark the order you want: dx dy means x is inner.
  2. Write the inner integral with limits for the inner variable only.
  3. Integrate inner, substitute its limits, and keep the result as a function of the outer variable.
  4. Integrate outer and substitute its limits.

On a rectangle every limit is constant. The order is free; pick the one that makes the inner integral easier.

Curved regions: type I and type II

Most real regions are not rectangles. Let R be a type I region: a ≤ x ≤ b and g1(x) ≤ y ≤ g2(x). For a fixed x, a vertical strip runs from the lower curve to the upper curve. Fubini gives

∬<sub>R</sub> f(x,y) dA = ∫<sub>a</sub><sup>b</sup> (∫<sub>g1(x)</sub><sup>g2(x)</sup> f(x,y) dy) dx

x runs from a to b; for each x, y runs from the lower curve g1(x) to the upper curve g2(x).

A type II region has c ≤ y ≤ d and h1(y) ≤ x ≤ h2(y). A horizontal strip runs from left curve to right curve.

∬<sub>R</sub> f(x,y) dA = ∫<sub>c</sub><sup>d</sup> (∫<sub>h1(y)</sub><sup>h2(y)</sup> f(x,y) dx) dy

y runs from c to d; for each y, x runs from the left curve h1(y) to the right curve h2(y).

The rule is the same: the inner limits may depend only on the outer variable, never on the inner one. If a region is both type I and type II, either order is valid. Choose the order that gives simpler limits or simpler antiderivatives. Swapping the order after drawing the region is the whole skill of 5.2.

For circular regions, cartesian limits often contain square roots. Polar coordinates simplify them; see 5.3. The same nesting gives triple integrals in 5.4, and setting f = 1 gives area in 5.5.

Worked example

Question. Evaluate ∬R (x + 2y) dA over R = [0,2]×[0,1], once with the order dy dx and once with dx dy.

  1. R is the rectangle 0 ≤ x ≤ 2, 0 ≤ y ≤ 1. The function f(x,y) = x + 2y is continuous, so Fubini applies in both orders.
  2. Order dy dx: inner integral ∫01 (x + 2y) dy = [xy + y²]01 = x(1) + 1² − 0 = x + 1.
  3. Outer integral ∫02 (x + 1) dx = [x²/2 + x]02 = (4/2 + 2) − 0 = 4.
  4. Order dx dy: inner integral ∫02 (x + 2y) dx = [x²/2 + 2xy]02 = 2 + 4y.
  5. Outer integral ∫01 (2 + 4y) dy = [2y + 2y²]01 = 2 + 2 = 4.

Answer. Both orders give ∬R (x + 2y) dA = 4.

Exam traps

  • Inner limits must belong to the inner variable. A y-limit on an x-integral is wrong even if the numbers look familiar.
  • After the inner integral, do not substitute the outer limits. The result still contains the outer variable, such as x + 1 in the example.
  • Constant limits in both directions describe a rectangle only. A triangle or a circle needs a variable limit in at least one integral.
  • Read the differentials from the inside out: ∬ f dy dx has dy inner, while ∬ f dx dy has dx inner. Exchanging them without redrawing the region changes the problem.
  • Fubini's safe condition is continuity on the region. For a discontinuous integrand, check convergence before swapping orders.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Numerical software rarely computes a double integral by a grid sum; it uses adaptive quadrature or Monte Carlo sampling, and Fubini justifies integrating one variable at a time inside those routines. Finite element codes build stiffness matrices by integrating over triangles and quadrilaterals, exactly the type I and type II bookkeeping above. In probability, the expectation of a function of two random variables is a double integral, and Fubini lets you take expectations in either order. Measure theory extends the theorem to much wilder functions under the names Fubini and Tonelli. 5.2 Change of order of integration

Changing the order of integration means computing the same double integral with dx and dy swapped. For a rectangle either order works by Fubini. For a region with curved or slanted edges, one order can produce an impossible inner integral; the other can be easy. You redraw the region, rewrite its inequalities with the new outer variable, and integrate. This appears in probability densities, heat flow and centre-of-mass problems. Only a few universities teach this as a separate exam skill.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for iterated integrals and reading limits from a region.

Read the region first

For an iterated integral ∫ (x=a to b) ∫ (y=g₁(x) to g₂(x)) f(x,y) dy dx, the inner limits y=g₁(x) and y=g₂(x) are curves. The outer limits x=a and x=b are constants. The region R is every point (x,y) with a≤x≤b and g₁(x)≤y≤g₂(x). This is a type I region: y is bounded by functions of x. A type II region uses c≤y≤d and h₁(y)≤x≤h₂(y): x is bounded by functions of y. The same R can have both descriptions. To change order, do not swap the four numbers. Sketch R, write both descriptions from the sketch, then put the new inner limits in terms of the new outer variable. The inner limits may contain the outer variable; the outer limits must be constants. Draw axes, mark the boundary curves, and shade R before writing any integral. A useful check: pick one point inside R and confirm it satisfies both descriptions.

Four steps and a warning

  1. Sketch R from the given limits, labelling where each boundary curve meets the axes.
  2. Choose the new outer variable: y for the order dx dy, or x for dy dx.
  3. Read the outer limits as the smallest and largest values of that variable inside R; they must be constants.
  4. For a fixed outer value, read the inner limits as the left and right (or bottom and top) edges of R; they may contain the outer variable.

Changing order is useful when the original inner integral has no elementary antiderivative. In the example, e^(y²) cannot be integrated in y, but after switching to dx the integrand gains a factor y and becomes y e^(y²).

A wrong sketch gives wrong bounds even if every step looks neat. The limits x=0 to 1 and y=x to 1 describe a triangle, not a square. The square 0≤x≤1, 0≤y≤1 is a different region. If a new outer limit contains x or y, stop; it is not an outer limit.

Worked example

Question. Evaluate I = ∫₀¹ ∫ₓ¹ e^(y²) dy dx by changing the order of integration.

  1. The inner integral ∫ e^(y²) dy has no elementary antiderivative, so change the order.
  2. The limits give 0≤x≤1 and x≤y≤1. The region R is the triangle with vertices (0,0), (0,1), (1,1).
  3. For a fixed y, x runs from 0 to y. The smallest and largest y values are 0 and 1. So R is also 0≤y≤1, 0≤x≤y.
  4. I = ∫₀¹ ∫₀ʸ e^(y²) dx dy = ∫₀¹ [x e^(y²)] from x=0 to x=y dy = ∫₀¹ y e^(y²) dy.
  5. Put u=y², so du=2y dy. The limits y=0 to 1 give u=0 to 1. Hence I = ½∫₀¹ e^u du = (e−1)/2 ≈ 0.8591409142295225, where e≈2.71828 is Euler's number.

Answer. The integral equals (e−1)/2, approximately 0.8591409142295225.

Exam traps

  • Swapping dx dy without redrawing: the new inner limits can depend on the new outer variable.
  • Using 0 to 1 for x inside the y-integral on the triangle 0≤x≤y≤1; that describes the unit square.
  • Leaving a variable in an outer limit. Outer limits must be constants.
  • Changing the order and stopping; many exam questions say 'change the order and hence evaluate'.

5.3 Change of variables: polar, cylindrical and spherical coordinates

Changing coordinates turns a hard double or triple integral into an easy one when the region is a disk, a cylinder or a sphere. The price is a local area or volume factor called the Jacobian. In polar coordinates the factor is r, in cylindrical coordinates it is also r, and in spherical coordinates it is ρ² sinφ. The method sets the new limits and puts the factor in the right place, so integrals over round regions become ordinary one-variable integrals.

You need double integrals as iterated integrals and the Jacobian from Unit 4.

Polar coordinates

Use polar coordinates when the region is a disk, a sector or a ring around the origin. Put x = r cosθ and y = r sinθ. Here r is the distance from the origin, r ≥ 0, and θ is the angle from the positive x-axis, usually 0 ≤ θ < 2π. A small polar rectangle has side dr in the radial direction and r dθ along the arc. Its area is not dr dθ; it is r dr dθ. The Jacobian gives the same result.

x = r cosθ, y = r sinθ, dA = r dr dθ

r is the distance from the origin; θ is the angle from the positive x-axis; dA is the area element in the xy-plane.

The Jacobian is J = ∂(x,y)/∂(r,θ) = r. Therefore ∬_D f(x,y) dA = ∬_S f(r cosθ, r sinθ) r dr dθ. Set the r-limits from the nearest and farthest points of the region, and the θ-limits from the angular spread. For the disk x²+y² ≤ 4, 0 ≤ r ≤ 2 and 0 ≤ θ ≤ 2π. The factor r must stay inside the integral; dropping it is the most common error. For changing order in Cartesian integrals, see 5.2.

Cylindrical coordinates

Cylindrical coordinates are polar coordinates with the height z kept unchanged. Write x = r cosθ, y = r sinθ, z = z. The angle θ is measured in the xy-plane from the positive x-axis, r is the distance from the z-axis, and z is the usual height. A small volume element is a polar rectangle of area r dr dθ pushed up by height dz, so dV = r dz dr dθ. The Jacobian is again r.

x = r cosθ, y = r sinθ, z = z, dV = r dz dr dθ

r is distance from the z-axis; θ is the angle in the xy-plane; z is height; dV is the volume element.

Use these coordinates for solids bounded by cylinders, cones and planes, such as a water tank, a circular pipe or a cone with a flat top. The z-limits come from the bottom and top surfaces, written as functions of r and θ. The order of dz, dr and dθ may be changed to suit the limits, but the factor r must remain. A triple integral in these coordinates is evaluated as three nested ordinary integrals; see 5.4. Keep the z-limits independent of z itself.

Spherical coordinates

Use spherical coordinates when the solid is a ball, a spherical shell, or a cone meeting a sphere. Let ρ be the distance from the origin, ρ ≥ 0. Let θ be the same angle as in polar coordinates, measured in the xy-plane. Let φ be the angle from the positive z-axis, with 0 ≤ φ ≤ π. Then x = ρ sinφ cosθ, y = ρ sinφ sinθ, z = ρ cosφ. The volume element is dV = ρ² sinφ dρ dφ dθ.

x = ρ sinφ cosθ, y = ρ sinφ sinθ, z = ρ cosφ, dV = ρ² sinφ dρ dφ dθ

ρ is distance from the origin; θ is angle in the xy-plane; φ is angle from the positive z-axis; dV is the volume element.

The Jacobian is ρ² sinφ. It is not ρ²; the sinφ is part of the geometry and cannot be omitted. For a ball of radius R centred at the origin, 0 ≤ ρ ≤ R, 0 ≤ φ ≤ π and 0 ≤ θ ≤ 2π. A cone z = c r has constant φ, so φ-limits are often simple. A sphere x²+y²+z² = R² becomes ρ = R. When the integrand depends only on ρ, the θ and φ integrals separate and give 4π, the surface area of a unit sphere.

Worked example

Question. Evaluate the double integral of e^(−(x²+y²)) over the disk x²+y² ≤ 4.

  1. The disk has radius 2, so in polar coordinates 0 ≤ r ≤ 2 and 0 ≤ θ ≤ 2π.
  2. Substitute x = r cosθ, y = r sinθ. Then x²+y² = r² and dA = r dr dθ.
  3. The integral becomes ∫ from θ=0 to 2π ∫ from r=0 to 2 e^(−r²) r dr dθ.
  4. For the inner integral, let u = r², so du = 2r dr and r dr = du/2. The limits r=0 to 2 become u=0 to 4.
  5. ∫ from r=0 to 2 of e^(−r²) r dr = (1/2)∫ from u=0 to 4 of e^(−u) du = (1/2)(1 − e^(−4)).
  6. The outer integral gives a factor 2π: 2π · (1/2)(1−e^(−4)) = π(1−e^(−4)).
  7. Numerically, e^(−4) = 0.0183156, so π(1−e^(−4)) = 3.0841.

Answer. The value is π(1−e^(−4)) ≈ 3.0841 square units.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the Jacobian: dA is r dr dθ, not dr dθ.
  • Using r outside its range: r is a distance, so r ≥ 0; θ is an angle, not a length.
  • In spherical coordinates writing dV = ρ² dρ dφ dθ and dropping sinφ.
  • Mixing angle names: in spherical coordinates φ is measured from the positive z-axis, while θ is measured in the xy-plane. Swapping them gives wrong limits.

5.4 Triple integrals

A triple integral adds up a function of three variables over a solid in space, and it is computed as three nested ordinary integrals. It exists because many quantities — mass of a block, charge inside a battery, heat in a room — pile up over thickness as well as over length and width. Limits are read from the inequalities that describe the solid, one variable at a time, from the innermost of Chapter 5.1 outward. Engineers use it for volume, centre of mass and flow totals.

You need: double integrals as iterated integrals and Fubini's theorem from Chapter 5.1, and the region-reading habits from Chapter 5.2.

From a box to a solid

A box B is the set of points (x, y, z) with a ≤ x ≤ b, c ≤ y ≤ d and p ≤ z ≤ q. All six limits are constants. The triple integral of f over B is written ∭B f(x, y, z) dV, where dV is a small volume element and f is the density-like quantity being added up.

Fubini's theorem for three variables says the order does not matter for a box. You may integrate x first, then y, then z, or any of the six orders, and get the same number. For a box this is written with all limits constant:

∭<sub>B</sub> f dV = ∫<sub>p</sub><sup>q</sup> ∫<sub>c</sub><sup>d</sup> ∫<sub>a</sub><sup>b</sup> f(x, y, z) dx dy dz

a, b are the x limits; c, d the y limits; p, q the z limits; f is the integrand; dx, dy, dz are the three ordinary integrations, done from inner to outer.

To evaluate, work from the inside out. The inner integral treats y and z as constants and produces a function of y and z. The middle integral kills y. The outer integral leaves a plain number.

For a solid that is not a box — a slab, a cylinder or a pyramid, for example — the outer limits stay constant but the inner ones become functions of the variables outside them. Suppose a solid S sits above a region R in the xy-plane and between the surfaces z = g₁(x, y) and z = g₂(x, y). Then dV = dz dy dx with z running from g₁ to g₂.

∭<sub>S</sub> f dV = ∬<sub>R</sub> [ ∫<sub>g₁(x,y)</sub><sup>g₂(x,y)</sup> f(x, y, z) dz ] dA

R is the shadow of the solid on the xy-plane; g₁ and g₂ are the lower and upper surfaces; dA is the area element dx dy; the inner integral is done first for fixed (x, y).

Reading limits is the whole skill. Sketch the solid. Ask which variable has the simplest constant range — usually z from floor to roof. That variable goes outermost. Then project the shadow and read the double integral limits exactly as in Chapter 5.1.

Choosing an order and using volume

  1. Write the inequalities that define the solid: for each variable, what is it between?
  2. Pick the variable whose limits are plain constants; make it the outer integral.
  3. For the next variable, fix the outer one and find its range inside the solid.
  4. For the innermost variable, fix the other two and read its range as a function of them.
  5. Integrate inside out, simplifying after each stage.
  6. Check the answer's sign and rough size: integrating 1 over the solid must give its volume.
  • The integrand must not depend on the innermost variable after that integration is done.
  • A dependent limit may contain only variables that are still free at that stage.
  • If an inner integral looks impossible, try swapping the order — the same trick as Chapter 5.2, one dimension up.
  • Setting f = 1 gives the volume of the solid; that is the topic of Chapter 5.5.
  • Cylindrical and spherical versions with a Jacobian live in Chapter 5.3.

A common use in Indian engineering courses is mass from density. If a metal block has density ρ(x, y, z) kilograms per cubic metre, then the mass is ∭ ρ dV, and its units come out in kilograms because dV carries m³.

Worked example

Question. Evaluate ∭B (x + y + z) dV where B is the unit cube 0 ≤ x ≤ 1, 0 ≤ y ≤ 1, 0 ≤ z ≤ 1.

Python program.

from fractions import Fraction

# after integrating x and y: 1 + z
# outer antiderivative: ∫ (1 + z) dz = z + z²/2
def outer_antiderivative(z):
    return z + z * z / 2

value = outer_antiderivative(Fraction(1)) - outer_antiderivative(Fraction(0))
print(value)
print(float(value))
  1. The solid is a box, so all six limits are constants and any order works. Take the order dx dy dz, with x innermost, y middle, z outer.
  2. Inner integral in x, with y and z held constant: ∫₀¹ (x + y + z) dx = [x²/2 + xy + xz] from 0 to 1 = 1/2 + y + z.
  3. Middle integral in y: ∫₀¹ (1/2 + y + z) dy = [y/2 + y²/2 + zy] from 0 to 1 = 1/2 + 1/2 + z = 1 + z.
  4. Outer integral in z: ∫₀¹ (1 + z) dz = [z + z²/2] from 0 to 1 = 1 + 1/2 = 3/2.
  5. Check by symmetry: each of the three single-variable pieces contributes ∫₀¹ x dx = 1/2, and there are three of them, giving 3/2. The answer is positive, as x + y + z ≥ 0 on the cube.

Output

3/2
1.5

Answer. The triple integral equals 3/2, a pure number with no units.

Exam traps

  • Using constant limits for a non-box solid. The outermost limits must be constants; inner limits may be constants or may depend only on variables outside them.
  • Putting a dependent limit on the wrong variable, for example writing z from 0 to x² when the solid is bounded by x = √z.
  • Forgetting that after the inner integration the variable is gone, so the next limit must not mention it.
  • Dropping the units: a density in kg/m³ times dV in m³ gives kg, but a bare integral of 1 gives a volume in m³, not a number.
  • Assuming order does not matter for a non-box solid. The value is the same, but the limits must be re-read for each new order.

Check yourself

Remember State Fubini's theorem for a triple integral over a box. 2 marks

For f continuous on the box a ≤ x ≤ b, c ≤ y ≤ d, p ≤ z ≤ q, the triple integral equals the three nested integrals ∫ₚq∫cd∫ab f dx dy dz, and the order of the three integrations may be changed freely.

Understand Why does the innermost integral's limit sometimes contain other variables while the outermost limit does not? 3 marks

Working from inside out, at the innermost stage y and z are fixed, so the range of x can depend on them. By the time we reach the outermost integral, all other variables have been integrated away, so its limits must be plain constants describing the full extent of the solid.

Apply Evaluate ∭S z dV where S is 0 ≤ x ≤ 1, 0 ≤ y ≤ 1, 0 ≤ z ≤ x. 4 marks

Inner in z: ∫₀ˣ z dz = x²/2. Middle in y, constant integrand: x²/2 times (1 − 0) = x²/2. Outer in x: ∫₀¹ x²/2 dx = 1/6. Final answer 1/6.

Apply Find the volume of the solid 0 ≤ x ≤ 2, 0 ≤ y ≤ 1, 0 ≤ z ≤ x + y by a triple integral of 1. 4 marks

Inner in z: x + y. Middle in y: xy + y²/2 from 0 to 1 gives x + 1/2. Outer in x: x²/2 + x/2 from 0 to 2 gives 2 + 1 = 3. Volume = 3 cubic units.

Analyze A student writes ∭S f dV = ∫₀¹∫₀¹∫₀¹ f dx dy dz for the solid bounded by z = x² + y² and z = 1. What is wrong, and what is the correct inner limit? 5 marks

The solid is not a box. In the student's order z is outermost, and z from 0 to 1 is the correct overall range. The error is the constant x and y limits from 0 to 1: at fixed z the cross-section is x²+y² ≤ z, not the unit square. Equivalently, change order and put z inner from x²+y² to 1 over the unit disc x²+y² ≤ 1: ∭ f dV = ∬disc ∫x²+y²1 f dz dA.

Analyze Without computing, argue that ∭B (x + y + z) dV over the unit cube is the same in all six orders of integration. 4 marks

All six limits are constants, so the box is a product of intervals. Fubini's theorem applies to the continuous integrand x + y + z on that box and allows any order. Symmetry confirms it: the sum separates into three identical single integrals each equal to 1/2.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Triple integrals become the volume integrals behind Gauss's divergence theorem in Unit 6, where a flux through a closed surface is traded for an integral over the enclosed solid. In engineering practice the same nesting appears in finite element software, which chops a part into small tetrahedra and sums a simple rule over each. In machine learning, Monte Carlo integration replaces the inner sums by random samples when the dimension climbs past four or five. Search for 'Gauss divergence theorem', 'Gaussian quadrature on tetrahedra' or 'Monte Carlo integration' to go further.

5.5 Area and volume using multiple integrals

A double integral of 1 over a region gives its area. A double integral of a height gives the volume under that surface. A triple integral of 1 gives the volume of a solid. Draw the region, write the limits, integrate. Engineers size curved roofs, domed tanks and curved panels this way. Only a few universities teach this as a topic on its own.

You need: double integrals as iterated integrals and polar coordinates with the Jacobian.

Area is the integral of 1

Cover a flat region D with tiny rectangles of area ΔA. Adding the pieces approximates the area; shrinking them turns the sum into a double integral. The integrand is 1, because each piece contributes only its own area.

Area(D) = ∬_D 1 dA = ∬_D dA

D is the region; dA is the area element, dx dy in Cartesian or r dr dθ in polar; the answer is in square units.

In polar coordinates dA = r dr dθ, and that extra r must never be dropped. For the unit disk r ≤ 1:

∫₀²π ∫₀¹ r dr dθ = 2π × ½ = π

θ runs 0 to 2π for one full turn; r runs 0 to 1; the area is π ≈ 3.1416 square units.

Volume under a surface and volume of a solid

Take a surface z = f(x,y) above D, with f ≥ 0 everywhere on D. A tiny base dA carries a thin column of height f(x,y), so the column volume is f(x,y) dA. Adding all the columns fills the space under the surface.

V = ∬_D f(x,y) dA

f(x,y) is the height above the point (x,y); D is the base region; V is in cubic units.

A solid E in space is measured the same way: integrand 1, volume element dV.

Volume(E) = ∭_E 1 dV

dV is dx dy dz, or r dz dr dθ in cylindrical coordinates, or ρ² sin φ dρ dφ dθ in spherical coordinates.

  1. Sketch D and mark its boundary.
  2. Pick coordinates: Cartesian for straight edges, polar for a circle centred at the origin.
  3. Set the inner limits first; they may depend on the outer variable.
  4. Integrate the inner variable, then the outer one.
  5. State units: square for area, cubic for volume.

Circular bases in polar

Polar coordinates make circular bases easy, because a circle centred at the origin becomes the simple bounds 0 ≤ r ≤ a, 0 ≤ θ ≤ 2π. Put x² + y² = r², replace dA by r dr dθ, and read the r limits off the boundary curve.

V = ∫_θ₁^θ₂ ∫_r₁^r₂ f(r cos θ, r sin θ) r dr dθ

r is the distance from the origin; θ is the angle in radians; the inner limits may depend on θ.

When the height depends on r alone, the θ integral simply multiplies the answer by the angle swept. Domes, tank ends and round silo roofs are the standard cases.

Worked example

Question. Find the volume under the surface z = 4 − x² − y² over the disk D: x² + y² ≤ 1. Give the exact value and a decimal.

  1. The height is positive on D: z = 4 at the centre, z = 3 at the rim. So V = ∬_D (4 − x² − y²) dA.
  2. Switch to polar: x² + y² = r², dA = r dr dθ, limits 0 ≤ r ≤ 1 and 0 ≤ θ ≤ 2π.
  3. V = ∫₀²π ∫₀¹ (4 − r²) r dr dθ = ∫₀²π ∫₀¹ (4r − r³) dr dθ.
  4. Inner integral: [2r² − r⁴/4] from r = 0 to r = 1 gives 2 − 0.25 = 1.75.
  5. Outer integral: 1.75 × 2π = 3.5π = 7π/2.
  6. Decimal value: 7π/2 ≈ 10.996 cubic units.

Answer. The volume under z = 4 − x² − y² over the unit disk is 7π/2 ≈ 10.996 cubic units.

Exam traps

  • Area uses integrand 1. Writing ∬_D (4 − x² − y²) dA gives a volume, not an area.
  • In polar, dropping the r from dA = r dr dθ gives a wrong value and wrong dimensions.
  • The formula ∬_D f dA is a volume only when f ≥ 0 on D; below the plane the integral counts negative volume.
  • θ from 0 to 2π belongs to a full circle centred at the origin only. A half disk or shifted disk needs its own θ limits.
  • Units: area in square units, volume in cubic units. A bare 7π/2 means nothing until you add cubic units.

Check yourself

Remember Write the double integral that gives the area of a plane region D. 2 marks

Area(D) = ∬_D 1 dA.

Remember What is dA in polar coordinates? 2 marks

dA = r dr dθ. The factor r is the Jacobian of the polar map.

Understand Why does ∬_D 1 dA equal the area of D? 3 marks

Each element dA is a small piece of area. The integral adds all the pieces without weighting them, so the total is the area of D.

Apply Use a double integral in polar form to find the area inside r = 3. 4 marks

∫₀²π ∫₀³ r dr dθ. Inner = 9/2, outer = 2π. Area = 9π ≈ 28.274 square units.

Apply Find the volume under the constant height z = 5 over the disk x² + y² ≤ 2. 5 marks

V = ∫₀²π ∫₀^√2 5 r dr dθ. Inner = 5 × (2/2) = 5, outer multiplies by 2π, so V = 10π ≈ 31.416 cubic units; this equals 5 × area of the disk = 5 × 2π.

Analyze A student evaluates ∬_D (4 − x² − y²) dA over the unit disk and calls the result the area of the disk. What did the student actually compute, and what is the correct area? 5 marks

The student computed the volume 7π/2 ≈ 10.996 cubic units. The area needs integrand 1: ∬_D 1 dA = π ≈ 3.1416 square units. The student used a height where an area was wanted.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Real designs are never integrated by hand: CAD software meshes a curved shell into small pieces and adds their volumes, which is this Riemann sum at scale. Finite element codes do the same for a car door or a turbine blade. To find the area of a curved skin instead of the volume beneath it, you need a surface integral, taken up in Unit 6. Search 'volume by double integration in polar form' or 'centroid by double integral' to go further.

Takeaways

  1. A double integral is just a sum of small pieces f(x,y) ΔA in the limit, so every rule is bookkeeping on pieces.
  2. Fubini turns one 2-D problem into two 1-D problems, and for a rectangle you may pick either order freely.
  3. For a non-rectangular region, the limits are read off a sketch: outer limits are numbers, inner limits may depend on the outer variable.
  4. Changing the order means redrawing the same region with strips running the other way; the value never changes, only the work does.
  5. A change of coordinates multiplies the integrand by the absolute value of the Jacobian; forgetting |J| is the most common error.
  6. Polar, cylindrical and spherical coordinates exist to make circular, cylindrical and spherical regions simple, and their Jacobians are r, r and ρ² sin φ.
  7. Triple integrals are set up outside-in: project the solid on a plane, then let the inner limits run from the lower surface to the upper surface.
  8. Area is the double integral of 1 and volume is the triple integral of 1; both are special cases of the same idea.

Glossary

Centre of mass
The point where the whole mass may be treated as concentrated; found with first moments.
Change of variables
Rewriting an integral in new coordinates, multiplying by the absolute Jacobian.
Cylindrical coordinates
Polar coordinates in the xy-plane plus the usual z height.
dA
The infinitesimal element of area in a double integral.
Density
Mass per unit area for a lamina, or per unit volume for a solid.
Double integral
The limit of a sum of f values times small areas over a plane region.
dV
The infinitesimal element of volume in a triple integral.
Fubini's theorem
A continuous function on a rectangle can be integrated in either order with the same result.
Improper integral
An integral over an unbounded region or with an unbounded integrand, defined by a limit.
Iterated integral
Two single integrals nested, evaluated from the inside out.
Jacobian
The determinant of the matrix of first partial derivatives of a coordinate map.
Lamina
A thin flat plate treated as a plane region with a density function.
Mesh size
The largest cell dimension in a partition; the double integral is the limit as it goes to zero.
Moment of inertia
The integral of density times squared distance from an axis.
Order of integration
The sequence in which the nested single integrals are performed.
Polar coordinates
Plane coordinates (r, θ) with x = r cos θ and y = r sin θ.
Projection
The shadow a solid casts on a coordinate plane, used to fix outer limits.
Region of integration
The plane region or solid over which a multiple integral is taken.
Riemann sum
A finite sum of function values times cell sizes that approaches the integral.
Spherical coordinates
Coordinates (ρ, θ, φ) with ρ the distance from the origin and φ the angle from the z-axis.
Type I region
A region described by vertical strips, with x limits constant.
Type II region
A region described by horizontal strips, with y limits constant.
