3.1 Ordinary points and regular singular points

A second-order linear ODE can look harmless until a power series is tried and the coefficients misbehave at the chosen point. This chapter sorts each point into ordinary, regular singular, or irregular singular before any series is written. The label picks the method: plain Taylor series at an ordinary point, Frobenius series at a regular singular point, and no simple series at an irregular one. The same labels appear in heat flow, vibrating drums, and electric fields, where Bessel and Legendre equations put singular points at the edge of the useful interval.

You need Unit 2, Higher Order Linear ODEs and basic Taylor series from calculus.

Standard form and the three labels

Every second-order linear homogeneous ODE can be written in standard form. Here y is the unknown function of x; y′ and y″ are its first and second derivatives; P(x) and Q(x) are known coefficient functions.

y″ + P(x)y′ + Q(x)y = 0

P(x) is the coefficient of y′ after the y″ coefficient is made 1; Q(x) is the coefficient of y; both are functions of x only.

A point x₀ is ordinary when P and Q are analytic at x₀. Analytic means the function has a convergent Taylor series in powers of (x − x₀) around x₀. If either coefficient fails this test, x₀ is singular. Singular is not a verdict of uselessness. It says the plain power series y = Σ aₙ(x − x₀)ⁿ may fail, so the type of blow-up must be inspected.

  • Ordinary point: P and Q analytic at x₀.
  • Singular point: not ordinary.
  • Regular singular point: a singular point whose blow-up is mild, defined in the next section.
  • Irregular singular point: singular but not regular.
  • Some syllabi say regular point for ordinary point, and regular singularity for regular singular point.

At an ordinary point, a plain Taylor series is the natural trial; see 3.2 Power series solutions about an ordinary point.

The regular singular test

At a singular point x₀, multiply P by one power of (x − x₀) and Q by two powers. If both products have finite limits as x → x₀, the point is regular singular. Equivalently, both products are analytic at x₀. The limits get their own symbols because they drive the indicial equation in 3.3 Frobenius method.

p₀ = lim (x→x₀) (x − x₀)P(x),   q₀ = lim (x→x₀) (x − x₀)²Q(x)

p₀ and q₀ are finite numbers when x₀ is a regular singular point; P and Q are the standard-form coefficients; the limit is taken along x ≠ x₀.

The powers are not arbitrary. They allow P to blow up no faster than 1/(x − x₀) and Q no faster than 1/(x − x₀)². A stronger blow-up makes the point irregular singular. A finite limit is enough; the function need not be defined exactly at x₀, because the limit ignores that one point.

Use this order every time: write standard form, test analyticity, then test the two limits only if the point is singular.

Quick checks: Bessel and Legendre

Bessel's equation of order ν is given below. Divide by x²: P(x) = 1/x and Q(x) = (x² − ν²)/x². At x = 0 both blow up, so x = 0 is singular. Then xP = 1 and x²Q = x² − ν², giving finite limits p₀ = 1 and q₀ = −ν². Hence x = 0 is a regular singular point. The solution is built in 3.5 Bessel functions.

x²y″ + xy′ + (x² − ν²)y = 0

ν is the order, a fixed real number; y″ and y′ are derivatives with respect to x.

Legendre's equation is (1 − x²)y″ − 2xy′ + n(n+1)y = 0. Standard form gives P(x) = −2x/(1 − x²) and Q(x) = n(n+1)/(1 − x²). At x = 1, both blow up. Compute (x − 1)P = 2x/(x + 1) and (x − 1)²Q = −n(n+1)(x − 1)/(x + 1). The limits are p₀ = 1 and q₀ = 0, so x = 1 is regular singular. The same check at x = −1 gives p₀ = 1 and q₀ = 0, so x = −1 is also regular singular. The polynomial solutions are in 3.4 Legendre polynomials.

Worked example

Question. Classify x = 0 and x = 1 for (x² − 1)y″ + xy′ + y = 0. Also state what happens at x = −1.

  1. Write standard form: divide by x² − 1 = (x − 1)(x + 1). P(x) = x/(x² − 1), Q(x) = 1/(x² − 1).
  2. At x = 0, x² − 1 = −1 ≠ 0. P(0) = 0 and Q(0) = −1, and both are analytic near 0. So x = 0 is an ordinary point.
  3. At x = 1 the denominator is zero, so the point is singular.
  4. (x − 1)P(x) = (x − 1)x/[(x − 1)(x + 1)] = x/(x + 1), which is analytic at x = 1. Its limit is p₀ = 1/2.
  5. (x − 1)²Q(x) = (x − 1)²/[(x − 1)(x + 1)] = (x − 1)/(x + 1), which is analytic at x = 1. Its limit is q₀ = 0. So x = 1 is a regular singular point.
  6. At x = −1, (x + 1)P(x) = x/(x − 1) and (x + 1)²Q(x) = (x + 1)/(x − 1). Both are finite at x = −1, with values 1/2 and 0. So x = −1 is also regular singular.

Answer. x = 0 is an ordinary point; x = 1 and x = −1 are regular singular points.

Exam traps

  • Calling x = 1 irregular only because P and Q blow up. Regularity asks whether (x − x₀)P and (x − x₀)²Q stay finite.
  • Testing the original y″ coefficient instead of first dividing to standard form. P and Q are read only after the y″ coefficient is 1.
  • Swapping the factors: P gets one power of (x − x₀), Q gets two. The reverse test gives wrong labels.
  • Forgetting that a finite limit is enough at a singular point. The coefficient need not be defined exactly at x₀; the limit can remove a removable blow-up.
  • Treating ordinary and regular as the same word. Ordinary means P and Q are analytic; regular describes a mild singular point.

3.2 Power series solutions about an ordinary point

At an ordinary point x₀, the coefficient functions of a second-order linear ODE are smooth enough for a power series. Write y = Σ aₙ(x−x₀)ⁿ, substitute, and the ODE turns into a recurrence for the coefficients aₙ. Two initial numbers, y(x₀) and y′(x₀), fix every coefficient. This method solves equations with variable coefficients that have no elementary closed form, such as y″ − xy = 0. It is the first step before Legendre and Bessel functions.

You need: 3.1 ordinary points and regular singular points, Unit 2 second-order linear ODEs, and Taylor series.

The ordinary-point setting

Write the second-order linear ODE as P(x)y″ + Q(x)y′ + R(x)y = 0. A point x₀ is ordinary when P(x₀) ≠ 0. Divide by P: y″ + p(x)y′ + q(x)y = 0, where p and q have Taylor series about x₀. Then a solution can be written as a power series about x₀. The symbol aₙ is the coefficient of (x−x₀)ⁿ; a₀ = y(x₀) and a₁ = y′(x₀). Some syllabi call this the Taylor series method. The convergence guarantee is geometric: the series converges at least up to the nearest singular point of p or q in the complex plane. For y″ − xy = 0 every point is ordinary and the radius is infinite. The method builds one coefficient at a time. It works because p and q are analytic at x₀.

y = Σ<sub>n=0</sub><sup>∞</sup> a<sub>n</sub>(x − x<sub>0</sub>)<sup>n</sup>

an is the coefficient of (x − x0)n; x0 is the ordinary point; a0 = y(x0) and a1 = y′(x0).

Substitution and index shift

  1. Assume y = Σ an(x−x0)n and differentiate term by term: y′ = Σn=1∞ n an(x−x0)n−1, y″ = Σn=2∞ n(n−1)an(x−x0)n−2.
  2. Put these series into the ODE. Multiply out terms such as (x−x0)Σ an(x−x0)n.
  3. Shift indices until every series uses the same power (x−x0)m. Do not compare xm with xm−1.
  4. Set the coefficient of each (x−x0)m to zero. The constant term gives one equation; the general m gives the recurrence.
  5. Solve the recurrence from a0 and a1 upward. Initial values fix a0 and a1; all later coefficients follow.

After substitution, two series may carry powers xᵐ and xᵐ⁻¹. Shift the index in one of them until every term carries the same power (x−x₀)ᵐ. Only then compare coefficients. The coefficient of each power must be zero because a power series that is zero on an interval has all coefficients zero. The lowest power usually fixes a₂; the general power gives a recurrence that starts from a₀ and a₁. In y″ + y = 0 the recurrence is aₘ₊₂ = −aₘ/((m+2)(m+1)), so coefficients two steps apart are coupled. In y″ − xy = 0 the extra x shifts the index once more, so the coupling is three steps apart. This is why the shift must be done before solving.

a<sub>m+2</sub> = −a<sub>m</sub> / ((m+2)(m+1))

for y″ + y = 0; m = 0, 1, 2, …; a0 and a1 are free constants.

Reading the series and its radius

A second-order equation needs two independent solutions. The series with a₀=1, a₁=0 is one; the series with a₀=0, a₁=1 is the other. For y″ − xy=0 these begin y₁=1+x³/6+x⁶/180+⋯ and y₂=x+x⁴/12+⋯. Only a₀ and a₁ are free. a₂ is not free: the lowest power in y″ is x⁰ and it fixes a₂. A recurrence gives coefficients one by one; write the first four or five values and check the pattern covers all n. The two free constants match the two initial conditions. The radius guarantee uses the complex plane. A coefficient can look smooth on the real line but have a singular point at x₀±i. Example: y″ + y/(1+x²)=0 has singular points at ±i, so a series about 0 converges for |x|<1. This matters near the edge of the interval.

Worked example

Question. Solve y″ − xy = 0 about x = 0 with y(0) = 1 and y′(0) = 0, up to the term x⁶.

  1. Check x₀ = 0. The coefficient of y″ is 1, so P(0) = 1 ≠ 0 and x = 0 is an ordinary point. Assume y = Σn=0∞ anxn.
  2. Apply the initial conditions: a0 = y(0) = 1 and a1 = y′(0) = 0.
  3. Differentiate the series: y″ = Σn=2∞ n(n−1)anxn−2.
  4. Substitute into y″ − xy = 0: Σn=2∞ n(n−1)anxn−2 − xΣn=0∞ anxn = 0.
  5. Shift the first series by m = n−2. Shift the second by m = n+1. The equation becomes 2a2 + Σm=1∞ [(m+2)(m+1)am+2 − am−1]xm = 0.
  6. Equate coefficients. The constant term gives 2a2 = 0, so a2 = 0. For m ≥ 1, (m+2)(m+1)am+2 = am−1, or an = an−3/(n(n−1)) for n ≥ 3.
  7. Compute the coefficients using a0 = 1 and a1 = 0: a3 = a0/(3·2) = 1/6; a4 = a1/(4·3) = 0; a5 = a2/(5·4) = 0; a6 = a3/(6·5) = (1/6)/30 = 1/180.
  8. Assemble the series up to x⁶: y = 1 + x³/6 + x⁶/180 + ⋯. The terms x¹, x², x⁴ and x⁵ have zero coefficients.

Answer. The solution is y = 1 + x³/6 + x⁶/180 + ⋯ up to the term x⁶.

Exam traps

  • Do not compare a term with xm to a term with xm−1; shift every series to the same power first.
  • a2 is not free. The lowest power in y″ fixes it; only a0 and a1 come from y(x0) and y′(x0).
  • The radius guarantee uses the complex plane. A real-looking coefficient can have singular points at ±i.
  • When differentiating y = Σ anxn, the first series in y″ starts at n=2, not n=0; starting wrongly changes a2.

3.3 Frobenius method

At a regular singular point x0, a plain Taylor series can fail because the coefficients of y′ and y blow up. The Frobenius method multiplies the series by (x − x0)^r. The exponent r is not assumed integer and is found first from the indicial equation. Then a recurrence gives the coefficients. This solves Bessel and Legendre equations near a singular boundary, including vibration of a circular membrane at its centre. Bessel's equation is solved this way in 3.5.

You need 3.1 for regular singular classification and 3.2 for shifting indices in series products.

From singular point to indicial equation

At a regular singular point x0, write the ODE as y″ + P(x)y′ + Q(x)y = 0. A singular point is regular when (x − x0)P(x) and (x − x0)²Q(x) are analytic at x0. For the rational coefficients in most B.Tech examples, this reduces to finite limits after removing the pole. Define p0 = limx→x0 (x − x0)P(x) and q0 = limx→x0 (x − x0)²Q(x). For x0 = 0 these are p0 = lim xP and q0 = lim x²Q.

A Taylor series can fail because P and Q are not analytic there. Frobenius inserts an unknown power: y = (x − x0)^r Σ an(x − x0)^n = Σ an(x − x0)n+r, with a0 ≠ 0. The number r need not be an integer.

Multiply the ODE by (x − x0)² before substituting. Then the lowest power is (x − x0)^r, and its coefficient is a0[r(r − 1) + p0 r + q0]. In the original standard form the corresponding power is (x − x0)^(r−2). Setting the coefficient to zero gives the indicial equation. Its roots r1, r2 are the indicial roots. They fix the form of the solution before any coefficient is computed. Putting r = 0 recovers the ordinary power-series case.

F(r) = r(r − 1) + p<sub>0</sub> r + q<sub>0</sub> = 0

r is the unknown exponent; p0 and q0 are the limits defined above; F(r) = 0 is the indicial equation.

The three root cases

Let r1 ≥ r2 be real roots of the indicial equation.

  • r1 − r2 not an integer: two independent Frobenius series exist, one from each root.
  • r1 − r2 a positive integer: r1 always gives a Frobenius series. r2 may give one, or the recurrence may break. Then y2 = C y1 ln(x − x0) + (x − x0)r2 Σ bn(x − x0)n.
  • r1 = r2: one series comes from r1. The second generally contains a logarithm, with the same form as above.

For complex roots r = α ± iβ, solutions carry (x − x0)^α cos(β ln|x − x0|) and (x − x0)^α sin(β ln|x − x0|). These are real on either side of x0. Most exams ask only for the larger real root; state the log form when roots are equal or differ by an integer. Some syllabi call this the Frobenius method at a regular singularity.

Building the series for the larger root

  1. Confirm x0 is regular singular. Put the ODE in standard form.
  2. Compute p0 and q0 from the limits.
  3. Solve F(r) = 0. Label r1 ≥ r2.
  4. Assume y = (x − x0)^r1 Σ a_n (x − x0)^n, with a0 ≠ 0.
  5. After multiplying by (x − x0)², substitute and set the coefficient of (x − x0)^(n+r1) to zero; in standard form set the coefficient of (x − x0)^(n+r1−2) to zero. This gives a recurrence.
  6. Set a0 = 1, compute a1, a2, ... and write y1.

The recurrence usually contains the bracket (n + r1)(n + r1 − 1) + p0(n + r1) + q0. For the larger root this bracket is nonzero for n ≥ 1, so division is safe. For the smaller root it can vanish, which is exactly where a logarithm or a free second series appears. The n = 0 equation is the indicial equation and puts no condition on a0.

Worked example

Question. Use the Frobenius method at x = 0 for 2x²y″ − xy′ + (1 + x)y = 0. Find the indicial roots and the series for the larger root through x⁴.

  1. Divide by 2x²: y″ − (1/(2x))y′ + (1 + x)/(2x²)y = 0. So P = −1/(2x) and Q = (1 + x)/(2x²).
  2. Limits: xP = −1/2, so p0 = −1/2. x²Q = 1/2 + x/2, so q0 = 1/2.
  3. Indicial equation: r(r − 1) − r/2 + 1/2 = 0. Multiply by 2: 2r² − 3r + 1 = 0 = (2r − 1)(r − 1). Roots r1 = 1 and r2 = 1/2.
  4. Take r = 1. Assume y = Σ a_n x^(n+1). Then y′ = Σ (n+1)a_n x^n and y″ = Σ n(n+1)a_n x^(n−1).
  5. Substitute: 2x²y″ = Σ 2n(n+1)a_n x^(n+1); −xy′ = Σ −(n+1)a_n x^(n+1); y = Σ a_n x^(n+1); xy = Σ a_n x^(n+2).
  6. Coefficient of x^(n+1): [2n(n+1) − (n+1) + 1]a_n + a_(n−1) = 0. The bracket is 2n² + n = n(2n+1). So n(2n+1)a_n + a_(n−1) = 0.
  7. Recurrence: a_n = −a_(n−1)/[n(2n+1)] for n ≥ 1. Set a0 = 1.
  8. n = 1: a1 = −1/(1×3) = −1/3 = −0.333333.
  9. n = 2: a2 = −(−1/3)/(2×5) = 1/30 = 0.033333.
  10. n = 3: a3 = −(1/30)/(3×7) = −1/630 = −0.001587.
  11. n = 4: a4 = −(−1/630)/(4×9) = 1/22680 = 0.000044.
  12. Since 1 − 1/2 = 1/2 is not an integer, a second independent Frobenius series comes from r = 1/2.

Exam traps

  • Using r where n + r belongs in the recurrence. The bracket must carry n + r, not r alone.
  • Dividing by a0 or setting a0 = 0. The leading coefficient a0 is free and must stay nonzero.
  • Assuming the smaller root always gives an independent ordinary Frobenius series. When roots differ by an integer, the second solution may need a logarithm.
  • Taking p0 = lim P and q0 = lim Q. The correct limits are p0 = lim (x − x0)P and q0 = lim (x − x0)²Q.
  • Forgetting to shift the index in the x·y product, so the recurrence is off by one.

3.4 Legendre's equation and Legendre polynomials

Legendre's equation is a second-order linear ODE with variable coefficients: (1 − x²)y″ − 2xy′ + n(n + 1)y = 0. For n = 0, 1, 2, …, one solution is a polynomial Pn(x). These polynomials are orthogonal on [−1, 1], so they expand functions the way sine and cosine do in Fourier series. They appear in physics and engineering: gravitational potential, electrostatics, heat flow in a sphere, and antenna patterns. The singular points x = ±1 explain why the series method needs care at the ends of the interval.

You need: 3.1 point classification, 3.2 power series about an ordinary point, and 3.3 Frobenius method for behaviour near x = ±1.

The equation and its singular points

Legendre's equation is a second-order linear ODE with variable coefficients. The parameter n controls which polynomial solution appears.

(1 − x²)y″ − 2xy′ + n(n + 1)y = 0

y = y(x); y′ = dy/dx; y″ = d²y/dx²; n is a constant. Polynomial solutions occur when n = 0, 1, 2, … .

In standard form, y″ − (2x/(1−x²))y′ + (n(n+1)/(1−x²))y = 0. At x = 0 the coefficients are analytic, so x = 0 is an ordinary point. At x = ±1 the denominator 1−x² is zero. After multiplying by 1−x², the coefficient of y″ has a simple zero, so x = ±1 are regular singular points (see 3.1).

The point x = 0 is the centre of the interval. The points x = ±1 are the ends where the coefficient of y″ vanishes.

  • At x = 0, 1−x² ≠ 0, so both coefficient functions in standard form are analytic there.
  • At x = ±1, 1−x² = 0, so a Frobenius series about an end needs the method of 3.3.

Rodrigues' formula and the first four polynomials

Rodrigues' formula builds each polynomial by differentiating (x²−1)n n times.

P<sub>n</sub>(x) = (1/(2<sup>n</sup> n!)) d<sup>n</sup>/dx<sup>n</sup> [(x² − 1)<sup>n</sup>]

n! = 1·2·…·n; 2n = 2 multiplied n times; dn/dxn is the n-th derivative.

  1. n = 0: P0(x) = 1/(20·0!) d0/dx0[(x²−1)0] = 1.
  2. n = 1: P1(x) = 1/(2·1!) d/dx[(x²−1)] = (1/2)(2x) = x.
  3. n = 2: (x²−1)² = x⁴ − 2x² + 1; second derivative = 12x² − 4. P2(x) = (12x² − 4)/(2²·2!) = (12x² − 4)/8 = (3x² − 1)/2.
  4. n = 3: (x²−1)³ = x⁶ − 3x⁴ + 3x² − 1; third derivative = 120x³ − 72x = 24(5x³ − 3x). P3(x) = 24(5x³ − 3x)/(2³·3!) = (5x³ − 3x)/2.
(n + 1)P<sub>n+1</sub>(x) = (2n + 1)xP<sub>n</sub>(x) − nP<sub>n−1</sub>(x)

n = 1, 2, 3, …; it gives the next polynomial from the two previous ones.

Parity is fixed by n: Pn(−x) = (−1)nPn(x). So P0 and P2 are even, and P1 and P3 are odd.

Orthogonality and norm

Legendre's equation is self-adjoint: d/dx[(1−x²)y′] + n(n+1)y = 0. This is a Sturm-Liouville equation with weight 1 on [−1, 1]; see 3.6. Solutions with different n are orthogonal.

∫<sub>−1</sub><sup>1</sup> P<sub>m</sub>(x)P<sub>n</sub>(x) dx = 0, m ≠ n

m and n are nonnegative integers; the weight is 1; the interval is [−1, 1].

∫<sub>−1</sub><sup>1</sup> [P<sub>n</sub>(x)]² dx = 2/(2n + 1)

n = 0, 1, 2, …; the integral is a pure number.

Any reasonable f on [−1, 1] expands as f(x) = Σ cnPn(x), with cn = ((2n + 1)/2)∫−11 f(x)Pn(x) dx. Orthogonality makes least-squares approximation simple: each cn depends only on f and Pn.

Some syllabi call these Legendre functions of the first kind. Here n is a nonnegative integer, so they are polynomials.

Worked example

Question. Using Rodrigues' formula, find P0(x), P1(x), P2(x) and P3(x). Hence evaluate ∫−11 P1(x)P3(x) dx.

  1. Rodrigues' formula is Pn(x) = 1/(2nn!) dn/dxn[(x²−1)n].
  2. n = 0: P0(x) = 1/(1·1) · 1 = 1.
  3. n = 1: P1(x) = 1/(2·1) · d/dx(x²−1) = (1/2)(2x) = x.
  4. n = 2: (x²−1)² = x⁴ − 2x² + 1; second derivative = 12x² − 4. P2(x) = (12x² − 4)/8 = (3x² − 1)/2.
  5. n = 3: (x²−1)³ = x⁶ − 3x⁴ + 3x² − 1; third derivative = 120x³ − 72x = 24(5x³ − 3x). P3(x) = 24(5x³ − 3x)/48 = (5x³ − 3x)/2.
  6. Multiply: P1(x)P3(x) = x·(5x³ − 3x)/2 = (5x⁴ − 3x²)/2.
  7. Integrate term by term: ∫−11 x⁴ dx = 2/5, and ∫−11 x² dx = 2/3.
  8. So ∫−11 P1P3 dx = (1/2)[5·(2/5) − 3·(2/3)] = (1/2)[2 − 2] = 0.

Answer. P0 = 1, P1 = x, P2 = (3x² − 1)/2, P3 = (5x³ − 3x)/2, and ∫−11 P1(x)P3(x) dx = 0.

Exam traps

  • Legendre orthogonality uses weight 1 and interval [−1, 1]; writing weight x or interval [0, 1] gives a different problem.
  • Pn is a polynomial only for n = 0, 1, 2, …; for other n the solution is a general Legendre function, not this polynomial.
  • The coefficient is n(n + 1), not n(n − 1), in Legendre's equation. A sign slip changes the polynomials.
  • Rodrigues' formula uses (x² − 1)n, not (1 − x²)n, and divides by 2nn!.

3.5 Bessel's equation and Bessel functions

Bessel's equation is x²y″ + xy′ + (x² − ν²)y = 0. It appears when you separate variables in a circle or cylinder: a drumhead, a hot rod, a microwave pipe. The point x = 0 is a regular singular point, so a Frobenius series gives Jν, the Bessel function of the first kind. Jν oscillates, has infinitely many positive zeros, and is orthogonal with weight x. Those zeros fix allowed frequencies and eigenvalues, and the orthogonality lets you expand a function in a Bessel series.

You need 3.1 for regular singular points, 3.3 for Frobenius series, and the Gamma function Γ.

Equation, indicial roots and series

Write ν ≥ 0 as the order. Divide the equation by x². The coefficients are 1/x and (x² − ν²)/x², so x = 0 is a regular singular point. Use a Frobenius series y = xr Σ amxm, with a0 ≠ 0. Put it into the equation. The lowest power xr gives a0(r² − ν²) = 0. So the indicial roots are r = ν and r = −ν. The trap is writing ±ν²; the roots are ±ν.

a<sub>m</sub> = −a<sub>m−2</sub> / ((m + ν)² − ν²) = −a<sub>m−2</sub> / (m(m + 2ν))

am is the coefficient of xm+ν for the root r = ν; m = 2, 3, 4, ...; a1 = 0, so all odd coefficients vanish.

Choose a0 = 1/(2νΓ(ν + 1)). The even coefficients then give the standard first-kind solution. Some syllabi call Jν the Bessel function of the first kind. For integer n ≥ 0, J−n(x) = (−1)nJn(x), so it is not a new independent solution.

J<sub>ν</sub>(x) = Σ<sub>m=0</sub><sup>∞</sup> (−1)<sup>m</sup> / (m! Γ(m + ν + 1)) (x/2)<sup>2m+ν</sup>

Jν is the Bessel function of order ν; m is the summation index; Γ is the Gamma function; x is the independent variable and ν ≥ 0.

J<sub>0</sub>(x) = 1 − x²/4 + x⁴/64 − x⁶/2304 + ...    J<sub>1</sub>(x) = x/2 − x³/16 + x⁵/384 − ...

The first terms come from putting ν = 0 and ν = 1 in the series.

Derivative rules, zeros and orthogonality

d/dx [x<sup>ν</sup>J<sub>ν</sub>(x)] = x<sup>ν</sup>J<sub>ν−1</sub>(x)    d/dx [x<sup>−ν</sup>J<sub>ν</sub>(x)] = −x<sup>−ν</sup>J<sub>ν+1</sub>(x)

These identities relate Bessel functions of neighbouring orders. They follow by differentiating the power series term by term.

From these identities come the recurrence relations Jν−1 + Jν+1 = (2ν/x)Jν and Jν−1 − Jν+1 = 2Jν′. Also J0(0) = 1 and Jν(0) = 0 for ν > 0. Jν has infinitely many positive zeros jν,1 < jν,2 < ... . For example, j0,1 ≈ 2.4048 and j1,1 ≈ 3.8317.

∫<sub>0</sub><sup>a</sup> x J<sub>ν</sub>(α<sub>m</sub>x) J<sub>ν</sub>(α<sub>n</sub>x) dx = 0 for m ≠ n

Take αm = jν,m/a on the interval [0, a]; jν,m are positive zeros of Jν. The weight x is essential.

The matching norm is ∫0a x Jν(αmx)² dx = (a²/2) Jν+1(jν,m)². This is the weighted orthogonality used in Fourier–Bessel series. The second solution Yν is not finite at x = 0; for integer ν it contains a log x term. Bessel orthogonality fits the Sturm–Liouville pattern in 3.6.

Where it is used

A circular drumhead of radius a gives the radial equation r²R″ + rR′ + (λ²r² − ν²)R = 0. Put x = λr. This is Bessel's equation. Finiteness at the centre r = 0 forces the Jν solution. The clamped edge R(a) = 0 gives Jν(λa) = 0, so λa = jν,m and λ = jν,m/a. The allowed frequencies are f = c jν,m/(2πa), where c is the wave speed. The weight x and the zeros jν,m then give the coefficients of a Fourier–Bessel expansion.

The same equation appears in heat flow in a cylinder and in electromagnetic waves in a circular waveguide. The order ν comes from the angular part: ν is an integer for a full circle. For a sector or annular sector, ν need not be an integer; for a full annulus it is still an integer.

3.6 Sturm-Liouville problems and orthogonal eigenfunctions

A second-order linear ODE with a parameter λ can be put in self-adjoint (Sturm–Liouville) form, (p y′)′ + (q + λw) y = 0. Then the eigenfunctions are orthogonal with weight w, so a function can be rebuilt as a sum of them. Heat flow in a rod, a drum, the hydrogen atom each end in such an expansion. This chapter reaches the form, proves orthogonality, and gives Legendre and Bessel weights.

You need second-order linear ODEs and the Wronskian (Unit 2), plus 3.4 and 3.5 for the Legendre and Bessel examples.

Putting an equation in self-adjoint form

A second-order linear ODE is in self-adjoint (Sturm–Liouville) form when it is written with the derivative of a product in front.

(p(x) y′)′ + (q(x) + λ w(x)) y = 0, a ≤ x ≤ b

p, q, w are real functions with p > 0 and w > 0 on (a, b); λ is the eigenvalue parameter; y(x) is the eigenfunction.

Expand the first term: p y″ + p′ y′ + (q + λw) y = 0. The y′ coefficient equals p′, not a free function. That link makes the operator self-adjoint and gives orthogonality.

Any a(x) y″ + b(x) y′ + c(x) y = 0 with a(x) ≠ 0 on the interval, and c(x) = c₀(x) + λ (λ entering linearly), reaches this form after dividing by a and multiplying by μ(x) = exp(∫ (b/a) dx).

  1. Divide by a: y″ + (b/a) y′ + (c/a) y = 0.
  2. Multiply by μ = exp(∫ (b/a) dx), the integrating factor from Unit 1.
  3. Then μ y″ + μ(b/a) y′ = (μ y′)′ because μ′ = μ b/a. So p = μ.
  4. In μ(c/a)y, the factor multiplying λ is w; the rest is q. If the λ-term in the original equation is λ d(x)y, then w = μ d/a.

Boundary conditions come in separated pairs, each touching one end.

α₁ y(a) + α₂ p(a) y′(a) = 0,   β₁ y(b) + β₂ p(b) y′(b) = 0

α₁, α₂ and β₁, β₂ are constants, not both zero in each pair; p stays in the condition to keep symmetry.

  • Regular problem: p > 0 on [a, b], p′ continuous, and q, w continuous on [a, b] with w > 0 there.
  • Singular problem: p vanishes at an end (Legendre at x = ±1 where p = 1 − x² → 0; Bessel at x = 0 where p = x → 0), or q or w blows up at an end (Bessel: q = −ν²/x → ∞ as x → 0), or the interval is infinite.

Lagrange identity, real eigenvalues, orthogonality

Take L y = (p y′)′ + q y and two functions u, v. The Lagrange identity turns the combination into one derivative.

u (p v′)′ − v (p u′)′ = d/dx [ p (u v′ − v u′) ]

u, v are twice differentiable on [a, b]; p is the same function as in the Sturm–Liouville form.

Integrate over [a, b]. The right side becomes p(u v′ − v u′) at the two ends. For separated conditions this boundary term is zero, so ∫ (u L v − v L u) dx = 0.

Set u = y_m, v = y_n, eigenfunctions for different eigenvalues. Since L y = −λw y, (λ_m − λ_n) ∫ w y_m y_n dx = 0. Because λ_m ≠ λ_n, the integral vanishes.

∫_a^b w(x) y_m(x) y_n(x) dx = 0, m ≠ n

w is the weight; without w the integral is generally not zero.

The same identity forces real eigenvalues. For a regular problem with separated boundary conditions they are real, simple and countable: λ₁ < λ₂ < λ₃ < … → ∞, with one eigenfunction each. Periodic conditions can repeat an eigenvalue with two eigenfunctions.

Dividing by the norm gives an orthonormal system.

φ_n = y_n / ‖y_n‖,   ‖y_n‖ = √(∫_a^b w y_n² dx),   ∫ w φ_m φ_n dx = δ_mn

δ_mn = 1 when m = n and 0 otherwise; ‖y_n‖ is the weighted norm.

The weights you meet: 1, 1 and x

Three standard problems cover most questions. Only Bessel has weight other than 1.

  • y″ + λ y = 0 on (0, π): already self-adjoint with p = 1, q = 0, w = 1. With y(0) = y(π) = 0, λ_n = n² and y_n = sin nx.
  • Legendre, ((1 − x²) y′)′ + n(n + 1) y = 0 on [−1, 1]: p = 1 − x², q = 0, w = 1, λ = n(n + 1). Hence ∫_{−1}^{1} P_m P_n dx = 0 for m ≠ n.
  • Bessel for J_ν(αx), (x y′)′ + (α²x − ν²/x) y = 0 on (0, 1): p = x, q = −ν²/x, w = x, λ = α². The weight is x, not 1.

∫₀^1 x J_ν(α_m x) J_ν(α_n x) dx = 0, m ≠ n

α_m and α_n are distinct positive zeros of J_ν; the factor x is the weight w(x) = x.

Legendre and Bessel are singular, yet orthogonality holds. The boundary term dies because p = 1 − x² → 0 at x = ±1, and because x J_ν′ → 0 as x → 0.

3.7 Sturm comparison and separation theorems

Sturm theory reads the zeros of solutions without solving the ODE. Abel's formula fixes the Wronskian, so linear independence is controlled by one exponential factor. Sturm separation says zeros of two independent solutions alternate. Sturm comparison says a larger coefficient q in y″ + q(x)y = 0 makes solutions oscillate faster, so zeros appear more often. These ideas explain why sin 2x has a zero between the zeros of sin x, and they justify the zero patterns of Legendre and Bessel functions used in heat, wave and potential problems.

You need: the Wronskian and linear independence from Unit 2, 3.6 Sturm-Liouville problems, and the zero patterns in 3.4 Legendre and 3.5 Bessel.

Abel's formula fixes the Wronskian

Write the ODE in standard form y″ + P(x)y′ + Q(x)y = 0. P and Q are continuous on an interval I. Let y1 and y2 be two solutions on I. Their Wronskian is W(x) = y1y2′ − y1′y2. If W(x) is not zero at one point of I, then y1 and y2 are linearly independent (see Unit 2, Linearly independent solutions and the Wronskian). Abel's formula gives W at every point from W at one point:

W(x) = W(x₀) exp(−∫<sub>x₀</sub><sup>x</sup> P(t) dt)

W(x) is the Wronskian at x; W(x₀) is its value at a chosen point x₀ in I; P(t) is the coefficient of y′; the integral runs from x₀ to x.

The exponential is never zero. Therefore W is either zero everywhere on I or nonzero everywhere on I. It cannot vanish at one point and be nonzero at another. To use Abel's formula, write the ODE in standard form, read P, compute W at the easiest point, and multiply by the exponential.

For y″ + y = 0, P = 0, so W(x) = W(0). With y1 = sin x and y2 = cos x, W(0) = −1, hence W(π/2) = −1. The next two theorems use this fixed nonzero W to compare zeros.

Sturm separation: zeros interlace

Sturm separation theorem. Let y1 and y2 be two linearly independent solutions of the same ODE y″ + P(x)y′ + Q(x)y = 0, with P and Q continuous on I. Then their zeros interlace. If a and b are consecutive zeros of y1, then y2 has exactly one zero in the open interval (a, b). Similarly, between consecutive zeros of y2 there is exactly one zero of y1.

At a zero of y1, W = −y1′y2. Since W ≠ 0 and y1′ ≠ 0 at a simple zero, y2 is not zero there. A sign argument using W and Rolle's theorem on y1/y2 forces exactly one zero of y2 inside (a, b). A second zero would force another zero of y1 before b.

Example: y″ + y = 0 has y1 = sin x and y2 = cos x. The zeros of sin x are 0, π, 2π, …; the zeros of cos x are π/2, 3π/2, … . One cos zero lies between each pair of consecutive sin zeros. The same interlacing is behind Sturm-Liouville eigenfunctions (see 3.6).

Sturm comparison: larger q oscillates faster

Sturm comparison theorem. Take two ODEs with no first-derivative term: y″ + q1(x)y = 0 and y″ + q2(x)y = 0 on an interval I, where q1 and q2 are continuous on I. Let u be a nontrivial solution of the q2 equation, and let a < b be two consecutive zeros of u. If q1(x) > q2(x) for all x in I, then every nontrivial solution v of the q1 equation has at least one zero in the open interval (a, b). A weaker form: if q1 ≥ q2 and q1 > q2 somewhere on I, the same conclusion holds provided v is not a constant multiple of u. The strict form q1 > q2 is the one used in the examples below. Some syllabi call this Sturm's fundamental comparison theorem.

q₁(x) > q₂(x) ⇒ zeros of y₁ come at least as often as zeros of y₂

y₁ solves y″ + q₁(x)y = 0; y₂ solves y″ + q₂(x)y = 0; q₁ and q₂ are continuous on the same interval, with q₁ > q₂.

The larger q acts like a stronger restoring force. It pulls the solution back to the axis sooner, so zeros come more often. For y″ + 4y = 0, q1 = 4; for y″ + y = 0, q2 = 1. Since 4 > 1, solutions of y″ + 4y = 0 oscillate faster. The slower solution sin x has consecutive zeros at 0 and π. The faster solution sin 2x has a zero at x = π/2 inside (0, π). On [0, 2π], sin 2x has zeros 0, π/2, π, 3π/2, 2π, while sin x has only 0, π, 2π.

Only a few universities prove the comparison theorem in detail. The proof uses the Wronskian of u and v and a sign argument on (a, b). The statement is what exams ask: compare q first, then place a zero of the faster solution between consecutive zeros of the slower one.

Worked example

Question. Take y₁ = sin 2x for y″ + 4y = 0 and y₂ = sin x for y″ + y = 0. (a) State the Sturm comparison conclusion on [0, π]. (b) Verify it with the zeros of y₂. (c) For y″ + y = 0 with u₁ = sin x and u₂ = cos x, compute W(0) and use Abel's formula to find W(π/2).

  1. Standard forms: y″ + 4y = 0 has q₁ = 4; y″ + y = 0 has q₂ = 1. Since q₁ > q₂, Sturm comparison applies with the faster equation first.
  2. Consecutive zeros of y₂ = sin x on [0, π] are x = 0 and x = π.
  3. The theorem says every nontrivial solution of y″ + 4y = 0 has at least one zero in (0, π). For y₁ = sin 2x, sin 2x = 0 when 2x = nπ, so x = nπ/2. The zero inside is x = π/2 ≈ 1.5708.
  4. For y″ + y = 0, P = 0. W(x) = u₁u₂′ − u₁′u₂. At x = 0: u₁ = 0, u₁′ = 1, u₂ = 1, u₂′ = 0. W(0) = 0·0 − 1·1 = −1.
  5. Abel: W(x) = W(0) exp(−∫₀ˣ 0 dt) = −1. At x = π/2 ≈ 1.5708, W(π/2) = −1. Direct check: u₁ = 1, u₁′ = 0, u₂ = 0, u₂′ = −1, so W = 1·(−1) − 0·0 = −1.

Answer. q₁ = 4 > q₂ = 1; sin 2x has a zero at π/2 ≈ 1.5708 between 0 and π; W(0) = −1 and W(π/2) = −1.

Exam traps

  • Reversing Sturm comparison: larger q makes zeros more frequent. Compare between consecutive zeros of the slower equation (smaller q), not the faster one.
  • Abel gives W(x) up to the exponential factor. W is constant only when P(x) = 0; do not write W(x) = W(x₀) when P ≠ 0.
  • Sturm separation needs two linearly independent solutions of the same ODE. It does not compare solutions of two different ODEs.
  • Zeros at endpoints: 'between consecutive zeros' means strictly inside the open interval (a, b), not at a or b.
  • For Sturm comparison, both equations must be in the form y″ + q(x)y = 0. A first derivative term P y′ changes the statement until you remove it.

Takeaways

  1. Classify the point before choosing a method: ordinary point needs a plain power series, regular singular point needs a Frobenius series, irregular point is not covered by either.
  2. The series solution about an ordinary point converges at least out to the nearest singular point of the equation.
  3. The indicial equation r(r − 1) + p₀r + q₀ = 0 gives the exponents; when the two roots differ by an integer or are equal, the second solution usually carries a log term.
  4. Legendre's equation has polynomial solutions only when the separation constant is n(n + 1) with n a whole number, and these Pₙ are orthogonal on [−1, 1] with weight 1.
  5. Bessel's equation gives Jν, which is finite at x = 0; J−ν gives a second independent solution unless ν is an integer.
  6. Multiplying a second-order linear ODE by an integrating factor always brings it to self-adjoint Sturm–Liouville form, which is what makes orthogonality work.
  7. Abel's formula delivers the Wronskian up to one constant, without solving the equation at all.
  8. Larger q in y″ + q(x)y = 0 makes solutions oscillate faster, and zeros of independent solutions interlace.

Glossary

Abel's identity
The statement that the Wronskian of two solutions of y″ + P y′ + Q y = 0 equals a constant times exp(−∫ P dx).
Analytic function
A function that can be written as a convergent Taylor series in a neighbourhood of the point in question.
Bessel function of the first kind
The solution Jν of Bessel's equation that stays finite at x = 0, given by a Frobenius series.
Bessel function of the second kind
The independent partner Yν, which blows up at x = 0 and carries a logarithmic term when ν is an integer.
Bessel's equation
The ODE x²y″ + xy′ + (x² − ν²)y = 0, arising in circular and cylindrical problems.
Eigenfunction
The nonzero solution of the boundary value problem belonging to a particular eigenvalue.
Eigenvalue
A value of the parameter λ for which the boundary value problem has a nonzero solution.
Frobenius method
The technique of seeking a solution in the form (x − x₀)^r Σ aₙ(x − x₀)ⁿ at a regular singular point.
Gamma function
The extension of the factorial to non-integers, defined by an integral and satisfying Γ(ν + 1) = νΓ(ν).
Indicial equation
The quadratic in r obtained from the lowest power of x after substitution; its roots are the exponents of the Frobenius solutions.
Irregular singular point
A singular point at which those limits fail to exist or fail to be analytic.
Legendre polynomial
The terminating polynomial solution Pₙ of Legendre's equation with n a non-negative integer, normalized by Pₙ(1) = 1.
Legendre's equation
The ODE (1 − x²)y″ − 2xy′ + n(n + 1)y = 0, arising from problems with spherical symmetry.
Ordinary point
A point where both coefficient functions P and Q of the standard-form second-order linear ODE are analytic.
Orthogonal functions
A family of functions whose products integrate to zero against a weight whenever the indices differ.
Power series
An infinite sum Σ aₙ(x − x₀)ⁿ which converges inside some interval or disc around x₀.
Radius of convergence
The number R such that the series converges absolutely for |x − x₀| < R and diverges outside.
Recurrence relation
A formula expressing each new coefficient of the series in terms of earlier ones.
Regular singular point
A singular point at which (x − x₀)P and (x − x₀)²Q stay analytic, so the Frobenius method applies.
Rodrigues' formula
The derivative formula Pₙ(x) = (1/(2ⁿ n!)) (dⁿ/dxⁿ)(x² − 1)ⁿ for the Legendre polynomial.
Self-adjoint equation
A second-order linear ODE written as (p y′)′ + q y = 0, so that Lagrange's identity and orthogonality follow directly.
Singular point
A point where P or Q fails to be analytic, so the equation cannot be treated by a plain power series there.
Sturm comparison theorem
A larger coefficient q(x) forces faster oscillation, so zeros appear more often.
Sturm separation theorem
Two linearly independent solutions of the same second-order linear ODE have interlacing zeros.
Weight function
The positive multiplier w(x) inside the orthogonality integral for a Sturm–Liouville problem.
Wronskian
The determinant W = y₁y₂′ − y₁′y₂, which is nonzero exactly when the two solutions are linearly independent.
