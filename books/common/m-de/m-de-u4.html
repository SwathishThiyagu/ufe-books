4.1 Formation of PDEs by eliminating arbitrary constants and functions

A partial differential equation (PDE) links a function z(x,y) to its partial derivatives. Often we know a family of surfaces with free constants or free functions. Eliminating those free choices by differentiation gives the PDE that all members satisfy. This is how a model becomes an equation: the constants disappear, the relation remains. Engineers use it when a measured family of surfaces must be written as one differential law, and examiners use it to test whether you can differentiate cleanly and eliminate completely.

You need partial differentiation and the chain rule from first-year calculus; p and q are defined here, and 4.2 shows what to do with a formed PDE.

The symbols p and q

Let z be the dependent variable and x, y the independent variables. Write z = z(x,y). The two first partial derivatives get short names:

p = ∂z/∂x, q = ∂z/∂y

p is the x-derivative with y held fixed; q is the y-derivative with x held fixed. A first-order PDE contains x, y, z, p and q, but no second derivatives such as ∂²z/∂x².

The order is the highest derivative present. Formation asks only for the equation; solving it is a separate job.

Arbitrary constants: differentiate and remove

Start with a relation F(x,y,z,a,b)=0, where a and b are arbitrary constants. Differentiate the relation with respect to x while y is fixed. Differentiate with respect to y while x is fixed. The two new equations contain a and b. Use them with the original relation to remove a and b.

  1. Write the given relation as F(x,y,z,a,b)=0.
  2. Differentiate with respect to x at fixed y; replace ∂z/∂x by p.
  3. Differentiate with respect to y at fixed x; replace ∂z/∂y by q.
  4. Eliminate a and b from the original relation and the two derivative equations.
  5. The result, containing only x, y, z, p, q, is the first-order PDE.

If only one constant remains, one derivative equation plus the original relation is usually enough. A constant that appears together with another may still vanish in one step.

F(x,y,z,a,b)=0 → Φ(x,y,z,p,q)=0

Φ is the formed PDE; a and b no longer appear. The arrow means elimination, not solving.

Arbitrary functions: chain rule

For one arbitrary function z = f(u), u = u(x,y), differentiate with respect to x and y by the chain rule. Let f′ be the derivative of f with respect to u.

p = f′(u) u<sub>x</sub>,  q = f′(u) u<sub>y</sub>

ux = ∂u/∂x, uy = ∂u/∂y, and f′ is d f / d u. The unknown function f′ appears in both equations.

Multiply the first equation by uy and the second by ux, then subtract. The f′ term cancels.

p uy − q ux = 0

This is the first-order PDE for the family z = f(u). It contains u and its first derivatives, but not f itself.

For a relation F(u,v)=0, differentiate with respect to x and y. A nonzero pair Fu, Fv exists only if the determinant is zero.

u<sub>x</sub> v<sub>y</sub> − u<sub>y</sub> v<sub>x</sub> = 0

This is the Jacobian condition ∂(u,v)/∂(x,y)=0. It is the first-order PDE for the family F(u,v)=0.

Two independent arbitrary functions, such as z=f(x)+g(y), normally give a second-order PDE; that belongs to Unit 5, not here.

Worked example

Question. Form the partial differential equation by eliminating the arbitrary constants a and b from z = ax + by + ab.

  1. Given z = ax + by + ab, with a and b arbitrary constants.
  2. Differentiate with respect to x at fixed y: ∂z/∂x = a, since by and ab are constant in x. So p = a.
  3. Differentiate with respect to y at fixed x: ∂z/∂y = b, so q = b.
  4. Substitute a = p and b = q into the original relation: z = p x + q y + p q.
  5. The constants a and b are gone. The result contains x, y, z, p, q only, so it is first order.

Answer. The required PDE is z = px + qy + pq.

Exam traps

  • Do not treat a and b as variables. They are constants, so ∂a/∂x = 0 and ∂b/∂y = 0.
  • Do not forget the chain rule in z = f(u). p = f′(u)ux, not just f′(u).
  • Do not mix the held-fixed variable. p is ∂z/∂x at fixed y; q is ∂z/∂y at fixed x.
  • Two arbitrary functions usually produce a second-order PDE. The first-order function formula applies to z=f(u) or F(u,v)=0.
  • Do not stop with a or b still present; elimination must be complete.

4.2 Standard types of first order nonlinear PDEs

First-order nonlinear partial differential equations (PDEs) often look harder than they are. Four standard shapes cover many exam questions. Write z for the unknown surface, p=∂z/∂x for its x-slope, and q=∂z/∂y for its y-slope. Each shape has a fixed substitution that removes one variable and leaves an ODE (ordinary differential equation) or a direct integration. The output is a complete integral, a two-parameter family of surfaces. These families model slope constraints in surface geometry and simple transport problems. Once you know the shape, the first step is automatic.

You need 4.1 for p and q, and Unit 1 for solving the reduced ODE.

Four standard shapes

Write z for the unknown function. Write p=∂z/∂x and q=∂z/∂y. A first-order PDE is nonlinear when p or q appears in a power (p²), in a product with q or z (pq, pz), or inside a function (sin p, eq). Multipliers that depend only on x and y, as in xp+q=z, keep the equation linear. Four shapes cover many exam questions. Each shape has one fixed move that removes a variable and leaves an ODE or a direct integration.

Type 1: f(p,q)=0. No x, y, or z. Set p=a and q=b. The constants must satisfy f(a,b)=0. Then dz=p dx+q dy becomes dz=a dx+b dy. Integrate: z=ax+by+c.

Type 2: f(z,p,q)=0. No x or y alone. Set u=x+ay. Then z is a function of u. p=dz/du and q=a dz/du. Substitute; solve the ODE in z(u); replace u by x+ay.

Type 3: f(x,p,q)=0. No y or z. Set q=a. Solve for p=φ(x,a). Then z=∫φ(x,a) dx + a y + c.

Type 4: f(y,p,q)=0. No x or z. Set p=a. Solve for q=ψ(y,a). Then z=a x + ∫ψ(y,a) dy + c.

The result z(x,y,a,c) is a complete integral: a two-parameter family. It is not the unique solution of an initial value problem; see 4.5.

Why the substitutions work

Type 1 follows from integration. p=a means ∂z/∂x=a, so z=ax+g(y). Then q=g′(y). The condition f(p,q)=0 becomes f(a,g′(y))=0. If it fixes g′(y)=b, then g=by+c. Hence z=ax+by+c with f(a,b)=0. The constants a,b,c are arbitrary except for f(a,b)=0.

Type 2 uses a change of variable. Put u=x+ay and look for solutions with z a function of u alone, so that p=dz/du and q=a dz/du. The original f(z,p,q)=0 becomes f(z, dz/du, a dz/du)=0, an ODE with u as the only independent variable. Solve it, then replace u by x+ay. The constant a stays as a parameter.

For type 3, choose q=a. The equation has no y or z, so this constant q removes both. The PDE f(x,p,a)=0 involves only x and p. Solve it for p=φ(x,a). Then dz=p dx+q dy = φ(x,a)dx + a dy. Integrate in x and y: z=∫φ(x,a)dx + a y + c. Type 4 is the mirror: p=a, f(y,a,q)=0, q=ψ(y,a), z=a x+∫ψ(y,a)dy+c.

A complete integral from any type carries two arbitrary constants (a and c, or a and b plus c). Some syllabi call the family a complete solution. Do not confuse it with a particular solution.

Clairaut's form and related methods

Clairaut's form is a useful extension. It has the shape z=px+qy+f(p,q), where f is a given function of p and q. Set p=a and q=b. Then z=ax+by+f(a,b) is a complete integral. This is the same type-1 move: the PDE contains only p and q after the z terms are moved. Check by substitution.

Some syllabi add a fifth method: for linear PDEs, try z=X(x)Y(y). This is separation of variables. It is not one of the four nonlinear types. Use it only when the PDE is linear and homogeneous enough to split into two ODEs. Second-order separation is in Unit 5.

The four types are entry points, not the whole subject. If an equation has extra terms, first try a change of variables that removes them. If that fails, use 4.4. For a linear equation Pp+Qq=R, with P,Q,R functions of x,y,z, use 4.3. The complete integral gives a family; a given curve picks one member, as in 4.5. The family is also the starting point for Pfaffian checks in 4.6.

Question. Solve p²+q²=1. (a) Write the complete integral. (b) For a=0.6 and c=3, take the positive branch, find b and z at x=1, y=2.

Exam traps

  • In f(z,p,q)=0 do not just set q=a. That still leaves z in the equation, so nothing reduces to a single-variable ODE. Use u=x+ay, p=dz/du, q=a dz/du.
  • In f(y,p,q)=0 set p=a, not q=a. Setting q=a belongs to f(x,p,q)=0.
  • For f(p,q)=0, a and b are not independent. Always enforce f(a,b)=0 before writing z=ax+by+c.
  • A complete integral is a two-parameter family, not one unique solution. An initial curve selects a member; see 4.5.
  • Do not use z=X(x)Y(y) as a fifth nonlinear type. It is separation for linear PDEs. Equations reducible to standard types are a separate topic.

4.3 Lagrange's linear equation

Lagrange's linear equation is a first-order PDE of the form P p + Q q = R. Here z is the unknown surface, p = ∂z/∂x, q = ∂z/∂y, and P, Q, R are functions of x, y, z. The method turns the PDE into two ordinary first integrals along characteristic curves. Two independent integrals u = c₁, v = c₂ give the general solution φ(u, v) = 0. Engineers meet such equations when a surface must satisfy a slope condition everywhere, for example in geometry, streamline surfaces and simple conservation models.

You need 4.1 for p and q, and exact differentials from Unit 1.

The equation and its auxiliary system

Write the PDE in standard form before doing anything else. It is called linear because P, Q and R do not contain p or q. They may contain x, y and z in any way. A solution is a surface z = f(x, y) whose partial derivatives satisfy the equation at every point of a region.

P p + Q q = R

P, Q, R are given functions of x, y, z; z is the unknown function of x and y; p = ∂z/∂x; q = ∂z/∂y.

Lagrange's idea is to move along special curves on the solution surface. Along such a curve, x, y and z all change together. The PDE becomes a system of ordinary differential equations. These are the auxiliary equations.

  1. Identify P, Q and R after moving every term to one side.
  2. Write the auxiliary equations dx/P = dy/Q = dz/R.
  3. Find two independent first integrals u = c₁ and v = c₂.
  4. Write the general solution as φ(u, v) = 0, or as u = f(v).

Multipliers and two first integrals

Introduce a parameter t along a characteristic curve. Then dx/P = dy/Q = dz/R = dt gives dx = P dt, dy = Q dt and dz = R dt. Choose multipliers l, m, n such that lP + mQ + nR = 0. Multiplying the three differentials and adding gives l dx + m dy + n dz = 0. If the left side is an exact differential dF, integrate it to get F = constant. That constant is one first integral.

Two first integrals u = c₁ and v = c₂ are independent when neither is a function of the other on the region. Equivalently, their gradients are not parallel there. One independent relation between u and v remains arbitrary. That is why a single first-order PDE has a whole family of solution surfaces, not one surface.

  • Common multiplier choices are 1, 1, 1 or x, y, z, but only when the sum condition holds.
  • A multiplier triple that gives a new constant is useful; one that repeats an old constant is not.
  • The general solution is φ(u, v) = 0 or u = f(v); φ and f are arbitrary functions.
  • A first-order PDE in two independent variables needs exactly two independent first integrals.

Geometrical meaning and scope

Each solution z = f(x, y) is an integral surface. The auxiliary equations give characteristic curves. Any characteristic curve that touches an integral surface lies wholly on that surface. Changing c₁ and c₂ selects different characteristic curves; the integral surface is woven from them. This picture explains why the general solution contains an arbitrary function: many surfaces can be built from the same family of curves.

Lagrange's method handles linear equations P p + Q q = R. Nonlinear first-order equations need Charpit's method. An initial curve selecting one surface is the Cauchy problem. Pfaffian equations appear in 4.6. Second-order PDEs start in Unit 5. Some syllabi call this topic Lagrange's linear PDE or the linear equation of first order.

Worked example

Question. Solve (y − z)p + (z − x)q = x − y. Also evaluate the two first integrals at the point (x, y, z) = (1, 2, 3).

  1. Identify the coefficients: P = y − z, Q = z − x, R = x − y.
  2. Write the auxiliary equations: dx/(y − z) = dy/(z − x) = dz/(x − y).
  3. Take multipliers 1, 1, 1. Their sum with the denominators is (y − z) + (z − x) + (x − y) = 0.
  4. Therefore dx + dy + dz = 0. Integrate: x + y + z = c₁.
  5. Take multipliers x, y, z. Their sum is x(y − z) + y(z − x) + z(x − y) = xy − xz + yz − xy + xz − yz = 0.
  6. Therefore x dx + y dy + z dz = 0. Integrate: x² + y² + z² = c₂, with the factor 2 absorbed into the constant.
  7. At (1, 2, 3): u = x + y + z = 1 + 2 + 3 = 6, and v = x² + y² + z² = 1 + 4 + 9 = 14.
  8. Independence at (1, 2, 3): gradients are (1, 1, 1) and (2, 4, 6), which are not parallel, so u = 6 and v = 14 are independent there.

Answer. The general solution is φ(x + y + z, x² + y² + z²) = 0, and at (1, 2, 3) the first integrals are u = 6 and v = 14.

Exam traps

  • The denominators are P, Q, R, not p, q, r. Writing dx/p = dy/q = dz/R mixes the unknown derivatives with the coefficients and is wrong.
  • Two first integrals must be independent. u = x + y and v = 2x + 2y give only one constant, not a general solution.
  • Multipliers must make lP + mQ + nR = 0. Checking l p + m q + n R = 0 is meaningless because p and q are unknown.
  • Do not force z as the subject unless an initial condition or the question asks for it. The implicit form φ(u, v) = 0 is the standard answer.
  • Constants may be scaled: x² + y² + z² = c and (x² + y² + z²)/2 = c describe the same family. Do not count them as two independent integrals.

4.4 Charpit's method

Charpit's method solves a general first-order PDE F(x, y, z, p, q) = 0 when the standard substitutions of 4.2 do not fit. It finds a second relation between p and q, solves the two relations for p and q, then integrates dz = p dx + q dy. The result is a complete integral with two constants. Engineers use it for nonlinear wave fronts, Hamilton–Jacobi problems in control, and any first-order surface equation that resists fixed tricks.

You need: 4.1, 4.2, 4.3, partial derivatives, and ordinary integration.

Why a second relation is needed

A first-order PDE in two independent variables x and y has the form F(x, y, z, p, q) = 0. Here z is the unknown surface, p = ∂z/∂x and q = ∂z/∂y. One equation with two unknown functions p and q leaves freedom. A complete integral must carry two arbitrary constants a and b.

F(x, y, z, p, q) = 0

z = z(x, y) is the surface; p = ∂z/∂x; q = ∂z/∂y; F is a given smooth function.

Charpit supplies a second equation f(x, y, z, p, q, a) = 0. It is chosen so that F = 0 and f = 0 can be solved for p and q. Then dz = p dx + q dy is integrated to get z = φ(x, y, a, b).

Use Charpit when 4.2 fails: for equations such as p q = z, p² + q² = z, or Clairaut-type z = p x + q y + f(p, q) with nonlinear terms. For linear Pp + Qq = R, Charpit reduces to Lagrange's method (4.3), so use that shorter route.

Charpit's auxiliary equations

dx/F_p = dy/F_q = dz/(p F_p + q F_q) = −dp/(F_x + p F_z) = −dq/(F_y + q F_z)

F_p = ∂F/∂p, and similarly F_x, F_y, F_z, F_q; p and q are evaluated from the surface.

Compute five partial derivatives: F_x, F_y, F_z, F_p, F_q. Write the chain. Pick a pair with easy integration. The usual pair is −dp/(F_x + p F_z) = −dq/(F_y + q F_z), or one side with dx or dy. Integrate to get f(...) = a.

Keep the minus signs. They come from the characteristic equations and flip signs when F_z is nonzero. For F = p q − z, F_z = −1, so −dp/(pF_z) = dp/p, not −dp/p.

  1. Write F = 0; find F_x, F_y, F_z, F_p, F_q.
  2. Write the chained equalities from the formula.
  3. Integrate one usable pair; name the constant a.
  4. Solve F = 0 and f = a for p and q.
  5. Put p and q into dz = p dx + q dy and integrate; name the new constant b.
  6. Write z = φ(x, y, a, b); verify by substitution.

From p and q to the complete integral

After step 4, p and q contain a and the variables. Substitute them into dz = p dx + q dy. Integrate in two stages: treat y as constant and integrate in x, then add an unknown function of y; differentiate the result with respect to y and match q. In many Charpit problems the variables separate directly, so one integration gives the answer.

Charpit's equations guarantee that the resulting expression is an exact differential, so the integration is path-independent. The final z has two constants a and b. A complete integral is not the general solution; it is a two-parameter family. Selecting constants to fit a curve is the Cauchy problem (4.5).

Check the answer by computing p and q from z and substituting into F. If F is not zero, an integration constant or sign is wrong. For Pfaffian integrability conditions, see 4.6.

Worked example

Question. Solve p q = z by Charpit's method, and verify the complete integral for a = 4, b = 1 at (x, y) = (0, 0).

Exam traps

  • Losing the minus signs in −dp and −dq. The denominators are F_x + pF_z and F_y + qF_z, so F_z = −1 changes the sign.
  • Forgetting F_z in the denominator. For F = p q − z, omitting F_z gives the wrong second relation and the wrong p, q.
  • Integrating dz = p dx + q dy while p and q still contain symbols. Substitute p and q as functions of x, y, z and a first.
  • Using only one constant. The first integral contributes a; the integration of dz contributes b. A complete integral needs both.
  • Dividing by p or q without noting the degenerate case. In p q = z, a = 0 forces p = 0 and z = 0, the trivial surface.

4.5 Cauchy problem for first order equations

The Cauchy problem for a first-order PDE asks for an integral surface z(x,y) that contains a given initial curve. The PDE is F(x,y,z,p,q)=0, where p=∂z/∂x and q=∂z/∂y. The curve is x=x₀(s), y=y₀(s), z=z₀(s). Along it we choose p₀,q₀ that satisfy F=0 and the strip condition. If the curve is not characteristic, the characteristics through the curve sweep out exactly one surface near it. This is how initial data on a boundary become a local solution, used when a first-order model starts from a measured curve.

You need Charpit's method for the characteristic equations, Lagrange's linear equation for the auxiliary system, and initial value ideas from Unit 1.

Data of the Cauchy problem

Write the PDE as F(x,y,z,p,q)=0. Here z is the unknown surface; p=∂z/∂x and q=∂z/∂y; F is a known function. The initial curve is given in parameter form x=x₀(s), y=y₀(s), z=z₀(s). The parameter s labels points on the curve. The curve lies in 3D space (x,y,z), and the surface must pass through every point of it.

The curve alone is not enough for a first-order PDE. The PDE contains p and q, so we must also give p₀(s)=p on the curve and q₀(s)=q on the curve. These are the derivative data. They must agree with the PDE and with the slope of z₀ along the curve. That agreement is the strip condition, covered next.

A pair (curve plus p₀,q₀) is called a strip. A strip is a narrow ribbon of surface that touches the curve with the correct first derivatives. The Cauchy problem asks for an integral surface containing this strip.

Strip condition and non-characteristic data

Differentiate z₀(s) along the curve. The chain rule gives dz₀/ds = p₀ dx₀/ds + q₀ dy₀/ds. This is the strip condition. It says the change of z along the initial curve must equal p times the change in x plus q times the change in y. Without it, p₀ and q₀ describe a different surface, not one containing the curve.

For each s, find p₀ and q₀ from two equations: F(x₀,y₀,z₀,p₀,q₀)=0 and the strip condition. Two equations in two unknowns usually give one or two branches. Each branch starts one family of characteristics. If the equations contradict, the data is inconsistent and no surface exists.

A curve is characteristic when its tangent (x₀′,y₀′) is parallel to (F_p,F_q) at every point. Then the initial curve is itself a characteristic. Characteristics starting on it stay on it, so they cannot sweep out a surface. The test for non-characteristic data is x₀′F_q − y₀′F_p ≠ 0. If this holds, a unique integral surface exists in a neighbourhood of the curve. Some syllabi call the whole construction Cauchy's method of characteristics.

Solving from the initial curve

Introduce a parameter t along each characteristic, with t=0 on the initial curve. Solve the characteristic ODEs.

dx/dt = F_p,  dy/dt = F_q,  dz/dt = p F_p + q F_q,  dp/dt = −(F_x + p F_z),  dq/dt = −(F_y + q F_z)

F_p=∂F/∂p and similarly for F_q,F_x,F_y,F_z; p=∂z/∂x, q=∂z/∂y; t is the parameter along a characteristic.

Start at x=x₀(s), y=y₀(s), z=z₀(s), p=p₀(s), q=q₀(s) when t=0. Integrate to get x(s,t), y(s,t), z(s,t). Eliminate s and t to write z as a function of x and y.

Near t=0, invert x,y to s,t when the Jacobian J=∂(x,y)/∂(s,t) is not zero. Then z=z(x,y) is the local integral surface. The condition J≠0 is the non-characteristic condition in coordinate form. If J=0, the data is characteristic or degenerate, and no unique surface follows.

The worked example below uses p+q=1 and the line x+y=0. The characteristic equations are simple because F_p=F_q=1 and p,q are constant along characteristics.

Worked example

Question. Solve the first-order PDE p+q=1 with the initial condition z=0 on the line x+y=0. Also find p and q on the solution and evaluate z at (x,y)=(1,3).

  1. 1. Write F=p+q−1=0. Parameterise the line x+y=0 by x₀=s, y₀=−s, z₀=0. Then dx₀/ds=1 and dy₀/ds=−1.
  2. 2. Strip condition: dz₀/ds=0, so 0=p₀(1)+q₀(−1)=p₀−q₀. Hence p₀=q₀. Put this in F=0: p₀+q₀−1=0, so 2p₀=1 and p₀=q₀=1/2.
  3. 3. Characteristic ODEs: dx/dt=F_p=1, dy/dt=F_q=1, dz/dt=pF_p+qF_q=p+q=1, dp/dt=0, dq/dt=0. Thus p=1/2 and q=1/2 along every characteristic.
  4. 4. Integrate from t=0: x=s+t, y=−s+t, z=t. Add the first two: x+y=2t, so t=(x+y)/2.
  5. 5. Therefore z=t=(x+y)/2. Check: p=∂z/∂x=1/2, q=∂z/∂y=1/2, so p+q=1. On x+y=0, z=0. At (1,3), z=(1+3)/2=2.

Answer. The solution is z=(x+y)/2, with p=q=1/2, and z(1,3)=2.

Exam traps

  • The initial curve alone is not enough: you must also supply p₀,q₀ satisfying F=0 and the strip condition. Many answers forget the strip and get inconsistent p₀,q₀.
  • Do not confuse the two parameters. s labels points on the initial curve; t moves along a characteristic. The final surface uses x,y after eliminating s,t.
  • Characteristic data gives no unique surface. Check x₀′F_q − y₀′F_p ≠ 0 before claiming uniqueness.
  • Sign error in the strip condition: dz₀/ds = p₀ dx₀/ds + q₀ dy₀/ds, not minus. If y₀(s) decreases, dy₀/ds is negative.
  • The solution is local. It is guaranteed only near the initial curve where the Jacobian is nonzero; do not extend blindly.

4.6 Total differential (Pfaffian) equations

A Pfaffian equation is a differential relation among three variables: P dx + Q dy + R dz = 0. It asks for a surface whose small displacements satisfy this relation. Most such equations have no surface. A curl-type test must hold first. When it does, the equation is integrable, and solutions form a family u(x,y,z)=c. Only a few universities teach this topic. It appears in thermodynamics, where heat and work differentials are often inexact, and in geometry, where small planes may or may not fit into a surface.

You need Lagrange's auxiliary system from 4.3 and exact differentials from Unit 1.

Pfaffian equation and its test

Here P, Q, R are smooth functions of x, y, z, not all zero. The symbols dx, dy, dz are small changes. A solution is a surface u(x,y,z)=c whose tangent displacements obey the equation. The necessary and sufficient local condition for an integrating factor is:

P(∂R/∂y − ∂Q/∂z) + Q(∂P/∂z − ∂R/∂x) + R(∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y) = 0

P, Q, R are the coefficients; each partial derivative holds the other two variables fixed.

Compute the six partial derivatives and substitute. A nonzero result means no family of surfaces. A zero result gives an integrating factor μ(x,y,z) such that μ(P dx+Q dy+R dz)=du. Then u=c gives the surfaces.

The test is one scalar equation, not three. A form can pass this test and still be non-exact, so integrability is weaker than exactness. The word local matters: P, Q, R must be smooth and not all zero near the point.

Exactness, solving, and geometry

Exactness is stronger. The form is exact when P=∂u/∂x, Q=∂u/∂y, R=∂u/∂z for one function u. Then ∂P/∂y=∂Q/∂x, ∂P/∂z=∂R/∂x, and ∂Q/∂z=∂R/∂y. Exact implies integrable. The converse fails: an integrable Pfaffian may need a multiplier before it becomes exact.

To solve, test integrability. Look for a multiplier by inspection: d(xy)=x dy+y dx, d(xyz)=yz dx+xz dy+xy dz, d(x²+y²+z²)=2x dx+2y dy+2z dz. If inspection fails, treat one variable as a parameter and solve the resulting ODE.

Geometrically, (P,Q,R) is normal to every allowed displacement (dx,dy,dz), because their dot product is zero. So solution surfaces u=c are orthogonal to curves tangent to (P,Q,R). Those curves satisfy dx/P=dy/Q=dz/R, <a href="#4.3">the auxiliary system of 4.3.

Worked example

Question. Solve yz dx + zx dy + xy dz = 0, and find the surface through the point (2,3,4).

  1. Identify P=yz, Q=zx, R=xy.
  2. Check exactness: ∂P/∂y=z=∂Q/∂x, ∂P/∂z=y=∂R/∂x, ∂Q/∂z=x=∂R/∂y.
  3. So the form is exact. Notice d(xyz)=yz dx+xz dy+xy dz.
  4. Integrate: u=xyz, hence xyz=c.
  5. At (2,3,4), c=2×3×4=24, so the surface is xyz=24.

Answer. The general solution is xyz=c; through (2,3,4) it is xyz=24.

Exam traps

  • Integrability is not exactness; a zero curl test does not give P=u_x, Q=u_y, R=u_z.
  • The six partials in exactness must match in pairs; checking only P_y=Q_x is not enough.
  • Do not drop the coefficient multiplying each bracket in the integrability condition.
  • P=0 at a point makes dx/P meaningless; use another variable or a multiplier.
  • A zero test guarantees only local surfaces; a single global surface may fail to exist where P, Q, R are not smooth.

Takeaways

  1. A relation with n arbitrary constants or functions needs n differentiations before the arbitrary elements can be removed, and that is how a PDE is formed.
  2. Each of the four standard types is solved by one fixed substitution that turns the PDE into an ODE.
  3. Lagrange's method reduces a linear PDE to two ordinary first integrals of dx/P = dy/Q = dz/R, and the answer is F(u, v) = 0.
  4. Charpit's method is the general tool: it works where the standard types fail, by building a second relation between p and q.
  5. Every solution of a first-order PDE is a surface, and each arbitrary constant in the complete integral gives a different member of the family.
  6. The Cauchy problem selects one surface out of the family by demanding that it contains a given initial curve.
  7. A Pfaffian equation P dx + Q dy + R dz = 0 is solvable only when the integrability (curl) condition holds; then it represents surfaces orthogonal to the curves dx/P = dy/Q = dz/R.
  8. Numerically, the method of lines converts the PDE into a system of ODEs in one variable and RK4 marches the initial curve forward.

Identify P = mz − ny, Q = nx − lz, R = ly − mx; Use multipliers l, m, n: lP + mQ + nR = 0, so l dx + m dy + n dz = 0; First integral: lx + my + nz = c₁; Use multipliers x, y, z: x dx + y dy + z dz = 0; Second integral: x² + y² + z² = c₂; General solution: F(lx + my + nz, x² + y² + z²) = 0.

Apply Using Charpit's method, solve p²x + q²y = z. 13 marks

Take f = p²x + q²y − z = 0, compute f_x = p², f_y = q², f_z = −1, f_p = 2px, f_q = 2qy; Write Charpit's chain and simplify to dp/p = dq/q (or the equivalent usable pair); Get p = aq, then substitute back: q²(ax + y) = z, so q = √(z/(ax + y)); Form dz = p dx + q dy and integrate; Report the complete integral with constants a and c.

Apply Solve the Pfaffian equation yz dx + zx dy + xy dz = 0 and interpret the answer geometrically. 12 marks

Test integrability, or note directly that the left side is d(xyz); So xyz = c is the solution; Each value of c gives one surface; together they form a family of surfaces; Verify by partial differentiation: P = u_x, Q = u_y, R = u_z with u = xyz.

Apply Solve p + q = 1 subject to z = x on the line y = 0 (a Cauchy problem). 13 marks

Characteristics satisfy dx/1 = dy/1 = dz/1; Two first integrals: x − y = c₁, z − x = c₂; General solution z = x + G(x − y); Impose z = x at y = 0: G(x) = 0, so z = x; Check that the initial curve is nowhere tangent to the characteristics, otherwise the data are not admissible.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze For the Pfaffian equation P dx + Q dy + R dz = 0, prove that its integral surfaces are orthogonal to the curves of the simultaneous system dx/P = dy/Q = dz/R, and use this to compare the two problems. 13 marks

Let the integral surface be u(x, y, z) = c, so du = u_x dx + u_y dy + u_z dz = 0 with (u_x, u_y, u_z) parallel to (P, Q, R); A tangent direction (dx, dy, dz) of the curve system is also parallel to (P, Q, R); The normal to the surface and the curve direction are therefore the same line, so the curves cut the surfaces at right angles; Conclude that solving the Pfaffian equation and solving the simultaneous equations are dual problems, related by the integrability condition.

Analyze Show that when the PDE is linear in p and q, Charpit's method reduces to Lagrange's method, and explain why Charpit is the more general tool. 13 marks

Take f = Pp + Qq − R with P, Q, R free of p and q; Then f_p = P, f_q = Q, and f_x + p f_z, f_y + q f_z give the Lagrange-type fractions; Show that the p and q equations become the same as the dx/P = dy/Q = dz/R chain; Conclude that Lagrange handles only the linear case; Charpit also covers nonlinear equations with no standard-type substitution.

Evaluate For the first-order PDE z_x = z_y with the initial data z = f(x) on y = 0, discuss existence, uniqueness and how the method of lines with fourth-order Runge–Kutta would compute the solution numerically. 13 marks

Characteristics: dx/1 = dy/(−1) = dz/0, so x + y = c and z is constant along them; Solution z = f(x + y) exists and is unique when f is differentiable; Method of lines: replace z_y by a finite difference on a y-grid, giving a system of ODEs in x (or vice versa) for the grid values; March with RK4 using step h; the local error per step is O(h⁵) and the global error is O(h⁴); Note that a discontinuity in f is carried along the characteristic x + y = constant, which can spoil the numerical solution.

Glossary

Arbitrary constant
A symbol standing for a fixed but unspecified number, removed from a relation by differentiation.
Arbitrary function
An unspecified function of a combination of variables, removed by differentiating with respect to each variable and eliminating.
Auxiliary equations
The simultaneous system dx/P = dy/Q = dz/R used to solve a Lagrange equation.
Cauchy problem
The problem of finding the integral surface of a PDE that contains a prescribed initial curve.
Characteristic
A curve along which the PDE reduces to ordinary differential equations.
Charpit's method
A general method that builds a second relation between p and q and then integrates dz = p dx + q dy.
Clairaut's form
The PDE z = px + qy + f(p, q), whose complete integral is z = ax + by + f(a, b).
Complete integral
A solution containing two independent arbitrary constants for a PDE in two independent variables.
Degree
The exponent of the highest-order derivative after the equation is cleared of radicals and fractions in derivatives.
Exact differential
A form P dx + Q dy + R dz that equals du for some function u.
General integral
The solution written as F(u, v) = 0, where F is an arbitrary function and u, v are first integrals.
Initial curve
A given space curve through which the required integral surface must pass.
Integrability condition
The identity P(R_y − Q_z) + Q(P_z − R_x) + R(Q_x − P_y) = 0 that a Pfaffian form must satisfy.
Integral surface
The surface z = φ(x, y) formed by the graph of a solution of the PDE.
Lagrange's equation
A first-order PDE linear in p and q, written Pp + Qq = R.
Linear PDE
A PDE in which the unknown and all its derivatives appear only to the first power and never multiplied together.
Method of characteristics
Solving a first-order PDE by following the characteristic curves from the initial data.
Method of lines
Replacing the derivatives in one variable by finite differences so the PDE becomes a system of ODEs in the other variable.
Multiplier
A factor used with the auxiliary equations so that the sum of the numerators becomes an exact differential.
Order
The order of the highest partial derivative appearing in the equation.
Partial differential equation
An equation relating an unknown function of several variables to its partial derivatives.
Particular integral
A solution obtained from the complete integral by giving fixed numerical values to its arbitrary constants.
Pfaffian equation
An equation P dx + Q dy + R dz = 0 relating three variables through a total differential.
Singular solution
A solution not obtainable from the complete integral by fixing constants; it is the envelope of the family of surfaces.
