Higher order linear ODEs look long, but they are built from two cheap pieces: a complementary function from the roots of a polynomial, and a particular integral from the shape of the right-hand side. 2.1 Linear ODEs with constant coefficients; characteristic equation

Many engineering systems have equations like y″ + 5y′ + 6y = 0: the unknown y and its derivatives appear linearly, and every coefficient is a constant. The trick that solves all such equations is to try y = e^(mx). Derivatives bring down powers of m, the exponential cancels, and what remains is an ordinary polynomial. Its roots tell you the complete solution. The same algebra appears in circuit transients, spring-mass-damper vibration and control-system response. This chapter handles only the homogeneous case, where the right side is zero.

You need first-order linear equations from Unit 1, school-level complex numbers, quadratic roots, and the idea of independent solutions (see 2.3).

The substitution y = e^(mx)

Write a homogeneous linear ODE with constant coefficients as shown below. Here y⁽ᵏ⁾ is the k-th derivative, each aₖ is a real constant, aₙ ≠ 0, and n is the order. The right side is zero because the equation is homogeneous.

aₙy⁽ⁿ⁾ + aₙ₋₁y⁽ⁿ⁻¹⁾ + … + a₁y′ + a₀y = 0

y⁽ᵏ⁾ is the k-th derivative of y; aₖ are real constants; aₙ ≠ 0; n is the order.

Try y = e^(mx). Then y′ = m e^(mx), y″ = m² e^(mx), and in general y⁽ᵏ⁾ = mᵏ e^(mx). Substitute these into the ODE.

e^(mx)(aₙmⁿ + aₙ₋₁mⁿ⁻¹ + … + a₁m + a₀) = 0

m is the trial exponent; e^(mx) is never zero, so the bracket must be zero.

Cancel e^(mx). The bracket is a polynomial equation of degree n. It is called the characteristic equation, also called the auxiliary equation. It has n roots counted with multiplicity, real or complex.

aₙmⁿ + aₙ₋₁mⁿ⁻¹ + … + a₁m + a₀ = 0

m is a root; the polynomial has degree n; its n roots generate the solution.

Three root types give three terms

Distinct real roots m₁, …, mₙ each give one exponential. The n terms are independent, so the complementary function is a sum with n arbitrary constants.

y_c = C₁e^(m₁x) + C₂e^(m₂x) + … + Cₙe^(mₙx)

m₁, …, mₙ are distinct real roots; C₁, …, Cₙ are arbitrary constants; y_c is the complementary function.

A root m repeated r times needs r independent terms. The first is e^(mx). The others are x e^(mx), x² e^(mx), up to x^(r−1)e^(mx). The x factor produces a new independent solution.

y_c = e^(mx)(C₁ + C₂x + … + Cᵣx^(r−1))

m is the repeated root; r is its multiplicity; C₁, …, Cᵣ are arbitrary constants.

Complex roots of a real polynomial come in conjugate pairs α ± iβ. Euler's formula e^(iθ)=cos θ + i sin θ turns the pair into real sine and cosine terms. The real part α stays in an exponential factor.

y_c = e^(αx)(C₁ cos βx + C₂ sin βx)

α is the real part; β is the imaginary part, with β ≠ 0; C₁ and C₂ are arbitrary constants; the roots are α ± iβ.

Building the complementary function

The complementary function, C.F., is the general solution of the homogeneous equation. It contains exactly n arbitrary constants, one for each root counted with multiplicity. Follow these steps.

  1. Write the ODE in standard form with all y-terms on the left and zero on the right.
  2. Replace y⁽ᵏ⁾ by mᵏ to form the characteristic polynomial.
  3. Solve the polynomial completely, grouping repeated roots and complex pairs.
  4. Write one term for each root or pair using the three rules, and attach constants C₁, C₂, ….
  5. Substitute back to check; the constants stay free until initial conditions are given.

The order n fixes the number of constants. A third-order equation has three constants. Two separate terms C₁e^(mx)+C₂e^(mx) for a repeated root collapse into one constant, so they cannot supply the missing independent solution. Non-homogeneous equations add a particular integral; see 2.2 and 2.4. Variable coefficients need other methods; see 2.6.

Worked example

Question. Solve y‴ − 6y″ + 11y′ − 6y = 0.

  1. Assume y = e^(mx). Then y′ = m e^(mx), y″ = m² e^(mx), and y‴ = m³ e^(mx).
  2. Substitute and cancel e^(mx): m³ − 6m² + 11m − 6 = 0.
  3. Test m = 1: 1 − 6 + 11 − 6 = 0. So m − 1 is a factor.
  4. Divide the cubic by m − 1. Synthetic coefficients 1, −6, 11, −6 with root 1 give quotient coefficients 1, −5, 6, remainder 0.
  5. Division detail: bring down 1. Multiply by 1 → 1. Add to −6 → −5. Multiply by 1 → −5. Add to 11 → 6. Multiply by 1 → 6. Add to −6 → 0.
  6. The quotient is m² − 5m + 6 = (m − 2)(m − 3).
  7. Roots: m₁ = 1, m₂ = 2, m₃ = 3. All are real and distinct.
  8. Each root gives one exponential: y = C₁eˣ + C₂e²ˣ + C₃e³ˣ, with C₁, C₂, C₃ arbitrary constants.

Answer. The general solution is y = C₁eˣ + C₂e²ˣ + C₃e³ˣ, with three arbitrary constants.

Exam traps

  • For a repeated root m, writing C₁e^(mx)+C₂e^(mx) is wrong. It is one term. Use e^(mx)(C₁+C₂x).
  • For complex roots α ± iβ, keep the e^(αx) factor. Dropping it leaves only e^(±iβx) and misses growth or decay.
  • Move every term to the left before reading coefficients. A sign slip in a₀ changes the roots.
  • Count constants. A fourth-order homogeneous ODE needs four arbitrary constants before initial conditions.
  • Do not use the characteristic polynomial for variable coefficients; that method needs constant coefficients (see 2.6 for Euler equations).

2.2 Particular integrals for exponential, sine, cosine and polynomial terms

Every linear ODE with constant coefficients splits into two jobs: solve the homogeneous equation, then find one extra solution that matches the right-hand side. Chapter 2.1 did the first job. This chapter does the second for four common shapes — an exponential e^(ax), a sine or cosine, and a polynomial — using a short operator rule that replaces guessing. Write the equation as f(D)y = R(x); the particular integral is (1/f(D))R(x), evaluated by inspection. A sum of such terms is handled by adding the separate answers.

You need: Chapter 2.1, especially the roots of the characteristic equation and what the complementary function is.

Write the equation with D, then invert the operator

Write D for d/dx, so Dy = y′ and D²y = y″. A linear ODE with constant coefficients becomes f(D)y = R(x). Here f is the same polynomial whose roots gave the complementary function in 2.1: for y″ − 3y′ + 2y = R(x), f(D) = D² − 3D + 2. The term R(x) is called the forcing term.

The C.F. solves f(D)y = 0. One more solution, the particular integral (P.I.), must supply R(x). Write it as y_p = (1/f(D))·R(x). That is not a fraction — it is shorthand for the operator that undoes f(D). Its value is that for four shapes of R(x) you can evaluate it by inspection, with no guessing.

y_p = (1/f(D)) R(x)

f(D) is the operator polynomial, R(x) the forcing term, and y_p any one solution of the full equation.

Exponential rule. Since D e^(ax) = a e^(ax), every power of D just multiplies by a, so f(D)e^(ax) = f(a)e^(ax). Divide and the answer is read off.

y_p = e^(ax) / f(a), f(a) ≠ 0

a is the constant in the exponent (it may be negative) and f(a) is f evaluated at a — a plain number, not an operator.

Failure case. If f(a) = 0 then a is a root of f(m) = 0, e^(ax) already sits in the C.F., and the formula divides by zero. Multiply by x once for each time a repeats as a root.

y_p = x^r e^(ax) / f^(r)(a)

r is the multiplicity of a as a root of f(m) = 0 and f^(r) is the r-th derivative of f. The usual case r = 1 needs f′(a).

Clash example: y″ − 3y′ + 2y = e^(2x). Roots are 1 and 2, so r = 1. f′(D) = 2D − 3 and f′(2) = 1, giving y_p = x e^(2x). Substituting back: (4 + 4x) − 3(1 + 2x) + 2x = 1. Correct.

Sine, cosine and polynomial right-hand sides

Sine and cosine rule. Because D² sin(ax + b) = −a² sin(ax + b) and the same holds for cosine, replace every D² in f(D) by −a² and simplify. If a lone D survives, multiply top and bottom by the conjugate (the same expression with the sign of D flipped). That recreates a D², replace it again, and the denominator becomes a real number.

y_p = (1/f(D)) sin(ax + b), with D² → −a²

a is the coefficient of x inside the bracket (the frequency) and b is the phase constant; the same rule serves cos(ax + b).

If the denominator becomes zero, ±ia are roots of f and both sin(ax + b) and cos(ax + b) are in the C.F. Then the P.I. is x times the result of using f′(D) in place of f(D).

Polynomial rule. For R a polynomial of degree n, divide 1 by f(D) as a long division in powers of D, keeping terms only up to D^n. Apply term by term, using D^k x^n = n!/(n−k)! · x^(n−k) when k ≤ n, and 0 when k > n. This needs f(0) ≠ 0.

1/f(D) = b₀ + b₁D + b₂D² + …

b₀ = 1/f(0), and later b's follow from multiplying out f(D)(b₀ + b₁D + …) = 1 and matching powers of D.

Quick check: for y″ + y = x, f(D) = D² + 1, so 1/(1 + D²) = 1 − D² + …, and y_p = x − 0 = x. If f(0) = 0 the series cannot start; use the trial method of 2.4 instead.

Sums, and building the final answer

Superposition. The operator is linear, so if R = R₁ + R₂ then y_p = y_p1 + y_p2. Solve each piece separately and add. The P.I. never carries new arbitrary constants; the only constants are C₁, C₂, … sitting in the C.F.

  • Count multiplicity from the roots of the C.F., never from the forcing term.

Worked example

Question. Solve y″ − 3y′ + 2y = e^(3x) + x. Give the general solution and verify the particular integral at x = 0.

  1. Characteristic equation: m² − 3m + 2 = 0, that is (m − 1)(m − 2) = 0, so m = 1, 2 and the C.F. is C₁e^x + C₂e^(2x).
  2. Clash check: for e^(3x), a = 3 and f(3) = 9 − 9 + 2 = 2 ≠ 0. For the polynomial x, f(0) = 2 ≠ 0. No clash, so no factor x anywhere.

  4. Polynomial part: try y_p2 = Bx + C. Then y′ = B and y″ = 0, so f(D)y_p2 = −3B + 2Bx + 2C = 2Bx + (−3B + 2C). Compare with x = 1·x + 0: 2B = 1 gives B = 1/2; then −3B + 2C = 0 gives 2C = 3/2, so C = 3/4.

  • No factor x when it is needed. For e^(2x) against roots 1 and 2, the trial A e^(2x) gives 0 = e^(2x) and goes nowhere; it must be A x e^(2x).

2.3 Linearly independent solutions and the Wronskian

A second order linear ODE needs two solutions before you can write its general solution. Not any two: they must be genuinely different, not one a constant multiple of the other. The Wronskian is one determinant that answers this yes-or-no question, and Abel's formula makes the answer cheap — check it at a single point of the interval. Every exam question that asks for a general solution, and every solver that builds one, rests on this test.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for how y = e^(mx) produces solutions, and 2×2 determinants from school algebra.

Linear dependence on an interval

Take functions y₁, y₂, …, y_n defined on an interval I. They are linearly dependent on I if you can find constants c₁, c₂, …, c_n, not all zero, such that c₁y₁ + c₂y₂ + … + c_n y_n = 0 for every x in I. If the only choice is c₁ = c₂ = … = c_n = 0, the functions are linearly independent on I.

The phrase 'for every x in I' carries the whole definition. Two functions may agree at one point and still be independent; dependence is a statement about the entire interval.

Example. y₁ = x and y₂ = 3x are dependent on any interval, because 3·x − 1·(3x) = 0 everywhere. y₁ = eˣ and y₂ = e²ˣ are independent: c₁eˣ + c₂e²ˣ = 0 for all x forces c₁ = c₂ = 0.

Superposition makes this the right question. If y₁ and y₂ solve a homogeneous linear ODE, so does every combination c₁y₁ + c₂y₂. The solutions form a vector space, and independence counts its dimension. A second order linear homogeneous ODE has a two-dimensional solution space; an nth order one has dimension n. So n independent solutions are exactly enough to describe every solution.

The Wronskian: one determinant

Build a table. Row 1 holds the functions, row 2 their first derivatives, row 3 their second derivatives, up to derivative n−1. The Wronskian W(y₁, …, y_n) is the determinant of that n×n table.

W(y₁, y₂) = y₁y₂′ − y₁′y₂

y₁ and y₂ are the two functions on I, and the prime means d/dx.

For three functions the table is three rows deep: rows y, y′, y″. The determinant of that 3×3 table is W.

The test. If W(x₀) ≠ 0 at even one point x₀ of I, the functions are linearly independent on I. Reason: suppose c₁y₁ + c₂y₂ = 0. Differentiate to get c₁y₁′ + c₂y₂′ = 0. At x₀ these are two linear equations in c₁ and c₂ whose determinant is W(x₀). A nonzero determinant forces c₁ = c₂ = 0.

Warning. The reverse fails for arbitrary functions. On (−1, 1) take y₁ = x² and y₂ = x|x|. They are independent, yet W = 0 at every point. So W = 0 alone does not prove dependence in general. For solutions of a linear ODE the story is cleaner — that is the next section.

Abel's formula and fundamental sets

Write the second order equation as y″ + P(x)y′ + Q(x)y = 0, with P and Q continuous on I. Abel's formula says W(x) = W₀ exp(−∫P dx), where W₀ = W(x₀) and the integral runs from x₀ to x.

Quick derivation: W′ = y₁y₂″ − y₁″y₂. Replace each y″ by −Py′ − Qy. The Q terms cancel, leaving W′ = −P W, a first order separable equation whose solution is the formula.

Consequence: exp is never zero, so W is either zero at every point of I or at no point. Checking one value settles the whole interval. For an nth order equation the same formula holds with P the coefficient of y^(n−1), after dividing by the leading coefficient.

Fundamental set. Any n solutions of an nth order linear homogeneous ODE with W ≠ 0 form a fundamental set. Every solution is y = c₁y₁ + … + c_n y_n, the general solution. Given n initial conditions y(x₀), y′(x₀), …, y^(n−1)(x₀), the constants solve a linear system whose determinant is W(x₀) ≠ 0, so they are unique. With variable coefficients, see Chapter 2.8 for how one known solution produces the second.

Worked example

Question. For y₁ = eˣ and y₂ = e²ˣ, form the Wronskian. Evaluate it at x = 0 and x = 1 and decide whether the pair is independent. Then repeat for y₁ = x and y₂ = 3x.

  1. Set up the determinant: W = |y₁ y₂; y₁′ y₂′| = y₁y₂′ − y₁′y₂.
  2. Differentiate: y₁′ = eˣ and y₂′ = 2e²ˣ.
  3. Substitute: W = eˣ(2e²ˣ) − eˣ(e²ˣ) = 2e³ˣ − e³ˣ = e³ˣ.
  4. At x = 0: W = e⁰ = 1. Nonzero, so eˣ and e²ˣ are linearly independent on every interval.
  5. At x = 1: W = e³ ≈ 20.0855. Also nonzero, the same conclusion.
  6. Second pair: y₁′ = 1 and y₂′ = 3, so W = x·3 − 1·3x = 0 for every x. The pair is dependent, since 3·x + (−1)·3x = 0.

Answer. W = e³ˣ ≠ 0 for the exponential pair, so it is independent; W = 0 for x and 3x, so that pair is dependent.

Exam traps

  • Reading W = 0 at one point as dependence. For solutions of a linear ODE this is safe because W is zero everywhere or nowhere; for arbitrary functions it is false, as x² and x|x| show.
  • Writing W = y₁′y₂ − y₁y₂′ and then using W = W₀exp(−∫P dx). Both orders appear in textbooks. This book fixes W = y₁y₂′ − y₁′y₂, so keep that choice in every step. A sign flip changes the sign of W but not whether W is zero, so independence verdicts are unchanged — just stay consistent.
  • Using the coefficient of y″ instead of y′ in Abel's formula. Divide the ODE by its leading coefficient first.
  • Judging independence by appearance. cos 2x and 2cos²x − 1 look different but are the same function; only the determinant decides.
  • Giving only two rows for three functions. The Wronskian of y₁, y₂, y₃ needs rows y, y′, y″.

2.4 Method of undetermined coefficients

The method of undetermined coefficients finds one particular solution of a constant-coefficient linear ODE when the right-hand side is built from polynomials, exponentials, sines, cosines, and their products or sums. You write a trial solution of the same shape with unknown constants, substitute it into the ODE, and compare coefficients. It replaces guessing by bookkeeping and gives the fast route to forced-response answers in circuits, vibrations, and control problems. General right-hand sides need Chapter 2.5.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the characteristic equation and Chapter 2.2 for the basic trial shapes.

The trial copies R(x)

To solve a non-homogeneous linear ODE with constant coefficients, split the answer as y = y_h + y_p. Here y_h is the complementary function from the homogeneous equation, and y_p is any one particular integral. The method of undetermined coefficients builds y_p by copying the shape of the right-hand side R(x). You write a trial with unknown constants A, B, C, … and fix them by substitution. The method works because differentiating a polynomial, an exponential eax, or a sine or cosine returns the same family of functions.

If R(x) is a polynomial Pn(x)=b₀+b₁x+…+bnxⁿ, the trial must include every power down to the constant, not only xⁿ. A degree-n polynomial needs n+1 constants. If R(x)=eaxPn(x), the trial is eax times a full polynomial. A lone sin ax or cos ax needs both A cos ax and B sin ax; a sine alone is not enough because differentiating sine gives cosine. A product such as x²e3x needs the product of the two trials. A sum needs the sum of the trials.

Products, sums, and the x repair

Two repair rules handle resonance. First solve the homogeneous equation using the characteristic equation (Chapter 2.1). If no part of the trial is already in y_h, use the trial as written. If a term is already a solution of the homogeneous equation, multiply the whole trial by x, and repeat as many times as the corresponding root repeats. For R(x)=eaxPn(x), the multiplier is xs, where s is the multiplicity of a as a root of the characteristic equation. For R(x)=sin ax or cos ax, the test uses the complex root ai: if ai solves the characteristic equation, multiply the whole trial by x. Multiplying only the top term is wrong; the whole trial moves.

Shape examples: for R(x)=x²e3x with no 3 among the roots, try e3x(Ax²+Bx+C). For R(x)=ex with a simple root 1, try Axex. For R(x)=sin 2x with ±2i roots, try x(A cos 2x+B sin 2x). For a sum R(x)=x+e2x, solve two separate trials and add them. For a product R(x)=e2xcos 3x, try e2x(A cos 3x+B sin 3x).

  1. Solve the homogeneous equation and list its roots.
  2. Write R(x) as a sum or product of allowed shapes.
  3. Write the basic trial for each shape.
  4. Apply xs if the trial already solves the homogeneous equation.
  5. Substitute the trial into the ODE.
  6. Collect polynomial, exponential, sine, and cosine terms.
  7. Compare coefficients and solve for the constants.
  8. Add yp to yh.

Where the method applies

The method is limited to constant-coefficient linear ODEs. The right-hand side must be a finite sum or product of polynomials, exponentials eax, sines, cosines, and constants. Functions such as tan x, sec x, ln x, 1/x, √x, or a general unknown f(x) do not have a finite trial family. Variable-coefficient equations also fall outside the method. For those, use variation of parameters in Chapter 2.5; for equations with x-dependent coefficients, see Chapters 2.6 and 2.8. The method remains the quickest check in forced linear systems: an AC source gives sine or cosine forcing, a switching supply gives exponential forcing, and a constant load gives polynomial forcing.

2.5 Method of variation of parameters

Some right-hand sides — sec x, tan x, ln x, 1/x — have no trial shape, so undetermined coefficients cannot touch them. Variation of parameters does not guess at all. It takes the two constants in the complementary function, turns them into functions of x, imposes two conditions, and reduces the problem to two integrals. It works for constant and variable coefficients alike and needs only that the right-hand side be continuous and the two homogeneous solutions independent. Control theory, circuit analysis and Green's function work all use it, because real forcing terms are rarely pure sines or exponentials.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the complementary function, Chapter 2.3 for the Wronskian, and standard integrals including ∫tan x dx = −ln|cos x|.

Let the two constants move

Undetermined coefficients needs a right-hand side shaped like eᵏˣ, sin kx, cos kx or a polynomial — see Chapter 2.4. A right-hand side such as sec x, tan x, ln x or 1/x has no such shape, so that method stops dead.

Variation of parameters has no such limit. Write the equation in standard form: y″ + P(x)y′ + Q(x)y = R(x), where P, Q and R are functions of x only. Its complementary function from Chapter 2.1 is y_c = C₁y₁ + C₂y₂, where y₁ and y₂ are two independent solutions of the homogeneous equation and C₁, C₂ are arbitrary constants.

The idea: the constants are the only free part of y_c, so let them vary with x. Put C₁ = u₁(x) and C₂ = u₂(x) and try y_p = u₁y₁ + u₂y₂. Two unknown functions need two conditions. The differential equation supplies only one; the second we are free to invent, and we invent it to make the algebra short.

Differentiate: y_p′ = u₁y₁′ + u₂y₂′ + (u₁′y₁ + u₂′y₂). Now impose condition 1, u₁′y₁ + u₂′y₂ = 0. Then y_p′ = u₁y₁′ + u₂y₂′, exactly the shape of y_c′. Differentiate again: y_p″ = u₁y₁″ + u₂y₂″ + u₁′y₁′ + u₂′y₂′.

Substitute y_p, y_p′ and y_p″ into the equation and collect the u₁ terms: u₁(y₁″ + Py₁′ + Qy₁) = 0, because y₁ solves the homogeneous equation. The u₂ terms vanish the same way. What survives is condition 2: u₁′y₁′ + u₂′y₂′ = R.

Solving the pair: the Wronskian appears

The two conditions form a linear system in the unknowns u₁′ and u₂′:

u₁′y₁ + u₂′y₂ = 0, u₁′y₁′ + u₂′y₂′ = R

y₁, y₂ are the two independent solutions of the homogeneous equation; R is the right-hand side after division by the leading coefficient; both equations hold for each x in the interval.

The determinant of this system is W = y₁y₂′ − y₁′y₂, the Wronskian from Chapter 2.3. Because y₁ and y₂ are independent, W ≠ 0, so Cramer's rule gives a unique answer.

u₁′ = −y₂R/W, u₂′ = y₁R/W

W = y₁y₂′ − y₁′y₂ is the Wronskian; R is the right-hand side of the standard form; the minus sign sits on u₁′ only.

Check the first condition: u₁′y₁ + u₂′y₂ = (−y₂Ry₁ + y₁Ry₂)/W = 0. It holds automatically, as it must.

  1. Write the equation as y″ + Py′ + Qy = R; divide by the leading coefficient if it is not 1.
  2. Solve the homogeneous equation and name the two independent solutions y₁ and y₂.
  3. Compute W = y₁y₂′ − y₁′y₂ and confirm W ≠ 0 on the interval.
  4. Form u₁′ = −y₂R/W and u₂′ = y₁R/W.
  5. Integrate both; drop the constants of integration.
  6. Write y_p = u₁y₁ + u₂y₂ and add it to y_c for the general solution.

What it costs

Undetermined coefficients is faster whenever it applies: it needs no integration. Variation of parameters always applies — any continuous R, constant or variable coefficients — but the price is two integrals, and those integrals are not always elementary. For R = e^(x²) the integrals have no closed form in familiar functions; the answer is then left as a definite integral, which is still a correct particular integral.

If the equation arrives as a₀(x)y″ + a₁(x)y′ + a₂(x)y = f(x), divide through by a₀(x) first. Then R = f/a₀, and the numerators carry f/a₀ rather than f. Skipping this step is the single most common mistake in the method.

The method extends unchanged to order n: n conditions, an n × n Wronskian determinant in the denominator, and n integrals. For second order the formulas above are all you need to memorise.

2.6 Cauchy-Euler and Legendre linear equations

A Cauchy-Euler equation has powers of x multiplying the derivatives: x²y″ + ⋯. The power of x matches the order of the derivative, so the substitution x = e^t makes every coefficient constant. You solve the new constant-coefficient equation in t and replace e^{kt} by x^k. Such equations model a bar with varying cross-section, radial heat flow and rotating shafts, where the coefficient scales with distance.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for the characteristic equation and Chapter 2.2 for particular integrals; also the laws of indices and logarithms.

Why the substitution works

A Cauchy-Euler equation (also called Euler-Cauchy or equidimensional) has the form

aₙxⁿy⁽ⁿ⁾ + ⋯ + a₂x²y″ + a₁xy′ + a₀y = f(x)

aₙ, …, a₀ are constants; y⁽ⁿ⁾ is the n-th derivative; f(x) is the right-hand side. The equation is equidimensional because every derivative term carries the same total power of x as the derivative order.

For x > 0 put x = e^t, so t = ln x and θ = d/dt. Since dx/dt = e^t = x, the chain rule gives dy/dx = (1/x)dy/dt. Hence xy′ = θy. Differentiate once more: x²y″ = θ(θ−1)y. In general x^k y⁽ᵏ⁾ = θ(θ−1)⋯(θ−k+1)y. Each power of x disappears, leaving a constant-coefficient operator in θ.

For x < 0, put x = −e^t and t = ln(−x); the operator formulas are the same and answers use |x|. Most exam questions state x > 0.

Solving in t, then returning to x

The substitution changes the equation but not the method. Solve the constant-coefficient equation in t using Chapter 2.1 and Chapter 2.2. Then translate back.

  1. Write x = e^t, θ = d/dt, and replace xy′ by θy, x²y″ by θ(θ−1)y, and so on.
  2. Replace x on the right by e^t, so f(x) becomes f(e^t).
  3. Find the complementary function in t from the characteristic equation in θ.
  4. Find a particular integral in t by any method from Chapters 2.2 to 2.5.
  5. Replace every e^{kt} by x^k and every t by ln x. The answer is in x.

Repeated roots add t e^{mt}, which becomes (ln x)x^m. Complex roots α ± iβ give x^α[C₁ cos(β ln x) + C₂ sin(β ln x)]. The final solution is valid on the interval x > 0 used for the logarithm.

Legendre linear equations and the x^m shortcut

A Legendre linear equation has powers of a linear expression a + bx instead of powers of x:

Aₙ(a + bx)ⁿy⁽ⁿ⁾ + ⋯ + A₁(a + bx)y′ + A₀y = f(x)

A₀, …, Aₙ and a, b are constants, with b ≠ 0; y⁽ⁿ⁾ is the n-th derivative; f(x) is the right-hand side. This is not Legendre's polynomial equation; that topic belongs to Unit 3.

Put a + bx = e^t, so t = ln(a + bx) and θ = d/dt. Since dt/dx = b/(a + bx), we have d/dx = (b/(a + bx)) d/dt, equivalently (a + bx)d/dx = bθ. Hence (a + bx)y′ = bθy. Also (a + bx)²y″ = b²θ(θ−1)y. The equation again becomes constant-coefficient in t. Use f((e^t − a)/b) on the right; after solving, replace t by ln(a + bx), with a + bx > 0.

For the homogeneous part there is a shortcut: try y = x^m in a Cauchy-Euler equation, or y = (a + bx)^m in a Legendre equation. For a Legendre equation with b ≠ 1, substitution gives b^k factors; the polynomial in m is the same as the θ-characteristic polynomial, including those b^k factors, not the plain Cauchy-Euler polynomial. For example, x²y″ + xy′ − y = 0 gives m(m−1) + m − 1 = (m−1)(m+1) = 0, so y = C₁x + C₂/x. This shortcut finds only the complementary function; a right-hand side still needs a particular integral.

2.7 Simultaneous linear ODEs with constant coefficients

Two unknown functions x(t) and y(t) are tied by two ODEs with constant coefficients. Write D = d/dt. Neither equation solves alone. Elimination applies an operator, substitutes the other equation, and leaves one higher-order ODE in one unknown. Solve it, then back-substitute for the second. Coupled circuits, two-tank mixing, and two-mass oscillations give this shape. Only a few universities teach this topic.

You need: Solving a constant-coefficient ODE from the characteristic equation (2.1) and particular integrals (2.2).

Two equations, two unknowns

A simultaneous system means two linear ODEs in two unknown functions of the same t, all coefficients constant. Write D = d/dt. A typical pair:

Dx + 2y = 3t, 3x + Dy = 0

x and y are unknown functions of t; D = d/dt; 2 and 3 are constants; 3t and 0 are known functions of t.

The order is the total of highest derivative orders, here 1 + 1 = 2. The general solution therefore carries two arbitrary constants in all, not two per equation. That count is the quick completeness check.

Matrix and Laplace methods also handle such pairs and scale to many equations. Elimination is the hand method for two: it gives one higher-order ODE, which 2.1 solves.

Elimination step by step

  1. Rewrite both equations with D, unknowns left, known functions right.
  2. Pick the unknown to eliminate, usually the simplest coefficient.
  3. Apply an operator to one equation so its coefficient matches the other's.
  4. Subtract or add to cancel that unknown; one ODE remains.
  5. Solve the remaining ODE; list every constant.
  6. Substitute back into the simpler original equation for the second unknown.

(P₁Q₂ − P₂Q₁)x = Q₂f − Q₁g

the system is P₁(D)x + Q₁(D)y = f(t) and P₂(D)x + Q₂(D)y = g(t); P₁, Q₁, P₂, Q₂ are polynomials in D with constant coefficients; f, g are known functions of t.

Operators with constant coefficients commute and multiply like polynomials. Here P₁Q₂ − P₂Q₁ = D·D − 3·2 = D² − 6 and Q₂f − Q₁g = D(3t) − 2·0 = 3, so x″ − 6x = 3.

Back-substitution and the constant count

Once x(t) is known, an original equation gives y: from Dx + 2y = 3t, y = (3t − Dx)/2. Differentiating known x supplies Dx; no new constant appears.

  • A new constant means you integrated instead of substituting; return to the algebraic original.
  • Check by putting both x and y into the unused equation.
  • If the operator determinant is zero, the equations are dependent (infinitely many solutions) or inconsistent (no solution); check which before dividing.

2.8 Second order linear ODEs with variable coefficients

With variable coefficients, the multipliers in front of y″, y′ and y change with x. You cannot write e^{mx} and get a polynomial. The chapter gives a practical route: if one nonzero solution is known, one integration supplies the second. The Wronskian then confirms independence, and variation of parameters gives the particular integral. This is used when a model has drifting damping, a variable spring, or a coefficient like 1/x, and when a textbook asks for a second solution after handing you the first.

You need: Chapter 2.3 for independence and the Wronskian, Chapter 2.5 for the particular-integral formula, and confident integration by substitution and parts.

Put it in standard form first

Start with a₀(x)y″ + a₁(x)y′ + a₂(x)y = f(x). At every x where a₀(x) ≠ 0, divide the whole equation by a₀(x). This gives the standard form y″ + P(x)y′ + Q(x)y = R(x). Here P = a₁/a₀, Q = a₂/a₀ and R = f/a₀. All formulas below use this form.

Suppose one nonzero solution y₁ of the homogeneous equation y″ + P y′ + Q y = 0 is known. Put y = v(x)y₁(x). Substitute y, y′, y″. The terms containing v cancel because y₁ solves the homogeneous equation. What remains is y₁v″ + (2y₁′ + P y₁)v′ = 0. Let w = v′. Then w′/w = −(2y₁′/y₁ + P). Integrate: w = C e−∫P dx/y₁². Integrate again to get v. The second solution is y₂ = y₁ ∫ e−∫P dx/y₁² dx. The constant C only multiplies y₂ by a number, so ignore it.

y₂ = y₁ ∫ e−∫P dx / y₁² dx

y₁ is the known nonzero solution; P is the coefficient of y′ in standard form; the inner ∫P dx is any antiderivative; the outer integral gives v.

  1. Divide by a₀(x) before reading P.
  2. Check that y₁ solves the homogeneous equation.
  3. Compute e−∫P dx and divide by y₁².
  4. Integrate once, multiply by y₁, and drop constant multiples.

Wronskian and variation of parameters

Abel's identity states W = y₁y₂′ − y₁′y₂ = W₀ e−∫P dx. W₀ is the value of the Wronskian at any convenient point; e−∫P dx is the same integrating factor as above. If W₀ ≠ 0, then W ≠ 0 everywhere, and y₁, y₂ are independent. If W₀ = 0, the two solutions are dependent; y₂ is not a second solution. See Chapter 2.3.

For the nonhomogeneous equation y″ + P y′ + Q y = R, the complementary function is y_c = C₁y₁ + C₂y₂. Variation of parameters, from Chapter 2.5, sets y_p = u₁y₁ + u₂y₂. Impose u₁′y₁ + u₂′y₂ = 0 and u₁′y₁′ + u₂′y₂′ = R. Solve the two linear equations: u₁′ = −y₂R/W and u₂′ = y₁R/W. Integrate to get u₁, u₂, then y_p. The full solution is y = C₁y₁ + C₂y₂ + y_p. The W in the denominator is the Wronskian of y₁ and y₂. If R = 0, then u₁′ = u₂′ = 0 and y_p = 0, as expected.

u₁′ = −y₂R/W, u₂′ = y₁R/W

W is the Wronskian of y₁ and y₂; R is the right-hand side after standard form; y_p = u₁y₁ + u₂y₂.

When a closed form exists

Most variable-coefficient equations do not have elementary closed forms. Two families do. A Cauchy-Euler equation has the shape x²y″ + a x y′ + b y = f(x); the substitution x = e^t, or t = ln x, turns it into a constant-coefficient equation. See Chapter 2.6. Some other equations become constant-coefficient after a suitable substitution; check for that before integrating. If no substitution works, use a power series about an ordinary point or a regular singular point, which is Unit 3. For numbers rather than formulas, use a numerical method such as fourth-order Runge-Kutta or finite differences. In practice, reduction of order is used when a solver or a symmetry argument has already produced one solution, and you need the second to write the general solution. Such equations are common when a material property changes with position.

2.9 Autonomous systems: phase plane, critical points and stability

Plot y against x and forget t for a moment. For an autonomous system x′ = F(x, y), y′ = G(x, y), time is hidden, so each solution is a curve in the xy-plane. Where both derivatives vanish the curve has nowhere to go: that is a critical point. Linearise there, read the two eigenvalues, and you know whether nearby motion falls in, spirals around, or runs away. Engineers use this to judge whether a power grid, a drone controller or a chemical reactor returns to steady state after a knock.

You need characteristic roots and eigenvalues from Chapter 2.1, and first-order separable equations from Unit 1.

An ODE system with no clock

An autonomous system has no t on the right-hand side: x′ = F(x, y), y′ = G(x, y). Here x and y are the dependent variables, often position and velocity, or two competing populations. The prime means d/dt, and F, G are smooth functions. Because t is absent, the point (x, y) alone fixes the velocity (F, G).

A solution is a pair (x(t), y(t)). Plot it in the xy-plane and drop the t labels; the curve is a trajectory. The xy-plane is the phase plane. Time still runs along the curve, and an arrow marks the direction as t increases, but the shape is fixed by the vector field.

A critical point (equilibrium point) is a point (x₀, y₀) with F(x₀, y₀) = 0 and G(x₀, y₀) = 0. A solution starting exactly there stays there forever. The real question is what a solution starting nearby does: return, circle, or escape.

The Jacobian and two numbers

Shift the origin to the critical point so it sits at (0, 0). Taylor expansion near it keeps only the linear terms:

x′ = a x + b y, y′ = c x + d y

a = Fx(x₀, y₀), b = Fy(x₀, y₀), c = Gx(x₀, y₀), d = Gy(x₀, y₀); subscripts mean partial derivatives.

The matrix of these coefficients is the Jacobian:

J = [[a, b], [c, d]] = [[F<sub>x</sub>, F<sub>y</sub>], [G<sub>x</sub>, G<sub>y</sub>]] at (x₀, y₀)

J is evaluated at the critical point only; it changes from point to point.

Try (x, y) = (u₁, u₂)e^(λt). Substitution gives J u = λ u, so λ is an eigenvalue and u an eigenvector. The eigenvalues solve

λ² − τλ + Δ = 0, λ = (τ ± √(τ² − 4Δ)) / 2

τ = λ₁ + λ₂ = a + d = trace J; Δ = λ₁λ₂ = ad − bc = det J.

Two numbers, τ and Δ, carry the local picture. The sign of Δ says whether the eigenvalues have the same sign (Δ > 0) or opposite signs (Δ < 0). The sign of τ says whether they grow (τ > 0) or decay (τ < 0). The discriminant τ² − 4Δ says real (≥ 0) or complex (< 0). When an eigenvalue has zero real part, linearisation is inconclusive and higher-order terms decide.

Worked example

Question. Find and classify the critical point of x′ = x, y′ = −2y. Solve the system directly and describe the trajectories.

  1. Write F(x, y) = x and G(x, y) = −2y. Set F = 0 and G = 0: x = 0 and −2y = 0, so y = 0. The only critical point is (0, 0).
  2. Compute the partial derivatives: Fx = 1, Fy = 0, Gx = 0, Gy = −2. The Jacobian at (0, 0) is J = [[1, 0], [0, −2]].
  3. J is diagonal, so its eigenvalues are the diagonal entries: λ₁ = 1 and λ₂ = −2.
  4. τ = λ₁ + λ₂ = 1 + (−2) = −1. Δ = λ₁λ₂ = 1 × (−2) = −2. Check: trace J = 1 + (−2) = −1 and det J = (1)(−2) − (0)(0) = −2, which agree.
  5. Δ = −2 < 0, so the eigenvalues have opposite signs. The critical point is a saddle. A saddle is unstable whatever the sign of τ.
  6. Solve directly. x′ = x gives x = C₁e^t. y′ = −2y gives y = C₂e^(−2t). Eliminate t: x² = C₁²e^(2t), so x²y = C₁²C₂e^(2t)e^(−2t) = C₁²C₂, a constant. The trajectories are the curves x²y = k, running out along the x-axis and in along the y-axis.

Answer. The critical point (0, 0) is a saddle with τ = −1 and Δ = −2; it is unstable, and the trajectories are x²y = constant.

Exam traps

  • A saddle is never stable, even though one eigenvalue is negative and one family of solutions does approach the origin.
  • When Δ = 0 or an eigenvalue has zero real part, the linear terms do not decide: neglected quadratic terms can turn a centre into a spiral. Check the nonlinear system.
  • τ and Δ are trace and determinant of the Jacobian at the critical point, not of some coefficient matrix evaluated elsewhere.
  • A stable node approaches straight in; a stable spiral curls in. The discriminant τ² − 4Δ separates them.
  • If t appears on the right-hand side, the system is not autonomous: the vector field changes with time and trajectories can cross.

Takeaways

  1. A constant-coefficient linear ODE turns into polynomial algebra through y=emx.
  2. The general solution is C.F. plus any one particular integral.
  3. For simple right-hand sides, the P.I. shape copies the right-hand side unless it clashes with the C.F.
  4. The Wronskian is the quick test for independence of solutions.
  5. Undetermined coefficients is fast but needs constant coefficients and a special right-hand side.
  6. Variation of parameters works for variable coefficients once two independent homogeneous solutions are known.
  7. A Cauchy-Euler equation becomes constant-coefficient under x=et.
  8. Coupled constant-coefficient ODEs can be eliminated into one higher-order ODE.
  9. One known solution of a second-order variable-coefficient ODE gives the second by reduction of order.
  10. Phase-plane critical points are classified by the eigenvalues of the Jacobian.

Glossary

Autonomous system
A system of ODEs whose right-hand sides do not depend explicitly on t.
Cauchy-Euler equation
A linear ODE whose coefficients are powers of x matching the derivative order, such as ax²y″+bxy′+cy=f(x).
Centre
A critical point with pure imaginary eigenvalues; nearby paths are closed curves around it.
Characteristic equation
The polynomial equation obtained by substituting y=emx into a constant-coefficient homogeneous linear ODE.
Complementary function
The general solution of the associated homogeneous equation.
Critical point
A point where both components of an autonomous system vanish.
Eigenvalue
A scalar λ for which Av=λv has a nonzero vector v; here used to classify critical points.
Fundamental set
A set of linearly independent solutions whose linear combinations give the general homogeneous solution.
Homogeneous linear ODE
A linear ODE whose right-hand side is zero.
Jacobian matrix
The matrix of first partial derivatives of P and Q, used to linearise an autonomous system near a critical point.
Legendre linear equation
A Cauchy-Euler type equation with px+q in place of x, solved by px+q=et.
Linear ODE
An ODE in which the unknown function and its derivatives appear only to the first power and are not multiplied together.
Nonhomogeneous linear ODE
A linear ODE whose right-hand side is a nonzero function of the independent variable.
Order
The order of an ODE is the order of the highest derivative present.
Particular integral
Any one solution of the nonhomogeneous equation, without arbitrary constants.
Phase plane
The xy-plane used to plot solution curves of an autonomous system when t is eliminated.
Reduction of order
A method that uses one known solution to find a second independent solution of a second-order linear ODE.
Resonance
The case where the right-hand side duplicates a term in the complementary function, forcing an extra factor xs in the trial solution.
Saddle point
A critical point with one positive and one negative real eigenvalue; nearby paths approach and then leave.
Spiral point
A critical point with complex eigenvalues and nonzero real part; nearby paths spiral in or out.
Stable critical point
A critical point to which nearby solution curves tend as t→∞.
Undetermined coefficients
A method that guesses a trial particular solution with unknown constants and solves for them by substitution.
Unstable critical point
A critical point from which nearby solution curves move away as t increases.
Variation of parameters
A method that lets the constants in the complementary function vary with x to build a particular integral.
Wronskian
The determinant W=y₁y₂′−y₂y₁′ used to test independence of two solutions.
