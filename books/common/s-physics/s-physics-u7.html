7.1 Classical free electron theory and electrical conductivity

A metal holds a gas of electrons free to move between fixed positive ions. A field accelerates them; collisions stop them, so they settle into a slow steady drift. This balance gives Ohm's law and conductivity σ = ne²τ/m. Drude proposed this around 1900. It explains why a copper wire obeys V = IR and why nichrome makes a good heater coil. It also fails in clear ways that point to quantum theory.

You need current, current density, drift velocity and electric field from Unit 3, and force and energy from Unit 1.

A metal as a gas of free electrons

A metal is a lattice of fixed positive ions with conduction electrons moving between them. The outermost electrons leave their atoms and belong to the whole solid; they carry current. Drude used a gas-in-a-box analogy.

The model rests on four assumptions:

  • Free: between collisions an electron feels no force from the ions or from other electrons.
  • Independent: electron–electron repulsion is ignored.
  • Random motion: velocities point in all directions, so the average velocity is zero when no field is applied.
  • Collisions: an electron strikes an ion after an average time τ, the relaxation time, and forgets the velocity gained. For a metal at room temperature τ is of order 10⁻¹⁴ s.

The symbol n is the number density of conduction electrons: free electrons per cubic metre. For copper, n = 8.5×10²⁸ m⁻³, roughly one free electron per atom. The model computes σ from these microscopic numbers.

Drift velocity, mobility and the relaxation time

Field E gives force F = eE and acceleration a = eE/m, with e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C and m = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg. Without collisions the current would grow forever; collisions prevent that.

An electron just after a collision gains speed eEt/m in a flight of duration t. Averaging flights with mean τ gives a small steady drift opposite E for the negative electron:

v_d = eEτ/m and μ = v_d/E = eτ/m

v_d is drift velocity in m/s; e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C; E is field in V/m; τ is relaxation time in s; m = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg; μ is mobility in m²/(V·s).

For copper with τ = 2.5×10⁻¹⁴ s, μ = (1.6×10⁻¹⁹)(2.5×10⁻¹⁴)/(9.11×10⁻³¹) ≈ 4.4×10⁻³ m²/(V·s). Drift is slow; it sits on top of fast random motion.

Conductivity and Ohm's law

Current density is charge crossing unit area per second. With n electrons per cubic metre, each drifting at v_d, the charge crossing one square metre per second is nev_d.

J = nev_d = (ne²τ/m)E = σE, σ = ne²τ/m

J is current density in A/m²; n is electron density in m⁻³; v_d is drift velocity in m/s; σ is conductivity in S/m; ρ = 1/σ = m/(ne²τ) is resistivity in Ω·m.

This is Ohm's law at the microscopic level. It is linear because v_d is proportional to E when τ does not depend on field. For a wire of length L and area A, R = ρL/A and V = IR follow. Copper, with n = 8.5×10²⁸ m⁻³ and τ = 2.5×10⁻¹⁴ s, gives σ ≈ 6.0×10⁷ S/m, close to measured 5.9×10⁷ S/m. Since τ was obtained from that measured value, this is a consistency check. An independent classical estimate of τ still lands within a factor of about two.

Temperature enters through τ:

  • Phonons: ions vibrate more at higher temperature, so electrons scatter more often and τ falls. Resistivity rises with T.
  • Impurities and defects: foreign atoms, vacancies or grain boundaries scatter electrons at any temperature. This adds a fixed residual resistivity.
  • The two add: ρ = ρ_phonon(T) + ρ_residual (Matthiessen's rule). Alloys such as nichrome and constantan have heavy impurity scattering, so high ρ and a weak temperature coefficient; hence heater coils and standard resistors.

What the classical model gets right and what it misses

  • Right — Ohm's law: a linear V–I relation comes out with no extra assumption.
  • Right — size of σ: computed conductivity of copper, silver and gold lands within a factor of about two of measured values.
  • Right — Wiedemann–Franz: κ/(σT) is nearly the same constant for all metals, and the electron gas gives this within a factor of about two.
  • Wrong — heat capacity: equipartition gives (3/2)k_B per electron, so metals should have huge electronic heat capacity. The measured electronic part is far smaller and grows linearly with T.
  • Wrong — ρ(T): the model predicts ρ ∝ √T because τ ∝ T⁻¹ᐟ². Measured ρ is nearly linear at high T and falls as T⁵ at low T.
  • Wrong — speed: the model assumes thermal speed near 10⁵ m/s. Electrons move at the Fermi speed, about 10⁶ m/s, even near absolute zero; see 7.2.
  • Wrong — insulators: every solid contains electrons, yet diamond does not conduct. Allowed bands are needed; see 7.3.

Worked example

Question. Copper has n = 8.5×10²⁸ m⁻³ and τ = 2.5×10⁻¹⁴ s. Take m = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg and e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C. (a) Find σ. (b) A copper wire of cross-section 1.0 mm² carries 2.0 A; find J and v_d.

  1. Data: n = 8.5×10²⁸ m⁻³, τ = 2.5×10⁻¹⁴ s, m = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg, e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C, I = 2.0 A, A = 1.0 mm² = 1.0×10⁻⁶ m².
  2. (a) e² = (1.6×10⁻¹⁹)² = 2.56×10⁻³⁸ C². Then ne²τ = (8.5×10²⁸)(2.56×10⁻³⁸)(2.5×10⁻¹⁴) = 5.44×10⁻²³.
  3. σ = ne²τ/m = 5.44×10⁻²³/9.11×10⁻³¹ = 5.97×10⁷ S/m, about 6.0×10⁷ S/m.
  4. (b) J = I/A = 2.0/(1.0×10⁻⁶) = 2.0×10⁶ A/m².
  5. ne = (8.5×10²⁸)(1.6×10⁻¹⁹) = 1.36×10¹⁰ C/m³, so v_d = J/(ne) = 2.0×10⁶/1.36×10¹⁰ = 1.47×10⁻⁴ m/s.
  6. Cross-check: E = J/σ = 3.35×10⁻² V/m; μ = eτ/m = 4.39×10⁻³ m²/(V·s); v_d = μE = 1.47×10⁻⁴ m/s. Drift is about 0.15 mm per second, far below random speed of about 10⁵ m/s.

Exam traps

  • Drift speed is not signal speed or electron speed. v_d ≈ 0.15 mm/s, yet a lamp glows at once because the field spreads along the wire at nearly light speed, and each electron moves randomly at the Fermi speed, about 10⁶ m/s.
  • Use e = 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ C as a magnitude in σ = ne²τ/m. Substituting −1.6×10⁻¹⁹ gives negative conductivity; σ, ρ and μ are positive.
  • n must be in m⁻³. A density per cm³ must be multiplied by 10⁶ first, or σ comes out 10⁶ times too small.
  • τ is mean time between collisions, not mean free path. Mean free path is λ = vτ, where v is fast random speed, never drift speed.
  • Do not say the classical theory explains resistivity versus temperature. It gets the direction right but the wrong low-temperature power law and the wrong electronic heat capacity; those need quantum statistics and band theory.

Analyze Alloying copper with nickel raises resistivity and flattens the resistivity–temperature curve. Explain using τ and Matthiessen's rule. 5 marks

Scattering rates add: 1/τ = 1/τ_phonon + 1/τ_impurity + 1/τ_defect, so ρ = ρ_phonon(T) + ρ_residual. Impurity atoms add a fixed scattering rate, raising ρ at every temperature. At low temperature the phonon term becomes small and the residual term dominates, so the curve flattens and the temperature coefficient falls.

Analyze Classical equipartition predicts electronic heat capacity (3/2)k_B per electron, but metals show a value about a hundred times smaller at room temperature that grows linearly with T. Which assumption fails, and why? 6 marks

The assumption that every electron can absorb thermal energy of order k_BT like a gas molecule fails. Electrons obey Fermi–Dirac statistics, so states are filled up to the Fermi energy and only the small fraction within about k_BT of it can take thermal energy. That fraction is about T/T_F; with the numerical factor 3, electronic heat capacity is roughly 3T/T_F times the classical value. It is about a hundred times smaller at room temperature and proportional to T; see 7.2.

7.2 Quantum free electron theory, Fermi energy and density of states

A metal holds about 10²⁹ free electrons per cubic metre. Classical theory treats them like gas molecules, all sharing thermal energy. Quantum theory says no: Pauli exclusion fills states from the bottom up, and at room temperature only a tiny fraction near the Fermi energy can move. This chapter builds the Fermi-Dirac distribution, the Fermi energy and the density of states. These ideas explain why metals conduct, why their electronic heat capacity is small, and they prepare the semiconductor chapters.

You need: the particle in a box and wave functions (see Unit 6, Quantum Mechanics), and the free-electron gas from 7.1.

Pauli exclusion and the Fermi-Dirac distribution

An electron in a metal is not alone. Pauli exclusion allows at most one electron in each quantum state. A state is labelled by momentum and spin, so one momentum state holds two electrons, one spin up and one spin down. At absolute zero the electrons fill the lowest states up to a highest energy. That energy is the Fermi energy EF. In a metal EF is several electron volts; copper has about 7 eV.

f(E) = 1 / [1 + exp((E − E<sub>F</sub>)/k<sub>B</sub>T)]

f(E) is the probability that a state of energy E is occupied; E is energy in joules; EF is the Fermi energy (at T > 0, the chemical potential); kB = 1.381×10⁻²³ J/K is the Boltzmann constant; T is absolute temperature in kelvin.

At T = 0, f = 1 below EF and 0 above: the occupation is a step. At T > 0 the step rounds over a width of a few kBT. At E = EF, f = 1/2. At 300 K, kBT = 0.0259 eV; for EF ≈ 7 eV only states within about 0.026 eV of the Fermi energy change.

The Fermi temperature is TF = EF/kB. Copper has TF ≈ 8×10⁴ K. Room temperature is far below this, so the electron gas is degenerate.

The Fermi surface is the surface E(k) = EF in wave-vector space. For free electrons it is a sphere with kF = (3π²n)1/3. This quantum model is often called the Sommerfeld theory.

Density of states

To count electrons, count states. In a box, allowed wave vectors fill k-space at one point per volume (2π)³/V. Including two spin states, the number per unit volume with |k| ≤ k is k³/(3π²). Put E = ħ²k²/2m and differentiate.

g(E) = (1/2π²)(2m/ħ²)3/2√E

g(E) is the density of states per unit volume per unit energy (states m⁻³ J⁻¹); m is the electron mass (or effective mass m* in a band); ħ = h/2π = 1.055×10⁻³⁴ J s is the reduced Planck constant; E is energy in joules.

g(E) is not constant: it rises as √E from zero at the bottom of the band. The number of states per m³ between E and E+dE is g(E)dE.

The electron density is n = ∫0∞ g(E)f(E)dE. At T = 0, f is 1 below EF and 0 above, so n = ∫0EF g(E)dE = (1/3π²)(2m/ħ²)3/2EF3/2. Invert: EF = (ħ²/2m)(3π²n)2/3. Doubling n raises EF by 22/3 = 1.59.

In a crystal the electron responds to the lattice as if its mass is an effective mass m*, a band parameter. For free-electron metals m* ≈ m; for semiconductors m* can be much smaller. See 7.3.

Fermi level and its temperature variation

At T > 0 the edge rounds. The energy where f = 1/2 is the chemical potential μ(T). At T = 0, μ = EF. For a 3D free-electron gas at low temperature, μ(T) ≈ EF[1 − (π²/12)(kBT/EF)²]. For copper at 300 K, kBT/EF ≈ 0.0037; the shift is about 8×10⁻⁵ eV. Metals keep the Fermi level almost fixed.

Some books call μ the Fermi level. In a semiconductor the Fermi level lies in the gap and moves with doping and temperature; that case is in 7.4.

At finite T the Fermi surface is not sharp. States within a few kBT above EF are partly occupied, and states within a few kBT below are partly empty. This fuzzy shell is the part that responds to an electric field or to heat. It explains why the electronic heat capacity is proportional to T, not the classical constant.

Worked example

Question. Copper has free-electron density n = 8.5×10²⁸ m⁻³. Take m = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg, ħ = 1.055×10⁻³⁴ J s and kB = 1.381×10⁻²³ J/K. Calculate the Fermi energy in joules and electron volts, and the Fermi temperature.

  1. Given n = 8.5×10²⁸ m⁻³, m = 9.11×10⁻³¹ kg, ħ = 1.055×10⁻³⁴ J s, kB = 1.381×10⁻²³ J/K.
  2. 3π²n = 3 × 9.8696 × 8.5×10²⁸ = 2.52×10³⁰ m⁻³.
  3. (3π²n)2/3 = (2.52×10³⁰)2/3 = 1.85×10²⁰ m⁻².
  4. ħ²/(2m) = (1.055×10⁻³⁴)²/(2×9.11×10⁻³¹) = 6.11×10⁻³⁹ J m².
  5. EF = 6.11×10⁻³⁹ × 1.85×10²⁰ = 1.13×10⁻¹⁸ J.
  6. In electron volts: EF = 1.13×10⁻¹⁸ / 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ = 7.06 eV.
  7. TF = EF/kB = 1.13×10⁻¹⁸ / 1.381×10⁻²³ = 8.19×10⁴ K.

Answer. EF ≈ 1.13×10⁻¹⁸ J ≈ 7.06 eV and TF ≈ 8.19×10⁴ K.

Exam traps

  • g(E) is not a constant. It rises as √E; do not pull it out of the integral n = ∫g(E)f(E)dE unless the energy range is very narrow.
  • EF is the highest occupied energy at T = 0. It is not kBT. At 300 K, kBT is only about 0.026 eV, while EF in a metal is a few eV.
  • Do not call EF the energy of the fastest electron at room temperature. At T > 0 the occupation edge is rounded: some states just above EF are occupied and some just below are empty.
  • The density of states formula already includes spin (factor 2). If you derive it, do not multiply by 2 again.
  • In a semiconductor the Fermi level lies in the gap and moves with doping and temperature; the metal formula EF ∝ n2/3 does not apply directly. See 7.4.

7.3 Energy bands; intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors

A crystal is a periodic array of atoms, so an electron inside it feels a repeating electric potential. That periodicity turns the sharp energy levels of a single atom into allowed energy bands separated by forbidden gaps. Metals, insulators and semiconductors are classified by gap size and band filling. A pure semiconductor has equal numbers of electrons and holes; doping adds donors or acceptors so one carrier type dominates. Band theory explains diodes, transistors, solar cells and every silicon chip.

You need 7.2 for Fermi-Dirac statistics and 7.1 for the free-electron picture; Unit 6, Particle in a box gives the confined-energy idea.

From levels to bands

An isolated atom has sharp energy levels. In a lattice the atoms repeat at distance a, and each level spreads into a band because neighbouring atoms perturb one another. The wave nature of the electron survives.

Bloch's theorem says an electron wave in a periodic potential has the form ψ(x) = u(x)e^{ikx}. Here u(x) repeats with the lattice, k is the crystal wave vector, and ħk is called crystal momentum. The allowed energies E(k) form continuous bands.

The Kronig-Penney model treats the lattice as a row of rectangular potential wells. Matching the wavefunction across the wells shows that allowed energies lie in bands and forbidden energies lie in gaps. At the zone boundary k = ±π/a a gap opens. The range −π/a to π/a is the first Brillouin zone in one dimension.

In the E-k diagram the lower band is the valence band and the upper band is the conduction band. The gap between them is E_g, the band gap, measured in electron-volts (eV). A metal has a partly filled band, so electrons move under any small field. An insulator has a full valence band and a large gap. A semiconductor has a small gap, commonly about 1 eV.

Intrinsic semiconductor

A pure semiconductor is called intrinsic. At 0 K its valence band is full and its conduction band is empty, so it is an insulator. At room temperature thermal energy breaks a few bonds. Each broken bond gives one electron in the conduction band and one hole, a missing electron, in the valence band. Therefore n = p = n_i, where n is electron concentration, p is hole concentration, and n_i is the intrinsic carrier concentration, in cm⁻³.

n<sub>i</sub>² = N<sub>C</sub> N<sub>V</sub> exp(−E<sub>g</sub> / k<sub>B</sub>T)

ni is the intrinsic carrier concentration in cm⁻³; NC is the effective density of states in the conduction band in cm⁻³; NV is the effective density of states in the valence band in cm⁻³; Eg is the band gap in eV; kB is the Boltzmann constant, 8.617×10⁻⁵ eV/K; T is absolute temperature in K.

N_C and N_V count how many states are available near each band edge; they are not the number of electrons. n_i depends exponentially on E_g and T. A larger gap gives a much smaller n_i. Warming a sample raises n_i steeply, so a pure semiconductor's conductivity rises with temperature. For silicon at 300 K, n_i is about 10¹⁰ cm⁻³, tiny compared with about 5×10²² atoms per cm³.

The Fermi level in an intrinsic sample lies near the middle of the gap. This is why both carriers matter. The product n p = n_i² holds in thermal equilibrium even after doping; 7.4 uses it.

Extrinsic semiconductor

Doping adds a small controlled amount of impurity. A donor atom has one more valence electron than the host, for example phosphorus in silicon. It donates an electron, so n > p and the material is n-type. The donor level sits just below the conduction band, E_C. The donated electron reaches the conduction band easily.

An acceptor atom has one fewer valence electron, for example boron in silicon. It accepts an electron from a bond, leaving a hole, so p > n and the material is p-type. The acceptor level sits just above the valence band, E_V. Holes are the majority carriers.

The Fermi level E_F moves toward the majority-carrier band. In n-type it moves up toward E_C; in p-type it moves down toward E_V. It does not sit on the donor or acceptor level. The impurity level is a fixed small distance from the band edge, while E_F depends on doping and temperature.

At room temperature almost all donors or acceptors are ionized. A donor gives a positive ion and a free electron; an acceptor gives a negative ion and a free hole. The crystal stays electrically neutral overall. In an n-type sample, holes are the minority carriers and obey p = n_i²/n. This relation and the exact E_F position are worked out in 7.4.

Worked example

Question. Silicon at 300 K has E_g = 1.1 eV, N_C = 2.8×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ and N_V = 1.0×10¹⁹ cm⁻³. Take k_BT = 0.0259 eV. Find n_i at 300 K.

  1. Compute the exponent: E_g / k_BT = 1.1 eV / 0.0259 eV = 42.47.
  2. Compute the exponential factor: exp(−42.47) = 3.59×10⁻¹⁹.
  3. Multiply the effective densities: N_C N_V = (2.8×10¹⁹ cm⁻³)(1.0×10¹⁹ cm⁻³) = 2.8×10³⁸ cm⁻⁶.
  4. Find n_i²: n_i² = (2.8×10³⁸ cm⁻⁶)(3.59×10⁻¹⁹) = 1.0×10²⁰ cm⁻⁶.
  5. Take the square root: n_i = √(1.0×10²⁰ cm⁻⁶) = 1.0×10¹⁰ cm⁻³.

Answer. n_i ≈ 1.0×10¹⁰ cm⁻³ at 300 K, so n = p ≈ 1.0×10¹⁰ cm⁻³ in pure silicon.

Exam traps

  • Intrinsic does not mean zero carriers. n_i is small but nonzero, and it rises exponentially with temperature.
  • Do not put the donor level at the Fermi level. The donor level sits a fixed small distance below E_C; E_F moves with doping and temperature.
  • The relation n p = n_i² holds only in thermal equilibrium. Light or an injected current creates excess carriers and breaks it.
  • A larger band gap gives a smaller n_i, not a larger one, because the factor exp(−E_g/k_BT) falls as E_g rises.
  • A hole is a missing electron in the valence band, not a proton. It behaves as a positive charge carrier.

7.4 Carrier concentration in N-type and P-type semiconductors

A doped semiconductor has far more electrons than holes, or the reverse. The dopant density decides which, and by how much. Fermi-Dirac statistics fixes the rest: the minority density, the Fermi level position, and how both move with temperature. Every diode, transistor, LED and solar cell depends on this balance.

You need 7.2 Fermi-Dirac statistics and the Fermi energy and 7.3 energy bands and the intrinsic carrier concentration n_i.

Carrier density from the Fermi level

A phosphorus atom in silicon donates one electron bound by about 45 meV. At room temperature most of these electrons reach the conduction band. Counting them is Fermi-Dirac book-keeping.

n = N_C exp[−(E_C − E_F)/k_BT]

n is the electron concentration (m⁻³ or cm⁻³); N_C is the effective density of states at the conduction band edge, in the same unit; E_C is the conduction band edge energy in eV; E_F is the Fermi energy in eV; k_B = 8.617×10⁻⁵ eV/K (1.381×10⁻²³ J/K) is the Boltzmann constant, so k_BT comes out in eV; T is the temperature in kelvin. At 300 K, k_BT = 0.0259 eV.

p = N_V exp[−(E_F − E_V)/k_BT]

p is the hole concentration; N_V is the effective density of states at the valence band edge; E_V is the valence band edge energy in eV. A hole is an empty state, so the further E_F sits above E_V, the fewer holes there are.

N_C and N_V come from the band curvature, that is, from the effective masses.

N_C = 2(2π m_e* k_BT / h²)^(3/2)

m_e* is the electron effective mass near the band edge; h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s is the Planck constant. N_V uses the hole effective mass m_h* in the same expression. For silicon at 300 K, N_C ≈ 2.8×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ and N_V ≈ 1.0×10¹⁹ cm⁻³.

Both formulas are the Boltzmann tail of f(E) = 1/(1 + exp[(E − E_F)/k_BT]). Once E − E_F exceeds about 3k_BT the 1 drops out and f(E) ≈ exp[−(E − E_F)/k_BT]. Multiply that tail by the number of available states to get the carrier formulas. This non-degenerate case covers the whole chapter.

  • Raising T at fixed E_F raises both n and p, because the tails reach further into the bands.
  • Raising E_F raises n and lowers p: the Fermi level is the knob.
  • Doping moves E_F; that is the whole mechanism of an extrinsic semiconductor.

Mass action, charge neutrality, and the doping answer

Two relations tie the four quantities together. The first is the law of mass action, from multiplying the two carrier formulas with E_g = E_C − E_V.

n p = n_i², n_i² = N_C N_V exp(−E_g/k_BT)

n_i is the intrinsic carrier concentration, about 1.0×10¹⁰ cm⁻³ for silicon at 300 K; E_g = E_C − E_V is the band gap, 1.12 eV for silicon. The product n p depends only on temperature and material, never on doping.

The second is charge neutrality: a piece of semiconductor carries no net charge, so mobile electrons plus negative acceptor ions balance holes plus positive donor ions.

n + N_A⁻ = p + N_D⁺

N_D⁺ is the ionised donor concentration (positive, the donors gave up electrons) and N_A⁻ the ionised acceptor concentration (negative), both in cm⁻³. At room temperature assume full ionisation: N_D⁺ = N_D and N_A⁻ = N_A.

  1. n-type, no acceptors: N_A = 0, so charge neutrality reads n = N_D + p.
  2. N_D far exceeds n_i, so p is negligible and the majority density is n ≈ N_D.
  3. Mass action then gives the minority density p = n_i²/N_D.
  4. Invert n = N_C exp[−(E_C − E_F)/k_BT] with n = N_D: E_C − E_F = k_BT ln(N_C/N_D).
  5. p-type: swap donors and acceptors to get p ≈ N_A, n ≈ n_i²/N_A and E_F − E_V = k_BT ln(N_V/N_A).

E_C − E_F is positive in n-type material: the Fermi level sits k_BT ln(N_C/N_D) below the conduction band edge, a few tenths of an eV in silicon — about 0.2 eV at N_D = 10¹⁶ cm⁻³, several k_BT. In p-type material E_F sits above E_V by k_BT ln(N_V/N_A). Heavier doping pushes the Fermi level closer to the band.

How carrier density changes with temperature

A doped crystal passes through three regimes as it warms.

  • Freeze-out, very low temperature. k_BT is far below the donor binding energy (about 45 meV for phosphorus in silicon), so electrons stay bound and n falls as T falls. For silicon this ends near 50 K.
  • Extrinsic, the working range. Every donor is ionised, n ≈ N_D stays roughly constant, while p = n_i²/N_D rises slowly. The Fermi level is locked at E_C − k_BT ln(N_C/N_D). Most devices live here.
  • Intrinsic, high temperature. n_i grows as T^(3/2) exp(−E_g/2k_BT) and eventually reaches N_D; past that n ≈ p ≈ n_i and E_F drifts back to mid-gap.

The flat middle region exists only because N_D far exceeds n_i. When the two are comparable, n ≈ N_D fails.

n = (N_D + √(N_D² + 4n_i²)) / 2

This is the exact solution of n = N_D + n_i²/n, with p = n_i²/n to follow. It assumes full ionisation: when n_i << N_D (extrinsic range) n → N_D, and when n_i >> N_D (intrinsic range) n → n_i. Below the freeze-out temperature the donors are not fully ionised and this formula no longer applies.

One limit: when N_D approaches N_C (about 2.8×10¹⁹ cm⁻³ for silicon), E_F enters the conduction band, the Boltzmann tail fails and the full Fermi-Dirac function is needed. That is degenerate doping, and this chapter stays below it.

Exam traps

  • Writing n = N_D when N_D is close to n_i, or at high temperature. Use n = (N_D + √(N_D² + 4n_i²))/2 there.
  • Mixing cm⁻³ and m⁻³: 1 m⁻³ is 10⁻⁶ cm⁻³, a factor of a million. Silicon data come in cm⁻³.
  • Sign slips in the Fermi level. In n-type material E_C − E_F is small and positive; in p-type the small quantity is E_F − E_V. E_F − E_C would be negative.

  • Calling N_C the number of electrons. N_C is the number of available states per unit volume at the band edge; the exponential decides how many are occupied.

7.5 Carrier transport: drift and diffusion

Carriers move for two reasons. An electric field pushes them: drift. A concentration gradient pushes them: diffusion. Most devices have both at once. This chapter writes each current in one dimension, combines them, and links the two coefficients through the Einstein relation. Solar cells, photodiodes and transistor bases all run on this balance.

You need: 7.3 and 7.4, plus the electric field from Unit 3, Electrostatics.

Drift: pushed by a field

An electric field E (V m⁻¹) exerts a force qE on a carrier. Lattice collisions interrupt the acceleration, so the carrier settles at a steady average drift velocity. Mobility μ is that velocity per unit field:

v_d = μ E

v_d is drift velocity (m s⁻¹); μ is mobility (m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹); E is field (V m⁻¹).

Electrons drift opposite to E; holes drift along E. Conventional current is opposite to electron motion, so both carrier types push current the same way:

J_n = n e μ_n E J_p = p e μ_p E

J is current density (A m⁻²); n, p are carrier densities (m⁻³); e = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C.

Dividing total J by E recovers Ohm's law:

σ = e (n μ_n + p μ_p)

σ is conductivity (S m⁻¹); resistivity ρ = 1/σ (Ω m).

Silicon at 300 K has μ_n ≈ 1350 and μ_p ≈ 480 cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹. Since 1 m² = 10⁴ cm², μ_n = 0.135 m² V⁻¹ s⁻¹ in SI.

Diffusion: pushed by a gradient

Carriers random-walk even with no field. If density is higher on the left, more cross right than left, and a net flow appears down the gradient. The gradient dn/dx carries units of m⁻⁴: density per metre, not per centimetre.

J_n = e D_n dn/dx J_p = − e D_p dp/dx

D_n, D_p are diffusion constants (m² s⁻¹).

The minus sign on the hole term matters. Holes are positive and flow down the gradient, so their current points down the gradient. Electrons are negative and also flow down the gradient, so their conventional current points up it.

Silicon at 300 K: D_n ≈ 35 cm² s⁻¹ and D_p ≈ 12 cm² s⁻¹, again divided by 10⁴ for SI. In a photodiode, light creates electron-hole pairs, and the built-in junction field separates them even at zero bias. Pairs made in the neutral bulk reach the junction by diffusion, so both transport mechanisms matter.

Total current and the Einstein relation

A real carrier feels both pushes at once. Add the two densities:

J = e(n μ_n + p μ_p) E + e( D_n dn/dx − D_p dp/dx )

The first term is drift, the second diffusion; symbols as above.

The two coefficients come from the same thermal motion. Mobility measures the response to a field; diffusion measures how fast a gradient is smoothed. Their ratio depends only on temperature:

D / μ = k_B T / e

k_B = 1.381×10⁻²³ J K⁻¹; T in kelvin; e = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ C. The ratio is a voltage. It holds separately for electrons and holes.

At 300 K, k_BT/e = 0.0259 V, the thermal voltage. So D_n = 0.026 × μ_n in SI: one measured mobility gives the diffusion constant free. The relation holds for lightly doped, non-degenerate material; at heavy doping a degeneracy factor slightly larger than 1 multiplies the right side.

In a solar cell the junction field sweeps carriers out of the depletion region while diffusion feeds them in from the neutral bulk. Good efficiency needs both terms of the total J.

Worked example

Question. In silicon at 300 K, n = 1.0×10¹⁶ cm⁻³, μ_n = 1350 cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹, E = 1000 V m⁻¹. Find the drift velocity and the electron drift current density.

  1. Density to SI: n = 1.0×10¹⁶ × 10⁶ = 1.0×10²² m⁻³.

  3. v_d = μ_n E = 0.135 × 1000 = 135 m s⁻¹.
  4. Charge density n e = 1.0×10²² × 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ = 1602 C m⁻³.

Exam traps

  • Hole diffusion carries a minus sign, electron diffusion does not. Swap them and the current points the wrong way.
  • Mobilities are quoted in cm² V⁻¹ s⁻¹. Divide by 10⁴ before putting them into SI formulas.
  • Einstein uses k_BT/e ≈ 0.026 V at 300 K, not T = 300 K itself.
  • Drift needs a field, diffusion needs a gradient. A sample with E = 0 can still carry current.
  • In a given field, electron and hole drift currents point the same way. Diffusion has no such rule. Add signed currents, J = J_n + J_p; either term may be negative, so the two can partly cancel.

7.6 Metal-semiconductor junctions: ohmic and Schottky contacts

A metal touching a semiconductor is not automatically a wire. When the metal work function Φ_M is larger than the semiconductor work function Φ_s, electrons leave the semiconductor, a depletion layer and a barrier appear, and current flows easily one way only: a Schottky contact. When the barrier is removed by a metal with Φ_M < Φ_s, or made thin by heavy doping so electrons tunnel, the contact is ohmic. Every transistor, solar cell and LED needs such contacts. Only a few universities teach this in first year.

You need bands and doping from 7.3, Fermi-level ideas from 7.4, and electric potential from Unit 3.

The barrier at a metal–semiconductor contact

Define the metal work function Φ_M as the energy to remove an electron from the metal's Fermi level to vacuum. Define the semiconductor electron affinity χ as the energy from the bottom of the conduction band E_C to vacuum. For n-type material, electrons sit near E_C; their Fermi level is (E_C − E_F) below E_C. Define the semiconductor work function Φ_s = χ + (E_C − E_F). On contact, electrons flow until one Fermi level remains. If Φ_M > Φ_s, electrons leave the semiconductor for the metal. Positive donor ions remain, the bands bend upward, and a depletion layer forms. The depletion width grows until the built-in potential V_bi stops further flow. The barrier height is Φ_B = Φ_M − χ; it is positive whenever Φ_M > χ. A positive Φ_B alone does not mean depletion. If Φ_M < Φ_s, electrons accumulate; in the range χ < Φ_M < Φ_s, Φ_B is positive but V_bi is negative, the bands bend down, and the contact is not rectifying.

For n-type, the ideal barrier is Φ_B = Φ_M − χ. The built-in potential is V_bi = Φ_B − (E_C − E_F)/e, with e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; the depletion condition is V_bi > 0. For p-type, the hole barrier is Φ_B = E_g + χ − Φ_M, where E_g is the band gap.

Ohmic contacts: remove or tunnel through the barrier

An ohmic contact has a linear I–V curve: V = IR in both directions. Two routes give it. First, choose a metal with Φ_M < Φ_s for n-type. Electrons then accumulate instead of depleting; no depletion barrier forms. Few metals are stable with such low work functions. Second, dope the semiconductor heavily, around 10¹⁹ to 10²⁰ cm⁻³. The depletion width at zero bias is W = √(2ε_s V_bi / (e N_D)). Here ε_s is the permittivity of the semiconductor (ε_s = ε_r ε_0, where ε_r is the relative permittivity and ε_0 is the vacuum permittivity, in F/m) and N_D is the donor concentration (in m⁻³); W is in metres. W shrinks as 1/√N_D. The barrier remains tall but becomes a few nanometres thin, so electrons tunnel through. This is the practical route in silicon chips: a heavily doped n⁺ or p⁺ patch under the metal. Contact resistance, not barrier height, then sets the loss. Tunnelling is the same quantum process described in the chapter on quantum tunnelling.

Reading the current–voltage curve

Under forward bias on an n-type Schottky contact, the metal is positive. The applied voltage opposes V_bi, so the barrier seen by electrons falls and current rises exponentially. Under reverse bias the barrier grows, the depletion layer widens, and only a small leakage current flows. The curve is rectifying. An ohmic contact shows a straight line through the origin; its slope is 1/R. At the interface the barrier height stays fixed in the ideal model; the applied voltage changes the band slope across the depletion layer. Heavy doping does not remove the barrier; it only makes it thin. Schottky diodes switch fast because they store no minority carriers, and they are used in power supplies. Their forward drop is a few tenths of a volt, smaller than a p–n silicon diode.

Worked example

Question. For n-Si, χ = 4.05 eV and E_C − E_F = 0.20 eV. A metal has Φ_M = 4.70 eV. Find the ideal Schottky barrier Φ_B and the built-in potential V_bi.

  1. The semiconductor is n-type, so use Φ_B = Φ_M − χ.
  2. Substitute: Φ_B = 4.70 eV − 4.05 eV = 0.65 eV.
  3. Use V_bi = Φ_B − (E_C − E_F)/e. The energy 0.20 eV divided by e gives 0.20 V.

  • An ohmic contact is not a zero-barrier contact. Heavy doping keeps the barrier tall but makes it thin enough to tunnel.
  • Barrier height Φ_B is in electron-volts; built-in potential V_bi is in volts. Divide the energy term by e before subtracting.
  • Do not confuse Φ_B with V_bi. For n-type they differ by (E_C − E_F)/e.
  • Forward bias lowers the barrier and reverse bias raises it; reversing the bias polarity (metal negative on n-type) reverses the I–V curve.

7.7 Magnetic materials: dia, para and ferromagnetism

A magnetic material is one whose atoms carry a magnetic dipole moment. Apply a field H and the material answers with a magnetization M. The sign and size of that answer split materials into three families. Diamagnets push back weakly, paramagnets follow weakly, ferromagnets align strongly in domains. That same physics sets the core of a transformer, the magnet on a fridge door and the contrast in an MRI scan.

You need: the magnetic field, the Biot–Savart law and Ampère's law from Unit 3 (Magnetostatics), and the idea of a current loop as a magnetic dipole.

Dipole moment, magnetization and susceptibility

A current loop is a magnetic dipole. An electron circling a nucleus is one such loop, and the electron's spin adds a moment of its own. A block of material holds many of these moments; their vector sum per unit volume is the magnetization M, in A/m. In an unmagnetized block the moments point every way, so M = 0. A field lines them up and M becomes non-zero.

B = μ₀(H + M)

B is the magnetic flux density in tesla (T); μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m is the permeability of free space; H is the magnetic field strength in A/m, fixed by the currents in the coil; M is the magnetization in A/m.

χ = M/H, μ_r = 1 + χ, B = μ₀ μ_r H

χ is the magnetic susceptibility, dimensionless and allowed to be negative; μ_r is the relative permeability, dimensionless; the last form holds only when M is proportional to H, which fails for a ferromagnet.

χ is the number that classifies a magnetic material. Its sign and size are the whole story: about −10⁻⁵ for a diamagnet, +10⁻⁵ to +10⁻³ for a paramagnet, and 10² to 10⁵ for a ferromagnet.

Diamagnetism and paramagnetism

Diamagnetism comes from induced currents. When H is switched on, the orbital motion of each electron readjusts so that the induced moment opposes H — Lenz's law acting at atomic scale. The response is weak, χ ≈ −10⁻⁵, and almost independent of temperature. Every material has it. Bismuth, copper, water and most organic compounds look diamagnetic because nothing stronger is present. In a superconductor the induced currents meet no resistance and χ = −1.

Paramagnetism needs a permanent moment, so it needs unpaired electrons. Thermal motion shakes these moments; H tilts them slightly towards itself. The result is small and positive, χ ≈ 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻³, and it fights temperature: hotter means less alignment. Oxygen and salts of ions such as Gd³⁺ behave this way.

χ = C/T, C = μ₀ n m² / (3k_B)

χ is the susceptibility; T is the absolute temperature in kelvin (K); C is the Curie constant in kelvin; n is the number of magnetic moments per cubic metre; m is one atomic moment in A m²; k_B = 1.38×10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann's constant; μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m.

So a plot of 1/χ against T is a straight line through the origin for a good paramagnet, and its slope is 1/C. That is how C is measured in the laboratory.

Ferromagnetism, domains and the Curie temperature

In iron, cobalt and nickel a quantum effect called the exchange interaction makes neighbouring moments line up parallel. The alignment is spontaneous, so a piece of iron splits into domains: small regions, already saturated in some direction, arranged so that the whole piece has M = 0. Under H, domains pointing near H grow at the expense of the others, then rotate. χ is huge and is not a constant.

M rises steeply and then flattens at the saturation magnetization M_s, where every domain points the same way. Increasing H further still raises B, but only through the μ₀H term, not through M.

Heat destroys the order. At the Curie temperature T_C the thermal energy k_BT becomes comparable to the exchange energy and the domains vanish. Above T_C the material is paramagnetic and obeys the Curie–Weiss law χ = C/(T − T_C). Iron has T_C ≈ 1043 K, about 770 °C, which is why a red-hot iron loses its magnetism.

Two relatives are worth naming. An antiferromagnet has two sublattices with equal and opposite moments, so the net M is zero; its order disappears at the Néel temperature T_N. A ferrimagnet has unequal opposite sublattices, so a net moment survives — the ferrites used in high-frequency transformer cores belong here.

Worked example

Question. A paramagnetic salt has magnetic susceptibility χ = 1.5×10⁻⁴ at 300 K. (a) Find its Curie constant C. (b) Find χ at 200 K, assuming the Curie law holds.

  1. Write the Curie law and rearrange it: χ = C/T, so C = χT.
  2. Substitute the given values: C = (1.5×10⁻⁴)(300 K) = 4.5×10⁻² K.
  3. Use the same C at the new temperature: χ = C/T = (4.5×10⁻² K)/(200 K) = 2.25×10⁻⁴.
  4. Check the ratio: cooling from 300 K to 200 K multiplies χ by 300/200 = 1.5, and 1.5×10⁻⁴ × 1.5 = 2.25×10⁻⁴.

Answer. C = 0.045 K, and χ at 200 K is 2.25×10⁻⁴ (dimensionless).

Exam traps

  • Diamagnetism is present in every material. Copper is not non-magnetic; its χ ≈ −10⁻⁵ is simply hidden whenever a paramagnetic impurity or a ferromagnetic phase is present.
  • χ of a ferromagnet is not a constant. Never substitute it into χ = C/T; above T_C use χ = C/(T − T_C), and below T_C there is no such law at all.
  • H and B are different quantities. H is in A/m, B is in tesla, and the link is B = μ₀(H + M). Dropping μ₀ gives an answer about 8×10⁵ times too large, since 1/μ₀ ≈ 7.96×10⁵ A m⁻¹ T⁻¹ (μ₀ = 1.26×10⁻⁶ H/m).
  • The Curie law is χ = C/T, not χ = CT. The Curie constant carries the unit kelvin.
  • A negative χ does not mean a material blocks field completely. Only a superconductor reaches χ = −1; an ordinary diamagnet still has μ_r slightly less than 1, but positive.

7.8 Hard and soft magnetic materials; hysteresis

A ferromagnet does not retrace its magnetization curve when the applied field is removed. The B-H plot traces a closed loop called the hysteresis loop. Its area is the energy lost as heat per unit volume per cycle. Soft magnetic materials have narrow loops, low coercivity and small loss; they go into transformer cores and inductors. Hard magnetic materials have wide loops, high coercivity and high retentivity; they become permanent magnets. The same loop explains why a transformer core heats up and why a fridge magnet keeps its field.

You need: Chapter 7.7 on domains and ferromagnetism.

Reading the B-H loop

Two fields describe a magnetic material. The magnetic field strength H is set by currents and is measured in ampere per metre (A/m). The magnetic flux density B is the resulting field inside the material and is measured in tesla (T). For a linear material B = μ0 μr H, where μ0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m is the vacuum permeability and μr is the relative permeability. For a ferromagnet the relation is not linear and not single-valued.

Start with an unmagnetized sample and raise H. B rises steeply, then bends over and saturates at Bs. Domains that already point along H grow, and domains that oppose H shrink or rotate. At high H all domains are aligned and further increase of H gives only the small slope μ0.

Now reduce H to zero. B does not return to zero. The value left at H = 0 is the retentivity B_r. Reverse H until B becomes zero; the magnitude of that reverse field is the coercivity H_c. Continue to negative saturation and back, and the path closes into a loop. Domain wall motion is easy at first and reversible; pinning at defects makes part of the motion irreversible, and that irreversibility opens the loop.

Loop area and power loss

The area of the closed B-H loop is the work done by the source on unit volume of material in one cycle. Write it as the formula below. W_h is the hysteresis loss per unit volume per cycle, in joule per cubic metre (J/m³); H is in A/m; B is in T; the integral goes once around the loop. The unit check is (A/m) × T = (A/m) × (N/(A·m)) = N/m² = J/m³.

W_h = ∮ H dB

W_h is the hysteresis energy loss per unit volume per cycle (J/m³); H is the magnetic field strength (A/m); B is the magnetic flux density (T); the integral is taken once around the closed B-H loop.

A core of volume V taken through the loop f times per second loses hysteresis power P_h = W_h V f. V is in cubic metres (m³) and f is in hertz (Hz = cycles per second). The loss rises linearly with frequency for a fixed loop. It also depends on the peak flux density Bm, because a larger Bm sweeps a larger loop.

Eddy currents give a second loss. Changing B induces currents inside the core, and their Joule heating adds to the hysteresis heat. Eddy-current loss grows roughly as f² and is reduced by making the core from thin, insulated laminated sheets or by using a high-resistivity ferrite. Lamination does not reduce hysteresis loss; that needs a soft material with a narrow loop.

Soft and hard magnetic materials

A soft magnetic material has a narrow loop, low coercivity H_c, high relative permeability μr and small hysteresis loss per cycle. It is easy to magnetize and easy to demagnetize. Silicon steel, soft ferrites and permalloy are common. Transformer cores, inductors, chokes and the magnetic cores of high-frequency switching supplies use soft materials because the core is taken around its loop many times every second.

A hard magnetic material has a wide loop, high coercivity and high retentivity. It is hard to demagnetize, so it holds a large field after the magnetizing source is removed. Alnico, hard ferrites, samarium-cobalt (SmCo) and neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) are used. Permanent magnets in motors, loudspeakers, door catches, magnetic separators and small generators use hard materials.

The product B_r H_c, or more strictly the maximum energy product (BH)_max, measures how much magnetic energy a hard magnet can supply in a gap. A large (BH)_max means a smaller magnet for the same job. A soft material with high Bs but low H_c is not a permanent magnet: it loses its field as soon as the current is switched off. 'Hard' here means magnetically hard, not mechanically hard.

Worked example

Question. A transformer core has volume 500 cm³. Its hysteresis loop has area 250 J/m³ per cycle at 50 Hz. Find the hysteresis power loss.

  1. Convert volume: V = 500 cm³ = 500 × 10⁻⁶ m³ = 0.0005 m³.
  2. Energy lost per cycle: E = area × V = 250 J/m³ × 0.0005 m³ = 0.125 J.
  3. Cycles per second: f = 50 Hz = 50 s⁻¹.
  4. Power: P = E × f = 0.125 J × 50 s⁻¹ = 6.25 W.

Answer. The hysteresis power loss is 6.25 W.

Exam traps

  • The loop area is energy per unit volume per cycle (J/m³), not power. To get power, multiply by core volume and frequency.
  • B is in tesla and H is in A/m. Do not add or plot them as if they had the same unit.
  • Retentivity B_r is the B left at H = 0. Coercivity H_c is the reverse H that makes B = 0. Do not swap the two.
  • A soft magnet needs low H_c and a narrow loop, not necessarily the highest Bs. A hard magnet needs high H_c, not high permeability.
  • Laminations cut eddy-current loss, not hysteresis loss. Hysteresis loss is cut by choosing a magnetically soft material.

Takeaways

  1. Treating electrons as a classical gas gives Ohm's law, σ = ne²τ/m, and the right order of magnitude for metal conductivity, but it cannot explain the tiny electronic heat capacity.
  2. Quantum statistics fix this: only electrons within about k_BT of the Fermi energy can be excited, so the classical value is multiplied by the small ratio T/T_F.
  3. The periodic lattice turns a continuous free-electron parabola into bands separated by gaps, and the gap size decides metal, semiconductor or insulator.
  4. Doping pins the majority carrier density at the dopant level; mass action law np = n_i² then fixes the minority carriers and the Fermi level.
  5. Total current in a semiconductor is drift plus diffusion, and the Einstein relation lets you keep only one of μ or D in the equations.
  6. A metal–semiconductor contact is ohmic when the barrier is negligible or thin enough to tunnel, and rectifying when a stable Schottky barrier forms.
  7. Diamagnets oppose the field weakly, paramagnets follow it weakly, and ferromagnets align in domains and can stay magnetised after the field is removed.
  8. The area of the B–H loop is the energy lost as heat per cycle, so transformers use narrow-loop soft magnets and loudspeakers use wide-loop hard magnets.

Glossary

Acceptor
An impurity atom, such as boron in silicon, that accepts an electron and leaves a hole, making the crystal p-type.
Bloch theorem
In a periodic potential the wavefunction has the form ψ(x) = u(x)e^{ikx}, where u has the period of the lattice.
Built-in potential
The equilibrium band bending at a junction, written V_bi, that balances diffusion and drift.
Curie temperature
The temperature above which a ferromagnet loses its spontaneous magnetisation and becomes paramagnetic; about 770 °C for iron.
Degenerate gas
An electron gas in which k_BT is much smaller than E_F, so quantum statistics matter and the classical equipartition result fails.
Diffusion current
Current caused by carriers moving from high to low concentration, J_n = eD_n dn/dx.
Donor
An impurity atom, such as phosphorus in silicon, that gives up an electron to the conduction band and makes the crystal n-type.
Drude model
The classical model of a metal as a gas of independent electrons that accelerate in a field and lose their drift at each collision.
Electron affinity
The energy released when an electron drops from vacuum into the bottom of a semiconductor's conduction band, written χ.
Energy product
The largest value of B×H around the demagnetisation curve; it measures how good a permanent magnet is.
Fermi sphere
The surface in k-space at 0 K that contains all the filled electron states, with radius k_F.
Fermi temperature
T_F = E_F/k_B, of order 10⁴–10⁵ K in metals; it is not a real temperature of the electrons.
Hole
A missing electron in an almost full band, which behaves as a positive charge with its own effective mass.
Ionisation energy of a dopant
The small energy (about 0.01–0.05 eV) needed to free a donor electron or to free a hole from an acceptor.
Kronig–Penney model
A one-dimensional lattice of rectangular potential wells, solved to show how allowed bands and forbidden gaps appear.
Mass action law
At thermal equilibrium np = n_i², whatever the doping level.
Mean free path
The average distance an electron travels between collisions, equal to v_th τ, typically a few tens of nanometres in a metal at room temperature.
Permeability
μ = μ_r μ_0, the constant that relates B to H in a material; μ_0 = 4π × 10⁻⁷ H/m.
Quasi-neutral region
Part of a semiconductor away from junctions where the space charge is negligible and n − p equals the fixed ion charge.
Reduced zone scheme
A way of drawing E–k that folds all bands into the first Brillouin zone, −π/a ≤ k ≤ π/a.
Retentivity
The flux density left in a material when the applied field is reduced to zero after saturation.
Susceptibility
χ = M/H, the ratio of magnetisation to applied field; it is small and negative for diamagnets and large and positive for ferromagnets.
Tunnelling contact
A contact so heavily doped that the barrier is thin and carriers cross by quantum tunnelling, giving a nearly linear I–V curve.
Work function
The energy needed to remove an electron from a metal to vacuum, written φ_m and measured in electron volts.
