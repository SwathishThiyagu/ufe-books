4.1 Reflection and refraction of light waves

At the boundary between two transparent media, one light wave becomes two: a reflected wave returns into the first medium, and a refracted wave enters the second with a changed direction and speed. The split is controlled by the refractive indices. The idea lets you design mirrors, lenses, optical fibres, anti-reflection coatings and every thin-film device in this unit. It also fixes the phase bookkeeping that later interference chapters depend on. This chapter states the laws, gives the normal-incidence reflectance formula, and explains the π phase change on reflection from a denser medium.

You need: Unit 3, electromagnetic waves and uniform plane waves; light is an electromagnetic wave whose electric field oscillates perpendicular to its direction of travel.

Wavefronts, rays, and the two laws

Light travels as a wave. A wavefront is a surface on which the electric field has the same phase. A ray is a line drawn perpendicular to the wavefront; it shows the direction in which energy travels. In a uniform medium the rays are straight. At a flat boundary the incident ray, the reflected ray, the refracted ray and the normal all lie in one plane. The normal is the line perpendicular to the boundary at the point where the ray strikes.

Huygens' construction treats every point on a wavefront as a source of small secondary wavelets; the new wavefront is their envelope. One line is enough here: it explains why a plane wave stays plane and why the ray bends when the speed changes. The laws follow.

Law of reflection: the angle of incidence i equals the angle of reflection r′. Both angles are measured from the normal, not from the surface. Law of refraction is Snell's law.

n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r

n₁ is the refractive index of the first medium; n₂ is the refractive index of the second medium; i is the angle of incidence in medium 1, measured from the normal; r is the angle of refraction in medium 2, measured from the normal.

When n₂ > n₁, r < i: the ray bends toward the normal. When n₂ < n₁, it bends away. Some syllabi call this reflection and refraction of EM waves.

Refractive index, speed, and total internal reflection

The refractive index n of a medium is the ratio of the speed of light in vacuum to the speed in the medium. Because v ≤ c, n ≥ 1. Air is close to n = 1.00; water is about 1.33; ordinary glass is about 1.50. The frequency stays the same when light crosses a boundary. The speed and wavelength change.

n = c / v

n is the refractive index, a pure number; c = 3 × 10⁸ m/s is the speed of light in vacuum; v is the speed of light in the medium, in m/s.

The wavelength in the second medium is λ₂ = λ₁ (n₁/n₂), where λ₁ and λ₂ are the wavelengths in medium 1 and medium 2. This changed speed is what bends the wavefront and therefore the ray.

Total internal reflection occurs when light tries to go from a denser medium (higher n) to a rarer medium (lower n). At the critical angle C, the refracted ray grazes the boundary at 90°. Snell's law gives n₁ sin C = n₂ sin 90° = n₂.

sin C = n₂ / n₁

C is the critical angle measured from the normal; n₁ is the index of the denser medium the light starts in; n₂ is the index of the rarer medium. For a medium of index n against air, n₂ = 1 and sin C = 1/n.

If i > C, no refracted ray enters the second medium; all the light is reflected back. This is the idea behind optical fibres, where light is trapped inside a core of higher index surrounded by cladding of lower index (see Unit 5, Fibre Optics).

How much reflects at normal incidence

At normal incidence the ray strikes along the normal, so i = 0° and r = 0°. The boundary still splits the wave. For a transparent boundary with no absorption, the reflectance R is the fraction of power reflected, and the transmittance T is the fraction transmitted.

R = ((n₁ − n₂)/(n₁ + n₂))², T = 1 − R

n₁ is the index of the medium the light comes from; n₂ is the index of the medium it enters; R is a pure number between 0 and 1; T is also a pure number.

For air to glass, n₁ = 1.00 and n₂ = 1.50, R = 0.04 and T = 0.96: 4% reflects, 96% enters. For glass to air the square is the same, so R is still 0.04 at normal incidence. A larger index jump reflects more.

There is a phase rule at reflection. When light reflects from a medium of higher refractive index (denser medium, n₂ > n₁), the reflected electric field changes sign. A sign change is a phase change of π radians, equivalent to half a wavelength. When light reflects from a lower-index medium (rarer medium, n₂ < n₁), there is no such phase change. This π shift is the reason a thin film can cancel reflection; it enters every thin-film calculation in 4.2 and 4.5.

At oblique incidence the reflected fraction depends on polarization. Brewster's angle is the angle at which the reflected light is completely polarized; it is named here and worked out in 4.7.

Worked example

Question. A ray of light in air (n₁ = 1.00) strikes a flat glass surface (n₂ = 1.50) at an angle of 30.0° to the normal. (a) Find the angle of refraction. (b) Find the reflectance and transmittance at normal incidence for the same air-glass boundary.

  1. Use Snell's law: n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r.
  2. Solve for sin r: sin r = (n₁/n₂) sin i = (1.00/1.50) × sin 30° = 0.6667 × 0.5000 = 0.3333.
  3. Find r = sin⁻¹(0.3333) = 19.5°. The ray bends toward the normal because n₂ > n₁.
  4. At normal incidence: R = ((n₁ − n₂)/(n₁ + n₂))² = ((1.00 − 1.50)/(1.00 + 1.50))² = (−0.50/2.50)² = (−0.20)² = 0.0400.
  5. T = 1 − R = 1 − 0.0400 = 0.9600 = 96.0%.

Answer. r = 19.5°, R = 0.0400 (4.00%), T = 0.9600 (96.0%).

Exam traps

  • Angles of incidence, reflection and refraction are always measured from the normal. Measuring from the glass surface gives wrong numbers and is the most common slip.
  • The formula R = ((n₁ − n₂)/(n₁ + n₂))² is for normal incidence only. At an angle, the reflected fraction also depends on polarization.
  • The π phase change happens only when light reflects from a denser medium (higher n). Reflection from a rarer medium has no π change.
  • T = 1 − R assumes a transparent boundary with no absorption. A real glass surface may absorb or scatter some light.
  • Total internal reflection needs light going from higher n to lower n. It cannot happen from air to glass, whatever the angle.

4.2 Interference of light and thin films

Two coherent light waves meet point by point. Their path difference decides whether they add to brightness or cancel to darkness. This steady pattern is interference. It explains the colours of a soap bubble, the anti-reflection coating on a camera lens, and the precision of an interferometer. The same rule—phase difference set by path difference—runs through all of them.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for reflection at a boundary and the phase change on reflection, and Unit 2 for superposition of waves.

Coherent waves and the interference condition

Two sources are coherent when they emit waves of the same frequency with a constant phase difference. A laser is a practical coherent source. Two separate bulbs are not coherent: their phases change randomly, so bright and dark regions move too fast to see. Sustained interference needs coherent sources, comparable amplitudes, and a path difference within the coherence length—the distance over which the phase relation stays fixed.

δ = (2π/λ) Δ

δ is phase difference in radians; Δ is path difference in metres; λ is wavelength in metres.

At a point, the two waves have electric fields E₁ and E₂. Superposition gives E = E₁ + E₂. Intensity is proportional to the square of the resultant amplitude. For phase difference δ, the intensity is:

I = I₁ + I₂ + 2√(I₁ I₂) cos δ

I₁ and I₂ are the intensities of the two waves; δ is their phase difference.

When I₁ = I₂ = I₀, this becomes I = 2I₀(1 + cos δ) = 4I₀ cos²(δ/2). The intensity ranges from 0 to 4I₀. Maxima occur at δ = 2mπ, so Δ = mλ. Minima occur at δ = (2m + 1)π, so Δ = (2m + 1)λ/2. Here m = 0, ±1, ±2, … is the order.

Young's double slit

In Young's double slit, a single slit makes light coherent. Two narrow slits S₁ and S₂, separated by d, act as coherent sources. A screen is at distance D. Light from S₂ travels farther by Δ = d sin θ to a point P at angle θ. For small θ, sin θ ≈ tan θ = x/D, so Δ ≈ d x/D.

β = λD/d

β is fringe width in metres; λ is wavelength in metres; D is slit-to-screen distance in metres; d is slit separation in metres.

Bright fringes occur at x = mλD/d. Dark fringes occur at x = (m + 1/2)λD/d. The central fringe at x = 0 is bright because Δ = 0. Neighbouring bright fringes are β apart. Fringe width grows with λ and D, and falls with d. In water, λ decreases, so β decreases. A red fringe is wider than a blue fringe for the same d and D.

Thin films

A thin film of thickness t and refractive index μ has two surfaces. Light reflects from the top surface and from the bottom surface. The two reflected waves recombine. The extra optical path inside the film is 2μt cos r, where r is the angle of refraction inside the film. At normal incidence r = 0 and cos r = 1.

One of the two reflections is from a denser medium and the other from a rarer medium, or the reverse. That single reversed reflection adds λ/2 to the path difference. The total path difference is 2μt cos r + λ/2. The sign of λ/2 does not change the conditions.

For reflected light, maxima occur when 2μt cos r = (2m + 1)λ/2. Minima occur when 2μt cos r = mλ. In transmitted light the conditions swap: maxima at 2μt cos r = mλ, minima at 2μt cos r = (2m + 1)λ/2. Some books write (2m − 1)λ/2 for the reflected maxima; it is the same set of odd multiples.

A soap bubble or oil slick shows colours because t varies across the surface. At each thickness, some wavelengths satisfy the bright condition and others the dark condition. An anti-reflection coating uses the same rule to cancel reflected light; see Chapter 4.5.

Worked example

Question. In Young's double-slit experiment, light of wavelength 600 nm falls on two slits 1.0 mm apart. The screen is 1.0 m from the slits. Find the fringe width and the distance of the third bright fringe from the centre.

  1. List the data: λ = 600 nm = 600 × 10⁻⁹ m = 6.00 × 10⁻⁷ m; d = 1.0 mm = 1.0 × 10⁻³ m; D = 1.0 m.
  2. Use the fringe-width formula: β = λD/d.
  3. Substitute: β = (6.00 × 10⁻⁷ × 1.0)/(1.0 × 10⁻³) = 6.00 × 10⁻⁴ m.
  4. Convert: β = 0.60 mm.
  5. The third bright fringe is at x₃ = 3β = 3 × 6.00 × 10⁻⁴ = 1.80 × 10⁻³ m = 1.80 mm.

Answer. Fringe width β = 6.0 × 10⁻⁴ m (0.0006 m, or 0.60 mm); the third bright fringe is at 1.80 × 10⁻³ m (0.0018 m, or 1.80 mm) from the centre.

Exam traps

  • Using 2μt = mλ for a bright reflected fringe. The extra λ/2 from one reversed reflection makes reflected maxima odd multiples of λ/2: 2μt cos r = (2m + 1)λ/2.
  • Dropping cos r at oblique incidence. The path inside the film is 2μt cos r, not 2μt, unless light falls normally.
  • Swapping reflected and transmitted conditions. In transmitted light the bright and dark conditions are interchanged.
  • Confusing path difference with phase difference. Multiply Δ by 2π/λ to get δ. A path difference of λ gives a phase difference of 2π, not zero.
  • Assuming any two sources interfere. Random phase from two bulbs gives no steady fringe pattern; coherence is required.

4.3 Air wedge and Newton's rings

A thin air film between two glass surfaces gives straight fringes or rings. The film thickness changes steadily, so each fringe marks a known multiple of half a wavelength. Counting fringes measures spacer thickness, lens radius, or surface flatness. Optical shops test gauge blocks and lens surfaces this way.

You need Chapter 4.2 for thin-film conditions and the half-wave phase change, and Chapter 4.1 for reflection at a denser medium.

The air wedge

Two flat glass plates touch along one edge; a hair or wire holds the other ends apart. The air film is wedge-shaped: zero at the contact edge, thicker toward the spacer. Monochromatic light of wavelength λ falls normally. One reflection is from the lower face of the top plate, the other from the upper face of the bottom plate. These waves interfere.

At distance x from the edge the film thickness is t = xθ, where θ is the wedge angle in radians. The film is thin, so tan θ ≈ θ and t is linear in x. The wave crossing the film travels down and back, extra path 2t. The reflection at the lower surface, air to glass, adds a phase π, that is λ/2.

2t + λ/2 = (2m + 1)λ/2  (dark, m = 0, 1, 2, …)     2t + λ/2 = mλ  (bright, m = 1, 2, 3, …)

t is air-film thickness, λ is wavelength in air, m is fringe order. Simplified: dark at 2t = mλ (m = 0, 1, 2, …), bright at 2t = (2m − 1)λ/2 (m = 1, 2, 3, …). m = 0 gives t = 0, the dark contact edge.

At contact t = 0. This satisfies 2t = mλ with m = 0, so the edge is dark. The first dark fringe is at t = λ/2, the second at t = λ. Since t grows linearly with x, fringes are straight, parallel to the edge, and equally spaced.

β = λ / (2θ)

β is fringe width, the spacing between neighbouring dark fringes measured perpendicular to the contact edge; λ is wavelength; θ is wedge angle in radians. With a liquid of refractive index μ filling the gap the path is 2μt, so β = λ/(2μθ).

If the plate has length L, the gap at the far end is d = Lθ = Lλ/(2β). Counting is quicker: each fringe step raises thickness by λ/2, so N fringes across the plate mean d = Nλ/2.

Newton's rings

A plano-convex lens of radius of curvature R rests curved face down on a flat glass plate. A thin air film fills the gap, zero where the lens touches and thicker outward. At radius r the film thickness is t = r²/(2R). The wedge has been rolled into a circle: thickness grows in every direction from a single point, so straight fringes become rings.

t = r² / (2R)

r is distance from the point of contact, R is radius of curvature of the lens surface, and t is the air-film thickness there. It follows from r² = R² − (R − t)² = 2Rt − t², dropping t² because t is a few wavelengths while R is tens of centimetres.

D_m² = 4mRλ  (dark)      D_m² = 2(2m − 1)Rλ  (bright)

D_m is diameter of the m-th ring in reflected light, m = 0, 1, 2, …; R is lens radius of curvature in metres; λ is wavelength in metres. Dark from 2t = mλ with t = r²/2R; bright from 2t = (2m − 1)λ/2.

The centre is dark, because t = 0 there and m = 0 satisfies the dark condition. The first dark ring is m = 1, not m = 0; the dark spot counts as ring zero. Diameters grow as √m, so the tenth ring is only about 3.2 times the first wide, and rings crowd together as you move out.

Two rings give R without knowing the contact point exactly: R = (D_m² − D_n²)/(4(m − n)λ). Measure diameters with a travelling microscope in metres and R comes out in metres. Put a liquid of index μ between lens and plate and the rings shrink, D_m² = 4mRλ/μ; R stays the same.

Doing it at the bench

  1. Clean lens and plate; place lens curved face down. The film must be air, not dust.
  2. Use a sodium lamp. A plain glass plate at 45° sends light down; view reflected rings from above.
  3. Focus a travelling microscope on the dark centre.
  4. Read the crosswire at the centre of each dark ring on both sides of the centre. Diameter = difference of readings.
  5. Turn the screw one way only to avoid backlash.
  6. Plot D_m² against m: straight line through origin, slope 4Rλ. R from slope or two-ring formula.

Worked example

Question. In reflected sodium light (λ = 589.3 nm), the 5th and 15th dark rings have diameters 2.47 mm and 4.27 mm. Find R and the first ring diameter.

  1. λ = 589.3 × 10⁻⁹ m, D₅ = 2.47 × 10⁻³ m, D₁₅ = 4.27 × 10⁻³ m.
  2. R = (D₁₅² − D₅²)/(4(15 − 5)λ).
  3. D₁₅² ≈ 18.23 × 10⁻⁶ m², D₅² ≈ 6.10 × 10⁻⁶ m², difference ≈ 12.13 × 10⁻⁶ m².
  4. Denominator = 4 × 10 × 589.3 × 10⁻⁹ = 2.357 × 10⁻⁵ m.
  5. R = 12.13 × 10⁻⁶/2.357 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.515 m.
  6. D₁ = √(4Rλ) = √(4 × 0.515 × 589.3 × 10⁻⁹) = 1.10 × 10⁻³ m = 1.10 mm.

Answer. R ≈ 0.52 m; D₁ = 1.10 mm.

Worked example 2

Question. An air wedge: plates 10 cm long, sodium light λ = 589.3 nm, fringes 1.47 mm apart. Find wedge angle and spacer thickness.

  1. β = 1.47 × 10⁻³ m, L = 0.10 m.
  2. β = λ/(2θ), so θ = λ/(2β) = 589.3 × 10⁻⁹/(2 × 1.47 × 10⁻³) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ rad.
  3. Spacer d = Lθ = 0.10 × 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ m.
  4. Check: d = Lλ/(2β) gives the same value.

Answer. θ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ rad; d = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ m = 20 μm.

Exam traps

  • The centre is dark: m = 0 is the dark spot, and the first dark ring is m = 1. Counting the spot as ring 1 shifts every later ring by one.
  • D_m² = 4mRλ uses diameter, not radius. Convert millimetres to metres before squaring: 4.27 mm is 4.27 × 10⁻³ m.
  • With a liquid in the gap both formulas change: wedge β = λ/(2μθ) and rings D_m² = 4mRλ/μ. Forgetting μ gives R or spacer thickness wrong by the factor μ.
  • Reflected and transmitted patterns are complementary: reflected centre is dark, transmitted centre is bright. Read which one the question asks about.

4.4 Michelson interferometer

The Michelson interferometer splits one beam into two paths with a half-silvered plate, sends each path to a mirror, and recombines the returning beams. Every fringe that crosses the field marks half a wavelength of mirror movement. It turns a tiny length into a countable number of fringes. Laboratories use it to measure wavelength, the thickness of a thin transparent sheet, and the refractive index of a gas. Only a few universities teach it in the wave-optics unit.

You need path difference and fringe counting from Chapters 4.2 and 4.3.

Splitting and recombining the beam

The instrument has a monochromatic source, a half-silvered plate at 45°, a fixed mirror M₁, and a movable mirror M₂ driven by a micrometer. The plate divides the beam: one part goes to M₁, the other to M₂. A compensating plate of the same glass and thickness sits in the M₁ arm. It equalizes the glass path so that the air paths alone decide the interference. The virtual image M₂′ of M₂, seen through the plate, forms an air film with M₁. Its thickness is t = d, the mirror separation. Rays with the same θ give one ring, so the fringes are circular. The central ray has θ = 0 and path difference 2d. If M₂ moves by d, N fringes cross and d = Nλ/2, because each fringe is one half wavelength of path change. Thus the interferometer is a thin-film experiment with a variable film thickness.

Δ = 2d cos θ

Δ is the path difference; d is the separation between M₁ and the virtual image M₂′; θ is the angle of the ray inside the air film.

d = Nλ/2

d is the mirror movement; N is the number of fringes crossing the field; λ is the wavelength.

What it measures

To measure wavelength, move M₂ slowly and count N fringes for a measured d. Then λ = 2d/N. For a thin transparent sheet of refractive index μ and thickness t inserted in one arm, the extra optical path is 2(μ − 1)t. If m fringes shift, t = mλ/(2(μ − 1)). For a gas cell of length L, filling it from vacuum to gas changes the path by 2L(μ − 1); counting N fringes gives μ = 1 + Nλ/(2L). A micrometer scale reads d directly. The centre is bright when 2d = mλ and dark when 2d = (m + ½)λ. The instrument is named after Michelson. A practical run needs a stable table because vibration adds fringe noise. The same fringe count checks small displacements, since one fringe equals λ/2 of mirror movement. Link to thin-film coatings for the same path-difference idea.

Worked example

Question. The movable mirror M₂ is moved by 0.100 mm and 400 fringes cross the field of view. Find the wavelength of the light.

  1. Convert d = 0.100 mm = 0.100 × 10⁻³ m = 1.00 × 10⁻⁴ m.
  2. The path difference changes by 2d because the beam goes to the mirror and returns.
  3. Each fringe corresponds to one λ of path change, so Nλ = 2d.
  4. λ = 2d/N = (2 × 1.00 × 10⁻⁴ m)/400 = 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m.
  5. Convert: 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m = 500 nm.

Answer. The wavelength is 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m, or 500 nm.

Exam traps

  • The path difference changes by 2d, not d. Writing d = Nλ instead of d = Nλ/2 is the standard slip.
  • d is the movement of M₂ or the change in film thickness, not the full distance from the plate to the mirror. Convert mm to m before using λ.
  • Circular fringes come from a fixed θ giving a fixed path difference; they are not caused by the mirror shape.
  • The compensating plate must match the beam splitter in glass and thickness. It corrects glass paths, not air paths.
  • A count at one point and a count of new rings are different observations; use the number crossing a fixed point for d = Nλ/2.

4.5 Anti-reflection coatings

A bare glass surface reflects about 4% of light at each face. In a camera lens with many elements, or a solar cell cover, these reflections waste light and make ghosts. An anti-reflection coating is a thin film, often one quarter of a wavelength thick, placed on the glass. The wave reflected from the air–film surface and the wave reflected from the film–glass surface arrive half a cycle apart and cancel. Only a few universities teach this topic in detail.

You need Chapter 4.2 for thin-film interference and path difference.

Why a bare surface reflects

At normal incidence, one air–glass boundary reflects R = ((μ₁ − μ₂)/(μ₁ + μ₂))². For air μ₁ = 1.00 and glass μ₂ = 1.50, R = (0.50/2.50)² = 0.04, that is 4%. A lens with six elements has twelve such surfaces; if nothing is done, roughly 40% of the light can be lost by reflection. The reflected light also bounces inside the lens and forms stray images. The cure is a film whose two reflected waves interfere destructively. Light entering the film from air is partly reflected at the air–film boundary. The rest crosses the film and is partly reflected at the film–glass boundary. These two reflected waves travel back into the air and superpose. Their path difference is set by the film thickness. Choose the thickness so the waves cancel, and the surface reflects little while most light passes through. The coating absorbs almost nothing; it uses destructive interference to send the reflected energy forward.

Quarter-wave layer and real coatings

Use a film of refractive index μ_f and thickness t, with air above (μ₁ = 1.00) and glass below (μ_s = 1.50). Let λ be the vacuum wavelength at which the coating is designed. For cancellation, the extra path in the film, 2μ_f t, must be λ/2. Hence μ_f t = λ/4 and t = λ/(4 μ_f). Here both reflections are reversed: air to film is low to high index, and film to glass is also low to high. Both reflected waves get the same phase change, so reflection adds no extra relative phase. The quarter-wave path alone supplies the half-cycle. This differs from a soap film in air, where only one reflection is reversed. At the design wavelength, the amplitude reflectance at normal incidence is r = (n₁ n_s − n_f²)/(n₁ n_s + n_f²). Here n₁, n_f and n_s are the indices of air, film and substrate; they equal μ₁, μ_f and μ_s. R = r². Set r = 0: n₁ n_s = n_f², so μ_f = √(μ₁ μ_s). For air and glass this is √(1.00 × 1.50) = 1.22. A film with that index gives exactly zero reflection at λ. No common hard film has index 1.22. Magnesium fluoride, MgF₂, has μ = 1.38. It cuts the 4% reflection to about 1.4% at the design wavelength, but not to zero. The coating is tuned to one wavelength. At other wavelengths the condition fails and reflection returns; the surface may look purple or green in white light. For camera lenses and binoculars, several layers of different indices are stacked. Each layer adds another reflected wave, and the design chooses thicknesses so the waves cancel over a broad band. Such broadband coatings can reduce reflection below 1% across the visible range. Single MgF₂ layers are used on spectacle glass, camera filters and solar cell covers, where one design wavelength and low cost matter.

Worked example

Question. A magnesium fluoride film (μ_f = 1.38) coats glass (μ_s = 1.50) and is designed for λ = 550 nm. Find the quarter-wave thickness, the ideal film index, and the residual reflectance.

  1. t = λ/(4μ_f) = 550 nm/(4 × 1.38) = 550/5.52 nm = 99.64 nm, about 100 nm.
  2. Ideal index: μ_f = √(μ₁μ_s) = √(1.00 × 1.50) = 1.2247, about 1.22.
  3. r = (1.00×1.50 − 1.38²)/(1.00×1.50 + 1.38²) = (1.50 − 1.9044)/(1.50 + 1.9044) = −0.4044/3.4044 = −0.1188.
  4. R = r² = (−0.1188)² = 0.0141, about 1.4%.

Answer. t = 99.64 nm ≈ 100 nm; ideal index 1.22; residual reflectance R = 0.0141 ≈ 1.4%.

Exam traps

  • Both reflections are reversed only when air < film < glass; do not use the one-reversal soap-film condition.
  • The condition is 2μ_f t = λ/2, so t = λ/(4μ_f), with λ in vacuum.
  • A single layer gives zero only at one design wavelength; it is not broadband.
  • MgF₂ at 1.38 is not the ideal 1.22 for glass, so some reflection remains.
  • If the film index is above the glass index, the second reflection is not reversed and the phase rule changes.

4.6 Diffraction and diffraction gratings

Diffraction is the spreading of light after it passes an edge or a narrow opening. Each point of the opening acts as a tiny source, and all these wavelets add and cancel. A diffraction grating is many narrow slits spaced regularly; it sends each wavelength to its own angle. This turns a mixed beam into a spectrum and lets a spectrometer measure wavelength. Here you learn the single-slit pattern, the grating equation, the highest order, resolving power and dispersive power.

You need superposition of two waves from Chapter 4.2; this chapter adds many wavelets.

Single slit: many wavelets

In Chapter 4.2 two waves added. Here every point of an opening sends a wavelet, and countless wavelets add. Fresnel diffraction is the near-field pattern. Fraunhofer diffraction is the far-field pattern with plane wavefronts; this chapter uses Fraunhofer, with the screen far away.

a sin θ = mλ, m = ±1, ±2, …

a is slit width in m; θ is angle from the straight-through direction in rad; λ is wavelength in m; m is a non-zero integer. m = 0 is the central maximum, not a minimum.

The first minima lie at sin θ = ±λ/a. For small θ, θ ≈ λ/a; this is the angular half-width of the central maximum. On a screen at distance D, the central maximum has linear width 2Dλ/a. The intensity follows (sin β/β)² with β = πa sin θ/λ; zeros occur at β = ±π, ±2π, … The central peak is twice as wide as a side peak.

Grating equation and orders

A plane transmission grating has many equal slits of width a separated by opaque gaps b. The grating element is d = a + b. If N is lines per metre, d = 1/N. Light falls normally.

d sin θ = mλ, m = 0, ±1, ±2, …

d is grating element in m; θ is angle of order m; λ is wavelength in m; m is order. m = 0 is the central white order.

For m ≠ 0, sin θ = mλ/d, so larger λ goes to larger θ. A grating therefore separates wavelengths. A CD track, a reflection grating, shows rainbow colours for the same reason.

The highest order follows from sin θ ≤ 1: m_max = floor(d/λ). An order with m > d/λ has sin θ > 1 and does not appear. For a given d, red light has fewer orders than violet. Some orders may be weak or absent when the single-slit pattern puts a minimum there; one mention is enough.

Resolving power and dispersion

Resolving power tells how close two wavelengths can be and still appear as two lines. R = λ/Δλ = m N_total, where λ is the mean wavelength in m, Δλ is the smallest resolvable wavelength difference in m, m is the order, and N_total is the total number of illuminated lines. N_total = N × L, with L the illuminated grating length in m. More lines and a higher order give sharper lines. R is dimensionless.

dθ/dλ = m/(d cos θ)

dθ/dλ is dispersive power in rad/m; d is grating element in m; θ is the order angle; m is order. It gives the angular spread per metre of wavelength.

In a spectrometer, measure θ for a known order and use d sin θ = mλ to find an unknown λ. For two close lines, R must exceed λ/Δλ. Larger m and smaller d spread the spectrum more, but m cannot exceed m_max.

Worked example

Question. A plane transmission grating has 5000 lines per cm. Light of wavelength 600 nm falls normally on it. Find the grating element in metres and centimetres, the angles of the first three orders, and the highest order visible.

  1. N = 5000 lines/cm = 5000 × 100 = 5.00 × 10⁵ lines/m.
  2. d = 1/N = 1/(5.00 × 10⁵) = 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ m = 2.00 × 10⁻⁴ cm.
  3. λ = 600 nm = 6.00 × 10⁻⁷ m.
  4. m = 1: sin θ₁ = (1)(6.00 × 10⁻⁷)/(2.00 × 10⁻⁶) = 0.300; θ₁ = 17.5°.
  5. m = 2: sin θ₂ = 0.600; θ₂ = 36.9°.
  6. m = 3: sin θ₃ = 0.900; θ₃ = 64.2°.
  7. Highest order: d/λ = 2.00 × 10⁻⁶/6.00 × 10⁻⁷ = 3.33; m_max = floor(3.33) = 3. The fourth order needs sin θ = 1.20 > 1.

Answer. d = 2.00 × 10⁻⁶ m (2.00 × 10⁻⁴ cm); θ₁ = 17.5°, θ₂ = 36.9°, θ₃ = 64.2°; highest order is 3.

Exam traps

  • For a grating use d = a + b. The relation a sin θ = mλ gives single-slit minima, not grating maxima.
  • Check sin θ = mλ/d ≤ 1 before naming an order. An order with m > d/λ does not exist, so write m_max = floor(d/λ).
  • The small-angle formula θ ≈ λ/a is only for λ much smaller than a. For a grating the angles are often large; use sin θ = mλ/d.
  • Resolving power uses N_total, the total number of illuminated lines, not N lines per metre. Doubling the illuminated width doubles N_total and improves resolution.
  • Dispersive power dθ/dλ = m/(d cos θ) is in rad per metre. Do not mix it with resolving power, which is dimensionless.

4.7 Polarization of light

Light is a transverse electromagnetic wave: its electric field oscillates at right angles to the direction of travel, and that oscillation has a direction. Polarization is control of that direction. A polarizer passes one component and blocks the perpendicular one, so two crossed polarizers cut the beam to zero — proof that light is transverse. Only a few universities teach this chapter. Sunglasses use it against road glare, LCD screens use it to switch pixels, and engineers use it to see stress inside clear plastic models.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for reflection at a boundary, and Unit 3 for transverse electromagnetic waves.

Transverse nature, and three descriptions

Sound travels as a longitudinal wave, so it cannot be polarized. Light can, because its oscillation is transverse. Rotate an analyser behind a fixed polarizer: at 0° light passes, at 90° the intensity is zero, at 180° it passes again. Nothing longitudinal can do this.

Unpolarized light has its electric field direction changing randomly and rapidly; sunlight and bulb light are like this. Plane polarized light has the field in one plane only.

Partially polarized light is a mixture of polarized and unpolarized light. Its degree of polarization is P = (Imax − Imin)/(Imax + Imin), where Imax and Imin are the largest and smallest intensities transmitted as the analyser is rotated; P = 0 is unpolarized light and P = 1 is plane polarized light.

I = I₀ cos²θ

I is the intensity after the analyser; I₀ is the intensity of plane polarized light falling on it; θ is the angle between the polarizer and analyser axes.

This is Malus's law. At θ = 90° the transmitted intensity is zero, which is the crossed-Polaroid test.

Four ways to produce polarized light

  • By reflection: at Brewster's angle the reflected ray is completely plane polarized, with its field perpendicular to the plane of incidence.
  • By dichroism: a Polaroid (H-sheet) is stretched polyvinyl alcohol doped with iodine. It absorbs one component and transmits the other. Cheap, large, used in every lab and in LCDs.
  • By scattering: light scattered by air molecules is partly polarized. Look at the sky at 90° from the sun through a Polaroid.
  • By double refraction: calcite splits a ray into an ordinary ray (obeys Snell's law, same refractive index in all directions) and an extraordinary ray (index depends on direction). The direction with no double refraction is the optic axis. A Nicol prism is a cut calcite crystal that uses total internal reflection to throw away the ordinary ray.

tan θ_p = μ

θ_p is Brewster's angle measured from the normal; μ is the refractive index of the reflecting medium. For glass, μ = 1.50 gives θ_p = 56.3°.

Wave plates, optical activity, uses

A quarter-wave plate introduces a path difference of λ/4 (phase difference π/2) between its two components. When the incident plane of polarization is at 45° to the optic axis, the two components have equal amplitude and the emergent light is circularly polarized; at other angles the emergent light is elliptically polarized.

A half-wave plate introduces λ/2 and rotates the plane of polarization by 2θ, where θ is the angle between the incident plane and the optic axis.

Optical activity: some substances, such as sugar solution and quartz, rotate the plane of polarization as light passes. The rotation is proportional to concentration and to path length, so a polarimeter measures sugar strength.

Uses: sunglasses against glare, LCD screens, and stress analysis — a loaded clear plastic model between crossed polarizers shows coloured stress bands.

Worked example

Question. Unpolarized light of intensity 64 W/m² falls on a Polaroid. A second Polaroid has its axis at 60° to the first. Find the intensity after each Polaroid, and the Brewster angle for glass of refractive index 1.50.

  1. First Polaroid: unpolarized light loses half its intensity, I₁ = 64/2 = 32.0 W/m².
  2. Angle between the two axes is θ = 60°.
  3. Malus's law: I₂ = I₁ cos²60° = 32.0 × (0.5)² = 32.0 × 0.25 = 8.0 W/m².
  4. Brewster: tan θ_p = μ = 1.50, so θ_p = tan⁻¹(1.50) = 56.31°, measured from the normal.

Answer. The intensities are 32.0 W/m² after the first Polaroid and 8.0 W/m² after the second; the Brewster angle is 56.31°.

Exam traps

  • Unpolarized light loses half its intensity at the first polarizer; skipping that factor of ½ doubles the final answer.
  • In Malus's law θ is the angle between the two polarizer axes, not the angle of incidence.
  • Brewster's angle is measured from the normal, not from the surface; 56.3° for glass, not 33.7°.
  • Only transverse waves can be polarized, so a question about polarizing sound has no answer.
  • At Brewster's angle θ_p + θ_r = 90°, where θ_r is the angle of refraction; so θ_r = 90° − θ_p (33.7° for glass), not θ_p + 90°.

Takeaways

  1. At any boundary a light wave splits into a reflected part and a refracted part, and the angles obey the law of reflection and Snell's law.
  2. Two coherent waves added point by point give a steady pattern of bright and dark fringes fixed by their path difference.
  3. In a thin film the reflected waves carry a path difference of about 2μt cos r, plus a half-wave loss when reflection happens at a denser medium.
  4. An air wedge converts fringe counting into a ruler: each fringe stands for a thickness change of λ/2.
  5. Newton's rings measure the radius of curvature of a lens from the ring radii, because rm² grows linearly with the ring number.
  6. A Michelson interferometer turns mirror movement into a fringe count, with each fringe equal to λ/2 of travel.
  7. A quarter-wave anti-reflection coating cancels reflection by making the two reflected waves equal in amplitude and opposite in phase.
  8. A grating spreads different wavelengths to different angles through (a + b) sin θ = mλ, which is how a spectrometer reads wavelength.
  9. Light is a transverse wave, so its electric field has a direction; a polarizer selects one direction and Malus's law gives the transmitted intensity.

Two glass plates touching at one edge, separated by a foil at the other, film of air between them; Thickness at distance x from the contact edge: t = xθ; Path difference 2t plus λ/2, so dark fringes where 2t = mλ; Fringe width β = λ/(2θ); consecutive fringes differ in thickness by λ/2; Measure β over many fringes with a travelling microscope, then tfoil = Lθ.

Apply Describe Newton's rings formed in reflected light. Derive rm² = mλR for the m-th dark ring and explain how the radius of curvature of the lens surface is determined. 13 marks

Plano-convex lens on a flat plate; air film thickness grows with radius; Geometry: t = r²/(2R) for small r; Path difference 2t + λ/2; dark when 2t = mλ; Hence rm² = mλR; the square of the radius grows linearly with ring number; Plot rm² against m: slope gives λR, so R = slope/λ.

Apply Draw and explain the Michelson interferometer. Show that a mirror movement of λ/2 shifts the pattern by one fringe, and give three applications. 13 marks

Half-silvered plate splits the beam into two arms; mirrors send them back; Compensating plate equalises the glass path in both arms; Optical path difference 2d cos θ; circular fringes; One fringe equals a path change of λ, hence mirror travel λ/2 per fringe; Applications: measuring small displacements, refractive index of gases, wavelength comparison, Fourier-transform spectroscopy.

Apply Describe a plane transmission grating and derive (a + b) sin θ = mλ. Explain how the wavelength of sodium light is measured with it. 12 marks

Grating: N parallel slits of width a and opaque spacing b, so the grating element is (a + b); Path difference between neighbouring slits is (a + b) sin θ; maxima when this equals mλ; Zero order is white; higher orders separate the wavelengths; Spectrometer: set the grating normal to the collimator, measure θ on both sides of the direct image; λ = (a + b) sin θ / m, using the mean of the two angles.

Apply In a Michelson interferometer, moving one mirror by 0.030 mm shifts 100 fringes across the field. Find the wavelength. In an air wedge with λ = 589 nm, 30 dark fringes span 1.5 cm; find the wedge angle and the foil thickness at 5 cm from the contact edge. 10 marks

Use 2d = Nλ, so λ = 2 × 3.0 × 10⁻⁵ m / 100 = 6.0 × 10⁻⁷ m = 600 nm; Fringe width β = 1.5 × 10⁻² m / 30 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ m; θ = λ/(2β) = 5.89 × 10⁻⁷ / (2 × 5.0 × 10⁻⁴) = 5.9 × 10⁻⁴ rad; Foil thickness at L = 0.05 m: t = Lθ ≈ 2.9 × 10⁻⁵ m = 29 μm.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare interference and diffraction point by point: origin of the fringes, width of the fringes, intensity distribution, and number of sources involved. 13 marks

Interference comes from two separate coherent wavefronts; diffraction from the infinite set of wavelets of one wavefront; Interference fringes are equally spaced and of equal intensity; diffraction fringes are unequal in width and fall off in intensity; In interference the dark fringes are truly dark; in diffraction the minima are not perfectly dark; Interference needs two sources; diffraction needs one slit or edge; Both follow the same superposition rule and the same path-difference conditions.

Analyze Explain why the centre of Newton's rings is dark in reflected light and bright in transmitted light. Describe the changes when a liquid of refractive index μ is placed between the lens and the plate. 12 marks

At the centre the film thickness tends to zero, so the only path difference is the half-wave loss on reflection; Hence reflected light cancels at the centre; in transmitted light the loss occurs once, so the centre is bright; With liquid of index μ the path becomes 2μt, so rings shrink: rm² = mλR/μ; If μ lies between 1 and the glass index the half-wave loss stays at the liquid-glass surface and the pattern keeps the same form; Ring size therefore measures the liquid's refractive index.

Analyze Explain why a grating produces much sharper maxima than two slits. Define resolving power of a grating, derive R = mN, and explain missing orders. 12 marks

With N slits the amplitude adds N times but the intensity N² times at the maxima, so peaks become sharp and narrow; Between principal maxima there are N − 1 minima and N − 2 weak secondary maxima; Resolving power R = λ/Δλ = mN; more slits or higher order resolves closer wavelengths; Missing orders occur when a diffraction minimum of one slit falls on an interference maximum of the grating; Condition: (a + b)/a = m/n for integers m and n.

Evaluate A silicon solar cell has refractive index 3.5. Choose a coating material and thickness that minimises reflection at 600 nm. Evaluate how well the same coating performs at 450 nm and at 750 nm, and state what design change would widen the low-reflection band. 14 marks

Ideal index μ = √3.5 ≈ 1.87; no common material matches exactly, so choose the nearest available and accept partial cancellation; Thickness t = λ/(4μ) ≈ 600/(4 × 1.87) ≈ 80 nm; At 450 nm and 750 nm the optical thickness is no longer λ/4, so the two reflected waves are not in exact antiphase and reflection rises; Reflection also rises because the two reflected amplitudes are unequal when μ ≠ √ns; A multilayer stack, or a graded-index layer, broadens the band at extra cost.

Glossary

Air wedge
Two glass plates meeting at a small angle, enclosing a film of air whose thickness increases steadily.
Anti-reflection coating
A thin film on a surface, a quarter wavelength thick, that makes reflected waves cancel and so raises transmission.
Brewster angle
The angle of incidence at which the reflected light is completely plane polarized, given by tan θp = n.
Coherent sources
Two sources whose waves keep a constant phase difference, so a steady interference pattern forms.
Diffraction
The spreading of light around obstacles and through narrow slits, from superposition of wavelets of one wavefront.
Diffraction grating
A plate with a large number of equally spaced parallel slits that disperses light into orders.
Fringe
A bright or dark band in an interference or diffraction pattern.
Fringe width
The distance between the centres of two neighbouring bright fringes, usually written β.
Grating element
The distance (a + b) between the centres of two neighbouring slits, with a the slit width and b the opaque gap.
Half-wave loss
An extra phase change of π, equivalent to a path change of λ/2, when light reflects from a denser medium.
Malus's law
The transmitted intensity through an analyser is I = I₀ cos²θ, with θ measured from the polarizer axis.
Michelson interferometer
An instrument that splits a beam into two arms with a half-silvered plate and recombines them to form fringes.
Newton's rings
Circular fringes formed by the air film between a plano-convex lens and a flat glass plate.
Normal
The perpendicular to the boundary at the point of incidence, from which all angles are measured.
Optical path length
The product μ × (geometrical distance), the distance the light would cover in vacuum in the same time.
Phase difference
The angle by which one wave leads another, equal to (2π/λ) times the path difference.
Polarization
The restriction of the electric field of a transverse wave to one direction or to a definite pattern of rotation.
Polarizer
A device that transmits only the component of the electric field along one axis.
Ray
A line drawn perpendicular to the wavefront, showing the direction of energy flow.
Refractive index
The ratio n = c/v of the speed of light in vacuum to its speed in the medium.
Resolving power of a grating
The ratio λ/Δλ, where Δλ is the smallest wavelength difference the grating can just separate.
Snell's law
The relation n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r obeyed by a ray crossing a boundary between two media.
Thin film interference
Interference between the waves reflected from the top and bottom surfaces of a film of thickness comparable to λ.
Wavefront
A surface joining all points of a wave that have the same phase at a given instant.
