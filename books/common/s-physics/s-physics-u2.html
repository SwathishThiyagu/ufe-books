2.1 Simple harmonic motion

Pull a spring and let go. It pulls back harder the farther you stretch it, so the motion repeats around the equilibrium point. When the restoring force is proportional to displacement, the motion is simple harmonic motion (SHM). The displacement follows a sine or cosine in time. The period depends on the mass and the stiffness, not on how far you pulled. This one model covers pendulums, vibrating machinery, clock escapements and the first step towards waves.

You need: Newton's second law and basic trigonometry from Unit 1.

Restoring force and the equation of motion

Stretch a spring by x metres from its natural length. It pulls back with F = -k x, where k is the spring constant in newtons per metre (N/m). The minus sign says the force points opposite to the displacement. This is the defining condition of simple harmonic motion (SHM): the restoring force, and therefore the acceleration, is proportional to displacement and directed towards equilibrium.

Newton's second law for a mass m (kg) on the spring is m a = -k x. Write a = d²x/dt². Then d²x/dt² = -(k/m) x. Define ω² = k/m, so ω = √(k/m) with ω in radians per second (rad/s). The equation becomes d²x/dt² = -ω² x.

m d²x/dt² = -k x  →  d²x/dt² = -ω²x,   ω² = k/m

m is mass in kg; x is displacement in m; k is spring constant in N/m; ω is angular frequency in rad/s; t is time in s.

The solution is x = A sin(ωt + φ). A is the amplitude in metres, the largest displacement from equilibrium. ω is the angular frequency in rad/s. φ is the phase constant in radians, fixed by the starting position and speed. A and φ come from the start; ω comes only from the system. That is why the period is fixed by the system, not by the amplitude.

Velocity, acceleration and the reference circle

Differentiate x = A sin(ωt + φ) once: v = Aω cos(ωt + φ). Speed is maximum at the centre (x = 0), where cos = ±1, so vmax = Aω. Differentiate again: a = -Aω² sin(ωt + φ) = -ω²x. Acceleration is maximum at the ends (x = ±A), where amax = Aω², and zero at the centre.

v = Aω cos(ωt + φ),   a = -ω²x = -Aω² sin(ωt + φ)

v is velocity in m/s; a is acceleration in m/s²; A is amplitude in m; ω is angular frequency in rad/s; φ is phase in rad; t is time in s; x is displacement in m.

Reference circle: a point moves round a circle of radius A at constant angular speed ω. Its projection on a diameter moves exactly as x = A sin(ωt + φ). One full circle takes T = 2π/ω seconds, the period. Frequency is f = 1/T = ω/(2π) hertz (Hz), so ω = 2πf. Some syllabi call ω the angular frequency or pulsatance; both mean the same rad/s quantity.

T = 2π/ω,   f = 1/T = ω/(2π),   v<sub>max</sub> = Aω,   a<sub>max</sub> = Aω²

T is period in s; f is frequency in Hz; ω is angular frequency in rad/s; A is amplitude in m.

Phase ωt + φ advances by 2π rad in one period. Two oscillations with different amplitudes but the same ω and φ cross zero at the same instants. Only the height changes, not the timing (figure).

Spring–mass and the simple pendulum

For a spring–mass system, ω = √(k/m) and T = 2π√(m/k). A stiffer spring (larger k) gives a shorter period. A larger mass gives a longer period. With k = 50 N/m and m = 0.50 kg, ω = 10 rad/s.

A simple pendulum is a small bob of mass m on a light string of length l metres. Pull the bob aside through a small angle θ. The restoring force along the arc is mg sinθ, with g = 9.8 m/s². For small θ in radians, sinθ ≈ θ, and the arc displacement is x = lθ. Then the restoring force is mgθ = (mg/l)x, so a = -(g/l)x. Compare with a = -ω²x: ω = √(g/l) and T = 2π√(l/g).

The small-angle condition is part of the formula. Keep θ below about 0.17 rad (10°). Beyond that, sinθ < θ, the restoring force is weaker than proportional, and the period rises with amplitude. The formula also assumes a light string, a point bob, no air drag and a rigid support. T does not contain m: heavy and light bobs of the same length have the same small-angle period.

Systemω (rad/s)T (s)
Spring–mass√(k/m)2π√(m/k)
Simple pendulum√(g/l)2π√(l/g)

Worked example

Question. A 0.50 kg mass hangs on a spring of stiffness 50 N/m. The mass is pulled down 2.0 cm from equilibrium and released. Find the angular frequency, the period and the maximum speed.

  1. List the data: m = 0.50 kg, k = 50 N/m, A = 2.0 cm = 0.020 m.
  2. Angular frequency: ω = √(k/m) = √(50/0.50) = √100 = 10 rad/s.
  3. Period: T = 2π/ω = 2π/10 = 0.628 s.
  4. Maximum speed: vmax = Aω = 0.020 × 10 = 0.20 m/s.

Answer. ω = 10 rad/s, T = 0.628 s, vmax = 0.20 m/s.

Exam traps

  • ω is not f. ω = 2πf rad/s; f = ω/(2π) Hz. Substituting ω for f puts a factor 2π into T, f and vmax.
  • Set the calculator to radians before evaluating sin(ωt + φ). Degrees give a wrong displacement except at a few angles.
  • Do not use T = 2π√(l/g) for large swings. The formula needs small θ; at 30° the real period is noticeably larger.
  • Convert amplitude to metres before computing vmax = Aω: 2.0 cm is 0.020 m, not 2.0 m.
  • At the extreme position the speed is zero, not maximum. Speed is maximum at the centre, where acceleration is zero.

2.2 Torsional pendulum and couple per unit twist

A torsional pendulum is a disc hanging from a thin wire. Twist the disc by an angle θ (rad) and the wire untwists itself. The wire applies a restoring torque τ (N m) opposite to θ, and for small twists τ = -Cθ. Here C is the couple per unit twist (N m/rad), the torque needed to hold one radian of twist. The disc then oscillates with period T = 2π√(I/C), where I (kg m²) is its moment of inertia. Torsion clocks, galvanometers and MEMS sensors use this motion. Only a few universities teach the full derivation at this depth.

You need: 2.1 SHM for the restoring-force pattern, and rigidity modulus from Unit 1, Elasticity.

Restoring torque and C

Hang a disc by a thin vertical wire. Twist the disc about the wire by θ rad. The wire is twisted, so its material tries to return to the straight state. It exerts a torque τ on the disc. For small θ, τ = -Cθ. The constant C is the couple per unit twist: the torque needed to hold the wire at one radian of twist. Its unit is N m/rad. Some syllabi call C the torsional stiffness or torsional constant.

τ = -Cθ

τ is the restoring torque in N m; C is the couple per unit twist in N m/rad; θ is the twist angle in rad. The minus sign means τ opposes θ.

The minus sign produces SHM, just like F = -kx in 2.1. A positive θ gives a negative τ. The figure plots this line for C = 0.40 N m/rad; the slope is -C and the line passes through the origin.

For a uniform wire, C comes from elasticity. C = πηr⁴/(2l). Here η (Pa) is the rigidity modulus, r (m) is the wire radius, and l (m) is the length between the fixed support and the disc. r is radius, not diameter. Because of r⁴, doubling r makes C 16 times larger; doubling l halves C. C is per radian. A value quoted per degree must be multiplied by 180/π before use.

Period of the disc

Let I (kg m²) be the moment of inertia of the disc about the wire axis. For a uniform disc of mass M and radius R, I = ½MR². Newton's second law for rotation gives Iα = τ, with α = d²θ/dt². Using τ = -Cθ, I d²θ/dt² = -Cθ, so d²θ/dt² + (C/I)θ = 0. This is the SHM equation with ω² = C/I.

T = 2π√(I/C), f = 1/T

T is the period in s; f is the frequency in Hz; I is the moment of inertia in kg m²; C is the couple per unit twist in N m/rad.

The period does not depend on the amplitude for small θ. A heavier or larger disc has larger I and a longer T. A stiffer or shorter wire has larger C and a shorter T. To find η, measure T with a known I, get C = 4π²I/T², then use η = 2lC/(πr⁴). Torsion galvanometers, old clocks and MEMS gyroscopes use this balance. Damping is ignored here; see 2.3.

Worked example

Question. A disc of moment of inertia I = 0.020 kg m² is suspended by a wire whose couple per unit twist is C = 0.080 N m/rad. Find its period of torsional oscillation.

  1. Write the period formula: T = 2π√(I/C).
  2. Substitute I = 0.020 kg m² and C = 0.080 N m/rad: I/C = 0.020/0.080 = 0.25 s².
  3. Take the square root: √0.25 = 0.50 s.
  4. Multiply by 2π: T = 2π × 0.50 = 3.14 s.

Answer. The period is T = 3.14 s, about π seconds.

Exam traps

  • Using the diameter as r in C = πηr⁴/(2l). The fourth power turns a factor 2 error into 16.
  • Forgetting that C is per radian. If C is given per degree, convert with 180/π before using T = 2π√(I/C).
  • Dropping the minus sign. It fixes the restoring direction; the period formula uses the magnitude C.
  • Confusing C (N m/rad) with rigidity modulus η (Pa). They are linked by geometry, not the same.
  • Using I = MR² for a solid disc; for a uniform disc about its centre I = ½MR².

2.3 Damped and forced oscillations; resonance

A real oscillator loses energy to friction, so its amplitude falls with time. This is damping. A periodic push can replace that energy, and the response becomes large when the push frequency matches a natural frequency. That is resonance. The same physics explains a car shock absorber settling after a speed breaker, a radio tuning to one station, a bridge swaying in wind, and a piezoelectric crystal vibrating strongly at its thickness resonance.

You need 2.1 Simple harmonic motion: x = A cos(ω₀t + φ), ω₀ = √(k/m), and energy in an oscillator.

Damping removes oscillator energy

Damping is the loss of oscillator energy to friction or other resistance. For slow motion in air or oil, take a damping force F_b = −b v, where v is speed in m/s and b is the damping constant in kg/s. Newton's second law for mass m in kg on a spring of stiffness k in N/m gives m x'' + b x' + k x = 0. The natural angular frequency is ω₀ = √(k/m) in rad/s. The damping ratio ζ = b/(2√(mk)) is dimensionless and compares damping with the critical value.

x = A e^(−bt/(2m)) cos(ω_d t + φ),  ω_d = √(ω₀² − (b/2m)²)

x is displacement in m; A is the amplitude coefficient at t = 0 in m, and the actual starting displacement is x(0) = A cos φ; b is damping constant in kg/s; m is mass in kg; φ is initial phase in rad; ω_d is damped angular frequency in rad/s; ω₀ = √(k/m) is natural angular frequency in rad/s.

For ζ < 1 the oscillator is underdamped: the motion oscillates with steadily falling amplitude at frequency ω_d. The factor e^(−bt/(2m)) is the decaying envelope. The damped period is T_d = 2π/ω_d. The logarithmic decrement is δ = (b/2m)T_d = 2πζ/√(1−ζ²), the log ratio of successive peak amplitudes. Because ω_d < ω₀, damping lowers the observed frequency; do not call ω_d the natural frequency. For ζ = 1 (critical damping) the motion returns to equilibrium in the shortest time without oscillating; critical damping occurs at b = 2√(mk). For ζ > 1 (overdamping) it returns more slowly, also without oscillation. A car shock absorber is designed near critical so the body settles after a speed breaker without bouncing.

Forced oscillation and resonance

A forced oscillator is driven by an external periodic force. The equation is m x'' + b x' + k x = F₀ cos ωt, where F₀ is the force amplitude in N and ω is the drive angular frequency in rad/s. The drive supplies energy every cycle; damping removes it. After start-up transients die out, the steady motion is x = A cos(ωt − φ).

A(ω) = (F₀/m) / √((ω₀² − ω²)² + (bω/m)²)

A is steady amplitude in m; F₀ is driving force amplitude in N; m is mass in kg; ω is drive angular frequency in rad/s; ω₀ = √(k/m) is natural angular frequency in rad/s; b is damping constant in kg/s.

At very low ω, A ≈ F₀/k: the spring controls and the mass follows the force. At very high ω, A → 0: the mass cannot keep up. Near ω = ω₀ the first term in the denominator is small. If b is small, A becomes large. This is resonance. The phase lag φ satisfies tan φ = (bω/m)/(ω₀² − ω²). It is small below resonance, passes 90° at ω = ω₀, and approaches 180° above resonance. The displacement amplitude peaks at ω_r = √(ω₀² − b²/(2m²)) when b² < 2mk. This amplitude resonance lies slightly below ω₀; only for zero damping is it exactly at ω₀. Some syllabi call this driven oscillation.

Quality factor and bandwidth

The quality factor Q measures how sharp a resonance is. Q = ω₀/Δω, where Δω is the full width of the power resonance curve at half its maximum value, in rad/s. The two frequencies at half power are ω₁ and ω₂, so Δω = ω₂ − ω₁. For the damped oscillator Q = mω₀/b = 1/(2ζ). High Q means small damping: a tall narrow peak, a long ringing time, and many cycles to build up or decay. Low Q means heavy damping: a low broad peak and quick settling. At ω = ω₀, for light damping, A ≈ F₀/(bω₀). A radio receiver uses a high-Q circuit to pick one station and reject nearby ones. A machine support uses damping to keep Q low and avoid large vibration. The figure shows two response curves with the same natural frequency; the lightly damped one has the taller, narrower peak near ω/ω₀ = 1.

Worked example

Question. A 0.20 kg mass on a spring of stiffness 80 N/m has damping constant b = 0.40 kg/s. Find the natural angular frequency ω₀, the damping ratio ζ, and the damped angular frequency ω_d.

  1. Write the data: m = 0.20 kg, k = 80 N/m, b = 0.40 kg/s.
  2. Natural angular frequency: ω₀ = √(k/m) = √(80/0.20) = √400 = 20 rad/s.
  3. Damping term: b/(2m) = 0.40/(2 × 0.20) = 1.00 s⁻¹.
  4. Damping ratio: ζ = b/(2√(mk)) = 0.40/(2√(0.20 × 80)) = 0.40/(2 × 4) = 0.05. Since ζ < 1, the motion is underdamped.
  5. Damped angular frequency: ω_d = √(ω₀² − (b/2m)²) = √(20² − 1²) = √399 = 19.974... ≈ 19.97 rad/s.

Answer. ω₀ = 20 rad/s, ζ = 0.05, and ω_d ≈ 19.97 rad/s.

Exam traps

  • Using ω_d as the natural frequency. ω_d = √(ω₀² − (b/2m)²) is the damped frequency; ω₀ = √(k/m) is the natural frequency.
  • Saying resonance is always exactly at ω₀. Amplitude resonance is at ω_r = √(ω₀² − b²/(2m²)); phase lag is 90° at ω₀.
  • Mixing ζ with b. ζ = b/(2√(mk)) is dimensionless; b has units kg/s.
  • Dropping the factor 2 in b/(2m). The amplitude envelope is e^(−bt/(2m)), not e^(−bt/m).
  • Using the peak amplitude formula at arbitrary ω. A(ω) must be computed from the full denominator.

2.4 Wave equation and waves on a stretched string

A plucked guitar string, a bridge cable and a sitar string all carry a disturbance that travels along them. This chapter writes the equation that any such disturbance must obey, the wave equation, and solves it for a stretched string. From the equation comes the speed v = √(T/μ), where T is tension and μ is mass per metre. The same equation, with a different speed, governs light in Unit 3, so the pattern learned here is reused everywhere.

You need: 2.1 Simple harmonic motion for ω and phase, and Newton's second law from Unit 1, Mechanics and Properties of Matter.

The wave equation from a small element

Stretch a string along the x-axis and hold it under tension. The tension T (newton, N) is the force each part of the string exerts on the part beside it, always along the string. The string has linear density μ (kilogram per metre, kg/m). Pluck it sideways and the string moves up and down: y(x, t) is the sideways displacement in metres of the point at position x metres at time t seconds. The motion is transverse, across the string's own length.

Take a short piece of the string between x and x + Δx. Tension pulls on it at both ends, along the tangents. Assume the slope ∂y/∂x is small, so the tension has the same magnitude T everywhere and the tangent angle θ obeys sin θ ≈ tan θ = ∂y/∂x.

Upward force at the right end = T(∂y/∂x) at x + Δx. Upward force at the left end = −T(∂y/∂x) at x. Net upward force = T[(∂y/∂x) at x + Δx − (∂y/∂x) at x] = T (∂²y/∂x²) Δx, using the definition of the derivative.

The mass of the piece is μ Δx and its upward acceleration is ∂²y/∂t². Newton's second law gives T (∂²y/∂x²) Δx = μ Δx (∂²y/∂t²). Cancel Δx:

∂²y/∂x² = (μ/T) ∂²y/∂t²

y is transverse displacement (m); x is position along the string (m); t is time (s); μ is linear density (kg/m); T is tension (N).

Every wave equation has the form ∂²y/∂x² = (1/v²) ∂²y/∂t², with v the wave speed. Comparing the two, 1/v² = μ/T, so v = √(T/μ). Nothing was assumed about the shape of the wave: the equation fell out of the forces alone.

Travelling waves and the sine wave

The wave equation is solved by any function whose argument is x − vt or x + vt:

y(x, t) = f(x − vt) + g(x + vt)

f is a shape travelling in the +x direction; g is a shape travelling in the −x direction; v is the speed (m/s).

Why: if you walk along at speed v in +x, the quantity x − vt stays fixed, so f is a rigid shape sliding along at speed v without changing form. The g term is the same picture going the other way. The string never moves forward; only the shape moves.

The most useful choice is sinusoidal:

y = A sin(kx − ωt + φ)

A is the amplitude (m); k = 2π/λ is the wave number (rad/m); λ is the wavelength (m); ω = 2πf is the angular frequency (rad/s); f is the frequency (Hz); φ is the starting phase (rad).

Hold the phase kx − ωt fixed. As t grows, x must grow to keep it fixed, and dx/dt = ω/k. So v = ω/k = fλ. One full wavelength passes a given point every period T₀ = 1/f seconds. Here T₀ is the period in seconds, not the tension T in newton.

Example: y = sin(x − t) with x in metres has k = 1 rad/m, ω = 1 rad/s and speed 1 m/s. At t = 0 a crest sits at x = 1.57 m; at t = 1 s the same crest sits at x = 2.57 m. The shape has shifted by 1 m, which is v × t.

Some books write A sin(ωt − kx) or A sin(2π(t/T − x/λ)). The sign choice only fixes where the phase starts; the speed and shape are the same.

Speed, tension and reflection at the ends

The result v = √(T/μ) says three things.

  • Tighter string, faster wave: doubling T multiplies v by √2 = 1.41.
  • Heavier string, slower wave: μ is mass per metre, not the total mass.
  • Speed does not depend on amplitude or on how hard you pluck, as long as the slope stays small.

On a guitar or a sitar, the tuning peg changes T and the finger changes the vibrating length; both change the note, through v and through the fixed-end condition described in 2.5.

At a fixed end the string cannot move, so a reflected crest returns as a trough; this is a phase change of π rad. At a free end, such as a ring sliding on a smooth rod, the end can move and the pulse returns upright, with no phase change. That is why a pulse sent down a rope tied to a wall comes back on the other side of the rope's axis.

The same wave equation and the same speed formula v = √(B/ρ) apply to sound in a pipe, where B is the bulk modulus (Pa) and ρ is the density (kg/m³). Pressure and displacement swap nodes and antinodes, so their reflection behaviour at a closed end is opposite (see 2.6). Unit 3 writes the identical equation for electric and magnetic fields, with v replaced by c.

Worked example

  • Using the total mass m in place of μ = m/L. Putting 0.030 into v = √(T/μ) gives √(120/0.030) = 63.25 m/s, too small by a factor √L = √1.5 = 1.22; μ must be mass per metre, not total mass.
  • Mixing units: T must be in newton and μ in kg/m. A mass quoted in grams must be divided by 1000 first.
  • Treating v = √(T/μ) and v = fλ as rival formulas. The first fixes the speed from the medium; the second only links v, f and λ, so you still need the length to get f.
  • Thinking the string itself moves along its length. Only the pattern moves; each bit of string moves transversely.
  • Dropping the 2π: k = 2π/λ and ω = 2πf, so v = ω/k, never ω × k.

2.5 Energy and power of waves; standing waves

A travelling wave moves energy, not matter. Each small piece of the string swings with the same amplitude and frequency, and hands its energy to the next piece. That is how a plucked guitar string pushes air, and how an antenna sends power outward. This chapter gives the formula for that power, then watches two equal waves travelling in opposite directions. They superpose into a standing wave: a pattern that oscillates in place, with dead points called nodes that no energy ever crosses.

You need the wave equation and the string speed v = √(T/μ) from 2.4, and the energy of SHM from 2.1.

Energy and power of a travelling wave

On a stretched string, every small element of length dx oscillates in simple harmonic motion. Its mass is μ dx, where μ is the mass per unit length in kg/m. For SHM the energy of a mass m with amplitude A and angular frequency ω = 2πf is ½ m ω² A². Putting m = μ dx, the energy in that element is ½ μ ω² A² dx. So the energy per unit length is u = ½ μ ω² A², in J/m.

In a time dt a fresh length v dt of string starts moving, where v is the wave speed. Power is energy per unit time, so P = u v = ½ μ v ω² A².

P = ½ μ v ω² A²

P is the average power in W; μ is the mass per unit length in kg/m; v is the wave speed in m/s; ω = 2πf is the angular frequency in rad/s; A is the amplitude in m.

Notice the squares. Double the amplitude and the power becomes four times larger. Double the frequency and again it is four times larger. For a wave spreading in a three-dimensional medium the same argument gives intensity I = ½ ρ v ω² A² in W/m², where ρ is the density in kg/m³ and I is power per unit area. Only a few universities teach this derivation at this depth; the result is what matters.

Two opposite waves make a standing wave

Send two waves of the same amplitude and frequency in opposite directions: y₁ = A sin(kx − ωt) and y₂ = A sin(kx + ωt). Here k = 2π/λ is the wave number in rad/m. Adding them with sin(C + D) + sin(C − D) = 2 sin C cos D, where C = kx and D = ωt, gives y = 2A sin(kx) cos(ωt).

The x part and the t part have separated. Each point of the string now just oscillates in time, with amplitude 2A|sin kx|. Where sin kx = 0 the amplitude is zero: these are nodes, at x = 0, λ/2, λ, … and they never move. Halfway between nodes |sin kx| = 1: these are antinodes, swinging through 2A. No energy travels along the string; it shifts back and forth between kinetic and elastic form, and never crosses a node.

A string fixed at both ends must have a node at x = 0 and at x = L, so L must hold a whole number of half wavelengths: L = nλ/2.

λₙ = 2L/n, fₙ = n v/(2L), n = 1, 2, 3, …

λₙ is the wavelength of the nth mode in m; L is the length between the fixed ends in m; fₙ is its frequency in Hz; v is the wave speed in m/s; n = 1 gives the fundamental.

So f₁ = v/(2L) and the higher modes are 2f₁, 3f₁, … Only this ladder of frequencies can exist on the string. That is why a guitar string sounds a musical note and not a hiss.

Worked example

Question. A string of mass per unit length μ = 0.020 kg/m is held at tension T = 120 N between two fixed points 1.5 m apart. (a) Find the wave speed and the fundamental frequency. (b) A travelling wave of frequency 100 Hz and amplitude 2.0 mm runs along the same string. Find its angular frequency and the average power it carries.

  1. v = √(T/μ) = √(120/0.020) = √6000 = 77.5 m/s.
  2. f₁ = v/(2L) = 77.5/(2 × 1.5) = 77.5/3.0 = 25.8 Hz.
  3. ω = 2πf = 2π × 100 = 628 rad/s.
  4. P = ½ μ v ω² A² = ½ × 0.020 × 77.5 × (628)² × (0.002)².
  5. Work the numbers: ½ × 0.020 = 0.010; 0.010 × 77.5 = 0.775; (628)² = 3.94 × 10⁵; (0.002)² = 4.0 × 10⁻⁶.
  6. P = 0.775 × 3.94 × 10⁵ × 4.0 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.775 × 1.578 = 1.22 W.

Answer. The wave speed is 77.5 m/s, the fundamental is 25.8 Hz, the angular frequency is 628 rad/s and the travelling wave carries 1.22 W.

Exam traps

  • Writing P = ½ μ v ω² A instead of A². Power depends on the square of the amplitude.
  • Dropping the factor ½. It comes from the average of sin² over one cycle.
  • Calling the fixed ends antinodes. A fixed end cannot move, so it is always a node; antinodes sit halfway between nodes.
  • Mixing μ and ρ. μ is mass per metre (kg/m) for a string; ρ is mass per cubic metre (kg/m³) for a bulk medium.
  • Thinking a standing wave carries energy from one end to the other. Its energy stays put, sloshing between kinetic and elastic form between two nodes.

2.6 Sound waves and acoustics

Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave: gas layers vibrate along the direction of travel, and ears read its frequency and intensity. Acoustics turns that wave into engineering numbers: pressure amplitude, speed, intensity, decibel level and room absorption. These numbers decide whether a lecture hall is clear, a cinema is comfortable, or a factory is dangerously loud. The same ideas size echo cancelling, noise barriers and ultrasound links. The pressure-wave model, decibel scale and Sabine’s room formula tie them together.

You need: wave equation and wave energy and intensity.

Pressure wave and intensity

Sound in a gas is a longitudinal wave: gas layers move back and forth along the direction of travel. The wave carries a pressure change p(x,t) = P₀ sin(kx − ωt). P₀ is pressure amplitude in pascal (Pa); k = 2π/λ is angular wavenumber in rad/m; ω = 2πf is angular frequency in rad/s; t is time in s. Intensity I is energy crossing unit area per second, in W/m². For a plane wave, I = P₀²/(2ρv). ρ is gas density in kg/m³; v is speed in m/s. Speed in air follows v = √(γRT/M). γ is the ratio of specific heats (dimensionless); R = 8.314 J/(mol K); T is absolute temperature in K; M is molar mass in kg/mol. At 293 K, air with γ = 1.40 and M = 0.029 kg/mol gives about 343 m/s. The formula assumes a plane wave in a uniform gas. Only a few universities teach acoustics at this depth.

Decibel scale

Human hearing spans a huge intensity range, so we use a logarithmic scale. Sound level is L = 10 log₁₀(I/I₀) dB. I is intensity in W/m²; I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m² is the reference at 1 kHz, near the hearing threshold. A change of 10 dB means ten times the intensity; 20 dB means one hundred times. Because intensity goes as pressure squared, L = 20 log₁₀(P₀/P₀,ref) for pressure in the same medium. The reference pressure in air is usually P₀,ref = 20 μPa. Ordinary talk is about 60 dB; a whisper about 30 dB; a loud concert can reach 110 dB. At 120 dB sound is near pain. Doubling distance from a point source in free space lowers intensity by factor 4, about 6 dB. A 3 dB rise is about twice the intensity. Use 10 log for intensity and 20 log for pressure. The decibel itself is dimensionless.

Room acoustics and Sabine’s formula

Inside a hall, sound reflects from walls, floor and ceiling. A hard surface returns most energy and creates echoes; a soft surface absorbs. The absorption coefficient α (dimensionless) is the fraction of incident energy absorbed. Effective absorption is A = Σ αᵢ Sᵢ, measured in m² sabins. Sᵢ is surface area in m². Sabine’s reverberation time T₆₀ is the time for sound to fall by 60 dB after the source stops: T₆₀ = 0.161 V/A. V is room volume in m³; A is total absorption in m² sabins. The constant 0.161 has unit s/m. Large V or small A gives long reverberation. Echo is a distinct reflected sound heard after about 0.1 s; reverberation is many overlapping reflections. Curtains, carpets and acoustic panels raise A and shorten T₆₀. A room with too long T₆₀ smears speech. A room with too short T₆₀ feels dead.

Worked example

Question. A hall has volume V = 500 m³ and total absorption A = 50 m² sabins. Find its reverberation time. Also find the sound level of intensity I = 10⁻⁵ W/m².

  1. Use Sabine’s formula: T₆₀ = 0.161 V/A.
  2. Put V = 500 m³ and A = 50 m² sabins: T₆₀ = 0.161 × 500/50 = 0.161 × 10 = 1.61 s.
  3. For sound level, use I₀ = 10⁻¹² W/m². Ratio I/I₀ = 10⁻⁵/10⁻¹² = 10⁷.
  4. L = 10 log₁₀(10⁷) = 10 × 7 = 70 dB. With one decimal, 70.0 dB.

Answer. T₆₀ = 1.61 s and L = 70.0 dB.

Exam traps

  • Using 20 log₁₀ for intensity level; 20 log₁₀ belongs to pressure or amplitude ratio.
  • Putting floor area in A; Sabine’s A is total absorption ΣαS, not room area.
  • Using T in °C in v = √(γRT/M); T must be kelvin.
  • Taking M in g/mol; use kg/mol so R = 8.314 J/(mol K) matches.
  • Using I₀ = 10⁻⁶ W/m²; the standard air reference is 10⁻¹² W/m².

2.7 Ultrasonics: production by piezoelectric method

Ultrasound is sound above 20 kHz, beyond hearing. A thin quartz plate, silvered on both faces and held between metal plates, is driven by a radio-frequency oscillator. The crystal changes thickness when a voltage is applied — the inverse piezoelectric effect — and at its thickness resonance it vibrates strongly and radiates an ultrasonic beam. The frequency comes from the plate's own thickness, not from the circuit, so it stays stable. Such transducers drive cleaning tanks, weld testing, medical scanners and sonar.

You need: 2.1 Simple harmonic motion and 2.3 resonance; the plated crystal is a driven oscillator.

The two piezoelectric effects

Squeeze certain crystals and charge appears on their faces, proportional to the stress and vanishing when it is removed. This is the piezoelectric effect. Quartz, Rochelle salt, barium titanate and lead zirconate titanate (PZT) show it. PZT is a cheap, strong ceramic; quartz is precise and stable.

The reverse also holds. Apply a voltage across those faces and the plate changes thickness; reverse the voltage and it shrinks instead of swelling. This is the inverse piezoelectric effect. An alternating voltage therefore makes the plate expand and contract at the supply frequency, pushing the surrounding air or water and starting a pressure wave. Production of ultrasound uses the inverse effect; detection uses the direct effect.

Construction: a crystal slab ground flat and parallel, silvered on both faces to form electrodes, and clamped between two metal plates that hold it and connect it to the circuit.

Thickness resonance

The plated plate is a vibrator with its own natural frequency, like a tuning fork. Its faces are free, so they swing with maximum amplitude while the middle stays still. That fundamental standing-wave pattern (2.5) fits half a wavelength into the thickness: λ = 2t.

f = v / (2t)

f is the fundamental thickness-resonance frequency in hertz; v is the longitudinal speed of sound in the crystal in m/s (about 5.7 × 10³ m/s for quartz); t is the plate thickness in metres.

Drive the plate at that frequency and its amplitude grows sharply — resonance, as with a pushed swing (2.3). Drive it elsewhere and it barely moves. Since t is fixed by grinding and v belongs to the crystal, f is fixed too. Thin plates give high frequencies: 2 mm of quartz gives about 1.4 MHz, 0.2 mm gives about 14 MHz. Harmonics 3f and 5f exist but are weaker; above about 20 MHz quartz becomes too fragile and PZT is used.

Driver and advantages

A radio-frequency oscillator feeds the two electrodes. Usually the crystal itself sits in the oscillator's feedback path, so the circuit locks to the crystal resonance and cannot drift. Only a few universities teach the circuit details; the principle is what the exam asks.

  • High frequency: megahertz from a plate a millimetre thick.
  • Stable: frequency is set by thickness, not by a coil or capacitor.
  • Rugged, small, cheap and low power.
  • One plate both transmits and, from a returning echo, generates a voltage by the direct effect.

Ultrasound means f > 20 kHz. Cleaning and flaw detection use 1–10 MHz; at 1 MHz in water (v = 1500 m/s) the wavelength is 1.5 mm. Applications are in 2.8.

Worked example

Question. A quartz plate 2.0 mm thick is used to produce ultrasound. The longitudinal speed of sound in quartz along the cut is 5.7 × 10³ m/s. (a) Find the fundamental thickness-resonance frequency. (b) Find the wavelength inside the crystal. (c) Find the frequency if the plate is lapped down to 1.0 mm.

  1. Convert the thickness: t = 2.0 mm = 2.0 × 10⁻³ m.
  2. At the fundamental, half a wavelength fits the thickness: λ = 2t = 2 × 2.0 × 10⁻³ = 4.0 × 10⁻³ m.
  3. Apply f = v/λ = 5.7 × 10³ ÷ 4.0 × 10⁻³ = 1.425 × 10⁶ Hz.
  4. Same result from f = v/(2t) = 5700 ÷ (2 × 0.002) = 5700 ÷ 0.004 = 1.425 × 10⁶ Hz ≈ 1.43 MHz.
  5. Check: λ = v/f = 5700 ÷ (1.425 × 10⁶) = 4.0 × 10⁻³ m = 4.0 mm, which is 2t.
  6. (c) With t = 1.0 × 10⁻³ m, f = 5700 ÷ (2 × 1.0 × 10⁻³) = 2.85 × 10⁶ Hz = 2.85 MHz; halving the thickness doubles the frequency.

Answer. The 2.0 mm plate resonates at 1.425 × 10⁶ Hz (1.43 MHz) with a wavelength of 4.0 × 10⁻³ m inside the crystal; at 1.0 mm the frequency is 2.85 × 10⁶ Hz.

Exam traps

  • Using the full thickness as one wavelength: the faces are antinodes, so t = λ/2 and f = v/(2t). The wrong answer v/t is twice the correct frequency.
  • Confusing the two effects: production is the inverse piezoelectric effect (voltage gives strain); the direct effect (strain gives voltage) is the receiver.
  • Putting the wrong speed in v: use the longitudinal speed in the crystal (about 5.7 × 10³ m/s for quartz), not 343 m/s for air or 1500 m/s for water.
  • Forgetting to convert millimetres to metres, which gives a frequency 1000 times too small.
  • Defining ultrasound as loud sound. It is sound above 20 kHz; loudness is a separate matter of amplitude.

2.8 Acoustic grating and applications of ultrasonics

A row of parallel plates spaced a few millimetres apart bends sound the way a ruled grating bends light. Sound of wavelength λ crossing it leaves in directions where d sin θ = nλ, with d the spacing. Because λ = v/f, the measured angle gives the speed of sound in the liquid between the plates. Ultrasound, sound above 20 kHz, uses the same wave physics for echo ranging, flaw detection, cleaning and machining. This chapter gives the grating formula and the main uses of ultrasound.

You need: 2.6 Sound waves and acoustics for λ = v/f, and 2.7 Ultrasonics: production by piezoelectric method for how the beam is made.

The acoustic grating

An acoustic grating is a row of parallel plates or narrow slits with equal spacing d, in metres. Sound from a piezoelectric transducer falls on it as a plane wave. Each gap radiates secondary wavelets, and the wavelets interfere, exactly as light does at a ruled grating.

Neighbouring gaps send waves to a distant point at angle θ. One wave travels an extra distance d sin θ compared with the next. They arrive in step when that extra distance is a whole number of wavelengths.

d sin θ = nλ

d is the grating spacing in metres; θ is the angle measured from the straight-through direction; n is the order, 0, ±1, ±2, …; λ is the wavelength in metres, equal to v/f, with v the wave speed in m/s and f the frequency in Hz.

n = 0 is the straight-through beam at θ = 0. It exists for every wavelength, so it tells you nothing about λ. Orders with nλ > d cannot appear, because sin θ cannot exceed 1.

Using λ = v/f, a measured angle gives the wave speed: v = f d sin θ / n. This is how the speed of sound in a liquid is measured in a small laboratory cell. Only a few universities teach the grating derivation; the applications are examined far more widely.

  1. Fill the cell with the liquid and drive the transducer at a known frequency f.
  2. Turn a microphone or detector on an arm and record the angle θ of the first-order maximum (n = 1).
  3. Compute λ = d sin θ, then v = f λ.

Applications of ultrasonics

Ultrasound is sound above 20 kHz. Its short wavelength keeps the beam narrow and makes it reflect sharply at any change of medium, which is why every application below works.

  • SONAR and echo ranging: a pulse goes out and returns in time t, so the distance is v t / 2. Used for sea depth, fish shoals and car parking sensors.
  • Non-destructive testing: a pulse enters a weld or casting and a crack reflects it early. The echo delay gives the flaw depth, and the part is not damaged.
  • Medical imaging: the same echo idea at a few MHz maps soft tissue; the Doppler shift adds blood flow.
  • Ultrasonic cleaning: cavitation bubbles form and collapse violently, blasting dirt off jewellery, circuit boards and surgical instruments.
  • Welding and drilling: vibration plus pressure welds plastics; a vibrating tool with abrasive slurry drills glass and ceramics.
  • Cavitation also erodes pump impellers. The effect that cleans is the effect that destroys, depending on where it happens.

Worked example

Question. In water the speed of sound is 1500 m/s. An acoustic grating has plate spacing d = 5.0 mm. A beam of frequency 1.0 MHz falls on it normally. (a) Find the wavelength in water. (b) Find the angle of the first-order maximum. (c) State whether a second-order maximum exists, and at what angle.

  1. Wavelength: λ = v/f = 1500 / (1.0 × 10⁶) = 1.5 × 10⁻³ m = 1.5 mm.
  2. Convert the spacing to the same unit: d = 5.0 mm = 5.0 × 10⁻³ m.
  3. First order, n = 1: sin θ₁ = λ/d = 1.5 / 5.0 = 0.30.
  4. θ₁ = sin⁻¹(0.30) = 17.5°.
  5. Second order, n = 2: sin θ₂ = 2λ/d = 3.0 / 5.0 = 0.60, which is less than 1, so the order exists.
  6. θ₂ = sin⁻¹(0.60) = 36.9°.

Exam traps

  • Mixing units in d sin θ = nλ: divide 1.5 mm by 5.0 mm, never by 5.0.
  • Using n = 0 in the formula to find an angle. The zero order is always at θ = 0 and carries no wavelength information.
  • Forgetting λ = v/f and putting the frequency into the grating formula in place of the wavelength.
  • Writing an angle when nλ > d. Then sin θ > 1 has no solution, and the correct answer is that the order does not exist.

Takeaways

  1. In SHM the restoring force is proportional to displacement, so the motion is sinusoidal and the period is fixed by mass and stiffness alone.
  2. A torsional pendulum is the rotational twin of a mass–spring system, with torque −Cθ replacing force −kx and moment of inertia replacing mass.
  3. Damping makes the amplitude decay exponentially and slightly lowers the frequency; a periodic drive can pump energy back in.
  4. Resonance is the large response that occurs when the drive frequency is close to the natural frequency, and the quality factor measures its sharpness.
  5. The wave equation links the curvature in space to the acceleration in time; for a string the speed follows from tension and mass per unit length.
  6. A travelling wave transports energy proportional to A² and ω², while a standing wave stores energy and transports none on average.
  7. Sound is a longitudinal pressure wave, and the decibel scale is logarithmic because the ear responds to ratios, not to absolute intensities.
  8. Room acoustics is decided by absorption: too little gives a long reverberation, too much gives a dead, muffled sound.
  9. A piezoelectric crystal converts an alternating voltage into mechanical vibration, and it radiates most strongly at its thickness resonance, well above 20 kHz.
  10. Ultrasonics works because short wavelengths travel in narrow beams and reflect sharply from boundaries, which is the basis of echo ranging, testing and cleaning.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Derive the wave equation for transverse waves on a stretched string by considering the net vertical force on a small element, and hence show that the wave speed is v = √(T/μ). State the assumptions made. 16 marks

Take an element of length dx and mass μ dx; Net transverse force from the difference of tension components; For small slope, sin θ ≈ tan θ = ∂y/∂x; Net force = T(∂²y/∂x²)dx; Apply Newton's second law with mass μ dx; Get ∂²y/∂x² = (μ/T) ∂²y/∂t²; Compare with standard form to read v = √(T/μ); Assumptions: small amplitude, uniform string, no damping, tension unchanged.

Evaluate Explain the formation of standing waves on a string fixed at both ends. Derive the positions of nodes and antinodes and the allowed frequencies, and compare the energy transport in a standing wave with that in a travelling wave. 16 marks

Superpose y₁ = A sin(ωt − kx) and y₂ = A sin(ωt + kx); Result y = 2A sin kx cos ωt; Nodes where sin kx = 0, that is x = nλ/2; Antinodes midway between nodes; Boundary condition y = 0 at both ends gives L = nλ/2; f_n = nv/2L, giving the harmonic series; Travelling wave carries power P = ½μvω²A²; standing wave carries zero average power but stores energy between nodes.

Evaluate Describe the piezoelectric method of producing ultrasonics. Explain the role of the thickness resonance, compare it briefly with the magnetostriction method, and state two applications of ultrasonics that depend on its short wavelength. 16 marks

Quartz or Rochelle salt or PZT slice between metal plates; Alternating voltage from a valve or transistor oscillator; Crystal changes dimensions at the driving frequency; Resonance when t = λ/2, giving f = v/2t; Piezoelectric: high frequency up to MHz, stable, small output; magnetostriction: lower frequency, larger power, needs nickel core; Applications: echo ranging needs narrow beams; non-destructive testing needs small flaws to be resolved; Also mention cleaning by cavitation.

Analyze Explain, with the resonance curve of a forced oscillator, why soldiers break step on a bridge and why a car suspension is deliberately damped. Relate the discussion to the quality factor. 13 marks

Driven amplitude peaks near natural frequency; Marching supplies a periodic force at a fixed frequency; If that frequency matches the bridge frequency, amplitude grows until failure; Breaking step spreads the driving frequencies; Suspension uses damping to flatten the peak and stop repeated bouncing; Large Q means a sharp peak, small Q a flat one; Design chooses Q to suit the job.

Glossary

Acoustic grating
A periodic array of obstacles or slits that diffracts sound waves into orders given by d sin θ = nλ.
Amplitude
The maximum displacement of an oscillator or a wave from its mean position.
Angular frequency
ω = 2πf, the rate at which the phase of an oscillation changes, in rad/s.
Cavitation
The formation and violent collapse of tiny vapour bubbles in a liquid under intense ultrasound.
Couple per unit twist
The torque required to twist a wire by one radian; C = πηr⁴/(2l).
Damping
The progressive loss of energy from an oscillating system, usually to friction or viscous drag.
Damping coefficient
The constant b in the damping force F = −bv, in kg/s.
Decibel
A logarithmic unit for comparing two powers or intensities, one bel being a ratio of ten.
Frequency
The number of oscillations per second, f = 1/T, measured in hertz.
Intensity
Wave power crossing unit area perpendicular to the direction of travel, in W/m².
Linear mass density
Mass per unit length of a string, μ, in kg/m.
Modulus of rigidity
The ratio of shear stress to shear strain in a material, denoted η or G, in N/m².
Moment of inertia
The rotational equivalent of mass, I = Σmr², in kg m².
Node
A point in a standing wave where the displacement is always zero.
Oscillation
A repeated to-and-fro motion about a fixed mean position.
Phase
The quantity (ωt + φ) that specifies the state of the oscillator at time t.
Piezoelectric effect
The production of a voltage by mechanical stress on certain crystals, and the reverse change of shape under voltage.
Quality factor
A dimensionless measure of how sharp a resonance is, Q = ω₀/Δω.
Resonance
The large-amplitude response that occurs when the driving frequency approaches the natural frequency.
Restoring force
The force that pulls an oscillator back towards its mean position, proportional to displacement in SHM.
Reverberation time
The time for sound in an enclosure to decay by 60 dB after the source stops.
Simple harmonic motion
Oscillation in which the restoring force is directly proportional to displacement and directed opposite to it.
Standing wave
A wave pattern with fixed nodes and antinodes, formed by two identical waves travelling in opposite directions.
Superposition principle
The rule that displacements from separate waves add algebraically at every point.
Time period
The time taken for one complete oscillation, measured in seconds.
Torsional pendulum
A disc or bar suspended by a wire, oscillating by twisting and untwisting the wire.
Ultrasonic wave
A longitudinal sound wave with frequency above about 20 kHz, inaudible to humans.
Wave equation
The partial differential equation whose solutions are travelling or standing waves of speed v.
