6.1 Black body radiation and Planck's hypothesis

A hot object does not glow equally at all wavelengths. Its spectrum has a peak, and the peak moves to shorter wavelengths as the object gets hotter. Classical physics could not explain this shape; its best formula predicted infinite energy at short wavelengths. In 1900 Planck fixed it by saying that the atoms in the hot body emit energy in small packets, or quanta, each of energy E = hν. This single idea started quantum mechanics, and the same curve is used today in pyrometry, star-temperature measurement and the cosmic microwave background.

You need: electromagnetic waves and intensity from Unit 3, and graph reading from Unit 2. The photon idea continues in 6.2.

The black body and its spectrum

A black body is an ideal absorber. It absorbs all radiation that falls on it and reflects nothing. The same object is also the best possible emitter at every wavelength. No real surface is perfect, but a small hole in a closed cavity kept at temperature T comes very close. Radiation that enters the hole bounces around inside and is absorbed before it can escape. The radiation that does escape is called cavity radiation, and its spectrum depends only on T, not on the wall material.

To record the spectrum, the escaping light is spread by a prism or grating, and the intensity per unit wavelength I(λ,T) is measured. I is power per unit area per unit wavelength, in W·m⁻² per m. At each T the curve is smooth: zero at λ = 0, a broad peak, and a long tail at large λ. As T rises, the whole curve rises and the peak moves to shorter λ.

λmax T = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K

λmax is the peak wavelength in m; T is the absolute temperature in K; the constant 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K is Wien's constant.

Rayleigh-Jeans and the ultraviolet catastrophe

Classical physics treats the cavity walls as charged oscillators and the radiation as waves. By equipartition, each mode of the field carries average energy kT, where k = 1.381 × 10⁻²³ J/K is Boltzmann's constant. The number of modes per unit volume between ν and ν + dν grows as 8πν²/c³, so the energy density is u(ν) = (8πν²/c³) kT.

In wavelength language this becomes the Rayleigh-Jeans law: I(λ,T) ∝ T/λ⁴. At long λ it agrees with experiment. At short λ it fails badly: as λ → 0, 1/λ⁴ → ∞, so the predicted intensity and the total emitted energy both become infinite. This absurd prediction is the ultraviolet catastrophe. The name marks the short-wavelength end of the spectrum; the divergence appears even earlier, in the far ultraviolet and X-rays.

The fault lies in the assumption that an oscillator can take any energy, including zero. Every short-wavelength mode then receives the same share kT, and since there are ever more such modes, the sum never stops.

Planck's quanta and the radiation law

Planck's fix was one assumption. The oscillators in the wall cannot take any energy. Their allowed energies are E = n h ν, with n = 0, 1, 2, ... Here ν is the oscillator frequency in Hz, n is a whole number called the quantum number, and h is a new constant, the Planck constant: h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s. Energy changes only when n jumps, and each jump emits or absorbs one quantum of energy E = hν. The reduced constant is ℏ = h/2π = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.

E = hν = hc/λ

E is the energy of one quantum in J; ν is frequency in Hz; λ is wavelength in m; h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s; c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s.

The average energy per mode is then not kT but hν ÷ (ehν/kT − 1). For long λ, hν is small compared with kT, and this reduces to kT, so classical physics is recovered. For short λ, hν ≫ kT, so the exponential is huge and the average energy is tiny. Short-wavelength modes are rarely excited, because one quantum of such a mode costs more energy than the thermal motion can supply.

I(λ,T) = (2πhc²/λ⁵) ÷ (ehc/λkT − 1)

I is the spectral radiance per unit wavelength; h is the Planck constant; c is the speed of light; λ is wavelength in m; k is the Boltzmann constant; T is temperature in K. The λ⁻⁵ factor counts modes; the exponential cuts them off at short λ.

The formula fits every measured curve with one constant h. Setting dI/dλ = 0 gives Wien's law; integrating over λ gives the T⁴ law, which is not derived here.

Worked example

Question. The surface of a star behaves as a black body at 6000 K. (a) Find the wavelength at which its spectrum peaks. (b) Find the frequency and the energy of one photon at that peak, in joules and in electron volts. Use b = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K, h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s and 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

  1. (a) Wien's law: λmax = b/T = (2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K) ÷ (6000 K) = 4.83 × 10⁻⁷ m = 483 nm.
  2. (b) Frequency at the peak: ν = c/λmax = (3.00 × 10⁸ m/s) ÷ (4.83 × 10⁻⁷ m) = 6.21 × 10¹⁴ Hz.
  3. Photon energy: E = hν = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s) × (6.211 × 10¹⁴ Hz) = 4.12 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  4. In electron volts: E = (4.116 × 10⁻¹⁹ J) ÷ (1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J/eV) = 2.57 eV.

Answer. λmax = 4.83 × 10⁻⁷ m = 483 nm; ν = 6.21 × 10¹⁴ Hz; E = 4.12 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 2.57 eV.

Exam traps

  • Using Wien's law to find total intensity. It gives only the peak wavelength; total emitted power needs the T⁴ law.
  • Forgetting units: b is in m·K, so T must be in kelvin and λmax comes out in metres, not nanometres.
  • Treating E = hν as a continuous classical energy. One quantum is hν, and a body emits in whole multiples nhν.
  • Swapping h and ℏ. h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s and ℏ = h/2π = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.
  • Thinking the peak shifts to longer wavelength as T rises. It shifts to shorter wavelength; the examiner's favourite graph asks you to mark this shift.

6.2 Photoelectric effect and Compton effect

Light of frequency ν delivers its energy in packets called photons, each carrying E = hν. Two experiments force this picture. In the photoelectric effect one photon is absorbed at a metal surface and one electron leaves. In the Compton effect one X-ray photon collides with a free electron, hands over part of its energy, and scatters with a longer wavelength. Both effects are in daily use: photocells and light meters, camera sensors, solar cells and X-ray imaging all depend on counting photons.

You need: Chapter 6.1 for the quantum E = hν, and Unit 3 for electromagnetic waves and photon momentum.

The photon

Light of frequency ν and wavelength λ travels as photons. One photon carries energy E = hν, with h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s (Planck's constant) and ν in hertz. In terms of wavelength, E = hc/λ, with c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s.

A photon has zero rest mass but carries momentum p = hν/c = h/λ. That momentum is what makes the Compton collision look like a game of billiards.

Intensity is a count of photons. The intensity of a beam is the number of photons crossing unit area per second, multiplied by the energy of one photon. Doubling the brightness doubles the photon rate; the energy per photon is fixed by the colour, that is, by the frequency.

Chapter 6.1 showed why Planck needed quanta for the black body spectrum. Here the quantum becomes a countable particle of light, and the two experiments below measure it directly.

The photoelectric effect

Ultraviolet light on a clean zinc plate knocks electrons out. Four observations need a particle picture.

  • Below a threshold frequency ν0 there are no electrons at all, however intense the light and however long you wait.
  • Above ν0 electrons appear within about 10⁻⁹ s, even in very faint light.
  • The maximum kinetic energy Kmax rises linearly with ν and does not depend on intensity.
  • The number of electrons per second, the photocurrent, is proportional to intensity.

One photon gives all its energy to one electron. The electron needs at least the work function φ to escape the metal; the rest becomes kinetic energy.

hν = φ + Kmax

hν is the photon energy (J); φ is the work function, the minimum energy needed to free an electron from the metal (J); Kmax is the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electron (J).

The threshold follows: φ = hν0, so ν0 = φ/h. Work functions are quoted in electron volts, 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; caesium is about 2.1 eV, platinum about 5.6 eV.

The stopping potential V0 measures Kmax. A negative voltage on the collector repels the electrons until the current falls to zero.

eV0 = Kmax = hν − φ

e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C is the electron charge; V0 is the stopping potential in volts. If Kmax is quoted in eV, its number equals V0 in volts.

Plot Kmax against ν. The line has slope h, x-intercept ν0 and y-intercept −φ. Plot V0 against ν instead: the line has slope h/e and intercept −φ/e. The intercept is negative, with magnitude φ/e, so it lies below the origin. The work function is φ = e × (magnitude of intercept). That is the standard determination of the work function of a photoelectric material. Each metal gives a parallel line with its own intercept.

The Compton effect

Compton sent X-rays of a single wavelength λ at graphite and measured the scattered X-rays at angle θ. He found the original λ and also a longer λ′. A wave picture cannot shift a wavelength; a photon picture can.

Treat the collision as a photon hitting a free electron at rest. Energy conservation gives hν = hν′ + Ke. Momentum conservation is a vector triangle: incoming photon momentum h/λ, outgoing photon momentum h/λ′ at angle θ, and the recoil electron momentum pe closing the triangle. Removing the electron terms gives the Compton shift.

λ′ − λ = (h/mec)(1 − cos θ)

λ and λ′ are the X-ray wavelengths before and after scattering (m); θ is the photon's scattering angle; me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg is the electron rest mass; h/mec = 2.43 pm is the Compton wavelength of the electron.

At θ = 0°, Δλ = 0. At θ = 90°, Δλ = 2.43 pm. At θ = 180°, Δλ = 4.86 pm, the largest shift. The shift depends on θ only, never on the incoming λ.

Because λ′ > λ, the scattered photon is less energetic and the recoil electron carries the difference. The formula assumes a loosely bound, effectively free electron; for a tightly bound electron the whole atom recoils and the shift is negligible.

Worked example

Question. Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on a metal of work function 2.0 eV. Find the photon energy in joules and in electron volts, the maximum kinetic energy of the photoelectrons, and the stopping potential. Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

  1. Convert the data: λ = 400 nm = 4.00 × 10⁻⁷ m and φ = 2.0 eV = 2.0 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.20 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  2. Photon energy: E = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸) / (4.00 × 10⁻⁷) = (1.989 × 10⁻²⁵) / (4.00 × 10⁻⁷) = 4.9725 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  3. In electron volts: E = 4.9725 × 10⁻¹⁹ / 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.1078 eV.
  4. Maximum kinetic energy: Kmax = E − φ = 3.1078 − 2.0 = 1.1078 eV, which is 1.1078 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.77 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  5. Stopping potential: eV0 = Kmax, so V0 = 1.1078 V, about 1.11 V.

Answer. Photon energy = 4.9725 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 3.1078 eV; Kmax = 1.1078 eV; stopping potential ≈ 1.11 V.

Worked example 2

Question. An X-ray photon of wavelength 100 pm is scattered by a free electron at θ = 90°. Find the Compton shift, the scattered wavelength and the kinetic energy of the recoil electron in eV. Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

  1. Compton wavelength: h/(mec) = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ / (9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 3.00 × 10⁸) = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ / 2.733 × 10⁻²² = 2.426 × 10⁻¹² m = 2.426 pm.
  2. At θ = 90°, cos θ = 0, so Δλ = 2.426 pm × (1 − 0) = 2.426 pm.
  3. Scattered wavelength: λ′ = 100 + 2.426 = 102.426 pm.
  4. Photon energies: hc = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ J·m, so E = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ / 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 1.989 × 10⁻¹⁵ J and E′ = 1.989 × 10⁻²⁵ / 1.02426 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 1.942 × 10⁻¹⁵ J.
  5. Recoil energy: Ke = E − E′ = 4.7 × 10⁻¹⁷ J = 4.7 × 10⁻¹⁷ / 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 294 eV.

Answer. Δλ = 2.426 pm, λ′ = 102.426 pm, and the recoil electron carries about 294 eV.

Exam traps

  • Bright light is not fast electrons. Intensity sets the number of photoelectrons per second; frequency sets Kmax and the stopping potential.
  • Work functions come in eV while hc/λ gives joules. Convert φ to joules (multiply by 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) before subtracting. If you do not, the φ term alone is wrong by that factor and Kmax is meaningless.
  • Below ν0, more intensity or a longer wait changes nothing. One photon meets one electron, so there is no build-up time.
  • In the Compton effect λ′ is always larger than λ, and Δλ depends only on θ. Writing λ′ = λ − Δλ is a common slip.
  • The Compton formula uses a free electron. In a heavy atom the whole atom recoils and the shift is too small to observe.

6.3 Wave-particle duality and de Broglie hypothesis

Light sometimes behaves as a particle with fixed energy and momentum, and sometimes as a wave. Matter does the same. Every moving particle has a matter wave of wavelength λ = h/p, where λ is in metres, h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s is Planck's constant, and p is momentum in kg·m/s. This de Broglie relation connects particle momentum to wave length. It explains electron diffraction, guides electron microscopes, and marks where quantum effects matter. A cricket ball has a wavelength so tiny that its wave nature is unobservable.

You need: photon momentum from 6.2, wave ideas from Unit 2, and relativistic momentum from Unit 1 for high energies.

Wave and particle in one object

Light forces two pictures. A photon carries energy E = hν, where ν is frequency, and momentum p = h/λ; it can eject one electron in the photoelectric effect or share energy in Compton scattering (6.2). Yet light also shows interference and diffraction. The photon is one object, tested in two ways.

De Broglie reversed the question. A particle with momentum p has a matter wave of wavelength λ = h/p. A matter wave is the wave whose intensity gives the probability of finding the particle; it is not a ripple of material. Electron diffraction from a crystal confirms this wave for electrons.

λ = h/p = h/(mv)

λ is the de Broglie wavelength in metres; h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s is Planck's constant; p is momentum in kg·m/s; m is mass in kg; v is speed in m/s. For a photon, p = h/λ = E/c, where c is the speed of light.

Some syllabi call this wave properties of particles. The same relation covers photons and electrons; only the momentum expression changes.

The relation and its numbers

For a slow electron accelerated from rest through V volts, the electric energy eV becomes kinetic energy p²/(2m), where e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C is the elementary charge. So p = √(2meV). Planck's relation then gives λ = h/√(2meV).

λ = 12.27/√V Å

V is the accelerating voltage in volts; λ is in ångström (1 Å = 10⁻¹⁰ m). This nonrelativistic form is accurate when the electron energy is much less than 511 keV.

At 100 V, λ = 12.27/√100 = 1.227 Å = 0.123 nm. A cricket ball of mass 0.16 kg moving at 30 m/s has p = 4.8 kg·m/s, so λ = 1.4 × 10⁻³⁴ m. That is far below any measurable slit, so its wave nature is invisible. The electron wavelength is comparable to atomic spacing, so crystals diffract electrons. At electron energies near 511 keV, use relativistic momentum from Unit 1.

Electron double slit and what λ means

Fire electrons one at a time at two narrow slits. Each electron arrives as one localized dot, yet many dots build an interference pattern. The fringe spacing depends on λ = h/p. This is wave-particle duality in one experiment: the electron is detected as a particle and guided by a matter wave.

If a detector records which slit each electron used, the pattern changes to two broad bands. The wave description needs both paths; knowing the path destroys the interference. This is why the wave is not a hidden push on a classical particle.

Do not read λ as the size of the electron. It is the wavelength of the probability amplitude, developed in 6.6. Large momentum means short wavelength and more particle-like behaviour. The double-slit test with electrons is the cleanest classroom picture; crystal diffraction is the usual laboratory proof, covered in 6.4.

Worked example

Question. An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 100 V. Find its de Broglie wavelength. Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, m = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

  1. Kinetic energy gained: K = eV = (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)(100 V) = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁷ J.
  2. Nonrelativistic momentum: p = √(2mK) = √(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁷ J) = 5.40 × 10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s.
  3. De Broglie wavelength: λ = h/p = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s)/(5.40 × 10⁻²⁴ kg·m/s) = 1.228 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.
  4. Convert: λ = 0.1228 nm = 1.228 Å, which rounds to 0.123 nm = 1.23 Å.

Answer. The de Broglie wavelength is 1.23 × 10⁻¹⁰ m, or 0.123 nm, or 1.23 Å.

Exam traps

  • Using p = mv at high voltage. When kinetic energy approaches 511 keV for an electron, use relativistic p = γmv; the low-voltage formula fails.
  • Do not mix volts and electron-volts. V in λ = 12.27/√V Å is volts; energy eV is in joules when e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
  • λ is not the size of the particle. It is the matter-wave wavelength; an electron can have λ larger than its own classical radius.
  • For a proton or ion, replace m and charge in p = √(2mqV); 12.27/√V Å is only for electrons.

6.4 Davisson-Germer experiment; group and phase velocity

In 1927 Davisson and Germer fired slow electrons at a nickel crystal and found sharp diffraction peaks at particular angles. Only waves diffract, so the peaks proved that a moving electron carries a matter wave of wavelength λ = h/p. The experiment also separates two speeds of a matter wave: the group velocity, which equals the electron's speed, and the phase velocity, which does not.

You need: λ = h/p from 6.3, v = ω/k and wave packets from Unit 2, Oscillations and Waves, and Bragg reflection nλ = 2d sin θ from Unit 4, Wave Optics.

The experiment: electrons on a nickel crystal

A particle of momentum p has wavelength λ = h/p. An electron accelerated through 54 V has energy 54 eV and wavelength near 1.7 Å. Atomic planes in a metal crystal are a few ångström apart, so a crystal is a ready-made grating for electrons.

An electron gun heats a filament and accelerates electrons through voltage V, giving each kinetic energy eV. The beam strikes a nickel target. A movable detector records the scattered current versus angle.

At first the current varied smoothly with angle. Then a liquid-air bottle broke, the nickel oxidised, and the target had to be heated for a long time in hydrogen to clean it. That heating also annealed the nickel into large single-crystal regions. The smooth curve became sharp peaks. With 54 V electrons, the strongest peak sat at 50° from the incident beam.

  • A particle bouncing off atoms would scatter broadly and smoothly.
  • A wave scattered from many parallel planes adds in phase only at certain angles, giving a sharp maximum.
  • The peak angle moved when the accelerating voltage, hence λ, changed — as a wave picture predicts.

This measured the de Broglie wavelength; it did not merely assume it.

Checking the peak with Bragg's law

A crystal is a set of parallel reflecting planes of spacing d. Waves from successive planes add in phase only when the extra path is a whole number of wavelengths.

nλ = 2d sin θ

n is the order (1, 2, 3 …), λ is the electron wavelength in metres, d is the spacing between neighbouring crystal planes in metres, and θ is the glancing angle between the electron beam and the plane itself (not the normal).

The beam turns through 2θ, so the detector reading is compared with 2θ. For nickel the relevant plane spacing is d = 2.03 Å. The worked example gives 48.55°, within about 3% of the observed 50°.

  1. Find the kinetic energy in joules: E = eV.
  2. Find the momentum: p = √(2mE), with m = 9.109 × 10⁻³¹ kg.
  3. Find the wavelength: λ = h/p, with h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s.
  4. Put n = 1 in nλ = 2d sin θ and solve for sin θ.
  5. Double θ and compare with the measured detector angle.

Group velocity and phase velocity

A pure sine wave has one wavelength and one frequency and travels at the phase velocity vp. A real particle is localised, so its matter wave is a wave packet: many sine waves with wave numbers near a central value k.

vp = ω/k vg = dω/dk

ω is the angular frequency in rad/s, k = 2π/λ is the wave number in rad/m, vp is the phase velocity (speed of one crest) and vg is the group velocity (speed of the packet envelope, and hence of the particle). Both are in m/s.

Inside a packet the crests run at vp, but the envelope — the part marking where the particle is — runs at vg. Two waves of nearly equal ω and k give an envelope moving at Δω/Δk, which becomes dω/dk for a narrow spread.

For a matter wave, ω = E/ℏ and k = p/ℏ, where E is total energy, p momentum and ℏ = h/2π. So vp = E/p and vg = dE/dp. For a free particle dE/dp is exactly the particle speed, so vg is the speed of the electron.

QuantityNon-relativisticRelativistic
vp = E/pv/2c²/v
vg = dE/dpvv
vp × vgv²/2c²

In the table v is the particle speed and c is the speed of light. Non-relativistically vp = v/2; relativistically vp = c²/v, which exceeds c. That is allowed, because a pure phase carries no information. The packet moves at vg = v, always below c.

6.5 Heisenberg's uncertainty principle

Classical physics draws a particle as a point with a sharp position and a sharp momentum at every instant. Quantum mechanics does not allow both to be sharp at the same time. The product of the spread in position, Δx, and the spread in momentum, Δp, has a smallest possible value, ℏ/2. The same limit links energy spread ΔE and time spread Δt. This uncertainty is not a bad measurement; it is a property of matter waves. It fixes natural spectral linewidths, the least kinetic energy of a trapped particle, and the limits of imaging an electron.

You need: matter waves, wave packets and group velocity, and single-slit diffraction from Unit 2.

The two bounds

Some syllabi call this Heisenberg's uncertainty principle. For a particle described by a matter wave, let Δx be the spread in position about its mean and Δp the spread in momentum about its mean. The bound is:

Δx Δp ≥ ℏ/2

Δx is the root-mean-square spread in position; Δp is the root-mean-square spread in momentum; ℏ = h/(2π) = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s is the reduced Planck constant.

ℏ is the reduced Planck constant and h is Planck's constant. A state with a very small Δx must have a large Δp, and the reverse. The bound uses this precise spread about the mean. Estimates treat Δx and Δp as typical ranges, so only the order of magnitude matters.

ΔE Δt ≥ ℏ/2

ΔE is the spread in energy; Δt is the lifetime of the state or the time available for a measurement.

A state with exactly known energy has ΔE = 0, so Δt is infinite: it lasts forever. For classical waves the same idea appears as Δx Δk ≥ 1/2, where Δk is the spread in wave number. For matter waves p = ℏk, so Δp = ℏ Δk, and the classical wave relation becomes Δx Δp ≥ ℏ/2.

Why a narrow slit broadens the pattern

Send an electron wave through a slit of width a. The slit limits where the electron can be, so Δx is of order a. After the slit, the wave diffracts. Unit 2 gives the first minimum of a single slit at a sin θ = λ, where λ is the de Broglie wavelength and θ is the angle from the centre.

Before the slit the momentum is along the beam, p = h/λ. After the slit the transverse momentum can be as large as p sin θ. Using the first minimum, p sin θ ≈ (h/λ)(λ/a) = h/a. Thus Δp is of order h/a. The product is Δx Δp ≈ a(h/a) = h, which is of the order of ℏ and obeys the bound.

Narrow slit: small Δx, broad diffraction, large Δp. Wide slit: large Δx, narrow diffraction, small Δp. The slit does not push the electron; the wave simply has no sharp transverse momentum until a measurement is made.

A classical trajectory needs a sharp x(t) and a sharp p(t) at each instant, so that the points join into a line. If Δx and Δp cannot both be small, no such sharp line exists. The electron has a cloud of possible positions and momenta, not a railway track.

Where the bound appears

  • Natural linewidth. An excited atom lives for a time τ. Treat τ as Δt. Then ΔE ≥ ℏ/(2τ), and the frequency width is Δν = ΔE/h ≈ 1/(4πτ). A lifetime of 10 ns gives Δν ≈ 8 MHz. This is the natural linewidth of the spectral line.
  • Zero-point energy. A particle trapped in a region of size L has Δx ≈ L, so Δp ≳ ℏ/(2L). Its kinetic energy cannot be zero: K ≈ (Δp)²/(2m) is of order ℏ²/(2mL²). In a harmonic trap the exact least energy is ½ℏω, where ω is the trap angular frequency. The particle keeps moving even at absolute zero.
  • Gamma-ray microscope, qualitatively. To locate an electron, a photon is scattered from it. A shorter photon wavelength λ gives a smaller Δx, but the photon carries momentum h/λ and gives a larger unknown kick to the electron, so Δp is larger. A longer wavelength gives a smaller kick but a blurred position. The product cannot fall below ℏ/2.

Worked example

Question. An electron is confined to a region of size Δx = 0.1 nm. Estimate the minimum momentum uncertainty and the kinetic energy from Δx Δp ≥ ℏ/2. Use ℏ = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s and m = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

  1. Convert the size: Δx = 0.1 nm = 0.1 × 10⁻⁹ m = 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.
  2. Rearrange the bound: Δp ≥ ℏ/(2Δx).
  3. Substitute: Δp ≥ 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ / (2 × 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 5.275 × 10⁻²⁵ kg·m/s, about 5.28 × 10⁻²⁵ kg·m/s.
  4. Use K ≈ (Δp)²/(2m). (5.275 × 10⁻²⁵)² = 2.7826 × 10⁻⁴⁹. Also 2m = 2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ = 1.822 × 10⁻³⁰ kg. Thus K = 2.7826 × 10⁻⁴⁹ / 1.822 × 10⁻³⁰ = 1.527 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, about 1.53 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  5. Convert to electronvolt using 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J: K = 1.527 × 10⁻¹⁹ / 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 0.953 eV, about 0.95 eV.

Answer. The minimum momentum uncertainty is about 5.28 × 10⁻²⁵ kg·m/s, and the corresponding kinetic energy is about 1.53 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, or 0.95 eV.

Exam traps

  • Δx Δp ≥ ℏ/2 is a lower bound, not an equality. Do not set Δx Δp = ℏ/2 unless the question asks for the minimum.
  • Use ℏ = h/(2π), not h. Mixing h and ℏ changes the answer by a factor 2π.
  • A particle in a trap can have average momentum zero while Δp is not zero. The spread enters the bound, not the average.
  • Δx is a spread about the mean, not the full width of a slit or box. The slit width or box length only sets the order of magnitude.
  • The energy-time relation does not mean energy disappears for a short time. It says a short-lived state has a spread in energy.

6.6 Wave function and its Born interpretation

A quantum particle is described by a wavefunction ψ(x,t), a complex number at every point and time. ψ is not directly measured. Its squared modulus |ψ|² gives the probability density of finding the particle there. This Born rule turns a mathematical wave into testable predictions: electron diffraction, atomic orbitals and tunnelling probabilities all use it. Normalisation then fixes the allowed shapes of ψ.

You need complex numbers and Gaussian integrals, plus 6.5 Heisenberg's uncertainty principle and the wave equation from Unit 2.

The wavefunction is a complex amplitude

For one particle in one dimension, ψ(x,t) is a complex-valued function of position x and time t. The star in ψ* means complex conjugate: if ψ = a + ib, then ψ* = a − ib. In three dimensions write ψ(r,t), where r is the position vector. The wavefunction is not a vibration of a material string. It is a probability amplitude, a mathematical object that carries phase.

Complex values matter. A real number has only size and sign; a complex number also has phase. Phase controls how two amplitudes add. In a two-slit electron experiment, the pattern on the screen comes from adding ψ from both slits before squaring. If ψ were just a probability, no interference would appear.

The wavefunction carries all available information about the state. From ψ you can find the probability of position, the probability of momentum by a Fourier transform, and the average energy as ⟨E⟩ = ∫ ψ* Ĥ ψ dV, where Ĥ is the Hamiltonian operator. Some syllabi call the list of its requirements the properties and significance of the wave function.

Born rule: |ψ|² is a probability density

Born's rule says the probability of finding the particle in a small volume dV around r at time t is dP = |ψ(r,t)|² dV. The quantity ρ = |ψ|² = ψ*ψ is the probability density. It is real and never negative. The probability itself is dimensionless; dV has units m³, so ρ has units m⁻³ and ψ has units m⁻³/² in three dimensions. In one dimension ψ has units m⁻¹/².

P(a ≤ x ≤ b) = ∫<sub>a</sub><sup>b</sup> |ψ(x,t)|² dx

P is the probability of finding the particle between x = a and x = b; ψ(x,t) is the wavefunction; |ψ|² is the probability density; dx is a small length element.

A large |ψ|² means the particle is likely to be found there in many identical measurements. A node, where ψ = 0, has zero probability density. The particle is not spread as a cloud of matter; the cloud is a map of probability. One measurement gives one position, not a fraction of a particle.

For a particle in three dimensions use dV = dx dy dz, or r² sinθ dr dθ dφ in spherical coordinates. The same rule applies to any observable: the probability is the squared modulus of the amplitude for that result.

Normalisation and allowed shapes

Total probability must be 1. For a particle somewhere in space, ∫ |ψ|² dV = 1, with the integral over all space. This is the normalisation condition. It fixes the overall size of ψ but not its phase. Multiplying ψ by eiφ, where φ is a real phase angle, changes nothing in |ψ|².

∫all space |ψ(r,t)|² dV = 1

ψ is normalised; dV is the volume element; the integral covers all space.

A physically acceptable wavefunction must be finite everywhere, single-valued, continuous, and have a continuous first derivative wherever the potential V is finite. It must also be square-integrable: the integral ∫ |ψ|² dV over all space must be finite, so that the normalisation constant can be found. Finite keeps probabilities from blowing up. Single-valued gives one probability at one point. Continuity keeps |ψ|² smooth enough to integrate. The derivative condition keeps momentum finite; it can fail at an infinite potential wall.

The average of an observable A in state ψ is ⟨A⟩ = ∫ ψ* Â ψ dV. Here Â is the operator for A, and ⟨A⟩ is the expectation value. For position, Â = x, so ⟨x⟩ = ∫ x |ψ|² dV. For momentum, Â = −iℏ d/dx in one dimension. The Schrödinger equation tells how ψ changes in time; see 6.7.

6.7 Schrodinger equation: time-dependent and time-independent

An electron in an atom is not a small ball; it is a wave with a shape. The Schrödinger equation is the rule that fixes that shape. Give it the particle's mass and the potential energy around it, and it returns the wavefunction. When the potential does not change with time, the equation splits into a space part and a rotating phase, and the space part hands you the allowed energies. Every energy level in physics — spectral lines, band gaps in semiconductors, laser transitions — comes out of this one equation.

You need: the wavefunction and its Born interpretation from 6.6, partial derivatives, and the classical wave equation from Unit 2, Oscillations and Waves.

The wave equation of matter

The Schrödinger equation does for matter waves what Newton's second law does for a cricket ball. You supply the mass m of the particle and the potential energy V it sits in, and out comes the wavefunction ψ(x, t) of 6.6. No classical argument produces this equation. Schrödinger put it forward in 1926 as a postulate, and it survives because every experiment on atoms, electrons and nuclei agrees with its predictions.

iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = −(ℏ²/2m) ∂²ψ/∂x² + Vψ

ψ = ψ(x, t) is the wavefunction (complex); i = √−1; ℏ = h/2π = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s is the reduced Planck constant; m is the particle mass in kg; ∂²ψ/∂x² is the second derivative of ψ with respect to x; V = V(x, t) is the potential energy in joules. Each side of the equation has the units of an energy multiplied by ψ.

The form is guided by the classical energy relation for one particle, E = p²/2m + V. Replace the momentum p by the operator −iℏ ∂/∂x, replace the energy E by the operator iℏ ∂/∂t, and let both act on ψ. The result is the time-dependent Schrödinger equation, or TDSE.

iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ, Ĥ = −(ℏ²/2m) ∂²/∂x² + V

Ĥ is the Hamiltonian operator, the quantum operator for total energy (kinetic plus potential), in joules; the hat marks an operator rather than a number. In three dimensions ∂²/∂x² becomes the Laplacian ∇².

Separating time: stationary states

Take a potential that depends on x only, never on t. The TDSE then splits into two ordinary equations, one in x and one in t.

  1. Guess a product solution ψ(x, t) = ψ(x)φ(t).
  2. Substitute: iℏ ψ dφ/dt = φ [−(ℏ²/2m) d²ψ/dx² + Vψ].
  3. Divide both sides by ψφ. The left side holds only t, the right side only x.
  4. Two functions of different variables can be equal everywhere only if both equal one constant. Call it E, in joules.
  5. Time part: iℏ dφ/dt = Eφ, so φ(t) = e^(−iEt/ℏ).
  6. Space part: −(ℏ²/2m) d²ψ/dx² + V(x)ψ = Eψ, the time-independent Schrödinger equation (TISE).

Ĥψ = Eψ

ψ = ψ(x) is the spatial part of the wavefunction; E is the separation constant and turns out to be the total energy in joules. The equation says the Hamiltonian, acting on ψ, returns the same function multiplied by a number.

An equation of the shape Ĥψ = Eψ is an eigenvalue equation. ψ is an eigenfunction of Ĥ and E is the matching eigenvalue. Only particular pairs (ψ, E) survive the boundary conditions: ψ must be finite, single-valued and continuous everywhere, dψ/dx must be continuous wherever V is finite, and ψ must fall to zero at infinity for a bound particle. Those conditions, more than the equation itself, are what make energy discrete.

The full wavefunction is ψ(x)e^(−iEt/ℏ). Its probability density is |ψ|² = |ψ(x)|² × |e^(−iEt/ℏ)|² = |ψ(x)|², with no t in it. The complex phase rotates, but no measurable quantity moves. Such a state is a stationary state and it carries a definite energy E. Some syllabi call the TISE the steady-state equation.

Free particle: k, ω and E

Set V = 0 everywhere. The TISE becomes −(ℏ²/2m) d²ψ/dx² = Eψ, whose solutions are ψ(x) = A e^(ikx) + B e^(−ikx). Putting the time factor back gives travelling plane waves, one running each way.

ψ(x, t) = A e^(i(kx − ωt)) + B e^(−i(kx + ωt)),   E = ℏ²k²/(2m),   p = ℏk,   ω = E/ℏ

k = 2π/λ is the wave number in m⁻¹ and λ is the de Broglie wavelength in metres; A and B are the amplitudes of the right-going and left-going waves; p is momentum in kg·m/s; ω is angular frequency in rad/s.

Energy grows as the square of the wave number: double k and E becomes four times larger. The phase velocity is ω/k = ℏk/2m, only half the particle speed. The group velocity dω/dk = ℏk/m = p/m is exactly the classical speed, the same result met in 6.4.

A single plane wave has the same |ψ|² at every x, so it cannot be normalised over infinite space. Physically it stands for a beam, normalised inside a large box, or for one component of a localised wave packet. Confining the particle instead of freeing it turns the continuous label k into a discrete set — that is the particle in a box, 6.8.

Worked example

Question. A free electron moves in a region where V = 0 and has wave number k = 1.0 × 10¹⁰ m⁻¹. Using the time-independent Schrödinger equation, find its energy in joules and in electronvolts, and the angular frequency ω of its time factor. Take ℏ = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s and m = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

  1. With V = 0 the TISE reads −(ℏ²/2m) d²ψ/dx² = Eψ.
  2. Try ψ = A e^(ikx). Then dψ/dx = ikA e^(ikx) and d²ψ/dx² = −k²A e^(ikx) = −k²ψ.
  3. Substitute: −(ℏ²/2m)(−k²ψ) = Eψ, so E = ℏ²k²/(2m).
  4. Compute ℏ² = (1.055 × 10⁻³⁴)² = 1.113 × 10⁻⁶⁸ J²·s² and k² = (1.0 × 10¹⁰)² = 1.0 × 10²⁰ m⁻². Numerator ℏ²k² = 1.113 × 10⁻⁴⁸ J²·s²·m⁻².
  5. Denominator 2m = 2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ = 1.822 × 10⁻³⁰ kg.
  6. E = 1.113 × 10⁻⁴⁸ ÷ 1.822 × 10⁻³⁰ = 6.11 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  7. Convert: 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, so E = 6.11 × 10⁻¹⁹ ÷ 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.81 eV.
  8. The time factor is e^(−iEt/ℏ), so ω = E/ℏ = 6.11 × 10⁻¹⁹ ÷ 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ = 5.79 × 10¹⁵ rad/s.

Answer. E = 6.11 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 3.81 eV, and the angular frequency is ω = 5.79 × 10¹⁵ rad/s.

Exam traps

  • Using the time-independent equation when V depends on time. The separation step fails, the state is not stationary, and you must return to the TDSE.
  • Dropping the boundary conditions. Ĥψ = Eψ admits solutions for a whole range of E; only those that are finite, single-valued, continuous, and vanish at infinity for a bound state are physical.
  • Sign and derivative slips: the kinetic term carries a minus sign, −(ℏ²/2m)∇², and the left side of the TDSE is iℏ ∂ψ/∂t, not −iℏ ∂ψ/∂t.
  • Mixing up ω and ν. ω = E/ℏ in rad/s while ν = E/h in Hz; the missing factor of 2π is the usual lost mark.
  • Reading e^(−iEt/ℏ) as motion. The phase rotates but |ψ|² stays constant; any observable change needs a superposition of at least two stationary states.

6.8 Particle in a box: one and three dimensions

Trap a particle between two rigid walls and its energy can no longer take any value. Only certain standing waves fit between the walls, so the allowed energies form a ladder: E₁, 4E₁, 9E₁ and so on. This is the particle in a box, the simplest problem where quantisation comes straight out of a boundary condition. The same idea in three dimensions explains the colours of quantum dots, the absorption of conjugated dye molecules and the level spacing in semiconductor nanostructures.

You need: the time-independent Schrodinger equation from 6.7, the Born meaning of |ψ|² from 6.6, the uncertainty principle from 6.5, boundary conditions and simple trigonometry.

The infinite well: a particle that cannot escape

Put a particle of mass m on the x-axis and trap it between rigid walls at x = 0 and x = L. Inside the box the potential energy is zero: V(x) = 0 for 0 < x < L. Outside, V = ∞, so the particle is never found there. This model is the infinite square well, or particle in a box.

Infinite potential outside means ψ = 0 outside. The wavefunction must be continuous at the walls, so ψ(0) = 0 and ψ(L) = 0. These two boundary conditions do the real work: they replace a continuous range of energies with a discrete list.

−(ℏ²/2m) d²ψ/dx² = Eψ (inside the box)

ℏ = h/2π is the reduced Planck constant, 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s; m is the particle mass in kg; E is the total energy in joules; ψ(x) is the wavefunction.

Write k² = 2mE/ℏ². Then d²ψ/dx² = −k²ψ, whose general solution is ψ = A sin kx + B cos kx, with A and B constants.

Apply ψ(0) = 0: B = 0, leaving ψ = A sin kx. Apply ψ(L) = 0: A sin kL = 0. A = 0 gives no particle at all, so sin kL = 0 and kL = nπ with n = 1, 2, 3, … The value n = 0 gives ψ = 0 everywhere and is rejected. Only wavelengths with k = nπ/L fit between the walls.

Wavefunctions, energies and nodes

ψn(x) = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L), n = 1, 2, 3, …

ψn is the normalised wavefunction of level n; L is the full box width in metres; x is position in metres. The factor √(2/L) comes from setting ∫|ψn|²dx = 1 between 0 and L.

En = n²h²/(8mL²)

En is the energy of level n in joules; h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s is Planck's constant; m is the particle mass in kg; L is the box width in metres; n = 1, 2, 3, … is the quantum number.

The lowest energy E1 = h²/(8mL²) is not zero. Confining the particle to a width L makes Δx of order L, so the uncertainty principle forces Δp ≥ ℏ/2L and the kinetic energy cannot vanish. This minimum is the zero-point energy.

Nodes: ψn has n − 1 zeros strictly inside the box. ψ1 is a single hump, ψ2 crosses zero at x = L/2, ψ3 crosses at L/3 and 2L/3. The probability density |ψn|² = (2/L) sin²(nπx/L) has n humps, so a highly excited particle is almost equally likely anywhere in the box.

Level spacing grows: En+1 − En = (2n + 1)h²/(8mL²). Gaps widen with n, unlike the equal gaps of a harmonic oscillator. The figure plots the first three wavefunctions.

Three dimensions and degeneracy

For a rectangular box with sides Lx, Ly, Lz, take V = 0 inside and ∞ outside, and write ψ(x, y, z) = X(x)Y(y)Z(z). Each factor obeys a one-dimensional box equation with its own quantum number.

E(nx, ny, nz) = (h²/8m)(nx²/Lx² + ny²/Ly² + nz²/Lz²)

nx, ny, nz = 1, 2, 3, … are the quantum numbers for the three directions; Lx, Ly, Lz are the side lengths in metres; m is the mass in kg; h is Planck's constant.

The wavefunction is a product of three sines, ψ = √(8/V) sin(nxπx/Lx) sin(nyπy/Ly) sin(nzπz/Lz), with V = LxLyLz the box volume. The probability density factorises into three one-dimensional densities.

For a cube, Lx = Ly = Lz = L, so E = (h²/8mL²)(nx² + ny² + nz²). Different quantum-number sets can share the same sum of squares, hence the same energy. The sets (2,1,1), (1,2,1) and (1,1,2) all give 2² + 1² + 1² = 6, so that level is three-fold degenerate. The ground state (1,1,1) is non-degenerate.

Degeneracy follows from symmetry. Stretch the box so Lx ≠ Ly ≠ Lz and the three states separate in energy. The number of states per unit energy range, the density of states, grows in three dimensions and enters the free-electron theory of Unit 7.

Where the box model is used

Real walls are finite. A finite well of depth V0 lets ψ decay exponentially into the walls instead of vanishing there. The energies then lie slightly below the infinite-box values and only a finite number of bound states fit. That exponential tail is what permits escape by tunnelling in 6.9.

  • Conjugated dye molecules: the π electrons move along a chain of length L; a longer chain means smaller E1 and absorption at longer wavelength, which is why such dyes are coloured.
  • Quantum dots: semiconductor crystals a few nanometres across; their size sets the gap and therefore the emitted colour.
  • Quantum-well lasers: thin semiconductor layers confine electrons, and the confined levels set the emission wavelength.

Two exam checks: n starts at 1, never 0; and L is the full width between the walls, not the half-width.

Worked example

Question. An electron is confined in a one-dimensional infinite box of width L = 1.0 nm. Find the first three energy levels in electron-volts. Use h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, m = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

  1. Write the formula: En = n²h²/(8mL²), so E1 = h²/(8mL²).
  2. Compute h² = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴)² = 4.390 × 10⁻⁶⁷ J²·s².
  3. Compute 8mL² = 8 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × (1.0 × 10⁻⁹)² = 7.288 × 10⁻⁴⁸ kg·m².
  4. E1 = 4.390 × 10⁻⁶⁷ ÷ 7.288 × 10⁻⁴⁸ = 6.024 × 10⁻²⁰ J.
  5. Convert: E1 = 6.024 × 10⁻²⁰ ÷ 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 0.376 eV.
  6. E2 = 2²E1 = 4 × 0.376 = 1.504 eV; E3 = 3²E1 = 9 × 0.376 = 3.384 eV.

Answer. E1 = 0.376 eV, E2 = 1.504 eV and E3 = 3.384 eV.

Exam traps

  • Using n = 0. Counting starts at n = 1; n = 0 makes ψ zero everywhere, so no particle exists.
  • Taking L as half the box width. L is the full distance between the walls; halving it multiplies every energy by 4.
  • Treating E as proportional to n. It goes as n², so doubling n quadruples the energy.
  • Mixing ℏ and h. En = n²h²/(8mL²) uses h; starting from −(ℏ²/2m)ψ″ needs ℏ = h/2π before comparing.
  • Claiming degeneracy for any box. (2,1,1), (1,2,1) and (1,1,2) share an energy only when Lx = Ly = Lz; in a rectangular box they split.

6.9 Barrier penetration and quantum tunnelling

A ball thrown at a wall taller than its energy always comes back. An electron does not. When its energy E is less than a barrier height V, its wavefunction still leaks into the barrier, decays exponentially across width a, and leaves a small amplitude on the far side. That is quantum tunnelling. Transmission falls exponentially with width, so a gap a few atoms wide can switch a large current. Tunnelling explains alpha decay, the tunnel diode, the scanning tunnelling microscope, and the fusion reactions that power the Sun.

You need: 6.7 The Schrodinger equation, 6.6 The wavefunction and Born's interpretation, 6.8 Particle in a box, and ordinary exponential decay.

A barrier the particle cannot climb

A particle of mass m moves along x with total energy E. At x = 0 it meets a rectangular barrier: potential energy V for 0 < x < a and zero outside. V and E are in joules, a is the barrier width in metres. Inside the barrier the kinetic energy would be E − V.

Classical mechanics stops here. If E < V then E − V is negative, kinetic energy cannot be negative, so the particle must turn back. Reflection probability is 1 and transmission is 0. A cricket ball thrown at a concrete wall obeys this.

Quantum mechanics does not stop here. The particle is a wavefunction ψ, and |ψ|² is a probability density (see 6.6). A wave meeting a change of potential is partly reflected and partly transmitted, as light is at glass. So there is a non-zero probability of detecting the particle beyond x = a even when E < V.

Nothing is climbed, and energy is conserved. The particle never possesses negative kinetic energy at any measured instant. It appears on the far side with the same energy E it started with, because a single particle cannot be caught halfway.

The decay inside the barrier

Start from the time-independent Schrodinger equation for a constant potential.

−(ℏ²/2m) d²ψ/dx² + Vψ = Eψ

ℏ = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s; m is mass in kg; ψ(x) is the wavefunction; V barrier height in J; E total energy in J.

Inside the barrier V > E, so (E − V) is negative. Rearranging gives d²ψ/dx² = κ²ψ with κ real and positive.

κ = √(2m(V − E)) / ℏ

κ decay constant in m⁻¹; V − E barrier excess in J; m mass in kg; ℏ = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.

The coefficient of ψ is positive, so solutions are e^(+κx) and e^(−κx), not sines and cosines. For a wide barrier the growing term is unphysical, leaving ψ(x) = A e^(−κx): the wave shrinks smoothly instead of oscillating.

The amplitude at x = a is smaller than at x = 0 by e^(−κa). The probability is the square.

T ≈ e^(−2κa)

T transmission probability (0 to 1); κ in m⁻¹; a width in m.

The standard thick-barrier result, good to leading order when κa ≫ 1, is T = 16E(V − E)/V² × e^(−2κa). The prefactor can be dropped only when E is far from V/2 or κa is very large, say κa > 5.

  • T is exponentially sensitive to a: doubling the width squares T.
  • T falls as √(V − E) grows, because κ grows that way.
  • A finite well shows the same mathematics: outside a well of finite depth the wavefunction decays as e^(−κx). The particle is partly found outside the well.

Where tunnelling is used

Alpha decay. An alpha particle inside a heavy nucleus is held by the strong force but pushed out by the Coulomb barrier of the protons. Its energy is far below that barrier, yet it escapes by tunnelling. Because T is exponential in energy, a small change in alpha energy changes the half-life by many powers of ten (Geiger–Nuttall relation).

Tunnel diode. A p-n junction (Unit 7, Metal-semiconductor junctions) is doped so heavily that the depletion layer is only a few nanometres wide. Electrons tunnel straight across it. The current-voltage curve has a region where current falls as voltage rises; this negative resistance makes the diode useful in high-frequency oscillators.

Scanning tunnelling microscope. A sharp metal tip is held a fraction of a nanometre above a conducting surface, with vacuum as the barrier. Electrons tunnel across and give a measurable current. A 0.1 nm change in gap changes current by about one order of magnitude, giving atomic-resolution images. Flash memory uses the same effect: electrons tunnel through a thin oxide layer onto a floating gate.

Nuclear fusion in the Sun. Protons in the solar core have thermal energies of a few keV, while their mutual Coulomb barrier is around an MeV. Without tunnelling, two protons could never fuse. The tiny transmission probability is enough, because the core holds an enormous number of protons.

Question. An electron of energy E = 1.0 eV faces a rectangular barrier of height V = 2.0 eV and width a = 0.5 nm. Using T ≈ e^(−2κa) with κ = √(2m(V − E))/ℏ, find κ, 2κa, and T. Take ℏ = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, m = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

  1. Convert energies to joules: V = 3.204 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, E = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, so V − E = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  2. 2m(V − E) = 2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 2.919 × 10⁻⁴⁹ in SI units.
  3. √(2m(V − E)) = 5.40 × 10⁻²⁵ kg·m/s. This is ℏκ, the magnitude of the imaginary momentum inside the barrier — the momentum a free particle with kinetic energy V − E would carry.
  4. κ = 5.40 × 10⁻²⁵ / 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ = 5.12 × 10⁹ m⁻¹.
  5. 2κa = 2 × 5.12 × 10⁹ × 0.5 × 10⁻⁹ = 5.12, dimensionless.

  7. Prefactor: 16E(V − E)/V² = 16 × 1.0 × 1.0 / 2.0² = 4. Fuller formula: T ≈ 4 × 5.98 × 10⁻³ ≈ 2.4 %, four times the simple estimate. They agree only to a factor of a few here. Drop the prefactor only when E is far from V/2 or κa > 5.

Exam traps

  • Using T ≈ e^(−2κa) when κa is not large, e.g. a thin barrier or E close to V; the prefactor 16E(V − E)/V² then matters.
  • Putting eV numbers into the square root instead of joules changes 2m(V − E) by about 10¹⁹, making κ wrong by about 10⁹–10¹⁰. Convert to joules first.
  • Writing κa instead of 2κa. Amplitude decays once across the width; probability decays twice.
  • Claiming negative kinetic energy inside the barrier. ψ decays there; no kinetic energy is measured inside.
  • Assuming T = 1 when E > V. A particle above a barrier is still partly reflected in general; T = 1 only when E ≫ V, or at resonance energies where the width holds a whole number of half-wavelengths.

Takeaways

  1. Planck saved the black-body spectrum only by allowing energy exchange in steps of hν, and that single assumption started quantum physics.
  2. One photon ejects one electron with maximum kinetic energy hν − φ, which explains why brighter light gives more electrons but not faster ones.
  3. Compton scattering shows a photon carrying momentum h/λ, so light must be treated as a particle in collisions.
  4. Every moving body has a de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p, which is measurable for electrons but unmeasurably small for a cricket ball.
  5. The Davisson-Germer diffraction pattern of electrons turned matter waves from an idea into an experiment.
  6. The wave packet travels at the group velocity, which equals the particle velocity; the phase velocity of a matter wave is not the particle speed.
  7. The uncertainty principle is a limit set by nature, not by the clumsiness of instruments.
  8. The wavefunction is not directly measurable; only |ψ|² has physical meaning, and it must be normalised.
  9. The Schrodinger equation plays the same role for matter that Newton's second law plays for classical particles.
  10. Trapping a particle between rigid walls allows only certain energies, so confinement itself causes quantisation.
  11. Tunnelling probability falls exponentially with barrier width and height, which powers the scanning tunnelling microscope and limits shrinking transistors.

Exam question bank

Long answers here almost always want a clean derivation, the boundary conditions written down explicitly, the physical meaning of the final result, and a labelled sketch of ψ or |ψ|².

Remember and understand

Understand State Planck's hypothesis. Explain how it removes the ultraviolet catastrophe and fits the observed black-body spectrum. 10 marks

Classical Rayleigh-Jeans law predicts intensity rising as 1/λ⁴ and infinite total energy; Wien's law fits short wavelengths only; both fail somewhere; Planck's assumption: oscillators emit or absorb energy only in multiples of hν; Average energy per oscillator becomes hν/(e^(hν/kT) − 1) instead of kT; Resulting curve peaks and falls; matches experiment at all wavelengths; Wien displacement λ_max T = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K appears as a consequence.

Understand Explain the photoelectric effect and Einstein's photoelectric equation. Use it to explain the effect of intensity and frequency on the emitted electrons. 10 marks

Define work function φ and threshold frequency ν₀ = φ/h; Einstein's equation: hν = φ + ½mv²_max; Stopping potential eV₀ = hν − φ and its use in finding φ; Intensity controls number of photons and hence photocurrent, not maximum energy; Frequency controls maximum kinetic energy; below ν₀ no emission at any intensity; Emission is instantaneous because one photon gives all its energy to one electron.

Understand What is the Compton effect? Derive the expression for the Compton shift and state its significance. 12 marks

X-ray photon of energy hν collides with a loosely bound electron at rest; Conservation of energy: hν + mₑc² = hν′ + √(p²c² + mₑ²c⁴); Conservation of momentum in two perpendicular directions; Eliminate electron momentum to get Δλ = (h/mₑc)(1 − cos θ); Compton wavelength h/mₑc = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m; Significance: photon has momentum h/λ, so light behaves as a particle in collisions; The unshifted line comes from tightly bound electrons of the whole atom.

Understand State the de Broglie hypothesis and describe the Davisson-Germer experiment that confirmed it. 12 marks

λ = h/p; for kinetic energy K, λ = h/√(2mK); For an electron accelerated through V volts, λ = 12.27/√V angstrom; Apparatus: electron gun, nickel target, movable detector, vacuum; Nickel crystal acts as a grating with known spacing; Diffraction maxima at particular angles, matching Bragg's law; Measured wavelength agrees with h/p, confirming matter waves; Wave nature is not visible for heavy bodies because λ is tiny.

Apply

Apply Derive the energy eigenvalues and normalised wavefunctions for a particle in a one-dimensional infinite potential well. 14 marks

Set V = 0 inside 0 < x < L and V = ∞ outside; Write the time-independent Schrodinger equation and solve: ψ = A sin kx + B cos kx; Boundary condition ψ(0) = 0 gives B = 0; Boundary condition ψ(L) = 0 gives kL = nπ, so k = nπ/L; Eₙ = n²h²/(8mL²), n = 1, 2, 3…; Normalise to get A = √(2/L); Show that n = 0 is not allowed and that energy is never zero; spacing grows with n.

Apply Obtain the transmission probability for a particle of energy E tunnelling through a rectangular barrier of height V₀ and width a, when E < V₀. Discuss the result. 12 marks

Three regions; oscillatory solutions outside, decaying exponential inside the barrier; Match ψ and dψ/dx at both boundaries; Transmission T ≈ 16 (E/V₀)(1 − E/V₀) e^(−2αa) with α = √(2m(V₀ − E))/ℏ; T falls exponentially with width a and with √(V₀ − E); T is not zero even for wide barriers, so classical reflection is not complete; Comment on sensitivity: a small change in a changes T a lot.

Apply Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on a metal of work function 2.0 eV. Find the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electrons, the stopping potential and the threshold wavelength. 8 marks

Photon energy E = hc/λ = 3.1 eV (use hc = 1240 eV·nm); K_max = E − φ = 1.1 eV; Stopping potential V₀ = 1.1 V; Threshold wavelength λ₀ = hc/φ = 620 nm; State clearly that intensity would not change these values.

Apply Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through 100 V and compare it with that of an electron at 10 keV and of a 1 g object moving at 10 m/s. 8 marks

λ = h/√(2meV) = 12.27/√V angstrom; At 100 V, λ ≈ 1.23 × 10⁻¹⁰ m, comparable to atomic spacing; At 10 keV, λ ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹¹ m, so the electron probes smaller distances; For the 1 g object, λ ≈ 6.6 × 10⁻³¹ m, far too small to detect; Conclude why diffraction is seen for electrons but not for everyday objects.

Apply Using the uncertainty principle, estimate the minimum kinetic energy of an electron confined to a region of size equal to a typical nucleus (10⁻¹⁴ m) and comment on the result. 8 marks

Take Δx ≈ 10⁻¹⁴ m and Δp ≥ ℏ/(2Δx); Δp ≈ 5.3 × 10⁻²¹ kg·m/s; K ≈ (Δp)²/2m ≈ 1 MeV or more; Compare with a few MeV of nuclear binding energy; Conclude that an electron cannot be held inside a nucleus.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Distinguish between phase velocity and group velocity for a matter wave. Show that the group velocity equals the particle velocity and that v_p v_g = c² for a relativistic particle. 12 marks

Definitions v_p = ω/k and v_g = dω/dk; A particle is a wave packet, a superposition of many k values; Compute ω from E = ℏω and p = ℏk, giving v_p = E/p; Differentiate to get v_g = dE/dp = p/m = particle velocity; For relativistic case v_g = v and v_p = c²/v, so v_p v_g = c²; Phase velocity carries no energy or information; only the packet does.

Evaluate Explain the Born interpretation of the wavefunction. List the conditions ψ must satisfy and why normalisation is necessary. 10 marks

ψ is a complex amplitude with no direct physical meaning; |ψ|² dx is the probability of finding the particle between x and x + dx; ψ must be single-valued, continuous, with continuous first derivative where V is finite; ψ must vanish at infinite potential walls and be finite everywhere; Normalisation ∫|ψ|² dx = 1 fixes the scale factor; Superposition and the measurement of an average value follow from this interpretation.

Evaluate Explain quantum tunnelling and its applications. Why is tunnelling more likely for light particles and thin barriers? 12 marks

Physics of the barrier region: exponentially decaying wavefunction; T depends on e^(−2αa); α grows with particle mass and barrier height; Electrons tunnel easily, protons much less, macroscopic bodies never; Applications: scanning tunnelling microscope, tunnel diode, Josephson junction, alpha decay, flash memory; STM resolution comes from the steep exponential dependence on gap width; Limitation: tunnelling leakage in very thin gate oxides of small transistors.

Evaluate Trace how black-body radiation, the photoelectric effect and the Compton effect together forced the collapse of classical physics and the birth of quantum mechanics. 16 marks

Classical wave theory: energy is continuous, and Maxwell's equations allow any amplitude; Black-body spectrum could not be fitted; Planck needed energy quanta hν; Photoelectric effect contradicted wave ideas: threshold frequency and instantaneous emission; Einstein extended the quantum idea to light itself and explained the threshold; Compton scattering provided momentum evidence: photon momentum h/λ; Common thread: energy and momentum are exchanged in discrete packets; Contrast with Newtonian determinism and continuous energy of classical mechanics.

Analyze Extend the one-dimensional infinite well to a three-dimensional cubical box of side L, obtain the energy eigenvalues, and discuss degeneracy. 14 marks

Separate variables: ψ = X(x)Y(y)Z(z); Each direction gives a one-dimensional box problem with the same boundary conditions; E = (h²/8mL²)(n_x² + n_y² + n_z²); Each state labelled by three quantum numbers, all positive integers; Lowest state (1,1,1) is non-degenerate; States like (1,1,2), (1,2,1), (2,1,1) have the same energy: degeneracy; Note how box size L shrinks the level spacing as the box grows.

Glossary

Absorption
Taking in of radiation by a body, which for a black body is complete at every wavelength.
Angular resolution
The smallest separation a probe can distinguish; a shorter wavelength gives a better resolution.
Barrier width
The thickness a particle must cross in tunnelling; transmission falls exponentially with it.
Bragg's law
nλ = 2d sin θ, the condition for constructive diffraction from crystal planes of spacing d.
Complementarity
The idea that wave and particle descriptions are both needed yet never applied in the same measurement.
Compton wavelength
The constant h/mₑc = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m that sets the size of the wavelength shift in Compton scattering.
Degeneracy
The situation where more than one quantum state has the same energy.
Eigenstate
A state of definite energy; the wavefunction is a solution of the time-independent Schrodinger equation for that energy.
Emission spectrum
The distribution of radiation given out by a body as a function of wavelength or frequency.
Expectation value
The average of many identical measurements of a quantity, obtained from the wavefunction.
Grating
A periodic structure of many parallel lines or planes that splits and diffracts a wave.
Matter wave
The wave associated with a moving particle, with wavelength λ = h/p and frequency ν = E/h.
Photocurrent
The current produced by photoelectrons in a photocell; its size depends on how many electrons are emitted per second.
Photoelectric equation
hν = φ + ½mv²_max, the energy balance for one photon ejecting one electron.
Probabilistic description
Predicting a distribution of many measurement results rather than the trajectory of a single particle.
Quantum limit
The value set by ℏ; classical physics is recovered when uncertainties become negligible compared with the system size.
Recoil electron
The electron set in motion in a Compton collision, which carries the lost energy and momentum.
Stopping potential
The reverse voltage just large enough to stop the fastest photoelectrons, giving their maximum kinetic energy as eV₀.
Transmission coefficient
The fraction of incident particles or waves that get through a barrier.
Wave packet
A group of waves of slightly different wavelengths that adds up to a localised pulse representing a particle.
Wien's displacement law
The wavelength of peak emission falls as temperature rises, λ_max T = 2.898 × 10⁻³ m·K.
Zero-point energy
The lowest allowed energy of a confined particle; in a box it is h²/8mL², not zero.
