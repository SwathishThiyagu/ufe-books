5.1 Spontaneous and stimulated emission; Einstein's coefficients

An atom in an excited energy level can give out light in two different ways. Spontaneous emission happens by itself, with a random direction and phase. Stimulated emission happens when a passing photon of the right energy makes the atom emit a second photon in phase and in the same direction. Absorption is the reverse: the atom takes a photon and rises to the upper level. Einstein's A and B coefficients put these three rates into one balance equation. The A/B ratio depends on frequency as ν³, a fact that sets the scale for lasers and for thermal light. Used in laser gain, LED light, and spectroscopy.

You need photon energy from Unit 6 and electromagnetic waves from Unit 3; the next step, population inversion, uses these rates.

Three processes at one pair of levels

Take one atom with two energy levels. E1 is the lower level and E2 is the upper level. The gap is E2 − E1 = hν. h is Planck's constant, 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s, and ν is the light frequency in hertz. Three processes can change the atom between these levels.

  • Absorption: a photon of energy hν hits an atom in E1. The atom absorbs the photon and moves to E2. The photon is gone. The rate is proportional to the number of atoms in E1 and to the light energy density.
  • Spontaneous emission: an atom in E2 drops to E1 without any incoming photon. It emits one photon of energy hν. The direction, phase and polarization are random. The rate is A21N2.
  • Stimulated emission: a photon of energy hν passes an atom already in E2. The atom drops to E1 and emits a second photon. The incoming photon is not absorbed. The two outgoing photons have the same frequency, phase, direction and polarization. The rate is B21N2u(ν), where u(ν) is defined in the next section.

The key difference is the trigger. Spontaneous emission starts from nothing. Stimulated emission is triggered by an existing photon and clones it. This cloning is why laser light can be coherent and directional.

Einstein's rate equation and coefficients

Let N1 and N2 be the number of atoms per cubic metre in E1 and E2. Let u(ν) be the energy density per unit frequency of the light field. u(ν) is measured in J m⁻³ Hz⁻¹. A21 is the spontaneous emission probability per second, in s⁻¹. B12 and B21 are the Einstein coefficients for absorption and stimulated emission. For non-degenerate levels, B12 = B21.

dN2/dt = −A21N2 − B21N2u(ν) + B12N1u(ν)

dN2/dt is the net rate of change of upper-level atoms per cubic metre per second; the first two terms remove atoms from E2; the third term adds atoms to E2 by absorption; N1 and N2 are atoms per cubic metre; u(ν) is energy density per unit frequency in J m⁻³ Hz⁻¹; A21 is in s⁻¹; B12 and B21 are the Einstein coefficients.

A21/B21 = 8πhν³/c³

A21 is the spontaneous emission probability per second; B21 is the stimulated emission coefficient; h is Planck's constant, 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s; ν is frequency in Hz; c is the speed of light in vacuum, 3.00×10⁸ m/s. With non-degenerate levels, B12 = B21.

At thermal equilibrium dN2/dt = 0. The light is black-body radiation. Matching the rate balance to Planck's law gives the two Einstein relations above. The second relation has ν³, so the ratio A21/B21 is not a constant. It rises steeply with frequency.

Why the ν³ factor matters

The A/B ratio grows as the cube of frequency. At radio frequencies, stimulated emission and absorption are strong compared with spontaneous emission. At visible frequencies, spontaneous emission is very strong. A21 is large, so an excited atom loses energy quickly unless the level is metastable. This is why laser levels are chosen with long spontaneous lifetime and why pumping is needed.

A stimulated photon is in phase and in the same direction as the incoming photon. One photon can create two, two can create four, and so on. That is amplification by stimulated emission. The light is coherent because all cloned photons share phase. The direction is set by the first photon.

For a level with only spontaneous emission, the spontaneous lifetime is τsp = 1/A21. A small A21 means a long-lived upper level. Such a level can store atoms long enough for stimulated emission to dominate. That is the bridge to population inversion and laser action.

Worked example

Question. A laser pulse carries 1.0 mJ at λ = 632.8 nm. Find the photon energy in joules and electronvolts, and the number of photons. Use h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s and c = 3.00×10⁸ m/s.

  1. Convert the wavelength: λ = 632.8 nm = 632.8×10⁻⁹ m = 6.328×10⁻⁷ m. Convert the pulse energy: Epulse = 1.0 mJ = 1.0×10⁻³ J.
  2. Compute the photon energy: E = hc/λ = (6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s)(3.00×10⁸ m/s)/(6.328×10⁻⁷ m). The numerator is 1.9878×10⁻²⁵ J m. Division gives E = 3.141×10⁻¹⁹ J ≈ 3.14×10⁻¹⁹ J.
  3. Convert to electronvolts: E = 3.141×10⁻¹⁹ J / 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J/eV = 1.961 eV ≈ 1.96 eV.
  4. Find the number of photons: N = Epulse/E = 1.0×10⁻³ J / 3.141×10⁻¹⁹ J = 3.183×10¹⁵ ≈ 3.2×10¹⁵ photons.

Exam traps

  • Spontaneous emission has no incoming photon; stimulated emission needs one and returns two photons with the same phase and direction.
  • Do not use A21/B21 as a constant. It changes as ν³, so the same atom has very different ratios at radio and visible frequencies.
  • In dN2/dt, spontaneous and stimulated emission both carry a minus sign; absorption carries a plus sign. Reversing a sign changes the equilibrium result.
  • B12 = B21 holds for non-degenerate levels. If a level has degeneracy g, the relation becomes g1B12 = g2B21; many exam answers lose marks by ignoring this.
  • Photon energy is hν. Convert λ = 632.8 nm to metres before using hc/λ; mixing nm with m gives a factor of 10⁹ error.

5.2 Population inversion, pumping and laser characteristics

A laser does not work by making atoms emit more often. It works by arranging more atoms in an upper energy level than in a lower one. That arrangement is population inversion. A pump supplies energy to create it, and two mirrors keep light passing through the gain medium many times. This chapter explains inversion, pumping, the threshold for laser action, and the four standard laser characteristics: monochromaticity, coherence, directionality and brightness. These ideas explain why a laser pointer is so different from a torch, and they set up the named lasers in 5.3–5.5.

You need: Einstein coefficients from 5.1 and the Boltzmann factor from Unit 6.

Thermal equilibrium gives absorption, not gain

Take two energy levels: lower level 1 with population N₁ and upper level 2 with population N₂. Population means number of atoms per unit volume. At thermal equilibrium the Boltzmann factor gives

N₂/N₁ = (g₂/g₁) exp(−ΔE/(k_B T))

g₂ and g₁ are degeneracies; ΔE = E₂ − E₁ is the transition energy in joules; k_B is Boltzmann's constant; T is absolute temperature in kelvin.

For equal degeneracies g₂ = g₁ = 1, the ratio is exp(−ΔE/(k_B T)). Visible photons have ΔE much larger than k_B T at room temperature, so N₂ is far smaller than N₁.

Light in the medium causes absorption 1→2 and stimulated emission 2→1. The rates are B₁₂N₁ρ and B₂₁N₂ρ, where ρ is the energy density of the radiation; B₁₂ and B₂₁ are equal only when g₁ = g₂. Absorption wins when N₁ > N₂. Net amplification needs N₂ > N₁. This is population inversion. It is a non-equilibrium state: the upper level holds more atoms than the lower one. With unequal degeneracies the condition is N₂/g₂ > N₁/g₁; the simple form N₂ > N₁ assumes equal degeneracies.

Metastable level and pumping

An ordinary excited atom returns by spontaneous emission in nanoseconds, too fast to store atoms. A laser uses a metastable level: an excited level with a long spontaneous lifetime, often milliseconds. Atoms collect there while the pump supplies energy. The metastable level is the upper laser level.

Pumping moves atoms from lower levels to the upper level. Common methods are optical pumping (a flashlamp or another laser supplies photons), electrical pumping (a gas discharge or forward-biased semiconductor junction supplies energetic electrons), and chemical pumping (a reaction produces excited molecules). The pump must maintain N₂ > N₁; it need not be light.

In a three-level scheme the lower laser level is the ground state. More than half the atoms must leave the ground state before inversion begins, so threshold pump power is high. Ruby is the standard example; see 5.3. In a four-level scheme the lower laser level sits above the ground state and empties quickly. Inversion starts when a few atoms reach the upper level, so threshold is much lower. Nd:YAG is a common four-level laser; see 5.4.

Optical cavity and threshold

The gain medium sits between two mirrors: M₁ is highly reflecting, M₂ is partially reflecting. Together they form an optical cavity or resonator. Light along the cavity axis passes through the medium many times. Each pass adds photons by stimulated emission. The partial mirror lets a small fraction leave as the output beam.

R₁ R₂ exp[2(g − α)L] ≥ 1

R₁, R₂ are mirror reflectances (0 to 1); g is gain coefficient per metre; α is loss coefficient per metre; L is gain-medium length in metres.

The left side is the fraction of light surviving one round trip. Below 1, light dies out. Above threshold, output grows until gain saturation limits it. Some syllabi call this the round-trip gain condition.

Continuous-wave (CW) operation gives steady output. Pulsed operation stores energy and releases it in short bursts. CW lasers need cooling because the pump heats the medium; pulsed lasers reach higher peak power.

Laser characteristics

Four standard characteristics separate laser light from lamp light.

  • Monochromaticity: the output occupies a narrow wavelength band Δλ around a central wavelength λ. Small Δλ/λ means a well-defined colour or frequency. No laser is perfectly single-wavelength; Δλ is small but nonzero.
  • Coherence: the wave keeps a fixed phase relation across the beam (spatial coherence) and along the beam for a coherence time (temporal coherence). This enables interference and holography; see Unit 4, Wave Optics.
  • Directionality: the beam spreads by a small divergence angle θ, so it stays narrow over long distances. A torch spreads light over a wide cone; a laser does not.
  • Brightness: power per unit area per unit solid angle. A laser can be bright at modest power because its beam is narrow in angle and area.

B_r = P/(A Ω)

B_r is brightness; P is power in watts; A is beam area in square metres; Ω is solid angle in steradians.

The four properties are separate, but brightness is not independent of the others: a beam that is narrow in area and divergence, and carries enough power, is bright.

Worked example

Question. For a transition of energy 1.96 eV at T = 300 K, find the thermal-equilibrium ratio N₂/N₁ for equal degeneracies. Compare it with an inverted ratio N₂/N₁ = 2. Use k_B = 8.617×10⁻⁵ eV/K.

  1. k_B T = (8.617×10⁻⁵ eV/K)(300 K) = 0.025851 eV.
  2. ΔE/(k_B T) = 1.96 eV / 0.025851 eV = 75.82 (dimensionless).
  3. For equal degeneracies, N₂/N₁ = exp(−75.82) = 1.2×10⁻³³.
  4. The inverted ratio N₂/N₁ = 2 is many orders of magnitude larger than the thermal-equilibrium value.

Answer. At 300 K, N₂/N₁ ≈ 1.2×10⁻³³, so an inverted ratio of 2 cannot occur thermally; pumping is needed.

Exam traps

  • A bigger pump does not always give laser output. Fast non-radiative decay or a short upper-level lifetime keeps N₂ below N₁, so inversion is never reached; and even with inversion, high cavity loss or low mirror reflectance can keep the round-trip factor below 1, so oscillation never starts.
  • Three-level lasers are not easier because they have fewer levels; they need more than half the atoms pumped, so their threshold is higher.
  • N₂ > N₁ is the equal-degeneracy form. With degeneracies g₁ and g₂, use N₂/g₂ > N₁/g₁.
  • Thermal equilibrium never gives inversion. Heating a medium increases both populations; it does not reverse the Boltzmann ratio.
  • Do not confuse brightness with power. A low-power laser with a narrow beam can be brighter than a high-power lamp.

5.3 Ruby and He-Ne lasers

Ruby and He-Ne were the first lasers built, and they still show the two basic pumping routes. Ruby is a sapphire crystal (Al₂O₃) holding a small fraction of Cr³⁺ ions; a flashlamp drives a three-level cycle and the output is a short red pulse at 694.3 nm. He-Ne is a gas mixture in a discharge tube; collisions hand energy from helium to neon, giving a continuous red beam at 632.8 nm. Both beams are red, so their numbers are easy to swap under exam pressure. Only a few universities teach this pair in detail.

You need 5.1 photon energy and stimulated emission and 5.2 population inversion, pumping and the optical cavity.

Ruby: a three-level crystal laser

Ruby is aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃, sapphire) doped with about 0.05 % chromium. The Cr³⁺ ions give the pink colour and do all the lasing. A rod a few centimetres long sits between two mirrors, one fully silvered and one partly silvered, so light passes through the rod many times.

  1. The flashlamp fires a bright white flash; Cr³⁺ ions absorb green and blue light and rise from the ground level to broad pump bands.
  2. Fast non-radiative decay drops them to the metastable level, which lives about 3 ms, so ions collect there.
  3. Pumping is strong enough that the metastable level holds more ions than the ground level: population inversion, because the laser transition ends at the ground level.
  4. A passing 694.3 nm photon triggers stimulated emission; the ion falls straight to the ground level and the light is amplified.
  5. The pulse escapes through the partial mirror as a short, high-energy red flash. A second flash repeats the cycle.

The lower laser level is the ground level itself. More than half of all Cr³⁺ ions must be lifted before gain appears, so the pump must be violent. That is why ruby is a three-level, pulsed laser: a flashlamp cannot fire continuously without overheating the rod.

He-Ne: energy transfer in a gas

The tube holds helium and neon in roughly 10:1 ratio at a few torr, with a DC discharge of about 1 kV. Helium does the absorbing; neon does the lasing.

  1. Electron collisions in the discharge excite helium atoms into long-lived metastable states.
  2. A metastable helium atom strikes a ground-state neon atom. The two energy levels nearly match, so energy transfers resonantly and neon is left in its 3s level.
  3. The neon 3s → 2p transition emits 632.8 nm, amplified between the mirrors.
  4. The neon 2p level empties quickly by spontaneous emission to the 1s level, and helium returns to ground at the tube walls.

Because the lower level (2p) clears itself, the inversion needs only a modest pump, and the beam is continuous: a few milliwatts at 632.8 nm with a very narrow linewidth. Compare the two: ruby gives joule pulses at 694.3 nm, He-Ne gives milliwatts steady at 632.8 nm.

Exam traps

  • Ruby is three-level and pulsed. Its laser transition ends at the ground state, so more than half the ions must be pumped; it never runs continuously.
  • Keep the reds apart: ruby is 694.3 nm, He-Ne is 632.8 nm. A question that says only 'red laser' is testing this.
  • In He-Ne the lasing atom is neon. Helium only absorbs discharge energy and passes it on by collision.
  • Ruby gives joule pulses, He-Ne gives milliwatts. Do not plug a ruby pulse energy into a continuous-power formula.
  • Ruby inversion is between the metastable level and the ground level, not between two excited levels.

5.4 Nd:YAG, CO2 and dye lasers

Three gain media show how a laser's medium fixes its light. Neodymium ions in a YAG crystal give 1064 nm pulses. Carbon dioxide gas gives 10.6 μm infrared power. Organic dye solution gives a broad visible band that can be tuned. The medium decides wavelength, power and tuning range. These lasers cut steel, weld metal, drill, do surgery and pump other lasers. Only a few universities teach this chapter in detail.

You need 5.1 photon energy and emission and 5.2 pumping and inversion.

Nd:YAG and CO2 lasers

Nd:YAG means neodymium-doped yttrium aluminium garnet, Y₃Al₅O₁₂. Nd³⁺ ions replace some Y³⁺ ions. A flashlamp or diode laser optically pumps the ions. They reach a short-lived band, drop to a metastable level, then lase to a lower level that empties fast. This four-level scheme makes inversion easy. The output is 1064 nm, near infrared, pulsed or continuous. Uses include drilling, marking, rangefinders and surgery. The host crystal conducts heat well. The gas tube can be long.

The CO2 laser uses a CO₂–N₂–He gas mixture. An electrical discharge excites N₂. N₂ transfers energy to CO₂. CO₂ lases at 10.6 μm, far infrared. He cools the gas and helps empty the lower level. Power can be high, continuous or pulsed. The 10.6 μm light is absorbed by many non-metals, so it cuts wood, cloth, acrylic and steel and welds metal. Gas flows to remove heat. Dye lasers and tuning

A dye laser uses an organic dye in a solvent, such as rhodamine 6G in ethanol. The dye has a broad visible gain band because many vibrational levels join each electronic state. An optical pump, a flashlamp or another laser, raises the molecules. A grating is a ruled surface that reflects one wavelength back, so turning the grating tunes the output. The band can cover tens of nanometres. Dye lasers serve spectroscopy, fluorescence microscopy and isotope separation. The liquid flows because pump light and laser light damage the dye. They are bulky but tunable. Tuning changes the grating angle, not the dye chemistry. The output stays visible for common dyes. A dye laser can also be pulsed for short flashes. The broad band gives many cavity modes; the grating picks one. Nd:YAG and CO2 are fixed-line lasers; dye is the tunable one. This is why dye lasers are used where one wavelength must be scanned.

Worked example

Question. A CO2 laser emits at λ = 10.6 μm. Find the photon energy in joules and in electronvolts, and the frequency.

  1. Convert: λ = 10.6 μm = 10.6 × 10⁻⁶ m = 1.06 × 10⁻⁵ m.
  2. Use E = hc/λ, with h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s.
  3. hc = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ = 1.988 × 10⁻²⁵ J m.
  4. E = (1.988 × 10⁻²⁵)/(1.06 × 10⁻⁵) = 1.87 × 10⁻²⁰ J.
  5. E = (1.87 × 10⁻²⁰)/(1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 0.117 eV.
  6. ν = c/λ = (3.00 × 10⁸)/(1.06 × 10⁻⁵) = 2.83 × 10¹³ Hz.

Answer. The photon energy is 1.87 × 10⁻²⁰ J, or 0.117 eV, and the frequency is 2.83 × 10¹³ Hz.

Exam traps

  • CO2 laser wavelength is 10.6 μm, not 10.6 nm.
  • Nd:YAG output is 1064 nm, not 1064 μm.
  • CO2 is pumped by an electrical discharge in gas; Nd:YAG and dye lasers are optically pumped.
  • Dye is tunable because its gain band is broad; Nd:YAG and CO2 are fixed-line.
  • Nd:YAG is four-level, not three-level like ruby; its lower laser level empties fast.

5.5 Semiconductor laser

A semiconductor laser is a forward-biased p-n junction that emits coherent light from a tiny crystal cavity. In a direct band gap material such as GaAs, injected electrons and holes recombine and release photons. The cleaved ends of the crystal act as mirrors, so light is amplified before it leaves. These lasers are small, efficient and easy to modulate. They are used in fibre links, barcode scanners, optical mice, CD players and laser pointers.

You need the p-n junction and energy bands (see Unit 7, Energy bands and intrinsic and extrinsic semiconductors) and the idea of population inversion from 5.2.

Direct band gap and photon energy

A semiconductor emits light when an electron in the conduction band falls into a hole in the valence band. The energy lost appears as a photon. In a direct band gap material, the conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum occur at the same crystal momentum, so the electron can fall without a phonon. GaAs is the standard example. Silicon has an indirect band gap: the electron must change momentum, usually with a phonon, so light emission is weak and silicon is a poor laser material.

λ ≈ hc / Eg

λ is the vacuum wavelength; h is Planck's constant; c is the speed of light; Eg is the band gap energy. With hc = 1240 eV nm and Eg in eV, λ in nm is 1240 / Eg.

The relation sets the wavelength. A larger Eg gives a shorter wavelength. For GaAs, Eg = 1.42 eV, so λ ≈ 1240/1.42 ≈ 873 nm, in the near infrared.

Only direct band gap materials give useful gain in a simple diode laser. Indirect materials heat up instead of emitting light.

Forward bias, inversion and threshold

The device is a p-n junction. Under forward bias, the n-side is negative and the p-side positive. Electrons are injected from n to p, and holes from p to n. In a thin active region near the junction, the injected carrier density becomes large. Recombination of electrons and holes then emits photons.

Population inversion means the occupation probability at the conduction-band edge is larger than at the valence-band edge. In terms of the quasi-Fermi levels, the separation must exceed the band gap energy: Efc − Efv > Eg, where Efc is the electron quasi-Fermi level, Efv is the hole quasi-Fermi level and Eg is the band gap energy. Forward bias alone does not guarantee it; the injection current must be high enough. The current at which gain equals cavity loss is the threshold current Ith. Below Ith the diode emits spontaneous light like an LED. Above Ith stimulated emission dominates and the output rises sharply. This is the inversion idea of 5.2 in a diode.

Two cleaved crystal faces at the ends act as mirrors. Light travels back and forth, is amplified, and a beam leaves through one facet.

  • Homojunction: one p-n junction of the same material; carrier confinement is weak.
  • Double heterostructure: a thin lower-band-gap active layer between two higher-band-gap layers; carriers and light are confined, so Ith is lower.

Laser versus LED and where it is used

An LED is also a forward-biased p-n junction, but it has no optical cavity and no threshold. Its light is spontaneous, with a broad spectrum and a wide beam. The laser has feedback, a threshold, and a narrow coherent beam. Near-infrared diode lasers of the GaAs family (roughly 780–850 nm) serve short-range links and optical mice; CD players use about 780 nm and barcode scanners about 650–680 nm. 5.6 Applications of lasers

A laser is chosen for a job by four numbers: wavelength, power, coherence and pulse length. Wavelength decides what absorbs the light. Power decides speed and depth. Coherence makes interference useful. Pulse length limits heat damage. These four numbers explain why a CO₂ laser cuts cloth but a semiconductor laser feeds a fibre link. Industry uses lasers to cut, weld and drill. Medicine uses them for eye and skin treatment. Communication uses them as fibre sources. Measurement uses them in rangefinders, interferometers and LIDAR, which is light detection and ranging. Shops use them in barcode scanners.

You need: 5.5 Semiconductor laser for the source, 5.4 for wavelength choice, and Unit 4 interference for measurement uses.

What decides the application

A laser is not chosen by name alone. Four properties decide the job: wavelength, power, coherence and pulse length. Wavelength is the colour or infrared line. Power is energy delivered per second. Coherence is how fixed the phase relation is across the beam. Pulse length is how long the light stays on. A cutter needs high average power and a spot that the material absorbs. A fibre link needs a small, fast semiconductor source. A hologram needs coherence. Eye surgery needs a pulse short enough to limit heat spread.

The same laser can serve different jobs when focused differently. A focused beam raises power density, the power per unit area, so material melts or vaporises. A broad beam heats a larger area. Pulsed operation gives high peak power with low average heating. This is why a pulsed Nd:YAG laser can break a membrane in the eye while a continuous CO₂ laser cuts cloth.

Industry, medicine and consumer uses

Material processing uses the laser as a controlled heat source. Cutting melts or vaporises a narrow line. Welding melts two edges so they join. Drilling makes a small hole, often in a hard material. CO₂ lasers at 10.6 µm cut cloth, wood and plastics. Nd:YAG lasers at 1064 nm weld and drill metals. The heat-affected zone is narrow because the energy arrives in a small spot for a short time. Indian sheet-metal shops use laser cutting for panels and enclosures.

Medicine uses the same idea with tissue. The wavelength must be absorbed by the target and not by unwanted tissue. Argon lasers at 488 nm and 514 nm are absorbed by blood and are used in retinal treatment. Nd:YAG at 1064 nm is used for posterior capsulotomy after cataract surgery. Excimer lasers at 193 nm shape the cornea. Dermatology uses pulsed dye lasers for vascular lesions and Nd:YAG for hair removal. Short pulses limit heat spread.

Consumer uses are common. A barcode scanner sweeps a low-power red laser diode or He-Ne beam across bars. Black bars absorb more and white gaps reflect more, so the reflected light carries the code. A laser printer writes an image on a photoconductor drum with a modulated beam. Optical storage uses a focused spot: CD at 780 nm, DVD at 650 nm and Blu-ray at 405 nm. Shorter wavelength gives a smaller spot and more data per disc.

Communication and measurement

Fibre communication uses a semiconductor laser as the source. The laser is modulated, meaning its output is switched or varied with the data. It couples into a fibre because its beam is narrow and bright. Silica fibre has low loss at 1310 nm and 1550 nm, so lasers at these wavelengths are common. A photodiode at the far end converts light back to current. Details of loss and repeaters are in 5.9.

Measurement uses coherence and timing. A laser rangefinder sends a pulse and times the echo. The pulse travels to the target and back, so distance is half the path. LIDAR scans a beam and measures many echo times to build a map. An interferometer splits a coherent beam into two paths and recombines them. A change in path length of one wavelength shifts the interference by one fringe. This measures small displacements; see Unit 4, interference.

d = c t / 2

d is distance to the target in metres; c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ is the speed of light; t is the echo time in seconds.

Spectroscopy uses a tunable dye laser or a narrow-line laser to probe absorption lines. Holography records the interference of an object beam and a reference beam; the theory belongs to Unit 4.

5.7 Optical fibre: principle and structure

An optical fibre is a glass or plastic thread that carries light along its length. It works by total internal reflection: light in a core of higher refractive index bounces off a cladding of lower refractive index. This keeps the ray inside the core for kilometres. Fibres carry internet and phone traffic, connect sensors, and make images in endoscopes. The key numbers are the critical angle, the numerical aperture and the acceptance angle. They tell how tightly light is trapped and how much light the fibre can gather.

You need refraction and total internal reflection (see Unit 4, Wave Optics) and the idea of an electromagnetic wave (see Unit 3, Electromagnetism and Maxwell's Equations).

The core, the cladding and total internal reflection

The core is the central glass or plastic path that carries light. Its refractive index is n1. The cladding surrounds the core and has a lower refractive index n2. A plastic buffer covers the cladding for mechanical protection. The outer jacket protects the whole cable from moisture, abrasion and bending. Only the core and cladding guide light.

Total internal reflection happens at the core–cladding boundary when light travels from the denser core to the rarer cladding. Measure the angle of incidence θi from the normal to the boundary. At the critical angle θc, the refracted ray grazes along the boundary. For θi > θc, no light crosses into the cladding; the ray reflects back into the core. This is total internal reflection.

θ<sub>c</sub> = sin<sup>−1</sup>(n<sub>2</sub> / n<sub>1</sub>)

θc is the critical angle measured from the normal at the core–cladding boundary; n1 is the core refractive index; n2 is the cladding refractive index. The formula needs n1 > n2.

A ray launched too steeply hits the boundary below θc and leaks out. A ray launched within the fibre's acceptance cone stays guided. The core must have n1 > n2; if the cladding index is higher, total internal reflection cannot occur at the core boundary.

Numerical aperture and acceptance angle

A meridional ray is a ray that travels in a plane containing the fibre axis. It crosses the axis after each reflection. Consider such a ray entering the flat fibre end from air at angle θa to the axis. At the air–core face, Snell's law gives sin θa = n1 sin θr, where θr is the angle inside the core to the axis. The ray then meets the core–cladding boundary at angle 90° − θr to the normal.

For total internal reflection, 90° − θr ≥ θc, so θr ≤ 90° − θc. The largest entry angle that is guided is θa. Put θr = 90° − θc in Snell's law: sin θa = n1 cos θc = n1√(1 − (n2/n1)2) = √(n12 − n22). The right side is the numerical aperture.

NA = √(n<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup> − n<sub>2</sub><sup>2</sup>)

NA is the numerical aperture, a dimensionless number; n1 and n2 are the core and cladding refractive indices. NA measures the light-gathering power of the fibre.

θa = sin−1(NA)

θa is the acceptance half-angle in air; NA is the numerical aperture. A ray entering within this half-angle to the axis is guided.

NA is dimensionless. A larger NA means a wider acceptance cone and more light gathered, but it also tends to support more guided modes. A smaller NA gives tighter guidance and a narrower cone. The acceptance angle is measured in air; inside another medium, replace the air index 1 by that medium's index.

Worked example

Question. A step-index optical fibre has core refractive index n1 = 1.50 and cladding refractive index n2 = 1.48. The fibre is in air. Find (a) the critical angle at the core–cladding boundary, (b) the numerical aperture, and (c) the acceptance angle in air.

  1. The critical angle uses the ratio n2/n1: n2/n1 = 1.48/1.50 = 0.9867.
  2. θc = sin−1(0.9867). In degrees, θc = 80.6°. The ray must hit the boundary above this angle.
  3. Square the indices: n1² = 1.50² = 2.2500 and n2² = 1.48² = 2.1904.
  4. Subtract: n1² − n2² = 2.2500 − 2.1904 = 0.0596.
  5. NA = √0.0596 = 0.2441, so NA = 0.244.
  6. θa = sin−1(0.2441) = 14.1°.

Answer. The critical angle is 80.6°, the numerical aperture is 0.244, and the acceptance angle in air is 14.1°.

Exam traps

  • For the critical angle, use θc = sin⁻¹(n2/n1), not n1/n2. Swapping the indices makes the ratio n1/n2 greater than 1, and sin⁻¹ of a value above 1 does not exist — the formula then wrongly predicts a critical angle where total internal reflection cannot happen.
  • NA is √(n1² − n2²), not n1 − n2. The square root of a difference of squares is not the difference of the indices.
  • The acceptance angle is the half-angle of the cone in air. Do not call 2θa the acceptance angle unless the question asks for the full cone angle.
  • NA has no unit. A value like 0.24 is a ratio, not 0.24°.
  • The buffer and jacket do not guide light. Only the core and cladding set the critical angle and NA.

5.8 Types of fibres: step-index, graded-index, single and multimode

An optical fibre is classified by how its refractive index changes across the core and by how many light paths it can carry. A step-index fibre changes index abruptly at the core–cladding boundary. A graded-index fibre changes it smoothly, so rays bend inside the core. A single-mode fibre has a tiny core and carries one mode; a multimode fibre has a wider core and carries many. These choices set dispersion and bandwidth in links such as campus networks and cable TV. Only a few universities teach this classification in detail.

You need: 5.7 core, cladding and numerical aperture.

Step-index and graded-index profiles

In a step-index fibre the core has refractive index n1 and the cladding has lower index n2. The change at core radius a is abrupt. Light is guided by total internal reflection at this boundary. A wide core allows many ray angles, called modes. Different modes take different path lengths, so they arrive at different times. This spreading is modal dispersion; it limits the bit rate.

A graded-index fibre has no sharp jump. Its index is highest on the axis and falls smoothly to n2 at radius a. A ray away from the axis bends back through lower-index glass, so its path curves. A longer curved path lies mostly in lower-index glass, where light travels faster. Arrival times become closer and modal dispersion falls. The profile is harder to manufacture.

Single-mode, multimode and the V number

Core size sets the mode count. A single-mode fibre has a core of a few micrometres. It carries one mode, has almost no modal dispersion, and gives high bandwidth over long links. A multimode fibre has a core of tens of micrometres. It carries many modes and is easier to connect, but modal dispersion limits its reach. Attenuation and link design are in 5.9.

V = (2πa/λ)√(n1² − n2²)

V is the normalized frequency; a is core radius in μm; λ is vacuum wavelength in μm; n1 is core index; n2 is cladding index. NA = √(n1² − n2²). Single mode needs V < 2.405. For step-index multimode, M ≈ V²/2, where M is the approximate mode count.

Using diameter instead of radius in V doubles V and multiplies M by four. A graded-index multimode fibre may need a different mode-count formula, but V < 2.405 is still the single-mode test.

5.9 Attenuation, fibre optic communication and fibre sensors

Attenuation is the loss of light power as it travels through a fibre. It is measured in decibels per kilometre, and it fixes how far a signal can go before a repeater must restore it. The same fibre also works as a sensor: temperature, strain, pressure or rotation change the light, and a detector turns that change into a measurement. This chapter connects loss budgets, the blocks of a fibre-optic link, and a few fibre-sensor examples.

You need: 5.7 for total internal reflection and fibre structure, 5.8 for single and multimode fibre, and 5.5 for the laser source.

Attenuation: the loss budget

A light pulse loses power for two reasons: the glass absorbs or scatters it, and the fibre bends. The loss is called attenuation. Engineers quote it in decibels per kilometre, dB/km, because decibels turn a large power ratio into a small number. One dB is not a power; it is a ratio.

α = (10 / L) log<sub>10</sub>(P<sub>in</sub> / P<sub>out</sub>)

α is attenuation in dB/km; L is the fibre length in km; Pin is the input optical power and Pout is the output optical power, both in the same unit (W or mW).

A loss of 3 dB means half the power is gone. A loss of 10 dB means one tenth remains. Attenuation is positive when Pout is less than Pin. The total loss in dB adds along the link, so a 10 km fibre with 0.2 dB/km loses 2 dB.

  • Absorption: impurities such as OH groups and metal ions take energy from the light and turn it into heat.
  • Rayleigh scattering: tiny density fluctuations in the glass scatter light in all directions. It grows at shorter wavelength, roughly as 1/λ⁴.
  • Bending loss: a sharp macro-bend or a small micro-bend lets light escape from the core instead of reflecting.

Silica fibre has low-loss windows near 1310 nm and 1550 nm. Typical single-mode values are about 0.35 dB/km at 1310 nm and 0.2 dB/km at 1550 nm. Attenuation sets repeater spacing: the receiver needs a minimum power to detect bits reliably.

A fibre-optic communication link

A fibre-optic link converts electrical data to light, sends the light through fibre, and converts it back. The figure shows the order of the blocks.

  1. Encoder and driver: formats the data and sets the current that drives the source.
  2. Source: an LED or a semiconductor laser. The LED is cheap and spreads light into many modes; the laser gives narrow, coherent light for long single-mode links.
  3. Optical fibre: carries the light by total internal reflection.
  4. Photodetector: a p-i-n photodiode or avalanche photodiode turns light into current.
  5. Amplifier and receiver: amplify the small current, filter it, and decide whether each bit is 0 or 1.

A repeater is a receiver plus a transmitter: it detects the weak, distorted signal, reshapes it, and sends a fresh pulse. An optical amplifier can boost light directly without converting to electricity. Repeater spacing is chosen from the loss budget. If α = 0.25 dB/km and the link can afford 25 dB, the spacing is 100 km, before margin for connectors and ageing.

Fibre sensors: measuring with light

A fibre sensor uses a fibre to measure a physical quantity. The quantity changes some property of the guided light: intensity, phase, wavelength or polarization. A detector and signal processor compare the light with a reference and output the measurement.

  • Intensity sensor: a microbend or displacement changes how much light leaks out; the received power gives pressure or position.
  • Fibre Bragg grating: a periodic index pattern reflects one wavelength. Strain or temperature stretches the grating and shifts that wavelength; the shift is the reading.
  • Interferometric sensor: phase changes between two paths measure acoustic pressure or tiny strain. A Sagnac loop measures rotation.

Fibre sensors are immune to electrical interference because the sensing signal is light. They do not create sparks, so they suit fuel stores and high-voltage areas. They are small and can be placed far from the electronics. Their limits are temperature cross-sensitivity and the cost of the optoelectronic readout.

Worked example

Question. A 2.0 km long optical fibre has an input power of 1.0 mW and an output power of 0.50 mW. Calculate the total loss in dB and the attenuation in dB/km.

  1. Identify the data: Pin = 1.0 mW, Pout = 0.50 mW, L = 2.0 km.
  2. Form the power ratio: Pin / Pout = 1.0 / 0.50 = 2.0. The ratio is dimensionless.
  3. Take the base-10 logarithm: log10(2.0) = 0.3010.
  4. Find the total loss: 10 × 0.3010 = 3.01 dB.
  5. Divide by the length in km: α = 3.01 dB / 2.0 km = 1.505 dB/km. To two significant figures, α = 1.5 dB/km.

Answer. The total loss is 3.01 dB and the attenuation is 1.5 dB/km (about 1.505 dB/km before rounding).

Exam traps

  • Attenuation uses log10, not ln. A natural-log answer is about 2.303 times larger.
  • Divide the total loss in dB by the length in km. A total loss is not dB/km.
  • A dB is a ratio, not a power. Do not add dB directly to mW.
  • Use both powers in the same unit. mW/mW is fine; mW/W is not.
  • Bending loss and Rayleigh scattering are both losses, but only Rayleigh scattering has a strong wavelength dependence.

Check yourself

Remember Define attenuation of an optical fibre and give its unit. 2 marks

Attenuation is the optical power loss per unit length, usually in dB/km. It is α = (10/L) log10(Pin/Pout), with L in km.

Understand Why does Rayleigh scattering become stronger at shorter wavelengths? 3 marks

Rayleigh scattering comes from density fluctuations smaller than the wavelength. Its coefficient scales roughly as 1/λ⁴, so blue or shorter light scatters more than infrared light.

Apply A 5.0 km fibre has Pin = 2.0 mW and Pout = 0.50 mW. Find the total loss in dB and α in dB/km. 4 marks

Analyze A 40 km link uses fibre with α = 0.25 dB/km. Input power is 1.0 mW. Find the output power in mW, ignoring connectors. 5 marks

Total loss = 0.25 × 40 = 10 dB. 10 log10(Pin/Pout) = 10, so Pin/Pout = 10. Pout = 1.0/10 = 0.10 mW.

Understand What does a repeater do in a long fibre link? 3 marks

It detects the weakened signal, reshapes and retimes it, and transmits a fresh optical pulse, so the next fibre section starts with enough power and low distortion.

Analyze Why is a laser preferred over an LED for a long single-mode link? 4 marks

A laser launches narrow, coherent light that couples well into a small single-mode core and has low chromatic dispersion; an LED spreads into many modes and couples poorly, so it suits short multimode links.

Remember Name three loss mechanisms in an optical fibre. 2 marks

Absorption by impurities, Rayleigh scattering, and bending loss (macro-bend or micro-bend).

Understand Why are fibre sensors useful near high-voltage equipment? 3 marks

The sensing signal is light in a dielectric fibre, so there is no electrical path, no electromagnetic pickup, and no spark risk.

Takeaways

  1. An atom can give out light on its own (spontaneous) or be forced by a passing photon (stimulated); only stimulated emission gives amplification.
  2. Einstein's A and B coefficients tie absorption, spontaneous emission and stimulated emission together, and give A₂₁/B₂₁ = 8πhν³/c³.
  3. Laser action needs population inversion, a gain medium, pumping and an optical cavity with two mirrors.
  4. Laser light is bright, coherent, directional and nearly monochromatic because of stimulated emission inside a resonator.
  5. Ruby is a three-level pulsed laser; He-Ne is a four-level gas laser that runs continuously by resonant transfer from helium to neon.
  6. The gain medium decides the wavelength: Nd:YAG gives 1064 nm, CO₂ gives 10.6 μm, dye lasers can be tuned widely.
  7. A semiconductor laser uses injected carriers, a direct band gap and cleaved facets, so it is tiny, efficient and cheap.
  8. An optical fibre guides light by total internal reflection in a core of higher refractive index than the cladding.
  9. Refractive-index profile and core size decide step versus graded and single-mode versus multimode operation.
  10. Attenuation and dispersion fix repeater spacing, and the same fibre can act as a sensor when light reacts to strain, temperature or current.

Glossary

Active medium
The material (solid, gas, liquid or semiconductor) that provides gain in a laser.
Attenuation
Loss of optical power along a fibre, quoted in decibels per kilometre.
Brightness
Power emitted per unit area per unit solid angle; lasers give very high values.
Buffer
A plastic coating over the cladding that protects the fibre from mechanical damage.
Cladding
The outer glass layer of lower refractive index that keeps light inside the core.
Coherence length
The distance over which a light wave keeps a predictable phase relation with itself.
Continuous wave
Laser output that remains on at a steady power.
Core
The central light-carrying region of a fibre, with refractive index higher than the cladding.
Direct band gap
A band gap in which electrons and holes recombine mainly by emitting photons.
Directionality
The property that laser light travels in a narrow beam with small divergence.
Four-level laser
A laser whose lower lasing level is empty at room temperature, so the threshold pump power is low.
Graded-index fibre
A fibre in which the refractive index decreases smoothly across the core.
Jacket
The outermost protective cable sheath holding one or more buffered fibres.
Laser diode
A p-n junction laser in which injected carriers recombine and the cleaved ends form the cavity.
Meridional ray
A ray that passes through the fibre axis after each reflection at the core-cladding boundary.
Metastable state
An excited state with an unusually long lifetime, used to store atoms before lasing.
Monochromaticity
Narrow spread of wavelengths in the output, measured by Δλ or Δν.
Numerical aperture
The sine of the largest acceptance angle, giving the light-gathering ability of a fibre.
Optical fibre
A thin glass or plastic thread that guides light along its core by total internal reflection.
Optical resonator
A pair of mirrors that reflects light through the gain medium many times and selects the lasing modes.
Population inversion
A condition where more atoms sit in the upper laser level than in the lower one.
Pulsed laser
A laser that delivers energy in short bursts rather than continuously.
Pumping
The process of supplying energy to create and maintain population inversion.
Spontaneous emission
Random emission of a photon when an excited atom falls to a lower level without any external light.
Step-index fibre
A fibre in which the refractive index changes abruptly from core to cladding.
Stimulated emission
Emission forced by an incoming photon, giving a second photon identical in phase, direction and wavelength.
