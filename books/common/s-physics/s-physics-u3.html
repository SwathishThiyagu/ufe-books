3.1 Vector calculus: gradient, divergence and curl theorems

Vector calculus turns a field into local numbers. A scalar field has a slope at each point; a vector field has a source strength and a circulation at each point. The del operator ∇ makes these three numbers: gradient, divergence, curl. Two theorems then connect the local numbers to integrals over lines, surfaces and volumes. Engineers use them to pass between a field inside a region and its values on the boundary, in electrostatics, magnetostatics and Maxwell's equations. This chapter builds the tools only; the physical laws come later.

You need: Unit 1 vector algebra (dot and cross products) and partial derivatives from first-semester mathematics.

Fields, gradient and the del operator

A scalar field assigns one number to each point, such as temperature T(x,y,z). A vector field assigns a vector, such as electric field E(x,y,z). The del operator is ∇ = (∂/∂x, ∂/∂y, ∂/∂z). It is a recipe that differentiates what follows: acting on a scalar φ gives a vector; dotting with a vector F gives a scalar; crossing with F gives a vector.

∇φ = (∂φ/∂x, ∂φ/∂y, ∂φ/∂z)

φ is a scalar field; ∇φ points towards the steepest rise of φ. Its magnitude is the rise per metre in that direction. If φ is potential in volts, ∇φ is in V/m.

The gradient also gives the fundamental theorem of gradients: the line integral of ∇φ along a path depends only on the endpoints. This is why a conservative force can be written as minus the gradient of a potential energy.

∫_a^b (∇φ)·dl = φ(b) − φ(a)

a and b are the endpoints; dl is the vector path element; the integral is along any path from a to b.

A line integral ∫_C F·dl adds the tangential part of F along curve C. A surface integral ∫_S F·dS adds the normal part through surface S; dS is the vector area element. A volume integral ∫_V ρ dτ adds over volume V; dτ = dx dy dz is the volume element. These three integrals appear in both theorems below.

Divergence and the divergence theorem

Divergence measures net outflow from a tiny volume. For F = F_x i + F_y j + F_z k, the formula below gives a scalar. Positive divergence means a source; negative means a sink; zero means whatever enters leaves.

∇·F = ∂F_x/∂x + ∂F_y/∂y + ∂F_z/∂z

F_x, F_y, F_z are the components of F along i, j, k; each derivative is taken at the point.

The divergence theorem, called Gauss's theorem in some syllabi, connects flux through a closed surface to sources inside. S is a closed surface bounding V. dS points outward, normal to S. dτ is the volume element. The left side is total outward flux; the right side adds source strength inside. The surface must be closed; an open sheet has no inside volume.

∮_S F·dS = ∫_V (∇·F) dτ

S is a closed surface bounding volume V; dS is the outward vector area element; dτ is the volume element; F has continuous partial derivatives.

With F = E and ∇·E = ρ/ε₀, it becomes Gauss's law for electricity (see Gauss's law). It converts a hard surface integral into a volume integral, or the reverse.

Curl, Stokes' theorem and identities

Curl measures circulation per unit area. The determinant form below gives a vector. Direction follows the right-hand rule: curl fingers along the small loop, thumb points along curl.

∇×F = (∂F_z/∂y − ∂F_y/∂z, ∂F_x/∂z − ∂F_z/∂x, ∂F_y/∂x − ∂F_x/∂y)

F = F_x i + F_y j + F_z k; each component is a function of x, y, z. The order of terms in each component matters.

Stokes' theorem, called the curl theorem, connects circulation around a loop to curl through any cap. C is a closed loop, S is any open surface capped by C, dS direction follows the right-hand rule from C, and dl is the line element. The left side is circulation; the right side adds curl through the cap.

∮_C F·dl = ∫_S (∇×F)·dS

C is a closed curve bounding open surface S; dl is the line element along C; dS is the vector area element of S; the normal is fixed by the right-hand rule.

Two identities follow: ∇×(∇φ) = 0 and ∇·(∇×F) = 0. The first says a gradient field has no circulation, so it is conservative. The second says a curl field has no sources. The Laplacian is ∇²φ = ∇·(∇φ) = ∂²φ/∂x² + ∂²φ/∂y² + ∂²φ/∂z². It appears in Poisson's equation (see electric potential). For a point charge q at r₀, some courses write ρ = q δ³(r − r₀), where δ³ is the three-dimensional Dirac delta; only a few universities teach this.

Worked example

Question. For the scalar field φ = x²yz and the vector field F = x²y i + yz j + xz k, evaluate at the point P(1,2,3): (a) ∇φ, (b) ∇·F, (c) ∇×F.

  1. Write the point coordinates: x = 1, y = 2, z = 3.
  2. Gradient: ∂φ/∂x = 2xyz = 2(1)(2)(3) = 12; ∂φ/∂y = x²z = (1)²(3) = 3; ∂φ/∂z = x²y = (1)²(2) = 2. So ∇φ = (12, 3, 2).
  3. Divergence: ∂F_x/∂x = 2xy = 2(1)(2) = 4; ∂F_y/∂y = z = 3; ∂F_z/∂z = x = 1. Sum = 4 + 3 + 1 = 8.
  4. Curl: x-component = ∂F_z/∂y − ∂F_y/∂z = 0 − y = −2; y-component = ∂F_x/∂z − ∂F_z/∂x = 0 − z = −3; z-component = ∂F_y/∂x − ∂F_x/∂y = 0 − x² = −1. So ∇×F = (−2, −3, −1).

Answer. At P(1,2,3), ∇φ = (12, 3, 2), ∇·F = 8, and ∇×F = (−2, −3, −1).

Exam traps

  • Divergence gives a scalar; curl gives a vector. Writing ∇·F as a vector or ∇×F as a scalar loses marks.
  • The divergence theorem needs a closed surface with an outward normal. An open surface belongs in Stokes' theorem.
  • In the curl determinant, the x-component is ∂F_z/∂y − ∂F_y/∂z, not the reverse. A sign swap changes the answer.
  • ∇·F = 0 does not mean F = 0; it means no sources or sinks. A uniform field has zero divergence and can still be large.
  • Gradient ∇φ points uphill, toward increasing φ. The electric field is E = −∇V, so E points downhill; the minus sign is an examiner's favourite.

3.2 Electrostatics: Coulomb's law and electric field

Electric charge is the property that makes two objects push or pull without touching. Coulomb's law gives that push or pull between two point charges: it falls as the square of the distance and points along the line joining them. Every electric field is this law added up over all charges. Engineers use it to find fields near power lines, inside capacitors, and around charged insulators before a circuit is designed.

You need vector addition from Unit 1 and the gradient and line integrals from Chapter 3.1.

Coulomb's law: force between two point charges

A point charge is a charge whose size is ignored; q₁ and q₂ are its values in coulombs (C), and r is the distance between them in metres (m). The force acts along the line joining the charges. Equal signs repel; opposite signs attract.

F = (1/(4πε₀)) |q₁q₂| / r²

F is force in newtons (N); q₁, q₂ are charges in coulombs (C); r is separation in metres (m); ε₀ is the permittivity of free space.

The constant 1/(4πε₀) equals 9×10⁹ N m² C⁻², and ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² C² N⁻¹ m⁻². In vector form, the force on charge 1 due to charge 2 is

F₁₂ = (1/(4πε₀)) (q₁q₂ / r²) r̂₁₂

F₁₂ is the force on charge 1 in N; r̂₁₂ is a unit vector from charge 2 to charge 1; r is the distance between them in m.

Substitute signs directly in the vector form: a negative product flips the force to point from 1 to 2. The magnitude formula removes signs; you then add the direction by hand. Use metres throughout because k is in SI units.

Superposition and the electric field

For many charges, compute each pair force and add the results as vectors. This is superposition. Do not add magnitudes unless all forces lie on one line and point the same way.

The electric field E at a point is the force per unit positive test charge placed there:

E = F / q₀

E is electric field in N/C; F is force on the test charge in N; q₀ is the test charge in C.

For one point charge Q, the field at distance r is

E = (1/(4πε₀)) Q / r² r̂

E is field in N/C; Q is source charge in C; r is distance in m; r̂ points radially outward from a positive Q.

The test charge q₀ is imagined small so it does not move the source charges. Once E is known, any charge q at that point feels F = qE.

  • Field lines start on positive charge and end on negative charge.
  • The tangent to a field line gives the direction of E at that point.
  • The number of lines through unit area shows the field strength.
  • Lines never cross, because E has one direction at a point.
  • For a point charge, lines are radial and evenly spaced in angle.

Symmetric charge distributions are easier with Gauss's law than with direct integration.

Dipole and continuous charge distributions

An electric dipole is two equal charges +q and −q separated by 2a. Its dipole moment p = q(2a) points from −q to +q.

E(axis) = (1/(4πε₀)) 2p / r³

E(axis) is the field on the dipole axis at distance r from the centre, for r much greater than a; p is dipole moment in C m.

E(equator) = (1/(4πε₀)) p / r³

E(equator) is the field on the perpendicular bisector; its direction is opposite to p.

A continuous distribution replaces q by small elements. For a line charge density λ (C/m), surface σ (C/m²), and volume ρ (C/m³), the element charges are dq = λ dl, dq = σ dA, dq = ρ dV.

E(r) = (1/(4πε₀)) ∫ (dq / R²) R̂

R is the vector from the element dq to the field point; R is its length; R̂ is its unit vector.

Write the three integrals: E = k ∫ λ dl R̂/R², E = k ∫ σ dA R̂/R², E = k ∫ ρ dV R̂/R². These are vector integrals; in Cartesian coordinates each component is integrated separately.

A point charge is the limit ρ = q δ³(r), where δ³ is the three-dimensional Dirac delta. Symmetric cases are quicker with Gauss's law.

Worked example

Question. A charge q₁ = +4 μC sits at the origin. A second charge q₂ = −3 μC sits at (0.20 m, 0). Find the force between them and the electric field at the position of q₂ due to q₁. Check that q₂E gives the same force.

  1. List data: q₁ = 4×10⁻⁶ C, q₂ = −3×10⁻⁶ C, r = 0.20 m, k = 9×10⁹ N m² C⁻².
  2. Magnitude: F = k |q₁q₂| / r² = 9×10⁹ × (4×10⁻⁶ × 3×10⁻⁶) / (0.20)².
  3. Product q₁q₂ magnitude = 12×10⁻¹² C². Divide by 0.04 m²: 3×10⁻¹⁰. Multiply by 9×10⁹: F = 2.7 N.
  4. Signs are opposite, so the force is attractive. q₂ is pulled toward the origin, that is along −x for q₂.
  5. Field due to q₁ at x = 0.20 m: E = k q₁ / r² = 9×10⁹ × 4×10⁻⁶ / 0.04 = 9×10⁵ N/C. Since q₁ is positive, E points along +x.
  6. Force from field: q₂E = (−3×10⁻⁶ C)(9×10⁵ N/C) = −2.7 N, which is 2.7 N along −x. This matches the direct force.

Answer. The force magnitude is 2.7 N, attractive toward the origin; the field due to q₁ at q₂ is 9×10⁵ N/C along +x.

Exam traps

  • Adding force magnitudes. Two 2 N forces at 60° do not give 4 N; add them as vectors.
  • Using r in centimetres with k in SI. Convert 20 cm to 0.20 m before squaring.
  • Reading μC as 10⁻³ C. μ means 10⁻⁶ C; mC is 10⁻³ C.
  • Forgetting that E due to q₁ at q₂'s position does not include q₂. The field is produced by other charges.
  • Dropping the sign in vector form and then guessing direction. Either use signs or use magnitude plus a drawn direction.

Understand Why define electric field as force per unit test charge instead of using force directly? 2 marks

Dividing by q₀ removes the test charge from the description. E then gives the source's effect per unit charge, and any charge q feels F = qE.

Apply Two charges, 2 μC and 5 μC, are 0.10 m apart in air. Find the force magnitude between them. 4 marks

F = 9×10⁹ × (2×10⁻⁶ × 5×10⁻⁶) / (0.10)² = 9×10⁹ × 10×10⁻¹² / 0.01 = 9 N. It is repulsive because both charges are positive.

Analyze Three equal positive charges sit at the corners of an equilateral triangle. What is the electric field at the centre? 4 marks

It is zero. Each field has the same magnitude and the three vectors are separated by 120°, so their vector sum is zero. Adding magnitudes would wrongly give three times one field.

At any point the electric field has one definite direction. If two field lines crossed, the field at the crossing would have two directions. So field lines cannot cross.

3.3 Gauss's law and its applications

Gauss's law: net electric flux through a closed surface depends only on charge inside. Outside charges add field but no net flux. It works because a point charge's field falls as 1/r², matching a sphere's area growth r², so flux is same at every radius. Engineers use it to get fields of lines, sheets, cables, spheres and conductors in one step.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for surface integrals and the divergence theorem, and Chapter 3.2 for a point charge's field.

Flux, and the two forms of Gauss's law

Electric flux counts field lines crossing a surface. For a closed surface, Φ = ∮ E·dS. E is field in N/C at a small patch, dS is that patch's outward area in m², and the dot product keeps only the perpendicular part. Flux is in N·m²/C.

Φ = ∮ E·dS = Q_enclosed / ε₀

Φ is net outward flux in N·m²/C; E is field in N/C; dS is outward area element in m²; Q_enclosed is net charge inside in C; ε₀ = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²) is permittivity of free space.

Net flux out of any closed surface equals enclosed charge divided by ε₀. The surface is imaginary. Outside charges give field at the surface, but their lines enter and leave, so they add no net Φ.

The divergence theorem from Chapter 3.1 gives the differential form.

∇·E = ρ / ε₀

∇·E is divergence of E in (N/C)/m; ρ is volume charge density in C/m³; ε₀ is as above.

Both forms say charge is the source of field lines. This is one of Maxwell's equations and returns in Chapter 3.8.

Line charge and sheet: pick the surface

  1. Spot the symmetry: flat, cylindrical or spherical.
  2. Draw a closed surface where E is constant and perpendicular, or parallel to the surface.
  3. Write Φ face by face: E times area, or zero.
  4. Get Q_enclosed from the charge density.
  5. Divide by ε₀ and solve for E.

Infinite line charge, density λ C/m. Take a coaxial cylinder of radius r and length L. On the curved wall E is radial and constant, so flux is E × 2πrL; on the flat caps E lies along the surface, so flux is zero. Enclosed charge is λL.

E = λ / (2π ε₀ r)

λ is charge per unit length in C/m; r is perpendicular distance from the line in m; E points radially away from a positive line.

Infinite sheet, surface charge density σ C/m². Take a pillbox of face area A crossing the sheet. Each face carries flux EA and sides carry none, so 2EA = σA/ε₀.

E = σ / (2ε₀)

σ is charge per unit area in C/m²; E is uniform, perpendicular to the sheet, points away from positive charge on both sides, and does not depend on distance.

Spherical shell and solid sphere

Thin shell of radius R carrying total charge Q. For r > R the Gaussian sphere encloses all Q, so the shell behaves like a point charge at its centre: E = kQ/r². For r < R it encloses no charge, so E = 0 inside.

E = Q / (4π ε₀ r²) = kQ / r²   (r > R);   E = 0   (r < R)

Q is total charge in C; r is distance from centre in m; R is shell radius in m; k = 1/(4πε₀) = 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C².

Solid sphere of radius R with Q uniform, so ρ = Q/((4/3)πR³). At radius r < R the Gaussian sphere encloses only Q r³/R³, so flux grows as r³ while area grows as r².

E = kQ r / R³ inside (r < R);   E = kQ / r² outside (r > R)

r is distance from centre in m; R is sphere radius in m; inside field points radially outward and is zero only at the centre.

Inside, E climbs linearly from zero to kQ/R² at the surface; outside it falls as 1/r². A shell and a solid ball with same Q and R have identical fields outside.

Conductors, and why the law matters

In electrostatic equilibrium the field inside a conductor is zero, else free electrons would drift. Any closed surface wholly inside the metal has zero flux, so encloses zero charge. All excess charge sits on the outer surface. A charge inside a cavity induces opposite charge on the inner wall, but outside sees only the outer surface — a hollow conductor shields its cavity from outside fields.

Just outside a conductor, take a pillbox with one face inside the metal (E = 0) and one just outside. Only the outer face carries flux, so EA = σA/ε₀.

E = σ / ε₀ (just outside a conductor)

σ is local surface charge density in C/m²; E is normal to the surface and points away from positive charge.

Charge collects where curvature is sharp, so σ and E are large at points. Air breaks down near 3 × 10⁶ V/m. Lightning rods are pointed so the high tip field ionises air and starts discharge. High-voltage busbars and corona rings are rounded so σ and E stay below breakdown. Gauss's law also gives the D-field form used in Chapter 3.5.

Worked example

Question. An infinite line carries uniform charge density λ = 5 μC/m. Using a cylindrical Gaussian surface, find E at r = 10 cm from the line. Take ε₀ = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² C² N⁻¹ m⁻².

  1. Symmetry: E is radial and equal on a coaxial cylinder.
  2. Gaussian surface: cylinder of radius r = 0.10 m and length L, coaxial with the line.
  3. Curved wall: dS is parallel to E, so flux is E × 2πrL.
  4. Flat caps: dS is perpendicular to E, so flux is 0 each.
  5. Total flux Φ = E × 2πrL.
  6. Enclosed charge: Q_enclosed = λL = 5 × 10⁻⁶ × L coulomb.
  7. Gauss's law: E × 2πrL = λL/ε₀. L cancels, giving E = λ/(2πε₀r).
  8. Denominator: 2π × 8.854 × 10⁻¹² × 0.10 = 5.563 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m).
  9. E = (5 × 10⁻⁶)/(5.563 × 10⁻¹²) = 8.99 × 10⁵ N/C.
  10. Direction: radially away from the line, since λ is positive.

Output

8.99

Answer. E = 8.99 × 10⁵ N/C, pointing radially away from the line.

Exam traps

  • Putting a charge outside the surface into Q_enclosed. It adds to E everywhere but gives zero net flux — zero flux never means zero field.
  • Using E = σ/ε₀ for a free thin sheet. A free sheet gives σ/2ε₀; σ/ε₀ belongs to a conductor surface, where field exists on one side only.
  • Losing the sign of Φ by dropping outward dS. Negative Q_enclosed means net inward flux.
  • Assuming E = 0 inside a uniformly charged solid sphere because it is zero inside a shell. Inside the ball E = kQr/R³, rising from zero at the centre.
  • Treating the shell as continuous at r = R. Just inside E is zero and just outside it is kQ/R²; the jump exists because the shell is taken as infinitely thin.

Remember State Gauss's law in integral form and name each symbol with its unit. 2 marks

Understand A point charge sits outside a closed surface. Is flux through the surface zero? Is field on the surface zero? 3 marks

Flux is zero: every line that enters also leaves. Field is not zero — the outside charge contributes to E at every surface point. Gauss's law constrains only net flux, not E.

Apply An infinite sheet carries σ = 2 μC/m². Find field at 5 cm and how it changes at 50 cm. 3 marks

E = σ/(2ε₀) = (2 × 10⁻⁶)/(2 × 8.854 × 10⁻¹²) = 1.13 × 10⁵ N/C. For an infinite sheet E does not depend on distance, so at 50 cm it is the same, 1.13 × 10⁵ N/C, normal to the sheet.

Apply A thin spherical shell of radius 0.2 m carries 4 μC. Find E at 0.1 m and 0.4 m from the centre. 4 marks

At r = 0.1 m < R the Gaussian sphere encloses no charge, so E = 0. At r = 0.4 m, E = kQ/r² = (9 × 10⁹)(4 × 10⁻⁶)/(0.4)² = 2.25 × 10⁵ N/C, radially outward.

Understand Why must excess charge on a conductor in equilibrium lie on its outer surface? 3 marks

Field inside a conductor at rest is zero, else charges would move. Any closed surface wholly inside the metal then has zero flux, so encloses zero charge. Charge can sit only on the boundary.

Analyze A solid sphere of radius R carries charge Q uniformly. Compare field at r = R/2 with field at r = 2R. 5 marks

Inside, E = kQr/R³, so at R/2, E = kQ/(2R²). Outside, E = kQ/r², so at 2R, E = kQ/(4R²). Ratio is 2 : 1 — twice as strong at R/2. Both point radially outward.

Analyze A cube sits in uniform field E along x-axis. What is net flux? What if charge q is at its centre? 3 marks

Uniform field: zero net flux, since lines entering one face leave the opposite face and Q_enclosed = 0. With q at centre, net flux becomes q/ε₀, shared equally by six faces — each face gets q/(6ε₀).

Understand What does ∇·E = ρ/ε₀ say at a point, and what is it in a charge-free region? 3 marks

Divergence of E at a point equals local charge density there divided by ε₀ — a measure of how strongly field lines start there. In a charge-free region ρ = 0, so ∇·E = 0 and field lines pass through without starting or ending.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Field solvers for antennas, PCBs and MEMS solve ∇·E = ρ/ε₀ on a mesh rather than summing Coulomb's law point by point. Replace charge by mass and the same law gives Gauss's law for gravity, used to model planetary interiors. High-voltage engineers shape corona rings and busbar ends so local σ stays under air's breakdown value. Search next: finite element electrostatics, corona discharge, Gauss's law for gravity.

3.4 Electric potential; Poisson's and Laplace's equations

Electric potential is the work per unit positive charge needed to bring a small test charge from a chosen reference point to a point, against the electric field. It replaces three field components with one scalar number. Engineers use it because voltage is easy to measure, and because charge fixes potential through Poisson's equation while empty regions obey Laplace's equation. Circuit nodes, conductors, and semiconductor junctions are all read through potential.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for gradient and divergence, Chapter 3.2 for the field of a point charge, and Chapter 3.3 for Gauss's law.

Potential from field, field from potential

Choose a reference point r₀ where you set V = 0. The potential at r is the work per unit positive charge done by an external agent in bringing a test charge from r₀ to r, against E. The field is conservative in electrostatics, so the path does not matter.

V(r) = −∫r₀r E·dl

V is potential in volts (J/C); E is electric field in V/m; dl is a small path step in metres; r₀ is the reference point where V = 0. The minus sign says the external agent does negative work when the charge moves along E.

For a point charge Q at the origin, with reference at infinity, V = kQ/r. Here k = 1/(4πε₀) = 8.99×10⁹ N·m²/C², Q is in coulombs, and r is in metres. A negative Q gives negative potential. Far from a dipole, V = k p cos θ / r², where p is the dipole moment in C·m and θ is the angle from the dipole axis.

E = −∇V = −(∂V/∂x i + ∂V/∂y j + ∂V/∂z k)

E is the electric field in V/m; ∇V is the gradient of V in V/m; i, j, k are unit vectors along x, y, z. The field points toward decreasing V.

An equipotential surface has one value of V. Field lines cut it at right angles, and moving a charge along it needs zero work. A conductor in electrostatics is an equipotential: free electrons move until the field inside is zero, so no potential difference remains between two points of the conductor. See Chapter 3.5 for what happens when a dielectric touches it.

Energy stored by a charge arrangement

Assemble charges one at a time. The work to bring qᵢ into the potential Vᵢ made by the charges already present is qᵢVᵢ. Summing over all charges counts each pair twice, so the total is half the sum.

W = ½ Σi qiVi

W is the electrostatic potential energy in joules; qᵢ is the i-th charge in coulombs; Vᵢ is the potential at qᵢ due to all other charges in volts. Do not include the infinite self-potential of a point charge.

For a continuous charge distribution, replace the sum by an integral over charge. The same energy can be written as an integral of the field over all space. The quantity ½ε₀E² is the energy stored per unit volume.

W = ½∫ρV dτ = ½ε₀∫E² dτ, u = ½ε₀E²

ρ is volume charge density in C/m³; V is potential in V; dτ is a small volume element in m³; ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m; E is field in V/m; u is energy density in J/m³. The integral runs over all space where the field exists.

Poisson's equation and Laplace's equation

Gauss's law in differential form is ∇·E = ρ/ε₀. Put E = −∇V. Then ∇·(−∇V) = ρ/ε₀, which gives Poisson's equation. Where there is no charge, ρ = 0, and it becomes Laplace's equation. The symbol ∇² is the Laplacian.

∇²V = ∂²V/∂x² + ∂²V/∂y² + ∂²V/∂z² = −ρ/ε₀;   ∇²V = 0 where ρ = 0

V is potential in volts; ρ is volume charge density in C/m³; ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m. Poisson's equation holds where charge sits; Laplace's equation holds only in charge-free regions.

A boundary condition is needed to pick one solution: V is continuous across any surface, including a charged one. If a surface carries charge density σ, the normal derivative of V jumps by σ/ε₀. With fixed boundary values, the solution inside a region is unique.

Two standard cases. Between infinite parallel plates at x = 0 and x = d, with V = 0 and V = V₀, Laplace in one dimension gives d²V/dx² = 0, so V = V₀x/d and E = −(V₀/d)i, uniform. Outside a conducting sphere of radius R carrying charge Q, V = kQ/r for r > R, the same as a point charge; inside, V = kQ/R is constant and E = 0. V is continuous at r = R.

Images and multipoles (not in every syllabus)

The method of images replaces a conducting boundary by a fictitious charge placed behind it. A charge q in front of a grounded conducting plane is equivalent, in the real region, to q plus an image −q at the mirror position. The image is not a real charge; it is a bookkeeping device that makes the boundary potential zero. It gives the force on q and the induced surface charge without solving Laplace's equation directly.

A multipole expansion writes the potential far from a charge cloud as a series. The first term is the monopole kQ/r. The next is the dipole k p cos θ / r², then the quadrupole falling as 1/r³. The first nonzero term dominates far away. This explains why a neutral molecule can still attract another charge, and it underlies antenna radiation patterns. Only a few universities develop these two tools in detail.

Worked example

Question. The potential in a region is V = x²y + yz volts, with x, y, z in metres. Find the electric field E and the volume charge density ρ at the point (1, 2, 3). Take ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m.

  1. Write the gradient components: ∂V/∂x = 2xy, ∂V/∂y = x² + z, ∂V/∂z = y.
  2. At (1, 2, 3): ∂V/∂x = 2(1)(2) = 4 V/m; ∂V/∂y = 1² + 3 = 4 V/m; ∂V/∂z = 2 V/m.
  3. Use E = −∇V. So E = (−4, −4, −2) V/m.
  4. Compute the Laplacian: ∇²V = ∂²V/∂x² + ∂²V/∂y² + ∂²V/∂z² = 2y + 0 + 0 = 4 V/m² at y = 2.
  5. Use Poisson's equation: ρ = −ε₀∇²V = −(8.854×10⁻¹²)(4) = −3.5416×10⁻¹¹ C/m³.

Answer. At (1, 2, 3), E = (−4 i − 4 j − 2 k) V/m and ρ = −3.5416×10⁻¹¹ C/m³.

Exam traps

  • Dropping the minus sign in E = −∇V: if V increases along +x, E points along −x.
  • Using Laplace's equation where charge is present. Laplace holds only for ρ = 0; use Poisson's equation when ρ is nonzero.
  • Forgetting the ½ in W = ½ΣqᵢVᵢ. Adding qᵢVᵢ for every charge counts each pair twice.
  • Thinking V = 0 at a point means E = 0 there. Field is the slope of V, not its value.
  • Mixing reference points. A point-charge potential kQ/r assumes V = 0 at infinity; only potential differences are physical.

3.5 Dielectrics, electric polarization and capacitors

A dielectric is an insulator with no free charge to give. Put it between charged plates and its molecules stretch or turn into tiny dipoles. That polarization leaves bound charge on the faces, weakens the field inside by the factor ε_r, and raises the capacitance by the same factor. Ceramic capacitors in a phone charger and plastic film capacitors in a fan regulator both work this way.

You need Chapter 3.3 for Gauss's law, and Chapter 3.4 for potential and stored energy.

Polar and nonpolar molecules

A dielectric is an insulator. Its electrons stay bound to their molecules, so it cannot supply charge to a circuit. It can only shift charge a little within each molecule, and that shift changes the field.

A nonpolar molecule has its centre of positive charge on top of its centre of negative charge, so it has no dipole moment of its own. Hydrogen, carbon dioxide and methane are examples. An applied field pulls the electrons one way and the nuclei the other, creating an induced dipole. Its moment is p = αE, where p is the dipole moment in C·m, α is the molecular polarizability in C·m²/V, and E is the field in V/m. Remove the field and the dipole disappears.

A polar molecule already has its charge centres apart. Water has a permanent dipole moment of 1.85 D, where 1 debye = 3.336×10⁻³⁰ C·m. With no field, thermal motion points the molecules in all directions and the net moment is zero. An applied field exerts a torque and turns them partly into line. Alignment is never complete, because thermal agitation keeps knocking them back.

One vector describes both effects. Polarization P is the total dipole moment per unit volume, in C/m². If there are N molecules per cubic metre each with average moment p, then P = Np. In a small field P = ε₀χ_eE, where χ_e is the electric susceptibility, a pure number, and ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m.

Bound charge and the displacement D

Polarization pushes charge to the surfaces. At a face where the dipoles point outwards, their positive ends stick out. The bound surface charge density there is σ_b = P·n̂, where n̂ is the outward unit normal and σ_b is in C/m². If P varies from place to place, charge also piles up inside with density ρ_b = −∇·P. This bound charge cannot be drained with a wire; it exists only while P exists.

Take two large plates carrying free charge density +σ and −σ. In vacuum the field is E₀ = σ/ε₀. Slide a slab between them and the bound charge on its faces opposes the plates. The field inside becomes E = (σ − σ_b)/ε₀, which works out to E = E₀/ε_r. The dielectric always weakens the field, never reverses it.

D = ε₀E + P, ∮D·dA = Q_free, ∇·D = ρ_free

D is the electric displacement in C/m²; E is the field in V/m; P is the polarization in C/m²; Q_free is the charge you put on the plates, ignoring bound charge; ρ_free is the free volume charge density in C/m³.

D is bookkeeping. Its flux out of a closed surface counts only free charge, so for a given plate charge D is the same with or without the slab. For a linear dielectric, D = ε₀(1 + χ_e)E = ε₀ε_rE, with ε_r = 1 + χ_e the relative permittivity. The older name dielectric constant, K, means the same number. Vacuum has χ_e = 0 and ε_r = 1.

Capacitors and the two battery cases

A capacitor is two conductors holding charges +Q and −Q at a potential difference V. Its capacitance is C = Q/V, in farad, where 1 F = 1 C/V. For parallel plates of area A and separation d with vacuum between, C₀ = ε₀A/d. Fill the gap with a dielectric of relative permittivity ε_r and C = ε₀ε_rA/d = ε_rC₀.

Capacitors in series share the same Q and add voltages: 1/C = 1/C₁ + 1/C₂ + …. Capacitors in parallel share the same V and add charge: C = C₁ + C₂ + ….

U = ½CV² = Q²/2C = ½QV, u = ½ε₀ε_rE²

U is the stored energy in joule; C is the capacitance in farad; V is the potential difference in volt; Q is the charge in coulomb; u is the energy density in J/m³; E is the field in the dielectric in V/m.

Now the two cases. With the battery still connected, V is fixed. Inserting the slab raises C by ε_r, so Q = CV rises by ε_r, E = V/d stays the same, and U rises by ε_r; the battery supplies the extra charge. With the battery removed, Q is fixed. C rises by ε_r, so V = Q/C falls to V₀/ε_r, E falls to E₀/ε_r, and U falls to U₀/ε_r. The lost energy went into pulling the slab into the gap — the field does mechanical work on it.

Every dielectric breaks down above some field. That limit is the dielectric strength, in V/m: about 3×10⁶ V/m for air at ordinary pressure, and several times more for mica or ceramic. Breakdown ionizes a path and a spark discharges the capacitor, so the plate separation must satisfy V < E_strength × d.

Worked example

Question. A parallel plate capacitor has plate area A = 0.02 m² and separation d = 1 mm. Mica of relative permittivity ε_r = 5 fills the gap, and a battery keeps V = 100 V across the plates. Find (a) the vacuum capacitance C₀, (b) the capacitance C with mica, (c) the free charge Q on each plate, (d) the field E in the mica, (e) the stored energy U, and (f) the bound surface charge density σ_b.

  1. Data: ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m, A = 0.02 m², d = 1×10⁻³ m, ε_r = 5, V = 100 V.
  2. Vacuum capacitance: C₀ = ε₀A/d = (8.854×10⁻¹² × 0.02)/(1×10⁻³) = 1.77×10⁻¹⁰ F = 177 pF.
  3. With mica: C = ε_rC₀ = 5 × 1.77×10⁻¹⁰ = 8.85×10⁻¹⁰ F = 885 pF.
  4. Battery connected, so V is held at 100 V: Q = CV = 8.85×10⁻¹⁰ × 100 = 8.85×10⁻⁸ C.
  5. Field: E = V/d = 100/(1×10⁻³) = 1.0×10⁵ V/m. Check with free charge: σ_free = Q/A = 8.85×10⁻⁸/0.02 = 4.43×10⁻⁶ C/m², and E = σ_free/(ε₀ε_r) = 4.43×10⁻⁶/(8.854×10⁻¹² × 5) = 1.0×10⁵ V/m.
  6. Energy: U = ½CV² = 0.5 × 8.85×10⁻¹⁰ × (100)² = 4.43×10⁻⁶ J.
  7. Bound charge: σ_b = σ_free(1 − 1/ε_r) = 4.43×10⁻⁶ × (1 − 0.2) = 3.54×10⁻⁶ C/m², of opposite sign to the free charge on each face.

Answer. C₀ = 177 pF, C = 885 pF, Q = 8.85×10⁻⁸ C, E = 1.0×10⁵ V/m, U = 4.43×10⁻⁶ J, and σ_b = 3.54×10⁻⁶ C/m².

Exam traps

  • Mixing up the two battery cases. With the battery connected V and E stay fixed and Q grows by ε_r; with the battery removed Q stays fixed and E and V fall to 1/ε_r.
  • Treating D and E as the same thing. D = ε₀ε_rE depends only on free charge for a given geometry; E is weakened by the bound charge. Their units differ too: C/m² against V/m.
  • Getting the sign of σ_b wrong. The bound charge is positive on the face where P points out of the dielectric, so it opposes the free charge on the plate it touches.
  • Using C = ε_rC₀ when the slab fills only part of the gap. That formula needs the whole gap filled; a partial slab is a dielectric and an air capacitor in series.

3.6 Magnetostatics: Biot-Savart law and Ampere's law

Magnetostatics is the study of magnetic fields produced by steady currents. A current-carrying wire creates a field around it, and the Biot–Savart law gives the field of every small element of that current. For highly symmetric currents, Ampere's law turns the same physics into one line of arithmetic. These rules size transformers, MRI coils and the magnetic sensors in a phone. This chapter builds both laws, applies them to a straight wire, a loop, a solenoid and a toroid, and ends with the H field used inside magnetic materials.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for curl and Stokes' theorem, and Chapter 3.5 for the D, P, χe pattern that M, H and μr repeat.

The Biot–Savart law

A steady current I is a stream of charge. Cut the wire into short vector elements I dl: dl points along the current and has length dl. Each element adds a small field dB at a point P. The Biot–Savart law says dB = (μ₀/4π) I dl × r̂ / r². Here r is the distance from the element to P, r̂ is the unit vector from the element to P, and μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T m A⁻¹ is the permeability of free space. The cross product gives direction: point fingers along dl, curl them towards r̂, and the thumb gives dB. Superposition adds dB over the whole wire. For an infinitely long straight wire the integral gives B = μ₀I/(2πr), with circular field lines around the wire.

dB = (μ₀/4π) I dl × r̂ / r²

dB is the field of the element in T; I is current in A; dl is the element vector in m; r̂ points from the element to the field point; r is distance in m; μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T m A⁻¹.

Ampere's law and symmetric currents

Ampere's law is the magnetic cousin of Gauss's law. Take a closed path and add B·dl along it. The result is μ₀ times the net current threading the path: ∮B·dl = μ₀I_enclosed. The sign follows the right-hand rule: curl fingers along the path, thumb points along positive current. The law always holds, but it is useful when symmetry makes B constant along the path. For a long straight wire choose a circle of radius r: B(2πr) = μ₀I, so B = μ₀I/(2πr). For a long solenoid with n turns per metre, a rectangular path gives B = μ₀nI inside and nearly zero outside. For a toroid with N turns, a circular path inside gives B = μ₀NI/(2πr). In differential form, ∇×B = μ₀J. J is current density in A m⁻²; the curl of B at a point is μ₀ times the current density there. Stokes' theorem connects the two forms.

Loop, axis and vector potential

At the centre of a circular loop of radius R, every current element is perpendicular to r̂, and the integral gives B = μ₀I/(2R). On the axis at distance z from the centre, B = μ₀IR²/[2(R²+z²)3/2]. At z=0 this reduces to the centre value. Far away, z ≫ R, it falls as 1/z³. A straight wire falls only as 1/r, so the loop's field is more local; the figure shows both. The direction on the axis follows the right-hand rule: curl fingers along the current, thumb gives B. A vector potential A also exists, with B = ∇×A. For a straight wire, A is parallel to the wire. A is not unique: adding ∇χ to A leaves B unchanged. Only a few universities teach A in the first course; it becomes useful in quantum mechanics and numerical field solvers.

Magnetic materials in brief

Inside a material, the field is changed by magnetization M, the magnetic moment per unit volume, in A m⁻¹. Define H = B/μ₀ − M, so B = μ₀(H + M). For a linear material M = χmH, giving B = μ₀(1+χm)H = μ₀μrH. Here χm is magnetic susceptibility, dimensionless, and μr = 1+χm is relative permeability. In free space M=0 and μr=1. An iron core in a solenoid raises B by about μr, which is why electromagnets use iron. The pattern repeats D = ε₀E + P from Chapter 3.5. Dia, para and ferromagnetism, domains and hysteresis belong to Unit 7, Magnetic materials. We only need H and μr here.

Worked example

Question. A long straight wire and a circular loop of radius 5 cm both carry 2 A. Find the field 10 cm from the straight wire and at the centre of the loop. Take μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T m A⁻¹.

  1. Write the data: I = 2 A, r = 0.10 m, R = 0.05 m, μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T m A⁻¹.
  2. For the straight wire, B = μ₀I/(2πr). Substitute: B = (4π×10⁻⁷ × 2)/(2π × 0.10).
  3. Cancel π, giving (4×10⁻⁷ × 2)/(2 × 0.10). Cancel the twos: B = 4×10⁻⁷/0.10 = 4×10⁻⁶ T = 4 μT.
  4. For the loop centre, B = μ₀I/(2R). Substitute: B = (4π×10⁻⁷ × 2)/(2 × 0.05).
  5. Numerator 8π×10⁻⁷; denominator 0.10. B = 80π×10⁻⁷ = 8π×10⁻⁶ T ≈ 2.51×10⁻⁵ T = 25.1 μT.

Answer. The straight wire gives 4 μT at 10 cm, and the loop centre gives about 25.1 μT.

Exam traps

  • dl × r̂ order: r̂ points from the element to the field point. The field dB lies along dl × r̂, perpendicular to both; r̂ × dl reverses that direction.
  • Ampere's law uses I_enclosed, not the total current in the circuit. A current outside the loop contributes zero net threading.
  • The simple Ampere law needs steady currents and high symmetry; for a finite wire or a loop it does not give B in one line.
  • Units: μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ T m A⁻¹, so r must be in metres. A 10 cm distance is 0.10 m, not 10.
  • Field at the centre of a loop is μ₀I/(2R), not μ₀I/(2πR); the 2π belongs to the straight wire.

3.7 Faraday's law, motional emf and inductance

A steady magnetic field does nothing to a stationary charge. Change the flux through a loop, or slide a wire across field lines, and a voltage appears — that is Faraday's law. Read the same law backwards and a coil resists any change in its own current, which is inductance. Generators, transformers, scooter ignition coils and the choke in a tube light all run on this one idea.

You need Chapter 3.6 for the magnetic field of currents and Chapter 3.1 for curl and Stokes' theorem.

Magnetic flux and Faraday's law

Magnetic flux Φ is the amount of magnetic field passing through a surface. Count only the part perpendicular to the surface.

Φ = ∫ B·dS

Φ is the flux in weber (Wb); B the magnetic field in tesla (T); dS a small area element with its outward normal, in m². For flat area A with uniform B perpendicular to it, Φ = BA, and 1 Wb = 1 T·m².

Faraday's law says the induced emf is the rate at which flux falls through the loop. A big steady field induces nothing; only change matters. A coil of N turns links the same flux N times.

ε = −N dΦ/dt

ε is the induced emf in volts; N the number of turns; Φ the flux through one turn in Wb; t the time in seconds.

In integral form, ∮E·dl = −dΦ/dt: walk once round a closed loop, add the tangential electric field, and you get the fall in flux through the loop. Shrink the loop to a point with Stokes' theorem and the same statement becomes local, ∇×E = −∂B/∂t. A changing magnetic field creates a circulating electric field even in empty space where there is no charge at all.

The minus sign is Lenz's law. The induced current makes its own field that opposes the change in flux: flux growing, induced field against B; flux falling, induced field along B. It opposes the change, never the flux itself. That is why a magnet dropped down a copper pipe sinks slowly, and why the induced current must be paid for in mechanical work.

Motional emf and eddy currents

Take a rod of length l moving at speed v in a uniform field B, with v, B and the rod mutually perpendicular. Every charge q in the rod feels a magnetic force qv×B, so charge piles up at the ends until the electric field it sets up balances the magnetic force: qE = qvB, giving E = vB.

ε = B l v

B is the field in T; l the rod length in m; v the speed in m/s, all three mutually perpendicular; ε the motional emf in volts.

The direction of v×B gives the force on positive charge, so it gives the positive terminal. Reverse v or reverse B and the emf reverses. If the rod moves parallel to B, v×B = 0 and the emf is zero however fast the rod moves.

Close the rod on rails through a resistor R and a current I = Blv/R flows. The field now pushes back on that current with F = BIl = B²l²v/R, opposite to the motion. To keep the rod going you supply mechanical power Fv = B²l²v²/R, which is exactly the electrical power I²R in the resistor. The drag is the price of the current.

A solid conductor sitting in a changing flux behaves like many shorted loops. Circulating eddy currents appear, heat the metal, and by Lenz drag the motion. Induction braking in trains and the damping of a moving-coil galvanometer use this; laminating a transformer core breaks up the loops and cuts the loss.

Inductance, energy and the LR circuit

A current in a coil makes flux through that same coil. Self-inductance is flux linkage per unit current.

L = NΦ/I

NΦ is the flux linkage in weber-turns; I the current in ampere; L the self-inductance in henry (H), with 1 H = 1 Wb/A = 1 V·s/A. For a long solenoid of n turns per metre, area A and length l: L = μ₀n²Al, with μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m.

L is fixed by the geometry and the core material, not by the current: double the current and the flux doubles with it. Two nearby coils share flux, and ε₂ = −M dI₁/dt defines the mutual inductance M in henry, the same either way round for a linear core. Transformers live on this, but that is outside this unit.

Building a current means pushing charge against the back emf, and that work is stored in the field: U = ½LI² joule. Spread over the volume, the magnetic energy density is u = B²/(2μ₀) J/m³. The energy sits in the field, not in the wire.

A circuit with source ε, resistor R and inductor L obeys ε = iR + L di/dt. Starting from zero, i = (ε/R)(1 − e^(−Rt/L)). The time constant τ = L/R is in seconds; at t = τ the current has reached 63% of its final ε/R, and by about 5τ it is effectively there. Short the source out and the current decays as i = i₀e^(−Rt/L). That dying current is what makes a spark when you break an inductive circuit.

Worked example

Question. A metal rod of length 0.2 m slides at a steady 3 m/s along two parallel rails, in a uniform magnetic field B = 0.5 T. The rod, its velocity and the field are mutually perpendicular. The rails are joined at one end by a resistor of 1.5 Ω. Find the induced emf, the current, the force needed to keep the rod moving, and check the power balance.

  2. I = ε/R = 0.3 / 1.5 = 0.2 A.
  3. Retarding force F = BIl = 0.5 × 0.2 × 0.2 = 0.02 N, opposite to v.
  4. Electrical power in the resistor: P = I²R = (0.2)² × 1.5 = 0.04 × 1.5 = 0.06 W.

Exam traps

  • Reversing Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change in flux, not the flux. A falling flux gets a current that tries to hold the flux up, not one that pushes it down.
  • Using Φ instead of dΦ/dt. A loop sitting in a strong steady field has zero emf; students often report a large one.
  • Forgetting the perpendicular condition in ε = Blv. A rod moving along B, or a loop whose plane contains B, gives zero motional emf for that orientation.
  • Saying L grows with current. Flux rises in proportion to current, so L = NΦ/I stays fixed; it is set by geometry and the core.
  • Unit slips: mH written as H, flux in gauss·cm² instead of weber, and τ = L/R left in milliseconds without conversion.

3.8 Maxwell's equations and electromagnetic boundary conditions

Maxwell's four equations are the complete bookkeeping of classical electricity and magnetism: charges make D, there are no magnetic charges, changing B makes E, and currents or changing D make H. The fourth equation exists because Ampere's law alone fails at a charging capacitor; a displacement current I_d = ε₀ dΦ_E/dt repairs it. These equations predict electromagnetic waves, so they sit under radio, mobile phones, radar, UPI card readers and every antenna.

You need: Gauss's law (3.3), D and polarization (3.5), Ampere's law (3.6) and Faraday's law (3.7).

The four equations, in two forms

Write the laws for free charge and free current in matter. The electric displacement D (C/m²) and magnetic field strength H (A/m) absorb the dielectric and magnetic response; E is electric field (V/m), B is magnetic flux density (T), ρ_free is free charge density (C/m³), and J is free current density (A/m²).

∮ D·dS = Q_free,enc; ∮ B·dS = 0; ∮ E·dl = −d/dt ∫ B·dS; ∮ H·dl = I_free,enc + d/dt ∫ D·dS

S is a closed surface for the first two and an open surface bounded by loop C for the last two; Q_free,enc and I_free,enc are free charge and free current enclosed; dS is the outward vector surface element and dl is the line element.

In differential form, ∇·D = ρ_free, ∇·B = 0, ∇×E = −∂B/∂t, ∇×H = J + ∂D/∂t. The names are Gauss for E, Gauss for B (no magnetic monopoles), Faraday, and Ampere–Maxwell.

The first and second are scalar statements about sources. The third and fourth are vector statements about circulation. Together they fix E and B everywhere once sources and boundary values are given.

The capacitor problem and displacement current

Take a capacitor charging through a wire. Choose a loop C around the wire and a surface S bounded by C. If S is the flat disc cutting the wire, Ampere's law gives ∮H·dl = I. If S is a balloon that passes through the capacitor gap, no conduction current crosses it, so the same law gives zero. The loop C is the same; the answer cannot depend on the surface.

Maxwell repaired this by adding the displacement current. The general displacement current through the chosen surface is I_d = d/dt ∫ D·dS. In vacuum D = ε₀E, so this reduces to I_d = ε₀ dΦ_E/dt with Φ_E = ∫E·dS (V·m).

I_d = d/dt ∫ D·dS

I_d is displacement current (A); D is electric displacement (C/m²); S is the chosen open surface. In vacuum D = ε₀E, so I_d = ε₀ dΦ_E/dt with Φ_E = ∫E·dS (V·m); ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m.

For the capacitor I_d = C dV/dt, where C is capacitance (F) and V is voltage (V). This equals the conduction current in the wire, so the full current I + I_d is continuous around the circuit.

The continuity equation follows: ∇·J = −∂ρ/∂t. Free charge leaving a small volume equals the fall of charge inside it. Displacement current does not mean charge jumps the gap; it is a changing electric field that produces the same magnetic effect.

Constitutive relations and boundary conditions

The four equations need links between D and E, B and H, and J and E. For a linear, isotropic medium these are D = ε₀εr E, B = μ₀μr H, J = σE. Here εr is relative permittivity (no unit), μr is relative permeability (no unit), σ is conductivity (S/m), μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m, and ε₀ is as above.

At an interface, integrate the equations over a thin pillbox or loop. Let n̂ point from medium 1 to medium 2. Then the tangential electric field is continuous, the normal D jumps by free surface charge ρ_s (C/m²), which some books call σ_f, the normal B is continuous, and the tangential H jumps by free surface current K (A/m).

QuantityBoundary conditionMeaning
E tangentialn̂ × (E₂ − E₁) = 0E_t continuous
D normaln̂ · (D₂ − D₁) = ρ_sjump equals free surface charge ρ_s
B normaln̂ · (B₂ − B₁) = 0B_n continuous
H tangentialn̂ × (H₂ − H₁) = Kjump equals free surface current

If there is no free surface charge, D_n is continuous; if there is no free surface current, H_t is continuous. These conditions let you join fields across a dielectric, a metal surface or a magnetic material without solving the whole region at once.

Why the four equations matter

The equations unify electricity, magnetism and optics. In free space J = 0 and ρ = 0, so the equations couple changing E and changing B. Taking the curl of Faraday's law and substituting Ampere–Maxwell gives a wave equation with speed c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) = 2.998×10⁸ m/s, the speed of light.

That prediction made light an electromagnetic wave. The same equations design antennas, transmission lines, microwave ovens, radar and wireless chargers. They also set the boundary conditions used in waveguides and optical fibres.

The displacement term is small at low frequency and large when fields change fast, so circuit theory works at 50 Hz while microwave design needs the full set. Maxwell's equations are four, but they contain all classical electric and magnetic facts once the medium relations are supplied. The next chapter solves the free-space wave equation. Reflection at surfaces uses the boundary conditions above and is treated in Unit 4.

3.9 Electromagnetic waves, plane waves and Poynting theorem

In empty space, with no charges and no currents, Maxwell's four equations still allow a self-sustaining disturbance: a changing electric field makes a magnetic field, which makes an electric field again, and the pattern travels forward at c = 3×10⁸ m/s. The two fields are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel, and they carry energy, momentum and pressure in a direction given by E × B. Every wireless link works on this: FM radio, Wi-Fi, a phone call on a Chennai street, radar, satellite TV, and the sunlight reaching a solar panel.

You need: Maxwell's equations and boundary conditions from Chapter 3.8, Faraday's law from Chapter 3.7, and the wave equation from Unit 2.

From Maxwell's equations to a wave equation

In empty space there is no charge and no current, so ρ = 0 and J = 0. Two equations then carry the whole story. Faraday's law says a changing magnetic field curls an electric field: ∇×E = −∂B/∂t. Ampere–Maxwell says a changing electric field curls a magnetic field: ∇×B = μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t. Each field generates the other, so a disturbance can keep itself alive with no charge to carry it.

  1. Take the curl of Faraday's law: ∇×(∇×E) = −∂(∇×B)/∂t.
  2. Put Ampere–Maxwell into the right side: ∇×(∇×E) = −μ₀ε₀ ∂²E/∂t².
  3. Use the identity ∇×(∇×E) = ∇(∇·E) − ∇²E. In free space ∇·E = 0, so the first term dies.
  4. Left with ∇²E = μ₀ε₀ ∂²E/∂t². The same steps on B give ∇²B = μ₀ε₀ ∂²B/∂t².

∇²E = μ₀ε₀ ∂²E/∂t² and c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀)

E is the electric field (V/m), ∇² is the Laplacian (second derivatives in x, y, z), t is time (s), μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m is the permeability of free space, ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m is the permittivity of free space, and c is the wave speed (m/s).

This has the same shape as the wave equation ∇²ψ = (1/v²) ∂²ψ/∂t² from Unit 2, with v = 1/√(μ₀ε₀). The numbers give c = 2.998×10⁸ m/s, the measured speed of light. Light is an electromagnetic wave.

Inside a medium, μ₀ becomes μ = μ₀μ_r and ε₀ becomes ε = ε₀ε_r, so v = 1/√(με) = c/n, where n = √(μ_rε_r) is the refractive index. For ordinary non-magnetic glass μ_r ≈ 1, so n ≈ √ε_r.

The plane wave: transverse, in phase, right-handed

The simplest solution is a plane wave: E has one value over any plane perpendicular to the direction of travel, and changes only along that direction and with time. Let the wave travel along +z.

E = E₀ sin(kz − ωt) along x,   B = B₀ sin(kz − ωt) along y

E₀ is the peak electric field (V/m), B₀ the peak magnetic field (T), k = 2π/λ is the wave number (rad/m) with λ the wavelength (m), ω = 2πf is the angular frequency (rad/s) with f the frequency (Hz), z is position (m) and t is time (s).

Three facts follow, and each one is worth a mark.

  • Transverse: ∇·E = 0 and ∇·B = 0 force both fields to lie in the wavefront plane, so neither has a component along z.
  • In phase, with fixed ratio: Faraday's law gives E₀/B₀ = c (or v in a medium). Both peak at the same instant.
  • Right-handed set: E × B points along the direction of travel. With E along x and B along y, E × B is along +z.

The phase kz − ωt stays constant when dz/dt = ω/k, so the pattern moves at the speed found above and ω = ck. In terms of frequency and wavelength, λ = c/f.

Energy flow: the Poynting vector

A wave carries energy. The electric field stores ½ε₀E² per unit volume (J/m³) and the magnetic field stores B²/(2μ₀). Since B = E/c the two are equal, so the total energy density is u = ε₀E², averaging to u_avg = ½ε₀E₀² over a cycle.

S = E × H = E × B/μ₀

S is the Poynting vector (W/m²), the energy crossing unit area per second in the direction of E × B; H = B/μ₀ is the magnetic field strength (A/m); μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m.

S swings between 0 and a peak twice per cycle. Meters and antennas respond to its average, the intensity:

I = ½ε₀cE₀² = E₀B₀/(2μ₀) = u_avg × c

I is the intensity (W/m²), E₀ the peak electric field (V/m), B₀ the peak magnetic field (T), c = 3×10⁸ m/s and u_avg the average energy density (J/m³).

Energy moving at speed c also carries momentum: the momentum density is S/c². A surface that absorbs the wave feels a pressure I/c; a perfect reflector feels 2I/c. Sunlight at 1.4 kW/m² pushes with about 4.7 μPa, small on a hand but useful on a solar sail over months.

Production by a dipole antenna and reception by a phone

A charge at rest makes a static field. A charge shaken up and down at frequency f makes fields that change at f; far from the antenna they detach and travel as a transverse wave of that same frequency. That is the oscillating dipole antenna: a straight rod with electrons sloshing between its ends. Wavelength follows from λ = c/f, and the rod radiates best when its length is about λ/2 — a half-wave dipole.

An FM station at 100 MHz radiates λ = 3 m, so its tower is a few metres tall. A phone at 1800 MHz works with λ ≈ 17 cm, so a half-wave antenna around 8 cm fits inside the handset. Reception is the reverse: the electric field of the passing wave drives electrons along the phone's antenna, making a tiny voltage at the same frequency, which the receiver amplifies, filters and decodes.

At a boundary between two media, the four boundary conditions of Chapter 3.8 decide how much of the wave reflects and how much bends; Unit 4 works out reflection and refraction of light from them.

Analyze In a plane wave the electric field is along +y and the wave travels along +z. Find the direction of B and of the energy flow, and justify it with E × B. 4 marks

E × B must point along +z. Since ŷ × (−x̂) = +ẑ, B points along −x. The Poynting vector S = E × H points along +z, the direction of travel, so energy flows the way the wave moves.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Radiation pressure is now a working technology: solar sails, optical tweezers that hold a single living cell, and laser cooling of atoms all use the momentum that light carries. In research the Poynting vector is split into spin and orbital parts to describe light carrying angular momentum, and near-field probes map energy flow around nanostructures smaller than a wavelength. Engineers designing 5G millimetre-wave links and satellite dishes solve this same wave equation with conducting boundaries added. Useful search words: optical tweezers, spin angular momentum of light, half-wave dipole radiation pattern.

Takeaways

  1. Vector calculus gives every field a local slope (gradient), a local source strength (divergence) and a local twist (curl), and the divergence and Stokes theorems turn these local numbers into integrals over volumes and surfaces.
  2. Coulomb's law plus superposition is the whole of electrostatics; Gauss's law is the same physics rewritten in a form that makes symmetric problems one-line arithmetic.
  3. Electric potential is a scalar that carries the same information as the field, and Poisson's and Laplace's equations give the field back from the charge distribution.
  4. A dielectric does not supply free charge; its molecules become dipoles, the polarization P weakens the field inside, and D = ε₀E + P keeps track of free charge alone.
  5. A steady current creates B with the right-hand rule; Ampère's law gives the field in one line whenever the current has enough symmetry.
  6. Faraday's law says a changing magnetic flux drives charges round a loop, and the same law explains why a coil opposes changes in its own current.
  7. Maxwell's four equations hold all classical electromagnetism once the displacement current is added to Ampère's law, and they predict that light is an electromagnetic wave.
  8. In empty space E and B travel together in phase, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel, at speed c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀) ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s.
  9. The Poynting vector gives the energy flow of a wave, and its average gives the intensity, which decides how much power an antenna or a cell phone receives.

Exam question bank

Syllabi stress derivations rather than statements: expect to derive Gauss's law results for standard symmetric shapes, get Poisson's equation from Gauss's law, and go all the way from Maxwell's equations to the wave equation and the Poynting theorem.

Remember and understand

Understand Define the gradient of a scalar field and the divergence and curl of a vector field. State the divergence theorem and Stokes' theorem, and explain what each side of each theorem measures. 12 marks

gradient = vector with magnitude of maximum rate of change, direction of steepest ascent; divergence = outward flux per unit volume, a scalar; curl = circulation per unit area, a vector; divergence theorem: surface integral of F over a closed surface equals volume integral of ∇·F; Stokes' theorem: line integral of F around a closed loop equals surface integral of ∇×F; meaning: sources and circulation of a field are fixed by local derivatives.

Remember State Coulomb's law in vector form for two point charges in free space. Define the electric field and state the superposition principle. 10 marks

F = q₁q₂ r̂/(4πε₀r²), with ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² F/m; like charges repel, unlike attract, force along the line of centres; E = F/q₀ in N/C; superposition: total E is the vector sum of the fields of individual charges; note the inverse-square dependence and the role of the medium (ε = ε₀ε_r).

Understand State Gauss's law in integral and differential form. Explain why it is useful for computing fields only for charge distributions with symmetry. 10 marks

∮E·dS = Q_enc/ε₀ and ∇·E = ρ/ε₀; flux depends only on enclosed charge, not on the shape of the surface; to pull E out of the integral, E must be constant in magnitude and normal to a chosen surface; hence the sphere, cylinder and plane are the standard cases; no symmetry means the integral must be done the hard way.

Understand Define electric potential and explain why it is a scalar while the electric field is a vector. Write Poisson's and Laplace's equations and state what each term means. 12 marks

V = W/q, work per unit charge from infinity; V is a scalar; E = −∇V recovers the vector field; a scalar plus one differentiation carries all three components of E; Poisson: ∇²V = −ρ/ε₀ with ρ the volume charge density in C/m³; Laplace: ∇²V = 0 where ρ = 0; helps solve problems with conductors held at fixed potentials.

Understand Define polarization P, electric displacement D and susceptibility χ_e. Explain how bound charges appear on the faces of a polarized dielectric slab. 12 marks

P = dipole moment per unit volume, C/m²; P = ε₀χ_e E and ε_r = 1 + χ_e; D = ε₀E + P, with ∇·D = ρ_free; inside the slab the dipoles cancel; at the surfaces one sign is left over; surface bound charge density σ_b = P·n̂; the bound charge creates a field opposing the applied field, reducing E inside by ε_r.

Remember State Faraday's law of induction and Lenz's law. Define self-inductance and mutual inductance and write the energy stored in an inductor. 12 marks

emf = −dΦ_B/dt, with Φ_B = ∫B·dS in Wb; Lenz's law: the induced current opposes the change in flux; motional emf = Bvl for a rod moving perpendicular to B; L = Φ_B/I in henry; emf = −L dI/dt; mutual inductance M: flux in one coil per unit current in the other; energy stored U = ½LI².

Apply

Apply A sphere of radius R carries a uniform volume charge density ρ. Using Gauss's law, find the electric field and potential at a point inside the sphere and at a point outside it. 13 marks

spherical symmetry: take a Gaussian sphere of radius r; outside (r > R): E = Q/(4πε₀r²) with Q = (4/3)πR³ρ; inside (r < R): E = ρr/(3ε₀), linear in r; potential outside: V = Q/(4πε₀r); potential inside: integrate E inward from R and get V = (ρ/6ε₀)(3R² − r²); check continuity of V at r = R.

Apply Derive the capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor when a dielectric slab of thickness t and relative permittivity ε_r fills part of the gap. Find the energy stored for a given charge and comment on the result. 12 marks

treat the filled and unfilled parts as two capacitors in series; E in the dielectric is E₀/ε_r for the same free charge; V = E₀(d − t) + (E₀/ε_r)t; C = ε₀A/[d − t + t/ε_r]; energy U = Q²/2C, so the dielectric increases stored energy at fixed Q; limiting checks: t = 0 and t = d.

Apply A coaxial cable carries a steady current I up the inner conductor and back down the outer conductor. Use Ampère's law to find B in the three regions and sketch its variation with distance. 12 marks

cylindrical symmetry: circular Amperian loops about the axis; inside the inner conductor (r < a): B = μ₀Ir/(2πa²); between the conductors (a < r < b): B = μ₀I/(2πr); outside the cable (r > c): enclosed current is zero, so B = 0; show B continuous at the conductor surfaces; note the 1/r fall in the gap and the right-hand-rule direction.

Apply A metal rod of length L slides with constant speed v on two parallel rails in a uniform magnetic field B perpendicular to the plane of the rails. Find the induced emf, the current, the force needed to keep the rod moving, and the power dissipated. 12 marks

flux Φ_B = BLx, so emf = BLv; current I = BLv/R for a circuit resistance R; Lenz's law fixes the direction: the field of the induced current opposes the motion; magnetic force on the rod = BIL = B²L²v/R, opposite to v; mechanical power = Fv = B²L²v²/R, equal to I²R; energy conservation check.

Apply Starting from Maxwell's equations in free space, derive the wave equation for E and for B, and show that the wave speed is c = 1/√(μ₀ε₀). 14 marks

take the curl of ∇×E = −∂B/∂t; use ∇×B = μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t and ∇·E = 0; use the identity ∇×(∇×E) = ∇(∇·E) − ∇²E; get ∇²E = μ₀ε₀ ∂²E/∂t², and the same for B; compare with ∇²E = (1/c²)∂²E/∂t²; insert μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ and ε₀ = 8.854×10⁻¹² to get c ≈ 3×10⁸ m/s.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Show that Ampère's law in the form ∮B·dl = μ₀I fails for a capacitor being charged. Introduce the displacement current, write the Ampère–Maxwell law, and show it is consistent with charge conservation. 13 marks

pick a loop round the wire and two surfaces bounded by it; flat surface gives μ₀I; balloon surface through the capacitor gives zero enclosed current; contradiction: the field between the plates is real and measurable; Maxwell's fix: add I_d = ε₀ dΦ_E/dt; check that ε₀ dΦ_E/dt equals the conduction current I for the capacitor; take divergence of ∇×B = μ₀J + μ₀ε₀ ∂E/∂t to recover ∇·J = −∂ρ/∂t.

Evaluate Derive the Poynting theorem from Maxwell's equations, state the meaning of each term, and use the Poynting vector to find the intensity of a plane electromagnetic wave of amplitude E₀. 14 marks

start from E·(∇×H) − H·(∇×E); substitute Maxwell's curl equations; obtain ∂u/∂t + ∇·S = −J·E, with u = ½ε₀E² + ½B²/μ₀; S = (1/μ₀)E×B is energy flow per unit area per second; plane wave: B = E/c and E, B perpendicular; average S = ½ε₀cE₀², equal to E₀B₀/(2μ₀).

Analyze Derive the boundary conditions obeyed by the normal and tangential components of E, D, B and H at an interface between two media. Use them to explain why a good conductor reflects electromagnetic waves strongly. 14 marks

pillbox across the surface: normal B is continuous, normal D jumps by σ_free; loop across the surface: tangential E is continuous, tangential H jumps by surface current; apply with E = 0 inside a good conductor; conduction current kills the transmitted wave within a skin depth; the reflected wave carries most of the energy; link this to a metal coating being an electromagnetic shield.

Glossary

Bound charge
Charge that appears on a dielectric because its dipoles line up, not because charge was added.
Breakdown voltage
The potential difference at which a dielectric suddenly conducts and sparks.
Capacitance
C = Q/V in farad, the charge a conductor pair holds per volt of potential difference.
Circulation
The line integral ∮F·dl of a field around a closed loop, measuring how much the field pushes round the loop.
Conservative field
A field whose circulation round every closed loop is zero, so it can be written as the gradient of a potential.
Displacement current
The term ε₀ dΦ_E/dt that acts like a current wherever the electric flux changes.
Eddy current
Circulating induced current in a bulk conductor placed in a changing magnetic field.
Electric dipole
A pair of equal and opposite charges a small distance apart, with moment p = qd pointing from −q to +q.
Equipotential surface
A surface on which the potential has the same value everywhere; the field is normal to it.
Flux
The integral ∫F·dS of a field over a surface, counting how much of the field passes through it.
Free charge
Charge placed on a conductor or dielectric from outside, the charge a battery supplies.
Lenz's law
The induced current flows in the direction that opposes the change of flux that produced it.
Magnetic field
The vector B, in tesla, that gives the force qv×B on a moving charge.
Magnetic flux
Φ_B = ∫B·dS over a surface, measured in weber.
Maxwell's equations
Four equations relating E, B, their sources and their time derivatives; they govern all classical electromagnetic behaviour.
Retarded time
The earlier time t − r/c at which a source must act for its effect to reach a point a distance r away now.
Right-hand rule
The rule that the field of a current circles the wire in the sense your right-hand fingers curl when the thumb points along the current.
Scalar field
A quantity with one value at each point in space, such as temperature or electric potential.
Skin depth
The distance over which a wave's amplitude falls to 1/e of its surface value inside a conductor.
Solenoid
A long coil of wire in which the magnetic field is nearly uniform inside and about zero outside.
Solid angle
The three-dimensional version of an angle, measured in steradians; the whole sphere is 4π sr.
Susceptibility
The constant χ_e in P = ε₀χ_eE; it measures how strongly a dielectric polarizes.
Vector field
A quantity with a magnitude and a direction at each point in space, such as electric field or current density.
Vector potential
The vector A with B = ∇×A, useful because ∇·B = 0 always holds.
