1.1 Newton's laws of motion and conservation laws

Every mechanics problem starts the same way: mark a boundary, add the forces crossing it, and write ΣF = dp/dt. Newton's three laws make this one recipe work for a stone and for a rocket. Forces inside a system come in equal and opposite pairs and cancel, so with no outside force the total momentum stays fixed. The same bookkeeping gives conservation of energy and of angular momentum. Orbits, braking, launches and a cricket stroke all follow from it.

You need school-level Newton's laws, vector components, and the derivative as a rate of change. Stress and strain start fresh in 1.2.

Force, inertia and the three laws

A force is a push or pull that changes a body's velocity, measured in newtons (N); 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². A body's resistance to change of velocity is inertia, measured by its mass m in kilograms.

First law: with zero net force a body stays at rest or moves with constant velocity in a straight line. It cannot be proved from anything simpler; it defines the frames where the other two laws hold.

Second law: the net force equals the rate of change of momentum. For constant mass this is ΣF = m a.

ΣF = dp/dt = m a (m constant)

ΣF is the net external force in N; p = m v is momentum in kg·m/s; v is velocity in m/s; t is time in s; a = dv/dt is acceleration in m/s².

A force acting for a short time gives an impulse: ΣF Δt = Δp. That is how a bat changes a ball's momentum in milliseconds.

Third law: if A pushes B, B pushes A with equal magnitude along the same line, opposite direction, same type of force. The two act on different bodies.

  • Momentum and force are vectors: p = m v points along v; resolve forces into components and add.
  • A 0.16 kg ball at 30 m/s carries p = 4.8 kg·m/s.

Frames of reference and Galilean transformation

An inertial frame is one where the first law holds: a free body moves in a straight line at constant speed. Ground-fixed frames are nearly inertial; a braking bus is not.

In a non-inertial frame we add a pseudo force −m A, with A the frame's acceleration. In a bus braking at 2 m/s² a 60 kg passenger feels 60 × 2 = 120 N forward.

Galilean transformation: frame S′ moves at constant velocity v along x relative to S; time is the same in both.

x′ = x − v t, u′ = u − v, t′ = t

x, u are position and velocity in S; x′, u′ the same in S′; v is the constant velocity of S′ along x, in m/s.

Velocities add like arrows. A swimmer of speed c in still water crossing a river of width d flowing at v, aiming straight across, takes d/c and drifts v d/c downstream. To land opposite, aim upstream; the crossing then takes d/√(c² − v²).

No experiment inside a smoothly moving closed train reveals its speed. This fails near light speed — see 1.7.

Conservation of momentum and the centre of mass

Split every force on a system into internal (between its parts) and external (from outside). By the third law internal forces come in equal, opposite pairs on the same line and cancel.

ΣF_ext = dP/dt, P = Σ mᵢ vᵢ

ΣF_ext is the vector sum of external forces in N; P is total momentum in kg·m/s; mᵢ, vᵢ are the mass and velocity of the i-th part.

If ΣF_ext = 0 then P is constant: conservation of linear momentum. It holds for collisions, explosions and recoil even when the internal forces are violent and unknown.

Recoil: a gun of mass M firing a bullet of mass m at speed v moves back at V = m v / M, since the total momentum started at zero.

F = u R

F is rocket thrust in N; u is the exhaust speed relative to the rocket in m/s; R = −dm/dt is the mass loss rate in kg/s.

Centre of mass: R_cm = (Σ mᵢ rᵢ)/Σ mᵢ. For a uniform rod of length L with x = 0 at one end, x_cm = (1/M)∫₀^L (M/L) x dx = L/2. It moves as if all the mass and all external forces sat there.

Energy, angular momentum and gravitation

Work along a path is W = ∫F·dr; for a constant force along the motion W = F d cos θ. The unit is the joule (J) = 1 N·m.

W_net = ½ m v² − ½ m u²

W_net is total work by all forces in J; m is mass in kg; u, v are initial and final speeds in m/s. This is the work–energy theorem.

A conservative force stores potential energy U with F = −dU/dx, and then KE + U is constant. Friction is not conservative: its work depends on the path and it turns mechanical energy into heat.

L = r × p, τ = dL/dt

L is angular momentum in kg·m²/s, magnitude m v r sin θ; r is the position vector from the origin in m; τ is torque in N·m. If Στ = 0, L stays constant.

A planet feels only the Sun's pull along the joining line, so torque about the Sun is zero and L is fixed. Area swept per second is L/(2m), constant — Kepler's second law.

F = G M m / r², T² = 4π² a³ / (G M)

F is gravitational attraction in N; G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg²; M, m are masses in kg; r is separation in m; T is orbital period in s; a is semi-major axis in m.

  • Lever: F₁ d₁ = F₂ d₂ about the fulcrum.
  • Archimedes: upthrust = weight of fluid displaced.
  • Copernican model: the Sun sits at the centre.
  • Kepler: elliptical orbits; equal areas in equal times; T² ∝ a³.

Worked example

Question. A cricket ball of mass 0.16 kg arrives at the bat at 30 m/s and leaves along the same line in the opposite direction at 20 m/s. Contact lasts 0.010 s. Take the incoming direction as positive. (a) Find the momentum before and after the stroke. (b) Find the change in momentum. (c) Find the average force on the ball.

  1. Data with signs: m = 0.16 kg, u = +30 m/s, v = −20 m/s, Δt = 0.010 s.
  2. Before: p₁ = 0.16 × 30 = 4.8 kg·m/s, towards the bat.
  3. After: p₂ = 0.16 × (−20) = −3.2 kg·m/s, back towards the bowler.
  4. Change: Δp = −3.2 − 4.8 = −8.0 kg·m/s; the magnitude 8.0 is 4.8 + 3.2, because the ball reversed.
  5. Average force: ΣF = Δp/Δt = −8.0 / 0.010 = −800 N.
  6. The minus sign says the force points opposite to the incoming ball.

Answer. The momentum changes by −8.0 kg·m/s and the bat exerts an average force of −800 N, i.e. 800 N directed opposite to the incoming ball.

Exam traps

  • Momentum is a vector. On reversal the change is 4.8 + 3.2 = 8.0 kg·m/s, not 4.8 − 3.2.
  • Internal forces cancel only for the whole system; for one part they are the only forces acting.
  • ΣF = dp/dt uses the net external force. A train at steady 80 km/h has many forces but their sum is zero.
  • Rocket thrust uses the exhaust speed relative to the rocket, never speed from the ground.
  • Kepler's second law means constant angular momentum: speed is largest nearest the Sun, not constant around the orbit.

Check yourself

Remember State Newton's third law. Why can internal forces not change the total momentum of a system? 2 marks

Action and reaction are equal, opposite, along the same line, on different bodies. Inside a system they pair to zero, so only external forces appear in ΣF_ext = dP/dt.

Understand A book rests on a table. Its weight and the table's upward push are equal and opposite. Are they an action–reaction pair? Explain. 3 marks

No. Both act on the book and cancel because its acceleration is zero. The reaction to the book's weight is the book pulling the Earth up; the reaction to the table's push is the book pressing down on the table.

Apply A 1200 kg car moving at 15 m/s brakes uniformly to rest in 3.0 s. Find the average braking force. 4 marks

p₁ = 1200 × 15 = 18 000 kg·m/s; p₂ = 0; Δp = −18 000 kg·m/s; F = −18 000/3.0 = −6000 N, i.e. 6000 N opposite the motion.

Apply A rocket burns fuel at 20 kg/s and ejects the gas at 2.5 km/s relative to the rocket. Find the thrust. 4 marks

u = 2500 m/s, R = 20 kg/s; F = u R = 2500 × 20 = 5.0 × 10⁴ N.

Analyze A pendulum hangs from the roof of a bus accelerating at A = 2 m/s². Find the angle it makes with the vertical, and say whether the bus frame is inertial. 5 marks

In the bus frame a pseudo force −m A acts, so tan θ = A/g = 2/9.8 = 0.204, θ ≈ 11.5°. The bus frame is non-inertial; from the ground T sin θ = m A and T cos θ = m g give the same angle.

Understand A swimmer aims straight across a river and never lands directly opposite the starting point. Why, and what must the swimmer do to land opposite? 3 marks

The current carries the swimmer downstream while crossing at speed c: time d/c, drift v d/c. Landing opposite needs an upstream component equal to v, so aim upstream; the crossing then takes d/√(c² − v²).

Analyze A satellite in an elliptical orbit is at r₁ = 7.0 × 10⁶ m from the Earth's centre with speed 9.0 × 10³ m/s. Find its speed at the farthest point, r₂ = 1.4 × 10⁷ m. 5 marks

The force is central, so L is conserved; at the apsides v ⊥ r, so r₁ v₁ = r₂ v₂. Hence v₂ = (7.0 × 10⁶ × 9.0 × 10³)/(1.4 × 10⁷) = 4.5 × 10³ m/s.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Launch vehicles are sized by this momentum bookkeeping: F = u R fixes the burn rate for a given exhaust speed, and staging drops dead mass. Spacecraft also use gravity assists (central-force scattering) and ion thrusters giving tiny thrust for months. Search for the rocket equation, specific impulse and Hohmann transfer orbit. The same centre-of-mass and momentum equations keep walking robots and simulated bodies stable.

1.2 Elasticity: stress, strain and elastic moduli

Stretch a rubber band, hang a load on a steel wire, or squeeze a balloon: each body pushes back. Elasticity turns that push into numbers. Stress spreads the force over the area carrying it; strain measures the fractional change in length, volume or shape. Their ratio is a constant of the material, but only while the deformation is reversible. Engineers use these numbers to size bridges, lift cables, railway rails and machine shafts. Only a few universities teach this topic at full depth.

You need: force and equilibrium from 1.1.

Stress, strain and three moduli

When a force stretches a body, the atoms inside pull back. The deforming force per unit area is stress: σ = F/A, with F in N perpendicular to area A in m², so stress is in N/m² = Pa. Strain is the fractional change: ε = ΔL/L, a pure ratio with no units. For small deformations stress is proportional to strain. The constant is the material's modulus, valid only inside the elastic limit.

Y = (F/A)/(ΔL/L) = FL/(AΔL)

Y is Young's modulus (Pa), F the axial force (N), A the cross-section (m²), L the original length (m), ΔL the extension (m).

A pull or push along the length uses Young's modulus Y. A squeeze from all sides uses bulk modulus K = −ΔP/(ΔV/V); ΔP is the pressure change (Pa) and ΔV/V the fractional volume change. The minus keeps K positive because volume falls when pressure rises. A sideways push on one face uses rigidity modulus G (some books write η or n) = (F/A)/(x/L), where x is the sideways shift of a face of height L. Poisson's ratio ν = lateral strain ÷ longitudinal strain; some books use the symbol µ for it. In this book σ always means stress. For metals ν is usually 0.2 to 0.4.

The stress–strain curve and stored energy

Plot stress against strain for mild steel. Up to the proportional limit the graph is a straight line: Hooke's law. The elastic limit is the largest stress that leaves no permanent set. Past the yield point the metal flows with little extra stress. Ultimate strength is the highest stress; then the specimen necks and breaks. Ductile materials such as mild steel have a long plastic region; brittle ones such as glass break soon after the elastic limit. On unloading after yield, the line runs parallel to the elastic line and leaves a permanent strain. A cyclic load traces a hysteresis loop; its area is energy lost as heat per unit volume per cycle.

For mild steel the proportional limit is near 200 MPa and yield near 250 MPa. The metal then stretches a long way: it breaks only at a strain of about 0.2, that is 20 %. So the strain axis in the figure is broken after the elastic part; the plastic and necking region is drawn compressed, not to scale.

The elastic energy stored per unit volume is u = ½ × stress × strain = ½σε = σ²/(2Y), in J/m³. For a wire, ΔL = FL/(AY); doubling the diameter quarters the extension. Steel has Y ≈ 2×10¹¹ Pa.

Worked example

Question. A steel wire 2.0 m long and 1.0 mm in diameter hangs a load of 5.0 kg. Take Y = 2.0×10¹¹ Pa and g = 9.8 m/s². Find the extension, the stress and the strain in the wire.

  1. Radius r = d/2 = 0.50 mm = 5.0×10⁻⁴ m.
  2. Area A = πr² = π(5.0×10⁻⁴)² = 7.854×10⁻⁷ m².
  3. Force F = mg = 5.0 × 9.8 = 49 N.
  4. Extension ΔL = FL/(AY) = (49 × 2.0) ÷ (7.854×10⁻⁷ × 2.0×10¹¹) = 98 ÷ 1.571×10⁵ = 6.24×10⁻⁴ m.
  5. Stress σ = F/A = 49 ÷ 7.854×10⁻⁷ = 6.24×10⁷ Pa = 62.4 MPa.
  6. Strain ε = σ/Y = 6.24×10⁷ ÷ 2.0×10¹¹ = 3.12×10⁻⁴. Check: ΔL/L = 6.24×10⁻⁴ ÷ 2.0 = 3.12×10⁻⁴.

Answer. The wire stretches 6.24×10⁻⁴ m (0.624 mm); stress is 6.24×10⁷ Pa (62.4 MPa); strain is 3.12×10⁻⁴.

Exam traps

  • Diameter is not radius. Using 1.0 mm as r in the area makes A four times too big and the extension four times too small.
  • Use the right modulus: a wire pulled lengthwise uses Y, a body squeezed all round uses K, a face pushed sideways uses G.
  • Strain is a pure ratio with no units; m/m is acceptable because it equals 1, but N/m² is a stress unit and wrong for strain.
  • The proportional limit and elastic limit are close but not the same; the straight line ends at the proportional limit.
  • Convert mm² to m² before using ΔL = FL/(AY); 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m².

Remember Define stress and strain. Give their SI units. 2 marks

Stress = F/A in N/m² = Pa; strain = ΔL/L, a pure ratio with no units.

Understand Why does the bulk modulus formula carry a minus sign? 3 marks

A pressure increase lowers volume, so ΔV is negative; the minus makes K positive.

Apply A wire 3.0 m long, 0.50 mm diameter, carries 100 N. Y = 2.0×10¹¹ Pa. Find ΔL. 4 marks

r = 2.5×10⁻⁴ m, A = 1.96×10⁻⁷ m², ΔL = FL/(AY) = 300 ÷ 3.93×10⁴ = 7.6×10⁻³ m = 7.6 mm.

Analyze A steel rod is loaded past yield and then unloaded. Describe its length and the unloading line. 4 marks

The unloading line is parallel to the elastic line; the rod remains longer by a permanent strain; residual stress may remain.

Apply Find the elastic energy per unit volume at stress 100 MPa in steel, Y = 2×10¹¹ Pa. 3 marks

u = σ²/(2Y) = (1×10⁸)² ÷ (4×10¹¹) = 2.5×10⁴ J/m³.

Analyze Same load, a wire of double length and double diameter. How does the extension change? 4 marks

ΔL ∝ L/A; L doubles, A becomes 4 times, so ΔL becomes 2/4 = 1/2 of the original.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Real materials are viscoelastic: they creep under steady load and relax under fixed strain, which matters for concrete in dams and solder joints in electronics. Shape-memory alloys such as nitinol return to a set shape on heating and are used in stents. Finite element software solves stress and strain on millions of tiny elements to design car bodies and aircraft wings. Search next for 'viscoelasticity', 'von Mises yield criterion' and 'fatigue limit'.

1.3 Bending of beams: cantilever and non-uniform bending

Load a ruler held out over the edge of a table and it dips. A beam bends the same way: the upper face stretches, the lower face compresses, and one thin layer in between keeps its length — the neutral surface. The resisting moment depends on the material through Y and on the shape through I, the second moment of area. That one relation, M = YI/R, fixes how far any beam sags. Engineers size bridge girders, balcony slabs and crane jibs with it, and a wrong depth turns a 5 mm sag into 50 mm. Only a few universities teach it as a full chapter.

You need: 1.2 Elasticity — stress, strain and Young's modulus Y, the material constant that appears in every formula here.

Neutral surface and the bending moment

Bend a straight beam into an arc of radius R. Fibres on the outer side lengthen, fibres on the inner side shorten, and one surface in between neither lengthens nor shortens: the neutral surface. Its line across any cross-section is the neutral axis. A fibre at distance y from that axis carries strain y/R, so its stress is Yy/R, with Y the Young's modulus in Pa.

Those stresses act on every element of area, and their moments about the neutral axis add to the bending moment M at that section. The sum is (Y/R)∫y² dA, and that integral is the second moment of area I. So the moment a section can carry and the curvature it takes are locked together.

M = YI/R

M is the bending moment in N m, the couple the loads produce at that section; Y is Young's modulus in Pa; I is the second moment of area about the neutral axis, in m⁴; R is the radius of curvature in m. A large YI means a large R, that is, gentle bending.

I = bd³/12 (rectangle), I = πr⁴/4 (circle)

b is the breadth in m; d is the depth in m, measured along the load — the dimension that bends; r is the radius of a circular section in m.

Cantilever and the centre-loaded beam

A cantilever is clamped at one end and free at the other. Hang a load W (newtons) at the free end. At distance x from the clamp, the load to the right of that section makes a moment M = W(L − x), where L is the length in m. For small sags the curvature 1/R is d²y/dx². Integrating twice, with zero slope and zero sag at the clamp, gives the free-end sag.

δ = WL³/(3YI) = 4WL³/(Ybd³)

δ is the free-end sag in m; W is the end load in N; L is the cantilever length in m; the second form uses I = bd³/12 for a rectangular strip of breadth b and depth d, both in m. Twice the depth sags eight times less, because of the d³.

Now rest a beam on two knife edges a distance l apart and hang W at the middle. Symmetry makes the slope zero at the centre, and the sag there is δ = Wl³/(4Ybd³). Rearranged, Y = Wl³/(4bd³δ) — the non-uniform bending experiment. Measure δ with a pin and a travelling microscope. Apply a pure couple at both ends instead and the curvature is the same everywhere; some syllabi call that uniform bending and run it as a separate experiment.

Why girders are I-shaped

Each element of area contributes A y² to I, so area far from the neutral axis earns far more resistance than area near it. An I-section puts two heavy flanges at the top and bottom and joins them with a thin web that mainly holds them apart. Mass sits where it works hardest. Railway bridge girders and crane jibs use this I-shape directly; a roof truss gets the same benefit by separating a top chord and a bottom chord with a light web of members.

Worked example

Question. A steel cantilever 0.50 m long, 20 mm wide and 5.0 mm thick carries a 0.50 kg load at its free end. Take Y = 2.0×10¹¹ Pa and g = 9.8 m/s². Find the second moment of area I about the neutral axis and the sag δ of the free end.

  1. Convert to metres: L = 0.50 m, b = 0.020 m, d = 0.0050 m. The thickness 5.0 mm is the depth, because the load bends the strip across that dimension.
  2. d³ = (0.0050)³ = 1.25×10⁻⁷ m³.
  3. I = bd³/12 = (0.020 × 1.25×10⁻⁷) ÷ 12 = 2.5×10⁻⁹ ÷ 12 = 2.083×10⁻¹⁰ m⁴ ≈ 2.1×10⁻¹⁰ m⁴.
  4. Load W = mg = 0.50 × 9.8 = 4.9 N.
  5. L³ = (0.50)³ = 0.125 m³.
  6. 3YI = 3 × 2.0×10¹¹ × 2.083×10⁻¹⁰ = 125.0 N m².
  7. δ = WL³/(3YI) = (4.9 × 0.125) ÷ 125.0 = 0.6125 ÷ 125.0 = 4.9×10⁻³ m.

Exam traps

  • In I = bd³/12 the cube sits on the dimension parallel to the load — the strip's thickness, not its breadth. Swapping b and d changes I by (b/d)².
  • L must be in metres, like I. A length left in mm makes L³ wrong by 10⁹.
  • Cantilever has the 3 (δ = WL³/3YI); the centre-loaded beam has the 4 (δ = Wl³/4Ybd³). Examiners love this swap.
  • In Y = Wl³/(4bd³δ), l is the distance between the knife edges, not the full length of a beam that overhangs them.
  • All these formulas assume small sag, δ much smaller than L. A strip bent into a semicircle is a different, non-linear problem.

Check yourself

Remember Define the neutral surface and the neutral axis of a bent beam. 2 marks

The neutral surface is the layer inside a bent beam whose length does not change; its trace on a cross-section is the neutral axis, and distances y are measured from it.

Understand Why does doubling the depth of a rectangular strip reduce the sag eight times, while doubling the breadth only halves it? 3 marks

δ = 4WL³/(Ybd³). Breadth enters to the first power, so doubling b halves δ. Depth enters as d³ because a deeper strip has more area AND that area sits farther from the neutral axis, so I grows eightfold.

Apply A cantilever has L = 0.40 m, b = 10 mm, d = 2.0 mm and carries W = 2.0 N. Take Y = 2.0×10¹¹ Pa. Find the free-end sag δ. 4 marks

I = bd³/12 = (0.010 × 8.0×10⁻⁹)/12 = 6.67×10⁻¹² m⁴. 3YI = 3 × 2.0×10¹¹ × 6.67×10⁻¹² = 4.0 N m². L³ = 0.064 m³. δ = (2.0 × 0.064)/4.0 = 0.032 m = 32 mm.

Apply A steel beam rests on knife edges 1.0 m apart, breadth 20 mm, depth 5.0 mm. A 1.0 kg load at the middle makes the centre sag 4.9 mm. Find Y. 5 marks

W = 1.0 × 9.8 = 9.8 N; l³ = 1.0 m³; d³ = 1.25×10⁻⁷ m³; δ = 4.9×10⁻³ m. Y = Wl³/(4bd³δ) = 9.8/(4 × 0.020 × 1.25×10⁻⁷ × 4.9×10⁻³) = 9.8/(4.9×10⁻¹¹) = 2.0×10¹¹ Pa.

Analyze In the pin-and-microscope experiment the load is hung at the centre of the span, not near one support. Why? 3 marks

At the centre the slope is zero by symmetry, so the sag formula Wl³/(4bd³) holds cleanly and the sag is largest, giving the smallest percentage error in reading δ with the microscope.

Analyze Two girders have the same mass and length; one is a solid rectangle, the other an I-section. Why does the I-section sag less? 4 marks

For the same load and span, the sag scales as δ ∝ 1/I. I = Σ A y², so the I-section puts more area into flanges far from the neutral axis and has the larger I; hence it sags less. Use δ = WL³/(3YI) only for the cantilever case.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Real structures are checked with finite-element software that solves the same M = YI/R relation over thousands of small elements. Beam theory here is the Euler–Bernoulli case: sections stay flat and shear is ignored, which fails for short, deep beams. Search for beam deflection boundary conditions, section modulus, and built-up girders if you go into civil or mechanical design. Composite and aluminium bridge decks are an active research area, chosen when a low mass with high YI matters.

1.4 Viscosity and Stokes' method

Viscosity is friction inside a flowing fluid. Honey pours slowly, water quickly; that difference is the material number η, the coefficient of viscosity. Engineers size oil pipelines, dampers and blood-flow models with it. Stokes' method measures η by dropping a small sphere into the fluid and timing its steady fall. Weight, upthrust and drag balance, and the terminal speed gives η. Only a few universities teach this measurement in full.

You need: 1.1 — Newton's laws for the force balance, and upthrust from school physics.

Friction between fluid layers

Picture fluid between a moving plate and a fixed plate. Each layer sticks to the plate it touches, so speed changes from zero to the plate's speed across the gap. The velocity gradient dv/dx is this change per metre. Newton's law of viscosity says drag per unit area is proportional to it:

F = ηA(dv/dx)

F is tangential force in N; A is plate area in m²; dv/dx is velocity gradient in s⁻¹; η is the coefficient of viscosity in Pa·s (N·s/m²).

One poise = 0.1 Pa·s. Water near 20 °C has η ≈ 1×10⁻³ Pa·s, glycerine ≈ 1.4 Pa·s near 20 °C (falling to ≈ 0.8 Pa·s near 27–30 °C), air ≈ 1.8×10⁻⁵ Pa·s. Streamline (laminar) flow has layers sliding without mixing; turbulent flow has eddies. The Reynolds number Re = ρvd/η, with ρ density, v typical speed and d typical length, tells which: low Re is laminar. This chapter stays slow and laminar.

Stokes' law, terminal speed and measurement

When a sphere of radius r falls slowly through a fluid of viscosity η, the drag is F = 6πηrv for speed v, provided flow is laminar, steady, and the fluid extends far from the sphere. Three forces act: weight W = (4/3)πr³ρg downward, upthrust U = (4/3)πr³σg upward, and drag F upward. ρ is the sphere's density and σ the fluid's, both in kg/m³. At terminal speed vt the ball stops accelerating, so W = U + F. Solving:

vt = 2r²(ρ − σ)g/(9η)

vt is terminal speed in m/s; r is sphere radius in m; ρ and σ are sphere and fluid densities in kg/m³; g = 9.8 m/s²; η is viscosity in Pa·s.

If ρ < σ, vt comes out negative and the sphere rises instead of falling (upthrust exceeds weight). The approach to terminal speed is quick for small balls; the figure shows the shape of the speed curve.

  1. Mark two levels a distance L apart in a tall jar.
  2. Measure the ball radius r with a micrometer, and densities ρ, σ.
  3. Drop the ball centrally; let it pass the first mark before timing.
  4. Time t between marks; v = L/t, then use the formula above for η.

η = 2r²(ρ − σ)g/(9v)

v is the measured steady speed in m/s; the other symbols are as above.

In a narrow tube, walls raise the drag: v is smaller and uncorrected η comes out too large. Keep temperature steady; η falls quickly when a liquid warms.

Worked example

Question. A steel ball of radius 2.0 mm and density 7800 kg/m³ falls in glycerine (at about 27 °C) of density 1260 kg/m³ and viscosity 0.83 Pa·s. Take g = 9.8 m/s². Find its terminal speed and the time to fall 20 cm between marks.

  1. r = 2.0×10⁻³ m; ρ − σ = 7800 − 1260 = 6540 kg/m³.
  2. r² = 4.0×10⁻⁶ m². Numerator = 2 × 4.0×10⁻⁶ × 6540 × 9.8 = 0.513 kg/s².
  3. 9η = 9 × 0.83 = 7.47 Pa·s. v_t = 0.513 ÷ 7.47 = 0.069 m/s = 6.9 cm/s.
  4. L = 0.20 m. t = L/v_t = 0.20 ÷ 0.069 = 2.9 s.

Answer. Terminal speed 6.9 cm/s; the ball takes 2.9 s between the marks.

Exam traps

  • Use ρ − σ, not ρ. A ball as dense as the liquid has zero terminal speed.
  • Use radius, not diameter, and square it. Doubling r quadruples v_t.
  • Convert poise: 1 poise = 0.1 Pa·s. Substituting 8.3 for 0.83 Pa·s is a factor-of-ten error.
  • Time only after terminal speed is reached; starting at the surface gives a short, wrong time.
  • In a narrow tube, uncorrected η is too large; the wall correction lowers it.

1.5 Surface tension and drop weight method

Surface tension is the pull along a liquid surface. A molecule inside the liquid has neighbours all around; a molecule at the surface has fewer, so it carries extra energy. The surface therefore behaves like a stretched membrane, and its tension is T = F/L, measured in N/m. Weighing drops that fall from a narrow tube measures T, because the drop breaks when its weight almost equals the force around the tube rim. Only a few universities teach the drop weight method in detail; it is a practical, low-cost experiment.

You need the force balance from 1.1.

Molecular origin and stretched surface

Inside a liquid, a molecule has neighbours all around and feels no net pull. At the surface it has liquid below and air above, so it has fewer neighbours and a net inward pull. Moving a molecule from the bulk to the surface needs work. The extra energy per unit area is the surface energy in J/m².

T = F/L

F is force in N along a line of length L in m; T is surface tension in N/m. The value in N/m equals the surface energy in J/m².

Water near 20 °C has T about 0.072 N/m. At a solid wall, the surface meets the wall at the angle of contact θ. Water on clean glass has small θ, wets the glass, and forms a concave meniscus. Mercury on glass has large θ and forms a convex meniscus. Curvature changes pressure: inside a liquid drop the excess pressure is 2T/r; inside a soap bubble it is 4T/r, with r the radius in m. A wetting liquid rises in a narrow tube to h = 2T cosθ/(ρgr), where ρ is density in kg/m³ and g = 9.8 m/s². Detergent lowers T; heating usually lowers it too.

Drop weight method

A tube of radius r is filled with liquid and held vertical. A drop grows at the lower tip. Gravity pulls the drop down with weight mg, where m is the mass of one drop in kg and g = 9.8 m/s². Surface tension pulls upward around the rim. The rim length is the circumference 2πr, so the upward force is 2πrT. The drop hangs while 2πrT is slightly larger than mg.

mg = 2πrT

m is mass of one drop in kg; g is 9.8 m/s²; r is tube tip radius in m; T is surface tension in N/m.

At the moment of falling, the neck is thin and part of the liquid stays behind, so the falling drop is smaller than the hanging drop. Tables give a correction factor f. The working formula is T = mg/(2πrf). Here f is a dimensionless number from tables that depends on r and the drop size; it is near 1 for a very narrow tube.

  1. Clean the tube and fill it with the liquid.
  2. Let n drops fall into a weighed dish. Weigh again to get total mass M in kg.
  3. Mass of one drop m = M/n.
  4. Read f for the tube radius from the correction table.
  5. Put m, r and f into T = mg/(2πrf).

Worked example

Question. Water drips from a tube of radius 1.0 mm. Thirty drops together weigh 1.20 g. The correction factor for this tube is f = 0.87. Find the mass of one drop, the weight of one drop, the uncorrected surface tension, and the corrected surface tension.

  1. Total mass M = 1.20 g = 1.20×10⁻³ kg.
  2. Mass of one drop m = M/n = 1.20×10⁻³ ÷ 30 = 4.00×10⁻⁵ kg.
  3. Weight mg = 4.00×10⁻⁵ × 9.8 = 3.92×10⁻⁴ N.
  4. Radius r = 1.0 mm = 1.0×10⁻³ m; rim length 2πr = 6.283×10⁻³ m.
  5. Without correction T = mg/(2πr) = 3.92×10⁻⁴ ÷ 6.283×10⁻³ = 0.0624 N/m.
  6. With correction T = 0.0624 ÷ 0.87 = 0.0717 N/m.

Answer. The corrected surface tension is 0.0717 N/m, about 0.072 N/m, which is water's value near room temperature.

Exam traps

  • The radius is the tube's radius at the tip, not the drop's radius.
  • Use the mass of one drop in T = mg/(2πrf), not the total mass of n drops.
  • The correction factor divides the uncorrected value; it does not multiply it.
  • Convert grams to kg and millimetres to m before putting numbers into the formula.
  • The surface tension force acts around the rim, length 2πr; it is not πr² or the area of the drop.

1.6 Thermal expansion, thermal stress and bimetallic strips

A solid expands when heated because its atoms vibrate over slightly larger distances. The change in length is proportional to the original length and to the temperature rise. A rail, bridge or pipeline must be allowed to move by that much. If the ends are clamped, the blocked expansion appears as thermal stress. The same effect bends a bimetallic strip, which switches a thermostat. Only a few universities teach the bimetallic strip in detail. This chapter gives the three standard results and the numbers engineers use.

You need: 1.2 — stress, strain and Young's modulus, because thermal stress is a strain held back by the material.

Linear, areal and volume expansion

A rod of original length L at temperature T expands by ΔL when its temperature rises by ΔT. The rule is ΔL = L α ΔT. α is the coefficient of linear expansion, the fractional length change per degree Celsius. Its unit is per °C; the number is the same per K because only a temperature difference enters. For a thin sheet, area grows by ΔA = A β ΔT with β ≈ 2α. For a solid block, volume grows by ΔV = V γ ΔT with γ ≈ 3α. These are small-change approximations for an isotropic solid, one whose properties are the same in every direction.

ΔL = L α ΔT

L is original length in m; ΔL is change in length in m; α is coefficient of linear expansion in per °C; ΔT is temperature change in °C or K.

Thermal stress and bimetallic strips

If both ends of a rod are fixed, the free expansion LαΔT is blocked. The material is compressed by the same strain that heating would have produced: strain = αΔT. Hooke's law then gives thermal stress σ = Y α ΔT, where Y is Young's modulus in N/m². The force on the supports is F = σA, with A the cross-section area in m². Stress does not depend on length; force does. A rail, bridge deck or pipeline therefore needs an expansion gap of at least LαΔT, and a bridge girder rests on roller supports at one end.

  • Bimetallic strip: two metals with different α are bonded.
  • On heating, the larger-α side becomes longer and lies on the outside of the bend.
  • The curvature grows with ΔT and with the difference α₁ − α₂.
  • It is used in thermostats, fire alarms and old clocks.

Worked example

Question. A steel rail is 20 m long and clamped at both ends at 25 °C. α = 12×10⁻⁶ per °C, Y = 2.0×10¹¹ Pa. The hottest day reaches 55 °C. (a) Find the gap an expansion joint must allow. (b) Find the thermal stress if the rail cannot move.

  1. ΔT = 55 − 25 = 30 °C.
  2. Free expansion ΔL = LαΔT = 20 × 12×10⁻⁶ × 30 = 7.2×10⁻³ m = 7.2 mm.
  3. Strain = αΔT = 12×10⁻⁶ × 30 = 3.6×10⁻⁴.
  4. Stress = Y × strain = 2.0×10¹¹ × 3.6×10⁻⁴ = 7.2×10⁷ Pa = 72 MPa.

Answer. The expansion joint must allow 7.2 mm; if blocked, the rail carries 72 MPa of thermal stress.

Exam traps

  • ΔT is the change, not the temperature. From 25 °C to 55 °C, ΔT = 30 °C, not 55 °C. The same number works in kelvin.
  • A coefficient quoted per °F is smaller than the per °C value; convert before putting it into LαΔT.
  • β ≈ 2α and γ ≈ 3α hold for an isotropic solid, not for a long rod where only length matters.
  • Thermal stress YαΔT is independent of length and area. Force σA depends on area, so a thicker rail carries more force at the same stress.
  • In a heated bimetallic strip, the larger-α metal is on the outside of the curve, not the inside.

1.7 Special relativity: postulates, length contraction, time dilation

Light moves at 3.00×10⁸ m/s in vacuum. That number comes out the same for a person on a platform and for a passenger in a train rushing past the lamp. Ordinary velocity addition cannot deliver that. Special relativity keeps c fixed and lets space and time adjust instead: a moving clock runs slow by the factor γ, and a moving rod shortens along its motion. GPS satellites, particle accelerators and cosmic-ray muons all depend on these corrections. Only a few universities teach this inside the mechanics unit.

You need 1.1 for inertial frames and Galilean velocity addition.

Inertial frames and the failure of Galilean addition

An inertial frame is one in which a body with no net force keeps a constant velocity (1.1). Two inertial frames moving at relative speed v compare positions through the Galilean transformation x′ = x − vt, where x is a position, t is the time and v is the frame speed. Velocities then subtract: u′ = u − v. A ball thrown at 10 m/s inside a train moving at 20 m/s travels at 30 m/s for the ground observer.

Light was expected to obey the same rule, travelling through a space-filling medium called the ether. Michelson and Morley split a beam, sent the two halves along perpendicular arms, and recombined them into fringes. An ether wind blowing along one arm would delay that beam. Rotating the whole apparatus by 90° swaps the arms, so the fringes should shift. No shift was ever seen. The ether wind does not exist, and the ether was dropped from physics.

Two postulates and lost simultaneity

Einstein replaced the ether with two postulates.

  • The laws of physics are the same in every inertial frame. No frame is special.
  • Light in vacuum travels at c = 3.00×10⁸ m/s for every inertial observer, whatever the motion of the source or of the observer.

The second postulate destroys absolute simultaneity. A lamp flashes at the centre of a moving coach. The passenger sees both ends lit together. A ground observer sees the rear wall move into the light and the front wall move away, so the rear is lit first. Both are correct: “at the same time” depends on the frame.

γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²)

γ (gamma) is the Lorentz factor, a pure number; v is the relative speed of the two frames and c = 3.00×10⁸ m/s. γ = 1 at rest, and γ grows without limit as v → c.

Time dilation and length contraction

Δt = γ Δτ

Δt is the time between two events measured in a frame where the clock moves; Δτ is the proper time, read by a clock present at both events. Proper time is the shortest time between those events, so you multiply it by γ, never divide.

L = L₀/γ

L₀ is the proper length, measured in the object's rest frame; L is the length measured in a frame moving at v along the object. Only the dimension along the motion shrinks.

  • Velocities do not simply add: u = (u′ + v)/(1 + u′v/c²). Put u′ = c and you get u = c again, as the postulate demands.
  • Coordinates mix: x′ = γ(x − vt) and t′ = γ(t − vx/c²).
  • The interval s² = c²t² − x² is the same in every inertial frame. It is the one quantity all observers agree on.
  • Momentum is p = γmv and energy is E = γmc². At rest this leaves E = mc², the rest energy.

Worked example

Question. A muon is created 3.0 km above sea level and falls straight down at v = 0.98c. Its proper lifetime is 2.2 μs. Take c = 3.00×10⁸ m/s. Find γ, the lifetime measured in the lab, and the distance travelled. Compare with the distance travelled if time dilation did not exist.

  1. v/c = 0.98, so v²/c² = 0.98² = 0.9604.
  2. 1 − 0.9604 = 0.0396, and √0.0396 = 0.199.
  3. γ = 1/0.199 = 5.03.
  4. The proper time is Δτ = 2.2 μs, so the lab lifetime is Δt = γΔτ = 5.03 × 2.2 μs = 11.1 μs, i.e. 11.1×10⁻⁶ s.
  5. The speed is v = 0.98 × 3.00×10⁸ = 2.94×10⁸ m/s.
  6. Distance in the lab = vΔt = 2.94×10⁸ × 11.1×10⁻⁶ = 3.25×10³ m.
  7. Without dilation the muon would live 2.2 μs and cover 2.94×10⁸ × 2.2×10⁻⁶ = 647 m.

Answer. γ = 5.03, the lab lifetime is 11.1 μs, and the muon covers 3.25×10³ m instead of 647 m, so it reaches the ground from 3.0 km.

Exam traps

  • Proper time is the shortest time between two events: Δt = γΔτ. Dividing by γ instead of multiplying gives a lifetime shorter than the rest-frame one, which is impossible.
  • Length contraction acts only along the direction of motion. A moving sphere flattens along v but keeps its full width across v; students often shrink all three dimensions.
  • γ uses v, the relative speed of the two frames. In glass light travels slower than c, but the c written in every relativity formula is still 3.00×10⁸ m/s.
  • A question about “the length of the train” is incomplete until it names the frame. Lengths and time gaps are frame-dependent; only c and the interval s² are shared.
  • γ is never less than 1, so the dilated time is always longer and the contracted length always shorter. Any answer that breaks this is a sign error.

It is a combination of a time gap and a distance gap between two events. Every inertial frame computes the same value for it, so it plays the role that distance plays in ordinary geometry. Frames disagree on t and x but never on s².

Takeaways

  1. Newton's third law, read as a statement about pairs of internal forces, is exactly what makes momentum, energy and angular momentum conserved.
  2. Every elastic modulus is a slope on the stress–strain graph and is meaningful only below the elastic limit.
  3. How far a beam sags depends on the shape of its cross-section through I_g, not just on the amount of material.
  4. Terminal velocity is the meeting point of three forces, and the formula rearranges to give η from a measured speed.
  5. The drop weight method turns surface tension into a mass and a radius, needing only a chemical balance.
  6. Blocking expansion converts a harmless length change into a stress YαΔT, which is why bridges have gaps and joints.
  7. The same γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²) produces both time dilation and length contraction.
  8. At speeds below a few hundred km/s, γ differs from 1 by about v²/2c², far too small to see in ordinary engineering.

Apply

Apply A steel cantilever 1.0 m long, 2.0 cm wide and 1.0 cm thick carries a 2.0 kg load at its free end. Take Y = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa. Find I_g and the depression. 12 marks

I_g = bd³/12 with b = 0.02 m, d = 0.01 m, giving 1.67 × 10⁻⁹ m⁴; Convert load to weight W = 19.6 N; Use δ = WL³/(3YI_g); Answer about 2.0 × 10⁻² m; Check that the stress stays below the elastic limit.

Apply A steel ball of radius 1.0 mm and density 7800 kg m⁻³ falls steadily at 0.50 m s⁻¹ through a liquid of density 1200 kg m⁻³. Find η. 10 marks

Rearrange to η = 2r²(ρ − σ)g/(9v_t); Substitute r = 1.0 × 10⁻³ m, ρ − σ = 6600 kg m⁻³; Answer about 2.9 × 10⁻² Pa s; Comment on the wall and end effects that spoil the result.

Apply 50 water drops fall from a tube of internal radius 1.5 mm and together weigh 2.4 × 10⁻³ kg. Taking the drop-weight correction factor as 0.6, find the surface tension. 10 marks

Mass of one drop, then weight mg; Formula T = mg/(2πr f); Substitute r = 1.5 × 10⁻³ m, f = 0.6; Answer about 8 × 10⁻² N m⁻¹; Compare with the accepted value for water and explain the gap.

Apply A steel rod is clamped at both ends at 20 °C and heated to 60 °C. Given Y = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa and α = 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ K⁻¹, find the thermal stress and the force on a 2.0 cm² cross-section. 10 marks

State that the rod cannot expand, so strain is forced to zero; σ = YαΔT with ΔT = 40 K; Answer 9.6 × 10⁷ Pa = 96 MPa; Force = σA with A = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m², giving 1.9 × 10⁴ N; Nature of the stress: compressive on heating.

Apply A rod of proper length 10 m moves at 0.8c along its length, and a particle of proper lifetime 2.2 μs moves at the same speed. Find γ, the measured length and the measured lifetime. 10 marks

γ = 1/√(1 − 0.64) = 1.67; L = L₀/γ = 6.0 m, contraction along the motion only; Δt = γΔτ = 3.7 μs; Stress that proper length and proper time are measured in the rest frame.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Why are bridge girders made with an I-shaped cross-section instead of a solid rectangle of the same mass? Use M = YI_g/R and δ = WL³/(3YI_g). 12 marks

Material near the neutral surface contributes little to I_g; Put most of the area far from the neutral axis, in the flanges; Same area, much larger I_g, so smaller stress and smaller sag; Depth enters as d³, so depth is more valuable than width; Thin webs can buckle locally, which sets a practical limit.

Analyze Explain how the Newtonian idea of an absolute frame was tested by Michelson and Morley, and what Einstein replaced it with. 13 marks

Galilean transformation and the assumed ether; Interferometer with two perpendicular paths, expected fringe shift on rotation; Null result, repeated at different seasons; Einstein's two postulates and the dropping of the ether; What becomes relative: simultaneity, length, time; The Newtonian limit at v much smaller than c.

Evaluate Compare the terminal velocity method and the drop weight method as laboratory measurements of fluid properties, including their main sources of error. 12 marks

Principle and measured quantities in each; Stokes method errors: wall effect, non-sphericity, temperature drift, convection; Drop weight errors: evaporation, wetting of the tube, drop left on the tip; Corrections used in each case; Which suits a given liquid, and which is faster.

Evaluate A 300 m steel bridge is erected at 20 °C and must survive 45 °C in summer. Discuss how expansion joints and girder cross-section both enter the design. 12 marks

ΔL = LαΔT = 300 × 1.2 × 10⁻⁵ × 25 ≈ 9 cm; If blocked, stress YαΔT ≈ 60 MPa; Expansion joints and bearings absorb the movement; Joints must not cut into the girder where I_g is needed; Bimetallic strips serve as the temperature sensors in such monitoring.

Evaluate A rail or a pipe is fixed at both ends. Discuss whether it is safer to allow free expansion or to accept thermal stress, using numbers for steel. 16 marks

σ = YαΔT for complete restraint; Numerical value for a 40 K swing in steel; Buckling risk when hot, tensile cracking when cold; Use of gaps, loops and sliding bearings; Cost and maintenance comparison; Design choice depends on length and temperature range.

Glossary

Angle of contact
The angle between the liquid surface and the solid wall, measured inside the liquid at the line of contact.
Capillary rise
Liquid climbing a narrow tube to height h = 2T cosθ/(ρgr), where θ is the contact angle.
Centre of mass
The point where the whole mass of a body may be taken to act for translation.
Ductility
The ability of a material to be drawn out into a wire without breaking, shown by a long plastic region.
Ether
A supposed all-pervading medium for light, which the Michelson–Morley experiment failed to detect.
Expansion joint
A deliberate gap or sliding bearing in a structure that lets it change length without stress.
Impulse
Force multiplied by the time it acts, J = FΔt, equal to the change in momentum.
Inertial frame
A frame of reference in which a body with no net force keeps a constant velocity.
Laminar flow
Smooth flow in parallel layers, with no mixing between them, the regime where Stokes' law applies.
Lorentz transformation
The algebraic rule connecting the coordinates of one inertial frame with another moving at speed v.
Momentum
The product of mass and velocity, p = mv, a vector in kg m s⁻¹.
Muon
A short-lived particle produced high in the atmosphere whose survival to ground level is evidence of time dilation.
Proper length
The length of an object measured in the frame in which it is at rest; the longest such length.
Proper time
The time between two events measured in the frame where they happen at the same place; the shortest such time.
Proportional limit
The stress up to which stress is exactly proportional to strain, the straight part of the graph.
Rest energy
The energy a mass has even at rest, E₀ = mc².
Reynolds number
Re = ρvd/η, the ratio of inertial to viscous forces; above about 2000 flow becomes turbulent in a pipe.
Shear
Deformation in which layers slide over each other, with no change of volume.
Simultaneity
Two events happening at the same time; this is frame-dependent once moving frames are compared.
Spacetime interval
s² = c²t² − (x² + y² + z²), the same value in every inertial frame.
Strain energy
Energy stored in a stretched body per unit volume, ½ × stress × strain.
Torque
Turning effect of a force, τ = rF sinθ, in N m.
Ultimate strength
The largest stress a material can carry before it breaks.
Upthrust
Buoyant force on a body in a fluid, equal to the weight of fluid displaced.
Yield point
The stress at which the material suddenly stretches with little extra load.
