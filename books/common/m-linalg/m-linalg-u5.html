5.1 Inner products and norms

An inner product takes two vectors and returns a single number. From that one number you get everything geometric: the length of a vector, the distance between two vectors, and the angle between them. This is how linear algebra talks about shape, not just algebra. It is used wherever a computer must compare signals, images or text: recommender systems score how similar two users are, and a telecom engineer measures how far a received symbol sits from the ideal one. Some syllabi call the inner product the scalar product or the dot product.

You need: vectors, linear combinations and basis from Unit 2, Vector Spaces.

The inner product as a rule

An inner product is a rule that takes two vectors u and v from a vector space and returns one number, written ⟨u, v⟩. The brackets ⟨ ⟩ are read as "inner product of". The rule is not arbitrary; it must obey three axioms over the reals.

  • Positive definite: ⟨u, u⟩ ≥ 0, and ⟨u, u⟩ = 0 only when u = 0.
  • Symmetric: ⟨u, v⟩ = ⟨v, u⟩.
  • Linear in the first slot: ⟨αu + βw, v⟩ = α⟨u, v⟩ + β⟨w, v⟩ for real numbers α and β.

Over the complex numbers the second axiom changes to ⟨u, v⟩ = conjugate of ⟨v, u⟩. Write the conjugate of a complex number z as z̄. Then ⟨u, αv⟩ = ᾱ⟨u, v⟩: the rule is linear in the first slot and conjugate-linear in the second. The quantity ⟨u, u⟩ is always a non-negative real number, even in a complex space.

⟨u, v⟩ = u₁v₁ + u₂v₂ + … + uₙvₙ

u = (u₁, …, uₙ) and v = (v₁, …, vₙ) are vectors in ℝⁿ and uᵢ, vᵢ are real numbers. This is the standard dot product on ℝⁿ.

⟨u, v⟩ = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ wᵢuᵢvᵢ, wᵢ > 0

w₁, …, wₙ are fixed positive weights. Each coordinate is counted more or less heavily. In ℝ² with w = (1, 4), u = (1, 1) and v = (1, −1) give ⟨u, v⟩ = 1·1·1 + 4·1·(−1) = 1 − 4 = −3.

⟨f, g⟩ = ∫₀¹ f(x)g(x) dx

f and g are polynomials in the variable x, and the integral runs from x = 0 to x = 1. For f(x) = 1 and g(x) = x, ⟨f, g⟩ = ∫₀¹ x dx = 1/2. Vectors are now functions, not lists of numbers.

Norm and distance

The inner product of a vector with itself gives its squared length. The norm is the square root of that.

‖u‖ = √⟨u, u⟩, d(u, v) = ‖u − v‖

‖u‖ is the norm (length) of u; d(u, v) is the distance between u and v; u − v is the vector difference.

The norm behaves as length should. ‖u‖ ≥ 0, with equality only for u = 0. Scaling a vector scales its length by the absolute value of the scalar: ‖cu‖ = |c|‖u‖. For example, ‖(3, 4)‖ = √(9 + 16) = 5 in ℝ², and ‖2·(3, 4)‖ = 10. Every non-zero vector has a unit vector u/‖u‖ of length 1 pointing the same way.

The weights change the lengths. With w = (1, 4) in ℝ², ‖(1, 1)‖ = √(1·1 + 4·1) = √5 ≈ 2.236, not √2. The same list of numbers carries different lengths under different inner products, so always state which rule you use.

In the polynomial space with ⟨f, g⟩ = ∫₀¹ f g dx, ⟨x, x⟩ = ∫₀¹ x² dx = 1/3, so ‖x‖ = 1/√3 ≈ 0.577. A shorter distance means two functions are close everywhere on [0, 1] in the average sense.

Two more properties, the triangle inequality and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, follow from the axioms and decide when a rule is a genuine norm; they are proved in 5.2.

Angle between two vectors

In ℝ² and ℝ³ the usual geometric formula for the dot product rearranges into a definition of angle that works in any inner product space.

cos θ = ⟨u, v⟩ / (‖u‖‖v‖)

θ is the angle between the non-zero vectors u and v, measured in radians or degrees; the denominator is the product of the two norms.

In ℝ², u = (3, 4) and v = (1, 0) give ⟨u, v⟩ = 3, ‖u‖ = 5, ‖v‖ = 1, so cos θ = 3/5 and θ ≈ 53.13°. The formula agrees with the picture drawn on paper.

A zero inner product means cos θ = 0, so θ = 90°: the vectors are perpendicular, or orthogonal. A negative inner product means an obtuse angle. Tests will ask you to compute the angle rather than to draw it, and the whole calculation rests on the three numbers ⟨u, v⟩, ‖u‖, ‖v‖.

Orthogonal and orthonormal sets, and their use as coordinates, are built on this single formula; see 5.3.

Worked example

Question. In ℝ³, using the standard dot product, take u = (1, 2, 2) and v = (3, 0, 4). Find ⟨u, v⟩, ‖u‖, ‖v‖, the angle θ between u and v, and the distance d(u, v).

  1. ⟨u, v⟩ = u₁v₁ + u₂v₂ + u₃v₃ = 1×3 + 2×0 + 2×4 = 3 + 0 + 8 = 11.
  2. ⟨u, u⟩ = 1² + 2² + 2² = 1 + 4 + 4 = 9, so ‖u‖ = √9 = 3.
  3. ⟨v, v⟩ = 3² + 0² + 4² = 9 + 0 + 16 = 25, so ‖v‖ = √25 = 5.
  4. cos θ = ⟨u, v⟩ / (‖u‖‖v‖) = 11 / (3 × 5) = 11/15 ≈ 0.7333.
  5. θ = cos⁻¹(0.7333) ≈ 42.83°.
  6. u − v = (1 − 3, 2 − 0, 2 − 4) = (−2, 2, −2). d(u, v) = ‖u − v‖ = √((−2)² + 2² + (−2)²) = √(4 + 4 + 4) = √12 ≈ 3.4641.

Answer. ⟨u, v⟩ = 11; ‖u‖ = 3 and ‖v‖ = 5; the angle θ ≈ 42.83°; the distance d(u, v) = √12 ≈ 3.4641.

Exam traps

  • The inner product of a vector with itself is not its length. ⟨(3, 4), (3, 4)⟩ = 25, while the length is √25 = 5. Always take the square root for the norm.
  • Over ℂ the inner product is conjugate-linear in the second slot, so ⟨u, v⟩ is the conjugate of ⟨v, u⟩. Swapping the order without conjugating gives a wrong sign on the imaginary part.
  • A weighted inner product needs every weight strictly positive. With w = (1, −1) you get ⟨(1, 1), (1, 1)⟩ = 0 for a non-zero vector, so the rule fails positive definiteness and is not an inner product.
  • ⟨u, v⟩ = 0 does not mean u or v is the zero vector. It means the two vectors are perpendicular. Examiners often give a zero inner product and ask what follows — the answer is orthogonality.
  • A negative inner product is not an error. It gives cos θ < 0, so θ is greater than 90°; report the angle in degrees or radians as asked.

5.2 Cauchy-Schwarz and triangle inequalities

The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality says no inner product can be larger in size than the product of the two lengths: |⟨u,v⟩| ≤ ‖u‖‖v‖. It exists because length and angle should obey ordinary geometry in every inner product space, not just in ℝ² or ℝ³. From it comes the triangle inequality for norms, the rule that a detour is never shorter than a straight line. Engineers use it to bound errors, check convergence, and justify least squares. Only a few universities teach this chapter as a separate topic.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for inner product, norm and angle.

The bound and its proof

For vectors u and v in an inner product space, Cauchy–Schwarz says the size of their inner product is at most the product of their lengths: |⟨u,v⟩| ≤ ‖u‖‖v‖. The absolute value is needed because ⟨u,v⟩ can be negative in real spaces and complex in complex spaces. Some syllabi call this the Cauchy–Bunyakovsky–Schwarz inequality.

|⟨u,v⟩| ≤ ‖u‖‖v‖

u and v are vectors; ⟨u,v⟩ is their inner product; ‖u‖ = √⟨u,u⟩ is the norm of u.

Proof. If v = 0, both sides are 0. Otherwise put t = ⟨u,v⟩/‖v‖² and use the fact that every squared length is non-negative:

  1. 0 ≤ ‖u − t v‖² = ⟨u,u⟩ − ⟨u,v⟩⟨v,u⟩/‖v‖².
  2. Because ⟨v,u⟩ is the conjugate of ⟨u,v⟩, the middle term is |⟨u,v⟩|²/‖v‖².
  3. Multiply by ‖v‖² > 0: 0 ≤ ‖u‖²‖v‖² − |⟨u,v⟩|².
  4. Rearrange: |⟨u,v⟩|² ≤ ‖u‖²‖v‖². Take positive square roots.
  5. Equality holds exactly when u − t v = 0, so u is a scalar multiple of v; if one vector is 0, equality also holds.

Triangle inequality, Pythagoras, parallelogram

Start from the expansion of ‖u + v‖²:

‖u + v‖² = ‖u‖² + 2 Re⟨u,v⟩ + ‖v‖² ≤ ‖u‖² + 2‖u‖‖v‖ + ‖v‖² = (‖u‖ + ‖v‖)².

Re⟨u,v⟩ is the real part of the inner product; for real spaces it is just ⟨u,v⟩.

Taking square roots gives the triangle inequality:

‖u + v‖ ≤ ‖u‖ + ‖v‖

u and v are vectors; ‖·‖ is the norm from the inner product.

For nonzero u and v, the same bound makes cos θ = ⟨u,v⟩/(‖u‖‖v‖) safe: Cauchy–Schwarz gives |cos θ| ≤ 1, so the angle θ = arccos(⟨u,v⟩/(‖u‖‖v‖)) is defined.

When ⟨u,v⟩ = 0, the cross term vanishes and Pythagoras holds:

‖u + v‖² = ‖u‖² + ‖v‖²

u and v are orthogonal vectors; ⟨u,v⟩ = 0.

Adding the expansions of ‖u + v‖² and ‖u − v‖² cancels the cross terms and gives the parallelogram law:

‖u + v‖² + ‖u − v‖² = 2‖u‖² + 2‖v‖²

u and v are any vectors in the inner product space.

Worked example

Question. For u = (1, 2, 2) and v = (3, 0, 4), verify |⟨u,v⟩| ≤ ‖u‖‖v‖ and ‖u + v‖ ≤ ‖u‖ + ‖v‖. Do either equalities hold?

  1. ⟨u,v⟩ = 1×3 + 2×0 + 2×4 = 3 + 0 + 8 = 11.
  2. ‖u‖ = √(1² + 2² + 2²) = √9 = 3.
  3. ‖v‖ = √(3² + 0² + 4²) = √25 = 5.
  4. ‖u‖‖v‖ = 3 × 5 = 15, so 11 ≤ 15. Equality fails because u and v are not scalar multiples.
  5. u + v = (1+3, 2+0, 2+4) = (4, 2, 6).
  6. ‖u + v‖ = √(4² + 2² + 6²) = √56 ≈ 7.48.
  7. ‖u‖ + ‖v‖ = 3 + 5 = 8, so 7.48 ≤ 8. Equality fails.

Answer. Both inequalities hold strictly: 11 ≤ 15 and 7.48 ≤ 8.

Exam traps

  • Dropping the absolute value: ⟨u,v⟩ ≤ ‖u‖‖v‖ is true but too weak; it does not bound negative dot products and does not give the angle rule.
  • Forgetting v = 0: the proof divides by ‖v‖², so treat v = 0 separately.
  • Equal sign in the triangle inequality: equality needs u = c v with c ≥ 0; opposite directions give ‖u+v‖ = |‖u‖−‖v‖|, not the sum.
  • Confusing Cauchy–Schwarz with the triangle inequality: one bounds an inner product, the other bounds a sum of vectors.
  • Squaring both sides of an inequality without checking non-negativity: square only after both sides are known non-negative.

5.3 Orthogonal and orthonormal vectors; orthogonal complement

Perpendicular vectors behave like clean coordinate axes: each direction is independent of the others. An orthonormal set is a collection of mutual right angles with unit length, so it acts as a ready-made coordinate system. The orthogonal complement of a subspace gathers every vector at right angles to it. These ideas power least squares, QR and SVD.

You need: 5.1 for inner product and norm, 5.2 for the inequalities, and Unit 2 for basis, dimension, null space and direct sums.

Orthogonal and orthonormal sets

Fix an inner product ⟨u,v⟩ on a real vector space V; the norm is ‖u‖ = √⟨u,u⟩. A set {u1,…,uk} is orthogonal when ⟨ui,uj⟩ = 0 for every i ≠ j. It is orthonormal when also ⟨ui,ui⟩ = 1 for each i.

An orthogonal set of nonzero vectors cannot be dependent. Suppose c1u1 + … + ckuk = 0. Take the inner product of both sides with uj: every term dies except cj⟨uj,uj⟩, so cj‖uj‖² = 0. Since uj ≠ 0, cj = 0. All coefficients are zero, so the set is independent.

Independent and spanning its own span, the set is a basis of span{u1,…,uk}. This is why perpendicular axes are so useful: no vector is wasted, and dimension equals the number of vectors.

Coordinates in an orthonormal basis

Let {e1,…,en} be an orthonormal basis of V. Every u in V has one expansion u = Σ ciei. Take the inner product with ej: only i = j survives, giving cj = ⟨u,ej⟩. So u = Σ ⟨u,ei⟩ei.

A square matrix Q is orthogonal when QᵀQ = I, equivalently its columns form an orthonormal set. Orthogonal matrices preserve inner products: ⟨Qx,Qy⟩ = xᵀQᵀQy = xᵀy. Hence lengths and angles do not change, which is why rotations are orthogonal.

For any orthonormal set e1,…,em (not necessarily a full basis) and any u, Bessel's inequality holds: Σi=1m |⟨u,ei⟩|² ≤ ‖u‖². When the set is a full orthonormal basis the inequality becomes equality, Parseval's identity: Σ |⟨u,ei⟩|² = ‖u‖². The sum is the squared norm of the orthogonal projection of u onto span{e1,…,em}.

Orthogonal complement

For a subspace W of V, the orthogonal complement is W⊥ = {v in V : ⟨v,w⟩ = 0 for all w in W}. It is itself a subspace: if v1, v2 are perpendicular to every w, so is αv1 + βv2.

Only the zero vector lies in both. If x is in W and in W⊥, then ⟨x,x⟩ = 0, so x = 0. Thus W ∩ W⊥ = {0}, not the empty set.

Dimensions add: dim W + dim W⊥ = dim V = n. The proof picks a basis of W, extends it to a basis of V (Unit 2), and applies Gram–Schmidt to the whole extended basis, starting with the basis of W; the added vectors then become a basis of W⊥. Since the two subspaces meet only at 0, every v in V splits uniquely as v = w + z with w in W and z in W⊥. We write V = W ⊕ W⊥.

For an m × n real matrix A, the null space N(A) is the orthogonal complement of the row space. A vector x satisfies Ax = 0 exactly when x is orthogonal to every row of A. So row space and null space are perpendicular and their dimensions add to n, which is the rank–nullity theorem again.

Worked example

Question. In ℝ³ let W = span{(1, 1, 0)}. Find W⊥ and check dim W + dim W⊥ = 3. Also verify that e₁ = (1, 1, 0)/√2, e₂ = (1, −1, 0)/√2, e₃ = (0, 0, 1) is orthonormal, and expand (2, 0, 3) in this basis.

  1. W = {t(1, 1, 0) : t in ℝ}. A vector x = (x, y, z) lies in W⊥ exactly when ⟨x, (1, 1, 0)⟩ = x + y = 0, so y = −x. Thus W⊥ = {(x, −x, z)} = span{(1, −1, 0), (0, 0, 1)}.
  2. dim W = 1 and dim W⊥ = 2, so dim W + dim W⊥ = 1 + 2 = 3 = dim ℝ³. The only common vector is 0.
  3. Check the three vectors: ‖e₁‖ = √(1² + 1²)/√2 = 1, ‖e₂‖ = √(1² + (−1)²)/√2 = 1, ‖e₃‖ = 1. Their pairwise dot products are 0, so the set is orthonormal.
  4. For u = (2, 0, 3), c₁ = ⟨u, e₁⟩ = (2 + 0 + 0)/√2 = √2 ≈ 1.414, c₂ = ⟨u, e₂⟩ = (2 − 0 + 0)/√2 = √2 ≈ 1.414, c₃ = ⟨u, e₃⟩ = 3.
  5. Check: √2 e₁ + √2 e₂ + 3e₃ = (1, 1, 0) + (1, −1, 0) + (0, 0, 3) = (2, 0, 3).

Answer. W⊥ = span{(1, −1, 0), (0, 0, 1)}, dim W + dim W⊥ = 1 + 2 = 3, and (2, 0, 3) = 1.414 e₁ + 1.414 e₂ + 3.0 e₃.

Exam traps

  • An orthogonal set containing the zero vector is not independent; the independence theorem needs the word nonzero.
  • W ∩ W⊥ is {0}, not the empty set. The zero vector lies in every subspace.
  • Orthogonal means pairwise dot product zero; orthonormal also requires each vector to have length 1. A perpendicular basis of length-5 vectors is orthogonal but not orthonormal.
  • Bessel is an inequality for any orthonormal set. Equality for a particular u holds exactly when u lies in span{e₁,…,e_m}; for a full orthonormal basis, Parseval's identity gives equality for every u.
  • For a matrix, the null space is the orthogonal complement of the row space, not of the column space.
  • Examiners often ask for dim W⊥. It is n − dim W, not n − 1.

5.4 Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization process

Gram–Schmidt turns any basis into an orthonormal basis. Fix e₁ along u₁. For each later vector, subtract its parts along the unit directions already fixed, then scale what remains to length one. The result is a set of perpendicular unit vectors that spans the same space. Orthonormal coordinates make projections, least-squares equations and QR factorization simple. Engineers use the process in computer graphics, signal processing and statistics, and it is the first algorithm in most numerical linear algebra courses.

You need: inner products and norms from Chapter 5.1, orthonormal sets from Chapter 5.3, and basis and span from Unit 2.

The step-by-step recipe

Start with a basis u₁, …, uₙ of a real inner-product space. Write ⟨x, y⟩ for the inner product and ‖x‖ = √⟨x, x⟩ for the length. The output is an orthonormal basis e₁, …, eₙ: each ‖eᵢ‖ = 1 and ⟨eᵢ, eⱼ⟩ = 0 for i ≠ j.

v₁ = u₁, e₁ = v₁/‖v₁‖; for k = 2, …, n: vₖ = uₖ − Σ (i = 1 to k−1) ⟨uₖ, eᵢ⟩ eᵢ, then eₖ = vₖ/‖vₖ‖

uₖ is the k-th input basis vector; eᵢ is the i-th unit direction already fixed; ⟨uₖ, eᵢ⟩ is a scalar coefficient; vₖ is the part of uₖ left after the earlier directions are removed; ‖vₖ‖ is its length.

  1. Set v₁ = u₁ and normalize: e₁ = v₁/‖v₁‖.
  2. For k = 2, subtract the shadow along e₁: v₂ = u₂ − ⟨u₂, e₁⟩e₁, then e₂ = v₂/‖v₂‖.
  3. For k = 3, subtract the shadows along e₁ and e₂: v₃ = u₃ − ⟨u₃, e₁⟩e₁ − ⟨u₃, e₂⟩e₂, then e₃ = v₃/‖v₃‖.
  4. Continue to k = n. At each step use all earlier unit vectors e₁, …, eₖ₋₁.

The subtraction is a projection. The vector ⟨uₖ, eᵢ⟩eᵢ is the shadow of uₖ along the unit direction eᵢ. Removing every shadow leaves a vector perpendicular to all earlier eᵢ.

Why the subtraction works

Check orthogonality. For j < k, ⟨vₖ, eⱼ⟩ = ⟨uₖ, eⱼ⟩ − Σ (i = 1 to k−1) ⟨uₖ, eᵢ⟩⟨eᵢ, eⱼ⟩. Only the i = j term survives, and ⟨eⱼ, eⱼ⟩ = 1. So ⟨vₖ, eⱼ⟩ = ⟨uₖ, eⱼ⟩ − ⟨uₖ, eⱼ⟩ = 0. Thus vₖ is perpendicular to e₁, …, eₖ₋₁. Dividing by the positive number ‖vₖ‖ keeps it perpendicular to them and makes its length 1, so eₖ is orthogonal to e₁, …, eₖ₋₁ with ‖eₖ‖ = 1.

Check span. At step k, every eᵢ is a combination of u₁, …, uₖ. Conversely uₖ = vₖ + Σ (i = 1 to k−1) ⟨uₖ, eᵢ⟩eᵢ, and vₖ = ‖vₖ‖eₖ, so uₖ is a combination of e₁, …, eₖ. Hence span{e₁, …, eₖ} = span{u₁, …, uₖ}. The dimension rises by one at each step, so vₖ ≠ 0. If vₖ were zero, uₖ would lie in the previous span, contradicting independence.

At k = n the two spans are the whole space. The set e₁, …, eₙ is therefore an orthonormal basis.

Unnormalized form and a computer note

You can avoid early normalization. Set vₖ = uₖ − Σ (i = 1 to k−1) (⟨uₖ, vᵢ⟩ / ⟨vᵢ, vᵢ⟩) vᵢ, then eₖ = vₖ/‖vₖ‖. The coefficient is ⟨uₖ, vᵢ⟩/‖vᵢ‖²: it uses the earlier orthogonal vector vᵢ, not the original uᵢ. With unit vectors the coefficient is simply ⟨uₖ, eᵢ⟩. Both forms give the same eₖ in exact arithmetic.

In floating point, modified Gram–Schmidt subtracts one direction at a time: first remove the e₁ part from uₖ, then the e₂ part from the updated vector, and so on. It costs about the same and loses less accuracy on a computer.

5.5 Orthogonal projection and least squares solutions

A projection drops a vector perpendicularly onto a subspace and keeps the closest point. That single idea solves two problems. It tells you the part of b that lies inside a plane or a line. It also rescues an inconsistent system Ax = b: when no x fits exactly, least squares chooses x so that Ax is the closest reachable point to b. The same calculation fits a line to data, removes noise from sensor readings, and appears inside GPS and machine-learning code.

You need: inner products and norms (5.1), orthogonal complements (5.3), Gram–Schmidt (5.4), column space (Unit 2), and solving square systems (Unit 1).

Projection onto a line or a subspace

Take a vector b and a subspace W. The point of W closest to b is the orthogonal projection of b on W, written p. If W is the line through one nonzero vector a, then p is the part of b along a:

p = (⟨b,a⟩ / ⟨a,a⟩) a = (⟨b,a⟩ / ‖a‖²) a

b and a are vectors, a ≠ 0; ⟨b,a⟩ is their inner product, a single number; ‖a‖ is the length of a, so ‖a‖² = ⟨a,a⟩.

The leftover e = b − p is perpendicular to a. The vectors p and e form a right triangle with b, so ‖b‖² = ‖p‖² + ‖e‖².

For a subspace with orthonormal basis e₁, …, ek, add the parts along each basis vector:

p = Σ<sub>i=1</sub><sup>k</sup> ⟨b,e<sub>i</sub>⟩ e<sub>i</sub> = QQᵀb

ei are orthonormal basis vectors of W; Q is the n×k matrix with the ei as columns; Qᵀb gives the k coefficients.

Best approximation: for every w in W with w ≠ p, ‖b−p‖ < ‖b−w‖. Write b−w = (b−p)+(p−w). The vector p−w lies in W, and b−p is orthogonal to W, so Pythagoras gives ‖b−w‖² = ‖b−p‖² + ‖p−w‖² > ‖b−p‖².

The projection matrix P = QQᵀ has three properties: P² = P, Pᵀ = P, and P projects every vector onto col(Q). The first follows from QᵀQ = I, since P² = QQᵀQQᵀ = Q(QᵀQ)Qᵀ = QQᵀ = P.

Least squares and the normal equations

When Ax = b has no solution, choose x to make ‖Ax − b‖ as small as possible. This is the least squares problem. As x runs over all k-vectors, Ax fills the column space of A. The best possible Ax is therefore the projection p of b onto col(A).

At the minimum the residual e = b − Ax is orthogonal to col(A). Every column of A lies in col(A), so Aᵀe = 0. Hence Aᵀ(b−Ax) = 0, which gives the normal equations:

AᵀAx = Aᵀb

A is m×k; x is the k×1 vector of unknowns; b is m×1. AᵀA is k×k and Aᵀb is k×1.

If the columns of A are independent, AᵀA is invertible and the solution x̂ = (AᵀA)⁻¹Aᵀb is unique. If columns are dependent, AᵀA has no inverse and many x can give the same best p.

  1. Form AᵀA and Aᵀb.
  2. Solve the k×k system AᵀAx = Aᵀb.
  3. Compute the projection p = Ax̂.
  4. Compute the residual e = b − p.
  5. Check Aᵀe = 0. If A has orthonormal columns, AᵀA = I and x̂ = Aᵀb.

What the answer means

The vector x̂ holds the coefficients of the fit, not the data values. In a line fit x̂ = (c₀, c₁)ᵀ. The vector p = Ax̂ holds the predicted y-values at the given x-values. The residual e = b − p holds the errors: observed minus predicted.

Least squares minimizes the sum of squared errors ‖e‖² = e₁² + … + em². A zero residual means the data lie exactly on the model. A nonzero residual is normal and does not mean the arithmetic is wrong.

Check your answer with two tests. First, Aᵀe = 0: the residual is perpendicular to every column. Second, p lies in col(A) because p = Ax̂. These tests catch most sign and algebra slips. The residual sum of squares is the smallest possible value of ‖Ax−b‖². It measures how much of b is outside col(A). If the model is rich enough to contain b, e = 0 and the system is consistent.

5.6 QR decomposition

QR decomposition writes a matrix A as Q times R. Q has orthonormal columns. R is upper triangular. The columns of Q come from running Gram–Schmidt on the columns of A. R stores the numbers used in that run. It exists so that least-squares problems and repeated solves can avoid forming AᵀA. Numerical libraries use it for regression, signal fitting and computer graphics. Only a few universities teach this chapter.

You need: Gram–Schmidt and least squares.

Building Q and R

Take A with columns a₁,…,aₙ in ℝᵐ. Run Gram–Schmidt from 5.4. At step j, subtract from aⱼ its parts along q₁,…,qⱼ₋₁: vⱼ = aⱼ − Σᵢ₌₁ʲ⁻¹ ⟨aⱼ,qᵢ⟩ qᵢ. Then qⱼ = vⱼ/‖vⱼ‖. The numbers subtracted at step j are the off-diagonal entries rᵢⱼ = ⟨aⱼ, qᵢ⟩ for i < j. The diagonal entry rⱼⱼ = ‖vⱼ‖ is the length used to normalise, not a number subtracted.

For i > j set rᵢⱼ = 0. Diagonal entries are positive because vⱼ ≠ 0 when columns are independent. The thin form has Q m×n and R n×n. The full form adds m−n extra orthonormal columns to Q and zero rows below R.

A = QR,   rᵢⱼ = ⟨aⱼ, qᵢ⟩ (i < j),   rⱼⱼ = ‖vⱼ‖ > 0

A is m×n with independent columns; Q is m×n with orthonormal columns; R is n×n upper triangular; aⱼ is column j of A; qᵢ is column i of Q; vⱼ is the Gram–Schmidt vector before normalising.

Least squares

Because QᵀQ = I, AᵀA = (QR)ᵀ(QR) = RᵀQᵀQR = RᵀR. The normal equations AᵀAx = Aᵀb from 5.5 become RᵀRx = RᵀQᵀb. Cancel Rᵀ when R is invertible: Rx = Qᵀb. R is upper triangular, so solve by back substitution. This avoids forming AᵀA, which can square the condition number and lose accuracy.

Rx = Qᵀb

x is the least-squares solution; R is n×n upper triangular; Qᵀb is n×1; b is the m×1 data vector.

A QR least-squares solve is two steps: form y = Qᵀb, then back-substitute Rx = y. The residual is ‖Ax − b‖. When columns are independent, R is invertible. If columns are dependent, R has a zero on the diagonal and thin QR with positive diagonal does not exist. In practice, Qᵀb is one matrix-vector product. Back substitution costs about n²/2 operations.

  • Using aᵢ instead of qᵢ in rᵢⱼ = ⟨aⱼ,qᵢ⟩; the second vector must be the orthonormal one.
  • Writing A = RQ or Q = AR; the triangular factor multiplies on the right.

5.7 Singular value decomposition (SVD)

Every matrix, even a rectangular one, can be written as a rotation, a stretch along perpendicular axes, and another rotation. That is the singular value decomposition: A = UΣVᵀ. It exists for every real matrix, so it does what diagonalization does for symmetric matrices, but without needing A to be square or symmetric. Use it to compress data, solve ill-conditioned systems, fit least-squares models, and read rank. Exams usually ask you to compute the factors from AᵀA and to read rank and condition number from the singular values.

You need eigenvalues and the spectral theorem from Unit 4, and orthonormal vectors from chapter 5.3.

The factorization

The SVD writes any real m × n matrix A as A = UΣVᵀ. U is m × m orthogonal, so UᵀU = I. V is n × n orthogonal, so VᵀV = I. Σ is m × n and has σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … ≥ 0 on its main diagonal; every other entry is zero. The numbers σᵢ are the singular values of A. Orthogonal matrices preserve lengths and angles, so U and V are rotations or reflections. Σ does the stretching: it multiplies the first coordinate by σ₁, the second by σ₂, and so on.

A = UΣVᵀ

A is the m × n matrix being factorized; U is m × m orthogonal; Σ is m × n with σ₁ ≥ σ₂ ≥ … ≥ σ_min(m,n) ≥ 0 on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere; V is n × n orthogonal; Vᵀ is the transpose of V.

Why does this always work? The matrix AᵀA is n × n, real, and symmetric. Also xᵀAᵀA x = ||Ax||² ≥ 0, so AᵀA is positive semidefinite. By the spectral theorem from Unit 4, it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors v₁,…,vₙ with real eigenvalues λ₁ ≥ … ≥ λₙ ≥ 0. Set σᵢ = √λᵢ. These are the singular values. For each σᵢ > 0 define uᵢ = Avᵢ/σᵢ. Then uᵢᵀuⱼ = (vᵢᵀAᵀAvⱼ)/(σᵢσⱼ) = (λⱼ vᵢᵀvⱼ)/(σᵢσⱼ) = 0 when i ≠ j, and 1 when i = j. So the uᵢ are orthonormal. If A has fewer nonzero singular values than m, fill the remaining columns of U with an orthonormal basis of the left null space, null(Aᵀ). Thus A = UΣVᵀ holds.

Computing the factors

Compute an SVD in four steps.

  1. Form AᵀA. It is n × n, symmetric, and positive semidefinite.
  2. Find its eigenvalues λ₁ ≥ λ₂ ≥ … ≥ λₙ ≥ 0 and choose orthonormal eigenvectors v₁,…,vₙ. Put them as columns of V.
  3. Set σᵢ = √λᵢ. These go on the diagonal of Σ in the same order; zeros go after the positive values.
  4. For each σᵢ > 0 set uᵢ = Avᵢ/σᵢ. If fewer than m such vectors appear, add an orthonormal basis of null(Aᵀ) to complete U.

The order matters. Column i of U, column i of V, and σᵢ belong to the same triple. If you reorder the singular values, reorder both columns in the same way. You may also compute A Aᵀ to get U directly; its nonzero eigenvalues are the same σᵢ². AᵀA is usually better for V because A has n columns.

  • Rank: rank A equals the number of nonzero singular values. Count σᵢ > 0, not the number of rows or columns.
  • Condition number: for a square invertible matrix, κ(A) = σ₁/σₙ. A large ratio means small input errors can become large solution errors. If σₙ = 0, the matrix is singular and κ is infinite.
  • Low-rank approximation: keeping only the first k terms, A ≈ σ₁u₁v₁ᵀ + … + σₖuₖvₖᵀ, gives the best rank-k approximation in the 2-norm (Eckart–Young).

Worked example

Question. Find the singular value decomposition of A = [[3,0],[4,5]]. Give U, Σ, V, the singular values to three decimal places, and rank A.

  1. Compute AᵀA. Aᵀ = [[3,4],[0,5]], so AᵀA = [[25,20],[20,25]].
  2. Find eigenvalues from det(AᵀA − λI) = (25−λ)² − 20² = λ² − 50λ + 225 = 0. The roots are λ₁ = 45 and λ₂ = 5.
  3. The singular values are σ₁ = √45 = 3√5 ≈ 6.708 and σ₂ = √5 ≈ 2.236. They are already in descending order.
  4. For λ₁ = 45 solve (AᵀA − 45I)v = 0, giving v₁ = (1,1)/√2. For λ₂ = 5 solve (AᵀA − 5I)v = 0, giving v₂ = (1,−1)/√2. Thus V = (1/√2)[[1,1],[1,−1]].
  5. Compute u₁ = Av₁/σ₁ = (3/√2, 9/√2)/(3√5) = (1,3)/√10. Compute u₂ = Av₂/σ₂ = (3/√2, −1/√2)/√5 = (3,−1)/√10. Thus U = (1/√10)[[1,3],[3,−1]].
  6. Set Σ = diag(3√5, √5). Both singular values are nonzero, so rank A = 2. The product UΣVᵀ returns [[3,0],[4,5]].

Answer. The SVD is A = UΣVᵀ with U = (1/√10)[[1,3],[3,−1]], Σ = diag(3√5, √5), V = (1/√2)[[1,1],[1,−1]], σ₁ ≈ 6.708, σ₂ ≈ 2.236, and rank A = 2.

Exam traps

  • Taking eigenvalues of A itself instead of AᵀA. That shortcut is valid only for symmetric positive semidefinite matrices; A = [[0,1],[0,0]] has eigenvalues 0, 0 but singular values 1, 0.
  • Sorting singular values but not reordering U and V. The i-th column of U, the i-th column of V, and σᵢ form one triple.
  • Using only AᵀA when m > n and forgetting the left null space. U must be m × m, so fill missing columns with an orthonormal basis of null(Aᵀ).
  • Calling rank the number of rows, columns, or eigenvalues. Rank is the count of nonzero singular values.
  • Dividing by zero when σᵢ = 0. Leave that uᵢ out of the formula Avᵢ/σᵢ and complete U from the left null space; the condition number is infinite.

Analyze Give a 2 × 2 matrix whose eigenvalues are both 0 but whose singular values are 1 and 0. Why does this not contradict the SVD? 5 marks

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. SVD drives principal component analysis, recommender systems, latent semantic analysis, and noise filtering. Practical libraries compute it by bidiagonalization and iterative QR, not by forming AᵀA, because forming AᵀA squares the condition number. Takeaways

  1. An inner product is the single extra rule that gives a vector space the ideas of length, distance and angle.
  2. Cauchy–Schwarz is the engine behind the triangle inequality, the angle formula and every bound on projection error.
  3. Orthonormal vectors behave like a clean coordinate system: coefficients are found by dot products, with no equations to solve.
  4. Gram–Schmidt is bookkeeping, not magic: each new vector loses its parts along the directions already fixed.
  5. The closest point of a subspace to b is the orthogonal projection of b, and its error vector is perpendicular to the subspace.
  6. Least squares replaces the unsolvable Ax = b by the always-solvable normal equations AᵀAx = Aᵀb.
  7. QR decomposition is Gram–Schmidt written in matrix form, and it is the numerically safe way to solve least squares.
  8. SVD works for every matrix, square or rectangular, and orders the directions by how much the matrix stretches them.
  9. The ratio σ_max / σ_min, the condition number, tells you how much a linear system can magnify round-off error.

Glossary

Bessel's inequality
For an orthonormal set u₁, …, uₖ, Σ ⟨v, uᵢ⟩² ≤ ‖v‖², with equality for a basis.
Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
|⟨u, v⟩| ≤ ‖u‖‖v‖ for all vectors u and v.
Condition number
κ(A) = σ_max / σ_min for an invertible matrix, measuring sensitivity to small changes in data.
Distance
d(u, v) = ‖u − v‖, the length of the straight segment between the two vectors.
Dot product
The standard inner product on Rⁿ, u · v = u₁v₁ + … + uₙvₙ.
Gram–Schmidt process
An algorithm converting a basis into an orthonormal basis by subtracting projections step by step.
Inner product
A symmetric, additive, positively definite pairing ⟨u, v⟩ that produces a scalar from two vectors.
Least squares solution
Any x̂ minimising the residual length ‖b − A x̂‖.
Low-rank approximation
A truncated SVD sum Aₖ = Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ σᵢ uᵢ vᵢᵀ, optimal among matrices of rank k.
Norm
The non-negative number ‖v‖ = √⟨v, v⟩ giving the length of v.
Normal equations
AᵀA x = Aᵀb, the square system solved to get least squares fits.
Orthogonal
Having inner product zero; written u ⊥ v.
Orthogonal complement
W⊥, the set of all vectors orthogonal to every vector of the subspace W.
Orthogonal projection
The shadow proj_W b of b on W, the unique vector of W whose difference with b is in W⊥.
Orthonormal
Both orthogonal to each other and of unit length.
Orthonormal basis
A basis whose vectors are orthonormal, so coordinates are found by inner products alone.
Parallelogram law
‖u + v‖² + ‖u − v‖² = 2‖u‖² + 2‖v‖², true in every inner product space.
Parseval's identity
For an orthonormal basis, ‖v‖² = Σ ⟨v, uᵢ⟩²; energy is preserved by the coordinates.
Pythagoras theorem (vectors)
If ⟨u, v⟩ = 0 then ‖u + v‖² = ‖u‖² + ‖v‖².
QR decomposition
A factorisation A = QR with Q having orthonormal columns and R upper triangular.
Rank
The dimension of the column space; the number of nonzero singular values or nonzero pivots.
Singular value
A non-negative number σᵢ = √λᵢ, where λᵢ is an eigenvalue of AᵀA.
Singular value decomposition
The factorisation A = UΣVᵀ valid for every real matrix.
Triangle inequality
‖u + v‖ ≤ ‖u‖ + ‖v‖, the norm version of 'a straight line is shortest'.
Unit vector
A vector whose norm is 1; any nonzero v gives the unit vector v / ‖v‖.
