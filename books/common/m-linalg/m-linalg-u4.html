4.1 Characteristic polynomial, eigenvalues and eigenvectors

A square matrix A turns most vectors into vectors pointing in a new direction. A few special vectors keep their line: A only scales them. That scale factor is an eigenvalue and the vector is an eigenvector. The equation Ax = λx hunts for these directions, and it collapses into a polynomial equation, det(A − λI) = 0, whose roots are the eigenvalues. Engineers use them to find vibration frequencies, search engines use the dominant one to rank pages, and data analysts use them to compress features. This chapter shows how to compute them by hand.

You need: determinants and the meaning of a singular matrix from Unit 1 (Matrices and Systems of Linear Equations), and null space and basis from Unit 2 (Vector Spaces).

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors defined

Take a square matrix A of order n, with real or complex entries. Multiply A by a column vector x of length n and you get another vector Ax. Most choices of x come out tilted. Some do not.

Definition. A scalar λ is an eigenvalue of A if there is a nonzero vector x with Ax = λx. Such an x is an eigenvector of A belonging to λ.

Read the equation as a sentence: transforming x by the matrix A gives the same vector as scaling x by the number λ. The direction survives; the length changes by |λ|.

x must be nonzero. The zero vector obeys A0 = λ0 for every λ, so it carries no information, and the definition excludes it.

  • λ = 1: x is untouched, a fixed direction.
  • λ = 0: A sends a nonzero vector to 0, so A is singular and has no inverse.
  • λ negative: the arrow reverses. |λ| > 1 stretches, |λ| < 1 shrinks.

For a diagonal matrix the standard basis vectors are eigenvectors. For A = diag(2, 3), A(1, 0) = (2, 0) = 2(1, 0) and A(0, 1) = (0, 3) = 3(0, 1), so 2 and 3 are the eigenvalues.

Some syllabi call eigenvalues latent roots and eigenvectors latent vectors; latent means hidden. The meaning is identical. A real matrix may have complex eigenvalues; only over C is every matrix guaranteed n eigenvalues.

The characteristic polynomial and characteristic equation

Start from Ax = λx with x ≠ 0. Write λx as λIx, where I is the n×n identity matrix. Then (A − λI)x = 0.

This is a homogeneous system whose coefficient matrix is A − λI. It has a nonzero solution exactly when that matrix is singular, because singular matrices have a null space larger than {0} (Unit 1 and Unit 2). So λ is an eigenvalue exactly when det(A − λI) = 0.

p(λ) = det(A − λI) = (−1)<sup>n</sup>λ<sup>n</sup> + c<sub>n−1</sub>λ<sup>n−1</sup> + ⋯ + det A

p(λ) is the characteristic polynomial of A; λ is a scalar variable; I is the identity matrix of order n; cn−1 is some number built from the entries of A; det A is the constant term.

p is a polynomial of degree n. The equation det(A − λI) = 0 is the characteristic equation; its roots are exactly the eigenvalues, and over C there are n of them counted with multiplicity.

Sign convention. Some books use det(λI − A). That polynomial is (−1)n times the one above. The roots do not change; only the overall sign flips. Pick one convention and stay with it. This chapter uses det(A − λI).

Algebraic multiplicity. If λ₀ is a root repeated k times, then k is its algebraic multiplicity. A root that occurs once has multiplicity 1.

Two shortcuts. For 2×2 A, det(A − λI) = λ² − (trace A)λ + det A. For a triangular matrix, A − λI is also triangular, so its determinant is (a₁₁ − λ)(a₂₂ − λ)⋯(ann − λ): the eigenvalues are just the diagonal entries, each repeated as often as it appears.

Eigenspaces: solving (A − λI)x = 0

Fix one eigenvalue λ. Its eigenvectors are the nonzero vectors in the null space of A − λI. That null space is a subspace called the eigenspace of A for λ, written Eλ.

  1. Subtract λ from every diagonal entry of A to get A − λI.
  2. Row reduce A − λI. Never try to invert it; it is singular by construction.
  3. Solve (A − λI)x = 0 in terms of the free variables; each free variable gives a basis vector of Eλ.
  4. Keep the nonzero vectors only, and check one: compute Ax and λx and confirm they match.

The dimension of Eλ is the geometric multiplicity of λ. It is at least 1 and never exceeds the algebraic multiplicity. The gap between the two decides diagonalization; see 4.2 and 4.4.

3×3 example. A = [[1, 0, 0], [0, 2, 1], [0, 0, 2]] is upper triangular, so p(λ) = (1 − λ)(2 − λ)² and the eigenvalues are 1 (multiplicity 1) and 2 (multiplicity 2). For λ = 2, A − 2I = [[−1, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1], [0, 0, 0]], giving −x₁ = 0 and x₃ = 0 with x₂ free. So E₂ is spanned by (0, 1, 0): one dimension against algebraic multiplicity 2.

Complex case. For R = [[0, −1], [1, 0]], det(R − λI) = λ² + 1, so λ = ±i. Over R there is no eigenvalue. Over C, R(1, −i) = (i, 1) = i(1, −i), so (1, −i) belongs to i.

Worked example

Question. For A = [[4, 1], [2, 3]], find the characteristic polynomial, both eigenvalues, and one eigenvector for each eigenvalue. Verify each eigenvector.

  1. Form A − λI = [[4 − λ, 1], [2, 3 − λ]].
  2. Take the determinant: (4 − λ)(3 − λ) − (1)(2) = 12 − 4λ − 3λ + λ² − 2 = λ² − 7λ + 10.
  3. Solve λ² − 7λ + 10 = 0. The discriminant is 49 − 40 = 9, so λ = (7 ± 3)/2, giving λ = 5 and λ = 2.
  4. Cross-check with the shortcuts: trace = 4 + 3 = 7 = 5 + 2, and det = 12 − 2 = 10 = 5 × 2. Both match.
  5. For λ = 5: A − 5I = [[−1, 1], [2, −2]]. Row reduction gives the single equation −x₁ + x₂ = 0, so x₂ = x₁. Choose x = (1, 1).
  6. Verify: A(1, 1) = (4 + 1, 2 + 3) = (5, 5) = 5(1, 1). Correct.
  7. For λ = 2: A − 2I = [[2, 1], [2, 1]]. Row reduction gives 2x₁ + x₂ = 0, so x₂ = −2x₁. Choose x = (1, −2).
  8. Verify: A(1, −2) = (4 − 2, 2 − 6) = (2, −4) = 2(1, −2). Correct.
  9. The two eigenvectors (1, 1) and (1, −2) are not multiples of each other, so together they span R².

Answer. The characteristic polynomial is λ² − 7λ + 10, the eigenvalues are λ = 5 and λ = 2, and eigenvectors are (1, 1) for λ = 5 and (1, −2) for λ = 2.

Exam traps

  • The zero vector is never an eigenvector. It satisfies Ax = λx for every λ, so writing x = 0 as an answer loses marks.
  • Find λ first, then substitute that λ back into A − λI. Do not guess an eigenvector without verifying Ax = λx.
  • Sign convention: det(λI − A) gives (−1)ⁿ times the polynomial from det(A − λI). The roots are the same. If the question fixes a convention, do not swap it halfway.
  • A real matrix with a negative discriminant has complex eigenvalues. Write λ = ±i style answers over C; do not claim the matrix has no eigenvalues unless the field is stated as R.
  • Algebraic multiplicity is not the number of independent eigenvectors. For [[2, 1], [0, 2]] the eigenvalue 2 has multiplicity 2 but its eigenspace has dimension 1.

Analyze A is upper triangular of order 3 with diagonal entries 1, 2, 2, and the eigenspace for 2 has dimension 1. Explain what this tells you about the eigenvalues and about diagonalizing A. 5 marks

Analyze Show that 0 is an eigenvalue of A if and only if A is singular. 6 marks

If 0 is an eigenvalue, some nonzero x has Ax = 0x = 0, so the null space of A is larger than {0} and A is singular. Conversely, if A is singular, some nonzero x satisfies Ax = 0 = 0x, so 0 is an eigenvalue with eigenvector x.

4.2 Properties of eigenvalues and eigenvectors

Eigenvalues look like isolated numbers from det(A − λI)=0, but they obey simple bookkeeping rules. Their sum is the trace, their product is the determinant, and powers, inverses and polynomials transform each eigenvalue in the same way. These rules let you check a long calculation in seconds, read eigenvalues of triangular and diagonal matrices by eye, and predict the behaviour of repeated operations such as a Markov chain or a circuit simulated many times. Multiplicities then tell you how many independent eigenvectors are available, which decides whether diagonalization is possible.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for det(A − λI)=0, matrix algebra, and complex numbers.

Trace, determinant and transformed eigenvalues

For an n×n matrix A, tr(A) is the sum of its diagonal entries. List the eigenvalues as λ1, …, λn, repeating each eigenvalue according to its algebraic multiplicity, its repeat count as a root of the characteristic polynomial.

tr(A) = Σ<sub>i=1</sub><sup>n</sup> λ<sub>i</sub>,   det(A) = Π<sub>i=1</sub><sup>n</sup> λ<sub>i</sub>

λi are the eigenvalues with algebraic multiplicity; tr(A) is the trace; det(A) is the determinant. The formulas hold over real or complex numbers.

A diagonal matrix has eigenvalues equal to its diagonal entries. A triangular matrix also has eigenvalues equal to its diagonal entries; the off-diagonal entries do not change the characteristic polynomial.

Transformed matrices keep the same eigenvectors and change only the eigenvalue:

  • Ak has eigenvalues λik for every positive integer k.
  • A−1 has eigenvalues 1/λi when det(A) ≠ 0; if det(A)=0, no inverse exists.
  • p(A) has eigenvalues p(λi) for every polynomial p(t); p(A)=amAm+…+a0I.

These rules do not extend to sums: eigenvalues of A+B are not generally λi(A)+λi(B).

Similarity and special matrices

If B=P−1AP, then A and B are similar. They represent the same linear map in different bases (see Chapter 4.4). Their characteristic polynomials are equal: det(B−λI)=det(P−1(A−λI)P)=det(A−λI). So similar matrices have the same eigenvalues with the same algebraic multiplicities. An eigenvector v of A becomes P−1v for B.

Special matrices force eigenvalues onto known sets. Let H* mean conjugate transpose.

  • Orthogonal Q (QTQ=I) and unitary U (U*U=I): every eigenvalue satisfies |λ|=1, so it lies on the unit circle.
  • Hermitian H (H*=H) and real symmetric A (AT=A): all eigenvalues are real.
  • Real skew-symmetric A (AT=−A): every eigenvalue is 0 or purely imaginary, and non-real ones occur in conjugate pairs.
  • Normal N (N*N=NN*): eigenvectors belonging to different eigenvalues are orthogonal. Real symmetric, orthogonal and skew-symmetric matrices are normal.

The real symmetric case is the spectral theorem, developed in Chapter 4.5.

Multiplicity and independent eigenvectors

For an eigenvalue λ, the algebraic multiplicity a(λ) is its number of repeats as a root of the characteristic polynomial. The geometric multiplicity g(λ) is the dimension of the null space of A−λI; it counts independent eigenvectors for λ.

1 ≤ g(λ) ≤ a(λ)

g(λ)=dim null(A−λI); a(λ) is the algebraic multiplicity. Equality g(λ)=a(λ) for every eigenvalue is exactly the condition for diagonalizability.

A shortage g(λ)<a(λ) means there are too few independent eigenvectors for that eigenvalue. A repeated eigenvalue can still be diagonalizable when g(λ)=a(λ).

Distinct eigenvalues always give independent eigenvectors. One proof: take a shortest relation c1v1+…+cmvm=0 with distinct λi and nonzero ci. Apply A−λmI. The last term dies, leaving a shorter relation among v1,…,vm−1, a contradiction.

Worked example

Question. A 2×2 matrix A has eigenvalues 2 and 5. Find tr(A), det(A), the eigenvalues of A², the eigenvalues of A⁻¹, and the eigenvalues of p(A) for p(t)=t²−3t+1.

  1. Trace = sum with multiplicity: 2+5=7.
  2. Determinant = product with multiplicity: 2×5=10. Since 10 ≠ 0, A⁻¹ exists.
  3. A² eigenvalues: 2²=4 and 5²=25.
  4. A⁻¹ eigenvalues: 1/2=0.5 and 1/5=0.2.
  5. p(2)=2²−3×2+1=4−6+1=−1.
  6. p(5)=5²−3×5+1=25−15+1=11.

Answer. The trace is 7, the determinant is 10; A² has eigenvalues 4 and 25; A⁻¹ has eigenvalues 0.5 and 0.2; p(A) has eigenvalues −1 and 11.

Exam traps

  • Trace and determinant give only the sum and product of eigenvalues, not the individual eigenvalues, unless the matrix is 2×2 or you have extra information.
  • Eigenvalues of A+B are not generally the sums of eigenvalues of A and B; test with two simple 2×2 matrices.
  • For Ak and p(A), the eigenvalues become λk and p(λ), but the eigenvectors stay the same; do not replace eigenvectors by powers.
  • A−1 has eigenvalues 1/λ only when det(A) ≠ 0; a zero eigenvalue means no inverse.
  • A repeated eigenvalue does not automatically mean failure of diagonalization; check whether geometric multiplicity equals algebraic multiplicity.

4.3 Cayley-Hamilton theorem and minimal polynomial

Take a square matrix A. Its characteristic polynomial p(λ) = det(λI − A) is built from A, so A can be fed back into it: replace λ by A, and replace the constant term by a multiple of the identity. Cayley-Hamilton says the result is always the zero matrix. This free identity turns high powers of A into low powers, gives a formula for A⁻¹ when det A ≠ 0, and leads to the minimal polynomial, the smallest polynomial that kills A. Exams ask for verification on 2×2 and 3×3 matrices and for these two uses.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for det(A − λI) and eigenvalues, and Unit 1 for matrix multiplication and inverses.

The theorem and its meaning

The characteristic polynomial of an n×n matrix A is p(λ) = det(λI − A). Expanding the determinant gives a monic polynomial of degree n.

p(λ) = λ<sup>n</sup> + c<sub>n−1</sub>λ<sup>n−1</sup> + ⋯ + c<sub>1</sub>λ + c<sub>0</sub>

p(λ) is the characteristic polynomial of A; the ck are its coefficients; c0 = p(0) = det(−A) = (−1)n det A; cn−1 = −trace(A).

Some books write det(A − λI). That is (−1)np(λ), and it also vanishes at A, so the theorem below is unchanged.

A<sup>n</sup> + c<sub>n−1</sub>A<sup>n−1</sup> + ⋯ + c<sub>1</sub>A + c<sub>0</sub>I = 0

A is the n×n matrix; I is the n×n identity; 0 is the n×n zero matrix. This is the Cayley-Hamilton theorem: every square matrix satisfies its own characteristic polynomial.

The right side is a matrix. Powers of A are matrix products, and c0I means the scalar c0 on the diagonal. The theorem is surprising because A has infinitely many powers, yet one relation of degree n controls all of them: An is a combination of I, A, …, An−1, and multiplying by A reduces every higher power the same way.

For n = 2, p(λ) = λ² − (tr A)λ + det A, so A² − (tr A)A + (det A)I = 0. Write A = [[a, b], [c, d]] and expand: every entry cancels. That is the whole 2×2 verification.

Reducing powers and finding the inverse

Two exam uses follow from one rearrangement. Solve p(A) = 0 for the highest power.

A<sup>n</sup> = −c<sub>n−1</sub>A<sup>n−1</sup> − ⋯ − c<sub>1</sub>A − c<sub>0</sub>I

the symbols are as above; the right side uses only powers below n.

For a 2×2 matrix, A² = (tr A)A − (det A)I. Multiply by A to get A³ = (tr A)A² − (det A)A, then replace A² again. Every power ends as αA + βI.

For the inverse, suppose c0 ≠ 0. With p(λ) = det(λI − A), c0 = (−1)n det A, so this is det A ≠ 0. Move c0I across and factor out A.

A(A<sup>n−1</sup> + c<sub>n−1</sub>A<sup>n−2</sup> + ⋯ + c<sub>1</sub>I) = −c<sub>0</sub>I

the bracket is an n×n matrix; −c0I is a nonzero multiple of the identity, so A is invertible.

Multiply by A⁻¹ and divide by −c0. For n = 2, c1 = −tr A and c0 = det A, giving the formula below.

A⁻¹ = (1/det A)((tr A)I − A), det A ≠ 0

tr A is the sum of the diagonal entries; det A is the determinant; I is the 2×2 identity. The order matters: it is (tr A)I − A, not A − (tr A)I.

These formulas beat Gauss-Jordan for small matrices with simple entries, and they are expected when a question says 'hence find A⁻¹' after a Cayley-Hamilton check.

Annihilating and minimal polynomials

An annihilating polynomial of A is any nonzero polynomial q(λ) with q(A) = 0. The characteristic polynomial is one, by Cayley-Hamilton. Among all annihilating polynomials, choose one of least degree and make its leading coefficient 1. That is the minimal polynomial m(λ). It is unique.

  • m divides every annihilating polynomial, so m divides p.
  • m and p have the same roots, namely the eigenvalues of A. Only multiplicities can differ.
  • deg m ≤ n, and m = p exactly when every eigenvalue has the same multiplicity in m as in p, that is, when the largest Jordan block for each eigenvalue is as large as its algebraic multiplicity.

Diagonalization criterion: A is diagonalizable over F if and only if m(λ) splits into distinct linear factors over F, that is m(λ) = (λ − λ1)(λ − λ2)⋯(λ − λk) with distinct λi. The full test comes in 4.4; the minimal polynomial gives the quickest version.

When m is smaller than p, and when it is not:

  • A = 2I (2×2): p(λ) = (λ − 2)², but A − 2I = 0, so m(λ) = λ − 2.
  • A = diag(2, 2, 3): p(λ) = (λ − 2)²(λ − 3), and (A − 2I)(A − 3I) = 0, so m(λ) = (λ − 2)(λ − 3). The degree drops because eigenvalue 2 has two independent eigenvectors.
  • And when it is not: A = [[2, 1], [0, 2]] has p(λ) = (λ − 2)², A − 2I = [[0, 1], [0, 0]] ≠ 0, and (A − 2I)² = 0, so m(λ) = (λ − 2)². Here m = p. A repeated eigenvalue alone does not lower the degree; too few eigenvectors does.

Reading m from data: distinct eigenvalues force m = p. With repeated eigenvalues, test (A − λI), (A − λI)², … and multiply over the distinct eigenvalues until the product kills A.

Worked example

Question. For A = [[1, 2], [0, 3]]: (a) verify the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, (b) use it to find A⁻¹, (c) state the minimal polynomial.

  1. Characteristic polynomial: λI − A = [[λ − 1, −2], [0, λ − 3]], so p(λ) = (λ − 1)(λ − 3) = λ² − 4λ + 3. Check: trace = 1 + 3 = 4, determinant = 3.
  2. A² = [[1, 2], [0, 3]]·[[1, 2], [0, 3]] = [[1, 8], [0, 9]].
  3. p(A) = A² − 4A + 3I = [[1, 8], [0, 9]] − [[4, 8], [0, 12]] + [[3, 0], [0, 3]] = 0. Verified.
  4. Inverse: multiply p(A) = 0 by A⁻¹: A − 4I + 3A⁻¹ = 0.
  5. So 3A⁻¹ = 4I − A = [[4, 0], [0, 4]] − [[1, 2], [0, 3]] = [[3, −2], [0, 1]].
  6. A⁻¹ = (1/3)[[3, −2], [0, 1]] = [[1, −2/3], [0, 1/3]]. Check: det A = 3 ≠ 0.
  7. Minimal polynomial: eigenvalues 1 and 3 are distinct. Any annihilator vanishes at both, so m(λ) = (λ − 1)(λ − 3) = λ² − 4λ + 3 = p(λ).

Answer. p(A) = 0 for A = [[1, 2], [0, 3]], A⁻¹ = [[1, −2/3], [0, 1/3]], and the minimal polynomial is λ² − 4λ + 3, equal to the characteristic polynomial.

Exam traps

  • p(A) = 0 means the zero matrix. Writing p(λ) = 0 for every scalar λ is false; p has only the n eigenvalues as roots.
  • The minimal polynomial divides p but can be a proper factor. Check 2I or diag(2, 2, 3) before assuming m = p.
  • A repeated eigenvalue does not by itself make m smaller than p. The block [[2, 1], [0, 2]] has m = p = (λ − 2)².
  • In A⁻¹ = (1/det A)((tr A)I − A), reversing the order gives −A⁻¹. Signs are the commonest lost marks.
  • For n = 3, det(A − λI) = −p(λ). That polynomial also kills A, because −p(A) = 0. But it is not monic, its constant term is det A rather than (−1)³det A, and its λ² coefficient is +tr A rather than −tr A. Feeding these into the inverse formula flips signs. So expand det(λI − A), or multiply det(A − λI) by (−1)³ before reading coefficients.

4.4 Similar matrices and diagonalization

Two matrices can describe the same linear map in different bases. They are called similar, and one is P⁻¹AP for an invertible change-of-basis matrix P. Similar matrices share eigenvalues, trace, determinant and rank. Diagonalization asks for the best basis: one in which the map becomes a diagonal matrix. That happens exactly when the matrix has enough independent eigenvectors. The idea powers vibration analysis, Markov chains, and solving systems by decoupling them. This chapter gives the test, the construction of P and D, and the multiplicity condition that decides when diagonalization is possible.

You need: characteristic polynomials and eigenvectors from 4.1, eigenvalue bookkeeping from 4.2, and change of basis from Unit 3.

Similar matrices: same map, different basis

Two n×n matrices A and B are similar if B = P⁻¹AP for some invertible matrix P. The matrix P is a change-of-basis matrix: it rewrites vectors in a new basis. In that new basis, the same linear map has matrix B. Similarity is an equivalence relation: reflexive, symmetric and transitive.

B = P⁻¹AP

A and B are n×n matrices over the same field; P is invertible. B is the matrix of the same linear map as A in the basis formed by the columns of P.

Similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial: det(B − λI) = det(P⁻¹(A − λI)P) = det(A − λI). The determinant of a product splits, and det(P⁻¹) det(P) = 1. So B and A have the same eigenvalues with the same algebraic multiplicities.

  • The trace is unchanged: tr(B) = tr(P⁻¹AP) = tr(AP P⁻¹) = tr(A).
  • The determinant is unchanged: det(B) = det(P⁻¹) det(A) det(P) = det(A).
  • The rank and nullity are unchanged, because P and P⁻¹ are invertible.
  • The minimal polynomial is unchanged.
  • The geometric multiplicity of each eigenvalue is unchanged.

The converse is false: two matrices with the same eigenvalues need not be similar. For example, [[1,0],[0,1]] and [[1,1],[0,1]] both have eigenvalue 1 twice, but only the first is diagonal.

The diagonalization test

A matrix A is diagonalizable if there exists an invertible P and a diagonal D such that P⁻¹AP = D. Equivalently A = PDP⁻¹. The diagonal entries of D are the eigenvalues of A, and the columns of P are matching eigenvectors.

Theorem. An n×n matrix A is diagonalizable if and only if it has n linearly independent eigenvectors.

Proof in one direction: if A = PDP⁻¹ with D = diag(λ₁,…,λₙ), then AP = PD. Column i of AP is A times column i of P. Column i of PD is λᵢ times column i of P. So each column of P is an eigenvector. Since P is invertible, its columns are independent.

Other direction: if v₁,…,vₙ are independent eigenvectors with A vᵢ = λᵢ vᵢ, put P = [v₁ … vₙ] and D = diag(λ₁,…,λₙ). Then AP = PD, so P⁻¹AP = D.

The order rule: column i of P must be an eigenvector for the eigenvalue in position i of D. Swapping eigenvectors without swapping eigenvalues gives the wrong product.

Multiplicity condition. For every eigenvalue λ, algebraic multiplicity = geometric multiplicity. The algebraic multiplicity is its multiplicity as a root of the characteristic polynomial. The geometric multiplicity is dim N(A − λI) = n − rank(A − λI). The total of the geometric multiplicities must reach n.

Distinct eigenvalues are sufficient but not necessary. If all n eigenvalues are distinct, their eigenvectors are automatically independent, so A is diagonalizable. If an eigenvalue repeats, diagonalization may still work when its eigenspace is large enough. Some syllabi call the distinct-eigenvalue case regular eigenvalues.

A defective matrix

A matrix is defective if some eigenvalue has geometric multiplicity strictly less than its algebraic multiplicity. A defective matrix is not diagonalizable.

Example: A = [[1,1],[0,1]]. Its characteristic polynomial is (1−λ)², so λ = 1 has algebraic multiplicity 2. The matrix A − I = [[0,1],[0,0]] has rank 1, so its nullity is 2 − 1 = 1. The geometric multiplicity is 1. There is only one independent eigenvector, for example (1,0). We cannot build a 2×2 invertible P from eigenvectors.

This A is a shear. No change of basis turns a shear into a diagonal map, because a diagonal map scales both basis directions independently and cannot mix them.

Repeated eigenvalues do not always cause failure. A = [[2,0],[0,2]] has λ = 2 with algebraic multiplicity 2, and A − 2I = 0 has nullity 2, so it is already diagonal.

Worked example

Question. Diagonalize A = [[4, 1], [2, 3]] by finding an invertible P and a diagonal D with P⁻¹AP = D.

  1. Compute the characteristic polynomial: det(A − λI) = det([[4 − λ, 1], [2, 3 − λ]]) = (4 − λ)(3 − λ) − 2 = λ² − 7λ + 10.
  2. Solve λ² − 7λ + 10 = 0: (λ − 5)(λ − 2) = 0, so λ₁ = 5 and λ₂ = 2.
  3. For λ₁ = 5: A − 5I = [[−1, 1], [2, −2]]. The equation (A − 5I)v = 0 gives −x₁ + x₂ = 0, so x₂ = x₁. Choose v₁ = (1, 1).
  4. For λ₂ = 2: A − 2I = [[2, 1], [2, 1]]. The equation gives 2x₁ + x₂ = 0, so x₂ = −2x₁. Choose v₂ = (1, −2).
  5. Check independence: det P = det([[1, 1], [1, −2]]) = 1(−2) − 1(1) = −3 ≠ 0. The eigenvectors are independent.
  6. Form P with v₁, v₂ as columns and D with matching eigenvalues: P = [[1, 1], [1, −2]], D = [[5, 0], [0, 2]].
  7. Verify: P⁻¹ = (1/−3)[[−2, −1], [−1, 1]] = [[2/3, 1/3], [1/3, −1/3]]. Then AP = [[5, 2], [5, −4]] and P⁻¹AP = [[5, 0], [0, 2]] = D.

Answer. P = [[1, 1], [1, −2]] and D = diag(5, 2), so P⁻¹AP = D.

Exam traps

  • Order mismatch: column i of P must pair with diagonal entry i of D.
  • A repeated eigenvalue is not automatic failure; check the geometric multiplicity.
  • Do not confuse algebraic multiplicity (root count) with geometric multiplicity (eigenspace dimension).
  • A diagonalizable matrix need not have distinct eigenvalues; the identity matrix is the simplest counterexample.
  • P must be invertible: independent eigenvectors are required, not just any list of eigenvectors.

4.5 Orthogonal diagonalization of real symmetric matrices

A real symmetric matrix equals its transpose. Covariance matrices, graph adjacency matrices, and stiffness matrices are symmetric. For such matrices, eigenvectors can be chosen orthonormal, so one orthogonal matrix Q diagonalizes A. This is orthogonal diagonalization. It turns coupled equations into independent directions. Engineers use it for principal axes, vibration modes, and data alignment. Only a few universities teach this as a separate topic; the calculation is short, but the normalization rules are strict.

You need: eigenvalues and eigenvectors from 4.1, diagonalization from 4.4, and Gram-Schmidt from Unit 5.

The spectral theorem

For a real symmetric matrix A, the spectral theorem gives three facts. Every eigenvalue is real. Eigenvectors from different eigenvalues are orthogonal. There is an orthonormal basis of Rⁿ made of eigenvectors of A.

The proof of orthogonality uses the dot product. For symmetric A, (A x)ᵀ y = xᵀ(A y). Put A x = λ x and A y = μ y. Then λ xᵀy = μ xᵀy, so (λ − μ)xᵀy = 0. If λ ≠ μ, then xᵀy = 0.

This is stronger than ordinary diagonalization. A general real matrix may have complex eigenvalues, and may have too few real eigenvectors to form a basis. A real symmetric matrix always has a full set of real eigenvectors, and they can be made perpendicular.

Building Q and D

Arrange orthonormal eigenvectors as columns of Q. Then QᵀQ = I, so Q is orthogonal. Put matching eigenvalues on the diagonal of D. Then QᵀAQ = D, equivalently A = QDQᵀ.

  1. Find eigenvalues from det(A − λI) = 0.
  2. For each eigenvalue, find a basis of its eigenspace, the null space of A − λI.
  3. If an eigenvalue repeats, apply Gram-Schmidt inside that eigenspace.
  4. Normalize every vector to length 1.
  5. Put these vectors as columns of Q; put eigenvalues in the same order in D.
  6. Check QᵀQ = I and QᵀAQ = D.

Column order in Q must match diagonal order in D. Swapping both is allowed; swapping only one changes the product.

QᵀAQ = D

Q is orthogonal with orthonormal eigenvectors as columns; D is diagonal with eigenvalues in matching order.

Worked example

Question. Orthogonally diagonalize A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]: find an orthogonal Q and a diagonal D with QᵀAQ = D.

  1. det(A − λI) = (2−λ)² − 1.
  2. (2−λ)² = 1, so 2−λ = ±1. Hence λ = 1 or 3.
  3. λ = 1: A − I = [[1, 1], [1, 1]], so x + y = 0. Choose v₁ = (1, −1).
  4. Normalize: ‖v₁‖ = √2, u₁ = (1/√2, −1/√2).
  5. λ = 3: A − 3I = [[−1, 1], [1, −1]], so −x + y = 0. Choose v₂ = (1, 1).
  6. Normalize: ‖v₂‖ = √2, u₂ = (1/√2, 1/√2).
  7. Q = [[1/√2, 1/√2], [−1/√2, 1/√2]], D = diag(1, 3).
  8. Check QᵀQ = I (columns are unit and perpendicular), so Q is orthogonal.
  9. Check QᵀAQ = D directly: AQ = QD = [[1/√2, 3/√2], [−1/√2, 3/√2]], so QᵀAQ = D. This follows from matching eigenvectors to diagonal entries, not from QᵀQ = I alone.

Answer. Q = [[1/√2, 1/√2], [−1/√2, 1/√2]] and D = diag(1, 3), so QᵀAQ = D.

Exam traps

  • Do not stop at independent eigenvectors. Normalize every vector; Q must satisfy QᵀQ = I.
  • For a repeated eigenvalue, independent eigenvectors may not be orthogonal. Apply Gram-Schmidt inside that eigenspace.
  • Match the column order of Q to the diagonal order of D.
  • A non-symmetric real matrix can have complex eigenvalues; the real symmetric guarantee does not apply.

4.6 Quadratic forms and reduction to canonical form

A quadratic form is a homogeneous degree-2 expression such as 2x² + 2xy + 2y². Written as xᵀAx with A symmetric, it hides a matrix whose eigenvalues control the shape. Change variables by an orthogonal matrix, and the cross terms vanish: the form becomes λ₁y₁² + ⋯ + λₙyₙ². This is used in coordinate geometry to rotate conics, in mechanics for kinetic energy and inertia, and in data science for variance along principal directions. A non-orthogonal change gives Lagrange reduction to a sum of squares.

You need: eigenvalues and eigenvectors (Chapter 4.1), diagonalization (Chapter 4.4), orthogonal diagonalization of symmetric matrices (Chapter 4.5), and basic coordinate geometry; orthogonality from Unit 5 helps.

Quadratic form and its symmetric matrix

A real quadratic form in n variables is a homogeneous polynomial of degree 2: every term has total degree two. In two variables it looks like ax² + 2hxy + by². The same expression is written xᵀAx with A symmetric. The matrix is [[a, h], [h, b]]. The diagonal entries a and b are the coefficients of x² and y². The off-diagonal entry h is half the coefficient of xy. In n variables, Q(x) = Σ aᵢᵢxᵢ² + 2Σᵢ<ⱼ aᵢⱼxᵢxⱼ. If a matrix B is not symmetric, replace it by (B+Bᵀ)/2; the value xᵀBx does not change because xᵀBx is a scalar equal to its transpose. Example: Q = 2x² + 2xy + 2y² has A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]]. The cross coefficient is 2, so h = 1. This matrix stores all coefficients in a form that eigenvalues can read.

Q(x) = xᵀAx = Σ aᵢᵢxᵢ² + 2Σᵢ<ⱼ aᵢⱼxᵢxⱼ

x is the column of variables; A = (aᵢⱼ) is symmetric; aᵢᵢ is the coefficient of xᵢ²; for i < j, aᵢⱼ is half the coefficient of xᵢxⱼ.

Orthogonal reduction to canonical form

Because A is real symmetric, Chapter 4.5 gives an orthogonal matrix P with PᵀAP = D = diag(λ₁, …, λₙ). The λᵢ are eigenvalues and the columns of P are orthonormal eigenvectors. Put x = Py. Then Q(x) = xᵀAx = yᵀPᵀAPy = λ₁y₁² + ⋯ + λₙyₙ². No cross terms remain. This is the orthogonal canonical form, also called reduction to principal axes.

  1. Form the symmetric matrix A from the coefficients.
  2. Solve det(A − λI) = 0 for the eigenvalues λ₁, …, λₙ.
  3. Find orthonormal eigenvectors; use Gram-Schmidt if an eigenvalue repeats.
  4. Place the eigenvectors as columns of P in the same order as the eigenvalues.
  5. Substitute x = Py to get λ₁y₁² + ⋯ + λₙyₙ².

A different reduction, Lagrange or congruent reduction, uses x = Cy with invertible C not necessarily orthogonal. Complete squares directly to get a sum of squares. The coefficients need not be eigenvalues, but the number of positive, negative and zero coefficients is fixed by Sylvester's law. Chapter 4.7 uses this for rank, index and signature. Some syllabi call either result canonical form or normal form.

General second-degree equation

The general second-degree equation is ax² + 2hxy + by² + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0. Its quadratic part has matrix A = [[a, h], [h, b]]. A rotation x = Py with P orthogonal removes the xy term and turns the quadratic part into λ₁y₁² + λ₂y₂². The linear terms change to 2g′y₁ + 2f′y₂, and c stays. If λ₁ and λ₂ are nonzero, complete squares: λ₁(y₁ + g′/λ₁)² + λ₂(y₂ + f′/λ₂)² = constant. A translation Y₁ = y₁ + g′/λ₁, Y₂ = y₂ + f′/λ₂ removes the linear terms. The result is λ₁Y₁² + λ₂Y₂² = K, a standard conic centred at the new origin. If one eigenvalue is zero, say λ₂ = 0, a linear term 2f′y₂ cannot be removed by translation when f′ ≠ 0, and the curve is a parabola. If f′ = 0, the equation gives a pair of parallel lines (or a single line, or no real points). Chapter 4.7 classifies these cases. For an ellipse, λ₁ and λ₂ have the same sign; for a hyperbola they have opposite signs.

Worked example

Question. Reduce Q(x, y) = 2x² + 2xy + 2y² to canonical form by an orthogonal change of variables. Give the orthogonal matrix and the canonical form.

  1. The cross coefficient is 2, so h = 1. The symmetric matrix is A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]].
  2. det(A − λI) = (2 − λ)² − 1 = λ² − 4λ + 3 = (λ − 3)(λ − 1). Eigenvalues: λ₁ = 3 and λ₂ = 1.
  3. For λ₁ = 3: (A − 3I)v = 0 gives −v₁ + v₂ = 0, so v₁ = v₂. Take v = (1, 1). Normalize: q₁ = (1/√2, 1/√2).
  4. For λ₂ = 1: (A − I)v = 0 gives v₁ + v₂ = 0, so v₁ = −v₂. Take v = (1, −1). Normalize: q₂ = (1/√2, −1/√2). Check q₁·q₂ = 0.
  5. P = [q₁ q₂] = (1/√2)[[1, 1], [1, −1]]. Then PᵀAP = diag(3, 1). Put [x, y]ᵀ = P[u, v]ᵀ, so x = (u + v)/√2 and y = (u − v)/√2.
  6. Substituting x = (u + v)/√2 and y = (u − v)/√2 into the form: 2x² + 2xy + 2y² = 3u² + v². Cross terms cancel.

Answer. An orthogonal change x = Py with P = (1/√2)[[1, 1], [1, −1]] gives the canonical form 3u² + v².

Exam traps

  • For ax² + 2hxy + by², the matrix is [[a, h], [h, b]], not [[a, 2h], [2h, b]]. The factor 2 is already inside the cross coefficient.
  • The change x = Py must use eigenvector columns in the same order as the eigenvalues; swapping columns swaps the coefficients.
  • An orthogonal change keeps the eigenvalues; a Lagrange congruent change can produce different coefficients, so do not compare them as eigenvalues.
  • In ax² + 2hxy + by² + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0, the linear coefficients are 2g and 2f; the vector is [g, f], not [2g, 2f].

4.7 Nature of quadratic forms: rank, index, signature, definiteness

A quadratic form turns into a sum of squares after a change of variables. The signs of those squares, not their sizes, classify the form. Count positive squares p, negative squares q, and zero squares z. Then rank = p + q. The index is p (number of positive squares) in the convention used here; some books take the index as the number of negative squares instead. The signature is p − q. These numbers stay fixed under any nonsingular change of variables, a fact called Sylvester's law of inertia. Engineers use them to test definiteness of energy and stiffness matrices, and to classify conics in coordinate geometry.

You need: Chapter 4.6 for reduction to squares, plus basic conic sections from coordinate geometry.

Signs in a canonical form

Start with a real quadratic form Q(x) = xTAx, where A is symmetric. A nonsingular change x = Py gives Q = Σ ciyi2. The coefficients ci can be positive, negative, or zero. Count each type: p positive, q negative, z zero. The total number of variables is n = p + q + z. The rank of the form is rank(A) = p + q. The index is p under the convention used here: index = number of positive squares. Some books call p the positive index and q the negative index. The signature is p − q. Some syllabi write signature as the pair (p, q); check the convention before you answer. Sylvester's law of inertia says p, q, and z do not change when the change of variables is nonsingular. Different reductions may give different coefficients, but the sign counts stay the same. For example, Q = 2u2 − 5v2 + 0w2 has p = 1, q = 1, z = 1, rank = 2, index = 1, signature = 0.

<i>Q</i> = Σ <i>c</i><sub>i</sub><i>y</i><sub>i</sub><sup>2</sup>

ci are real canonical coefficients; yi are new variables; p counts positive coefficients; q counts negative coefficients; z counts zero coefficients.

Definiteness and conic type

The sign counts give a quick classification. A form is positive definite when Q > 0 for every nonzero vector. That happens exactly when p = n, so q = z = 0. It is negative definite when p = 0 and q = n. A semidefinite form allows zeros at nonzero vectors. Positive semidefinite means q = 0 and z > 0; negative semidefinite means p = 0 and z > 0. An indefinite form has both p > 0 and q > 0. Then Q takes both signs. A signature of 0 alone does not prove definiteness: x2 − y2 has p = 1, q = 1, signature = 0, but it is indefinite.

For the general second-degree equation ax2 + 2hxy + by2 + 2gx + 2fy + c = 0, study the quadratic part ax2 + 2hxy + by2. Its matrix is [[a, h], [h, b]]. Here p is the number of positive eigenvalues and q is the number of negative eigenvalues of this 2×2 matrix. Definite gives an ellipse, or an imaginary ellipse (no real points at all), or a single point (one real point). Indefinite gives a hyperbola, or a pair of intersecting lines. Semidefinite with one zero eigenvalue gives a parabola, or parallel lines, or no real locus. The discriminant h2 − ab helps: h2 − ab < 0 means the two eigenvalues have the same sign, so the quadratic part is definite and the curve is an ellipse type; h2 − ab = 0 means it is semidefinite; h2 − ab > 0 means it is indefinite. Translation and rotation details are in Chapter 4.6.

  7. For the conic, the quadratic part is x2 + 4y2. Its matrix is [[1, 0], [0, 4]], with eigenvalues 1 and 4. Both are positive, so p = 2, q = 0.
  8. The equation is x2 + 4y2 = 4, that is x2/4 + y2/1 = 1, an ellipse.

Answer. The form has rank 3, index 2 and signature 1, and is indefinite; the conic is an ellipse.

Exam traps

  • Index and signature conventions vary: here index counts positive squares and signature is p − q. Some books use the pair (p, q) as signature. State your convention.
  • Signs decide the type, not sizes. 100x2 − y2 is indefinite even though the positive coefficient is large.
  • rank = p + q, not the number of variables. Zero squares reduce rank.
  • Definiteness needs all nonzero vectors. Checking only the diagonal entries can fail when cross terms such as xy are present.
  • For a conic, one zero eigenvalue means semidefinite; that gives a parabola or parallel lines, not automatically a parabola.

Takeaways

  1. Eigenvalues are the roots of det(λI − A) = 0, and the eigenvectors for a given λ are the nonzero vectors in the null space of A − λI.
  2. The trace gives the sum of the eigenvalues and the determinant gives their product, both counted with algebraic multiplicity.
  3. For each eigenvalue, 1 ≤ geometric multiplicity ≤ algebraic multiplicity; equality for all eigenvalues is exactly the condition for diagonalizability.
  4. The Cayley–Hamilton theorem turns the characteristic polynomial into a matrix identity p(A) = 0, which gives fast ways to compute A⁻¹ and high powers of A.
  5. A is diagonalizable exactly when it has n linearly independent eigenvectors, which then form the columns of the diagonalizing matrix P.
  6. A real symmetric matrix always has real eigenvalues and a full set of mutually orthogonal eigenvectors, so it can be diagonalized by an orthogonal matrix.
  7. A quadratic form with matrix A becomes a sum of squares under a change of variables; an orthogonal change uses the eigenvalues as the coefficients.
  8. Rank, index and signature of a quadratic form do not depend on which reduction you choose, so they classify the form and the conic it represents.

Glossary

Algebraic multiplicity
How many times an eigenvalue repeats as a root of the characteristic polynomial.
Canonical form
A sum of squared new variables obtained from a quadratic form by an invertible linear change of variables.
Cayley–Hamilton theorem
The statement that p(A) = 0 whenever p is the characteristic polynomial of A.
Characteristic equation
The equation det(A − λI) = 0 whose roots are the eigenvalues.
Characteristic polynomial
The polynomial det(λI − A) of degree n in λ.
Defective matrix
A matrix whose geometric multiplicity is smaller than the algebraic multiplicity for some eigenvalue, so it is not diagonalizable.
Diagonalizable
Similar to a diagonal matrix, that is, A = PDP⁻¹ with D diagonal.
Eigenspace
The null space of A − λI, containing the zero vector and all eigenvectors for λ.
Eigenvalue
A scalar λ for which Ax = λx has a nonzero solution x.
Eigenvector
A nonzero vector x that is mapped by A to a scalar multiple of itself.
Geometric multiplicity
The number of independent eigenvectors available for one eigenvalue.
Hermitian matrix
A complex square matrix equal to its own conjugate transpose.
Indefinite
A quadratic form that takes both positive and negative values, so its eigenvalues have both signs.
Index of a quadratic form
The number of negative squares in a canonical form.
Minimal polynomial
The lowest-degree monic polynomial m with m(A) = 0.
Normal matrix
A matrix that commutes with its conjugate transpose, AA* = A*A.
Orthogonal matrix
A real square matrix Q with QᵀQ = I, whose columns form an orthonormal basis.
Positive definite
q(x) > 0 for every nonzero x; all eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix are positive.
Quadratic form
A homogeneous polynomial of degree two in several variables, written xᵀAx with A symmetric.
Rank of a quadratic form
The number of nonzero squares in a canonical form, equal to the rank of its symmetric matrix.
Signature
The difference p − q between the numbers of positive and negative squares.
Similar matrices
Matrices A and B with B = P⁻¹AP for some invertible P; they share eigenvalues, trace, determinant and characteristic polynomial.
Spectrum
The set of all eigenvalues of a matrix, with multiplicities.
Sylvester's law of inertia
The numbers of positive, negative and zero squares in a canonical form depend only on the matrix, not on the change of variables chosen.
Unitary matrix
A complex square matrix U with U*U = I.
