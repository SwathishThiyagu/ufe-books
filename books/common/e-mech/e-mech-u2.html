2.1 Principle of transmissibility

A force on a rigid body can be moved anywhere along its own line of action without changing its external effect. The line of action is the infinite straight line along which the force acts; it passes through the point of application. This rule lets you redraw a free-body diagram with the force at a joint, corner or common point. It is used in beam and frame problems, in support reactions, and in reducing force systems. The rule is called the principle of transmissibility. It applies to the external effect on a rigid body, not to the internal forces inside it.

You need Unit 1: force as a vector and resolution of forces.

Line of action and the principle

A force is a vector: it has magnitude, direction and a point of application. Draw the force as an arrow from its point of application. The line of action is the infinite straight line along which the force acts. It passes through the point of application and runs in the direction of the force arrow. For a force of 100 N at A, the line of action passes through A and follows the arrow.

A rigid body is an ideal body whose points keep the same distance apart under load. No real body is perfectly rigid, but steel plates, beams and machine parts are treated as rigid for external reactions.

Principle of transmissibility: the external effect of a force on a rigid body is unchanged when the force is moved to any other point on its line of action, provided magnitude and direction stay the same. In vector language, forces on a rigid body are sliding vectors. Only three things count: magnitude, direction and line of action. The point of application on that line does not count.

Two uses and one warning

Use 1: slide a force to a joint or corner. In a free-body diagram of a bracket, a cable force may act at a hole. For external reactions you may draw it at the pin centre if the line of action passes through both.

Use 2: slide forces to a common point before adding them. Two forces on a rigid body are slid along their own lines of action until they meet at the point where the two lines intersect. The two forces are then replaced by their vector sum acting at that intersection point (or by a resultant plus a couple if the lines are parallel). Only for collinear forces is the 'common line of action' language correct. This prepares the reduction of a force system in chapter 2.5. For moments, see chapter 2.2; a slide along the line keeps the perpendicular distance from any point O to the line unchanged, so the moment about O is unchanged.

Warning: the internal force changes. Slide a 100 N pull from one end of a rope to the other and the rope's internal tension distribution is not the same. The principle fails for deformable bodies such as ropes, springs and members you are checking for stress. It never allows turning the force, changing its magnitude, or moving it to a parallel line.

Steps and limits

To use the principle:

  1. Draw the force arrow. Extend it to get the line of action.
  2. Choose a new point on that same line, such as a joint or corner.
  3. Redraw the same magnitude and direction at the new point.
  4. Do not slide to a parallel line. That changes the line of action and the moment.

Check: The perpendicular distance from any moment centre to the line of action is unchanged by the slide, so M_O = F d is unchanged.

M_O = F × d

F is force in N; d is perpendicular distance from O to the line of action in m; M_O is moment about O in N·m.

Some syllabi call this the principle of transmissibility of forces. The name comes from transmitting a force along its line. It is a modelling rule for external effects. It does not say the body is flexible or that every point feels the same force.

Worked example

Question. A rigid plate is pinned at O. A force F = 100 N acts at point A on the plate. Its line of action passes at a perpendicular distance 0.4 m from O. The force is slid to point B on the same line of action; B is 1.2 m from O measured along the line of action. Find the moment of the force about O in both positions. State what happens to the pin reactions and to the internal force in the plate.

  1. Identify the line of action. Sliding from A to B keeps the same line.
  2. The perpendicular distance from O to the line is d = 0.4 m in both positions. The 1.2 m is along the line, not perpendicular to it.
  3. Moment magnitude is M_O = F d = 100 N × 0.4 m = 40 N·m.
  4. The force tends to turn the plate the same way in both positions, so the moment vector is unchanged.
  5. External pin reactions depend on the force's line, magnitude and direction, so they are unchanged. Internal force in the plate can change because the load path changes.

Answer. The moment about O is 40 N·m in both positions; pin reactions are unchanged, but the internal force in the plate is not.

Exam traps

  • Moving a force to a parallel line is not transmissibility. The line of action must be the same straight line.
  • The principle changes where the force is drawn, not its magnitude or direction.
  • External reactions may stay the same while internal forces and stresses change. Do not use the principle to find stress in a deformable member.
  • A force cannot be slid to a point that is not on its line of action just because it is convenient.
  • The distance used in M_O = F d is perpendicular to the line of action, not the distance along the line.

2.2 Moment of a force about a point and an axis

A force does two jobs on a rigid body: it pushes the body along a line, and it twists the body about a point or an axis. The moment measures that twist. It explains why a long spanner loosens a rusted bolt, why a door handle sits far from the hinge, and why a crane can tip when a load swings. This chapter builds the scalar rule M = F d, the vector rule M_O = r × F, and the axis rule M_AB = u · (r × F).

You need Unit 1 vector algebra and the cross product, plus 2.1 for the line of action.

Turning effect about a point

Fix a point O on a body. Call O the moment centre. A force F acts at some point A. The moment of F about O measures how strongly F tries to rotate the body about O. The size is M = F d. Here F is the force in newtons (N), d is the perpendicular distance in metres (m) from O to the line of action of F, and M is the moment in newton metres (N·m).

The distance d is not the distance OA. It is the shortest distance from O to the line along which F acts. A force aimed straight at O has d = 0 and gives no moment about O. A force at the same distance but more perpendicular gives a larger moment.

In a plane, use a sign: anticlockwise is positive, clockwise is negative. In space, use the right-hand rule: curl the fingers from r to F, and the thumb points along M_O. The moment changes if you choose a different point O, because both d and the direction of turning can change.

Sliding F along its line of action does not change M about a fixed O. That is the principle of transmissibility in action. It lets you pick a convenient point on the line when you compute r.

Vector moment about a point

Draw a position vector r from O to any point on the line of action of F. The vector moment is M_O = r × F. This single cross product carries both the size and the axis of the turning effect. Its direction is perpendicular to both r and F, as the right-hand rule gives.

The size checks out. The cross product has magnitude r F sinθ, where θ is the angle between r and F. The perpendicular distance is d = r sinθ. So |M_O| = F d, the same scalar rule as before.

M_O = r × F = (yF_z − zF_y)i + (zF_x − xF_z)j + (xF_y − yF_x)k

r = x i + y j + z k is the position vector from O to the force point, in m; F = F_x i + F_y j + F_z k is the force, in N; M_O is the moment vector, in N·m.

Order matters. r × F and F × r have opposite signs. Take r from the moment point to the point where the force acts. A common exam slip is to reverse the order and lose the sign.

Moment about an axis

A door has a hinge axis. A push on the door may have a large moment about a point on the hinge, but only the part along the hinge actually turns the door. The moment about an axis is the component of M_O along that axis.

Let the axis pass through points A and B. Define the unit vector u = (B − A)/|B − A|. Unit means |u| = 1. Pick r from any point on the axis to the point of application of F. Then M_AB = u · (r × F).

M_AB = u · (r × F) = u_x(yF_z − zF_y) + u_y(zF_x − xF_z) + u_z(xF_y − yF_x)

u = u_x i + u_y j + u_z k is the unit vector along the axis AB; r = x i + y j + z k runs from any point on the axis to the force point, in m; F is the force in N; M_AB is the scalar moment about the axis, in N·m.

This is a scalar triple product. Written as a 3×3 determinant, the first row is u, the second row is r, and the third row is F. The result is a signed scalar, not a vector. Its sign gives the sense along u.

  • F is parallel to the axis: r × F is perpendicular to u, so the dot product is zero.
  • F cuts the axis: r and F are parallel, so r × F = 0.
  • The axis passes through the point of application of F: r = 0.

Some syllabi call this the moment of a force about a line. Check that u is a unit vector before you dot. Using a non-unit vector along the axis gives a wrong scale.

Worked example

Question. A force F = 3i + 4j − 2k N acts at the point A(1, 2, 3) m. Find its moment about the origin O, and its moment about the x-axis.

  1. Write the position vector from O to A: r = 1i + 2j + 3k m.
  2. Use the component form of M_O = r × F. Here x = 1, y = 2, z = 3 and F_x = 3, F_y = 4, F_z = −2.
  3. M_x = yF_z − zF_y = 2(−2) − 3(4) = −4 − 12 = −16 N·m.
  4. M_y = zF_x − xF_z = 3(3) − 1(−2) = 9 + 2 = 11 N·m.
  5. M_z = xF_y − yF_x = 1(4) − 2(3) = 4 − 6 = −2 N·m.
  6. So M_O = −16i + 11j − 2k N·m.
  7. The x-axis has unit vector u = i. The moment about the x-axis is M_x itself: M_AB = u · M_O = −16 N·m.
  8. The negative sign means 16 N·m clockwise when you look along +x.

Answer. M_O = −16i + 11j − 2k N·m, and the moment about the x-axis is −16 N·m, i.e. 16 N·m clockwise looking along +x.

Exam traps

  • Writing F × r instead of r × F flips the sign of M_O. Take r from the moment point to the force point.
  • In M = F d, d is the perpendicular distance to the line of action, not the distance OA to the point of application.
  • Moment about an axis needs a unit vector u. A vector along the axis with length not 1 scales M_AB wrongly.
  • Moment has units N·m; do not drop the metre or call it N. The same unit as energy does not make moment an energy.
  • Moment about an axis is a scalar component. Do not report it as a vector unless the question asks for the moment vector about a point.

2.3 Varignon's theorem

One force can be split into components. Varignon's theorem says the turning effect of the whole force about a point equals the sum of the turning effects of its parts about that same point. This matters because the perpendicular distance to an inclined force is awkward to measure, while components lying along and across a beam have distances you can read straight off a drawing. Everyone who analyses a crane jib, a bracket or a leaning ladder uses this shortcut to turn one difficult force into two easy ones.

You need: Chapter 2.2 for M = Fd with its sign convention, and Unit 1, resolution and composition of forces, for Fx = F cos θ and Fy = F sin θ.

The statement, and why it exists

A force F acts at point A of a rigid body. Replace it by any number of components F₁, F₂, …, Fₙ that also act at A and whose vector sum is F. Varignon's theorem states that the moment of F about a point O equals the sum of the moments of the components about the same point O:

M<sub>O</sub> = Σ s<sub>i</sub> F<sub>i</sub> d<sub>i</sub>

MO is the moment of the force about O (N·m); Fi are the component magnitudes (N); di are the perpendicular distances from O to each component's line of action (m), with di ≥ 0; si = +1 for an anticlockwise moment and si = −1 for a clockwise moment.

The theorem exists because of one practical difficulty. For a force at 30° to the horizontal, finding the perpendicular distance from O to its line of action needs geometry: extend the line, drop a perpendicular, solve a triangle. For its horizontal and vertical components, the perpendicular distances are simply a vertical and a horizontal distance on your sketch. So you trade one hard distance for two easy ones.

  • Inclined forces: split into horizontal and vertical, then add two moments instead of hunting one perpendicular distance.
  • One force into two convenient ones: a force on a ladder can be split along the ladder and across it, matching the geometry you already have.
  • Chain of parts: the same idea applies to a force already given as components, or to three components in space — add all the moments.
  • Moment about an axis: take moments of the components about the axis instead of the axis distance of the whole force. See Chapter 2.2.

Proof in a plane

Place O at the origin of x–y axes. The point of application A has coordinates (x, y) in metres, and the force has components Fx and Fy in newtons, both acting at A.

Only the vertical component has a horizontal lever arm: Fy acts on a vertical line through A, whose perpendicular distance from O is x. Its moment is about the z-axis through O (perpendicular to the plane), and it is anticlockwise, so the term is +x Fy. Only the horizontal component has a vertical lever arm: Fx acts on a horizontal line at height y, whose perpendicular distance from O is y. Its moment is also about the z-axis through O and is clockwise, so the term is −y Fx.

M_O = x Fy − y Fx

x, y are the coordinates of the point of application A (m); Fx, Fy are the force components at A (N); M_O is the moment about O (N·m), anticlockwise positive.

Every extra force or component you add at A contributes one more term x Fy − y Fx with the same x and y, and the terms simply add. That is the theorem for any number of components. The perpendicular distance is not needed at all.

When it holds and when it fails

The theorem is about one force and its own parts, so the parts must act at the same point of application A as the original force. Two relaxations are allowed, and one is not.

  • Point O may be anywhere: on the body, off the body, on the line of action (then M_O = 0).
  • A component may be slid along its own line of action, because a force can be moved along its line of action without changing its moment — Chapter 2.1.
  • A component may not be moved to a different, parallel line. Its perpendicular distance from O changes, so its moment changes, and the sum no longer equals M_O.
  • A force already given as two components is not a special case: the theorem is being used in reverse.

Sign discipline is where marks are lost. Fix one sense as positive before you start, write each term with its sign, and keep the sign in the arithmetic. A clockwise component moment does not disappear; it subtracts.

Worked example

Question. A force of 50 N acts at point A of a rigid body. Point A is 3 m to the right of and 1 m above point O. The force lies in the plane of the paper and points up and to the right at 30° to the horizontal. Using Varignon's theorem, find (a) the moment of the force about O, and (b) the perpendicular distance from O to its line of action.

  1. Resolve the force at A. F_x = 50 cos 30° = 43.3 N to the right; F_y = 50 sin 30° = 25 N upwards. Both act at A.
  2. Take anticlockwise as positive. F_y acts on a vertical line 3 m from O (x = 3 m) and turns anticlockwise: moment = +3 × 25 = +75 N·m.
  3. F_x acts on a horizontal line 1 m above O (y = 1 m) and turns clockwise: moment = −1 × 43.3 = −43.3 N·m.
  4. Add the moments: M_O = x F_y − y F_x = 75 − 43.3 = 31.7 N·m, positive, so anticlockwise.
  5. Perpendicular distance: for a single force M_O = F d, so d = M_O / F = 31.7 / 50 = 0.634 m.
  6. The 50 N force is steeper than OA (30° against about 18.4°), so its line of action passes below O, with the perpendicular foot down and to the right. A force along that line pointing up and right turns the body anticlockwise about O; the positive sign agrees.

Answer. The moment about O is 31.7 N·m anticlockwise, and the perpendicular distance from O to the line of action is 0.634 m.

Worked example 2

Question. A 60 N force acts at a point 2 m to the left of O and 4 m above O, pointing down and to the right at 40° below the horizontal. Find its moment about O.

  1. Coordinates of A: x = −2 m, y = +4 m.
  2. Components: F_x = 60 cos 40° = 46.0 N to the right; F_y = 60 sin 40° = 38.6 N downwards, so F_y = −38.6 N.
  3. Sign for F_x: rightward, so positive. Sign for F_y: downward, so negative.
  4. M_O = x F_y − y F_x = (−2)(−38.6) − (4)(46.0) = 77.1 − 183.9 = −106.8 N·m.
  5. Negative means clockwise: 106.8 N·m clockwise.

Answer. The moment about O is 106.8 N·m clockwise.

Exam traps

  • Using the slant distance OA instead of the perpendicular distance to the line of action: 3 m and 1 m fix the point, not the lever arm.
  • Dropping the sign of one component's moment: the two terms often oppose, and 75 + 43.3 instead of 75 − 43.3 gives a wrong total and a wrong sense.
  • Mixing units: distances in mm with forces in kN must be converted before adding moments, and the answer carries N·m or kN·m, never N.
  • Placing a component on a parallel line instead of at the same point of application, which silently changes its moment.
  • Writing the clockwise term first and then calling the anticlockwise result negative out of habit; fix the positive sense at the start and state it in the answer.

2.4 Couples and force-couple systems

A couple is two equal, opposite, parallel forces that do not share a line of action. It has no net force, only a turning effect. The couple moment is M = F d, where F is the force magnitude and d is the perpendicular distance between the two lines of action. This moment is a free vector: it is the same about every point and can be drawn anywhere on the body. Engineers use couples to model steering wheels, taps, screwdrivers and any pure twist. A force moved to a new point becomes a force plus a couple, called a force-couple system.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for moving a force along its line of action, and Chapter 2.2 for the moment of a force about a point and an axis.

What a couple is

Take two forces of equal magnitude F. They are opposite in direction and parallel. Their lines of action are separated by a perpendicular distance d. If the lines are not the same, the two forces form a couple. A couple has zero resultant force, so it cannot move the body as a whole. It only tends to rotate the body. A steering wheel turned by two hands is a common picture: one hand pushes up, the other pulls down, with equal forces. The moment of a couple has magnitude M = F d. The unit is N·m. In two dimensions, choose counterclockwise as positive. Then a clockwise couple is negative. In three dimensions the couple vector is perpendicular to the plane of the two forces. Its sense follows the right-hand rule: curl the fingers in the sense in which the couple tends to rotate the body, and the thumb gives the couple vector. Looking along the vector, the couple turns the body counterclockwise. A couple with d = 0 is not a couple; the forces cancel along one line. Do not call a single force a couple.

M = F d

F is the magnitude of each force, in N; d is the perpendicular distance between their lines of action, in m; M is the couple moment, in N·m.

The couple moment is a free vector

Choose any point P. The moment of the couple about P is the sum of the moments of the two forces: (r₁ − r_P) × F + (r₂ − r_P) × (−F) = (r₁ − r₂) × F. The term in r_P drops out, so the sum is F d and does not depend on P. This is why a couple moment is called a free vector. You may draw it at any point of the rigid body, and it produces the same external effect. Two couples with the same M are equivalent. They may have different F and d; only the product F d and the sense matter. For example, 40 N at 0.5 m and 100 N at 0.2 m both give 20 N·m. Couples add as vectors. In one plane, add their signed scalar moments. In three dimensions, add their vectors. The moment of a couple about a line is the projection of the couple vector on the unit vector of the line.

M_line = M · u

M is the couple vector, in N·m; u is the unit vector along the line; M_line is the scalar moment about the line, in N·m.

Force-couple system

Suppose a force F acts at point A. You want the same external effect with a force at point O. Add a pair of forces at O: F and −F. They cancel, so nothing changes. Now the original F at A and the −F at O form a couple. The remaining F acts at O. The result is a force F at O plus a couple M = r × F. Here r is the position vector from O to A. In a plane, M = x F_y − y F_x, where x and y are the coordinates of A relative to O. The sign of M follows the right-hand rule. This step is called translating a force to a parallel position. The force and couple together are a force-couple system. The couple is a free vector, so you can draw it anywhere. Use this to reduce a system of forces to one point; see Chapter 2.5. It also prepares equilibrium equations in Chapter 2.7.

M_O = r × F

M_O is the couple vector in N·m when the force is moved to O; r is the position vector from O to A, in m; F is the force vector, in N.

Worked example

Question. A force of 120 N acts vertically downward at A(0.6, 0) m on a plate. Replace it by an equivalent force-couple system at the origin O. Take i along x and j along y, and counterclockwise positive.

  1. 1. Write the position vector of A from O: r = 0.6 i m. The coordinates are x = 0.6 m and y = 0 m.
  2. 2. Write the force: F = −120 j N, so F_x = 0 and F_y = −120 N.
  3. 3. Compute the moment about O: M_O = r × F = x F_y − y F_x = (0.6)(−120) − (0)(0) = −72 N·m.
  4. 4. Read the sign: the vector is −72 k N·m, where k is out of the page. The minus sign means clockwise. The magnitude is 72 N·m.
  5. 5. State the equivalent system at O: force F = −120 j N, that is 120 N vertically down, plus a couple M = −72 k N·m, that is 72 N·m clockwise.
  6. 6. Check the free-vector property: the couple in this system is a free vector, so its 72 N·m bending effect can be drawn anywhere on the plate. But the couple belongs to the force-couple system. If the force is translated to a different point O', the force stays 120 N downward and the couple changes to r' × F, because the moment arm from O' to A is different.

Answer. At O, the equivalent system is a 120 N force vertically downward plus a clockwise couple of 72 N·m (vector −72k N·m).

Exam traps

  • Thinking the couple moment changes when you choose another reference point. It does not; a couple moment is a free vector.
  • Using the distance between the two points of application instead of the perpendicular distance between the two lines of action.
  • Adding the two forces of a couple to get a nonzero resultant. The resultant force of a couple is zero.
  • Writing the unit of a couple moment as N/m or J. The correct unit is N·m.
  • Confusing a force-couple system with two separate loads. The couple is the moment of the shifted force about the new point.

2.5 Equivalent force systems; resultant of non-concurrent forces

A rigid body may carry many forces at many points. The same net push and the same net turning effect can be produced by one force and one couple. This chapter reduces any force system to a resultant R at a chosen point O plus a moment M_O about O. It then tests when the pair collapses to a single force. The idea is used to find reactions on beams, machines and plates without solving every force separately, and to spot a leftover pure couple.

You need: moments, Varignon's theorem and couples.

Reduction at a chosen point

Two force systems are equivalent when they give the same sum of forces and the same sum of moments about one point. If both match at one point O, they match at every point of a rigid body. The reduced pair is R and M_O. R is the resultant force vector in N. M_O is the resultant moment vector about O in N·m. For each force F_i acting at point r_i, add F_i to R and add r_i × F_i to M_O. The point O is only a bookkeeping point; it may lie outside the body. This reduction is the base for reactions and for checking whether a complicated loading can be replaced by one force.

R = ΣF, M_O = Σ M_O = Σ (r × F)

R is the resultant force vector in N; F is each force vector in N; M_O is the resultant moment vector about O in N·m; r is the position vector from O to the point of application in m; × is the vector cross product; Σ means sum over all forces.

Use the same point O for every force when you compare two systems. Changing O changes M_O, but not R. The equivalence test must use one chosen point.

Single force, wrench or couple

When R ≠ 0, split M_O into a part along R and a part perpendicular to R. Shift the force by a perpendicular distance to cancel the perpendicular part. A force plus a parallel couple remains: a wrench. If R·M_O = 0, the parallel part is zero, so only a single force is left. Its perpendicular offset from O is d = |M_O| / |R|. The vector relation is r × R = M_O. A nonzero dot product means M_O has a component along R; a single force cannot create that, because r × R is always perpendicular to R.

  • R·M_O = 0 and R ≠ 0: one force of magnitude R at perpendicular distance d = |M_O| / |R| from O.
  • R = 0 and M_O ≠ 0: a pure couple; no single force can replace it.
  • R parallel to M_O: a wrench, meaning a force with a couple along its own line.
  • R = 0 and M_O = 0: equilibrium under the reduced system; see equilibrium of rigid bodies.

d = |M_O| / |R|

d is the perpendicular distance from O to the line of action in m; |M_O| is the magnitude of the moment about O in N·m; |R| is the magnitude of the resultant force in N.

Coplanar non-concurrent forces

For forces in the xy plane, resolve every force into x and y components. Sum the components to get R_x and R_y. Sum moments about O with one sign convention. Take anticlockwise positive. Then use Varignon's theorem to place the resultant line.

R_x = ΣF_x,   R_y = ΣF_y,   |R| = √(R_x² + R_y²),   θ = tan⁻¹(R_y / R_x)

R_x and R_y are resultant components in N; F_x and F_y are force components in N; |R| is the resultant magnitude in N; θ is the angle from the +x axis.

M_O = Σ(xF_y − yF_x)

M_O is the moment about O in N·m, anticlockwise positive; x, y are the coordinates of the point of application in m; F_x, F_y are the force components in N.

  1. Choose O and write the sign convention for moments.
  2. Resolve each force into x and y components.
  3. Add components to get R_x and R_y, then |R| and θ.
  4. Add moments about O to get M_O.
  5. Locate the line of action: if R_y ≠ 0, the x-intercept is x = M_O / R_y at y = 0; if R_y = 0, the line is y = −M_O / R_x.

A distributed load is handled by the same reduction. Its resultant equals the area under the load diagram and acts at the centroid of that area. Centroids of areas are covered in Unit 4, Centroids and Moments of Inertia.

Worked example

Question. Three coplanar forces act on a plate: F₁ = 10 N along +y at A(0, 0), F₂ = 20 N along +x at B(0, 2) m, and F₃ = 15 N along −y at C(4, 0) m. Find the resultant and where its line of action cuts the x-axis.

  1. Take +x right, +y up, and anticlockwise moment positive about O(0, 0).
  2. Write the forces: F₁ = 10j N at (0, 0); F₂ = 20i N at (0, 2) m; F₃ = −15j N at (4, 0) m.
  3. Sum forces: R = (0 + 20 + 0)i + (10 + 0 − 15)j = 20i − 5j N.
  4. Magnitude: |R| = √(20² + (−5)²) = √425 = 20.6 N.
  5. Direction: tan θ = |R_y| / R_x = 5 / 20 = 0.25, so θ = 14.0° below the +x axis.
  6. Moment of F₁ about O: 0·10 − 0·0 = 0.
  7. Moment of F₂ about O: 0·0 − 2·20 = −40 N·m, clockwise.
  8. Moment of F₃ about O: 4·(−15) − 0·0 = −60 N·m, clockwise.
  9. Total moment: M_O = 0 − 40 − 60 = −100 N·m, clockwise.
  10. On the x-axis y = 0. The moment of R there is xR_y = x(−5). Set x(−5) = −100, so x = 20 m.

Answer. The resultant is 20.6 N at 14.0° below the +x axis, cutting the x-axis at 20 m from O and turning the plate clockwise.

Exam traps

  • Do not sum moments about different points for different forces; choose O once and keep it.
  • R = 0 does not mean equilibrium when M_O ≠ 0; a pure couple is left over.
  • Keep one sign convention. In the xy plane M_O = Σ(xF_y − yF_x) is anticlockwise positive, not yF_x − xF_y.
  • For a single-force offset, use d = |M_O| / |R| with M_O about the same O; do not mix a moment about another point.
  • Convert cm or mm to m before computing moments, so force times distance gives N·m.

2.6 Types of supports and support reactions

A support is the part of a structure that touches the ground or another body. It stops some motions and lets others happen. In return, it pushes or pulls on the body with reaction forces, and sometimes with a reaction moment. The number of reactions is fixed by the support type, not by the load. Engineers use this count to draw a free body diagram correctly and to check whether a problem can be solved with statics alone. The same idea appears in a pole fixed to the ground, a bridge roller on a pier, and a door hinge.

You need: moment of a force about a point and the free body diagram from Unit 1.

Each support removes motion and gives reaction

A rigid body in a plane can slide along x, slide along y, and rotate. A support removes one or more of these motions. Each removed motion produces one reaction component. The reaction is the force or moment the support applies to the body.

Drawing the free body diagram

Follow these steps for every problem.

  1. Isolate the body and sketch it alone.
  2. Replace each support by the reaction components from the table.
  3. Choose x and y axes and a positive sense for moments.
  4. Assume a direction for each unknown and draw the arrow.
  5. Write the equilibrium equations from Chapter 2.7.
  6. Solve. If an answer is negative, keep the magnitude and reverse the arrow.

The assumed direction is free. Pick any arrow. A negative answer means the true direction is opposite. Keep the magnitude and reverse the arrow. Do not change the sign twice. Write the units on every reaction: N for force, N·m for moment. A reaction moment appears only where rotation is prevented.

The free body diagram is the bridge between the real structure and the equations. If the diagram is wrong, the algebra cannot save it. Check the count after drawing: one arrow for a roller, two for a pin, three for a fixed support.

Counting unknowns and determinacy

For one rigid body in a plane, statics gives three equations: ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0, ΣM = 0. Count the unknown reaction components. Three unknowns: statics alone can solve the body. Fewer than three: the body is not fully restrained; it may be a mechanism. More than three: the body is statically indeterminate. You need extra information such as compatibility of deformation.

A pin plus a roller gives 2 + 1 = 3, determinate. A fixed support plus a roller gives 3 + 1 = 4, indeterminate to one degree. Two pins give 4, indeterminate unless an internal hinge supplies an extra equation.

Count before you solve. This habit saves time in exams and catches impossible models. A support that supplies more reactions than the body needs may still be safe, but the reactions depend on how the parts deform.

In space there are six equations. A ball-and-socket gives three unknowns. A fixed base gives six. Friction at a rough support is left to Unit 3.

Worked example

Question. A vertical pole AB is 3 m tall. It is fixed to the ground at A. At the free top end B, a horizontal force of 200 N acts to the right and a vertical load of 500 N acts downward. Find the reaction force and reaction moment at A.

  1. Draw the pole alone. At A the fixed support can give Ax, Ay and MA. Assume Ax to the right, Ay upward, and MA anticlockwise.
  2. ΣFx = 0: Ax + 200 N = 0, so Ax = −200 N. The minus sign means 200 N to the left.
  3. ΣFy = 0: Ay − 500 N = 0, so Ay = 500 N upward.
  4. Take moments about A. The 200 N force acts at height 3 m. Its moment is 3 m × 200 N = 600 N·m clockwise.
  5. The 500 N load acts along the pole. Its line of action passes through A, so its moment about A is zero.
  6. ΣMA = 0: MA − 600 N·m = 0, so MA = 600 N·m anticlockwise.

Answer. At A: 200 N to the left, 500 N upward, and a 600 N·m anticlockwise moment.

Exam traps

  • A pin or hinge gets two force components, never a moment. If you add M at a pin, you have changed it into a fixed support.
  • A roller on a horizontal floor gives one vertical force. On an inclined surface the force is perpendicular to the surface, not vertical. It has both x and y components.
  • A negative reaction is not an error. It means the true direction is opposite to your arrow. Keep the magnitude and reverse the arrow.
  • More than three unknown reactions on one planar body does not always mean the problem is impossible; it means statics alone is not enough. Check for extra equations from another body or an internal hinge before calling it indeterminate.
  • Moment is in N·m and force is in N. Do not add them, and do not drop the units in the final answer.

2.7 Equilibrium of rigid bodies in two and three dimensions

A rigid body is in equilibrium when it does not accelerate: the forces on it cancel and their turning effects also cancel. Written as equations, the sum of all forces is zero and the sum of all moments about any chosen point is zero. In a plane this gives three scalar equations, so a support system with three unknown reactions can be solved. The idea is used everywhere a body must stay put: a beam on a wall, a ladder against a wall, a shelf bracket, or a machine base bolted to a floor.

You need moments, support reactions, and equivalent force systems, plus equilibrium of a particle from Unit 1.

Three equations in a plane

A rigid body in a plane has three possible motions to stop: sliding along x, sliding along y, and turning in the plane. Equilibrium stops all three. The force equations stop the sliding; the moment equation stops the turning. The conditions are ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0, and ΣMO = 0, where x and y are any two perpendicular axes in the plane and O is any point in the plane.

ΣF<sub>x</sub> = 0, ΣF<sub>y</sub> = 0, ΣM<sub>O</sub> = 0

ΣFx and ΣFy are the sums of force components along the axes, in N; ΣMO is the sum of moments about O, in N·m; counterclockwise moments are positive.

The point O is free. A body that is in equilibrium has zero moment about every point, so you may choose whichever point makes the arithmetic easiest. The three equations are not independent for every choice: do not take all three as moment equations about points on one line, because those equations repeat one another. Three moment equations are valid only about three non-collinear points.

Three equations solve at most three unknown reaction components. A roller gives one, a pin gives two, a fixed support gives three. If your free body diagram has four unknowns, the body is statically indeterminate: equilibrium alone cannot find them, and you need compatibility of deformation.

Choosing equations to save work

Every problem follows the same order. Draw the free body diagram first. Replace each support by its reaction components, using the support table from types of supports. Include the body's own weight at its centre of gravity. Only then write equations.

  1. Draw the free body diagram: isolate the body, show all applied forces and all reactions, and label unknown components.
  2. Choose axes along the most forces to reduce components.
  3. Choose a moment point where one or more unknown forces pass, because their moment is zero there.
  4. Write ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0, and ΣMO = 0; solve for the unknowns.
  5. Check with an equation you did not use, such as ΣM about another point.

The moment point is the main labour-saving choice. A force whose line of action passes through O has zero moment about O, so O removes that unknown from the moment equation. In the figure, the recipe puts the moment equation after the force equations, but the order can change: if the force equations would contain two unknowns each, take moments first about a point that kills one of them.

Other valid sets exist. You may replace one force equation by a moment equation about a second point, giving two moment equations and one force equation. You may use three moment equations about three non-collinear points. These sets are equivalent to the first set, not extra equations. A common mistake is to write four equations for three unknowns and treat the fourth as a check; it is a check only if it is independent.

Space adds three more equations

In three dimensions a body can slide along three axes and rotate about three axes. Six equations are needed: ΣFx, ΣFy, ΣFz, and ΣMx, ΣMy, ΣMz, all equal to zero. The force equations stop sliding; the moment equations stop rotation about the axes. Six equations solve at most six unknown reaction components.

ΣF<sub>x</sub> = ΣF<sub>y</sub> = ΣF<sub>z</sub> = 0, ΣM<sub>x</sub> = ΣM<sub>y</sub> = ΣM<sub>z</sub> = 0

the F sums are force components in N along three perpendicular axes; the M sums are moments about those axes in N·m.

The vector form is ΣF = 0 and ΣMO = 0, where F is a force vector and MO is the moment vector about point O. In space, the moment of a force about an axis is found from the component of the moment vector along that axis. A ball-and-socket support gives three force reactions and no moment; a fixed support gives three force and three moment reactions. If there are more than six unknowns, the body is statically indeterminate.

Use the same method: free body diagram, axes, moment point or moment axes that remove unknowns, solve, check. A useful check in space is to take moments about a different point; the moments of all forces about that point must still sum to zero.

Worked example

Question. A massless beam AB of length 3 m is hinged to a wall at A and held by a cable at B. The cable makes 45° with the beam and pulls up and toward the wall. A load of 500 N hangs from the beam at 2 m from A. Find the cable tension and the hinge reaction.

  1. Draw the free body diagram. At the hinge A show Ax and Ay. At B show the cable tension T at 45° above the beam, pointing up and toward the wall. At 2 m from A show the 500 N load acting down.
  2. Take x along the beam away from the wall and y upward. The cable has components T cos 45° toward the wall and T sin 45° upward.
  3. Take moments about A. The hinge reactions and the horizontal component of T pass through A, so only T sin 45° and the 500 N load have moments. Counterclockwise is positive: (T sin 45°)(3 m) − (500 N)(2 m) = 0.
  4. Solve: 3(0.7071 T) = 1000, so T = 1000 ÷ 2.1213 = 471.4 N.
  5. ΣFx = 0: Ax − T cos 45° = 0, so Ax = 471.4 × 0.7071 = 333.3 N. The positive sign means Ax acts away from the wall.
  6. ΣFy = 0: Ay + T sin 45° − 500 N = 0, so Ay = 500 − 333.3 = 166.7 N upward.

2.8 Two-force and three-force bodies

A body loaded at only two points has a simple rule: the two forces must lie on the line joining the points. A body loaded with three coplanar forces in equilibrium has a related rule: the three lines of action meet at one point, or all run parallel. These rules give the direction of an unknown hinge reaction before you solve equations, and they expose a wrong free body diagram fast. They are used in links, brackets, tie rods and machine frames.

You need: support reactions, moments and rigid-body equilibrium.

Two-force bodies

A two-force body is a rigid body acted on by forces at only two points. It has no applied couple, and its own weight is neglected or already counted in the two forces. Let the points be A and B. The line of action is the infinite line along which a force acts.

Take moments about A. The force at A has zero moment about A. For total moment to be zero, the force at B must also have zero moment about A. For a non-zero force, that is possible only if its line of action passes through A. By the same argument about B, the force at A acts along AB. So both forces act along the line joining the two points.

Force equilibrium then makes them equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. The member is in tension if the forces pull A and B apart, and in compression if they push them together.

Common two-force members: a short link in a frame, a tie rod, a cable with no distributed load, and a bar loaded only at its two pin joints. A beam carrying its own weight is not a two-force body. Neither is a shaft with a torque, nor a bar with a load at a third point.

F<sub>A</sub> = −F<sub>B</sub>, both along line AB

FA is the force at A, FB is the force at B, and the minus sign means opposite direction.

Three-force bodies

A three-force body has three coplanar forces acting on it and no applied couple. If it is in equilibrium, the three lines of action are concurrent (meet at one point) or parallel.

Proof for the concurrent case: let the lines of F1 and F2 meet at O. Take moments about O. F1 and F2 pass through O, so their moments about O are zero. The total moment must be zero, so F3 must also have zero moment about O. A non-zero force has zero moment about O only when its line of action passes through O. Hence all three lines meet at O.

If F1 and F2 are parallel, they meet at infinity. Then F3 must also be parallel to them. Three parallel forces can balance: two upward at the ends and one downward between them, with magnitudes fixed by force and moment equations.

Use the rule to find the direction of a hinge reaction. In a bracket hinged at A, pulled by a cable at B and loaded by weight at G, extend the cable line and the weight line to P. The hinge reaction at A must act along AP. You still find its magnitude from equilibrium equations. Some syllabi call this a three-force member; others just say three coplanar forces in equilibrium.

Using the rules in problems

  1. Draw the free body diagram and mark every line of action.
  2. Count the forces. Two points only and no couple means a two-force member.
  3. For three coplanar forces, extend two known lines to their intersection P.
  4. Draw the unknown reaction through P, or parallel to the other two if they are parallel.
  5. Take moments about a point that removes one unknown, then use force equilibrium.

The rules give directions, not magnitudes. A common error is to stop after drawing the reaction line. You must still satisfy ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0 and ΣM = 0.

In the rod example below, the weight line is vertical at x = 0.5 m and the string line is horizontal at y = 1.732 m. Their intersection is P = (0.5, 1.732) m. The hinge reaction line is AP. That one geometric fact turns a three-unknown problem into a simple moment equation.

Worked example

Question. A uniform rod AB of length 2 m and weight 300 N is hinged to a vertical wall at A. End B is above A and held by a horizontal string. The rod makes 30° with the wall. Find the string tension and the hinge reaction.

  1. Take A as origin, x to the right, y upward. B is at (2 sin30, 2 cos30) = (1.0, 1.732) m.
  2. Weight W = 300 N acts vertically through the midpoint, whose x-coordinate is 1 sin30 = 0.5 m. Its line of action is x = 0.5 m.
  3. The string at B is horizontal, so its line of action is y = 1.732 m.
  4. The weight line and string line meet at P = (0.5, 1.732) m. By the three-force rule, the hinge reaction at A acts along AP. Its angle θ from the wall is tanθ = 0.5/1.732 = 0.2887, so θ = 16.1°.
  5. Take moments about A. The perpendicular distance from A to the string line is 1.732 m; from A to the weight line is 0.5 m. The string tension T acts left. T(1.732) = 300(0.5).
  6. T = 150 / 1.732 = 86.6 N.
  7. Force equilibrium: Rx = T = 86.6 N to the right, Ry = W = 300 N upward.
  8. R = √(86.6² + 300²) = 312.2 N. Its direction is the same 16.1° from the wall.

Answer. The string tension is 86.6 N, and the hinge reaction is 312.2 N acting at 16.1° from the wall.

Exam traps

  • Calling a member two-force when it carries weight, a couple, or a load at a third point.
  • Treating two-force forces as merely concurrent. They must be collinear along the line joining the loaded points.
  • Using three-force concurrency when the forces are not coplanar or when an applied couple acts.
  • In the rod example, taking the moment arm of the string as 2 m. The perpendicular distance is 2 cos30 = 1.732 m.
  • Drawing the hinge reaction along the rod. In a three-force problem its line is set by the intersection of the other two lines, not by the member axis.

2.9 Principle of virtual work

Virtual work is a way to solve equilibrium without writing every support reaction. You imagine a tiny, allowed displacement of the system, keeping time frozen, and add the work done by the active forces. At equilibrium that total is zero for every such imaginary displacement. The method is used for linkages, ladders, folding stands and connected beams, where ordinary force and moment equations would carry several unknown reactions. One coordinate, often an angle θ, describes the whole motion.

You need equilibrium of rigid bodies, the dot product from Unit 1, and basic differentiation.

Virtual displacement and virtual work

A virtual displacement δr is an imaginary, infinitesimal change in position. It must be allowed by the supports, and it is taken with time frozen. A pin allows no movement at the pin. A roller on a floor may move along the floor but not into it. A virtual displacement is not a real motion: no load is changed, no time passes, and no velocity is needed.

δU = F · δr = F δr cos θ

δU is virtual work (J), F is the force vector (N), δr is the virtual displacement (m), F and δr are magnitudes, and θ is the angle between them. Work is positive if the force helps the displacement and negative if it opposes.

Ideal reactions do no work. At a smooth pin the point cannot move. At a roller the reaction is normal to the allowed displacement. In a rigid link or an inextensible cable the constraint forces act in equal and opposite pairs, so their total work cancels. Only active forces—weights, applied loads, spring forces—enter the sum.

The principle and the method

The principle of virtual work states: a system in equilibrium has δU = 0 for every virtual displacement allowed by its supports. If δU ≠ 0 for an allowed displacement, the system is not in equilibrium. It tends to move in the direction for which δU is positive—opposite to the assumed displacement when δU < 0.

δU = Σ (F_xi δx_i + F_yi δy_i) = 0

δU is total virtual work (J); F_xi and F_yi are components of active force i (N); δx_i and δy_i are components of its virtual displacement (m). For one coordinate θ, δx = (dx/dθ) δθ and δy = (dy/dθ) δθ.

  1. Choose one coordinate θ that fixes the shape, such as a rod angle.
  2. Write x(θ) and y(θ) for every point where an active force acts.
  3. Differentiate to get δx and δy in terms of δθ.
  4. Add the work of each active force with its sign.
  5. Set the total to zero, divide by δθ, and solve for the unknown.

Support reactions disappear, so a ladder or connected-beam problem may need one equation instead of three. A sign in the answer gives direction: a negative P means P acts opposite to the direction you assumed.

Worked example

Question. A uniform rod AB of length 2 m and weight 200 N is hinged to a vertical wall at A and held by a horizontal force P applied at B. The rod lies above A and makes 30° with the wall. Take x positive away from the wall and y positive upward. Find P by virtual work, giving both sign and direction.

  1. Let θ=30° be the angle between the rod and the wall, L=2 m, and W=200 N at the midpoint G. Assume P positive in the +x direction, away from the wall.
  2. Coordinates: x_B = L sinθ = 2 sinθ, y_B = L cosθ = 2 cosθ; x_G = 1 sinθ, y_G = 1 cosθ. Only the horizontal motion of B and vertical motion of G enter later.
  3. Differentiate: δx_B = 2 cosθ δθ, δy_G = −sinθ δθ. At θ=30°, cosθ=0.8660 and sinθ=0.5000.
  4. Weight: W = (0, −200) N. δU_W = (−200) δy_G = 200 sinθ δθ = 200(0.5000) δθ = 100 δθ J.
  5. Force P: P = (P, 0). δU_P = P δx_B = P(2 cosθ δθ) = 1.732 P δθ J.
  6. Total: δU = (100 + 1.732P) δθ = 0. For all δθ, 1.732P + 100 = 0, so P = −57.7 N. Equivalent: P = −100 tanθ = −100(0.5774) = −57.7 N.

Answer. P = −57.7 N on the assumed +x direction, so its magnitude is 57.7 N and it acts toward the wall.

Exam traps

  • Giving work to ideal reactions at a smooth pin or roller. The pin point has δr = 0; the roller reaction is perpendicular to the allowed displacement.
  • Using a real displacement. A virtual displacement is imaginary, infinitesimal and taken with time frozen; supports and cable lengths stay fixed.
  • Forgetting the chain rule: δx = (dx/dθ) δθ. Do not write δx = dx or leave out δθ.
  • Sign slip: δU = F·δr = F δr cosθ. Opposite force and displacement gives negative work. A negative unknown means reverse the assumed arrow.
  • Including internal forces twice. Tension in an ideal rigid link or cable does no net work; use only external active loads.

Takeaways

  1. Transmissibility lets you slide a force along its own line of action, so only the line matters for the outside world — never slide it sideways.
  2. A moment about a point is a vector r × F; its magnitude is F·d and its direction is the axis about which the force tends to turn the body.
  3. The moment about an axis is just the projection of the moment vector onto that axis, found quickly with a scalar triple product.
  4. Varignon's theorem turns an awkward inclined force into two easy perpendicular components, one of which usually has zero moment arm.
  5. A couple has a moment but no net force, and that moment is the same about every point, which is why it is called a free vector.
  6. Any force system reduces to one resultant force at a chosen point plus one couple; special cases collapse it into a single force or a pure couple.
  7. Supports buy freedom with reactions: roller 1, pin/hinge 2, fixed 3 unknowns in a plane — count them before writing equations.
  8. Equilibrium of a rigid body means ΣF = 0 and ΣM = 0 about any one point; in three dimensions that is six scalar equations.
  9. A two-force member carries force only along the line joining its two pins; a three-force body in equilibrium has its three lines of action concurrent or parallel.
  10. Virtual work removes the support reactions from the calculation, so a problem with many unknowns can be solved with one scalar equation.

Remember and understand

Understand State the principle of transmissibility. Explain why it is valid for a rigid body but not for a deformable body or a body where internal effects matter. 8 marks

Statement: a force may be moved along its line of action without changing the external effect on a rigid body; Reason: the external effect is fixed by the force vector and its line of action, and a rigid body transmits load without changing shape; Counter-example: a rod pulled at one end versus pushed at the other has the same external reactions but opposite internal tension/compression; Consequence: forces on rigid bodies are sliding vectors, not free vectors.

Remember Define the moment of a force about a point and about an axis. Write the vector expressions, name every term, and give the SI unit. 8 marks

M_O = r × F with r the position vector from O to any point on the line of action; Magnitude M = F·d, d the perpendicular distance (moment arm); M about axis with unit vector n̂: M = n̂ · (r × F); Units: newton metre (N·m); direction by right-hand rule; Zero cases: line of action passes through the point, or is parallel to the axis.

Understand State Varignon's theorem and show, with a diagram, how it is used to take the moment of an inclined force about a point. 8 marks

Understand What is a couple? Prove that the moment of a couple is the same about every point in space, and explain why the couple moment is called a free vector. 8 marks

Two equal, opposite, parallel forces with different lines of action; Take moments about an arbitrary point O: the terms containing r cancel, leaving M = F × d; Result depends only on F and the perpendicular distance d, not on O; Hence the couple moment can be drawn anywhere; it is a free vector; A couple produces rotation only, no translation, because ΣF = 0.

Glossary

Couple
A pair of equal, opposite, parallel forces that produce rotation but no translation.
Couple moment
The moment of a couple, F × d, identical about every point in space.
Degree of freedom
The number of independent displacements a system can undergo.
Equivalent systems
Two force systems that give the same resultant force and the same moment about every point.
Force-couple system
A force together with a couple, obtained when a force is shifted to a parallel position.
Free vector
A vector whose effect is unchanged when it is moved anywhere in space; a couple moment behaves this way.
Free-body diagram
A sketch of the isolated body showing all applied loads and all support reactions.
Line of action
The infinite straight line along which a force acts.
Moment about a point
The turning effect of a force about a point, equal to r × F and measured in N·m.
Moment about an axis
The component of the moment vector along a given axis, n̂ · (r × F).
Moment arm
The perpendicular distance from a point or axis to the line of action of a force.
Resultant
The single force (and possibly couple) that replaces a whole system of forces.
Right-hand rule
A way to fix the sense of a moment vector: curl fingers from r to F, the thumb gives the direction.
Rigid body
A body whose points keep fixed distances from one another under load.
Sliding vector
A vector whose line of action is fixed but whose point of application along that line may change.
Statically determinate
A case in which the unknown reactions equal the number of independent equilibrium equations.
Support reaction
The force or moment a support exerts to prevent the motion it restrains.
Three-force body
A coplanar body in equilibrium under three forces, which must be concurrent or parallel.
Transmissibility
The rule that a force may slide along its line of action without changing its external effect on a rigid body.
Two-force body
A body loaded only at two points, so that the two forces must be equal, opposite and collinear.
Varignon's theorem
The moment of a resultant about a point equals the sum of the moments of its components about that point.
Virtual displacement
An imaginary infinitesimal displacement allowed by the supports, with real forces unchanged.
Virtual work
The work done by real forces during a virtual displacement, δU = Σ F · δr.
Wrench
The simplest reduction of a general space force system: one force plus a couple moment parallel to it.
