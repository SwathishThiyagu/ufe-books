1.1 Fundamentals of mechanics: idealizations, laws and units

A crane, a bicycle frame, a rice sack on a rope — each is a real body with an awkward shape that bends under load. Mechanics cannot solve that directly, so it swaps the body for an ideal model: a particle, or a rigid body. Newton's three laws then act on the model, in consistent SI units, and the answer comes out as a force with a magnitude and a direction. This chapter builds that chain — model, law, unit — because every later problem in statics and dynamics stands on it.

The subject, and the models it uses

Engineering mechanics studies bodies at rest or in motion under forces. Statics deals with bodies at rest. Dynamics deals with bodies in motion, and splits again: kinematics is the geometry of motion — how position, velocity and acceleration change with time, without asking what causes them — and kinetics is the study of the forces that cause that motion (see Unit 5, Kinematics and Kinetics of Particles).

Three idealizations do most of the work.

  • Particle: all the mass sits at one point, so size is ignored. Fair when the body is small compared with the distances involved, or when rotation does not matter.
  • Rigid body: the distance between any two of its points never changes. Deformation is handed to a later subject.
  • Continuum: matter is spread smoothly, so density can be defined at a point.

The fundamental quantities are length, mass and time. Force is built from them, as the next section shows.

Newton's laws, weight, and line of action

First law. A body stays at rest, or moves with constant velocity in a straight line, unless a net external force acts on it. This defines force as whatever changes motion, and it is the parent of equilibrium: zero net force means no change of velocity (see 1.6).

Second law. The net force equals the rate of change of momentum. For constant mass, F = ma: F is the net force in newtons (N), m the mass in kilograms (kg), a the acceleration in metres per second squared (m/s²).

Third law. If A pushes B, B pushes A with an equal and opposite force. The pair acts on two different bodies, so both never appear on one free body diagram (see 1.5).

W = m g

W is weight in N; m is mass in kg; g is the acceleration due to gravity, 9.81 m/s² near Earth's surface. It comes from Newton's gravitation law F = G m₁m₂/r², with G = 6.674 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg², by putting m₁ = Earth's mass and r = Earth's radius.

Mass is a property of the body, the same on the Moon. Weight is a force, and changes with g. The line of action of a force is the straight line along which it acts; for a rigid body, sliding a force along its own line of action changes nothing (Unit 2).

Units, the kgf, and the homogeneity check

SI has seven base units; mechanics needs three — the metre (m) for length, the kilogram (kg) for mass, the second (s) for time. Force is derived: 1 newton gives a 1 kg mass an acceleration of 1 m/s², so 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². A 100 g apple weighs roughly 1 N.

Prefixes scale these: k = 10³, M = 10⁶, m = 10⁻³, µ = 10⁻⁶. So 1 kN = 1000 N. The kilogram-force (kgf) is the weight of a 1 kg mass under standard gravity: 1 kgf = 9.81 N. Tyre gauges and older drawings still use it. Convert before substituting, and never add kgf to N inside one equation.

Every equation derived from physical laws is homogeneous: each term carries the same units. Empirical formulas fitted to data are the exception — their constants carry units so the terms still match. Test v² = u² + 2as. The left side is (m/s)² = m²/s²; u² is m²/s²; and 2as is m/s² × m = m²/s². All three agree, so the equation passes. The number 2 has no units. A mismatch means either the formula is misremembered or a conversion was missed — a ten-second check that catches most unit errors.

Worked example

Question. A 25 kg sack of rice hangs from a single rope. Take g = 9.81 m/s². (a) Find the weight of the sack in N and in kgf. (b) The rope is rated for 300 N — is it safe? (c) Check whether v² = u² + 2as is homogeneous in SI units.

  1. (a) Weight is mass times g: W = m g = 25 kg × 9.81 m/s² = 245.25 N.
  2. Convert to kgf by dividing by 9.81 N/kgf: 245.25 N ÷ 9.81 N/kgf = 25 kgf. This is expected: the weight of a 25 kg mass is 25 kgf by definition under standard gravity.
  3. (b) Compare with the rating: 245.25 N < 300 N, so the rope is safe. The margin is 300 − 245.25 = 54.75 N.
  4. (c) Units of each term of v² = u² + 2as: [v²] = m²/s²; [u²] = m²/s²; [2as] = (m/s²)(m) = m²/s². All three terms agree, so the equation is homogeneous. The constant 2 is dimensionless and does not affect the check.

Answer. The weight is 245.25 N, that is 25 kgf; the rope is safe because 245.25 N is less than its 300 N rating, with 54.75 N to spare; and v² = u² + 2as is homogeneous, since every term has units m²/s².

Exam traps

  • Reporting 25 kg as the weight. Mass is in kg, weight is a force in N; the weight here is 245.25 N.
  • Mixing kgf and N in one equation. Convert everything to N first, or write kgf throughout, never both.
  • Using g = 10 m/s² when the paper says 9.81, or the reverse. Use the g the question gives.
  • Drawing action and reaction on the same free body diagram and cancelling them. They act on two different bodies.
  • Treating a body as a particle when its size decides the answer — a ladder against a wall cannot topple if it is a point.

Analyze A student says the reaction to the weight of a hanging sack is the tension in the rope. Is this correct? 5 marks

No. By the third law the partner of Earth pulling the sack down is the sack pulling Earth up, with equal magnitude. Tension is a separate contact force from the rope. The sack hangs in equilibrium because tension balances weight, but that is a balance of two different forces on one body, not an action–reaction pair.

Analyze A ladder leans against a wall. Explain why the particle model fails here and which idealization you must use. 6 marks

A particle has no size and no orientation, so it has no point of contact with the wall or the floor and no lever arm. It cannot tip, slide, or feel a moment. The ladder's behaviour depends on where the forces act along its length, so it must be treated as a rigid body, with geometry and moments included (Unit 2, and ladder friction in Unit 3).

1.2 Scalars, vectors and vector algebra

A force is not just a push of some size; it also points somewhere. Mechanics therefore treats force as a vector: a quantity with magnitude and direction that adds by geometry, not by simple arithmetic. Two 3 N pulls at right angles give about 4.24 N, not 6 N. This chapter sets up vector notation, addition and subtraction, the dot and cross products, and i, j, k components. These tools turn concurrent-force problems into short component sums and prepare the equilibrium work in the next chapters.

You need: Chapter 1.1 for force as a quantity, units and the particle model.

Scalars, vectors, notation, equality

A scalar has size only: mass 5 kg, time 12 s, temperature 40 °C, work 60 J. A vector has size and direction: displacement 30 m north, velocity 12 m/s east, force 20 N along a rope. Force is a vector because its effect changes when its direction changes.

Write a vector as A (bold) or with an arrow. Its size is A or |A|, a positive scalar in the same unit. A unit vector has size 1 and keeps direction.

Â = A / A

Â is the unit vector along A; A is the magnitude of A; Â has no unit.

The position vector r of a point P is the arrow from the origin O to P. It fixes where P is relative to O.

r = x i + y j + z k

r is the position vector in metres; x, y, z are the coordinates of P in metres; i, j, k are unit vectors along the x, y, z axes.

Two vectors are equal when their magnitudes and directions match. In particle mechanics all forces act at one point, so two forces with the same magnitude and direction are equivalent even if drawn at different places on the page. For a rigid body the line of action matters; see Unit 2.

Adding and subtracting vectors

The triangle law adds two vectors. Place the tail of B at the head of A; the resultant R runs from the tail of A to the head of B.

  1. Draw A to scale with an arrow.
  2. From the head of A, draw B to scale.
  3. Draw R = A + B from the start of A to the end of B.
  4. Read the length of R for magnitude and its angle for direction.

The parallelogram law gives the same result. Draw A and B from one point, complete the parallelogram, and take the diagonal from that point as A + B. This is why two forces at a point are combined by the diagonal, not by adding their sizes.

Subtraction is addition of the negative: A − B = A + (−B). The vector −B has the same size as B and the opposite direction. Multiplying by a scalar c changes the size to |c|B; if c is negative, the direction reverses.

Sizes add only when the vectors are parallel and in the same direction. At any other angle, use the triangle or parallelogram construction, or add components.

Components, dot product and cross product

In three dimensions a vector is fixed by three components along i, j, k.

<b>A</b> = A<sub>x</sub> i + A<sub>y</sub> j + A<sub>z</sub> k

Ax, Ay, Az are the components in the same unit as A; i, j, k are unit vectors along x, y, z.

A = √(A<sub>x</sub><sup>2</sup> + A<sub>y</sub><sup>2</sup> + A<sub>z</sub><sup>2</sup>)

A is the magnitude of A, in the same unit as its components.

The dot product multiplies two vectors to give a scalar. The angle θ is measured between the two vectors placed tail to tail.

A·B = AB cos θ

A·B is a scalar; A and B are the magnitudes; θ is the angle between them, in degrees or radians.

The cross product gives a vector perpendicular to both. Its direction follows the right-hand rule: curl the fingers from A to B, and the thumb points along A × B.

|A × B| = AB sin θ

|A × B| is the magnitude of the cross product; θ is the smaller angle between the vectors, 0° to 180°.

For vectors in the xy plane, A × B = (AxBy − AyBx) k. A positive result points along +k, out of the page. The angle between two vectors follows from cos θ = A·B/(AB).

Component addition, resolution and resultants of several forces are worked out in Chapter 1.3 and Chapter 1.4.

Worked example

Question. Forces F1 = 3i + 4j N and F2 = 4i + 3j N act at a particle. Find F1 + F2, F1 − F2, F1·F2, F1 × F2, and the angle between the two forces.

  1. Add components: F1 + F2 = (3 + 4)i + (4 + 3)j = 7i + 7j N.
  2. Magnitude = √(7² + 7²) = 7√2 = 9.90 N. Direction = tan−1(7/7) = 45° from the +x axis.
  3. Subtract components: F1 − F2 = (3 − 4)i + (4 − 3)j = −i + j N.
  4. Magnitude = √((−1)² + 1²) = √2 = 1.41 N. Direction = 135° from the +x axis.
  5. Dot product: F1·F2 = (3)(4) + (4)(3) = 12 + 12 = 24 N².
  6. Magnitudes: F1 = √(3² + 4²) = 5 N; F2 = √(4² + 3²) = 5 N.
  7. cos θ = 24/(5 × 5) = 0.96, so θ = 16.26°.
  8. Cross product in the plane: F1 × F2 = (3 × 3 − 4 × 4)k = −7k N². Its magnitude is 7 N² and it points into the page. Check: 5 × 5 × sin 16.26° = 7.00 N².

Answer. F1 + F2 = 7i + 7j N (9.90 N at 45°); F1 − F2 = −i + j N (1.41 N at 135°); F1·F2 = 24 N²; F1 × F2 = −7k N²; the angle is 16.26°.

Exam traps

  • Adding magnitudes as if vectors were scalars: 3 N + 4 N is 7 N only when the forces are parallel and in the same direction.
  • Writing a vector answer as a bare number, such as 9.90 N, with no direction. A vector answer needs magnitude and direction, or components.
  • Using the dot-product angle as a bearing. cos θ = A·B/(AB) gives the angle between the vectors, 0° to 180°, not the angle from the x-axis.
  • Reversing a cross product: A × B = −(B × A). The sign decides whether the vector points out of or into the page.
  • Mixing units or calculator modes. Components must share one unit before adding, and θ must match the degree or radian setting.

1.3 Resolution and composition of forces

A single force can be replaced by two or more forces that add to it. That replacement is resolution. The reverse, composition, rebuilds one force from several. Engineers resolve a slanted force into horizontal and vertical parts because those parts enter equilibrium equations separately. They compose a set of pulls into one resultant. The same idea handles a rope tension, a wind load on a hoarding, or a crane cable, and it is the first calculation step in most statics problems.

You need: Chapter 1.2 for vector components, unit vectors and the dot product.

Rectangular resolution

Take a force F of 50 N at θ = 30° from the positive x-axis. Drop a perpendicular from the tip of the arrow to each axis. The two perpendicular sides are the rectangular components. They replace the original force exactly, because their vector sum returns F.

F<sub>x</sub> = F cos θ,   F<sub>y</sub> = F sin θ

F is the force magnitude in N; θ is the angle from the positive x-axis, measured counterclockwise; Fx and Fy are signed components in N.

θ is measured counterclockwise from +x. In quadrant I both signs are positive. In quadrant II x is negative and y positive. In quadrant III both negative. In quadrant IV x positive and y negative. Decide the signs from the sketch before you substitute. Do not let the calculator choose for you.

  1. Draw the force arrow and mark θ from the positive x-axis.
  2. Write the signs for the quadrant on the sketch: I (+,+), II (−,+), III (−,−), IV (+,−).
  3. Substitute Fx = F cos θ and Fy = F sin θ with those signs.
  4. Check that √(Fx² + Fy²) equals F.

A force of 50 N at 30° gives Fx = 50 cos 30° = 43.3 N and Fy = 50 sin 30° = 25.0 N. A force of 50 N at 150° gives Fx = −43.3 N and Fy = +25.0 N. The magnitude is still 50 N.

Oblique resolution with the sine rule

Two chosen directions need not be at right angles. Suppose a force F is to be split along line a and line b. Draw F as one side of a triangle. Draw the two components P and Q as the other sides. The triangle closes, so the sine rule applies.

The angle opposite P is the angle between F and line b. The angle opposite Q is the angle between F and line a. The angle opposite F is the angle between the two chosen lines. Use magnitudes; put the negative sign back if a component must point opposite its chosen arrow.

P / sin γ = Q / sin β = F / sin α

F is the force being resolved in N; P is the component along line a; Q is along line b; α is the angle between lines a and b; β is the angle between F and line a; γ is the angle between F and line b.

In the worked example the force is at 30°, the chosen lines are at 0° and 75°, so β = 30°, γ = 45°, α = 75°. The sine rule then gives P = 36.6 N and Q = 25.9 N.

Composition of two forces

Composition is the reverse. Two forces P and Q act at a point with angle α between their directions. Complete the parallelogram. The diagonal from the point is the resultant R.

R = √(P² + Q² + 2PQ cos α)

P and Q are the two forces in N; α is the angle between their directions; R is the resultant magnitude in N.

tan θ = Q sin α / (P + Q cos α)

θ is the angle from P toward Q; P, Q, α are as above.

The formula uses the angle between P and Q, not the angle between R and either force. Many wrong answers come from that swap. Check the special cases in the table.

Some syllabi call composition the resultant of two forces and resolution splitting a force into components. For three or more forces, add components instead; see Chapter 1.4.

Worked example

Question. A force of 50 N acts at 30° above the horizontal. (a) Resolve it into horizontal and vertical components. (b) Resolve the same force along the horizontal and along a line at 75° above the horizontal.

  1. (a) Take θ = 30° from the positive x-axis. The horizontal component is Fx = 50 cos 30°.
  2. cos 30° = 0.8660, so Fx = 50 × 0.8660 = 43.3 N.
  3. The vertical component is Fy = 50 sin 30°. sin 30° = 0.5000, so Fy = 50 × 0.5000 = 25.0 N.
  4. (b) The chosen directions are 0° and 75°. The force is at 30°. Angle between F and horizontal = 30°. Angle between F and 75° line = 45°. Angle between the two chosen lines = 75°.
  5. In the force triangle: P (horizontal) is opposite 45°, Q (along 75°) is opposite 30°, and F = 50 N is opposite 75°.
  6. Sine rule: P / sin 45° = Q / sin 30° = 50 / sin 75°.
  7. sin 45° = 0.7071, sin 30° = 0.5000, sin 75° = 0.9659. P = 50 × 0.7071 / 0.9659 = 36.6 N.
  8. Q = 50 × 0.5000 / 0.9659 = 25.9 N.
  9. Check in rectangular form: 36.6i + 25.9(cos 75° i + sin 75° j) = (36.6 + 6.70)i + 25.0j = 43.3i + 25.0j N.

Answer. Fx = 43.3 N, Fy = 25.0 N; and along the chosen lines P = 36.6 N horizontal and Q = 25.9 N at 75°.

Exam traps

  • If θ is measured from the vertical, the horizontal part is F sin θ and the vertical part is F cos θ. Do not copy F cos θ for x without checking where θ starts.
  • Dropping the minus sign in quadrants II or III. A force of 50 N at 150° has Fx = −43.3 N, not +43.3 N.
  • In oblique resolution, putting an angle in the wrong place of the sine rule. Label the side opposite each angle before dividing.
  • Using the angle between R and P instead of the angle between P and Q in the parallelogram formula. α is between the two forces.
  • Using tan θ = Q sin α/(P + Q cos α) but reporting θ from Q. θ is measured from P toward Q.

1.4 Resultant of concurrent forces in a plane

Several forces act at one point: ropes on a ring, a bolt on a bracket, a hook carrying a load. Mechanics replaces them with one force doing the same job — the resultant. Forces are vectors, so their sizes do not add. Add the x parts to get Rx, add the y parts to get Ry, then rebuild one force. Every particle statics problem starts here.

You need: Chapter 1.2 for vector addition, and Chapter 1.3 for resolving a force into components.

Write each force as components

Put O at the meeting point. Take +x right, +y up. Measure θ anticlockwise from +x. A 40 N force straight down has θ = 270°.

F<sub>x</sub> = F cos θ,   F<sub>y</sub> = F sin θ

F is the force magnitude in newtons; θ is its angle in degrees anticlockwise from +x; Fx and Fy are the horizontal and vertical parts in newtons, each carrying its own sign.

Signs come from the calculator. cos 150° = −0.866 and sin 150° = +0.5, so 75 N at 150° gives −64.95 N and +37.5 N. Adding sizes, 60 + 100 + 75 + 40 = 275 N, is wrong.

R<sub>x</sub> = ΣF<sub>x</sub> = F<sub>1</sub>cos θ<sub>1</sub> + F<sub>2</sub>cos θ<sub>2</sub> + … ,   R<sub>y</sub> = ΣF<sub>y</sub> = F<sub>1</sub>sin θ<sub>1</sub> + F<sub>2</sub>sin θ<sub>2</sub> + …

Rx and Ry are the two totals in newtons; Σ means add over every force, one term per force.

  1. Draw axes through O and fix the sign convention.
  2. Convert every angle to θ anticlockwise from +x. Change slopes, bearings or angles from +y first.
  3. Compute F cos θ and F sin θ, keeping calculator signs.
  4. Add x parts to get Rx; add y parts to get Ry.

Rebuild one force from the two totals

Rx and Ry are perpendicular, so Pythagoras and tangent give size and direction.

R = √(R<sub>x</sub>² + R<sub>y</sub>²),   α = tan⁻¹(|R<sub>y</sub>| / |R<sub>x</sub>|)

R is the magnitude of the resultant in newtons; α is the acute angle in degrees between the resultant and the x-axis, always positive. If Rx = 0, the resultant is vertical: θ = 90° when Ry > 0, θ = 270° when Ry < 0, and R = |Ry|.

α alone is not the answer. tan⁻¹ returns a first-quadrant angle; place the resultant in its actual quadrant.

The force polygon checks the arithmetic

Draw forces tip to tail. Order is free because vector addition is commutative. Arrow from first tail to last tip is R.

The figure: 60 N along +x, 100 N at 60°, 75 N at 150°, 40 N down; closing arrow from O to E is 95.4 N.

If the last tip returns to the start, the polygon closes and R = 0. That is equilibrium; Chapter 1.6 turns it into equations.

  • A rough scale drawing catches a missing force or a 180° slip.
  • A closed sketch is not proof; confirm Rx = 0 and Ry = 0 to two decimal places.
  • Reversing order must give the same closing arrow.

Special cases and exam wording

  • Collinear: forces on one line add algebraically: 30 N right plus 20 N left is 10 N right.
  • Two forces F1 and F2 with angle γ between them: R = √(F1² + F2² + 2F1F2cos γ). At γ = 90° this is √(F1² + F2²); at γ = 180° it is |F1 − F2|.
  • n equal forces F spaced 360°/n apart give R = 0. Three 100 N forces at 0°, 120°, 240° cancel exactly.
  • Usual wording: 'determine the resultant of the force system and its direction with the x-axis'. Magnitude and quadrant carry marks.
  • Some syllabi call this 'resultant of several concurrent forces in a plane'. Method is identical.

Worked example

Question. Four ropes pull on a ring at O. F1 = 60 N at 0°, F2 = 100 N at 60°, F3 = 75 N at 150° and F4 = 40 N at 270°, all angles measured anticlockwise from +x. Determine the resultant and its direction with the x-axis.

  1. Axes: +x right, +y up, θ anticlockwise. All forces act at O, so concurrent.
  2. x parts: F1cos 0° = 60 × 1 = 60.00 N; F2cos 60° = 100 × 0.5 = 50.00 N; F3cos 150° = 75 × (−0.8660) = −64.95 N; F4cos 270° = 40 × 0 = 0 N.
  3. Rx = 60.00 + 50.00 − 64.95 + 0 = 45.05 N (write 45.0 N).
  4. y parts: F1sin 0° = 0 N; F2sin 60° = 100 × 0.8660 = 86.60 N; F3sin 150° = 75 × 0.5 = 37.50 N; F4sin 270° = 40 × (−1) = −40.00 N.
  5. Ry = 0 + 86.60 + 37.50 − 40.00 = 84.10 N (write 84.1 N).
  6. Magnitude: R = √(45.05² + 84.10²) = √(2029.5 + 7072.8) = √9102.3 = 95.4 N.
  7. Direction: α = tan⁻¹(84.10 / 45.05) = tan⁻¹(1.867) = 61.8°. Rx > 0 and Ry > 0, so first quadrant and θ = α.
  8. Rough visual check: the tip-to-tail sketch closes from O to E at about 95.4 N at 61.8°. The real check is Rx = 45.05 N and Ry = 84.10 N.

Answer. R = 95.4 N acting at 61.8° anticlockwise from +x, that is 61.8° above the horizontal to the right.

Exam traps

  • Writing θ = tan⁻¹(Ry/Rx) when Rx < 0. Use α = tan⁻¹(|Ry|/|Rx|), then place the resultant with the sign table: if Rx < 0 and Ry > 0, θ = 180° − α; if both are negative, θ = 180° + α; if Rx > 0 and Ry < 0, θ = 360° − α. Raw tan⁻¹(Ry/Rx) is 180° away only when Rx < 0.
  • Adding magnitudes: 60 + 100 + 75 + 40 = 275 N. Valid only when all forces are collinear and in the same sense.
  • Angles from +y, from a wall, or as a slope 3 vertical to 4 horizontal: convert to θ anticlockwise from +x before cos or sin.
  • Calculator in radian mode: sin 60 gives −0.305 instead of 0.866.
  • Reporting R without quadrant, or saying '61.8° with the x-axis' when it points down and left. Direction needs magnitude and quadrant.

1.5 Free body diagram

An equilibrium equation talks about forces on one body. So cut that body free of everything touching it and replace each support by the force it gives. That drawing is the free body diagram (FBD). Every statics problem — a lamp on a ceiling, a signboard on a bracket, a block on a ramp — starts here. Get the diagram right and the rest is arithmetic.

You need Chapter 1.3 (resolving forces) and Chapter 1.4 (adding forces at a point).

Cut the body free

Newton's laws apply to a chosen body. They say nothing about the supports. Remove those supports and replace each by the force it gave the body. What remains is the free body: the body alone, with arrows for every external force on it.

This follows from Newton's third law. If a wall pushes a block, the block pushes the wall back equally. Only the wall's push acts on the block. The other force belongs on the wall's diagram. One diagram, one body.

A free body diagram is that drawing. Each arrow carries a label, a direction and a magnitude, or a symbol if unknown. The equations of Chapter 1.6 are written straight from the arrows.

Six steps, in order

  1. Choose the body: a block, ring or knot. If a rope tension is asked, the knot may be easier than the block.
  2. Draw the body alone, a simple outline. No walls, floor, or rope drawn as an object.
  3. Add the weight W = mg, straight down, at the centroid. m is mass in kg; g = 9.81 m/s². Weight never tilts with a slope.
  4. Replace each support by its reaction. A smooth surface gives a normal reaction N, perpendicular to the surface. A rope gives tension T along the rope, away from the body. A pin gives two unknown components.
  5. Add every applied load: hanging weight, push, pull, wind.
  6. Check the count. Every arrow must have a named source, and every support you cut must appear as an arrow.

Most marks are lost by forgetting one support, not by slipping on signs.

What never appears on the diagram

  • Internal forces: tension inside a single rod, force between the two halves of one body.
  • Forces the body exerts on others: the block's push on the plane, the rope's pull on the ceiling. These belong on the other body's diagram.
  • The support itself — the wall, floor, pin or rope drawn as an object. Once you cut the support away, the support is gone from the picture and only the reaction force it gave remains as an arrow.

The inclined plane is the classic test. A block rests on a plane tilted at angle θ to the horizontal. The normal reaction is perpendicular to the sloped surface, so it makes angle θ with the vertical. The weight stays vertical. Many students draw the weight along the slope; it never is.

W = mg, N ⊥ surface, T along the rope

W is weight in N, m is mass in kg, g = 9.81 m/s², N is the normal reaction in N perpendicular to the smooth surface, T is tension in N acting away from the body along the rope. Ropes pull only; smooth surfaces push only.

From diagram to equations

Choose axes. On a slope, take x along the plane and y perpendicular to it. Resolve every arrow onto these axes, as in Chapter 1.3. Then write ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 for a particle at rest, as in Chapter 1.6. Two equations, two unknowns at most.

Rigid bodies and moments come in Unit 2. Friction on an incline comes in Unit 3.

Worked example

Question. A block of mass 10 kg rests on a smooth plane inclined at 30° to the horizontal. A string parallel to the plane holds it from sliding down. Take g = 9.81 m/s². Draw the free body diagram and find the tension in the string and the normal reaction from the plane.

  1. Body: the block. Cut away the plane and the string.

  6. Perpendicular to the plane: N − W cos 30° = 0, so N = 98.1 × 0.8660 = 84.96 N.
  7. Check: T is along the plane and N is perpendicular, so T ⊥ N and together they balance W. T² + N² = 49.05² + 84.96² = 2406 + 7218 = 9624 = 98.1² = W². Agrees. Equivalently, T = W sin 30°, N = W cos 30°, and the squares add to W².

1.6 Equilibrium of a particle; Lami's theorem

A particle is a body whose size we ignore, so every force on it passes through one point (see Chapter 1.1). Equilibrium means those forces add to zero; one vector equation decides the problem. The same calculation holds a hanging lamp, a knot in a crane rope and a cable between two poles. In a plane it gives two scalar equations. With exactly three forces it becomes Lami's theorem.

You need Chapter 1.4 for vector sums of concurrent forces and Chapter 1.5 for free body diagrams.

The condition for equilibrium

A particle is in equilibrium when the vector sum of forces on it is zero. Newton's second law then gives zero acceleration: the particle is at rest or moves in a straight line at constant speed. Statics uses the at-rest case — a knot, hook or joint that does not move.

ΣF = 0

ΣF is the vector sum of every external force. Zero means the forces cancel in direction and size, not that each is zero.

Resolve each force into components (see Chapter 1.3). Choose x and y axes. A force of magnitude F at angle θ from the positive x axis has components F cos θ along x and F sin θ along y. The vector equation splits into two scalar equations.

ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0

Fx and Fy are components in newtons (N); θ is measured anticlockwise from positive x. Both equations must hold together.

Two equations fix at most two unknowns — usually two tension magnitudes, or one tension and one angle. Three unknown magnitudes cannot be found from statics alone.

  1. Draw the free body diagram of the particle only.
  2. Count the forces. More than three usually means use components.
  3. Choose axes; align one axis with an unknown force if that removes an angle.
  4. Write ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0, keep signs by direction, and solve.
  5. Substitute both answers into the other equation as a check.

Lami's theorem

Suppose exactly three coplanar forces meet at one point and keep a particle in equilibrium. Then each force divided by the sine of the angle between the other two forces gives the same number.

P / sin α = Q / sin β = R / sin γ

P, Q, R are magnitudes in N; α is the angle between Q and R (facing P); β is between P and R; γ is between P and Q. The three angles sum to 360°.

Proof. Draw the three forces tip to tail. Their sum is zero, so they close into a triangle with sides P, Q, R. Inside that triangle, the angle between sides Q and R is 180° − α, because Q and R keep their directions. The sine rule gives P / sin A = Q / sin B = R / sin C, and sin(180° − α) = sin α. Replacing each triangle angle gives Lami's theorem.

The naming trips people up. α is the angle made by the other two force lines, opening towards P — not the angle beside P. In the lamp below, T₁ pairs with 150°, not its own 30° tilt.

  • Exactly three forces, all meeting at one point, all in one plane. Four or more forces break the triangle argument.
  • Every force must appear once on the left side; the weight counts as a force.
  • Use Lami for a quick check even when you solved by components.

Choosing the method

Lami's theorem is a shortcut, not a separate law, so it cannot give an answer that components would miss. Use it when three angles are given and the forces lie awkwardly between the axes. Use components when forces are along x and y, when there are four or more forces, or when one force direction is unknown.

A crane hook often carries three forces: two sling tensions and the load. If sling angles are given, Lami gives both tensions quickly. With a tag line attached there are four forces, and only component equations are safe.

Measure each angle between the two force lines themselves. Check that the three angles add to 360°. If one angle comes out negative, you measured between the wrong pair of forces.

Worked example

Question. A lamp of weight 100 N hangs from a knot O. String OA runs 30° above the horizontal towards the left; string OB runs 60° above the horizontal towards the right. The knot is at rest. Find the tensions T₁ in OA and T₂ in OB, using Lami's theorem, and verify by components.

  1. Free body diagram of O: three forces only — T₁ along OA at 150° from positive x, T₂ along OB at 60°, W = 100 N straight down at 270°.
  2. Angle between the strings = 180° − 30° − 60° = 90°. This is γ, the angle facing W.
  3. Angle facing T₁ is between T₂ and W: 90° + 60° = 150°. Call it α.
  4. Angle facing T₂ is between T₁ and W: 90° + 30° = 120°. Call it β. Check: 90° + 150° + 120° = 360°.
  5. Lami: T₁ / sin 150° = T₂ / sin 120° = 100 / sin 90°.
  6. The common value is 100 / 1 = 100 N. So T₁ = 100 × sin 150° = 100 × 0.5 = 50 N.
  7. T₂ = 100 × sin 120° = 100 × 0.866 = 86.6 N.
  8. x check: 86.6 cos 60° − 50 cos 30° = 43.3 − 43.3 = 0.
  9. y check: 86.6 sin 60° + 50 sin 30° − 100 = 75 + 25 − 100 = 0. Both equations hold.

Answer. T₁ = 50 N and T₂ = 86.6 N, both tensions pulling away from the knot.

Exam traps

  • Dividing a force by the sine of an angle next to it instead of the angle between the other two forces. T₁ goes with 150°, never with its own 30°.
  • Using Lami's theorem on four or more forces, or on forces that do not all pass through one point. The closed triangle proof fails.
  • Leaving the weight out of the free body diagram, then wondering why the two tensions do not balance each other.
  • Measuring every angle from a different reference line and mixing signs in ΣFx and ΣFy. Fix one axis and measure all angles from it.
  • Calculator set to radians while the question is in degrees, or typing sin 120 as a negative number without thinking.

1.7 Forces in space: resultant and equilibrium

Many real forces do not lie in one plane: a cable from a tower, a tripod leg, a corner of a cupboard. In space a force needs three components, not two. The same vector rules still work, but equilibrium now gives three equations and a resultant needs direction cosines. The method builds a force from two points, adds several such forces, and uses the three sums to find unknown forces on a particle. It is the three-dimensional form of the plane resultant and equilibrium you already know.

You need: vector algebra, resolution and composition, free body diagrams and particle equilibrium.

A three-dimensional frame and direction cosines

Place the origin O at any convenient point. Draw x, y and z axes so that curling the fingers of the right hand from x to y makes the thumb point along z. This is the right-hand rule. The unit vectors along the axes are i, j and k. A point A has coordinates (x₁, y₁, z₁) in metres; B has (x₂, y₂, z₂). The vector from A to B is r = (x₂−x₁)i + (y₂−y₁)j + (z₂−z₁)k. Subtract tail coordinates from head coordinates.

|r| = √(rx² + ry² + rz²),  n̂ = r/|r|,  F = F n̂

rx, ry, rz are the components of r in metres; |r| is the length in metres; n̂ is the unit vector along r; the left F is the force vector in newtons; the right F is its magnitude in newtons.

The components of F are Fx = F cos θx, Fy = F cos θy and Fz = F cos θz. θx is the angle between F and the positive x-axis, and similarly for y and z. The direction cosines are cos θx = rx/|r|, cos θy = ry/|r| and cos θz = rz/|r|. They satisfy cos²θx + cos²θy + cos²θz = 1. A vector from B to A is −r, so reversing the order reverses every component and every direction cosine.

Resultant of concurrent forces in space

Concurrent forces all act at the same particle. Write each force as three components with signs. Add the x components, the y components and the z components separately: Rx = ΣFx, Ry = ΣFy and Rz = ΣFz. The resultant is R = Rx i + Ry j + Rz k. Its magnitude is |R| = √(Rx² + Ry² + Rz²). Its direction cosines are cos θx = Rx/|R|, cos θy = Ry/|R| and cos θz = Rz/|R|. Here θx, θy, θz are the angles between R and the positive axes. The identity cos²θx + cos²θy + cos²θz = 1 is a check. There is no single plane angle for a space force; direction cosines replace it. If the three totals are all zero, the forces have no resultant and the particle is in equilibrium.

  1. Write every force from its magnitude and direction cosines, or from two points using n̂.
  2. Add all x components with signs; repeat for y and z.
  3. Compute R and its direction cosines from the three totals.
  4. Check that the squares of the direction cosines add to 1.

Equilibrium of a particle in space

A particle is in equilibrium when the vector sum of all forces on it is zero. In space this gives three scalar equations: ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0 and ΣFz = 0. Each equation is independent. With known lines of action, these equations can find at most three unknown scalar magnitudes or angles. A cable carries tension along its own line and pulls the particle toward its support (away from the particle, along the cable). A two-force member may push or pull: a strut (compression) pushes, a tie (tension) pulls. Assume a direction, and a negative answer means the opposite direction. Draw a three-dimensional free body diagram first: mark axes, the particle, and every force with its line and assumed arrow. Some syllabi call this 'equilibrium of a particle in space'. Three-dimensional support reactions are not solved here; see Unit 2, Equilibrium of rigid bodies in two and three dimensions.

ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0, ΣFz = 0

Fx, Fy, Fz are the x, y, z components of every force on the particle in newtons; the sums include signs.

  4. r_AC = (5−1)i + (−10−2)j + (3−0)k = 4i − 12j + 3k m. |r_AC| = √(4²+(−12)²+3²) = 13 m.
  5. F₂ = (130/13)(4i − 12j + 3k) = 40i − 120j + 30k N.
  6. R = F₁ + F₂ = (60+40)i + (80−120)j + (240+30)k = 100i − 40j + 270k N. |R| = √(100²+(−40)²+270²) = √84500 = 290.7 N.
  7. Equilibrium needs F₁ + F₂ + F₃ = 0, so F₃ = −R = −100i + 40j − 270k N. Its magnitude is the same, 290.7 N.

Answer. F₁ = 60i + 80j + 240k N with direction cosines 0.231, 0.308, 0.923; F₂ = 40i − 120j + 30k N; R = 100i − 40j + 270k N of magnitude 290.7 N; and F₃ = −100i + 40j − 270k N of magnitude 290.7 N.

Exam traps

  • Using raw coordinates x₂, y₂, z₂ instead of coordinate differences; the vector must start at A.
  • Forgetting to divide by |AB|, so the force is magnitude times r rather than magnitude times n̂.
  • Taking the line B to A when the force is stated A to B; reverse order reverses signs.
  • Adding magnitudes or using one plane angle; space forces add component by component.
  • Expecting three equilibrium equations to solve four unknown cable tensions.

Remember Write the three scalar equations of equilibrium for a particle in space. 2 marks

Understand Why do the direction cosines satisfy cos²θx + cos²θy + cos²θz = 1? 3 marks

They are the components of a unit vector; a unit vector has length 1, so the sum of squared components is 1.

Apply A 50 N force acts from P(1, 0, 2) m to Q(3, 4, 2) m. Write it as a vector. 4 marks

Resultant of cables is 30i + 40j + 50k N; fourth force = −30i − 40j − 50k N, magnitude 70.7 N.

Understand In a 3D free body diagram, which way does a cable tension on the particle point? 3 marks

Away from the particle along the cable, because a cable pulls.

Apply A direction has cos θx = 0.4 and cos θy = 0.5. Find the positive cos θz. 4 marks

cos²θz = 1 − 0.4² − 0.5² = 0.59; cos θz = √0.59 = 0.768.

Analyze A particle has ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0, but ΣFz = 12 N. Is it in equilibrium? Explain. 6 marks

No. Equilibrium requires all three sums zero. The net force is 12 N along z, so the particle accelerates unless another force supplies −12 N along z.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. In practice, three-dimensional force balance is the daily tool of riggers, crane planners and spacecraft engineers: a spreader bar with four slings becomes a particle equilibrium problem when the bar is small compared with the sling lengths, so all slings meet at one point and the bar's own weight is neglected. Finite element software builds the same three equations at every node, then solves thousands of them together. Search for 'space truss analysis', 'cable net structures' or '3D equilibrium particle' to see the next step. The same direction-cosine idea reappears in computer graphics, where a surface normal is a unit vector.

Takeaways

  1. Ideal models such as the particle and the rigid body make real machines solvable with Newton's laws.
  2. Units must be consistent; use SI N, kg and m/s², and convert kgf to N before calculating.
  3. Forces are vectors, so they add by the triangle or parallelogram law or by components, never by adding their sizes alone.
  4. Resolution and composition are inverse operations; rectangular components use cos and sin of the angle from an axis.
  5. For concurrent coplanar forces, Rx = ΣFx and Ry = ΣFy; the resultant has magnitude √(Rx² + Ry²) and direction tan θ = Ry/Rx.
  6. A free-body diagram shows only external forces after supports are removed, and it is the starting point for equilibrium equations.
  7. A particle is in equilibrium when ΣF = 0, which gives ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 in a plane.
  8. Lami's theorem applies only to exactly three coplanar concurrent forces in equilibrium; in space, equilibrium gives three component equations and a resultant needs direction cosines.

Collinear forces

One of two or more vectors whose vector sum equals a given vector.

Process of replacing several forces by one resultant force.

Forces whose lines of action pass through a common point.

Ideal model in which matter is treated as continuous rather than made of separate particles.

Forces whose lines of action lie in one plane.

Cosine of the angle between a vector and a coordinate axis.

Study of bodies whose motion changes under forces.

Condition in which the vector sum of all forces on a particle is zero.

Sketch of an isolated body showing only the external forces acting on it.

Force equal to the weight of one kilogram under standard gravity, about 9.80665 N.
Kinematics
Study of motion without considering the forces that cause it.
Kinetics
Study of motion and the forces that produce or change it.
Lami's theorem
For three coplanar concurrent forces in equilibrium, each force divided by the sine of the angle between the other two is constant.
Mechanics
Branch of science that studies forces and the motion or equilibrium they produce.
Newton
SI unit of force; 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².

Rule that adds two vectors by the diagonal of the parallelogram formed from them.
Particle
Ideal body with size ignored so that all forces act at one point.

Process of replacing one force by its components.
Resultant
Single force that replaces a system of forces with the same effect.

Body in which the distance between any two points remains fixed.
Scalar
Quantity described by magnitude alone.
Statics
Study of bodies that are at rest or in equilibrium under forces.

Rule that adds two vectors by placing them head to tail and drawing the closing side.
Unit vector
Vector with magnitude one, used to show direction.

Quantity described by magnitude and direction.
