3.1 Simple trusses: method of joints

A simple truss is a triangle-based framework of straight members joined by pins. Loads act only at the joints, so each member carries axial force: tension or compression. The method of joints solves the truss one joint at a time, using two equilibrium equations per joint. It exists because a whole truss has too many unknowns at once, but each joint is a particle in equilibrium. You see the idea in roof trusses over factory sheds, railway platform shelters, crane jibs and transmission towers.

You need: equilibrium of a particle and free-body diagrams from Unit 1, and support reactions from Unit 2.

The simple truss model

A truss is a structure of straight members joined at their ends. The joints are nodes. A simple truss is built from triangles, so it keeps its shape under load. Roofs, platform sheds and tower bracings use trusses because a triangle carries load with light members.

Four assumptions make hand analysis possible. Members are light and straight. Joints are frictionless pins. Loads and reactions act only at joints. Members carry only axial force, either tension or compression. These assumptions ignore member weight and bending; real gusset plates add small moments, but axial forces are still close.

m = 2j − 3

m is the number of members and j is the number of joints. A truss with this count is perfect: stable and statically determinate. If m > 2j − 3 the truss has extra members and is statically indeterminate. If m < 2j − 3 the truss is short of members and is a mechanism: it changes shape under load and is not stable. The count is necessary, not sufficient: the members must also form triangles.

Sign convention and method of joints

Use tension positive. At each joint, draw every unknown member force as an arrow pulling away from the joint. A positive answer means tension. A negative answer means compression: the member pushes the joint, but you still drew the arrow outward and let the sign tell you.

  1. Find support reactions from the whole-truss free-body diagram.
  2. Choose a joint with at most two unknown member forces. A support joint often qualifies.
  3. Draw that joint alone. Show known loads, reactions, and unknown member forces as arrows away from the joint.
  4. Resolve along horizontal and vertical: ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0.
  5. Solve the two equations. Keep each sign; do not flip a compression member to tension midway.
  6. Move to the next joint. Use the last joint as a check.

ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 at every joint

ΣFx is the sum of horizontal force components on the joint and ΣFy is the sum of vertical components; both are zero because the joint is a particle in equilibrium.

Start where at most two member forces are unknown. If a joint has three or more unknowns, do not start there; use the method of sections when you need only a few member forces.

Zero-force members and checks

A zero-force member carries no load for the given loading. Find these first; they reduce work. At a joint with two non-collinear members and no external load or reaction, both members are zero-force members. At a joint with three members, two collinear and the third not collinear, the third is zero-force if no load acts there.

  • A zero-force member is still part of the structure. It holds shape under other loads.
  • Remove it only for the current load case, never from the real structure.
  • A negative answer is compression, not an error.
  • Check the last joint: both sums should close to zero. A large residual means a sign slip.

A zero-force member keeps the truss stable and helps it carry later loads. It is zero only in the load case you are solving.

Worked example

Question. A pin-jointed truss has joints A(0,0), B(4,0), C(2,3). Members are AB, AC and BC. A 10 kN load acts vertically down at C. A is a pin and B is a roller on the horizontal. Find the forces in AC, BC and AB by the method of joints. Use tension positive.

  1. Geometry: AB = 4 m. AC = BC = √(2² + 3²) = √13 = 3.606 m. For AC and BC, sin θ = 3/√13 = 0.8321 and cos θ = 2/√13 = 0.5547, where θ = 56.31° is the angle above horizontal.
  2. Whole-truss reactions. No horizontal load gives Ax = 0. Moments about A: By × 4 − 10 × 2 = 0, so By = 5 kN up. ΣFy = 0 gives Ay + By − 10 = 0, so Ay = 5 kN up.
  3. Joint A. Unknowns T_AC and T_AB are drawn as tension away from A: T_AB to the right, T_AC along A→C. Known Ay = 5 kN up. ΣFy: 5 + T_AC sin θ = 0.
  4. Solve: T_AC = −5 / (3/√13) = −6.009 kN. ΣFx: T_AB + T_AC cos θ = 0, so T_AB = −(−6.009)(2/√13) = +3.333 kN.
  5. Joint B. By = 5 kN up. T_AB pulls left; T_BC pulls along B→C, with components (−T_BC cos θ, +T_BC sin θ). ΣFy: 5 + T_BC sin θ = 0, so T_BC = −6.009 kN.
  6. Check at B: ΣFx = −T_AB − T_BC cos θ = −3.333 − (−6.009)(0.5547) = 0. Check at C: each compression member pushes C upward with 6.009 × 0.8321 = 5.000 kN; the two upward 5 kN forces balance the 10 kN downward load.

Answer. AC = −6.01 kN (6.01 kN compression), BC = −6.01 kN (6.01 kN compression), AB = +3.33 kN (3.33 kN tension).

Exam traps

  • Starting a joint before finding support reactions. The joint then has an unknown reaction that two equations cannot handle.
  • Drawing a compression member arrow toward the joint while keeping the sign positive. Keep tension positive and draw every unknown arrow away; let the minus sign mean compression.
  • Using the wrong angle at B or C. At A the member AC rises to the right; at B the member BC rises to the left. Resolve with the components that match the joint.
  • Deleting zero-force members from the drawing or the real truss. They are zero only for this load case.
  • Treating m = 2j − 3 as proof of stability. A bad arrangement can still be a mechanism; triangles are required.

3.2 Method of sections

The method of sections finds a few member forces in a truss without solving every joint. You draw an imaginary cut through the truss, take one part as a free body, and apply three equilibrium equations to that part. It exists because joint-by-joint work is wasteful when a design check asks only for one or two members. In practice it is used to check the chord or diagonal near a heavy load, to size a member in a roof truss, or to verify software output for a bridge panel.

You need Chapter 3.1 for tension and compression signs, and Unit 2 for free body diagrams, supports and equilibrium of rigid bodies.

The cut and the free body

The method of joints in Chapter 3.1 starts at one joint and works across the whole truss. The method of sections starts with a cut. A section is an imaginary line drawn through members. It separates the truss into two parts. Each part is then a rigid body in equilibrium under its own external forces and the cut member forces.

Use a section when only a few member forces are wanted. A single section normally cuts at most three members with unknown forces. Three equations are available for a planar body: ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0 and ΣM = 0. If the cut meets four unknown members, the three equations cannot find all four. If one of those four is already known or is a zero-force member, the cut is still usable.

A cut member force is drawn as tension unless you already expect compression. Tension pulls away from the cut face on the chosen part. Compression pushes toward the cut face. After solving, a plus sign means tension and a minus sign means compression. Some syllabi call this the method of sections.

ΣF<sub>x</sub> = 0, ΣF<sub>y</sub> = 0, ΣM<sub>P</sub> = 0

ΣFx and ΣFy are sums of force components on the chosen part; ΣMP is the sum of moments about any point P in the plane; P need not lie on the part.

Choose the cut and the moment point

The cut should pass through the member you want. It should also pass through no more than two other unknown members. Pick the part with fewer loads and reactions; that part gives shorter equations.

  1. Find the support reactions from the whole truss if the chosen part contains a support.
  2. Draw the section through the wanted member. Take one part as the free body.
  3. Show every external load and support reaction that acts on that part. Leave out forces that act only on the other part.
  4. Draw each cut member force as tension, pulling away from the cut face.
  5. For a moment equation, choose P where the lines of action of two unknown cut members meet. Those two then have zero moment about P.
  6. If two unknown cut members are parallel, use ΣF in the direction perpendicular to them. Their components in that direction are zero.
  7. Solve. A positive result is tension; a negative result is compression.

A moment point can be outside the free body. Equilibrium of a rigid body allows moments about any point in the plane. This is the trick that makes the method fast.

Check the answer at a joint

A section result is easy to check. Put the force back at a joint on the cut and test ΣFx = 0 and ΣFy = 0 there. Use the support reaction at that joint if it has one. The check catches a wrong sign, a missed reaction, or a wrong angle.

Keep units consistent. Member forces are in kN if loads are in kN; lengths may stay in metres because they cancel in the angle ratios. Write the sign and the word tension or compression in the final answer. A bare negative number is not enough in an exam.

A section does not have to be vertical. Any line that separates the truss into two parts works. The line may pass through several members, but only three unknown member forces can be handled by the three equations. In the worked example, a vertical section cuts EF, CE and BC, and the right part gives CE directly.

Worked example

Question. For the truss A(0,0), B(2,0), C(4,0), D(0,3), E(2,3), F(4,3), members AB, BC, DE, EF, AD, BE, CF, AE and CE carry a 10 kN downward load at E. A is a pin and C is a roller. Use a vertical section cutting EF, CE and BC to find the force in CE. Check the result at joint C.

  1. Take moments about A for the whole truss. The 10 kN load at E is 2 m from A; the roller reaction Cy is 4 m from A. ΣMA = 0 gives Cy × 4 m − 10 kN × 2 m = 0, so Cy = 5 kN upward. ΣFy = 0 gives Ay = 5 kN upward.
  2. Cut vertically between x = 2 m and x = 4 m. The cut members are EF, CE and BC. Take the right part C-F. On this part, the only external force is Cy = 5 kN upward at C. The 10 kN load at E is on the left part, so it is not included.
  3. Let TCE be tension in CE. At C it pulls toward E, in direction (−2, 3)/√13. EF and BC are horizontal, so they have no vertical component.
  4. Sum vertical forces on the right part: 5 kN + TCE(3/√13) = 0. Hence TCE = −5√13/3 kN = −6.009 kN.
  5. The negative sign means compression. The force in CE is 6.01 kN compression.
  6. Check: take moments about E for the right part. ΣME = 0 gives TBC × 3 m = 5 kN × 2 m, so TBC = 3.33 kN tension.
  7. At joint C, BC pulls left with 3.33 kN. CE compression pushes down-right with components 3.33 kN right and 5 kN down. The roller reaction is 5 kN up. These balance in both directions.

Answer. CE = −6.01 kN; the member is in compression with magnitude 6.01 kN.

Exam traps

  • Cutting four unknown members and trying to solve with three equations. A section normally handles at most three unknown member forces.
  • Leaving out a support reaction on the chosen part. If the part contains C, the roller reaction at C belongs in the equations.
  • Using the whole truss after the cut. Each equation must use only forces on the chosen free body.
  • Reporting −6.01 kN without saying compression. In exams, give magnitude and the word tension or compression.
  • Taking moments about a point that lies on all unknown lines. If two unknowns pass through the point, the equation cannot find them.

3.3 Frames and machines

A truss member is loaded only at its two ends, so its internal force is axial. A frame has at least one member loaded at three or more points or along its length; that member carries bending, and its force direction cannot be guessed. Solve by taking the frame apart: draw each member as a free body, show shared pin forces equal and opposite, and use each body's three equilibrium equations. Machines are frames frozen in one position. Wall brackets, nutcrackers, cycle brakes and scissor lifts are analysed this way.

You need: equilibrium of rigid bodies in two and three dimensions, free-body diagrams and two-force and three-force bodies from Unit 2.

Truss or frame: the test

A truss member is pin-jointed at both ends and carries nothing between them. Exactly two forces act, so they are equal, opposite and along the line joining the pins. This makes the method of joints fast.

A frame is a pin-jointed structure with at least one member that fails that test: it has a load between its ends, connects to three or more pins, or runs past a pin. Then three or more forces act on it, not along its axis; it carries bending as well as direct force, and its internal force direction cannot be read off geometry.

Machines are frames whose parts move: pliers, a cycle brake, a door closer, an excavator arm. Freeze one position and analyse it as a frame. Ignore friction here; friction starts at 3.4.

Every member that is not two-force needs its own free-body diagram with all loads and pin forces.

Order of work

  1. Count unknowns: an unknown pin reaction gives two components; an unknown two-force member gives one magnitude.
  2. Mark two-force members: pinned at exactly two points with no other load; force along the pin line.
  3. Isolate remaining members with loads, pin reactions and two-force forces.
  4. Pair shared pin forces equal and opposite; never draw the same pin force twice in the same direction.
  5. Write ΣFx = 0, ΣFy = 0, ΣM = 0 per member; take moments about a pin to remove unknowns there.
  6. Check the whole frame as one free body.

F on B from link = T (C − B) / |C − B|

B and C are pin position vectors in m; |C − B| is link length in m; T is link force in N, positive as tension so the arrow on the joint points towards C.

Choose a pin for moments and a direction for resolution before writing equations.

Machines and the three-force shortcut

A member with exactly three forces must have their lines of action meeting at one point, or all parallel. From Unit 2, a three-force body is concurrent. This fixes an unknown reaction direction without algebra and checks a finished solution.

A machine has effort and output load at different points; their ratio is mechanical advantage. Statics gives every pin force — jaw of a nutcracker, brake pad pin. Sizing those pins is strength of materials, but forces come from here. Only equilibrium is used; motion is in Unit 6.

Worked example

Question. Horizontal member AB is 3 m long and pinned at A. A two-force link BC, 5 m long, joins B to a fixed pin C that lies 4 m vertically above A. A load of 12 kN acts straight down at B. Find the force in link BC and the reaction at A.

  1. Place A = (0, 0), B = (3, 0) and C = (0, 4), all in m. Check the link length: BC = √(3² + 4²) = 5 m, which matches.
  2. AB is not two-force: reaction at A, the 12 kN load at B and the link force at B act — three forces. A two-force member has forces at exactly two points and nothing else. Take AB as its own free body.
  3. Link BC is two-force. Unit vector from B to C is (−3, 4)/5 = (−0.6, +0.8). Force on B from the link is T(−0.6, 0.8), with T in kN, positive for tension.
  4. Take moments about A for member AB. Only the forces at B have a moment, and the lever arm is 3 m: ΣM_A = 3(0.8T − 12) = 0, so 0.8T = 12 and T = 15 kN.
  5. Positive T means tension: the link pulls B towards C with 15 kN.
  6. ΣFx on AB: A_x − 0.6(15) = 0, so A_x = +9 kN, that is 9 kN to the right.
  7. ΣFy on AB: A_y + 0.8(15) − 12 = 0, so A_y = 0.
  8. Reaction at A = 9 kN horizontal to the right, along AB. The three-force rule forces this: the load and link force meet at B, so the reaction at A also passes through B.

Answer. The link carries 15 kN in tension. The reaction at A is 9 kN horizontal to the right; its vertical component is zero.

Worked example 2

Question. Same layout: AB = 3 m, C 4 m above A. Put the 12 kN load at the midpoint of AB. Find the link force and the reaction at A.

  1. BC = 5 m; unit vector B to C = (−0.6, 0.8).
  2. ΣM_A on AB: link moment 3(0.8T), load moment 1.5 × 12. So 2.4T − 18 = 0, giving T = 7.5 kN tension.
  3. ΣFx: A_x − 0.6(7.5) = 0, so A_x = 4.5 kN to the right.
  4. ΣFy: A_y + 0.8(7.5) − 12 = 0, so A_y = 6 kN up.
  5. Reaction magnitude = √(4.5² + 6²) = 7.5 kN, angle tan⁻¹(6/4.5) = 53.1° above horizontal.

Exam traps

  • Calling a member two-force because it looks straight. A load between its ends or a third pin destroys that.
  • Drawing a shared pin force in the same direction on both members. It is equal and opposite on the two free bodies.
  • Assuming the reaction at A is vertical. That forces A_x = 0, contradicting ΣFx = 0, so the error shows. In this geometry the true reaction lies along AB, so this example cannot catch an 'along the member' guess.
  • Putting the link force along the beam. A two-force force follows the link line, not the beam.
  • Reversing the arrow after a negative T. Keep the tension-positive arrow fixed and read the minus sign at the end.

3.4 Laws of friction; coefficient and angle of friction

Friction is the force at a contact that opposes sliding, or the tendency to slide. It exists because real surfaces are rough at small scale and atoms attract across the contact. Dry friction follows simple laws: the friction force opposes impending motion, and its maximum is μN, where μ is the coefficient of friction and N is the normal reaction. Engineers use these laws to size brakes, clutches, ramps and bolt threads, and to check that a ladder, block or machine will not slip.

You need: equilibrium of a particle and free-body diagrams from Unit 1 and Unit 2; the free-body habit is used again in frames and machines.

Three states of dry friction

Dry friction acts at the contact between two surfaces that touch without oil or grease. Push a heavy box on a floor with a small horizontal force P. The box does not move: static friction F balances P. Increase P and F grows, but only up to a limit. At the limit the box is about to slide; this is limiting friction. Once it slides, kinetic friction acts, and it is smaller than the limiting value.

Static friction is a response, not a fixed value. It takes only the value needed for equilibrium, up to its limit.

  • Friction acts along the common tangent, opposite the impending or actual sliding.
  • Limiting static friction is proportional to the normal reaction: Fmax = μsN. μs is the static coefficient; N is the normal reaction in newtons.
  • Kinetic friction is Fk = μkN. μk is the kinetic coefficient. Usually μk < μs, so kinetic friction is smaller than limiting static friction.
  • For ordinary hard surfaces, friction is nearly independent of apparent contact area and of low sliding speed.

Some syllabi call these Coulomb's laws of friction.

Coefficient and angle of friction

Divide the limiting friction by the normal reaction to get the coefficient of friction. For static limiting friction μs = Fmax/N; for sliding μk = Fk/N. Both coefficients have no unit.

μ<sub>s</sub> = F<sub>max</sub> / N,  φ = tan⁻¹ μ<sub>s</sub>

μs is the static coefficient (no unit); Fmax is limiting friction in N; N is normal reaction in N; φ is the angle of friction in degrees.

The angle of friction is a geometric way to say the same thing. The resultant of N and Fmax makes angle φ with N, so tan φ = Fmax/N = μs. A rough surface with μs=0.4 has φ=21.8°.

For a block on an incline, the steepest angle at which it stays put is the angle of repose α = tan⁻¹ μs. The angle of repose is measured by tilting the surface until motion is just about to start. On an incline N = W cos θ, not W; the full treatment is in block on a rough incline.

Solving a friction problem

Use the laws in a fixed order so you do not mix static and kinetic friction.

  1. Draw the free body diagram. Show N perpendicular to the surface and friction along the surface, opposing the impending motion.
  2. Find N from equilibrium in the direction perpendicular to the surface. Horizontal floor: N = W. Incline: N = W cos θ.
  3. Compute the limiting static friction Fmax = μsN.
  4. Compare the applied tangential force P with Fmax. If P ≤ Fmax, the body is static and F = P. If P > Fmax, it slides and F = μkN.
  5. Use μs for the angle of friction while the body is at the limit; use μk only when it is already sliding.

The most common mistake is to write F = μN while the body is still static below the limit. Static friction can be any value from zero up to μsN. It equals the applied tangential force until that force reaches the limit. Check the direction last: friction always opposes relative motion or its tendency, not the applied force by name.

Worked example

Question. A 200 N block rests on a horizontal floor. The coefficients are μs = 0.4 and μk = 0.3. Find (a) the limiting friction, (b) the friction when the applied horizontal force is 60 N, and (c) the angle of friction.

  1. The floor is horizontal, so the normal reaction balances the weight: N = W = 200 N.
  2. Limiting friction: Fmax = μsN = 0.4 × 200 = 80 N.
  3. The applied force is 60 N, which is less than 80 N. The block is static, so friction balances the applied force: F = 60 N.
  4. The kinetic coefficient is not used because the block is not sliding. The number μkN = 60 N is a coincidence of these data.
  5. Angle of friction: φ = tan⁻¹ μs = tan⁻¹ 0.4 = 21.8°.

Answer. Limiting friction = 80 N; friction at 60 N applied = 60 N; angle of friction φ = 21.8°.

Exam traps

  • Using μk while the block is still static. Check P against μsN first.
  • Using N = W on an incline. Resolve perpendicular to the surface; there N = W cos θ.
  • Writing F = μN for a static body below the limit. Static friction equals the applied tangential force, not μsN.
  • Dropping units or treating μ as a force. μ is a pure ratio; friction is in newtons.
  • Pointing friction along the applied force instead of opposite impending motion.

3.5 Block resting on a rough inclined plane

On a rough incline, gravity has two jobs: W sin θ pulls the block down the slope, and W cos θ presses it into the slope. The slope pushes back with normal reaction N = W cos θ, so the largest friction available is μN = μW cos θ. A force P parallel to the plane must beat W sin θ but not exceed the friction limit. This decides whether a load on a ramp, a truck on a ghat road, or a block in a lab will stay put.

You need: free-body diagrams and particle equilibrium from Equilibrium of a particle, and the friction limit from Laws of friction.

Resolve the weight and find the normal reaction

Draw the free body diagram with axes along and perpendicular to the plane. The weight W acts vertically down. Split it into two perpendicular parts: W sin θ down the slope and W cos θ into the slope. Here W is the weight in N, θ is the angle the plane makes with the horizontal, and both parts are in N. The normal reaction N acts perpendicular to the plane, away from it. No motion occurs perpendicular to the plane, so forces balance: N = W cos θ. For W = 100 N and θ = 30°, N = 100 × cos 30° = 86.6 N.

Friction acts along the plane, not along the weight. Its maximum value is μN, so on an incline the limit is μW cos θ, not μW. Let μ be the coefficient of static friction; it has no units. For μ = 0.3, μN = 0.3 × 86.6 = 26.0 N. The normal reaction is smaller than the weight because only the perpendicular component W cos θ of the weight presses on the plane; the rest, W sin θ, acts along the plane. A steep plane has small cos θ, so it offers little normal reaction and little friction.

N = W cos θ, f_max = μN = μW cos θ

N is the normal reaction in N; W is the weight in N; θ is the plane angle to the horizontal in degrees; μ is the coefficient of static friction, no units; f_max is the limiting friction in N.

Friction direction and the safe range of P

Take P as a force applied parallel to the plane and directed up the slope. For impending motion down the plane, friction points up the plane. Then P + f = W sin θ, so P = W sin θ − f. At the limit f = μW cos θ, this is the smallest force that can hold the block: P_min = W sin θ − μW cos θ.

For impending motion up the plane, friction points down the plane. Then P = W sin θ + f, and the largest force before sliding starts is P_max = W sin θ + μW cos θ. Any P between P_min and P_max keeps the block in equilibrium. If θ ≤ φ, then P_min ≤ 0 and the block stands on its own; the safe range is then 0 ≤ P ≤ P_max, and P_min only gives the down-the-plane force needed to start sliding down. Use P_min = W sin θ − μW cos θ only when θ > φ. With W = 100 N, θ = 30°, μ = 0.3: W sin θ = 50 N, μW cos θ = 26.0 N, so 24.0 N ≤ P ≤ 76.0 N. At P_min friction is up; at P_max friction is down.

P_min = W sin θ − μW cos θ,   P_max = W sin θ + μW cos θ

P_min is the smallest force in N for equilibrium; P_max is the largest force in N for equilibrium; W is the weight in N; θ is the plane angle in degrees; μ is the coefficient of static friction, no units.

The angle of repose is the steepest angle at which the block stays without any P. Set P_min = 0: W sin θ = μW cos θ, so tan θ = μ. Hence the angle of repose is φ = tan⁻¹ μ, where φ is measured from the horizontal. If θ < φ, the block stays with P = 0. If θ = φ, it is just about to slide. If θ > φ, the block slides down unless a force P is applied. Equivalently, the block stays by itself only when θ ≤ φ, that is μ ≥ tan θ. Some syllabi call the whole topic equilibrium on a rough inclined plane.

Procedure for any rough-incline problem

  1. Draw the free body diagram. Show W vertically down, N perpendicular to the plane, friction f along the plane, and P parallel to the plane.
  2. Resolve W. Compute W sin θ along the plane and N = W cos θ perpendicular to the plane.
  3. Compute the friction limit f_max = μN. Never use N = W on an incline.
  4. Decide impending motion. Down the plane means friction acts up the plane. Up the plane means friction acts down the plane.
  5. Write equilibrium along the plane with the correct signs. If θ > φ, quote P_min ≤ P ≤ P_max; if θ ≤ φ, use 0 ≤ P ≤ P_max.
  6. If no P is applied, compare θ with the angle of repose φ = tan⁻¹ μ.

Worked example

Question. A block of weight 100 N rests on a plane inclined at 30° to the horizontal. The coefficient of static friction is μs = 0.3. A force P is applied parallel to the plane. Find the range of P that keeps the block in equilibrium.

  1. Resolve the weight: W sin θ = 100 × sin 30° = 50.0 N down the plane; W cos θ = 100 × cos 30° = 86.6 N into the plane.
  2. Normal reaction: N = W cos θ = 86.6 N.
  3. Limiting friction: f_max = μs N = 0.3 × 86.6 = 25.98 N, about 26.0 N.
  4. Impending motion down the plane: friction acts up. P_min + f_max = W sin θ, so P_min = 50.0 − 26.0 = 24.0 N.
  5. Impending motion up the plane: friction acts down. P_max = W sin θ + f_max = 50.0 + 26.0 = 76.0 N.
  6. The block stays in equilibrium for 24.0 N ≤ P ≤ 76.0 N. Below 24.0 N it slides down; above 76.0 N it slides up.

Answer. The required range is 24.0 N ≤ P ≤ 76.0 N.

Exam traps

  • Using N = W instead of N = W cos θ. On an incline the normal reaction is reduced by cos θ, so friction is also reduced.
  • Putting friction in the wrong direction. Friction always opposes impending motion: up the plane when the block is about to slide down, down the plane when it is about to slide up.
  • Writing P_min = W sin θ − μW instead of W sin θ − μW cos θ. The friction limit uses the normal reaction, not the full weight.
  • Forgetting that μ has no units. Friction force, normal reaction and P are all in N.
  • Treating P_min and P_max as exact motion values. They are the limits of equilibrium; any value between them is also safe.

3.6 Wedge friction

A wedge is a small triangular block driven under a load. It changes a modest horizontal push into a large normal force because its sloping face is a shallow inclined plane. Both the sloping face and the flat base are rough, so friction appears twice. Wedges lift machine beds, split wood, lock door stops and hold railway fishplates. The method is: separate the wedge and the load, draw both free bodies, and use the friction angle to find the push P needed to raise or lower the load, including self-locking.

You need: 3.4 friction laws and angle of friction and 3.5 inclined-plane equilibrium.

Two rough faces make the wedge

A wedge has a small angle α between its sloping face and its horizontal base. A load W rests on the sloping face. A horizontal push P drives the wedge forward, and the load rises along the slope. The load moves a short vertical distance while the wedge moves a longer horizontal distance. That is the force multiplication.

Both faces are rough. On the sloping face, the wedge pushes the load with normal N₁ and friction μN₁. On the floor, the floor pushes the wedge with normal N₂ and friction μN₂. Ignoring either friction gives a wrong P. A wedge is not a one-face inclined plane.

  • A wedge under a machine leg levels the machine.
  • A pair of wedges adjusts a machine-tool slide.
  • A door wedge holds a door open.
  • A splitting wedge turns a hammer blow into large pressure inside wood.

Free-body diagrams and friction angle

Draw the block and the wedge as separate free bodies. The block is held by a smooth vertical guide, so it moves only vertically. The guide gives a horizontal reaction H but no friction.

On the block: W acts down. The wedge gives normal N₁ perpendicular to the sloping face and friction f₁ = μN₁ down the slope, opposing the block's upward slip. Vertical equilibrium gives N₁ cos α − f₁ sin α = W.

On the wedge: the block's normal and friction reverse. The floor gives normal N₂ up and friction f₂ = μN₂ to the left, opposite the wedge's motion. At impending slip, use f₁ = μN₁ and f₂ = μN₂.

P = N₁ sin α + f₁ cos α + f₂

P is the horizontal push on the wedge; N₁ is the normal on the sloping face; f₁ = μN₁ is the friction on that face; f₂ = μN₂ is the floor friction; α is the wedge angle. The block presses back on the slope, so the normal component N₁ sin α and both friction components all resist the push.

Vertical equilibrium of the wedge gives N₂ = N₁ cos α − f₁ sin α. The block equation makes N₂ equal W in this arrangement. The friction angle φ = tan⁻¹ μ combines N and f on a face into one resultant R. R makes angle φ with the normal. On the wedge, P, R₁ from the block and R₂ from the floor form a force triangle.

Self-locking and direction checks

Self-locking means the wedge stays in place after P is removed. Friction on both faces then holds the load. A small wedge angle and large friction help. To test it, set P = 0 and reverse both friction senses at the same time: the load now squeezes the wedge back out, so the block tends to slide down the slope, friction on the block acts up the slope, and both friction forces on the wedge reverse. Recompute N₁ and N₂, then find the floor friction needed. If that is within μN₂, the wedge is self-locking; if not, the wedge squeezes out.

Directions change with motion. For raising, friction on the block is down the slope. For lowering, friction on the block is up the slope and friction on the wedge reverses. Recompute both normals; do not reuse the raising signs.

A quick check on the numbers: N₁ is not W. While raising, N₁ = W / (cos α − μ sin α), so it is larger than W. The floor normal N₂ comes from wedge vertical equilibrium, not from a guess.

Worked example

Question. A 15° wedge is driven under a 500 N block. The block is held by a smooth vertical guide. The coefficient of friction is 0.25 on the sloping face and 0.25 on the floor. Find the horizontal force P needed to raise the block.

  1. Given: wedge angle α = 15°, block weight W = 500 N, μ = 0.25 on both faces. The weight of the wedge is neglected and the guide is smooth, so the floor normal N₂ carries only the load transmitted from the block. The friction angle is φ = tan⁻¹(0.25) = 14.04°, but the equations do not need it.
  2. For impending upward motion, friction on the block acts down the slope. Block vertical equilibrium: N₁ cos 15° − μN₁ sin 15° = W.
  3. Substitute μ = 0.25 and W = 500 N: N₁(0.9659 − 0.25 × 0.2588) = 500, so N₁ × 0.9012 = 500.
  4. N₁ = 500 / 0.9012 = 554.80 N. Then f₁ = μN₁ = 0.25 × 554.80 = 138.70 N.
  5. Wedge vertical equilibrium: N₂ + f₁ sin 15° = N₁ cos 15°. So N₂ = 554.80 × 0.9659 − 138.70 × 0.2588 = 500.0 N.
  6. Floor friction: f₂ = μN₂ = 0.25 × 500.0 = 125.0 N.
  7. Wedge horizontal equilibrium: P = N₁ sin 15° + f₁ cos 15° + f₂ = 554.80 × 0.2588 + 138.70 × 0.9659 + 125.0. The block's normal pushes the wedge back out, so its horizontal component adds to the push.
  8. P = 143.59 + 133.97 + 125.0 = 402.56 N.

Answer. The horizontal push needed to raise the block is P = 402.56 N ≈ 402.6 N in the direction of the push.

Exam traps

  • Showing friction on only the sloping face. The floor under the wedge is also rough; f₂ = μN₂ adds to the push P.
  • Taking N₁ equal to W. The sloping normal is N₁ = W / (cos α − μ sin α) while raising, so it is larger than W.
  • Using the raising friction directions for lowering. Reverse friction on both faces when the block is being lowered.
  • Mixing the wedge angle with the friction angle. α sets the slope; φ = tan⁻¹ μ sets the tilt of the resultant reaction.
  • Forgetting the smooth guide reaction H. It does not enter P here, but it keeps the block from moving sideways.

3.7 Ladder friction

A ladder is a rigid body standing in equilibrium on friction at two contacts. The floor pushes up and grips sideways; the wall pushes back. Take away the floor friction and the ladder slides out — that is the whole engineering idea. Ladder friction appears wherever a body leans on two surfaces and depends on grip: a painter's ladder, a leaning beam, a shoring strut, a bamboo pole propped against a wall at a site. This chapter draws the free body diagram, writes the three equilibrium equations, and checks whether the friction available is enough to stop the slip.

You need: the friction limit μN and the coefficient of friction from Chapter 3.4, and equilibrium of a rigid body with moments from Unit 2, Statics of Rigid Bodies.

The ladder as one free body

Take the ladder as one rigid body AB. Its length is L metres and its weight W newtons acts at the midpoint, L/2 along the ladder. A person of weight P newtons stands at point C, a distance a metres measured along the ladder from the floor. The ladder makes an angle θ with the horizontal floor.

At the foot A the rough floor gives two reactions: the normal N_A, vertical and upward, and friction F, horizontal and pointing toward the wall. Friction points that way because the foot wants to slide away from the wall.

At the top B the wall is normally taken as smooth. A smooth contact pushes only along its normal, so B carries a single force N_B, horizontal and pointing away from the wall. There is no vertical force at B.

That leaves three unknowns — N_A, F and N_B — and the three equilibrium equations of a rigid body. A rough wall would add a fourth unknown and needs one more assumption, as the quiz shows.

Three equations, moment centre at the foot

Take moments about A. Both N_A and F act through A, so they drop out and N_B is the only unknown left. This is why A is the right moment centre.

ΣM_A = 0:  N_B · L sin θ = W · (L/2) cos θ + P · a cos θ

N_B is the wall normal in N; W is the ladder weight in N; P is the person's weight in N; L is the ladder length in m; a is the person's distance along the ladder in m; θ is the angle between the ladder and the floor in degrees.

N_B = cot θ · (W/2 + P a / L)

cot θ = cos θ / sin θ, the reciprocal of tan θ; the bracket is a force in N.

  1. ΣFy = 0 gives N_A = W + P. The floor carries both weights.
  2. ΣFx = 0 gives F = N_B. The floor friction equals the wall push.
  3. ΣM_A = 0 gives N_B = cot θ (W/2 + P a/L).
  4. Required friction is F = N_B. Available friction is μN_A. If F ≤ μN_A the ladder stands; if F > μN_A it slips.

Each moment arm is measured perpendicular to its force: L sin θ for the horizontal wall push, (L/2) cos θ for the ladder weight, a cos θ for the person. Measuring a along the floor instead of along the ladder is the commonest error.

Minimum angle and minimum μ

Put required friction equal to available friction. That is the state of impending slip, and it gives the boundary of the safe zone.

tan θ ≥ (W/2 + P a/L) ÷ [ μ (W + P) ]

θ is the smallest angle of the ladder with the floor for which it will not slip; μ is the coefficient of friction at the floor.

μ ≥ cot θ · (W/2 + P a/L) ÷ (W + P)

μ is the smallest coefficient of floor friction that holds the ladder at a given θ.

  • Take W = 200 N, L = 5 m, P = 600 N, a = 3 m, μ = 0.3: W/2 + P a/L = 100 + 600 × 3/5 = 460 N, and μ(W + P) = 0.3 × 800 = 240 N.
  • So tan θ ≥ 460/240 = 1.917, giving θ ≥ 62.4°. At 60° the ladder is too shallow.
  • At 60°, cot 60° = 0.5774, so the required friction is 460 × 0.5774 = 265.6 N against 240 N available.
  • The minimum μ at 60° is 460 × 0.5774/800 = 0.332.
  • Making the ladder steeper — a larger θ, foot nearer the wall — lowers cot θ and always helps. A person climbing higher raises a, so the risk grows for a fixed μ.

Worked example

Question. A ladder of length 5 m and weight 200 N leans against a smooth vertical wall at 60° to the horizontal floor. A person of weight 600 N stands on it at a point 3 m along the ladder from the floor. The coefficient of friction between the ladder foot and the floor is 0.3. Find the friction the floor must supply, the maximum friction available, and state whether the ladder slips. Also find the smallest angle at which the ladder would stand.

  1. Vertical equilibrium: N_A = W + P = 200 + 600 = 800 N.
  2. Moments about the foot A, one direction positive: N_B × 5 sin 60° = 200 × 2.5 cos 60° + 600 × 3 cos 60°.
  3. Numerically sin 60° = 0.8660 and cos 60° = 0.5, so N_B × 4.330 = 200 × 1.25 + 600 × 1.5 = 250 + 900 = 1150 N·m.
  4. N_B = 1150 / 4.330 = 265.6 N.
  5. Horizontal equilibrium: the floor friction must equal the wall push, F = N_B = 265.6 N.
  6. Available friction: μN_A = 0.3 × 800 = 240.0 N.
  7. Compare: 265.6 N needed against 240.0 N available. The ladder slips, short by 25.6 N.
  8. Minimum angle: W/2 + P a/L = 100 + 360 = 460 N, so tan θ ≥ 460/240 = 1.917 and θ ≥ 62.4°.

Answer. The floor must supply 265.6 N but can supply only 240.0 N, so the ladder slips at 60°; it needs at least 62.4° from the floor to stand.

Exam traps

  • Measuring the person's position along the floor instead of along the ladder. The moment arm is a cos θ, not a.
  • Using L instead of (L/2) cos θ as the moment arm of the ladder weight. The weight acts at the midpoint, perpendicular distance (L/2) cos θ from the foot.
  • Forgetting that the floor normal is W + P. It carries the ladder and the person, and μ multiplies that total.
  • Taking moments about the top B when the wall is smooth. That leaves N_A and F both unknown in one equation; take moments about A instead, where two unknowns vanish.

3.8 Belt friction: flat and V-belts

A belt wrapped round a pulley can carry a large tight-side tension and a much smaller slack-side tension because friction acts along the whole curved contact. The ratio of these tensions grows exponentially with the coefficient of friction and the angle of contact. A V-belt sits in a groove, so the same friction produces a larger ratio. This is why V-belts drive machines, why a rope round a bollard holds a boat, and why a capstan lets one person hold a ship. The next sections derive both ratios and apply them.

You need: the laws of dry friction and the coefficient μ from Chapter 3.4.

Deriving the flat-belt ratio

Take a small piece of belt in contact with the pulley over a small angle dθ. Let T be the tension at one end of the piece and T + dT at the other. The tension rises toward the tight side, so dT is positive in the direction in which the belt tends to slip. The pulley pushes outward on the piece with normal force dN. Friction μ dN acts along the pulley surface and opposes that slip.

Resolve forces on the piece. In the radial direction, the two tension forces have inward components that balance dN: dN = T dθ. The term dT dθ/2 is second order and is dropped. In the tangential direction, friction balances the tension change: μ dN = dT.

Combine: μ T dθ = dT, so dT/T = μ dθ. Integrate from the slack side to the tight side. The left side gives ln(T_tight/T_slack); the right side gives μθ.

T<sub>tight</sub> / T<sub>slack</sub> = e<sup>μθ</sup>

T_tight is the tight-side tension in N; T_slack is the slack-side tension in N; μ is the coefficient of friction, a pure number; θ is the total angle of contact in radians; e = 2.718... is the base of natural logarithms.

This is the limiting (maximum) ratio, reached only when the belt is on the point of slipping. A belt carrying less than its limiting torque has Ttight/Tslack smaller than eμθ. The worked example below assumes the belt is just about to slip.

The ratio is exponential because every small arc adds friction proportional to the local tension. A larger tension creates a larger friction force, which supports a still larger tension difference. θ is the total wrap angle, not the angle of one small element. A 120° contact is 2.094 rad, not 120.

V-belt: the groove angle

A V-belt has a trapezium section. It runs in a pulley groove with total angle 2β, so β is the half groove angle. β is measured between one sloping face of the groove and the centre plane. The belt touches two faces, not one.

Let R be the total radial reaction needed to balance the tension forces, as dN was for the flat belt. Each face carries normal force N. The radial components of the two normals add: 2N sin β = R, so N = R/(2 sin β). Friction on both faces is 2 μ N = μ R / sin β. The friction is therefore the same as a flat belt with an effective coefficient μ/sin β.

T<sub>tight</sub> / T<sub>slack</sub> = e<sup>μθ / sin β</sup>

β is the half groove angle in radians; 2β is the full groove angle; T_tight, T_slack, μ and θ are as defined for the flat belt.

For β = 20°, sin β = 0.342, so the effective coefficient is about 2.92 times μ. With μ = 0.3, the effective value is 0.877. The same μ and θ give a much larger ratio than a flat belt. This is the reason V-belts are used where a small wrap angle or a compact drive must transmit torque without slip.

Using the ratio

Use the ratio in four steps.

  1. Mark the tight side and the slack side. The ratio Ttight/Tslack is greater than 1; the limiting value eμθ (for a flat belt) applies only at impending slip, so a belt carrying less than its limiting torque has a smaller ratio.
  2. Convert the contact angle to radians: θ_rad = θ_deg × π / 180.
  3. Flat belt: substitute μ and θ. V-belt: substitute the half angle β in sin β; never use the full groove angle 2β.
  4. Solve for the unknown tension. Check that the tight tension comes out larger than the slack tension.

For the example below, the belt is at impending slip, μ = 0.3 and θ = 120° = 2.094 rad. The flat-belt ratio is e0.6283 = 1.874. The V-belt ratio with β = 20° is e0.6283/0.3420 = e1.837 = 6.279. A V-belt therefore holds a much smaller slack tension for the same tight tension.

Some syllabi call this rope friction or capstan friction. The same formula applies to a rope round a post, a bollard or a capstan. The rope does not need a groove; the capstan effect comes from the curved contact alone. Torque and power transmission belong to design courses.

Worked example

Question. A flat belt on a pulley has μ = 0.3, angle of contact 120°, and tight tension 500 N. Assume the belt is on the point of slipping. Find the slack tension. Repeat for a V-belt in a 40° groove, taking half angle β = 20°.

  1. Convert the contact angle: θ = 120° × π / 180 = 2.094 rad.
  2. Flat belt exponent: μθ = 0.3 × 2.094 = 0.6283. Then e0.6283 = 1.874.
  3. Flat-belt slack tension: T_slack = 500 / 1.874 = 266.8 N.
  4. V-belt half angle β = 20°, so sin β = sin 20° = 0.3420. The exponent is μθ / sin β = 0.6283 / 0.3420 = 1.837.
  5. Then e1.837 = 6.279. V-belt slack tension: T_slack = 500 / 6.279 = 79.6 N.
  6. Check: both slack tensions are less than the tight tension, as they must be. The V-belt gives the smaller slack tension for the same tight tension.

Answer. The flat-belt slack tension is 266.8 N (about 267 N), and the V-belt slack tension is 79.6 N.

Exam traps

  • Putting θ in degrees. A 120° contact must enter the formula as 2.094 rad.
  • Using the full groove angle 2β instead of the half angle β. For a 40° groove, β = 20°.
  • Inverting the ratio. T_tight/T_slack is greater than 1; a result below 1 means the sides were swapped.
  • Check the calculator's angle mode. sin 20° = 0.342 and sin 40° = 0.643; entering the full groove angle or leaving the calculator in radian mode both give a wrong exponent.
  • Forgetting that θ is the actual wrap angle. It is not always 180° or 360°; a small pulley or a crossed belt changes it.

3.9 Screw jack

A screw jack lifts a heavy load with a small effort by turning an inclined plane that has been wrapped around a cylinder. That is all a screw thread is. Friction on the thread, measured by the friction angle φ, decides how much torque you must apply, how much mechanical advantage you get, how efficient the jack is, and whether the load stays up when you let go. Screw jacks raise cars in garages and level heavy machinery on shop floors, and the same thread analysis sizes the lead screw of a lathe.

You need: Chapter 3.4 for the friction angle φ = tan⁻¹ μ, and moments about a point from Unit 2, Statics of Rigid Bodies.

Lead, mean radius and lead angle

A screw thread is an inclined plane wrapped round a cylinder. One full turn of the screw moves the nut a distance L along the axis; L is the lead. The pitch is the gap between neighbouring thread crests. On a single-start thread the lead equals the pitch; on a double-start thread the lead is twice the pitch. The formulas need the lead, so read the question for the number of starts.

Unroll one turn of the thread at the mean radius r and you get a right triangle. The base is 2πr, the length of one turn. The height is the lead L. The angle of this ramp is the lead angle α.

tan α = L / (2π r)

L is the lead (mm), r is the mean radius (mm) where the thread carries load, and α is the lead angle (use the same angle mode on your calculator throughout).

The mean radius is roughly halfway between the outside radius and the core radius of the screw. Use radius everywhere. Putting the diameter d in place of r doubles the torque, and halves tan α, so the lead angle comes out roughly half its true value.

  1. Find the lead L = pitch × number of starts (in mm), then convert it to metres.
  2. Write the mean radius in metres: r.
  3. Compute tan α = L/(2πr), then α.
  4. Compute the friction angle φ = tan⁻¹ μ from Chapter 3.4.
  5. Get the torque from T = W r tan(α + φ), then the effort from P = T/R.

Torque, effort and efficiency

The thread is a rough incline, so this is the block-on-incline idea of Chapter 3.5 bent into a circle, close to the wedge of Chapter 3.6. While the load rises, friction on the thread opposes the motion, so the reaction from the thread is tilted by φ from the normal, against the turning direction. Resolving along and across the incline gives a driving force W tan(α + φ) at the mean radius.

T = W r tan(α + φ)

T is the torque on the screw (N·m), W is the load (N), r is the mean radius (m), α is the lead angle and φ = tan⁻¹ μ is the friction angle.

P = T / R

P is the effort at the handle (N) and R is the handle radius (m). The handle is a lever, so a long handle turns a large torque with a small push.

Mechanical advantage is load divided by effort: W/P = R/(r tan(α + φ)). It is a pure number with no units. Raising the load by one lead takes one full turn, so the useful work is W L and the work you supply is P × 2πR.

η = W L / (P × 2πR) = tan α / tan(α + φ)

η is the efficiency, a fraction between 0 and 1; multiply by 100 for a percentage.

The two forms agree because L = 2πr tan α and P × 2πR = 2πrW tan(α + φ). Real jacks run at about 15% to 40% efficiency, which is why a car jack needs many turns for a short lift. The lost work heats the thread.

Self-locking

Release the handle with the load still up. The load now tries to screw the jack down, so friction reverses and helps you. The load can turn the screw by itself only if α is bigger than φ. The condition for a self-locking jack is therefore φ ≥ α, that is μ ≥ tan α.

Self-locking has a price. At the boundary φ = α the efficiency is tan α / tan 2α = (1 − tan²α)/2, a little under 0.5. So no self-locking jack can be better than about 50% efficient, and most are far worse. That is a fair trade: a jack that unwinds under a car is a disaster.

Two refinements are left out here. A V-thread groove raises the effective friction to about μ/cos β, where β is the half angle of the thread. A collar under the load adds its own friction torque. Both belong to a machine-design course.

Worked example

Question. A screw jack has mean radius r = 25 mm and lead L = 5 mm (single start). The coefficient of friction between screw and nut is μ = 0.15. A load W = 10 kN is to be raised. Find (a) the torque needed on the screw, (b) the effort at the end of a handle of radius R = 300 mm, (c) the efficiency, and (d) state whether the jack is self-locking.

  1. Convert to metres and newtons: r = 0.025 m, L = 0.005 m, R = 0.3 m, W = 10 000 N.
  2. tan α = L/(2πr) = 0.005/(2π × 0.025) = 0.005/0.15708 = 0.03183, so α = 1.82°.
  3. Friction angle φ = tan⁻¹ 0.15 = 8.53°.
  4. α + φ = 1.82° + 8.53° = 10.35°, and tan 10.35° = 0.1827.
  5. Torque T = W r tan(α + φ) = 10 000 × 0.025 × 0.1827 = 45.7 N·m.
  6. Effort P = T/R = 45.68/0.3 = 152 N.
  7. Efficiency η = tan α / tan(α + φ) = 0.03183/0.1827 = 0.174, that is 17.4%.
  8. Compare angles: φ = 8.53° is greater than α = 1.82°, so the jack is self-locking.

Answer. T = 45.7 N·m, P ≈ 152 N, η ≈ 17.4%, and since φ = 8.53° > α = 1.82° the jack is self-locking.

Exam traps

  • Pitch for lead: a double-start thread with 3 mm pitch has L = 6 mm. Using 3 mm halves tan α and roughly halves the lead angle, and it lowers the torque by only a few per cent because φ dominates α + φ.
  • Diameter for radius in both L/(2πr) and W r tan(α + φ).
  • Stopping at the torque when the question asks for the effort; divide the torque by the handle radius R.
  • Calling a jack self-locking because μ looks large; you must compare φ with α, not μ with 1.
  • Calculator in the wrong angle mode, or writing η = tan(α + φ)/tan α and reporting an efficiency above 100%.

Takeaways

  1. A simple truss is solved because each joint gives only two equations, so you start where just two member forces are unknown and work outward.
  2. The method of sections is faster when you need only a few member forces, because one cut and three equations give them without solving the whole truss.
  3. Frames and machines must be dismembered, since a member with three or more forces is not a two-force member and its force direction is unknown.
  4. Dry friction is bounded: the reaction can tilt up to the angle of friction φ = tan⁻¹ μ and no further, and that single idea solves most friction problems.
  5. On a rough incline the friction limit shrinks with cos θ, so the safe range of the applied force narrows as the plane gets steeper.
  6. A wedge, a screw jack and a belt all convert friction from a nuisance into a mechanism for multiplying force or gripping a load.
  7. A V-belt grips far better than a flat belt of the same μ because the groove angle divides the effective coefficient by sin β.
  8. A screw jack is efficient only when the helix angle is well above the friction angle, and those are exactly the conditions that stop it self-locking.

Torque to raise T = W(d/2) tan(α + φ); efficiency η = tan α / tan(α + φ); Larger α raises efficiency but reduces the margin of self-locking; Self-locking needs φ ≥ α; at φ = α efficiency is tan α / tan 2α, that is, below 50 percent; Friction angle depends on the thread and lubrication; square thread has lower φ than V-thread; Recommendation: choose α well below φ for safety, accept low efficiency, use a collar-friction term for accuracy.

Glossary

Angle of contact
The angle, in radians, over which the belt touches the pulley; it sets the tension ratio together with μ.
Angle of repose
The inclination of a rough plane at which a block just begins to slide, numerically equal to the angle of friction.
Belt drive
A pair of pulleys joined by a belt, transmitting power through the difference of the two belt tensions.
Coefficient of friction
The dimensionless ratio of limiting friction to normal reaction between two given surfaces.
Compression
An axial member force that pushes the joint, so the member is shortened.
Cone of friction
The cone of semi-vertical angle φ swept by the resultant reaction as it tilts around the normal.
Efficiency
Useful work output divided by work input; for a screw jack, tan α / tan(α + φ).
Free-body diagram
A sketch of one body alone with every external force drawn on it, the starting point of every statics solution.
Impending motion
The state just before sliding starts, where friction has reached its limit μN.
Joint
A pin connection where two or more members of a truss meet and where external loads are applied.
Kinetic friction
Friction acting while surfaces slide; usually slightly smaller than the limiting static value.
Lead
The axial distance a screw advances in one complete turn, equal to pitch for a single-start thread.
Pin joint
A connection that can transmit force in any direction but cannot transmit a moment.
Pitch
The axial distance between consecutive threads of a screw.
Reaction
The force or moment a support exerts on a body to keep it in place, found from the equilibrium equations.
Roller support
A support that gives only a reaction normal to the surface it rests on.
Screw thread
A helical ridge on a cylinder that converts rotation into axial motion, as in a screw jack.
Self-locking
The property of a screw or wedge by which friction alone holds the load and prevents it from running back.
Static friction
Friction that acts while the body is still at rest, adjustable up to the limiting value μN.
Tension
An axial member force that pulls the joint, so the member is stretched.
Truss
A framework of straight members pin-jointed at their ends, loaded only at the joints, used for roofs, bridges and towers.
Wedge
A triangular block used to lift, split or clamp, working by friction on two inclined faces.
