5.1 Rectilinear motion: uniform and variable acceleration

A particle moving on a straight line has one coordinate s. Its velocity v is the rate of change of s, and its acceleration a is the rate of change of v. The three are linked by calculus, so if you know any one as a function of time, speed or position, you can find the others. This matters for trains, lifts, cars and pistons: braking distance, safe headway and stopping time all come from these equations.

You need: Unit 1 for units and sign sense, and school calculus for differentiation and integration.

One coordinate, three linked quantities

Fix a positive direction along the line and an origin. The position is s, in metres, measured from the origin along that direction. A positive s means the particle is on the positive side; a negative s means the opposite side.

Velocity v is the rate of change of position: v = ds/dt. It is in m/s. Acceleration a is the rate of change of velocity: a = dv/dt. It is in m/s². The chain rule gives a third form: a = dv/dt = (dv/ds)(ds/dt) = v dv/ds.

v = ds/dt, a = dv/dt = d²s/dt² = v dv/ds

s is position in m along the fixed positive direction; t is time in s; v is velocity in m/s; a is acceleration in m/s².

  • If v is constant, a = 0; this is uniform velocity.
  • If a is constant, v changes by equal amounts in equal times; this is uniform acceleration.
  • If neither is constant, the motion is non-uniform and you must integrate.
  • Some syllabi call this rectilinear translation.

On an s-t graph, the slope is v. On a v-t graph, the slope is a and the area under the curve between two times is the displacement Δs. Area below the t-axis counts as negative displacement.

Uniform acceleration: four working formulas

Let u be the velocity at t = 0 and v the velocity at time t. Let s be the displacement in that time. For constant a, integrate dv = a dt: v = u + at. Integrate v = ds/dt: s = ut + ½at². Eliminate t from the two: v² = u² + 2as. For constant a the average velocity is (u + v)/2, so s = ((u + v)/2)t.

v = u + at; s = ut + ½at²; v² = u² + 2as; s = ((u + v)/2)t

u is the initial velocity in m/s at t = 0; v is the final velocity in m/s at time t in s; a is the constant acceleration in m/s²; s is the displacement in m.

  1. Choose the positive direction and write it down.
  2. List u, v, a, s, t with signs and units.
  3. Count knowns; pick the equation with one unknown.
  4. Solve, then check that the sign of v makes physical sense.

Use the formula that omits the quantity you neither know nor want. Keep every sign: if the positive direction is forward, braking has a negative a. A negative a alone does not mean the particle moves backward; it means the change in v is negative. Convert km/h to m/s by dividing by 3.6 before using the formulas.

When acceleration is not constant

Most real motion does not keep a constant acceleration. Braking force changes with speed, a spring force changes with position, and engine thrust changes with time. Then v = u + at is invalid. Use calculus and the initial conditions.

Case 1: a = f(t). Write dv/dt = f(t), so dv = f(t) dt. Integrate from t = 0, v = u to t, v: v = u + ∫₀ᵗ f(τ)dτ. Then s = s₀ + ∫₀ᵗ v(τ)dτ.

Case 2: a = f(v). Write dv/dt = f(v), separate: dv/f(v) = dt. Integrate both sides. Use the initial v at t = 0 to fix the constant. A drag force gives this form.

Case 3: a = f(s). Use a = v dv/ds: v dv = f(s) ds. Integrate from s₀ to s and from u to v: ∫ᵤᵛ v dv = ∫ₛ₀ˢ f(s) ds. A spring or a position-dependent force gives this form.

In every case, apply the initial conditions after integrating. Check units at each step. For graphs: slope of s-t is v, slope of v-t is a, area under v-t is displacement. If v changes sign, split the area to get distance. Curved paths need tangential and normal components; see 5.2.

Worked example

Question. A train starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at 0.5 m/s² for 20 s. It then brakes uniformly at 1 m/s² until it stops. Find the maximum velocity, the total time and the total distance covered.

  1. Take the direction of motion as positive. First stage: u = 0, a₁ = +0.5 m/s², t₁ = 20 s.
  2. v_max = u + a₁t₁ = 0 + 0.5 × 20 = 10 m/s.
  3. s₁ = ut₁ + ½a₁t₁² = 0 + ½ × 0.5 × 20² = 100 m.
  4. Second stage: initial velocity = 10 m/s, final velocity = 0, a₂ = −1 m/s². From 0 = 10 + (−1)t₂, t₂ = 10 s.
  5. s₂ = 10 × 10 + ½ × (−1) × 10² = 100 − 50 = 50 m.
  6. Total time = 20 + 10 = 30 s. Total distance = 100 + 50 = 150 m.

Answer. Maximum velocity = 10 m/s, total time = 30 s, total distance = 150 m.

Exam traps

  • Using v = u + at when a is not constant. That formula assumes a is fixed; for a = f(t), a = f(v) or a = f(s), integrate instead.
  • Dropping signs during braking. If forward is positive, a = −1 m/s² but v stays positive, so s keeps increasing.
  • Treating area under v-t as distance when v crosses zero. Area gives displacement; take absolute values of each part for distance.
  • Mixing km/h and m/s. Divide km/h by 3.6 before substituting.
  • Using (u + v)/2 as average velocity for variable acceleration. It holds only for constant a.

5.2 Curvilinear motion: tangential-normal and radial-transverse components

On a curved path, velocity points along the tangent. Acceleration has two perpendicular parts: one changes the speed along the tangent, the other changes the direction toward the centre of curvature. This split explains why a car can keep a steady speed on a bend and still accelerate. Engineers use it for road curves, railway tracks, rotating arms, robot links and satellite paths. The same idea also appears in polar form, where radial and transverse components describe motion measured from a fixed point.

You need 5.1 for v = ds/dt and Unit 1 for resolving vectors at right angles.

Velocity along the tangent, acceleration in two parts

Take a point P on a curved path. Let s be distance measured along the path from a fixed origin, and let v = ds/dt be the speed in m/s. The velocity vector is always tangent to the path: v = v et, where et is the unit tangent vector. The path also has a unit normal en that points toward the centre of curvature. At P, let ρ (Greek rho, in metres) be the radius of curvature. The acceleration has two perpendicular parts. Tangential acceleration at = dv/dt changes speed. Normal acceleration an = v²/ρ changes direction. Total a = √(at² + an²). Its angle from the tangent is tan⁻¹(an/at), leaning toward the centre. When at = 0 the angle is 90° from the tangent: the acceleration is purely normal, pointing to the centre of curvature. an is never outward. On a circle ρ is constant; on any other curve use ρ at that point. Some syllabi call these intrinsic coordinates or path variables.

a<sub>t</sub> = dv/dt, a<sub>n</sub> = v²/ρ, a = √(a<sub>t</sub>² + a<sub>n</sub>²)

v is speed in m/s; ρ is radius of curvature in m; at and an are acceleration components in m/s².

Polar coordinates: radial and transverse

Use polar coordinates when position is naturally a length and an angle, for example a rotating arm. Let r be distance from the pole in metres and θ the angle in radians. Unit vector er points outward along r; eθ points in the direction of increasing θ. A dot over a symbol means d/dt. The velocity components are vr = ṙ and vθ = rθ̇. The acceleration components are ar = r̈ − rθ̇² and aθ = rθ̈ + 2ṙθ̇. Read r̈ as how fast the radial velocity ṙ is changing; −rθ̇² as the inward turning term; 2ṙθ̇ as a term that appears because both radius and angle change. Rectangular components are the special case θ = 0, when ar becomes ẍ and aθ becomes ÿ.

a<sub>r</sub> = r̈ − rθ̇², a<sub>θ</sub> = rθ̈ + 2ṙθ̇

r is distance from the pole in m; θ is angle in rad; each dot is one time derivative; ar, aθ are in m/s².

Worked example

Question. A car enters a curve of radius 50 m at 20 m/s. The driver is still pressing the accelerator, so the speed is rising at 3 m/s². Find the magnitude and direction of the total acceleration at that instant.

  1. Speed v = 20 m/s and radius of curvature ρ = 50 m. Tangential acceleration is given: a_t = dv/dt = 3 m/s² along the tangent.
  2. Normal acceleration: a_n = v²/ρ = 20²/50 = 400/50 = 8 m/s², directed toward the centre.
  3. Total magnitude: a = √(a_t² + a_n²) = √(3² + 8²) = √73 = 8.54 m/s².
  4. Direction: angle from tangent = tan⁻¹(a_n/a_t) = tan⁻¹(8/3) = 69.4°, toward the centre of curvature.

Answer. The total acceleration is 8.54 m/s² at 69.4° from the tangent toward the centre of curvature.

Exam traps

  • Using a_n = v²/r only for circles: on any curved path use the radius of curvature ρ at that point.
  • Pointing a_n away from the centre: normal acceleration always points toward the centre of curvature.
  • Adding a_t and a_n as ordinary numbers: they are perpendicular, so use √(a_t² + a_n²).
  • In polar form, dropping −rθ̇² or 2ṙθ̇, or using degrees instead of radians for θ̇ and θ̈.
  • Calling a_t the total acceleration: a_t changes only speed; the total includes the direction-changing part.

5.3 Projectile motion

A projectile is a particle thrown into the air and then left to gravity. Its horizontal and vertical motions do not interfere. Horizontally the velocity stays constant, because no horizontal force acts. Vertically the particle has the constant acceleration g = 9.81 m/s² downward. Two straight-line problems run at once. This is how you find where a cricket ball lands and where a package dropped from a drone hits the ground. Air resistance is ignored throughout.

You need 5.1 for constant-acceleration formulas and 5.2 for resolving velocity into components.

Two motions at once

Let u be launch speed in m/s and θ the angle above the horizontal. Take x horizontal and y upward from the launch point. Air resistance is ignored, so the only force is weight mg downward.

x = (u cosθ) t, vx = u cosθ

x is horizontal displacement in m at time t in s; u is launch speed in m/s; θ is launch angle; vx is constant horizontal velocity in m/s.

y = (u sinθ) t − ½ g t²,   v<sub>y</sub> = u sinθ − g t

y is vertical displacement in m; g = 9.81 m/s²; vy is vertical velocity in m/s at time t.

Horizontal motion is uniform. Vertical motion is constant acceleration from 5.1. At the top vy = 0.

The equations share t. A ball kicked at 20 m/s at 30° keeps vx = 17.3 m/s, while vy starts at 10 m/s and loses 9.81 m/s each second. The path bends only because vy changes; vx does not.

The path is a parabola

The trajectory is y as a function of x. From x = (u cosθ)t, t = x/(u cosθ).

  1. Substitute into y = (u sinθ)t − ½gt².
  2. Replace sinθ/cosθ by tanθ and 1/cos²θ by sec²θ.
  3. Recognise a quadratic in x.

y = x tanθ − g x² / (2u² cos²θ)

y is height in m at horizontal distance x in m; θ is launch angle; u is launch speed in m/s; g = 9.81 m/s².

This is a quadratic in x, so the path is a parabola opening downward. The apex is at x = u² sin2θ/(2g), where vy = 0.

The figure plots it for u = 20 m/s and θ = 30°.

Doubling u makes a wider, higher parabola. Rise and fall are mirror images on level ground.

The curve crosses y = 0 at launch and at the range R. It is steepest at those points and flat at the apex. The same parabola describes a fountain jet and a thrown stone.

Level ground formulas

Level ground means launch and landing at the same height. Set y = 0 and solve.

T = 2u sinθ / g

T is time of flight in s; the factor 2 is for the up and down halves.

R = u² sin2θ / g

R is horizontal range in m on level ground; sin2θ = 2 sinθ cosθ.

H = u² sin²θ / (2g)

H is maximum height in m above launch.

R is largest at θ = 45°, where sin2θ = 1 and R = u²/g. Angles 30° and 60° give the same range.

Horizontal projection from height h: initial vy = 0, so h = ½gt², t = √(2h/g), range = u√(2h/g).

Use level-ground formulas only when heights match; otherwise solve the quadratic.

Worked example

Question. A ball is kicked from level ground at u = 20 m/s at θ = 30° to the horizontal. Take g = 9.81 m/s². Find the time of flight, the maximum height and the horizontal range.

  1. u sinθ = 20 sin30° = 10 m/s.
  2. u cosθ = 20 cos30° = 17.32 m/s, constant.
  3. T = 2(10)/9.81 = 2.04 s.
  4. H = 10²/(2 × 9.81) = 5.10 m.
  5. R = 20² sin60°/9.81 = 400 × 0.866/9.81 = 35.31 m.

Answer. Time of flight 2.04 s, maximum height 5.10 m, horizontal range 35.31 m.

Exam traps

  • Swapping components: cosθ goes with x, sinθ with y.
  • Using the range formula when landing height differs from launch height.
  • Taking g as 10 m/s² when the paper says 9.81.
  • Assuming vy = 0 at landing; on level ground it is −u sinθ.
  • Using degrees in a calculator set to radians.

5.4 Relative motion and constrained motion

Every velocity you quote is measured from some frame. A driver feels rain slant even when it falls straight down; a swimmer who aims straight across a river lands downstream. Relative motion gives the rule for changing frames. Constrained motion handles the other side: a string, a cable or a contact ties two bodies, so one coordinate fixes the other. These two ideas turn many moving-body problems into simple vector algebra or one differentiation. They are used in lifts, cranes, conveyor belts, river crossings and vehicle safety.

You need vector subtraction from Unit 1 and differentiation of position with respect to time from 5.1.

Relative velocity in a translating frame

Let frame B move relative to frame A. Both frames translate; neither rotates. The velocity of particle A as seen from B is written v_A/B. Measured velocities obey a simple vector sum.

v_A = v_B + v_A/B

v_A is the velocity of A measured in the ground frame; v_B is the velocity of frame B in the ground frame; v_A/B is the velocity of A as seen from B.

Rearrange: v_A/B = v_A − v_B. Subtract the observer's velocity as a vector, not as a number. The order matters: v_A/B = −v_B/A. The same rule holds for acceleration when B has constant acceleration: a_A = a_B + a_A/B.

Rain example. Rain falls vertically at 10 m/s. A car moves horizontally at 15 m/s. Take east as +x and up as +y. v_rain = (0, −10) m/s and v_car = (15, 0) m/s. Then v_rain/car = v_rain − v_car = (−15, −10) m/s.

|v_rel| = √(15² + 10²) = 18.0 m/s; θ = tan⁻¹(15/10) = 56.3° from vertical

v_rel is the velocity of rain relative to the car; θ is the angle between v_rel and the vertical.

River crossings and perpendicular roads

A swimmer has speed v_s in still water. The river current has speed v_c. If the swimmer heads straight across, the ground velocity is the vector sum v_s + v_c. The crossing time is width ÷ v_s, because the current does not change the across-river component. The downstream drift is v_c × crossing time.

To cross with no drift, the swimmer angles upstream. Let θ be the angle upstream from the straight-across direction. The upstream component must cancel the current: v_s sin θ = v_c. The across-river speed is then v_s cos θ. A boat going upstream has ground speed v_b − v_c; going downstream it has v_b + v_c, where v_b is the boat speed in still water.

Two vehicles on perpendicular roads use the same subtraction. If car A moves north at 12 m/s and car B moves east at 9 m/s, then v_A = (0, 12) m/s and v_B = (9, 0) m/s. The velocity of A as seen from B is v_A/B = (−9, 12) m/s. Its magnitude is √(9² + 12²) = 15 m/s. The angle from north is tan⁻¹(9/12) = 36.9° toward the west.

Constrained motion: string, pulley and ladder

A constraint removes freedom. A string, a cable or a contact ties two bodies, so the system has one degree of freedom: one coordinate fixes the other. Write the total string length from a fixed datum, then differentiate.

  1. Mark a fixed datum, such as the pulley centre or the floor, and choose a positive direction for each moving body.
  2. Write the total string length L as a sum of segments, each in terms of coordinates such as x_A and y_B.
  3. Differentiate L with respect to time and set dL/dt = 0. This gives the velocity relation.
  4. Differentiate again to get the acceleration relation.

Block A on a table is joined by a string over a pulley to hanging block B. Let x_A be the distance from the pulley to A along the table, and y_B be the downward distance from the pulley to B. Then L = x_A + y_B + constant. Differentiating gives v_A + v_B = 0, so v_A = −v_B; the magnitudes are equal and the directions are opposite along the string. Differentiating again gives a_A = −a_B.

Two blocks on inclines over a pulley follow L = s_1 + s_2 + constant, with s_1 and s_2 measured away from the pulley along each incline. Then v_1 + v_2 = 0 and a_1 + a_2 = 0, if both coordinates are positive away from the pulley.

A ladder against a wall is a contact constraint. Let x be the distance of the foot from the wall and y the height of the top. For ladder length l, x² + y² = l². Differentiate: 2x v_x + 2y v_y = 0, so v_y = −(x/y)v_x. Differentiate again: v_x² + x a_x + v_y² + y a_y = 0.

Worked example

Question. Rain falls vertically at 10 m/s. A car moves horizontally at 15 m/s. Find the magnitude and direction of the velocity of the rain as seen by the driver. State the angle from the vertical and whether the rain appears to come from the front or the rear.

  1. Take +x along the car's motion and +y upward. Then v_rain = (0, −10) m/s and v_car = (15, 0) m/s.
  2. The velocity of rain relative to the car is v_rain/car = v_rain − v_car = (0, −10) − (15, 0) = (−15, −10) m/s.
  3. Magnitude: |v_rain/car| = √((−15)² + (−10)²) = √(225 + 100) = √325 = 18.0 m/s.
  4. Angle from vertical: θ = tan⁻¹(horizontal component ÷ vertical component) = tan⁻¹(15/10) = 56.3°.
  5. The horizontal component is opposite the car's motion, so in the car frame the rain comes from the front, toward the windscreen.

Answer. The rain appears to the driver as 18.0 m/s at 56.3° from the vertical, coming from the front.

Exam traps

  • Subtracting in the wrong order. v_A/B = v_A − v_B, so v_A/B is the velocity of A measured by B, not the reverse. Reversing the order reverses the direction.
  • Using the relative velocity formula in a rotating frame. v_A = v_B + v_A/B holds for translating frames. A turning car frame needs extra terms that are outside this syllabus.
  • Measuring string length from a moving pulley instead of a fixed datum. Then the length is not constant, dL/dt is not zero, and the velocity relation is wrong.
  • Mixing up river components. Upstream ground speed is v_b − v_c; downstream is v_b + v_c. A swimmer crossing straight uses v_s for the across component, not the total ground speed.
  • Dropping squared velocity terms in the ladder acceleration equation. The second derivative of x² + y² = l² gives v_x² + x a_x + v_y² + y a_y = 0, not x a_x + y a_y = 0.

5.5 Newton's second law and D'Alembert's principle

A particle changes velocity only because forces act on it, and Newton's second law states that exactly: ΣF = ma. In an inertial frame this vector equation, split into components, answers every acceleration question for machines and vehicles. D'Alembert's principle adds a fictitious inertia force −ma and turns the same problem into statics. This chapter covers the free body diagram, three component forms and the dynamic-equilibrium trick, then works a crate on a rough floor.

You need: free body diagrams and resolution of forces (Unit 1), the laws of friction and μk (Unit 3), and the acceleration components in 5.2.

The second law and what an inertial frame means

Newton's second law for a particle says the net force on it equals its mass times its acceleration.

ΣF = m a

ΣF is the vector sum of every real force on the particle, in N; m is its mass in kg; a is its acceleration in m/s², measured in an inertial frame. The equation is a vector equation, so it holds separately along every chosen direction.

An inertial frame does not itself accelerate. The ground is close enough for a trolley on a factory floor. A braking bus is not inertial: a book on its floor slides forward with no horizontal force, so ΣF = 0 while a ≠ 0 and the law fails. In an accelerating frame add a pseudo-force −mA, where A is the frame's acceleration.

The law as written is for one particle: a body whose parts do not move relative to each other, or one whose rotation you can ignore.

  1. Draw the free body diagram: weight mg, normals N, tensions T, friction f, applied forces, and nothing else.
  2. Choose axes so that the acceleration has as few components as possible.
  3. Write ΣF = ma along each axis, fixing the positive sense in advance.
  4. Solve the unknowns. Find each normal reaction before you use friction.
  5. Check that a points the same way as the net force, and that units are N, kg, m/s² only.

The law in three component systems

Rectangular axes (x, y, z) fit forces with fixed directions: weight down, normal perpendicular to the floor, a rope at a stated angle, friction along the surface.

ΣF<sub>x</sub> = m a<sub>x</sub>,  ΣF<sub>y</sub> = m a<sub>y</sub>,  ΣF<sub>z</sub> = m a<sub>z</sub>

ΣFx is the sum of x-components of all forces in N; ax is the x-component of acceleration in m/s², and likewise for y and z.

Normal-tangential axes (t, n) fit a particle whose path is known. Take t along the tangent, in the direction of motion, and n along the principal normal, pointing to the centre of curvature.

ΣF<sub>t</sub> = m a<sub>t</sub> = m dv/dt,  ΣF<sub>n</sub> = m a<sub>n</sub> = m v²/ρ

v is the speed in m/s, ρ is the radius of curvature in m, at changes the speed and an changes the direction. The n-equation demands a force even when the speed is constant, as for a car on a bend.

Cylindrical axes (r, θ, z) fit a particle on a rotating arm or a slider on a rotating rod: write ΣFr = m ar, ΣFθ = m aθ, ΣFz = m az, taking ar and aθ from the chapter on cylindrical components.

On a rough surface friction is one more force in ΣF: get N from the equation perpendicular to sliding, then put f = μkN opposite to the sliding velocity, where μk is the coefficient of kinetic friction. Never assume N = mg; use the perpendicular equation.

D'Alembert's principle and dynamic equilibrium

Move the right side of the second law to the left.

ΣF − m a = 0, that is Σ(F − m a) = 0

−m a is the inertia force: magnitude m a in N, direction opposite to the acceleration. Nothing physically pushes with it; it is a bookkeeping device.

With the inertia force added, the forces sum to zero, the body is in dynamic equilibrium, and every tool of statics applies unchanged. Moments balance too: ΣMO of the real forces plus the moment of −m a about O is zero, which lets you take moments about a point to kill unknown reactions. A lift cage or bracket becomes a statics problem. For the particle of this chapter the single inertia force is enough. A rigid body that spins as well also needs the inertia couple of magnitude IGα opposite to the angular acceleration, where IG is the mass moment of inertia about the centre of mass in kg·m² and α the angular acceleration in rad/s².

  • Never mix the two forms in one problem. Either draw the real free body diagram and write ΣF = m a, or add −m a and write ΣF = 0. Doing both counts m a twice.
  • The inertia force acts through the centre of mass, so its moment arm is measured from there.
  • The principle changes nothing physical; it only changes which set of equations you write.

Systems of particles. Sum the equation over all particles; internal forces cancel in pairs and only external forces survive.

ΣFext = M acm

ΣFext is the sum of external forces in N, M the total mass in kg, and acm the acceleration of the centre of mass in m/s². A cricket bat flung into the air spins wildly, yet its centre of mass follows the simple parabola of a single particle of mass M.

Worked example

Question. A 50 kg crate rests on a horizontal floor. A rope pulls it with tension T = 200 N at 30° above the horizontal. The coefficient of kinetic friction between crate and floor is μk = 0.25 and g = 9.81 m/s². The crate slides. Find its acceleration.

  1. Weight: mg = 50 × 9.81 = 490.5 N, vertical, downwards.
  2. Tension components: T cos30° = 200 × 0.8660 = 173.2 N horizontal; T sin30° = 200 × 0.5000 = 100.0 N vertical, upwards.
  3. Vertical direction: the crate does not leave the floor, so ay = 0. ΣFy = N + T sin30° − mg = 0, hence N = 490.5 − 100.0 = 390.5 N.
  4. Friction, opposing the sliding: f = μkN = 0.25 × 390.5 = 97.6 N.
  5. Horizontal direction: ΣFx = T cos30° − f = 173.2 − 97.6 = 75.6 N.
  6. Second law: a = ΣFx/m = 75.6/50 = 1.51 m/s², along the floor in the direction of the pull.
  7. D'Alembert check: add an inertia force of 50 × 1.512 = 75.6 N backwards (about 75.5 N if you keep a = 1.51 m/s²); then 173.2 = 97.6 + 75.6, so the forces balance.

Answer. The crate accelerates at 1.51 m/s² along the floor, in the direction of the pull.

Exam traps

  • Writing ΣF = m a while the inertia force is also drawn on the diagram. That counts m a twice: the net real force is shared between two m a terms, so you report half the true acceleration. Choose one form and stay with it.
  • Putting N = mg when the rope is inclined. An upward pull at angle θ gives N = mg − T sinθ, smaller than mg, so friction μkN drops as well; a downward push raises N and raises friction.
  • Applying ΣF = m a inside an accelerating lift, train or trolley without the pseudo-force −mA for that frame.
  • On a curved path, forgetting the normal equation. Even at constant speed an = v²/ρ needs its own force, so the net force is not along the tangent.
  • Feeding a tension given in kgf straight into ΣF = m a. Multiply by 9.81 to get newtons first.

5.6 Work-energy principle and conservation of energy

The work-energy principle replaces force and acceleration bookkeeping with a scalar ledger of energy. The work done by all forces on a particle equals the change in its kinetic energy, ½mv². When a force is conservative — its work between two points depends only on those points, not on the path — that work can be stored as potential energy and traded back. This lets you find speed from height, spring compression, or braking distance without knowing the time. Engineers use it for ramps, pile drivers, vehicle stopping distances, and roller-coaster loops.

You need: 5.5 for forces and free body diagrams, Unit 3 for friction, and 5.1 for the kinematic equations.

Work of a force

Work measures energy transferred by a force. For a constant force F acting while a particle moves a straight distance d, with α the angle between the force and the displacement, the work is:

U = F d cos α

U is work in joules (J); F is the constant force in newtons (N); d is the straight displacement in metres (m); α is the angle between F and the displacement. One joule is one newton-metre.

A force perpendicular to the motion does zero work because cos 90° = 0. For a variable force, split the path into small steps dr. Each step contributes F · dr, and the total is U = ∫ F · dr from r₁ to r₂. The dot product keeps only the along-path part, and the integral follows the actual path.

  • Weight W = mg: U = −W Δy = −mg(y₂ − y₁). Positive when the particle falls, negative when it rises.
  • Spring force F = −kx: U = −½k(x₂² − x₁²). x is extension from natural length in metres, k is stiffness in N/m.
  • Kinetic friction f = μN: U = −f s = −μN s. s is the distance travelled on the rough surface, μ is the coefficient of kinetic friction, N is the normal force.

Power is the rate of doing work: P = dU/dt = F · v. P is power in watts (W), v is velocity in m/s. One watt is one joule per second.

Kinetic energy and the work-energy principle

Kinetic energy is the energy a particle has because of its speed:

T = ½ m v²

T is kinetic energy in joules; m is mass in kg; v is speed in m/s. T is a scalar and cannot be negative.

The work-energy principle for one particle is:

U₁₋₂ = T₂ − T₁

U₁₋₂ is the total work done by every force on the particle between state 1 and state 2; T₁ and T₂ are the kinetic energies at those states.

The principle follows from Newton's second law. Write F = ma = m dv/dt, take the dot product with dr = v dt, and integrate along the path. All forces count: weight, spring, friction, normal, tension, and applied forces.

For a system of particles, U_ext + U_int = ΔT. U_ext is the work of external forces and U_int is the work of internal forces. In a rigid body the internal distances do not change, so U_int = 0. In a linked system, internal works cancel if the links are ideal.

The principle gives speed, not direction. A particle moving in a circle at constant speed has ΔT = 0, so the net work on it is zero even though its velocity direction changes.

Conservative forces and potential energy

A force is conservative if the work it does between two positions depends only on those positions, not on the path taken. Gravity and the force of an ideal spring are conservative. Friction is non-conservative because its work depends on path length.

For a conservative force, define a potential energy V so that the work done by the force is V₁ − V₂ = −ΔV. In one dimension, the force is:

F = −dV/ds

F is the conservative force in N; V is potential energy in J; s is position in m. In three dimensions this becomes F = −∇V.

  • Near Earth's surface, V = mgy. y is height above a chosen datum in m, m is mass in kg, g = 9.81 m/s².
  • For a spring, V = ½kx². x is extension or compression from natural length in m, k is stiffness in N/m.

If only conservative forces do work, mechanical energy E = T + V is constant:

T₁ + V₁ = T₂ + V₂

T is kinetic energy, V is potential energy, and the subscripts 1 and 2 are the two states.

A block sliding down a smooth incline trades V for T: it loses mgh of potential and gains ½mv² of kinetic. Some syllabi call V the potential function; the associated force field is then conservative.

Fix the datum y = 0 once for the whole problem. Using different datums at state 1 and state 2 breaks the equation.

Non-conservative work and the alternative form

When non-conservative forces such as friction or an external push act, split the total work: U₁₋₂ = U_c + U_nc. U_c is the work of conservative forces and U_nc is the work of non-conservative forces. Since U_c = −ΔV, the work-energy principle becomes:

U_nc = ΔT + ΔV

U_nc is the work of non-conservative forces in J; ΔT = T₂ − T₁ is the change in kinetic energy; ΔV = V₂ − V₁ is the change in potential energy.

Equivalently, the work of non-conservative forces equals the change in total mechanical energy E = T + V. If U_nc is negative, mechanical energy decreases. Friction converts it to heat and sound; the energy leaves the mechanical account but does not disappear.

The work of friction is −μN s, so it depends on the path length s. A longer route on a rough floor loses more energy. This is why a sliding block stops sooner on a rough patch and why braking distance grows with speed.

Use energy when you want a speed at a position and the forces are known as functions of position. Use Newton's second law or impulse and momentum when you need direction, time, or force during an impact.

Worked example

Question. A 2 kg block starts from rest and slides 5 m down a smooth incline of 30° to the horizontal. It then meets a rough horizontal floor with μ = 0.3. Find its speed at the bottom of the incline and the distance it slides before stopping. Take g = 9.81 m/s².

  1. Assume the block moves onto the floor smoothly with no loss of speed at the junction.
  2. Height fallen: h = 5 sin 30° = 2.5 m.
  3. On the smooth incline the normal force does zero work. Conservation of energy: mgh = ½mv², so v = √(2gh) = √(2 × 9.81 × 2.5) = √49.05 = 7.00 m/s.
  4. At the bottom, T = ½mv² = ½ × 2 × 49.05 = 49.05 J.
  5. On the rough floor, N = mg = 2 × 9.81 = 19.62 N and friction f = μN = 0.3 × 19.62 = 5.886 N.
  6. Work-energy on the floor: −f d = 0 − T, so d = T/f = 49.05 / 5.886 = 8.33 m.

Answer. Speed at the bottom is 7.0 m/s and the block stops after 8.33 m on the rough floor.

Exam traps

  • Giving friction positive work. Kinetic friction opposes sliding, so its work is −μN s; using +μN s adds energy that does not exist.
  • Choosing different datums for V at state 1 and state 2. Fix y = 0 once; only ΔV enters the equation.
  • Using U = F d cos α when F or α changes along the path. Integrate F · dr instead.
  • Expecting energy to give direction. Work-energy gives speed magnitude only; direction needs force or momentum analysis.
  • Forgetting that normal force can do work if the surface itself moves. On a fixed surface it is perpendicular to the motion; on a moving cart it may not be.

5.7 Impulse and momentum principle

A force that acts for a short time changes a particle's motion by an amount that depends on both the force and how long it acts. That product is impulse, measured in newton seconds (N·s). The impulse-momentum principle says the total impulse on a particle equals the change in its momentum. Engineers use it for cricket-ball hits, hammer blows, rocket thrust, car crashes and recoil, where the force is large, changes quickly and is hard to write as a simple function of time.

You need F = ma from 5.5 and resolving a vector into components from 5.2.

Impulse is area under the force-time graph

A constant force F acting for a time Δt gives an impulse I = F Δt. Real forces change during contact: a bat on a ball, a hammer on a nail, a car on a barrier. Divide the contact time into small intervals. On each interval the force is almost constant, so the impulse on that interval is F Δt. Adding the intervals gives I = ∫ F dt from t₁ to t₂. Impulse is a vector in N·s, pointing along the force.

I = ∫ F dt (from t₁ to t₂)

I is linear impulse in N·s; F is the force in N at time t in s; t₁ and t₂ are the start and end of the contact in s.

On a force-time graph, impulse is the area under the curve. The peak force is not the average force. The average force over the contact is F_avg = I / Δt, where Δt = t₂ − t₁. It is the height of a rectangle that has the same area and the same base as the actual force-time curve. For a triangular pulse of peak F_max and base Δt, the area is ½ F_max Δt.

  • For components: I_x = ∫ F_x dt, and similarly for y and z.
  • The unit N·s equals kg·m/s, the unit of momentum.

Impulse-momentum principle and conservation

Momentum is p = mv, with m in kg and v in m/s, so p is in kg·m/s. Newton's second law is F = ma = m dv/dt. Integrate both sides from time 1 to time 2: ∫F dt = m v₂ − m v₁. Rearranged, m v₁ + ∫F dt = m v₂. This is the impulse-momentum principle, also called the impulse-momentum theorem.

m v₁ + ∫ F dt = m v₂

m is mass in kg; v₁ and v₂ are initial and final velocities in m/s; ∫F dt is the total external impulse in N·s.

Use it component by component. In the x direction, m v₁ₓ + ∫Fₓ dt = m v₂ₓ. If the external impulse in a direction is zero, momentum in that direction does not change. Internal forces between parts of a system cancel in pairs. For a system of particles, ΣF_ext = d(Σmv)/dt. Recoil of a gun, an explosion, and a bullet embedding in a block all follow this rule along a line. The next chapter 5.8 adds how much speed is returned in a collision.

  1. Choose a positive direction and mark it on a sketch.
  2. Write every velocity with its sign in that direction.
  3. Compute the total external impulse in that direction; if it is zero, use momentum conservation.
  4. Solve for the unknown velocity or impulse, then check units.

Moment of momentum about a point

Moment of momentum about a point O, also called angular momentum, is H_O = r × m v. Here r is the position vector from O to the particle in m, m is mass in kg, and v is velocity in m/s. H_O is in kg·m²/s and is perpendicular to the plane of r and v by the right-hand rule. For plane motion its magnitude is m v d, where d is the perpendicular distance from O to the line of velocity; take anticlockwise as positive.

ΣM_O = dH_O / dt   and   ∫ M_O dt = H_O2 − H_O1

H_O is moment of momentum about O in kg·m²/s; ΣM_O is total moment about O in N·m; ∫M_O dt is angular impulse in N·m·s.

The first equation is the moment-of-momentum equation for a particle. The second defines angular impulse: the moment of a force integrated over time equals the change in moment of momentum. When the force is central—its line always passes through O—the moment about O is zero, so H_O stays constant. A satellite under gravity from the Earth and a ball tied to a string are examples. Conservation of angular momentum is the same statement with zero external moment.

Worked example

Question. A cricket ball of mass 0.15 kg arrives at a bat at 30 m/s. The bat sends it straight back along the same line at 40 m/s. The bat is in contact with the ball for 0.01 s. Find (a) the impulse on the ball and (b) the average force exerted by the bat.

  1. Take the outgoing direction, away from the bat, as positive. Then the initial velocity v₁ = −30 m/s and the final velocity v₂ = +40 m/s.
  2. Initial momentum p₁ = m v₁ = 0.15 × (−30) = −4.5 kg·m/s.
  3. Final momentum p₂ = m v₂ = 0.15 × 40 = +6.0 kg·m/s.
  4. Impulse I = p₂ − p₁ = 6.0 − (−4.5) = 10.5 N·s. The positive sign means the impulse is outgoing.
  5. Average force F_avg = I / Δt = 10.5 / 0.01 = 1050 N, acting outgoing.

Answer. The impulse on the ball is 10.5 N·s outgoing, and the average force is 1050 N outgoing.

Exam traps

  • Signs: a ball hit straight back has a change of velocity equal to the sum of the speeds, not the difference. Choose a positive direction and keep it.
  • Impulse is the whole area under the force-time graph, not peak force times time. For a triangle use ½ × base × height.
  • Impulse and momentum have the same units, N·s = kg·m/s. Do not report impulse in N or in joules.
  • Momentum is conserved in a direction only when the external impulse in that direction is zero. Internal forces cancel, but friction, gravity or a wall can change momentum.
  • Average force is impulse divided by contact time, not the peak force. For a triangular pulse the peak is twice the average force.

5.8 Impact: direct and oblique central impact

Two bodies meet, push hard for a very short time, and separate. The forces are internal to the pair, so total momentum does not change. A single number, the coefficient of restitution, says how much of the approach speed along the common normal is returned as separation speed. That normal is the line of impact. Direct impact keeps every velocity on that line; oblique impact has sideways velocity too. The same two equations settle both cases. Car bumpers, cricket bat and ball, and train buffers all use this model.

You need: impulse and momentum from 5.7, and resolving vectors from 5.2.

The line of impact and central impact

A collision lasts a very short time. During that time the push between the bodies is much larger than other forces, so the pair can be treated as isolated. The common normal at the point of contact is the line of impact. It is the only direction in which the collision changes velocity.

Central impact means both centres of mass lie on the line of impact. Then the bodies do not start to spin, and particle theory is enough. Direct central impact means all velocities lie on that line before and after. Oblique central impact means each velocity has a component perpendicular to the line as well.

e = (v₂′ − v₁′)/(v₁ − v₂)

v₁ and v₂ are velocities along the positive line of impact just before impact; v₁′ and v₂′ are the velocities just after; e is the coefficient of restitution, with 0 ≤ e ≤ 1. Use the same positive direction for all four.

e = 1 is perfectly elastic: separation speed equals approach speed. e = 0 is perfectly plastic: the bodies move together after impact. Real metal, wood and rubber have e between these limits.

Two equations solve a direct impact

For direct central impact write momentum along the line and restitution along the same line. The actual contact force need not be known.

m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ = m₁v₁′ + m₂v₂′

m₁ and m₂ are the masses; v₁ and v₂ are velocities before impact along the line; v₁′ and v₂′ are velocities after impact along the same line.

v₂′ − v₁′ = e(v₁ − v₂)

e is the coefficient of restitution; the right side uses the approach speed along the line.

These are two linear equations in v₁′ and v₂′. Solve them together. If e = 0, both bodies share one final velocity v = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/(m₁ + m₂).

  1. Choose one positive direction along the line of impact. Put signs on all four velocities.
  2. Substitute masses and signed velocities into the momentum equation.
  3. Substitute e and the approach speed into the restitution equation.
  4. Solve the two equations for the two unknown final velocities.
  5. Find kinetic energy before and after; energy lost = T − T′.

Kinetic energy T = ½m₁v₁² + ½m₂v₂². It always falls when e < 1; the lost mechanical energy becomes heat, sound and permanent deformation.

Oblique central impact

Resolve each velocity into a component along the line of impact and a component perpendicular to it. Call them v₁n, v₁t, v₂n, v₂t before, and v₁n′, v₁t′, v₂n′, v₂t′ after.

m₁v₁n + m₂v₂n = m₁v₁n′ + m₂v₂n′

subscript n means the component along the line of impact; momentum is conserved along this line.

v₁t′ = v₁t, v₂t′ = v₂t

subscript t means the component perpendicular to the line of impact; these pass through unchanged when the contact is smooth.

v₂n′ − v₁n′ = e(v₁n − v₂n)

e applies only along the line of impact, never to the perpendicular components.

The perpendicular components pass through unchanged. Restitution is never applied to them. After finding the components, get the final speed of each body from v′ = √(v_n′² + v_t′²). The total kinetic energy uses these full speeds.

For a ball hitting a fixed smooth wall, the wall's mass is effectively infinite. The line of impact is normal to the wall. The normal component reverses with factor e; the tangential component is unchanged. That is why a ball can leave a wall at a different angle from the one at which it arrived.

Worked example

Question. Ball A of mass 4 kg moves at 6 m/s and overtakes ball B of mass 2 kg moving at 3 m/s along the same straight line. The coefficient of restitution is 0.5. Find the velocities just after impact and the final kinetic energy. Take the original direction as positive.

  1. Let v_A′ and v_B′ be the final velocities in the positive direction. Momentum: 4(6) + 2(3) = 30 kg·m/s, so 4v_A′ + 2v_B′ = 30, or 2v_A′ + v_B′ = 15.
  2. Restitution: v_B′ − v_A′ = e(u_A − u_B) = 0.5(6 − 3) = 1.5 m/s. Hence v_B′ = v_A′ + 1.5.
  3. Substitute: 2v_A′ + (v_A′ + 1.5) = 15, so 3v_A′ = 13.5 and v_A′ = 4.5 m/s. Then v_B′ = 6.0 m/s.
  4. Check momentum: 4(4.5) + 2(6.0) = 18 + 12 = 30 kg·m/s.
  5. Kinetic energy before: ½(4)(6²) + ½(2)(3²) = 72 + 9 = 81 J. After: ½(4)(4.5²) + ½(2)(6²) = 40.5 + 36 = 76.5 J. Loss = 4.5 J, positive because e < 1.

Answer. v_A′ = 4.5 m/s and v_B′ = 6.0 m/s in the original direction; final kinetic energy is 76.5 J.

Exam traps

  • Restitution applies only along the line of impact. In oblique impact the perpendicular components do not change; applying e to them is wrong.
  • Fix one positive direction along the line and keep signs on all four velocities. Mixing speed and velocity signs makes e come out negative or above 1.
  • e = 1 means no kinetic energy loss, not that final speeds equal initial speeds. e = 0 means a common final velocity, not zero velocity.
  • Central impact needs both centres on the line of impact. If the line misses a centre, rotation appears and this particle model is incomplete; see Unit 6.
  • In oblique impact, momentum is a vector. Conserve it component by component; do not add total momentum magnitudes.

Takeaways

  1. Differentiation links position, velocity and acceleration on a straight line; integration goes the other way.
  2. Constant acceleration is the special case with ready formulas; variable acceleration needs calculus or a = v dv/dx.
  3. Velocity is always tangent to the path, and normal acceleration v²/ρ is the part that changes direction.
  4. Radial-transverse components are the natural choice when the radius and angle both change.
  5. A projectile is two independent straight-line motions: constant horizontal velocity and vertical acceleration g.
  6. Relative velocity depends on the chosen frame, and a string or contact adds a constraint equation.
  7. Newton's second law holds in an inertial frame, and adding −ma gives D'Alembert equilibrium.
  8. Net work changes kinetic energy, and conservative forces let you trade kinetic for potential energy.
  9. Impulse equals change in momentum, and zero external impulse means momentum is conserved.
  10. In impact, momentum is conserved along the line of impact, while the coefficient of restitution fixes the relative speed after collision.

Glossary

Acceleration
The rate of change of velocity with time; a = dv/dt, a vector.
Angular acceleration
The rate of change of angular velocity; α = dω/dt, measured in rad/s².
Angular velocity
The rate of change of angle with time; ω = dθ/dt, measured in rad/s.
Central impact
Impact in which the contact force acts along the line joining the centres of the two bodies.
Coefficient of restitution
A number e between 0 and 1 that gives the ratio of relative speed after impact to relative speed before impact along the line of impact.
Conservative force
A force whose work between two points is independent of the path, so a potential energy can be defined.
Constraint
A restriction such as a string, track or contact that limits possible motion and gives a relation between coordinates.
Displacement
The change in position vector from initial to final point; it is not the same as distance travelled.
Elastic collision
A collision in which kinetic energy is conserved; e = 1 for direct central impact of smooth bodies.
Frame of reference
A coordinate system with a clock from which position, velocity and acceleration are measured.
Impulse
The time integral of force, J = ∫F dt; it equals the change in linear momentum.
Inelastic collision
A collision in which kinetic energy is not conserved; e < 1, and e = 0 for perfectly plastic impact.
Inertia force
The fictitious force −ma added in D'Alembert's principle to write a dynamics problem as equilibrium.
Inertial frame
A frame in which a free particle remains at rest or in uniform straight-line motion; Newton's laws hold.
Kinetic energy
Energy of motion; K = ½mv² for a particle of mass m and speed v.
Line of impact
The common normal at the contact point during impact; momentum is conserved along this line.
Linear momentum
The product p = mv of mass and velocity; a vector conserved when net external impulse is zero.
Mechanical energy
The sum of kinetic and potential energy of a particle or system.
Non-uniform motion
Motion in which velocity changes with time, so acceleration is nonzero.
Particle
A body whose size and shape are ignored and whose mass is treated as concentrated at a point.
Position vector
A vector from the origin of a chosen frame to the particle; it fixes location in space.
Potential energy
Energy stored in a conservative force field by virtue of position; examples are mgh and ½kx².
Radius of curvature
The radius ρ of the circle that best fits the path at a point; used in a_n = v²/ρ.
Uniform motion
Motion with constant velocity on a straight line, so acceleration is zero.
Velocity
The rate of change of position with time; v = dr/dt, a vector tangent to the path.
Work
Work done by a force is W = ∫F·dr; it is a scalar measured in joules.
