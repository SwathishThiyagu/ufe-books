6.1 Translation and rotation about a fixed axis

Most machines move in one of two simple ways. A lift car slides up its shaft, and every bolt of the car has the same velocity and the same acceleration. A ceiling-fan blade spins about a fixed shaft, and every point of the blade travels a circle about that shaft; the farther a point is from the shaft, the faster it moves. This chapter gives the kinematics of both motions. It defines angular displacement, angular velocity and angular acceleration for fixed-axis rotation, then links them to the ordinary linear velocity and acceleration of a point at radius r. Every later dynamics calculation for wheels, shafts and rotors starts here.

You need: particle kinematics from Unit 5 (rectilinear and curvilinear motion, tangential and normal components) and comfortable use of angles measured in radians.

Rigid body, translation, fixed-axis rotation

A rigid body is a collection of particles in which the distance between any two particles never changes. No real body is perfectly rigid, but a steel shaft or a concrete beam deforms so little that the model works.

Translation is motion in which every line drawn on the body keeps its direction. Every point then has the same velocity and the same acceleration at a given instant. A lift car, a train on a straight track and a block sliding down an incline all translate. A chair on a Ferris wheel also translates, because it stays upright while its centre moves on a circle; the body itself does not turn.

Fixed-axis rotation is motion in which every point of the body travels a circle whose centre lies on one fixed line, the axis of rotation. Points lying on the axis do not move at all. A fan blade, a door on its hinges and a flywheel on a shaft rotate about a fixed axis. Each point only changes its position along its own circle, so one angle describes the whole body. Some syllabi call this 'rotation about a fixed axis'.

Choose a sign convention once and keep it: say anticlockwise is positive and clockwise is negative. A spin that slows down then has angular acceleration of the opposite sign to its angular velocity.

Angular displacement, velocity, acceleration

Fix a reference line in space and let θ be the angle a radius of the body makes with it. θ is the angular displacement, in radians (rad). One full turn is 2π rad. One radian is the angle for which the arc length equals the radius, so an arc of length s on a circle of radius r subtends θ = s/r.

Angular velocity ω is the rate of change of θ, and angular acceleration α is the rate of change of ω. Both belong to the body as a whole: every point shares the same θ, ω and α.

ω = dθ/dt α = dω/dt = d²θ/dt²

θ is the angular displacement (rad); t is time (s); ω is the angular velocity (rad/s); α is the angular acceleration (rad/s²).

When α is constant, three equations link the quantities, exactly as their rectilinear twins do.

ω = ω₀ + αt    θ = ω₀t + ½αt²    ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ

ω₀ is the angular velocity at t = 0 (rad/s); ω is the angular velocity at time t (rad/s); α is the constant angular acceleration (rad/s²); θ is the angular displacement in that time (rad); t is the time (s).

  1. Convert every speed to rad/s first: rad/s = rpm × 2π/60.
  2. List ω₀, ω, α, θ, t and mark which one is unknown.
  3. Pick the equation that holds the unknown and no other unknown.
  4. Substitute with units, then check the sign of the answer against your convention.

Velocity and acceleration of a point at radius r

Take a point P at perpendicular distance r from the fixed axis. Its speed along its circle is v = ωr, directed along the tangent. This is why ω must be in rad/s. A shaft turning at 60 rpm has ω = 60 × 2π/60 = 6.283 rad/s, so a point at r = 0.5 m moves at 3.14 m/s, not 30 m/s.

v = ωr a_t = αr a_n = ω²r = v²/r

v is the speed along the tangent (m/s); a_t is the tangential acceleration (m/s²), along the tangent, changing the speed; a_n is the normal or centripetal acceleration (m/s²), directed towards the axis, changing only the direction of the velocity; r is the perpendicular distance from the axis (m); ω is in rad/s; α is in rad/s².

The two acceleration parts are perpendicular, so the total acceleration has magnitude a = √(a_t² + a_n²). A body spinning at steady speed has α = 0 and only a_n survives. A body starting from rest has ω = 0, so only a_t exists at that first instant.

These relations size real hardware: the rim stress in a flywheel grows with ω²r, bearings must carry the pull that a_n creates, and the safe working speed of a grinding wheel is set by a_n at its rim.

Worked example

Question. A flywheel starts from rest with constant angular acceleration α = 2.5 rad/s². Find its angular velocity and its angular displacement after t = 4.0 s. At that instant, find the tangential and normal accelerations of a point 0.30 m from the axis.

  1. Data: ω₀ = 0 rad/s (starts from rest), α = 2.5 rad/s², t = 4.0 s, r = 0.30 m.
  2. Angular velocity: ω = ω₀ + αt = 0 + 2.5 × 4.0 = 10 rad/s.
  3. Angular displacement: θ = ω₀t + ½αt² = 0 + 0.5 × 2.5 × (4.0)² = 0.5 × 2.5 × 16 = 20 rad, which is 20/(2π) ≈ 3.2 turns.
  4. Check with the third equation: ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ gives 10² = 0 + 2 × 2.5 × 20 = 100. Both sides are 100, so the values agree.
  5. Tangential acceleration: a_t = αr = 2.5 × 0.30 = 0.75 m/s², along the tangent.
  6. Normal acceleration: a_n = ω²r = (10)² × 0.30 = 100 × 0.30 = 30 m/s², directed towards the axis.
  7. Total acceleration: a = √(0.75² + 30²) = √(0.5625 + 900) = √900.56 = 30.01 m/s², almost entirely towards the axis.

Exam traps

  • Feeding ω in degrees per second or rpm straight into v = ωr. Convert first: rad/s = rpm × 2π/60.
  • Reporting only a_t and forgetting a_n = ω²r. In the example a_n is 40 times larger than a_t, so the omission is badly wrong.
  • Mixing degrees and radians in ω² = ω₀² + 2αθ; the equation only holds when α is in rad/s² and θ in rad.
  • Ignoring the sign of α for a slowing wheel. Its α is opposite to ω, and using +α gives an absurd ω.
  • Confusing the body's ω, which is the same for all points, with a point's linear velocity, which grows with r.

Analyze At t = 0 a pulley has ω = 10 rad/s anticlockwise and constant α = 4.0 rad/s² clockwise. When does it stop, and what happens after that? 5 marks

Take anticlockwise positive: ω₀ = +10 rad/s, α = −4.0 rad/s². Setting ω = 0 gives t = ω₀/(−α) = 10/4.0 = 2.5 s. The displacement to that point is θ = 10 × 2.5 − ½ × 4.0 × 2.5² = 12.5 rad. After 2.5 s the pulley reverses and spins clockwise, still with α = −4.0 rad/s².

Analyze Can a point of a rotating rigid body have zero total acceleration while the body spins? Explain. 5 marks

Only if the point lies on the axis, where r = 0. For any r > 0 the normal part a_n = ω²r is positive whenever ω ≠ 0, even if α = 0 and a_t = 0. So an off-axis point always accelerates towards the axis; that inward acceleration is what keeps it on its circle.

Yes. Every line on the book keeps its direction, so every point has the same velocity and acceleration. A cabin on a Ferris wheel translates along a curved path: its centre moves on a circle but the cabin stays upright and does not rotate.

6.2 General plane motion

A rigid body in general plane motion slides and turns at the same time. Every point stays in a plane parallel to a fixed plane. Choose any point A on the body as a base; the body translates with A and rotates about A. This splitting turns a hard motion into two simple ones. You meet it in a rolling wheel, a connecting rod in an engine, and a door swinging as its hinge moves. The same idea gives the velocity and acceleration of every point.

You need: translation and fixed-axis rotation from 6.1, and relative motion of particles from Unit 5, Kinematics and Kinetics of Particles.

Splitting the motion into two parts

Plane motion means every point of the body stays in a plane parallel to one fixed plane. Draw the body in that plane and choose a base point A. The body reaches its new position by translating every point with A, then rotating the body about A. Translation gives every point the velocity v_A. Rotation about A gives point B the extra velocity ω × r_{B/A}, where ω is the angular velocity of the body in rad/s and r_{B/A} is the position vector from A to B in metres. In plane motion ω is perpendicular to the plane, so the extra velocity has magnitude ω r_{B/A} and points perpendicular to AB.

v_B = v_A + ω × r_{B/A}

v_B and v_A are the velocities of points B and A (m/s); ω is the body's angular velocity (rad/s); r_{B/A} is the vector from A to B (m); × is the vector cross product. The relative term is perpendicular to AB.

Acceleration has three terms

Differentiate the velocity equation. The base accelerates with a_A. Rotation about A adds a tangential part and a centripetal part. The angular acceleration α (rad/s²) gives the tangential term α × r_{B/A}, of magnitude α r_{B/A}, perpendicular to AB. The spin gives the centripetal term −ω² r_{B/A}, of magnitude ω² r_{B/A}, pointing from B to A. So a_B = a_A + α × r_{B/A} − ω² r_{B/A}. The minus sign matters: the centripetal term points toward the base A, not away from it. Some syllabi call these the absolute and relative velocity and acceleration equations.

a_B = a_A + α × r_{B/A} − ω² r_{B/A}

a_B, a_A are accelerations (m/s²); α is angular acceleration (rad/s²); ω is angular velocity (rad/s); r_{B/A} is the vector from A to B (m). The α × r term is tangential; the −ω² r term points from B to A.

Rolling without slipping

A wheel of radius R rolls without slipping on a fixed road. Let G be its centre and C the contact point. No slipping means the contact point has zero velocity at that instant: v_C = 0. Put C as the base in v_G = v_C + ω × r_{G/C}; this gives v_G = ωR. Differentiating gives a_G = αR for the centre on a straight road. The top point T has v_T = v_G + ωR = 2v_G because its rotation velocity and the centre velocity point the same way. The contact point has v_C = 0, so it is the instantaneous centre for that instant; see 6.4.

  • Rolling without slipping: v_G = ωR, a_G = αR, v_C = 0.
  • Top point: v_T = 2v_G.
  • Contact point is the instantaneous centre for that instant.

Worked example

Question. A wheel of radius 0.40 m rolls without slipping on a horizontal road. Its centre moves at 3.0 m/s. Find (a) the angular velocity of the wheel, (b) the speed of the top point, and (c) the speed of the contact point.

  1. Data: R = 0.40 m, v_G = 3.0 m/s. Rolling without slipping means the contact point C has v_C = 0.
  2. Angular velocity: v_G = ω R, so ω = v_G / R = 3.0 / 0.40 = 7.5 rad/s.
  3. Top point: v_T = v_G + ω R = 3.0 + 7.5 × 0.40 = 3.0 + 3.0 = 6.0 m/s.
  4. Contact point: v_C = v_G − ω R = 3.0 − 3.0 = 0 m/s.

Answer. ω = 7.5 rad/s, top point speed = 6.0 m/s, contact point speed = 0 m/s.

Exam traps

  • Adding ωR to every point in the same direction. On a rolling wheel the top point gets +ωR, the bottom point gets −ωR, and the contact point has zero velocity.
  • Dropping the centripetal term −ω² r in acceleration. A point on a rotating body always has ω² r toward the centre even when α = 0.
  • Using r_{B/A} in the wrong direction. r_{B/A} points from A to B; reversing it reverses the relative velocity and tangential acceleration.
  • Treating the instantaneous centre as a fixed point. It moves from instant to instant; only for that instant is the body in pure rotation about it.
  • Mixing rad/s with rev/min. Convert revolutions per minute to rad/s by multiplying by 2π/60 before using v = ωr.

6.3 Velocity and acceleration by relative motion method

The relative motion method finds the velocity and acceleration of one point of a rigid body when another point is known. Choose point A as the base. Point B moves with A, and also rotates about A. The velocity equation adds ω × r. The acceleration equation adds α × r and the inward term −ω²r. The method is used for slider-crank engines, four-bar linkages, pump mechanisms and door closers. Constraints such as a slider moving in a straight guide supply the missing directions, so two component equations solve two unknowns.

You need: 6.2 general plane motion, vector components, and the relative-motion ideas from Unit 5.

Velocity of one point relative to another

Points A and B are fixed on the same rigid body. The distance AB cannot change. So B can only move around A by rotation. Write the absolute velocity of B as the vector sum of the absolute velocity of A and the velocity of B relative to A. Some syllabi call this the relative velocity method.

v<sub>B</sub> = v<sub>A</sub> + ω × r<sub>B/A</sub>

vB is the velocity of point B in m/s; vA is the velocity of point A in m/s; ω is the angular velocity of the body in rad/s, positive anticlockwise; rB/A is the position vector from A to B in m.

The relative velocity vB/A = ω × rB/A has magnitude ωr. Its direction is perpendicular to AB. For plane motion, rotate AB by 90° in the direction of ω. If ω is anticlockwise, rotate AB anticlockwise.

A velocity polygon is the triangle or polygon formed by these vectors. Draw vA first. Add vB/A from its tip. The closing vector from the start to the final tip is vB. The polygon closes because vB = vA + vB/A. Use the known constraint directions: a slider point moves along its guide; a pin on a fixed link moves perpendicular to that link.

  1. Choose a base point with known velocity, usually a crank pin.
  2. Write rB/A from A to B using the given length and angle.
  3. Write vB and vA with their known constraint directions.
  4. Use ω = ωk and solve the x and y component equations.
  5. Check that the relative velocity is perpendicular to AB.

Acceleration of one point relative to another

Differentiate the velocity equation. The angular velocity may change, so add α × r. The rotating vector ω × r also changes direction, which gives −ω²r.

a<sub>B</sub> = a<sub>A</sub> + α × r<sub>B/A</sub> − ω² r<sub>B/A</sub>

aB and aA are absolute accelerations in m/s²; α is angular acceleration in rad/s², positive anticlockwise; ω is angular velocity in rad/s; rB/A is the position vector from A to B in m.

The tangential term α × r has magnitude αr and is perpendicular to AB. The normal term −ω²r has magnitude ω²r and points from B toward A. This is the centripetal direction of B's path relative to A.

An acceleration polygon is drawn like the velocity polygon: aA, then α × r, then −ω²r, then close to aB. A slider's acceleration is along the guide, but a pin's acceleration has two components. Solve x and y components. Keep ω in rad/s; squaring a degree value is a common error.

  • Normal term: magnitude ω²r, always from B toward A.
  • Tangential term: magnitude αr, perpendicular to AB.
  • α is unknown in many linkage problems; one component equation gives it.

Slider-crank and four-bar at one position

A slider-crank has crank OA, connecting rod AB and slider B. A moves perpendicular to OA. B moves along the slider axis. Use vB = vA + ωAB × rB/A to find ωAB and vB. Then use the acceleration equation to find αAB and aB.

A four-bar has crank O2A, coupler AB and rocker O3B. Points A and B move perpendicular to their own fixed-link radii. The coupler's angular velocity comes from the velocity equation. Acceleration uses the normal accelerations of both cranks and the coupler.

Choose axes along AB and perpendicular to AB. The relative velocity ω × r has no component along AB, so the components of vA and vB along AB are equal. This shortcut removes one unknown before you solve. For acceleration, resolve along and perpendicular to AB; the normal term lies along AB.

Only one position is needed at a time. Repeat the same equations for the next crank angle. Plot vB or aB against crank angle to see the engine's speed variation.

Worked example

Question. Bar AB is 1.2 m long and makes 30° with the horizontal. End A moves horizontally to the right at 2.0 m/s. End B is constrained to move vertically. Find the angular velocity of the bar and the velocity of B.

  1. Take i to the right, j upward and k out of the page. Then rB/A = 1.2(cos 30° i + sin 30° j) = 1.039 i + 0.600 j m.
  2. vA = 2.0 i m/s. B is constrained to a vertical guide, so vB = vB j, where vB is its signed upward speed in m/s.
  3. Let ω = ωk. Then ω × rB/A = ωk × (1.039 i + 0.600 j) = −0.600ω i + 1.039ω j.
  4. The velocity equation is vB j = 2.0 i − 0.600ω i + 1.039ω j. Match i: 0 = 2.0 − 0.600ω, so ω = 3.333 rad/s. The positive sign means anticlockwise.
  5. Match j: vB = 1.039 × 3.333 = 3.464 m/s. The positive sign means upward.

Answer. ω = 3.33 rad/s anticlockwise and v_B = 3.46 m/s upward.

Exam traps

  • Treating ωL as v_B instead of v_B/A. The relative velocity is perpendicular to AB, not along AB.
  • Dropping the −ω²r term in acceleration, or writing ωr instead of ω²r.
  • Giving the normal acceleration the wrong direction. −ω²r points from B toward A, not from A to B.
  • Using a velocity triangle when the directions are not perpendicular to AB and assuming a right angle that is not there.
  • Mixing degrees and radians, or mixing m/s with rad/s in the same equation.

6.4 Instantaneous centre of rotation

At one instant, a rigid body in plane motion can be treated as if it were rotating about a single point, the instantaneous centre I. That point has zero velocity at that instant. Draw a line perpendicular to the velocity at any point; I lies somewhere on that line. Two points give two lines, and their intersection is I. Then v = ωr gives every point's speed about I. This is a quick velocity tool for linkages, rolling wheels and sliding bars.

You need: 6.2 for plane motion as translation plus rotation, 6.3 for vB = vA + ω × rBA, and basic geometry of perpendiculars.

The point with zero velocity at this instant

At a chosen instant, every point P of a rigid body in plane motion has some velocity vP. Draw the line through P perpendicular to vP. If the body is rotating about some point I at that instant, then vP must be perpendicular to the line IP. So I lies on that perpendicular line. Two points P and Q give two perpendiculars; where they meet is the instantaneous centre I. The point I can lie on the body, outside it, or far away; it is a geometric construction, not a physical pin.

Use I exactly as a fixed centre for this one instant. The speed of P about I is vP = ω rP, where vP is the speed of P in m/s, ω is the angular speed of the body in rad/s, and rP is the distance IP in m. The velocity direction is perpendicular to IP, with the sense given by the rotation. At I, r = 0, so v = 0.

vP = ω rP

vP is speed of point P in m/s; ω is angular speed in rad/s; rP is distance from I to P in m.

When two velocities are parallel and unequal, their perpendiculars coincide, so I lies on the line joining the points; place it so the distances from I are in the ratio of the speeds. When the velocities are equal and parallel, the body has pure translation and I is at infinity. Some syllabi call this the instantaneous center of rotation, or ICR.

Rolling wheel and bar sliding on floor and wall

A wheel of radius R rolling without slipping is the standard example. At the instant shown, the contact point C with the ground has zero velocity, so C is the instantaneous centre. The centre O is distance R from C, so vO = ωR. The top point T is distance 2R from C, so vT = 2ωR, in the same direction as O but twice the speed. This is why the top of a rolling wheel moves faster than the hub.

A bar with one end on a floor and the other on a wall is another common case. End A slides along the floor, so its velocity is horizontal; the perpendicular at A is vertical. End B slides along the wall, so its velocity is vertical; the perpendicular at B is horizontal. Their intersection I is the instantaneous centre. Distances IA and IB follow from the geometry at that instant. Then vA = ω IA and vB = ω IB. As the bar slides, I moves, so the construction must be repeated at each instant.

The worked example below is the floor-wall bar: at this instant end A is on the floor and end B is on the wall. The same two perpendiculars locate I, and the same formula gives ω.

Use it for velocity only

The instantaneous centre is a velocity shortcut. In general plane motion the instantaneous centre is not a fixed axis: the material point at I has zero velocity, but generally nonzero acceleration, and I generally moves to a new position at the next instant. Do not use fixed-axis acceleration formulas such as aP = ω² rP or aP = α rP about a moving I. Use 6.3 for acceleration. Exception: if the body rotates about a fixed axis, I is fixed on that axis, the acceleration at I is zero, and fixed-axis formulas apply about that axis.

If I is at infinity, the body has pure translation: ω = 0 and every point has the same velocity. The formula v = ωr then needs a limit, not a finite radius. If the body rotates about a fixed axis, I is on that axis. For general plane motion, I changes with time and is not generally the mass centre. The relative-motion method and the instantaneous-centre method give the same velocity; choose the one with less geometry. Kinetics needs forces and the mass centre, so move to 6.5 after velocity.

Worked example

Question. At one instant, end A of a 1.5 m bar is at (0.9, 0) m and moves right at 2.0 m/s. End B is at (0, 1.2) m and moves down at 1.5 m/s. Locate the instantaneous centre I and find the angular velocity of the bar.

  1. Check the bar length: AB = √((0.9 − 0)² + (0 − 1.2)²) = √(0.81 + 1.44) = √2.25 = 1.5 m.
  2. Velocity at A is horizontal to the right. Its perpendicular is the vertical line x = 0.9 m.
  3. Velocity at B is vertical downward. Its perpendicular is the horizontal line y = 1.2 m.
  4. The intersection is I = (0.9, 1.2) m.
  5. Distances: IA = 1.2 m and IB = 0.9 m.
  6. At A: v_A = ω IA, so ω = 2.0 ÷ 1.2 = 1.6667 rad/s. At B: ω = 1.5 ÷ 0.9 = 1.6667 rad/s, which agrees.
  7. Direction: A moves right about I, so the rotation is counterclockwise; B moves down about I in the same sense.

Answer. I = (0.9, 1.2) m and the angular velocity is 1.6667 rad/s counterclockwise.

Exam traps

The material point at I has zero velocity, but not generally zero acceleration, and I moves. Exception: fixed-axis rotation, where I is fixed and fixed-axis formulas apply about that axis.
  • Treating a moving I as fixed in general plane motion. I generally moves, so at the next instant it is a different point. Exception: fixed-axis rotation, where I is a fixed point on the axis.
  • Forgetting direction: v is perpendicular to the line from I to the point; a sign slip reverses the sense of rotation.
  • In rolling without slipping, taking the wheel centre as I. The contact point with the ground is I at that instant.
  • For pure translation, trying to use a finite I. I is at infinity and ω = 0.

6.5 Kinetics of rigid bodies: forces and accelerations

A rigid body in plane motion obeys two equations at once: the resultant force sets the acceleration of its mass centre, and the resultant moment about the mass centre sets its angular acceleration. These are Newton's second law and its rotational partner, the Euler equation. They turn a drawing of forces into numbers for a machine part, a rolling drum, or a vehicle. The method is always the same: draw the free-body diagram, draw the kinetic diagram, write the two equations, and solve. This chapter applies that method to translation, fixed-axis rotation and rolling without slip.

You need: 6.1 for translation and fixed-axis rotation, 6.2 for plane motion, and the mass moment of inertia from Unit 4, Mass Moment of Inertia; Unit 5 gives Newton's second law for particles.

Force, mass centre and angular acceleration

For plane motion, a rigid body has mass m in kg and mass moment of inertia IG in kg m² about an axis through its mass centre G, perpendicular to the plane of motion. The acceleration of G is aG, with components aGx and aGy in m/s². The angular acceleration is α in rad/s². Pick a positive sense for α and keep it.

ΣF<sub>x</sub> = m a<sub>Gx</sub>,   ΣF<sub>y</sub> = m a<sub>Gy</sub>,   ΣM<sub>G</sub> = I<sub>G</sub> α

ΣFx and ΣFy are the sums of external force components in N; ΣMG is the sum of external moments about G in N m; m is mass in kg; IG is the mass moment of inertia about the centroidal axis in kg m²; α is angular acceleration in rad/s².

The force equations say G moves like a particle of mass m under the resultant force. The moment equation says only the resultant moment about G changes the spin. A force whose line of action passes through G has zero moment about G, so it cannot produce α. Internal forces cancel in pairs and do not appear.

For rotation about a fixed axis O, take moments about O: ΣMO = IO α, where IO = IG + m d², d is the distance OG in m. This removes the unknown reaction at O from the moment equation. Use either the G set or the O equation, not both as independent equations unless you are checking.

Free-body diagram and kinetic diagram

The free-body diagram (FBD) shows the body alone with every external force: weight mg, normal reactions, friction, applied loads, support reactions and applied couples. Do not draw m aG or IG α on the FBD. Those belong on the kinetic diagram (KD).

  1. Choose axes x and y, and mark the positive sense of α.
  2. Draw the FBD with all real forces and couples.
  3. Beside it draw the KD: an arrow m aG at G, and a curved arrow IG α for angular acceleration.
  4. Write ΣFx = m aGx, ΣFy = m aGy, ΣMG = IG α.
  5. Add the kinematic constraint, such as aG = r α for rolling without slip.
  6. Solve and check units: N for force, N m for moment, m/s² for aG, rad/s² for α.

D'Alembert's principle gives the same equations in equilibrium form. Add an inertia force −m aG at G and an inertia couple −IG α to the FBD. Then ΣF = 0 and ΣMG = 0 for the body plus inertia terms. This is only a bookkeeping trick; the physics is still Newton's second law.

Translation, fixed-axis rotation and rolling

Translation only: α = 0, and every point has the same acceleration aG. The moment equation gives ΣMG = 0, so the resultant force passes through G when there is no applied couple.

Fixed-axis rotation about O: G moves on a circle of radius d = OG. Its acceleration has a tangential part d α and a normal part d ω², where ω is angular velocity in rad/s. The equation ΣMO = IO α gives α from the moments about O. The force equations then give the reaction at O.

Rolling without slip on a fixed surface: the contact point has zero velocity at that instant. For a wheel or cylinder of radius r, aG = r α and vG = r ω, with matching positive senses. The friction force at contact is static and unknown. It is not μN unless slip is just starting. Friction usually supplies the moment that spins the body, so it appears in both the force and moment equations.

  • Force through G: no moment about G, so it translates the body and friction may cause spin.
  • Force at the rim: has a moment about G and also changes aG.
  • Uniform cylinder rolling under a force at its centre: friction is one third of the applied force; see the worked example.

Worked example

Question. A uniform solid cylinder of mass 10 kg and radius 0.20 m rolls without slipping on a horizontal plane. A horizontal force P = 40 N pulls at the centre, parallel to the plane. Find the acceleration of the centre and the static friction force at the contact.

  1. Data: m = 10 kg, r = 0.20 m, P = 40 N. The centroidal mass moment of inertia is IG = ½ m r² = ½ × 10 × (0.20)² = 0.200 kg m².
  2. Take +x to the right. P acts at G to the right. Static friction f acts to the left at the bottom contact. Normal reaction N and weight mg are vertical and cancel; there is no vertical acceleration.
  3. Force equation: P − f = m a, where a is the acceleration of G to the right in m/s².
  4. Moment about G: only f has a moment. Friction to the left at the bottom gives a clockwise moment f r. Take clockwise α positive. Rolling without slip gives a = α r, so α = a/r.
  5. Moment equation: f r = IG α = IG (a/r). Hence f = IG a / r² = (0.200/0.0400) a = 5.00 a, with a in m/s² and f in N.
  6. Substitute in the force equation: 40 − 5.00a = 10a. Then 40 = 15a, so a = 2.667 m/s². Also α = a/r = 13.333 rad/s².
  7. Friction: f = 5.00 × 2.667 = 13.333 N. Check: P − f = 26.667 N = m a = 10 × 2.667 N; f r = 2.667 N m = IG α = 0.200 × 13.333 N m.

Answer. The centre accelerates at a = 2.667 m/s² to the right, and static friction is f = 13.333 N to the left.

Exam traps

  • Drawing m aG or IG α on the free-body diagram as if they were real loads. They belong on the kinetic diagram; in D'Alembert form they carry a minus sign.
  • Taking moments about G but using IO, or leaving out the moment of friction about G. Use IG for ΣMG and IO only for ΣMO.
  • Setting rolling friction equal to μN. Static friction in rolling without slip is found from the equations; μN is only the limiting value when slip starts.
  • Mismatching signs in aG = r α. If aG is to the right for a wheel rolling right, α is clockwise.
  • Using ΣF = m aG for a point other than G, or ΣM = I α about an accelerating point without the correction term. For general plane motion, write the moment equation about G.

6.6 Angular momentum and angular impulse of rigid bodies

Angular momentum is the rotational version of linear momentum. For a rigid body it combines the mass moment of inertia with the angular velocity. A moment acting for some time gives an angular impulse, and that impulse changes the body's angular momentum. This idea explains how a brake stops a flywheel, how a clutch brings two shafts to the same speed, and why a coasting rotor keeps its spin. It also gives a quick method for problems where forces and times are known but angular accelerations are awkward to find. The same equation works for fixed-axis rotation and for the rotation part of plane motion.

You need 6.5 for M = Iα, Unit 4 for mass moment of inertia, and Unit 5 for impulse and momentum.

Angular momentum of a rigid body

For plane motion, let ω be the angular velocity of the body in rad/s. Let I_G be the mass moment of inertia about an axis through the mass centre G, perpendicular to the plane of motion, in kg·m². The angular momentum about G is H_G = I_G ω. H_G is in kg·m²/s, and its sense follows the right-hand rule: curl the fingers in the direction of rotation, the thumb points along H_G. In a plane problem we often use a signed scalar; counterclockwise positive, clockwise negative.

H_G = I_G ω, H_O = I_O ω

H_G is angular momentum about the mass centre G in kg·m²/s; I_G is the mass moment of inertia about G in kg·m²; ω is angular velocity in rad/s; H_O and I_O refer to a fixed axis through O, perpendicular to the plane.

For a body rotating about a fixed axis through O, perpendicular to the plane, H_O = I_O ω. Here I_O is the mass moment of inertia about that fixed axis. This simple form is special to fixed-axis rotation. In general plane motion, H about a point that is not G also contains a term from the translation of G, so do not replace I_O by I_G blindly.

Why use H? Linear momentum p = m v measures translation; H measures rotation. A heavy flywheel with large I_G stores a large H at modest ω. A small motor rotor has small I_G, so it changes speed quickly. The quantity also tells the direction of spin: reversing ω reverses H.

Angular impulse and angular impulse-momentum

A moment M about an axis acts for a time. Its angular impulse about that axis is J_θ = ∫ M dt. The unit is N·m·s, which is the same as kg·m²/s. For a constant moment, J_θ = M t. Angular impulse is not a force times a distance; it is a moment times a time.

H₂ − H₁ = ∫ M dt

H₂ and H₁ are angular momenta about the chosen axis in kg·m²/s; M is the external moment about that axis in N·m; t is time in s; ∫ M dt is the angular impulse in N·m·s.

Start from M = Iα = I dω/dt and integrate from time t₁ to t₂. The result is I(ω₂ − ω₁) = ∫ M dt, or H₂ − H₁ = ∫ M dt. This is the angular impulse-momentum relation. Some syllabi call it the moment-of-momentum relation. Choose a positive sense first. A brake opposes rotation, so its moment and its angular impulse are negative when the initial spin is positive.

Conservation of angular momentum

If the external moment about an axis is zero, then ∫ M dt = 0 about that axis. The angular impulse-momentum relation gives H₂ = H₁. Angular momentum about that axis is conserved. Internal forces and internal moments cancel in pairs, so they cannot change the total H of the system.

A coasting flywheel keeps H constant if bearing friction is negligible. A skater who pulls the arms in changes I and therefore ω, while H stays constant because the external moment about the vertical axis is small. In a clutch, internal friction changes the H of each plate, but the total H of both plates is conserved about the shaft axis.

Conservation holds about the chosen axis, not automatically about every point. A force whose line passes through the axis gives no moment about that axis, so it can change linear momentum while leaving H unchanged. In eccentric impact, the impact force may not pass through G; its moment about G gives an angular impulse and changes H_G. A full 3D treatment needs the inertia tensor and Euler's equations because H is not generally parallel to ω.

Worked example

Question. A flywheel has mass moment of inertia I = 5.0 kg·m² about its axis and spins at 20 rad/s. A brake applies a constant opposing moment of 10 N·m. Find the time to stop and the angular impulse of the brake.

  1. Take the initial direction of spin as positive. Then ω₁ = 20 rad/s, ω₂ = 0 rad/s, I = 5.0 kg·m², and the brake moment is M = −10 N·m.
  2. Initial angular momentum: H₁ = Iω₁ = 5.0 × 20 = 100 kg·m²/s.
  3. Final angular momentum: H₂ = Iω₂ = 5.0 × 0 = 0 kg·m²/s.
  4. Change in angular momentum: H₂ − H₁ = 0 − 100 = −100 kg·m²/s.
  5. For a constant moment, the angular impulse is M t. So −100 = (−10)t, which gives t = 10 s.
  6. Angular impulse of the brake: ∫M dt = M t = (−10)(10) = −100 N·m·s. Its magnitude is 100 N·m·s, opposite to the spin.

Answer. The flywheel stops in 10 s, and the brake's angular impulse has magnitude 100 N·m·s opposing the spin.

  • Using H = Iω with I taken about a point that is neither the mass centre G nor a point on the fixed rotation axis. In fixed-axis rotation, I about any point on the axis (not only G) is allowed; in general plane motion, H about a point off the axis also needs the r × m v_G term.
  • Losing the sign of M or angular impulse. A brake opposes rotation, so ΔH and ∫M dt are negative if the initial spin is positive.
  • Feeding rpm into Iω. Convert ω to rad/s first: ω = 2πN/60 for N in rpm.
  • Thinking zero external force means H is conserved. H about an axis needs zero external moment about that axis; a force through the axis changes linear momentum but not H about that axis.
  • Confusing angular impulse ∫M dt with moment M. The impulse depends on how long the moment acts.

6.7 Work-energy and momentum methods for rigid bodies

Two bookkeeping methods solve most rigid-body problems without internal forces. Work-energy tracks energy: forces and couples do work, changing kinetic energy — translation of the mass centre plus rotation about it. Impulse-momentum tracks momentum: forces over time change linear momentum, couples over time change angular momentum. Pick by the unknown asked: speed or distance → energy; time, impulse or blow → momentum. Engineers use these for rolling wheels, flywheels, brakes, clutches and stopping distance.

You need the kinetic energy split for plane motion from 6.2, mass moment of inertia from Unit 4, angular momentum from 6.6, and particle work-energy and impulse-momentum from Unit 5.

Kinetic energy and the work-energy principle

A rigid body in plane motion has two kinetic-energy parts: mass-centre translation and spin about the centre. Add both.

T = ½ m v_G² + ½ I_G ω²

T is kinetic energy (J); m is mass (kg); v_G is mass-centre speed (m/s); I_G is mass moment of inertia about the centre (kg·m²); ω is angular speed (rad/s).

A force F through displacement dr does work F·dr, so U = ∫F·dr. A couple M through angle dθ does work M dθ, so U = ∫M dθ. Units: J when F is N, dr is m, M is N·m, θ is rad.

U₁→₂ = T₂ − T₁

U₁→₂ is total work of all forces and couples from position 1 to 2; T₁ and T₂ are kinetic energies (J) at those positions.

For rolling without slip, v_G = ωr, so T = ½ m v_G² (1 + I_G/(m r²)). A uniform disk (I_G = ½ m r²) gives T = ¾ m v_G²; a hoop gives T = m v_G². Same speed, different energy — mass distribution matters.

Potential energy, conservation, and rolling friction

Gravity stores V = m g h, where h is height above a chosen datum. A spring stores V = ½ k x², where k is stiffness (N/m) and x is extension (m).

T₁ + V₁ = T₂ + V₂

T₁, T₂ are kinetic energies (J) and V₁, V₂ potential energies (J) at positions 1 and 2. It holds only when conservative forces (gravity, springs) are the only ones doing work. If friction, a motor or a brake acts, add their work U_nc: T₁ + V₁ + U_nc = T₂ + V₂.

Rolling without slip: the contact point is instantaneously at rest, so static friction does no work. Do not add friction work for a rolling wheel. Friction still converts energy between translation and rotation, which makes the disk spin. If the body slips, kinetic friction does negative work — include it.

The normal reaction does no work: it is perpendicular to the displacement of the contact point, which is instantaneously at rest. Gravity acts through the mass centre G, so it has no moment about G and changes only translation; its work is m g h. Because that work depends only on the vertical drop h, a rolling body reaches the bottom at the same speed whatever the incline angle.

Impulse and momentum for a rigid body

Impulse changes momentum, now in two equations.

m v_G1 + Σ∫F dt = m v_G2

m is mass (kg); v_G1, v_G2 are mass-centre velocities (m/s); Σ∫F dt is linear impulse (N·s).

I_G ω₁ + Σ∫M_G dt = I_G ω₂

I_G is mass moment of inertia about G (kg·m²); ω₁, ω₂ are angular speeds (rad/s); Σ∫M_G dt is angular impulse about G (N·m·s). Take moments about G or a fixed point, never an arbitrary accelerating point.

Choose momentum when time, impulse or a blow is given or asked. Choose work-energy when speed or distance is asked. If both are asked, solve energy first and feed speed into momentum, or the reverse.

Rigid-body impact is taught in only a few universities. For direct central impact, the line of impact passes through both mass centres; linear momentum of the pair is conserved along that line, and coefficient of restitution e relates contact-point velocities along the same line. The rest is particle impact algebra from Unit 5.

Worked example

Question. A uniform disk of mass 8.0 kg and radius 0.25 m rolls without slipping down an incline from rest. The vertical drop of its centre is 1.2 m. Find the speed of its centre and its angular speed at the bottom. Take g = 9.81 m/s².

  1. I_G = ½ m r² = ½ × 8.0 × (0.25)² = 0.25 kg·m².
  2. Rolling gives ω = v_G / r = v_G / 0.25 = 4 v_G rad/s.
  3. T = ½ m v_G² + ½ I_G ω² = ½(8.0)v_G² + ½(0.25)(4 v_G)² = 4.0 v_G² + 2.0 v_G² = 6.0 v_G².
  4. Gravity alone does work; static friction does none. U = m g h = 8.0 × 9.81 × 1.2 = 94.18 J.
  5. 94.18 = 6.0 v_G², so v_G² = 15.696 m²/s².
  6. v_G = √15.696 = 3.96 m/s.
  7. ω = 4 × 3.96 = 15.85 rad/s.

Answer. The centre moves at 3.96 m/s and the disk spins at 15.85 rad/s at the bottom.

Worked example 2

Question. A uniform disk of mass 6.0 kg and radius 0.20 m rests on frictionless ice. A constant horizontal force of 9.0 N is applied tangentially at the top edge for 2.5 s, starting from rest. Find the speed of its centre and its angular speed at the end of 2.5 s.

  1. I_G = ½ m r² = ½ × 6.0 × (0.20)² = 0.12 kg·m².
  2. Linear impulse = F t = 9.0 × 2.5 = 22.5 N·s.
  3. m v_G = 22.5, so v_G = 22.5 / 6.0 = 3.75 m/s.
  4. Moment about G: F r = 9.0 × 0.20 = 1.80 N·m.
  5. Angular impulse = F r t = 1.80 × 2.5 = 4.50 N·m·s.
  6. I_G ω = 4.50, so ω = 4.50 / 0.12 = 37.5 rad/s.

Answer. The centre moves at 3.75 m/s and the disk spins at 37.5 rad/s.

  • Using T = ½ m v_G² and forgetting ½ I_G ω². For the rolling disk above the answer comes out √(3/2) times too big.
  • Adding friction work for a body rolling without slip. Static friction at the contact point does no work; it only shifts energy between translation and rotation.
  • Writing ½ I_G ω² where the energy needs ½ I_C ω² about the instantaneous contact point, or using I_C without first forming it as I_G + m r². For pure rolling, T = ½ I_C ω² with I_C = I_G + m r² is valid and gives the same result.
  • Feeding ω in rev/min into a formula that wants rad/s. Convert first: ω = 2π N / 60.
  • Taking the angular impulse equation about the contact point or an arbitrary accelerating point. It holds about G or a fixed point.
  • Choosing work-energy when the question asks for time. Work-energy has no t in it; use impulse-momentum.

Check yourself

Remember Write the kinetic energy of a rigid body in plane motion and define each symbol. 2 marks

T = ½ m v_G² + ½ I_G ω², where m is mass (kg), v_G is mass-centre speed (m/s), I_G is mass moment of inertia about the centre (kg·m²) and ω is angular speed (rad/s).

Remember State the work-energy equation for a rigid body and give the unit of each term. 2 marks

T₁ + V₁ + U_nc = T₂ + V₂. Every term is in joules; U_nc is the work of non-conservative forces and couples.

Understand Why does static friction at the contact point of a wheel rolling without slip do no work? 3 marks

The contact point is instantaneously at rest, so the displacement of the point where friction acts is zero at that instant. Work is force times displacement of the point of application, so it is zero even though the force is not.

Understand When do you use impulse-momentum instead of work-energy for a rigid body? 3 marks

When the question gives or asks for time, impulse or a blow, or forces vary with time. Use work-energy when speed, distance or height is asked and forces are known along the path.

Apply A uniform hoop of mass 2.0 kg and radius 0.40 m rolls without slipping with centre speed 3.0 m/s. Find its kinetic energy. 4 marks

I_G = m r² = 2.0 × 0.16 = 0.32 kg·m². ω = v/r = 3.0/0.40 = 7.5 rad/s. T = ½(2.0)(3.0)² + ½(0.32)(7.5)² = 9.0 + 9.0 = 18 J.

Apply A flywheel with I_G = 0.50 kg·m² spins at 20 rad/s. A constant braking couple of 2.5 N·m acts. How long does it take to stop? 4 marks

Angular impulse: I_G ω₁ + ΣM_G t = 0, so 0.50 × 20 − 2.5 t = 0, giving t = 10 / 2.5 = 4.0 s.

Analyze Two identical disks start from rest at the same height on identical inclines. One rolls without slip, the other slides on a frictionless surface. Which reaches the bottom first, and why? 5 marks

The sliding disk. It puts all of m g h into translation, so v = √(2gh). The rolling disk splits the same energy into translation and rotation, so v_G = √(4gh/3), which is smaller. Same energy, but the rolling disk must also spin.

Analyze Why must the angular impulse equation be written about the mass centre or a fixed point, not an arbitrary moving point? 5 marks

The form I_G ω₁ + Σ∫M_G dt = I_G ω₂ comes from taking moments about a point where angular momentum is simply Iω. For an accelerating point an extra term involving the point's acceleration and mass-centre velocity appears, so the simple form fails.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Energy methods scale into multibody simulation: robotics code builds a mass matrix and checks a solver by watching energy drift. Regenerative braking in electric cars and metro trains recovers ½ I ω² from the motor rotor into the battery. Flywheel grid storage uses T = ½ I ω² with composite rotors in vacuum.

6.8 Basics of mechanical vibrations

A spring pulls or pushes a mass back toward equilibrium, so the mass overshoots and repeats the same motion. This back-and-forth is mechanical vibration. When the restoring force is proportional to displacement, the motion is simple harmonic motion (SHM). Its natural angular frequency depends only on stiffness k and mass m. The same mathematics describes a car on its suspension, a building in an earthquake, and a quartz watch crystal. This chapter covers free undamped vibration: the mass oscillates with no friction and no outside pushing.

You need: Newton's second law and spring force F = −kx from Unit 5, and the force–acceleration relation from 6.5.

Simple harmonic motion and a spring

Simple harmonic motion is motion in which the acceleration is proportional to displacement from equilibrium and directed opposite to it. The standard displacement equation is:

x = A sin(ω_n t + φ)

x is displacement from equilibrium in m; A is amplitude in m; ω_n is natural angular frequency in rad/s; t is time in s; φ is phase angle in rad.

The subscript n marks the natural frequency: the frequency the system has when left alone. For a mass m on a spring of stiffness k, put the spring force into Newton's second law.

  1. Write the force on the mass: F = −k x. The minus sign means the force points back toward equilibrium.
  2. Use Newton's second law: m a = −k x.
  3. Replace acceleration by the second derivative of displacement: m d²x/dt² = −k x.
  4. Divide by m: d²x/dt² = −(k/m) x.
  5. Compare with the SHM condition a = −ω_n² x. This gives ω_n² = k/m.

ω_n = √(k/m)

ω_n is natural angular frequency in rad/s; k is spring stiffness in N/m; m is mass in kg.

The period T is the time for one full cycle. Frequency f is the number of cycles per second.

T = 2π/ω_n, f = 1/T = ω_n/(2π)

T is period in s; f is frequency in Hz; ω_n is natural angular frequency in rad/s; π ≈ 3.1416.

  • More mass gives lower ω_n and longer period.
  • A stiffer spring gives higher ω_n and shorter period.
  • In linear SHM, amplitude A does not change ω_n or T.
  • For a hanging mass, gravity only shifts the equilibrium position. The oscillation frequency is still √(k/m).

Some syllabi call this free vibration or mechanical vibrations.

Displacement, velocity and acceleration

Differentiate displacement once for velocity and twice for acceleration.

v = dx/dt = Aω_n cos(ω_n t + φ),   a = dv/dt = −Aω_n² sin(ω_n t + φ) = −ω_n² x

v is velocity in m/s; a is acceleration in m/s²; A is amplitude in m; ω_n is natural angular frequency in rad/s; x is displacement in m.

At equilibrium, x = 0. Speed is maximum there, v_max = Aω_n, and acceleration is zero. At the extremes, x = ±A. Speed is zero there, and acceleration has maximum magnitude a_max = Aω_n², pointing toward equilibrium.

The phase φ sets the starting point. Pull down and release from rest: x = A cos(ω_n t) if down is positive. Push through equilibrium at t = 0: x = A sin(ω_n t). Both choices have the same ω_n and T.

Units, conversion and real limits

ω_n is in rad/s. Frequency f is in Hz (cycles per second). They are linked by ω_n = 2πf, so f = 1 Hz corresponds to ω_n = 2π rad/s. Period T is in s. Never quote ω_n as a frequency in Hz.

Convert stiffness before substituting. 1 N/cm = 100 N/m, and 1 N/mm = 1000 N/m. A spring rated 8 N/cm is k = 800 N/m.

Free vibration ignores friction. Real systems have damping, which slowly reduces amplitude. Forced vibration near the natural frequency causes resonance, where a small periodic force can build a large amplitude. Damped and forced vibration are beyond this chapter.

For rotational vibration, mass moment of inertia replaces mass. Use Unit 4 for I, and 6.7 for an energy check.

Worked example

Question. A 2.0 kg mass hangs on a spring of stiffness k = 800 N/m. It is pulled down 0.05 m from equilibrium and released from rest. Find the angular frequency, period, frequency and maximum speed.

  1. List the data: m = 2.0 kg, k = 800 N/m, A = 0.05 m. The release is from rest at the pulled position, so this position is an extreme and A is the amplitude.
  2. Find angular frequency: ω_n = √(k/m) = √(800/2.0) = √400 = 20 rad/s.
  3. Find period: T = 2π/ω_n = 2π/20 = π/10 = 0.314 s to three significant figures.
  4. Find frequency: f = 1/T = 1/0.314 = 3.18 Hz. Equivalently, f = ω_n/(2π) = 20/(2π) = 3.18 Hz.
  5. Maximum speed occurs at equilibrium: v_max = Aω_n = 0.05 × 20 = 1.0 m/s.
  6. The hanging weight only shifts equilibrium downward. It does not change ω_n, T, f or v_max.

Answer. ω_n = 20 rad/s, T = 0.314 s, f = 3.18 Hz and v_max = 1.0 m/s.

Exam traps

  • Mixing ω_n in rad/s with f in Hz. A value of 20 rad/s is about 3.18 Hz, not 20 Hz.
  • Using k in N/cm without converting. 8 N/cm is 800 N/m. If you substitute k = 8, you are out by a factor of 100 in k, so ω_n (and f) comes out 10 times too small.
  • Writing T = 1/ω_n instead of T = 2π/ω_n. The factor 2π is the common lost mark.
  • Thinking a larger amplitude changes the natural frequency of a linear spring-mass system. It does not.
  • Getting the sign of acceleration wrong. In SHM, a = −ω_n² x always points toward equilibrium, opposite to x.

Remember Give the SI units of ω_n, f and T. 2 marks

Understand Why is the natural frequency of a mass on a spring independent of amplitude? 3 marks

The restoring force is F = −kx, proportional to x. Newton's second law gives a = −(k/m)x. Comparing with a = −ω_n²x gives ω_n = √(k/m). Amplitude cancels out, so ω_n does not depend on A.

Apply A 0.50 kg mass is on a spring of stiffness 200 N/m. Find ω_n, T and f. 4 marks

Apply In SHM with amplitude 0.020 m and ω_n = 10 rad/s, find the maximum speed and maximum acceleration magnitude. 3 marks

Analyze A spring-mass system has stiffness k and mass m. If k is made four times larger and m is made two times larger, how do ω_n and T change? 4 marks

New ω_n = √(4k / 2m) = √2 × √(k/m). So ω_n increases by a factor √2 ≈ 1.41. T = 2π/ω_n, so T decreases by a factor √2 ≈ 0.707.

Analyze At which positions in SHM are speed and acceleration maximum? Explain using x. 3 marks

At equilibrium x = 0, speed is maximum (Aω_n) and acceleration is zero. At extremes x = ±A, speed is zero and acceleration magnitude is maximum (Aω_n²), directed toward equilibrium.

The constant weight shifts the equilibrium position downward. About that new equilibrium, the extra force is still −kx, so ω_n = √(k/m) is unchanged.

Takeaways

  1. Any rigid-body motion can be built from translation of a chosen base point plus rotation about that point.
  2. In fixed-axis rotation every point has the same ω and α, but linear speed grows with distance from the axis as v = ωr.
  3. The relative velocity equation v_B = v_A + ω × r_B/A solves planar velocity problems with one vector triangle.
  4. The relative acceleration equation carries an extra centripetal term ω²r, which points from B towards A.
  5. The instantaneous centre turns a general plane-motion velocity problem into a pure-rotation problem about a point with zero velocity.
  6. For plane kinetics use ΣF = m a_G and ΣM_G = I_G α together; forces alone never fix the rotation.
  7. Angular momentum about G is simply I_G ω, so angular impulse changes spin exactly as linear impulse changes linear momentum.
  8. Work-energy is the fast route when you know displacements but not time; impulse-momentum is the fast route when you know time or a collision.
  9. A spring-mass system oscillates with ω_n = √(k/m), fixed by stiffness and mass alone and not by the starting pull.

α = dω/dt, the time rate of change of angular velocity, in rad/s².

The angle θ turned by a rotating body, measured in radians.

H_G = I_G ω about the mass centre for plane motion, in kg·m²/s.

The locus traced by the instantaneous centre as the motion proceeds.

I = I_G + m d², giving I about any axis a distance d from a parallel axis through the mass centre.

T = 2π/ω_n, the time taken for one complete oscillation, in seconds.
Plane motion
Motion in which every point of the body stays in a plane parallel to one fixed plane.
Radius of gyration
k = √(I/m), the distance from the axis at which the whole mass could be placed to give the same I.
Relative acceleration method
Solving acceleration problems with a_B = a_A + α × r_B/A − ω² r_B/A.

Solving velocity problems with v_B = v_A + ω × r_B/A, usually by a vector triangle or polygon.
Rigid body
An ideal body whose points keep fixed distances from one another, so it cannot stretch or bend.
Rotational kinetic energy
½ I_G ω², the energy held in a body's spin about its mass centre.
Simple harmonic motion
Oscillation with acceleration proportional to displacement and directed towards the mean position.

k, the restoring force per unit displacement of a spring, in N/m.
Tangential acceleration
a_t = α r, the part of a point's acceleration along its circular path.

Motion in which every point of the body has the same velocity and acceleration at any instant.
