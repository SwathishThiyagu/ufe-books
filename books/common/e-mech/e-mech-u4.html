4.1 Centre of gravity and centroids of lines and areas

Every body has weight, and that weight can be treated as one force acting at a single point: the centre of gravity. For a thin wire or a flat plate, the same averaging idea gives a purely geometric point, the centroid. Engineers use it to balance shafts, place lifting hooks, check that a crane will not tip, and locate the centroid of a composite beam section. The centroid is a weighted average of positions: weight by length for a line, by area for a surface. This chapter sets up that average and the standard results you should know.

You need Unit 1 for the idea of a force as a vector with a line of action, and first-year integration for the element dA.

Centre of gravity, centre of mass, centroid

Every small piece of a body has a weight ΔW. The total weight W is the sum of those small weights: W = Σ ΔW. In 4.2 this sum becomes an integral. The line of action of the total weight passes through one point, the centre of gravity G. If the gravitational field g (in N/kg) is the same at every point of the body, the total weight acts through the centre of mass, so G and the centre of mass are the same point. Density need not be uniform for this. For a thin wire or a flat plate of uniform density, density cancels out and the point depends only on shape. That geometric point is the centroid. Some syllabi call it the centre of area.

x̄ = Σ x dA / A, ȳ = Σ y dA / A

x̄ and ȳ are the centroid coordinates in mm; x and y are the coordinates of the element dA in mm; dA is the element area in mm²; A is the total area in mm²; Σ means sum over all elements.

Multiply both sides by A: A x̄ = Σ x dA. The quantity A x̄ is the first moment of area about the y-axis, written Qy. Its unit is mm² × mm = mm³. For a line, replace area by length: L x̄ = Σ x dL. This is the first moment of length about the y-axis, unit mm × mm = mm².

A x̄ = Σ x dA = Qy

Qy is the first moment of area about the y-axis in mm³; A is area in mm²; x̄ is the centroid's x-coordinate in mm.

Symmetry saves work. If a figure has one axis of symmetry, the centroid lies on that axis. If it has two axes of symmetry, the centroid is at their intersection. The move from summing elements to integrating them is in 4.2.

Three standard results for lines

Learn three standard results. A straight line of length L has its centroid at the midpoint. A semicircular arc of radius r has its centroid on the axis of symmetry at distance 2r/π from the centre. For r = 50 mm, that is 31.8 mm. A quarter-circular arc of radius r has its centroid at 2r/π from each of the two radii that form its ends.

Worked example: L-shaped wire

Worked example

Question. A uniform wire is bent into an L. A 300 mm length lies along the x-axis from (0, 0) to (300, 0). A 400 mm length stands vertically from (300, 0) to (300, 400). All lengths are in mm. Find the centroid of the wire.

  1. Segment 1 is the horizontal piece: L1 = 300 mm, centroid (x1, y1) = (150, 0) mm.
  2. Segment 2 is the vertical piece: L2 = 400 mm, centroid (x2, y2) = (300, 200) mm.
  3. Total length L = 300 + 400 = 700 mm.
  4. x̄ = (L1x1 + L2x2) / L = (300 × 150 + 400 × 300) / 700 = 165000 / 700 = 235.7 mm.
  5. ȳ = (L1y1 + L2y2) / L = (300 × 0 + 400 × 200) / 700 = 80000 / 700 = 114.3 mm.
  6. The centroid is (235.7, 114.3) mm. It lies outside the wire itself, which is normal for a bent line.

Answer. The centroid of the L-shaped wire is at x̄ = 235.7 mm and ȳ = 114.3 mm.

Exam traps

  • Weighting by the wrong quantity: length for a wire, area for a plate, volume for a solid. The formula changes with the element.
  • Forgetting that a hole carries a negative area or negative volume in the sum, so its moment is subtracted.
  • Using 4r/3π for a semicircular arc. The arc centroid is 2r/π from the centre; 4r/3π belongs to a semicircular area.
  • Mixing units of first moment: area first moment is mm³, line first moment is mm².
  • Assuming the centroid must lie inside the material. An L-shaped wire, a ring and a horse-shoe all have centroids outside the body.

4.2 Centroids by integration and of composite areas

A centroid is the point where a shape would balance. For a rectangle or a circle it sits at a known place. For a shape bounded by an arbitrary curve, the average of all the small elements becomes an integral, and you integrate x dA and y dA over the area. For a shape built from rectangles, circles and triangles, the same average is taken row by row in a table. The two methods are one idea: a weighted average of positions, weighted by area, length or volume.

You need 4.1 for the centroid definition and the first moment of area, and first-year integration of polynomials.

Integration: one strip at a time

A shape bounded by a curve has no single width and height, so you cut it into thin strips. Take a vertical strip of width dx at position x, running from the x-axis up to the curve y = f(x). The strip is a thin rectangle: its area is dA = y dx and it balances at half its height, so its centroid is at (x, y/2).

x̄ = ∫ x dA / ∫ dA = ∫ x y dx / ∫ y dx

x is the horizontal position of the strip in mm; y is the curve height in mm; dA = y dx is the strip area in mm²; the integral runs between the left and right ends of the area.

ȳ = ∫ (y/2) dA / ∫ dA = ∫ (y²/2) dx / ∫ y dx

ȳ is the height of the centroid in mm; y²/2 comes from (height y) × (centroid height y/2).

  1. Sketch the area and mark the x and y axes.
  2. Choose vertical strips when y = f(x) is one curve; choose horizontal strips when x = g(y) is simpler.
  3. Write dA and the strip centroid: (x, y/2) for vertical, (x/2, y) for horizontal.
  4. Integrate numerator and denominator between the limits.
  5. Divide to get x̄ and ȳ; round to three significant figures.

If the area lies between two curves y₁ and y₂, the strip height is y₂ − y₁ and its centroid is at (y₁ + y₂)/2. Double integration is the same idea in two directions: x̄ = ∫∫ x dy dx / ∫∫ dy dx; use it only when a single strip would have to be split.

L = ∫ ds = ∫ √(1 + (dy/dx)²) dx

L is the length of a plane curve in mm; ds = √(dx² + dy²) is a small piece of the curve. Then x̄ = ∫ x ds / L and ȳ = ∫ y ds / L.

Composite areas: the same average as a table

A composite area is built from standard pieces — rectangles, triangles, circles — whose centroids you already know from 4.1. The centroid of the whole is the area-weighted average of the piece centroids:

x̄ = Σ Aᵢ x̄ᵢ / Σ Aᵢ, ȳ = Σ Aᵢ ȳᵢ / Σ Aᵢ

Aᵢ is the area of piece i in mm²; x̄ᵢ and ȳᵢ are its centroid coordinates in mm, all measured from the same pair of reference axes.

Make a table with one row per piece: label, A, x̄, ȳ, A x̄, A ȳ. Enter a hole as a negative area; its centroid coordinates stay positive. Add each column, then divide. The total row gives the answer. The reference lines must be the same for every row — write them on the sketch before you start.

Composite lines use length in place of area: x̄ = Σ Lᵢ x̄ᵢ / Σ Lᵢ. A bent wire of a straight part and a semicircular arc is two rows; the straight part's centroid is at its midpoint.

Composite volumes

A composite volume uses volume in place of area. For a solid made of a cylinder, a cone and a block, list each part's volume Vᵢ and centroid x̄ᵢ, ȳᵢ, z̄ᵢ, then:

x̄ = Σ Vᵢ x̄ᵢ / Σ Vᵢ

Vᵢ is the volume of part i in mm³; x̄ᵢ is its centroid coordinate in mm; the sum runs over all parts. Use the same formula for ȳ and z̄.

By integration, a volume is cut into thin discs or slices: dV = A(y) dy for a disc of area A at height y, with centroid at the disc centre. Keep every length in millimetres before multiplying; mm × mm × mm gives mm³, but mixing mm and cm gives a wrong total.

Worked example

Question. A T-section has a web 20 mm wide and 80 mm tall, and a flange 100 mm wide and 20 mm thick on top of the web. Find the centroid. Take y upward from the bottom of the web and x from the left edge of the 100 mm flange.

  1. Take y from the bottom of the web and x from the left edge of the 100 mm flange. The section is symmetric about the vertical centre line, so x̄ = 50 mm by inspection; the flange is 100 mm wide and the web is centred on it.
  2. Web: width 20 mm, height 80 mm. A₁ = 20 × 80 = 1600 mm². Its centroid is at half its height: ȳ₁ = 80/2 = 40 mm.
  3. Flange: width 100 mm, thickness 20 mm, sitting on top of the web. A₂ = 100 × 20 = 2000 mm². Its centroid is 80 mm (web height) + 20/2 mm = 90 mm above the base.
  4. First moments: A₁ȳ₁ = 1600 × 40 = 64000 mm³. A₂ȳ₂ = 2000 × 90 = 180000 mm³. Sum = 244000 mm³.
  5. Total area: A = 1600 + 2000 = 3600 mm².
  6. ȳ = ΣAȳ / ΣA = 244000 / 3600 = 67.777... mm = 67.8 mm above the base.

Answer. The centroid is 67.8 mm above the bottom of the web, on the vertical centre line (x̄ = 50 mm from the left edge of the flange).

Exam traps

  • Taking the centroid height of a rectangle as its full height instead of half of it.
  • Measuring x̄ and ȳ from different reference lines for different rows of the table.
  • Forgetting that a hole is a negative area but its centroid coordinates are still positive distances from the reference lines.
  • Mixing mm and cm in the same table; convert first, then multiply.
  • Using the composite table for a shape whose parts overlap; split the shape into non-overlapping pieces.

4.3 Theorems of Pappus-Guldinus

A curve or a flat area rotated about an axis in its plane sweeps out a surface or a solid. The Pappus-Guldinus theorems say this sweep is simple: the swept size equals the generating length or area times the distance travelled by its centroid. Engineers use them to get paint or plating area, to estimate material for a turned part, and to find a centroid from a measured volume.

You need the centroid of a line and of an area from 4.1 and 4.2.

The two theorems

Take a plane curve of length L mm and a plane area A mm². Put an axis XX in the same plane. Let x̄ mm be the perpendicular distance from XX to the centroid of the curve or area. The axis must stay outside the shape: it may touch the boundary but must not cut the curve or the area. If it cuts, parts of the sweep land on top of each other and the count is wrong.

S = 2π x̄ L

S is the swept surface area in mm²; L is the length of the generating curve in mm; x̄ is the distance from the axis to the curve centroid in mm; 2π x̄ is the path of the centroid in one full turn.

A full turn moves the centroid along a circle of radius x̄, so its path is 2π x̄. Multiplying this path by the curve length gives the swept surface area.

V = 2π x̄ A

V is the swept volume in mm³; A is the generating plane area in mm²; x̄ is the distance from the axis to the area centroid in mm.

The same path times the area gives the swept volume. Both theorems assume one complete revolution. For a half turn, replace 2π by π; for an angle θ radians, replace 2π by θ.

From centroid to surface or volume

  1. Choose the generating curve for a surface, or the generating area for a volume.
  2. Find its centroid by 4.2 and measure x̄ perpendicular to the axis.
  3. Check that the axis lies in the plane and does not cut the shape.
  4. Apply S = 2π x̄ L or V = 2π x̄ A, keeping all lengths in the same unit.

A right circular cone shows the curve form. Its slant line has length l and centroid at its midpoint. If the base radius is r, that midpoint is r/2 from the axis. So the lateral surface is S = 2π(r/2)l = πrl.

A hemispherical shell shows the same form. A semicircular arc of radius R has length πR and centroid 2R/π from its diameter. Rotating it about that diameter gives S = 2π(2R/π)(πR) = 4πR².

The area form gives material volumes. A belt loop on a pulley rim has cross-section area A and centroid at radius x̄ from the pulley axis; its material volume is V = 2π x̄ A. A solid of revolution turned on a lathe has the same volume as the profile area swept about the spindle axis.

From surface or volume to centroid

The theorems also run backward. If a surface area S and its generating curve length L are known, the centroid distance is x̄ = S/(2πL). If a volume V and its generating area A are known, then x̄ = V/(2πA). This is useful when a part is measured or weighed and its centroid is wanted.

For a composite volume, split it into simple solids. Let Vᵢ be the volume of part i and x̄ᵢ its centroid distance from the chosen axis. The whole centroid is the weighted average x̄ = ΣVᵢx̄ᵢ / ΣVᵢ. Holes are treated as negative volumes, just as in composite areas.

  • Use a measured S with x̄ = S/(2πL) only when the sweep is one full turn.
  • Use a measured V with x̄ = V/(2πA) only when the axis does not cut the generating area.
  • Keep S in mm² and V in mm³; mixing them with cm or m changes x̄ by a factor of 10 or 1000.

Worked example

Question. A ring of circular cross-section is made by revolving a circle of radius 50 mm whose centre is 200 mm from the axis. Find the surface area and the volume.

  1. The generating curve is the circle perimeter. L = 2πr = 2π × 50 = 314.16 mm. Its centroid is the circle centre, so x̄ = 200 mm. The axis does not cut the circle because 200 mm > 50 mm.
  2. Surface area: S = 2π x̄ L = 2π × 200 × 314.16 = 394784.18 mm². In square metres this is 0.394784 m².
  3. The generating area is the disk. A = πr² = π × 50² = 7853.98 mm². Its centroid is again x̄ = 200 mm from the axis.
  4. Volume: V = 2π x̄ A = 2π × 200 × 7853.98 = 9869604.40 mm³. In powers of ten this is 9.869604 × 10⁶ mm³.

Answer. Surface area = 394784.18 mm² and volume = 9869604.40 mm³.

Exam traps

  • Using the distance from the axis to the nearest edge instead of to the centroid. For the ring, the edge distances are 150 mm and 250 mm, not 200 mm.
  • Applying the theorem when the axis cuts the area or curve. The swept parts overlap, so 2π x̄ A or 2π x̄ L no longer counts distinct material.
  • Dropping the 2 in 2π. One full revolution is 2π x̄, not π x̄; π x̄ is a half turn.
  • Mixing the two formulas: using curve length L in the volume formula, or area A in the surface formula.
  • For a cone, using l/2 as x̄ instead of r/2. The centroid of the slant line is r/2 from the axis, where r is the base radius.

4.4 Second moment of area and moment of inertia

A beam's bending stiffness depends less on how much material the cross-section has than on how far that material sits from the neutral axis; the second moment of area measures that spread. Take every small piece of the cross-section, multiply its area by the square of its distance from the axis, and add. The result, written I, carries units of mm⁴. It appears wherever bending or deflection is calculated: steel joists, timber beams, a bus chassis, an aircraft wing spar.

You need: 4.1 for the element of area and its distance from an axis, and basic integration for the rectangle derivation.

The idea and the definition

Take a flat area: a beam cross-section, a plate, a hatch cover. Fix an axis in its plane. Cut the area into tiny pieces of area dA, and let y be the perpendicular distance from the axis to a piece. The second moment of area about that axis is the sum of y² dA over all the pieces.

Iₓ = ∫ y² dA

Iₓ is the second moment of area about the x-axis, in mm⁴; dA is the area of an element, in mm²; y is the perpendicular distance of that element from the x-axis, in mm; the integral covers the whole area.

Units come out as mm² × mm² = mm⁴, length to the fourth power. Every answer must carry mm⁴ or m⁴; a bare number is incomplete.

The name needs care. Many books call this the “moment of inertia of an area”. No mass is involved and nothing turns; the quantity is pure geometry, area weighted by squared distance. The name survives because the algebra is identical to the mass moment of inertia, where mass replaces area (see 4.8). Some syllabi say “second moment of area” for exactly this reason. Both names mean the same integral.

Why the square? When a beam bends, a fibre at distance y from the neutral axis stretches in proportion to y and carries stress in proportion to that stretch; the moment it resists is its force times y again. Two factors of y. Material far from the axis therefore does far more work than material near it, which is why steel joists are I-shaped rather than square.

Rectangle by a strip, centroidal and base

Take a rectangle of width b and depth h. The centroidal axis is parallel to the base and passes through the centre, so the strip at height y from that axis runs from y = −h/2 to y = +h/2.

  1. Choose the axis. Here it is the horizontal centroidal axis.
  2. Take a strip parallel to the axis at distance y, of thickness dy. Its area is dA = b dy.
  3. Write the integrand: y² dA = b y² dy.
  4. Integrate between the limits: I = ∫ b y² dy = b[y³/3] from −h/2 to h/2.
  5. Put in the limits: (b/3)(h³/8 + h³/8) = b h³/12.

For the base as axis the strip runs from y = 0 to y = h, and the same steps give I = b h³/3, four times larger. The parallel axis theorem of 4.5 checks this: the area b h sits at distance h/2 from the base, so b h³/12 + b h (h/2)² = b h³/12 + b h³/4 = b h³/3.

Two warnings. The formula b h³/12 belongs to the centroidal axis only; about any other axis, integrate again or use the parallel axis theorem. And h is always the dimension perpendicular to the axis you are taking, not a fixed label for the longer side.

For a circle every diameter passes through the centroid and gives the same value, so any diameter may be named; for other shapes the axis must always be stated. A hollow circle uses the outer D and the inner d in the same bracket, subtracted, because the hole takes away the material nearest the axis.

Scaling. Take a plank 100 mm wide. At 100 mm depth, I = 100 × 100³/12 = 8.33 × 10⁶ mm⁴. At 200 mm depth, I = 100 × 200³/12 = 66.67 × 10⁶ mm⁴. Twice the area, eight times the stiffness. Doubling the width would give only twice. The extra material in a deep beam sits near the outer fibres, where y² is large; extra material added sideways sits near the axis, where it earns little.

That is the design argument for the I-section, the box girder and the hollow tube: keep the material away from the neutral axis. It is also why turning a plank on its edge changes a springy board into a stiff beam. A beam's stiffness in bending comes from I, not from A; two sections of equal area can differ severalfold.

Worked example

Question. A timber beam has a rectangular cross-section 120 mm wide and 200 mm deep. (a) Find the second moment of area about the centroidal axis parallel to the base.   3. (b) About the base: I = b h³/3 = 120 × 8,000,000 / 3 = 320,000,000 mm⁴ = 320 × 10⁶ mm⁴.

Exam traps

4.5 Parallel and perpendicular axis theorems

A second moment measures how far area sits from an axis. Moving the axis away from the centroid must increase it. The parallel axis theorem gives the exact increase: add area times distance squared. The perpendicular axis theorem links two in-plane second moments of a thin flat area to the polar one about a normal axis. Both theorems turn a table of standard shapes into a fast way to size an I-beam, a channel or a bracket without integrating again.

You need 4.2 for locating a composite centroid and 4.4 for the standard Ī values of rectangles and circles.

The parallel axis theorem

The parallel axis theorem shifts a second moment from a centroidal axis to any parallel axis. Write:

I = Ī + A d²

I is the second moment about the chosen axis, in mm⁴; Ī is the second moment about the parallel centroidal axis, in mm⁴; A is the area, in mm²; d is the distance between the two axes, in mm.

The term A d² is positive. So I is always larger than Ī. Area farther from the axis resists bending more, so the centroidal axis gives the smallest second moment among all parallel axes.

The formula uses Ī, the centroidal value. You cannot put a second moment about the base into Ī and then add A d² again. If a table gives I about an edge, first move it to the centroid. For a rectangle b × h, Ī about the horizontal centroidal axis is b h³ / 12. About the base it is b h³ / 3. The difference is A (h/2)² = b h × h²/4 = b h³/4, as the theorem requires.

The perpendicular axis theorem

The perpendicular axis theorem applies to a thin flat area lying in the xy plane. Take the x and y axes in the plane and the z axis normal to it, all through the same point O. Then:

Ix + Iy = J = Iz

Ix and Iy are second moments about the in-plane axes, in mm⁴; J is the polar second moment about O, in mm⁴; Iz is the second moment about the normal axis through O, in mm⁴.

If O is the centroid, all three are centroidal. The theorem holds because each element of area is at distance r from O, with r² = x² + y². Summing area times r² splits into area times x² plus area times y². For a rectangle b × h about its centroid, Ix = b h³/12 and Iy = h b³/12, so J = b h (b² + h²)/12.

Do not use this on a thick plate or a long shaft. There, an element at a distance z from the middle plane adds z² to Ix and to Iy, but nothing to the polar second moment about the normal axis. So Ix + Iy comes out larger than J and the theorem fails.

Composite sections: add own Ī and A d²

Composite sections are built from rectangles: an I-section, a T-section, a channel, an angle, or a rectangle with a hole. The second moment about a chosen centroidal axis is the sum over parts:

I = Σ (Ī_i + A_i d_i²)

Ī_i is each part's own second moment about its own centroidal axis parallel to the chosen axis, in mm⁴; A_i is its area, in mm²; d_i is the distance from the part centroid to the section centroid, in mm.

  1. Draw the section and choose a reference line, usually the base.
  2. Split it into rectangles. Treat a hole as a negative area.
  3. Find ȳ of the whole section by the area table of 4.2.
  4. Draw the required centroidal axis at ȳ.
  5. For each rectangle, write its Ī from 4.4, its A, and its d from the part centroid to the section centroid.
  6. Add Ī + A d² for every part. Subtract for holes.

The final I is about the section's centroidal axis, not about the reference line. If the question asks for an edge axis, use the parallel axis theorem once more from the centroid.

Worked example

Question. A T-section has a web 20 mm wide and 80 mm tall and a flange 100 mm wide and 20 mm thick. The flange sits on top of the web. Find (a) the centroid height above the base and (b) the second moment about the horizontal centroidal axis.

4.6 Polar moment of inertia and radius of gyration

A beam resists bending; a shaft resists twisting. Bending depends on the second moment about an axis, which you met in 4.4. Twisting depends on the second moment about a point — the polar moment of inertia J. Take every element of area, multiply by the square of its distance from a chosen point, and add. That single number decides how stiff a circular shaft is in torsion. The radius of gyration turns the same number into a distance you can picture: the size of a thin ring that would behave the same way.

You need: 4.4 for second moments and 4.5 for the perpendicular axis theorem.

The second moment about a point

Pick a point O in the plane of a flat area A. Take a small element of area dA at a straight-line distance r from O. The polar moment of inertia about O adds r² dA over the whole area:

J = ∫ r² dA

J is the polar moment of area about O, in mm⁴; r is the distance from O to the element dA, in mm; dA is the element area, in mm². The name polar is old: r is measured from a pole (a point), not from an axis line.

Draw x and y axes through O. For any point, r² = x² + y². So J = ∫(x² + y²)dA = ∫x²dA + ∫y²dA = Ix + Iy. Here Ix and Iy are the second moments about the x and y axes, both passing through the same point O, in mm⁴. This is the perpendicular axis theorem of 4.5, read as a definition: the polar moment about a point equals the sum of two rectangular moments about any two perpendicular axes through that point.

The condition is a flat area and the same point. If O is the centroid, J is the centroidal polar moment. If O is any other point, Ix and Iy must both be taken about axes through that other point, not about the centroidal axes. Adding a centroidal Ix to a non-centroidal Iy is the commonest error in this chapter.

Circles, shafts and standard values

For a solid circle of diameter d, take a ring of radius r and width dr. Its area is dA = 2πr dr. Then J = ∫ from 0 to d/2 of r²(2πr dr) = 2π[r⁴/4] = πd⁴/32. For a hollow circle with outside diameter D and inside diameter d, subtract the hole: J = π(D⁴ − d⁴)/32.

ShapePolar second moment about the centroid
Solid circle, diameter dπd⁴/32
Hollow circle, outside D, inside dπ(D⁴ − d⁴)/32
Rectangle b × hbh(b² + h²)/12

By symmetry a circle has Ix = Iy about any pair of perpendicular diameters. Since J = Ix + Iy, each diameter gives I = J/2 = πd⁴/64. That is the value you already use for a circular beam in bending.

A circular shaft under torque twists by an amount that carries J in the denominator: a larger J means a stiffer shaft. The full torsion formula belongs to a strength of materials course. Here the point is that J is the property of the cross-section that resists twist.

Radius of gyration

The radius of gyration is the distance at which the whole area could be concentrated, as a thin ring or line, and give the same second moment. Define k = √(I/A) and kₚ = √(J/A). Then I = A k² and J = A kₚ². Here k and kₚ are lengths in mm, I and J are in mm⁴, and A is in mm². k is the radius of gyration about an axis; kₚ is the polar radius of gyration about a point.

For a rectangle b × h, about the centroidal axis parallel to b: I = bh³/12 and A = bh, so k = √(h²/12) = h/√12 = 0.2887h. About the base the same rectangle gives k = h/√3, larger because the area is further from the base.

For a solid circle of diameter d about a diameter, k = √(I/A) = √((πd⁴/64)/(πd²/4)) = √(d²/16) = d/4. About the centre, kₚ = √(J/A) = √((πd⁴/32)/(πd²/4)) = √(d²/8) = d/√8 = 0.3536d. A hollow circle has larger radii of gyration for the same outside diameter, because its area sits further out.

Worked example

Question. A solid steel shaft has diameter 60 mm. Find its cross-sectional area, the polar moment of area about the centre, the second moment about a diameter, and both radii of gyration.

4.7 Product of inertia; principal axes and principal moments

The product of inertia measures how lopsided a flat area is about a pair of perpendicular axes. For a small element of area dA at coordinates (x, y), the contribution is xy dA; adding over the area gives Ixy = ∫xy dA. Unlike Ix and Iy, Ixy can be positive, negative, or zero. For every flat area there is a pair of principal axes about which Ixy vanishes and the second moments reach their largest and smallest values. Engineers use these axes to find the strong and weak bending directions of an unsymmetrical beam or a rolled section.

You need: 4.4 for Ix and Iy, and 4.5 for shifting a second moment.

Product of inertia and its sign

Take two perpendicular axes x and y in the plane of a flat area. Let dA be a small element at coordinates (x, y), measured in mm. Its product of inertia about the pair is xy dA. The product for the whole area is:

Ixy = ∫ x y dA

Ixy is the product of inertia about the x and y axes, in mm⁴; x and y are signed distances in mm from the two axes to the element dA.

Ix and Iy are always positive, but Ixy carries a sign. Elements in quadrants I and III give positive xy; elements in quadrants II and IV give negative xy. The net sign tells which way the area leans. If either axis is an axis of symmetry, each element has a mirror partner with the opposite x or y, so Ixy = 0. If one axis is a principal axis, Ixy = 0 by definition.

For axes moved parallel to the centroidal pair, the shift is:

Ixy = Īxy + A x̄ ȳ

Īxy is the centroidal product of inertia; A is the area in mm²; x̄ and ȳ are the signed distances in mm from the centroid to the new origin.

For a composite area, use Ixy = Σ(Īxy,i + Ai x̄i ȳi), summed over all parts about the same reference axes. Here Īxy,i is the centroidal product of part i, Ai its area, and x̄i, ȳi its centroid coordinates from those axes. Keep the signs.

Rotation, principal axes, and Mohr's circle

Rotate the axes by θ counterclockwise from x to x′. The three quantities change as:

Ix′ = (Ix + Iy)/2 + ((Ix − Iy)/2) cos 2θ − Ixy sin 2θ ; Iy′ = (Ix + Iy)/2 − ((Ix − Iy)/2) cos 2θ + Ixy sin 2θ ; Ix′y′ = ((Ix − Iy)/2) sin 2θ + Ixy cos 2θ

θ is the angle from the old x-axis to the new x-axis, positive counterclockwise; Ix, Iy and Ixy are about the old axes.

At a principal axis, Ix′y′ = 0. Setting the product formula to zero gives the angle:

tan 2θp = −2Ixy / (Ix − Iy)

θp is the angle from the x-axis to a principal axis; the two roots differ by 90°, so the principal axes are perpendicular.

The principal moments are:

I₁,₂ = (Ix + Iy)/2 ± √(((Ix − Iy)/2)² + Ixy²)

I₁ is the larger principal moment and I₂ the smaller; the square root is the radius R of the Mohr circle.

The sum is constant: I₁ + I₂ = Ix + Iy. Mohr's circle for inertia plots I horizontally and Ixy vertically, with Ixy positive upward. Its centre is C = (Ix + Iy)/2 and its radius is R = √(((Ix − Iy)/2)² + Ixy²). The circle cuts the horizontal axis at I₁ and I₂. The angle on the circle is 2θp, measured counterclockwise from the x-axis point (Ix, Ixy) to the principal point, the same sense as a counterclockwise turn of the axes. A few courses go on to the full three-dimensional inertia tensor; for a flat area, two second moments and one product are enough.

Solving a principal-axes problem

Use this order in an exam.

  1. Sketch the area and mark the centroidal x and y axes.
  2. Get Ix and Iy from 4.4 or a standard table.
  3. Compute Īxy with its sign. If the axes are not centroidal, shift with Ixy = Īxy + A x̄ ȳ.
  4. Compute C = (Ix + Iy)/2 and R = √(((Ix − Iy)/2)² + Ixy²).
  5. Write I₁ = C + R and I₂ = C − R.
  6. Find θp from tan 2θp = −2Ixy/(Ix − Iy). Check the root by putting θ into Ix′; it must equal I₁ or I₂.
  7. Draw the principal axes and label the larger moment. The other principal axis is 90° away.

Worked example

Question. A right-angled triangle has base b = 60 mm along the x-axis and height h = 90 mm along the y-axis. The right angle is at the origin. Find Ix, Iy and Ixy about centroidal axes parallel to the legs. Then find the principal moments and the angle of the principal axes from the x-axis.

  1. Area A = (1/2) × 60 × 90 = 2700 mm². The centroid is at x̄ = 20 mm and ȳ = 30 mm from the legs.
  2. Ix about the centroidal axis parallel to the base: Ix = b h³/36 = 60 × 729000 / 36 = 1,215,000 mm⁴.
  3. Iy about the centroidal axis parallel to the height: Iy = h b³/36 = 90 × 216000 / 36 = 540,000 mm⁴.
  4. Ixy about the centroidal axes: Ixy = −b²h²/72 = −(3600 × 8100)/72 = −405,000 mm⁴. The negative sign comes from the area lying mostly in quadrants II and IV about the centroidal pair.
  5. Centre: C = (Ix + Iy)/2 = (1,215,000 + 540,000)/2 = 877,500 mm⁴. Half difference: (Ix − Iy)/2 = 337,500 mm⁴.
  6. Radius: R = √(337500² + 405000²) = 527,192 mm⁴.
  7. Principal moments: I₁ = C + R = 877,500 + 527,192 = 1,404,692 mm⁴, about 1.405 × 10⁶ mm⁴. I₂ = C − R = 877,500 − 527,192 = 350,308 mm⁴, about 0.350 × 10⁶ mm⁴.
  8. Angle: tan 2θp = −2Ixy/(Ix − Iy) = −2(−405000)/675000 = 1.2. So 2θp = 50.2° and θp = 25.1° from the x-axis. Substitution into Ix′ gives I₁, so the larger principal moment is at 25.1°.

Exam traps

  • Losing the sign of Ixy: a negative product rotates the principal axis the other way from a positive one of the same magnitude.
  • Mixing quantities taken about different axes — for example Ix and Iy about the centroidal pair but Ixy about the legs. All three must be about the same pair. If the question asks for principal centroidal axes, shift Ixy with Ixy = Īxy + A x̄ ȳ first.
  • Reporting 2θp as θp; the formula gives twice the angle. The two values of 2θp differ by 180°, so the two principal directions θp differ by 90°.
  • Assuming every centroidal pair is principal; Ixy is zero only for a symmetry pair or a principal pair.
  • Writing units of Ixy as mm² or kg m²; for an area it is mm⁴, while a mass product of inertia uses kg m².

Check yourself

Remember Define the product of inertia of a plane area about the x and y axes, with its unit. 2 marks

Ixy = ∫xy dA, where x and y are signed coordinates of element dA; unit mm⁴.

Understand Why is Ixy zero when the y-axis is an axis of symmetry? 3 marks

Each element at (x, y) has a mirror partner at (−x, y). The products xy and −xy cancel in pairs, so the integral is zero.

Apply For an area, Ix = 8.0 × 10⁶ mm⁴, Iy = 4.0 × 10⁶ mm⁴, Ixy = 3.0 × 10⁶ mm⁴. Find I₁ and I₂. 4 marks

C = (8 + 4)/2 × 10⁶ = 6.0 × 10⁶ mm⁴. Half difference = 2.0 × 10⁶ mm⁴. R = √(2² + 3²) × 10⁶ = 3.606 × 10⁶ mm⁴. I₁ = 9.606 × 10⁶ mm⁴, I₂ = 2.394 × 10⁶ mm⁴.

Analyze A non-square rectangle has centroidal axes x, y tilted at 30° to its sides. Is Ixy zero? Explain. 4 marks

No. The sides are symmetry axes, so Ixy is zero only when x or y is along a side. A 30° tilted pair gives a nonzero product because the mirror cancellation no longer works about both axes.

Remember Write the formula for the angle of the principal axes. 2 marks

tan 2θp = −2Ixy/(Ix − Iy), where θp is measured from the x-axis.

Understand On Mohr's circle for inertia, why is Ixy plotted positive upward? 3 marks

With Ixy positive upward, the point (Ix′, Ix′y′) moves counterclockwise by 2θ when the axes turn counterclockwise by θ. The angle 2θp is read counterclockwise on the circle in the same sense as the physical rotation. The centre, radius and principal values do not depend on the plotting choice.

4.8 Mass moment of inertia of plates and solids

A body with mass resists being spun up. The resistance is not just the mass; it also depends on how far each bit of mass lies from the axis. Mass moment of inertia turns that idea into one integral, I = ∫ r² dm, with dm in kg, r in m and I in kg·m². It lets you compare a solid disc, a ring and a flywheel, and it appears wherever a machine part must be accelerated in rotation: vehicle wheels, pump shafts, motors, and the blades of a fan.

You need: second moment of area for the element idea, parallel axis theorem for shifting, and radius of gyration for I = m k².

The integral and the radius of gyration

In second moment of area, each area element was multiplied by the square of its distance from an axis. For a real body, replace area by mass. A kilogram near the axis is easy to spin; the same kilogram at the rim of a wheel is much harder. The mass moment of inertia measures that difference.

Take an element of mass dm at perpendicular distance r from the chosen axis. Then I = ∫ r² dm. Here dm is in kg, r is in m, and I is in kg·m². For a uniform body of density ρ in kg/m³, dm = ρ dV, and the total mass is m = ρV. The element volume dV must be in m³.

I = ∫ r² dm = ρ ∫ r² dV

I is the mass moment of inertia about the axis (kg·m²); r is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the element (m); dm is the element mass (kg); ρ is density (kg/m³); dV is the element volume (m³).

The same body has different I about different axes. A centroidal axis passes through the centre of mass. For a parallel axis a distance d away, I = Ī + m d², where Ī is the centroidal value, m is the total mass in kg and d is in m. This is the parallel axis theorem with mass in place of area.

Write I = m k², where k is the radius of gyration in m and m is in kg. Then k = √(I/m). It is the single distance at which the whole mass could be placed to give the same I. It is not necessarily equal to the outer radius of the body.

The edge formula uses the side of length a as the edge axis and b as the perpendicular width. For the prism, the centroidal axis is parallel to side c; permute the letters for the other two directions.

For the cylinder, r is the radius and h is the height. The transverse centroidal axis is perpendicular to the cylinder axis and passes through the centre of mass. For the cone and sphere, r is the base or sphere radius. A thin ring has all its mass at radius r, so its value is simply m r² about the central normal axis.

Work in metres and kilograms before substituting. A radius of 200 mm is 0.200 m, and 200² is not 0.200².

Composite bodies

A composite body is split into standard parts. Holes are parts with negative mass. The total I is the sum of the parts about one chosen axis.

  1. Choose the axis for the whole body and draw it.
  2. For each part, find its mass m_i and the perpendicular distance d_i from the chosen axis to its own centroidal axis parallel to it.
  3. Take the centroidal value Ī_i from the table and shift it: I_i = Ī_i + m_i d_i².
  4. Add all I_i. Subtract the contribution of a hole.
  5. Check units in kg·m² and find k = √(I/m).

A flywheel works this way. The rim has most of its mass far from the axis, so it adds a large m d² and gives a large I for a modest total mass. A solid hub near the axis adds little. That is why a ring of the same mass and radius at the same angular speed can store more rotational energy than a solid disc. How I is used in rotation appears in Unit 6.

Worked example

Question. A solid steel disc has radius 200 mm, thickness 50 mm and density 7850 kg/m³. Find its mass, its mass moment of inertia about the central axis, its mass moment of inertia about a diameter through the centre, and its radius of gyration about the central axis. Use metres and kilograms.

  1. Convert to metres: r = 0.200 m and h = 0.050 m.
  2. Volume V = π r² h = π × (0.200)² × 0.050 = 6.283 × 10⁻³ m³.
  3. Mass m = ρV = 7850 × 6.283 × 10⁻³ = 49.323 kg.
  4. About the central axis, use I = ½ m r² = 0.5 × 49.323 × (0.200)² = 0.986 kg·m².
  5. About a diameter, use I = m(3r² + h²)/12. Here 3r² = 0.1200 m², h² = 0.0025 m², and their sum is 0.1225 m². Then I = 49.323 × 0.1225 / 12 = 6.042 / 12 = 0.504 kg·m².
  6. Radius of gyration about the central axis: k = √(I/m) = √(0.986 / 49.323) = 0.141 m.

Answer. The mass is 49.323 kg, the moment about the central axis is 0.986 kg·m², about a diameter is 0.504 kg·m², and the radius of gyration about the central axis is 0.141 m.

Exam traps

  • Using millimetres for r while the mass is in kilograms. The answer becomes 10⁶ times too large. Convert every length to metres first.
  • Shifting with area A d² from Unit 4 instead of mass m d². The parallel axis rule for mass uses the body's mass.
  • Using ½ m r² for a thin ring. That value belongs to a solid cylinder; a thin ring about its central normal axis is m r².
  • Quoting a formula without naming the axis. A cylinder has ½ m r² about its axis but a different value about a transverse centroidal axis.
  • Putting volume in mm³ with density in kg/m³. The mass comes out a billion times too large. Use m³.

It gives the distance k where the whole mass could be placed to give the same I, since I = m k². It is not generally the actual outer radius, and it is not a physical point on the body.

Takeaways

  1. The centroid is only a weighted average of position: length-weighted for a wire, area-weighted for a lamina, mass-weighted for a solid.
  2. When a boundary is a curve, that average turns into an integral; when the shape is made of standard pieces, it turns into an addition table with holes counted as negative areas.
  3. Pappus-Guldinus converts one centroid calculation into a surface area or a volume, provided the sweep does not cut through the generating curve or area.
  4. The second moment of area is a sum of area × distance², so material far from the axis counts far more than material near it — that is why an I-section beats a rectangle of the same area.
  5. The parallel axis theorem lets you start from a centroidal value that is tabulated and shift it to any other parallel axis; the perpendicular axis theorem links J with I_x and I_y for a thin plate.
  6. The radius of gyration turns a second moment into an equivalent distance, which makes comparisons between sections easy.
  7. For every flat area there is a pair of principal axes at an angle θ_p = ½ tan⁻¹[−2I_xy/(I_x − I_y)] where I_xy vanishes and the second moments are extreme; I_x + I_y stays the same at every angle.
  8. In dynamics the same distance-squared sum with masses gives the mass moment of inertia, and thin plates relate it to the area values through I_z = ρ t J_z.
  9. Sketches, a clear table of elements and correct units (mm³ for first moments, mm⁴ for second moments, kg·m² for mass moments) carry most of the marks in this unit.

Glossary

Axis of symmetry
A line such that the figure is its own mirror image across it; the product of inertia involving such an axis is zero.
Centre of gravity
The point through which the resultant weight of a body acts for every orientation of the body.
Centre of mass
The point where the mass of a body may be treated as concentrated; it coincides with the centre of gravity where g is uniform.
Centroid
The geometric centre of a line, area or volume, found as the position average of its elements weighted by length, area or volume.
Centroidal axis
Any axis that passes through the centroid of the area or body; the second moment about it is the smallest for that direction.
Composite area
A plane figure split into standard pieces such as rectangles, triangles and circles, some of which may be holes.
First moment of area
The quantity ∫x dA or ∫y dA about an axis, measured in mm³, which vanishes about a centroidal axis.
Flywheel
A heavy rotating wheel whose large mass moment of inertia smooths out speed fluctuations in an engine or press.
Lamina
A thin flat plate whose thickness is small enough to be neglected when finding its centroid or second moment.
Mass moment of inertia
The sum ∫r² dm of each mass element times the square of its distance from an axis, in kg·m², the rotational counterpart of mass.
Mohr's circle for moments of inertia
A graphical construction on axes of I and I_xy that gives the second moments about rotated axes and locates the principal axes visually.
Moment of inertia tensor
A 3 × 3 array of second moments and products of inertia that fully describes the rotational inertia of a rigid body about any axis through a point.
Neutral axis
The line in a bent beam cross-section where the bending stress is zero; it passes through the centroid of the section.
Parallel axis theorem
The rule I = I_c + A d² that shifts a second moment from a centroidal axis to a parallel axis a distance d away.
Perpendicular axis theorem
The rule J_z = I_x + I_y for a thin flat area, relating the polar second moment to two perpendicular in-plane second moments.
Polar second moment of area
The sum ∫r² dA about a point, where r is the distance of each element from that point, equal to I_x + I_y for a thin plate.
Principal axes
The perpendicular pair of axes through a point for which the product of inertia of the area is zero.
Principal moments of inertia
The largest and smallest second moments of an area, occurring about the principal axes and always adding up to I_x + I_y.
Product of inertia
The quantity I_xy = ∫xy dA, measuring the lack of symmetry of an area about a pair of axes.
Radius of gyration
The distance k = √(I/A) at which the whole area could be placed and still give the same second moment.
Second moment of area
The sum ∫y² dA of each element of area times the square of its distance from an axis, in mm⁴.
Spandrel
The area between a curve such as a parabola and the coordinate axes, often used as an integration example for centroids.
Theorem of Pappus-Guldinus
A rule giving the surface or volume swept by a curve or area rotated about an external coplanar axis, as length or area times the distance moved by the centroid.
