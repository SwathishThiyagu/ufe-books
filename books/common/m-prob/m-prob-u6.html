6.1 Hypotheses, type I and II errors, significance and p-values

A hypothesis test asks whether sample data are consistent with a stated model. You assume a null hypothesis H0, choose an alternative H1 that carries the claim you want to support, and measure the distance between the data and H0 with a test statistic. Because samples vary, a decision rule can raise a false alarm. The test fixes, before sampling, a small probability α for that false alarm. This logic is used in quality control, A/B tests on apps, clinical trials and checking whether a UPI payment failure rate has changed.

You need Unit 2 (probability) and Unit 5 (sampling distributions and standard error); the test statistics in this unit are built in Chapter 6.2 onward.

Hypotheses, test statistic and rejection region

The null hypothesis H0 is the claim assumed true while the test runs. It usually states no change or no effect, such as H0: μ = 1000 h for the mean battery life μ. The alternative hypothesis H1 is the claim you want evidence for. It can be one-sided with H1: μ < 1000 h or H1: μ > 1000 h, or two-sided with H1: μ ≠ 1000 h. H0 always contains the equality used to compute the null distribution. H1 decides the direction of the evidence.

T = (estimate − null value) / standard error

T is the test statistic; estimate is the sample quantity; null value is the value in H0; standard error is the standard deviation of the estimate.

A test statistic T is a number computed from the sample whose distribution is known when H0 is true. Large |T| means the sample is far from H0. The critical region, also called the rejection region, is the set of T values that lead to rejecting H0. Its boundary is chosen from α. For a mean with known σ, T = (x̄ − μ0)/(σ/√n); x̄ is the sample mean, μ0 the null mean, σ the population standard deviation and n the sample size. Chapters 6.2 to 6.5 give the standard statistics.

Type I error, type II error and power

The level of significance α is the maximum probability of rejecting H0 when H0 is true. It is fixed before the sample is taken. A type I error is rejecting a true H0; its probability is α. A type II error is failing to reject a false H0; its probability is β. Power is 1 − β, the probability of rejecting H0 when H0 is false. A test with high power detects a real departure from H0 more often.

H0 trueType I error, prob αCorrect, prob 1 − α
H0 falseCorrect, prob 1 − βType II error, prob β

Some syllabi call α the size of the test and call the rejection region the critical region. For fixed sample size, lowering α makes a type I error less likely but usually raises β. Taking a larger sample can lower both errors. “Fail to reject H0” is not the same as “H0 is true”; the data may simply be too weak to show a difference.

p-value, decision rule and confidence intervals

The p-value is the smallest α at which the observed statistic would reject H0. It is computed after the sample. For a one-sided test, it is the probability, under H0, of a statistic as extreme as or more extreme than the observed one in the direction of H1. For a two-sided test with a symmetric null distribution, double the one-tailed area. In the battery example, z = −2.1 gives one-tailed p = P(Z ≤ −2.1) = 0.0179.

Decision rule: reject H0 if p ≤ α, or equivalently if T lies in the critical region. Otherwise fail to reject H0. The p-value is not the probability that H0 is true. It is a probability of the data, computed assuming H0. A small p says the data are unlikely under H0, not that H0 has a small probability.

Confidence intervals give the same decision for two-sided tests. A two-sided test of H0: μ = μ0 at level α rejects H0 exactly when μ0 lies outside the 100(1 − α)% confidence interval for μ. Unit 5 gives interval estimation. For one-sided tests the match is with a one-sided bound.

Worked example

Question. A battery maker claims a mean life of 1000 h. Test H0: μ = 1000 h against H1: μ < 1000 h at α = 0.05. A sample gives test statistic z = −2.1. Find the p-value and decide. Use the standard normal table.

  2. The significance level is already fixed: α = 0.05.
  3. The observed test statistic is z = −2.1. It has no units.
  4. The p-value is the lower-tail probability: p = P(Z ≤ −2.1). From the standard normal table, Φ(−2.1) = 0.0179.
  5. Compare p with α: 0.0179 ≤ 0.05.
  6. Decision: reject H0. The sample supports the claim that the mean battery life is less than 1000 h.

Exam traps

  • The p-value is not P(H0 true). It is P(data as extreme or more extreme | H0).
  • α is chosen before the sample. Changing α after seeing p is data snooping.
  • One-sided and two-sided p-values differ. For a symmetric two-sided test, p = 2 × one-tailed area; do not double a one-sided p unless the test is two-sided.
  • Type I and type II errors are not interchangeable: type I rejects a true H0; type II fails to reject a false H0. Power = 1 − β, not α.
  • “Fail to reject H0” does not prove H0. A small sample may have low power and miss a real effect.

6.2 Large sample tests for means and proportions

A large sample turns an average into an approximately normal quantity. That is the whole engine of this chapter: subtract the claimed value from the estimate, divide by the standard error, and compare the result with the normal tables. The same recipe tests a mean, the difference of two means, a proportion, or the difference of two proportions. It is used to check a supplier's claim on bulb life, a hospital's claim on a cure rate, or a firm's claim that two payment channels fail equally often.

You need: the normal distribution (Unit 3), the central limit theorem (Unit 4), standard error and confidence intervals (Unit 5), and the logic of 6.1.

One mean: the z-statistic

A sample mean is a random quantity. If the population mean is μ0 and the population standard deviation is σ, then x̄ has mean μ0 and standard error σ/√n. When n is large the central limit theorem (Unit 4) says x̄ is close to normal, whatever the shape of the population.

z = (x̄ − μ0) ÷ (σ/√n)

x̄ is the sample mean; μ0 is the claimed population mean; σ is the population standard deviation (use the sample value s when n ≥ 30); n is the sample size. Under H0, z is roughly standard normal, so normal tables give the p-value.

Population σ is rarely known. For a large sample you may put the sample standard deviation s = √[Σ(xi − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1)] in its place; the extra error is small once n ≥ 30. That is the large-sample condition. Below 30 use the t-test of 6.3.

Decision rule: reject H0 when |z| exceeds the critical value. A two-sided test puts half of α in each tail. A one-sided test puts all of α in one tail, so its critical value is smaller.

Two means, and the interval link

Take two independent samples with means x̄1, x̄2, sizes n1, n2 and sample standard deviations s1, s2. Under H0: μ1 = μ2 the difference x̄1 − x̄2 has mean 0. Variances of independent quantities add, so the standard error is √(s1²/n1 + s2²/n2).

z = (x̄1 − x̄2) ÷ √(s1²/n1 + s2²/n2)

x̄1, x̄2 are the two sample means; s1², s2² are the two sample variances; n1, n2 are the two sample sizes. Both samples should be large. The test answers questions such as whether two warehouses have the same mean delivery time.

Confidence interval link. A two-sided test at level α rejects H0 exactly when μ0 falls outside the 100(1 − α)% interval x̄ ± zα/2 σ/√n, where zα/2 is the standard normal critical value for the chosen confidence level. For 95%, z0.025 = 1.96. For the bulb case below, the 95% interval is 990 ± 1.96 × 5 = 990 ± 9.8, that is (980.2, 999.8) hours. The value 1000 lies outside it, matching the rejection. Reporting the interval as well as the verdict shows the size of the effect in hours, not just significance.

Proportions, one and two

Count x successes in n trials and set p̂ = x/n. Test H0: p = p0 with the statistic below. The standard error uses p0, not p̂, because the test is carried out as if H0 were true. The normal approximation is adequate when np0 ≥ 5 and n(1 − p0) ≥ 5; some syllabi ask for 10 instead of 5.

z = (p̂ − p0) ÷ √(p0(1 − p0)/n)

p̂ = x/n is the sample proportion; p0 is the claimed proportion; n is the number of trials.

Two proportions. Under H0: p1 = p2 both samples estimate one common proportion, so pool them: p̂ = (x1 + x2)/(n1 + n2). Then z = (p̂1 − p̂2) ÷ √(p̂(1 − p̂)(1/n1 + 1/n2)). The two-proportion z-test needs the counts of successes and failures in both samples to be large enough; a common rule is x1, n1 − x1, x2, n2 − x2 each at least 5 (some syllabi say 10). Example: 40 of 500 Android users abandon a checkout, 30 of 400 iOS users do. p̂1 = 0.080, p̂2 = 0.075, pooled p̂ = 70/900 = 0.0778, standard error = √(0.0778 × 0.9222 × 0.0045) = 0.0180, and z = 0.005/0.0180 ≈ 0.28. No evidence of a difference. For a confidence interval of p1 − p2, use the unpooled standard error √(p̂1(1 − p̂1)/n1 + p̂2(1 − p̂2)/n2), because an interval must not assume H0.

Worked example

Question. A manufacturer claims the mean life of a bulb is 1000 hours. A sample of 100 bulbs gives a mean life of 990 hours. The population standard deviation is known to be 50 hours. Test H0: μ = 1000 h against H1: μ ≠ 1000 h at α = 0.05. Also give the p-value and the 95% confidence interval.

  1. State the hypotheses: H0: μ = 1000 h, H1: μ ≠ 1000 h. Two-sided test, α = 0.05, critical values ±1.96.
  2. Standard error: σ/√n = 50/√100 = 50/10 = 5 h.
  3. Test statistic: z = (x̄ − μ0)/(σ/√n) = (990 − 1000)/5 = −10/5 = −2.00.
  4. Compare: |z| = 2.00 > 1.96, so reject H0.
  5. p-value: P(|Z| > 2.00) = 2 × 0.02275 = 0.0455, which is below 0.05, agreeing with the rejection.
  6. 95% confidence interval: 990 ± 1.96 × 5 = 990 ± 9.8, giving (980.2, 999.8) h. The claimed 1000 h lies outside, again matching.

Answer. Reject H0 at the 5% level: the sample mean of 990 h differs significantly from the claimed 1000 h (z = −2.00, p = 0.0455, 95% CI 980.2 h to 999.8 h).

Exam traps

  • Dividing by s instead of σ/√n — the denominator is always a standard error, so the √n must be there.
  • Using s with a small sample: if n < 30 and σ is unknown, the correct test is the t-test, not z.
  • Mixing the critical values: 1.96 is two-sided 5%, 1.645 is one-sided 5%. Check the alternative before choosing.
  • Putting p̂ into the standard error of a one-proportion test. Under H0 the standard error is built from p0.
  • Comparing two proportions without pooling. Under H0 both samples share one proportion, so the pooled estimate belongs in the standard error.

Analyze A 95% confidence interval for a mean is (12.4, 15.6). A two-sided test at 5% is run with H0: μ = 16 and then with H0: μ = 13. Predict both verdicts without computing z, and explain. 5 marks

Analyze A student tests a mean with n = 25 and unknown σ by using s in the z formula and the value 1.96. Identify the two errors and state the correct procedure. 6 marks

First, n = 25 is below 30, so the large-sample normal approximation is not justified. The correct test uses the t distribution with 24 degrees of freedom, and the critical value is read from the t table, not 1.96.

6.3 Small sample tests: Student's t-test

When the population standard deviation σ is unknown and the sample is small, we replace σ by the sample standard deviation s. That extra estimation makes the standardized mean more variable, so it follows Student's t distribution with n − 1 degrees of freedom. The t-test uses this curve to judge a claim about a mean: packet weights, two drug batches, before-after studies such as a new UPI flow and payment time.

You need the t distribution and confidence intervals from Unit 5, and H₀, critical region and p-value from Chapter 6.1.

Why the reference curve changes

The z-test divides the gap between sample mean and claimed mean by σ/√n. In small studies σ is unknown, so we use s from the same data. The denominator is then an estimate and varies from sample to sample.

With small n, s can come out too small, and dividing by a small number inflates the ratio. To keep the false-alarm rate at α, critical values move outwards. Student's t curve has heavier tails and larger critical values than the normal curve.

t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s/√n), df = n − 1

x̄ is the sample mean; μ₀ is the mean claimed by H₀; s is the sample standard deviation with divisor n − 1; n is the sample size; df is the degrees of freedom of the t curve used for the critical value.

One piece of information is spent on x̄, so only n − 1 deviations can vary freely. As n grows, t approaches the standard normal; beyond about n = 30 the two tables give almost the same critical values, which is why the z-test of Chapter 6.2 is safe for large samples.

Three designs, three formulas

The word t-test covers three designs. Choose the formula from the design, not from the size of the numbers.

One sample. Compare the mean of one sample with a claimed value μ₀. One number per unit, df = n − 1.

Two independent samples. Two separate groups, such as two machines, with no pairing between units. Assume both populations are normal with the same variance. Pool the two sample variances, then compare the two means.

s_p² = ((n₁ − 1)s₁² + (n₂ − 1)s₂²) / (n₁ + n₂ − 2)

s_p² is the pooled variance; s₁² and s₂² are the sample variances of group 1 and group 2; n₁ and n₂ are the sample sizes; n₁ + n₂ − 2 is the total degrees of freedom.

t = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / (s_p √(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)),   df = n₁ + n₂ − 2

x̄₁ and x̄₂ are the two sample means; s_p is the pooled standard deviation, the square root of s_p²; the denominator is the standard error of the difference of means.

Paired data. The same unit is measured twice, before and after. Reduce each pair to one number d, the difference, and run a one-sample t-test on the differences with μ₀ = 0.

t = d̄ / (s_d/√n), df = n − 1

d̄ is the mean of the n differences; s_d is the standard deviation of the differences; n is the number of pairs.

Deciding, and reporting an interval

  1. Write H₀ and H₁ and fix α. One-tailed or two-tailed decides which column of the t table you read.
  2. Compute x̄ and s (d̄ and s_d for paired data).
  3. Compute the standard error: s/√n for one sample, s_p√(1/n₁ + 1/n₂) for two, s_d/√n for paired.
  4. Compute t and its degrees of freedom.
  5. Read t(α, df) for one-tailed, t(α/2, df) for two-tailed.
  6. Reject H₀ when t falls in the tail named by H₁: t > t(α, df) for H₁: μ > μ₀, t < −t(α, df) for H₁: μ < μ₀. Only for two-tailed do you reject when |t| > t(α/2, df).
  7. State the conclusion in the units of the problem.

The same numbers give a confidence interval. Replace 1.96 by the t critical value.

x̄ ± t(α/2, n−1) × s/√n

t(α/2, n−1) is the two-tailed critical value with n − 1 degrees of freedom; the half-width t × s/√n is the margin of error in the units of the data.

  • Observations must be independent. Ten readings in one minute from one machine are not independent.
  • The population should be roughly normal; with large n the central limit theorem rescues you.
  • The pooled two-sample form needs equal population variances. Check with the F-test of Chapter 6.4.
  • When variances clearly differ, Welch's t-test is used; only a few universities teach it in the first course.

Worked example

Question. A random sample of 10 biscuit packets has mean weight 248 g and sample standard deviation 6 g. The label claims 250 g. Test at α = 0.05 whether the true mean is less than 250 g, using t(0.05, 9) = 1.833. Also give a 95% confidence interval, using t(0.025, 9) = 2.262.

  1. H₀: μ = 250 g. H₁: μ < 250 g, left tail. α = 0.05, n = 10, df = 9.
  2. Standard error = 6/√10 = 1.8974 g.
  3. t = (248 − 250)/1.8974 = −1.0541.
  4. Critical value t(0.05, 9) = 1.833; reject when t < −1.833.
  5. −1.0541 > −1.833, so not in the rejection region.
  6. Fail to reject H₀. A shortfall of 2 g is within sampling error for n = 10.
  7. Confidence interval: 248 ± 2.262 × 1.8974 = 248 ± 4.2920, giving (243.71 g, 252.29 g). The claimed 250 g lies inside it, agreeing with the test.

Answer. t = −1.0541 with df = 9, smaller in size than 1.833, so we fail to reject H₀; the 95% confidence interval is (243.71 g, 252.29 g).

Worked example 2

Question. A clinic records the fall in systolic blood pressure (in mmHg) for 6 patients after a drug: 4, 6, 3, 5, 7, 5. Test at α = 0.05 whether the drug lowers blood pressure on average. Use t(0.05, 5) = 2.015.

  1. Each patient gives one difference d, so this is a paired test. H₀: μ_d = 0. H₁: μ_d > 0. df = 5.
  2. d̄ = 30/6 = 5.0000 mmHg.
  3. Squared deviations from d̄: 1 + 1 + 4 + 0 + 4 + 0 = 10.
  4. s_d² = 10/5 = 2.0000, so s_d = 1.4142 mmHg.
  5. Standard error = 1.4142/2.4495 = 0.5774 mmHg.
  6. t = 5.0000/0.5774 = 8.6603.
  7. 8.6603 > 2.015, so reject H₀. The mean fall is significantly greater than zero.

Answer. t = 8.6603 with df = 5, which exceeds 2.015, so we reject H₀: the drug lowers systolic pressure by a mean of 5.0000 mmHg.

Exam traps

  • Degrees of freedom slip: n − 1 for one sample and paired data, but n₁ + n₂ − 2 for pooled two-sample. Writing n₁ + n₂ − 1 is the classic error.
  • Using 1.96 when n is small. The t critical value is larger, so 1.96 rejects too often and inflates the type I error.
  • Losing the sign. For one-tailed compare the signed t with the one-tailed critical value; for two-tailed compare |t| with t(α/2, df). Read direction from the sign of t before writing the conclusion.
  • Treating paired data as two independent samples. Difference the pairs first; ignoring pairing inflates the standard error and can hide a real effect.
  • Confusing pooled variance with a plain average. The pooled value weights each variance by n₁ − 1 and n₂ − 1, so a small group cannot swing it much, but a very small group makes s_p² unreliable.

Analyze Two designs study the same drug: (a) 20 patients measured before and after, (b) 20 patients on drug compared with 20 different patients on placebo. Which gives the smaller standard error of the effect, and why? 5 marks

Design (a). Pairing removes patient-to-patient variation, so the standard error of d̄ depends only on the spread of differences, usually much smaller than the spread between patients. 6.4 F-test for equality of variances

The F-test for equality of variances compares two sample variances. If two independent samples come from normal populations with the same variance, the ratio F = s1²/s2² follows an F distribution. A ratio far from 1 is evidence that the population variances differ. Engineers use it to compare fill-volume spread from two machines, variability of two suppliers, or measurement error from two instruments. This chapter gives the test procedure, the degrees of freedom, and the table reading. It links the F ratio to the ANOVA idea in Chapter 6.6.

You need Unit 5 (the F distribution), Unit 3 (the normal distribution), and Chapter 6.1 for null hypotheses, p-values and decisions.

The variance ratio

The test starts with two independent random samples. Sample 1 has size n1 and sample variance s1². Sample 2 has size n2 and sample variance s2². Each sample variance estimates its population variance, σ1² and σ2². If the populations are normal and σ1² = σ2², the ratio of the sample variances has a known distribution. That distribution is the F distribution from Unit 5.

F = s1² / s2², df1 = n1 − 1, df2 = n2 − 1

s1² and s2² are the sample variances; n1 and n2 are the sample sizes; df1 is the numerator degrees of freedom and df2 is the denominator degrees of freedom.

Why a ratio? A single sample variance can be large or small by chance. The ratio cancels the common population variance. Under H0: σ1² = σ2², F should be near 1. Values far above 1 suggest σ1² > σ2². Values far below 1 suggest σ1² < σ2². The F distribution is skewed right and takes only positive values. Its shape depends on both df1 and df2.

The assumptions are independent random samples and normal populations. The test is sensitive to non-normality: a heavy-tailed population can produce false alarms. Some syllabi call it the variance ratio test.

One-tailed and two-tailed procedures

Fix the alternative before looking at the data. A one-tailed test asks whether one variance is larger. A two-tailed test asks whether the variances differ in either direction. For a one-tailed test with H1: σ1² > σ2², put sample 1 on top. If the data give s2² > s1², the test cannot reject in that direction; you can still compute F = s1²/s2² and get a value below 1.

  1. State H0: σ1² = σ2² and H1: σ1² > σ2², or σ1² < σ2², or σ1² ≠ σ2².
  2. Choose the significance level α.
  3. Compute s1² and s2² with divisor n − 1 for each sample.
  4. For H1: σ1² > σ2², set F = s1²/s2² with df1 = n1 − 1 and df2 = n2 − 1. For H1: σ1² < σ2², swap the labels or use F = s2²/s1².
  5. For H1: σ1² ≠ σ2², put the larger sample variance in the numerator. Then F ≥ 1, df1 belongs to that sample, and df2 belongs to the other sample. Compare with F(α/2, df1, df2).
  6. Reject H0 if F is greater than the critical value. Otherwise fail to reject H0.

For the one-tailed upper test, the critical value is F(α, df1, df2). For the two-tailed test, use α/2 because both tails matter. Tables usually give upper-tail values. That is why the larger variance goes on top in the two-tailed case; the lower tail is handled by the test structure, not by a negative F. With software, use the p-value. Reject when p ≤ α.

Reading the table and reporting

F tables are indexed by df1 across the top and df2 down the side. The order matters: F(0.05, 9, 11) is not F(0.05, 11, 9). Always write the df pair in the order (numerator, denominator). Report the sample sizes, sample variances, F statistic, df pair, critical value or p-value, and decision. A conclusion in words: “At the 5% level, the evidence shows that Machine 1 has a larger variance than Machine 2.” Do not say “accept H0”; say “fail to reject H0”. The F-test does not tell you which mean is larger. It only compares spread. Use it before a two-sample t-test when the equal-variance assumption matters; see Chapter 6.3. For more than two groups, the same F ratio appears in ANOVA; see Chapter 6.6.

Worked example

Question. Two machines fill bottles. Machine 1: n1 = 10, s1 = 4.0 ml. Machine 2: n2 = 12, s2 = 2.0 ml. Test H0: σ1² = σ2² against H1: σ1² > σ2² at α = 0.05. Use F(0.05, 9, 11) = 2.90.

  1. Machine 1 variance: s1² = 4.0² = 16.0 ml². Machine 2 variance: s2² = 2.0² = 4.0 ml².
  2. Degrees of freedom: df1 = n1 − 1 = 10 − 1 = 9. df2 = n2 − 1 = 12 − 1 = 11.
  3. The alternative is σ1² > σ2², so sample 1 stays in the numerator. F = s1²/s2² = 16.0/4.0 = 4.00.
  4. Critical value from the F table: F(0.05, 9, 11) = 2.90.
  5. Compare: 4.00 > 2.90. Reject H0 at the 5% level.
  6. Conclusion: the evidence shows that Machine 1 has a larger fill-volume variance than Machine 2.

Answer. At the 5% level, reject H0: the evidence shows that Machine 1 has a larger fill-volume variance than Machine 2.

Exam traps

  • For a two-tailed test use α/2 in the F table. Using α gives a critical value that is too small and rejects H0 too often.
  • Check the df order: F(0.05, 9, 11) is not F(0.05, 11, 9). The first df is the numerator variance's df.
  • For H1: σ1² > σ2², keep sample 1 in the numerator. Do not automatically put the larger variance on top unless you also swap the hypotheses and df.
  • The F-test assumes normal populations. A skewed sample can give a small p-value from skew, not from unequal variances.
  • Sample variances use divisor n − 1. Using n gives a biased ratio and wrong df.

6.5 Chi-square tests: goodness of fit and independence

Counts are common in real data: how many students pass, how many defects in a batch, how many customers choose each brand. A chi-square test compares such observed counts with expected counts from a model. Goodness of fit checks one claimed distribution. The independence test checks whether two attributes in a table are related. Both use the same statistic: squared differences divided by expected counts, summed. Large values give evidence against the null model. The test is used in quality control, biology, marketing and exam fairness checks.

You need: hypothesis testing ideas from Chapter 6.1 and the chi-square distribution from Unit 5.

Counts and the χ² statistic

Suppose data fall into k categories. Under the null hypothesis H0, each category has an expected frequency E. The observed frequency is O. The chi-square statistic adds the squared differences, scaled by E.

χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E

O is the observed frequency in a category or cell; E is the expected frequency under H0; the sum runs over all k categories or all table cells; df means degrees of freedom.

Squaring removes the sign. Dividing by E standardizes the gap: a count of 5 above expected matters more when E = 10 than when E = 100. If H0 is true, χ² follows a chi-square distribution with df. The test is right-tailed: only large values of χ² reject H0.

Expected counts should be at least 5. If some are smaller, combine adjacent categories. This keeps the chi-square approximation usable.

Goodness of fit

A goodness-of-fit test checks one categorical variable against a claimed probability distribution.

  1. State H0: the probabilities of the k categories are p1, p2, ..., pk. State H1: at least one probability differs.
  2. Let n be the total count. Compute each expected count as E_i = n × p_i.
  3. Check every E_i ≥ 5. Combine categories if needed, and reduce k to the number of categories used.
  4. Compute χ² = Σ (O_i − E_i)² / E_i.
  5. Find df = k − 1 − m. Here k is the number of categories after combining, and m is the number of parameters estimated from the data. If the p_i are fully specified, m = 0.
  6. Reject H0 if χ² is greater than the critical value χ²(α, df), or if the p-value is below α.

Estimating a parameter from the data reduces df by 1 for each estimated parameter, because the model was fitted to those same data. For example, fitting a Poisson model estimates λ, so m = 1.

Independence in a contingency table

An r × c contingency table cross-classifies two attributes: r rows and c columns. The null hypothesis says the row attribute and the column attribute are independent. Some syllabi call this the chi-square test of independence or association of attributes.

E_ij = (R_i × C_j) / N

E_ij is the expected count in row i and column j; R_i is the total of row i; C_j is the total of column j; N is the grand total.

Compute χ² with the same formula, summing over all r × c cells. Use df = (r − 1)(c − 1), not rc − 1. Large χ² values reject independence. Every expected cell count should be at least 5; if not, combine rows or columns.

For a 2 × 2 table, some syllabi mention Yates continuity correction: replace |O − E| by max(0, |O − E| − 0.5) before squaring. Use it only when the question asks for it.

Worked example

Question. A die is rolled 120 times. The counts for faces 1 to 6 are 15, 22, 20, 18, 25 and 20. Test whether the die is fair at α = 0.05 using a chi-square goodness-of-fit test.

  1. H0: the die is fair, so each face has probability 1/6. H1: at least one face probability differs from 1/6.
  2. Total rolls n = 120. Expected count for each face is E = 120 × 1/6 = 20. All expected counts are 20, which is at least 5.
  3. Face 1: O = 15, E = 20, difference = −5, squared = 25, term = 25/20 = 1.25.
  4. Face 2: O = 22, E = 20, difference = 2, squared = 4, term = 4/20 = 0.20.
  5. Face 3: O = 20, E = 20, difference = 0, term = 0.
  6. Face 4: O = 18, E = 20, difference = −2, squared = 4, term = 4/20 = 0.20.
  7. Face 5: O = 25, E = 20, difference = 5, squared = 25, term = 25/20 = 1.25.
  8. Face 6: O = 20, E = 20, difference = 0, term = 0.
  9. χ² = 1.25 + 0.20 + 0 + 0.20 + 1.25 + 0 = 2.90.
  10. df = k − 1 − m = 6 − 1 − 0 = 5. No parameter was estimated from the data.
  11. The 5% critical value is χ²(0.05, 5) = 11.07.
  12. Since 2.90 < 11.07, fail to reject H0.

Answer. χ² = 2.90 with 5 df; the 5% critical value is 11.07; fail to reject fairness of the die at α = 0.05.

Exam traps

  • For independence in an r × c table, df = (r − 1)(c − 1), not rc − 1. A 2 × 3 table has df = (2 − 1)(3 − 1) = 2.
  • Expected counts in a contingency table come from row totals, column totals and the grand total. Do not copy the observed counts into E.
  • If an expected count is below 5, combine categories or rows/columns. Do not use the chi-square test on very small expected counts without correction.
  • Reject H0 only for large χ² values. A small χ² means the observed counts are close to expected, so it is not evidence against H0.
  • Yates continuity correction is for a 2 × 2 table only, and only when the question asks for it. It lowers χ² slightly.

6.6 Analysis of variance: one-way and two-way classification

ANOVA tests whether three or more groups have the same mean. Comparing every pair with t-tests would raise the chance of a false alarm. ANOVA splits the total variation of the data into a between-group part and a within-group part, then compares their average sizes with an F ratio. A large F says the group means differ by more than ordinary scatter. One-way ANOVA has one factor; two-way ANOVA has row and column factors. Quality control, agriculture, medicine and any A/B test with several variants use it.

You need the F ratio from Chapter 6.4, the F distribution from Unit 5, and the null hypothesis idea from Chapter 6.1.

One test instead of many t-tests

A factory checks rod length on k machines. With k=5, pairwise comparison needs 10 t-tests. Each test at α=0.05 has a 5% chance of a false alarm even when all machines are equal. Ten tests make at least one false alarm likely. ANOVA replaces the ten tests with one question: are all k means equal?

The one-way model writes each observation as a sum of three parts.

X<sub>ij</sub> = μ + τ<sub>i</sub> + ε<sub>ij</sub>

Xij is observation j in group i; μ is the overall mean; τi is the fixed effect of group i, with Στi = 0; εij is the random error, independent and normal with mean 0 and common variance σ². i = 1, …, k and j = 1, …, ni.

The null hypothesis is H0: τ1 = τ2 = … = τk = 0, so all means are equal. The alternative is that at least one τi is not zero. The test uses the F distribution from Chapter 6.4.

Sums of squares, degrees of freedom, F

Let N = n1 + … + nk be the total number of observations. Write X̄i for the mean of group i and X̄·· for the grand mean of all N observations.

SST = Σ<sub>i</sub> Σ<sub>j</sub> (X<sub>ij</sub> − X̄<sub>··</sub>)²

SST is the total sum of squares: the squared distance of every observation from the grand mean. It measures all variation in the data.

SSB = Σ<sub>i</sub> n<sub>i</sub> (X̄<sub>i</sub> − X̄<sub>··</sub>)²

SSB is the between-group sum of squares. Each group mean is weighted by its sample size ni. Some syllabi call it the treatment sum of squares.

SSE = Σ<sub>i</sub> Σ<sub>j</sub> (X<sub>ij</sub> − X̄<sub>i</sub>)²

SSE is the within-group or error sum of squares: squared distance of each observation from its own group mean.

The three parts obey SST = SSB + SSE. If group means are far apart, SSB is large compared with SSE.

  1. State H0: all group means equal, and H1: at least one differs. Fix α.
  2. Compute the k group means and the grand mean.
  3. Compute SSB and SSE; get SST as their sum or directly.
  4. Degrees of freedom: k − 1 for between, N − k for within, N − 1 for total.
  5. Mean squares: MSB = SSB/(k − 1) and MSE = SSE/(N − k).
  6. F = MSB/MSE. Compare with the critical value Fα(k − 1, N − k). Reject H0 if F is larger.
  • Observations are independent.
  • Errors are normal with mean 0 and the same variance σ² in every group.
  • Group effects add; there is no interaction in one-way ANOVA.
  • The F test is fairly robust to mild non-normality when group sizes are equal.

Two-way classification without replication

Two-way ANOVA studies two factors at once. A row factor has r levels and a column factor has c levels. One observation is taken in each row-column cell, so there are rc observations. Example: r machines and c operators, one rod from each machine-operator pair.

X<sub>ij</sub> = μ + α<sub>i</sub> + β<sub>j</sub> + ε<sub>ij</sub>

Xij is the observation in row i and column j; μ is the overall mean; αi is the row effect; βj is the column effect; εij is the error, independent normal with mean 0 and variance σ².

The model has no interaction term. It assumes the row effect is the same at every column and the column effect is the same at every row. Without repeated observations in each cell, interaction cannot be separated from error.

SS<sub>row</sub> = c Σ<sub>i</sub> (X̄<sub>i·</sub> − X̄<sub>··</sub>)², SS<sub>col</sub> = r Σ<sub>j</sub> (X̄<sub>·j</sub> − X̄<sub>··</sub>)²

SSrow measures row-mean differences; SScol measures column-mean differences; X̄i· is row i mean, X̄·j is column j mean. SSE = SST − SSrow − SScol.

  1. Degrees of freedom: r − 1 for rows, c − 1 for columns, (r − 1)(c − 1) for error, rc − 1 for total.
  2. Mean squares: MSrow = SSrow/(r − 1), MScol = SScol/(c − 1), MSE = SSE/[(r − 1)(c − 1)].
  3. Test rows with F = MSrow/MSE and columns with F = MScol/MSE.
  4. Compare each F with its critical value using the error degrees of freedom.

Interaction needs replication in each cell and is covered only by a few universities. Post-hoc comparisons after a significant F are also beyond this chapter.

Worked example

Question. Three machines make rods. Four rod lengths (mm) per machine are M1: 9, 10, 11, 10; M2: 11, 12, 13, 12; M3: 13, 14, 15, 14. Test at α = 0.05 whether the mean rod lengths are equal. Use one-way ANOVA.

  1. H0: μ1 = μ2 = μ3; H1: at least one mean differs. α = 0.05.
  2. Group means: M1 mean = 40/4 = 10 mm; M2 mean = 48/4 = 12 mm; M3 mean = 56/4 = 14 mm. Grand mean = 144/12 = 12 mm.
  3. SSB = 4[(10−12)² + (12−12)² + (14−12)²] = 4[4 + 0 + 4] = 32 mm².
  4. SSE: M1 deviations −1, 0, 1, 0 give squares 1 + 0 + 1 + 0 = 2; M2 gives 2; M3 gives 2. SSE = 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 mm². SST = SSB + SSE = 38 mm².
  5. df between = k − 1 = 3 − 1 = 2. df within = N − k = 12 − 3 = 9. df total = 11.
  6. MSB = 32/2 = 16 mm². MSE = 6/9 = 0.667 mm². F = 16/0.667 = 24.
  7. The critical value F(0.05, 2, 9) = 4.26. Since 24 > 4.26, reject H0.

Answer. Reject equality of means; the three machines do not have the same mean rod length at the 5% level.

Exam traps

  • Use k − 1 and N − k. Swapping them gives the wrong F and the wrong table look-up.
  • Do not run repeated t-tests for three or more means; the chance of at least one type I error grows with the number of tests.
  • SSB and SSE are sums of squares (mm²); MSB and MSE are mean squares (mm²); F is a ratio with no units.
  • In two-way ANOVA without replication, an apparent interaction cannot be tested; it is mixed into the error.
  • A large F rejects equality of means but does not say which mean differs. That needs a post-hoc test.

Takeaways

  1. A hypothesis test asks whether sample data are too far from what H₀ predicts, with the allowed false-alarm rate fixed in advance as α.
  2. Type I and Type II errors pull in opposite directions: lowering α for a fixed sample size raises β, so the only way to shrink both is a larger sample.
  3. The p-value is evidence against H₀ on a continuous scale; α is the decision line fixed before the data are seen.
  4. For n ≥ 30 the central limit theorem lets you use z even when σ is unknown and replaced by s.
  5. When n is small and σ is unknown, s fluctuates a lot, so the correct reference curve is Student's t with n − 1 degrees of freedom, which has heavier tails than the normal.
  6. The F test compares two variances as a ratio; the ratio is close to 1 when the population variances are equal.
  7. Chi-square tests work on counts, not on measured values, and need every expected count to be at least about 5.
  8. ANOVA compares several means at once by splitting total variation into between-group and within-group parts, then taking an F ratio of the two mean squares.

Apply

Apply A sample of 100 bulbs shows a mean life of 1200 hours with a standard deviation of 100 hours. Test at the 5% level the claim that the population mean life is 1250 hours. 10 marks

H₀: μ = 1250, H₁: μ ≠ 1250 (two-tailed); z = (1200 − 1250)/(100/√100) = −5.0; Critical values ±1.96 at α = 0.05; |z| = 5 > 1.96, so reject H₀; p-value is far below 0.05; the claim is not supported.

Apply In two large cities, 400 of 1000 sampled users in city A and 450 of 1200 in city B use a payment app. Test whether the two proportions differ at the 1% level. 12 marks

H₀: p₁ = p₂ against H₁: p₁ ≠ p₂; Pooled p̂ = (400 + 450)/(1000 + 1200) = 0.386; SE = √(p̂(1 − p̂)(1/n₁ + 1/n₂)); z = (0.40 − 0.375)/SE, compare with ±2.58; Check that the samples are large and independent.

Apply A sample of 10 packets from a filling machine gives a mean weight of 495 g and a sample standard deviation of 8 g. Test at the 5% level whether the average fill is below the stated 500 g. 13 marks

σ unknown and n small, so use Student's t; H₀: μ = 500, H₁: μ < 500 (one-tailed); t = (495 − 500)/(8/√10) = −1.98 with ν = 9; Table value t₀.₀₅, ₉ = 1.833, so reject H₀; State the assumption of a normal population.

Apply Two machines produce rods whose lengths are measured on 10 rods each. Explain, with a worked calculation, how the paired t-test differs from the two-sample t-test and when each is used. 13 marks

Paired design: same item measured twice, so test the differences d; t = d̄/(s_d/√n) with n − 1 degrees of freedom on the differences; Independent samples: pooled variance s_p², degrees of freedom n₁ + n₂ − 2; Why pairing removes item-to-item variation; Choice of design follows how the data were collected, not the arithmetic.

Apply Two samples of sizes 10 and 12 from normal populations give variances 24 and 9. Test at the 5% level whether the population variances are equal. 10 marks

H₀: σ₁² = σ₂², H₁: σ₁² ≠ σ₂²; F = s₁²/s₂² = 24/9 = 2.67 with (9, 11) degrees of freedom; Two-tailed test: compare with F₀.₀₂₅(9, 11) and the reciprocal lower tail; Conclude whether the ratio is significant; Note the F test is sensitive to non-normality.

Apply A die is thrown 120 times and the six faces appear 15, 22, 25, 18, 20 and 20 times. Test at the 5% level whether the die is fair. 12 marks

H₀: the die is fair, so each expected count is 20; χ² = Σ (O − E)²/E = 3.0; ν = 6 − 1 = 5 (no parameter estimated); Table value χ²₀.₀₅, ₅ = 11.07; 3.0 < 11.07, do not reject; Conclude the deviations are consistent with chance.

Apply A survey cross-classifies 200 people by gender and by preference for three schemes. Show how to set up the chi-square test of independence and compute the degrees of freedom. 12 marks

H₀: the two attributes are independent; Expected count = (row total × column total)/grand total; Sum (O − E)²/E over all cells; ν = (r − 1)(c − 1); for 2 × 3 this is 2; Every expected count should be at least 5, else merge categories; Reject independence if χ² exceeds the table value.

Analyze Compare the z-test, Student's t-test, F-test and chi-square test: the question each answers, the assumptions each needs, and how they are connected. 14 marks

z: means or proportions, large sample or known σ, normal reference curve; t: means, σ unknown, small sample, heavier tails than normal; F: ratio of two variances; also the test for equality of several means in ANOVA; For two groups, t² equals the ANOVA F — the same information, different bookkeeping; Chi-square: counts and variances of normal samples; squared standard normal is χ²₁; Choosing wrongly (e.g. t when σ is known) costs power or validity.

Evaluate A supplier claims a mean strength of 50 MPa. Explain step by step how you would set up and carry out the test, how you would choose between a z and a t procedure, and how the consequences of Type I and Type II errors affect the choice of α. 16 marks

Evaluate Why is analysis of variance preferred to running separate t-tests on every pair of means? Explain the error-rate problem and the role of the within-group variance. 12 marks

k groups give k(k − 1)/2 pairwise tests; each at α, the chance of at least one false alarm grows; ANOVA tests all means equal in one test at a fixed α; MSW is the pooled estimate of the common σ² and sets the yardstick; F = MSB/MSW; a large ratio means group means are spread more than chance allows; If only some pairs differ, follow-up multiple-comparison procedures are needed.

Glossary

Acceptance region
The set of test-statistic values for which H₀ is not rejected.
Between-group variation
Variation of group means about the overall mean; it grows when the group means really differ.
Central limit theorem
For large n, the sample mean is approximately normally distributed whatever the shape of the population.
Chi-square distribution
The distribution of a sum of squares of independent standard normal variables; skewed right, with mean equal to its degrees of freedom.
Composite hypothesis
A hypothesis that allows a range of values, such as H₁: μ > 50.
Consistent test
A test whose power tends to 1 as the sample size grows.
Contingency table
A two-way table of counts classifying items by two attributes.
Critical value
The boundary value of the test statistic that separates the acceptance and rejection regions at a given α.
Degrees of freedom
The count of independent deviations available after the sample has been used to estimate parameters.
Expected frequency
The count predicted for a category under H₀, for example npᵢ or (row total × column total)/grand total.
F distribution
The distribution of the ratio of two independent chi-square variables each divided by its degrees of freedom; used for variance ratios.
Factor
The categorical variable whose levels define the groups in an ANOVA.
Multiple comparison
A follow-up procedure that compares individual pairs of means while holding the overall error rate fixed.
Observed frequency
The count actually recorded in a category or cell of a table.
One-tailed test
A test where the critical region lies entirely in one tail, used for directional alternatives.
Pooled variance
A combined estimate of a common variance, s_p² = [(n₁ − 1)s₁² + (n₂ − 1)s₂²]/(n₁ + n₂ − 2).
Simple hypothesis
A hypothesis that fixes the parameter completely, such as H₀: μ = 50.
Standard error
The standard deviation of the sampling distribution of a statistic, such as σ/√n for a sample mean.
Statistical hypothesis
A statement about a population parameter that is either true or false but is tested using sample data.
Student's t distribution
A symmetric bell-shaped distribution with heavier tails than the normal, indexed by degrees of freedom; arises when σ is estimated by s.
Test of significance
A test in which a statistic is compared with its standard error to judge whether a difference could be due to chance.
Two-tailed test
A test where the critical region is split between both tails, used for alternatives of the form μ ≠ μ₀.
Within-group variation
Variation of observations about their own group mean; it measures the background noise.
Yates' correction
A continuity correction applied to the chi-square test in a 2 × 2 table to reduce overestimation of significance.
