5.1 Population, sample and statistic; random sampling

Every survey, every quality check and every A/B test on a shopping app starts with a small set of observed values. That set is a sample. The full collection it comes from is the population. A number computed from the sample, such as the mean, is a statistic. Because the sample is chosen at random, the statistic changes from sample to sample. Its probability distribution is called a sampling distribution. This chapter sets up these words carefully, because estimation in the rest of Unit 5 depends on them.

You need: Unit 1 data summaries and Unit 2 probability basics.

Population, parameter, sample, statistic

A population is the complete set of units under study. Its size is N. The units can be people, UPI transactions, bulbs from a factory, or cricket scores. A sample is a subset of n units selected from the population. N is the population size and n is the sample size; both are counts, so N = 4 and n = 2 are possible small values in a textbook example.

A parameter is a fixed number that describes the population. The population mean is μ, the population variance is σ², and a population proportion is p. These symbols are fixed for a given population, even if we do not know their values.

A statistic is a function of the sample values only. It may use x₁, x₂, …, xₙ and n. It may not use μ, σ², p, or any other unknown parameter. The sample mean is a statistic:

x̄ = (1/n) Σ<sub>i=1</sub><sup>n</sup> x<sub>i</sub>

x̄ (read x-bar) is the sample mean; n is the number of observations; xi is the i-th sample value.

The sample variance S², the sample range, and the largest sample value are also statistics. A statistic is random before the sample is drawn and fixed after it is drawn. Some syllabi call it a sample statistic.

Random sampling and the sampling distribution

Random sampling is a rule for choosing units without favouring any of them. In simple random sampling without replacement, every subset of n distinct units from the N population units has the same chance. There are C(N, n) such subsets, where C(N, n) is the number of ways to choose n units from N. Each subset has probability 1/C(N, n). In simple random sampling with replacement, we draw n times and allow repeats; each ordered sample has probability 1/N^n.

The rule matters because it controls the statistic. Draw a different sample and x̄ changes. Over all repeated samples, x̄ has a probability distribution. That distribution is the sampling distribution of x̄. It lists the possible values of the statistic and their probabilities, or gives their density for a continuous statistic.

For example, take the population 2, 4, 6, 8 with N = 4. Samples of size n = 2 without replacement give six subsets: (2,4), (2,6), (2,8), (4,6), (4,8), (6,8). Each has probability 1/6. Their sample means are 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7. So the sampling distribution puts probability 1/6 on 3, 1/6 on 4, 2/6 on 5, 1/6 on 6, and 1/6 on 7. The mean of this distribution is 5, which equals the population mean μ. The general theory of the sample mean and variance is in 5.2.

A statistic used to estimate a parameter is called an estimator. The particular value from one sample is an estimate. Estimators are judged in 5.4.

Standard error, sampling error and non-sampling error

The standard error of a statistic is the standard deviation of its sampling distribution. It is written SE. For the sample mean, SE(x̄) measures the typical distance between x̄ and its own mean μ. When sampling is with replacement, or the population is infinite, SE(x̄) = σ/√n. When sampling is without replacement from a finite population of size N, multiply by the finite population correction √((N−n)/(N−1)).

SE(x̄) = (σ/√n) × √((N−n)/(N−1))

σ is the population standard deviation; n is the sample size; N is the population size; SE(x̄) is the standard error of the sample mean.

The correction is close to 1 when n is small compared with N. It becomes 0 when n = N, because a census has no sampling error. These formulas use σ, so a statistician must estimate σ in practice; that step belongs to 5.4.

Sampling error is the difference between a statistic and the parameter that comes from looking at only part of the population. It is random and usually shrinks as n grows. Non-sampling error comes from a wrong sampling frame, non-response, a faulty measuring instrument, a data-entry mistake, or a biased question. A larger sample does not remove non-sampling error; it can even repeat the same bias more times. In a UPI transaction study, a missing set of small merchants is non-sampling error, while the gap between the sample mean and μ is sampling error.

Worked example

Question. Daily UPI transaction amounts, in hundreds of rupees, for four customers are 2, 4, 6 and 8. Treat these four values as the population. (a) Find μ and σ². (b) List every simple random sample of size n = 2 without replacement and compute x̄. (c) Form the sampling distribution of x̄ and verify its mean equals μ.

  1. The population size is N = 4. The population mean is μ = (2+4+6+8)/4 = 20/4 = 5 hundred rupees.
  2. The population variance is σ² = [(2−5)²+(4−5)²+(6−5)²+(8−5)²]/4 = (9+1+1+9)/4 = 20/4 = 5 (hundred rupees)². The population standard deviation is σ = √5 ≈ 2.236 hundred rupees.
  3. The number of samples without replacement is C(4,2) = 6.
  4. Sample (2,4): x̄ = (2+4)/2 = 3 hundred rupees.
  5. Sample (2,6): x̄ = (2+6)/2 = 4 hundred rupees.
  6. Sample (2,8): x̄ = (2+8)/2 = 5 hundred rupees.
  7. Sample (4,6): x̄ = (4+6)/2 = 5 hundred rupees.
  8. Sample (4,8): x̄ = (4+8)/2 = 6 hundred rupees.
  9. Sample (6,8): x̄ = (6+8)/2 = 7 hundred rupees.
  10. Each of the six samples has probability 1/6, so P(x̄=3)=1/6, P(x̄=4)=1/6, P(x̄=5)=2/6, P(x̄=6)=1/6, P(x̄=7)=1/6.
  11. The mean of the sampling distribution is (3+4+5+5+6+7)/6 = 30/6 = 5 hundred rupees, which equals μ.

Answer. The sampling distribution is P(x̄=3)=1/6, P(x̄=4)=1/6, P(x̄=5)=1/3, P(x̄=6)=1/6, P(x̄=7)=1/6, and its mean is 5 hundred rupees, equal to μ.

Exam traps

  • Treating μ as a statistic. μ is a fixed population parameter; x̄ is the statistic that changes from sample to sample.
  • Writing a statistic that contains an unknown parameter, such as (x̄−μ)/σ when μ and σ are unknown. A statistic must be computable from sample values alone.
  • Using 1/N^n for simple random sampling without replacement. Without replacement the probability of each subset of size n is 1/C(N,n), not 1/N^n.
  • Calling the sample standard deviation S the standard error. Standard error is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of a statistic.
  • Confusing sampling error with a data-entry mistake. Sampling error comes from observing only part of the population; non-sampling error comes from frames, non-response, measurement or recording.

5.2 Sampling distribution of the mean and variance

A sample mean is not a fixed number; draw a new sample and it changes. Its sampling distribution describes that change, with centre μ and spread σ/√n. The sample variance has its own law, tied to chi-square for normal data. These two distributions turn a sample into an estimate with a known error. Engineers use them in quality control, A/B tests and average load checks, such as the mean time to settle a UPI payment. This chapter gives the formulas and the normal and chi-square results, without re-teaching the central limit theorem.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for random samples and statistics, Unit 3 for the normal distribution, and Unit 4 for the central limit theorem.

The sample mean and its standard error

Suppose X₁, X₂, …, Xₙ are n independent observations from a population with mean μ and variance σ². The sample mean is x̄ = (Σ Xᵢ)/n. It is a random variable: before you draw the sample, each Xᵢ is random, so x̄ is random. Its sampling distribution is the probability distribution of x̄ over repeated samples of size n.

For any random sample with finite σ², E(x̄) = μ and Var(x̄) = σ²/n. The standard error of x̄ is SE(x̄) = σ/√n. It measures the typical distance between x̄ and μ. Quadrupling n halves the standard error. When σ is unknown, replace it by the sample standard deviation s and call s/√n the estimated standard error. Write the word estimated in reports.

By the central limit theorem (see Unit 4), for large n, x̄ is approximately normal: x̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n), even when the population is not normal. If the population is normal, this result is exact for every n. The figure shows μ = 50, σ = 10 and n = 4, 16, 25; the curve narrows as n rises.

x̄ = (Σᵢ Xᵢ)/n

x̄ is the sample mean; Xᵢ is the ith observation; n is the sample size.

E(x̄) = μ, Var(x̄) = σ²/n, SE(x̄) = σ/√n

μ is the population mean; σ is the population standard deviation; SE is the standard error.

The sample variance and its chi-square distribution

The sample variance is S² = Σ(Xᵢ − x̄)²/(n−1). The n deviations Xᵢ − x̄ always add to zero, so only n−1 of them can vary freely. Dividing by n−1 instead of n makes E(S²) = σ² for a random sample. So S² is an unbiased estimator of the population variance (see Chapter 5.4).

If the population is normal, the exact sampling distribution is chi-square: (n−1)S²/σ² ~ χ²ₙ₋₁. Here χ²ₙ₋₁ is the chi-square distribution with n−1 degrees of freedom (df). The df count is the number of independent pieces of information left after x̄ has been used to estimate μ. For a normal population, x̄ and S² are independent. That independence is special to normal samples and is used to build t and F statistics (see Chapter 5.3).

The mean of a χ²ᵥ variable is ν. Put ν = n−1: E[(n−1)S²/σ²] = n−1, so E(S²) = σ². Do not use the chi-square law for small samples from a non-normal population; it needs the normal population.

S² = Σ(Xᵢ − x̄)²/(n−1)

S² is the sample variance; Xᵢ is the ith observation; x̄ is the sample mean; n is the sample size.

(n−1)S²/σ² ~ χ²ₙ₋₁

n is the sample size; S² is the sample variance; σ² is the population variance; χ²ₙ₋₁ is chi-square with n−1 df; the result requires a normal population.

Using the standard error in practice

To standardise x̄, subtract μ and divide by the standard error: Z = (x̄ − μ)/(σ/√n). When x̄ is normal, Z is standard normal, so you can read probabilities from the normal table (Unit 3). Use σ/√n when σ is known. When σ is unknown and the population is normal, use s/√n and the t distribution (see Chapter 5.3).

If you sample without replacement from a finite population of size N, the variance of x̄ is multiplied by the finite population correction (N−n)/(N−1). Then SE(x̄) = (σ/√n)√((N−n)/(N−1)). This is an extension. Most problems in this chapter assume an infinite population or sampling with replacement, so the correction is 1.

For a non-normal population and small n, the normal curve for x̄ may be poor. A common rough rule is n ≥ 30, but no single number is exact. If the population is very skewed, use a larger n.

Z = (x̄ − μ)/(σ/√n)

Z is the standard normal variable; x̄ is the sample mean; μ is the population mean; σ is the population standard deviation; n is the sample size.

SE(x̄) = (σ/√n)√((N−n)/(N−1))

N is the population size; n is the sample size; the square-root factor is the finite population correction.

Worked example

Question. A normal population has mean μ = 50 and standard deviation σ = 10. A sample of n = 25 is drawn. Find P(46 ≤ x̄ ≤ 54).

  1. The standard error is σ/√n = 10/√25 = 10/5 = 2.
  2. Standardise the lower limit: z = (46 − 50)/2 = −4/2 = −2.
  3. Standardise the upper limit: z = (54 − 50)/2 = 4/2 = 2.
  4. From the standard normal table, P(−2 ≤ Z ≤ 2) ≈ 0.954.
  5. So the probability is about 0.954, or 95.4%.

Answer. The probability is about 0.954, or 95.4%.

Exam traps

  • Using s/√n but calling it σ/√n. When σ is unknown, say estimated standard error.
  • Treating x̄ as normal for small n from a non-normal population. The central limit theorem needs large n; the normal result is exact only for a normal population.
  • Using denominator n in S² and then comparing to chi-square with n df. The correct df is n−1 because μ is estimated by x̄.
  • Applying (n−1)S²/σ² ~ χ²ₙ₋₁ to non-normal data with small n. The exact chi-square law needs a normal population.
  • Confusing the population standard deviation σ with the standard error σ/√n. The standard error shrinks as n rises; σ does not.

The n deviations from x̄ sum to zero, so only n−1 are free. Dividing by n−1 makes E(S²) = σ².

5.3 Chi-square, t and F distributions

Three distributions grow out of normal samples: chi-square, t and F. They describe sample variances and ratios of variances, and they let us use a sample mean when the population standard deviation is unknown. Chi-square is a sum of squared standard normals. t is a standard normal divided by a scaled chi-square. F is a ratio of two scaled chi-squares. They are used for means with unknown σ, variance checks, goodness of fit, variance ratios and ANOVA. This chapter defines each one, gives its mean and variance, and shows how the degrees of freedom fix its shape.

You need: the normal distribution (see Unit 3, Normal distribution) and the sampling distributions of the mean and variance from Chapter 5.2.

Chi-square distribution

The chi-square distribution with ν degrees of freedom is written χ²(ν). Take ν independent standard normal variables Z₁, ..., Z_ν. Square each and add: χ²(ν) = Z₁² + ... + Z_ν². The degrees of freedom ν is the number of independent squared normals. The distribution is positive and right-skewed. Its mean is ν and its variance is 2ν. If ν = 10, the mean is 10 and the variance is 20. Chi-square appears when we study sample variance: for a normal population, (n−1)S²/σ² has χ²(n−1) df. Here n is sample size, S² is sample variance and σ² is population variance. It also measures how far observed counts are from expected counts in a goodness-of-fit test. Those tests are in Unit 6.

χ²(ν) = Z₁² + Z₂² + ... + Zν²

Z1, ..., Zν are independent standard normal variables; ν is the degrees of freedom.

t distribution

The t distribution is built from one standard normal and one chi-square. Let Z ~ N(0,1) and V ~ χ²(ν) be independent. Define T = Z / √(V/ν). Then T has a t distribution with ν degrees of freedom, written t(ν). The denominator √(V/ν) is random, so T has heavier tails than the standard normal. The t distribution is symmetric about 0, like the normal. As ν grows, t(ν) gets close to N(0,1); for small ν the tails are noticeably thick. Some syllabi call it Student's t distribution. We use t when the population standard deviation σ is unknown and the sample is small. For a sample of size n from a normal population, (X̄ − μ)/(S/√n) has t(n−1) df. Here X̄ is the sample mean, μ is the population mean, S is the sample standard deviation, and n is the sample size.

T = Z / √(V/ν)

Z ~ N(0,1); V ~ χ²(ν); Z and V are independent; ν is the degrees of freedom.

F distribution

The F distribution compares two chi-square variables. Let U ~ χ²(ν₁) and V ~ χ²(ν₂) be independent. Define F = (U/ν₁) / (V/ν₂). Then F has an F distribution with (ν₁, ν₂) degrees of freedom. ν₁ is the numerator df and ν₂ is the denominator df. F is positive and right-skewed, never symmetric. The order of df matters: F(5,10) is not F(10,5). If F ~ F(ν₁, ν₂), then 1/F ~ F(ν₂, ν₁). We use F for ratios of variances. For two independent normal samples, S₁²/S₂² scaled by the population variances has an F distribution. We also use F in analysis of variance (ANOVA) to compare several group means. Those test procedures are in Unit 6.

F = (U/ν₁) / (V/ν₂)

U ~ χ²(ν₁); V ~ χ²(ν₂); U and V are independent; ν₁ and ν₂ are the numerator and denominator degrees of freedom.

Worked example

Question. Let Z ~ N(0,1) and V ~ χ²(10) be independent, and let T = Z/√(V/10). Find P(T ≤ 1.812). Also let U ~ χ²(5) and W ~ χ²(10) be independent, and let F = (U/5)/(W/10). Find the upper 5% point of F.

  1. By definition, T has a t distribution with ν = 10 df.
  2. From the t table for 10 df, the 95th percentile is 1.812. So P(T ≤ 1.812) = 0.95.
  3. F has an F distribution with numerator df ν₁ = 5 and denominator df ν₂ = 10.
  4. From the F table for (5,10) df, P(F ≤ 3.33) = 0.95.
  5. The upper 5% point leaves area 0.05 in the right tail, so it is F = 3.33.

Answer. P(T ≤ 1.812) = 0.95, and the upper 5% point of F(5,10) is 3.33.

Exam traps

  • Using t when σ is known: if the population standard deviation is known, use the standard normal z, not t.
  • Mixing degrees of freedom: chi-square uses one ν, t uses one ν, F uses a pair (ν₁, ν₂).
  • Reversing numerator and denominator in F: F(5,10) and F(10,5) are different; 1/F swaps the two df.
  • Confusing mean and variance of chi-square: the mean is ν, the variance is 2ν, not ν².
  • Treating F as symmetric: F is positive and skewed, so upper and lower points are not negatives of each other.

The t distribution is behind Welch's t-test, used when two groups have unequal variances. Chi-square appears in model fit checks and in count data. 5.4 Point estimation: unbiased and consistent estimators

A point estimator is one number computed from a sample to guess an unknown population parameter. The sample mean x̄ guesses μ; the sample proportion p̂ guesses p; the sample variance S² guesses σ². Two estimators can both look reasonable but differ in average error and spread. Bias measures the average miss. Variance measures sample-to-sample scatter. Mean squared error combines both. Consistency asks whether the estimator improves as the sample grows. These checks are used before an estimate is trusted: a UPI fraud-rate survey, a hospital audit, or a Chennai power-cut study.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for statistic and random sampling, Chapter 5.2 for sampling distributions, and Unit 2 for expectation and variance.

Estimator, estimate and bias

A parameter is a fixed unknown number of the population, written θ. An estimator T is a statistic: a function of the sample only. Its observed value t is an estimate. T is random because the sample is random; t is one outcome. The bias of T is B(T)=E(T)−θ. E(T) is the expected value over repeated samples of the same size. T is unbiased when B(T)=0, so E(T)=θ. Unbiased does not mean t equals θ in one sample. It means the long-run average of t over many samples equals θ. The sample mean x̄ is unbiased for μ: E(x̄)=μ. The sample proportion p̂ is unbiased for p: E(p̂)=p. The sample variance S²=Σ(Xi−x̄)²/(n−1) is unbiased for σ². The standard deviation s=√S² is not unbiased for σ in normal samples; E(s)<σ. For a biased estimator, the average of t sits away from θ; this is a systematic error, not random scatter.

B(T) = E(T) − θ

B(T) is the bias; E(T) is the expected value of estimator T over repeated samples; θ is the true parameter.

Variance, MSE and efficiency

Bias is one part of error. A second part is variance. For an estimator T, Var(T)=E[(T−E(T))²] measures how much t moves from sample to sample. Mean squared error is MSE(T)=E[(T−θ)²]. It equals variance plus squared bias: MSE(T)=Var(T)+[B(T)]². Derivation: add and subtract E(T) inside the square; the cross term has expectation zero. For two unbiased estimators T1 and T2, both have MSE equal to variance. The one with smaller variance is more efficient. Relative efficiency is Var(T2)/Var(T1) when T1 is compared with T2. If T1 has variance 1.6667 and T2 has variance 5, T1 is more efficient; relative efficiency is 5/1.6667=3. When estimators are biased, compare MSE, not variance alone.

MSE(T) = Var(T) + [B(T)]²

MSE(T) is mean squared error; Var(T) is the variance of T; B(T)=E(T)−θ is the bias.

Consistency

Consistency is a large-sample property. Let Tn be an estimator from a sample of size n. Tn is consistent for θ if Tn → θ in probability as n → ∞. That means for every fixed ε>0, P(|Tn−θ| ≥ ε) → 0. A sufficient condition is bias(Tn) → 0 and Var(Tn) → 0. Chebyshev's inequality gives P(|Tn−θ| ≥ ε) ≤ MSE(Tn)/ε². If MSE → 0, the right side → 0, so Tn is consistent. The condition is sufficient, not necessary. The sample mean is consistent when the population variance is finite: Var(x̄)=σ²/n → 0 and bias is 0. The first observation T2 has variance σ²=5 even when n grows, so it is not consistent. Unbiased alone does not give consistency. Sufficiency, a related property, says the statistic keeps all information about θ; it is a separate idea and not required for consistency. The maximum likelihood estimator is judged by the same checks; see Chapter 5.5.

P(|Tn − θ| ≥ ε) → 0 as n → ∞

Tn is the estimator from n observations; θ is the parameter; ε is any fixed positive number.

Worked example

Question. A population has values 2, 4, 6, 8. Samples of size n = 2 are drawn without replacement. Let T1 = x̄ be the sample mean and T2 = the first observation in the sample. (a) Find μ and σ². (b) Show T1 and T2 are unbiased for μ. (c) Find Var(T1) and Var(T2). (d) Compare their efficiency by MSE. (e) State which estimator is consistent as n grows.

  1. Population mean: μ = (2+4+6+8)/4 = 20/4 = 5.
  2. Population variance: σ² = [(2−5)²+(4−5)²+(6−5)²+(8−5)²]/4 = (9+1+1+9)/4 = 20/4 = 5.
  3. The six unordered samples of size 2 without replacement and their means are: {2,4}→3, {2,6}→4, {2,8}→5, {4,6}→5, {4,8}→6, {6,8}→7.
  4. E(T1) = (3+4+5+5+6+7)/6 = 30/6 = 5. Bias(T1)=5−5=0, so T1 is unbiased.
  5. For T2, the first observation is equally likely to be any of 2,4,6,8. E(T2)=(2+4+6+8)/4=5. Bias(T2)=0, so T2 is unbiased.
  6. Var(T1) = [(3−5)²+(4−5)²+(5−5)²+(5−5)²+(6−5)²+(7−5)²]/6 = (4+1+0+0+1+4)/6 = 10/6 = 1.6667. Check by formula: Var(T1) = (σ²/n)·((N−n)/(N−1)) = (5/2)·(2/3) = 5/3 = 1.6667, with N=4.
  7. Var(T2) = σ² = 5, because one observation has the population distribution.
  8. MSE(T1)=Var(T1)+0²=1.6667. MSE(T2)=5+0²=5. T1 has smaller MSE, so T1 is more efficient. Relative efficiency = 5/1.6667 = 3.
  9. In the usual asymptotic model where n can grow with fixed σ², Var(T1)=σ²/n tends to 0; with bias 0, T1 is consistent. T2 keeps variance 5 however large n is, so T2 is not consistent. For this fixed finite population n cannot exceed 4; the formula shows the n→∞ pattern.

Answer. μ = 5, σ² = 5; E(T1) = E(T2) = 5 so both are unbiased; Var(T1) = 5/3 ≈ 1.6667 and Var(T2) = 5; MSE(T1) ≈ 1.6667 < MSE(T2) = 5, so T1 is more efficient; T1 is consistent as n grows, T2 is not.

Exam traps

  • Unbiased means the average over repeated samples equals θ, not that the estimate equals θ in the observed sample.
  • S² with denominator n−1 is unbiased for σ²; the version with denominator n is biased. Also s=√S² is not unbiased for σ.
  • MSE = Var + bias². Do not compare only variances of biased estimators and call the smaller-variance one better.
  • Consistency is a limit as n→∞. An unbiased estimator can be inconsistent if its variance does not go to 0.
  • In sampling without replacement from a finite population, include the finite population correction (N−n)/(N−1) in Var(x̄).

5.5 Method of maximum likelihood

The maximum likelihood estimate is the parameter value that makes the observed sample most likely. You write the chance of the data as a function of the unknown parameter, then choose the value that maximises it. The method turns estimation into a calculus problem: differentiate, set to zero, solve. It works for Bernoulli, binomial, Poisson, exponential and normal models, and it extends to regression and machine learning. Engineers use it to fit failure rates, click probabilities and noise variances from data. This chapter shows the likelihood, the log-likelihood and the check that the answer is a maximum.

You need: expectation and variance (Unit 2), the standard distributions (Unit 3), derivatives from calculus, and Chapter 5.4 on unbiased and consistent estimators.

Likelihood and log-likelihood

Let x₁, x₂, …, xₙ be observed values from a distribution with unknown parameter θ. The pmf or pdf at one value is f(x; θ): a probability for discrete data, a density for continuous data. The likelihood treats the data as fixed and θ as variable: L(θ) = Π f(xᵢ; θ), where Π means product. In the discrete case use P(X = xᵢ; θ) in place of f(xᵢ; θ). L(θ) is not the probability of θ. It is the chance of seeing this sample, viewed as a function of θ. The maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) θ̂ is the value that maximises L(θ). Products are awkward to differentiate, so use the log-likelihood ℓ(θ) = ln L(θ). Because ln is increasing, L and ℓ peak at the same θ. Differentiate ℓ, set dℓ/dθ = 0, solve, and check d²ℓ/dθ² < 0 at the root or check the boundary.

L(θ) = Πᵢ₌₁ⁿ f(xᵢ; θ),   ℓ(θ) = ln L(θ) = Σᵢ₌₁ⁿ ln f(xᵢ; θ)

θ is the unknown parameter; x₁,…,xₙ are the observed values; f(xᵢ; θ) is the pmf or pdf at xᵢ; Π is the product over i = 1 to n; Σ is the sum over i = 1 to n.

The procedure and invariance

  1. Write L(θ) as a product over all observations.
  2. Take ℓ(θ) = ln L(θ). Drop additive constants that do not depend on θ when you only need the maximiser.
  3. Differentiate ℓ with respect to θ.
  4. Set dℓ/dθ = 0 and solve for θ.
  5. Check d²ℓ/dθ² < 0 at the solution, or compare with the boundary values when θ has limits.

Invariance: if θ̂ is the MLE of θ and g is a function, then g(θ̂) is the MLE of g(θ). For example, if λ̂ is the MLE of a Poisson rate λ, then the MLE of 1/λ is 1/λ̂. This saves work when a question asks for a transformed parameter. A boundary answer can occur: for p in [0, 1], the maximum of L(p) may be at p = 0 or p = 1 if all trials are failures or all are successes. The same steps work for discrete and continuous models.

Common MLEs and bias

Let x̄ = (1/n) Σ xᵢ be the sample mean. The list gives standard one-parameter or two-parameter MLEs.

  • Bernoulli(p): p̂ = x̄, the fraction of successes in 0/1 data.
  • Poisson(λ): λ̂ = x̄.
  • Exponential(rate λ): λ̂ = 1/x̄; the mean is 1/λ.
  • Normal(μ, σ²): μ̂ = x̄ and σ̂² = (1/n) Σ (xᵢ − x̄)². The divisor is n, not n − 1.

The normal variance MLE is biased: E[σ̂²] = σ²(n − 1)/n, so it underestimates σ² on average. An MLE can be biased even when it is consistent. Bias is judged separately; see Chapter 5.4. In large samples the bias becomes small. The unbiased estimator S² = (1/(n−1)) Σ (xᵢ − x̄)² is not the MLE of σ², but it is often preferred in small samples. For the Bernoulli model, p̂ = x̄ is unbiased; for the Poisson model, λ̂ = x̄ is also unbiased.

Worked example

Question. In 10 independent UPI payment attempts on a slow network, 7 succeed. Model the number of successes as Binomial(10, p). Find the maximum likelihood estimate of p. Show the likelihood, log-likelihood, derivative and second-derivative check.

  1. Let X ~ Binomial(n = 10, p). The observed value is x = 7. The likelihood for this observation is L(p) = C(10,7) p⁷ (1−p)³, where C(10,7) = 120 is the binomial coefficient.
  2. The constant 120 does not depend on p. Maximising L(p) is the same as maximising p⁷ (1−p)³.
  3. Log-likelihood: ℓ(p) = ln 120 + 7 ln p + 3 ln(1−p), for 0 < p < 1.
  4. Derivative: dℓ/dp = 7/p − 3/(1−p).
  5. Set dℓ/dp = 0: 7/p = 3/(1−p). Cross multiply: 7(1−p) = 3p, so 7 − 7p = 3p, 7 = 10p, and p̂ = 0.7.
  6. Second derivative: d²ℓ/dp² = −7/p² − 3/(1−p)². At p = 0.7 this is −7/0.49 − 3/0.09 = −14.2857 − 33.3333 = −47.6190, which is negative, so p = 0.7 is a maximum.

Answer. The maximum likelihood estimate is p̂ = 0.7, so the estimated success probability is 0.7 (70%).

Exam traps

  • Confusing likelihood with the probability of θ. L(θ) is the chance of the observed data as a function of θ, not a probability distribution over θ.
  • Dropping a factor that depends on θ. You may drop constants and additive terms in ℓ, but not a term such as p⁷ or (1−p)³.
  • Setting the derivative to zero without checking the second derivative or the boundary. A stationary point can be a minimum, and the maximum can be at p = 0 or p = 1.
  • Using n − 1 in the normal variance MLE. The MLE divides by n; the unbiased estimator divides by n − 1.
  • Assuming every MLE is unbiased. The normal variance MLE is biased downward.

5.6 Interval estimation and confidence intervals

A point estimate gives one number. Interval estimation gives a range of plausible values for an unknown population parameter. The range is random because it is built from a random sample. A 95% confidence interval is made by a rule that traps the true parameter in 95% of repeated samples, in the long run. Engineers use it for mean strength of concrete, average delivery time, proportion of failed UPI payments and variance of a process. Some syllabi call the result an interval estimate.

You need: point estimation, sampling distributions of the mean and variance, the t and chi-square distributions, and the normal distribution from Unit 3.

The rule and the coverage meaning

A point estimate gives one number. An interval estimator gives two random limits, L and U, built from the sample. After you observe the sample, the limits become two numbers l and u. The confidence coefficient is 1−α. Here α is the total tail probability left outside the interval. For a two-sided interval, α/2 goes in each tail. Common values are 95% (α = 0.05) and 99% (α = 0.01).

P(L ≤ θ ≤ U) = 1 − α

θ is the unknown population parameter; L and U are random lower and upper limits; 1−α is the confidence coefficient, such as 0.95.

Coverage is a long-run property of the rule. If you repeat the sampling and interval calculation many times, about 95% of the intervals from a 95% rule contain θ. After one interval is computed, do not say it has 95% probability to contain θ. It either contains θ or it does not; the probability statement belongs to the random limits before the data are seen.

  • A wider interval has higher confidence for the same sample.
  • A larger n makes the interval narrower.
  • A prediction interval for one future observation is wider than a confidence interval for a mean.

Mean with known and unknown σ

For a normal population with known σ, the standard error of x̄ is σ/√n. The interval is:

x̄ ± z1−α/2 × σ/√n

x̄ is the sample mean; σ is the population standard deviation; n is sample size; z1−α/2 is the normal value with area 1−α/2 to its left. For 95%, z = 1.96.

When σ is unknown, replace σ by s and z by t. The interval is:

x̄ ± t1−α/2, n−1 × s/√n

s is the sample standard deviation; n−1 is degrees of freedom; tq,ν is the t value with area q to its left. For 95% and ν = 15, t = 2.131.

This t interval is exact for a normal population and approximate for large n from other populations. The margin of error is E = critical value × standard error. To plan a sample size for a mean, use n = (z σ / E)², rounded up. Using z instead of t when n is small makes the interval too narrow.

Variance and a large-sample proportion

For a normal population, a 100(1−α)% interval for the variance σ² uses the chi-square distribution.

((n−1)s² / χ²<sub>α/2, n−1</sub>, (n−1)s² / χ²<sub>1−α/2, n−1</sub>)

s² is the sample variance; n−1 is degrees of freedom; χ²p,ν cuts off area p to its right. The interval for σ is the square root of these limits.

For a large sample and a proportion p, the point estimate is p̂ = X/n, where X is the number with the trait. The interval is:

p̂ ± z1−α/2 × √(p̂(1−p̂)/n)

p̂ is the sample proportion; n is sample size; the normal approximation needs n p̂ and n(1−p̂) to be at least about 5 to 10.

Use this proportion interval only when the counts are large enough. For small counts, exact or score intervals are better. The margin of error again is critical value × standard error. For planning a proportion sample size, use n = z² p(1−p)/E², with a guessed p; p = 0.5 is the safe worst case.

Worked example

Question. A random sample of n = 16 from a normal population gives x̄ = 52 and s = 8. The population standard deviation σ is unknown. Find a 95% confidence interval for the population mean μ. Use the t table.

  1. The parameter is μ. σ is unknown and n = 16 is small, so use the t interval, not z.
  2. Degrees of freedom ν = n − 1 = 16 − 1 = 15.
  3. For 95% confidence, α = 0.05 and α/2 = 0.025. The left-tail area is 1 − α/2 = 0.975. From the t table, t0.975,15 = 2.131.
  4. Standard error = s/√n = 8/√16 = 8/4 = 2.
  5. Margin of error = t × standard error = 2.131 × 2 = 4.262.
  6. Lower limit = 52 − 4.262 = 47.738. Upper limit = 52 + 4.262 = 56.262.

Answer. The 95% confidence interval for μ is (47.738, 56.262), about (47.74, 56.26).

Exam traps

  • Using z instead of t when σ is unknown and n is small; this makes the interval too narrow.
  • Saying the observed interval has 95% probability to contain μ. Coverage is the long-run fraction of intervals from the rule, not a probability for one fixed interval.
  • Swapping the two chi-square denominators in a variance interval. The lower limit uses the larger chi-square value, so the lower limit stays below the upper limit.
  • Rounding the planned sample size down. Round up, otherwise the margin of error can exceed the target E.
  • Using the normal proportion interval when n p̂ or n(1−p̂) is small; the normal approximation is poor there.

Takeaways

  1. A statistic is a random variable, so it has a distribution; estimation is the study of that distribution.
  2. With replacement sampling, E(x̄) = μ and Var(x̄) = σ²/n, so the standard error falls only as fast as 1/√n.
  3. The central limit theorem makes x̄ approximately normal for large n even when the population is not normal.
  4. S² is unbiased for σ², and (n−1)S²/σ² has a chi-square distribution when the population is normal.
  5. The t distribution replaces σ by S and adds the extra uncertainty of a small sample.
  6. MSE = variance + bias², so a slightly biased estimator with much smaller variance can be the better choice.
  7. The maximum likelihood method turns a product of densities into a sum of logs and usually gives a consistent, efficient estimator.
  8. A 95% confidence interval is a procedure that covers the parameter in 95% of repeated samples, not a 95% chance for this one interval.
  9. Chi-square, t and F are all built from normal samples: squares, ratios, and ratios of averages of squares.

Likelihood for Bernoulli: p^Σx (1−p)^(n−Σx); Log-likelihood differentiation gives p̂ = x̄; Poisson: ℓ = −nλ + Σx log λ, giving λ̂ = x̄; E(p̂) = p and Var(p̂) = p(1−p)/n, so unbiased and consistent; Same for λ̂: mean λ, variance λ/n, consistency follows.

Apply Derive a 100(1 − α)% confidence interval for the mean of a normal population when σ is known, and explain carefully what the confidence level means. 12 marks

Pivot Z = (x̄ − μ)/(σ/√n) ~ N(0,1); Choose z_{α/2} with P(−z ≤ Z ≤ z) = 1 − α; Invert to get x̄ ± z_{α/2} σ/√n; The interval is random; 1 − α is the long-run coverage over repeated samples; Numerical example with n, x̄, σ and α.

Apply A random sample of 16 items from a normal population gives x̄ = 42.5 and s = 4.0. Construct a 95% confidence interval for μ without assuming σ is known, and explain why the t distribution is used. 12 marks

σ unknown, so use the pivot (x̄ − μ)/(S/√n) ~ t(15); t value with 15 degrees of freedom at 2.5% tail; Interval x̄ ± t s/√n, i.e. 42.5 ± 2.131 × 1.0; State the assumption of normality and independent observations; Interpretation as coverage, not as probability for this fixed μ.

Apply Derive a 100(1 − α)% confidence interval for the variance σ² of a normal population using the chi-square distribution, and for the ratio of two variances using the F distribution. 13 marks

Pivot (n−1)S²/σ² ~ χ²(n−1); Choose two chi-square quantiles to split α equally; Invert to get the interval for σ²; take square roots for σ; F case: pivot (S₁²/σ₁²)/(S₂²/σ₂²) ~ F(n₁−1, n₂−1); Intervals are not symmetric, and the F limits are reciprocals of each other.

Apply Obtain the maximum likelihood estimators of μ and σ² for a normal sample, and hence construct the large-sample confidence interval for μ that uses the estimated variance. 12 marks

Log-likelihood −(n/2)log(2πσ²) − Σ(xᵢ − μ)²/(2σ²); Differentiate in μ and σ² to get μ̂ = x̄ and σ̂² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)²/n; Second derivative or information check for a maximum; Substitute σ̂ in the pivot and use the normal approximation for large n; Note the difference between σ̂² and the unbiased S².

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Show that the sample variance S² = Σ(Xᵢ − x̄)²/(n−1) is unbiased for σ², and that (n−1)S²/σ² has a chi-square distribution with n−1 degrees of freedom when the population is normal. 13 marks

Write Σ(Xᵢ − x̄)² = Σ(Xᵢ − μ)² − n(x̄ − μ)²; Take expectations using E[(Xᵢ − μ)²] = σ² and Var(x̄) = σ²/n; Result E(S²) = σ²; the divisor n−1 corrects the downward bias; For normality, (Xᵢ − μ)/σ are independent standard normals and the sum of squares splits into a chi-square plus the independent mean term; Independence of x̄ and S² in normal samples is the key step.

Analyze Compare the method of moments and the method of maximum likelihood. Illustrate with the normal distribution and state the invariance property of maximum likelihood estimates. 12 marks

Method of moments equates sample moments to population moments; MLE maximises the likelihood; for normal it gives μ̂ = x̄ and σ̂² = Σ(Xᵢ − x̄)²/n; The MLE of σ² is biased but consistent; the moment estimator coincides here; Invariance: if θ̂ maximises L, then g(θ̂) maximises the likelihood of g(θ); Trade-off: MLE needs a distributional assumption but is usually efficient.

Evaluate A 95% confidence interval computed from one sample is [3.2, 4.8]. Explain why it is wrong to say the parameter lies in this interval with probability 0.95, and evaluate the effect of sample size and confidence level on the width. 10 marks

Parameter is a fixed number; the endpoints are random; Coverage probability belongs to the procedure, not to the computed interval; Width falls as 1/√n; quadrupling n halves the width; Raising confidence level widens the interval; Trade-off between precision and confidence; also mention the role of assumptions.

Analyze For a random sample from N(μ, σ²) with σ unknown, derive the sampling distribution of x̄ and of (x̄ − μ)/(S/√n), and use the result to obtain both an interval estimate and a test for μ. 14 marks

x̄ ~ N(μ, σ²/n); standardisation gives a standard normal; (n−1)S²/σ² ~ χ²(n−1) and is independent of x̄; Ratio of a standard normal to the root of an independent chi-square over its degrees of freedom is t(n−1); Interval x̄ ± t_{α/2} S/√n; Test statistic t = (x̄ − μ₀)/(S/√n) with n−1 degrees of freedom; reject for large |t|.

Evaluate Two estimators of a parameter are available: one unbiased with large variance, one slightly biased with small variance. Explain how mean squared error settles the choice, and relate the argument to consistency. 10 marks

MSE = Var + bias²; compare the two numbers, not the bias alone; Give a numerical comparison to show the biased one can win; Consistency needs both bias and variance to vanish as n → ∞, so both estimators can be consistent; Finite-sample choice may differ from large-sample choice; Mention that bias can sometimes be traded deliberately, as with n−1 versus n divisors.

Glossary

Asymptotic normality
The property that a standardised estimator approaches a normal distribution as n grows.
Bias
b(θ) = E(T) − θ, the average signed error of an estimator over repeated samples.
Chi-square goodness of fit
A test comparing observed counts with expected counts, statistic Σ(O − E)²/E, using a chi-square reference distribution.
Confidence level
The long-run proportion 1 − α of samples whose interval covers the true parameter.
Coverage probability
P(L ≤ θ ≤ U) computed over the sampling distribution of the interval endpoints.
Critical value
The quantile of a reference distribution beyond which the chosen tail probability α lies.
Degrees of freedom
The number of independent squared deviations entering a statistic; n−1 for a single sample variance.
Efficiency
Of two unbiased estimators, the one with the smaller variance is more efficient.
Estimate
The numerical value the estimator takes on one particular sample.
Estimator
A statistic used to guess an unknown parameter; it is a random variable before the data are seen.
Interval estimator
A rule giving a lower and upper limit from the sample, together with a confidence level.
Invariance property
If θ̂ is the maximum likelihood estimate of θ, then g(θ̂) is the maximum likelihood estimate of g(θ).
Likelihood function
L(θ) = Π f(xᵢ; θ) read as a function of θ with the data held fixed.
Pivot
A quantity combining data and parameter whose distribution is free of unknown parameters.
Pooled variance
A weighted average of two sample variances used when two populations are assumed to have equal variance.
Population variance
σ² = E[(X − μ)²], a fixed unknown parameter of the population distribution.
Random sample
Independent observations, each having the same distribution as the population.
Sample proportion
p̂ = X/n, the fraction of successes in n independent trials, with mean p and variance p(1−p)/n.
Sampling frame
The list of units from which the sample is actually drawn.
Score function
The derivative of the log-likelihood with respect to the parameter; it is zero at the maximum likelihood estimate.
Standard error of the mean
σ/√n when σ is known; S/√n when it is estimated from the sample.
Standard normal variable
Z = (X − μ)/σ, with mean 0 and variance 1.
Sufficient statistic
A statistic that captures all the information in the sample about the parameter.
Tail probability
The area beyond a critical value in one or both tails of a distribution.
