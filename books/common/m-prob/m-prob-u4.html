4.1 Joint distributions of two random variables

Two random variables often travel together. One observation gives a pair (X, Y), a point on the plane. A joint distribution assigns a probability to every region of that plane. For a fair die rolled twice, X can count 1s and Y can count 2s. For a phone, X can be download time and Y upload time. The joint pmf, joint pdf or joint CDF lets you compute P((X, Y) ∈ A), not only P(X ≤ x) or P(Y ≤ y). This is the base for marginals and independence.

You need Unit 2: random variables, pmf, pdf, CDF and expectation.

Joint pmf, joint pdf and support

For two discrete random variables X and Y, the joint pmf is p(x,y)=P(X=x,Y=y). It gives the probability of the single pair (x,y). Two rules hold: p(x,y) ≥ 0 for all pairs, and Σ_x Σ_y p(x,y)=1, where both sums run over all possible values. The support is the set of pairs with p(x,y)>0.

For continuous X and Y, the joint pdf f(x,y) is a density, not a probability. A region A gets probability by a double integral: P((X,Y)∈A)=∬_A f(x,y) dx dy. Also f(x,y) ≥ 0 and ∬_{R²} f(x,y) dx dy = 1. A single point has probability 0. A line segment also has probability 0 when the density is finite.

A rectangle [a,b] × [c,d] uses x from a to b and y from c to d. A triangle uses bounds taken from its picture. For example, over 0≤y≤x≤1, the inner y-limit is 0 to x and the outer x-limit is 0 to 1. Do not replace the triangle by the full square 0≤x≤1, 0≤y≤1.

P((X,Y) ∈ A) = ∬_A f(x,y) dx dy

f(x,y) is the joint pdf; A is a region in the plane; dx dy shows integration over x and y.

Joint CDF and rectangles

The joint CDF is F(x,y)=P(X≤x, Y≤y). For discrete variables, F(x,y)=Σ_{u≤x} Σ_{v≤y} p(u,v). For continuous variables, F(x,y)=∫_{-∞}^{x} ∫_{-∞}^{y} f(u,v) dv du. It is non-decreasing in each argument, right-continuous in each, and tends to 0 at either -∞ and to 1 at (+∞,+∞).

A rectangle probability comes from four CDF values. For a<b and c<d, P(a<X≤b, c<Y≤d)=F(b,d)-F(a,d)-F(b,c)+F(a,c). The two negative terms remove strips, and the last term adds back the corner removed twice.

P(a<X≤b, c<Y≤d) = F(b,d) - F(a,d) - F(b,c) + F(a,c)

F is the joint CDF; a,b,c,d are fixed numbers with a<b and c<d.

For continuous variables, differentiating the CDF gives the pdf: f(x,y)=∂²F/(∂x∂y) at points where the derivative exists. The CDF stores all joint information in one function.

New variables and the Jacobian

Let U=g(X,Y) and V=h(X,Y). Suppose the map from (x,y) to (u,v) is one-to-one and differentiable. Solve for x=x(u,v) and y=y(u,v). The Jacobian is J=∂(u,v)/∂(x,y), the determinant of the 2×2 matrix of partial derivatives. Then f_{UV}(u,v)=f_{XY}(x,y)/|J|, with x,y replaced by their formulas in u,v. If the map is not one-to-one, split into pieces and add the pieces.

J = det [[∂u/∂x, ∂u/∂y], [∂v/∂x, ∂v/∂y]]

J is the Jacobian; u and v are the new variables; x and y are the old variables.

  1. Write u=g(x,y), v=h(x,y) and solve for x,y in terms of u,v.
  2. Compute the four partial derivatives and the determinant J.
  3. Take the absolute value |J|. If J=0 at a point, treat that point separately.
  4. Substitute x(u,v), y(u,v) into f_XY and divide by |J|.
  5. Check that the result integrates to 1 over the image region.

Example: U=X+Y and V=X-Y. Then x=(u+v)/2 and y=(u-v)/2. The derivatives give J=-2, so |J|=2. Hence f_{UV}(u,v)=½ f_{XY}((u+v)/2,(u-v)/2).

Question. A fair die is rolled twice. X = number of 1s, Y = number of 2s. (a) Find P(X=1,Y=1). (b) Find P(X+Y≥1).

Exam traps

  • A joint pdf value f(x,y) is not a probability. It can exceed 1. Only a double integral over an area gives probability.
  • Do not use a rectangle when the region is a triangle. Read the inner and outer limits from the picture.
  • In the Jacobian formula use |J| with J=∂(u,v)/∂(x,y). If you compute the inverse Jacobian ∂(x,y)/∂(u,v), multiply by it, do not divide.
  • In the rectangle CDF formula, the signs are + - - +. A sign slip changes the probability.
  • The joint is not the product of marginals unless the variables are independent. That product is a special case, not the definition.

4.2 Marginal and conditional distributions

A joint distribution describes two random variables together. If you only care about one of them, you sum or integrate the joint over the other variable. This gives the marginal distribution. If you know the value of one variable, divide the joint by the marginal of the variable whose value is known (to condition X on Y=y, divide by p_Y(y) or f_Y(y)). This gives the conditional distribution. Marginals and conditionals are used in risk scoring, queuing, signal processing, and any model with two linked measurements. They also prepare the way for independence, covariance, and the multivariate normal.

You need: 4.1 Joint distributions of two random variables for the joint pmf, joint pdf, and joint CDF.

Marginals: sum or integrate one variable out

Start with two discrete random variables X and Y. The joint pmf p(x,y) gives P(X=x, Y=y). The marginal pmf of X is p_X(x) = Σ_y p(x,y). The sum runs over every value y that Y can take. For each fixed x, you add the probabilities in that row. The result is the pmf of X alone. The marginal pmf of Y is p_Y(y) = Σ_x p(x,y).

p_X(x) = Σ_y p(x,y), p_Y(y) = Σ_x p(x,y)

p(x,y) is the joint pmf; p_X(x) is the marginal pmf of X; p_Y(y) is the marginal pmf of Y; the sums run over all possible values of the other variable.

On a joint probability table, read marginals from the margins. Add across each row to get p_X(x). Add down each column to get p_Y(y). The grand total is 1.

For continuous variables, replace sums by integrals. The joint pdf f(x,y) gives probability by volume under the surface. The marginal pdf of X is f_X(x) = ∫ f(x,y) dy. The integral runs over all y where the joint is positive. The marginal pdf of Y is f_Y(y) = ∫ f(x,y) dx. For the marginal of X, y is integrated out. For the marginal of Y, x is integrated out.

f_X(x) = ∫ f(x,y) dy, f_Y(y) = ∫ f(x,y) dx

f(x,y) is the joint pdf; f_X and f_Y are marginal pdfs; each integral runs over the support of the variable being removed.

Always write the support. A marginal is zero outside the range of its variable. In the worked example, f_Y(y)=2y only for 0<y<1.

Conditionals: divide the joint by the marginal

For discrete X and Y, the conditional pmf of X given Y=y is p(x|y) = p(x,y) / p_Y(y), for p_Y(y)>0. It is a pmf in x for each fixed y. The conditional pmf of Y given X=x is p(y|x) = p(x,y) / p_X(x), for p_X(x)>0. Divide by the marginal of the variable after the vertical bar. To condition X on Y, divide by p_Y(y).

p(x|y) = p(x,y) / p_Y(y),   f(x|y) = f(x,y) / f_Y(y)

p(x|y) is the conditional pmf; f(x|y) is the conditional pdf; p_Y(y) and f_Y(y) are marginals of Y; both formulas need a positive denominator.

For continuous variables, f(x|y)=f(x,y)/f_Y(y), for f_Y(y)>0. This is a pdf in x. The conditional pdf of Y given X=x is f(y|x)=f(x,y)/f_X(x), for f_X(x)>0. The denominator is always the marginal of the conditioning variable.

The conditional CDF follows by integrating the conditional pdf. F(x|y)=P(X≤x|Y=y)=∫_{-∞}^x f(t|y) dt. For discrete variables, sum p(t|y) over t≤x.

Keep the support. f(x|y)=1/y in the worked example is valid only for 0<x<y (and 0<y<1); it is zero outside that region. This follows from dividing by f_Y(y)=2y, which is positive only on 0<y<1. A conditional distribution is a distribution in its own right: it is non-negative and integrates or sums to 1 over x. Independence is the special case where the conditional equals the marginal. That case is 4.3 Independent random variables.

Conditional expectation and total expectation

Conditional expectation is the mean of the conditional distribution. For discrete variables, E[X|Y=y]=Σ_x x p(x|y). For continuous variables, E[X|Y=y]=∫ x f(x|y) dx, with the integral running over the support of X for the fixed y; in the worked example E[X|Y=y]=∫_0^y x(1/y) dx. It is a function of y. Before Y is observed, E[X|Y] is a random variable. After Y=y is observed, it is a number.

Conditional variance is Var(X|Y=y)=E[X²|Y=y] − (E[X|Y=y])². It measures spread of X around its conditional mean, for fixed y.

The total expectation rule says E[X]=E[E[X|Y]]. For discrete Y, E[X]=Σ_y E[X|Y=y] p_Y(y). For continuous Y, E[X]=∫ E[X|Y=y] f_Y(y) dy. Average the conditional means with the marginal weights. The worked example checks 1/3 = 1/3. Use the rule when a direct mean is hard but the mean given Y is easy.

Worked example

Question. Let X and Y have joint density f(x,y)=2 for 0<x<y<1, and zero elsewhere. (a) Find the marginal f_Y(y). (b) Find the marginal f_X(x). (c) Find the conditional density f(x|y). (d) Find E[X|Y=y]. (e) Verify the total expectation rule using the marginals.

  1. Fix y with 0<y<1. Then x runs from 0 to y. So f_Y(y)=∫_0^y 2 dx = 2[y-0] = 2y. Outside 0<y<1, f_Y(y)=0.
  2. Fix x with 0<x<1. Then y runs from x to 1. So f_X(x)=∫_x^1 2 dy = 2[1-x] = 2(1-x). Outside 0<x<1, f_X(x)=0.
  3. Divide the joint by f_Y(y). For 0<y<1 and 0<x<y, f(x|y)=f(x,y)/f_Y(y)=2/(2y)=1/y. Outside 0<x<y it is zero.
  4. Compute E[X|Y=y]=∫_0^y x(1/y) dx = (1/y)(y²/2) = y/2.
  5. Compute E[X] from f_X: E[X]=∫_0^1 x·2(1-x) dx = 2∫_0^1 (x-x²) dx = 2(1/2-1/3)=2(1/6)=1/3.
  6. Compute E[Y] from f_Y: E[Y]=∫_0^1 y·2y dy = 2∫_0^1 y² dy = 2(1/3)=2/3. Then E[Y/2]=(1/2)(2/3)=1/3. The two values match.

Answer. The marginals are f_Y(y)=2y for 0<y<1 and f_X(x)=2(1−x) for 0<x<1; the conditional density is f(x|y)=1/y for 0<x<y; E[X|Y=y]=y/2; and the total expectation check gives E[X]=1/3 and E[Y/2]=1/3.

Exam traps

  • Dividing by the wrong marginal. For f(x|y), divide by f_Y(y). Do not divide by f_X(x).
  • Losing the support. The conditional density 1/y is zero outside 0<x<y; it is not defined for x≥y.
  • Forgetting the denominator condition. A conditional is defined only where the marginal in the denominator is positive.
  • Treating a marginal as a joint. p_X(x) answers a question about X alone; it does not give P(X=x, Y=y).
  • Using total expectation with an unweighted average. E[X]=Σ E[X|Y=y] p_Y(y); the weights are marginal probabilities.

4.3 Independent random variables

Two random variables are independent when the value of one carries no information about the other. The joint distribution then factorizes: the probability of every pair is the product of the two marginal probabilities. This lets you multiply probabilities of separate measurements, split expectations into products, and justify the law of large numbers and the central limit theorem. It is used in quality control, A/B tests, reliability of components, and in the normal models behind portfolio and signal-noise calculations.

You need: 4.1, 4.2, and independent events from Unit 2.

Definition and the rectangle test

For discrete X and Y with joint pmf p(x,y) and marginals p_X(x)=P(X=x), p_Y(y)=P(Y=y), independence means p(x,y)=p_X(x)p_Y(y) for every pair (x,y). Equivalently, the conditional pmf pY|X(y|x)=p(x,y)/p_X(x) equals p_Y(y) whenever p_X(x)>0: learning X=x does not change the distribution of Y.

For continuous variables replace pmf by pdf f. Independence means f(x,y)=f_X(x)f_Y(y) for all x,y. The conditional density fY|X(y|x)=f(x,y)/f_X(x) then equals f_Y(y).

The support, the set where the density is positive, must be a rectangle: a product of an x-interval and a y-interval. For example, f(x,y)=2 on 0<x<1, 0<y<1 has rectangular support. A triangular support forces dependence: for g(x,y)=2 on 0<x<y<1, the formula is constant, but Y must exceed X, so knowing X=0.9 forces Y>0.9.

From a table, multiply each row total by each column total. Every cell probability must equal that product. With counts, compare a cell with row total × column total ÷ grand total.

p(x,y) = p_X(x) p_Y(y) for all x, y

p(x,y) is the joint pmf; p_X and p_Y are the marginal pmfs of X and Y.

Products of functions, many variables, and pairwise traps

Independence passes through functions: if X and Y are independent, then g(X) and h(Y) are independent for fixed functions g and h. Therefore E[g(X)h(Y)] = E[g(X)]E[h(Y)] whenever the expectations exist. Setting g(x)=x and h(y)=y gives E[XY]=E[X]E[Y]. The converse is false: E[XY]=E[X]E[Y] does not prove independence; see 4.4.

For n variables X1,...,Xn, mutual independence means the joint pmf or pdf equals the product of all n marginals: f(x1,...,xn)=f1(x1)...fn(xn). Every subcollection must also factorize. For events A1,...,An, independence means P(A1∩...∩An)=P(A1)...P(An) and the same for every subcollection.

Pairwise independence only checks each pair. Toss two fair coins. Let A be first coin heads, B be second coin heads, C be the coins differ. Each pair is independent, but P(A∩B∩C)=0 while P(A)P(B)P(C)=1/8. Pairwise independence is not mutual independence.

  1. Write the joint pmf or pdf and both marginals.
  2. Mark the support. If it is not a rectangle, stop: dependent.
  3. Multiply the marginals at a general point and compare with the joint.
  4. If equal everywhere on the rectangle, independent; one unequal point proves dependence.
  5. For a table, use row total × column total ÷ grand total.

Why it matters

Random sampling models observations as independent copies of one variable. Then the joint density is a product, so likelihoods multiply; this is used in maximum likelihood in Unit 5.

Reliability uses the product rule. Let p1 and p2 be the survival probabilities of two independent units. If the system survives when at least one unit survives (parallel redundancy), the survival probability is 1−(1−p1)(1−p2). If both units must survive (series), it is p1p2.

The law of large numbers and central limit theorem (4.5, 4.6) assume independent observations. In the multivariate normal (4.7), zero covariance plus normality gives independence, but that shortcut does not hold for other distributions.

Worked example

Question. The random variables X and Y have joint density f(x,y)=6e−2x−3y for x>0, y>0. (a) Find the marginals f_X and f_Y. (b) Show X and Y are independent. (c) Find P(X>1, Y>2). (d) A second pair has g(x,y)=2 on 0<x<y<1. Find its marginals and show the pair is dependent.

  1. The support is the first quadrant x>0, y>0, a rectangle.
  2. f_X(x)=∫0∞ 6e−2x−3y dy = 6e−2x · (1/3) = 2e−2x, x>0.
  3. f_Y(y)=∫0∞ 6e−2x−3y dx = 6e−3y · (1/2) = 3e−3y, y>0.
  4. Product f_X(x)f_Y(y)=2e−2x · 3e−3y = 6e−2x−3y = f(x,y) for all x>0, y>0. Support is a rectangle, so X and Y are independent.
  5. P(X>1, Y>2)=∫1∞∫2∞ f dxdy = [∫1∞2e−2x dx][∫2∞3e−3y dy] = e−2 · e−6 = e−8 ≈ 0.000335.
  6. For g(x,y)=2 on 0<x<y<1: f_X(x)=∫x1 2 dy = 2(1−x), 0<x<1.
  7. f_Y(y)=∫0y 2 dx = 2y, 0<y<1.
  8. Product f_X(x)f_Y(y)=4y(1−x). At (x,y)=(0.25,0.5), joint=2 and product=4·0.5·0.75=1.5. Since 1.5≠2, dependent.

Answer. X and Y are independent with P(X>1,Y>2)=e−8≈0.000335; the second pair is dependent because its triangular support and product 4y(1−x) do not match g=2.

Exam traps

  • Checking only the formula: 6e−2x−3y splits, but a density on a triangle can also split. Check the support shape.
  • Forgetting 'for all x,y': equality at one point proves nothing; one unequal point proves dependence.
  • Using counts instead of probabilities in a table: compare a cell count with row total × column total ÷ grand total.
  • Confusing pairwise with mutual independence. For three or more variables, every subcollection must factorize.
  • Assuming E[XY]=E[X]E[Y] proves independence. It only proves zero covariance; dependence can hide in higher moments.

In machine learning, naive Bayes assumes independent features given the class, and the assumption often works despite being false. In finance, independence across assets fails in crashes, so copulas are used to model dependence. Search next for mutual information, copulas, and conditional independence.

4.4 Covariance of two random variables

Covariance measures whether two random variables move together in a straight-line sense. It averages the product of their deviations from their own means. A positive value says high X tends to come with high Y; a negative value says high X tends to come with low Y; zero says there is no linear co-movement. Its units are units of X times units of Y, so its size is hard to read. Analysts use it in portfolio risk, quality control and signal processing, where two measurements must be judged together.

You need: joint distributions from 4.1, independence from 4.3, and expectation and variance from Unit 2.

Definition and the meaning of its sign

For random variables X and Y with means μX = E[X] and μY = E[Y], covariance is the expected product of their deviations.

Cov(X,Y) = E[(X − μX)(Y − μY)] = E[XY] − μX μY

Cov(X,Y) is the covariance; E[·] is expectation; μX and μY are the means; E[XY] is the joint second moment. The second form is usually easier: find E[XY] from the joint law and subtract the product of the means.

Each deviation is positive when the variable is above its mean. If X and Y tend to be above their means together, the product is mostly positive and covariance is positive. If one tends to be above its mean while the other is below, the product is mostly negative and covariance is negative. The deviations cancel in the average when there is no consistent linear pattern, giving zero.

Because the deviations carry units, Cov(X,Y) carries units of X times units of Y. A covariance of 12 rupee·marks is not directly comparable with one of 0.3 metre·second. Only the sign is immediately readable. The magnitude depends on the scales of X and Y.

Rules for covariance

Covariance is symmetric: Cov(X,Y) = Cov(Y,X). Setting Y = X gives Cov(X,X) = E[(X − μX)²] = Var(X). So variance is a special covariance.

It is bilinear: for constants a, b, c and d, Cov(aX + b, cY + d) = ac Cov(X,Y). Adding a constant does not change covariance; multiplying one variable by a scales covariance by a. Also Cov(X + Y, Z) = Cov(X,Z) + Cov(Y,Z). These rules let us expand variances without returning to the joint table.

Var(X ± Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) ± 2Cov(X,Y)

The plus sign belongs to X + Y and the minus sign to X − Y. When X and Y are independent, Cov(X,Y) = 0, so the variance of a sum is the sum of variances.

Joint moments are E[X^i Y^j] for non-negative integers i and j. Covariance uses the moment E[XY] with i = j = 1. The joint moment generating function is M(s,t) = E[e^(sX + tY)] for real s and t near zero. Its partial derivatives at (0,0) give the joint moments, when the mgf exists. The joint mgf is developed further with the multivariate normal in 4.7.

Correlation coefficient and best line

Covariance has units, so we divide by the standard deviations. The correlation coefficient is ρ = Cov(X,Y)/(σX σY), where ρ is the correlation coefficient and σX, σY are the standard deviations of X and Y. ρ has no units and always satisfies −1 ≤ ρ ≤ 1. The value +1 means Y is an increasing straight-line function of X; −1 means a decreasing straight line; 0 means no linear co-movement. Some syllabi call ρ the coefficient of linear correlation.

ŷ = μY + ρ(σY/σX)(x − μX)

ŷ is the predicted mean of Y when X = x; μX and μY are the means; σX and σY are the standard deviations; ρ is the correlation. The slope is ρ σY/σX. The line passes through (μX, μY). The fraction of variance explained is ρ².

Independence implies Cov(X,Y) = 0 and hence ρ = 0, because E[XY] = E[X]E[Y]. The converse is false in general. Let X be symmetric about 0 and let Y = X². Then Cov(X,Y) = E[X³] − E[X]E[X²] = 0, yet Y is completely determined by X. So ρ = 0 says only that no straight-line trend was detected. Outside the normal case, zero correlation does not mean independence. For jointly normal variables, ρ = 0 does imply independence; see 4.7.

Worked example

Question. Two fair dice are thrown. Let X be the number of 1s and Y the number of 2s. Find Cov(X,Y), Var(X), and the correlation coefficient ρ. Interpret the sign.

  1. Only the outcome with one 1 and one 2 gives X = 1, Y = 1 and product 1. Its probability is 2/36, because either die can be the 1. So E[XY] = 1×1×(2/36) = 1/18.
  2. The marginal mean is μX = 2×(1/6) = 1/3. By symmetry μY = 1/3.
  3. Cov(X,Y) = E[XY] − μX μY = 1/18 − (1/3)(1/3) = 1/18 − 1/9 = −1/18 ≈ −0.056.
  4. The variance is Var(X) = 2×(1/6)×(5/6) = 10/36 = 5/18 ≈ 0.278. By symmetry Var(Y) = 5/18.
  5. ρ = Cov(X,Y)/(σX σY) = (−1/18)/(√(5/18)√(5/18)) = (−1/18)/(5/18) = −1/5 = −0.2.
  6. The negative sign says outcomes with more 1s tend to have fewer 2s.

Answer. Cov(X,Y) = −1/18 ≈ −0.056 in count², Var(X) = Var(Y) = 5/18 ≈ 0.278, and ρ = −0.2, so 1s and 2s move in opposite directions.

Exam traps

  • Concluding that ρ = 0 means independence. Outside the normal case, zero correlation rules out only a straight-line trend.
  • Writing Var(X + Y) = Var X + Var Y without the 2Cov(X,Y) term when X and Y are dependent.
  • Using E[XY] as covariance. Always subtract μX μY from E[XY].
  • Reading the size of covariance as strength. Covariance depends on units; use ρ for a scale-free measure.
  • For Var(X − Y), using plus instead of minus: the correct form is Var X + Var Y − 2Cov(X,Y).

4.5 Law of large numbers

Toss a coin a thousand times and the proportion of heads lands near its true probability. Toss it ten times and it can be anything. The law of large numbers makes that precise: as independent observations grow, the sample mean converges to the population mean, and the chance of any fixed gap goes to zero. It is why polls need only a few thousand people and how simulation estimates integrals.

You need expectation, variance and Chebyshev's inequality from Unit 2, and independence from 4.3.

The law in one line

Take n independent observations X₁, …, Xₙ, each with mean μ and finite variance σ². Their average is X̄_n = (X₁ + … + Xₙ)/n. Here μ is the population mean, σ² the population variance, n the number of observations, and X̄_n (say "X-bar sub n") the sample mean.

P(|X̄_n − μ| ≥ ε) → 0 as n → ∞, for every ε > 0

X̄_n is the sample mean of n independent observations; μ is the population mean; ε (epsilon) is a tolerance in the same units as X; the bars mean absolute value; the arrow means the probability tends to 0 as n grows. This is the weak law of large numbers.

Before you count, X̄_n is random. The law says its distribution collapses onto μ. This is convergence in probability. For large fixed n one sample mean can still be off, but such runs get rarer.

Proof from Chebyshev's inequality

Two numbers decide everything. Expectation is linear, so E[X̄_n] = (1/n)(E[X₁] + … + E[Xₙ]) = (nμ)/n = μ. Independence is not needed here.

Independence makes variances add, so Var(X₁ + … + Xₙ) = nσ² (cross terms vanish, as in 4.4). Dividing by n divides variance by n², so Var(X̄_n) = nσ²/n² = σ²/n. The variance of the average shrinks like 1/n; its standard deviation like 1/√n.

P(|Y − E[Y]| ≥ ε) ≤ Var(Y)/ε²

Y is any random variable with finite variance; E[Y] is its mean; Var(Y) is its variance; ε > 0 is the tolerance. This is Chebyshev's inequality from Unit 2.

Put Y = X̄_n. Its mean is μ and variance σ²/n, so P(|X̄_n − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ (σ²/n)/ε² = σ²/(nε²). With σ and ε fixed, the right side tends to 0 as n → ∞. That is the proof.

n ≥ σ²/(ε²δ)

σ² is the variance of one observation; ε is the tolerance; δ is the largest probability of missing μ by ε or more that you accept. Solving σ²/(nε²) ≤ δ gives this sample size.

Proportions and three uses

A proportion is a sample mean. Code heads as 1 and tails as 0. Then X̄_n is the observed fraction of heads, p̂. One toss has mean p and variance p(1 − p), so P(|p̂ − p| ≥ ε) ≤ p(1 − p)/(nε²). Here p is the true heads probability and p̂ the observed proportion.

Opinion polls. A random sample of a few thousand voters puts p̂ within about 2 or 3 percentage points. The bound contains n, not population size: 1.4 billion people need the same sample as 14 million, if the sample is random.

Insurance. A company sells thousands of independent policies. Average claim per policy is a sample mean, so it settles near the expected claim μ. The premium sits a little above μ, and the buffer covers total payout with high probability.

Monte Carlo integration. To estimate ∫₀¹ g(x) dx, draw U₁, …, Uₙ uniform on (0,1) and average g(Uᵢ). Each g(Uᵢ) has mean equal to the integral, so the average converges to it. Simulation of queues, risk and physics uses the same trick.

The strong law, and what the law does not say

The strong law of large numbers states P(lim_{n→∞} X̄_n = μ) = 1: with probability one the sequence X̄_n converges to μ, not merely in probability. It implies the weak law. The proof needs more machinery, so it is quoted without proof.

The law does not make past results even out. After ten heads the next toss is still heads with probability p; a coin has no memory. What happens is dilution, not repayment. Let H and T be the counts of heads and tails. The proportion H/n = X̄_n tends to p, so (H − T)/n → 2p − 1. For a fair coin (p = 0.5), H − T is typically of order √n. For p ≠ 0.5, the gap |H − T| grows linearly like n|2p − 1|, with fluctuations of order √n. The early surplus is swamped in the proportion, not in the gap.

For a fixed small n the law says nothing useful. At n = 10 the bound σ²/(nε²) often exceeds 1. It also says nothing about the shape of the error around μ.

Worked example

Question. A coin lands heads with probability p = 0.4. It is tossed n = 2500 times, and X̄ is the observed proportion of heads. Use Chebyshev's inequality to bound P(|X̄ − 0.4| ≥ 0.02).

  1. Code toss i as Xᵢ = 1 for heads and 0 for tails. Each Xᵢ is Bernoulli with mean μ = p = 0.4 and variance σ² = p(1 − p) = 0.4 × 0.6 = 0.24.
  2. The observed proportion is X̄ = (X₁ + … + X₂₅₀₀)/2500, so E[X̄] = μ = 0.4.
  3. By independence, Var(X̄) = σ²/n = 0.24/2500 = 0.000096.
  4. The tolerance is ε = 0.02, so ε² = 0.0004.
  5. Chebyshev: P(|X̄ − 0.4| ≥ 0.02) ≤ 0.000096/0.0004 = 0.24.
  6. The bound is an upper bound only. The exact chance is smaller, but Chebyshev needs no shape assumption.

Answer. Chebyshev gives P(|X̄ − 0.4| ≥ 0.02) ≤ 0.24, that is, a chance of at most 24%.

Exam traps

  • Gambler's fallacy: after ten heads, believing a tail is due. Tosses are independent; the proportion converges because both counts grow, not because the surplus is repaid.
  • Treating the Chebyshev bound σ²/(nε²) as exact. It is an upper bound, ignores shape, and is often too large.
  • Confusing the law with the central limit theorem (4.6). The law says X̄_n → μ; the CLT describes the error around μ as roughly normal with standard deviation σ/√n.
  • Believing the law equalises heads and tails. For a fair coin H − T is typically of order √n; for p ≠ 0.5, |H − T| grows like n|2p − 1|. Only H/n settles to p.
  • Using σ²/n on correlated observations — rainfall, stock prices, queue lengths — without checking independence.

4.6 Central limit theorem

Add up many independent random variables that all follow the same distribution, then standardize the sum. The result is approximately standard normal, whatever the original distribution looked like. That is the central limit theorem, and it is the reason the normal curve turns up where nothing was normal. It turns a sum of a hundred or a million pieces into one table lookup. Engineers use it for tolerance stack-ups, auditors for error in a ledger, pollsters for the error bar on a survey, and banks for the spread of daily UPI settlement totals.

You need: the normal curve and its table of Φ, plus the binomial distribution, from Unit 3; and 4.5, which says the sample mean closes on μ, while this chapter says how the leftover gap is spread.

The theorem: standardizing a sum fixes its shape

Take n independent random variables X₁, X₂, …, X_n with the same distribution, the same mean μ, and the same finite variance σ². Put S_n = X₁ + X₂ + … + X_n. As n grows, the distribution of the standardized sum takes one fixed shape, the standard normal, whatever the parent distribution was — uniform, exponential, binomial, anything with a finite variance.

S_n itself does not settle down: its mean nμ and its variance nσ² both grow with n. Variances of independent variables add, so Var(S_n) = nσ² and the standard deviation of S_n is σ√n. Subtract the mean and divide by that standard deviation to reach a fixed scale.

Z_n = (S_n − nμ) / (σ√n) → N(0, 1)

S_n is the sum X₁ + … + X_n; μ and σ² are the mean and variance of one term, with 0 < σ² < ∞; n is the number of terms; Z_n is the standardized sum; → means the distribution of Z_n tends to the standard normal as n → ∞.

To use it, convert the limits on S_n into standard units and read the table of Φ.

P(S_n ≤ a) ≈ Φ( (a − nμ) / (σ√n) )

a is a limit in the units of S_n; nμ is the mean of S_n; σ√n is its standard deviation; Φ is the standard normal CDF from Unit 3, the area to the left of a z-value.

The sample mean is the same theorem in another dress. Since X̄_n = S_n/n, dividing top and bottom of Z_n by n moves the √n down beside σ.

(X̄_n − μ) / (σ/√n) ≈ N(0, 1)

X̄_n is the sample mean; σ/√n is the standard error of the mean, the typical size of the gap between X̄_n and μ. 4.5 says the gap closes; this formula says how the remaining gap is spread.

Working the numbers, and the ±0.5 correction

  1. Find μ and σ for one term; compute nμ and σ√n and keep them exact.
  2. Write the question as an interval for S_n, such as P(40 ≤ S_n ≤ 60).
  3. If S_n counts whole items, widen each limit by 0.5: 39.5 and 60.5.
  4. Standardize each limit with z = (limit − nμ)/(σ√n).
  5. Read Φ at each z and subtract; symmetrical limits give 2Φ(z) − 1.

The normal curve is continuous and puts zero probability on any single point. A count sits on the integers. The event 40 ≤ X ≤ 60 covers the whole-number blocks 40, 41, …, 60, and the strip from 39.5 to 60.5 covers exactly those blocks and nothing else. Drop the ±0.5 and the strip is half a unit too short at each end. In the middle that costs very little; in the tail it is serious, because P(X ≤ 40) needs the whole block up to 40.5, while the normal gives area only up to 40.

The binomial as a special case, and how big n must be

The binomial is a sum of n independent Bernoulli trials: each trial is 1 with probability p and 0 with probability 1 − p, so μ = p and σ² = p(1 − p). The theorem then becomes the normal approximation to the binomial.

P(X ≤ k) ≈ Φ( (k + 0.5 − np) / √(np(1−p)) )

X ~ Binomial(n, p); k is an integer count of successes; k + 0.5 is the continuity correction; np is the mean of X; np(1−p) is its variance.

How large must n be? The usual rule of thumb is n ≥ 30. The real test is the shape of the parent, and skew is what decides. A symmetric parent needs only a handful of terms: the binomial with p = 0.5 at n = 10 already sits almost on the normal curve in the figure. A skewed parent needs far more. For the binomial the working test is np ≥ 5 and n(1−p) ≥ 5, which keeps the mean about two standard deviations from both ends; with p = 0.05 that needs n around 100. When the parent is already normal, every n is exact, not approximate.

Worked example

4.7 Multivariate normal distribution

A random vector is multivariate normal when every linear combination of its parts is normal. Then the whole distribution is fixed by two objects: the mean vector μ and the covariance matrix Σ. This chapter is taught in only a few universities. The model describes heights and weights measured together, returns on several assets, and correlated noise in communication receivers. It is the natural many-variable version of the normal curve, and it keeps the normal shape under averaging and linear filtering.

You need: joint distributions, marginals and conditionals, covariance, and the normal distribution from Unit 3.

Mean vector and covariance matrix

A random vector packs n random variables into one column X = (X₁, …, Xₙ)ᵀ. Its mean vector μ = E[X] holds the n means. Its covariance matrix Σ has entry Σᵢⱼ = Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ) in row i, column j. The matrix is symmetric, Σᵀ = Σ, and positive semidefinite: aᵀΣa ≥ 0 for every vector a. Positive semidefiniteness is forced because aᵀX is a real random variable and aᵀΣa is its variance.

μ = E[X], Σᵢⱼ = Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ)

X is a column vector of n random variables; μᵢ is the mean of Xᵢ; Σᵢⱼ is the covariance of Xᵢ and Xⱼ; Σ is symmetric and positive semidefinite.

When Σ is invertible, the joint density in ℝⁿ is the multivariate normal density. The exponent is a squared distance measured in covariance units.

f(x) = (2π)^(−n/2) |Σ|^(−1/2) exp(−½ (x − μ)ᵀ Σ⁻¹ (x − μ))

x is a point in ℝⁿ; |Σ| is the determinant; Σ⁻¹ is the inverse; n is the number of variables.

When Σ is singular, the vector lies on a lower-dimensional plane and no density exists in ℝⁿ. The characteristic function then defines the law: φ(t) = exp(i tᵀμ − ½ tᵀΣt). Here t is a real vector; i is the imaginary unit; the exponent is complex. The density formula is the usual working form when Σ⁻¹ exists.

Bivariate case and conditional slices

For n = 2, write the pair as (X, Y) with means μX and μY, standard deviations σX and σY, and correlation ρ. The covariance matrix is Σ = [[σX², ρσXσY], [ρσXσY, σY²]].

Positive definiteness requires |ρ| < 1 when both variances are positive. The density contours are ellipses centred at (μX, μY). The correlation ρ controls how far the ellipses tilt; ρ = 0 gives axis-aligned ellipses.

Marginals are normal: X ~ N(μX, σX²) and Y ~ N(μY, σY²). A vertical slice at X = x gives a normal conditional law. Its mean and variance are:

E[Y | X = x] = μY + ρ(σY/σX)(x − μX)

ρ is the correlation coefficient; σY/σX is the scale ratio; x is the fixed value of X.

Var(Y | X = x) = σY²(1 − ρ²)

σY² is the variance of Y; ρ² is the squared correlation; the result does not depend on x.

The conditional mean is a straight line in x with slope ρσY/σX. The factor 1 − ρ² is the fraction of Y's variance left after X is known. If ρ = 0, the slice mean is μY and the slice variance is σY².

Linear maps, whitening and independence

Take a constant matrix A of size m×n and a constant vector b of size m×1. Form Y = AX + b. Then Y is multivariate normal with mean Aμ + b and covariance AΣAᵀ. The normal shape survives every linear map.

Y = AX + b ⇒ Y ~ N(Aμ + b, AΣAᵀ)

A is an m×n matrix; b is an m×1 vector; AΣAᵀ is the new covariance matrix.

For normal vectors, zero covariance implies independence. If two components of a multivariate normal vector are uncorrelated, their joint density factors into the product of their marginals. This shortcut is special to the normal family. For general vectors, zero covariance only removes linear association; it does not give independence.

Decorrelation, also called whitening, removes all covariance. Let Σ^(−1/2) be the inverse of a symmetric square root of Σ, and set Z = Σ^(−1/2)(X − μ). Then E[Z] = 0 and Cov(Z) = I, the identity matrix. The components of Z are independent standard normals. In the worked example Σ = [[1, 1], [1, 4]], so whitening turns the correlated pair into independent standard normal scores.

Worked example

Question. (X, Y) is bivariate normal with μX = 1, μY = 2, σX = 1, σY = 2 and ρ = 0.5. Find Σ, the conditional mean and variance of Y given X = 2, and P(Y > 3 | X = 2).

  1. Cov(X, Y) = ρσXσY = 0.5 × 1 × 2 = 1.
  2. Σ = [[σX², Cov(X,Y)], [Cov(X,Y), σY²]] = [[1, 1], [1, 4]].
  3. Conditional mean = μY + ρ(σY/σX)(x − μX) = 2 + 0.5 × (2/1) × (2 − 1) = 3.
  4. Conditional variance = σY²(1 − ρ²) = 4 × (1 − 0.25) = 3.
  5. Given X = 2, Y is normal with mean 3 and variance 3. The threshold 3 equals the mean, so P(Y > 3 | X = 2) = 0.5.

Answer. Σ = [[1, 1], [1, 4]], E[Y | X = 2] = 3, Var(Y | X = 2) = 3, and P(Y > 3 | X = 2) = 0.5.

Exam traps

  • Using σY² as the conditional variance instead of σY²(1 − ρ²).
  • Applying 'uncorrelated means independent' to vectors that are not multivariate normal; there it is false.
  • Forgetting that Σ must be symmetric and positive semidefinite; a negative determinant is impossible.
  • Using the density formula when Σ is singular; then no density exists in ℝⁿ.
  • Swapping the slope ρσY/σX with its reciprocal ρσX/σY.

Takeaways

  1. A pair of random variables is described completely by one joint function on the plane; marginals and conditionals are just sums, integrals and ratios of that one function.
  2. Two variables are independent exactly when the joint is the product of the marginals at every point, not merely when their covariance is zero.
  3. Covariance measures only linear co-movement; it changes if you change units, so correlation is the unit-free version.
  4. Independence gives E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] and forces zero covariance, but zero covariance forces independence only in the jointly normal case.
  5. The weak law says the sample mean converges in probability to the population mean; Chebyshev's inequality gives the standard proof.
  6. The central limit theorem adds a rate and a shape: the standardized sum settles onto a standard normal curve, so probabilities can be read from Φ tables.
  7. The normal approximation to a binomial or Poisson sum needs a continuity correction when the underlying variable is discrete.
  8. A multivariate normal is fixed entirely by its mean vector and covariance matrix, and its parts are independent exactly when that matrix is diagonal.

Glossary

Almost sure convergence
Z_n → Z with probability 1, meaning the set of outcomes where the sequence fails to converge has probability 0.
Bivariate normal
The two-dimensional case of the multivariate normal, fixed by two means, two standard deviations and one correlation.
Central limit theorem
The result that the standardized sum of i.i.d. variables with finite variance converges in distribution to the standard normal.
Conditional distribution
The distribution of Y after X = x is fixed, equal to the joint divided by the marginal of X.
Conditional expectation
E[Y | X = x], the mean of the conditional distribution of Y at a fixed value x of X.
Continuity correction
A half-unit adjustment that improves a normal approximation to a discrete probability.
Convergence in probability
Z_n → Z in probability when P(|Z_n − Z| ≥ ε) → 0 for every fixed ε > 0.
Correlation coefficient
Cov(X, Y)/(σ_X σ_Y), a scale-free measure of linear association lying between −1 and 1.
Correlation matrix
The matrix of correlations between the components of a random vector, obtained by scaling the covariance matrix.
Covariance
E[(X − μ_X)(Y − μ_Y)], the average product of the two deviations from their means.
Covariance matrix
The symmetric matrix whose (i, j) entry is Cov(X_i, X_j) and whose diagonal holds the variances.
Independence of random variables
The property that the joint distribution factorizes into the product of the marginals everywhere.
Joint CDF
F(x, y) = P(X ≤ x, Y ≤ y), defined for all pairs of real numbers.
Joint distribution
The complete probability law of a pair (X, Y), giving the probability of every region of the plane.
Joint pdf
A non-negative function f(x, y) whose double integral over any region gives the probability that (X, Y) falls in that region.
Joint pmf
The function p(x, y) = P(X = x, Y = y) for discrete variables, summing to 1 over all pairs.
Law of large numbers
The result that the sample mean of i.i.d. observations approaches the population mean as n grows.
Marginal distribution
The distribution of one variable obtained by summing or integrating the joint over all values of the other.
Mean vector
The vector whose entries are the means of the components of a random vector.
Multivariate normal
A random vector whose every linear combination of components is normally distributed.
Positive semi-definite
A symmetric matrix M with aᵗMa ≥ 0 for every vector a; every covariance matrix has this property.
Standardizing
Subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation so the new variable has mean 0 and variance 1.
Support
The set of points where a joint pmf or pdf is positive, which must be stated when writing conditionals.
Uncorrelated
Having zero covariance, so that E[XY] = E[X]E[Y].
