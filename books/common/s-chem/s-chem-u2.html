2.1 Electromagnetic radiation and its interaction with matter

Light is an oscillating electric and magnetic field travelling through space. Its colour, and the energy it can deliver to a molecule, are fixed by one number: frequency. Matter absorbs a photon only when the photon energy matches the gap between two allowed levels. This single rule explains why some solutions look coloured, why IR spectra identify functional groups, and why sunlight drives photochemistry. It also sets the design of every spectrometer, from a ₹15,000 school colorimeter to a hospital MRI scanner.

You need Unit 1, Chemical Bonding and Coordination Compounds: wave–particle duality, photon energy E = hν, and the idea of quantized levels.

Wave parameters: λ, ν, c and wavenumber

Electromagnetic (EM) radiation is a travelling wave with an electric field and a magnetic field oscillating at right angles to each other and to the direction of travel. The wavelength λ is the distance between successive crests, measured in metres (m) or nanometres (1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m). The frequency ν is the number of oscillations per second, in hertz (1 Hz = 1 s⁻¹).

In vacuum all EM waves travel at c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. The three quantities are linked by c = νλ. Because c is fixed, λ and ν move in opposite directions: a short wavelength means a high frequency.

c = νλ

ν is frequency in Hz; λ is wavelength in m; c is the speed of light in vacuum, 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

Wavenumber ν̃ is the number of waves in one centimetre: ν̃ = 1/λ when λ is in cm. It is a reciprocal length, in cm⁻¹, not a length. IR spectra use 4000–400 cm⁻¹ because the numbers are convenient and proportional to energy. Higher ν̃ means higher frequency and higher photon energy.

ν̃ = 1/λ

λ is wavelength in cm; ν̃ is wavenumber in cm⁻¹.

The EM family runs from radio waves (λ of metres) through microwaves, infrared, visible (about 400–700 nm), ultraviolet, X-rays, to gamma rays (λ below 10⁻¹¹ m). X-rays and gamma rays are ionizing. Radiofrequency flips nuclear spins in NMR (Chapter 2.6); microwaves rotate molecules (Chapter 2.5); infrared excites vibrations (Chapter 2.4); visible and UV excite electrons (Chapter 2.3).

Photon energy and the matching condition

Light also behaves as a stream of photons. One photon carries energy E = hν, where h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s is Planck's constant. Using c = νλ gives E = hc/λ. In electronvolts, E(eV) ≈ 1240/λ(nm). At 500 nm, E ≈ 2.48 eV.

E = hν = hc/λ

E is photon energy in J; h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; ν is frequency in Hz; c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; λ is wavelength in m.

A molecule has discrete energy levels. It absorbs a photon only when the photon energy equals the gap: hν = ΔE = E_upper − E_lower. The photon disappears and the molecule is promoted. Emission is the reverse. This is one-photon absorption; two-photon processes need much higher intensity and are not the usual case.

A matching gap is necessary but not sufficient. Selection rules, set by symmetry and conserved angular momentum, decide whether a transition is allowed. A simple harmonic oscillator absorbs IR only with Δv = ±1; a rigid rotor absorbs microwaves with ΔJ = ±1; electronic transitions usually keep spin, ΔS = 0. 'Forbidden' lines can appear weakly when rules break down.

Intensity is different from photon energy. Intensity counts photons per second per unit area. A brighter beam excites more molecules, but each photon still has E = hν. Bright red light (about 700 nm, 1.8 eV) cannot cross a 3 eV gap, however intense it is. Absorbance, the quantity measured in a colorimeter, is covered in Chapter 2.2.

Absorption, emission and line broadening

An absorption spectrum is a plot of radiation missing after passing through a sample. Each dip or line sits at a frequency whose photon energy matches a gap. An emission spectrum shows the frequencies given out when excited species fall back. Line position gives the gap; line strength depends on the number of absorbing molecules, the population difference and the transition probability.

No spectral line is infinitely sharp. Natural lifetime broadening comes from the uncertainty relation ΔE Δt ≥ ħ/2, where ħ = h/2π: a short-lived excited state has a poorly defined energy. Collisions and pressure broaden lines in gases and liquids. Doppler motion broadens lines in hot gases. Instrument resolution adds its own width. Broadening carries information about lifetime, temperature and environment, so it is measured, not merely ignored.

The same one-photon rule underlies UV-visible, IR and NMR spectra, but each technique probes a different kind of gap. Photochemistry begins after absorption: only absorbed photons can drive reaction (Chapter 2.8). Some syllabi call this topic interaction of electromagnetic radiation with matter; the essential content is the matching condition plus selection rules.

Worked example

Question. A green laser pointer used in a classroom has wavelength 500 nm. (a) Find the photon energy in joules. (b) Convert it to electronvolts. (c) Find the wavenumber in cm⁻¹. Use h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and 1 eV = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.

  1. Convert wavelength: λ = 500 nm = 500 × 10⁻⁹ m = 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m.
  2. Write the photon-energy formula: E = hc/λ.
  3. Numerator = hc = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s)(3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹) = 1.9878 × 10⁻²⁵ J m.
  4. Divide by λ: E = 1.9878 × 10⁻²⁵ J m / 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m = 3.9756 × 10⁻¹⁹ J ≈ 3.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  5. Convert to electronvolts: E = 3.9756 × 10⁻¹⁹ J / 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ J eV⁻¹ = 2.4816 eV ≈ 2.48 eV. This agrees with 1240/500 = 2.48.
  6. Convert wavelength to cm: λ = 500 × 10⁻⁷ cm = 5.00 × 10⁻⁵ cm. Then ν̃ = 1/λ = 1/(5.00 × 10⁻⁵ cm) = 2.00 × 10⁴ cm⁻¹.

Answer. The 500 nm photon has energy 3.98 × 10⁻¹⁹ J, or 2.48 eV, and the wavenumber is 2.00 × 10⁴ cm⁻¹.

Exam traps

  • Using λ = 500 in E = hc/λ without converting nanometres to metres gives an answer 10⁹ times too small.
  • For wavenumber, convert to cm first: 500 nm is 5.00 × 10⁻⁵ cm, so ν̃ = 2.00 × 10⁴ cm⁻¹. Wavenumber is reciprocal length, not a length; a larger wavenumber means a shorter wavelength.
  • Energy and wavelength move oppositely, while frequency and energy move together. A 400 nm photon carries more energy than a 700 nm photon.
  • Bright light is not automatically high-energy light: intensity changes the photon count, not hν. A 500 nm photon is not ionizing.
  • A matched energy gap does not guarantee strong absorption; selection rules can make a transition weak or forbidden.

2.2 Beer-Lambert law

A coloured solution looks darker when it is more concentrated or when light passes through more of it. The Beer-Lambert law turns that observation into a number: absorbance A is proportional to concentration c and path length l, with a proportionality constant ε fixed by the substance, wavelength and solvent. Because A is easy to measure with a colorimeter or UV-visible spectrophotometer, the law is used to find unknown concentrations: drug assay, iron in water, dye strength in a textile bath, and glucose in a clinical sample. This chapter derives A = εcl and shows its limits.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for photon energy and absorption, plus logarithms to base 10.

From transmitted light to absorbance

A beam of intensity I₀ enters a cell of path length l and leaves with intensity I. Transmittance is T = I/I₀, a dimensionless fraction from 0 to 1; percent transmittance is %T = 100T. Absorbance is A = log₁₀(I₀/I) = −log₁₀ T. T = 0.1 gives A = 1; T = 0.01 gives A = 2.

Why A and not T? Split the cell into thin slices. Each slice removes the same fraction of the light entering it, so dI/I = −k dl. Integrating gives I = I₀e^(−kl), or in base 10, I = I₀ × 10^(−εcl). Doubling c doubles the absorbers in each cm³; doubling l doubles the number of slices. Both double A. Transmittance falls exponentially.

A = εcl

A is absorbance (no units); ε is molar absorptivity (L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹); c is concentration (mol L⁻¹); l is path length (cm).

T = I/I₀ = 10⁻ᴬ

T is transmittance (0 to 1); I is transmitted intensity; I₀ is incident intensity; A is absorbance.

ε depends on wavelength, solvent and temperature. It is largest at λmax, the wavelength of strongest absorption. The electronic transitions behind ε are in Chapter 2.3.

Blank, calibration and mixtures

Cuvettes and solvents absorb and reflect a little. A blank is the same cuvette with solvent and all reagents except the analyte. Set A = 0 on the blank, or subtract its reading: A_corrected = A_measured − A_blank. Without this, every point carries a constant offset.

Calibration: measure A for at least four standards of known c at one wavelength. Plot A against c. A straight line through the origin has slope εl. Then c_unknown = A_unknown/(slope). Use the same cuvette, wavelength and temperature for standards and sample.

A_total = A₁ + A₂ + ... = l Σ ε_i c_i

A_total is measured absorbance; ε_i and c_i are molar absorptivity and concentration of species i; l is path length in cm; the sum runs over all absorbing species.

Absorbance adds because T_total = T₁T₂..., so −log T_total = −log T₁ − log T₂ ... . Transmittance multiplies. Two unknowns need two wavelengths and two equations. A colorimeter is a filter photometer: lamp → filter → cuvette → detector → meter. A UV-visible spectrophotometer uses a prism or grating monochromator and can scan wavelength. Both display A.

When the straight line bends

At high concentration, molecules interact, ε changes and the plot bends; work below about 0.01 M, with A between 0.1 and 1.0. If A is too high, dilute and multiply c back.

Stray light sets a ceiling. The detector always sees some light I_s that bypassed the sample. At high A the true I is tiny, I_s dominates, measured T is too high and A reads too low. The curve flattens near A ≈ 2-3, depending on the instrument.

The law is strictly for monochromatic light. A wide bandpass averages different ε values and spoils linearity. Turbidity, fluorescence, dirty or mismatched cuvettes, a wrong blank, and pH-dependent species also cause deviations. Use a narrow band at λmax, clean matched cells, and a buffer. Absorbance also tells photochemistry how much light is available; see Chapter 2.8.

Worked example

Question. A dye solution in a 1.0 cm cell has absorbance 0.48 at 520 nm. Its molar absorptivity at 520 nm is 1.2 × 10⁴ L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹. Find the concentration, the slope of A versus c in units of 10⁻⁵ M, and the transmittance.

  1. Write the law: A = εcl.
  2. Rearrange for concentration: c = A/(εl).
  3. Substitute: c = 0.48 / (1.2 × 10⁴ L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹ × 1.0 cm) = 0.48 / (1.2 × 10⁴) mol L⁻¹.
  4. Divide: c = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol L⁻¹ = 40 µmol L⁻¹.
  5. Slope when c is in 10⁻⁵ M: A = (1.2 × 10⁴ × 1.0 × 10⁻⁵) c = 0.12 c, so slope = 0.12 per 10⁻⁵ M.
  6. Transmittance: T = 10⁻ᴬ = 10⁻⁰·⁴⁸ = 0.331, so about 33% of the light passes.

Answer. c = 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol L⁻¹ (40 µM), calibration slope 0.12 per 10⁻⁵ M, and T = 0.331.

Exam traps

  • Using percent transmittance directly: A = 2 − log₁₀(%T), because %T = 100T. A 50% T solution has A = 0.301, not 50.
  • Forgetting the blank: cell and solvent absorbance add a constant offset to every reading.
  • Adding transmittances for a mixture. Add absorbances; transmittances multiply.
  • Using ε from another wavelength or solvent. ε is fixed only for a given wavelength, solvent and temperature.
  • Mixing units: c must be mol L⁻¹ and l cm for ε in L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹; convert mM or µM first.

2.3 UV-visible spectroscopy: electronic transitions and chromophores

UV-visible light carries just enough energy to move an electron from a filled molecular orbital to an empty one. The wavelength absorbed tells you the size of that gap; for a fixed concentration and path length, the height reflects the molar absorptivity ε, which measures how likely the jump is. Dilute solutions in a spectrophotometer can identify conjugated molecules, follow a reaction, or measure a drug in a tablet. The same absorption gives colour: a dye absorbs some visible wavelengths and transmits the rest to your eye.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for photon energy, and Unit 1 molecular orbital theory for σ, π and n orbitals.

The four jumps that matter

Molecular orbitals come in filled and empty pairs. A σ bond uses a low-energy σ orbital; its empty partner is σ*. A π bond has π and π*. A lone pair sits in a non-bonding n orbital. UV-visible photons, roughly 200-800 nm, lift one electron from a filled orbital to an empty one. The energy gap ΔE sets the wavelength: a larger gap gives a shorter wavelength.

  • σ→σ*: σ electron to σ*. Largest gap, below about 200 nm; alkanes absorb in vacuum UV.
  • n→σ*: lone pair to σ*. About 150-250 nm; alcohols, amines and alkyl halides.
  • π→π*: π to π*. Isolated C=C near 170 nm; conjugation pushes it into 200-400 nm; strong band.
  • n→π*: lone pair to π*. About 250-400 nm; carbonyl compounds; weak band.

The same gap decides colour. A molecule that absorbs blue-green light near 480-500 nm looks red or orange; absorption near 400 nm leaves yellow-green.

Chromophore, auxochrome and shifts

A chromophore is the part of a molecule responsible for absorption: C=C, C=O, C≡C, NO₂ or an aromatic ring. It supplies the electronic transition. A chromophore is not automatically a coloured compound; the absorption must fall in the visible range. A chromophore with a lone pair on an adjacent atom can show n→π*.

An auxochrome is a saturated group with lone pairs, such as -OH, -NH₂, -OR or -Cl. It does not give a separate absorption band in the near-UV/visible region above about 250 nm; it modifies the chromophore's band. Attached to a chromophore, it shifts λmax and changes intensity by sharing lone-pair electron density.

Conjugation joins π orbitals across alternating single and double bonds. More overlapping p orbitals make a longer box, so the π→π* gap falls and λmax moves to longer wavelength. Ethene absorbs near 170 nm; butadiene near 217 nm; longer polyenes reach visible light. This is why β-carotene is orange and many dyes are coloured.

Bathochromic (red) shift: λmax moves to longer wavelength. Hypsochromic (blue) shift: λmax moves to shorter wavelength. Hyperchromic: ε increases. Hypochromic: ε decreases. Solvent can cause these; polar solvents often shift n→π* blue and π→π* red.

Reading a UV-visible spectrum

A UV-visible spectrum plots absorbance A or molar absorptivity ε against wavelength. λmax is the wavelength of maximum absorption. ε at λmax measures intensity: n→π* bands are often weak, with ε about 10-100 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹; π→π* bands are strong, with ε about 10³-10⁵ L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹. A low ε (not a low concentration) means a less allowed transition. For the same concentration and path length, an n→π* band is weaker than a π→π* band because its ε is smaller. See Chapter 2.2 for A = εcl.

Allowed means symmetry and spin permit the jump; forbidden means low probability, not zero. n→π* is formally forbidden and weak. Most π→π* transitions are allowed and strong, but some are symmetry-forbidden and weak; check the molecule.

Woodward-Fieser rules are a bookkeeping convention for simple dienes and enones: start from a base λmax and add increments for substituents and ring residues. Some syllabi name them; the full table belongs in a specialist text. Do not use one λmax as proof of a structure. Use it with IR and NMR to show conjugation and substituents.

Worked example

Question. A compound absorbs at λmax = 300 nm. Calculate the photon energy in eV and the energy per mole of photons in kJ mol⁻¹. Use 1 eV per photon = 96.485 kJ mol⁻¹.

  1. Use E = hc/λ. With λ in nm, E(eV) = 1240/λ(nm).
  2. E = 1240/300 = 4.133 eV.
  3. Round to 4.13 eV for three significant figures.
  4. Energy per mole = 4.133 × 96.485 = 398.8 kJ mol⁻¹.

Answer. The photon energy is 4.13 eV, and one mole of such photons carries 398.8 kJ mol⁻¹.

Exam traps

  • Every coloured compound has a chromophore, but not every chromophore gives colour. Check conjugation and whether absorption falls in 400-700 nm.
  • n→π* and π→π* are not interchangeable. The weak band is often the forbidden n→π* jump, not an impurity.
  • Longer λmax does not always mean stronger absorption. Bathochromic and hyperchromic are independent changes.
  • Solvent shifts matter. Do not compare λmax in hexane with λmax in water without noting the solvent.
  • One λmax cannot prove a structure. Conjugation length and substituents both change the band position.

2.4 IR spectroscopy: modes of vibration and group frequencies

Infrared (IR) spectroscopy shines IR light through a sample and records which wavenumbers are absorbed. Absorption makes a bond stretch or bend, not an electron jump. The pattern of absorbed wavenumbers acts like a fingerprint of functional groups. It is used to identify plastics, check the purity of medicines, monitor paint curing and detect gases. This chapter explains the vibrating-spring model, the selection rule, common group frequencies and the FTIR instrument.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for photon energy, and Unit 1 bonding for bond polarity.

The bond as a vibrating spring

A covalent bond is not rigid. The two atoms can move closer and farther apart. For small displacements the bond behaves like a spring. The force constant k measures stiffness: a C≡C bond has larger k than a C–C single bond. The reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂) accounts for both atoms; m₁ and m₂ are atomic masses in kg.

The harmonic oscillator model gives the wavenumber of the fundamental vibration.

ν̃ = (1 / (2πc)) √(k / μ)

ν̃ is wavenumber in m⁻¹ (divide by 100 for cm⁻¹); c is the speed of light = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹; k is the force constant in N m⁻¹; μ is the reduced mass in kg.

ν is frequency in Hz and ν̃ is wavenumber in m⁻¹. A larger k or a smaller μ gives a higher wavenumber. The model also gives evenly spaced levels v = 0, 1, 2, ... with energy E_v = (v + ½)hν, where h is Planck's constant.

Two rules decide whether IR light is absorbed. First, the photon energy must match a level gap; for the harmonic oscillator the selection rule is Δv = ±1. Second, the vibration must change the dipole moment. A dipole moment measures charge separation. The symmetric stretch of CO₂ has no dipole change and is IR inactive; the asymmetric stretch is active. At large displacements real bonds are anharmonic: levels crowd and the bond can break.

Stretching, bending and group frequencies

A stretching vibration changes bond length and appears at higher wavenumber. A bending vibration changes bond angle; scissoring, rocking, wagging and twisting are bending names. Bending bands are usually lower in wavenumber and weaker. Heavy atoms lower the wavenumber because μ is larger.

The IR spectrum has two useful zones. Group frequencies from about 4000 to 1500 cm⁻¹ show which functional group is present. The fingerprint region from about 1500 to 400 cm⁻¹ is a complex pattern; compare it with a known spectrum instead of assigning every peak.

GroupWavenumber (cm⁻¹)What it tells you

N–H3300–3500Amine or amide; often one or two bands
C–H2850–3100Alkane, alkene or arene

Hydrogen bonding weakens an O–H bond, lowers k and moves the O–H stretch to lower wavenumber. It also broadens the band because many H-bond lengths exist. A dilute alcohol has a sharper O–H near 3600 cm⁻¹; a concentrated alcohol has a broad band near 3300 cm⁻¹. Carbonyl bands also shift slightly when hydrogen bonded.

FTIR and reading the spectrum

A dispersive IR instrument scans one wavelength at a time. FTIR measures all wavelengths together and uses a Fourier transform to separate them. The light path is:

  1. IR source emits broad infrared light.
  2. Michelson interferometer splits the beam; one mirror moves.
  3. Recombined beam passes through the sample.
  4. Detector records intensity versus mirror position.
  5. Computer performs Fourier transform to give transmittance versus wavenumber.

The sample is placed in the beam as a thin film, a KBr pellet or on an ATR crystal. The detector records transmittance T, the fraction of light passing through. Absorbance A = log₁₀(1/T). A peak in an IR spectrum is a dip in transmittance.

Typical uses: identify an unknown polymer, check that a medicine contains the right polymorph, monitor curing of a paint, or detect methane leaks. A strong C=O band near 1715 cm⁻¹ plus a broad O–H band near 3300 cm⁻¹ suggests a carboxylic acid. The fingerprint region confirms identity by matching the whole pattern.

Worked example

Question. The fundamental vibration of HCl is at 2886 cm⁻¹. The reduced mass is 1.627 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. Calculate the vibration frequency ν and the force constant k.

  1. Convert wavenumber to m⁻¹: ν̃ = 2886 cm⁻¹ × 100 = 2.886 × 10⁵ m⁻¹.
  2. Frequency: ν = cν̃ = (3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹)(2.886 × 10⁵ m⁻¹) = 8.658 × 10¹³ s⁻¹.
  3. Angular frequency: ω = 2πν = 2π(8.658 × 10¹³) = 5.44 × 10¹⁴ rad s⁻¹.
  4. Square: ω² = (5.44 × 10¹⁴)² = 2.96 × 10²⁹ s⁻².
  5. Force constant: k = ω²μ = (2.96 × 10²⁹ s⁻²)(1.627 × 10⁻²⁷ kg) = 481 N m⁻¹.

Answer. ν = 8.658 × 10¹³ Hz and k = 481 N m⁻¹, about 4.8 × 10² N m⁻¹.

  • Using the fingerprint region as a group-frequency lookup. It is a whole-pattern comparison, not a list of every peak.

2.5 Rotational spectroscopy and the rigid rotor

Rotational spectroscopy studies microwave absorption by molecules turning in space. The rigid rotor model treats a diatomic molecule as two masses at a fixed distance, with no stretching. Quantum mechanics allows only certain rotational energies, labelled by J = 0, 1, 2, ... . Line spacing gives the moment of inertia and the bond length. The method is used for small gas-phase molecules, isotopic analysis and molecules in space. Only a few universities teach this topic.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for photon energy and Chapter 2.4 for quantized molecular motion; basic moment of inertia.

Quantized rotation

A diatomic molecule has two nuclei joined by a bond. The rigid rotor model puts the masses at a fixed separation r and lets them rotate about the centre of mass. The bond does not bend or stretch. The rotation is described by I = μr², where μ = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂) is the reduced mass, m₁ and m₂ are the atomic masses, and r is the bond length. For CO, μ = 1.139 × 10⁻²⁶ kg. The model ignores vibration.

E_J = h²J(J+1)/(8π²I), J = 0, 1, 2, ...

E_J is rotational energy in joules; h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s; I is moment of inertia in kg m²; J is the rotational quantum number.

The level J = 0 has zero rotational energy. Each level has 2J+1 orientations, so it is (2J+1)-fold degenerate. The gap between J and J+1 is ΔE = h²(J+1)/(4π²I), which grows with J.

Rotational constant

Spectroscopists divide energy by hc and write levels in cm⁻¹: F(J) = B J(J+1). The rotational constant is B = h/(8π²cI). Here c = 2.998 × 10¹⁰ cm s⁻¹ is the speed of light, I is in kg m², and B is in cm⁻¹. For CO, B = 1.93 cm⁻¹.

A molecule absorbs a microwave photon only when ΔJ = ±1. The absorption from J to J+1 has wavenumber ν̃ = 2B(J+1). The first line, J = 0 → 1, is at 2B. The next line is at 4B. Adjacent lines are separated by 2B. Equal spacing is the fingerprint of a rigid rotor. Measure the spacing, divide by 2 to get B, then get I. Isotopic substitution changes μ and B, so the spacing shifts. This spacing is the most direct measurement in rotational spectroscopy. The method works best for light gas-phase molecules with large B.

From spectrum to bond length

A microwave spectrum gives a series of lines. Use these steps.

  1. Measure the spacing between adjacent lines; this is 2B.
  2. Find B in cm⁻¹ by halving the spacing.
  3. Compute I = h/(8π²cB), with c in cm s⁻¹.
  4. Compute μ from atomic masses in kg.
  5. Find r = √(I/μ).

For CO, the first line is at 3.86 cm⁻¹, so B = 1.93 cm⁻¹. This gives I = 1.45 × 10⁻⁴⁶ kg m² and r = 1.13 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.113 nm. Isotopic substitution, such as ¹²C¹⁶O to ¹³C¹⁶O, changes μ and B, so the line spacing changes. A real bond stretches slightly as it rotates; advanced texts replace the rigid rotor with a non-rigid rotor and add centrifugal distortion. Measure many lines to reduce error. The result is in metres; multiply by 10⁹ for nm. The rigid rotor is a model. The model is still useful for small molecules.

Worked example

Question. The first rotational line of CO is at 3.86 cm⁻¹. Take B = 1.93 cm⁻¹, μ = 1.139 × 10⁻²⁶ kg, h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 2.998 × 10¹⁰ cm s⁻¹. Find I and r.

  1. First line: J = 0 → 1, so 2B = 3.86 cm⁻¹ and B = 1.93 cm⁻¹.
  2. I = h/(8π²cB), with c in cm s⁻¹.
  3. I = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ / (4.57 × 10¹²) = 1.45 × 10⁻⁴⁶ kg m².
  4. r = √(I/μ) = √(1.45 × 10⁻⁴⁶ / 1.139 × 10⁻²⁶) = √(1.27 × 10⁻²⁰).
  5. r = 1.13 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.113 nm.

Answer. I = 1.45 × 10⁻⁴⁶ kg m² and r = 1.13 × 10⁻¹⁰ m (0.113 nm).

Exam traps

  • Line spacing is 2B, not B.
  • Use reduced mass μ, not atomic masses.
  • B in cm⁻¹ needs c in cm s⁻¹.
  • J = 0 is allowed; it has zero energy.
  • Real bonds stretch; high-J lines shift (centrifugal distortion).

2.6 NMR spectroscopy

NMR spectroscopy, also called nuclear magnetic resonance, uses radio waves to flip the spins of nuclei in a strong magnetic field. A nucleus such as ¹H absorbs only at a frequency set by its local electron cloud. The spectrum has peaks at chemical shifts in ppm, areas that count protons and splitting that reports neighbouring protons. Chemists use it to identify organic molecules, check purity, follow reactions and test polymers.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for resonance and photon energy, and Unit 1 for molecular structure and equivalent atoms.

Nuclear spin in a magnetic field

Nuclei with a nonzero spin quantum number I behave like tiny bar magnets. The proton ¹H has I = 1/2; so does ¹³C. Nuclei with I = 0, such as ¹²C and ¹⁶O, have no magnetic moment and are NMR-silent. In a field B₀, a spin-1/2 nucleus has two energy levels, aligned and opposed. The small population difference between them creates the signal.

Each nucleus precesses at its Larmor frequency. Radiofrequency pulses at this frequency excite the spins; the receiver records the decaying signal, and a Fourier transform converts it to a spectrum.

ν = γB₀/(2π)

ν is the Larmor frequency in Hz; γ is the gyromagnetic ratio in rad s⁻¹ T⁻¹; B₀ is the magnetic field in tesla. A 9.4 T magnet gives about 400 MHz for ¹H.

Shielding and chemical shift

Electrons around a nucleus circulate in B₀ and create a small opposing field. This shielding lowers the field felt by the nucleus: B_eff = B₀(1 − σ), where σ is the shielding constant. More shielding means a lower frequency, called upfield. Less shielding means a higher frequency, called downfield. The effect is tiny, so we report it relative to tetramethylsilane, TMS, whose 12 equivalent protons are strongly shielded. TMS is defined as δ = 0.

δ = (ν_sample − ν_TMS)/ν₀ × 10⁶

δ is the chemical shift in ppm; ν_sample and ν_TMS are signal frequencies in Hz; ν₀ is the spectrometer frequency in Hz. ppm cancels field strength, so δ is the same on 400 MHz and 600 MHz machines.

Typical ¹H shifts: alkyl 0.9–1.5 ppm, alkene 4.5–6.0, aromatic 6.5–8.0, aldehyde 9–10, carboxylic acid 10–12. Integration measures peak area, not height. The area is proportional to the number of equivalent protons, so a 3H methyl group has three times the area of a 1H CH proton. Exchangeable OH and NH protons are often broad and their area is less reliable.

Spin–spin splitting

Neighbouring non-equivalent protons split a signal. For n equivalent protons on adjacent atoms, the signal has n + 1 lines. The intensities follow Pascal's triangle: doublet 1:1, triplet 1:2:1, quartet 1:3:3:1. Equivalent protons do not split each other. The gap between lines is the coupling constant J, measured in Hz. It is independent of the spectrometer field; the same gap in ppm becomes smaller on a higher-field machine.

J (Hz) = Δδ (ppm) × ν₀ (MHz)

Δδ is line separation in ppm; ν₀ is spectrometer frequency in MHz. On a 400 MHz instrument, lines at 4.00 and 4.10 ppm are separated by 0.10 ppm, so J = 0.10 × 400 = 40 Hz.

  1. Count the number of signals: each set of equivalent protons gives one signal.
  2. Read δ from the centre of the signal.
  3. Integrate the area to get the proton count ratio.
  4. Apply n + 1 to each signal to find neighbouring equivalent protons.
  5. Measure J in Hz from the line spacing.

Instrument and applications

An NMR spectrometer has a superconducting magnet, a probe holding the sample, a radiofrequency transmitter, a receiver and a computer. The sample is dissolved in a deuterated solvent such as CDCl₃ or D₂O; the solvent gives its own weak signal, not the analyte. A small amount of TMS is added as the zero reference. Modern instruments use pulsed radiofrequency and Fourier transform, so a spectrum is acquired in seconds.

NMR identifies functional groups and connects atoms in a molecule. It confirms whether a synthesis worked, measures purity, follows reaction kinetics, and finds the tacticity of polymers. It also quantifies mixtures because area is proportional to proton count. NMR is less sensitive than UV-visible absorption, so it usually needs milligrams of sample.

Worked example

Question. A proton signal on a 400 MHz NMR spectrometer is 1600 Hz downfield from TMS. (a) Find its chemical shift in ppm. (b) A doublet has lines at 4.00 and 4.10 ppm. Find its coupling constant J in Hz.

  1. The spectrometer frequency is ν₀ = 400 MHz = 400 × 10⁶ Hz.
  2. Use δ = Δν/ν₀ × 10⁶ = 1600/(400 × 10⁶) × 10⁶.
  3. δ = 4.0 ppm.
  4. Line separation Δδ = 4.10 − 4.00 = 0.10 ppm.
  5. J = Δδ × ν₀ = 0.10 ppm × 400 MHz = 40 Hz.

Answer. δ = 4.0 ppm and J = 40 Hz.

Exam traps

  • Chemical shift δ is in ppm and does not depend on spectrometer frequency; a line separation in Hz does. Do not report δ in Hz.
  • Integration uses peak area, not peak height. A broad OH peak can look small but still contain one proton.
  • The n + 1 rule counts equivalent protons on neighbouring atoms only; equivalent protons on the same atom do not split each other.
  • Downfield means higher δ and more deshielding. Upfield means lower δ and more shielding; TMS is at 0.
  • Exchangeable OH and NH protons can be broad and may not show simple n + 1 splitting. Do not force a triplet or quartet on them.

2.7 Born-Oppenheimer approximation and Franck-Condon principle

The Born-Oppenheimer approximation says electrons move much faster than nuclei, so their motions can be treated separately. The Franck-Condon principle uses that separation: during an electronic absorption, the nuclei hardly move, so the transition is vertical on a potential-energy diagram. The chapter explains potential-energy curves, vibrational levels and the overlap of vibrational wavefunctions, and shows why UV-visible bands have vibrational structure. It is used to read band shapes in UV-visible spectra, to understand colour, and to interpret photochemical excitation before relaxation and reaction steps.

You need: Chapter 2.1 for photon energy, Chapter 2.3 for electronic transitions, and Chapter 2.4 for vibrational levels.

Why separate electrons from nuclei

An electron has mass 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg. A proton has mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, about 1836 times larger. In a molecule the nuclei move slowly compared with the electrons. When a photon excites an electron, the electron cloud changes in about 10⁻¹⁵ s, while a bond vibration takes about 10⁻¹³ s. The nuclei hardly move during the electronic jump.

Born and Oppenheimer turned this into a working approximation. Fix the nuclear positions R. Solve the electronic problem for those fixed nuclei. Add the nuclear repulsion to the electronic energy to get the total potential-energy curve U(R) that controls nuclear motion. R is the internuclear distance. The molecular wavefunction, with rotation ignored, is a product: Ψ ≈ ψ_elec(r;R) χ_nuc(R). Here r stands for electron coordinates, ψ_elec is the electronic wavefunction, and χ_nuc is the nuclear vibrational wavefunction. Rotation is not resolved here.

Ψ(r, R) ≈ ψ_elec(r; R) χ_nuc(R)

Ψ is the molecular wavefunction with rotation ignored; ψ_elec is the electronic part for fixed nuclei; χ_nuc is the nuclear vibrational part (rotation ignored here); r is the set of electron coordinates; R is the set of internuclear distances.

The electronic energy depends on R only as a parameter. At each R the electrons adjust instantly. The nuclei then move on the single curve U(R). This separation is the Born-Oppenheimer approximation. It fails when two electronic states come very close, but it works well for ordinary UV-visible absorption.

Potential-energy curves and vibrational levels

Every electronic state has its own potential-energy curve. The ground-state curve has a minimum at the equilibrium internuclear distance R_e. At shorter R the nuclei repel strongly and U rises. At longer R the curve flattens towards dissociation. The excited-state curve lies higher. Its minimum may sit at a different R_e, because the excited electron changes bonding.

Vibrational levels are quantized inside each curve. For a harmonic model, E_v = (v + ½)hν. v = 0, 1, 2, ... is the vibrational quantum number; ν is the vibrational frequency; h is the Planck constant. The lowest level is v = 0 with zero-point energy ½hν. At room temperature most molecules occupy v = 0. Each level is drawn as a horizontal line inside the well.

  • R_e is the internuclear distance at the minimum of a curve.
  • A vertical line keeps R fixed; a slanted line would let R change during the jump.
  • The excited molecule later relaxes to its own minimum, but that happens after absorption.

Franck-Condon principle and band shapes

The Franck-Condon principle says an electronic transition is fast compared with nuclear motion. On a U(R) diagram, draw the transition as a vertical line at fixed R. R does not change during absorption. The electronic state changes; the vibrational wavefunction does not have time to change.

For a given allowed electronic transition, the vibronic intensity is proportional to |μ_el|² |S|². μ_el is the electronic transition dipole moment. S is the overlap integral: S = ∫ χ_v'* χ_v'' dR. χ_v'' is the vibrational wavefunction of the lower electronic state, with quantum number v''. χ_v' is the vibrational wavefunction of the upper state, with v'. The Franck-Condon factor |S|² controls the pattern of vibrational line intensities. A large |S|² gives a strong line. A near-zero |S|² gives a weak line. When μ_el is nearly constant over the relevant R, |S|² alone sets the relative intensities.

Different v' levels give lines at slightly different photon energies. The complete UV-visible band is the envelope of these lines. If the two curves have the same R_e, the 0→0 line is strongest. If the excited curve is shifted, the vertical jump from R_e of the ground state lands where upper-state wavefunctions with v' > 0 have large amplitude. Then the band peaks at 0→2 or 0→3, for example. This is why band shapes carry vibrational information.

Worked example

Question. A C=O chromophore absorbs ultraviolet light at 280 nm. (a) Find the photon energy in electronvolts. (b) Explain why the electronic transition is drawn vertical on a potential-energy diagram.

  1. Convert wavelength to photon energy with E(eV) = 1240 / λ(nm). The constant 1240 is hc in eV nm.
  2. Put λ = 280 nm: E = 1240 / 280 = 4.4286 eV, which rounds to 4.43 eV.
  3. In joules, E = hc/λ = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s)(2.998 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹)/(280 × 10⁻⁹ m) = 7.10 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. The eV value is the usual UV-visible unit.
  4. The electronic jump takes about 10⁻¹⁵ s; a C=O vibration takes about 10⁻¹³ s. The nuclei are nearly fixed during absorption.
  5. Draw a vertical line at fixed internuclear distance from the ground curve to the excited curve. The molecule later relaxes to the excited-state minimum, but that is not the absorbing step.

Answer. The photon energy is 4.43 eV; the transition is vertical because the nuclei hardly move during the fast electronic jump.

Exam traps

  • Saying the nuclei move during absorption. In the Franck-Condon picture R is fixed for the jump; nuclear relaxation comes afterwards.
  • Confusing the vertical transition with the equilibrium geometry of the excited state. The vertical landing point is usually not the excited-state minimum.
  • Assuming the excited-state curve always has the same R_e as the ground state. A different R_e shifts the strongest vibrational line.
  • Forgetting that the overlap is between vibrational wavefunctions, not between electronic wavefunctions.
  • Using E = 1240/λ with λ in metres. The 1240 shortcut needs λ in nanometres to give eV.

2.8 Laws of photochemistry and quantum yield

Photochemistry starts with absorbed light. Two laws make this precise: Grotthuss–Draper says only light that a substance absorbs can change it, and Stark–Einstein says one absorbed photon activates one molecule in the primary act. Quantum yield Φ compares product molecules with absorbed photons. It can be below, equal to or far above one. The idea is used in photolithography, solar-fuel research, pollution control and actinometry, where light intensity is measured by a chemical reaction rather than a meter.

You need: photon energy and absorption from Chapter 2.1, electronic transitions from Chapter 2.3, and vertical transitions from Chapter 2.7.

The two laws and the primary act

Call the light entering the cell I₀, the light leaving it Iₜ, and the light taken up by the sample Iₐ. All three are intensities, in W m⁻² or photons m⁻² s⁻¹. For a cell of negligible reflection, Iₐ = I₀ − Iₜ. Only Iₐ is counted in photochemistry; Iₜ passes through and does nothing chemically.

Iₐ = I₀ − Iₜ

I₀ is the incident light intensity; Iₜ is the transmitted light intensity; Iₐ is the absorbed light intensity. Use the same unit for all three.

Grotthuss–Draper law: only radiation absorbed by a molecule can produce a photochemical change. Light of a wavelength that the molecule does not absorb gives no reaction, however intense it is.

Stark–Einstein law, or photochemical equivalence: each photon absorbed in the primary act activates one molecule. One photon, one molecule—not one photon, many molecules. The primary act is A + hν → A*, where A is a molecule, hν is one photon of energy hν, and A* is an electronically excited molecule. The star marks excitation, not a charge.

The excited molecule then enters a secondary process. It may form product, lose energy as heat or light, react with a solvent, or start a chain. The primary act is fixed by light; the secondary steps are ordinary chemical kinetics. Some syllabi call these two statements the laws of photochemistry.

Quantum yield: below, equal and above one

Quantum yield Φ is the number of product molecules formed per photon absorbed. For a reaction that consumes a reactant, count reactant molecules consumed instead of product molecules. Use the same counting unit above and below the line.

Φ = N(product) / N(photons absorbed)

N(product) is the amount of product formed, in molecules or moles; N(photons absorbed) is the amount of light absorbed, in photons or moles of photons. One mole of photons is one einstein.

Φ = 3.0 means three product molecules per absorbed photon. It is dimensionless, not a percentage.

  • Φ < 1: the excited molecule loses energy by fluorescence, heat, or collisional quenching before it reacts. Fewer product molecules form than photons absorbed.
  • Φ = 1: every absorbed photon gives one product molecule. This is the simple case the Stark–Einstein law suggests.
  • Φ > 1: one photon starts a chain reaction. The hydrogen–chlorine reaction is the standard example; one initial excited molecule can lead to many product molecules, so Φ can be thousands. A quenching step lowers Φ.
  • Φ is not a rate. It is a count ratio. The rate of product formation also depends on light intensity, concentration, and temperature; see Unit 3.

Measuring photons: actinometry

Actinometry measures photon dose with a chemical reaction of known quantum yield. The standard is a chemical actinometer, such as ferrioxalate, chosen to absorb at the same wavelength as the experiment.

N(photons) = n(product, actinometer) / Φ(actinometer)

N(photons) is the amount of light absorbed, in moles of photons; n(product, actinometer) is the moles of product formed by the actinometer; Φ(actinometer) is its known quantum yield.

  1. Fill the same cell with actinometer solution; use the same wavelength and geometry as the sample.
  2. Irradiate for a measured time and measure the moles of actinometer product.
  3. Divide by the known Φ to get the moles of photons absorbed.
  4. Run the sample under identical conditions; measure product and calculate Φ(sample) = n(product, sample) / N(photons).

Same cell, same wavelength, same path length: these make the absorbed fraction comparable. Use Beer–Lambert from Chapter 2.2 if the absorbance differs. A power meter measures incident power; actinometry counts absorbed photons that actually react.

Worked example

Question. A photochemical reaction absorbs 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol of photons and forms 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol of product. Find the quantum yield.

  1. Write the definition: Φ = n(product) / n(photons absorbed).
  2. Put n(product) = 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol and n(photons absorbed) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol.
  3. Divide: Φ = 6.0 × 10⁻⁵ / 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 3.0.
  4. The mole units cancel, so Φ is dimensionless. A value 3.0 means 3.0 product molecules per absorbed photon.

Answer. Φ = 3.0, so the reaction forms 3.0 molecules of product per absorbed photon.

Exam traps

  • Using incident light I₀ in place of absorbed light Iₐ. Transmitted light does not react.
  • Assuming Φ can never exceed 1. Chain reactions give Φ > 1; quenching gives Φ < 1.
  • Mixing up primary and secondary processes. Stark–Einstein fixes one photon per activated molecule in the primary act, not one photon per final product.
  • Forgetting that quantum yield is dimensionless but needs matching moles or molecules in numerator and denominator. Do not divide moles of product by a photon count.
  • In actinometry, using the unknown reaction's Φ to count photons. Count photons with the standard actinometer first.

The excited molecule may lose energy by fluorescence, heat, or quenching before it forms product, so fewer product molecules form than photons absorbed.

Apply A system absorbs 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol of photons and forms 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol of product. Find Φ. 4 marks

Φ = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ / 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.25.

Analyze A reaction has Φ = 50. Does this violate the Stark–Einstein law? Explain. 5 marks

No. The primary act still uses one photon to activate one molecule. A secondary chain reaction then makes many product molecules per initial excited molecule.

Understand Distinguish absorbed light from incident light in a photochemical experiment. 3 marks

Incident light I₀ enters the cell. Absorbed light Iₐ is the part taken up by the sample, Iₐ = I₀ − Iₜ; only Iₐ drives reaction.

Apply An actinometer with Φ = 0.80 forms 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol of product. How many moles of photons were absorbed? 4 marks

N(photons) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ / 0.80 = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ mol of photons.

Analyze Why should the sample and the actinometer use the same cell and wavelength? 5 marks

The same cell and wavelength give the same geometry and comparable absorbance, so the actinometer's photon count applies to the sample experiment.

2.9 Jablonski diagram: fluorescence and phosphorescence

A Jablonski diagram is a map of what happens after a molecule absorbs light. It places the ground singlet S₀, first excited singlet S₁ and first triplet T₁ as horizontal energy levels, with small vibrational steps on each. The molecule loses extra energy as heat, then emits light either as fast fluorescence from S₁ or slow phosphorescence from T₁. Only a few universities teach this. It explains glow-in-the-dark stars, fluorescent dyes in biology and oxygen sensors.

You need: UV-visible absorption and chromophores, the Franck-Condon vertical transition, and quantum yield.

Singlet and triplet levels

Draw energy upward. S₀ is the ground singlet: all electron spins paired, total spin S = 0, multiplicity 2S + 1 = 1. S₁ is the first excited singlet. T₁ is the first triplet: one electron has flipped spin, S = 1, multiplicity 3. A triplet lies lower than the singlet of the same electron configuration because exchange repulsion is smaller. The superscript 0 marks the lowest vibrational level; higher vibrational levels are drawn as close lines above each electronic level.

Absorption from S₀ goes vertically to a higher vibrational level of S₁ or above, following the Franck-Condon principle. The molecule then relaxes vibrationally to the bottom of S₁ in picoseconds. Kasha's rule says emission normally starts from the lowest vibrational level of the lowest excited state of that multiplicity. So fluorescence is S₁ → S₀ and phosphorescence is T₁ → S₀. The 0–0 gap is the energy between S₀⁰ and S₁⁰; absorption usually reaches above it, so the band is broad.

Four competing routes

After absorption, four routes compete. Vibrational relaxation dumps vibrational energy into the surroundings as heat and is very fast. Internal conversion moves the molecule from S₁ to S₀ without a photon, keeping spin unchanged. Fluorescence is the radiative S₁→S₀ jump; it is spin allowed and typically takes 10⁻⁹ to 10⁻⁷ s. Intersystem crossing changes singlet to triplet; it needs spin–orbit coupling and is slower. Phosphorescence is the radiative T₁→S₀ jump. Because it changes spin, it is forbidden and slow, with lifetimes from microseconds to seconds. In a dark room, phosphorescence outlasts the exciting lamp; fluorescence stops at once. A heavy atom near the chromophore increases spin–orbit coupling, raises k_ISC and makes phosphorescence easier to see. In a rigid glass at 77 K, collisions are slow, so phosphorescence lasts longer than in a fluid solution at room temperature.

Φ_F = k_F / (k_F + k_IC + k_ISC + k_q[Q])

Φ_F is the fluorescence quantum yield; k_F, k_IC and k_ISC are rate constants in s⁻¹ for fluorescence, internal conversion and intersystem crossing; k_q is the quenching rate constant and [Q] is quencher concentration. The observed lifetime is τ = 1/(k_F + k_IC + k_ISC + k_q[Q]).

Stokes shift

Emission always has lower energy than absorption, so it appears at longer wavelength. The difference in wavenumber is the Stokes shift. Wavenumber is proportional to photon energy: E = hcν̃, where E is photon energy, h is Planck's constant, c is the speed of light and ν̃ is wavenumber in cm⁻¹. For a dye absorbing at 350 nm and emitting at 450 nm, the shift is 6 349 cm⁻¹ (worked below). This loss comes from vibrational relaxation before emission and solvent rearrangement. When spectra are plotted, absorption and emission often look roughly mirror images, with a gap between their maxima. A large shift helps separate excitation light from emitted light in a fluorescence microscope: filters pass 350 nm in and detect 450 nm out. The emitted photon therefore carries less energy than the absorbed photon; the missing energy becomes heat. The shift is normally reported in cm⁻¹, not nm, because energy differences add correctly in wavenumber.

Worked example

Question. A fluorophore absorbs at 350 nm and emits at 450 nm. Find the Stokes shift in cm⁻¹.

  1. 1. Convert absorption wavelength to wavenumber: ν̃_abs = 10⁷ nm cm⁻¹ / 350 nm = 28 571 cm⁻¹.
  2. 2. Convert emission wavelength: ν̃_em = 10⁷ / 450 = 22 222 cm⁻¹.
  3. 3. Stokes shift = ν̃_abs − ν̃_em = 28 571 − 22 222 = 6 349 cm⁻¹.

Answer. The Stokes shift is 6 349 cm⁻¹.

Exam traps

  • Phosphorescence is not slow fluorescence: it starts from triplet T₁ and changes spin; fluorescence starts from singlet S₁ and keeps spin.
  • Emission is at longer wavelength, so a 350 nm absorption cannot give a 300 nm emission from the same chromophore.
  • Intersystem crossing is a spin change; internal conversion keeps multiplicity.
  • Kasha's rule refers to emission from the lowest vibrational level of the excited state, not from the level first populated by absorption.

Takeaways

  1. A molecule absorbs light only when the photon energy hν matches the gap between two of its own energy levels, so each technique sees a different kind of gap.
  2. Beer–Lambert makes colour measurement quantitative: absorbance is a straight-line function of concentration, which is why colorimeters work.
  3. In UV-visible spectroscopy the gap is electronic, so conjugation and chromophores decide both the wavelength and how strongly the sample absorbs.
  4. IR spectroscopy reports vibrations, so functional groups can be read off a spectrum in their characteristic wavenumber ranges.
  5. Microwave lines of a rigid rotor are equally spaced by 2B, which turns a spectrum into a moment of inertia and then a bond length.
  6. NMR converts chemical environment into chemical shift and the number of neighbours into splitting, giving a map of the carbon skeleton and its hydrogens.
  7. Born–Oppenheimer and Franck–Condon together explain why electronic bands are broad and carry vibrational structure.
  8. Photochemistry begins only after a photon is absorbed, and quantum yield tells you how many product molecules one absorbed photon actually delivers.
  9. The Jablonski diagram sorts out the competing fates of an excited molecule and explains why phosphorescence is slow and long-lived.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare UV-visible, infrared and NMR spectroscopy as tools for identifying an unknown organic liquid. What information does each supply, and in what order would you use them? 14 marks

Glossary

Anisotropic shielding
Shielding of a nucleus that depends on orientation, as in aromatic rings where ring current shifts protons downfield.
Calibration curve
A plot of absorbance against known concentration, used to read off an unknown concentration.
Chain reaction
A self-propagating sequence of steps in which one photon produces many product molecules, giving quantum yield above one.
Colorimeter
An instrument that compares the colour intensity of a solution with a standard using a fixed wavelength filter.
Degrees of freedom
The number of independent ways a molecule can move; 3N total, giving 3N − 6 vibrational modes for a non-linear molecule.
Downfield shift
A larger chemical shift (higher δ), meaning the nucleus is less shielded and resonates at higher frequency.
Electromagnetic spectrum
The full range of radiation from gamma rays to radio waves, all travelling at c in vacuum and differing only in frequency.
Fingerprint region
The 1400–600 cm⁻¹ part of an IR spectrum, rich in coupled modes and useful for confirming identity.
Frequency
Number of wave cycles passing a point per second, measured in hertz (Hz).
Internal conversion
A non-radiative transition between electronic states of the same spin multiplicity.
Intersystem crossing
A non-radiative change of spin state, for example from an excited singlet to a triplet.
Molar absorptivity
The absorbance of a 1 mol L⁻¹ solution in a 1 cm cell; a measure of how strongly a species absorbs at a given wavelength.
Moment of inertia
I = μr² for a diatomic molecule, with r the bond length; it fixes the rotational energy levels.
n → π* transition
Excitation of a lone-pair electron to a π* orbital; weak and at longer wavelength than π → π*.
Quenching
Loss of excitation energy through collision or energy transfer to another molecule, reducing emission intensity.
Red shift
A move of the absorption maximum to longer wavelength and lower energy, usually due to conjugation or an auxochrome.
Reduced mass
μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂), the effective mass used in the vibration and rotation of a diatomic molecule.
Spectrophotometer
An instrument that measures absorbance as a function of wavelength using a monochromator or grating.
Spectroscopy
The study of how matter absorbs or emits electromagnetic radiation, used to deduce structure and measure concentration.
Stokes shift
The energy difference between the absorption and emission maxima, caused by vibrational relaxation in the excited state.
Transmittance
The fraction T = I/I₀ of incident light that passes through the sample, often quoted as a percentage.
Wavelength
Distance between two successive crests of a wave, usually in nm for UV-visible and in μm or cm⁻¹ for infrared.
π → π* transition
Excitation of an electron from a bonding π orbital to an antibonding π* orbital, typically strong and in the UV region.
