3.1 Thermodynamic functions: enthalpy, entropy and free energy

Energy changes are easy to measure and easy to misread. A reaction that releases heat can still refuse to happen. Enthalpy counts heat at constant pressure, entropy counts spreading, and free energy combines both so its sign decides direction. Industry sizes reactors and boilers with these functions; electrochemistry predicts cell voltage from free energy.

You need school-level mole concept, stoichiometry, and balanced chemical equations.

System, work and enthalpy

A system is what you study; surroundings are the rest. A closed system exchanges heat, not matter. An open system exchanges both. An isolated system exchanges neither. One visible phase is homogeneous; two or more is heterogeneous.

ΔU = q + w

ΔU = internal-energy change (J); q = heat added to system (J); w = work done on system (J).

q is positive when heat enters; w is positive when work is done on the system. U is a state function: it depends on present state, not route. q and w are path functions; q + w is fixed.

Most reactions run in open vessels at constant pressure, where expansion work PV is hidden inside enthalpy.

H = U + PV

H = enthalpy (J); U = internal energy (J); P = pressure (Pa); V = volume (m³).

At constant pressure q_p = ΔH. Heat capacity is C = q/ΔT in J K⁻¹; at constant pressure q_p = C_p ΔT, so C_p = ΔH/ΔT.

Entropy and the second law

The first law allows every energy-balanced change. The second law picks direction: total entropy of system plus surroundings never decreases in a spontaneous change.

ΔS = q_rev / T

ΔS = entropy change (J K⁻¹); q_rev = reversible heat (J); T = kelvin temperature.

Entropy measures how widely energy spreads. Melting, boiling, and raising gas molecules increase entropy. Molar units: J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Standard entropies S° are tabulated at 298 K and 1 bar.

ΔS° = ΣS°(products) − ΣS°(reactants)

ΔS° = standard entropy change (J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹); S° = molar standard entropy.

ΔH° = ΣΔH°f(products) − ΣΔH°f(reactants)

ΔH° = standard enthalpy change (J); ΔH°f = standard enthalpy of formation (J mol⁻¹).

Bond enthalpies give rough answers; formation enthalpies give accurate ones. Rates are separate; see 3.2.

Free energy decides spontaneity

G = H − TS

G = Gibbs free energy (J); H = enthalpy (J); T = kelvin; S = entropy (J K⁻¹).

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS

ΔG = free-energy change (J); at constant T and P, its sign decides direction.

ΔG < 0 means the forward reaction goes; ΔG > 0 means it does not go and the reverse does. ΔG = 0 means equilibrium. Under standard conditions ΔG° = 0 gives K = 1.

R = 8.314 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹; K = equilibrium constant; large negative ΔG° means products dominate.

Free energy and EMF

Only a few universities teach this bridge inside thermodynamics. Cell construction stays in Unit 4.

ΔG° = −nFE°

n = moles electrons; F = 96500 C mol⁻¹; E° = standard cell potential (V); ΔG° in J.

E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q

Q = reaction quotient. At 298 K, 2.303RT/F = 0.0592 V (and RT/F = 0.0257 V), so E = E° − (0.0592/n) log₁₀ Q.

Negative ΔG° gives positive E°; positive E drives current. Reduction potentials are in Unit 4.

Worked example

Question. Zn(s)+Cu²⁺(aq)→Zn²⁺(aq)+Cu(s): ΔG°=−212 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K. Find E° and E when [Zn²⁺]=0.10 M, [Cu²⁺]=1.0 M. n=2, F=96500 C mol⁻¹. (5 marks)

Exam traps

  • Predicting from ΔH alone. ΔG decides.
  • Using °C in TΔS; T must be kelvin.
  • Mixing kJ and J in ΔH − TΔS; convert first.
  • Writing Q upside down; Q = products/reactants, each raised to its coefficient.
  • Using ΔG° when concentrations are nonstandard; Nernst gives E.

3.2 Reaction kinetics: rate laws and order of reaction

Reaction kinetics asks how fast a reaction goes. The rate is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. A rate law turns measured concentrations into an order and a rate constant. Experiment gives the order; the balanced equation does not. Industry uses these numbers to size reactors, set shelf lives for medicines, and predict how fast pollutants break down.

You need: Chapter 3.1 for energy changes, and basic concentration units such as mol L⁻¹.

Rate, rate law and order

For a reactant A, average rate = −Δ[A]/Δt. [A] is concentration in mol L⁻¹, t is time in s, and the minus sign makes the rate positive because A is used up. For a reactant, instantaneous rate = −(slope of the tangent to the concentration-time curve); the slope itself is negative. This value is used in rate laws.

For A + B → products, a common rate law is rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ. Here k is the rate constant, m is the order in A, and n is the order in B. The overall order is m + n. Orders are measured, usually small whole numbers or zero; they can be fractional. The order in a reactant is not its stoichiometric coefficient unless the reaction is a single elementary step.

rate = k[A]m[B]n

rate is in mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹; k is the rate constant; [A] and [B] are concentrations in mol L⁻¹; m and n are the orders in A and B.

Molecularity is the number of reactant species that take part in one elementary step: 1 for a unimolecular step, 2 for a bimolecular step. It is a mechanism count and is always a whole number. Order is sometimes called reaction order. Molecularity applies to one elementary step and has no meaning for an overall reaction. For an elementary step, order and molecularity often match.

Overall orderUnits of k
0mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹
1s⁻¹
2L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹

Zero, first and second order

Zero order: rate = k. Concentration falls linearly: [A] = [A]₀ − kt. [A]₀ is initial concentration. A zero-order reactant can still appear in the balanced equation; something else limits the rate, such as a catalyst surface.

First order: rate = k[A]. The integrated law is ln([A]₀/[A]) = kt. Rearranged, [A] = [A]₀ e−kt, equivalently [A] = [A]₀ exp(−kt); here k is in s⁻¹ and t is in s. The half-life t½ is the time for [A] to fall to [A]₀/2: t½ = 0.693/k. It does not depend on [A]₀.

ln([A]₀/[A]) = kt

[A]₀ is the initial concentration, [A] is the concentration at time t, k is the first-order rate constant in s⁻¹, and t is time in s.

Second order in one reactant: rate = k[A]². The integrated law is 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt, and t½ = 1/(k[A]₀).

Finding order and the temperature effect

Use the method of initial rates. Run trials with one concentration changed at a time and measure the rate at the start. For two trials, compare rate and concentration. Take logarithms to solve for the order. Repeat for each reactant. Then k = rate/([A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ).

rate₂/rate₁ = ([A]₂/[A]₁)m

subscripts 1 and 2 label two trials; m is the order in A.

Order can also be found graphically: plot [A] against t, ln[A] against t, and 1/[A] against t; the plot that is a straight line gives the order (0, 1 and 2 respectively), and the slope gives k.

Temperature changes k through the Arrhenius equation. A larger activation energy Ea makes k more sensitive to temperature. Near room temperature, many reactions roughly double k for a 10 K rise, but use the equation, not the rule.

k = A e−Ea/RT

A is the pre-exponential factor with the same units as k; Ea is the activation energy in J mol⁻¹; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is temperature in K.

Worked example

Question. For A + B → products, the initial rates are: Trial 1, [A]₀ = 0.10 M, [B]₀ = 0.10 M, rate = 2.0 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹; Trial 2, [A]₀ = 0.20 M, [B]₀ = 0.10 M, rate = 8.0 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹; Trial 3, [A]₀ = 0.10 M, [B]₀ = 0.20 M, rate = 2.0 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹. Find the order in A, the order in B, the overall order and k.

  1. Compare Trials 1 and 2. [B]₀ is fixed at 0.10 M. [A]₀ changes from 0.10 M to 0.20 M, so [A]₂/[A]₁ = 0.20/0.10 = 2. The rate changes from 2.0 × 10⁻³ to 8.0 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹, so rate₂/rate₁ = 8.0/2.0 = 4.
  2. Solve for the order in A: 4 = 2ᵐ, so m = 2.
  3. Compare Trials 1 and 3. [A]₀ is fixed at 0.10 M. [B]₀ changes from 0.10 M to 0.20 M, so [B]₃/[B]₁ = 2. The rate stays 2.0 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹, so rate₃/rate₁ = 2.0/2.0 = 1.
  4. Solve for the order in B: 1 = 2ⁿ, so n = 0.
  5. Overall order = m + n = 2 + 0 = 2.
  6. Find k from Trial 1: k = rate/([A]₀²[B]₀⁰) = (2.0 × 10⁻³ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹)/(0.10 mol L⁻¹)² = 0.20 L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹.

Answer. The order in A is 2, the order in B is 0, the overall order is 2, and k = 0.20 L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹.

Exam traps

  • Assuming order equals the stoichiometric coefficients in the balanced equation. Order is experimental; coefficients belong to the balanced equation.
  • Forgetting that a zero-order reactant can still appear in the balanced equation. Its concentration does not affect the rate in that range.
  • Using t½ = 0.693/k for every reaction. This half-life formula is only for first order.
  • Dropping the units of k. The units change with overall order: s⁻¹ for first order, L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ for second order.
  • Mixing average rate and instantaneous rate, or missing the minus sign for a reactant.

3.3 Reaction mechanisms; kinetic versus thermodynamic control

A reaction mechanism is the step-by-step path from reactants to products. Two routes from the same reactant can give two different products. The faster route has the lower activation energy and gives the kinetic product. The more stable product has the lower Gibbs free energy and is the thermodynamic product. The fastest product is not always the most stable one. Temperature decides which product dominates. Chemists use this choice when setting conditions for a synthesis, testing drug stability, or controlling a polymer process.

You need 3.1 for Gibbs free energy and 3.2 for rate laws and rate constants.

Mechanism, elementary steps and the rate-determining step

A mechanism lists the elementary steps from reactants to products. An elementary step is a single collision or bond change. Its molecularity is the number of species that take part in that step: one is unimolecular, two is bimolecular, three is termolecular and rare. Molecularity is defined only for an elementary step. The overall order comes from experiment, as in 3.2.

The rate-determining step (RDS) is the slowest step in the sequence. It controls the overall rate. If one step has a much higher barrier than the others, treat it as the RDS.

An intermediate forms and is then consumed; it does not appear in the overall equation. For a short-lived intermediate I, the steady-state approximation sets its rate of change to about zero:

d[I]/dt ≈ 0

[I] is the concentration of the intermediate I at time t; the approximation holds when I is consumed as fast as it forms.

This turns a mechanism into an algebraic rate law. The approximation fails if the intermediate accumulates. A mechanism is accepted only if its rate law matches measured data and it explains the products. Trapping an intermediate, by low temperature or a fast reagent, gives extra evidence.

Energy profile and activation energy

An energy profile plots enthalpy or Gibbs free energy against reaction coordinate. The reaction coordinate is a progress variable from reactants to products. The top of the curve is the transition state, not an intermediate. Activation energy Ea is the height from reactants to the transition state. It is a kinetic quantity: it sets the rate.

k = A exp(−Ea/RT)

k is the rate constant; A is the pre-exponential factor; Ea is the activation energy in J mol⁻¹; R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹; T is temperature in K.

A small Ea gives a large k. A large Ea gives a small k. The product energy level is a thermodynamic quantity. A lower product level means greater stability, that is, lower Gibbs free energy. Lower Ea does not imply a more stable product.

The figure shows the route to B with Ea = 60 kJ mol⁻¹. The route to C has Ea = 90 kJ mol⁻¹. C ends 20 kJ mol⁻¹ below B. At ordinary conditions B forms faster; C is the more stable product.

Kinetic versus thermodynamic control

Kinetic control means the product distribution is set by relative rates. The kinetic product forms faster because its route has the lower Ea. Thermodynamic control means the distribution is set by equilibrium. The thermodynamic product is more stable because it has the lower Gibbs free energy. The two controls agree only by accident.

At low temperature, the factor exp(−Ea/RT) changes steeply with Ea, so the lower-barrier route dominates. At high temperature, both rates become large, and if the products can interconvert, equilibrium gives the more stable product.

A catalyst lowers Ea by providing a new path. It speeds the reaction but does not change the equilibrium constant. A catalyst can favour a kinetic product if it lowers only that route. It cannot make a thermodynamically unfavourable product stable.

Trapping a reactive intermediate means capturing it before it reacts further. Low temperature, dilution, or a fast added reagent can do this. Trapping proves the intermediate exists and can stop a reaction at a chosen stage. In practice, temperature and catalyst are the two handles used to choose B or C.

Worked example

Question. Two parallel first-order routes start from A. Route A → B has Ea(B) = 60 kJ mol⁻¹. Route A → C has Ea(C) = 90 kJ mol⁻¹. Product C is 20 kJ mol⁻¹ more stable than B. The pre-exponential factors are similar. (a) Which product is favoured at low temperature? (b) Which at high temperature? (c) Name the control in each case.

  1. Compare the barriers: Ea(B) = 60 kJ mol⁻¹ and Ea(C) = 90 kJ mol⁻¹, so B has the lower barrier.
  2. At low temperature, k = A exp(−Ea/RT). The lower Ea gives the larger k, so B forms faster and B dominates.
  3. Compare stability: C is 20 kJ mol⁻¹ more stable than B, so C is the thermodynamic product.
  4. At high temperature, both rates are large. If B and C can interconvert, equilibrium favours the more stable product C.
  5. Low temperature: kinetic control gives B. High temperature: thermodynamic control gives C.

Answer. At low temperature B is favoured by kinetic control because Ea(B) = 60 kJ mol⁻¹ is lower than Ea(C) = 90 kJ mol⁻¹; at high temperature C is favoured by thermodynamic control because C is 20 kJ mol⁻¹ more stable than B.

Exam traps

  • Lower Ea means faster, not more stable. A low barrier is kinetic; a low product energy is thermodynamic.
  • The kinetic product is not always impossible to isolate. It is less stable than the thermodynamic product, but a high barrier can stop conversion.
  • A catalyst does not change the equilibrium constant. It changes the path and the rate.
  • Molecularity belongs to an elementary step. Overall order is experimental and can be zero, fractional, or negative.
  • The steady-state approximation is for reactive intermediates, not for reactants or final products.

3.4 Phase rule: phase, component and degree of freedom

The phase rule counts how many intensive variables a system can change before a phase disappears. A phase is a distinct homogeneous part of the system; a component is the smallest set of chemical species that describes every phase; a degree of freedom is one independent intensive variable, such as temperature or pressure. Gibbs wrote this count as F = C − P + 2. The rule is used to read phase diagrams, to check whether a reported equilibrium is even possible, and to decide how many phases an alloy or a salt can hold at equilibrium.

You need: state functions and free energy from Chapter 3.1, plus basic chemical equilibrium (K and extent of reaction) from first-year chemistry.

Phase, component and degree of freedom

A phase is a physically distinct, homogeneous part of a system. Ice floating in water gives two phases, one solid and one liquid. Gases mix completely, so a gas mixture is always one phase. Ethanol and water form one liquid phase; oil and water form two.

A component is the smallest number of independent chemical species needed to describe the composition of every phase. Pure water has C = 1 even though H₂O is slightly ionised. Ice, liquid water and dissolved sugar together have C = 2, water and sugar.

The degree of freedom F is the number of intensive variables — temperature, pressure, and the composition of each phase — that you can change independently without a phase appearing or disappearing. F = 0 is invariance: the phases coexist at one fixed set of values only, as at the triple point of water.

Deriving F = C − P + 2

Count the intensive variables. Temperature and pressure give 2. Inside one phase the mole fractions add up to 1, so only C − 1 composition variables are independent; with P phases that is P(C − 1).

Count the constraints. At equilibrium the chemical potential of a component is the same in every phase that contains it. One component spread over P phases gives P − 1 equations, so C components give C(P − 1) equations.

F = [P(C − 1) + 2] − [C(P − 1)] = PC − P + 2 − CP + C = C − P + 2

F is the number of degrees of freedom; C is the number of components; P is the number of phases present; the 2 counts the non-composition intensive variables, normally temperature T and pressure p.

This is the Gibbs phase rule. Solids and liquids change very little with pressure, so pressure is usually held fixed at 1 atm. One variable is then lost and the reduced phase rule applies:

F = C − P + 1

F is the degrees of freedom at constant pressure; C is the number of components; P is the number of phases. Only a few universities teach this derivation in full; most use the result directly.

Counting C when the species react

Species you can write down are not the same as components. Each independent reaction links the species and cuts the count:

C = S − R

C is the number of components; S is the number of chemical species present; R is the number of independent equilibrium reactions, meaning no reaction is a sum of the others. For H₂, I₂ and HI in one gas phase, S = 3 and the single reaction H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI gives R = 1, so C = 2 and F = 2 − 1 + 2 = 3.

Charged species need one more cut. Electroneutrality, or a fixed ratio such as [H⁺] = [OH⁻], is an extra restriction a:

C = S − R − a

a is the number of extra restrictions such as electroneutrality. For pure water, S = 3 (H₂O, H⁺, OH⁻), R = 1 and a = 1, so C = 3 − 1 − 1 = 1.

Worked example

Question. A closed vessel holds CaCO₃(s), CaO(s) and CO₂(g) at equilibrium: CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g). Find S, R, C, P and F, then find F when the pressure is fixed at 1 bar.

  1. Write the species: CaCO₃, CaO and CO₂, so S = 3.
  2. Count independent reactions: only the decomposition, so R = 1.
  3. C = S − R − a = 3 − 1 − 0 = 2, since no ions are present (a = 0).
  4. Count phases: solid CaCO₃, solid CaO and gaseous CO₂. The two solids do not dissolve in each other, so they stay separate phases: P = 3.
  5. Gibbs rule: F = C − P + 2 = 2 − 3 + 2 = 1.
  6. At fixed pressure: F = C − P + 1 = 2 − 3 + 1 = 0, so the decomposition happens at one temperature only.

Answer. C = 2, P = 3 and F = 1 (pick the temperature, and the equilibrium CO₂ pressure follows); at 1 bar, F = 0.

Exam traps

  • Counting species as components: for CaCO₃ ⇌ CaO + CO₂ the answer is C = 3 − 1 = 2, not 3.
  • Using F = C − P + 2 out of habit after fixing the pressure. Fixed pressure means the reduced rule, C − P + 1.
  • Treating every gas as its own phase. A gas mixture is one phase, while two solids that do not dissolve in each other are two.
  • Missing the electroneutrality cut in ionic systems, which would wrongly give water C = 3 instead of C = 1.

3.5 One component system: water

Water can exist as ice, liquid water or vapour. A pressure–temperature phase diagram maps which phase is stable, where two phases coexist, and where all three meet. The phase rule gives a simple freedom count; the diagram gives the shape. Engineers use it for steam generation, refrigeration, freeze-drying and weather models. Only a few universities teach the full diagram, so treat this as an advanced sketch.

You need: the phase rule from Chapter 3.4.

Three regions, three curves

Water is a one-component system: the only component is H₂O. A phase is a physically distinct part, so ice, liquid water and vapour are three phases. Plot pressure p on the y-axis and temperature T on the x-axis. Ice is stable at low T and high p; liquid water at higher T and high p; vapour at low p or high T. Inside a region one phase exists, and T and p can change independently over a small range.

Three curves separate the regions. The sublimation curve runs to the triple point; ice and vapour coexist. The vaporization curve runs from the triple point to the critical point; liquid and vapour coexist. The fusion curve runs from the triple point upward; ice and liquid coexist. The triple point is 273.16 K and 611 Pa, where all three phases coexist. The critical point is 647 K and 22.1 MPa. Above it liquid and vapour cannot be told apart.

Freedom from the phase rule

At equilibrium the phase rule is F = C − P + 2. F is the number of degrees of freedom: independent intensive variables you can change without losing a phase. C is the number of components. P is the number of phases. For water C = 1.

Inside a region P = 1, so F = 1 − 1 + 2 = 2. You can change T and p a little and stay in the same region. On a boundary curve P = 2, so F = 1 − 2 + 2 = 1. Choose T and the curve fixes p, or choose p and it fixes T. At the triple point P = 3, so F = 1 − 3 + 2 = 0. Pressure and temperature are fixed at 273.16 K and 611 Pa until one phase disappears. The same count explains why a boiling point needs a stated pressure: at 1 atm water boils at 373 K.

Why the fusion curve leans left

The fusion curve of water leans left, not right. Its slope is negative: raising pressure lowers the melting point. The Clapeyron equation gives the slope: dp/dT = ΔH_fus/(T ΔV_fus). ΔH_fus is the enthalpy of fusion, T is absolute temperature, and ΔV_fus is the volume change when ice melts. For ice to water, volume decreases, so ΔV_fus is negative. The enthalpy change is positive, so dp/dT is negative.

Most substances expand on melting, so their fusion curve leans right. Ice is unusual because its open hydrogen-bonded structure collapses on melting. A pressure increase therefore favours the denser liquid phase. The effect is small: ice melts slightly below 273.15 K under moderate pressure. This negative slope is a favourite exam point. Do not call 273.15 K and 1 atm the triple point; that is the normal melting point. The triple point is 273.16 K and 611 Pa.

Worked example

Question. Water has a triple point at 273.16 K and 611 Pa. Find F at the triple point and along the liquid-vapour curve.

  1. Identify the component: C = 1, because only H₂O is present.
  2. At the triple point, P = 3: ice, liquid and vapour coexist.
  3. Apply F = C − P + 2 = 1 − 3 + 2 = 0.
  4. Along the liquid-vapour curve, P = 2: liquid and vapour coexist.
  5. Apply F = C − P + 2 = 1 − 2 + 2 = 1.

Answer. F = 0 at the triple point and F = 1 along the liquid-vapour curve.

Exam traps

  • Calling 273.15 K and 1 atm the triple point. That is the normal melting point; the triple point is 273.16 K and 611 Pa.
  • Using pressure p as P in F = C − P + 2. P is the number of phases.
  • Saying the fusion curve has a positive slope. For water it is negative because ice is less dense than liquid water.
  • Forgetting that above the critical point liquid and vapour are one phase, not two.
  • Mixing up sublimation and vaporization: sublimation is solid to vapour, below the triple point.

3.6 Two component systems: Pb-Ag system and reduced phase rule

For alloys, pressure is fixed at 1 atm, so the phase rule loses one variable. The reduced phase rule F = C − P + 1 counts the intensive degrees of freedom left. The lead–silver system is the standard simple eutectic: two metals, no compound, one lowest melting point. Foundries use such diagrams to choose pouring temperatures; solder and fuse alloys sit near eutectics.

You need phase rule terms and one-component diagrams.

The reduced phase rule

At constant pressure, the ordinary phase rule F = C − P + 2 becomes F = C − P + 1. C is the number of components; P is the number of phases; F is the number of intensive variables (temperature, composition) you can change without losing a phase. Fixing pressure removes one degree of freedom.

F = C − P + 1

F is the reduced degrees of freedom; C is the number of components (2 for Pb and Ag); P is the number of phases present.

For Pb–Ag, C = 2. One liquid phase gives P = 1, so F = 2: temperature and composition may change. Two phases give F = 1. Three phases give F = 0. At zero freedom the temperature and all phase compositions are fixed; this is the eutectic point.

The Pb–Ag diagram

The diagram plots temperature T in °C against mass% Ag. Pure Pb melts at 327 °C; pure Ag at 961 °C. The two liquidus lines meet at the eutectic: 303 °C and 2.5 mass% Ag. Above the liquidus is one liquid. Left of 2.5% is primary Pb plus liquid; right is primary Ag plus liquid. Below 303 °C are two solids, Pb and Ag.

At the eutectic, liquid ↔ solid Pb + solid Ag. A tie line at 303 °C joins the two solid compositions (pure Pb and pure Ag) and the liquid at 2.5% Ag. The lever rule gives the amount of each phase: mass of phase = (opposite segment length ÷ total tie-line length) × total mass.

Alloys below 2.5% Ag are hypo-eutectic; above are hyper-eutectic. A 5% Ag alloy is hyper-eutectic, so primary Ag appears first on cooling.

Cooling curves and compound formers

A eutectic alloy cools with one flat halt at 303 °C until all liquid freezes; F = 0. An off-eutectic alloy first shows a break at its liquidus, where one solid appears and F = 1. It then reaches 303 °C and halts at F = 0. After freezing, temperature falls again.

If the two components form a compound that melts congruently, the diagram has a peak at the compound's composition. Zn–Mg is one example. Only a few universities teach this diagram; compound details belong to a materials course.

Worked example

Question. An alloy is made from 5.0 g Ag and 95.0 g Pb. Find mass% Ag, classify it, F at the eutectic with liquid and both solids, and F at 400 °C in the liquid region.

  1. Total mass = 5.0 g + 95.0 g = 100.0 g.
  2. Mass% Ag = (5.0 g ÷ 100.0 g) × 100 = 5.0%.
  3. Eutectic is 2.5 mass% Ag; 5.0% > 2.5%, so hyper-eutectic.
  4. At eutectic: P = 3 (liquid, solid Pb, solid Ag), C = 2. F = 2 − 3 + 1 = 0.
  5. At 400 °C: above liquidus, P = 1. F = 2 − 1 + 1 = 2.

Answer. The alloy is 5.0 mass% Ag, hyper-eutectic; F = 0 at the eutectic and F = 2 at 400 °C in the liquid.

Exam traps

  • Never use F = C − P + 2 when pressure is fixed; the reduced rule is F = C − P + 1.
  • The eutectic is a mixture of two solids, not a compound.
  • At the eutectic, P = 3 (liquid + solid Pb + solid Ag), so F = 0.
  • Mass% and mole% are different; this diagram uses mass% Ag.
  • F = 0 does not mean nothing changes; it means no intensive variable can change without losing a phase.

Takeaways

  1. Energy alone does not decide whether a change happens; enthalpy and entropy act together, and Gibbs free energy combines them into one test.
  2. ΔG = ΔH − TΔS shows why heating can turn a non-spontaneous reaction spontaneous: the −TΔS term grows.
  3. A rate law comes from experiment, not from the balanced equation; order and molecularity are therefore different ideas.
  4. The rate constant rises exponentially with temperature, so a 10 K rise roughly doubles many reaction rates near room temperature.
  5. A mechanism is a sequence of elementary steps; the slowest step writes the rate law and the other steps stay hidden.
  6. Fastest product and stablest product differ; low temperature and short time favour the kinetic product, high temperature and long time the thermodynamic one.
  7. The phase rule counts freedom: more phases mean fewer variables you can change without one phase disappearing.
  8. Water's phase diagram has a triple point where F = 0 and a critical point where the liquid-vapour boundary simply ends.
  9. The Pb-Ag system freezes at a single eutectic temperature, and the reduced phase rule gives F′ = 0 there for fixed pressure.

Glossary

Activation energy
The extra energy a collision needs before products can form.
Alloy
A solid mixture of two or more metals, often with a eutectic or compound in its phase diagram.
Catalyst
A substance that lowers the activation energy and speeds a reaction without being consumed.
Clausius-Clapeyron equation
A relation linking vapour pressure to temperature and the enthalpy of vapourisation.
Condensed system
A system of solids and liquids only, where the pressure effect is small enough to ignore.
Congruent melting
Melting of a compound to a liquid of the same composition, so the compound survives as a phase.
Endothermic
A change that absorbs heat from the surroundings, so ΔH is positive.
Exothermic
A change that releases heat to the surroundings, so ΔH is negative.
Extensive variable
A property that depends on the amount of material, such as volume, mass and total energy.
Heterogeneous system
A system with two or more phases, such as ice floating in water.
Homogeneous system
A system with a single phase throughout, such as a salt solution.
Intensive variable
A property that does not depend on the amount of material, such as temperature, pressure and mole fraction.
Internal energy (U)
The total energy stored inside a system, including kinetic and potential energy of its particles.
Phase boundary
A curve on a phase diagram where two phases coexist in equilibrium.
Pseudo-first-order reaction
A second order reaction run with one reactant in large excess, so its concentration looks constant and the order behaves as one.
Reaction coordinate
A one-dimensional progress variable used to plot energy along the path from reactants to products.
Second law of thermodynamics
The entropy of an isolated system never decreases; spontaneous changes raise the total entropy.
Standard state
Pure substance at 1 bar pressure, with solutions at 1 mol L⁻¹, usually at 298 K.
State function
A property whose value depends only on the present state, not on the path taken, such as H, S and G.
Sublimation
Direct change from solid to vapour without passing through the liquid state.
Surroundings
Everything outside the system that can exchange energy or matter with it.
Thermodynamics
The study of energy changes, direction and extent of change, without reference to time.
Transition state
The highest energy arrangement on the reaction path, at the top of the activation barrier.
Vapour pressure
The pressure of vapour in equilibrium with its liquid or solid at a given temperature.
