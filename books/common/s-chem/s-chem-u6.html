6.1 Nanomaterials: classification and size-dependent properties

A nanomaterial is a solid whose particles, fibres, sheets or pores have at least one dimension between about 1 nm and 100 nm. At that size a large share of the atoms sits on the surface, and the electrons are squeezed into a space only tens of atoms wide. Both changes alter melting point, colour, strength and chemical reactivity. Sunscreen, self-cleaning glass, gas sensors, battery electrodes and drug carriers all use these effects. The chemistry inside the particle is still ordinary ZnO or TiO₂; only the size is new.

You need the bonding in solids (ionic, covalent, metallic) from Unit 1, Chemical Bonding and Coordination Compounds, to see why an atom at a surface is held differently from an atom inside the bulk.

The 1–100 nm window and why the surface wins

A nanomaterial has at least one dimension between 1 nm and 100 nm. One nanometre is 10⁻⁹ m. An atom is about 0.1–0.3 nm across, so a 10 nm particle is roughly 30–100 atoms wide. The 100 nm ceiling is a convention, not a sharp physical edge: properties slide steadily as size falls.

Two effects make a nanomaterial behave unlike the same solid in bulk. First, the surface. A surface atom has fewer neighbours, unsatisfied bonds and higher energy than an atom inside. Second, quantum confinement: when the particle is smaller than the electron's wavelength (more precisely, the exciton Bohr radius, a few nanometres in most semiconductors), the allowed electron energies shift.

SA/V = 6/a

SA is the surface area of a cube in m², V its volume in m³, a its edge in m, and SA/V the surface area per unit volume in m⁻¹. A sphere of diameter d gives SA/V = 6/d, so the 6/d form is general.

The share of atoms lying on the surface follows the same 1/a law. Take an atomic diameter d ≈ 0.3 nm and a cube of edge a. The fraction of surface atoms is roughly 6d/a: about 18 % for a = 10 nm, about 0.0002 % for a = 1 mm. Half a gram of 10 nm powder can carry more than 50 m² of exposed surface.

Classifying nanomaterials: dimension first, then composition

The most useful classification counts how many directions are nanoscale. That number decides which motions are confined.

A nanotube is 1D even though it looks like a rolled sheet: electrons move freely along the tube but are confined across it. Graphene is 2D. A compacted solid made of 20 nm grains, or a solid full of nanoscale pores, is 3D — it is still a nanomaterial because its internal surfaces are nanoscale.

  • Carbon-based: fullerenes such as C₆₀, carbon nanotubes, graphene.
  • Metal oxides and metals: ZnO, TiO₂, Fe₃O₄, nano-silver, nano-gold.
  • Organic and hybrid: polymer nanoparticles, micelles, dendrimers, organic–inorganic hybrids.

Carbon forms and quantum dots get their own treatment in 6.3; making these materials is covered in 6.2.

What size changes: confinement, melting point, colour, reactivity

Quantum confinement. An electron inside a particle behaves like a particle in a box (Unit 1). Shrink the box to a few nanometres and the allowed levels spread apart, widening the gap between the highest filled and the lowest empty level. A 5 nm CdSe dot glows red, a 2 nm dot of the same material glows blue: the colour is set by size, with no change of chemical formula. The band picture behind this is in 6.4.

Melting point depression. Surface atoms are held by fewer bonds, so they leave the solid first. A nanoparticle melts below the bulk melting point, and the gap widens as size falls. The same weak bonding makes surfaces chemically hungry: nanoscale metals sinter at lower temperature and catalyse reactions that the bulk metal ignores.

ZnO and TiO₂ are the standard teaching examples. Both are wide-band-gap oxides — ZnO about 3.2 eV, TiO₂ about 3.0–3.2 eV — so they absorb ultraviolet light below about 390 nm (ZnO and anatase TiO₂) and up to about 410 nm for rutile TiO₂. In a nanostructure most of the material is within a short distance of a surface, so the electron–hole pairs made by UV light reach the surface instead of recombining inside. There they form radicals that break down organic dirt. That is photocatalysis: self-cleaning glass, air purifiers, and sunscreen, where the same UV absorption protects the skin. Bulk powder does the same chemistry far more slowly.

Worked example

Question. A cube of ZnO has edge 10 nm. A second cube of the same ZnO has edge 1 cm. The density of ZnO is 5.6 g cm⁻³. (a) Calculate the surface-area-to-volume ratio of each cube in m⁻¹. (b) How many times more surface per unit volume does the small cube carry? (c) Estimate the total surface area of 1 g of the 10 nm cubes.

  1. Convert the small edge: a₁ = 10 nm = 10 × 10⁻⁹ m = 1.0 × 10⁻⁸ m.
  2. Apply SA/V = 6/a: (SA/V)₁ = 6 ÷ (1.0 × 10⁻⁸ m) = 6.0 × 10⁸ m⁻¹.
  3. Convert the large edge: a₂ = 1 cm = 1.0 × 10⁻² m.
  4. (SA/V)₂ = 6 ÷ (1.0 × 10⁻² m) = 6.0 × 10² m⁻¹ = 600 m⁻¹.
  5. Ratio = (6.0 × 10⁸) ÷ (6.0 × 10²) = 1.0 × 10⁶, so the nanoscale cube has a million times more surface per unit volume.
  6. Volume of 1 g of ZnO = mass ÷ density = 1 g ÷ 5.6 g cm⁻³ = 0.1786 cm³ = 0.1786 × 10⁻⁶ m³ = 1.786 × 10⁻⁷ m³.
  7. Total surface area = (SA/V)₁ × volume = (6.0 × 10⁸ m⁻¹) × (1.786 × 10⁻⁷ m³) = 107.14 m².

Exam traps

  • Surface-area-to-volume ratio has unit m⁻¹, not m². Only a total surface area is in m²; m² g⁻¹ is a specific surface area from a BET measurement, a different quantity.
  • A smaller particle has more surface per unit volume. Total surface area also depends on how much material you have, so the two numbers must never be swapped.
  • A nanotube is 1D, not 2D, even though it is a rolled sheet. Count the directions that are nanoscale, not the shape you see.
  • The red or blue colour of a CdSe quantum dot is a size effect from confined electrons, not a dye or an impurity.
  • Gold nanoparticles are coloured by surface plasmon resonance, a different size effect (see later topic).
  • The 1–100 nm range is a convention, and properties change continuously inside it. Never quote a property 'for nanomaterials' without stating the particle size.

6.2 Synthesis of nanomaterials: top-down and bottom-up

A nanomaterial can be made in only two directions. Top-down starts with a bulk solid and breaks it into smaller pieces. Bottom-up starts with atoms or molecules and builds them into a nanostructure. Industry uses top-down for quick, large-area work such as grinding and patterning, and bottom-up for controlled purity and shape, as in sol-gel coatings or CVD films. The route decides particle size spread, defects, cost and the final application.

You need 6.1: what makes a material 'nano', and why surface area and confinement change properties.

Two directions to the same size

Top-down synthesis takes a bulk solid and reduces its size. Mechanical grinding, cutting, etching and lithography all remove material or break it apart. Because the starting material is already a solid, the process is fast and can cover large areas. Some syllabi call this the destructive route because bulk matter is destroyed to make nanoparticles.

Bottom-up synthesis starts from atoms, ions or molecules. These units assemble into particles, tubes or films by chemical reaction or deposition. The build-up is controlled by concentration, temperature, pH and time. Some syllabi call this the constructive route.

Neither route is automatically better. A top-down mill can make kilograms of powder, but the particles vary in size and carry defects. A bottom-up reaction can give narrow size and high purity, but it is slower and the small particles may clump together.

S/V = 6/d

S is surface area in m², V is volume in m³, and d is the edge length of a cube in m. As d falls, S/V rises, so nanosized powders are more reactive.

Top-down: break bulk matter

Ball milling uses a rotating drum filled with hard balls of steel, zirconia or tungsten carbide. The balls impact the powder, and repeated fracture and cold welding reduce the particle size. It is simple and scales to kilograms, but ball wear contaminates the powder and the size distribution is wide.

Lithography writes a pattern into a resist using light, electrons or X-rays. Development and etching then transfer the pattern to the solid. It gives precise shapes for electronics, but the equipment is costly and the process works layer by layer.

Etching removes material through a mask using acid or plasma, making trenches and pillars. It is used with lithography. Over-etching can damage side walls and leave rough surfaces.

Laser ablation strikes a target with a pulsed laser in liquid or vacuum. The hot plume condenses into nanoparticles. It is clean and fast, but the yield is low and the laser is expensive.

All top-down routes share a weakness: breaking a solid creates fresh surfaces, but it also creates cracks, amorphous layers and a spread of sizes. The product often needs sieving or classification before use.

Bottom-up: build from molecules, and how to choose

Chemical synthesis of nanomaterials means any wet-chemical route—sol-gel, co-precipitation, hydrothermal or microemulsion—where a reaction in solution sets the size.

  • Sol-gel: a metal alkoxide or salt hydrolyses and condenses into a gel; drying and calcination give oxide nanoparticles or films. It mixes components at molecular level, but the gel shrinks and cracks on drying.
  • Co-precipitation: two soluble salts are mixed and a base makes an insoluble product. Washing and heating give the oxide. It is cheap and fast, but pH and temperature must be controlled to avoid mixed phases.
  • Hydrothermal: water and precursors react in a sealed autoclave above 100 °C and high pressure. It gives crystalline products with controlled shape, but needs a pressure vessel.
  • Microemulsion: water, oil, surfactant and cosurfactant form nanosized droplets that act as reactors. Particle size follows droplet size, but surfactant must be removed.
  • Chemical vapour deposition (CVD): precursor gases react on a heated substrate and deposit a solid film or particles. It gives conformal, pure coatings, but needs volatile precursors; see 6.7 for thin-film detail.
  • Biological synthesis: plant extracts, bacteria or fungi reduce metal ions to nanoparticles. It is green and cheap, but shape control is poor.

ZnO nanostructures are often made by sol-gel from zinc acetate and a base. TiO2 is often made by co-precipitation from a titanium salt and ammonium hydroxide, followed by calcination. For TiO2, calcination temperature decides the phase: lower temperature favours anatase, higher favours rutile.

RouteStrengthWeakness
Top-downSimple, large scale, works on many solidsWide size distribution, defects, contamination
Bottom-upNarrow size, high purity, shape controlSlow, needs stabilisers, particles can agglomerate

Worked example

Question. A top-down ball mill reduces cubic particles of edge 100 μm to 100 nm. Find the factor by which (a) the volume of each particle falls, and (b) the surface-area-to-volume ratio rises.

  1. Convert the edges to metres: 100 μm = 100 × 10⁻⁶ m = 1×10⁻⁴ m. 100 nm = 100 × 10⁻⁹ m = 1×10⁻⁷ m.
  2. Edge ratio = (1×10⁻⁴ m) ÷ (1×10⁻⁷ m) = 1×10³ = 1000.
  3. Volume of a cube = d³. Volume factor = (d_initial/d_final)³ = 1000³ = 1×10⁹. Each particle's volume falls by 1×10⁹.
  4. S/V = 6/d, so the surface-area-to-volume ratio rises by d_initial/d_final = 1000.

Answer. The volume falls by a factor of 1×10⁹, and the surface-area-to-volume ratio rises by a factor of 1000.

Exam traps

  • Top-down is not always cheaper or better: lithography is costly, and milling introduces defects and a wide size distribution.
  • Bottom-up is not automatically pure: nanoparticles can agglomerate, and stabilisers may remain on the surface.
  • Sol-gel and co-precipitation are different: sol-gel forms a gel network from molecular precursors; co-precipitation forms an insoluble salt from mixed solutions.
  • Bottom-up does not mean biological only: CVD, sol-gel and hydrothermal routes are chemical bottom-up methods.
  • Any fine powder is not a nanomaterial: the size must be below about 100 nm, and the properties must change with size.

6.3 Quantum dots, nanotubes and their applications

A quantum dot is a semiconductor crystal a few nanometres across. A nanotube is a sheet of carbon atoms rolled into a cylinder. Both are nanomaterials, but their behaviour comes from geometry. In a quantum dot, size sets the band gap and the colour of light absorbed or emitted. In a nanotube, the roll direction, called chirality, decides whether it is metallic or semiconducting. These materials appear in displays, sensors, bioimaging, strong composites and small transistors. Only a few universities teach this as a full topic.

You need: 6.1 for size-dependent properties and 6.4 for band gap; the Unit 1 particle in a box is the model for confinement.

Quantum dots: size sets colour

A quantum dot is a semiconductor crystal with all three dimensions below about 10 nm. Electrons and holes inside it cannot move freely. They are confined like a particle in a box. Confinement turns a continuous band into discrete levels and raises the lowest electron level above the highest hole level. The separation is the band gap, Eg.

E = hc ÷ λ ≈ Eg

E is photon energy in eV or J; h is Planck's constant; c is speed of light; λ is wavelength; Eg is band gap.

A smaller dot gives stronger confinement, a larger Eg, a higher photon energy and a shorter wavelength. So small CdSe dots emit blue light and larger dots emit red. The semiconductor material sets the rough range: CdSe and InP cover visible light, PbS covers infrared.

Uses include QLED displays, bioimaging labels, photodetectors, sensors and solar cells. In a display, dot size is chosen to set each pixel colour.

Nanotubes: rolled graphene and chirality

A carbon nanotube is graphene rolled into a cylinder. A single-walled nanotube (SWCNT) has one wall; a multi-walled nanotube (MWCNT) has several nested cylinders. The roll is fixed by two integers (n, m). Armchair tubes have (n, n); zigzag tubes have (n, 0); all other pairs are chiral.

In the simple zone-folding picture, a tube is metallic when n − m is divisible by 3, and semiconducting otherwise. Armchair tubes are metallic. A zigzag tube is metallic only when n is a multiple of 3. This is why chirality, not only diameter, decides electrical type.

Nanotubes are very strong and conduct heat and electricity well. They are used in polymer composites, field-effect transistors, gas sensors and conductive films. See Unit 5 for composite processing.

The strength comes from the carbon-carbon bond network; the electrical behaviour comes from how the π electrons wrap around the cylinder. A batch of nanotubes is usually a mixture, so separation by type is a practical problem.

Applications of nanomaterials

Nanomaterials are used because small size gives high surface area and tunable electronic structure. Common uses: displays and lighting; drug delivery carriers; catalysts; gas and biosensors; energy storage in batteries and supercapacitors (see Unit 4).

Quantum dots are chosen when colour tuning matters. Nanotubes are chosen when strength, conductivity or a tiny channel matters. Metal and metal-oxide nanoparticles are common catalysts and sensors. Porous nanomaterials store ions in batteries and supercapacitors.

Catalysis is a large near-term use: more surface atoms mean more active sites per gram. The same property can be a risk: high surface area also means high reactivity, and small particles can enter the body. Applications therefore need control of size, shape, purity and surface coating, as covered in 6.1 and 6.2.

Worked example

Question. A CdSe quantum dot emits light of wavelength 600 nm. Find the photon energy in eV. Use hc = 1240 eV·nm.

  1. Write the data: λ = 600 nm and hc = 1240 eV·nm.
  2. Use E = hc ÷ λ.
  3. Substitute: E = 1240 eV·nm ÷ 600 nm.
  4. The unit nm cancels: E = 2.0667 eV.
  5. Round to two decimal places: E = 2.07 eV.

Answer. The photon energy is 2.07 eV.

Exam traps

  • Smaller dot → larger gap → shorter wavelength (bluer). Reversing this is the common error.
  • Photon energy is inversely proportional to wavelength: 600 nm is lower energy than 500 nm.
  • Band gap may be in eV or J, and wavelength in nm; convert units before dividing.
  • Chirality (n, m) matters: an armchair tube is metallic, but not every nanotube is.
  • High surface area is not the same as high band gap; size effects and surface effects are different.

6.4 Semiconductors and band structure of solids

Solids differ because their electrons are allowed only in energy bands. The highest filled band is the valence band. The next band is the conduction band. The gap between them is the band gap Eg. Metals have no gap, insulators have a large gap, semiconductors have a small gap. Doping shifts the Fermi level and adds donor or acceptor levels. This controls conductivity in silicon chips, solar cells, LEDs and UPI card readers. Only a few universities teach this band-structure tutorial.

You need Unit 1, Chemical Bonding and Coordination Compounds: molecular orbital theory and bonding in solids.

Bands decide metal, insulator or semiconductor

A band is a range of allowed electron energies in a solid. The valence band is the highest band that is normally filled. The conduction band is the next band, normally empty at 0 K. The band gap Eg is the energy gap between them. A metal has a partly filled conduction band, so electrons move easily. An insulator has a large Eg, so very few electrons reach the conduction band. A semiconductor has a small Eg, about 1 eV. At 300 K, some electrons cross it. The Fermi level is the energy where the probability of occupation is 1/2 at equilibrium. In an intrinsic semiconductor it lies near the middle of the gap. Absorption: a photon creates an electron-hole pair only if hν ≥ Eg.

E = hc ÷ λ

E is photon energy in eV; h is Planck's constant; c is the speed of light; λ is wavelength in nm. With hc = 1240 eV·nm, E = 1240 ÷ λ.

Doping adds levels and moves the Fermi level

Some syllabi call this 'semiconductor materials'. Doping means adding a small amount of impurity to a pure semiconductor. Pure silicon is intrinsic. Group V donors such as P or As add an electron that sits on a donor level just below the conduction band. The material is n-type: electrons are the majority carriers. Group III acceptors such as B or Al create a hole on an acceptor level just above the valence band. The material is p-type: holes are the majority carriers. Doping raises conductivity but does not close Eg. The Fermi level moves toward the conduction band for n-type and toward the valence band for p-type. The crystal stays electrically neutral.

  • n-type: donor level near conduction band; electrons are majority carriers.
  • p-type: acceptor level near valence band; holes are majority carriers.
  • Direct gap: GaAs; a photon alone excites an electron. Indirect gap: Si and Ge; a phonon is also needed.

Worked example

Question. Silicon has a band gap Eg = 1.12 eV at 300 K. Find the longest wavelength of light that silicon can absorb. Use hc = 1240 eV·nm.

  1. A photon is absorbed by a band-to-band transition only if its energy is at least Eg.
  2. The longest wavelength corresponds to the minimum photon energy, E = Eg = 1.12 eV.
  3. Use E = hc ÷ λ, so λ = hc ÷ Eg.
  4. Substitute: λ = 1240 eV·nm ÷ 1.12 eV = 1107.14 nm.
  5. Convert to micrometres: 1107.14 nm = 1.10714 μm.

Answer. λ = 1107.14 nm ≈ 1107 nm (1.10714 μm), so silicon can absorb light up to about 1107 nm.

Exam traps

  • Doping raises conductivity but does not close the band gap; the gap of silicon stays near 1.12 eV at low doping.
  • n-type means donor electrons, not a negatively charged crystal; the crystal remains neutral.
  • Do not swap the levels: donor levels sit near the conduction band, acceptor levels near the valence band.
  • The longest absorbed wavelength uses the minimum energy Eg: λ = hc ÷ Eg, not Eg ÷ hc.
  • Direct and indirect gaps differ in momentum; GaAs is direct, Si and Ge are indirect, so their absorption strengths differ.

In GaAs the conduction-band minimum and valence-band maximum have the same momentum, so a photon alone can excite an electron. In Si they have different momentum, so a phonon is also needed.

Not for the exam. Real chips use doping profiles, carrier mobility and contact metals, not just a single donor level. Solar cells and photodiodes use the absorption edge; LEDs need a direct gap for efficient light emission. Search next: Hall effect, carrier concentration, p-n junction band diagram, and thin-film deposition (see 6.7).

6.5 Magnetic and superconducting materials

Magnetic order and superconductivity are collective solid-state behaviours. A magnetic material responds to an applied field H: its magnetisation M can add to or oppose H. A superconductor carries current with zero resistance below a critical temperature Tc and expels magnetic field from its interior. Industry uses soft magnets in transformer cores, hard magnets in motors, and superconductors in MRI scanners, maglev trains and power cables. Only a few universities teach superconductors in first-year chemistry.

You need: 6.4 for the band picture and Unit 1 crystal-field theory for unpaired electrons.

Magnetic materials: five responses

An applied field H (A/m) magnetises a solid. The internal field is B = μ0(H + M), where B is in tesla, μ0 = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m, and M (A/m) is magnetisation. Susceptibility χ = M/H decides the class.

  • Diamagnetic: χ is small and negative; no unpaired electrons; weakly repelled. Water and copper are examples.
  • Paramagnetic: χ is small and positive; unpaired electrons; thermal motion randomises them; weakly attracted. O₂ and Cu²⁺ complexes are examples.
  • Ferromagnetic: domains align spontaneously below the Curie temperature; large χ; Fe, Co and Ni.
  • Ferrimagnetic: two antiparallel sublattices have unequal moments, leaving a net M; magnetite Fe₃O₄.
  • Antiferromagnetic: equal antiparallel moments cancel; MnO; small positive χ.

Hard magnets have high coercivity and a wide hysteresis loop; they stay magnetised. NdFeB is used in motors and speakers. Soft magnets have low coercivity and a narrow loop; silicon steel is used in transformer cores.

Superconductors: zero resistance and the Meissner effect

Below Tc a superconductor has zero electrical resistance. It also expels magnetic field from its interior: the Meissner effect. Inside, B = 0, so μ0(H + M) = 0 and M = −H. The susceptibility is χ = −1; this is perfect diamagnetism.

  • Type I: one critical field Hc. Field is expelled completely up to Hc, then superconductivity breaks abruptly. Examples: Pb, Hg.
  • Type II: two fields Hc1 and Hc2. Between them, magnetic flux enters as vortices (mixed state), but resistance stays zero. Examples: Nb₃Sn, YBCO.
  • High-Tc cuprates: YBa₂Cu₃O₇−x has Tc about 90 K, above liquid nitrogen's 77 K. This made cheap cooling possible.

Uses: MRI scanners (NbTi coils), maglev trains (levitation from expelled field), and power cables (no I²R loss). A perfect conductor traps flux; a superconductor expels it.

Worked example

Question. A type-I superconductor is at 4 K in an applied magnetic flux density B = 0.10 T. Inside, B = 0. Find the magnetisation M. Use μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ H/m.

  1. For a type-I superconductor below Hc, the Meissner state has B = 0 inside.
  2. Use B = μ₀(H + M). Set B = 0.
  3. In free space the applied flux density is 0.10 T, so H = B/μ₀ = 0.10 ÷ (4π×10⁻⁷) = 7.96×10⁴ A/m.
  4. Then 0 = μ₀(H + M), so M = −H = −7.96×10⁴ A/m.

Answer. M = −7.96×10⁴ A/m (χ = −1).

Exam traps

  • Zero resistance alone does not explain levitation: a perfect conductor traps magnetic field inside, while a superconductor expels it.
  • Type II is not 'no field penetration'. Between Hc1 and Hc2 flux enters as vortices, but resistance stays zero.
  • M and H have units A/m; B is in tesla. χ is dimensionless. Mixing these units loses marks.
  • Ferrimagnetic is not ferromagnetic. Ferri has unequal antiparallel sublattices; ferro has aligned domains.
  • Hard magnet means high coercivity (permanent magnet). Soft magnet means low coercivity (transformer core). The words are about coercivity, not hardness.

6.6 Liquid crystals: thermotropic and lyotropic; LCDs

A liquid crystal flows like a liquid but keeps some orientational order, like a solid. That order can be changed by temperature, by concentration in a solvent, or by a voltage. Thermotropic liquid crystals change phase with temperature; lyotropic ones change with concentration. The twisted-nematic liquid-crystal display (LCD) uses a thin liquid-crystal layer between crossed polarisers: a voltage untwists the molecules and switches light. Televisions, phones, calculators and instrument panels use this effect. Only a few universities teach liquid crystals in this advanced materials unit.

You need: Unit 2 on polarisation of light, and Unit 5 on polymer properties if composites are taught.

Order without a fixed lattice

A liquid crystal is a state between the solid and the liquid. Molecules keep a preferred direction, called the director, but they can flow. The order can be orientational (molecules point the same way) or positional (molecules sit in layers). Thermotropic liquid crystals change phase with temperature. The nematic phase has orientational order only. Smectic phases add layers, with positional order along one direction. The cholesteric phase is a chiral nematic: the director twists in a helix. Lyotropic liquid crystals change phase with concentration in a solvent. Amphiphilic molecules, with a water-loving head and a water-hating tail, form micelles, cylindrical phases or lamellar sheets as concentration rises. Soap solutions and cell membranes are everyday examples. A display needs a thermotropic nematic mixture that is liquid over the working temperature range. Not every liquid-crystal phase is useful in a display: smectic and cholesteric phases have other uses, such as temperature sensors. Liquid-crystal composites, where liquid-crystal droplets sit in a polymer, are related; polymer-matrix composites belong to Unit 5.

The twisted-nematic LCD

The twisted-nematic (TN) cell is the common LCD element. It sandwiches a nematic layer, about 5 μm thick, between transparent indium tin oxide (ITO) electrodes. Rubbed polymer surfaces at the two plates force the director to turn through 90° from front to back. The cell sits between two polarisers. The front polariser passes one plane of polarised light. The twisted liquid crystal rotates that plane by 90°, so light passes the back analyser when the polarisers are crossed. Apply a voltage above a threshold: the electric field aligns the nematic along the field, the twist unwinds, and the analyser blocks the light. So the cell is bright with no voltage and dark with voltage in this crossed-polariser arrangement. Displays add a colour filter with red, green and blue patches, and a backlight behind the stack. Each pixel is three subpixels; the voltage on each subpixel sets its brightness. The stack in the figure follows this order: front polariser, colour filter, liquid-crystal layer, ITO electrode, back polariser (analyser), backlight. Liquid-crystal composites, such as polymer-dispersed liquid crystals, scatter or transmit light and need no analyser; they are a separate advanced topic.

Δn = ne − no; R = Δn·d

Δn is birefringence (no unit); ne is extraordinary refractive index; no is ordinary refractive index; R is phase retardation; d is cell thickness.

Worked example

Question. A nematic liquid crystal has ordinary refractive index n_o = 1.50 and extraordinary refractive index n_e = 1.70. The cell thickness is d = 5.0 μm. Find the birefringence Δn and the phase retardation Δn·d in nm.

  1. 1. Birefringence is the difference: Δn = n_e − n_o. Here Δn = 1.70 − 1.50 = 0.20 (no unit).
  2. 2. Retardation is R = Δn·d. Keep d in micrometres first: R = 0.20 × 5.0 μm = 1.0 μm.
  3. 3. Convert to nanometres: 1.0 μm = 1000 nm.

Answer. Δn = 0.20 and the retardation is 1000 nm (1.0 μm).

Exam traps

  • Thermotropic is set by temperature; lyotropic by concentration in a solvent.
  • Nematic has orientational order only; smectic adds layers.
  • Voltage untwists the TN cell; the rubbed surfaces create the twist.
  • Not every liquid-crystal phase is used in an LCD.
  • Use Δn = n_e − n_o, and keep d in the same unit before converting to nm.

6.7 Thin films: PVD and CVD techniques

Thin films are layers from a few nanometres to a few micrometres thick, grown atom by atom or molecule by molecule on a surface. The method decides thickness control, sidewall coverage, uniformity and cost. Physical vapour deposition (PVD) turns a solid source into vapour that condenses. Chemical vapour deposition (CVD) reacts precursor gases on a heated substrate. Both appear in phone chips, solar cells, mirrors, cutting tools and packaging. Only a few universities teach these techniques in detail.

You need: 6.2 bottom-up synthesis for atom-by-atom growth, and Unit 4 electroplating for a wet coating comparison.

Film formation and PVD

A film forms in four stages. A source supplies material. Transport carries it to the substrate. Deposition sticks it. Growth builds islands that join into a continuous layer. Vacuum is common: it gives vapour atoms a long mean free path, so they reach the wafer without colliding with air.

PVD is physical: a solid becomes vapour, then condenses. In thermal evaporation, a boat or crucible heats the source in vacuum; aluminium, gold and silver films are made this way. In sputtering, argon ions from a plasma bombard a target and knock out atoms. Sputtering gives better adhesion and can deposit alloys. PVD is line-of-sight: atoms travel straight, so sidewalls in a deep trench stay shadowed. It suits metals and simple oxides.

Substrate temperature is usually moderate, which helps plastic substrates. Film thickness is monitored by a quartz crystal microbalance. PVD does not require a surface chemical reaction.

CVD and choosing a method

CVD is chemical. Precursor gases enter a reactor, diffuse to the substrate, adsorb, react on the hot surface, and leave by-products that desorb. The film is the solid product. Thermal CVD needs a substrate often at several hundred degrees Celsius. Plasma-enhanced CVD (PECVD) uses a plasma to break precursor molecules, so the substrate can be cooler; it is common for silicon dioxide and silicon nitride.

Because gas reaches every exposed surface, CVD is conformal: it coats trench bottoms and sidewalls to nearly the same thickness. Precursors must be volatile, safe enough to handle, and react cleanly. Silane (SiH₄) with oxygen gives SiO₂; silane with ammonia gives Si₃N₄. Growth can be limited by gas transport or by surface reaction; the slower step controls rate.

Use PVD for metals, simple oxides, decorative and hard coatings, and mirrors. Use CVD for dielectric layers, semiconductor films, solar-cell coatings and uniform coverage of steps. PVD usually runs at lower substrate temperature and has no precursor chemistry. CVD is better for trenches but needs volatile precursors and often more heat or plasma.

Thickness in PVD is set by deposition rate × time. In CVD it is set by gas flow, pressure, temperature and time. Uniformity depends on wafer rotation and gas distribution. A hybrid choice is common: PVD for a metal contact, CVD for an insulating layer over it. Neither method is automatically better; the geometry and material decide. Adhesion improves after substrate cleaning.

Worked example

Question. A copper film of thickness 100 nm is deposited on a 1.0 cm × 1.0 cm wafer. Density of copper = 8.9 g/cm³. Find the mass of copper deposited in g and μg.

  1. Area = 1.0 cm × 1.0 cm = 1.0 cm².
  2. Thickness = 100 nm. Since 1 nm = 1×10⁻⁷ cm, thickness = 1.00×10⁻⁵ cm.
  3. Volume = area × thickness = 1.0 cm² × 1.00×10⁻⁵ cm = 1.00×10⁻⁵ cm³.
  4. Mass = density × volume = 8.9 g/cm³ × 1.00×10⁻⁵ cm³ = 8.9×10⁻⁵ g.
  5. Convert: 1 g = 10⁶ μg, so mass = 8.9×10⁻⁵ × 10⁶ μg = 89 μg.

Answer. The mass is 8.9×10⁻⁵ g, equal to 89 μg.

Exam traps

  • PVD is line-of-sight, so it may not coat deep sidewalls; CVD is conformal. Do not swap these.
  • Sputtering is physical, not a chemical reaction, even though a plasma is present.
  • Convert nm to cm carefully: 1 nm = 1×10⁻⁷ cm, not 10⁻⁹ cm.
  • Conformal means equal thickness on sidewalls and bottom; it does not mean single crystal.
  • CVD needs volatile precursors; a non-volatile solid cannot be fed as a gas.

Takeaways

  1. Below about 100 nm the fraction of atoms sitting on the surface becomes large, and behaviour changes from bulk-like to size-controlled.
  2. Quantum confinement raises the band gap as a crystal shrinks, which is why quantum dot colour depends on particle size.
  3. Nanomaterials are made either by breaking bulk matter down (top-down) or by building up from atoms (bottom-up); each direction has its own defects and cost profile.
  4. Band structure, not chemistry alone, decides whether a solid conducts like a metal, insulates, or semiconducts.
  5. Doping shifts the Fermi level and changes carrier type, and it is the basis of every diode, transistor and solar cell.
  6. Superconductors carry current with zero resistance and expel magnetic field; both effects end above a critical temperature.
  7. Magnetic order disappears above the Curie temperature, so magnet choice is always a temperature decision.
  8. Liquid crystals keep orientational order while flowing, and a small applied voltage can rotate that order — the basis of LCDs.
  9. PVD gives controlled, dense films but poor step coverage; CVD gives conformal coatings but needs volatile precursors and higher substrate temperature.

Glossary

Band gap
The energy gap between the top of the valence band and the bottom of the conduction band.
Carbon nanotube
A seamless cylindrical roll of graphene, either single-walled or multi-walled.
Cholesteric phase
A twisted nematic arrangement in which the director rotates in a helix with a characteristic pitch.
Conduction band
The lowest empty band in a solid through which electrons can carry current.
Diamagnetism
Weak magnetisation opposite to the applied field, shown by all materials but often masked.
Director
A unit vector describing the average molecular orientation in a liquid-crystal phase.
Fermi level
The energy level with a 50 per cent occupation probability at thermal equilibrium.
Ferrimagnetism
Antiparallel alignment of unequal magnetic moments, giving a net spontaneous magnetisation.
Graphene
A single sheet of carbon atoms one atom thick, arranged in a hexagonal lattice.
Hysteresis loop
The B-H loop traced when a magnetic field is cycled; its area is energy lost per cycle.
Meissner effect
Complete expulsion of magnetic flux from a superconductor cooled below its critical temperature.
Mesogen
A molecule that can form a liquid-crystalline phase because of its rigid rod or disc shape.
n-type semiconductor
A semiconductor doped with a donor impurity, so that electrons are the majority carriers.
Nanoparticle
A particle with all three dimensions between about 1 nm and 100 nm.
Nanowire
A one-dimensional nanostructure that is nanometre-wide but much longer in one direction.
p-type semiconductor
A semiconductor doped with an acceptor impurity, so that holes are the majority carriers.
Paramagnetism
Weak magnetisation in the same direction as the applied field, with no order once the field is removed.
Quantum dot
A semiconductor nanocrystal small enough in all three directions to show size-tunable, discrete energy levels.
Smectic phase
A liquid-crystal phase with molecules arranged in layers as well as aligned.
Sputtering
A PVD process in which energetic ions knock atoms off a target, which then deposit on the substrate.
Superconductivity
The state below a critical temperature in which electrical resistance is exactly zero.
Thin film
A layer of material from a few nanometres to a few micrometres thick on a substrate.
Type II superconductor
A superconductor that admits magnetic flux as vortices between two critical fields, allowing higher operating fields.
Valence band
The highest filled band of energy levels in a solid at 0 K.
