4.1 Electrochemical cells and single electrode potential

A cell turns a chemical redox reaction into electrical work by splitting it into two half-reactions held apart. Each half-reaction sits on its own metal electrode dipped in its own ion solution, and each has a single electrode potential. Join the electrodes with a wire and the solutions with a salt bridge, and electrons flow. The cell EMF is the difference between the two electrode potentials. Phone batteries, the corrosion cell on a wet gate and every pH meter reading rest on this one idea, so the sign conventions must be handled carefully.

You need: school-level redox and oxidation numbers (see Unit 1, Chemical Bonding and Coordination Compounds, for oxidation states in complexes).

Two half-cells make one cell

A galvanic cell (some syllabi say voltaic cell) converts the energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electricity. Drop zinc into copper sulphate solution and zinc dissolves while copper plates out, but the energy leaves as heat and no current flows. Keep the two half-reactions in separate compartments and the same electron transfer must push through an outside wire. That separation is the whole trick.

In the Daniell cell, a zinc rod stands in 1 M ZnSO₄ and a copper rod stands in 1 M CuSO₄. Zinc oxidises: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. This electrode is the anode, where oxidation happens. Copper ions reduce: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. This electrode is the cathode, where reduction happens. Electrons leave zinc, travel through the wire and reach copper; conventional current flows the opposite way.

A salt bridge is a tube of KCl set in agar gel that joins the two solutions. Positive ions move towards the cathode compartment and negative ions towards the anode compartment. It closes the circuit and keeps both solutions electrically neutral without letting them mix. Without it, charge builds up at the electrodes within microseconds and the cell stops.

  1. Write the anode half-cell on the left and the cathode half-cell on the right.
  2. Use a single vertical bar | for a phase boundary, such as metal | solution.
  3. Use a double bar || for the salt bridge.
  4. Write the concentration after each species: Zn | Zn²⁺ (1 M) || Cu²⁺ (1 M) | Cu.

Single electrode potential and the hydrogen zero

A metal dipped in a solution of its own ions develops a potential difference across the metal–solution interface. Two opposing tendencies act: metal atoms dissolve as ions and leave electrons on the metal, and ions from the solution deposit back on the metal. When the two rates become equal, a steady charge separation sits at the surface. That potential difference is the single electrode potential of the couple.

A single electrode potential cannot be measured alone, because a voltmeter needs a second terminal. Every electrode potential is therefore measured against a chosen reference, the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE). The SHE is a platinum foil coated with platinum black, dipped in 1 M H⁺ solution, with H₂ gas bubbled at 1 bar, at 298 K. Its half-reaction is 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂. By convention its potential is fixed at exactly 0.00 V.

2H⁺ (aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂ (g), E° = 0.00 V

E° is the standard electrode potential in volts; the H⁺ activity is 1 and the H₂ pressure is 1 bar at 298 K.

Standard conditions are 298 K, 1 bar for gases, 1 M for dissolved species (strictly, unit activity) and a pure electrode. Under these conditions the measured value is a standard electrode potential, written E°. The sign tells you the direction: E°(Zn²⁺/Zn) = −0.76 V, so zinc is less noble than hydrogen and its half-cell tends to oxidise, giving electrons away. E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) = +0.34 V, so copper tends to reduce and take electrons.

The electrochemical series and cell EMF

Half-reactionE° (V)
Li⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Li−3.05
Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn−0.76
2H⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ H₂0.00
Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Cu+0.34
Ag⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ Ag+0.80

Arrange couples by E° and you get the electrochemical series. The more negative the value, the more active the metal and the stronger its tendency to oxidise. A metal high in the series displaces a metal below it from its salt solution: zinc displaces copper, copper does not displace zinc. When two metals touch in a conducting liquid, the more negative one becomes the anode and corrodes (see Unit 4, corrosion).

E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode

E°cell is the standard cell EMF in volts, E°cathode is the standard potential of the couple that reduces, and E°anode is the standard potential of the couple that oxidises, each taken with its own sign.

When you combine two couples, the one with the more positive E° becomes the cathode if the cell runs spontaneously. A positive E°cell means the reaction as written is spontaneous and the cell delivers that voltage on open circuit. These values hold only at standard conditions; change a concentration and the potential shifts, which is the Nernst equation in 4.2.

Worked example

Question. For the Daniell cell Zn | Zn²⁺ (1 M) || Cu²⁺ (1 M) | Cu at 298 K, the standard electrode potentials are E°(Zn²⁺/Zn) = −0.76 V and E°(Cu²⁺/Cu) = +0.34 V. (a) Write the anode and cathode half-reactions. (b) Calculate the standard cell EMF.

  1. Identify the couple that oxidises. Zinc has the more negative potential, so zinc is the anode: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻.
  2. The copper couple reduces at the cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu.
  3. Write the formula: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode.
  4. Substitute with signs: E°cell = (+0.34 V) − (−0.76 V).
  5. Subtract a negative number: 0.34 + 0.76 = 1.10 V.
  6. Check the sign: E°cell is positive, so the cell reaction is spontaneous as written.

Answer. The cell EMF is E°cell = 1.10 V, with zinc as anode and copper as cathode.

Exam traps

  • Do not add the two electrode potentials with their signs and then forget the anode sign. Use E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode; the zinc electrode enters as −0.76 V, not +0.76 V.
  • The anode is the negative terminal in a galvanic cell but the positive terminal in an electrolytic cell. In both, oxidation still happens at the anode.
  • Reversing a half-reaction flips the sign of E°, but doubling the half-reaction does not double E°. Potential is intensive; only ΔG and n scale.
  • The salt bridge is not a wire. Electrons travel through the metal wire; the bridge carries ions and only completes the circuit.
  • E° = 0.00 V belongs to the SHE only: 1 M H⁺, 1 bar H₂, 298 K, platinum black. A bare platinum wire in dilute acid is not a hydrogen electrode.

4.2 Nernst equation; free energy and EMF

An electrode potential is not a fixed number. Change the ion concentration around an electrode and its potential moves. The Nernst equation gives that shift, linking measured EMF to the reaction quotient Q. Read through ΔG = −nFE, the same equation ties cell voltage to Gibbs free energy and to the equilibrium constant K. Engineers use it to see how a battery's voltage sags as it drains, and to turn a measured voltage into a concentration. Only a few universities teach the full thermodynamic depth; the working equations below are what exams ask.

You need: 4.1 for cell EMF and single electrode potentials, and Unit 3 for Gibbs free energy and the equilibrium constant.

The Nernst equation

A cell gives EMF because its reaction is not at equilibrium. As products build up, the driving force falls. The Nernst equation puts that fall into numbers, using Q, the reaction quotient: products over reactants, each raised to its stoichiometric coefficient.

E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q

E is the cell EMF in volts; E° is the standard EMF in volts, measured with every species at unit activity (1 M for solutes, 1 bar for gases); R = 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ is the gas constant; T is temperature in kelvin; n is the number of electrons in the balanced cell reaction; F = 96485 C mol⁻¹ is the Faraday constant, the charge on one mole of electrons; Q is the reaction quotient.

At 298 K the constants collapse into one number: RT/F = 8.314 × 298 ÷ 96485 = 0.0257 V, and ln x = 2.303 log₁₀ x, which gives 0.0592 V.

E = E° − (0.0592/n) log₁₀ Q

valid only at 298 K; 0.0592 has units of volts; the log is base 10.

  • Metal/metal-ion: for Mⁿ⁺ + ne⁻ → M, Q = 1/[Mⁿ⁺], so E = E° + (0.0592/n) log[Mⁿ⁺]. The potential rises with ion concentration.
  • Gas electrode: for 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂, Q = p(H₂)/[H⁺]², so E = E° − (0.0592/2) log(p(H₂)/[H⁺]²).
  • Redox electrode: for Fe³⁺ + e⁻ → Fe²⁺, Q = [Fe²⁺]/[Fe³⁺], so E = E° − 0.0592 log([Fe²⁺]/[Fe³⁺]).

Free energy and EMF

A cell that delivers charge does electrical work. Sending n faradays through a potential difference of E volts delivers nFE joules, and at constant temperature and pressure that work comes out of the Gibbs free energy.

ΔG = −nFE

ΔG is the Gibbs free energy change of the cell reaction in J mol⁻¹; n is electrons transferred; F = 96485 C mol⁻¹; E is the cell EMF in volts, and 1 C × 1 V = 1 J.

The sign carries the meaning. A positive E gives a negative ΔG, so the reaction runs and the cell works. A dead battery has E = 0 and Q = K.

ΔG° = −nFE° = −RT ln K

ΔG° is the standard free energy change in J mol⁻¹, E° the standard EMF in volts, K the equilibrium constant of the cell reaction.

E° = (RT/nF) ln K = (0.0592/n) log₁₀ K at 298 K

a large positive E° means K ≫ 1; at n = 1, every 0.0592 V is a tenfold change in K.

Temperature coefficient. Because ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, measuring E at several temperatures separates the two: ΔS = nF(∂E/∂T) and ΔH = −nFE + nFT(∂E/∂T), at constant pressure. An EMF that falls as temperature rises means a negative ΔS.

Worked example

Question. For the cell Zn | Zn²⁺(0.10 M) || Cu²⁺(1.0 M) | Cu at 298 K, E° = 1.10 V and F = 96485 C mol⁻¹. Write the cell reaction, calculate the cell EMF, and calculate ΔG in kJ mol⁻¹.

  1. Cell reaction: Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu. Zinc is oxidised, copper ion is reduced, and n = 2 electrons.
  2. Reaction quotient: Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺] = 0.10/1.0 = 0.10. The solids Zn and Cu have activity 1 and do not appear.
  3. log Q = log₁₀ 0.10 = −1.00.
  4. Nernst equation: E = 1.10 − (0.0592/2)(−1.00) = 1.10 + 0.0296 = 1.1296 V, about 1.13 V.
  5. Free energy: ΔG = −nFE = −2 × 96485 × 1.1296 = −217979 J mol⁻¹ = −218 kJ mol⁻¹.
  6. Check the sign: ΔG is negative and E is above E°, both consistent with Q < 1.

Output

1.1296
-218.0

Answer. E = 1.1296 V (about 1.13 V) and ΔG = −218 kJ mol⁻¹, so the cell reaction is spontaneous as written.

Exam traps

  • 0.0592 V goes with log base 10. Using it beside ln inflates the answer by 2.303.
  • Q is products over reactants, and pure solids and liquids have activity 1, so Zn and Cu metal never appear in Q.
  • n is the number of electrons in the balanced cell reaction, not the charge on one ion; for Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, n = 2.
  • ΔG = −nFE gives joules per mole. Divide by 1000 before writing kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Keep the minus sign in E = E° − (0.0592/n) log Q. If Q < 1, log Q is negative and E lies above E°.

4.3 Reference electrodes: calomel and glass electrodes

A reference electrode is a half-cell whose potential stays fixed, so the unknown electrode beside it can be read from a voltmeter. Without one, a single electrode potential cannot be measured, because only differences exist. Two references appear in most syllabi. The calomel electrode uses mercury and mercurous chloride in saturated KCl and sits at +0.241 V vs SHE at 25 °C. The glass electrode uses a thin pH-sensitive bulb and shifts by 0.0592 V per pH unit at 298 K. Both serve in pH meters and potentiometry. Only a few universities teach them in this detail.

You need 4.1 for single electrode potentials and 4.2 for the Nernst equation.

The calomel electrode

The standard hydrogen electrode is awkward daily: it needs hydrogen gas and platinised platinum held at 1 bar against 1 M H⁺. A secondary reference electrode instead holds a steady potential that was fixed once against the SHE. Potentiometry uses it as the quiet partner beside the electrode under study (see 4.4). It must pass almost no current, or its own potential drifts.

  • A mercury pool at the bottom, with a platinum wire sealed through the glass for contact.
  • A paste of mercury and mercurous chloride (calomel, Hg₂Cl₂) spread over the pool.
  • Potassium chloride solution above the paste; saturated KCl gives the saturated calomel electrode, SCE.
  • A side tube ending in a porous plug, which forms the junction with the test solution.
Hg₂Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Hg + 2Cl⁻    E = E° − (0.0592/2) log[Cl⁻]²

E is the electrode potential in volts vs SHE; E° is the standard potential of the Hg₂Cl₂/Hg couple; [Cl⁻] is the chloride concentration in mol L⁻¹; 0.0592 V is 2.303RT/F at 298 K.

Only chloride concentration and temperature enter, so the potential is reproducible. Saturated KCl at 25 °C gives +0.241 V vs SHE. That is the value exams mean unless another concentration is stated.

The glass electrode

A thin bulb of pH-sensitive glass, roughly 0.1 mm thick, seals the end of a tube. Inside sit 0.1 M HCl and a silver wire coated with AgCl — a small Ag/AgCl electrode acting as the inner reference. When the bulb dips into the test solution, H⁺ ions exchange with the hydrated gel layer on the glass surface and build a potential difference across the membrane.

E(glass) = E°(glass) − 0.0592 × pH

E(glass) is the membrane potential in volts; E°(glass) is a constant for that particular bulb; pH = −log[H⁺]; 0.0592 V is the slope at 298 K, so one pH unit changes the reading by 59.2 mV.

In use the bulb is paired with an SCE: glass | test solution || KCl (saturated) | Hg₂Cl₂, Hg. The cell EMF follows pH in a straight line, and the meter is calibrated with two buffer solutions of known pH before a reading is trusted.

Advantages: nothing is added to the sample, oxidising and coloured solutions are fine, and the range is about pH 1 to 12. Limits: the bulb is fragile and its resistance is very high, so the meter must draw almost no current.

Worked example

Question. A saturated calomel electrode has a potential of +0.241 V vs SHE. It is coupled with a copper electrode dipping into 1 M Cu²⁺, and copper is the cathode. The voltmeter reads 0.099 V. Find the potential of the copper electrode vs SHE.

  1. For a cell, EMF = E(cathode) − E(anode).
  2. Copper is the cathode, so EMF = E(Cu) − E(SCE).
  3. Substitute: 0.099 V = E(Cu) − 0.241 V.
  4. Rearrange: E(Cu) = 0.099 V + 0.241 V = 0.340 V vs SHE.
  5. Check against the standard value for Cu²⁺/Cu, about +0.34 V — the sign and size agree.

Output

0.340

Answer. The copper electrode potential is +0.340 V vs SHE.

Exam traps

  • Do not set the saturated calomel electrode at 0.00 V. Zero belongs to the standard hydrogen electrode; the SCE is about +0.241 V vs SHE at 25 °C.
  • The SCE potential shifts with KCl concentration and with temperature, so a quoted 0.241 V is for saturated KCl at 25 °C only.
  • Sign slip: EMF = E(cathode) − E(anode). Fix which electrode is the cathode before adding or subtracting the reference potential.
  • The glass electrode slope is 0.0592 V per pH unit at 298 K, not 0.0592 mV, and the slope itself changes with temperature.
  • Calomel is the reference; glass is the indicator. In a pH cell the glass bulb is the electrode that responds to the sample, and the SCE only holds the other end steady.

4.4 Potentiometry and conductance of electrolytes

Potentiometry measures a cell EMF while almost no current flows, so the reading follows the Nernst equation and gives the concentration of an ion. Conductance measures how easily a solution carries current, which depends on the number and mobility of ions. Together they find endpoints without an indicator and check electrolyte strength. A pH meter in a water plant is potentiometry; a laboratory conductivity meter checking distilled water or battery acid is conductometry. The cells, formulas and titration curves below make both methods reliable.

You need: cells and electrode potentials from 4.1, the Nernst equation from 4.2, and reference electrodes from 4.3; basic resistance from school physics.

Potentiometry: EMF at almost zero current

Potentiometry finds concentration from the EMF of a cell when current is nearly zero. The cell has a reference electrode of fixed known potential and an indicator electrode that responds to the analyte. A high-impedance voltmeter, with input resistance near 10¹² Ω, measures the EMF. Almost no current flows, so the solution composition stays unchanged and the Nernst equation applies.

<i>E</i><sub>cell</sub> = <i>E</i><sub>indicator</sub> − <i>E</i><sub>reference</sub>

Ecell is the measured cell EMF in volts; Eindicator is the potential of the indicator electrode in volts; Ereference is the fixed potential of the reference electrode in volts. The sign reverses if the meter leads are reversed.

For a glass pH electrode the cell EMF changes by about 59 mV per pH unit at 25 °C. That is why a pH meter must be calibrated with buffer solutions of known pH.

  1. Put a known volume of analyte in a beaker with the indicator and reference electrodes.
  2. Fill the burette with titrant and stir gently.
  3. Add titrant in small portions and record the stable EMF after each addition.
  4. Plot EMF against titrant volume.
  5. Take the endpoint at the steepest part of the jump, where ΔE/ΔV is largest, and read the volume.

Acid-base titrations use a glass pH electrode. Redox titrations use an inert platinum indicator electrode. Precipitation titrations, such as chloride with silver nitrate, use a silver indicator electrode. The method works for coloured or turbid solutions where an indicator dye would be hard to see.

Conductance: specific, molar and equivalent

Conductance is the ease with which a solution carries current. Resistance R is measured with an AC bridge, not DC, because DC would polarise the electrodes and change the solution near them. The cell constant l/A corrects for the geometry of the conductivity cell.

κ = (<i>l</i>/<i>A</i>) / <i>R</i> = <i>G</i> (<i>l</i>/<i>A</i>)

κ is specific conductance in S cm⁻¹; l is the distance between electrodes in cm; A is the electrode area in cm²; R is resistance in Ω; G = 1/R is conductance in S; l/A is the cell constant in cm⁻¹.

Specific conductance κ is the conductance of a 1 cm cube of solution. Its unit is S cm⁻¹. The cell constant l/A is found by measuring R for a standard KCl solution of known κ.

Λm = 1000 κ / M

Λm is molar conductance in S cm² mol⁻¹; κ is specific conductance in S cm⁻¹; M is concentration in mol L⁻¹; 1000 converts litres to cm³.

Equivalent conductance Λeq uses normality N in eq L⁻¹: Λeq = 1000κ/N. Strong electrolytes are fully ionised. Their molar conductance rises only slightly on dilution and levels off at Λm∞. Weak electrolytes are partly ionised; dilution increases the degree of ionisation, so Λm rises sharply towards Λm∞. Kohlrausch law gives the limiting value from independent ions: Λm∞ = ν₊λ₊∞ + ν₋λ₋∞. Here ν₊ and ν₋ are the numbers of cations and anions per formula unit, and λ₊∞ and λ₋∞ are their ionic conductances at infinite dilution.

Conductance increases with temperature and depends on the nature of the ions. H⁺ and OH⁻ have unusually high mobilities in water, so acids and bases conduct much better than salts of similar concentration.

Conductometric titration curves

A conductometric titration follows conductance as titrant is added. Some syllabi call this conductometry. No indicator is needed. The endpoint is the intersection of the straight-line parts of the curve.

For HCl titrated with NaOH, before the endpoint H⁺ ions of high mobility are replaced by Na⁺ ions of lower mobility. Conductance falls in a straight line. At the equivalence volume only NaCl remains, so conductance is minimum. After the endpoint, excess Na⁺ and OH⁻ are added; OH⁻ has high mobility, so conductance rises. The curve is a V with the minimum at the endpoint.

For a weak acid titrated with a strong base, the weak acid is poorly ionised. Adding NaOH forms a fully ionised salt, so conductance rises before the endpoint. After the endpoint, excess OH⁻ gives a steeper rise. The endpoint is the intersection of the two straight lines, not a minimum.

The same method detects precipitation and redox endpoints when the ions or ionic species change. It is common in water analysis, quality control of battery acid, and checking distilled water purity. Compare potentiometry: there the endpoint is a sharp EMF jump; here it is a change of slope in conductance.

Worked example

Question. 25.0 mL of hydrochloric acid is titrated conductometrically with 0.100 M NaOH. The conductance is minimum at 25.0 mL. Find the concentration of HCl.

  1. Write the data: volume of HCl = 25.0 mL = 0.0250 L; concentration of NaOH = 0.100 M; volume of NaOH at the minimum = 25.0 mL = 0.0250 L.
  2. Moles of NaOH = concentration × volume = 0.100 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0250 L = 0.00250 mol.
  3. The reaction is HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O. It is 1:1, so moles of HCl = 0.00250 mol.
  4. Concentration of HCl = moles / volume = 0.00250 mol / 0.0250 L = 0.100 M.

Answer. The concentration of HCl is 0.100 M.

Exam traps

  • Do not take the minimum as the endpoint for every conductometric titration. For a weak acid with a strong base, use the intersection of the straight-line branches.
  • A potentiometric EMF must be measured at near-zero current. A low-impedance meter draws current and shifts the reading.
  • In Λm = 1000κ/M, κ must be in S cm⁻¹ and M in mol L⁻¹; a concentration given in mol m⁻³ makes the answer 1000 times too small, and R must first be converted to κ = (l/A)/R.
  • The potentiometric endpoint is the steepest slope, maximum ΔE/ΔV, not the first or last reading of the jump.
  • Conductance includes every ion present. After the endpoint the excess titrant, not the analyte, controls the slope.

4.5 Batteries: primary and secondary; performance parameters

A battery turns a chemical redox reaction into electrical energy. Primary batteries are used once and discarded; secondary batteries can be recharged by reversing the reaction. Performance is not one number: voltage, capacity, energy density, power density, self-discharge and cycle life decide whether a battery suits a remote, a phone, a UPS or an electric vehicle. This chapter classifies batteries, defines the standard parameters with units, and shows how to convert ampere-hours to watt-hours without mixing charge and energy.

You need: cell EMF, half-reactions and electrode potentials from <a href='#electrochemical-cells-and-single-electrode-potential'>4.1; the Nernst equation from <a href='#nernst-equation-free-energy-and-emf'>4.2 explains why voltage falls as reactants are used.

Primary and secondary batteries

An electrochemical cell produces electrical work from a spontaneous redox reaction. A battery is one or more cells connected in series or parallel, with terminals and packaging. In series, cell voltages add and capacity stays the same. In parallel, capacity adds and voltage stays the same.

A primary battery is made for one discharge. Its active materials are sealed, and the products are hard to convert back. Common examples are the zinc-carbon dry cell, the alkaline cell and the button cell in a watch. A secondary battery is rechargeable. Current in the reverse direction restores the active materials. Lead-acid, nickel-cadmium (Ni-Cd) and lithium-ion are the main secondary types. Construction of lead-acid and Li-ion cells is in <a href='#lead-acid-and-lithium-ion-batteries'>4.6.

A practical battery must meet several requirements at once:

  • High energy density, so the battery is light and small for the energy it stores.
  • Stable voltage during discharge, so the device works as the battery drains.
  • Low internal resistance, so it can supply a large current without a big voltage drop.
  • Long cycle life for secondary cells, safety under abuse, low cost and low self-discharge during storage.

Some syllabi call this energy systems or battery technology.

Performance parameters with units

The open-circuit voltage of a cell is its EMF, set by the electrode potentials. Under load, the terminal voltage is lower: Vterminal = EMF − I r, where I is current in amperes (A) and r is internal resistance in ohms (Ω). Nominal voltage is the label value, such as 1.5 V for a dry cell, 1.2 V for Ni-Cd, 3.7 V for a Li-ion cell, or 12 V for a lead-acid pack.

Q = I × t

Q is charge in ampere-hours (Ah); I is current in amperes (A); t is time in hours (h).

E = Q × V

E is energy in watt-hours (Wh); Q is charge in Ah; V is voltage in volts (V).

C-rate = I ÷ Qrated

C-rate is the discharge rate in h⁻¹; I is current in A; Qrated is rated capacity in Ah. 1C means the full capacity is delivered in 1 h. DoD = (Qused ÷ Qrated) × 100%, where Qused is charge taken out in Ah. Coulombic efficiency = (Qdischarge ÷ Qcharge) × 100%, where both charges are in Ah.

Wh kg⁻¹ is also called specific energy, and some books use energy density only for Wh L⁻¹; here we say which unit we mean.

Reading a battery label

A label such as 12 V, 100 Ah does not mean 100 Ah of energy. It means 100 Ah of charge at about 12 V. Convert to watt-hours first. Then compare chemistries by energy density and by power density.

  1. Write the rated voltage V and rated capacity Qrated in Ah.
  2. Find stored energy E = Qrated × V in Wh. Divide by 1000 for kWh.
  3. For a load current I, find C-rate = I ÷ Qrated and ideal time t = Qrated ÷ I.
  4. Find charge used Qused = I × t and depth of discharge DoD = Qused ÷ Qrated × 100%.
  5. Check cycle life, self-discharge and the allowed DoD for the chemistry before choosing the battery.

A lead-acid battery used in an inverter is cheap and gives high current, but it is heavy and should not be deep-discharged often. A Li-ion phone battery stores more energy per kilogram but needs a protection circuit. The same Ah rating can therefore mean very different stored energy.

Worked example

Question. A 12 V, 100 Ah lead-acid battery is discharged at a constant 5 A for 10 h. (a) Find its stored energy in Wh and kWh. (b) Find the charge used in Ah. (c) Find the depth of discharge. (d) Find the C-rate of the discharge.

  1. Rated voltage V = 12 V; rated capacity Qrated = 100 Ah.
  2. Stored energy E = Qrated × V = 100 Ah × 12 V = 1200 Wh.
  3. In kilowatt-hours, E = 1200 Wh ÷ 1000 = 1.2 kWh.
  4. Charge used Qused = I × t = 5 A × 10 h = 50 Ah.
  5. Depth of discharge DoD = (Qused ÷ Qrated) × 100% = (50 Ah ÷ 100 Ah) × 100% = 50%.
  6. C-rate = I ÷ Qrated = 5 A ÷ 100 Ah = 0.05 h⁻¹, written 0.05C. This is a slow 20 h discharge.

Answer. The battery stores 1200 Wh (1.2 kWh); it uses 50 Ah, reaches 50% depth of discharge, and runs at 0.05C.

Exam traps

  • Ah is charge, not energy. A 12 V 100 Ah battery stores 1200 Wh; a 6 V 100 Ah battery stores 600 Wh. Convert with E = Q × V before comparing.
  • Nominal voltage is not the same as EMF or terminal voltage. Under load, Vterminal = EMF − I r, so a 12 V battery may read 11 V while cranking a motor.
  • 1C does not mean one coulomb. It means a current equal to the rated Ah value; 0.05C on 100 Ah is 5 A, which takes about 20 h.
  • Depth of discharge is not state of charge. 50% DoD means 50% used; 50% state of charge means 50% remains.
  • Primary does not mean low capacity, and secondary does not mean unlimited cycles. Button cells are primary; lead-acid is secondary but has limited cycle life.

4.6 Lead-acid and lithium-ion batteries

A lead-acid battery stores energy using lead, lead dioxide and dilute sulfuric acid. A lithium-ion battery shuttles lithium ions between graphite and a metal oxide. Both are secondary cells: discharge is spontaneous, recharge is forced. Lead-acid is cheap and gives high current, so it starts cars and runs inverters and UPS units. Li-ion gives more energy per kilogram, so it powers phones, laptops and electric vehicles. This chapter writes the reactions, compares about 2.1 V per lead-acid cell with about 3.6–3.8 V per Li-ion cell that has a layered oxide cathode such as LiCoO₂, notes that an LiFePO₄ cell gives about 3.2–3.3 V, and explains the six-cell 12 V pack.

You need: cell EMF and electrode potentials from 4.1, ΔG = −nFE from 4.2, and battery terms from 4.5.

Lead-acid cell: Pb, PbO₂, H₂SO₄

Only a few universities teach this at construction-reaction depth. On discharge, Pb is the negative plate and the anode: oxidation gives electrons. PbO₂ is the positive plate and the cathode: reduction takes electrons. Dilute H₂SO₄ is the electrolyte; a porous separator stops the plates touching but lets ions pass. Each cell gives about 2.1 V; six cells in series give a 12 V battery. Recharge reverses both half-reactions.

Pb + SO₄²⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2e⁻

Pb is the anode; SO₄²⁻ is sulfate ion; e⁻ is an electron.

PbO₂ + 4H⁺ + SO₄²⁻ + 2e⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2H₂O

PbO₂ is the cathode; H⁺ is hydrogen ion; H₂O is water.

Pb + PbO₂ + 2H₂SO₄ → 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O

overall discharge; reverse it for recharge.

The key change on discharge is that both plates are converted to lead sulfate (PbSO₄) and the acid is diluted. This is why a discharged cell has weaker acid.

Li-ion cell: shuttle and overvoltage

A Li-ion cell uses a graphite anode, a cathode of LiCoO₂ or LiFePO₄, and LiPF₆ salt in an organic solvent. A separator blocks electron contact. Li⁺ ions are intercalated: inserted into layered solids without breaking their structure. On discharge, Li⁺ leaves graphite, crosses the electrolyte, and enters the cathode; electrons go through the load. Charge reverses both flows. One cell gives about 3.6–3.8 V with a layered oxide cathode such as LiCoO₂; an LiFePO₄ cell gives about 3.2–3.3 V.

Advantages: high energy density, low self-discharge, no memory effect. Disadvantages: costly, needs protection circuits, and can catch fire if overcharged or overheated. Under heavy load, internal resistance and polarization cause overvoltage: terminal voltage falls below open-circuit EMF, so less energy reaches the load and heat rises.

Lead-acid is cheap and delivers high surge current, but is heavy and has low energy density. Li-ion is light and energy-dense, but needs careful voltage limits. The electrolyte carries ions, not electrons. This shuttle gives the name rocking-chair battery.

Worked example

Question. A lead-acid cell has E = 2.10 V and the cell reaction transfers n = 2 electrons. Find ΔG per cell in J mol⁻¹ and kJ mol⁻¹.

  1. Use ΔG = −nFE. Here n is electrons per reaction, F is Faraday constant 96485 C mol⁻¹, and E is cell EMF 2.10 V.
  2. Substitute: ΔG = −2 × 96485 × 2.10.
  3. Compute: 2 × 96485 = 192970; 192970 × 2.10 = 405237.
  4. Apply the sign: ΔG = −405237 J mol⁻¹.
  5. Convert: −405237 ÷ 1000 = −405.237 kJ mol⁻¹, about −405 kJ mol⁻¹.

Answer. ΔG = −405,237 J mol⁻¹ = −405.237 kJ mol⁻¹ ≈ −405 kJ mol⁻¹ per cell.

Exam traps

  • A lead-acid cell is about 2.1 V, not 12 V. A 12 V battery has six cells in series.
  • Do not reverse the discharge reactions when writing recharge; reverse both half-reactions and the overall reaction.
  • In Li-ion, Li⁺ moves through the electrolyte and electrons move through the external circuit. Do not send electrons through the electrolyte.
  • PbO₂ is the cathode on discharge, not the anode. Pb is oxidized.
  • 3.6–3.8 V is per Li-ion cell with a layered oxide cathode, not per pack. LiFePO₄ cells give about 3.2–3.3 V.

4.7 Fuel cells and supercapacitors

A fuel cell is an open electrochemical device. It takes fuel and oxidant continuously and makes electricity, water and heat. A supercapacitor stores charge at a high-area surface or by fast surface redox, so it gives bursts of power. Both sit beside batteries: fuel cells suit long runs with refuelling, supercapacitors suit short high-power pulses. You meet them in hydrogen buses, telecom backup, regenerative braking and grid smoothing. This chapter covers the H₂–O₂ cell reactions, alkaline and PEM constructions, and the EDLC, pseudocapacitor and hybrid models with their formulas.

You need: electrode potentials and EMF from 4.1, ΔG = −nFE from 4.2, and battery terms from 4.5.

Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell: reactions and voltage

A fuel cell has two porous electrodes separated by an electrolyte. Hydrogen gas, the fuel, enters the anode. Oxygen gas, the oxidant, enters the cathode. The electrolyte lets ions pass but blocks electrons, so electrons travel through the outside circuit and do work.

Anode: H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻; cathode: ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O; overall: H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O; E° = 1.23 V

H₂ is hydrogen, H⁺ is a proton, e⁻ is an electron, O₂ is oxygen, H₂O is water; E° is the standard cell potential at 25 °C and 1 bar.

The standard potential 1.23 V is the thermodynamic upper limit. Under load the working voltage is lower. Activation overpotential is extra voltage to start the electrode reactions. Ohmic overpotential is the drop from electrolyte and contact resistance. Concentration overpotential appears when reactants run low. Efficiency is electrical energy out divided by chemical energy in, so these losses lower it.

Alkaline and PEM fuel cells

The alkaline fuel cell (AFC) uses concentrated KOH as electrolyte. Hydroxide ions OH⁻ carry charge. At the anode hydrogen reacts with OH⁻ to form water and electrons. At the cathode oxygen, water and electrons form OH⁻ again.

Anode: H₂ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O + 2e⁻; cathode: ½O₂ + H₂O + 2e⁻ → 2OH⁻; overall: H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O

OH⁻ is hydroxide ion; other symbols are as before.

AFCs use nickel catalysts, work near 60–90 °C and give high efficiency. Their weakness is CO₂. Even small CO₂ in air reacts with KOH to form carbonate, which blocks pores and lowers conductivity. So AFCs need pure H₂ and O₂, which limits them to space and sealed systems.

The proton-exchange membrane fuel cell (PEMFC) uses a solid polymer membrane that conducts H⁺. The anode reaction is the same as in the acid H₂–O₂ cell. The cathode reaction is also the same. The membrane is thin, so the cell starts quickly and has high power density. It needs platinum catalysts, is sensitive to CO, and the membrane is costly. Vehicles and small backup units use PEMFCs.

Supercapacitors: EDLC and pseudocapacitance

A supercapacitor, also called an ultracapacitor, stores charge without a long chemical reaction. Capacitance C is charge stored per volt: C = Q/V. Q is charge in coulombs (C), V is voltage in volts (V), and C is capacitance in farads (F).

C = Q/V; E = ½CV²; P = V²/(4R)

E is stored energy in joules (J); P is maximum power in watts (W); R is the equivalent series resistance in ohms (Ω).

An electric double-layer capacitor (EDLC) uses two high-surface-area carbon electrodes. Ions from the electrolyte line up at the surface and form a double layer. No charge crosses the interface, so the EDLC charges and discharges fast and lasts many cycles. Its energy density is low compared with a battery.

A pseudocapacitor stores charge by fast surface redox (faradaic) reactions, for example in metal oxides or conducting polymers. It has higher capacitance than an EDLC but slower response and shorter cycle life. A hybrid supercapacitor pairs one EDLC electrode with one faradaic or battery-like electrode. It trades some power for more energy.

Worked example

Question. A H₂–O₂ fuel cell operates at 1.00 V and delivers 0.500 A for one hour. Find the power, the energy in joules, and the energy in watt-hours.

  1. List data: V = 1.00 V, I = 0.500 A, t = 1 h = 3600 s.
  2. Power: P = V I = 1.00 V × 0.500 A = 0.500 W.
  3. Energy in joules: E = P t = 0.500 W × 3600 s = 1800 J.
  4. Energy in watt-hours: E = P t = 0.500 W × 1 h = 0.500 Wh. Check: 0.500 Wh × 3600 J/Wh = 1800 J.

Answer. The cell delivers 0.500 W, 1800 J, and 0.500 Wh in one hour.

Exam traps

  • A fuel cell is not a storage battery. It must receive fuel and oxidant continuously; if the gas supply stops, the cell stops. A battery carries its reactants inside.
  • 1.23 V is the standard thermodynamic voltage, not the terminal voltage under load. Activation, ohmic and concentration overpotentials make the working voltage lower.
  • In an AFC the charge carrier is OH⁻; in a PEMFC it is H⁺. Copying the acid reactions into an alkaline cell gives the wrong electrode equations.
  • For a supercapacitor, energy depends on V². Doubling the voltage gives four times the energy, but the electrolyte decomposition voltage sets a hard limit. The power formula uses 4R, where R is the equivalent series resistance, not the load resistance alone.

4.8 Corrosion: mechanisms, galvanic series and influencing factors

Corrosion eats metal the way a short-circuited battery eats its anode. Iron, steel, copper and zinc give up electrons to their surroundings and try to return to the oxide they came from. The attack is electrochemical, so it runs on the electrode potentials from 4.1. An engineer needs three things: the mechanism, a ranking of metals by how readily they corrode in real water, and the factors that speed the process up. Watch a pipeline or a bicycle in Chennai's monsoon and you are seeing that chemistry at work.

You need: Half-cell reactions and the sign convention for galvanic cells from 4.1, and the Nernst equation from 4.2.

Dry corrosion and wet corrosion

Corrosion is the destruction of a metal by chemical or electrochemical attack from its surroundings. The metal is oxidised: it loses electrons and becomes an ion, an oxide or a salt. There are two routes.

Dry corrosion needs no liquid water. A hot metal surface meets oxygen or a gas such as SO₂ and a thin oxide film grows directly: 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃. Furnace parts, boiler tubes and turbine blades at high temperature fail this way. Whether the film protects depends on whether it is dense. Al₂O₃ and Cr₂O₃ are tight and stick to the metal. Iron oxide is flaky, cracks off and exposes fresh metal.

Wet corrosion is electrochemical and causes most real damage. The metal touches an electrolyte — rain, seawater, soil water, a thin moist film — and tiny local cells form on one surface. At the anode the metal dissolves: M → Mⁿ⁺ + ne⁻, where M is the metal and n its valency. At the cathode something is reduced. In acidic solution: 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂. In neutral, aerated solution: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻.

Electrons travel through the metal from anode to cathode; ions travel through the water. The loop is closed, so the anode keeps dissolving. A millimetre apart on the same steel is enough: an impurity, a grain boundary or a scratch starts the cell.

Rusting of iron

Iron in moist, near-neutral air dissolves at an anodic spot: Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻. At a nearby cathodic spot, dissolved oxygen accepts those electrons: O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻. Double the anodic reaction so the electrons balance, then add the two: 2Fe + O₂ + 2H₂O → 2Fe(OH)₂.

Ferrous hydroxide is unstable in air. More dissolved oxygen oxidises it: 4Fe(OH)₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4Fe(OH)₃. The ferric hydroxide slowly loses water to give hydrated ferric oxide, Fe₂O₃·xH₂O, which is rust. The x only counts the water molecules trapped in the flaky solid.

Three consequences follow. Rust is porous and does not protect, so water keeps reaching the metal and the rate never falls off. Both oxygen and water are needed: dry air, or water with no dissolved oxygen, barely attacks iron, while salt water attacks fast because the dissolved ions carry current. Oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor patches become cathode and anode, which drives differential aeration corrosion under a water drop, under a gasket or inside a crevice.

The galvanic series

The galvanic series ranks metals and alloys by the potential actually measured on them in a real electrolyte, usually seawater. From active to noble: Mg, Zn, Al, Fe, Pb, Sn, Cu, Ag, Au. Two metals far apart make a strong couple and the upper one corrodes. Metals next to each other are nearly safe together.

This is not the standard electrode potential series of 4.1. That series gives one fixed number for a pure element in a 1 M solution of its own ion. The galvanic series depends on the environment: the same metal shifts with the electrolyte, alloys get their own entries, and the values are not reproducible to the millivolt. Use the galvanic series for couples; use electrode potentials for cell EMF.

The passive film explains most disagreements between the two lists. Aluminium, and stainless steel (iron plus chromium), grow a thin tough oxide skin and behave far nobler than the bare metal. Break that film locally — chloride ions do it well — and the exposed metal is a small active anode facing a large passive cathode. It pits fast.

What controls the corrosion rate

  • Position in the series: of two metals in contact, the more active is the anode and corrodes.
  • Purity: impurities and second phases are cathodic to the matrix, so impure zinc corrodes faster than pure zinc.
  • Passive film: a dense Al₂O₃ or Cr₂O₃ skin slows attack; a broken skin concentrates it.
  • Moisture: below a critical humidity (roughly 60% for steel) the electrolyte film cannot form and corrosion almost stops.
  • pH: iron corrodes rapidly in acid; zinc and aluminium are amphoteric and also corrode in strong alkali.
  • Oxygen and chloride: oxygen drives the cathodic reaction; chloride breaks films and raises conductivity.
  • Temperature: a 10 °C rise roughly doubles the rate, until dissolved oxygen starts escaping.
  • Stress: a stressed region is anodic to the unstressed one, so cracks grow.
  • Galvanic coupling and area ratio: a small anode joined to a large cathode corrodes very fast, because the whole cathodic current is squeezed through a tiny anodic area.

Common forms: uniform (whole surface, predictable), galvanic (at the joint of two metals), pitting (small deep holes), crevice (under gaskets and deposits), and stress-corrosion cracking (stress plus a corrosive). Rate is quoted as thickness lost per year, mm year⁻¹, or as mass loss, mdd (mg dm⁻² day⁻¹). A copper pipe losing 0.05 mm year⁻¹ lasts decades; 0.5 mm year⁻¹ means a leak in a year or two.

Worked example

Question. A steel plate of area 1.0 cm² is bolted to a copper plate of area 100 cm² and both are buried in moist soil. The galvanic current flowing between them is 10 µA. (a) Find the anodic current density on the steel. (b) Repeat if the steel plate had been 100 cm² and the copper 1.0 cm², with the same current. (c) State which arrangement is more dangerous.

  1. Steel sits above copper in the galvanic series, so steel is the anode and dissolves: Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻. The copper is the cathode and is protected.
  2. In a galvanic couple every electron that leaves the anode crosses to the cathode, so the anodic current equals the measured current: I = 10 µA, with anode area A = 1.0 cm².
  3. Anodic current density i = I ÷ A = 10 µA ÷ 1.0 cm² = 10 µA cm⁻².
  4. Part (b): with A = 100 cm², i = 10 µA ÷ 100 cm² = 0.10 µA cm⁻².
  5. Same current, 100 times the area, so one hundredth the current density and one hundredth the corrosion per unit area.

Answer. i = 10 µA cm⁻² when the 1.0 cm² steel is joined to the 100 cm² copper (small anode, large cathode: dangerous), and i = 0.10 µA cm⁻² when the steel is the large plate. The small-anode case corrodes 100 times faster per unit area.

Exam traps

  • The galvanic series is not the standard electrode potential series. It depends on the electrolyte, it includes alloys, and it gives no EMF you can put into the Nernst equation.
  • Small anode joined to large cathode is the dangerous case; large anode joined to small cathode is safe. Writing it the other way round loses marks every year. Galvanising works because the zinc coating is a large anode protecting a small exposed area of steel.
  • The corroding metal is the anode, not the cathode. Cathodic protection protects a structure by forcing it to be the cathode.
  • In neutral aerated water the cathode reaction is O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻, not 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂. Hydrogen evolution dominates only in strongly acidic solution.
  • mm year⁻¹ is thickness loss and mdd is mass loss per area per day; you cannot convert one to the other without the metal's density.

4.9 Corrosion control: cathodic protection and protective coatings

Corrosion eats steel where an electrolyte, usually oxygen, and an anodic site meet. Some soils and closed systems corrode even with little oxygen. Control means breaking that electrochemical cell. One way is cathodic protection: supply electrons so the steel becomes the cathode and stops dissolving. Another is a protective coating: a barrier keeps water and oxygen away from the surface. A third is an inhibitor: a chemical that slows the electrode reactions. These methods are used on buried pipelines, ship hulls, water tanks, bridge rebars and car bodies. This chapter explains the methods, their limits and the design rules that make them work.

You need: 4.8 for the corrosion cell and galvanic series, and the electrode reactions in 4.1 and 4.2.

Cathodic protection: two ways to supply electrons

At an anodic site, metal dissolves: M → Mⁿ⁺ + n e⁻. Here M is the metal atom and n is the number of electrons. At the cathode, oxygen or hydrogen ions accept electrons. Cathodic protection makes the whole steel surface the cathode. An external source supplies the electrons that the anodic reaction would otherwise release. The steel potential shifts negative and dissolution stops.

Sacrificial anode. Connect the steel to a metal that is more active in the galvanic series, such as zinc, magnesium or an aluminium alloy. This anode corrodes in place of the steel. The steel is the cathode. The method needs no power supply and suits buried pipelines, water tanks and ship hulls. The anode is consumed and must be replaced.

Impressed current. Use a DC supply. Connect its positive terminal to an inert anode, such as graphite or mixed metal oxide, and its negative terminal to the steel. The anode sits in the electrolyte, soil or water. The supply pushes current from the anode through the electrolyte to the steel. The steel becomes the cathode. This method suits large structures and high-resistivity soil. The current can be adjusted.

The protection potential for buried steel is about −0.85 V against a copper-copper sulfate reference electrode. A second criterion is a negative shift of 100 mV from the natural potential. The current needed depends on area, coating quality and soil resistivity:

I = i × A

I is the total protection current in amperes (A); i is the required current density in amperes per square metre (A m⁻²); A is the protected area in square metres (m²). If i is in mA m⁻², I comes out in mA.

Too much current is overprotection. It produces hydrogen at the steel, can disbond coatings and can embrittle high-strength steel. Set the current by potential measurement, not by guess.

Coatings: paint, metal and oxide layers

Coatings block the electrolyte or change the surface chemistry. Good surface preparation matters more than thick paint. A paint has a pigment, a binder and a solvent. The binder forms the film; the pigment gives colour and can inhibit corrosion. Varnish is a clear resin coating. Enamel is a hard, often heat-cured coating.

  • Galvanizing dips steel in molten zinc or electrodeposits zinc. Zinc is anodic to steel. A scratch exposes steel, but the nearby zinc corrodes first and protects the steel. This is sacrificial protection.
  • Tinning coats steel with tin. Tin is cathodic to steel. A scratch exposes steel and tin. The small exposed steel area becomes the anode and the large tin area the cathode. The anode current density is high and the steel pits rapidly. Tinplate food cans need a lacquer.
  • Electroplating deposits a metal such as nickel, chromium or zinc from a solution using current. It gives a decorative or hard layer. A thin plating with pores can make corrosion worse if the plated metal is cathodic to the base.
  • Anodizing makes aluminium the anode in an electrolyte, so a thick aluminium oxide layer grows. The layer is hard and can be dyed.

Conversion coatings change the metal surface into a phosphate or chromate layer. They improve paint adhesion. A multilayer coating combines a primer, an intermediate coat and a topcoat. The primer bonds and inhibits, the intermediate coat gives barrier thickness, and the topcoat resists weather and abrasion. Each layer has a job; one thick layer is not the same.

Inhibitors and design rules

Inhibitors are chemicals added in small amounts to water, acid or a closed system. An anodic inhibitor forms a passive oxide film on the anode, for example nitrite, molybdate or chromate. If the dose is too low, it covers only part of the surface and pitting can become worse. A cathodic inhibitor slows the cathodic reaction, often by precipitating a film at cathodic sites. A mixed inhibitor adsorbs on both sites; many organic amines work this way. Inhibitors are common in cooling water, boiler water, pickling acids and closed loops.

Design rules reduce the chance of a corrosion cell:

  • Avoid dissimilar metal joints. If they are unavoidable, insulate them and keep the less noble metal large compared with the noble one.
  • Drain moisture. Shape the part so water runs off and cannot collect in crevices.
  • Avoid crevices and trapped water under washers, gaskets and coatings.
  • Use inhibitors where the same water is recirculated.
  • Maintain coatings. A small break in a barrier coating can become a small anode beside a large cathode.
  • Design for inspection and replacement of anodes.

Cathodic protection and coatings work together. A coating lowers the current needed; cathodic protection covers the breaks. Do not use a coating that disbonds under cathodic protection. Check the protection potential after installation and at intervals.

Worked example

Question. A steel water tank has a wetted area of 20 m². The coating is in poor condition, and the required protection current density is 10 mA m⁻². Find the total protection current in mA and in A.

  1. Area A = 20 m².
  2. Current density i = 10 mA m⁻².
  3. Use I = i × A = 10 mA m⁻² × 20 m² = 200 mA.
  4. Convert to amperes: 200 mA ÷ 1000 = 0.20 A.
  5. The current is 200 mA or 0.20 A.

Answer. The total protection current is 200 mA, which is 0.20 A.

Exam traps

  • In cathodic protection the protected steel is the cathode. The sacrificial anode is the metal that corrodes. Reversing these two loses marks.
  • Overprotection is not extra safety. Too negative a potential evolves hydrogen, disbonds coatings and can crack high-strength steel.
  • Galvanizing and tinning are not the same. Zinc is anodic to steel and protects a scratch; tin is cathodic, so a scratch in tinplate leaves a small steel anode beside a large tin cathode and the exposed steel corrodes fast.
  • A low dose of an anodic inhibitor can worsen pitting. The inhibitor must cover the whole surface.
  • Keep current-density units consistent. mA m⁻² × m² gives mA; divide by 1000 for A. Do not mix cm² with m².

Takeaways

  1. A galvanic cell separates the two halves of a redox reaction so electrons travel an outside wire and do work.
  2. Cell EMF is always E(cathode) − E(anode); a positive EMF means the cell reaction is spontaneous.
  3. The Nernst equation shows that potential is not fixed by nature alone — concentration changes it, by about 0.059 V per tenfold change for a one-electron step at 298 K.
  4. Electrode potentials become useful only against a fixed reference, which is why the SHE, calomel and glass electrodes exist.
  5. A battery is judged by voltage per cell, capacity in Ah, energy density in Wh/kg and the number of charge cycles it survives.
  6. Lead-acid is cheap and heavy; Li-ion is light and costly — the design choice depends on weight and cycle life more than on chemistry.
  7. A fuel cell stores its energy outside the cell, so it never goes flat until the fuel line is closed.
  8. Corrosion is a short-circuited galvanic cell on the metal surface; stop either the anode, the cathode or the electrolyte path and corrosion stops.
  9. Cathodic protection and coatings work by opposite ideas: cathodic protection makes the metal the cathode, coatings simply keep the electrolyte away.

The electrode where oxidation happens; electrons leave the cell from it.

Depositing a metal coating on a cathode by electrolysis of a salt solution of the coating metal.
EMF
Electromotive force, the open-circuit potential difference between the two electrodes of a cell, in volts.
Faraday constant
Charge on one mole of electrons, 96485 C/mol, written F.
Half-cell
One electrode dipped in its own ion solution, physically one half of a galvanic cell.
Inhibitor
A substance added in small amount that slows corrosion, usually by adsorbing on the metal surface.
Molar conductivity
Conductivity divided by molar concentration, Λm = 1000κ/M, in S cm² mol⁻¹.
Overvoltage
The extra voltage needed above the reversible value to drive an electrode reaction at a useful rate.
Oxidation
Loss of electrons by a species, which makes it more positive in charge.
Passivation
Formation of a thin adherent oxide film that slows further corrosion, as in aluminium and stainless steel.
Polarization
Shift of an electrode potential away from its equilibrium value when current flows.
Redox couple
A pair such as Cu²⁺/Cu that can be interconverted by gain or loss of electrons.
Reduction
Gain of electrons by a species, which makes it less positive in charge.
Reference electrode
An electrode of known, stable potential against which an unknown potential is measured.
Standard conditions
For electrode potentials: 298 K, 1 bar for gases, and 1 M activity for dissolved species.
