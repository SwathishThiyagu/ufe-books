7.1 Water quality parameters, standards and hardness

A water supply is judged by a short list of measured numbers: how clear it is, what it contains, and whether bacteria are present. Each number is compared with a standard. Hardness is the one number that packs calcium and magnesium together, expressed as calcium carbonate, so any two waters can be compared. This chapter lists the parameters and units, gives the drinking-water limits, and shows the hardness calculation that examiners ask for.

You need: Unit 1, coordination compounds, for why EDTA binds Ca²⁺ tightly at pH 10; here titration is only a method.

The short list of measured numbers

Water quality is a set of measured numbers compared with a standard. Physical tests describe appearance: turbidity in NTU (nephelometric turbidity units), colour in Hazen units, odour as threshold odour number, and temperature in °C. Chemical tests give pH, TDS (total dissolved solids, mg/L), conductivity (µS/cm), chloride, sulphate, nitrate, fluoride and iron (each mg/L), dissolved oxygen, BOD (biochemical oxygen demand, mg/L) and COD (chemical oxygen demand, mg/L). Biological tests count coliform bacteria as MPN (most probable number) per 100 mL.

The BIS 10500 drinking-water standard gives an acceptable limit and a permissible limit. The permissible limit applies only when no better source exists.

Hardness is Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ in disguise

Hardness is the soap-consuming power of water, caused almost entirely by Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺. It is reported as if all these ions were CaCO₃, so one number compares any water.

H = m × 50 ÷ E

H is hardness as CaCO₃ in mg/L; m is the mass concentration of the ion in mg/L; E is the equivalent weight of the ion (Ca²⁺ = 20, Mg²⁺ = 12); 50 is the equivalent weight of CaCO₃.

Units: 1 meq/L = 50 mg/L as CaCO₃; 1 ppm = 1 mg/L in dilute water; 1 °French = 10 mg/L as CaCO₃; 1 °Clark = 14.3 mg/L as CaCO₃.

Temporary hardness comes from bicarbonates of Ca and Mg and is removed by boiling. Permanent hardness comes from chlorides, sulphates and nitrates and needs softening (see 7.2). Total hardness is the sum of the two. Classification: soft below 60, moderately hard 60–120, hard 120–180, very hard above 180 mg/L as CaCO₃.

EDTA titration in one minute

Hardness is measured by titrating with EDTA (ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid) at pH 10, buffered with ammonia. Eriochrome Black T forms a wine-red complex with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺; at the end point EDTA takes the ions and the colour turns blue. The EDTA volume gives total hardness. Only a few universities teach this chapter in full depth; the calculation matters more than instrument detail.

Worked example

Question. A groundwater sample contains Ca²⁺ = 80 mg/L and Mg²⁺ = 24 mg/L, with no other hardness-causing ions. Find the total hardness as CaCO₃ in mg/L and in meq/L, and say whether it meets the BIS acceptable limit of 200 mg/L. Take equivalent weights Ca²⁺ = 20, Mg²⁺ = 12, CaCO₃ = 50.

  1. Ca²⁺ contribution = 80 mg/L × 50 ÷ 20 = 200 mg/L as CaCO₃.
  2. Mg²⁺ contribution = 24 mg/L × 50 ÷ 12 = 100 mg/L as CaCO₃.
  3. Total hardness = 200 + 100 = 300 mg/L as CaCO₃.
  4. In meq/L: 300 ÷ 50 = 6 meq/L.
  5. Compare: 300 is above the acceptable limit 200 but below the permissible 600, so the water is used only when no better source exists.

Answer. Total hardness is 300 mg/L as CaCO₃, equal to 6 meq/L; it fails the acceptable limit but stays within the permissible limit.

Exam traps

  • Reporting 80 mg/L Ca²⁺ as 80 mg/L hardness; you must convert with ×50 ÷ 20.
  • Using the molecular weight 100 of CaCO₃ instead of its equivalent weight 50.
  • Mixing temporary and permanent hardness; both are included in total hardness.
  • Forgetting the pH 10 buffer in EDTA titration; the end point becomes poor and the result wrong.
  • Treating the permissible limit as normal; it applies only when no alternative source exists.

7.2 Boiler feed water, boiler troubles and water softening

A boiler turns water into steam, so impurities left in feed water concentrate in the drum. The targets are simple: hardness near zero, low oxygen, feed-water pH about 8.5–9.5, boiler-water pH about 10–11, low silica, controlled alkalinity. This chapter links each trouble—scale, sludge, corrosion, caustic embrittlement and carryover—to its impurity, then gives the external softening or internal conditioning that removes or controls it.

You need Chapter 7.1: hardness as CaCO₃ is the input to every dose calculation here.

Troubles and their causes

Water targets are numbers: hardness near zero, low dissolved oxygen, feed-water pH about 8.5–9.5, boiler-water pH about 10–11, low silica, controlled alkalinity. Every trouble comes from an impurity that misses one target.

Scale is a hard deposit of CaCO₃, CaSO₄, Mg(OH)₂ or silica on tubes. Its thermal conductivity is low, so the tube overheats, fuel is wasted and tubes fail. External softening removes scale formers before entry.

Sludge is a loose precipitate. It settles in the drum and is removed by blowdown.

Corrosion is oxygen, CO₂ or acid attack. Oxygen pits steel; CO₂ lowers pH. Mechanical deaeration strips oxygen; hydrazine or sodium sulphite removes the rest.

Caustic embrittlement is NaOH concentrating in crevices, rivet holes and bends. The steel cracks. Sodium sulphate or phosphate conditioning prevents it.

Carryover is water leaving with steam. Priming is sudden; foaming is stable froth. High alkalinity, oil and suspended solids stabilise foam. Antifoam and blowdown control it.

Softening outside and conditioning inside

Lime-soda process: lime removes CO₂ and carbonate hardness; soda removes non-carbonate hardness. Cold lime-soda leaves about 30–60 mg/L hardness; hot gives less.

  • CO₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCO₃↓ + H₂O
  • Ca(HCO₃)₂ + Ca(OH)₂ → 2CaCO₃↓ + 2H₂O
  • Mg(HCO₃)₂ + 2Ca(OH)₂ → 2CaCO₃↓ + Mg(OH)₂↓ + 2H₂O
  • CaSO₄ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃↓ + Na₂SO₄
  • MgSO₄ + Ca(OH)₂ → Mg(OH)₂↓ + CaSO₄
Lime as CaO = (56/100)[CO₂ + Ca(HCO₃)₂ + 2 Mg(HCO₃)₂ + MgSO₄ + other Mg non-carbonate]

all concentrations in mg/L as CaCO₃; 56/100 converts CaCO₃ equivalent to CaO; the factor 2 is two Ca(OH)₂ per Mg(HCO₃)₂; count MgSO₄ and every other magnesium non-carbonate hardness in the bracket.

Lime adds calcium, so it can only remove magnesium non-carbonate hardness. Calcium non-carbonate hardness such as CaSO₄ and CaCl₂ needs soda ash, not lime.

Soda as Na₂CO₃ = (106/100)(non-carbonate hardness)

non-carbonate hardness in mg/L as CaCO₃; 106/100 converts CaCO₃ equivalent to Na₂CO₃. Equivalent masses: CaO 56, Ca(OH)₂ 74, Na₂CO₃ 106, CaCO₃ 100.

Zeolite is a sodium aluminosilicate: Na₂Ze + Ca²⁺ → CaZe + 2Na⁺. Brine NaCl regenerates it.

Demineralisation: cation resin in H⁺ form replaces cations with H⁺; anion resin in OH⁻ form replaces anions with OH⁻. Two-bed units run in series; a mixed bed gives very low conductivity.

Internal conditioning adds phosphate, carbonate, calgon (sodium hexametaphosphate) or colloidal material. Calgon disperses precipitates. Blowdown removes sludge and dissolved solids. Some syllabi call this internal treatment; only a few universities examine the dosing arithmetic.

Worked example

Question. A boiler feed water contains free CO₂ = 44 mg/L, calcium bicarbonate hardness = 100 mg/L as CaCO₃, magnesium bicarbonate hardness = 50 mg/L as CaCO₃, and calcium sulphate hardness = 80 mg/L as CaCO₃. Calculate the lime (as CaO) and soda (as Na₂CO₃) needed for 1000 L.

  1. CO₂ as CaCO₃ = 44 × (100/44) = 100 mg/L.
  2. Lime = 0.56 × (100 + 100 + 2 × 50) = 0.56 × 300 = 168 mg/L as CaO.
  3. Soda = 1.06 × 80 = 84.8 mg/L as Na₂CO₃. The 80 mg/L is the non-carbonate hardness.
  4. For 1000 L: lime = 168 mg/L × 1000 L = 168000 mg = 168 g; soda = 84.8 mg/L × 1000 L = 84800 mg = 84.8 g.

Answer. For 1000 L, lime required is 168 g as CaO and soda required is 84.8 g as Na₂CO₃.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the factor 2 on Mg(HCO₃)₂ in the lime formula.
  • Mixing masses of CaO, Ca(OH)₂, CaCO₃ and Na₂CO₃ without converting through equivalents.
  • Calling sludge scale: sludge is loose and blowdown removes it; scale is hard and needs softening.
  • Thinking zeolite removes all ions: it exchanges hardness cations for sodium ions only.
  • Treating caustic embrittlement as oxygen pitting: embrittlement is NaOH cracking in crevices.

7.3 Municipal water treatment and disinfection

A city water plant is a fixed train of steps. Each step removes one class of impurity: screens hold back leaves, aeration oxidises iron and manganese, coagulation turns fine mud into settleable floc, filters polish the water, and disinfection kills pathogens. The train ends with a small chlorine residual that protects water as it travels through mains to taps. The same sequence treats river, lake or reservoir water for millions of people. Only a few universities teach this chapter; the exam emphasis is the sequence and the dose arithmetic.

You need: Chapter 7.1 for the turbidity, coliform and pH limits, and the viscosity idea behind Stokes' law.

The treatment train

Raw water enters through an intake. Coarse screens stop branches and rags; fine screens stop leaves and grit. The water then falls over weirs in an aerator. Air oxidises soluble Fe²⁺ to insoluble Fe(OH)₃ and Mn²⁺ to MnO₂, which later settle or filter out. Aeration also strips CO₂, raising pH, and removes odour.

Coagulation follows. Alum, Al₂(SO₄)₃·18H₂O, ferric chloride or polyaluminium chloride is dosed and mixed fast. The positive metal ions neutralise the negative charge on clay particles, so they stick together. Alkalinity must be present; if it is low, lime is added. The best pH band is about 6.5–7.5 for alum. Slow mixing, called flocculation, grows the floc to millimetre size.

In sedimentation the floc settles. Stokes' law gives v = d²(ρp − ρ)g ÷ 18η, where v is settling velocity (m/s), d is particle diameter (m), ρp is particle density (kg/m³), ρ is water density (kg/m³), g is 9.81 m/s², and η is dynamic viscosity (Pa·s). Bigger, denser flocs settle faster.

Filtration is the final polish. Slow sand beds run at 0.1–0.3 m/h and develop a biological layer. Rapid sand beds run at 4–8 m/h and are cleaned by backwashing.

Disinfection and dose

Disinfection is a controlled kill of bacteria and viruses, and it must leave a guard in the pipe.

Chlorine added to water forms two species: Cl₂ + H₂O → HOCl + HCl, and HOCl ⇌ H⁺ + OCl⁻. HOCl is the strong disinfectant; OCl⁻ is weak. The equilibrium shifts with pH. Below pH 7 most chlorine is HOCl, so disinfection is fast. Above pH 8 most is OCl⁻, so the dose must rise.

The breakpoint curve plots residual chlorine against dose. At first chlorine is used up by reducing agents and organics; then chloramines form; past the breakpoint free chlorine appears. Chloramines last longer but kill more slowly. Ozone is a stronger oxidant and leaves no residual. UV light damages DNA and leaves no residual. A plant usually ends with chlorine, carrying 0.2–0.5 mg/L free residual to the taps.

Bleaching powder is dosed by its available chlorine, the percentage of its mass that behaves as chlorine. Mass needed = chlorine mass ÷ available fraction.

Worked example

Question. A service reservoir holds 50,000 L of filtered water. The required dose is 0.5 mg/L of available chlorine, and the bleaching powder in stock is 30% available chlorine. Find the mass of bleaching powder to add.

  1. Chlorine needed = 0.5 mg/L × 50,000 L = 25,000 mg = 25 g.
  2. Available fraction = 30% = 0.30.
  3. Bleaching powder = 25 g ÷ 0.30 = 83.3 g.

Answer. Add 83.3 g of bleaching powder, which supplies 25 g of available chlorine.

Exam traps

  • Using the coagulant dose as the chlorine dose. Alum removes turbidity; chlorine disinfects.
  • Forgetting to divide by available chlorine. A 30% powder needs about 3.3 times the chlorine mass, not the same mass.
  • Saying chlorine residual is zero after disinfection. A 0.2–0.5 mg/L residual must remain.
  • Mixing up slow and rapid sand beds. Slow beds are 0.1–0.3 m/h with a biological layer; rapid beds are 4–8 m/h and need backwashing.
  • Reversing Stokes' law. Larger d and larger density difference raise v; larger η lowers v.

7.4 Desalination: reverse osmosis and electrodialysis

Ordinary treatment stops at suspended solids, colour and bacteria. Dissolved salt passes straight through. Sea water carries about 35,000 mg/L of total dissolved solids (TDS), and drinking water limits sit near 500 mg/L, so a coastal town cannot reach 7.1's figures by filtration alone. Desalination removes the salt itself. Two membrane processes do most of that work: reverse osmosis pushes water through a membrane and leaves the ions behind, while electrodialysis pulls the ions through membranes with a DC field. Both run on the Indian coast. Only a few universities teach this chapter.

You need 7.1 for the TDS and chloride limits, and Unit 4 (electrochemistry) for ions and conduction under a field.

Reverse osmosis: pushing water past the osmotic pressure

Osmosis is water's own drift across a semipermeable membrane, from the dilute side to the concentrated side. The pressure that just stops that drift is the osmotic pressure π. Van 't Hoff's law gives it.

π = iCRT

π is the osmotic pressure in atm; i is the number of ions one formula unit gives (2 for NaCl); C is the salt concentration in mol/L; R = 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K); T is the absolute temperature in K.

Push the feed side harder than π and the flow reverses. Water is squeezed through the membrane while the dissolved ions are held back. That is reverse osmosis (RO). The applied pressure must sit above π, not at it: at exactly π, nothing flows either way.

A module never converts all the feed. The fraction that becomes product is the recovery, and the rest leaves as brine. Salt stays behind while water leaves, so the brine grows more concentrated along the module and the local π rises with distance. The feed pressure must beat the highest π in the module, not the average.

  • Membranes: cellulose acetate, or polyamide thin-film composite for higher flux and better salt rejection.
  • Modules: flat sheets wound in a spiral around a perforated product tube, giving large area in a small shell.
  • Pretreatment: filters and an antiscalant, so calcium carbonate and sulphate do not crystallise on the surface.
  • Working pressure: about 15–30 bar for brackish water, 50–80 bar for sea water.

Electrodialysis: moving the ions instead

Electrodialysis uses the ions themselves. A stack alternates cation-exchange membranes (they pass only cations) with anion-exchange membranes, and a DC field is applied across the two end electrodes. Cations drift toward the cathode, anions toward the anode. In one compartment the cation membrane lets cations out and the anion membrane blocks them, so the water is stripped of salt. In the next compartment ions arrive from both sides and become concentrated.

Energy depends on the number of ions removed, so electrodialysis suits brackish water of a few thousand mg/L. At sea-water strength the current needed makes it far too costly. The polarity is reversed periodically, which lifts deposited scale off the membranes and controls fouling.

Thermal desalination, mainly multi-stage flash, evaporates water in stages at falling pressure and condenses it; it is chosen where cheap waste heat exists.

Worked example

Question. Sea water is modelled as 0.5 mol/L NaCl at 300 K, with i = 2 and R = 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K). Find the osmotic pressure and the minimum feed pressure needed for reverse osmosis.

  1. Write the data: i = 2, C = 0.5 mol/L, R = 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K), T = 300 K.
  2. Apply van 't Hoff's law: π = iCRT.
  3. Substitute: π = 2 × 0.5 × 0.0821 × 300.
  4. Multiply: 2 × 0.5 = 1; 1 × 0.0821 = 0.0821; 0.0821 × 300 = 24.63 atm.
  5. Convert to bar: 24.63 × 1.013 = 24.95 bar, about 25 bar.
  6. Water crosses the membrane only when the applied pressure exceeds π, so the feed must be pushed above about 25 bar.

Answer. π = 24.6 atm (about 25 bar), so the minimum feed pressure is just above 25 bar; real sea-water plants run at 50–80 bar because recovery concentrates the brine and pressure drops along the module.

Exam traps

  • Using π itself as the operating pressure instead of a pressure above it; at exactly π there is no permeate flow.
  • Forgetting that recovery raises the brine concentration, so π climbs along the module and the feed pressure must beat the highest value.
  • Swapping the driving forces: reverse osmosis is driven by pressure, electrodialysis by a DC electric field.
  • Unit slip: C in mol/L with R = 0.0821 returns atm, not bar or pascal; 1 atm ≈ 1.013 bar.
  • Using i = 2 for NaCl but forgetting i = 1 for a sugar or other non-dissociating solute.

Check yourself

Remember State van 't Hoff's law for osmotic pressure and name each symbol with its unit. 2 marks

π = iCRT, with π in atm, i unitless, C in mol/L, R = 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K) and T in K.

Understand Why must the applied pressure be greater than the osmotic pressure rather than equal to it? 3 marks

At exactly π the water flow is balanced and the net permeate is zero. Only the excess over π, the net driving pressure, pushes water through, so the feed pressure must exceed π.

Apply Brackish water is modelled as 0.05 mol/L NaCl at 298 K, i = 2, R = 0.0821 L·atm/(mol·K). Find π. 3 marks

π = 2 × 0.05 × 0.0821 × 298 = 2.45 atm, about 2.5 bar.

Analyze Explain why electrodialysis is uneconomical for sea water while it is fine for brackish water. 4 marks

Its energy scales with the quantity of salt removed. Sea water holds roughly ten times the salt of brackish water, so the current and the energy per cubic metre rise by about the same factor, while reverse osmosis energy scales mainly with pressure and volume.

Understand What does periodic polarity reversal achieve in an electrodialysis stack? 2 marks

It swaps the depleting and concentrating compartments, so scale that formed on a membrane is redissolved or pushed away, controlling fouling without stopping the plant.

Analyze A sea-water RO plant runs at 60 bar while the bulk osmotic pressure is 25 bar. Give two reasons for the extra pressure. 4 marks

The brine at the outlet is more concentrated than the feed, so its π is well above 25 bar; and pressure losses along the module and the need for a useful flux at the far end add to the requirement.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Large Indian coastal plants pair reverse osmosis with energy-recovery devices that take pressure out of the brine stream and return it to the feed, which is what brought sea-water costs down sharply. Research now looks at fouling-resistant membrane coatings, forward osmosis using a draw solution, and capacitive deionisation for low-salinity streams. Electrodialysis reverses into electrodialysis reversal for difficult feeds, and bipolar-membrane stacks are used to split salt into acid and base. Worth searching next: specific energy consumption in kWh per m³, and the brine-disposal rules that decide whether a plant can be built at all.

Takeaways

  1. Water quality is judged by measured parameters compared with drinking-water or feed-water standards.
  2. Hardness is the sum of Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ expressed as CaCO₃ equivalent, not the separate ion concentrations.
  3. Carbonate hardness is removed by boiling; non-carbonate hardness needs chemical or ion-exchange softening.
  4. Every boiler trouble has a source impurity: hardness makes scale, oxygen and CO₂ cause corrosion, high NaOH can cause caustic embrittlement.
  5. External softening removes hardness before the boiler; internal conditioning controls the small amount left inside.
  6. A municipal plant follows a fixed train: screening, coagulation and flocculation, sedimentation, filtration and disinfection.
  7. Chlorination must leave a residual to protect water in the pipe network after the plant.
  8. RO uses pressure above osmotic pressure to push water through a membrane; electrodialysis uses a DC field to pull ions through selective membranes.

Remember and understand

Understand Define hardness of water. Distinguish carbonate hardness from non-carbonate hardness, and write the expression for total hardness as CaCO₃. 8 marks

Hardness is Ca²⁺ plus Mg²⁺ expressed as CaCO₃; Carbonate hardness comes from bicarbonates and is removed by boiling; Non-carbonate hardness comes from chlorides and sulfates and is not removed by boiling; Total hardness = Ca hardness + Mg hardness as CaCO₃; Report in mg/L as CaCO₃.

Remember List the common water quality parameters used for drinking water and boiler feed water. State what each parameter measures. 8 marks

pH; Turbidity; TDS; Hardness; Alkalinity; Dissolved oxygen; BOD and COD; Silica, chloride, iron and residual chlorine; Boiler feed limits are stricter for hardness, oxygen and silica.

Understand Explain scale and sludge formation in boilers. Give the cause, effect and one control measure for each. 8 marks

Scale from CaCO₃, Mg(OH)₂ and CaSO₄ on hot surfaces; Scale lowers heat transfer and can overheat metal; Control scale by softening and blowdown; Sludge is a loose precipitate that can settle and choke; Control sludge by blowdown and phosphate treatment.

Understand Describe the sequence of steps in a municipal water treatment plant and state the impurity removed at each step. 8 marks

Screening removes large solids; Coagulation and flocculation remove colloidal turbidity; Sedimentation removes flocs; Filtration removes fine particles; Disinfection kills pathogens; Residual chlorine protects the distribution system.

Apply

Apply A water sample contains Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺. Explain how you would calculate total hardness as CaCO₃ from their concentrations. Include the conversion factors and units. 12 marks

Convert mg Ca²⁺/L to mg CaCO₃/L using 100/40; Convert mg Mg²⁺/L to mg CaCO₃/L using 100/24.3; Total hardness = Ca hardness + Mg hardness; Report as mg/L as CaCO₃; Show a sample calculation; Mention EDTA titration for measurement.

Apply Compare lime-soda softening with zeolite softening. Write the main reactions and state one advantage and one limitation of each. 12 marks

Lime-soda uses Ca(OH)₂ and Na₂CO₃; Lime removes temporary hardness and Mg; Soda removes non-carbonate Ca; Lime-soda produces sludge; Zeolite exchanges Na⁺ for Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺; Zeolite needs NaCl regeneration and is not for turbid water.

Apply Explain breakpoint chlorination with a simple graph of residual chlorine against chlorine dose. Why is breakpoint chlorination used in municipal supply? 12 marks

Chlorine demand from reducing and organic matter; Combined chlorine forms first; Breakpoint occurs at minimum residual; Free residual appears after breakpoint; Ensures disinfection and leaves residual; Avoids taste and odour from excess combined chlorine.

Apply Describe reverse osmosis with a labelled diagram. Explain why the applied pressure must exceed the osmotic pressure and name the pretreatment needed for a hard feed water. 12 marks

Semipermeable membrane; Applied pressure greater than osmotic pressure; Water permeates and ions are rejected; Concentrate stream and recovery; Pretreatment removes hardness, turbidity and chlorine; Scaling control and energy cost.

Apply Draw an electrodialysis stack and explain how cation- and anion-selective membranes separate ions into dilute and concentrate streams. 12 marks

DC electrodes; Alternating cation and anion membranes; Cations move toward cathode and anions toward anode; Salt concentration alternates in compartments; Dilute and concentrate streams leave the stack; Polarity reversal helps control fouling.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze A boiler shows scale on tubes, corrosion pits and occasional caustic cracking. Relate each trouble to a likely feed-water impurity and propose a control measure. 12 marks

Scale from Ca and Mg hardness; soften feed and control blowdown; Corrosion pits from dissolved O₂ and CO₂; deaerate and use oxygen scavenger; Caustic cracking from high NaOH at stressed seams; control alkalinity and use phosphate or coordinated pH; Testing and monitoring of feed and boiler water.

Analyze Compare external and internal treatment of boiler feed water. For a plant using very hard water, recommend a treatment train and justify each unit. 14 marks

External treatment: lime-soda, zeolite or demineralisation before boiler; Internal treatment: phosphate, hydrazine, amines and blowdown; Hard water needs external softening first; Deaeration follows softening; Internal conditioning handles residual hardness and oxygen; Consider economics and steam purity.

Evaluate A city source is turbid and contaminated with sewage. Evaluate the treatment train needed to produce safe drinking water and explain where chlorination should be applied. 14 marks

Coagulation and flocculation for turbidity; Sedimentation and filtration; Disinfection after filtration; Chlorine dose and contact time; Residual chlorine in distribution; Monitor pH and turbidity and avoid excess trihalomethane.

Evaluate A textile plant has hard, brackish well water. Compare softening followed by reverse osmosis with electrodialysis, and recommend one route with reasons. 16 marks

Hardness causes scaling; soften before membrane processes; RO removes ions and organics; ED removes ions only and needs pretreatment for turbidity and hardness; Compare energy, recovery, fouling and brine disposal; Recommend RO for high TDS with organic load; Recommend ED for brackish water with low organics; Justify with cost and product quality.

Glossary

Alkalinity
The capacity of water to neutralise acid, mainly from bicarbonate, carbonate and hydroxide ions, reported as mg/L as CaCO₃.
BOD
The oxygen consumed by microorganisms while oxidising organic matter in water, usually over five days and reported as mg/L.
Boiler corrosion
Attack on boiler metal by dissolved oxygen, carbon dioxide or acid, producing pits and thinning.
CaCO₃ equivalent
The mass of CaCO₃ that would give the same number of equivalents as the ion being reported.
Caustic embrittlement
Cracking of boiler steel along grain boundaries caused by concentrated NaOH at stressed seams.
Chlorination
Disinfection of water with chlorine gas, hypochlorite or hypochlorous acid.
Coagulation
Destabilisation of colloidal particles by a chemical coagulant so that they can stick together.
COD
The oxygen equivalent of organic matter oxidised by a strong chemical oxidant, reported as mg/L.
Dissolved oxygen
The concentration of dissolved oxygen gas in water, usually mg/L.
Electrodialysis
A desalination process in which a DC electric field moves ions through ion-selective membranes into concentrate streams.
Flocculation
Gentle mixing that brings destabilised particles into contact so they grow into larger flocs.
Hardness
The concentration of Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ in water, expressed as mg CaCO₃ per litre.
Ion exchange
Reversible exchange of ions between a solid resin and a solution.
Lime-soda process
Precipitation softening with Ca(OH)₂ and Na₂CO₃, which convert soluble hardness ions into CaCO₃ and Mg(OH)₂.
Osmotic pressure
The pressure that must be applied to a solution to stop net flow of solvent through a semipermeable membrane.
Priming
The carryover of droplets of boiler water into steam.
Regeneration
Restoring an exhausted ion-exchange resin by washing it with a concentrated solution such as NaCl.
Residual chlorine
The chlorine species left after treatment that protect the distribution system against microbial regrowth.
Reverse osmosis
A pressure-driven membrane process that passes water while rejecting most dissolved ions when the applied pressure exceeds osmotic pressure.
Scale
A hard deposit such as CaCO₃ or Mg(OH)₂ that forms on hot boiler surfaces.
Sludge
A loose precipitate suspended in boiler water that can settle in low-flow regions.
Softening
Removal of Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ from water to reduce hardness.
Total dissolved solids
The total mass of dissolved solids in a known volume of water, usually reported as mg/L.
Turbidity
The cloudiness of water caused by suspended particles, measured in nephelometric turbidity units.
Water quality
The set of physical, chemical and biological measurements used to judge whether water is fit for a stated use.
Zeolite
A natural or synthetic ion-exchange material that swaps Na⁺ for Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ in water softening.
