5.1 Polymers and macromolecules: terms and classification

A polymer is a large molecule built by joining many small monomer units. The repeat unit is the part that repeats along the chain, and the degree of polymerisation tells how many such units are joined. Polymers are classified by source, heat behaviour, chain shape, and number of monomer types. This classification helps predict properties and choose materials, from a PET water bottle to a jute bag. Understanding these terms is the first step to naming and using polymers correctly.

You need Unit 1 for covalent bonding and the tetrahedral carbon backbone.

Monomer, repeat unit and degree of polymerisation

A monomer is a small molecule that can join with others. Ethylene (C₂H₄) is a monomer. A polymer forms when many monomers join. The part that repeats along the chain is the repeat unit (some syllabi call it the mer). For polyethylene, the repeat unit is –CH₂–CH₂–.

The degree of polymerisation (DP) is the number of repeat units in one polymer chain. DP is a whole number. A chain with DP = 500 has 500 repeat units. An oligomer is a short polymer with DP between about 2 and 10. It behaves more like a small molecule.

For a chain to grow, each monomer must have functionality of at least two. Functionality is the number of reactive sites that can form bonds. Ethylene has a double bond that opens to give two bonds, so functionality = 2. A molecule with only one reactive site cannot extend a chain.

The molar mass of a polymer sample is an average because chains have different DP values. The DP worked out from a sample's molar mass is therefore an average degree of polymerisation, not the DP of one chain. We name average molar mass here and give formulas in 5.7.

Homopolymers and copolymers

A homopolymer has only one type of monomer. Polyethylene from ethylene only is a homopolymer. A copolymer has two or more types of monomer joined in the same chain. The arrangement matters.

  • Random copolymer: monomers arranged randomly. Example: styrene-butadiene rubber.
  • Alternating copolymer: monomers alternate strictly A-B-A-B.
  • Block copolymer: long stretches of one monomer then long stretches of another. Example: SBS rubber (styrene-butadiene-styrene).
  • Graft copolymer: a chain of one monomer is attached as a branch to a backbone of another.

The number of monomer types is a classification axis: homopolymer vs copolymer. Tacticity, the order of side groups, is separate and covered in 5.2.

Classification by source, heat behaviour and chain shape

By source: Natural polymers come from plants or animals. Examples: starch, cellulose (jute), natural rubber. Semi-synthetic polymers are natural polymers modified chemically. Example: cellulose acetate. Synthetic polymers are made in factories. Examples: polyethylene (PE), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), PET.

By heat behaviour: Thermoplastic softens on heating and can be reshaped. Examples: PE, PVC. Thermoset does not soften on heating because chains are heavily cross-linked. Example: bakelite. Elastomer is a polymer that stretches and returns when the force is removed. Conventional rubbers are lightly cross-linked after vulcanisation, and natural rubber itself is an elastomer.

By chain shape: Linear chains have no branches. Example: HDPE. Branched chains have side branches. Example: LDPE. Cross-linked chains are joined by covalent bonds between chains. Example: bakelite.

By mechanism: chain-growth (often addition) and step-growth (often condensation). The two pairs are not identical: chain-growth is not always addition, and step-growth is not always condensation. Polyurethane forms by step-growth but not by condensation. See 5.3 for mechanisms.

Worked example

Question. A polystyrene sample has molar mass 52,000 g/mol. Its repeat unit is C₈H₈, molar mass 104 g/mol. Find the average degree of polymerisation. Then find the molar mass of a polypropene (C₃H₆, 42 g/mol) sample with the same average DP.

  1. Average DP = molar mass of sample ÷ molar mass of repeat unit.
  2. For polystyrene: average DP = 52,000 g/mol ÷ 104 g/mol = 500.
  3. For polypropene with same average DP: molar mass = average DP × molar mass of repeat unit = 500 × 42 g/mol = 21,000 g/mol.

Answer. The average degree of polymerisation is 500, and the polypropene sample has molar mass 21,000 g/mol.

Exam traps

  • DP counts repeat units, not atoms. A DP of 500 means 500 repeat units, not 500 carbon atoms.
  • For condensation polymers, the repeat unit molar mass is not the monomer molar mass because a small molecule (like water) leaves. Always use the repeat unit formula.
  • Thermoplastic softens on heating; thermoset does not. Bakelite is a thermoset, not a thermoplastic.
  • Homopolymer has one monomer type; copolymer has two or more. Do not call a copolymer a homopolymer just because one monomer dominates.
  • Natural rubber is an elastomer, but not all elastomers are natural. Vulcanised rubber is lightly cross-linked; a thermoset is heavily cross-linked.

5.2 Tacticity of polymers

Tacticity is the order in which side groups sit along a polymer chain that has stereocentres. A vinyl polymer –[CH₂–CHX]ₙ– has a stereocentre at every second carbon, and each centre can be right- or left-handed. The three ideal orders are all on one side (isotactic), strictly alternating (syndiotactic), and random (atactic). The idea exists because this order changes how chains pack, so it changes crystallinity, stiffness and softening point. It is used when choosing polypropene for a car bumper, a food container or a fibre.

You need 5.1 for repeat units, and Unit 1 for the tetrahedral carbon.

Stereocentres in a vinyl chain

A vinyl polymer has the repeat unit –[CH₂–CHX]ₙ–. Here n is the number of repeat units, and X is the side group: CH₃ in polypropene, Cl in poly(vinyl chloride), C₆H₅ in polystyrene. Carbon is tetrahedral, so the CHX carbon carries four different groups: H, X, the chain on its left, and the chain on its right. That carbon is a stereocentre.

–[CH₂–CHX]ₙ–

n is the number of repeat units; X is the side group (CH₃, Cl, C₆H₅); the CHX carbon is the stereocentre.

At the CHX carbon, the fourth group besides H and X is the chain itself, read in one direction or the other. In a real chain the two ends are not the same: different end groups, different lengths, and a different R/S sequence further along. So the two directions count as two different groups, and the CHX carbon has four different groups. In an idealised endless regular chain the two directions are constitutionally identical, which is why some syllabi call this a pseudoasymmetric centre. The argument still holds once end groups and the configurational sequence are counted.

At each stereocentre the side group X can have one of two configurations, often labelled R and S. Changing R to S needs bond breaking. That makes tacticity a configurational property. Rotation about single bonds changes shape but not tacticity; such shapes are conformations.

Not every polymer has this choice. Polyethene, –[CH₂–CH₂]ₙ–, has two H atoms on every carbon, so no carbon carries four different groups. It has no stereocentre and tacticity does not apply to it. Polypropene, –[CH₂–CH(CH₃)]ₙ–, does have stereocentres, so tacticity applies.

The three ideal arrangements

The tree in the figure separates the three ideal tacticities. Real chains often sit between them, and a sample is described by fractions, not by one word only.

  • Isotactic: every stereocentre has the same configuration; all X groups lie on one side of the zig-zag chain. The chain can form a helix.
  • Syndiotactic: the configurations alternate; X groups alternate sides in a regular way.
  • Atactic: the configurations are random; X groups have no long-range order. The stereocentres still exist.

An isotactic chain can be all R or all S. Both are isotactic because the groups have the same relative orientation. A syndiotactic chain alternates R, S, R, S. An atactic chain has no repeat in this pattern; the side groups look random.

These are ideal limits. A commercial sample may be mostly isotactic with some stereo-errors. Stereo-errors break up the regular packing. Tacticity is a relative description; the absolute R or S label does not change the name.

Dyads, triads and why they matter

A dyad is a pair of neighbouring stereocentres. Write m for a meso dyad: the two centres have the same configuration. Write r for a racemo dyad: they have opposite configurations. A triad is three neighbouring centres. The three triad types are mm, mr and rr. Here mr covers both orders of one m dyad and one r dyad.

m + r = 1; mm + mr + rr = 1

m and r are dyad fractions; mm, mr and rr are triad fractions; each fraction is between 0 and 1.

For an ideal isotactic chain, m = 1 and mm = 1. For an ideal syndiotactic chain, r = 1 and rr = 1. For a fully atactic random chain, m = r = 0.50, so mm = 0.25, mr = 0.50 and rr = 0.25. These are probabilities for a random chain, not a measured sample.

Regular chains pack into crystals. Isotactic polypropene can crystallise, so it is stiff and has a useful softening point. Atactic polypropene cannot pack well, so it is softer and stickier. The full property chain is in 5.5. The catalysts that force one tacticity are in 5.4.

Worked example

Question. A short vinyl polymer –[CH₂–CHX]ₙ– has 5 stereocentres. (a) How many configurational isomers exist if each centre is free to be R or S? (b) In a fully atactic chain, what dyad and triad fractions are expected?

  1. Each stereocentre has two configurations, R and S.
  2. The centres are independent in this count, so the number of configurational isomers is 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 2⁵.
  3. 2⁵ = 32 configurational isomers.
  4. For a fully atactic chain, the probability of m at a dyad is 0.50 and of r is 0.50.
  5. Triad mm = 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25; rr = 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25; mr = 1 − 0.25 − 0.25 = 0.50.

Answer. The chain has 32 configurational isomers; the atactic fractions are m = r = 0.50 and mm = 0.25, mr = 0.50, rr = 0.25.

Exam traps

  • Atactic does not mean 'no stereocentres'. It means the stereocentres exist, but their R/S sequence is random; polyethene is the polymer with no stereocentres.
  • Isotactic polymer in bulk is not optically active. Equal numbers of right- and left-handed helices usually form, so their optical rotations cancel; do not label the whole sample optically active.
  • Tacticity is configurational. Rotating a single bond, stretching a chain or dissolving it changes conformation, not tacticity; only bond breaking changes R to S.
  • For a fully atactic chain, do not write mm = mr = rr = 1/3. Random dyads give m = r = 0.50, so mm = 0.25, mr = 0.50, rr = 0.25.

5.3 Types and mechanisms of polymerization

Every polymer chain is built by one of two routes. In chain growth, an initiator creates a reactive centre and monomer units add to it one at a time; the chain stays alive until it dies. In step growth, any two molecules that carry the right groups can join, so dimers, trimers and long chains all react with one another. Industry picks the route from the monomer: PVC, polystyrene and polyethylene come from chain growth, PET bottles and nylon come from step growth. This chapter follows both mechanisms step by step.

You need: 5.1 for monomer, repeat unit and functionality; see Unit 3, Reaction kinetics, for rate ideas if a question asks for kinetics.

Addition and condensation: three contrasts

An addition monomer carries a double bond, C=C, or a ring. A condensation monomer carries two reactive groups, such as –COOH and –OH. That one difference decides everything else.

Addition is usually the same as chain growth, and condensation is usually the same as step growth, but not always — polyurethane is step growth without any condensation, so decide the mechanism from the monomer and the chemistry, not from the label.

  • Small molecule: chain growth releases nothing, so every monomer atom enters the chain. Step growth usually releases a small molecule — water from a diacid plus a diol, HCl from a diacid chloride plus a diamine. Polyurethane is an exception: diisocyanate plus diol releases nothing.
  • Molar mass: in chain growth the first chains reach full length within seconds, and extra monomer only makes more chains. In step growth every species grows together, so the degree of polymerisation stays small until almost all groups have reacted.
  • Monomers: chain growth needs one monomer with unsaturation plus a trace of initiator. Step growth needs two bifunctional monomers weighed in matched amounts, or one monomer carrying two different groups.

Because of the second point, the two routes need different reactor times. A chain-growth run can be stopped as soon as the monomer is used up; a step-growth run must be pushed to the very end, often under vacuum, or the product is a sticky oligomer.

Free-radical chain growth, step by step

Most addition polymers — polyethylene, PVC, polystyrene, poly(methyl methacrylate) — are made with free radicals. Four steps repeat millions of times.

  • Initiation. A peroxide or an azo compound splits on heating: I–I → 2 I•. The dot marks an unpaired electron, that is, a radical. The radical adds to one monomer: I• + CH₂=CHX → I–CH₂–CH•X. Here X is the side group — H for ethene, Cl for vinyl chloride, C₆H₅ for styrene, COOCH₃ for methyl methacrylate.
  • Propagation. The new radical attacks another monomer and the radical moves to the far end: ~CH₂–CH•X + CH₂=CHX → ~CH₂–CHX–CH₂–CH•X. The tilde ~ stands for the rest of the chain. Units add head to tail, so the backbone reads –CH₂–CHX–CH₂–CHX–.
  • Termination by combination. Two radicals join into one dead chain: ~CH₂–CH•X + •CHX–CH₂~ → ~CH₂–CHX–CHX–CH₂~.
  • Termination by disproportionation. One radical pulls a hydrogen atom from the other. Two dead chains form, one ending in a double bond: ~CH₂–CH•X + •CHX–CH₂~ → ~CH₂–CH₂X + CHX=CH~.
  • Chain transfer. A radical abstracts an atom from solvent, monomer or another chain, giving a dead chain and a new small radical: ~CH₂–CH•X + HS–R → ~CH₂–CH₂X + •S–R. Molar mass falls, but the overall rate barely changes.

The chain length is fixed at the instant of termination, which is why the molar mass of a free-radical polymer depends on how fast termination competes with propagation.

Cationic, anionic and ring-opening routes

Cationic. A proton acid opens the double bond of an electron-rich monomer. Boron trifluoride with a trace of water, or sulphuric acid, is the usual initiator. The growing end is a carbocation such as ~C⁺(CH₃)₂, and polyisobutylene, the base of butyl rubber, is made this way. Styrene also polymerises cationically.

Anionic. An organolithium compound or sodium naphthalenide starts an anion, ~CH₂–CH⁻X. Styrene and butadiene polymerise cleanly in hexane with butyllithium. The chains stay alive while monomer remains, so the process is called living and gives a narrow spread of chain lengths.

Ring-opening. A cyclic monomer opens its ring and joins the chain end. Caprolactam gives nylon-6; ethylene oxide gives poly(ethylene glycol). No atom is lost, but the ring has opened, so the repeat unit is an open chain and not the ring-shaped monomer. Coordination routes with a transition metal catalyst are covered in 5.4.

Step growth: functionality two or more

A step-growth monomer must carry at least two functional groups. A molecule with only one group caps a chain and acts as an impurity. Two monomers of type A–A and B–B, or one monomer of type A–B, give a linear chain.

X̄ₙ = 1 ÷ (1 − p)

X̄ₙ is the number-average degree of polymerisation (monomer units per chain); p is the extent of reaction, the fraction of functional groups that have reacted, between 0 and 1.

The relation assumes equal numbers of A and B groups. Put p = 0.90 and X̄ₙ is 10; p = 0.99 gives 100; p = 0.999 gives 1000. A fibre or a bottle resin therefore needs conversion above 0.99, which is why PET and nylon reactors are driven hard, often under vacuum, to strip out water and push p upward.

A small imbalance between A and B groups caps the chain length, so the two monomers are weighed carefully. Networks need some monomer with three groups; that is discussed with structure and properties in 5.5.

Worked example

Question. 1.00 mol of ethene is polymerised in the presence of 2.00 mmol of benzoyl peroxide, (C₆H₅CO)₂O₂. Each peroxide molecule breaks into two radicals, and each radical starts one chain (initiator efficiency taken as 1). (a) If termination is entirely by disproportionation, find the number of polymer chains and the number-average degree of polymerisation. (b) Repeat the calculation for termination by combination. Take N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

  1. Moles of initiator: n_I = 2.00 mmol = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol.
  2. Initiator molecules = n_I × N_A = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 1.2044 × 10²¹ molecules.
  3. Benzoyl peroxide splits as (C₆H₅CO)₂O₂ → 2 C₆H₅COO•, so each molecule gives two radicals. Radicals = 2 × 1.2044 × 10²¹ = 2.4088 × 10²¹ radicals.
  4. (a) Disproportionation gives one dead chain per radical, so chains = 2.4088 × 10²¹.
  5. Monomer molecules = 1.00 mol × 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 6.022 × 10²³.
  6. DP = 6.022 × 10²³ ÷ 2.4088 × 10²¹ = 250.
  7. (b) Combination joins two radicals into one chain, so chains = 2.4088 × 10²¹ ÷ 2 = 1.2044 × 10²¹.
  8. DP = 6.022 × 10²³ ÷ 1.2044 × 10²¹ = 500.

Answer. (a) 2.4088 × 10²¹ chains with number-average DP 250; (b) 1.2044 × 10²¹ chains with DP 500.

Exam traps

  • Condensation does not always release water. Diisocyanate plus diol, the polyurethane route, releases nothing at all. Decide from the monomer, not from the chapter heading.
  • Ring-opening polymerisation keeps every atom, but the ring has opened, so the repeat unit is an open chain and not the ring-shaped monomer. Caprolactam, C₆H₁₁NO, gives the repeat unit –[NH–(CH₂)₅–CO]–.
  • Chain transfer lowers molar mass without lowering the rate much. It does not stop polymerisation; it moves the radical onto a new molecule, which then carries on growing.
  • Termination by combination halves the number of chains and doubles DP compared with disproportionation. Mixing the two inside one DP calculation is the commonest slip.
  • High conversion alone does not give high molar mass in step growth: the A and B groups must also be balanced. The relation X̄ₙ = 1 ÷ (1 − p) assumes exact stoichiometry.

5.4 Ziegler-Natta polymerization

Ziegler-Natta polymerization builds linear, stereoregular polymers from alkenes by inserting one monomer at a time into a metal-carbon bond. A transition metal compound such as TiCl4 is activated by an alkyl aluminium such as Al(C2H5)3, reduced to solid TiCl3, and left with a vacant site. Propene gives largely isotactic polypropene; ethene gives linear high-density polyethylene. Industry uses it for bottles, pipes, films and automotive parts. Only a few universities teach this mechanism in detail.

You need: 5.2 for tacticity and 5.3 for chain growth.

Catalyst and active site

TiCl4 is a transition metal compound. Al(C2H5)3, triethyl aluminium or AlEt3, is the alkyl aluminium co-catalyst. The aluminium compound is the activator: it alkylates titanium and reduces Ti(IV) to Ti(III). A simplified overall reaction is:

2TiCl4 + 2Al(C2H5)3 → 2TiCl3(s) + 2Al(C2H5)2Cl + C2H4 + C2H6

TiCl4 is titanium tetrachloride; Al(C2H5)3 is triethyl aluminium; TiCl3(s) is solid titanium trichloride; C2H4 is ethene and C2H6 is ethane, formed as by-products.

The solid TiCl3 has a surface. Each surface titanium ion has one vacant coordination position. This vacant site is the active centre. An alkene donates its pi electrons into it. The growing chain stays attached by a Ti-C bond.

  1. Mix TiCl4 with Al(C2H5)3; solid TiCl3 particles form.
  2. An ethyl group attaches to surface Ti, leaving one vacant position.
  3. An alkene molecule coordinates at the vacant position.
  4. The growing alkyl chain migrates to the bound alkene, making a new Ti-C bond and freeing the site.
  5. The cycle repeats; each pass adds one monomer unit.

Insertion and stereoregularity

In insertion, the Ti-C bond breaks and a new Ti-C bond forms at the other end of the alkene. The chain shifts by one monomer unit. The vacant site returns, so the next alkene binds in the same way. This is coordination-insertion polymerization, a chain-growth method.

The solid surface is rigid. It allows the alkene to approach in only one orientation. For propene, the methyl group is forced to one side. Most repeat units therefore have the same tacticity: the polymer is largely isotactic. A small atactic fraction usually forms too and is removed in the plant. The same control gives linear high-density polyethylene (HDPE). Linear chains pack well, so HDPE is stiff and dense.

  • Metallocene catalysts are soluble single-site catalysts that follow the same coordination-insertion mechanism but are not Ziegler-Natta catalysts; they give very uniform polymers.
  • The Phillips process uses chromium oxide on silica to make HDPE.
  • Ziegler-Natta polypropene is used in car parts and woven sacks.

Worked example

5.5 Effect of polymer structure on properties

Polymer properties are decided before the product is shaped: by how regular each chain is, how much it branches, whether chains are joined to each other, and how long they are. Regular unbranched chains pack closely and form crystalline regions. Irregular chains pack loosely and stay amorphous. Packing controls density, stiffness, softening point, transparency and flexibility. This is why a polyethylene bag, a water pipe and cable insulation can all be polyethylene yet behave differently.

You need: 5.1 for polymer terms and classification, 5.2 for tacticity, and 5.3 for branching routes.

Regular chains pack; irregular chains do not

A linear chain with small, regularly placed side groups can slide beside its neighbours. van der Waals forces and dipole forces then hold short stretches in ordered, parallel bundles. These ordered regions are crystallites; the rest is amorphous. Isotactic and syndiotactic chains can crystallise. Atactic chains cannot, because side groups stick out randomly and keep neighbours apart. See 5.2 for tacticity.

w<sub>c</sub> = ρ<sub>c</sub>(ρ − ρ<sub>a</sub>) ÷ [ρ(ρ<sub>c</sub> − ρ<sub>a</sub>)]

wc is mass fraction crystalline; ρ is sample density; ρa is density of fully amorphous sample; ρc is density of fully crystalline sample. All densities in g/cm³.

Crystallinity raises density because chains fit closer. It also raises stiffness and melting point, and lowers transparency because crystallites scatter light. A clear bag is mostly amorphous; a stiff milk jug is more crystalline. The same trade-off explains why high-density polyethylene is opaque and strong while low-density polyethylene is translucent and flexible.

Packing is not all-or-nothing. A polymer is semicrystalline: crystallites sit in an amorphous matrix. The fraction wc is measured from density. The worked example below uses this formula.

Branching, cross-linking, molar mass and forces

Branching blocks packing. Low-density polyethylene (LDPE) has many long and short branches, so chains cannot align well; it is soft, translucent and low melting. High-density polyethylene (HDPE) is almost linear; it packs well, is denser, stiffer and used for pipes and bottles. Branching routes are in 5.3.

Cross-linking ties chains into a network. A thermoset such as cured epoxy cannot be melted or dissolved because the network holds its shape. Vulcanised rubber has sulphur bridges between chains. Cross-linking raises stiffness and prevents flow, but too much cross-linking makes rubber hard and brittle.

Molar mass also matters. Longer chains entangle more, so strength and viscosity rise with average molar mass. Very short chains act like a lubricant and weaken the material. Molar mass averages are covered in 5.7.

Intermolecular forces add to packing. Nylon chains have amide groups that hydrogen-bond, so nylon is strong and has a high melting point even without covalent cross-links. PVC has polar C–Cl dipoles; dipole forces make it rigid at room temperature. Polyethylene has only weak dispersion forces, so it softens at lower temperature. Named polymers are in 5.6.

Glass transition and melting

Two temperatures describe softening. The glass transition temperature Tg is the temperature below which amorphous regions are glassy and rigid; above Tg they become rubbery and can move. Tg is not a sharp melting point; it is a range measured by a standard method.

The melting point Tm is the temperature at which crystalline regions melt. A semicrystalline polymer has both Tg and Tm; an amorphous polymer has only Tg. High crystallinity raises Tm and stiffness. Plasticiser molecules sit between chains, lower Tg and make PVC flexible for wires and upholstery.

Elastomers are a special case. Above Tg, rubber chains are coiled and tangled. A pull straightens and uncoils them; on release, thermal motion recoils them. Sulphur cross-links stop chains from sliding past each other, so the rubber returns to shape instead of flowing. This is elastomer elasticity.

At room temperature, natural rubber and silicone rubber are rubbery; polystyrene and PMMA are glassy; LDPE is soft and waxy; HDPE is stiff.

  • Do not confuse branching with cross-linking: branches prevent packing but do not join chains; cross-links join chains and stop flow.

  • Do not use volume fraction in the density formula unless stated; the formula gives mass fraction wc. Keep all densities in the same units.
  • Atactic does not mean branched. Atactic means random side-group orientation; branching means side chains.

A named polymer is a fact sheet: monomer, repeat unit, growth type, use. Addition polymers use one alkene monomer and lose nothing. Natural rubber is cis-polyisoprene (isoprene, C₅H₈); cis double bonds make chains coil and snap back. Vulcanisation heats raw rubber with sulfur, forming bridges. Silicones (polysiloxanes) have an Si–O–Si backbone with methyl groups. Strong Si–O bonds give heat stability; the flexible backbone gives oiliness. Uses: lubricating oils and water-repelling sealants for tiles and glass.

5.7 Molecular mass of polymers

A polymer sample is not one molecule but many chains of different lengths. Its molar mass is therefore an average, and different experiments weight the chains differently. The number average counts every chain once; the weight average counts long chains more. Industry quotes molar mass and polydispersity to control processing, strength and solubility. The average you report must match the method you used: osmometry gives the number average, light scattering gives the weight average.

You need: 5.1 for degree of polymerization and 5.3 for how chains form.

Why a sample has an average

Chains start and stop at different moments during polymerization. A sample therefore contains a distribution: many short chains, many long chains, and a spread in between. Write Ni for the number of chains whose molar mass is Mi (Mi in g/mol, Ni a pure count). The index i runs over every chain length present.

M<sub>n</sub> = Σ N<sub>i</sub> M<sub>i</sub> / Σ N<sub>i</sub>

Mn is the number-average molar mass in g/mol; Ni is the number of chains of molar mass Mi; the sum runs over all chain lengths.

This is the total mass divided by the total number of chains. Every chain counts once, whether it is short or long. Short chains are numerous but carry little mass, so they pull Mn down.

M<sub>w</sub> = Σ N<sub>i</sub> M<sub>i</sub><sup>2</sup> / Σ N<sub>i</sub> M<sub>i</sub>

Mw is the weight-average molar mass in g/mol; the extra Mi in the numerator weights each chain by its mass.

This is the same as Σ wi Mi, where wi is the mass fraction of chains of molar mass Mi. Long chains carry much mass, so Mw rises above Mn.

The ratio PDI = Mw / Mn is the polydispersity index. PDI = 1 means all chains are equal. A larger PDI means a broader distribution. The figure compares a narrow sample with a broad sample that has the same peak. How molar mass changes strength is in 5.5.

The four averages

Mn and Mw are the two most quoted. Two more appear in polymer data sheets.

  • Viscosity-average molar mass Mv comes from solution viscosity. It is defined by Mv = (Σ Ni Mi1+a / Σ Ni Mi)1/a, where a is the Mark-Houwink exponent. For flexible chains a is often about 0.5 to 0.8.
  • Z-average molar mass Mz = Σ Ni Mi3 / Σ Ni Mi2. It is very sensitive to the longest chains.

For a polydisperse sample, Mn ≤ Mv ≤ Mw ≤ Mz. The gaps grow as the distribution broadens. PDI = Mw / Mn is the usual measure of breadth.

Mv is not a simple count average. The exponent a shifts the weighting toward the longer chains, which occupy more hydrodynamic volume and raise the viscosity more than short chains do. Mz is used when the tail of very long chains matters, for example in GPC data analysis.

Measuring the average

Each method weights chains by a different property, so each gives a particular average.

  • End-group analysis: titrate a known end group. If each chain has one titratable group, moles of group equal moles of chains. Divide sample mass by moles of chains to get Mn. Works for low molar mass and known end groups.
  • Osmometry: measure osmotic pressure π of a dilute solution. The intercept of π/c against c gives RT/Mn, where c is concentration in kg/m³, R is the gas constant 8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, and T is kelvin. This gives Mn; with c in kg/m³ the intercept gives Mn in kg/mol, so multiply by 1000 to report it in g/mol.
  • Light scattering: scattered intensity grows with Mw. A Zimm plot extrapolates to zero angle and zero concentration. This gives Mw; full theory belongs to a physics course.
  • Viscometry: measure intrinsic viscosity [η] and use [η] = K Mva. K and a are constants for a polymer-solvent pair at a temperature. This gives Mv.
  • Gel permeation chromatography (GPC): molecules flow through porous beads. Small chains enter more pores and elute later. A detector records the full distribution, so all averages can be computed.

For routine quality control, GPC is the workhorse. For absolute Mw, light scattering is used. For absolute Mn at low mass, osmometry or end-group titration is used.

Worked example

Question. A polymer sample contains 10 g of chains with molar mass 10,000 g/mol and 30 g of chains with molar mass 40,000 g/mol. Find Mn, Mw and PDI.

  1. Moles of chains in first fraction = 10 g ÷ 10,000 g/mol = 0.0010 mol.
  2. Moles in second fraction = 30 g ÷ 40,000 g/mol = 0.00075 mol.
  3. Total moles = 0.0010 + 0.00075 = 0.00175 mol; total mass = 10 g + 30 g = 40 g.
  4. Mn = 40 g ÷ 0.00175 mol = 22,857 g/mol (3 s.f.).
  5. Mass fractions: w1 = 10/40 = 0.25; w2 = 30/40 = 0.75.
  6. Mw = 0.25 × 10,000 + 0.75 × 40,000 = 2,500 + 30,000 = 32,500 g/mol.
  7. PDI = 32,500 ÷ 22,857 = 1.42.

Answer. Mn ≈ 22,857 g/mol, Mw = 32,500 g/mol and PDI ≈ 1.42.

  • PDI = Mw / Mn is dimensionless. Convert both averages to the same unit (both g/mol or both kg/mol) before dividing; never mix them.
  • Do not average molar masses by simple arithmetic mean. Use the formula for the average the question names.
  • Mv lies between Mn and Mw, not above Mw, for normal polydisperse samples.

5.8 Conducting polymers

Some plastics carry current. A conducting polymer has a backbone of alternating single and double bonds, so its π electrons are delocalised along the chain. Chemical doping — oxidation or reduction — creates charged defects called polarons and bipolarons. These charges move along the chain and hop between chains, turning an insulator into a conductor. Conductivities reach 103 S/cm, far below copper but enough for antistatic coatings, sensors, OLEDs, electromagnetic interference shielding and supercapacitor electrodes.

You need 5.1 for the polymer chain, and Unit 6 for the band picture of solids.

Conjugation and the band gap

A polymer chain is conjugated when its carbon atoms alternate single and double bonds: –CH=CH–CH=CH–. Each double bond contributes a π bond. The p orbitals on neighbouring carbons overlap sideways, so the π electrons are not fixed to one pair of atoms; they spread over the whole conjugated segment. This delocalisation gives the chain a gap of a few electronvolts, so long conjugated segments absorb visible light and the polymer looks coloured; short segments absorb in the ultraviolet. The same delocalisation lets the chain carry charge.

In trans-polyacetylene, the simplest conjugated polymer, the single and double bonds alternate in a regular pattern. This bond alternation opens an energy gap between the filled π band and the empty π* band. The gap is about 1.5 eV. At room temperature only a tiny fraction of electrons can cross a 1.5 eV gap, so undoped trans-polyacetylene is an insulator. The gap is the polymer version of the band gap in a semiconductor; see Unit 6.

The gap is small enough to modify by chemistry. Adding or removing a few electrons per chain creates new electronic levels inside the gap. Those levels are the resting places for the charges that carry current after doping.

Doping, polarons and bipolarons

Doping here means chemical oxidation or reduction of the conjugated chain, not adding impurity atoms to a semiconductor lattice. In p-type doping the chain is oxidised: electrons are removed. Iodine vapour is the classic oxidant for polyacetylene. In n-type doping the chain is reduced: electrons are added, for example by sodium. The counter-ion sits nearby to keep the film neutral overall.

Removing one electron creates a radical cation on the chain: one positive charge and one unpaired electron. This defect is a polaron. Removing a second electron near the same place gives a bipolaron: two positive charges with no unpaired electron. Both distort the chain locally and produce levels inside the gap. A polaron or bipolaron can move along the chain by shifting the bond pattern, and it can hop from one chain to the next.

Conductivity σ is quoted in siemens per centimetre (S/cm); 1 S/cm = 100 S/m. Undoped conjugated polymers are near 10−10 S/cm. Doped films reach 10−3 to 103 S/cm, and the best samples approach 105 S/cm. Between chains, hopping is the weak step, so order and morphology matter.

Thin films are measured by the four-probe method. Four contacts sit in a line; the outer two pass a current I, and the inner two measure the voltage V. This avoids errors from contact resistance. Most conducting polymers are intractable — they do not melt or dissolve easily — so they are processed as dispersions, such as PEDOT:PSS in water.

Three conducting polymers and uses

  • Polyaniline: made from aniline, cheap and stable. Acid doping gives a green conducting salt. Uses: antistatic coatings on packaging and electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding, where a conductive layer blocks stray radio signals.
  • Polypyrrole: formed by electropolymerisation as a tough film. Uses: gas and glucose sensors, and supercapacitor electrodes that store charge at the polymer surface.
  • PEDOT:PSS: the conducting polymer PEDOT (poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)) charge-balanced by the polyanion PSS (polystyrene sulfonate), supplied as a water dispersion. Uses: transparent hole-injecting layer in organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs), and antistatic coatings.

The common thread is a conjugated backbone plus a dopant. Dopant level, chain order and processing decide the final conductivity. A film for an antistatic bag needs only slight conduction; a supercapacitor electrode needs high surface area and fast ion access; an OLED layer needs transparency and the right work function.

Worked example

Question. A doped polyaniline film is 2.0 cm long, 1.0 cm wide and 0.10 cm thick. Its resistance along the length is 40 Ω. Find the conductivity in S/cm and in S/m. Compare with copper, about 105 S/cm.

  1. The cross-section area is A = width × thickness = 1.0 cm × 0.10 cm = 0.10 cm².
  2. Resistance is R = ρ l / A, so resistivity ρ = R A / l. Conductivity σ = 1/ρ = l / (R A).
  3. Put in l = 2.0 cm, R = 40 Ω, A = 0.10 cm²: σ = 2.0 cm / (40 Ω × 0.10 cm²) = 2.0 / 4.0 S/cm = 0.50 S/cm.
  4. Convert: 1 S/cm = 100 S/m, so σ = 0.50 × 100 = 50 S/m.
  5. Copper is about 105 S/cm, which is 2 × 105 times larger than 0.50 S/cm. The film conducts, but it is far below a metal.

Answer. The film conductivity is 0.50 S/cm (50 S/m); copper is about 2 × 105 times more conductive.

Exam traps

  • Doping is chemical oxidation or reduction of the chain. It is not the addition of impurity atoms to a semiconductor lattice; that is semiconductor doping in Unit 6.
  • p-type means the polymer chain loses electrons and becomes positively charged. Do not read p as positive holes added from outside; the oxidant removes electrons.
  • S/cm and S/m differ by a factor of 100: 1 S/cm = 100 S/m. Convert before comparing with copper.
  • A polaron is a radical cation (or radical anion), not just a bare charge. A bipolaron has two charges and no unpaired electron.
  • High doping does not always mean high conductivity. Too much dopant can distort chains, reduce order, and lower mobility.

A bipolaron is two charges paired on the chain with no unpaired electron. A polaron is one charge plus one unpaired electron, so it is a radical ion.

Apply A doped polypyrrole film is 5.0 cm long, 2.0 cm wide and 0.050 cm thick. Its resistance along the length is 100 Ω. Find σ in S/cm. 4 marks

A = 2.0 cm × 0.050 cm = 0.10 cm². σ = l / (R A) = 5.0 cm / (100 Ω × 0.10 cm²) = 0.50 S/cm.

Analyze Why does interchain hopping limit conductivity in a conducting polymer film? 4 marks

Charges move quickly along a conjugated chain but must jump between chains. Disorder, poor chain contacts and insulating side groups raise the barrier to hopping, so the film conductivity is lower than along a single chain.

Analyze A student measures a film with two probes and gets a lower conductivity than with four probes. Explain. 5 marks

Two-probe resistance includes contact resistance and wire resistance in series with the film. Four-probe measurement passes current through the outer contacts and measures voltage across the inner contacts, so contact resistance is not included.

5.9 Composites: polymer and ceramic matrix composites

A composite is two materials that stay separate but work as a team. One, the matrix, holds the shape, spreads the load and shields the other from water, air and rubbing. The other, the reinforcement, is stiff and strong and carries most of the load. Alone each is ordinary. Joined, the pair gives far more stiffness and strength per kilogram than the matrix alone, and more than a metal part of the same weight — though never more than the bare fibre along its own axis. Boat hulls, wind-turbine blades, cricket-bat handles, brake discs and cutting tools are composites. This chapter covers what each part does, the main families, how they are made, and the one formula you must be able to use.

You need: 5.5 for how packing decides stiffness and density, and 5.6 for epoxy and polyester resins.

The two parts and the bond between them

A composite has two materials that do not dissolve into each other. The matrix is the continuous phase: it holds the reinforcement in place, hands load from one fibre to the next, and shields the fibre from water, air and rubbing. The reinforcement is the dispersed phase — fibres, whiskers or particles stiffer and stronger than the matrix. The matrix alone is weak; loose fibre cannot be shaped or protected. Together they make a part far better than the matrix alone.

Load enters the fibre only through the interface. Pull the composite and the matrix shears, passing stress to the fibre surface. A coupling agent such as a silane on glass improves this grip. A weak bond lets fibres slide out — pull-out — and the part fails far below the fibre's own strength. A bond that is too stiff makes the part brittle: nothing slides, so nothing absorbs energy. Good design wants a bond strong enough to carry load and weak enough to let fibres debond and bridge a growing crack.

  • Continuous fibre: long and aligned, load carried along the length; stiffest along the axis.
  • Chopped or woven fibre: short or interlaced, drapes over a mould, lower stiffness.
  • Particulate: powder or flakes; raises hardness and wear resistance, carries little tensile load.

A laminate is not a fibre form: it is a way of stacking plies at chosen angles, so the plate can be stiff in the directions you pick — a cross-ply laminate in two, a quasi-isotropic one in all in-plane directions.

Polymer matrix composites and how they are made

The matrix is a resin, usually epoxy or polyester (5.6). Polymer matrix composites lead the market because they are light — 1.5 to 2.0 g/cm³ — and cure at room temperature. Their weakness is heat: epoxy matrices soften or degrade near 120–150 °C and polyester well below that, so a polymer matrix composite stays away from exhausts and furnaces.

  • Glass (E-glass): cheapest; Ef ≈ 70 GPa, density 2.5 g/cm³, insulating; water tanks, boat hulls, circuit boards.
  • Carbon: Ef 230–600 GPa, density ≈ 1.8 g/cm³, conducts electricity, costly; aircraft panels, bicycle frames, and the handles and toe guards of some cricket bats (the blade itself is willow).
  • Aramid: very tough, density ≈ 1.45 g/cm³, weak in compression; helmets, ropes, bullet-resistant vests.
  1. Hand lay-up: glass mat laid in an open mould, resin brushed in, cured in air. Cheap and slow, one smooth face, plenty of labour. Boat hulls and water tanks.
  2. Pultrusion: continuous tows pulled through a resin bath and then a heated die. Constant cross-section only — rods, ladders, cable trays.
  3. Resin transfer moulding: dry fibre preform sealed in a closed mould, resin pumped in under pressure. Two smooth faces, controlled fibre fraction, less styrene vapour. Blade parts and car panels.

Ceramic matrix composites

Ceramics stay hard to 1000 °C and beyond, but one crack shatters them. A ceramic matrix composite puts ceramic fibres in a ceramic matrix; as a crack opens, fibres bridge it and pull out, absorbing energy. The part does not become ductile — it becomes damage tolerant.

  • SiC–SiC: silicon carbide fibres in a silicon carbide matrix. Made by chemical vapour infiltration, where a chloride gas seeps into the fibre preform and deposits SiC, or by hot pressing. Turbine shrouds, heat shields and high-performance car brake discs. (Aircraft brakes are carbon–carbon, a different family.)
  • Al2O3–SiC: alumina with silicon carbide whiskers, hot pressed into cutting tool inserts that run hot and fast.

Hot pressing gives dense, simple shapes; infiltration gives complex shapes with some porosity left. Both cost far more than a polymer composite, so ceramics go only where hardness or heat decides the sale.

Rule of mixtures

Pull a unidirectional composite along its fibres and fibre and matrix stretch by the same amount, so the load is shared in proportion to stiffness. The composite stiffness is the volume-weighted average of the two.

E<sub>c</sub> = V<sub>f</sub>E<sub>f</sub> + V<sub>m</sub>E<sub>m</sub>

Ec is the composite modulus along the fibres (GPa); Vf and Vm are the volume fractions of fibre and matrix, with Vf + Vm = 1; Ef and Em are the moduli of fibre and matrix (GPa).

ρ<sub>c</sub> = V<sub>f</sub>ρ<sub>f</sub> + V<sub>m</sub>ρ<sub>m</sub>

ρc is composite density and ρf, ρm are fibre and matrix densities, all in g/cm³; the same volume weighting applies.

Divide stiffness by density to get the specific modulus, Ec ÷ ρc, in GPa per g/cm³. This number, not stiffness alone, decides whether a part is worth its weight.

  • Holds for continuous, aligned fibres, loaded along the axis, with a good bond and equal strain.
  • Across the fibres the modulus is far lower, and this rule does not apply there.
  • Short or random fibres fall below the prediction; the rule is an upper bound for them.
  • Vf above about 0.65 leaves too little resin to wet and hold the fibres.

Worked example

Question. A unidirectional glass fibre–epoxy lamina has fibre volume fraction Vf = 0.60. E-glass has Ef = 70 GPa and ρf = 2.5 g/cm³; the epoxy has Em = 3.5 GPa and ρm = 1.2 g/cm³. Find (i) the modulus along the fibres, (ii) the density, and (iii) the specific modulus, comparing it with epoxy alone.

  1. Matrix volume fraction: Vm = 1 − 0.60 = 0.40.
  2. Modulus along the fibres: Ec = VfEf + VmEm = 0.60 × 70 + 0.40 × 3.5 = 42 + 1.4 = 43.4 GPa.
  3. Density: ρc = 0.60 × 2.5 + 0.40 × 1.2 = 1.5 + 0.48 = 1.98 g/cm³.
  4. Specific modulus of the composite: 43.4 ÷ 1.98 = 21.9 GPa per g/cm³.
  5. Epoxy alone: 3.5 ÷ 1.2 = 2.9 GPa per g/cm³. The composite is about 7.5 times stiffer per kilogram.

Answer. Ec = 43.4 GPa, ρc = 1.98 g/cm³ and specific modulus 21.9 GPa per g/cm³, against 2.9 for epoxy alone.

Exam traps

  • Volume fraction, not weight fraction. Vf + Vm = 1 by volume; feeding weight percentages into the rule of mixtures gives a wrong modulus.
  • The rule of mixtures is for continuous aligned fibres loaded along the axis. Across the fibres, or for a chopped-strand mat, it overestimates stiffness badly.
  • Composite density is a weighted average and always lies between the two densities — never their sum, and never the fibre density alone.
  • A ceramic matrix composite gains damage tolerance, not ductility. It still does not yield and stretch like a metal.
  • Pultrusion makes a constant cross-section only. A part whose section changes needs resin transfer moulding or lay-up.

Check yourself

Remember Define matrix and reinforcement in a composite. 2 marks

The matrix is the continuous phase that holds the reinforcement, transfers load and protects it. The reinforcement is the dispersed phase — fibre, whisker or particle — that is stiffer and stronger and carries most of the load.

Remember Name the three fibres commonly used in polymer matrix composites and give one use of each. 3 marks

Glass: water tanks, boat hulls, circuit boards. Carbon: aircraft panels, bicycle frames. Aramid: helmets, ropes, bullet-resistant vests.

Understand Why does a very weak fibre–matrix bond lower the strength of a composite? 3 marks

Load reaches the fibre only through the interface. If the bond is weak, the matrix cannot shear stress into the fibre, so fibres pull out and the part fails at a load far below the fibre strength.

Apply A unidirectional carbon–epoxy lamina has Vf = 0.50, Ef = 230 GPa, Em = 3.5 GPa, ρf = 1.8 g/cm³ and ρm = 1.2 g/cm³. Find Ec, ρc and the specific modulus. 4 marks

Vm = 0.50. Ec = 0.50 × 230 + 0.50 × 3.5 = 115 + 1.75 = 116.75 GPa. ρc = 0.50 × 1.8 + 0.50 × 1.2 = 0.9 + 0.6 = 1.5 g/cm³. Specific modulus = 116.75 ÷ 1.5 = 77.8 GPa per g/cm³.

Analyze Why does the rule of mixtures overestimate the modulus of a randomly oriented fibre mat? 4 marks

The rule assumes every fibre is aligned with the load and shares the same strain. In a random mat most fibres lie at an angle, so they carry less of the load and the ones across the pull contribute almost nothing; the measured modulus falls well below the prediction.

Analyze A sliding part must run at 900 °C under load. Choose a polymer or a ceramic matrix composite and justify the choice. 4 marks

Ceramic matrix composite. Epoxy matrices soften or degrade near 120–150 °C and polyester well below that, so neither can survive 900 °C. A CMC such as SiC–SiC keeps its hardness at that temperature and its fibres bridge cracks, so it tolerates damage instead of shattering.

Takeaways

  1. A polymer is a macromolecule named from its repeat unit, and its classification follows monomer type, chain structure and thermal behaviour.
  2. Tacticity controls how regularly side groups sit along a stereocentre chain, so it controls packing and crystallinity.
  3. Addition polymerization is chain growth from an initiator; condensation polymerization is step growth between bifunctional monomers.
  4. Ziegler–Natta catalysts insert alkene monomers in a fixed orientation and can make isotactic polymers.
  5. Branching, cross-linking and crystallinity change chain packing, and packing sets density, stiffness and softening point.
  6. Each important polymer is best learned as monomer, repeat unit, mechanism and one use.
  7. A polymer sample has a distribution of chain lengths, so Mn, Mw and PDI must be matched to the measurement method.
  8. A conjugated backbone plus doping turns an insulating polymer into a conducting polymer.
  9. A composite combines a matrix and a reinforcement so the pair gives higher specific modulus than either phase alone.
