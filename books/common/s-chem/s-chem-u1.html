1.1 Wave-particle duality and the uncertainty principle

Light and matter are not purely waves or purely particles. Light carries energy in packets called photons, yet it bends and interferes like a wave. An electron can diffract like a wave, yet it lands as one dot on a detector. This chapter builds that picture from black-body radiation, Planck's quantum, the photoelectric effect, de Broglie waves and electron diffraction. It then states Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, which limits how sharply position and momentum can be fixed together. These ideas explain atomic spectra, electron microscopes and why electrons in atoms do not fall into the nucleus. Only a few universities teach this in first semester.

You need school physics: waves, photons and kinetic energy; the next step, putting matter waves into an equation, is in 1.2.

Black-body radiation, quanta and the photoelectric effect

A black body absorbs and emits all wavelengths. Its spectrum is continuous; as temperature T rises, the peak moves to shorter wavelength. Classical wave theory failed at short wavelength. In 1900 Planck proposed that atoms emit or absorb energy only in packets called quanta. One quantum has energy E = hν, where h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s is Planck's constant and ν is frequency in s⁻¹. An oscillator has E = nhν, with n = 0, 1, 2, ...

Light of frequency ν falls on a metal and ejects electrons. Emission starts only above a threshold ν₀, and it is immediate. The fastest electron has KE_max = hν − φ, where φ is the work function in J. Brighter light gives more electrons, not faster ones. Wave theory predicts energy from intensity, so it fails. Light therefore arrives as photons, each with energy hν and momentum p = h/λ, where λ is wavelength in m. Interference and diffraction show the wave face of light.

Matter waves and Heisenberg's uncertainty principle

Louis de Broglie proposed that matter has wavelength λ = h/p = h/(mv). Here p is momentum in kg m s⁻¹, m is mass in kg and v is speed in m s⁻¹. For a moving electron this wavelength is of atomic size. A beam of electrons passed through a thin crystal forms diffraction rings. The measured ring spacing agrees with λ = h/p. Large objects have tiny λ, so their waves are not seen.

Heisenberg's uncertainty principle says position x and momentum p cannot both be sharp. For repeated measurements, Δx Δp ≥ ħ/2. Here Δx is the standard deviation of position in m, Δp is the standard deviation of momentum in kg m s⁻¹, and ħ = h/(2π) = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. The limit is not caused by clumsy instruments. An electron confined to Δx ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m has Δp ≥ ħ/(2Δx) ≈ 5 × 10⁻²⁵ kg m s⁻¹, comparable with atomic momenta. So an electron cannot sit still at a point. This is why atoms have minimum energy; 1.2 turns the idea into an equation.

Worked example

Question. A photon has wavelength 400 nm. Its momentum is known to 0.1%. Find its momentum and the minimum position uncertainty. Use h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and ħ = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ J s.

  1. λ = 400 nm = 400 × 10⁻⁹ m = 4.00 × 10⁻⁷ m.
  2. p = h/λ = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ / 4.00 × 10⁻⁷ = 1.6565 × 10⁻²⁷ ≈ 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg m s⁻¹.
  3. Δp = 0.001p = 1.66 × 10⁻³⁰ kg m s⁻¹.
  4. Δx ≥ ħ/(2Δp) = 1.055 × 10⁻³⁴ / (2 × 1.66 × 10⁻³⁰) = 3.18 × 10⁻⁵ m ≈ 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ m.

Answer. p = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg m s⁻¹; minimum Δx = 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ m = 0.032 mm.

Exam traps

  • Use ħ/2, not h/2. h = 2πħ.
  • Δp = 0.1% of p is 0.001p, not 0.1p.
  • Δx is the standard deviation of many position measurements, not the particle's diameter.
  • Photon momentum is h/λ; a photon has zero rest mass, so p = mc does not apply.
  • Electron diffraction shows matter waves; the photoelectric effect shows light particles.

1.2 Schrodinger equation: particle in a box, hydrogen atom

The Schrödinger equation is quantum mechanics' wave equation. Its solutions, wavefunctions, give allowed energies. We solve a particle in a one-dimensional box, the simplest bound system; its energies are quantised as n². We then write hydrogen wavefunctions as radial times angular parts and read the quantum numbers n, l and m. These ideas explain orbitals, spectra and periodic trends. Only a few universities teach this in Unit 1.

You need 1.1 for wave-particle duality and uncertainty.

The equation and the meaning of ψ

For a particle in a fixed potential V(x), the time-independent Schrödinger equation in one dimension is:

−(ħ²/2m) d²ψ/dx² + V(x)ψ = Eψ

ħ = h/2π is reduced Planck constant (1.055×10⁻³⁴ J s); m is mass in kg; V is potential energy in J; E is total energy in J; ψ is the wavefunction.

Born rule: |ψ|² is probability density, the probability per unit volume of finding the particle. Probability in a small volume dV is |ψ|²dV. For a bound particle, ∫|ψ|²dV = 1.

Allowed energies come from boundary conditions. ψ must be single-valued, continuous and finite. Measurement gives an eigenvalue of the operator for that observable; for energy the operator is H = −(ħ²/2m)d²/dx² + V.

This is why a confined particle cannot have any energy; only discrete E values survive.

The equation is linear: if ψ₁ and ψ₂ solve it, so does c₁ψ₁ + c₂ψ₂.

Particle in a one-dimensional box

Let V = 0 for 0 < x < L and V = ∞ outside. The walls force ψ(0) = ψ(L) = 0.

ψ_n(x) = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L)

n = 1,2,3,... is the quantum number; L is box length in m; x is position in m.

E_n = n²h²/(8mL²)

h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s; m is particle mass in kg; L is length in m.

The ground state n = 1 has energy E₁ > 0. This zero-point energy follows from confinement and the uncertainty principle.

  • ψ_n has n−1 nodes inside the box.
  • Doubling n quadruples E_n because E_n ∝ n².
  • The gap E_{n+1} − E_n = (2n+1)h²/(8mL²) grows with n.

Use it for electrons in thin layers, quantum dots and simple conjugated chains.

The probability density |ψ_n|² has maxima where the particle is most likely. For n = 1 the maximum is at the centre.

No state n = 0 exists because sin(0) = 0 everywhere.

Hydrogen atom: radial and angular parts

For one electron at distance r from a proton, V(r) = −e²/(4πε₀r). Spherical coordinates separate the equation:

ψ_nlm(r,θ,φ) = R_nl(r) Y_lm(θ,φ)

R_nl is the radial part; Y_lm is the angular part; r is distance from nucleus; θ,φ are angular coordinates.

n = 1,2,... is the principal quantum number. l = 0,1,...,n−1 is the orbital angular momentum quantum number. m = −l,...,0,...,+l is the magnetic quantum number.

E_n = −13.6 eV/n²

13.6 eV is the hydrogen ground-state magnitude; n is principal quantum number.

Only n fixes energy in hydrogen. l sets shape: l = 0 is s (spherical), l = 1 is p (dumbbell), l = 2 is d. m sets orientation; there are 2l+1 values.

ψ_1s = (1/√π)(1/a₀)^{3/2} e^{−r/a₀}

a₀ = 52.9 pm is the Bohr radius.

Radial nodes are n−l−1; angular nodes are l. These orbitals feed the screening in 1.3 and bonding in 1.4.

Worked example

Question. An electron is confined in a one-dimensional box of length L = 1.00 nm. Use m = 9.109×10⁻³¹ kg and h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s. Find the ground-state energy in joules and electronvolts.

  1. Convert L: 1.00 nm = 1.00×10⁻⁹ m.
  2. Compute denominator: 8mL² = 8 × 9.109×10⁻³¹ × (1.00×10⁻⁹)² = 7.287×10⁻⁴⁸ kg m².
  3. Compute numerator: h² = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)² = 4.390×10⁻⁶⁷ J² s².
  4. E₁ = 4.390×10⁻⁶⁷ / 7.287×10⁻⁴⁸ = 6.02×10⁻²⁰ J.
  5. In eV: E₁ = 6.02×10⁻²⁰ / 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ = 0.376 eV.

Answer. The ground-state energy is E₁ = 6.02 × 10⁻²⁰ J = 0.376 eV.

Exam traps

  • Box energy goes as 1/L²: doubling L divides E by 4, not 2.
  • n starts at 1; n = 0 gives ψ = 0 everywhere and is not a state.
  • Do not mix h and ħ: the box formula uses h²/(8mL²); with ħ it is π²ħ²/(2mL²).
  • For H, n alone sets energy; l and m do not change E in the one-electron atom.
  • |ψ|² is probability density, not probability; probability needs volume integration.

1.3 Effective nuclear charge, Slater's rules and periodic properties

An outer electron does not feel the full positive charge of the nucleus. Inner electrons repel it and cancel part of the pull. The net charge it feels is the effective nuclear charge, Zeff. This single number explains why atoms shrink across a period, why ionisation energy rises, and why electronegativity changes. It is used whenever a chemist compares sizes or bond strengths. Only a few universities teach this in first-year chemistry.

You need orbitals and quantum numbers from 1.2.

Nuclear charge and screening

An atom with atomic number Z has Z protons in its nucleus. An outer electron does not feel all of that charge. Electrons closer to the nucleus repel it and cancel part of the pull. The cancelled amount is the screening constant S, in units of elementary charge. The net pull is the effective nuclear charge Zeff. S is not a physical shield; it counts how much inner charge is hidden from that electron. Core electrons screen strongly. Electrons in the same outer shell screen weakly. The outer electron in sodium feels Z = 11 but S = 8.80, so Zeff = 2.20, much less than 11. Zeff controls how tightly the outer electron is held.

Zeff = Z − S

Z is the atomic number (number of protons); S is the screening constant in units of elementary charge; Zeff is the effective nuclear charge felt by the electron.

Slater's rules for ns and np electrons

Slater's rules estimate S for an electron in an ns or np orbital. Write the electron configuration in groups. Count other electrons in the same n shell as 0.35 each (0.30 if n = 1). Count electrons in the n−1 shell as 0.85 each. Count electrons in n−2 and lower shells as 1.00 each. Do not count the chosen electron itself. Add these contributions to get S. For nd and nf electrons the counting changes: same-group electrons count 0.35, and all electrons in lower groups count 1.00. Do not use the 0.85 rule there. Example: chlorine has Z = 17 and configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁵. For one 3p electron, the same shell has 6 other electrons: 6 × 0.35 = 2.10. The n=2 shell has 8 electrons: 8 × 0.85 = 6.80. The n=1 shell has 2: 2 × 1.00 = 2.00. S = 10.90, so Zeff = 17 − 10.90 = 6.10. The outer electron feels about 6.1 units, not 17.

From Zeff to periodic trends

Across a period, protons are added but new electrons enter the same outer shell. Same-shell screening is weak, so Zeff rises. A higher Zeff pulls the outer shell inward: atomic radius decreases. More energy is needed to remove the electron: ionisation energy rises. Electron affinity becomes more negative, and electronegativity rises. Down a group, a new shell is added. Inner shells screen efficiently and the outer electron is farther from the nucleus. Zeff rises only slowly because each new core adds about one proton and one screening electron. The extra shell dominates: radius increases, ionisation energy falls, electronegativity falls. These trends explain why sodium loses an electron easily and chlorine gains one. They also feed ionic bonding, see 1.5.

Worked example

Question. Find Zeff for the 3s electron of sodium (Z = 11). Its configuration is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹.

  1. Identify the electron: one 3s electron, so n = 3.
  2. Same-shell other electrons: no other electron in n = 3, contribution = 0 × 0.35 = 0.
  3. n−1 shell (n = 2): 2s² 2p⁶ has 8 electrons, contribution = 8 × 0.85 = 6.80.
  4. n−2 and lower (n = 1): 1s² has 2 electrons, contribution = 2 × 1.00 = 2.00.
  5. Add: S = 0 + 6.80 + 2.00 = 8.80.
  6. Subtract: Zeff = Z − S = 11 − 8.80 = 2.20.

1.4 Molecular orbital theory: diatomics, butadiene and benzene

Molecular orbital theory replaces the shared-pair bond with electrons in orbitals spread over the whole molecule. Atomic orbitals combine into bonding orbitals, lower in energy, and antibonding orbitals, higher. Filling these orbitals explains bond order, magnetism and the delocalised π electrons of butadiene and benzene. It is used where a Lewis structure fails, such as the paramagnetism of O2 or the stability of aromatic rings.

You need 1.2 for wavefunctions and 1.3 for orbital energies.

Atomic orbitals combine into molecular orbitals

Two atomic orbitals on neighbouring atoms combine in two ways. Add the wavefunctions: electron density builds between the nuclei, energy falls, and a bonding molecular orbital (MO) forms. Subtract them: a node appears between the nuclei, energy rises, and an antibonding MO forms. Antibonding orbitals carry a star, such as σ*.

A σ orbital comes from head-on overlap along the internuclear line. A π orbital comes from side-on p overlap and has a nodal plane along that line.

The number of MOs equals the number of atomic orbitals combined. Two atomic orbitals give two MOs. Four p orbitals give four π MOs.

Bond order measures net bonding.

BO = (Nb − Na) / 2

BO is bond order; Nb is the number of electrons in bonding MOs; Na is the number in antibonding MOs.

BO > 0 means a net bond. BO = 0 means no stable bond. The highest occupied molecular orbital is the HOMO. The lowest unoccupied molecular orbital is the LUMO. The HOMO–LUMO gap controls absorption and reactivity.

MO diagrams of diatomic molecules

Fill MOs in increasing energy. Use the Aufbau principle, the Pauli principle (two electrons per orbital, opposite spins) and Hund's rule (spread electrons over degenerate orbitals before pairing).

H2: two electrons fill σ1s². BO = (2 − 0)/2 = 1. He2: four electrons give σ1s² σ*1s². BO = (2 − 2)/2 = 0. No stable bond forms.

For second-row diatomics, 2s orbitals give σ2s and σ*2s. The six 2p orbitals give one σ2p, two π2p, two π*2p and one σ*2p. For Li2 to N2, σ2p lies below π2p. For O2 and F2, π2p lies below σ2p. Mixing of 2s and 2p orbitals causes this switch.

π MOs of butadiene

Butadiene, CH2=CH–CH=CH2, has four carbon atoms in a chain. Each carbon contributes one p orbital perpendicular to the molecular plane. Four p orbitals give four π MOs.

Label them ψ1 to ψ4 by increasing energy. ψ1 has zero nodes, ψ2 one node, ψ3 two nodes, ψ4 three nodes. ψ1 and ψ2 are bonding; ψ3 and ψ4 are antibonding.

Four π electrons fill ψ1² ψ2². ψ2 is the HOMO; ψ3 is the LUMO. The electrons spread over all four carbons, so the π system is delocalised. This lowers the energy compared with two separate double bonds.

The HOMO has larger coefficients on the terminal carbons. Electrophiles therefore attack the ends in many reactions. The reaction mechanisms are covered in organic chemistry.

π MOs of benzene

Benzene, C6H6, has six carbons in a ring. Each carbon contributes one p orbital perpendicular to the ring. Six p orbitals give six π MOs.

Three are bonding and three are antibonding. The lowest bonding MO has zero nodes. Above it is a degenerate pair with one node; some syllabi label this pair e1g. Degenerate means equal energy. Above that is a degenerate pair with two nodes, then one antibonding MO with three nodes.

Six π electrons fill the three lowest MOs: one single MO and the degenerate pair. This is a closed shell. The HOMO is the degenerate one-node pair; the LUMO is the two-node pair.

All six C–C bonds are equal because the π electrons are spread over the ring. The filled bonding MOs give aromatic stabilisation, which is why benzene resists addition that would break the ring.

Worked example

Question. Use molecular orbital theory to find the bond order and the number of unpaired electrons in O2.

  1. Write the valence configuration: σ2s² σ*2s² σ2p² π2p⁴ π*2p².
  2. Count bonding electrons: σ2s² = 2, σ2p² = 2, π2p⁴ = 4. Total Nb = 8.
  3. Count antibonding electrons: σ*2s² = 2, π*2p² = 2. Total Na = 4.
  4. Bond order = (8 − 4)/2 = 2.
  5. The two π*2p electrons occupy separate degenerate orbitals with parallel spins by Hund's rule.
  6. So unpaired electrons = 2; O2 is paramagnetic.

Answer. Bond order = 2 and there are 2 unpaired electrons, so O2 is paramagnetic.

Exam traps

  • O2 has a double bond but is paramagnetic: a Lewis structure with all electrons paired misses the two unpaired π* electrons.
  • Do not put σ* or π* below the bonding orbitals. Antibonding means higher energy.
  • For N2 the order is σ2p below π2p; for O2 and F2 it is π2p below σ2p. Mixing 2s and 2p causes the switch.
  • Bond order counts net pairs, not total electrons. A filled core cancels and does not change BO.
  • HOMO is filled and LUMO is empty. Reversing them gives the wrong reactivity.

1.5 Bonding in solids: ionic, covalent, metallic and hydrogen

A solid stays together because its particles attract. Four common patterns are ionic, covalent network, metallic and hydrogen bonding. The pattern sets melting point, electrical conductivity and solubility. Sodium chloride is ionic, diamond is covalent network, iron is metallic and ice is hydrogen bonded. Weak van der Waals forces also hold molecular solids such as solid carbon dioxide. Engineers use these ideas when choosing salts for molten baths, diamond coatings for cutting tools, steel for structures, and ice for cold storage.

You need 1.4 for covalent bonds and molecular orbitals.

Four bonding patterns

An ionic solid is a three-dimensional lattice of cations and anions. Electrostatic attraction holds each ion to neighbours of opposite charge. NaCl is the standard example: Na+ and Cl− alternate in a cubic pattern. The ions are not molecules. The formula NaCl gives the simplest ratio 1:1. Strong attraction gives a high melting point, hardness and brittleness. Solid NaCl does not conduct because ions are fixed. Molten or dissolved NaCl conducts because ions move.

A covalent network solid has atoms joined by a continuous web of covalent bonds. Diamond is carbon atoms in a tetrahedral network. Each carbon uses four bonds, so the whole crystal is one giant molecule. Bond breaking needs much energy, so diamond has a very high melting point and is very hard. It does not conduct because electrons are localised in sigma bonds.

A metallic solid has a lattice of positive metal ions in a sea of delocalised electrons. Iron is the example. The electrons belong to the whole crystal, not to one atom. This explains metallic lustre, malleability and electrical conductivity in the solid state. Layers can slide without breaking directed bonds.

A hydrogen-bonded solid contains molecules held by hydrogen bonds. A hydrogen bond is the attraction between a hydrogen atom already bonded to a highly electronegative atom (N, O or F) and a lone pair on a nearby N, O or F atom, written X–H···Y. Ice is water molecules linked by O–H···O bonds. Each water molecule can donate two and accept two hydrogen bonds, giving an open tetrahedral network. The bonds are weaker than covalent or ionic bonds, so ice melts near 0 °C. The open network makes ice less dense than liquid water. Van der Waals solids, such as solid CO2, are held by weak induced-dipole forces; they are soft and melt or sublime at low temperature.

Radius ratio predicts coordination number

In an ionic crystal, the coordination number CN is the number of nearest ions of opposite charge around an ion. The cation is usually smaller. The radius ratio r+/r− measures how the sizes fit. At the lower limit of a range the anions just touch and the cation exactly fills the hole; inside the range the cation is slightly larger than the hole and pushes the anions apart. The ratio ranges below are rules of thumb for binary ionic solids.

  • 0.225 to 0.414 gives CN 4, tetrahedral.
  • 0.414 to 0.732 gives CN 6, octahedral.
  • 0.732 to 1.0 gives CN 8, cubic.

r+ / r−

r+ is the cation radius and r− is the anion radius, both in pm; the ratio is dimensionless.

  1. Put both ionic radii in the same unit, usually pm.
  2. Divide the cation radius by the anion radius.
  3. Compare the ratio with the ranges above.
  4. Choose CN and geometry; then check charge balance.
  5. Remember that lattice energy, temperature and covalency can change the real structure.

Charge balance is essential: a 2+ cation and 1− anion cannot simply form a 1:1 NaCl-type lattice; the formula must be neutral. A ratio below 0.225 often gives lower coordination, such as CN 3. Above 1.0 the cation is larger than the anion, so the labels swap in practice. The prediction is not a proof.

1.6 Coordination compounds: EAN and 18-electron rule

Coordination compounds are metal centres surrounded by ligands. Electron counting asks how many electrons sit on the metal. Two bookkeeping rules answer it. The effective atomic number (EAN) rule compares the metal's total electron count with the next noble gas. The 18-electron rule counts only valence electrons and asks for 18. These rules explain why [Ni(CO)4] is stable, why [Fe(CN)6]4− reaches a noble-gas count, and how ferrocene fits. They guide catalyst and medicine design, but the applications are handled elsewhere.

You need: electron configuration and Aufbau filling from 1.3; MO ideas from 1.4.

The vocabulary of electron counting

Electron counting needs four terms. A ligand is an ion or molecule that donates an electron pair to a metal. The coordination number (CN) is the number of donor atoms bonded to the metal. The oxidation state (x) is the charge the metal would carry if each ligand were removed with its shared pair. Hapticity (η) is the number of ligand atoms attached to the metal in one ligand; η5 means five atoms touch the metal.

  • CO is neutral and donates 2 electrons.
  • Halides X− donate 2 electrons.
  • Phosphines PR3 are neutral 2-electron donors.
  • An η5-C5H5− ligand donates 6 electrons; an η6-arene donates 6; an η4-diene donates 4; an η2-alkene donates 2.

Count ligand electrons as L. For monodentate 2-electron donors L = 2 × CN. For η ligands, use the actual donor electrons, not 2 × hapticity.

EAN rule and 18-electron rule

EAN rule. EAN is the total number of electrons around the metal, core plus valence. Use EAN = Z − x + L. Z is the metal's atomic number, x is its oxidation state, and L is the total electrons donated by ligands. For [Fe(CN)6]4−, Z = 26, six CN− give x = +2, and L = 12. EAN = 26 − 2 + 12 = 36, the krypton count. Some syllabi call this the effective atomic number rule.

18-electron rule. Count only valence electrons: V = d + L. For a first-row transition metal, d = group number − x. The target is V = 18. For [Fe(CN)6]4−, Fe2+ is d6, so V = 6 + 12 = 18. The EAN is 36 because it includes the argon core of Fe2+; the valence count is 18. Both rules agree here, but they count different sets.

EAN = Z − x + L; V = d + L

EAN is total electrons around the metal; Z is metal atomic number; x is oxidation state; L is electrons donated by all ligands; V is valence electron count; d is valence d electrons.

Ni(CO)4: Ni is group 10 and x = 0, so d = 10. Four CO give L = 8. V = 10 + 8 = 18. EAN = 28 − 0 + 8 = 36. Ferrocene Fe(C5H5)2: each C5H5− is η5 and donates 6 electrons. Fe2+ is d6. V = 6 + 2 × 6 = 18; EAN = 26 − 2 + 12 = 36.

Where the rule stops

Not every stable complex has 18 electrons. Square-planar d8 complexes such as [PtCl4]2− have Pt2+ d8 plus four Cl− donors: V = 8 + 8 = 16. The 16-electron count is stable for this geometry. Many catalytic intermediates are 16-electron or 14-electron. Use the 18-electron rule as a guide for organometallic carbonyls and related complexes, not as a law for every coordination compound.

Early metals with few d electrons, strong π-donor ligands, and crowded ligands can break the rule. Counting also fails if you mix ionic and neutral-ligand conventions. Choose one convention and keep it. A metal with an odd electron count often forms a metal–metal bond or gains a ligand to reach 18.

Worked example

Question. For [Ni(CO)4], find the oxidation state of nickel, the number of valence electrons on the metal centre, the 18-electron count and the EAN. State whether the complex satisfies the 18-electron rule.

  1. The complex is neutral and has four CO ligands. CO is neutral, so Ni oxidation state x = 0.
  2. Nickel has atomic number Z = 28. For Ni0, count 10 valence d electrons, so d = 10.
  3. Each CO is a neutral 2-electron donor. L = 4 × 2 = 8 electrons.
  4. Valence count V = d + L = 10 + 8 = 18 electrons.
  5. EAN = Z − x + L = 28 − 0 + 8 = 36, which is the krypton count.
  6. V = 18, so [Ni(CO)4] satisfies the 18-electron rule.

Answer. For [Ni(CO)4], the valence count is 18 electrons and the EAN is 36; it satisfies the 18-electron rule.

Exam traps

  • CO is a neutral two-electron donor; do not count it as CO2+ or add charge to the metal.
  • EAN includes core electrons; the 18-electron rule counts valence electrons only. [Fe(CN)6]4− has EAN 36 but a valence count of 18.
  • η5-C5H5− is a 6-electron donor, not a 5-electron or 10-electron donor. Count hapticity and charge together.
  • Halides are X− two-electron donors in ionic counting. If you use the neutral-ligand method, count X as one electron and change the metal oxidation state; never mix the two methods.
  • The 18-electron rule is not universal. Square-planar d8 complexes such as [PtCl4]2− are stable with 16 electrons.

1.7 Crystal field theory: d-orbital splitting in complexes

In a free metal ion, the five d orbitals have the same energy. When ligands come near, their electron clouds repel the metal d electrons unequally. Some d orbitals point straight at the ligands and rise in energy; others point between them and stay lower. This splitting is called crystal field splitting. The model that explains it, and the size of the gap, is crystal field theory. The pattern and size of the splitting depend on geometry (octahedral, tetrahedral, square planar) and on how strong the ligand field is. It explains why complexes are coloured, why some are magnetic, and why d⁴ to d⁷ complexes choose high-spin or low-spin arrangements.

You need: 1.6 for complex formation, oxidation states and ligand naming.

Why the five d orbitals split

In a free metal ion, the five d orbitals — dxy, dxz, dyz, dx²−y² and dz² — have the same energy. They are degenerate. Ligands carry lone-pair electrons. When six ligands approach along the ±x, ±y and ±z axes in an octahedral complex, their electron clouds repel the metal d electrons. The repulsion is not equal for all five orbitals.

The dx²−y² and dz² orbitals point along the axes, straight at the ligands. They feel strong repulsion and rise in energy. The dxy, dxz and dyz orbitals lie between the axes. They feel less repulsion and stay lower. The energy gap between the two sets is Δo (o for octahedral). The average energy of the five orbitals is the barycentre. Relative to it, the upper two orbitals rise by +0.6Δo and the lower three fall by −0.4Δo. The total shift is zero: 2(+0.6) + 3(−0.4) = 0. This keeps the barycentre fixed.

E(eg) = +0.6Δ<sub>o</sub>;  E(t<sub>2g</sub>) = −0.4Δ<sub>o</sub>

Δo is the octahedral crystal field splitting energy; eg is the upper set (dx²−y², dz²); t2g is the lower set (dxy, dxz, dyz).

Octahedral, tetrahedral and square planar patterns

In an octahedral complex, the two upper orbitals are labelled eg and the three lower orbitals are labelled t2g. The size of Δo depends on the metal ion and on the ligands. Strong-field ligands such as CN− and CO give a large Δo. Weak-field ligands such as H2O and Cl− give a smaller Δo.

In a tetrahedral complex, four ligands sit at alternate corners of a cube. No d orbital points directly at the ligands. The splitting is smaller and inverted. The lower set is e (dx²−y², dz²) at −0.6Δt and the upper set is t2 (dxy, dxz, dyz) at +0.4Δt. The relation is Δt = (4/9)Δo. Fewer ligands and no direct overlap make Δt much smaller than Δo.

A square planar complex is an extreme distortion of octahedral geometry with the two axial ligands removed. It is common for d⁸ ions such as Pt(II) and Pd(II). The dx²−y² orbital points directly at the four ligands and is raised most. The other orbitals lie lower, roughly in the order dxy > dz² > dxz, dyz, with dxz and dyz degenerate and lowest. Splitting is large, so square planar complexes are usually low spin.

CFSE and high-spin versus low-spin

Crystal field stabilisation energy (CFSE) is the net energy change when d electrons occupy the split orbitals, measured from the barycentre. A negative CFSE means the complex is stabilised. For octahedral complexes, count electrons in t2g (nt2g) and in eg (neg). Use the formula below. Pairing energy P is the repulsion cost of putting two electrons in one orbital.

CFSE<sub>oct</sub> = (−0.4 n<sub>t2g</sub> + 0.6 n<sub>eg</sub>)Δ<sub>o</sub>;  CFSE<sub>tet</sub> = (−0.6 n<sub>e</sub> + 0.4 n<sub>t2</sub>)Δ<sub>t</sub>

nt2g, neg, ne, nt2 are the numbers of electrons in those orbital sets; Δo and Δt are the splitting energies in the two geometries.

For d⁴ to d⁷ ions in an octahedral field, two arrangements compete. If Δo < P, electrons spread out over all five orbitals: high spin. If Δo > P, electrons pair in the lower t2g set: low spin. Take d⁴. High spin is t2g³ eg¹, CFSE = −1.2Δo + 0.6Δo = −0.6Δo. Low spin is t2g⁴, CFSE = −1.6Δo + P. Low spin wins when −1.6Δo + P < −0.6Δo, that is Δo > P. Ions d¹ to d³ and d⁸ to d¹⁰ have no such choice.

Tetrahedral Δt is only about 4/9 of Δo. It is usually smaller than the pairing energy, so tetrahedral complexes are almost always high spin. Colour and magnetic moments follow from these splittings; they are covered in 1.8.

Worked example

Question. Find the crystal field stabilisation energy (CFSE) of the complex ion [Cr(H2O)6]3+. The ligand H2O is weak-field. Use Δo for the octahedral splitting.

  1. Let the oxidation state of Cr be x. The complex has charge +3 and six neutral H2O ligands: x + 6(0) = +3, so x = +3.
  2. Cr has atomic number 24. Cr3+ has 24 − 3 = 21 electrons. The argon core holds 18 electrons. The remaining d electrons are 21 − 18 = 3. Configuration is d³.
  3. Six H2O ligands give octahedral geometry. The d orbitals split into t2g (lower) and eg (upper).
  4. For d³, the three electrons fill the lower t2g set singly: t2g³ eg⁰. No electron pairing is needed, so the pairing energy does not enter.
  5. CFSE = 3 × (−0.4Δo) + 0 × (+0.6Δo) = −1.2Δo.

Answer. CFSE = −1.2Δo; the negative sign means the d³ configuration is stabilised by 1.2Δo.

Exam traps

  • Do not treat Δt as equal to Δo. Tetrahedral splitting is Δt = (4/9)Δo, much smaller, so most tetrahedral complexes are high spin.
  • Do not swap the labels. In octahedral fields eg is higher and t2g is lower. In tetrahedral fields e is lower and t2 is higher.
  • CFSE is negative when the complex is stabilised. A positive CFSE means destabilisation. Pairing energy P must be added when electrons are forced to pair in one orbital.
  • Square planar is not the same as octahedral. The dx²−y² orbital is strongly raised in square planar complexes such as Pt(II) and Pd(II).
  • High-spin and low-spin choices exist only for d⁴ to d⁷ in octahedral fields. d¹ to d³ and d⁸ to d¹⁰ have only one filling pattern.

1.8 Colour and magnetic properties of complexes

Many transition-metal complexes are coloured and magnetic. Both facts come from the d electrons. Ligands split the five d orbitals, creating a gap Δo (the octahedral crystal-field splitting, defined below). Light whose photon energy matches Δo is absorbed, and the eye sees the complementary colour. Unpaired d electrons give a magnetic moment. The same ideas explain why [Fe(CN)6]3− is low spin with one unpaired electron, while [Fe(H2O)6]3+ is high spin. Only a few universities teach this depth. These ideas guide pigment design and magnetic measurements.

You need 1.7 for d-orbital splitting, high-spin and low-spin filling.

Colour from absorbed light

White light contains wavelengths from about 400 nm to 700 nm. A complex absorbs some wavelengths and lets the rest pass. The colour you see is the complementary colour of the absorbed band. If a solution absorbs green light near 500 nm, it looks red or orange. This is why a plot of absorbance against wavelength peaks at the absorbed colour, not the observed colour.

Δo = hc/λ

Δo is the octahedral crystal-field gap in J; h = 6.626 × 10−34 J s is Planck's constant; c = 3.00 × 108 m/s is the speed of light; λ is the absorbed wavelength in m. For a mole, multiply by Avogadro's number.

An octahedral complex has t2g and eg sets separated by Δo. A d–d transition moves an electron from t2g to eg. Absorption is strongest when photon energy hc/λ equals Δo. Larger Δo means shorter absorbed wavelength. Strong-field ligands such as CN− raise Δo; weak-field ligands such as H2O give a smaller gap. Many d–d transitions are weak because they are Laporte-forbidden, so colours are often pale. Charge-transfer bands can be much stronger.

Magnetism and the spin-only formula

Each unpaired electron has spin, so it acts like a tiny magnet. A complex with n unpaired d electrons has magnetic moment μ. The spin-only formula is μ = √(n(n+2)) BM, where BM is the Bohr magneton, μB = 9.274 × 10−24 J/T. n is the number of unpaired electrons, not the total d electrons. Paired electrons cancel and give no spin contribution. A complex with n = 0 is diamagnetic and has μ = 0 BM; low-spin d6 [Fe(CN)6]4− is one example.

Octahedral Fe3+ is d5. With weak-field H2O, the five d electrons occupy all five orbitals singly: high spin, n = 5, μ = √(5×7) = 5.92 BM. With strong-field CN−, the t2g set fills first: low spin, n = 1, μ = √(1×3) = 1.73 BM. The table compares the two.

1.9 Hard and soft acids and bases (HSAB)

Hard and soft acids and bases (HSAB) is a rule for predicting which ions and molecules prefer each other. A hard acid or base is small, highly charged and holds its electrons tightly, so it is not easily polarised. A soft acid or base is large, lightly charged and easily polarised. Hard prefers hard, soft prefers soft. Chemists use this to choose precipitating agents, explain chelation, and select catalysts. Only a few universities teach HSAB as a named topic.

You need: 1.5 for ionic bonding and 1.6 for metal ions.

Hardness, softness and polarisability

An acid is an electron-pair acceptor; a base is an electron-pair donor. In HSAB, hardness is about charge density, not pH. Charge density is charge divided by volume. A small ion with a high charge has high charge density. Its electron cloud is held tightly and resists distortion. Such an acid or base is hard. A large ion with a low charge has low charge density. Its outer electrons are loosely held and the cloud distorts easily. Such an acid or base is soft. Polarisability is the ease with which an electron cloud is distorted. High polarisability means soft; low polarisability means hard.

Hard acids include H+, Mg2+ and Al3+. Hard bases include F−, OH−, H2O and NH3. Soft acids include Ag+, Hg2+ and Cu+. Soft bases include I−, S2−, CN− and CO. Borderline acids such as Fe2+ and Cu2+ sit between the two groups. The rule is simple: hard–hard pairs form strong ionic or electrostatic bonds; soft–soft pairs form strong covalent bonds; hard–soft pairs are weaker.

charge density = q ÷ V

q is the charge on the ion in coulombs; V is its volume in cubic metres.

Using HSAB to predict stability

Silver iodide, AgI, is far less soluble than silver fluoride, AgF, in water. HSAB explains the trend. Ag+ is large and soft. I− is large and soft, so the Ag+–I− interaction has strong covalent character. F− is small and hard; it pairs poorly with soft Ag+. Therefore AgI is favoured and AgF is less favoured. This predicts precipitation: adding I− to Ag+ gives AgI even when F− is present. The same logic explains why soft metal ions like Hg2+ bind strongly to soft sulfur donors in proteins. Chelating ligands are chosen with matching hardness: a hard metal such as Al3+ prefers hard O or F donors; a soft metal such as Hg2+ prefers S donors. In catalysis, a soft metal surface binds soft reactants more strongly; a hard support binds hard groups. Do not use pH to classify hardness: HCl is a strong acid in water, but chloride is a borderline-to-soft base; the proton H+ is hard.

Worked example

Question. A mixture contains Ag+ ions and both F− and I− at equal concentration. Using HSAB, predict which silver halide forms first, and explain why.

  1. Identify the acid: Ag+ is large and has low charge density, so it is soft.
  2. Identify the bases: F− is small with high charge density, so it is hard; I− is large and polarisable, so it is soft.
  3. Match: soft Ag+ with soft I− gives the stronger interaction; hard F− pairs poorly with soft Ag+.
  4. Predict: AgI forms first and is less soluble; AgF remains more soluble.

Answer. AgI forms first because soft Ag+ pairs with soft I−; AgF is less favoured because hard F− pairs poorly with soft Ag+.

Exam traps

  • Hardness is not acid strength. HCl is a strong acid in water, but H+ is hard and Cl− is borderline-to-soft; pH does not classify HSAB.
  • Size beats charge count: Li+ is hard and Cs+ is soft, even though both carry +1.
  • Cu2+ is borderline; Cu+ is soft. Do not treat all copper ions as one class.
  • Do not swap the bond descriptions: hard–hard is ionic or electrostatic; soft–soft is covalent.
  • A hard base does not always give an insoluble salt. Match the acid before predicting precipitation.

CO is a soft base; a soft metal centre such as Pd2+ or Ag+ binds it strongly by soft–soft matching. A hard centre such as Al3+ would bind CO poorly.

Analyze Why is pH not a valid HSAB scale? 5 marks

pH measures H+ activity in water. HSAB hardness depends on charge density and polarisability of the acid or base. A strong acid can contain a hard cation and a soft anion, so pH cannot classify both. Hardness is not acidity.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. HSAB is used in medicinal chemistry to design chelating drugs that remove toxic soft metals such as Hg2+ and Pb2+ from the body. In environmental chemistry, soft–soft binding explains why sulfide minerals trap heavy metal ions. Catalyst designers match a metal centre to a reactant. Takeaways

  1. Light and electrons show both wave and particle behaviour; the uncertainty principle limits simultaneous position and momentum knowledge.
  2. The Schrödinger equation gives allowed wavefunctions and quantised energies; particle-in-a-box energy rises as n²/L².
  3. Inner electrons screen the nucleus, so Zeff controls size, ionisation energy and electronegativity trends.
  4. MO theory fills bonding and antibonding orbitals; bond order predicts bond strength and magnetism.
  5. Conjugated π systems such as butadiene and benzene have delocalised MOs; benzene's filled bonding shell gives aromatic stability.
  6. Ionic, covalent, metallic and hydrogen bonding produce different melting points, conductivity and solubility.
  7. Coordination compounds are counted by coordination number, oxidation state, EAN and the 18-electron rule.
  8. Crystal field splitting explains colour, magnetism and ligand-field preferences in transition metal complexes.
  9. HSAB predicts stable acid–base pairs: hard with hard, soft with soft.

Glossary

18-electron rule
The rule that many stable organometallic complexes have 18 valence electrons around the metal.
Bond order
Half the difference between bonding and antibonding electrons in a molecule.
Coordination number
The number of ligand donor atoms directly bonded to the central metal ion.
Covalent bond
A bond formed by sharing one or more pairs of electrons between atoms.
Crystal field splitting
The separation of d-orbital energies caused by the electrostatic field of ligands in a complex.
EAN
Effective atomic number, the electron count around a metal centre calculated from the metal atomic number, oxidation state and ligand donations.
Effective nuclear charge
The net positive charge felt by an electron after other electrons screen the nucleus, Zeff = Z − S.
Electronegativity
The tendency of an atom in a molecule to attract shared electrons to itself.
HOMO
The highest occupied molecular orbital in a molecule.
HSAB principle
The principle that hard acids prefer hard bases and soft acids prefer soft bases.
Hydrogen bond
A strong dipole–dipole attraction in which H bonded to N, O or F interacts with a lone pair on another electronegative atom.
Ionic bond
The electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
Ionisation energy
The minimum energy needed to remove one electron from a gaseous atom or ion.
Ligand
An ion or molecule that donates an electron pair to a metal ion to form a coordination bond.
LUMO
The lowest unoccupied molecular orbital in a molecule.
Metallic bond
A bonding model in which delocalised valence electrons hold together a lattice of positive metal ions.
Molecular orbital
An orbital that belongs to a whole molecule and is built from a linear combination of atomic orbitals.
Oxidation state
The charge an atom would have if all its bonds were treated as ionic, assigned by electron counting rules.
Paramagnetism
Weak attraction of a substance into a magnetic field due to one or more unpaired electrons.
Particle in a box
A model of a particle confined between two impenetrable walls, giving En = n²h²/(8mL²).
Probability density
The quantity |ψ|², which gives the probability per unit volume of finding a particle at a point.
Slater's rules
A set of empirical rules used to estimate the screening constant S for an electron in an atom.
Spectrochemical series
An ordered list of ligands by their ability to split d orbitals; strong-field ligands give larger Δ.
Uncertainty principle
The rule that Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2, where ħ = h/(2π), so position and momentum cannot both be exact at the same time.
Wavefunction
A mathematical function ψ that describes the state of a quantum system; |ψ|² gives probability density.
Wave–particle duality
The property that light and matter show both wave-like and particle-like behaviour depending on the experiment.
