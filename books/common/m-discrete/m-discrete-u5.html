5.1 Lattices as posets and as algebraic systems

A lattice is an ordered set where every two elements have a least upper bound (join) and a greatest lower bound (meet). Order gives one definition; two operations with four laws give another, describing the same object. Lattices underlie Boolean algebra, set families closed under union and intersection, and divisibility. Circuit minimisation and query planners use join and meet.

You need Unit 2: partial orders, posets and Hasse diagrams — reflexivity, antisymmetry, transitivity, comparability.

Bounds of a pair, and the poset definition

In a poset (L, ≤), an upper bound of a, b is any u with a ≤ u and b ≤ u. A lower bound is any l with l ≤ a and l ≤ b. Bounds may be missing or many.

The least upper bound is the upper bound below every other. The greatest lower bound is the lower bound above every other. Antisymmetry makes each unique when it exists.

a ∨ b = lub{a, b}, a ∧ b = glb{a, b}

a ∨ b is the join of a and b, also written sup(a, b); a ∧ b is the meet, also written inf(a, b). Read ∨ as join and ∧ as meet. In a Boolean lattice they behave like OR and AND, but here they are order concepts.

(L, ≤) is a lattice when every pair a, b has join and meet in L. Pairs suffice: by induction every nonempty finite subset has both. Infinite subsets may fail; the rationals have no least upper bound for numbers whose square is below 2.

Four small posets put to the test

Comparable pairs are easy: if a ≤ b then a ∨ b = b and a ∧ b = a. Trouble comes from incomparable pairs.

  • Chain: positive integers under ≤; join=max, meet=min. Every chain is a lattice.
  • Diamond: {1, 2, 3, 6} under divisibility; 2, 3 incomparable, upper bound 6 only, lower 1 only, so 2 ∨ 3 = 6 and 2 ∧ 3 = 1. Lattice.
  • Divisors of 12: A = {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12}; join = lcm, meet = gcd, both stay in A.
  • Failure: B = {2, 3, 6, 12}. Upper bounds of 2 and 3 are 6 and 12; 6 divides 12, so 2 ∨ 3 = 6 exists. But 2 and 3 have no common lower bound in B because 1 is not in B, so 2 ∧ 3 does not exist. Thus B is not a lattice. For a missing join use {2, 3, 12, 18}: upper bounds of 2, 3 are 12 and 18, neither divides the other, so 2 ∨ 3 does not exist.

Lattice as an algebraic system

An algebraic lattice is a set L with binary ∨, ∧ such that for all a, b, c:

  • Idempotent: a ∨ a = a and a ∧ a = a.
  • Commutative: a ∨ b = b ∨ a and a ∧ b = b ∧ a.
  • Associative: (a ∨ b) ∨ c = a ∨ (b ∨ c), and the same with ∧.
  • Absorption: a ∨ (a ∧ b) = a and a ∧ (a ∨ b) = a.

No order, bottom or top is assumed. The views are equivalent. A poset lattice satisfies the laws. Conversely, define a ≤ b to mean a ∧ b = a.

  1. Reflexive: a ∧ a = a.
  2. Antisymmetric: if a ∧ b = a and b ∧ a = b, then a = a ∧ b = b ∧ a = b.
  3. Transitive: if a ∧ b = a and b ∧ c = b, then a ∧ c = (a ∧ b) ∧ c = a ∧ (b ∧ c) = a ∧ b = a.
  4. Lower bound: (a ∧ b) ∧ a = a ∧ b, so a ∧ b ≤ a; similarly ≤ b. Greatest: if x ≤ a, b then x ∧ (a ∧ b) = (x ∧ a) ∧ b = x ∧ b = x, so x ≤ a ∧ b.
  5. Upper bound: a ≤ a ∨ b is absorption a ∧ (a ∨ b) = a; the other law gives b ≤ a ∨ b. Least: if a ≤ x, b ≤ x then (a ∨ b) ∨ x = a ∨ (b ∨ x) = a ∨ x = x, so a ∨ b ≤ x.

Thus a ∧ b is glb and a ∨ b is lub. Duality: swap ∨ with ∧ and reverse ≤; true statements stay true. Some syllabi call these the poset and algebraic versions.

Worked example

Question. Let A = {1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 12} be ordered by divisibility, so a ≤ b means a divides b. (a) Find 4 ∨ 6 and 4 ∧ 6. (b) Find 2 ∨ 3 and 2 ∧ 3. (c) Find 3 ∨ 4 and 3 ∧ 4. (d) Decide whether A is a lattice. (5 marks)

  1. a ≤ b when b = k·a. The covers are the edges of the figure.
  2. For 4, 6: common multiples in A: 12 only, so join 12. Common divisors: 1, 2; greatest 2, so meet 2.
  3. For 2, 3: upper bounds 6, 12; 6 divides 12, so join 6. Lower bound only 1, so meet 1.
  4. For 3, 4: lcm 12, gcd 1, so join 12, meet 1.
  5. The incomparable pairs are {2, 3}, {3, 4}, {4, 6}; each has join and meet in A. Comparable pairs are automatic. So A is a lattice.
  6. Under divisibility join = lcm and meet = gcd; both stay inside A for all 36 ordered pairs (a, b), that is, for every choice of a and b in A.

Output

4 ∧ 6 = 2
2 ∨ 3 = 6
2 ∧ 3 = 1
3 ∨ 4 = 12

Answer. A is a lattice, with 4 ∨ 6 = 12, 4 ∧ 6 = 2, 2 ∨ 3 = 6, 2 ∧ 3 = 1, 3 ∨ 4 = 12 and 3 ∧ 4 = 1.

Exam traps

  • Naming any upper bound the least upper bound. In {2, 3, 12, 18} under divisibility, 12 and 18 are both upper bounds of 2 and 3, and neither divides the other, so 2 and 3 have no least upper bound.
  • Checking only comparable pairs. Those always give join=larger, meet=smaller; marks lie in incomparable pairs.
  • Swapping join and meet. Join is upper (lcm under divisibility), meet lower (gcd).
  • Believing a lattice needs bottom and top. Only pairs need bounds; positive integers under ≤ form a lattice with no top.
  • Confusing lattice with Hasse diagram. Diagram is a picture; lattice is set with order.

5.2 Properties of lattices and sublattices

A lattice is a poset in which every pair has a join and a meet. The order definition forces a fixed set of identities: idempotent, commutative, associative and absorption laws. These laws let you simplify expressions without drawing the Hasse diagram. A sublattice is a nonempty subset that is closed under both join and meet, with the order inherited from the parent lattice. Closure tests decide whether a smaller set of divisors, states or bit patterns is a valid lattice in its own right; checking only one operation is a common mistake.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for the poset definition and the join and meet operations.

Laws forced by the order

Take a lattice L with order ≤. For a,b∈L, a∨b is the least element that is ≥ both a and b; a∧b is the greatest element that is ≤ both. The order therefore forces four families of identities.

LawJoin formMeet form
Idempotenta∨a=aa∧a=a
Commutativea∨b=b∨aa∧b=b∧a
Associative(a∨b)∨c=a∨(b∨c)(a∧b)∧c=a∧(b∧c)
Absorptiona∨(a∧b)=aa∧(a∨b)=a

Idempotent and commutative follow from sets. The set {a,a} is just {a}, so its least upper bound and greatest lower bound are both a. The set {a,b} equals {b,a}, so the order of the inputs cannot change the answer.

Associative: (a∨b)∨c and a∨(b∨c) are both the least upper bound of {a,b,c}; the same set argument gives the meet law. Absorption: a∧b≤a, so a is an upper bound of a and a∧b. Every upper bound u of those two has u≥a, hence a is the least such upper bound. This proves a∨(a∧b)=a. Swap ∨ with ∧ and reverse ≤ to get the dual law a∧(a∨b)=a.

a ∨ (a ∧ b) = a, a ∧ (a ∨ b) = a

a and b are elements of L; the second identity is the dual of the first, obtained by swapping ∨ with ∧.

Monotonicity

Monotonicity says order survives when the same c is joined or met on both sides. If a≤b then the join with c and the meet with c also satisfy the same direction.

a ≤ b implies a∨c ≤ b∨c and a∧c ≤ b∧c

a,b,c∈L; ∨ is join and ∧ is meet.

For join, b∨c is ≥b≥a and ≥c, so it is an upper bound of a and c. The least upper bound a∨c is therefore ≤b∨c. For meet, a∧c≤a≤b and a∧c≤c, so a∧c is a lower bound of b and c. The greatest lower bound b∧c is therefore ≥a∧c.

Use monotonicity when a value is replaced by a larger one in a merge point: the join can only rise, and the meet can only rise as well. It is the licence for step-by-step comparisons of lattice expressions. It also lets you compare two expressions without listing every element of L.

Sublattices and the closure test

A sublattice is a nonempty subset S of L such that for all a,b∈S, a∨b∈S and a∧b∈S. The operations are computed in L, not in S. S inherits the order from L. If S is closed under both operations, S is itself a lattice under the inherited order.

  1. Check that S is nonempty.
  2. Take any a,b∈S and compute a∨b and a∧b in L.
  3. Reject S if either result is outside S; accept only when both stay inside for every pair.

A subposet is any subset of L with the inherited order; it need not be closed. Every sublattice is a subposet, but not every subposet is a sublattice.

Bounded case: if L has least element 0 and greatest element 1, a bounded sublattice that keeps the same bounds must contain 0 and 1. A sublattice can have its own 0 and 1 different from L; then it is not a bounded sublattice of L in the same sense. See Chapter 5.4 for bounded lattices. Some syllabi write sub-lattice.

Worked example

Question. In A={1,2,3,4,6,12} ordered by divisibility, test S={1,2,4,12} and T={1,2,3,12} for being sublattices. Show the needed joins and meets.

  1. In divisibility order, x≤y means x divides y. So a∨b=lcm(a,b) and a∧b=gcd(a,b).
  2. For S, 2∨4=lcm(2,4)=4, 2∧4=gcd(2,4)=2, 4∨12=lcm(4,12)=12, 4∧12=gcd(4,12)=4, 2∨12=lcm(2,12)=12, 2∧12=gcd(2,12)=2.
  3. With the least element 1, 1∨x=x and 1∧x=1 for every x in S. Equal pairs are covered by idempotence. Every result lies in S, and S is nonempty, so S is a sublattice.
  4. For T, 2∨3=lcm(2,3)=6. The value 6 is not in T, so T is not closed under join and is not a sublattice. T is still a subposet because it inherits the divisibility order.

Answer. S={1,2,4,12} is a sublattice; T={1,2,3,12} is not, because 2∨3=6 leaves T.

Exam traps

  • Checking closure under only join or only meet. A sublattice must stay inside S under both operations.
  • Computing join and meet inside S after deleting elements. Compute them in the parent lattice L; the result may fall outside S.
  • Treating any nonempty subset as a sublattice. A subposet only inherits the order; it need not be closed.
  • Forgetting the nonempty condition. Under this definition the empty set is not a sublattice.
  • For a bounded sublattice, forgetting to keep the same 0 and 1 as the parent lattice when the bounds must be preserved.

5.3 Direct product and lattice homomorphism

A direct product builds a new lattice from two lattices by pairing elements. The order compares each coordinate, and join and meet work coordinate by coordinate. A lattice homomorphism is a map between lattices that keeps both operations: h(a∨b)=h(a)∨h(b) and h(a∧b)=h(a)∧h(b). These ideas let us build larger ordered systems from smaller ones and compare their structure. They appear in product orders in computer science, in combining truth values, and in digital design. Only a few universities teach this as a separate topic.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for join, meet and order, and Unit 2 for bijective functions.

Direct product

A direct product pairs elements of two lattices. Let L1 and L2 be lattices. The product L1×L2 has all ordered pairs (a,b), with a∈L1 and b∈L2. Its order compares both coordinates.

(a,b) ≤ (c,d) ⇔ a ≤ c and b ≤ d

(a,b) and (c,d) are pairs in L1×L2; ≤ on the left is the product order; ≤ on the right are the orders in L1 and L2.

For pairs, write (a,b)≤(c,d) only when both a≤c and b≤d hold. One coordinate alone is not enough. The join and meet are coordinatewise.

(a,b) ∨ (c,d) = (a ∨ c, b ∨ d),  (a,b) ∧ (c,d) = (a ∧ c, b ∧ d)

∨ and ∧ on the left are product operations; on the right they are the operations in L1 and L2.

The result is a lattice: the pair of coordinatewise least upper bounds is the least upper bound in the product, and the same holds for greatest lower bounds. To check this, use the defining inequalities in each coordinate. Products of three or more lattices use longer tuples and the same coordinate rule. This construction works for any two lattices, finite or infinite. Some syllabi call this the product lattice. The figure shows L×L for L={0,1}.

Lattice homomorphism

A lattice homomorphism is a map h:L→M between lattices that preserves join and meet.

h(a ∨ b) = h(a) ∨ h(b),  h(a ∧ b) = h(a) ∧ h(b)

a,b are elements of L; ∨ on the left is in L and on the right in M; similarly for ∧.

Here a,b are elements of L. Use the same symbols in both lattices, but evaluate each symbol in its own lattice. A bijective homomorphism is an isomorphism. Every homomorphism is order-preserving: if a≤b, then a∨b=b, so h(a)∨h(b)=h(b), hence h(a)≤h(b). The converse fails. To test a map, check both identities for all a,b. A map that preserves join may fail meet, and a map that preserves meet may fail join. The kernel idea from groups is not used here; congruences are a later topic. For bounded lattices, some texts also require h(0)=0 and h(1)=1.

Projection and a warning

For L1×L2, the first projection is π1(a,b)=a. It is a homomorphism:

π1((a,b) ∨ (c,d)) = π1(a∨c, b∨d) = a∨c = π1(a,b) ∨ π1(c,d)

a,c∈L1 and b,d∈L2; π1 returns the first coordinate.

The meet check is the same, with ∧. The second projection π2(a,b)=b is also a homomorphism. Now take f:L×L→L by f(a,b)=a∨b. If (a,b)≤(c,d), then a≤c and b≤d, so a∨b≤c∨d; thus f is order-preserving. It need not preserve meet. For L={0,1}, f(1,0)=1 and f(0,1)=1, so f(1,0)∧f(0,1)=1. But (1,0)∧(0,1)=(0,0), and f(0,0)=0. Since 0≠1, f is not a meet-homomorphism. A map must satisfy both identities to be a lattice homomorphism.

Exam traps

  • Order-preserving does not imply lattice homomorphism; check both operations.
  • Do not swap join and meet in h(a∨b) or h(a∧b).
  • In L1×L2, (a,b)≤(c,d) needs both coordinate inequalities, not one.

5.4 Special lattices: distributive, complemented and bounded

Some lattices have a bottom element 0 and a top element 1. Some give every element a complement. Some obey a distributive law. These extra rules separate useful lattices from general ones. A bounded distributive complemented lattice is a Boolean lattice, the structure behind logic circuits and set algebra. This chapter names the three extra laws, gives examples and counterexamples, and shows why distributivity makes complements unique.

You need: Chapter 5.1 for join and meet, and Chapter 5.2 for lattice identities.

Bounded lattices and complements

A bounded lattice has a least element 0 and a greatest element 1. For every element a, the order satisfies 0 ≤ a ≤ 1. The identities a∨0=a and a∧1=a hold, where ∨ is join (least upper bound), ∧ is meet (greatest lower bound), 0 is the bottom, and 1 is the top. Some syllabi call 0 and 1 the least and greatest elements; do not read them as the numbers zero and one.

a ∨ 0 = a, a ∧ 1 = a

a is any element; 0 is the least element; 1 is the greatest element; ∨ is join; ∧ is meet.

An element b is a complement of a when a∨b=1 and a∧b=0. A complemented lattice is a bounded lattice in which every element has at least one complement. It does not promise a unique complement. In the diamond M3, the element a has both b and c as complements; this lattice is complemented but its complements are not unique.

a ∨ b = 1, a ∧ b = 0

b is a complement of a; 0 is the bottom; 1 is the top; ∨ is join; ∧ is meet.

Distributive lattices

A distributive lattice obeys a∧(b∨c)=(a∧b)∨(a∧c) for all elements a, b and c. The dual law a∨(b∧c)=(a∨b)∧(a∨c) is equivalent in a lattice. Chains are distributive: put all elements on one line, and join is max, meet is min. The divisor lattice of 12 is distributive: elements are {1,2,3,4,6,12}, join is lcm, meet is gcd, bottom is 1, top is 12. Check one triple, for example 2∧(3∨4)=2∧12=2 and (2∧3)∨(2∧4)=1∨2=2. The diamond M3 and the pentagon N5 are the standard non-distributive lattices. M3 has three incomparable middle elements; N5 has five elements in a pentagon order.

a ∧ (b ∨ c) = (a ∧ b) ∨ (a ∧ c)

a, b, c are lattice elements; ∧ is meet; ∨ is join.

Unique complements and Boolean lattices

In a distributive lattice a complement is unique. Suppose b and c both complement a. Then b = b∧1 = b∧(a∨c) = (b∧a)∨(b∧c) = 0∨(b∧c) = b∧c. Similarly c = b∧c, so b = c. The proof uses a∨c=1, a∧b=0, and distributivity. A Boolean lattice is a bounded distributive complemented lattice. In it every element has exactly one complement, often written a′ or ¬a. The power set of a set is the standard example: join is union, meet is intersection, complement is set difference from the whole set, 0 is ∅, and 1 is the whole set. Chapter 5.5 turns this definition into Boolean algebra laws.

Worked example

Exam traps

  • Complemented means every element has at least one complement; it does not mean unique. Uniqueness needs distributivity.
  • 0 and 1 in a lattice are bounds, not the integers zero and one.
  • In any lattice a∧(b∨c) ≥ (a∧b)∨(a∧c) is not generally equality; distributivity is the equality case.
  • A chain is distributive, but a chain with three elements is not complemented; do not call every chain Boolean.
  • To disprove distributivity, one triple with unequal sides is enough; M3 and N5 are the standard counterexamples.

5.5 Boolean algebra and its laws

A Boolean algebra is a lattice with three extra powers: it has a smallest element 0 and a largest element 1, meet distributes over join (and join over meet), and every element a has a complement a′ with a∨a′=1 and a∧a′=0. This short set of laws is enough to prove the usual rules of sets and logic. Circuit designers use it to simplify gates; database engineers use it to rewrite conditions; students meet it in truth tables and set identities. Its two definitions—order-based and equation-based—are equivalent.

You need: Chapter 5.4 for bounded, complemented and distributive lattices.

Two equivalent definitions

A Boolean algebra can be described in two equivalent ways. The first uses order. Start with a lattice (B,∨,∧). It is bounded: there are elements 0 and 1 with 0≤a≤1 for all a. It is distributive: meet distributes over join and join over meet. It is complemented: for each a there is a′ with a∨a′=1 and a∧a′=0. A lattice with all four properties is a Boolean algebra, and we require 0≠1. The second description forgets order and lists equations. Let B be a set with two operations ∨ and ∧, one unary operation ′, and constants 0 and 1. For all a,b,c∈B the commutative, distributive, identity and complement laws hold, and 0≠1. The two descriptions match: from the lattice conditions you get the equations, and from the equations you can define a≤b iff a∨b=b and recover a bounded distributive complemented lattice. Some books call the equation list Huntington's postulates. Some syllabi write + and · for ∨ and ∧, but ∨ and ∧ keep the lattice connection clear.

a∨(b∧c) = (a∨b)∧(a∨c), a∧(b∨c) = (a∧b)∨(a∧c), a∨a′ = 1, a∧a′ = 0, 0 ≠ 1

a, b, c are elements of B; ∨ is join; ∧ is meet; ′ is complement; 0 and 1 are the bounds.

Laws derived from the axioms

From the compact list many familiar laws follow. Idempotent: a∨a=a and a∧a=a. Proof: a∨a=(a∨a)∧1=(a∨a)∧(a∨a′)=a∨(a∧a′)=a∨0=a. Bounded laws: a∨0=a, a∧0=0, a∨1=1, a∧1=a. Absorption: a∨(a∧b)=a and a∧(a∨b)=a. Involution: (a′)′=a. De Morgan: (a∨b)′=a′∧b′ and (a∧b)′=a′∨b′. These are theorems, not extra assumptions. A quick way to prove De Morgan is to show that a′∧b′ is the complement of a∨b: (a∨b)∨(a′∧b′)=1 and (a∨b)∧(a′∧b′)=0, then use uniqueness of complement. Complement is unique: if x and y both satisfy a∨x=1, a∧x=0, a∨y=1, a∧y=0, then x=x∧1=x∧(a∨y)=(x∧a)∨(x∧y)=0∨(x∧y)=x∧y, and similarly y=x∧y, so x=y. Duality: any true identity remains true when ∨ and ∧ are swapped and 0 and 1 are swapped. Example: a∨(a∧b)=a duals to a∧(a∨b)=a.

Examples and duality in action

The smallest Boolean algebra is B2={0,1}. Join is max, meet is min, and complement is 1−a: 0′=1, 1′=0. The power set P(S) of any set S is a Boolean algebra: join is union, meet is intersection, complement is S−A, zero is ∅ and one is S. For S={1,2,3}, take A={1,2} and C={2,3}. Then A∨C={1,2,3}, A∧C={2}, A′={3}, C′={1}. De Morgan holds: (A∨C)′=∅ and A′∧C′={3}∩{1}=∅. Also A∨A′={1,2,3} and A∧A′=∅, so A′ is the complement of A. The order in P(S) is subset: A≤C means A⊆C. The same algebra appears in logic: ∨ is OR, ∧ is AND, ′ is NOT, 0 is false, 1 is true. Duality says swapping ∨ with ∧ and 0 with 1 turns a valid law into another valid law. Complement uniqueness is why the notation a′ is safe. Boolean polynomials and normal forms use these laws in Chapter 5.6.

Question. Let U={1,2,3} and B=P(U). Let A={1,2} and C={2,3}. In the Boolean algebra (B,∪,∩,′,∅,U), compute A∨C, A∧C, A′, C′. Then verify De Morgan's law (A∨C)′=A′∧C′ and the complement law A∨A′=U. Give the sizes of the six sets A∨C, A∧C, A′, C′, (A∨C)′ and A′∧C′.

Exam traps

  • Mixing the distributive laws. The correct law is a∨(b∧c)=(a∨b)∧(a∨c). Writing (a∨b)∧c leaves c outside the first bracket.
  • Forgetting 0≠1. A one-element set {0} with 0=1 fails the definition.
  • Using the wrong De Morgan law. (a∨b)′=a′∧b′, not a′∨b′.
  • Assuming a complemented bounded lattice is automatically Boolean. Distributivity is also required; the diamond lattice is complemented but not distributive.
  • Believing complements can be many. In a Boolean algebra each a has exactly one complement a′.

5.6 Boolean functions and Boolean polynomials

Every Boolean function takes n bits to one bit, and every such function can be written as a Boolean polynomial: a formula built only from variables, AND, OR and NOT. The two standard forms are canonical DNF, an OR of minterms, and canonical CNF, an AND of maxterms. These forms turn a truth table into an equation. Circuit designers use them to build adders and comparators, and examiners use them to test whether you can move between a table and a formula. Only a few universities treat Boolean functions as a separate chapter.

You need: normal forms from Unit 1 and Boolean algebra laws from Chapter 5.5.

Functions, literals, minterms and maxterms

A Boolean function of n variables is a map f:{0,1}^n→{0,1}. It takes an n-bit input and returns one bit. For n=2 the inputs are 00, 01, 10, 11. A Boolean polynomial is an expression in variables using OR (∨), AND (∧) and NOT (′). Example: x′y+xy′. The expression is not unique; the canonical forms give one standard choice.

A literal is a variable or its complement. A minterm is an AND of n literals, one for each variable, either xi or xi′. For x,y the minterms are x′y′, x′y, xy′, xy. A maxterm is an OR of n literals, one for each variable. For x,y the maxterms are x+y, x+y′, x′+y, x′+y′.

f = ∨_{i:f(a_i)=1} m_i = ∧_{i:f(a_i)=0} M_i

f is the Boolean function; a_i is the i-th input bit string in the table; m_i is the minterm true only at a_i; M_i is the maxterm false only at a_i.

Canonical DNF and canonical CNF

Canonical DNF is the OR of the minterms for which f=1. Canonical CNF is the AND of the maxterms for which f=0. Every Boolean function has both forms. For n variables there are 2^n minterms and 2^n maxterms. Some syllabi call DNF sum of products and CNF product of sums.

  1. List all 2^n input rows and compute f for each row.
  2. For each row with f=1, write its minterm.
  3. OR those minterms: this is the canonical DNF.
  4. For each row with f=0, write its maxterm.
  5. AND those maxterms: this is the canonical CNF.

A minterm is 1 only on its own row. A maxterm is 0 only on its own row. So the OR of selected minterms copies the 1 rows, and the AND of selected maxterms copies the 0 rows. Do not mix: maxterms never go in a DNF, and a minterm must contain every variable.

Worked example

Question. For f(x,y)=x′y+xy′, write the canonical DNF and canonical CNF, and evaluate both at inputs 10 and 11.

  1. Rows: 00→0, 01→1, 10→1, 11→0.
  2. f=1 at 01 and 10. Minterms: m1=x′y, m2=xy′.
  3. Canonical DNF: f=x′y+xy′.
  4. f=0 at 00 and 11. Maxterms: M0=x+y, M3=x′+y′.
  5. Canonical CNF: f=(x+y)(x′+y′).
  6. At 10: DNF=0·0+1·1=1; CNF=(1+0)(0+1)=1.
  7. At 11: DNF=0·1+1·0=0; CNF=(1+1)(0+0)=0.

Answer. The canonical DNF is x′y+xy′, the canonical CNF is (x+y)(x′+y′); at input 10 both give 1, and at input 11 both give 0.

Exam traps

  • Using maxterms in a DNF or minterms in a CNF. DNF uses minterms where f=1; CNF uses maxterms where f=0.
  • Leaving a variable out of a minterm or maxterm. Canonical forms use every variable exactly once in every term.
  • Reading a 0 bit as a complemented literal in a maxterm. In a maxterm, bit 0 gives the uncomplemented variable and bit 1 gives the complemented variable.
  • Confusing canonical DNF with a simplified DNF. xy′+x′y is canonical for XOR; adding x′y′ is wrong because it makes f=1 at 00.

5.7 Sub-Boolean algebras and Boolean homomorphism

A sub-Boolean algebra is a smaller Boolean algebra sitting inside a given one. It must contain 0 and 1 and stay closed under join, meet and complement. A Boolean homomorphism is a map between Boolean algebras that preserves those three operations and both constants. These ideas exist to compare algebras and to recognise when a subset or an image behaves exactly like the whole. They appear in digital logic, where a small set of gates may generate a full switching algebra, and in database query systems, where filters form Boolean subalgebras. Only a few universities teach this as a separate topic.

You need: Chapter 5.3 for lattice homomorphism and Chapter 5.5 for Boolean laws.

Sub-Boolean algebra

Let B be a Boolean algebra. A subset S⊆B is a sub-Boolean algebra when 0∈S, 1∈S, and for all a,b∈S, a∨b∈S, a∧b∈S and a′∈S. Some syllabi call S a Boolean subalgebra. The five conditions matter together. A sublattice needs only join and meet closure, so it can miss 0, 1 or complements. To test a candidate S: check 0 and 1; check every join of two elements; check every meet; check every complement. Stop when one check fails. Inside P({1,2,3}), where U={1,2,3}, S={∅,{1},{2,3},U} passes. For example {1}∨{2,3}=U and {1}∧{2,3}=∅. The set T={∅,{1},{2},{1,2,3}} fails because {1}∨{2}={1,2} is missing.

S is a sub-Boolean algebra ⇔ 0,1∈S and S is closed under ∨, ∧, ′

S⊆B; 0 and 1 are the constants of B; ′ is complement in B.

Boolean homomorphism

A Boolean homomorphism h:B→B₂ is a map that preserves all Boolean operations and constants. If h is bijective, it is a Boolean isomorphism. The image h(B) is always a sub-Boolean algebra of B₂. A lattice homomorphism may preserve ∨ and ∧ yet fail complement or constants, so it is not automatically a Boolean homomorphism. Test h(A)=1 when 1∈A, else 0, from P(U) to {0,1}. For join: h(A∪C)=1 iff 1∈A∪C iff 1∈A or 1∈C iff h(A)∨h(C)=1. Meet and complement follow similarly. h(∅)=0 and h(U)=1, so h is a Boolean homomorphism.

h(a∨b)=h(a)∨h(b), h(a∧b)=h(a)∧h(b), h(a′)=h(a)′, h(0)=0, h(1)=1

h:B→B₂; a,b∈B; operations on the right are in B₂.

Worked example

Question. Let U={1,2,3}, B=P(U), S={∅,{1},{2,3},U}. (a) Show S is a sub-Boolean algebra of B. (b) For h:B→{0,1}, h(A)=1 if 1∈A else 0, use A={1,2} and C={2,3} to verify the join, meet and complement laws and the constants.

  3. Meets: {1}∧{2,3}=∅; ∅∧X=∅; X∧X=X. All are in S.
  4. Complements: ∅′=U, U′=∅, {1}′={2,3}, {2,3}′={1}. All are in S. So S is a sub-Boolean algebra.
  5. h(A)=h({1,2})=1 and h(C)=h({2,3})=0.
  6. Join: A∨C=U, h(U)=1; h(A)∨h(C)=1∨0=1.
  7. Meet: A∧C={2}, h({2})=0; h(A)∧h(C)=1∧0=0.
  8. Complement: A′={3}, h(A′)=0; h(A)′=1′=0.
  9. Constants: h(∅)=0 and h(U)=1.
  10. These values verify the five identities for the chosen A and C; the general argument in Section 2 proves h is a Boolean homomorphism.

Exam traps

  • Checking only join and meet closure. A sub-Boolean algebra also needs complement closure and both 0 and 1.
  • Forgetting that a Boolean homomorphism must send 0 to 0 and 1 to 1; a lattice homomorphism need not.
  • Confusing a sub-Boolean algebra with a sublattice: every sub-Boolean algebra is a sublattice, but not conversely.
  • Assuming h(B) must equal B₂. A homomorphism's image is only a sub-Boolean algebra of the codomain.

Takeaways

  1. A lattice is one idea seen twice: a poset with pairwise joins and meets, or a set with two well-behaved operations, and the two views give the same objects.
  2. The order and the operations translate into each other by the rule a ≤ b ⇔ a ∧ b = a ⇔ a ∨ b = b, so you can prove facts in whichever language is easier.
  3. Idempotent, commutative, associative and absorption laws hold in every lattice and are all you need to do algebra with ∨ and ∧.
  4. A sublattice must be closed under join and meet computed in the big lattice; closure under one operation alone is not enough.
  5. Products and homomorphisms build new lattices from old ones and preserve exactly the structure the operations carry.
  6. Adding boundedness, complements and distributivity one by one leads to the Boolean lattice, where every element has a well-defined complement.
  7. Boolean algebra is a compact axiom system: from a handful of identities you can derive De Morgan's laws, the 0–1 rules and the uniqueness of complement.
  8. Every Boolean function becomes a Boolean polynomial, and the truth table hands you the DNF (from the 1-rows) and the CNF (from the 0-rows) directly.
  9. Sub-Boolean algebras and Boolean homomorphisms are the structural tools: they let you compare algebras and carry identities from one to another.

Exam question bank

Long answers in this unit are mostly proofs from the axioms plus one worked example: state the definitions, verify closure or a law line by line, then confirm with a small Hasse diagram or truth table.

Remember and understand

Remember Define a partial order and a poset. Define a lattice as a poset, and state the join and meet axioms of a lattice as an algebraic system. 10 marks

Reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive conditions with symbols; LUB and GLB definition for a pair a, b; Closure, idempotent, commutative, associative, absorption laws; One example (divisors of 12 under divisibility) and one non-example (the two-element poset with no join).

Understand Explain why a lattice can be described both as a poset and as an algebraic system. Show how the partial order is recovered from the operations and how the operations are recovered from the order. 12 marks

Define a ≤ b by a ∧ b = a and prove this is a partial order; Show LUB of a, b is a ∨ b and GLB is a ∧ b; Derive idempotent and absorption laws from the order definition; State the equivalence theorem and note it lets you swap viewpoints in proofs.

Understand State the idempotent, commutative, associative and absorption laws for lattices, and prove the absorption law a ∨ (a ∧ b) = a from the poset definition. 10 marks

All four laws with symbols and variable meanings; Use a ∧ b ≤ a, so a is an upper bound of a and a ∧ b; a is the least upper bound because a ∨ (a ∧ b) ≤ a; Combine with a ≤ a ∨ (a ∧ b) and antisymmetry.

Remember Define sublattice, bounded lattice, complemented lattice and distributive lattice. Give one example of each and one lattice that is bounded but not complemented. 10 marks

Closure of a nonempty subset under both operations; Bounds 0 and 1 with 0 ≤ a ≤ 1; Complement condition a ∨ b = 1 and a ∧ b = 0; Distributive identities, plus the chain 0 < a < 1 as a bounded, non-complemented example.

Understand Define a Boolean algebra as an algebraic system and list its laws. Explain why every Boolean algebra is a bounded distributive complemented lattice. 12 marks

Carrier B, operations ∨, ∧, ′, constants 0 and 1; Idempotent, commutative, associative, absorption, distributive laws; Complement laws a ∨ a′ = 1, a ∧ a′ = 0; Show the derived order a ≤ b ⇔ a ∧ b = a makes B a lattice, then a Boolean lattice.

Apply

Apply For the poset of divisors of 30 under divisibility, find the join and meet of every pair, identify all sublattices of size 4, and decide whether the lattice is bounded, complemented and distributive. 13 marks

Join is lcm, meet is gcd; Hasse diagram with 1 at the bottom and 30 at the top; Sublattice test by closure under lcm and gcd; Complement of d is 30/d, so complemented; check distributivity by the gcd/lcm identity.

Apply Take L₁ as the chain 0 < 1 and L₂ as the four-element Boolean lattice. Construct the direct product L₁ × L₂, draw its Hasse diagram, and define a homomorphism from it onto L₁, verifying the homomorphism conditions. 13 marks

Elements are ordered pairs, operations coordinatewise; Eight elements with order (a,b) ≤ (a′,b′) iff a ≤ a′ and b ≤ b′; Map to first coordinate; check join, meet are preserved; Note the map is onto but not one-one, so not an isomorphism.

Apply A Boolean function of three variables x, y, z is 1 exactly on the rows 011, 101, 110, 111. Write its DNF using minterms and its CNF using maxterms, and simplify the DNF. 12 marks

Minterms x′yz, xy′z, xyz′, xyz; CNF from the four 0-rows using maxterms; Group terms to get xy + x′yz + xy′z and simplify to xy + yz + xz style result; Check simplification against the original truth table.

Apply Using the laws of Boolean algebra, simplify the polynomial (a ∧ b′) ∨ (a ∧ b) ∨ (a′ ∧ b) and prove each step names a law. 10 marks

Distributive law to factor a out of the first two terms; Complement law a ∧ (b ∨ b′) = a ∧ 1 = a; Absorption or distribution to reach a ∨ b; Justify every identity used by name.

Apply Let B be the power set of {1, 2, 3} under union, intersection and complement. Decide whether {∅, {1}, {1,2,3}} and {∅, {1}, {2}, {1,2,3}} are sub-Boolean algebras, and whether the map sending each set to its size is a Boolean homomorphism. 12 marks

Closure under union and intersection first; Complement of {1} must lie in the subset; Size map fails: sizes add under union, and 0 and 1 are not preserved in the required sense; Conclusion: first is not closed, second is a sub-Boolean algebra.

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Prove that a lattice is distributive if and only if every element has at most one complement in a bounded lattice setting, and use this to show that the diamond lattice M₅ is not distributive. 16 marks

Assume distributive; suppose b and c both complement a; Compute b = b ∧ 1 = b ∧ (a ∨ c) and distribute; Use a ∧ b = 0 and a ∨ b = 1 to reduce to c; In M₅ the middle element has two complements, so no distributivity; Careful statement of the lattice-theoretic criterion used.

Analyze Prove De Morgan's laws (a ∨ b)′ = a′ ∧ b′ and (a ∧ b)′ = a′ ∨ b′ from the Boolean algebra axioms, and explain why the proof needs distributivity and uniqueness of complement. 14 marks

Show (a ∨ b) ∨ (a′ ∧ b′) = 1 by distribution; Show (a ∨ b) ∧ (a′ ∧ b′) = 0; Invoke uniqueness of complement in a distributive complemented lattice; Dual argument for the second law.

Evaluate Show that every Boolean function of n variables has a DNF representation, that the minterm expansion is unique up to the order of terms, and explain why each minterm is 1 on exactly one assignment. 14 marks

Each minterm is a product of n literals, one per variable; Evaluate a minterm on an assignment: it is 1 only if each literal matches; Sum over the 1-rows reproduces the function value on every row; Uniqueness: two different sets of minterms differ on some row.

Analyze Let h: B₁ → B₂ be a Boolean homomorphism. Prove that h(0) = 0, h(1) = 1, and that the image h(B₁) is a sub-Boolean algebra of B₂. Give an example of a lattice homomorphism that is not a Boolean homomorphism. 16 marks

Preservation of join, meet, complement and both constants; Image contains 0 and 1, closed under the three operations; Counterexample: a monotone map on a bounded lattice that preserves join and meet but not complement; Distinguish lattice homomorphism from Boolean homomorphism.

Glossary

Boolean algebra
A bounded, complemented, distributive lattice, or the equivalent axiom system (B, ∨, ∧, ′, 0, 1).
Boolean function
A map from {0,1}ⁿ to {0,1}, usually given by a truth table.
Boolean polynomial
An expression built from variables and the constants 0, 1 using ∨, ∧ and ′.
Bounded lattice
A lattice possessing a least element 0 and a greatest element 1.
CNF
Conjunctive normal form: an AND of OR terms, canonical when all terms are maxterms.
Comparable elements
Elements a, b with a ≤ b or b ≤ a; otherwise they are incomparable.
Complement
An element b with a ∨ b = 1 and a ∧ b = 0 for a given a.
Complemented lattice
A bounded lattice in which every element has at least one complement.
Direct product
The lattice of ordered pairs from two lattices, with operations applied coordinate by coordinate.
Distributive lattice
A lattice satisfying a ∧ (b ∨ c) = (a ∧ b) ∨ (a ∧ c) and its dual.
DNF
Disjunctive normal form: an OR of AND terms, canonical when all terms are minterms.
Greatest lower bound
The largest of all lower bounds of a pair; written a ∧ b.
Hasse diagram
A drawing of a finite poset with elements as dots, covering pairs joined upward, and no arrows or loops.
Join
Another name for the least upper bound operation ∨.
Lattice
A poset in which every pair of elements has a join and a meet, or equivalently a set with two idempotent, commutative, associative, absorbing operations.
Lattice homomorphism
A map preserving join and meet for all pairs of elements.
Lattice isomorphism
A bijective lattice homomorphism; the two lattices then have identical structure.
Least upper bound
The smallest of all upper bounds of a pair; written a ∨ b.
Maxterm
A sum of all n variables, each plain or complemented, false on exactly one assignment.
Meet
Another name for the greatest lower bound operation ∧.
Minterm
A product of all n variables, each plain or complemented, true on exactly one assignment.
Partial order
A reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive relation on a set.
Poset
A set together with a partial order on it.
Sub-Boolean algebra
A subset containing 0 and 1 and closed under ∨, ∧ and ′.
Sublattice
A nonempty subset closed under the join and meet of the whole lattice.
Upper bound
An element u with a ≤ u and b ≤ u for the pair under study.
