1.1 Security goals: confidentiality, integrity, availability (CIA)

Every security requirement you meet this year is one of three promises about data and service. Keep the data secret — confidentiality. Keep the data correct — integrity. Keep the service reachable — availability. A UPI payment shows all three at once: the amount must stay private, must not change in transit, and the bank's server must answer before the app times out. Engineers call these the CIA triad. Almost every exam question about a security incident is really asking which of the three broke.

Three promises about one message

Confidentiality means only allowed parties can read the data. A UPI payment leaves your phone carrying the payee address, the amount and an encrypted PIN block. On a shop's open Wi-Fi, a sniffer can read every field the app fails to encrypt. Confidentiality is the promise that it cannot.

Integrity means the data is exactly what the sender wrote. A mark sheet row says 18 out of 20 and someone edits it to 20. Nothing crashes, no error is shown, the record simply says something else. Integrity fails when a change happens and nobody detects it.

Availability means the service answers when a real user needs it. Tatkal opens at 10:00 and lakhs of people refresh the IRCTC page at that second; the site must still take bookings. Availability fails when the service is slow, down or unreachable.

A control usually protects one goal and costs another. The next chapter separates what you promise (a service) from the tool that delivers it (a mechanism).

The extra goals and the DAD set

Some syllabi list three more goals beside CIA.

  • Authentication: each party proves who it is before the exchange. Your app verifies the bank's certificate; you prove yourself with a PIN.
  • Accountability: every action is traceable to a named entity. The bank log shows which employee opened which account, and when.
  • Non-repudiation: a party cannot later deny an action it really did. A digitally signed payment instruction cannot be waved away as 'I never sent that'.

The mirror image of CIA is the failure set some syllabi call DAD: disclosure breaks confidentiality, alteration breaks integrity, destruction (or denial) breaks availability. Describe an attack by the goal it breaks and the marking becomes easy.

Controls trade off, and trust must be stated

Controls compete for the same resources. Encrypting every field of a payment request protects confidentiality, but CPU cost per request rises, queues grow, and at peak the service slows — availability drops. Locking an account after three wrong PINs stops guessing, but an attacker who knows your username can lock you out on purpose. Security is a balance, not a maximum.

The balance is written down as a trust and threat model. It answers four questions.

  1. Who is trusted: users, admins, the cloud provider, the certificate authority.
  2. What each trusted party can do: read a row, sign a certificate, restart a server.
  3. What you assume they will not do: steal data, collude, or make careless mistakes.
  4. What happens when an assumption is false: a lost admin laptop counts as a disclosure event.

Availability is quoted as a percentage over a stated period, never as the sentence 'the server is up'.

A = (T − D) ÷ T × 100

A is availability in percent; T is the total minutes in the stated period (days × 24 × 60); D is the minutes the service was unusable.

Worked example

Question. A college result portal must run continuously for 30 days. (a) It was unusable for 108 minutes. Compute its availability. (b) A student edits his own marks row directly in the database. (c) A mark sheet PDF is readable by anyone holding the link, with no login. (d) Only exam-cell staff can publish results, and each publish is digitally signed. Name the goal broken or provided in (b), (c) and (d).

  1. (a) Total minutes in the stated period: T = 30 days × 24 hours × 60 minutes = 43200 minutes.
  2. Usable minutes: T − D = 43200 − 108 = 43092 minutes.
  3. Availability: A = 43092 ÷ 43200 = 0.9975.
  4. In percent: A = 0.9975 × 100 = 99.75%.
  5. (b) The record changed and nobody detected it: integrity is broken.
  6. (c) Data is readable by people who were never authorised: confidentiality is broken.
  7. (d) Staff identity is proved, and a signature stops later denial: authentication and non-repudiation are provided.

Exam traps

  • Writing 'security means confidentiality'. Marks come from naming the goal a stated incident broke: a crash is availability, a silent edit is integrity, a leak is confidentiality.
  • Quoting availability as 'the server is up'. It is a percentage over a declared period, so always write the period and the down minutes.
  • Assuming encryption gives integrity. It hides data from outsiders; a party who holds the key can still change it undetected. Detecting change needs a MAC or a signature (see Unit 4).
  • Treating authentication and non-repudiation as one idea. A shared password authenticates but can be denied later; a digital signature cannot.
  • Mismatching the DAD set. Disclosure pairs with confidentiality, alteration with integrity, destruction with availability — never cross them.

1.2 Security attacks, services and mechanisms

A security course needs three words kept apart. An attack is an action that breaks something. A service is a promise the system makes to its users, such as "nobody else can read this". A mechanism is the tool that keeps that promise, such as encryption. The service and mechanism lists come from X.800, an ITU-T standard. The four attack classes come from the OSI attack taxonomy used in the standard security texts. Exam questions ask you to move between the three columns.

You need 1.1, because each service here protects one of the three goals defined there.

Passive and active attacks

Watch one message travel from sender to receiver and ask what an attacker can do. There are four answers.

  • Interruption: the message or the service is destroyed or blocked. Goal: availability.
  • Interception: an unauthorised party reads the message. Goal: confidentiality.
  • Modification: the message is changed before it arrives. Goal: integrity.
  • Fabrication: a new message is inserted and made to look like it came from a real sender. Goals: integrity and authentication.

These four are the attack classes. Every named attack is one of them. A man-in-the-middle attack is interception plus modification. A flood that drowns a server is interruption.

A second cut is passive and active. A passive attack only observes. Two forms: release of message contents, and traffic analysis, which reads who talks to whom, when, and how many bytes, even when the bytes are scrambled. Nothing changes, so no checksum fails and detection is almost impossible. Defence is prevention: encryption, traffic padding, careful routing.

An active attack changes the data stream or the service: interruption, modification, fabrication, masquerade, replay. Something now looks wrong, so the aim is detection plus recovery. Only interception is purely passive; the other three classes are active.

Where does replay sit? The copying is passive interception; the re-insertion is active. Its class is contested. Most texts call it modification of the message stream, because an old message is put in place of the fresh one expected. Some list it as fabrication, because a copy is inserted. Either answer is accepted; say which you mean.

The six services

A security service is a guarantee the system gives its users. The standard list has six items, from X.800. A service says what is promised, never how it is done.

  • Authentication: peer entity authentication proves who the other side is at the start of a connection. Data origin authentication proves which party created one message, with or without a connection.
  • Access control: only the listed users, under the listed conditions, may use a resource.
  • Data confidentiality: content is unreadable to others. Four forms: connection, connectionless, selective field, and traffic flow (the pattern and volume are hidden too).
  • Data integrity: the data arrives exactly as sent, with no change, insertion, deletion or replay.
  • Non-repudiation: a party cannot later deny a message. Two forms: proof of origin, and proof of delivery.
  • Availability: the service answers when asked.

Two pairs are easy to confuse. Integrity is not confidentiality: a readable message can still be protected against tampering, and an unreadable one can still be tampered with. Non-repudiation is not authentication: authentication convinces the receiver at the time, while non-repudiation convinces a third party, such as a court, months later.

The eight mechanisms

A mechanism is the tool or procedure that delivers a service. The standard list has eight. Some syllabi call mechanisms security controls and split them into preventive, detective and corrective.

  • Encipherment: turning plaintext into ciphertext. Delivers confidentiality, and supports integrity and authentication when a key is involved.
  • Digital signature: data plus a private-key transformation that anyone can check. Delivers integrity, origin authentication and non-repudiation.
  • Access control mechanisms: passwords, access lists, capabilities, roles.
  • Data integrity mechanisms: a check value or sequence number attached to the data, so any change is visible.
  • Authentication exchange: a message protocol, such as challenge and response, that tests a claim of identity.
  • Traffic padding: extra bits or extra messages, so the real length and timing are hidden.
  • Routing control: choosing trusted links and relays, or changing route when one is suspect.
  • Notarization: a trusted third party records facts about a message, such as time, origin and delivery.

The mapping is many-to-many. Non-repudiation needs a signature plus a timestamp from a notary. One signature gives integrity, origin authentication and non-repudiation in one action.

Worked example

Question. An employee downloads the salary file from the office server. For each event give the attack class (if any), the service attacked, and one mechanism that would have helped. Compute the change in rupees and the outage in minutes and seconds. (a) A switch mirror copies the packets, but the file body is unreadable. (b) The amount field changes in transit from ₹45,000 to ₹95,000. (c) The file server stops answering for 3 hours. (d) After the transfer the sender denies sending the file.

  1. (a) The attacker only copies; nothing is altered. Attack class = interception, passive. The contents stayed safe because encipherment was already applied. What is still exposed is the pattern of who sent what to whom and when; traffic padding and careful routing reduce that.
  2. (b) The value inside the message changed. Attack class = modification, active. Service attacked = data integrity. Mechanism = a MAC or a digital signature over the amount field.
  3. (c) The service stopped. Attack class = interruption, active. Service attacked = availability. Mechanism = redundancy, plus rate limiting at the edge. No cipher helps here.
  4. (d) The message was genuine, but the sender now refuses to admit it. This is not one of the four message classes; it is the X.800 threat of repudiation. The receiver cannot later prove the sender sent the file, so the missing service is non-repudiation, met by a digital signature with a notarised timestamp. If a class must be named, note only that a false denial by a party is treated as fabrication by the receiving side.
  5. Arithmetic for the amount: 95,000 − 45,000 = ₹50,000 change.
  6. Arithmetic for the outage: 3 hours × 60 = 180 minutes; 180 × 60 = 10,800 seconds.

Answer. The amount changed by ₹50,000 and the outage lasted 180 minutes (10,800 seconds). The events map to interception (encipherment already protected the contents), modification (MAC or signature), interruption (redundancy), and repudiation (non-repudiation service, digital signature with notarised timestamp).

Exam traps

  • Mixing the three columns. Writing "encryption" when a service is asked for, or "confidentiality" when a mechanism is asked for, loses marks. Read what the question wants: what breaks, what is promised, or how it is done.
  • Calling a replay passive. The copying is passive interception, but the re-insertion is active. Say "modification of the message stream, sometimes listed as fabrication".
  • Treating authentication and non-repudiation as one service. Authentication convinces the receiver at the time; non-repudiation must convince a third party later.
  • Assuming encryption covers all of confidentiality. Traffic analysis still works on an encrypted stream, so traffic-flow confidentiality needs padding and routing control as well.
  • Writing "encryption" as the mechanism against a DoS attack. Availability is met by redundancy, rate limiting and routing control, not by secrecy.

1.3 Threats, vulnerabilities and attack vectors

A threat is only a possibility. It becomes a loss when it meets a weakness in something you own — an unpatched server, a reused password. This chapter gives each word its own job: asset, threat, threat agent, vulnerability, exposure, risk, attack vector, attack surface. It then shows how organisations keep these words in catalogs, leaning on the public lists CVE, CWE and CVSS, and how one number — risk, likelihood times impact — turns fear into a budget line. Every security purchase, from a patch project to a firewall, is argued with this arithmetic.

You need: 1.1 for the goals each risk is measured against, and 1.2 for the word 'attack'.

Asset, threat, vulnerability, risk

Four words carry most of the marks here. An asset is anything worth protecting: a server, a customer database, a login identity, the trust in a UPI app. A threat is a possible action that can damage an asset. The threat agent is who or what carries it out — a ransomware crew, a careless employee, a flood.

A vulnerability is the weakness that lets the threat succeed: an unpatched library, a blank admin password, an open S3 bucket. Without a vulnerability a threat stays imaginary. Without a threat a vulnerability stays harmless. Put them together and you get a threat–vulnerability pair — one sentence of the form 'this actor can use this weakness'. Exposure is how much sits open to that pair: 100 servers, one database, 4,000 accounts.

Risk is the expected size of the loss if the pair plays out. It is not the same as any of the three words above. A threat asks who acts, a vulnerability asks what is weak, and risk asks how much will this cost me per year.

Attack vector and attack surface

An attack vector is the path the attacker uses to reach the weakness. The same weakness often has several vectors: an internet-facing SSH port is a remote vector, a USB stick dropped in the lobby is a physical vector, a phishing mail that steals the password is a human vector (see 1.6).

The attack surface is everything an attacker can reach at all: open ports, exposed APIs, employee mailboxes, third-party libraries, cloud storage buckets. Vector is a route; surface is the whole boundary of what is touchable.

Shrinking the surface is the cheapest defence: close unused ports, delete stale accounts, drop the library nobody imports. You cannot list vectors until you know the surface, which is why an asset inventory comes before everything else. Network-level vectors such as port scanning are in 1.4.

Catalogs: the lists an organisation keeps

Two living lists sit behind every risk register. The threat catalog records actors and their methods: ransomware crews, insider fraud, scripted scanning bots. The vulnerability catalog records known weaknesses in your own systems, each with an owner, the date found, the vector and the fix status. Both are updated on every scan, audit and incident.

  • CVE — Common Vulnerabilities and Exposures. One ID per specific flaw, written CVE-YYYY-NNNN. It names a flaw; it does not score it.
  • CWE — Common Weakness Enumeration. Groups flaws into classes such as buffer overflow or SQL injection, so you can fix a whole family at once.
  • CVSS — Common Vulnerability Scoring System. A severity score from 0 to 10, where 10 is the worst.

The working form of the catalog is the threat–vulnerability pair table: one row per sentence 'this actor can use this weakness', with columns for asset, threat agent, weakness, vector, current control and risk. Some syllabi call this compiling observations from organisational risk documents into a fixed table format, one column per field.

Risk as arithmetic

Risk exists so that a security argument can be checked. Two formulas cover the exam.

Risk = L × I

L is likelihood, the probability the pair succeeds within a stated period, as a fraction from 0 to 1. I is impact, the money lost if it does, in rupees. Risk is then in rupees per that period.

SLE = AV × EF, ALE = SLE × ARO

AV is the asset value in rupees. EF is the exposure factor, the fraction of the asset destroyed per incident (0 to 1). SLE is the single loss expectancy, rupees per incident. ARO is the annualised rate of occurrence, incidents per year. ALE is the annualised loss expectancy, rupees per year.

A control is a change that lowers L, lowers I, or removes the vulnerability outright. Buy it when the drop in risk is bigger than its cost. That single comparison decides most security budgets.

Worked example

Question. A firm runs 400 internal web servers. A scan finds that 25% of them use an unpatched library with a known remote-code-execution bug. The security team estimates the chance of a successful exploit in a year at 0.20. One successful exploit costs ₹50,00,000 in downtime, forensics and fines. (a) How many servers are exposed? (b) What is the annual risk in rupees? (c) A patch project costs ₹3,00,000 and cuts the yearly chance to 0.04. What is the new risk? (d) What is the net yearly saving?

  1. Exposed servers = fraction vulnerable × total servers = 0.25 × 400 = 100 servers.
  2. Annual risk = likelihood × impact = 0.20 × ₹50,00,000 = ₹10,00,000 per year.
  3. After the patch, likelihood becomes 0.04, so new risk = 0.04 × ₹50,00,000 = ₹2,00,000 per year.
  4. Risk removed = ₹10,00,000 − ₹2,00,000 = ₹8,00,000 per year.
  5. Net saving = risk removed − control cost = ₹8,00,000 − ₹3,00,000 = ₹5,00,000 per year.
  6. The saving is positive, so the patch project is worth funding on risk grounds alone.

Answer. 100 servers are exposed, the annual risk is ₹10,00,000, the patch cuts it to ₹2,00,000, and the net yearly saving is ₹5,00,000.

Exam traps

  • Calling a vulnerability a threat, or a threat a risk. A threat is who or what can act; a vulnerability is the weakness that lets it work; risk is the expected damage, in rupees or in a severity score.
  • Treating CVE, CWE and CVSS as one thing. CVE identifies a single flaw, CWE names the class of weakness, CVSS gives a 0 to 10 severity. A CVE entry does not carry a score by itself.
  • Mixing up attack vector and attack surface. The vector is the route used for one attack; the surface is everything reachable, whether or not anyone is attacking today.
  • Writing likelihood as 20 instead of 0.20 in Risk = L × I, which inflates the answer by a factor of 100. L is a fraction; the impact carries the rupees.
  • Forgetting to subtract the control cost. A control that lowers risk is not automatically worth buying; only the saving net of its price counts.

1.4 Network attacks: DoS, DDoS, MITM, replay, port scanning

Network attacks usually drown a service, sit in the middle of a conversation, or capture and resend a message. This chapter names each family, shows the mechanism, and gives the fix. It also covers port scanning and the LAN tricks behind a man-in-the-middle position.

You need 1.2 for passive and active attacks, and 1.3 for attack vector and attack surface.

Drowning a service: DoS and DDoS

A denial-of-service (DoS) attack makes a service unusable for legitimate users, attacking availability, the A in CIA (see 1.1). One machine can do this against a small target. A distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack uses thousands of hosts, usually a botnet (see 1.7). Scale changes the defence.

  • Volumetric: fills the network link, measured in bits per second (Mbps, Gbps). The server sits idle while the pipe is full.
  • Protocol-level: exhausts a state table in the server or firewall. A SYN flood is the standard case.
  • Application-layer: valid-looking requests burn server CPU. Low bandwidth, high damage; an HTTP flood asking for an expensive search page is the usual example.

A SYN flood attacks the TCP handshake. The server reserves a half-open table slot on seeing a SYN, before it knows the client is real.

  1. Attacker sends a TCP SYN with a spoofed source address.
  2. Server reserves a half-open slot and replies SYN-ACK.
  3. The final ACK never comes; the address is not the attacker's.
  4. The slot stays until a timer expires. A few thousand SYNs per second fill a table of a few thousand entries, and real clients are refused.

The fix is the SYN cookie: the server allocates nothing, packs connection state into the SYN-ACK sequence number, and creates a table entry only when a valid ACK returns. Reflection and amplification turn a small attacker into a big flood. In reflection the attacker spoofs the victim's address, so replies go to the victim. In amplification the reply is larger than the request. Open DNS resolvers and NTP servers are classic reflectors.

A = R ÷ Q

A is the amplification factor (a pure number); R is the reply size in bytes; Q is the request size in bytes. Load reaching the victim = A × the attacker's sending rate.

Sitting in the middle: MITM

In a man-in-the-middle (MITM) attack the attacker stands between client and server and relays traffic, so both sides believe they talk directly. It breaks confidentiality and integrity and lets the attacker edit messages. The attacker must first get into the path.

  • ARP cache poisoning on a local network.
  • DNS spoofing: a forged answer reaches the client before the real resolver's.
  • A rogue Wi-Fi access point using a trusted network name (SSID).
  • BGP hijack: a network announces an IP prefix it does not own.

ARP poisoning is the LAN version. ARP has no authentication: any host may claim any IP address. The attacker broadcasts 'the gateway 192.168.1.1 is at my MAC address'. Client and gateway update their ARP caches and send frames to the attacker, who forwards them unchanged. Every later packet, including clear-text passwords, passes through the attacker.

Three more LAN tricks build the same position. MAC flooding fills the switch CAM table with fake entries; a full table makes the switch flood frames out of every port. DHCP starvation exhausts the DHCP pool, so a rogue server can answer and name the attacker as default gateway. VLAN hopping moves the attacker into a VLAN that should be out of reach.

Reusing a captured message: replay

A replay attack records a valid message and sends it again later. Encryption does not stop it: the recorded message is valid ciphertext and the server decrypts it happily. One captured 'transfer ₹5,000 to account X' replayed ten times moves ₹50,000. The defence is freshness: the receiver must tell a new message from an old one.

  • Nonce: a random value used once. The server sends a fresh challenge; the reply must contain it. A replayed reply carries the old nonce and is rejected.
  • Timestamp: accept only if the message time is within a window, say ±2 minutes. Needs loosely synchronised clocks.
  • Sequence number: accept only if the number is greater than the last seen in this session. Cheap, but the receiver must store that value.

The figure shows the sequence-number version. The attacker forwards seq = 1000, then resends the same seq = 1000. A server that checks freshness rejects the duplicate; one that only decrypts accepts it again.

Port scanning: reading the replies

A port scan probes TCP or UDP ports to learn which services are listening. It is the reconnaissance step before an attack and the first thing an administrator runs against their own firewall. The replies matter, not the number of ports tried. A port looks open, closed or filtered.

For FIN and NULL scans an open port and a filtered port both stay silent, so the result is reported as open|filtered, exactly as with UDP.

A TCP connect scan finishes the handshake, so the service logs the connection; it needs no special privilege. A SYN scan sends SYN, reads SYN-ACK or RST, then sends RST before the connection completes; it needs raw sockets. FIN and NULL scans send unusual flag combinations; a closed port answers RST while an open port ignores them, which slips past simple stateless filters. UDP scans rely on ICMP port-unreachable for closed ports. nmap is the common tool, and its state names (open, closed, filtered, open|filtered) are the ones above. Scanning a host you do not own, or have no written permission to test, is illegal in most places, including under India's IT Act.

Worked example

Question. A botnet of 50,000 hosts sends 200 HTTP requests per second each at one web server. (a) What request rate reaches the server? (b) The server serves 20,000 requests per second; how many times over capacity is that? (c) Each request has a different spoofed source address. Why does per-IP rate limiting fail, and what is the correct defence? (d) A scan shows 22, 80, 443 open and 3306 filtered. Explain.

  1. (a) Offered load = 50,000 × 200 = 10,000,000 requests per second.
  2. (b) Overload ratio = 10,000,000 ÷ 20,000 = 500.
  3. (c) Different spoofed addresses defeat per-IP rate limiting. Local filtering saves CPU but not bandwidth, because load arrives on the target's own link. The defence must be upstream: scrubbing centres or a CDN, anycast to spread traffic, and SYN cookies so half-open state is not allocated.
  4. (d) 22, 80 and 443 open means SSH, HTTP and HTTPS answered. 3306 is MySQL; filtered means no reply, so a firewall silently dropped the probes. The host is up, so silence is policy, not a crash.

Answer. The botnet offers 10,000,000 requests per second, 500 times capacity; spoofed addresses defeat per-IP filtering, so the fix is upstream scrubbing plus SYN cookies, and the filtered port 3306 is dropped by a firewall rule.

Exam traps

  • Calling DDoS 'many DoS'. One address cannot be blocked, so you need upstream filtering or anycast, not a bigger blocklist.
  • Believing encryption stops replay. A captured ciphertext is still valid; only freshness (nonce, timestamp, sequence number) stops the resend.
  • Mixing up reflection and amplification. Reflection is who receives the reply; amplification is the reply being larger.
  • Confusing closed with filtered in a scan. Closed means RST came back, so the host is up and no service listens. Filtered means nothing came back.
  • Treating a SYN flood as bandwidth. It exhausts the half-open table using little bandwidth; SYN cookies are the fix, not a bigger link.

1.5 Malware: viruses, worms and trojans

Malware is short for malicious software: any program written to damage a system, steal from it, or take control of it. Every family name — virus, worm, trojan — answers one question: what does this code need in order to run and reach the next machine? A virus needs a host file and usually a user who opens it. A worm needs only a network. A trojan needs a user's trust, because the user installs it on purpose. The family name drives real decisions: what a signature can catch, what one patch can stop, and what only user habit can prevent.

You need: Read 1.3 for threat, vulnerability and exposure; malware usually enters through an unpatched service.

Classify by what the code needs

Malware spreads by one of three routes, and the route gives the family its name.

  • Needs a host file plus a user action → virus.
  • Needs only a network connection → worm.
  • Needs a user who trusts it → trojan.

The payload is the harmful job the code does: delete files, encrypt them, open a remote shell. The vector is how it arrives: an email attachment, a pirated installer, a USB stick, an unpatched network service an attacker scans (see 1.4).

The family name says nothing about the damage. A quiet trojan that opens a backdoor can cost a bank far more than a noisy virus that garbles a few files.

Virus: a host file and a user's hand

A virus is code attached to another program or file, called the host. It runs when the host runs. It cannot travel alone: something must carry the infected file to a new machine, and a user or the operating system must open it. Common subtypes:

  • Boot sector virus: infects the first sector of a disk and runs before the operating system loads.
  • Macro virus: written in a document's macro language, so it travels inside a word-processor or spreadsheet file and runs when the document opens.
  • Polymorphic virus: rewrites its own bytes on every copy, often encrypting the body with a fresh key, so a fixed byte signature no longer matches.
  • Stealth virus: hides its change by intercepting the operating system's read of the file and returning the clean original.

A virus needs both a host to live in and a trigger to start. That is why attachments, shared drives and pirated software remain lasting vectors: each one asks a person to click.

Worm: the network is the host

A worm is standalone code. It copies itself across a network through a service on the target machine, with no host file and no user action. It finds victims by scanning address ranges and trying the same exploit on each.

The Morris worm of 1988 was the first to get wide public attention. No user opened anything: it used a bug in the mail program, a bug in another network service, and a list of common weak passwords. Every success produced a new machine running the same code.

This changes defence. A worm offers one door to close: patch the service it exploits and the outbreak stops, even on machines whose users do nothing. A virus of the same speed still waits for people to open a file, so patching the network changes nothing.

Trojan, payloads and the lifecycle

A trojan is a program the user installs on purpose because it looks useful — a video codec, a game crack, a free system cleaner — with something else inside. It does not copy itself; the attacker or the user moves it. The payload is whatever the author chose:

  • Backdoor: keeps a hidden login or remote shell open for the attacker (see 1.7).
  • Rootkit: hides files, processes and open ports from the operating system's own tools.
  • Logic bomb: stays silent until a programmed condition is met — a date, a missing file, a login name.
  • Spyware: records keystrokes, screens and browsing.
  • Ransomware: encrypts files and demands payment, often by UPI or cryptocurrency.

Most malware, whatever the family, moves through the same five stages:

  1. Delivery: the code reaches the machine (attachment, download, network service).
  2. Execution: the code starts running.
  3. Persistence: it arranges to run again after a reboot — a registry run key on Windows, a cron entry or systemd unit on Linux.
  4. Payload: the harmful job happens.
  5. Propagation: it spreads to further machines; a trojan may skip this step entirely.

The program flaw a worm exploits — a buffer overflow, a missing input check — is separate from the malware that uses it. Fixing such flaws is the subject of Unit 6.

Worked example

Question. A worm doubles the number of infected machines every 10 minutes. It starts with 1 infected machine at 09:00. (a) How many machines are infected at 09:10, 09:20 and 09:30? (b) How many at 10:00? (c) Between which two doubling steps does the count pass 1,00,000 machines, and at what clock times? (d) Why does patching one bug stop this outbreak, while a virus with the same doubling time would not be stopped by one patch?

  1. Each 10-minute interval multiplies the count by 2, so after k intervals N = 2^k, starting from 1 machine.
  2. 09:10 is k = 1, giving 2 machines; 09:20 gives 2² = 4; 09:30 gives 2³ = 8.
  3. 09:00 to 10:00 is 60 minutes, so k = 60 ÷ 10 = 6 and N = 2⁶ = 64 machines.
  4. Step 16: 2¹⁶ = 65,536 machines at 09:00 + 160 min = 11:40. Step 17: 2¹⁷ = 1,31,072 at 11:50. Since 65,536 < 1,00,000 < 1,31,072, the mark is crossed between 11:40 and 11:50.
  5. (d) A worm needs no user action, so every reachable unpatched machine is a victim and one patch closes the only door. A virus with the same speed would still need 64 users to open the infected file by 10:00, so the network patch alone would not stop it.

Answer. 2, 4 and 8 machines at the first three steps; 64 machines at 10:00; the 1,00,000 mark is passed between step 16 at 11:40 (65,536 machines) and step 17 at 11:50 (1,31,072 machines).

Worked example 2

Question. Same worm, but the number of infected machines triples every 10 minutes, starting from 1 machine at 09:00. How many machines at 09:30, and after how many intervals does the count first exceed 1,00,000?

  1. After k intervals N = 3^k, from 1 machine.
  2. 09:30 is k = 3, so N = 3³ = 27 machines.
  3. 3¹⁰ = 59,049 and 3¹¹ = 1,77,147. Since 59,049 < 1,00,000 < 1,77,147, the count first exceeds 1,00,000 at k = 11 intervals, that is at 09:00 + 110 min = 10:50.

Answer. 27 machines at 09:30; the count first exceeds 1,00,000 after 11 intervals, at 10:50, with 1,77,147 machines.

Exam traps

  • Calling every piece of bad software a virus. If it spreads alone over the network it is a worm; if it hides inside a program the user wanted, it is a trojan.
  • Saying a worm infects files. A worm is standalone code; it may drop files, but it does not need a host file to spread.
  • Assuming a logic bomb runs on a timer. It runs on any programmed condition — a date, a file deletion, a login name.
  • Forgetting that a trojan can have zero propagation. A trojan that only opens a backdoor never spreads by itself.

1.6 Social engineering and phishing

Social engineering is an attack on the person, not on the machine. The attacker does not break the cipher or the firewall. He calls the help desk, copies a bank's logo, and asks for one small favour until the password arrives. Phishing is the mass version of this, sent by mail, SMS or voice call. It exists because the cheapest way into a system is to be invited in by someone who has a valid login. It is used against bank and UPI customers, job seekers, students waiting for a scholarship, and staff of any organisation that has a mail server.

You need: 1.3 for threat, vulnerability and attack vector, because phishing is an attack vector that never touches the network.

The levers an attacker pulls

Social engineering is any attack that makes a person, not a machine, give up a secret or break a rule. The attacker never solves the cipher. He becomes a trusted voice on the phone. Four levers do most of the work: authority (I am calling from the bank's security team), urgency (your account closes in 30 minutes), fear (a fraud has been detected in your name), and helpfulness or greed (claim your refund). Every good script pulls at least two of them at once.

  • Pretexting: the attacker invents a story and an identity, such as an IT engineer with a ticket number, an auditor, or a new joiner who lost the VPN password.
  • Baiting: something attractive is left where the victim will find it, such as a USB stick labelled Salary_2024 or a free download that asks for a login.
  • Tailgating: the attacker walks through a door behind a badge-holding employee, hands full, and never shows a badge.
  • Quid pro quo: a service is offered in return for cooperation, such as free IT support that needs the user to install a remote-access tool.

Only baiting needs technology, and that part is ordinary malware (1.5). The rest need a phone and confidence. That is why they survive every firewall upgrade.

Phishing and its variants

Phishing is social engineering delivered as a message: mail, SMS or phone call. It is one-to-many, cheap, and measured in fractions of a rupee per target. The variants differ only in aim and channel.

Read the anatomy of a typical mail. The display name says ICICI Bank Alert, but the address ends in mail-secure.top. The domain is a lookalike: icici-bank-verify.com instead of icicibank.com. There is a deadline of a few hours. The button leads to a page with the real logo that asks for the net-banking password and then for the OTP. The OTP request is the giveaway: no bank asks for an OTP that it has just sent you.

Lookalike domains are built by typosquatting. The letters rn replace m (rnicrosoft), the digit 0 replaces the letter o, an extra hyphen is inserted, or .co replaces .com. Some use characters from another script, such as the Cyrillic a, which looks identical to the Latin a. Read a domain from the right: the word just before .com is the real owner, and everything to its left is chosen by the sender.

Three layers of defence

No single control stops phishing, so stack three.

  • People: short repeated training, a one-click report button in the mail client, and no blame when someone reports that they clicked. A reported mail protects everyone else in the company.
  • Mail: SPF lists the servers allowed to send for a domain. DKIM signs the message so tampering in transit is visible. DMARC tells the receiver what to do when SPF and DKIM both fail: none, quarantine or reject.
  • Technology: multi-factor authentication, a password manager that will not autofill on a lookalike domain, and link inspection by hovering before clicking or by opening the site from a saved bookmark.

E = N × p_click × p_cred × L

E is the expected loss in rupees; N is the number of mails sent; p_click is the fraction of receivers who click the link; p_cred is the fraction of clickers who type a password; L is the average loss per taken account, in rupees.

Red-flag checklist. Any one item means stop and verify by a second channel, such as calling the number on the back of your card.

  • The greeting is generic, or your name is spelt wrongly.
  • The domain differs from the official one by a character, a hyphen or the suffix.
  • A deadline of hours, or a threat that the account will be closed or frozen.
  • The link opens a login page, or the mail asks for an OTP, PIN or CVV.
  • Bank details for a payment have changed, or the sender asks for secrecy.
  • You are asked to install something or to switch off a security setting.

Worked example

Question. A phishing campaign sends 10,000 mails. 5% of receivers click the link, 10% of those who click type their password, and the average loss per taken account is ₹40,000. (a) Find the number of clicks. (b) Find the number of credentials captured. (c) Find the expected loss. (d) Training halves the click rate and multi-factor authentication blocks every password-only login. Find the loss from taken accounts after both changes.

  1. Clicks = p_click × N = 0.05 × 10,000 = 500 clicks.
  2. Credentials captured = p_cred × clicks = 0.10 × 500 = 50 accounts.
  3. Expected loss = 50 × ₹40,000 = ₹20,00,000.
  4. New click rate = 0.05 ÷ 2 = 0.025, so clicks = 0.025 × 10,000 = 250 clicks.
  5. Credentials captured = 0.10 × 250 = 25 passwords.
  6. Multi-factor authentication rejects all 25 password-only logins, so taken accounts = 0.
  7. New loss = 0 × ₹40,000 = ₹0. Only the training cost remains.

Answer. Expected loss falls from ₹20,00,000 to ₹0 once training halves the clicks and multi-factor authentication stops password-only logins.

  • Mixing up pharming and phishing. Pharming needs no click; the address bar shows the correct name and the redirection happens at DNS or in the hosts file.
  • Mixing up smishing and vishing. Smishing is SMS, vishing is a voice call.
  • Claiming multi-factor authentication ends phishing. It stops password-only reuse, but a real-time proxy page or a forwarded OTP can still get through, so it is a backstop and not a cure.
  • Believing SPF, DKIM and DMARC prove a mail is honest. They only authenticate the sending domain; a freshly registered lookalike domain can pass all three.

1.7 Bots, botnets and remote access attacks

A bot is a computer that someone else controls over the network. A botnet is a group of such computers, run by one botmaster through a command-and-control channel. Botnets exist because one infected laptop is weak, but twenty thousand are a business: they can drown a website, send spam, try stolen passwords or mine crypto. Remote access attacks are how the attacker gets that first control and keeps it. You meet botnets in DDoS-for-hire pages, in credential-stuffing runs against banks and UPI apps, and in cheap CCTV cameras that join a flood without their owner knowing.

You need: 1.4 for DDoS and port scanning, and 1.5 for how the infection arrives.

Bot, botnet, botmaster and the C2 channel

A bot is a host running a small agent that an attacker controls over the network. The owner may keep using it and may never notice. A botnet is the set of all bots under one controller. The controller is the botmaster, also called the herder. The botmaster does not log in to each machine by hand. Each bot contacts a command-and-control (C2) server for orders. The C2 channel is what makes a botnet: malware with no way to receive orders is not a botnet.

  • Centralised C2: one server or a small set, often over IRC or HTTP/HTTPS. Simple to run, easy to block if found.
  • Peer-to-peer C2: bots talk to other bots. No single server to seize, harder to take down.
  • Fast flux: the C2 domain resolves to many changing IP addresses, so one blocklist entry does not stop it.
  • Domain generation algorithm (DGA): the bot computes many candidate domain names per day; the botmaster registers only the one that will be used.

A bot does not hold a connection open all day. It beacons: it sends a short check-in after every beacon interval, for example every 60 s. Jitter is a random extra delay, say 20%, added to that interval. Jitter stops the check-ins from looking like a metronome in network traffic.

B = 3600 ÷ T

B is the number of beacons per bot per hour; T is the beacon interval in seconds.

Lifecycle: spread, rally, command, monetise

A botnet is built and used in four stages. The stages overlap; a large botnet is always recruiting.

  1. Spread: the attacker infects hosts through phishing, an exploit, or a weak remote access service. Chapter 1.5 covers the malware side.
  2. Rally: each new bot beacons to the C2 server and registers. The botmaster now has an inventory of usable machines.
  3. Command: the botmaster sends a task to all bots or to a selected group. The task may run for minutes or months.
  4. Monetise: the botnet is rented or used directly. Common products are DDoS-for-hire, spam, credential stuffing, click fraud and crypto mining.

A single bot can be sold many times. One machine may send spam at noon, join a DDoS flood at night and mine coins in between. That is why the number of bots matters less than the number of tasks the botmaster can run.

Worked example

Question. A credential-stuffing botnet has 20,000 bots. Each bot tries 50 logins per hour. The success rate is 0.1%. (a) How many login attempts per hour? (b) How many successful logins per hour? (c) If each bot beacons every 60 s, how many beacons per hour reach the C2 server? (d) What does blocking the C2 domain change?

  1. Bot count N = 20,000. Login attempts per bot = 50 per hour.
  2. (a) Total attempts per hour = N × 50 = 20,000 × 50 = 10,00,000 attempts per hour.
  3. Success rate p = 0.1% = 0.001. Successful logins per hour = 0.001 × 10,00,000 = 1000 successful logins per hour.
  4. Beacon interval T = 60 s. Checks per bot per hour = 3600 ÷ 60 = 60.
  5. (c) Total beacons per hour = 20,000 × 60 = 12,00,000 beacons per hour.
  6. (d) Blocking the C2 domain stops new commands. It does not remove the bot agent. Rate limiting at the login page plus MFA reduces successful logins to 0.

Answer. The botnet makes 10,00,000 attempts per hour, 1000 successful logins per hour and 12,00,000 beacons per hour; blocking the C2 domain stops new commands but does not clean the bots, so rate limiting plus MFA is needed to take successes to 0.

Exam traps

  • Bot vs virus: a virus is code that copies itself into files; a bot is a host already under remote control.
  • Botnet without C2: examiners expect you to name the channel. Malware that never receives orders is not a botnet.
  • Botnet equals DDoS: DDoS is one product. Spam, credential stuffing, click fraud and mining are also botnet work.
  • Blocking C2 cleans the host: it does not. The agent stays and may switch to a new domain or server.
  • RAT vs remote admin tool: both give remote control; the difference is consent and purpose, not the protocol.

Check yourself

Remember Define bot and botnet. 2 marks

A bot is a host controlled remotely by an attacker's agent. A botnet is the set of bots controlled by one botmaster through a C2 channel.

Remember What is a C2 channel? 2 marks

The channel a bot uses to receive orders from the botmaster, such as IRC, HTTP/HTTPS or peer-to-peer links.

Understand Why does a botnet add jitter to its beacon interval? 3 marks

Fixed-period beacons are easy to spot as a repeating pattern. Jitter adds a random delay so check-ins blend into normal traffic.

Understand Compare centralised and peer-to-peer C2. 3 marks

Centralised C2 has one or a few servers; it is simple but a single point of failure. Peer-to-peer C2 has bots talk to each other; it is harder to seize but harder to coordinate.

Apply A botnet has 5,000 bots, each trying 40 logins per hour, with 0.2% success. Find attempts per hour and successful logins per hour. 4 marks

Attempts = 5,000 × 40 = 2,00,000 per hour. Successes = 0.002 × 2,00,000 = 400 per hour.

Apply 4,000 bots beacon every 90 s. How many beacons per hour reach the C2 server? 4 marks

Checks per bot per hour = 3600 ÷ 90 = 40. Total = 4,000 × 40 = 1,60,000 beacons per hour.

Analyze A takedown seizes the C2 server and blocks its domain. Why is the botnet not gone? 5 marks

The bots are still infected. They may use a DGA or fast flux to find a new C2, and the agent remains until each host is cleaned.

Analyze Why is an RDP port open to the internet a botnet recruitment risk? 5 marks

Attackers scan for port 3389 and try weak or reused passwords. One success gives a desktop that can join a botnet without any malware file being opened.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Real incident response uses DNS sinkholes: map known C2 domains to a safe server and count which internal hosts still ask for them. Threat-intelligence feeds share botnet indicators, and law-enforcement takedowns seize C2 domains or arrest operators. IoT botnets such as Mirai showed that default passwords on cameras and routers can build a flood army in hours. Search for 'botnet takedown', 'DGA detection' and 'RDP exposure' if you want the operations side.

Takeaways

  1. Every security requirement you will meet is one of three promises: keep it secret, keep it correct, keep it reachable.
  2. Attacks are what the enemy does, services are what you promise the user, and mechanisms are the tools that deliver the promise — keep the three words apart in an exam.
  3. A threat alone causes no loss; loss happens only where a threat meets a vulnerability, and risk puts a number on that meeting.
  4. Most network attacks reduce to three shapes: drown the service, sit in the middle of the conversation, or replay a captured message.
  5. Malware is best classified by what it needs to spread — a host file, a live network, or a user's trust.
  6. Social engineering cannot be patched, so defence is a trained habit backed by a technical control that stops the mistake becoming a breach.
  7. A botnet is a rented army; the command-and-control channel with regular beacons is both its strength and the point where defenders can detect it.
  8. CIA is not a checklist to tick once — each goal pulls against the others, and a design is a balance chosen for a stated threat model.

Understand Distinguish between a security attack, a security service and a security mechanism. Give one example of each and show how they link. 8 marks

Attack = action that compromises security; service = guarantee offered to users; mechanism = tool or procedure that implements the service; Example attack: interception; example service: data confidentiality; example mechanism: encipherment; Clear statement that a mechanism counters an attack in order to deliver a service; One line on why confusing the terms leads to bad design.

Understand Classify security attacks into passive and active categories with sub-types, and place interception, modification, fabrication and replay in the correct class. 8 marks

Passive: eavesdropping/interception, traffic analysis — goal is information, no change to data; Active: masquerade, replay, modification of messages, denial of service, fabrication; Correct placement of each named attack; Note that passive attacks are hard to detect, active attacks are easier to detect but harder to prevent.

Understand Explain how a virus, a worm and a trojan differ in what they need to spread and what damage they do. 8 marks

Virus: attaches to a host file or boot sector, needs a user or program to run it, infects other files; Worm: standalone, spreads over the network by itself, no host file needed; Trojan: appears useful, relies on user trust, does not self-replicate; One example of payload for each and a note on propagation versus payload as separate ideas.

Apply Design an anti-phishing programme for a bank's customers and staff that pairs a user habit with a technical backstop. Explain why each part is needed. 13 marks

Analyze and evaluate

Analyze Compare the CIA triad with the DAD triad. During a live DDoS on a power utility's billing site, which goal fails first and which goal should the response team protect at the cost of the others? Justify. 13 marks

Correct expansion of DAD: disclosure, alteration, destruction; Mapping of each DAD item to the CIA goal it breaks; Analysis showing availability fails first in a DDoS, while integrity and confidentiality remain intact; Argument for prioritising availability of the control system over billing, with a stated reason; Note that deliberately taking a service offline to preserve integrity is a valid trade-off.

Evaluate A hospital asks you to rank its cyber risk. Using the relationship risk depends on threat, vulnerability and impact, evaluate three scenarios and justify a spending order. 13 marks

Explicit use of risk = likelihood × impact; Three concrete scenarios (ransomware on patient records, unpatched imaging device, phishing of staff); Likelihood from threat exposure and known vulnerabilities, impact from patient safety and legal duty; Justified ranking with a lower-cost control for the highest risk first; Note that a low-likelihood, high-impact case still needs a contingency plan.

Evaluate A vendor claims its signature-based antivirus alone is enough. Evaluate this claim against a polymorphic virus and ransomware, and propose a layered defence. 13 marks

Explains that a polymorphic virus changes its bytes, so signatures miss it; Ransomware often arrives through a user action and encrypts quickly, so detection time matters; Layer arguments: behaviour and heuristic detection, patching, least privilege, backup and restore testing, network segmentation; Evaluation of the vendor claim as necessary but not sufficient; Mentions the false-positive cost of aggressive detection.

Identifies bots and a command-and-control channel with periodic beacons; Explains jitter as random delay added to beacon timing to avoid fixed-interval detection; Detection approaches: periodic-beacon analysis, DNS or flow records, threat intelligence matches, traffic size patterns; Response: block at the perimeter, isolate hosts, find the initial infection vector, preserve evidence; Warning that blocking one C2 address may push the botnet to a backup channel.

Evaluate A manager says: 'We have a firewall and antivirus, so we are secure.' Evaluate this statement using the ideas of attack surface, threat, vulnerability and mechanism, and state what a sound security posture would add. 13 marks

Firewall and antivirus are mechanisms covering only part of the attack surface; Names uncovered surfaces: people (social engineering), remote access and botnet recruitment, unpatched software, wireless; Separation of threat, vulnerability, exposure and risk with a short example; Adds: patch management, MFA, least privilege, logging and monitoring, backups, incident response, user training; Concludes that security is a process with a stated threat model, not a product.

Glossary

Accountability
Being able to tie every action to an identified subject, so misuse can be traced.
Asset
Anything of value to the organisation — data, a service, a device, or its reputation.
Attack vector
The path an attacker uses to reach a target, such as email attachment, open port or USB drive.
Authentication
Proving that a subject is who it claims to be, before any access is granted.
Backdoor
A hidden way into a system that bypasses normal authentication, left by an attacker or a developer.
Baiting
Leaving something tempting — a labelled USB stick, a free download — for the victim to pick up.
Beacon
A small periodic message a bot sends to its C2 server to ask for orders or report that it is alive.
Bot
A single infected machine that takes orders from a remote operator.
Botmaster
The person who controls a botnet and rents or uses it.
Command-and-control (C2)
The channel through which a botmaster sends instructions to bots and receives results.
Control
Any safeguard — technical, procedural or physical — that reduces risk.
CWE
Common Weakness Enumeration; a dictionary of software weakness types rather than individual bugs.
Encipherment
The mechanism of transforming data with a key so unauthorised readers get nothing useful.
Exposure
The state of being open to loss because a vulnerability exists and a threat can reach it.
Jitter
Random variation added to beacon timing so the traffic does not look like a fixed clock.
Logic bomb
Malicious code that stays dormant until a condition — a date, a login, a file change — is met.
Penetration testing
Authorised simulated attack on a system to find exploitable weaknesses before an adversary does.
Port scanning
Probing a range of ports on a host to learn which services are listening and therefore reachable.
Quid pro quo
Offering a small favour or service in exchange for information or access.
Social engineering
Getting people to reveal information or perform actions by manipulating trust rather than breaking technology.
Tailgating
Following an authorised person through a door or gate without presenting credentials.
Threat agent
The person, group or program that actually carries out a threat.
Threat catalog
A maintained list of known threats, used as a checklist when analysing a system.
Trojan
A program that looks useful but carries hidden harmful function; it does not self-replicate.
Virus
Malicious code that inserts itself into a host file or boot sector and runs when that host runs.
Worm
Self-contained malicious code that spreads across a network without a host file or user action.
