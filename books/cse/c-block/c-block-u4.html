4.1 Cryptocurrency fundamentals and history

Cryptocurrency is digital money whose ownership is written on a shared ledger and proved by cryptographic keys, so a payment needs no bank's approval. It exists because copying a file is free, and money must not be copyable. You meet it as bitcoin, as ether, and as tokens inside games and metaverse worlds. This chapter sets out the vocabulary, the double-spending problem it answers, and the timeline from 1980s e-cash to Bitcoin's scaling upgrades.

You need: Unit 1 for distributed ledgers, decentralisation and block structure, and Unit 2 for hash and key intuition.

Coins, tokens and where the money lives

A cryptocurrency is digital money in which ownership is recorded on a distributed ledger and a transfer is a digitally signed message. No central office approves each payment; the network checks the signature and the record.

Two words get mixed up. A coin is the native money of a blockchain: BTC on Bitcoin, ETH on Ethereum. A token is money issued on top of an existing chain by a small program, a smart contract (see Unit 5). A non-fungible token (NFT) is a token whose units are unique; metaverse worlds use NFTs for land and wearables (see Unit 6).

A distributed ledger is one record copied across many nodes that agree on its contents and order. Unit 1 teaches the general idea; here you need only the consequence: everybody can check the record, and nobody holds the only copy.

Ownership is proved with keys. A private key signs a spend; the matching public key and address let any node verify it (Unit 2). A wallet is software or hardware that stores keys and addresses. It does not store coins; the coins are ledger entries that the wallet spends by signing (see 4.6).

You usually get your first coins from an exchange, a business that matches buyers and sellers and holds rupee balances for you. If the exchange holds your keys, it holds your coins too.

The double-spending problem

A digital file copies perfectly and costs nothing to copy. Money cannot behave like that. If Alice owns one unit and sends the same file to Bob and to Carol, she has spent twice. This is double spending.

Physical cash avoids it because a note sits in one pocket only. A bank transfer avoids it because one trusted bookkeeper keeps your balance, checks it, and refuses the second payment.

Trust costs something. The issuer can refuse a payment, freeze an account, fail, or take days to settle across borders. An online merchant also cannot tell a copied coin from an original one, so purely digital money without an issuer looked impossible.

Bitcoin's answer needs three ingredients: a public record that everyone can read and verify; one agreed order of transactions, so the first spend of a coin wins and later ones are rejected; and a costly way to write the next page, so rewriting old pages is not worth the effort. The cost is a proof-of-work puzzle. See 4.5 for how confirmations make a double spend impractical.

From e-cash to Bitcoin, in safe steps

  1. 1980s, blind signatures: an issuer signs a digital coin without seeing it, so the coin cannot be linked back to the buyer. This gives untraceable e-cash, but a central issuer still stops double spending and can switch the system off.
  2. 1990s, hashcash and b-money: hashcash makes a sender solve a small puzzle before sending mail, so spam becomes expensive. b-money (1998) proposes money minted by such puzzles and recorded in a ledger every participant keeps; bit gold, described a few years later, adds timestamped, chained records. Neither settles who decides the order of payments.
  3. 2008: the Bitcoin whitepaper, published under the name Satoshi Nakamoto, describes peer-to-peer electronic cash using hash-linked blocks and proof of work.
  4. January 2009: the first block, the genesis block, is mined and the network starts.
  5. May 2010: a programmer pays 10,000 BTC for two pizzas. It is the first well-known purchase of a physical good with bitcoin.
  6. 2017 and 2021, scaling and privacy upgrades: SegWit (2017) packs more transactions into a block and fixes transaction malleability, where a third party could alter a signature's encoding and so change the transaction id; it also makes signature hashing linear in the number of signatures. Taproot (2021) makes complex spends look like simple ones. Both are soft forks (Unit 3).

Notice the pattern. Every piece already existed. Bitcoin's contribution was joining them so that no single party controls the record.

Exchanges and who regulates

A cryptocurrency exchange is a marketplace. You place an order to buy or sell at a price, it matches you with the opposite order, and it charges a fee. Prices come from this order book, not from a government. Custodial exchanges hold your coins for you, which is convenient and risky; non-custodial ones only connect you to the market, and you keep your keys.

Each party in the system wants something different from the rules.

  • Users want safety, clear tax rules and the ability to withdraw.
  • Exchanges want licences and banking access.
  • Miners and node operators want cheap power and no blocking.
  • Banks and payment firms want money-laundering risk controlled.
  • The government and tax authority want reporting and a share of gains.

Rules differ by country and change often. India taxes gains from virtual digital assets and requires exchanges to report large trades; some countries licence exchanges and a few ban them. Check the current rule instead of quoting a rate from an old textbook.

Worked example

Question. A phone wallet shows 0.05 BTC. One bitcoin trades at ₹40,00,000. (a) What is the wallet worth in rupees? (b) What does the wallet actually store?

  1. Price of one bitcoin: P = ₹40,00,000 per BTC.
  2. Holding: B = 0.05 BTC.
  3. Value V = B × P = 0.05 × 40,00,000 = ₹2,00,000.
  4. Check in the smallest unit: 1 BTC = 10,00,00,000 satoshi, so 0.05 BTC = 0.05 × 10,00,00,000 = 50,00,000 satoshi.
  5. Part (b): the wallet stores private keys and addresses. The balance is the sum of unspent outputs on the blockchain that those keys can spend.

Answer. The wallet is worth ₹2,00,000, and it stores keys and addresses, not coins.

Exam traps

  • Writing 'the wallet holds the bitcoins'. The wallet holds keys; the ledger holds unspent outputs, so lost keys mean lost coins (see 4.6).
  • Using Bitcoin and blockchain as synonyms. Blockchain is the shared hash-linked record; Bitcoin is one application of it, and Unit 1 covers the general structure.
  • Saying cryptocurrency is untraceable. Every payment is public; hiding who owns an address is a separate problem (see 4.8).
  • Mixing coin and token. A token lives on another chain's ledger and follows that chain's rules.
  • Claiming pre-Bitcoin e-cash solved double spending without trust. It did not; a central issuer always checked.

4.2 Bitcoin protocol: transactions and blocks

A Bitcoin transaction is not a balance update. It spends whole earlier outputs and creates new outputs, each locked to an address. A block collects many such transactions and seals them under one 80-byte header, so one proof of work protects the whole batch. This design lets strangers agree on who owns what without a bank. It is used for every on-chain payment, from a tea stall that accepts Bitcoin to a large exchange withdrawal. Some syllabi call this the Bitcoin protocol, or construction and working of Bitcoin.

You need: Unit 1 for block structure and Unit 2 for hash pointers and Merkle trees; Chapter 4.1 for the ledger idea.

Transactions spend outputs

Bitcoin does not store an account balance. It stores unspent transaction outputs, or UTXOs. A UTXO is a chunk of bitcoin with an owner condition and a value. A wallet's spendable balance is the sum of the UTXOs it can unlock. See Chapter 4.6.

A transaction has inputs and outputs. An input names an earlier UTXO by its transaction ID and output index, then supplies unlocking data. An output has a value in satoshi and a locking script. One bitcoin is 100,000,000 satoshi. A payment usually has one or more inputs and two outputs: one to the payee, one change output back to the payer.

  1. Pick UTXOs whose total value is enough for the payment and fee.
  2. Write each input: previous txid, output index, unlocking data.
  3. Write the payee output with its value and locking script.
  4. Write the change output to a fresh address owned by the payer.
  5. Sign the inputs.

Σ inputs = Σ outputs + fee

Σ inputs is the total value of spent UTXOs in satoshi; Σ outputs is the total value of new outputs in satoshi; fee is the miner's payment. Fee is not a field in the transaction.

The transaction ID (txid) is the double SHA-256 hash of the serialized transaction. It identifies the transaction, not the block. A fee is implied: if outputs total less than inputs, the difference is the fee.

Header commits to a batch

A block is a header plus a transaction list. The header is 80 bytes and has six fields: version (4 bytes), previous block hash (32 bytes), Merkle root (32 bytes), timestamp (4 bytes), bits (4 bytes), nonce (4 bytes). The previous block hash is the hash pointer that makes the chain. The Merkle root is a single hash that commits to every transaction ID in the block; Unit 2 shows how the tree is built. Timestamp is the miner's clock reading. Bits is a compact encoding of the target threshold. Nonce is a 32-bit counter miners vary.

The block hash is the double SHA-256 of the 80-byte header. A valid block has block hash ≤ target. The header does not contain transactions; it contains the Merkle root, so one small header stands for the whole batch.

The first transaction in every block is the coinbase transaction. It has one input with no earlier output, and its outputs create the block subsidy plus the fees of all included transactions. Only one coinbase transaction is allowed per block. Miners choose transactions from their mempool, the set of valid unconfirmed transactions each node keeps. A block is a batch because one proof of work and one header hash protect many payments at once. Change one transaction and the Merkle root changes, so the header hash changes. Mining economics is in Chapter 4.4.

SegWit and malleability

Before SegWit, the unlocking data for each input sits in a field called scriptSig inside the base transaction. The signature is part of that data. A third party can sometimes change scriptSig without breaking the signature, for example by changing how a push is encoded. The transaction stays valid but its txid changes. This is transaction malleability. It hurts chains of unconfirmed transactions that refer to an earlier txid.

SegWit, short for Segregated Witness, separates the witness from the base transaction. The witness holds signatures and other unlocking data. The base transaction keeps the inputs and outputs, and the txid is computed over the base transaction only. Old nodes can still process the base transaction, because they treat the witness program as anyone-can-spend and skip the signature check; only upgraded nodes verify the witness. The witness is committed separately, so changing a signature no longer changes the txid. SegWit reduces malleability and gives a cleaner transaction ID. The script details are in Chapter 4.3.

Worked example

Question. Alice owns one UTXO of 1.0 BTC. She pays Bob 0.3 BTC and wants a fee of 0.0001 BTC. Write the input and output values, compute the change output, and state the fee. Use satoshi where useful.

  1. 1. The wallet selects one UTXO worth 1.0 BTC = 100,000,000 satoshi. That is the only input.
  2. 2. The payee output to Bob is 0.3 BTC = 30,000,000 satoshi.
  3. 3. The stated fee is 0.0001 BTC = 10,000 satoshi.
  4. 4. Change = 1.0 − 0.3 − 0.0001 = 0.6999 BTC = 69,990,000 satoshi, sent to Alice's change address.
  5. 5. Total outputs = 0.3 + 0.6999 = 0.9999 BTC = 99,990,000 satoshi.
  6. 6. Implied fee = 1.0 − 0.9999 = 0.0001 BTC = 10,000 satoshi. It is not written as an output.

Answer. One input and two outputs; Alice's change output is 0.6999 BTC, and the miner can claim a 0.0001 BTC fee from the difference.

Exam traps

  • Treating the fee as an output. The fee is Σ inputs − Σ outputs. If you write the fee as an extra output, the outputs then equal the inputs, so the implied fee becomes zero and the miner gets no fee at all. The fee must stay as a gap between inputs and outputs.
  • Forgetting the change output. Then the whole leftover becomes a fee. In the worked example, the fee is 0.0001 BTC, not 0.7 BTC.
  • Mixing the coinbase transaction with a coinbase exchange or wallet. The coinbase transaction has no earlier output and creates new coins plus fees.
  • Saying the block header stores all transactions. It stores only the Merkle root.
  • Calling the block hash the transaction ID, or the transaction ID the block hash. They hash different data.

4.3 Bitcoin scripts

Every Bitcoin output carries a tiny program called a locking script. To spend that coin, the spender must supply data that makes the program finish with a single true value on the stack. The language is called Bitcoin script. It is deliberately small: no loops, no backward jumps, so every script stops after a fixed number of steps. This is how Bitcoin says "only the holder of this key may spend" without asking anyone's permission. Wallets hide the details; every full node runs the script on every transaction.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for inputs, outputs and outpoints, and Unit 2 for hash functions and ECDSA signature checks.

A stack machine with no loops

Each output of a transaction stores a locking script, called scriptPubKey: the condition for spending those coins. The input that spends the output carries an unlocking script, called scriptSig: the data that satisfies the condition. A node runs scriptSig first, keeps the stack it leaves, and then runs scriptPubKey on that stack, left to right. (Before 2010 the two were joined and run once; they were split to close a bug that let a crafted scriptSig skip the lock.)

The machine has one data structure, a stack: last in, first out. An instruction is called an opcode and takes one byte. Push opcodes place a constant on top of the stack; other opcodes pop items, compute, and push the result. Common ones: OP_DUP copies the top item, OP_HASH160 applies SHA-256 then RIPEMD-160, OP_EQUALVERIFY compares two items and aborts if they differ, OP_CHECKSIG verifies an ECDSA signature against a public key.

There are no loops, no backward jumps, no recursion. The number of steps is fixed when the script is written. So script is not Turing complete, and that is on purpose: every full node runs every script many times, so a script that could run forever would be a free denial-of-service tool. The cost of a script is known before it runs.

Success rule after the run: the stack must not be empty and the top item must be true (any non-zero value). Standard nodes also require exactly one item left on the stack. An abort, an empty stack, or a false top item makes the transaction invalid and the coins unspendable.

P2PKH: pay to a public key hash

The commonest pattern is Pay to Public Key Hash, P2PKH. Its locking script has five pieces, and the spender pushes two items in front of them.

OP_DUP OP_HASH160 <pubkey hash> OP_EQUALVERIFY OP_CHECKSIG

pubkey hash is 20 bytes, equal to RIPEMD-160(SHA-256(public key)); angle brackets mean "push this constant".

  1. scriptSig pushes sig, then pub. Stack from bottom: sig, pub.
  2. OP_DUP copies the top item. Stack: sig, pub, pub.
  3. OP_HASH160 replaces that copy with its 20-byte hash Hpk. Stack: sig, pub, Hpk.
  4. The constant in the locking script is pushed. Stack: sig, pub, Hpk, h.
  5. OP_EQUALVERIFY pops h and Hpk and aborts if they differ. Stack: sig, pub.
  6. OP_CHECKSIG pops pub and sig, verifies the signature over this transaction, and pushes TRUE. Stack: TRUE.

The address you give a friend is this 20-byte hash with a version byte and a checksum, encoded in Base58Check — see Chapter 4.6. Hashing keeps the address short and keeps the full public key hidden until the coin is spent.

P2SH, multisig and timelocks

Pay to Script Hash, P2SH, locks coins to a hash instead of a key. The locking script is OP_HASH160 <script hash> OP_EQUAL, where script hash is 20 bytes, equal to HASH160 of the redeem script. The spender's scriptSig pushes the full redeem script plus the data that script needs; the hash of the redeem script must equal the hash in the output, and then the redeem script itself runs. The sender never needs to know the spending condition, only its hash, and the condition stays off the chain until the coin is spent.

Multisig uses OP_M <pub1> <pub2> ... <pubN> OP_N OP_CHECKMULTISIG: m of the n keys must sign. A historical bug makes OP_CHECKMULTISIG pop one element too many, so the unlocking data starts with OP_0 as a dummy. Businesses use 2-of-3 for joint accounts, escrow and payment channels.

Timelocks put time into the condition. OP_CHECKLOCKTIMEVERIFY (CLTV) reads the transaction's nLockTime field and fails if it is below the pushed value, or if the input is marked final. It does not spend anything by itself; a CHECKSIG is still needed. OP_CHECKSEQUENCEVERIFY (CSV) does the same with time measured from when the output was confirmed. Together they express "A may spend after block 800000, B may spend now", which is the seed of a payment channel.

Witness scripts and standard scripts

SegWit moved the unlocking data out of scriptSig into a separate witness field. For P2WPKH the locking script is OP_0 <20-byte hash of the public key>, that is, a push of 0 followed by a 20-byte push. The scriptSig is empty, and the witness supplies sig and pub. The signature is no longer part of the data that the transaction id commits to, which removes transaction malleability; block weight rules are covered in Chapter 4.2.

A script is standard if the default relay policy accepts it: P2PKH, P2SH, P2WPKH, P2WSH, P2TR, bare multisig up to three keys, and OP_RETURN with at most 80 bytes of data. Scripts outside this list are still valid under consensus rules if a miner includes them; nodes simply do not forward them by default. Keep that gap in mind: a policy bug is not a chain split, a script-interpretation bug is.

Worked example

Question. A P2PKH output holds 0.25 BTC locked to the 20-byte hash h. The spender supplies sig and pub, and HASH160(pub) equals h. This output is the only input of the spending transaction, and the fee is 0.00012 BTC. Trace the stack after every opcode, state the peak stack depth, and compute the value the spender can pay to a new output.

  1. scriptSig pushes sig, then pub. Stack (bottom to top): sig, pub. Depth 2 items.
  2. OP_DUP copies pub. Stack: sig, pub, pub. Depth 3.
  3. OP_HASH160 replaces the top pub with Hpk = HASH160(pub). Stack: sig, pub, Hpk. Depth 3.
  4. The locking script pushes h. Stack: sig, pub, Hpk, h. Depth 4, the peak.
  5. OP_EQUALVERIFY pops h and Hpk. They are equal (this is the condition of the spend), so no abort. Stack: sig, pub. Depth 2.
  6. OP_CHECKSIG pops pub and sig, verifies sig over this transaction, and pushes TRUE. Stack: TRUE. Depth 1.
  7. Value check: input 0.25 BTC minus fee 0.00012 BTC gives 0.24988 BTC for the new output.

Answer. The run ends with the single item TRUE, the peak stack depth is 4 items, and the spender can move 0.24988 BTC.

Exam traps

  • scriptSig and scriptPubKey are not joined into one script. scriptSig runs first, then scriptPubKey runs on the stack it leaves; a valid run leaves a true top item, and an abort means the spend is rejected.
  • OP_EQUALVERIFY is OP_EQUAL plus OP_VERIFY: it deletes both operands. If the hash does not match, the script aborts and OP_CHECKSIG never runs.
  • The hash in a P2PKH script is HASH160 of the public key, not the address text. The address adds a version byte and a 4-byte checksum before Base58Check encoding.
  • Script has no loops and is not Turing complete, so answers that talk about infinite loops or running out of gas inside a Bitcoin script are wrong.
  • Non-standard does not mean invalid. Consensus rules decide validity; relay policy decides whether a node forwards the transaction.

4.4 Mining strategy and block rewards

A miner spends electricity to find a block and earns a block reward: subsidy plus fees. The subsidy halves every 210,000 blocks; total supply stops at 21,000,000 BTC. Miners rank transactions by fee per byte, join pools to smooth income, and can try tricks such as hiding blocks. This chapter does the revenue-minus-cost arithmetic that decides whether a rig switches on.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for blocks and the coinbase transaction, and Proof of Work from Unit 3.

The block reward: subsidy plus fees

A miner puts a coinbase transaction first in a valid block. It pays new coins plus all fees in that block.

block reward = subsidy + Σ fees

subsidy: new coins allowed, in BTC. Σ fees: all transaction fees in the block, in BTC. Both go to the miner's coinbase output.

The subsidy depends on block height, not date.

subsidy = 50 ÷ 2ⁿ,  n = floor(height ÷ 210,000)

height: block position, from 0 at genesis. n: number of halvings. floor drops the fraction.

Supply is capped. Each era mints 210,000 × subsidy, so total is 210,000 × (50 + 25 + 12.5 + …) = 210,000 × 100 ≈ 21,000,000 BTC, minus rounding losses.

Choosing transactions and weighing risk

A miner is paid only if the block survives on the longest chain. A stale block earns nothing. A surviving coinbase output cannot be spent for 100 blocks: coinbase maturity.

Block space is limited, so rank transactions by fee per byte, not total fee.

  1. Take mempool transactions.
  2. Divide each fee by size in bytes.
  3. Sort highest fee per byte first.
  4. Add until the block is full.
  5. Write total fee into the coinbase.

E = (S + F) × (h ÷ H) − C

E: expected profit per block attempt, in rupees. S: subsidy and F: fees, in BTC, converted to rupees. h: miner hash rate; H: network hash rate; h ÷ H: chance of finding the next block. C: electricity plus hardware cost over the average time to find one network block, in rupees.

A rig with 1% of network hash rate expects 1% of rewards. If electricity costs more, honest mining loses money and the rig switches off.

Pools, shares and selfish mining

Solo mining has high variance. A pool gathers hash power and pays when a member's hash falls below an easier target than the block target. A share proves work, not a block.

  • Pay-per-share (PPS): fixed pay per share; the pool carries variance and charges a fee.
  • Proportional: each block reward splits among shares in that round, so miner income varies with luck.

Selfish mining withholds a found block and mines a private chain on top, so honest miners waste work on a chain that will lose. The pool releases blocks when the public chain nearly catches up. The attacker earns more than its hash share above roughly one third of the network in the original analysis; later work shows the threshold can fall to about one quarter depending on how other miners choose between competing chains. In every case it wastes total network revenue.

4.5 Bitcoin network, payments and double spending

A Bitcoin payment is a signed transaction that travels from the customer's wallet to peers, to miners, and into a block. The network does not mark money as sent at once. The block that includes the payment is its first confirmation, and each block built on top adds one more. Confirmations make reversal costly, so a merchant can trade waiting time against risk. A payment travels through gossip, mempool and block propagation; race, Finney and 51% double spends show why zero-confirmation payments are risky.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for transactions and blocks, and Chapter 4.4 for miner incentives.

Nodes, gossip and the mempool

Three kinds of node matter. A full node stores the whole blockchain and checks every transaction and block against the rules: signatures, scripts and no double spend. A miner is a full node that also builds blocks and spends hash power; it chooses transactions by fee (Chapter 4.4). An SPV client stores only block headers and asks full nodes for a Merkle proof that a transaction is in a block; it trusts the longest chain but not every transaction. A wallet holds keys and signs; it may be full, SPV or a server-backed app (Chapter 4.6).

A new transaction is gossiped. The wallet sends it to a few peers. Each peer validates it, puts it in its mempool (the pool of unconfirmed transactions) and forwards it to its peers. The mempool is local, not one global queue. Miners pick from their own mempool, usually highest fee first. When a miner finds a block, it sends the block header and short transaction IDs; peers rebuild the full block from their mempools. This compact relay cuts bandwidth and delay. Delay matters: a transaction is only as safe as the number of blocks built after it.

Confirmations, orphans and the ten-minute target

The protocol targets a mean block interval of 600 s, or 10 minutes. Difficulty adjusts so that the expected time between blocks stays near this target. A transaction gets its first confirmation when a block includes it, and one more for every block built on top of that block. If a transaction is in block height h, then once blocks h+1, ... h+k−1 exist on the same chain, it has k confirmations. This is a mean, not a promise; a block can arrive in 1 minute or 40 minutes.

E[Tk] = k × 600 s

E[Tk] is the mean time to k confirmations, k is the confirmation count, and 600 s is the mean block interval (10 minutes).

Two miners can find blocks at the same height. Nodes follow the chain with most work. One block becomes stale (an orphan). A transaction in the stale block is usually put back in the mempool and mined again. A reorganisation replaces one or more blocks. Depth 1 is common; depth 6 is rare. Miners lose the stale block's subsidy and fees, so they try to propagate fast.

Double-spending attacks

A double spend is two conflicting transactions that spend the same output. Only one can enter the valid chain. Three attacks matter.

Race attack. The customer sends a payment to the merchant and a conflicting transaction to the network or a miner. If the merchant releases goods at zero confirmations, the other transaction may confirm first.

Finney attack. A miner puts a self-payment in a block but does not broadcast the block. The miner then buys goods with the same output from a zero-confirmation merchant. After getting the goods, the miner releases the block. The self-payment wins.

51% attacker. An attacker with more than half the hash rate builds a private chain faster than the public chain on average. The attacker can reverse its own transaction, double spend its own coins, or censor transactions. It cannot forge another owner's signature, so it cannot steal coins from a wallet.

Zero-confirmation payment is risky because the merchant has no block commitment. One confirmation removes the simple race and Finney cases for a non-miner, but a lucky attacker can still reverse one block. Six confirmations make a reorg very costly. Payment patterns such as multisig escrow, micropayment channels and timed lotteries sit above these base rules. Escrow can use multisig (Chapter 4.3), and small fast payments need channels (Chapter 4.9).

Worked example

Question. A merchant sells a phone for 0.01 BTC. BTC price is ₹50,00,000. The customer pays 0.0102 BTC, of which 0.0002 BTC is the miner fee. The merchant ships after 1 confirmation. (a) Find the rupee price, fee and total paid. (b) Find the mean wait for 1 and 6 confirmations at the 10-minute target. (c) Explain why 6 confirmations reduce double-spend risk.

  1. Item price in rupees = 0.01 BTC × ₹50,00,000 per BTC = ₹50,000.
  2. Fee in rupees = 0.0002 BTC × ₹50,00,000 per BTC = ₹1,000.
  3. Total paid in BTC = 0.01 BTC + 0.0002 BTC = 0.0102 BTC.
  4. Total paid in rupees = 0.0102 BTC × ₹50,00,000 per BTC = ₹51,000.
  5. Mean block interval = 600 s = 10 minutes.
  6. Mean wait for 1 confirmation = 1 × 10 minutes = 10 minutes.
  7. Mean wait for 6 confirmations = 6 × 10 minutes = 60 minutes.
  8. One confirmation can be reversed by a lucky attacker or a race/Finney attack at zero conf. Six blocks make a reorganisation much harder because the attacker must build a longer private chain against the honest network.

Answer. The item costs ₹50,000, the fee is ₹1,000, the customer pays 0.0102 BTC (₹51,000) in total; one confirmation takes about 10 minutes on average and six about 60 minutes, so six confirmations make a reversal much harder.

Exam traps

  • A 51% attacker cannot steal coins from a wallet; it can reverse or censor its own transactions because signatures remain unforgeable.
  • A confirmation is a block, not a minute; 10 minutes is a mean, so a payment can take longer or shorter.
  • Zero-confirmation is not safe because the transaction appears in a mempool; mempools are local and conflicting transactions are possible.
  • A stale block is not a double spend by itself; its valid transactions are usually re-mined in the next block.
  • The miner fee goes to the miner, not to the merchant; do not subtract it from the item price.

4.6 Bitcoin wallets

A Bitcoin wallet is a key manager, not a purse. The coins never leave the blockchain; they sit there as unspent transaction outputs (UTXOs) locked to addresses. The wallet holds the private keys that unlock those outputs, builds a transaction from them, signs it, and broadcasts it. Wallets exist because raw keys are unusable by hand: software must track your outputs, choose which ones to spend, compute change, and keep one reliable backup. The same job, done with different storage, runs a phone app for small payments, a hardware device kept in a safe, and an exchange account that holds your balance for you.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for UTXOs and change outputs, and Unit 2 for keys, addresses and signatures.

A wallet manages keys; the coins stay on the chain

A Bitcoin wallet stores private keys, watches the chain for outputs those keys can unlock, builds transactions, and signs them. The coins never enter the wallet. They stay on the ledger as unspent transaction outputs (UTXOs), each locked to an address, as Chapter 4.2 explains. Delete the app and the UTXOs are still there; what you may have lost is the key that unlocks them.

Two questions classify every wallet: is the key online or offline, and is the key yours or a company's?

  • Hot wallet: the key sits on a device connected to the internet, such as a phone app, a desktop program or a browser extension. It pays quickly and is exposed to malware.
  • Cold wallet: the key is created and kept offline. A hardware wallet signs inside its own chip and sends out only the signature, so the key never reaches the computer.
  • Custodial wallet: an exchange or payment app holds the keys and shows you a balance. You hold a claim on the operator, not keys.
  • Non-custodial wallet: you hold the keys, and with them the whole responsibility.

Addresses come in three common forms. P2PKH addresses start with 1 and lock to a hash of a public key. P2SH addresses start with 3 and lock to a script hash, the usual home of multisignature. Native SegWit addresses start with bc1 and use bech32 encoding, which pays lower fees because signature bytes are discounted. All three are public: anyone may pay in, only the key holder may spend out.

One seed phrase, a whole tree of keys

A wallet that stores each private key separately needs one backup per key. A deterministic wallet derives every key from a single starting secret, so one backup restores all of them. In a hierarchical deterministic (HD) wallet those keys form a tree, which is why one device can serve several accounts and thousands of addresses.

  1. BIP39 turns fresh randomness into 12 or 24 common English words plus a checksum. This is the seed phrase, also called the recovery phrase. The same words always regenerate the same keys.
  2. PBKDF2 stretches the phrase into a 512-bit seed.
  3. BIP32 hashes that seed with HMAC-SHA512 to give the master private key and a master chain code.
  4. Each child key comes from the parent key, the chain code and an index number, hashed together. The index is the position in the tree.
  5. A hardened step, written with an apostrophe as in 0', mixes in the parent private key, so a leaked parent public key cannot produce hardened children.

m / 84' / 0' / 0' / 1 / k

m is the master key; 84' is the purpose (BIP84, native SegWit; some wallets write 44' for legacy or 49' for P2SH-SegWit); 0' is the coin type, Bitcoin; the next 0' is the account number; 1 is the change chain, while 0 is the external receiving chain; k is the address index, starting at 0.

Two chains, not one. The external chain hands out a fresh receiving address for each payment; the change chain receives the leftover of each spend. Reusing one address publicly links all its payments, a privacy leak explained in Chapter 4.8. A new address costs nothing to make.

Selecting UTXOs, paying, and keeping the change

A spend starts from the wallet's own UTXO list. The wallet selects inputs that cover the payment plus the fee, sends the payment to the payee, and returns the remainder to itself.

  1. List the UTXOs the keys control; their sum is the spendable balance.
  2. Choose a subset worth at least payment + fee. Common rules: largest first for fewest inputs, smallest first to clear dust, or an exact-match search.
  3. Add a change output to a fresh address on the change chain, unless the change is dust, in which case it is dropped and the miner collects it.
  4. Sign every input with the key for its address, then broadcast.

change = Σ inputs − payment − fee

Σ inputs is the total value of the selected UTXOs, payment is the amount sent to the payee, and fee is what the miner may claim; all three in the same unit, BTC or satoshi (1 BTC = 10⁸ satoshi).

Coin control is manual selection: you pick the UTXOs, as you would pick which notes to hand over. It matters for privacy, because merging a KYC-exchange output with a private one links them, and for fees, because many small inputs make a large transaction.

Back up the seed phrase on paper or metal, in two places, never as a photo and never typed into a website. A lost or broken hardware wallet is replaced by restoring the phrase on a new device. A leaked phrase is a total loss: whoever reads it can sign, and a confirmed transaction cannot be reversed.

Worked example

Question. A wallet controls three UTXOs: 0.4 BTC, 0.3 BTC and 0.2 BTC. It must pay 0.6 BTC to a merchant with a fee of 0.0005 BTC. Which UTXOs should it choose, and what change does it create?

  1. Spendable balance = 0.4 + 0.3 + 0.2 = 0.9 BTC.
  2. Total needed = payment + fee = 0.6 + 0.0005 = 0.6005 BTC.
  3. Try the pair 0.4 + 0.3 = 0.7 BTC. Change = 0.7 − 0.6 − 0.0005 = 0.0995 BTC. Positive, so the pair works.
  4. Check the smaller pair 0.4 + 0.2 = 0.6 BTC. Change = 0.6 − 0.6 − 0.0005 = −0.0005 BTC. Negative, so it cannot pay the fee and fails.
  5. The pair 0.3 + 0.2 = 0.5 BTC is below 0.6 BTC, so it fails at once.
  6. The 0.0995 BTC change goes out as a new output to a fresh address on the change chain.

Answer. Spend the 0.4 BTC and 0.3 BTC UTXOs; the transaction pays 0.6 BTC, a fee of 0.0005 BTC, and returns 0.0995 BTC change to the wallet's change chain.

Exam traps

  • Saying the wallet file or the app contains the coins. It contains keys; the coins are UTXOs on the chain.
  • Treating the seed phrase as a password. A password can be reset by an operator; the seed phrase is the key itself and nobody can reset it.
  • Calling the whole input-output gap a fee. The gap minus the change output is the fee; the change is your own money coming back.
  • Mixing address prefixes: 1 is P2PKH, 3 is P2SH, bc1 is bech32 SegWit.
  • Forgetting that an apostrophe in a derivation path means hardened, so m/84'/0'/0'/0/0 differs from m/84/0/0/0/0.

4.7 Alternative coins: Namecoin and Zcash

Bitcoin showed that a public ledger can work without a bank. An altcoin keeps that base and changes one feature. Namecoin keeps Bitcoin's unspent-output ledger and adds a naming registry for .bit names. Zcash keeps the ledger and adds shielded payments using zero-knowledge proofs. Both exist to test whether one changed feature is worth the cost. You meet them in payments, privacy tools and decentralised naming, but also in exam questions that ask what is hidden and what is public.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for altcoin history and Chapter 4.2 for the unspent-output model; Unit 2 for hash and commitment intuition.

One base, one changed feature

An altcoin is a cryptocurrency other than Bitcoin. Most altcoins copy the core design: transactions spend earlier outputs, miners order them in blocks, and nodes check the same rules. The design is copied because it already solves ownership and double spending. The altcoin then changes one rule to serve a new use. Some syllabi call this group 'Alternative Coins' and place Namecoin, Primecoin and Zcash together.

Namecoin changes the data that a transaction can carry. Besides coins, its chain stores key-value records for names. Zcash changes what a transaction reveals. It can hide the sender, receiver and amount while still proving the spend is valid. Primecoin changes the Proof-of-Work puzzle: it searches for chains of prime numbers instead of hashes with many leading zeros. For Primecoin, learn its one-line idea; spend your time on Namecoin and Zcash. The shared lesson: an altcoin is not automatically better; it trades one property for another.

Namecoin and the .bit namespace

Namecoin is a fork of Bitcoin that adds a naming system. A .bit name is a key stored on the Namecoin chain. The value is a small record: an IP address, a public key, a DNS entry, or a pointer to other data. Owners update the value by sending a Namecoin transaction that the network checks. The name is not rented from a company; it is controlled by whoever holds the matching private key. This is why people call it decentralised DNS. A normal browser cannot open .bit directly. It needs a resolver that reads Namecoin, or a browser extension. The .bit namespace does not replace the ICANN root; it is a parallel naming system.

Merged mining lets one mining effort secure two chains. Rule for students: the chain whose block hash is committed is the child, and the chain that carries that commitment in its coinbase transaction is the parent. Namecoin is the child and Bitcoin is the parent. A miner hashes a Namecoin candidate block and puts that hash inside the coinbase transaction of a Bitcoin candidate block. Then the miner searches for a Bitcoin nonce. If the Bitcoin block meets Bitcoin's target, the miner earns Bitcoin. If the same Bitcoin block also meets Namecoin's target, then the Bitcoin block header plus its coinbase act as the proof, and with it the Namecoin block becomes valid. The chains stay separate; only the work is shared. This raises Namecoin's security because Bitcoin's large hash power can help protect it. It does not make Namecoin a Bitcoin side chain in the simple sense; it is a separate chain that borrows proof of work.

  1. Build a Namecoin candidate block N.
  2. Hash N and put that hash into the coinbase transaction of a Bitcoin candidate block B.
  3. Search for a nonce that makes B meet Bitcoin's target.
  4. Broadcast B to Bitcoin; the miner earns Bitcoin.
  5. If B's hash also meets Namecoin's target, submit B's header and coinbase (the proof) together with N to Namecoin; N is then a valid Namecoin block.

Zcash: transparent and shielded

Zcash uses two address types. A transparent address, often written t-addr, works like a Bitcoin address. Its inputs, outputs and amounts are public. A shielded address, z-addr, puts coins into a shielded pool. A shielded transaction proves that the sender owns enough coins and that no coin is created from nothing, without showing which coins were spent or where they went. The tool is a zero-knowledge proof called zk-SNARK: the prover convinces verifiers that a statement is true while revealing nothing beyond the statement. You do not need the mathematics here; Unit 2's hash and commitment ideas are enough to see why the proof binds the transaction.

Four transaction forms exist: transparent to transparent (t to t), transparent to shielded (t to z), shielded to shielded (z to z) and shielded to transparent (z to t). Only z to z hides sender, receiver and amount. In t to z the sender is public but the receiver and the amount are hidden. In z to t the receiving address and the amount are public but the sender is hidden; this is the form students mistake for full privacy. A fully shielded payment hides sender, receiver and amount. The public ledger still records that a valid shielded transaction occurred and that the shielded pool's balance changed. A transparent payment in Zcash is as public as Bitcoin. Privacy is therefore a choice, not a default.

Some versions need a trusted setup ceremony, and wallets or exchanges may not support shielded addresses. Regulators may treat shielded coins differently from transparent coins. Zcash is used when the payment amount or the parties are sensitive, not as a general replacement for Bitcoin.

Worked example

Question. A transparent Zcash transaction moves 1.5 ZEC and the public ledger shows sender, receiver and amount. A shielded transaction of 1.5 ZEC hides those. At ₹4,000 per ZEC, what rupee value moves in each, and what does the public see?

  1. Amount moved = 1.5 ZEC in both transactions.
  2. Price = ₹4,000 per ZEC.
  3. Rupee value = 1.5 ZEC × ₹4,000 per ZEC = ₹6,000.
  4. Transparent transaction: the public sees the sender, the receiver and the amount 1.5 ZEC. The ₹6,000 is only your conversion at ₹4,000 per ZEC, not data on the ledger.
  5. Shielded transaction: the public sees only a valid proof and a change in the shielded pool; it does not see sender, receiver or amount.
  6. Both move 1.5 ZEC; the difference is what the ledger reveals.

Answer. Both transactions move 1.5 ZEC, which is ₹6,000 at ₹4,000 per ZEC. The transparent one reveals sender, receiver and amount on the ledger; the shielded one reveals only a valid proof and a change in the shielded pool. The rupee figure is your own conversion, not ledger data.

Exam traps

  • Zcash is not anonymous by default: transparent addresses behave like Bitcoin; only shielded transactions hide parties and amount.
  • Namecoin is not a renamed Bitcoin copy: it stores name records and is commonly merge-mined with Bitcoin; the chains remain separate.
  • Merged mining shares proof of work, not coins: a Bitcoin block reward is not paid in Namecoin, and Namecoin names are not Bitcoin transactions.
  • The child chain is the one whose block hash is committed; the parent chain carries that commitment in its coinbase. For Namecoin, Bitcoin is the parent.
  • Primecoin uses prime chains for Proof of Work; do not call it a privacy coin.
  • A shielded transaction hides the amount, but the public ledger still shows a valid proof and a shielded-pool change; do not say it leaves no trace.

Rupee value = 2 ZEC × ₹3,500 per ZEC = ₹7,000. The shielded transaction hides sender, receiver and amount; the public sees a valid proof and a shielded-pool change.

The customer loses amount and party privacy. A transparent transaction publishes sender, receiver and amount on the ledger. Off-chain data can link an address to a person. A shielded transaction would hide those details, but this merchant does not accept it.

4.8 Anonymity and privacy in cryptocurrencies

Bitcoin payments are public, but names are not attached. Each address is a pseudonym. Because every transaction stays on the ledger, address reuse, co-spent inputs and data from KYC exchanges can join those pseudonyms into one identity. This chapter explains that linkage, then the tools that reduce it: mixers, CoinJoin, confidential amounts, ring signatures and shielded pools. It matters to users who want financial privacy, to exchanges that must follow rules, and to investigators who trace stolen coins.

You need: Chapter 4.2 for UTXOs and Chapter 4.5 for how transactions spread.

Pseudonymity, not anonymity

Bitcoin is pseudonymous. An address is a pseudonym: a string that stands in for a key owner. The blockchain records the address, amount and time of every payment. It does not record a name, a phone number or a PAN. Anyone can read the ledger. This is public auditability, not privacy.

Two things reduce privacy: address reuse and co-spending. Address reuse means receiving many payments at one address, so one public label collects your history. Co-spending is stronger. A transaction spends unspent outputs (UTXOs, from Chapter 4.2). If one transaction spends outputs from A1 and A2, the common-input-ownership heuristic infers that one wallet controls both keys. A valid spend needs a signature for every input, so the builder had both private keys. Exchanges, wallets and chain-analysis firms use this heuristic to cluster addresses.

Clustering puts addresses into one guessed owner. Taint follows a coin's history: if a coin passed through a flagged address, later holders may be treated as suspicious. Taint is a risk score, not proof. A mixer adds hops; it does not erase history.

Chain analysis and the KYC link

Chain analysis reads public ledger data to guess ownership and flow. It uses several signals:

  • common-input clustering;
  • change outputs: one output pays the recipient, the remainder returns to the sender's new change address;
  • timing and amounts: a withdrawal at 10:02 and a deposit at 10:05 may be the same coins;
  • off-chain data: invoices, forum posts, shipping records, leaked databases.

The strongest link is a KYC exchange. KYC means Know Your Customer: the exchange verifies a legal name, address and ID. When the customer withdraws bitcoin, the exchange records the withdrawal address. A lawful request can then connect that address to a verified person. Clustering spreads the label to co-spent addresses.

A single transaction that spends A1 and A2 lets the heuristic link them, and if the exchange knows the name behind one input, that name spreads to the rest of the cluster. One KYC withdrawal can expose a cluster.

Privacy techniques

Mixers break the direct link. A custodial mixer takes coins, pools them, and pays out from a different address, minus a fee. It relies on trust: the operator can steal or keep records. CoinJoin is trustless in the sense that no operator holds the coins: several users build one transaction with many inputs and many equal-value outputs. Many implementations use a coordinator that cannot steal funds but may learn the input-output mapping unless the round is blinded. An outside observer sees many possible owners for each output. CoinJoin does not hide amounts, and poor timing or unusual amounts can still leak links.

Confidential transactions hide the amount, not the addresses. They use cryptographic commitments so the network can check that inputs equal outputs without seeing values. Monero uses ring signatures and RingCT: a real input hides among decoys, and amounts are hidden by commitments. Zcash has shielded pools that hide sender, receiver and amount using zero-knowledge proofs, as Chapter 4.7 introduces. Transparent Zcash transactions behave like Bitcoin.

Privacy coins and mixers face legal scrutiny. Many exchanges delist coins with strong anonymity, and some jurisdictions require reporting. Privacy is legal in many places; using it to evade tax or launder money is not. A mixer does not make coins clean.

Worked example

Question. Address A1 receives 0.8 BTC from a KYC exchange. A2 holds 0.3 BTC. One transaction spends both inputs and pays 1.09 BTC to E, with a 0.01 BTC fee. What does the common-input heuristic infer, and what does the exchange know?

  1. List the inputs: A1 has 0.8 BTC and A2 has 0.3 BTC.
  2. Sum the inputs: 0.8 + 0.3 = 1.1 BTC.
  3. Add the output and fee: 1.09 + 0.01 = 1.1 BTC. Inputs equal outputs plus fee, so the transaction is balanced.
  4. Apply the common-input heuristic: one transaction spends A1 and A2, so one wallet likely controls both keys.
  5. The exchange has a KYC record for the withdrawal to A1. By clustering, it can attach that verified name to A2. It may also see E as the recipient of 1.09 BTC.

Answer. The heuristic links A1 and A2 to one owner, and the exchange can attach its KYC name for A1 to A2; the transaction moves 1.09 BTC to E with a 0.01 BTC fee.

Exam traps

  • Saying Bitcoin is anonymous. Every payment is public and permanent; addresses are pseudonyms, not names.
  • Treating co-spending as proof. It is a heuristic; exchange co-spending can create false clusters.
  • Saying a mixer makes coins clean. It adds a hop; taint remains and chain analysis may cross it.
  • Confusing CoinJoin with confidential transactions. CoinJoin hides links; confidential transactions hide amounts.
  • Thinking KYC links only deposits. The exchange also records withdrawal addresses, which seed clustering.

4.9 Limitations of Bitcoin

Bitcoin is secure and open, but it processes only a few transactions per second. That limit is not one bug: it comes from design choices that keep blocks small, confirmations slow and full nodes cheap to run. Raising throughput raises disk and bandwidth costs, which can reduce the number of independent full nodes and shift power toward large miners. This chapter separates technical limits (throughput, block interval, scripting, quantum risk), economic limits (fees, volatility, mining pools) and legal limits (regulation, forks), then shows where the Lightning Network helps and where it does not.

You need: blocks and transactions from Chapter 4.2, mining rewards from Chapter 4.4, and confirmations from Chapter 4.5.

The scalability trilemma

A blockchain must balance three goals: throughput, security and decentralisation. Throughput is how many transactions it settles per second. Security is the cost of rewriting history. Decentralisation is how many independent people can check the rules without asking a trusted party. The trilemma says you cannot maximise all three at once; improving one usually costs another. Some syllabi call this the theoretical limitations of Bitcoin.

TPS ≈ (transactions per block) ÷ (block interval in seconds)

TPS is transactions per second; transactions per block is the count in one block; block interval is the average time between blocks, about 600 s in Bitcoin.

Bitcoin's block interval targets 10 minutes, or 600 s. A shorter interval gives faster confirmations but more competing blocks and more stale work. A longer interval is safer but slower for users. The block size or weight sets the numerator. Bigger blocks carry more transactions, but every full node must store and relay them. That raises disk, memory and bandwidth costs, so fewer people can run a full node. Fewer independent nodes make the network easier to influence, which is the decentralisation cost in the figure.

Blocks, weight and fees

Before SegWit, a Bitcoin block was capped at 1 MB, using decimal bytes. With simple transactions around 250 bytes, that gives about 4,000 transactions per block. After SegWit, the rule is block weight, not raw size. The cap is 4,000,000 weight units. Each non-witness byte weighs 4 units; witness bytes weigh 1 unit. The base block is still capped at 1,000,000 bytes, so SegWit changes the counting rule rather than removing the block limit.

When demand exceeds block space, users bid fees per virtual byte. Miners pick high-fee transactions first, as covered in Chapter 4.4. Fee spikes make small payments uneconomic: a ₹50 coffee paid on-chain could carry a fee larger than its value. The mempool holds waiting transactions; during busy periods a payment can sit for hours. Fees also become a larger part of miner income as the block subsidy halves over time.

Beyond the block: energy, pools and law

Proof of Work secures Bitcoin by spending electricity. That energy use is a real cost, even when miners use surplus power. Mining also concentrates: large pools coordinate hash power, and a few pools can control most blocks. Pool members can switch, so this is not the same as one miner owning the coins, but it is a centralisation risk. High fees and slow confirmations make Bitcoin poor for everyday retail payments in its base layer.

  • Volatility: the rupee price can swing sharply, so a merchant or salary payer cannot plan easily.
  • Limited scripting: Bitcoin scripts are deliberately small; complex conditions need layers or other chains, covered in Unit 5.
  • Quantum risk: a future large quantum computer could break ECDSA; Bitcoin could migrate by fork, but that is hard.
  • Regulation and forks: tax, KYC at exchanges and legal bans vary by country; a hard fork can split the chain and the community.
  • Lightning Network: payment channels settle most payments off-chain and use the chain only to open and close; it helps small frequent payments, but needs liquidity and online watchfulness.

Lightning is a partial answer, not a full replacement. It does not make global on-chain settlement cheap for every user, and large settlements still touch the base chain.

Worked example

Question. A Bitcoin block is 1 MB, where 1 MB = 1,000,000 bytes, and a simple transaction is about 250 bytes. Blocks arrive every 10 minutes on average. (a) How many transactions fit in one block? (b) What is the throughput in transactions per second? (c) How long does 100,000 transactions take in blocks, minutes and hours?

  1. Transactions per block = 1,000,000 bytes ÷ 250 bytes per transaction = 4,000 transactions.
  2. Block interval = 10 min × 60 s/min = 600 s.
  3. Throughput = 4,000 ÷ 600 s = 6.6667 tps, about 6.7 tps.
  4. Blocks for 100,000 = 100,000 ÷ 4,000 = 25 blocks.
  5. Time = 25 × 10 min = 250 min.
  6. In hours = 250 ÷ 60 = 4.1667 h, about 4 h 10 min.

Answer. A block holds 4,000 transactions, throughput is about 6.7 transactions per second, and 100,000 transactions take 25 blocks, 250 minutes or about 4 h 10 min.

Exam traps

  • Do not mix block size in bytes with block weight in weight units. Before SegWit the cap was 1 MB; after SegWit the cap is 4,000,000 weight units, with the base block still capped at 1,000,000 bytes.
  • Do not say Bitcoin cannot scale at all. SegWit, batching and the Lightning Network are partial answers; the base layer still has limited throughput.
  • The 10-minute block interval is an average target, not a fixed timer. A block can arrive sooner or later.
  • The trilemma is a design trade-off, not a law of physics; engineering can soften it, but not make all three free.
  • Fee spikes are a symptom of scarce block space; they are not the same as the block subsidy, and miners receive both.

Takeaways

  1. Cryptocurrency replaces per-payment bank approval with a shared ledger plus cryptographic proof of ownership.
  2. A Bitcoin transaction spends earlier UTXOs and creates new outputs, while a block batches such transactions under one hash-linked header.
  3. Bitcoin script is the lock on each output; a valid spend is a script run that leaves exactly TRUE.
  4. Miners maximise expected revenue by choosing transactions and hash power after accounting for subsidy, fees and costs.
  5. Waiting for confirmations makes double spending impractical because an attacker must overtake accumulated proof of work.
  6. A wallet stores keys and signs transactions; the coins themselves remain unspent outputs on the blockchain.
  7. Altcoins keep Bitcoin's core design but change one feature, such as Namecoin's naming registry or Zcash's shielded payments.
  8. Bitcoin is pseudonymous, not anonymous: public transaction links plus off-chain data can reveal address ownership.
  9. Bitcoin trades throughput and flexibility for security and decentralisation, so its limits are technical, economic and legal at once.

Glossary

51% attack
Control of a majority of hash power, enough to reorg recent blocks and double spend.
Address
A short encoded form of a public key hash used to receive bitcoin.
Altcoin
A cryptocurrency that copies Bitcoin's core design but changes some feature.
Bitcoin
A peer-to-peer cryptocurrency system whose ledger is maintained by proof-of-work mining.
Block
A batch of transactions with a header that links it to the previous block.
Blockchain
A hash-linked chain of blocks that records agreed transaction history.
Cold storage
Keeping private keys offline so online attackers cannot reach them.
Difficulty
A measure of how hard it is to find a block hash below the target.
ECDSA
Elliptic Curve Digital Signature Algorithm, used by Bitcoin to sign transactions.
Fee
The amount a spender gives to the miner, equal to input value minus output value.
Hash function
A function that maps data to a fixed-size fingerprint; Bitcoin uses SHA-256.
HD wallet
A wallet that derives many keys from one seed using a deterministic hierarchy.
Hot wallet
A wallet connected to the internet, convenient but more exposed to attack.
Merkle tree
A binary hash tree that summarises transactions and supports inclusion proofs.
Mining
Repeated hashing to find a valid block header under the proof-of-work target.
Nonce
A header field miners change while searching for a valid proof-of-work hash.
P2P network
A network where nodes connect directly and relay data without a central server.
Private key
A secret number that authorises spending from the corresponding public key.
Proof of work
A costly proof, found by hashing, that a block was produced with real effort.
Public key
A number derived from a private key, used to verify signatures and form addresses.
Satoshi Nakamoto
The pseudonymous author or authors of the original Bitcoin design.
SegWit
Segregated Witness, a Bitcoin upgrade that moves witness data and fixes transaction malleability.
SPV client
A lightweight client that checks proof-of-work headers and Merkle proofs, not all transactions.
UTXO
Unspent transaction output, the spendable unit of bitcoin value.
