4.1 Populations, samples and random sampling

A population is the whole group a question is about: every UPI payment made in India today, every voter in Tamil Nadu, every bulb a factory makes this month. You can rarely measure all of it, because it costs too much, takes too long, or destroys what you measure (a bulb tested until it burns out cannot be sold). So you measure a sample, a smaller group taken from the population, and reason from it back to the whole. That reasoning is inferential statistics, and it can be trusted only when chance chooses the sample.

You need: mean, variance and standard deviation from Unit 3, Measures of central tendency and variability, and data collection from Unit 1, Data sources and data collection.

Population, sample, parameter, statistic

The population is the complete set of units a question is about; its size is N. A sample is the part you observe; its size is n. A number that describes the population is a parameter; the same kind of number computed from a sample is a statistic. A parameter is fixed but unknown. A statistic is known but changes from one sample to the next. A statistic used to guess a parameter is an estimator (Chapter 4.3). Greek letters name parameters and Latin letters name statistics:

QuantityPopulation parameterSample statistic
SizeNn
Meanμ (mu) = ΣX / Nx̄ (x-bar) = Σx / n
Varianceσ² = Σ(X − μ)² / Ns² = Σ(x − x̄)² / (n − 1)
Standard deviationσ (sigma)s
Proportionpp̂ (p-hat) = count / n

s² divides by n − 1 because the deviations x − x̄ always add up to zero, so only n − 1 of them are free; this makes s² an unbiased estimate of σ². The list you actually draw from (a roll list, a customer database, a voter list) is the sampling frame. If the frame leaves out part of the population, no drawing method can repair it.

Probability sampling methods

In probability sampling every unit has a known, non-zero chance of being chosen, and a random device, not the data collector, decides who is in. Four methods appear in every syllabus (Figure 4.1.1):

  1. Simple random sampling (SRS). Every possible sample of size n has the same chance. Number the units 1 to N and draw n numbers by lottery or computer. Without replacement there are C(N, n) possible samples; for N = 5 and n = 2 that is 10.
  2. Systematic sampling. Compute the interval k = N / n, pick a random start r from 1 to k, then take units r, r + k, r + 2k, … It is quick on a list, but it fails when the list repeats with period k: every 7th entry of a daily sales list is always the same weekday.
  3. Stratified sampling. Split the population into strata that differ from each other (year of study, state, income band) and draw an SRS inside each. With proportional allocation, a stratum of size Nh gets nh = n × Nh / N units. No group can be missed, and the estimate is usually more precise than an SRS of the same size.
  4. Cluster sampling. Split the population into clusters that each look like a small copy of it (classrooms, villages, apartment blocks), choose some clusters at random and measure every unit inside them. It is cheap when travel is costly, but less precise than an SRS of the same n, because units in one cluster tend to be alike.

Multi-stage sampling chains them: random districts, then random villages in each, then random households. Large national surveys work this way.

Non-probability samples and bias

A non-probability sample is chosen by convenience or judgment: people who answer an Instagram poll, the first 50 shoppers at a mall gate, a quota filled by the interviewer. It is fine for a pilot study, but no formula can say how far its results are from the truth.

The gap between a statistic and its parameter has two sources. Sampling error is the chance gap from seeing only part of the population; it shrinks as n grows, and Chapter 4.2 measures it. Bias is a steady push in one direction, and a bigger sample does not remove it. Selection bias: the method leaves some units out (a phone survey misses people without phones). Non-response bias: those who reply differ from those who do not. Response bias: leading questions or answers people think are expected.

Drawing samples in Python

In Python, random.sample draws an SRS without replacement, and pandas draws a proportional stratified sample with groupby(...).sample(frac=...). A fixed seed makes each draw repeatable.

import random
import pandas as pd
random.seed(42)
srs = random.sample(range(1, 1201), 60)      # SRS of 60 roll numbers
print(sorted(srs)[:8])
df = pd.DataFrame({"roll": range(1, 1201),
                   "year": [1]*480 + [2]*360 + [3]*240 + [4]*120})
strat = df.groupby("year").sample(frac=0.05, random_state=1)
print(strat["year"].value_counts().sort_index().to_dict())
[14, 52, 55, 62, 66, 89, 94, 143]
{1: 24, 2: 18, 3: 12, 4: 6}

Worked example

Question. An engineering college in Coimbatore has 1,200 students: 480 in first year, 360 in second, 240 in third and 120 in fourth. The placement cell will survey 60 of them about internships. (a) Name the population, the sample, one parameter and one statistic. (b) Find the proportional stratified allocation. (c) Set up a systematic sample from the roll list with random start 7; give the first four and the last roll numbers. (d) The cell instead surveys the first 60 students to reach the canteen on Monday. Name the method and the problem. (e) In a proper random sample of 60, 21 students would take an unpaid internship. Estimate the proportion for the college.

  1. (a) Population: all 1,200 students. Sample: the 60 surveyed. Parameter: p, the proportion of all 1,200 who would take an unpaid internship. Statistic: p̂, that proportion among the 60.
  2. (b) nh = 60 × Nh / 1,200 = Nh / 20: first year 24, second 18, third 12, fourth 6. Check: 24 + 18 + 12 + 6 = 60.
  3. (c) k = 1,200 / 60 = 20. Roll numbers 7, 27, 47, 67, …; the 60th is 7 + 59 × 20 = 1,187.
  4. (d) Convenience sampling. Early arrivals (hostellers living close by, students without a morning lab) may differ from the rest: selection bias, which a bigger convenience sample would not fix.
  5. (e) p̂ = 21 / 60 = 0.35, about 35% of the college or 0.35 × 1,200 = 420 students. Chapter 4.2 adds the margin of error.

Exam traps

  • μ, σ and p are fixed population values; x̄, s and p̂ come from a sample and vary. "The sample mean μ" loses marks.
  • Stratified takes some units from every group (groups differ); cluster takes all units from a few groups (groups look alike). "Distinguish with an example" is the favourite question.
  • Random does not mean haphazard: "any 60 students I meet" is a convenience sample.
  • A huge biased sample is still biased. An online poll with one lakh replies can be worse than a random sample of 1,000.
  • s² divides by n − 1 and σ² by N; calculators have both keys (σn and σn−1).

4.2 Sampling distributions and standard error

Draw a sample and compute its mean; draw another sample of the same size and the mean comes out different. The sampling distribution of a statistic is the distribution of its values over all possible samples of one size, and its standard deviation is the standard error (SE). The standard error says how far a sample result is likely to sit from the true parameter. Every confidence interval and every test in this unit is built on it.

You need: parameters and statistics from Chapter 4.1, and the normal curve and z-scores from Unit 3, Normal distribution and z-scores.

A sampling distribution built by hand

Take a tiny population of four numbers, 2, 4, 6 and 8. Its mean is μ = 20 / 4 = 5. Its variance is σ² = [(−3)² + (−1)² + 1² + 3²] / 4 = 20 / 4 = 5, so σ = √5 = 2.24.

Now list every sample of size n = 2 drawn with replacement: 4 × 4 = 16 ordered samples, (2, 2), (2, 4), …, (8, 8). Work out the mean of each and count how often each mean occurs:

x̄2345678Total
Frequency f123432116
f × (x̄ − 5)²983038940

The mean of the 16 sample means is (1×2 + 2×3 + 3×4 + 4×5 + 3×6 + 2×7 + 1×8) / 16 = 80 / 16 = 5, exactly μ. Their variance is 40 / 16 = 2.5, which is σ² / n = 5 / 2. Figure 4.2.1 shows three facts that hold in general:

  • The sampling distribution of x̄ is centred on μ: x̄ is an unbiased estimator of μ.
  • It is narrower than the population: its standard deviation is σ / √n = 2.24 / √2 = 1.58.
  • It is more bell-shaped than the population: a flat population already gives a triangle at n = 2.

The standard error

The standard error of the mean is

SE(x̄) = σ / √n        σ = population SD, n = sample size

When σ is unknown, which is usual, replace it by the sample SD s and write SE = s / √n. For a sample proportion p̂ the standard error is SE(p̂) = √(p(1 − p) / n), with p̂ in place of p when p is unknown.

When you sample without replacement from a small population, multiply the SE by the finite population correction √((N − n) / (N − 1)). For the four numbers above, the 6 samples without replacement have means 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, with variance 1.667 = 2.5 × 2 / 3. The correction matters only when n is more than about 5% of N.

Because n sits under a square root, precision is expensive: to halve the SE you need four times the sample. Keep SD and SE apart. The SD describes how individual values spread; the SE describes how a statistic such as x̄ spreads from sample to sample.

The central limit theorem

The central limit theorem (CLT) says: if random samples of size n are drawn from any population with mean μ and finite standard deviation σ, then for large n the sampling distribution of x̄ is approximately normal with mean μ and standard deviation σ / √n. A common rule of thumb is n ≥ 30; a strongly skewed population needs more, and a normal population gives a normal x̄ for every n. So you can turn a sample mean into a z-score and use the normal table:

z = (x̄ − μ) / (σ / √n)

A NumPy simulation in Python shows it. Waiting times that follow an exponential distribution with mean 2 minutes are very skewed (σ is also 2), yet 10,000 sample means of n = 30 centre on 2 with spread close to 2 / √30:

import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
means = rng.exponential(scale=2, size=(10_000, 30)).mean(axis=1)
print(round(means.mean(), 3), round(means.std(), 3), round(2/np.sqrt(30), 3))
1.998 0.358 0.365

Confidence intervals from the standard error

A single number such as x̄ is a point estimate. An interval estimate adds a margin of error. Since x̄ lies within 1.96 SE of μ in 95% of samples, the interval

x̄ ± z* × σ / √n      z* = 1.645 (90%), 1.96 (95%), 2.576 (99%)

is a confidence interval. The margin of error is E = z* × σ / √n, and solving for n gives the sample size for a chosen margin: n = (z* σ / E)², rounded up. "95% confidence" means that 95% of intervals built this way, over repeated samples, contain μ. Higher confidence gives a wider interval, and a bigger n a narrower one. Manipal lists confidence intervals in this unit; for small samples with unknown σ, use t in place of z (Chapter 4.5).

For a proportion: p̂ ± z* √(p̂(1 − p̂) / n). In Chapter 4.1, 21 of 60 students gave p̂ = 0.35, so SE = √(0.35 × 0.65 / 60) = 0.0616 and the 95% interval is 0.35 ± 0.121, that is 0.229 to 0.471.

Worked example

Question. A food-delivery company in Chennai knows its delivery times have mean μ = 32 minutes and standard deviation σ = 8 minutes. A random sample of 64 orders is taken. (a) Find the standard error of the sample mean. (b) Find P(x̄ > 34). (c) Find P(31 < x̄ < 33). (d) After new routing software, a sample of 64 orders has x̄ = 30.5 minutes; assume σ is still 8. Find a 95% confidence interval for the new mean. (e) How many orders are needed for a 95% margin of error of 0.5 minutes?

  1. (a) SE = σ / √n = 8 / √64 = 8 / 8 = 1 minute.
  2. (b) z = (34 − 32) / 1 = 2.00. P(Z > 2.00) = 1 − 0.9772 = 0.0228, about 2 samples in 100.
  3. (c) z runs from (31 − 32) / 1 = −1 to (33 − 32) / 1 = +1. P(−1 < Z < 1) = 0.8413 − 0.1587 = 0.6826.
  4. (d) 30.5 ± 1.96 × 1 = 30.5 ± 1.96, so the interval is 28.54 to 32.46 minutes. It still contains 32, so this sample does not yet show that the software lowered the mean (Chapter 4.4 makes this a test).
  5. (e) n = (1.96 × 8 / 0.5)² = (31.36)² = 983.4. Round up: 984 orders.

Exam traps

  • SE = σ / √n, not σ / n. With σ = 8 and n = 64 the SE is 1, not 0.125.
  • The CLT is about the distribution of x̄, not the data. A sample of 100 skewed values is still skewed; their mean is approximately normal.
  • SD and SE are not the same: quote the SD to describe data and the SE to describe an estimate.
  • Always round a required sample size up; 983.4 becomes 984, not 983.
  • "There is a 95% probability that μ lies in 28.54 to 32.46" is the wrong reading; the 95% belongs to the method, not to one interval.

4.3 Parameter estimation: maximum likelihood and Bayesian

An estimator is a rule that turns a sample into a guess for an unknown parameter, such as the failure rate of a payment gateway. There are two principled ways to build one. Maximum likelihood picks the parameter value that makes the observed data most probable. Bayesian estimation starts from a prior belief about the parameter and updates it with the data by Bayes' rule. Maximum likelihood is behind the loss functions of machine learning; Bayesian updating is behind spam filters.

You need: Chapter 4.2, the binomial and Poisson distributions, and setting a derivative to zero.

Estimators and what makes one good

Write θ (theta) for the unknown parameter and θ̂ (theta-hat) for its estimator. Its value on one sample is an estimate: a point estimate if a single value, an interval estimate if a range (Chapter 4.2). Three properties judge an estimator:

  • Unbiased: its sampling distribution is centred on the truth, E[θ̂] = θ. x̄ is unbiased for μ, and s² (dividing by n − 1) is unbiased for σ². Dividing by n instead gives a variance that is too small on average by the factor (n − 1) / n.
  • Consistent: θ̂ gets closer to θ as n grows.
  • Efficient: among unbiased estimators it has the smallest variance, so the smallest standard error.

Bias and spread combine in the mean squared error: MSE = Var(θ̂) + Bias².

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE)

Suppose the data x₁, …, xₙ are independent draws from a distribution with probability (or density) f(x; θ). The likelihood is the probability of the whole observed sample, read as a function of θ with the data held fixed:

L(θ) = f(x₁; θ) × f(x₂; θ) × … × f(xₙ; θ)
ℓ(θ) = ln L(θ) = Σ ln f(xᵢ; θ)        (log-likelihood)

The maximum likelihood estimate θ̂ is the value of θ that makes L(θ) largest. Taking the log turns the product into a sum and does not move the maximum, because ln is an increasing function. The procedure:

  1. Write the likelihood L(θ) for the whole sample.
  2. Take logs to get ℓ(θ).
  3. Differentiate with respect to θ and set dℓ/dθ = 0.
  4. Solve for θ; check that d²ℓ/dθ² < 0, so it is a maximum.

For k successes in n yes/no trials with success probability p (Bernoulli):

L(p)   = p^k (1 − p)^(n − k)
ℓ(p)   = k ln p + (n − k) ln(1 − p)
dℓ/dp  = k / p − (n − k) / (1 − p) = 0
       ⇒ k(1 − p) = (n − k) p   ⇒   p̂ = k / n

The same steps give the standard results: Poisson λ̂ = x̄; exponential λ̂ = 1 / x̄; normal μ̂ = x̄ and σ̂² = (1/n) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)², which divides by n and so is slightly biased. For large n, MLEs are consistent, close to unbiased and as efficient as possible. They are also invariant: the MLE of any function g(θ) is g(θ̂).

Bayesian estimation

The Bayesian view treats θ itself as uncertain and describes that uncertainty with a probability distribution. Before the data you hold a prior P(θ); after the data you hold a posterior P(θ | data), given by Bayes' rule:

P(θ | data) = P(data | θ) × P(θ) / P(data)
posterior   ∝ likelihood × prior

P(data) only rescales the posterior. For a proportion p the usual prior is the Beta(a, b) distribution, with density proportional to p^(a−1) (1 − p)^(b−1) and mean a / (a + b). It behaves like a earlier successes and b earlier failures. With a binomial likelihood of k successes in n trials, the posterior is again a Beta, because the powers simply add:

prior Beta(a, b)  +  k successes in n  →  posterior Beta(a + k, b + n − k)
posterior mean = (a + k) / (a + b + n)
posterior mode (MAP) = (a + k − 1) / (a + b + n − 2)

Such a prior is called conjugate. The posterior gives point estimates (its mean, or its mode, the maximum a posteriori or MAP estimate) and a credible interval: its middle 95%, read as "θ lies here with probability 0.95, given the prior and data". With a flat prior Beta(1, 1), the MAP equals the MLE k / n. As n grows, the data outweigh any reasonable prior and the two methods agree (Figure 4.3.1).

Worked example 1: MLE for a Poisson rate

Question. A payment gateway logs these server outages on 6 days: 2, 0, 3, 1, 2, 4. Assume the daily count is Poisson with rate λ, f(x; λ) = e−λ λx / x!. Derive the MLE of λ, compute it, and estimate the probability of no outage tomorrow.

  1. ℓ(λ) = Σ [−λ + xᵢ ln λ − ln(xᵢ!)] = −nλ + (Σxᵢ) ln λ − Σ ln(xᵢ!).
  2. dℓ/dλ = −n + Σxᵢ / λ = 0, so λ̂ = Σxᵢ / n = x̄. The second derivative −Σxᵢ / λ² is negative, so this is a maximum.
  3. Σxᵢ = 2 + 0 + 3 + 1 + 2 + 4 = 12 and n = 6, so λ̂ = 2.0 outages per day. Check: ℓ(1.5) = −10.49, ℓ(2.0) = −10.04, ℓ(2.5) = −10.36; the largest is at 2.0.
  4. By invariance, P̂(X = 0) = e−λ̂ = e−2 = 0.135, about one day in seven with no outage.

Worked example 2: Bayesian update of a proportion

Question. A UPI app launches a split-the-bill feature. Of the first 10 users who try it, 7 use it again within a week. (a) Give the MLE of the repeat rate p. (b) The product team's prior is Beta(2, 2), centred on 0.5. Find the posterior, its mean and its MAP estimate. (c) Software gives the posterior's middle 95% as 0.386 to 0.861; interpret it. (d) After 100 users with 70 repeats, find the posterior mean.

  1. (a) p̂ = k / n = 7 / 10 = 0.70.
  2. (b) Posterior Beta(2 + 7, 2 + 3) = Beta(9, 5). Mean = 9 / 14 = 0.643. MAP = (9 − 1) / (9 + 5 − 2) = 8 / 12 = 0.667. Both sit between the prior's 0.5 and the data's 0.70.
  3. (c) Given this prior and data, p lies between 0.386 and 0.861 with probability 0.95; ten users leave a wide range.
  4. (d) Posterior Beta(2 + 70, 2 + 30) = Beta(72, 32), mean 72 / 104 = 0.692, now very close to the MLE 0.70: the data outweigh the prior.

Exam traps

  • The likelihood is a function of θ with the data fixed. It is not a probability distribution over θ and need not add up to 1.
  • The MLE of a normal variance divides by n, so it is biased; s² divides by n − 1.
  • Posterior mean, MAP and MLE are three different numbers; 0.643, 0.667 and 0.70 above.
  • A 95% credible interval is a probability statement about θ; a 95% confidence interval is a statement about the method (Chapter 4.2).

4.4 Hypothesis testing: decision rules, p-values and significance

A hypothesis test uses a sample to decide between two claims about a population, while keeping the chance of a false alarm small and known in advance. Does a new UPI checkout page reduce failed payments? Is a packing machine filling less than the label says? Every test in Chapters 4.5 to 4.7 follows the same steps set out here; only the formula for the test statistic changes.

You need: the standard error and the CLT from Chapter 4.2, and reading the normal table from Unit 3.

Null and alternative hypotheses

The null hypothesis H₀ is the "no change, no effect" claim, and it always contains the equality: μ = 500 g. The alternative hypothesis H₁ (also written Hₐ) is what you want evidence for. It takes one of three forms, and the question decides which, before you look at the data:

  • μ ≠ 500: two-tailed test (a difference either way matters).
  • μ > 500: right-tailed (one-tailed) test.
  • μ < 500: left-tailed (one-tailed) test.

Think of a court. H₀ is "the accused is innocent". The court convicts only when the evidence is strong; a verdict of "not guilty" does not prove innocence, it says the evidence was not enough. A test is built the same way: we either reject H₀ or fail to reject H₀, and never "prove" it.

The test statistic measures how far the sample sits from H₀, in standard errors. For a mean with known σ it is z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ / √n), where μ₀ is the value stated in H₀.

Decision rules: critical values and p-values

The significance level α is the probability of rejecting H₀ when it is actually true that you are willing to accept. It is fixed before the data are seen, usually at 0.05. There are two equivalent decision rules.

Critical value rule. The rejection region (critical region) is the set of test-statistic values so extreme that, if H₀ were true, they would occur with probability only α. Reject H₀ if the statistic falls inside it. For z:

αTwo-tailedRight-tailedLeft-tailed
0.10|z| > 1.645z > 1.282z < −1.282
0.05|z| > 1.960z > 1.645z < −1.645
0.01|z| > 2.576z > 2.326z < −2.326

p-value rule. The p-value is the probability, computed as if H₀ were true, of getting a test statistic at least as extreme as the one observed. Reject H₀ if p ≤ α. For a two-tailed z-test, p = 2 × P(Z ≥ |z|); for a one-tailed test, use only the tail named in H₁. A result with p ≤ α is called statistically significant at level α.

The two rules always give the same decision (Figure 4.4.1). The p-value tells you more: p = 0.049 and p = 0.0001 both reject at 5%, but the second is far stronger evidence.

Two kinds of error, and power

DecisionH₀ is trueH₀ is false
Reject H₀Type I error (false alarm), probability αCorrect, probability 1 − β (the power)
Fail to reject H₀Correct, probability 1 − αType II error (missed effect), probability β

For a fixed sample size, lowering α makes the rejection region smaller, so β rises. Only a larger n, or a larger true effect, lowers both. Power grows with n, with α, and with the size of the true effect, and falls as σ grows.

Statistical significance is not practical importance. With a very large sample, a difference too small to matter becomes "significant". Report the effect size (the difference in real units, or Cohen's d = difference / σ) and a confidence interval alongside the p-value. A two-tailed test at level α rejects H₀: μ = μ₀ exactly when μ₀ lies outside the (1 − α) confidence interval of Chapter 4.2.

The procedure for any test

Anna Univ lists this as the "t-test procedure"; the steps are the same for z, t, χ² and F.

  1. State H₀ and H₁ in symbols and decide one- or two-tailed.
  2. Choose α.
  3. Pick the test, check its conditions, and compute the test statistic.
  4. Find the critical value from the table, or the p-value.
  5. Decide: reject H₀ or fail to reject H₀.
  6. State the conclusion in the words of the problem, with α.

Worked example 1: a two-tailed z-test

Question. A machine fills 500 g packets of atta. Fill weights have σ = 10 g. A sample of 25 packets has mean 504.8 g. At α = 0.05, is the machine's mean fill different from 500 g? Would the decision change at α = 0.01?

  1. H₀: μ = 500 g; H₁: μ ≠ 500 g (two-tailed: over- and under-filling both matter).
  2. α = 0.05, so the critical values are ±1.96.
  3. SE = σ / √n = 10 / √25 = 2 g. z = (504.8 − 500) / 2 = 2.40.
  4. p = 2 × P(Z ≥ 2.40) = 2 × (1 − 0.9918) = 2 × 0.0082 = 0.0164.
  5. |z| = 2.40 > 1.96, and p = 0.0164 < 0.05: reject H₀.
  6. At the 5% level, the mean fill differs from 500 g; the machine appears to overfill by about 4.8 g. At α = 0.01 the critical value is 2.576 and 2.40 < 2.576 (p = 0.0164 > 0.01), so we would fail to reject. Always state α.

Worked example 2: power of the same test

Question. Suppose the machine's true mean is actually 505 g. What is the probability that the test above (n = 25, α = 0.05) detects this? What if n = 100?

  1. The test fails to reject when x̄ lies within 500 ± 1.96 × 2, that is 496.08 to 503.92 g.
  2. If μ = 505: P(x̄ > 503.92) = P(Z > (503.92 − 505) / 2) = P(Z > −0.54) = 0.7054. P(x̄ < 496.08) = P(Z < −4.46), practically 0.
  3. Power ≈ 0.705, so β ≈ 0.295: almost a 30% chance of missing a 5 g overfill.
  4. With n = 100, SE = 1 and the upper cut-off is 501.96. P(Z > (501.96 − 505) / 1) = P(Z > −3.04) = 0.999. A bigger sample catches the same shift almost every time.

Exam traps

  • "Fail to reject H₀" is not "accept H₀" and never "H₀ is proved".
  • The p-value is not the probability that H₀ is true; it is computed by assuming H₀ is true.
  • Decide one- or two-tailed from the question, not from the data. At 5%, one-tailed z uses 1.645 and two-tailed uses 1.96.
  • The equality sign always goes in H₀ (=, ≤ or ≥), never in H₁.
  • Significant is not the same as important: report the effect size too.

4.5 z-tests and t-tests

The z-test and the t-test compare means: one sample mean against a claimed value, or the means of two groups. Use z when the population standard deviation σ is known or the sample is large; use t when σ has to be estimated from a small sample. W. S. Gosset, a chemist at the Guinness brewery in Dublin, published the t-test in 1908 under the pen name "Student" to judge small batches; it is still the most used test in data analysis.

You need: the six-step procedure and p-values from Chapter 4.4, and the standard error from Chapter 4.2.

z or t, and the sampling distribution of t

For a mean, both tests divide the gap between x̄ and the claimed value μ₀ by a standard error:

z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ / √n)      σ known, or n ≥ 30          → normal table
t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n)      σ unknown, s = sample SD   → t table, df = n − 1

Because s changes from sample to sample, t varies more than z. Its sampling distribution, the t distribution, is bell-shaped and symmetric about 0 but has heavier tails, so its critical values are larger (Figure 4.5.1). It has one parameter, the degrees of freedom (df), the number of independent pieces of information left after estimating the mean: n − 1 for one sample. As df grows, t approaches the normal. The t-test assumes the population is roughly normal; for n of about 30 or more the CLT makes it safe anyway.

One-sample tests, including a proportion

The one-sample z- and t-tests use the formulas above. For a proportion, with large n, the test statistic is

z = (p̂ − p₀) / √(p₀ (1 − p₀) / n)      valid when n p₀ ≥ 10 and n (1 − p₀) ≥ 10

Example: a payment gateway promises a failure rate of at most 2%. In 1,000 UPI transactions, 29 fail. H₀: p = 0.02, H₁: p > 0.02. p̂ = 0.029, SE = √(0.02 × 0.98 / 1000) = 0.00443, z = 0.009 / 0.00443 = 2.03 > 1.645, so reject H₀ at 5% (p = 0.021): failures exceed the promise.

Two independent samples

Anna Univ lists this as the "t-test for two independent samples": two separate groups, such as two sections or two app versions. H₀: μ₁ = μ₂. With large samples use z = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / √(σ₁²/n₁ + σ₂²/n₂). With small samples and similar spreads, pool the two variances:

sp² = [(n₁ − 1) s₁² + (n₂ − 1) s₂²] / (n₁ + n₂ − 2)      pooled variance
t   = (x̄₁ − x̄₂) / (sp √(1/n₁ + 1/n₂))                 df = n₁ + n₂ − 2

If the spreads differ clearly, use Welch's t-test, with SE = √(s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂) and a df that software computes. R uses Welch by default; SciPy's ttest_ind pools unless you pass equal_var=False.

Two related (paired) samples

When the same subjects are measured twice (before and after coaching) or come in matched pairs (twins, left and right eye), compute the difference d for each pair and run a one-sample t-test on the d values against 0:

t = d̄ / (s_d / √n)        n = number of pairs, df = n − 1

Pairing removes the person-to-person variation, so a paired test is much more sensitive. Anna Univ calls this the "t-test for two related samples".

When data are far from normal and n is small, rank-based non-parametric tests replace t: Mann–Whitney U for two independent samples, Wilcoxon signed-rank for paired data. IITB lists non-parametric tests here.

Question. A phone maker claims a mean battery life of 10 hours on a standard test. A reviewer tests 10 phones: 9.2, 9.8, 10.1, 9.5, 9.0, 9.7, 9.9, 9.4, 9.6 and 9.3 hours. At α = 0.05, is the mean life less than 10 hours?

  1. H₀: μ = 10; H₁: μ < 10 (left-tailed). σ is unknown and n = 10, so use t with df = 9.
  2. x̄ = 95.5 / 10 = 9.55 h.
  3. Deviations: −0.35, 0.25, 0.55, −0.05, −0.55, 0.15, 0.35, −0.15, 0.05, −0.25. Their squares add to 1.025, so s² = 1.025 / 9 = 0.1139 and s = 0.3375 h.
  4. SE = 0.3375 / √10 = 0.3375 / 3.162 = 0.1067 h. t = (9.55 − 10) / 0.1067 = −4.22.
  5. Critical value for df = 9, one-tailed 5%: −1.833. Since −4.22 < −1.833, reject H₀ (p = 0.0011).
  6. The mean battery life is below 10 hours. A 95% interval is 9.55 ± 2.262 × 0.1067 = 9.55 ± 0.24, that is 9.31 to 9.79 h.

  • In the proportion test the SE uses p₀ from H₀; in the confidence interval it uses p̂.

4.6 Chi-square test

The chi-square (χ²) test works on counts, not means: how many shoppers chose UPI, card or cash; how many times each face of a die came up. It asks whether the observed counts are too far from the counts a hypothesis predicts to be explained by chance. It has two everyday uses: goodness of fit (does one categorical variable follow a claimed pattern?) and independence (are two categorical variables related?). Data scientists also use the χ² score to choose categorical features for a model.

You need: the test procedure from Chapter 4.4, and frequency tables from Unit 3, Frequency distributions.

The chi-square statistic and distribution

For each category (or cell of a table), compare the observed count O with the expected count E that H₀ predicts:

χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E        sum over all categories or cells

Each term is a squared gap scaled by what was expected, so a gap of 10 counts more when only 20 were expected than when 500 were. χ² is 0 when every O equals its E and grows as the fit gets worse. If H₀ is true, χ² approximately follows the chi-square distribution with the right degrees of freedom: it starts at 0, is skewed to the right, and has mean equal to df (Figure 4.6.1). Since only large values count against H₀, the test is always right-tailed.

Conditions: the data are counts of independent observations (not percentages or means), and every expected count is at least 5; merge small categories if not.

Goodness of fit

H₀: the population follows the claimed proportions p₁, p₂, …, pk. The expected counts are Ei = n × pi, and df = k − 1, minus one more for each parameter estimated from the data.

Example: a die is rolled 120 times and shows faces 1 to 6 with counts 25, 17, 15, 23, 24 and 16. If the die is fair, E = 120 / 6 = 20 for each face. χ² = (25 + 9 + 25 + 9 + 16 + 16) / 20 = 100 / 20 = 5.00, with df = 6 − 1 = 5. The critical value is 11.070 and 5.00 is far below it (p = 0.42), so we fail to reject H₀: no evidence that the die is unfair.

Test of independence in a contingency table

A contingency table counts observations by two categorical variables, with r rows and c columns. H₀: the two variables are independent. If they are, the share of each column should be the same in every row, which gives

E(row i, column j) = (row i total × column j total) / grand total
df = (r − 1)(c − 1)

A significant χ² shows the variables are associated; it does not show cause, and on its own it does not say how strong the link is. Cramér's V = √(χ² / (n × (min(r, c) − 1))) measures strength from 0 (none) to 1 (perfect). Look at the cell contributions (O − E)² / E to see where the association lies. Anna Univ lists "introduction to chi-square tests" after ANOVA, and NITK lists the chi-square test as one of its three tests; both mean these two uses.

NIT Trichy reaches this test through "chi-square based filtering". In a classification problem, cross-tabulate each categorical feature against the class label and compute χ². A large χ² means the feature and the class are not independent, so the feature carries information about the class; a small one means it can be dropped. scikit-learn's SelectKBest(chi2, k=…) keeps the k highest-scoring features (the features must be non-negative counts or indicators). 4.7 ANOVA and the F-test

To compare three or more group means (three revision methods, four fertilisers, five ad designs), running a t-test on every pair raises the chance of a false alarm. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests all the means at once. It compares how far the group means spread from each other with how far individual values spread inside their groups; the ratio is the F statistic. The F distribution also tests two variances. Anna Univ titles its unit "Analysis of Variance"; NITK, Manipal and SRM also list ANOVA.

You need: t-tests from Chapter 4.5 and the test procedure from Chapter 4.4.

Why not many t-tests? The F distribution

Three groups give 3 pairs; five groups give 10. Each test at α = 0.05 has its own 5% false-alarm chance, so over 3 independent tests the chance of at least one is 1 − 0.95³ = 0.14, and over 10 it is 0.40. ANOVA asks one question at level α.

The F distribution is the distribution of a ratio of two independent variance estimates. It is never negative, is skewed to the right, and has two degrees of freedom: df₁ for the numerator and df₂ for the denominator. If the two variances are equal, F is close to 1.

F-test for two variances. H₀: σ₁² = σ₂². Compute F = s₁² / s₂² with the larger variance on top, and df = (n₁ − 1, n₂ − 1). Example: machine 1 fills 13 bottles with s₁² = 16 ml², machine 2 fills 11 with s₂² = 6.4 ml². F = 16 / 6.4 = 2.50 with df (12, 10). For H₁: σ₁² > σ₂² at 5%, the critical value F(0.05; 12, 10) is 2.913; 2.50 is smaller, so we fail to reject (p = 0.078). For a two-tailed test use F(0.025; 12, 10) = 3.621.

One-way ANOVA

There are k groups, nᵢ values in group i, N values in all; x̄ᵢ is a group mean and x̄ the grand mean. H₀: μ₁ = μ₂ = … = μk. H₁: at least one mean differs. The total variation splits into two parts:

SST = SSB + SSW
SST = Σ (x − x̄)²             total sum of squares,     df = N − 1
SSB = Σ nᵢ (x̄ᵢ − x̄)²         between groups,           df = k − 1
SSW = Σ Σ (x − x̄ᵢ)²          within groups (error),    df = N − k
MSB = SSB / (k − 1)    MSW = SSW / (N − k)    F = MSB / MSW

MSW estimates the common variance σ² whatever the means are. MSB estimates σ² too when H₀ is true, but grows when the means differ. So F is near 1 under H₀, and a large F is evidence against it: the test is right-tailed, with df (k − 1, N − k). Results go in an ANOVA table (Source, SS, df, MS, F), as in the worked example.

For hand calculation, the shortcut formulas avoid fractions: with grand total T and group totals Tᵢ, the correction factor is CF = T² / N, SST = Σx² − CF and SSB = Σ(Tᵢ² / nᵢ) − CF.

ANOVA assumes independent observations, roughly normal groups and equal variances (Levene's test checks the last). A significant F says only that some mean differs; a post-hoc test such as Tukey's HSD finds which pairs. The effect size η² = SSB / SST is the share of variation explained by the groups.

Two-factor ANOVA and its three F-tests

Anna Univ lists "two-factor experiments" and "three F-tests". Here two factors act together, say factor A, revision method (a levels), and factor B, time of study, morning or night (b levels), with r students in each of the a × b cells. The total splits four ways:

SST = SSA + SSB + SSAB + SSE
df:   a − 1,  b − 1,  (a − 1)(b − 1),  ab(r − 1);   total abr − 1
F_A = MSA / MSE    F_B = MSB / MSE    F_AB = MSAB / MSE

The three F-tests ask: does factor A matter (main effect of A)? Does factor B matter (main effect of B)? Does the effect of A depend on the level of B (interaction)? Read the interaction first: if past papers help a lot in the morning but little at night, a main effect alone misleads. An interaction plot with non-parallel lines shows it.

In Python, scipy.stats.f_oneway runs one-way ANOVA; for two factors, statsmodels fits a linear model and prints the table with anova_lm.

from scipy import stats
a = [5, 7, 6, 8, 4]; b = [8, 9, 7, 10, 11]; c = [6, 5, 7, 4, 8]
F, p = stats.f_oneway(a, b, c)
print(f"F = {F:.2f}, p = {p:.4f}")
F = 6.00, p = 0.0156

Worked example

Question. Fifteen students are split at random among three revision methods and then take the same quiz (out of 12). A, re-reading notes: 5, 7, 6, 8, 4. B, solving past papers: 8, 9, 7, 10, 11. C, watching videos: 6, 5, 7, 4, 8. At α = 0.05, do the mean scores differ?

  1. H₀: μA = μB = μC; H₁: at least one mean differs. k = 3, nᵢ = 5, N = 15.
  2. Group totals 30, 45, 30; means 6, 9, 6. Grand total T = 105, grand mean 7.
  3. SSB = 5 × [(6 − 7)² + (9 − 7)² + (6 − 7)²] = 5 × (1 + 4 + 1) = 30.
  4. SSW: A: 1 + 1 + 0 + 4 + 4 = 10; B: 1 + 0 + 4 + 1 + 4 = 10; C: 0 + 1 + 1 + 4 + 4 = 10. SSW = 30. Check: Σx² = 795, CF = 105² / 15 = 735, SST = 795 − 735 = 60 = 30 + 30.
  5. MSB = 30 / 2 = 15; MSW = 30 / 12 = 2.5; F = 15 / 2.5 = 6.00 with df (2, 12).
  6. F(0.05; 2, 12) = 3.885. 6.00 > 3.885: reject H₀ (p = 0.016). The methods do not all give the same mean; past papers (mean 9) look best, which a Tukey test would confirm. η² = 30 / 60 = 0.50.

Exam traps

  • H₁ is "at least one mean differs", not "all the means differ".
  • Within-groups df is N − k, not N − 1; the df must add up: 2 + 12 = 14.
  • ANOVA's F is right-tailed. An F below 1 is never significant.
  • A significant F does not say which groups differ; that needs a post-hoc test.
  • In the variance F-test, put the larger variance on top and keep the df in the same order.

Takeaways

  1. Inference reasons from a random sample to a population. A bigger sample shrinks chance error but never cures bias.
  2. Every statistic has a sampling distribution; its standard deviation, the standard error, falls as 1 / √n.
  3. The central limit theorem makes x̄ approximately normal for large n, which is why z and t methods work so widely.
  4. Maximum likelihood picks the parameter that makes the data most probable; Bayes combines a prior with the likelihood. With plenty of data they agree.
  5. A test fixes α first, computes a statistic, and rejects H₀ when p ≤ α. "Fail to reject" never means "H₀ is proved".
  6. Choose the test by the data: one or two means → z or t (paired when the same subjects are measured twice); three or more means → ANOVA; counts in categories → χ².
  7. χ² and ANOVA F-tests are right-tailed; z and t tests can be one- or two-tailed, decided from the question.
  8. Report an effect size and a confidence interval with every p-value: significant is not the same as important.

Glossary

Population
The complete set of units a question is about; size N.
Sample
The part of the population actually observed; size n.
Sampling frame
The list from which the sample is drawn.
Parameter
A fixed, usually unknown number describing a population, such as μ or σ.
Statistic
A number computed from a sample, such as x̄ or s; it varies between samples.
Estimator
A rule that turns a sample into a guess for a parameter.
Stratified sampling
Random samples taken separately inside groups (strata) that differ from each other.
Cluster sampling
Whole groups chosen at random and every unit in them measured.
Bias
A steady push of a statistic away from the parameter that a larger sample does not remove.
Sampling distribution
The distribution of a statistic over all possible samples of one size.
Standard error
The standard deviation of a sampling distribution; for the mean, σ / √n.
Central limit theorem
For large n, the sample mean is approximately normal whatever the population's shape.
Confidence interval
A range built so that a stated share (such as 95%) of such ranges contain the parameter.
Likelihood
The probability of the observed data, viewed as a function of the parameter.
Maximum likelihood estimate
The parameter value that makes the observed data most probable.
Prior, posterior
Beliefs about a parameter before and after seeing the data, linked by Bayes' rule.
Credible interval
A range holding a stated probability of the posterior distribution.
Null hypothesis (H₀)
The "no effect, no difference" claim that a test tries to reject; it holds the equality.
Alternative hypothesis (H₁)
The claim the test looks for evidence of; one- or two-tailed.
Significance level (α)
The accepted probability of rejecting a true H₀, fixed in advance.
p-value
The probability, if H₀ is true, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed.
Type I, Type II error
Rejecting a true H₀ (probability α); failing to reject a false H₀ (probability β).
Power
The probability of rejecting H₀ when it is false, 1 − β.
Degrees of freedom
The number of independent values left after estimating what the statistic needs.
t distribution
A bell curve with heavier tails than the normal, used when σ is estimated by s.
Contingency table
A table of counts classified by two categorical variables.
F distribution
The distribution of a ratio of two independent variance estimates, with two df.
ANOVA
Analysis of variance: tests equality of several means by comparing between-group and within-group variation.
Interaction
In two-factor ANOVA, when the effect of one factor depends on the level of the other.
