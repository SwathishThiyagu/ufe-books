1.1 What data science is: need, benefits and uses

Data science is the work of turning data into knowledge and decisions. It joins three skills: programming, to collect and handle data; mathematics and statistics, to find patterns and say how sure we are; and knowledge of the field the data comes from, so that the questions and answers make sense. It exists because organisations now record almost everything they do, and no person can read millions of rows. It is used wherever evidence can improve a decision, from UPI fraud checks to rain forecasts.

Defining data science

A working definition: data science is the use of scientific methods, statistics and computing to extract useful knowledge from data and act on it. "Scientific" matters. A data scientist states a question, collects evidence, tests an answer and reports how certain it is, as in a laboratory. The product is not the data or the chart; it is a decision, a forecast or an app feature that is better because of the data.

The three skills are often drawn as overlapping circles, after a well-known diagram by Drew Conway. Each overlap of two skills without the third is something else. Programming plus statistics, without domain knowledge, is machine learning: powerful methods with no feel for which question matters. Statistics plus domain knowledge, without programming, is traditional research: sound, but slow on large data. Programming plus domain knowledge, without statistics, is the danger zone: code that produces confident numbers nobody has tested. Data science is the centre, where all three meet.

The need for data science

  1. Data is produced everywhere. Every UPI payment, IRCTC booking, phone call and app tap leaves a record.
  2. Storage and computing became cheap. Cloud services rent thousands of processors by the hour, so analysing all the data, not a small sample, is affordable.
  3. Free tools. Python (pandas, scikit-learn) and R put strong methods on every laptop.
  4. Decisions under uncertainty. Managers must decide before the future is known. Data science puts a number on the risk ("80% chance this loan is repaid").
  5. Competition. A firm that sets prices and stock from evidence beats one that relies on habit.

Data science tasks fall into a few classes. Classification predicts a category (is this payment fraud?). Regression predicts a number (the price of a used car). Clustering groups similar items without labels (customer segments). Association analysis finds items that go together (bread with butter). Anomaly detection flags the unusual (a sudden spike in a meter reading). Time series forecasting predicts the next values of a sequence (next week's demand). Tasks that learn from past examples with known answers are supervised; clustering and association, which have no answers to learn from, are unsupervised. Unit 5 teaches the algorithms for these tasks.

Benefits and uses

The benefits fall under six heads: better decisions, lower cost, personal service, early warning of risk, new products, and discovery of patterns nobody suspected. The uses, sector by sector:

  • Banking: fraud scores for each card or UPI payment; credit scores for loans.
  • Retail: recommendations ("customers who bought this also bought"), demand forecasts for warehouses.
  • Healthcare: reading X-rays and eye scans, tracking dengue outbreaks.
  • Transport: travel times in map apps, the chance a waitlisted ticket is confirmed.
  • Energy: forecasting electricity demand so that a city like Chennai can buy enough power for hot afternoons and avoid power cuts.
  • Agriculture: rainfall forecasts, crop yield estimates, pest alerts by SMS.
  • Sports: cricket field placements, bowling plans, auction values.

Worked example

Question (8 marks). An online grocery app has 1,00,000 customers. Each promotional SMS costs ₹2, and each customer who orders after it brings a margin of ₹150. If the app messages everyone, 2% order. A data science model ranks customers by their chance of ordering; if the app messages only the top 20,000, 7% of them order. (a) Find the net gain (margin minus SMS cost) of each plan. (b) Find the margin earned per rupee spent and the lift of the model. (c) State one more benefit and one risk of the targeted plan.

Solution.

  1. Mass plan. Cost = 1,00,000 × ₹2 = ₹2,00,000. Orders = 2% of 1,00,000 = 2,000. Margin = 2,000 × ₹150 = ₹3,00,000. Net gain = ₹3,00,000 − ₹2,00,000 = ₹1,00,000.
  2. Targeted plan. Cost = 20,000 × ₹2 = ₹40,000. Orders = 7% of 20,000 = 1,400. Margin = 1,400 × ₹150 = ₹2,10,000. Net gain = ₹2,10,000 − ₹40,000 = ₹1,70,000.
  3. Margin per rupee: mass = 3,00,000 ÷ 2,00,000 = ₹1.50; targeted = 2,10,000 ÷ 40,000 = ₹5.25. Lift = response rate of the targeted group ÷ overall response rate = 7% ÷ 2% = 3.5: the chosen customers are 3.5 times as likely to order.
  4. The targeted plan gets 600 fewer orders but earns ₹70,000 more. Another benefit: 80,000 customers are not disturbed, so fewer of them block the app's messages. Risk: the model learns from past behaviour; if it is wrong or biased, good customers are never contacted. A small random control group, messaged anyway, shows whether the model still works.

Exam traps

  • Data science is not a synonym for big data or machine learning. Machine learning is one tool inside it; big data is a problem of size and speed.
  • "Explain the need for data science" wants reasons (volume, cheap computing, free tools, uncertainty, competition), not only a definition.
  • For "benefits and uses", give sector-wise examples that name the decision being improved; a list of buzzwords scores low.
  • Students swap the two side overlaps in the Venn diagram. Danger zone = programming + domain, no statistics. Traditional research = statistics + domain, no programming.
  • Algorithm and model are different words: k-means is an algorithm; the cluster centres it finds for your data are the model.

1.2 Facets of data: structured, semi-structured and unstructured

Data comes in different forms, called its facets. The most important split is by how much structure it carries. Structured data fits a fixed table of rows and columns; semi-structured data carries its own labels, as in JSON and XML; unstructured data, such as free text, photos and sound, has no predefined model at all. The form decides how you store the data, how you query it and which methods can analyse it, so it is the first thing a data scientist checks.

Data, information and knowledge

Data is raw recorded values with no context: 38.2. Information is data given context and meaning: "the temperature in Chennai at 3 p.m. on 12 May was 38.2 °C". Knowledge is a pattern learned from much information that guides action: "when the afternoon temperature passes 38 °C, Chennai's power demand peaks, so buy extra power for those days". Some books add a fourth level, wisdom (knowing when to apply the knowledge), and draw the four as the DIKW pyramid. Data science is the climb from the bottom of this pyramid to the top.

The three levels of structure

Structured data follows a schema, a fixed description of the columns and their types, decided before any data is stored. Every row of a railway booking table has the same columns: PNR, name, train number, fare. Relational databases (MySQL, Oracle, PostgreSQL), CSV files and spreadsheets hold structured data, and SQL queries it directly.

Semi-structured data has no fixed table, but each value carries a label (a key or a tag) that says what it is. Records may differ: one booking has a meal choice, another does not. The data is self-describing. JSON (from web APIs), XML, email headers and many log files are semi-structured. Document databases such as MongoDB store it (Unit 6).

Unstructured data has no predefined data model. A review, a scanned form, a voice note or a CCTV clip has internal pattern (grammar, pixels, sound waves) but no fields a database can query. Analysing it needs extra methods: natural language processing for text, computer vision for images, speech recognition for audio.

The seven facets in the textbook

Cielen, Meysman and Ali's Introducing Data Science names seven facets. Each one maps onto the three levels above:

  • Structured: tables with a schema.
  • Unstructured: content with no model, such as free-form documents.
  • Natural language: a special, hard kind of unstructured data (reviews, chats, tweets). Meaning depends on context, sarcasm and language; Indian text mixes Hindi, Tamil and English in one sentence.
  • Machine-generated: data created by computers and devices without a person typing: server logs, call records, smart-meter readings. Huge in volume, usually structured or semi-structured.
  • Graph-based (network): things joined by relationships, stored as nodes and edges: friends on a social network, stations on a metro map. Graph databases query it.
  • Audio, image and video: unstructured media that need speech and vision models, such as reading number plates from traffic cameras.
  • Streaming: data that arrives continuously and is processed as it flows, such as live cricket scores or a stream of UPI payments. Streaming describes how data arrives, not its structure.

Reading semi-structured data in Python

The Python program below reads one booking in JSON and the same booking in XML, using only the standard library. In XML, values sit inside named tags (<name>) or in attributes (train="12657").

import json
import xml.etree.ElementTree as ET

js = '{"train": 12657, "name": "Priya", "fare": 845, "meals": ["veg"]}'
rec = json.loads(js)                  # JSON text -> Python dict
print(rec["name"], rec["fare"] + 25, rec["meals"][0])

xml = """<booking train="12657">
  <name>Priya</name>
  <fare currency="INR">845</fare>
</booking>"""
root = ET.fromstring(xml)             # XML text -> element tree
fare = root.find("fare")
print(root.tag, root.get("train"), root.find("name").text,
      fare.get("currency"), int(fare.text) + 25)

Output:

Priya 870 veg
booking 12657 Priya INR 870

JSON numbers arrive as numbers, so 845 + 25 works at once. XML text always arrives as a string, so the program converts it with int() first.

Worked example

Question (6 marks). (a) Classify each as structured, semi-structured or unstructured: (i) the IRCTC bookings table in a relational database, (ii) a weather API reply in JSON, (iii) a 30-second voice note, (iv) an email, (v) CCTV footage. (b) A booking is stored as the CSV line 4512367890,Priya S,12657,845 or as the JSON line {"pnr":"4512367890","name":"Priya S","train":12657,"fare":845}. Each line ends with one newline byte, and each character takes one byte. Find the storage for 10 lakh (1,000,000) bookings in each form and explain the difference.

Solution.

  1. (a) (i) Structured: fixed columns and a schema. (ii) Semi-structured: keys label each value. (iii) Unstructured: audio. (iv) Semi-structured: the headers (From, To, Date, Subject) are labelled fields, the body is free text. (v) Unstructured video; streaming if watched live.
  2. (b) CSV line: count the characters. 10 (PNR) + 1 + 7 ("Priya S") + 1 + 5 (train) + 1 + 3 (fare) = 28, plus the newline = 29 bytes.
  3. JSON line: the same values plus the keys, quotes, colons and braces = 62 characters, plus the newline = 63 bytes.
  4. For 1,000,000 bookings: CSV = 29,000,000 bytes = 29 MB; JSON = 63,000,000 bytes = 63 MB. Ratio = 63 ÷ 29 ≈ 2.17.
  5. The extra 34 bytes per booking are the key names and quotes, repeated in every record. This is the price of being self-describing: the CSV states its columns once (in a header line or a schema), JSON states them every time. Compression and column formats such as Parquet remove most of this overhead.

Exam traps

  • Semi-structured is not "partly unstructured". It is data whose structure travels with it in tags or keys instead of a fixed schema.
  • Unstructured does not mean "no pattern at all". A photo is a grid of pixels; it means no predefined model a database can query.
  • Machine-generated and streaming are facets of origin and arrival, not levels of structure. A sensor stream can be perfectly structured.
  • Email is the examiner's favourite borderline case. Answer "semi-structured" and say why: labelled headers, free-text body.
  • Data, information and knowledge: a bare number is data; once it has context it is information.

1.3 The data science process: goal to presentation

The data science process is the sequence of steps that takes a project from a question to a working result: set the research goal, retrieve the data, prepare it, explore it, build models, and present and automate the result. Following it stops a team from jumping to models before the question is clear or the data is clean.

You need: the forms data takes (Chapter 1.2).

The six steps

  1. Set the research goal. Agree with the people who need the result (the stakeholders) on what is to be found, why, and how success will be measured. Write it in a project charter: the research goal, the mission and context, how the analysis will be done, the resources needed, proof that it is achievable, the deliverables with a measure of success, and a timeline. "Cut food waste in the hostel mess by 15% this term" is a goal; "use AI in the mess" is not.
  2. Retrieve data. Find the data and get access to it. Look inside the organisation first: databases, data warehouses (cleaned data kept for reporting), data marts (a warehouse slice for one department) and data lakes (raw data in any form). Then look outside: open data, web APIs, purchased data (Chapter 1.5). Check quality as soon as it arrives.
  3. Prepare data. Three sub-steps. Cleansing removes errors: typing mistakes, impossible values (an age of 250), extra spaces, mixed codes ("M", "Male"), mixed units (grams and kilograms), duplicates, missing values and outliers. Integrating combines sources: joining two tables on a common key, or appending tables with the same columns. Transforming reshapes variables for modelling: new columns, log scales, dummy (0/1) variables for categories, fewer variables. Unit 2 teaches the tools.
  4. Explore data. Exploratory data analysis (EDA) looks at the data through summaries and plots (histograms, box plots, scatter plots) to see distributions, relationships and oddities. It often finds errors that step 3 missed. Unit 3 covers it.
  5. Build the model. Choose a model type and its input variables (features), fit it on part of the data, then diagnose it and compare models on data the model has not seen (a hold-out or test set). Units 4 and 5 teach the methods.

Worked example

Question (10 marks). A hostel mess for 320 students cooks 300 dinners every night and throws away the leftovers. The token system gives the dinners eaten over two weeks:

WeekMonTueWedThuFriSatSun
12502462542480232178172
2252blank250 (two rows)244228182176

Apply the six steps to plan how many dinners to cook. Use week 1 to build the forecast and week 2 to test it. Food costs ₹40 a plate.

Solution.

  1. Goal. Forecast dinners for each day and cook the forecast plus a 5% buffer. Success: no day runs short, and at least 15% fewer plates are cooked than the 2,100 a week cooked now (7 × 300).
  2. Retrieve. Export the 15 rows from the token system as a CSV file (structured data).
  3. Prepare. Thursday week 1 = 2,480 is impossible with 320 students; the paper register shows 248, a typing error, so correct it. Delete the duplicate Wednesday row. Tuesday week 2 is blank (machine down): leave it out. Transform: add a column "day type" = weekday (Mon–Fri) or weekend. 13 clean rows remain.
  4. Explore. Week 1 weekday mean = (250 + 246 + 254 + 248 + 232) ÷ 5 = 1,230 ÷ 5 = 246. Weekend mean = (178 + 172) ÷ 2 = 175. Pattern: about 70 fewer students eat on weekends.
  5. Model. Forecast = week 1 mean for that day type. Test on week 2's six days. Absolute errors: weekdays |252 − 246| = 6, |250 − 246| = 4, |244 − 246| = 2, |228 − 246| = 18; weekend |182 − 175| = 7, |176 − 175| = 1. Mean absolute error (MAE) = 38 ÷ 6 = 6.3 plates. The old rule "always 300" has errors 48, 50, 56, 72, 118, 124: MAE = 468 ÷ 6 = 78 plates.
  6. Present. Cook 246 × 1.05 = 258.3 → 259 on weekdays and 175 × 1.05 = 183.75 → 184 on weekends (round up). Weekly plates = 5 × 259 + 2 × 184 = 1,663, a saving of 2,100 − 1,663 = 437 plates (20.8%), worth 437 × ₹40 = ₹17,480 a week. Check: week 2's highest weekday (252) and weekend (182) counts are below 259 and 184, so no day runs short. Goal met. Automate: a script reads each day's token count and prints the next day's number for the cook.

Exam traps

  • The process is a loop, not a straight line. A diagram without feedback arrows loses marks.
  • Students skip step 1. Without a measurable goal you cannot say whether the project worked.
  • Cleansing, integrating and transforming are three sub-steps of data preparation, not three separate steps of the process.
  • Testing a model on the same rows it was built from makes it look better than it is; always test on unseen data.
  • Do not mix Cielen's six steps with CRISP-DM's six phases in one answer. Name your version.

1.4 Types of analytics: descriptive, predictive and prescriptive

Analytics is the use of data to answer questions, and the questions come at increasing depth. Descriptive analytics asks what happened; predictive analytics asks what is likely to happen; prescriptive analytics asks what we should do about it. Many books add diagnostic analytics (why did it happen?) between the first two. Each type needs more data, more skill and more trust in the method, and gives more value to the decision.

You need: the steps of a project (Chapter 1.3).

The four types

TypeQuestionTypical methodsExample
DescriptiveWhat happened?Counts, totals, means, percentages, charts, dashboardsTickets booked per day last month
DiagnosticWhy did it happen?Drill-down, segment comparison, correlationWhy sales fell in November
PredictiveWhat will happen?Regression, classification, time series forecastingChance that a loan is not repaid
PrescriptiveWhat should we do?Optimisation, simulation, expected-value decisionsHow many trains to run on a festival day

Descriptive analytics summarises data that already exists. A college's pass percentage per subject, a shop's monthly sales, a hospital's beds in use: all descriptive. Most reports and dashboards in any organisation are descriptive, and every other type starts from them. Unit 3 teaches its tools.

Diagnostic analytics explains a descriptive result. If a supermarket chain's November sales fell, a drill-down (from state to city to store) may show that only Chennai stores near flooded roads fell. Comparing these stores with others, and checking the rainfall data, points to a cause. Correlation alone does not prove cause; the analyst checks other explanations before acting.

Predictive analytics estimates a value we do not know yet, usually a future one, from patterns in past data. The answer is an estimate with uncertainty: "152 puffs, give or take 5", "a 12% chance this borrower defaults". Prediction is not always about the future: a fraud model predicts whether a payment happening now is genuine. Unit 5 teaches the models.

Prescriptive analytics recommends an action. It takes a prediction, adds the goal (most profit, least waste) and the limits (budget, staff, stock), and compares the options. Methods include optimisation such as linear programming, simulation of what-if scenarios, and expected-value decisions. Dynamic pricing of tickets and route planning for delivery vans are prescriptive.

How the types work together

The types are not rivals; a project climbs them. A railway first describes past bookings for a festival week, diagnoses why some trains overflowed, predicts this year's demand per route, and finally prescribes how many extra coaches to attach to each train within the coaches available. Skipping a step is risky: a prediction built without first describing the data can miss a data error, and a prescription is only as good as the prediction inside it. As we climb, the decision moves from people (who read a dashboard and decide) towards the system (which recommends or even takes the action).

Worked example

Question (12 marks). A bakery in Coimbatore sold these puffs on six days: 120, 128, 131, 137, 140, 148. A puff costs ₹12 to make and sells for ₹20; unsold puffs are thrown away. (a) Descriptive: find the total, the mean and the rise from day 1 to day 6. (b) Predictive: fit the least-squares line y = a + bx, with x the day number and y the puffs sold, and forecast day 7. (c) Prescriptive: the owner believes day-7 demand will be 147, 152 or 157 with probabilities 0.25, 0.5 and 0.25. Should she bake 147, 152 or 157 puffs?

Solution.

  1. (a) Total = 120 + 128 + 131 + 137 + 140 + 148 = 804. Mean = 804 ÷ 6 = 134 puffs a day. Rise = 148 − 120 = 28 puffs (23% more).
  2. (b) Means: x̄ = (1 + 2 + … + 6) ÷ 6 = 3.5, ȳ = 134. Deviations x − x̄: −2.5, −1.5, −0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 2.5. Deviations y − ȳ: −14, −6, −3, 3, 6, 14.
  3. Slope b = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) ÷ Σ(x − x̄)². Products: 35, 9, 1.5, 1.5, 9, 35, sum 91. Squares: 6.25, 2.25, 0.25, 0.25, 2.25, 6.25, sum 17.5. So b = 91 ÷ 17.5 = 5.2 puffs per day.
  4. Intercept a = ȳ − b·x̄ = 134 − 5.2 × 3.5 = 134 − 18.2 = 115.8. Line: ŷ = 115.8 + 5.2x. Forecast: ŷ(7) = 115.8 + 36.4 = 152.2 ≈ 152 puffs.
  5. (c) Profit = 20 × (puffs sold) − 12 × (puffs baked), where puffs sold = the smaller of baked and demand. Bake 147: every case sells 147, profit = 147 × 8 = ₹1,176. Expected profit = ₹1,176.
  6. Bake 152: demand 147 gives 2,940 − 1,824 = ₹1,116; demand 152 or 157 gives 3,040 − 1,824 = ₹1,216. Expected = 0.25 × 1,116 + 0.75 × 1,216 = 279 + 912 = ₹1,191.
  7. Bake 157: demand 147 gives 2,940 − 1,884 = ₹1,056; 152 gives ₹1,156; 157 gives ₹1,256. Expected = 264 + 578 + 314 = ₹1,156.
  8. Decision: bake 152 puffs, the highest expected profit (₹1,191). Part (a) described, part (b) predicted, part (c) prescribed: the same data answered three different questions.

Exam traps

  • Predictive says what will happen; prescriptive says what to do. "Demand will be 152" is predictive; "bake 152" is prescriptive.
  • If a question asks for "three types", give descriptive, predictive and prescriptive, then mention diagnostic in one line for an extra mark.
  • Descriptive is not "the useless one". Every other type depends on it, and most dashboards are descriptive.
  • Prediction is not only about the future: classifying today's payment as fraud is predictive.
  • In the least-squares slope, divide by Σ(x − x̄)², not by n. Here 91 ÷ 17.5 = 5.2; the rise per day from first to last point (28 ÷ 5 = 5.6) is a different, cruder number.

Prescriptive analytics is the bridge to operations research: linear and integer programming, queueing models and simulation. Airlines and railways use revenue management systems that set fares seat by seat, and delivery firms solve vehicle routing problems every morning. 1.5 Data sources and data collection

A data source is where data comes from: a survey, a sensor, a company database, a government portal, a web API or a web page. Data collection is the planned gathering of data from those sources for a stated goal. Every later step depends on it: a model built on badly collected data gives confident wrong answers ("garbage in, garbage out"). This chapter is step 2 of the process, "retrieving data". Some syllabi list it as "Data Acquisition: gather information from different sources; Web APIs; Open Data Sources; Web Scrapping", others as "Data sources; data collection".

You need: step 2 of the process (Chapter 1.3) and the forms of data (Chapter 1.2).

Primary and secondary, internal and external

Primary data is collected by you, for your goal: you control what is measured and how. It costs time and money but fits the question exactly. Secondary data was collected earlier by someone else for another purpose: company records, census tables, a public dataset. It is quick and cheap, but you must check what it really measures, how old it is and what is missing.

A second split is by owner. Internal sources belong to the organisation (its sales database, app logs, HR records); external sources come from outside (open government data, purchased data, social media). Look inside first: the data is free and about your own customers.

  • Primary methods: surveys and questionnaires (a Google Form), interviews, observation (counting vehicles at a junction), experiments such as an A/B test (show two versions of an app screen to two random halves of users and compare), and sensors or IoT devices you install.
  • Secondary sources: internal databases and logs; open data portals such as India's Open Government Data platform (data.gov.in) and Census of India tables; public dataset repositories such as the UCI Machine Learning Repository and Kaggle; web APIs; web pages; data bought from vendors.

Big data sources (Unit 6) are mostly machine-made and continuous: click logs, sensors, social media, payments. They differ in scale and speed, not in kind.

Collecting from the web: open data, APIs and scraping

Open data portals publish files (CSV, JSON, XML) for anyone to download, under a stated licence. A web API (application programming interface) is a documented address that returns data to programs: you send a request such as …/readings?station=Alandur&date=2026-05-12, usually with an API key, and get a JSON reply. APIs limit how many requests you may send per minute (a rate limit) and split long results into pages (pagination). Web scraping downloads pages written for people and pulls values out of their HTML tags. Use it only when no API or file exists, follow the site's terms and its robots.txt file, and expect the code to break when the page design changes.

The Python program below handles both kinds of reply. In a real program each string would come from requests.get(url).text; here they are typed in so the output is fixed. It uses the json module and the BeautifulSoup library.

import json
from bs4 import BeautifulSoup

# 1. Web API: the reply is JSON text
reply = '{"station": "Alandur", "readings": [{"hour": 9, "pm25": 41}, {"hour": 10, "pm25": 58}]}'
data = json.loads(reply)
print(data["station"], [r["pm25"] for r in data["readings"]])

# 2. Web scraping: the page is HTML; pick out the table cells
page = """<table>
  <tr><td>Chennai</td><td>34</td></tr>
  <tr><td>Madurai</td><td>36</td></tr>
</table>"""
soup = BeautifulSoup(page, "html.parser")
for row in soup.find_all("tr"):
    city, temp = [td.text for td in row.find_all("td")]
    print(city, int(temp))

Output:

Alandur [41, 58]
Chennai 34
Madurai 36

Collecting well: quality, bias and consent

Check quality while collecting, when errors are cheapest to fix. The usual dimensions are accuracy (values are correct), completeness (nothing expected is missing), consistency (the same fact agrees across sources), timeliness (recent enough), validity (values fit their rules, such as a 6-digit PIN code) and uniqueness (no duplicates).

Most collection takes a sample rather than everyone (Unit 4 treats sampling). The danger is bias: a sample that differs from the population in a way that matters. An online survey misses people without smartphones (selection bias); a complaints box hears mostly from angry customers (voluntary-response bias); people who do not reply may differ from those who do (non-response bias). A bigger sample does not cure bias; a better method does.

Personal data needs consent and a clear purpose. In India the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 sets these duties. Collect only what the goal needs, remove names and phone numbers before analysis where possible, and credit the licence of open data (Unit 6 covers governance).

Worked example

Question (10 marks). A student team studies air quality in Chennai. (a) They install 20 PM2.5 sensors, each sending one reading every 10 minutes for 30 days; each reading is stored as a 40-byte row. Find the expected rows and storage. Only 84,240 rows arrive: find the completeness. (b) They also need 3 years of hourly readings for 10 government stations from a web API that returns 500 records per request and allows 100 requests per minute. Find the number of requests and the minutes needed. (c) A survey form goes to 1,200 residents and 216 reply. Find the response rate and name one bias. (d) Classify each source as primary or secondary.

Solution.

  1. (a) Readings per sensor per day = 6 per hour × 24 = 144. For 30 days: 144 × 30 = 4,320. For 20 sensors: 4,320 × 20 = 86,400 rows. Storage = 86,400 × 40 = 3,456,000 bytes ≈ 3.46 MB.
  2. Completeness = 84,240 ÷ 86,400 = 0.975 = 97.5%. Missing = 2,160 rows = 144 × 15, exactly 15 days of one sensor: check for a sensor that went offline rather than random gaps.
  3. (b) Records = 10 stations × 24 hours × 365 days × 3 years = 2,62,800. Requests = 2,62,800 ÷ 500 = 525.6, rounded up to 526 (the last page is part-full).
  4. At 100 requests a minute: five full minutes give 500 requests and the last 26 go in the sixth minute, so the download needs 6 minutes. The script must pause between batches or the server refuses requests.
  5. (c) Response rate = 216 ÷ 1,200 = 18%. Bias: residents with breathing problems may be more likely to reply (voluntary-response bias), and an online form misses people without smartphones (selection bias).
  6. (d) Sensors and the survey: primary (collected by the team for this goal). Government station data from the API: secondary and external.

Exam traps

  • Primary or secondary depends on who collected the data and why, not on its format. The same CSV is primary for the agency that made it and secondary for you.
  • An API is not scraping. An API is a door the provider offers for programs; scraping reads pages meant for people.
  • Round the number of API requests up: 525.6 pages means 526 requests.
  • A large sample does not remove bias; a biased method gives the same wrong answer with more confidence.
  • "Public on the web" does not mean free to reuse. Check the licence and the terms of the site.

Takeaways

  1. Data science joins programming, statistics and domain knowledge to turn data into decisions. It is needed because data is plentiful, computing is cheap and decisions are uncertain.
  2. Data is raw values; information is data in context; knowledge is a pattern that guides action.
  3. Structure decides the tools: structured data uses a schema and SQL, semi-structured data carries its own keys (JSON, XML), unstructured data needs NLP, vision or speech methods.
  4. Every project runs goal, retrieve, prepare, explore, model, present, and loops back whenever a step exposes a problem.
  5. A goal must be measurable and written in a project charter; a model must be tested on data it has not seen.
  6. Descriptive analytics says what happened, diagnostic why, predictive what will happen, and prescriptive what to do; each builds on the one before.
  7. Primary data is collected for your goal; secondary data already exists. Check quality and bias while collecting, not after.
  8. Web data comes from open portals, APIs (with keys, rate limits and pages) and, only when nothing else exists, scraping within the site's terms.

Glossary

A/B test
An experiment that shows two versions to two random groups and compares an outcome.
Algorithm
A step-by-step recipe for a task, such as k-means for clustering.
Analytics
The use of data to answer questions and support decisions.
API (web)
A documented address that returns data to programs, usually as JSON.
Bias (sampling)
A systematic difference between the collected data and the population it should represent.
CRISP-DM
Cross-Industry Standard Process for Data Mining: a six-phase project model.
Data cleansing
Finding and fixing errors, duplicates and missing values.
Data lake
A store that keeps raw data of any form until it is needed.
Data mart
A slice of a data warehouse for one department.
Data warehouse
A store of cleaned, integrated data kept for reporting and analysis.
Descriptive analytics
Summaries of what has already happened.
Diagnostic analytics
Analysis of why something happened.
EDA
Exploratory data analysis: summaries and plots to understand data before modelling.
Facets of data
The different forms data takes, by structure, origin and arrival.
Hold-out set
Data kept aside and used only to test a model.
JSON
JavaScript Object Notation: text of keys and values, the common format of API replies.
Machine-generated data
Data created by devices and programs without a person typing it.
Model
What an algorithm produces from data; it makes predictions or summaries.
Pagination
Splitting a long API result into pages fetched one request at a time.
Predictive analytics
Estimating unknown or future values from patterns in past data.
Prescriptive analytics
Recommending the best action under a goal and constraints.
Primary data
Data you collect yourself for your goal.
Project charter
The document that fixes a project's goal, method, resources, deliverables, success measure and timeline.
Rate limit
The most requests an API accepts in a period.
Schema
A fixed description of a dataset's fields and their types.
Secondary data
Data collected earlier by someone else for another purpose.
Semi-structured data
Data that carries its own labels (keys or tags) instead of a fixed schema.
Streaming data
Data that arrives continuously and is processed as it flows.
Unstructured data
Data with no predefined model, such as text, images, audio and video.
Web scraping
Extracting values from the HTML of web pages written for people.
