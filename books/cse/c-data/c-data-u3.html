3.1 Frequency distributions and their graphs

A frequency distribution is a table of how many observations take each value, or fall in each class of values. It turns a long list of numbers into a shape you can see: where most values sit, how far they spread, whether any lie apart. Its graphs are the first pictures drawn of any numeric column.

Building a grouped frequency table

Group many values (marks, rupees, minutes) into classes of equal width:

  1. Range = largest value − smallest value.
  2. Number of classes k, usually 5 to 10. Sturges' rule gives a start: k ≈ 1 + 3.322 log₁₀ n, where n is the number of observations.
  3. Class width h ≈ range ÷ k, rounded up to a convenient number (5, 10, 20).
  4. Make classes non-overlapping. In the exclusive method, 40–50 holds 40 up to just below 50.
  5. Count each class (frequency f). Add relative frequency f/n and cumulative frequency, the running total.

The three graphs and the shape they show

  • Histogram: one bar per class, bars touching because the scale is continuous; bar height is the frequency.
  • Frequency polygon: frequency plotted at each class midpoint, (lower + upper)/2, and joined, closed at the empty classes' midpoints on either side.
  • Ogive (cumulative frequency curve): cumulative frequency plotted at each upper class boundary. Read across from n/2 to get the median, from n/4 and 3n/4 for the quartiles.

Then read the shape: symmetric (one central peak), positively skewed (long right tail, like incomes), negatively skewed (long left tail, like marks in an easy test) or bimodal (two peaks, often two groups mixed). An outlier sits apart across an empty gap; check it before deleting it (Unit 2, Handling errors and outliers).

3.2 Measures of central tendency and variability

A measure of central tendency is the one value that best represents a column: the mean, median or mode. A measure of variability says how far the values typically lie from that centre: range, interquartile range, variance and standard deviation. You need both, because two columns can share a centre and still differ completely: two batters who each average 40 runs, one scoring 35 to 45 every innings and the other 0 or 100.

You need: frequency tables and class midpoints (Chapter 3.1).

Mean, median and mode

  • Mean x̄ = Σx ÷ n, where Σx is the sum of the values and n their count. For a whole population it is written μ. A weighted mean Σwx ÷ Σw gives some values more say; your CGPA is grade points weighted by credits. For grouped data, x̄ = Σfm ÷ Σf, with m the class midpoint and f its frequency.
  • Median: sort the values; the median is the one at position (n + 1)/2. For even n, it is the average of the two middle values.
  • Mode: the most frequent value; there can be one, several or none. It is the only average for categories (the modal payment mode at a canteen is UPI).

Choose by shape: the mean for symmetric data without outliers (it uses every value), the median for skewed data or outliers (an extreme value hardly moves it), the mode for categories. In a positively skewed distribution the long right tail drags the mean up, so mean > median > mode; in a negatively skewed one the order reverses. For moderately skewed data Karl Pearson's empirical relation holds roughly: mode ≈ 3 × median − 2 × mean.

Range, quartiles and standard deviation

  • Range = maximum − minimum. Quick, but one outlier changes it completely.
  • Quartiles Q1, Q2 (the median) and Q3 cut the sorted data into four equal parts. Indian textbooks place Q1 at position (n + 1)/4 and Q3 at 3(n + 1)/4. The interquartile range IQR = Q3 − Q1 is the spread of the middle 50%, and outliers cannot move it. Tukey's rule calls a value an outlier if it lies below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR (the two "fences").
  • Variance is the average squared distance from the mean. Population: σ² = Σ(x − μ)² ÷ N. Sample: s² = Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1). The standard deviation is its square root, s = √s², and is back in the data's own units.
  • Coefficient of variation CV = (s ÷ x̄) × 100%. It has no units, so it compares spread between columns with different means or units: the batter with the lower CV is the more consistent one.

The n − 1 (Bessel's correction) is there because dividing by n makes the sample variance too small, on average, as an estimate of σ².

For qualitative (nominal) data there is no numeric spread; say how evenly the values spread across categories (the more even, the more variable). For ranked (ordinal) data such as ratings poor < fair < good < excellent, use the median and IQR of the ranks, not the mean and s, because the steps between ranks need not be equal.

Moments, skewness and kurtosis

The r-th central moment is mr = Σ(x − x̄)r ÷ n. The first is always 0, the second is the variance (divisor n); the third and fourth, made unit-free, measure lopsidedness and tail weight:

  • Skewness γ₁ = m₃ ÷ m₂3/2: 0 for symmetric data, positive for a long right tail. A quicker version is Pearson's coefficient Sk = 3(x̄ − median) ÷ s.
  • Kurtosis β₂ = m₄ ÷ m₂², which is 3 for a normal curve. Excess kurtosis β₂ − 3 above 0 means heavier tails than normal (leptokurtic), below 0 lighter (platykurtic).

Worked example

Question. Delivery times in minutes for 11 food orders in Chennai are 18, 22, 24, 25, 25, 27, 29, 31, 34, 36, 59. Find the mean, median, mode, range, quartiles, IQR, sample standard deviation and CV; test for outliers; find the moment skewness and say which centre and spread you would report. (10 marks)

  1. The data are sorted and n = 11. Mean x̄ = 330 ÷ 11 = 30 min. Median = 6th value = 27 min. Mode = 25 min (twice).
  2. Range = 59 − 18 = 41 min. Q1 = value at (11 + 1)/4 = 3rd = 24; Q3 = 9th = 34; IQR = 10 min.
  3. Fences: 24 − 1.5 × 10 = 9 and 34 + 1.5 × 10 = 49. Since 59 > 49, 59 is an outlier.
  4. Deviations d = x − 30: −12, −8, −6, −5, −5, −3, −1, 1, 4, 6, 29. Squares: 144, 64, 36, 25, 25, 9, 1, 1, 16, 36, 841; Σd² = 1198.
  5. s² = 1198 ÷ (11 − 1) = 119.8 min², so s = √119.8 = 10.95 min. CV = 10.95 ÷ 30 × 100 = 36.5%.
  6. Moments (divisor n): m₂ = 1198 ÷ 11 = 108.9; cubes of d add to 21 936, so m₃ = 21 936 ÷ 11 = 1994.2. γ₁ = 1994.2 ÷ 108.91.5 = 1994.2 ÷ 1136.6 = 1.75, a strong positive skew. Pearson's Sk = 3(30 − 27) ÷ 10.95 = 0.82, also positive.
  7. Report the median 27 min and IQR 10 min. Check: drop the 59 and the mean falls by 2.9 to 27.1, while the median moves by only 1, to 26.

Worked example 2: grouped mean

Question. Find the mean of the grouped marks in Chapter 3.1 (classes 30–40 to 80–90, f = 1, 3, 6, 9, 4, 2). (3 marks)

Exam traps

  • Sample variance divides by n − 1, population variance by N. pandas .std() uses n − 1; NumPy's np.std uses n unless you pass ddof=1.
  • Variance is in squared units (min²); give the standard deviation in the data's units.
  • For even n the median is the average of the two middle values, not either one of them.
  • pandas' skew() uses a small-sample correction (2.04 here, not 1.75) and kurt() returns excess kurtosis, so neither matches the hand formula exactly.
  • Favourite question: "Which average for income data, and why?" Median, because the right tail pulls the mean up.

3.3 Normal distribution and z-scores

The normal distribution is the bell-shaped curve that many measurements follow closely: heights, measurement errors, marks in a large exam and, by the central limit theorem (Unit 4), the averages of repeated samples. Two numbers fix it completely, the mean μ and the standard deviation σ. A z-score says how many standard deviations a value lies from the mean. That one number lets you compare values measured on different scales and read off probabilities from a single table.

You need: mean and standard deviation (Chapter 3.2).

The normal curve and the 68–95–99.7 rule

Its probability density function is

f(x) = (1 ÷ (σ√(2π))) × e^(−(x − μ)² ÷ (2σ²))

where x is a value, μ the mean, σ the standard deviation, π ≈ 3.1416 and e ≈ 2.7183. We write X ~ N(μ, σ²). You will not integrate it in an exam; you need its properties:

  • Bell-shaped and symmetric about μ, so mean = median = mode = μ.
  • Total area under the curve is 1; the area over an interval is the probability of landing in it.
  • The tails come ever closer to the axis but never touch it.
  • The curve changes from bending down to bending up (its inflection points) at μ − σ and μ + σ. A larger σ gives a lower, wider bell.

The empirical rule: 68.27% of values lie within μ ± σ, 95.45% within μ ± 2σ and 99.73% within μ ± 3σ. Exams often round these to 68, 95 and 99.7.

z-scores and the standard normal table

The z-score of a value x is z = (x − μ) ÷ σ. Positive z is above the mean, negative below; z = −2 means two standard deviations below. Converting every value to z gives the standard normal distribution Z ~ N(0, 1), so one table serves every normal variable. The table gives Φ(z) = P(Z ≤ z), the area to the left of z.

  1. Below a value: P(X ≤ x) = Φ(z).
  2. Above a value: P(X > x) = 1 − Φ(z).
  3. Between two values: P(a < X < b) = Φ(zb) − Φ(za).
  4. Negative z: Φ(−z) = 1 − Φ(z), by symmetry.
  5. Backwards, from a percentage to a value: find z from the table, then x = μ + zσ.

In data science, z-scores flag outliers (|z| > 3 is rare for normal data) and standardise columns before methods that compare distances, such as k-means (Unit 2, normalization; Unit 5, clustering).

Is the data normal? The QQ plot

Before using the rule, check the shape. A histogram gives a first look. For the i-th, find the normal quantile Φ⁻¹((i − 0.5) ÷ n); plot each data value against its quantile. Points on a straight line mean the data are close to normal. A curve bending upward means right skew; an S-shape with both ends pulling away means heavier tails than normal; one point far off the line is an outlier. In Python, scipy.stats.probplot(x, dist="norm", plot=plt) or statsmodels.api.qqplot(x, line="s") draws it.

Worked example

Question. Marks of 400 students in a Data Science end-semester exam are normally distributed with μ = 62 and σ = 8. (a) Find the z-score of 74. (b) How many students score above 74? (c) What percentage score between 54 and 70? (d) The pass mark is 50; how many fail? (e) The top 5% get an O grade; find the lowest whole mark for an O. (10 marks)

  1. (a) z = (74 − 62) ÷ 8 = 12 ÷ 8 = 1.5.
  2. (b) P(X > 74) = 1 − Φ(1.5) = 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668. Students = 0.0668 × 400 = 26.7, so about 27 students.
  3. (c) z for 54 = −1, for 70 = +1. P = Φ(1) − Φ(−1) = 0.8413 − 0.1587 = 0.6826, so 68.26% (the empirical rule's 68%).
  4. (d) z = (50 − 62) ÷ 8 = −1.5. P(X < 50) = Φ(−1.5) = 1 − 0.9332 = 0.0668; 0.0668 × 400 ≈ 27 students fail, the mirror image of (b).
  5. (e) We need Φ(z) = 0.95, so z = 1.645. x = 62 + 1.645 × 8 = 62 + 13.16 = 75.16. Marks are whole numbers, so the lowest O mark is 76.

Worked example 2: comparing two subjects

Question. Priya scored 78 in Maths (class μ = 65, σ = 10) and 70 in Physics (μ = 58, σ = 6). In which subject did she do relatively better? (4 marks)

z(Maths) = (78 − 65) ÷ 10 = 1.3; z(Physics) = (70 − 58) ÷ 6 = 2.0. Φ(1.3) = 0.9032 and Φ(2.0) = 0.9772, so she beat about 90% of the class in Maths and about 98% in Physics. Her lower raw mark, in Physics, is the stronger result.

Exam traps

  • Check your table. Some Indian tables give the area from 0 to z, which is Φ(z) − 0.5; add 0.5 before using the rules above.
  • "More than" needs 1 − Φ(z). Forgetting it gives 93.32% instead of 6.68%.
  • N(62, 64) means σ² = 64, so σ = 8, not 64.
  • Not all data are normal: incomes and waiting times are right-skewed. Check with a histogram or QQ plot before applying the 68–95–99.7 rule.

3.4 Correlation and covariance

Covariance and correlation measure whether two numeric variables move together: when one is above its mean, is the other usually above its mean too (positive), below it (negative), or neither? Correlation is covariance rescaled to lie between −1 and +1, so it can be compared across any pair of columns. Data scientists use it to spot related and redundant features, to check a straight-line model is worth fitting (Unit 5, regression) and, in finance, to see which stocks rise and fall together. Seven of the ten universities list this chapter.

You need: deviations and standard deviation (Chapter 3.2).

Covariance

For n pairs (x, y), the sample covariance is

cov(X, Y) = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) ÷ (n − 1)

where x̄ and ȳ are the two means (a population covariance divides by N). Each pair contributes the product of its two deviations. A point above both means, or below both, gives a positive product; a point above one mean and below the other gives a negative product. If most points lie in the first kind of region, the sum is positive.

Covariance carries the product of the two units (here hour × marks), so its size depends on the units: measure study time in minutes instead of hours and the covariance becomes 60 times larger, though nothing real changed. That is why it is hard to read on its own. Note that cov(X, X) = var(X). For many columns, the covariance matrix holds every variance on its diagonal and every covariance off it, and is symmetric; pandas gives it with df.cov().

Pearson's correlation coefficient r

r = cov(X, Y) ÷ (s_x × s_y)
  = Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) ÷ √[ Σ(x − x̄)² × Σ(y − ȳ)² ]

where sx and sy are the two standard deviations. Its properties are favourite exam questions:

  • −1 ≤ r ≤ +1. r = +1 or −1 only when every point lies on one straight line.
  • It has no units, and it does not change under a change of origin or scale (adding a constant to x, or multiplying x by a positive constant); a negative multiplier flips only its sign.
  • It measures linear association only. A perfect U-shaped pattern can give r = 0.
  • One outlier can raise or lower it a lot.
  • A rough reading: |r| ≥ 0.7 strong, 0.3 to 0.7 moderate, below 0.3 weak. Fields differ.

r², the coefficient of determination, is the share of the variation in y that a straight line on x accounts for. The line itself is Unit 5.

Spearman's rank correlation

For ranked data, or a relationship that always rises but not in a straight line, rank each variable from 1 to n and use

ρ = 1 − 6Σd² ÷ (n(n² − 1))

where d is the difference between an item's two ranks. Tied values share the average of their ranks (a tie for 2nd and 3rd gives 2.5 each); with ties, compute Pearson's r on the ranks instead. Because only order counts, an outlier cannot dominate ρ. In pandas: df.corr(method="spearman").

Correlation is not causation

Ice-cream sales and drowning deaths both rise in summer: they correlate because hot weather drives both, not because one causes the other. A hidden variable like that is a confounder (Chapter 3.5 finds one in a food-delivery dataset). Anscombe's quartet is four small datasets with the same means, the same r of about 0.82 and the same fitted line, yet one is a clean line, one a curve and two are driven by a single outlier. Always plot before you trust r.

Worked example 2: Spearman's ρ

Question. Find the rank correlation for the same six students. (4 marks)

Only one pair of students swaps order, so ρ is close to 1.

Exam traps

  • In Spearman's formula the denominator is n(n² − 1), and d is a difference of ranks, not of values.
  • r(x, y) = r(y, x), but the regression of y on x differs from x on y (Unit 5).
  • Favourite question: "Correlation does not imply causation. Explain with an example." Give the confounder.

3.5 Univariate and bivariate exploratory data analysis

Exploratory data analysis (EDA) is the first open-ended look at a dataset: summaries and plots that show what each variable is like, catch errors and outliers, reveal relationships and raise questions, all before any model is built. John Tukey named it and argued for it in his 1977 book Exploratory Data Analysis. Univariate analysis looks at one variable at a time; bivariate analysis looks at two together. Seven of the ten universities list it.

You need: pandas DataFrames (Unit 2, Pandas Series and DataFrames), summary measures (Chapter 3.2) and correlation (Chapter 3.4).

The EDA routine

  1. Meet the data: df.shape (rows, columns), df.head(), df.info() (types and non-null counts) and df.isna().sum(). Fixing what you find is Unit 2's job.
  2. Type every column: numerical (continuous, like minutes, or discrete, like items per order) or categorical (nominal, like city, or ordinal, like a 1–5 star rating).
  3. Univariate pass over every column.
  4. Bivariate pass over the pairs that matter, above all each feature against the target you care about.
  5. Bring in a third variable: split by a group or colour the points by it.
  6. Write down findings, oddities and questions. They decide the cleaning and the model.

Univariate analysis

Numerical column: report centre, spread, shape and outliers. df.describe() gives count, mean, standard deviation, minimum, the three quartiles and maximum in one table; skew() adds the shape. Draw a histogram or box plot (Chapter 3.7). Compare mean with median: a mean well above the median means a right tail. Check that minimum and maximum are possible at all; an age of −3 or 250 is a data error.

Categorical column: value_counts() gives counts and value_counts(normalize=True) gives proportions; draw a bar chart. Look for rare categories, spelling variants ("Chennai" and "chennai" counted apart) and imbalance (95% of rows in one class will matter for classification in Unit 5).

3.6 Plotting with Matplotlib: subplots, axes, labels, legends

Matplotlib is Python's base plotting library; pandas' .plot() and every Seaborn chart are drawn by it underneath. It lets you control every part of a picture: how many plots sit in one figure, the range and ticks of each axis, titles, labels, legends, notes and arrows, the style, and the file you save.

You need: Python lists and NumPy arrays (Unit 2). Code here runs in any Jupyter notebook after import matplotlib.pyplot as plt.

Figure, Axes and the two ways to code

A Figure is the whole canvas: the window or image file. An Axes is one plot inside it, with its own x-axis and y-axis, ticks, title and the four border lines called spines. One figure can hold many Axes. Everything drawn, from a line to a tick label, is an Artist.

Matplotlib has two interfaces. The pyplot style, plt.plot(x, y) then plt.title("..."), acts on whichever Axes is "current"; it is quick for one plot. The object-oriented style, fig, ax = plt.subplots() then ax.plot(x, y) and ax.set_title("..."), names each Axes, so it is the safe choice for several plots. The names map simply: plt.xlabel becomes ax.set_xlabel, plt.xlim becomes ax.set_xlim.

Line properties can be set by a short format string or by keywords. "o-" is circle markers on a solid line, "s--" squares on a dashed line, "r:" a red dotted line. The keyword forms are color, linestyle ("-", "--", ":", "-."), linewidth, marker, markersize and alpha (transparency, 0 to 1).

Subplots

fig, axs = plt.subplots(nrows, ncols, figsize=(w, h)) makes a grid of Axes in a figure w inches wide and h inches high. For one row or column, axs is a 1-D NumPy array (axs[0], axs[1]); for a grid it is 2-D (axs[row, col]); for 1 × 1 it is a single Axes. Loop over all panels with for ax in axs.flat:. sharex=True or sharey=True gives panels one common scale. The older call plt.subplot(2, 2, 3) picks the third panel of a 2 × 2 grid, counting from 1 along rows, so it is the bottom-left one. fig.suptitle() puts one title over all panels, and fig.tight_layout() moves them apart so labels do not overlap.

Axes, ticks, labels, legends and annotations

  • Limits and scale: ax.set_xlim(0, 10), ax.set_ylim(0, 180), ax.set_yscale("log").
  • Ticks: ax.set_xticks([0, 5, 10]) sets positions, ax.set_xticklabels(["low", "mid", "high"]) sets their text, and ax.tick_params(axis="x", labelrotation=45) turns long labels.
  • Labels: ax.set_title(), ax.set_xlabel(), ax.set_ylabel(); put units in the label, such as "Cups sold (hundreds)".
  • Legend: give each plotting call label="Tea", then call ax.legend(loc="upper right"). loc="best" lets Matplotlib find an empty corner.
  • Drawing on a plot: ax.text(x, y, "note") writes at data coordinates; ax.annotate("Summer dip", xy=(4, 95), xytext=(2.6, 40), arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->")) adds an arrow from the text to the point; ax.axhline(100, linestyle=":") draws a reference line; ax.grid(True) adds a grid.

Saving, styles and 3-D plots

fig.savefig("plot.png", dpi=300, bbox_inches="tight") writes the file; the extension picks the format (png, jpg, pdf, svg), dpi sets dots per inch, and bbox_inches="tight" trims white margins. In a script, save before plt.show(), which may close the figure. plt.style.use("ggplot") switches the whole look (plt.style.available lists the styles) and plt.rcParams["font.size"] = 12 changes one default.

3.7 Common charts: bar, pie, histogram, box and scatter

Five charts cover most pictures in a report or an exam answer, and each answers one kind of question. A bar chart compares amounts across categories; a pie chart shows shares of one whole; a histogram shows the shape of one numeric column; a box plot shows its quartiles and outliers and compares groups; a scatter plot shows how two numeric columns relate. Pick the chart from the question, not from its looks.

You need: Matplotlib's Figure and Axes (Chapter 3.6), quartiles and the 1.5 × IQR rule (Chapter 3.2).

Bar and pie charts

Start a bar chart's value axis at zero; a cut axis makes a 5% difference look like 50%. Sort bars by value unless the categories have a natural order. For two series per category, draw grouped bars by shifting the x positions by the bar width, or stacked bars by passing bottom= the first series to the second bar call.

In a pie chart each slice's angle is (value ÷ total) × 360°. autopct="%1.1f%%" prints each share, startangle=90 starts the first slice at the top, and explode pulls one slice out. Pies fail with many slices or near-equal shares, because eyes compare lengths far better than angles; a sorted bar chart then says the same thing more clearly.

Histogram and box plot

ax.hist(x, bins=10, edgecolor="black") draws a histogram; bins can be a count or a list of edges, and density=True scales the bars so their total area is 1. The bin count changes the picture, so try two or three (Chapter 3.1). A histogram is not a bar chart: its classes lie on a number scale in fixed order and touch, while a bar chart's categories are separate and can be reordered.

A box plot (Tukey's box-and-whisker plot) draws a box from Q1 to Q3 with a line at the median. Whiskers run out to the most extreme values still inside the fences Q1 − 1.5 × IQR and Q3 + 1.5 × IQR; values beyond are drawn as separate points (Matplotlib calls them fliers; whis=1.5 is the default). Box plots side by side compare many groups at a glance. Matplotlib computes quartiles by NumPy's linear interpolation, so its box can differ slightly from a hand calculation.

Scatter, bubble, line and error bars

ax.scatter(x, y, s=sizes, c=colours, alpha=0.6) plots one point per row. Make s proportional to a third column and it becomes a bubble chart (city population as bubble size, say); colour by group with c. Use a line chart only when x is ordered, usually time. ax.errorbar(x, means, yerr=sds, capsize=4) adds error bars that show the spread or uncertainty around each mean.

"Coherence and aesthetics" means the same colour for the same thing across charts, no 3-D effects on bars or pies, and nothing on the page that carries no data.

Seaborn in one page

After import seaborn as sns and sns.set_theme(style="whitegrid"), every function takes data=df and column names. Its functions come in two kinds:

  • Axes-level functions draw into one Matplotlib Axes and accept ax=: sns.histplot(data=df, x="marks", kde=True), sns.kdeplot, sns.boxplot(data=df, x="city", y="minutes"), sns.violinplot, sns.countplot, sns.barplot (mean with an error bar), sns.scatterplot(..., hue="section") and sns.heatmap.
  • Figure-level functions build a whole figure, often with one panel per group: sns.relplot, sns.displot, sns.catplot(..., col="city"), sns.jointplot and sns.pairplot. They return a grid object, not an Axes.

Since Seaborn draws with Matplotlib, ax.set_title(), plt.savefig() and the rest of Chapter 3.6 still apply. A KDE plot (kernel density estimate) puts a small bell curve on every data point and adds them up; the sum, scaled to area 1, is a smooth estimate of the distribution. The bandwidth sets how wide each bell is (bw_adjust in Seaborn): too small gives a spiky curve, too large hides real peaks.

Heatmaps

A heatmap shows a table of numbers as coloured cells. Its most common use is the correlation matrix:

corr = df.corr(numeric_only=True)
sns.heatmap(corr, annot=True, fmt=".2f", cmap="coolwarm", vmin=-1, vmax=1, center=0)

annot=True writes each value in its cell and fmt=".2f" gives two decimals. For correlations use a diverging colour map (coolwarm, RdBu), where 0 is neutral and the two signs get opposite colours, and fix vmin=-1, vmax=1 so a colour means the same in every chart. For counts use a sequential map (Blues, viridis). mask=np.triu(np.ones_like(corr, dtype=bool)) hides the repeated upper triangle. Heatmaps also show pivot tables (orders by weekday and hour), confusion matrices (Unit 5) and missing-value maps (df.isna()).

Pair plots

sns.pairplot(df, vars=["hours", "phone", "marks"], hue="section", diag_kind="kde", corner=True) draws a k × k grid for k numeric columns. The panel in row i and column j is a scatter plot of column j (x) against column i (y); the diagonal shows each column's own histogram or KDE, since a column plotted against itself would be a useless straight line. Panels above and below the diagonal are mirror images, and corner=True drops the upper ones. With hue, points are coloured by group, which shows at once whether the groups separate (the classic iris flower data split cleanly by petal size). A pair plot has k² panels and k(k − 1)/2 distinct pairs, so for more than six or seven columns read the heatmap first.

Plotly for interactive plots

Plotly Express makes a chart in one call: import plotly.express as px, then fig = px.scatter(df, x="hours", y="marks", color="section", size="attendance", hover_data=["name"]), then fig.show() in a notebook or fig.write_html("marks.html") for a page anyone can open in a browser. The same pattern gives px.histogram, px.box, px.bar, px.line and px.pie; px.imshow(corr, text_auto=".2f", zmin=-1, zmax=1, color_continuous_scale="RdBu") is the heatmap and px.scatter_matrix(df, dimensions=[...]) the pair plot. The reader can hover for exact values, drag to zoom, double-click to reset and click a legend entry to hide that series. The plots run on the plotly.js JavaScript library, and Plotly's Dash framework builds dashboards from them. Use Matplotlib and Seaborn for printed reports, Plotly for notebooks, web pages and dashboards.

Question. For 10 students we have weekly study hours, attendance (%), daily phone hours and marks. Compute the correlation matrix, draw it as a heatmap and a pair plot with Seaborn, and answer: (a) which column relates most strongly to marks; (b) which relation is negative; (c) how many panels the pair plot of three columns has; (d) the Plotly versions. import pandas as pd, seaborn as sns, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "hours":      [2, 3, 5, 6, 8, 9, 4, 7, 5, 6],
    "attendance": [72, 90, 68, 85, 80, 94, 88, 70, 92, 76],
    "phone":      [4, 5, 3, 6, 2, 2, 6, 3, 2, 5],

Exam traps

  • A sequential colour map on a correlation heatmap makes −0.6 and +0.6 look unrelated; use a diverging map centred on 0 with vmin=-1, vmax=1.
  • In pandas 2 and later, df.corr() on a DataFrame with text columns raises an error; pass numeric_only=True or select the numeric columns.
  • Figure-level functions (pairplot, relplot) ignore ax=; they make their own figure and return a grid object.
  • Plotly figures are shown with fig.show() and saved with write_html; saving a PNG with fig.write_image needs the extra kaleido package.

Takeaways

  1. Look before you model: a frequency table and its histogram show centre, spread, shape and outliers at once.
  2. Always report a centre with a spread: median and IQR for skewed data or data with outliers, mean and standard deviation for symmetric data.
  3. Sample variance divides by n − 1; the standard deviation is in the data's units; the CV compares spread across different scales.
  4. A z-score, z = (x − μ) ÷ σ, puts any value on the standard normal scale, where Φ(z) gives the share below it; check normality with a QQ plot first.
  5. Pearson's r measures straight-line association only, has no units, lies between −1 and +1, and does not prove cause; Spearman's ρ works on ranks.
  6. EDA goes by variable type: univariate (describe, value_counts), bivariate (corr, groupby, crosstab), then a third variable to catch confounders.
  7. In Matplotlib a Figure holds Axes; build them with plt.subplots and set titles, ticks, labels, legends and notes on each Axes; pixels = inches × dpi.
  8. Choose a chart from the question: bar compares, pie shares, histogram shows shape, box shows spread and outliers, scatter shows a relation.
  9. Seaborn's heatmap and pair plot show many variables at once; Plotly makes the same charts interactive in a browser.

Glossary

Axes (Matplotlib)
One plot inside a Figure, with its own x-axis, y-axis, ticks, title and spines.
Bivariate analysis
Study of how two variables vary together: scatter and r, group summaries, crosstabs.
Box plot
A box from Q1 to Q3 with a median line, whiskers to the last values inside the 1.5 × IQR fences, and outliers as points.
Bubble chart
A scatter plot whose point sizes show a third numeric variable.
Class boundary
The value where two adjacent classes meet, such as 29.5 between 20–29 and 30–39.
Coefficient of variation
s ÷ x̄ × 100%: a unit-free spread for comparing columns.
Confounder
A third variable that drives two others and creates a correlation between them.
Covariance
Average product of paired deviations, Σ(x − x̄)(y − ȳ) ÷ (n − 1); its sign shows the direction of a relation.
Crosstab
A table of counts for every pair of categories of two categorical variables (pd.crosstab).
Cumulative frequency
The running total of frequencies up to a class.
Exploratory data analysis (EDA)
Summaries and plots used to understand a dataset before modelling.
Figure (Matplotlib)
The whole canvas or image that holds one or more Axes.
Frequency distribution
A table of how many observations take each value or fall in each class.
Heatmap
A matrix drawn as coloured cells, most often a correlation matrix.
Histogram
Touching bars over class intervals of a numeric variable, heights equal to frequencies.
Interquartile range (IQR)
Q3 − Q1, the spread of the middle 50% of the data.
KDE plot
A smooth density curve made by adding a small kernel (bell curve) at each data point.
Kurtosis
Tail weight, β₂ = m₄ ÷ m₂²; 3 for a normal curve.
Legend
The key that names each plotted series, built from the label= arguments.
Normal distribution
The symmetric bell-shaped distribution N(μ, σ²), fixed by its mean and standard deviation.
Ogive
Cumulative frequency plotted against upper class boundaries; the median is read at n/2.
Pair plot
A grid of scatter plots for every pair of columns, with each column's distribution on the diagonal.
Pearson's r
Covariance divided by both standard deviations; strength of straight-line association, −1 to +1.
Pivot table
A summary grid of one variable aggregated over two keys (df.pivot_table).
QQ plot
Sorted data plotted against normal quantiles; a straight line means close to normal.
Skewness
Lopsidedness of a distribution; positive for a long right tail.
Spearman's ρ
Rank correlation, 1 − 6Σd² ÷ (n(n² − 1)), for ordinal data and monotone relations.
Standard deviation
Square root of the variance, the typical distance of values from the mean, in the data's units.
Univariate analysis
Description of one variable on its own: centre, spread, shape, outliers or category counts.
z-score
(x − μ) ÷ σ: how many standard deviations a value lies from the mean.
