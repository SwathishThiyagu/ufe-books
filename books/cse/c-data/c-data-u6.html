6.1 Big data: characteristics, sources and types

Big data is data that is too large, arrives too fast, or comes in too many forms for one computer and a traditional database to store and analyse in the time the answer is needed. The definition is relative: a data set that was big in 2005 fits on a laptop today. The term became common when phones, sensors, web logs, social media and digital payments began producing data faster than single machines could keep up. That pushed computing from one large server to clusters of many ordinary ones, which is what the rest of this unit is about.

You need: structured, semi-structured and unstructured data from Unit 1 (Facets of data), and Unit 1 (Data sources and data collection).

The five Vs

Big data is usually described by a list of properties that all start with V. In 2001 the analyst Doug Laney (META Group) named the first three; industry later added two more, and the five-V list is the standard exam answer.

  • Volume: how much data there is. Measured in terabytes (1 TB = 10¹² bytes), petabytes (1 PB = 10¹⁵ bytes) and exabytes (1 EB = 10¹⁸ bytes). A hospital that stores every CT scan, or a city that keeps every CCTV recording, reaches petabytes.
  • Velocity: how fast data arrives and how quickly it must be acted on. Some data is processed in daily batches (payroll); some must be answered in milliseconds, like a fraud check that has to finish before a UPI payment completes.
  • Variety: how many forms the data takes: tables, JSON logs, emails, chat text, images, audio and video, often about the same customer.
  • Veracity: how far the data can be trusted. Big data is noisy: bot clicks, duplicate records, missing fields, a faulty sensor that reports 0 °C in a Chennai summer.
  • Value: the useful insight or money the data can produce, such as fraud caught or sales gained. Data with no value is only a storage bill.

Some books add variability (the rate or meaning of data changes, as with festival-season sales spikes or a word whose sense changes with context) and visualization, giving seven Vs. If a question asks for “the characteristics of big data”, write the five with an example each, then mention the extra two.

Where big data comes from

Sources are grouped by who or what creates the data.

  • Human-generated: social media posts, product reviews, emails, search queries, chat messages, uploaded photos and videos.
  • Machine-generated: server and application logs, website clickstreams (every page a user opens), sensor and IoT readings (smart electricity meters, traffic cameras), GPS traces from cab and delivery apps, satellite images. This is the fastest-growing group, because machines never sleep.
  • Business transactions: bank and UPI payments, e-commerce orders, IRCTC ticket bookings, telecom call detail records (one row per call or SMS).
  • Science and public data: genome sequencers, telescopes, weather models, and open government data such as data.gov.in.

Types of big data and why one computer is not enough

By structure, big data falls into the three facets of Unit 1:

  • Structured: a fixed schema of rows and columns, stored in relational tables and queried with SQL. Payments, bookings and call records.
  • Semi-structured: no fixed schema, but tags or keys describe each field. JSON and XML files, CSV logs, and emails (fixed header fields plus a free-text body).
  • Unstructured: no predefined model at all. Free text, images, audio and video. Most new data is of this kind, and it is the hardest to analyse.

By movement, data is either at rest (stored, processed in batches; Chapters 6.2 to 6.4) or in motion (a stream processed as it arrives; Chapter 6.6).

Why not just buy a bigger server? Reading is the bottleneck. A hard disk reads at roughly 100 MB/s, so reading 1 TB (10⁶ MB) from one disk takes 10⁶ ÷ 100 = 10,000 s, almost 2.8 hours. If the same terabyte is spread over 100 disks on 100 machines that read in parallel, each reads 10 GB and the job takes 100 s. Buying a bigger machine is called scaling up (vertical scaling); it is expensive and has a ceiling. Adding more ordinary machines is scaling out (horizontal scaling); it is cheap and has almost no ceiling, but machines fail, so the software must expect failure. That trade is the design brief of Hadoop.

Worked example

Question. A state electricity board installs 20 lakh smart meters. Each meter sends a 250-byte JSON reading every 15 minutes. (a) Find the average number of readings per second. (b) Find the data volume per day and per year. (c) The readings are kept for one year in HDFS, which stores 3 copies of everything; 25% extra space is kept free for temporary files. How much raw disk is needed? (d) If every meter sends within the first 60 seconds of each 15-minute slot, what is the peak rate? (e) Classify by type: the readings, the meter master table (meter id, address, tariff), and recorded complaint calls. (8 marks)

  1. Meters = 20 lakh = 2,000,000. Readings per meter per day = 24 × 4 = 96. Readings per day = 2,000,000 × 96 = 192,000,000.
  2. (a) A day has 86,400 s. Average rate = 192,000,000 ÷ 86,400 = 2,222 readings per second.
  3. (b) Per day = 192,000,000 × 250 B = 48,000,000,000 B = 48 GB. Per year = 48 × 365 = 17,520 GB = 17.52 TB.
  4. (c) With 3 copies: 17.52 × 3 = 52.56 TB. Keeping 25% free means the data may fill only 75% of the disks: 52.56 ÷ 0.75 = 70.08 TB of raw disk. With 8 TB per server that is 70.08 ÷ 8 = 8.76, so 9 servers.
  5. (d) Peak = 2,000,000 ÷ 60 = 33,333 readings per second, 15 times the average. The system must be sized for the peak, not the average.
  6. (e) Readings in JSON: semi-structured. Meter master table: structured. Recorded calls (audio): unstructured.

What it shows. One modest reading becomes 17.5 TB a year (volume), arrives in bursts of 33,000 per second (velocity), and sits next to tables and audio (variety). No single server holds it, which is why the data is spread across nine or more machines.

Exam traps

  • Big data is not only “large data”. A modest but very fast stream, or a mix of forms that a relational database cannot hold, is also big data. Name all five Vs.
  • Veracity is about trust and quality; value is about usefulness. Do not swap them.
  • JSON, XML and emails are semi-structured, not unstructured: they carry keys or tags. A scanned PDF is unstructured.
  • Units: storage uses 1 TB = 10¹² bytes; memory often uses 1 TiB = 2⁴⁰ bytes. Network speeds are in bits: 1 Gbps ≈ 125 MB/s, not 1,000 MB/s.
  • Favourite question: “Define big data. Explain its characteristics with examples, and distinguish structured, semi-structured and unstructured data.”

6.2 Hadoop architecture and HDFS

Apache Hadoop is open-source software that stores and processes very large data sets on a cluster of ordinary computers (commodity hardware). It rests on two ideas: a distributed file system, HDFS (Hadoop Distributed File System), that cuts every file into large blocks and keeps copies of each block on different machines, so a dead disk loses nothing; and sending the program to the machines that hold the data instead of pulling terabytes over the network. Hadoop grew out of Doug Cutting and Mike Cafarella’s Nutch web-crawler project, following Google’s papers on its file system (GFS, 2003) and on MapReduce (2004), and became a separate Apache project in 2006 with Yahoo as its main early user.

You need: Chapter 6.1 (why big data systems scale out).

The parts of Hadoop

Hadoop has four core modules. Hadoop Common holds shared libraries. HDFS stores data. YARN (Yet Another Resource Negotiator) hands out the cluster’s CPU and memory to jobs (Chapter 6.4). MapReduce is the batch processing model (Chapter 6.3). The cluster follows a master–worker design: a few master machines run the NameNode (for HDFS) and the ResourceManager (for YARN); every worker machine runs a DataNode and a NodeManager, so storage and computation sit on the same machines.

In Hadoop 1 a single JobTracker managed resources and tracked every job; it became a bottleneck and ran only MapReduce, so Hadoop 2 (2013) moved resource management into YARN. HDFS assumes that hardware failure is normal, files are large, data is read in long streams (throughput matters more than delay), and files are written once and read many times: you may append, but not edit in the middle.

NameNode, DataNodes and blocks

  • Block: the unit of storage, 128 MB by default in Hadoop 2 and 3 (64 MB in Hadoop 1). Large blocks keep the disk’s seek time small compared with the transfer time and keep the NameNode’s list of blocks short. A block that holds less than 128 MB uses only its real size on disk.
  • NameNode (master): keeps the namespace (directory tree, file names, owners, permissions) and the mapping file → blocks → DataNodes. All of it is held in RAM for speed. The namespace is saved on disk in two files: fsimage, a snapshot, and the edit log, every change since that snapshot. Block locations are not saved; DataNodes report them when the cluster starts.
  • DataNode (worker): stores blocks as ordinary files on its local disks and serves reads and writes. It sends a heartbeat to the NameNode every 3 seconds and a block report (the list of blocks it holds) periodically. If heartbeats stop for about 10 minutes, the NameNode marks the node dead and copies its blocks from the surviving replicas to other nodes.
  • Secondary NameNode: every hour or so it merges the edit log into a new fsimage (a checkpoint), so the NameNode restarts quickly. It is not a backup NameNode. For high availability, Hadoop 2 runs an active and a standby NameNode that share the edit log through JournalNodes, with ZooKeeper switching to the standby if the active one fails.

A rule of thumb is about 150 bytes of NameNode RAM per file, directory or block, so the number of files, not their size, limits a cluster. Millions of tiny files fill the NameNode long before the disks: the small files problem.

Replication, rack awareness, reads and writes

Each block is stored 3 times by default (the replication factor, settable per file). Machines sit in racks that share one network switch, so a switch failure takes out a whole rack. The default placement is therefore rack-aware: the first replica goes on the writer’s own node (or a random node if the client is outside the cluster), the second on a node in a different rack, and the third on another node in that same second rack. A block then survives the loss of a whole rack, while the write crosses between racks only once.

Writing a file (Figure 6.2.1):

  1. The client asks the NameNode to create the file; the NameNode checks that it does not exist and that the client has permission.
  2. For each block, the NameNode picks three DataNodes and returns their addresses.
  3. The client streams the block in small packets to the first DataNode, which forwards each packet to the second, which forwards it to the third: the replication pipeline.
  4. Acknowledgements travel back along the pipeline. When the block is full, the client asks for the next block’s DataNodes.
  5. The client closes the file and the NameNode records it as complete.

To read, the client asks the NameNode for the block locations, sorted by distance, and reads each block straight from the nearest DataNode. File data never passes through the NameNode, which is why one NameNode can serve thousands of DataNodes. On the command line, hdfs dfs -put sales.csv /data/ copies a file in and hdfs dfs -ls /data lists it; the second column is the replication factor:

-rw-r--r--   3 hduser supergroup  524288000 2026-10-20 10:15 /data/sales.csv

Worked example

Question. A client outside the cluster writes a 500 MB file sales.csv to HDFS with default settings. The cluster has two racks of three DataNodes each (DN1–DN3 in Rack 1, DN4–DN6 in Rack 2), and each DataNode has 4 TB of disk. (a) How many blocks, of what sizes? (b) How many block replicas and how much raw disk does the file use? (c) Give a placement that follows the default rack-aware policy. (d) How many DataNodes can fail at once without losing data? (e) Estimate the NameNode memory for this file, and for the same 500 MB stored as 50,000 files of 10 KB. (f) How much data can the cluster hold if 25% of the disk is kept free? (10 marks)

  1. (a) 500 ÷ 128 = 3.9, so 4 blocks: B1, B2, B3 of 128 MB (384 MB) and B4 of 500 − 384 = 116 MB.
  2. (b) 4 blocks × 3 = 12 replicas. Raw disk = 500 × 3 = 1,500 MB, not 4 × 128 × 3 = 1,536 MB, because B4 is not padded to 128 MB.
  3. (c) Rule: first replica on a random node, second on a node in the other rack, third on a different node in that same other rack. One valid placement (Figure 6.2.1): B1 → DN1, DN5, DN6; B2 → DN4, DN2, DN3; B3 → DN3, DN4, DN5; B4 → DN6, DN1, DN2. Each DataNode holds exactly 2 replicas.
  4. (d) Every block has copies on 3 different nodes, so any 2 DataNodes can fail together and at least one copy of each block remains. Every block also has copies in both racks, so a whole rack (3 nodes) can fail too.
  5. (e) One file + 4 blocks = 5 objects × 150 B ≈ 750 bytes. As 50,000 small files: 50,000 files + 50,000 blocks = 100,000 objects × 150 B = 15,000,000 B ≈ 15 MB, 20,000 times more for the same data.
  6. (f) Raw disk = 6 × 4 = 24 TB. Usable = 24 × 0.75 = 18 TB. With 3 replicas, data held = 18 ÷ 3 = 6 TB.

What it shows. Replication triples the disk but survives two node failures or a rack failure. Size a cluster as “usable disk ÷ 3”, and keep files large.

Exam traps

  • The Secondary NameNode is not a hot standby or backup. It only merges the edit log into the fsimage. The standby NameNode of a high-availability setup is a different thing.
  • The last block is not padded: a 1 MB file uses 1 MB × 3 of disk, not 128 MB × 3.
  • File data never flows through the NameNode; only metadata does.
  • Default block size is 128 MB in Hadoop 2 and 3 but 64 MB in Hadoop 1. State the version you assume.
  • HDFS is a poor choice for many small files, low-latency lookups and in-place updates; those needs go to HBase (Chapter 6.5).

6.3 MapReduce programming model

MapReduce is a programming model for processing huge data sets in parallel on a cluster. The programmer writes only two functions: map, which turns each input record into key–value pairs, and reduce, which combines all the values that share a key. The framework does everything else: it splits the input, runs many map tasks next to the data, sorts and moves the intermediate pairs to the reducers, re-runs tasks that fail and writes the result to HDFS. Jeffrey Dean and Sanjay Ghemawat described it at Google in 2004; Hadoop MapReduce is the open-source version. Counting, grouping, joining and indexing large files all fit this pattern.

You need: Chapter 6.2 (HDFS blocks and DataNodes).

Map, shuffle and reduce

The two functions have fixed shapes:

map:    (k1, v1)        → list of (k2, v2)
reduce: (k2, list of v2) → list of (k3, v3)

For a text file, Hadoop gives map the byte offset of a line as k1 and the line itself as v1. A job runs in these phases:

  1. Split: the input is divided into input splits, normally one per HDFS block, and one map task is started per split. A RecordReader turns the split into records.
  2. Map: each map task calls map() once per record and collects the output pairs in a memory buffer.
  3. Partition: each pair is assigned to one of R reducers, by default partition = hash(key) mod R. All pairs with the same key reach the same reducer.
  4. Combine (optional): a combiner, a mini-reduce run on one mapper’s own output, adds up pairs locally to cut network traffic.
  5. Shuffle and sort: each reducer fetches its partition from every mapper; the framework merges the pieces and sorts them by key, so each key arrives once with the list of all its values. This is the most expensive phase, because data crosses the network.
  6. Reduce: reduce() is called once per key. Output goes to HDFS as files named part-r-00000, part-r-00001, and so on, one per reducer.

The scheduler tries to run each map task on a node that holds a replica of its split (data locality), or failing that in the same rack. Map output is written to the mapper’s local disk, not HDFS, because it is temporary. If a task fails it is simply re-run on another node; if one task is unusually slow (a straggler), a duplicate copy may be started and the first to finish wins (speculative execution).

Word count in Python with Hadoop Streaming

Hadoop Streaming runs any program that reads standard input and writes standard output as a mapper or reducer, with key and value separated by a tab. The mapper in Python:

# mapper.py
import sys
for line in sys.stdin:
    for word in line.lower().split():
        print(f"{word}\t1")

The reducer relies on the sort: all lines for one word arrive together, so it adds until the word changes.

# reducer.py
import sys
current, count = None, 0
for line in sys.stdin:
    word, n = line.rstrip("\n").split("\t")
    if word == current:
        count += int(n)
    else:
        if current is not None:
            print(f"{current}\t{count}")
        current, count = word, int(n)
if current is not None:
    print(f"{current}\t{count}")

You can test the whole model on one machine with a pipe, where sort plays the shuffle: cat input.txt | python3 mapper.py | sort | python3 reducer.py. On the cluster the same two files are passed to the hadoop-streaming jar with -mapper, -reducer, -input and -output. In Java, the mapper extends Mapper<LongWritable, Text, Text, IntWritable> and the reducer extends Reducer<Text, IntWritable, Text, IntWritable>.

Combiners, and where MapReduce is a poor fit

A combiner is allowed only when the operation gives the same answer however the values are grouped, that is, when it is commutative and associative: sum, count, maximum, minimum. Average is not: avg(avg(1, 2), avg(3)) = avg(1.5, 3) = 2.25, but avg(1, 2, 3) = 2. The fix is to emit (sum, count) pairs, combine those, and divide only in the reducer. The framework may run a combiner zero, one or several times, so the job must be correct without it.

Every MapReduce job reads from HDFS and writes back to HDFS. Algorithms that loop, such as k-means clustering (Unit 5) or PageRank, must chain one job per iteration and pay that disk cost each time. That cost is why Spark (Chapter 6.6) replaced MapReduce for most new work.

Exam traps

  • The number of map tasks is set by the number of input splits, not by the programmer. The number of reducers is set by the programmer (default 1).
  • A combiner is an optimisation, not a phase you can rely on: it may run zero or many times. Never use it to compute an average directly.
  • Reducer input is sorted by key; the values in each list are in no particular order.
  • Map output goes to local disk; only reduce output goes to HDFS. A job with zero reducers (map-only, such as filtering) has no shuffle.
  • Favourite question: “Explain MapReduce with the word count example and a neat diagram.” Draw Figure 6.3.1 with your own words.

Combiners emit (sum, count): mapper 1 gives (140, 2), mapper 2 gives (90, 1). The reducer adds them to (230, 3) and divides: 230 ÷ 3 = 76.67. Averaging the averages would give (70 + 90) ÷ 2 = 80, which is wrong.

Analyze One key holds 60% of all pairs. What happens to the job, and what can you do? 4 marks

That key’s reducer gets 60% of the work and finishes long after the others (data skew), so the job is as slow as that one reducer. Use a combiner to shrink the data first, or split the hot key into sub-keys (salting) and merge the partial results in a second step.

Beyond the syllabus

This is not for the exam. Few new jobs are written in raw MapReduce today: Hive runs on Tez or Spark, and Spark runs most batch work. The map–shuffle–reduce idea lives on in every distributed engine, from Spark’s stages to Apache Beam and Google Cloud Dataflow. Search for “MapReduce design patterns” and the Dean–Ghemawat paper for joins, inverted indexes and secondary sort.

6.4 Hadoop ecosystem tools: YARN, Hive and Pig

HDFS and MapReduce alone are powerful but hard to use and hard to share. YARN (Yet Another Resource Negotiator) is the cluster’s operating system: it hands out memory and CPU so that MapReduce, Spark and Hive jobs from many users run side by side. Hive lets analysts query files in HDFS with HiveQL, a dialect of SQL. Pig lets programmers write a data flow, step by step, in Pig Latin. Both turn a few lines into jobs that YARN runs. Around them sit the rest of the Hadoop ecosystem: Sqoop moves tables between relational databases and HDFS, Flume collects log streams, Oozie schedules chains of jobs, ZooKeeper coordinates distributed services, and HBase (Chapter 6.5) stores records.

You need: Chapter 6.2 (NameNode, DataNodes) and Chapter 6.3 (map and reduce tasks); SQL GROUP BY, which works like the Pandas grouping of Unit 2 (Data wrangling and hierarchical indexing).

YARN: ResourceManager, NodeManagers and containers

  • ResourceManager (RM), one per cluster, has two parts. The Scheduler divides the cluster’s resources among applications according to a policy; it does not watch tasks. The ApplicationsManager accepts submitted jobs and starts (and if needed restarts) each job’s ApplicationMaster.
  • NodeManager (NM), one per worker node, launches containers, watches their memory and CPU use and reports to the RM by heartbeat.
  • ApplicationMaster (AM), one per application, asks the RM for containers, tells NodeManagers to start tasks in them and tracks progress and failures. For a MapReduce job it is the MRAppMaster.
  • Container: a slice of one node’s resources, such as 4 GB of memory and 1 virtual core, in which one task runs.
  1. The client submits the application to the ResourceManager.
  2. The ApplicationsManager picks a node and has its NodeManager launch the ApplicationMaster in a container.
  3. The AM registers with the RM and requests containers, naming the nodes that hold its input blocks.
  4. The Scheduler grants containers on particular nodes, and the AM asks those NodeManagers to start the tasks.
  5. Tasks report to the AM; when all finish, the AM unregisters and its containers are freed.

The Scheduler follows one of three policies: FIFO (first come, first served), Capacity (queues with guaranteed shares, say 70% for production and 30% for research, which may borrow idle capacity) or Fair (resources shared equally among running jobs over time).

Hive: SQL on files in HDFS

Hive was built at Facebook so that analysts who knew SQL could query Hadoop data without writing Java. A Hive table is a directory of files in HDFS; its schema (column names and types, file format, location) is kept in the metastore, a small relational database. Hive uses schema-on-read: files are copied in as they are and checked against the schema only when queried. A query passes from the client (Beeline or a JDBC/ODBC connection to HiveServer2) to the driver, the compiler (which parses it and checks names against the metastore), the optimizer, and finally the execution engine, which runs it as MapReduce, Tez or Spark jobs on YARN.

Two table kinds matter. Dropping a managed (internal) table deletes its data; dropping an external table deletes only the metastore entry. Large tables are partitioned (one subdirectory per value, such as dt=2026-10-01, so a query for one day reads one directory) and may be bucketed (rows hashed into a fixed number of files). Hive is for batch analysis over large tables, with answers in seconds to minutes; it is not for single-row transactions.

Pig: data flows in Pig Latin

Pig was built at Yahoo for programmers who think in steps rather than in one SQL statement. Each Pig Latin statement takes a relation (a bag of tuples) and produces a new one: LOAD, FILTER, FOREACH … GENERATE, GROUP, JOIN, ORDER, DISTINCT, then STORE (write to HDFS) or DUMP (print). Pig is lazy: it only builds a plan until a STORE or DUMP, then compiles the whole flow into jobs. Its data model is atom, tuple, bag and map, and it handles semi-structured data without a declared schema. You run it in the Grunt shell or as a script, in local mode or on the cluster.

CREATE EXTERNAL TABLE sales (order_id INT, city STRING, amount INT)

sales  = LOAD '/data/sales' USING PigStorage(',')
         AS (order_id:int, city:chararray, amount:int);

totals = FOREACH bycity GENERATE group AS city,
         SUM(big.amount) AS total, COUNT(big) AS orders;

6.5 NoSQL stores: HBase, MongoDB and Cassandra

NoSQL (“not only SQL”) databases give up some features of relational databases, such as fixed tables, joins and multi-row transactions, so that data can be spread over many servers and change shape without a schema change. They come in four data models: key–value, document, column-family (wide-column) and graph. This chapter covers the three stores the syllabi name: HBase (column-family, on top of HDFS), MongoDB (document) and Cassandra (wide-column with no master). They hold user profiles, product catalogues, chat messages and sensor time series, where one relational server cannot keep up with the reads and writes.

You need: Chapter 6.2 (HDFS, replication); relational tables and keys from your database course.

Why NoSQL: models, scale-out and CAP

A relational database keeps a fixed schema, joins tables, guarantees ACID transactions (atomic, consistent, isolated, durable) and usually grows by buying a bigger server. A NoSQL store grows by sharding (splitting rows across nodes by a key, using either the hash of the key or ranges of it) and replication (copies on several nodes). The high-level architecture is the same for most: a client talks to a router or coordinator, which finds the shard for the key; each shard lives on several nodes; and a membership service tracks which nodes are alive. Many NoSQL stores relax ACID to BASE: basically available, soft state, eventually consistent, meaning replicas may briefly disagree but converge.

The CAP theorem (Eric Brewer, 2000; proved by Gilbert and Lynch, 2002) says that when a network partition (P) splits the nodes, a distributed store must choose between consistency (C: every read sees the latest write) and availability (A: every request gets a non-error answer). HBase and MongoDB with default settings choose consistency (CP); Cassandra chooses availability (AP) but lets each query ask for more consistency.

HBase: Bigtable on HDFS

HBase is modelled on Google’s Bigtable (2006) and stores its files in HDFS. It adds what HDFS lacks: fast random reads and writes of single rows. A table has rows sorted by row key; columns are grouped into column families declared when the table is created, but new column qualifiers can be added to any row at any time. Each cell is addressed as (row key, family:qualifier, timestamp) and keeps several versions. Rows are split into regions (ranges of row keys) served by RegionServers; the HMaster assigns regions and handles schema changes, and ZooKeeper tracks which servers are alive. A write goes first to a write-ahead log and an in-memory MemStore, which is later flushed to files (HFiles) in HDFS. Reads and writes of one row are strongly consistent.

create 'student', 'info', 'marks'
put 'student', 'R101', 'info:name', 'Asha'
put 'student', 'R101', 'marks:DS', '88'
get 'student', 'R101'

MongoDB: documents

MongoDB stores documents in BSON (binary JSON) inside collections. Every document has a unique _id and may be up to 16 MB. Documents in one collection need not share fields, and related data is nested (an order holds its items as an array) instead of joined. Queries filter on any field and can use indexes; the aggregation pipeline ($match, $group, $sort) does GROUP BY-style work. A replica set has one primary that takes writes and secondaries that copy it, with automatic election of a new primary on failure; sharding spreads a collection over servers by a shard key.

db.orders.find({ city: "Chennai", amount: { $gte: 500 } })
db.orders.aggregate([ { $group: { _id: "$city", total: { $sum: "$amount" } } } ])

Cassandra: a ring with no master

Cassandra was built at Facebook, combining Amazon Dynamo’s ring and replication with Bigtable’s data model. Every node is equal (peer to peer); nodes learn about each other by a gossip protocol, so there is no single point of failure. Each row’s partition key is hashed to a token on a ring; the node whose token range contains it owns the row, and copies go to the next RF − 1 nodes clockwise, where RF is the replication factor. Queries in CQL (Cassandra Query Language) look like SQL but must name the partition key and cannot join. Writes go to a commit log and an in-memory memtable, later flushed to SSTables, so writes are very fast. Each query chooses a consistency level: ONE, QUORUM or ALL, with QUORUM = ⌊RF ÷ 2⌋ + 1 replicas. If a read waits for R replicas and a write for W, then R + W > RF guarantees that the read meets at least one replica holding the latest write.

Worked example

Question. A Cassandra cluster has four nodes A, B, C, D with tokens 0, 25, 50, 75 on a ring of 0–99. Each node owns the range from just after the previous token up to its own token; replicas go to the next nodes clockwise. RF = 3. (a) A row’s partition key hashes to 37. Which nodes store it? (b) With QUORUM writes and QUORUM reads, how many replicas must answer, and is every read guaranteed to see the latest write? (c) How many of the row’s replicas can be down while QUORUM still works? (d) Repeat (b) for ONE/ONE. (e) Which nodes store a key that hashes to 90? (8 marks)

  1. (a) Clockwise order: A(0) → B(25) → C(50) → D(75) → back to A. 37 lies in (25, 50], so the owner is C; the next two clockwise are D and A.
  2. (b) QUORUM = ⌊3 ÷ 2⌋ + 1 = 1 + 1 = 2 replicas for each read and write. R + W = 2 + 2 = 4 > 3, so any 2 replicas read share at least one with the 2 written: yes, strongly consistent.
  3. (c) 3 − 2 = 1 replica may be down.
  4. (d) ONE/ONE: R + W = 1 + 1 = 2, not greater than 3. The read may hit a replica the write has not reached yet, so it may return stale data; consistency is eventual, but latency is lowest and two replicas may be down.
  5. (e) 90 lies after D(75) and wraps round to A(0): range (75, 99] ∪ [0, 0] belongs to A; replicas A, B, C.

What it shows. Consistency in Cassandra is a dial set per query. With RF = 5, QUORUM = 3 and up to 2 replicas may fail.

Exam traps

  • NoSQL means “not only SQL”, not “no SQL”: CQL and Hive-style queries exist. It also does not mean “no schema”: HBase needs column families, Cassandra needs table definitions.
  • The C of CAP (every read sees the latest write) is not the C of ACID (data obeys integrity rules).
  • The strong-consistency rule is R + W > RF, strictly greater. R + W = RF is not enough.
  • HBase is not a relational database on Hadoop: no joins, no SQL of its own, sorted by row key only. MongoDB stores BSON, not JSON text.
  • Favourite question: “Compare RDBMS and NoSQL” and “Explain the CAP theorem with examples”.

6.6 Apache Spark and stream processing

Apache Spark is a cluster computing engine that keeps intermediate data in memory instead of writing it to HDFS after every step. That makes loops (machine learning, graph algorithms) and interactive queries far faster than chains of MapReduce jobs. One engine offers SQL and DataFrames, streaming, machine learning (MLlib) and graph processing (GraphX), in Python, Scala, Java and R. Spark began at UC Berkeley’s AMPLab in 2009 and runs on YARN, Kubernetes or its own cluster manager, usually reading HDFS or cloud storage. Stream processing handles data in motion: payments, clicks and sensor readings processed seconds after they happen, often read from Apache Kafka.

You need: Chapter 6.3 (map, shuffle, reduce) and Chapter 6.4 (YARN). Python lambda functions, as used with Pandas in Unit 2.

RDDs, transformations and actions

Spark’s core data structure is the RDD (resilient distributed dataset): an immutable collection split into partitions spread over the cluster. It is resilient because each RDD remembers its lineage, the chain of operations that built it; if a node dies, Spark recomputes the lost partitions from the lineage instead of keeping copies.

  • Transformations build a new RDD from an old one and are lazy: nothing runs yet. Examples: map, filter, flatMap, reduceByKey, groupByKey, join.
  • Actions return a result to the driver or write output, and trigger the actual work. Examples: count, collect, take, reduce, saveAsTextFile.
  • A narrow transformation (map, filter) builds each output partition from one input partition, so no data moves. A wide transformation (reduceByKey, groupByKey, join) needs a shuffle, exactly as in MapReduce.

When an action is called, the driver (the program holding the SparkSession) turns the lineage into a DAG (directed acyclic graph) of operations and cuts it into stages at every shuffle. Each stage runs one task per partition on executors, the worker processes that the cluster manager starts. cache() keeps an RDD in executor memory so later actions reuse it; this is what makes a 20-iteration k-means fast. Today most code uses DataFrames (an RDD with named columns, optimised by Spark’s Catalyst optimizer) and Spark SQL, but the execution model is the same.

Word count in PySpark, on the same four lines as Chapter 6.3, stored as two partitions:

from pyspark.sql import SparkSession
spark = SparkSession.builder.appName("wc").getOrCreate()
lines  = spark.sparkContext.textFile("hdfs:///data/chat.txt", 2)
counts = (lines.flatMap(lambda l: l.lower().split())
               .map(lambda w: (w, 1))
               .reduceByKey(lambda a, b: a + b))
print(counts.collect())

Output, in some order: [('cricket', 2), ('pay', 3), ('upi', 4)]. Notice that reduceByKey adds values inside each partition before the shuffle, like a MapReduce combiner; groupByKey would ship every (word, 1) pair.

Stream processing: Kafka, Spark Streaming and windows

Batch processing works on bounded data: a file with an end. A stream is unbounded: events keep arriving and results must keep updating with low delay. Three pieces are taught:

  • Apache Kafka (built at LinkedIn) is a distributed, durable log. Producers append events to a topic; each topic is split into partitions, each an ordered, append-only log in which every event has an offset. Brokers store and replicate the partitions. Consumers in a consumer group divide the partitions among themselves and remember their offsets, so events can be replayed after a failure.
  • Spark Streaming (the older DStream API) cuts the stream into micro-batches, one small RDD every batch interval such as 2 seconds. Structured Streaming treats the stream as a table that keeps growing and reuses the DataFrame API; it also runs as micro-batches by default, so latency is around a second.
  • Apache Flink processes each event as it arrives (true streaming), giving lower latency and strong support for event-time processing.

Aggregates on a stream are computed over windows. A tumbling window has a fixed size and no overlap (every 10 s). A sliding window has a size and a smaller slide (10 s long, starting every 5 s), so windows overlap and each event falls in size ÷ slide = 2 of them. A session window closes after a gap with no events. Windows should use event time (when the payment happened), not processing time (when Spark saw it), and a watermark says how late an event may arrive and still be counted. In Structured Streaming:

counts = (payments
          .withWatermark("event_time", "30 seconds")
          .groupBy(window("event_time", "10 seconds", "5 seconds"), "merchant")
          .count())

6.7 Data warehouses and data lakes

A data warehouse is a central store of cleaned, integrated, historical data, organised for analysis and reports and kept separate from the operational databases that run the business. A data lake stores raw data of every type at low cost in its original format, and applies a structure only when someone reads it. A warehouse answers known business questions fast and reliably (“sales by state by quarter”); a lake keeps everything for data science and for questions nobody has asked yet. Most large organisations run both, or a lakehouse that combines them.

You need: Unit 2 (Data integration and ETL processes), Chapter 6.2 (HDFS) and Chapter 6.4 (Hive).

The warehouse: definition, OLTP and OLAP

Bill Inmon defined a data warehouse as a subject-oriented, integrated, time-variant and non-volatile collection of data that supports management decisions. Subject-oriented: organised around sales, customers and products, not around one application. Integrated: codes, names and units made consistent across sources (“TN”, “Tamil Nadu” and “33” become one value). Time-variant: history is kept and every fact carries a date. Non-volatile: data is loaded and read, not updated in place.

Data flows from sources through ETL (extract, transform, load) in a staging area, into the warehouse, and from there into data marts, smaller subject-specific subsets such as a sales mart. Analysts reach it through an OLAP server and BI (business intelligence) tools. Inmon’s approach is top-down (build the enterprise warehouse first, then marts); Ralph Kimball’s is bottom-up (build dimensional marts that share common, “conformed” dimensions).

Dimensional modelling and OLAP operations

  • A fact table holds the numeric measures of a business event (quantity, amount) and a foreign key to each dimension. Its grain says what one row means, such as one product on one bill line. It is the largest table.
  • Dimension tables hold the descriptive attributes used to filter and group: date (day, month, quarter, year), store (city, state), product (brand, category).
  • Star schema: one fact table joined directly to denormalised dimensions (Figure 6.7.1). One join per dimension, simple and fast.
  • Snowflake schema: dimensions normalised into sub-tables (store → city → state). Less redundancy, more joins.
  • Fact constellation (galaxy): several fact tables, such as sales and shipments, sharing dimensions.

The data can be pictured as a cube with one axis per dimension and a measure in each cell. The five OLAP operations are roll-up (aggregate up a hierarchy, city → state, or drop a dimension), drill-down (the reverse, quarter → month), slice (fix one dimension to one value), dice (select a sub-cube with conditions on two or more dimensions) and pivot (rotate the axes of the report). Measures may be additive (sales add across every dimension), semi-additive (a bank balance adds across accounts but not across days) or non-additive (a profit percentage).

Data lakes and lakehouses

A data lake lands data as it comes (CSV, JSON, logs, images, audio) in cheap storage, HDFS or a cloud object store, usually in zones: raw (exactly as received), curated (cleaned, deduplicated, in columnar formats such as Parquet) and consumption (ready for reports or models). It uses schema-on-read, the opposite of the warehouse’s schema-on-write. This suits ELT: load first, transform later inside the lake with Spark or Hive. Without a catalogue, ownership and quality checks, a lake becomes a data swamp that nobody can search or trust (Chapter 6.8). A lakehouse adds open table formats (Delta Lake, Apache Iceberg, Apache Hudi) that give lake files ACID transactions, schemas and time travel, so one copy of the data serves both BI and machine learning.

6.8 Data security, privacy and governance

Big data platforms hold data about people: payments, locations, health records, browsing. Security keeps that data from being read, changed or destroyed by the wrong people. Privacy decides what personal data may be collected and used at all, and protects the people it describes. Data governance is the set of roles, policies and processes that keep data accurate, documented, secure and lawful through its whole life. India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act) and the European Union’s GDPR turn these into legal duties with large penalties, so every data scientist needs the basics.

You need: Chapters 6.2 to 6.7 (where the data sits); Unit 1 (Data sources and data collection).

Security: the CIA triad on a Hadoop cluster

Security aims at three properties: confidentiality (only authorised people read the data), integrity (data is not altered without detection) and availability (data can be used when needed). Out of the box, Hadoop trusts whatever user name a client claims, so a production cluster adds layers, each answering one question (Figure 6.8.1):

  • Perimeter: the cluster sits on a private network behind a firewall; users enter through a gateway such as Apache Knox.
  • Authentication (who are you?): Hadoop’s secure mode uses Kerberos. A user logs in to the Key Distribution Centre once and receives a ticket; every service, NameNode and DataNodes included, checks that ticket instead of a password.
  • Authorization (what may you do?): HDFS has Unix-style permissions (owner, group, others; read, write, execute) and access control lists (ACLs). Apache Ranger manages central policies down to a Hive column, with row filters and column masking (“analysts see only the last 4 digits of the phone number”).
  • Encryption: in transit with TLS; at rest with HDFS encryption zones, directories whose files are encrypted with keys held in a key management server (KMS), so a stolen disk is unreadable.
  • Auditing: every access is logged (who, what, when, allowed or denied) so misuse can be found and proved.

Privacy: personal data and how to protect it

Personal data is any data about an identifiable person: name, Aadhaar number, phone, email, face photo. Direct identifiers name a person by themselves. Quasi-identifiers such as age, gender and PIN code look harmless, but combined they often single out one person, so deleting the name column is not enough. The techniques, from weakest to strongest:

  • Data minimisation: do not collect what the purpose does not need.
  • Masking: hide part of a value for display or testing (XXXX-XXXX-4821).
  • Pseudonymisation or tokenisation: replace an identifier with a token; whoever holds the mapping table can reverse it, so the data is still personal data.
  • Anonymisation: make re-identification impossible in practice, for example by generalisation (age 23 → 20–29) and suppression (delete a value or a row). A table is k-anonymous if every combination of quasi-identifier values appears in at least k rows, so each person hides in a crowd of k. l-diversity further requires at least l different sensitive values in each such group.

Under the DPDP Act, 2023, the person is the Data Principal, the organisation that decides why and how data is processed is the Data Fiduciary, and a company processing on its behalf is a Data Processor. Processing needs a lawful purpose and, for most uses, consent that is free, specific, informed and unambiguous, given after a clear notice. Data Principals have rights to access, correction, erasure and grievance redressal. Fiduciaries must protect the data with reasonable security safeguards and report breaches to the Data Protection Board of India and to the people affected; failing to take those safeguards can cost up to ₹250 crore. The EU’s GDPR (applied from May 2018) has similar ideas, adds rights such as data portability, and fines up to €20 million or 4% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher.

Governance: roles, metadata and lineage

Governance assigns people and rules. The data owner (a business head) is accountable for a data set and approves access; the data steward defines terms and watches quality; the data custodian (the IT team) stores, secures and backs it up. Data is classified (public, internal, confidential, restricted) and each class gets its controls. Data quality is measured on accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity and uniqueness. A data catalogue lists what data exists, what each column means and who owns it, and lineage records where each column came from and which jobs changed it (Apache Atlas does this for Hadoop). A lifecycle policy covers creation, storage, use, sharing, archiving and deletion, with retention periods. This is what keeps a lake from becoming a swamp (Chapter 6.7).

Question. A hospital wants to share the table below with researchers. (a) Classify the columns. (b) Find k after removing names. (c) Find k after generalising age to decades and PIN to five digits (60001*, 60002*). (d) Make the table at least 3-anonymous and check l-diversity. (e) What is the largest chance of re-identifying a person in your final table? (8 marks)

  2. (b) With names removed, every (age, gender, PIN) triple is different, so each group has 1 row: k = 1. Anyone who knows Bala is a 27-year-old man from 600020 learns he has asthma.
  3. (c) Groups: (20–29, M, 60001*) = rows 1, 5, 7 (3 rows); (20–29, M, 60002*) = row 2 alone (1 row); (30–39, F, 60002*) = rows 3, 4, 6, 8 (4 rows). k is the smallest group: k = 1, because Bala is still alone.

Exam traps

  • Privacy is not the same as security. A perfectly secured database still breaks privacy if it holds data collected without consent or uses it for another purpose.
  • Removing names is not anonymisation; quasi-identifiers re-identify people. Pseudonymised data is still personal data.
  • Authentication asks who you are (Kerberos); authorization asks what you may do (ACLs, Ranger).
  • In the DPDP Act the person is the Data Principal and the company is the Data Fiduciary, not the other way round.
  • Favourite question: “What are the security and privacy challenges of big data, and how does the Hadoop ecosystem address them?”

Takeaways

  1. Big data is defined by the five Vs, not by size alone, and it forces scale-out on clusters of ordinary machines that are expected to fail.
  2. HDFS cuts files into 128 MB blocks with three rack-aware replicas. The NameNode holds metadata in RAM; DataNodes hold the blocks; file data never passes through the NameNode.
  3. MapReduce is map (emit key–value pairs), shuffle (group by key across the network) and reduce (aggregate). Combiners cut traffic but only for associative operations.
  4. YARN shares the cluster: one ResourceManager, a NodeManager per node, an ApplicationMaster per job and containers for tasks. Hive (SQL) and Pig (data flows) compile into jobs that YARN runs.
  5. NoSQL stores trade joins and full ACID for scale and flexible schemas: HBase is consistent and sits on HDFS, MongoDB stores documents, Cassandra is a masterless ring whose reads see the latest write when R + W > RF.
  6. Spark keeps data in memory as RDDs and DataFrames, recovers lost partitions from lineage and splits work into stages at shuffles. Streams, often from Kafka, are aggregated over windows by event time.
  7. Warehouses (schema-on-write, star schemas, OLAP) give trusted answers to known questions; lakes (schema-on-read, raw zones) keep everything; lakehouses combine the two.
  8. Security (Kerberos, Ranger, encryption, audit), privacy (minimise, mask, tokenise, anonymise, consent under the DPDP Act) and governance (owners, catalogue, lineage, quality) apply at every layer.
