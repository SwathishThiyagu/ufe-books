4.1 Geometric modeling: object shape and visual appearance

A virtual object is stored in two parts. Geometry records the shape: where the surface sits in space. Appearance records how that surface looks: its colour, shine and pattern. The headset draws nothing until both parts exist. A helmet with the right shape but no material looks like grey plastic; a rich material on the wrong shape still looks wrong. Modelling is the step that writes both parts into the scene file, before transforms, physics or rendering touch the object. The rendering pipeline reads this data later.

You need: Unit 1, components of a VR system, and comfort with 3D coordinates (x, y, z), plus the idea of a scene file from model management.

Shape stored as a polygon mesh

Shape is stored as a polygon mesh. A mesh has three records. A vertex is a point in space, written as three numbers (x, y, z) in metres. An edge joins two vertices. A face is a closed loop of edges that encloses a flat area. Graphics hardware draws triangles, so faces are triangles or are split into triangles.

A triangle stores three vertex indices. Two triangles that share a corner store that corner once in the vertex array. Both triangles refer to it from the index array. This is an indexed mesh. A grid of 100 × 100 quads has 10,201 shared vertices and 20,000 triangles.

A normal is a unit vector at right angles to a surface. It tells the shader which way the surface faces. The face normal comes from the cross product of two triangle edges.

n = (v<sub>1</sub> − v<sub>0</sub>) × (v<sub>2</sub> − v<sub>0</sub>), then n̂ = n ÷ |n|

v0, v1, v2 are the corner positions in metres; × is the cross product; |n| is the length of n; n̂ is the unit face normal.

Tessellation splits a coarse polygon into smaller triangles. It rounds a curved silhouette and adds detail near the camera. Steps to build a mesh: (1) list the vertices, (2) list triangles as index triples, (3) compute face and vertex normals, (4) tessellate where smoothness is needed.

Curves, surfaces, implicit shapes, voxels and points

Not every shape is a triangle mesh. A parametric curve is a point that moves with one number t. A Bézier curve is P(t) = Σ Bi(t) Pi for t in [0, 1]. Pi are control points in metres; Bi(t) are blending functions. The curve is smooth and stays inside the convex hull of its control points. A designer places a few control points to get a car body line.

A parametric surface uses two numbers (u, v). It maps a flat square of parameters into 3D. This is how a helmet shell or a chair seat is modelled before it is tessellated into triangles.

An implicit surface is written as f(x, y, z) = 0. A sphere of radius r centred at the origin is x² + y² + z² − r² = 0. The sign of f tells inside from outside in one test. Metaballs and smooth blends between shapes use this form.

A voxel is a small cube in a regular 3D grid. Each voxel stores whether it is filled, or a density value. Medical scans arrive this way. Point clouds are unordered lists of 3D points from a depth camera or laser scanner. They have no faces and no normals, so a meshing step is needed before ordinary rendering.

Appearance: material, texture and shader

Appearance is stored as material values plus texture data. A material describes how the surface responds to light. Diffuse colour is the base colour seen under broad light. It is stored as three floats, one each for red, green and blue, usually from 0 to 1. Specular level sets the strength of the bright highlight. Roughness sets how spread out that highlight is: a low value gives a sharp dot, a high value gives a broad sheen.

Texture is a 2D image laid over the surface. UV mapping gives every vertex a pair (u, v). Both numbers lie between 0 and 1, whatever the image size in pixels. The rasteriser interpolates u and v across each triangle and reads the image at that point. Vertices store the coordinates, never the whole image.

A shader is a small program that combines these inputs with the lights. A vertex shader can pass normals and UVs forward; a fragment shader can look up the texture and compute the diffuse plus specular colour. Unit 5 covers writing shaders.

Worked example

Question. A square wall is stored as two triangles sharing four vertices. Each vertex stores a position (3 floats) and a UV pair (2 floats); each float is 4 bytes. Each triangle stores three uint16 indices. The wall material stores diffuse colour as 3 floats. Find the total bytes.

  1. Number of vertices = 4; number of triangles = 2.
  2. Position bytes = 4 vertices × 3 floats × 4 bytes = 48 B.
  3. UV bytes = 4 vertices × 2 floats × 4 bytes = 32 B.
  4. Index bytes = 2 triangles × 3 indices × 2 bytes = 12 B (uint16 is 2 bytes).
  5. Diffuse colour bytes = 3 floats × 4 bytes = 12 B.
  6. Total = 48 + 32 + 12 + 12 = 104 B.

Answer. The wall model occupies 104 bytes.

Exam traps

  • Counting one vertex per triangle: shared corners are stored once and reused through the index buffer.
  • Mixing float count with byte count: 3 floats is 12 bytes, not 3 bytes.
  • Storing a full texture image at every vertex: a vertex carries only its (u, v) coordinates.
  • Treating a normal as a position: a normal is a direction, not a point in metres.
  • Forgetting that a quad wall is two triangles when counting indices.

4.2 Kinematics modeling: transformation matrices and object hierarchies

In VR every object must know where it is. A hand controller, a virtual hammer, a robot arm — each has a position and an orientation in the world. Kinematics modeling stores that as a 4 × 4 matrix and links objects into a parent-child tree. A child is described only relative to its parent, so moving the parent moves the whole family. The world matrix of any object is its parent's world matrix multiplied by its own local matrix. Scene graphs in every VR toolkit and game engine work this way.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for what a vertex is, plus school-level vectors and matrices.

One matrix holds position, rotation and scale

A 3D point is written (x, y, z). Kinematics adds a fourth number, 1, giving the homogeneous coordinate (x, y, z, 1). That extra 1 is what lets one matrix both rotate and translate a point.

M = [ r₁₁ r₁₂ r₁₃ tₓ ; r₂₁ r₂₂ r₂₃ t_y ; r₃₁ r₃₂ r₃₃ t_z ; 0 0 0 1 ]

M is the 4 × 4 local transform; the top-left 3 × 3 block r holds rotation or scale; (tₓ, t_y, t_z) is the object's origin in the parent frame, in metres; the bottom row 0 0 0 1 keeps the coordinates homogeneous.

Read the matrix in two parts. The 3 × 3 block is rotation, and its three columns are the object's own axes written in the parent frame: column 1 is where local +x points, column 2 is local +y, column 3 is local +z. The last column is the object's origin. Scale goes on the diagonal of the 3 × 3 block.

p′ = M · p

p is a vertex written as the column (x, y, z, 1); M is the transform matrix; p′ is the transformed vertex (x′, y′, z′, 1). Each row of M is dotted with the column p.

A 3 × 3 matrix cannot translate: it always leaves the origin fixed. The fourth column and fourth row fix that. The same trick makes matrices composable — two moves collapse into one matrix, so a thousand vertices cost one multiply each.

Rz(θ) = [ cos θ −sin θ 0 ; sin θ cos θ 0 ; 0 0 1 ]

Rz(θ) rotates a point by θ degrees anticlockwise about the z axis in the xy plane; θ is measured in degrees here, radians inside the maths.

Multiplication order carries meaning

Matrix multiplication is not commutative: A · B is usually not B · A. So the order must be fixed by convention. VR systems use column vectors, meaning the vertex sits on the right and the factor nearest the vertex acts first.

M = T · R · S

T is the translation matrix, R the rotation, S the scale; the rightmost factor S acts on the vertex first, then R, then T.

If you write S · R · T instead, the translation column gets scaled and rotated too, and the object lands somewhere else. The two matrices look similar and behave differently.

Rotation angles are easy for one axis and awkward for three; Chapter 4.8 covers yaw, pitch, roll and quaternions for general orientation.

Object hierarchies and the scene graph

A scene graph is a tree of objects. The root is the world. Every node stores one local matrix L, relative to its parent, plus a mesh from Chapter 4.1.

W = W_parent · L

W is the node's world matrix, W_parent the world matrix of its parent, and L the node's own local matrix. The parent is always on the left.

Two things stay fixed inside a parent: the child's local matrix, and every distance measured in the parent's frame. A robot base at (10, 0) rotated 90° holds an arm at local (2, 0). Turn the base and the arm's local (2, 0) never changes — only where that (2, 0) lands in world space. The figure below shows the tree. Move the parent and the whole subtree moves; move a leaf and nothing else does.

The cost is recomputation. A change near the root invalidates every node below it, so toolkits walk the tree top-down once per frame and rebuild world matrices. Renderers then read one flat world matrix per draw call.

Viewing the world: the look-at matrix

The camera is an object too. To draw, every vertex must move into camera space, so the renderer multiplies by a view matrix V, which is the inverse of the camera's world matrix: V = W_cam⁻¹.

The look-at method builds V from three inputs: eye (where the camera is), target (what it looks at) and up (roughly world +y, which fixes roll). Take forward f = normalize(target − eye), right r = normalize(f × up), then true up u = r × f. Those three unit vectors become the rows of the rotation part.

V = [ rₓ r_y r_z −r·eye ; uₓ u_y u_z −u·eye ; −fₓ −f_y −f_z f·eye ; 0 0 0 1 ]

r, u, f are the unit right, up and forward vectors; eye is the camera position; the dot products shift the world so the camera sits at the origin looking down −z.

Projection, clipping and the rest of the pipeline are in Chapter 4.5. For this chapter, remember only that the view matrix is one more transform matrix in the same chain.

Worked example

Question. A robot base sits at (10, 0, 0) m and is rotated 90° anticlockwise about the z axis. The arm's local origin is (2, 0, 0) in the base frame. Find the arm's world position and orientation.

  1. Write the base's local matrix: it is translated to (10, 0, 0) and rotated 90°, so M_base = T(10, 0, 0) · Rz(90°).
  2. Fill Rz(90°) with cos 90° = 0 and sin 90° = 1: rows are [0 −1 0; 1 0 0; 0 0 1].
  3. Write the arm's local origin as a column: p = (2, 0, 0, 1).
  4. Rotate first: Rz(90°) · (2, 0, 0) = (0×2 − 1×0, 1×2 + 0×0, 0) = (0, 2, 0) m.
  5. Then translate: (10, 0, 0) + (0, 2, 0) = (10, 2, 0) m.
  6. Orientation: the base's local +x axis maps to Rz(90°) · (1, 0, 0) = (0, 1, 0), so the arm points along world +y. Total rotation is 90° about z.

Answer. The arm's world origin is (10, 2, 0) m with an orientation of 90° anticlockwise about the z axis.

Exam traps

  • Parent × local, not local × parent. A base turned 90° sends an arm at local (2, 0) to a world offset of (0, 2), never (2, 0).
  • Column vectors put the translation in the last column; row vectors put it in the last row. Mixing the two conventions silently transposes every matrix in the answer.
  • A parent's rotation moves the child's position as well as its axes. Many answers rotate the axes and then forget the offset.
  • T · R is not R · T. Rotating after a translation turns the translation column as well, so the object lands elsewhere.
  • A non-uniform parent scale shears children: a child rotated 45° inside a parent scaled (2, 1, 1) no longer has perpendicular axes.

Analyze A parent node is scaled by 2 on all three axes. Its child has local origin (1, 1, 0) and a local rotation of 45° about z. What changes in the child's world matrix? 4 marks

The world origin moves to (2, 2, 0) because the offset is scaled. The 45° rotation is unchanged, since uniform scale commutes with rotation; a non-uniform scale would shear it. Every distance measured inside the parent doubles.

Analyze Why does moving a leaf node leave the root's world matrix alone, while moving the root forces every node to be recomputed? 4 marks

A node's world matrix depends only on its ancestors. A leaf is nobody's ancestor, so only its own matrix changes. The root appears as a factor in every descendant's product, so changing it changes all of them.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Production engines store transforms as position, quaternion and scale (TRS) and rebuild the 4 × 4 matrix only when one of the three changes, because quaternions interpolate without gimbal lock and use less memory. Skinned characters use dual quaternions so a joint's rotation does not shrink the mesh. GPUs receive world matrices in bulk, and instancing draws thousands of copies of one mesh with a matrix per copy. Search for scene graph traversal, TRS decomposition and dual quaternion skinning to go further.

4.3 Physical modeling: collision detection and force computation

Physical modeling adds a layer that answers two questions: are two objects touching, and what force results? Collision detection finds contact. Force computation turns that contact into a push, a spring force, or a surface change. In VR this layer lets a hand push a virtual door, a tool press into a soft organ, or a haptic device resist your fingers. The computation must be fast enough for every frame, often within a few milliseconds. This chapter covers the detection stages, bounding volumes, penetration depth, penalty springs, mass-spring surfaces, and force smoothing.

You need: Chapter 4.1 for shape data and Chapter 4.2 for world transforms.

Broad phase and narrow phase

Collision detection runs in two stages. The broad phase is a cheap reject test. It compares simple bounding volumes around each body. A sphere has centre C and radius r. An axis-aligned bounding box (AABB) has edges parallel to the world axes. An oriented bounding box (OBB) rotates with the object, so it fits a long tilted object more tightly. The broad phase may report extra pairs; that is safe. It must not miss a true pair. Spatial hashing and sweep-and-prune are common broad-phase methods.

Only pairs that pass broad phase go to narrow phase. Narrow phase uses the real geometry. For two spheres, let C1 and C2 be centres in metres and r1 and r2 be radii in metres. The centre distance is d. A collision exists when d < r1 + r2. The contact normal is the unit vector n = (C2 − C1)/d. It points from body 1 to body 2. The penetration depth is x = (r1 + r2) − d. Positive x means overlap. Zero or negative x means no contact.

d = √((X2−X1)² + (Y2−Y1)² + (Z2−Z1)²),  x = (r1 + r2) − d

C1=(X1,Y1,Z1) and C2=(X2,Y2,Z2) are sphere centres in metres; r1 and r2 are radii in metres; d is centre distance in metres; x is penetration depth in metres; contact exists when x > 0.

  1. Transform each body to world space with the hierarchy from Chapter 4.2.
  2. Build a bounding volume for each body and run the broad-phase test.
  3. Run the exact narrow-phase test only on candidate pairs.
  4. If overlap exists, output the contact point, unit normal n, and penetration x.

Penetration depth and penalty force

A penalty method treats overlap as a spring. The spring is not drawn; it is a mathematical force. Hooke's law gives the force magnitude F = k x. Here k is spring stiffness in newtons per metre (N/m), x is penetration depth in metres, and F is force in newtons (N). The force vector on body 2 is F2 = k x n. The force on body 1 is F1 = −k x n. This pair obeys Newton's third law: equal and opposite. The force acts only while x > 0.

F = k x, F2 = k x n, F1 = −k x n

k is stiffness in N/m; x is penetration in m; F is force magnitude in N; n is the unit contact normal from body 1 to body 2; F1 and F2 are force vectors in N.

An impulse method is an alternative. It changes velocity at impact and then separates the bodies. A penalty method is simpler and lets small overlap remain. It can feel springy if k is low. Large k needs small time steps or the simulation can become unstable. The choice depends on the scene.

Raw force can jump when contact starts or ends. Force smoothing reduces this. A first-order low-pass filter is F_smooth(t) = α F(t) + (1−α) F_smooth(t−1). α is a number from 0 to 1. A small α gives heavy smoothing and more delay. A large α follows the raw force closely. The smoothed value is then scaled and clamped to the haptic device's force range. That mapping step belongs here; device hardware is covered in Unit 3, Haptic displays and force feedback devices.

Deformable surfaces and haptic force output

Soft objects need surface change, not only rigid push. A mass-spring model represents the surface as particles at mesh vertices. Springs connect neighbouring particles. For one spring between particles A and B, let L be current length, L0 rest length, and u the unit vector from A to B. The elastic force on A is FA = −k_s (L − L0) u. k_s is spring stiffness in N/m. If L > L0, the force pulls A toward B. Damping c can be added using relative velocity. The simulator integrates these forces over a small time step to move the vertices.

FA = −k_s (L − L0) u

k_s is spring stiffness in N/m; L is current spring length in m; L0 is rest length in m; u is the unit vector from particle A to particle B; FA is the force on particle A in N.

Penalty springs and mass-spring meshes can work together. The penalty force stops a tool from sinking too far. The mass-spring mesh spreads the deformation over nearby vertices. A surgical trainer may use this for a virtual liver. A seat prototype may use it for cushion compression.

  1. Broad phase: build candidate pairs.
  2. Narrow phase: compute contact point, normal n, and penetration x.
  3. Force: use a penalty spring or impulse to compute vector F.
  4. Smooth: apply the low-pass filter to force magnitude.
  5. Clamp and map: scale to device limits and send to haptic output.
  6. Respond: update rigid motion or deform the surface.

The same pipeline runs for every simulated frame. Detection alone is not response; the force or deformation step must follow.

Worked example

Question. Two spheres each of radius 0.5 m have centres 0.9 m apart. A penalty spring with k = 200 N/m acts along the centre line. Find the penetration depth and the force magnitude.

  1. Sum of radii R = r1 + r2 = 0.5 m + 0.5 m = 1.0 m.
  2. Centre distance d = 0.9 m.
  3. Penetration x = R − d = 1.0 m − 0.9 m = 0.1 m.
  4. Force magnitude F = k x = 200 N/m × 0.1 m = 20 N.
  5. The force acts along the contact normal, the line from one centre to the other. If C1=(0,0,0) and C2=(0.9,0,0) m, n=(1,0,0). The force on body 1 is 20 N in the −x direction; the force on body 2 is 20 N in the +x direction.

Output

0.1
20

Answer. Penetration depth is 0.1 m and force magnitude is 20 N along the contact normal.

Exam traps

  • Use the sum of both radii. In the worked example, using only one radius gives 0.5 m instead of 1.0 m.
  • Force is a vector along the contact normal. The sign tells which body is pushed; magnitude alone is not the full answer.
  • Collision detection alone is not collision response. Narrow phase reports contact; a penalty force, impulse or deformation must still be applied.
  • Broad-phase false positives are acceptable; false negatives are not. The bounding volume must fully enclose the object.
  • Keep units consistent: k in N/m and x in m give F in N. Mixing cm or mm changes the force by a factor of 100 or 1000.

4.4 Behavior modeling and model management

A virtual world is more than shapes. Each object needs rules that say what it does when a user grabs it, when a timer fires or when another object touches it. That is behavior modeling. The world also needs a database that stores every object, mesh, light, camera and panel in one searchable place. That is model management. Together they let a VR scene grow from a few test objects to a room, a building or a city without losing track of what exists and what should run.

You need: object shape and appearance and transformation hierarchies.

Behavior modeling: scripts, states and events

A behavior is a rule or set of rules attached to an object. It changes the object's attributes, plays feedback, or sends a message. The rule is stored in a script record: a small program with conditions and actions. A lamp script may check a switch attribute; when it is true, the script sets the light record on and plays a click sound. The script belongs to the object instance. It does not belong to each level of detail (LOD) mesh.

A state machine is a common way to write behavior. It lists states, such as closed, opening, open and closing for a door. An event, such as a hand press or a timer tick, triggers a transition only when its guard condition (a true/false test) is true. The transition runs an action. The door example: event press in state closed with guard unlocked → state opening; after 1 s timer → state open. Feedback is the visible, audible or haptic result: a door creak, a button colour change, a short vibration.

Events can come from input, collision, a timer or another script. Behavior runs in the world update loop: read events, test guards, change states, update attributes. The authoring tool stores the script with the object; playback runs it. Keep physics separate: physics computes forces in 4.3; behavior decides what to do with the result.

Model management: one database for the whole world

Model management is the scene database. It stores every record the world needs and gives each one an identity, a type and a place. World space is the common 3D coordinate frame for the whole scene. A point in world space is written (x, y, z) in metres, where x, y and z are distances along three axes. Each object also has a local transform: position and orientation relative to its parent. The hierarchy in 4.2 turns local transforms into world coordinates.

Record types include: geometry (tessellated data: vertices, triangles, normals, texture coordinates); appearance; LOD records (several mesh versions plus switch distances); bounding volume (a simple box or sphere around an object for fast tests); scripts and attributes (named values such as health, colour, speed); cullers and occluders (records that help discard unseen objects; selection is in 4.6); lights; cameras; 2D controls and control panels (buttons, sliders, labels); and room, stage or area descriptions (named regions with bounds).

Authoring writes these records; playback loads them, builds the hierarchy, starts scripts and draws frames. A culler is not the same as an occluder: a culler tests view volume, an occluder hides objects behind a large surface. A VR toolkit is only the container for the database; toolkit APIs and game engines are in Unit 5.

Counting records without double-counting

A managed world is counted by instances, not by rooms or by LODs. Let R be rooms, O objects per room, L LOD meshes per object, S scripts per object. The count starts from object instances and then multiplies by mesh or script records.

N<sub>obj</sub> = R × O; N<sub>mesh</sub> = N<sub>obj</sub> × L; N<sub>script</sub> = N<sub>obj</sub> × S

R is rooms; O is objects per room; L is LOD meshes per object; S is scripts per object; Nobj, Nmesh and Nscript are object, mesh and script record counts.

Use this when a question gives rooms, objects, LODs and scripts. The LOD count multiplies meshes only; it does not multiply scripts. A room with 50 objects and 3 LODs has 150 mesh records but 50 object records. If each object has 2 scripts, the room has 100 script records. Search and storage grow with the number of records, so model management needs names, types and bounds for queries.

World authoring and playback both use the same database. Authoring adds or edits records: a new chair, a changed script, a new light. Playback opens the database read-only, loads only needed rooms, and runs the update loop. A room description is a named area with bounds; it lets a query ask for 'all objects in room 2' without scanning the whole world. A control panel is a 2D record placed in the world; its buttons send events to scripts.

Worked example

Question. A VR world has 4 rooms. Each room has 50 objects. Each object has 3 LOD meshes and 2 scripts. Count the object instances, the mesh records and the script records.

  1. Rooms R = 4; objects per room O = 50.
  2. Object instances Nobj = R × O = 4 × 50 = 200 objects.
  3. Each object has L = 3 LOD mesh records. Mesh records Nmesh = 200 × 3 = 600 mesh records.
  4. Each object has S = 2 script records. Script records Nscript = 200 × 2 = 400 script records.

Answer. The world has 200 object instances, 600 mesh records and 400 script records.

Exam traps

  • A script attaches to an object instance, not to each LOD. A 3-LOD object still has one record per script, not three.
  • World space is one common frame; local position is relative to a parent. Do not add parent and child positions as if both were already world coordinates.
  • Model management stores records. Rendering, culling choice and LOD switching are separate stages; see 4.5 and 4.6.
  • A state is not a transition. 'Door open' is a state; 'press closes it' is an event that triggers a transition.
  • A bounding volume is a cheap approximation, not the visible mesh. It may be larger than the object.

4.5 Graphics and rendering pipeline

A VR headset must draw two slightly different images, many times a second, from a scene of millions of triangles. The graphics pipeline is the fixed order of steps that does it: every vertex passes through model, world and view transforms, projection, clipping and a viewport map, then rasterisation and fragment shading give the pixels their colour. The order matters because a mistake at one stage cannot be repaired later — a missing perspective divide cannot be undone during shading. Every GPU driver implements this chain, and every VR toolkit assumes you know it.

You need: chapter 4.2 for matrix multiplication and chapter 4.1 for what a mesh stores.

The pipeline as an ordered chain

A mesh is stored in local coordinates (chapter 4.1). The pipeline turns those coordinates into pixel colours in a fixed order.

Model transform places the object inside its parent's frame. World transform gives world coordinates by multiplying up the hierarchy (chapter 4.2). View transform moves everything so the camera sits at the origin looking along one axis. Projection turns the visible pyramid into a cube. Clipping cuts away geometry outside that cube. The perspective divide gives normalised device coordinates (NDC), where every surviving point has x, y and z between −1 and 1. Viewport maps x and y to pixel columns and rows. Rasterisation lists the pixels each triangle covers, and fragment shading gives each covered pixel its colour.

The order is forced. Shading needs a pixel, so shading is last. Clipping needs depth in front of the camera, so it comes before the divide. For stereo, the late stages run twice with two view matrices whose camera centres sit about 63 mm apart — the interpupillary distance.

A VR frame at 90 Hz must finish in 11 ms, for both eyes.

Model, world and view matrices

Each object in the scene database carries a local transform M. Multiplying up the parent chain gives the parent's accumulated matrix W, so a local vertex p becomes p_world = W · M · p. The object's own world matrix is the product W · M.

The camera is an object too. Its world matrix C says where the headset is, so the view matrix is its inverse: V = C⁻¹. Built directly from an eye position e and a target t with up hint u, the camera axes are:

  • forward f = normalize(t − e)
  • right r = normalize(f × u)
  • true up u′ = r × f

The look-at matrix rotates r, u′ and −f onto the x, y and z axes, then translates e to the origin. A point straight ahead of the camera now has x = y = 0 and negative z.

p_clip = P · V · W · M · p

P is the projection matrix, V the view, W the world transform, M the model transform, and p = (x, y, z, 1) is the local vertex. Matrices act right to left: M first, P last.

Both eyes share the product W · M. Only V and P differ between them.

Projection and the perspective divide

Put the camera at the origin looking along +z, with an image plane at distance f in front. This section uses a +z-forward camera, so points in front of the eye have positive z; the image-plane formulas below use that convention. f is the focal length, in scene units, not pixels. A camera-space point (x, y, z) casts a ray from the eye, and similar triangles give where it crosses the plane:

x′ = f · x / z y′ = f · y / z

x′, y′ are image-plane coordinates in scene units; x, y, z are camera-space coordinates; f is focal length. Doubling z halves the image size, which is why distant objects look small.

A GPU does the same job with one 4×4 matrix. The projection matrix P copies z into the fourth clip coordinate w_c, scales y by 1/tan(θ/2) and x by 1/(a · tan(θ/2)), where θ is the vertical field of view and a = width/height is the aspect ratio. After P the vertex is in clip space, (x_c, y_c, z_c, w_c), and hardware divides:

x_ndc = x_c / w_c    y_ndc = y_c / w_c    z_ndc = z_c / w_c

the subscript c means clip space, ndc means normalised device coordinates. This is the perspective divide; w_c is zero at the eye and negative behind the camera.

The divide is why clipping comes first. Dropping it leaves points in clip space: nothing shrinks with distance, and the image collapses.

Clipping, NDC, viewport and fragments

Hardware clips against the near plane in clip space, before the divide, testing w > 0. After the divide, every surviving point lies in NDC: −1 ≤ x ≤ 1, −1 ≤ y ≤ 1, −1 ≤ z ≤ 1 (some APIs use 0 ≤ z ≤ 1). Clipping creates new edges at the boundary. Clip in clip space or NDC, never after the viewport map.

x_px = X + (x_ndc + 1) · W / 2        y_px = Y + (1 − y_ndc) · H / 2

W and H are viewport width and height in pixels, (X, Y) is the top-left corner of the viewport in pixels, rows count downward, and x_px, y_px are pixel indices. The y term is flipped for that reason; a bottom-left origin would need y_px = Y + (y_ndc + 1) · H / 2. z_ndc goes to the depth buffer.

Rasterisation visits the pixels inside each triangle and emits fragments. Each fragment carries interpolated depth, normal and texture coordinates. Texture coordinates are interpolated with a division by w; without it a textured floor in perspective looks stretched.

Fragment shading runs a program per fragment: sample the texture, apply lighting, blend if transparent. The depth test then discards fragments lying behind ones already drawn. In VR this tail runs twice per frame, once per eye, and the two images must reach the displays close together (chapter 4.7). Culling work is cut earlier, as chapter 4.6 describes.

Exam traps

  • Dropping the perspective divide by w or z: points stay in clip space, and the scene neither shrinks with distance nor lines up with the camera.
  • Mixing conventions: a camera looking down −z instead of +z flips the sign of z and of the projection's w row. Pick one convention and keep it.
  • Clipping after the viewport transform: pixel coordinates cannot be mapped back to 3D cleanly. Clip in clip space or NDC.
  • Confusing the view matrix with the projection matrix: view rotates and translates the world, projection scales x and y and writes w, with no translation.
  • Thinking the viewport map moves the object in 3D. It only turns NDC into pixel indices; depth is unchanged.

4.6 Level of detail, culling and large environments

A VR headset must hand a new frame to each eye about 90 times a second. That gives roughly 11 ms per frame for everything. A large world may hold a hundred thousand objects and billions of triangles, far more than any GPU can draw in that time. So the renderer stops being honest: it draws only the objects the user can see, and only at the detail the eye can resolve. This chapter covers those two savings, level of detail and culling, and how together they let a large environment run in real time.

You need: chapter 4.5 for the order of pipeline stages, and chapter 4.4 for the model records that carry several LOD versions of one object.

Level of detail: paying for detail only where it shows

A triangle smaller than a pixel is wasted work. A bench 3 m away needs its slats modelled. The same bench 60 m away covers a few dozen pixels, and a flat box looks the same. Level of detail (LOD) stores one object as several models at different triangle counts and picks one at draw time. LOD0 is the full model, LOD1 might be half the triangles, LOD2 a quarter.

Discrete LOD switches sharply at a distance threshold. The switch is cheap but visible as a pop. Two fixes are standard: switch at slightly different distances in each direction (hysteresis), or blend two neighbouring levels over a short range so the shape morphs.

Continuous LOD stores no copies. A progressive mesh records the ordered list of edge collapses that simplify the full model, so the renderer rebuilds any triangle count between the finest and the coarsest by replaying part of that list.

Choose the level by screen size, not raw distance, because pixel size also depends on resolution and field of view.

s = (h × H) ÷ (2 d tan(θ ÷ 2))

s is the object's height on screen in pixels, h its real height in metres, H the viewport height in pixels, d the distance from the camera in metres, and θ the vertical field of view in degrees.

  1. Read the object's bounding sphere centre and radius from its model record.
  2. Project the radius to pixels with the formula above.
  3. Pick the coarsest level whose projected size is at least the threshold, say 40 px.
  4. Keep the current level until the size falls below 0.8 × the threshold, so the switch does not flicker.

Culling: dropping what cannot be seen

The view frustum is the volume the camera can see. It is a pyramid with its top cut off, bounded by six planes: near, far, left, right, top and bottom. Test each object's bounding sphere, centre c and radius r, against the six planes. The sphere is outside when, for at least one plane, the signed distance from c to that plane is less than −r. That is six dot products and no triangles touched.

Backface culling drops triangles that face away from the camera. Take front faces as counter-clockwise on screen, the usual OpenGL convention. A triangle that winds clockwise on screen is then a back face. The convention is set by the graphics API, so state it before using the rule. On a closed mesh this removes about half the triangles, and in VR it pays twice because each eye needs its own image.

Frustum culling keeps the building in front of you even when a nearer wall hides it. Occlusion culling removes what something nearer blocks. Three ways to do it: cells and portals, a precomputed potentially visible set (PVS) per cell, and hardware occlusion queries that ask the GPU how many samples of a bounding box would pass the depth test, that is, how much of it is actually visible.

Do per-object frustum culling on the CPU first, before submitting anything to be drawn. Backface culling then costs nothing extra because the hardware does it during rasterisation. Issue occlusion queries last, because each one costs a round trip to the GPU.

Cells, portals and large environments

A building splits into cells (rooms) joined by portals (doors and windows). From the camera's cell, only cells reachable through a chain of visible portals can be seen. Each hop clips the portal to its parent's screen rectangle; when the rectangle is empty the walk stops, and every cell beyond it is never drawn.

Outdoors there are no portals, so terrain is tessellated into square tiles, each carrying its own LOD. A quadtree subdivides a tile only while its estimated screen error exceeds a threshold, so the ground near the feet is fine and the far hills are coarse. This quadtree terrain scheme is a form of chunked LOD. Geomipmapping is a related scheme: a fixed grid of square patches, each patch drawn at one of a few discrete levels, with neighbouring patch edges stitched so no cracks appear.

Memory follows the same rule. Only LODs near the head stay resident; a streaming manager loads tiles and models as the user moves and evicts the rest. Each model record from chapter 4.4 carries its LOD list and bounding volume, so the scene database answers "what is in this cell" quickly.

Static geometry merges into one buffer, and repeated objects draw with instancing, which keeps the count of draw calls small. Every culling and LOD saving is repeated for the second eye, so each removed object saves twice in a headset.

Worked example

Question. A VR scene contains 10,000 objects, each with 1,000 triangles. Frustum culling keeps 30% of the objects, occlusion culling keeps 50% of those, and the chosen LOD levels keep 40% of the triangles of each surviving object. Find the number of objects drawn, the triangles rendered per eye per frame, and the triangle rate needed at 90 frames per second.

  1. Frustum culling: 10,000 × 0.30 = 3,000 objects survive.
  2. Occlusion culling: 3,000 × 0.50 = 1,500 objects are actually drawn.
  3. Triangles before LOD: 1,500 objects × 1,000 triangles = 1,500,000 triangles.
  4. After LOD: 1,500,000 × 0.40 = 600,000 triangles per eye per frame.
  5. Rate needed: 600,000 triangles × 90 frames per second = 54,000,000 triangles per second.

Exam traps

  • Culling fractions multiply, they do not add: keeping 30% then 50% leaves 15% of the objects, not 20%.
  • LOD reduces triangles per object; it does not remove objects. Report object counts and triangle counts separately.
  • Occlusion culling needs an occluder that is solid and large enough to hide whole objects. Open sky hides nothing and a wire fence hides almost nothing, so flat desert ground gains little. Hills, buildings and walls are real occluders, so outdoor scenes do gain from it.
  • Screen size uses the distance to the bounding-sphere centre, not the nearest vertex. Measuring to the nearest vertex over-estimates the projected size, so a long object stays at high detail far too long. The centre rule errs the other way: a long object may drop to a coarse level slightly early, which shows as a pop, so pad the radius.
  • Test cheap first. An occlusion query on every object before frustum culling costs more than it saves.

4.7 Computing architectures for VR

A VR headset must show a new picture every few milliseconds, and the machine behind it must finish all the work for that picture inside that time. That time is the frame budget. A computing architecture for VR is the arrangement of CPU, GPU, memory and network that makes the work fit. Wired PC headsets, untethered headsets and multi-machine VR rooms each use a different arrangement. This chapter covers the two jobs the machine does, how machines are arranged, and the latency chain that decides whether a frame arrives on time.

You need: the rendering pipeline in Chapter 4.5 and the load-reduction ideas in Chapter 4.6.

Two jobs: scene update and rendering

Every VR frame is two kinds of work, done by two different processors.

The CPU, the general-purpose processor, does the scene update. It reads the newest head and hand tracker samples, runs the physics step of Chapter 4.3, recomputes world transforms from the hierarchy of Chapter 4.2, applies behaviour rules, and decides which objects survive culling and level of detail. It ends by handing the GPU a list of draw commands.

The GPU, the graphics processor, does the rendering. It runs the pipeline of Chapter 4.5: transform vertices, clip, rasterize, shade pixels, once for the left eye and once for the right.

The split exists because the two jobs suit different hardware. The CPU runs a few complicated tasks full of decisions and scattered memory access. The GPU runs one simple operation over millions of vertices and pixels at the same time.

Stereo adds load but not independence. Both eyes show the same scene, so the scene update is done once and shading twice. That is why VR costs roughly double the drawing work of the same scene on a flat screen.

Workstation-based, distributed and standalone systems

A workstation-based VR system keeps all the work in one desktop machine: a fast CPU, a discrete GPU, and a cable to the headset. It is the simplest arrangement and gives the shortest path from tracker to display, because nothing crosses a network. Design reviews, surgical planning and driving simulators often use it.

A distributed VR system spreads the work over several machines. One common pattern is a render server that draws the frame plus a display client that only shows it. Others are one GPU per eye, one PC per wall in a room-scale projection setup, or the physics step on a separate node. Distribution scales past the power of one machine, but every network hop adds delay, and the nodes must be synchronised so both eyes and all walls show the same instant of the scene.

A standalone headset goes the other way: one system-on-chip with a mobile GPU and shared memory. It is designed for watts and heat, not peak speed, so the scene is simplified to fit the power budget.

Inside any of these, parallel processing fills the frame. Several CPU cores take physics, culling and scripting at once, and the GPU shades millions of pixels per frame, thousands at a time per pass, or splits the screen into tiles.

Frame budget and motion-to-photon latency

The frame budget is the time allowed for one frame.

B = 1000 ÷ f

B is the frame budget in milliseconds; f is the display refresh rate in hertz, which is the same number as frames per second.

At 90 Hz the budget is 11.1 ms. At 120 Hz it is 8.3 ms.

The stages run in series: tracker sample, CPU scene update, GPU render, then display scan-out and pixel response. Their sum is the motion-to-photon latency, the delay from a real head movement to the photons leaving the screen. When that sum is larger than B, the frame misses its slot, and the user sees judder plus a lag between head and image.

Three ideas buy time. Pipelining: while the GPU draws frame n, the CPU prepares frame n+1, so the stages overlap. Late latching and reprojection: sample the tracker as late as possible, and if a frame is late, warp the previous image with the newest orientation instead of drawing from scratch. Load reduction: culling, level of detail and cheaper shading.

With no overlap the frames come out one after another, and the sustained rate is f_max = 1000 ÷ T, where T is the whole chain in milliseconds. With pipelining the stages overlap, so the display can be fed once every slowest-stage time. In the chain below the slowest stage is the GPU at 5 ms, so a pipelined system can feed the panel at up to 200 Hz. Each image is still 12 ms old. At 90 Hz the panel is therefore fed on time, and the cost appears as about 12 ms of lag, not as a fall to 83 frames per second.

Parallelism cannot beat one limit. The display refreshes on its own clock, so scan-out time is fixed, and a frame that is not ready when the scan begins waits for the next refresh.

Worked example

Question. A VR system targets 90 Hz. One frame takes 2 ms of tracking, 3 ms of CPU scene update, 5 ms of GPU rendering and 2 ms of display scan-out and response. Does it meet its frame budget? What is the highest sustained frame rate, without and with pipelining?

  1. Budget: B = 1000 ÷ f = 1000 ÷ 90 = 11.1 ms per frame.
  2. Total latency: T = 2 + 3 + 5 + 2 = 12 ms.
  3. Compare: 12 ms > 11.1 ms, so a frame misses its slot by 12 − 11.1 = 0.9 ms.
  4. No overlap: f_max = 1000 ÷ T = 1000 ÷ 12 = 83.3 frames per second.
  5. Pipelined: stages overlap, so frames leave at the slowest-stage rate, the GPU's 5 ms, i.e. up to 200 Hz. Each image is still 12 ms old.

Answer. In a series chain it misses the budget: 12 ms against 11.1 ms, sustaining only 83.3 frames per second. Pipelined, it can feed the 90 Hz panel on time at up to 200 Hz, but every image lags by 12 ms.

Exam traps

  • Unit slip: 90 Hz gives about 11.1 ms per frame, not 90 ms. Convert hertz to milliseconds with 1000 ÷ f.
  • Latency is not frame rate. Pipelining raises the frame rate without shortening any single frame's delay from movement to photons.
  • Stereo does not double the CPU scene update. The scene is updated once and shaded twice, so the extra cost falls on the GPU.
  • Parallel processing does not shorten display scan-out. The panel refreshes on its own clock and that floor stays fixed.
  • Distributed rendering does not reduce latency. It adds network delay and needs synchronisation, so it is chosen for scale, not for speed.

Budget = 1000 ÷ 120 = 8.3 ms. Latency = 1.5 + 2.5 + 4 + 2 = 10 ms, so the series chain misses by 1.7 ms and sustains 1000 ÷ 10 = 100 frames per second. Pipelined, frames leave at the slowest stage, the GPU at 4 ms, up to 250 Hz, so the 120 Hz panel can be fed on time with about 10 ms of lag.

Analyze A lab replaces one workstation with a render server plus a display client on a network. Explain the effect on latency and on the size of scene the lab can run. 5 marks

Scene size can grow, because the render server can be far more powerful than one workstation. Latency grows by the network hop and any compression, and the two machines must be synchronised. If the extra delay pushes the chain past the budget, the lab gains scene size and loses comfort.

Analyze Reprojection warps the previous frame with the newest head orientation instead of drawing a new one. Why does this help, and what goes wrong? 4 marks

It removes much of the perceived lag from head movement, because the image matches the current orientation without waiting for a full render. Moving objects and parallax are wrong, since old positions are reused, and repeated warping blurs edges. It hides a missed frame rather than fixing it.

Understand Why do standalone headsets run simpler scenes than wired workstations? 3 marks

The system-on-chip shares memory with the GPU and works inside a power and heat limit, so peak speed is lower. Scene detail is cut to keep the latency chain inside the frame budget.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Latency work is moving into the headset: asynchronous timewarp and late latching run on the headset's own processor, patching a dropped frame before it reaches the panel. Foveated rendering shades the centre of gaze fully and the edges cheaply, which is a computing-architecture decision as much as a graphics one. Cloud and edge VR stream rendered frames over a network, making network delay the dominant term and pushing research into prediction and compression.

4.8 3D rotations: yaw, pitch, roll and quaternions

An orientation says which way an object faces. Yaw, pitch and roll store it as three readable angles. A unit quaternion stores it as four numbers that never lock. A VR headset reports orientation many times a second, so the renderer must combine rotations without the Euler failure. This chapter gives both forms, the half-angle rule, and conversion.

You need Chapter 4.2 for homogeneous transforms and basic trigonometry.

Yaw, pitch and roll, and gimbal lock

Fix three axes to the object: up, side (right) and forward (facing). Each Euler angle turns about one axis.

  • Yaw turns about up: left-right, like shaking your head no. A compass heading is yaw.
  • Pitch turns about side: the nod, looking up or down.
  • Roll turns about forward: head tilt, one ear toward a shoulder.

(yaw, pitch, roll) is the orientation in degrees or radians. Each number has a plain meaning.

Order changes the answer: yaw 90° then pitch 90° differs from pitch first then yaw.

R = R<sub>up</sub>(yaw) · R<sub>side</sub>(pitch) · R<sub>forward</sub>(roll)

R is the final 3×3 rotation matrix. Roll applies first; yaw last. Angles in radians. Some systems use another order.

A mechanical gimbal has nested rings, one angle per ring. At pitch = ±90°, yaw and roll rings share an axis. One degree of freedom disappears; different yaw/roll pairs give the same orientation. This is gimbal lock. The cure: store one unit axis n̂ plus one angle θ (axis-angle), which has no order and no lock.

Quaternions

A quaternion is q = w + xi + yj + zk with i² = j² = k² = ijk = −1. Store four reals (w, x, y, z). A rotation quaternion is unit length.

q = (cos(θ/2), sin(θ/2)·n̂<sub>x</sub>, sin(θ/2)·n̂<sub>y</sub>, sin(θ/2)·n̂<sub>z</sub>), with w² + x² + y² + z² = 1

θ is rotation angle in radians; n̂ = (n̂x, n̂y, n̂z) is the unit axis. The angle is halved.

  • Composition: q_total = q₂ · q₁ applies q₁ first, then q₂. Quaternion product is not commutative.
  • Inverse: for a unit quaternion it is the conjugate q* = (w, −x, −y, −z).
  • Double cover: q and −q are the same orientation, so interpolation must pick the nearer sign.
  • Rotate a point: p′ = p + 2w(u × p) + 2u × (u × p), where u = (x, y, z) and × is cross product.
  • To a matrix: entries are quadratic in w, x, y, z; for example m₀₀ = 1 − 2(y² + z²).

After many multiplications, length drifts from 1. Divide q by √(w² + x² + y² + z²) every frame. Head trackers send orientation in this form.

Worked example

Question. A controller is rotated 90° about the z (forward) axis. Write the unit quaternion, rotate (1, 0, 0), and check one matrix element.

  1. θ = 90° = π/2 rad, so θ/2 = 45°. cos 45° = sin 45° = 0.7071.
  2. Axis n̂ = (0, 0, 1): w = 0.7071, x = y = 0, z = 0.7071. Length: 0.5 + 0.5 = 1.
  3. u = (0, 0, 0.7071). u × p = (0, 0, 0.7071) × (1, 0, 0) = (0, 0.7071, 0).
  4. u × (u × p) = (0, 0, 0.7071) × (0, 0.7071, 0) = (−0.5, 0, 0).
  5. p′ = (1, 0, 0) + 2(0.7071)(0, 0.7071, 0) + 2(−0.5, 0, 0) = (0, 1, 0).
  6. m₀₀ = 1 − 2(y² + z²) = 1 − 2(0.5) = 0, matching p′'s zero x.

Output

q = (0.7071, 0.0, 0.0, 0.7071)
p′ = (0.0, 1.0, 0.0)

Answer. q = 0.7071 + 0i + 0j + 0.7071k; p′ = (0, 1, 0).

Exam traps

  • Half angle, not full: 90° gives cos 45° = 0.7071.
  • Quaternion product is not commutative; q₂·q₁ applies q₁ first.
  • Re-normalise by √(w²+x²+y²+z²) after composing, or the matrix gains scale.
  • Euler order and intrinsic/extrinsic axes change the result.
  • Inverse of a unit quaternion is the conjugate, not component reciprocals.

At pitch ±90°, yaw and roll rings share an axis, so one number stops independent work. A quaternion stores axis and angle with one length constraint and no ordered rings, so no orientation loses a degree.

Analyze A headset composes 60 quaternions a second. After an hour the stored quaternion has length 1.004. Cause and fix? 5 marks

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Blending orientations uses slerp, spherical linear interpolation of quaternions. Animation rigs use dual quaternions so rotation and translation blend without shrinking. Robotics uses the exponential map on SO(3).

Takeaways

  1. A virtual object is geometry plus appearance, and both must be stored before the object can be placed or drawn.
  2. An indexed mesh with vertex normals and UV coordinates is the usual way to store a textured, lit surface compactly.
  3. Every world transform is a chain of matrix products running down the parent-child hierarchy from root to object.
  4. Physics modelling has two jobs: detect contact cheaply with bounding volumes, then compute forces that change motion or surface shape.
  5. Behaviour rules turn a static scene into a reactive one, and model management keeps that growing scene searchable.
  6. The graphics pipeline runs in a fixed order: model, view, projection, clipping, perspective divide, viewport, rasterisation and fragment shading.
  7. Real-time VR stays inside the frame budget by drawing only what is visible (culling) and only at the detail needed (LOD).
  8. VR needs parallel hardware close to the eye, because a missed frame is felt as sickness, not just as a slow picture.
  9. Yaw, pitch and roll are readable but suffer gimbal lock; unit quaternions avoid it and interpolate smoothly.

Glossary

Appearance
The surface description (colour, roughness, specular level, textures) that decides how light leaves the object.
Axis-angle representation
A rotation stored as one unit axis plus one angle turned about that axis.
Bounding volume
A simple enclosing shape such as a sphere or box used for fast rejection tests in culling and collision.
Broad phase
The cheap first stage of collision detection that uses bounding volumes or spatial grids to discard object pairs.
Collision response
What the system does after a contact is found: change velocity, deform the surface, play a sound or trigger a rule.
Depth buffer
A per-pixel store of the nearest depth so far, used to decide which fragment wins when surfaces overlap.
Draw call
One instruction from the CPU to the GPU to draw a batch of geometry; too many of them make the CPU the bottleneck.
Edge
The straight line joining two vertices of a polygon.
Face
A flat polygon, usually a triangle, bounded by edges and used as the drawing unit of a mesh.
Fill rate
The number of pixels a GPU can shade per second, the usual limit when many large or transparent surfaces overlap.
Frame budget
The time allowed for one frame, equal to 1000 divided by the refresh rate in hertz, so about 11 ms at 90 Hz.
Frustum
The truncated pyramid of space visible to a camera, bounded by the near plane, far plane and four side planes.
Geometry
The shape of a virtual object, stored as vertices, edges and faces in a mesh or point set.
Gimbal lock
The loss of one rotational degree of freedom when two axes of a three-angle rotation line up.
Homogeneous coordinates
Writing a 3D point as four numbers (x, y, z, w) so translation, rotation and perspective can all be done by matrix multiplication.
Indexed mesh
A mesh stored as a vertex array plus a triangle array that refers to vertices by number, avoiding duplicate data.
Narrow phase
The exact second stage that tests the actual triangles of the surviving pairs and reports the contact point.
Normal
A unit vector perpendicular to a surface at a point, used to compute lighting and to test which side faces the camera.
Occluder
A large nearby object that hides objects behind it and can be used to skip drawing what it blocks.
Rigid transform
A transform made only of rotation and translation, which keeps all distances and angles unchanged.
Scene graph
A tree of nodes holding transforms, geometry and behaviour, where children inherit their parent's transform.
State machine
A behaviour model written as a set of states plus the events that move an object from one state to another.
Texture mapping
Attaching a 2D image to a surface by giving each vertex UV coordinates into that image.
Vertex
A single point of a 3D model, usually stored with its position, normal and texture coordinates.
