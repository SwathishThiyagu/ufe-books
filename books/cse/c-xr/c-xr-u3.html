3.1 Output devices for 3D interfaces and how to choose

A VR system has three output channels: visual, auditory and haptic. Each channel needs a display that turns numbers in the scene into a signal the body can sense. The right device is not the most expensive one. It is the cheapest device that meets the task's real numbers: field of view, angular resolution, latency, update rate, users, mobility, hygiene and cost. This chapter gives the output half of the system block diagram, the units for each selection number, and a weighted scoring method. The worked example uses a fixed rupee budget.

You need Unit 1: components of a VR system, the Three I's, and VR versus 3D computer graphics, plus the display families in 3.3.

Three output channels

Output devices form the half of the VR system that faces the user's senses. The digital scene goes to three displays: a graphics display makes a 2D image for the eye, a sound display makes sound at the two ears, and a haptic display makes force or skin deformation on the body. The input half, trackers and controllers, measures the user; see Unit 2, Input Devices and Tracking.

Visual output is the most demanding channel. A head-mounted display must fill a wide field of view and keep angular resolution high while the head turns. Sound output is easier to satisfy: headphones can place cues at each ear, as the chapter on 3D sound displays explains. Haptic output is split by body part, as the chapter on haptic displays explains. A tracker is an input device; a display is an output device. Swapping them is a common diagram error.

Numbers that decide the choice

Selection uses measurable numbers. Write each number with its unit before comparing products.

  • Field of view (degrees): the angle the display covers. A wider field of view fills more of the viewer's natural view.
  • Angular resolution (pixels per degree, ppd): pixels per degree of view. Higher ppd means a sharper image at the same field of view.
  • Latency (ms): delay from a head movement to the matching image. Lower is better.
  • Update rate (Hz): images per second. Higher update rate gives smoother motion. Hz means cycles per second.
  • Stereo or mono: stereo sends one image to each eye; mono sends the same image to both.
  • Head tracking: the display follows head orientation. A head-mounted display needs it; a fixed monitor does not.
  • Simultaneous users: how many people can view or interact at one time.
  • Mobility: fixed room equipment or a wearable unit.
  • Hygiene: shared face contact and cleaning effort.
  • Cost (rupees): purchase price plus maintenance.

A single number can hide a bad choice. 1920×1080 total pixels on a large monitor can be less sharp than 2160×2160 per eye on a head-mounted display, because sharpness depends on ppd, not total pixels alone.

Weighted scoring in five steps

Some syllabi call this choosing output devices for 3D user interfaces. A weighted score makes the trade-offs visible.

  1. List the requirements with units, such as six users, head motion, 20 ppd, 20 ms latency and 5,00,000 rupees.
  2. Remove any candidate that fails a must-have, such as budget or user count.
  3. Give each remaining requirement a weight w from 0 to 1; all weights sum to 1.
  4. Score each candidate s from 0 to 5 on each requirement.
  5. Compute S = Σ wᵢ sᵢ. Higher S is better. Keep cost as a hard limit unless you score it too.

S = Σ wᵢ sᵢ

S is the total weighted score; wᵢ is the weight of requirement i, from 0 to 1; sᵢ is the candidate's score on requirement i, from 0 to 5; the sum runs over all requirements.

The eye and hand set the budget

The eye and hand are far more demanding than the ear. A small sound timing error is tolerated in many tasks. A visual lag or a weak force cue is noticed at once. So visual and haptic choice drives the budget. Spend on the display and the haptic device first. Use headphones or simple speakers for the audio channel, and link that choice to 3D sound displays.

The task decides the split. A walkthrough for many viewers needs a large display; a surgical rehearsal needs head motion and hand force; a desk design review needs stereo and fine hand control. Audio rarely changes the shortlist. Cost is not the only limit: space, power, cleaning time and staff training also count. A cheap device that needs a dark room or daily calibration can cost more over a semester, so put recurring costs in the same budget line.

Worked example

Question. A lab has ₹5,00,000. It must support (a) six students viewing one walkthrough together, (b) one student doing fine stereo work at a desk, and (c) one student rehearsing a surgical task needing head motion and hand force. Options: stereo monitor ₹1,50,000 (one user, no head motion, 1920×1080 total); head-mounted display ₹3,00,000 (one user, head motion, 2160×2160 per eye, about 20 pixels per degree); force-feedback arm ₹2,00,000; CAVE ₹40,00,000. Choose a set inside the budget and state what is deferred.

  1. Budget B = 5,00,000 rupees. CAVE cost = 40,00,000 rupees. CAVE / B = 40,00,000 / 5,00,000 = 8, so the CAVE is 8 times the budget. Drop it for requirement (a).
  2. Requirement (a) needs six simultaneous viewers. The only listed device that serves six is the CAVE at 40,00,000 rupees. It is out of budget, so group viewing is deferred; the monitor is a one-user device. Requirement (b) needs fine stereo at a desk with no head motion. Use the stereo monitor: 1,50,000 rupees. Remaining = 5,00,000 − 1,50,000 = 3,50,000 rupees.
  3. Requirement (c) needs head motion and hand force. The head-mounted display provides head motion, about 20 ppd per eye, for one user. Add it: 1,50,000 + 3,00,000 = 4,50,000 rupees. Remaining = 5,00,000 − 4,50,000 = 50,000 rupees. The hand force still needs the arm.
  4. Add the force arm: 4,50,000 + 2,00,000 = 6,50,000 rupees. This is 6,50,000 − 5,00,000 = 1,50,000 rupees over budget. Defer the arm to next year. Group viewing for six is also deferred because the CAVE is 8 times the budget.

Answer. Buy the stereo monitor and the head-mounted display for 4,50,000 rupees, leaving 50,000 rupees. Requirement (b) is met, and the head-motion part of (c) is met. Defer the force-feedback arm because the full set costs 6,50,000 rupees, which is 1,50,000 rupees over the 5,00,000 rupee budget. Defer group viewing for six because the only six-user device, the CAVE, costs 40,00,000 rupees.

Exam traps

  • Picking the highest-specification device first. Write the task numbers and the budget first.
  • Swapping input and output devices. A tracker measures the head; a display shows the image.
  • Comparing total pixels with per-eye pixels. 1920×1080 total is not the same as 2160×2160 per eye.
  • Treating latency and update rate as the same. Latency is a delay in ms; update rate is images per second in Hz.
  • Forgetting users and hygiene. One head-mounted display serves one viewer; a shared face pad needs cleaning.

3.2 Human visual system and visual perception

Every visual display is built for the human eye, a sensor with hard limits. It resolves fine detail only in a tiny central patch, covers roughly 200° of the world with both eyes, and reads depth from a handful of cues. Those numbers tell a designer how many pixels a headset needs, how wide the optics must be, and how much stereo disparity is safe. This chapter covers the eye's structure, its acuity, its field of view and its depth cues, then uses them to explain eye strain in head-mounted displays.

You need school-level optics (a lens forms an image) and the idea that the retina holds light-sensitive cells; see 3.1 for how the visual channel sits beside audio and haptics.

The eye as an optical instrument

Light passes the cornea, a clear curved surface that supplies about two thirds of the eye's bending power. The iris sets the pupil width, roughly 2 mm in bright light to 8 mm in dim light, trading brightness against sharpness. The lens then fine-tunes focus by changing shape, and that action is accommodation.

Cornea and lens form an image on the retina at the back of the eye. The fovea, a pit about 1.5 mm wide (near 5° of visual angle), sits at its centre and is packed with cones. The optic nerve leaves through the blind spot, which has no photoreceptors.

  • Cones: about 5 million per eye, three colour types, need daylight — photopic vision; densest at the fovea.
  • Rods: about 100 million per eye, no colour, work in dim light — scotopic vision; almost absent at the fovea, so a faint star shows better slightly off-centre.
  • Consequence: fine colour detail is seen only in the central few degrees; the rest of the field is coarse and nearly colour-blind.

Photoreceptors do not report absolute brightness; they adapt to the local level over seconds to minutes. That is why a dark room becomes usable after a while, and why a sudden light hurts.

Acuity: the smallest angle the eye can resolve

Acuity is the smallest angle two points can subtend and still be seen as separate. At the fovea in good light it is about 1 arcminute, one sixtieth of a degree, set by the spacing of cones.

θ_min ≈ 1 arcmin = 2.91 × 10⁻⁴ rad

θ_min is the minimum resolvable angle; 1 arcminute = 1/60 degree = 2.91 × 10⁻⁴ radians.

A display reaches that limit when each pixel subtends 1 arcminute, which is 60 pixels per degree. The pixel pitch needed at viewing distance D follows.

p = 2D tan(θ_min / 2) ≈ D θ_min

p is pixel pitch in metres (centre to centre), D is viewing distance in metres, θ_min = 2.91 × 10⁻⁴ rad. At D = 2 m, p ≈ 0.58 mm.

Angle, not size, decides sharpness: a large screen watched from far away and a small screen watched from close look equally sharp if their pixels subtend the same angle. Doubling both size and distance changes nothing.

One eye sees about 160° horizontally and 135° vertically. Two eyes together cover about 200° horizontally with roughly 120° of binocular overlap. Only the central few degrees are sharp, so the eye samples the scene with fast jumps called saccades, about three per second.

Depth cues and why head-mounted displays strain the eyes

Depth perception uses three cue families: binocular (both eyes), monocular (one eye, also present in a photograph) and oculomotor (signals from the eye muscles).

  • Binocular: retinal disparity. An object at distance Z falls on slightly different retinal positions in the two eyes, and the brain converts that shift into depth. The eyes also rotate to meet at the object; that convergence angle is an extra cue. See 3.4 for how displays manufacture disparity.
  • Monocular: occlusion (near hides far), relative size, linear perspective (parallels converge), shading, texture gradient, and motion parallax (near objects sweep faster as you move).
  • Oculomotor: convergence (eyes turn in) and accommodation (lens changes shape). Both are reliable only within a few metres.

A head-mounted display focuses its image at a fixed optical distance, for example 2 m, whatever the scene shows. If the scene puts an object at 0.4 m, the eyes converge for 0.4 m while the lens focuses for 2 m. This vergence–accommodation conflict is the main source of eye strain and headache in stereo headsets, and it limits how close rendered objects may appear. Designers keep disparity modest so the eyes never have to diverge.

The visual system also adapts. After long exposure the eyes re-tune, and on removal users may briefly misjudge distance; strong disparities or unusual fields of view leave measurable after-effects. Chapter 3.3 turns these numbers into display choices.

Worked example

Question. A stereo pair is shown on a screen 2 m from a viewer whose eye separation is 6.5 cm. Find the separation between the left and right images on the screen for an object that must appear (a) 1 m away and (b) 4 m away. Use s = e(D/Z − 1), where s is the on-screen separation in cm, e is eye separation, D is the screen distance and Z is the wanted depth in cm.

  1. Write the data in centimetres: e = 6.5 cm, D = 200 cm, and the wanted depths Z = 100 cm and Z = 400 cm.
  2. (a) D/Z = 200/100 = 2. Then D/Z − 1 = 1 and s = 6.5 × 1 = 6.5 cm.
  3. Positive s means crossed disparity: the left image is placed to the right of the right image, so the lines of sight cross in front of the screen at 1 m.
  4. (b) D/Z = 200/400 = 0.5. Then D/Z − 1 = −0.5 and s = 6.5 × (−0.5) = −3.25 cm.
  5. Negative s means uncrossed disparity: the images sit in the usual order, 3.25 cm apart, and the lines of sight meet behind the screen at 4 m.

Answer. The on-screen separations are 6.5 cm crossed for a 1 m object and 3.25 cm uncrossed (−3.25 cm) for a 4 m object.

Exam traps

  • Accommodation is the lens changing focus; convergence is the eyes turning in. They are different oculomotor cues, and an HMD pulls them apart.
  • Acuity depends on the angle a pixel subtends, not on screen size. A bigger screen farther away is not sharper.
  • Crossed disparity places the object nearer than the screen; uncrossed places it farther. Mixing the sign flips the depth.
  • The fovea holds cones and almost no rods, so night vision is weak exactly where daylight acuity is best.
  • About 200° is the field of both eyes with the head still; one eye alone gives about 160°.

3.3 Graphics displays: personal, head-mounted and large-volume

A graphics display is classed by how much of your field of view it fills. A personal display sits on a desk or in your hand and fills a small angle. A head-mounted display puts a lens and a small panel in front of each eye, so the image fills a large angle and moves with your head. A large-volume display projects on walls or a dome and fills the room. Some syllabi call all of this graphics display and split it into personal, head-mounted and large-volume. The class sets the optics, the pixel size you need and the space you must provide.

You need: Chapter 3.2 for acuity and field of view.

Personal displays: pixels, distance and angle

A personal graphics display is a screen used by one viewer at a fixed viewing distance: a desk monitor, a stereo monitor, or a phone held in a viewer. The panel has a pixel pitch p, the centre-to-centre distance between pixels, in millimetres. At viewing distance d, one pixel subtends θ ≈ p/d radians. This angular pixel size, not the pixel count, decides whether the eye sees smooth edges. A 0.25 mm pitch at 0.6 m gives θ = 0.25×10⁻³ / 0.60 = 4.2×10⁻⁴ rad ≈ 1.4 arcmin. The eye resolves about 1 arcmin, so the pixel is just visible as a dot; a denser panel or a larger distance hides it.

θ ≈ p/d

θ is the angle subtended by one pixel in radians; p is the pixel pitch in metres; d is the viewing distance in metres.

For a phone held 10 cm from a magnifying viewer, the same panel can fill a wide field of view; the lens changes the effective distance and hence the angular size. Personal stereo monitors send different images to the two eyes and are covered in Chapter 3.4. The personal display is cheap and comfortable but fills only the angle of the screen. Move your head and the world does not follow, unless the system adds head tracking.

Head-mounted displays: lens, virtual image and eye box

A head-mounted display (HMD) fixes one small panel per eye, or one panel split between the eyes, a few centimetres from the eye. A magnifying lens makes a virtual image of the panel. The eye focuses on that virtual image, not on the panel, so the panel can be close while the image appears far enough for comfort. The eye is relaxed when focusing at about 1 to 3 m or beyond. A real HMD therefore places its virtual image at that range; a 20 cm image forces strong accommodation and is tiring.

1/f = 1/v − 1/u, m = v/u

f is the lens focal length in metres, positive for a converging lens; u is the object distance from the lens in metres, negative when the object is on the incident side; v is the image distance in metres, negative for a virtual image on the same side as the object; m is the linear magnification, positive when the image is upright.

The field of view is set by the lens size and the eye distance. The exit pupil is the image of the lens aperture; the eye box is the region in which the eye pupil can sit and still see the full image. Interpupillary distance (IPD) adjustment moves the two eyepieces to match the distance between the user's pupils. Weight matters: a heavy HMD on the head becomes a torque, so the centre of mass should sit close to the face.

An opaque HMD blocks the real world and gives immersive VR. An optical see-through HMD uses a combiner to add computer light to the real view. A video see-through HMD uses cameras and shows the combined video on opaque displays. The last two are AR displays; see Unit 6.

Large-volume displays: CAVE, powerwall, dome and fish-tank

A large-volume display fills a room or a large surface. A CAVE has four to six projection walls, often three walls and the floor, with the viewer inside. Projectors throw images on the walls; software uses the viewer's head position to compute the correct perspective for each wall, so the images join at the edges. Overlapping projector edges are blended in brightness, and the geometry is calibrated, or seams appear. A powerwall is one very large flat screen, often tiled from projectors; it gives a wide field but no floor or side wrap. A dome projects on a curved surface, useful for sky or driving views. Fish-tank VR is the cheap case: a normal monitor with head tracking, so the screen acts as a window into a 3D scene. It gives motion parallax without a room.

The CAVE gives a wide field of view and lets several users stand together. It needs a room, projectors, calibration and dark walls. The wide field is its advantage; the room is its cost.

Worked example

Question. A head-mounted display has a lens of focal length 5 cm and its panel 4 cm behind the lens. Find the position of the virtual image, the magnification, and the angle subtended by one 10 micrometre pixel. Take the eye at the lens for the angle estimate.

  1. Use Cartesian signs: light travels left to right, so distances to the right of the lens are positive and to the left are negative. The panel is on the incident side: u = −4 cm. The lens is converging: f = +5 cm.
  2. Apply 1/f = 1/v − 1/u, which rearranges to 1/v = 1/f + 1/u. Then 1/v = 1/5 + 1/(−4) = 0.20 − 0.25 = −0.05 cm⁻¹. So v = −20.0 cm.
  3. The image distance is negative, so the image is virtual and lies on the same side as the panel, 20.0 cm from the lens.
  4. Magnification m = v/u = (−20.0)/(−4.0) = 5.0. The image is upright and 5 times larger than the panel.
  5. The panel pixel is p = 10 μm = 10×10⁻⁶ m. Its image size is 5.0 × 10×10⁻⁶ = 50×10⁻⁶ m.
  6. The image is 20 cm = 0.20 m from the lens. The pixel angle is θ = (50×10⁻⁶)/0.20 = 2.5×10⁻⁴ rad.
  7. Convert to arcminutes: 2.5×10⁻⁴ × (180/π) × 60 = 0.859 arcmin ≈ 0.86 arcmin, near the eye's 1 arcmin acuity limit.

Answer. The virtual image is at v = −20.0 cm (20.0 cm from the lens on the panel side), the magnification is m = 5.0, and one 10 μm pixel subtends 2.5×10⁻⁴ rad ≈ 0.86 arcmin. A real HMD would place the virtual image at 1 to 3 m for comfortable focus.

Exam traps

  • Confusing pixel count with resolution: the eye sees pixels per degree, and lens magnification changes the angular pixel size.
  • Using object and image distances with the wrong sign: measure u and v from the lens, positive to the right; a panel on the left has u negative.
  • Forgetting that a virtual image at 20 cm is not comfortable: a real HMD places it at 1 to 3 m, which changes the choice of f and u.
  • Treating exit pupil and eye box as the same: the exit pupil is the image of the aperture; the eye box is the allowed range of pupil positions.
  • Assuming a CAVE needs no calibration: projector blending and projection geometry must be aligned or seams and distorted edges appear.

3.4 Stereoscopic display technology

A stereoscopic display sends a slightly different image to each eye. The brain compares the two and reads the difference as depth. A single flat screen cannot do this alone, so every stereo display is really two displays — two images, two optical paths or two time slots — arranged so the left eye sees only the left image. Cinema, VR headsets and 3D monitors all use this trick. This chapter gives the geometry that decides where a point appears, then the main technologies and the limits that decide whether the result is comfortable.

You need: Chapter 3.2 on binocular depth cues, and Chapter 3.3 on display families.

Disparity decides where each point appears

The two eyes sit about e = 63 mm apart. Look at a point at distance Z through a screen at distance D. That point lands at two different places on the screen, one as seen by each eye. The gap between those places is the disparity, and the brain turns it into depth.

s = e (D/Z − 1)

s = signed separation of the two images measured on the screen, in mm, positive when the point appears in front of the screen; e = interocular distance, about 63 mm for an adult; D = distance from the eyes to the screen, in mm; Z = distance from the eyes to the point being shown, in mm.

Read the sign. If Z < D, the disparity is crossed: the left eye's copy sits to the right of the right eye's copy, so the eyes converge in front of the screen and the point appears in front of the glass. If Z > D, s is negative, the images are uncrossed, and the point sits behind the screen. Z = D gives s = 0 and the point sits on the glass. That is the zero disparity plane; a stereo scene is built by choosing a screen plane and pushing objects to either side of it.

Numbers: e = 63 mm, D = 500 mm, Z = 400 mm. Then s = 63 × (500/400 − 1) = 63 × 0.25 = 15.75 mm, positive, so the point floats 100 mm in front of the screen.

The eyes focus on the screen at D but converge on the point at Z. That mismatch is vergence–accommodation conflict, the main source of stereo eye strain. Keep disparity small: a common comfort guideline is about 1° of visual angle, which at D = 500 mm is 500 tan 1° ≈ 8.7 mm of separation. Large crossed disparity hurts more than large uncrossed, because near objects demand more convergence.

Time multiplexing: one panel, two frames

An active shutter system uses one panel and shows frames in turn: left, right, left, right. Liquid-crystal glasses darken the eye that should not see the current frame. Panel and glasses must agree on which frame is which, so an emitter on the display sends an infrared pulse before each frame; some systems use a cable instead.

f_eye = f_panel / 2 t = 1000 / f_panel

f_eye = frames per second reaching each eye; f_panel = panel refresh rate in Hz, counting both eyes' frames; t = duration of one slot in milliseconds.

The figure is a 120 Hz panel. Each slot lasts 1/120 s = 8.3 ms. The left image is on screen during slot L1 and only the left shutter is open; then R1 with the right shutter open. Each eye therefore receives 60 frames per second, and one eye's frames are 16.7 ms apart.

Drop the panel to 60 Hz and each eye gets 30 frames per second — visible flicker — and every slot is 16.7 ms long, so the wrong image stays on screen longer. That is why shutter systems ask for at least 100–120 Hz. Pixel response time must also fit inside the slot, or the tail of the left image is still glowing when the right shutter opens.

That leak is crosstalk, also called ghosting: the fraction of one eye's image that reaches the other eye, quoted as a percentage. Under about 1% looks clean; a few percent shows as a double edge on high-contrast objects and adds strain.

Colour, polarisation and space instead of time

  • Anaglyph. The two images are printed in complementary colours, usually red and cyan, and seen through matching filters; the red filter passes the red image and blocks the cyan one. Cheap on paper and on any screen, but colour is destroyed.
  • Linear polarisation. Two images are shown through polarising filters at 90° to each other and the glasses carry the matching pair. Separation collapses if the viewer tilts their head.
  • Circular polarisation. Same idea with left- and right-handed circular filters, which survive head tilt. Cinemas use it, with a silvered screen that keeps polarisation intact.
  • Lenticular. A sheet of cylindrical lenses in front of the panel sends even pixel columns to one eye and odd columns to the other, so no glasses are needed.
  • Parallax barrier. A grid of thin slits does the same job by blocking light instead of bending it; it costs brightness as well as resolution.
  • Head-mounted display. Two small panels, one per eye, with fixed lenses. Disparity is exact and crosstalk depends on the panels and optics, not on the room; the cost is weight and a per-user interpupillary distance setting.

The lens and barrier methods are autostereoscopic: no glasses, but each eye sees only half the pixels, so horizontal resolution halves, and the viewer must stay in a narrow band of positions — the sweet spots — or the eyes swap and depth inverts.

Worked example

Question. A shutter-glasses stereo display runs its panel at 120 Hz. (a) How many frames per second does each eye receive? (b) How long is each slot, and how far apart are two frames for the same eye? (c) The panel is switched to 60 Hz. Repeat (a) and (b) and state what the viewer notices.

  4. Consecutive frames for the same eye have the other eye's slot between them, so the interval is 2 × 8.33 = 16.67 ms, which is 1/60 s.
  5. At 60 Hz: f_eye = 60/2 = 30 frames per second, and one slot lasts 1/60 s = 16.67 ms.
  6. 30 frames per second per eye flickers visibly, and the longer 16.7 ms slot lets more of the wrong image leak through the shutter. Shutter systems therefore need a panel of at least 100–120 Hz.

Answer. Each eye receives 60 frames per second at 120 Hz (8.33 ms slots, 16.67 ms apart), and 30 frames per second at 60 Hz, which flickers visibly.

3.5 Human auditory system and 3D sound displays

The ears locate a sound from small differences between the two eardrums: arrival time, loudness and the filtering of the pinna. A 3D sound display exists because a visual scene without direction-aware audio feels flat, and because audio can point to events outside the screen. It is used in VR headsets, AR navigation, flight simulators, games and telepresence. The display must control the cues at each ear, not just play stereo. This chapter covers the ear, the three localisation cues and the main sound display types.

You need Chapter 3.1 for the output channel view; no earlier acoustics is assumed.

The ear as a frequency analyser

Sound is a pressure wave in air. The outer ear, or pinna, collects it; the ear canal guides it to the eardrum, a thin membrane. Three small bones, the ossicles, pass the vibration to the cochlea. The cochlea is a coiled, fluid-filled tube. Along its basilar membrane, high frequencies peak near the base and low frequencies near the apex. Hair cells turn that motion into nerve pulses on the auditory nerve. This place code makes the cochlea a frequency analyser. A healthy young ear hears about 20 Hz to 20 kHz; one hertz is one cycle per second. The upper limit falls with age and noise damage. The pinna is not a simple funnel: its ridges create small reflections and notches that depend on direction. That is why the shape of your own ear matters.

Three cues for direction

Direction comes from three cues. The first is interaural time difference (ITD), the arrival-time gap between ears.

ITD = (d × sin θ) / c

ITD is the interaural time difference in seconds; d is the ear spacing, about 0.175 m; θ is the angle from straight ahead in degrees; c is the speed of sound, about 343 m/s.

Using d = 0.175 m and c = 343 m/s, the straight-line formula gives a maximum of about 0.5 ms at θ = 90°. The commonly quoted maximum is about 0.6–0.7 ms; it is larger because sound also travels around the curved surface of the head, which the straight-line formula ignores. ITD is useful below about 1.5 kHz because low-frequency waves have long wavelengths and the phase difference between ears is unambiguous. Above that, phase wraps.

The second cue is interaural level difference (ILD). The head blocks short wavelengths, so the near ear receives more high-frequency energy. ILD is a few dB to about 20 dB and is useful above about 1.5 kHz. The third cue is the direction-dependent filtering of the pinna, head and torso. It is captured by the head-related transfer function (HRTF): the ratio of the pressure at an eardrum to the pressure in the free field for a given direction and frequency. HRTF supplies spectral notches that help with elevation and front-back.

Front-back confusion happens because a front and a rear source can give similar ITD and ILD; the set of such directions is a cone of confusion. Small head movements break the tie: the brain compares the change in cues with the known movement. A static binaural render therefore feels less external than a head-tracked one.

Sound display families

Sound displays differ in how many ears they control.

  • One loudspeaker gives no direction. It is enough for alarms and speech.
  • A stereo pair uses two loudspeakers. Panning sets a phantom source between them by level and delay. It works only near the sweet spot and mainly in the horizontal plane.
  • A multi-speaker surround system places speakers around the listener. It adds directions but still depends on room and listener position.
  • Wave field synthesis uses a line of many closely spaced loudspeakers. Each speaker gets a delay and gain so their wavefronts add to the wavefront of a virtual source. One line creates a convincing front stage, not full 3D around the room.
  • Headphones with binaural rendering feed each ear a signal filtered by an HRTF. This is the usual VR choice. A stereo loudspeaker pair can also deliver binaural audio, but the left loudspeaker reaches both ears. Cross-talk cancellation filters each channel so the left speaker cancels at the right ear and vice versa. It needs a fixed or tracked listener.

Head tracking updates the HRTF as the head turns. This gives dynamic cues, reduces front-back errors and keeps the scene fixed in space. Audio-visual sync tolerates tens of milliseconds, more for audio lagging video than for audio leading it: audio that leads the picture is noticed beyond roughly 45 ms, while audio lagging the picture is tolerated up to roughly 125 ms. Interactive audio needs low latency; a common target is under about 20–40 ms.

Worked example

Question. A click comes from 30 degrees to the right of straight ahead. The ears are 17.5 cm apart and the speed of sound is 343 m/s. Find the interaural time difference and the delay in samples at 44.1 kHz.

  1. Ear spacing d = 0.175 m; angle θ = 30°; speed c = 343 m/s; sample rate f_s = 44100 Hz.
  2. Extra path length = d × sin θ = 0.175 × sin 30° = 0.175 × 0.5 = 0.0875 m.
  3. ITD = extra path / c = 0.0875 / 343 = 2.55102 × 10⁻⁴ s.
  4. In milliseconds, ITD = 2.55102 × 10⁻⁴ × 1000 = 0.255102 ms.
  5. Delay in samples = ITD × f_s = 2.55102 × 10⁻⁴ × 44100 = 11.25 samples, rounded to 11 samples.

Answer. The extra path is 0.0875 m, the ITD is 2.55102 × 10⁻⁴ s (0.255102 ms), and the far-ear delay is 11.25 samples, rounded to 11 samples at 44.1 kHz.

Exam traps

  • Using ITD for all frequencies. Above about 1.5 kHz the phase difference is ambiguous, and the brain uses level difference and pinna filtering instead.
  • Forgetting front-back confusion. A front and a rear source can share ITD and ILD; the pinna and small head movements resolve the tie.
  • Delaying the wrong ear. Sound arrives first at the near ear, so the far ear is delayed. A right-side source means the left ear is delayed.
  • Treating HRTF as ordinary headphone equalisation. An HRTF is direction and frequency dependent; a fixed equaliser is not.
  • Calling stereo panning full 3D audio. Plain stereo has no elevation and weak front-back cues without HRTF and head tracking.

3.6 Haptic displays and force feedback devices

A haptic display creates controlled force, vibration or skin deformation on the user. It exists because seeing and hearing a virtual object is not enough when the task needs contact: pressing a virtual button, feeling a tool touch tissue, or holding a virtual cricket bat. Haptic displays are used in surgical simulators, teleoperation, assembly planning, game controllers and phone keyboards. This chapter groups them by what they push: the whole limb (force feedback) or only the skin (tactile feedback).

You need: Chapter 3.1 for the three output channels.

Force feedback pushes the limb

Force feedback, also called kinesthetic feedback, pushes the arm or hand. It controls force at the handle in newtons (N) or torque at a joint in newton-metres (N·m). A grounded arm is fixed to a table and ends in a pen or handle. Motors at the joints resist your hand. The Phantom family is a common pen-style example. Its workspace is a box of tens of centimetres, its maximum handle force is about 3 N to 4 N, and it updates at about 1 kHz.

An exoskeleton is worn on the arm or hand. It controls joint torque, often 0.1 N·m to 1 N·m, and finger force of a few N; its workspace follows the human joint range. A cable-driven system has motors on a frame and cables to the handle. It can cover metres and pull tens to hundreds of N, with update rates from 100 Hz to 500 Hz. A motion platform moves the whole body. It controls displacement in metres and updates at tens of hertz. All force devices have a saturation force: the maximum they can push. They also have a finite workspace.

Tactile feedback acts on the skin

Tactile feedback acts on the skin. Vibrotactile actuators are the most common. An eccentric rotating mass (ERM) motor spins an off-centre mass; a linear resonant actuator (LRA) drives a mass along one axis at resonance; a piezoelectric actuator bends a plate. They control vibration frequency in hertz (Hz) and amplitude in micrometres (µm). ERM and LRA units often run at 100 Hz to 300 Hz with amplitudes of a few µm to hundreds of µm, and update at about 1 kHz.

A pin array moves small pins up and down by about 0.5 mm to 1 mm, with millinewtons (mN) per pin; update is around 100 Hz. A thermal display uses Peltier cells to heat or cool the skin by a few °C, but it is slow, about 1 Hz to 10 Hz. An air-jet display blows a small nozzle of air at the skin; its spatial resolution is a few millimetres and update is tens of hertz. An electrotactile display passes small currents through electrodes to excite nerves; it has no moving parts and controls current in milliamperes and pulse width in microseconds.

Surface haptics adds a tactile effect to a touchscreen. It may vibrate the glass or control friction under the finger with a lateral force of a few mN. Phones use it for key clicks; car dashboards use it to confirm a touch without looking.

Workspace, force and loop rate

The hand is a fine sensor, but a haptic device has a small workspace compared with graphics. A desktop arm may move your hand 20 cm; a head-mounted display fills your whole field of view. The visual channel can update at 60 Hz to 120 Hz. A haptic loop often needs about 1 kHz, because a stiff virtual wall becomes unstable at low loop rates (see Chapter 3.8). 3.7 Human haptics: touch, tactile and kinesthetic sensing

Touch gives VR two channels at once. One sits in the skin and reports contact, edges, stretch and vibration. The other sits in muscles, tendons and joints and reports where the limb is, how fast it moves and how hard it pushes. A designer who knows which receptor carries which signal can pick the right actuator, the right pin pitch and the right frequency band instead of guessing. The same numbers decide whether a pin array, a vibration motor or a force-feedback arm is worth its cost in a surgical trainer, a training simulator or a phone.

You need: Chapter 3.6 for the device families.

Two senses inside one hand

Touch is two senses. When you lift a steel tumbler, the cutaneous system in the skin reports contact: pressure, edges, stretch and vibration. The kinesthetic system in muscles, tendons and joints reports the limb itself: position, speed and force. Most VR systems feed only the skin, so the user feels contact but never weight.

Mechanoreceptors are nerve endings in the epidermis and dermis, the outer and middle layers of skin, sitting above a fat layer. Two words classify them. Slow adapting (SA) receptors keep firing while a stimulus is held. Fast adapting (RA) receptors fire when the stimulus changes and fall silent when it is steady. Type I has a small receptive field near the surface; type II has a large field deeper down.

Density, two-point threshold and pin pitch

Receptor density is not uniform. The fingertip pad carries the highest density of Merkel and Meissner endings in the body; the palm has far fewer, the back of the hand and the forearm fewer still. Density sets the two-point discrimination threshold: the smallest gap at which two simultaneous touches feel like two points rather than one. It is about 2 to 3 mm on the fingertip pad, about 10 mm on the palm and a few centimetres on the back. Touch your forearm with two pencil tips and move them apart to feel the threshold yourself.

This number fixes the pitch of a pin array, a plate of small pins that rise and fall to draw shapes on the skin. Pitch is the centre-to-centre distance between pins. Pins placed closer than the threshold land inside one sensory patch and cannot be told apart, so you pay for actuators and drive electronics and gain nothing. The rule is pitch ≈ threshold: 2 to 3 mm at the fingertip, around 10 mm for a palm patch. A refreshable Braille cell, at roughly 2.5 mm dot spacing, sits exactly at the fingertip limit. The palm patch in Figure 3.7.1 uses a 5 mm pitch, which is finer than the palm can resolve; at the 10 mm palm threshold the same 10 mm patch needs only 2 × 2 = 4 pins.

N = (L/p + 1)²

N is the number of pins in a square array; L is the side length of the patch in mm; p is the pitch in mm; L/p is the number of intervals along one side; adding 1 gives the number of pins on that side, because n intervals need n + 1 pins.

The kinesthetic sense: length, force and angle

Muscle spindles lie parallel to the muscle fibres, wrapped in a small capsule with a sensory nerve around them. Stretching the muscle stretches the spindle, and its firing rate reports both muscle length and the speed of change. Golgi tendon organs sit where muscle meets tendon, in series with the fibres, and report force rather than length. Joint capsule receptors signal extreme angles and rotation speed, but most joint position sense comes from spindles and tendon organs combined. That combined sense is proprioception.

Proprioception is coarse next to touch. People match a joint angle to within a few degrees, roughly 2 to 5 degrees at the elbow, against 2 mm at the fingertip. Force is judged with a just-noticeable difference (JND) of roughly 5 to 10 percent: the force must change by that fraction of its base level to be felt. Stiffness is judged more coarsely still, at roughly 10 to 15 percent.

Three effects matter in design. Adaptation: a constant force fades within seconds, so a device holding a steady push feels weaker than it is. Masking: a strong vibration hides a weaker one nearby, so neighbouring pins should not run at similar frequencies. Cross-channel: vibration applied while the finger presses changes the perceived stiffness of the surface. The engineering payoff is a simple mapping from receptor to hardware.

  • Pacinian (RA-II): vibrotactile actuator at 200 to 300 Hz, for alarms, texture and contact events.
  • Merkel (SA-I): static pin displacement, for edges, shapes and Braille.
  • Meissner (RA-I): 5 to 50 Hz flutter, for slip and surface change.
  • Ruffini (SA-II): tangential skin stretch, for friction and grip cues.
  • Spindles and tendon organs: force or torque feedback, for weight and stiffness.

Worked example

Question. A fingertip pad carries a square pin array of 1 cm × 1 cm with pins on a 2 mm pitch. How many pins does it need? A palm patch of the same 1 cm × 1 cm is built at 5 mm pitch, finer than the palm's 10 mm threshold but still much coarser than the fingertip's 2 mm. How many pins does the palm patch need, and how many times fewer is that?

  1. Side length L = 1 cm = 10 mm. Fingertip pitch p = 2 mm.
  2. Intervals along one side = L/p = 10/2 = 5.
  3. Pin positions along one side = 5 + 1 = 6, at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8 and 10 mm.
  4. Total fingertip pins = 6 × 6 = 36.
  5. Palm pitch p = 5 mm. Intervals along one side = 10/5 = 2.
  6. Pin positions along one side = 2 + 1 = 3, so palm pins = 3 × 3 = 9.
  7. Ratio = 36/9 = 4. The same area needs four times fewer pins at the coarser pitch.

Answer. 36 pins on the fingertip array and 9 on the palm patch, so the palm patch needs 4 times fewer pins for the same area.

Analyze A haptic glove must signal object slip and also object weight. Which receptors must each cue target, and which effect limits the design? 5 marks

Slip is a change over time, so drive RA-I (Meissner) flutter at 5 to 50 Hz, helped by SA-II (Ruffini) tangential stretch for friction. Weight is a sustained force, so use a kinesthetic force-feedback mechanism that loads the tendons, targeting spindles and Golgi tendon organs. Adaptation limits the weight cue, since a held force fades within seconds and must be refreshed or modulated; masking limits the slip cue, since strong pins hide weak neighbours.

Ultrasonic mid-air haptics and electrotactile arrays try to remove the moving parts altogether. Neuromuscular stimulation aims to drive muscles directly through electrodes on the skin. 3.8 Haptic rendering, control and teleoperation

A haptic display feels real only if the computer keeps up with your hand. Haptic rendering is a control loop: read hand position, compute force, drive motors, repeat hundreds or thousands of times a second. Split the loop across two arms and you get teleoperation. The hand is fast and stiff and notices a few milliseconds of delay. Uses: surgical trainers, simulators, remote robot arms.

You need: Chapter 3.6, Chapter 3.7, and basic feedback and stability.

The rendering loop

  1. Read handle position x from the encoders, in metres.
  2. Collision: find the nearest point on the surface outside the wall, the proxy point x_wall.
  3. Compute the force with the spring-damper law.
  4. Send the force, as motor current, to the actuators.
  5. Repeat next tick. A typical rate is 1 kHz, so T = 1 ms.

F = k(x_wall − x) − bv

F is force in newtons; k is wall stiffness in N/m; x_wall is wall surface position in metres; x is handle position in metres; b is damping in N·s/m; v is handle velocity in m/s. The first term pushes the hand out of the wall; the second brakes it.

The hand is stiff and fast. Fingertip contact stiffness is tens of thousands of newtons per metre. A slow loop delivers force late: the wall feels spongy or the loop buzzes. Haptics runs on its own thread at 1 kHz; graphics draws at 60 to 90 Hz.

Stability: stiffness, damping, loop rate

b > kT/2

b is damping in N·s/m; k is wall stiffness in N/m; T is loop period in seconds. This is a rule of thumb for a virtual wall, not a proof.

With k = 2000 N/m and T = 1 ms, b must exceed 1 N·s/m. With T = 10 ms, b must exceed 10 N·s/m. Ten times the damping feels like thick treacle.

When you cannot raise the loop rate, smooth instead: interpolate force between ticks, filter the force signal, or lower k. A softer stable wall beats a stiff wall that buzzes.

Teleoperation: master, slave and scaling

A master arm sits with the operator; a slave arm sits at the remote site. Both carry encoders and motors. The master sends position to the slave controller; the slave sends back position or measured force.

Two common architectures. In position-position, master position commands the slave, and slave position is turned into a master force through a virtual spring. In position-force (master sends position, slave returns force), also called force reflection, the slave returns its measured contact force directly. Position-position is more robust when the slave is stiff; position-force feels contact sooner.

F_master = α F_slave

F_master is the force the operator feels in newtons; F_slave is the slave's measured contact force in newtons; α is the force scale factor, a plain number. Use α < 1 when the slave is strong, as with a large industrial arm. Use α > 1 when the slave is weak, as with a tiny surgical tool, so its small forces are magnified for the operator to feel.

Skipping force scaling is a common mistake: the felt force is too large to control or too small to notice. Delay also breaks the loop; even 50 ms causes oscillation, and time-domain passivity is one standard fix. The display is the plant, the computer is the controller, and your hand closes the loop.

Worked example

Question. A virtual wall has stiffness k = 2000 N/m. Using b > kT/2, find the minimum damping b for T = 1 ms and T = 10 ms, and say what the user feels.

  1. Write the rule: b > kT/2, with k in N/m, T in seconds, b in N·s/m.
  2. T = 1 ms = 0.001 s, so kT/2 = 2000 × 0.001 ÷ 2 = 1 N·s/m.
  3. T = 10 ms = 0.01 s, so kT/2 = 2000 × 0.01 ÷ 2 = 10 N·s/m.
  4. The slower loop needs 10 times more damping, so the wall feels thick and treacly instead of crisp. This is why haptics runs at 1 kHz while graphics runs at 60 to 90 Hz.

Answer. The damping must exceed 1 N·s/m at 1 kHz and 10 N·s/m at 100 Hz.

Exam traps

  • Thinking a stiffer virtual wall is always better. With a slow loop, more stiffness means buzzing; fix it with a faster loop or more damping, not a bigger k.
  • Using T in milliseconds in b > kT/2. T must be in seconds: 1 ms is 0.001, not 1.
  • Forgetting force scaling in teleoperation, so the operator feels a force the master arm cannot safely deliver.
  • Confusing the 1 kHz haptic loop with the 60 to 90 Hz graphics frame rate; they are separate threads.
  • A sign slip in F = k(x_wall − x): the force must push the hand away from the wall, not pull it in.

Takeaways

  1. A VR or AR system outputs through three channels, visual, auditory and haptic, and each channel must match a human limit.
  2. Choose the cheapest device that meets the task's real numbers for field of view, resolution, latency, force and comfort.
  3. The eye resolves about one arcminute at the fovea, so pixel density and optics decide whether a display looks sharp.
  4. Stereoscopic displays work by disparity, but crosstalk and vergence–accommodation conflict limit comfort.
  5. The ears localise sound from interaural time and level differences plus head-related filtering.
  6. Haptic displays are classed by what they move: the whole limb or only the skin.
  7. Human haptics has a cutaneous channel and a kinesthetic channel, with different receptors and adaptation behaviour.
  8. Haptic rendering is a control loop; stiffness, loop rate and damping decide stability, so teleoperation needs force scaling.

Glossary

Anaglyph
A stereoscopic method that uses red and cyan filters with matching coloured images to separate the eyes.
Autostereoscopic display
A display that gives stereo depth without glasses, using a parallax barrier, lenticular sheet or similar optics.
CAVE
A room-sized large-volume display where images are projected on walls and sometimes the floor.
Circular polarisation
A stereo projection method that uses clockwise and anticlockwise polarised light and matching glasses; it keeps separation when the head tilts.
Convergence
Inward turning of the eyes when they look at a near object.
Depth perception
The visual ability to judge distance and three-dimensional shape from multiple cues.
Disparity
The position difference of an object's image on the two retinas.
Fovea
The small central retinal region of highest acuity.
Ghosting
Visible leakage of one eye's image into the other eye; another name for crosstalk in stereo displays.
Haptic rendering
The real-time calculation and output of forces or tactile signals in response to user motion.
Head-related transfer function (HRTF)
A measured filter that describes how a sound from a given direction reaches each ear.
Interaural level difference (ILD)
The loudness difference of a sound at the two ears, used mainly for high-frequency localisation.
Kinesthetic
The sense of limb position, movement and force from muscles, tendons and joints.
Lenticular display
An autostereoscopic display that uses a lens array to send different image columns to different eyes.
Linear polarisation
A stereo projection method that uses two orthogonal linear polarisations; separation degrades if the head tilts.
Mechanoreceptor
A sensory cell or end organ in the skin that responds to mechanical deformation.
Motion parallax
The relative motion of near and far objects when the viewer moves, a strong depth cue.
Parallax barrier
A mask with slits placed in front of a display so each eye sees different columns of pixels.
Personal display
A display used by one person at close range, such as a monitor, phone or tablet.
Pupillary distance
The distance between the centres of the two pupils, used to set stereo separation in head-mounted displays.
Sound localisation
The auditory process of estimating a sound's direction and distance.
Tactile
Relating to touch sensation in the skin, including vibration, pressure and texture.
Teleoperation
Remote control of a machine by a human operator, often with force feedback.
Zero disparity plane
The depth plane in a stereo display where the two eye images coincide.
