1.1 What VR, AR, MR and XR are

Four names sit on one question: how much of what you see is real, and how much is computed. VR replaces the world. AR adds to it. MR locks the two together. XR is the family name. Exams test whether you can place a product on this map and justify the placing. The same map guides a developer choosing a display for training, retail or surgery.

Four names, four mixes

VR (virtual reality) is a computer-generated, interactive 3D world that replaces the real one. You explore it through a tracked display, usually a head-mounted display. Tracked means the system measures your head position and orientation and updates the view from that measurement. A virtual environment is the simulated world itself: its objects, physics and coordinates.

AR (augmented reality) lays virtual content over the user's view of the real world. The view may come through a camera (video see-through) or through a see-through glass display (optical see-through). Exams use three tests. It combines real and virtual. It is registered in 3D. It is interactive in real time. Registered in 3D means a virtual object holds a fixed place relative to real objects as the camera moves. Real time means the overlay updates fast enough not to swim.

MR (mixed reality) combines real and virtual so the two share geometry. A virtual cup stands on your real desk. Your real hand passes in front and hides the cup. Anchoring to real geometry and occlusion by real geometry are the marks.

XR (extended reality) is the umbrella term for VR, AR and MR. It is not a fourth display type. A product is XR because it belongs to one of the three. Chapter 1.6 turns these tests into a side-by-side comparison.

The reality–virtuality continuum

The reality–virtuality continuum, drawn by Paul Milgram, arranges environments by how much virtual content they contain. The left end is a real environment with no virtual content. The right end is a virtual environment with no real content. AR sits near the real end: virtual added to real. Augmented virtuality sits near the virtual end: real added to virtual. VR is the right end.

Some syllabi call the two middle points 'mixed reality'. Some call the whole span 'the mixed reality spectrum'. Use the definition given in your question paper and state it. The line is not a quality scale. VR is not better than AR; each fits a different task.

The figure shows the order. Notice that AR and VR sit on one line, not on two unrelated lines. The ends of the line are the real environment (left) and the virtual environment (right); AR sits near the real end and VR at the virtual end.

Depth ladder and virtual environment

The depth ladder describes how much of your senses the display takes over. Non-immersive VR uses an ordinary screen, keyboard and mouse. You see the room around the screen. Semi-immersive VR uses a large display or projection wall and some tracking. You still see part of the room. Fully immersive VR uses a head-mounted display or a room-sized projection cave and blocks the real world.

Depth is not the same as position on the continuum. A fully immersive headset can show AR if its cameras pass the real world through to your eyes. A desktop screen can show a virtual environment. The display decides depth; the content mix decides the continuum position.

A 360° video is a panorama, not a virtual environment, when your viewpoint is fixed to the recorded point and no world model responds. Dragging the view changes the look direction only.

Worked example

Question. Classify each product as VR, AR, MR or neither. Give the reason in one line. (i) A phone shows a 3D sofa standing against your actual wall, and it stays against that wall as you walk around. (ii) A headset shows an aircraft cockpit; your room is not visible at all. (iii) A headset shows your room, and a virtual ball rolls under the real table and vanishes behind its leg. (iv) A phone plays a 360° video that you drag with your finger.

  1. Test (i): a live camera view, a virtual sofa, and the sofa stays against the actual wall as the phone moves. The overlay combines real and virtual, is registered in 3D, and responds in real time. Answer: AR.
  2. Test (ii): the headset shows a computer-generated cockpit and your room is not visible. The real world is replaced. Answer: VR.
  3. Test (iii): the headset shows your room and a virtual ball moves under the real table and is hidden by the real table leg. Real and virtual share geometry, and the real leg occludes the virtual ball. Answer: MR.
  4. Test (iv): the phone plays a recorded 360° video. The viewpoint is fixed to the recorded point, there is no tracking of the room, and no virtual object is registered in 3D. Answer: neither; it is a panorama on a screen.

Answer. (i) AR, (ii) VR, (iii) MR, (iv) neither — a 360° video is a panorama, not VR or AR.

Exam traps

  • Calling any 3D picture on a phone 'AR'. AR needs registration with the real world, in 3D, in real time.
  • Calling a 360° video 'VR'. VR needs a tracked viewpoint and a responsive world, not a fixed recorded panorama.
  • Treating MR as a synonym for AR. MR needs anchoring and occlusion by real geometry; AR needs only the three tests.
  • Saying XR is more immersive than VR. XR is the umbrella; VR is one member.
  • Forgetting the third AR test: interactive in real time. A static overlay that lags is not AR by the exam definition.

1.2 History and development of virtual reality

Virtual reality did not begin in 2016 on a shop shelf. It arrived in three waves: flat-picture depth experiments and moving cockpit trainers before computers; a laboratory era in the 1980s and 1990s that built the first tracked head-mounted displays and gave the field its name; and a consumer wave from 2012 that pushed the same parts into cheap factory-made hardware. The waves explain why each part of a VR system exists (see 1.5), and they stop you from handing the whole invention to one person.

You need: 1.1 for the terms VR and virtual environment.

Depth before computers: 1838 to 1962

The first piece of VR was a viewing instrument: two drawings seen through lenses or mirrors, one for each eye. In 1838 Charles Wheatstone described the stereoscope: two drawings of one scene, made from viewpoints a little apart, one for each eye. The brain fuses the pair into a single object with depth. The lesson is large — depth needs no solid object, only two correct flat views. Every stereo headset still uses this trick; the display hardware is in Unit 3, Stereoscopic display technology.

The second half of VR is the loop: the user acts, the machine answers. Flight trainers supplied it. From the 1920s and 1930s, trainers such as Edwin Link's put a trainee inside a mock cockpit on a moving platform. The pilot read instruments and moved the controls; the rig tilted in reply. Nothing was drawn on a screen, yet the body felt motion and the pilot made real decisions, while an instructor watched and scored without risk.

In 1962 Morton Heilig patented the Sensorama, a booth for one person. It played stereo film with stereo sound, tilted and vibrated the seat, blew wind at the face and released smells. It worked, but the film was fixed: the viewer could not look elsewhere or change events. What it added was multisensory presentation — proof that one machine can address eye, ear, skin and nose together.

The laboratory era names the field

In 1965 Ivan Sutherland wrote a short essay on the ultimate display. A screen, he argued, should be a window into a world the computer builds, not a surface with marks on it. He pushed the idea to its edge: a display could, in principle, control the existence of matter in a room. That was a target, not a product, and the field chased it for fifty years.

In 1968 Sutherland's group built the first head-mounted display whose computer-generated image was driven by head tracking. Two small cathode-ray tubes, one per eye, showed the computer image. A mechanical arm measured where the head pointed. Displays and arm hung from the ceiling above the user, which is why the rig is remembered as the Sword of Damocles. The important part was the loop: turn your head, and the image turns with you. That loop, not the helmet, is what makes it VR; 1.4 explains why.

VPL Research, founded by Jaron Lanier in the 1980s, sold the hardware that made the idea famous. The DataGlove measured how the fingers bent, so a hand could act inside the world. The EyePhone was its head-mounted display. VPL also popularised the phrase virtual reality, which then displaced the older names used for the same idea.

NASA's VIEW system in the late 1980s put a head-mounted display, a glove, head tracking and the computer driving the scene into one working machine for astronaut training and remote operation — the parts of 1.5 working together. In 1992 came the CAVE: a room whose walls are projection screens, viewed through stereo glasses by several people at once, with no helmet. In 1995 Nintendo's Virtual Boy, a red monochrome display on a stand, proved uncomfortable to use and sold badly.

The consumer wave from 2012

In 2012 Palmer Luckey's Oculus Rift raised money on Kickstarter. It used cheap phone-grade motion sensors, a wide field of view and a low price. Developer kits reached thousands of programmers, and the field restarted after a quiet decade.

In 2014 phone-based viewers arrived: a phone as the screen, two plastic lenses, a cardboard or plastic shell. They cost very little and put stereo VR into millions of hands, but their tracking was poor. In 2016 consumer headsets reached shop shelves, with tracked hand controllers and room-scale tracking.

A few headsets around 2016 began to place cameras on the headset itself (inside-out tracking), so the headset watched the room and needed no external boxes. The first widely sold headsets of that year still used external cameras or base stations; inside-out tracking became the norm in standalone headsets a few years later. Unit 2 covers how such a tracker measures orientation.

  • Wave 1, before 1965: depth from two flat views, motion cueing, several senses at once.
  • Wave 2, 1965 to the 1990s: the tracked head-mounted display, the sensing glove, the name, the shared room.
  • Wave 3, 2012 onwards: low cost and wide field of view from the start, inside-out tracking arriving later in the wave, factory-scale production.

Worked example

Question. Four VR landmarks are given: the CAVE, the Oculus Rift Kickstarter campaign, Sutherland's tracked head-mounted display, and the Sensorama. (a) Arrange them oldest first with the year of each. (b) Find the gap in years between each consecutive pair. (c) State the largest gap. (d) In one line each, say what the landmark added.

  1. Collect the years: Sensorama 1962, Sutherland's tracked head-mounted display 1968, CAVE 1992, Oculus Rift Kickstarter 2012.
  2. Sort by year: 1962 < 1968 < 1992 < 2012, so the order is Sensorama, head-mounted display, CAVE, Rift campaign.
  3. First gap: 1968 − 1962 = 6 years.
  4. Second gap: 1992 − 1968 = 24 years.
  5. Third gap: 2012 − 1992 = 20 years.
  6. Largest gap = 24 years, between the 1968 tracked display and the 1992 CAVE.
  7. Contributions: Sensorama added multisensory presentation; the 1968 display added head tracking driving a computer-generated view; the CAVE added a room-sized view shared by several people; the Rift campaign added low-cost consumer head tracking with a wide field of view.

Answer. Order and years: Sensorama 1962, tracked head-mounted display 1968, CAVE 1992, Rift Kickstarter 2012; gaps are 6 years, 24 years and 20 years, so the largest gap is 24 years.

Exam traps

  • Giving one person everything. Sutherland built the first head-mounted display whose computer-generated image was driven by head tracking; Lanier's company VPL built the DataGlove and popularised the name; the CAVE came about two decades later.
  • Claiming VR was invented in the 2010s. The Rift campaign of 2012 revived an idea whose tracked head-mounted display already existed in 1968.
  • Calling the Sensorama a head-mounted display. It was a fixed booth for one seated person, with a recorded film and no head tracking.
  • Reading the Sword of Damocles as a product or a company. It is the nickname of one ceiling-hung laboratory rig, named for the arm and displays hanging over the user's head.
  • Mixing up the CAVE with a headset. The CAVE is a room of projection screens that several people can share without wearing a display.

1.3 Three I's: immersion, interaction and imagination

Any VR, AR or MR experience is judged on three things: immersion, interaction and imagination. The three I's give a simple checklist for designers and examiners. Immersion asks how completely the system surrounds your senses and blocks out the real world. Interaction asks whether you can act and get a timely reply. Imagination asks whether the virtual world offers a scenario worth entering and rules you can learn. A weakness in one I spoils the whole experience. The three I's appear in product reviews, usability tests and the first unit of many VR courses.

You need: 1.1 for VR and virtual environment.

Immersion: the system's push into the world

Immersion is the degree to which the hardware and software wrap the user in the virtual world. It is a property of the system, not a feeling. It has two parts. Sensory immersion comes from the display and the sound system. Mental immersion comes from the story, the task and your engagement.

Sensory immersion depends on measurable system properties: field of view, stereo display, resolution, frame rate, latency, spatial sound and haptics. A wide field of view covers more of your eye. Stereo gives each eye a slightly different image. High resolution and frame rate keep the image sharp and steady. Low latency keeps the image in step with your head. Spatial sound places a voice at your left ear. Haptics adds touch and force.

Mental immersion depends on content: a clear goal, a believable world, and a task that holds attention. A simple game with a strong task can immerse more than a photorealistic scene with nothing to do.

Keep two words apart. Immersion is a property of the system. Presence is the user's feeling of 'being there'. A system can be highly immersive by design, yet a tired user may feel little presence. Immersion helps presence, but the two are not the same word.

Interaction: the loop that must keep time

Interaction is the two-way exchange between the user and the system. The system reads input from the user and returns feedback. Input includes head tracking, hand tracking, data gloves, voice and controllers. Feedback includes image, sound and force.

Real-time response is the hard requirement. Head tracking that lags by more than about 20 ms between your motion and the matching image causes discomfort. The world arrives late. Your eyes see one position; your inner ear reports another. The conflict can cause dizziness, headache and nausea.

Interaction quality is not only speed. The mapping must feel natural. Turning your head should turn the view by the same angle. Reaching for a virtual switch should move the virtual hand by the same distance. Feedback must match the action: a virtual ball should sound like a ball when it hits a wall.

  • Head tracking: reads the orientation and position of the head.
  • Hand tracking or gloves: read finger and hand pose.
  • Voice: reads spoken commands.
  • Controllers: read buttons, triggers and sticks.

Imagination: the content and the mental model

Imagination is the designer's content and the user's mental model. The designer chooses the scenario, the rules and the difficulty. The user builds a mental model of what the world allows. A flight simulator lets you fly a plane without fuel cost or risk. A molecular viewer lets you walk through a molecule. A surgical trainer lets you rehearse a cut before you touch a patient.

Imagination is not only fantasy. It is the ability to do something that real life forbids: shrink to the size of a cell, pause a chemical reaction, repeat a dangerous procedure ten times. The rules must be clear. If the world breaks its own rules, the mental model fails and the other two I's cannot save the experience.

Some syllabi write the three I's as immersion, interaction and information. In most VR texts the third is imagination. Information is part of the content, but it is too narrow: imagination covers scenario, rules and the user's mental model.

Worked example

Question. A surgical trainer gives the trainee a headset with a 110° field of view and stereo sound, a tracked glove on each hand, and a virtual patient that bleeds when cut. (a) Name the three I's in this system. (b) The head-tracking lag is measured at 100 ms. The discomfort threshold is about 20 ms. Which I fails first, and what does the user feel? Give the excess lag and the ratio.

  1. Immersion: the 110° field of view, the stereo headset display, and the stereo sound are sensory system properties.
  2. Interaction: the tracked gloves read hand motion, and a cut returns visible bleeding and sound. This is the input-feedback loop.
  3. Imagination: the surgical scenario, the tissue rules, and rehearsal without a live patient are content and mental model.
  4. Lag check: measured lag = 100 ms; limit = 20 ms; excess = 100 − 20 = 80 ms; ratio = 100 ÷ 20 = 5.
  5. Failure order: interaction fails first. The image arrives 100 ms after the head motion. The eyes see one position while the inner ear reports another. This sensory conflict causes dizziness and loss of presence.

Answer. Immersion is the 110° stereo view and stereo sound; interaction is the tracked gloves plus bleeding and sound feedback; imagination is the surgical scenario. Interaction fails first: 100 ms exceeds the 20 ms limit by 80 ms (5 times), causing dizziness and loss of presence.

Exam traps

  • Writing immersion = good graphics. Field of view and latency matter more than polygon count.
  • Using immersion and presence as synonyms. Immersion is a system property; presence is the user's feeling.
  • Forgetting that mental immersion is not a display property. Story and task can immerse with simple graphics.
  • Missing the real-time limit. Head-tracking lag above about 20 ms causes discomfort, no matter how good the content.
  • Assuming the third I is always information. Most VR texts use imagination; information is a narrower reading.

1.4 VR versus 3D computer graphics; benefits of VR

A 3D computer graphics scene is built once and a camera path is chosen by an animator; the viewer watches a fixed film. VR closes a loop: trackers read the user's head and hands, the simulation updates, and the renderer draws a fresh view for each eye before the user notices the delay. That change from a spectator's picture to a participant's picture is the whole difference. It is used in surgical rehearsal, fire drills, machine training, building design review and rehabilitation.

You need: 1.1 for the definition and 1.3 for why latency breaks immersion.

Two different jobs

In 3D computer graphics, an artist builds geometry, materials and lights, then sets a camera path. The renderer may take seconds or minutes for one frame. The frames are stored and played back. The viewer is a spectator of an image the animator chose.

VR keeps the same geometry and rendering tools but changes who chooses the camera. Trackers report the user's pose: position (x, y, z) and orientation (yaw, pitch, roll). That is six degrees of freedom, or 6 DoF. The simulation updates the world, the renderer draws a left-eye and a right-eye view, the displays show them, and the user reacts. The loop from components is now closed.

The requirements follow from the loop: real-time rendering, stereo, a wide field of view, low latency, and 6 DoF tracking. Remove the loop and you have a 3D animation, not VR. See 1.1 for the boundary between VR and other XR forms.

The frame budget and latency

At 90 Hz the display shows 90 frames each second. One frame budget is 1000 / 90 ≈ 11.1 ms. At 120 Hz it is 1000 / 120 ≈ 8.3 ms. All tracking, simulation and rendering for that frame must fit inside this budget. The budget is per frame, not per second.

frame budget (ms) = 1000 ÷ refresh rate (Hz)

refresh rate is the number of frames the display shows each second; the budget is the time allowed for one frame.

Motion-to-photon latency is the time from a real head movement to the light from the new view reaching the eye. It is the sum of tracking delay, simulation time, rendering time and display delay. A common target is under about 20 ms. Above that, the world swims behind the head and many users feel sick; see 1.3.

Stereo doubles the drawing work, and a wide field of view adds pixels. A dropped frame is visible as a stutter. Tracking types belong to Unit 2, displays to Unit 3, and the rendering pipeline to Unit 4.

What VR buys you, and what it costs

VR pays for its complexity in places where the real thing is costly, rare or unsafe.

  • Training with no risk and no consumables: a fire drill with no foam, a surgical step with no patient, a machine start-up with no scrap.
  • Seeing what is too big, too small, too far or too dangerous: a building before it is built, a molecule, a live power grid.
  • Remote presence: an expert inspects a site or guides a repair without travelling.
  • Rehabilitation and pain distraction: gentle reaching games, burn-care scenarios.
  • Education: a cell, a Diesel engine or a historical site at full scale.
  • Design review and prototyping: catch a clash before concrete is poured, which saves money.

The limits are real. A headset costs more than a monitor. Cybersickness appears when latency or optical mismatch is bad. Physical feedback is missing, so a virtual drill has no weight or kickback. Content takes skilled effort to build. Human factors and safety are in Unit 5; AR limits differ because the real world stays visible, as 1.6 explains.

Worked example

Question. A headset refreshes at 90 Hz. One frame therefore has 1000/90 ≈ 11.1 ms. In a test, simulation takes 2 ms, rendering takes 7 ms and the tracker-to-display path adds 4 ms. (a) What is the motion-to-photon latency? (b) Does it meet a 20 ms target? (c) At 120 Hz the frame budget falls to 1000/120 ≈ 8.3 ms. The same 9 ms of simulation plus rendering no longer fits. Explain the result.

  1. Frame budget at 90 Hz = 1000 / 90 = 11.111 ms ≈ 11.1 ms.
  2. Latency = simulation + rendering + tracker-to-display path = 2 + 7 + 4 = 13 ms.
  3. Compare with the target: 13 ms < 20 ms, so it meets the target.
  4. Frame budget at 120 Hz = 1000 / 120 = 8.333 ms ≈ 8.3 ms.
  5. Simulation plus rendering = 2 + 7 = 9 ms. Since 9 ms > 8.3 ms, one frame cannot finish in one refresh period; frames are dropped and the view stutters.

Answer. The latency is 13 ms, which meets a 20 ms target; at 120 Hz the budget is 8.3 ms, so the same 9 ms of simulation plus rendering does not fit.

Exam traps

  • Calling any 3D graphics seen in a headset VR. The marks are for the closed real-time loop, two eye views, and the latency budget; a pre-rendered walkthrough at 5 frames per second is 3D graphics.
  • Confusing refresh rate with latency. 90 Hz gives a frame budget of 1000/90 ≈ 11.1 ms, not 90 ms, and motion-to-photon latency is a separate sum.
  • Counting 6 DoF wrongly. It is three position values plus three orientation angles, not six position sensors or six separate trackers.
  • Treating low latency as optional. Examiners want the loop, stereo, 6 DoF tracking and a motion-to-photon target under roughly 20 ms.

Understand Why must the camera pose in VR come from the user rather than an animator? 3 marks

VR is a closed loop: the view must match the tracked head so the user's motion changes the image immediately; an animator's path makes the viewer a spectator.

Apply A display refreshes at 72 Hz. Find the frame budget in milliseconds. 3 marks

1000 / 72 ≈ 13.9 ms.

Apply Simulation takes 3 ms, rendering takes 6 ms, and the tracker-to-display path takes 5 ms. Find the latency. Does it meet 20 ms? At 120 Hz, does 9 ms of simulation plus rendering fit? 4 marks

Latency = 3 + 6 + 5 = 14 ms. Yes, 14 < 20 ms. At 120 Hz the budget is 1000/120 ≈ 8.3 ms, so 9 ms does not fit.

Analyze A pre-rendered 3D walkthrough runs at 5 frames per second and lets the user look around with key presses. Why is it not VR? 4 marks

There is no continuous tracked pose, no per-eye real-time rendering tied to head motion, and the motion-to-photon delay is huge; it is 3D graphics played back.

Understand Give three benefits and one limit of VR. 5 marks

Benefits: safe training with no risk or consumables; seeing things too big, small or dangerous; design review that saves money. Limit: headset cost, cybersickness, missing physical feedback or content effort.

Analyze Why can a 120 Hz headset demand more optimisation than a 90 Hz one? 5 marks

The frame budget drops from 11.1 ms to 8.3 ms; the same scene must finish sooner or frames drop, so a smoother display is harder to feed.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Modern headsets use foveated rendering, where only the gaze region is drawn at full detail, to buy back frame time. Varifocal and light-field displays try to fix the vergence-accommodation conflict that causes eye strain. Research measures motion-to-photon latency with photodiodes and high-speed cameras, not by feel. Pass-through cameras blur the line between VR and video see-through AR. Search for 'foveated rendering', 'motion-to-photon measurement' and 'vergence-accommodation conflict'.

1.5 Components of a VR system

A VR system is not one device. It is four parts working as one loop: input devices that sense the user, output devices that stimulate the senses, a computer that runs the world model and simulation, and the human at the end of both. The human is a part because human senses set the requirements. Designers use this split to budget cost, find the weak link and debug an experience. A cheap phone viewer and a laboratory CAVE both contain the same four parts; only the hardware and software inside each part change.

You need: 1.1 for the terms and 1.4 for the closed loop these parts implement.

Four parts in one closed loop

Every VR system has four parts. Input devices sense what the user does: head turn, hand position, button press, spoken command. Output devices stimulate the user's senses: eyes, ears, skin and muscles. The computer and software hold a model of the virtual world and update it. The human is the fourth part, because the loop closes through a person.

The split is practical. If a training simulation feels wrong, the fault can sit in any part: a tracker that drifts, a display that lags, a physics engine that lets a virtual cup pass through a table, or a task that asks the eyes to do work they cannot do. Naming the four parts turns a vague complaint into a checklist.

The human belongs on the list because human limits set the requirements. A display must update fast enough that the eye reads motion as continuous. A sound must arrive close enough in time to the picture that the brain binds them. A haptic device must push back within the range a hand can feel. The interface is matched to human limits, not the other way round. This closed loop is the difference between VR and ordinary 3D graphics, as 1.4 explains.

Head-mounted displays, haptic devices and the inside of trackers are taken apart later. Here each part gets a name and a role.

Input and output devices

Input devices fall into three groups. 3D position trackers measure where a body part is and how it is oriented. The main types are mechanical (linked arms), magnetic (coils in a field), ultrasonic (sound pulses), optical (cameras and markers) and inertial (accelerometers and gyroscopes). Data gloves and gesture input devices measure finger bend and hand shape. Navigation and manipulation interfaces let the user move and act: 3D mice, joysticks, wands and voice commands. Unit 2 covers how each works.

Output devices also fall into three groups. Graphics displays give the eyes a stereo image. Personal displays are worn on the head, such as a head-mounted display (HMD). Large-volume displays surround the user, such as a CAVE (a room whose walls are screens), a dome, or a wall. Sound displays give the ears spatial audio, so a virtual sound can come from the left or above. Haptic displays give the skin and muscles force or vibration: a glove that resists your fingers, a controller that shakes, a tool that pushes back. Unit 3 covers optics, spatial audio and haptic rendering.

The output set decides how many senses are engaged. A phone viewer engages sight and sound. A CAVE engages sight over a wide field and can add sound. A haptic rig adds touch and force. More output channels usually mean more immersion, but only if each channel is fast and consistent with the others.

The computer and the software half

The computer is the part that holds and changes the virtual world. Its software has four jobs.

World model or scene database. This stores what exists: objects, their shapes, colours, positions, materials and links. A virtual classroom might store 30 desks, a board and a teacher avatar.

Simulation engine. This applies rules and physics and updates state. If the user pushes a virtual box, the engine computes the new position, checks collisions and decides whether the box slides or stops. It also runs scripted behaviour, such as a door opening when a hand touches it.

Rendering engine. This draws the world from the user's current viewpoint. It takes the world model and the tracked head and hand data and produces the left and right eye images, the sound mix and, where present, haptic signals.

VR toolkit or game engine. This ties the pieces together: it loads the scene, connects tracker drivers, schedules the simulation step, calls the renderer and handles input events. Some syllabi call the software half the virtual world generator, because it generates the world the user experiences.

The parts run in a loop. Read trackers, update simulation, render, send output, repeat. If one stage is slow, the whole loop misses its deadline and the user feels lag. The figure draws the four parts as this loop, using the student's phone setup from the worked example.

Exam traps

  • Calling the headset the whole VR system. Marks are for input, output, computer/software and the human.
  • Forgetting the software half: the world model and simulation engine are part of the system, not optional extras.
  • Mixing input and output: a tracker is input; a haptic glove that pushes back is output, while a data glove that senses finger bend is input.
  • Treating the human as outside the system. Human senses set update rate, field of view and force limits.
  • Saying more sensors always means more immersion. A 3-DoF orientation tracker cannot give positional lean, and missing output channels cap immersion too.

The output side, specifically sound timing against graphics timing, or the runtime that schedules both. The human brain binds sight and sound only within a short window, so a 200 ms mismatch breaks the illusion. Fix the timing in the runtime and sound output, not the world model.

Understand What does the simulation engine do that the rendering engine does not? 3 marks

The simulation engine applies rules and physics and updates the state of the world. The rendering engine draws that state from the user's viewpoint. Simulation decides what happens; rendering decides how it looks and sounds.

Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Industry splits the same four parts across OpenXR runtimes, game engines such as Unity and Unreal, and haptic middleware; the split helps teams divide work. Research on foveated rendering, inside-out tracking and wearable haptics changes the hardware layer, not the four-part model. Digital twins and teleoperation add a real sensor stream to the input side. Search for OpenXR, WebXR, scene graph, haptic rendering and motion-to-photon latency to go deeper.

1.6 Differences between AR, VR and MR

VR, AR and MR answer one question: how much of the real world should the computer keep, and how much should it replace? VR replaces your view entirely. AR draws virtual content on top of the real view. MR lets a virtual object sit behind your real coffee table. The difference is not the headset or the brand. It is three tests you can state and measure: what reaches your eye, whether the system knows where the room is, and whether virtual content is anchored to and hidden by real things.

You need: 1.1 for the four definitions and 1.5 for the components compared here.

Three tests, not a product list

A difference you can defend in an exam comes from a test, not a feeling. Products change every year; the tests do not.

Test 1 — how much of the real world reaches your eye. In VR the display is opaque: no light from the room reaches you, and the computer supplies every pixel. In AR the real world is the background and virtual content is drawn over it. MR is also see-through, but the system additionally reads the shape of the room.

Test 2 — does the system know where the real world is? VR needs only your own pose: where your head and hands are inside its own coordinate frame. The room is never drawn, so no map of it is needed. Modern headsets get that pose by SLAM or visual-inertial odometry, but they do not register content to real surfaces. AR and MR must register the virtual world to the real one, so a label sits on the right engine part and not 20 cm to its left. Registration uses markers, natural features or SLAM; the mechanics are in Unit 6.

Test 3 — is virtual content anchored to real objects and hidden by them? In simple AR a virtual arrow floats over everything, and the real table in front never covers it. In MR the arrow goes behind the real table when the table is nearer. That needs depth: the system must know the table is 0.6 m away and the arrow is 1.2 m away.

hidden if z_v > z_r ; draw if z_v ≤ z_r

z_v is the distance from your eye to the virtual surface, in metres; z_r is the distance from your eye to the nearest real surface along the same line of sight, in metres. The system writes real depths into the depth buffer first, then draws the virtual object; the usual depth test hides pixels that fail.

The definition used in this chapter. MR = AR plus per-pixel depth of the real scene. Anchoring to a plane or marker already exists in AR; MR adds metric depth, so virtual content is placed in 3D and correctly hidden by real objects. Some syllabi fold MR into AR; write your definition in the first line and stay consistent.

Phone AR sits at the low end of tests 2 and 3. The phone knows its own pose in the room but usually not that the real coffee table stands in front of your virtual sofa. A headset with depth sensors does. That is the practical line between AR and MR today.

Content, interaction, mobility, cost and safety

  • Content. VR is fully synthetic: every object is modelled. AR overlays a few virtual objects on a real view. MR merges both, and virtual objects respond to real surfaces — rest on the real floor, stop at the real wall.
  • Interaction. VR uses controllers, tracked hands and gaze. AR often needs free hands, so input is voice, gaze or a phone tap; a technician holds a spanner, not a controller. MR allows direct 3D manipulation of objects in the room.
  • Mobility. Phone AR is the most mobile, because it is the phone you already carry. VR headsets are tethered or limited by battery.
  • Cost. In the usual consumer range, phone AR is cheapest, a plain VR headset comes next, and a headset with real-scene depth for occlusion costs the most.
  • Safety. In VR you cannot see the room, so clear the floor before you start. In AR and MR the room is visible, but you can still walk into a door while watching the overlay.

Choose by the tests, not by fashion. Use VR when the real world would only get in the way: a surgical drill, a flight deck, a factory line not built yet. Use AR when the real world is the subject and the user needs free hands. Use MR when virtual objects must behave as physically present — fit, occlusion, layout.

Worked example

Question. For each case choose VR, AR or MR and justify in one line. (a) A medical student practises a knee operation with no patient. (b) A technician servicing a tractor engine needs both hands free with the manual in view. (c) A shopper checks whether a 2.4 m sofa fits a 2.7 m wall, and wants the sofa to look right behind a coffee table 0.6 m in front of the sofa. Compute the clearance left on the wall.

  1. Test 1, is the real world needed? (a) No, the anatomy must replace the room, so VR. (b) Yes, the engine is the subject, so AR or MR. (c) Yes, the wall and floor are the subject, so AR or MR.
  2. Test 2, must the system know where the real world is? (a) Only head and tool pose, which VR tracks already. (b) The overlay must sit on the correct engine part, so registration is needed, so AR. (c) The sofa must sit on the real floor against the real wall, so registration is needed, so AR or MR.
  3. Test 3, must real objects hide virtual ones? (b) Nothing has to hide the overlay; free hands and a stable overlay are enough, so AR. (c) The sofa must disappear behind the coffee table at 0.6 m, so occlusion is needed, so MR.
  4. Clearance on the wall: 2.7 m − 2.4 m = 0.3 m of total spare width.
  5. If the sofa is centred, that spare width splits into 0.3 m ÷ 2 = 0.15 m on each side.
  6. Answers: (a) VR, (b) AR, (c) MR — AR only if the shopper accepts a sofa that floats in front of the coffee table.

Answer. (a) VR, (b) AR, (c) MR; the sofa leaves 0.3 m of clearance on the wall, that is 0.15 m on each side when centred.

Exam traps

  • "VR is more immersive" is not a difference. It names a feeling, not a test. Answer with the three tests.
  • AR and MR are not separated by the headset. A phone does AR; a headset with cameras can do either. Depth sensing and occlusion separate them.
  • Do not call a camera pass-through view on a VR headset "AR" by reflex. If it anchors content to the room and hides it behind real objects, it is MR here.
  • Fix your definition of MR in the first line. If you place MR inside AR, do not later describe it as standing between AR and VR.
  • Occlusion needs depth of the real scene. A marker gives pose, not depth; the marker alone cannot hide a virtual object.

Understand Why does VR not need to register content to the real room, while AR does? 3 marks

Understand Give one reason a phone AR app rarely hides a virtual object behind a real chair. 3 marks

The phone usually has no depth map of the room, so it does not know the chair is nearer than the virtual object. Without z_r it cannot test z_v > z_r, so it simply draws the object on top.

Apply A worker repairing a transformer must keep both hands on a wrench while reading the next step of a procedure. Which technology, and which test decides it? 4 marks

AR. The real transformer is the subject (test 1 keeps the real view) and the hands must stay free, so the overlay is voice or gaze controlled. No real object has to hide the overlay, so test 3 does not force MR.

Apply A furniture app must place a virtual sofa on the real floor and hide its lower edge behind a real sofa in front. Which test decides the technology, and what is the choice? 4 marks

Test 3, occlusion and anchoring. The app needs a depth map of the real sofa, so the choice is MR; plain AR would draw the virtual sofa over the real one.

Analyze A headset shows the room through its cameras, tracks surfaces with depth sensors, and draws a virtual lamp that disappears behind the real sofa when the user walks past. Classify the system by all three tests and justify the label. 6 marks

Test 1: the real room reaches the eye through camera pass-through, so it is not VR. Test 2: it registers virtual content to real surfaces using depth sensors, satisfying the AR requirement. Beyond the syllabus

Not for the exam. Pass-through on consumer headsets blurs the line: the same device is VR with the cameras off and MR with them on and depth reconstruction running. Research is pushing dense depth out of a single moving camera, so occlusion stops needing a dedicated depth sensor. Takeaways

  1. VR, AR, MR and XR are four mixes of real and computer-generated content, not four unrelated technologies.
  2. The reality–virtuality continuum runs from the real environment to a fully virtual environment, with AR and augmented virtuality in between.
  3. VR history is best remembered as three waves: early stereo and simulator work, the 1980s–90s laboratory era, and the 2010s consumer wave.
  4. Every VR experience is judged on immersion, interaction and imagination; one weak element spoils the whole experience.
  5. 3D computer graphics produces a picture chosen by the animator, while VR produces a picture chosen by the user's head and hands in real time.
  6. A VR system has four parts: input devices, output devices, a computer running the world model and simulation, and the human user.
  7. VR, AR and MR differ on how much real world reaches the eye, whether the system knows where the real world is, and whether virtual content is anchored to real objects.
  8. VR benefits include safe training, clear visualization of complex data, remote collaboration and repeatable practice.
